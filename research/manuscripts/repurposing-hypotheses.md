## Abstract

Extraskeletal myxoid chondrosarcoma (EMC) is an ultra-rare *NR4A3*-rearranged sarcoma, classified
by the World Health Organization among tumours of uncertain differentiation, with an indolent but
relentlessly metastasising course and no established systemic therapy. We mapped its documented
molecular and microenvironmental vulnerabilities to existing agents not yet reported in it, graded
every hypothesis on an explicit evidence tier, and report the measured yield of three approaches to
generating candidates. Literature-driven curation produced 12 of the 14 candidates. A reproducible
target-to-drug enumeration over nine genes contributed 2, on three of the eight vulnerability axes.
A pretrained graph foundation model, run zero-shot, contributed none. Across the menu, evidence
strength and novelty are anti-correlated: the one candidate with EMC clinical evidence, imatinib in
the *KIT*-mutant minority, is also the only one already reported; every untried candidate rests on
preclinical or model-screen data; and the cell combining novelty with clinical evidence is empty.
Two rationales for the proteasome lead were pre-specified, run, and both returned negative, leaving
that lead resting on a published ex-vivo measurement made by others in one patient-derived model.
The graph model placed the two most clinically active agents in EMC in its bottom quintile, and did
so equally at two commoner sarcoma disease nodes, so the divergence is not specific to the EMC node;
the cause is not settled by that test. No efficacy is claimed for any agent named here.

*Keywords:* extraskeletal myxoid chondrosarcoma; *NR4A3*; drug repurposing; rare sarcoma; evidence
grading; knowledge-graph models.

## 1. Introduction

### 1.1 The disease and the unmet need

EMC is a rare soft-tissue sarcoma defined molecularly by rearrangement of *NR4A3*, also called
*NOR1*, *CHN* or *TEC*, most often as the *EWSR1::NR4A3* fusion and less commonly as
*TAF15::NR4A3* and other variants [1,2]. Despite the name, it is not a cartilaginous tumour: the
World Health Organization classifies it among malignant mesenchymal neoplasms of uncertain
differentiation, as the authors of the ex-vivo models used below state in describing it [13]. The
fusion produces a chimeric transcription factor that drives the transcriptional programme of the
tumour, including activation of target genes through chromatin modification [3], which makes EMC a
prototypical fusion-addicted transcription-factor cancer. The clinical course is paradoxical:
typically slow-growing and associated with prolonged survival, yet carrying a high cumulative rate
of local recurrence and of distant, especially pulmonary, metastasis over years to decades [1].
Even once metastatic the course can be protracted, and outcomes vary widely by era and by
completeness of surgery [4].

Surgery, with or without radiotherapy, controls localised disease, but no systemic therapy is
established in advanced EMC. The anti-angiogenic multikinase inhibitors are the most consistently
active class, on two reports. In a multicentre, single-arm, open-label phase 2 trial of pazopanib
(NCT02066285), 26 patients entered, 23 met the eligibility criteria for the modified
intention-to-treat analysis, and 22 were evaluable for the primary endpoint, of whom four (18%, 95%
CI 1 to 36) had a RECIST objective response [5]. In a retrospective series of 10 consecutive
patients treated with sunitinib on named-patient use, six had a partial response, two had stable
disease and two progressed [6]. A median progression-free survival of 19 months is widely quoted
for the pazopanib trial; the value reaching this manuscript is read from the 2025 comprehensive
review's account of that trial rather than from the primary report, and is used here as a secondary
reading [1]. Response rates for anthracycline-based chemotherapy are low [1].

Clinical next-generation sequencing of advanced EMC characteristically reveals no recurrent,
directly actionable driver mutation beyond the defining fusion [7]. *KIT* mutations are rare, and
the two reported classes are not equivalent. An exon-11 in-frame deletion, which is the
imatinib-sensitive class in gastrointestinal stromal tumour, was found in 1 of 20 EMCs in one
series [8]; a second series reports a *KIT* p.E554K mutation in 2 of 48, and no source consulted
here characterises that variant as imatinib-sensitive [2]. The index treated case carried an
exon-11 mutation [9]. Summing the two fractions to a single actionable frequency would therefore
overstate the addressable minority. CD117/KIT protein, by contrast, is detectable by
immunohistochemistry in a substantial but variable proportion of cases, approximately 53% in one
series and approximately 84% of 31 cases in another [2,10]. That distinction matters clinically,
because imatinib acts on the activating mutation rather than on protein expression alone. EMC
therefore sits in a difficult space: too rare for adequately powered de-novo trials, and without an
obvious single druggable mutation to anchor a targeted-therapy programme.

### 1.2 The case for repurposing, and what this review adds

Re-deploying agents that are already approved, or that carry substantial human safety data, suits
this setting. It removes the largest cost and time barriers of de-novo development, lets a
candidate move quickly to a biomarker-matched study, and aligns with the economics of neglected
diseases, where off-patent agents lack a commercial sponsor to fund confirmatory trials. The
difficulty is choosing which existing agents to test, rationally and transparently, without
overstating the evidence.

The standing reference for EMC is the 2025 comprehensive review cited above, which covers
pathogenesis, diagnosis and management through to advanced disease, and which states independently
that no clinically validated agent directly targets *NR4A3* [1]. The present review is
complementary to it, and adds a systematic, reproducible enumeration of agents *not yet reported*
in EMC, an explicit evidence tier attached to each, the structural reading that follows from
tabulating evidence strength against novelty, and the measured yield of each generation approach,
including two that yielded nothing. Nothing already covered by that review is claimed as novel
here, and the anti-angiogenic class, the anthracycline backbone and pazopanib in particular are
treated throughout as established context rather than as findings. One item of older prior art
bears on the menu: a 2007 review of chondrosarcoma named methylthioadenosine phosphorylase among
targets it described as validated by translational research in that tumour group [11], and the same
abstract describes EMC as a distinct, fusion-defined entity. Because EMC is not a chondrosarcoma,
prior art in the cartilaginous tumours bounds nothing about EMC, and no novelty claim below is
qualified by it.

## 2. Methods

### 2.1 Scope and inclusion

We considered only agents that already exist as approved drugs or carry substantial human safety
data, that have a mechanistic rationale against a documented EMC vulnerability, and that have not,
to our knowledge, been reported as tried in EMC. Candidates are assigned to eight vulnerability
axes: angiogenesis; the *NR4A3* fusion and its transcriptional programme; PPARγ and
nuclear-receptor signalling; epigenetic dependencies; the cell cycle; apoptosis and proteostasis;
the immune microenvironment; and receptor tyrosine kinase drivers. One candidate, an unexplained
hit from a kinome screen, carries no axis, because its mechanism in EMC is undefined. The axis is a
field of the deposited dataset rather than a statement made only in prose.

### 2.2 Evidence tiers and the triage score

Each candidate is graded by the strength and proximity of its supporting evidence. T3 denotes
prospective or substantial clinical evidence in EMC; T2, a case-level signal in EMC or in a very
close relative; T1, a preclinical or in-vitro signal, or a strong analogy in a related
fusion-driven sarcoma; T0, mechanistic rationale only. The tier states how speculative a hypothesis
is, not the expected effect size. As applied to this menu the scale is three-valued: nothing
reaches T3, because no agent has prospective or substantial clinical evidence in EMC, and the
highest grade awarded is the T2 of a single published case.

Independently of tier, each candidate is scored 0 to 3 on six criteria: EMC-specific evidence,
mechanistic fit, availability, tolerability, presence of a selection biomarker, and novelty, giving
a composite of 0 to 18. This is a transparent, equally weighted triage heuristic for ordering a
menu, not a calibrated probability of success (section 6), and it orders nothing in this
manuscript: summing evidence with novelty, tolerability and availability floats the already-known
agent to the top, and the resulting order is easily misread as a discovery ranking. Per-criterion
scores, rationale, risks and citations for every candidate are in the accompanying dataset
(section 8), which also records an ordinal `evidenceStrength` field used there for sorting.

### 2.3 Candidate generation and its coverage

Candidates were generated by expert, literature-driven curation and by a systematic target-to-drug
enumeration, and a pretrained graph foundation model was run as a third, independent check.

The enumeration queried the nine genes in the project's EMC target list — *KIT*, *KDR*, *FLT1*,
*FLT4*, *PDGFRA*, *PDGFRB*, *RET*, *MET* and *PPARG* — against the DGIdb interaction database [12]
through its GraphQL interface on 20 June 2026; the artifact records no database release version.
The implemented filter is approval status alone: every interaction whose drug carries DGIdb's
`approved` flag was retained, and the recorded interaction types were never filtered on. That
returned 106 approved drugs, of which 10 were already in the hand-built catalogue, 2 were agents
with documented EMC activity, and 94 were not previously considered. The 94 are a raw target-reach
list rather than a candidate set: they include aspirin, dexamethasone, letrozole, cytarabine,
leucovorin, estradiol valerate, apigenin, gentian violet and rabeprazole, a proton pump inhibitor
whose *KDR*, *FLT1*, *FLT4*, *PDGFRA* and *PDGFRB* edges are database artifacts. Triage was on
mechanism, by hand, and its product was the anti-angiogenic multikinase cluster, broadened to
nintedanib, axitinib, vandetanib, tivozanib, sorafenib and fruquintinib, together with alternative
*KIT* inhibitors for the mutation-defined subset.

Two properties of that yield bound what the enumeration can be said to have done. First, the nine
genes reach three of the eight axes: angiogenesis, PPARγ and nuclear-receptor signalling, and the
receptor-kinase axis through *KIT* alone. There was no target for the fusion and transcriptional
programme, epigenetic dependencies, the cell cycle, apoptosis and proteostasis, or the immune
microenvironment, and the enumeration contributed no candidate outside the kinase and
nuclear-receptor axes. Second, the overlap between the enumeration and the curated catalogue runs
partly through implausible database edges: of the 10 agreements, carfilzomib matched through a
*KDR* edge, venetoclax through a *KIT* edge, and palbociclib through *RET* and *MET* edges, none of
which is the drug's actual target or the reason a curator selected it, and entrectinib matched
through kinase edges rather than through *NTRK*, which is not in the target list at all. The
convergence claim is therefore restricted to the angiogenesis and *KIT* clusters, where the target
edges are real; there the enumeration reproduces the curated cluster and mitigates single-rater
coverage bias.

As a third line we ran the pretrained TxGNN graph foundation model [14] zero-shot on the EMC
knowledge-graph node and ranked all 7,957 drugs by indication score. Three properties of that setup
constrain the reading in section 3.3: the released checkpoint is the held-out complex-disease
split, so no disease node in it is a clean data-rich control; the underlying knowledge graph is of
approximately 2023 vintage, so two enumerated agents, fruquintinib and anlotinib, are absent from
it entirely and are excluded rather than ranked low; and EMC is present as a single grouped node.

### 2.4 The prior-art screen

"Not yet reported in EMC" was assessed by structured review of the EMC literature corpus underlying
this project, by the enumeration's gap analysis, and by a Europe PMC screen run on 9 August 2026.
That screen was not dedicated to this question: it covered five query buckets serving four separate
publication endpoints, returned 322 records and 238 full texts in total, and was screened by hand.
Its repurposing bucket returned zero records, so there was no repurposing literature to screen. The
committed record does not carry the query strings, so the search is not reproducible from it, and
no positive control was included, so the queries' sensitivity is unknown. The per-bucket record
counts were 1 for the *PRMT5* and *MTAP* query, 0 for replication stress, 3 for the surfaceome
query, 0 for repurposing and 1 for antisense.

The enumeration arm's operational definition of untried is narrower still. Its exclusion list is
built from the project's clinical registry by taking the first alphabetic token of each agent name,
which yields ten roots, of which six are drug names (pazopanib, sunitinib, imatinib, trabectedin,
apatinib, anlotinib) and four are not (anthracycline, immune, genomic, *NR4A3*). For that arm,
untried means absent from a ten-entry internal list, and exactly two of the 106 enumerated drugs
were classified as known-active in EMC. The status of any individual agent is time-sensitive and
should be re-checked with a dated, documented database search at the point of submission.

### 2.5 Two pre-specified tests of the proteasome rationale

Two in-silico tests were written and committed before their data were retrieved, and both bear on
the proteasome candidate discussed in section 3.2.

The first read CRISPR gene-effect scores from a public dependency dataset (DepMap public release
24Q4) for proteasome and proteostasis genes across the 91 sarcoma cell lines carrying a screened
value. No EMC cell line exists in that dataset, so the read is a prior transferred from other
sarcomas rather than an EMC measurement. Each gene was scored by the fraction of lines below the
−0.5 dependency threshold, and by selectivity, defined as the mean gene effect outside the sarcoma
set minus the mean inside it. *SQSTM1* and *NFE2L1* were named in advance as internal controls.

The second scored six proteostasis-related transcriptional modules on two public expression series,
contrasting EMC against the comparator sarcomas on each platform. On GSE24369, 6 EMC are contrasted
against 29 comparators, of which 17 are low-grade fibromyxoid sarcoma, 6 desmoid fibromatosis and 6
fibrosarcoma; low-grade fibromyxoid sarcoma is itself FET-rearranged, so a transcriptional
programme shared by fusion-driven sarcomas would be subtracted out of that contrast. On GSE4303, 10
EMC are contrasted against 3 dermatofibrosarcoma protuberans and 3 gastrointestinal stromal
tumours; that platform is a two-colour complementary-DNA array reporting log-ratios against a
reference pool, with about 63% of probes mapping to a gene symbol and 1,662 of 1,973 requested
genes measurable, so it is a weak instrument and a null on it is close to uninformative. The module
definitions, the threshold of |t| ≥ 2, and the four readings the rule could emit were all fixed
before retrieval. The pre-specified decision rule grades a contrast between two load modules and
two machinery modules, so four of the six scored modules enter it; the remaining two, bounce-back
and integrated stress and degradative alternatives, were pre-declared context and are scored but
not read by the rule. The stated prior expectation was a null, because transcript abundance is a
weak instrument for a dependency.

### 2.6 Use of AI tools in the research process

The candidate enumeration, the graph-model run, the two pre-specified in-silico tests and the
citation-integrity checks described above were executed by an AI coding agent operating on a
version-controlled repository under the author's direction, using Anthropic Claude. Every step is a
committed script with a committed output artifact, so each result is re-derivable offline without
any AI tool. The author specified the inclusion rules, the evidence tiers and both pre-specified
tests before the corresponding data were retrieved, and verified every reported value against the
artifact that produced it. Manuscript-preparation use is declared separately in section 9.

### 2.7 Citation integrity and the patient firewall

Every clinical and biological claim in the underlying catalogue is tied to a primary source; any
claim that could not be so grounded was tagged for verification and resolved to a primary reference
or removed, and an automated check enforces zero unresolved claims. A firewall separates these
hypotheses from any patient-facing material: a candidate may migrate into the project's cited
clinical registry only on reaching direct EMC clinical evidence, and then only after clinician
review (section 6). Under the tier scale as applied here that threshold currently admits nothing.

## 3. What the three approaches returned

### 3.1 Evidence against novelty

Curation and enumeration produced 14 existing-drug candidates. Their most durable feature is not
the list but its shape: EMC-specific evidence strength and novelty pull in opposite directions.
Table 1 arranges the candidates on those two axes.

*Table 1.* The evidence-by-novelty map of the 14 candidates.

| EMC evidence (down) / Novelty (across) | Known (tried) | Partly novel | Novel (untried) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Clinical, EMC case | Imatinib (*KIT*-mutant) | — | — |
| Clinical, class level | — | VEGFR multikinase inhibitors | — |
| In vivo, animal EMC model | — | — | Zaltoprofen (PPARγ) |
| Ex vivo, EMC models | — | Anthracycline with carfilzomib or venetoclax | Carfilzomib; venetoclax; HDAC inhibitors; brigatinib |
| Genomic or immunohistochemical | — | — | CDK4/6 inhibition (palbociclib) |
| Mechanistic only | — | — | Pioglitazone (PPARγ); NTRK inhibitors; *NR4A3*-directed modulation; BET and CDK7 to CDK9 inhibitors; mRNA vaccine with checkpoint inhibitor |

The only candidate with EMC clinical evidence, imatinib in the *KIT*-mutant subset, is also the
only one already reported in the disease, and it rests on a single published patient. Every
genuinely untried hypothesis rests on preclinical, model-screen or mechanistic data. The cells that
would hold an untried agent with EMC clinical evidence are empty, and they are empty for a
structural reason rather than an accidental one: in a tumour this rare, the only way an agent
acquires clinical evidence is by being tried, so evidence and novelty cannot both be high. That
shape is a property of how an ultra-rare disease accumulates evidence, and it should reproduce in
other tumours of similar incidence.

Imatinib's grade is the one place where this reading depends on a judgement. A single case report
is a case-level signal, which is T2 under the scale in section 2.2; the alternative would be an
explicit rule admitting a biomarker-matched single case with a durable response to T3, which would
define the top tier by what the one available candidate happens to have. We take the first reading,
which empties the clinical row of Table 1 of anything prospective and makes the structural claim
starker.

### 3.2 The proteasome lead and two failed rationales

The ex-vivo killing that places carfilzomib on the list is the measurement of Bangerter and
colleagues [13]. This work added two independent in-silico arguments for why a proteasome inhibitor
might be selectively effective in this disease. Both were pre-specified, both were run, and both
returned negative.

*The dependency data offer nothing to select on.* Across the 91 sarcoma cell lines screened in the
public CRISPR dependency data, *PSMB1*, *PSMC1*, *PSMD1* and *VCP* are dependencies in 100% of
lines, and the target of carfilzomib, *PSMB5*, in 97.8%, with selectivity against the rest of the
panel between −0.10 and +0.17. A gene required in every line of the class and equally required
outside it cannot ground a selective argument. *SQSTM1* at 0% and *NFE2L1* at 7.7% behaved as the
pre-named internal controls, so the null result is a reading rather than a failed measurement. No
EMC line is among the 91 (section 2.5).

*The proteostatic-load explanation is not supported.* The transcriptional read asked whether this
myxoid, matrix-secreting tumour carries the folding and disposal burden that would make degradative
capacity limiting, with the grading rule committed before the data were retrieved. On the
35-tumour platform (GSE24369) the six modules scored +0.64 (20S core), +0.63 (19S regulatory),
−0.20 (secretory and matrix-load proxy), +2.11 (unfolded protein response), +0.57 (bounce-back and
integrated stress) and +0.88 (degradative alternatives). The rule fired on the unfolded-protein
response, while the secretory and matrix-load proxy, which is the module carrying the actual
argument, was flat. On the 16-tumour platform (GSE4303) the same six scored +0.57, −0.97, −0.81,
−0.89, +3.56 and +1.88 in that order. The load and machinery modules did not move on either
platform, which is the null the rule reports.

One module did move on GSE4303, and it is reported here because it bears on the same lead rather
than because it supports it: bounce-back and integrated stress reached t = +3.56, the largest
module value on either platform, and the eight most extreme genes of that read on that array
include *ATF3*, *ATF4* and *DDIT3*. The pre-specified rule does not read that module, and it is not
treated as evidence for the candidate for a reason recorded before the data were seen: the
bounce-back response that limits proteasome inhibitors is driven by post-translational processing
of NFE2L1, which a transcript array cannot see, so a module of transcripts is a weak proxy for it.
The reading is a null on the argument that was made, with a scored context module that neither
supports nor refutes it and that a stronger instrument would have to settle.

The candidate stays on the list, because an ex-vivo result in cells that carry this disease
outweighs two failed in-silico rationales for it: those rationales concerned mechanism, and their
failure does not unmake a measurement. The case for selectivity does not survive. No evidence here
indicates that a proteasome inhibitor would spare normal tissue in this disease more than in any
other, and no such claim is made. Our recorded confidence in the route is correspondingly low, and
the remaining validation it needs is a clinical series that no computational work can supply.

A caution about the underlying screen applies to every row that cites it. The 40-drug screen was
run on USZ20-EMC1 alone; carfilzomib, doxorubicin and venetoclax were subsequently tested in both
models, and venetoclax showed no response as a monotherapy [13]. The superseded reading of that
screen is registered in Appendix A.

### 3.3 The graph foundation model and the sparsity stress test

The graph foundation model diverged from both other methods and promoted nothing. Its top-ranked
compounds at the EMC node are dominated by metabolic and lysosomal-storage-disease agents with no
EMC mechanism, and the mechanism-derived and enumeration-derived agents rank low: pazopanib at the
19.3rd percentile of 7,957 drugs and sunitinib at the 19.8th, the two agents with the most EMC
clinical evidence, and imatinib at the 25.2nd.

We pre-specified a stress test to ask whether this reflects the rarity of EMC, by re-running the
model at two commoner sarcoma disease nodes (Table 2). It did not rescue the leads. The same agents
ranked no better at either comparator, the same compound ranked first at all three nodes, five of
each top 15 are shared, and pazopanib and sunitinib sit in the bottom quintile at all three. The
low ranking of clinically active agents is therefore not a property unique to the EMC node.

That test does not settle the cause, and the manuscript does not claim it does. The argument
requires comparators that are denser in the knowledge graph, and what is available is comparators
that are commoner in the population; neither the run nor its artifact records a node degree, an
edge count or an indication count for any of the three disease nodes. The released checkpoint is
also the held-out complex-disease split, which prevents any of the three from serving as a clean
data-rich control. The two candidate explanations — a sparse neighbourhood around EMC, and a
general property of this checkpoint's indication ranking — are not separated by this comparison.

Neither is EMC a relative of the two comparators. The chondrosarcoma node was selected on the
availability of a graph node bearing EMC's historical name; a genuinely close comparator would be
another FET-fusion sarcoma, and none was available. They are commoner comparator disease nodes,
not relatives.

| Soft-tissue sarcoma | 17.0 (15.0 to 18.9) | masitinib, 61.2 | 18.5 | 16.8 |

Five of the 33 queried agents are excluded from those medians, and the reason is a defect in our
own code that we report rather than repair silently. The routine that located a queried agent in
the ranking matched by substring against a list sorted by descending score, so it returned the
highest-scoring compound whose name contained the query. Doxorubicin resolved to
13-deoxydoxorubicin and to zoptarelin doxorubicin, apatinib to lapatinib, and ifosfamide to
palifosfamide; an earlier version of this analysis reported the first of these as the best-ranked
agent of the whole exercise. Because a queried agent's own name contains its own query string, the
substring match returns a compound scoring at least as high as the agent itself, so every affected
percentile, and the medians computed over them, were upper bounds. The matcher is corrected. The
true ranks of those three agents are not recoverable from the deposited outputs, which retain only
the highest-ranked 100 drugs, and re-deriving them requires re-running the model; they are reported
as unknown rather than estimated. The other two exclusions, fruquintinib and anlotinib, are absent
from the graph and are excluded rather than ranked low.

No prediction from the model was promoted to a candidate, and no generation method is treated as
authoritative here.

### 4.3 The PPARγ axis and its unresolved direction

Two candidates sit on the PPARγ axis, and the axis has a sign that the menu did not previously
state. A dedicated literature adjudication was run and is recorded in full in the repository
(section 8); the short form follows.

*PPARG* over-expression in EMC is concordant across two independent cohorts, 10 EMCs against 26
other sarcomas [18] and 3 fusion-positive EMCs against 137 other sarcomas with PPARγ protein
detected by Western blot and immunohistochemistry [19]. The fusion can transactivate a response
element in the *PPARG* promoter, shown by band-shift and transient transfection [19], with the
caveat carried by that source that this is a heterologous reporter in transfected cells rather than
an endogenous EMC readout.

Which direction of intervention follows is unsettled. The two EMC expression studies that report
*PPARG* proposed opposite directions from the same observation. Subramanian and colleagues wrote
that "Small molecule inhibitors for PPARG exist and PPARG could be a potential therapeutic target
for EMC" [18]; Filion and colleagues wrote that "the direct up-regulation of PPARG by EWSR1/NR4A3
demonstrated here provides a rationale for studies of these agents", meaning PPARγ agonists [19].
Both are proposals in discussion sections and neither is backed by an EMC functional experiment.
The two are not evenly matched: Filion and colleagues note of the earlier cohort that it "used
samples in which the diagnosis of EMC was not independently confirmed by testing for EWS/NR4A3 or
other related but less common EMC-specific gene fusions", whereas their own three cases were
verified fusion-positive by RT-PCR [19]. That is an asymmetry in evidence quality rather than a
resolution.

One functional experiment has tested the direction, and it favours agonism [15]. Its in-vitro work
used H-EMC-SS, a line that Cellosaurus annotates, citing a primary source, as not harbouring an
*EWSR1* fusion. That paper is not open access and its full text has not been retrieved here, so
whether the mouse experiment used the same line is unknown and cannot be stated in either
direction. Separately, PPARγ transcriptional output has not been measured in EMC. Every EMC reading
is of receptor abundance, and abundance does not imply an activated receptor.

The PPARγ lead is not withdrawn, since it remains the only candidate here with an in-vivo signal,
but its evidence line is narrower than the label "in vivo, animal EMC" conveys, and the sign of the
axis is an open question. Nothing here asserts that agonism or antagonism is the useful direction,
and nothing here asserts efficacy for pioglitazone, zaltoprofen or any thiazolidinedione in EMC.

### 4.4 The quiet genome and the two weakest candidates

Clinical sequencing of metastatic EMC finds no recurrent actionable mutation, the *KIT*-mutant
cases being rare exceptions [7], so the strategy is to target fusion and lineage biology and to
mine unbiased patient-derived-model screens [13,16]. The two lowest-graded candidates are listed
for completeness rather than for action. NTRK inhibition rests on expression rather than on a
fusion. An mRNA-neoantigen vaccine with a checkpoint inhibitor is included by analogy to adjuvant
melanoma [20], tempered by the lipid-nanoparticle immunogenicity that complicates mRNA
therapeutics [21], and its vaccine component has no EMC data. The checkpoint component is not
untried: a prospective phase 2 EMC cohort of sunitinib plus nivolumab, reported as a conference
abstract with no full paper indexed, accrued 24 patients of whom 23 were evaluable, recorded 16
progression-free at 6 months, 2 partial responses and 12-month overall survival of 90%, and its
own abstract leaves two internal counts unreconciled [17]. That is the most relevant EMC evidence
for this candidate, it is a modest signal at best, and it constrains the immune axis rather than
supporting it.

## 5. Prioritisation and a path to testing

We prioritise by evidence tier against feasibility, where feasibility combines availability
(approved ahead of investigational with safety data), tolerability, and whether a selection
biomarker exists. Three practical tranches follow.

*Tranche 1, biomarker-defined.* Imatinib in the *KIT*-mutant subset carries the only direct EMC
clinical evidence in the menu: one patient with a *KIT* exon-11 mutation and 3 years of disease
stabilisation [9]. It is approved, well characterised and biomarker-defined, and the evidence is a
single case. What would be needed before any clinical step could be contemplated is a response rate
in *KIT*-mutant EMC beyond single cases, a variant in a class a source characterises as
imatinib-sensitive, and sarcoma-specialist judgement with formal ethics oversight and informed
consent; the first of those is a question no computational work can answer. Imatinib is not a
hypothesis awaiting promotion into patient-facing material: the project's cited registry already
lists it as an off-label, biomarker-restricted option attributed to that single case report,
because it is an agent already reported in EMC rather than a candidate that crossed the firewall.

*Tranche 2, shelf-ready class extension.* The VEGFR multikinase extension, comprising regorafenib,
cabozantinib and lenvatinib together with the enumeration-surfaced nintedanib, sorafenib, axitinib,
vandetanib, tivozanib and fruquintinib, builds on the established active class in EMC and needs no
new biomarker; activity of the class in unselected soft-tissue sarcoma is exemplified by
cabozantinib [22]. These are candidates for histology-inclusive basket trials of anti-angiogenic
agents in sarcoma, or for prospective registry-embedded cohorts, with the caveat that they probably
share the eventual resistance seen with pazopanib. Regional approval status varies across the class
and would need establishing, from regulatory sources rather than from a drug-target database,
before any such design.

*Tranche 3, preclinical replication first.* The PPARγ and lineage candidates (zaltoprofen and
pioglitazone, read with section 4.3), the cell-cycle candidate (CDK4/6), the epigenetic candidates
(HDAC, BET, CDK7 to CDK9) and the apoptotic and proteostatic candidates (venetoclax, carfilzomib)
rest on in-vivo or patient-derived-model screen signals rather than on clinical data. Their natural
next step is replication in the existing patient-derived EMC models, prioritising hits that recur
across models and that pair logically with the current anthracycline backbone, before any clinical
consideration.

### 5.1 Shared infrastructure

Across all three tranches, the rarity of the disease argues for shared infrastructure, and this is
the recommendation of this review that a reader can act on without a laboratory. Three components
are within reach of existing practice. Centralised molecular profiling would establish, for the
first time, how often an imatinib-sensitive-class *KIT* variant actually occurs, which is the
denominator Tranche 1 lacks and which no published series is large enough to supply alone.
Multi-institution basket and registry-embedded designs are the only trial forms an incidence this
low can support, and the two EMC-specific prospective cohorts that exist were both built that way
[5,17]. Contribution of real-world off-label experience to public registries such as CURE ID [23]
would begin to close the gap identified in section 7: the denominator of agents already tried in
EMC and found wanting is unknown, because negative off-label experience is almost never published,
and every "not yet reported" claim in this paper is bounded by that absence. A shared repository of
the patient-derived EMC models would let a screen hit in one model be tested in another, which
section 7 identifies as unreported for every functional lead in Table 4.

## 6. Limitations and ethics

These are hypotheses rather than recommendations, and no efficacy is claimed. Several specific
limitations bound their strength.

First, most candidates are supported by preclinical, in-vitro or model-screen data rather than by
EMC clinical evidence, none reaches T3, and the dominant rationale is lineage and fusion biology
because the EMC genome is recurrently quiet. Target-level plausibility does not guarantee clinical
activity.

Second, candidate generation was overwhelmingly single-rater curation: 12 of the 14 candidates
carry no enumeration provenance at all, and the reproducible enumeration reaches three of the eight
axes (section 2.3). The coverage bias it was introduced to mitigate is mitigated on the kinase and
nuclear-receptor axes and nowhere else. The priority score remains an expert-elicited, equally
weighted heuristic rather than a calibrated probability, and it orders nothing here.

Third, the graph foundation model diverged from the mechanism-derived and enumeration-derived leads
and promoted nothing (section 3.3). The stress test shows the divergence is not confined to the EMC
node; it does not establish the cause, and this manuscript makes no causal claim from it.

Fourth, biomarker-restricted candidates, imatinib in particular, apply only to a molecularly
defined minority and must not be generalised. The size of that minority is itself uncertain: the
two reported *KIT* variant classes are not equivalent and only one is characterised as
imatinib-sensitive (section 1.1).

Fifth, one axis carries an unresolved direction of effect. The two primary EMC expression studies
that report *PPARG* proposed opposite pharmacological directions from the same over-expression
observation [18,19]; the single functional experiment bearing on the question used a cell line
whose EMC identity the curated record does not support [15]; and PPARγ transcriptional output has
never been measured in EMC tissue. Whether the mouse experiment in that study used the same cell
line as its in-vitro half has not been established, because the paper is not open access and its
full text was not retrieved for this work, so the strength of the only in-vivo signal in the menu
cannot currently be stated. The PPARγ candidates are therefore presented as an axis worth testing
rather than as a direction that can be stated (section 4.3).

Sixth, the assessment that an agent is untried in EMC rests on a screen of titles and abstracts
whose queries are not recorded and whose sensitivity was never measured against a positive control,
and on a curated corpus (section 2.4). An absence there means that nothing is indexed on that
pairing, not that no such work exists, and a result buried in the supplementary material of a
larger paper would be invisible to it. The novelty claim attaching to each of the 14 candidates
rests on a search whose recall has not been demonstrated.

Ethically, the chief risk is false hope: a plausible-sounding mechanism can be mistaken by a
frightened patient for an available treatment. We address this structurally. A firewall keeps
graded hypotheses out of all patient-facing material unless they reach direct EMC clinical
evidence, and then only after clinician review; under the scale as applied here, nothing in the
menu reaches that threshold. Any clinical step requires sarcoma-specialist judgement and, where
applicable, formal ethics oversight and informed consent. Every clinical and biological claim in
the underlying catalogue is cited to a primary source.

## 7. Critical view

The evidence ceiling in this disease is lower than the volume of published work suggests. The only
prospective trials specific to EMC are a single-arm phase 2 of pazopanib with 22 patients evaluable
for its primary endpoint and a response-rate interval spanning 1 to 36 per cent [5], and a phase 2
cohort of sunitinib plus nivolumab with 23 evaluable patients that exists only as a conference
abstract whose internal counts do not reconcile [17]. Everything else is retrospective series
[6,24,25], single cases, or ex-vivo work in one or two patient-derived models [13,16]. Systemic
therapy in EMC is therefore chosen on evidence that would not support a recommendation in a common
tumour, and a review of this literature should say so before it adds fourteen more agents to it.

Three specific gaps bound anything built on that base, including this paper. Reproducibility across
EMC models is untested: two independent patient-derived model systems exist [13,16], neither report
describes testing a hit from the other, and the prior-art screen and curated corpus used here return
no report that does, so the field cannot presently say whether a screen hit in one EMC model
replicates in another. The denominator of agents already tried in EMC and found wanting
is unknown, because negative off-label experience in ultra-rare disease is almost never published;
that bounds every claim of the form "not yet reported" in this paper and in any successor, and it
is the strongest argument for the registry contribution described in section 5.1. And the strength
of the single in-vivo signal in the menu turns on a cell line identity that a paywall currently
prevents anyone from checking (section 4.3).

Graph-model repurposing is being adopted in rare disease faster than it is being validated in it.
The result reported in section 3.3 is one data point against that adoption: a released,
peer-reviewed checkpoint placed the two agents with the most EMC clinical evidence in its bottom
quintile, at three disease nodes, and its highest-ranked compounds have no plausible relation to
the tumour. That is not a refutation of the method, and our stress test could not separate a sparse
neighbourhood from a general property of the checkpoint. It is a reason to require, of any such
model applied to a rare tumour, that it recover the agents already known to work there before its
novel predictions are given weight.

What would change our reading of the menu. For the PPARγ axis, a measurement of PPARγ
transcriptional output in EMC tissue and a functional test of direction; a result favouring
antagonism would remove both PPARγ candidates as written. For the proteasome lead, a failure to
replicate carfilzomib sensitivity in the second patient-derived model would remove it, and its two
in-silico rationales have already failed. For the class extension, evidence that the eventual
resistance to pazopanib is class-wide rather than agent-specific would collapse Tranche 2 into a
sequencing question. For imatinib, a series establishing that the response rate in *KIT*-mutant EMC
is no higher than in unselected disease would empty the only occupied cell of Table 1.

The principal weakness is that no step of this work is a wet-laboratory experiment or a clinical
observation, and none of it can become either without a collaborator. Every lead above terminates
in a test that someone else has to run. A sarcoma clinician or translational researcher has not
reviewed these candidates, and that review is recommended before any clinical-facing use of this
menu; the specific items it would have to cover are the tier assignments in Table 3, the size of
the addressable *KIT*-mutant population in section 1.1, and every entry in the final column of
Table 4. What is offered here is the ordering of a search, not evidence that anything in it works.
