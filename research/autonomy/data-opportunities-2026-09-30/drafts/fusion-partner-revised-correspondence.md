# Fusion partner in extraskeletal myxoid chondrosarcoma: separating prognosis from treatment prediction

Whether the gene fused to NR4A3, termed its fusion partner, predicts outcome in extraskeletal myxoid chondrosarcoma (EMC) is a different question from whether it predicts benefit from a treatment. Fusion-partner associations with morphology or survival cannot establish partner-specific drug sensitivity. We conduct a targeted audit of selected published cohorts, distinguishing primary-source findings from secondary-source reconstructions. This audit is not a systematic review.

An earlier secondary-source reconstruction of Agaram et al.'s cohort used disease-death counts of 3/7 TAF15::NR4A3 and 1/16 EWSR1::NR4A3.[1] Those inputs give crude proportions of 42.9% (Wilson 95% interval, 15.8–75.0%) and 6.25% (1.1–28.3%). The reconstruction also reports unequal mean follow-up, 21.7 versus 43.3 months. A subsequent exact public-source audit did not recover a verified primary patient Table 1 from the tested routes. We therefore retain these as explicitly qualified secondary-source calculations, rather than newly verified patient records. They are not time-specific survival estimates or adjusted, causal partner effects, and the patient rows cannot be pooled without primary-source validation.

Huang et al.’s primary abstract reports 58 cases: 46 EWSR1, nine TAF15, two TCF12, and one unidentified NR4A3 partner.[2] TAF15::NR4A3 was associated with size >10 cm, present in 78% of that subgroup, and shorter univariate disease-specific survival. Only size >10 cm and metastasis at presentation remained independently prognostic in the reported multivariable analysis. This supports caution about interpreting partner as an independent prognostic factor; it does not establish that partner has no effect. Partner-stratified death counts from an earlier Table 1 transcription were not independently verified against the primary report and are not pooled here.

Suemitsu et al.’s primary abstract describes 18 patients and 20 specimens, with 14 EWSR1, two TAF15, one TCF12, and one FUS fusion.[3] Secondary genetic alterations were associated with poorer disease-free and overall survival. Fusion subtype was not significantly associated with overall survival. This null result does not prove absence of a partner effect, particularly with only two TAF15 cases. Possible patient overlap with Agaram’s earlier series from the same institution remains unresolved; the cohorts are not treated as independent evidence for pooling.

Treatment-response evidence requires a separate interpretation. The pazopanib phase 2 trial NCT02066285 enrolled and treated 26 patients; 23 entered the modified intention-to-treat population, and 22 were response-evaluable, with four objective responses assessed by Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors (RECIST).[4] Its primary abstract gives no partner breakdown. Two secondary reviews report three TAF15 cases without responses and identify all responders as EWSR1.[5,6] Assuming all three TAF15 cases were response-evaluable, the reconstruction is 0/3 TAF15 versus 4/19 non-TAF15. The observed TAF15 response proportion is 0%, with an exact two-sided 95% Clopper–Pearson interval of 0–70.8%. Zero observed events therefore yield an estimate with substantial uncertainty. The 19-patient comparator cannot be relabeled EWSR1 without the complete evaluable partner distribution. If one TAF15 case was the patient who died before the primary analysis, the sensitivity reconstruction becomes 0/2 versus 4/20.

Even fully verified response counts would describe patients who received one treatment. A partner-response association within a single-arm cohort could reflect underlying prognosis, disease burden, or other differences between groups. Treatment prediction requires comparing treatment effects between partners through a partner-by-treatment comparison, ideally in a randomized study or an appropriately controlled analysis. Larger treated cohorts or a first observed TAF15 response would sharpen response estimates but would not, by themselves, supply that comparison.

The available record therefore supports reporting fusion partner as a candidate study variable while keeping prognosis and treatment prediction separate. Future reports should identify each partner, response-evaluable status, endpoint, tumour size, stage, prior therapy, and follow-up. These data would permit clearer within-study estimates and better-controlled comparisons. They do not currently establish partner-specific treatment benefit or justify a prescribing rule. ## Original genomic source and callability appendix

The original Foundation source contains 75 EMC profile identifiers: 63 with reported EWSR1–NR4A3 and 12 with reported TAF15–NR4A3 calls. These are source profiles, not 75 independently verified clinical patients. The 76 rearrangement annotations attached to those profiles are also not an extra patient. Using original source identifiers, all 28 frozen fusion-partner/reported-co-alteration contrasts had Benjamini–Hochberg q=1. Isolated TP53/PTEN source calls did not establish partner-specific enrichment.

No MTAP/CDKN2A/CDKN2B EMC alteration was reported in these rows, but the export lacks specimen-specific negative-callability and assay-modality manifests. Absence from the reported-call table is not a verified negative genotype; MTAP was outside the listed original panel. The source contains no matched treatment or individual follow-up endpoints, and genomic evidence contributed to final diagnosis in some cases. These qualifications prevent using the repaired source identities to infer fusion-partner prognosis, drug response or absolute genotype.

The [original 75-profile calls and all 28 contrasts](../deep-analysis/results/Foundation-75-EMC-original-calls-actual.json) and [final primary-table access audit](../deep-analysis/results/Agaram-exact-final-public-patient-table-access.json) retain the source identities and limitations. The [Foundation identity correspondence](foundation-source-identity-correspondence.md) is the distinct campaign contribution; these partner/callability results form its supporting appendix. This existing clinical-letter revision is not a separate new genomic discovery.
