# Grouped validation and score transport in a public sarcoma methylation dataset

Dear Editor,

Published molecular datasets permit questions about model transport to be studied without collecting new tissue. In sarcoma methylation profiling, a useful distinction is the stability of the predicted class versus the stability of the score used to decide whether a prediction is sufficiently confident. We reanalyzed the public reference and validation data associated with the sarcoma methylation classifier of Koelsche and colleagues [1]. Our question concerns validation design and score interpretation within this existing taxonomy.

We acquired both author-processed GEO matrices for GSE140686, retaining all 408,768 CpG rows common to the released 450K and EPIC files. Primary supplemental workbooks linked 1,077 reference profiles and 428 validation profiles to the source annotations. Beta values with absent detection measurements or detection P > 0.01 were treated as missing; profiles with more than 5% missing common CpGs would have been excluded. All 1,077 reference and 428 validation profiles passed this rule. Median imputation and ANOVA selection of 500 CpGs used training observations only. The classifier was a fixed 200-tree random forest with balanced subsample weights. This is a reproducible analysis of released processed measurements, rather than a reproduction of the original raw-intensity normalization, exclusion manifest, feature selection, or calibration algorithm.

We froze fivefold sample, supplier, and physical-chip partitions before inspecting their performance. The common comparison comprised 658 reference profiles in 23 classes, each with at least ten profiles and at least five training profiles in every partition. Holding out suppliers changed both provenance and the biological composition of the training data. Consequently, we also repeated the comparison using class-specific training quotas shared across all partitions, totaling 315 training profiles per fold. The same evaluation profiles were retained throughout.

With the original training sizes, sample, supplier, and chip partitions yielded class agreement of 97.9%, 95.6%, and 97.6%, respectively. Their multiclass Brier scores, calculated by summing squared errors across classes, were 0.0973, 0.1776, and 0.1031. The supplier-minus-sample difference was 0.0803, with a descriptive supplier-cluster bootstrap interval of 0.0454–0.1162. Equalizing training budgets substantially reduced this difference: agreement became 97.4%, 96.8%, and 97.7%, and Brier scores became 0.1223, 0.1433, and 0.1325. The residual supplier-minus-sample difference was 0.0211 (interval 0.0084–0.0367), while the chip-minus-sample difference was 0.0102 (0.0011–0.0193). These intervals resample saved evaluation losses over 26 supplier or 286 chip clusters; they do not include uncertainty from refitting models.

A provenance-only model using supplier, chip, and DNA preparation achieved 61.4% class agreement under sample partitioning, compared with 9.0% for training-class prevalence. Its agreement fell to 9.4% when suppliers were held out. This pattern demonstrates that source annotations contain predictive case-mix information. It does not establish that methylation predictions are caused by technical artifacts: supplier, preparation, histology, referral, and biological selection remain entangled. The training-budget analysis likewise shows why a single grouped-versus-random comparison should not be interpreted as a pure batch-effect estimate.

For external score transport, we fitted a separate all-reference model and a temperature transformation using only reference fivefold out-of-fold votes and reference class labels. The fitted temperature was 0.3115. Across the 428 validation profiles, maximum-vote class agreed with the published classifier's maximum-score class in 358 profiles (83.6%). At a numerical threshold of 0.9, raw votes assigned 57 profiles, all agreeing with that algorithm comparator. Temperature-transformed scores assigned 311 profiles, of which 297 agreed (95.5%). Because temperature preserves class ordering, these changes concern score scale and assignment coverage rather than improved class discrimination. Agreement with the published algorithm is not independent diagnostic accuracy, and its original calibrated threshold is not interchangeable with our raw-vote threshold.

We separately examined author-reported molecular annotations. Before inspecting our external predictions, we froze explicit hallmark-fusion-to-class rules and excluded absent, unclear, historical, or taxonomically ambiguous annotations. The resulting subset contained 120 profiles in 12 classes. The original classifier and our maximum-vote predictions each agreed with the fusion-compatible label in 117 profiles (97.5%). The original classifier assigned 113 profiles at its published 0.9 threshold, all concordant. Our raw votes assigned 44, all concordant; the temperature transformation assigned 115, including one discordant prediction. Thus an increase in assignment coverage carried a measurable exception even when overall class ordering was unchanged.

This exception also exposes a limitation of aggregate score comparisons. One fusion-supported profile had zero raw votes for its compatible class. We added a frozen epsilon to input votes, normalized them, and applied the temperature in log space without clipping output probabilities. At epsilon = 10^-6, mean negative log likelihood against fusion-compatible labels changed only from 0.4389 to 0.4359, despite a Brier improvement from 0.1123 to 0.0375. At epsilon = 10^-4 it changed from 0.4053 to 0.3127; at 10^-8 it worsened from 0.4772 to 0.5591. Threshold coverage and discordance were unchanged across these sensitivity analyses. Reported log-score improvement therefore depends on how a zero-vote failure is represented and should accompany the underlying case.

The zero-vote profile was annotated with COL1A1::PDGFB and a compatible dermatofibrosarcoma protuberans label. Its transformed maximum score reached 0.9758 for a different class. The only validation profile annotated with EWSR1::NR4A3 was predicted outside the EMC-compatible class by both algorithms and remained below 0.9 under our transformation. A single annotated EMC profile cannot estimate EMC diagnostic sensitivity. Molecular workup may also have followed morphology or methylation results, so these reported fusion annotations are neither blinded adjudication nor an unbiased validation cohort. Excluding the one physical-chip prefix shared by reference and validation sets retained 118 molecularly annotated profiles and the same three class discordances.

Our analysis supports reporting grouped validation, training support, assignment coverage, and individual high-confidence exceptions together. Supplier partitioning asks a meaningful transport question, but its losses mix sample availability with source and biological composition. Reference-fitted score sharpening can increase apparent coverage without adding discrimination or establishing clinical calibration. Clinical use would require independently adjudicated outcomes and a separate validation design.

Data and code: GEO GSE140686 and the primary supplemental workbooks provide the measured inputs. The analysis scripts, frozen partitions, per-profile evaluation tables, exclusion ledger, source digests, and final results are retained in the accompanying research repository. The analyzed processed matrices contain 408,768 common CpGs; the primary publication describes a different raw-intensity processing pipeline and retained-probe manifest. Raw IDAT reprocessing was not undertaken for these processed-data classification claims. No new patient material was collected.
