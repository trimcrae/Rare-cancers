# Grouped validation and score transport in a public sarcoma methylation dataset

> Working revision, 2 October 2026. Incorporates the bounded source review and fixed-prediction reaggregation, reproduced by the successful [cloud replay](https://github.com/trimcrae/Rare-cancers/actions/runs/37009409052) at `b066a7e7bcffe4892c78f2d9462cc70b4d14945b`. This is computational verification, not an ultra submission review or publication clearance. The frozen source manuscript remains unchanged.

Dear Editor,

Published molecular datasets permit questions about model transport to be studied without collecting new tissue. In sarcoma methylation profiling, a useful distinction is the stability of the predicted class versus the stability of the score used to decide whether a prediction is sufficiently confident. Independent studies have already documented variation in assignment coverage and performance across tumor classes [1–3], and an expanded hierarchical classifier has subsequently been reported [4]. We reanalyzed the public data associated with the original classifier [1] to examine grouped validation, training support and score interpretation within its existing taxonomy. This is a robustness analysis of a separate fixed model, not a reproduction or evaluation of the current clinical classifier.

We acquired both author-processed GEO matrices for GSE140686, retaining all 408,768 CpG rows common to the released 450K and EPIC files. Primary supplemental workbooks linked 1,077 reference profiles and 428 validation profiles to source annotations. Beta values with absent detection measurements or detection P > 0.01 were treated as missing; profiles with more than 5% missing common CpGs would have been excluded. All profiles passed this rule. Median imputation and ANOVA selection of 500 CpGs used training observations only. The classifier was a fixed 200-tree random forest with balanced subsample weights. We did not reproduce the original raw-intensity normalization, retained-probe manifest, feature selection or calibration algorithm.

We froze fivefold sample, supplier and physical-chip partitions before inspecting performance. The common comparison comprised 658 reference profiles in 23 classes, each with at least ten profiles and at least five training profiles in every partition. The EMC class was absent from this common panel. Holding out suppliers changed both provenance and the biological composition of the training data. We therefore repeated the comparison using class-specific training quotas shared across partitions, totaling 315 training profiles per fold, while retaining the same evaluation profiles.

With the original training sizes, sample, supplier and chip partitions yielded class agreement of 97.9%, 95.6% and 97.6%, respectively. Their multiclass Brier scores, calculated by summing squared errors across classes, were 0.0973, 0.1776 and 0.1031. The supplier-minus-sample difference was 0.0803, with a descriptive supplier-cluster bootstrap interval of 0.0454–0.1162. Equalizing training budgets substantially reduced this difference: agreement became 97.4%, 96.8% and 97.7%, and Brier scores became 0.1223, 0.1433 and 0.1325. The residual supplier-minus-sample difference was 0.0211 (interval 0.0084–0.0367), while the chip-minus-sample difference was 0.0102 (0.0011–0.0193). These intervals resample saved evaluation losses over 26 supplier or 286 chip clusters. They are conditional on the fitted models and do not include refitting variability or account for unmeasured patient clustering.

A provenance-only model using supplier, chip and DNA preparation achieved 61.4% class agreement under sample partitioning, compared with 9.0% for training-class prevalence. Its agreement fell to 9.4% when suppliers were held out. Source annotations thus contained predictive case-mix information. This does not establish technical causation: supplier, preparation, histology, referral and biological selection remain entangled. The training-budget analysis similarly shows why a grouped-versus-random comparison should not be interpreted as a pure batch-effect estimate.

For external score transport, we fitted a separate all-reference model across 65 classes and a temperature transformation using only reference fivefold out-of-fold votes and class labels. The fitted temperature was 0.3115. Across the 428 validation profiles, maximum-vote class agreed with the published classifier's maximum-score class in 358 profiles (83.6%). At a numerical threshold of 0.9, raw votes assigned 57 profiles, all agreeing with that algorithm comparator. Temperature-transformed scores assigned 311 profiles, of which 297 agreed (95.5%). Temperature preserves within-profile class ordering; no maximum-score class assignment changed. These results concern score scale and assignment coverage. Agreement with the published algorithm is not independent diagnostic accuracy, and its calibrated threshold is not interchangeable with our raw-vote threshold.

Before inspecting our external predictions, we froze explicit hallmark-fusion-to-class rules and excluded absent, unclear, historical or taxonomically ambiguous molecular annotations. The resulting subset contained 120 profiles in 12 classes. The original classifier and our maximum-vote predictions each agreed with the fusion-compatible label in 117 profiles (97.5%). The original classifier assigned 113 profiles at its published 0.9 threshold, all concordant. Our raw votes assigned 44, all concordant; the temperature transformation assigned 115, including one discordant prediction. Increased assignment coverage therefore included a high-confidence exception despite unchanged maximum-score class assignments.

One fusion-supported profile had zero raw votes for its compatible class. We floored input votes at a fixed epsilon, normalized them and applied the temperature in log space without clipping output probabilities. At epsilon = 10^-6, profile-weighted mean negative log likelihood against fusion-compatible labels changed only from 0.4389 to 0.4359, despite a Brier improvement from 0.1123 to 0.0375. At epsilon = 10^-4, negative log likelihood changed from 0.4053 to 0.3127; at 10^-8 it worsened from 0.4772 to 0.5591. Threshold coverage and discordance were unchanged. Log-score improvement therefore depended on how a zero-vote failure was represented.

Case weighting also mattered. In a post hoc sensitivity assigning equal weight to each of the 12 fusion-compatible classes, mean class-specific negative log likelihood increased from 1.0743 to 1.8438, while the corresponding Brier score decreased from 0.2365 to 0.1933. These arithmetic summaries used the same saved predictions at epsilon = 10^-6, without refitting or exclusions. Four classes supplied 91 of the 120 profiles, and three classes had one profile each. Equal-class weighting addresses a different, highly uncertain target distribution; it does not correct the original weighting. Improved average Brier score did not imply uniformly improved scores across classes, and neither Brier nor log loss alone establishes calibration.

The zero-vote profile was annotated with COL1A1::PDGFB and a compatible dermatofibrosarcoma protuberans label. Its transformed maximum score reached 0.9758 for a different class. The only validation profile annotated with EWSR1::NR4A3 was predicted outside the EMC-compatible class by both algorithms and remained below 0.9 under our transformation. A single annotated EMC profile cannot estimate EMC diagnostic sensitivity. Molecular workup may also have followed morphology or methylation results, so these author-reported fusion annotations are neither blinded adjudication nor an unbiased validation cohort.

Reference and validation sets had no shared IDAT identifiers, but this establishes array-identifier separation rather than patient independence. Excluding the one shared physical-chip prefix retained 118 molecularly annotated profiles and the same three class discordances. Repeated specimens from one patient cannot be excluded from the available identifiers. Our results also should not be numerically compared with the expanded classifier's class-level metrics without reconciling taxonomy, probability aggregation and target definitions [4].

Our analysis supports reporting grouped validation, training support, assignment coverage, case weighting and individual high-confidence exceptions together. Supplier partitioning asks a meaningful transport question, but its losses mix sample availability with source and biological composition. Reference-fitted score sharpening can increase coverage without changing any maximum-score class assignment or establishing clinical calibration. Clinical evaluation would require patient-linked independent data and independently adjudicated outcomes.
