## Abstract
SUMOylation, a post-translational modification involving the covalent attachment of small ubiquitin-like modifier (SUMO) proteins to target substrates, plays a pivotal role at the intersection of gut health and disease, influencing various aspects of intestinal physiology and pathology. This review provides a comprehensive examination of SUMOylation’s diverse roles within the gut microenvironment. We examine its critical roles in maintaining epithelial barrier integrity, regulating immune responses, and mediating host-microbe interactions, thereby highlighting the complex molecular mechanisms that underpin gut homeostasis. Furthermore, we explore the impact of SUMOylation dysregulation in various intestinal disorders, including inflammatory bowel diseases and colorectal cancer, highlighting its implications as a potential diagnostic biomarker and therapeutic target. By integrating current research findings, this review offers valuable insights into the dynamic interplay between SUMOylation and gut health, paving the way for novel therapeutic strategies aimed at restoring intestinal equilibrium and combating associated pathologies.

## Background
Post-translational modifications (PTMs) are biochemical alterations that occur on proteins after synthesis, expanding the functional diversity of the proteome. These modifications affect protein structure, stability, localization, and activity, thereby regulating various cellular processes [ ]. Contemporary research has identified an increasing array of PTMs, spanning phosphorylation, ubiquitination, acetylation, methylation, glycosylation, neddylation, palmitoylation, ectodomain shedding, and SUMOylation [ , ]. SUMOylation, first reported in 1996, entails the covalent bonding of small ubiquitin-like modifier (SUMO) proteins to specific target proteins [ , ]. SUMO proteins, also known as sentrin, GMP1, UBl1, and Pic1, are structurally similar to ubiquitin but functionally distinct [ ]. Although absent in prokaryotes and archaea, SUMO is ubiquitously present in eukaryotic organisms [ ]. Extensive studies of SUMO protein structure have revealed that they typically consist of approximately 100 amino acids and adopt a β-grasp fold. This fold, characterized by a central β-sheet surrounded by α-helices, is conserved among SUMO family members and is essential for their structural stability and interactions with binding partners [ , ]. SUMO proteins are initially synthesized as inactive precursors requiring processing by SUMO-specific proteases (SENPs) to expose a C-terminal di-glycine motif essential for conjugation. The conjugation process involves an enzymatic cascade similar to ubiquitination, with E1 (SUMO-activating enzyme), E2 (SUMO-conjugating enzyme), and E3 (SUMO ligase) enzymes facilitating the transfer of SUMO to target proteins [ , ]. Extensive documentation of extranuclear SUMOylation demonstrates its occurrence across all tissues, cells, and organelles [ , ].
SUMOylation plays a pivotal role in regulating a wide array of cellular processes. Its importance stems from its capacity to alter protein functionality, stability, localization, and interactions, thereby influencing on numerous physiological and pathological processes [ – ]. Because of the wide range and intricacy of SUMOylation targets, SUMOylation processes are linked to multiple cellular functions, including gene expression regulation, genome integrity maintenance, cell cycle regulation, stress response, and developmental processes [ ]. Hence, SUMOylation is a dynamic and versatile PTM that regulates diverse cellular functions and contributes to maintaining cellular homeostasis and organismal health. Recent cumulative studies have indicated that SUMOylation has emerged as a critical regulator in various physiological systems, including neurobiology [ – ], immunity [ , – ], development [ , ], metabolism [ – ], and aging [ – ]. Overall, SUMOylation serves as a fundamental mechanism for fine-tuning cellular processes and integrating diverse signaling inputs to ensure the proper functioning of physiological systems. Understanding its roles and regulatory mechanisms provides insights into disease mechanisms and potential therapeutic targets.
Mounting evidence indicates that SUMOylation significantly impacts various aspects of gut health. The gut health landscape encompasses a fascinating interplay between the intestinal mucosal barrier, immune responses, and the dynamic community of microorganisms known as the gut microbiota [ ]. At the forefront of this intricate system lies the intestinal mucosal barrier, a multifaceted defense mechanism crucial for maintaining gut homeostasis and protecting against harmful pathogens [ , ]. SUMOylation plays a vital role in preserving the integrity of the intestinal epithelial barrier by regulating the function of junctional proteins such as occludins and zonula occludens (ZO). These proteins are pivotal for tight junction formation and barrier integrity [ – ]. Furthermore, SUMOylation influences immune cell signaling pathways and the expression of inflammatory mediators in the gut mucosa. It regulates the activity of transcription factors and cytokines involved in immune responses, including interferon-gamma (IFN-γ), tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α), interleukin-10 (IL-10), transforming growth factor-beta (TGF-β), and nuclear factor kappa-light-chain-enhancer of activated B cells (NF-κB), which play critical roles in the pathogenesis of inflammatory bowel diseases (IBD) and other inflammatory conditions [ – ]. Additionally, SUMOylation is involved in regulating cellular processes essential for intestinal homeostasis, including cell proliferation, differentiation, and apoptosis. For example, SUMOylation modulates the activity of key proteins regulating these processes, including transcription factors, cell cycle regulators, and apoptosis-inducing factors [ ]. Dysregulated SUMOylation can disrupt the balance between cell turnover and renewal in the intestinal epithelium, contributing to the development of intestinal disorders such as colorectal cancer (CRC) [ , ]. The human gut microecosystem is a remarkably complex entity, with the gut microbiota serving as a central player in human well-being through intricate interactions with the host [ , ]. Several studies have indicated that SUMOylation interacts with the gut microbiota to modulate immune responses, barrier integrity, and cellular functions in the intestine [ ]. Furthermore, the composition and activity of gut microbiota can influence SUMOylation patterns, impacting cellular processes crucial for intestinal homeostasis [ – ]. Nowadays, dysregulation of SUMOylation has been implicated in the pathogenesis of various intestinal diseases, including IBD [ – ], CRC [ , , , ], and infectious enteritis [ , , ]. Altered SUMOylation patterns have been observed in diseased gut tissues and animal models of gut disorders, highlighting its potential as a diagnostic marker and therapeutic target. Investigating the role of SUMOylation in disease pathogenesis provides opportunities for developing novel therapeutic interventions aimed at restoring normal SUMOylation levels and function.
In this review, we will first delve into the molecular intricacies of SUMOylation, elucidating the enzymatic mechanisms involved, the structural characteristics of SUMO proteins and their targets, and the various pathways. SUMOylation modulates protein functionality, stability, localization, and interactions. Next, we will examine the latest research on the role of SUMOylation in gut physiology and pathology, providing a basis for understanding its complex role in maintaining intestinal homeostasis and responding to pathological insults. By comprehensively examining SUMOylation’s impact on various cellular processes within the gut, this review aims to highlight its therapeutic potential in managing intestinal disorders.

## Overview of SUMOylation

## SUMO proteins and their targets
In mammals, there are five main types of SUMO proteins: SUMO1, SUMO2, SUMO3, SUMO4, and SUMO5 [ ]. These SUMO proteins share similar conjugation mechanisms and often target the same lysine residues on substrate proteins. However, they can also exhibit distinct preferences for specific target proteins and may play unique roles in cellular processes and disease pathways. SUMO1 was the first SUMO protein to be discovered and characterized. It shares approximately 50% sequence identity with SUMO2 and SUMO3 [ ]. Previous studies have suggested that SUMO1 is predominantly localized in the nucleus, although it is also present in the cytoplasm. Within the nucleus, SUMO1 is associated with nuclear bodies such as promyelocytic leukemia (PML) nuclear bodies [ , ] and Cajal bodies [ , ]. Compared to SUMO2 and SUMO3, SUMO1 is typically conjugated to a smaller set of target proteins and engages in various cellular processes such as transcriptional regulation, DNA repair, chromatin organization, and nuclear transport [ ]. SUMO2 shares a high degree of sequence similarity with SUMO3, and they are often collectively referred to as SUMO2/3. SUMO2/3 can form polymeric chains through SUMOylation, which can serve as signals for protein degradation or alter protein-protein interactions [ , ]. SUMO2/3 modification is often induced in response to various cellular stressors, including heat shock, DNA damage, and oxidative stress. Although SUMO2 and SUMO3 are primarily localized in the nucleus, they are also present in the cytoplasm and exhibit dynamic localization patterns. Under stress conditions, such as heat shock or DNA damage, these SUMO proteins can aggregate into nuclear foci [ ]. SUMO4, sharing structural similarities with SUMO2 and SUMO3, is expressed in a tissue-specific manner, predominantly in the kidney, spleen, and lymph nodes [ , ]. SUMO4 has been associated with autoimmune diseases, including type 1 diabetes, where it may influence immune responses and inflammation [ – ]. SUMO5, the most recently identified member, is exclusively present in specific organs, including the testicles and hematopoietic system. It is thought to play a role in regulating the formation and stability of nuclear bodies containing PML [ ].
The composition of SUMO proteins has been thoroughly investigated. At the C-terminus of SUMO proteins, a glycine-glycine (GG) motif facilitates their conjugation to target proteins during SUMOylation. This motif forms a thioester bond with the catalytic cysteine residue of the E2 conjugating enzyme (Ubc9) during the conjugation process [ ]. Additionally, SUMOylation consensus motifs, such as ψKxE/D (where ψ represents a hydrophobic residue, K is the target lysine, and x is any amino acid, E/D for either glutamic acid or aspartic acid), are recognized by the SUMOylation machinery for the conjugation of SUMO to target proteins [ ]. The primary target of SUMOylation is the ε-amino group of lysine residues within target proteins. These lysine residues are often located within specific SUMOylation consensus motifs, which are recognized by the SUMOylation machinery for modification [ ]. Moreover, SUMOylation often targets lysine residues within intrinsically disordered regions of proteins. These disordered regions offer flexibility and accessibility, allowing SUMOylation enzymes to effectively identify and modify these lysine clusters, which are prone to SUMOylation [ ]. Additionally, SUMOylation targets often contain interaction domains that facilitate binding to SUMO or SUMO-interacting motifs (SIMs) found in other proteins. These interaction domains enable the formation of protein complexes and modulate the functional outcomes of SUMOylation [ – ]. Based on the fact that SUMOylation targets contain signals or motifs that govern their subcellular localization, SUMOylation can influence the nuclear-cytoplasmic shuttling of proteins by altering their interaction with nuclear import/export factors or modifying their localization signals. Overall, SUMO proteins and their targets are critical for the dynamic and specific regulation of cellular processes through SUMOylation.

## SUMOylation process
SUMOylation is a dynamic and highly regulated process involving several enzymatic components. The key players in the SUMOylation pathway include SUMO proteins, the E1 activating enzyme (SAE1/SAE2 heterodimer), the E2 conjugating enzyme (Ubc9), E3 ligases, and SENPs. The following section will describe the SUMOylation process and the associated enzymatic machinery in detail (Fig.  ).
 SUMO proteins are synthesized as inactive precursors that mature through a series of steps. Initially, the C-terminus of SUMO is processed by SENPs to expose a di-glycine motif essential for conjugation [ ]. SENPs, also known as Sentrin, possess a conserved catalytic domain at C48 (with 20–60% sequence similarity) and display variability in their NH2-terminal domain. Six human SENPs (SENP1, 2, 3, 5, 6, and 7) have been identified based on their homology to the yeast SUMO proteases Ulp1 and Ulp2 [ , ]. Following maturation, the processed SUMO is activated by the E1 activating enzyme (namely SAE1/SAE2 heterodimer) in an ATP-dependent manner, forming a thioester bond between the C-terminal glycine of SUMO and the active-site cysteine of SAE2 [ ]. The activated SUMO is then transferred to the E2 conjugating enzyme, Ubc9, resulting in a thioester-linked SUMO-Ubc9 intermediate [ ]. Ubc9 is currently the only known E2 enzyme involved in SUMOylation [ ]. It interacts with target proteins containing a SUMOylation consensus motif, ψKxE/D [ ]. E3 ligases facilitate the transfer of SUMO from Ubc9 to the lysine residue within the target protein, promoting the formation of an isopeptide bond between the C-terminal glycine of SUMO and the ε-amino group of the target lysine [ ]. The presence of substrate protein-specific E3 ligase is a pivotal determinant in the SUMOylation modification of the substrate protein. Different SUMO E3 ligases recognize specific target proteins or substrates and contribute to the diversity and specificity of SUMOylation [ ]. SUMO E3 ligases encompass various families, including the SP-RING domain family, elongases family, and TRIM family. Additionally, there are proteins like PC2, NUP358, RanBP2, and others that have not yet been definitively classified [ , ]. Because SUMOylation is a reversible process, SUMO moieties can be removed from target proteins by DeSUMOylation proteases [ ]. Scholars have categorized SUMO proteases into distinct groups: the SENPs, USPL1, HINT1, and the DeSI family [ ]. Among these, SENPs have garnered significant attention [ , , ]. DeSUMOylation protease catalyzes the cleavage of the isopeptide bond between SUMO and the target protein, releasing free SUMO and restoring the unmodified state of the target protein [ ]. Moreover, SENPs also process newly synthesized SUMO precursors and modify SUMO chains by cleaving polySUMOylation chains or removing SUMO from multi-SUMOylated proteins [ ]. Thus, SUMOylation is a tightly regulated process orchestrated by a coordinated interplay between various enzymatic components.

## Biological function of SUMOylation
Growing evidence suggests that SUMOylation regulates various cellular processes, including protein function, stability, localization, and interactions, with implications for numerous physiological and pathological processes.

## Regulating protein function
The function of a protein is dictated by its unique structure, which determines its interactions with other molecules and its ability to perform specific tasks within the cell or organism [ ]. SUMOylation can modify the activity of target proteins by modulating their enzymatic activity, DNA-binding ability, or interaction with other proteins. Additionally, SUMOylation acts as a regulatory switch, turning proteins on or off in response to cellular signals, stressors, or developmental cues [ ]. By fine-tuning protein function, SUMOylation helps to maintain cellular homeostasis and allows adaptation to changing environmental conditions [ ]. For instance, Mukkawar V et al. demonstrated the SUMOylation of PSTOL1 in planta, showing its impact on phosphorylation activity. Their findings also highlighted the role of SUMOylation in modulating PSTOL1 activity during plant responses to phosphate starvation in rice and Arabidopsis [ ].

## Modulating protein stability
SUMOylation impacts the stability of target proteins by altering their susceptibility to degradation via the ubiquitin-proteasome system or other proteolytic pathways. This process finely tunes protein turnover rates, facilitating the rapid elimination of damaged or misfolded proteins and preventing the accumulation of harmful protein aggregates [ ]. Various studies have identified the glial maturation factor (GMF) as a key inflammatory mediator within the central nervous system (CNS), where it stimulates glial cells to release inflammatory mediators [ , ]. A study suggested that SUMOylation of Glia maturation factor β (GMFΒ) led to its increased protein stability and subcellular translocation. The exact sites of SUMOylation are K20, K35, K58, or K97 and deSUMOylation of GMFB downregulates multiple signaling pathways, including the Jak-STAT signaling pathway, the NF-κB signaling pathway, and the p38 pathway [ ].

## Managing the localization of proteins
SUMOylation regulates the subcellular localization of target proteins, directing them to specific cellular compartments such as the nucleus, cytoplasm, or membrane. This mechanism aids in organizing protein complexes at particular subcellular locations, thus orchestrating cellular processes such as signal transduction, gene expression, and membrane trafficking [ , ]. For instance, Claessens LA et al. demonstrated that SENP6 removes SUMO2/3 polymers from several DNA damage response proteins, such as BRCA1-BARD1, 53BP1, BLM, and ERCC1-XPF. Under normal conditions, SENP6 keeps these proteins in a state of low SUMOylation and reverses their polySUMOylation following hydroxyurea-induced stress. Furthermore, SUMO2/3 and DNA damage response proteins aggregate in nuclear bodies independently of PML, facilitated by multivalent SUMO-SIM interactions. These findings underscore SENP6’s coordinated control over SUMOylated DNA damage response proteins, influencing their timely localization at DNA damage sites and the state of nuclear condensation [ ].

## SUMOylation and ubiquitination
SUMOylation works alongside other PTMs, including phosphorylation [ ], acetylation [ , ], and ubiquitination [ ] to finely regulate protein behavior and function. This interplay between different PTMs adds another layer of complexity to cellular regulation and allows for precise control of biological processes. SUMOylation and ubiquitination differ mainly in their roles: SUMOylation typically influences protein interactions and localization, while ubiquitination primarily tags proteins for degradation [ ]. SUMOylation can counteract ubiquitination by competing for the same lysine residues on substrates. For instance, IκBα, an inhibitor of NF-κB, which undergoes proteasomal degradation upon TNF receptor stimulation via phosphorylation-mediated ubiquitination of K21 and K22 residues. However, SUMOylation of IκBα on K21 prevents its signal-induced degradation, leading to the sequestration of inactive NF-κB in the cytoplasm and thereby limiting its transcriptional activity in the nucleus [ , ]. In some cases, SUMOylation and ubiquitination can also cooperate in signal transduction. For instance, IκBα may be simultaneously modified by both SUMO and ubiquitin, forming SUMO-ubiquitin hybrid chains that facilitate its degradation [ ].

## SUMOylation and inflammasome
Inflammasomes are multiprotein complexes that activate inflammatory responses by cleaving pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-1β and IL-18. SUMOylation regulates inflammasome components such as NLRP3 and ASC, influencing their assembly, stability, and activation. This post-translational modification modulates the function of NLRP3 in response to cellular stress or infection [ , ]. NLRP3 undergoes baseline SUMOylation at Lys 689, mediated by the SUMO E3 ligase, MAPL, which possibly inhibits inflammasome activation. Upon stimulation with nigericin, deSUMOylation at this site is facilitated by SENP6 and SENP7 [ ]. Conversely, SUMOylation at Lys 204 by the E3 ligase SUMO1 enhances inflammasome assembly, whereas deSUMOylation at this site by SENP3 reduces activation [ ]. Recent studies have also demonstrated that the assembly function of ASC (apoptosis-associated speck-like protein containing a CARD) in inflammasomes is regulated by its SUMO modification, with nuclear ZBTB16 (zinc-finger and BTB domain-containing protein 16) identified as a promoter of this SUMOylation process [ ].
Therefore, SUMOylation serves as a versatile and dynamic mechanism for regulating protein function, stability, localization, and interactions in response to cellular stressors. Dysregulation of SUMOylation can disrupt normal cellular processes and contribute to the pathogenesis of various diseases, highlighting its importance in maintaining cellular homeostasis and health.

## Role of SUMOylation in gut physiology
The intestinal mucosa serves as a crucial interface for human interaction with the external environment. As a barrier, it shields the intestine from harmful agents like bacteria and toxins, preventing their entry into the bloodstream [ ]. The intestinal barrier comprises several layers: a physical barrier, a chemical barrier, an immune barrier, and a microbial barrier [ ]. SUMOylation emerges as a key regulatory mechanism that regulates various aspects of gut physiology, especially in the colon. This includes the regulation of intestinal epithelial barrier function, mucosal immune responses, and host-microbiota interactions (Fig.  ).
 

## Regulation of intestinal epithelial barrier function
The intestinal epithelial barrier, present in both large and small intestines, is a remarkably dynamic tissue that undergoes self-renewal approximately every 4 to 7 days. This perpetual renewal process is enabled by a cluster of stem cells positioned at the base of the intestinal crypts. Within this specialized environment, intestinal epithelial cells (IECs) proliferate and differentiate, generating various specialized cell types. Among these, Paneth cells and goblet cells play a significant roles by producing antimicrobial peptides that protect against potential pathogens and influence the microbiota [ , ]. Liang S et al. demonstrated that treatment with 2-D08, an inhibitor of UBC9, or mutation of the SUMOylation modification site (K233A) inhibited β-catenin SUMOylation, leading to its enhanced membrane localization and repression of Wnt/β-catenin signaling. Additionally, CBX4, an E3 ligase, has been discovered to facilitate the SUMOylation of β-catenin. Downregulation of CBX4 enhances β-catenin’s membrane localization and enhances the function of the epithelial barrier [ ]. Similarly, researchers have demonstrated that generating Ubc9-inducible knockout mice results in the cessation of proliferation and induction of apoptosis in the small intestine. This deficiency also disrupts the regulation of stem cells within intestinal crypts, constrains Paneth cell proliferation, and influences the differentiation and functionality of goblet cells [ ]. Interestingly, goblet cells present on the bronchial epithelia are also influenced by SUMOylation [ ]. Additionally, SUMOylation of transcription factors can modulate their DNA-binding activity, transcriptional activation/repression, and interaction with cofactors, thereby influencing the expression of genes encoding tight junction proteins, mucins, and antimicrobial peptides. For example, the knockdown of Ubc9 in epithelial cells leads to a significant upregulation of inflammatory gene expression, a process mediated by the reduction in Akt1 and its SUMOylated form. Quantitative PCR (qPCR) data have shown pronounced changes in the expression of inflammatory genes, with notable activation observed in genes encoding pro-inflammatory regulators such as RelA, cFos, cJun, and others [ ].
SUMOylation influences the assembly and organization of tight junction proteins, such as claudins and ZO, which are essential components of the intestinal epithelial barrier. Research has identified associations between ZO-2 and the SUMO-conjugating enzyme Ubc9, as well as the SUMO-specific proteases SENP1 and SENP3. These studies have demonstrated endogenous SUMO1 modification of ZO-2 and identified lysine 730 as a potential SUMOylation site. Confirmation of SUMOylation has also been achieved through Ubc9 fusion-directed SUMOylation techniques [ ]. Furthermore, the same researchers demonstrated that the stability of ZO-2 protein and the integrity of tight junctions are regulated by sites of polyubiquitination and SUMOylation [ ]. Claudin-2, another type of tight junction protein, exhibits entirely different outcomes. Through an in vitro SUMOylation assay, lysine 218 (K218) was identified as a conjugation site on claudin-2, and substitution of this lysine with arginine prevented SUMOylation [ ]. Additionally, the availability of oxygen is a crucial factor that regulates the homeostatic function of cells within the intestinal mucosa [ ]. Hypoxia-inducible factor (HIF) is one of the most recognized sensors in this context. HIF activity is mainly controlled by the stability and transactivation capabilities of HIFα, which are modulated by O2-regulated proteins like prolyl-hydroxylases (PHDs) and factor-inhibiting HIF (FIH) [ ]. The HIF pathway significantly influences epithelial cell function and development by inducing protective transcriptional responses [ ]. Treatment with TAK-981, a newly developed selective inhibitor of the SUMO pathway, reduces overall SUMO-1 and SUMO-2/3 modifications and increases HIF-1α protein levels. Conversely, overexpression of SUMO-1 and SUMO-2/3 leads to reduced HIF-1α protein levels in response to dimethyloxalylglycine (DMOG), a pan-hydroxylase inhibitor [ ]. Overall, SUMOylation regulates intestinal epithelial barrier function by modulating tight junctions, cell-cell adhesion, and transcriptional responses.

## Modulation of mucosal immune responses
The mucosal immune system, present in tissues such as the respiratory, gastrointestinal, and urogenital tracts, defends the body against pathogens while maintaining tolerance to harmless antigens like food and commensal microbes [ ]. SUMOylation significantly modulates mucosal immune responses in the gut, influencing both innate and adaptive immune processes. Furthermore, SUMOylation can modify transcription factors involved in mucosal immune responses, affecting their activity and the expression of genes involved in inflammation, immune cell differentiation, and tolerance induction. For instance, SUMOylation of transcription factors such as NF-κB [ ], c-Fos [ ], and STAT1 [ ] proteins can modulate their transcriptional activity, thereby regulating the expression of cytokines and chemokines crucial for mucosal immune function. Specifically, SUMOylation elements play a significant role in influencing the activity of NF-κB transactivation in humans. PIAS1, PIAS3, and PIAS4 interact with RelA to inhibit NF-κB activity. PIAS3 suppresses NF-κB through the promotion of RelA SUMOylation, while PIAS4 hinders TRIF’s ability to activate NF-κB [ – ]. SUMOylation also regulates signaling pathways essential for mucosal immune responses, including Toll-like receptor (TLR) signaling and IL-17 signaling. PIAS1 (also known as Ddxbp1), an E3 ligase, influences the TLR4/NF-κB/Cdc42 signaling pathway by acting as an inhibitor of activated STAT1 [ ]. IL-17, produced by various immune cells, including T helper 17 (Th17) cells, γδ T cells, and innate lymphoid cells (ILCs), exerts its effects on IECs, stromal cells, and other immune cells within the gut mucosa. Singh AK et al. demonstrated that IL-17 signaling is implicated in the SUMOylation of ROR-γt [ ].
Gut immunity depends significantly on the complex interactions among various immune cells, which together protect the intestinal environment from pathogens while maintaining tolerance to commensal microbes and dietary antigens. SUMOylation can impact the function of immune cells, including dendritic cells (DCs), macrophages [ ], and T cells, which play crucial roles in mucosal immune responses. The dysregulation of ROR-γt-mediated IL-17 transcription is central to several inflammatory disorders. Researchers have demonstrated that Ubc9 interacts with a conserved GKAE motif in ROR-γt, promoting its SUMOylation of ROR-γt and consequently inhibiting IL-17 expression. Mechanistically, the SUMOylation of ROR-γt enhances the recruitment of histone deacetylase 2 (HDAC2) to the IL-17 promoter, leading to the repression of IL-17 transcription [ ]. Those researchers also illustrated that HIF-1α interacts with a CpG island within the Ubc9 gene promoter, leading to hypermethylation and subsequent downregulation of Ubc9 expression. Using CRISPR-Cas9 technology to inhibit HIF-1α normalized Ubc9 levels and mitigated IL-17 expression in Th17 cells [ ]. Furthermore, inflammatory cytokine-driven upregulation of SENP2 in pathogenic Th17 cells acts as a regulatory mechanism to decrease the SUMOylation and nuclear translocation of Smad4. This reduction helps to limit the overproduction of IL-17 A and granulocyte-macrophage colony-stimulating factor (GM-CSF) [ ]. Under normal conditions, SENP7 expression is suppressed through a direct interaction with SIAH2, which promotes its ubiquitination. However, during inflammation, SENP7 expression is upregulated, altering its interactions and contributing to the expansion of localized proinflammatory γδ T cells [ ]. DCs, as antigen-presenting cells (APCs), play a crucial role in bridging innate and adaptive immune responses [ ]. Research has indicated that Ubc9-mediated SUMOylation stabilizes the recombination signal binding protein for immunoglobulin kappa J region (RBPJ), protecting it from ubiquitin-mediated degradation. This stabilization enhances the transcription of class II major histocompatibility complex transactivator (Ciita), thereby promoting major histocompatibility complex (MHC) II expression in DCs [ ]. In summary, SUMOylation plays a key role in regulating mucosal immune responses within the gut by modifying transcription factors, regulating inflammatory signaling pathways, and modulating immune cell activation.

## Influence on gut microbiota-host interactions
In 2010, Ribet D et al. demonstrated that infection with Listeria monocytogenes reduces cellular SUMO-conjugated protein level. This reduction is driven by the bacterial virulence factor listeriolysin O (LLO), which induces proteasome-independent degradation of Ubc9 and proteasome-dependent degradation of certain SUMOylated proteins [ ]. The interplay between gut microbiota-host interactions and SUMOylation is an area of emerging research interest. Although the specific mechanisms linking these two processes remain unclear, several potential interactions have been identified. Evidence shows that gut microbiota, including pathogens such as Shigella and Salmonella, can dynamically influence SUMOylation processes in the intestines, thereby impacting various aspects of cellular function and host health [ , ]. Furthermore, both gut microbiota and SUMOylation are implicated in metabolic regulation. Dysbiosis of the gut microbiota has been associated with metabolic disorders such as obesity and diabetes, whereas SUMOylation regulates metabolic processes including glucose and lipid metabolism [ ]. Over the past decade, a broad spectrum of gut microbiota-derived metabolites, including short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs), branched-chain amino acids, microbial tryptophan catabolites, trimethylamine-N-oxide, secondary bile acids, bacterial vitamins, and polyamines, have been identified and subjected to functional studies [ ]. Ezzine C et al. suggested that SCFAs and branched-chain fatty acids (BCFAs), synthesized by the gut microbiota, enhance protein SUMOylation in intestinal cells in a pH-dependent manner [ ]. Given the reversibility of SUMOylation, a dynamic equilibrium exists between SUMOylation and deSUMOylation under normal conditions [ ]. However, SCFAs and BCFAs deactivate intestinal deSUMOylases, resulting in the hyperSUMOylation of nuclear matrix-associated proteins. Functionally, BCFAs inhibit the NF-κB pathway and reduce the expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL8 and CCL20) [ ]. SUMOylation also plays a role in the regulation of epithelial barrier function in the gut, which is critical for maintaining gut homeostasis and preventing the translocation of bacteria and microbial products into the bloodstream. Gut microbiota and its metabolites have been shown to influence epithelial barrier function. They may modulate SUMOylation processes in epithelial cells to maintain barrier integrity [ ]. Notably, mounting evidence suggests for a bidirectional communication axis between the gut and the brain, known as the gut-brain axis, which influences neurological function and behavior. SUMOylation has been implicated in neuronal development, synaptic plasticity, and neurotransmitter release. Gut microbiota can produce metabolites (purines, tryptophan, and lipids) that affect neurological function [ , ], and it is conceivable that they may influence SUMOylation processes in neurons or glial cells to modulate brain function.
SUMOylation regulates mucosal immune responses, while the gut microbiota’s impact on host immune responses suggests they may also influence SUMOylation processes within the gut. For instance, it has been proposed that Ubc9 haploinsufficiency increases transcriptional activation of inflammatory response genes, including Ifn-c, Cxcl-1, and Il-6, following Shigella infection [ ]. Moreover, certain studies have indicated that SUMOylation restricts Shigella invasion and the formation of actin-rich foci, with the SUMOylation of RhoGDIa playing a regulatory role in Shigella entry [ ]. The pathogenesis of Shigella encompasses the rupture, invasion, and inflammatory destruction of the colonic epithelial lining, leading to mucosal damage [ ]. In studies utilizing mice with reduced levels of the SUMO E2 enzyme, researchers discovered that SUMOylation regulates intestinal permeability, acting to limit epithelial invasion and regulate mucosal inflammation [ ]. Overall, while the specific mechanisms associating gut microbiota-host interactions with SUMOylation are not yet fully understood, there is increasing recognition of the potential interplay between these processes and their implications for health and disease.

## Diagnostic and therapeutic implications of SUMOylation in intestinal diseases

## IBD
IBD, encompassing ulcerative colitis (UC) and Crohn’s disease (CD), is a persistent gastrointestinal condition affecting millions worldwide [ ]. The relationship between SUMOylation and IBD involves intricate interactions among SUMO proteins, SUMOylation-related enzymes, and SENPs (Fig.  ). Dysregulation of SUMOylation and alterations in the expression or activity of SUMOylation-related enzymes can impact immune response, inflammation, and tissue homeostasis in the intestinal tract. Aberrant SUMOylation has been associated with increased inflammatory signaling pathways and modulation of immune cell function, which are central to the pathogenesis of IBD. In CD, the intestinal mucosa hosts adherent-invasive Escherichia coli (AIEC), which can adhere to and invade IECs, survive within macrophages, and provoke a pro-inflammatory reaction. Infection with AIEC LF82 has been shown to reduce SUMO-conjugated protein levels in human intestinal epithelial T84 cells by upregulating miR-18, which targets PIAS3 mRNA involved in SUMOylation. This leads to decreased Ubc9 expression, impairing LF82-induced autophagy, increasing bacterial proliferation, and inflammation [ ]. It was increasingly evident that dysregulation of Ubc9 expression or activity may contribute to aberrant SUMOylation patterns, leading to dysregulated immune responses, inflammation, and tissue damage in IBD. In a murine model of IBD induced by dextran sulfate sodium (DSS), colonic inflammation was accompanied by reduced global SUMOylation of the colonic epithelium. This observation was attributed to decreased Ubc9 levels. Mass spectrometric analysis revealed distinct SUMOylation patterns in DSS-treated mice, with alterations in key cellular regulators such as Akt1. Th17 cells, mainly found in the intestinal lamina propria, protect against pathogens. In IBD, mucosal Th17 cells are abundant, especially during active stages, and secrete cytokines like IL-17 A, IL-17 F, IL-21, IL-22, and IL-26, contributing to disease progression [ ]. Singh AK et al. demonstrated that Ubc9 interacts with a conserved GKAE motif in ROR-γt, promoting its SUMOylation and suppressing IL-17 expression. Th17 cells expressing SUMOylation-defective ROR-γt induce severe colitis in Rag1–/– mice. Mechanistically, the SUMOylation of ROR-γt enhances HDAC2 binding to the IL-17 promoter, thereby suppressing IL-17 transcription [ ]. Similarly, other studies reported reduced Ubc9 expression, the E2 enzyme targeting ROR-γt for SUMOylation, in the colonic mucosa of patients with UC. This reduction may be due to HIF-1α binds to a CpG island in the Ubc9 gene promoter, causing hypermethylation and decreased Ubc9 expression [ ]. Additionally, previous studies on Ubc9 in DCs indicated that the loss of Ubc9 inhibits RBPJ SUMOylation, reducing Ciita transcription and attenuating MHC class II gene expression in DCs, resulting in impaired antigen processing and priming of effector CD4+ T cells, ultimately protecting mice from DSS-induced colitis [ ].
 SENPs, responsible for deconjugating SUMO from target proteins, play a critical role in maintaining the balance of SUMOylation and deSUMOylation. Dysregulation of SENP activity can lead to aberrant SUMOylation patterns and contribute to IBD pathology. For instance, MSC-AS1 expression was found to be upregulated in the intestinal tissue of patients with CD and TNBS-induced colitis mice. It interacts with SENP1, hindering SNIP1 deSUMOylation and its activity, potentially exacerbating epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) and contributing to intestinal fibrosis [ ]. Likewise, a SENP2-mediated pathway in pathogenic Th17 cells that limits inflammatory colitis has been reported. Higher SENP2 levels are found in pathogenic Th17 cells. Deleting SENP2 in T-cell lineages exacerbates the severity of colitis in mice. This is associated with increased GM-CSF+IL-17 A+ Th17 cells and severe intestinal dysbiosis [ ]. SENP7, another deSUMOylase, is upregulated in IECs in both human IBD and a mouse model. Its expression is downregulated by SIAH2 in a steady state but not during IBD. Upregulated SENP7 in inflamed tissue alters its interactome, leading to an expansion of localized proinflammatory γδ T cells. Knockdown of SENP7 or depletion of γδ T cells in vivo abrogates DSS-induced gut inflammation [ ]. Furthermore, PIAS1, an E3 SUMO ligase, has emerged as a central therapeutic target due to its regulation of various inflammatory and cellular regulators, such as NF-κB and STAT1 [ , ]. Recent evidence shows that PIAS1 is downregulated in case of intestinal inflammation, as observed in both human colon tissues and a DSS-induced mice colitis model [ ]. This finding highlights the significant role of SUMOylation in IBD, emphasizing the need to understand the molecular mechanisms of SUMOylation dynamics in gastrointestinal health and disease. Targeting SUMOylation pathways could potentially lead to the development of novel therapeutic strategies for IBD treatment.
Therapeutic interventions that modulate the SUMOylation of specific target proteins implicated in intestinal diseases, such as transcription factors (e.g., NF-κB, Smad4) and epithelial junction proteins (e.g., ZO-2, claudins), could offer precision-based treatment strategies [ , , , ]. These approaches may include small molecule inhibitors or activators designed to disrupt or enhance the SUMOylation of specific target proteins, thereby modulating their activity and function in disease processes. Prabhu et al. utilized nanogels to deliver PIAS1 (Protein Inhibitor of Activated STAT1)-encoding plasmid DNA orally. PIAS1 is crucial for managing NF-κB-mediated inflammatory signaling, making it a pivotal therapeutic target for IBD. Their study demonstrated that delivering the plasmid with nanogels derived from TAC6 effectively reduced gut inflammation in a murine colitis model [ ]. Furthermore, strategies may involve modulating the SUMOylation of immune signaling molecules (e.g., IL-17, IL-8) to suppress the secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines (e.g., IL-17 A, IL-8, INF-γ, TNF-α) and dampen immune responses in conditions such as IBD [ , , , ]. ROR-γt, a specialized transcription factor essential for Th17 cell development, is involved in the pathogenesis of IBD through its SUMOylation, making it an attractive drug target for treating Th17-related diseases [ ].

## CRC
CRC ranks as the third most commonly diagnosed cancer. It leads to cancer-related mortality among men and holds the second position for women, following breast cancer, among adults under 50 years of age [ , ]. There is a growing body of evidence that several enzymes involved in SUMOylation, including SUMO proteins, E1 activating enzymes, E2 conjugating enzymes, E3 ligases, and SENPs, play crucial roles in CRC. SUMOylation affects the activity, stability, and subcellular localization of key CRC-related proteins, including tumor suppressors, oncogenes, and DNA repair factors (Fig.  ).
 SUMO proteins are crucial in colorectal cancer (CRC), influencing various cellular processes that are key to tumorigenesis and disease progression. These processes include transcriptional regulation, DNA repair, protein stability, and cell cycle control. Dysregulation of SUMOylation, the process by which SUMO proteins covalently attach to target proteins, has been associated with CRC development and metastasis. Hypermethylation of tumor suppressor genes is a common contributor to tumor initiation and progression [ ]. Since DNA methylation is a reversible process, alterations in methylation patterns present potential targets for therapeutic intervention. The three primary DNA methyltransferases (DNMTs)—DNMT1, DNMT3a, and DNMT3b—add methyl groups to cytosine residues at carbon 5, leading to DNA hypermethylation [ ]. Research has revealed two main mechanisms for DNA demethylation, with the ten-eleven translocation (TET) family playing a critical role in converting 5-methylcytosine (5mC) back to unmethylated cytosine [ , ]. Cheng et al. found that SUMO1 promotes the SUMOylation of DNMT1, enhancing its stability and leading to increased DNA methylation. This process contributes to the suppression of the VWC2 gene in colorectal cancer (CRC), thereby advancing cancer progression [ ]. Additionally, TET3, which is upregulated in various tumors, including colon cancer, is linked to the malignant phenotype and radiosensitivity of CRC cells. Overexpression of TET3 is associated with increased G2/M phase arrest, enhanced radiation-induced apoptosis, DNA damage, and clonal suppression in CRC cells. SUMOylation of TET3 stabilizes the protein without altering its nuclear localization, with the binding site for TET3 and SUMO2/3 spanning amino acids 833–1795, excluding specific lysine residues [ ]. Furthermore, hypoxia was found to increase the levels of HIF-1α, Slug, and SUMO-1, while also inducing Slug SUMOylation. This PTM of Slug significantly influences the progression of EMT by decreasing E-cadherin levels and boosting the expressions of Snail and N-cadherin [ ]. In CRC, reduced expression of circ-GALNT16 is inversely associated with poor prognosis. Circ-GALNT16 inhibits CRC cell proliferation and metastasis both in vitro and in vivo. Mechanistically, circ-GALNT16 interacts with the KH3 domain of heterogeneous nuclear ribonucleoprotein K (hnRNPK), promoting its SUMOylation. This interaction also facilitates the formation of the hnRNPK-p53 complex by enhancing hnRNPK SUMOylation [ ]. Recently, treatment with the SUMO1 degrader lead compound HB007 was found to reduce StarD7 mRNA and protein levels while increasing endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress and reactive oxygen species (ROS) production in colon cancer cells and three-dimensional (3D) organoids. The anticancer activity of this compound is attributed to the reduction in StarD7 levels, which is mediated by SUMO1 degradation, deSUMOylation, and subsequent degradation of T cell-specific transcription factor 4 (TCF4) [ ].
The SUMO E1 activating enzyme is a heterodimer composed of SAE1 and SAE2 subunits (also known as AOS1 and UBA2, respectively). Elevated SAE1 expression in patients with CRC correlates with M stage and poorer overall survival. Knocking down SAE1 resulted in notable changes, including PARP cleavage, reduced levels of cyclin D1 and p-NF-κB/NF-κB proteins, diminished colony formation, and increased expression of cleaved PARP and cleaved caspase-3 following 6 Gy radiation exposure [ ]. Moreover, Ubc9 is a unique E2 conjugating enzyme responsible for SUMOylation. Dysregulation of Ubc9 can affect global SUMOylation levels and contribute to CRC development and progression. López I et al. demonstrated that loss of Apc leads to a significant increase in the development of intestinal adenomas in Ubc9+/− mice, which develop more than twice as many adenomas as their wild-type counterparts and have a markedly reduced survival rate. This effect is associated with a decrease in global SUMOylation levels within the adenomas, emphasizing the crucial role of Ubc9 in responding to oncogenic stress. Additionally, Ubc9+/− mice show a notable increase in the number of Lgr5+ crypt-based columnar (CBC) cells compared to wild-type mice. These CBC cells exhibit heightened stemness and gene expression profiles linked to cancer and inflammation [ ]. While Ubc9 primarily mediates SUMOylation in the absence of E3 ligases, E3 ligases are generally essential for determining the specificity and efficiency of SUMOylation in most cases [ ]. Several E3 ligases have been implicated in CRC, including PIAS [ , ], Ran Binding Protein 2 (RanBP2) [ , ], and Chromobox Protein Homolog 4 (CBX4) [ , ]. These E3 ligases are responsible for targeting specific substrates for SUMOylation, which in turn affects their activity, stability, and localization within CRC cells.
SENPs are a family of proteases responsible for the removal of SUMO proteins from target proteins through process known as deSUMOylation. In CRC, SENPs play crucial roles in regulating the SUMOylation status of various proteins involved in tumorigenesis and disease progression. SENPs regulate the SUMOylation of tumor suppressor proteins such as p53, p21, and Apc in CRC. By deSUMOylating these proteins, SENPs may enhance their tumor-suppressive functions, thereby inhibiting CRC growth and metastasis. For instance, strains of Escherichia coli producing colibactin have been shown to accelerate tumor growth in both xenograft and AOM/DSS models. This tumor growth is associated with cellular senescence driven by the accumulation of SUMO-conjugated p53. Furthermore, MicroRNA-20a-5p has been identified as a key regulator, targeting SENP1, which is crucial for p53 deSUMOylation [ ]. Recent RNA sequencing studies have pinpointed serpin family E member 1 (Serpine1) as a transcriptional target of circ-GALNT16. Rescue assays demonstrated that circ-GALNT16 regulates Serpine1 expression by inhibiting SENP2-mediated deSUMOylation of hnRNPK. This regulation affects the sequence-specific DNA binding ability of the hnRNPK-p53 transcriptional complex [ ]. Moreover, SENPs play critical roles in the DNA damage response by regulating the SUMOylation status of DDR proteins. Dysregulation of SENPs in CRC can impair the DNA repair process, leading to genomic instability and tumorigenesis. Studies have highlighted SENP5 as a key factor in radioresistance. High-content screening and tissue array analyses from CRC patients have identified SENP5 as a potent radioresistant gene. Elevated SENP5 expression is associated with increased resistance to radiotherapy. Knockdown of SENP5 has been shown to significantly enhance radiosensitivity in CRC cells, both in vitro and in vivo, while also playing a critical role in efficient DNA damage repair, especially in homologous recombination (HR). SUMO mass spectrometry analysis has identified H2AZ as a deSUMOylation substrate of SENP5, affecting the SUMOylation balance of H2AZ in HR repair and contributing to cancer resistance [ ]. Additionally, SENP1 has been implicated in CRC development. Circular RNA circ_0089153 functions as a competing endogenous RNA (ceRNA), targeting miR-198, which regulates SENP1 expression post-transcriptionally. This interaction highlights the complex network of regulatory mechanisms involving SENPs in CRC pathology [ ].
Various proteins serve as substrates for SUMOylation, contributing to tumorigenesis and disease progression in CRC. SUMOylation of tumor suppressors such as p53, APC, and p21 can alter their activity, stability, and subcellular localization, impacting their tumor-suppressive functions [ , ]. Furthermore, SUMOylation of oncogenic proteins, including β-catenin, c-Myc [ ], IQGAP1 [ ], and ANXA4 [ ] can enhance their transcriptional activity and promote CRC development by dysregulating Wnt signaling and other oncogenic pathways. A 2017 study revealed that IQGAP1 is predominantly SUMOylated by SUMO1 at the K1445 residue. This modification stabilizes IQGAP1 by reducing its ubiquitination, which in turn enhances CRC cell growth, migration, and tumorigenesis in vivo through the activation of ERK, MEK, and Akt phosphorylation [ ]. Collectively, SUMOylation plays a multifaceted role in CRC pathogenesis by regulating oncogenic signaling pathways, tumor suppressor proteins, EMT, DNA damage response, and genomic stability. Dysregulated SUMOylation may contribute to tumor initiation, progression, metastasis, and therapeutic resistance in CRC.
Treatment with HB007, a SUMO1 degrader, lowers StarD7 levels, and induces ER stress and ROS production in colon cancer cells and 3D organoids. This effect is mediated through the degradation of SUMO1, leading to the deSUMOylation and subsequent degradation of TCF4, which inhibits StarD7 transcription in cancer cells, organoids, and patient-derived xenografts [ ]. SAE-1 has been identified as an independent prognostic biomarker for CRC, highlighting its potential as a therapeutic target. Furthermore, PIAS3 holds promise as a biomarker for early cancer detection and therapy in human CRC. PIAS3 regulates autoregulatory loops (PIAS3/NF-κB/miR-18a and PIAS3/STAT3/miR-18a) to control cell proliferation. In vitro, validation has demonstrated that manipulation of these loops through PIAS3 overexpression or miR-18a knockdown suppresses proliferation in CRC xenografts. Enhancing PIAS3 or reducing miR-18a in AOM-DSS-induced mice decreases tumor burden, while PIAS3 knockdown in CAC mice accelerates tumor growth [ ]. Moreover, SUMOylation of IQGAP1 has been shown to drive CRC tumorigenesis by enhancing AKT-ERK signaling. Thus, targeting the SUMOylation of IQGAP1 presents a potentially novel treatment strategy for CRC [ ].

## Other intestinal conditions
Apart from IBD and CRC, SUMOylation is also implicated in the pathogenesis of several other intestinal conditions. Viral gastroenteritis encompasses a range of conditions, with severity varying based on the specific virus and individual host factors. Rotaviruses, noroviruses, sapoviruses, astroviruses, and certain adenoviruses are commonly associated with acute gastroenteritis [ ]. Pathogens such as rotavirus [ , ] and adenovirus [ – ] have been shown to manipulate the host SUMOylation machinery to promote their replication and evade the immune response. Rotavirus, a major cause of infectious enteritis in children, infects and damages the epithelial cells lining the small intestine, leading to symptoms such as diarrhea, vomiting, and dehydration. Research indicates that SUMOylation facilitates rotavirus replication and protein production. Specifically, SUMO proteins covalently attach to several rotavirus proteins, including VP1, VP2, NSP2, VP6, and NSP5, and interact noncovalently with VP1, VP2, and NSP2. Despite alterations in NSP5 that affect its SUMOylation, the protein largely retains its functionality. This includes its interactions with VP1 and NSP2, the formation of viroplasm-like structures with NSP2, and the ability to compensate for NSP5 deficiencies in infected cells [ ]. Salmonella is typically present in the normal gut microbiota of reptiles but can also cause disease in them. SUMOylation has been implicated in the host response to salmonella infection and the pathogenesis of enteritis [ , ]. Similarly, certain strains of Escherichia coli (E. coli), such as enterotoxigenic E. coli (ETEC) and enteropathogenic E. coli (EPEC), are important causes of infectious enteritis, particularly in developing countries. SUMOylation plays a role in the host response to E. coli infection, influencing the pathogenesis of gastroenteritis [ , , ].
Intestinal ischemia-reperfusion (I/R) injury is a critical condition frequently encountered in clinical settings, including during procedures such as cardiopulmonary bypass surgery, liver transplantation, abdominal aortic aneurysm surgery, and bowel resection and transplantation [ , ]. This study investigates the role of Ubc9 in modulating inflammation following I/R injury, particularly focusing on its interaction with SUMO1 levels in a mouse model of intestinal I/R injury. Results indicate that increased Ubc9 expression or SUMO1 deficiency can dampen intestinal inflammation post-I/R injury, suggesting a potential regulatory role for the SUMOylation pathway in modulating the inflammatory response linked to intestinal ischemia/reperfusion [ ]. Furthermore, obesity is linked to alterations in gut microbiota and increased intestinal permeability. Factors such as diet, genetics, circadian rhythms, medications, probiotics, and antibiotics influence gut microbiota composition, which in turn impacts obesity development [ – ]. SUMOylation has been linked to metabolic regulation, insulin signaling, and adipocyte differentiation, suggesting its involvement in the pathogenesis of obesity-related gut disorders [ – ].
TAK-981, a selective inhibitor of SUMOylation, boosts immune responses against cancers by enhancing the proportions of activated CD8+ T cells and natural killer (NK) cells, while also reactivating IFN1 signaling in pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) [ ]. TAK-981-treated mice exhibited increased IFN1 gene expression in blood cells and splenocytes. Additionally, ex vivo treatment of DCs enhanced their IFN1-dependent activation, leading to improved antigen presentation and T cell priming. TAK-981 also directly stimulated T cell activation and increased their sensitivity to antigens. In mouse models, TAK-981 inhibited tumor growth by enhancing IFN1 signaling and CD8+ T cell activity, along with increased intratumoral T and NK cell activation. Furthermore, combining TAK-981 with anti-PD1 or anti-CTLA4 antibodies significantly improved survival in tumor-bearing mice [ ].
Therefore, aside from IBD and CRC, SUMOylation plays a critical role in several other intestinal conditions, including intestinal infections, I/R injury, and metabolic disorders-related conditions. Dysregulated SUMOylation can contribute to disease pathogenesis by influencing epithelial barrier function, immune responses, gastrointestinal motility, visceral sensitivity, and host-microbiota interactions. Targeting SUMOylation presents a promising strategy for the treatment of intestinal diseases by modulating key cellular processes involved in gut homeostasis, inflammation, barrier function, and tumorigenesis (Table  ). Tailoring therapeutic interventions targeting SUMOylation to individual patients based on their SUMOylation profiles, genetic background, and disease characteristics could enhance treatment efficacy and minimize off-target effects. Additionally, precision medicine approaches may involve identifying biomarkers of SUMOylation dysregulation or patient stratification based on SUMOylation status to guide personalized therapeutic strategies for gastrointestinal diseases.
 

## Challenges and future directions
Research on SUMOylation has advanced our understanding of various cellular processes, including gene expression, DNA repair, and protein localization. However, like any area of research, certain shortcomings and challenges need to be addressed. Despite these challenges, several promising prospects offer potential advancements in the field.

## Challenges
Studying SUMOylation presents several technical challenges due to the transient and reversible nature of SUMO modifications. Detection methods need to be sensitive enough to capture these modifications in vivo and in real time. Furthermore, SUMO proteins can modify numerous target proteins, leading to potential redundancy and complexity in understanding the precise functional consequences of these modifications. The effects of SUMOylation can also vary significantly depending on the cellular context, such as cell type, developmental stage, or environmental conditions, making it difficult to generalize findings across different biological systems. Additionally, SUMOylation often works alongside other PTMs like phosphorylation, ubiquitination, and acetylation. Understanding the crosstalk and coordination among these modifications is crucial but complex. Despite its involvement in various diseases, including cancer and neurodegenerative disorders, the precise roles of SUMOylation in disease pathogenesis are still being clarified.

## Future directions
Targeting SUMOylation pathways holds significant potential for developing new therapeutic strategies for diseases where dysregulation of SUMOylation contributes to pathogenesis. Developing small molecule inhibitors or activators of SUMOylation enzymes could be promising. Advances in detection and analysis technologies, such as proximity labeling, mass spectrometry, and live-cell imaging, are essential for gaining more accurate and comprehensive insights into SUMOylation dynamics and its functional implications. Moreover, integrating systems biology approaches, such as computational modeling and network analysis, will help in understanding the global impact of SUMOylation on cellular pathways and identifying key nodes for therapeutic intervention. Given the critical role of SUMOylation in epigenetic regulation and gene expression, investigating how SUMO modifications influence chromatin structure and transcriptional activity could offer deeper insights into gene expression mechanisms. Additionally, leveraging SUMOylation for biotechnological applications, such as enhancing protein stability or modifying protein localization, presents opportunities for innovation in both biomedicine and biotechnology.

## Conclusion
In conclusion, emerging research on SUMOylation underscores its pivotal role in both gut physiology and pathology. From regulating intestinal barrier function to modulating immune responses and influencing gut microbiota composition, SUMOylation emerges as a critical molecular mechanism shaping various aspects of gut health. Therapeutic strategies targeting SUMOylation pathways offer promise for the treatment of intestinal diseases by modulating epithelial barrier function, immune responses, host-microbiota interactions, and tumorigenesis. Further exploration of SUMOylation dynamics promises deeper insights into gut homeostasis and offers potential therapeutic avenues for managing gut-related disorders.
