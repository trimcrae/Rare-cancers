Under an oxidizing environment, cysteine thiols can be oxidized to form sulfenic acid (‐SOH), and two sulfenylated cysteines can then form a disulfide bond (R‐S‐S‐R) (Figure  ).[
 
] Further oxidation of cysteine can yield sulfinic (‐SO2H) and sulfonic (‐SO3H) acids, which are typically irreversible modifications that permanently damage the protein.[
 
] During oxidative stress, abnormal disulfide bond formation is a common outcome and can induce proteins to adopt misfolded conformations.[
 
] Nitrosative stress, often related to oxidative stress, involves reactive nitrogen species (RNS) such as nitric oxide (NO). NO can react with superoxide to form peroxynitrite (ONOO−), a highly reactive oxidant that similarly induces disulfide bond formation through cysteine oxidation.[
 
] In addition to cysteine oxidation, oxidative stress can generate other covalent crosslinks in proteins; for example, ROS or RNS can induce the formation of tyrosyl radicals, leading to dityrosine bonds between protein tyrosine residues (Figure  ).[
 
] Such dityrosine crosslinks have been observed in various contexts ranging from normal aging to pathological protein aggregation in diseases.[
 
]

Aging is a major factor contributing to chronic oxidative stress.[
 
] Mitochondria, the primary source of ROS in cells, become increasingly inefficient with age and leak more ROS during respiration.[
 
] The age‐related mitochondrial dysfunction exacerbates the cellular oxidative environment and can impair processes like protein folding and other redox‐sensitive pathways including those in the endoplasmic reticulum (ER).[
 , 
] Concurrently, antioxidant defense mechanisms decline with age, further tipping the balance toward a pro‐oxidant state. The cumulative effect is an intracellular environment that favors abnormal disulfide bond formation, protein misfolding, and aggregation. Oxidative stress thus significantly contributes to the pathological disulfide crosslinking of proteins, laying the groundwork for many neurodegenerative and systemic amyloid diseases.

2.2

## ER Stress
The ER is essential for protein synthesis, proper protein folding, and quality control of secretory and membrane proteins.[
 
] Under physiological conditions, the ER provides an oxidizing environment that facilitates the formation of physiological disulfide bonds, which help nascent proteins fold into their correct conformations (thereby reducing conformational entropy and stabilizing the protein) (Figure  ).[
 
] This folding process is assisted by ER‐resident chaperones and foldases, such as protein disulfide isomerase (PDI), which catalyzes disulfide bond formation and rearrangement, and other chaperones that prevent misfolded proteins form aggregating. When the protein‐folding capacity of the ER is overwhelmed—by factors such as an excessive load of client proteins, oxidative damage, or disturbances in calcium homeostasis—ER stress occurs.[
 
] Cells respond to ER stress by activating the unfolded protein response (UPR), a signaling network that aims to restore ER homeostasis. The UPR transiently attenuates general protein synthesis, upregulates molecular chaperones, and enhances the degradation of misfolded proteins via the ER‐associated degradation (ERAD) pathway.[
 
] PDI and other foldases are often upregulated during UPR to increase the ER's folding capacity. Notably, PDI can form, isomerize, or break disulfide bonds in substrate proteins, which under proper conditions helps proteins attain correct folding.[
 
]

Under prolonged or severe ER stress, the UPR may fail to compensate, and misfolded proteins begin to accumulate in the ER lumen.[
 
] In such cases, proteins that normally fold correctly may form improper or excessive disulfide bonds. These non‐native disulfide crosslinks can lock proteins in aberrant conformations, making them aggregation‐prone.[
 
] Indeed, accumulation of disulfide‐crosslinked misfolded proteins in the ER is a feature of several amyloid diseases and is often linked to the dysfunction of key ER chaperones like PDI.[
 , , 
] PDI itself plays a paradoxical role: while it normally prevents aggregation, under stress conditions PDI can become overwhelmed or inactivated. In ALS models, for example, PDI is upregulated and has been found co‐localized with misfolded SOD1 and TDP‐43 aggregates in motor neurons.[
 , 
] PDI overexpression can reduce the aggregation and toxicity of misfolded SOD1, whereas PDI inactivation (for instance, by S‐nitrosylation during nitrosative stress) exacerbates protein misfolding and aggregation.[
 , 
] Elevated PDI levels have also been observed in other neurodegenerative diseases such as PD and AD; PDI co‐localizes with α‐synuclein aggregates in PD and with tau neurofibrillary tangles in AD.[
 , 
] These findings suggest that while PDI is part of the protective stress response, its upregulation in chronically stressed cells may be insufficient to prevent, or may even inadvertently contribute to, the formation of disulfide‐crosslinked oligomeric aggregates. For instance, if PDI is overwhelmed or modified by oxidative/nitrosative stress, it might fail to resolve improper disulfide bonds, allowing oligomers to accumulate. This underscores the idea that oxidative stress and ER stress often act in concert to drive pathological disulfide crosslinking and protein aggregation, with PDI dysfunction acting as a potential nodal point in this process.
In summary, both oxidative stress and ER stress create cellular conditions that favor the formation of abnormal disulfide bonds, leading to protein misfolding and aggregation. These processes play critical roles in the development of neurodegenerative diseases as well as systemic amyloid conditions. When the cell's quality control mechanisms (UPR, antioxidant systems, proteasome, and autophagy) become overwhelmed, misfolded proteins accumulate as toxic aggregates that ultimately cause cellular dysfunction and death. Therapeutic strategies aimed at reducing oxidative stress (e.g., antioxidants, mitochondrial protectors) or alleviating ER stress (enhancing protein‐folding capacity, boosting degradation pathways) are therefore of great interest for slowing amyloid disease progression. A deeper understanding of the molecular links between protein misfolding, oxidative stress, and disulfide crosslinking will be crucial for developing novel interventions for these devastating diseases.

## Amyloidogenic Proteins Associated with Disulfide‐Crosslinking
This section delves into specific amyloidogenic proteins to illustrate the significant impact of abnormal disulfide bond formation on protein aggregation and disease pathogenesis. We focus on tau, TDP‐43, SOD1, and TTR, four prominent cysteine‐containing proteins central to major human neurodegenerative or systemic diseases where aberrant disulfide crosslinking is a well‐established driver of their pathological aggregation. Their selection is purposeful: these proteins serve as key paradigms where the capacity to form disulfide bonds is intrinsically linked to their pathological behavior. For each, a significant body of evidence highlights how aberrant disulfide crosslinking—particularly the formation of non‐native, often intermolecular, crosslinks—is strongly implicated as a crucial factor contributing to their misfolding, aggregation, and subsequent pathogenicity. While other cysteine‐containing proteins like cystatin C, β2‐microglobulin, and immunoglobulin light chains can also form pathological disulfide bonds,[
 , 
] these four represent some of the most extensively studied and compelling cases demonstrating the diverse and significant ways disulfide bond formation can influence and drive amyloidogenesis.
3.1

## Tau
Tau is a highly soluble protein primarily expressed in neurons, where it regulates the assembly and stability of microtubules.[
 
] The microtubule‐binding domain of tau is enriched in positively charged lysine and arginine residues that bind to the negatively charged microtubule surface via electrostatic interactions.[
 
] Under physiological conditions, tau's binding to microtubules is dynamic and regulated by phosphorylation, which introduces negative charges on tau and modulates its affinity for microtubules.[
 
] Hyperphosphorylation of tau during disease results in tau detaching from microtubules, after which it accumulates in the cytosol and undergoes additional modifications such as acetylation, truncation, and oxidation.[
 
] These changes promote tau misfolding and aggregation, leading to the formation of neurofibrillary tangles (NFTs) pathological hallmarks of tauopathies including AD, progressive supranuclear palsy (PSP), and frontotemporal dementia (FTD).[
 
]

A critical aspect of tau pathology is the formation of toxic oligomeric tau species that act as seeds for further aggregation, propagate pathology throughout the brain, and ultimately induce neuronal death.[
 
] Full‐length tau contains two cysteine residues (Cys291 and Cys322) in its microtubule‐binding domain, which play pivotal roles in tau aggregation under conditions of oxidative stress (Figure  ).[
 
] ROS such as H2O2 can oxidize these cysteine thiols to form disulfide linkages. Intermolecular disulfide bonds involving Cys322 (between tau molecules) promote tau dimerization and oligomerization, serving as nucleation cores for subsequent fibril growth.[
 
] Intramolecular disulfide bonds between Cys291 and Cys322, on the contrary, constrain tau's conformation and may modulate its aggregation propensity. Some studies suggest that an intramolecular Cys291–Cys322 bond can reduce intermolecular aggregation under certain conditions,[
 
] although the dynamic nature of this bond might still permit structural rearrangements that facilitate aggregation in the long run. In addition to direct disulfide bonding, nitrosative stress can target tau: S‐nitrosylation of tau's cysteine residues has been shown to promote tau aggregation, likely by fostering subsequent disulfide bond formation or other oxidative modifications.[
 
]

 Disulfide‐crosslinked tau oligomers are notably toxic.[
 , 
] They are abnormally stable and resistant to proteolytic degradation, which means they accumulate and overload the cell's protein quality control systems.[
 
] Tau oligomers can impair proteasome and autophagy function, leading to further protein homeostasis deficits. Moreover, disulfide‐stabilized tau oligomers act as “seeds” that accelerate aggregation of normal tau in a prion‐like fashion.[
 
] This seeded aggregation allows tau pathology to spread to previously healthy neurons, as toxic oligomers can transfer to neighboring cells and induce misfolding of native tau. The cell‐to‐cell propagation of tau aggregates has been demonstrated in experimental models and is thought to underlie the progressive neurodegeneration observed in tauopathies.

3.2

## TDP‐43
TAR DNA‐binding protein 43 (TDP‐43) is a ubiquitously expressed RNA/DNA‐binding protein that plays critical roles in RNA processing, including transcription, pre‐mRNA splicing, and mRNA stabilization.[
 
] In neurons, TDP‐43 is predominantly nuclear and binds to UG‐rich sequence regions of RNA, regulating alternative splicing and gene expression programs. TDP‐43 normally exists as a monomer and a homodimer; dimerization is mediated by its N‐terminal domain and is important for its physiological function in RNA binding.[
 
]

Under disease conditions, TDP‐43 undergoes mislocalization from the nucleus to the cytoplasm and forms insoluble cytoplasmic aggregates.[
 
] Pathologically aggregated TDP‐43 is a defining feature of ALS and frontotemporal lobar degeneration (FTLD), together termed TDP‐43 proteinopathies.[
 
] In these diseases, TDP‐43 is often found to be abnormally phosphorylated, ubiquitinated, cleaved into C‐terminal fragments, and oxidized.[
 
] Such modifications contribute to both a loss of TDP‐43's normal nuclear functions and a toxic gain function due to aggregate formation.
Recent research highlights that disulfide‐crosslinked TDP‐43 oligomers form at early stages of TDP‐43 aggregation and may nucleate the pathological cascade.[
 
] TDP‐43 contains six cysteine residues in its sequence. Four of these (Cys173, Cys175, Cys198, and Cys244) are highly conserved and clustered within the two RNA recognition motifs (RRM1 and RRM2) of TDP‐43.[
 
] Under oxidative stress, specific cysteine pairs in TDP‐43 form disulfide bonds: Cys173 can pair with Cys175, and Cys198 with Cys244, triggering structural changes that expose aggregation‐prone regions of the protein (Figure  ).[
 , 
] Nitrosative stress can also affect these cysteines; S‐nitrosylation of Cys173 or Cys175, for instance, promotes an intramolecular disulfide bond between them, which in turn increases the exposure of Cys244 on the protein surface.[
 
] This conformational shift favors subsequent intermolecular disulfide bond formation between Cys244 residues on separate TDP‐43 molecules.[
 
] The net effect is the generation of TDP‐43 dimers and higher‐order oligomers that are stabilized by disulfide crosslinks. These disulfide‐crosslinked TDP‐43 oligomers are highly insoluble and resistant to degradation, allowing them to accumulate.[
 , 
] They have been shown to impair RNA metabolism (by SEQUESTering TDP‐43 away from its normal RNA targets) and to exert neurotoxic effects on cells.
Interestingly, the formation of disulfide‐linked TDP‐43 oligomers via the RRMs appears to influence the assembly of TDP‐43's N‐terminal domain. There is evidence that oxidation‐induced oligomerization can promote aberrant higher‐order assembly (tetramerization) of the TDP‐43N‐terminal domain.[
 
] It remains unclear whether this N‐terminal tetramerization is part of a protective response or a further toxic effect, on the contrary, TDP‐43's normal function may involve a dimer‐of‐dimers structure for certain activities; /in contrast, the tetramers observed under oxidative conditions might be malformed and contribute to pathology. Regardless, it is evident that cysteine oxidation and disulfide crosslinking play a direct regulatory role in TDP‐43 aggregation and function.
Genetic evidence also implicates cysteine‐dependent aggregation in TDP‐43 proteinopathies.[
 
] Mutations such as G348C and S379C in the TDP‐43 gene (TARDBP) have been identified in ALS patients. The G348C mutation (found in familial ALS) introduces an extra cysteine in the C‐terminal glycine‐rich domain of TDP‐43.[
 
] This additional cysteine greatly enhances the propensity of TDP‐43 to form disulfide‐crosslinked oligomers under oxidative conditions.[
 , 
] Similarly, S379C introduces a cysteine near the C‐terminus and is thought to promote disulfide bonding and accelerate aggregation, although it is less studied than G348C.[
 
] These cysteine mutations underscore the role of disulfide‐linked oligomers in TDP‐43 pathology and highlight the importance of targeting aberrant TDP‐43 crosslinking in diseases like ALS and FTLD.[
 , 
] Indeed, disrupting TDP‐43 oligomerization or enhancing clearance of crosslinked species represents a potential therapeutic avenue under investigation.

## SOD1
SOD1 is an antioxidant enzyme that protects cells from oxidative damage by catalyzing the conversion of superoxide radicals (O2
•−) into molecular oxygen and hydrogen peroxide.[
 
] SOD1 is highly conserved and is expressed in many tissues, with particularly high levels in neurons.[
 
] The enzyme predominantly resides in the cytosol but is also present in the mitochondrial intermembrane space, where it detoxifies superoxide generated by the respiratory chain.[
 , 
] Functional SOD1 is a homodimer; each monomer binds one copper ion and zinc ion, both of which are critical for activity and stability.[
 , 
] The copper ion participates directly in the dismutation of superoxide, while the zinc ion helps maintain the enzyme's proper fold. The dimeric form is the active state of SOD1 and is essential for efficient radical scavenging.[
 
]

Human SOD1 contains four cysteine residues: Cys6, Cys57, Cys111, and Cys146 (Figure  ). In the native enzyme, Cys57 and Cys146 form an intramolecular disulfide bond that is crucial for SOD1's structural integrity.[
 
] By contrast, Cys6 and Cys111 remain as free thiols; Cys6 is buried within the protein core, whereas Cys111 is exposed on the protein surface.[
 
] This surface accessibility makes Cys111 particularly prone to oxidative modifications. Under oxidative stress, Cys111 can be readily oxidized or form mixed disulfides (e.g., glutathionylation), which destabilizes the SOD1 dimer and promotes its dissociation into monomers.[
 , 
] Oxidation of SOD1 can also trigger aberrant disulfide chemistry: in metal‐replete (holo) SOD1, an oxidized Cys111 (or Cys6) can attack the native Cys57‐Cys146 disulfide, leading to disulfide exchange or “scrambling” that links SOD1 monomers together.[
 
] This result in intermolecular disulfide bonds between SOD1 molecules, driving the formation of SOD1 oligomers and aggregates. In metal‐depleted (apo) SOD1, the protein is even more prone to misfolding: loss of the stabilizing metals causes the enzyme to partially unfold, exposing Cys6 and Cys111. In this state, Cys6 can form a disulfide bond with Cys111, and both free thiols can also attack the Cys57–Cys146 bond.[
 
] Consequently, apo‐SOD1 readily forms abnormal species containing two disulfide linkages (Cys6‐Cys111 in addition to the intact Cys57‐Cy146).[
 
] These non‐native disulfide crosslinks convert SOD1 into an aggregation‐prone conformation, which tends to form insoluble oligomers and inclusions. The presence of disulfide‐crosslinked SOD1 aggregates has been confirmed in ALS models and patient tissues.
ALS‐associated mutations in SOD1 often exacerbate these pathogenic processes. Many SOD1 mutations destabilize the native dimer and/or interfere with metal binding, which in both cases makes the enzyme more susceptible to misfolding and disulfide bond disruption.[
 , , 
] Well‐known mutations such as A4V, D90A, and G93A greatly increase the propensity of SOD1 to misfold and aggregate.[
 , , , 
] The A4V mutation, in particular, is one of the most aggressive mutations linked to ALS, typically causing rapidly progressing disease.[
 
] This mutation is located near the N‐terminus of SOD1 and significantly destabilizes the protein; it has been shown to promote the loss of the Cys57–Cys146 disulfide bond, thereby exposing those cysteines and triggering aberrant aggregation pathways. Such SOD1 mutations generally confer a toxic gain of function, wherein misfolded and aggregated SOD1 directly harms motor neurons. The accumulation of SOD1‐rich inclusions in motor neurons is associated with mitochondrial dysfunction, disrupted axonal transport, and eventually cell death, contributing to the muscle weakness and paralysis characteristic of ALS.[
 , , , 
]

## TTR
Transthyretin (TTR) is a transport protein that carries the thyroxine (T4) and retinol (viatamin A, via the retinol‐binding protein) in the bloodstream and cerebrospinal fluid.[
 
] The name “transthyretin” reflects this dual transport role (transporter of T4 and retinol). TTR is produced largely in the liver (for plasma secretion) and in the choroid plexus (for CSF secretion), and it circulates at high concentrations to ensure adequate delivery of T4 and retinol to tissues.[
 , 
] In its native state, TTR assembles into a stable homotetramer, which is essential for its function and stability.[
 
] Each TTR tetramer can bind two molecules of thyroxine in pockets located at the dimer‐dimer interfaces; binding of T4 (or small‐molecule stabilizers) stabilizes the tetrameric structure.
Under pathological conditions, TTR tetramers can dissociate into monomers, which then misfold and aggregate into amyloid fibrils.[
 , 
] This aggregation causes amyloidogenic TTR (ATTR) amyloidosis, a group of disorders in which TTR amyloid deposits accumulate in various tissues and lead to progressive organ dysfunction. For example, in familial amyloid polyneuropathy and cardiomyopathy (forms of hereditary ATTR), mutant TTR amyloid deposits damage peripheral nerves and heart muscle, respectively. Wild‐type TTR can also aggregate over time, leading to senile systemic amyloidosis, often with cardiac involvement, in the elderly.[
 
]

TTR contains a single cysteine residue at position 10 (Cys10) in each monomer, which is not engaged in any disulfide bond in the native tetramer (Figure  ).[
 
] In the physiological state, Cys10 remains buried at the tetramer interface. However, Cys10 is highly susceptible to oxidative modifications, such as S‐sulfonation (oxidation to cysteine‐SO3H) or S‐cysteinylation (forming a mixed disulfide with a free cysteine or cysteine‐containing molecule). These modifications add bulky or charged groups to Cys10 and destabilized the TTR tetramer by weakening the interactions between monomers.[
 
] As a result, oxidatively modified TTR tetramers are more prone to dissociate into monomers. Once dissociated, the exposed Cys10 on each TTR monomer can form a disulfide bond with the Cys10 of another monomer. This leads to the formation of TTR dimers linked by Cys10‐Cys10 disulfide bonds. Disulfide‐crosslinked TTR dimers can further assemble into higher‐order oligomers or incorporate into amyloid fibrils, although the single cysteine per monomer inherently limits crosslinking to primarily dimers.[
 , 
] In wild‐type TTR amyloidosis (senile systemic amyloidosis), disulfide‐linked TTR species are indeed detected but the aggregation largely proceeds via non‐covalent interactions, and it progresses relatively slowly over decades.[
 
] This corresponds to the typically late onset and slow progression of disease in wild‐type TTR amyloidosis.[
 
]

By contrast, hereditary ATTR (hATTR) is driven by point mutations in the TTR gene that destabilize the tetramer, greatly accelerating misfolding and aggregation.[
 
] Common pathogenic mutations include V30M, L55P, and Y114C, among many others. These mutations lower the kinetic stability of the tetramer, causing it to fall apart more readily into monomers that aggregate. The Y114C mutation is particularly notable because it introduces an extra cysteine at position 114 in TTR, giving each monomer a second thiol in addition to Cys10.[
 , 
] The presence of two cysteines per monomer allows for more complex disulfide crosslinking between TTR molecules. In the case of Y114C, mutant TTR can form not just Cys10‐Cys10 dimers but also disulfide bonds involving Cys114. Studies have shown that Y114C TTR forms unique disulfide‐linked species: for instance, some Y114C molecules form Cys114‐Cys114 disulfide dimers that prevent them from assembling into standard amyloid fibrils. Instead, Y114C tends to form high‐molecular‐weight disulfide‐linked aggregates and unstable oligomers.[
 
] Interestingly, the disulfide crosslinking in Y114C can sometimes prevent the formation of the typical amyloid fibril structure: Cys114‐linked TTR dimers and oligomers represent an alternative aggregated form of TTR that is distinct from classical fibrils.[
 
] Nonetheless, these non‐standard aggregates can still deposit in tissues and cause disease, even if their morphology differs from standard amyloid. A recent study also highlighted that factors like the cellular redox environment and macromolecular crowding strongly influence TTR aggregation pathways. Under certain conditions, even wild‐type TTR can form disulfide‐crosslinked nonnative dimers (referred to as NNTTR) via Cys10, which contribute to tetramer destabilization and may facilitate amyloid formation.[
 
] Understanding these mutation‐specific and environment‐specific pathways is important for designing therapies, for example, small molecules that stabilize the tetramer or reagents that prevent harmful disulfide bond formation in TTR.
In summary, while tau, TDP‐43, SOD1, and TTR each possess unique native characteristics and aggregation pathways, a unifying pathological role for disulfide crosslinking emerges. Commonly, aberrant intermolecular disulfide bonds are formed under oxidative stress or ER stress, often involving previously unengaged or destabilized cysteine residues. These non‐native disulfide linkages consistently stabilize misfolded monomers or oligomers, acting as critical nucleation centers or building blocks for further aggregation, and imparting resistance to proteolytic degradation. Moreover, disulfide‐stabilized oligomers contribute to the prion‐like propagation of pathology. Specificities in their disulfide crosslinking exist: Tau, with two cysteines (Cys291, Cys322), primarily forms intermolecular disulfide‐linked dimers and oligomers that seed neurofibrillary tangles. TDP‐43, with multiple conserved cysteines in its RRM domains, undergoes specific intra‐ and intermolecular disulfide bonding (e.g., Cys173‐Cys175 and Cys198‐Cys244), which exposes aggregation‐prone regions and influences N‐terminal assembly. SOD1's aggregation is notably influenced by the disruption of its native intramolecular Cys57‐Cys146 bond and oxidative modification of its exposed Cys111, leading to aberrant intermolecular links. TTR, having a single Cys10 per monomer, primarily forms Cys10‐Cys10 disulfide‐linked dimers upon tetramer dissociation that then aggregate. Pathogenic mutations in TDP‐43 (e.g., G348C or S379C) and TTR (e.g., Y114C) can introduce additional cysteines or enhance existing cysteine reactivity, altering the complexity and kinetics of disulfide crosslinking and aggregation pathways. These commonalities highlight disulfide crosslinking as a fundamental pathogenic mechanism, while the specific molecular details offer protein‐specific targets for therapeutic intervention.

## Therapeutic Approaches
Significant progress has been made in managing the symptoms of amyloidogenic diseases, but truly disease‐modifying or curative therapies remain elusive. A deeper understanding of amyloidogenesis has inspired several potential therapeutic strategies, including alleviating oxidative and ER stress, reducing the production of amyloidogenic proteins, stabilizing native protein conformations to prevent misfolding, inhibiting pathological protein–protein interactions, and enhancing the clearance of aggregates (Table  ).
 Excessive ROS can induce protein misfolding and aggregation, and sustained accumulation of misfolded proteins triggers ER stress. Therefore, antioxidants and ER stress modulators have been explored as therapies to mitigate amyloid formation. Edaravone, an FDA‐approved free‐radical scavenger for ALS, has been shown to reduce oxidative damage in motor neurons and slow ALS progression,[
 , , 
] acting as an antioxidant to mitigate oxidative stress‐induced pathological processes.[
 
] Another antioxidant, tauroursodeoxycholic acid (TUDCA), can diminish oxidative stress by preventing protein aggregation and mitochondrial dysfunction.[
 , 
] TUDCA also ameliorates ER stress by enhancing the cell's protein‐folding capacity and stabilizing UPR function.[
 , 
] In a phase II ALS trial, TUDCA was found to be safe and to slow functional decline, and a recent study further reported that ALS patients treated with TUDCA had a longer median survival than untreated controls.[
 , 
] However, in a recent phase III ALS clinical trial, TUDCA did not demonstrate a significant difference from placebo in slowing disease progression among patients. Antioxidants alone do not directly eliminate existing misfolded proteins, so their benefits are limited when used as monotherapy. Combination treatments–for example, using antioxidants alongside other anti‐amyloid interventions–may yield greater therapeutic effects.
Reducing the synthesis of aggregation‐prone proteins can effectively prevent misfolding, whether the protein is wild‐type (but overproduced or poorly cleared) or mutant. Gene‐silencing therapies have shown considerable promise in this regard. In AD, an antisense oligonucleotide (ASO) called BIIB080 targets the MAPT mRNA (encoding tau) to reduce tau production.[
 
] In a phase I trial, BIIB080 reduced cerebrospinal fluid tau levels and slowed cognitive decline, supporting its advancement to phase II trials in early AD.[
 , 
] Similarly, in SOD1‐linked ALS, the ASO tofersen—recently given accelerated approval by the FDA—binds SOD1 mRNA and promotes its degradation via RNase H.[
 
] Clinical trials of tofersen have demonstrated lowered SOD1 protein in cerebrospinal fluid and a reduction in plasma neurofilament light chain (a marker of neurodegeneration).[
 , 
] Ongoing studies are determining whether tofersen can appreciably slow ALS progression in the long term. For hereditary ATTR amyloidosis, two gene‐silencing agents that suppress TTR production are already approved: inotersen (an ASO) and patisiran (a small interfering RNA). Both therapies act on the liver (the primary source of circulating TTR) to reduce TTR synthesis, thereby preventing accumulation of both mutant and wild‐type TTR amyloid.[
 , 
] These approaches illustrate that reducing the supply of amyloidogenic proteins can forestall the downstream aggregation cascade.
Preventing the initial conformational changes that lead to oligomer formation is another promising strategy. For example, stabilizers of the native tetrameric state of TTR have yielded effective treatments for TTR amyloidosis. Tafamidis and acoramidis (AG10) are small molecules that bind to the thyroxine‐binding sites of the TTR tetramer, stabilizing the complex and preventing its dissociation into misfolding‐prone monomers.[
 , 
] Clinical findings from pivotal trials, including the ATTR‐ACT trial, confirm Tafamidis is an approved drug that has demonstrated significant efficacy in slowing disease progression in ATTR cardiomyopathy by maintaining TTR tetramer stability and reducing amyloidogenic precursor availability.[
 , 
] Acoramidis has also demonstrated near‐complete stabilization of the TTR tetramer in clinical studies.[
 
] In neurodegenerative diseases, inhibiting aberrant protein–protein interactions is being explored to prevent oligomerization. One example is TRx0237 (LMTX), a derivative of methylene blue designed to inhibit tau–tau interactions.[
 
] Trials of LMTX in AD and FTD yielded mixed results; while primary endpoints were not met, post‐hoc analyses suggested that in some patients LMTX might have reduced tau aggregation and oxidative stress markers.[
 , , 
] Although these findings are preliminary, they hint at the potential of tau aggregation inhibitors to slow disease progression. Another compound, DA‐7503, derived from the calcium sensitizer levosimendan,[
 
] is a novel agent specifically targeting disulfide‐crosslinked tau oligomers. In preclinical models of tauopathy, DA‐7503 reduced the accumulation of disulfide‐linked tau aggregates and improved memory and cognition.[
 
] These promising results have led to the initiation of a phase I clinical trial of DA‐7503, highlighting its potential as a new therapeutic for tau‐related neurodegeneration.
Many amyloidogenic diseases exhibit impairment of proteolytic systems, including the ubiquitin—proteasome system and the autophagy—lysosome pathway.[
 , 
] Thus, boosting the clearance of misfolded proteins and aggregates is a logical therapeutic approach. Immunotherapy with antibodies is one way to target and neutralize extracellular aggregates, aiding their removal.[
 
] For instance, JNJ‐63 733 657 is a monoclonal antibody that recognizes a proline‐rich region of tau (with high affinity for tau phosphorylated at Thr217). This antibody has been shown to bind and promote the clearance of pathological tau “seeds” in cell and mouse models using AD patient brain extracts.[
 
] In a phase I trial, JNJ‐63 733 657 dose‐dependently lowered levels of phosphorylated tau in cerebrospinal fluid without safety concerns, and it is now in phase II testing.[
 
] Another example is PRX‐004, an antibody directed against amyloidogenic forms of TTR, which aims to neutralize circulating misfolded TTR and enhance clearance of TTR deposits.[
 
] A phase I trial showed that PRX‐004 was well tolerated and hinted at removal of TTR deposits in ATTR patients, and PRX‐004 is currently in phase II development. Beyond antibodies, small molecules that activate protein clearance are being investigated. For example, rapamycin and tamoxifen activate autophagy and have been shown to reduce TDP‐43 pathology in animal models.[
 
] However, in clinical studies of ALS, these drugs produced only modest benefits, indicating that simply ramping up autophagy may not be sufficient or may need to be combined with other approaches.[
 , 
]

A limitation of antibody therapy is that antibodies generally do not penetrate cells efficiently; since many toxic aggregates are intracellular (e.g., TDP‐43, SOD1, tau inside neurons), this poses a challenge. To address intracellular aggregates, novel targeted protein degradation (TPD) technologies have been developed. These approaches use bifunctional molecules to tag disease proteins for destruction by the cell's own degradation machinery. One such technology is proteolysis‐targeting chimeras (PROTACs), which are heterobifunctional molecules that bind a target protein on one end and an E3 ubiquitin ligase on the other, causing ubiquitination and subsequent proteasomal degradation of the target.[
 
] PROTACs can, in principle, eliminate intracellular proteins that are not readily druggable by traditional inhibitors. A novel small‐molecule PROTAC, C004019, was recently shown to selectively degrade tau, clearing pathological tau and improving cognitive function in an AD mouse model.[
 
] Another promising compound is QC‐01‐175, a PROTAC‐like degrader that specifically targets aberrant tau in neuron models derived from FTD patients, effectively lowering insoluble tau levels.[
 , 
] Similarly, autophagy‐targeting chimeras (AUTOTACs) are designed to harness the autophagy–lysosome pathway for protein degradation.[
 , 
] AUTOTACs consist of a molecule that binds the target protein and a motif that recruits it to autophagosomes for lysosomal destruction. A proof‐of‐concept AUTOTAC was shown to clear aggregated tau in a mouse model, reducing tau pathology, and an AUTOTAC‐based therapy is now in a phase I trial to evaluate its safety and early efficacy in humans. These emerging TPD approaches represent a frontier in treating protein aggregation diseases by directly disposing of the culprit proteins.
While these strategies hold promise, a fundamental challenge for many CNS‐targeted therapies, including several discussed earlier, is effectively crossing the blood–brain barrier (BBB). Small molecules like Edaravone have limited BBB permeability, reflected in the need for high and repeated dosing to maintain effective CNS levels.[
 
] Large biologics like monoclonal antibodies face an even greater challenge, with typically less than 0.1% crossing the intact BBB.[
 
] Consequently, therapies like aducanumab required high intravenous doses to achieve amyloid clearance, contributing to costs and side effects.[
 
] Moreover, once across the BBB, therapeutic molecules may be subject to intracerebral clearance or degradation via microglia and CLS lymphatic drainage.[
 
] These factors are critical in evaluating and designing treatments for amyloid diseases. Future strategies, such as BBB‐opening techniques or brain‐penetrant antibody engineering, aim to improve drug delivery to the brain.[
 , 
]

Although substantial progress has been made, curative treatments remain elusive due to the multifactorial nature of these diseases. The development and reception of anti‐amyloid drugs for AD critically illustrates this complexity. Aducanumab received FDA approval under the accelerated pathway (amid considerable controversy),[
 , 
] and Lecanemab showed a modest cognitive benefit and was approved in early 2023.[
 
] These examples highlight the immense challenges, including achieving clinical improvement even if plaques are cleared, and side effects like ARIA (amyloid‐related imaging abnormalities).[
 
] It is likely that a combination of strategies will be required—simultaneously reducing the production of amyloidogenic proteins, stabilizing their native conformations, preventing toxic oligomer interactions, and enhancing clearance of aggregates. Continued research into the molecular mechanisms of amyloidogenesis, including the role of pathological disulfide crosslinking, will guide the development of these next‐generation therapies.

5

## Summary
Disulfide bonds in proteins play a decisive role in amyloidogenesis. While the contribution of disulfide crosslinking to amyloid disease pathology is not yet fully understood, growing evidence suggests that aberrant disulfide bond formation is involved in the rate‐limiting steps of many aggregation processes. Such disulfide bonds not only stabilize oligomers but also enhance their resistance to degradation, thereby potentially accelerating the conversion of soluble proteins into toxic amyloid deposits. This mechanistic insight into disulfide‐mediated aggregation provides an important foundation for developing targeted therapeutic interventions. By interfering with pathological disulfide crosslinking or enhancing the clearance of disulfide‐stabilized species, it may be possible to slow or prevent the progression of a range of amyloid diseases.
