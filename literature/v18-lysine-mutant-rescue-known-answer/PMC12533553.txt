## Abstract

## Introduction:
Proteases are essential enzymes that regulate protein turnover and activate signaling pathways through targeted peptide bond cleavage. While traditionally regarded as degradative agents, proteases are now recognized for their diverse roles in health and disease, particularly in cancer and viral infections. Advances in high-throughput, mass spectrometry-based technologies have enabled proteome-wide identification of protease substrates, revealing numerous potential therapeutic targets. As large-scale approaches yield expansive substrate lists, it is increasingly important to understand the roles of disease-related proteases within their specific biological contexts.

## Areas covered:
Peptide-level chemical libraries have provided more practical insights, facilitating the development of protease-targeted interventions. However, early efforts to derive inhibitors from these substrates faced challenges due to enzymatic redundancy and substrate promiscuity. Consequently, emerging research has shifted toward harnessing proteolytic activity for conditional activation of therapeutics. Since proteolytic activation can amplify therapeutic effects, protease-activated strategies, such as protease-cleavable linkers in antibody-drug conjugates, have gained interest and are now being applied to other therapeutic modalities.

## Expert opinion:
We believe that identifying substrates activated by disease-associated proteases, enabled by recent technological advances, will lead to deeper biological insights. When combined with peptide-level techniques, these discoveries can drive the development of efficient therapeutic interventions with amplified effects.

Keywords: Cancer therapeutics, conditional activation, endogenous substrates, mass spectrometry, peptide substrate profiling, Prodrugs, Proteases, tumor microenvironment

## PLAIN LANGUAGE SUMMARY
Proteases are important enzymes that help break down proteins and turn on key signals in the body. While they were once mainly seen as tools for degradation, we now know that limited proteolysis can play many roles in health and disease, especially in cancer and viral infections. Thanks to new advances in high-speed technologies like mass spectrometry, scientists can now identify many of the proteins these enzymes act on, opening the door to new treatment options. But simply finding these targets isn’t enough; it’s also important to understand how each enzyme works in the body. Studies using short protein-like molecules called peptides have helped researchers design better ways to target these enzymes. Early attempts to block proteases with drugs resulted in only a limited number of successful cases, partly because these enzymes often act on many different targets as well as many different enzymes acting on the same target. Although these efforts continue, recent research has shifted to using protease activity to our advantage. For example, special chemical linkers that respond to proteases can help release drugs only where they’re needed, such as in tumors. We believe that, with today’s improved tools and a better understanding of how proteases work in disease, researchers can design more precise and powerful treatments by tapping into these enzymes’ natural functions.

## Introduction
Proteases are enzymes that hydrolyze amide bonds in peptides and proteins, comprising approximately 2% of the human genome [ ]. Ubiquitously expressed, they play a central role in maintaining homeostasis through protein turnover and must therefore be tightly regulated by endogenous mechanisms [ ]. This strict regulation highlights how dysregulated proteolysis can contribute to the development of various diseases [ ]. Proteases are typically classified by three main criteria: the key catalytic residue in their active site, the substrate residue they recognize and cleave, and the region of the substrate they target such as internal or terminal sites. For instance, pepsin, the first identified protease, is an aspartic protease that utilizes an aspartate residue for catalysis [ , ]. Trypsin, which cleaves after positively charged residues, can be further classified into lysine- and arginine-specific variants [ , ]. Carboxypeptidase A, which targets only C-terminal residues, serves as a representative exopeptidase [ ].
Proteases play crucial roles in biology, particularly by activating proteins or signaling pathways through proteolytic cleavage [ , ]. Our understanding of their functions has deepened beyond bulk protein degradation, especially as their strong links to disease have become increasingly apparent. For instance, matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs), often overexpressed in the tumor microenvironment (TME), are key players not only in remodeling the extracellular matrix (ECM) but also in promoting metastasis [ , ]. These early insights sparked a wave of research into the pathological roles and natural substrates of proteases. The advent of advanced, high-throughput techniques such as mass spectrometry (MS) has accelerated progress, enabling proteome-wide identification of protease substrates [ ].
As a result, proteases have emerged as attractive druggable targets. Their roles in remodeling the ECM [ ], promoting metastasis [ ], and initiating signaling cascades [ ] have driven the development of inhibitors, a traditional, stoichiometric approach to protease-targeted therapeutics [ ]. However, despite substantial investment, only a limited number of inhibitors, mostly targeting enzymes associated with hypertension, blood dissolution, and viral infections, have been approved to date, likely due to the redundancy, substrate promiscuity, and widespread expression of proteases [ ]. Rather than viewing these features as drawbacks, researchers have begun to exploit them. Given the pivotal roles of proteases in diverse biological contexts including the activation of other proteases and proteins, emerging research has shifted toward harnessing proteolytic activity itself [ ]. A notable breakthrough was the development of protease-cleavable linkers that can be selectively activated within the protease-rich environment [ ]. Enabled by various strategies to identify protease-responsive sequences, these linkers have become a foundational element in antibody-drug conjugate (ADC) design and have found applications beyond that field [ ].
This review outlines the progression from the identification of protease-activated targets to the development of novel therapeutic strategies inspired by these insights. Recent advances in high-throughput methodologies, spanning both chemical and biological approaches, have emerged as powerful tools for uncovering protease-activated substrates and pinpointing precise cleavage sequences. The discussion then moves to various peptide libraries designed to provide more focused and optimized substrate profiles. Finally, the review discusses therapeutic applications, highlighting how both setbacks and breakthroughs have shaped and refined ongoing research efforts.

2.

## How to identify natural protease-activated substrates to elucidate protease functions
2.1.

## Emergence of proteolytic activation
Traditionally, protease substrate profiling was approached on a case-by-case basis, relying heavily on researchers’ intuition and insight. Early studies focused on proteases readily available from animal sources with pepsin being one of the first to be studied due to its abundance from the stomach. Researchers discovered that pepsin becomes active through a conformational change from its inactive precursor, pepsinogen, at around pH 2 [ , ]. Further studies revealed that active pepsin can, in turn, activate additional pepsinogen molecules, offering a new perspective on proteolytic activation [ ]. Trypsin and chymotrypsin, extracted from the pancreas, were also early research targets and are now among the most thoroughly characterized endopeptidases in the human digestive system [ , ]. Because of their enzymatic activity under relatively mild conditions (around pH 8), and their rich availability from animal sources, they have become indispensable tools in modern science, widely used in MS [ ] and tissue culture [ ]. Trypsin is synthesized as an inactive zymogen, trypsinogen, which is secreted by the pancreas into the small intestine and activated by enteropeptidase through proteolytic cleavage [ ]. These examples represent a fundamental principle of protease biology: proteases can activate other proteases by cleaving inhibitory domains in their zymogen forms. Blood clotting is a noteworthy example, primarily regulated by an interconnected chain of zymogen activations that amplify the initial trauma. The cascade of events is initiated by both intrinsic and extrinsic factors and culminates in fibrin clot formation, driven by thrombin that is activated from its zymogen, prothrombin [ ]
Proteases are also known to play a key role in activating immune responses [ ]. Caspases (Cysteinyl ASPartate-specific proteASEs) have become a well-established protease family and remain a major focus of research due to their essential function as initiators of apoptosis [ ]. Interest in this field surged when the interleukin-1β (IL-1β)-converting enzyme (ICE) [ – ], now known as caspase-1, gained attention in immunology due to its role in activating IL-1β through proteolytic cleavage, thereby initiating immune and inflammatory responses [ ]. Simultaneously, CED-3 (Caenorhabditis elegans cell death protein 3) was identified as a key player in apoptosis in C. elegans [ ]. It was later shown that ICE and CED-3 are functionally homologous [ ], which led to the broader recognition of caspase activation as a central mechanism in apoptosis [ ] and provided compelling evidence that caspases act as essential executioners of this process [ ]. In these examples, the hydrolytic event leading to peptide bond cleavage is irreversible, and can provide a ‘one way’ signal in complex biological signaling pathways [ ]. Together, these multidisciplinary studies revealed that proteases do more than degrade proteins; they also activate biologically critical pathways [ ].
Researchers soon recognized the need for efficient, high-throughput screening methods that are more analytical and less dependent on individual insight. This shift highlighted the importance of systematic strategies capable of identifying protease-activated substrates on a proteome-wide scale. The advent of proteome-scale analysis has uncovered numerous biological processes regulated by proteolytic activity within cells and also in the cellular microenvironment, encouraging the development of a variety of innovative methodologies to tackle this challenge.

2.2.

## Degradomics
With the advent of Liquid Chromatography-Mass Spectrometry (LC-MS), the field of proteomics rapidly expanded, providing powerful tools to investigate protein degradation. This advancement gave rise to degradomics, a large-scale analysis focused on identifying the substrates targeted by specific proteases and understanding how proteolysis can regulate biological processes [ ]. Two-Dimensional Polyacrylamide Gel Electrophoresis (2D-PAGE), coupled with MS was the first large scale approach to probe the degradome of a protease [ ]. Though limited in its scope, it is nonetheless insightful. This technique uses two modes of gel separation, typically isoelectric focusing followed by SDS-PAGE, to achieve improved resolution of complex protein mixtures [ ]. Well-separated protein spots can be excised from the gel, digested with trypsin, and analyzed by MS ( ). This approach was employed to identify MMP-14 substrates in human plasma [ ]. Proteins that appeared as new spots after the addition of MMP-14 were subjected to peptide mass fingerprinting, revealing substrates such as apolipoproteins A-1 and E, which are involved in lipid metabolism and whose dysregulation may contribute to cardiovascular disease and dyslipidemia [ ]. Similarly, this method was used to identify substrates of other proteases, including Pim1 from the mitochondrial matrix, a protease closely linked to mitochondrial biogenesis and cellular stress responses [ ]. However, 2D-PAGE is limited by low reproducibility, limited sensitivity, and poor accessibility to membrane proteins [ ].
 The PROtein TOpography and Migration Analysis Platform (PROTOMAP) marked a major advancement in gel-based degradomics [ ]. In this method, protein samples are first separated by SDS-PAGE, and each lane is then excised and sliced into small segments at fixed intervals. Each gel fragment is digested with trypsin and analyzed by LC-MS/MS. The resulting peptide fragments are compiled into peptographs, where the horizontal axis represents sequence coverage and the vertical axis reflects the molecular weight of potential substrate candidates. Substrate identification is based on spectral counts corresponding to sequences of interest ( ). PROTOMAP enabled the detection of degradomic changes in apoptotic Jurkat cells and metastatic hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) cell lines [ ]. It has also been applied to clinical samples to identify early biomarkers of HCC by comparing tumor tissue with adjacent non-tumor and normal liver tissues [ ]. Among the various biomarkers discovered, the transcriptional regulator STAT1, which can be inactivated by caspase activity, was notably upregulated in HCC tissues [ ]. While gel-based degradomics have provided valuable insights into the biological context of proteolytic activity, they fall short in pinpointing the exact cleavage sites of proteases.

2.3.

## N-terminomics
Each proteolytic event generates one new N-terminus and one C-terminus, prompting researchers to explore ways to leverage the presence of these newly formed termini for biological insight. Since primary N-terminal amines are significantly more reactive than C-terminal carboxylic acids, most methodologies have focused on the N-terminus, giving rise to the field of N-terminomics [ ]. A major challenge in this field is distinguishing and isolating newly generated N-termini (neo-N-termini) by the protease of interest from internal N-termini produced by tryptic digestion during sample preparation. To overcome this, two main enrichment strategies have been developed: negative and positive enrichment. Negative enrichment involves selectively removing tryptic peptides to enrich native and neo-N-termini, while positive enrichment directly targets and isolates these termini from the sample.
Combinatorial Fractional Diagonal Chromatography (COFRADIC) is a representative negative enrichment technique in N-terminomics [ ]. In this method, native and neo-N-termini are chemically blocked via acetylation. Subsequent tryptic digestion generates new N-termini, which are selectively labeled using 2,4,6-trinitrobenzenesulfonic acid (TNBS). These newly formed tryptic N-terminal free amines react with TNBS, increasing their hydrophobicity and resulting in longer retention times during HPLC analysis. As a result, acetylated native and neo-N-terminal peptides can be isolated and analyzed by LC-MS/MS ( ). Using this approach, 93 potential caspase cleavage sites were identified across 71 proteins involved in Fas-induced apoptosis in the Jurkat cell line [ ]. Caspase-2, −3, and −7 were also profiled for substrate specificity and natural targets, revealing a high degree of overlap among the three enzymes [ ]. A total of 68 cleavage sites were identified in 61 proteins, underscoring the promiscuous nature of caspase activity. One such site was found in eukaryotic translation initiation factor 4B (eIF4B), which appeared to be preferentially cleaved by caspase-2, suggesting a potential role in apoptotic regulation [ ]. COFRADIC was further applied to compile a substrate catalog for HIV-1 protease in host cells, identifying cleavage of DDX5, a host RNA helicase, as a possible contributor to enhanced viral replication and infectivity [ ]. Despite its demonstrated utility, COFRADIC remains costly, labor-intensive, and technically demanding, motivating the development of more accessible and scalable alternatives [ ].
Terminal Amine Isotopic Labeling of Substrates (TAILS) is an alternative negative enrichment method, offering advantages such as unbiased N-terminal specificity and requiring smaller sample quantities [ ]. TAILS employs isotopic labeling reagents like tandem mass tags (TMT). Internal tryptic peptides are selectively captured by a high molecular weight dendritic polyglycerol aldehyde polymer (HPG-ALD), which induces their precipitation. The unbound native and neo-N-terminal peptides remain in solution and can be easily purified by ultracentrifugation ( ). This methodology has enabled a range of discoveries. Cathepsin D (CTSD) was profiled using TAILS, revealing that CTSD cleaves SPARC, an extracellular Ca2+-binding protein, which leads to the detachment of triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) cells and promotes metastasis [ ]. In the Rip1-Tag2 PanNET mouse model, TAILS identified prothymosin alpha (PTMA), a nuclear oncoprotein, as a substrate of cathepsin L (CTSL) [ ]. Cleavage at Gly77 disrupts PTMA’s bipartite nuclear localization signal, enhancing its anti-apoptotic activity and promoting tumor proliferation. MALT1, a paracaspase, was also shown to cleave heme-oxidized IRP2 ubiquitin ligase (HOIL1), an event that may alter the balance of proteolytic degradation pathways [ ].
Subtiligase enables a positive enrichment approach, which employs enzymatic biotinylation using an engineered form of subtiligase, an enzyme derived from the nonspecific protease subtilisin [ ] that has been reengineered to selectively modify N-terminal amines [ , ]. By leveraging the high affinity of biotin for streptavidin and incorporating Tobacco Etch Virus (TEV) protease cleavage, the protocol was significantly streamlined ( ). This technique has been applied to identify caspase substrates. For example, this subtiligase-based enrichment was used to reprogram caspase-7 specificity and examine its proteomic impact [ ]. The approach was further extended to viral proteases, demonstrating that the Zika virus NS2B-NS3 protease cleaves host proteins such as eIF4G1, thereby influencing viral infectivity and pathogenesis [ ].

2.4.

## Activity-based probes
While N-terminomics focuses on the identification of native and neo-N-termini, an alternative approach emphasizes the direct monitoring of protease activity. Activity-based probes (ABPs) have been developed to covalently bind to active proteases, enabling their isolation and subsequent identification [ ]. These probes facilitate the direct detection of proteolytic activity and are valuable tools for discovering novel proteases and elucidating their functional roles. When conjugated to peptidomimetic substrates or target-specific binders tagged with dyes or epitopes, ABPs become highly versatile, suitable for applications such as immunoblotting [ ], in-gel fluorescence [ ], MS [ ], and even in vivo imaging [ ]. A diverse array of chemical moieties has been designed to selectively target specific protease families, including cysteine, serine, and aspartate proteases. For example, cysteine proteases can be studied using various electrophilic ABPs such as acyloxymethylketone, alkyne, α,β-unsaturated ketone, azaepoxide, diazomethylketone, epoxysuccinate, halomethylketone, phenoxymethylketone, and vinylsulfone, and each offers distinct advantages depending on the experimental context [ ]. This approach has successfully captured intermediates of caspase-7 activation, as certain probes can recognize both active and zymogen forms, offering valuable insights into the protease activation landscape in large-scale studies [ ].

2.5.

## Remaining challenges after large-scale analysis and the emergence of peptide-level substrate optimization
Thanks to substantial effort from multiple laboratories, numerous protease-activated targets have been profiled. Much of that information has been curated in MEROPS: the database of proteolytic enzymes, their substrates and inhibitors [ ]. However, connecting these substrates to their biological contexts is just as important as identifying them. In terms of therapeutic potential, the mere identification of proteolytic substrates does not guarantee functional relevance to disease. Large-scale profiling should be followed by focused investigation into the biological roles of individual substrates. Integration with other underexplored large-scale datasets may further support this effort. For example, C-terminomics holds valuable biological insight, as free C-termini are more abundant than N-termini and are closely associated with hormone activation and signaling cascades [ , ]. C-terminomics originally required prior amine-blocking steps before enriching peptide fragments via free C-termini, which is why it was overshadowed by N-terminomics [ ]. However, Profiling Protein C-Termini by Enzymatic Labeling (ProC-TEL) has been developed as a positive enrichment method that avoids additional amine-blocking steps. This method uses carboxypeptidase Y (CPY) for C-terminal biotinylation. Once CPY is engineered for improved efficiency, C-terminomics may emerge as a valuable tool for investigating previously unexplored areas [ ]. A deeper understanding can also be gained by considering structural contexts of peptide substate sequences [ , ]. Since gaining a deep understanding of proteolytic events remains challenging and requires continued effort, researchers have been working to translate this knowledge into more practical therapeutic applications.
As structural analyses of proteases increasingly reveal that their activity is largely dictated by the short peptide sequences surrounding the cleavage site [ ], the focus has shifted toward profiling protease specificity and optimizing substrates at the peptide level. Peptides offer a practical and accessible means of probing protease function. Central to this approach is the discovery of efficient peptide substrates. To support this effort, peptide-based libraries have been developed and applied in various high-throughput screening methods to identify optimal cleavage sequences.

3.

## Screening and optimizing peptide substrates for therapeutic use
3.1.

## Positional scanning substrate combinatorial library
The Positional Scanning Substrate Combinatorial Library (PS-SCL) is one of the more widely used methods for screening protease specificity [ ]. Thanks to the advent of solid-phase peptide synthesis (SPPS) [ ], this approach employs chemically synthesized tetrapeptide libraries conjugated with fluorophores at the C-terminus. When a protease cleaves the peptide bond adjacent to the fluorophore, the fluorophore is released, producing a measurable fluorescent signal. The library covers a four-residue window from P4 to P1, where P1 denotes the amino acid immediately adjacent to the scissile bond, with numbering increasing toward the N-terminus ( ). While P1 residues are typically limited to a few specific amino acids for each protease [ ], residues at positions P2 to P4, which interact with the S2 to S4 binding pockets, play a crucial role in defining substrate specificity [ ].
 The first enzyme to which PS-SCL was applied was ICE [ ]. The authors confirmed a strong preference for Asp at the P1 position and identified WEHD as the optimal substrate sequence for ICE. This finding was initially controversial, as YVAD had previously been considered optimal [ ], and the natural cleavage site in pro-IL-1β is YVHD [ ]. However, subsequent studies consistently supported WEHD as a superior substrate, underscoring the power of PS-SCL in refining protease substrate specificities. Building on this, the application of PS-SCL was extended to profile substrate specificities across the caspase family. This effort discovered that caspases could be grouped based on their preferred cleavage motifs: inflammatory caspases (caspase-1, −4, and −5) prefer (W/L)EHD sequences; caspase-2, −3, and −7 favor DEXD motifs (where X represents any amino acid); and caspase-6, −8, and −9 prefer (L/V)EXD [ ]. This work highlighted both redundancy and promiscuity in caspase substrate recognition, while emphasizing the critical role of the P4 residue in conferring selectivity. Of note, caspases have long been prominent therapeutic targets due to their well-established biological functions. As well-characterized proteases, they have frequently served as proof-of-concept models for evaluating new substrate-profiling technologies. To simplify the labor-intensive synthesis of peptide libraries, researchers transitioned from using 7-amino-4-methylcoumarin (AMC) to 7-amino-4-carbamoylmethylcoumarin (ACC) [ ]. ACC can be conjugated to a solid support, allowing for direct sequential coupling of amino acids onto the ACC-resin and streamlining library synthesis that included randomization of all four positions, P1-P4. This ACC-based approach has since been applied to profile a wide range of proteases, including granzymes [ ], human cathepsins [ ], kallikreins [ ] and many others.

3.2.

## Hybrid combinatorial substrate library
Researchers recognized that incorporating unnatural amino acids (UAAs) into peptide substrates can enhance both specificity and serum stability, addressing the vulnerability of natural peptides to degradation in the human body [ ]. To support this goal, the hybrid combinatorial substrate library (HyCoSuL) was developed to incorporate a broad spectrum of UAAs [ ]. The library includes post-translationally modified residues such as citrulline, hydroxyproline, and acetyl-lysine, as well as -CH2 group homologs, D-amino acids, and various derivatives of the 20 canonical amino acids. When applied to the caspase family, this strategy demonstrated that UAAs can effectively discriminate among caspases with overlapping and promiscuous substrate preferences [ ]. Building on this platform, HyCoSuL was also combined with ABPs to develop inhibitors targeting the SARS-CoV-2 main protease (Mpro) [ ]. In this case, a vinylsulfone warhead was attached to the C-terminus of Ac-Abu-Tle-Leu-Gln, a peptide sequence identified by HyCoSuL as highly selective for Mpro.

3.3.

## Counter selection substrate library
The HyCoSuL approach was further refined into the Counter Selection Substrate Library (CoSeSuL) to achieve enhanced substrate specificity [ ]. This method involves the rational selection of amino acids or UAAs based on comparative substrate preferences between the target protease and closely related proteases. For instance, in identifying legumain-specific peptide substrates, substrate preferences of legumain and caspases, both capable of cleaving after Asp, were compared. While Thr was strongly preferred by legumain, it was also recognized by caspases. To increase specificity, Ser was substituted for Thr at the P2 position, effectively reducing cross-reactivity and improving selectivity for legumain.

3.4.

## DNA-Encoded noncanonical substrate library
The concept of a tetrapeptide library was retained and evolved into a more advanced protease profiling technique called DENSL (DNA-Encoded Noncanonical Substrate Library) [ ]. DENSL employs a tetrapeptide library conjugated with biotin at the N-terminus and a DNA tag at the C-terminus to improve analytical precision. Upon protease cleavage, the released DNA tags are recovered from the supernatant, amplified by PCR using flanking sequences, and then analyzed by next-generation sequencing (NGS) ( ). Similar to HyCoSuL, DENSL incorporates unnatural amino acids to expand substrate diversity. This method was applied to profile the substrate specificity of fibroblast activation protein (FAP).

3.5.

## Peptide substrate phage display
Instead of relying on chemically synthesized peptide libraries, some researchers have turned to DNA-encoded libraries. Peptide substrate libraries constructed via phage display leverage phage biology to outsource the labor-intensive process of library construction [ ]. Initially, a 5-amino-acid DNA library was generated through random mutagenesis. Phage populations displaying peptides that were either cleaved or uncleaved by the target protease could be collected, recycled, and enriched to identify sequences sensitive or resistant to proteolytic cleavage ( ). More recently, this method has been combined with NGS to achieve deeper sequence coverage [ ]. Using a 10-amino-acid DNA library followed by NGS analysis, favorable peptide substrates spanning positions P4 to P2’ were identified. Caspases served as a proof of concept, with the findings closely matching previously reported substrate specificities for these enzymes.

3.6.

## Multiplex substrate profiling by mass spectrometry
Instead of traditional tetrapeptide libraries, which are limited in exploring the extended substrate binding pockets on either side of the scissile bond, a novel methodology known as Multiplex Substrate Profiling by Mass Spectrometry (MSP-MS) was developed [ ]. This approach leverages LC-MS/MS analysis to quantitatively evaluate a library of substrate motifs eight amino acids in length through comprehensive, high-throughput screening. Its strength lies in a carefully designed peptide library comprising 228 tetradecapeptides, systematically designed to provide maximum physico-chemical diversity in a minimum number of peptides ( ). MSP-MS enables both the identification of octamer substrate motifs and the direct quantification and kinetic characterization of proteolytic activity. This platform has been used to profile peptide substrates for differentiating pancreatic cysts [ ]. Additionally, MSP-MS facilitates comprehensive analysis of peptide substrate specificity within the cancer secretome, as shown in profiling studies of the breast cancer secretome [ ]. A recent review provides further examples of the methodology [ ].

3.7.

## Utilizing protease-specific peptides in therapeutic interventions: from inhibition to activation
Interest in inhibiting disease-related proteolytic activity was sparked by the discovery of natural inhibitors of MMPs, known as Tissue Inhibitors of MetalloProteinases (TIMPs) [ , ]. Designing chemical inhibitors for MMPs was relatively straightforward because these enzymes uniquely contain a Zn2+ ion in their active site [ ]. Hydroxamates, which readily coordinate with Zn2+, were initially developed to inhibit MMP enzymatic activity. However, their high reactivity and low specificity led to unpredictable effects in the human body [ ]. Despite more than three decades of research, only one MMP inhibitor, a doxycycline-based compound, doxycycline hyclate, has been approved by the FDA and it was identified serendipitously when prescribed as an antibiotic [ ].
Luckily, there have been successful cases where target proteases function within specific biological contexts. A prime example is angiotensin-converting enzyme (ACE) inhibitors [ ]. ACE is a key protease that regulates blood pressure and electrolyte balance by converting angiotensin I into angiotensin II [ ]. Various ACE inhibitors, such as captopril and enalapril, were developed by mimicking the enzyme’s natural substrates to lower blood pressure. However, these drugs often caused side effects like dry cough. Researchers then shifted focus to inhibiting renin, an enzyme acting earlier in the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system (RAAS) signaling cascade, achieving similar blood pressure control with fewer side effects [ ]. This case highlights the importance of considering proteolytic activation within its broader biological context.
Viruses are excellent therapeutic targets for protease inhibition because they encode unique proteases essential for their life cycle [ ]. Viruses hijack the host’s translational machinery to produce large polyproteins, which are subsequently cleaved and activated by viral proteases [ ]. This specific biological context has facilitated successful inhibitor discovery. The first FDA-approved viral protease inhibitor, saquinavir, targeted the Human Immunodeficiency Virus (HIV) and was developed as a peptidomimetic inhibitor mimicking the natural substrates of the HIV protease [ ]. A key concept arising from HIV protease inhibitor research is the substrate envelope, the common structural volume shared by many different substrates of a target protease [ ]. Designing inhibitors that fit within this substrate envelope can reduce the likelihood of drug resistance, as protrusions beyond this envelope tend to drive mutations in viral proteases. These successes have extended to other viral proteases, such as Mpro, resulting in the FDA approval of nirmatrelvir, which inactivates Mpro through covalent inhibition. Researchers continue to optimize these inhibitors to enhance their pharmaceutical properties [ , ].
Building on previous successes, optimized target-specific peptide substrates identified through the aforementioned approaches were initially converted into inhibitors by conjugating peptidomimetic sequences to reactive chemical groups, such as aldehydes and ketones to achieve proteolytic inactivation [ ]. Despite extensive clinical efforts, no inhibitors derived from these methods have yet achieved clinical success. Traditional protease inhibition is stoichiometrically limited, as inhibitors act at a strict 1-to-1 ratio, restricting drug efficacy. Moreover, achieving target-specific inhibition is extremely challenging due to the redundancy and overlapping activities of proteases.
PROteolysis TArgeting Chimera (PROTAC) technology has also been employed to directly degrade disease-related proteases. This bifunctional molecule binds both a target protease and an E3 ubiquitin ligase, bringing them into proximity and directing the protease for ubiquitination and subsequent degradation. Methionine aminopeptidase-2 (MetAP-2), which plays a crucial role in angiogenesis, was the first protease targeted. However, no PROTAC-based approach has yet been approved by the FDA, largely due to poor cell permeability and limited target selectivity [ ].
These repeated setbacks have prompted a shift in perspective toward alternative strategies. Inspired by natural mechanisms of protease activation and cellular regulation, researchers began to harness the redundancy of proteolysis rather than suppress it, allowing for amplification of proteolytic signals. This paradigm shift sparked growing interest in protease-cleavable linkers, where proteolytic activity is not inhibited but directly leveraged as a functional element in therapeutic design [ ].

3.8.

## Protease-activatable imaging platforms and therapeutics
Peptide-based platforms have been actively developed to enable protease-activated visual readouts. One example involves a phenylalanine-lysine dipeptide-based substrate, in which the N-terminus is masked by a benzyloxycarbonyl group (zFK) ( ). A fluorescent dye is conjugated to the C-terminus, while a corresponding quencher is attached to the P1 residue (Lys) near the dye, creating a fluorescence resonance energy transfer (FRET) pair that prevents nonspecific activation. Upon cleavage by cathepsins, the C-terminal dye is released and emits fluorescence. This platform was used to image cathepsin activity in cancer cells for real-time imaging and surgical guidance [ , ]. The system was further expanded by incorporating two quenchers connected by different protease substrates, targeting both cathepsins and caspase-3, to function as an AND gate ( ). In this design, both substrates must be cleaved to activate fluorescence, resulting in improved signal-to-noise ratio and better surgical guidance in a breast cancer mouse model compared to single-substrate probes [ ].
 FRET has also been applied to monitor T-cell-mediated anticancer activity via granzyme B. To improve the design beyond the IEPD motif, a dipeptide (Ala-Leu) was added to the prime side. The resulting fluorogenic substrate was labeled with a BODIPY fluorophore at the N-terminus and a Dabcyl quencher at the C-terminus ( ) [ ]. It successfully visualized granzyme B activity in CD8+ T cells. Additionally, an IEPD sequence conjugated with a C-terminal aldehyde group was used as a selective granzyme B inhibitor, highlighting the versatility of optimized peptide substrates [ ].
The Restricted Interaction Peptide (RIP) platform was also employed to monitor granzyme B activity using a distinct strategy. A RIP consists of three modules: an N-terminal membrane-binding domain (MBD), derived from the antimicrobial peptide temporin L, which enables interaction with the cell membrane; a C-terminal peptide masking domain (PMD) that binds tightly to the MBD to suppress nonspecific membrane interaction; and a central protease-cleavable domain often identified through MSP-MS analysis ( ). Upon cleavage by a target transmembrane or secreted protease, the PMD is released, allowing the MBD to undergo a disorder-to-order transition and engage the membrane [ ]. An octapeptide (IEPDVSQV) was inserted into the cleavable domain, radiolabeled, and successfully used to detect the granzyme B activity associated with cytotoxic T cells that were activated with checkpoint inhibitors [ ].
Activatable cell-penetrating peptide (ACPP) represents another promising platform based on a concept similar to RIP. ACPP employs poly-Arg sequences for cell penetration, which are initially masked by poly-Glu via a linker containing a protease-cleavable substrate ( ). This design allows for protease-dependent internalization. An MMP-2-cleavable ACPP demonstrated successful localization in an HT-1080 xenograft mouse model [ ].
Developing protease-activatable imaging agents can serve as an important steppingstone toward novel therapeutics by providing valuable insights into how lead compounds behave and deliver payloads to target regions, even at low doses. Ideally, fluorescent dyes or radioisotopes used in imaging can be readily substituted with cytotoxic agents, enabling targeted drug delivery, particularly in peptide-based platforms with extended, target-specific substrates such as RIP and ACPP. For example, ACPP has been conjugated with the protease-activated prodrug Val-Cit-PAB-MMAE (VCMMAE), which releases the cytotoxic agent MMAE upon proteolytic activation, to radiosensitize cancer cells [ ].
VCMMAE is the most well-known protease-activated prodrug, built on the valine-citrulline (Val-Cit) linker. It was originally developed to target cathepsin B (CTSB), a lysosomal protease that is frequently overexpressed in the TME [ ]. Researchers have conjugated drugs such as monomethyl auristatin E (MMAE) and doxorubicin to this linker. However, the peptide bond between citrulline and the drug was initially not sufficiently accessible for efficient cleavage [ ]. This limitation was overcome by introducing a self-immolative spacer, p-aminobenzyl (PAB), which enabled complete proteolytic cleavage and the release of intact, scar-free drugs [ ]. The resulting Val-Cit-PAB linker became a canonical motif in ADCs and remains a critical tool in the field ( ) [ ]. In this elegant design, proteolytic activation was employed in a practical way while the antibody component provided target specificity. VCMMAE opened a new avenue where proteolytic activation directly governs drug release and serves as a key therapeutic module that can be combined with other treatment modalities. The Val-Cit linker has since been optimized by adding a glutamic acid residue at the P3 position, improving serum stability while preserving protease sensitivity and therapeutic efficacy, an excellent example of how protease-activated prodrugs can be refined for better specificity and performance [ ].
Harnessing proteolytic activity has further expanded across diverse therapeutic modalities. For example, Probody therapeutics use antibodies masked with protease-cleavable linkers to minimize off-target toxicity by remaining inactive until tumor-associated proteases within the TME activate them [ ]. Similarly, T-cell engagers can incorporate proteolytic linkers to ensure activation occurs only upon cleavage by tumor-specific proteases, enabling localized therapeutic action [ ]. Moreover, peptide masks can be engineered for cleavage by multiple disease-associated proteases, improving activation efficiency and therapeutic precision [ ]. Ongoing collaboration between academia and industry continues to advance this promising strategy, highlighting its potential to shape the future of protease-targeted therapies [ ].

4.

## Conclusion
With the rapid advancement of scientific technologies, the diverse roles of proteases in the human body have been increasingly elucidated. In earlier stages, researchers relied on their own hypotheses to identify potential natural substrates for target proteases, often confirming proteolytic activity through targeted experiments. As technology progressed, particularly with the rise of MS, high-throughput proteomic analyses became possible. Large-scale studies began with gel-based methods, which were limited by low reproducibility, and later evolved into N-terminomics, assisted by chemical strategies designed to enrich neo-N-termini. These large-scale approaches are sometimes combined with ABPs to improve specificity and analytical convenience. This expanding encyclopedia of protease substrates, especially those associated with diseases, has opened up new avenues for therapeutic intervention.
Proteases play critical roles in diseases such as cancer and viral infections as well as other diseases and have attracted considerable attention as druggable targets. Structural studies revealed that the four amino acids extending from the scissile bond toward the N-terminus are key determinants of substrate specificity, leading to the development of various chemically synthesized tetrapeptide libraries. To profile protease substrate preferences, fluorogenic libraries labeled with dyes at the C-terminus were introduced and widely adopted. PS-SCL marked the beginning of this approach, which later evolved into HyCoSuL and subsequently CoSeSuL.
Peptide substrates identified through these methods were chemically modified by conjugating drugs or reporter groups. However, these stoichiometric approaches have achieved limited clinical success, largely due to challenges in attaining both high specificity and therapeutic efficacy. To overcome these limitations, researchers turned to an alternative strategy: leveraging the natural redundancy of proteolytic activities in the human body. This approach amplifies proteolytic signals, enabling their translation into measurable outputs such as fluorescence, radioactivity, or drug release, ultimately improving therapeutic efficacy. One major advancement in this direction was the development of the Val-Cit linker, designed for protease-triggered drug release. Its utility was greatly enhanced by the discovery of the self-immolative PAB linker, making it the most widely used proteolytic linker in ADC research.
Interest in proteases continues to grow, as recent studies have uncovered new roles for these enzymes in cancer biology. One notable example is lysosomal leucine aminopeptidase, which plays a critical role in nutrient uptake in pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma, highlighting it as a novel druggable target [ ]. As the trajectory of kinase research has shown [ ], the field of protease-targeting therapeutics holds tremendous potential, especially with the help of advanced technologies and deepened mechanistic understanding of protease functions in disease. We anticipate that increasingly sophisticated techniques and creative approaches will be applied to protease-related research, leading to valuable insights and the development of promising new therapeutic interventions.

## Expert opinion
As researchers continue to harness proteolytic activity for therapeutic applications, a wide range of proteolytic linkers has been developed and successfully conjugated to a variety of therapeutic modalities, including antibodies. Early linker designs were relatively simple, but modern versions have become increasingly sophisticated, incorporating diverse chemical groups to enhance target specificity, improve stability, and enable conditional cleavage only in the presence of the desired proteolytic activity [ ]. These linkers are now paired with a broad array of cytotoxic agents, expanding the scope of payloads that can be delivered in a controlled manner [ ]. ADCs featuring such protease-cleavable linkers have received consistent FDA approval over the past decade, firmly establishing them as a major and reliable therapeutic class [ ]. Their success has inspired continued investment into refining linker chemistry to achieve even greater selectivity and therapeutic indices.
The potential of proteolytic linkers extends beyond ADCs to more complex modalities, including conditionally activated antibodies, cytokines, and T-cell engagers. In addition to the Probody platform discussed above, the INDUKINE protein complex consists of cytokine, half-life extending, and inactivation domains, which can be combined by proteolytic linkers. Target proteolytic activity releases each component, triggering a localized immune response [ ]. This approach can be combined with other modalities to improve specificity and efficacy toward the target tissue. Protease-activatable linkers have also been applied in T-cell engager engineering, as in TRACTR and TRACIR, where tumor-binding and T-cell-binding domains are masked by cleavable linkers and activated only by target proteolytic activity near tumors [ , ]. With steady contributions from academia and industry, including ongoing clinical trials, protease-targeting therapeutics have proven both promising and practical.
MS-based techniques have been a driving force in these advances, delivering unprecedented resolution and throughput. We expect MS-based analyses to continue thriving, especially with new chemical modifications tailored to proteomics. One promising direction is integrating small-molecule inhibitors such as UAMC-1110, a FAP inhibitor [ ], into MS workflows for more focused, target-specific insights in relevant biological contexts. Ongoing technical innovations in MS are also expanding both the depth and the reliability of proteomic analyses. For instance, high-Field Asymmetric waveform Ion Mobility Spectrometry (FAIMS) [ ] and Data-Independent Acquisition (DIA) [ ] have emerged as powerful strategies, enabling deeper proteomic exploration even when sample quantities are limited.
Although this review centers on the paradigm shift toward harnessing proteolytic activity for therapeutics, traditional protease inhibitor discovery remains highly valuable, particularly as artificial intelligence (AI) emerges as a powerful tool. AI-guided drug design can distinguish between protease variants, provide structural insights, and accelerate medicinal chemistry efforts such as structure-activity relationship (SAR) studies and structure-guided drug design. Given that proteases typically have well-defined enzymatic cavities, they are well suited for computational modeling. AI is expected to shorten the timeline for high-throughput docking-based screening, specificity validation, and structural tuning of the drug candidates, fueling excitement in the field [ – ]. Conversely, the approaches described in this review can also be used to design therapeutic proteases that can overcome the limitations of current therapeutic modalities such as small molecules and antibodies [ , ].
Identifying the endogenous substrates for any given protease continues to befuddle progress. As mentioned above, because a protease cleaves a particular protein in vitro does not necessarily mean that it does so in a biological context that is relevant. Recent technological advances are opening new avenues for uncovering disease-related roles of proteases. Just as kinases became dominant drug targets following discoveries of their central role in cancer biology and the ability to selectively inhibit them, proteases are poised to attract similar levels of attention and investment. With the rapid advancement of artificial intelligence and the growing repository of protease substrate data, it is becoming increasingly feasible to predict proteolytic specificity and rationally design therapeutic modalities incorporating protease-cleavable linkers [ ]. We believe that continued integration of computational tools into protease research will usher in a new chapter of discovery and therapeutic innovation for prodrugs and protease inhibitors.

Advances in technology have driven deeper research into proteases and their biological roles.
Large-scale proteomic approaches have emerged beyond individual protease profiling to identify novel protease-activated substrates.
With disease-associated proteases considered druggable targets, various peptide-based methods have been developed.
Research focus is shifting from protease inhibition to harnessing protease activity for activating drug-related modalities.
Applying recent technical innovations to protease studies holds promise for discovering new therapeutic interventions.
