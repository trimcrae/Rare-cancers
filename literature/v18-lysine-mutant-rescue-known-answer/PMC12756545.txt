Traditional proteomics often provides static snapshots of protein expression, leaving unanswered questions about how proteins respond to stimuli and affect cellular functions. Limited proteolysis coupled with mass spectrometry (LiP-MS) has emerged as a powerful technique for exploring protein structure and function under near-natural conditions. Studies have revealed that LiP-MS is invaluable for structural and functional proteomics because it offers novel insights into protein dynamics. In this review, we summarise the current applications of LiP-MS in diverse areas such as the discovery and identification of drug targets, metabolite action mechanisms, proteome dynamics, protein interactions, and disease biomarkers. We also address the critical challenges in ongoing research and discuss their broader implications for advancing our understanding of protein biology and drug discovery. LiP-MS holds significant promise for accelerating biomarker and therapeutic target development as well as advancing molecular biology research in animals, plants, and microorganisms.

## Introduction
The primary objective of protein research is to understand the activity, structure, dynamics, interactions, and responses of proteins to stimuli [ ]. Protein function is regulated by expression, interactions, chemical modifications, and structural changes [ ]. Therefore, studying the protein structure is essential for uncovering the biological roles and mechanisms of proteins. Currently, the commonly used approaches for studying protein structure include biophysical methods [ , , ], chemical proteomics [ ], and structural proteomics [ ]. Biophysical methods, such as nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) and Förster resonance energy transfer (FRET), can be used to monitor protein conformational changes. While FRET is applicable to in vivo studies, NMR is typically restricted to in vitro or near-native conditions due to its sensitivity and technical limitations, and is not suitable for studying complex biological extracts. Chemical proteomics identifies target proteins using chemically derived small molecules; however, this approach has some limitations [ , ]. Modifying small molecules may affect their biological activity and binding specificity, potentially affecting protein-drug interactions and disrupting protein conformation. This complicates the interpretation of findings in complex biological systems [ ]. Structural proteomics primarily focuses on globular proteins and large non-covalent protein complexes, but offers limited insight into intrinsically disordered proteins (IDPs) or proteins with intrinsically disordered regions (IDRs) [ ].
In the early 1990s, protein hydrolysis combined with mass spectrometry (MS) was introduced, but initially only provided static protein expression data. MS has become a cornerstone of proteomics, facilitating systemic protein expression analyses [ ]. The LiP-MS approach [ ] capitalizes on the differential susceptibility of natively conformed proteins to proteolytic digestion, yielding a distinct peptide fingerprint. When coupled with mass spectrometry, this fingerprint enables the elucidation of protein structural features. In the native state of proteins, certain regions may be shielded from protease cleavage due to steric hindrance. Upon ligand binding or conformational changes, these protected regions may be exposed or exhibit altered sensitivity. Proteinase K (PK), a nonspecific serine protease with broad substrate specificity, is used for the partial proteolysis of proteins. This enzyme cleaves peptide bonds across various amino acid residues and functions effectively under a wide range of pH and temperature conditions, making it suitable for the processing of complex biological samples. Cleavage sites depend on the three-dimensional (3D) structure and local amino acid sequence of the protein, resulting in a limited number of peptides owing to structural constraints. After denaturation, the protein is digested with trypsin to produce peptides amenable to MS. By comparing peptides generated under different conditions such as ligand binding or environmental changes, it is possible to infer structural alterations in the protein. Protein interactions with other molecules may induce conformational changes that influence protease cleavage patterns. By analysing the differences in peptide profiles under interacting and non-interacting conditions, binding sites and structural impacts can be identified.
MS is sensitive enough to detect small structural changes and conformational changes in low-abundance proteins. It is also sufficiently sensitive to quantify changes in protein structural sites. By integrating LiP with advanced MS techniques ( ), such as parallel reaction monitoring (PRM), selected reaction monitoring (SRM), data-dependent acquisition (DDA), and data-independent acquisition (DIA), researchers can study conformational changes in specific proteins across entire protein networks. Unlike traditional protein crystallography, LiP-MS does not require protein crystallisation, which makes it ideal for proteins that are difficult or impossible to crystallise. In addition, this technique avoids the need for special labelling, enrichment, or purification, thereby minimising experimental interference. Thus, LiP-MS is highly suitable for analysing complex biological samples, such as native cell lysates, tissues, blood, and macromolecular complexes. Although current applications are primarily performed in lysed samples rather than intact cellular environments, LiP-MS remains a powerful tool for investigating large-scale protein structural changes and mapping protein-protein and protein-small molecule (metabolite and drug) interaction networks. These features make the method invaluable for disease biomarker discovery and drug target research. The broad potential of LiP-MS suggests that it will play an increasingly significant role in future proteomics and structural biology studies. 
In this review, we discuss the recent advances in LiP-MS technology and provide an in-depth overview of its applications in drug discovery, disease research, protein dynamics, and protein interactions ( ) [ , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , ]). We also offer a systematic analysis of the current progress in this field and its future prospects.

## LiP-MS method workflow
Proteins are extracted from biological samples including cells, tissues, blood, and cerebrospinal fluid, and protease inhibitors are added to prevent protein degradation. Proteins are maintained in their native environments and are subjected to various stimuli. The samples are then incubated with PK for 5 min, followed by the addition of sodium deoxycholate to inhibit enzymatic activity and induce protein denaturation. High temperatures are then used to inactivate the enzymes. After reduction and alkylation, samples are digested with the specific proteases trypsin and Lys-C. Formic acid is added to the precipitate, sodium deoxycholate is removed, and desalting is performed. The resulting peptide mixture is then analysed using MS.
This workflow enables the assessment of structural dynamics and conformational changes in proteins under various conditions, providing insights into protein-ligand interactions and other structural modifications.

## Development of LiP-MS
At the Federal Polytechnic Institute in 2014, Picotti and co-workers [ ] introduced the LiP-MS method to study proteins within bacterial and fungal cell systems. When applied to yeast cells, this method revealed that proteome-wide reversible protein aggregation was influenced by sequence-coding properties. The aggregated proteins exhibited characteristics similar to those of supramolecular amphiphiles, in which the aggregation-prone regions were hydrophobic and the non-aggregation-prone regions were hydrophilic [ ].
In 2018, a new tool called LiP-small molecule mapping (LiP-SMap) was developed to explore structural changes in proteins during their interactions with metabolites, drug targets, and other proteins [ ]. This approach systematically detects proteins within protein-small molecule complexes that are sensitive to broad-specificity proteases, identifying protein regions that undergo conformational changes upon binding to specific molecules. This enabled a more precise understanding of molecular interactions and their effects on protein function. A notable application of LiP-SMap was in the study of the bacterial Calvin cycle, where it uncovered the metabolite-level regulation of enzymes in the Calvin cycle and central carbon metabolism, expanding research beyond bacteria and fungi to include small molecules and metabolites [ ].
In 2020, LiP-Quant was introduced, representing a major advancement in quantifying protein-molecule interactions [ ]. This method measures the binding affinity of molecules to proteins (i.e., the binding parameter) and assesses their impact on protein stability and activity. By integrating machine learning with LiP-MS, LiP-Quant enables the global detection of drug targets across various compound concentrations while reducing false positives and experimental errors. This provides a quantitative framework for studying drug discovery and protein function.
Overall, LiP-MS and its derivatives are powerful tools for investigating the structural and functional alterations of proteins under diverse biological conditions, including responses to drugs, environmental adaptations, and distinguishing between pathological and normal states. These technologies facilitate a deeper understanding of complex interaction networks, disease mechanisms, and organismal responses to environmental challenges.

## LiP-MS current applications
4.1

## Discovery of drug targets
4.1.1

## Discovery of target proteins of natural products (NPs)
NPs are rich sources of potential drug candidates because of their structural diversity. Identifying the protein targets of NPs is critical to understand their mechanisms of action and facilitate drug discovery. For example, Llamas et al. [ ] expressed aggregation-prone fragments of the human Huntington protein (HTT) with an extended polyglutamine (polyQ) region (Q69) in Arabidopsis, revealing that stromal processing peptidase (SPP), a protein responsible for cleaving chloroplast transit peptides, was the most enriched interactor of Q69. These findings suggest that targeting SPP is a potential strategy for the treatment of Huntington’s disease and other polyQ-related disorders. LiP-MS methods have also identified myristic acid, a naturally occurring biologically active triterpene, interacts with fatty acid synthase (FAS), revealing its therapeutic potential [ ]. Additionally, the cyclic peptide α-amanitin, known for its hepatotoxicity, interacts with proteins involved in protein synthesis and RNA polymerase II (RNAPII), shedding light on its toxic effects in hepatocytes ( A) [ ]. In the area of cardiovascular disease, benzoylaconitine, a NP, has been shown to inhibit angiotensin II-induced hypertrophy and fibrosis in rat cardiomyocytes and fibroblasts. Zhang et al. [ ] demonstrated that benzoylaconitine directly binds to angiotensin-converting enzyme 2 (ACE2) using LiP-MS, suggesting its potential for the treatment of heart failure. Similarly, artemisinin, a NP historically used to treat malaria, was shown to exhibit anti-cancer activity. Ferraro et al. [ ] reported that artemisinin and its derivative 8-vinyl artemisinin directly interacted with filament proteins in HeLa cell lysates, offering insights into its broader therapeutic effects. 
Traditional plant proteomics and metabolomics methods have limitations in identifying NP protein targets. However, the advent of LiP-MS has revolutionised this field, particularly in studies focusing on the active ingredients in traditional Chinese medicine (TCM). This approach has significantly improved our understanding of the molecular targets of TCM compounds and continues to expand their applications.

4.1.2

## Discovery of synthetic drug target proteins
Synthetic drugs address the limitations of natural medicines by offering precise design, which enhances drug development and provides effective treatments for challenging diseases. For instance, using LiP-MS, researchers discovered that 2,3-benzodiazepine-4-one crosses the blood-brain barrier (BBB) and inhibits brain glycogen phosphorylase (PYGB) activity in glioblastoma multiforme (GBM) [ ]. This inhibition disrupts cell growth and the cell cycle, positioning PYGB as a potential therapeutic target for GBM. Therefore, 2,3-benzodiazepine-4-one has emerged as an anticancer agent that negatively regulates glucose uptake and metabolism. During the coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) pandemic, Shuster et al. [ ] identified the S protein of severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2) as a precise binding site for arbidol using LiP-MS technology. Glucocorticoids have also been shown to bind multiple sites on the S1 protein of SARS-CoV-2, inducing conformational changes that block the virus from binding to the ACE2 receptor, thus presenting a new therapeutic strategy against SARS-CoV-2 [ ].
In addition to discovering new drugs, LiP-MS technology has been used to re-evaluate FDA-approved drug libraries to discover new drug targets [ ]. For example, using LiP-SMap, lomitapide was identified as an inhibitor of protein phosphatase 2A (PP2A) and repositioned as a potential therapeutic agent for colorectal cancer [ ]. Additionally, LiP-MS provides multidimensional characterisation of drug effects, expanding the search for small-molecule antibacterial agents [ ]. Jones et al. [ ] used this technology to propose a binding-site hypothesis for small molecule enhancer of rapamycin 28 (SMER28) and its small-molecule mimics, identifying a druggable metastable site and a regulatory mechanism for the enzyme valosin-containing protein (VCP). Therefore, LiP-MS has emerged as a robust tool in drug discovery and reassessment, facilitating the identification of novel drugs and targets and revealing new therapeutic applications for existing drugs, thereby significantly expediting drug development.

4.2

## Study on the mechanism of metabolites
LiP-MS has been instrumental in enhancing our understanding of endogenous substances and their mechanisms of action in organisms. By correlating protein stability with chain length and subcellular localisation, LiP-MS can predict the stability of proteins across the human proteome. In addition, this method successfully identified bile acid (BA)-interacting proteins and confirmed the endogenous phytohormonal activity of bile acid 2-hydroxylase (BA2H). The discovery that HeLa cells, HEK293T cells, and primary splenocytes cannot convert bile acid-α carbon 13 labeled (BA-α13C) to salicylic acid-α carbon 13 labeled (SA-α13C) suggests a potential role for phytohormones in mammals [ ]. Zhang et al. analysed proteomic structural changes in lipopolysaccharide (LPS)-activated macrophages using LiP-MS and identified 386 structural protein hydrolysis fingerprints from 230 proteins ( B) [ ]. Significant structural alterations of heat shock protein 60 (HSP60) have attracted considerable attention. Similarly, Sztacho et al. [ ] identified 515 proteins with phosphatidylinositol 4,5-bisphosphate (PIP2) binding capacity, highlighting PIP2 as the most prominent nucleoprotein effector in regions fundamental to gene expression and DNA repair.
LiP-MS has also been used in T cell research. Geiger et al. [ ] used this technique to identify proteins with high levels of l-arginine modification, revealing their critical roles in T-cell metabolism. Furthermore, LiP-MS has been employed to study plant stress signalling, specifically the regulatory role of abscisic acid in plants under abiotic stress [ ]. These findings offer valuable insights into the stress response mechanisms in plants and provide technical guidance for identifying molecular targets relevant to crop engineering and stress tolerance.
LiP-MS has significantly contributed to diverse fields such as proteomics, immunology, lipid signalling, T-cell metabolism, and plant stress responses. This technology holds tremendous promise, and is expected to play an even greater role in advancing biomedical and agricultural research.

4.3

## Analysis of changes in proteome dynamics
4.3.1

## Conformational changes of protein
Protein structures undergo conformational changes due to various factors, such as ligand binding, protein-protein interactions (PPIs), post-translational modifications (PTMs), and protein aggregation. Barret et al. [ ] used crosslinking MS and LiP-MS to map conformational changes in proteins. Their work sheds light on how calmodulin (CaM), a crucial ion channel regulator, behaves both in vitro and in natural membranes, particularly in tissues of medical interest. Similarly, research on silk microcrystals under heat stress has revealed changes in protein fragmentation patterns and secondary structures through specific peptide content alterations [ ]. A proposed degradation model for thermally aged proteins was constructed by integrating mechanical and crystallographic analyses. Domnauer et al. [ ] explored the effects of prolonged temperature changes on budding yeast and noted that chaperones could mediate a shift in thermotolerant proteins, leading to their redistribution within cells. Leuenberger et al. [ ] investigated temperature-dependent conformational changes in Escherichia coli (E. coli), thermoacidophilic bacteria, Staphylococcus saprophyticus, and HeLa cells, and showed that shorter, more abundant proteins tend to be more stable [ ]. Further research on abiotic stress in plants using LiP-MS revealed structural alterations in proteins following stress treatments ( C) [ ].
Conformational changes also influence enzyme activity. Backe et al. [ ] studied the molecular chaperone HSP90 in complex with the autophagy-initiating kinases autophagy-related gene 1 (Atg1) (yeast) and Unc-51 like autophagy activating kinase 1 (Ulk1) (mammals), and found that changes in kinase conformation affected HSP90 activity. Wrobel et al. [ ] found that the small molecule SMER28 binds to VCP (p97) and enhances ATPase activity. Recent studies, such as the work by Meng et al. [ ] on receptor interacting protein kinase (RIPK1) inhibition, have highlighted the importance of kinases in biology, demonstrating the synergistic inhibition of RIPK1 by necrostatin-34 and necrostatin-1 (NEC-1). LiP-MS has emerged as a valuable tool for probing protein structure and function across a wide range of applications ranging from basic research to drug development.

4.3.2

## Protein folding
IDRs of proteins, which lack a stable folded structure, are often rich in PTM sites and are prime targets for in vivo biotinylation studies. Subcellular biotin mapping could potentially be applied to the study of multi-transmembrane proteins such as G protein-coupled receptors (GPCRs). LiP-MS has proven crucial for characterising protein folding [ ], with comparative studies of homologous proteins across species revealing differences in foldability [ , ]. Denatured enzymes often rely on chaperones for proper folding ( D) [ ], although some proteins fail to fold completely and escape the chaperone surveillance system [ , ]. LiP-MS experiments have been instrumental in identifying misfolded proteins and determining their solubility in vivo [ ].
In protein folding research, LiP-MS is also used to examine protein refolding kinetics on a proteome-wide scale. Studies on E. coli have shown that approximately one-third of its proteome is intrinsically unable to refold within physiological timeframes. Low refolding rates are often associated with specific protein properties and folding patterns [ ]. Moreover, LiP-MS allows the assessment of protein conformation and structural stability in response to synonymous mutations and aging. These changes can be tracked through their susceptibility to proteolytic cleavage by enzymes, such as PK [ ], revealing how mutations and cellular senescence contribute to the misfolding of proteins and the creation of substable proteomic networks [ , , ].

4.4

## Study of protein interactions
LiP-MS technology has proven to be a powerful tool for discovering protein-binding sites, contributing significantly to the study of protein-protein and protein-small molecule interactions across a wide range of organisms, including microorganisms, viruses, animals, and plants. Traditional interactomic methods often struggle to identify structure-specific PPIs or precise interaction interfaces. Recently, affinity purification coupled with MS (AP-MS) has become commonly used to study these interactions. One such study by Piazza et al. identified a new network of interactions and binding sites within E. coli ( A) [ ]. Their findings revealed 1,678 protein-metabolite interactions and 7,345 potential binding sites in the E. coli metabolite-protein interactome. Sporre et al. [ ] applied LiP-SMap to uncover novel regulatory interactions between metabolites and enzymes central to carbon metabolism in four bacterial strains that utilise the Calvin cycle, including Synechocystis sp. Additionally, research on respiratory syncytial virus fusion (RSVF) glycoprotein has highlighted its interaction with site-specific neutralizing monoclonal antibodies [ ]. Protein-polysaccharide complexes also exhibit specific binding sites, as shown by Hu et al. [ ], who mapped the binding regions of whey (the raw material for whey protein products e.g., WPI mentioned earlier) isolate protein-leech polysaccharide complexes by characterising their in vitro digestibility. In plants, protein-protein and protein-metabolite interactions in leaves have been extensively studied, with research identifying the protein targets of small molecules involved in the regulation of leaf growth [ , ]. LiP-MS offers great potential for uncovering molecular targets related to stress tolerance and presents opportunities for crop engineering. Furthermore, MS-based techniques hold promise for exploring the 3D structures of proteins [ ] ( B), thereby enhancing our understanding of physiology and molecular biology. 

4.5

## Discovery of disease biomarkers
LiP-MS has been instrumental in identifying disease biomarkers, particularly proteins with structural changes, thus opening new avenues for drug discovery and treatment strategies [ , ]. As a versatile tool in structural biology, LiP-MS facilitates detailed studies of IDPs and insoluble aggregates associated with diseases. This technique is particularly valuable for studying neurodegenerative diseases such as Alzheimer’s disease and systemic sclerosis by offering insights into protein aggregation mechanisms. For example, LiP-MS method compares high-abundance test samples (e.g., aged mouse cerebrospinal fluid and mouse cerebrospinal fluid (mCSF)) with low-abundance control samples (e.g., young mCSF). Using treatments including denaturation + LysC/trypsin (16 h) and LiP + PK (5 min), it detects α-synuclein (α-syn), a Parkinson’s disease-linked protein serving as a neurodegenerative disease biomarker, and enables differentiation between protein abundance changes and structural alterations ( C) [ ]. It also helps to elucidate structural differences by revealing cleavage site accessibility and regions involved in intermolecular interactions [ ]. This technology provides critical structural information regarding complex protein targets in neurodegenerative diseases, helping to address knowledge gaps regarding the various pathways of protein aggregation. Notably, a previously unknown covalent immune complex, Cd5-like antigen-immunoglobulin M (CD5L-IgM), increased significantly with age in rat cerebrospinal fluid [ ]. In cancer research, LiP-MS revealed how the folliculin 10 (FLCN-10) peptide interacts with lactate dehydrogenase A (LDHA), exposing a soft region within LDHA’s “antigenic peptide” that could be targeted in cancer therapies [ ]. LiP-MS has also contributed to the understanding of the structure and regulation of pyruvate kinase isoforms, such as cell division cycle 19 (Cdc19) in yeast and its human homologue pyruvate kinase M2 (PKM2). The reversible aggregation of these enzymes, which display amyloid-like structures, offers insights into pathological processes such as oral squamous cell carcinoma [ ] and systemic sclerosis [ ]. The utilisation of Lip-MS not only enhances the understanding of disease mechanisms but also provides valuable information for the development of diagnostic tools and therapeutic interventions.

4.6

## Other applications
The integration of LiP-MS with other analytical methods has revealed new research possibilities. One such method, ProTstab, combines LiP-MS-derived cellular stability data with a gradient-enhanced regression tree machine learning approach to predict protein stability on a large scale [ ]. Similarly, a combination of LiP-MS and protein chip techniques has been employed to assess cellular protein stability [ ]. LiP-MS also has a significant potential for investigating the impact of materials and drug delivery systems in physiological contexts. For example, LiP using trypsin and chymotrypsin has been effectively used to map structural changes occurring during protein-nanoparticle interactions, shedding light on how nanoparticles behave in biological systems [ ]. Moreover, LiP-MS can be applied to various research fields. These include determining the full-length structure of the membrane-bound adenylate cyclase from Mycobacterium tuberculosis [ ], identifying electrophilic and metal adducts in haemoglobin [ ], and studying the regulation of the immunogenicity and allergenicity of allergens [ , , ]. These findings suggest that LiP-MS not only enhances experimental research but also provides deep insights into molecular disease mechanisms [ ].

## Conclusion and future perspectives
This review systematically compiles the recent advances in LiP-MS and highlights its role in drug target identification, metabolite mechanism resolution, proteome dynamics, protein interaction studies, and disease biomarker discovery. Although chemical proteomics remains dominant in protein structure studies, challenges persist, such as the availability of high-quality chemical probes and in situ analysis of drug-binding events. With its unmodified approach, LiP-MS presents unique advantages for structural proteomics and drug research. It is expected to facilitate deeper functional analyses of biomolecules, enhance our understanding of drug mechanisms, and accelerate drug discovery and development.
Despite its promise, the LiP-MS technology faces challenges in various applications. Although it can detect structural changes in proteins, it cannot identify the underlying mechanisms, such as PTMs, aggregation, or changes in enzyme activity. Owing to their strong hydrophobicity, membrane proteins typically require embedding within lipid bilayers or lipid-mimetic environments to maintain their functional structure. In addition, limited protease accessibility to certain regions further contributes to the challenges of applying LiP-MS to membrane proteins. Other limitations include incomplete sequence coverage and difficulties identifying specific LiP-binding sites. Additionally, LiP-MS is primarily applied to soluble proteins, whereas membrane proteins, which account for over 60% of drug targets, remain relatively unexplored. Although some progress has been made in solubilising membrane proteins using mild detergents, the study of these proteins remains challenging, especially in vivo and in intracellular contexts. The functional consequences of drug therapy cannot be directly reflected by LiP-MS alone. Therefore, a combination of physical interaction and functional analyses is necessary. Although chemical proteomics has limitations, it remains a valuable tool for target identification, particularly through strategies such as biochemical enrichment and proteasomal screening. The use of LiP-MS for drug development has significant potential in the emerging field of targeted protein degradation. This technique aids in understanding ligand-protein interactions, target specificity, and ligand binding, further supporting the development of degraders.
Although the combined use of multiple techniques enables the counting of highly overlapping target proteins, it relies on changes in protein structure to alter its degradation. Therefore, care must be taken to ensure that the changes in the activity of the target protein result in structural changes. The potential of LiP-MS for target deconvolution is currently hindered by the limited availability of databases and analytical datasets. In future, combining LiP-MS with artificial intelligence (AI) may unlock novel possibilities for compound target identification. However, several aspects require improvement to enhance the application of the LiP-MS technology:i)Method optimization. Proteomics currently suffers from low throughput and incomplete coverage. MS techniques tend to favour peptides that are easily hydrolysed and analysed, but trypsin, commonly used in LiP-MS, produces short peptides (≤9 amino acids on average). Combining Lys-C and trypsin improved coverage and exploring other hydrolase combinations could further optimise the results.
ii)Nano- or single-cell proteomics. Single-cell proteomics is gaining prominence because it enables the study of variations in protein expression at the cellular level. Advancements in MS sensitivity for single-cell analysis will enhance the capabilities of LiP-MS for small cell populations, enabling more comprehensive analyses.
iii)Integration of histologies. The integration of data from multiple fields, such as genomics, proteomics, transcriptomics, and translational genomics, creates information-rich datasets that can drive drug discovery. Achieving this integration requires not only unifying technical conditions but also the effective merging of multi-omics data.
iv)Combination of target discovery techniques. A single proteomic technique is insufficient to accurately identify drug targets or mechanisms of action. Combining LiP-MS with other methods such as AP, cellular thermal shift assay (CETSA)-MS, native MS, and cryoelectron microscopy can reduce false positives and provide deeper insights into protein dynamics. In particular, native MS has shown great promise in directly detecting protein-NP interactions, and serves as a powerful orthogonal method for validating ligand engagement [ , , , ].

In summary, LiP-MS has significant potential for proteomics and drug target identification. However, further refinement and integration with complementary technologies are necessary to fully realise their applications in the biomedical field.
