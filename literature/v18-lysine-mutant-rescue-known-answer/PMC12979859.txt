## Abstract
Despite advances in therapeutic regimens for managing cancer progression, ovarian cancer (OVC) still depends on platinum-based chemotherapy as its first-line treatment. Acquired resistance is accompanied by abnormal alterations in epigenetic regulation; however, in-depth mechanistic studies on cisplatin-resistant OVC are lacking. Herein, we show that abnormal overexpression of histone lysine demethylase 5B (KDM5B), but not KDM5A, strongly correlates with cisplatin resistance and OVC tumor progression. Genome-wide sequencing data revealed that KDM5B removes H3K4me3 from the promoter of dual-specificity phosphatase 4 (DUSP4), activating the MAPK pathway to increase cisplatin resistance. We also found that KDM5B protein stability is dynamically controlled via the ubiquitin-proteasome system (UPS), which is mediated by ubiquitin-specific protease 7 (USP7), F-box and WD repeat domain-containing 7 (FBXW7), and homeodomain-interacting protein kinase 1 (HIPK1). KDM5B and USP7 depletion effectively resensitizes OVC to cisplatin resistance, whereas DUSP4 silencing results in resistance in vitro and in vivo. Targeting KDM5B and USP7 synergistically represses tumor progression and increases sensitivity to cisplatin. Overall, we propose two new UPS-associated proteins, USP7 and FBXW7, which are responsible for abnormal KDM5B protein regulation, and suggest a novel mechanism to overcome cisplatin resistance in OVC by targeting the KDM5B–DUSP4 axis.

## Introduction
Ovarian cancer (OVC) ranks as the third most common gynecological malignancy worldwide, but it remains the most lethal. Approximately 75% of OVC patients are diagnosed at advanced FIGO stage III or IV, often with distant metastases. , The current standard of care for OVC patients includes optimal cytoreductive surgery followed by platinum-based chemotherapy. Up to 80% of OVC patients initially respond to platinum-based chemotherapy, but approximately 70% of responders develop platinum resistance and relapse. Many therapeutic regimens and clinical trials are aimed at overcoming and treating platinum-resistant OVC via combinations of different chemotherapeutic modalities. – Nonetheless, resistance to platinum-based drugs such as cisplatin remains a formidable barrier to improving overall survival (OS; <12 months) and progression-free survival (PFS; <4 months). , Therefore, understanding the mechanisms of drug resistance in OVC is crucial for identifying reliable biomarkers that may predict patients’ responses to therapies and improve treatment outcomes.
Recent studies have increased our understanding of how deregulation of epigenetic factors and abnormal alterations in the chromatin landscape contribute to drug resistance in OVC. For example, abnormal overexpression of histone deacetylases is positively correlated with cisplatin resistance in OVC cell lines. Similarly, the overexpression of the histone lysine methyltransferase EZH2 has also been found to contribute to acquired cisplatin resistance in OVC. Among the various epigenetic regulators, the histone lysine demethylase 5 (KDM5) protein family, primarily KDM5A and KDM5B, has gained increasing attention in the context of tumorigenesis and chemoresistance in different cancers, including head and neck cancer, prostate cancer, breast cancer, gastric cancer, and OVC. KDM5 enzymes specifically remove tri- and dimethyl groups from histone H3 on lysine 4 (H3K4me3/2), thereby repressing gene transcription. Although KDM5A and KDM5B share structural homology and overlapping substrate specificity, emerging evidence suggests that these demethylases exhibit distinct, context-specific biological roles and regulatory functions across various cancers. However, their relative contributions to OVC progression and cisplatin resistance remain unexplored. In this study, we directly compared the functions of KDM5A and KDM5B in cisplatin-resistant OVC to delineate their distinct contributions to chemoresistance.
Dual-specificity phosphatase 4 (DUSP4) is a negative regulator of the mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) pathway that dephosphorylates threonine and/or tyrosine residues of MAP kinases (ERK1/2, JNK, and p38). DUSP4 is recognized as a tumor suppressor in several cancers, including OVC, , breast cancer, – glioma, , and lung cancer. In contrast, several other studies have implicated DUSP4 as an oncogene in esophageal squamous cell carcinoma, breast cancer, and pancreatic cancer. The role of DUSP4 in tumorigenesis is highly context dependent and, in some cancers (e.g., breast cancer), remains controversial. In OVC, previous studies support the tumor-suppressive role of DUSP4. , , , Here, we provide further evidence that the downregulation of DUSP4 through abnormally overexpressed KDM5B leads to chemoresistance in OVC.
Protein turnover is a dynamic process crucial for maintaining various cellular processes and signaling pathways. The ubiquitin‒proteasome system (UPS) is the primary pathway for selective protein degradation and is composed of E1, E2, and E3 ubiquitin (Ub) ligases, deubiquitinases (DUBs), and the proteasome. Numerous studies have shown that dysregulation of the UPS leads to the development of many diseases, including cancers. , Among the various UPS proteins, F-box and WD repeat domain-containing 7 (FBXW7), which is well known as a tumor suppressor involved in chemoresistance in many cancers, serves as a crucial component of the SKP1-CUL1-F-box (SCF) E3 ligase complex. – In contrast, USP7 is a well-known oncogenic DUB that plays a pivotal role in promoting tumorigenesis and mediating chemoresistance in multiple cancer types. , Although FBXW7 and USP7 are known to regulate cancer progression and treatment resistance, their specific roles in OVC chemoresistance remain poorly understood.
Through this study, we investigated the intricate interplay between KDM5 proteins and cisplatin resistance in OVC. Comparative analyses demonstrated that KDM5B depletion, but not KDM5A depletion, restored cisplatin sensitivity in OVC cells in vitro and in vivo. RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) and chromatin immunoprecipitation sequencing (ChIP-seq) analysis revealed that KDM5B, but not KDM5A, represses DUSP4 gene expression and activates MAPK signaling, increasing cell proliferation and cisplatin resistance. Moreover, depletion of DUSP4 or overexpression of KDM5B induced cisplatin resistance in cisplatin-sensitive OVC cells. We also identified two crucial UPS proteins involved in the chemoresistance of OVC, USP7 and FBXW7, which stabilize and degrade KDM5B, respectively. We also demonstrate for the first time that HIPK1 phosphorylates Thr264 of KDM5B, generating a phosphodegron that promotes recognition by FBXW7. Together, our findings highlight a novel and promising therapeutic strategy for overcoming cisplatin resistance in OVC patients by targeting the KDM5B-DUSP4 axis and its upstream UPS.

## KDM5 protein levels are elevated in cisplatin-resistant OVC
To investigate the role of histone lysine demethylases (KDMs) in OVC chemoresistance and recurrence, we first performed a comprehensive bioinformatics analysis via public databases. TCGA database analysis of 13 different KDMs in recurrent and nonrecurrent OVC revealed that a member of the KDM5 subfamily, particularly KDM5B, was the third most highly expressed KDM in recurrent OVC samples (Fig. ). Given that KDM1A and KDM2A have previously been implicated in cisplatin resistance and tumorigenesis in OVC, , , we focused our investigation on elucidating the role of KDM5 in this context. GEO database (GSE1926) analysis revealed that KDM5B was slightly overexpressed (p = 0.06) in carboplatin-resistant patient-derived OVC cells, whereas KDM5A was significantly downregulated (Supplementary Fig. ). Consistent with these findings, ROC curve analysis demonstrated that only KDM5B expression was significantly higher in nonresponders than in responders to cisplatin treatment (Fig. and Supplementary Fig. ). Indeed, KDM5B was also transcriptionally increased in metastatic (M) OVCs compared with primary tumors (T) and normal ovaries (N), and KDM5B exceeded KDM5A expression in all three groups (Fig. ). KDM5B expression also progressively increased with OVC progression (Supplementary Fig. ) and tended toward higher levels in platinum-resistant OVC samples than in platinum-sensitive samples (mean 2296 vs 1764; p = 0.12; Supplementary Fig. ). In contrast, KDM5A expression levels remained similar between the two groups (mean 1065 vs 1142; p = 0.63; Supplementary Fig. ). Finally, a volcano plot comparing cisplatin-sensitive and cisplatin-resistant A2780 cells (GSE33482), an OVC cancer cell line, revealed that KDM5B is significantly upregulated in resistant cells, whereas KDM5A is not (Fig. ).
 To validate these findings in vitro, we used the cisplatin-sensitive parental SKOV-3 OVC cell line (IC50 = 1.60 μM) and its cisplatin-resistant derivative SKOV-3 CDDP OVC cell line (IC50 = 15.91 μM), which differ in cisplatin sensitivity by approximately tenfold (Fig. and Supplementary Fig. ). Surprisingly, in contrast to the bioinformatics analysis data, both the protein and mRNA levels of KDM5A and KDM5B were increased in the SKOV-3 CDDP cells (Fig. ). Bcl-6 and XIAP are well-known cisplatin resistance markers , that are upregulated in the SKOV-3 CDDP cell line. To determine whether other KDMs could also play a part in cisplatin resistance, we utilized six different KDM inhibitors, namely, GSK-J4 (KDM6A and KDM6B inhibitor), SD70 (KDM4C inhibitor), PBIT (KDM5 inhibitor), GSK-LSD1 (KDM1A inhibitor), IOX1 (2-oxoglutarate oxygenase inhibitor), and JIB-04 (pan-Jumonji inhibitor), and compared the changes in viability between parental SKOV-3 cells and SKOV-3 CDDP cells (Fig. ). Among the several KDM inhibitors tested, both SD70 and PBIT effectively decreased the viability of parental SKOV-3 and SKOV-3 CDDP cells. However, compared with parental SKOV-3 cells, only PBIT significantly reduced the viability of SKOV-3 CDDP cells. The IC50 and GI50 analyses consistently revealed that, compared with parental SKOV-3 cells, SKOV-3 CDDP cells are more sensitive to KDM5 inhibition, with IC50 values of 6.70 μM vs 12.72 μM (Fig. ) and GI50 values of 7.93 μM vs 52.70 μM at 72 h (Supplementary Fig. ). PBIT is known to inhibit both KDM5B (IC50 ≈ 3.00 μM) and KDM5A (IC50 ≈ 6.00 μM). Because both KDM5A and KDM5B are upregulated in cisplatin-resistant cells and PBIT inhibits both proteins, we generated KDM5A and KDM5B double-knockout (DKO) cell lines via the CRISPR-Cas9 system (Fig. ). Compared with control SKOV-3 CDDP cells, SKOV-3 CDDP DKO cells presented increased sensitivity to cisplatin, whereas SKOV-3 DKO cells presented no changes in cisplatin sensitivity compared with the SKOV-3 control cells (Fig. , l, Supplementary Fig. , and Supplementary Table ). These findings suggest that either KDM5A or KDM5B, or both, are critical mediators of cisplatin resistance in OVC cells.
To delineate the changes in the transcriptional landscape associated with acquired cisplatin resistance, we conducted RNA-seq on parental SKOV-3 and SKOV-3 CDDP cells. Given that KDM5A and KDM5B are upregulated in cisplatin-resistant cells and act as transcriptional corepressors, we focused our analysis on the downregulated pathways and biological processes in resistant cells. Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis revealed that among the top 60 downregulated biological process (BP) terms were genes related to histone modification, the cellular response to chemical stress, and MAPK kinase activity (Fig. ). Consistently, KEGG pathway analysis highlighted the marked suppression of MAPK signaling pathway-related genes (Fig. ). Together, these results suggest that KDM5-driven repression of major cellular pathways is involved in the acquisition of OVC cisplatin resistance.

## KDM5B is responsible for cisplatin resistance in OVC
To determine whether cisplatin resistance in OVC arises from dysregulation of KDM5A, KDM5B, or both, we generated KDM5A single-knockout (KO) and KDM5B single-KO OVC cell lines (Fig. ). Surprisingly, only KDM5B-deficient SKOV-3 CDDP cells regained sensitivity to cisplatin (Fig. , Supplementary Fig. , and Supplementary Table ). Because cisplatin induces cytotoxicity via DNA damage-induced apoptosis, we evaluated changes in the expression of apoptotic markers in parental SKOV-3 and SKOV-3 CDDP cells treated with cisplatin. Cisplatin treatment markedly increased apoptosis in KDM5B-depleted SKOV-3 CDDP cells, as shown by increased PARP cleavage and caspase-3 activation, whereas KDM5A depletion had no significant effect (Fig. and Supplementary Fig. ). Notably, parental SKOV-3 cells also presented increased levels of cleaved-PARP and active-caspase 3 after KDM5B depletion (Supplementary Fig. ), but this effect was more evident in SKOV-3 CDDP cells. Annexin-V and propidium iodide (PI) staining apoptosis assays confirmed that only KDM5B depletion increased cisplatin-induced cell death in SKOV-3 CDDP cells (Fig. and Supplementary Fig. ). Next, to determine whether pharmacological inhibition recapitulates the genetic ablation results, we treated cells with PBIT in combination with cisplatin. PBIT and cisplatin synergistically decreased cell viability in both SKOV-3 CDDP and parental SKOV-3 cells, yielding combination index (CI) values below 1.00, but a more pronounced effect was detected in the resistant cell line (Fig. and Supplementary Fig. ). Similarly, the drug combination effectively impaired the colony-forming ability of SKOV-3 CDDP cells but only modestly impaired the colony-forming ability of parental SKOV-3 cells (Fig. ).
 To confirm that this dependency on KDM5B extends beyond the SKOV-3 model, we used another set of cisplatin-sensitive and cisplatin-resistant cell lines, OVCAR-5 and OVCAR-5 CDDP, respectively (Supplementary Fig. ). Consistent with the SKOV-3 model, the OVCAR-5 CDDP cells presented higher KDM5B levels than the parental OVCAR-5 cells did (Fig. ). Depletion of KDM5B in both parental OVCAR-5 and OVCAR-5 CDDP sensitized both cell lines to cisplatin, reducing their IC50 values by 14-fold and 8-fold, respectively (Fig. , Supplementary Fig. , and Supplementary Table ). KDM5B depletion also markedly impaired colony formation in each cell line (Fig. ). Moreover, the OVCAR-5 CDDP cells (IC50 = 5.63 μM) responded more sensitively to PBIT than did the parental OVCAR-5 cells (IC50 = 8.40 μM) (Supplementary Fig. ). Combination treatment with PBIT and cisplatin synergistically reduced cell viability in both cell lines, with a significantly stronger effect on OVCAR-5 CDDP than on parental OVCAR-5 (Fig. and Supplementary Fig. ).
Next, we sought to determine whether ectopic overexpression (OE) of KDM5B alone confers cisplatin resistance in OVC cell lines. We first measured basal KDM5A and KDM5B expression levels across multiple OVC cell lines to identify cell lines with low or high KDM5B levels (Supplementary Fig. ). Expression profiling revealed that parental SKOV-3, OVCAR-3, OVCAR-8, and Caov-3 cells presented low KDM5B levels, whereas the OVCAR-5 cell line presented high KDM5B levels. Accordingly, we generated stable KDM5B OE cell lines in low-KDM5B SKOV-3, OVCAR-3, OVCAR-8, and Caov-3 cells to assess the impact of increased KDM5B expression (Fig. and Supplementary Fig. ). Compared with the control, KDM5B OE in parental SKOV-3 and OVCAR-3 cells increased the IC50 values of cisplatin by fourfold and twofold, respectively (Fig. and Supplementary Table ). Similarly, KDM5B OE in OVCAR-8 and Caov-3 cells also increased the IC50 values of cisplatin by approximately twofold relative to those in control cells (Supplementary Fig. and Supplementary Table ). Taken together, these data suggest that elevated KDM5B may act as the key mediator of cisplatin resistance in OVC cells.
To determine whether endogenous KDM5B protein levels predict innate cisplatin sensitivity, we measured the cisplatin IC50 values of five parental cell lines: a high-KDM5B, p53-null (OVCAR-5) cell line and four low-KDM5B, p53-null or p53-mutant (OVCAR-3, SKOV-3, OVCAR-8, and Caov-3) cell lines (Supplementary Fig. ). As expected, in line with their high KDM5B level, OVCAR-5 cells presented higher cisplatin IC50 values than did the other parental cell lines. We next evaluated PBIT sensitivity in the high-KDM5B group (SKOV-3 CDDP, OVCAR-5, and OVCAR-5 CDDP) and the low-KDM5B group (SKOV-3, OVCAR-3, OVCAR-8, and Caov-3) (Supplementary Fig. ). Here, PBIT sensitivity was positively correlated with KDM5B levels: high-KDM5B cell lines were significantly more susceptible to KDM5 inhibition than low-KDM5B lines were. In addition to SKOV-3 and OVCAR-5 cells, OVCAR-3, OVCAR-8, and Caov-3 cells also presented CI values below 1.00 when treated with PBIT and cisplatin, confirming their synergism (Supplementary Fig. ). Similarly, combining cisplatin with a KDM5B-specific inhibitor, AS8351, and a pan-KDM5 inhibitor, CPI-455, produced synergistic antiproliferative effects (Supplementary Fig. ), underscoring the preclinical potential of targeting KDM5B in cisplatin-resistant OVC.

## The KDM5B-DUSP4 axis confers cisplatin resistance in OVC
To further elucidate the molecular mechanism by which KDM5B contributes to cisplatin resistance, we analyzed RNA-seq data from SKOV-3 and SKOV-3 CDDP cells (Fig. ). KEGG pathway enrichment analysis (Fig. ) revealed that the MAPK signaling cascade was the most significantly transcriptionally repressed pathway in cisplatin-resistant cells. Cisplatin exerts cytotoxic effects by forming DNA adducts that ultimately trigger apoptosis. Because the MAPK pathway mediates key responses to DNA damage, including cisplatin-induced apoptosis, we next aimed to identify the MAPK pathway genes regulated by KDM5B that may contribute to cisplatin resistance (Fig. ). Among the 875 genes downregulated upon resistance, 55 genes were associated with the MAPK signaling cascade. To identify which of these genes are directly controlled by KDM5B, we utilized KDM5 DKO SKOV-3 CDDP cells to eliminate functional compensation that arises from overlapping histone substrates. Among the 10 MAPK pathway genes (ANGPT2, EREG, IL1A, TNF, PTPRR, DUSP4, ANGPT1, AREG, PLA2G4A, DDIT3) with the largest log2FoldChange decreases upon acquisition of resistance (Supplementary Table ), only ANGPT2, EREG, IL1A, and AREG were significantly restored in SKOV-3 CDDP DKO cells compared with SKOV-3 CDDP controls (padj < 0.05; Supplementary Fig. ). None of these genes were significantly altered in the SKOV-3 DKO cells, likely because baseline KDM5 levels are already low in parental cells.
 To validate the RNA-seq results, we performed real-time quantitative PCR (RT‒qPCR) to assess the mRNA levels of the 10 most downregulated MAPK genes in SKOV-3 CDDP cells compared with those in parental SKOV-3 cells (Fig. ). ANGPT2, IL1A, PTPRR, DUSP4, and PLA2G4A were markedly downregulated in SKOV-3 CDDP cells, with approximately 0.0006-, 0.03-, 0.17-, 0.17-, and 0.08-fold reductions in expression levels, respectively. Among the downregulated genes, only PTPRR, DUSP4, and PLA2G4A were restored following KDM5 DKO in SKOV-3 CDDP cells, with 7.5- to 9.9-, 2.3- to 4.2-, and 15.9- to 21.3-fold increases, respectively (Fig. ).
To determine which of the three genes are directly regulated by KDM5B, we next examined their mRNA and protein levels following KDM5B depletion. Although PTPRR and PLA2G4A were markedly reduced in SKOV-3 CDDP cells compared with parental cells, neither gene showed consistent increases in mRNA or protein levels upon KDM5B knockout, suggesting that these genes may not be primary target genes of KDM5B (Supplementary Fig. ). In contrast, DUSP4 was not only downregulated in SKOV-3 CDDP cells but also consistently induced by KDM5B loss (Fig. ). Notably, DUSP4 was the only gene that appeared in both the KEGG and GO gene sets (Fig. ); therefore, we focused our subsequent analyses on DUSP4.
DUSP4 is a well-known tumor suppressor implicated in chemoresistance across multiple cancers, – making it a strong candidate for functional relevance. To clarify whether KDM5A or KDM5B regulates DUSP4, we examined its protein expression in KDM5A- or KDM5B-depleted SKOV-3 and SKOV-3 CDDP cells (Fig. ). Consistent with the RNA-seq results, the DUSP4 protein level was significantly lower in the SKOV-3 CDDP cells than in the parental cells. While simultaneous depletion of KDM5A and KDM5B partially restored DUSP4 levels, KDM5B single-KO alone markedly rescued DUSP4 expression. KDM5A single-KO had little to no effect on either protein or mRNA levels, indicating that KDM5B is the dominant repressor of DUSP4 in this context (Fig. ). Consistent with the role of DUSP4 as a MAPK phosphatase, the upregulation of DUSP4 following KDM5B depletion decreased phospho-p38 and phospho-ERK levels, attenuating MAPK signaling (Fig. ). Furthermore, DUSP4 was increased in the OVCAR-5 KDM5B KO cells and reduced in the Caov-3 KDM5B-OE cells, suggesting that the KDM5B-mediated repression of DUSP4 is consistent across OVC models (Supplementary Figs. and ). Together, these findings suggest that KDM5B represses DUSP4 gene expression as a corepressor to modulate MAPK signaling and promote cisplatin resistance.
To identify the regulatory element through which KDM5B regulates DUSP4 transcription, a promoter tiling assay was performed. Human DUSP4 encodes three alternatively spliced variants (variant 1, variant 2, and variant X1) across two exons, with exon 1 encoding variant 1 and exon 2 encoding variants 2 and X1. Among the three variants, variant 1 is predominant regardless of the tissue type, so we focused on the first promoter upstream of exon 1 of DUSP4 (NM_001394) (Fig. ). Chromatin immunoprecipitation-quantitative PCR (ChIP‒qPCR) revealed that KDM5B specifically binds to region 2 of this promoter, which is between -946 bp and -697 bp upstream of the transcription start site (TSS) (Fig. ). Consistent with its demethylase activity, KDM5B-high SKOV-3 CDDP cells showed reduced H3K4me3 at the DUSP4 promoter compared to KDM5B-low SKOV-3 cells (Fig. ). To validate whether these changes were reflected on a genome-wide scale, we performed ChIP-seq analysis of H3K4me3 in SKOV-3, SKOV-3 CDDP, and SKOV-3 CDDP KDM5B knockout cells. Global TSS-centered profiles (± 1 kb) revealed no widespread shifts in promoter H3K4me3 levels across cell lines (Supplementary Fig. ). However, H3K4me3 levels at the DUSP4 promoter decreased in KDM5B-high SKOV-3 CDDP cells and were restored upon KDM5B depletion, supporting a locus-specific regulatory effect of KDM5B on DUSP4 (Fig. ).
To further assess whether the catalytic activity of KDM5B is required for the regulatory effect of KDM5B on DUSP4 promoter activity, we performed a luciferase reporter assay using KDM5B wild-type (WT) and its catalytically dead mutant (KDM5B H499Y) (Supplementary Fig. ). DUSP4 promoter activity was significantly higher in cells expressing KDM5B H499Y than in those expressing KDM5B WT, which is consistent with the inability of the catalytically dead mutant to remove H3K4me3 from the DUSP4 promoter. In line with these results, DUSP4 promoter activity was lower in SKOV-3 CDDP cells than in parental SKOV-3 cells, whereas KDM5B depletion restored this activity (Supplementary Fig. ). Consistently, DUSP4 protein expression was suppressed by overexpression of KDM5B WT in SKOV-3 cells, whereas KDM5B H499Y overexpression increased it (Supplementary Fig. ). Furthermore, overexpression of KDM5B H499Y (IC50 = 2.90 μM) failed to induce cisplatin resistance and remained close to that of control SKOV-3 cells (IC50 = 1.60 μM) (Supplementary Fig. ), underscoring that the catalytic activity of KDM5B is essential for mediating cisplatin resistance via DUSP4 regulation.
Next, to assess the functional importance of the KDM5B‒DUSP4 axis in cisplatin resistance, DUSP4 was knocked out in KDM5B-depleted SKOV-3 CDDP cells (Fig. ). When KDM5B was depleted from SKOV-3 CDDP cells, sensitivity to cisplatin was restored to a level comparable to that of SKOV-3 parental cells (Fig. ). However, when DUSP4 was depleted in KDM5B-knockout SKOV-3 CDDP cells, the restored sensitivity was lost, and the IC50 values increased to levels comparable to those of SKOV-3 CDDP (Fig. and Supplementary Fig. ). Furthermore, compared with control cells, both the SKOV-3 and SKOV-3 CDDP cell lines without DUSP4 showed increased cisplatin IC50 values, each becoming slightly more resistant to cisplatin (Supplementary Fig. ). These results further support the idea that the KDM5B‒DUSP4 axis plays a significant role in cisplatin resistance.
Moreover, simultaneous knockout of DUSP4 and KDM5B in SKOV-3 CDDP cells suppressed the expression of apoptosis markers after cisplatin treatment, confirming that cisplatin resistance depends on the KDM5B‒DUSP4 axis (Fig. ). Moreover, under prolonged cisplatin exposure, DUSP4/KDM5B DKO cells exhibited a loss of drug sensitivity, resembling the resistant phenotype (Fig. ). Similarly, genotype‒tissue expression (GTEx) multigene analysis revealed that, compared with KDM5B, DUSP4 showed a lower transcript per million (TPM) value in ovarian tissues (Fig. ). Survival analysis of the GSE17260 dataset revealed that OVC patients with low DUSP4 expression had poorer survival outcomes than did those with high DUSP4 expression (Fig. ). Together, these data establish KDM5B-DUSP4-MAPK signaling as a crucial contributor to cisplatin resistance in OVC and confirm that DUSP4 is a potential therapeutic target for overcoming chemoresistance.

## The USP7‒KDM5B axis confers resistance to cisplatin in OVC
Because KDM5B protein levels are elevated in cisplatin-resistant cells, we investigated whether posttranslational regulation contributes to KDM5B accumulation by identifying the upstream DUB that stabilizes KDM5B. Accordingly, we screened a panel of DUB inhibitors in SKOV-3 OVC cells. Surprisingly, the inhibition of USP7 with P5091 and PR-619 caused a dramatic, dose-dependent reduction in KDM5B protein levels (Supplementary Fig. ). Treatment of SKOV-3 cells with increasing concentrations of P5091 elicited a dose-dependent reduction in both KDM5A and KDM5B protein levels (Supplementary Fig. ). However, co-IP revealed that only KDM5B, not KDM5A, directly interacts with USP7 (Fig. and Supplementary Fig. ). KDM5B and USP7 also interact endogenously in SKOV-3 CDDP cells (Supplementary Fig. ). Immunofluorescence (IF) further confirmed the colocalization of USP7 and KDM5B under both exogenous and endogenous conditions (Supplementary Fig. ). Given that only KDM5B physically associates with USP7 despite parallel decreases in KDM5A levels, subsequent analyses were performed on the USP7‒KDM5B axis.
 To further understand the molecular mechanisms involving USP7 and KDM5B, we next established USP7 KO cell lines in SKOV-3 and SKOV-3 CDDP cells (Supplementary Fig. ). Consistent with the co-IP and IF data, USP7 depletion selectively reduced the levels of KDM5B, but not those of KDM5A, in both cell lines. Furthermore, re-expression of USP7 in USP7 KO OVC cells restored KDM5B protein levels (Supplementary Fig. ). Treatment of USP7-depleted cells with the proteasome inhibitor MG132 fully rescued KDM5B expression (Fig. ), indicating that USP7 stabilizes KDM5B levels via the proteasome-mediated pathway. To assess KDM5B stability directly, we performed cycloheximide (CHX) chase assays in control vs USP7 KO cells (Fig. ). Compared with control cells, USP7-depleted cells presented a significantly shorter KDM5B half-life, and their baseline KDM5B levels were lower even before CHX treatment at 0 h. Together, these results confirm that USP7 is required for KDM5B protein stability via the proteasome-mediated pathway.
To further verify that USP7 is the specific DUB for KDM5B, we performed in vivo ubiquitination assays under multiple conditions. Compared with control cells, USP7-depleted cells showed significantly higher KDM5B ubiquitination (Supplementary Fig. ). To determine which ubiquitin linkage USP7 removes, USP7 KO cells were incubated with excess K48-linked ubiquitin or K63-linked ubiquitin, and changes in KDM5B ubiquitination levels were assessed. In both the SKOV-3 and SKOV-3 CDDP cell lines, the loss of USP7 led to a clear increase in K48-linked ubiquitination of KDM5B, whereas K63-linked ubiquitination was unchanged (Fig. and Supplementary Fig. ). In line with this result, OE of USP7 in HEK293T cells led to a significant decrease in K48-linked ubiquitin on KDM5B, whereas K63-linked ubiquitination remained unchanged or slightly reduced (Fig. ). Moreover, the amount of K48-linked ubiquitin on the KDM5B protein increased when the catalytically mutated USP7 (USP7 C233S) was ectopically introduced (Supplementary Fig. ).
To identify the ubiquitination site on KDM5B that mediates its proteasomal degradation, we retrieved eight predicted lysine (K) residues from PhosphoSitePlus (Fig. ) and mutated each residue to arginine (R) to block ubiquitination. Among these, four K-to-R mutants, K100R, K956R, K1011R, and K1276R, presented prolonged half-lives in SKOV-3 cells compared with WT KDM5B (Fig. and Supplementary Fig. ), whereas the remaining mutants presented no significant changes (Supplementary Fig. ). To determine whether any one of these lysine sites is more critical for proteasomal targeting, we generated a 4KR mutant (K100R, K956R, K1011R, and K1276R), which was more stable than WT KDM5B (Fig. ). However, comparisons of the degree of ubiquitination across each single K to R mutant and the 4KR mutant revealed no significant differences, indicating that neither any individual lysine nor their combination predominates in directing the proteasomal degradation of KDM5B (Fig. and Supplementary Fig. ).
Finally, to understand the relationship between the USP7‒KDM5B axis and cisplatin resistance, KDM5B was ectopically introduced into USP7 KO SKOV-3 and SKOV-3 CDDP cells (Supplementary Fig. ). The evaluation of cell viability in these cell lines revealed that USP7 KO in SKOV-3 CDDP cells sharply reduced the IC50 values from 19.49 μM to 5.23 μM, which was concomitant with the loss of KDM5B (Fig. and Supplementary Fig. ). Remarkably, re-expression of KDM5B in USP7-deficient SKOV-3 CDDP cells fully restored the IC50 values (>100 μM), despite the absence of USP7. In contrast, USP7 depletion alone in SKOV-3 parental cells had only a minimal effect on cisplatin sensitivity, lowering the IC50 from 1.70 μM to 1.04 μM. However, KDM5B overexpression in USP7-depleted SKOV-3 parental cells elicited an increase in the IC50 from 1.70 μM to 3.35 μM, demonstrating that KDM5B is both necessary and sufficient to confer cisplatin resistance even in the absence of USP7. The combination of P5901 and cisplatin synergistically increased the sensitivity of SKOV-3 CDDP cells to cisplatin (Fig. and Supplementary Fig. ), whereas this synergistic effect was much weaker in SKOV-3 parental cells (Supplementary Fig. ). Spearman’s correlation analysis of the TCGA cohort revealed a positive relationship between USP7 and KDM5B expression (Supplementary Fig. ). In line with these findings, Kaplan–Meier analysis of the GSE14764 dataset demonstrated that OVC patients with higher levels of USP7 had a lower survival probability (Supplementary Fig. ). Taken together, these results underscore USP7 as a pivotal upstream regulator of KDM5B in driving cisplatin resistance in OVC.

## The SCFFBXW7 E3 ligase complex facilitates KDM5B degradation via HIPK1-mediated phosphorylation
To our knowledge, no E3 ligase complex has previously been shown to target KDM5B for degradation. Here, we demonstrate for the first time that a Cullin-RING E3 ubiquitin ligase (CRL) complex ubiquitinates and regulates KDM5B protein turnover. To identify which CRL is responsible, we performed a co-IP assay after coexpressing Flag-tagged KDM5B with six distinct Myc-tagged Cullin (CUL) proteins (Fig. ). Among the six CRL complexes, KDM5B bound the most strongly to CRLs containing CUL1 and CUL7. To determine which substrate adapters mediate these interactions, we overexpressed seven different FBXW proteins known to recruit substrates to CRLs (Fig. ). Co-IP assays demonstrated that KDM5B binds the most strongly to FBXW7, a substrate that recognizes the adapter protein of the SCF E3 ligase (Fig. and Supplementary Fig. ). FBXW7 selectively recognizes phosphorylated degron motifs with the consensus L/I-L/I/P‒T-P-XX-S/E. Using conserved-domain analysis and PhosphoSitePlus, we identified the degron motif in KDM5B (residues 262–268) and predicted threonine (T) 264 within it to be a phosphorylation site (Fig. ). To validate whether phosphorylation at T264 of KDM5B facilitates its interaction with FBXW7, we generated phospho-dead (T264A) and phospho-mimetic (T264D) KDM5B mutants and performed a co-IP assay (Supplementary Fig. ) and an in vivo ubiquitination assay (Fig. ). Compared with WT, the T264A mutation weakened the interaction between FBXW7 and KDM5B and reduced KDM5B ubiquitination, whereas the T264D mutation increased their interaction and subsequently increased KDM5B ubiquitination. Together, these data demonstrate that FBXW7, a substrate recognition component of the SCFFBXW7 ubiquitin ligase, interacts with and targets KDM5B for polyubiquitination and degradation.
 To further confirm that FBXW7 is responsible for CUL1 E3 ligase-mediated protein degradation of KDM5B, we generated FBXW7-depleted OVC cells (Fig. ). Depletion of FBXW7 led to a clear increase in KDM5B in SKOV-3 parental cells, which presented low KDM5B levels. In contrast, SKOV-3 CDDP cells showed a less pronounced change upon FBXW7 loss, likely because their high basal KDM5B levels reached a saturation point upon its loss. Cyclin E2 was detected as a positive control to validate the FBXW7 depletion efficiency. CHX chase assays confirmed that FBXW7 depletion dramatically stabilized KDM5B in both cell lines (Fig. ). Ectopic FBXW7 expression in HEK293T cells increased K48-linked ubiquitin on KDM5B (Fig. and Supplementary Fig. ), whereas K63-linked ubiquitination remained unchanged (Fig. ), which is consistent with the proteasome-mediated turnover mechanism of the UPS. Additionally, the polyubiquitination of T264D KDM5B was readily detected via an in vitro ubiquitination assay in the presence of the E1, E2, and SCFFBXW7 complexes (Fig. and Supplementary Fig. ). Together, these results indicate that FBXW7 targets KDM5B for subsequent K48-linked degradation via the UPS.
We next focused on defining which kinase may be responsible for KDM5B phosphorylation at T264 within the FBXW7 binding motif. We utilized the kinase prediction tool in PhosphoSitePlus and identified the top ten candidate kinases that phosphorylate KDM5B on T264 (Supplementary Table ). To define the true kinase, we overexpressed the top two candidates, homeodomain-interacting protein kinase (HIPK1) and dual-specificity tyrosine phosphorylation-regulated kinase 1B (DYRK1B), in SKOV-3 OVC cells (Fig. and Supplementary Fig. ). The results revealed that only HIPK1 overexpression decreased KDM5B protein stability, and only HIPK1 directly interacted with KDM5B (Fig. and Supplementary Fig. ). Consistently, ectopic expression of HIPK1 led to a dramatic decrease in the FBXW7–KDM5B interaction, whereas the KDM5B T264A mutation reduced this effect (Fig. ). In addition to HIPK1 and DYRK1B, we tested whether GSK3α and GSK3β could also phosphorylate KDM5B, given their known roles in phosphorylating multiple FBXW7 substrates. – However, treatment with the GSK3-specific inhibitor CHIR-99201 (Laduviglusib) did not significantly alter KDM5B levels (Supplementary Fig. ). The overexpression of neither GSK3α nor GSK3β subsequently induced notable changes in KDM5B stability (Supplementary Fig. ), further supporting the role of HIPK1 as the specific kinase for FBXW7-mediated KDM5B protein degradation. Consistently, Spearman’s correlation analysis revealed a positive relationship between HIPK1 and FBXW7 (Supplementary Fig. ). To evaluate the clinical relevance of this axis, we analyzed the survival rates of high-FBXW7- and high-HIPK1-expressing OVC patients in the GSE64885 and GSE9391 datasets, respectively (Supplementary Fig. ). Notably, both groups of patients had better prognoses than those with low FBXW7 and low HIPK1. Complementary GTEx analysis demonstrated that the TPM value of FBXW7 is lower than that of KDM5B in ovarian tissues, further corroborating the regulatory role of FBXW7 over KDM5B (Fig. ). Taken together, these results indicate that HIPK1 phosphorylates KDM5B at the T264 of the FBXW7 binding motif, facilitating KDM5B–FBXW7 recognition and leading to subsequent proteasomal degradation.

## KDM5B depletion restores cisplatin sensitivity in vivo
To confirm whether abnormal expression of KDM5B is crucial for cisplatin resistance, we generated xenograft and orthotopic mouse models using the SKOV-3 parental and SKOV-3 CDDP cell lines (Fig. ). For xenograft models, KDM5B-depleted SKOV-3 parental and CDDP-resistant cells were injected subcutaneously into the flanks of nude mice. When the tumors reached a size of 150 mm3, we administered saline or 5 mg/kg cisplatin every 3 to 4 days. The tumor volume and weight of KDM5B-depleted SKOV-3 parental cells treated with cisplatin (Group D’) were slightly lower ( ~ 30% and ~40%, respectively) than those of control cells treated with cisplatin (Group C’), indicating modestly increased cisplatin sensitivity (Supplementary Fig. ). In contrast, KDM5B depletion in the SKOV-3 CDDP xenografts dramatically decreased the final tumor volume by 66% and the tumor mass by ~73% (Group C vs D) (Fig. ). Depletion of DUSP4 in these KDM5B-deficient, SKOV-3 CDDP tumors partially restored cisplatin resistance, with tumor metrics returning toward untreated levels (groups E and F). No changes in body weight were observed over time (Fig. ).
 To mimic ovarian tumor dissemination, we injected the SKOV-3-overexpressing luciferase (Luc) cell line, SKOV-3 CDDP Luc, and their knockout derivatives into the peritoneal cavity of nude mice (Fig. , Supplementary Figs. and ). Bioluminescent imaging over three weeks confirmed that, compared with saline (Group C), cisplatin suppressed the tumor burden most effectively in the KDM5B-knockout SKOV-3 CDDP mice (Group D), with the signal flux being reduced by ~80% (Fig. ). No changes in body weight were observed over time (Fig. ). Compared with control mice treated with cisplatin (Group C’), parental KDM5B-knockout mice treated with cisplatin (Group D’) also presented decreased tumor growth but to a lesser extent (Supplementary Figs. and ). Codepletion of DUSP4 in KDM5B-knockout SKOV-3 CDDP cells again slightly restored cisplatin resistance (Supplementary Figs. and ). Immunohistochemistry (IHC) analysis further confirmed that cisplatin markedly reduced Ki-67 staining in KDM5B-deficient xenografts (Fig. ). We also showed that KDM5B depletion elevates DUSP4 expression in tissues (Fig. ), demonstrating that targeting the KDM5B–DUSP4 axis effectively decreases cisplatin resistance in OVC in vivo, which is consistent with our in vitro findings.
Finally, to further evaluate the in vivo therapeutic potential of targeting KDM5B to overcome cisplatin resistance, we inhibited its upstream regulator, USP7, via the USP7 inhibitor P5091. Compared with vehicle or either agent alone, coadministration of P5091 (10 mg/kg) and cisplatin (2.5 mg/kg) in SKOV-3 CDDP xenografts markedly reduced tumor volume and weight (Supplementary Fig. ). No changes in body weight were observed over time (Supplementary Fig. ). Together, these findings highlight that targeting both upstream regulators and downstream effectors of KDM5B is a promising strategy for overcoming cisplatin resistance in OVC.

## USP7- and FBXW7-mediated dysregulation of the KDM5B–DUSP4 axis leads to poorer prognosis in OVC patients
We next sought to evaluate the clinical implications of the ubiquitin-dependent regulation of the KDM5B–DUSP4 axis. To address this, we first examined complex formation among USP7, FBXW7, and KDM5B in cells. We subjected SKOV-3 CDDP-containing cell lysates to size-exclusion chromatography (SEC) and probed each fraction via Western blotting (Fig. ). Endogenous KDM5B and USP7 coeluted in high-molecular-weight fractions 19–21, whereas KDM5B and FBXW7 coeluted in slightly later fractions 21–23, which is consistent with their assembly into discrete protein complexes. Notably, genetic ablation of KDM5B caused both USP7 (fractions 20–23) and FBXW7 (fractions 24–25) to shift toward lower-molecular-weight fractions, confirming their interaction with KDM5B in OVC cells. We recapitulated these findings via the use of purified proteins in vitro. When KDM5B and USP7 were mixed together, the elution peak shifted leftward relative to that of USP7 alone, indicating the formation of a larger complex (Fig. and Supplementary Fig. ). Together, these data confirm that KDM5B is associated with USP7 and FBXW7 in OVC cells.
 Next, we aimed to examine the clinical relevance of KDM5B, USP7, DUSP4, and FBXW7 in OVC tumorigenesis and cisplatin resistance. We first surveyed KDM5B expression in patient tissues via the Human Protein Atlas. KDM5B staining was markedly greater in ovarian carcinoma tissues than in normal ovarian epithelium samples (Fig. ). Tissue microarray (TMA) analysis of OVC patient samples revealed that KDM5B expression increased with increasing tumor stage, with the highest levels observed in Stage III samples (Fig. and Supplementary Fig. ). When stratified by cancer stage, Stage I-II tumors presented low KDM5B and high DUSP4 expression, whereas Stage III tumors presented the opposite pattern, with high KDM5B and low DUSP4 expression. These trends were significant in both subgroups (Wilcoxon matched-pairs test: Stages I–II, p = 0.002; Stage III, p = 0.004) (Fig. ). However, KDM5B and DUSP4 did not significantly correlate across all stages (Supplementary Fig. ), suggesting that KDM5B-dependent suppression of DUSP4 is more relevant to chemoresistance than to tumorigenesis. On the other hand, KDM5B levels strongly correlated with USP7 across all stages (Fig. ), suggesting that USP7 regulates KDM5B regardless of tumor stage (Supplementary Fig. ). Additionally, analysis of pharmacogenomic profiles from CellMinerCDB demonstrated that KDM5B and USP7 expression levels were increased across a panel of OVC cell lines with low cisplatin drug activity, whereas DUSP4 and FBXW7 levels were decreased in these cells (Fig. ). The same expression patterns were observed in recurrent OVC patient tumor data obtained from the TCGA database (Supplementary Fig. ) and in cisplatin-resistant cell lines from the GSE148003 dataset (Supplementary Fig. ). Finally, we assessed the prognostic value of KDM5B, USP7, and DUSP4 in platinum-treated OVC patients via Kaplan‒Meier plotter. K‒M survival analyses revealed that patients with high KDM5B or USP7 expression presented shorter survival outcomes (Fig. ), whereas those with low levels of DUSP4 presented improved survival outcomes (Fig. ). In contrast, KDM5A and FBXW7 did not significantly differ between the high- and low-expression groups (Supplementary Fig. ). When patients were stratified by combined expression patterns, the group with high KDM5B and low DUSP4 expression presented shorter PFS and OS than did the remaining group (Fig. ). KDM5A showed no apparent association with DUSP4 levels in terms of clinical outcomes (Supplementary Fig. ). Collectively, these data implicate the USP7-/FBXW7-KDM5B-DUSP4 axis not only in cisplatin resistance but also as a potential therapeutic target across drug-resistant OVC.

## Discussion
Drug resistance in OVC is mediated by diverse, evolving epigenetic alterations that remain incompletely characterized. For example, aberrant overexpression of histone deacetylases has been implicated in cisplatin resistance in ovarian tumors, and modifications of linker histone H1.4 that alter chromatin accessibility confer resistance to PARP inhibitors in OVC models. Moreover, KDM5B overexpression has been linked to endocrine therapy resistance in breast cancer and chemoresistance in gastric cancer via XRCC1, underscoring the broader role of epigenetic dysregulation in the drug response. In the present study, we extend these observations by demonstrating that KDM5B, when dysregulated by USP7 and FBXW7, suppresses DUSP4 to establish and maintain cisplatin resistance in OVC (Fig. ).
 Here, we comprehensively characterized and evaluated the role of KDM5 proteins in cisplatin resistance by utilizing KDM5A/KDM5B DKO cell lines and single-KO cell lines. Direct comparison of KDM5A- and KDM5B-KO cells revealed that loss of KDM5B, but not of KDM5A, restored cisplatin sensitivity in resistant OVC models. Mechanistically, our genome-wide and locus-specific analyses demonstrated that KDM5B represses DUSP4 by removing the activating H3K4me3 mark at its promoter. RNA-seq of KDM5 DKO SKOV-3 CDDP cells revealed a 2.2-fold increase in the number of DUSP4 transcripts (log2FoldChange = 1.14; padj = 0.44; Supplementary Table ). Although this change did not pass genome-wide multiple-testing correction, together with the robust rescue of the DUSP4 protein (Fig. ) and the expected reduction in phospho-ERK/p38 (Fig. ), these data suggest that DUSP4 is a bona-fide transcriptional target repressed by KDM5B in cisplatin-resistant cells. Moreover, consistent with the well-established tumor-suppressive functions and role of DUSP4 in drug resistance across multiple cancers, – , we found that DUSP4 deletion in KDM5B-deficient cells restored cisplatin resistance both in vitro and in vivo, confirming that DUSP4 mediates KDM5B-induced cisplatin resistance in OVC. On the basis of our findings concerning the KDM5B‒DUSP4 axis, we next asked whether KDM5A plays a comparable role. In contrast to KDM5B, KDM5A neither induced cisplatin resistance nor altered DUSP4 expression. These data align with studies demonstrating that KDM5A and KDM5B play distinct roles in biological processes and gene regulation across different cellular contexts. Thus, despite their enzymatic similarity, KDM5A and KDM5B play nonredundant roles in gene regulation, and the specific recruitment of KDM5B to the DUSP4 locus underlies its unique contribution to cisplatin resistance in OVC.
The UPS is crucial for proper regulation of protein turnover, and its dysregulation contributes to various diseases, including cancer. – Recent work has shown that distinct DUBs drive tumorigenesis and drug resistance across multiple cancers. , In particular, USP38 and USP7 have been reported to stabilize KDM5B under inflammatory conditions and in head and neck cancer, respectively. , Consistent with these reports, we demonstrate here that USP7-mediated deubiquitination of KDM5B is also critical for cisplatin resistance in OVC. Notably, we identified four evolutionarily conserved lysine sites (K100, K956, K1011, and K1276) that are modified with K48-linked ubiquitin chains to target KDM5B for proteasomal degradation. Moreover, we identified the SCFFBXW7 E3 ubiquitin ligase complex as another regulator of KDM5B stability, defining a novel regulatory axis. We further showed that HIPK1-mediated phosphorylation at Thr264 generates the phosphodegron required for FBXW7 recognition and subsequent proteasomal turnover. Interestingly, the FBXW7 KO model did not clearly correlate with cisplatin sensitivity in our ovarian tumor models (Supplementary Fig. ), likely reflecting the interconnected feedback between KDM5B and FBXW7. A previous study showed that KDM5B epigenetically regulates FBXW7 expression by removing H3K4me3 from its promoter. Taken together, these data suggest a feedback loop in which KDM5B suppresses FBXW7 expression, thereby limiting FBXW7-mediated ubiquitination and turnover of KDM5B. In cisplatin-resistant cells, elevated KDM5B suppresses FBXW7 expression, thereby reducing KDM5B turnover and increasing its accumulation. Conversely, in cisplatin-sensitive cells, active FBXW7 keeps KDM5B levels low and thereby sustains its own expression. This regulatory loop may contribute to the stability of KDM5B in a chemoresistant state and allow OVC cells to adapt to drug exposure. Therapeutic approaches that disrupt this loop to increase FBXW7 activity or inhibit HIPK1-dependent phosphorylation may disrupt KDM5B stabilization and offer therapeutic avenues to overcome chemoresistance. Further studies are needed to clarify the molecular dynamics of this regulatory mechanism and validate its impact on the platinum response.
Our study underscores the critical role of KDM5B in cisplatin-resistant OVCs, but the high degree of structural conservation of the JmjC catalytic domain across lysine demethylases makes direct inhibition challenging. For example, inhibitors such as PBIT, a widely used KDM5 inhibitor, can affect both KDM5B and KDM5A in a dose-dependent manner. Recent advances in structural biology may facilitate the development of specific inhibitors that selectively disrupt the interactions between KDM5B and its binding partners. Our in vitro data demonstrate that USP7 and FBXW7 interact with KDM5B, laying the groundwork for future structural studies and aiding the development of inhibitors that disrupt these protein‒protein interactions.
In addition to targeting protein‒protein interactions, targeting the upstream or downstream targets of KDM5B may serve as an alternative therapeutic strategy. One promising strategy is to target USP7, a well-characterized DUB with established roles in tumorigenesis and chemoresistance. – Here, we show that USP7 depletion decreases KDM5B levels and restores cisplatin sensitivity and that combining the USP7 inhibitor P5091 with cisplatin produces a synergistic antiproliferative effect both in vitro and in vivo. Similar findings in head and neck cancer patients further support this approach. Targeting the FBXW7–KDM5B axis may constitute another therapeutic strategy. Given that FBXW7 promotes proteasomal degradation as a SCFFBXW7 E3 ligase complex, proteolysis-targeting chimera (PROTAC)-based approaches could be leveraged to increase targeted degradation of KDM5B. Taken together, our findings suggest that targeting the KDM5B regulatory network may help overcome cisplatin resistance and improve the therapeutic outcomes of patients with OVC and potentially other cancers.
Despite the strengths of our study, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, our data rely primarily on established OVC cell lines, which, although widely used for mechanistic studies, may not fully recapitulate the genomic diversity and microenvironmental complexity of patient tumors. Although we validated key findings via the use of chemoresistant xenografts and orthotopic mouse models, the translational relevance of these therapeutic strategies could be further enhanced by incorporating primary cells, patient-derived organoids, or patient-derived xenograft models. Second, our investigation focused on a single downstream gene of KDM5B, DUSP4, despite the likelihood that other KDM5B-regulated genes may also contribute to the chemoresistant phenotype. Future studies are necessary to identify these additional targets and delineate their roles in KDM5B-mediated chemoresistance.
In conclusion, our data provide novel mechanistic insight into the USP7- and FBXW7/HIPK1-mediated KDM5B–DUSP4 axes and reveal key vulnerabilities driving cisplatin resistance in OVC. This study not only suggests novel biomarkers to predict therapeutic outcomes in chemoresistant patients but also offers a novel therapeutic strategy for overcoming drug resistance in OVC and other malignancies.

## Cell lines
Cisplatin-resistant human OVC cell lines and their parental cell lines (SKOV-3, SKOV-3 CDDP, OVCAR-5, OVCAR-5 CDDP) were gifts from Dr. Oliver Dorigo, Stanford University. SKOV-3, SKOV-3 CDDP, OVCAR-5, and OVCAR-5 CDDP cells were cultured in RPMI-1640 medium supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin‒streptomycin. These cells were cultured according to the manufacturer’s instructions. All the cells were incubated in humidified chambers at 37 °C with 5% CO2.

## Cell lysis and Western blot (immunoblotting)
To obtain whole protein lysates, the cell culture medium was removed completely, and the cells were washed twice with ice-cold PBS. After cell harvest, the cells were lysed in ice-cold NP-40 lysis buffer [50 mM HEPES (pH 7.4), 150 mM NaCl, 1% (v/v) NP-40, 1 mM EDTA, 25 mM glycerol phosphate, and protease inhibitors] and centrifuged at 14,000 rpm at 4 °C for 10 min. The protein concentration was measured via the Bradford assay (Biosesang, Yongin-si, Korea). A total of 30 μg to 80 μg of protein was separated by SDS‒PAGE and transferred to a 0.45 μm nitrocellulose membrane (10600002, Cytiva, Marlborough, MA, USA) via a Mini Trans-Blot Cell or Trans-BlotR Turbo Blotting System (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA). The membranes were blocked in 0.05% PBS-T containing 5% skim milk and incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies under constant agitation. Subsequently, anti-mouse or anti-rabbit HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (Jackson ImmunoResearch, West Grove, PA) were applied for 3 h at RT under constant agitation. The membranes were washed with 0.05% PBS-T, and chemiluminescent signals were detected via chemiluminescence substrates (Thermo Fisher, Waltham, MA, USA) acquired with Fusion Solo S (Vilber, Seoul, Korea). Proteins were quantified with loading controls (α-tubulin or GAPDH) via ImageJ software (Bethesda, Maryland, USA). All the antibodies used in this research can be found in the Supplementary Materials.

## Cell viability
Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) assays were used to measure cell viability. The cells were seeded in 96-well plates at a density of 3000 cells per well in 200 μl of complete growth medium and incubated overnight at 37 °C with 5% CO2 before being treated with drugs at the indicated concentrations. After drug treatment, the cells were incubated again for 24 h, 48 h, or 72 h and treated with CCK-8 reagent (Dojindo Molecular Technologies Inc., Kumamoto, Japan) for another 3 h. Absorbance was measured via an Infinite 200 PRO Microplate Reader (TECAN, Mannedorf, Switzerland) at 450 nm. The cell viability (%) was calculated by dividing the absorbance of the treated cells by the absorbance of the control cells. Dose‒response curves were plotted via a nonlinear regression model, and the GI50 and IC50 values were determined from the fitted curves. The reported IC50 values are derived from experiments that were performed in at least 3 independent assays, each containing 3 to 5 replicates. Dose-by-dose comparisons of cell viability in dose‒response assays using a single drug between two or more cell lines were conducted via one-way ANOVA with appropriate multiple comparison corrections (Dunnett’s or Tukey’s post hoc tests). Dose-by-dose comparisons of dose‒response assays using two or more drugs were conducted via two-way ANOVA with Bonferroni’s or Tukey’s post hoc test for multiple comparisons correction. All the statistical analyses were performed via GraphPad Prism 8 (GraphPad Software, Boston, MA, USA).

## Chromatin immunoprecipitation quantitative PCR (ChIP‒qPCR) and ChIP‒seq
Prior to cell lysis, the cells were crosslinked with 1% formaldehyde for 7 min at 37 °C to preserve protein‒DNA complexes. The cells were lysed, and the chromatin was sheared to an average size of 200 bp to 500 bp via sonication for 5 min at 20% amplitude. Immunoprecipitation was performed with the following indicated antibodies: KDM5B (ab181089, Abcam, 1:50) and H3K4me3 (ABE305, Millipore, 1:50). A rabbit IgG antibody (A01008, GenScript, 1:1000) was used as a negative control.
ChIP‒qPCR was performed via TOPreal™ SYBR Green qPCR PreMIX (RT500M, Enzynomics, Daejeon, Korea) according to the manufacturer’s protocol on an Applied Biosystems 7500 System (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA, USA). Input (1%) controls were used for all ChIP‒qPCR calculations. The ChIP‒qPCR primers designed according to the promoter sequences of the genes of interest can be found in Supplementary Table . All the experiments were performed in biological triplicate.
For ChIP-seq, both ChIP and input (1%) DNAs were amplified and subjected to DNA library construction via an Illumina DNA Prep Kit (Illumina Inc., San Diego, CA, USA) following the manufacturer’s instructions. The libraries were quantified via KAPA Library Quantification kits for Illumina Sequencing platforms according to the qPCR Quantification Protocol Guide (#KK4854, KAPA BIOSYSTEMS) and quantified via D5000 Screen Tape (Agilent Technologies, Waldbronn, Germany). Indexed libraries were then submitted to an Illumina NovaSeqX (Illumina, Inc., San Diego, CA, USA), and paired-end (151 × 2 bp) sequencing was performed by EGSbio Incorporated (Hwaseong-si, Gyeongi-do, Korea).
ChIP-Seq data processing entailed the trimming of adapter sequences from both ends (10 bp) via Trim Galore (version 0.6.7). Subsequent alignment of the trimmed reads to the human reference genome GRCh38 was achieved via bwa (version 0.7.18). Only mappings with a quality score above 10 were retained and organized via SAMtools (version 1.15.1), after which duplicates were removed via the Picard toolkit (MarkDuplicates version 3.2.0-1). Peaks calling for H3K4me3 were conducted with MACS3 (version 3.0.3), excluding regions within ENCODE blacklist areas. Peaks were annotated with ChIPseeker (version 1.38.0) and visualized via the deepTools Toolkit (version 3.5.5). Promoter regions were identified as H3K4me3 peaks located within ±1 kilobase (kb) of any TSS, as defined in ChIPpeakAnno (version 3.36.1).

## Colony formation assay
The cells were seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 1000 cells per well. If drug treatment was needed, it was performed the day after the cells were seeded. The cells were incubated at 37 °C with 5% CO2 for 10 days and stained with crystal violet staining solution [1% (v/v) paraformaldehyde, 0.05% (v/v) crystal violet, and 1% (v/v) methanol] for 20 min. For quantification, the number of grown colonies was counted and divided by the control to obtain the relative colony count. All the experiments were performed in biological triplicate.

## Lentivirus production
To establish stable cell lines, lentivirus particles were used. To harvest lentivirus particles, HEK293T cells were seeded and cotransfected with the lentiviral plasmid pMD2.G and psPAX2, using Lipofectamine 2000 (Thermo Fisher, Waltham, MA, USA) transfection reagent. The culture medium was collected after 48 h of transfection. The collected virus particles were stored at -80 °C until use. For knockout cell lines, lentiviruses carrying sgRNAs (cloned and inserted into the lentiCRISPRv2 vector with puromycin or blasticidin selection markers) against KDM5B (gKDM5B), KDM5A (gKDM5A), USP7 (gUSP7), FBXW7 (gFBXW7), and DUSP4 (gDUSP4) were harvested. The sgRNA sequences used to target each protein can be found in Supplementary Table . For the overexpression cell lines, lentiviruses carrying pLVX-EIBla-KDM5B, pLVX-EIBla-USP7, and pLVX-EIBla-DUSP4 were cloned and harvested.

## Establishment of stable knockout and overexpression cell lines
Cancer cells were seeded at a density of 1 × 105 cells/well in 6-well plates and transduced with the harvested lentivirus 1–3 times. Then, successfully transduced cells were selected via the appropriate selection drugs, 2 μg/ml puromycin (Enzo Life Sciences Inc., Farmingdale, NY, USA) or 5 μg/ml blasticidin (Biomax, Guri-si, Korea), for 3–5 days. After selection, knockout or overexpression efficiency was confirmed by Western blot analyses for at least three subsequent passages to verify target protein expression. All established stable knockout and overexpression cell lines were maintained under continuous antibiotic selection (0.5 μg/ml puromycin or 0.5 μg/ml blasticidin) and used within 10 passages after validation. Protein expression levels were routinely validated by Western blotting before each experiment.

## Expression and purification of human KDM5B
Full-length human KDM5B (WT) and the KDM5B phospho-mimic mutant (T264D) were cloned and inserted into the Bac-to-Bac baculovirus expression system. Recombinant baculoviruses were generated and used to infect Spodoptera frugiperda (Sf9) insect cells at a density of 3 × 10⁶ cells/ml. Infected cells were cultured at 27 °C for 60‒72 h and harvested by centrifugation at 2500×g for 10 min. Cell pellets were resuspended in lysis buffer containing 20 mM HEPES pH 7.5, 100 mM NaCl, 5 mM β-mercaptoethanol, and protease inhibitor cocktail (500 μM AEBSF, 1 μM E-64, 1 μM leupeptin, and 0.15 μM aprotinin). The cells were lysed via a Dounce homogenizer, and the lysate was subsequently centrifuged at 15,000 rpm for 1 h at 4 °C to remove debris.
After centrifugation, the supernatant was incubated with His60 Ni Superflow Resin (Takara Bio) at 4 °C with gentle rotation for 3 h to allow binding. The resin was collected via low-speed centrifugation and washed with buffer containing 20 mM HEPES (pH 7.5), 100 mM NaCl, 40 mM imidazole, and 5 mM β-mercaptoethanol. Bound proteins were eluted with buffer containing 20 mM HEPES (pH 7.5), 150 mM NaCl, 400 mM imidazole, and 5 mM β-mercaptoethanol. The eluate was desalted via a PD-10 column (GE Healthcare) into 20 mM HEPES (pH 7.5) and 100 mM NaCl and used immediately for subsequent experiments.

## Expression and purification of FBXW7–SKP1 and CUL1–RBX1 complexes
Human FBXW7 and SKP1 constructs were designed on the basis of the sequences reported by Welcker et al. and cloned and inserted into a pETDuet vector for coexpression in E. coli BL21(DE3) CodonPlus cells. Similarly, Cullin1 (CUL1) and RBX1 constructs were designed according to Cao et al. and cloned and inserted into a pRSF-Duet vector for coexpression in E. coli BL21(DE3) CodonPlus. Cultures were grown at 37 °C to an O.D. of 0.6 and induced with 0.5 mM IPTG, followed by overnight incubation at 25 °C. The cells were harvested via centrifugation and lysed via sonication in lysis buffer containing 20 mM Tris-HCl (pH 8.0), 200 mM NaCl, 5 mM β-mercaptoethanol, and protease inhibitors.
The supernatant was incubated with His60 Ni Superflow Resin (Takara Bio) at 4 °C for 3 h, followed by washing with buffer containing 20 mM Tris-HCl (pH 8.0), 200 mM NaCl, 5 mM β-mercaptoethanol, and 30 mM imidazole. Proteins were eluted with buffer containing 20 mM Tris-HCl (pH 8.0), 200 mM NaCl, 5 mM β-mercaptoethanol, and 300 mM imidazole, and His-tags were removed by overnight TEV protease digestion at 4 °C. The samples were purified via size exclusion chromatography (SEC) via a Superdex 200 Increase 10/300 GL column (Cytiva) equilibrated in SEC buffer (20 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8.0, and 200 mM NaCl). To assemble the complex, equimolar amounts of FBXW7-SKP1 and CUL1-RBX1 were mixed and incubated for 36 h at 4 °C. The mixture was purified by SEC, and fractions corresponding to the fully assembled complex were pooled and used for downstream assays.

## Annexin-V/PI staining apoptosis assay
To perform an apoptosis assay via Annexin-V/propidium iodide (PI) staining, 1 × 106 cells were seeded in 100 mm culture dishes. The following day, the cells were treated with 20 μM cisplatin and incubated for 48 h. For sample preparation, the cells were detached from the plates with trypsin-EDTA and washed twice with ice-cold PBS to remove excess trypsin. The cells were then resuspended in 1× binding buffer and stained with 5 μl of PI and 0.7 μl of Annexin-V in the dark for 15 min at RT via a FITC Annexin-V Apoptosis Detection Kit (556547, BD Biosciences, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA). The stained cells were analyzed via CytoFLEX (Beckman Coulter Life Sciences, Indianapolis, IN, USA). Statistical analyses were performed via one-way ANOVA with Tukey’s post hoc test via GraphPad Prism 8 (GraphPad Software).

## Histone extraction
The cells were prepared at a density of 2 × 106 cells per plate and lysed with freshly prepared hypotonic lysis buffer (10 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8.0, 1 mM KCl, 1.5 mM MgCl2, 1 mM DTT, protease and phosphatase inhibitor cocktail) by incubation on a rotator at 4 °C for 30 min. Then, the obtained nuclei were resuspended in 0.4 N H2SO4, and a final concentration of 33% trichloroacetic acid was used to precipitate the histones. The obtained histones were then analyzed through Coomassie blue staining and Western blotting with primary antibodies against H3K4me3 (ABE305, Millipore, 1:1000) and histone H3 (06-755, Millipore, 1:500).

## Immunofluorescence (IF) assay
A total of 1 × 105 cells were seeded on the coverslip the day before analysis and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 min at RT. The cells were permeabilized with 0.2% Triton-X 100 on ice for 25 min, and blocking was performed for 1 h at RT with 3% bovine serum albumin. The samples were incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C. The primary antibodies used were against Flag (M185-3L, MBL, 1:500), HA (M180-3, MBL, 1:500), KDM5B (ab181089, Abcam, 1:500), and USP7 (sc-137008, Santa Cruz, 1:250). Then, the samples were incubated with Alexa Fluor 488- or Alexa Fluor 594 dye-conjugated secondary antibodies (Jackson ImmunoResearch, West Grove, PA, USA) diluted 1:500 in blocking solution for 2 h. The cell nuclei were stained with DAPI (1:2000) at RT for 5 min. The coverslips were mounted and analyzed with an LSM710 confocal laser scanning microscope (ZEISS, Oberkochen, Germany).

## IHC staining and TMA analysis
Fresh tissues were obtained from xenograft studies and were prepared as formaldehyde-fixed, paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tissue sections. The tissue sections were deparaffinized at 65 °C for 1 h and rehydrated from xylene through a graded ethanol series to 70% ethanol. Standard heat-induced epitope retrieval was performed via sodium citrate buffer (pH 6.0) at 95–100 °C for 20 min. Endogenous peroxidase activity was quenched with 3% H2O2, followed by blocking with 2.5% normal horse serum for 1 h at RT.
For tissue staining, the tissue sections were incubated at 4 °C overnight with the following primary antibodies diluted in 2.5% normal horse serum: KDM5B (ab181089, Abcam, 1:200), DUSP4 (ab216576, Abcam, 1:50), and Ki-67 (ab16667, Abcam, 1:20). For visualization of the stained tissues, an ABC-HRP kit (PK-7200, Vector Laboratories, Newark, CA, USA) and a DAB substrate kit (SK-4100, Vector Laboratories) were used according to the manufacturers’ instructions. The sections were counterstained with Mayer’s hematoxylin (S3309, Agilent Dako, Santa Clara, CA, USA) and dehydrated in ascending ethanol solutions to xylene. The sections were mounted on glass slides using mounting medium (BMT-100, Biognost) for visualization. Whole-slide images were obtained via a panoramic digital slide scanner (Pannoramic MIDI) (3DHISTECH, Budapest, Hungary) or a microscope slide scanner, Ocus40 (Grandium, Tampere, Finland). The percentage of positive cells in each section obtained from three independent tissues was quantified via Fiji software.
For TMA analysis, OVC clinical TMAs were purchased from TissueArray.com (Derwood, MD, USA). The tissue slides were prebaked at 65 °C for 1 h, processed for IHC as described above, and stained with antibodies against KDM5B (ab181089, Abcam, 1:200), DUSP4 (ab216576, Abcam, 1:50), and USP7 (sc-137008, Santa Cruz, 1:50). Images were acquired via an Ocus40 slide scanner. Quantification of TMA staining was performed via Fiji software in at least three different randomly selected areas per core. Statistical comparisons between matched-pairs samples were performed via the two-tailed Wilcoxon matched-pairs signed-rank test. Correlation analyses between genes were performed via Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient. All the statistical analyses were conducted via GraphPad Prism 8 (GraphPad Software).

## Coimmunoprecipitation (Co-IP)
A total of 1 mg of protein lysate was incubated overnight either with appropriate primary antibodies mixed with protein A/G agarose beads (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Santa Cruz, CA, USA) or with Flag-/c-Myc-/HA-tagged magnetic beads alone (MedChemExpress, Monmouth Junction, NJ, USA) at 4 °C. The beads were collected via centrifugation or magnetic stand, washed three to four times with lysis buffer or 0.5% PBS-T, and resuspended in 1× SDS loading buffer. The immunoprecipitated samples were eluted by heating at 95 °C for 5 min. The eluates were separated by SDS‒PAGE, followed by Western blotting for protein detection as described.

## In vitro ubiquitination assay
Protein extracts were prepared as described above. Protein concentrations were measured via a BCA protein assay (23227, Thermo Fisher, Waltham, MA, USA). In vitro ubiquitination was performed via a ubiquitinylation kit (BML-UW29920, Enzo Life Sciences, Farmingdale, NY, USA) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, 100 nM E1, 1 μM E2 (UbcH3 and UbcH5a), 100 nM purified SCFFBXW7 complex, and 1 mM purified KDM5B were mixed with IPP, DTT, and Mg-ATP. The reaction mixture was incubated at 37 °C for 90 min. Then, the ubiquitination reaction was quenched, and the mixture was subjected to Western blotting for further analysis.

## In vivo ubiquitination assay
HEK293T or SKOV-3 cells were seeded in 100 mm culture plates at a density of 1 × 106 cells. Appropriate DNA constructs were cotransfected with Lipofectamine 2000 transfection reagent (Invitrogen, Waltham, MA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Then, the cells were treated with 10 μM to 20 μM MG132 at 37 °C for 8 h before the cell lysate was harvested. The cell lysates were prepared as described above. A total of 1 mg of protein lysate was subjected to co-IP using appropriate primary antibodies, followed by Western blotting analysis as described. The primary antibodies used in this research can be found in the Supplementary Materials.

## In vivo experiments and bioluminescence imaging
All the animal procedures were approved by the IACUC of Yonsei University (Seoul, Korea), and all the experiments were conducted in accordance with the approved guidelines and regulations (approval IDs: IACUC-202302-1644-01, IACUC-202402-1805-01, IACUC-202503-2021-01). All the mice were housed in a specific pathogen-free facility at Yonsei University. The mice were provided with sterilized food and water ad libitum.
For in vivo subcutaneous xenograft tumor models, SKOV-3 and SKOV-3 CDDP cells and their derived KO cells (2 × 106 cells/injection) mixed with 50% (v/v) Matrigel (Corning Life Sciences, Kennebunkport, ME, USA) were subcutaneously injected into the flanks of 4- to 6-week-old randomized (n = 6 per group) female BALB/c nude mice (Orient Bio, Seongnam-si, Korea). The weights of the mice were recorded, and the tumor sizes were measured every 3–4 days beginning on the day of injection via a caliper. The tumor volume (mm3) was calculated as width (mm) × length (mm) × length (mm) 0.5. When the average tumor size reached 150 mm3, the mice were randomly divided into two groups of six for each cell line: a control group and a group treated with cisplatin. Saline and cisplatin (5 mg/kg) were administered intraperitoneally twice a week (every 3 to 4 days) until the tumors in the control group reached an average volume of 1000 mm3. At the end of the experiment, the xenograft tissues were removed, weighed, and prepared for IHC staining.
For cotreatment xenograft experiments involving a USP7 inhibitor (P5091) and cisplatin, SKOV-3 CDDP cells were injected subcutaneously as described above. Once the tumors reached ~150 mm3, the mice were randomized into four groups: the control (vehicle), P5091-treated, cisplatin-treated, and P5091 + cisplatin-cotreated groups (n = 5 per group). Ten milligrams per/kg P5091 [4% NMP (N-methyl-2-pyrrolidone), 4% Tween-80, and 92% DPBS], 2.5 mg/kg cisplatin or vehicle was administered intraperitoneally every two days for two weeks.
For the intraperitoneal orthotopic mouse model, randomized 4-week-old female BALB/c nude mice were injected with 5 106 luciferase-overexpressing SKOV-3, SKOV-3 gKDM5B, SKOV-3 CDDP, SKOV-3 CDDP gKDM5B, or SKOV-3 CDDP gKDM5B/gDUSP4 cells suspended in 150 μl of DPBS. Three to four days after cell implantation, the mice of each cell line were randomized into two groups (n = 5 per group), and saline and cisplatin (5 mg/kg) were administered intraperitoneally twice a week. Mouse body weights were recorded. Bioluminescence images were obtained every 3 to 4 days for 2 to 5 weeks (as indicated) via an in vivo imaging system, VISQUE InVivo Smart-LF (VIEWORKS, Anyang-si, Korea). At the end of the study, all the mice were sacrificed via CO2 asphyxiation. Statistical comparisons of tumor volume, tumor weight, and body weight between groups were performed via one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post hoc test via GraphPad Prism 8 (GraphPad Software).

## Luciferase reporter assay
A DUSP4 promoter fragment encompassing approximately: −1800 bp upstream to +260 bp downstream of the TSS was amplified from human genomic DNA via PCR with the following primers: forward, 5’-AGAAATATCTTGATGCAATGCCTCTT-3’; reverse, 5’-GAGAGCCTCTTCTTCCCTGTCC-3’. The amplified fragment was digested with appropriate restriction enzymes and subcloned and inserted into the pGL3-Basic vector (Promega, Madison, WI, USA) upstream of the firefly luciferase gene (referred to as pGL3-DUSP4). All the constructs were verified via Sanger sequencing on both strands to confirm their correct insertion prior to usage.
For luciferase reporter assays, HEK293T or SKOV-3 cells were seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 2 × 105 cells/well and cotransfected with pGL3-DUSP4 alone or with Myc-KDM5B or Myc-KDM5B-H499Y for 24 h. The cells were then lysed, and the luciferase activity was measured via the Luciferase Reporter Assay System (Promega) on an Infinite 200 PRO Microplate Reader (TECAN). Luminescence values were normalized to the total protein concentration quantified via the BSA assay. Equal protein loading was confirmed by Western blotting. Normalized luciferase activities are expressed as the fold change relative to the control. All experiments were performed at least three independent times, each measured in duplicate. Statistical analyses were performed via unpaired t-tests for comparisons between two samples or one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post hoc test for multiple group comparisons via GraphPad Prism 8 (GraphPad Software).

## Protein stability assay (cycloheximide chase assay)
For protein stability assays, 5 105 of the indicated cell lines were seeded in 6-well plates. Then, the cells were treated with 2.5 μg/ml or 5 μg/ml cycloheximide (C7698; Sigma‒Aldrich) for the indicated durations. The cells were collected, lysed, and analyzed via Western blotting as described previously. Statistical analyses were performed via one-way ANOVA with Tukey’s post hoc test via GraphPad Prism 8 (GraphPad Software).

## RNA preparation and real-time quantitative PCR (RT‒qPCR)
The cells were seeded in 100 mm culture plates at a density of 1 106 cells/plate. Total RNA was extracted via the FavorPrep™ Total RNA Isolation Kit (Favorgen, Vienna, Austria) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. For cDNA synthesis, 1 µg of total RNA was reverse transcribed via EasyScript® Reverse Transcriptase (TransGen Biotech, Beijing, China). Quantitative PCR (qPCR) was performed via TOPreal™ SYBR Green qPCR PreMIX (RT500M, Enzynomics) according to the manufacturer’s protocol on an Applied Biosystems 7500 System (Applied Biosystems). Relative mRNA expression levels were calculated via the ΔΔCt method and normalized to the expression level of GAPDH in each sample. The data are presented as fold change [2(−ΔΔCt)] values relative to the control unless otherwise stated. Statistical analyses were performed via one-way or two-way ANOVA with appropriate post hoc tests. All reactions were run in independent biological triplicates. The qPCR primers used in this study can be found in Supplementary Table .

## RNA library preparation and sequencing (RNA-seq)
Total RNA was extracted via the FavorPrep™ Total RNA Isolation Kit (Favorgen, Vienna, Austria) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Total RNA samples were quantified via the Quant-iT™ RiboGreen® RNA Assay Kit (#R11490, Invitrogen), and RNA integrity was assessed via the Agilent TapeStation 2200 system (#G2964AA, Agilent) with the RNA ScreenTape assay (#5067-5576, Agilent). Only samples with RNA integrity number (RIN) values greater than 7.0 were used for downstream processing. RNA sequencing libraries were constructed via the NEBNext® Ultra™ II Directional RNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina® (#E7760S, New England Biolabs, Ipswich, MA, USA), which incorporates poly(A) mRNA magnetic isolation, according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Libraries were pooled in equimolar amounts and sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform to generate paired-end 2 × 150 bp reads. All RNA-seq experimental procedures were performed by EGSbio Incorporated.

## Statistical analysis
Statistical analyses were performed with GraphPad Prism 8 (GraphPad Software). All the data are presented as the means standard deviations (SDs) unless otherwise stated. All experiments were independently performed at least three times for biological replication. Statistical comparisons between two groups were performed via unpaired Student’s t-tests (two-tailed). Statistical comparisons between more than two groups were performed via one-way ANOVA or two-way ANOVA with Dunnett’s, Bonferroni’s, or Tukey’s post hoc test. Drug synergism was analyzed via median dose‒effect analysis via Calcusyn software (Biosoft, Cambridge, U.K.). For TMA analysis, statistical comparisons between matched-pair samples were performed via the two-tailed Wilcoxon matched-pairs signed-rank test. Correlation analyses between genes were performed via Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient. Statistical significance is represented in the figures by *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001, $P < 0.05, $$P < 0.01, $$$P < 0.001, #P < 0.05, ##P < 0.01, and ###P < 0.001.
