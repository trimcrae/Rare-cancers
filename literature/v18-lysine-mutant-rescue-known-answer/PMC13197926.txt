Protein kinases, which are crucial regulators of diverse cellular processes such as endolysosomal trafficking, neuroinflammation, and mitochondrial homeostasis, are frequently dysregulated in these conditions, making them attractive drug targets. This review explores the therapeutic potential of targeting key kinases implicated in neurodegeneration, specifically p38 MAPK, BTK, c-Abl/ABL1, CDK5, GSK3, JNK, LRRK2, and PINK1. We delve into their specific roles in disease pathophysiology, current therapeutic strategies, and the structural insights guiding our understanding of these kinases and the development of more selective inhibitors. This review highlights the urgent need for continued research to identify new targets and translate these scientific breakthroughs into effective therapies for patients.

## Significance Statement
This review outlines the roles of protein kinases in neurodegenerative diseases and highlights emerging strategies for their therapeutic modulation. By integrating current knowledge of kinase signaling, drug development, and pharmacokinetics, this work provides a timely and practical framework to guide the development of disease-modifying treatments in an area of pressing clinical need.

## Key Structural Advances Guiding Kinase Drug Design
PTEN-induced kinase 1 dimerization interface: Recent structural work reveals that PTEN-induced kinase 1 (PINK1) adopts a stable dimeric assembly that stabilizes its active conformation and supports ubiquitin phosphorylation. Defining this interface provides a mechanistic basis for small-molecule strategies aimed at promoting PINK1 dimer formation or mimicking its stabilizing contacts. Such approaches offer a path toward therapeutic activation of mitophagy in PINK1-linked and potentially sporadic Parkinson disease.
Leucine-rich repeat kinase 2 closed versus open states: Leucine-rich repeat kinase 2 structural studies now capture the kinase in distinct closed (inactive) and open (active) conformations. These global rearrangements reshape the ATP pocket, explain the differential binding modes of type I versus type II inhibitors, and reveal new surfaces for targeting. These insights are directly informing efforts to design more selective, central nervous system-penetrant leucine-rich repeat kinase 2 inhibitors and PROteolysis-TArgeting Chimera degraders.
Allosteric pockets in Bruton tyrosine kinases: High-resolution structures have uncovered previously unrecognized allosteric pockets flanking the Src homology 2 (SH2)–kinase interface and activation loop. These sites enable reversible, noncovalent Bruton tyrosine kinase inhibition that circumvents C481-mediated resistance and supports the development of central nervous system-penetrant agents for progressive multiple sclerosis.

I

## Introduction
The global burden of neurodegenerative diseases is rising significantly across the world. This is partly because age is a primary risk factor and the global population is aging, but also because the incidence of some of these diseases is increasing. Conditions such as Alzheimer disease (AD), Parkinson disease (PD), amyotrophic lateral sclerosis, and multiple sclerosis (MS) are all increasing in prevalence, highlighting the need for novel therapies to delay disease progression and treat the root causes. , The projected number of new cases of Alzheimer’s and Parkinson’s is expected to double by 2050, with associated health care costs exceeding $1 trillion annually. , Nearly all current therapies for these diseases focus on symptomatic relief, but disease-modifying therapies (DMTs) are needed to halt or reverse disease progression rather than delay the end-stage of disease. One challenge for the development of DMTs is the identification of actionable targets that can be modulated by small molecules. A promising strategy to target neurodegeneration is to restore disrupted cellular pathways by modulating kinases. Kinases are vital to every aspect of disease biology as they play a crucial role in regulating essential cellular processes, including cell growth, neuroinflammation, mitochondrial dynamics, and apoptosis. Specifically, mutations, deletions and posttranslational modifications of kinases or their substrates have been directly associated with neurodegenerative diseases. In AD, the focus is on kinases that phosphorylate tau and amyloid-β (Aβ), including glycogen synthase kinase-3 (GSK3), c-Jun N-terminal kinase (JNK), and cyclin dependent kinase 5 (CDK5). Some of these kinases have also been implicated in PD. There are 2 other kinases, leucine-rich repeat kinase 2 (LRRK2) and PTEN-induced kinase 1 (PINK1), whose mutations contribute to directly disease pathology by impairing intracellular trafficking and mitophagy, respectively. , However, beyond the clearcut familial forms, it is important to emphasize that the causes of neurodegeneration are unknown.
This review aims to explore kinases as viable therapeutic targets for the treatment or prevention of several neurodegenerative diseases discussed. We will review the evidence for the involvement of the kinases in the etiology of AD, PD, and MS and the current drug development landscape of 8 different kinases implicated in the same diseases. We will also provide an in-depth structural analysis of small molecules targeting these kinases. Additionally, we will address recent advancements in techniques designed to overcome challenges associated with drug delivery across the blood–brain barrier (BBB).

II

## Evidence for involvement of kinases in human neurodegeneration
Nearly all current therapies for neurodegenerative diseases offer symptomatic relief rather than altering the underlying disease course. In this context, kinases have emerged as attractive candidates for DMTs, as they regulate processes central to neurodegeneration, including protein aggregation, mitochondrial homeostasis, stress signaling, and neuroinflammation. However, unlike other fields where therapeutic strategies often originate from clear human genetic drivers, the development of kinase-targeted therapies in neurodegeneration has typically followed a bottom-up trajectory. In most cases, kinase involvement was first established in cellular and animal models, with human validation occurring only later. This reflects the practical limitations of neurology research, including limited access to central nervous system (CNS) tissue, a lack of early biomarkers, the chronic and slowly progressive nature of disease, and the difficulty of longitudinal brain sampling. As a result, the strength of human evidence supporting kinase modulation varies widely across targets.
For some kinases, human pathology provides direct evidence of kinase involvement. In AD, postmortem studies have demonstrated increased activation of stress-responsive kinases. Overactive p38 mitogen-activated protein kinases (MAPK) and elevated expression of its upstream activator microtubule associated protein (MAP) kinase kinase 6 (MKK6) have been reported in AD brains compared with controls. There is currently no direct evidence to our knowledge showing that p38 activity increases with age, however there are increased levels of phosphorylated p38 levels found in the blood of patients correlated to disease progression. p38α inhibitors have reached human testing in other indications and early Alzheimer’s studies have emphasized target engagement and safety, but clear disease-modifying benefit has not been demonstrated, due primarily to BBB penetration and selectivity challenges.
Similar to p38 MAPK, increased JNK activation has been reported in human postmortem brains from patients with AD, coinciding with neurofibrillary pathology and following Braak staging patterns. In PD, elevated JNK activity has also been observed in human substantia nigra tissue, consistent with findings from dopaminergic neurotoxin models such as 1-methyl-4-phenyl-1,2,3,6-tetrahydropyridine (MPTP) and 6-OHDA. , There is currently no direct human longitudinal evidence demonstrating that JNK activity increases as a function of aging. However, accelerated-aging OXYS rats, a model of spontaneous neurodegenerative-like decline, show increased JNK3 phosphorylation with age relative to Wistar controls, suggesting a potential link between aging biology and JNK activation that has not yet been confirmed in humans. Clinically, JNK inhibition has been explored as a neuroprotective strategy, but CNS-penetrant JNK inhibitors have faced selectivity, tolerability, and pharmacokinetic challenges, and no JNK-directed approach has yet demonstrated disease-modifying benefit in patients with AD or PD. Thus, although human tissue studies support JNK pathway involvement in neurodegeneration, therapeutic targeting remains at a preclinical stage with no established DMT-level evidence in humans.
Given glycogen synthase kinase 3β (GSK3β) constitutive activity, most studies on this kinase will measure protein levels instead, as it will be correlated with activity. GSK3β proteins levels were found to be elevated in AD brain and plasma in several cohorts, tau exposure has been shown to further increase GSK3β activity. , In regards to aging, there is limited human longitudinal evidence for a rise in GSK3β activity with age. However, there is evidence that GSK3β levels are increased in cerebral cortex, hippocampus, and septal nuclei of aged Sprague–Dawley rat brains. Clinically, multiple GSK3 inhibitors have been tested, but none have demonstrated disease-modifying efficacy in AD, largely because of challenges surrounding CNS penetration, selectivity, and safety.
Interest in c-Abl as a therapeutic target emerged from postmortem tissue in both AD and PD. In AD, Aβ has similarly been shown to activate c-Abl, driving tau pathology and neuronal injury, positioning c-Abl as a clinically relevant target in disease progression. Activated c-Abl and phospho-Abl isoforms are enriched in AD hippocampus and colocalize with AD lesions, supporting pathway engagement in human tissue. In PD, c-Abl activation has been detected in the striatum and the substantia nigra in human postmortem tissue. Clear age-trajectory data for c-Abl activity in normal human aging are limited. One PD study reports age-dependent increases in α-syn Y39 phosphorylation consistent with increased c-Abl activity in diseased brains, but longitudinal human aging data outside disease are lacking. Small-molecule c-Abl inhibitors attenuate degeneration in preclinical models, but convincing disease-modifying effects in patients have not yet been demonstrated. Clinically, multiple c-Abl inhibitors have entered PD trials; nilotinib achieved acceptable safety but showed low cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) exposure and no clear efficacy signal in randomized studies, indicating that target class feasibility is plausible but not yet disease-modifying in humans.
Bruton tyrosine kinase (BTK) was first identified through Bruton’s 1952 description of X-linked agammaglobulinemia, and later genetically defined in 1993 when BTK loss-of-function mutations were shown to arrest B-cell development at the pre-B stage. , Subsequent discovery of ectopic B-cell follicles in the meninges of patients with progressive MS combined with BTK’s role in B-cell receptor signaling and innate immune activation, motivated therapeutic development aimed at modulating B-cell and myeloid responses without complete immune depletion. Currently, age-related BTK activity levels have not been explored to our knowledge. Clinically, the oral BTK inhibitor evobrutinib reduced new gadolinium-enhancing magnetic resonance imaging lesions versus placebo in a randomized phase II MS trial, demonstrating central target engagement on an accepted imaging biomarker. Whether BTK inhibition will deliver DMT benefit long-term is currently under phase III investigation, and age-related changes in BTK activity remain unexplored.
In contrast with the above kinases, which were prioritized based on pathological signaling studies, LRRK2 and PINK1 were identified through genetic discoveries in familial PD. Mutations in the PARK8 locus led to cloning of the LRRK2 gene and subsequent identification of pathogenic variants such as R1441C and the widespread G2019S substitution, establishing LRRK2 as an important cause of late-onset autosomal-dominant PD. , , Age-related LRRK2 activity are not established. Follow-up studies demonstrated that kinase hyperactivity is central to disease pathology, paving the way for development of LRRK2 kinase inhibitors. There are currently LRRK2 inhibitors and PROteolysis-TArgeting Chimeras (PROTACs) in development, but disease-modifying benefit has not yet been demonstrated.
Similarly, PINK1 loss-of-function mutations were shown to cause autosomal-recessive early-onset PD, establishing its essential role in mitochondrial quality control and mitophagy. For aging, the mitophagy marker phospho-Ser65 ubiquitin, generated by PINK1, increases in human brain with age and in Lewy body diseases, though it remains to be explored whether this pattern is from accumulation of mitochondrial damage and reduced clearance, or age-dependent increase of PINK1 catalytic activity itself. However, unlike LRRK2, no selective PINK1 agonist has yet entered clinical trials, largely because of the absence of potent, selective small-molecule activators and challenges associated with stabilizing the kinase in its active conformation. Recent mouse studies demonstrate that PINK1 activation can enhance mitochondrial clearance and even reduce amyloid pathology in models of neurodegeneration, but whether therapeutic activation of PINK1 can prevent PD or modify disease course in humans remains unknown. 
Together, these examples highlight that human evidence for kinase involvement ranges from causal genetic validation to postmortem biochemical association, to inference from robust preclinical models when direct human data are not yet accessible. Establishing causality and timing in human disease remains a major challenge, and model systems continue to play an essential role in bridging mechanistic insight with clinical pathology.

## Kinases as drug targets
Kinases are enzymes that catalyze the transfer of a phosphate group, typically from ATP to a substrate molecule using divalent cations such as magnesium or manganese for this process. Some kinases are capable of using GTP as a phosphate source in addition to ATP, such as CamKII (for autophosphorylation and substrate phosphorylation but GTP less preferred and less efficient), protein kinase C δ (for autophosphorylation but ATP preferred), and CK2. , , All kinases share elements in common, which we will briefly review here. The kinase fold is separated in the N-terminal and C-terminal lobes, between which the ATP molecule binds. The ATP-binding pocket includes a hinge region, which forms hydrogen bonds with the adenine ring to position the ATP molecule for catalysis ( ). The adenine binding pocket also comprises a “gatekeeper” residue, which forms interactions with the adenine ring. The gatekeeper residue varies across kinases and is a significant selectivity determinant for drugs. The nucleotide also interacts with the P-loop (also called the Gly-rich loop), which binds the β and γ phosphates and positions them for catalysis. Kinases include an invariant lysine residue that binds the β phosphate. Catalysis itself is mediated by 2 aspartates that play distinct roles. The first aspartate is part of the invariant Asp-Phe-Gly (DFG) motif, and its sidechain interact with a divalent cation that helps increase the reactivity of the phosphodiester bond between the β and γ phosphates. The second aspartate is part of the invariant His-Arg-Asp motif, which interacts with the substrate donor hydroxyl group and increases its nucleophilicity. These core elements of catalysis are themselves under the fine control of other more peripheral elements that control activation of the reaction, such as the activation loop, which coordinate substrate binding and catalysis, as well as the αC-helix, which help mold the ATP binding pocket. The key to many kinases’ actions is the ability to engage with a given substrate or substrate class and then coordinate the transfer of a phosphate group to that substrate. These substrates can be other proteins, but many (most) kinases also autophosphorylate, and this step is often necessary for the phosphorylation of their cognate nonself substrate. 
Phosphorylation mediated by kinases are critical for regulating a wide array of cellular activities, including signal transduction, metabolism, and cell cycle progression. Kinases represent approximately 2% of all coding genes in the human genome, comprising a total of 518 protein kinases, including 385 protein-serine/threonine kinases, 90 protein-tyrosine kinases, and 43 protein-tyrosine kinase–like enzymes. For the sake of this review, we will focus on protein kinases, but kinases also comprise lipid and sugar kinases that play a myriad roles in metabolism. A phylogenetic analysis of human protein kinases show that they share various degrees of sequence homology and that kinases can be classified into 10 subclasses. Sequence homology between divergent classes of kinases is typically in the range 20% to 25% and areas of high sequence conservation are concentrated around the ATP binding pocket and residues that help form the N- and C-lobe hydrophobic cores.
The involvement of protein kinases in several diseases, in particular cancers, made them primary targets for the development of drugs. The first small-molecule kinase inhibitor, imatinib, was approved by the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) in 2001 for the treatment of chronic myeloid leukemia, marking a pivotal milestone in kinase-targeted therapeutics. Imatinib, also known as Gleevec, is a type I (DFG-in, ATP-competitive) inhibitor of the Abelson tyrosine kinase (ABL1, or c-Abl) that was initially reported in 1992. The success of imatinib served as a precedent for the development of numerous kinase inhibitors targeting both receptor and nonreceptor kinases. However, these therapeutic advances were rapidly hampered by the realization that the ATP-binding site of kinases is highly conserved, making ATP-competitive inhibitors highly promiscuous and lacking specificity. Advances in structural biology have enabled the identification of shared and distinct molecular mechanisms in kinases that propelled the development of more selective inhibitors, addressing concerns about off-target effects and toxicity. As of January 1, 2024, there are 80 effective small-molecule kinase inhibitors approved for clinical use by the FDA. Here, we will discuss general mechanisms used by these inhibitors, as they will be relevant for the discussion around drugs in development for neurodegeneration.
A

## Mechanisms of protein kinase-targeted drugs
There are different types of kinase inhibitors each with distinct advantages. Type I kinase inhibitors bind to the catalytic site of the kinase where the conserved DFG motif is in the DFG-in or active conformation (meaning the loop is oriented into the binding pocket) and the αC helix is also in. Type I inhibitors fully occupy and are restricted to the ATP binding pocket and typically engage with the hinge region and other elements that are common to all kinases, which make them less selective. Type I are classical ATP-competitive inhibitors and thus must compete with cellular ATP levels that are in the range 0.1 to 1 mM. Because the Km of kinases for ATP is typically in the range 10 to 100 μM (circa 10-fold lower than the concentration of ATP), they must bind with low nanomolar affinities to achieve significant inhibition. They can nonetheless be highly useful, and many type I inhibitors have been developed as drugs, such as erlotinib, which targets epidermal growth factor receptor in non-small cell lung cancer. Type II inhibitors are more selective, interact with the αC helix and can maintain the DFG aspartate in the “out” conformation, which is inactive because it is unable to coordinate the Mg2+ ion. Type II inhibitors also occupy the ATP-binding pocket including the hinge region, but they also interact with other elements outside the pocket, which are less conserved. The prototypical type II inhibitor is the c-Abl inhibitor imatinib described above. Type III inhibitors on the other hand bind allosterically in the cleft formed by the 2 lobes of the ATP-binding pocket, but without overlapping ATP itself. Thus, they display noncompetitive or uncompetitive mechanisms of inhibition. Some of the first examples of type III inhibitors are trametinib, a potent mitogen-activated protein kinase kinase 1 inhibitor, as well as p38 inhibitor that were first shown to bind adjacent and not overlapping with the ATP-binding site. Type I ½ inhibitors bind in the ATP-binding pocket as well, but they induce a distinct DFG-in but αC-out conformation. Type I and I ½ inhibitors are considered to be promiscuous because of the conservation of the ATP-binding pocket among Ser/Thr and Tyr kinases. Type II and III inhibitors are more selective compared with type I and I ½ inhibitors since they interact allosterically with nonconserved regions of the kinases. Type II and III inhibitors are both sometimes considered allosteric inhibitors, although technically type II have a mixed mode of inhibition (both orthosteric and allosteric).
Although inhibition has been by far the most common type of mechanism used by kinase-targeted drugs, in many cases it would be desirable to increase their activity. For example, PINK1 is a kinase whose loss of function is causative of early-onset PD, therefore, researchers are looking to increase its activity to restore mitophagy. Serendipitously, it was discovered that some inhibitors can paradoxically activate kinases; this topic will be addressed in the outlook.
A final general point to consider about kinase inhibitors is their pharmacokinetic properties. Most kinase inhibitors developed to date are administered orally. This is typically associated with molecules that fall within the acceptable limits of the Lipinski’s rules and have molecular weights <500 Da. Only 3 are not orally bioavailable: netarsudil, given as an eye drop, and temsirolimus and trilaciclib, which are given intravenously and used to treat advanced renal cell carcinoma and to decrease the risk of myelosuppression, respectively. Pharmacokinetics is critical to consider in future drug design endeavors, as drugs targeted to the central nervous system will have to cross the BBB, which can further impede the development of compounds for neurodegenerative diseases.
Although activation of kinases by inhibitors through paradoxical agonism has been observed, there are only a limited number of kinase activators that have been discovered. The development of kinase activators presents a significant opportunity for treatment of neurodegenerative diseases, as will be discussed later in this review.

## Alzheimer disease
AD is currently the most common neurodegenerative disease and affects memory and cognitive function. The symptoms of AD are typically categorized into early, middle, and late stage. AD’s morphological changes can be seen in several brain regions, primarily in the hippocampus and several regions of the cortex that control learning and memory. The symptoms of the early stage are characterized by mild memory lapses, difficulty with memorization and concentration whereas symptoms in the middle stage, the longest stage, include more noticeable cognitive decline and difficulty performing daily tasks. Those in the late stage of the disease will experience severe impairment in memory, inability to recognize speech or eat. The majority of cases of AD are sporadic, whereas approximately 5% of cases are familial. Women are twice as likely to develop this disease as compared with men; however, the exact reason is still up for debate. 
A hallmark of AD is the presence of Aβ plaques, characterized by extracellular deposits of aggregated Aβ peptides forming insoluble plaques in the brain. Hyperphosphorylation and aggregation of the microtubule-associated protein tau is also seen in AD, as it is the main component of neurofibrillary tangles (NFTs). These 2 pathologies are associated with dysregulation of synaptic function and the loss of neurons. Firstly, Aβ aggregates are formed when there is abnormal proteolytic cleavage of amyloid precursor protein (APP), a protein responsible for plasticity and synaptic repair, by β-secretase (BACE1) and γ-secretase complexes. These plaques are thought to drive cognitive decline because they disrupt synaptic function, induce neuroinflammation, and contribute to neuronal death. The formation of these aggregates is linked to numerous factors including genetic mutations in APP, age-related clearance mechanism impairments, and mutations in presenilin 1 and 2 (PSEN1/2), which are part of the γ-secretase complex. 
Secondly, the microtubule-associated protein tau is responsible for stabilizing neuronal microtubules under physiological conditions and whose aggregated form affects axonal transport of proteins and organelles. , Tau has several different post translational modifications including phosphorylation at 28 sites that regulate its function. However, in disease pathology such as AD, this number of phosphorylated sites increases. There are 85 potential phosphorylation sites on tau, however, the exact number of sites that are phosphorylated at a given time in AD is known. , It was recently shown that phosphorylated tau, and not unmodified tau, can form fibrils spontaneously. These fibrils trigger a spike in mitochondrial superoxide, induced apoptosis, caused cell death at submicromolar concentrations, and were able to convert nonmodified tau into an aggregation-prone cytotoxic species that eventually form NFTs. There is mounting evidence that tau tangles correlate better with disease progression than amyloid plaques, although some suggest that Aβ plaques might initiate the cascade of event that lead to tau hyperphosphorylation. In any case, tau phosphorylation is clearly associated with pathogenicity and should be investigated. There are several kinases that phosphorylate tau, including p38 MAPK, JNK, GSK3, and CDK5, which we will discuss in this review and is summarized in . However, as there are over 20 protein kinases that can phosphorylate tau, targeting them individually might not be an optimal strategy for AD. 
Currently symptomatic treatments available for patients with AD focus on enhancing neurotransmitter function to attenuate cognitive decline and memory loss. The primary pharmacological intervention is administration of cholinesterase inhibitors such as donepezil, rivastigmine, and galantamine, which increase acetylcholine levels in the brain to support cognitive function by preventing its breakdown. Indeed, acetylcholine plays a pivotal part in the body’s memory and cognition, and decreased levels cholinergic neurons has been associated with the disease. Both rivastigmine and galantamine are indicated in patients with mild to moderate stages of the disease, whereas donepezil is approved for all stages. However, acetylcholine levels in the brain decrease naturally as we age, therefore decreasing the efficacy of these treatments. Moreover, overactivation of the N-methyl-D-aspartate glutamate receptor activation has been implicated in AD since binding of glutamate, which is upregulated in disease, increase the free cytosolic intracellular Ca2+. The toxic levels of Ca2+ leads to the eventual loss of synaptic function and neuronal death. Therefore, some patients are given N-methyl-D-aspartate receptor antagonists to regulate glutamate activity to improve cognitive function in moderate to severe AD cases. One example of which is the noncompetitive antagonist, memantine. These drugs, however, do not halt or slow cognitive decline. Thus, significant efforts have been made to develop disease-modifying treatments (DMTs), primarily aimed to decrease the amount of Aβ plaques in the brain. Biogen developed a monoclonal antibody, aducanumab, which successfully reduced Aβ plaques found in AD patient brain, as observed by positron emission tomography imaging. Despite this groundbreaking finding, they have modest effects on cognition and cause amyloid-related imaging abnormalities, which indicate cerebral edema, lesions and swelling, with 20 patients experiencing at least one serious adverse event and 2 requiring hospitalizations. Recently, Biogen has abandoned aducanumab and collaborated with Eisai on lecanemab. Alternatively, Elli Lilly is working on donanemab in patients who have amyloid buildup in the brain but have not yet shown any symptoms. There remains a need for DMTs and more so, protective therapies before degeneration takes place. Accordingly, this review focuses on p38 MAPK, c-Abl, CDK5, GSK3, and JNK as kinase targets under active clinical investigation for AD, with current trials for p38, c-Abl, and GSK3 summarized in .

## Multiple sclerosis
MS is a chronic autoimmune and inflammatory disease of the CNS that primarily affects the brain and spinal cord with a mean age of onset between 20 and 30 years of age. MS is characterized by demyelination of neuronal axons in the white and gray matter and the loss of oligodendrocytes, delaying communication between the brain and other parts of the body. The demyelination process is also associated with increased activity of astrocytes and the formation of gliotic scars. The clinical presentation of MS is variable between patients but is categorized into relapsing-remitting MS (RRMS), the most common form, and progressive types, such as secondary progressive MS (SPMS) and primary progressive MS (PPMS). Early symptoms of MS include visual disturbances, numbness, weakness, and fatigue, whereas progressive stages involve worsening motor dysfunction, spasticity, and cognitive decline. About 75% of those with MS are women; however, the interplay between genetic, hormonal, or environmental factors, which is thought to contribute is still unclear. Given that MS is an autoimmune disease and that these diseases are more prevalent in females because of differences in immune system, sex-based immune differences might also account for the higher prevalence of MS in women as compared with men. There are only symptomatic treatments for MS that primarily focus on the management of relapses, slowing disease progression, and alleviating specific symptoms. Treatment of acute relapses involves administration of high-dose corticosteroids such as methylprednisolone to suppress inflammatory cytokine activation and prevention of immune cells from entering the CNS. Acute relapses can also be managed with using DMTs such as the new oral medication dimethyl fumarate, fingolimod, and teriflunomide, which modulate immune responses and reduce the frequency of relapses. , Symptomatic therapies address issues such as spasticity, pain, bladder dysfunction, and fatigue.
The disease process of MS involves the demyelination of neuronal sheaths, oligodendrocyte loss, and axonal damage by an immune-mediated attack driven predominantly by autoreactive T and B lymphocytes that infiltrate the CNS. The resulting lesions in the white matter lead to the disruption of nerve conductivity thereby leading to the symptoms experienced. Resulting lesions can, however, become remyelinated with the recruitment of differentiated oligodendrocyte progenitor cells. Despite advancements in treatment, MS remains a lifelong condition with no cure.
B cells are fundamental players in the pathophysiology of MS, especially since antibodies against the CD20 protein marker on B cells has proven to have great therapeutic effect. Therefore, the science has shifted away from using anti-CD20+ drugs such as rituximab because it is not CNS penetrant, it depletes all B cells include those required for normal function and causes hypogammaglobulinemia and vasculitis. Research also found that only peripheral, and not central bone marrow B-cell tolerance checkpoint was defective in those with MS and this defect is linked to increased activation and homeostatic proliferation of mature B cells that contribute to the autoimmune process of MS. These B cells can then enter the CNS since the BBB is damaged, in fact B cells and autoreactive T cells will secrete proinflammatory cytokines that will allow the flow of these immune cells through, when it is normally very restricted. This dysregulated B cell-activation is governed by an intracellular kinase cascade. More specifically, BTKs are enzymes downstream of the B cell receptors that drive B cell proliferation and differentiation, antigen presentation, and cytokine secretion. In addition to B cells, BTK is expressed in microglia where it contributes to inflammatory signaling and phagocytic activation, suggesting BTK plays a dual role in both adaptive and innate immune mechanisms relevant to MS. 
Recent evidence also points to a link between Epstein–Barr virus (EBV) infections and MS pathogenesis. The risk of MS indeed increases 32-fold after EBV infection in longitudinal studies. Furthermore, EBV-interacting proteins are enriched in MS genetic susceptibility loci. Given that latent persistent EBV primarily targets memory B cells, this may provide a mechanism for the limited therapeutic efficacy of anti-CD20+ antibodies. Thus, targeting EBV-specific epitopes and B-cell-specific signaling pathways and malfunction represent promising avenues. Clinically, emerging evidence demonstrates BTK involvement in human MS. Ectopic B-cell follicles in the meninges of patients with progressive MS support the role of compartmentalized B-cell activation, and BTK activity has been detected in MS lesions and activated immune cells in patient samples. Phase II clinical trial data showed that the BTK inhibitor evobrutinib significantly reduced new gadolinium-enhancing magnetic resonance imaging lesions versus placebo, confirming CNS target engagement. Multiple BTK inhibitors, such as tolebrutinib and fenebrutinib, are currently in phase III trials to determine whether BTK inhibition can modify long-term disability progression. Kinases play central roles in cellular signaling in cells of the adaptive immune system, and enzymes such as BTKs, which control B cells proliferation, represent ideal targets to treat MS. Overall, BTK remains the most advanced kinase target under investigation for MS and is the only kinase currently being tested in late-stage clinical trials for potential disease-modifying effects. ## Parkinson disease
PD is the most prevalent movement disorder, affecting approximately 2% to 3% of individuals over the age of 65 years. Approximate 5% to 10% of cases are hereditary and monogenic, whereas the rest are sporadic with a significant genetic susceptibility component of about 35%. Mutations in genes such as SNCA (α-synuclein), LRRK2, PINK1, DJ-1, and Parkin have been implicated in familial forms of PD. Symptoms of PD include bradykinesia, resting tremor, rigidity, and postural instability due to the progressive loss of dopaminergic neurons in the substantia nigra pars compacta, which leads to dopamine depletion in the striatum and impairments in motor control. There are also nonmotor symptoms such as autonomic dysfunction, sleep disturbances, psychiatric difficulties (eg, depression, psychosis, and anxiety), and cognitive decline. Currently, there are more males than females with PD, at a ratio of 3:2, respectively. In terms of sporadic PD, there are some environmental factors that might contribute to the higher incidence of PD in males, as pesticide exposure, such as rotenone or paraquat, is associated with PD, and males are more likely exposed since men are more frequently exposed because of higher occupational involvement in agricultural work. In terms of familial PD, there have been no sex differences reported in PD patients with LRRK2 mutations. On the other hand, no demographic information is available regarding the sex distribution of PINK1 mutations. Finally, c-Abl, CDK5, and JNK, there are no genetic mutations that contribute to their role in PD, and therefore no available information on their sex difference in disease in terms of genetic mutation and there is no available data regarding sex differences in kinase activity in humans. Taken together, it is difficult to make a general claim about the sex difference as more research taking these factors into account needs to be done. The interplay between these kinases in PD has been illustrated in . 
PD, similarly to the other diseases discussed, only has symptomatic treatments available. These treatments aim to restore dopamine levels by administering levodopa alongside carbidopa. Levodopa is an amino acid precursor that is transported by the large amino acid transporter at the BBB and is decarboxylated in the central nervous system to make dopamine. Because levodopa undergoes decarboxylation in the periphery by DOPA-decarboxylase, most of the drug does not reach the brain as dopamine does not cross the BBB. To bypass this, levodopa is coadministered with carbidopa, a peripheral inhibitor of DOPA-decarboxylase, resulting in a higher concentration of levodopa reaching the brain. A concern that arises with the administration of levodopa is the diminishing responsiveness to drug treatment over time as well as increased dyskinesia with prolonged treatment. Levodopa increases dopamine levels; frequent maintenance doses are used to maintain dopamine levels but unavoidably leads to a drop in dopamine levels in between doses, which causes “off-times.” The fluctuation in levels of dopamine in plasma and the altered presynaptic and postsynaptic levels of nigrostriatal neurons seem to mediate levodopa induced dyskinesia. Extended-release L-DOPA formulations are now administered to reduce both off-time and dyskinesia. Other therapeutic approaches include dopamine agonists pramipexole and ropinirole are also used to activate receptors by mimicking dopamine, as well as monoamine oxidase-B inhibitors such as rasagiline and selegiline to prevent the breakdown of dopamine. However, none of these treatments modify the course of the disease progression. Clinical trials targeting LRRK2, PINK1, and c-Abl are underway across multiple phases, and these are summarized in . 
A hallmark of PD is the presence of Lewy bodies in the brain of patients, which are composed of α-synuclein protein aggregates encoded by the SNCA gene. In normal physiological conditions, α-synuclein is thought to be interacting with synaptobrevin to stimulate the formation of soluble N-ethylmaleimide-sensitive factor attachment protein receptor vesicles and the release of neurotransmitters. Synucleinopathy has been found in 90% of PD cases, but loss of function of the protein does not lead to any PD symptoms or striatal degeneration in mice. , , Large quantities of unfolded α-synuclein tends to aggregate and form oligomers and fibrils and lead to impairment of neuronal communications and is believed to spread between neurons in a prion-like fashion. , , A major area for the development of DMTs is targeting α-synuclein aggregation. Intriguingly, although most α-synuclein is not phosphorylated in healthy controls, pathogenic aggregates are massively (>90%) phosphorylated at Ser129. This site is phosphorylated by multiple kinases, with Polo-like kinase 2 (Plk2) being the most prominent one. Ser129 phosphorylation can be removed by the phosphatase PP2A. Plk2 Ser129 phosphorylation promotes autophagic clearance of α-synuclein and appears to enhance its effect in facilitating neurotransmission. This would suggest that this modification is protective and indeed evidence suggests that phosphorylation does take place in healthy individuals, which leads to synuclein clearance. Plk2 activation or PP2A inhibition may therefore appear as promising therapeutic avenues, but increasing Plk2 activity may have unwanted oncogenic effects. Taken together, these findings highlight PD as a multikinase disorder in which dysregulation of α-synuclein biology, mitochondrial stress pathways, and inflammatory signaling all converge, underscoring the need for kinase-targeted, DMTs.

## p38 mitogen-activated protein kinases
p38 MAPKs are proline-directed serine/threonine kinases and are responsible for coordinating several cellular processes including cell growth, differentiation, survival, and apoptosis under conditions of stress such as reactive oxygen species, cytokine release, and infection. This kinase was discovered in 1994 and ascribed 4 different functions: a protein phosphorylated after lipopolysaccharides stimulation, as RK, a protein activated in response to arsenite, heat shock or osmotic stress, as P40 activated in response to interleukin 1, and as CSBP2, the target of pyridinyl imidazole compounds such as SB203580 with anti-inflammatory properties. , , , The p38 MAPK family is composed of 4 distinct proteins, p38α (MAPK14), p38β (MAPK11), p38γ (MAPK12), p38δ (MAPK13), p38α, and p38β are ubiquitously expressed (including the brain), whereas p38γ and p38δ are more tissue specific, with p38γ found primarily in skeletal muscle, and p38δ in the lungs, kidneys, pancreas, small intestine, and testis. The different proteins all have different downstream targets and sensitivity to chemical inhibitors.
p38 MAPKs can be activated through both a canonical MAPK pathway and 2 noncanonical pathways. In the canonical pathway, p38 is phosphorylated by MAP2K (MKK3 and MKK6), which in turn requires activation by phosphorylation through MAP3K, in response to stimuli such as tumor necrosis factor-α and oxidative stress. MAP3K can also phosphorylate MKK4, another MAP2K, which can either dual-phosphorylate p38 or JNKs on Thr and Tyr residues in the activation loop thus activating 2 distinct signaling pathways relevant to AD pathology. Specifically, p38 is phosphorylated on Thr180 and Tyr182 by MAP2Ks. MKK3 activates p38 particularly in response to cellular stress, injury, and inflammation. MKK6 works in parallel with MKK3 to regulate stress and inflammatory responses. MKK4, or SEK-1, is involved in stress signaling pathways triggered by DNA damage, cytokines, and other cellular insults. Noncanonical pathways include TAK1-binding protein 1 (TAB1)-mediated autophosphorylation of p38, which bypasses upstream MAP3K signaling by binding the MAP3K protein TAK1 (transforming growth factor-β–activated protein kinase 1) directly. TAB1 is both an activator and a substrate of p38, with TAB1 being phosphorylated on Ser423, Thr431, and Ser438 in vitro by p38. Another noncanonical pathway involves the phosphorylation of p38 by ZAP70 during T-cell receptor signaling, followed by p38 autophosphorylation. ZAP70 has also been shown to indirectly activate p38 by phosphorylating linker for activation of T-cells and Src homology 2 domain-containing leukocyte phosphoprotein of 76 kDa, which activates the MAP2K signaling pathway. Active p38 binds substrates through docking interactions and recognizes the specific proline-directed phosphorylation motif (usually Ser-Pro or Thr-Pro). p38 has a multitude of substrates not limited to other kinases, transcription factors, and transcriptional regulators. Of note, p38 phosphorylates tau at residues Ser202, Thr205, Ser396, Ser404, and Thr231 (main sites), and c-jun at residues S63 and 73 (JNK substrate). ,

## Role of p38 mitogen-activated protein kinases in Alzheimer disease
p38 MAPK is implicated in multiple pathological processes linked to AD, including neuroinflammation, tau NFTs, and Aβ plaque formation. Overactivation of p38 MAPK has been shown to upregulate BACE1 expression, the enzyme responsible for cleaving APP into toxic Aβ peptides, thereby enhancing Aβ production and plaque formation. Additionally, Aβ plaques themselves can further activate p38 MAPK, creating a feed-forward cycle of inflammation, oxidative stress, and neuronal damage. Neuronal p38α signaling in transgenic AD mouse model contributes to increase Aβ deposition in the brain that triggers an inflammatory response, ultimately leading to cognitive deficits, synaptic plasticity dysfunction, and memory impairment. Interestingly, p38γ phosphorylation of tau at specific residues may inhibit Aβ toxicity, contrasting the pathological roles of p38α and p38β. However, high levels of p38γ in the forebrain of APP-transgenic mice leads to exacerbation of cognitive dysfunction. The impact of the pharmacological regulation of p38γ in the context of AD remains unexplored, as this isoform is usually targeted in carcinomas. In a mouse model of tauopathy, researchers used MW181, an inhibitor selective for the p38α isoform, and found a significant reduction in tau phosphorylation at certain key residues and a decrease in tau aggregates. Based on its physico-chemical properties (rule-of-four compliant, ie, MW<400, clogP<4, PSA<80Å2), MW181 is likely brain-penetrant, but to our knowledge this has not been measured experimentally ( ). p38 MAPK also contributes to AD pathology by regulating the activity of the small GTPase RAB5, which is involved in early endosomal trafficking. Overactive p38 MAPK indirectly contributes to RAB5 overactivation by phosphorylating guanine nucleotide exchange factors, which in turn activate RAB5 and leading to impaired endosomal trafficking, enlarged endosomes, and disrupted lysosomal degradation. This dysfunction promotes APP accumulation and processing by β-secretase, which leads to an increased production of Aβ peptides. These findings highlight the complex role of p38 MAPK in AD pathogenesis, in terms of both tau phosphorylation and Aβ plaques, and the potential of isoform-specific inhibitors as therapeutic strategies.

## Role of p38 mitogen-activated protein kinases in Parkinson disease and other neurodegenerative diseases
p38 MAPK is a regulator of neuroinflammation as it plays a role in proinflammatory cytokine signaling and may play a role in MS. Activation of this kinase increases the production of inflammatory mediators, exacerbating demyelination and neuronal damage. Inhibitors aiming at helping MS would target proinflammatory cytokine signaling. However, many efforts of targeting p38 MAPK are either in early stages or have been discontinued. There are several challenges in the development of p38 MAPK inhibitors for MS, as many trials have been discontinued because of limited efficacy compared with alternative treatments, such as BTKs inhibitors. p38 has also been implicated in PD, in particular activation of p38α and mislocalization of p38γ in PD and dementia with Lewy bodies, another synucleinopathy involving the formation of Lewy bodies in the cortex. Inhibition of p38α with the SKF-86002 inhibitor reduced neuroinflammation and improved motor behavioral deficits in a mouse model of PD that overexpresses human α-synuclein. The effect appears to be mediated by inhibition of microglial activation, as SKF-86002 had no significant effect in induced pluripotent stem cell-derived neurons carrying the pathogenic A53T variant.

## Current status of therapeutic development
The therapeutic goal of targeting p38 MAPK in AD is to inhibit phosphorylation of key downstream substrates that contribute to the complex pathological network that leads to Aβ plaque formation, NFTs, and neuroinflammation. In 2021, CervoMed conducted phase IIb clinical trials with neflamapimod, also called VX745, a type II selective p38α MAPK inhibitor on patients with mild AD symptoms. Neflamapimod crosses the BBB, with CSF/plasma unbound ration near unity ( ). Originally, this compound was used in rheumatoid arthritis, which was discontinued because of CNS-related adverse effects. The goal was to target the p38 MAPK-dependent Rab5 pathway, which is involved in endosomal dysfunction. However, the study found no significant improvement in episodic memory after 24 weeks of treatment. They did detect a reduction in biomarkers such as total tau, suggesting potential modulation of disease-relevant processes. Although there are no further trials for neflamapimod in AD at this time, ongoing research includes trials in DLB as preclinical trials showed decreased RAB5 activity and Tau phosphorylation. CervoMed has announced plans to initiate a phase III clinical trial for neflamapimod in dementia with Lewy bodies in 2025. As of October 2025, the company announced positive results from their phase IIb trial as neflamapimod achieved exposure-dependent improvement on clinical dementia rating scores, reflecting better cognition, and reduced risk of clinical progression (CervoMed).
Other p38 MAPK inhibitors include MW150, a brain-penetrant drug developed by Neurokine Therapeutics that demonstrated preclinical efficacy in both AD and MS. MW150 is a p38α isoform selective inhibitor that targets the neuroinflammatory response by reducing proinflammatory cytokine production and preventing synaptic dysfunction, thereby protecting against neuronal damage. MW150 was found to be safe, well tolerated, and reached clinically relevant blood levels after daily oral administration in healthy volunteers in a 2018 phase I study. In 2022, the company began a phase IIa clinical trial, which should have been completed in August 2024, however, there are no updates available at this time.

## Structural analysis
There are over 265 experimentally determined structures of the p38 MAPK family, with 251 belonging to the p38α isoform. All p38 MAPK have a simple bilobal structure kinase domain arrangement ( A). The first structure of p38α, released in 1998, revealed a bilobal kinase fold, with a well defined ATP-binding cleft and activation loop. Since then, there have been 250 additional structures of the p38α isoform reported, bound to different inhibitors or substrates/activators. Although there is a structure with the TAB1 interactor ( B), there are currently no structures of p38 MAPK with any other of its substrates. p38α achieves substrate selectivity via a docking groove located on the C-lobe, called the common docking site. This groove recognizes short linear motifs present on substrates and regulators. The structure of p38α bound to the TAB1 docking peptide exemplifies this interaction, revealing an F-X-F-X-F motif that anchors the substrate for phosphorylation. , Recently, the first structure of p38α in complex with its upstream kinase MKK6 was resolved by cryo-electron microscopy (cryo-EM) (PDB 8A8M), providing key insights into MAPK activation and signaling specificity. 
The mechanism allowing p38 to bind tau involves docking-mediated recruitment of tau to the common docking of p38α, followed by site-specific phosphorylation at proline-directed motifs. Tau, an intrinsically disordered protein, contains multiple proline-directed serine/threonine (S/T-P) motifs, which are recognized by MAPKs such as p38α. These motifs facilitate the binding of tau to the common docking site on p38α, positioning tau for efficient phosphorylation. However, physiological substrates are typically anchored by a F-X-F-X-F sequence upstream of the S/T-P phosphorylation site. This interaction is enhanced under stress conditions and contributes to tau pathology in neurodegenerative diseases such as AD. However, there are no direct biophysical evidence for how tau is phosphorylated by p38.
There are 3 structures of p38α bound to the type II inhibitor neflamapimod (VX-745), a small molecule inhibitor currently in clinical trial for the treatment of AD ( C). The structures exhibit the stereotypical “DFG out” conformation, with the inhibitor occupying the ATP binding site. There are 4 structures of the p38β isoform, including one with nilotinib and 2 with a dihydroquinazolinone. Four structures of the p38γ isoform have also been determined, the first one of which was published in 1999 and is the first structure of any of the p38 isoform bound to a nucleotide, a nonhydrolysable ATP analog adenylyl-imidodiphosphtae (AMP-PNP). There are 8 structures of the p38δ isoform, with the first one being published in 2008, however, all of them lacking the 13 last amino acids. There are several structures of this isoform in complex with inhibitors developed for COPD. Overall, these hundreds of structures enabled researchers to establish firm bases for the potency and selectivity of inhibitors, enabling the design of isoform-selective inhibitors such as MW150.

## Bruton tyrosine kinase
BTK is a nonreceptor tyrosine kinase responsible for several signaling pathways including B cell development, differentiation, and antigen receptor signaling. It was originally discovered in patients diagnosed with X-linked agammaglobulinemia, as their BTK levels in B cells were depleted. , BTK is a member of the Tec family of kinases and plays an essential role in B-cell receptor signaling. Upon antigen engagement, BTK is recruited to the plasma membrane via interactions with phosphatidylinositol-3,4,5-trisphosphate. There, it becomes activated through phosphorylation by Src-family kinases such as Lyn or by Syk. Recruitment to phosphatidylinositol-3,4,5-trisphosphate on membranes can also directly activate BTK kinase activity. Activated BTK phosphorylates phospholipase C-γ 2, leading to the generation of inositol triphosphate and diacylglycerol. This cascade triggers calcium signaling and activation of transcription factors such as nuclear factor kappa-light-chain-enhancer of activated B cells (NF-κB), promoting B cell survival, antigen presentation, and autoantibody production.
1

## Role of Bruton tyrosine kinase in multiple sclerosis
Although no mutations in BTK are associated with MS, overactivation of this kinase has been linked to the disease. More specifically, increased BTK activity leads to increased B-cell proliferation, antigen presentation and can induce systemic autoimmunity. , Additionally, overactivation of BTK has been linked to increased survival of autoreactive B cells since they become more resistant to apoptosis, a phenomenon observed in experimental autoimmune encephalomyelitis, a preclinical model of MS. BTK overactivity can also activate proinflammatory signaling pathways of microglia, which then release interleukin 6 and tumor necrosis factor-α, 2 cytokines that exacerbate CNS inflammation and contribute to myelin and neuronal damage. The sustained proinflammatory environment generated by the affected microglia lead to disruption of the BBB, which allows the infiltration of leukocytes and contributes to the neurodegeneration. Phosphorylation and activation of BTK contributes to MS pathogenesis by promoting B cell survival, antigen presentation, and autoantibody production. Specifically, activated BTK phosphorylates phosphorylates phospholipase C-γ 2, initiating calcium signaling and NF-κB activation downstream of the B cell receptor. In parallel, BTK is also expressed in microglia, where it amplifies neuroinflammation by mediating Toll-like receptor-induced cytokine release. , 
Targeting BTK in cancers has led to the emergence of resistance in the kinase active site. For example, the C481S mutation prevents the binding of known BTK inhibitors, such as ibrutinib, leading to therapeutic resistance in chronic lymphocytic leukemia (CLL). Inhibition of BTK impairs the ability of B cells to act as antigen presenting cells, without decreasing their number or impairing their physiological such as antibody production, B-cell signaling and myeloid cell activation. The B cells are thus unable to act as antigen presenting cells, which also decreases its capacity to activate naïve T cells, the proposed mechanism of action of BTK inhibitors. Therefore, preventing potent B-cell antigen presenting cell development was proposed as one of the central mechanisms underlying BTK inhibitors efficacy. Moreover, inhibiting BTK is beneficial not only for halting disease progression, but to also promote remyelination through a complementary pathway. Recent findings indeed suggest that BTK inhibitors enhance oligodendrocyte precursor cell differentiation and maturation, which are critical steps in remyelination, as demonstrated in experimental autoimmune encephalomyelitis models. This highlights a dual role of BTK inhibitors in addressing neuroinflammation and promoting CNS repair.

## Current status of therapeutic development
There are 4 FDA approved BTK inhibitors for the treatment of B-cell malignancies, ibrutinib, acalabrutinib, zanubrutinib, and pirtobrutinib, however, none of them are indicated for autoimmune disorders. However, ibrutinib, a first generation irreversible BTK inhibitor, is no longer recommended because of its adverse events including but not limited to anemia, thrombocytopenia, and liver toxicity. It was originally approved for use in CLL, mantle cell lymphoma, and Waldenström’s macroglobulinemia. On the other hand, acalabrutinib is a second generation irreversible BTK inhibitors with increased selectivity for BTK, with a safety profile better than ibrutinib although it is known to have adverse cardiac effects. Zanubrutinib is another second generation irreversible BTK inhibitor with a more favorable safety profile compared with the 2 others mentioned above and was discovered through structure-activity relationships. , Because of their poor safety profiles and low CNS penetrance, these kinase inhibitors were deemed unsuitable for prolonged treatment, as would be required in patients with MS. These BTK inhibitors form a covalent bond with the sidechain of Cys481 in the kinase domain, which prevents ATP binding and confers selectivity to the kinase. However, this reactivity also is at the source of their poor safety profiles.
There are currently several clinical trials for MS in progress for small molecule inhibitors of BTK including: tolebrutinib, evobrutinib, fenebrutinib, remibrutinib, orelabrutinib, and BIIB091. These BTKi’s differ based on enzyme binding, half-life IC50, and binding selectivity. Tolebrutinib, evobrutinib, fenebrutinib, and remibrutinib are all in phase III trials. Tolebrutinib is an irreversible second generation BTKi, which binds with an in vitro IC50 of 0.7 nM, the most potent of the BTKis. The tolebrutinib phase IIb trial, funded by Sanofi, demonstrated a dose-dependent decrease in the number of gadolinium-enhancing lesions shown in patients and good drug tolerance. It is being looked at for treatment of MS at all stages (RRMS, PPMS, and SPMS). Tolebrutinib works by inhibiting BTK both in the periphery and in CNS-resident microglial cells, setting it apart from other BTKi’s that cannot cross the BBB. Furthermore, it reached pharmacologically active concentrations in the CNS. It has passed onto phase III clinical trials and is now actively recruiting patients; however, it has been placed on partial clinical hold by the FDA because of liver enzyme abnormalities. Despite this, the trial in September 2024 showed a 31% delay in disease progression in SPMS, although results with relapsing MS were unsatisfactory. In early 2025, the trials resumed with ongoing discussion about the regulatory approval for tolebrutinib despite the FDA hold in place. Compared with ibrutinib, evobrutinib, and fenebrutinib, tolebrutinib has a lower total surface polar area, which leads to increased CNS penetrance ( ), higher potency, and faster covalent reaction rates of BTK inhibition. 
Evobrutinib is another irreversible BTKi with an IC50 of 33.5 nM and which had a successful phase II trial for MS. However, all phase III trials seem to have been terminated. Merck is investigating this compound for RRMS in spite of its moderate CNS penetrance. Compared with teriflunomide, it did not meet primary endpoints in RRMS in 2 phase III trials; it has been placed on partial hold by the FDA because of abnormal for liver enzyme abnormalities where the enzymes were increased in about 5% of the treatment group. However, given BTKi’s mechanism of action, it would be worth redoing the trials in groups of patients with SPMS and PPMS since evobrutinib decreased the volume of slowly expanding lesions, suggesting it might be selectively efficacious against smouldering MS seen in progressive disease subtype. Merck do, however, state as of December 2023 that they will complete a full evaluation of the clinical trial data and will work with investigators on the future presentation and publication of the results. As of March 2024, the company seems to have discontinued trials for evobrutinib for MS.
Many other BTK inhibitors are currently under investigations. Fenebrutinib is in phase III trials used for RRMS (compared against teriflunomide) and PPMS (compared against ocrelizumab). It has an IC50 of 6.2 nM and is a noncovalent type I inhibitor, binding to the kinase domain via hydrogen bonds that triggers a conformational shift, rendering the kinase inert and unable to activate downstream effectors. Developed by Roche/Genentech, this inhibitor has moderate CNS penetrance but has a favorable safety profile. Remibrutinib is a covalent inhibitor from Novartis in phase III trials for RRMS only and showed a good safety profile and an IC50 of 1.3 nM. Orelabrutinib is a covalent inhibitor in phase II trials but has been placed on partial hold by the FDA because of abnormalities in liver enzyme. BIIB091 is a reversible BTKi with high selectively compared with the others mentioned above, as it sequesters the Tyr551 (a phosphorylation site for Lyn and BLK kinases) into an inactive conformation. It is under investigation by Biogen for relapsing MS and phase II clinical trials are actively recruiting. It binds the DFG-in conformation and thus represents a new type of allosteric inhibition intermediate between type I and type II. BIIB091 decreases inflammation by selectively inhibiting BTK, thereby disrupting B cell receptor and Fc receptor signaling pathways. This inhibition leads to reduced activation of B cells and myeloid cells, culminating in decreased production of proinflammatory cytokines and autoantibodies. , BIIB091 blocked B-cell activation and antigen production in animal models. As of June 2025, Biogen has started recruiting patients for a phase II clinical trial in adults with relapsing forms of MS.

## Structural analysis of Bruton tyrosine kinase
BTK is comprised of several domain including a N-terminal region consisting of a pleckstrin homology (PH) domain combined with a zinc-finger, a proline-rich TEC homology domain, SH3 and SH2 domains that interact with proline-rich motifs and phosphotyrosine peptide motifs sequences respectively, and a kinase catalytic domain ( A). These N-terminal domains help localize BTK to the B cell receptor complex and facilitate assembly of downstream signaling scaffolds. BTK substrate recognition is mediated by the kinase domain, but the specificity is mediated by the N-terminal SH3 and SH2 domains. There are 133 experimentally solved crystal structures of BTK, encompassing various domain and conformational states. There is currently no structure of full length BTK, with most corresponding to a.a. 389 to 659 (kinase domain) or 1 to 270 (N-terminal region). The first structure was of the SH3 domain of BTK, published in 1998, but a more comprehensive structure of the N-terminal PH domain was published in 1999. , Consistent with previous in vitro studies, the structure of full-length BTK predicted by AlphaFold 3 shows that the SH3 and SH2 domains of BTK form autoinhibitory interactions with the kinase domain, whereas the PH domain is independent from the kinase domain ( B). The interactions mediated by the SH3 and SH2 are highly similar to those found within the c-Abl kinase and help maintain the kinase inactive. 
Although there are no structures of BTK in complex with full-length physiological substrates, phosphorylation of phosphorylates phospholipase C-γ 2 and B-cell linker protein, has been validated through biochemical or cellular studies. , Structural models of related kinases suggest that BTK engages these substrates via transient docking interactions near the activation segment and catalytic cleft. BTK also functions in innate immune cells, where it supports inflammatory signaling, though structural insights into these roles remain limited.
Subsequent structures provided insight into BTK in both active and inactive conformations, bound to a wide array of type I and type II inhibitors, and in complexes mimicking autoinhibitory or signaling/active states. Among these structures, several capture BTK in complex with clinically relevant inhibitors such as ibrutinib, acalabrutinib, zanubrutinib, and evobrutinib. These molecules covalently modify Cys481 within the ATP-binding site, locking BTK in an inactive conformation ( C). The structure of BTK with ibrutinib demonstrates the irreversible binding stabilizing the kinase in a DFG-in, but catalytically inactive state. However, ibrutinib does not trap Tyr551 into a rigid (low B factors) inactive conformation, which correlates with higher potency against Fcg receptor signaling. Through structure activity relationships and optimization of pharmacokinetics properties (reduced basicity to reduce off-targeting human Ether-a-go-go related gene, good lipophilic ligand efficiency, and microsomal stability), Biogen discovered BIIB091, which interacts with Tyr551 via a trimethyl group while maintaining interactions with the hinge region of the nucleotide-binding site ( D). These selective reversible BTK inhibitors, as well as other brain-penetrant covalent inhibitors such as BIIB129 show favorable safety profiles in preclinical models and could be used in the treatment of MS. , Although there are no allosteric inhibitors of BTK that have reached the clinic, high affinity protein binders targeting the SH2 domain inhibit BTK kinase activity. Intriguingly, the binder did not interfere with the SH2 domain ability to bind phospho-tyrosine peptides, suggesting that allosteric interactions between the SH2 and kinase domains are critical for activation of the latter. Future work could explore modes of inhibition targeting allosteric interactions with the SH2 or SH3 domains, as well as inhibiting the PH domain binding to phosphatidylinositol-3,4,5-trisphosphate, which would block membrane recruitment.

## c-Abl
c-Abl is a nonreceptor tyrosine kinase encoded by the ABL1 gene, which was first identified as the mammalian homolog of the oncogenic gene product of the Abelson murine leukemia virus. The gene codes for 2 splice isoforms: Abl1a and Abl1b, the latter being also known as c-Abl. In the nervous system, c-Abl is critical for normal neuronal development by phosphorylating downstream targets such as CDK5. It phosphorylates CDK5 on Tyr15 via CABLES1, a binding protein, and facilitates CDK5/p35 activation, which is crucial in developing neurons. However, in adult neurons, c-Abl remains largely inactive under normal conditions. In neurodegeneration, c-Abl is overactive in response to oxidative stress and Aβ oligomers, the exact mechanism will be discussed in a later section.
The Abl1a isoform consists of an SH3 and SH2 domains, a kinase domain, and a C-terminal actin binding region ( A). The more common Abl1b isoform contains an additional myristoylated N-cap region, which maintains the protein in an autoinhibited conformation. It can be released when the myristoyl lipid chain inserts into the plasma membrane upon binding to upstream activators. The SH3 and SH2 also bind to the kinase domain and help maintain an inactive conformation. Thus, SH3 ligands such as PxxP-containing motifs in c-Cbl or c-Jun, located at the plasma membrane, can induce c-Abl activation. Likewise, phospho-tyrosine residues that are phosphorylated in signal transduction (eg, c-Jun or receptor tyrosine kinases) bind to the SH2 domain and thus can activate c-Abl as well. Oligomerization via the C-terminal domain or via clustering on the plasma membrane can also lead to activation, as it stimulates autophosphorylation in trans. Autophosphorylation at Tyr412 in the activation loop leads to an increase in catalytic activity, which then enables rapid phosphorylation of its substrates, which typically also bind to the SH2 domain. This creates a positive feedback loop that leads to enhanced substrate recruitment, which in turn maintain c-Abl in a state of activation locally. Thus, c-Abl multiple domains and autoinhibitory interactions makes this kinase a hub for the integration of multiple signaling pathways, which can arise from normal or aberrant processes.

## Role of c-Abl in Alzheimer disease
In AD, c-Abl becomes overactivated in response to oxidative stress and Aβ oligomers. Oxidative stress promotes c-Abl activation since the stress signals generated can activate upstream kinases such as ataxia-telangiectasia mutated, which then phosphorylate and activate c-Abl. Furthermore, Aβ oligomers can bind directly to synaptic receptor tyrosine kinases, EphA4, in synapses and trigger its phosphorylation and inducing c-Abl activation. c-Abl overactivity leads to pathological mechanisms such as plaque formation, tau hyperphosphorylation, NFT formation, and neuronal death. One of the major pathways is c-Abl overactivity leading to tau phosphorylation on residues Tyr18, Tyr197, Tyr310, and Tyr394 (numbering for Tau-441 isoform, Uniprot P10636-8), which stimulates NFT formation. Among these, phosphorylation at Tyr394 is significant as it is consistently found in NFTs in the brain of patients with AD, where it enhances tau aggregation, activates apoptotic pathways, and contributes to neurodegeneration. , The phosphorylated tau species are more prone to misfolding and aggregation, leading to the formation of NFTs. Interestingly, phosphorylation at Tyr18, Tyr197, and Tyr394 were observed in patients with AD, whereas phosphorylation at Tyr394 alone is present in healthy individuals, emphasizing that aberrant phosphorylation of tau is linked to AD. Given the multiple protein kinases that phosphorylate tau, inhibiting tau phosphorylation by c-Abl alone may not fully stop or prevent disease progression. Furthermore, the effects of tau phosphorylation depend on the specific residues modified. Phosphorylation at Tyr310 alone, or at Tyr18, Tyr29, and Tyr197, or all 5 tyrosine residues (Tyr18, Tyr29, Tyr197, Tyr310, and Tyr394) has been shown to prevent tau aggregation, which would be beneficial, but also alter its lipid and microtubule-binding properties, which may be detrimental. This suggests that not all tau phosphorylation events contribute equally to disease, or some may even have protective roles. Given the multiple protein kinases that phosphorylate tau, inhibiting tau phosphorylation alone may not fully stop or prevent disease progression. c-Abl inhibitors have nevertheless been explored as a therapeutic strategy and showed potential in reducing tau phosphorylation and improving autophagy in AD models.

## Role of c-Abl in Parkinson disease
In PD, c-Abl is overactivated in response to oxidative stress and α-synuclein aggregation. In the presence of oxidative stress and DNA damage, the ataxia-telangiectasia mutated kinase will become active and will in turn activate c-Abl. One major consequence of c-Abl activation is its ability to phosphorylate key substrates involved in neuronal homeostasis, which lead to protein aggregation, mitochondrial dysfunction, and cell death. One of the most well characterized effects of c-Abl overactivation is its direct phosphorylation of α-synuclein at Tyr39, which enhances its aggregation and neurotoxicity. This phosphorylation contributes to Lewy body formation and neuronal loss. Additionally, increased c-Abl activity has been detected in the striatum of patients with PD, where it was initially reported to phosphorylate Parkin, an E3 ubiquitin ligase required for mitophagy. , Both groups reported that Parkin is phosphorylated by c-Abl at Tyr143, which inhibits its activity and thus would impair mitochondrial quality control and increase oxidative stress. However, recent findings from our lab contradicts this model. We indeed found that Parkin was not significantly phosphorylated by c-Abl and furthermore found that phospho-mimetic mutations at Tyr143 enhanced Parkin’s activity rather than inhibit it. These findings suggest that c-Abl’s effects on Parkin may be indirect or not mediated through Parkin at all. Beyond α-synuclein and Parkin, c-Abl modulates several other key proteins implicated in PD, including Parkin interacting substrate, FBP1, and synphilin-1. Some studies have suggested that c-Abl inhibition by imatinib leads to the accumulation of AIMP2, a proposed neurotoxic factor linked to α-syn aggregation. However, unlike most Parkin substrates, AIMP2 is not on mitochondria and the mechanism by which it is ubiquitinated by Parkin remains unclear, raising questions about whether this is the correct mechanism by which c-Abl mediates AIMP2 degradation. Instead, the c-Abl-mediated disruption of autophagy may be a more relevant contributor to AIMP2 accumulation. Additionally, c-Abl activity influences autophagy through the p53 signaling pathway. c-Abl phosphorylates and activates p53, a tumor suppressor protein that inhibits autophagy. This effect promotes the accumulation of toxic α-synuclein aggregates and impairs neuronal proteostasis, exacerbating neurodegeneration. Importantly, inhibition of c-Abl with drugs such as nilotinib has been shown to restore autophagic clearance of α-synuclein, protect SN neurons, and improves motor performance providing a potential therapeutic strategy in PD models. Together, these findings underscore c-Abl’s complex role in PD pathology, affecting multiple pathways including α-synuclein aggregation, mitochondrial dysfunction, and autophagy suppression.
c-Abl integrates several stress-responsive pathways relevant to both AD and PD through phosphorylation of key neuronal substrates. Collectively, these downstream actions place c-Abl at a convergence point of cellular stress, aggregation pathways, and neuronal survival programs. Although its pleiotropic signaling and mixed clinical trial outcomes raise concerns about target specificity, c-Abl continues to be pursued therapeutically because its activation is consistently observed in human AD and PD brain tissue and because multiple converging pathological processes appear to depend on its dysregulation. However, c-Abl is one of many kinases that modify tau, α-synuclein, and stress-response pathways, suggesting that targeting it alone may not meaningfully alter the trajectory of multifactorial neurodegeneration. As a result, although c-Abl remains mechanistically relevant and consistently activated in patient tissue, current evidence suggests it may not be the most tractable or specific target for DMT unless more selective and CNS-penetrant modulators are developed. This challenge is not unique to c-Abl; JNK and GSK3β inhibitors face similar limitations because these kinases sit at highly interconnected nodes of stress, apoptotic, and metabolic pathways. Their broad substrate profiles mirror the concerns surrounding c-Abl, suggesting that although they remain mechanistically relevant, none may represent an ideal stand-alone target without more selective, context-specific modulators.

## Current status of therapeutic development
Although there is conflicting evidence on the pathogenesis mechanism of c-Abl in neurodegenerative diseases, several trials were aimed at this kinase. Nilotinib is a small molecule approved originally for use in chronic myeloid leukemia; however, it was also shown to prevent neurodegeneration in murine AD models. In AD, nilotinib reduces aberrant c-Abl phosphorylation improves autophagy, reduces Aβ levels, and restores memory deficits. Additionally, inhibition of c-Abl by nilotinib was able to restore autophagy, decrease accumulation of α-synuclein and delay disease onset. Nilotinib also promotes α-synuclein clearance by the autophagy and proteasome pathways. The compound likely exerts its effect by decreasing c-Abl phosphorylation of CDK5, also involved in PD pathogenesis. Although early clinical trials suggested some biomarker improvements such as levels of hyperphosphorylated tau and dopamine metabolites (homovanillic acid and 3,4 dihydroxyphenylacetic acid), subsequent double-blind studies failed to demonstrate significant clinical benefits, likely because of challenges such as limited brain penetration ( ) and uncertainties regarding its long-term safety. Moreover, the heterogeneity in the trials might also be assigned to variable criteria and endpoints selected. A systematic review revealed an age-dependent effect of nilotinib, where older patient at early stages of the disease had better outcome to nilotinib as compared with younger patients in the more progressed stages. 
Another c-Abl inhibitor, IkT-148009 also known as risvodetinib, is currently undergoing phase II double-blind placebo clinical trial for PD by ABLi Therapeutics, but the compound was previously licensed under Inhibikase Therapeutics (NCT05424276). This compound suppressed c-Abl activation, which reduced α-synuclein pathology and preserved dopaminergic neurons in mice. Unlike other c-Abl inhibitors, risvodetinib showed improved brain penetration and a favorable safety profile in mice models. The results of the phase II trial were released in October 2025, they reported no adverse effects, and the side effect profile was comparable to that of placebo. Exploratory findings in this trial revealed a dose-dependent decreased in α-synuclein levels in a subset of patients. Many other compounds are being investigated in animal and cell models, including imatinib, and INNO-406 in PD and dexibuprofen in AD. , , , Radotinib has recently gone from animal models to a phase II clinical trial, which is currently recruiting patients. Despite negative results from early clinical trials, c-Abl remains a compelling target because its activation is consistently detected in human AD and PD brain tissue, where it colocalizes with tau and α-synuclein pathology. , , The lack of efficacy observed with nilotinib likely reflects limitations in CNS penetration and dose tolerability rather than absence of target relevance, especially given the new results with risvodetinib. Together, this indicates that c-Abl biology remains strongly implicated in human disease, and that optimized pharmacology will determine future therapeutic success.

## Structural analysis
There are over 80 experimental structures of human c-Abl, with various domain deletions and bound to various inhibitors. All structures encompass the N-terminal segment up to the kinase domain, with no experimental structure for the C-terminus, which is predicted to be disordered for the most part except at the very C-terminus. The crystal structure of autoinhibited c-Abl reveals how the SH2, SH3, and kinase domains form a compact, self-inhibited assembly, resembling the regulatory configuration seen in Src family kinases but with distinct features, including a myristoyl binding pocket inside the kinase domain that is unique to c-Abl ( B). The SH2 domain docks onto the C-lobe of the kinase, constraining it in an inactive orientation. Likewise, binding of the myristoyl group, which is covalently attached to the N-terminus of the protein before the SH3 domain, maintains the kinase inactive. The C-terminal region after the kinase domain contains segments that contribute to the nuclear export of c-Abl, as well as a C-terminal actin-binding domain that couples c-Abl to the cytoskeleton. Activation of the kinase can be achieved via 3 different mechanisms , : (1) binding of peptide ligands such as phospho-tyrosine binding to the SH2 domain or proline-rich peptides binding to the SH3 domain; (2) autophosphorylation at Tyr412 in the activation loop, which maintains the kinase constitutively active; and (3) displacement of the myristoyl group upon insertion into the plasma membrane, which disrupts autoinhibitory interactions. Once activated, c-Abl then phosphorylates tyrosine residues in substrates such as Crk and Tau. The consensus motif for c-Abl substrates typically involves a tyrosine within a flexible or unstructured region, sometimes preceded by hydrophobic or proline-rich sequences, which are abundant in Tau. The structure of the c-Abl kinase bound to an ATP-peptide substrate mimetic shows how the substrate adopts an elongated configuration with the phenyl ring of the sidechain extending toward the nucleotide-binding site with the DFG motif in the active “in” conformation ( C). Substrate specificity is further conferred by binding to the SH3, SH2, and C-terminal region. However, there is no precise molecular understanding of how tau sites engage with c-Abl, nor how Aβ or other protein aggregates may aberrantly activate c-Abl.
Various structure-based approaches have been used to develop potent c-Abl inhibitors. Imatinib and related compounds (nilotinib) were the first clinically successful ATP-competitive inhibitor developed to target the inactive conformation of the c-Abl kinase domain (type II). Structural studies showed that imatinib binds deeply within the ATP-binding cleft, stabilizing the kinase in an inactive “DFG-out” conformation, where the conserved Asp-Phe-Gly motif of the activation loop flips outward, preventing catalysis ( D). This mode of binding explains imatinib’s selectivity and dependence on a specific conformational state. Nilotinib differs from imatinib by making additional contacts via a trifluoromethyl group that increases the affinity and selectivity for c-Abl versus other tyrosine kinases ( E). Although radotinib is structurally similar to imatinib/nilotinib, INNO-406 is a dual specificity inhibitor and target the Lyn tyrosine kinase. Targeting Lyn may show additional benefits for neurodegeneration, as inhibiting this kinase proved beneficial in the treatment of chorea-acanthocytosis in Vps13a−/− mice, as this disease is caused by the loss of Vps13A, a vacuolar sorting protein closely related to Vps13C, which is implicated in familial PD. In addition to targeting the active site, significant progress has been made toward the development of allosteric inhibitors targeting the myristoyl binding site, including the first in class inhibitor asciminib. , However, these allosteric inhibitors have not yet been tested in neurodegenerative disorders but could prove beneficial if they are selective and show good brain penetration.

## Cyclin dependent kinase 5
CDK5, also known as tau protein kinase II, is a proline-directed serine/threonine kinase responsible for neuronal migration, synaptic transmission, axonal guidance, and cytoskeletal stability. CDK5 was initially identified by biochemical purification from bovine brain and because of its close sequence homology to human CDC2. Under physiological conditions, CDK5 activity is tightly and directly regulated through its interaction with neuronal activators p35 and p39 (a p35 isoform), which tether it to specific subcellular locations and regulate its kinase activity). , , Once activated, CDK5 phosphorylates multiple substrates to maintain neuronal integrity. CDK5 also regulates calcium influx through phosphorylation of voltage-dependent calcium channels, thereby increasing calcium entry into presynaptic terminals and activating neurotransmitter release. This calcium influx also increases the level of calpain, a protease that cleaves the C-terminal myristoylation on proteins p35 and p39 and turn them into p25 and p29, respectively. This cleavage of p35 into p25 removes regulatory membrane-anchoring and produces hyperactive p25-CDK5 complex that translocates to the nucleus. Pathological conditions linked to neurodegeneration can increase calpain activity, leading to aberrant CDK5 activation via p35/p25 processing. This activation and mislocalization results in excessive phosphorylation of CDK5 substrates such as tau. , Similarly to the p25-CDK5 complex, the p29-CDK5 also results in mislocalization and aberrant activation of the complex. 
In neuronal migration, CDK5 phosphorylates doublecortin and nuclear distribution element-like 1, thus stabilizing microtubules and enabling accurate migration of neurons during brain development. CDK5 also regulates axonal transport and cytoskeletal dynamics by phosphorylating tau, ensuring microtubule stability and the proper transport of vesicles and organelles along axons. In synaptic transmission, CDK5 modulates neurotransmitter release by phosphorylating synapsin III, involved in neuronal migration, and Munc18, facilitating synaptic vesicle docking and exocytosis. , CDK5 also plays a role in endocytosis by phosphorylating proteins such as dynamin 1 and amphiphysin 1, ensuring efficient recycling of synaptic vesicles. Hyperactive CDK5 phosphorylates neurofilament proteins, such as NF-L and NF-H, leading to their abnormal aggregation and impaired axonal transport, a key feature of amyotrophic lateral sclerosis, as well as AD and PD (see sections below). CDK5 also phosphorylates mitochondrial proteins, including Drp1, a GTPase required for mitochondrial fission, and which is associated with N-methyl-D-aspartate-induced neuronal loss. 
1

## Role of cyclin dependent kinase 5 in Alzheimer disease
In AD, CDK5 is aberrantly activated and mislocalized due to the cleavage of its regulatory proteins, p35 and p39, into p25 and p29 respectively by calpain, a calcium-dependent protease. The p25 and p29 fragment binds CDK5 with higher affinity and longer half-lives, leading to hyperactivation and mislocalization of the kinase, and altered substrate specificity. These resulting complexes, as mentioned previously, will lead to hyperactivation of CDK5, which alters its substrate specificity, localization and leads to hyperactivation, which all drive multiple pathological processes in AD. These include tau hyperphosphorylation, APP misprocessing, and synaptic dysfunction. CDK5 hyperphosphorylates tau at multiple residues, including Ser202, Thr205, Ser235, and Ser404, which enhances tau aggregation and the formation of NFTs and reduces its ability to stabilize microtubules. At the same time, hyperactivated CDK5 disrupts APP trafficking and cleavage, promoting formation of Aβ plaques through phosphorylation of Thr668. CDK5 has also been shown to regulate β-secretase enzyme (BACE1). CDK5 inhibition can increase BACE1 expression, stability and activity thus promoting Aβ accumulation. The elevated Aβ levels will in turn activate calpain, increasing production of p25 thus creating a feedforward loop that exacerbates Aβ accumulation. Indeed, addition of CDK5 inhibitor CP681301 alone or in conjunction with Aβ42 significantly increased BACE1 and cleaved caspase-3 levels in neurons but a decrease in CDK5 protein levels. Caspase 3 cleaves tau at residue Asp421, which generates tau fragment that are susceptible to form aggregates and NFTs. These trends were also observed with a different CDK5 inhibitor, roscovitine, suggesting it is CDK5 inhibition specific and not due to CP681301.

## Role of cyclin dependent kinase 5 in Parkinson disease
CDK5 has also been involved in the pathogenesis of PD via its aberrant activation that promote pathological mechanisms. Under physiological conditions, CDK5 activity is tightly regulated by its neuronal activators p35 and p39. However, in PD, increased calpain activity, notably induced by increased cytosolic calcium levels, leads to the cleavage of p35 into p25, forming a hyperactive and mislocalized CDK5/p25 complex that can cause neuronal death by promoting mitochondrial dysfunction, and activating apoptotic pathways as seen above. This dysregulated CDK5 activity is observed in MPTP-induced mouse models of PD and pharmacological inhibition of CDK5 provides neuroprotection against loss of dopaminergic neurons in the nigrostriatal dopamine pathway induced by MPTP. CDK5 also phosphorylates Sirtuin 2 (SIRT2) at Ser331 and Ser335, leading to the translocation of SIRT2 from the cytoplasm to the nucleus. This nuclear localization of SIRT2 results in the deacetylation of histone H3 at Lys56, which subsequently triggers apoptotic pathways and contributes to the degeneration of dopaminergic neurons in PD models. Furthermore, inhibition of CDK5 phosphorylation of SIRT2 rescues loss of primary culture neurons treated with MPP+.

## Current status of therapeutic development
Roscovitine and dinaciclib are the 2 main CDK5 inhibitors that are currently being investigated in cancer therapeutics. However, roscovitine, known also as CYC202 and seliciclib, is a purine derivative and is promiscuous as it targets CDK2, CDK7, and CDK9, as a consequence of its similarity to ATP and the high conservation of the adenine binding site. It does however have increased selectivity for CDK5, inhibiting the CDK5/p35 complex with an IC50 of 0.16 μM, as compared with IC50s of 0.45 μM for CDK1/cyclin B, 0.7 μM for CDK2/cyclin A, 0.46 μM for CDK7/cyclin H, and 0.78 μM for CDK9/cyclin T1. , , The drug has undergone phase II clinical trials for non-small cell lung cancer and Cushing disease. There are no clinical trials for AD as of today.
Another inhibitor, dinaciclib (SCH-727965), targets CDK1, CDK2, CDK5, and CDK9. This drug has been evaluated in clinical trials for various cancers as well, including CLL and multiple myeloma. As of now, there are no CDK5-specific inhibitors approved for clinical use, and the development of these inhibitors remains a challenge because of the structural similarities within the CDK family. Ongoing research aims to design more selective CDK5 inhibitors to minimize off-target effects and improve therapeutic outcomes. In summary, although several CDK inhibitors have been investigated in clinical trials, their lack of specificity for CDK5 have limited their use. The development of selective CDK5 inhibitors is crucial for advancing therapeutic strategies targeting CDK5-related pathologies.

## Structural analysis
There are 10 experimental structures of human CDK5 in complex with its activator p25, either in the apo state or bound to small-molecule inhibitors. CDK5 has a simple bilobal kinase domain structure ( A). The crystal structure of CDK5 bound to p25 reveals how activation is achieved through stabilization of the activation loop ( B). This interaction mimics the interaction observed between cyclins and other CDKs and likewise stabilizes the αC-helix in an active conformation and positions key catalytic residues in the ATP-binding site, including the DFG motif in the “in” conformation ( B). Unlike other CDKs, CDK5 is not regulated by phosphorylation of the activation loop; instead, conformational stabilization by p25/p35 is sufficient for activity. Similar to other CDKs, CDK5 then preferentially phosphorylates substrates with a (S/T)PX(K/H/R) motif, , often found in cytoskeletal or synaptic proteins such as tau, dynamin, and MAP2, as determined from proteomics study in CDK5−/− mice. Further, the crystal structure of the CDK5-p25 complex reveals that the activator p25 contributes to substrate specificity. Indeed, a model based on this structure suggests that Glu240 of p25 may interact with a positively charged residue at the +3 position of the substrate peptide, highlighting the role of p25 in substrate recognition. The substrate binding site on CDK5 has been mapped by mutagenesis and corresponds to the typical interface observed in other CDKs. However, the exact structural basis for substrate specificity outside the minimal consensus motif remains unclear, and it is still debated how pathological conditions such as p25 overproduction alter CDK5 substrate targeting or localization. 
The structure of inhibitors bound to CDK5 are all typical of type I interactions, with the molecules occupying the ATP-binding site and with the “DFG-in” conformation typical of active kinases ( , C and D). The structure with roscovitine reveals why this class of ATP analog inhibitor have limited specificity, with the binding pocket being highly similar to other CDKs. However, recent structure-activity relationship and exploitation of the interaction with Lys33 in the binding pocket led to the discovery of compounds with selectivity over 200-fold with respect to other CDKs. The structure of CDK5 bound to one of these compounds show how they mediate more extensive contacts with Asp144 in the DFG motif, and more generally have a larger binding footprint in the active site ( E). The lead compound from this series, GFB-12811, exhibit excellent pharmacokinetics properties and could become an interesting chemical probe for testing CDK5 inhibition in neurodegenerative disorders.

## Glycogen synthase kinase 3
Glycogen synthase kinase 3 (GSK3), also referred to as tyrosine protein kinase inhibitor, is a serine/threonine kinase that was first identified in the 1980s and then characterized for its role in phosphorylating and inactivating glycogen synthase in rabbit skeletal muscle. Since then, there has been over 100 substrates identified and nearly 500 substrates pending validation, however, some argue those 100 do not meet the criteria to be considered bona fide targets. , , There are 2 known GSK3 isoforms, GSK3α and GSK3β, which are encoded by 2 distinct genes and share 98% sequence similarity in their kinase domains. GSK3 is involved in various physiological processes including cell metabolism, proliferation, and apoptosis. One notable substrate of GSK3 is PSEN1, a component of the γ-secretase complex that cleaves APP. GSK3β phosphorylates PSEN1 at Ser353 and Ser357, which inhibits APP cleavage and enhances Aβ production, a key factor in AD pathology. Additionally, PSEN1 interacts with GSK3β and tau protein, and activating mutations in PSEN1 also strengthens binding to GSK3β and increases its kinase activity toward tau. GSK3 activity is tightly regulated by upstream signaling pathways. For instance, insulin signaling leads to the activation of protein kinase B, which phosphorylates and inhibits GSK3, thereby modulating glycogen synthesis and glucose metabolism. 
Given its involvement in cellular processes closely associated with neurodegenerative diseases, GSK3 is a target for therapeutic interventions. Inhibitors of GSK3 are being explored for the treatment of conditions such as AD, bipolar disorder, and diabetes, aiming to modulate its activity and restore normal cellular functions.

## Role of glycogen synthase kinase 3 in Alzheimer disease
GSK3β is expressed at higher levels than GSK3a in the CNS and its expression increases with age. , Particularly overactivation of GSK disrupts neuronal growth, development and synaptic function. GSK3β is extensively studied in the context of AD because of its role in regulating tau phosphorylation, APP cleavage, neuroinflammation, and oxidative stress. In AD, GSK3β is chronically overactivated, and was found to increase phosphorylation of one of its major substrates, tau. Notably, tau phosphorylation by GSK3β requires prior priming phosphorylation by other kinases such as JNK, CDK5, or p38 MAPK. GSK3β phosphorylates that contribute to tau, specifically on Thr231, which induces detachment from microtubules, its aggregation, and NFT formation. In addition to its effect on tau, GSK3β enhances APP processing by upregulating β-secretase (BACE1) expression and activity thereby increasing Aβ production. It does so through NFK-β/p65 nuclear translocation and binding to the BACE1 promoter sites and modulates γ-secretase activity by direct interaction and regulation of PS1 activity and cellular localization. 
In addition to its effect on tau, GSK3β enhances APP processing by upregulating β-secretase (BACE1) expression and activity. , This leads to increased cleavage of APP along the amyloidogenic pathway, resulting in elevated production of Aβ peptides, which aggregate into extracellular amyloid plaques. Aβ itself may feedback to further activate GSK3β, creating a self-reinforcing loop of toxicity. This dual role of GSK3β in modulating both intracellular tau pathology and extracellular Aβ accumulation highlights its central contribution to AD progression and supports its prioritization as a therapeutic target.

## Role of glycogen synthase kinase 3 in Parkinson disease
Although most widely studied in AD, GSK3β has also been implicated in the pathogenesis of PD. Preclinical studies suggest that GSK3β contributes to neuronal degeneration by promoting mitochondrial dysfunction and oxidative stress, partially through downstream effects on complex I of the electron transport chain and increased production of reactive oxygen species. , Additionally, GSK3β activity has been shown to enhance α-synuclein aggregation and cytotoxicity, likely through phosphorylation at residues such as Ser129, which is associated with Lewy body formation. Inhibition of GSK3β has been found to reduce α-synuclein inclusions and protect dopaminergic neurons in rodent and cell-based PD models. , 
GSK3β is also involved in microglial activation and proinflammatory cytokine release, further contributing to dopaminergic neuron loss. Its regulation of NF-κB signaling, along with its crosstalk with Toll-like receptor pathways, positions GSK3β as a mediator of chronic neuroinflammation in PD. Inhibiting GSK3β in these contexts has been shown to attenuate both neuroinflammation and neuronal death. Challenges include achieving brain-selective, isoform-specific inhibition and avoiding systemic toxicity, as GSK3β is ubiquitously expressed and involved in essential metabolic and developmental pathways. Nonetheless, GSK3β remains a promising candidate for disease-modifying intervention in PD, particularly for strategies aiming to mitigate neurodegeneration, α-synuclein pathology, and chronic inflammation.

## Current status of therapeutic development
It is difficult to therapeutically target GSK3 in disease because the 2 isoforms have very similar substrate binding pockets despite their distinct physiological roles. This requires highly selective inhibitors that utilize the regions outside the nucleotide binding pocket. Thus, a type II or III inhibitor would be the most promising for selective inhibition in neurological disease since it would bind unique regions of GSK3β. Among current GSK3 inhibitors, lithium is currently the most studied and is known to inhibit GSK3 via competition with magnesium, GSK3’s cofactor. In transgenic AD models expressing pathogenic APP and PSEN1, lithium treatment decreased levels of pathogenic insoluble Aβ. Another study found patients who had received high doses of lithium did have an increase trend for developing dementia. 
Pilot clinical trials using lithium chloride, a weak and nonspecific GSK3β inhibitor already approved for bipolar disorder, have been conducted in patients with mild cognitive impairment and early-stage AD. At therapeutic concentrations, lithium chloride reduces GSK3β activity by approximately 25%, with no major adverse effects reported. , At therapeutic concentrations, lithium chloride reduces GSK3β activity by approximately 25%, with no major adverse effects reported. , In small-scale studies, lithium chloride treatment led to improved cognitive performance and a reduction in tau phosphorylation, suggesting that it may offer potential as a DMT for AD.
Several synthetic GSK3 inhibitors have also reached clinical development but were discontinued because of toxicity. AZD2858, a highly selective GSK3β inhibitor, effectively reduced tau phosphorylation and gliosis in preclinical models, but its in vivo use resulted in adverse effects that precluded chronic administration. Similarly, AZD1080, which showed promise in vitro and in animal models, was terminated during phase I trials because of nephrotoxicity. , 
To date, Tideglusib remains the only GSK3 inhibitor to advance to phase II clinical trials for both AD and progressive supranuclear palsy. Although it was well tolerated in 2 small trials and had no side effects/off target effects, it failed to show significant cognitive improvement despite neuroprotective effect in preclinical models, and no further development has been pursued. No pharmacokinetics data is available for this drug, but it is predicted to be a P-glycoprotein (P-gp) substrate and thus its CNS penetration is likely low. In PD, despite strong preclinical evidence implicating GSK3β in neurodegeneration, inflammation, and α-synuclein aggregation, clinical translation has lagged. A phase I trial evaluating a GSK3β inhibitor is currently active and recruiting, but no results have yet been posted. The absence of advanced trials highlights ongoing challenges in achieving brain-specific, isoform-selective inhibition without systemic toxicity; however, it also underscores a clear opportunity for therapeutic development in PD.

## Structural analysis
There are 88 structures of human GSK3β. The first structure of GSK3β was published in 2001, revealing a bilobal kinase domain ( A), an ATP-binding cleft and a short activation loop, consistent with its constitutive activity in cells. Unlike many kinases, GSK3β does not require phosphorylation of its activation loop for basal catalytic activity. This intrinsic activity is supported by an autophosphorylated Tyr216, but phosphorylation at this site is not strictly required for function. , The first structures of human GSK3β included phosphorylated Tyr216, , which interacts with a basic pocket located 15 Å away from the active site ( B). Phospho-Tyr216 stabilizes the activation loop but has only modest effect on catalytic activity. Activation of GSK3β is mostly achieved through the recruitment of substrates within its vicinity. GSK3β recognizes substrates through a distinctive priming-dependent mechanism, requiring a prephosphorylated residue located 4 amino acids C-terminal to the GSK3β target site. This phosphate docks into a basic pocket near the active site, aligning the substrate for efficient catalysis. Structural studies of mouse GSK3β with phosphorylated substrate peptides, such as β-catenin, confirm this mechanism ( C). Additional complexes, such as GSK3β bound to regulatory peptides such as axin ( B), illustrate how scaffold proteins position the kinase for substrate engagement and can increase catenin phosphorylation by several 1000-fold. Despite extensive structural and biochemical characterization, there are currently no structures of GSK3β in complex with full-length physiological substrates. 
There are multiple structures of GSK3β bound to ATP-competitive inhibitors, including several of clinical interest. The structure of GSK3β with CHIR99021 shows how a selective inhibitor binds deeply within the ATP-binding site, stabilized by interactions with the hinge region and the DFG-in conformation upstream of the activation loop ( D). However, CHIR99021 and other compounds such as tideglusib or SB-216763 inhibit many other kinases including CK1 and CDK2, and likely have poor brain penetration because of their large size and P-gp drug efflux. This prompted the development of BRD1652, which shows increased selectivity (>40-fold) for GSK3β and improved brain penetration, where it reduces tau phosphorylation. In parallel, another group discovered 2-(anilino)pyrimidine-4-carboxamides as potent and highly selective inhibitors of both GSK3β and GSK3α, as well as imidazo[1,2-b]pyridazines as GSK3β inhibitors. , Both classes have good brain penetration and can reduce tau phosphorylation. The structure of one of those compounds show typical type I interactions with the hinge region and with the DFG motif in the “in” conformation ( E). Using computational screening approaches to identify novel scaffolds, type II inhibitors of GSK3β and GSK3α have been developed, but their crystal structures have not been solved and it is unclear if these inhibitors genuinely induce the DFG-out conformation. , However, they do reduce tau phosphorylation with nanomolar potency. Other allosteric inhibitors have been reported, but they have poor potencies and further work is required to create high potency allosteric inhibitor that may not only target the ATP-binding site, but perhaps also substrate engagement.

## c-Jun N-terminal kinase
JNK is a serine/threonine kinase that was initially discovered for its ability to phosphorylate the N-terminus of the transcription factor c-Jun. JNK belongs to the MAPK family, which regulates various cellular signaling pathways linked to cell survival and stress responses. In the nervous system, JNK has specific roles in axon guidance, synaptic plasticity, and microtubule dynamics, but its overactivation is linked to neurodegenerative diseases. Out of the 3 JNK isoforms, JNK1 (MAPK8) and JNK2 (MAPK9) are ubiquitously expressed in the body, however, JNK3 (MAPK10) is selectively expressed in the brain, heart and testis and plays a dominant role in neurodegeneration. JNK is activated via dual phosphorylation on a conserved Thr-Pro-Tyr (T-X-Y) motif in its activation loop by MAP2Ks, with MKK4 preferentially phosphorylating the tyrosine and MKK7 the threonine residue. These MAP2Ks are also in turn phosphorylated by MAP3Ks including MEKK1-4, mixed lineage kinase, ASK1, and TAK1. Activated JNK phosphorylates a wide range of substrates, most notably c-Jun, tau, p53, and ATF2, which collectively regulate stress responses, apoptosis, and transcriptional reprogramming. Although c-Jun and p53 are well established mediators of JNK-induced neuronal apoptosis, ATF2 phosphorylation is also functionally relevant, as it can modulate inflammatory gene expression in microglia and has been implicated in neuronal stress responses in both AD and PD models. However, its role is less extensively studied compared with c-Jun or tau.
Once activated, JNK translocates to the nucleus, where it phosphorylates c-Jun and other transcription factors, modulating gene expression involved in cell survival, cell death, and neuroinflammation. Mitochondrial recruitment of JNK has also been reported through scaffold proteins such as Sab. The recruitment of activated JNK to mitochondria leads to the phosphorylation of outer membrane proteins including Sab as well as Bcl-2 and BH3 proteins, which contribute to mitochondrial dysfunction by increasing reactive oxygen species generation, inhibiting respiration and inducing apoptosis. , 
1

## Role of c-Jun N-terminal kinase in Alzheimer disease
In AD, JNK is hyperactivated in response to oxidative stress, Aβ accumulation, and neuroinflammation, contributing to tau hyperphosphorylation, NFT formation, and neuronal apoptosis. One of the key roles of JNK in AD is its phosphorylation of tau at multiple residues, including Thr181, Ser202, Thr205, promoting tau aggregation and disrupting microtubule stability. JNK also induces phosphorylation of tau indirectly by activation of other kinases such as GSK3b, for example, by phosphorylating its repressor, protein kinase B. JNK also influences APP processing and Aβ production. Phosphorylation of APP at Thr668 by JNK is required for cleavage by BACE1 and γ-secretase. Thus, JNK hyperactivation leads to increased Aβ generation and plaque formation. JNK-mediated phosphorylation of BACE1 enhances its stability, further increasing amyloidogenic processing. Additionally, JNK activation can be sustained by Aβ, creating a feed-forward loop that exacerbates neurotoxicity. One prevailing hypothesis is that phospho-JNK is linked to the amount of Aβ, as administration of Aβ in the brain of mice increased the amount of phospho-JNK. There is also colocalization of JNK and Aβ. JNK can mediate the phosphorylation of Bim and Bax, 2 proapoptotic proteins. Bax forms a complex with Bcl-2, but JNK-mediated phosphorylation of Bcl-2 causes complex dissociation and freeing Bax to oligomerize and form a pore at the outer mitochondrial membrane (OMM), leading to cytochrome C release and caspase activation. Inhibiting JNK in mice through an inhibitor was shown decrease the MPTP-induced dissociation of Bax from the Bcl-2/Bax complex.

## Role of c-Jun N-terminal kinase in Parkinson disease
In PD, JNK is implicated in dopaminergic neuron loss, α-synuclein aggregation, and mitochondrial dysfunction. JNK3 is the predominant isoform activated in PD and is required for MPTP-induced neuronal death. One of JNK’s major pathological effects is indirectly enhancing α-synuclein aggregation by promoting oxidative stress and mitochondrial dysfunction. Although phosphorylation of α-synuclein at Tyr39 has been proposed as a pathogenic modification, this site is more strongly linked to kinases such as c-Abl and not definitively to JNK. In PD models, such as the MPTP-induced mouse model, JNK2/3 phosphorylates ATF2 at threonine 69 (Thr69) within dopaminergic neurons of the substantia nigra. This phosphorylation event is associated with increased neuronal death. Importantly, mice with dopaminergic neuron-specific deletion of ATF2 exhibit marked resistance to MPTP-induced neurodegeneration, underscoring the detrimental role of JNK-mediated ATF2 activation in PD pathogenesis. Additionally, JNK activation enhances NF-κB-mediated transcription of proinflammatory cytokines, contributing to chronic neuroinflammation in PD. JNK also regulates dopaminergic neuronal apoptosis through mitochondrial pathways through an increase of the Bax to Bcl-2 ratio, as explained in the section above. Finally, preventing JNK recruitment to mitochondria via Sab reduces neurodegeneration induced by 6-hydroxydopamine, which further makes the cases that inhibiting JNK might be a valuable therapeutic strategy.

## Current status of therapeutic development
Given its role in apoptosis, tau hyperphosphorylation, and neuroinflammation, JNK inhibitors have been explored as potential treatments for AD and PD. In AD, JNK inhibition reduces tau phosphorylation, NFT formation, and Aβ-induced toxicity. SP600125, a reversible ATP-competitive JNK inhibitor, has neuroprotective effects in AD APPswe/PS1dE9 mice model by reducing tau pathology and improving behavioral measures of cognitive deficits. Another compound, CC-930, reduced neuroinflammation and apoptosis in preclinical studies. In PD, JNK inhibition protects dopaminergic neurons from apoptosis and decreases α-synuclein aggregation. SR-3306, a brain-penetrant pan-JNK inhibitor, has been tested in preclinical PD models, and was able to reduce dopaminergic neuron loss and oxidative stress. Despite strong preclinical efficacy, no JNK inhibitors have yet advanced to human clinical trials in neurodegenerative disease, possibly because of concerns about isoform selectivity and long-term safety.

## Structural analysis
JNK proteins adopt the canonical bilobal kinase fold, with an N-terminal lobe containing the glycine-rich loop and ATP-binding site, and a C-terminal lobe that houses the substrate-binding elements and catalytic machinery ( A). Embedded within the activation loop of the C-lobe is a Thr-Pro-Tyr (T-X-Y) motif, a hallmark of MAP kinases, which must be dually phosphorylated by MAP2Ks (MKK4 and MKK7) to induce full catalytic activity. Phosphorylation of both threonine and tyrosine residues within this flexible segment triggers conformational changes that align the catalytic residues and stabilize the active conformation. The first crystal structure of unphosphorylated JNK3 bound to a nonhydrolyzable ATP analog shows that the kinase adopts an active configuration in the active site (DFG-in), with electron density visible for the activation loop including the TPY motif ( B) but has higher B factors indicating flexibility. Because there is no crystal structure of phosphorylated JNK, we have used AlphaFold 3 to generate a model of JNK3 phosphorylated at Thr221 and Tyr223 and bound to ATP and a docking peptide from JIP1 ( C). The high-confidence model (ipTM = 0.8) shows that the activation loop is stabilized by interactions between phospho-Thr221 and Arg188 and between phospho-Tyr223 and Arg227, consistent with homologous structures of phosphorylated ERK2. Then, JNK recognizes substrates through interactions involving short linear motifs that bind to docking sites adjacent to the nucleotide binding site ( C). , These D-motifs, found on substrates and scaffold proteins, consist of a basic residue-rich region followed by hydrophobic residues. This motif binds a complementary groove on JNK’s C-lobe surface, increasing binding affinity and positioning the phosphorylation site within reach of the catalytic cleft, which contributes to substrate selectivity and binding kinetics. The structures of JNK3 or JNK1 in complex with D-motifs from the scaffold proteins JIP1, , NFAT4, ATF2, and SAB show that the peptides make extensive contacts within the docking groove. Intriguingly, these structures also show that the docking peptides stabilize an autoinhibited conformation, with the activation loop forming a helix that bind into the nucleotide-binding site. It is proposed that this peptide-induced autoinhibition is necessary to prevent phosphorylation of the substrate in the absence of activation loop phosphorylation, which makes the active site accessible. 
Pharmacological inhibition of JNK has largely relied on ATP-competitive inhibitors. There are many experimental structures of JNK kinases, including 38 of JNK1, 5 of JNK2, and 60 of JNK3, most of which are bound to pharmacological inhibitors. A wide number of scaffolds have been reported to inhibit JNK kinases, including imidazole-pyrimidines, anthrapyrazolones including the widely used SP600125, 6-anilinoindazoles, benzothiazol-2-yl, quinolines, and piperazine amides. These compounds inhibit JNK1/2/3 with nanomolar potencies but are not highly selective and inhibit other kinases. For example, SP600125 inhibits at least 13 other kinases (PKD1, CHK2, CK1, Aurora B, etc) with potencies similar or better than for JNK kinases. , To address this, various strategies have been deployed to discover more selective JNK inhibitors. The aminopurine CC-930 inhibits JNK3 with IC50 of 6 nM, but still inhibits ERK1 and p38α with IC50 of 0.5 and 3.4 μM, respectively. The structure of CC-930 bound to JNK3 reveals a classical type I mode of inhibition, with the molecule occupying the ATP binding site in the “DFG-in” configuration ( D). To overcome the selectivity issue stemming from the highly conserved active site of MAP kinases, Zhang et al developed irreversible covalent inhibitors targeting a cysteine residue that is conserved in all JNK kinases and not found at the same position in other kinases. The structure of one such covalent inhibitor bound to JNK3 shows that it binds to the ATP binding site with the warhead extending beyond to form a covalent bond with the thiol group of Cys154 ( E). This strategy was extended further to develop subnanomolar covalent inhibitors of JNK3 with high selectivity, starting from dual JNK/p38 inhibitor scaffolds. More recent work includes the discovery of reversible inhibitor that are highly selective for the JNK3 isotype versus JNK1. , These JNK3-selective inhibitors display good BBB penetration, making them promising candidates for neurodegenerative therapy.

## Leucine-rich repeat kinase 2
LRRK2 is a large 286 kDa protein that has both serine/threonine kinase activity and GTPase activity. The protein consists of an N-terminus containing several domains with repeat regions called the Armadillo (ARM), Ankyrin, and Leucine-rich repeat, followed by GTPase, kinase, and WD40 domains ( A). Missense dominant mutations in this protein cause familial late-onset PD, which was discovered by genome-wide linkage analysis, which led to the designation of the PARK8 locus, which was later associated with the protein LRRK2. , LRRK2 is primarily involved in endolysosomal trafficking via phosphorylation of Rab GTPases as well as interactions with microtubules and membranes. , , , The best characterized substrates of LRRK2’s kinase in this context are the Rab GTPases Rab8a and Rab10, but LRRK2 can also phosphorylate Rab35 and Rab43. Rab10 facilitates the transport of cargo from the Golgi apparatus to the plasma membrane. Upon phosphorylation of Thr73 in Rab10 by LRRK2, Rab10’s affinity for its GDP dissociation inhibitors decreases, leading to altered membrane association and vesicular trafficking. Rab29 recruits LRRK2 to the trans-Golgi network and endosomal membranes, thus facilitating the phosphorylation of downstream substrates at these sites, notably by interacting with the Golgi-associated retrograde protein complex, which regulates membrane fusion. LRRK2 orchestrates tubulation of lysosomes, which leads to the formation of vesicles carrying cargo to be degraded. Rab phosphorylation by LRRK2 also perturbs formation of cilia, which are microtubule-containing organelles implicated in cellular signaling and immunity. , This is likely linked to LRRK2’s ability to directly bind microtubules through oligomerization of the ROC-COR-kinase-WD40 domains, which effectively blocks the transport of cargo on microtubules. , An additional level of regulation is conferred by binding to Rab12 via the ARM domain of LRRK2. , Rab12 is recruited to damaged lysosomes and enables the phosphorylation of Rab10 at these sites. A recent study also found that recruitment and activation of LRRK2 on damaged lysosomes is dependent on the binding of GABAA receptor protein (GABARAP), another mediator of intracellular trafficking and autophagy. Rab12 also inhibits ciliogenesis in the brain by promoting phosphorylation of Rab10, which in turn leads to the recruitment of the protein RILPL1 to centrosomes, which blocks ciliogenesis. RILPL1 binds to the mother centriole, from where the basal body forms and from which stems the cilium. Overall, these studies all point to LRRK2 activation being restricted to specific set of conditions linking the microtubules/cilia network to impaired endolysosomal vesicles, where it phosphorylates Rab10 and facilitates the repair of the vesicle and its cargo. 
Although interactions with Rab GTPases, membranes, and microtubules regulate where LRRK2’s kinase is activated, it does not explain how it is activated. LRRK2 is a member of a superfamily of proteins called ROCO, which includes LRRK1 and LRRK2 in vertebrates as well as other related proteins in nematodes and arthropods. The defining feature of this family is a Ras-related GTPase or Ras of complex proteins (ROC) domain followed by tandem C-terminal of ROC (COR) domains. Several studies have shown that binding of GTP to the ROC domain stimulates the kinase activity of LRRK1 and LRRK2 in an allosteric manner. , , , The activity of the kinase thus depends on whether the ROC domain is bound to GTP (“on” state) or GDP (“off” state). As observed in other GTPases, LRRK2 uses GTPase activating factors (GAPs) such as ArfGAP1 and guanine nucleotide exchange factors such as CalDAG-GEFI to mediate this conversion between the 2 states, but it has also been proposed that these activities are intrinsic to LRRK2 and regulated by interactions with microtubules and oligomerization. , , , LRRK2 also undergoes autophosphorylation, which takes place intramolecularly, but yet depends on dimerization. The main autophosphorylation site is Ser1292, which is required for its association with microtubules and is a proxy of the overall activation of LRRK2. Other phosphorylation sites in LRRK2 are found at Ser910 and Ser935 and induce the binding to 14-3-3 proteins, a scaffold protein family that regulates LRRK2’s cytoplasmic localization. These 2 sites are phosphorylated by the IkappaB kinase family as well as, in a LRRK2-kinase-dependent manner. , Thus, LRRK2 is a highly regulated kinase that requires GTP binding to the ROC-COR domain, upstream phosphorylation, and autophosphorylation to enable substrate phosphorylation at specific sites, consistent with a role as an integrator or coincidence detector for events in endolysosomal trafficking.

## Role of Leucine-rich repeat kinase 2 in Parkinson disease
There are 7 genetic variants in LRRK2 that are considered “definitely pathogenic” and cause familial PD. In addition, genome-wide association studies find many single-nucleotide polymorphisms at the LRRK2 locus, which is among the most significant, along with SNCA, GBA1, and MCCC1/TMEM175. Familial mutations are dominant, account for 5% to 40% of all PD cases in different populations and have incomplete penetrance that depends on age and ancestry. For instance, the G2019S mutation has a penetrance of 17% to 34% in Ashkenazi Jewish populations by the age of 80 years. Mutations typically lead to the overactivation of LRRK2’s kinase activity, as evidenced by an increase in Rab10 phosphorylation and autophosphorylation at Ser1292 in different cell models. The most common pathogenic variants are found in the kinase and GTPase domains. The G2019S mutation, which is the most frequent LRRK2-associated PD mutation, is in the kinase domain and is part of the “DYG” loop that coordinates the ATP-Mg2+ essential for catalysis. Other pathogenic mutations include R1441C/H/G in the ROC GTPase domain, Y1699C in the COR-B domain, and I2020T located immediately after the “DYG” motif in the kinase domain. Mutations in the ROC-COR domains decrease its GTPase activity and prolong the active state of LRRK2, , whereby G2019S/I2020T directly increase the basal and stimulated kinase activity. , , 
Although LRRK2 mutations have been shown to modulate several pathways implicated in PD, an emerging consensus is that these mutations increase inflammation and modulate immunity. Evidence includes LRRK2 variant N2081D, which increases susceptibility to Crohn’s disease, an inflammatory bowel disease, variants that increase inflammatory responses to leprosy infections, and high levels of LRRK2 in circulating and tissue immune cells that modulate response to microbial infections. , Furthermore, a mouse model of LRRK2-G2019S results in increased phosphorylation of MAPK kinases, such as MKK4, which are associated with inflammation, ultimately activating neuronal death signaling pathways. Through MKK activation, LRRK2 modulates the JNK-c-Jun and p38 MAPK pathways, which are implicated in the progressive degeneration of SNpc dopaminergic neurons. In macrophages, LRRK2 can also be activated endogenously by STimulator of INterferon Genes (STING), a mediator of the innate immune response, via GABARAP binding on damaged lysosome. Aberrant activation of LRRK2 via mutations can therefore create an adverse immune response that leads to cell death.
LRRK2 mutations also aggravate other molecular responses associated with PD pathology. For instance, LRRK2-G2019S interferes with chaperone-mediated autophagy and exacerbates the toxicity induced by the α-synuclein A53T familial PD mutation. However, although α-synuclein aggregates (Lewy bodies) are a hallmark of idiopathic PD, only 50% to 80% of patients with LRRK2 PD display this pathology. On the other hand, most LRRK2 carriers display tau pathology, and animal models of LRRK2 PD, such as mice overexpressing LRRK2-G2019S or R1441C, show tau hyperphosphorylation associated with tauopathy. , Given that tau is associated with microtubules, it is not surprising that LRRK2 mutations affects the propensity of this protein to be phosphorylated and aggregates. Active LRRK2 inhibits the motility of kinesin and dynein on microtubules and facilitates the tubulation and sorting of ruptured lysosomes, which is also taking place on microtubules. , Furthermore, pathogenic LRRK2 mutations decrease the probability of ciliation upstream of the tau tubulin kinase 2 via Rab10 phosphorylation. Pathogenic mutations in Lrrk2 (R1441C) or Pink1 (knock out) in mice induce defects in ciliogenesis in the brain, which interferes with Hedgehog signaling and production of glial-derived neurotrophic factors that are neuroprotective. 
Finally, LRRK2 associates with other proteins implicated in PD. The mutation D620N in VPS35, a core component of the retromer complex, causes autosomal dominant PD. The mutation perturbs lysosomal proteins and causes Rab10 hyperphosphorylation by LRRK2, which leads to the formation of a complex of RILPL1 and TMEM55B on lysosomes. This leads to the degradation of RILPL1 and causes a decrease in ciliogenesis that can be reversed by inhibition of LRRK2 kinase activity. Genome-wide association studies also identified 2 susceptibility loci, RAB29 and SH3GL2, which interact with LRRK2. As explained above, the RAB29 protein, also known as RAB7L1, binds directly to LRRK2 and recruits it to endolysosomal membranes. SH3GL2 encodes Endophilin A1, a BAR-SH3 protein implicated in the endolysosomal trafficking of synaptic vesicles. LRRK2 was reported to phosphorylate Endophilin A1 at Ser75 in the lipid-binding BAR domain, which modulates macroautophagy in synaptic terminals coupled to calcium and electrophysiological activity. , The mutation S71R in RAB32 is a risk factor for PD and was reported to enhance the activity of LRRK2 via binding to the N-terminal domains of LRRK2, which again leads to perturbations of endolysosomal trafficking. Together, these studies strongly suggest that hyperactivation of LRRK2, via mutations in LRRK2 or other associated proteins, decreases endolysosomal trafficking, reduces ciliogenesis, and induces tau aggregation, which together contribute to neurodegeneration.

## Current status of therapeutic development
Intensive efforts are being deployed to identify inhibitors of LRRK2 that dampen its hyperactivity, which is strongly associated with PD pathology. Denali Therapeutics and Biogen together have launched clinical trials for the LRRK2 inhibitor BIIB122 (DNL151), last updated in June 2023. A phase III trial for BIIB122 was initiated in September 2022 but was terminated by Biogen because of the complexity of the study. However, the phase II clinical trial is actively recruiting patients at early-stage PD. DNL151 and the related compound DNL201 (previously named GNE-0877) are brain-penetrant, type I ATP-competitive inhibitors. , DNL201 inhibits LRRK2 Ser935/Ser1292 and Rab10 Thr73 phosphorylation in cells with IC50 in the range 10-60 nM, and engage these targets in vivo as assessed in blood, urine, and peripheral blood mononuclear cell. These drugs were tested in both healthy individuals and patients with PD, and found to be generally well tolerated with no serious adverse effect reported, but some mild events of hypotension were observed in the treatment group. Only 4 patients who did experience hypotension had pre-existing conditions of hypotension or orthostatic hypotension since it is an autonomic symptom of PD, and the hypotension of the 2 other patients resolved while continuing the drug study. Other type I LRRK2 inhibitors that are structurally unrelated to DNL201 include GNE-7915, PFE-360, and MLi-2, which potently reduce LRRK2 autophosphorylation in macaque brain, lung, and peripheral blood mononuclear cell. These inhibitors, however, cause on-target histopathologic changes in lungs that raised safety concerns, but those changes are reversible and do not induce significant lung deficits. Finally, BIIB094 is an antisense oligonucleotide against LRRK2 administered intrathecally that is currently in phase I clinical trial. Antisense oligonucleotides developed by Biogen effectively knockdown LRRK2 in mouse peritoneal macrophages and reduce antigen presentation via lysosomal tubule formation. Thus, LRRK2 inhibition is a promising therapeutic for PD, but multiple challenges remain, notably regarding patient stratification (idiopathic vs LRRK2 mutations carrier) safety concerns related to on-target toxicity, and peripheral versus central nervous system targeting.

## Structural analysis
The first structure of the ROC-COR-kinase-WD40 (RCKW) domains from human LRRK2 (a.a. 1327–2527) was solved by cryo-EM and revealed extensive interactions between the kinase and the COR-A/B and WD40 domains ( B). The ROC domain is bound to GDP and interacts with the COR-B domain via Arg1441 and Tyr1699, which are 2 PD mutation sites, thus explaining how allosteric interactions with the ROC-COR is critical to control LRRK2’s kinase activity. The WD40 domain, which form dimers via a β-propeller subdomain that regulates LRRK2 activity, interacts with the kinase domain via a long C-terminal helix that plays a critical role in regulating domain crosstalks and autophosphorylation. Integrative modeling of the RCKW domains inside a low-resolution electron microscopy map of a LRRK2 filament decorating a microtubule shows that the filament is formed via homotypic contacts between WD40 domains as well as interactions between the COR domains. However, the structure can only be fitted with the kinase domain in the “closed” conformation, because the “open” conformation observed in the cryo-EM structure of unliganded LRRK2 leads to steric clashes between the COR domains from 2 neighboring LRRK2 molecules. The “closed” conformation corresponds to the active, “DFG-in” conformation, and indeed favors microtubule binding and inhibition of the motility of motor proteins dynein and kinesin. Later structural work on full-length LRRK2 found that RAB29 binds to the N-terminal ARM domain ( C). The RAB29-LRRK2 complex was solved in different oligomeric states (monomer, dimer, and tetramer), which show how LRRK2 oligomers can interact with multiple membrane-anchored RAB29 molecules. The tetramer contains 2 central and 2 peripheral copies, which adopts distinct conformations in the RCKW domains. The central copies, which are in proximity to the membrane, adopt a “closed” ATP-bound active conformation, which is compatible with the LRRK2 filaments bound to microtubules. This work shows how multiple RAB29 molecules can activate LRRK2 via oligomerization at the membrane. The structure of the RAB12-LRRK2 complex shows that RAB12 also binds to the ARM domain ( C), but at a distinct, nonoverlapping position with respect to RAB29. AlphaFold modeling shows that GABARAP also binds to the ARM domain, highlighting the essential role of the LRRK2 N-terminus in regulating its localization and activation.
Structural work also reveals how different types of inhibitors have distinct outcomes on their activities. The structures of LRRK2-RCKW (WT, G2019S, and I2020T variants) bound to MLi-2 (type I) and GZD-824 (type II) were solved by cryo-EM and show key differences in the conformation of the kinase domain. The structure bound to MLi-2 is the “closed” active form of the kinase, where the “DYG” loop (or “DYS” for G2019S) adopts the “in” conformation ( D). This same conformation was also observed in the central copies of the LRRK2 tetramer bound to ATP as well as bound to the type I inhibitor DNL201. The structures also explain why MLi-2 displays a lower affinity for G2019S compared with the WT and I2020T variants, as the G2019S variant induces the loss of an interaction between MLi-2 and His1998 in LRRK2. By contrast, the structure bound to GZD-824 shows an “open” inactive form of the kinase, where the “DYG” loop adopts the “out” conformation ( E). Comparison of structures of RCKW and full-length LRRK2 bound to these inhibitors show they induce different domain crosstalks and global conformations, which in turn explain how these inhibitors modulate LRRK2’s properties. Indeed, previous work showed that type I LRRK2 inhibitors mimic the active conformation and thus reduce the motility of dynein and kinesin on microtubules, whereas type II inhibitors do not. In 2024, the structures of WT LRRK2-RCKW domains bound to 2 type I inhibitors, LRRK2-IN-1 and GNE-7915, and 3 type II inhibitors rebastinib, ponatinib, and GZD-824 were also solved by cryo-EM and reveal highly conformations to those reported previously. Thus, although type I inhibitors do reduce Rab10 phosphorylation, they may be undesirable as they induce conformations similar to the active, pathogenic conformation. Future work should aim to develop potent and selective type II LRRK2 inhibitors, as inhibitors such as GZD-824 are not selective and were designed to target tyrosine kinases.

## PTEN-induced putative kinase 1
PTEN-induced putative kinase 1 (PINK1) is a serine/threonine kinase that plays a pivotal role in initiating a mitochondrial quality control pathway. First discovered in the context of ovarian cancer cells, loss of function mutations in PINK1 were found to cause familial early-onset PD. Under physiological conditions, PINK1’s N-terminal mitochondrial targeting sequence directs it to the OMM where it inserts into the translocase of the outer membrane (TOM) complex. , , , , It then undergoes further import into the inner mitochondrial membrane (IMM) via the translocase of the inner membrane (TIM) complex. Once inserted in the IMM, PINK1 is cleaved by the mitochondrial processing peptidase in the matrix and the presenilin-associated rhomboid-like protease in the IMM. , , This cleavage generates a truncated form of PINK1 that retro-translocates back into the cytosol, where it is subsequently ubiquitinated and degraded by the proteasome. , This rapid turnover ensures that functional, healthy mitochondria do not accumulate PINK1. However, in response to mitochondrial damage, which can be triggered by various stressors such as loss of membrane potential (depolarization), reactive oxygen species, or accumulation of unfolded proteins, PINK1’s import into the IMM and subsequent cleavage are inhibited. , , This leads to the stabilization and accumulation of full-length PINK1 on the OMM, primarily associated with the TOM complex. The accumulated PINK1 molecules then undergo dimerization and transautophosphorylation at Ser228 within their kinase domain. , , This autophosphorylation event induces a conformational change in PINK1’s insert-3, which is crucial for its activation and subsequent interaction with its downstream substrates ubiquitin and Parkin. , Activated PINK1 then phosphorylates ubiquitin (Ub) at Ser65 on chains that are already tethered to nearby OMM proteins. These chains of phosphorylated ubiquitin (pUb) act as a specific “eat-me” signal, recruiting the E3 ubiquitin ligase Parkin to the damaged mitochondrial surface. Upon recruitment, Parkin itself becomes phosphorylated by PINK1 at Ser65 on its ubiquitin-like domain, which is essential for Parkin’s full activation. , , , Activated Parkin then ubiquitinates a plethora of OMM proteins, thus expanding the pool of ubiquitinated substrates available for PINK1 phosphorylation. , This creates a powerful feed-forward loop, leading to the massive decoration of the damaged mitochondria with poly-pUb chains, which ultimately tags them for degradation via selective autophagy, a process known as mitophagy, or formation of mitochondria-derived vesicles that carry damaged protein cargo directly to lysosomes. , This intricate regulatory cascade ensures that only terminally damaged mitochondria are efficiently removed from the cell, maintaining overall mitochondrial health and preventing the accumulation of dysfunctional organelles.

## Role of PTEN-induced putative kinase 1 in Parkinson disease
Loss-of-function mutations in the PINK1 gene are a well established cause of autosomal recessive early-onset PD. Over 100 pathogenic mutations, including more than 30 missense variants, have been identified, leading to either reduced PINK1 protein levels, impaired mitochondrial localization, or loss of kinase activity. , , The clinical manifestation of PINK1-associated PD typically includes bradykinesia, rigidity, and tremor, with an earlier age of onset and a slower disease progression. At the cellular level, these mutations disrupt the crucial mitochondrial quality control pathway. When PINK1 function is compromised, damaged mitochondria are no longer efficiently tagged with pUb and, consequently, fail to recruit and activate Parkin. This leads to a failure in mitochondrial quality control, resulting in the accumulation of dysfunctional mitochondria proteins. The current understanding in the field indicates that disruption of the Parkin/PINK1 pathways leads to a reduced turnover of mitochondrial proteins especially under stress conditions. , Given their unique electrical properties and extensive axonal arborization, SNpc dopaminergic neurons experience higher levels of basal mitochondrial stress, rendering them particularly vulnerable to these defects in mitochondrial quality control. , Although the precise mechanisms by which mitochondrial impairments instigate neurodegeneration are still being elucidated, inflammatory responses involving both innate and adaptive immunity are likely implicated. , Studies in Drosophila melanogaster with pink1 or parkin deletions or loss-of-function mutations faithfully recapitulate key features of PD, including neuron loss, motor deficits, mitochondrial abnormalities, and innate immune responses. , , Although the mouse Pink1-knockout exhibits no neurodegeneration under normal conditions, gut infections or mitochondrial DNA damage can induce selective neurodegeneration in these animals following an aberrant T-cell response. ,

## Current status of therapeutic development
Despite the clear genetic and mechanistic link between PINK1 dysfunction and PD, the development of direct therapeutic modulators for PINK1 remains largely in preclinical stages, with no PINK1 activators having advanced to late-stage clinical trials. In 2013, kinetin was reported to activate PINK1-dependent Parkin recruitment to mitochondria, suggesting it could restore mitophagy in cellular models. The model proposed involved the metabolism of kinetin into kinetin triphosphate (KTP) in cells, which would be used as a neo-substrate and phosphate donor by PINK1 to phosphorylate substrates with a lower Km than ATP. Another group synthesized a ProTide derivative of kinetin riboside, a prodrug that bypasses glycosylation and phosphorylation steps and thus should enhance PINK1 activity in cells. However, these compounds induced mild activation in cells, and subsequent structural analyses indicated that kinetin could not directly bind to the nucleotide-binding pocket of PINK1 because of steric clashes in the nucleotide binding pocket, casting doubt on a direct activation mechanism. Starting from kinetin, the company Mitokinin developed a PINK1 activator called MTK458, a brain-penetrant small molecule that stimulate PINK1 activity in cellular assays and mice. In the absence of mitochondrial depolarization, MTK458 is unable to activate PINK1, but in the presence of low doses of mitochondrial uncouplers such as carbonyl cyanide m-chlorophenyl hydrazone or antimycin/oligomycin A, MTK458 induces PINK1 stabilization and generation of pUb in a dose-dependent manner (IC50 = 500 nM). Furthermore, the compound rescues proteotoxicity induced by misfolded proteins in cells and ameliorates α-synuclein pathology induced by injection of preformed fibrils in mouse brain. Although MTK458 is a promising compound, its exact binding site and molecular mechanism remain unclear. Recent work suggests that MTK458 may act by lowering the cell’s threshold for mitochondrial damage detection through the induction of stress. 
Mitokinin is now part of AbbVie and the PINK1 activator’s derivative ABBV-1088 is undergoing a phase I trial. This trial was terminated in October of 2025, for which the reason remains undisclosed. On the other hand, the possibility of inhibiting PINK1 is also being explored as a tool for probing its biology. Recently, our group identified PRT062607 as an inhibitor of PINK1 with an IC50 in the low micromolar range (1–3 μM). Although this compound inhibits tyrosine kinases such as SYK with 500 to 1000 fold greater potency, it can serve as a template to make more potent and selective PINK1 inhibitors. These compounds could serve as a valuable chemical probe to further dissect PINK1’s role following acute inhibition. For instance, PRT062607 has been used to demonstrate that isoginkgetin, a bioflavonoid that induces mitophagy, increases generation of pUb via PINK1. Furthermore, the concept of “paradoxical agonism,” where an inhibitor might induce a desired active conformation or stabilization that overcomes enzymatic inhibition, as observed for rapidely accelerated fibrosarcoma (RAF) inhibitors, has not yet been demonstrated for PINK1 but could prove useful.

## Structural analysis
PINK1’s architecture provides key insights into its unique regulatory mechanisms and substrate specificity. The kinase consists of a canonical bilobal kinase domain, characteristic of most protein kinases, sandwiched between a N-terminal and C-terminal extensions (NTE-CTE), which play a role in modulating its stability and activity ( A). PINK1 also harbors an atypical N-terminal mitochondrial targeting sequence, a transmembrane helix, and 3 unique inserts (Insert 1, 2, and 3) within its kinase domain. These additional elements are critical for its specific mitochondrial localization and activation mechanism. The first structures of PINK1 were determined using insect orthologs that are more stable and express well in Escherichia coli, specifically from the beetle Tribolium castaneum and the common louse Pediculus humanus corporis. , These structures, as well as our own biophysical work, showed that autophosphorylation at Ser228 (human numbering) and Insert 3 are necessary for the phosphorylation of ubiquitin and Parkin. The structure of PhPINK1 in complex with ubiquitin showed how this phosphorylation event induces conformational rearrangements, particularly in the αC helix and Insert 3 within the kinase domain, which are necessary to prime PINK1 for its subsequent phosphorylation of ubiquitin and Parkin ( B). PINK1’s substrate specificity is remarkably narrow; to date, ubiquitin and Parkin are the only confirmed endogenous substrates in cells, underscoring its highly specialized role in mitochondrial turnover. Our work also showed how PINK1 autophosphorylation at Ser228 takes place exclusively in trans, which implies that it must oligomerize to get activated, in line with previous observations. , , We next solved the crystal structure of the entire cytosolic domain of PINK1, which revealed the dimer conducive to autophosphorylation. The structure was solved in both the apo state and bound to the ATP analog AMP-PNP, which hydrolyzed in situ and transferred the phosphate to the acceptor serine, thus providing a unique snapshot of the kinase reaction immediately after the phosphate transfer ( C). Our structures, as well as the cryo-EM structure of another insect PINK1 ortholog, shows how proximal and distal interactions are critical to maintain the dimer in place and position Ser228 for autophosphorylation, and revealed the critical role played by Insert 2 in maintaining this interaction. This work also showed that the NTE-CTE interact with each other and bind the C-lobe of the kinase domain, and we and others found that this interaction was critical for PINK1 to form a complex with TOM, a prerequisite step for the stabilization and activation of PINK1 by autophosphorylation. 
Subsequent proteomics and biochemical studies found that PINK1 forms a complex not only with TOM, but also with the TIM23 complex to form an 800 kDa complex, and that the NTE-CTE module binds directly to TOM20. , , Disruption of either TOM20 or TIM23 interactions, either by gene disruption and with PD mutations in PINK1, is sufficient to prevent PINK1 stabilization and activation and abrogation of mitophagy. The next breakthrough in 2025 was the reporting of the first cryo-EM structure of full-length human PINK1 bound to the TOM complex. The structure comprises a PINK1 dimer spanning 2 TOM complexes joined via a voltage-dependent anion channel 2 dimer in the outer membrane ( D). The PINK1 dimer is similar to the dimer structures of insect PINK1 and shows that it binds TOM20 via the NTE-CTE as predicted by AlphaFold in our previous work. The structure also reveals key interactions of the segment preceding the NTE inside the TOM40 pore, providing for the first time a snapshot of how nuclear-encoded mitochondrial proteins are imported. The structure raises interesting questions about the activation mechanism of PINK1. For instance, the structure shows several disulfide bonds, notably between the 2 Cys166 at the PINK1 dimer interface. However, this disulfide bond cannot be permanent, because this dimer would need to dissociate in order to bind ubiquitin via the same interface. Furthermore, recent affinity purification mass spectrometry experiments did not observe significant enrichment of voltage-dependent anion channel 2 along with PINK1, whereas enrichment of TOM and TIM23 subunits were observed. , Future work should aim to elucidate the role of oxidation in PINK1 activation and subsequent substrate phosphorylation, as well as how TIM23 interacts with PINK1 under stress conditions.
Structural work on pharmacological modulators of PINK1 is scarce. Structures with ATP analogs bound in the pocket located between the N-lobe and the C-lobe show typical interactions with hinge and catalytic residues. , , , The KTP base, which includes a furfuryl group, cannot bind to the active site because of a clash with the gatekeeper residue Met318. The mutation M318G enables KTP to activate PINK1 and phosphorylate ubiquitin, but then this mutation (which is not naturally occurring) cannot function with ATP. Thus, it remains doubtful that KTP activates PINK1 directly. The mechanism of action of MTK458 also remains unclear, but there is evidence that this compound engages in the vicinity of PINK1 using nanoBRET. Furthermore, MTK458’s action on mitophagy is entirely PINK1-dependent. However, we found that addition of MTK458 does not increase, nor decrease, PINK1 ubiquitin kinase activity in vitro, suggesting that it is not acting via the kinase activity directly, but more likely on its mechanism of stabilization linked to mitochondrial damage. Evidence from recent studies proposes instead that MTK458 primes cells to detect damage more readily, an effect linked to its induction of stress pathways. Finally, our lab solved the structures of PINK1 bound to 2 pharmacological inhibitors, CYC116 and PRT062607. The structure with the most potent inhibitor, PRT062607, reveals interactions that are typical of type I inhibitors, including interactions with catalytic aspartate residues that maintains the “DFG-in” conformation ( E). Intriguingly, the αi helix, located in Insert 2, was disordered in the structure with PRT062607, but ordered in the structure with the nucleotide analog AMP-PN, in spite of both structures being solved in the same crystallographic space group and forming the same dimer. This suggests that the compound inhibits PINK1 not only by competing with ATP, but also allosterically destabilizing Insert 2, thus inhibiting autophosphorylation. This structure will serve as a canvas for the synthesis of more potent PINK1 inhibitors.

## Outlook: Charting future directions in kinase modulation for neurodegenerative diseases
The escalating global burden of neurodegenerative diseases, including AD, PD, and MS, underscores an urgent and unmet medical need for DMTs. Current treatments primarily offer symptomatic relief, doing little to arrest or reverse the relentless progression of neuronal degeneration. The critical roles of protein kinases in regulating virtually all cellular processes, coupled with their documented dysregulation in neurodegenerative pathologies, position them as promising therapeutic targets. Although the field has traditionally prioritized highly selective kinase inhibitors, emerging evidence suggests that multikinase or polypharmacology profiles may be advantageous for complex neurodegenerative diseases, where multiple parallel pathways converge on neuronal dysfunction. Dual-target strategies such as LRRK2/GSK3β or CDK5/GSK3β inhibition can provide broader pathway normalization but must be balanced against safety concerns that often arise from peripheral off-target interactions rather than true CNS on-target toxicity. As such, rational polypharmacology, guided by structural selectivity, CNS penetration, and minimization of peripheral liabilities, may represent a viable direction for future therapeutic development. The past 2 decades have witnessed a remarkable acceleration in our understanding of kinase biology, providing an unprecedented foundation for developing novel strategies aimed at neuroprotection and disease modification.

## Allosteric and nonconventional kinase modulation
Although conventional type I (DFG-in, ATP-competitive) inhibitors have been successful, their inherent promiscuity due to the conserved ATP-binding site necessitates a shift toward more selective approaches. Future directions will increasingly leverage type II and type III (allosteric) inhibitors, which exploit less conserved regions outside the ATP-binding pocket. This focus on allosteric modulation, as exemplified by the c-Abl inhibitor asciminib, allows for greater specificity and potentially fewer off-target effects, a crucial consideration for chronic neurodegenerative conditions. Our review highlights the structural insights into kinases such as p38 MAPK, BTK, c-Abl, CDK5, GSK3, JNK, LRRK2, and PINK1, underscoring how detailed structural understanding guides the design of highly selective compounds that engage unique conformational states or allosteric sites. Beyond inhibition, the concept of kinase activation through “paradoxical agonism” presents an intriguing, albeit less explored, avenue. As discussed for RAF kinases and inositol-requiring enzyme 1 (Ire1), and suggested for PINK1, some inhibitors can unexpectedly enhance kinase activity. This could be particularly beneficial for neurodegenerative diseases characterized by a loss-of-function in specific kinases, such as PINK1. Further research into identifying and characterizing such activators, as well as elucidating their precise mechanisms, represents a significant opportunity for therapeutic development.
Among them are inhibitors engaging the RAF family of kinases, composed of the 3 RAF paralogs: RAF (“A-RAF”), BRAF, and CRAF. These inhibitors were developed with the intent of reducing the activity of BRAF but instead induced a paradoxical activation by promoting the dimerization of the RAF kinase domains. RAF isomers need to reposition their αC helix in order to adopt a catalytically active conformation and carry out phosphorylation of its substrates. Through the binding of the inhibitor, the isomers would dimerize and the αC helix would thus adopt an active conformation on both proteins (despite only one monomer having the inhibitor bound). One such compound is Zm-336372, which was identified as an inhibitor of multiple RAF isoforms, but in vivo data alternatively shows activation of the CRAF isoform. Similarly, Ire1 is also activated by the small molecule inhibitor 1-tertbutyl-3-naphthalen-1-ylmethyl-1H-pyrazolo[3,4-d] pyrimidin-4-ylemine (1NM-PP1). Ire1 is an endoplasmic reticulum transmembrane protein involved in the activation of the unfolded protein response. When Ire1 senses signals, its attached chaperones will dissociate and instead engage with unfolded proteins. Surprisingly, inactive mutants of Ire1 were rescued by 1NM-PP1 and produced a full unfolded protein response response. Researchers have found that the occupancy of the active site by the inhibitor, even in the absence/inability of phosphorylation, was able to change conformation and lead to active protein response.

## Blood–brain barrier penetration
A major challenge in developing CNS-acting drugs is the BBB, a highly selective physiological barrier that restricts the entry of many small molecules and nearly all large molecules into the brain. , Historically, many kinase inhibitors developed for systemic diseases, such as cancer, exhibited poor brain penetrance, limiting their utility in neurological diseases (eg, nilotinib, see ). For example, crizotinib is a potent MNNG-ethyl-transforming gene (Met) and anaplastic lymphoma kinase inhibitor, but its poor brain penetrance limits its potency against glioblastoma. However, innovative strategies are being deployed to overcome this. One strategy involves rational drug design to enhance brain penetrance. This means optimizing physicochemical properties (eg, molecular weight, lipophilicity, and polar surface area) to favor passive diffusion across the BBB, while also avoiding substrates of efflux transporters such as P-gp. The review touches upon this with BTK inhibitors such as tolebrutinib, which is designed for CNS penetrance, and the LRRK2 inhibitors DNL151 and DNL201, which are brain-penetrant type I (DFG-in, ATP-competitive) inhibitors. However, in some instances, it may be desirable to target only the CNS-resident population of a given enzyme while sparing the same enzyme in the rest of the body. Groundbreaking work from the Shokat laboratory showcased the strategy of “binary pharmacology,” whereby a brain permeable inhibitor of mTOR, Rapalink-1, is coadministered with a brain impermeable FKB12 ligand called Rapablock that inhibits Rapalink-1’s action in the periphery. This strategy was used to achieve mTOR inhibition in glioblastoma xenografts, while minimizing mTOR inhibition in other tissues. While in oncology, this principle of targeting distinct sites to achieve superior potency and selectivity, or to overcome resistance, directly informs efforts to design brain-penetrant compounds that are not only potent but also evade efflux mechanisms or engage specific transport systems. Beyond small molecule design, localized drug delivery systems are gaining traction. Focused ultrasound, often in conjunction with microbubbles or nanodroplets, has emerged as a noninvasive technique to transiently and locally disrupt the BBB, allowing therapeutic agents, including larger molecules, to reach specific brain regions. This method offers the advantage of precision and reduced systemic exposure, which could be particularly beneficial for compounds with off-target toxicities. Other emerging strategies include molecular Trojan horses, such as antibodies engineered to bind to endogenous receptors on the BBB for receptor-mediated transcytosis, such as antibodies binding to the transferrin receptor. , Antibody-drug conjugates are also gaining traction, and although most antibody-drug conjugates combine cell receptor-specific antibodies with cytotoxic compounds such as auristatin E, it is conceivable that kinase inhibitors could be coupled to antibodies as well. Nanotechnology-based delivery systems (nanoparticles, liposomes, etc) also hold promise for delivery of small molecules. For example, lipid nanoparticles loaded with tight junction-modulating peptides were used to deliver the irreversible ErbB kinase inhibitor afatinib across a BBB model. Finally, intranasal administration can increase the brain penetrance of kinase inhibitors, especially when coadministered with P-gp substrates that prevent elimination, such as imatinib coadministered intranasally with elacridar and pantoprazole.

## Proteolysis-targeting chimeras
A particularly interesting approach in targeted therapeutics is the development of PROTACs. Unlike traditional small-molecule inhibitors that block protein function, PROTACs are heterobifunctional molecules that induce the degradation of target proteins by hijacking the cell’s ubiquitin-proteasome system. A PROTAC typically consists of 3 parts: a ligand that binds to the target protein, a ligand that binds to an E3 ubiquitin ligase, and a linker connecting the 2. By bringing the target protein and the E3 ligase into proximity, the PROTAC facilitates the ubiquitination of the target, marking it for degradation by the proteasome. For kinases, PROTACs offer several distinct advantages. They can achieve catalytic degradation of the target, meaning a single PROTAC molecule can mediate the degradation of multiple kinase molecules, leading to more sustained and potentially deeper inhibition. This mechanism can also bypass resistance mechanisms that emerge with traditional inhibitors, especially in the context of cancer, but potentially also in neurodegeneration. Furthermore, PROTACs can target “undruggable” kinases or scaffolding proteins that lack enzymatic activity but are crucial to disease pathology, expanding the therapeutic target space. Many PROTACs targeting kinases are currently under development or undergoing clinical trials. BTK has been a prime target for PROTACs, with molecules targeting wildtype or ibrutinib-resistant C481S mutant BTK and harnessing Cereblon and DCAF1 E3 ligases. , The BTK (and IKZF1/3) degrader NX-2127 is currently in phase I trials for patients with CLL. In total, there are about 9 BTK PROTACS in trials, with 1 in phase II and 1 in phase III, although they are for cancers and not MS. PROTACs targeting c-Abl are also being developed for the treatment of leukemia but could be repurposed for neurodegenerative diseases. , However, PROTACs introduce significant pharmacokinetic challenges, primarily due to their larger molecular size. Whereas traditional small-molecule drugs typically adhere to Lipinski’s Rule of 5 (molecular weight < 500 Da, <5 H-bond donor, <10 H-bond acceptor) for oral bioavailability and membrane permeability, PROTACs often exceed 800 to 1000 Da. This increased size impedes their ability to passively diffuse across biological membranes, including the BBB. Although at an earlier stage of development, PROTACs targeting LRRK2 have been reported and degrade the protein in cells. LRRK2 PROTACs not only reduce the kinase activity of LRRK2, but also eliminate its scaffolding role, which is also contributing to PD pathology. A promising LRRK2 PROTAC, XL01126, is orally bioavailable and brain-penetrant in mice, and functions as a fast, potent, and highly cooperative Von Hippel-Lindau-recruiting degrader that achieves robust LRRK2 knockdown in cells, providing a useful in vivo probe and starting point for PD drug development . Despite this promising example, most PROTACs continue to face substantial barriers to CNS delivery. As emphasized in recent analyses, their high molecular weight, extensive polarity, and large hydrogen-bonding capacity place them far outside the physicochemical space compatible with efficient BBB penetration. These characteristics strongly limit passive diffusion and make PROTACs vulnerable to active efflux by P-gp and breast cancer resistant protein, resulting in low brain exposure even when systemic levels are adequate. Designing brain-penetrant PROTACs requires sophisticated medicinal chemistry strategies, such as reducing overall molecular size while maintaining potency, incorporating specific motifs for active transport across the BBB, or utilizing advanced delivery methods such as targeted nanoparticles. Overcoming these pharmacokinetic hurdles is crucial for PROTACs to realize their full potential in CNS disorders. Nevertheless, a PROTAC degrader of LRRK2, ARV-102, under the company Arvinas has successfully met all endpoints for a phase I clinical trial in October 2025. It was well tolerated by patients but also demonstrated dose-dependent CSF exposure while decreasing biomarkers relevant to the lysosomal and neuroinflammatory microglial pathways.

VI

## Conclusion
Dysregulated kinase signaling is increasingly recognized as a central driver of neurodegeneration in disorders such as AD, PD, amyotrophic lateral sclerosis, and MS. Aberrant kinase activity contributes to key pathological processes, including tau hyperphosphorylation, α-synuclein aggregation, neuroinflammation, and neuronal death. A growing body of structural, biochemical, and pharmacological evidence has enabled the rational design of inhibitors targeting specific kinases implicated in disease progression, including CDK5, GSK3, LRRK2, BTK, JNK, p38 MAPK, c-Abl, and PINK1. Although several kinase inhibitors have shown promise in preclinical neurodegenerative models, translation to clinical use has been limited by challenges such as achieving brain penetrance, maintaining isoform specificity, and minimizing off-target toxicity. Newer strategies, including allosteric inhibitors, PROTACs, and paradoxical kinase activation, offer more nuanced approaches to modulate kinase function and may address some of these limitations. However, few compounds have advanced to clinical trials in neurodegeneration, and long-term safety data in the aging brain remain sparse. Continued progress will depend on improved disease modeling, robust biomarker development, and better understanding of kinase dynamics in specific cell types and disease stages. Moreover, delivery across the BBB and chronic dosing safety must be addressed to enable viable therapies. Despite these barriers, kinase targeting remains one of the most mechanistically grounded and tractable strategies in neurodegenerative disease drug development. With increased focus on precision pharmacology and rational combination therapies, there is strong potential for kinase modulators to become central to future disease-modifying treatments in neurology.
