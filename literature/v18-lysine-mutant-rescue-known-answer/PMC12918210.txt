## Background
Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) remains a major therapeutic challenge, highlighting the need for agents that engage non-apoptotic regulated cell death pathways. Ginsenoside compound K (CK), a bioactive metabolite derived from ginseng saponins, has demonstrated antitumor activity, however, its efficacy and underlying mechanisms in HCC are not well defined. Given the emerging role of ferroptosis in HCC, we investigated whether CK suppresses HCC by inducing this iron-dependent form of cell death.

## Methods
The antitumor effects of CK were evaluated in HepG2 and Hep3B cells and in a xenograft mouse model. Ferroptotic cell death was assessed by measuring reactive oxygen species (ROS), ferrous iron (Fe²⁺), malondialdehyde (MDA), and glutathione (GSH) levels, and by rescue experiments using ferroptosis inhibitors (N-acetylcysteine, NAC; Trolox) or GPX4 overexpression. Mechanistic studies were performed using RNA sequencing, ubiquitination assays, and genetic manipulation of OTUB2 expression.

## Results
CK inhibited HCC cell proliferation in a dose-dependent manner and robustly induced ferroptosis hallmarks, including ROS accumulation, enhanced lipid peroxidation, and GSH depletion. These cytotoxic effects were largely abrogated by ferroptosis inhibitors or GPX4 overexpression, confirming a ferroptosis-dependent mechanism. In vivo, CK significantly reduced tumor growth in xenograft models and recapitulated key ferroptosis features. Mechanistically, CK was identified as a functional inhibitor of the deubiquitinase OTUB2. Suppression of OTUB2 promoted ubiquitin-dependent degradation of GPX4, a central negative regulator of ferroptosis, thereby sensitizing HCC cells to ferroptosis.

## Conclusions
Our findings identify CK as a novel OTUB2 inhibitor that induces ferroptosis in HCC by destabilizing GPX4. This work delineates a previously unrecognized OTUB2/GPX4 regulatory axis in ferroptosis and supports CK as a promising candidate for further translational development for HCC therapy.

## Introduction
Liver cancer remains one of the most prevalent malignancies worldwide, characterized by high incidence and mortality rates [ ]. According to data from the Global Burden of Disease Study [ ], the incidence and mortality of liver cancer have consistently risen over the past few decades. Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) is the most common type of primary liver cancer, exhibiting significant regional variations in incidence and being associated with various etiologies, including hepatitis B virus (HBV), hepatitis C virus (HCV), and non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) [ ]. Despite advancements in early diagnosis and treatment of liver cancer, most patients are diagnosed at advanced stages, resulting in poor prognosis [ ]. Targeted therapies for HCC have been widely applied in clinical practice, yet long-term use may lead to issues of drug resistance [ , ]. Utilizing the patient’s own immune system to combat HCC cells, for instance through immune checkpoint inhibitors, has demonstrated notable efficacy in a subset of patients [ , ]. However, many patients respond poorly to these treatments, highlighting the need for further research into the immune microenvironment of HCC and strategies to optimize immunotherapy.
Ferroptosis, a form of iron-dependent programmed cell death characterized by lipid peroxidation, exhibits distinct morphological features compared to other forms of cell death such as apoptosis, autophagy, and necrosis [ , ]. Key characteristics of ferroptosis include mitochondria that are smaller in volume, denser, and have increased mitochondrial membrane potential, along with reduced and disappeared cristae, and ruptured outer membranes [ ]. Numerous organelles are involved in the ferroptosis process, which requires the presence of iron ions [ ]. Various factors can influence the occurrence of ferroptosis by regulating iron ion transport and metabolism. The direct trigger of ferroptosis is lipid peroxidation of the cell membrane. Enzymes encoded by Acyl-CoA synthetase long-chain family 4 (ACSL4) and Lysophosphatidylcholine Acyltransferase 3 (LPCAT3) are crucial for the esterification and insertion of polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFA) into membrane phospholipids (PL) [ ]. Phospholipids with inserted PUFA contain unstable bis-allylic hydrogen atoms, making them particularly susceptible to lipid peroxidation and thus executing ferroptosis [ ]. Curcumin can promote ferroptosis in HCC cells by upregulating ACSL4, thereby inhibiting HCC proliferation [ ]. Lipid peroxidation occurs in membrane phospholipids, especially those containing phosphatidylethanolamine (PE), leading to cell ferroptosis [ ]. The GPX4 enzyme is a star molecule in ferroptosis research. It converts lipid peroxides into lipid alcohols, inhibiting the formation of lipid reactive oxygen species and thereby mitigating lipid peroxidation and inhibiting ferroptosis [ ]. Therefore, reducing GPX4 levels or inhibiting its activity through drugs can increase cellular lipid peroxide levels [ ]. In colorectal cancer, RSL3 can inactivate GPX4, leading to elevated reactive oxygen species levels and subsequently inducing ferroptosis [ ]. In triple-negative breast cancer, Formononetin triggers ferroptosis by regulating the mTORC1/SREBP1/SCD1 pathway. During the process of GPX4 reducing lipid peroxides, GPX4 requires reduced glutathione (GSH) as a substrate [ ]. Thus, reducing cysteine and other raw materials for GSH synthesis can decrease GPX4 levels and induce ferroptosis. Tumor cells uptake cysteine through the Xc- system on the cell membrane, which is composed of SLC7A11 and SLC3A2 [ ]. Erastin reduces cysteine intake by inhibiting the Xc- system, thereby lowering GSH levels and ultimately leading to ferroptosis. FSP1 is another molecule that inhibits ferroptosis by reducing lipid peroxidation independently of GPX4 [ ]. Specifically, FSP1 can inhibit ferroptosis by reducing intracellular lipid peroxide radicals through the reduction of coenzyme Q [ , ].
It has been reported that ginsenoside RK1 induces ferroptosis in HCC cells in an FSP1-dependent manner, thereby suppressing tumor proliferation [ ]. Furthermore, ginsenoside RK1 targets hexokinase 2 (HK2) in hepatic stellate cells (HSCs), activating the ACSL4/LPCAT3/ALOX5 signaling pathway to promote HSC ferroptosis, which significantly alleviates carbon tetrachloride (CCl₄)-induced liver fibrosis in mice [ ].
On the other hand, ginsenoside Rh2 upregulates the transcription factor IRF1, which suppresses the expression of the SLC7A11, thereby promoting HSC ferroptosis and effectively ameliorating liver fibrosis [ ]. In a model of ulcerative colitis (UC), ginsenoside Rh2 inhibits ferroptosis by targeting miR-125a-5p, alleviating colon tissue damage and inflammation in DSS-induced mice, a process involving modulation of SP1 expression [ ]. 
Research on ginsenoside Rg3 is relatively well-established, with numerous studies focusing on its role in ferroptosis. For instance, ginsenoside Rg3 upregulates ACSL4 to promote HSC ferroptosis and inhibit the progression of liver fibrosis [ ]. Additionally, it mitigates myocardial ischemia/reperfusion-induced ferroptosis via the keap1/Nrf2/GPX4 signaling pathway [ ]. In the context of tumor immunity, ginsenoside Rg3 induces ferroptosis in gallbladder carcinoma by activating CD8⁺ T-cell immune function through the circFOXP1–miR-4477a–PD-L1 axis [ ].
Although ginsenosides RK1, Rh2, Rg3, and the focus of our study—ginsenoside CK—have all demonstrated the ability to induce ferroptosis in different disease contexts, their mechanisms of action are distinctly different [ ]. The root of these functional differences lies in their structural diversity. As summarized in Table , these ginsenosides belong to the triterpenoid saponin family and share a dammarane-type tetracyclic triterpene skeleton. However, they differ in the number, type, and linkage positions of their sugar moieties. It is precisely these structural variations that determine their unique biological activities [ ].
Furthermore, previous studies have indicated that ginsenoside CK exhibits a relatively low IC₅₀ value against hepatocellular carcinoma cells in specific cell lines and genetic backgrounds, suggesting its high potential as a ferroptosis inducer. However, its clinical translation faces challenges, particularly due to the unclear in vivo metabolic pathways and pharmacokinetic characteristics. Further integrated pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic studies are essential to elucidate the in vivo processes of ginsenoside CK and establish effective blood concentration monitoring methods, which are crucial for advancing its application as a ferroptosis inducer in hepatocellular carcinoma treatment.

 

However, the current ferroptosis inducers suffer from low specificity and can lead to the development of tumor cell resistance after prolonged use. Therefore, the search for novel ferroptosis inducers holds great promise in the field of oncology. This study is dedicated to exploring the effects of CK on the viability of HCC cells and its potential to induce ferroptosis. We employed a comprehensive array of experimental methods, including CCK-8 assays, Western blotting, qPCR, co-immunoprecipitation, immunofluorescence co-localization, and in vivo xenograft models. These methods were selected to thoroughly assess the biological effects of CK and elucidate its underlying molecular mechanisms. By integrating in vitro and in vivo analyses, we aim to evaluate the therapeutic potential of CK in HCC.
The primary objective of this research is to uncover the molecular mechanisms through which CK induces ferroptosis in HCC cells [ – ]. Specifically, we intend to determine how CK modulates the expression and stability of GPX4, thereby disrupting cellular antioxidant defenses and promoting ferroptotic cell death. Through this comprehensive approach, we strive to provide insights into the mechanisms underlying the action of CK and its potential as a therapeutic agent for HCC.
Therefore, we believe that in-depth investigation into the ability of ginsenoside CK to induce ferroptosis in hepatocellular carcinoma cells and its specific molecular mechanisms holds clear scientific significance and provides a valuable exploratory direction for developing novel therapeutic strategies against HCC.
Collectively, our findings not only identify ginsenoside CK as a compelling ferroptosis inducer but also decipher its mode of action, offering a mechanistic foundation for the application of TCM-derived small molecules in HCC.

## Materials and methods

## Antibodies and reagents
Antibodies used in this study were as follows: anti-GPX4 (Abclonal, AE005), anti-GAPDH (Abclonal, A19056), anti-Ubiquitin (Abclonal, A19686), anti-His (Abclonal, AE086), anti-Flag (Abclonal, AE092), anti-CAT(Abclonal, A11220), anti-SOD (Abclonal, A12537), anti-TXN (Abclonal, A4024), anti-ACSL4 (Abclonal, A20414), anti-PTGS2 (Abclonal, A3560), anti-Ki67 (Abclonal, A20018), anti-OTUB2 (Abclonal, A16251), anti-HA (Abclonal, AE105). Other reagents used in this study were: CK(> 98%purity) (MedchemExpress, HY-N0904), Ferrostatin-1 (MedchemExpress, HY-100579), Liproxstatin-1 (MedchemExpress, HY-12726), Desferrioxamine(MedchemExpress, HY-B0988), Deferasirox(MedchemExpress, HY-17359),Acetylcysteine(MedchemExpress, HY-B0215), Trolox(MedchemExpress, HY-101445), L-Cysteine(MedchemExpress, HY-Y0337), Cycloheximide(MedchemExpress, HY-12320), MG132 (MedchemExpress, HY-13259), Chloroquine (MedchemExpress, HY-17589 A).

## Cell culture and transfection
All cell lines used in this study, including the human embryonic kidney cell line HEK293T and the human hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) cell lines HepG2 and Hep3B, were purchased from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC). Cell line identities were confirmed by short tandem repeat (STR) analysis provided by the supplier. All cells utilized in this study were cultured in either RPMI 1640 or DMEM medium (meilunbiom, MA0315 and PWL035 respectively) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (EpiZyme, CY103). Transient transfection of plasmids into the cells was achieved using the jetPRIME reagent (Polyplus, 101000046), following the manufacturer’s instructions.

## Plasmids construction
GPX4 shRNA and negative control shRNA were synthesized by Shanghai GeneChem Co., Ltd. The day before the transfection, cells were plated at a density of 2 × 10^5 cells/mL. When the cell density reached 70%, the jetPRIME transfection reagent was used to transfect the vectors or empty plasmids into HepG2 or 293T cells according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

## Immunofluorescence
Cells were initially plated on cell culture slides and allowed to grow before being exposed to different concentrations of CK for a duration of 24 h. Post-treatment, the cells were fixed using 4% paraformaldehyde and subsequently blocked with a protein-free rapid blocking solution for 15 min to minimize non-specific binding. The primary antibody was then incubated overnight at 4 °C, followed by three sequential washes with PBS to remove unbound antibodies. The secondary antibody was subsequently applied and incubated at room temperature for 1 h. Fluorescence images were obtained using an inverted fluorescence microscope, and the fluorescence intensity of each experimental group was quantified using Image J software for further analysis.

## GSH assay
GSH levels were quantified with a specific assay kit (Beyotime, S0053) following the vendor’s protocol. Cells from different groups were collected by centrifugation, and then 3 times the volume of protein remover S was added. After two rapid freeze-thaw cycles using liquid nitrogen and a 37 °C water bath, the supernatant was taken. Following the instructions provided in the kit, a standard curve was plotted, and the GSH content of the samples was determined accordingly.

## MDA assay
MDA levels were quantified with a specific assay kit (Beyotime, S0131S) following the vendor’s protocol. After processing the various samples, lysis buffer was added, followed by centrifugation to obtain the supernatant. Protein concentrations were then determined using the BCA method. Subsequent steps were carried out according to the kit instructions. The absorbance of the samples was measured at 532 nm, and the MDA content was determined based on the standard curve.

## Iron content assay
For cell samples, 300 µl of lysis buffer was added to every 1 × 10^6 cells. For tissue samples, 270 µl of lysis buffer was added to every 30 g of tissue. After centrifugation, the supernatants were collected for subsequent analysis. Iron standard solutions (1mmol/L) were serially diluted to concentrations of 50, 25, 12.5, 6.25, and 3.125µmol/L to construct a standard curve. Subsequent steps were performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The absorbance of the samples was measured at 593 nm. The level of Fe²⁺ was measured with the ferroOrange fluorescent probe (Dojindo, F374).

## ROS assay
After treating the cells according to the respective groups, the medium was replaced. The DCFH-DA probe was diluted at a ratio of 1:1000 and the cells were incubated at 37 °C for 30 min. Following three washes with serum-free medium, the cells were collected by centrifugation and the relative ROS content was measured using a fluorescence microplate reader.

## Cysteine uptake assasy
Cells were seeded into a 96-well plate and incubated overnight. After removing the medium, they were washed with pre-warmed HBSS, then incubated with HBSS for 5 min. Subsequently, cells were exposed to either BPA uptake solution or HBSS (for controls) for another 5 min, followed by three washes with HBSS. Finally, a working solution was added, and the cells were incubated for 5 more minutes before being analyzed using a microplate reader.

## Western blot
For the analysis of protein expression via Western blotting, cellular lysates were obtained through the addition of a lysis buffer, enriched with protease and phosphatase inhibitors, and incubated for 30 min on ice. Subsequently, the lysates were subjected to centrifugation at 12,000 revolutions per minute (rpm) for 15 min at 4 °C. The resultant supernatants were then harvested and their protein concentrations determined utilizing the bicinchoninic acid (BCA) assay. Protein samples, ranging from 20 to 50 micrograms (µg), were diluted in a 5× sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS) loading buffer, denatured by boiling at 100 °C for 10 min, resolved on SDS-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (PAGE) gels, and subsequently transferred onto polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes. The membranes were then treated with a protein-free rapid blocking solution for 1 h at ambient temperature, followed by overnight incubation with specific primary antibodies at 4 °C. Afterwards, the membranes were exposed to horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-labeled secondary antibodies for an additional hour at room temperature. The immunoreactive bands were visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) system and captured by an image reader. Densitometric quantification was carried out using ImageJ software. The obtained data were adjusted for background noise and normalized relative to glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) as an internal reference control.

## Co-immunoprecipitation assay (Co-IP)
Cells were collected into 1.5 ml centrifuge tubes, and lysis buffer was added. After centrifugation, the supernatant was collected and mixed with 5 µg of GPX4 antibody. The mixture was then placed on a rotator and incubated overnight. Subsequently, 25µL of protein A/G magnetic beads were added to the antigen-antibody complex and incubated at 4 °C overnight. Following three washes, the samples were subjected to immunoblotting analysis.

## Protein degradation assay
To investigate the degradation of GPX4, CHX (20 µg/ml) was added to HepG2 cells and incubated for 24 h, with or without the addition of MG132 (10 µM) and CQ (10µM). Whole-cell lysates were then prepared for Western blot analysis. Additionally, to study the stability of the protein, HepG2 cells were exposed to 20 µM CK for 0, 3, 6, 12, 24, or 48 h, and the protein expression of GPX4 was analyzed using Western blot.

## Ubiquitination assay
In the ubiquitination assay, 293T cells were transiently transfected with plasmids encoding UB, K6, K11, K27, K29, K33, K48, K63, and their corresponding lysine-to-arginine mutants (K6R, K11R, K27R, K29R, K33R, K48R, and K63R). Following a 24-hour incubation, the cells were treated with or without 20µM CK for an additional 24 h. Subsequently, cell lysates were generated and subjected to immunoprecipitation using protein A/G magnetic beads. The precipitated proteins were eluted and analyzed via immunoblotting (IB).

## RNA extraction and semi-quantitative RT-PCR
Utilize the RNA Extraction Kit (Abclonal, RK30120) to extract total RNA from cells and tissues. Subsequently, synthesize cDNA using the Reverse Transcription Kit (Abclonal, RK20429) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Conduct PCR analysis of mRNA expression using 2 × Taq Plus Master Mix (Vazyme, P211-01). We confirmed the specificity of the PCR primers through BLAST analysis, and the presence of a single peak in the melt curve further affirmed the specificity of the PCR products. The primer sequences used are shown in Table .

 

## Animal experiments
Four-week-old nude mice (weighing 16–18 g) were obtained from the Experimental Animal Center of Shanghai University of Traditional Chinese Medicine (Shanghai, China). In brief, 2 × 10^6 HepG2 cells were suspended in a mixture of 100µL PBS and Matrigel (1:1, v/v) and then implanted subcutaneously into the dorsal flank region of the mice. Once the tumor volume reached approximately 50 mm³, the mice were randomly assigned to five groups (n = 6) for treatment as indicated. The mice were housed in a specific-pathogen-free environment with a 12-hour light/12-hour dark cycle and had free access to water and food. All animal experiments were conducted in accordance with protocols approved by the Ethical Committee of Experimental Animals at the Shanghai University of Traditional Chinese Medicine Experimental Animal Center (Ethics Code: PZSHUTCM2304180001). Tumor volume and body weight were measured three times a week. Tumor volume was calculated using the formula: V = π/6 × (Length × Width²). At the conclusion of the study, the mice were euthanized.

## Immunohistochemistry (IHC)
For immunohistochemical (IHC) examination, tissue slides of HCC specimens underwent a process of deparaffinization, followed by rehydration through a graded alcohol series, and subsequently, antigen retrieval was achieved using sodium citrate buffer. The tumor sections were then subjected to blocking with a solution comprising 5% normal goat serum, 0.1% Triton X-100, and 3% H2O2 in PBS for a duration of 60 min at room temperature. Subsequently, the sections were incubated with the respective primary antibodies at 4 °C overnight. IHC staining was conducted utilizing horseradish peroxidase (HRP) conjugates, with detection facilitated by DAB. Nuclei were counterstained using Hoechst dye. Microscopic imaging was employed to capture the stained sections.

## Statistical analysis
Statistical comparisons were conducted using either unpaired Student’s t-tests or one-way ANOVA, depending on the number of groups involved. The results are expressed as mean values accompanied by their respective standard deviations (SD). A threshold of P < 0.05 was established to denote statistical significance. Significance levels are indicated as follows: * for P < 0.05, ** for P < 0.01, *** for P < 0.001, and ns for no significant difference. All statistical analyses were carried out utilizing GraphPad Prism software.

## Results

## CK inhibits HCC proliferation in vitro
To assess the anti-proliferative effect of CK on hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), we treated HepG2 and Hep3B cell lines with escalating doses of the compound. CK significantly suppressed cell viability at concentrations as low as 4µM (P < 0.05; Fig.  A, B). Dose-response analysis yielded IC₅₀ values of 11.8µM for HepG2 and 12.5µM for Hep3B cells. Accordingly, three concentrations—5, 10, and 20µM—were selected for subsequent experiments and designated as low (CKL), medium (CKM), and high (CKH) dose groups, respectively.
Strikingly, CK treatment triggered a dose-dependent perturbation in key ferroptotic markers. We observed a concerted decrease in intracellular glutathione (GSH) alongside a marked accumulation of malondialdehyde (MDA), ferrous ions (Fe²⁺), and reactive oxygen species (ROS) (Fig.  C–J). These collective findings indicate that the anti-HCC activity of CK is mediated, at least in part, by sensitizing cells to ferroptosis.

 

## CK induces ferroptosis in HCC cells
To investigate the mode of cell death induced by ginsenoside CK in hepatocellular carcinoma cells, we employed several cell death inhibitors—Z-VAD-FMK (apoptosis inhibitor), Nec-1 (necroptosis inhibitor), and 3-MA (autophagy inhibitor)—in combination with CK and assessed the resulting cell viability using the CCK-8 assay. Combined inhibitor assays demonstrated that ginsenoside CK induces a hybrid form of cell death in hepatocellular carcinoma cells, primarily mediated by apoptosis and ferroptosis (Figure ).
To elucidate the underlying biochemical pathway through which CK sensitizes HCC cells to ferroptosis, we employed classic ferroptosis inhibitors, including the lipid peroxide radical scavengers Fer-1 and Lip-1, the iron chelators DFO and DFX, as well as the antioxidants NAC and Trolox. We found that all these ferroptosis inhibitors could reverse the cytotoxic effects of CK on HCC cells (P < 0.05), with the ROS scavengers NAC and Trolox exhibiting the most pronounced reversal effects (Fig. A, B). These results suggest that CK may induce ferroptosis by increasing the accumulation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) within HCC cells. Additionally, exogenous addition of NAC and Trolox dose-dependently enhanced the viability of HCC cells (Fig. C). Furthermore, NAC and Trolox increased intracellular GSH levels and decreased MDA content (Fig. D, E). We also observed that CK reduced the uptake capacity of cysteine in HCC cells (Fig. F). Exogenous cysteine supplementation significantly suppressed CK-induced ferroptosis in HCC cells (Fig. G). Moreover, exogenous cysteine increased intracellular GSH levels, thereby enhancing ferroptosis resistance in HCC cells (Fig. H). Changes in mitochondrial morphology are considered a hallmark of ferroptosis. We examined the mitochondrial morphology in HCC cells treated with CK and found that the mitochondrial structure was significantly damaged, characterized by reduced volume, decreased cristae, and increased membrane density. We also observed the ballooning phenotype in HCC cells, characterized by the formation of translucent, rounded cells largely devoid of cytoplasmic content—a specific morphological hallmark acquired during ferroptosis [ ]. The ferroptosis inhibitor Fer-1 reversed this phenotype (Fig. I). Consistent with the hallmarks of ferroptosis, CK treatment markedly elevated intracellular ferrous iron (Fe²⁺) levels (Figure ), as quantified by FerroOrange staining in HepG2 and Hep3B cells [ ].

 

## CK promotes ferroptosis by inhibiting GPX4 enzymatic activity
To elucidate the mechanism by which CK promotes ROS and lipid peroxide accumulation in HCC cells, we analyzed the expression of key antioxidant enzymes. CK specifically and dose-dependently downregulated GPX4 at both protein (Fig. A–D) and mRNA levels (Fig. E–F), whereas the expression of SOD, CAT, and TXN remained unaltered. Immunofluorescence staining further validated the dose-dependent reduction of GPX4 in HepG2 and Hep3B cells (Fig. G–J). Concomitantly, CK induced a pronounced increase in lipid peroxides, which was completely abolished by the ferroptosis inhibitor Fer-1 (Fig. K–N). CK treatment caused a significant shift in the fluorescence emission from red to green, reflected by an increased green/red ratio (Fig. ) [ ]. Collectively, these findings indicate that CK impairs cellular antioxidant defense primarily by suppressing GPX4 expression, leading to lipid peroxide accumulation and consequent ferroptosis.

 

## Overexpression of GPX4 can rescue the ability of CK to promote ferroptosis in HCC cells
To establish the functional significance of GPX4 in CK-induced ferroptosis, we overexpressed GPX4 in HCC cells. Successful transfection was confirmed at both protein and mRNA levels (Fig.  A–C). Notably, GPX4 overexpression markedly rescued the CK-induced suppression of HCC cell proliferation (Fig.  D, E) and restored intracellular GSH levels while reducing MDA accumulation (Fig.  F–I). Together, these results demonstrate that GPX4 is a critical mediator of CK-triggered ferroptosis in HCC.

 

## CK suppresses the proliferation of HCC in vivo
To evaluate the anti-tumor efficacy of CK in vivo, we established an HCC xenograft model (Fig.  A). After 30 days of treatment, CK administration resulted in a significant reduction in tumor weight, with the high-dose group exhibiting the strongest suppression and outperforming sorafenib in terms of efficacy. This inhibitory effect was notably reversed by the ferroptosis inhibitor Fer-1 (Fig.  B, C). CK also markedly attenuated tumor growth kinetics (Fig.  D) without affecting body weight (Fig.  E) or inducing hepatotoxicity, as indicated by stable ALT and AST levels (Fig.  H, I). Mechanistically, CK decreased GSH and increased MDA levels in tumor tissues (Fig.  F, G). Collectively, these results demonstrate that CK effectively inhibits HCC proliferation in vivo through a ferroptosis-dependent mechanism.

 

## CK induces ferroptosis in HCC in vivo through downregulation of GPX4
To substantiate the role of ferroptosis in CK-mediated tumor suppression in vivo, we analyzed key ferroptosis-related markers in xenograft tissues. Immunoblotting revealed that CK downregulated GPX4 while upregulating ACSL4 and PTGS2 protein expression (Fig.  A–D). Consistent with these findings, RT-qPCR analysis demonstrated corresponding alterations at the transcriptional level (Fig.  E–G). Furthermore, CK administration significantly elevated intratumoral iron levels, exhibiting a more pronounced pro-ferroptotic effect than sorafenib (Fig.  H). Histopathological evaluation corroborated that CK suppressed tumor cell proliferation concomitant with reduced GPX4 expression (Fig.  I–K). Collectively, these in vivo data firmly establish ferroptosis as a crucial mechanism underlying the anti-HCC activity of CK.

 

## CK regulates glutamine metabolism in tumor tissues to induce ferroptosis
To elucidate the molecular pathway through which CK modulates ferroptosis, we first profiled deubiquitinating enzyme (DUB) activity in HCC cells. CK treatment resulted in a dose-dependent suppression of DUB activity, with the high-dose group showing inhibition comparable to the canonical DUB inhibitor N-ethylmaleimide (NEM) (Fig.  A, B).
RNA sequencing of HepG2 cells treated with CK or DMSO (n = 3) revealed robust between-group separation in principal component analysis (Fig.  C). We identified 414 differentially expressed genes (DEGs; FDR < 0.05, |log₂FC| > 1), with the top 20 shown in Fig.  E. Cross-referencing DEGs against a DUB database highlighted three ferroptosis-associated DUBs: TRIM2, OTUB2, and USP13 (Fig.  F), whose positions are annotated in the DEG volcano plot (Fig.  D).
Functional enrichment analysis (GO/KEGG/GSEA) further suggested that CK reprograms glutamine metabolism to sensitize tumor cells to ferroptosis (Fig. 7G–I). Collectively, these findings support a model in which CK may induce ferroptosis by targeting OTUB2, TRIM2, or USP13, leading to decreased GPX4 protein levels.

 

## CK downregulates OTUB2-mediated K48-linked ubiquitination and degradation of GPX4 protein
To validate the hypothesis that CK induces ferroptosis by modulating deubiquitinating enzyme (DUB) activity to promote GPX4 degradation, we first performed molecular docking of CK with GPX4, SOD, CAT, and TXN. The results indicated moderate binding affinity between CK and GPX4 (− 6.9 kcal/mol), while the strongest interaction was observed with CAT (− 8.8 kcal/mol), suggesting that CK is unlikely to exert its effects via direct binding to GPX4 (Fig.  A). Co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) assays confirmed an interaction between GPX4 and OTUB2 (Fig.  B). Exogenous CK treatment markedly destabilized GPX4 protein and shortened its half-life (Fig.  C). Moreover, CK dose-dependently reduced OTUB2 expression in HCC cells (Fig.  D–F). These findings support the notion that CK may downregulate OTUB2, thereby enhancing GPX4 ubiquitination and degradation, ultimately leading to ferroptosis in HCC.
To further investigate this mechanism, we constructed plasmids encoding HA-UB and His-GPX4, along with a series of ubiquitin mutants containing lysine-to-arginine substitutions at positions K6, K11, K27, K29, K33, K48, and K63. Subsequent Co-IP experiments demonstrated that mutation at the K48 site (K48R) substantially reduced GPX4 ubiquitination, whereas the highest level of ubiquitination was observed with K48-linked ubiquitin chains (Fig.  I–L), indicating that CK primarily mediates K48-linked polyubiquitination of GPX4.
We next examined the pathway of GPX4 degradation using the autophagy inhibitor chloroquine (CQ) and the proteasome inhibitor MG132. Treatment with MG132 significantly stabilized GPX4 and prolonged its half-life (Fig.  M), supporting the conclusion that GPX4 is degraded predominantly via the ubiquitin-proteasome system. Furthermore, CK was found to enhance GPX4 ubiquitination (Fig.  O), while thermal shift assays indicated that CK stabilizes OTUB2 protein (Fig.  P). To validate ginsenoside CK as a GPX4 degrader, we analyzed GPX4 amino acid sequences across species using the UniProt database, identifying 17 potential ubiquitination sites (K11, K47, K58, K75, K107, K117, K126, K132, K148, K152, K154, K162, K167, K172, K178, and K191). Based on ubiquitination characteristics, we constructed the GPX4-4KR mutant by mutating the C-terminal hotspot region residues K162/K167/K172/K178 in GPX4-WT. Treatment with ginsenoside CK revealed that it enhances ubiquitination-dependent degradation of GPX4-WT but fails to degrade GPX4-4KR. This confirms that ginsenoside CK functions as a GPX4 degrader(Fig. ).
In summary, our results demonstrate that CK downregulates the deubiquitinase OTUB2, thereby promoting K48-linked ubiquitination and proteasomal degradation of GPX4. This impairs cellular antioxidant capacity, leading to lipid peroxidation accumulation, disruption of phospholipid membranes, and ultimately, ferroptosis in HCC cells.

 

## OTUB2 overexpression confers resistance to CK-induced ferroptosis in HCC cells
To investigate the functional role of OTUB2, we overexpressed it in HepG2 and Hep3B cells and evaluated cell viability as well as GSH and MDA levels following CK treatment. Successful transfection and efficient upregulation of OTUB2 were confirmed by Western blot and qPCR, which showed significantly elevated levels of both OTUB2 protein and mRNA in the overexpression groups compared to the empty vector controls (Fig. A-C).
Cell viability assays revealed that OTUB2 overexpression had no significant effect on proliferation in the DMSO-treated groups. However, under CK treatment, OTUB2-overexpressing cells exhibited markedly higher viability than empty vector controls, indicating that OTUB2 attenuates the anti-proliferative effect of CK on HCC cells (Fig. D-E).
Furthermore, CK treatment significantly reduced intracellular GSH levels, while OTUB2 overexpression alleviated this depletion. Similarly, the CK-induced increase in MDA levels was suppressed by OTUB2 overexpression (Fig. F-I).
In summary, OTUB2 overexpression counteracts CK-induced ferroptosis in HepG2 and Hep3B cells by mitigating GSH depletion and limiting lipid peroxidation.

## CK selectively induces ferroptosis in HCC cells but spares normal hepatocytes
To evaluate the potential cytotoxicity of ginsenoside CK within the tested dose range, we treated the normal human hepatocyte line L02 with increasing concentrations of CK (0–256µM) for 24 h. A significant reduction in cell viability was observed only at concentrations ≥ 64µM (Fig.  A), indicating that CK selectively inhibits the proliferation of HCC cells without exerting cytotoxic effects on normal hepatocytes at doses ≤ 20µM. Consistent with this, LDH release and colony formation assays confirmed the absence of significant toxicity in L02 cells within this safe concentration window (Fig.  B–D). Furthermore, analysis of ferroptosis-related markers—including GSH, MDA, and Fe²⁺—revealed that CK (5–20 µM) did not induce ferroptosis in normal hepatocytes. Similarly, GPX4 expression remained unaltered at both the protein and mRNA levels in CK-treated L02 cells (Fig.  E–G).

 

## Discussion
CK, a rare ginsenoside, is a natural product with a wide range of biological activities. It is the major metabolite and ultimate absorption form of diol-type ginsenosides in the human intestine. Previous studies have shown that CK can induce apoptosis in triple-negative breast cancer cells by targeting glutamine metabolism [ ]. It also exerts anti-gastric cancer effects by regulating the PI3K/AKT/NF-κB signaling pathway [ ]. Additionally, CK can induce apoptosis in human cervical cancer cells by modulating autophagy and endoplasmic reticulum stress [ ]. Furthermore, it has been reported that CK can induce apoptosis in HCC cells by inhibiting glycolysis and AKT/mTOR/c-Myc signaling [ ]. Our study aims to investigate whether CK can target ferroptosis in HCC cells to inhibit their proliferation and explore the underlying molecular mechanisms.
Ferroptosis, an iron-dependent form of programmed cell death, represents a promising therapeutic strategy for targeting tumor cells [ , ]. Therefore, the search for small molecule compounds with good ferroptosis-inducing ability and favorable safety profiles holds potential for novel cancer treatments [ ]. CK significantly inhibits HCC proliferation both in vitro and in vivo, displaying notable potency with a low IC₅₀. We further uncovered that CK induces ferroptosis in HCC cells, as evidenced by the inhibition of GPX4, subsequent accumulation of lipid peroxides, and a characteristic shift in ferroptotic markers—including elevated ROS, Fe²⁺, and MDA, alongside reduced GSH. Notably, the cell death induced by CK was markedly attenuated by the ferroptosis inhibitors NAC and Trolox, confirming the dependence on this pathway. These findings provide compelling evidence that CK exerts its anti-tumor effects, at least in part, through the induction of ferroptosis.
From an immunological perspective, while this study primarily focused on the direct effects of CK on HCC cells, it is plausible that CK may also influence the tumor microenvironment, including immune cell populations. The induction of ferroptosis could alter the immunogenicity of HCC cells, making them more susceptible to immune surveillance and attack [ ]. Moreover, the reduction in GSH levels and increase in ROS could create a pro-inflammatory microenvironment, which is known to enhance the activity of cytotoxic T lymphocytes (CTLs) and natural killer (NK) cells [ , ]. Although direct immunomodulatory effects of CK were not the primary focus of this study, these potential interactions highlight the broader implications of CK’s mechanisms in shaping the immune response against HCC. Future studies should explore these immunological dimensions to fully understand the therapeutic potential of CK in cancer treatment.
While our study provides valuable insights into the molecular mechanisms underlying CK-induced ferroptosis in HCC, several limitations warrant consideration. First, the findings were primarily derived from in vitro and in vivo models and thus require validation in clinical samples to assess their translational potential. Second, the limited sample size in certain experiments may have affected the statistical power and generalizability of the results. Additionally, although we evaluated CK in normal hepatocytes and observed no significant toxicity within the tested dose range, its potential off-target effects in normal tissues in vivo were not comprehensively assessed, raising concerns about their potential off-target toxicity. Finally, the exploration of combinatorial therapies, particularly the synergistic effects of CK with other anticancer agents, remains underexplored and warrants further investigation. Addressing these limitations in future studies will be crucial for advancing the clinical applicability of CK as a therapeutic agent.
Further experiments revealed that CK can promote the K48 ubiquitination and degradation of GPX4 by reducing OTUB2 levels. Specifically, CK reduces the expression level of OTUB2, thereby weakening its deubiquitinating protective effect on GPX4. OTUB2, a type of deubiquitinating enzyme, has been shown to be highly expressed in a variety of human malignancies, whereas its expression levels in adjacent normal tissues and non-cancerous tissues are relatively low [ ]. This distinct expression pattern of OTUB2 suggests a close correlation with the onset and progression of tumors [ , ]. In HCC tissues, OTUB2 is upregulated, indicating a poor prognosis for patients. Furthermore, knocking down OTUB2 significantly reduces the cell viability of HCC [ ]. OTUB2 inhibits the proliferation of HCC by promotes the deubiquitination and degradation of PJA1 [ ].
This reduction in OTUB2, in turn, promotes K48-linked ubiquitination of GPX4 and subsequent proteasomal degradation. This mechanism may be closely related to the antioxidant and anti-apoptotic effects of CK in various disease models. To further validate this mechanism, we conducted in vitro ubiquitination experiments and cell experiments with proteasome inhibitors. The results showed that in cells treated with CK, the ubiquitination level of GPX4 was significantly increased. Additionally, when the proteasome inhibitor MG132 was added, the degradation of GPX4 was effectively inhibited, indicating that CK indeed regulates the stability of GPX4 through the ubiquitin-proteasome pathway.
Furthermore, we examined changes in intracellular ferroptosis-related indicators following CK treatment. The results revealed that CK treatment significantly increased the accumulation of intracellular lipid peroxides, decreased intracellular GSH levels, and promoted the accumulation of intracellular iron ions. These changes are consistent with the characteristics of ferroptosis. These findings further support the hypothesis that CK induces ferroptosis by regulating the stability of GPX4.
In summary, our study elucidates the critical role of GPX4 in the ferroptosis of HCC cells mediated by CK, providing a robust mechanistic framework for its anticancer activity. The significant suppression of tumor growth observed both in vitro and in vivo underscores the therapeutic potential of CK as a novel ferroptosis-inducing agent. However, to fully realize its clinical promise, future studies should focus on validating these findings in human cohorts, assessing its safety profile in normal tissues, and exploring combinatorial strategies to enhance its therapeutic efficacy. These efforts will be essential for translating our findings into practical therapeutic interventions for HCC.
