## Abstract
Butyrophilins (BTNs) act as critical sensors of phosphoantigens (pAgs), mediating the immune response to these small molecules. The natural ligands for BTN3A1 include the exogenous (E)-4-hydroxy-3-methyl-but-2-enyl diphosphate (HMBPP) and endogenous pAgs dimethyl allyl diphosphate (DMAPP) and isopentenyl diphosphate (IPP), all of which can bind to BTN3A1 followed by activation of γ9δ2 T cells. BTN3A1 shares similar structural domains and exhibits high amino acid sequence homology with BTN3A3. However, the potential functions of BTN3A3 and whether it possesses ligands of its own remain unknown. Here, we show that a single point mutation to BTN3A3 allows it to detect HMBPP, suggesting that the protein may have its own ligand. Mevalonate diphosphate (MPP), a naturally occurring endogenous compound, exhibits high docking scores with both BTN3A1 and BTN3A3. Functional testing revealed that MPP activates γ9δ2 T cells, albeit with a higher EC50 (300 µM) compared to DMAPP (7.1 µM) and IPP (30 µM). 31P NMR experiments confirmed MPP binding to both BTN3A1 and BTN3A3 internal domains while exposing the ability of BTN2A1 to form a complex with BTN3A3-MPP. 6-Fluoromevalonate (6-FM), an inhibitor targeting MPP decarboxylase, also activated γ9δ2 T cells. LC-MS showed that 6-FM-treated CHO-K1 cells accumulated substantial MPP while risedronate, an inhibitor of farnesyl diphosphate synthase, primarily increased DMAPP/IPP. Interestingly, in coculture experiments, transfection of CHO-K1 cells with either BTN2A1 + BTN3A1 or BTN2A1 + BTN3A3 enabled their response to 6-FM. Both BTN2A1 + BTN3A3-transfected CHO-K1 cells and BTN3A1 knockout K562 cells could detect 6-FM, despite their absence of BTN3A1. Our findings provide evidence that MPP functions as a natural ligand of BTN3A3 and establish a foundation for elucidating biological functions of BTN3A3 complementary to the established BTN3A1 pathway.

## Introduction
Gamma-delta (γδ) T cells represent a unique and important subset within the T lymphocyte family [ , ]. Unlike conventional αβ T cells, γδ T cells express T cell receptors (TCRs) composed of one γ chain and one δ chain [ ]. The γδ T cells exhibit features of both innate and adaptive immunity, enabling them to rapidly respond to infections, tumors, and other threats [ , ]. Although they constitute only a small proportion (approximately 1–5%) of peripheral blood T cells, they are enriched in specific tissues such as the skin [ ], intestine, lung, and reproductive tract [ , ]. The γδ T cells are capable of recognizing a distinct class of antigens that are not presented via classical MHC pathways, including small phosphorylated metabolites, lipid molecules, and stress-induced ligands, allowing for rapid activation without the need for traditional antigen processing [ , ]. In recent years, the roles of γδ T cells in infection defense [ ], tumor immunology [ ], tissue repair [ ], and autoimmune diseases [ ] have been increasingly elucidated [ ], positioning them as key targets in tumor immunotherapy and immune regulation research [ ]. Based on differences in their TCR composition, human γδ T cells can be subdivided into several subsets, among which the γ9δ2 T cells are the predominant population in peripheral blood [ ], exhibiting high sensitivity to phosphorylated metabolites (aka phosphoantigens, pAgs) [ ]. 
Early studies in this field noted that γ9δ2 T cells could be activated and expanded in response to bacteria and mycobacteria in a manner that was dependent upon their TCR. Fractionation experiments led to the identification of small molecule phosphates, and specifically alkyl phosphates, as the antigenic component. This is in stark contrast to peptide antigens presented by MHC and detected by canonical TCRs. It soon came to be recognized that some pAgs could be produced by human cells (endogenous pAgs), while others were specific to pathogens (exogenous pAgs). Of the endogenous pAgs, intermediates of the mevalonate pathway, such as dimethylallyl diphosphate (DMAPP) and isopentenyl diphosphate (IPP), were identified [ , ]. At the same time, the exogenous pAg (E)−4-hydroxy-3-methyl-but-2-enyl diphosphate (HMBPP), a product of the non-mevalonate, or methylerythritol phosphate, pathway is now widely recognized as the most potent naturally occurring pAg. These small diphosphates can be viewed as physiological building blocks, essential for the production of sterols and other biochemicals critical to cell survival. It was not until years later that butyrophilin 3A1 (BTN3A1) was identified as the key receptor for HMBPP and other pAgs [ ].
The butyrophilin (BTN) protein family belongs to the immunoglobulin superfamily and contains six expressed proteins, BTN1A1, BTN2A1, BTN2A2, BTN3A1, BTN3A2 and BTN3A3 [ ]. The structures of all BTN proteins consist of an IgV domain, an IgC domain, a transmembrane region, and (with the exception of BTN3A2) an intracellular B30.2 domain [ ]. Among the six BTN proteins, BTN1A1 was initially identified in mammary gland secretions [ , ] while other family members have more recently been implicated in immune regulation [ ]. BTN proteins are involved not only in immune surveillance during tumor development, infection [ ], and tissue damage, but also in the pathogenesis of autoimmunity and chronic inflammation [ ]. Moreover, members of the BTN family play important roles in modulating T cell activity, maintaining peripheral tolerance, and preventing excessive immune responses [ ].
BTN3A1 and BTN2A1 have received the most attention due to their critical roles in regulating γ9δ2 T cell activation by sensing pAgs [ , , ]. The intracellular B30.2 domain of BTN3A1 binds to pAgs, leading to conformational changes and the recruitment of BTN2A1 via its intracellular B30.2 domain to form a phosphoantigen receptor complex [ – ]. This complex is a tetramer, which contains a dimer of BTN2A1 [ ], and BTN3A1, which dimerizes with either BTN3A2, BTN3A3, or a second molecule of BTN3A1 [ ]. Changes induced by internal pAg binding propagate to the extracellular domains of BTN3A1 and BTN2A1 and are detected by the TCR of γ9δ2 T cells, which triggers their activation [ – ]. Specifically, the IgV domains of both BTN3 and BTN2A1 on the pAg-containing cell form interactions in trans with the TCR of the γ9δ2 T cells, stimulating TCR signaling and response. Interestingly, although BTN3A1 binds to HMBPP, its IgV domain is dispensable in the presence of BTN3A2, suggesting orientation to the complex [ ]. The IgV domains of BTN3A1, BTN3A2, and BTN3A3 are identical except for a single lysine to arginine variant.
Most models of pAg detection conclude that the currently known pAgs function by binding to BTN3A1 [ , , ]. Although BTN3A3 shares significant structural and sequence similarity with BTN3A1, its functional role has not yet been fully elucidated. Certainly, several studies have indicated that BTN3A3 is involved in the detection of pAgs by BTN3A1 [ ]. Its role in HMBPP detection has been proposed to form a dimer with BTN3A1 that promotes stability and surface expression of the complex, and like BTN3A2 can form interactions with the TCR following pAg binding to BTN3A1. In contrast, BTN3A3 has not yet been recognized as a pAg detection molecule. BTN3A3 has reduced binding to HMBPP, as it contains an arginine residue at position 381 [ ]. This is in contrast to the histidine found at that position in BTN3A1 which directly interacts with the allylic alcohol of HMBPP to stabilize the ligand-protein interaction [ ]. A single-point mutant, BTN3A3 R381H, could enable binding of HMBPP as confirmed by physicochemical experiments [ , ]. Furthermore, BTN3A3 provided a minimal rescue response in BTN3 deficient cells in response to bisphosphonate treatment and allowed better rescue response to agonistic antibodies [ ]. These are important clues, leading us to think that BTN3A3 may have a distinct natural ligand of its own. Here, we describe a naturally occurring pAg that can activate γ9δ2 T cells via BTN3A3.

## Results

## Single point mutants of BTN3A3 can form a complex with phosphoantigens to activate γ9δ2 T cells
BTN3A3 shares 87% identity with BTN3A1 in their B30.2 domains (Sup. Fig. ). Despite these similarities, BTN3A3 has not been found to detect pAgs, in contrast to BTN3A1. To explore whether BTN3A3 could be endowed with functions that mimic BTN3A1, we replaced a key amino acid in BTN3A3 with the corresponding amino acid located at the same position of BTN3A1 (BTN3A3 R381H). We introduced the mutation and validated its effects through biochemical and cellular assays. Internal domain constructs of the BTN proteins were expressed and purified using E. coli for interaction tests via isothermal titration calorimetry (ITC) and nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR), while full length BTNs were transfected into CHO-K1 cells for coculturing with γ9δ2 T cells to test activation (Fig.  A).
 The BTN3A3 R381H site was chosen as prior ITC studies suggested the homologous residue contributed to pAg binding to BTN3A1 [ , ]. In agreement with these studies, wild-type BTN3A3 binds to HMBPP but this interaction is extremely weak (no Kd could be confidently defined, Fig. A, Suppl. Fig.  ). In contrast, the BTN3A3 R381H mutant shows strong affinity (Kd = 0.78 µM, Fig. B). Furthermore, HMBPP could promote the interaction of BTN3A3 R381H with BTN2A1 (Kd = 1.79 µM, Fig. C).
We were curious whether the interaction between BTN2A1 and BTN3A3 R381H mutant in the internal domains could activate γ9δ2 T cells in a manner similar to BTN2A1 and BTN3A1 in the context of the full-length proteins. To avoid endogenously expressed BTN proteins, we used the hamster cell line CHO-K1 as the testing platform because rodents completely lack a pAg detection system, endogenous BTN2A1 and BTN3 proteins, and γ9δ2 T cells. The CHO system allows for expression of different combinations of BTN2/3 proteins without interference from endogenous proteins [ ]. In coculture activation assays, the combination of BTN2A1 with BTN3A3 R381H successfully activated γ9δ2 T cells in response to pAg (Fig. D), similar to a prior report [ ]. Moreover, this combination exhibited a stronger activation effect than the BTN2A1 and BTN3A1.
We were also curious about whether a mutation to BTN2A2, M506T, would similarly allow for a T cell response, as a prior study had shown this residue to allow for the internal domains to interact [ ]. In agreement with the prior study, we too observed binding between the internal domains of BTN3A1 and BTN2A2 M506T (Kd = 0.025 µM, Sup. Fig. F). Furthermore, the internal domains of 3A3 R381H and BTN2A2 M506T also interacted (Kd = 0.031 µM, Sup. Fig. G). However, neither of these combinations were functional in the context of the full length proteins in the cellular assays (Sup. Fig. H and I), so further studies with this mutation were not pursued.
Given the structural similarity between BTN3A3 and BTN3A1, and the single point mutation enabling BTN3A3 to substitute for BTN3A1 interacting with HMBPP, BTN2A1 and γ9δ2 TCR, we hypothesized that BTN3A3 might have a natural ligand similar to HMBPP for BTN3A1. In the surface model of BTN3A1, its pocket contains three grooves that can accommodate the hydroxyl group and two phosphate groups of HMBPP (Fig.  E). In contrast, the pocket structure of BTN3A3 has only two large grooves, with a central protrusion (R381) blocking the middle. If a phosphate compound can bind BTN3A3, its structure and length are likely to differ from HMBPP.

## MPP activates γ9δ2 T cells
Figure shows the full-length protein sequences of BTN3A3 and BTN3A1 (Fig.  A). Overall, BTN3A3 shares 84.99% sequence identity with BTN3A1, and 85.51% identity when the signal peptide is excluded. Their IgV domains are almost the same with only a difference in one amino acid. Of the three BTN3A isoforms, BTN3A2 lacks a B30.2 domain, while BTN3A3 contains a distinct 70-amino-acid-long C-terminal tail of unknown three-dimensional structure (Fig.  B). This tail may be intrinsically disordered and it is unclear what its functional role is. The ligand binding B30.2 domains are also quite similar, both in structure (Fig.  E) and sequence (Fig.  A). Given the similarities of BTN3A3 to BTN3A1, and the activity of the BTN3A3 R381H mutant (Fig.  ), BTN3A3 appears to have all of the features required to serve as a pAg detecting molecule. We anticipated that its ligands would likely be diphosphates similar in structure to HMBPP, but not HMBPP itself.
 To search for a BTN3A3 ligand, we performed docking tests, evaluating multiple pAg analogs, either artificially designed diphosphates similar to HMBPP or natural phosphates and diphosphates (Sup. Fig. ). Among these compounds, three designed molecules showed high docking scores (−8.94 to −10.09). Additionally, a natural compound, mevalonate-5-diphosphate (MPP), occupied both pockets of BTN3A3, whereas the three designed compounds occupied only one pocket (Sup. Fig. B). Addition of MPP to γ9δ2 T cells resulted in their activation (Fig.  C), suggesting that MPP may act as a pAg. Because MPP is immediately upstream of known pAgs IPP and DMAPP in the mevalonate pathway, we initially attributed its pAg activity to its cellular conversion to IPP and DMAPP, which are known ligands of BTN3A1. However, we still viewed it surprising that MPP could dock into BTN binding pockets.
To investigate this issue further, additional molecular docking analysis revealed similar binding modes for IPP, DMAPP, and MPP with both BTN3A1 and BTN3A3. In each case, the head group of the ligand inserts into a groove in the binding pocket, while the two phosphate groups occupy the remaining two grooves (Fig.  D). The docking scores for BTN3A1 were comparable among the three ligands, with IPP scoring slightly lower (IPP: −7.9, DMAPP: −9.7, MPP: −9.1). In contrast, the binding pocket of BTN3A3 is partitioned into two sub-regions by a positively charged protrusion formed by R381. This portioning is not present in BTN3A1 which contains the histidine at position 381. The arginine enhances the positive electrostatic potential in the binding pocket of BTN3A3. Despite this difference, IPP, DMAPP, and MPP adopted similar binding conformations in BTN3A3, with the ligand head group occupying one groove and the diphosphate moiety positioned in another. Among the three compounds, MPP exhibited the strongest binding affinity to BTN3A3 (docking score: −8.3), while IPP and DMAPP showed scores indicating relatively weaker interactions (−5.6 and − 6.0, respectively). These findings collectively suggest that BTN3A3 likely retains the ability to bind pAgs and participate in antigen presentation, similar to BTN3A1. Moreover, MPP may represent a previously unrecognized endogenous ligand of BTN3A3. A caveat for our molecular docking models is that it is performed on a rigid protein model based on the crystal structures. It is possible that these grooved regions could be more varied and dynamic in solution.

## Dose-dependent activation of γ9δ2 T cells by MPP
Next, we evaluated the direct activation potency towards purified effector γ9δ2 T cells of several structurally similar pAgs or potential pAgs (Fig.  ). These compounds included the major microbial-derived natural pAg (HMBPP), endogenous pAgs (IPP and DMAPP), and several synthetic pAgs with the core pharmacophore (Fig.  A). They also included the newly identified natural pAg (MPP) and its precursors (Fig.  B), and indirect pAgs (Fig.  C).
 Among these compounds, the microbial-derived pAg HMBPP exhibited the highest potency (EC50 = 0.19 nM) (Fig.  A). The EC50 values of the human-derived pAgs were substantially weaker (30 µM for IPP and 7.1 µM for DMAPP) as both compounds lack the terminal hydroxyl group present in HMBPP. The synthetic analog C-HMBPP maintained potent activity (EC50 = 0.35 nM), comparable to that of HMBPP. In contrast, C,C-HMBPP, in which both the C–O–P and P–O–P bridging oxygens are replaced by carbon atoms, showed a drastic reduction in potency (EC50 = 34 µM), nearly 100,000-fold weaker than C-HMBPP. A further derivative, C,C-MBPP (lacking both bridging oxygens and the terminal hydroxyl group) exhibited even lower potency (EC50 = ~ 2.6 mM), ~ 100-fold weaker than C, C-HMBPP. C-HMBP, which lacks one phosphate group compared to C-HMBPP, showed moderate potency (EC50 = 1.8 µM), weaker than C-HMBPP but stronger than C, C-HMBPP. In contrast, the prodrug form of C-HMBP (pro-C-HMBP) displayed significantly enhanced activity (EC50 = 1.5 nM), nearly 1,000-fold more potent than its parent compound.
MPP was active as in Fig.  , but at relatively low potency, with an EC50 of 300 µM (Fig.  B). The structurally related MPP precursors, mevalonate-5-phosphate (MP) and mevalonate, failed to activate γ9δ2 T cells within the tested final concentration range (10 nM to 1 mM). Notably, MPP differs from HMBPP by possessing a longer carbon chain, lacking the olefin, with a terminal carboxyl group. These structural changes may alter the probability of binding to the B30.2 pAg binding pocket.
Figure C depicts the potency of 6-fluoromevalonate (6-FM) and risedronate, which are compounds that are known inhibitors of enzymes in the mevalonate pathway, MPPD and FDPS, respectively. The inhibition of these proteins would alter concentrations of endogenous pAgs (IPP and DMAPP), so the potency seen for risedronate was expected as it is known to increase pAg levels. However, the potency of 6-FM was surprising because it was expected to decrease levels of endogenous pAgs.
Together, these results allow us to delineate a detailed structure–activity relationship (SAR) for pAgs (Table  ). HMBPP represents the most potent and structurally complete pAg. Loss of its terminal hydroxyl group, removal of one phosphate, or substitution of bridging oxygen atoms with carbon in the diphosphate moiety each result in a progressive loss of BTN protein binding affinity and corresponding reduction in γ9δ2 T cell activation. From a structural perspective, the core pharmacophore for γ9δ2 T cell recognition appears to be one phosphate group attached to a methylbutenyl side chain, though surprisingly MPP, with its structural differences, retains some activity, suggesting a different mechanism.
 Taken together, the established pAgs demonstrate similar structure- activity relationships [ ] in this assay relative to our well described proliferation assay. A caveat to this analysis is that it assumes the compounds have similar Emax values, as some of the weaker pAgs did not plateau at our highest concentration. The current approach has allowed us the ability to more precisely determine the potency of weaker pAgs, such as MPP, and exclude the activity of related compounds mevalonate and MP up to 1 mM concentrations.

## Binding of MPP and other pAgs to BTN3A3 and BTN3A1
We next examined the binding of MPP to BTN3A3. We used ITC to test the ability of MPP and other pAgs to bind to BTN3A3 (Sup. Fig. ). Here, we found that BTN3A3 was able to bind to each pAg tested, but the binding was too weak to accurately determine a Kd value. Still, there was a notable difference in enthalpy, where MPP displayed a stronger change in enthalpy for binding to BTN3A3 compared to HMBPP, IPP or DMAPP. Because the strength of MPP interaction with BTN3A3 was not strong enough to be quantified by ITC at the protein concentration we were able to achieve, we turned to NMR, which is better situated for analysis of low affinity interactions. We performed 31P NMR spectroscopy to examine the potential interactions of IPP, DMAPP, MPP, or HMBPP with BTN3A3 or BTN3A1 by observing the phosphorus atoms within the diphosphate moiety (Fig. ). For each protein, the full internal domain constructs were used [ , ]. For IPP, both the α- and β-phosphate phosphorus atoms exhibited chemical shift perturbations upon interaction with either BTN3A3 or BTN3A1. These changes in shifts were more pronounced at a 2:1 IPP: protein molar ratio compared to a 10:1 ratio, indicating a larger population of bound ligand under near-stoichiometric conditions. The chemical shift perturbations induced by BTN3A1 were greater than those induced by BTN3A3. Similarly, DMAPP also underwent chemical shift perturbations in both its α- and β-phosphate phosphorus peaks upon interaction with either BTN3A3 or BTN3A1. Stronger shifts were observed for 4:1 DMAPP: BTN3A1 compared to 2:1 DMAPP: BTN3A3. These results suggest that IPP and DMAPP bind stronger to BTN3A1 but also interact with BTN3A3.
 In contrast, MPP displayed a different pattern. The α-phosphate showed minimal shift changes while the β-phosphate exhibited clear chemical shift perturbations in the presence of either BTN3A3 or BTN3A1. Stronger shift changes were seen at the 2:1 ratio. Interestingly, MPP underwent a more pronounced chemical shift perturbation in response to BTN3A3 than BTN3A1, which suggests that MPP binds stronger to BTN3A3. However, it also interacts with BTN3A1.
For HMBPP, both α- and β-phosphate chemical shifts were changed upon interaction with BTN3A3, with greater shifts again observed at the 2:1 ratio compared to the 10:1 condition. Thus, HMBPP can interact with BTN3A3. For BTN3A1, significant line broadening was observed at a 10:1 ratio, which aligns well with the established high binding affinity reported in the literature. Our previously published data had no observable peaks at a 1:1 ratio for BTN3A1 [ ]. Altogether, these pAg:3A3 and pAg:3A1 31P NMR results support the hypothesis that MPP binds to BTN3A3, in addition to reinforcing known interactions between BTN3A1 and canonical pAgs such as IPP, DMAPP, and HMBPP. A caveat for comparing chemical shift perturbations between BTN3A1 and BTN3A3 is that it assumes similar local environments of the phosphorus atoms in the bound state. It is possible that the environments are not similar, reducing accuracy of the direct comparison.

## Differential interactions of the BTN3-pAg complexes with BTN2A1
In the current model of HMBPP detection, HMBPP acts as a molecular glue to promote the binding of the B30.2 domains of BTN3A1 and BTN2A1. We wanted to investigate whether MPP similarly could promote the interaction of the BTN3A3 internal domain with the BTN2A1 internal domain. We first examined whether IPP, DMAPP, and MPP could interact with BTN2A1 (Fig. ). Our previous study had already shown that BTN2A1 does not bind to HMBPP by 31P NMR [ ]. Here, no changes were observed for the β-phosphate of IPP and DMAPP, while their α-phosphates exhibited very slight chemical shift perturbations. In the case of MPP, we observed a chemical shift change of the β-phosphate at an MPP:2A1 ratio of 1:1. Based on these NMR observations, we infer that MPP may bind to BTN2A1 (Fig. A). Molecular docking studies (Sup. Fig. ) are consistent with this experimental data.
 We next examined whether IPP, DMAPP, MPP, and HMBPP exhibit enhanced interactions when BTN2A1 is added into BTN3A3 (Fig.  B) or BTN3A1 (Fig.  C), compared to interactions with BTN3A3 or BTN3A1 alone. For IPP, the combination with BTN3A3 + BTN2A1 induced a mild chemical shift perturbation and minimal line broadening of the α-phosphate signal. In contrast, the BTN3A1 + BTN2A1 combination resulted in more pronounced line broadening for both α- and β-phosphate peaks, indicating that BTN2A1 binds more robustly to BTN3A1–IPP instead of BTN3A3-IPP. Similarly, for DMAPP, a slight chemical shift perturbation was observed at the α-phosphate in the presence of BTN3A3 + 2A1 compared with BTN3A3 only with DMAPP. However, in the BTN3A1 + BTN2A1 condition, BTN2A1 induced a pronounced chemical shift perturbation at the α-phosphate and noticeable line broadening and chemical shift perturbation at the β-phosphate of DMAPP. These results support that DMAPP binds strongly to BTN3A1– BTN2A1 and may interact weakly with BTN3A3– BTN2A1.
For MPP, only a chemical shift change of the α-phosphate was observed in the presence of BTN3A3 + BTN2A1, while its interaction with BTN3A1 + BTN2A1 was subtle. These chemical shift perturbations, compared with IPP or DMAPP binding to BTN3A1 + BTN2A1, are insufficient to support our hypothesis that MPP binds to either BTN3A3– BTN2A1 or BTN3A1– BTN2A1 to form a complex. In contrast, HMBPP showed line broadening at the β-phosphate in addition to the minor chemical shift changes at the α-phosphate when combined with BTN3A3 + BTN2A1. When tested with BTN3A1 + BTN2A1, the observed β-phosphate peaks are close to the peaks of the free ligand, which is indicative that the bound complex of BTN3A1-HMBPP becomes fully saturated to become BTN3A1-HMBPP- BTN2A1. Thus, we do not see the BTN3A1-HMBPP- BTN2A1 complex due to slow exchange, but there is excess free ligand that can be observed. Still, there is some modest chemical shift perturbation of the α-phosphate for HMBPP in the presence of BTN3A1 + BTN2A1.
Although MPP was capable of binding to BTN3A3 and BTN2A1 individually, the NMR signals observed in the combined BTN3A3 + BTN2A1 condition did not show the strong interaction patterns seen with IPP or DMAPP in the BTN3A1 + BTN2A1 context. Therefore, it remains unclear from this data whether the individual interactions of MPP with BTN3A3 and MPP with BTN2A1 can promote formation of a BTN3A3-MPP-BTN2A1 complex. ## Confirmation of the BTN3A3-MPP-BTN2A1 complex
One limitation to our 31P NMR experiments is the overall sensitivity for observation, which necessitated the usage of higher ligand concentrations. This meant that sample conditions were generally at a stoichiometric (1:1) ratio or with excess ligand. In the case of weak association, the population of unbound ligand would be high comparative to the bound ligand, which would make observations through chemical shift perturbations or line broadening difficult. An alternative approach is to observe the protein, rather than the small molecule, by incorporating fluorine atoms into its residues using 5-fluorotryptophan. The addition of excess ligand would be expected to better saturate the system, pushing the observable signal closer to the bound protein state (Fig.  ).
 Figure A shows the seven tryptophan residues of the BTN3A3 construct. There are three Trp residues that reside in the core (W397, W407, W432) and four Trp residues exposed to the solvent (W314, W332, W380, W422). The solvent exposed residues are located in the JM region (W314, W332) or near the pAg binding pocket (W380, W422). The exposed residues are visible due to the difference in relaxation times as the core residues would have much lower signal-to-noise (around − 123.4 ppm) compared to the solvent exposed residues (around − 125 ppm) (Fig.  B). Due to the nature of the overlapping peaks of the seven Trp residues, it is impossible to assess finer binding details. The addition of MPP at a 1:4 ratio could potentially affect the Trp residues near the binding pocket (W380, W422), but the signal overlap obscures any potential perturbations.
However, we still can observe that there is a large drop in signal to noise when BTN2A1 is added in addition to MPP (Fig.  B). This is an indication that a larger complex is formed between BTN3A3-MPP-BTN2A1 as the complex would have a lower sensitivity due to larger size and slower rotational correlation rate. The decrease in sensitivity is also observed to be concentration dependent as the increase of MPP from a 1:1:2 ratio to 1:1:4 ratio further impairs the observable signal. Furthermore, the drop in sensitivity is not due to protein-protein interaction or crowding as the observed signal of BTN3A3 in the presence of BTN2A1 is comparable to BTN3A3 alone.

## 6-FM activates γ9δ2 T cells through both BTN2A1-BTN3A3-BTN3A2 and BTN2A1-BTN3A1-BTN3A2 complexes
Having established that MPP displays pAg activity and interacts with BTN3A3, we next wanted to evaluate the specific BTNs required for cellular detection of MPP (Fig. ). Figure A shows the mevalonate pathway in eukaryotes. The mevalonate pathway, also known as the HMG-CoA reductase pathway, is a crucial metabolic pathway that produces isoprenoids, such as cholesterol, dolichol, ubiquinone, and prenyl groups that are essential for protein modification. In this pathway, HMG-CoA is converted to mevalonate by HMG-CoA reductase, the rate-limiting step of the pathway [ ]. Mevalonate undergoes phosphorylation by mevalonate kinase to MP, which undergoes phosphorylation by phosphomevalonate kinase to MPP. Then MPP undergoes decarboxylation by MPP decarboxylase (MPPD) to generate IPP, which is in equilibrium with DMAPP maintained by isopentenyl diphosphate isomerase. Both IPP and DMAPP are the basic 5-carbon isoprene units. IPP and DMAPP combine to form larger isoprenoids by farnesyl diphosphate synthase (FDPS). It is well established that nitrogenous bisphosphonate drugs such as risedronate (Ris) directly inhibit FDPS, leading to the elimination downstream larger isoprenoids and accumulation upstream diphosphates (IPP and DMAPP), which are detected by BTN3A1.
 We initially suspected that the pAg activity of MPP would result from its conversion to IPP/DMAPP which could engage BTN3A1. To address this issue, we revisited 6-FM. 6-FM is a cell permeable prodrug that undergoes ring opening, followed by conversion by mevalonate kinase and phosphomevalonate kinase to 6-FM-5-diphosphate (6-FM-PP) [ ]. The 6-FM-PP is thought to inhibit MPPD, which would be expected to reduce downstream products IPP and DMAPP and increase the concentrations of the reactant MPP, parallel to the mechanism of risedronate, which reduces FPP and increases IPP and DMAPP.
We again tested the effect of 6-FM on γ9δ2 T cells (Fig.  B). 6-FM activated γ9δ2 T cells at concentrations of 100 µM and 1 mM, and the simultaneous treatment of 100 µM 6-FM and 100 µM MPP resulted in stronger activation. To determine whether 6-FM and MPP are detected by BTN3A1 similar to HMBPP, we transfected full-length BTN2A1 + BTN3A2, BTN2A1 + BTN3A2 + BTN3A3, and BTN2A1 + BTN3A2 + BTN3A1 into CHO-K1 cells and incubated with 6-FM and MPP. The transfected and pAg-loaded CHO-K1 cells were cocultured with γ9δ2 T cells. We were able to observe that 6-FM and MPP can activate γ9δ2 T cells through BTN2A1 + BTN3A2 + BTN3A3, with no BTN3A1 present in the system. The combination of BTN2A1 + BTN3A2 + BTN3A1 can also activate γ9δ2 T cells by 6-FM (Fig.  C). However, a particularly intriguing aspect is that the BTN2A1 + BTN3A2 + BTN3A3 combination works for 6-FM but does not work for risedronate (Fig.  D). Only the BTN2A1 + BTN3A2 + BTN3A1 combination works to detect risedronate and activate γ9δ2 T cells. This result implies that MPP accumulation in response to 6-FM can simultaneously activate BTN3A3 and BTN3A1, while IPP and DMAPP accumulation in response to risedronate can only activate BTN3A1 and not BTN3A3.
Although we observed activation of γ9δ2 T cells by BTN2A1 + BTN3A2 + BTN3A3 through 6-FM stimulation, the response was relatively low; normal IFN-γ production in coculture by synthetic pAg is much stronger. Therefore, a longer coculture time of 48 h was tested. Here, we observed that the degree of γ9δ2 T cell activation of upon 6-FM stimulation on BTN3A3 and BTN3A1 increased by approximately threefold compared to the 20-hour coculture (Fig.  E), while the effect of risedronate stimulation reached nearly an eightfold increase (Fig.  F). Notably, even with an extended coculture time, BTN3A3 still did not show responsiveness to risedronate.
Given the novelty of the pAg detection by BTN3A3, we further tested BTN3A1 knockout K562 cells, which were previously shown to abrogate the HMBPP response. These cells were still able to activate γ9δ2 T cells upon 6-FM stimulation (Fig.  G).
The purpose of adding BTN3A2 in the combination is to enhance the response of BTN3A3 and BTN3A1, as our previous research found that BTN3A2 enhanced BTN2A1 + BTN3A1 induced γ9δ2 T cell activation. To determine whether BTN3A2 is necessary for the BTN3A3-mediated detection of MPP, we removed BTN3A2 and used a longer coculture time to see if the BTN3A3 or BTN3A1 still worked the same way. The combination of BTN2A1 + BTN3A3 can still activate γ9δ2 T cells after 6-FM stimulation, and 6-FM + MPP together got significantly stronger activation (Fig.  H). BTN2A1 + BTN3A1 can still activate γ9δ2 T cells upon 6-FM stimulation. Similarly, risedronate only works with BTN2A1 + BTN3A1 but not with BTN2A1 + BTN3A3 (Fig.  I).

## 6-FM increases MPP while risedronate increases DMAPP/IPP, but not MPP, in CHO-K1 cells
Finally, given the pattern of activity of 6-FM, we wanted to verify that these inhibitors indeed have the expected differential effects on the mevalonate pathway metabolites. We used LC-MS to examine the effects of 6-FM and risedronate on the cellular accumulation of MPP, DMAPP, and IPP (Fig.  ; Table  ). In Fig.  , the four vertical panels on the left show the untreated CHO-K1 cell lysates. From 0 to 20 h, no significant changes or prominent compounds were observed, as expected. The black peaks at the top correspond to an unidentified compound of the same mass as 6-FM-PP, but different retention time, found naturally and stably in the lysates. MPP and 6-FM-PP eluted at 6.43–6.46 min and 6.36–6.38 min, respectively, and could be identified by their different masses and the characteristic fluorine. DMAPP and IPP have identical masses, and due to their small difference in polarity of the olefin isomers relative to the highly polar diphosphate could not be separated, and they co-eluted at 5.66–5.67 min. For 6-FM-treated CHO-K1 cells, at 2 h, we observed the conversion of 6-FM to 6-FM-PP, along with a striking increase in MPP concentration—from undetectable levels in untreated cells to an integrated peak intensity of 1200 at 2 h. DMAPP/IPP exhibited a slightly elevated peak compared to baseline, with an intensity of 38. At 5 h post 6-FM treatment, MPP accumulation further increased, reaching a peak intensity of 3000, while 6-FM-PP and DMAPP/IPP showed minor increases. By 20 h, the MPP peak intensity rose to 8200, whereas 6-FM-PP and DMAPP/IPP reached intensities of 750 and 210, respectively. Based on calculated concentrations, the average MPP level in 6-FM-treated CHO-K1 cells at 20 h was 11 µM (from three replicates), while the average DMAPP/IPP concentration was 0.67 µM, making MPP about 17 times more abundant than DMAPP/IPP, which is close to the EC50 ratio of MPP: DMAPP/IPP = 16:1.
 In risedronate-treated CHO-K1 cells, MPP remained undetectable at 2 h (intensity = 0), while DMAPP/IPP reached an intensity of 180. At 5 h, DMAPP/IPP accumulation showed little change (intensity = 180), whereas MPP increased slightly to an intensity of 10. By 20 h, DMAPP/IPP intensity surged to 2500, while MPP reached only 33. Given the high negative charge of risedronate, its cellular entry is expected to be slower than the charge neutral 6-FM, resulting in a delay metabolite increase. The calculated average concentrations at 20 h were 4.0 µM for DMAPP/IPP and 0.028 µM for MPP, indicating DMAPP/IPP was about 140-fold higher than MPP. Comparative analysis revealed that after 20 h of treatment, 6-FM induced substantial MPP accumulation with minimal DMAPP/IPP buildup, whereas risedronate primarily triggered DMAPP/IPP accumulation with negligible MPP production. Given the EC50 of MPP, the trace levels of MPP under risedronate treatment are likely insufficient to exhibit activity. These findings support the functional tests, in that 6-FM increases MPP and works through BTN3A3 and/or BTN3A1 while risedronate increases IPP/DMAPP and works only through BTN3A1. Some residual minor IPP/DMAPP accumulation in response to 6-FM was surprisingly observed, which could contribute to the dual activity of 6-FM acting through both BTN3A3 and BTN3A1.

## Discussion
In summary, we present here that BTN3A3 can be activated to stimulate γ9δ2 T cells. The evidence chain points to MPP, a newly identified endogenous pAg, as the key mediator with low but likely physiologically relevant potency. The strongest evidence presented here includes: (1) BTN3A3 R381H mutation can fully detect HMBPP; (2) MPP and 6-FM activate γ9δ2 T cells; (3) MPP binds to WT BTN3A3; (4) BTN2A1 can form a complex with BTN3A3 + MPP; (5) 6-FM activates γ9δ2 T cells in the absence of BTN3A1; (6) treatment of CHO-K1 cells with 6-FM, but not risedronate, leads to significant MPP accumulation; and (7) the pattern of activity is different between 6-FM and risedronate due to their differential impact on the mevalonate pathway.
In the SAR study, we tested numerous structurally similar pAgs and analogs, determining their EC50 values. Based on these results, we identified the core structural motif of BTN3A1-targeted pAgs: a single phosphate group with a methyl-butenyl side chain. Further modifications, such as diphosphate structures or terminal hydroxyl groups, significantly enhanced activity. At the same time, MPP contains a different core motif, which suggests a different mechanism of action. This discovery provides valuable guidance for defining pAgs and designing future drugs targeting this pathway and whether specific binding towards BTN3A3 versus BTN3A1 can be engineered.
The 31P NMR results provide strong support for MPP binding to BTN3A3, and similarly strong support for DMAPP, IPP, and HMBPP binding to BTN3A1. Upon introduction of BTN2A1, the formation of complexes between DMAPP, IPP, HMBPP and the BTN3A1-BTN2A1 combination is well-supported by the observed line broadening. While MPP shows further chemical shifts in the presence of BTN3A3 and BTN2A1, the line broadening is not as pronounced as seen with DMAPP, IPP, and HMBPP in the BTN3A1-2A1 system. Although these results do not strongly support the formation of a stable MPP-BTN3A3-2A1 complex, this is, in part, due to the usage of ligand in excess of protein. The larger population of free ligand would make it more difficult to observe the bound state. Our 19F approach helped to alleviate this issue as we can label BTN3A3 while adding ligand in excess, allowing better saturation of 19F-BTN3A3. The concentration dependent decrease in signal sensitivity provides evidence that BTN2A1 is able to form a complex with BTN3A3-MPP.
The NMR data presented was acquired in PBS solution with phosphate used as the reference for 31P frequencies adjustment. It was observed that inorganic phosphate exhibited its own chemical shift perturbation, which we believe is due to the presence of protein (Sup. Fig. ). Experiments with external referencing showed that this chemical shift perturbation of inorganic phosphate was real and the shift was more pronounced with increasing protein concentration. Thus, it would appear that the chemical shift of the phosphate moiety is a dynamic system based on solvent interactions (hydration shells). These solvation effects are likely to influence the diphosphates of the pAgs as well (Sup. Fig. ), making the chemical shift perturbations a bit more complex as it depends both on solvation effects and protein binding. For instance, the chemical shift perturbations seen for BTN2A1 with MPP may exhibit a stronger solvation effect. That may explain why we see the shift only for the MPP with BTN2A1, but not when BTN2A1 is added in addition to BTN3A1 or BTN3A3. If the binding of MPP to BTN2A1 is real, it could suggest an alternative possibility that independent binding of MPP to both BTN3A3 and BTN2A1 may induce some conformational changes or other associations that collectively lead to γ9δ2 T cell activation.
Nonetheless, internal referencing to inorganic phosphate is still able to show relative chemical shift perturbations of the pAgs, reflecting effects from protein-ligand interactions. Additional peak broadening is separate from solvation effects since line broadening is predominantly due to a slower rotational correlation rate due to protein association. The usage of inorganic phosphate as the buffer and internal reference also affects the binding affinity of the pAgs. BTN3A1 and IPP exhibited stronger chemical shift perturbations and line broadening when experiments are performed in Tris buffer compared to phosphate buffer (Sup. Fig. ). The binding pocket of BTN3A1 and BTN3A3 has been shown to bind diphosphate pAgs, so it is not unexpected that inorganic phosphate may competitively bind and, thus, impact the binding of pAgs. This may be biologically relevant as the intracellular concentration of phosphate may be what keeps the BTN proteins in an inactive state and they become active only once the concentration of pAg is able to exceed a certain threshold.
We used several cellular approaches to investigate the response to MPP and 6-FM. CHO-K1 cells do not naturally contain any BTN molecules, allowing us to selectively express different combinations of the BTNs. Minimal expression of BTN2A1 and BTN3A3 was sufficient to give a response to 6-FM. At the same time, depletion of BTN3A1 from K562 cells failed to abrogate their response to 6-FM. These findings change our understanding of the model of pAg detection, whereas BTN3A1 or BTN3A3 can bind to pAgs, with each protein primarily binding to HMBPP or MPP, respectively. It remains unclear whether a more potent exogenous BTN3A3 ligand exists.
A key question arising from our data is how specific the MPP binding is to BTN3A3 relative to BTN3A1, as both the NMR showed binding of BTN3A1 to MPP and the cellular studies showed a pAg response to MPP in the absence of BTN3A3. The LC-MS results demonstrate that after 20-hour 6-FM treatment, cells accumulate large amounts of MPP (11 µM) along with smaller quantities of DMAPP/IPP (0.67 µM), giving a ratio of 17:1 - close to the ratio of their EC50 values (16:1). The high EC50 of MPP is consistent with our NMR data that suggests association to BTN3A1 or BTN3A3 is fairly weak. Therefore, complex formation necessitates MPP to be in higher concentrations. Under these conditions, both BTN2A1 + BTN3A3 and BTN2A1 + BTN3A1 combinations show activation in response to 6-FM. Conversely, in risedronate-treated cells where DMAPP/IPP accumulates to high levels (4.0 µM) while MPP remains minimal (0.028 µM), only the BTN2A1 + BTN3A1 combination shows activation, with BTN2A1 + BTN3A3 remaining inactive. These findings may suggest that in 6-FM treated cells, γ9δ2 T cell activation occurs primarily through MPP acting on the BTN2A1 + BTN3A3, but also occurs with the small amount of DMAPP/IPP acting on BTN2A1 + BTN3A1. In risedronate-treated cells, activation occurs exclusively through DMAPP/IPP acting on BTN2A1 + BTN3A1, as the negligible amount of MPP produced is insufficient to activate the BTN2A1 + BTN3A3. This leads us to propose that MPP primarily activates through BTN3A3 while DMAPP/IPP primarily activate through BTN3A1.
It should be noted that small diphosphates, such as HMBPP, and small bisphosphonates, such as risedronate, are restricted in their diffusion across biological membranes [ ]. The uptake of bisphosphonates is dependent upon solute carrier 37 (SLC37) [ ] while the uptake of HMBPP is energy dependent [ ]. As MPP has a similar charge to mass ratio, it too may likely need an active transport process to enter antigen detecting/presenting cells. Alternatively, as an endogenous pAg, there may be some consequences of infection or stress that cause it to become elevated intercellularly without the need for transport. Further studies on the physiological conditions leading to elevated MPP are certainly warranted.
Regarding the mevalonate pathway, it was somewhat surprising that risedronate treatment only elevated IPP/DMAPP, and not MPP, considering the latter is only one more step upstream in the pathway. It is possible that the irreversible MPPD reaction is not product inhibited, or alternatively, that there is some unrecognized feedback mechanism from IPP/DMAPP to the enzymes further upstream in the pathway [ ]. Future studies are needed to clarify this finding.

## Isothermal titration calorimetry (ITC)
The ITC experiments were conducted using a TA low-volume nano-ITC instrument. Briefly, the instrument was cleaned and the degassed dd-water was added into the reference cell. Test samples were also degassed before use. The titrand was loaded into the sample cell, and the titrant into the injection syringe. After the system was stabilized, the titration was performed, and the heat release data was recorded to analyze the interaction between the samples. The ITC buffer was 50 mM Tris, 100 mM NaCl, 5 mM 2-mercaptoethanol, pH 7.5. The experiment parameters were 25 °C, 20 injections, 2.5 µL per injection, 200 s injection interval, and 200 rpm stirring rate. The data were analyzed using NanoAnalyze (TA Instruments).

## Docking
Small molecule docking studies were performed using Maestro 13.8. Specifically, PDB protein data (BTN3A1 PDB: 5ZXK; BTN3A3 PDB: 5ZZ3; BTN2A1 PDB: 8IGT) and BTN3A3 models generated by AlphaFold were imported into Maestro 13.8, and protein preparation was carried out using the OPLS_2005 force field. The grid was set to a size of 24 Å. Ligand preparation was completed using the OPLS_2005 force field with pH 7 ± 2 charge and chirality generation. Docking was performed in extra precision (XP) Glide mode.

## Cell culture
CHO-K1 cells were cultured at 37 °C and 5% CO2 in CHO medium (DMEM containing 1.5 g/L sodium bicarbonate with 10% (v/v) heat-inactivated FBS, 10 mM non-essential amino acids, and 1x penicillin-streptomycin). The γ9δ2 T cells were cultured at 37 °C and 5% CO2 in T cell medium (RPMI-1640 medium containing 1.5 g/L sodium bicarbonate with 10% v/v heat-inactivated FBS, 0.0004% v/v 2-mercaptoethanol, 10 mM sodium pyruvate, 10 mM non-essential amino acids, 10 mM HEPES pH 7.6 and 1x penicillin-streptomycin). The K562 cells were cultured at 37 °C and 5% CO2 in T cell medium as well.

## γ9δ2 T cell isolation and expansion
The γ9δ2 T cells were isolated and expanded from human peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) as described [ ]. Briefly, PBMCs were purified from human buffy coat by density centrifugation. PBMCs were resuspended in T cell medium and stimulated with 100 nM HMBPP for 72 h and cultured for up to 14 days in an incubator at 37 °C and 5% CO2. Human IL-2 (5 ng/ml) was supplemented every 3 days. After 12–14 days, γ9δ2 T cells were purified by negative selection using the Miltenyi TCR γ/δ+ T Cell Isolation Kit (#130–092–892). Cells purity was assessed by flow cytometry using PE anti-CD3 (Clone UCHT1) and APC anti-TCR γ/δ (Clone B1) and was >95% (Sup. Fig. ).

## γ9δ2 T cell direct ELISA
The γ9δ2 T cells were counted and adjusted to 100,000 cells/mL, then 120 µL of the cells and 40 µL of medium were added into each well of a 96 well plate. Then 20 µL of the pAgs or potential pAgs at varying doses were added into the 96 well plate. The treated γ9δ2 T cells were cultured for 20 h, then the IFN-γ secreted from γ9δ2 T cells was detected by ELISA kit according to the manufacturer’s protocol.

## BTN protein expression and purification
The internal domain constructs of the BTNs were cloned into the NdeI and XhoI cut sites of the pET21b vector as described previously [ , ]. For this study, we applied similar approaches to BTN2A2 (287–523) and BTN3A3 (272–584), using the following primers: BTN2A2-287-F, 5’-GCT ATC CAT ATG AAG AAA CTT CAA AGG GAA AAA AAG AT-3’; BTN2A2-R, 5’-GCT ATC CTC GAG TAG GCT CTG GTG GGT C-3’; BTN3A3-272-F, 5’-GCT ATC CAT ATG AGA CAA CAG AAG GAA AAA ATT GCT-3’; BTN3A3-R, 5’-GCT ATC CTC GAG GTA AAG TGC TTC AGT GCG TG-3’. The sequences were confirmed by Sanger sequencing.
A glycerol stock of Rosetta 2 pLysS cells carrying the pET21b plasmid with the protein of interest was used to start an overnight culture in Luria Bertani (LB) broth at 37 °C with a shaking speed of 225 RPM. The next day, the overnight culture was transferred to a larger flask containing LB broth (40x dilution) and cultured until the OD600 reached 0.4–0.6. The flask was transferred to a room temperature shaker and incubated for 1 h. The culture was induced by adding a final concentration of 1 mM IPTG and protein expression was carried out overnight (~ 18 h) at room temperature. The culture was pelleted down and stored at −20 °C until needed.
For 5-fluorotryptophan labeling, a glycerol stock of Rosetta 2 pLysS cells carrying the pET21b plasmid with the protein of interest was used to start an overnight culture in LB at 37 °C with a shaking speed of 225 RPM. The next day, the overnight culture was pelleted to remove excess LB before transferring to M9 minimal media (40x dilution) with glucose and ammonium chloride as the sole carbon and nitrogen sources, respectively. Once the culture reached an OD600 of 0.4–0.6, the flask was transferred to a room temperature shaker and incubated for 1 h. The appropriate amount of 5-fluoroindole was dissolved in 1 mL of DMSO and added to the media for a final concentration of 100 mg/L. The culture was allowed to shake for 30 min before it was induced by adding a final concentration of 1 mM IPTG. Protein expression experiments were carried out overnight (~ 18 h) at room temperature. The culture was pelleted down and stored at −20 °C until needed.
For purification, the cell pellets were resuspended in buffer (50 mM sodium phosphate, 200 mM sodium chloride, 5 mM imidazole, 10 mM 2-mercaptoethanol, 1 mM PMSF, pH 7.4) and lysed by three passages through a French press homogenizer. The lysate was centrifuged at 10,000 x g for an hour at 4 °C and then the supernatant is incubated with Ni-NTA resin (Qiagen) at 4 °C for 45 min. The resin was washed (50 mM sodium phosphate, 200 mM sodium chloride, 20 mM imidazole, 10 mM 2-mercaptoethanol, pH 7.4) prior to eluting the protein in elution buffer (50 mM sodium phosphate, 200 mM sodium chloride, 300 mM imidazole, 10 mM 2-mercaptoethanol, pH 7.4).The eluted protein was further purified using a Superdex 75 16/600 Hi-load Column equilibrated with the final buffer. Protein fractions were collected and concentrated if needed for further studies.

## 31P NMR
Samples were prepared in a buffer consisting of 50 mM sodium phosphate, 100 mM sodium chloride, 5 mM 2-mercaptoethanol, 10% D2O, and 1 mM DSS, pH 7.5. Data collection was performed on a Bruker AVANCE 500 MHz spectrometer, equipped with a BBFO smart probe, at 25 °C without decoupling. Data processing and analysis was done using MestReNova provided by NMRBox [ ]. Spectra were aligned based on the inorganic phosphate peak, which was set to 2.18 ppm. Intensities between spectra were normalized to account for differences in concentration using the concentration of the inorganic phosphate peak as a reference point. Baseline was corrected automatically and a 10 Hz line broadening function was used. For measuring peak width, peak deconvolution was used to delineate the peaks in the doublet (β-phosphate) and the doublet of triplets (α-phosphate). Due to the nature of the overlapping peaks for the α-phosphate, only peak widths for the β-phosphate were used for comparison purposes. Protein concentrations for each sample varied between 0.1 mM and 1 mM whereas ligand concentrations varied between 0.5 mM and 2 mM. A complete table of the concentrations for each sample can be found in the supplemental information (Sup. Table ).

## 19F NMR
Samples were prepared in a buffer consisting of 50 mM sodium phosphate, 100 mM sodium chloride, 5 mM 2-mercaptoethanol, 10% D2O, 1 mM sodium 2,2-dimethyl-2-silapentane-5-sulfonate (DSS), 0.1 mM trifluoroacetic acid (TFA), and pH 7.5. Data collection was performed on a Bruker AVANCE 500 MHz spectrometer, equipped with a BBFO smart probe, at 25 °C with proton decoupling. Data processing and analysis was done using MestReNova provided by NMRBox [ ]. Spectra were aligned based on the TFA peak, which was set to −75.6 ppm. Intensities between spectra were normalized to account for differences in concentration using the concentration of TFA as a reference point. Baseline was corrected automatically and a 20 Hz line broadening function was used. Sample concentrations of protein ranged from 0.9 mM to 1.3 mM whereas ligand concentration varied between 2 mM and 5 mM. A complete table of the concentrations can be found in the supplemental information (Sup. Table ).
