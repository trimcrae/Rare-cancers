## ABSTRACT
Steroid‐induced osteonecrosis of the femoral head is a severe osteoarticular condition resulting from glucocorticoid overuse, characterized by femoral head bone structure collapse and cell death, now predominant among nontraumatic femoral head necroses. The increasing clinical use of glucocorticoids has led to a rise in the incidence of steroid‐induced osteonecrosis of the femoral head, yet its precise molecular mechanisms remain incompletely understood, posing challenges for clinical management. This review proposes that the “GC‐induced metabolic‐inflammatory‐oxidative stress vicious cycle” serves as the core driver propelling the activation of the SONFH multi‐pathway PCD network. Centered on this thesis, the review systematically examines the synergistic and antagonistic interactions among PCD pathways—including pyroptosis, autophagy, and ferroptosis—in SONFH, emphasizing the pivotal role of mitochondrial dysfunction and ROS bursts. This framework not only integrates the independent functions of each PCD pathway but also reveals their interwoven molecular networks, offering novel perspectives for developing multi‐target synergistic therapeutic strategies.
Keywords: autophagy, ferroptosis, necroptosis, programmed cell death, pyroptosis, steroid‐induced osteonecrosis of the femoral head

## Introduction
Steroid‐induced osteonecrosis of the femoral head (SONFH) is primarily caused by excessive glucocorticoid (GC) use, resulting in significant subchondral deformation, femoral head cell death, and structural changes leading to femoral head collapse. The incidence of SONFH has been progressively rising due to the widespread use of GC. Current literature indicates that SONFH has become the most prevalent form of nontraumatic femoral head necrosis, constituting 51% of cases [ , ]. For instance, patients with systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) and severe acute respiratory distress syndrome (SARS) receiving GC treatment exhibit an increased risk of SONFH [ , , ]. Programmed cell death (PCD) is a regulated cell death process influenced by intrinsic signals and external stimuli. PCD encompasses various forms such as pyroptosis, autophagy, ferroptosis, cuprotosis, necroptosis, and apoptosis [ ]. The pathogenesis of SONFH remains unclear, with proposed theories including apoptosis, autophagy, lipid metabolism disorders, endothelial cell injury, coagulation abnormalities, and oxidative stress [ ]. Nonetheless, as research progresses on SONFH and PCD, multiple PCD pathways have emerged as crucial in the management and recovery of SONFH. The pathogenesis of SONFH is complex, with traditional theories predominantly focusing on single pathways. Recent research on PCD suggests the existence of multilevel, time‐dependent network regulation. This paper adopts this perspective, proposing that the “GC‐induced metabolic‐inflammatory‐oxidative stress vicious cycle” constitutes the pivotal mechanism initiating and amplifying the PCD network (Figure  ; Table  ).
 Subsequent sections will systematically analyze the positioning and interactions of various PCD pathways within this cycle, centered upon this core argument.

## Methods
A comprehensive literature search was conducted via PubMed, Web of Science, and the China National Knowledge Infrastructure (CNKI) database. The search scope encompassed research publications on SONFH and PCD published between 2000 and 2025. Search terms included SONFH, pyroptosis, autophagy, ferroptosis, necroptosis, cuproptosis, oxeiptosis, NETosis, alkaliptosis, and disulfidptosis.
Inclusion criteria encompassed all research designs investigating PCD in SONFH. Studies unrelated to the core theme were excluded. Furthermore, clinical studies or trials published solely as abstracts without full text or supplementary data were also excluded. The initial database search yielded 1512 publications. Following abstract screening and exclusion of irrelevant studies, 81 high‐quality relevant publications were ultimately included. A brief flowchart is presented in Figure  .

## Pyroptosis
3.1.

## Molecular Mechanisms of Pyroptosis
Pyroptosis is a PCD process characterized by the formation of cell membrane pores, membrane rupture, and the subsequent release of cellular contents and inflammatory factors. These events are orchestrated by gasdermin proteins, specifically GSDMB, GSDMC, gasdermin D (GSDMD), GSDME, and GSDMF (also known as PJVK/DFNB59) [ ]. Pyroptosis involves three main pathways: The classical pathway mediated by caspase‐1, the nonclassical pathway involving caspase‐4/‐5/‐11, and alternative pathways triggered by caspase‐3/‐8, granzyme A/B, and streptococcal pyrogenic exotoxin B (SpeB) [ ]. In SONFH, GC promotes disease progression by regulating fibroblast growth factor 23 (FGF23) and the pyroptosis pathway. GC treatment upregulates FGF23 expression or activity, thereby activating the NLRP3 inflammasome; NLRP3 assembly leads to caspase‐1 activation, wherein caspase‐1 cleaves GSDMD, generating an N‐terminal fragment that forms pores in the cell membrane, thereby triggering pyroptosis. Inhibiting FGF23 attenuates NLRP3 inflammasome activation and pyroptosis, consequently alleviating SONFH [ ]. This form of cell death has garnered significant attention in recent research due to its implications in various diseases. Despite this interest, the precise mechanisms underlying pyroptosis remain incompletely understood, necessitating further in‐depth investigation through extensive studies.

3.2.

## Pyroptosis and SONFH
Hyperactivation of pyroptosis in response to pathological factors such as hyperglycemia, oxidative stress, and inflammatory injury has been implicated in bone metabolism‐associated cell death and the pathogenesis of SONFH. The nucleotide‐binding oligomerization domain‐like receptor protein 3 (NLRP3) and associated cytokines, crucial immune signaling complexes that trigger pyroptosis, are integral to the inflammatory response process and play a pivotal role in regulating bone metabolism and SONFH homeostasis [ ]. Chai et al. [ ] observed variations in key cellular pyroptosis molecules, including NLRP3, caspase‐1/‐4/‐5, and IL‐1β, in peripheral blood samples from SONFH and non‐SONFH individuals. Similarly, Fang et al. [ ] reported upregulation of NLRP3, GSDMD, and caspase‐1 protein expression in SONFH patients. The pyroptosis pathway in SONFH involves intricate regulatory mechanisms. Recent investigations have demonstrated that NLRP3 inflammasomes mediate pyroptosis in bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs), leading to disrupted osteoblast and osteoclast differentiation balance, a critical factor in SONFH progression [ , ]. Furthermore, genetic knockdown of the caspase‐1 gene in mice significantly inhibited osteoclast bone resorption function and reduced IL‐10/‐12 and tumor necrosis factor‐alpha (TNF‐α) expression levels, aligning with the blockade of the upstream pyroptosis pathway. These findings underscore the intricate interplay of pyroptosis in SONFH pathogenesis and its impact on bone cell dynamics [ ].
In essence, pyroptosis is triggered in SONFH, and impeding cellular juxtaposition can efficiently regulate the disease advancement of SONFH, specifically by targeting the suppression of NLRP3 and caspase‐1/‐4 to facilitate SONFH recovery. Nonetheless, additional research is warranted to elucidate the precise mechanism of pyroptosis in SONFH.

## Autophagy
4.1.

## Molecular Mechanisms of Autophagy
Autophagy, a cellular self‐degradation process, is crucial for maintaining cellular energy balance and responding to nutritional stress during critical developmental stages. In addition, autophagy is involved in eliminating misfolded proteins, clearing damaged organelles such as mitochondria, endoplasmic reticulum, and peroxisomes, and eradicating intracellular pathogens [ ]. The role of autophagy is dual in various diseases. For instance, in early tumorigenesis, autophagy suppresses tumor growth by modulating cellular quality control mechanisms and oxidative stress pathways, thereby preserving genomic stability and reducing tissue damage and inflammation [ ]. Conversely, in advanced tumor stages, autophagy supports cancer cell survival by providing nutrients and facilitating immune evasion [ ]. In SONFH, GC activates autophagy through multiple molecular pathways. On the one hand, it activates protective autophagy by inhibiting SGK1 [ ], whilst also inducing autophagy via the AMPK/mTOR pathway [ ] and the PI3K/Akt/mTOR pathway [ ] to alleviate osteocyte apoptosis. Furthermore, GC influences bone metabolism via BNIP3L/NIX [ ] and PINK1/Parkin‐mediated mitochondrial autophagy [ ], whilst SIRT3 regulation links mitochondrial autophagy to ferroptosis [ ]. Recent research efforts have focused on elucidating the intricate mechanisms of autophagy, revealing its indispensable contribution to the pathogenesis of numerous diseases.

4.2.

## Autophagy and SONFH
Autophagy is a crucial component in the pathogenesis of SONFH, with key regulatory elements including BNIP3, HIF‐1α, AMPK, and Beclin‐1. BNIP3, a marker of mitochondrial autophagy, is activated in hypoxic conditions through the HIF‐1α pathway, facilitating the removal of damaged mitochondria and thereby reducing osteoblast apoptosis induced by GC [ ]. AMPK activation enhances autophagy activity, thereby inhibiting GC‐induced osteoblast apoptosis, while drugs such as aucubin significantly reduce cell apoptosis by activating AMPK [ ]. Beclin‐1 and LC3‐II are core proteins involved in autophagosome formation. In osteoblasts treated with methylprednisolone, the expression levels of LC3‐II and Beclin‐1 gradually increased over time. Therefore, methylprednisolone increases the level of autophagy in osteoblasts by promoting the expression of Beclin‐1 and LC3‐II [ ]. This will affect the interaction between osteoblasts and osteoclasts, thereby triggering SONFH [ ]. This suggests that excessive autophagy may also trigger SONFH. Moreover, the HIF‐1α/BNIP3 axis is pivotal in balancing autophagy and apoptosis in hypoxic conditions, with its downregulation exacerbating osteoblast death [ ]. These pathway dysregulations contribute to an imbalance in bone metabolism, accelerating femoral head necrosis. Therapeutic advancements targeting these pathways have emerged. For instance, human umbilical cord mesenchymal stem cells (hUCMSCs) have shown substantial improvement in GC‐induced microstructural femoral head damage by restoring autophagy levels, reducing osteoblast apoptosis through Beclin‐1 and LC3‐II upregulation [ ]. Astragalus polysaccharide (APS) promotes autophagy, inhibits apoptosis, and mitigates necrotic progression via the miR‐206/HIF‐1α/BNIP3 axis [ ]. In addition, HIF‐1α overexpression or aucubin treatment safeguards osteoblasts by activating AMPK, enhancing mitochondrial autophagy, and reducing reactive oxygen species (ROS) accumulation [ ]. Recent investigations have highlighted that mitochondrial autophagy modulated by the SIRT3/BNIP3 pathway prevents ferroptosis, with isovitexin offering significant protection against necrosis by targeting SIRT3 [ ]. These targeted interventions present promising avenues for SONFH treatment.
In essence, dysregulated expression of autophagy‐related factors and signaling pathways, such as BNIP3, HIF‐1α, and AMPK, constitutes the central pathogenic mechanism of SONFH. Reestablishing autophagic homeostasis through interventions like stem cell therapy, natural compounds, or gene modulation holds promise for mitigating the condition. The prospect of multi‐target combination therapy and translational implementation warrants additional investigation in forthcoming studies.

## Ferroptosis
5.1.

## Molecular Mechanisms of Ferroptosis
Ferroptosis is a form of regulated cell death that is dependent on iron and characterized by dysregulation in iron homeostasis, lipid peroxidation, and impairment of antioxidant defenses. The primary driver of ferroptosis is the iron‐mediated Fenton reaction, leading to the accumulation of ROS that induce peroxidation of polyunsaturated fatty acid (PUFA) phospholipids, ultimately disrupting cell membrane integrity [ ]. Key regulatory pathways involve the inactivation of glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4) and inhibition of the System Xc‐glutathione axis, both of which diminish the capacity to detoxify lipid peroxidation, thereby exacerbating ferroptosis [ ]. Mitochondria also play a crucial role in promoting ferroptosis by enhancing lipid peroxidation and ROS production [ ]. Recent studies have identified ferroptosis suppressor protein 1 (FSP1) as a regulator that inhibits lipid peroxidation through the coenzyme Q10–NAD(P)H pathway independently of GPX4, presenting a novel target for regulating ferroptosis [ ]. Enzymes involved in lipid metabolism, such as ACSL4 and LOXs, further contribute to the amplification of ferroptosis signaling by modifying phospholipids to facilitate peroxidation [ ]. Furthermore, GC can induce mitochondrial autophagy by downregulating SIRT3 [ ], activate the JAK2/STAT3 pathway [ ], inhibit the nuclear factor E2‐related factor 2 (NRF2)/GPX4 antioxidant axis [ ], and regulate signals involving PDK4, BTRC/NRF2, and TLR4/SLC7A11/GPX4 to activate the ferroptosis pathway. Collectively, these intricate molecular mechanisms form a complex regulatory network of ferroptosis that holds pathological significance in conditions such as cancer, neurodegenerative diseases, and infections.

5.2.

## Ferroptosis and SONFH
Ferroptosis primarily involves lipid peroxidation, disruptions in iron metabolism, and anomalies in antioxidant pathways. Key regulators include Nrf2, GPX4, and growth differentiation factor 15 (GDF15). GC impedes the Nrf2 signaling pathway and diminishes GPX4 function by inducing oxidative stress, leading to lipid peroxidation buildup and triggering osteoblast ferroptosis [ ]. Moreover, GDF15, a stress‐responsive factor, is downregulated by GC, worsening mitochondrial dysfunction and iron accumulation [ ]. Another critical pathway is the miR‐150‐3p/BTRC/Nrf2 axis, where BTRC hinders Nrf2's antioxidant role by degrading it through ubiquitination. Conversely, BMSCs exosome miR‐150‐3p can counteract BTRC, reinstating Nrf2 function and mitigating ferroptosis [ ]. Fan et al. [ ] identified that abnormalities in the SIRT3/BNIP3 pathway disrupt mitochondrial autophagy, iron metabolism, and promote osteoblast death. Understanding these signaling pathways offers novel insights for treating SONFH. Exogenous melatonin mitigates femoral head microstructural damage by activating GDF15 signaling, suppressing ferroptosis, and enhancing BMSCs function [ ]. The BMSCs‐derived exosomal miR‐150‐3p bolsters Nrf2‐mediated antioxidant defense, reducing lipid peroxidation and osteoblastic cell death by targeting BTRC [ ]. Isovitexin, a natural compound, inhibits BNIP3‐mediated pathological mitochondrial autophagy, restores mitochondrial balance, and prevents ferroptosis by upregulating SIRT3 [ ]. In addition, utilizing machine learning to screen genes like SOCS1 and MYCN could identify potential biomarkers whose modulation of the immune microenvironment, such as macrophage M1 polarization, may impact ferroptosis progression [ ].
In essence, ferroptosis propels the progression of SONFH via pathways involving Nrf2, GDF15, and SIRT3. Targeted therapeutic approaches, such as exosomal miRNAs and melatonin, exhibit promising therapeutic efficacy. Future research should focus on validating biomarkers using multi‐omics methodologies and investigating the interplay between ferroptosis and the immune microenvironment to enhance clinical management strategies.

## Cuprotosis
6.1.

## Molecular Mechanisms of Cuprotosis
Cuprotosis is a mitochondria‐dependent cell death pathway triggered by the excessive accumulation of Cu2+. The central mechanism involves copper's abnormal interaction with lipoylated proteins in the tricarboxylic acid cycle (TCA). Cu2+ directly binds to lipoylation‐modified TCA cycle enzymes, such as the pyruvate dehydrogenase complex, leading to protein dysfunction. This disruption impairs electron transfer in the mitochondrial respiratory chain, causing a loss of mitochondrial membrane potential and a collapse of energy metabolism [ ]. Furthermore, copper's toxic effects are associated with inducing the aberrant aggregation of iron–sulfur cluster proteins (Fe–S), resulting in mitochondrial proteotoxic stress and ROS bursts [ ]. This process is regulated by the copper chaperone protein FDX1, which facilitates copper targeting to TCA cycle enzymes by modulating lipid acylation modifications. Inhibitors of FDX1 effectively prevent cuprotosis. Cuprotosis is distinguished from other forms of PCD, such as apoptosis and necrotic apoptosis, by its unique molecular features, copper‐dependent nature, and mitochondrial metabolic imbalance characteristics. These distinct features make cuprotosis a promising target for treating diseases associated with copper metabolism disorders.

6.2.

## Cuprotosis and SONFH
Genes involved in copper metabolism (e.g., PNP and SLC2A1) and cuprotosis (DLD, PDHB, and MTF1) are pivotal in the pathogenesis of SONFH. GC disrupts intracellular copper balance by downregulating PNP and SLC2A1 expression, activating the cuprotosis signaling pathway. The overexpression of DLD and PDHB impairs mitochondrial TCA function, leading to oxidative stress and lipid peroxidation. Meanwhile, heightened MTF1 activity exacerbates copper‐induced cell death. These molecular cascades collectively impair osteoblast function, disrupt bone metabolism, and culminate in the structural collapse of the femoral head [ ].
Targeting copper metabolic pathways offers novel avenues for the treatment of SONFH. Enhancing cellular energy metabolism through pharmacological intervention of SLC2A1 or suppressing DLD/PDHB activity to mitigate mitochondrial dysfunction represents promising strategies [ ]. The association between cuproptosis and SONFH remains inadequately explored, and a comprehensive investigation into the interplay between cuproptosis and SONFH may yield innovative therapeutic approaches for SONFH treatment, holding significant research promise.

## Necroptosis
7.1.

## Molecular Mechanisms of Necroptosis
Necroptosis is a regulated, proinflammatory form of PCD that occurs independently of caspase activity within the cytosol. It is orchestrated by receptor‐interacting protein kinase 1 (RIPK1), receptor‐interacting protein kinase 3 (RIPK3), and mixed lineage kinase domain‐like (MLKL). In situations where the apoptotic pathway is suppressed, such as when caspase‐8 is inactive, death receptors like TNFR1 or pattern‐recognition receptors such as ZBP1 stimulate RIPK1. This leads to the formation of a necrosome with RIPK3 through the RIP homotypic interaction motif (RHIM), which subsequently phosphorylates and activates MLKL [ ]. Following MLKL oligomerization, it relocates to the cell membrane, disrupting its integrity, causing the release of cellular contents, and inciting inflammation [ ]. Moreover, ZBP1 plays a role in recognizing Z‐type RNAs during viral infections, enhancing the immune response by activating the RIPK3–MLKL axis in conjunction with NLRP3 inflammatory vesicles, thereby inducing PANoptosis—a composite cell death mechanism involving pyroptosis, apoptosis, and necroptosis [ ]. This process relies on the kinase function of RIPK1, operates independently of caspases, and is intricately regulated alongside apoptosis (caspase‐dependent) and pyroptosis (gasdermin‐dependent) at the molecular level [ ]. In SONFH, GC induces substantial mitochondrial ROS production and suppresses AMPK activity, thereby activating the core necrotic apoptosis signaling pathway RIPK1/RIPK3/MLKL, ultimately leading to osteocyte death. This process may also involve synergistic effects of cellular energy metabolism disorders, such as ATP depletion [ , , , ].

7.2.

## Necroptosis and SONFH
In SONFH, GC induces the activation of the RIPK1/RIPK3 complex, facilitating the phosphorylation of MLKL through the upregulation of TNF‐α levels. This cascade ultimately results in the disruption of the membranes of bone microvascular endothelial cells (BMECs) and the subsequent release of proinflammatory mediators. The aberrant increase in TNF‐α levels further intensifies RIPK1‐mediated necroptosis, culminating in compromised microcirculation within the femoral head and osteoclast demise. Moreover, the persistent activation of the RIPK3–MLKL axis collaborates with oxidative stress to perturb the equilibrium of bone tissue and expedite the progression of necrosis [ ].
The TNF‐α‐specific DNA aptamer, AptTNF‐α, functions by inhibiting RIPK1 activation through direct binding to TNF‐α. This interaction prevents TNF‐α from engaging with its receptor, ultimately leading to a decrease in MLKL phosphorylation and necroptosis in BMECs [ ]. Although necroptosis has gained significant attention in recent years, its association with SONFH remains poorly understood, presenting a promising avenue for further investigation.

8.

## The Emerging PCD Pathway in SONFH
The direct relationship between emerging PCD pathways (such as oxeiptosis, NETosis, alkaliptosis, and disulfidptosis) and SONFH currently lacks conclusive evidence and is primarily speculative, based on common mechanisms such as oxidative stress, immune imbalance, and metabolic disorders. For example, oxeiptosis is ROS‐driven and highly consistent with GC‐induced oxidative damage; NETosis may exacerbate bone resorption by releasing inflammatory factors and inhibiting osteoblast differentiation; while alkaliptosis and disulfidptosis involve acid–base homeostasis and disulfide bond stress, respectively, their roles in bone homeostasis regulation have not been directly validated, but there are already clues related to bone diseases. Overall, these emerging pathways are still speculative, but due to their potential roles in inflammation, energy metabolism, and microenvironment regulation, they hold significant research value. Future studies should establish causal relationships through in vivo experiments and multi‐omics analyses, thereby providing new theoretical and practical directions for multi‐targeted treatment of SONFH.
8.1.

## Oxeiptosis and SONFH
Oxeiptosis is a distinct form of ROS‐mediated cell death that exhibits characteristics of PCD similar to apoptosis. Key regulators of this process include KEAP1, PGAM5, and AIFM1, which govern its unique molecular and morphological attributes [ ]. Oxeiptosis is associated with oxidative stress mechanisms, which play a pivotal role in the development of SONFH [ ]. Prolonged GC exposure induces excessive ROS production, resulting in osteoblast apoptosis and disruption of the bone marrow microenvironment. GC treatment has been shown to enhance ROS generation by upregulating NADPH oxidase (NOX) family proteins, leading to oxidative DNA damage and mitochondrial dysfunction, ultimately culminating in programmed osteoblast death [ , ]. Furthermore, oxidative stress exacerbates bone damage by inhibiting the Nrf2 antioxidant pathway and impeding cellular scavenging of free radicals [ ]. Studies have demonstrated that GC further intensifies oxidative stress by downregulating α2‐macroglobulin (α2M), activating SIRT2‐mediated BMP2 deacetylation, and perturbing the balance between osteogenesis and lipogenesis [ ]. Interventions targeting oxidative stress, such as protein disulfide isomerase (PDI) inhibition or administration of vitamins C and E, have been shown to enhance osteoblast survival and ameliorate pathological changes in femoral head necrosis by modulating ROS levels [ , ]. In addition, Han et al. [ ] identified a significant association between oxeiptosis and musculoskeletal disorders (MSD). Although there is currently no direct evidence indicating a clear correlation between oxeiptosis and SONFH, the author speculates based on the role of oxidative stress in SONFH and the fact that oxeiptosis is driven by ROS to induce PCD. However, the direct mechanistic link between oxeiptosis and SONFH still requires further experimental validation. Nevertheless, this provides a potential avenue for the treatment of SONFH.

8.2.

## NETosis and SONFH
Neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs) represent a novel mechanism of pathogen eradication by neutrophils, complementing traditional processes such as phagocytosis and degranulation [ ]. The formation of NETs, termed NETosis, is typically triggered by various stimuli, including bacterial, fungal and viral infections, and proinflammatory cytokines. NETosis plays a pivotal role in bone metabolism by influencing the inflammatory milieu and oxidative stress. NETs are characterized by histones, citrullinated proteins, and proinflammatory mediators like IL‐6 and TNF‐α, which can potentially promote bone resorption by activating osteoclast differentiation pathways such as the RANKL/osteoprotegerin (OPG) axis [ ]. Research indicates that components like myeloperoxidase (MPO) and ROS within NETs can suppress the expression of genes crucial for osteoblast differentiation (e.g., RUNX2, OCN), thereby impeding bone formation [ ]. Moreover, the release of S100A11 protein during NETosis exacerbates the imbalance in bone metabolism by stimulating neutrophils to secrete IL‐6 and TNF‐α, indirectly fostering osteoclast activity while inhibiting OPG production [ ]. In conditions like rheumatoid arthritis, NETs accelerate the degradation of articular cartilage and bone matrix by activating the inflammatory response of synovial fibroblasts and upregulating matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs) [ ]. Notably, NETosis‐induced oxidative stress can hinder the osteogenic differentiation of mesenchymal stem cells by suppressing the Wnt/β‐catenin pathway, further disrupting bone homeostasis [ ]. In summary, NETosis disrupts bone homeostasis and thereby affects osteogenesis. Although there is currently no evidence to suggest that NETosis is involved in the development of SONFH, existing research indicates that NETosis is involved in the onset and progression of various skeletal diseases. Therefore, it is speculated that NETosis may have an indirect relationship with the pathogenesis of SONFH. Consequently, NETosis could potentially become a new therapeutic target for SONFH; however, the direct mechanistic link between NETosis and SONFH requires further experimental validation.

8.3.

## Alkaliptosis and SONFH
Alkaliptosis modulates bone homeostasis by regulating cellular metabolism and mineralization processes. Studies have demonstrated that an alkaline pH range of 7.8–8.4 significantly boosts the proliferative activity of osteoblast MC3T3‐E1, enhances alkaline phosphatase activity, and facilitates mineralized nodule formation. This effect is mediated through the upregulation of ATF4 and DMP1 genes, leading to accelerated osteoblast differentiation into bone cells [ ]. Chronic metabolic alkalosis has been associated with improved bone density by suppressing osteoclastogenesis and reducing bone resorption rates via the inhibition of the RANKL signaling pathway [ ]. Clinical observations have indicated that hypercalcemia induced by lactoalkali syndrome can disrupt bone turnover equilibrium, resulting in inhibited bone formation and persistent osteolysis. However, timely correction of the alkali load can restore bone metabolic balance [ ]. Furthermore, the impact of an alkaline environment on bone metabolism is dual‐fold: moderate alkalinization stimulates bone formation by enhancing Wnt/β‐catenin signaling, while excessive alkalinization may disrupt calcium and phosphorus metabolism by inhibiting carbonic anhydrase activity, thereby increasing the risk of ectopic calcification [ ]. Therefore, these mechanisms may indirectly influence the pathogenesis of SONFH by regulating cellular metabolism and mineralization processes through alkaliptosis, but the direct mechanism linking them to SONFH still requires further experimental verification. Thus, maintaining acid–base balance holds potential clinical application value for bone regeneration therapy in SONFH.

8.4.

## Disulfidptosis and SONFH
Disulfidptosis, a novel form of cell demise triggered by intracellular disulfide bond stress, has emerged as a pivotal regulator of bone metabolism and associated pathological processes. Research indicates that disulfide‐induced cell death impacts osteoclast differentiation and bone remodeling through mechanisms involving metabolic reprogramming and cystine accumulation. For instance, Zhong et al. [ ] illustrated that inhibitors of TXNRD1 prompted disulfide‐induced cell death in osteoclast precursor cells by upregulating NFATc1, leading to the inhibition of osteoclastogenesis and mitigation of bone loss in an osteoporosis model. Similarly, Zhang et al. [ ] identified diagnostic clusters (e.g., FLNA, SLC7A11) linked to disulfide‐induced cell death in osteoporosis, associating their expression with immune dysregulation and osteoclast activity. In the context of intervertebral disc degeneration, Wu et al. [ ] observed that glucose deprivation triggered disulfide‐induced cell death in nucleus pulposus cells through SLC7A11‐mediated disulfide bond stress, underscoring its significance in the nutrient‐deficient bone microenvironment. In addition, Sun et al. [ ] found that dexamethasone can inhibit the expression of SLC7A11 through the P53 pathway, thereby reducing GPX4 activity and inducing ferroptosis. On the other hand, Yang et al. [ ] found in their study that knockdown of the long noncoding RNA XR_877193.1 can inhibit ferroptosis by activating the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway and promote osteogenic differentiation, a process that may be related to the upregulation of SLC7A11. Furthermore, Pan et al. [ ] suggested RPN1, a protein implicated in disulfide enzyme deficiency, as a potential target for osteoporosis treatment, demonstrating that kaempferol mitigates bone loss by modulating RPN1 expression. These findings collectively emphasize that disulfide enzyme deficiency is a key pathway in bone metabolism disorders. Therefore, it is speculated that disulfidptosis is indirectly related to the pathogenesis of SONFH, but further experimental verification is needed to confirm its direct mechanism.

## Interactions in PCD Networks
9.1.

## The Core Driving Role of ROS
ROS accumulation in ferroptosis leads to lipid peroxidation, triggering osteoblast death by inhibiting the GPX4 and Nrf2 pathways [ , ]. In oxeiptosis, ROS trigger caspase‐independent death through the KEAP1/PGAM5/AIFM1 axis [ , ]. ROS can also be found in the processes of pyroptosis and necroptosis. ROS activates the NLRP3 inflammasome and synergizes with TNF‐α to promote RIPK1/MLKL‐mediated necroptosis [ , ].

9.2.

## The Pivotal Role of Mitochondrial Dysfunction
Based on the latest research findings, cuproptosis is directly causally linked to mitochondrial dysfunction. The accumulation of Cu2+ disrupts the TCA cycle, leading to mitochondrial energy collapse [ , ]. Cu2+ accumulates specifically within mitochondria, binding to lipoylated mitochondrial enzymes (such as DLAT) to induce abnormal oligomerization and protein toxicity stress, directly disrupting mitochondrial respiratory chain function. This process is accompanied by significant alkalization of the mitochondrial matrix and abnormal accumulation of sulfite , leading to the collapse of the mitochondrial membrane potential and energy metabolism failure, ultimately triggering PCD. Mitochondria are thus regarded as the core target organ of cuproptosis, with disruptions in their microenvironment (such as pH and metabolic imbalance) being hallmark features of this death pattern [ , ]. At the same time, dysregulation of the SIRT3/BNIP3 pathway in autophagy and ferroptosis simultaneously inhibits mitochondrial autophagy and promotes ferroptosis [ ].

9.3.

## PCD Activation Timing and Its Relative Importance
Based on the above analysis, PCD activation may occur in the following sequence during the early stage (initial hormone exposure): Autophagy is activated first, and GC disrupts the interaction between osteoblasts and osteoclasts by promoting the expression of Beclin‐1/LC3‐II, thereby causing osteoblasts to undergo excessive autophagy and inhibiting the expression of AMPK, which reduces the level of autophagy [ , ]. Subsequently, oxidative stress occurs, GC induces ROS accumulation, and triggers ferroptosis and oxygen‐induced cell death [ , , ]. Mid‐stage (inflammation amplification and metabolic imbalance): At this point, pyroptosis begins, the NLRP3/GSDMD pathway is activated, inflammatory factors such as IL‐1β are released, and immune cells are recruited [ , ]. At the same time, cuproptosis begins to participate, and abnormalities in copper metabolism genes (PNP/SLC2A1) lead to the collapse of the mitochondrial TCA cycle [ ]. Late stage (bone collapse): The TNF‐α/RIPK1/MLKL axis in necrotic apoptosis damages BMECs, accelerating ischemia and vascular damage [ ]. Finally, NETosis and disulfidptosis promote bone resorption through immune microenvironment dysregulation (such as macrophage polarization) and cystine metabolism abnormalities [ , ].
Currently, there are no relevant studies that can prove the relative importance of each PCD process in SONFH. The author speculates here based on factors such as the temporal sequence of PCD occurrence, synergistic and antagonistic relationships, and common targets. Ferroptosis and autophagy are core mechanisms that run through the entire process of SONFH occurrence and development; pyroptosis and necroptosis amplify inflammatory damage during the progression of SONFH, while cuproptosis and other emerging pathways currently have limited evidence but significant potential. Future studies employing temporal multi‐omics, live‐cell imaging, and lineage‐tracing techniques are essential to validate the sequential interplay of these PCD processes in SONFH. Establishing such temporal causality would provide a more robust mechanistic foundation for therapeutic intervention design.

10.

## Conclude
The pathogenesis of SONFH is intricately linked to various PCD pathways, encompassing focal death, autophagy, ferroptosis, cuprotosis, necroptosis, and emerging PCD pathways. GC has been observed to induce focal death by activating the NLRP3 inflammasome/GSDMD axis, resulting in the release of inflammatory mediators and perturbation of bone metabolism. Concurrently, GC suppresses autophagy‐related pathways (e.g., AMPK/HIF‐1α/BNIP3), leading to osteoblast dysfunction and mitochondrial impairment. Ferroptosis exacerbates osteoblast demise through dysregulation of Nrf2/GPX4 and lipid peroxidation, while anomalies in copper metabolism genes (PNP/SLC2A1) prompt cuprotosis‐mediated cell death via mitochondrial TCA cycle disruptions. Activation of the RIPK1/MLKL pathway in necroptosis further disrupts BMEC equilibrium, perpetuating a detrimental cycle. Oxeiptosis, a novel ROS‐dependent cell death modality, aligns closely with GC‐induced osteoblast impairment by modulating oxidative stress through the KEAP1/PGAM5 axis, albeit with limited direct evidence in SONFH. NETosis may indirectly exacerbate bone resorption and microenvironmental imbalance by releasing proinflammatory factors (e.g., IL‐6, TNF‐α) and suppressing osteogenic differentiation genes (e.g., RUNX2). Alkaliptosis and disulfidptosis impact bone homeostasis via acid–base metabolic reprogramming and disulfide bond stress, respectively. While their associations with SONFH necessitate experimental validation, they are proposed to be pivotal in bone metabolism. Despite the need for experimental confirmation of their relevance to SONFH, these pathways have been implicated in regulating bone diseases such as osteoporosis. These PCD pathways present potential therapeutic targets for SONFH; however, further elucidation of the interplay between PCD molecular mechanisms and SONFH is imperative to offer novel clinical interventions. It should be emphasized that the existing evidence from the aforementioned studies primarily derives from correlational research. The precise pathogenic role of these pathways in driving SONFH requires further experimental validation. Distinguishing whether these observed molecular events constitute pathogenic drivers or incidental phenomena is crucial for definitively identifying therapeutic targets.

11.

## Future Treatment Directions and Measures for SONFH
All of the aforementioned PCD pathways provide potential intervention targets for the treatment of SONFH. For example, pyroptosis alleviates inflammatory damage by inhibiting NLRP3/caspase‐1; autophagy utilizes hUCMSCs or drugs (APSs, aucubin) to activate the AMPK/BNIP3 axis and repair bone metabolism; ferroptosis relies on melatonin or exosomal miR‐150‐3p to enhance Nrf2/GPX4 antioxidant defense; cuproptosis regulates the SLC2A1 gene or copper chelators to maintain mitochondrial homeostasis; and necroptosis employs TNF‐α aptamers to block the RIPK1/MLKL pathway and protect vascular endothelium. In terms of clinical translation, by leveraging multi‐target synergistic approaches, targeting the core drivers of ROS (simultaneously inhibiting ferroptosis/oxeiptosis) and mitochondrial hubs (coordinated regulation of autophagy/cuproptosis), combined strategies such as “AMPK activators + ferroptosis inhibitors” are developed. Through staged interventions, early‐stage treatment focuses on regulating autophagy/ferroptosis (e.g., stem cell therapy), mid‐stage treatment blocks pyroptosis/cuproptosis, and late‐stage treatment inhibits necroptosis, optimizing the treatment sequence for SONFH. In summary, overcoming the treatment challenges of SONFH requires integrating the interactive mechanisms of the PCD network (such as the inflammation‐metabolism‐oxidative stress triangle), and prioritizing clinically validated targeted strategies (such as autophagy activation and ferroptosis inhibition) while exploring emerging targets like cuproptosis/disulfidptosis. Future research should prioritize validating the efficacy of biomarkers through multi‐omics approaches, defining the interactions between the immune microenvironment and PCD, and establishing in vivo causal relationships for newly emerging PCD pathways. Ultimately, the goal is to transition from “delaying collapse” to “promoting bone regeneration.”

12.

## Discussion
The feasibility of multi‐target combination therapy in SONFH is increasingly gaining attention, with its theoretical basis rooted in the significant temporal, complementary, and mutually amplifying effects between different PCD pathways. Previous studies suggest that inhibiting a single pathway often fails to halt disease progression, as SONFH is not driven by a single mechanism but rather by the interplay of multiple mechanisms, including autophagy imbalance, ferroptosis, pyroptosis, cuproptosis, and necroptosis. In the early stages, GC can directly inhibit AMPK, leading to impaired autophagy and mitochondrial dysfunction, thereby creating a conducive environment for subsequent ferroptosis and oxidative stress. At this stage, inhibiting ferroptosis alone may still exacerbate damage due to impaired autophagy and ROS accumulation; however, restoring autophagy through AMPK activation or stem cell therapy, combined with ferroptosis inhibitors, can weaken lipid peroxidation chain reactions at the metabolic source, significantly enhancing therapeutic efficacy. In the mid‐stage, inflammatory responses are amplified, and NLRP3/GSDMD‐mediated pyroptosis, combined with copper metabolism disorders, disrupts mitochondrial homeostasis, driving further osteocyte apoptosis. At this stage, combined intervention with pyroptosis inhibitors and copper chelators, in conjunction with the early‐stage foundational regimen (AMPK activation + ferroptosis inhibition), can effectively break the vicious cycle of inflammation and metabolism. In the late stage, the RIPK1/MLKL axis drives necroptosis, leading to irreversible damage and collapse of bone microvasculature. At this point, adjunctive vascular protective strategies such as TNF‐α aptamers may help slow disease progression. Overall, multi‐targeted interventions not only compensate for the limitations of single‐target approaches but also enable precise combination at different stages to simultaneously alleviate metabolic imbalance, inhibit inflammatory amplification, and maintain vascular homeostasis at various disease progression milestones, thereby maximizing the clinical objectives of “delaying collapse and promoting bone regeneration.” In particular, the dual‐targeted basic regimen centered on “AMPK activation + ferroptosis inhibition,” combined with the dynamic addition of pyroptosis or cuproptosis inhibition strategies based on inflammatory and metabolic phenotypes, possesses clear mechanistic advantages and high clinical feasibility. This multi‐targeted combined therapy, with mitochondrial homeostasis as the hub and ROS and metabolic regulation as the main axis, may become an important future development direction for SONFH intervention (Table  ).
 
13.

## Limitations
This review has certain limitations when summarizing potential therapeutic strategies for different PCD regulatory pathways in SONFH. First, in most cellular experimental studies, the drug concentrations used often differ from clinically achievable doses, sometimes even far exceeding the safe clinical range. Consequently, their efficacy and feasibility lack authentic dose‐related evidence, potentially leading to overestimation or underestimation of experimental results in clinical applications. Second, while some studies demonstrate positive mechanisms in vitro, systematic validation in animal models remains lacking. Crucially, whether similar protective or reparative effects can be replicated in steroid‐induced femoral head necrosis models has not been fully confirmed, limiting the reliability of translating basic research findings to clinical practice. Furthermore, many conclusions rely heavily on mechanistic speculation rather than large‐scale, publicly available expression data such as transcriptomic, proteomic, or single‐cell sequencing to support the causal relationship between key target alterations and therapeutic outcomes. Consequently, these shortcomings not only diminish the persuasiveness of findings but also underscore the need for subsequent studies to strengthen dose–response rationality, animal model validation, and multidimensional data support. Such improvements are essential to advance these novel therapeutic strategies from the laboratory to clinical practice.

14.

## Discuss Challenges
Multiple challenges remain in exploring potential therapeutic strategies for the modulation of PCD in SONFH. First, target specificity is one of the central challenges. Many pathways, such as NLRP3 inflammatory vesicles, AMPK/BNIP3, and RIPK1/MLKL, are not only involved in the pathological process of osteonecrosis but also play important roles in immune homeostasis, metabolic regulation, and cellular stress response. Therefore, in the absence of highly selective interventions, the therapeutic process is highly susceptible to bringing off‐target effects and inducing immunosuppression or vascular‐related complications. Secondly, in terms of delivery systems, especially for nucleic acid‐based therapeutic molecules such as miRNAs or siRNAs, there is still a lack of efficient, safe, and controllable delivery platforms, and how to achieve specific localization to bone tissues, improve stability, and reduce systemic side effects are key bottlenecks for clinical translation. Furthermore, many of the existing results are mainly in cellular experiments or limited animal models, and it is still difficult to cross the “preclinical studies—human trials” gap, especially in terms of the feasibility of drug dosage and the lack of systematic verification of long‐term efficacy. In addition, it is often difficult to address the complex metabolic, inflammatory, and vascular damage involved in femoral head necrosis with a single pathway, which highlights the need for multi‐targeted interventions or “combinatorial strategies,” such as the combined regulation of ferroptosis, inflammatory apoptosis, and autophagy, to achieve synergistic effects. In conclusion, future research needs to make breakthroughs in target precision, optimization of nucleic acid delivery systems, cross‐model validation, and multi‐target integrated intervention design in order to promote the steady translation of these emerging therapeutic strategies into clinical practice.
