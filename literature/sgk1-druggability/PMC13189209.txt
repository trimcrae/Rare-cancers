## Abstract

## Purpose
This study aimed to investigate the role of serum- and glucocorticoid-inducible kinase 1 (SGK1) in dry eye disease (DED) pathogenesis and its underlying molecular mechanisms.

## Methods
We established in vitro hyperosmotic models using human corneal epithelial cells (HCECs) and in vivo DED models induced by benzalkonium chloride in C57BL/6 mice. SGK1 was inhibited pharmacologically with GSK650394, and genetic knockdown of Forkhead box O3a (FoxO3a) was performed using small interfering RNA. Comprehensive assessments included western blot for apoptosis and autophagy markers, flow cytometry for apoptosis and reactive oxygen species (ROS) detection, mRFP-GFP-LC3 lentiviral transfection for autophagic flux monitoring, transmission electron microscopy for autophagic ultrastructure, co-immunoprecipitation for protein interactions, immunofluorescence staining for cellular localization, and histological examinations of ocular tissues.

## Results
SGK1 expression was significantly upregulated in both hyperosmotic-treated HCECs and the corneal epithelium of DED mice. SGK1 inhibition alleviated ocular surface damage, restored tear secretion, and reduced apoptosis and oxidative stress in vivo and in vitro. Mechanistically, SGK1 inhibition enhances autophagic flux by promoting FoxO3a nuclear translocation and activation, whereas FoxO3a knockdown abolishes the protective effects of SGK1 inhibition. Autophagy induction with rapamycin replicated the protective effects against DED-related damage.

## Conclusions
Inhibition of SGK1 alleviates apoptosis and oxidative stress in DED by activating FoxO3a-mediated autophagy. Our findings suggest that the SGK1/FoxO3a/autophagy axis may play a critical role in the pathogenesis of DED, and SGK1 may represent a potential therapeutic target for this condition.

Keywords: SGK1, dry eye disease, autophagy, FoxO3a, oxidative stress

Dry eye disease (DED) is a common multifactorial ocular surface disorder with a global prevalence of 5% to 50%. Patients with DED frequently experience symptoms such as ocular burning, pain, dryness, and discomfort. Prominent conjunctival hyperemia and punctate erosions on the corneal epithelium constitute typical clinical signs of DED. DED significantly impairs patients’ quality of life and imposes substantial socioeconomic burdens. However, it is currently regarded as an incurable condition requiring lifelong management to restore and maintain ocular surface homeostasis, thereby reducing clinical symptoms and ocular surface damage. 
The pathological hallmarks of DED include loss of tear film stability, accompanied by elevated tear osmolarity and ocular surface inflammation. Tear hyperosmolarity, resulting from insufficient tear production or excessive evaporation, serves as a central mechanism triggering inflammatory damage, symptom generation, and compensatory responses in DED. Hyperosmotic stress induces a cascade of inflammatory reactions in corneal and conjunctival epithelial cells, leading to a vicious cycle of tear film breakup and cellular apoptosis. Excessive production of reactive oxygen species (ROS) under hyperosmotic conditions also plays a critical role in DED progression. Overaccumulation of ROS induces severe oxidative stress, causing damage to human corneal epithelial cells (HCECs). 
Serum- and glucocorticoid-inducible kinase 1 (SGK1), a serine/threonine kinase upregulated under various cellular stresses such as hyperosmolarity and oxidative stress, acts as a key mediator in multiple high-salt-promoted pathological processes. , SGK1 regulates the development of pro-inflammatory cells and activates pro-inflammatory transcription factors, highlighting its potential as a therapeutic target in inflammatory and immune diseases. Studies have shown that SGK1 inhibitors can alleviate oxidative stress and apoptosis in various diseases. , SGK1 also participates in the regulation of autophagy signaling pathways by catalyzing the phosphorylation of multiple target proteins. 
Autophagy is a cellular self-degradation process, involving multiple phosphorylation events catalyzed by protein kinases during autophagosome formation and other stages. It plays a significant role in the pathogenesis of various inflammatory diseases. , Inflammation is often associated with intracellular accumulation of damaged organelles, microbes, and other macromolecules; activation of autophagy can counteract inflammation by clearing these components. Our prior research demonstrated that enhancing autophagic flux and suppressing excessive ROS production reduce apoptosis in DED. SGK1 inhibitors can activate autophagy, positioning SGK1 as a novel therapeutic target for autophagy regulation in multiple chronic diseases. However, the role of SGK1 in DED remains unexplored.
This study investigated whether SGK1 participates in the pathogenesis of DED and its potential mechanisms. Our findings reveal that SGK1 expression is upregulated in DED, and SGK1 inhibition activates FoxO3a-mediated autophagy while alleviating oxidative stress and apoptosis. Our results provide the first evidence, to our knowledge, that SGK1 plays a critical role in the development of DED, suggesting that SGK1 inhibition may hold therapeutic potential for this condition.

## Methods

## Animals and DED Mouse Model
Female C57BL/6 mice (6–8 weeks old) from Yangzhou University Animal Center (Yangzhou, China) were housed under standard conditions. All animal experiments were approved by Animal Research Ethics Committee of Nanjing Drum Tower Hospital (approval no. 2025AE01001) and conducted in compliance with guidelines set by the Vision and Ophthalmology Research Society, under the supervision of the Laboratory Animal Management Committee of Jiangsu Province.
DED was induced by instillations of 5 µL of 0.15% benzalkonium chloride (BAC; Macklin Biochemical, Shanghai, China) into both eyes twice daily for 7 days. Mice were then randomly assigned to the DED group and various treatment groups, with BAC administration maintained throughout the subsequent 7 days. The GSK650394 group received 5 µL of 5-mM GSK650394 eye drops (TargetMol Chemicals, Boston, MA, USA) twice daily, the rapamycin (RAPA; Selleck Chemicals, Shanghai, China) group received 5 µL of 0.25% RAPA eye drops twice daily, the dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO; TargetMol Chemicals) group received 5 µL of PBS containing 0.1% DMSO twice daily serving as a solvent control, and the 3-methyladenine (3-MA; Selleck Chemicals) group received 5 µL of 0.5% 3-MA eye drops twice daily in addition to GSK650394 treatment. After 7 days of treatment, mice were euthanized for tissue collection.

## Cell Culture and Cell Transfection
The immortalized human corneal epithelial cell line HCE-2 (CRL-11135; American Type Culture Collection, Manassas, VA, USA) was cultured in Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium/Nutrient Mixture F-12 (DMEM/F-12) medium with 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% streptomycin–penicillin (HyCyte, Suzhou, China) at 37°C with 5% CO2. Cells were divided into experimental groups and treated for 24 hours as follows: control (no treatment); sodium chloride (NaCl) group (hypertonic medium with 90-mM NaCl, serving as the hyperosmotic disease model control); GSK650394 group (hypertonic medium with 2.5-, 5-, or 10-µM GSK650394); RAPA group (hypertonic medium with 200-nM RAPA); DMSO group (hypertonic medium with 0.1% DMSO, serving as the solvent control for both the GSK650394 and RAPA treatments); and 3-MA group (cells pretreated with 5-mM 3-MA for 2 hours, then incubated with hyperosmotic medium containing 5 µM GSK650394).
The small interfering RNA (siRNA) targeting Forkhead box O3a (FoxO3a) was synthesized by Hycyte Biological Co., Ltd. (Suzhou, China) with the following sequences: sense, 5′-CCAGGGAAGUUUGGUCAAUTT-3′; antisense, 5′-AUUGACCAAACUUCCCUGGTT-3′. HCECs were transfected with FoxO3a siRNA or negative control siRNA using Invitrogen Lipofectamine 3000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) reagent, following the manufacturer's protocol.

## Corneal Fluorescein Staining and Tear Secretion Test
Corneal fluorescein staining was performed on days 0, 7, and 14 using 2 µL of 0.25% sodium fluorescein (Jingming, Tianjin, China). After gentle eyelid closure to distribute the dye, the ocular surface was rinsed with 0.9% saline and examined under cobalt blue light. Corneal damage was quantified using the corneal fluorescein score, where the cornea was divided into four quadrants and scored: 0 (no staining), 1 (sparse punctate staining), 2 (dense punctate staining), and 3 (patchy or confluent staining). Total scores represented the sum of quadrant scores. Tear secretion was measured on days 0, 7, and 14 using phenol red–impregnated cotton threads (Jingming) placed in the inferior conjunctival sac for 1 minute. The wetted length was measured consistently by the same investigator.

## High-Throughput RNA Sequencing
TRIzol reagent (Vazyme, Nanjing, China) was used to isolate total RNA from HCECs, untreated or subjected to hyperosmotic treatment for 24 hours. RNA concentration and purity were determined using a microspectrophotometer (Nano-500 Micro Spectrophotometer; Allsheng Instruments, Hangzhou, China). Library preparation and RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) were performed by LC Bio Technology Co., Ltd. (Hangzhou, China). Differential expression analysis was conducted with DESeq2 to identify differentially expressed genes (DEGs) based on |log2 fold change| > 1 and false discovery rate <0.05.

## Quantitative Real-Time PCR
Total RNA was extracted from HCECs using TRIzol reagent, followed by cDNA synthesis from 1 µg RNA with HiScript II Q Select RT SuperMix (Vazyme). Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) was performed on the Applied Biosystems QuantStudio 6 Flex Real-Time PCR System (Thermo Fisher Scientific) with a ChamQ Universal SYBR qRT-PCR Kit (Vazyme). Relative gene expression was calculated by the 2−ΔΔCt method with β-actin as endogenous control. Primer sequences are provided in the  .
 

## Flow Cytometry Analysis
Apoptosis in HCECs was analyzed by flow cytometry using an Annexin V-FITC/PI Apoptosis Kit (APExBIO, Boston, MA, USA) according to the manufacturer's protocol. Fluorescence data were acquired on an Accuri C6 flow cytometer (BD Biosciences, San Jose, CA, USA) and analyzed with FlowJo 10.0 software. The apoptotic rate was determined by combining early (annexin V+/PI–) and late (annexin V+/PI+) apoptotic cells populations.

## Western Blot
Total protein was extracted from HCECs using radioimmunoprecipitation assay (RIPA) lysis buffer (Beyotime, Shanghai, China), followed by centrifugation to collect the supernatant. Protein concentration was determined with a BCA assay kit (Vazyme). Proteins were separated by sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE), transferred to polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes (MilliporeSigma, Burlington, MA, USA), and probed with the following primary antibodies: β-actin (66009-1-Ig, 1:50,000; Proteintech, Rosemont, IL, USA), SGK1 (CY5613, 1:1000; Abways, Shanghai, China), Bcl-2 (AFRM81143, 1:1000; AiFang, Shanghai, China), Bax (60267-1-Ig, 1:5000; Proteintech), caspase 3 (66470-2-Ig, 1:1000; Proteintech), LC3A/B (12741S, 1:1000; Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA, USA), p62 (18420-1-AP, 1:5000; Proteintech), FoxO3a (CY5898, 1:1000; Abways), and p-FoxO3a (CY5562, 1:1000; Abways). Horseradish peroxidase–conjugated anti-rabbit or anti-mouse IgG secondary antibodies (1:5000; Wanlei, Shanghai, China) were applied for detection. Protein bands were visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) detection kit (Vazyme) and quantified with ImageJ (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MA, USA).
For protein extraction from mouse corneas, the following detailed procedure was performed: Immediately after enucleation, the cornea was carefully dissected from the eyeball at 4°C using micro-dissecting scissors and fine forceps under a stereomicroscope. For each biological replicate, five corneas were pooled to obtain sufficient protein yield. The pooled corneal tissues were transferred to a prechilled grinding tube containing stainless-steel grinding beads, and 100 µL of RIPA lysis buffer (Beyotime) was added. Homogenization was performed using a 3D Centrifugal Cryogenic Grinder (Jingxin, Hangzhou, China). The subsequent steps for centrifugation, quantification, and immunoblotting were identical to those used for HCEC lysates.

## Immunofluorescence Staining
HCECs were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, and mouse corneal sections underwent deparaffinization and antigen retrieval. After blocking with 5% BSA containing 0.5% Triton X-100, samples were incubated overnight at 4°C with the following primary antibodies: SGK1 (28454-1-AP, 1:250; Proteintech) and FoxO3a (10849-1-AP, 1:250; Proteintech). Following incubation with CoraLite594-conjugated secondary antibody (SA00013-4, 1:500; Proteintech) or CoraLite488-conjugated secondary antibody (SA00013-2, 1:500; Proteintech) and 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI; ab104139; Abcam, Cambridge, UK) counterstaining, images were captured using the APEXVIEW APX100 fluorescence microscope (Evident Scientific, Tokyo, Japan).

## Cell Viability Assay
Cell viability was measured by Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8; Vazyme) in HCECs seeded at 4 × 103 cells/well in 96-well plates. After treatments, 10-µL CCK-8 reagent was added to each well, followed by 1-hour incubation at 37°C. Absorbance at 450 nm was measured using the Spark microplate reader (Tecan, Männedorf, Switzerland). For time–course experiments, cell viability was additionally assessed at 12 and 48 hours post-treatment.

## Hematoxylin and Eosin Staining and Periodic Acid–Schiff Staining
Mouse eyeball tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 24 hours, dehydrated, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned at 4-µm thickness. For histological evaluation, sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) using a commercial kit (Servicebio, Wuhan, China) or subjected to periodic acid–Schiff (PAS) staining following standard protocols. Representative images were captured using an optical microscope (ZEISS, Oberkochen, Germany).

## TUNEL Staining
Mouse eyeball tissues were embedded in optimal cutting temperature (O.C.T.) glue and stored at –80°C overnight. Sagittal frozen sections (10-µm thickness) were prepared for TUNEL assay following the manufacturer's protocol (Roche, Basel, Switzerland). After TUNEL staining, sections were counterstained with DAPI and imaged using a Thunder fluorescence microscope (Leica, Wetzlar, Germany).

## Intracellular ROS Activity
Intracellular ROS levels were detected using a commercial assay kit (Beyotime). Treated HCECs were incubated with 2′,7′-dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate (DCFH-DA; 1:1000) at 37°C for 30 minutes. Fluorescence was visualized with the Leica Thunder microscope (excitation/emission, 488/525 nm) and quantified by an Accuri C6 flow cytometer (BD Biosciences, USA). Data were analyzed using FlowJo 10.0.

## Detection of Malondialdehyde, Catalase, and Superoxide Dismutase
HCECs were ultrasonicated and centrifuged to collect supernatants. Malondialdehyde (MDA) content, catalase (CAT), and superoxide dismutase (SOD) activities were measured using commercial kits (Beyotime) according to manufacturer's instructions with the Tecan Spark microplate reader.

## Autophagic Flux Assay
HCECs were transduced with mRFP-GFP-LC3 lentivirus (multiplicity of infection = 100; Genechem, Shanghai, China) for 48 to 72 hours, followed by puromycin selection to establish stable expression. After treatment, autophagic flux was assessed using the Evident Scientific APEXVIEW APX100 microscope, where yellow puncta (GFP+/mRFP+) represent autophagosomes and red puncta (GFP–/mRFP+) indicate autolysosomes.

## Transmission Electron Microscopy
HCECs were fixed in glutaraldehyde, washed with PBS, and dehydrated through an ethanol series before epoxy resin embedding. Ultrathin sections were stained with uranyl acetate and lead citrate, and examined by transmission electron microscopy (TEM; Hitachi, Tokyo, Japan) for autophagic vacuoles and organellar alterations.

## Co-Immunoprecipitation
Co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) was performed using HCEC lysates prepared with immunoprecipitation-compatible buffer containing protease/phosphatase inhibitors. Lysates were incubated overnight at 4°C with anti-SGK1 (CY5613; Abways), anti-FoxO3a (10849-1-AP; Proteintech), or control IgG (AC005; ABclonal, Woburn, MA, USA), followed by 2-hour incubation with protein A/G magnetic beads (TargetMol Chemicals). After washing, bound proteins were eluted and analyzed by western blot with anti–heavy-/light-chain secondary antibody (M21008; Abmart, Shanghai, China) and ECL detection.

## Statistical Analysis
All quantitative data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) from at least three independent experiments. Statistical analyses were performed using Prism 9.0 (GraphPad, Boston, MA, USA). Comparisons between two groups were analyzed by Student's t-test, and one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey's post hoc test was applied for multiple-group comparisons. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

## Results

## SGK1 Expression Is Upregulated in Hyperosmotic-Treated HCECs and Corneal Epithelium of DED Mice
We first established in vivo and in vitro DED models. BAC-induced mice showed time-dependent corneal damage by fluorescein staining ( A,  B), epithelial thinning via H&E staining ( C), and progressive tear secretion reduction ( D). HCECs were cultured in medium supplemented with NaCl to mimic the hyperosmotic microenvironment of DED. Compared with the control group, hyperosmotic-treated HCECs exhibited a significantly increased apoptosis rate ( E,  F).
 To identify key regulators involved in the pathogenesis of DED, we performed RNA-seq on three untreated normal HCEC samples and three hyperosmotic-treated HCEC samples. A volcano plot illustrated the overall gene expression profile ( G), and a heatmap displayed the DEGs ( H). To further explore the biological implications of mRNA alterations between normal and hyperosmotic-treated HCECs, Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) and Gene Ontology (GO) analyses were conducted. These analyses suggested significant associations between DED and cellular autophagy, oxidative stress, and apoptosis regulation ( I,  J). SGK1, a gene closely associated with hyperosmotic stress, was selected for its marked upregulation.
The mRNA expression level of SGK1 was significantly elevated in hyperosmotic-treated HCECs ( K). Western blot and immunofluorescence staining further confirmed that SGK1 protein expression was notably increased in hyperosmotic-treated HCECs ( L–N). Consistently, upregulated SGK1 protein expression was also observed in the corneal epithelium of DED mice ( O–Q).

## SGK1 Inhibition Attenuates Apoptosis and Oxidative Stress in Hyperosmotic-Treated HCECs
To investigate the potential role of SGK1 in the pathogenesis of DED, we employed GSK650394, a highly selective SGK1 inhibitor. CCK-8 assays at 24 hours showed that hyperosmotic stress reduced HCEC viability, which was rescued by 5-µM GSK650394 ( A), establishing this concentration for subsequent studies. Additional time–course experiments at 12 and 48 hours confirmed the sustained protective effect of this concentration ( A). The DMSO control group was exposed to hyperosmotic medium containing the same concentration of solvent used for GSK650394 delivery. GSK650394 effectively suppressed SGK1 expression and reversed hyperosmosis-induced apoptotic alterations, as evidenced by reduced levels of pro-apoptotic proteins Bax and cleaved caspase 3 (cCasp3) along with restored Bcl-2 expression ( B,  C). Flow cytometry confirmed that SGK1 inhibition significantly attenuated hyperosmotic-induced apoptosis ( D,  E).
 Furthermore, GSK650394 suppressed intracellular ROS accumulation ( F–H). Hyperosmotic stress increased MDA, a lipid peroxidation product, while inhibiting the activities of the antioxidant enzymes SOD and CAT. Treatment with GSK650394 reduced MDA production and significantly enhanced SOD and CAT activities ( I–K). These results demonstrate that SGK1 inhibition alleviates both apoptosis and oxidative stress in hyperosmotic-treated HCECs.

## SGK1 Inhibition Alleviates Ocular Surface Damage in DED Mice
Topical GSK650394 application in DED mice significantly reduced corneal epithelial SGK1 protein ( A). DED mice showed impaired tear secretion and enlarged corneal fluorescein staining areas, which were respectively restored and reduced after 7-day GSK650394 treatment ( B–D). The treatment also increased corneal epithelial thickness ( E), rescued conjunctival goblet cell density ( F), and reduced epithelial apoptosis ( G). These findings demonstrate that SGK1 inhibition alleviates ocular surface damage, improves tear secretion, and suppresses apoptosis in DED mice.
 

## SGK1 Inhibition Promotes Autophagy in DED
To investigate whether SGK1 participates in the regulation of autophagy in DED, we analyzed autophagy-related proteins in HCECs and mouse corneas. Hyperosmotic stress increased LC3-Ⅱ and decreased p62 in HCECs, effects enhanced by GSK650394 ( A,  B). DED mice corneas showed similar changes after SGK1 inhibition ( C,  D). Consistently, hyperosmotic HCECs expressing monomeric red fluorescent protein (mRFP)-green fluorescent protein (GFP)-microtubule-associated protein light chain 3 (LC3) displayed increased autophagosomes and autolysosomes, further enhanced by GSK650394 ( E,  F). TEM confirmed more autophagic vacuoles in GSK650394-treated cells ( G). Thus, although hyperosmotic stress mildly activates autophagy, SGK1 inhibition further enhances autophagic flux in DED models.
 

## SGK1 Inhibition Promotes Autophagy Through FoxO3a Activation
KEGG analysis identifying FoxO signaling pathway enrichment under hyperosmotic stress led us to focus on FoxO3a, a key autophagy-related transcription factor ( I). Co-IP revealed a direct protein–protein interaction between SGK1 and FoxO3a ( A). In hyperosmotic-treated HCECs, although FoxO3a expression was significantly decreased, phosphorylated FoxO3a (p-FoxO3a) was markedly increased. Because phosphorylation typically promotes cytoplasmic retention and inhibits the transcriptional activity of FoxO3a, these changes suggest suppressed FoxO3a function under hyperosmotic stress. GSK650394 treatment reversed this pattern, enhancing FoxO3a expression while reducing p-FoxO3a levels ( B,  C) and promoted FoxO3a nuclear translocation ( D), indicating its activation. Immunofluorescence confirmed GSK650394 restored FoxO3a in the corneal epithelium of DED mice ( E).
 FoxO3a knockdown validated its necessity for SGK1-mediated autophagy regulation. Compared to the siNC group, the siFoxO3a group exhibited significantly reduced expression of FoxO3a at both mRNA ( F) and protein levels ( G,  H), confirming successful knockdown. Although GSK650394 increased LC3-Ⅱ expression and decreased p62 expression, these pro-autophagic effects were reversed by FoxO3a knockdown ( I,  J). Consistently, FoxO3a knockdown eliminated GSK650394-induced autophagosome/autolysosome formation ( K,  L) and the accumulation of autophagic vacuoles as revealed by TEM ( M). Specifically, TEM images showed that GSK650394 treatment under hyperosmotic stress induced the formation of characteristic autophagic vacuoles, including early autophagosomes (double-membrane structures engulfing cytoplasmic contents) and late autolysosomes (single-membrane structures containing degrading materials). This ultrastructural evidence of enhanced autophagic flux was substantially diminished upon FoxO3a knockdown. These results demonstrate that SGK1 inhibition promotes autophagy and enhances autophagic flux by activating FoxO3a.

## Activation of Autophagy Mitigates Apoptosis and Oxidative Stress in DED
To validate the functional role of autophagy in DED progression, we used RAPA, a well-characterized autophagic activator. The DMSO control group was exposed to hyperosmotic medium containing the same solvent concentration used for RAPA delivery. Western blot analysis confirmed that RAPA significantly increased LC3-Ⅱ levels and decreased p62 levels compared to the DMSO group, verifying successful autophagy induction ( A,  B). Concurrently, RAPA significantly reduced pro-apoptotic and enhanced anti-apoptotic protein expression ( A,  B). The mRFP-GFP-LC3 assay further corroborated RAPA-mediated autophagy activation ( C,  D). Flow cytometric analysis demonstrated that RAPA significantly reduced apoptosis ( E,  F), indicating an anti-apoptotic function of autophagy in DED. It also markedly attenuated intracellular ROS levels ( G–I) and reduced MDA content ( J), while enhancing CAT and SOD activities ( K,  L), demonstrating the antioxidant capacity of autophagy activation. In vivo, RAPA reduced corneal fluorescein staining area ( M,  N), improved tear secretion ( O), and restored corneal epithelial thickness ( P,  Q). These findings confirm that autophagy activation alleviates DED through anti-apoptotic and antioxidant mechanisms.
 To further confirm whether the protective effects of SGK1 inhibition are mediated through autophagy, we performed rescue experiments using the autophagy inhibitor 3-MA. In DED mice treated with GSK650394, co-administration of 3-MA significantly reversed the therapeutic benefits of SGK1 inhibition, as evidenced by decreased tear secretion ( B) and increased corneal fluorescein staining area ( C, D). In vitro, 3-MA treatment significantly suppressed the protective effect of GSK650394 against hyperosmolarity-induced apoptosis in HCECs ( E, F). These results indicate that the therapeutic effects of SGK1 inhibition are dependent on functional autophagy ( ).

## Discussion
This study identified SGK1 as a significantly upregulated kinase in DED models. SGK1 inhibition alleviated ocular surface damage, restored tear secretion, and reduced apoptosis in DED mice while attenuating hyperosmolarity-induced injury in HCECs through FoxO3a-mediated autophagy activation. These findings reveal a previously unrecognized SGK1/FoxO3a/autophagy axis in DED pathogenesis.
DED pathogenesis involves disruption of the ocular surface functional unit, leading to tear film instability and hyperosmotic stress. , Hyperosmolarity induces apoptosis in ocular surface cells, – creating a vicious cycle of barrier disruption and inflammation. Concurrent oxidative stress promotes further damage, , highlighting the therapeutic potential of targeting these pathways.
SGK1, a member of the AGC protein kinase family, phosphorylates serine and threonine residues of target proteins and plays crucial roles in regulating apoptosis, proliferation, and inflammatory immune responses. Its expression is modulated by various stimuli, including hyperosmotic stress, cell volume changes, oxidative stress, and DNA damage. SGK1 has been demonstrated to be essential for promoting inflammatory cell differentiation and upregulating pro-inflammatory cytokines. Elevated SGK1 levels have been identified as a potential biomarker for several inflammatory conditions. , Previous studies have shown that the SGK1 inhibitor GSK650394 exhibits protective effects by reducing ROS and oxidative stress in cardiac ischemia–reperfusion injury and attenuating HCEC apoptosis in viral keratitis. In the present study, we report for the first time, to our knowledge, that SGK1 expression is upregulated in DED. Importantly, SGK1 inhibition alleviated corneal and conjunctival damage and apoptosis in DED mice, restored tear secretion, and reduced hyperosmolarity-induced oxidative stress and apoptosis in HCECs.
Autophagy activation has been recognized as an effective approach to suppress oxidative stress and apoptosis in DED. , Although the phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase (PI3K)/mechanistic target of rapamycin (mTOR) pathway represents the primary regulatory axis for autophagy, SGK1 also plays a pivotal role in this network. Interestingly, SGK1 appears to function as an endogenous autophagy suppressor, as mTOR-mediated SGK1 activation leads to subsequent downregulation of key autophagy-initiation genes. This regulatory relationship is further supported by evidence showing impaired autophagy in SGK1-overexpressing transgenic mice and enhanced autophagic flux in SGK1-deficient models. The consistent observation that SGK1 inhibition upregulates autophagy across different disease contexts has positioned SGK1 as a promising therapeutic target for modulating autophagy in chronic disorders. For example, in prostate cancer, SGK1 inhibition was found to suppress tumor metastasis through autophagy activation, and SGK1 knockdown protected chondrocytes in osteoarthritis via similar mechanisms. Our current study extends these findings to DED, demonstrating that SGK1 inhibition activates autophagy and enhances autophagic flux, thereby providing protection against ocular surface damage in this disease.
FoxO3a, a key transcription factor downstream of SGK1, belongs to the Fox family and plays pivotal roles in regulating autophagy, apoptosis, and oxidative stress responses. Its subcellular localization and transcriptional activity are predominantly controlled by post-translational modifications, with phosphorylation being the most significant regulatory mechanism. , Phosphorylated FoxO3a translocated from the nucleus to the cytoplasm, leading to its functional inactivation and subsequent degradation, thereby suppressing FoxO3a-dependent transcription. As a master transcriptional regulator of autophagy in both physiological and pathological states, FoxO3a activates genes essential for autophagosome formation and lysosomal fusion, , including LC3 and beclin1. Emerging evidence demonstrates that pharmacological activation of FoxO3a promotes cytoprotective autophagy in various disease models. For example, berberine induces FoxO3a nuclear translocation and suppresses its phosphorylation to enhance autophagy and reduce apoptosis in acute kidney injury, whereas FoxO3a-mediated autophagy activation alleviates oxidative stress in hematopoietic stem cells. 
SGK1 directly phosphorylates and inactivates FoxO3a, promoting its nuclear exclusion and inhibiting its transcriptional activity. Recent studies have revealed that SGK1 inhibition enhances FoxO3a activity, thereby suppressing inflammation and preventing fibrosis in inflammatory bowel disease and upregulating FoxO3a-dependent expression of antioxidant enzymes CAT and SOD to reduce ROS levels. Our current findings align with these observations, demonstrating that SGK1 inhibition in DED upregulates FoxO3a expression, reduces its phosphorylation, and promotes its nuclear accumulation. Furthermore, we established that the enhanced autophagic flux following SGK1 inhibition is mechanistically dependent on FoxO3a activation, as FoxO3a knockdown abolished these protective effects. These results collectively indicate that SGK1 inhibition promotes autophagy in DED through FoxO3a-mediated signaling, providing a novel mechanistic basis for targeting this pathway in DED management.
An interesting observation was the concurrent upregulation of the anti-apoptotic protein Bcl-2 and enhancement of autophagy following SGK1 inhibition. This apparent discrepancy highlights the complex crosstalk between apoptosis and autophagy. Although Bcl-2 is known to bind and inhibit the autophagy initiator Beclin 1, this interaction is highly dynamic and regulated by post-translational modifications. Rather than acting as a simple switch, the Beclin 1–Bcl-2 complex is thought to function as a rheostat, fine-tuning autophagy levels to maintain cellular homeostasis and prevent excessive self-digestion. In our model, SGK1 inhibition may promote cell survival primarily by upregulating Bcl-2 to suppress apoptosis while concurrently activating other signals, such as via FoxO3a, that modify the Beclin 1 complex or otherwise shift the rheostatic balance to permit a level of pro-survival autophagy. This coordinated response likely represents an integrated cellular adaptation to hyperosmotic stress.
Autophagy is an evolutionarily conserved recycling process in eukaryotic cells that plays vital roles in cellular survival and homeostasis maintenance through lysosomal degradation of organelles, proteins, and macromolecules, with subsequent recycling of breakdown products. This highly responsive process serves as a fundamental cellular response to various stress conditions affecting cellular homeostasis. During oxidative stress, ROS can induce autophagy, which in turn clears oxidatively damaged mitochondria, endoplasmic reticulum components, peroxisomes, and proteins, thereby delaying cell death. Consequently, mild autophagy activation typically accompanies the pathogenesis of DED. Previous studies have demonstrated autophagy occurrence in the lacrimal glands of mouse DED models and increased LC3-Ⅱ expression in HCECs under hyperosmotic stress. However, this stress-induced autophagy remains limited in DED, insufficient to completely remove damaged organelles and cytoplasmic components through lysosomal pathways to maintain corneal homeostasis. Because the endogenous autophagic response is partial and inadequate, enhancing autophagic activity may improve clinical outcomes in DED patients. Our previous investigations have identified several pharmacological approaches to augment autophagy in DED: Salidroside activates autophagy through the AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK)/sirtuin 1 (SIRT1) pathway to alleviate oxidative stress ; SIRT3 promotes autophagy via deacetylation of FoxO1 ; and evodiamine enhances autophagic flux through the p53/mTOR pathway to mitigate oxidative damage. In line with this concept, the present study demonstrated that the autophagy activator RAPA protects the ocular surface in DED mice, and in vitro experiments confirmed that autophagy activation reduces both oxidative stress and apoptosis in hyperosmotic-treated HCECs.

## Conclusions
Our study demonstrated SGK1 upregulation in DED and identified SGK1 inhibition as protective through FoxO3a-mediated autophagy. Although our data strongly support FoxO3a as a key mediator, SGK1 is known to influence autophagy through other pathways, such as by modulating mTOR activity or phosphorylating Unc-51-like kinase 1 (ULK1). It is plausible that, in DED, SGK1 inhibition exerts a coordinated effect on multiple nodes within the autophagy regulatory network. The predominant role of FoxO3a in our model may reflect cell type- or stress-specific signaling priorities. Therefore, although this study focused on the SGK1/FoxO3a axis, we acknowledge that other downstream effectors of SGK1 may also contribute to DED pathogenesis. The translational potential of SGK1 inhibition in DED is promising but requires careful evaluation. Although the inhibitor GSK650394 has shown efficacy in preclinical models of other diseases, , a thorough assessment of its safety profile for ocular use is imperative before clinical translation. Critical unknowns include potential off-target effects on related kinases, long-term impacts on corneal homeostasis and wound healing, and specific toxicity to other ocular tissues such as the lens and retina. Future work should aim to develop optimized formulations to enhance corneal bioavailability and minimize systemic exposure, paving the way for clinical assessment.
