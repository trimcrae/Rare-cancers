## Abstract
Spinal cord injury (SCI) is accompanied by a significant microglia-associated inflammatory response that is associated with secondary tissue damage and poorer functional outcomes. Serum and glucocorticoid-regulated kinase 1 (SGK1) has been implicated in the regulation of cell survival and neuronal excitability in various diseases. However, the role and cell-specific mechanism of SGK1 in SCI remain to be elucidated. In this study, we observed that SGK1 was predominantly expressed in microglia located at the lesion margin during the early phase of SCI in a mouse contusion model. Inhibition of SGK1 by GSK650394 has been shown to promote neural repair while simultaneously suppressing neuroinflammation and mitochondrial oxidative stress. Mechanistically, the inhibition of SGK1 results in a reduction of FoxO1 phosphorylation and the promotion of nuclear import, consequently inducing microglial mitophagy and promoting mitochondrial homeostasis, leading to the suppression of absent in melanoma 2 (AIM2) related pyroptosis and the conversion of microglia into a neuroprotective M2 phenotype. In particular, AIM2 overexpression or deletion effectively interfered with the influence of SGK1-FoxO1 on the modulation of SCI. In conclusion, the present findings provide a potential therapeutic strategy for the treatment of SCI.

## Introduction
Spinal cord injury (SCI) is a serious traumatic event that disrupts nerve cell connections and induces irreversible functional deficits. To date, numerous efforts have been made to explore optimal therapeutic strategies for SCI treatment through preclinical or animal studies, including gene intervention, novel drug research and development, targeted new biomaterials, while no significant breakthrough has been achieved, particularly in the context of implementing these strategies in individual clinical cases [ – ]. Consequently, the prevailing treatment for patients with SCI primarily depends on surgical neural decompression during the early stages. These discrepancies may be attributable to the multifaceted pathophysiological mechanisms underlying secondary injuries. Therefore, exploring a potential mechanism is essential to identify novel therapeutic targets for the treatment of SCI.
Microglia act as the innate immune effectors of the central nervous system (CNS), maintaining inflammatory balance across a spectrum of physiological and pathological conditions. In pathological processes, microglia undergo activation and proliferation, subsequently migrating to the site of injury to eliminate the debris and produce neurotrophic factors [ ]. On the other hand, microglia have been observed to induce peripheral leukocyte infiltration and secrete pro-inflammatory cytokines, thereby exacerbating the inflammatory response [ ]. It has been reported that the effect of microglia is dependent on phenotype, the M1 phenotype is pro-inflammatory while the M2 phenotype is neuroprotective. Converting microglia to the M2 phenotype has been shown to facilitate functional recovery after neural injury [ ]. Therefore, focused on the regulation of activated microglia is imperative for the development of effective treatment strategies for SCI.
The accumulated evidences have demonstrated that inflammasomes play a critical role in the process of neuroinflammation, this role involves the identification of damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) and the subsequent induction of pro-inflammatory caspase-mediated pyroptosis [ , ]. As one of the inflammasomes, absent in melanoma 2 (AIM2) has been proposed in various research focusing on the CNS. In the normal brain development, AIM2 is known to be activated in response to DNA damage, while deficiencies in AIM2 and defects in the sensor of DNA damage have been associated with the onset of anxiety-related behaviors [ ]. In the process of neural injury, AIM2 activation induces pyroptosis and the release of inflammatory mediators, and the inhibition of AIM2 is beneficial for the neural recovery [ ]. A paradoxical protective effect is exerted by AIM2 even in the disease state. Inhibition of AIM2 enhances the microglia activation and neuroinflammation during the experimental autoimmune encephalomyelitis (EAE) [ ]. This distinctive finding is also found in the pathological process of antiviral-related neuroinflammation in mouse models of Parkinson’s disease [ ]. Given the polyhedric roles of AIM2, further exploration is necessary to substantiate the evidence regarding AIM2 in SCI. Furthermore, recent studies have indicated that mitochondrial function plays a pivotal role in AIM2 regulation. Specifically, it has been demonstrated that mitochondrial oxidative stress and mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) leakage in microglia can trigger AIM2 activation and pyroptosis following SCI [ ]. However, the upstream kinase–transcriptional circuitry that restores mitophagy to regulate this axis remains incompletely defined.
Serum and glucocorticoid-regulated kinase 1 (SGK1) belongs to the family of AGC kinases and is highly expressed in the CNS. It exhibits ubiquitous distribution across cerebral regions and demonstrates significant upregulation under diverse neuropathological conditions. It has been reported that the elevation of SGK1 leads to an increase in Tau phosphorylation and a subsequent destabilization of microtubules, thus contributing to the progression of Alzheimer’s disease [ ]. In addition, SGK1 levels increased in the hippocampus and impaired myelination in diabetes-associated cognitive dysfunction [ ]. In the context of SCI, recent research has indicated that neural SGK1 inhibition exacerbates depressive-like behaviors following injury [ ]. The specific contribution of SGK1 to functional restoration following SCI remains largely unexplored. From a mechanistic perspective, SGK1 has been identified as a pivotal mediator in orchestrating the coordination between mitochondrial activity and ATP synthesis under conditions of oxidative stress, regulating ferroptosis within Nrf2 dependent pathways, these evidences suggested that SGK1 plays a critical role in maintaining mitochondrial homeostasis [ , ]. Furthermore, SGK1 has been postulated as a pivotal modulator of immune-mediated regulation over metastatic growth in hepatocellular carcinoma in dependent on the inflammatory cell induced necroptosis [ ]. However, the role of SGK1 in the mitochondrial regulatory axis that constrains AIM2-mediated pyroptosis after SCI remains to be elucidated.
In this study, we investigated the effect of SGK1 on SCI as well as the underlying mechanisms, with a particular focus on the role AIM2-triggered inflammatory response in microglia through the use of an experimental mouse model in vivo and BV2 cells in vitro. Utilizing a selective SGK1 inhibitor, a comprehensive investigation was conducted to assess the hypothesis that the targeting of SGK1 constitutes a promising therapeutic approach for SCI.

## Methods and materials

## Analysis of public microarray and scRNA-seq datasets from spinal cord
The publicly available microarray expression datasets GSE5296 and GSE47681 were obtained via the NCBI GEO repository. Raw microarray data were preprocessed using standard procedures including background correction, normalization, and probe summarization. Batch effects among studies were adjusted using the ComBat algorithm (sva package, R) prior to subsequent analyses. A LOESS regression model was then used to characterize the time-course expression pattern of SGK1. The scRNA-seq data was obtained from Figshare, as previously reported [ ]. The study analyzed processed transcriptomic data from 26 spinal cord samples, including uninjured samples (n = 6), and samples at 1(n = 3), 3 (n = 6), 7 (n = 6), and 14 (n = 5) days post-injury (dpi). Low-dimensional embeddings were computed by Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) in Seurat. To identify the differential genes in the microglia isolated from the uninjured and 7 dpi groups, Gene Ontology (GO) databases were used for pathway enrichment analysis, adjusted P < 0.05. We investigated the expression of genes including SGK1, AIM2, Caspase1, GSDMD, PINK1, CTSB, LAMP1, ATG5, HO-1 and SOD2 across these clusters and within the uninjured and 7 dpi groups.

## Network pharmacology analysis
A comprehensive collection of disease-related targets associated with SCI was obtained from the GeneCards, OMIM, and DisGeNET databases. This collection was then curated to remove duplicated entries, resulting in a unified set of disease targets. The molecular structure and SMILES information of the compound GSK650394 were obtained from the PubChem database, and its potential targets were inferred through the SwissTargetPrediction, SEA, and SuperPred platforms. The intersecting genes between the drug targets and disease targets were determined and illustrated using the VennDiagram package within R. Enrichment analysis was used to annotate the biological functions of these genes. GO enrichment and KEGG pathway analyses were conducted to characterize the gene functions and to identify pathways that might mediate the therapeutic effects of GSK650394. All enrichment analyses and visualizations were executed using R software packages including DOSE, clusterProfiler, colorspace, stringi, enrichplot, and ggplot2.

## Animals and ethics statement
Female wild-type C57BL/6 mice (20–25 g) were supplied by the Experimental Animal Center at Wenzhou Medical University (License No. SYXK [ZJ] 2020-0014; Zhejiang, China). AIM2 global knockout (AIM2 KO) mice were generated by Shanghai Model Organisms Center, Inc. (Shanghai, China). All procedures were accordance with the China NIH Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals and had been approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Wenzhou Medical University (Approval No. XMSQ-2024-358).

## Animal model of SCI
Animals were anesthetized with 1% sodium pentobarbital (50 mg/kg, intraperitoneal). A dorsal thoracic midline incision was made after ensuring an appropriate depth of anesthesia, and paraspinal muscles were separated to access the vertebral laminae. To facilitate visualization of the dorsal surface of the cord, the T9–T10 laminae were surgically excised. Using a weight-drop impactor, a 10-g rod was allowed to fall 20 mm to strike the exposed spinal cord, thereby establishing a contusion SCI model. The wound was closed with 4 − 0 nonabsorbable silk sutures. Subsequent to the surgical procedure, the mice were maintained at a temperature-regulated environment until they exhibited full alertness, and administered a subcutaneous injection of meloxicam (1 mg/kg) to alleviate any discomfort. To prevent urinary retention, the bladder was manually expressed two times per day until spontaneous voiding resumed. In contrast, the animals that underwent sham surgery underwent T9–T10 laminectomy without contusion.

## Adeno-associated virus (AAV) vector packaging and injection
The AAV-CD68-AIM2-mouse-EGFP and the corresponding control AAV-CD68-EGFP were constructed and packaged by Suzhou GenePharma Biotechnology Co., Ltd. The viral genome titer was determined by qPCR (1 × 1012 vg/mL). Two weeks prior to the injury, 12 µL of AAV was administered intrathecally. Successful injection was judged by an immediate tail-shaking or tail-swing behaviors. To evaluate AAV-AIM2 transduction, spinal cords were harvested at day 3 post-injury, processed for tissue sections, and EGFP and Iba1 co-immunostaining was imaged by fluorescence microscopy in Sham and SCI mice.

## Validation of AIM2 KO mice
Approximately 1 cm of tail tip was obtained from each animal, and genomic DNA was prepared in accordance with the protocol supplied by the Tissue Genomic DNA Extraction Kit (TIANGEN, DP304). The target gene fragment was subsequently amplified via PCR, the primer sequences including:
P1 (WT), 5’→3’, TCACAAGAACATGACACAGGACTA;
P2 (Common), 5’→3’, ACACATGTGCGTATGTGTATGCCT;
P3 (Mutant), 5’→3’, GCACAAATAACCCACATAACCTG.
A 1% agarose gel was used for electrophoresis (120 V, 30 min). After the run, gels were imaged on a documentation system with the suitable fluorescence channel/wavelength.

## Drug administration and group design
GSK650394 (GSK6), a selective SGK1 inhibitor, was utilized for pharmacological intervention. GSK650394 for animal use was dissolved and formulated in a solution comprising 10% DMSO, 40% PEG300, 5% Tween-80, 45% saline. This solution was subjected to brief sonication to ensure the uniformity of the mixture. Mice received intraperitoneal injections of GSK650394 (15 mg/kg). The FoxO1 inhibitor AS1842856 was administered intraperitoneally at 20 mg/kg using the same vehicle. Sham-operated animals were injected with an equal volume of vehicle. Mice were assigned to the following groups: a Sham group, a GSK650394 group, a SCI group, a SCI + GSK650394 group, a SCI + GSK650394 + AS1842856 group, a SCI + 3-MA group, a SCI + GSK650394 + 3-MA group, an AAV-Control group, an AAV-AIM2 group, an AAV-Control + SCI group, an AAV-AIM2 + SCI group, an AAV-Control + SCI+ GSK650394 group, an AAV-AIM2 + SCI + GSK650394 group, an AIM2 KO group, an AIM2 KO + SCI group, a SCI + AS1842856 group, an AIM2 KO + SCI + AS1842856 group.

## Cell culture and drug administration
BV2 microglial cells were purchased from Shanghai FuHeng Biology Co., Ltd. (China) and cultured at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO₂. Cells were maintained under standard conditions in DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% P/S. Oxidative stress was induced by tert-butyl hydroperoxide (TBHP, 100 µM). GSK6 and AS1842856 were used at 10 µM and 1 µM, respectively. The cells were treated under the following conditions: Control, TBHP, LPS, starvation, GSK650394, AS1842856, TBHP + GSK650394, TBHP + AS1842856, and TBHP + GSK650394 + AS1842856.

## Locomotion recovery evaluation

## Basso mouse scale (BMS)
The Basso Mouse Scale assessment was performed by placing mice in an open arena and allowing them to move freely for a period of five minutes. Their behavior was then observed for two minutes to assign scores. The evaluation criteria encompassed a multifaceted assessment of hind limb movement, coordination between limbs, paw placement, trunk support, and tail alignment. Scores were assigned on a scale ranging from 0, indicating complete motor paralysis, to 9, indicating normal and coordinated hindlimb function.

## Inclined plane test
During the inclined plane assessment, the mice were set on an adjustable platform covered with a rubber layer. The tilt angle was gradually increased until the animal could no longer maintain its position for 5 s, with the maximum sustainable angle recorded as an indicator of hind limb strength.

## Footprint analysis
For footprint analysis, the hind paws of the subjects were marked with red dye, while the forepaws were marked with blue dye. Subsequently, the mice were guided through a narrow corridor while their gait patterns and stride parameters were meticulously documented. All behavioral assessments were performed by two independent investigators under blinded experimental conditions to ensure objective scoring.

## Locomotor trajectory acquisition and analysis
Hindlimb locomotor trajectories were recorded using a multi-camera Vicon motion capture system while mice crawled straightly on a transparent flat platform. Miniature passive reflective markers were placed over the L6 vertebra, sacrum, hip, knee, ankle, and distal fifth metatarsal. Three-dimensional marker trajectories were captured at a frequency of 120 Hz, reconstructed and low-pass filtered in Vicon Nexus. These trajectories were then exported for further analysis. Stride events were identified from metatarsal marker displacement, and only strides with continuous marker visibility were analyzed. The foot trajectories, stride length, maximal toe height, and hindlimb range of motion were computed using the MATLAB.

## Tissue preparation and histology
At pre-set time points, the animals were euthanized using sodium pentobarbital, prepared at a concentration of 8% (w/v), with a dosage of 40 mg/kg. Following the perfusion of the animals with normal saline, 15-mm spinal cord sections centered on the injury site were meticulously dissected and rapidly frozen at − 80 °C for subsequent Western blot analysis. For histological preparation, the mice were subjected to transcardial perfusion with a solution of physiological saline, followed by a solution of 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA). A 10-mm segment of the spinal cord was then isolated and placed in 4% PFA for an additional 24 h of post-fixation. Finally, the tissue was processed into paraffin for subsequent slicing. For histology staining, tissue sections sequentially underwent deparaffinization through three rounds of xylene immersion, each lasting 15 min, and rehydration via a graded ethanol series from 100% to 70% ethanol, with 5 min per concentration. Following PBS rinsing using 0.01 M buffer at pH 7.4 for 5 min, the specimens were ready for next evaluation. For immunohistochemistry (IHC) staining, the deparaffinized spinal cord sections were subjected to an incubation with 3% H₂O₂ in methanol for 10 min and subsequently blocked with serum solution for 30 min. The sections were then subjected to an incubation with an anti-SGK1 primary antibody (1:100 dilution) for 12 h, subsequently followed by an incubation with HRP-conjugated secondary antibody for 2 h. Subsequently, the sections were subjected to an incubation with 3,3’-diaminobenzidine tetrahydrochloride (DAB) for a period of 10 min. Sections were also stained with hematoxylin and eosin (HE) in combination with luxol fast blue (LFB). The images were obtained using an Olympus light microscope (Tokyo, Japan).

## Western blot
Using an OMNI Prep device, the tissues were homogenized in cold RIPA buffer enriched with a cocktail of phosphatase and protease inhibitors. A total of 3–5 repetitions (60 s each) were executed, ensuring a 5-minute cooling phase followed every cycle to preserve the protein content. After centrifugation at 12,000 × g for 30 min, the clarified supernatant was analyzed for protein concentration via a BCA assay according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The membrane and nuclear proteins were prepared using a sequential extraction protocol (NE-PER reagents, Thermo Fisher). For Western blot analysis, 60 µg of total protein was loaded onto 12% polyacrylamide gels. Following electrophoresis, the proteins were electroblotted onto Millipore PVDF membranes (0.45 μm pore size). To minimize non-specific binding, the membranes were blocked using 5% nonfat milk for two hours at ambient temperature, followed by an overnight incubation with primary antibodies at 4 °C. The dilution ratio of primary antibodies were listed in Supplemental materials. After washing in TBST, the membranes were incubated with HRP-linked secondary antibodies for 2 h, followed by a final series of three TBST washes. The detection of signals was accomplished through the utilization of ECL, followed by imaging with the ChemiDoc XRS+ system (Bio-Rad).

## Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)
To obtain an accurate assessment of inflammatory cytokines expression in the samples, we strictly followed the kit instructions and used the ELISA method to measure the concentrations of IL-4, IL-6, IL-10 and TNF-α in tissue homogenates and cell culture supernatants (Elabscience, E-EL-M0043, E-EL-M0044, E-EL-M0046, E-EL-M3063). Samples, standards, and blank wells were loaded onto the 96-well plate, after which the biotin-labeled antibody solution and the HRP-conjugated enzyme solution and subsequently the substrate reagent were introduced. Following the required incubation and wash cycles, a stop buffer was applied, and the absorbance at 450 nm was recorded using a TECAN microplate reader.

## TUNEL assay
The assessment of cell death in tissue sections was evaluated by an in situ TUNEL assay using a commercial kit (Yeasen Biochemical, 40307ES60,). We incubated the sections in 0.1% Triton X-100 for 30 min to achieve permeabilization. The TUNEL reaction mixture (50 µL) was then applied, followed by incubation at 37 °C for 1–2 h. TUNEL-positive cells were imaged using a Keyence BZ-X800E microscope and quantified with ImageJ (v2.1).

## Immunofluorescence (IF) staining
Spinal cord segments were harvested at predefined post-injury intervals and processed for immunofluorescence analysis. Spinal cord segments were processed using a consistent staining workflow. The paraffin embedded sections were cleared through graded xylene/ethanol steps and returned to aqueous conditions by rehydration, followed by PBS rinses. Sections underwent microwave-based retrieval in 10.2 mM sodium citrate (pH 6.0) maintained at 95 °C for 20 min. Afterwards, the slides were permeabilized in PBS containing 0.1% Triton X-100 for 10 min at room temperature, followed by blocking with 10% BSA/PBS for 1 h to minimize nonspecific binding. Primary antibodies were incubated at 4 °C for 16 h, followed by three PBS washes. The dilution ratio of primary antibodies were listed in Supplemental materials. FITC-conjugated secondary antibodies were used for detection (RT, 1 h), and DAPI was subsequently applied to label nuclei. Images were acquired using a Keyence BZ-X800E microscope under fixed acquisition settings. For quantification, six randomly selected fields were analyzed per section across six biological replicates.

## Oxidative stress assessment in spinal cord
DHE staining (Beyotime, S0064S) was utilised to detect reactive oxygen species (ROS) production in the spinal cord, with cell localisation clarified via co-localisation with Iba1. The spinal cord sections were dewaxed, hydrated, washed, blocked, and incubated with Iba1 antibody at 4 °C for 12–16 h. Subsequently, the sections were subjected to treatment with fluorescent secondary antibodies and DHE solution (5 µmol/L). Subsequent to this, the samples were subjected to an incubation process in a hydrostatic bath maintained at a temperature of 37 °C for two hours. Finally, sections were sealed with an anti-fluorescence quencher containing DAPI. Microscopic images were obtained using a Keyence BZ-X800E microscope. Furthermore, the level of oxidative stress in the spinal cord was measured by SOD (Beyotime, S0087) and MDA (Beyotime, S0131S) kit in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions.

## Bulk RNA-seq of BV2 Cells and differential expression analysis
BV2 cells were analyzed under two conditions: TBHP alone and TBHP combined with GSK6. Each condition included five independent culture dishes (n = 5 biological replicates). The Hiplot platform was used to run a DESeq2 workflow for differential expression profiling. Genes were considered differentially expressed when the adjusted P value was < 0.05 and the fold change was ≥ 2. GO and KEGG enrichment analyses were performed using Hiplot’s Functional Enrichment tool, with hypergeometric testing and Benjamini–Hochberg correction. Enriched terms were selected at adjusted P < 0.05.

## Assessment of mitochondrial function
To determine the level of mitochondrial ROS, BV2 cells were incubated with a 10 µM MitoSOX Red staining solution (Beyotime, S0061M) in the dark at 37 °C for 20 min following drug administration. Following the incubation period, the upper layer of the mixture was extracted by aspiration, and the cells were washed with PBS.
The levels of mitochondrial ROS were subsequently assessed using a fluorescence microscope. Furthermore, a mitochondrial DNA isolation kit (Abcam, ab65321) was utilised for the extraction of cytosolic mtDNA. The level of cytosolic mtDNA was measured by quantifying the mean level of cytochrome c oxidase 1 via RT-PCR, normalised by 18 S [ ]. The mitochondrial membrane potential (MMP) of BV2 cells was determined by employing a JC-1 fluorescent probe (Beyotime Biotechnology, C2006). Following treatment period in 6-well plates, the cells were harvested and subjected to JC-1 staining at a temperature of 37 °C for a period of 20 min. Fluorescence images were acquired by Keyence BZ-X800E microscope, and signals were quantified with ImageJ (v2.1).

## Flow cytometry analysis
The 2′,7′-dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate (H2DCFDA, Beyotime Biotechnology, C1190) fluorescence was used as a readout for intracellular ROS in BV2 cells. After the indicated treatments, the cells were collected and rinsed, followed by incubation with H2DCFDA (10 µM) at 37 °C for 20–30 min. Samples were gently agitated every 5 min to facilitate dye loading. The cells were then washed, resuspended in PBS, and analyzed on a flow cytometer (Ex 488 nm/Em 525 nm). At least 10,000 events were acquired per sample, DCF mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) was quantified using FlowJo (v10.9.0).

## Statistics
All data are reported as the mean ± SD. Graphpad prism (v8.0.2) was used for all statistical analysis. The number of independent biological replicates (n) is provided in each figure legends. The normality of the data was assessed using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov normality test, and the variance homogeneity was examined using Levene’s test. p > 0.01 was considered to indicate a normal distribution. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used for comparisons across multiple groups, with Tukey’s post-test used for pairwise differences. Nonnormally distributed data were analyzed using nonparametric Kruskal-Wallis ANOVA followed by Dunn’s post hoc test. A two-way repeated-measures ANOVA framework was used for time-course behavioral analyses, with multiple comparisons controlled using LSD test. p < 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance.

## Results

## SGK1 is increased in microglia after SCI
Previous studies have reported increased SGK1 expression in neurodegenerative diseases and ischemic brain injuries [ , ]. Here, we evaluated changes in SGK1 expression in SCI using public GEO microarray datasets. Bioinformatics analysis revealed that SGK1 is upregulated in the injured spinal cord at various time points (Fig. A). To verify these results, we established a moderate spinal cord contusion model, and western blot showed increased SGK1 levels (Fig. B-C). IHC staining demonstrated that SGK1 was predominantly expressed in the lesion margin after SCI (Fig. D-E). Additionally, a single-cell sequencing dataset was obtained from the published research and reanalyzed using the R package [ ]. Cell types in sham and post-injury samples were visualized using split UMAP projections (Figure S1A), which showed prominent SGK1 expression in the microglial cluster in both the split view and the overall UMAP overlay (Figure S1B and Fig. F). Furthermore, the dot plot suggested that SGK1 expression increases significantly at 3 and 7 dpi (Fig. G). GO enrichment analysis indicated that differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in microglia were enriched in pathways associated with inflammation, oxidative stress, and autophagy (Fig. H). In microglia at 3 and 7 dpi, higher expression of SGK1, AIM2, and selected autophagy and oxidative stress related genes was observed (Fig. I). Together, these findings suggest that SGK1 in microglia is linked to autophagy and oxidative stress during SCI pathology. To further confirm SGK1 upregulation in different cell types, SGK1 was co-stained with NeuN, GFAP, and Iba1, respectively. Notably, SGK1 upregulation was most prominent in microglia (Fig. J-M). Subsequently, immunofluorescence revealed increased SGK1 levels in BV2 cells subjected to TBHP, LPS, or starvation stimulation (Figure S2A-B). Western blot analysis showed similar trends in BV2 cells (Figure S2C-D).It has been reported that SGK1 is associated with oxidative stress regulation in CNS [ ]. To explore the effect of oxidative stress on SGK1 in BV2 cells, the level of SGK1 was detected at various time points. The western blot indicated a more than threefold increase changes in the abundance of SGK1 at 6 h after TBHP treatment (Figure S2E-F). Thus, the TBHP stimulation was selected for cell administration in vitro. Collectively, these data indicated that SGK1 was associated with the progression of SCI.
 

## SGK1 inhibition promotes function recovery and neural repair after SCI
A previous study suggested that inhibiting SGK1 kinase activity is beneficial for cell homeostasis [ ]. Thus, mice were treated daily with GSK650394 to repress the level of SGK1 phosphorylation (Fig. A-B). HE staining revealed that SCI mice with SGK1 inhibition had a smaller lesion area than SCI mice without SGK1 inhibition (Fig. C-D). LFB staining further demonstrated that SGK1 inhibition preserves myelinated white matter after SCI. This is reflected by an increased LFB-positive area in GSK650394-treated mice compared to untreated SCI mice (Figure S3A-B). TUNEL evaluation demonstrated a decreased number of TUNEL-positive cells in SGK1-inhibited SCI mice compared to SCI mice (Figure S3C-D). Furthermore, immunofluorescence staining with SYP and TUJ1 confirmed the effect of neuronal and synaptic integrity by SGK1 inhibition. Compared with the SCI group, the synapse intensity in the SCI mice treated with GSK650394 was significantly higher (Fig. E-F). To determine the role of SGK1 in behavioral function after SCI, we assessed motor function in mice using the BMS score, inclined plane test, footprint analysis, and hindlimb movement analysis. BMS score results demonstrated that SCI mice receiving GSK650394 treatment exhibited significantly improved motor capacity, as evidenced by higher BMS scores, especially 28 and 42 dpi (Fig. G). Consistently, the inclined plane test revealed that the treatment group exhibited a higher angle and hindlimb muscle strength relative to the injured group (Fig. H). Footprint analysis showed that SCI mice treated with GSK650394 exhibited significantly improved motor coordination, as evidenced by increased stride length compared to SCI mice (Fig. I-J). In addition, by labeling the toes, ankles, knees, hips, and crests of mice, we captured and analyzed the dynamic movement of the hind limbs (Fig. K). Continuous hindlimb movement revealed that only the sham and GSK650394 treatment groups exhibited a swing gait and assortative movement, as evidenced by the red lines in Fig. L and M. Further analysis of ankle angles indicated that the angles were lower in the movement of the sham and GSK650394 treatment mice. SCI mice had greater ankle angles, suggesting that their movement mainly depends on toe dragging. However, SCI mice treated with GSK650394 had lower ankle angles, indicating that the ankle joint gained partial flexion and extension movement to replace toe dragging (Fig. L and N). Therefore, these data demonstrate that GSK650394 inhibits SGK1, reducing neural tissue damage and facilitating functional recovery in SCI mice.
 

## SGK1 inhibition promotes mitochondrial homeostasis
To further determine the mechanism by which SGK1 enhances function recovery and tissue repair, we identified the downstream target genes of SGK1 by performing mRNA sequencing in BV2 cells. A comparison of the TBHP group with the GSK650394 treatment group revealed that the former exhibited a total of 1,030 downregulated genes and 1,600 upregulated genes (Fig. A). GO analysis demonstrated that the molecular functions of these different genes were primarily enriched in the mechanisms including autophagy, mitochondrion, neuroinflammation response, and programmed cell death (Fig. B). KEGG analysis revealed that the functions of the DEGs were predominantly enriched in the pathways including TNF signaling pathway and FoxO signaling pathway (Fig. C). According to the GO analysis and previous studies, SGK1 is implicated in mitochondrial regulation [ ]. Therefore, the present study investigated the influence of SGK1 on oxidative stress and mitochondrial function. Western blot analysis revealed that HO-1 and SOD2 protein levels were significantly elevated in mice with SCI treated with GSK650394 (Fig. D-E). DHE staining revealed that administration of GSK650394 after SCI significantly reduced DHE-positive microglia cells relative to untreated SCI mice (Fig. F-G). The results of the SOD and MDA kit analysis further corroborated these findings, revealing an increase in the level of SOD and a reduction in the level of MDA in the GSK650394 + SCI group relative to the SCI group (Fig. H-I). Consequently, the present study demonstrated that SGK1 inhibition is beneficial for the repressing oxidative stress. In an in vitro experiment, the level of mitochondrial oxidative stress was assessed by mitoSOX staining in BV2 cells. The results demonstrated that, in the TBHP and GSK650394 treatment group, the intensity of mitoSOX was remarkably decreased in comparison with the TBHP group (Fig. J-K). An evaluation of mitochondrial function was conducted through the utilization of JC-1 staining. The findings demonstrated a conspicuous enhancement in mitochondrial membrane potential subsequent to the administration of GSK650394 in TBHP-treated cells (Figs. L-M). Furthermore, the positive ratio of H2DCFDA was lower in BV2 cells treated with GSK650394 and TBHP than in those treated with TBHP alone (Fig. N-O). Taken together, these findings indicated the pivotal role of SGK1 in the regulation of mitochondrial function in microglia.
 

## SGK1 inhibition promotes FoxO1 mediated mitophagy
Given the effect of SGK1 in regulating of mitochondrial function and oxidative stress, a subsequent investigation was undertaken to elucidate the underlying mechanism of SGK1 within the mitochondrial context. The evidence has demonstrated that the FoxO1 pathway and mitophagy are implicated in the SGK1 inhibition process as evidenced by mRNA sequencing (Fig. B-C). To further verify these results, we investigated whether SGK1 inhibition can improve mitophagy in microglia. As demonstrated in Fig. A-B, treatment with GSK650394 resulted in an augmentation of mitophagy and lysosome-related markers in SCI mice. Furthermore, immunofluorescence staining of LC3B with TOM20 was conducted on BV2 cells. The results demonstrated that the group receiving a combination of GSK650394 and TBHP exhibited a greater number of co-localization dots for LC3B and TOM20 in comparison to the TBHP group (Fig. C). These outcomes revealed that SGK1 inhibition by GSK650394 can effectively activate mitophagy in microglia. Furthermore, we investigated the impact of SGK1 on FoxO1. The results of the FoxO1 transcription factor activity analysis indicated increased activity after GSK650394 treatment (Figure S4A). Furthermore, we employed the SwissTargetPrediction, SEA, and SuperPred to ascertain the impact of SGK1 on FoxO1. The results revealed that a total of 10,047 targets were recognized, and 173 shared targets were discovered between GSK650394 and disease (Fig. D). These common targets were subjected to GO enrichment analysis, and the results were found to be largely consistent with those of GO enrichment from mRNA sequencing (Fig. E). Furthermore, the KEGG enrichment analysis identified the FoxO1 signaling as a common target (Fig. F). Consequently, we hypothesized that the SGK1-mediated mitophagy is associated with FoxO1 signaling. We conducted experiments to measure the level of SGK1 and FoxO1 phosphorylation subsequent to treatment with TBHP and GSK650394. In comparison with the control group, TBHP treatment resulted in increased phosphorylation of SGK1 and FoxO1, these alterations were suppressed by GSK650394 treatment (Fig. G-H). Moreover, it has been documented that FoxO1 phosphorylation serves as a regulatory mechanism that controls the movement of FoxO1 from the cytoplasm to the nucleus, thereby modulating additional transcription processes [ ]. As demonstrated in Fig. I-J, the results indicated a significant increase in nuclear FoxO1 after GSK650394 treatment. A multitude of studies have identified the capacity of FoxO1 to regulate autophagy by means of promoting the transcription of molecules associated with autophagy [ , ]. To further prove that GSK650394 induced mitophagy is related to FoxO1, inhibition of FoxO1 activity by AS1842856, an inhibitor of FoxO1, was employed. The results of the western blot demonstrated that the treatment with AS1842856 led to a significant decrease in the level of FoxO1 phosphorylation, whereas no effect was observed on SGK1 activity (Figure S4B-C). Furthermore, the AS1842856 treatment effectively repressed GSK650394-mediated mitophagy, as evidenced by the decreased levels of Parkin, PINK1, and LC3B, and the increased level of P62 (Fig. K-L). Mechanistically, the data support a model in which SGK1 inhibition promotes FoxO1 nuclear import, enhances mitophagy-driven mitochondrial quality control.
 

## SGK1 mediates microglial AIM2 inflammasome and pyroptosis
According to previous studies, mtDNA leakage is responsible for inflammasome activation mediated by mitochondrial dysfunction [ ]. Therefore, we speculated that SGK1 is involved in regulating mtDNA location. Immunofluorescence analysis of TOM20 with dsDNA provided additional evidence that inhibiting SGK1 decreases mtDNA leakage (Fig. A-B). Consistently, extracting dissociative mtDNA in the cytoplasm quantified this level and confirmed the ability of SGK1 inhibition to reduce mtDNA leakage from the mitochondria to the cytoplasm (Fig. C). Previous studies have shown that AIM2 activation and pyroptosis are key drivers of inflammatory responses after SCI [ , ]. Thus, we proceeded to clarify the potential effect of SGK1 on microglia in vivo and in vitro. After SCI, western blot revealed significant upregulation of AIM2, GSDMD-N, cleaved caspase-1, IL-1β, and IL-18 compared to sham-operated mice, indicating activation of AIM2-dependent pyroptosis. In contrast, GSK650394 significantly lowered the levels of these pyroptosis-associated proteins compared to the untreated SCI group (Fig. D-H). Immunofluorescence analysis revealed a significant accumulation of AIM2-positive microglia in the spinal cord lesion area, substantially suppressed by GSK650394 treatment (Figs. I-J). Consistent with the in vivo results, immunofluorescence results from BV2 cells supported these findings, suggesting lower AIM2 immunofluorescence intensity in the GSK650394 + TBHP treatment groups than in the TBHP group (Figs. K-L). Similarly, the number of GSDMD-N-positive microglia was reduced in SCI mice treated with GSK650394 (Figs. M-N). Meanwhile, BV2 cell data further showed that cells subjected to TBHP increased IL-18 levels, but this increase was restricted by SGK1 inhibition (Figs. O-P). Together, these findings suggest that inhibiting SGK1 attenuates AIM2 related pyroptosis.
 

## SGK1 inhibition reduces neuroinflammation
Given the role of SGK1 in SCI progression and its presence in microglia, we hypothesized that the neuroprotective effect of SGK1 inhibition involves modulating inflammatory responses in the injured spinal cord. Initially, we evaluated the levels of inflammatory mediators, including iNOS, TNF-α, and COX-2, at the lesion site by western blot. This analysis revealed decreased levels of these mediators after SGK1 inhibition (Fig.  A-D). ELISA analyses demonstrated that mice treated with GSK650394 exhibited significant decreases in pro-inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α and IL-6) and concomitant increases in anti-inflammatory cytokines (IL-10 and IL-4) (Fig.  E). Western blot analysis showed that Arg-1 expression was low in the Sham and Sham + GSK650394 groups, increased after SCI, and further increased in the SCI + GSK650394 group (Figure S5A-B). These findings suggested that GSK650394 treatment may promote a shift in microglia toward an M2-like phenotype after SCI. Additionally, immunofluorescence revealed that the number of TNF-α and Iba1-positive cells increased after SCI, though SGK1 inhibition remarkably suppressed this alteration (Fig.  F–G). Similarly, a significantly lower level of iNOS in Iba1-positive cells was observed in SCI mice treated with GSK650394 compared to SCI mice (Fig.  H-I). At 7 dpi, immunofluorescence staining for CD11b and GFAP in the spinal cord revealed substantial immune cell infiltration and accumulation at the lesion margin after SCI, whereas the number of CD11b-positive cells was markedly reduced in the SGK1 inhibition group compared with the SCI group (Fig.  J-K). Western blot results showed that TBHP exposure noticeably increased TNF-α, iNOS, and COX-2 production, which was partially counteracted by SGK1 inhibition (Fig.  L–O). Furthermore, immunofluorescence results revealed that BV2 cells treated with TBHP highly expressed TNF-α, whereas treatment with GSK650394 decreased this tendency (Fig.  P–Q). These data suggest that SGK1 inhibition by GSK650394 suppresses the neuroinflammatory response in microglia.
 

## Suppression of FoxO1-mitophagy abrogates the protective effects of SGK1 inhibition
Given the role of SGK1 in regulating FoxO1-mitophagy and inflammatory response in microglia, we hypothesized that the protective effects of SGK1 inhibition are related to the activation of FoxO1-mitophagy. First, we treated mice with 3-MA to inhibit autophagy, noting significant LC3 suppression and P62 accumulation (Fig.  A-B). After mitophagy was inhibited, ELISA results showed a significant increase in TNF-α and a decrease in IL-10 (Fig.  C-D). Additionally, western blot results showed that 3-MA abolished the inhibitory regulation of SGK1 inhibition on AIM2 and pyroptosis markers (Fig.  E-F). To determine the specific alteration of AIM2 in microglia, we performed immunofluorescence staining with Iba1 and AIM2. The results demonstrated that the decreased number of Iba1 and AIM2 positive cells in the GSK650394 + SCI group was reversed by the addition of 3-MA (Fig.  G-H). Similarly, the GSK650394 + TBHP + 3-MA group exhibited damaged mitochondrial function compared to the GSK650394 + TBHP group, as demonstrated by JC-1 and MitoSOX staining results (Figs.  I-L). These results indicated that SGK1 inhibition mediates inflammation suppression and mitochondrial homeostasis, which is partially dependent on mitophagy regulation. To prove that FoxO1 is also involved in SGK1-inhibition-induced protection, FoxO1 repression by AS1842856 was examined. Importantly, AS1842856 administration abrogated the inhibitory effects of GSK650394 on pyroptosis-associated proteins (Fig.  M-N), definitively linking SGK1-driven FoxO1 phosphorylation to pyroptotic microglia. These outcomes suggest that FoxO1 and mitophagy are involved in the regulation of mitochondrial function and AIM2-induced pyroptosis by SGK1 inhibition.
 

## AIM2 involved in SGK1 inhibition mediated neuroprotection
To further clarify the involvement of AIM2 in orchestrating SGK1 regulating inflammatory response after SCI, AIM2 knockout and overexpression models were established in mice. As demonstrated in Fig.  A-B, AAV-AIM2 significantly augmented AIM2 expression in the spinal cord. Furthermore, the results of the immunofluorescence-stained EGFP with Iba1 (Fig.  C) and the stained AIM2 with Iba1 (Figure S6A) demonstrated that the upregulated AIM2 was primarily located in microglia in both the Sham and SCI groups. The construction of AIM2 KO mice has been successfully achieved (Figs.  D, and S6B-C). The results of western blot demonstrated that AIM2 overexpression exacerbates the pyroptosis and inflammation, and reverses GSK650394 mediated protection in inflammation (Fig.  E-F). On the other hand, the findings depicted in Fig.  G-H demonstrated that, in comparison with the SCI group, FoxO1 inhibition significantly promoted pyroptosis in mice. However, the supplementation of AIM2 KO resulted in the repression of this promoting effect and a reduction in the levels of cleaved-caspase-1, GSDMD-N, IL-18, IL-1β, and COX-2. Furthermore, BMS scoring demonstrated that AIM2 KO partially rescued the locomotor deficits induced by FoxO1 inhibition, as evidenced by higher BMS scores in the SCI + AS1842856 + AIM2 KO group than in the SCI + AS1842856 group (Figure S6D-E). The subsequent footprint analysis indicated that AIM2 KO abolished the deteriorative effects of FoxO1 inhibition on hindlimb movement (Fig.  I-J). Subsequent immunofluorescence staining with SYP and TUJ1 revealed that the SCI + AS1842856 + AIM2 KO group reversed the decline in synapse intensity caused by the SCI + AS1842856 group (Fig.  K-L). Taken together, these data revealed that microglial AIM2 is involved in the regulation of SGK1/FoxO1 on pyroptosis and behavior function.
 

## Discussion
SCI constitutes a substantial global public health problem, for which an effective therapeutic strategy is lacking. The primary treatment currently available is surgical decompression, which is performed in the early stages of the condition. The pathological process of SCI is complicated and several potential mechanisms have not been well-established. Thus, providing insight into the novel mechanisms for the pathogenesis of SCI is critical for enhancing clinical treatments. In the current study, we focused on the molecular role of SGK1 in the neuroinflammation through regulating microglial AIM2 inflammasome in SCI. Here, we demonstrated that elevated levels of SGK1 are predominantly expressed in microglia. The administration of an SGK1 inhibitor has been shown to effectively suppress inflammation, enhance tissue repair, and promote functional recovery. Mechanistically, SGK1 inhibition decreases FoxO1 phosphorylation, thereby enhancing FoxO1 nuclear import and promoting mitophagy and mitochondrial homeostasis. Furthermore, mitochondrial homeostasis suppresses the ROS accumulation and mtDNA release, subsequently represses AIM2 related pyroptosis activation. More significantly, we confirmed through AIM2 overexpression or deletion in SCI mice that SGK1-FoxO1 mediated neuroprotection is partially related to the regulatory effect of AIM2-mediated pyroptosis. Collectively, the data indicated a novel role for SGK1 in the progression of SCI and suggested a potential therapeutic approach for SGK1 inhibitor treatment by repressing neuroinflammation in SCI (Fig.  ).
 The SGK family contains SGK1, SGK2, and SGK3 and encodes serine/threonine protein kinases involved in the molecular processes of the cellular stress response by regulating ion channels, membrane transporters, and transcription factors. Previous studies have implicated the SGK family in the pathological progression of carcinoma, cardiac remodeling, and aortic dissection [ , – ]. Among SGK isoforms, SGK1 has notably received extensive attention in the CNS field. SGK1 inhibition in neurons has been shown to reduce HDAC6, decrease Tau phosphorylation, and destabilize microtubules in Alzheimer’s disease (AD) [ ]. Additionally, increased SGK1 levels have been associated with impaired myelination in the hippocampus. SGK1 knockdown has been shown to reverse demyelination via the NDRG1-dependent pathway in diabetes-associated cognitive dysfunction [ ]. Consistent with these findings, our results revealed that SGK1 levels are significantly higher in public data and in a mouse model of traumatic SCI. Furthermore, we proved that SGK1 inhibition promotes spinal cord tissue repair and function recovery through SGK1-specific inhibitor administration. Regarding cell location, previous evidence has suggested that increased SGK1 can be observed in neurons, astrocytes, microglia, and oligodendrocytes [ ]. Currently, we have observed that SGK1 is primarily localized in microglia, with significant upregulation in this cell type. We surmised that this alteration is mainly attributed to the lesion site with widespread microglia proliferation and infiltration.
SGK1 is primarily mentioned as a regulator of ion homeostasis and inflammation modulation. Previous reports have shown that SGK1 induces macrophage migration and proliferation via NF-κB pathways [ , ]. Additionally, a previous study demonstrated that SGK1 is involved in the inhibition of Tregs and the development of Th17 cells during autoimmune tissue inflammation [ ]. The inflammatory response mediated by microglia is the most prevalent and significant mechanism in CNS biological processes. In Parkinson’s disease and cerebral ischemia, inhibiting SGK1 in glial cells has been considered a potential strategy for alleviating neuroinflammation [ , ]. Given the critical involvement of SGK1 in the microglia after SCI, we investigated the effect of SGK1 on regulation of inflammation. Similar to previous evidence, our research proved that SGK1 inhibition benefits the repression of microglia activation and the accumulation of inflammatory mediators in the lesion margin.
This study also emphasized the important role of the AIM2 inflammasome as a key target of SGK1 in modulating inflammation. Previous studies have shown that AIM2 activation occurs in neurotrauma and neurodegenerative disorders, including SCI, ischemic stroke, and cognitive disorder [ , , ]. AIM2 is considered not only to promote inflammation, but also to be involved in immune surveillance and inflammation inhibition. A study found that SGK1 inhibition improved Ang II-stimulated IL-1β secretion and caspase-1 activation in macrophages by blocking the NLRP3 inflammasome during hypertensive cardiac remodeling [ ]. However, no study has revealed the specific effect of SGK1 on AIM2. Here, we demonstrated that inhibiting SGK1 reduces the level of the AIM2 inflammasome. This is a crucial mechanism by which SGK1 is associated with neuroinflammation and neural damage in SCI. Furthermore, specific studies have revealed that SGK1 inhibition exerts a critical effect on programmed cell death, including ferroptosis, necroptosis, and pyroptosis [ , , ]. It is well established that AIM2 acts as an essential activator that mediates pyroptosis progression [ ]. Consistent with these findings, we observed that SGK1 inhibition reduces the level of pyroptotic markers. These results provide evidence that SGK1 regulates neuroinflammation via AIM2-mediated pyroptosis and highlight the important role of AIM2 in mediating SGK1-induced neural injury in SCI progression.
Mitochondrial dysfunction is a common feature of CNS diseases, and oxidative stress and impeded mitophagy are the most prevalent and critical mechanisms in the pathological process. Mitophagy, the process by which mitochondria are degraded, is usually linked to the removal of excess ROS and DAMPs. This process protects cells from oxidative stress and genomic damage. Restoring mitophagy is considered the most effective way to maintain mitochondrial homeostasis and inhibit AIM2 [ , ]. Additionally, mitophagy has been shown to be activated by SGK1 in muscle cells, where it enhances PINK1 expression and alleviates symptoms of diabetic sarcopenia [ ]. Our results indicated that SCI mice have high levels of oxidative stress, mtDNA release, and mitophagy flux obstruction in their microglia. Meanwhile, SGK1 inhibition significantly improved these alterations, resulting in the inhibition of AIM2 and ultimately repressing pyroptosis and inflammation.
Building on prior studies that connected microglial mitochondrial dysregulation with AIM2 activation after SCI, our work identified SGK1-FoxO1-mitophagy as a distinct upstream regulatory module that converges on the mtDNA/AIM2 inflammatory response. Our study also highlighted the importance of transcriptional control of mitochondrial quality as a key point of intervention. FoxO1, a member of the Forkhead box class O protein family, is considered a critical transcription factor in regulating glucose and lipid metabolism, cell cycle arrest, oxidative stress, and the inflammatory response. It has been reported that FoxO1 positively regulates mitophagy via PINK1/Parkin activation [ , ]. Additionally, SGK1 can directly bind to FoxO1, mediating FoxO1 phosphorylation and suppressing its activity. This leads to FoxO1 being localized to the cytoplasm instead of the nucleus, causing it to lose its capability of mediating downstream modulation [ ]. These findings were also confirmed in the current study. Our results showed that inhibiting SGK1 reduces FoxO1 phosphorylation, promoting FoxO1 entry into the nucleus. There, it participates in mitophagy activation and is associated with inflammation in microglia.
Despite the comprehensive detection of the novel role of SGK1 in SCI, several inherent limitations must be acknowledged. First, SGK1 regulation is primarily achieved through pharmacological inhibition, which can lead to undesirable off-target effects. Consequently, employing microglia-specific gene knockout mice may yield more reliable results. Additionally, the current dosage and duration of pharmaceutical treatment may not be optimal, so further experimentation is necessary to determine an appropriate treatment timeframe and dosage before clinical application. Furthermore, the in vitro experiments in this study were primarily conducted using the BV2 microglial cell line. While BV2 cells are a convenient model for mechanistic studies, they cannot fully replicate the biological characteristics and functional heterogeneity of primary microglia in vivo. The SGK1-regulated inflammatory response depends on several mechanisms, including the SMAD2/3-TGFβ axis, the Foxp3-IL pathway, and the NLRP3 inflammasome. These mechanisms may be involved in SGK1-inhibition-mediated neuroprotection [ , , ]. Moreover, the in vitro experiments were primarily conducted using TBHP treatment due to the critical role of SGK1 in oxidative stress and mitochondria. Previous evidence has suggested that ROS-regulated SGK1 is associated with SIRT6 and TORC2 [ – ]. However, the upstream regulation of SGK1 in SCI remains unclear. Nevertheless, our study provides a more in-depth understanding of how SGK1 regulates mitophagy, AIM2, and pyroptosis after SCI.
In conclusion, our study revealed the neuroprotective effects of inhibiting SGK1 with GSK650394 in SCI mice. Specifically, SGK1 is mainly upregulated in microglia after SCI. SGK1 inhibition suppresses FoxO1 phosphorylation and promotes FoxO1 entry into the nucleus. FoxO1 enhances mitophagy activation, reduces DAMPs release, represses AIM2 activation, and promotes functional recovery after SCI. Therefore, this study suggests that SGK1 inhibition by GSK650394 could be a potential target for further pharmacological development of SCI.
