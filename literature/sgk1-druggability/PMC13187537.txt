## Abstract
Mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists (MRA) exert anti-atherosclerotic properties in vivo through their combined effects on various cells and tissues. We previously found a significant interaction between TNFα-induced inflammatory pathways and mineralocorticoid receptor signaling, particularly involving serum/glucocorticoid-regulated kinase 1 (SGK1), in vascular endothelial cells. This study further elucidated the specific roles of SGK1 in vascular endothelial function. In SGK1-knockdown human aortic endothelial cells (HAECs), TNFα-induced inflammation-related molecules (e.g., E-selectin, VCAM-1, PAI-1, and MCP-1) were significantly downregulated compared to controls. Monocyte adhesion was also significantly reduced in SGK1-knockdown human umbilical vein endothelial cells, regardless of TNFα stimulation. Additionally, in these SGK1-knockdown HAECs, there was a significant attenuation of the TNFα-induced reduction of both total and phosphorylated protein levels of endothelial nitric oxide synthase (eNOS), accompanied by increased phosphorylation of protein kinase B (AKT) and adenosine monophosphate-activated protein kinase (AMPK). The upregulation of eNOS caused by SGK1 knockdown occurred at the transcriptional level alongside an increase in Krüppel-like factor 2 (KLF2) mRNA. Importantly, KLF2 knockdown negated this effect. The increase in eNOS protein levels observed with SGK1 knockdown was not seen in HAECs with combined SGK1 and KLF2 knockdown. These findings suggest a notable role of SGK1 in promoting inflammation and inhibiting vasodilation through multiple mechanisms, propositioning SGK1 as a potential therapeutic target for protecting vascular endothelial function.
Keywords: SGK1, KLF2, Atherosclerosis, eNOS, Inflammation, Vascular endothelium

## Introduction
Metabolic syndrome is a major global health concern associated with a heightened risk of cardiovascular events, affecting more than one-quarter of the global population [ ]. Accordingly, early preventive interventions are essential for reducing disease burden. While lifestyle modifications, such as increased physical activity and dietary changes, are widely recognized as first-line strategies, sustaining these over time can be difficult [ ]. Therefore, alternative therapeutic approaches, including pharmacological interventions, are increasingly required.
Metabolic syndrome is characterized by the accumulation of excess visceral fat, often referred to as "ectopic fat" [ ]. This condition is strongly associated with chronic inflammation, which arises from a persistent imbalance between pro-inflammatory adipokines (e.g., TNFα and IL-1β) and anti-inflammatory adipokines (e.g., adiponectin) secreted by adipose tissue [ ]. For instance, leptin released from visceral fat stimulates CYP11B2 activity in the adrenal gland, leading to increased aldosterone secretion and the activation of the renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system. In addition, mineralocorticoid receptors (MR) activation in adipose tissue promotes adipocyte differentiation. In animal studies, inhibition of MR has been shown to reduce atherosclerosis without influencing blood pressure, highlighting the significant role of MR and inflammatory signals in the development of metabolic syndrome.
Various vascular cells, including endothelial cells, smooth muscle cells, monocytes, and macrophages, contribute to the onset and progression of atherosclerosis [ ], although the role of MR varies across these different types of cells [ ]. Thus, it is essential to investigate the relationship between MR signaling and inflammatory signaling in each type of vascular cell; such insights may facilitate the development of targeted therapies for atherosclerosis associated with metabolic syndrome. Our previous study on vascular endothelial cells revealed that stimulation with the pro-inflammatory cytokine TNFα induces a delayed expression of SGK1, a key molecule in MR signaling [ ]. We also found that esaxerenone, an MR antagonist, reduces TNFα-induced NF-κB activity. These findings suggest the presence of crosstalk between inflammatory signaling pathways activated by pro-inflammatory cytokines and MR signaling in vascular endothelial cells, thereby indicating the potential clinical benefits of MR antagonists.
SGK1, a downstream target of MR, plays an important role in regulating ion channel expression and is associated with renal tubular sodium reabsorption and elevated blood pressure [ ]. In humans, gain-of-function SGK1 variants—defined by the combined presence of single-nucleotide polymorphisms in intron 6 (rs1743966) and exon 8 (rs1057293)—have been linked to increased blood pressure [ ]. These same SGK1 variants are also associated with a higher risk of ischemic stroke, and this association appears to be partially independent of blood pressure [ ]. In addition, SGK1 has been shown to promote coagulation by increasing tissue factor expression, suggesting a possible role in endothelial dysfunction [ ]. A study by Borst et al. demonstrated that atherosclerotic lesions were less severe in apoE(−/−) SGK1(−/−) mice compared to apoE(−/−) SGK1(+/+) mice. However, their study primarily focused on the role of SGK1 in monocytes and macrophages, leaving its impact on endothelial function largely unexplored [ ]. The present study aims to further elucidate the role of SGK1 in vascular endothelial function using an in vitro cell culture model.

## Materials and methods
2.1.

## Materials
Recombinant human TNFα was purchased from ReliaTech GmbH (Wolfenbüttel, Germany) and FUJIFILM Wako Chemical Co. (Kanagawa, Japan). EMD638683, an SGK1 inhibitor, was purchased from Selleck Biotechnology Ltd (Kanagawa, Japan).

2.2.

## Cell culture
Human aortic endothelial cells (HAECs) and human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs) were purchased from Lonza (Walkersville, MD, US) and cultured in endothelial cell basal medium-2 (EBM-2; PromoCell GmbH, Heidelberg, Germany), supplemented with 2% fetal bovine serum and the provided growth factors along with Zell Shield (Minerva Biolabs, Berlin, Germany). The cultures were maintained under the condition of 5% CO2 and 37 °C. HAECs and HUVECs were used for experiments at the passage of 5-7 and 5-9, respectively. THP-1 cells were purchased from RIKEN BRC Cell Bank (Ibaraki, Japan) and maintained RPMI 1640 (Wako, Osaka, Japan) with 10% FCS and Zell Shield.

2.3.

## SGK1 and KLF2 knockdown with siRNA and treatment with TNFα and EMD638683, an SGK1 inhibitor
To knock down of SGK1 and KLF2 in HAECs, a mixture containing four types of siRNA (“M-003027-05-0005” and “M-006928-01-0005” for siRNA of SGK1 and KLF2, respectively; Horizon Discovery, Cambridge, UK) was used for transfection. The transfection reagent DharmaFECT4 (Horizon Discovery) was applied, and scrambled RNA served as the control. Two days after gene transfection, the cells were stimulated with TNFα.
For the experiments to measure mRNA levels, HAECs were treated with TNFα at a concentration of 10 pg/mL for 4 h. For the Western blot experiments, we used TNFα at the indicated concentration and duration. To inhibit SGK1 activity in HAECs, the cells were pretreated with 25 μM EMD638683 for 24 h prior to the TNFα treatment at the indicated concentration and duration.

2.4.

## Quantitative reverse transcription PCR (qRT-PCR)
Total RNA was extracted from HAECs using Sepasol-RNA I Super G (Nakalai tesque Inc.). An equal amount of total RNA from each sample was utilized for reverse transcription with the QuantiTect Reverse Transcription Kit (Qiagen, Venlo, Netherlands). The resulting cDNA was then employed for qRT-PCR using the SYBR Premix Ex TaqII (Takara Bio Inc., Shiga, Japan) and the specific primer set as previously described [ ] (Supplemental Table). The real-time qRT-PCR was performed on the CFX96 Real-Time PCR Detecting System (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, US). The mRNA expression levels of each gene were normalized to 18S rRNA or 36B4. The relative gene expression levels were calculated using the 2–(ΔΔCT) method.

2.5.

## Western blot analysis
Total protein was extracted from HAECs with RIPA buffer containing both phosphatase (PhosSTOP™; Roche, Basel, Switzerland) and protease inhibitors (cOmplete™ Protease Inhibitor Cocktail Tablets; Roche). The extracted protein solutions were combined with Laemmli buffer and subjected to sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE). Next, the proteins were transferred to nitrocellulose membranes (Bio-Rad). These membranes were incubated with primary antibodies against mouse anti-eNOS (1:2500 dilution; BD Biosciences (#610296), Franklin Lakes, NJ, US), mouse anti-phosphorylated eNOSSer1177 antibodies (1:2500 dilution; BD Biosciences (#612392)), rabbit anti-AKT (1:2500 dilution; Cell Signaling Technology (#9272), Danvers, MA, US), rabbit anti-phosphorylated AKTSer473 (1:1000 dilution; Cell Signaling Technology (#9271)), rabbit anti-AMPK (1:2500 dilution; Cell Signaling Technology (#2532)), rabbit anti-phosphorylated AMPKThr172 (1:1000 dilution; Cell Signaling Technology (#2535)), rabbit anti-FOXO1 (1:2500 dilution; Cell Signaling Technology (#2880)), rabbit anti-phosphorylated FOXO1Ser256 (1:1000 dilution; Cell Signaling Technology (#9461)), rabbit anti-SGK1 (1:2500 dilution; Cell Signaling Technology (#12103)), or rabbit anti-phosphorylated SGK1Ser422 antibodies (1:2000 dilution; abcam (#ab55281), Cambridge, UK). Then, incubation with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibody (1:10,000–20,000 dilution; Jackson ImmunoResearch, West Grove, PA, US) was conducted. For normalization, an HRP-conjugated mouse anti-β-actin antibody (1:50,000 dilution ProteinTech (#HRP-60008), Rosemont, IL, US) was used. We captured protein bands with a chemiluminescence detection system (LI-COR Biosciences, Lincoln, NE, US) for quantitative analysis, ensuring that signals remained within the linear dynamic range to avoid saturation. We quantified bands by densitometry in ImageJ software (NIH). We normalized target protein intensity to the corresponding loading control (β-actin). The data are derived from three independent experiments, except for D, which includes four experiments, to ensure statistical rigor and reproducibility.
 
2.6.

## Monocyte adhesion assay
SGK1-knockdown HUVECs were generated using siRNA and cultured on collagen-coated 6-well dish. After 48 h, HUVECs were incubated with 100 ng/mL of TNFα or vehicle for 24 h. THP-1 cells were labelled with 2′,7′-bis-(2-carboxyethyl)-5-(and-6)-carboxyfluorescein-acetoxymethyl ester derivative (BCECF-AM; Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) at 37 °C for 1 h. The fluorescence-labelled THP-1 cells and HUVECs were co-cultured at room temperature for 30 min with shaking. Attached THP-1 cells together with HUVECs were lysed in 0.1% SDS, and the fluorescence intensity was measured using microplate reader (SH-9000; Corona Electric Co., Ltd, Ibaraki, Japan) with the excitation and emission wavelength of 488 nm and 562 nm, respectively.

2.7.

## Statistical analyses
All data were statistically analyzed using JMP pro15 (SAS Institute Inc) from at least 3 independent experiments and are presented as mean ± SEM. Shapiro–Wilk's test was used to assess the normality distribution of the data. Statistical difference was calculated by a two-tailed, unpaired Student's t-test for two-group comparison, or one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) for comparisons of three or more groups followed by Dunnett post-hoc or Tukey post-hoc test. Statistical differences were considered as significant at p< 0.05, and were displayed as ∗ p< 0.05, ∗∗p< 0.01, or ∗∗∗p < 0.001.

3.

## Results
3.1.

## SGK1-knockdown HAECs demonstrated resistance to TNFα-induced inflammation
SGK1-knockdown HAECs were generated to investigate the role of SGK1 in vascular endothelial cells. SGK1 siRNA (siSGK1)-transfected HAECs showed significantly reduced levels of SGK1 mRNA, as confirmed by qRT-PCR ( A), and SGK1 protein was undetectable ( B). In HAECs transfected with scrambled RNA (Scr), TNFα increased both total SGK1 protein levels and phosphorylation at Ser422, consistent with previous findings [ ]. These effects were significantly attenuated in SGK1-knockdown HAECs ( B).
 Atherosclerosis is initiated by the activation of endothelial cells, triggered by inflammatory cytokines released from monocytes, macrophages, and other sources. Therefore, we evaluated the sensitivity of SGK1-knockdown HAECs to TNFα-induced inflammation ( C). In our previous studies, SGK1 overexpression led to NF-κB activation, as demonstrated by reporter assays in HepG2 cells [ ]. Consistent with this, SGK1-knockdown HAECs showed significantly lower expression of inflammation-related molecules (i.e., E-selectin, ICAM-1, VCAM-1, and MCP-1) compared with controls, and the TNFα-induced upregulation of these molecules was markedly suppressed. Consequently, SGK1-knockdown HAECs displayed resistance to TNFα-induced inflammation.
Expression of these adhesion molecules contributes to monocyte adhesion to vascular endothelial cells in atherosclerosis pathogenesis. Therefore, we compared monocyte adhesion between SGK1-knockdown endothelial cells and controls ( D). Even in the absence of TNFα stimulation, THP-1 cell adhesion to SGK1-knockdown HUVECs was significantly lower than that in control cells. Although TNFα stimulation increased the number of THP-1 cells attaching to HUVECs, the number of THP-1 cells adhering to SGK1-knockdown HUVECs remained significantly reduced compared to controls.

3.2.

## TNFα-induced reduction of eNOS expression is attenuated in SGK1-knockdown HAECs
Vascular endothelial dysfunction is closely associated with atherosclerosis, particularly due to impaired nitric oxide (NO) synthesis. We therefore explored the impact of SGK1 knockdown on eNOS levels. In control cells, TNFα decreased eNOS protein levels in a dose-dependent manner ( E). However, in SGK1-knockdown HAECs, basal eNOS protein levels were significantly higher than those in controls ( F). Although TNFα also downregulated eNOS protein levels in SGK1-knockdown HAECs, these levels remained significantly elevated compared with controls.

3.3.

## SGK1 inhibition replicates the effects of SGK1-knockdown in HAECs
Next, we investigated whether the SGK1 inhibitor EMD638683 could replicate the effects of siRNA-mediated SGK1 inhibition. Based on a previous study demonstrating the efficacy of EMD638683 in HAECs [ ], we treated cells with 25 μM EMD638683 for 24 h. Pretreatment with EMD638683 significantly reduced, or showed a tendency to reduce, TNFα-induced mRNA expression levels of various inflammation-related molecules compared to the control group ( G). Additionally, under TNFα stimulation, eNOS protein levels were increased in HAECs pretreated with EMD638683 relative to control group ( H).

3.4.

## Increased eNOS phosphorylation along with AKT and AMPK activation in SGK1-knockdown HAECs
In the vascular endothelium, eNOS activity is associated with phosphorylation at the Ser1177 site. In the SGK1-knockdown HAECs, there were not only increased total and phosphorylated eNOS protein levels, but also higher phosphorylation ratios (phospho/total) compared with control group ( A and B, and the ). Several kinases, including AMPK, AKT, and protein A, contribute to the phosphorylation of eNOS. In SGK1-knockdown HAECs, both total and phosphorylated levels of AKT and AMPK proteins were significantly elevated, even in the absence of TNFα stimulation; however, their phospho/total ratios remained similar. After TNFα treatment, the total and phosphorylated levels of AMPK were higher in SGK1-knockdown HAECs compared to controls. Although the total and phosphorylated levels of AKT did not differ significantly between the two groups in the presence of TNFα, phosphorylation of FOXO1—a surrogate marker of AKT activation—was significantly increased, indicating enhanced AKT activity.
 
3.5.

## KLF2 mediates the impact of SGK1 knockdown on eNOS
SGK1 knockdown resulted in increased total and phosphorylated eNOS protein levels. Given that KLF2 is a critical transcription regulator of eNOS, we investigated KLF2 expression following TNFα stimulation ( A). In HAECs, KLF2 mRNA levels decreased from 2 to 16 h post-TNFα stimulation. This decrease was inversely related to the delayed increase in SGK1 expression, as previously reported [ ], suggesting a potential regulatory relationship between SGK1 and KLF2. Indeed, in SGK1-knockdown HAECs, both KLF2 mRNA and eNOS mRNA levels were significantly elevated ( B). To further assess this relationship, we examined the effects of additional KLF2 knockdown on the increase in eNOS expression following SGK1 knockdown ( C). HAECs with combined SGK1 and KLF2 knockdown showed similarly reduced SGK1 levels, along with significantly lower KLF2 and eNOS mRNA levels compared with cells subjected to SGK1 knockdown alone. KLF2 protein levels could not be evaluated due to the unavailability of suitable antibodies for detecting endogenous KLF2. Nevertheless, eNOS protein levels were elevated in SGK1-knockdown HAECs, but this elevation was significantly reduced in SGK1/KLF2 double-knockdown HAECs, which also showed a significant reduction in phosphorylated eNOS protein levels ( D).

## Discussion
This study examined the impact of SGK1, a key molecule in the MR signaling pathway, on vascular endothelial function. SGK1 knockdown reduced the expression of various TNFα-induced inflammation-related molecules, as well as restored the TNFα-induced decrease in eNOS protein levels and its phosphorylation. This effect was accompanied by increased phosphorylation of AMPK and AKT. Moreover, SGK1 knockdown promoted transcriptional upregulation of eNOS through an increase in KLF2 mRNA expression. Collectively, these findings suggest that SGK1 inhibition improves endothelial dysfunction associated with excessive MR signaling and TNFα-induced inflammation.
Metabolic syndrome is strongly associated with the development of cardiovascular diseases, which arise from chronic low-grade inflammation that damages the vascular endothelium [ , ]. This chronic inflammation is driven by an imbalance between pro-inflammatory and anti-inflammatory cytokines secreted by adipose tissue [ ]. Additionally, MR activation is observed in individuals with metabolic syndrome [ ]. In healthy endothelial cells, MR activation promotes vasodilation; however, in damaged endothelial tissue, it can lead to endothelial dysfunction (reviewed in Ref. [ ]). Our previous study demonstrated that MR activation interacts with inflammatory cytokine signaling, creating a vicious cycle that exacerbates inflammatory responses [ ]. Furthermore, clinically available MR antagonists have been shown to disrupt this harmful interaction and mitigate its pro-inflammatory effects, suggesting their potential therapeutic benefit. Given that SGK1 is a key molecule in these signaling pathways, the present study investigated its role in the vascular endothelium as a potential target for future clinical applications.
MR signaling has both acute non-genomic and chronic genomic effects on eNOS activation and NO production in the vascular endothelium. Aldosterone increases NO production in healthy individuals [ , ], and enhances eNOS activity while stimulating NO synthesis in vascular endothelial cells in vitro by activating PI3K activity [ , , , ]. In contrast, in animal models of heart failure and hypertension, chronically elevated MR signaling results in decreased NO production and reduced endothelial eNOS expression due to oxidative stress [ , ]. Notably, eNOS phosphorylation is preserved in the endothelial cells of endothelial cell-specific MR knockout mice subjected to diet-induced obesity [ ]. However, the precise mechanisms underlying these processes remain unclear [ , , ]. The present study demonstrated that SGK1 knockdown reverses the TNFα-induced reduction in both eNOS protein levels and phosphorylation. These findings suggest that, under conditions of chronic inflammation, SGK1 inhibition exerts protective effects beyond the modulation of MR genomic signaling alone.
The activity of eNOS is strictly regulated through the phosphorylation of Ser1177, activated by the kinases AKT and AMPK [ , ]. The AKT-induced phosphorylation of eNOS is well-established and occurs via the PI3K/AKT pathway, activated by growth factors such as vascular endothelial growth factor and insulin-like growth factor 1 [ ]. AMPK can also directly phosphorylate Ser1177 in eNOS in an AKT-independent manner, specifically in response to various metabolic stressors [ ]. In this study, SGK1 knockdown increased the protein levels of both AKT and AMPK and enhanced their phosphorylation at Ser473 and Thr172, respectively. Since SGK1 and AKT both belong to the AGC kinase family (i.e., cAMP-dependent, cGMP-dependent, and protein kinase C), they share similar structures and regulatory mechanisms. Therefore, their protein expression and activity may be regulated through functional complementation, although their interaction remains unclear [ ]. Several studies have indicated an increase in AKT phosphorylation in models involving MR antagonists and tissue-specific MR knockout; however, the evidence regarding this is conflicting [ , ]. The connection between SGK1 and AKT, which operates downstream of MR signaling, has not been fully elucidated. Additionally, MR activation is known to contribute to metabolic dysregulation, which is associated with reduced AMPK activity. This relationship is especially critical in the context of obesity, insulin resistance, and related vascular complications [ , ]. Re-analysis of RNA sequencing data using different tissues, specifically the cervical cancer cell line ME180, supported the findings observed in the vascular endothelium pathway ([ ] and ). SGK1 knockdown increased mRNA levels of AMPK, AKT, and additional molecules associated with PI3K-AKT-mTOR signaling. Although it remains unclear whether the observed increases in AMPK and AKT mRNA levels are due to enhanced transcription or altered mRNA stability, these findings suggest that the elevated mRNA levels may contribute to increased AMPK and AKT protein expression through translation. In addition, SGK1 in the liver has been reported to inhibit AMPK phosphorylation and exacerbate insulin resistance [ ]. These observations imply that SGK1 modulates AMPK and AKT activity through multiple mechanisms. In vascular endothelial cells, SGK1 knockdown may upregulate the expression and activation of AKT and AMPK, thereby enhancing eNOS activity.
In SGK1-knockdown HAECs, eNOS showed not only increased phosphorylation but also increased expression levels. KLF2 plays a critical role in the transcriptional regulation of eNOS, and its activity is closely linked to its expression levels [ ]. The mechanisms regulating KLF2 expression in the vascular endothelium, particularly in response to blood flow and inflammation, have been extensively studied. Laminar blood flow activates various signaling pathways through mechanosensors that collectively increase KLF2 expression, including Src, mitogen‐activated protein kinase kinase kinase 2/3 (MEKK2/3), mitogen‐activated protein kinase kinase 5 (MEK5), extracellular signal regulated kinase 5 (ERK5), and myocyte enhancer factor 2 (MEF2) [ , ]. Conversely, turbulent blood flow enhances the binding of histone deacetylases 5 (HDAC5) to MEF2 in the nucleus, accompanied by the formation of a complex involving activated NF-κB, HDAC4/5, and MEF2, which leads to decreased KLF2 expression [ , ]. In our reporter assay using the KLF2 promoter, treatment with an SGK1 inhibitor increased the transcriptional activity of the KLF2 gene (data not shown). This finding suggests that the increase in KLF2 expression due to SGK1 inhibition is at least partially mediated by enhanced transcriptional activity of KLF2, which may have subsequently contributed to the increase in eNOS expression. Furthermore, as noted in this study, the reduction in eNOS protein levels due to TNFα is reported to be mediated by NF-κB-dependent mechanisms that destabilize eNOS mRNA and repress transcription of the eNOS promoter [ , ]. The decrease in NF-κB activity associated with SGK1 inhibition may also contribute to the increase in eNOS expression.
It has been reported that angiopoietin increases KLF2 expression by MEF2 activation through enhanced Tie2 and PI3K/AKT pathways [ ], and that elevated blood glucose levels suppress KLF2 transcriptional activity through increased nuclear accumulation of FOXO1 [ ]. In the present study, increased phosphorylation of AKT and FOXO1 was observed in SGK-knockdown HAECs, suggesting that enhanced AKT activity due to SGK1 knockdown promotes MEF2 activation by facilitating the nuclear export of phosphorylated FOXO1, ultimately leading to increased KLF2 transcription. AMPK activation in SGK1-knockdown HAECs may further increase KLF2 transcriptional activity by promoting nuclear export of HDAC5 via increased phosphorylation at Ser498, as has been observed with metformin, an AMPK activator [ ]. Yanni Xu et al. identified suberanilohydroxamic acid (SAHA), an HDAC5 inhibitor, as an enhancer of KLF2 expression and demonstrated that its administration reduced atherosclerotic lesions in apoE knockout mice in vivo [ ]. In addition to increasing eNOS expression, SAHA has been shown to suppress the expression of various adhesion molecules and reduce monocyte adhesion in vitro vascular endothelial cell models. These findings support the possibility that increased KLF2 expression resulting from SGK1 inhibition contributes to anti-inflammatory effects through multiple pathways.
This study highlights the significance of SGK1 in vascular endothelial cells, suggesting a role in the interaction between MR and TNFα signaling and in the regulation of endothelial functions, including vasodilation (via KLF2 and eNOS) and inflammation (via NF-κB). However, this study is subject to several limitations. First, endogenous KLF2 protein levels were not evaluated. Although KLF2 may be involved in the increased eNOS expression associated with SGK1 knockdown, no antibody was available to detect endogenous KLF2 protein. Nevertheless, the increases in KLF2 mRNA levels and KLF2 promoter activity that accompanied SGK1 knockdown provide supporting evidence for increased protein expression. Second, in vivo validation, such as studies using vascular endothelial cell-specific SGK1-knockout model, is required to confirm these findings. Future studies incorporating these approaches will further clarify the therapeutic potential of targeting SGK1 in atherosclerosis.
