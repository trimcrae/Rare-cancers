## Abstract
Cells die extensively each day under physiological and pathological conditions, yet dying cells and cell corpses are not merely passive endpoints. Increasing evidence shows that they actively shape their microenvironment during death-program execution, signal release or exposure, corpse processing, and responses of neighboring cells and phagocytes. We here summarize six major roles of dying cells and cell corpses: promoting tissue turnover and remodeling, supporting neighboring-cell survival, mediating intercellular communication, providing metabolic substrates and rewiring phagocyte metabolism, facilitating corpse recognition and clearance, and modulating immune responses. Overall, they provide a broader framework for understanding cell death and identifying therapeutic opportunities.
Subject terms: Cell death, Cell signalling

This Review summarizes how dying cells and cell corpses actively shape tissue remodeling, neighboring-cell survival, intercellular communication, phagocyte metabolism, corpse clearance and immune responses in physiology and disease.

## Introduction
From the perspective of the history of biological research, there has consistently been a tendency to focus on the role of living cells, as well as the interactions and communications among them, while dying cells and cell corpses are often regarded as “silent” or “useless.” However, a series of recent pioneering studies on cell death and efferocytosis—the process by which phagocytes remove cell corpses—have gradually uncovered the secrets of dying cells and cell corpses , . Conceptually, the process of cell death can be divided into two broad stages: (1) initiation and execution of a death program, during which the cell is still metabolically active but irreversibly committed to die, and (2) a post-mortem phase in which the cell corpse and its fragments are processed and cleared. In this review, we use “dying cells” to denote cells in the execution phase of a defined death pathway, whereas “cell corpses” refers to post-mortem cellular remains. When the death modality or physical state is known, we use more specific terms, such as apoptotic cells, pyroptotic cells, necroptotic cells, or apoptotic bodies.
Cell death processes can be classified into two main categories: regulated cell death (RCD) and accidental cell death (ACD) . Generally, ACD is characterized as an uncontrolled biological process, while RCD involves a tightly regulated signaling cascade along with defined molecular effector mechanisms . RCD encompasses various forms, including apoptosis, pyroptosis, necroptosis, autophagy-dependent cell death, ferroptosis, and cuproptosis – . Although these modalities share some upstream triggers, they differ markedly in morphology, molecular machinery and immunological outcomes. For the benefit of non-specialist readers, we briefly summarize the hallmark features and core regulators of the main RCD modalities in Box  and Fig.  . Studies on cell dynamics have demonstrated that upon the initiation of various cell death programs, the substances actively released by dying cells during the execution phase, as well as the rate of this release, vary significantly. This variation influences the impact of cell death on surrounding cells and ultimately determines distinct biological effects , . For instance, the key protein GSDMD can be cleaved into GSDMD-N and GSDMD-C fragments by Caspase-1/4/5/11 during pyroptosis. GSDMD-NT binds to the intracellular leaflet and forms pores with diameters ranging from 10 to 33 nm. This pore formation mediates the rapid and non-selective release of cytoplasmic contents into the extracellular space, potentially triggering a robust immune response as demonstrated in mouse macrophages and human monocytic THP-1 cells . In contrast, following the initiation of the apoptosis program, the membrane of dying cells consistently maintains stability while undergoing shrinkage and invagination. This process leads to the division and encapsulation of the cytoplasm, resulting in the formation of apoptotic bodies that are subsequently recognized and phagocytosed by surrounding cells and phagocytes . However, the role of dying cells, resulting from various forms of cell death, is highly complex, as these cells can alter their original fate through negative feedback regulation. For instance, in mammalian cells, although necroptosis is regarded as an “immunogenic” form of cell death, the activity of Complex III (ESCRT-III) during this process can be activated by mixed-lineage kinase domain-like protein (MLKL). This activation aids in preserving plasma membrane integrity and postpones cell membrane permeabilization, thereby delaying the release of inflammatory cytokines , .
 Nearly 100 billion cells undergo apoptosis in the human body each day , with caspase-dependent apoptosis accounting for approximately 90% of homeostatic cell turnover . The high-efficiency clearance of apoptotic cells holds significant physiological importance; excessive accumulation of “cell corpses” can result in defects in tissue turnover and ultimately contribute to the development of related diseases , . Over the past decade, research has demonstrated that the clearance of cell corpses is intricately linked to their active regulation. Additionally, the mechanisms by which multiple dying cells enhance phagocytosis by macrophages and neighboring cells have been progressively elucidated. The phagocytosis of cell corpses by macrophages encompasses four primary processes: recruitment, binding, internalization, and degradation . During recruitment, dying cells release soluble “find-me” signals to attract phagocytes. In contrast, “eat-me” signals such as phosphatidylserine are exposed on the cell surface as a consequence of the intrinsic death program . In mammalian macrophages, the phagocytosis of cell corpses can produce derivative metabolites, including glucose, nucleic acids, amino acids, and cholesterol, etc . These metabolites can be utilized by phagocytic cells to generate energy, thereby sustaining high-intensity phagocytosis. Additionally, apoptotic cells may release Panx1-dependent metabolites that influence macrophage gene expression and facilitate the polarization of macrophages toward the M2 phenotype, which in turn enhances cell clearance .
This review aims to summarize the functions of dying cells and cell corpses under both pathological and physiological conditions. It highlights their roles in promoting cellular turnover, protecting neighboring cells, facilitating information exchange between cells, supplying fuel for sustaining phagocytosis, clearing cell corpses, and modulating the immune response within disease microenvironments. We primarily focus on the regulatory roles of cell death in pathological and physiological contexts.
To provide a clearer conceptual framework, we organize this review around three interconnected levels: (i) execution-phase events in dying cells, (ii) signals generated, released, or exposed by dying cells and cell corpses, and (iii) the responses of neighboring cells, phagocytes, and tissues. Within this framework, we summarize six major functional consequences, including epithelial turnover and tissue remodeling, survival-promoting signaling in neighboring cells, intercellular communication, metabolic rewiring, corpse clearance, and immunomodulation. This structure is intended to distinguish the intrinsic cell death program from death-associated signaling and recipient-cell responses, while preserving the biological continuity among these processes.
Box 1 Major forms of regulated cell death and their hallmark featuresApoptosis Apoptosis is a caspase-dependent, non-lytic form of cell death generally considered immunologically silent. Morphologically, it exhibits cell shrinkage, chromatin condensation, nuclear fragmentation, and membrane blebbing that forms apoptotic bodies. Key markers include the activation of executioner Caspases-3/-7 and regulation by BCL-2 family proteins .
Pyroptosis Pyroptosis is an inflammatory, lytic cell death triggered by infection or danger signals. It is characterized by rapid plasma-membrane permeabilization, cellular swelling, and the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines. Hallmark features include the cleavage of Gasdermin D (GSDMD) by inflammatory Caspases (1/4/5/11) and the release of IL-1β/IL-18 .
Necroptosis Necroptosis is a programmed, caspase-independent necrotic death. It results in cell swelling, membrane rupture, and the robust release of damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs). Key regulators include the RIPK1/RIPK3 necrosome complex and the phosphorylation/oligomerization of MLKL .
Ferroptosis Ferroptosis is an iron-dependent regulated necrosis driven by lethal lipid peroxidation. Morphologically distinct, it features shrunken mitochondria with increased membrane density and loss of cristae. Core markers include SLC7A11 (System Xc⁻), GPX4, and lipid ROS accumulation .
Cuproptosis Cuproptosis is a copper-dependent death triggered by copper binding to lipoylated TCA cycle enzymes, causing proteotoxic stress. Key markers include the aggregation of lipoylated proteins (e.g., DLAT), loss of Fe-S cluster proteins, and regulation by FDX1 and copper transporters .

Mitoxyperilysis

Mitoxyperilysis is an immunogenic death driven by innate immune activation and metabolic stress. It involves the translocation of damaged mitochondria to the plasma membrane periphery. Markers include excessive mitochondrial ROS and membrane lipid peroxidation, independent of Gasdermins or MLKL .

Disulfidptosis

Disulfidptosis is triggered by glucose starvation in SLC7A11-high cells due to disulfide accumulation from NADPH depletion. It specifically targets the actin cytoskeleton, causing actin filament collapse. Key markers include high SLC7A11 expression and aberrant disulfide bonding .

Erebosis

Erebosis is a physiological, non-inflammatory turnover mechanism (e.g., in gut epithelium). It involves the progressive loss of the cytoskeleton, adhesion molecules, and organelles (mitochondria/nuclei) without canonical caspase activation. It represents an enzyme-driven degradation process .

Alkaliptosis

Alkaliptosis is driven by intracellular alkalinization, typically due to CA9 downregulation. The pH rise leads to DNA damage, metabolic dysfunction, and membrane lysis. Key markers include CA9 downregulation and elevated intracellular pH .

## Roles of dying cells in healthy physiology and diseases
The biological effects attributed to dying cells are largely mediated by signals and intracellular components released during the execution phase or after membrane permeabilization. As a releaser, the substances released by dying cells are largely determined by the type of cell and the specific process of cell death. Specifically, the process of cell death is contingent upon the type of programmed death that is initiated, as well as the associated feedback regulation mechanisms. This process is crucial for the formation of channels on the cell membrane surface and can even lead to the rupture of the plasma membrane during cell death – . Various types of cells, including neutrophils, stem cells, and tumor cells, can produce distinct effects – . As a receiver, the impact on other living cells primarily depends on their response to specific substances and the nature of the cells involved. In particular, the response of cells to these substances is closely linked to factors such as the type, distance, route, and quantity of the substances. For example, five major metabolic derivatives derived from cell corpses, including cholesterol, provide energy for macrophages. Small molecules released by dying cells typically diffuse freely to adjacent cells, where they bind to surface receptors, thereby enhancing the apoptosis resistance or proliferative activity of neighboring cells. Furthermore, apoptotic bodies released by apoptotic cells can function nearby or reach distant sites with blood circulation , . When epithelial cells undergo apoptosis, cell corpses are extruded to maintain a quantitative balance and protect the integrity of the epithelial barrier. Notably, macrophages are a prime example of cells that are particularly responsive to signals due to their functional roles. In addition, epithelial cells, tumor cells, dendritic cells, fibroblasts, and other cell types can also receive signals. The variety of substances released by dying cells, including cytokines and apoptotic bodies, contributes to the distinct roles of different cell types. Subsequently, the functions of dying cells are categorized into six types, which will be described in detail.

## Roles of apoptotic cells in epithelial turnover and tissue remodeling
Cellular turnover is particularly pronounced in epithelial tissues, where cell loss and cell replacement are tightly coupled. In this context, regulated apoptosis and subsequent extrusion of apoptotic cells contribute to maintaining epithelial cell numbers and barrier integrity. Following caspase activation, apoptotic cells undergo ‘cell extrusion’, a process in which they are mechanically expelled from the epithelium while neighboring cells rearrange to close the gap shown in zebrafish epithelium . Caspase activation also drives morphological changes in apoptotic cells through the regulation of E-cadherin cleavage and microtubule degradation. These processes facilitate the disintegration of junctions between apoptotic cells and their neighboring cells, while also promoting the apical contraction of the apoptotic cells . As shown in Fig.  , caspase signaling from apoptotic cells promotes the release of biologically active lipids, specifically sphingosine-1-phosphate (S1P), which activates S1P2 receptors (in Madin-Darby canine kidney (MDCK) epithelial cells) . This activation enhances the organization of actin and microtubules in surrounding cells through ROCK-mediated targeting of p115 RhoGEF, directing their aggregation toward the boundaries of the apoptotic cells (demonstrated in MDCK cells, human bronchial epithelial cells, and the epidermis of live zebrafish) – . Consequently, apoptotic cells form actin/myosin rings. Ultimately, these actin/myosin rings contract at the interface between the apoptotic cells and neighboring cells, leading to the extrusion of apoptotic cells studied in MDCK canine kidney epithelial cells . This process can facilitate the shedding of infected cells during bacterial invasion, exemplified by the removal of intestinal epithelial cells infected by Salmonella .
 Gudipaty et al. demonstrated in MDCK epithelial cells that the mechanosensitive ion channel Piezo1 exerts dual functions under distinct mechanical contexts . On the one hand, under conditions of excessive cellular crowding, Piezo1 senses compressive stress and becomes activated, thereby triggering cell extrusion to reduce epithelial density. On the other hand, as cells are extruded, the local mechanical environment undergoes a reversal from compression to tension (stretch). In response to this shift, neighboring cells engage a series of well-characterized feedback mechanisms, including transient ERK activation , and YAP activation occurring in cells adjacent to apoptotic or extruding cells . In contrast, although Piezo1 can also sense the resulting tissue stretch at this stage and promote compensatory proliferation through upregulation of cyclin B expression , its specific activation in the immediate neighbors of extruding cells has not been formally demonstrated. Therefore, we distinguish Piezo1-dependent, tension-based proliferative responses from the locally observed ERK and YAP feedback mechanisms that have been directly documented.
In the Drosophila midgut, apoptosis of individual enterocytes can activate EGFR signaling in nearby stem cells within an ~25 μm radius (‘activation zone’), providing a local feedback mechanism linking cell loss to stem cell division. When the number of cells is insufficient, additional stem cells may enter the activation zone, thereby inducing further divisions , . In addition, in human HCC samples, EGFR-dependent hepatic macrophage activation mediates tumor cell regeneration following hepatocyte death . Caspases in apoptotic cells facilitate tissue renewal, regenerate damaged structures, and promote compensatory proliferation of lost tissue, a process referred to as apoptosis-induced proliferation (AiP) . Apoptotic cells also release mitogens (wg/wnt, egf/spi, etc.), which can promote the activation of pro-proliferative signaling pathways such as Wnt, EGFR, and MAPK in adjacent cells, and inhibit anti-proliferative signaling pathways such as Hippo , . It has been observed that cells adjacent to apoptotic cells can display non-autonomous JNK-dependent positive expansion. This phenomenon results in increased caspase activity and may further activate a broader AiP response, thereby promoting cell turnover , . In the oxidative stress environment of ulcerative colitis, the JNK signaling pathway plays a proliferative role . The AiP mechanism also plays a role in the regeneration of non-epithelial tissues. Apoptotic hepatocytes promote the expression of PGE2 and Hedgehog, which contribute to liver regeneration. This process is associated with the AiP mechanism, in which effector caspases-3 and caspases-7 are implicated , . AiP may have detrimental effects in cancer. Research has demonstrated that the activation of caspases in tumor cells enhances VEGF-A production, which ultimately facilitates tumor angiogenesis .

## Apoptosis-associated signaling promotes the survival of neighboring cells
Recent studies have increasingly demonstrated that apoptotic cells can release small molecules, such as cytokines, during the process of cell death. These molecules typically diffuse freely to neighboring cells and bind to their surface receptors. Ultimately, the apoptotic resistance of surrounding cells can be enhanced, or the proliferative activity of surrounding cells can be activated to form resistance to adverse factors.
ERK signaling has anti-apoptotic functions – . Recent studies have shown that apoptotic epithelial cells trigger transient ERK activity waves in neighboring cells, rather than in the dying cells themselves. This transient ERK activation inhibits caspase activation in adjacent cells and thereby protects surrounding cells from elimination , . In addition to ERK pulses, synchronized Akt pulses have also been observed and may help maintain epithelial homeostasis , , . Under conditions of high epithelial turnover, this apoptosis-induced ERK signaling may be particularly important for limiting excessive cell loss and preserving epithelial barrier integrity – .
Similar protective responses have also been described in Drosophila. In wing imaginal discs, cells surviving apoptotic insults acquire transient resistance to subsequent cell death, a phenomenon termed the “Mahakali effect” . Notably, the Mahakali effect can propagate over distances of approximately 100 µm or more , . The pro-apoptotic protein Hid plays a critical role in the regulation of this phenomenon. Activated ERK signaling can directly phosphorylate Hid, inhibiting its pro-apoptotic function and thus contributing to the survival of neighboring cells during the Mahakali effect , . This ERK-mediated regulation of Hid underscores the importance of survival signaling in protecting cells from apoptosis under stress conditions.

## Apoptotic bodies mediate intercellular communication
Another message from dying cells is mediated by apoptotic bodies (ApoBDs). During apoptosis, cells undergo distinct morphological changes that ultimately result in the fragmentation of apoptotic cells into smaller components known as ApoBDs . The formation and function of ApoBDs are briefly summarized in Fig.  . ApoBDs transport proteins, DNA, RNA, and other molecules to regulate immunity, genetically reprogram receptor cells and facilitate tissue repair. They play a significant role in tumors, cardiovascular diseases, autoimmune disorders, and various other conditions. Unlike apoptotic cells, which produce signaling molecules to communicate with surrounding cells, apoptotic bodies can participate in the circulatory system to regulate distant cells in mouse models , , .
 Apoptotic cells can produce three types of extracellular vesicles: apoptotic bodies (ApoBDs), microvesicles (MVs), and exosomes . Unlike exosomes and microvesicles, ApoBDs are exclusively found in apoptotic cells. ApoBDs are larger in size, ranging from 50 to 5000 nm, and contain internal organelles. They are formed after the apoptotic cell breakdown process, following apoptotic membrane blebbing and the formation of membrane protrusions . ApoBDs are subsequently phagocytosed by macrophages and fused with lysosomes within these cells. In comparison to other extracellular vesicles, understanding of ApoBDs remains limited; however, the rapid expansion of research on ApoBDs over the past five years has begun to elucidate their unique physiological roles. The role of ApoBDs can promote cell clearance and carry functional biomolecules, thereby contributing to information transmission and immune regulation. ApoBDs facilitate the clearance of cell corpses by emitting “eat me” signals and enhancing phagocytic activity . Additionally, in apoptotic human monocytes, membrane blebbing and protrusion formation aid in the distribution of nuclear material to ApoBDs , . When recipient cells engulf ApoBDs, their contents—including proteins, lipids, DNA, and microRNAs—are released. These molecules can initiate downstream biological responses or signaling pathways in recipient cells, facilitating various forms of intercellular communication under both physiological and pathological conditions.
ApoBDs facilitate the transfer of proteins to professional phagocytes, including macrophages and dendritic cells, which play a crucial role in regulating immune responses. For instance, thymocyte-derived ApoBDs contain a variety of nuclear proteins and heat shock protein 90 (HSP90), which can significantly influence the potential for T cell maturation within the thymus using mouse thymocytes . Likewise, ApoBDs produced by apoptotic lymphoblasts contain nuclear proteins, including histones 1, 2 A, 2B, 3, and 4, as well as the ribonucleoprotein La/SSB. These immunogenic molecules are ingested by phagocytes along with apoptotic bodies, thereby playing a role in the regulation of immune response . ApoBDs facilitate the transport of genomic DNA to recipient cells, thereby enabling the epigenetic reprogramming of these cells. For example, DNA in lymphoma cell-derived ApoBDs is phagocytosed by surrounding fibroblasts, which results in the integration of lymphoma-derived DNA into the fibroblast genome . In addition, microRNAs carried by ApoBDs are transferred to recipient endothelial cells, leading to the induction of CXCL12 expression, which subsequently facilitates tissue repair. MicroRNA-126 is enriched in ApoBDs and inhibits the regulator of G protein signaling 16, thereby allowing CXCR4 to initiate an autoregulatory feedback loop that enhances the expression of CXCL12. In a mouse model of atherosclerosis, repeated administration of ApoBDs containing microRNA-126 resulted in atheroprotective effects, potentially mediated by the induction of CXCL12 expression in the luminal cells of aortic root plaques and the recruitment of endothelial progenitor cells .

## Metabolic functions of cell corpses and corpse clearance
After cell corpses are phagocytosed by macrophages, macrophage cytoskeletal rearrangement and phagocytosis of apoptotic bodies are highly energy-intensive processes . As shown in Fig.  , once the material from cell corpses is processed by macrophages, it can be converted into metabolic derivatives. These metabolite derivatives serve as a crucial energy source for the sustained activity of macrophages. Additionally, these metabolic derivatives can produce other small metabolic molecules throughout the metabolic process. Some of these substances function as signaling molecules that regulate gene expression in macrophages, while others can be secreted by the cells to influence neighboring cells . The continuous intake of cell corpses, combined with the high metabolic activity of macrophages, contributes to the accumulation of substances such as cholesterol and leads to an excessive buildup of toxic compounds, including reactive oxygen species (ROS), within the cells , . Small molecule metabolites produced by various metabolic pathways may also be effective in mitigating adverse conditions. In conclusion, metabolism serves as a critical link between ‘life’ and ‘death,’ closely connecting the roles of cell corpses and the functions of macrophages. Below, we describe the metabolic processes and biochemical effects of five major metabolic derivatives derived from cell corpses (cholesterol, fatty acids, amino acids, glucose, and nucleotides) on macrophages.
 

## Cholesterol
Macrophages encounter significant amounts of cholesterol after phagocytosing cell corpses, making the rate of cholesterol processing a crucial determinant of their phagocytic efficiency. Excess cholesterol is processed within the lysosome and subsequently esterified for storage, leading to the formation of cytotoxic foam cells in macrophages. Lysosomal acid lipase (LAL) is a distinctive acid hydrolase responsible for the degradation of fatty substances within the lysosomal compartment of cells. Lipid droplets are specific intracellular structures that store cholesteryl esters. In a mouse model, LAL catalyzes the hydrolysis of cholesteryl esters found in lipid droplets that have fused with lysosomes, thereby releasing energy that can be utilized by macrophages , . LAL hydrolyzed lipid droplets fused with cholesteryl esters in lysosomes can also support the efflux of free cholesterol to stimulate endocytosis and promote continuous corpse uptake. Lack of LAL can lead to Wolman disease (WD) and cholesteric ester storage disease (CESD), with clinical manifestations including hepatosplenomegaly and adrenal calcification based on studies in LAL-deficient mice and human patients . Another study demonstrated that peroxisome proliferator-activated receptors (PPARs) and liver X receptors (LXRs), which are involved in cellular lipid homeostasis, can bind to ligands such as fatty acids and oxysterols in conjunction with retinoids. Ultimately, PPARs and LXRs enhance macrophage phagocytosis by increasing the expression of ABCA1 to promote cholesterol efflux, while also upregulating the transcription of phagocytic receptors (e.g., Mer and CD36) and opsonins (e.g., C1qb and MFG-E8) , .

## Fatty acids
β-fatty acid oxidation is the primary mechanism for the decomposition of fatty acids and the production of energy. This process generates a substantial amount of ATP, which serves as a critical energy source for the body. M1 macrophages predominantly depend on aerobic glycolysis, while M2 macrophages primarily utilize β-fatty acid oxidation to enhance mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) . The examination of the metabolome of activated macrophages during exocytosis revealed that the elevation of the anti-inflammatory cytokine IL-10 was not associated with glycolysis but was linked to β-oxidation in necroptotic cells. The results of this study demonstrate that the phagocytosis of necroptotic cells by macrophages leads to an increase in cellular fatty acids, which promote mitochondrial respiration and activate NAD+-dependent signaling cascades, thereby exerting anti-inflammatory effects in wound healing and organ repair following myocardial infarction . Another study showed that uptake of triacylglycerol substrates by CD36 and subsequent intracellular lipolysis by LAL promoted M2-type macrophages via interleukin 4 receptor alpha (IL-4Rα)-driven alternative activation . In addition, a study on atherosclerosis found that large amounts of thromboxane released by necroptotic cells impede the rapid clearance of these cell corpses by disrupting oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) in macrophages. In the mouse atherosclerosis model, the pro-catabolic mediator Resolvin D1 (RvD1) enhances AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK) signaling, which promotes fatty acid oxidation and OXPHOS, thereby enhancing the pro-catalytic phenotype of macrophages and facilitating the clearance of necrotic cells .

## Amino acids
Macrophages can uptake and utilize amino acids derived from cell corpses, primarily consisting of glutamine, arginine, and ornithine. Glutaminase 1 (GLS1)-mediated glutaminolysis, facilitated by non-classical glutamine transamination, supplies energy to these macrophages . Interleukin-4 (IL-4)-induced reparative macrophages are a subtype of macrophages that polarize under the induction of IL-4 signaling, characterized by their anti-inflammatory and tissue-repair functions. IL-4-induced reparative macrophages utilize GLS1-mediated glutaminolysis and glutamate transamination to channel glutamate into the malate-aspartate shuttle, thereby facilitating OXPHOS . This mechanism fulfills the elevated energy requirements associated with cytoskeletal rearrangement during phagocytosis and serves to complement the TCA. In addition to providing energy, glutamine is catalyzed by alanine aminotransferase or glutamate dehydrogenase to produce α-ketoglutarate. αKG promotes alternative activation of M2 macrophages through histone demethylation JMJD3-dependent metabolism and epigenetic reprogramming . The induction of macrophage phenotype repolarization from M1 to M2 may be anticipated to prevent obesity or type 2 diabetes . Additionally, glutamine has been demonstrated to serve as a precursor for the synthesis of NADPH and the antioxidant glutathione (GSH), which is employed to scavenge ROS generated by highly oxidatively efficient macrophages. Consequently, macrophages can also utilize glutamine to detoxify the elevated levels of ROS produced by their enforced oxidative metabolism, thereby maintaining high ATP production for actin remodeling and overall efficiency . Arginine and ornithine, derived from cell corpses, can be metabolized into putrescine by macrophage arginase 1 (Arg1) and ornithine decarboxylase (ODC). Putrescine enhances the HuR-mediated stabilization of the mRNA encoding the GTP exchange factor Dbl and activates the actin-regulating protein Rac1, thereby promoting subsequent rounds of apoptotic cell internalization and enabling continued phagocytosis .

## Glucose uptake in the process of macrophage efferocytosis
During efferocytosis, macrophages increase glucose uptake and rely on aerobic glycolysis to support cytoskeletal remodeling and continuous corpse uptake, as evidenced by enhanced glucose consumption from the extracellular medium and elevated lactate release . Using models of apoptotic-cell clearance, Morioka et al. identified a solute carrier (SLC) gene program that is specifically induced in phagocytes during efferocytosis. Within this program, SLC2A1 (GLUT1) mediates increased glucose uptake and drives a shift toward aerobic glycolysis, whereas oxidative phosphorylation is relatively down-regulated. Importantly, phosphatidylserine exposed on apoptotic cells is sufficient to trigger this metabolic switch, indicating that “eat-me” signals from dying cells reprogram macrophage metabolism, even though the main source of glucose is the extracellular milieu rather than the apoptotic cells themselves. Other SLC transporters, such as SLC16A1, are also upregulated and promote lactate export, which in turn contributes to M2-like polarization and the establishment of an anti-inflammatory microenvironment that favors efficient clearance of cell corpses .

## Nucleotides
Recent studies have shown that cell corpse-derived nucleotides can promote macrophage proliferation . Brennan et al. propose that nucleotides derived from phagolysosomal DNase2a hydrolysis of DNA in apoptotic cells activate the DNA-dependent protein kinase DNA-PK. DNA-PK is composed of a catalytic subunit known as DNA-PKcs (gene name Prkdc) and a nucleotide-binding domain, which can induce Akt phosphorylation at serine 473 by activating the mTORC2 complex, thereby promoting macrophage proliferation. Additionally, this pathway promotes the proliferation of non-inflammatory macrophages by enhancing the expression of the pro-proliferative transcription factor Myc. In a model of atherosclerosis regression, the silencing of macrophage Rictor or DNase2a hinders efferocyte proliferation, impairs apoptotic cell clearance, and disrupts plaque stabilization .

## Dying cell-derived signals that promote cell-corpse clearance
The signals emitted by dying cells have been identified as promoting cellular clearance. Cell clearance relies on efferocytosis, which involves four stages: the discovery of cells, binding, internalization, and degradation. Initially, dying cells release a “find-me” signal via the Panx1 channel, which attracts macrophages to their vicinity. Subsequently, dying cells express a specific surface marker known as the “eat-me” signal (Table  ). Macrophages bind directly or indirectly to ‘eat-me’ signals via cell surface receptors, which activate the RHO family of small GTPases, such as RAC1. This activation subsequently triggers the actin-related protein 2/3 (ARP2/3) complex, leading to actin polymerization that forms a phagocytic cup and internalizes AC . Additionally, dying cells release specific metabolites through the Panx1 channel. These metabolites, along with “find-me” and “eat-me” signals, facilitate the clearance of cell corpses and avoid promoting the release of inflammatory factors and immune destruction (Fig.  ).
 

## Dying apoptotic cells release metabolites to facilitate cellular clearance
Dying apoptotic cells can selectively release certain soluble metabolites that influence the physiological functions of neighboring cells. Medina et al. identified five ‘conserved’ metabolites by comparing those enriched or released in apoptotic supernatants following Fas, UV, or toxin-mediated apoptosis in Jurkat cells, thymocytes, and macrophages using human Jurkat T cells, mouse thymocytes, and mouse bone marrow-derived macrophages. The five metabolites identified are AMP, GMP, creatine, spermidine, and glycerol 3-phosphate , . The sixth metabolite, ATP, was detected using luciferase. These six metabolites are found in normal cells and are selectively released to perform their functions when cells begin to die . The selective release of nucleotides and metabolites from apoptotic cells is closely associated with the Panx1 channel . Panx1 is a widely expressed homoheptameric membrane channel involved in various physiological and pathophysiological processes, including apoptotic cell clearance. During apoptosis, activation of the extrinsic pathway by death ligands such as FasL or TNF-α activates caspase-8 and subsequently the executioner caspases-3 and -7. Panx1 is a direct substrate of caspase-3/7; cleavage of its C-terminal tail removes its autoinhibitory domain and opens the Panx1 channel , . This caspase-dependent activation of Panx1 enables apoptotic cells to release ATP and UTP as “find-me” signals, which recruit phagocytes to the site of cell death. This process does not transport metabolites into macrophages through Panx1, but instead involves nucleotides and other soluble signals released from the dying cell that subsequently modulate macrophage responses. Panx1-dependent signaling can influence phagocytic gene expression related to anti-inflammatory macrophage functions, including glycosylation pathways (Uap1, Ugdh), IL-10 transcription, inflammation-resolution programs (Pbx1, Ptgs2), and metabolic regulation (Slc14a1, Sgk1). M2 macrophages facilitate inflammation resolution, engulf apoptotic cells, maintain tissue integrity, and release anti-inflammatory mediators . Collectively, this class of metabolites enhances gene expression that biases phagocytes towards an anti-inflammatory macrophage phenotype, promotes the differentiation of macrophages into M2-type macrophages, and facilitates cell corpse clearance , . Furthermore, the anti-inflammatory effects of these metabolite mixtures have been validated in mouse disease models of lung transplant rejection and inflammatory arthritis .

## Apoptotic cells release “find-me” signals to recruit phagocytes
When cells are dying, they release soluble signals into the surrounding environment to attract macrophages and enhance their clearance capacity. These signals are referred to as “find-me” signals and are released through the Panx1 channel . In mammalian cell phagocytosis, four primary “find-me” signals have been identified: nucleotide, CX3CL1, lysophosphatidylcholine (LPC), and S1P. The production of these signals is associated with the activity of caspases during the apoptotic process . The following sections describe the production and release of four types of substances by apoptotic cells. (1) Lauber et al. demonstrated that calcium-independent phospholipase A2 (iPLA2), upon cleavage by caspases, may be responsible for the release of LPC . Recent studies have indicated that the lipid transporter ABCA1 can facilitate the release of LPCs during apoptosis . (2) There is still controversy over the creation of S1P. Gude et al. suggested that apoptosis induction may elevate sphingosine kinase 1 (SphK1) expression. The resulting increase in SphK1 is linked to enhanced S1P generation, which may contribute to S1P-mediated macrophage recruitment toward supernatants derived from apoptotic cells . However, it has been demonstrated that S1P kinase 2 (SphK2) is a target of caspase 1 and that cleaved fragments of SphK2 can be released from apoptotic cells into the extracellular space, where they generate S1P . (3) CX3CL1 release during apoptosis has been reported to require caspase activity, although the shedding step is likely mediated indirectly by extracellular ADAM proteases rather than by direct caspase cleavage. Truman et al. further suggested that, in apoptotic Burkitt lymphoma cells, CX3CL1 can be discharged at an early stage in association with microparticles. Its receptor, CX3CR1, is thought to contribute importantly to chemokine sensing and to the migration of monocytes in both in vitro and in vivo settings . (4) After Elliott et al. induced apoptosis through various methods, including Fas cross-linking, UV treatment, and etoposide etc., nucleotide release was shown to occur in Jurkat cells, primary thymocytes, as well as MCF-7 cells, and lung epithelial cells, with this release being dependent on caspase activity . In addition, the ‘find-me’ signal is released through the Panx1 channel, interacting with receptors on phagocytes to promote M2 macrophage activation and facilitate the clearance of cell corpses. Among these signals, LPC binds to G2A receptors on macrophages. Tabea et al. demonstrated that G2A activation in macrophages initiates the MyD88-PI3K-AKT signaling pathway and leads to the transient release of MMP9, which triggers cytoskeletal remodeling and migration .

## Apoptotic cells expose “eat-me” signals to promote corpse uptake
Sphingosine 1-phosphate (S1P) binds to the S1PR1 receptor on macrophages, promoting the expression of hypoxia-inducible factor 1α (HIF-1α) in these cells. Erythropoietin (EPO) enhances the expression of peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor-γ (PPARγ) through the Jak2-ERK-C/EBPβ signaling cascade, facilitating the clearance of apoptotic cells by macrophages , . Furthermore, interference with EPO signaling has been shown to improve disease progression in mice with systemic lupus erythematosus .
In mammals, CX3CL1 signals through CX3CR1 on macrophages to support recruitment/chemotaxis, whereas milk fat globule epidermal growth factor (EGF) factor 8 (MFG-E8) acts as a bridging opsonin that links phosphatidylserine on apoptotic cells to αvβ3/αvβ5 integrins on phagocytes . Recently, Lu et al. found that MFG-E8 could reprogram macrophages to an anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype and promote phagocytosis of apoptotic cells , .
The ATP/UTP receptor P2Y2 serves as a crucial sensor for nucleotides released by apoptotic cells. Maria et al. discovered that the activation of P2Y2 can initiate ERK1/2 phosphorylation . Furthermore, the activation of the ERK pathway has been shown to facilitate the transformation of macrophages into M2 macrophages, thereby enhancing the clearance of apoptotic cells .

## Apoptotic cells expose “eat-me“ signals and boost cellular clearance
As macrophages encounter apoptotic cells, apoptotic cells expose specific surface cues that identify them for engulfment. These molecules function as phagocytic “eat-me” signals, among which PtdSer is regarded as the best-characterized and most prominent example. PtdSer in living cells is usually present in the inner lobule, and it is expressed outside the cell in a caspase-dependent manner during apoptosis . The surface of dying cells displays numerous membrane vesicles that expose phosphatidylserine (PtdSer). In the case where PtdSer fails in exposure or mistakenly occurs, it can lead to various immunological and hematological diseases, including Scott syndrome and Systemic lupus erythematosus . PtdSer is recognized through two primary mechanisms. First, it may be engaged directly by macrophage surface receptors such as CD300b, BAI1, TIM4, and Stabilin-2. Second, its recognition can be mediated indirectly by soluble bridging molecules, notably Gas6/Pros1 and MFG-E8. In this context, Gas6/Pros1 mainly signals through the TAM family receptors Mer and Axl, whereas MFG-E8 links apoptotic cells to αvβ3 and αvβ5 integrins on macrophages . When the “eat me” signal on the surface of apoptotic cells is recognized by receptors on the surface of surrounding macrophages, it activates Rac1, which promotes actin polymerization to form actin patches. This process leads to the formation and accumulation of phagocytic cups, ultimately facilitating the clearance of the cells , .

## Immunomodulatory effects of dying cells and cell corpses in the disease microenvironment
Dying cells themselves can also shine as a unique immune modulator in a variety of diseases. Within inflammatory and tumor microenvironments, these dying cells play a significant immunomodulatory role by interacting with immune cells, particularly dendritic cells and T cells, present in the surrounding environment – . Certain chemotherapeutic agents, tumor-lysing viruses, physicochemical treatments, photodynamic therapy, and radiation therapy trigger tumor cell death, which can cause dying cells to release DAMPs to drive adaptive immunity , . DAMPs can function as adjuvants or danger signals for the immune system and are recognized by innate pattern recognition receptors (PRRs) expressed on dendritic cells, which then initiate a cellular cascade . However, these DAMPs do not always initiate adaptive immune responses; they do so only when dying cells present new epitopes and exhibit enhanced antigenicity. Such neo-epitopes may be encoded by microbial genes or by host genes that undergo mutations during oncogenesis and tumor progression , . DAMPs manifest in various forms, including extracellular ATP, Annexin A1 (ANXA1), calreticulin (CALR), high mobility group box-1 (HMGB1), and type I interferons (IFN-I) . Specifically, in mouse tumor models, dying cells facilitate the activation of inflammasomes in dendritic cells through the release of P2RX7 purinergic signals , which is required to drive initial lymphocyte responses by IL-17-producing γδ T cells (Fig.  ) . In addition, the release of ANXA1 from dying cells promotes the homing of dendritic cells, and the exposed calreticulin CALR (CRT) on dying cells can bind to the dendritic cell LRP1 receptor to promote antigen uptake. The release of HMGB1 and the binding of the PRR receptor TLR4 on dendritic cells mediate a potent adjuvant-like effect and may also mediate potent pro-inflammatory effects to stimulate efficient processing and cross-presentation of antigens from dying cells . The cell death pathway also determines the immunomodulatory role of dying cells within the disease microenvironment. In dying necrotic and pyroptotic cells, the release of DAMPs and the subsequent activation of PRRs can promote the activation and maturation of dendritic cells, thereby activating antitumor immunity , . In dying ferroptotic cancer cells, DAMPs are released but fail to elicit antitumor immunity . Notably, dying cells from the classical apoptotic pathway can stimulate the production of immunosuppressive cytokines by macrophages, facilitate the proliferation of residual cancer tissue, and release growth factors and proliferation factors to protect other cancer cells from anticancer agent-induced apoptosis in mammalian systems , .
 

## Therapeutic targeting of cell death, dying cells, and cell corpses in disease
Currently, dying cells are primarily utilized in three strategies for disease treatment: blocking or promoting the generation and transmission of cell death signals; directly utilizing or simulating the signals produced by dying cells; and enhancing the responsiveness of affected cells to these signals. For instance, blocking cell death signals can be achieved by using neutralizing antibodies against NINJ1 to inhibit plasma membrane rupture and inflammation . Conversely, promoting immunogenic cell death signals with chemotherapeutic agents like oxaliplatin can enhance antitumor immunity . Direct utilization of apoptotic cell signals is exemplified by the administration of apoptotic bodies enriched with microRNA-126 to promote vascular protection and tissue repair in atherosclerosis models . Additionally, metabolites released from apoptotic cells have been shown to resolve inflammation in models of arthritis . Finally, enhancing cellular responsiveness is demonstrated by the use of CD47 blockade antibodies, which disrupt the “don’t eat me” signal and augment phagocytic clearance of tumor cells . These strategies demonstrate significant potential for application in the treatment of bacterial and viral infections, inflammation, and tumors.

## Inflammatory therapy
Inflammatory forms of regulated cell death, such as pyroptosis and necroptosis, and secondary necrosis of apoptotic cells, can initiate robust inflammatory responses by causing plasma-membrane rupture and release of pro-inflammatory cytosolic contents and DAMPs. In order to inhibit related inflammation, it is crucial to target and inhibit signaling pathways. NINJ1 is a cell surface protein that mediates plasma membrane rupture and releases proinflammatory cytoplasmic contents. Specific antibodies can obstruct the oligomerization of NINJ1, thereby effectively diminishing the inflammation caused by excessive cell death . In addition, the gasdermin protein family is crucial in the regulation of pyroptosis, and various inhibitors of gasdermin D (GSDMD), including necrosulfonamide (NSA) and disulfiram (DSF), have demonstrated potential therapeutic benefits across a range of diseases . Previous studies have demonstrated that RIPK3 and MLKL can initiate a robust inflammatory response, and inhibitors targeting these proteins present viable options for inflammatory treatment . In addition, monoclonal antibodies targeting TNF-α have been shown to mitigate the inflammatory response induced by cell corpses, thereby significantly modulating the disease process. The metabolites of cell corpses can promote the phagocytic function of macrophages, and the pharmacology of metabolites may become a new strategy to inhibit inflammation and alleviate chronic inflammatory diseases .

## Autoimmune disease treatment
Improper clearance of apoptotic cells can result in the accumulation of cellular debris and intrinsic antigens, which may trigger abnormal immune responses. Targeted therapies, including monoclonal antibodies, can specifically target B cells, thereby promoting the efficient clearance of cell corpses and reducing inflammation and autoimmune responses. Monoclonal antibodies targeting phosphatidylserine can help the immune system recognize and clear these cells, thereby reducing disease activity. Tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α) is a pleiotropic proinflammatory mediator in the pathophysiological process of systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE). Monoclonal antibodies targeting TNF-α can inhibit its pro-inflammatory effect in autoimmune diseases. B cell activating factor (BAFF), a member of the TNF superfamily, has been approved for the treatment of SLE through the use of antibodies against it . In addition, dysregulation of the JAK/STAT pathway is implicated in SLE, and JAK inhibitors have entered clinical trials .

## Tumor treatment
Therapeutic strategies can induce apoptosis, pyroptosis, or necroptosis in tumor cells, which have complex and dual effects in cancer treatment . Deletion of Gasdermin-E (GSDME) reduces tumor burden in some cancer models, and the expression level of RIPK3 or MLKL is closely related to the prognosis of patients with different cancers , . Enhancing the induction of cell death signals is a critical strategy for antitumor therapy. Various drugs inhibit cancer cell proliferation through distinct mechanisms of cell death. For example, metformin causes pyroptosis by inducing mitochondrial dysfunction , . In addition, emerging therapies such as nanoparticles (NPs) can penetrate the cell barrier, target and induce a variety of programmed cell death, and inhibit cancer cell proliferation , , . Small-molecule compounds can induce ferroptosis of tumor cells. Copper ionophores can promote cuproptosis in cancer cells . Additionally, some monoclonal antibodies, such as those targeting HER2, can also directly induce cancer cell apoptosis . Using apoptotic tumor cells to activate the immune system also presents a novel approach to cancer treatment. Moreover, modulation of the intrinsic apoptotic pathway via inhibition of anti-apoptotic BCL-2 family proteins has shown considerable promise. Venetoclax (ABT-199), a highly selective BCL-2 inhibitor, has been approved for certain hematologic malignancies and functions by restoring pro-apoptotic signaling. However, resistance to venetoclax frequently emerges, often driven by compensatory overexpression of other anti-apoptotic proteins such as MCL-1 or BCL-xL, as well as metabolic adaptations in cancer cells – .

## Anti-infectious therapy
Programmed cell death is not only important for maintaining homeostasis but also represents a key mechanism of innate defense. When pathogens invade, infected cells often undergo regulated forms of cell death, and the resulting apoptotic or pyroptotic corpses are rapidly engulfed by professional phagocytes. This efferocytic removal of pathogen-bearing cells helps to physically eliminate intracellular microbes and limits their spread. In parallel, danger signals and antigens released from dying cells can be sensed by dendritic cells to activate the immune system and promote T- and B-cell-mediated antimicrobial responses. Enhancing pyroptotic pathways in infected cells may therefore represent a promising strategy for anti-infective therapy . However, necroptosis can, in some contexts, exacerbate tissue damage and worsen disease progression , , underscoring the need for precise modulation of these cell-death pathways in future anti-infective research.

## Discussion
Despite rapid progress in the field, research on dying cells and cell corpses remains constrained by technological and methodological limitations. A major challenge lies in accurately distinguishing dying cells from fully formed cell corpses, as well as from transiently stressed but viable cells, particularly in complex tissue environments. The development of high-resolution in situ detection strategies, single-cell and spatial multi-omics approaches, and real-time lineage tracing technologies will be essential for precisely mapping the dynamic progression of cell death and corpse processing in vivo. Furthermore, the mechanistic diversity of regulated cell death modalities necessitates continued investigation. Distinct death programs differ not only in molecular execution but also in membrane integrity, metabolite release, DAMP exposure, and immunological consequences. Equally important is understanding how neighboring cells and professional phagocytes interpret and respond to these death-associated signals under different physiological and pathological contexts. Based on current knowledge, the biological functions of dying cells and cell corpses can be conceptualized at four interconnected levels. (1) At the single-cell level, functional outcomes are largely determined by the specific death modality engaged and the regulatory feedback mechanisms activated during execution. (2) At the multicellular and tissue levels, the cumulative burden of dying cells and the efficiency of their clearance critically influence tissue architecture, mechanical stability, and inflammatory tone. (3) At the local microenvironmental level, dying cells primarily exert non-autonomous effects on neighboring cells, and these outcomes depend on cell type, receptor expression, signal range, and species-specific regulatory networks. (4) At the organismal level, balanced cell death and efficient corpse clearance are indispensable for maintaining systemic homeostasis, whereas dysregulation contributes to inflammatory disorders, autoimmunity, degenerative diseases, and cancer. A deeper mechanistic understanding of how death signals are generated, propagated, and resolved will enable more precise therapeutic manipulation of cell death pathways. Rather than representing a passive endpoint, dying cells and cell corpses constitute dynamic signaling entities that actively shape tissue remodeling, immune regulation, and metabolic adaptation. By integrating evidence across model organisms and mammalian systems while preserving species-specific distinctions, this review aims to provide a conceptual framework for understanding the regulatory functions of cell death. Continued advances in this field may facilitate the development of therapeutic strategies that either enhance beneficial clearance and resolution programs or selectively modulate immunogenic and inflammatory death responses in refractory diseases.
