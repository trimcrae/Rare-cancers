Current therapies remain empirical, lacking mechanistic understanding of how cells transduce mechanical forces into pathological responses. The mechanosensitive ion channel Piezo1 has emerged as a critical mechanotransducer, yet exhibits a fundamental paradox: endothelial Piezo1 deletion worsens edema while excessive activation disrupts barriers and triggers inflammatory cell death. Conventional “protective versus injurious” frameworks cannot explain these opposing outcomes. We propose the “Context-Dependent Rheostat” model, wherein Piezo1 outcomes depend on mechanical dose, cellular context, and inflammatory milieu. This review synthesizes evidence demonstrating that Piezo1 responses exhibit quantitative inflection points rather than fixed directional effects, with temporal dynamics, cell-type-specific thresholds, and inflammatory priming determining functional transitions. Specifically, endothelial cells preserve barrier integrity within a wider strain tolerance window, whereas epithelial cells exhibit substantially narrower damage thresholds; acute stretch activates protective chromatin remodeling, while sustained ventilation drives ataxia telangiectasia and Rad3-related/checkpoint kinase 1 (ATR/Chk1)-mediated genomic collapse and cellular senescence. Beyond cell-autonomous responses, macrophages emerge as candidate signal-hub cells integrating mechanosensory inputs from endothelial, epithelial, and neutrophil compartments, representing a high-value therapeutic node. This framework fundamentally reframes VILI therapeutics from global Piezo1 modulation toward precision interventions: temporal stage-specific targeting, context-specific threshold resetting, and biomarker-guided stratification. Candidate strategies span nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2 (NRF2) activators for early-phase cytoprotection, Toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4) antagonism, and ATR/Chk1 inhibitors or senolytic combinations for late-phase intervention, guided by circulating DNA-damage marker γH2AX and senescence-associated secretory phenotype markers. We provide a translational roadmap enabling VILI therapy to transition from empirical ventilation adjustment toward mechanistically-guided precision medicine with actionable intervention points.

## Introduction
Mechanical ventilation is a critical life-support intervention, yet paradoxically contributes to ventilator-induced lung injury (VILI), affecting approximately 24% of mechanically ventilated patients and elevating mortality to 40–45% in acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) patients despite lung-protective ventilation strategies ( - ). VILI constitutes a critical iatrogenic determinant of ARDS onset and prognosis, with up to 23% of mechanically ventilated patients fulfilling ARDS diagnostic criteria ( , ). Although the “four-hit theory” (volutrauma, barotrauma, atelectrauma, and biotrauma) has guided lung-protective strategies, it remains descriptive rather than mechanistic, failing to answer: which molecular sensor decodes mechanical forces into cellular fate decisions? Two observations anchor this enigma. First, mechanical ventilation induces systemic DNA damage in humans, with γ-H2AX+ circulating leukocytes increasing 3-fold within 24 hours of high tidal volume ventilation ( ). Second, 18 cmH2O positive end-expiratory pressure (PEEP) increases lung injury scores 2-fold in lean mice but reduces them 40% in obese mice, demonstrating identical inputs produce opposite outcomes ( ). Traditional frameworks describe VILI’s multifaceted pathogenesis ( ) but cannot explain this context-dependency at the molecular level ( ). Unraveling this “mechanotransduction black box” is critical to advancing beyond empirical protocols.
 The mechanosensitive ion channel Piezo1 has emerged as a key decoder in VILI ( ). Stretch-induced conformational changes open its Ca2⁺-permeable pore, initiating downstream cascades ( - ). Mechanical force via Piezo1 directly activates the ataxia telangiectasia and rad3-related protein (ATR)-checkpoint kinase 1 (Chk1) DNA damage response pathway, suggesting that ATR functions as a broader “Mechanical Stress Response” pathway ( , ). Sustained activation drives VILI progression through genomic collapse ( - ), sterile inflammation ( , ), ferroptosis ( ), and neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs) ( ). Yet Piezo1 activation produces diametrically opposed outcomes. In endothelial cells, Piezo1 knockout paradoxically worsens VILI – increasing pulmonary edema 60% and protein leak 2-fold—proving that Piezo1 maintains barrier protection ( ). Conversely, pathological overstretch increases barrier permeability 2.5-fold ( ). Physiological activation induces protective chromatin remodeling (nuclear “softening” via H3K9me3 demethylation as a preemptive “airbag”) ( , ), while pathological activation triggers ATR-Chk1-mediated genomic collapse ( ). In macrophages, baseline activation suppresses interleukin-6 (IL-6) and tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α) by 50% ( ), yet combining activation with inflammatory priming (lipopolysaccharide, LPS) triggers ferroptotic death in cells. Same sensor, opposite outcomes—why?
The “Janus-faced protein” framework acknowledges Piezo1’s bidirectionality ( ) but cannot predict when protection flips to injury, explain why cell types exhibit different thresholds, or guide therapeutic timing. Protective chromatin remodeling (<4 h) versus pathological DNA damage (>24 h) suggests that VILI arises from decompensation of adaptive responses under excessive loads ( - ). What molecular determinants and mechanical thresholds govern Piezo1’s functional switch?
We propose that Piezo1 functions as a “Context-Dependent Rheostat” rather than a binary switch. Functional output depends on: (I) mechanical parameters (intensity, duration, frequency determining adaptive vs. exhausted responses); (II) cell type-specific signaling landscapes (endothelial cells favor barrier stabilization; epithelial cells exhibit DNA damage susceptibility; macrophages amplify inflammation); and (III) molecular crosstalk with ATR ( ), Toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4) ( ), and nuclear lamins ( ). This model generates testable predictions: TLR4 activation reduces endothelial strain tolerance from 25% to 18% ( ); cell-specific inflection points define therapeutic windows (endothelial ~20–25% vs. epithelial ~15–18% strain); temporal dynamics separate adaptive phases (<6 h) from pathological exhaustion (>24 h) ( ). This framework advocates precision tuning – not “inhibit Piezo1” but “reset thresholds” and “enhance adaptation”—transforming VILI management from empirical protocols to mechanistically-guided precision therapy.

## The core sensor: biological properties of the Piezo1 channel

## Structure and gating mechanism of Piezo1
Piezo1’s capacity to decode mechanical forces into pathological or protective signals is fundamentally encoded in its unique molecular architecture and cell-specific expression patterns. Structurally, Piezo1 forms a massive trimeric complex resembling a three-bladed propeller, with peripheral “blades” acting as mechanosensors that cooperatively control a central Ca2⁺-permeable pore ( , ). This influx of Ca2⁺ constitutes the primary signaling event initiating downstream cascades ( , , ). Piezo1’s gating mechanism integrates two complementary models: the “force-from-lipid” (FFL) model, whereby increased membrane tension during VILI directly flattens Piezo1’s dome-like structure to open the central pore, and the “force-from-filament” (FFF) model, wherein cytoskeletal tethering enables Piezo1 to integrate mechanical signals over larger cellular areas ( , ). This dual-mechanism design establishes Piezo1 as an efficient “force-to-electrical signal transducer”, precisely converting crude physical forces into complex Ca2⁺ signals that drive mechanically-induced pathology ( , ). Critically, the installation of this mechanosensor across lung cells with disparate—or even antagonistic—functions creates the source of Piezo1’s functional paradox in VILI.

## Cell-specific distribution of Piezo1 in the lungs
The endothelium represents the epicenter of this paradox ( , left panels). Under physiological mechanical tension, Piezo1 activation in endothelial cells triggers protective cascades: Ca2⁺ influx activates calpain, which selectively degrades Src kinase, thereby stabilizing adherens junctions and preserving vascular barrier integrity ( ) ( ). However, the RhoA-Rock pathway exhibits context-dependent effects ( ): moderate activation enhances barrier function through cytoskeletal remodeling ( ), while excessive activation drives cell cycle dysregulation and contributes to barrier dysfunction ( ). Under pathological conditions, high-magnitude mechanical stress drives calpain to degrade vascular endothelial cadherin (VE-cadherin) instead of Src, leading to β-catenin dissociation and adherens junction disruption ( ) ( , ). This stark contradiction within endothelial cells—protection versus permeability—hinges on mechanical thresholds and molecular context. Consistent with their role as primary mechanical sentinels of the alveolar-capillary barrier under continuous hemodynamic forces, endothelial cells exhibit robust Piezo1-mediated mechanosensing activity, which sustains constitutive barrier-protective Ca2⁺ signaling at physiological stretch levels ( , ). Direct evidence from endothelial-specific Piezo1 knockout studies confirms this duality: knockout paradoxically worsens VILI, manifesting as increased vascular permeability and edema, demonstrating that basal Piezo1 activity maintains an endogenous protective pathway whose failure under excessive mechanical loads contributes to VILI pathogenesis ( , ).
 In macrophages, Piezo1 functions as a bifunctional immune regulator ( , right panels). Piezo1-mediated mechanosensing in macrophages is dynamically regulated: basal Piezo1 activity supports anti-inflammatory homeostasis, whereas inflammatory priming with LPS or damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) augments Piezo1 signaling competence, fundamentally reprogramming downstream pathway engagement from cytoprotective toward pro-inflammatory outputs ( , ). Under moderate mechanical tension, Piezo1 activation promotes anti-inflammatory responses: Ca2⁺ influx inhibits nuclear factor kappa B (NF-κB) signaling and reduces the synthesis of pro-inflammatory cytokines, including IL-6, TNF-α, and interleukin-1 beta (IL-1β), thereby shifting macrophages toward tissue repair phenotypes ( ) ( , ). Conversely, when mechanical stress is coupled with inflammatory priming (LPS/PAMPs), the same Piezo1 activation engages TLR4 crosstalk, triggering iron overload, ferroptosis, and cellular apoptosis ( ) ( , ). This mechanoinflammatory amplification loop—wherein Piezo1 and TLR4 synergistically drive macrophage death and inflammatory mediator release—exacerbates VILI ( ). Neutrophils exhibit an inverted pattern: physiological Piezo1 activation during capillary transit upregulates vascular endothelial growth factor A (VEGFA), promoting vascular repair ( ), whereas reduced Piezo1 activity under low shear stress paradoxically induces destructive NET formation ( ). In airway smooth muscle cells, high-intensity stretch downregulates Piezo1 expression, and this loss-of-function drives pathological cell migration ( ). Piezo1’s essential role in fetal lung pressure sensing further underscores therapeutic caution ( ).
In contrast to endothelial cells, alveolar epithelial cells exhibit comparatively limited Piezo1-mediated mechanoadaptive capacity, rendering them more susceptible to mechanical overload: DNA damage response pathways are activated at lower strain thresholds (~15–18%) in epithelial cells, reflecting a narrower protective window and greater intrinsic vulnerability to VILI-associated genomic stress ( , ). This cell-specific distribution pattern—wherein divergent Piezo1 mechanosensing capacities across endothelial, immune, and epithelial compartments establish distinct activation thresholds and downstream signaling landscapes—constitutes the fundamental basis for Piezo1’s paradoxical roles in VILI ( , ). systematically catalogs representative studies supporting these bidirectional functions, detailing experimental designs, cell types, pharmacological tools (agonists: Yoda1; antagonists: GsMTx4, siRNA), and key signaling pathways (Piezo1/Calpain/Src, Piezo1/TLR4/NF-κB, Piezo1/ATR/Chk1).
 

## Bidirectional roles of Piezo1 in VILI
Piezo1’s functional dichotomy manifests across multiple lung cell types through distinct molecular mechanisms. In endothelial cells, substrate-selective calpain activation determines barrier stabilization versus disruption ( ). In epithelial cells, mechanical stress triggers nuclear adaptation, DNA damage responses, and inflammatory signaling ( ). The following sections dissect these cell-type-specific pathways and determinants governing protective-to-injurious transitions.
 

## Protective mechanisms

## Endothelial barrier stabilization via the calpain-Src axis
Endothelial Piezo1 maintains alveolar-capillary barrier integrity through selective substrate targeting ( ). Mechanical stretch activates Piezo1, triggering localized Ca2⁺ transients that recruit calpain to adherens junctions. Calpain selectively degrades Src kinase, preventing Src-mediated VE-cadherin phosphorylation and preserving barrier function ( ). Endothelial-specific Piezo1 knockout paradoxically exacerbates VILI, manifesting as increased vascular permeability and pulmonary edema, while pharmacological activation with Yoda1 alleviates experimental VILI ( ). This mechanism is directly supported by endothelial-specific knockout models and pharmacological validation in vivo, representing the most robustly characterized protective pathway of Piezo1 in VILI.

## Epithelial nuclear adaptation through chromatin remodeling
Epithelial cells employ Piezo1-mediated nuclear mechanoadaptation as preemptive defense against genomic damage ( ). Piezo1 channels in the endoplasmic reticulum release Ca2⁺, initiating heterochromatin remodeling through H3K9me3 demethylation ( ). This epigenetic modification reduces chromatin compaction, creating a “nuclear airbag” that absorbs mechanical stress without DNA damage ( ). This protective program operates during early stress exposure (<4 hours) ( ). This nuclear mechanoadaptive program has been rigorously characterized in mechanically stressed cells, with ongoing research extending its relevance to pulmonary epithelial cells under VILI-relevant mechanical conditions ( ).

## Macrophage anti-inflammatory signaling
Under baseline conditions, Piezo1-mediated Ca2⁺ influx in macrophages suppresses NF-κB activity, reducing IL-6, TNF-α, and IL-1β synthesis while promoting M2 macrophage polarization ( ) ( , ). Neutrophil Piezo1 activation during capillary transit upregulates VEGFA, supporting vascular homeostasis ( ). However, inflammatory priming with LPS or DAMPs reprograms Piezo1 toward pro-inflammatory and pro-apoptotic pathways ( ) ( , , ). This anti-inflammatory role is supported by convergent evidence from macrophage cell lines and animal models of acute lung injury, with the precise contribution of macrophage Piezo1 to the protective phase of VILI representing a promising area for further investigation ( , ).

## Injurious mechanisms

## Barrier disruption through multiple pathways
Endothelial barrier collapse: In endothelial cells, pathological overstretch drives substrate switching: excessive Ca2⁺ waves activate promiscuous calpain activity, directly cleaving VE-cadherin instead of Src, leading to β-catenin dissociation and adherens junction disruption ( ) ( , ). Concurrently, Piezo1 activates RhoA/ROCK1-mediated cytoskeletal contraction, synergizing with glycocalyx degradation and high mobility group box 1 (HMGB1) release to perpetuate vascular leak ( ).
Epithelial barrier disruption: In alveolar epithelial cells, excessive mechanical stress drives Piezo1-mediated activation of a disintegrin and metalloproteinase 10/17 (ADAM10/17) metalloproteases, which shed junctional proteins including junctional adhesion molecule A (JAM-A) and amphiregulin (AREG), leading to epithelial barrier disruption and pulmonary edema ( ) ( ). This injury mechanism is supported by multiple independent in vivo studies employing genetic and pharmacological approaches in rodent VILI models and human pulmonary cells, encompassing both endothelial barrier collapse and epithelial junction disruption, and constituting the strongest direct evidence among all mechanisms described in this review ( , , ).

## DNA damage response activation in epithelial cells
Mechanical stress activates DNA damage checkpoints through Piezo1 independent of genotoxic insults ( ) ( ). Stretch induces reactive oxygen and nitrogen species production that link mechanical forces to ATR-ATRIP complex recruitment ( , , ). ATR phosphorylates Chk1, which inhibits cell division cycle 25 (Cdc25) phosphatases and enforces G2/M checkpoint arrest ( - ). Persistent activation triggers replication fork collapse and mitotic catastrophe ( ). Alterations in the lamin-A/B ratio activate p53/p21 signaling ( ) ( ), where p21 provides short-term anti-apoptotic protection but enforces cellular senescence ( , ). Senescent cells subsequently promote fibrotic remodeling ( ). The translational relevance of this pathway to lung epithelial cells is supported by the conserved nature of Piezo1-ATR mechanocoupling across cell types and corroborated by direct clinical evidence of mechanical ventilation-induced DNA damage and senescence in lung tissue ( , ).

## Pro-inflammatory cascade and epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT)
Piezo1 orchestrates sterile inflammation through converging pathways. The channel synergizes with TLR4, lowering inflammatory signaling thresholds ( ). Piezo1-mediated Ca2⁺ influx drives adenosine triphosphate (ATP) release, activating P2X purinoceptor 7 (P2X7)-NLR family pyrin domain-containing protein 3 (NLRP3) inflammasome and caspase-1-dependent IL-1β maturation ( , ). Mechanical stress triggers mitochondrial damage and cytosolic mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) release, engaging cyclic GMP-AMP synthase (cGAS)-stimulator of interferon genes (STING) to induce type I interferons ( , ). These danger signals recruit neutrophils that release NETs ( ), while reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation creates immunogenic DAMPs ( - ), sustaining inflammatory cycles.
In epithelial cells, sustained Piezo1 activation and ATP signaling initiate EMT, characterized by E-cadherin downregulation and upregulation of mesenchymal markers [alpha-smooth muscle actin (α-SMA), Vimentin] ( ) ( ). This phenotypic switch disrupts epithelial barrier function and activates fibroblast proliferation, bridging acute inflammatory injury to chronic fibrotic remodeling ( , , ). ASK1-ER stress pathways release pro-fibrotic extracellular vesicles ( ) ( ), propagating fibrogenic signals beyond directly stressed cells. Direct evidence for Piezo1-mediated EMT and fibrotic remodeling is provided by dedicated VILI models, while upstream inflammatory cascades are supported by mechanistic studies in related disease contexts ( , ).

## Determinants of functional switching

## Mechanical thresholds and temporal dynamics
Piezo1 exhibits cell-type-specific, dose-dependent thresholds separating protection from injury. In endothelial cells, physiological stretch (≤15% strain, <6 h) maintains barrier protection via Calpain-Src signaling, while pathological overstretch (>20–25%, >24 h) triggers VE-cadherin cleavage and barrier disruption ( , , ). Epithelial cells exhibit lower damage thresholds (~15–18% strain), rapidly activating ATR-Chk1 and senescence programs. Temporal dynamics critically modulate outcomes: acute stretch (<6 h) activates protective chromatin remodeling ( ), while chronic ventilation (>24 h) exhausts cellular buffering, depletes nucleotide pools ( ), and promotes senescence ( , , ).

## Cell type-specific signaling landscapes
Cellular context determines Piezo1 functional valence. Endothelial cells exhibit protection-biased responses with high thresholds (~20–25% strain) ( , ), while alveolar epithelial cells activate ATR-Chk1 and senescence programs at lower thresholds (~15–20%) ( , ). Macrophages demonstrate exceptional context-dependency: basal activation suppresses inflammation ( ), but LPS priming induces ferroptosis ( ). Airway smooth muscle cells show inverted patterns where stretch-induced Piezo1 downregulation paradoxically enhances pathological migration ( ).

## Molecular microenvironment and pathway competition
Inflammatory priming with LPS or DAMPs reprograms Piezo1 through TLR4 crosstalk, converting physiological stretch into pathological amplification ( ). Redox status modulates outcomes: nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2 (NRF2) activity determines whether ROS stabilizes as adaptive signaling or escalates into damaging cascades ( , ). Pathway competition shapes fate: p53-p21 provides short-term anti-apoptotic protection but enforces senescence ( , ), while death-associated protein kinase 1 (DAPK1) activation triggers death under severe stress ( ). ASK1-ER stress pathways release pro-fibrotic extracellular vesicles ( ) ( ), propagating signals beyond directly stressed cells. The mechanochemical integration producing opposite high-PEEP outcomes in obese versus lean rats exemplifies this molecular context-dependency ( ), underscoring that mechanical dose, cellular identity, temporal evolution, and microenvironmental signaling collectively determine whether Piezo1 protects or injures.

## A context-dependent rheostat model
Piezo1’s contradictory roles in VILI—protective barrier stabilization versus destructive disruption, anti-inflammatory versus pro-inflammatory—impede therapeutic development ( , ). We propose a unifying “Context-Dependent Rheostat” model: Piezo1 functions as a biological information processor decoding mechanical “dose” within specific cellular and molecular “contexts” ( ). The model reconciles contradictory findings in by positing that functional output depends on three interdependent variables: mechanical force parameters (dose-timing), cellular hardware (cell type and state), and molecular software (signaling networks and microenvironment).
 

## Mechanical dose and temporal dynamics
Piezo1 exhibits dose-dependent thresholds separating protection from injury. Both loss-of-function and gain-of-function mutations cause severe pathology, revealing a physiological “activity window” ( ). Even timing parameters like inspiratory-to-expiratory ratio significantly modulate endothelial injury ( ), underscoring temporal patterning as critical as force amplitude.
Experimental evidence demonstrates nonlinear dose-response relationships. In endothelial cells, barrier protection operates at ≤15% cyclic stretch and reverses to disruption beyond 20–25% strain, creating a relatively wide protective window ( , , ). Epithelial cells exhibit a narrower range, with damage responses activating at ~15–18% strain—lower than endothelial thresholds, reflecting greater mechanical vulnerability ( ). Macrophages demonstrate context-dependent thresholds determined by inflammatory priming rather than fixed strain values ( , ). These cell-type-specific inflection points underscore that “safe” mechanical ventilation parameters must account for the most vulnerable cell population.
Temporal dynamics critically modulate outcomes. Acute stretch (<6 h) activates protective pathways—chromatin remodeling peaks at 2–4 h ( ), Src degradation ( ). Sustained stretch triggers compensatory feedback: endothelial Piezo1 downregulates while macrophage Piezo1 paradoxically increases ( , ). Chronic ventilation (>24 h) exhausts protective mechanisms—ATR-Chk1 hyperactivation depletes nucleotide pools ( , ), senescence programs dominate ( - ), nuclear buffering collapses ( ). This temporal trajectory defines therapeutic windows: early enhancement of protection versus late prevention of fibrotic transition ( ).

## Cellular context and microenvironment
Cell type constitutes the model’s core hardware, translating identical mechanical signals into opposite outputs ( ). Pulmonary endothelial cells exhibit substrate switching: under inflammatory stretch, calpain cleaves VE-cadherin, disrupting barriers; under physiological stretch, calpain degrades Src, stabilizing barriers ( ). Epithelial Piezo1 drives ATP release and fibrosis ( ), while airway smooth muscle downregulation promotes pathological remodeling ( ). Immune cells exemplify context-dependency: neutrophils maintain homeostasis ( ) or trigger NETosis ( ); macrophages simultaneously engage anti-inflammatory (M2 polarization) and pro-death (ferroptosis) pathways ( ).
Microenvironmental factors reprogram functional outputs. Macro-level physiological states (obesity) fundamentally reshape responses to identical ventilation ( ). At the molecular level, TLR4 activation recruits Piezo1 to lipid rafts, lowering activation thresholds ~30% and creating feed-forward amplification: inflammation → mechanical sensitization → amplified inflammation ( ). This mechanochemical integration converts physiological stretch into pathological responses. Redox status modulates outcomes—NRF2 activity determines whether Piezo1-induced ROS stabilizes as adaptive signaling or escalates into damaging cascades ( , ). Disrupting TLR4-Piezo1 crosstalk or antioxidant supplementation may reset thresholds and restore protective signaling ( , ).

## Network-level integration
Piezo1 functional valence emerges from network interactions rather than linear pathways. Three regulatory motifs govern outcomes: the Ca2⁺-ROS-DNA damage response (DDR) axis links mechanical stress to genomic responses ( , , ); the p21/DAPK1 survival-death balance determines cell fate under stress ( , , ); and TLR4-Piezo1 mechanoinflammatory crosstalk creates feed-forward amplification loops. Antioxidant capacity ( , ), pathway competition ( , ), and molecular scaffolds [lncRNAs ( - ), protein complexes ( , )] modulate these networks, with excessive stretch disrupting protective circuit architecture ( , ). Therapeutic interventions targeting network nodes (TLR4 antagonism, antioxidant supplementation) may prove more effective than direct Piezo1 modulation by resetting systemic thresholds.
Beyond intracellular signaling, Piezo1 responses across cell types are interconnected through paracrine circuits that collectively amplify VILI pathogenesis. Mechanically stressed epithelial and endothelial cells release paracrine mediators—including ATP, mtDNA, and pro-fibrotic extracellular vesicles—that activate Piezo1-associated signaling in neighboring immune cells and fibroblasts ( , , ). Within this intercellular network, macrophages emerge as candidate “signal hub cells”: they receive convergent mechanosensory inputs from endothelial, epithelial, and neutrophil compartments, and their Piezo1 functional state—protective under homeostatic conditions, pro-inflammatory under inflammatory priming—determines whether these converging signals resolve or amplify VILI ( , , , ). Targeting this macrophage hub, rather than individual upstream pathways, may represent a more efficient therapeutic node for resetting the pathological network.

## Model synthesis and implications
The Context-Dependent Rheostat model unifies contradictory evidence ( ). Physiological forces acting on homeostatic cells activate protection—barrier stabilization ( ), nuclear adaptation ( ), anti-inflammation ( , ). Pathological forces on pre-sensitized cells or inflammatory microenvironments activate injury—barrier disruption ( , , ), DNA damage ( , ), inflammatory amplification ( ).
This framework generates testable predictions: (I) therapeutic efficacy depends on mechanical dose and timing; (II) cell-type-specific targeting is essential; (III) microenvironmental modulation (anti-inflammatory pretreatment, antioxidants) may reset thresholds ( , , ); (IV) network-level interventions [TLR4 antagonism, p21/DAPK1 modulation ( , , )] may prove more effective than targeting Piezo1 directly. Viewing Piezo1 as a tunable rheostat rather than an on/off switch ( ) enables “context-appropriate” therapeutic strategies based on precise modulation rather than blanket inhibition or activation.

## Therapeutic implications
Translating Piezo1 into VILI therapeutics confronts a fundamental challenge: its context-dependent functionality defies binary inhibition or activation strategies ( , , ). The “Context-Dependent Rheostat” model necessitates precision-tuning approaches that account for dose-dependence, cell-type specificity, and temporal dynamics rather than uniform targeting.

## Challenges in targeting Piezo1
Piezo1’s dual protective and injurious functions create a fundamental challenge: global inhibition abolishes endogenous barrier stabilization ( ) and nuclear mechanoadaptation ( ), while global activation exacerbates pathological signaling ( , , ). This bidirectionality invalidates simple on/off therapeutic strategies.
Current tools cannot distinguish Piezo1’s context-dependent states ( ). The peptide inhibitor GsMTx4 demonstrates protection in preclinical models ( , ) but faces druggability barriers (poor bioavailability, immunogenicity) ( ). The chemical agonist Yoda1 fails to replicate force-specific signaling—cells distinguish mechanical from chemical activation, coupling them to distinct downstream networks ( - ). These limitations preclude clinical translation.
Piezo1 exhibits a narrow physiological activity window: both loss-of-function and gain-of-function mutations cause cardiomyopathy ( ). Systemic inhibition risks muscle atrophy (disrupting Piezo1-dependent stem cell regeneration) ( ), NET formation (from neutrophil Piezo1 downregulation) ( ), and airway remodeling ( ). These pleiotropic effects parallel hematologic toxicities observed with DNA damage response inhibitors in oncology ( ), underscoring the need for strategies transcending global Piezo1 modulation ( , ).

## Precision-tuning strategies
Rather than inhibiting Piezo1 directly, targeting downstream pathogenic nodes offers refined control while preserving physiological Piezo1 functions. Repurposing oncology inhibitors—ATR inhibitors (berzosertib) ( , ), Chk1 inhibitors (prexasertib) ( )—provides validated tools for blocking DDR pathway hyperactivation in mechanically stressed cells ( - ). Alternative targets include the Chk1/Cdc25 hub governing cell fate decisions ( , , ), molecular scaffolds facilitating ATR-Chk1 activation ( , ), and parallel pathogenic pathways: the Piezo1/mtDNA/cGAS/STING innate immune axis ( , ), Piezo1-TLR4 mechanoinflammatory amplification (TAK-242, a TLR4 antagonist) ( ), and Piezo1-ferroptosis programs (ferrostatin-1) ( ). Comprehensive strategies addressing these parallel mechanisms may prove essential given extensive network redundancy.
Repurposing oncology drugs requires rigorous safety-efficacy evaluation. ATR/Chk1 inhibitors cause dose-limiting hematologic toxicities: berzosertib induces grade 3–4 neutropenia in 40% of cancer patients, while prexasertib causes thrombocytopenia in 35% ( )—potentially catastrophic in ARDS patients with baseline immune dysfunction. Mitigating strategies include shorter treatment duration (3–7 days rather than months), lower doses targeting stressed populations, and biomarker-guided dosing (adjusting by circulating γH2AX and pChk1 levels) ( , ). Alternative agents with superior safety profiles—TLR4 antagonists (TAK-242) ( ), senolytics (dasatinib + quercetin with established safety data) ( )—warrant prioritization. Rigorous preclinical validation with comprehensive toxicity monitoring represents the essential gating step.
Effective therapeutic implementation requires integration of temporal context and biomarker-guided patient stratification. Early-phase interventions (<6 h) should prioritize enhancement of protective mechanisms: NRF2 activation counters oxidative DNA damage before irreversible genomic injury is established, as demonstrated in lung injury models ( , ), while optimization of ventilation parameters prevents mechanical threshold crossing ( ). Circulating IL-6 and TNF-α levels provide early-phase patient stratification indicators ( , ). Late-phase interventions (>24 h) must target established pathological processes: senolytic therapies (dasatinib + quercetin) selectively eliminate fibrogenic senescent cells driving progressive lung remodeling ( ), while ATR/Chk1 inhibitors (berzosertib, prexasertib) target mechanically stressed cell populations exhibiting pathway hyperactivation ( , ). Late-phase biomarker monitoring should incorporate circulating γH2AX and pChk1 levels to identify DDR-hyperactivated populations warranting ATR/Chk1 inhibition ( ), alongside senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP) markers to guide senolytic therapy ( , ). This temporal stratification framework transforms VILI management from uniform drug administration toward individualized, stage-appropriate precision intervention.

## Context-appropriate implementation
Therapeutic efficacy depends on temporal context. Early interventions (<6 h) should enhance protective mechanisms (NRF2 activation counters oxidative DNA damage) (51,80), while late interventions (>24 h) must target established pathology [senolytics eliminate fibrogenic senescent cells (26); ATR/Chk1 inhibitors selectively kill mechanically stressed populations exhibiting pathway hyperactivation (101,102)].
Optimal strategies combine damage inhibition with protection enhancement. Context-appropriate interventions—accounting for mechanical dose, inflammatory state, cell type, and temporal evolution—represent the path forward, with treatment efficacy depending on precisely modulating signaling contexts rather than uniformly targeting Piezo1 ( , ).

## Conclusions and future directions
This review establishes that Piezo1’s seemingly contradictory roles in VILI reflect context-dependent functionality rather than true paradox ( , , ). The “Context-Dependent Rheostat” model demonstrates that functional valence—protection versus injury—depends on mechanical dose-timing ( ), cell-type specificity ( ), pathological stage ( - ), and downstream network competition ( , ). This framework fundamentally reframes Piezo1 from a binary pathogenic switch to a sophisticated biological decoder, invalidating therapeutic strategies based on global inhibition or activation. Future VILI therapy must shift from shutting down Piezo1 to precisely modulating the pathological contexts dictating its function.

## Critical knowledge gaps
Despite progress, critical gaps impede precision therapeutic translation. First, quantitative threshold determination: while we know cell-type-specific inflection points exist, the molecular mechanisms governing these thresholds remain unclear ( ). What molecular determinants—lamin composition, cytoskeletal architecture ( , ), Ca2⁺ buffering ( )—explain cell-type-specific strain tolerance? Second, temporal switch mechanisms: we observe chromatin remodeling (<4 h) ( ) transitioning to DNA damage (>24 h) ( , ), but the molecular timers governing this flip are unknown. Is it nucleotide pool depletion ( ), ROS accumulation ( , ), or loss of epigenetic buffering ( )? Third, cell-state heterogeneity: single-cell studies are needed to determine whether “mechanically addicted” populations represent distinct cell states or exist along a continuum of mechanical stress responses ( , ).
Current pharmacological tools—non-selective agonists (Yoda1) and peptide inhibitors (GsMTx4) ( , )—cannot distinguish Piezo1’s context-dependent states. Development of allosteric modulators recognizing specific conformational states (e.g., protective versus injurious modes) ( , ) represents an urgent priority. Such tools would enable state-selective modulation: activating protective Piezo1 signaling in early ARDS while inhibiting pathological activation in late phases. Concurrently, identifying biomarkers that predict individual patients’ Piezo1 response states [mechanical sensitivity, inflammatory priming status ( , ), temporal trajectory ( , )] will enable precision patient stratification. These advances require interdisciplinary collaboration integrating structural biology, medicinal chemistry, and clinical biomarker development.

## Translational path forward
The Context-Dependent Rheostat model suggests a state-selective therapeutic paradigm: cells chronically exposed to pathological mechanical stretch exhibit “mechanically addicted” phenotypes characterized by ATR-Chk1 hyperactivation ( , ) and senescence marker accumulation ( , ). These mechanically stressed populations display selective vulnerability to pathway inhibition ( , )—similar to oncogene addiction in cancer ( )—while homeostatic cells remain unaffected due to lower baseline pathway activity. Biomarker-guided patient stratification [circulating γH2AX levels ( ), plasma lamin-A fragments ( ), senescence-associated markers ( , )] will identify mechanically addicted populations and define therapeutic windows.
Optimal approaches integrate temporal and cellular context. Early interventions (<6 h) should enhance protective mechanisms: NRF2 activation (e.g., sulforaphane) counters oxidative DNA damage ( , ), while optimizing ventilation parameters prevents threshold crossing ( ). Late interventions (>24 h) must target established pathology: senolytic therapies (dasatinib + quercetin) eliminate senescent cells driving fibrosis ( , ), while ATR/Chk1 inhibitors (repurposed from oncology) selectively kill mechanically addicted populations ( , ). Combination strategies simultaneously block pathogenic nodes [STING inhibition ( , ), TLR4 antagonism ( )] while augmenting protective pathways [barrier stabilization ( , ), antioxidant capacity ( )]. Clinical trial design must move beyond one-size-fits-all protocols, stratifying patients by mechanical ventilation parameters, inflammatory status (IL-6, TNF-α levels), and temporal evolution (early ARDS vs. late fibroproliferative phase) ( , ).
Realizing this therapeutic vision requires a structured, phased research roadmap ( ). In the near term (years 1–3), foundational priorities include quantifying the molecular determinants of cell-type-specific mechanical thresholds, establishing validated Piezo1-state biomarker panels (circulating γH2AX, pChk1, and plasma lamin-A fragments), and constructing multi-cellular VILI organ-on-chip platforms to interrogate intercellular paracrine signaling circuits. In the intermediate term (years 3–5), translational efforts should focus on systematic preclinical validation of ATR/Chk1 inhibitors and senolytic agents in VILI animal models with comprehensive toxicity profiling, alongside prospective clinical cohort studies to establish diagnostic thresholds for candidate biomarkers in mechanically ventilated ARDS patients. In the long term (years 5–10), biomarker-guided phase I/II clinical trials should evaluate temporally stratified intervention protocols, progressing toward multi-center randomized controlled trials that stratify patients by inflammatory status, mechanical ventilation parameters, and disease phase. This roadmap transitions Piezo1-based VILI therapeutics from mechanistic insight to bedside application through iterative, evidence-driven advancement.
 By reframing Piezo1 as a context-dependent rheostat rather than a binary switch, we transform a confounding paradox into a tractable problem amenable to rational, mechanism-based intervention.
