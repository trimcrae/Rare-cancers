They are unresponsive to treatments, and patients relapse quickly on DNA-damaging chemotherapies. PDAC stroma consists of extracellular matrix proteins (ECM), secreted by cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs). Here we show an unexpected link between CAF-secreted ECM proteins and enhanced DNA repair. We identify NDRG1 (N-myc downstream-regulated gene 1) as a key mediator that senses signals from the ECM via adhesion receptors and serum and glucocorticoid-activated kinase. We establish NDRG1 as a DNA repair factor that physically associates with replication forks, maintains DNA replication, resolves stalled forks caused by chemotherapies and is involved in reducing R-loops, RNA–DNA hybrids known to cause genomic instability. NDRG1 is highly expressed in PDAC tumours and its high expression correlates with poor disease-specific survival and poor response to chemotherapy. In conclusion, our data reveal an unexpected role for CAF-secreted ECM proteins in promoting DNA repair via NDRG1, mechanistically linking tumour stroma to replication fork homeostasis and R-loop regulation.

Kozlova et al. report a functional role for NDRG1 in regulating R-loops and DNA repair, thereby linking cancer-associated fibroblast-derived extracellular matrix to chemotherapy responses in pancreatic cancer.

## Main
Pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) is a highly lethal cancer with a 13% 5-year survival rate and rising incidence. Projections show that about 67,440 new cases will be diagnosed in the United States in 2025 and 51,980 patients will die of the disease The standard-of-care treatment is chemotherapy, with combination treatment regimen FOLFIRINOX (folinic acid-fluorouracil-irinotecan-oxaliplatin) being most effective though often toxic and most commonly reserved for patients with better performance status. An alternative standard-of-care treatment is gemcitabine (GEM) in combination with albumin-bound paclitaxel or as a single agent, depending on patient-specific factors .These therapies primarily induce DNA damage or stall DNA replication, yet PDAC tumours typically relapse rapidly leading to less than 1 year of overall survival . Understanding the drivers of relapse is critical to improving treatment. Therefore, a critical need exists to understand what drives treatment relapse in PDAC, and to develop more effective treatment strategies.
Relapse can occur through multiple mechanisms, including altered drug metabolism or availability, enhanced DNA repair or activation of prosurvival signalling. A major contributor to treatment resistance in PDAC is the dense stroma, which limits drug diffusion and promotes drug degradation . In addition, high stromal and extracellular matrix (ECM) content provides various survival benefits for cancer cells. Due to the high ECM content, PDAC tumours exist in nutrient-poor environments and rely on scavenging pathways such as macropinocytosis . Our previous work showed that in starved environments cells uptake ECM proteins as a source of nutrients . Similarly, under nutrient-restrictive conditions, PDAC cells uptake stroma-secreted alanine and collagen-derived proline to sustain survival.
Cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) are a highly heterogeneous stromal cell population , , responsible for the abundant secretion of ECM proteins . They originate from resident fibroblasts, mesenchymal stem cells and pancreatic stellate cells. Activation of resident fibroblasts via inflammation, alcohol metabolites, growth factors (for example, TGF-β) or hypoxia leads to trans-differentiation into α-SMA-expressing CAFs that secrete abundant ECM into the tumour microenvironment (TME).
As we previously found a significant increase in ECM secretion by starved primary human fibroblasts , we hypothesized that the nutrient-poor environment in PDAC might activate the pancreatic CAFs and lead to enhanced ECM secretion. While stroma-rich tumours are known to be highly resistant to anti-cancer treatments – , a mechanistic basis of direct stromal contribution to DNA repair leading to chemoresistance is not known. Therefore, this work aimed to identify a mechanistic connection between the ECM and DNA repair in PDAC.

## Results

## Nutrient deprivation activates pancreatic fibroblasts, leading to increased secretion of ECM proteins and increased tolerance to DNA-damaging agents
Given the nutrient-poor conditions and desmoplasia frequently observed in PDAC, we wanted to assess how nutrient starvation influences patient-derived pancreatic CAFs (Extended Data Fig. ). Serum and lipid starvation of CAFs significantly enhanced ECM production (Fig. and Extended Data Fig. ), similar to what has been observed under hypoxic conditions . Because CAFs are linked to tumour growth and treatment resistance , we assessed whether the CAF-conditioned medium (CAF-CM) collected from starved fibroblasts could induce treatment resistance. Indeed, CAF-CM increased cancer cell proliferation (Extended Data Fig. ) and conferred resistance to multiple PDAC chemotherapies, including GEM, SN-38, 5-FU and oxaliplatin (Extended Data Fig. ). 
As these agents work by inflicting DNA damage, we examined whether CAF-CM from different lines reduces chemotherapy-induced DNA damage. We analysed DNA damage by yH2AX staining of cells treated with CAF-CM versus non-conditioned medium (NC) in the presence of chemotherapies. yH2AX staining revealed significantly less DNA damage in cells treated with CAF-CM compared with NC (Fig. and Extended Data Fig. ). These findings were confirmed by alkaline Comet assay that measures levels of DNA damage on a single cell level, and used Olive Tail Moment (OTM) as a read-out , demonstrating that CAF-CM decreases DNA damage (Fig. ).
Although cells in the TME, such as CAFs, can promote tumour cell survival through secretion of metabolites and deoxycytidine that competes with GEM , , our data indicate broader protection against multiple chemotherapies. Size fractionation of CAF-CM showed that metabolites (<3 kDa) provided some protection against GEM as previously reported, whereas the high molecular weight fraction (>3 kDa) conferred significantly stronger protection (Fig. and Extended Data Fig. ). Boiling abolished this effect (Extended Data Fig. ), indicating a protein-mediated resistance mechanism. Overall, these data show that CAF-CM protects PDAC cells from chemotherapy by reducing DNA damage, likely through secreted proteins.
Given that CAFs secrete abundant ECM proteins, we tested whether ECM components are responsible for the increased tolerance towards DNA-damaging agents. Addition of purified collagen I, fibronectin and laminin reduced γH2AX foci following SN-38 or GEM treatment, indicating decreased DNA damage (Fig. and Extended Data Fig. ). Although most ECM proteins were protective, responses varied, suggesting the effect being mediated by multiple integrin heterodimers. Indeed, integrin expression in PDAC cell lines was heterogeneous and further modulated by CAF-CM (Extended Data Fig. ). RGD peptides alone were ineffective, indicating that larger or polymerized ECM proteins are required for protection.

## Phospho-N-myc downstream-regulated gene 1 (NDRG1) is upregulated in PDAC cells in response to CAF-CM
To gain more mechanistic insight into how PDAC cells respond to CAF-CM we performed reverse-phase protein array (RPPA) analysis of ~304 proteins and phospho-proteins across major cancer signalling pathways in three PDAC cell lines (BxPC-3, HPAC and SW1990) treated with GEM. CAF-CM induced multiple prosurvival proteins at 72 h, including p-STAT3, EGFR, IGF1R and ECM-responsive proteins p-Src and p-FAK. However, the top upregulated protein was phospho-NDRG1 (Thr346), accompanied by increased total NDRG1 levels (Fig. ). 
NDRG1 is a stress-response protein with context-dependent roles in cancer. Although described as a cell-migration suppressor in prostate and gastric cancers cell lines , , it is associated with poor prognosis in several other malignancies such as glioma, inflammatory breast, gallbladder and liver cancer – . Given its strong induction by CAF-CM, we investigated this observation in PDAC.
CAF-CM increased both total and phospho-NDRG1 over time (Extended Data Fig. ). Because serum-starved CAFs secrete high levels of ECM (Fig. and Extended Data Fig. ), we tested whether ECM proteins stimulate NDRG1 phosphorylation. Multiple soluble ECM proteins induced pNDRG1 (Fig. ). Blocking integrin β1 or β4 reduced pNDRG1 in the presence of CAF-CM, and pharmacological inhibition of major integrin-activated kinases c-Src or FAK prevented NDRG1 phosphorylation (Fig. ). Conversely, overexpression of Src or FAK reduced chemotherapy-induced DNA damage even without ECM or CAF-CM (Extended Data Fig. ). These findings identify the integrin–Src–FAK axis as a driver of ECM-induced NDRG1 phosphorylation.
NDRG1 is phosphorylated at its C-terminal Thr346 (and adjacent identical sites Thr356/Thr366) by AGC kinases such as AKT and SGK , . Using the AKT inhibitor MK-2206 and a previously unreported, highly selective SGK1-3 inhibitor BLU6340 (Supplementary Figs. and and Supplementary Table ), we found that SGK inhibition abolished CAF-CM-induced NDRG1 pThr346 phosphorylation, whereas AKT inhibition had no effect (Fig. and Extended Data Fig. ), identifying SGK1-3 as the primary kinases for NDRG1 phosphorylation in PDAC. Notably, NDRG1 phosphorylation correlated with increased total NDRG1 levels (Fig. and Extended Data Fig. ). Because both SGK and NDRG1 have been reported to localize to the nucleus and cytoplasm – , we examined NDRG1 distribution following SGK inhibition and observed a reduction in nuclear NDRG1 upon BLU6340 treatment (Extended Data Fig. ).

## NDRG1 expression correlates negatively with patient survival and efficacy of gemcitabine treatment
Because ECM proteins stimulated NDRG1 phosphorylation, we examined its role in desmoplastic PDAC. Although NDRG1 has been described as both a metastasis suppressor , , and as a tumour-promoting protein – , , analysis of a human PDAC tissue microarray (TMA) PA805b (58 tumours and 20 healthy pancreas samples) showed significantly elevated phospho- and total NDRG1 in tumours compared with healthy tissue (Fig. ). We found that 80% of normal samples were negative for phospho-NDRG1, whereas nearly 50% of PDAC cases were phospho-NDRG1 positive. While total NDRG1 was detectable in most tissue sections, 82% of tumours exhibited moderate-to-strong staining (score 2 or 3), significantly higher than normal pancreas (Fig. ). 
Analysis of The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) dataset revealed NDRG1 locus amplification in 11% of PDAC cases (Extended Data Fig. ). Patients with NDRG1 amplification had significantly reduced overall survival (Extended Data Fig. ). In addition, high phospho-NDRG1 levels in tumour samples were associated with shorter median overall survival (Extended Data Fig. ).
In PDAC resections from patients treated with standard-of-care regimens (GEM/Abraxane or FOLFIRINOX and stereotactic body radiation therapy), pNDRG1–positive lesions were surrounded by fibrotic stroma (Fig. ). Given that NDRG1 phosphorylation is induced by the ECM–integrin–FAK–SGK1 pathway (Fig. ), we hypothesized that phosphorylation depends on proximity to ECM. Because human PDAC resections do not have a defined ‘tumour edge’ but rather present as clusters of neoplastic glands (often comprising at about 10% or less of the resection specimen) dispersed in a fibrotic stroma, we assessed GEM-treated SW1990 mouse xenografts (Fig. ). Quantitative spatial analysis showed that tumour regions adjacent to stroma border had the highest phospho-NDRG1 signal and were highly proliferative, as indicated by Ki67 positivity (Fig. and Extended Data Fig. ).
Interrogation of International Cancer Genome Consortium (ICGC) data showed that NDRG1 expression is enriched in the squamous (basal-like) PDAC molecular subtype (Fig. ), known to be associated with the worst PDAC prognosis . Among 237 post-surgical PDAC patients treated with adjuvant GEM, high NDRG1 expression correlated with shorter median disease-specific survival (mDSS, 15.9 versus 25 months) (Extended Data Fig. ). To determine whether NDRG1 expression might be indicative of clinical response to GEM, patients who received GEM in the adjuvant setting were stratified into high- and low-NDRG1-expressing groups. GEM-treated patients with high NDRG1 expression had significantly shorter disease-specific survival than those with low NDRG1 expression (mDSS 17 versus 34 months, respectively) (Fig. ).
Overall, the analyses of the two PDAC patient cohorts TCGA and ICGC (187 and 237 patients, respectively), together with protein data from patient issues, suggest that high NDRG1 expression is prognostic for worse survival and may predict therapeutic response to GEM.

## Loss of NDRG1 increases DNA damage and impairs tumour growth
Given the association between high NDRG1 expression and poor GEM-treated patient survival, we examined whether NDRG1 expression or its phosphorylation contributes to PDAC tumour growth. Inhibition of SGKs with BLU6340 or SGK1-3 knockdown reduced CAF-mediated protection and increased DNA damage in GEM- and SN-38-treated PDAC cells (Fig. and Extended Data Fig. ). Similarly, NDRG1 knockdown increased γH2AX foci and DNA damage in response to GEM and SN-38, but not 5-FU (Fig. , Extended Data Fig. ). In addition, NDRG1 depletion also elevated basal DNA damage, which was further exacerbated by chemotherapy (Fig. ). 
Additionally, CRISPR/Cas9-mediated NDRG1 knockout (KO) reduced PDAC cell proliferation and sensitized CAF-CM–treated cells to GEM and SN-38 (Extended Data Fig. ). Although SGK inhibition increased DNA damage, BLU6340-treated cells maintained normal proliferation rates (Extended Data Fig. ) and showed only modest GEM sensitization compared with NDRG1 knockout (KO) cells, which displayed markedly higher γH2AX levels (Extended Data Fig. ). These findings suggest that NDRG1 loss has a stronger impact than pharmacological SGK inhibition.
Because DNA damage predominantly occurs during S phase, we analysed cell cycle dynamics upon SGK inhibition or NDRG1 deficiency (Extended Data Fig. ). We also synchronized cells at the G1/S border and monitored cell cycle progression through S phase. Both BLU6340 treatment and NDRG1 deficiency led to a slower S phase entry and an inability of cells to progress through S phase at the same pace as the CNTR cells (Extended Data Fig. ). However, BLU6340-treated cells exited S phase similarly to controls, while NDRG1 KO cells accumulated in late S phase and progressed more slowly to G2/M. Because the KO cells spent longer in S phase, this might explain the heightened sensitivity of NDRG1 KO cells to DNA-damaging agents compared with BLU6340-treated ones.
Finally, we wanted to assess the role of NDRG1 in tumour formation in vivo. While control cells (CNTR) formed distinct tumours (eight of ten), NDRG1 KO cells were less tumourigenic (six of ten) and significantly smaller (Extended Data Fig. ). In doxycycline-inducible NDRG1 knockdown and KO models treated with GEM, NDRG1-deficient tumours exhibited reduced growth, decreased Ki67 positivity, and increased γH2AX compared with controls (Fig. and Extended Data Fig. ).
Collectively, these data indicate that NDRG1 expression leads to overall higher tumour growth rate and reduces DNA damage in the presence of DNA-damaging agents.

## NDRG1 has a direct role in DNA replication and repair
GEM and SN-38 induce replication stress by stalling replication forks and generating double-strand breaks (DSBs) . Because NDRG1 loss sensitized PDAC cells specifically to these agents and led to S phase defects, we investigated whether NDRG1 plays a direct role in DNA replication, repair or in the protection of replication forks. Using mass spectrometry-coupled isolation of proteins on nascent DNA (iPOND), we detected both total and phosphorylated NDRG1 at active and stalled replication forks (Fig. ). This is consistent with previous report that detected NDRG1 at active replication forks in HCT-116 cells . These data also support another previous report, which showed and interaction between NDRG1 and PCNA, the DNA polymerase clamp, a marker of active replication forks . 
Next, to assess NDRG1 role more specifically in DNA replication, we performed reporter assays distinguishing DSB-induced homologous recombination (HR) from stalled fork–associated HR in mouse embryonic stem cells that carry an HR reporter system (6xTer-I-SceI-GFP) at the Rosa26 locus (Extended Data Fig. ). NDRG1 knockdown reduced HR efficiency at stalled forks by ~50% but did not significantly affect classical DSB-induced HR, indicating a specific role for NDRG1 in resolving replication-associated lesions rather than canonical DSB repair.
Although NDRG1 is considered to be a potentially inactive member of the α/β hydrolase enzyme family , the crystal structure of the NDRG1 hydrolase fold (aa 31–319) revealed putative enzyme active site residues, Asp64 and His194, located near the position of the canonical α/β hydrolase catalytic triad (Ser-His-Asp) . To test its relevance, we re-expressed wild-type (WT) NDRG1 or an H194A mutant in NDRG1-null cells (Fig. ). While wt NDRG1 expression restored proliferation and relieved DNA damage, the proliferation rate and DNA damage levels of the H194A NDRG1 expressing cells phenocopied the NDRG1 KO (Fig. and Extended Data Fig. ).
Next, to assess NDRG1 role on replication fork progression we used the DNA fibre spreading technique (Fig. and Extended Data Fig. ). SW1990 and AsPC CNTR, NDRG1 KO and KO add-back cells (WT or H194A), were pulsed successively with two thymidine analogues (CldU and IdU) and the IdU-labelled DNA fibre tracks were measured. NDRG1 KO and the H194A add-back both led to shorter IdU fibre track length further confirming that NDRG1 loss, or the H194A mutation causes replication defects. Later, we stalled the replication fork after the CldU pulse by adding hydroxyurea (HU) for 1 h, followed by release from HU into IdU pulse. Similarly, NDRG1 KO and the H194A mutant impaired fork restart (Fig. and Extended Data Fig. ), whereas WT NDRG1 rescue restored normal fork dynamics.
SGK inhibition led to shorter fibre lengths and impaired replication fork recovery in CNTR cells and WT NDRG1-rescued cells but had very little effect on NDRG1 KO cells or H194A cells (Fig. ). Of note, both short- and long-term SGK inhibition slowed replication, indicating that SGK-dependent phosphorylation of NDRG1 is required for maintaining replication fork homeostasis (Fig. and Extended Data Fig. ).
Because the stromal tumour microenvironment may influence replication dynamics, we tested the effect of the conditioned medium from CAFs on non-damaged and stalled fork dynamics in the presence or absence of NDRG1, or with and without BLU6340 (Extended Data Fig. ). Consistent with our findings, CAF-CM increased replication fork speed and enhanced recovery from HU-induced fork stalling in control cells, but failed to do so in NDRG1-deficient or SGK-inhibited cells, suggesting that the CAF-CM effect on replication fork dynamics is dependent on NDRG1 and NDRG1 phosphorylation by SGK1.
These data suggest that NDRG1 and its phosphorylation by SGK1 are important for normal fork progression and stalled fork repair and that His194 is a critical residue for NDRG1 function in maintaining replication fork homeostasis.

## NDRG1 prevents R-loop accumulation
Replication fork slowing and stalling can be caused by many factors. To determine whether the replication fork defects caused by NDRG1 loss are due to the overall slowing of global replication, or the replication fork encountering obstacles, we analysed sister fork symmetry . If there is an overall slowing of replication, we would anticipate both of the sister forks to slow at a similar rate, maintaining symmetry. However, if there are obstacles, we would anticipate observing only one of the sister forks slowing, leading to an asymmetric pattern of sister replication tracts. We analysed this by DNA fibre assay and saw that NDRG1-depleted or BLU6340-treated cells showed substantially higher asymmetry of sister replication tracts compared with control cells (Fig. ), suggesting that NDRG1 deficiency increases the frequency of replication fork-stalling events. As R-loops are well-established impediments to replication fork progression we examined their involvement. Expression of WT RNase H1 enzyme (an endonuclease able to eliminate RNA–DNA hybrids), but not a catalytically inactive mutant (D210N or dRNH1), restored replication track length and reduced DNA damage in NDRG1-deficient cells, indicating that fork stalling results from R-loop accumulation (Fig. and Extended Data Fig. ). Consistently, by utilizing a recombinant catalytically dead green fluorescent protein (GFP)-tagged RNase H1 (GFP–dRNH1) as a probe for R-loop abundance we found that NDRG1 loss, SGK inhibition or H194A NDRG1 expression increased nuclear RNA–DNA hybrids, both at baseline and following chemotherapy exposure (Fig. , Extended Data Fig. ). Similar data were obtained using the S9.6 antibody (Extended Data Fig. ). 
We next asked whether expressing RNase H1 would rescue the increased DNA damage that we observed when the NDRG1 KO or H194A cells were treated with chemotherapy. Indeed, expression of RNase H1 but not the dRNase H1 was able to reduce yH2AX foci levels to those observed in CNTR cells (Fig. and Extended Data Fig. ).
R-loop-induced fork stalling is a major feature of transcription–replication conflicts (TRCs) and R-loop-induced DNA damage in the S/G2 phase . TRCs occur when replication and transcription machineries collide. TRC analysis by proximity-ligation assay (PLA) revealed that NDRG1-perturbed (KO, H194A) or BLU6340-treated cells exhibited significantly increased TRCs during S phase (Fig. and Extended Data Fig. ). While control and WT-rescued cells resolved these conflicts as replication progressed, NDRG1-deficient cells failed to do so, perhaps explaining the prolonged S phase we had observed with NDRG1 loss. 
Finally, we investigated whether blocking transcription (that reduces R-loops) would rescue normal replication speed in NDRG1 KO cells. Indeed, general transcription inhibitors, α-amanitin and flavopiridol, were able to revert fibre lengths closer to the ones observed in non-perturbed cells (Fig. and Extended Data Fig. ). α-amanitin was more effective in reverting the fibres back to normal length compared with flavopiridol, likely because flavopiridol, a CDK9 inhibitor (which also targets other CDKs), does not fully inhibit transcription, whereas α-amanitin is a highly selective inhibitor of POL-II and POL-III (ref. ).
Altogether, our study describes an unexpected function of NDRG1 in protecting replication forks from stalling by reducing the R-loop burden, and that this pathway is stimulated by CAF-secreted ECM proteins and requires signalling through the integrin–Src–FAK–SGK1 axis linking CAFs and ECM proteins to enhanced DNA repair and R-loop processing (Fig. ).

## Discussion
Our study provides mechanistic insight into how CAF-secreted ECM proteins reduce chemotherapy-induced DNA damage in PDAC. We show that lipid and serum starvation enhances ECM secretion by CAFs, stimulating SGK-dependent phosphorylation of NDRG1 downstream of integrin–Src–FAK signalling (Figs. , ). Analysis of PDAC cohorts shows that NDRG1 is highly expressed in the squamous/basal-like subtype and correlates with poor overall survival and reduced response to DNA-damaging chemotherapy (Fig. ). Mechanistically, phosphorylated NDRG1 localizes to active and stalled replication forks, limits R-loop accumulation, prevents fork stalling and promotes DNA repair, particularly in tumours exposed to DNA-damaging agents (Figs. – ). Because chemotherapy-treated tumours rely on replication fork homeostasis, NDRG1 loss increases genomic instability, reducing tumour fitness and sensitizing cells to fork-stalling agents.
Elevated ECM deposition and stromal density drive chemotherapy resistance in breast, pancreatic, lung, and ovarian cancers , , – , our study mechanistically links ECM signalling to DNA repair. NDRG1’s role in cancer has remained controversial, being described as both metastasis suppressive and tumour promoting. While some reports associate NDRG1 with favourable PDAC outcome , TCGA and ICGC datasets show frequent amplification and overexpression in solid tumours, particularly in PDAC. This discrepancy may be lineage dependent. Notably, NDRG1 mRNA is enriched in the aggressive squamous/basal PDAC subtype characterized by high replication stress, suggesting a context-specific function.
High NDRG1 expression has been associated with resistance to anti-cancer therapies, including temozolomide in glioma via stabilization of O6-methylguanine DNA methyl transferase (MGMT) , where SGK1-mediated phosphorylation of NDRG1 was required for interaction with DNA repair proteins. NDRG1 has also been implicated in radiotherapy resistance in colorectal cancer , and doxorubicin resistance in hepatocellular carcinoma . Consistently, our pancreatic cancer cohort analysis shows worse disease-specific survival in patients with high NDRG1 expression following adjuvant GEM treatment (Fig. ).
Genomic instability underlies cancer and neurodegeneration, and mutations in DNA damage response genes are known to contribute to neuropathies . Mutations in the hydrolase domain of NDRG1 cause Charcot-Marie-Tooth disease type 4D (CMT4D), a neuropathy. Of note, a genome-wide siRNA screen revealed increased DNA damage upon knockdown of several CMT-associated genes, including NDRG1 (ref. ), suggesting a broader role in genome maintenance. Our study suggests that CMT4D mutations may impair NDRG1’s DNA repair functions, contributing to pathology.
Notably, R-loop dysregulation, which promotes replication stress and DNA damage, is also linked to neurological disorders . Multiple helicases and RNA-dependent ATPases implicated in neurological diseases, such as SETX, FANCM, AQR, DDX19, DDX and BLM resolve R-loops . Notably, our data indicate that NDRG1 knockdown or SGK inhibition induced R-loop accumulation comparable to SETX depletion (Fig. ) and our recent work identified multiple RNA binding proteins as nuclear NDRG1 binding partners . Given that NDRG1 knockout and H194A mutant cells fail to resolve TRCs, we propose that NDRG1 restrains R-loop buildup.
Although NDRG1 lacks canonical catalytic activity, a structural study allowed speculating about the possible enzymatic activity of NDRG1 . The substrate for NDRG1’s putative activity is yet to be discovered; if any. However, the H194A NDRG1 mutant failed to restore resistance to fork-stalling agents, increased DNA damage, slowed fork progression, and elevated R-loops and TRCs (Figs. and ), indicating that His194 is critical for NDRG1 function in addition to SGK1-mediated phosphorylation. Because the resolved crystal structure lacks the flexible C terminus containing SGK1 phosphorylation sites, it remains possible that phosphorylation regulates NDRG1 localization, conformational activation or protein–protein interactions necessary for DNA repair.
Overall, our data support further investigation into the mechanism of NDRG1 function at replication forks and encourages the development of alternative treatment strategies for patients with PDAC with high NDRG1 expression. This could be achieved by targeting DNA repair functions of NDRG1 in combination with other DNA-damaging agents, fork-stalling agents and targeted therapies against molecular players involved in R-loop associated DNA damage.

## Chemicals
Small molecule SGK1 inhibitor BLU6340 was made by Blueprint Medicines. BLU6340 is an orthosteric type-1 kinase inhibitor with an isoquinolin-3-amine hinge binding motif. The structure and characteristics of BLU6340 are listed in Supplementary Fig. , and synthesis information is provided in Supplementary Fig. . The cell effects of BLU6340 were determined in MDA-MD-436 cells (ATCC:HTB-130). The cells were plated in 384-well plates at a density of 20,000 cells per well in RPMI-1640 medium supplemented with 10% heat inactivated FBS. At 24 h post-plating, the cells were treated for 1 h at 37 °C with either dimethylsulfoxide (DMSO) or BLU6340 in a ten-point, threefold dose response with a top concentration of 10 µM. The average half-maximum inhibitory concentration (IC50) from five independent assays measuring BLU6340-mediated inhibition of pNDRG1 was 33.1 nM with a s.d. of 20.4 nM. ## CAF-CM preparation
Immortalized human pancreatic stellate cells, referred to as CAFs in the study, were a gift from R. Hwang . Pancreatic cancer cell lines and pancreatic CAFs were routinely maintained in DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS, 50 IU ml−1 penicillin and 50 μg ml−1 streptomycin. To obtain conditioned medium from CAFs, CAFs were plated onto 15 cm plates, and when reached 90% confluency the medium was changed from 10% FBS to 0% FBS. After 3–4 days, the medium was collected, spun down to remove cell debris and sterile-filtered through 0.22-µm filter. The medium was either snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen (LN) or used fresh. When indicated, pancreatic cancer cell lines were incubated in NC medium (2% FBS) or in CAF-CM (2% FBS) for the course of the experiment. Fractionation of the CAF-CM was performed via centrifugation using the 3-kDa cutoff filter (Amicon Ultra, MWCO 3 kDa).

## DNA-damage foci immunostaining, microscopy and quantification
Cells were plated on coverslips and once adhered, treated with the indicated chemotherapies either in the presence of 10% FBS, NC or CAF-CM for the duration of the experiment. Later, the cells were fixed for 20 min in ice-cold 90% methanol, rinsed in PBS, kept in blocking buffer (1× PBS, 5% goat serum and 0.3% Triton X-100) for 60 min and further incubated with mouse yH2AX primary antibody (1:100 dilution, Millipore) overnight. The next day coverslips were incubated with corresponding secondary Alexa Fluor-conjugated antibodies (1:250 dilution, Thermo Fisher Scientific) at room temperature for 60 min. Coverslips were washed in PBS, counterstained with 4,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) and mounted using a Shandon Immumount (9990402). The signal was visualized using either a Zeiss LSM 880 Upright Confocal System or Keyence BZ-X800 immunofluorescent microscopes. Image analyses (intensity measurements) were performed by Fiji/ImageJ , and foci counts per nucleus were obtained using a modified speckle-counting pipeline from CellProfiler (v.4.0) . An appropriate nucleus size for each cell line was chosen, along with the speckle size. Counts per each nucleus were obtained and plots per treatment type per replicate were generated in either GraphPad Prism or R/R studio followed by statistical analyses.

## Comet assay
HPAC, SW1990 and SUIT-2 cells were plated on six-well plates (200 × 103 per well). The next day, the medium was changed to CAF-CM and cells were treated with either GEM 2 µM, SN-38 0.5 µM or 5-FU 100 µM overnight. When indicated, cells were pre-treated with SGK1 inhibitor BLU6340 (250 nM for SW1990 and 500 nM for HPAC) for 24 h, before the addition of CAF-CM with chemotherapies ± BLU6340. Later, alkaline single cell electrophoresis was performed using a Comet Assay High Throughput kit (Trevigen, R&D 4252-040-K) following the manufacturer’s instruction. In brief, cells were trypsinized, pellets were collected and washed twice in cold PBS. Cells were diluted in PBS without Ca2+ and Mg2+ and diluted 1:10 with low-melting agarose. A drop of agarose/cell mix was spread on a Comet assay slide, solidified at 4 °C in the dark and submerged into lysis buffer overnight at 4 °C. The next day, the Comet slide was incubated in alkaline unwinding solution for 40 min at room temperature and subjected to electrophoresis at 17 V for 34 min. After the run, cells were washed in distilled water and 70% ethanol, dried at 37 °C for 6 h and stained with SyBR-Gold nucleic acid stain. Images were taken on Nikon Eclipse TE2000S or Keyence BZ-X800 immunofluorescent microscopes using a ×10 objective. Comets were evaluated using CometScore 2.0 free software and the Olive Tail Moment of each individual Comet was plotted per treatment type per replicate using R/R Studio Software. Data were analysed using the nested mixed-effects analysis with Holm-corrected pairwise comparisons for the evaluation of statistical significance.

## Matrix feeding assays, and Src and FAK inhibitor cell-based assays
Cells were plated on coverslips on day 0, serum-starved on day 1 overnight and the next day, ECM proteins were added to the cells for 3 h (FN1 20 µg ml−1, collagen 1 30 µg ml−1, laminin 20 µg ml−1 or RGD peptide 100 µg ml−1), and the chemotherapies were added for 24 h (SN-38 250 nM and GEM 0.5 μM). Cells were fixed with ice-cold methanol and stained for yH2AX foci. The RGD peptide was obtained from Selleck Chemicals (S8008).
Cells were starved overnight and inhibitors were added 45 min before CAF-CM was added to the cells (saracatinib 2 µM and defactinib 2 µM). Three hours later, cells were collected. Matrix proteins were added at indicated concentrations and incubated on cells for 3 h before collecting lysates (Matrigel 3% vol/vol, fibronectin at 0.01 mg ml−1, collagen 1 at 0.04 mg ml−1 and laminin at 0.01 mg ml−1). Matrix proteins were purchased from Corning (Matrigel, 354230) and Sigma (laminin L2020, fibronectin F1141 and collagen 1 C3867).

## Xenograft studies and tissue staining
All animal studies were performed according to protocols approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee at BIDMC (067-2022). Mice were housed in Allentown ventilated cages with 60 air changes per hour following a 12-h light–dark cycle, at 21–22 °C and between 40–60% humidity. For all experiments athymic male nude mice (NU/J) were used (The Jackson Laboratory, 002019). SW1990 tumour cells (2 × 106) and AsPC tumour cells (1 × 106) were injected subcutaneously into one flank per mouse in 1:1 mix of Matrigel and PBS when the mice were 8–12 weeks old. Tumour growth was monitored three times weekly. Once the tumours reached 200 mm3 in volume animals were treated with GEM 25 mg kg−1 twice per week. The entire cohort of animals was killed when tumour size of at least one animal reached close to 1,000 mm3, which is the maximum tumour size approved by our ethics committee. At no point was the maximum tumour size exceeded. Animal numbers for each specific experiment are noted in figure legends. No statistical methods were used to pre-determine sample sizes for these experiments. Mice were randomized to treatment arms.
Xenograft tumour tissues were paraffin embedded and cut, deparaffinized in xylene and rehydrated in a descending ethanol series. NDRG1 staining was performed as described above. For immunofluorescent staining deparaffinized sections were subjected to antigen unmasking in SignalStain Citrate Unmasking Solution (CST, 14746) followed by permeabilization in 1% Triton X-100, and blocking of nonspecific binding in TBST/5% normal goat serum solution. Sections were later stained with mouse anti-fibronectin (ab6328-250, Abcam), pan-cytokeratin (AE1/AE3), Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated (53-9003-82, eBioscience) and rabbit phospho-NDRG1 (5482S, CST) or Ki67 (D3B5) rabbit monoclonal antibody (Alexa Fluor 647 conjugate) (12075, CST). Appropriate secondary antibodies were used and whole section images were acquired using an Olympus BX-UCB whole-slide scanner equipped with an Olympus UPLSAPO ×20 and VS-ASW (v2.7) software. Image analysis was performed in Qupath as described in the next section.

## Spatial analysis of the phospho-NDRG1 and Ki67 signal intensity in relation to a modelled stromal border
A computational framework combining StarDist , QuPath and Python was developed for the analysis of the whole tumour xenograft images stained for DAPI, pan-cytokeratin and fibronectin, as well as for phospho-NDRG1 or Ki67. StarDist in QuPath was used for deep learning-based cell nuclei detection, using the pre-trained ‘dsb2018_heavy_augment.pb’ model for DAPI-stained nuclei in two-dimensional (2D) fluorescence microscopy images. Cell boundaries were inferred with QuPath’s built-in cell expansion algorithm (5-µm radius). Nuclei with an area falling below the fifth percentile (around 15 µm2) were excluded. Cytokeratin, pNDRG1 and Ki67-positive cells were identified via QuPath’s built-in random forest model (‘Random Tree’) trained on a manually curated representative cell set. Stromal borders were modelled to separate areas with high and low fibronectin content. High-fibronectin areas, modelled as stromal regions, were annotated in each image using a threshold-based pixel classifier .
Initial, manually determined, absolute threshold values from a subset of images were converted to their percentile values in their corresponding cell distribution, and then applied to the rest of the images by maintaining the same percentile value. This way, threshold values were fixed algorithmically thus minimizing subjective/operator bias. Best fit was determined using weighted least-squares. The fibronectin channel was blurred with Gaussian smoothing (σ = 10) before thresholding. Finally, the 2D signed distance (in µm) of each cell to its nearest stromal edge was calculated, with negative values indicating cells inside stromal regions. The class of each cell, along with its signal intensity measurements for the four channels, were exported for further data analysis and visualization in Python. Cells were grouped into bins, and within each fixed-size bin the distance from stromal border was calculated as the midpoint of the bin position, and the signal intensity as the mean with its s.e. Results are presented as a mean distance from stromal border on the x axis and the mean signal intensity on the left y axis with a fixed bin size of 10 µm. The cell count of each bin is presented on the right y axis. All QuPath scripts developed for this study, as well as Python notebooks used for visualization, are available at https://github.com/HMS-IAC/stroma-spatial-analysis. The pipeline for the analysis of pNDRG1 and Ki67 distribution in AsPC xenografts from Extended Data Fig. is available and described previously .

## Isolation of proteins on nascent DNA
The iPOND assay was performed as previously described with slight modifications. The 400 × 106 HPAC cells were pulsed with 10 µM 5-ethynyl-2′-deoxyuridine (EdU) for 15 min. For a thymidine chase condition, cells were treated with 10 µM thymidine for 30 min and 4 mM HU was added after the EdU pulse to stall replication forks. Cells were subsequently fixed using 1% formaldehyde for 20 min at room temperature. The crosslinking reaction was quenched using 1.25 M glycine and cells were washed three times with PBS. Cells were incubated with 0.25% Triton X-100 in PBS for 30 min at room temperature and pelleted. Permeabilization was stopped with 0.5% BSA in PBS. Cells were then pelleted again and washed with PBS. After centrifugation, cells were resuspended with the click reaction cocktail using biotin azide (B10184, Thermo Fisher) and incubated for 2 h at room temperature on a rotator. After centrifugation, the click reaction was stopped by resuspending cells in PBS containing 0.5% BSA. Cells were then pelleted and washed with PBS twice. Cells were resuspended in lysis buffer and sonicated. Lysates were cleared, then incubated with streptavidin-agarose beads overnight at 4 °C in the dark. The beads were washed once with lysis buffer, once with 1 M NaCl, then twice with lysis buffer. To elute proteins bound to nascent DNA, 2× SDS Laemmli sample buffer was added to packed beads (1:1, v/v). Samples from biological replicates were pooled, incubated at 95 °C for 30 min and followed by immunoblotting.

## DNA fibre spreading
DNA fibres were performed as described previously . In brief, cells were sequentially pulsed with two thymidine analogues 50 µM CldU (Sigma, C6891) and 150 µM IdU (Sigma, 17125) with 2× PBS washes in between. When fork stalling was required, cells were pulsed with CldU, washed with PBS, treated with 2 mM HU for 1 h, washed with PBS and pulsed with IdU. BLU6340 treatment was performed at 250 nM either overnight or at 500 nM for 30 min as indicated in figure legends. Pre-treatment with transcription inhibitors α-amanitin (10 mg ml−1) and flavopiridol (10 µM) was performed for 30 min before the first pulse. Cells were then trypsinized and resuspended in PBS, 2.5 µl were pipetted on the top of SuperFrost plus slides (48311-703, VWR). After 4 min, 7.5 µl spreading buffer (0.5% SDS, 200 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.4 and 0.5 mM EDTA) was mixed with the cells for additional 2 min. Two glass slides were made per condition per experiment. Slides were tilted at 15 degrees to allow DNA fibres to run down the glass slide. Later, the fibres were air dried and then fixed in 3:1 methanol:acetic acid solution for 2 min, followed by the 2.5 M HCl treatment for 30 min and 3% BSA/PBST blocking for 1 h. Primary antibody incubation was performed for 1 h with anti-CldU (ab6326 Abcam, 1:100 dilution) and anti-IdU (BD-347580, 1:20 dilution). Following three washes with PBS, fibres were stained with appropriate secondary Alexa Fluor-conjugated antibodies for 30 min, washed, air dried and mounted. Slides were imaged with a Zeiss LSM 880 Upright Confocal System, ×40 or ×63 PlanApo oil immersion objective. Measurement of replication structures was performed manually using Fiji . ## Statistics and reproducibility
Data analysis was performed blindly. For simple comparison of means, data were first checked for normality; if distributed normally data were then analysed with paired or unpaired two-tailed Student’s t-test. If not normally distributed, data were instead analysed with paired Wilcoxon rank-sum (Mann–Whitney U) test. When comparing means from multiple conditions (for example medium or other treatment/cell types), Dunn’s Test was employed, which carries out pairwise non-parametric comparison of means. Groups of Wilcoxon rank-sum test results were adjusted for multiple comparisons using the Bonferroni–Holm method. Any other statistical method used is mentioned in each legend. The number of times experiments were performed and the number of the cells analysed are indicated in each legend. Exact P values are shown where possible. Overall no statistical method was used to pre-determine sample size. Tumours that had lost NDRG1 knockdown were excluded from statistical analysis and marked with red asterisk in the figures. Any contaminated wells in cell biology experiments were excluded from analyses.
For more complete statistical analysis taking biological replicates into effect, nested mixed-effects analysis was carried out in R using the ‘lme4’ and ‘lmerTest’ packages. Nested analysis is used to recognize that each set of experiments is carried out in levels, where cellular features (for example foci counts and OTM values) where enumerated across discrete replicates, which themselves were divided across conditions (for example medium type or other treatment/cell types). Mixed-effects models were constructed using both nested and crossed random-effects models to account for variance in the data. For example, in the case of experiments that utilized different conditions, the replicate number was treated as a crossed random effect, while replicate number within condition was treated as a separate nested random effect. Here, the given value (for example condition) was treated as a categorical fixed effect, allowing for regression analysis to consider both per-replicate variance as well as replicate-dependent condition variance.
To ensure that the main assumptions of mixed-effects regression are fulfilled (linearity and normality of residuals) all mixed-effects models were tested using the R ‘Performance’ library. When models were found to not fulfil these assumptions, the data were normalized. The R ‘BestNormalize’ package was used to identify the optimal normalization method; in this manuscript the optimal method for all datasets was found to be the Ordered Quantile normalizing transformation, applied to datasets using the R orderNorm function call. Transformed datasets were confirmed via the ‘Performance::check_model’ function for residual normality and linearity.
When comparing data with two conditions, each condition was coded into a simple binary and used as a level in the regression analysis. For cases where multiple conditions were tested, the conditions were coded as string variable levels in the model, and the ‘emmeans’ package was used to compare each condition pairwise. Comparisons carried out via the emmeans package were adjusted for multiple comparisons using the Bonferroni–Holm method.
As datasets represent three biological replicates, we validated that the potentially limited variance from the replicate levels did not affect the mixed-effects regression calculation. This is because the inverse of the variance is used in the standard restricted maximum likelihood estimate when calculating parameters for mixed-effects analysis. To validate each model, calculations were repeated using a partial Bayesian mixed-effects method (R ‘blme’ library and ‘blmer’ function), where regularizing priors were employed to achieve a maximum a posteriori estimation before fitting the model . The P values calculated were found equivalent to those generated via standard mixed effects.
Spider-plot data from the in vivo experiments were analysed using mixed-effects analysis. Tumour volume data were determined to be non-normal; here it was normalized using a log transform. Mixed-effects models using the data were checked using the ‘check_model’ method with residuals found to be effectively normal and linear. For analysis of the time-series data, three fixed variables were used: day after implantation (a time variable), knockdown status (a categorical variable) and an interaction term between day and knockdown status. The interaction term represents the knockdown-dependent tumour growth rate and is therefore the focus of this analysis. Animal number was included as a crossed random variable.

## Code availability
All QuPath scripts developed for this study, as well as Python notebooks used for visualization, are available at https://github.com/HMS-IAC/stroma-spatial-analysis. Targeted relative quantification of polar metabolites was performed with TraceFinder v.5.1 (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and data from TraceFinder was further consolidated and normalized with an in-house R script available at GitHub, which performed technical and biological normalization and quality control steps (https://github.com/FrozenGas/KanarekLabTraceFinderRScripts/blob/main/MS_data_script_v2.4_20221018.R).
