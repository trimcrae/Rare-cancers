## Abstract
Excessive protein intake is one of the main accelerators for chronic kidney disease including diabetic kidney disease (DKD). DKD is known to be a leading cause of end-stage renal disease; however, detailed molecular mechanisms remain unclear. Natriuretic peptides (NPs)/guanylyl cyclase-A (GC-A) signaling has been reported to exert protective effects against aldosterone-induced kidney injury. To investigate the role of NPs/GC-A pathway against DKD progression, we administered streptozotocin to systemic GC-A knockout (KO) mice and endothelial cell-specific GC-A conditional knockout (EC-GC-A cKO) mice, and fed high-protein diet (HPD) for 4 weeks. Furthermore, human glomerular endothelial (GE) cells were transfected with siRNA for GC-A and stimulated with high-protein and high-glucose medium. Systemic GC-A KO mice and EC-GC-A cKO mice with HPD showed increased albuminuria with glomerular enlargement, interstitial fibrosis and upregulation of proinflammatory and profibrotic mRNA expression. In systemic GC-A KO mice with HPD, genes related to renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system, including mineralocorticoid receptor (MR) pathway, were also upregulated, and finerenone administration ameliorated diabetes- and HPD-induced kidney injury. GC-A knockdown enhanced TGFB1 and SERPINE1 mRNA expression in GE cells, and these changes were ameliorated by SGK1 inhibitor. These findings reveal the significance of NPs/GC-A pathway in HPD-induced kidney injury and the effectiveness of MR/SGK1 pathway inhibition.

## Introduction
Chronic kidney disease (CKD) carries a significant global disease burden, contributing to both morbidity and mortality worldwide, and serving as a major risk factor for cardiovascular disease . Although CKD includes a wide spectrum of etiology, diabetic kidney disease (DKD) is a predominant cause and its increasing prevalence mirrors the global rise in diabetes . Although hyperglycemia is an essential factor in its development, the pathogenesis of DKD is multifactorial, involving metabolic disturbances, hemodynamic imbalances, inflammation and fibrosis . Activation of the renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system (RAAS) is a pivotal contributor to DKD progression. The harmful effects of RAAS on the aggravation of DKD are diverse, including vasoconstriction, promotion of interstitial fibrosis, and induction of tubular and podocyte damage – . Recently, RAAS inhibitors such as angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitor (ACEi) and angiotensin II receptor blocker (ARB) have become established components of standard therapy for patients with proteinuria , and have been reported to maintain renal function by attenuating renal fibrosis and podocyte injury.
The natriuretic peptides (NPs), including atrial natriuretic peptide (ANP) and brain natriuretic peptide (BNP), are also known to counteract the deleterious effect of aldosterone in renal diseases. ANP and BNP bind to guanylyl cyclase-A (GC-A) receptors and exert natriuretic, vasodilatory, and cardioprotective effects. While ANP inhibits renin secretion and aldosterone production, NPs/GC-A signaling also has additional renoprotective mechanisms against aldosterone-induced injury . Indeed, our previous experiments using systemic GC-A knockout (KO) mice demonstrated that the NPs/GC-A pathway plays a protective role against aldosterone-induced podocyte injury . Collectively, these findings implicate the NPs/GC-A pathway as having a protective role in the progression of DKD.
Long-term dietary high protein intake is an established risk factor for CKD progression, which induces glomerular hyperfiltration and proinflammatory responses. In CKD patients, it has been reported that dietary high protein load results in gut dysbiosis and increases the generation of gut-derived uremic toxins, which induce oxidative stress, inflammation and fibrosis, resulting in increased cardiovascular risk and CKD progression . While dietary high protein load is recognized as a significant factor in exacerbating DKD progression, the underlying molecular mechanism has yet to be elucidated. Conversely, clinical systematic reviews and meta-analyses indicated that restricted protein intake benefits patients with CKD by slowing the decline in kidney function and reducing proteinuria, thereby potentially delaying progression to end stage renal disease , . However, the clinical application of protein restriction is controversial, especially in elderly patients with sarcopenia, as previous retrospective cohort study has demonstrated that low protein intake was related to lower skeletal muscle mass and higher all-cause mortality in elderly patients . Therefore, the present study aimed to investigate the role of NPs/GC-A pathway in development of diabetic nephropathy with dietary high protein load by using systemic or cell-specific GC-A knockout mice.

## Results

## Systemic GC-A deletion with high-protein diet exacerbated glomerular injury and interstitial fibrosis in STZ-induced diabetic mice
First, to investigate the relationship between protein overload and GC-A signaling, wild-type (WT) and systemic GC-A KO mice were fed with normal diet (ND) or high-protein diet (HPD) for 4 weeks after induction of diabetes by STZ (streptozotocin) administration (Fig.  a). HPD showed no influence on Npr1 (the gene encodes GC-A) mRNA expression in WT mice, whereas Npr1 mRNA expression was undetectable in GC-A KO mice (Fig.  b). Double immunofluorescence (IF) staining for GC-A and nephrin (podocyte marker) or Tie2 (endothelial marker) revealed that GC-A was expressed in podocytes and vascular endothelial cells in WT glomeruli, not in KO mice (Fig.  c). Six-hour fasting blood glucose (FBG) levels were elevated by STZ, but HbA1c and FBG remained similar among all groups (Supplementary Fig. S1a and b). While body weights were unchanged between WT and GC-A KO mice with ND, HPD induced significant weight loss in GC-A KO mice (Fig.  d). Urine volume, water intake and food intake were increased after the induction of diabetes, with no significant differences among all groups (Supplementary Fig. S1c-e). GC-A KO mice showed higher systolic blood pressure (SBP) at the initiation of the study compared to WT mice, but no significant changes were observed at the end of the study, except in the KO + HPD and WT + ND groups (Fig.  e). HPD aggravated urinary albumin creatinine ratio (UACR) in both WT and KO mice, with KO + HPD exhibiting significantly higher UACR than WT + HPD (Fig.  f). Kidney weight per body weight was unchanged between WT + ND and WT + HPD groups, but significantly increased in KO + HPD group (Fig.  g). Serum creatinine (Cr), blood urea nitrogen (BUN) levels, and calculated creatinine clearance (CCr) were not significantly different among all groups (Fig.  h and Supplementary Fig. S1f). Serum aldosterone concentrations were also comparable (Fig.  i). PAS staining showed juxtamedullary glomerular enlargement in KO + HPD mice compared to the other groups (Fig.  j), while superficial glomeruli showed slight Bowman’s capsule enlargement (Supplementary Fig. S1g). Sirius Red staining also demonstrated increased interstitial fibrosis in KO + HPD group compared to the other groups (Fig.  k). These results suggest that HPD treatment aggravates glomerular and interstitial injury in diabetic GC-A KO mice.
 

## Systemic GC-A deletion with high-protein diet aggravated podocyte injury and glomerular inflammation in STZ-induced diabetic mice
To further characterize renal pathology, we assessed podocyte injury and inflammation. Immunohistochemical (IHC) staining for WT-1 showed reduced podocyte number in KO + HPD mice (Fig.  a). Nephrin immunofluorescence was also decreased in KO + HPD glomeruli, suggesting severe podocyte damage (Fig.  b). Electron microscopic analysis revealed foot process effacement and thickening of glomerular basement membrane (GBM) in KO + HPD group (Fig.  c). MAC2-positive macrophages (arrowheads) were significantly increased in KO + HPD glomeruli, indicating exacerbated glomerular inflammation (Fig.  d). Glomerular mRNA expression of Tgfb1, Ctgf, Serpine1, Ccl2, and Emr1 was elevated in KO + HPD group (Fig.  e), indicating enhanced fibrosis and inflammation. Moreover, glomerular expression of adhesion molecules Icam1 and Vcam1 was also upregulated in KO + HPD group, suggesting concomitant glomerular endothelial injury (Fig.  f). In whole kidney analysis, angiotensinogen (Agt) and serum and glucocorticoid regulated kinase 1 (Sgk1) mRNA was elevated in KO + HPD compared to WT + HPD, whereas renin (Ren1) was similarly upregulated in both groups (Fig.  g). These results indicate that the combination of HPD and systemic GC-A deletion promotes glomerular inflammation and fibrosis, potentially through endothelial dysfunction and enhanced intrarenal RAAS activity.
 

## Podocyte-specific GC-A deletion did not affect glomerular injury induced by high-protein diet in STZ-induced diabetic mice
To explore the role of GC-A in podocytes, we next generated podocyte-specific GC-A conditional knockout (pod-cKO) mice by crossing Nephrin-Cre mice with GC-A floxed mice. Following STZ administration, control (CT) and pod-cKO mice were fed with HPD for 4 weeks. (Fig.  a). Glomerular Npr1 mRNA expression was decreased by approximately 50% in pod-cKO mice (Fig.  b). HbA1c were similar in both groups (Fig.  c), and FBG, body weight and SBP of both groups were similar throughout the experiment (Fig.  d–f). Unexpectedly, pod-cKO + HPD mice showed similar UACR, kidney weight per body weight, serum Cr, and BUN levels compared to CT + HPD mice (Fig.  g–i). PAS staining revealed slight juxtamedullary glomerular enlargement in pod-cKO + HPD mice (Fig.  j). Sirius Red staining indicated a trend toward increased fibrosis in pod-cKO + HPD mice, though not statistically significant (Fig.  k). Glomerular expression of Tgfb1, Ctgf, Fn1, Serpine1, Ccl2, and Emr1 was not different between the groups (Fig.  l). These results suggest that podocyte-specific GC-A deletion has no additional effect on glomerular injury or interstitial fibrosis induced by HPD treatment.
 

## Endothelial cell-specific GC-A deletion with high-protein diet demonstrated mild glomerular injury and interstitial fibrosis in STZ-induced diabetic mice
To explore the role of GC-A in vascular endothelial cells, we generated endothelial cell-specific GC-A conditional knockout (EC-cKO) mice by crossing Tie2-Cre mice with GC-A floxed mice. Control and EC-cKO mice were treated with STZ and HPD, and were designated as CT + HPD (control mice fed with HPD) and EC-cKO + HPD (EC-cKO mice fed with HPD) groups, respectively (Fig.  a). Whole kidney Npr1 mRNA expression was decreased by approximately 70% in EC-cKO mice (Fig.  b). Double IF staining confirmed endothelial cell-specific GC-A deletion: GC-A co-localized with Tie2 in control mice, but was absent in Tie2-positive areas in EC-cKO glomeruli and vessels (Fig.  c). FBG and HbA1c levels were comparable between the two groups (Supplementary Fig. S2a and b). EC-cKO + HPD group showed significant weight loss 2 weeks after STZ, which was similar to systemic KO + HPD group (Fig.  d). Urine volume, water intake and food intake did not differ significantly between the two groups throughout the experiment (Supplementary Fig. S2c–e). SBP was consistently elevated by 5–10 mmHg in EC-cKO + HPD group compared to CT group (Fig.  e). UACR showed mild increase in EC-cKO + HPD group after 6 or 8 weeks, although this elevation was less pronounced than that observed in HPD-treated systemic GC-A KO mice (Fig.  f). Kidney weight was also increased in EC-cKO + HPD group (Fig.  g), but not in serum Cr, BUN levels (Fig.  h), and CCr (Supplementary Fig. S2f). PAS staining revealed enlarged juxtamedullary Bowman’s capsule area in EC-cKO + HPD group (Fig.  i), and Sirius Red staining also showed expanded fibrosis areas (Fig.  j). These results indicate that endothelial GC-A contributed to glomerular protection against protein overload with diabetes.
 

## Endothelial cell-specific GC-A deletion with high-protein diet aggravated podocyte injury but did not significantly induce glomerular inflammation in STZ-induced diabetic mice
IHC staining revealed that the number of glomerular WT-1-positive cells was decreased in EC-cKO + HPD group (Fig.  a), and IF staining showed decreased expression of nephrin (Fig.  b), suggesting podocyte loss in EC-cKO + HPD glomeruli. Electron microscopic analysis confirmed foot process effacement and GBM thickening (Fig.  c). IHC staining showed that MAC2-positive macrophages tended to be increased in the glomeruli of EC-cKO + HPD group, though not statistically significant (Fig.  d). Glomerular expression of Ctgf, Fn1, and Serpine1 was elevated in EC-cKO + HPD group, but the expression of Tgfb1 and proinflammatory genes such as Ccl2 and Emr1 was not significantly different between the groups (Fig.  e). In whole kidney analysis, expression of Fn1, Serpine1, Lcn2, and Sgk1 was upregulated in EC-cKO + HPD group, while the expression of Agt was not significantly upregulated (Fig.  f). Thus, while endothelial cell-specific GC-A deletion did not significantly induce glomerular inflammation in STZ-induced diabetic mice with HPD, profibrotic gene expression and mineralocorticoid receptor (MR)-induced SGK1 gene expression were upregulated similar to what was observed in systemic GC-A KO mice.
 

## Mineralocorticoid receptor blockade ameliorated HPD-induced glomerular and interstitial injury in GC-A KO mice
To investigate the contribution of enhanced MR signaling to the aggravated glomerular injury in systemic GC-A KO mice under HPD, we administered a MR inhibitor finerenone (1 mg/kg/day) for 4 weeks. The experimental protocol was the same as that used for systemic GC-A KO and EC-cKO mice, and mice were divided into HPD (systemic GC-A KO mice fed with HPD) and HPD + fine (systemic GC-A KO mice fed with finerenone-supplemented HPD) groups (Fig.  a). Serum aldosterone concentration was elevated in HPD + fine group, suggesting effective MR inhibition (Fig.  b). Finerenone did not affect FBG or HbA1c levels (Supplementary Fig. S3a and b). Significant weight loss observed in HPD group was prevented by finerenone (Fig.  c). Urine volume, water intake, and food intake were not affected by finerenone (Supplementary Fig. S3c-e). SBP was similar between the two groups, which is consistent with characteristic of finerenone (Fig.  d). Crucially, finerenone treatment significantly ameliorated the elevated urinary albumin excretion observed in HPD group (Fig.  e), and reduced kidney weight per body weight (Fig.  f), whereas serum Cr, BUN levels (Fig.  g), and CCr (Supplementary Fig. S3f) were not affected. Histologically, finerenone treatment attenuated the enlargement of Bowman’s capsule area observed in the HPD group (Fig.  h) and significantly reduced interstitial fibrotic area (Fig.  i). The number of glomerular WT-1-positive cells (Fig.  j) and expression of nephrin (Fig.  k) were improved in HPD + fine group. Glomerular expression of Ctgf, Fn1, and Serpine1 was decreased in HPD + fine group, and Sgk1 expression showed decreasing trend (Fig.  l), indicating that fibrotic changes were ameliorated in HPD + fine glomeruli. In whole kidney analysis, Agt mRNA expression was suppressed in HPD + fine group (Fig.  m). These results demonstrate that NPs/GC-A pathway counteracts the high glucose- and HPD-induced activation of MR pathway, thereby preventing glomerular and interstitial injury.
 

## Loss of GC-A signaling promotes endothelial cell injury after high glucose and excessive amino acids load in vitro
We finally examined the role of NPs/GC-A pathway in high glucose- and high amino acid-induced endothelial injury in vitro. Cultured human glomerular endothelial (GE) cells were transfected with siRNA targeting NPR1 (si-NPR1) or negative control RNA (si-NC), treated with high glucose (30.5 mM) and high amino acids (4 to 10-fold vs. control) together with aldosterone (10–9 M), and harvested after 24 h (Fig.  a). Cells were cultured with 4 types of media: CT (control), AA (high amino acids), HG (high glucose), and GA (high glucose and high amino acids). The expression of NPR1 mRNA was reduced to approximately one-third by si-NPR1 transfection (Fig.  b). Knockdown of NPR1 and loading of GA upregulated expression of TGFB1, SERPINE1, and IL6 in cultured endothelial cells (Fig.  b). In addition, under 2% FBS-supplemented condition, FN1 and SGK1 expression was increased, and CTGF expression also tended to increase in si-NPR1/GA cells (Supplementary Fig. S4). These results indicate that the absence of NPs/GC-A signaling in endothelial cells increases the production of endothelial cell-derived profibrotic factors under the condition of high glucose and excessive amino acids. To assess the involvement of MR/SGK1 pathway in this response, GE cells were transfected with si-NC or si-NPR1, and cultured for 24 h with aldosterone (10–9 M) and SGK1 inhibitor GSK650394 (1 µM) (Fig.  c). Knockdown of NPR1 with GA loading upregulated gene expression of TGFB1 and SERPINE1, which was downregulated by administration of GSK650394 (Fig.  d). These results suggest that SGK1 inhibition prevents GC-A knockdown endothelial cells from profibrotic changes induced by high glucose and excessive amino acids, highlighting the significance of MR pathway in HPD-induced endothelial cell injury. Collectively, these in vitro data indicate that NPs/GC-A signaling normally protects endothelial cells against profibrotic stimuli associated with high glucose and amino acid excess, and that decrease of this signaling sensitizes the cells to injury mediated via MR/SGK1 pathway.
 

## Discussion
Natriuretic peptides are integral to the regulation of blood pressure and fluid balance, and exert protective effects on various organs. We here demonstrated that both systemic and endothelial cell-specific GC-A deletion aggravated both glomerular and interstitial damage induced by diabetes with high-protein load. Although our group previously demonstrated that NPs/GC-A pathway protects podocytes from aldosterone-induced injury, this is the first report to highlight its significance in HPD-induced DKD deterioration. In the present study, systemic GC-A KO mice with STZ administration and HPD also exhibited increased albuminuria and significant weight loss compared to WT mice. HPD is known to aggravate glomerular injury in both humans and mice through multiple mechanisms. BTBR ob/ob mice or db/db mice fed with HPD showed glomerular hyperfiltration, increased albuminuria, mesangial matrix expansion, and interstitial fibrosis , . HPD-treated BTBR ob/ob mice showed upregulation of angiopoietin-2 and downregulation of angiopoietin-1, indicating endothelial cell stress responses. In diabetic patients, excessive amino acid intake and chronic hyperglycemia promote the formation of advanced glycation end products (AGEs), contributing to progressive kidney injury . HPD dilates glomerular afferent arterioles and suppresses tubulo-glomerular feedback, exacerbating glomerular hyperfiltration . Although ANP administration also dilates glomerular afferent arterioles and increases glomerular filtration rate (GFR) via cGMP production , , it reduces renal fibrosis in Dahl salt-sensitive rats , suggesting that dilation of afferent arterioles and increased GFR alone do not account for glomerular injury observed in GC-A KO mice. HPD treatment under diabetic condition is also reported to enhance RAAS in podocytes through increased AGEs , and increased AGEs also enhance angiotensinogen expression in the proximal tubules . Since aldosterone is known to deteriorate podocytes and endothelial cells, activation of MR pathways likely plays a crucial role in HPD-induced renal injury.
Systemic GC-A KO mice with aldosterone administration exhibited severe glomerular damage and massive albuminuria, indicating the NPs/GC-A pathway functionally antagonize RAAS. In this study, mRNA expression of Agt was upregulated in GC-A KO mice with HPD, suggesting that RAAS hyperactivation may be one of the contributors to deterioration of renal damage. A previous report demonstrated that aldosterone-treated rats exhibited perivascular leukocyte infiltration and increased expression of proinflammatory mRNAs , suggesting that activation of MR pathway may contribute to renal inflammation in HPD-treated GC-A KO mice. Histological and molecular analyses revealed marked exacerbation of glomerular inflammation and endothelial damage in these mice, likely mediated by MR pathway upregulation.
GC-A is expressed throughout the nephron, including podocytes, proximal tubule cells, collecting ducts, and endothelial cells , . Our group previously reported that podocyte-specific GC-A conditional knockout (pod-cKO) mice with uninephrectomy, aldosterone treatment, and high-salt diet exhibited marked urinary albumin excretion without an elevation of systolic blood pressure . We therefore hypothesized that GC-A in podocyte plays a definitive role in protecting against DKD injury. However, pod-cKO mice treated with STZ and HPD showed no significant phenotype, including weight loss, elevated urinary albumin excretion, or kidney enlargement. Given that systemic GC-A KO mice demonstrated endothelial damage induced by HPD, endothelial cell-specific GC-A knockout mice were generated to investigate the role of endothelial GC-A. Endothelial NPs/GC-A pathway has been reported to exert vascular endothelial protective effects, including the suppression of endothelial cell hyperpermeability and angiogenesis . Sabrane et al. reported that EC-GC-A cKO mice exhibit salt-resistant hypertension and cardiac hypertrophy as well as systemic GC-A KO mice . In our present study, EC-cKO mice fed with HPD showed weight loss and renal enlargement similar to systemic GC-A KO mice, but exhibited only a slight change in urinary albumin excretion, whereas systemic GC-A KO mice exhibited a 25-fold increase compared to control mice. While HPD-treated EC-cKO mice showed podocyte loss and interstitial fibrosis, glomerular macrophage infiltration and proinflammatory mRNA expression remained unchanged. These differential phenotypic manifestations suggest that GC-A in endothelial cells predominantly regulates vascular homeostasis and protects against renal fibrosis, whereas GC-A in podocytes or tubular cells play a crucial role in glomerular inflammation. Given that elevated renal Sgk1 mRNA expression indicated upregulation of MR pathway in EC-cKO mice, endothelial GC-A suppressed this pathway and conferred protection against DKD.
To further investigate the influence of MR pathway in HPD-treated GC-A KO mice, systemic GC-A KO mice were fed with finerenone-supplemented HPD. Finerenone is a non-steroidal MR antagonist that was approved for medical use in the United States in 2021 to reduce the risk of kidney function decline, cardiovascular death, and hospitalization for heart failure in adults with CKD associated with type 2 diabetes . Finerenone administration ameliorated renal deterioration in HPD-treated GC-A KO mice without altering blood pressure or HbA1c, indicating that inhibition of MR pathway is an effective treatment for preventing HPD-induced worsening of DKD. Previous reports have shown that aldosterone administration induces profibrotic gene expression such as TGF-β and CTGF, accompanied by renal fibrosis and glomerulosclerosis , . In the present study, we confirmed that inhibition of MR pathway by finerenone treatment downregulated glomerular profibrotic and proinflammatory mRNA expression in vivo.
In vitro experiments further demonstrated the efficacy of MR pathway suppression in preventing endothelial damage. In GE cells, GC-A knockdown induced profibrotic gene expression following exposure to high glucose and excessive amino acids, whereas neither condition alone significantly upregulated these mRNAs expression. These findings suggest that NPs/GC-A pathway exerts protective effects against endothelial cell injury induced by high glucose and excessive amino acids. Consistent with in vivo results, SGK1 gene expression was upregulated in GC-A knockdown endothelial cells after treatment with high glucose and excessive amino acids with 2% FBS. SGK1 has been implicated in renal inflammation and fibrosis in response to excess mineralocorticoid signaling , and is elevated in various kidney diseases, including DKD, glomerulonephritis, and obstructive nephropathy . MR-induced SGK1 expression inhibits the degradation of TGF-β signaling mediators, including Smad2/3, contributing to fibrosis development . In our present study, SGK1 inhibitor downregulated the expression of profibrotic genes in cultured endothelial cells, indicating that MR/SGK1 pathway blockade can counteract profibrotic factors induced by high glucose and excessive amino acids under insufficient NPs/GC-A signaling. These findings reveal that endothelial GC-A demonstrate protective effects against HPD-induced upregulation of profibrotic and proinflammatory mediators, including TGF-β, provoked by MR/SGK1 pathway activation.
This investigation has several limitations. The effect of blood pressure on renal injury was not examined in detail. Systemic or EC-specific GC-A KO mice exhibited higher blood pressure, and hypertension is a well-known exacerbating factor in CKD. However, the differences in blood pressure between control mice and systemic or EC-specific GC-A KO mice were approximately 10 mmHg, and thus considered to have a slight effect in glomerular injury. Additionally, the cause of severe weight loss of KO + HPD mice and EC-cKO + HPD mice was not elucidated. It is hypothesized that the combination of STZ-induced insulin deficiency and systemic RAAS hyperactivation could lead to cachexia, resulting in the loss of skeletal muscle and adipose tissue. Finally, as undesirable outcomes of high-protein load and GC-A deficiency, factors other than hyperactivation of RAAS and MR/SGK1 pathway have not been explored. For example, high-protein load may activate AGEs/RAGE pathway and exacerbate glomerular and interstitial injury. Additionally, NPs/GC-A pathway may reduce glomerular and interstitial damage through its anti-inflammatory effects independent of RAAS.
In conclusion, high-protein diet aggravated podocyte and interstitial injury in systemic and endothelial cell-specific GC-A knockout diabetic mice via MR pathway, suggesting that MR pathway inhibition may serve as a therapeutic strategy to prevent DKD aggravation induced by HPD.

## Methods

## Animal experiments
All animal experiments were performed in accordance with Fundamental Guidelines for Proper Conduct of Animal Experiment and Related Activities in Academic Research Institutions and were approved by the Animal Experimentation Committee of Kyoto University Graduate School of Medicine (Approval No. MedKyo23154). All methods were carried out in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations including ARRIVE guidelines. Systemic GC-A KO mice, originally generated on 129/SVJ background as previously described , were kindly provided by Dr. Ichiro Kishimoto from Toyooka Hospital. These mice were backcrossed with C57BL/6 J mice more than eight times, and wild-type littermates were used as control mice. Podocyte-specific (pod) and endothelial-cell-specific (EC)-GC-A cKO mice were generated by crossing Nephrin-Cre (C57BL/6N background) or Tie2-Cre mice (C57BL/6 background), respectively, with GC-A floxed mice (C57BL/6 J background), as previously described . Nephrin-Cre mice were provided by Dr. Taiji Matsusaka (Tokai University School of Medicine, Isehara, Japan), and GC-A floxed mice were provided by Dr. Takeshi Tokudome (Yokohama City University School of Medicine, Yokohama, Japan). Tie2-Cre mice were purchased from The Jackson Laboratory (Bar Harbor, ME, USA). GC-A floxed mice without Cre were used as control mice. All mice were maintained in a specific pathogen free facility with controlled light (12/12 light–dark cycle) and temperature (20–22 °C) at the Institute of Laboratory Animals of Graduate School of Medicine, Kyoto University. Water was provided ad libitum. Mice were anesthetized using intraperitoneal administration of medetomidine (0.75 mg/kg), butorphanol (5 mg/kg), and midazolam (4 mg/kg), and were sacrificed in a state of deep sedation at 8 weeks after induction of diabetes, and kidneys were collected for mRNA analyses and histological examination. The numbers of mice in each experimental group were n = 5 in WT + ND and KO + ND, n = 8 in WT + HPD and KO + HPD, n = 9 in CT + HPD and EC-cKO + HPD, n = 5 in pod-cKO + HPD, and n = 6 in HPD + fine.

## Induction of diabetes and protein overload
Male systemic GC-A KO mice, pod-GC-A cKO mice, EC-GC-A cKO mice, and their control mice received single intraperitoneal injection of streptozotocin (STZ, 150 mg/kg) at 6 weeks of age. STZ was freshly made in 0.1 mol/L sodium citrate buffer. The onset of diabetes was assessed at 10 weeks of age by measurement of 6-h fasting blood glucose (FBG) levels using blood glucose strips (SANWA KAGAKU KENKYUSHO CO., LTD., Nagoya, Japan). Mice with FBG below 300 mg/dL were excluded from the experiments. All mice were fed a standard diet (F-2, Funabashi farm, Chiba, Japan) after STZ injection for 4 weeks, and then fed a HPD (40 kcal% protein: D03091001, Research Diets, Inc., New Brunswick, NJ) or a normal diet (ND: D12450J, Research Diets, Inc.) for next 4 weeks. For mineralocorticoid receptor inhibition, finerenone was administered orally (1 mg/kg/day) via HPD containing 10 mg/kg finerenone (D23060501, Research Diets, Inc.).

## Reagents and antibodies
Streptozotocin (195-15154) was obtained from FUJIFILM Wako Junyaku Corporation (Osaka, Japan). Finerenone and SGK1 inhibitor (GSK650394) were obtained from Selleck Biotechnology (Kanagawa, Japan). Neoamiyu was obtained from EA Pharma CO., Ltd. (Tokyo, Japan). Primary antibodies used for immunohistochemical and immunofluorescence studies were anti-GC-A antibody (GTX109810, GeneTex, Inc., Irvine, CA, USA), anti-MAC2 antibody (CL8942F, Cedarlane, Ontario, Canada), anti-Wilms tumor 1 (WT1) antibody (sc-15421, Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Dallas, TX), anti-nephrin antibody (AF3159, R&D Systems, Inc., Minneapolis, MN, USA), and anti-Tie2 antibody (AF762, R&D Systems, Inc.). Antibodies were diluted 1:100 for immunohistochemical and immunofluorescence staining.

## Blood pressure measurement and urine collection
Blood pressure was measured indirectly by using the tail-cuff method (MK-2000ST, Muromachi Kikai, Tokyo, Japan). Urine samples were collected from metabolic cages (Shinano manufacturing, Tokyo, Japan) for 24 h and urine volumes were measured.

## Blood and urine parameter measurements
Urinary albumin levels were measured with albumin ELISA kits (FUJIFILM Wako Junyaku Corporation, Osaka, Japan). Serum and urinary creatinine, urea nitrogen, sodium, and potassium levels were measured by enzymatic methods (SRL, Tokyo, Japan).

## Collecting glomeruli by sieving methods
Glomeruli were isolated by the graded sieving methods as described previously , . Briefly, we removed and discarded perirenal fat and medulla with scissors and sharp forceps and cut kidneys into small pieces. Three sieves (100 μm, 75 μm, 30 μm) were piled up in order from top, and small pieces of kidney were placed top of the sieves and minced with plunger flange. Smaller segment went downside of the sieves and washed with ice-cold PBS. The glomeruli remained on the top of 30 μm sieve and collected by pipetting. Collected glomeruli were centrifuged at 15,000 rpm. for 5 min at 4℃, and the pellet was resuspended in PBS, and an aliquot was mounted on a glass slide for stereomicroscopic examination. Glomeruli were verified to constitute more than 80% of the total isolate. The pellets were used for RNA and protein extraction.

## Histology and Immunohistochemistry
Renal sections (1 μm thick) were obtained from paraffin-embedded tissue blocks and stained with Periodic acid-Schiff (PAS) and Sirius Red as previously described . The stained samples were examined by light microscopy (IX81, Olympus, Tokyo, Japan), and electron microscopic examination was performed using an electron microscope (H-7600, Hitachi, Tokyo, Japan). Mesangial areas in juxtamedullary glomeruli were measured using MetaMorph Software (Molecular Devices, LLC., San Jose, CA, USA), and Sirius Red-positive fibrotic areas were measured using ImageJ software (https://imagej.nih.gov/ij/, ver 1.54). For immunohistochemical analyses of mouse WT-1 and MAC2, sections were processed as preciously described . Briefly, the sections were deparaffinized, followed by antigen retrieval by microwave or trypsinization, and then reacted with each primary antibody. The number of positive cells in a glomerulus was measured for WT-1 and MAC2.

## Immunofluorescence staining
Four μm frozen sections were fixed with cold acetone for double immunofluorescence staining of mouse GC-A, nephrin, and Tie-2. For double staining, the sections were incubated with goat anti-nephrin antibody or goat anti-Tie2 antibody and rabbit anti-GC-A antibody for 1 h and then incubated with FITC-conjugated donkey anti-goat IgG (AB_2340400, Jackson ImmunoResearch Inc., West Grove, PA, USA) and Rhodamine-conjugated donkey anti-rabbit IgG for 1 h. The samples were then developed with a fluorescence microscope (IX-81, Olympus, Tokyo, Japan).

## RNA isolation and real-time quantitative polymerase chain reaction (PCR)
Total RNA was extracted using AllPrep DNA/RNA/Protein Mini Kit (QIAGEN, Venlo, Netherlands) and FastGene RNA Basic Kit (Nippon Genetics, Tokyo, Japan). ReverTra Ace qPCR RT Master Mix (TOYOBO, Osaka, Japan) was used for the synthesis of first-strand complementary DNA from total RNA as described previously . Applied Biosystems TaqMan gene expression assay probes were applied to measure the expression levels of mouse Npr1, Tgfb1, Ctgf, Fn1, Serpine1, Ccl2, Emr1, Icam1, Vcam1, Agt, Sgk1, Ren1, Actb and human NPR1, TGFB1, CTGF, FN1, SERPINE1, IL6, SGK1, ACTB. The sequences of primers and probes are listed in Supplementary Table 1. Real-time quantitative PCR was performed using the StepOnePlus Realtime PCR system (Applied Biosystems, Waltham, MA, USA) with THUNDERBIRD probe qPCR Mix (TOYOBO). The expression level of each mRNA was expressed relative to the Actb mRNA level.

## Cell culture, transfection and immunocytochemistry
Glomerular endothelial cells (primary human glomerular microvascular endothelial cells, Cell Systems, Kirkland, WA, USA) were cultured with Medium Kit without Serum and with CultureBoost-R™ (4Z3-500-R, Cell Systems). Cells were made quiescent in EGM-2 Endothelial Cell Growth Medium-2 BulletKit medium (Lonza, Basel, Switzerland) with 2% FBS for 24 h before stimulation, and EGM-2 without 2% FBS and hydrocortisone was used during stimulation. Cells were transfected with NPR1 small interfering RNA (#SI1022076, QIAGEN) or negative control RNA (QIAGEN) by a pulse of electroporation with Nucleofector II (Lonza) and Nucleofector Kit for primary Mammalian Epithelial cells (Lonza). Neoamiyu (EA Pharma CO., Ltd) was used for high amino acid load, and GSK650394 was used for pharmacological inhibition of SGK1.

## Statistical analysis
Data are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Variables were evaluated using a two-tailed unpaired Student’s t-test or one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with the Tukey-Kramer post hoc test for comparison of multiple means. Log-transformed data was used for UACR analysis. All statistical analysis was performed with Graphpad Prism software (v.10.00, GraphPad, San Diego, CA, USA). P-values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.
