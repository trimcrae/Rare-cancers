The development of small molecule inhibitors, such as tyrosine kinase inhibitors, has revolutionised treatments of several diseases, particularly cancer . Currently, there are 80 FDA-approved therapeutic agents that target approximately two dozen different protein kinases. Of these, 69 kinase inhibitors are prescribed for the treatment of neoplasms, while the remaining are focused on the treatment of chronic inflammatory diseases .
Kinase-targeted therapies hold also great promise for treating neurodegenerative diseases as they modulate key pathological processes, including tau and alpha-synuclein phosphorylation, protein aggregation, and oxidative stress among others . However, their clinical success has been hindered by challenges such as lack of validated targets, and poor blood-brain barrier (BBB) penetration of the developed inhibitors . Identifying underexplored kinases as potential drug targets for neurodegenerative diseases, combined with a focused effort to develop brain-permeable molecules, could pave the way for innovative CNS drug development in the near future . This represents a major issue that needs to be addressed, as the global number of patients with neurological disorders is projected to rise further due to population growth and ageing .
In light of these urgent problems, it was found that the underexplored serum and glucocorticoid-regulated kinase 1 (SGK1) plays a significant role in the occurrence and development of different neurodegenerative diseases, suggesting that the regulation of SGK1 may be a therapeutic target for neurological disorders , . SGK1 is a serine/threonine kinase that, when phosphorylated by other key regulators such as mTOR or PDK1, plays a pivotal role in cellular responses to stress, hormones, and environmental stimuli, being highly conserved throughout eukaryotic evolution . Although there are no specific SGK1 inhibitors currently on the market, the absence of severe side effects in two SGK1 knock-out mice models is highly promising , , and should encourage researchers to identify potent and selective SGK1 inhibitors and to explore their therapeutic potential in vivo. SGK1 has been described as a modifier of tau pathology in Alzheimer’s disease (AD). Single-cell transcriptomic studies have indicated that SGK1 levels are significantly increased in cortical excitatory neurons of AD patients . This increase has also been observed in isolated neurons and microglial cells from P301S and 5XFAD AD-related mouse models, respectively , . Furthermore, SGK1 is also up-regulated in the prefrontal cortex of both animal models, as well as in post-mortem AD patients’ samples . Accordingly, treatment with SGK1 inhibitors may open new therapeutic avenues for this devastating neurodegenerative disease.
However, until now, efforts in the development of SGK1 inhibitors have been focused on cancer pathologies and other peripheral diseases, with scant consideration of brain permeability . In fact, only a few SGK1 inhibitors are known, and for the majority of these compounds, CNS-permeability is either poor or not reported. Using the SwissADME tool of the Swiss Institute of Bioinformatics, they are classified as brain non-permeable ( ) .
 Taken together, these considerations highlight the need for novel SGK1 inhibitors with the ability to penetrate the CNS. In this regard, computational approaches offer a powerful strategy for exploring the vast chemical space of drug-like molecules and identifying promising candidates. Computer-aided drug design encompasses a wide variety of techniques, although the absence of the ideal algorithm for guaranteed success remains a challenge . Historically, ligand-based methods have represented a strategic approach focused on identifying bioactive scaffolds associated with specific biological activities, which then serve as templates for the design of new compounds. These methods have evolved incorporating chemical fingerprints derived from topological indices and/or shape-based screening techniques, enabling their application to large chemical libraries . Another key strategy is the structure-based approach, which has become the dominant paradigm in drug discovery. This method has gained prominence thanks to advances in obtaining protein structures by experimental and artificial intelligence-driven computational modelling. However, some authors argue that the ongoing productivity crisis in drug discovery may be attributed to an excessively dependence on structure-based strategies .
Considering this background, the present study is focused on the discovery of novel SGK1 inhibitors capable of crossing the BBB. A combined ligand- and structure-based virtual screening has been followed using the European Chemical and Biological Library (ECBL) as the source of drug candidates , . Data were experimentally validated following in vitro SGK1 evaluation of the best compounds identified in the virtual screening, and testing the brain permeability by Parallel Artificial Membrane Permeability Assay (PAMPA) methodology. This workflow led to the identification of a novel SGK1 inhibitor with submicromolar potency and brain permeable. The potential neuroprotective profile of this compound was evaluated in a cellular model of AD, using SH-SY5Y cells exposed to the neurotoxic insult okadaic acid . Furthermore, a systems pharmacology analysis was performed to investigate the broader role of SGK1 inhibition in AD-related pathways.

## Materials and methods

## Ligand preparation
ECBL library (199,341 compounds) was prepared using the Schrödinger LigPrep module (Schrödinger Release 2020–3). Briefly, hydrogens were added if necessary, counterions were removed, the possible protonation states for pH 7.4 ± 2.0 (Epik) , along with the corresponding tautomers were predicted. Finally, the stereochemistry of the compounds was retained, in case a chiral centre was not experimentally defined, both possibilities were generated. The force field used was OPLS3e .

## Ligand-based virtual screening: shape screening
For the selection of reference molecules to be used in the ligand-based virtual screening (LBVS), the ZINC database (v.ZINC20) was used to obtain the described SGK1 inhibitors. Initially, only those compounds with an IC50 below 300 nM were considered (pKi > 6.54, according to the database), resulting in 78 compounds (Table S1). To avoid structural redundancy, the molecules were coded as fingerprints with Schrödinger’s Canvas tool and grouped into 10 sets based on their similarity using the Tanimoto coefficient. The centroid of each cluster was selected as the reference molecule, obtaining 10 compounds for further stages.
The shape screening methodology was performed with the Schrödinger Phase module . The conformation of the 10 reference molecules was obtained by docking experiments against SGK1. For each compound that was screened, a collection of 10 conformers was generated. All molecules were represented as pharmacophoric spheres of 2 Å radius, corresponding to features defined by the program (H-bond donors and acceptors, hydrophobic groups, aromatic rings, and positive or negative ionisation zones). The algorithm identifies pairs of pharmacophoric triplets with similar geometries and environments between the reference molecule and the screened molecule, aligning them by least squares fitting. Subsequently, the best overlap is calculated using the volume shared by both molecules in spheres of the same type. The best 1,500 compounds of the ECBL were retained.

## Preparation of SGK1 structures
SGK1 crystallographic structures were obtained from the Protein Data Bank database (PDBs 2R5T , 3HDM , 3HDN ). The structures were prepared with the Schrödinger Protein Preparation Wizard module . Briefly, the corresponding hydrogens were added, the most probable ionisation states were generated for a pH of 7.4 ± 2.0 and the remaining loops and side chains were modelled with Prime . Cosolvents and ions were removed. The H-bond network was optimised with PROPKA at pH 7.4 and the system was minimised with the OPLS3e force field . Finally, water molecules were removed.

## Structure-based virtual screening: ensemble docking
For each crystallographic structure, the docking grid was defined by centreing the box using the co-crystallized ligands as reference. The dimensions of the grid were set by default. The 1500 compounds previously selected in the LBVS approach were screened using the Schrödinger Glide module with the standard precision (SP) mode. Then, 50% of the best SP-docking solutions were selected and screened using the extra precision (XP) mode as post-processing. The penalties for each state calculated with Epik were added to the scoring function (see section 2.1). It was established as a pass criterion that only those molecules obtaining a minimum score of −6.000 kcal·mol−1 could be selected. For each molecule, the best scoring pose was considered as the potential bioactive conformation. No restrictions were placed on the conformational search for ligand within the binding site. All other variables were defined by default.

## Molecular dynamics simulations
In order to analyse the stability of the protein-ligand system, the Desmond program was used as part of the Schrödinger software package. The selected force field was OPLS4 . The protein-ligand complex was obtained according to the general docking protocol. Initially, the partial charges of the ligand (ESP, electrostatic potential charges) were parameterised using the Jaguar module and after a geometrical optimisation with the B3LYP-D3/6-31G** basis functions . The protein-ligand complex was solvated in an orthorhombic box of water molecules (SPC model), neutralised with counterions and physiological concentrations of NaCl (0.15 M) were added. The system was equilibrated with the default protocol of the program. The NPT ensemble was used during the production stage at 300 K and 1.01325 bar. The selected thermostat and barostat were Nose-Hover chain and Martyna-Tobias-Klein, respectively. Integration of the equation of motion was performed every 2 fs. Trajectory frames were stored every 100 ps. The replicates shown in the paper correspond to the 2007, 2008 and 2009 seed values. Each replicate was performed with a duration of 1 µs. The simulation analysis data were obtained with the Simulation Interactions Diagram module. Only those interactions with a minimum frequency of 25% were taken into consideration. In the case of describing the behaviour of the binding site, this was defined as those atoms of the protein that are at a distance ≤ 5 Å from the ligand. The binding free energy was calculated using the MM-GBSA approximation . For each trajectory, the system coordinates were extracted every 10 ns and analysed using the thermal_mmgbsa.py script included in the Schrödinger software package.

## Inhibitory activity against SGK1
Kinase inhibition assays were done at the MRC Unit at the University of Dundee. Briefly, SGK1 (5–20 mU diluted in 20 mM MOPS pH 7.5, 1 mM EDTA, 0.01% Brij35, 5% glycerol, 0.1% β-mercaptoethanol, 1 mg/mL BSA) was evaluated using a modified peptide (GRPRTSSFAEGKK) in a final volume of 25.5 µL of a buffer solution containing 8 mM MOPS pH 7.0 and 0.2 mM EDTA. 30 µM was the substrate peptide concentration, along with 10 mM magnesium acetate and 0.02 mM [33P-g-ATP] (50–1000 cpm/pmol). The reaction was carried out for 30 min at room temperature. Assays were quenched by the addition of 5 µL of 0.5 M (3%) orthophosphoric acid and collected on P81 Unifilter plates, which contained a washing solution of 50 mM orthophosphoric acid. Experiments were performed in duplicates.

## PAMPA assay
Ten commercial drugs of known BBB permeability (caffeine, enoxacin, hydrocortisone, desipramine, ofloxacin, piroxicam, testosterone, promazine, verapamil and atenolol) were included in each experiment to validate the analysis set. Controls and compounds of interest (1–2 mg) were dissolved in 5 ml of assay buffer, which consists of a 7:3 solution of PBS (pH 7.4) and EtOH. Samples were filtered (PDVF 20 μm filters, Symta) and UV absorbance was quantified by scanning (220–400 nm) (VarioskanTM, TermoFisher), identifying between 2 and 5 wavelengths at which each compound absorbs. Once the initial absorbance of the samples was determined, 180 μL of each was added to the donor plate (Millipore), previously covered with 4 μL porcine brain lipid (Avanti Polar Lipids) dissolved in dodecane (20 mg·mL-1, Sigma-Aldrich). The acceptor plate was filled with 180 μL of assay buffer. Subsequently, the donor plate was stacked on top of the acceptor plate in a sandwich-like configuration for 2.5 h at room temperature. At the end of the incubation time, the donor plate was removed and the UV absorbance on the acceptor plate was quantified at the wavelengths recorded for each compound (final absorbance) to calculate the concentration of the compound able to cross the lipid barrier. Pe was calculated according to the Equation (1) . Every sample was analysed in triplicate wells per experiment and results are given as the mean standard deviation (SD) of two independent experiments.

Pe: effective permeability (cm·s −1), Vd: donor plate volume (cm3), Vr: acceptor plate volume (cm−3), S: well surface (0.266 cm2), t: incubation time (s), A0: initial absorbance, Ar: final absorbance.

## Assesment of hERG activity
The hERG inhibition assay was performed using HEK293 cells stably transfected with the hERG channel, employing the FluxOR™ Potassium Ion Channel Assay (Invitrogen). This assay uses a fluorogenic indicator dye and was conducted on a FLIPR TETRA system (Molecular Devices). After removing the culture media from the plates, 50 μL of loading buffer containing the FluxOR™ dye mix was added to each well. Dye loading was carried out at room temperature (25 °C) for 60 min, after which the loading solution was manually removed. Cells were washed once with assay buffer before adding 50 μL of the test compounds in assay buffer. Plates were incubated for 30 min at room temperature to allow compound equilibration. The stimulation buffer (Tl2SO4 + K2SO4) was prepared according to the manufacturer’s instructions. It was injected using the FLIPR TETRA to initiate the kinetic measurement from time point T0 (stimulation) to T120 s. Data were analysed using Genedata Screener software. Compound 3 was prepared in DMSO at 10 mM and tested at a maximum concentration of 100 μM in a 10-point, 1:2 serial dilution series, in triplicate. Then, IC50 value was calculated. Astemizole (20 μM) was used as a positive control, and 1% DMSO served as a negative control.

## Voltage-gated sodium channel assay (Nav1.5)
The assay is conducted using the FLIPR® Membrane Potential Assay dye to detect changes in membrane potential induced by compounds that modulate or block voltage-gated Na+ channels. Measurements are performed using the FLIPR Tetra High-Throughput Cellular Screening System (Molecular Devices) employing the Nav1.5-HEK293 cell line, which stably expressed the Nav1.5 sodium channel. Compound 3 was prepared in DMSO at a stock concentration of 10 mM and evaluated at a maximum concentration of 100 μM in a 10-point, 1:2 serial dilution series, each in triplicate to calculate de IC50 value. A positive control (50 μM tetracaine) and a negative control (1% DMSO) were included on each plate. A dose-response curve for tetracaine was also assayed as a standard. Veratridine is used to maintain the sodium channel in an open state by preventing inactivation .

## Calcium channel assay (Cav1.2)
Cav1.2-HEK293 cell line, which stably expresses the Cav1.2 calcium channel was used. They were seeded into microtiter plates and incubated for 24 h. After this period, cells were loaded with Fluo-4 dye in assay buffer (146.2 mM NaCl, 5.8 mM KCl, 0.005 mM CaCl2, 1.7 mM MgCl2, 10 mM HEPES; pH 7.2) for 30 min at room temperature. The dye is then removed manually. Plates are washed once with depolarisation buffer (127 mM NaCl, 25 mM KCl, 0.005 mM CaCl2, 1.7 mM MgCl2, 10 mM HEPES; pH 7.2) before the addition of test compounds. Compound 3 was prepared in DMSO at a stock concentration of 10 mM and tested at a maximum concentration of 100 μM in a 10-point, 1:2 serial dilution series, in triplicate, to calculate the IC50 value. A positive control (500 nM Nicardipine) and a negative control (1% DMSO) are included on each plate. Plates were then incubated at room temperature for 30 min with the compounds. Stimulation buffer (119 mM NaCl, 25 mM KCl, 4 mM CaCl2, 1.7 mM MgCl2, 10 mM HEPES; pH 7.2) was injected into the plates using the FLIPR Tetra system to record kinetic responses from T0 to T90 s. Data are analysed using Genedata Screener software.

## Neuroprotection in SH-SY5Y cells damaged with okadaic acid
SH-SY5Y cells were grown in DMEM (Gibco™, ThermoFisher Scientific) supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin/streptomycin at 37 °C and 5% CO2. Cells were seeded onto 96-well plates at 40.000 cells per well. After 24 h, cells were incubated with the evaluated compound at the indicated concentration for 1 h. Right after, okadaic acid was added at a final concentration of 30 nM and incubated for 24 h. Afterwards, 0.5 mg·mL−1 MTT solution was added to each condition and incubated at 37 °C and 5% CO2 for 3 h. Finally, culture media was carefully removed, and the attached formazan crystals were dissolved un 150 µL of DMSO. UV-absorbance was measured at 560 nM (GloMax® Discover Microplate Reader, Promega Biotech Ibérica, SL).

## Systems pharmacology
In order to build the protein-protein interactions network (PPIn), the ChEMBL (v0.31) and Therapeutic Target (v.8.1.01) databases were selected. Briefly, compounds with therapeutic indication for AD were selected using both databases. Subsequently, their associated targets were retained and used as main nodes of the network. To expand the network, for each node, a maximum of 20 first neighbours were added using the STRING database , as well as the set of associated connections between nodes. To filter interactions, criteria were set to be experimentally validated by direct methods (escore ≥ 0.7) or extensively documented in multiple databases (dscore ≥ 0.7). The detailed workflow can be found at the following link: https://github.com/ramirezlab/WIKI/tree/master/NetworkPharmacology. The calculation of second neighbours of SGK1 was performed using the NetworkX library (v.2.8.4) . The list of nodes of interest was obtained through the Open Targets platform (v.25.03) , using the search term “Alzheimer’s Disease” and applying a score filter of 0.05 (1725 targets).

## Results and discussion

## Mixed ligand- and structure- based virtual screening
Given the availability of both reported SGK1 inhibitors and crystallographic structures at the time of this study, a mixed virtual screening strategy was employed, comprising an initial ligand-based phase followed by a subsequent structure-based step. A critical factor in LBVS is the selection of potent and chemically diverse reference compounds to avoid structural redundancy and enhance predictive performance. For this reason, only inhibitors with an IC50 ≤ 300 nM were considered as reference compounds, yielding a collection of 78 compounds (Table S1). These compounds were encoded as molecular fingerprints using Schrödinger’s Canvas software, and subsequently clustered into ten groups based on structural similarity using the Tanimoto coefficient. The centroid molecule of each cluster was selected as its representative. Based on their potential binding mode to the SGK1 hinge region, the analysis resulted in three molecules with the 1H-pyrazolo[3,4-b]pyrazin-3-amine scaffold, three molecules derived from 1H-pyrrolo[2,3-b]pyridine and four miscellaneous structures, including 1H-pyrazolo[3,4-d]pyrimidine, acetohydrazine, 2,4-diaminopyridine and 1H-pyrazole-5-amine ( ).
 As a first step in the screening strategy, the shape screening methodology of the Schrödinger software was employed. ECBL chemical library was screened using the shape of the 10 reference active molecules. From this process, only the best 1,500 candidates (1325 unique compounds excluding tautomers and protonation states) were used for the following steps. The similarity of these candidates was found to range from 0.501 to 0.666 ( ).
 Following the initial ligand-based screening, the 1500 selected candidates were screened using a structure-based strategy, where 3 crystallographic structures (PDBs: 2R5T , 3HDM , and 3HDN ) of SGK1 were used. For this purpose, the compounds were screened with the Schrödinger Glide module at SP accuracy against the three protein structures. The top 50% SP-docking solutions were re-screened at XP precision for refinement. Compounds with XP docking scores ≤ −6.000 kcal·mol−1 across all three protein structures were retained, yielding a total of 190 molecules. For each compound, only the best-scoring pose among the three structures was considered for further analysis. These candidates were visually inspected, and 11 molecules were selected based on several criteria: the interaction mode, the XP-docking score obtained, the formation of critical interactions such as H-bond with the hinge region, and the commercial availability of these compounds. Compounds depicted in were obtained from commercial suppliers for in vitro evaluation.
 

## In vitro evaluation of the selected compounds
The inhibitory activities of the selected compounds were evaluated in vitro using human recombinant SGK1. Initially, all the identified compounds were tested at a fixed concentration of 10 μM. Only when the inhibition was greater than 50%, a dose–response analysis was conducted to determine IC50 values. Of the 11 compounds that were evaluated ( ), the IC50 values of three compounds were calculated: Z1280094148 (compound 1, 12.12 ± 1.11 µM), Z152570346 (compound 2, 7.08 ± 3.28 µM), and Z804448124 (compound 3, 0.66 ± 0.25 µM) ( and ).
 

## Evaluation of oral and blood brain barrier permeability
For a compound to be considered a viable drug candidate, adequate oral absorption is a fundamental requirement. Accordingly, the physicochemical properties of the three selected compounds were first evaluated using the QikProp program, which predicted good oral absorption for all of them (Table S2). Moreover, as this study focuses on SGK1 as a pharmacological target for CNS diseases, the ability of inhibitors to cross the BBB is a critical requirement for therapeutic efficacy. This presents a particular challenge in SGK1 inhibitor development, as many existing compounds demonstrate limited BBB permeability or lack comprehensive pharmacokinetic data related to CNS exposure. Therefore, in addition to predicting oral absorption, the brain permeability of the three hits identified in the previous virtual screening was calculated using the SwissADME tool ( ).
 According to the model under consideration, only compound 3 would demonstrate the ability to cross the BBB, while compounds 1 and 2 would only be able to reach the systemic circulation without reaching the CNS. In order to confirm this hypothesis, the effective permeability (Pe) of the three compounds was determined using the using a parallel artificial membrane permeability assay (PAMPA) ( ). In the case of compounds 1 and 2, the characterisation of their permeabilities proved to be unfeasible due to their insolubility in the buffer solution. In contrast, compound 3 exhibited elevated Pe values, thus being designated as CNS permeable (CNS +) and emerging as a noteworthy candidate for further development.

## Study of the binding mode of the compound 3
Compound 3 exhibited notable inhibitory SGK1 activity within the submicromolar range. Docking studies revealed that, this compound binds to the hinge region through a double H-bond interaction, which is a hallmark of this class of inhibitors. In addition, a π-cation interaction with the catalytic Lys127 and an extra H-bond with the Glu226 can be observed ( ).
 Given the remarkable activity of the SGK1 inhibitor, compound 3, we proceeded to study its binding mode through long-term molecular dynamics simulations (MDs) using the kinase crystallographic structure (3HDM) . The MDs were conducted in three replicates of 1 µs each ( , Table S3).
The RMSD of the alpha carbons (Cα) of the protein remains stable throughout the three replicas, as well as the active centre of the kinase. In addition, as can be seen from the RMSF calculation for the Cα of the protein, the largest fluctuations can be associated with the intrinsic mobility of two solvent-exposed loop-type secondary structures, including in this pair the activation loop (aa 134–150, 248–264).
Regarding the ligand, the original conformation remains stable in the three replicas at the beginning of the simulation ( ). However, a new conformation is acquired in the three trajectories in the range of 200–400 ns, which again presents an acceptable stability until the end of the simulation. RMSF analysis of the ligand identified the phenyl ring as the most flexible region, suggesting that the conformational transition may be driven by changes in its orientation. This was further supported by analysis of the dihedral angle formed between the phenyl and pyrrole rings (atoms 12, 14, 18, and 19), which showed significant variation within the 200–400 ns interval (Figure S1), coinciding with the conformational shift.
 According to these results, two different binding modes are proposed: the first one, where the ligand maintains a conformation close to coplanarity (similar to the original docking pose), and the second one, where the phenyl ring rotates and becomes embedded in another hydrophobic space at the N-terminal lobe. In order to elucidate which binding mode should be considered for further optimisation, each trajectory was separated into two parts based on the conformation of the compound (Replica 1: from 0 to 350 ns and from 400 to 1000 ns, Replica 2: from 0 to 300 ns and from 400 to 1000 ns, Replica 3: from 0 to 200 ns and from 300 to 1000 ns) (Table S3). Subsequently, the corresponding parts of each replicate were concatenated and analysed separately (initial conformation or new conformation).
The frequency of protein-ligand interactions was quantified using Schrödinger’s Simulation Interactions Diagram tool ( ). Although there are several differences between the two binding modes, the new conformation appears to arrange the phenyl ring in a more favourable orientation to interact with the catalytic lysine (74% vs. 94% frequency of interaction), a key feature of this type of inhibitors. Finally, the binding free energy (ΔG) of the two binding modes was calculated. For this purpose, the frames of each of the parts were taken every 10 ns and this energy was calculated using the Molecular Mechanics with Generalised Born Model and Surface Area (MM-GBSA) approach ( ) . The results indicated that the alternative conformation is energetically more favourable (ΔG = −53.28 vs. −48.55 kcal·mol−1), with the difference mainly attributed to electrostatic interactions (–36.48 vs. −29.57 kcal·mol−1).
 

## In vitro study of cardiovascular safety
SGK1 is an important signalling molecule in cardiovascular physiology and pathology by regulation of sodium and potassium channels . Thus, evaluation of the potential cardiotoxicity of SGK1 inhibitor compound 3 in hERG, Nav1.5 and Cav1.2 channels was performed given that cardiovascular safety concerns remain the leading cause of drug attrition at the preclinical drug development stage .
Data obtained for ionic channel inhibition in HEK293 cells are reported in . Compounds having an IC50 < 10 µM are considered as cardiotoxic. Results point to the cardiovascular safety of the SGK1 inhibitor compound 3 with high values of IC50 while the positive controls, astemizole, tetracaine and nicarpidine for hERG, Nav1.5 and Cav1.2 respectively, showed great risk of cardiotoxicity.
 

## Neuroprotective evaluation of the compound 3
Since the SGK1 inhibitor 3 was able to permeate the CNS, we proceeded to study its neuroprotective potential in vitro in a well-established AD cellular model . To this end, the human neuroblastoma cell line SH-SY5Y was exposed to okadaic acid (OA). OA has been demonstrated to produce high molecular weight aggregates of hyperphosphorylated tau, thus rendering it a suitable model for the study of AD. To select the appropriate dose, the potential toxicity of the compound at varying concentrations was evaluated. The concentrations that were examined did not demonstrate any signs of toxicity. Accordingly, the concentration of 5 μM was selected for the purpose of evaluating the potential neuroprotective effects of compound 3 ( ). The compound GSK650394 was utilised as a positive control, a known SGK1 inhibitor that had previously demonstrated neuroprotective effects in this model . At the selected concentration (5 µM), compound 3 demonstrated the capacity to restore cell viability in the presence of OA equally than the reference compound ( ).
 Despite the available data, it is important to note that key aspects of this compound’s characterisation remain to be addressed. In particular, given its ATP-competitive mechanism of action, a comprehensive evaluation of its selectivity profile will be essential in the next stages of medicinal chemistry optimisation. Nonetheless, considering its SGK1 inhibitory activity, in vitro neuroprotective potential, and favourable cardiovascular safety profile, compound 3 represents a promising starting point for the development of brain-permeable SGK1 inhibitors, being kinase selectivity an important challenge to be addressed during the hit-to-lead optimisation.

## Study of the role of SGK1 in AD from a systems pharmacology approach
Given the observed neuroprotective effect of pharmacological inhibition of SGK1 by compound 3, it is interesting to elucidate the mechanisms by which this inhibition modifies molecular pathways within a cellular environment, particularly those implicated in AD-related contexts. For this purpose, a systems pharmacology approach was used.
Initially, the ChEMBL , and Therapeutic Target Database were utilised to compile an initial list of targets that have demonstrated relevance in Alzheimer’s disease. Secondly, we proceeded to construct a protein-protein interaction network (PPIn) with these targets and, in addition, the reported first neighbours using the STRING database, resulting in a total of 4,586 targets (nodes) and 26,297 connections (edges). Utilising this PPIn, we proceeded to elucidate which targets of interest were proximate to SGK1 from a graph topological perspective. To determine which nodes should be studied in relation to SGK1, the Open Targets database , was used, retaining only those nodes with a score greater or equal to 0.05 according to the database scoring function, resulting in a total of 1,725 nodes. Thereafter, the shortest paths algorithm (https://networkx.org/documentation/stable/reference/algorithms/shortest_paths.html) was employed to determine if, within this list of 1725 targets, there were first or second neighbours of SGK1 ( and Table S4).
 This analysis demonstrated that FOXO3, CREB1, and mTOR exhibited a high degree of connectivity with the remaining nodes, thereby establishing their role as intermediaries that facilitate the connection between SGK1 and the majority of the second neighbours. Furthermore, these three targets have been implicated in AD, exhibiting a notably high score (0.56, 0.49, and 0.40, respectively, according to Open Targets Platform). Regarding SGK1 s neighbours, significant targets such as CDK5, GSK3β, and p53 (0.68, 0.55, and 0.49, respectively) appeared. It is noteworthy that other targets, including AKT1, CREBBP, PRKAB1, and PRKAG2, exhibit a connection degree that exceeds 1 (Table S4), suggesting the presence of multiple short pathways involving these targets and SGK1. A noteworthy observation is that FOXO1/3 and CREB1, both of which are transcription factors, function as intermediaries for 20 of the 33 s neighbours ( ). Given SGK1’s ability to phosphorylate and regulate these transcription factors , it is conceivable that the inhibition of SGK1 may lead to altered levels of several of these targets. Although the relationship between FOXO transcription factors and AD is unclear, it has been described how the activity of these transcription factors is able to protect from neuronal death caused by βA . A clear correlation between low levels of FOXO family activity and an increase in βA and tau phosphorylation has also been documented . Furthermore, it appears that an increase in FOXO3 activity in astrocytes promotes the clearance of βA plaques . On the other hand, SGK1 is capable of phosphorylating CREB1 at Ser113, although the pathological implications that this could have are unknown. However, an alteration in the phosphorylation and activity levels of this transcription factor has been described in several neurodegenerative diseases . Ultimately, this analysis proposes the regulation of the FOXO and CREB pathways by SGK1 activity as new avenues for exploration.

## Conclusions
The combination of computational and experimental strategies, specifically a mixed ligand- and target-based virtual screening approach using the European Chemical Biology Library, has led to the identification of a novel brain-permeable heterocyclic compound with submicromolar SGK1 inhibitory activity. This molecule has demonstrated neuroprotective effects in a cellular model of Alzheimer’s disease, along with a favourable cardiovascular safety profile. Moreover, systems pharmacology analysis identified the FOXO1/FOXO3/CREB1 axis as the principal signalling pathway associated with SGK1 in Alzheimer’s pathology.
In summary, the SGK1 inhibitor pyrrole 3 identified in this study represents a promising starting point for the development of a new class of therapeutic agents, while also contributing valuable insights into the underexplored biology of SGK1.
