## Abstract
Target of rapamycin (TOR) is an evolutionarily conserved serine/threonine kinase that plays a central role in regulating biological growth, development, and stress responses in eukaryotes. However, the TOR signaling pathway has not been thoroughly studied in apple (Malus domestica). Here, through sequence alignment with homologous genes in Arabidopsis thaliana, 14 conserved members of the TOR signaling pathway, encoded by 28 sequences, were identified in the apple genome. A comprehensive analysis of these members was subsequently performed by integrating their structural features, phylogenetic relationships, and expression profiles under low‐nitrogen stress conditions. The results showed that the functional motifs of these members are highly conserved across species, while there are significant differences in the physicochemical properties of each member in apple. Subcellular localization predictions indicated that most members are likely localized to the nucleus; a few may reside in the cytoplasm or chloroplast. Quantitative PCR analysis showed that TOR pathway members are differentially regulated under low‐nitrogen stress, suggesting their potential involvement in nitrogen stress response. Furthermore, MdTORs were found to directly interact with several autophagy‐related (ATG) proteins in apple plants, in addition to its canonical target ATG13. Collectively, this study systematically characterizes the components of the TOR pathway in the apple genome, examines their expression dynamics under low nitrogen stress, and identifies novel interactions between TOR and ATGs. These research findings broaden our understanding of TOR‐regulated autophagic pathways, provide a valuable foundation for future studies into their regulatory mechanisms, and also provide data support for clarifying the responses of TOR signaling pathway members in apple to low‐nitrogen stress.

## Plain Language Summary
Target of rapamycin (TOR) is an evolutionarily conserved serine/threonine kinase that plays an essential role in regulating biological growth, development, and stress responses in eukaryotes. Here, we identified all conserved members in the TOR pathway in the apple genome via analysis of their homologs in Arabidopsis. A total of 28 sequences encoding 14 typical members in the TOR pathway were identified in the apple genome. It was found that the gene expression pattern is induced by low‐nitrogen stress, and the expression of these genes reaches a peak on the third or fouth day. MdTORs can directly interact with several autophagy‐related protein (ATG) proteins in apple plants aside from its conserved target protein ATG13. Therefore, our results deepen the understanding of the mechanism by which TOR regulates autophagy and provide valuable data for future studies on the regulation of the TOR signaling pathway in apples under low‐nitrogen stress.

## Chromosome distribution and collinearity analysis of members in the TOR pathway in the apple genome
With the exception of TOR1, which could not anchor to any of the 17 apple chromosomes, the other 27 sequences were distributed on 13 different chromosomes. Chr 12 contained the highest number of genes (4 genes); chr 4 contained 3 genes. Chr 1, −5, −7, −8, −9, −13, −14, −15, and −17 each contained two genes. Ch 6 and −10 had the fewest genes, with only 1 gene each. No members were found on chr 2, −3, −11, and −16 (Figure  ).
 Collinearity analysis was performed on TOR signaling pathway members in apple; a heatmap was used in the first circle to depict the chromosome density, and a line plot was used in the second circle to represent the chromosome density. Duplicated regions between apple TOR signaling pathway members and other genes were denoted by different colored lines connecting the genes. Segmental duplication of MdTOR2 was observed on chromosome 10; segmental duplications were also observed for MdS6K1 and MdS6K3. Furthermore, segmental duplication of MdTCTP1 was observed on chromosome 16, and segmental duplications of MdRPS6A1 and MdRPS6A2 were observed on chromosome 15. All other genes demonstrated evidence of segmental duplication, with the exception of MdFKBP12, MdTCTP2, MdTOR1, and MdS6K2 (Figure  ).
 Comparative genomics was employed to investigate the evolutionary relationships between Arabidopsis and apple (Figure  ). The results showed that 10 out of the 28 members in the TOR pathway in apple had 18 pairs of collinear relationships with Arabidopsis (Table ). The majority of them showed only a single pair of collinear relationships with Arabidopsis, and MdPDK1‐1 had two pairs of collinear relationships between apple and Arabidopsis; MdKIN1 and MdKIN2 each had three pairs of collinear relationships, and MdCIPK12‐1 had four pairs of collinear relationships with Arabidopsis. This suggested that members in the TOR signaling pathway from different species may have originated from the same ancestor and play similar roles in plants.

## The expression patterns of members in the TOR pathway in apple under low‐nitrogen stress
The expression patterns of members in the TOR signaling pathway were examined in the roots of Malus hupehensis subjected to low‐nitrogen treatment. We collected samples at 0, 6, and 12 h, and 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, and 7 days and performed qPCR analysis. The results were visualized in the form of a heatmap using TBtools. As shown in Figure  , most of the detected genes were first gradually induced to a peak by low‐nitrogen stress, and then their expression level decreased; their highest levels were reached at 3 or 4 days. LST8‐1, KIN2, RPS6A1, RAPTOR2, TOR1, PDK1‐2, E2FA2, PTEN1, and S6K3 reached their highest expression levels at 3 days and were 1.7, 1.7, 1.4, 2.0, 1.6, 1.2, 1.7, 2.0, 1.7, 1.9, and 2.2 times their initial levels, respectively. At 4 days, the expression levels of S6k2, TAP46‐1, TAP46‐2, CIPK12‐1, CIPK12‐2, KIN1, and RPS6a2 were 2.9, 2.7, 2.1, 3.3, 2.5, 3.7, and 1.4 times their initial levels, respectively. These results indicated that the TOR pathway was also probably involved in the response to low nitrogen in apple plants.

## TORs in the apple genome interact with several autophagy‐related proteins (ATGs)
TOR is the conserved upstream regulator of autophagy by phosphorylating ATG13. We also found that TOR might directly interact with ATGs in Malus hupehensis during the response to low‐nitrogen stress (D. Li et al., ). To confirm the interaction between TOR and ATGs, we employed several ATGs in the apple genome and conducted Y2H analysis. The CDSs of both TOR1 and TOR2 were nearly 7500 bp; given the difficulty of cloning the whole CDS of TORs into the target vector, we cut them into five different segments according to their domains. As shown in Figure  , eight ATGs, including ATG2, ATG4a, ATG5b, ATG8c, ATG8i, ATG10, ATG13a, and ATG13b, were found to directly interact with different TOR truncations in the Y2H assay; the others could not interact with any of the TOR truncations. Although the similarity in the sequences and domains of TOR1 and TOR2 was high, they did not share the same downstream ATGs. They even interacted with the same ATG, and the functional domains were different. For example, TOR1, but not TOR2, can interact with ATG2. The third and the fifth truncations of TOR2 and the first, third, and fourth truncations of TOR1 interacted with ATG4a. ATG8c can interact with each truncation of both TOR1 and TOR2. In addition, the third truncation of TOR1 can interact with all the detected ATGs. These results suggested that except for ATG13, TOR can directly interact with ATGs through its various domains. There might be other mechanisms underlying the regulatory relationships between TOR and autophagy in apple plants.

## DISCUSSION
TOR, a conserved serine/threonine protein kinase in eukaryotes, plays a pivotal role in regulating cell growth and metabolism in response to environmental cues. In plants, an increasing number of studies have clarified the significance of the TOR signaling pathway in controlling plant growth and stress responses across various developmental stages (Liu & Xiong, ). In nutrient metabolism, TOR responds to sugar signals and senses changes in cellular metabolism and bioenergy status to regulate plant energy signal transduction. Once activated by glucose, TOR phosphorylates and activates the transcription factors E2Fa/E2Fb. This activation enhances cell division in the root meristem and shoot apical meristem, thereby promoting root growth and true leaf formation, respectively (L. Li et al., ).
TOR also mediates crosstalk between sugar signaling and brassinosteroid signaling. Glucose‐activated TOR can inhibit autophagy to stabilize BZR1 (a positive regulator of brassinosteroid signaling), thereby promoting cell growth in the hypocotyl (Zhang et al., ). Key upstream signals activated by TOR include organic N molecules. The signaling pathway with TOR as the core includes multiple components; the members in its conserved signaling pathway have been analyzed in humans, yeast, Arabidopsis, and microalgae, and they have similar conserved members in their respective signaling pathways (Mallén‐Ponce et al., ; Otsubo & Yamamato, ; Tatebe & Shiozaki, ). In this study, we aligned sequences from Arabidopsis and humans with the apple genome database and identified key members of the TOR signaling pathway in apple.
The composition of TOR signaling pathways in plants significantly differed from that in humans or yeast. In the apple genome, a total of 14 members and 28 genes were identified. Despite the presence of two TOR proteins in apples, key components of the TORC2 pathway are absent, and only the TORC1 pathway was detected, which is consistent with the results of studies of Arabidopsis and cotton (Y. Song et al., ). Alignment of these genes with their homologs in Arabidopsis revealed that their sequences were highly similar, indicating that the TOR pathway was highly conserved in apple and Arabidopsis. In addition, 10 of the TOR signaling pathway members in apple had 18 collinear relationships with Arabidopsis based on an inter‐species collinearity analysis, indicating their possible common ancestry and similar functions in plants. There were seven genes encoding the core components of the TORC1 complex, six genes encoding the upstream components, and 15 genes encoding downstream components in the apple genome. Both TOR and RAPTOR in Arabidopsis have a single clone, and there were two sequences encoding them in the apple genome. LST8, PDK1, PTEN1, ROL, KIN, TAP46, CIPK12, TCTP, PTEN, and E2FA were also encoded by two sequences in the apple genome. S6K was encoded by three sequences. FKBP12 was identical in the Arabidopsis and apple genome and comprised a single CDS. Genes encoding members of the TOR signaling pathway were higher in apple than in Arabidopsis, and most of them were derived from WGD or segmental duplications; only TOR1, S6K2, FKBP12, and TCTP2 were derived from dispersed and tandem duplications. In the apple genome, 70% and 26.1% of the duplications stemmed from WGD and tandem duplications, respectively (Yuet et al., ). Previous studies have shown that several gene families such as MYB, NAC, and DREB in apple are amplified by WGD and tandem duplications (Cao et al., ; Su et al., ; Zhao et al., ). Gene replication and tandem repeats play an important role in the distribution of genes in plants (Cannon et al., ; Kong et al., ). TOR signaling pathway members in Arabidopsis were distributed across five chromosomes; in apple, they were unevenly distributed across 13 chromosomes. Gene duplications contribute to genomic expansion and the diversification of gene functions, and genes involved in transcriptional regulation, signal transduction, and stress responses tended to have paralogs (Maere et al., ; Panchy et al., ). For example, the aluminum‐activated malate transporters (ALMTs) are related to abiotic stress. In Arabidopsis, the AtALMT family has 14 members, most of which are associated with aluminum tolerance (Hoekenga et al., ), and the same was the case for MsALMT1 from Medicago sativa and HlALMT1 from Holcus lanatus (Q. Chen, Wu, et al., ; Z. C. Chen, Yokosho, et al., ). However, OsALMT1 in rice is involved in the responses to salt stress and osmotic stress (Lu et al., ). In addition, sucrose transporters or carriers (SUTs or SUCs) function as sucrose/H+ symporters. MdSUT2 in apple plants is a homologous gene of the Arabidopsis vacuolar sucrose transporter AtSUT2. AtSUT2 reduces the sensitivity to sucrose during seed germination and the seedling stage and enhances sucrose transport activity (Barker et al., ). MdSUT2 significantly improves the tolerance of apple calli to abiotic stresses, such as salt and drought (Ma et al., ). Although the TOR signaling pathway is highly conserved, the expansion of coding genes in the apple genome may support the diversification of TOR signaling functions in apple plants.
TOR is a key regulator of plant growth under low‐nitrogen stress. In nitrogen‐deprived seedlings, TOR activity is significantly inhibited, while exogenous supplementation of nitrate, ammonium, or amino acids can rapidly restore its kinase activity (L. Song et al., ). In this study, we found that the expression levels of most genes in the TOR pathway initially increased and then decreased, which indicates that the TOR signaling pathway responds to low‐nitrogen stress. Previous studies of apple plants showed that TOR can alter hormone metabolism and autophagy activity in apple seedlings in response to low‐nitrogen stress (D. Li et al., ). The inhibition of TOR activity increases plant autophagy activity; during N starvation, autophagy can degrade damaged organelles, activate N‐related proteins to promote N absorption and assimilation (Huo et al., ; Zhen et al., ). Autophagy is an important downstream pathway of TOR. TOR is a key negative regulator of the initiation of autophagy; inhibition of TOR can increase the autophagic activity of plants to adapt to nutritional stress. The gradual assembly of ATG proteins is a key step in autophagosome biogenesis during the initiation of autophagy; TOR inhibits the activation of autophagy by directly phosphorylating the Ser258 of ATG13 to regulate the formation of the ATG1‐ATG13‐ATG17 complex (Yao et al., ). In addition, TOR can modulate the phosphorylation of ATG14l to regulate the early process of autophagy (Matsunaga et al., ). An increasing number of studies have shown that TOR can regulate the process of autophagy by directly interacting with ATGs. We also found that TOR might interact with a large number of ATG proteins after constructing the protein–protein interaction network (D. Li et al., ). In this work, we further verified the interaction between TOR and ATGs via Y2H assays. The results showed that there were physical interactions between MdTORs and autophagy proteins such as MdATG2, MdATG4a, MdATG5b, MdATG8c, MdATG8i, MdATG10, MdATG13a, and MdATG13b. MdATG4a and MdATG5b directly interacted with the kinase domain of MdTOR2, and MdATG8c interacts with both the kinase domains of MdTOR1 and MdTOR2, indicating that they may be the phosphorylation substrates of TOR in apple plants. In addition, most of the ATGs interacted with the first, third, or fourth segments of TORs. These domains consist of a multi‐helical fold, featuring two curved layers of α‐helices arranged in a regular right‐handed superhelix. The superhelical structure provides a large solvent‐accessible surface, making it highly suitable for binding large substrates such as proteins and nucleic acids (Groves & Barford, ). Research indicates that RAPTOR interacts with the N‐terminal region (amino acids 1–1482) of mTOR, which contains all the HEAT motifs, and the overall structure of the N‐terminal portion of mTOR is required for its binding to RAPTOR (Kim et al., ). In addition, the interactions mediated by the HEAT domains are diverse. They can bind to both proteins and RNAs. eIF4G has three HEAT domains and exists in two isoforms, which may show certain selectivity biases toward different mRNAs (Jackson et al., ). The HEAT1 domain of DAP5 can also bind to RNAs (Marash & Kimchi, ). These results suggest that, aside from the conserved ATG13, TOR may regulate the activity of autophagy in multiple ways. However, which one plays the major regulatory role, TOR1 or TOR2, and how they work in regulating autophagy or other biological processes, requires further study.
It should be noted that this study focused on the identification, expression pattern analysis, and verification of interaction with ATG proteins of TOR signaling pathway members in apple. It is preliminarily speculated that the conserved members of the TOR pathway in apple may play important roles in response to low‐nitrogen stress, and TOR can also regulate ATGs. However, we have not yet conducted in‐depth investigations into the physiological functions and molecular mechanisms of these members in the TOR signaling pathway during low‐nitrogen stress responses, nor have we further verified the mechanism of TOR‐mediated regulation of ATGs. Naturally, these aspects have been incorporated into our future research agenda. Moving forward, it will be necessary to employ transgenic technology and other approaches to analyze the specific functions of these members one by one, and to verify whether MdTOR can directly phosphorylate the interacting ATG proteins through in vitro kinase activity assays, thereby constructing a comprehensive TOR regulatory network.

## CONCLUSION
In conclusion, we identified 14 members and 28 genes involved in the TOR signaling pathway in the apple genome. They were mapped to 13 chromosomes; their protein sequence lengths ranged from 112 aa to 2465 aa, and their isoelectric points ranged from 4.55 to 10.7. Most TOR signaling pathway members are located in the cell nucleus, with a few located in the cell membrane or extracellular space. Many of them exhibit collinearity with Arabidopsis members. In addition, the majority of the members in the TOR signaling pathway in the apple genome can be induced by low‐nitrogen stress. Aside from the conserved downstream target ATG13, MdTORs can physically interact with autophagy proteins, such as MdATG2, MdATG4a, MdATG5b, MdATG8c MdATG8i, and MdATG10. This systematic analysis of the key components of the TOR signaling pathway in the apple genome reveals the diversification of the TOR signaling pathway in plants. Their expression under low‐nitrogen stress and the interaction between TOR and several ATG proteins will aid further study of the TOR pathway under low‐nitrogen stress and provide valuable reference data.
