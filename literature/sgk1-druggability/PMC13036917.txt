## Abstract

## Objective
The efficacy of doxorubicin (DOX) in treating various cancers is hindered by its associated cardiotoxicity, limiting its clinical utility. Mitophagy dysfunction and impaired mitochondrial function may be significantly involved in the development of DOX-induced cardiotoxicity (DIC). Tax1 (T-cell leukemia virus type I)-binding protein 1 (TAX1BP1) is an autophagic receptor; however, its role in DIC remains elusive. Therefore, the objective of this study was to assess the influence of TAX1BP1 overexpression on DIC and its molecular mechanisms, focusing specifically on mPTP opening and mitophagy in cardiomyocytes.

## Methods
Male C57BL/6J mice were intravenously administered recombinant adeno-associated virus serotype-9 (AAV9) carrying Flag-TAX1BP1 and cardiac troponin T (cTNT) promoter (AAV9-cTNT-TAX1BP1). Mice were randomly subjected to a DOX challenge with weekly administration of 4 mg/kg body weight for 5 weeks.

## Results
TAX1BP1 levels were notably decreased in the cardiac tissues of DIC mice. Cardiac TAX1BP1 overexpression alleviated cardiac remodeling and dysfunction in mice with DIC, with little effect from TAX1BP1 overexpression alone. TAX1BP1 overexpression improved mitochondrial dysfunction and promoted mitophagy in DIC cardiomyocytes. Furthermore, TAX1BP1 interacted with mTOR through its SKICH domain and facilitated the assembly of the mTORC2 complex, thereby initiating SGK1 phosphorylation, limiting the aggregation of VDAC1 and preventing the opening of the mitochondrial permeability transition pore (mPTP) in the cardiomyocytes facing DOX challenge.

## Conclusion
Collectively, our study highlights the significant role of TAX1BP1 in preventing mPTP opening and enhancing mitophagy, which contributes to the mitigation of mitochondrial and cardiac abnormalities associated with DIC. Our findings highlighted the potential therapeutic benefits of targeting TAX1BP1 in patients with DIC.

## Introduction
Doxorubicin (DOX) is commonly used as a chemotherapeutic agent to treat various types of malignancies, including a wide range of hematological malignancies and solid tumors. However, prolonged administration of DOX to patients may result in progressive and irreversible cardiovascular toxicities (Wu et al. ). Sustained DOX accumulation triggers characteristic clinical manifestations of impaired left ventricular systolic function, cardiac arrhythmia, and congestive heart failure(Bi et al. ; Zhang et al. ). Several underlying mechanisms have been suggested to explain the pathogenesis of DOX-induced cardiotoxicity (DIC), including mitochondrial dysfunction, oxidative stress, inflammatory responses, DNA damage, autophagy derangement, and subsequent cell death(Wu et al. ; Yu et al. ; Yang et al. ; Ding et al. ). Nonetheless, few feasible and effective treatment options are available to prevent or reverse the poor prognosis associated with DIC.
Several studies have demonstrated the contribution of mitochondrial malfunction to the development of adverse cardiac remodeling and failure during DIC progression(Ding et al. ; Dhingra et al. ; Wallace et al. ; Zhang et al. ). It has been shown that DOX administration impairs mitochondrial bioenergetics, unbalances mitochondrial dynamics, dysregulates mitophagy, mitochondrial permeability, transition pore (mPTP) opening, and depolarization, and subsequently causes cell death in cardiomyocytes(Wang et al. ; Dhingra et al. ). Previous studies have shown that DOX accumulation could stimulate mitochondrial dysfunctions including mPTP opening, thereby inducing cytochrome c releasing into the cytoplasm and activating the apoptotic pathway(Marechal et al. ; Du et al. ). Mitophagy is a highly conserved process for removing and recycling damaged mitochondria, depending on PINK1/Parkin- or other autophagic receptor-mediated pathways (Wang et al. ). Several studies have suggested a protective role of mitophagy in DIC cardiomyocytes (Wu et al. ; He et al. ; Wang et al. ). Therefore, mPTP opening and mitophagy are gaining attention as alternative therapeutic strategies for the treatment of DIC.
Tax1 (T-cell leukemia virus type I)-binding protein 1 (TAX1BP1) has been recognized as a crucial player in terminating pro-inflammatory signaling pathways(White et al. ). Previous studies have shown that TAX1BP1 not only facilitates the recruitment of E3 ligases to specific substrates, leading to the formation of a complex structure but also functions as a receptor for autophagy, capturing ubiquitylated substrates for specific degradation through autophagic processes, including mitophagy and xenophagy(Sarango et al. ; Xu et al. ; Verstrepen et al. ). Furthermore, our data revealed that TAX1BP1 contributes to the maintenance of mitochondrial, mitophageal, and cardiac functions. However, the role of TAX1BP1 in DIC-treated cardiomyocytes remains unclear.
Therefore, the objective of this study was to assess the influence of TAX1BP1 overexpression on DIC and its molecular mechanisms, focusing specifically on mPTP opening and mitophagy in cardiomyocytes. Our data suggested that TAX1BP1 levels were reduced in the cardiac tissues of mice subjected to DOX stimulation. TAX1BP1 overexpression alleviated DOX-induced cardiac dysfunction, mitochondrial impairment, and cardiomyocyte mPTP opening. To explore the possible molecular mechanisms behind TAX1BP1 regulation of mitochondrial damage in the presence of DOX, the possible contributions of mitophagy and mPTP opening were examined. Our investigation revealed the ability of TAX1BP1 to interact with mTORC, a pivotal element governing serum- and glucocorticoid-regulated kinase 1 (SGK1) phosphorylation, and voltage-dependent anion channel 1 (VDAC1)-mediated mPTP opening, aimed at preventing cytochrome c release and averting cardiomyocyte apoptosis triggered by DOX. These findings support the potential therapeutic target for TAX1BP1 in cardiomyocyte mitochondrial dysfunction and DIC.

## Materials and methods

## Experimental animal models
To achieve TAX1BP1 overexpression, male C57BL/6J mice (6 weeks of age) received an intravenous injection of recombinant adeno-associated virus serotype-9 (AAV9) containing Flag-TAX1BP1 under the control of the cardiac troponin T (cTNT) promoter (AAV9-cTNT-TAX1BP1) at a concentration of 5 × 1011 particles in 100 µL (diluted in sterile PBS) (Obio Technology, Shanghai, China). Three weeks later, an examination of the efficiency of TAX1BP1 overexpression was carried out in the heart tissues of mice(Xu et al. , ). The mice were randomly subjected to the DOX challenge. Briefly, a model involving intraperitoneal administration of DOX (Beyotime Institute of Biotechnology, Shanghai, China) dissolved in saline at a dose of 4 mg/kg body weight once a week for 5 weeks (resulting in a cumulative dose of 20 mg/kg) was adopted for the study(Yang et al. ). Mice not treated with DOX were administered an equivalent amount of saline solution.

## Evaluation of cardiac function using ultrasound measurements
Isoflurane was used to induce anesthesia in the mice for the duration of the procedure. Heart imaging was conducted by transthoracic echocardiography using a Vevo 2100 system equipped with an 18 MHz linear transducer (Visual Sonics, Toronto, Canada). The end-diastolic diameter (LVEDD) and end-systolic diameter (LVESD) of the left ventricular chamber were measured in anesthetized mice. The ejection fraction (EF) and fractional shortening (FS) were calculated based on recorded measurements(Xu et al. , ).

## Immunohistochemistry
After administering anesthesia by isoflurane, the hearts of the mice were subjected to exercise and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 24 h. Subsequently, the specimens were encased in paraffin, tissue samples were cut into sections, and treated with 5% BSA for blocking. After that, they were exposed to the primary antibody TAX1BP1 (1:50) overnight at 4 °C. Next, a secondary antibody labeled with horseradish peroxidase (1:200) was added. Cardiac fibrosis was evaluated using Masson’s trichrome staining, whereas the estimation of the cardiomyocyte cross-sectional area was based on wheat germ agglutinin staining (Sigma-Aldrich, USA). Quantification of positively stained regions or the extent of fibrosis was conducted using the Image J software (National Institute of Health, Version 1.8)(Xu et al. , ).

## Evaluation of myocardial apoptosis
To evaluate myocardial apoptosis, TUNEL staining was performed using an In Situ Cell Death Detection Kit (Roche, Basel, Switzerland). Apoptosis levels were assessed by counting the number of TUNEL-positive nuclei per 100 cell nuclei based on microscopic images captured using a Leica microscope (40× objective)(Xu et al. ).

## Isolation and treatment of cardiomyocytes
The isolation of adult murine cardiomyocytes (AMCMs) was performed using a previously established method(Xu et al. ). Following the anesthesia of adult mice and removal of their hearts, enzymatic perfusion with collagenase I (Worthington, Lakewood, USA) was performed. Subsequently, upon gradual restoration of extracellular Ca2+, the AMCMs were seeded onto laminin (5 µg/mL, Sigma-Aldrich, USA). A successful outcome was determined by the attainment of 80–90% rod-shaped cardiomyocytes.
Neonatal mouse cardiomyocytes (NMCMs) were isolated as described previously (Yu et al. ). Briefly, hearts from 1 d-old C57BL/6 mice were excised and subjected to collagenase I digestion. Following 90 min of selective pre-plating to eliminate fibroblasts, NMCMs were seeded onto culture plates coated with gelatin at a density of approximately 2 × 105 cells per cm2. Cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum albumin (Gibco, Waltham, MA, USA).
Adenoviruses carrying LacZ and TAX1BP1 were used to transduce cells at the specified multiplicity of infection (MOI), according to the provided guidelines(Xu et al. ). The NMCMs and AMCMs were exposed to DOX at a specified concentration (dissolved in DMSO) for 24 h. The cells were treated with 3-methyladenine (3-MA) (M9281, Sigma-Aldrich, 5 mmol/L) for 18 h to inhibit autophagic induction. Cells were treated with 5 µmol/L GSK650394 (MedChemExpress, New Jersey, USA), an SGK1 GTPase inhibitor, for 2 h to evaluate the potential involvement of SGK1 with or without ad-TAX1BP1 treatment over 48 h. Cells were cultured in a serum-free medium with dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) used as a control.

## Assessment of mitochondrial function
Tetrame thylrhodamine methyl ester (TMRM; Thermo Fisher Scientific) was used to assess mitochondrial membrane potential (MMP)(Xu et al. , ). Mitochondrial reactive oxygen species (ROS) were evaluated with 2′,7′-dichlorofuorescein-diacetate (DCFH-DA, Beyotime Institute of Biotechnology, Shanghai, China). Laser confocal microscopy (Leica, Heidelberg, Germany) was used to investigate ROS fluorescence (Xu et al. ). mPTP opening was measured by incubating cardiomyocytes with calcein-acetoxymethyl ester (Calcein/AM, 5 µM) and Cobalt Chloride (CoCl2, 5 mmol/L) for 30 min, according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The loss of green fluorescence indicated the opening of the mPTP.

## Evaluation of mitophagy using the mt-keima assay and GFP-RFP-LC3 assay
Mitophagy was detected using mt-keima (HanBio Technology, Shanghai, China), a lysosomal protease-resistant fluorescent probe, with its excitation wavelength shifting from 457 to 561 nm in the acidic environment of lysosomes(Xu et al. , ). Cardiomyocytes were transduced with mt-keima adenoviruses at a MOI of 20 for 48 h. Fluorescent imaging was performed using a laser confocal microscope (Leica, Heidelberg, Germany) and analyzed using ImageJ software.
AMCMs were transduced with RFP-GFP-LC3 adenoviral vector (HanBio Technology Co. Ltd., Shanghai, China). Briefly, isolated AMCMs were cultured with adenoviruses at a final MOI of 20 for 48 h at 37 ◦C. Then, images were obtained through a Leica confocal microscope. Autophagic flux was evaluated by the number of yellow puncta (early autophagosomes) and red puncta (late autolysosomes).

## Immunofluorescence analysis
Cell fixation was performed using 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min. After permeabilizing and blocking for 1 h, the cells underwent overnight incubation at 4 ℃ with specific primary antibodies (Supplemental Table 1). Next, the corresponding Alexa Fluor-conjugated secondary antibodies (Cell Signaling Technology) were applied to the cells for further incubation. Immunofluorescence was performed using a laser confocal microscope equipped with a 63× oil immersion lens and activated by laser excitation at 488 and 543 nm (Leica, Heidelberg, Germany). ImageJ software (Fiji) was used for data analysis.

## Plasmid transfection
AC-16 cells were cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS. Plasmid transfection was initiated when the cells reached 70–80% confluence. TAX1BP1 with a FLAG-tag and mTOR with an HA-tag were inserted into the pcDNA3.1(+) plasmids. The plasmids (Genecefe Biotechnology Corporation, Wuxi, China) were introduced into AC-16 cells using the Lipofectamine 3000 transfection reagent (Invitrogen).

## Mitochondrial purification
Mitochondrial components from the heart tissues were extracted using a mitochondrial isolation kit (ab110168, Abcam), according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Xu et al. ). The heart tissues (30–50 mg) were disrupted in an isolation buffer and homogenized. Subsequently, the resulting pellets were centrifugation at 1,000 g for 10 min. The supernatants were preserved and subsequent centrifuged at 12,000× g for 15 min. This procedure was repeated three times. The sediment was collected and reconstituted using a RIPA buffer and then subjected to analysis using a BCA Protein Assay Kit (Beyotime Institute of Biotechnology, Shanghai, China).

## Duolink proximity ligations analysis (PLA)
Duolink PLA (Sigma) was used to detect protein interactions (< 40 nm) as individual fluorescent dots (Xu et al. , ). After overnight incubation with the specific primary antibodies, the cells were treated with the corresponding mouse-plus and rabbit-minus probes. Imaging using a laser confocal microscope (Leica, Germany) revealed the immunofluorescence.

## Immunoprecipitation and Immunoblot analysis
AC-16 cells overexpressing the candidate proteins (Flag-TAX1BP1, HA-mTOR) were collected using lysis buffer (50 mmol/L Tris-HCl pH 7.5, 150 mmol/L NaCl, and 1% NP-40). Following centrifugation at 12,000 rpm for 30 min, the supernatants were immunoprecipitated with either anti-HA or anti-FLAG antibodies using protein A/G agarose beads. The resulting precipitate was subjected to western blot analysis. Samples (25–50 µg) were analyzed using 10–12% SDS-PAGE, followed by the transfer onto PVDF membranes. After the blocking process, the membranes were exposed to primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C followed by treatment with the HRP-conjugated secondary antibody for 1 h (Supplemental Table 1). Bands were detected using enhanced chemiluminescence luminal reagents (Bio-Rad Laboratories Inc., Hercules, CA, USA), and gray value measurements were conducted using the Image Lab 3.0 software package (National Institutes of Health).

## Statistical analysis
All data presented are represented as the mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). Data were analyzed using the GraphPad Prism 8.0 software (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). A two-tailed Student’s t-test was used to compare the two groups. Multiple groups were compared using one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s test. Statistical significance was set at p<0.05.

## Results

## TAX1BP1 was downregulated in the cardiomyocytes with doxorubicin challenge and heart tissues of mice with DIC
RNA sequencing data (GSE3586) from 13 patients diagnosed with dilated cardiomyopathy (DCM) and 15 patients with non-failing (NF) hearts were analyzed using the R package(Love et al. ). Examination of individual gene levels indicated a decrease in TAX1BP1 expression in heart tissues from patients with DCM compared to that in non-dilated controls (Fig.  A). Moreover, Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway functional enrichment analysis identified differential expression of genes related to “Citrate cycle (TCA cycle)” between DCM and NF hearts (Fig.  B), highlighting the significance of maintaining metabolic balance in heart failure.
 NMCMs were isolated and added with different concentrations of DOX stimulation (0.5, 1, and 2 µM) for 24 h. TAX1BP1 levels decreased in the presence of DOX insult at the dosage of 1 and 2 µM (Fig.  C). Next, a mouse model of DIC was established via intraperitoneal administration of DOX (4 mg/kg, five doses, once per week). The findings indicated a decrease in TAX1BP1 levels in heart tissues after DOX (Fig.  D-E). Immunohistochemical analysis confirmed a similar reduction in TAX1BP1 levels (Fig.  F-G).

## TAX1BP1 overexpression alleviated cardiac remodeling and contracted dysfunction in DIC
To investigate the impact of TAX1BP1 on cardiac function, mice were administered AAV-cTNT-TAX1BP1 or AAV-cTNT-NC via tail vein injections 3 weeks before receiving DOX intraperitoneally. Validation of TAX1BP1 viral transduction was confirmed using western blotting (Fig.  A-B). Five weeks after the DOX challenge, cardiac and morphological characteristics were examined in AAV-cTNT-NC and AAV-cTNT-TAX1BP1 mice. DOX accumulation notably decreased the EF and FS, the effects of which were partially improved by cardiac TAX1BP1 overexpression, with little effect of TAX1BP1 overexpression itself (Fig.  C-D and Table S3). Moreover, the ratio of heart weight normalized to tibial length were remarkedly decreased in DIC mice compared with sham mice, the effect of which was partially reversed by TAX1BP1 overexpression (Fig.  E). Additionally, the mRNA levels of atrial natriuretic peptide (ANP), B-type natriuretic peptide (BNP), and b-myosin heavy chain (β-MHC), markers of HF, were notably elevated following DOX accumulation, but this effect was partially reversed in mice with TAX1BP1 overexpression (Fig.  F). Heart abnormalities were evident in the DIC mice, characterized by a reduction in heart size, which was alleviated by TAX1BP1 overexpression (Fig.  G). Additionally, TAX1BP1 overexpression in the DIC models exhibited a protective role against cardiac fibrosis (Fig.  G-I). Analysis of the cardiomyocyte cross-sectional area revealed a decrease in these parameters following DIC, the effects of which were partially reversed by TAX1BP1 overexpression (Fig.  G, J). TAX1BP1 overexpression did not affect cardiac geometric properties or indicators of HF. Following DIC, a significant increase in apoptosis was detected by TUNEL staining, indicating the reversal of these effects upon TAX1BP1 overexpression. In contrast, the presence of TAX1BP1 alone did not affect apoptosis (Fig.  H, K).
 

## TAX1BP1 overexpression improved mitochondrial dysfunction in doxorubicin-induced cardiotoxicity
To assess the effect of TAX1BP1 on DOX-induced mitochondrial abnormalities, the levels of mitochondrial respiratory electron transport chain complexes I, II, III, IV, and V were analyzed by western blotting. The results revealed a decrease in the levels of respiratory chain components caused by DIC, which was alleviated by TAX1BP1 overexpression (Fig.  A-B). Furthermore, mitochondrial function was assessed in AMCMs isolated from DIC mice, with or without cardiac TAX1BP1 overexpression. Our findings indicated a reduction in MMP and an increase in mitochondrial ROS production following exposure to DIC. These effects were partially reversed by the overexpression of TAX1BP1, whereas the impact of the viral vector alone was minimal (Fig.  C-F).
 Ample evidence indicates that cytochrome c is released from damaged mitochondria, leading to the activation of caspases and eventually to cell death through apoptosis and necrosis(Kook et al. ). Our data showed that cytochrome c levels were notably increased in the cytoplasm of heart tissues from mice with DIC, an effect that was partially counteracted by TAX1BP1 overexpression (Fig.  J-K). These findings reveal that TAX1BP1 transduction can maintain mitochondrial function in cardiomyocytes or the myocardium following DOX accumulation.

## TAX1BP1 overexpression promoted mitophagy in cardiomyocytes of DIC
Suppression of mitophagy leads to the retention of dysfunctional mitochondria, exacerbates mitochondrial ROS production, and impairs mitochondrial function(Tong et al. ; Ajoolabady et al. ). Hence, autophay and mitophagy in cardiomyocytes after DIC was monitored. Our results demonstrated that both LC3 and Parkin levels were elevated in the whole heart tissue lysates from DIC mice, regardless of cardiac TAX1BP1 overexpression (Fig.S3A-C). Additionally, we assessed autophagic flux activity using RFP-GFP-LC3 staining (Fig.S3D-E). Our findings revealed a slight reduction in autophagosomes (RFP-GFP-LC3, yellow dots) and a significant increase in autolysosomes (RFP-LC3, red dots) within cardiomyocytes following DOX stress. Notably, TAX1BP1 overexpression significantly increased the number of autophagosomes while also enhancing the presence of autolysosomes. These results suggest that DOX inhibits autophagic flux and that TAX1BP1 overexpression mitigates the suppression of this process induced by DOX. Then, to investigate the impact of TAX1BP1 on mitophagy in cardiomyocytes under DOX stress, further research was conducted. Our data shown in Fig.  A-C indicated a significant reduction in LC3II and parkin levels in the mitochondrial fractions in the hearts of mice with DIC, which was partially alleviated by TAX1BP1 overexpression. Moreover, further analysis demonstrated a decreased mt-keima ratio in adult mouse cardiomyocytes from DIC mice, the effects of which were mitigated by TAX1BP1 overexpression. TAX1BP1 overexpression alone had little discernible effect on mitophagy (Fig.  D-E). Furthermore, colocalization between LC3, parkin and the mitochondrial marker (Tom20) was assessed using immunofluorescence. Our data indicate an increase in total LC3 and Parkin levels within DOX-stimulated cardiomyocytes; however, colocalization between LC3 and Tom20 as well as between Parkin and Tom20 was reduced in these cells treated with DOX. Importantly, TAX1BP1 overexpression improved the colocalization of either LC3 or Parkin with Tom20 under conditions of DOX stimulation (Fig.  F-G and Fig.S4). Collectively, these findings indicated that DOX inhibited mitophagy within cardiomyocytes while TAX1BP1 overexpression counteracted the suppression of mitophagy induced by DOX.
 

## TAX1BP1-offered protective role on myocardial function in DIC was canceled by 3-MA administration
To examine whether TAX1BP1 is involved in promoting mitophagy in cardiomyocytes under DOX insult, 3-MA treatment was used to block autophagosome formation in cardiomyocytes with or without TAX1BP1 overexpression. The data indicated that the protective effect of TAX1BP1 on mitophagy was abolished following 3-MA administration, as evidenced by alterations in LC3II and parkin levels in the mitochondrial fractions, along with changes in the mt-keima ratio in cardiomyocytes under DOX accumulation conditions (Fig.  A-E). Furthermore, our results revealed a decreased MMP in NMCMs following DOX challenge, with partial mitigation of these effects through TAX1BP1 overexpression. However, 3-MA treatment prevented the beneficial effects of TAX1BP1 on mitochondrial function in cardiomyocytes treated with DOX (Fig.  F-G).
 

## TAX1BP1 overexpression promoted mTORC2 complex formation in cardiomyocytes of DIC
TAX1BP1 was identified as an important receptor to participate in mitophagy and as an autophagy receptor to sequester ubiquitylated substrates for degradation(White et al. ). TAX1BP1 also provides critical amino acids that activate mTOR complex formation and enable the metabolic transition of immune cells(Whang et al. ). It has been suggested that TAX1BP1 overexpression promoted mitophagy in DIC cardiomyocytes. However, the effect of TAX1BP1 on mTOR complex formation in DIC remains unknown. To determine the possible role of TAX1BP1 in mTOR complex formation in cardiomyocytes following DOX stimulation, a thorough investigation of the direct interaction between TAX1BP1 and mTOR was conducted to uncover potential mechanisms. Our results revealed a robust binding interaction between TAX1BP1 and mTOR in cardiomyocytes using co-IP, IF, and PLA analyses (Fig.  A-C). Protein levels of mTORC1 and mTORC2 complexes were also evaluated in DIC with or without TAX1BP1 overexpression. Our data suggested that the levels of the mTORC1 complex, including Raptor, p-S473-AKT/AKT, and p-S6K/S6K, were notably increased in cardiomyocytes following DOX exposure, whereas the levels of mTOR, Raptor, p-S473-AKT/AKT, and p-S6K/S6K were unaffected by TAX1BP1 overexpression in cardiomyocytes upon DOX stress. However, the levels of the mTORC2 complex, including Rictor and p-SGK1/SGK1, were significantly downregulated in the myocardium of DIC mice, and these effects were reversed in response to TAX1BP1 overexpression (Fig.  D-J). These findings indicated that mTORC2 complex formation may be upregulated by TAX1BP1 overexpression in DIC.
 

## TAX1BP1 regulated mPTP opening via mTORC2-SGK1-VDAC1 pathway
mTORC2 was involved in the regulation of mitochondrial permeability through physical interactions of mTORC2 with the regulatory molecules of the mitochondrial permeability transition pore (mPTP), negatively regulating the abundance of the outer mitochondrial membrane protein VDAC1 through SGK1(Zhou et al. ). The mPTP opening, which is regulated by VDAC1, adenine nucleotide translocators (ANT), and cyclophilin D (CypD), a polyprotein complex embedded in mitochondrial membranes, permits the diffusion of molecules to flow freely through the mitochondrial membrane(Kwong and Molkentin ). Recent evidence has revealed that mPTP opening also plays a crucial role in mitochondrial quality control as mPTP opening depolarizes mitochondria, promotes mitochondrial ROS accumulation, cellular apoptosis, and necrotic cell death(Kwong and Molkentin ; Song et al. ). Thus, the levels of mPTP opening-related proteins, including VDAC1, ANT, and CypD, were evaluated in DIC with or without TAX1BP1 overexpression. Our data suggested that the levels of VDAC1 dimerization were notably increased in the myocardium upon DOX exposure, the effects of which were mitigated by TAX1BP1 overexpression. However, ANT and CypD levels were unaffected in the hearts of TAX1BP1 overexpression with or without DOX stress (Fig.  A-D). The mPTP opening was measured by loading cardiomyocytes with calcein-acetoxymethyl ester (calcein/AM) and CoCl2 fluorescence-quenching methods (Sulkshane et al. ). Cells subjected to DOX stress exhibited lower calcein fluorescence than those treated with PBS, indicating mPTP opening. TAX1BP1 overexpression alleviated the suppression of calcein fluorescence intensity, indicating that TAX1BP1 overexpression improved mPTP opening in cells upon DOX treatment (Fig.  E-F).
 GSK650394, an SGK1 GTPase inhibitor, was used to evaluate the potential involvement of the mTORC2-pSGK1-VDAC1 pathway in TAX1BP1-induced mPTP closure in DIC. Our results showed that p-SGK1/SGK1 levels were suppressed, and VDAC1 dimerization or aggregation was upregulated in heart tissues upon DOX challenge, which was attenuated by TAX1BP1 overexpression. Interestingly, GSK650394 overtly inhibited the effects of TAX1BP1 on SGK1 phosphorylation and VDAC1 aggregation (Fig.  G-J). Consistently, the administration of GSK650394 also reversed the effects of TAX1BP1 overexpression on collapsed MMP, cytochrome C release into the cytoplasm, and apoptosis in cardiomyocytes following DOX challenge (Fig.  H-L). These findings indicated that TAX1BP1 overexpression promotes the formation of the mTORC2 complex and the activation of SGK1, which suppresses the aggregation of VDAC1 and inhibits mPTP opening, resulting in the maintenance of mitochondrial homeostasis.

## TAX1BP1 interacted with mTOR via its SKICH domain
Given that TAX1BP1 may regulate mPTP opening depending on its interaction with mTOR, possible mechanisms of the TAX1BP1-mTOR interaction were explored, and the regulation of mTORC2-SGK1-VDAC1 in the presence of TAX1BP1 overexpression in cardiomyocytes challenged with DOX was noted. TAX1BP1 contains several distinct domains, including the SKIP carboxyl homology (SKICH) domain (aa 1–144), LC3-interacting region (LIR, aa 150–180), coiled coil (aa 185–589), and zinc finger (ZF) domain (aa 727–780) (Fig.  A)(Fu et al. ). To this end, FLAG-tagged TAX1BP1 mutants targeting the SKICH and ZF domains were constructed.
 Following the transfection of AC-16 cells with truncated versions of Flag-tagged TAX1BP1 plasmids along with HA-tagged mTOR plasmids, CO-IP and PLA analyses were conducted. Our data revealed that the TAX1BP1 segment (aa 1–144) was responsible for the TAX1BP1-mTOR interaction without a viable contribution from the ZF segments of TAX1BP1 (aa 727–780) (Fig.  B-C). Next, the mPTP opening was determined in DOX-challenged AC-16 cells in the presence of the mutant plasmids. The data in Fig.  D-E showed that DOX overtly increased mPTP opening with an improved response upon TAX1BP1 full-length (FL) overexpression. Deletion of the SKICH domain of TAX1BP1 (aa 1–144) failed to duplicate TAX1BP1 (FL)-evoked responses. These findings suggested that TAX1BP1 exerts an mPTP-opening response through the interaction of its SKICH domain with mTOR.

## Discussion
This study highlights the significant role of TAX1BP1, an autophagic receptor, in protection against DIC. Our results revealed reduced TAX1BP1 levels in patients with DCM and murine hearts following DIC. TAX1BP1 overexpression effectively improved cardiac contraction dysfunction in DIC mice by improving mitochondrial dysfunction and rescuing mitophagy to attenuate DIC. Mechanistically, our data revealed that TAX1BP1 interacts with mTOR through its SKICH domain and promotes the formation of the mTORC2 complex, which activates SGK1 phosphorylation, suppresses VDAC1 dimerization, and inhibits mPTP opening, resulting in the maintenance of mitochondrial homeostasis in DIC cardiomyocytes (Fig.  F). This study suggests the potential therapeutic avenue involving TAX1BP1 for managing DIC by modulating mitophagy and ameliorating mPTP opening in cardiomyocytes.
Cumulative evidence suggests that the clinical application of DOX, a potent chemotherapeutic agent utilized for various tumors and hematological malignancies, is hampered by its cardiotoxicity and heart failure(Bosman et al. ). This results in cardiac toxicity and the development of heart failure, ultimately leading to chronic DIC, typically presenting after an accumulation of treatment over a median of 7 years, characterized by thinning of ventricular walls, progressive decline of the left ventricular EF, and ultimately HF(Wu et al. ). Published investigations indicate several mechanisms have been postulated for DIC, including DNA damage, abnormalities in mitochondrial structure, disruption of mitophagy, mPTP opening, oxidative stress, and multiple forms of cell death. Impairment of mitochondrial function may be a major contributing factor to DIC pathogenesis (Wu et al. ; Yu et al. ; Zhang et al. ; Bosman et al. ).
DOX stress induces mPTP opening, mitochondrial ROS production, mitochondrial calcium overload, and necrotic cell death. Support for this concept is in our findings, as well as in the successful improvement of DOX-induced mitochondrial dysfunction and mPTP opening by cardiomyocyte-specific overexpression of TAX1BP1 using AAV9-mediated delivery, without notable adverse effects. Previous studies have highlighted the fundamental significance of mitophagy in DIC. Mitophagy, a specialized form of autophagy, removes mitochondrial fragments via activated mPTP from DOX-challenged cardiac myocytes. Numerous investigations have demonstrated a decline in mitophagy mediated by the PINK1/Parkin pathway in the context of DOX-induced cardiac toxicity(Hoshino et al. ; Hull et al. ). This results in the restoration of mitophagy and mitigation of DOX cardiotoxicity through the upregulation of parkin expression in cardiomyocytes (Hoshino et al. ; Hull et al. ; Yin et al. ). To date, the precise mechanisms linking the mPTP to mitophagy regulation in DIC have not been clearly elucidated. Thus, exploring alternative mechanisms and pharmacotherapies is imperative to prevent DOX cardiotoxicity.
TAX1BP1, an autophagy receptor, suppresses inflammatory reactions. A previous study by our group also showed that TAX1BP1 overexpression attenuated myocardial ischemic injury by alleviating mitochondrial dysfunction and inhibiting inflammasome activation in cardiomyocytes(Xu et al. ). Despite the progress made in understanding the significance of TAX1BP1 in maintaining mitochondrial homeostasis, further studies are warranted to better understand the role of TAX1BP1 in mitochondrial quality control in DIC. Our current study suggests that cardiac TAX1BP1 overexpression improves cardiac contractile and mitochondrial functions. Further investigation revealed that TAX1BP1 inhibits mPTP opening by promoting mTORC2 complex formation and inhibiting VDAC1 aggregation. A protective role for TAX1BP1 in mitochondrial dysfunction and autophagy in STZ-induced diabetic cardiomyopathy has been proposed(Xiao et al. ). However, TAX1BP1 induced cardiomyocyte apoptosis, which was initiated by aortic banding surgery and angiotensin II-induced HF(Wu et al. ). These findings suggest that TAX1BP1 functions in the heart under different stress conditions. During the progression of pressure overload-induced HF, the authors showed that cardiomyocyte autophagy increased, and TAX1BP1-increased autophagy may further exacerbate cell death in the pressure overload-induced HF model by angiotensin II(Wu et al. ). During the chronic accumulation of DOX, our results suggested that autophagy and mitophagy were significantly downregulated in cardiomyocytes and TAX1BP1-mediated mitophagy activation alleviated mitochondrial and cardiac dysfunction, which is consistent with our previous study.
Furthermore, an interaction between TAX1BP1 and mTOR has been identified, which plays a vital role in the regulation of the insulin-PI3K-Akt signaling pathway and contributes to cell survival(Sciarretta et al. ; Heikamp et al. ). There are two mTOR complexes, mTOR complex 1 (mTORC1) and mTOR complex 2 (mTORC2), which are controlled by distinct companion proteins and play different roles in cellular physiopathologic mechanisms (Laplante and Sabatini ). The mTORC1 complex comprises Raptor, which activates p70S6K/S6 and facilitates protein translation and synthesis, resulting in the stimulation of cellular growth(Laplante and Sabatini ; Sciarretta et al. ). The mTORC2 complex comprises a rapamycin-insensitive companion of TOR (Rictor) that activates Akt and PKC, and is recognized for its role in the regulation of cardiomyocyte cell survival and energy metabolism(Heikamp et al. ; Laplante and Sabatini ). Previous studies have shown that mTOR regulation induces protective effects in the heart(Sciarretta et al. , ). mTORC2 is known to maintain mitochondrial function at the ER membrane associated with mitochondria(Betz et al. ). SGK1 has been reported to localize to mitochondria under stress, which can be activated by phosphorylation at serine 422 by mTORC2(Saha et al. ). The regulation of mitochondrial permeability by mTORC2 and SGK1 involves a physical interaction between mTORC2 and mPTP regulatory molecules, which modulate the levels of VDAC1 at the outer mitochondrial membrane via SGK1(Zhou et al. ). Our data indicated that cardiac overexpression of TAX1BP1 upregulates the interaction between TAX1BP1 and mTOR, subsequently promoting mTORC2 complex formation and activating the SGK1-VDAC1 pathway, which further confirmed the involvement of mTORC2 in regulating mPTP opening. Taken together, the use of AAV to deliver TAX1BP1 may offer a potential treatment option for cardiomyopathy in DIC.
Study limitations: Firstly, AAV9 was employed to induce TAX1BP1 overexpression in mouse hearts through intravenous injection in this study. Thus, transgenic mice would be warranted for the gain-of-function study for TAX1BP1 in DIC. Secondly, further research is needed to investigate the protective role of TAX1BP1 against DIC by first administering DOX and subsequently delivering AAV-TAX1BP1 overexpression. This approach aligns with the therapeutic effects expected from TAX1BP1 supplementation following clinical DOX accumulation. Additionally, TAX1BP1 was shown to interact with mTOR via its SKICH domain, more concrete structural details would be needed with more sitespecific mutation studies. Furthermore, the levels of TAX1BP1 could not be validated in human heart samples, and RNA sequencing datasets from cardiac tissue of patients experiencing doxorubicin-induced cardiotoxicity were unavailable in the GEO database due to significant challenges in accessing human samples. Last but not least, the effects of TAX1BP1 were not assessed in tumor-bearing mouse model of DIC.

## Conclusion
Overall, our results provide convincing evidence that TAX1BP1 provides defense against DIC and mitochondrial injury via the inhibition of mPTP opening and upregulation of mitophagy in cardiomyocytes. Mechanistically, TAX1BP1 binds and recruits mTOR to the mitochondria to activate the mTORC2-SGK1 pathway and inhibit VDAC1 aggregation, thus preventing mPTP opening and cardiomyocyte apoptosis during DOX challenge. Further research is warranted to elucidate the cardiac phenotype in TAX1BP1 knockout or transgenic mice. The development of pharmaceuticals targeting TAX1BP1 is crucial for the potential creation of novel small-molecule treatments, presenting a promising approach to the management of DIC.
