## Abstract
Diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (DLBCL) is the most common subtype of non-Hodgkin lymphoma and exhibits significant molecular heterogeneity, leading to varied treatment responses among patients. Although Chimeric Antigen Receptor T-cell therapy (CAR-T) has made remarkable progress in relapsed/refractory (R/R) DLBCL, particularly in patients resistant to standard treatments, the therapeutic efficacy of CAR-T therapy remains inconsistent for high-risk subtypes, such as double-hit/triple-hit lymphoma (DHL/THL) and TP53 mutations. This paper focuses on the molecular subtyping of DLBCL and its application in CAR-T therapy. By analyzing factors such as metabolic stress, immune evasion, and epigenetic regulation, this study reveals how these mechanisms influence the function and efficacy of CAR-T cells. Furthermore, it discusses strategies for overcoming treatment challenges in high-risk subtypes through molecular profiling, metabolic modulation, epigenetic interventions, and multi-target combination therapies. The paper also explores future research directions in CAR-T cell engineering and immune evasion mechanisms. This research provides a novel theoretical framework for personalized treatment and offers valuable insights for clinical treatment strategies for high-risk DLBCL patients.

## Introduction
Diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (DLBCL) is the most common and aggressive subtype of non-Hodgkin lymphoma (NHL), and the heterogeneity in its clinical presentation and molecular characteristics poses a significant challenge for optimizing treatment strategies. Despite the significant improvement in patient survival outcomes with the R-CHOP (Rituximab, Cyclophosphamide, Hydroxydaunorubicin, Vincristine, and Prednisone) regimen, approximately 30%–40% of high-risk or relapsed patients still experience treatment failure. This is particularly evident in molecular subtypes characterized by double-hit/triple-hit lymphoma (DHL/THL), double-expressor lymphoma (DEL), and TP53 mutations, which have a five-year survival rate of less than 30% [ – ]. This phenomenon highlights the limitations of traditional treatment strategies in addressing the molecular complexity of DLBCL. In recent years, molecular profiling technologies—such as gene expression profiling, whole-genome sequencing, and single-cell multi-omics—have progressively revealed the extensive heterogeneity of DLBCL [ ]. From the classic Germinal Center B-cell-like (GCB) and Activated B-cell-like (ABC) subtypes to the integrated C1–C5 classification system, molecular profiling not only elucidates key pathways in drug resistance mechanisms but also unveils the remodeling of the tumor microenvironment (TME) through metabolic-epigenetic interactions [ – ]. However, the challenge remains in effectively translating these molecular profiling insights into precision therapeutic strategies, which continues to be a persistent issue in translational research. Chimeric Antigen Receptor T-cell therapy (CAR-T) has emerged as a breakthrough treatment for relapsed/refractory (R/R) DLBCL, yet its efficacy still varies significantly among high-risk subtypes. Studies indicate that these variations are closely related to metabolic stress (such as lactate accumulation and lipid metabolism imbalance) and epigenetic silencing (such as DNA hypermethylation and histone modification abnormalities) in the TME [ , ]. For example, in the DHL/THL subtype, mutations in MYC, BCL2, and BCL6 significantly impair CAR-T efficacy, while the TP53 mutations commonly associated with DLBCL also exacerbate CAR-T cell exhaustion [ , – ]. These findings suggest that overcoming the therapeutic bottleneck requires the use of molecular profiling approaches to clarify drug resistance mechanisms and design corresponding targeted combination strategies. This review summarizes the progress of DLBCL CAR-T therapy guided by molecular profiling, with a focus on the interplay of metabolic-epigenetic mechanisms in high-risk subtypes. Furthermore, this paper explores the translational potential of multi-target designs, combination interventions, and novel engineering strategies, aiming to establish a theoretical framework for personalized treatment and propose future research directions.

## Molecular subtyping system of DLBCL
DLBCL is a highly heterogeneous and aggressive B-cell malignancy, with its molecular heterogeneity profoundly influencing clinical behavior and treatment outcomes. In 2000, Alizadeh et al. first classified DLBCL into two major subtypes through whole-genome expression profiling: the GCB subtype and the ABC subtype [ ]. The GCB subtype is often associated with driver gene mutations such as EZH2 (approximately 30%), BCL2, and CREBBP, and typically responds well to standard R-CHOP chemotherapy, with a three-year survival rate of approximately 75%. In contrast, the ABC subtype is characterized by persistent activation of the NF-κB signaling pathway, high-frequency MYD88 (approximately 30%) and CD79B (approximately 20%) mutations, and poorer prognosis, with a three-year survival rate of approximately 58% [ , , ]. Although the traditional cell-of-origin (COO) classification is widely used in clinical practice, its ability to identify high-risk subtypes, such as DHL/THL and TP53 mutations, is limited, restricting its value in the context of precision medicine [ ].
With the advancement of multi-omics technologies, the DLBCL classification system has evolved from a one-dimensional model based on gene expression to an integrated framework incorporating genomics, transcriptomics, and immune microenvironment characteristics. In 2018, Schmitz et al. proposed a five-class system through whole-exome sequencing, including MCD (MYD88/CD79B mutations), BN2 (BCL6/NOTCH2 translocations), N1 (NOTCH1 mutations), EZB (EZH2/BCL2 mutations), and A53 (TP53 mutations) subtypes [ ]. This model clarified the relationship between genetic mutations and treatment response, laying the foundation for the development of targeted therapies. Among them, MCD and N1 subtypes have the worst prognosis (five-year survival rate below 40%), while EZB and BN2 subtypes have relatively better prognoses. In the same year, Chapuy et al. proposed the C1–C5 classification system based on genomic architecture and immune microenvironment characteristics, emphasizing the critical role of the TME in the pathogenesis of DLBCL [ ]. The C1 subtype (oxidative phosphorylation pathway activation with BCL6/NOTCH2 mutations) and the C2 subtype (TP53 mutations with genomic instability) are highly associated with the ABC phenotype, while the C3 subtype (BCL2/EZH2 mutations with active glycolysis) and C4 subtype (RAS/JAK–STAT pathway activation) exhibit GCB characteristics. The C3 subtype overlaps significantly with DHL/THL, and its MYC/BCL2 co-expression significantly weakens CAR-T efficacy by synergistically inhibiting the mitochondrial apoptosis pathway [ , ]. Furthermore, the C2 subtype is characterized by significant immune cell infiltration, suggesting its potential for response to immune checkpoint inhibitors such as programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1) antibodies [ ]. In 2020, Wright et al. developed the LymphGen tool based on the Schmitz model, adding the ST2 subtype (SGK1/TET2 mutations), which further enhanced the molecular resolution of DLBCL classification [ ].
The Schmitz classification focuses on genetic driving mechanisms, the Chapuy model incorporates immune microenvironment factors, and LymphGen further optimizes the classification framework, improving subtype identification accuracy and clinical relevance (Table . Overview of the Evolution of DLBCL Subtyping Systems). DLBCL molecular subtyping is gradually transitioning from the expression-based COO classification to a multi-dimensional model integrating mutation profiles and immune landscape features, laying the foundation for combined therapeutic strategies based on metabolic characteristics [ ].
 

## High-Risk subtypes of DLBCL

## Subtypes driven by single mutations

## A53 subtype
The A53 subtype is characterized by TP53 mutations and deletions. The TP53 gene, located on the short arm of chromosome 17 (17p13.1), is a critical tumor suppressor gene that primarily functions by encoding the p53 protein. The p53 protein plays an essential role under cellular stress, regulating cell-cycle progression, DNA repair, apoptosis, senescence, and autophagy [ , ]. It inhibits CDK4/6-mediated cell-cycle progression by activating p21 (CDKN1A), promotes intrinsic apoptosis through interactions with BCL2, BCL-XL, and BAX, and induces extrinsic apoptosis by activating TRAIL receptors and FAS [ , ]. TP53 mutations—including point mutations and 17p deletions—are among the most common genetic alterations in human cancers, occurring in approximately 20%–30% of DLBCL cases [ , ]. These mutations are typically associated with an adverse prognosis [ ]. Most TP53 mutations occur within the DNA-binding domain of p53, and immunohistochemistry reveals that p53 protein overexpression or loss correlates with TP53 mutation status [ , ]. Patients harboring mutant p53 exhibit significantly lower remission rates after R-CHOP therapy than those with wild-type p53, suggesting that immunohistochemistry may serve as an effective tool for predicting TP53 mutations and therapeutic response. Functionally, TP53 mutations disrupt G1/S and G2/M checkpoints, reduce DNA repair capacity, and increase genomic instability, thereby enhancing immune evasion, promoting clonal heterogeneity, and inducing T-cell exhaustion. Loss of p53 not only impairs tumor differentiation but also exacerbates T-cell exhaustion by up-regulating programmed death ligand 1 (PD-L1) and down-regulating Major Histocompatibility Complex (MHC)-II, thereby driving tumor progression. Studies have shown that p53 loss synergizes with constitutive NF-κB activation and immune-evasion mechanisms in ABC-type DLBCL [ , ]. Clinically, TP53-mutated DLBCL shows poor response to standard therapies (such as R-CHOP), with median overall survival (OS) approximately 40% shorter than that of wild-type patients [ , , ]. Moreover, TP53 mutations frequently coexist with MYC overexpression, further exacerbating therapeutic resistance [ – ]. The double-hit feature involving TP53 mutations predicts poor survival in GCB-type DLBCL patients receiving R-CHOP therapy, with a subset progressing to relapsed or refractory disease, thereby validating TP53 mutation as an independent adverse prognostic factor in DLBCL [ , ].

## N1 subtype
The N1 subtype is primarily driven by activating mutations in NOTCH1, leading to persistent activation of the NOTCH signaling pathway and excessive release of the NICD fragment. The released NICD enters the nucleus, binds to transcription factors, and initiates the transcription of downstream genes, such as HES1 and HEY1 [ ]. The activation of these genes promotes cell-cycle progression and reduces apoptosis, thereby driving tumorigenesis and progression. Notably, the expression of the HES1 gene inhibits the function of CD8⁺ T cells, thereby weakening their anti-tumor effects [ , ]. NOTCH1 signaling also modulates regulatory T cell (Treg) function by promoting the secretion of IL-10, further suppressing immune responses and weakening tumor-specific immune responses, thus leading to reduced efficacy of immunotherapies, particularly CAR-T therapy [ , ]. Furthermore, activation of NOTCH1 is associated with increased secretion of PD-L1 in macrophages, which further inhibits T-cell anti-tumor effects and promotes immune evasion [ ]. This immune evasion characteristic renders N1 subtype DLBCL patients resistant to standard therapies (such as R-CHOP) and immunotherapies, leading to poor prognosis [ ]. To overcome this immune evasion mechanism, several therapeutic strategies have been proposed. For instance, inhibiting γ-secretase or targeting the IL-33 signaling axis may reverse Treg-mediated immune suppression, thereby enhancing tumor-specific immune responses [ ]. Additionally, Chidamide (an HDAC inhibitor) restores T-cell anti-tumor immunity by inhibiting the NOTCH1/NFATC1 signaling pathway, showing particular potential in ABC DLBCL [ ]. Genomic screening has also identified sarco/endoplasmic reticulum calcium ATPase (SERCA) as a potential therapeutic target for cancers associated with NOTCH1 mutations [ ]. NOTCH1 mutations are closely associated with poor prognosis in DLBCL and may serve as an important prognostic marker, providing further insights into therapeutic directions and prognostic assessment tools for clinical practice [ ].

## Subtypes defined by multiple mutational mechanisms

## MCD subtype
The MCD subtype is defined by the simultaneous presence of MYD88 and CD79B mutations. These mutations synergistically promote tumor cell proliferation and immune evasion through distinct signaling pathways. The MYD88 mutation activates the NF-κB and JAK2-STAT3 pathways via the TLR/IL-1R signaling axis, thereby enhancing tumor cell proliferation and anti-apoptotic properties. The CD79B mutation enhances the activity of the B-cell receptor (BCR) signaling pathway, further promoting tumor cell growth and survival [ , , ]. The synergy between these mutations leads to the sustained activation of these signaling pathways, ultimately resulting in immune evasion characteristics and enhanced proliferative capacity in tumor cells [ ]. The JAK2-STAT3 pathway plays a crucial role in this process by promoting tumor progression through the upregulation of oncogenes and further enhancing immune evasion by inhibiting immune mediator function and increasing immunosuppressive factors [ ]. Moreover, STAT3 acts as a key signaling molecule in tumor-immune cell interactions, regulating immune cell functions and thereby influencing the immune response [ ]. Due to the sustained activation of these mutations and signaling pathways, patients with the MCD subtype typically exhibit elevated expression of PD-L1 and T-cell exhaustion, leading to poor response to standard R-CHOP chemotherapy. The five-year OS rate for the MCD subtype is less than 50% [ ]. The MCD subtype exhibits distinct molecular mechanisms and clinical features, and targeting these mutations could enhance therapeutic efficacy. The molecular heterogeneity of CD79 suggests that combined targeting of CD79A and CD79B may enhance the therapeutic efficacy against B-cell tumors [ ]. These mutations hold significant clinical relevance, influencing both treatment strategies and prognosis [ , ]. Recent studies have shown that Bruton’s tyrosine kinase (BTK) inhibitors, such as ibrutinib, effectively block the BCR-NF-κB signaling axis, and when combined with R-CHOP, the overall response rate (ORR) increases from 70.6% to 100%, with a one-year progression-free survival (PFS) rate rising to 88.9% [ ]. As a key regulator in the MCD subtype, STAT3 offers new directions for targeted therapy. Research indicates that STAT3 not only promotes proliferation in tumor cells but also exacerbates DLBCL cell proliferation by regulating the transcriptional activity of CCL4 [ ]. Therefore, targeting the STAT3 signaling pathway holds promise for enhancing the efficacy of immunotherapy. Chidamide, a histone deacetylase (HDAC) inhibitor, induces apoptosis in DLBCL cells by inhibiting the HDACs/STAT3/BCL2 signaling pathway [ ]. Clinical trials of combined BTK inhibitors (such as Acalbrutinib) and STAT3 inhibitors (such as Danvatirsen and AZD9150) in R/R DLBCL have shown significant therapeutic effects, providing clinical evidence supporting combination therapies targeting the STAT3 signaling pathway [ ].

## BN2 subtype
The BN2 subtype belongs to the intermediate-risk molecular cluster, primarily driven by BCL6 translocations and NOTCH2 mutations [ – ]. In the BN2 subtype, dysregulated BCL6 expression inhibits PRDM1-mediated plasma cell differentiation, maintaining tumor cells in a high-proliferation state, thereby promoting malignant tumor progression [ ]. NOTCH2 mutations activate the NOTCH signaling pathway, promoting B-cell survival and proliferation while upregulating immune-suppressive cell populations (such as Treg) and immune checkpoints, thus enhancing immune evasion mechanisms and further driving tumor progression [ – ]. Additionally, studies suggest that the BN2 subtype is associated with the activation of the PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling axis, which may underlie some of its treatment resistance. The sustained activation of this pathway enhances tumor cell proliferation while inhibiting apoptotic pathways, thereby increasing drug resistance [ ]. Although the BN2 subtype typically responds well to R-CHOP therapy, with a five-year OS rate of approximately 65%, its immunosuppressive TME may lead to poor response to CAR-T therapy [ ]. Combining immune checkpoint inhibitors or other immune-enhancing strategies may improve immune therapy responses in BN2 subtype patients. For the treatment of BN2 subtype, drugs targeting the BCL6, PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling axis, and NOTCH2 signaling pathways are under development.

## EZB subtype
The primary features of the EZB subtype are EZH2 mutations and BCL2 gene translocations. EZH2 mutations increase H3K27 trimethylation (H3K27me3), leading to the silencing of genes associated with B-cell differentiation, thereby inhibiting further differentiation of germinal center B cells and causing their stagnation at the germinal center stage [ ]. This epigenetic alteration not only affects normal B-cell function but also promotes tumor cells to maintain a proliferative state, thereby enhancing the malignant characteristics of DLBCL. BCL2 gene translocations typically occur between the BCL2 gene on chromosome 18 and the immunoglobulin heavy chain gene on chromosome 14, resulting in BCL2 overexpression. BCL2 protein inhibits apoptosis, maintaining tumor cell survival and enhancing resistance, thereby driving tumor progression [ , ]. Although the EZB subtype typically responds well to R-CHOP therapy, with a five-year survival rate of approximately 68%, the prognosis of patients significantly worsens when TP53 mutations coexist. TP53 mutations typically lead to cell-cycle dysregulation and increased genomic instability in tumor cells, making patients with the EZB subtype prone to resistance and relapse [ ]. This adverse prognostic feature suggests that a single chemotherapy regimen may not be sufficient to effectively control tumor progression in cases harboring both EZH2 and TP53 mutations. Targeting the epigenetic dysregulation in the EZB subtype has become a new therapeutic direction. EZH2 inhibitors (such as Tazemetostat) restore repressed gene expression by inhibiting the methyltransferase activity of EZH2, promoting B-cell differentiation and inhibiting tumor cell proliferation [ ]. Additionally, BCL2 inhibitors (such as Venetoclax) enhance therapeutic efficacy by restoring programmed cell death, particularly by inhibiting BCL2’s protective function. The combination of both has shown synergistic anti-tumor effects, making it a promising strategy for treating EZB subtype DLBCL [ – ].

## ST2 subtype
The ST2 subtype is typically associated with activating mutations in SGK1 and functional inactivation of TET2, both of which play key roles in treatment resistance [ , ]. SGK1 mutations activate the mTORC2 signaling pathway, promoting phosphorylation of FOXO1/3a and BAD, thereby enhancing cell survival, inhibiting apoptosis, and helping tumor cells evade normal apoptotic mechanisms [ ]. Meanwhile, TET2 inactivation leads to DNA hypermethylation, suppressing the expression of antigen-presentation molecules (such as HLA-DR and CD74) and inducing upregulation of PD-L1, thereby impairing the immune system’s ability to recognize and eliminate tumor cells [ ]. The synergy between these molecular alterations forms a typical immune evasion phenotype, rendering the ST2 subtype significantly resistant to standard immunotherapy and chemotherapy. A novel approach involving multi-target combination therapies has been proposed to address the molecular abnormalities and immunosuppressive features of the ST2 subtype. SGK1 inhibitors (such as Enzastaurin) restore tumor cell sensitivity to apoptosis by blocking the SGK1-FOXO axis, thereby improving therapeutic efficacy [ ]. Demethylating agents (such as Azacitidine and Decitabine) can reverse DNA hypermethylation caused by TET2 inactivation, inhibit PD-L1 overexpression, and enhance the immune system’s ability to recognize tumor cells [ , ]. mTOR inhibitors (such as Everolimus) overcome immune evasion mechanisms by inhibiting SGK1-mTOR co-signaling and show significant therapeutic effects when combined with chemotherapy [ ]. Furthermore, dual inhibition strategies targeting the PI3K/AKT/SGK pathway (such as the combination of PI3K inhibitors like Idelalisib and SGK1 inhibitors) have shown potential efficacy [ ]. The use of immune checkpoint inhibitors helps alleviate immunosuppression, restore immune surveillance, and further enhance the immune system’s ability to eliminate tumor cells [ ].

## Other subtypes
Chromosomal abnormalities involving MYC, BCL2, and/or BCL6 translocations define the most aggressive molecular subtypes of DLBCL, commonly referred to as “double-hit” and “triple-hit” lymphomas. DHL is defined by the coexistence of MYC rearrangement with either BCL2 or BCL6 rearrangement, accounting for approximately 5%−10% of DLBCL cases. THL is defined by the simultaneous translocation of MYC, BCL2, and BCL6, representing a more aggressive subtype, accounting for approximately 1% of DLBCL cases [ , ]. MYC regulates cellular metabolic pathways, promoting ATP production and the synthesis of cellular building blocks, thus fueling cell expansion and DNA replication. MYC mutations play a crucial role by driving uncontrolled cell growth and proliferation [ ]. Overexpression of BCL2 enhances tumor cell survival by inhibiting mitochondrial apoptosis [ , ]. BCL6 translocations maintain the tumor cell proliferative state by inhibiting terminal differentiation factors, such as PRDM1 [ ]. The synergy between these three factors activates multiple oncogenic signaling pathways, collectively shaping a TME characterized by high proliferative capacity and anti-apoptotic traits. Consequently, the THL subtype exhibits a more aggressive clinical phenotype, with a median PFS of only 9.3 months [ ]. Studies have shown that CAR-T cell therapy has become the preferred second-line treatment for high-risk R/R DLBCL patients, while autologous hematopoietic cell transplantation (auto-HCT) remains a treatment option in specific cases [ ]. Furthermore, the combination of DA-EPOCH-R and the oral proteasome inhibitor Ixazomib has shown good efficacy in treating DHL/THL patients, with a 12-month PFS rate of 53% [ ]. Targeted therapeutic strategies show promising potential. BCL2 inhibitors (such as Venetoclax) and inhibitors targeting the NF-κB signaling pathway (such as BTK inhibitors) have emerged as novel therapeutic options, with these targeted agents offering the potential to inhibit tumor cell survival and overcome resistance. Although these emerging therapies demonstrate some clinical potential, further exploration is needed to optimize treatment regimens for high-risk DLBCL subtypes to improve long-term prognosis. ## Epigenetic and metabolic regulation driving differential efficacy of CAR-T therapy in DLBCL
In recent years, the relationship between the molecular subtypes of DLBCL and the efficacy of CAR-T has garnered widespread attention in the scientific community. Increasing evidence suggests that the functional differences of CAR-T cells in different DLBCL subtypes are driven by the complex interplay between epigenetic regulation and metabolic reprogramming. These dual regulatory mechanisms not only shape the immune landscape of the TME but also directly impact the functional state, metabolic adaptability, and in vivo persistence of CAR-T cells—ultimately influencing clinical responses and treatment outcomes. (Fig.  . Mechanisms of CAR-T Cell Therapy Resistance in High-Risk DLBCL).
 At the epigenetic level, common EZH2 mutations in the GCB subtype silence differentiation-related genes by enhancing H3K27me3 modifications, thereby hindering GCB differentiation [ ]. EZH2 also represses multiple genes associated with T-cell effector function and memory formation (such as TBX21, IFNG, and PRF1), thereby impairing the effector function and persistence of CD8⁺ T cells [ ]. Additionally, EZH2 plays a key regulatory role in T cells, modulating anti-tumor immune responses [ ]. These mechanisms enable tumor cells to evade host immune surveillance and immune therapy interventions, thereby increasing treatment difficulty.
Tumor metabolic reprogramming further modulates CAR-T cell efficacy, a phenomenon that is particularly pronounced in the ABC subtype. The TME of this subtype exhibits significantly elevated lactate concentrations (8–12 mM), much higher than the 3–5 mM typically observed in the GCB subtype [ ]. Elevated lactate levels impair ATP production by inhibiting the phosphorylation of the key glycolytic enzyme PKM2, which is essential for energy metabolism [ ]. At the same time, lactate activates the GPR81-dependent mTORC1 signaling pathway, inducing T-cell differentiation into a TIM-3⁺PD-1⁺ exhausted T-cell phenotype [ ]. Moreover, lactate-driven histone lactylation (especially at the H3K18la site) silences the mitochondrial biogenesis regulator PPARGC1A, reducing mitochondrial membrane potential and exacerbating bioenergetic dysfunction and functional impairment in T cells [ ]. A study reported that in the ABC subtype, elevated expression of MCT4, responsible for lactate efflux, was significantly negatively correlated with the peak expansion of CAR-T cells, indicating that impaired lactate clearance is a major metabolic constraint limiting CAR-T efficacy [ , ].

## Progress in CAR-T therapy for high-risk subtypes of DLBCL
DLBCL is the most common type of NHL. Although the standard R-CHOP regimen cures more than 60% of patients, and the addition of Polatuzumab increases PFS from 70% to 76.7%, approximately 40% of patients still experience relapse after first-line treatment [ , ]. Even in patients who undergo salvage autologous stem cell transplantation, the risk of relapse remains high [ , ]. Therefore, new treatment strategies for R/R DLBCL are crucial. In recent years, CAR-T therapy has made significant progress in treating R/R DLBCL. Three FDA-approved CAR-T products—Axicabtagene Ciloleucel (Axi-cel), Tisagenlecleucel (Tisa-cel), and Lisocabtagene Maraleucel (Liso-cel)—have shown strong clinical efficacy in key clinical trials. Studies have shown that the ORR after CAR-T treatment ranges from 52% to 82%, with a complete response rate (CR) of 40% to 52% [ , – ]. The ZUMA-1 study evaluated the efficacy of KTE-C19 (anti-CD19 CAR-T therapy) in refractory DLBCL, with an ORR of 71% and a CR of 57% [ ]. Furthermore, real-world studies have validated similar efficacy. In two multicenter studies in the United States, the best ORR for Axi-cel was 70% and 82%, with CR rates of 50% and 64%, respectively, and adverse events were within manageable levels [ , ]. In a multicenter study in Spain, Tisa-cel achieved an ORR of 60%, a CR rate of 32%, and a median PFS of 3 months [ ]. Therefore, CAR-T therapy has become the new standard treatment for R/R DLBCL, significantly improving patient survival rates. However, patients with certain high-risk subtypes (such as DHL and THL) and those with TP53 mutations have poorer treatment responses and shorter durations of remission.
DHL/THL patients typically present with MYC rearrangements alongside BCL2 and/or BCL6 rearrangements, exhibiting high invasiveness and poor prognosis. These patients show high resistance to conventional chemotherapy and high relapse rates. CAR-T therapy shows superior efficacy compared to traditional treatments in DHL/THL subtypes. In the ZUMA-7 trial, DHL/THL patients receiving Axi-cel had an ORR of 41% and a median PFS of 5.2 months. Although this was lower than in patients without genetic rearrangements (ORR 76%, PFS 14.1 months), it was still superior to standard chemotherapy. In elderly patients (≥ 65 years), the ORR for DHL/THL was 88%, significantly higher than the 52% in the standard treatment group [ ]. Additionally, the ZUMA-12 study showed that Axi-cel as first-line treatment for high-risk DLBCL in DHL/THL patients had an ORR of 89%, CR of 78%, and a 24-month durable remission rate of 71%, supporting the early use of CAR-T in high-risk patients [ ]. The new-generation CAR-T product, Relmacabtagene Autoleucel (Relma-cel), demonstrated an ORR of 60.3% in DHL/THL and DEL patients, with low toxicity rates, suggesting good safety and promising prospects in high-risk DLBCL [ ]. Although CAR-T therapy shows promising efficacy in DHL/THL subtypes, its efficacy remains inferior compared to standard DLBCL patients. Real-world data further confirm this finding. A study from the University of Pennsylvania (n = 50) reported that the ORR for the DEL/DHL group was 53%, comparable to the 55% in the non-high-risk group (p = 0.87), but the median duration of complete remission was significantly shorter in the high-risk group (8.1 months vs. 14.6 months, p = 0.03) [ ]. Additionally, a European multicenter study (n = 70) reported that the CR rate for DHL/DEL patients was 39% at 3 months post-infusion, with a 52% relapse rate within 6 months, significantly higher than the 32% in the non-high-risk group (p = 0.04). These studies emphasize the need to explore maintenance therapy strategies to extend the remission period in high-risk patients [ ]. The poor efficacy of CAR-T therapy in DHL/THL subtypes may be related to the high genomic instability and complex genetic rearrangements of tumor cells. These mutations enable tumor cells to escape immune surveillance under immunotherapy. DHL/THL patients often exhibit a strongly immunosuppressive microenvironment, further diminishing the anti-tumor efficacy of CAR-T cells. Real-world data also show that high-risk patients have significantly shorter complete remission durations and higher relapse risks, which are closely associated with the TME, CAR-T cell persistence, and tumor cell resistance mechanisms.

TP53 mutations are an important adverse prognostic marker in A53 subtype DLBCL, often associated with resistance and shorter survival [ , ]. Several studies have shown that CAR-T therapy demonstrates significant efficacy compared to conventional therapies, offering benefits to patients with TP53 mutations. For example, subgroup analysis of the ZUMA-7 trial showed that TP53-mutated A53 DLBCL patients treated with Axi-cel had a median OS of 30.88 months, significantly higher than the 12.55 months in the standard treatment group [ ]. Additionally, a retrospective analysis by Porpaczy et al. found that although TP53 mutations shorten survival in conventional therapy, the negative impact of TP53 mutations is relatively small in CAR-T treatment, suggesting that CAR-T can help mitigate the poor prognosis associated with TP53 mutations [ ]. However, despite the efficacy of CAR-T therapy in A53 subtype DLBCL, its efficacy remains inferior compared to standard DLBCL patients. The primary reasons are genomic instability and the specific immune microenvironment. TP53-mutated patients often exhibit upregulation of immunosuppressive receptors (such as LAG-3 and TIM-3), leading to T-cell exhaustion, which limits CAR-T cell persistence and cytotoxic activity [ ]. The JULIET study found that the ORR for TP53-mutated patients was only 33%, with a median PFS of 3.8 months, significantly lower than the ORR (68%) and PFS (11.6 months) of TP53 wild-type patients [ ]. Moreover, when TP53 mutations coexist with other genetic abnormalities, such as MYC/BCL2 double expression, CAR-T efficacy is poorer and prognosis worsens [ , ]. Emerging studies suggest that combining CAR-T with autologous stem cell transplantation (ASCT) has been shown to significantly improve prognosis in TP53-mutated patients. Research showed that the 24-month OS in the combination treatment group was 68%, significantly higher than the 32% in the CAR-T-alone group [ ]. This improvement may stem from pre-treatment strategies that deplete immunosuppressive cells (such as Tregs and MDSCs), improving the TME and enhancing CAR-T cell expansion and anti-tumor effects. Dual-target CAR19/22 T-cell therapy has also shown higher remission rates, PFS, and OS in A53 subtype DLBCL patients, either alone or in combination with autologous stem cell transplantation [ , ]. Case reports have also shown that TP53-mutated A53 DLBCL patients achieve complete molecular remission following CD19 CAR-T infusion after autologous stem cell transplantation, with good tolerance [ ]. Therefore, the combination of CAR-T and ASCT may become an effective strategy for improving the prognosis of TP53-mutated A53 DLBCL in the future.

## Future research directions

## New target and multi-target breakthroughs
Antigen loss, T-cell dysfunction, and tumor immune evasion are key factors undermining the long-term efficacy of CAR-T in high-risk DLBCL subtypes [ ]. Therefore, identifying and validating new immunotherapy targets has become a crucial approach to circumvent immune tolerance. CD47, the so-called “don’t eat me” signal, is highly expressed in various B-cell malignancies, and inhibits macrophage-mediated phagocytosis by binding to signal regulatory protein alpha (SIRPα) [ ]. The bispecific antibody TG-1801 (CD19×CD47) can block this interaction, promote macrophage-mediated tumor cell clearance, and demonstrate strong anti-tumor activity in preclinical models [ ]. Similarly, the CD20×CD47 bispecific antibody IMM0306 has shown preliminary efficacy in B-cell NHL, providing a promising complement to non-CAR-T-based immunotherapies [ ]. Moreover, small molecule CD38 inhibitors have been shown to impair the metabolic adaptability of tumor-infiltrating Treg, triggering a strong anti-tumor immune response, providing a theoretical basis for DLBCL target development [ ]. Another promising target is receptor tyrosine kinase-like orphan receptor 1 (ROR1), a highly specific tumor-associated antigen that is expressed at very low levels in normal tissues but is overexpressed in certain DLBCL subgroups. The ROR1 small molecule inhibitor (KAN0441571C) effectively inhibits lymphoma cell proliferation by inducing apoptosis, providing new evidence to support ROR1 as a treatment target for DLBCL [ ]. CAR-T cells targeting ROR1 have shown significant anti-tumor activity in preclinical models, particularly in patients who relapsed after anti-CD19 CAR-T cell therapy [ ]. Additionally, combining CD19, CD3, and co-stimulatory receptors (such as CD2) in a trispecific antibody may enhance T-cell activation through multiple pathways, improving tumor cell clearance in antigenically heterogeneous environments [ ].
Compared to traditional single-target CAR-T cells, multi-target CAR-T cells (such as CD19/CD20, CD19/CD22, CD19/BCMA, CD19/CD38) provide broader antigen coverage, reducing antigen loss or immune evasion due to modulation. CD19/CD20 dual-target CAR-T is one of the earliest and most widely studied combinations. TanCAR7, a next-generation CD19/CD20 dual-target CAR-T product, achieved good results in 87 R/R NHL patients, with an ORR of 78%, and most patients experienced mild to moderate cytokine release syndrome (CRS), highlighting its good safety and efficacy [ ]. CD19/CD20 bispecific CAR-T based on naïve/memory T cells exhibited an ORR of 90% and a CR of 70%, with good safety [ ]. Additionally, CD19/CD20 dual CAR-Ts driven by the Sleeping Beauty transposon system and bidirectional promoters have shown significant anti-tumor activity both in vitro and in vivo, further supporting the potential of dual-target CAR-Ts [ ]. CD19/CD22 dual-target CAR-T has also been extensively studied. In a study by Wei et al., CD19/CD22 CAR-T treatment in R/R DHL patients achieved an ORR of 83.3%, CR rate of 25%, and a 12-month OS rate of 75%. The study also discovered new BCL2 mutations and subclonal evolution associated with T-cell dysfunction, emphasizing the importance of molecular characterization in optimizing CAR-T efficacy [ ]. Further studies have shown that combining CD19/CD22 CAR-T with ASCT significantly improves long-term survival in DHL patients [ ]. In the ZUMA-14 study, the ORR of CD19/CD22 CAR-T increased from 41% to 78%. Single-cell transcriptomic analysis revealed that this dual-target design synergistically activates the PI3K-AKT-mTOR signaling pathway, increasing the proportion of central memory T cells (TCM), thereby prolonging CAR-T cell persistence and reducing relapse of CD19-negative disease [ ]. The novel CD19/CD22 bi-CAR-T (CT120) achieved an ORR of 65.2% and a CR of 56.5% in clinical trials, effectively reducing relapses associated with antigen escape [ ]. A meta-analysis further indicated that CD19/CD22 CAR-T exhibited better partial response (PR) rates and lower incidence of ICANS in hematologic malignancies compared to CD19/CD20 CAR-T [ ]. CD19/BCMA dual-target CAR-T (ARI0003) has shown superior anti-tumor effects compared to conventional CD19 CAR-T in preclinical studies of NHL and has entered phase I clinical trials (NCT06097455), offering a new direction for overcoming resistance caused by CD19 loss [ ]. Additionally, CD19/CD38 tandem CAR-T has demonstrated significant anti-tumor activity and good efficacy, especially in CD19 or CD38-positive cells. This strategy is compatible with existing clinical production processes and offers an effective solution to overcome immune evasion after CD19 CAR-T therapy [ ].

## Optimization and innovation of CAR-T structure
The continued development of CAR-T therapy largely depends on the optimization of its structure, particularly in enhancing T-cell potency, extending in vivo persistence, and reducing treatment-related toxicities. In the CAR structure, the co-stimulatory domain plays a central role in regulating T-cell activation, metabolism, and persistence. Different co-stimulatory molecules have distinct effects: CD28 induces rapid effector T-cell activation, suitable for clinical scenarios requiring rapid tumor clearance, whereas 4-1BB supports T-cell persistence and memory formation, making it ideal for treatment strategies aimed at long-term remission [ ]. Therefore, tailoring the co-stimulatory domain to specific clinical contexts is crucial for balancing efficacy and safety. To enhance treatment safety, inducible “suicide switch” systems have become a key safety mechanism. Caspase-9 (iCasp9) is the most widely used system, and can induce selective apoptosis of CAR-T cells through small molecule inducers in cases of life-threatening CRS or immune effector cell-associated neurotoxicity syndrome (ICANS) [ ]. Zhou et al. reported that in a clinical study of DLBCL patients, CAR-T therapy equipped with iCasp9 showed an ORR of 67%, a CR of 43%, with only 14% of patients experiencing mild to moderate CRS and no severe adverse events, demonstrating good safety and controllability [ ].
As our understanding of TME-mediated immune evasion deepens, new generations of CAR-T designs continue to emerge, with armored CAR-T being an important direction. By integrating PD-1/CD28 chimeric receptors or secreting PD-1 Fc fusion proteins, armored CAR-T can effectively reverse the PD-1/PD-L1 axis-mediated inhibitory effects. In patients with NHL and chronic lymphocytic leukemia (CLL), armored CAR-T demonstrated a 57% CR rate, with no severe CRS or ICANS, indicating its durable anti-tumor activity in immunosuppressive environments [ , ]. Other armored strategies focus on metabolic and checkpoint regulation. For example, IL-7 armored CAR-T maintains a less differentiated state of T cells, prolonging their in vivo persistence. PD-1 knockout CAR-T achieved an ORR of 78% and long-term CR in R/R B-NHL, with controllable toxicity [ , ]. In clinical validation, huCART19-IL18 achieved an 81% response rate (CR 52%) in patients who had failed previous CD19 CAR-T treatments, further proving the clinical feasibility of the armored design [ ]. Meanwhile, the TRUCK (T-cell reprogramming CAR-T) strategy introduces cytokines such as IL-7, IL-12, IL-15, or IL-18 into CAR-T cells, enabling antigen-dependent or controllable secretion, thereby reshaping the local immune microenvironment and enhancing effector functions [ ]. Studies show that using NR4A2, RGS16, or IFN-γ promoter-driven factor expression can enhance activity while maintaining safety; iTRUCK19.18 induces IL-18 secretion via doxycycline and T-cell Receptor (TCR) dual activation, while IFN-α/β-based self-limiting loop designs reduce toxicity in scenarios requiring short-term clearance [ – ]. Overall, armored CAR-T and TRUCK strategies overcome TME limitations by regulating immune checkpoints, optimizing metabolism, and enabling precise cytokine release, providing new engineered approaches for achieving safer and more durable efficacy.
To overcome the limitations of autologous CAR-T, such as long preparation times, high costs, and variable T-cell quality, the development of universal allogeneic CAR-T (UCAR-T) platforms is accelerating. CRISPR/Cas9 TCR knockout reduces the risk of graft-versus-host disease (GVHD) and shortens preparation time; modular CAR-T enables multi-target applications through interchangeable antibody fragments; nanoparticle-mediated in vivo generation simplifies processes and reduces costs [ – ]. However, the clinical translation of UCAR-T still faces multiple challenges. GVHD remains the most prominent safety issue. Although TCR knockout, HLA matching, and unconventional cell applications can reduce the risk, how to avoid GVHD while retaining the graft-versus-leukemia (GvL) effect remains a challenge [ – ]. Meanwhile, host immune surveillance can rapidly eliminate allogeneic cells. Strategies like HLA deficiency and immune evasion molecule expression are being explored, but excessive suppression must be approached with caution [ ]. In manufacturing, donor variability, process complexity, and cost remain bottlenecks. While iPSC cell banks and non-viral vector methods show promise, they require significant time and investment for validation [ – ]. Chimeric Antigen Receptor Natural Killer cells (CAR-NK), as a complementary strategy, naturally avoids GVHD by not relying on MHC recognition and has a lower risk of CRS/ICANS, making it another “off-the-shelf” product [ ]. However, its transient persistence and freezing stability still limit its application. Additionally, UCAR-T lacks long-term clinical data, faces complex regulatory approval, and requires substantial investment in GMP infrastructure, limiting its widespread accessibility.

## Combination therapy of CAR-T and targeted agents

## BCL2 inhibitors
The abnormal expression of BCL2 family proteins, particularly in the EZB subtype of DLBCL, as well as in DHL/THL, is often associated with chemotherapy resistance. Venetoclax, a BCL2 inhibitor, functions by mimicking the BH3 domain, alleviating mitochondrial inhibition of apoptosis, thereby enhancing the tumor-killing efficacy of CD19 CAR-T cells [ – ]. Moreover, Venetoclax has demonstrated significant immunomodulatory effects by inducing intrinsic apoptosis in MYC/BCL2 double-expressing lymphoma cells and activating CD8⁺ T cells. The clinical efficacy of Venetoclax has been validated in studies. The CAVALLI Phase II trial showed that BCL2-positive patients receiving Venetoclax combined with R-CHOP therapy had a 2-year PFS rate of 80%, significantly higher than the control group’s 62%. Although higher hematologic adverse events were observed, with grade 3–4 neutropenia occurring in 48% of patients, the mortality rate remained low (2%). These results suggest that this combination therapy has significant efficacy in patients with high BCL2 expression [ ].
The combination of Venetoclax with ibrutinib (a BTK inhibitor) has exhibited notable synergistic effects in DLBCL cells with high BCL2 expression, particularly in cases caused by BCL2 amplification or translocation. This combination effectively overcomes Venetoclax resistance and enhances therapeutic outcomes [ ]. In patients with R/R DLBCL, especially those with the non-GCB subtype and BCL2 overexpression, the Venetoclax-ibrutinib combination has demonstrated favorable efficacy, with an ORR of 61.5%. Of these, 23.1% of patients achieved complete remission (CR), with significant improvements in remission duration (median 11 months) and PFS (median 5.6 months). Despite adverse events primarily involving neutropenia and diarrhea, the overall toxicity was manageable [ ]. For DHL, an aggressive subtype, the combination of the second-generation BTK inhibitor Zanubrutinib and the BCL2 inhibitor navitoclax significantly increased the sensitivity of DHL cells to navitoclax and effectively inhibited tumor growth through apoptosis and ferroptosis [ ].
The PI3K pathway remains an effective therapeutic target in hematologic malignancies, and the combination of the PI3Kδ inhibitor Roginolisib (IOA-244) and Venetoclax has shown significant synergistic anti-tumor effects in DLBCL and chronic lymphocytic leukemia (CLL) cell lines. This combination therapy further enhances the effects of Venetoclax by inhibiting the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway, increasing BIM expression, and promoting the ubiquitination degradation of MCL1 [ ]. Similarly, the PI3K inhibitor BR101801 combined with Venetoclax has demonstrated significant synergistic anti-tumor effects in mouse models by targeting C-MYC/BCL2/MCL1 triple pathways, reducing the expression of C-MYC and MCL-1, inhibiting cell proliferation, inducing cell cycle arrest, and increasing apoptosis [ ].
The combination of the HDAC inhibitor Chidamide and Venetoclax significantly elevated BIM levels, induced apoptosis, and inhibited tumor growth by downregulating the expression of MYC, BCL2, and TP53. This combined therapy exhibited strong in vivo therapeutic potential in xenograft mouse models [ ]. Furthermore, a study by Zhang et al. demonstrated that Chidamide induces apoptosis in a concentration- and time-dependent manner, downregulates BCL2 expression, and inhibits the expression and phosphorylation of STAT3, thereby exerting anti-tumor effects. This study suggests that Chidamide induces apoptosis in DLBCL cells through the inhibition of the HDACs/STAT3/BCL2 pathway, showing therapeutic potential [ ].
Additionally, molecular alterations in both EZH2 and BCL2 frequently occur concurrently in DLBCL. Targeting both EZH2 and BCL2 inhibitors significantly improves therapeutic outcomes in specific genetic backgrounds (e.g., EZH2 mutations and IGH/BCL2 translocations) by upregulating pro-apoptotic BCL2 family members, which increases cell sensitivity to Venetoclax [ ].
Concerning the resistance issues in DLBCL, Chong et al. (2023) identified the hyperphosphorylation of BCL2 family proteins as a key mechanism underlying Venetoclax resistance. They proposed that targeting this phosphorylation process could restore Venetoclax sensitivity in resistant cells [ ]. Further research has shown that BCL2 inhibitors can reverse MCL-1-mediated resistance, particularly in TP53-mutated models. By downregulating the MCL-1/BIM complex ratio, BCL2 inhibitors restore apoptosis sensitivity, providing new directions for cancer treatment [ ]. For patients with R/R DLBCL after CAR-T treatment, Venetoclax-based combination therapy has shown significant efficacy. A retrospective study conducted by Zhu et al. (2023) at the First Affiliated Hospital of Soochow University, involving 10 CAR-T therapy failure patients with R/R DLBCL, showed an objective response rate (ORR) of 80% and a complete remission (CR) rate of 30%. These data indicate that Venetoclax-based combination therapy not only has significant efficacy for CAR-T failure R/R DLBCL patients but also has manageable toxicity, offering an effective treatment option for such patients [ ].

## Immune checkpoint inhibitors
Abnormal activation of immune checkpoint pathways is one of the key mechanisms underlying CAR-T therapy failure, especially in T-cell rich subtypes such as T-cell/histiocyte-rich large B-cell lymphoma (THRLBCL), where the overexpression of PD-L1 (up to 90%) highlights the role of the PD-1/PD-L1 signaling axis in immune suppression [ ]. In the treatment of R/R DLBCL, PD-L1 overexpression is associated with poorer prognosis. Therefore, inhibition of the PD-1 pathway has become a key therapeutic strategy in an immunologically competent environment [ ]. Although the efficacy of PD-1 inhibitors alone is relatively limited, nivolumab has shown some activity in R/R DLBCL patients who failed autologous hematopoietic cell transplantation (HCT) or were ineligible for it, though the ORR is low. However, when combined with conventional chemotherapy (such as R-CHOP), the efficacy improves significantly, especially in PD-L1-positive patients, where the combination therapy significantly improves PFS [ , ].
In addition, PD-1 inhibitors combined with other therapies, such as EZH2 inhibitors and CAR-T cell therapy, have also shown therapeutic potential. The combination of atezolizumab and Tazemetostat demonstrated good safety but moderate anti-tumor activity [ ]. When used in combination with CAR-T, PD-1 inhibitors have demonstrated enhanced efficacy. The ZUMA-6 trial showed that the combination of Axi-cel with the anti-PD-L1 monoclonal antibody atezolizumab resulted in a complete remission (CR) rate of 58% in R/R DLBCL patients [ ]. Pembrolizumab combined with Tisa-cel showed good safety, and although it did not significantly enhance CAR-T cell expansion, it still showed potential to improve therapeutic outcomes [ ]. The combination of Chidamide and Sintilimab in patients progressing after CAR-T therapy also demonstrated significant efficacy, with an ORR of 57.1% [ ]. Although CAR-T therapy has made significant progress in R/R DLBCL, a large number of patients remain refractory or experience disease progression. The combination of PD-1 inhibitors with chemotherapy as a rescue therapy after CAR-T treatment has been effective in some patients, suggesting that PD-1 inhibition retains therapeutic potential even after CAR-T failure [ ]. Maintenance therapy with PD-1 inhibitors has also shown positive effects in improving survival benefits for patients [ ]. Immunotherapeutic synergy in dual-target CAR-T therapies has also shown promise. In the AUTO3 trial, the combination of CD19/CD22 dual-target CAR-T cells with pembrolizumab resulted in an ORR of 66%, and the proportion of memory phenotype T cells was 2.3 times higher compared to monotherapy [ ].
In addition to PD-1 inhibitors, the CD47/SIRPα axis plays a crucial role in immune evasion in DLBCL. By binding to the SIRPα receptor on macrophages, CD47 sends a “don’t eat me” signal, inhibiting phagocytosis by immune cells, which has become a major obstacle in treating DLBCL. Studies have shown that CD47 and SIRPα expression is elevated in the lymph nodes of R/R DLBCL patients, and overexpression of CD47 significantly reduces Tafasitamab-mediated antibody-dependent cellular phagocytosis (ADCP). The combination of anti-CD47 antibodies significantly enhances macrophage phagocytic activity and improves the anti-tumor effect of Tafasitamab [ ]. Berberine, by modulating CD47 expression, enhances macrophage phagocytosis, and when used in combination with anti-CD47 antibodies and rituximab, it shows significant anti-tumor effects [ ]. The combination of CD47 inhibitors with rituximab in DLBCL patients achieved a 40% ORR [ ]. TTI-621, a CD47 blockade agent, showed a 29% remission rate in DLBCL patients when combined with rituximab [ ].

## TP53 mutation reactivation

TP53 and its product p53 have long been considered “undruggable” targets, as the ideal therapeutic goal is to restore the normal conformation of mutated p53, which is not the usual mechanism of action for small-molecule inhibitors [ ]. However, recent studies have revealed that DLBCL with TP53 mutations may have targetable molecular vulnerabilities, offering direction for new therapeutic strategies.
First, epigenetic studies have shown a strong correlation between the high expression of p53 and EZH2, and p53-induced inhibition of the EZH2 promoter is ineffective in TP53-mutant cells. This suggests that EZH2 inhibitors could be potential therapeutic options [ ]. Moreover, the gene expression profile of TP53-mutant DLBCL shows activation of the PI3K pathway, providing a rationale for exploring PI3K inhibitors [ ]. Chen et al. [ ] found that rhubarb and its derivative E35 could downregulate TP53 expression and inhibit the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway in a time- and dose-dependent manner, inhibiting DLBCL cell proliferation and inducing apoptosis. This suggests that TP53 may be a critical target for rhubarb’s anti-tumor effects. In terms of immune regulation, RNA sequencing revealed that TP53-mutant DLBCL often accompanies downregulation of the interferon and apoptosis pathways, with a reduction in CD8⁺ T cell infiltration [ ]. Notably, the combination of R-CHOP with the DNA methyltransferase inhibitor decitabine has been shown to improve response rates in some TP53-mutant patients, accompanied by enhanced T cell responses and increased interferon-γ levels [ ]. Fang et al. [ ] further revealed that TP53-mutant DLBCL inhibits endogenous retrovirus (ERV) expression via high expression of SUV39H1 and upregulation of H3K9me3, creating an immunosuppressive microenvironment. Decitabine can reverse this epigenetic state, activate ERV expression, restore the interferon pathway, and T cell function, thereby improving patient treatment responses. This suggests that the ERV regulatory axis may serve as a novel epigenetic therapeutic target for TP53-mutant DLBCL. In terms of small molecule drugs, APR-246 (Eprenetapopt) is a reactivator that can restore the conformation of mutated p53 and upregulate the ferroptosis marker ACSL4. In preclinical studies, the combination of APR-246 and CAR-T therapy reduced tumor volume by 82% [ ]. Furthermore, metabolic pathways also show potential value. Sun et al. [ ] reported that Indoleamine 2,3-Dioxygenase 1 (IDO1) is significantly upregulated in DLBCL and associated with poor prognosis, as it promotes MDM2 expression to suppress p53 function. IDO1 inhibitors (such as Epacadostat) can downregulate MDM2, restore p53 function, induce cell cycle arrest and apoptosis, and enhance CAR-T cell proliferation and anti-tumor effects. Metabolic vulnerability is also a crucial feature of TP53-mutant tumors. Cai et al. [ ] found that DLBCL patients exhibit characteristics of active glutamine metabolism, with high levels of glutamine associated with poor prognosis, while its metabolic product α-ketoglutarate (α-KG) has protective effects. The cell-permeable derivative DM-αKG can effectively inhibit the growth of DLBCL and DHL by inducing ROS accumulation and TP53-mediated ferroptosis, suggesting that targeting glutamine metabolism could be a feasible therapeutic approach.

## BTK inhibitors
In the MCD subtype of DLBCL, chronic activation of the BCR signaling pathway maintains an immunosuppressive TME through the BTK-NF-κB axis. BTK inhibitors (such as ibrutinib) block this pathway, reduce PD-L1 expression, and limit Treg cell infiltration, thereby improving the tumor immune microenvironment [ ]. Furthermore, ibrutinib also enhances macrophage-mediated antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity by inhibiting JAK2, further improving the therapeutic outcomes in aggressive B-cell lymphomas [ ].
Clinical studies have demonstrated that several BTK inhibitors in combination therapies exhibit significant efficacy and good tolerability in relapsed or refractory DLBCL (R/R DLBCL). In first-line treatment, Zanubrutinib combined with R-CHOP (ZR-CHOP) significantly improved the complete remission rate in DHL patients, particularly in those with Ki67 >75% or p53 >50%, and extended PFS, suggesting potential benefits in high-risk subtypes [ ]. A study conducted by Yuan et al. showed that Zanubrutinib combined with salvage chemotherapy achieved a 74.1% ORR and a median PFS of 8.1 months in 27 R/R DLBCL patients, with overall good tolerability. For those with poor responses to BTK inhibitors and carrying NOTCH2 or TP53 mutations, the ORR increased to 75% with CD19-CAR-T cell therapy, and the median PFS was 14.5 months, further demonstrating the synergistic advantages of immunotherapy [ ]. A single-center real-world study demonstrated that the MCD subtype of DLBCL showed significant initial responses to BTK inhibitors, indicating their potential value for early intervention. Additionally, single-cell sequencing analysis revealed that BTK inhibitor combination therapy significantly reduced the proportion of terminally exhausted T cells (TEX) while increasing the proportion of stem cell-like memory T cells (TSCM), highlighting the critical role of BTK inhibitors in T cell differentiation reprogramming and the alleviation of T cell exhaustion [ ].
Although BTK inhibitors show significant efficacy in B-cell lymphomas, resistance remains a widespread issue with complex mechanisms. Studies have found that downregulation of FOXO3a and PTEN leads to sustained activation of the PI3K/AKT pathway [ ]; upregulation of PI3K-β further drives abnormal PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling [ ]; and activation of the PDGFD-EGFR axis also plays an important role in resistance [ ]. Furthermore, EGR1-PDP1-mediated oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) metabolic reprogramming provides sufficient energy support to resistant cells [ ]. In response to these resistance mechanisms, several combination inhibition strategies have been proposed: PI3K/AKT inhibitors (such as idelalisib and MK2206) can restore drug sensitivity, OXPHOS inhibitors (such as IM156 and metformin) effectively block the metabolic adaptation of resistant cells, and exportin-1 inhibitors like Selinexor promote cell apoptosis by restoring the nuclear localization of FOXO3a [ – ]. Additionally, new-generation BTK inhibitors and multi-target combination therapies are continuously emerging. The non-covalent inhibitor Pirtobrutinib effectively suppresses ERK1/2 pro-survival signaling, overcoming resistance associated with MYD88 mutations [ ]; the irreversible inhibitor S-016, which has higher selectivity, when combined with VEGFR/CSF1R inhibitors like SYHA1813, improves resistance by modulating the TME [ ]; the second-generation inhibitor TL-895 demonstrated significant anti-tumor efficacy in MCL and DLBCL models [ ]. Moreover, Pirtobrutinib combined with Venetoclax can effectively restore drug sensitivity in cases of BCL10 mutations causing abnormal NF-κB activation and multi-drug resistance, offering new treatment options for R/R cases [ ].

## EZH2 inhibitors
As the role of EZH2 in the molecular pathogenesis of lymphoma becomes increasingly clear, various targeted drugs and combination strategies have been developed. McCabe et al. (2012) first demonstrated that the highly selective inhibitor GSK126 significantly reduces H3K27me3 and inhibits the growth of EZH2-mutant lymphomas, laying the foundation for clinical translation [ ]. The anti-tumor activity of EZH2 inhibitors primarily relies on the inhibition of H3K27 trimethylation, rather than monomethylation, further emphasizing its crucial role in epigenetic regulation [ ].
The selective oral EZH2 inhibitor Tazemetostat has entered clinical trials and has shown acceptable safety and preliminary efficacy in R/R B-cell lymphomas [ ]. Tazemetostat enhances the immune response against lymphoma by upregulating the expression of CCL17/TARC in B-cell lymphomas, promoting the recruitment of T cells [ ]. Additionally, studies have shown that Tazemetostat improves the efficacy of T cell-based immunotherapies, enhancing immune responses and T cell function, thereby boosting the anti-tumor immune therapeutic effect [ ]. Isshiki et al. (2025) reported that EZH2 inhibition reprograms the transcriptional state of lymphoma cells, enhancing their immunogenicity, and improves the functional activity of CAR-T cells without damaging them. Specifically, this effect manifests as a reduction in Treg cells, promotion of memory CD8 + CAR-T cell formation, alleviation of T cell exhaustion, and restoration of granzyme B and perforin expression, while enhancing CAR-T cell recruitment and cytotoxicity within the TME. These findings suggest that EZH2 inhibition significantly improves the efficacy of immunotherapy by enhancing lymphoma immunogenicity and directly boosting CAR-T cell function. Relevant strategies are currently undergoing clinical trials (NCT05934838, NCT05994235), offering new insights into optimizing immune therapies for B-cell lymphomas [ ]. At the same time, novel inhibitors are expanding the scope of EZH2-targeted therapies. The irreversible EZH2 inhibitor IHMT-337, developed by Mei et al. (2023), promotes EZH2 degradation and targets CDK4 transcription, effectively inhibiting DLBCL growth [ ]. The EZH1/2 dual inhibitor EZH-1-P2 reported by An et al. (2024) upregulates p53 and blocks macrophage polarization, combining anti-proliferative and immune-regulatory effects [ ]. Additionally, various combination epigenetic strategies have shown synergistic effects. For example, the combination of DOT1L and EZH2 inhibition can induce a “differentiation crisis” and enhance immunogenicity [ , ]. EZH2 + CDK9 inhibition can reverse the abnormal accumulation of H3K27me3 [ ], while EZH2 + BRD4 inhibition works by blocking MYC pathways, with YPEL2 serving as a potential therapeutic marker [ ]. However, resistance to EZH2 inhibitors remains a major challenge, with mechanisms including activation of the IGF-1R/PI3K/AKT/MAPK pathways, acquired EZH2 mutations [ ], USP47-stabilized EZH2 mutations [ ], and iron homeostasis dysregulation mediated by TfR-1, which inhibits ferroptosis [ ]. Therefore, developing new inhibitors and combining epigenetic or signaling pathway-targeted drugs remains a critical direction for enhancing the efficacy of EZH2-targeted therapies.

## Bispecific antibodies
The CD3/CD19 bispecific antibody Blinatumomab was approved in 2015 for the treatment of B-cell acute lymphoblastic leukemia (B-ALL) and has demonstrated therapeutic potential in other lymphoid malignancies [ , ]. Building on the success of Blinatumomab, researchers further developed the CD3/CD20 bispecific antibody. Glofitamab is a 2:1 bispecific antibody targeting CD20×CD3, which enhances immune responses by directing T cells to attack malignant B cells. It is the first approved antibody of this type [ , ]. Glofitamab has shown significant efficacy in patients with R/R DLBCL. In a Phase II clinical trial, 39% of 155 R/R DLBCL patients who had received at least two lines of treatment achieved complete remission (CR), with a 35% CR rate among patients who had failed CAR-T therapy [ ]. Real-world data from a multi-center study in Israel also supports its efficacy, with an objective response rate (ORR) of 34%, including 14% complete remissions [ ]. Another study showed that Glofitamab significantly prolonged OS in R/R DLBCL patients after CAR-T therapy failure, with a median OS of 14.7 months. The metabolic response rate was 76.1%, with a complete metabolic response rate of 45.7%, and the treatment was well tolerated [ ]. Therefore, Glofitamab offers an important treatment option for R/R DLBCL patients after CAR-T therapy failure. Epcoritamab is another subcutaneously administered CD3×CD20 bispecific antibody. In an indirect comparison study by Salles et al., Epcoritamab showed no significant difference in efficacy compared to CAR-T (Axi-cel), with ORRs of 73.5% and 74.3%, and CR rates of 48.7% and 54.5%, respectively, suggesting similar therapeutic potential for both [ ]. In the EpcoreNHL-1 expanded cohort (157 patients), the ORR was 63%, with a CR rate of 39%. Among patients who had received CAR-T treatment, the ORR was 54%, whereas initial treatment patients had an ORR of 69%. The most common adverse events included CRS (, 49.7%), fever (23.6%), and fatigue (22.9%), with overall good tolerability [ ].
The clinical effectiveness of bispecific antibodies (BsAbs) is influenced by various resistance mechanisms, including inherent tumor cell mechanisms, T cell dysfunction, and immune-suppressive factors in the TME. Inherent mechanisms, such as the loss of target antigens (e.g., CD20 loss), are common resistance mechanisms [ ]. In the TME, T cell dysfunction, including the activation of Treg, downregulation of TCR, and T cell exhaustion, further contributes to resistance. Another possible resistance mechanism involves extrinsic T cell mechanisms, such as the recruitment of immune-suppressive myeloid cells or stromal cells, such as tumor-associated macrophages, myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs), and cancer-associated fibroblasts [ , ]. To overcome these resistance mechanisms, researchers are exploring alternative targets and multi-target bispecific antibodies. For example, Hutchings et al. developed a CD19 × 4-1BBL-targeting bispecific antibody (RO7227166) that achieved a 39% complete remission in DLBCL patients [ ]. Other new targets include CD47 (e.g., TG-1801, ORR 23%) and CD20/CD47 combinations (IMM0306), the latter having blood cell reduction as a dose-limiting toxicity [ , ]. The development of ROR1-targeting and CD19/CD3/CD2 trispecific antibodies is also ongoing, with promising potential to enhance immune responses [ , ].

## Discussion
High-risk DLBCL exhibits significant molecular heterogeneity, which is the underlying cause of the differential response to CAR-T. In recent years, precision treatment strategies based on molecular subtyping have gradually revealed the resistance mechanisms that affect treatment efficacy (Table  . High-Risk Subtypes of DLBCL and Treatment Strategies). These mechanisms not only impact the effectiveness of existing therapies but also provide new research directions for overcoming therapeutic bottlenecks. By deeply analyzing these molecular mechanisms, researchers can design more precise combination therapies, such as those combining immune checkpoint inhibitors, metabolic reprogramming modulators, and epigenetic regulators, to overcome the limitations of current treatments. Although significant progress has been made, challenges remain. Most metabolic interventions are still in the preclinical or early stages, and although universal CAR-T cells can be rapidly applied, their persistence is limited, and they are susceptible to host immune-mediated clearance. Furthermore, the current molecular subtyping system has not yet been fully integrated into routine clinical practice, limiting its application value in addressing the biological complexity of DLBCL in real-world settings. Future research should prioritize the development of integrated multi-modal treatment strategies, such as combination regimens involving metabolic reprogramming, epigenetic reprogramming, and immune checkpoint modulation. Developing logic-gated or controllable CAR-T structures and stratified treatment frameworks, based on real-world and multi-omics datasets, is critical for advancing precision immunotherapy for high-risk DLBCL.
