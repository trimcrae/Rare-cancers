## Abstract
Primary Ewing sarcoma of the kidney (ESK) is an exceptionally rare and aggressive malignancy, representing less than 1% of renal tumors. It typically affects young adults and often presents at an advanced stage, leading to a poor prognosis. Diagnosis requires a multidisciplinary approach integrating histopathology, immunohistochemistry, and molecular analysis. A 29-year-old male patient presented with a one-and-a-half-year history of right flank pain and one year of painless, intermittent hematuria. Initial management at a local hospital for a suspected inflammatory lesion was unsuccessful. A contrast CT urogram revealed a large (19 cm), locally invasive right renal mass with aortocaval lymphadenopathy. Histopathological examination of a biopsy specimen revealed a malignant round cell tumor. Immunohistochemistry was positive for vimentin, NKX2.2, CD99, and synaptophysin, confirming the diagnosis of Ewing sarcoma. A staging PET-CT demonstrated metastatic disease involving the liver, inferior vena cava (with tumor thrombus), lymph nodes, and multiple skeletal sites. The patient received eight cycles of alternating VDC/IE (vincristine, doxorubicin, cyclophosphamide / ifosfamide, etoposide) chemotherapy, resulting in a significant reduction in the primary tumor and resolution of the IVC thrombus and skeletal metastases. He subsequently underwent cytoreductive nephrectomy. The final pathology confirmed a 7 cm Ewing sarcoma with negative margins and no lymph node involvement. Unfortunately, the patient did not receive adjuvant chemotherapy and expired four months post-surgery. ESK is a diagnostic and therapeutic challenge. This case underscores the utility of a multimodal approach, where neoadjuvant chemotherapy can effectively downstage even metastatic disease, facilitating surgical resection. However, the overall prognosis remains poor, particularly for metastatic presentations, highlighting the urgent need for more effective and less toxic therapeutic regimens.

## Introduction
Ewing sarcoma (ES) is a primitive neuroectodermal tumor arising from neural crest cells, categorized as a small round cell tumor with aggressive biological behavior. While it commonly occurs in bones and soft tissues, primary renal Ewing sarcoma (ESK) is extremely rare, accounting for less than 1% of all renal masses [ ]. Clinically, patients most frequently present with flank pain, followed by hematuria and a palpable retroperitoneal mass [ ]. The diagnosis is challenging and necessitates a multidisciplinary approach involving radiological imaging, histopathological examination (HPE), and molecular analysis.
The standard treatment involves a multimodal strategy of surgical resection, chemotherapy, and radiotherapy. The traditional chemotherapy protocol alternates between the VDC (vincristine, doxorubicin, cyclophosphamide) and IE (ifosfamide, etoposide) regimens [ ]. The prognosis for ESK is generally grim, with approximately 66% of patients diagnosed with stage IV disease at presentation. Those with metastasis have a median overall survival of around 24 months [ ]. This paper reviews the relevant literature and presents a case of a 29-year-old male patient with primary ESK who presented with metastatic disease.

## Case presentation
A 29-year-old male patient presented to the Urology Department with a complaint of right flank pain for one and a half years and a one-year history of visible, painless, intermittent hematuria without clots. Initially managed at a local hospital for a suspected inflammatory lesion of the right kidney, his symptoms persisted. A subsequent contrast CT urography revealed a large (19 cm, 1690 gm), locally infiltrating, hypodense, exophytic mass in the right kidney with enlarged aortocaval lymph nodes (Figure ).
 The patient was referred to our tertiary care center for further management. A biopsy was performed, and histopathology was suggestive of a malignant round cell tumor, composed of sheets of small, round, uniform cells with scanty cytoplasm. Immunohistochemistry (IHC) showed tumor positivity for vimentin, NKX2.2, CD99 (Figure ), and synaptophysin, leading to a diagnosis of Ewing sarcoma of the kidney.
 A staging PET-CT scan revealed a hypermetabolic mass (15.3x14.2 cm) in the right renal fossa (SUVmax 13.5) infiltrating the liver, abutting the posterior thoracic wall, and extending into the inferior vena cava (IVC) as a tumor thrombus (Figure ).
 Additionally, there were enlarged metabolically active perinephric, aortocaval, and mesenteric lymph nodes, along with multiple lytic skeletal metastases.
The patient received eight cycles of alternating VDC/IE regimen chemotherapy at the State Cancer Institute. A post-chemotherapy PET-CT evaluation showed a significant reduction in the primary mass size (5.5x5x5 cm, SUVmax 3.5), with the resolution of the IVC thrombus, lymph node involvement, and skeletal lesions (Figure ).
 Following a marked radiologic and metabolic response to systemic chemotherapy, the patient underwent cytoreductive nephrectomy as part of a multidisciplinary treatment strategy aimed at achieving local disease control after downstaging of metastatic disease (Figure ).
 The final histopathology report confirmed a malignant round cell neoplasm (Figure ).
 Ewing sarcoma, measuring 7x5x4.5 cm, predominantly is located in the renal cortex, with medullary replacement (pT2) and negative surgical margins. No sarcomatoid or rhabdoid features were identified. Right para-aortic lymph node sampling was negative for malignancy. Despite an excellent initial response to neoadjuvant chemotherapy and successful surgical resection, the patient had aggressive systemic disease at presentation and did not receive planned postoperative therapy and died four months after surgery.

## Discussion
Ewing sarcoma is a well-known aggressive small round cell tumor, but its primary occurrence in the kidney is exceptionally rare, with an estimated incidence of 0.4 per million [ ]. First described by Seemayer et al. in 1975 [ ], approximately 200 cases have been reported worldwide, predominantly in young males, with a mean age of 30.4 years [ ]. ESK is highly aggressive, and a meta-analysis by Risi et al. of 116 cases found that 66% of patients had stage IV disease at diagnosis, with the most common symptoms being pain (54%), hematuria (29%), and a palpable mass (28%) [ ]. It is postulated that ESK may arise from neural cells that invaginate into the kidney during development [ ].
The clinical and radiological features of ESK are non-specific, often mimicking more common renal malignancies like renal cell carcinoma, leading to diagnostic challenges [ ]. A definitive diagnosis requires a biopsy supplemented by IHC and molecular analysis. Histologically, ESK consists of small, round, blue cells. IHC typically shows positivity for CD99 (highly sensitive but not specific) and neural markers like vimentin, synaptophysin, and neuron-specific enolase (NSE) [ ]. Our case showed positivity for vimentin, NKX2.2, CD99, and synaptophysin. Molecular confirmation involves identifying the pathognomonic translocation t(11;22)(q24;q12), which creates the EWSR1-FLI1 fusion gene, present in approximately 90% of cases [ , ]. Although molecular confirmation of EWSR1 rearrangement was not available in our patient, the diagnosis was supported by the characteristic histomorphology, the immunophenotypic profile including CD99 and NKX2.2 positivity, and clinicoradiological correlation after the exclusion of other small round cell tumors.
Imaging plays a crucial role in staging and response assessment. Fluorodeoxyglucose (FDG) PET-CT has high sensitivity (87%), specificity (97%), and accuracy (94%) for detecting distant metastases in Ewing sarcoma [ , ]. In our case, PET-CT was instrumental in identifying the full extent of metastatic disease and in evaluating the excellent response to neoadjuvant chemotherapy.
There is no standardized treatment protocol for ESK, but a multimodal approach is universally adopted. The chemotherapy backbone, extrapolated from the treatment of skeletal ES, involves alternating VDC and IE regimens, which has been shown to improve outcomes in non-metastatic disease [ ]. However, this regimen is associated with significant hematological (anemia, thrombocytopenia, neutropenia) and non-hematological (infections, mucositis) toxicities [ ]. Our patient demonstrated a remarkable response to this neoadjuvant chemotherapy, which downsized the tumor and resolved metastatic deposits, facilitating a complete surgical resection. This highlights the potential benefit of aggressive chemotherapy even in metastatic settings.
Despite standard treatments, outcomes remain poor due to the high rate of metastatic presentation and the development of multidrug resistance. Research into novel therapeutic strategies is ongoing. Genomic studies indicate that mutations in TP53 and STAG2 are associated with a poorer prognosis [ , ], potentially opening avenues for targeted therapies. Additionally, newly approved antiangiogenic agents like Apatinib have shown promise in case reports and may represent a future therapeutic option [ ].
The prognosis for ESK remains grim, particularly for metastatic disease. The analysis by Risi et al. reported a median overall survival of 24 months for patients with M1 disease. They also found a clinical benefit in 74% of treated M1 patients, with a median progression-free survival of 22 months, underscoring that chemotherapy is an effective strategy for metastatic ESK [ ].

## Conclusions
Primary renal Ewing sarcoma is a rare and aggressive malignancy with a propensity for metastatic spread at diagnosis, leading to a poor prognosis. Diagnosis relies on a high index of suspicion and an integrated analysis of histomorphology, immunohistochemistry, and, ideally, molecular-genetic testing. A multimodal treatment strategy incorporating chemotherapy, surgery, and radiotherapy can provide clinical benefit, as demonstrated by the significant response in our metastatic case. However, the toxicities of current regimens and the high mortality rate underscore the urgent need for the development of optimized, potent, and targeted therapeutic protocols to improve patient survival.
