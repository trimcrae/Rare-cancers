To improve immunotherapy for this cancer, understanding how the immune cells in the tumor microenvironment associate with histological subtype, disease progression and current therapies is vital. To evaluate the immune infiltrate in synovial sarcoma in relation to histological subtype, disease progression and in response to cytotoxic treatment, we performed immunodetection of T cells, CD68+ myeloid cells, endothelial cells and keratin on a series of 41 synovial sarcoma patients at various stages of disease. The immune composition of synovial sarcoma was dominated by CD68+ myeloid cells of which a substantial part was of the CD163+ immunosuppressive phenotype, which increased after chemotherapy or radiotherapy. Biphasic synovial sarcomas were more densely infiltrated by both T cells and myeloid cells than monophasic synovial sarcomas. In these tumors, the immune and endothelial cells were mostly located within the stromal like, spindle cell compartment and excluded from the epithelial compartment, greatly resembling the spatial organization of healthy epithelium such as in the colon. Together these data demonstrate that biphasic synovial sarcoma is immunologically different from monophasic synovial sarcoma and might be more susceptible to immunotherapies such as adoptive T-cell therapy. Finally, T-cell infiltration in primary synovial sarcoma was associated with prolonged overall survival of patients which suggests that intratumoral T cells may demonstrate anti-tumor activity.

## Introduction
Synovial sarcoma is an aggressive soft-tissue tumor of mesenchymal origin that classically is defined by a translocation between chromosomes 18 and X which results in an in-frame fusion of the gene SS18 with SSX1, SSX2 or SSX4 [ ]. Two main histological subtypes of synovial sarcoma are recognized: monophasic and biphasic synovial sarcoma. Biphasic synovial sarcomas consist of two compartments which grow intermingled: one compartment with spindle-shaped cells that have limited cytoplasm and are arranged in dense cellular sheets and an epithelial compartment in which the cancer cells show various degrees of epithelial-like differentiation and expression of epithelial markers including keratin [ ]. Monophasic synovial sarcomas consist entirely of the spindle cell compartment that is also present in biphasic synovial sarcoma [ ]. SS18-SSX1 positive tumors show a biphasic morphology in about half the cases while this is about 10% for SS18-SSX2 positive tumors [ , ], suggesting that the epithelial differentiation in biphasic synovial sarcoma is a process that is endogenously driven by the genetic aberration in these cancer cells. Analysis of a large US cohort of patients showed that biphasic synovial sarcoma patients have a slightly better survival than monophasic synovial sarcoma patients [ , ], although these results could not be reproduced by studies that analyzed 2 other cohorts [ , ].
Treatment of synovial sarcoma consists primarily of surgical removal of the tumor which is supplemented by (neo-) adjuvant chemotherapy and/or radiotherapy. While these therapies are effective in a proportion of patients with primary disease, outcomes of synovial sarcoma patients with metastatic disease are poor, with a 10-year survival rate below 10%, stressing the need for better therapies [ , ]. Inspired by the success in other tumors, immune checkpoint blockade (ICB) therapies have been explored to treat metastatic synovial sarcoma. ICB works by targeting immune checkpoint molecules that are present in the tumor microenvironment (TME). These immune checkpoints, including the PD-1/PD-L1 axis and CTLA-4, dampen immune responses by inhibiting T-cell functionality. By blocking CTLA-4 or PD-1/PD-L1, these therapies potentiate cancer-directed T cells that are present in the tumor. T cells mainly recognize tumors via aberrant proteins that arise from the mutated DNA within a tumor. Therefore, the more DNA damage is present in a tumor, the more tumor-reactive T cells may be present within the tumor which translates into a higher likelihood that a patient benefits from ICB [ ]. In highly mutated malignancies, such as cutaneous melanoma, good clinical responses are seen to ICB, and ICB has been incorporated into standard treatment regimens [ ]. In intensively pretreated, advanced synovial sarcoma, however, ICB showed no clinical benefit to patients [ – ], which can be attributed to the genetic stability and low tumor mutational burden in this disease [ , ].
The lack of naturally occurring tumor-reactive T cells in synovial sarcoma sparked the interest to ex vivo create tumor-reactive T cells for adoptive T-cell therapy (ACT). In these therapies, autologous T cells from patients can be equipped with a new tumor-reactive T-cell receptor (TCR) to redirect these cells toward the tumor. One ACT trial used NY-ESO-1 TCR-engineered T cells and showed an overall response rate of 44% [ ]. A trial using MAGE-A4 TCR-engineered T cells reported similar results in synovial sarcoma with 7/16 (44%) of synovial sarcoma patients responding [ ]. However, most responses were partial, with only 1/45 patients in the NY-ESO-1 trial having a complete response according to RECISTv1.1 criteria and no complete responders in the MAGE-A4 trial which illustrates that further improvement of ACT strategies for synovial sarcoma is needed[ , ].
To improve immunotherapy in synovial sarcoma, knowledge is needed about the immune microenvironment of this cancer type. Previously, it was shown that high numbers of intratumoral CD8 T cells in synovial sarcoma correlate with a favorable prognosis [ ], while conversely, high numbers of PD-1+ cells in the tumor marginal borders of synovial sarcoma samples correlated with a worse progression free survival [ ]. High numbers of intratumoral CD163+ myeloid cells correlated with a poor prognosis for synovial sarcoma patients in the study of Oike et al. [ ]. Umakoshi et al. found a strong relation between high levels of marginal macrophage infiltration and poorer survival rates in a combined cohort of various high grade soft-tissue sarcomas [ ]. Interestingly, macrophage infiltration was reported to be induced by radiotherapy in soft-tissue sarcomas [ ].
Although data from multiple studies suggest that the immune system plays an important role in synovial sarcoma biology, one key element is currently understudied: the relationship between histological subtype (monophasic vs biphasic) and the immune composition within synovial sarcoma.
Here, we investigate the immune microenvironment of synovial sarcoma and correlate infiltration of T cells, myeloid cells, and microvessel density to the survival of patients. Furthermore, we explore differences in the immune composition between monophasic and biphasic synovial sarcoma, during disease progression, and as a result of traditional cytotoxic treatment with chemotherapy and radiotherapy. Finally, we describe how epithelial differentiation in biphasic synovial sarcoma locally affects immune infiltration.

## Materials and methods

## Patient materials
Patient materials were derived from the bone and soft-tissue tumor biobank at Leiden University Medical Centre and coded according to the ethical guidelines described in “Code for Proper Secondary Use of Human Tissue in the Netherlands” of the Dutch Federation of Medical Scientific Societies, as reviewed and approved by the Medical Ethical Board Leiden-Den Haag-Delft (B20.034). Written informed consent was acquired from the patients that were included in this biobank. For some patients that were included in the biobank before 2015, a waiver of consent was obtained by the institutional medical ethical board. Synovial sarcoma tissue microarrays (TMA) were created previously [ ] and contained samples from 71 formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tumor resections derived from 44 patients. From three patients, there was only a local recurrent sample on the TMA, and these patients were excluded from the analysis. All synovial sarcoma samples included were SSX/SS18 translocation positive as was confirmed by PCR or fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) with fusion gene specific probes.

## Five-color immunofluorescent labeling of synovial sarcoma tissues
Five-color immunofluorescence labeling was performed as described previously by our laboratory [ ] using primary antibodies against CD68 (Clone D4B9C, 1:4000, Cell Signaling #76437S), CD3e (Clone EP449E, 1:100, Abcam Cat. # ab52959), CD31 (Clone 89C2, 1:1600, Cell Signaling Cat. # 3528S) and pan-keratin (Clone AE1/AE3 eFluor 660, 1:100, Thermo Scientific # 50-9003-82 and Clone C11 Alexa Fluor 647, 1:100, Santa Cruz, Cat. #sc-8018). Slides were scanned using a Zeiss Axio Scan.Z1 slide scanner followed by sequential immunohistochemistry for SS18-SSX fusion protein using the primary antibody clone E9X9V (1:40, Cell Signaling #72,364) and rescanning with the Zeiss Axio Scan.Z1 slide scanner. The full staining protocol and analysis method are described in the supplementary data [ – ].

## Imaging mass cytometry
Imaging mass cytometry was performed as described earlier [ ]. In short, 4-µm-thick FFPE tissue slides were deparaffinized in xylene, followed by ethanol washing and rehydration. Heat-mediated antigen retrieval was performed in a 0.01 M citrate buffer (pH 6.0) in a microwave for 10 min. Slides were cooled and blocked with Superblock PBS Blocking Buffer (Thermo Scientific Cat. # 57,515) followed by overnight incubation of the SSX2 antibody at 4 °C. Slides were washed (all washes are 3 times 5 min in PBS 1%BSA) and incubated with a goat-anti-mouse PE labeled antibody (Southern Biotechnology Associates # 1070–09) for 1 h at rt followed by washing. Next, the slide was incubated with a mouse anti-PE antibody which was labeled with 156Gd (DVS Biosciences, Cat. # 3156005B). After washing, half of the directly labeled antibodies were incubated for 5 h at rt (Table ), followed by washing and overnight incubation with the other half of the antibodies (Table ). Slides were washed, and an iridium DNA intercalator (Fluidigm) was incubated for 5 min followed by a final wash in H2O and drying. Slides were scanned using a Hyperion Imaging Mass Cytometer and analyzed as described in the supplemental data [ , , , ].

## Statistics, data visualization and survival analysis
Data were plotted using GraphPad Prism 9.0.1 (LCC). For statistical analysis comparing infiltration between patient groups, a Mann–Whitney U test was performed. For a paired comparison of infiltration in the epithelial and spindle cell compartments of biphasic synovial sarcomas, a Wilcoxon test was performed. The Kaplan–Meier plotter in SPSS version 25 (IBM) was used to estimate survival functions for patients with a primary synovial sarcoma according to infiltration of different immune cell subsets or histological subtype. Overall survival was defined as the time interval between resection date of the investigated tumor sample and the date of death, taking censoring into account. Because some patients underwent cytotoxic therapy before tumor sampling, we stratified the patients on whether they had received such therapy or not before tumor sampling. Differences in survival according to histological findings were investigated using a pooled log-rank test over both the treated and untreated strata. A p-value ≤ 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

## Results

## Biphasic synovial sarcoma is more densely infiltrated by immune cells than monophasic synovial sarcoma
To quantify the main immune cells in synovial sarcoma and to correlate their abundance to clinical parameters, we collected tumor samples derived from a total of 41 patients. Patient characteristics, and histological subtypes are summarized in Table  . From several patients, multiple tumor samples were included. Treatment naïve primary tumors were available from 21 patients. From 17 patients, we included primary tumors after cytotoxic therapy with either chemotherapy, radiotherapy or both. There was no difference in age or tumor size between patients of which treated or untreated tumors were included. Furthermore, there were untreated metastatic samples available from 15 patients. To quantify the number of T cells, myeloid cells and endothelial cells, and to detect epithelial differentiation in biphasic synovial sarcoma, we applied immunofluorescent labeling for CD3, CD31, CD68 and keratin on all included tumor samples (Fig.  A-B). To exactly detect tumor borders, immunohistochemistry for the SS18-SSX fusion protein was performed on the same tissue slides on which immunofluorescence was performed and thereafter images were digitally overlaid and tumor borders were manually drawn (Figure ). Analysis of the number of intratumoral immune cells per mm2 of tumor tissue in treatment-naïve primary patients revealed that the tumor microenvironment of synovial sarcoma is dominated by CD68+ myeloid cells. The number of intratumoral myeloid cells was tenfold higher than the number of intratumoral T cells in both monophasic and biphasic synovial sarcomas (Fig.  A-D). Furthermore, we observed that biphasic synovial sarcomas are more densely infiltrated by both T cells (p = 0.0073) and myeloid cells (p = 0.0387), whereas we did not observe a difference in microvessel density between the two histological subtypes (p = 0.3431 (Fig.  C–E). These data show that the immune microenvironment of untreated synovial sarcoma is dominated by myeloid cells and that biphasic synovial sarcomas are more densely infiltrated by immune cells than monophasic synovial sarcomas. 

## T-cell infiltration in primary synovial sarcoma is associated with prolonged survival
Because cytotoxic therapy is commonly used as a first-line treatment in synovial sarcoma, we investigated whether T-cell infiltration is influenced by cytotoxic therapy. We compared T-cell infiltration in treatment-naïve primary synovial sarcoma patients to patients who underwent chemotherapy or radiotherapy before sampling. In our unpaired analysis, we found no difference in levels of T-cell infiltration between tumors that were pretreated with cytotoxic therapy and those that were treatment naïve (p = 0.243) (Fig.  A), nor was there a difference when we compared samples treated with chemotherapy vs. radiotherapy (Figure S2). 
To investigate whether T-cell infiltration affects the survival of synovial sarcoma patients, we correlated T-cell infiltration in primary synovial sarcoma to the survival of patients. Patients were divided into a group with high T-cell infiltration and a group with low T-cell infiltration which was separated at the median. For statistical testing, the 35 patients were stratified on whether the patient had undergone cytotoxic therapy (n = 14) before tumor sampling or were treatment naïve (n = 21) (Table  ). The patients with the 50% highest intratumoral T-cell infiltration in primary synovial sarcoma had a significantly better overall survival than patients with the 50% lowest T-cell infiltration (p = 0.012) (Fig.  B). Because we found that biphasic synovial sarcomas have more intratumoral T cells than monophasic synovial sarcomas and intratumoral T cells correlated with survival, we investigated whether there was a difference in survival between monophasic and biphasic synovial sarcomas in our cohort. Survival analysis showed no difference in survival between monophasic and biphasic synovial sarcoma in our relatively small cohort (p = 0.403) (Fig.  C). Because most clinical studies investigate immunotherapeutic strategies in metastatic synovial sarcoma patients and not in patients with primary tumors, we investigated whether T-cell infiltration differed between metastatic and primary tumors by comparing T-cell infiltration in primary, treatment-naïve synovial sarcoma to metastatic synovial sarcoma samples. Metastatic synovial sarcomas were more densely infiltrated by T cells than primary tumors (p = 0.0180) (Fig.  A). There was no correlation between T-cell infiltration and localization of metastatic lesions in lung or lymph node tissue (Figure S3).
Together, these data demonstrate that T-cell infiltration of synovial sarcoma is not influenced by cytotoxic therapy and that in primary synovial sarcoma higher numbers of T cells are associated with a favorable prognosis.

## Cytotoxic therapy leads to influx of CD163+ myeloid cells into synovial sarcoma
To investigate whether myeloid cell infiltration in synovial sarcoma is influenced by cytotoxic therapy, we quantified myeloid cell infiltration, defined as the number of CD68+ cells/mm2 in primary tumors that were treatment-naïve, and primary tumors from patients that had undergone cytotoxic therapy prior to sampling. Although there were no differences in T-cell infiltration between these groups, we detected significantly more intratumoral CD68+ myeloid cells in primary tumors from patients that had undergone cytotoxic therapy prior to sampling compared to treatment naïve primary synovial sarcoma patients (p = 0.0286) (Fig.  A). There was no difference in CD68+ myeloid cell infiltration between patients that received chemotherapy vs patients that received radiotherapy (Figure S2). When we compared untreated primary tumors with metastatic lesions, we found, similar as for T cells, that CD68+ myeloid cells were present in significantly higher numbers in metastatic disease than in primary disease (p = 0.0034) (Fig.  A). In contrast to intratumoral T-cell frequency which correlated with survival, the number of intratumoral myeloid cells in primary synovial sarcoma did not correlate with survival of patients (p = 0.894) (Fig.  B). 
To investigate whether the myeloid cells in synovial sarcoma were CD163+ (as a marker for anti-inflammatory cells) or CD11c+ (as a marker for pro-inflammatory cells), we performed imaging mass cytometry (IMC) on six treatment-naïve primary synovial sarcomas and seven primary synovial sarcomas that underwent cytotoxic therapy before sampling. Single-cell segmentation was performed as described in the methods section, and tSNE embedding and manual gating were performed to quantify the proportion of all main cell lineages in the tumor microenvironment (Figure S4 and S5A). When myeloid cells were defined as CD14+ and/or CD68+ and/or CD204+ cells, we confirmed our fluorescence data that demonstrated that cytotoxic therapy of primary synovial sarcoma led to an increase in myeloid cell infiltration (p = 0.014) (Fig.  A). This increase in myeloid cells was due to a significant increase in CD163+ anti-inflammatory myeloid cells which was visible both at the relative level within the myeloid compartment as well as in absolute cell numbers (Fig.  B and S5). Overall, there were markedly more CD163+ myeloid cells than CD11c+ pro-inflammatory myeloid cells (Fig.  B-C). Cytotoxic therapy did not change the frequency of CD11c+ myeloid cells in our patient cohort (p = 0.8357) (Fig.  C). 
Together, these data show that a significant proportion of myeloid cells within synovial sarcoma has a CD163+ anti-inflammatory phenotype which is further increased upon cytotoxic therapy.

## Vascularization in synovial sarcoma is not influenced by cytotoxic therapy or disease progression and is not associated with survival
To study whether vascularization is influenced by cytotoxic therapy or differs between primary and metastatic synovial sarcoma, we quantified the number of CD31-positive endothelial cells in the same patient cohort. No differences were observed in microvessel density between untreated primary synovial sarcomas and those that underwent treatment with cytotoxic therapy (p = 0.4325) (Fig.  A), and there was no difference between samples that received chemotherapy vs radiotherapy (Figure S2). Unlike our findings for T cells and macrophages that were more abundant in metastatic disease, there was no difference in microvessel density between primary synovial sarcoma and metastatic disease (p = 0.5052) (Fig.  A). To investigate whether vascularization of primary tumors was associated with survival of synovial sarcoma patients, we compared survival of the 50% of patients with the highest microvessel density with the 50% of patients with the lowest microvessel cell density. There was no relation between microvessel cell numbers and survival in synovial sarcoma (p = 0.995) (Fig.  B). 

## In biphasic synovial sarcoma, the majority of T cells, myeloid cells and blood vessels is excluded from the epithelial compartment and located within the spindle cell compartment
Because biphasic synovial sarcomas are more densely infiltrated with immune cells than monophasic synovial sarcomas and the spindle cell compartment in these tumors morphologically resembles monophasic synovial sarcoma, we hypothesized there would be more immune cells in the epithelial compartment of biphasic tumors than in their spindle cell compartment. To test this hypothesis, we investigated the spatial distribution of immune cells in biphasic synovial sarcoma patients. Surprisingly, the epithelial component of biphasic synovial sarcoma, defined as keratin-positive areas, showed markedly less infiltration of immune cells and fewer blood vessels (Figs.  A–C, upper panels) as compared to the spindle cell component within the same tumor that was defined as keratin-negative areas. To quantify our observation, we used a watershed machine learning algorithm to separate the epithelial compartment from the spindle cell compartment on the basis of keratin staining and investigated the distribution of T cells, myeloid cells and endothelial cells within these compartments on a per tumor basis. We analyzed 13 biphasic synovial sarcoma tumors in which keratin-positive and negative areas were detected, which were derived from nine patients. In 8/9 patients, we observed markedly more T cells within the keratin-negative compartment of the tumor as compared to the keratin-positive compartment (p = 0.0012) (Fig.  A). A similar pattern was seen for the myeloid cells: 8/9 patients showed clearly more CD68+ myeloid cells/mm2 of tumor tissue in the keratin-negative compartment than in the keratin-positive compartment (p = 0.0005) (Fig.  B). The difference for microvessel density was even stronger: in all tumors, the vast majority of endothelial cells was located within the keratin-negative compartment, whereas in the keratin-positive compartment, barely any endothelial cells were measured (p = 0.0002) (Fig.  C). 
To investigate whether the exclusion of myeloid cells from the epithelial compartment in biphasic synovial sarcoma is limited to a certain phenotype or is similar for all myeloid cells, we quantified the spatial interaction of myeloid cell subsets with keratin+ and keratin− cells in all four biphasic synovial sarcomas for which we had IMC data available. To this end, we used the permutation-based algorithm as described in the methods section which showed that CD163+ and CD163− myeloid cells were equally excluded from the epithelial compartment in these tumors (Fig.  D–E). A similar pattern was observed when the myeloid cells were divided into CD11c+/CD11c− cells which were both equally excluded from the keratin-positive tumor areas (data not shown).
Together these data show that within biphasic synovial sarcomas, the immune cells reside primarily within the spindle cell compartment which is generally believed to resemble monophasic synovial sarcoma. Contrary to the morphological similarities, these data show that the spindle cell compartment of biphasic synovial sarcoma is markedly more infiltrated than monophasic synovial sarcomas which indicates that the microenvironment of this compartment is immunologically different from monophasic synovial sarcoma.

## Discussion
In this study, we show that in primary synovial sarcoma, higher numbers of intratumoral T cells were associated with a prolonged overall survival of patients, suggesting that T cells play a role in preventing disease progression. This seems to be contradicted by our finding that metastatic synovial sarcomas showed increased levels of T-cell infiltration compared to primary synovial sarcoma, but the difference between primary and metastatic cancer may mainly be explained by the different tissue environments these tumors are located in. Primary synovial sarcomas arise in connective tissues that intrinsically have low immune infiltration while metastases mainly arise in lung and lymph node tissues that attract immune cells. We demonstrated that the tumor microenvironment of synovial sarcoma is dominated by myeloid cells, of which the CD163+ immunosuppressive compartment was markedly expanded after cytotoxic therapy which indicates that these myeloid cells are recruited to clear debris of tumor cells that were killed by the cytotoxic therapy. Finally, we showed that biphasic synovial sarcomas were more densely infiltrated by immune cells than monophasic synovial sarcomas. Because the main difference between these histological subtypes is the presence of an epithelial component in biphasic synovial sarcoma, we initially assumed that the epithelial component would be responsible for the increased number of immune cells. However, in contrast to our hypothesis, the immune cells in biphasic synovial sarcoma were mainly located in the spindle cell compartment and excluded from the epithelial compartment which indicates that the spindle cell compartment of biphasic synovial sarcoma is immunologically different from monophasic synovial sarcoma despite their morphological similarities.
Immune checkpoint inhibition (ICB) in synovial sarcoma patients showed limited responses in patients with advanced disease, suggesting a lack of tumor-reactive T cells in most metastatic lesions [ – ] although we found more intratumoral T cells in metastatic synovial sarcoma than in primary synovial sarcoma. These seemingly conflicting data could be explained by the difference in baseline infiltration of the tissues compartments that primary and metastatic synovial sarcomas reside in. Synovial sarcomas arise from connective tissues that contain low counts of T cells which suggests that the intratumoral T cells we detected in primary tumors had actively migrated into the tumor. In contrast, metastatic synovial sarcoma mainly grows in lung or lymph node tissues that by themselves are homing sites of T cells. Therefore, a large proportion of the T cells in metastatic synovial sarcoma are likely bystander T cells that may be trapped from the surrounding tissue and not reactive T cells that infiltrated the tumor which would also explain the low response rates of ICB in metastatic synovial sarcoma. In contrast, the correlation between T-cell infiltration and survival in primary disease suggests the presence of a low degree of pre-existing anti-tumor immunity that might be potentiated in a setting of neoadjuvant ICB before surgery in primary synovial sarcoma patients. Exploring such an approach is also supported by results in glioblastoma, similar to synovial sarcoma a low-mutational burden cancer, in which blocking PD-1 after surgery showed no effect while PD-1 blockade before surgery resulted in increased overall survival of patients in a randomized controlled trial [ , ]. The influx of CD163+ myeloid cells after cytotoxic therapy in synovial sarcoma aligns with previous observations in soft-tissue sarcomas [ ]. This increase is the end-result of the process in which the damage caused by the cytotoxic therapy is cleared. The recruitment of CD163+ myeloid cells by cytotoxic therapy raises the question whether cytotoxic therapy would synergize with immunotherapeutic approaches by killing part of the tumor cells and inducing tissue inflammation as seen in non-small cell lung cancer [ ] or if it would hamper the process by increasing the influx of CD163+ myeloid cells, which has implications for the optimal timing of immunotherapy and cytotoxic therapy.
The local exclusion of immune cells and endothelial cells from the epithelial compartment in biphasic synovial sarcoma illustrates a specific tissue organization that can also be observed in healthy epithelial tissues: most immune cells and endothelial cells are located in the stromal compartment and excluded from the epithelial compartment [ ]. Furthermore, the epithelial cells in healthy epithelium are always supported by a stromal compartment that is separated from the epithelium by a basement membrane [ ]. In biphasic synovial sarcoma, Guarino also found that basement membrane components collagen type IV and laminin separate the spindle cell and epithelial compartments [ ]. Because the epithelial compartment of biphasic synovial sarcoma is always intermingled with a spindle cell compartment, usually separated by a basement membrane and the spatial organization is similar to that of healthy epithelium, we propose that a similar relation exists between the two compartments of biphasic synovial sarcoma, wherein the epithelial compartment is supported by the spindle cell compartment. The presence of a basement membrane and other epithelial proteins such as tight junctions might be responsible for the exclusion of immune cells from the epithelial compartment of biphasic synovial sarcoma. Based on morphology, the spindle cell compartment in biphasic synovial sarcoma is generally believed to resemble monophasic synovial sarcomas which consists entirely of similarly looking, spindle-shaped cells [ ]. Nevertheless, our data on immune infiltration suggests otherwise. Biphasic synovial sarcomas are infiltrated by significantly more immune cells than monophasic synovial sarcomas and the immune cells mainly reside within the spindle cell compartment and not in the epithelial compartment. This shows that the microenvironment of the spindle cell compartment of biphasic synovial sarcoma is immunologically different from monophasic synovial sarcoma. It remains an open question whether the immune cells are attracted by the spindle cell compartment itself, or that the epithelial compartment is responsible for the difference, for example by excreting chemoattractant cytokines.
Even though we found no difference in overall survival between patients with monophasic or biphasic synovial sarcoma in the absence of immunotherapy, the higher level of immune infiltration in biphasic synovial sarcoma could become relevant in a setting of immunotherapy with either ICB or adoptive cell therapy. Another immunological difference between monophasic and biphasic synovial sarcoma is that biphasic synovial sarcoma has significantly higher HLA class I expression levels than monophasic synovial sarcoma, and HLA is more highly expressed on cancer cells of the epithelial compartment than of cancer cells of the spindle cell compartment [ ]. Because HLA class I expression on tumor cells is needed to present peptides to T cells, this might have implications for the response to immunotherapy as well. In-depth analysis of clinical trials may elucidate whether monophasic or biphasic synovial sarcomas respond differently to ICB and/or adoptive cell therapy since clinical trials thus far have not specifically reported outcomes of ICB or ACT based on the histological subtype of synovial sarcoma [ – , , ].
The main limitation of our study is the relatively small patient cohort available for analysis. While our findings are statistically significant, they need further validation in larger, independent cohorts, especially the data that involves patient survival analysis.
In summary, we demonstrated that intratumoral T cells in primary synovial sarcoma are prognostic for patient outcomes which suggests that the use of ICB in a neoadjuvant setting might be explored. Furthermore, we showed that biphasic synovial sarcomas are more densely infiltrated by immune cells than monophasic synovial sarcoma and that biphasic synovial sarcoma has a tissue organization that is similar to healthy epithelium in which the immune cells are mainly located in the spindle cell compartment. These findings may have implications for immunotherapy in synovial sarcoma and suggest that biphasic synovial sarcoma might respond better to immunotherapy than monophasic synovial sarcoma.
