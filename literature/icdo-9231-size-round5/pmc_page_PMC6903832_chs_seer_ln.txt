 Abstract

 Background

 Regional lymph node involvement is thought to be rare in patients with chondrosarcoma, but its actual prevalence is unclear. Additionally, it is often not considered when prognostic factors are analyzed in patients with chondrosarcoma. However, it has been well established that lymph node involvement is a poor prognostic marker in patients with many types of bone and soft tissue sarcoma, including rhabdomyosarcoma, osteosarcoma, and Ewing’s sarcoma. Although lymph node metastases are rare among all sarcoma types, it is important to consider whether lymph node metastases should be assessed in patients with chondrosarcoma because these metastases may impact survival.

 Questions/purposes

 (1) What is the reported prevalence of regional lymph node involvement in patients with chondrosarcoma? (2) Do patients who have chondrosarcomas with regional lymph node involvement have different clinicopathologic presentations and survival than patients without regional lymph node involvement? (3) Is regional lymph node involvement independently associated with prognosis in patients with chondrosarcoma?

 Methods

 The data of patients with chondrosarcoma registered in the Surveillance Epidemiology and End Results database (SEER) (1988-2015) were analyzed for the reported prevalence of regional lymph node involvement and its relationship with clinicopathologic features and the 5-year overall survival rate. From 1988 to 2015, 5528 patients with chondrosarcoma were registered in the SEER database. After screening by the inclusion criterion—chondrosarcoma as the first primary tumor, diagnosis with histology confirmation, patients with active followup and available information about regional node status—3374 patients met the inclusion criteria and were analyzed. Demographics and clinicopathologic data were compared using chi-square or Fisher’s exact tests. Logistic regression analysis was used to assess the adjusted odds ratio. The overall survival rate was estimated with Kaplan-Meier curves and log-rank tests. Univariate and multivariate analyses of overall survival were performed with Cox proportional hazard models. In addition, a series of sensitivity analyses were performed to assess the robustness of the final Cox proportional hazard model.

 Results

 Forty-four patients (1.3%) were recorded in the database as having regional lymph node involvement at the time of the primary diagnosis. Lymph node metastases were more likely to be reported in an extraskeletal primary site (3% [13 of 426] versus 1% [31 of 2948], adjusted odds ratio [OR] = 2.9, 95% CI, 1.5–5.8; p = 0.003) for bone primary sites and tumors with maximum diameter ≥ 8 cm (2% [26 of 1045] versus 1% [10 of 1075], adjusted OR = 2.9, 95% CI, 1.3–6.3; p = 0.008) and poorer differentiation (4% [24 of 608] versus 1% [14 of 2308], adjusted OR = 4.0, 95% CI, 2.0–8.2; p < 0.001), and in those with distant metastases (7% [14 of 203] versus 1% [30 of 3148], adjusted OR = 3.5, 95% CI, 1.7–7.1, p = 0.001). The 5-year overall survival rates of patients with and without regional lymph node involvement were 28% (95% CI, 15–42%) and 77% (95% CI, 75–78%), respectively (p < 0.001). After controlling for age, sex, race, grade, metastatic status, size, and histologic subtype, the presence of regional lymph node involvement was associated with poorer survival (hazard ratio, 2.20; 95% CI, 1.50–3.24; p < 0.001); this finding was confirmed in several sensitivity analyses.

 Conclusion

 The prevalence of regional lymph node involvement in patients with chondrosarcoma was 1.3% in the SEER database. Although chondrosarcomas are rare, patients with chondrosarcomas who have regional node metastases have a poorer prognosis than those who have not reported to have them. This may underrepresent the true proportion of patients with lymph node metastases given the inaccuracies of reporting in this database, but we believe these findings indicate that clinicians should examine patients more carefully for chondrosarcoma with lymph node metastases. Future studies are needed to assess potential treatment strategies to improve the prognosis of these patients.

 Level of Evidence

 Level III, prognostic study.

 Introduction

 After osteosarcoma, chondrosarcoma is the second most prevalent type of primary malignant bone sarcoma, accounting for approximately 20% to 30% of all skeletal sarcomas [ 3 , 26 ]. Effective clinical treatment is primarily restricted to surgical resection because this disease is not sensitive to radiation or chemotherapy [ 11 , 16 ], partially because it has a low mitotic index and poor vascularity [ 4 , 11 , 35 ].

 Identifying prognostic factors is valuable for clinical decision making. Metastatic patterns in bone malignancies are typically hematogenous, with the lungs and bone being the most prevalent metastatic sites [ 19 , 23 , 25 ]. Distant metastases have been demonstrated to be independently associated with poorer survival in patients with chondrosarcoma [ 21 , 35 ]. Regional lymph node involvement is thought to be rare in chondrosarcoma, although the prevalence of regional lymph node involvement has not been well defined in previous studies, which were mainly case reports [ 13 , 18 ] and a single-institution retrospective study with a small sample size (49 patients) [ 9 ] or were focused on chondrosarcoma arising in a specific site (larynx) [ 7 ]. Likewise, the effect of regional lymph node involvement on the survival of patients with chondrosarcoma has not been systematically evaluated. However, although lymph node metastases are also rare in other bone and soft tissue sarcomas, the status of regional lymph nodes has been used to stratify risk in patients with rhabdomyosarcoma, Ewing’s sarcoma, and osteosarcoma [ 1 , 24 , 29 , 36 ]. Lymph node metastases in patients with chondrosarcoma may also be associated with poorer survival, and this may be important to consider when planning treatment or advising patients about their condition. However, currently, the proportion of patients with nodal metastases at the time of diagnosis and the impact of having these metastases is not well understood.

 With this in mind, we asked: (1) What is the reported prevalence of regional lymph node involvement in patients with chondrosarcoma? (2) Do patients who have chondrosarcomas with regional lymph node involvement have different clinicopathologic presentations and survival than patients without regional lymph node involvement? (3) Is regional lymph node involvement independently associated with prognosis in patients with chondrosarcoma?

 Patients and Methods

 Patient Selection

 To answer these questions, we evaluated the clinical characteristics and outcomes of patients with chondrosarcoma with or without regional lymph node involvement using a large population with adequate length of followup. Data were obtained from the US National Cancer Institute’s Surveillance Epidemiology and End Results (SEER) database, which consists of patient-level data derived from 18 cancer registries and covers approximately 28% of the US population [ 20 ]. Patients with chondrosarcoma who were diagnosed between 1988 and 2015 and who were enrolled in the SEER database with their corresponding details were extracted using the SEER*Stat version 8.3.5 software (National Cancer Institute, Bethesda, MD, USA). Two authors (LW, CT) independently extracted and reviewed the collected data to ensure adequate quality (Fig. 1 ), when there was a discrepancy, these two authors cross-reviewed and reached an agreement before conducting the analyses.

 We chose the SEER database because it is the only comprehensive source of population-based information in the United States that contains stage (tumor size, lymph node involvement, and distant metastasis) of cancer at the time of diagnosis and patient survival data. Currently, SEER covers approximately 30% of the US population, thus encompassing a substantial sample size that would not otherwise be possible with any single or multi-institutional experiences. Although the SEER database has several shortcomings, including a limited number of variables available and loss of several outcomes of interest, such as local or distant recurrence, we did not feel those were disqualifying for our purposes because most of the variables that may affect the patient prognosis, such as age, tumor size, grade, and metastatic status as well as the outcome of interest (5-year survival status) in this study were all included.

 The inclusion criteria were a diagnosis of chondrosarcoma that was made in a living patient, not from autopsy or a death certificate only; a diagnosis confirmed with microscopy, not with radiology or a clinical diagnosis only; patients with chondrosarcoma as the first primary malignancy; and patients with available information about regional lymph node involvement. We excluded 404 patients with extraskeletal myxoid chondrosarcoma because it is a misnomer to call it a real chondrosarcoma; we also excluded approximately one-fifth of the potentially eligible patients (899 of 4273) because the lymph node status was not reported (Fig. 1 ).

 The International Classification of Diseases for Oncology morphology codes for patients with chondrosarcoma who were eligible for inclusion in this study were 9220/3, chondrosarcoma not otherwise specified; 9240/3, mesenchymal chondrosarcoma; 9221/3, juxtacortical chondrosarcoma; 9231/3, myxoid chondrosarcoma; 9242/3, clear cell chondrosarcoma; and 9243/3, dedifferentiated chondrosarcoma. Patients with chondrosarcoma of bone and soft tissue were also included in this analysis, skeletal (axial bone, extremity bone, and bone [not other specified]) and extraskeletal (arising in site other than bone) chondrosarcoma were classified based on the International Classification of Diseases for Oncology topography codes in the SEER database. From 1988 to 2015, 5528 patients with chondrosarcoma were enrolled in the SEER database. A total of 3374 patients met the inclusion criteria and were included in this study (Table 1 ).

 We examined regional lymph node involvement by using all available relevant SEER data fields, including the extent of disease 10 coding schemes; collaborative staging lymph node status; American Joint Committee on Cancer Staging coding systems; and regional lymph node data field. Collaborative staging data is specific to patients with a diagnosis from 2004 to the present, while extent of disease 10 covers patients with a diagnosis from 1988 to 2003, Both American Joint Committee on Cancer Staging and regional node data field cover patients with a diagnosis from 1988 to the present. All data fields were determined with imaging, physical examination, and/or pathology findings; only regional node data field relied exclusively upon pathologic confirmation. To ensure data credibility, we compared each field with the others to assess internal consistency when patients had available data in multiple fields. Patients with conflicting data about regional status were excluded and accounted for the number of patients with unknown regional node status.

 Covariates

 We evaluated patient characteristics and survival outcomes based on the presence or absence of regional lymph node involvement. We regarded patients with distant lymph node metastasis but without regional node status record as having unknown regional node status. Demographic variables in the analyses included age, sex, race, and year of diagnosis. Clinical characteristics in the analyses included tumor site, grade, metastatic status, size, and histologic subtype. Distant metastasis refers to a primary tumor that has spread to distant organs or distant lymph nodes. The SEER database also provides data on the length of followup and patient’s vital status at the time of last followup, enabling us to calculate the overall survival rate.

 Statistical Analyses

 We separated patients into two groups: those with and those without regional lymph node involvement. For categorical variables, we used the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, and we used two sample t-tests for continuous variables to determine the differences in demographic and tumor characteristics between these two groups. Overall survival was estimated using the Kaplan-Meier curve, and a log-rank test was applied to assess for a difference in survival between the groups. The median followup time of the research group was 108 months (interquartile range, 57-166 months), 22% (728 of 3374) patients lost to followup before 5 years and regarded as censored patients. We used a Cox proportional hazard regression analysis to determine the hazard ratio (HR) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses were performed using 5-year overall survival as the outcome measure. The choice of variables in the multivariate analysis was based on the p value (< 0.1) of the variable under consideration in the univariate analysis (see Table; Supplemental Digital Content 1, http://links.lww.com/CORR/A201 ). Proportional hazards assumption was evaluated with time-dependent covariates. SEER*Stat version 8.3.5 (National Cancer Institute, Bethesda, MD, USA) was used to access the SEER database. All statistical analyses were performed using STATA, version 14.0 (Stata Corp, College Station, TX, USA).

 Sensitivity Analyses

 Along with our main analyses, we performed five sensitivity analyses to test the robustness of the Cox proportional hazard model. In the first analysis, we excluded patients with unknown tumor size to reduce the potential bias caused by this group of patients. The second analysis involved only patients with skeletal chondrosarcoma, which was a more typical origin of this malignancy. To diminish the potential impact of improved treatment within years, the third sensitivity analysis was restricted to patients diagnosed from 2010 to 2015. Next, we also conducted an analysis of patients with grade III or IV chondrosarcoma. The final sensitivity analysis only included patients with pathologically confirmed regional node status to avoid potential bias from the diagnosis method of lymph node involvement.

 Results

 Regional Lymph Node Involvement in Chondrosarcoma Is Rare

 We found that 44 of 3374 (1.3%) patients were reported to have regional lymph node involvement at the time of the initial diagnosis (see Table; Supplemental Digital Content 2, http://links.lww.com/CORR/A202 ) and 3330 (98.7%) did not have regional lymph node involvement. One patient had inconsistent records of regional node involvement in the SEER database and we excluded that individual as a patient with unknown regional node status.

 Patient Characteristics Differed Between Patients with and without Regional Node Involvement

 All demographic features, including age, sex, race, and year of diagnosis, were similar in patients with and without regional lymph node involvement (Table 1 ). With respect to tumor characteristics, patients with regional lymph node involvement were more likely to have extraskeletal tumors than those without (3% [13 of 426] versus 1% [31 of 2948], adjusted OR (odds ratio) = 2.9, 95% CI, 1.5–5.8; p = 0.003), poorer differentiation (Grade III or IV: 4% [24 of 608] versus 1% [14 of 2308], adjusted OR = 4.0, 95% CI, 2.0–8.2; p < 0.001), distant metastatic disease (7% [14 of 203] versus 1% [30 of 3148], adjusted OR = 3.5, 95% CI, 1.7–7.1; p = 0.001), and tumor dimensions ≥ 8 cm (2% [26 of 1045] versus 1% [10 of 1075], adjusted OR = 2.9, 95% CI, 1.3–6.3; p = 0.008).

 Regional Lymph Node Involvement is Independently Associated with Poorer Survival in Patients with Chondrosarcoma

 The 5-year overall survival of patients with chondrosarcoma with regional lymph node involvement was inferior to that of patients without regional lymph node involvement (28%, 95% CI, 15–42 versus 77%, 95% CI, 75–78; p < 0.001) (Fig. 2 A). A subgroup analysis also confirmed these results. For patients with extraskeletal chondrosarcoma, regional lymph node involvement was also associated with worse 5-year survival: 36% for patients with regional lymph node involvement (95% CI, 12–61) and 74% for those without (95% CI, 69–79); p < 0.001) (Fig. 2 B). For patients presenting with metastatic disease, regional lymph node involvement was associated with worse 5-year overall survival; the estimated rate was 0 for those with regional lymph node involvement and 17% for those without (95% CI, 11–22; p = 0.002) (Fig. 2 C). For patients without metastatic disease, regional lymph node involvement also indicated inferior 5-year overall survival rates: 42% for those with regional lymph node involvement (95% CI, 24–60) and 80% for those without (95% CI, 79–82; p < 0.001) (Fig. 2 D). In Grade I/II tumors, the 5-year overall survival was 39% (95% CI, 13–65) in patients with regional lymph node involvement and 85% (95% CI, 83–86; p < 0.001) (Fig. 2 E) in patients without it. In Grade III/IV tumors, the 5-year overall survival was 20% (95% CI, 7–38) in patients with regional lymph node involvement and 48% (95% CI, 44–53), in patients without it (p < 0.001) (Fig. 2 F).

 Next, we built a multivariate Cox proportional hazard model to assess the independent impact of regional node involvement on the 5-year overall survival. Based on the univariate analysis results (see Table; Supplemental Digital Content 1, http://links.lww.com/CORR/A201 ), covariates included in the Cox model were age, sex, race, grade, metastatic status, size, and histologic type. All of the covariates met the proportional hazard assumption. The estimated HR of death in patients with regional lymph node involvement was 2.20 (95% CI, 1.50–3.24; p < 0.001) compared with patients without regional lymph node involvement (Table 2 ). The credibility of our Cox proportional hazard model was further confirmed with the following five sensitivity analyses (Table 3 ). First, because information on the tumor size was not available for 18% (624 of 3374) of the enrolled patients, we excluded these patients in our final Cox model, with a similar result (HR, 1.93; 95% CI, 1.19–3.13; p = 0.003) between patients with regional lymph node involvement and those without for death. The second sensitivity analysis was identical to the final model but only used patients with skeletal chondrosarcoma (n = 2948), a more typical site of origin for this disease. This model repeated the results of our final model (HR, 2.92; 95% CI, 1.88–4.55; p < 0.001) for patients with regional lymph node involvement compared with patients without. Given recent developments in chondrosarcoma therapy, in the third sensitivity analysis, we used patients with a diagnosis in a recent time period from 2010 to 2015 (n = 1202). Again, this new model confirmed the results of our final model (HR, 2.69; 95% CI, 1.16–6.20; p = 0.021 for death in patients with regional lymph node involvement compared with patients without). The fourth sensitivity analysis was performed for patients who had a poorly differentiated chondrosarcoma (Grade III or IV, n = 608), and we obtained a similar result to those seen in our original model (HR, 2.07; 95% CI, 1.24–3.43; p = 0.005 for death in patients with regional lymph node involvement compared with patients without). The final sensitivity analysis was performed by restricting patient cohort to patients with only pathologically confirmed positive (n = 21) and negative (n = 253) regional lymph nodes based on the regional nodes data field in the SEER database, we found regional node involvement remained predictive of inferior survival compared with patients without regional node involvement (HR, 3.00; 95% CI, 1.62–5.57; p < 0.001).

 Discussion

 Chondrosarcoma is a heterogeneous group of aggressive tumors characterized by the production of a cartilaginous matrix by malignant chondrocytes [ 29 ]. Factors associated with poorer survival in patients with chondrosarcoma include older age at the time of diagnosis, the presence of distant metastasis, and larger or higher-grade tumors [ 5 , 8 , 12 , 17 , 27 ]. Regional lymph node metastasis is thought to be rare in patients with chondrosarcoma, although the prevalence of regional lymph node involvement was poorly defined in existing reports [ 7 , 9 , 13 , 18 ]. Likewise, the impact of regional lymph node involvement on chondrosarcoma is also poorly understood, and we could find no reports on the survival of patients with and without lymph node metastasis. In this study, we confirmed that regional lymph node involvement is rare in patients with chondrosarcoma, with a prevalence of 1.3%. We also found patients with regional node metastasis had different tumor characteristics compared with patients without regional node metastasis. Most importantly, we identified that regional lymph node involvement independently indicated poorer survival in patients with chondrosarcoma; this may be important to consider when planning treatment or advising patients about their condition.

 There were several inherent limitations to the use of the SEER registry. The SEER database allowed us to examine a rare subgroup of patients who had chondrosarcoma within a relatively large cohort, but the SEER database did not provide detailed information about the patients’ treatments. For example, records on chemotherapy plans, detailed surgical methods, and management of regional lymph nodes with radiation or surgical resection were not available. Therefore, it was impossible to evaluate the effect of different management approaches in these patients. We think that knowledge about the regional lymph node status is important in guiding future therapeutic regimens and informing patients about their prognosis. Additionally, we could not make specific therapeutic recommendations for patients with regional lymph node involvement without complete treatment details. Patients who have chondrosarcoma with regional lymph node involvement may benefit from aggressive local control of infiltrated lymph nodes via surgical resection or radiotherapy, but there are no data to support or refute this idea. Second, because examining the status of regional lymph node involvement is not a routine practice in the care of patients with chondrosarcoma, we excluded 21% of patients with chondrosarcoma in the SEER database because their lymph node status was unknown. Thus, we may have underestimated the true prevalence of lymph node metastasis in patients with bone and soft tissue chondrosarcomas. Whether more complete data would influence our findings needs future validation in a clinical trial or other population-based research studies, but we strongly feel that awareness of the possible presence of lymph node metastases is important and underappreciated by clinicians. Moreover, an inherent limitation of all SEER-based studies was the lack of central pathology review and uniformity of laboratory techniques in each registries SEER included, which may cause potential discrepancy of the histologic diagnosis. We could not guarantee patients diagnosed with extraskeletal “Chondrosarcoma, not other specified” did not have extraskeletal myxoid chondrosarcoma, which is not considered a chondrosarcoma, but we have tried to diminish the potential inaccuracies by only including patients with histological confirmation and excluding those patients with extraskeletal myxoid chondrosarcoma. In addition, we found that regional node involvement is not an independent prognosis factor in extraskeletal myxoid chondrosarcoma patients (see Table, Supplemental Digital Content 3, http://links.lww.com/CORR/A203 ), thus we can speculate that regional lymph node involvement would still remain predictive of inferior prognosis after excluding the potential extraskeletal myxoid chondrosarcoma from the group of “not other specified chondrosarcoma” patients. Another concern is the potential discrepancy of the accuracy of lymph node metastasis since only one SEER data field (positive regional node) provides data on histologically confirmed regional nodes, while other data fields were based on a composite of clinical, radiographic and/or pathologic data. This is rational because lymph node disease could now be detected more feasibly with advanced imaging equipment and technology, such as positron emission tomography-computed tomography (PET-CT); however, false positives occur, thus we cannot confirm that all 44 patients with regional node disease are histologically positive. Given this condition, we performed a subanalysis with only patients having pathologically confirmed lymph node status, and the results still showed that regional node involvement was associated with poorer survival. We also noted that this study incorporated a large number of patients across the spectrum of chondrosarcoma, and a series of sensitivity analyses confirmed the accuracy of the present study's results.

 Our results regarding lymph node involvement were similar to those of two previous studies. One study [ 8 ] reported that one of 46 patients (2.2%) with nonosseous chondrosarcoma and another [ 7 ] found that five of 150 patients (3.3%) with larynx chondrosarcoma had regional lymph node involvement. This suggests that when a diagnosis of extraskeletal chondrosarcoma is made, the regional lymph nodes should be examined carefully. Our rate is similar to that reported in other studies on osteosarcoma (0.8%-2.7%) [ 2 , 31 , 33 ]. The rarity of regional lymph node metastasis in patients with bone sarcoma may be partially attributed to the paucity of lymphatic channels in bone tissue. Edwards et al. [ 6 ] reported that lymphatic vessels were lacking in either normal bones or benign and malignant bone tumors, but that they could be found in tumors that have extended into the periosteum and surrounding soft tissues, suggesting that extraskeletal chondrosarcoma tends to be involved in the regional lymph nodes, and a chondrosarcoma with regional lymph node involvement is more likely to have a larger tumor diameter.

 More importantly, our study is the first we know of to indicate that patients with chondrosarcoma and regional lymph node involvement have inferior 5-year survival outcomes. Previously, the paucity evidence about the impact of regional node involvement on the prognosis of chondrosarcoma might just result from its rare prevalence. However, several reports provide hints for the potential adverse effects of regional lymph node involvement on chondrosarcoma [ 7 , 12 , 34 ]. Giuffrida et al. [ 12 ] reported a large sample size study that included 2890 patients with chondrosarcoma to identify its prognostic characteristics, although regional node status was not a covariate their analysis model. They found that chondrosarcoma at the regional stage, meaning that the tumor had extended to the regional nodes and/or adjacent tissues, had inferior survival compared with chondrosarcoma patients with local disease only. In a review of 150 patients with larynx chondrosarcoma, Ferlito et al. [ 7 ] found that three of five patients with cervical node involvement died within 3 years after surgery, and the other two patients did not have followup data. This suggested that regional node involvement had a negative impact on larynx chondrosarcoma patients given that patients with chondrosarcoma in the larynx without lymph node metastases exhibited excellent long-term survival after surgery [ 22 , 32 ]. In patients with osteosarcoma, regional nodal metastasis, whether alone or coexistent with other metastatic disease was associated with poorer survival [ 34 ], indicating that regional node involvement might facilitate tumor progression; however, the exact mechanism remains elusive.

 We also showed that after controlling for age, sex, race, tumor size, metastatic status, grade, and histologic subtype, the reported presence of regional lymph node metastasis was associated with a worse prognosis than for patients without reported lymph node metastasis. A series of sensitivity analyses further verified that regional lymph node involvement was an independent and inferior indicator of prognosis in patients with chondrosarcoma. Distant metastatic disease has been demonstrated to be the most important predictor of poor survival in chondrosarcoma [ 5 , 10 , 30 ]; however, we found evidence that regional node involvement was also associated with poorer survival even in patients without metastatic disease at initial presentation. This may be in part because the invaded regional lymph nodes serve as a transfer station and initial “selection” sites where tumor cells with metastatic competence seed, expand, and are selected for increasingly immunosuppressive variants which then spread to other sites [ 14 , 37 ]. This is similar to the regional progression and risk of recurrence that has been observed in patients with bladder and breast cancer who have regional lymph node disease [ 15 , 28 ]. However, this relationship cannot be assessed in the SEER database because only the survival status of the last followup was recorded. Given the strong adverse prognostic impact of regional lymph node metastasis, we recommend that suspicious regional lymph nodes found during a physical examination or imaging undergo a biopsy to confirm the regional lymph node’s status and accurately diagnose this rare subgroup. This will help clinicians identify patients with chondrosarcoma who have a higher survival risk, particularly among patients without distant metastatic lesions. We could not assess whether or not a lymph node resection will affect survival in patients with involved regional nodes.

 In conclusion, our study provided evidence that there were differences in tumor characteristics and survival among patients who had chondrosarcomas, with or without regional lymph node involvement. The prevalence of lymph node metastasis in the patients from the SEER database is likely is an underestimate, but it indicates that more careful assessment might be important both for advising patients about their prognosis and for developing better treatment options for patients who have lymph node metastases. In light of the independent association between regional lymph node involvement and poorer survival, patients with chondrosarcomas who present with lymph node involvement may be reasonable candidates for novel therapeutic strategies and clinical trials that are currently applied to patients with distant metastases and poorly differentiated chondrosarcomas. The biological mechanisms underlying tumor dissemination via the regional lymph nodes, and the association of this finding with a greater risk of death, both merit further research.
