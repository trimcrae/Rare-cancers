## Key Points
•Targeted lipid nanoparticles deliver fusion transcript-specific siRNA to primary AML cells, achieving fusion gene knockdown.
•Loss of RUNX1::RUNX1T1 promotes granulocytic and eosinophilic differentiation to more normal states while depleting stem cell populations.

## Abstract
Chromosomal rearrangements that generate novel fusion genes are a hallmark of acute myeloid leukemia (AML). Depletion experiments in cell line models have suggested that their continued expression is required for maintaining their leukemic phenotype and that fusion genes therefore represent ideal cancer-specific therapeutic targets. However, the extent to which this result holds true for the different stages of hematopoietic development in primary cells and whether therapeutic agents can be efficiently delivered to those cells is still unclear. In this study, we demonstrate that primary AML cells harboring the chromosomal translocation t(8;21) are critically dependent on the corresponding fusion gene, RUNX1::RUNX1T1, to suppress differentiation and maintain stemness. Silencing RUNX1::RUNX1T1 expression using small interfering RNA (siRNA)–loaded lipid nanoparticles induces substantial changes in chromatin accessibility, thereby redirecting the leukemia-associated transcriptional network toward a myeloid differentiation program. Single-cell analyses reveal that this transcriptional reprogramming is associated with the depletion of immature stem and progenitor-like cell populations, accompanied by an expansion of granulocytic and eosinophilic/mast cell–like populations with impaired self-renewal capacity. These findings underscore the essential role of RUNX1::RUNX1T1 in sustaining AML and highlight the therapeutic potential of targeting fusion gene expression in primary AML cells.

While t(8:21) acute myeloid leukemia (AML) has a favorable prognosis, recurrences are common and new therapies are needed. Derevyanko and colleagues used an innovative lipid nanoparticle (LNP) delivery system and chemically modified small interfering RNAs (siRNAs) to target the RUNX1::RUNX1T1 fusion breakpoint in samples from patients with t(8:21) AML. They found that transient silencing of RUNX1::RUNX1T1 fusion gene expression is sufficient to decrease stem cell self-renewal and cause partial myeloid differentiation. These data reveal insights into the requirement for expression of the fusion and may suggest a future therapeutic pathway.

## Introduction
Current treatment for acute myeloid leukemia (AML) relies heavily on intensive chemotherapy, which is associated with significant toxicities, profoundly affecting patients' quality of life. , , Despite these aggressive regimens, a significant proportion of 60% to 70% of adult patients with AML aged ≤60 years ultimately succumb to the disease. Pediatric patients have a better prognosis, with an overall survival of ∼70%. , However, due to their longer life expectancy, pediatric survivors are disproportionately affected by the long-term adverse effects of chemotherapy. , This highlights the urgent need for more targeted, leukemia-specific therapies for AML.
A hallmark of AML is various chromosomal rearrangements, which result in the expression of leukemia-specific fusion proteins in ∼30% of adult AML cases and >50% of pediatric AML cases. , Expression of these proteins is leukemia initiating and is required to maintain the leukemogenicity of AML cells, thus representing highly attractive therapeutic targets. However, many AML fusion genes encode transcriptional regulators, which are notoriously challenging to target using conventional drug design due to a lack of stably structured, druggable regions. Moreover, these regions are often shared with the wild-type protein encoded by the intact allele, leading to undesirable on-target toxicities. 
Among AML fusion genes, RUNX1::RUNX1T1 is the most prevalent recurrent genetic aberration in pediatric and younger adult AML, occurring in 10% to 15% of cases. Prior studies have highlighted the role of RUNX1::RUNX1T1 in driving leukemic self-renewal and impeding differentiation in cell lines and transgenic models. , , , However, these models fail to fully capture the intratumoral and interpatient heterogeneity of AML. The role of leukemic fusion genes in primary patient-derived AML cells remains poorly understood, largely due to the experimental challenges in perturbing fusion gene expression specifically within these cells.
Critical questions remain unanswered. How does the loss of fusion gene expression affect the viability, differentiation, and self-renewal of various AML cell populations, and how do these effects vary between patients? What differentiation pathways are activated upon gene silencing, and can restoration of fusion gene expression reverse differentiation? Is there a “point of no return” when AML cells lose stemness entirely, or can they retain or regain self-renewal despite adopting a differentiated phenotype?
Addressing these questions necessitates tools that can specifically inhibit fusion gene expression in primary AML cells. Previous work, including ours, has demonstrated the feasibility of targeting fusion genes using small interfering RNAs (siRNAs) designed to specifically bind the fusion junction site. , , , Encapsulation of these siRNAs in lipid nanoparticles (LNPs) facilitates efficient and specific knockdown of fusion gene expression in cell lines and patient-derived cells, both in vitro and in mice undergoing xenotransplantation. , , Leveraging the power of this system, we have now applied ex vivo manipulation of patient-derived AML cells to study the impact of fusion gene knockdown. Our data reveal a strict dependency of RUNX1::RUNX1T1-expressing AML cells on continuous fusion gene expression. Even temporary silencing of the gene exerts profound and lasting effects on leukemic self-renewal and differentiation, underscoring the therapeutic potential of targeting fusion genes in AML.

## Methods

## Patient characteristics
Bone marrow (BM) aspirates from patients with AML from the Princess Máxima Center Biobank Facility (project approval numbers PMC2019.059, PMC2020.184, and PMCLAB2021.207) were used in this study after signing of an informed consent form by the patients or their legal representatives for use of their material for research purposes. Relevant characteristics of patients can be found in , available on the Blood website. All primary AML samples examined were from t(8;21)-positive diagnostic pediatric patients, aged between 3 and 12 years. A patient-derived xenograft (PDX) was generated from an adult patient at relapse and carried a KIT D816V and a TET2 T1393fs mutation with variant allelic frequencies of 48% and 94%, respectively. 

## Cell culture
Mesenchymal stromal cells (MSCs) were obtained from healthy human BM and cultivated as previously described. Patient-derived t(8;21) leukemic cells were cocultured on human BM-derived MSCs feeders and cultivated with AML expansion medium containing 135 nM UM171 or 1.35 μM UM729, peptides, and cytokines. , 

## LNP treatment
LNPs were prepared as previously described. , , If not otherwise indicated, PDX and primary AML cells were seeded at a density of 106 cells per mL in AML expansion medium on MSCs as described above and treated with 4 μg/mL siRNA LDV (4-((N’-2-methylphenyl)ureido)-phenylacetyl-l-leucyl-l-α-aspartyl-l-valyl-conjugate)-functionalized LNPs (LDV-LNPs). The next day, the cells were diluted to a density of 5 × 105 cells per mL to allow for expansion and collected after indicated time points for analysis. This procedure was repeated every 72 hours for a total of 3 siRNA/vehicle doses, and 3 days after the last dose, all the groups were harvested and subjected to assay for transposase-accessible chromatin with sequencing (ATAC-seq) and RNA sequencing (RNA-seq). ## Results

## An LNP formulation for gene knockdown in patient-derived AML cells
Primary AML cells are notoriously hard to transfect. To perturb gene expression in both primary and PDX AML cells, we therefore developed targeted LNPs designed for the delivery of RUNX1::RUNX1T1 and mismatch control siRNAs ( A-B) to specific cells. In previous work, we demonstrated that siRNA-loaded LNPs effectively suppressed RUNX1::RUNX1T1 expression in t(8;21)-positive AML cell lines, both in vitro and in immunodeficient mice that underwent xenotransplantation. To enhance siRNA delivery and efficacy in primary AML cells, we functionalized LNPs with the high-affinity ligand LDV. This ligand targets very late antigen 4, an integrin α4-β1 heterodimer that is commonly expressed on hematopoietic cells but rarely on other lineages. , , 
 We evaluated LNP uptake and siRNA knockdown efficacy in primary AML cells cocultured with human BM MSCs under serum-free conditions ( A). LDV-LNPs exhibited a fivefold and ninefold improvement in uptake in primary AML cells at 6 and 24 hours, respectively, compared to nonfunctionalized LNPs ( C; A-B). Uptake was neither patient specific nor fusion gene dependent, as demonstrated by the improved uptake observed in additional normal karyotype AML BM aspirate sample ( B).
Using the RUNX1::RUNX1T1 fusion transcript as the target, we encapsulated an siRNA complementary to the fusion site of RUNX1::RUNX1T1 messenger RNA and monitored its knockdown at both transcript and protein levels in primary AML cells ( A-B). Treatment of 3 RUNX1::RUNX1T1-positive samples from patients with AML and 1 PDX sample with LDV-LNPs, administered on days 0, 3, and 6 of coculture, resulted in a sustained twofold to threefold reduction in RUNX1::RUNX1T1 transcripts and a greater than threefold reduction in the corresponding fusion protein levels ( D-E; C-D). Depletion of the RUNX1::RUNX1T1 protein persisted for >10 days after the final LNP treatment when examined in patient A ( E). In contrast, expression of wild-type RUNX1 protein, which is not expressed from the RUNX1::RUNX1T1 fusion allele, remained unaffected, underscoring the precision of the siRNA in targeting the fusion transcript ( E). To confirm the specificity of the knockdown in primary cells, we performed intracellular staining for RUNX1T1, revealing a marked loss of the nuclear fusion protein ( F-G).
In summary, these findings demonstrate the feasibility of effectively perturbing fusion gene expression ex vivo in primary AML cells.

## Silencing of RUNX1::RUNX1T1 alters chromatin accessibility
To investigate the impact of RUNX1::RUNX1T1 modulation on chromatin accessibility, we performed ATAC-seq on AML PDX cells, identifying 57 339 distal open chromatin sites. RUNX1::RUNX1T1 knockdown altered chromatin accessibility in 3953 (6.9%) and 2596 of these sites (4.5%), increasing and decreasing accessibility, respectively ( A; A-D). Sites with increased accessibility were enriched for C/EBP motifs, whereas those with decreased accessibility were enriched for AP-1 motifs ( B-C). Both AP-1 and C/EBP family transcription factors are pivotal in myeloid differentiation. , , Moreover, AP-1 motifs are associated with genes relevant for leukemic proliferation in t(8;21) AML. , , Interestingly, RUNX1::RUNX1T1 knockdown also yielded a gain of MYB binding sites, whereas motifs for erythroblast transformation specific (ETS) and RUNX were both gained and lost, indicating a reorganization of RUNX and ETS binding ( C). Comparing these chromatin changes to sites determined to be lineage specific, that is, sites that were not accessible in other hematopoietic cell types revealed that regions losing accessibility corresponded to hematopoietic stem cell/multipotent progenitor–specific patterns, whereas newly accessible regions reflected granulocyte-macrophage progenitor (GMP)-, monocyte-, and neutrophil-specific profiles ( D).
 The C/EBP transcription factor family shares a common DNA-binding motif and is critical for granulocytic differentiation. , , Repression of C/EBPα is associated with the differentiation block in t(8;21) AML, and CEBPE is a direct target of RUNX1::RUNX1T1-mediated repression ( E). , 
RUNX1::RUNX1T1 knockdown upregulated CEBPA, CEBPB, and CEBPE transcript levels and increased accessibility of C/EBPε-associated cis-regulatory regions ( ; E-G). Integration of chromatin immunoprecipitation sequencing and DNaseI-hypersensitive site sequencing data from the t(8;21) AML cell line Kasumi-1 confirmed that RUNX1::RUNX1T1 depletion alters the same chromatin site downstream of CEBPE in both Kasumi-1 and patient-derived cells ( E). Chromatin immunoprecipitation sequencing analysis further revealed that this region is occupied by RUNX1::RUNX1T1 in Kasumi-1 cells, indicating direct regulation of CEBPE expression in patient-derived AML cells ( E). Extending this analysis to additional differentiation-associated loci, including CTSG, RNASE2, and the immune checkpoint gene VSIR, revealed similar patterns of chromatin accessibility. These findings suggest that loss of RUNX1::RUNX1T1 binding, accompanied by increased C/EBPα binding at these accessible sites, leads to a comparable activation of gene expression ( E-G). , 
In summary, these data suggest that RUNX1::RUNX1T1 depletion (1) disrupts a stem cell-specific chromatin landscape and (2) alleviates the differentiation block in t(8;21) AML by activating a C/EBPα- and C/EBPε-driven transcriptional program, thereby promoting myeloid differentiation.

## RUNX1::RUNX1T1 depletion activates differentiation-linked transcriptional programs
To correlate gene expression changes with chromatin status in patient-derived AML cells, we conducted bulk RNA-seq on t(8;21) AML PDX cells after RUNX1::RUNX1T1 knockdown. Principal component analysis of bulk RNA-seq results robustly separated cells treated with LDV-LNP-RUNX1::RUNX1T1 siRNA (siRE) from those treated with LDV-LNP–mismatch control (siMM; A). Previous studies in AML cell lines have demonstrated that RUNX1::RUNX1T1 knockdown shifts transcriptional programs from self-renewal to differentiation, whereas its expression in hematopoietic stem and progenitor cells (HSPCs) promotes self-renewal. , , , , , Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) confirmed this pattern in RUNX1::RUNX1T1-depleted AML PDX cells ( A; B-C). Differential gene expression analysis identified 576 upregulated and 941 downregulated genes (adjusted P < .001; log2 fold change > 1; base mean ≥ 50; B-C; D; ). Among these were known transcriptional targets of RUNX1::RUNX1T1 linked to granulocytic and neutrophilic differentiation (eg, FUT7, ELANE, and CTSG) and VSIR ( B-C). , , 
 Intriguingly, RUNX1::RUNX1T1 silencing also upregulated eosinophil-associated genes (RNASE2, RNASE3, PRG2, and PRG3; B-C), suggesting differentiation potential toward neutrophilic and eosinophilic lineages. GSEA for BM cell type markers supported this observation, showing a shift from a transcriptome characteristic of immature cells to that of granulocytes, eosinophils, and mast cells ( D; E). Collectively, these results demonstrate that loss of RUNX1::RUNX1T1 activates transcriptional programs promoting granulocytic and eosinophilic differentiation.

## Loss of RUNX1::RUNX1T1 drives differentiation of primary AML cells
To investigate the effects of LNP treatment and RUNX1::RUNX1T1 depletion on primary cell subpopulations and their fate in patient-derived AML, we performed single-cell RNA-seq (scRNA-seq) on PDX and 2 RUNX1::RUNX1T1-positive primary BM aspirates treated with siRE- or siMM-carrying LNPs ( A; ). After quality control, filtering, and exclusion of MSCs through single nucleotide polymorphism–based demultiplexing and MSC marker gene analysis ( B-D), we obtained 8732, 7021, and 3108 cells from patients with AML A, B, and C (PDX), respectively. RUNX1T1 expression is substantially lower in normal HSPCs than the fusion gene and was therefore used as a surrogate marker for RUNX1::RUNX1T1 to identify leukemic cells. Patient A and B contained, next to leukemic cells, normal hematopoietic cells ( A-C), whereas no human nonleukemic cells were identified in the patient C PDX sample, as expected ( A).
 Analysis of nonleukemic clusters shown in the uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) from both LDV-LNP-siRE-treated and LDV-LNP-siMM-treated conditions revealed the presence of HSPCs, myeloid and erythroid progenitors, B, T, and natural killer cells ( D). LNP-mediated siRE delivery did not affect normal hematopoietic cell fates, with transcriptomic analysis showing no significant differences between the 2 conditions ( C,E-F; E-F). siRE-LNP treatment induced robust RUNX1::RUNX1T1 knockdown in leukemic cells, demonstrated by reduced RUNX1T1 reads ( G top row). Here, knockdown led to significant transcriptional changes in RUNX1::RUNX1T1 target genes, including the downregulation of the HSPC marker CD34 and upregulation of NFE2, a key driver of erythroid, megakaryocytic, and granulocytic differentiation ( G middle and bottom rows). The transcriptomic shift after RUNX1::RUNX1T1 silencing ( B) was consistent with shifts observed in bulk RNA-seq of AML PDX samples ( G).
Integration of altered gene expression across the 3 patient samples identified 216 significantly upregulated and 7403 downregulated genes (adjusted P < .001) after RUNX1::RUNX1T1 silencing, indicating a substantial reorganization of transcriptional networks ( ). This reorganization was reflected in the UMAP and principal component analysis, which revealed a pronounced shift in the composition of the integrated cell populations after knockdown ( H-I; H). Interestingly, pseudotime analysis suggested this shift exhibited different directions and that this bidirectional myeloid differentiation originates at the intersection of the 2 most immature cell clusters, which are likely to harbor the leukemia stem cells (LSCs; J). In conclusion, loss of RUNX1::RUNX1T1 facilitates differentiation of immature leukemic cells along distinct cell fates.

## RUNX1::RUNX1T1 depletion enhances bidirectional granulocytic and eosinophilic differentiation
To characterize differentiation trajectories, we analyzed gene expression in cells from control and RUNX1::RUNX1T1-depleted groups. Cluster analysis identified 9 distinct clusters ( ), with 2 central clusters (clusters 2 and 6) disappearing upon RUNX1::RUNX1T1 depletion ( A). The remaining 5 clusters were shared between control and knockdown cells. However, RUNX1::RUNX1T1 knockdown caused an additional shift along the UMAP, away from the center leading to a twofold to fourfold increase in clusters 0, 4, and 7 and twofold and eightfold decreases in clusters 3 and 8, respectively, on the one side, as well as a threefold increase in cluster 1 on the other side ( A; ). These results indicate that AML cells retain limited differentiation capacity with the fusion gene, and its silencing promotes progression along 2 distinct lineages.
 These shifts correlated with increased expression of granulocytic genes (eg, ELANE, CTSG, RETN, and SERPINA1) in clusters 0, 3, and 4 and eosinophilic genes (eg, PRG2, CLC, RNASE2) in clusters 1, 5, and 6 ( B-C). Expression of CD24, a marker of granulocytic maturation and a “don’t eat me” signal for macrophages, was also upregulated after knockdown ( C), , highlighting advanced granulocytic and eosinophilic differentiation. Overall, RUNX1::RUNX1T1 knockdown facilitates differentiation toward both granulocytic and eosinophilic fates and depletes immature leukemic populations.
To examine the differentiation patterns observed in RUNX1::RUNX1T1-silenced leukemic cell populations in more detail, we projected our scRNA-seq data sets from control and knockdown cells onto a human hematopoiesis reference map comprising 55 distinct cell states ( A). This analysis identified 11 leukemic states resembling normal hematopoietic cell states ( D; B). Leukemic cells with transcriptional profiles resembling multipotent progenitors, lymphomyeloid primed progenitors, megakaryocytic/erythrocytic progenitors, early GMPs, and lymphoid progenitors were in the joint region of the UMAP, which was lost upon RUNX1::RUNX1T1 silencing ( A,D; B-C and A). In contrast, more mature states, that is, late GMPs, promonocytes, monocytes, and eosinophilic/basophilic/mast precursors, were preserved or expanded. Projection confidence scores increased in both directions of differentiation upon knockdown ( E-F; B), indicating a shift from abnormal to more physiologic differentiation. Module scoring for lineage-specific genes corroborated these findings ( C).
To investigate these trajectories, we grouped RUNX1::RUNX1T1-depleted cells into 2 superclusters ( A; A-B). Supercluster I (clusters 0, 3, and 7) consisted of ∼75% late GMP–like cells ( A and B), whereas supercluster II (clusters 1 and 5) was ∼90% eosinophilic/basophilic/mast cell precursor–like ( B). Projection confidence was again higher after knockdown ( C), confirming the restoration of normal differentiation pathways. These 2 differentiation trajectories were further corroborated by the analysis of cell type module scores between the 2 superclusters and the detection of CD125+ CD193+ eosinophilic-like and CD14+ CD15+ late GMP–like subclusters by flow cytometry ( C-E).
 Next, we asked which genes that are differentially expressed between the superclusters were affected by RUNX1::RUNX1T1 silencing. Intersection of differentially expressed genes between the superclusters with those with and without RUNX1::RUNX1T1 knockdown revealed 271 and 234 genes that are (1) upregulated in knockdown cells and (2) are differentially expressed between superclusters I and II, respectively ( D). For the supercluster I/knockdown set, STRING analysis identified a cluster comprising genes related to neutrophilic granules, a ribosomal cluster, and a chaperone gene cluster ( E; F-G). In the supercluster II/knockdown group, only 1 cluster was found comprising genes associated with eosinophilic granules along with CEBPE ( F-G left).
The suggested involvement of C/EBPε in the regulation of key eosinophilic genes, together with the elevated expression of several other members of this transcription factor family upon RUNX1::RUNX1T1 silencing, prompted us to examine their differential expression across the 2 superclusters in more detail. Although CEBPA and CEBPB showed comparable expression patterns and levels between the 2 superclusters, CEBPD and CEBPE showed opposing patterns, with CEBPD being predominantly expressed in supercluster I and CEBPE accumulating in supercluster II upon RUNX1::RUNX1T1 silencing ( G). To further investigate the interplay between the different C/EBP members, we combined bulk ATAC-seq and scRNA-seq data to construct transcription factor networks for the 2 superclusters, with and without RUNX1::RUNX1T1 knockdown. This analysis suggests complementary roles of C/EBPδ and C/EPBε in the regulation of gene expression of late GMPs and eosinophilic precursors ( A-B).
We then focused on interleukin-5 receptor α (IL5RA; encoding CD125), a canonical eosinophil marker. Previously, we showed that t(8;21) AML expresses high IL5RA and contains IL5RA+ LSCs dependent on IL-5 and vascular endothelial growth factor signaling for cell cycle entry. Here, IL5RA transcript and CD125 surface protein were found in both immature and eosinophil-like leukemic cells ( E and C left). Moreover, we previously identified GATA2 binding at 2 IL5RA enhancer regions, implying transcriptional regulation by the GATA family. Consistently, GATA2 expression was higher in eosinophilic supercluster cells ( ; C right), whereas granulocytic-lineage cells showed reduced GATA2 and lacked IL5RA expression ( C). These findings suggest that IL5RA+ t(8;21) LSCs are epigenetically primed for eosinophilic differentiation.
To confirm this idea, we assessed chromatin accessibility at sites associated with the differentially expressed genes from each supercluster in the PDX ( H), which revealed that their chromatin was accessible at sites associated with the 2 supercluster trajectories, both with and without RUNX1::RUNX1T1 knockdown. We have previously shown that the PDX maintains the chromatin accessibility profile of the original t(8;21) patient cells. However, these same sites were not accessible in healthy CD34+ peripheral blood stem cells or non-t(8;21) primary AML cells. Importantly, projection of scRNA-seq data from samples from patients with AML onto the reference map demonstrated that untreated RUNX1::RUNX1T1-positive AMLs show a similar bidirectional differentiation predisposition pattern as control cells, providing additional proof for an imprinted bidirectional differentiation program ( D). , In aggregate, these data suggest that RUNX1::RUNX1T1 binding allows for limited differentiation toward 2 cell fates, which then resumes after RUNX1::RUNX1T1 silencing.

## RUNX1::RUNX1T1 knockdown depletes immature leukemic cell populations and impairs self-renewal
AML is sustained by LSCs that poorly respond to current treatment regimens and are a source of relapse. , The reduction in the stem cell-specific chromatin patterns in combination with the enhanced granulocytic and eosinophilic differentiation potential prompted us to examine whether the depletion of RUNX1::RUNX1T1 eliminates LSC-enriched immature cell populations and diminishes leukemic self-renewal capacity. Supporting this hypothesis, CD34-expressing leukemic cell populations exhibit a high hematopoietic stem cell score, and pseudotime analysis identified these cell populations as the origin of the bidirectional differentiation ( J and A-B). These populations are also lost after RUNX1::RUNX1T1 silencing and account for the overall reduction in hematopoietic stemness signatures, as indicated by GSEA ( A-C; A).
 Flow cytometric analysis of 8 CD markers confirmed a consistent loss of the most immature subpopulations across the PDX and all 3 patient samples examined ( D; B-D). Notably, patient samples exhibited heterogeneity in immature phenotypes with CD34+CD38– cells in patients B, C, and D and CD34+CD38+ cells in patient A ( B-D).
To specifically examine the loss of stemness in LSCs, we analyzed stemness-related transcriptional programs by assessing the LSC6 module score in relation to RUNX1::RUNX1T1 status ( E). 
RUNX1::RUNX1T1 silencing significantly decreased the LSC6 module score across all 3 patient samples, with the central clusters exhibiting the highest scores ( E-F). Furthermore, analysis of cell cycle–associated gene expression revealed that these cells preferentially reside in the G0/G1 phase, indicative of the quiescent state characteristic of LSCs ( E).
Treatment of patient cells with siRNA-LNPs did not affect proliferation in the short term ( G). We therefore examined the long-term impact of RUNX1::RUNX1T1 silencing on self-renewal of patient-derived AML cells in limiting dilution assays ( G). Proliferation of leukemic cells was examined 5 weeks after 3 sequential siRNA treatments. Extreme limiting dilution analysis uncovered a highly significant (P < .001; likelihood ratio test) fivefold reduced frequency of leukemia-restoring cells (LRCs) after RUNX1::RUNX1T1 knockdown, whereas mismatch control cells showed no significant difference in LRC frequencies compared to mock-treated cells ( G).
To further examine the role of RUNX1::RUNX1T1 in maintaining stemness of leukemic cells, we transplanted immunodeficient NSG mice with PDX cells derived from patient C that had been pretreated with LNPs harboring active (siRE) or mismatch siRNA (siMM; H). Determination of BM engraftment 2 months after transplantation revealed successful engraftment in 3 of 5 animals transplanted with siMM-LNPs, whereas none of the 5 mice transplanted with knockdown cells showed engraftment ( I-J; F). These data show that RUNX1::RUNX1T1 silencing effectively diminishes the leukemia-initiating cell population.
Collectively, these findings suggest that RUNX1::RUNX1T1 maintains leukemic self-renewal potential by preventing differentiation along 2 distinct trajectories: granulocyte- and eosinophil-like lineages. Moreover, we show that therapeutic targeting of RUNX1::RUNX1T1 leads to the loss of cellular immaturity and self-renewal capacity in primary t(8;21) AML along a continuum of cell types.

## Discussion
In this study, we used an innovative siRNA delivery approach in combination with an experimentally amenable ex vivo culture to investigate how direct manipulation of a leukemic fusion gene affects the balance of leukemic self-renewal and differentiation in patient-derived AML cells. Here, we show that leukemic self-renewal of primary AML is strictly dependent on continuous RUNX1::RUNX1T1 expression, in agreement with previous work in cell lines. , , , In addition, we demonstrate that the cellular composition of primary AML drastically changes upon RUNX1::RUNX1T1 silencing. Knockdown of RUNX1::RUNX1T1 by a fusion site–specific siRNA drives bidirectional granulocytic and eosinophilic differentiation at the expense of LSC-enriched immature cell populations.
The generally accepted hierarchical model of AML places LSCs at the top of the hierarchy as the sole cell type with self-renewal capacity and sole source of relapse. , This very strict organization has been recently challenged by the observation that more mature AML cell populations can be a source of relapse by regaining an LSC-like phenotype. , , , Eosinophilic cell states have also been described to regain self-renewal capability and induce relapse in a murine AML model with inducible PU.1 (also known as SPI1) knockdown. These findings raise the question whether RUNX1::RUNX1T1 silencing alone is sufficient to eliminate leukemia-restoring capacity or whether these fusion gene–positive descendants can lead to disease recurrence.
Our combined results reveal the existence of immature populations with granulocytic and eosinophilic potential capable of a limited degree of differentiation along these 2 lineages. Loss of RUNX1::RUNX1T1 silencing leads to the almost complete elimination of the immature populations and the emergence of cells of more advanced eosinophilic and granulocytic phenotype. Notably, differentiation progressed despite the presence of UM171/UM729 and StemRegenin 1, 2 strong inhibitors of hematopoietic differentiation that block CoREST or aryl hydrocarbon receptor, respectively. , , Nevertheless, these inhibitors are likely to have limited the terminal differentiation of knockdown cells toward mature cell differentiation states. However, even this limited differentiation is paralleled by a substantially reduced ability of RUNX1::RUNX1T1-depleted cells to reinitiate leukemic proliferation under limiting dilution over a period of 5 weeks and a complete loss of engraftment capacity in immunodeficient mice. Although we cannot formally exclude an LRC potential for these differentiated populations, these findings strongly suggest that transient depletion of RUNX1::RUNX1T1 impairs leukemic self-renewal in a long-lasting manner, depleting self-renewal ability along the entire continuum of leukemic cell types.
Based on recent transcriptome analyses, eosinophils, basophils, and mast cells form a distinct differentiation clade separate from erythroid/megakaryocytic, granulocyte/monocytic, and lymphoid differentiation. In contrast to the differentiation into eosinophils, the potential of t(8;21) AML to differentiate to mast cells and a link to mastocytosis is well described. , Moreover, RUNX1::RUNX1T1-positive mast cells have been recently identified as a source of persisting molecular measurable residual disease in disease-free patients, sometimes together with the expansion of fusion gene–positive basophils. , We have previously shown that t(8;21)-positive LSC-enriched cell populations express eosinophilic markers and use IL-5 signaling for cell cycle reentry while preserving self-renewal capacity. Our chromatin accessibility pattern analyses indicate that these LSCs are already poised for differentiation toward eosinophilic and granulocytic lineages. Notably, eosinophilic differentiation occurs without external addition of IL-5. Because our RNA-seq data did not detect significant IL5 transcript levels, this implies an IL-5-independent induction of eosinophilic differentiation. This idea is supported by the finding that C/EBPα and GATA2 cooperate in inducing eosinophilic differentiation. Furthermore, GATA2 binds to an enhancer of CEBPE, activating its expression, and may cooperate with this C/EBP member to drive expression of eosinophilic marker genes. Comparison of the expression of transcription factor genes between the granulocytic and eosinophilic superclusters revealed a similar induction of CEBPA expression in both superclusters upon RUNX1::RUNX1T1 silencing, whereas GATA2 and CEBPE expression were substantially higher in the eosinophilic supercluster. These data suggest that increasing levels of C/EBPα in conjunction with GATA2 and C/EBPε promote eosinophilic differentiation. Therefore, RUNX1::RUNX1T1 silencing does not open new avenues of differentiation but facilitates differentiation by opening transcriptional programs along epigenetically predetermined paths.
In aggregate, we show that RUNX1::RUNX1T1-expressing AML cells have the intrinsic potential to differentiate along granulocytic and eosinophilic lineages. The transient silencing of the fusion gene by siRNA-loaded LNPs leads to progressed bidirectional differentiation, the elimination of LSC populations, and the loss of leukemic self-renewal. These findings warrant further research in the application of fusion gene knockdown strategies as a leukemia-specific therapeutic approach.
