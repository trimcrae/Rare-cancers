## Abstract
Recent discoveries have highlighted that small monovalent molecules can leverage a variety of cellular mechanisms to induce degradation of a specific target protein. This perspective exemplifies and contextualizes these mechanisms within drug discovery, with a focus on identification of novel monovalent degraders via cell-based high-throughput screening (HTS) methodologies. Considerations for screening strategies, assay technologies and mechanistic deconvolution are discussed, with emphasis on identification of degrader compounds from diverse compound collections.
Subject terms: Drug discovery, High-throughput screening

## Introduction
Targeted protein degradation (TPD) is increasingly considered a viable approach to develop therapeutics for targets that are challenging to address with traditional small-molecule inhibitors. To date, the TPD drug discovery field has primarily focused on degraders that function by recruiting intracellular ubiquitin ligases (E3s) to therapeutically relevant targets. These small-molecule degraders generally fall into two main categories: PROTAC-type degraders, formally known as heterobifunctional or heterobivalent degraders, and monovalent degraders, which include molecular glues , . Key parameters to assess potency of degraders are DC50 and Dmax. The DC50 is equivalent to IC50 for inhibitors and describes the compound concentration required for half maximal degradation, whereas Dmax denotes the maximum degradation achievable for a given compound. Note that degraders may be partial, where the proportion of target degraded plateaus before complete depletion .
PROTACs are generated from known ligands for the protein of interest (POI) and the ubiquitin ligase, connected to each other via a linker, whilst monovalent degraders historically have been discovered serendipitously. More recently, rational efforts to identify molecular glue degraders have been made by generating focussed compound libraries, where the ligase or target ligands are decorated with appendages, enabling a search for compounds with molecular glue degrader activity within the resulting compound space – .
These and related strategies, such as hydrophobic tagging exemplified by the Selective Estrogen Receptor Downregulators (SERDs) , , are knowledge-based, involving derivatization of known ligands to confer degrader activity. In contrast, unbiased screening of highly diverse compound libraries takes a ligand-agnostic approach, aiming not at a specific E3 ligase but at harnessing the broad range of E3s and endogenous cellular pathways capable of inducing degradation of the POI. When conducted in live-cell systems, such screens have the potential to uncover compounds that trigger degradation through a variety of distinct mechanisms.
Although it is relatively straightforward to generate bifunctional degraders with in vitro activity, it is challenging to make these compounds orally bioavailable, or, for example, with properties that allow CNS penetration. Notably, both of these criteria are possible to achieve, but likely require significant medicinal chemistry efforts .
A key driver for investment in the discovery of monovalent degraders is that these compounds are generally more drug-like, with pharmacokinetic properties that align better with oral administration, as compared to bifunctional compounds. This advantage is set against the risk of conducting drug discovery campaigns to identify monovalent degraders, where significant time, effort and investment is likely required for hit identification and mechanistic deconvolution.
This perspective highlights cell-based screening strategies for discovering molecular glue and monovalent degraders, emphasizing HTS-compatible methods and the diverse cellular pathways compounds can exploit, followed by approaches to uncover the mechanisms underlying target degradation. Examples of compounds have been included to highlight the structural diversity of molecules demonstrated to induce POI degradation.

## Cellular mechanisms for degraders
Directly acting molecular glue degraders (MGDs) bind a target and contribute directly to surface interactions between both the target and the E3 ligase, or vice versa (Fig. ). The most well-known examples are the phthalimide-derivatives (IMiDs), including thalidomide, which elicit molecular glue effects with a range of distinct neo-substrates as they bind to cereblon – . Although IMiDs act as direct molecular glues, conformational changes in cereblon upon compound binding also contribute to neo-substrate recruitment .
 A more recent example is (S)-ACE-OH (Fig. ), a metabolite of acepromazine identified as a molecular glue degrader of nuclear pore proteins, via recruitment of the ubiquitin ligase TRIM21 to NUP98 . In this study, (S)-ACE-OH was identified via a phenotypic HTS, utilizing cell-viability as readout. To mechanistically understand the activity of (S)-ACE-OH, the authors used both CRISPR screening, which identified TRIM21 as a driver of anti-cancer activity, as well as quantitative proteomics, which identified NUP98 and other nuclear pore proteins as the (S)-ACE-OH driven neo-substrates of TRIM21. Interestingly, evidence suggests that recruitment of TRIM21 to NUP98 within the nuclear pore complex may induce ubiquitination and degradation of proteins in close proximity to NUP98, and in this context NUP98 may also be considered as an adaptor for (S)-ACE-OH driven degradation of these (see below).
A recent separate study identified another TRIM21 ligand, HGC652 (Fig. ), which also induces nuclear pore protein degradation . In contrast to the cell-based method that was utilized for (S)-ACE-OH, HGC652 was identified as a TRIM21 ligand in a DNA encoded library (DEL) screen, using purified TRIM21 protein; subsequent compound characterization in cellular assays revealed degrader activity of HGC652. Despite being structurally distinct, both (S)-ACE-OH and HGC652 (Fig. ) exhibit similar TRIM21 neo-substrate profiles. Moreover, recent independent studies report two additional TRIM21 MGDs for nuclear pore proteins (PRLX 93936 and BMS-214662; Fig. ), highlighting that TRIM21 can accommodate diverse chemotypes to induce degradation of this family of proteins , .
A tentative further example of a directly acting MGD is reported by King et al., who combined phenotypic antiproliferative screening of a covalent ligand library with chemoproteomics. This approach identified EN450, a fragment-like electrophile that covalently modifies a cysteine in the E2 ubiquitin-conjugating enzyme UBE2D. EN450 binding induces a direct interaction of UBE2D with NF-κB, resulting in NF-κB degradation. While the precise mechanism remains unclear, it is interesting to note that an E2, in contrast to an E3, is recruited to the POI .
These, and earlier discoveries, including the sulfonamide group of degraders (indisulam, E7070, CQS) which function through DCAF15 and the CCNK degrader (R)-CR8 that recruits DDB1 – , highlight how cell-based methods enable access to ubiquitin ligases previously unexplored for TPD.
In contrast to directly acting MGDs, adaptor MGDs induce degradation of a protein that is bound to the direct binding partner of the compound. Here, the compound does not directly engage the POI (Fig. ).
As mentioned above, (R)-CR8 (Fig. ) induces degradation of CCNK, however CCNK is not the direct binding partner for the compound. Instead, (R)-CR8 binds directly to CDK12, which in turn induces binding to the ubiquitin-ligase component DDB1. CDK12, however, is not efficiently degraded, but acts largely as an adaptor, positioning its bound cyclin, CCNK, for ubiquitination and degradation mediated by the recruited DDB1 . Following (R)-CR8, a number of different CDK12 ligands have been identified that function through the same mechanism; interestingly, smaller compounds, such as 919278 and dCemm3 (Fig. ) , , can potently induce a ternary complex between CDK12 and DDB1, highlighting that glues can be identified from compound collections that contain lead-like molecules. Notably, CT7439, a CCNK molecular glue degrader developed by Carrick Therapeutics (Boston, MA, USA), is currently in phase 1 clinical evaluation .
In contrast to (R)-CR8, CT7439, and 919278, all of which were identified as degraders serendipitously, dCemm3 was discovered through a rational phenotypic screening strategy, designed to identify molecular glue degraders. This approach leveraged differential cytotoxicity in cells with either impaired or intact cullin-RING ligase (CRL) activity, enabling the identification of compounds whose cytotoxic effects are dependent on functional CRL complexes .
CCNK appears to be the only well-established example of a POI degraded by an adaptor-based glue mechanism, however, the concept has further support from the field of bifunctional molecules, where ‘bridged PROTACs’ have been developed to degrade a POI associated with the direct PROTAC binding partner .
MGD compounds operating through this mechanism can circumvent the absence of a druggable pocket on the POI by binding instead to a pocket on an associated protein, leading to induced proximity with a ubiquitin ligase. One potential limitation of this mechanism is that the adaptor protein itself may also be subject to degradation, an outcome that could be either advantageous or undesirable, depending on the context. Given that most cellular proteins exist within either transient or stable complexes, adaptor-based MGDs enable degrader screening for POIs traditionally considered undruggable.
In addition to the mechanisms outlined above, degraders may also exert their effects through allosteric modulation. Allosterically acting degraders trigger a conformational change in their direct binding partner, facilitating recruitment of another protein, which ultimately induces degradation of the POI (Fig. ). These compounds generally do not directly contribute to the interface between the POI and the ubiquitin ligase.
A recent example is provided Roy et al., who have identified two closely related compounds, VVD-065 and VVD-130037 (Fig. ), which function as allosteric degraders of NRF2 . The authors used mass spectrometry-based chemoproteomics to screen a library of electrophilic small molecules for covalent binders of KEAP1, aiming to identify inhibitors. Unexpectedly, the compounds were observed to act as KEAP1 activators rather than inhibitors. Mechanistic studies revealed that VVD-065 induces conformational changes in KEAP1, the physiological E3 for NRF2, that enhance KEAP1 interaction with the CUL3 ligase scaffold, thereby increasing the ubiquitylation and degradation of NRF2. This example illustrates a compound-induced allosteric modulation of a ubiquitin ligase, where the compound exerts its effect without directly binding the degraded target.
Another example of an allosteric degrader comes from Kwanten et al., where the authors initially showed that selinexor, a covalent inhibitor of the nuclear export protein XPO1, induces degradation of XPO1 via the ASB8 ubiquitin ligase substrate receptor . In a follow-up study, the team demonstrated that covalent binding of selinexor triggers a conformational change in XPO1 that enables its recognition by ASB8. Notably, selinexor does not appear to directly mediate the interaction between XPO1 and ASB8, highlighting its role as an allosteric modulator.
Similarly, Hennes et al., recently conducted a cell-based HTS which identified compounds that degrade the IDO1 enzyme. The screening assay was based on measuring kynurenine, the product of the IDO1 enzyme; compounds that lowered kynurenine levels were taken forward for mechanistic studies, revealing IDO1 degradation as the underlying mode of action. Their findings suggest that this degradation is likely triggered by compound-induced conformational changes in IDO1, which enhance its recognition by KLHDC3, its physiological ubiquitin ligase. The initial hit compound, iDeg-1 (Fig. ), is a pseudo-natural product (PNP) compound with modest degrader potency for IDO1. PNPs are compounds that have been designed to combine fragments of natural products, resulting in compounds with relatively high structural complexity , and it is interesting to consider such compounds for degrader screens. Further work led to iDeg-6 (Fig. ), a potent degrader of IDO1 (DC50 = 6.5 nM, Dmax = 70% at 100 nM) .
Note that compounds that function via induced conformational changes may also directly contribute to the interface between the POI and the E3. This is exemplified by Hsia et al., where bifunctional compounds were designed to act intramolecularly, rearranging the domain architecture of BRD4 . This conformational rearrangement leads to induced interaction with either the DCAF16 or DCAF11 ubiquitin ligases via a glue effect. In principle, such intramolecular domain reorganizations are also available to monovalent compounds for conformationally driven molecular glue degrader effects.
Another interesting mechanistic basis for induced degradation is compound-induced multimerization of the POI, which can lead to increased turnover mediated by ubiquitin ligases (Fig. ).
A study by Słabicki et al. demonstrated that BCL6 degradation can be achieved through induced polymerization. In this process, the small molecule BI-3802 (Fig. ) promotes BCL6 polymerization, which in turn facilitates its degradation via recruitment of SIAH1, the physiological non-cullin E3 ligase for BCL6 . BI-3802 was discovered after optimizing a hit compound from a biophysical HTS, where fluorescence polarization was used to identify compounds interrupting the interaction of the BCL6 BTB domain with a peptide binding partner .
Mechanistically, BI-3802 functions as a molecular glue, inducing the formation of BCL6 filaments from BCL6 homodimers normally present in the cell. The polymerisation process enhances the interaction between BCL6 and SIAH1, thus leading to effective polyubiquitin transfer and ultimately to efficient BCL6 degradation through the proteasome system . Several molecules derived from BI-3802 have been reported, and further optimisation led to compound CCT369260, which showed BCL6 degradation in vivo after oral administration in a lymphoma xenograft mouse model .
This example illustrates that compounds can induce degradation by driving formation of POI multimers. This mechanism is further supported by a study that employed a bifunctional approach to induce BCL6 polymerisation with consequent proteasomal degradation. Here, the BCL6 HBiD D3 compound exhibits a DC50 of 19.42 pM in Farage cells after 24 h treatment, with sustained degradation up to 72 h, demonstrating that this mechanism of induced degradation can achieve high potency .
Proteins that form complexes are generally more stable within those complexes than the individual complex sub-units in isolation, and therefore disruption of protein-protein interactions (PPIs) has the potential to drive degradation of a target (Fig. ) .
Conceptually, an example is provided by Xu et al., where mutations disrupting the interaction between the proteins MEIOB and SPATA22 were observed to induce degradation of both proteins. Transitioning to a pharmacological approach, the authors generated a bimolecular fluorescence complementation (BiFC) assay to identify compounds inhibiting the MEIOB-SPATA22 interaction. This assay assesses PPIs through the reconstitution of a split yellow fluorescent protein (YFP), with each fragment fused to one of the target proteins. Using this system, a high-content imaging based (HCI) HTS was performed, identifying the natural product isocotoin (Fig. ). Though no mechanistic analysis was performed, compound treatment inhibited the MEIOB-SPATA22 interaction and induced degradation of both proteins, mirroring the genetic evidence and suggesting destabilization of the un-complexed proteins .
Another example of PPIs governing protein stability is provided by the interaction of the HSP90 chaperone with its cognate binding partners. HSP90 mediates its chaperone function by assisting in protein folding and stabilization . Inhibition of HSP90 leads to destabilization of its clients , and conversely small molecules that bind to client proteins can also disrupt interaction with HSP90 and induce degradation. For instance, several kinase inhibitors have been shown to destabilize their target kinases by preventing their association with HSP90. Mechanistically, this has been demonstrated to be due to blocking binding to the HSP90 co-chaperone CDC37 , , thereby hindering access to HSP90 and triggering destabilization and degradation.
An interesting concept in the degrader field is that compounds may induce degradation indirectly via interference with signalling pathways. Phosphorylation has long been known to regulate degradation via the generation of phosphodegrons, which are peptide motifs that when phosphorylated are recognized by ubiquitin ligases such as β-TrCP, leading to ubiquitylation and degradation of the phosphorylated form of the protein .
Recently, Scholes et al., demonstrated induced degradation of the LYN kinase, observed following treatment of cells with the SI-3 compound . SI-3 (Fig. ) rapidly and potently leads to degradation of LYN (DC50 = 9 nM, T1/2 = 10 min), however, while SI-3 binds to and inhibits LYN, SI-3 was developed to target CSK (compound 13 in first report ), and the data suggests that it is the inhibition of this kinase which dictates LYN degradation.
The mechanism appears to be complex, and may involve activation of LYN via inhibition of CSK , . Active LYN may auto-phosphorylate, or may be phosphorylated by other kinases, ultimately leading to generation of a phospho-degron in LYN, which is suggested to be recognized by the CBL ubiquitin ligase, leading to rapid and potent LYN degradation. Whereas a compound with the complexity of SI-3 is unlikely to be present in HTS collections, the example illustrates the concept of induced degradation via small-molecule interference with kinase signalling pathways.
Besides the modes-of-action outlined above, subcellular localization has been reported to influence protein turnover rates (Fig. ), possibly through mechanisms that promote assembly of proteins into compartment-specific multimeric complexes , . In an example from Scholes et al., the authors characterized induced degradation of the BLK kinase by TAK285 (Fig. ), an inhibitor of HER1/2 .
Overall, the data indicates that TAK285 degrades BLK by changing its localization from the plasma membrane to the cytosol, where ubiquitin-proteasome mediated BLK turnover is elevated. The degradation is rapid and modestly potent, with a DC50 of 1 µM at two hours of treatment. The re-localization and increased turnover of BLK is achieved via a complex and not fully delineated mechanism that surprisingly involves γ-secretase activity, as well as loss of BLK myristylation. Interestingly, direct binding of TAK285 to BLK appears to be dispensable for the induced degradation, highlighting how compounds may access indirect mechanisms within the cell to influence turnover of a given target.
Finally, compounds may induce degradation of specific targets via destabilization of the native POI structure (Fig. ), leading to a partially unfolded state that can be recognized and directed to degradation by the cellular protein quality control machinery .
This approach was explored by Alboreggia et al., where covalent ligands destabilizing the PIN1 protein were generated, e.g., compound 158G12 (Fig. ). A correlation between biophysical thermal destabilization and PIN1 degradation in cellular systems was observed and shown to be dependent on the proteasomal pathway, where compounds displayed modest degrader potency (DC50 ≈ 500 nM; DMax = 100%) .
A similar idea has been explored by Sibylla Biotech (Verona, VR, Italy), where a computational approach is leveraged to identify and target transient pockets in partially folded proteins as they are being synthesized. Binding in a transient pocket prevents completion of protein folding and stabilizes an intermediately folded state of the target, leading to recognition of this as a misfolded polypeptide by the protein quality control machinery , with subsequent degradation , . This concept was demonstrated on PrP, the prion protein, where the small molecule SM875 (Fig. ) was shown to induce PrP degradation via the lysosomal pathway.

## Screening strategies
As described in the previous sections, a cell-based approach for degrader hit identification has the advantage of accessing a range of mechanisms that can induce degradation of the target, including recruitment of any one of the expressed ubiquitin ligases.
Unbiased approaches run at scale for degrader hit identification are currently not widely available. HTS is a well-established universal method in the drug discovery industry used to rapidly identify novel chemical starting points. HTS involves the seamless integration of medicinal chemistry, biology, informatics and advanced automation technology. HTS assays must be robust and scalable to run hundreds-of-thousands of compounds and must be amenable to automated processes. Consequently, it is necessary to miniaturize assays to at least 384-well format, and assay technologies are required to be compatible with this scale.
To develop an HTS compatible assay for finding small molecule degraders, it is a distinct advantage to have access to a reference degrader, such as a bifunctional, PROTAC-type, compound. In cases where this is not available, a bifunctional degrader may be generated from known ligands for the POI and a ubiquitin ligase, connected to each other via a linker. Although this approach will extend timelines and cost for an HTS, generation of a reference compound will allow for robust assessment of statistical parameters (Z’, signal-to-background) that are key for a successful HTS campaign (Fig. ). If it is not possible to obtain a reference degrader, assay development and validation may be undertaken using genetic methods, e.g., by using an isogenic cell-line that has the POI knocked-out or by knocking down the POI using siRNA. While genetic approaches can be used as controls in HTS, they are not ideal for full-scale screening campaigns, as they do not account for variability introduced by compound handling and liquid dispensing processes. Alternatively, an HTS may be run in the absence of a positive control, where statistical criteria for assigning hits are built from the distribution of signal within the population of test compounds.
 High throughput cell-based approaches can be split into two categories: endogenous and engineered systems. The endogenous approach relies on access to a cell system that natively expresses the protein of interest at levels that are detectable with a screening compatible readout. Protein degradation can be monitored using High Content Imaging (HCI), Time-Resolved Förster Resonance Energy Transfer (TR-FRET) technologies, and Amplified Luminescent Proximity Homogeneous Assay (AlphaLISA™) or Amplified Luminescent Proximity Homogeneous Assay Screen (AlphaScreen®), as detailed below. These readouts are all compatible with HTS criteria, however, are restricted by the availability of good primary antibodies for detection of the endogenous target.
Importantly, the steady-state level of a POI is determined by the balance between its synthesis and degradation rates, both of which can be regulated depending on cellular context, such as signalling states or cell-type. In contrast, exogenous protein expression, often utilized in engineered approaches, is achieved via the artificial introduction of a gene into the cell, typically bypassing the normal regulatory mechanisms. This usually results in an abnormally high rate of protein production and elevated steady-state levels of the POI. Such high levels of the POI may overload the proteasome , , reducing the observable maximum degradation, Dmax, leading to potential false negatives. Additionally, the induced rate of degradation for a given compound may be low relative to rate of exogenous protein expression from a strong promoter, leading to no significant decrease in POI levels; in contrast native expression levels may allow for a clear detection degrader activity for the same compound .
Exogenous overexpression may also impact the POI in other ways, including mis-localization or aggregation within the cell, changes in post-translational modifications, changes in protein complex formation due to altered stoichiometry and changes in cellular signalling pathways, e.g., by overexpression of enzymes such as kinases . Speculatively, such effects may alter the POI exposure to relevant E3 ligases, change the natural half-life of the POI and impact overall cellular protein homeostasis by activation of cellular stress response pathways.
As such, overexpression may create artificial conditions, either favourable or unfavourable, for induced degradation, which may not reflect the effect of a compound on the native POI, highlighting the need to validate degrader compounds using the endogenously expressed protein.
Engineered systems are based on fusing a tag to the target protein to enable detection based on measuring luminescence or fluorescence, generally offering high sensitivity and a good signal to background ratio. Tags are most easily introduced using an exogenous expression system; however, endogenous tagging can be achieved via CRISPR-based gene editing. Whilst this preserves the endogenous regulation of protein expression, it is important to note that the tag may influence induced degradation of the target either positively or negatively, and in a screening setting this is reflected in the number of false positives and negatives, respectively.
In this context, access to a reference compound aids assay development by enabling validation of engineered systems against the endogenous POI through DC50 and Dmax measurements. However, in unbiased cellular screens, it is important to note that validation using a reference compound that recruits a given E3 ligase does not reveal how POI engineering or overexpression affects other cellular pathways or effectors targeted by screening compounds.
In addition, compound treatment for extended durations can introduce confounding effects such as nonspecific compound toxicity, which may skew degradation profiles and lead to false positives. Incorporating cell viability counter screens in the screening cascade (Fig. ) helps identify compounds that are generally cytotoxic and exclude them from further analysis .
Ultimately, the choice of screening approach and technology is guided by the availability of suitable reagents for the POI, as well as by practical considerations including cost, timelines, and access to necessary instrumentation. In the following sections, we review technologies applicable to HTS campaigns for degrader discovery, covering both endogenous and engineered approaches. As a general principle, when feasible, screening against the endogenous target is preferred, as it offers a more physiologically relevant context. However, tag-based engineered systems serve as a valuable alternative when endogenous screening is impractical.
TR-FRET has been adopted in different commercial versions, including Homogeneous Time-Resolved Fluorescence (HTRF™) , Lanthanide chelate excite (LANCE™), and LanthaScreen. In this perspective, we use HTRF™ as an example of TR-FRET technology.
HTRF™ and AlphaScreen®/AlphaLISA™ are 384/1536-well compatible assays suited for HTS. Both technologies are homogenous and provide fast and robust methods for quantification of endogenous protein levels in cell lysates, scalable for large libraries.
HTRF™ and Alpha-technologies are based on utilizing two separate antibodies that recognize distinct epitopes in the POI. In HTRF™ these antibodies are conjugated to acceptor and donor fluorophores, so that upon binding to the POI a FRET signal proportional to the POI abundance is generated. For AlphaLISA™ and AlphaScreen®, the antibodies are coupled to donor and acceptor beads, which upon target binding are brought into close proximity. In contrast to HTRF™, excitation of the donor beads generates an oxygen singlet, which diffuse to produce a chemical reaction in a proximal acceptor bead, leading to light emission proportional to the amount of target protein . Each technology has distinct strengths and limitations when used for monitoring protein degradation in HTS.
An advantage of HTRF™ is the time-resolved fluorescence, which minimizes background autofluorescence by using a delayed read after excitation. Conversely, AlphaScreen®/AlphaLISA™ may be more sensitive than HTRF , which provides an advantage for detection of low abundance proteins. Ultimately, the choice of screening technology is dictated by the POI and the assay performance with respect to required criteria for HTS.
As for all screening technologies, both HTRF™ and Alpha-technologies are subject to interference from screening compounds, due to e.g., emission or absorption of light in the measured wavelengths, light scattering, singlet oxygen quenching or other types of interference , . Additionally, the primary antibodies may lose recognition of targets due to masking of epitopes by e.g., posttranslational modification, conformational changes or complex formation with other protein partners, potentially leading to false positives . As such, the use appropriate counter screening and orthogonal validation methods are key to identify true hits.
Ultimately, both HTRF™ and AlphaScreen®/AlphaLISA™ are powerful approaches, fully aligned with HTS workflows, allowing the rapid screening of hundreds of thousands of compounds for degrader activity against an endogenous target in any relevant cell model.
An alternative to HTRF™ and AlphaLISA® is the HiBiT system from Promega® (Madison, WI, USA). The HiBiT system is a sensitive, quantitative, and HTS-compatible luminescence-based assay suitable for TPD. The POI is fused to the 11 amino acid HiBiT tag, which pairs with a co-expressed or exogenously added LgBiT peptide (17.6 kDa), reconstituting NanoLuc luciferase, producing a luminescent signal proportional to the abundance of the POI . This assay is homogenous, quantitative and highly sensitive, making it ideal for detecting low-expressed proteins. Given the small size of the HiBiT-tag, the method can be considered to be relatively non-invasive, and can monitor POI abundance without requiring selective antibodies . The long half-life of the luminescent signal supports large-scale screening, aligning fully with HTS workflows.
As mentioned above, tags can be introduced at endogenous gene loci via CRISPR-based genome editing, ensuring that native expression levels are preserved. In studies comparing endogenously integrated HiBiT-tagged proteins to transiently overexpressed constructs, the latter exhibited slower degradation rates, delayed recovery, and shifted DC50 values, indicating advantages with maintaining endogenous expression levels .
In an HTS setting, endpoint reads are employed for practical reasons, however HiBiT technology has the benefit of allowing live-cell measures over time if LgBiT is co-expressed. At the hit characterization and validation stages, degradation kinetics may be monitored in real time in live cells for a smaller set of compounds, generating metrics such as rate of degradation, time to reach Dmax, time at Dmax, and recovery rate , , .
The HiBiT system is a robust and powerful tool for degrader HTS, however, as is the case for most assay systems, it is susceptible to artifacts that can lead to false positives . Compounds may disrupt the HiBiT-LgBiT interaction or inhibit the activity of the reconstituted NanoLuciferase; such compounds can be removed using a HiBiT control protein assay . Moreover, lysine residues in the HiBiT and LgBiT tags may serve as unintended ubiquitination sites and substituting these lysines for arginines, as shown in recent studies , can improve assay fidelity. Overall, HiBiT provides an effective solution for degrader HTS campaigns, with excellent sensitivity and high compliance with HTS criteria.
Immunofluorescence (IF) microscopy is becoming an increasingly attractive option for monitoring protein degradation after compound treatment, as it examines protein expression in intact cells and only requires suitable selective primary antibodies. Advances in automated microscopy have enabled a transition from traditional low throughput microscopy to higher throughput automated microscopy platforms that are compatible with drug discovery and compound screening workflows. Such high content screening (HCS), or HCI systems combine high powered microscopes with plate-based detection formats, allowing high throughput automated and quantitative fluorescent microscopy. This combination of automated microscopy and complex image analysis algorithms is designed to maximise data output from a single well and is compatible with single- or multi-cell imaging in miniaturised 384-well assay formats. Whilst this enables imaging-based HTS, it should be noted that throughput is generally lower than e.g., HTRF™ or HiBiT-based methods because the technique is not homogenous and can require large data handling capabilities.
IF is compatible with the detection of endogenous protein expression, provided that the physiological expression level of the POI is sufficiently high, and does not require engineered cell lines for over-expression of tagged proteins , . In addition to immortalised cell lines, IF has been successfully used to monitor protein expression in human primary cells , or tissues , that may be more predictive of clinical behaviour of compounds. While versatile, from an HTS perspective, imaging is limited to adherent cell models and not suitable for suspension cells.
One of the main advantages with imaging is that HCI systems can measure multiple wavelengths simultaneously. This makes the technique amenable to the multiplexing of different fluorescent probes to assay several different outputs from the same cell population. This usually includes a nuclear stain to monitor cell number and may also include cellular stains that give a visual representation of cells and can indicate unanticipated changes in cell health due to compound treatment. Imaging therefore allows simultaneous monitoring of cell health and target degradation in a single well after compound treatment to discard gross cell death as the mechanism behind loss of target protein. Assays can also be designed to monitor selectivity of the degrader against closely related family members, or the activation of downstream targets through post-translational modification (e.g., phosphorylation). An additional advantage with imaging, as opposed to other methodologies, is that it is in principle possible to monitor changes in the subcellular localization of the POI.
HCI enables single cell and population assays in a variety of cells and is highly compatible with compound screening for protein degraders, in either single concentration mode for hit finding or in concentration-response mode for more advanced drug discovery programmes.
Overall, HCI is a valuable approach for assessing endogenous POIs, and serves as an excellent alternative to HTRF™ and AlphaLISA™ technologies, particularly in cases where these assays are unavailable or unsuitable for the target under investigation.

## Library strategies
Irrespective of the screening approach taken, the nature and the quality of the screening library deployed will, naturally, influence the resultant hit output. In the pursuit of degrader motifs, most prospective cell-based screening to date has been in the pursuit of molecular glues and, in general, has been primarily based around the IMiD motif, which binds cereblon and recruits cognate protein partners. Whilst successful, this approach has led to several emergent issues:
The IMiD scaffold itself, as exemplified in glues such as pomalidomide and lenalidomide, is intrinsically unstable in vivo and compounds derived from this core suffer from PK limitations. More recent derivatives, with alterations to the core chemotype, have helped to overcome these issues , .
The prevalence of the IMiD core in the molecular glue repertoire has created an increasingly crowded space for intellectual property protection and freedom to operate for novel molecules.
The overt reliance on the IMiD core promotes the identification of degrader interactions mediated by cereblon as the cognate E3 ligase.
Given these challenges, unbiased live-cell screening for degrader motifs offers a number of advantages:
Increased chemical diversity opens up novel intellectual property space for exploitation and protection.
Hits can be assessed for their DMPK potential, and prioritisation of the hits for optimisation can help better position for long-term success.
In cell-based assays, start points are self-selected toward cell-penetrant molecules, easing the optimisation pathway.
Perhaps more importantly, however, is the ability to assess these chemotypes for their ability to create novel neoepitope binding interactions between the POI, and the entire repertoire of cellular E3 ligases. Combining unbiased cellular screening with a diverse, lead-like (MW < 450) screening library allows opportunities to identify novel glue motifs that can recruit any of the 600 or so cellular E3 ligases to the protein of interest and lead to its degradation. In addition to this, any of the other described cellular mechanisms may also be exploited by lead-like compounds to induce POI degradation. Once identified, these hits can then be further assessed, to confirm their activity and select the most optimal start points for further optimisation.
Optimization of degrader chemotypes is often best achieved outside the cellular context through the use of simplified biochemical assays. These assays enable rapid generation of structure–activity relationship (SAR) data and facilitate iterative progression through the design–make–test-analyse cycle (Fig. ). This, however, requires knowledge of the mechanism of action, to understand if the compound is acting via binding to a specific E3, adaptor protein or functioning via any of the other mechanisms discussed. Therefore, a robust strategy for the mechanistic deconvolution of the induced degradation is essential to guide rational optimization.

## Mechanistic deconvolution
As discussed above, utilising cell-based screening methods enables the identification of compounds that may act through a variety of cellular mechanisms, including degradation driven by any expressed ubiquitin-ligase. Therefore, mechanistic deconvolution of hits is required to identify the direct binding partner of the compound, which will aid in fully delineating the mechanistic basis for the degradation. This in turn enables optimization, structural, biochemical and biophysical studies, and an understanding of any potential liabilities associated with the mechanism.
The most straightforward method of mechanistic analysis is the use of existing small-molecule inhibitors of the main protein degradation pathways in the cell: the ubiquitin-proteasome pathway and the lysosomal pathway.
Well-characterized inhibitors, such as bortezomib or MG132, which inhibit the proteasome , or MLN7243 , which inhibits the ubiquitin-activating enzyme, UBE1, can establish dependence on the ubiquitin proteasome pathway. The NAE1 inhibitor MLN4924 , can be further used to investigate if such degradation is mediated by a cullin-RING ubiquitin-ligase. Involvement of the lysosomal pathway can be determined by use of e.g., bafilomycin A1 or chloroquine, which will attenuate degradation via this mechanism .
Compounds that non-selectively inhibit protein synthesis will decrease levels of short-lived proteins, such that a compound may appear to degrade a given target, but in reality, is interfering with global protein production within the cell. This mechanism would be largely unwanted, and to probe this, an assessment of the hit compound can be made with respect to its effect on known short-lived proteins unrelated to the target in question, such as MYC, MDM2 or MCL1. Protein synthesis inhibitors, such as cycloheximide, may also be utilized, where a compound that specifically induces degradation of the target would be expected to exhibit a degrader effect above any effect observed with cycloheximide alone , unless degradation is mediated by e.g., a rapidly turned over ubiquitin-ligase.
Although the use of inhibitors can quickly and cheaply provide an indication of the mechanistic basis for degradation, this approach will not provide sufficient detail to enable a full understanding of how compounds function, which will require target identification utilizing approaches described below (Fig. ).
CRISPR screening is a genetic method that can be used to individually knock-out expression of genes in cultured cells, allowing for identification of proteins and pathways involved in the small-molecule induced degradation . This approach may be used as a follow-up to pathway inhibitors, e.g., if the mechanism of induced degradation can be demonstrated to be driven by the ubiquitin-proteasome system, a focussed CRISPR screen on ubiquitin-ligase components may deliver the specific ligase involved.
An example of this method is the identification of DCAF16 as the ligase responsible for the induced degradation of BRD4 by the compound GNE11. In this study, 1158 genes associated with the ubiquitin-proteasome system were individually screened (arrayed screen). Genes which upon depletion rescued the degradation of an engineered BRD4-HiBiT protein were selected as candidates, leading to the discovery of DCAF16 . Larger or genome wide CRISPR screens may be required to identify mechanisms of degradation that include proteins outside of the ubiquitin-proteasome system, and this may involve pooled screening and more complex protocols and resources.
An example of a pooled CRISPR screen for mechanistic deconvolution of a molecular glue degrader is, again, a study of GNE11. In this example, the authors screened 2852 genes by simultaneously transducing the CRISPR library into a cell population expressing an engineered fluorescent fusion-protein of the target (BRD4-eGFP). By using fluorescence activated cell sorting (FACS) to collect cells where degradation had been abrogated, followed by gRNA sequencing to identify the gene knocked out, candidate proteins mediating degradation were identified, highlighting DCAF16 .
In contrast to the genetic approach used in CRISPR screens, Cellular Thermal Shift Assay (CETSA®) is a technique to directly assess target engagement by a compound. The principle of CETSA® is based on a difference in protein melting temperature upon binding to a small-molecule ligand. Most often, ligand binding to a protein leads to a stabilizing effect, where a higher temperature is required for thermal unfolding of the liganded protein compared to the unliganded state. By heating cells or cell-lysates in the presence and absence of a given compound, followed by removal of proteins rendered insoluble by melting, targets that bind to the compound can be identified by quantitative proteomics . This method is referred to as CETSA-MS, and has been demonstrated in a proof-of-concept study for IMiD compounds, where clear engagement to CRBN could be observed . Note that proteins that are degraded, such as the POI, will decrease in abundance upon compound treatment of live cells, independently of effects on thermal stability due to ligand binding, and this must be accounted for when designing experiments and interpreting results. To mitigate potential confounding effects of compound-induced degradation on target identification, CETSA-MS can be performed in cell lysates, where active degradation pathways are absent.
Similar to CETSA-MS, Limited Proteolysis Mass Spectrometry (LiP-MS) is a proteome-wide technique used to directly identify targets that engage with a small molecule , . LiP-MS relies on brief proteolytic digestion of cell-lysates under native conditions. Binding of a small molecule to a protein can induce conformational changes that either expose or shield proteolytic cleavage sites, leading to changes in composition of peptides that originate from digestion of that protein. Detection of such changes in a concentration-dependent manner using mass spectrometry allows identification of candidate targets for the small molecule compound. Since LiP-MS is peptide centred, specific peptides that are influenced by small molecule binding may be mapped onto an existing structure, or an AlphaFold prediction, of the target, thus providing information on the tentative compound binding site.
LiP-MS was recently used to investigate the target profile of the non-degrading molecular glue NST-628, which identified MEK1 and MEK2, previously established targets of this molecule , . Although NST-628 does not function as a degrader, this study highlights the utility of the approach in interrogating the proteome for targets that engage with molecular glue compounds.
Other mass spectrometry-based methods, including an approach termed ‘drug affinity responsive target stability’ (DARTS), which is similar to LiP-MS, have also been reported, however detailed descriptions of these are beyond the scope of this short perspective and we direct the reader to excellent reviews by others , . As for CETSA-MS, both LiP-MS and DARTS experiments can be undertaken by treating cell-lysates with compounds, which avoids potential issues with induced degradation of proteins that engage with the compound.
Affinity purification is a direct method for identification of small molecule targets. In this approach, the small molecule is either linked to an affinity tag such as biotin or immobilized on a resin, like agarose beads. The derivatized compound is then used to isolate binding partners from cells or cell-lysates, with identification of proteins using mass spectrometry.
This method was utilized to identify cereblon as the target of thalidomide, where thalidomide was modified with a carboxylic acid group to facilitate attachment to ferriteglycidyl methacrylate beads . Mass spectrometry then identified both cereblon and DDB1 as proteins interacting with thalidomide.
There are various other examples of compounds for which the cellular targets have been identified utilizing different types of affinity purification, highlighting the utility of this approach . A caveat is that modification of the original hit-compounds is needed, which requires chemistry resources and may abrogate binding to the target, a risk that is difficult to assess in the absence of any structural data to guide the selection of attachment point for e.g., biotin or a functional group for immobilization to solid support.

## Outlook
As highlighted in this perspective, a small molecule compound can degrade a therapeutically relevant protein through various cellular mechanisms. Because of this, unbiased live-cell screens provide an effective way to identify monovalent degraders that act through effectors or pathways that would be challenging to target using a priori design.
Although powerful, a significant challenge with cell-based screening is the mechanistic deconvolution. Depending on the specific pathway involved, this may be relatively straightforward, or may require significant effort, with an inherent risk that the identified mechanism will be incompatible with development of the compound for therapeutic purposes.
Alongside cell-based methods, biophysical techniques are available to screen for compounds that induce proximity between two selected proteins, e.g., a POI and a chosen ubiquitin ligase. Whereas such methods are powerful, they are limited by the need for purified protein, which may be difficult or impossible to obtain for certain target classes, such as intrinsically disordered proteins. The reduced complexity of cell-free systems is a clear advantage, significantly simplifying mechanistic validation and allowing the selection of a particular effector protein, e.g., a ubiquitin ligase that may be relevant for the therapeutic area. Whilst these are strong advantages, bona fide induced proximity observed in a cell-free system is required to translate into an effect, i.e., degradation, within a live cell. Since spatial orientation and ancillary factors play important roles in productive ternary complex formation for ubiquitin ligase-based degraders, induced proximity in a cell-free system alone does not necessarily translate into a compound that has cellular activity.
With development of higher throughput proteomics technology , cell-based screens for compounds that modulate protein abundance may be undertaken against the detectable proteome. This approach was investigated by Steger et al., where the authors mapped the neo-substrates of cereblon in response to treatment with different IMiD compounds . This approach would in principle also be compatible with an unbiased screen using a diverse compound library, where the readout is the effects on protein abundance for either a particular target or multiple targets with therapeutic value. A drawback with current mass spectrometry-based technologies is that, in the contexts of screening large compound libraries, the throughput is not yet feasible. In this context, the concept of screening pooled compound mixtures against the proteome is particularly compelling, as it could substantially increase throughput. Speculatively, by evaluating the effects of compound mixtures on the expressed profile of therapeutically relevant proteins, it becomes possible to dramatically expand the number of interrogated combinations involving compounds, targets, and effector proteins.
As the induced proximity field matures, small molecules identified through unbiased cellular screens are expected to expand beyond degraders to include other effector outcomes, such as protein stabilizers, a modality currently being explored via bifunctional strategies , . Over the next few years, it will be exciting to observe how these unbiased screening approaches tap into the broad and largely unexplored landscape of cellular mechanisms to uncover novel induced-proximity compounds and ultimately deliver new therapeutic solutions to patients.
