## Abstract
Heterobifunctional small degrader molecules that hijack endogenous E3 ubiquitin ligases have attracted attention for the rapid and irreversible knock-down of target proteins via ubiquitination. However, the formation of appropriately oriented E3 ligase–target complexes is required for efficient ubiquitination of the target, which complicates the molecular optimization and leads to acquired drug resistance caused by the loss of E3 ligase activity and mutations at the E3–target interfaces. Here, we report on indirect ubiquitination as a chemical strategy for E3-indepedent ubiquitin-tethering to the target substrate. Comprising a ligand molecule and a ubiquitin moiety, the designed chimeric molecule enables the ubiquitination of the target proteins via non-covalent interactions, which lead to the proteasomal degradation of recombinant Bcl-2 and NF-κB p50, and intracellular endogenous Bcl-2. Indirect ubiquitination offers a design platform for non-covalent tethering of a ubiquitin-based proteolytic modifier to be added in the molecular toolbox for the targeted protein degradation.

 
Subject terms: Peptides, Ubiquitins, Ubiquitylated proteins

Heterobifunctional small degrader molecules that tether endogenous E3 ubiquitin ligases are promising substrates for targeted protein degradation, however, the precise formation of the E3 ligase–target complex remains challenging and limits their application. Here, the authors introduce indirect ubiquitination of the target proteins via non-covalent interactions that bypass E3 ligases, enabling efficient protein degradation.

## Introduction
Inducers of targeted protein degradation that hijack proteasome- or lysosome-dependent pathways constitute a fascinating class of chemical compounds that enable the instant and selective knockdown of native proteins . Of the existing categories, proteolysis-targeting chimeras (PROTACs) and specific and non-genetic IAP-dependent protein erasers (SNIPERs) have been developed to target intracellular proteins , . Spatial proximation of the target and the E3 ubiquitin ligase mediated by the heterobivalent chimeric molecule facilitates ubiquitination of the target, which leads to its degradation through the ubiquitin–proteasome system. In phase I clinical trials reported in 2020, a PROTAC targeting the androgen receptor (AR) ARV-110 was reported to induce the degradation of the AR in tumors of patients with prostate cancer. This trial provided the first evidence of a PROTAC working as expected with clinically acceptable safety in humans . The early signs of the drug-like properties of PROTACs , provide evidence of the robustness of the targeted protein degradation as a platform for drug discovery. In particular, the potential knockdown of all associated functions of the target proteins by irreversible degradation allows the targeting of proteins that were once regarded as undruggable targets – , including multifunctional proteins with scaffolding capabilities – , pseudo-enzymes , and nucleic acid-binding proteins – .
However, PROTACs and related technologies face potential problems derived from their dependency on endogenous E3 ubiquitin ligases for substrate ubiquitination. The development of PROTACs requires intensive molecular optimization – and/or sophisticated structure-based design – to induce the proper interaction between an E3 ligase and a target protein for efficient ubiquitination of a non-native substrate. There is increasing evidence that the ubiquitination efficiency of target proteins is not determined merely by the affinity between the target and an E3 ligase , but is also highly dependent on both the stability and conformation of their ternary complex and the accessibility of lysine residues on the target , – . These complex criteria necessitate careful molecular optimization with respect to ligands for the target and E3 , , and require a linker that modulates the E3–target complex so that it adopts a conformation that is optimal for ubiquitination , .
The efficacy of PROTACs is also highly dependent on the intracellular activity – and distribution of the E3 ligases of interest. Recent studies have revealed the risk of the acquisition of drug resistance after long-term treatment with a PROTAC resulting from loss of activity of the target-E3 ligases and related factors , and mutations at the target–E3 interface that affect the stability of their ternary complex . Given the potential limitations of E3-dependent targeted protein degradation, the chemical knockdown of target proteins without the need for endogenous E3 ligases has attracted strong attention – . Targeted ubiquitination independent of endogenous ubiquitination machineries would offer widely applicable platforms for the targeted degradation and ubiquitin-mediated control of native proteins.
We have established indirect ubiquitination that enables the non-covalent tethering of proteolytic ubiquitin-based modifiers to target proteins independent of endogenous ubiquitination enzymes (Fig. ). The ubiquitin–ligand chimera, a key molecule to indirect ubiquitination, readily modifies the target protein with ubiquitin “indirectly” via non-covalent interaction between a ligand moiety and the target, which leads to ubiquitin-mediated protein degradation. A system for indirect ubiquitination would provide expanded opportunities for induced protein degradation by allowing the targeted ubiquitination of proteins regardless of the complexity and impairment of the activities of endogenous ubiquitination machineries.
 

## Results

## Induced degradation of Bcl-2 using a peptide-based ubiquitin–ligand conjugate
For proof of concept of indirect ubiquitination, we designed a chimeric molecule comprising two essential moieties: ubiquitin which mediates proteasomal degradation; and a ligand which binds to a target protein (Fig. ). The designed chimeric molecule would tether (poly)ubiquitin to the target via the non-covalent interaction with a ligand moiety (Fig. ). We first adopted a peptide as the ligand moiety and focused on the anti-apoptotic protein Bcl-2 as a model oncogenic protein. By using peptide as a ligand, the full-length ubiquitin–ligand chimera can be encoded in a nucleic acid sequence for their easy preparation. We also selected UbG75V/G76V (UbVV) as a ubiquitin unit that is resistant to the intracellular deubiquitylation, as reported previously , envisioning its intracellular application. Based on the consideration above, we prepared, by protein expression from E. coli, a ubiquitin–ligand chimera consisting of a linearly conjugated UbVV dimer (M1(UbVV)2) and a peptide ligand, PepL16 (16 amino acids [aa] in length) (Supplementary Fig. ). The ligand is derived from the α-helical domains of the pro-apoptotic BH3-only proteins recognized by Bcl-2. The binding of M1(UbVV)2–PepL16 to Bcl-2 was confirmed by evaluating the interaction between the M1(UbVV)2–ligands and recombinant Bcl-2 with C-terminal His-tag (Bcl-2(2-211)-His6) using biolayer interferometry (BLI) (KD = 0.34 μM) (Fig. ). The clear signal response in the BLI measurement proved that PepL16 retained its affinity for Bcl-2 even when conjugated to M1(UbVV)2 (Fig. ). We then evaluated the degradation of Bcl-2(2-211)-His6 induced by M1(UbVV)2–PepL16. When 2.5 µM M1(UbVV)2–PepL16 or M1(UbVV)2–Cys (a no-ligand negative control sample) was mixed with 0.1 µM Bcl-2(2-211)-His6 in the presence of a bovine 26S proteasome, we observed strong reduction of the Bcl-2 level in the M1(UbVV)2–PepL16-treated group compared with that of the M1(UbVV)2–Cys-treated group (Fig. ). The Bcl-2 level decreased by 70–85% (75% on average, the triplicated results) in the M1(UbVV)2–PepL16-treated group relative to that of the M1(UbVV)2–Cys-treated group (Fig. ), indicating that the degradation of Bcl-2 is dependent on the ligand moiety of M1(UbVV)2–PepL16. The requirement of the ligand moiety for the strong reduction of the Bcl-2 level indicates that M1(UbVV)2 tethered to the target via the ligands induced the proteasomal degradation. Thus, the basic concept of indirect ubiquitination-mediated target proteolysis was demonstrated.
 

## The design variability of the proteolytic ubiquitin-ligand chimera
We evaluated the Bcl-2 degradation using a ligand with chemical properties different from PepL16 to test the design variability of the ubiquitin-ligand chimeras for target proteolysis. Specifically, we adopted PepL20 (20 aa in length) as an alternative peptide ligand, which is longer than PepL16 by four amino acids (Fig. ), to evaluate the effect of the extension of the linker region between the ubiquitin and ligand moieties on the proteolytic activity. The structural prediction based on AlphaFold 3 implied that the rigid end of proximal UbVV (Leu71) of M1(UbVV)2–PepL20 is away from the contact site of Bcl-2 and the ligand by approximately 10 Å, corresponding to 2–3 aa in length, compared with that of M1(UbVV)2–PepL16 (Fig. ). In addition, the main chain of Arg72 and Leu73 at the UbVV C-terminal flanked by Leu71 and the ligand seems flexible in M1(UbVV)2–PepL20 without forming a characteristic structure, while Arg72 and Leu73 of M1(UbVV)2–PepL16 are involved in a helical-like structure (Fig. ). The evaluation of the M1(UbVV)2–PepL20-mediated degradation of Bcl-2 would provide insight into the applicability of flexible and extended linkers as a design guideline for the ubiquitin-ligand chimera potentially generalizable to a variety of proteins.
 First, we tested the affinity of M1(UbVV)2–PepL20 to Bcl-2. The BLI measurement confirmed that M1(UbVV)2–PepL20 interacts with Bcl-2(2-211)-His6 with an affinity (KD = 1.1 μM) a little lower than M1(UbVV)2–PepL16 (Fig. ). We then mixed M1(UbVV)2–PepL20 (2.5 µM) with Bcl-2(2–211)-His6 (0.1 µM) in the presence of the 26S bovine proteasome to evaluate the ability of M1(UbVV)2–PepL20 to induce Bcl-2 degradation. We observed a clear reduction of the Bcl-2 level in the group treated with M1(UbVV)2– PepL20 compared with that in the group incubated without the proteasome (proteasome-free group) (Fig. ). This Bcl-2 reduction was almost completely recovered by the addition of MG132 (Fig. ), indicating that M1(UbVV)2–PepL20-mediated degradation of Bcl-2 was proteasome-dependent. In addition, M1(UbVV)2–Cys failed to induce the obvious reduction of the Bcl-2 level from the level of the proteasome-free group (Fig. ), recapitulating that the tethering of M1(UbVV)2 to the target is critical to the proteasomal degradation of Bcl-2. The decrease of Bcl-2 level induced by M1(UbVV)2–PepL20 was 60% on average when normalized to the level of the M1(UbVV)2–Cys treated group (Fig. ). The efficiency of Bcl-2 degradation mediated by M1(UbVV)2–PepL20 was slightly lower than that induced by M1(UbVV)2–PepL16 (75% degradation). However, the clear degradation of Bcl-2 by M1(UbVV)2–L20 suggests that the extended linker is acceptable for the design of the proteolytic ubiquitin-ligand chimeras in the system of indirect ubiquitination. The applicability of the flexible and long linker is an important guideline for the design of ubiquitin-ligand chimeras in indirect ubiquitination because the ligand moiety can be kept apart from the ubiquitin moiety to minimize the interference in their functions caused by their spatial proximity.

## Evaluation of the expandability of the target scope with ubiquitin–DNA chimeras
We assessed the potential generalizability of indirect ubiquitination to other targets. To test the expandability of indirect ubiquitination, it is important to ask whether (i) ligand types different from peptides are applicable to design degraders targeting other proteins in addition to Bcl-2, and (ii) types of ubiquitin affect the potential efficacy of induced proteolysis. With respect to the point (i), we focused on nuclear factor κB (NF-κB) as the second target. NF-κB is one of the transcriptional factors that have been regarded as “undruggable” and is implicated in serious diseases, including cancer and autoimmune disorders . If DNA-based ligands are adoptable, the system of indirect ubiquitination would be extended to targeting the pathogenic DNA-binding proteins, including the transcription factors, for their proteolysis (Fig. ), in addition to protein-binding proteins that can be targeted by peptide ligands as shown for Bcl-2 (Figs. , ). Regarding point (ii), we adopted monoubiquitin for the ubiquitin moiety to test the feasibility of simplifying the molecular design for ready chemical conjugation of ubiquitin and non-peptide ligands. Related to this point, a previous study reported that monoubiquitination is sufficient for proteolysis of a large group of proteins .
 We designed a conjugate of monoubiquitin (Ub) and a short hairpin DNA for indirect ubiquitination and induced degradation of NF-κB, based on the consideration above. In particular, the short hairpin DNA with an NF-κB recognition sequence (5′–GGGAAATCCC–3′) was selected (named NFdecoy) as a ligand due to its efficient trapping of the NF-κB subunit p50 confirmed by the electrophoretic mobility shift assay (EMSA) (Supplementary Figs. , ). The 3′-terminal of NFdecoy was labeled with fluorescein (FAM) for easy detection. To synthesize the Ub–NFdecoy chimera that enables indirect ubiquitination of NF-κB (Fig. ), a maleimide moiety was introduced at the 5′-terminal of amine-modified NFdecoy by coupling with succinimidyl-4-(N-maleimidomethyl)cyclohexane-1-carboxylate (SMCC) (Supplementary Fig. ). The maleimide-functionalized NFdecoy was subsequently linked via Michael addition with ubiquitin, which has a cysteine residue at its C-terminal (C77), to yield the conjugate Ub–NFdecoy (Fig. ). The linker region derived from maleimide and cyclohexyl groups might improve the accessibility of NFdecoy by NF-κB by keeping ubiquitin away from NFdecoy. As expected, it was confirmed by EMSA that Ub–NFdecoy was bound to recombinant p50, a subunit of NF-κB, as efficiently as NFdecoy (Fig. ).
Ub–NFdecoy was applied to induce the proteasome-dependent degradation of NF-κB in vitro using recombinant p50. In this cell-free assay, we used a yeast 26S proteasome as a surrogate for a mammalian proteasome due to its relative ease of preparation in a larger amount for detailed evaluation of the relationship between the binding of the ubiquitin-ligand chimera to the target and the degradation efficiency. When 1 µM Ub–NFdecoy was incubated with 0.25 µM p50 in the presence of a yeast 26S proteasome at 37 °C, the p50 level was reduced to 24% after 1 h incubation, and the half maximal degradation concentration (DC50) was 0.20 μM (Fig. ). The value of DC50 matched the apparent dissociation constant (KD,App = 0.26 μM) of p50 and NFdecoy estimated by EMSA (Fig. and Supplementary Fig. ), which indicated that the binding of Ub–NFdecoy is a critical step of p50 degradation. By contrast, p50 degradation was inhibited strongly by the addition of the proteasome inhibitor MG132 (Fig. , left). No significant reduction in the p50 level was observed when Ub or NFdecoy was mixed separately with p50 (Fig. , left). The requirement of both Ub–NFdecoy and active proteasome for the p50 degradation indicates that the DNA-based ligand is well compatible with indirect ubiquitination to target the DNA-binding protein for its proteasome-dependent degradation. In addition, UbAT–NFdecoy (Fig. ), in which UbR72A/R74T (UbAT) was used as a ubiquitin moiety that is resistant to enzymatic deubiquitylation , trapped p50 and induced its degradation as efficiently as Ub–NFdecoy (Fig. , right and Supplementary Fig. ). The efficient degradation of p50 induced by UbAT–NFdecoy suggested that C-terminal mutation of Ub had little effect on the target proteolysis. Thus, indirect ubiquitination is potentially applicable to target different proteins other than Bcl-2 for its proteolysis, and the ligand type and the ubiquitin moiety are replaceable for expanding the scope of targets, allowing flexible molecular design, and imparting the resistant to enzymatic decomposition and deubiquitylation to the ubiquitin–ligand chimera.

## Intracellular expression of the degraders for induced Bcl-2 degradation
We assessed the feasibility of intracellular proteolysis of Bcl-2 by indirect ubiquitination using the M1(UbVV)2–ligands, which induced the efficient degradation of Bcl-2 in cell-free assay (Figs. B, C and ). Since the peptide-based M1(UbVV)2–ligands are encodable in nucleic acid sequences, they can be delivered into cells in the form of nucleic acid via an established carrier for their long-term expression and retention in cells. To this aim, we first tested the proteolytic activity of M1(UbVV)2 in cell using a fusion protein of M1(UbVV)2 and enhanced green fluorescent protein (EGFP) tagged with hemagglutinin (HA) peptide at its C-terminal (EGFP-HA) (Fig. ). We found that M1(UbVV)2 clearly shortened the lifetime of the substrate protein (Fig. ). The reduction in the M1(UbVV)2–EGFP-HA level was strongly suppressed by proteasome inhibitors (MG132 and bortezomib) but not by lysosome inhibitors (pepstatin A + E-64d) (Fig. and Supplementary Fig. ). The recovery of the protein level by the proteasome inhibitors indicates that the proteolysis induced by M1(UbVV)2 is dependent on the ubiquitin–proteasome pathway. In addition, little inhibition of M1(UbVV)2–EGFP-HA degradation was observed with the use of TAK-243, an E1 inhibitor, which suggests that M1(UbVV)2 can induce substrate degradation without further polyubiquitination (Fig. ). The proteolytic activity of M1(UbVV)2 independent of additional polyubiquitination was also supported by the degradation of EGFP-HA fused with M1(UbVVK0)2, in which all lysine residues in the ubiquitin units were replaced fully with arginine residues to block major ubiquitination sites (Supplementary Fig. ). Thus, M1(UbVV)2 is also active in the intracellular proteolysis of the substrate.
 Given the intracellular proteolytic activity of M1(UbVV)2, we applied the plasmid that encodes the M1(UbVV)2–ligands under control of the cytomegalovirus (CMV) promoter to inducing the Bcl-2 degradation in living cells (HeLa cells) (Fig. ). A significant reduction in the Bcl-2 level (79% by M1(UbVV)2–PepL20 and 53% by M1(UbVV)2–PepL16 on average, the triplicated results) was observed at 23 h post-transfection compared with that of the untransfected cells (Fig. ). Addition of MG132 strongly recovered the decreased Bcl-2 levels (Fig. ), which suggests that the M1(UbVV)2–ligands induced degradation of Bcl-2 in a proteasome-dependent manner as shown for M1(UbVV)2–EGFP-HA (Fig. and Supplementary Fig. ). We note that it is possible that the M1(UbVV)2–ligands might undergo the additional ubiquitination to facilitate the target proteolysis, and to what degree the M1(UbVV)2 moiety of M1(UbVV)2–ligands induced the target degradation without ubiquitination remain to be examined at the current stage. However, these findings demonstrate that indirect ubiquitination mediated by the ubiquitin–ligand chimeras is applicable to trigger the target proteolysis in the cellular context as well.
As shown by the observation that M1(UbVV)2–PepL20 that has lower affinity to Bcl-2 (Figs. and ) induced the stronger degradation of Bcl-2 in cell over M1(UbVV)2–PepL16 (Fig. ), the efficiency of the proteolysis induced by M1(UbVV)2–PepL16 and PepL20 in cell was not necessarily correlated with their affinity to the target. There should be two points to be considered to discuss possible reasons for this apparent discrepancy. The first point is the difference in the expression level and intracellular stability between M1(UbVV)2–PepL16 and –PepL20. As shown for Ub–NFdecoy in Fig. , the degradation efficiency is highly dependent on the concentration of the degrader, which indicates that the percentage of the target bound to the ubiquitin-ligand chimera is critical for the proteolytic efficiency in indirect ubiquitination. However, the difference in the expression level between M1(UbVV)2–PepL16 and PepL20 might not be a major factor to explain their difference in the efficiency of induced degradation of Bcl-2, because M1(UbVV)2–PepL20 was likely to be expressed less efficiently in HeLa cell than M1(UbVV)2–PepL16 at 24 h post-transfection (Supplementary Fig. ). Another potentially important difference between M1(UbVV)2–PepL16 and PepL20 is the spatial exposure of M1(UbVV)2 in a complex with Bcl-2. As discussed before and shown in the predicted complexes of the M1(UbVV)2–ligands and Bcl-2 (Fig. ), the flexible C-terminal region and the rigid end (Leu71) of the proximal ubiquitin unit of M1(UbVV)2–PepL16 come close to the binding interface between Bcl-2 and the ligand moiety. In addition, Arg72 and Leu73, neighboring Leu71, were predicted to form a helical-like structure, which implies that the C-terminal region of M1(UbVV)2–PepL16 was substantially rigidified. Conversely, the Leu71 of M1(UbVV)2–PepL20 was around 10 Å more distant from the binding interface compared with M1(UbVV)2–PepL16, a distance that corresponds to 2–3 aa in length (Fig. , expanded window). Arg72 and Leu73 of M1(UbVV)2–PepL20 did not exhibit a characteristic structure in the predicted complex, which suggests that they retain flexibility (Fig. ). The spatial apartness of the rigid end of the M1(UbVV)2 moiety from the binding interface and the relative flexibility at the C-terminus of the proximal ubiquitin unit of M1(UbVV)2–PepL20 can expand a conformational variation of the M1(UbVV)2 moiety (Supplementary Fig. ). Judged from the better ability of M1(UbVV)2–PepL16 to induce the proteasomal Bcl-2 degradation in cell-free assay (Figs. B, C and ), these structural differences between M1(UbVV)2–PepL20 and PepL16 were not likely to affect the recognition of M1(UbVV)2 by the proteasome itself. However, it is generally known that adapter proteins, which recognize ubiquitinated proteins to deliver them to the proteasome, also play important roles in efficient proteasomal degradation of ubiquitinated substrates in cell . Based on the considerations above, it is plausible that the higher conformational variation of the M1(UbVV)2 in M1(UbVV)2–PepL20 might increase its accessibility to the adapter proteins for efficient delivery of Bcl-2 to the proteasome.

## Discussion
In this study, we demonstrate that indirect ubiquitination, the non-covalent attachment of ubiquitin to the target proteins with the ligand–ubiquitin chimeras, triggers the proteasome-dependent degradation of the target proteins. We found that the ubiquitin–ligand chimeras with the peptide and DNA-based ligands designed to target Bcl-2 and NF-κB as model oncogenic proteins induced the strong reduction of the target proteins depending on the 26S proteasomes in vitro (Figs. – ). The degradation efficiency of Bcl-2 and p50 reached 60–75% in 1 h (Figs. , , ), which is compatible with that of a covalently ubiquitinated substrate (monoubiquitinated globin: 53% in 1 h) . The high proteolytic efficiency achieved by indirect ubiquitination indicates that the ubiquitin tethered to the proteins with a non-covalent bond also works as a chemical mediator of the substrate degradation. To support this point, the degradation of the target proteins was highly associated with the binding between the ligands and the targets, as shown by the dependency of the proteolysis on the ligand moiety (Figs. , , ) and the correspondence between KD and DC50 (Figs. ). Thus, indirect ubiquitination offers a simple methodology for non-enzymatic tethering of ubiquitin to the proteins for their proteasome-dependent proteolysis.
We also found that the M1(UbVV)2–ligand chimeras that are resistant against deubiquitylation induced the proteolysis of the target Bcl-2 in cellular contexts (Fig. ). The resistance against intracellular deubiquitylation should be critical to keep the ubiquitin and the ligand conjugated during the time required for trapping and subsequent delivery of the target to the proteasomes. In accordance with this consideration, a recent report by Shulman’s group has described the possible association of polyubiquitin chains between the susceptibility to deubiquitylation and the rate of proteasomal degradation, in which polyubiquitin chains that are less easily deubiquitylated are more capable of inducing strong substrate proteolysis . In general, the C-terminal of ubiquitin should be mutated to impart resistance to the deubiquitylation , , , . However, the mutations at the C-terminal were found to be acceptable for the proteasomal degradation of the target proteins induced by indirect ubiquitination without impairing the proteolytic efficiency (Figs. , , ). Thus, the ubiquitin–ligand with the high intracellular stability would be a robust platform for chemical ubiquitination aimed at inducing protein degradation in the cellular context.
We further investigated the Bcl-2 level at 16 h post-transfection, which is 7 h earlier than that of Fig. (23 h post-transfection), to gain insight into the time required to induce the target proteolysis by indirect ubiquitination, adopting the intracellular expression of the degrader from the plasmid. We observed clear reduction of the Bcl-2 level at that time point, but the degree of the Bcl-2 degradation (around 60%) was slightly less than that at 24 h post-transfection by 10–20 percentage points (Supplementary Fig. ). The clear but intermediate level of the Bcl-2 reduction at 16-h post-transfection indicates that the target proteolysis started to be induced before that time point but did not reach the steady state in which the degradation and expression of Bcl-2 were balanced. This time course of the Bcl-2 degradation is consistent with the observation that the expression level of M1(UbVV)2–PepL20 started to increase from 12 h post-transfection to 24 h (Supplementary Fig. ). Thus, the indirect ubiquitination that adopts a plasmid to express a peptide-based degrader in the cell of interest should require around 1 day for the target proteolysis. The time required for the degradation (~24 h) mediated by indirect ubiquitination is not exceptionally longer compared with that required for PROTAC-based proteolysis. Although the typical time required for PROTACs to reach the maximum degradation is 12 h or less, some PROTACs are reported to require nearly 24 h to complete the target degradation , . Previous studies suggested that the kinetics of PROTAC-mediated degradation are highly associated with the kinetics of the ubiquitination of target proteins, thereby the low expression level of E3 ligases in cells and/or the instability of E3–target complexes can retard the rate of target proteolysis induced by the PROTACs , .
A potential challenge that indirect ubiquitination will face is the delivery of the ubiquitin–ligand chimera as a biomacromolecule in vivo. One of the possible solutions to this issue is adopting the peptide-based ubiquitin–ligand chimera that can be delivered in the form of nucleic acids (e.g., DNA plasmid and messenger RNA), as shown at the cellular level in Fig. of this study, since delivery systems of the nucleic acids are under clinical use and further improvement . On the other hand, the scope of the target proteins will be expanded if non-peptide ligands (e.g., oligonucleotides and small molecules), which cannot be encoded in the nucleic sequences, are adoptable for intracellular indirect ubiquitination. The ubiquitin–ligand chimera based on the non-peptide ligand has been proven to be capable of inducing the target proteolysis by the efficient proteasomal degradation of the NF-κB p50 subunit triggered by Ub–NFdecoy in cell-free assay (Fig. ). The key step to this challenge would be thereby the development of the efficient method to deliver the ubiquitin–ligand chimera into cell with concentration adequate for efficient tethering of ubiquitin to the target proteins. The protein carriers, which are under intensive development , should be potential solutions to this issue, given that ubiquitin is a protein. Investigation of the compatibility of ubiquitin–small molecule and ubiquitin–DNA fusions with those protein carriers at the cell and in vivo levels would provide key insight to achieve efficient delivery of ubiquitin–ligand chimeras with various types of ligand. In combination with the delivery systems of biomacromolecules suitable for the ubiquitin–ligand chimeras, indirect ubiquitination would offer a robust platform to tackle issues related to the enzyme-mediated protein ubiquitination and degradation, including drug resistance caused by loss of E3 activity, and differential localization of E3 ligases and target proteins – .

## Methods

## General information
Reagents and solvents were purchased from standard suppliers and used without further purification. Column chromatography for the purification and characterization of the expressed ubiquitin units and ubiquitin–DNA conjugates was performed using an ÄKTATM purifier 10 (Cytiva) or a high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) system comprising JASCO modules (PDA detector: MD-4015, RHPLC pump: PU-4180, software: ChromNAV ver. 2) equipped with the inert systems. The concentrations of ubiquitin units and ubiquitin–DNA conjugates were measured by UV absorbance at 280 and 260 nm, respectively, using a NanoDrop One (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The protein concentration of the cell extracts was measured using the bicinchoninic acid protein assay (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Mass spectrometry was performed using a microflex-NAC (Bruker) for matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization time-of-flight mass spectrometry (MALDI-TOF MS). 3′-FAM (fluorescein) and 5′-amine-modified oligonucleotides were purchased as HPLC grade from FASMAC Co., Ltd. The sequences of the oligonucleotides used in this study are provided in Supplementary Fig. . EGFP-HA, M1(UbVV)2–EGFP-HA, M1(UbVV)2–PepL20, and M1(UbVV)2– PepL16 were cloned into the pCMV vector (Clontech) for intracellular expression. Chemical luminescent imaging for Western blotting was performed using FUSION Solo S (Vilber), ImageQuant LAS 4000 (Cytiva), or ChemiDoc XRS+ (Bio-Rad) instruments. Fluorescent imaging of native polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (PAGE) gels was performed using a Gel Doc Go imaging system (Bio-Rad).

## Evaluation of affinity between M1(UbVV)2-peptide ligands and Bcl-2 using biolayer interferometry (BLI)
Octet HIS1K biosensors (anti-penta-His sensors) were utilized on a Sartorius Octet N1 system. The initial baseline measurement was performed for 30 s using an assay buffer (20 mM sodium phosphate, pH 7.2, 150 mM NaCl, 0.1% (w/v) BSA, and 0.02% (v/v) Tween-20). The ligand, 2 µM Bcl-2(2-211)-His×6, was loaded to the sensor for 120 s, and the baseline was measured for 30 s using the assay buffer. Then, M1(UbVV)2–PepL20 or –PepL16 dissolved in the assay buffer was applied on the drop holder as an analyte and the association was monitored for 60 s. Then, the solution in the drop holder was replaced with the fresh assay buffer and the dissociation was measured for 60 s. The sensorgram was subtracted with a blank run obtained using the assay buffer and step-corrected using Octet N1 software. The corrected sensorgrams obtained using different concentrations of M1(UbVV)2–ligands were fitted by the one-to-one binding model using the global fitting mode in Octet N1 software. The measurements for each M1(UbVV)2–ligand were triplicated and the binding parameters were calculated by averaging the values from respective measurements.

## In vitro degradation of recombinant Bcl-2
C-terminal His-tagged Bcl-2 (Bcl-2 (2–211)-His6) (0.2 µM) and the M1(UbVV)2–ligands (M1(UbVV)2–PepL20, –PepL16 and –Cys) (5.0 µM) were first incubated in 5 µL degradation buffer (40 mM Tris pH 7.1, 40 mM NaCl, 5 mM MgCl2, and 2 mM 2-mercaptoethanol) supplemented with 2.5 mM TCEP at 37 °C for 30 min. After the incubation, bovine 26S proteasome (100 nM), ATP (8 mM) and Glycerol (20%) in 5 µL degradation buffer were added with 100 μM MG132, where indicated (total reaction volume was 10 µL), and the mixture was further incubated at 37 °C for 1 h. After the 1-h incubation, the reaction was quenched by adding 3.5 µL of 4 × SDS loading buffer (240 mM Tris·HCl, pH 6.8, 8% (w/v) SDS, 40% (v/v) glycerol, and 5% (w/v) 2-mercaptoethanol). The proteins were separated by 16% sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS)-PAGE and analyzed by Western blotting using a mouse anti-Bcl-2 antibody (SantaCruz, sc-7382, 1:2000), and an HRP-conjugated anti-IgG secondary antibody (Proteintech, SA00001-1, 1:5000).

## Synthesis of maleimide-functionalized oligonucleotides
Oligonucleotide (33 μM) modified with an amino group at the 5′-terminal and 6-FAM at the 3′-terminal was mixed with SMCC (300 μM) in a sodium phosphate buffer (12.5 mM, pH 7.5, 160 μL) containing 50% (v/v) N,N-dimethylformamide (DMF). The reaction was performed at room temperature for 100 min. The reaction was monitored by HPLC (10–25% MeCN/0.1 M triethylamine acetate gradient) (Supplementary Fig. ). Residual SMCC was removed by ethanol precipitation. Briefly, the reaction mixture was supplemented with 5.3 μL of 3 M sodium acetate (pH 5.2) and 2 μL of ethachinmate (Nippon Gene). Ethanol (400 μL) was added, and the mixture was centrifuged at 12,000×g for 5 min. The supernatant was removed, and the precipitate was rinsed twice with ice-cold 70% ethanol. The residue was left in a plastic tube at room temperature until dry while shielded from light to yield maleimide-functionalized oligonucleotides.

## Synthesis of ubiquitin–DNA conjugates
Maleimide-functionalized oligonucleotides synthesized above (7–20 µM) and UbC77 or UbATC77 (40 eq.) were mixed in a sodium phosphate buffer (20 mM, pH 7.0), and the reaction mixture was incubated overnight at room temperature. After incubation, the mixture was heated at 50 °C for 20 min and cooled slowly to room temperature on a heat block to anneal the hairpin DNA. The ubiquitin–DNA conjugate and unreacted ubiquitin were then separated by size-exclusion chromatography using a SuperdexTM 75 Increase 10/300 GL column (Cytiva). The products were eluted with one column volume of S buffer (20 mM sodium phosphate, pH 7.2, 150 mM NaCl). The fractions containing the pure ubiquitin–DNA conjugate were pooled and concentrated in Amicon Ultra centrifugal filter units (MWCO 10 kDa) (Merck). The products were characterized using 12% native PAGE (150 V, 30 min, 1× Tris-borate-ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (TBE) buffer) followed by gel fluorescence imaging using a Gel Doc EZ Imager (Bio-Rad).

## In vitro trapping of NF-κB p50 analysed by EMSA
NF-κB p50 (500 nM, Cayman Chemical) was first incubated in a binding buffer (10 mM 4-(2-hydroxyethyl)-1-piperazineethanesulfonic acid (HEPES), pH 7.9, 50 mM KCl, 2.5 mM tris(2-carboxyethyl)phosphine (TCEP), 10% glycerol, 100 μM ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA), 0.05% Nonidet P-40 (NP-40)) at room temperature for 30 min. The 3′-FAM-labeled free DNA or ubiquitin–DNA conjugates (25 nM) were mixed with the reaction mixture in the presence or absence of the competitor DNA (1 μM) and incubated at room temperature for 30 min. The competitor DNA (1 μM) was NFdecoy without the FAM label. The mixture was resolved by 8% native PAGE (0.5 × TBE, 4 °C, 150 V, 40 min), and the FAM-labeled DNA and its complex with p50 were detected using a Gel Doc EZ Imager (Bio-Rad). The band intensity was quantified using ImageJ software.

## In vitro degradation of NF-κB p50
NF-κB p50 (0.30 µM, Cayman Chemical) was first incubated in a degradation buffer (25 mM HEPES pH 7.5, 50 mM NaCl, 1 mM TCEP, 10% glycerol, 0.05% NP-40) at room temperature for 30 min. The ubiquitin–DNA conjugates, ubiquitin, or oligonucleotides were added to the reaction mixture at 1 µM or varied concentrations as indicated in Fig. , and incubated at room temperature for 30 min. After the incubation, yeast 26S proteasome (100 nM), ATP (5 mM), and MgCl2 (5 mM) were added with MG132 (100 μM) where indicated, and the mixture was incubated at 37 °C for 1 h. The proteins were separated by 10% sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS)-PAGE and analysed by Western blotting using a rabbit anti-p50 antibody (Proteintech, 15506-1-AP, 1:1000), and an HRP-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG secondary antibody (Proteintech, SA00001-2, 1:5000). The concentrations indicated in the brackets are the final concentrations.

## Cell culture
HeLa cells (ATCC) was cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin (PS) mixed solution (Nacalai Tesque Inc.). DMEM was supplemented with the same additives throughout this study unless otherwise indicated. All cells were maintained in 5% CO2 in a humidified incubator at 37 °C.

## Chase assay to measure the degradation of Ub–EGFP fusion proteins
Cells were seeded into a 24-well plate (0.5 mL standard growth medium, 2 × 104 cells per well) and grown to ~60% confluence in DMEM for 24 h at 37 °C under 5% CO2, and the cells were then washed with PBS twice. The medium was changed to Opti-MEM (100 μL, Gibco) supplemented with the constructed plasmid (125 ng) and LipofectamineTM 3000 (0.6 µL, Invitrogen), which were premixed in Opti-MEM. After the 4 h incubation, the medium was changed to DMEM. The cells were incubated further for 16 h at 37 °C under 5% CO2. The cells were treated with CHX (50 μg/mL in 0.1% dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO)) and then exposed to inhibitors for 0–6 h (as indicated) at 37 °C under 5% CO2. The inhibitors were MG132 (10 μM in 0.1% DMSO), bortezomib (10 nM in 0.1% DMSO), pepstatin A and E-64d (10 μg/mL in 0.1% DMSO), and TAK-243 (1 μM in 0.1% DMSO) as well as CHX. The cells were then lysed with radioimmunoprecipitation assay (RIPA) buffer (Nacalai Tesque Inc.) following the manufacturer’s protocol. The protein levels were measured by Western blotting using rabbit anti-HA (Proteintech, 51064-2-AP, 1:1000), anti-ubiquitin (Proteintech, 10201-2-AP, 1:1000), anti-β-actin (Proteintech, 20536-1-AP, 1:1000) and anti-α-tubulin (Proteintech, 11224-1-AP, 1:1000) antibodies as primary antibodies, and an HRP-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG (Proteintech, SA00001-2, 1:5000) as a secondary antibody. HA and ubiquitin levels were normalized to the β-actin or α-tubulin level in the same lanes.

## Evaluation of the intracellular degradation of Bcl-2 induced by M1(UbVV)2–ligands expressed from the plasmid
Cells were seeded into a 24-well plate (0.5 mL standard growth medium, 2 × 104 cells per well) and grown to ~60% confluence in DMEM at 37 °C under 5% CO2 for 24 h. The cells were washed with PBS twice. The medium was changed to Opti-MEM (320 μL, Gibco) supplemented with the constructed plasmid (400 ng) and LipofectamineTM 3000 (2.0 µL, Invitrogen), which were premixed in Opti-MEM. After the 4 h incubation, the medium was changed to DMEM. The cells were incubated further for 3 h at 37 °C under 5% CO2 and then treated with the inhibitor MG132 (10 μM in 0.1% DMSO) or nothing at 37 °C under 5% CO2 for 16 h. Cells were then lysed with RIPA buffer (Nacalai Tesque Inc.) by following the manufacturer’s protocol. The protein levels were measured by Western blotting using rabbit anti-ubiquitin (Proteintech, 10201-2-AP, 1:1000), mouse anti-Bcl-2 (SantaCruz, sc-7382, 1:1000), and rabbit anti-α-tubulin (Proteintech, 11224-1-AP, 1:1000) antibodies as primary antibodies, and HRP-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG (Proteintech, SA00001-1, 1:2000) and anti-rabbit IgG (Proteintech, SA00001-2, 1:2000) as secondary antibodies. Bcl-2 and ubiquitin levels were normalized to the α-tubulin level in the same lane.
