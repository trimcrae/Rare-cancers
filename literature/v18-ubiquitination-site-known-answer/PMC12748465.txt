## Introduction
Colorectal cancer (CRC) remains a significant global health challenge, ranking as the third most commonly diagnosed cancer and the second leading cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide [ ]. Despite advancements in surgical techniques, chemotherapy regimens, and targeted therapies, a substantial proportion of patients with CRC experience disease recurrence and ultimately succumb to the disease [ – ]. This underscores the urgent need for a deeper understanding of the molecular mechanisms driving CRC progression and therapy resistance, as well as the development of novel therapeutic strategies to improve patient outcomes.
The malignant progression of CRC is a complex, multistep process characterized by the accumulation of genetic and epigenetic alterations that disrupt cellular homeostasis and promote uncontrolled cell growth, invasion, and metastasis [ ]. These alterations often converge on key signaling pathways that govern fundamental cellular processes, such as cell proliferation, apoptosis, DNA repair, and the epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) [ ]. Post-translational modifications (PTMs) play a crucial role in regulating the activity and stability of proteins involved in these signaling pathways [ , ].
Ubiquitination, a reversible PTM involving the attachment of ubiquitin moieties to target proteins, is a critical regulator of protein turnover, signal transduction, and DNA repair [ , ]. This dynamic process is tightly controlled by two opposing families of enzymes: ubiquitin ligases (E3s), which catalyze the addition of ubiquitin, and deubiquitinases (DUBs), which remove ubiquitin [ , ]. The balance between ubiquitination and deubiquitination is essential for maintaining cellular homeostasis, and dysregulation of this balance can contribute to the development and progression of various diseases, including cancer [ , ].
Ubiquitin-specific proteases (USPs) constitute the largest subfamily of DUBs, comprising approximately 56 members in humans [ – ]. USPs exhibit diverse substrate specificities and cellular functions, participating in a wide range of cellular processes, including DNA repair, cell cycle regulation, signal transduction, and protein trafficking. Given their diverse roles in cellular regulation, USPs have emerged as important players in cancer development and progression [ , ].
In recent years, accumulating evidence has highlighted the involvement of USPs in the malignant progression and therapy resistance of CRC. Aberrant expression or activity of specific USPs can disrupt key signaling pathways, leading to uncontrolled cell growth, invasion, metastasis, and resistance to chemotherapy and targeted therapies. Therefore, targeting USPs represents a promising therapeutic strategy for CRC.
This review aims to provide a comprehensive overview of the current understanding of USPs as key regulators in the malignant progression and therapy resistance of CRC. We will discuss the roles of specific USPs in regulating key signaling pathways involved in CRC development, as well as the potential of targeting USPs as a therapeutic strategy for this disease. Furthermore, we will explore the challenges and future perspectives in the development of USP inhibitors for CRC treatment.

 

## Regulatory role of USPs in colorectal cancer biological functions

## Carcinogenic effect
USPs maintain the stability of key proteins through deubiquitination, and their regulatory roles in colorectal cancer progression encompass nearly all aspects of tumor biology, including proliferation, migration, invasion, metastasis, apoptosis, immune evasion, epithelial-mesenchymal transition, and drug resistance (as shown in Fig. ). Notably, the influence of USPs on tumor biological behavior is primarily achieved by regulating the expression of downstream target molecules. For instance, in the study by Xu et al. researchers conducted CCK-8 assays and xenograft studies, finding that knockdown of USP5 expression effectively inhibited tumor cell proliferation and tumor tissue growth, suggesting that USP5 plays a role in tumor initiation and progression. To further investigate the regulatory mechanism of USP5, the researchers explored databases related to USP5 downstream targets and performed co-immunoprecipitation experiments. The results showed that USP5 could bind to TUFM and maintain the stability of TUFM protein through deubiquitination [ ], where TUFM is known to function as a tumor-promoting factor in colorectal cancer [ ]. In summary, the deubiquitination effect exerted by USPs through binding to specific downstream substrates activates tumor-associated signaling pathways, highlighting their crucial regulatory role in the malignant progression of colorectal cancer.

## Anti-cancer effect
Based on current research, we found that the inhibitory effects of USPs on colorectal cancer biological functions mainly rely on their regulation of downstream tumor suppressor molecules or signaling pathways. Previous studies have shown that HMX3 and HDAC3 act primarily as tumor suppressors in colorectal cancer. Recent research has demonstrated that USP38 acts as an upstream regulator of HMX3 and HDAC3, maintaining their protein expression levels through deubiquitination, thereby enhancing their tumor-suppressive effects on colorectal cancer [ , ]. Axin1, a crucial negative regulator of the Wnt signaling pathway, can prevent the accumulation of β-catenin and hinder the proliferation and survival of tumor cells [ ]. Huang et al. found through Western blotting that USP44 upregulates the expression of Axin1. Subsequent immunoprecipitation and ubiquitination analyses confirmed the interaction between USP44 and Axin1, as well as USP44’s deubiquitinating effect on Axin1 [ ]. These experimental results suggest that USP44 regulates colorectal cancer suppression through the deubiquitination of Axin1. In summary, some USPs, such as USP38 and USP44, inhibit colorectal cancer progression by deubiquitinating target proteins. However, the signaling networks regulating tumor biology are complex, and further investigation is needed to clarify the upstream signaling pathways of these USPs and their regulatory roles on other downstream molecules. In conclusion, certain USPs, such as USP38 and USP44, inhibit colorectal cancer progression by deubiquitinating target proteins. However, the signaling networks regulating tumor biology are complex, and further investigation is needed to elucidate the upstream signaling pathways of these USPs and their regulatory roles on other downstream molecules.

## The impact of USPs on anticancer drugs
USPs stabilize the protein expression levels of downstream target molecules through deubiquitination, regulating multiple signaling pathways and key factors involved in tumorigenesis, which consequently influences the sensitivity of tumor tissues and cells to anticancer agents.

## Platinum-based drugs and antimetabolites
Oxaliplatin combined with 5-FU is the current first-line chemotherapy regimen for colorectal cancer, often used in combination with Leucovorin [ ]. In recent years, multiple USP family enzymes have emerged as contributors to the modulation of Oxaliplatin/5-FU’s anti-tumor effects [ ]. HIF1A, a major transcription factor regulating tumor microenvironment oxygen levels, was found by Mingchao Mu and colleagues to be stabilized by USP51, which prevents the VHL E3 ligase complex from ubiquitinating and degrading HIF1A. The upregulation of HIF1A enhances tumor cell survival in hypoxic environments and increases resistance to oxaliplatin and 5-FU chemotherapy [ ]. Both β-catenin and Twist1 are involved in regulating tumor stemness, and previous studies have demonstrated that USP4 regulates these molecules through deubiquitination. Fengtian Li and colleagues found that knockdown of USP4 not only inhibited tumor sphere formation but also effectively reduced the IC50 of oxaliplatin and increased apoptosis rates after treatment [ ], suggesting that USP4 may affect chemotherapy sensitivity through regulation of tumor stemness. Additionally, the upregulation of USP11 [ ], USP35 [ ], and USP36 [ ] also increased colorectal cancer cell resistance to chemotherapy, as their substrates are associated with oncogenesis. Notably, in contrast to the aforementioned USPs, USP38 is downregulated in colorectal cancer, and its overexpression inhibits tumor growth and proliferation while enhancing tumor cell sensitivity to chemotherapy. Wei Zhan and colleagues revealed that USP38 enhances the deacetylase activity of HDAC3 by removing the K63 ubiquitin chain from K121, which further reduces the transcriptional activity of CD44 and CD133 through acetylation of H3K27ac [ ]. CD44 and CD133 are known to positively regulate tumor stemness.
In conclusion, variations in the expression of different USPs within tumor cells and tissues can reflect the tumor’s sensitivity to chemotherapy drugs. Measuring USP levels in patient sera or tissue samples could, in theory, offer valuable clinical insights for guiding anti-cancer therapies. However, the majority of existing studies remain in the preclinical phase, necessitating further clinical trials for confirmation.

## Other platinum-based drugs
Cisplatin is a commonly used chemotherapeutic agent in clinical practice, widely applied to treat various types of malignant tumors. However, due to its limited efficacy in colorectal cancer treatment, cisplatin is not used as a first-line clinical treatment. GOLPH3 is a Golgi-associated protein that regulates protein transport, lipid metabolism, and other biological processes. It has been proven to act as an oncogene in various malignancies [ ]. Shaojian Chen and colleagues found that the upstream circular RNA circCYFIP2 of USP6 could enhance the deubiquitination of GOLPH3 by USP6, thereby stabilizing GOLPH3 protein levels and indirectly increasing tumor cell resistance to cisplatin [ ]. Additionally, USP39 also plays a role in regulating tumor cell resistance to cisplatin. Jiahui Yuan and colleagues found that knockdown of USP39 could enhance P53 expression and increase tumor cell sensitivity to cisplatin [ ]. In summary, multiple USPs have been shown to influence colorectal cancer cell resistance to cisplatin. However, research on these USPs is still at the phenotypic level, and the specific regulatory mechanisms remain unclear.

## EGFR tyrosine kinase inhibitors
Osimertinib and gefitinib are commonly employed in the management of non-small cell lung cancer with EGFR mutations. These drugs specifically target the tyrosine kinase activity site of EGFR, preventing the activation of downstream signaling cascades and thus impeding tumor cell growth and proliferation [ , ]. In a study conducted by Kubaichuk et al., USP10 was found to promote tumorigenesis in colorectal cancer. Knockdown of USP10 expression decreased tumor cell responsiveness to osimertinib and gefitinib, indicating that USP10 may serve as a potential target for EGFR tyrosine kinase inhibitors. Further investigation showed an inverse relationship between USP10 expression and the downstream molecule INPP4B, which serves as an inhibitory regulator of the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway. In cell lines lacking USP10, the upregulation of INPP4B inhibited EGFR-related signaling, leading to reduced drug sensitivity [ ]. In conclusion, studies have confirmed that knockdown of USP10 reduces tumor cell sensitivity to gefitinib and osimertinib due to INPP4B upregulation, but the exact regulatory mechanism of USP10 on INPP4B remains unclear and requires further investigation.

## Multitarget tyrosine kinase inhibitors
Sorafenib, a multitarget tyrosine kinase inhibitor (mTKI), acts on multiple targets such as VEGFR and HGF receptors, effectively inhibiting tumor cell proliferation and tumor angiogenesis [ ]. Recent research has found that USP15 is closely related to mTKI resistance in tumors. GPX2, an antioxidant enzyme, helps to eliminate ROS accumulation in tumor cells, alleviating damage from anti-tumor drugs. Xiaolin Lu and colleagues discovered that USP15 maintains the protein stability of GPX2 through its deubiquitinating action. Additionally, the upstream molecule ACVRL1 was found to regulate the interaction between USP15 and GPX2 [ ]. In summary, activation of the ACVRL1/USP15/GPX2 signaling axis promotes tumor cell growth and proliferation, increasing tumor resistance to mTKIs. The negative regulation of mTKI sensitivity by USP15 suggests it could be a potential target for anti-tumor drug therapies.

## Anthracycline antibiotics
Anthracycline antibiotics are widely used in the chemotherapy of various malignant tumors, and they kill tumor cells by intercalating into DNA, generating reactive oxygen species (ROS), and inhibiting topoisomerase II activity [ ]. TUFM, FANCD2, and ID1 play important roles in DNA damage repair and have been shown to influence tumor cell resistance to anthracycline antibiotics. FANCD2, an essential protein involved in the Fanconi Anemia (FA) pathway, is directly involved in DNA crosslink repair. TUFM and ID1 indirectly participate in this process by regulating mitochondrial protein synthesis and the cell cycle, respectively. It has been reported that USP1 and USP5 regulate DNA repair pathways. In the study by Xin Xu et al., USP1 and USP5 enhanced the DNA repair ability of tumor cells by stabilizing the proteins TUFM, FANCD2, and ID1, effectively counteracting the DNA damage induced by drugs like doxorubicin [ , ]. In conclusion, the pro-cancer effects of USP1 and USP5 suggest that they could be potential targets for DNA damage-based therapies, and inhibitors developed to target USP1 and USP5 could theoretically increase the sensitivity of colorectal cancer patients with overexpression of these USPs to chemotherapy.

## Immune checkpoint inhibitors
PD-L1 is a protein found on tumor cell membranes, while PD-1 is a receptor expressed on immune cells. The interaction between PD-L1 and PD-1 suppresses immune cell activation, thereby promoting immune evasion by tumors [ ]. Currently, therapeutic approaches aimed at the PD-1/PD-L1 pathway have demonstrated encouraging outcomes in treating colorectal cancer individuals with microsatellite instability (MSI-H) or deficient mismatch repair (dMMR). Studies have suggested that USP21 expression can influence the response of colorectal cancer to anti-PD-1/PD-L1 therapies. In research led by Haihang Nie and colleagues, an analysis of immune checkpoint profiles revealed a notable negative relationship between USP21 levels and PD-L1 expression. Subsequent validation in patient cohorts receiving various treatment regimens confirmed that anti-PD-1/PD-L1 therapy exhibited greater efficacy in patients with low USP21 expression compared to those with higher USP21 expression [ ]. Therefore, based on the current body of research, we suggest that USP21 may represent a potential target for anti-PD-1/PD-L1 treatment.

 

## Regulatory mechanisms involved by USPs in colorectal cancer
USPs have emerged as important regulators of various signaling pathways involved in the malignant progression of CRC. Aberrant expression or activity of specific USPs can disrupt these signaling pathways, leading to uncontrolled cell growth, invasion, metastasis, and resistance to therapy. In this section, we will discuss the roles of specific USPs in regulating key signaling pathways involved in CRC development.

## Wnt signaling pathway
The Wnt signaling pathway plays a critical role in regulating cell proliferation, differentiation, migration, and tissue development, thereby maintaining cellular homeostasis. In malignant tumors, abnormal activation of the Wnt pathway can drive tumor growth, invasion, metastasis, and the maintenance of tumor stemness, while also affecting changes in the tumor microenvironment. Research has shown that the onset and progression of most colorectal cancer lesions are associated with the Wnt pathway, with APC mutations being the most common. Currently, several members of the USP family have been found to participate in regulating the activation of the Wnt signaling pathway, with USP7 being more extensively studied compared to other USPs. β-catenin is a key transcription factor in the Wnt pathway, and USP7 can directly interact with the β-TrCP binding motif on the N-terminus of β-catenin, maintaining the stability of β-catenin expression through deubiquitination [ – ]. Additionally, USP7 can indirectly regulate the expression of β-catenin. Studies have shown that LRRC42 and DDX3X influence β-catenin expression, and USP7 indirectly regulates β-catenin protein stability by inhibiting the ubiquitin-specific proteasomal degradation of LRRC42 and DDX3X [ , ]. In summary, the direct and indirect regulatory effects of USP7 on β-catenin demonstrate the diversity of its regulation. It is also important to note that USP7 expression is regulated by upstream molecules. Zhu et al. found that the N-terminal of ZMIZ2 can directly bind to the MATH domain of USP7, and through influencing USP7 protein expression, ZMIZ2 indirectly regulates USP7 downstream signaling pathways [ ]. In addition to USP7, USP4, USP6NL, USP25, and USP39 have also been shown to regulate the expression of β-catenin. Among them, USP4 has a unique regulatory effect on β-catenin. Yun et al. discovered that USP4 not only removes the K48-linked ubiquitin chain from β-catenin via its own C311 catalytic site, preventing β-catenin from being degraded by the proteasome, but also functions as a nuclear localization factor for β-catenin, promoting its transport from the cytoplasm to the nucleus, thereby enhancing its transcriptional activity [ ], reflecting the multifunctional regulatory role of USP4 (as shown in Fig. ).
In conclusion, the deubiquitination activity of USPs is crucial for maintaining β-catenin protein levels and activating the Wnt signaling pathway. However, current research focuses mainly on a few USP members, such as USP7 and USP4. Further studies are needed to explore the regulatory mechanisms of other USP family members on β-catenin protein.

## PI3K/Akt/mTOR signaling pathway
The PI3K signaling cascade is indispensable for maintaining cellular homeostasis, orchestrating cell survival, proliferation, and metabolic processes. Dysregulation of this pathway is a hallmark of many malignancies. Ubiquitin-specific proteases (USPs) exert multifaceted regulatory roles within the PI3K/Akt/mTOR axis. For instance, silencing USP22 in HCT116 cells led to a marked reduction in Akt phosphorylation at Ser473 and Thr308, as evidenced by Western blot analysis, suggesting that USP22 may potentiate Akt pathway activation [ ]. Although the precise substrate of USP22 in this context remains to be fully elucidated, it is conceivable that USP22 stabilizes a positive regulator of Akt, such as PDK1, via deubiquitination, thereby facilitating Akt phosphorylation at Thr308. This hypothesis warrants further investigation through techniques such as co-immunoprecipitation and ubiquitination assays.
Conversely, Kubaichuk et al. reported that silencing USP10 expression resulted in elevated phosphorylation of mTOR and its downstream effector, S6 kinase [ ], implying that USP10 may suppress Akt pathway activation. In this scenario, USP10 may directly deubiquitinate and stabilize PTEN, a phosphatase that antagonizes PI3K signaling by dephosphorylating PIP3. By augmenting PTEN levels, USP10 would dampen PI3K/Akt/mTOR signaling. This regulatory mechanism could be further validated by examining the interaction between USP10 and PTEN, as well as the ubiquitination status of PTEN, upon USP10 knockdown.
PLOD2, an enzyme involved in collagen biosynthesis, catalyzes the hydroxylation of lysine residues within the endoplasmic reticulum, facilitating collagen cross-linking. PLOD2 overexpression is frequently observed in colorectal cancer [ ]. Lan et al. demonstrated that USP15 interacts with PLOD2 via immunoprecipitation. Subsequent Western blot experiments revealed that PLOD2 upregulation diminished USP15 ubiquitination while augmenting Akt and mTOR phosphorylation [ ], suggesting that the PLOD2/USP15 axis modulates PI3K/Akt/mTOR pathway activation. The precise mechanism by which PLOD2 influences USP15 ubiquitination remains unclear, but it is conceivable that PLOD2 modifies USP15, reducing its activity and leading to increased ubiquitination and degradation of a negative regulator of the PI3K/Akt/mTOR pathway. Further studies are needed to identify the specific E3 ligase responsible for USP15 ubiquitination and the nature of the modification induced by PLOD2.
VCP, an ATP-dependent disaggregase, governs diverse biological processes, including protein degradation and autophagy [ ]. Sun et al. discovered that USP11 indirectly influences the PI3K/Akt/mTOR pathway by stabilizing VCP protein expression, thereby promoting autophagy in colorectal cancer cells [ ] (as shown in Fig. ). USP11 likely deubiquitinates VCP, preventing its degradation and ensuring its proper function in autophagy. The interplay between VCP-mediated autophagy and the PI3K/Akt/mTOR pathway is intricate, but autophagy can often serve as a negative feedback mechanism to constrain PI3K/Akt/mTOR signaling. Future research could explore the specific lysine residues on VCP that are targeted by USP11 and the impact of USP11 on VCP’s ATPase activity.
In summary, a growing body of evidence indicates that USPs play a significant role in regulating the PI3K/Akt/mTOR signaling pathway. While the majority of these USPs appear to promote PI3K pathway activation, a subset, such as USP10, exerts an inhibitory effect, underscoring the complexity of USP-mediated regulation of PI3K signaling. Furthermore, it is important to acknowledge that many studies on USPs remain at the phenotypic level, and future research is warranted to elucidate their regulatory mechanisms. Future investigations should prioritize identifying the specific substrates of these USPs and the lysine residues targeted for deubiquitination, employing techniques such as quantitative proteomics, ubiquitination site mapping, and CRISPR-based genetic screens. A comprehensive understanding of these molecular interactions is crucial for developing targeted therapies that modulate PI3K/Akt/mTOR signaling in colorectal cancer.

 

## MAPK pathway
The MAPK signaling cascade is a pivotal intracellular pathway that governs cell growth, proliferation, differentiation, migration, and a plethora of other biological processes. Aberrant activation of this pathway is frequently observed in malignancies, fostering tumor cell growth and proliferation. ZEB1, a key transcription factor in the epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) process, promotes tumor cell invasion and migration [ ]. Sun et al. reported that, unlike most USP family members, USP10 removes K27-linked polyubiquitin chains from substrates rather than K48 chains, leading to accelerated substrate degradation. Upon ERK activation within the MAPK pathway, USP10 is phosphorylated at S236, which diminishes its binding affinity for the downstream target protein ZEB1, thereby maintaining ZEB1 protein stability [ ]. This represents a unique instance of USP10 promoting the degradation of a tumor suppressor, rather than stabilizing an oncogene, highlighting the context-dependent nature of USP function.
In conclusion, the identification of the ERK-USP10-ZEB1 signaling axis suggests that USP10 is a key regulatory node downstream of the MAPK signaling pathway. By modulating ZEB1 expression, USP10 indirectly governs malignant biological behaviors of tumor cells, such as migration, invasion, and metastasis (as shown in Fig.  ). While the study focuses on ZEB1, it is conceivable that USP10 also regulates the ubiquitination and stability of other key components of the MAPK pathway, such as Ras, Raf, MEK, and ERK. Further research is needed to explore these possibilities, potentially through the use of CRISPR-based screening approaches to identify novel USP10 substrates within the MAPK pathway. Furthermore, investigating the role of USP10 phosphorylation in regulating its activity and substrate specificity would provide valuable insights into the dynamic regulation of MAPK signaling.

## Hippo pathway
The Hippo signaling pathway plays a dominant role in regulating cell proliferation and apoptosis, serving a vital function in preserving cellular homeostasis. YAP is a key transcriptional co-activator within the Hippo pathway. Upon activation, YAP translocates to the nucleus and activates genes involved in cell proliferation, promoting cell growth [ ]. Pan and colleagues demonstrated that USP47 and YAP protein levels are significantly elevated in colorectal cancer. USP47 interacts with YAP, facilitating its deubiquitination and thereby stabilizing YAP. This stabilization further contributes to colorectal cancer progression [ ]. Specifically, USP47 removes ubiquitin chains from YAP at lysine residues K497 and K513, preventing its degradation and promoting its nuclear translocation, as confirmed by site-directed mutagenesis studies. These findings suggest that targeting USP47 could be a promising therapeutic strategy for inhibiting YAP activity in colorectal cancer. The modulatory impact of USP47 on YAP implies its potential to indirectly influence the malignant progression of tumors by governing the abnormal activation of the Hippo signaling cascade (as shown in Fig. ).

## P53
P53 is central to governing the cell cycle, cellular senescence, apoptosis, and DNA damage repair, enabling cells to counteract genomic mutations and the effects of DNA damage. However, mutations and inactivation of p53 regulatory factors in malignancies result in a loss of tumor-suppressive function [ , ]. It has been reported that RBM28 regulates the transcriptional activity of p53. Xu et al. found that USP36 can bind to RBM28, removing K48-linked ubiquitin chains from its K162 residue, and indirectly regulating p53 expression [ ]. By stabilizing RBM28, USP36 promotes its interaction with p53 mRNA, enhancing p53 translation, as demonstrated by ribosome profiling experiments. This suggests that USP36 plays a critical role in maintaining p53 protein levels and function in colorectal cancer cells.
Additionally, USP7 also regulates p53 expression. Yang et al. found that the upstream molecule STAT3 recruits HDAC1 to occupy the USP7 promoter region, affecting USP7’s regulation of p53 [ ]. Furthermore, similar to STAT3, MLF2 can also influence p53 protein expression by inhibiting the binding between USP7 and p53 [ ]. In conclusion, current research has shown that multiple upstream target molecules of USP7 can indirectly regulate p53 expression through USP7. However, the specific mechanisms by which USP7 regulates p53 remain unclear. It is plausible that USP7 directly deubiquitinates p53 at lysine residue K370, preventing its interaction with MDM2 and subsequent degradation, a hypothesis that could be tested using ubiquitination assays and mass spectrometry analysis of p53 ubiquitination sites.
Notably, USP39 can also regulate p53 expression, but unlike USP7 and USP36, USP39’s regulatory effect does not alter the ubiquitination levels of p53 or p21 [ , ].
In previous studies, researchers have demonstrated that USP39 is involved in the regulation of pre-mRNA splicing of specific genes, in collaboration with splicing factors such as the U4/U6.U5 tri-snRNP complex and the SF3b complex, which affects splicing efficiency. Similarly, the regulatory effect of USP39 on p53 may occur at the post-transcriptional modification stage rather than at the post-translational modification stage (as shown in Fig.  ). This suggests that USP39 may influence the splicing of p53 mRNA, leading to the generation of different p53 isoforms with altered functions. Future research should focus on identifying the specific p53 isoforms regulated by USP39 and their functional effects in colorectal cancer cells.

 

## Potential of USPs as early screening and prognostic indicators in clinical practice
CEA and CA19-9 are currently the most common serum biomarkers used for early screening and monitoring prognosis in colorectal cancer. Although they are widely used in clinical practice, their actual effectiveness is limited. CEA lacks sufficient specificity and sensitivity, while CA19-9, although somewhat accurate in monitoring the progression and recurrence of colorectal cancer, is not ideal for early-stage screening, lacking adequate specificity. An ideal colorectal cancer biomarker should have high sensitivity and specificity to assist doctors in better monitoring tumor development and providing more rational treatment options. Previous studies have shown that USP44 is downregulated in colorectal cancer, although the specific reason for this downregulation remains unclear. In recent years, with the deepening of mechanistic research, Mathew A. Sloane and colleagues discovered that the fundamental cause of the downregulation of USP44 expression in colorectal adenomas is due to the hypermethylation of the CpG island in the USP44 promoter. At the beginning of the study, the researchers first selected 18 colorectal cancer cell lines for analysis and found that in all these cell lines, the USP44 promoter was fully methylated. In contrast, no methylation of USP44 was observed in normal colon cells. Subsequently, the researchers further selected 89 cases of colorectal adenoma tissues for analysis. The results showed that 89% (79/89) of the colorectal adenoma tissues had a hypermethylated USP44 promoter, and this methylation was independent of the adenoma location. In normal colon mucosa, only 6% (3/51) of the tissues exhibited hypermethylation of USP44 [ ].This suggests that the hypermethylation of the USP44 promoter is correlated with the occurrence of colorectal adenomas, providing certain insights for the early screening of colorectal cancer. However, it is important to note that although the screening of USP44 promoter methylation in colorectal tissues has high sensitivity, the sample size in the current experiment is small, and the data support is insufficient. Therefore, it is recommended to increase the sample size in future studies to ensure the accuracy of the experiment.
In conclusion, based on existing research, we believe that multi-USP combined detection for early screening of colorectal cancer outperforms traditional tumor marker screenings such as CEA and CA19-9. Firstly, multi-USP combined detection offers higher sensitivity, as the expression of several USPs in colorectal cancer cells and tissues shows significant differences compared to normal tissues (e.g., USP44 and USP38). Secondly, this combined detection also demonstrates high specificity. In contrast to CEA, which is widely present in various tumors and normal tissues, multi-USP combined detection can effectively distinguish colorectal cancer from other malignancies, thereby reducing the likelihood of misdiagnosis. Additionally, multi-USP combined detection offers functional diversity, as it can not only be used for early tumor screening but also assess tumor sensitivity to drugs and predict poor prognosis in tumors.
Evaluating the prognosis of tumor patients through the detection of relevant biomarkers is a current research hotspot. Based on current studies, we find that most USPs are abnormally overexpressed in colorectal cancer, including USP1 [ ], USP4 [ ], USP5 [ ], USP6 [ ], USP7 [ , ], USP10 [ , ], USP11 [ , ], USP14 [ ], USP15 [ , ], USP21 [ , , ], USP22 [ ], USP26 [ ], USP29 [ , ], USP35 [ ], USP36 [ , , ], USP39 [ , ], USP47 [ ], USP51 [ ], and USP54 [ ]. Additionally, only a few USPs are downregulated in colorectal cancer tissues, including USP3 [ ], USP33 [ ], USP38 [ , ], and USP44 [ ] (as shown in Table ). In clinical pathology-related studies, USPs associated with clinical tumor staging include USP3, USP5, USP6, USP10, USP11, USP14, USP15, USP21, USP22, USP29, USP33, USP39, and USP47. Among them, USP29, USP39, and USP47 are related to tumor size. USP10, USP11, USP14, USP15, USP22, USP29, USP33, and USP47 are associated with lymph node metastasis. USP3, USP10, USP11, USP14, and USP15 are related to distant metastasis. USP6, USP11, USP15, USP22, and USP51 are associated with pathological staging. USP22 is related to the degree of differentiation, and USP11 and USP15 are related to the degree of invasion. USP51 is associated with tumor microvascular density. Moreover, USP6 and USP22 are not only related to pathological staging but also to AJCC staging. In prognostic-related studies, USP1, USP3, USP4, USP6, USP7, USP10, USP11, USP15, USP21, USP22, USP26, USP36, USP47, USP51, and USP39 are closely related to survival outcome indicators such as OS, RFS, DFI, and DFS. Except for USP3, which is negatively correlated with shorter DFS and OS, the rest of the USPs show a positive correlation. For example, Lixiong Luo et al. found that through the analysis of RNA-seq data from the TCGA-COAD cohort combined with Kaplan-Meier survival analysis, high expression of USP36 was associated with poorer OS and DFI, and patients with elevated USP36 expression had worse prognoses [ ]. In summary, these studies have shown that USP expression is closely associated with the clinical and pathological characteristics of colorectal cancer. By detecting the expression levels of different USPs in tumor tissues, we can provide predictions for the subsequent progression of the tumor.

 

## Recent advances in USP inhibitor research
In the research on targeted therapy for colorectal cancer, several USP inhibitors have been developed, although they are still in the preclinical stage and have not yet entered clinical studies. P5091 is a small molecule compound that was initially considered an inhibitor of HDAC3. In recent years, researchers have discovered that it can also suppress the deubiquitination activity of USP7. After binding with β-catenin, USP7 increases its protein stability, thereby promoting the activation of the Wnt signaling pathway. Nevertheless, when P5091 binds with USP7, the activation of this signaling axis is suppressed [ ]. PTL is another USP7 inhibitor. Xue Li et al. found that in PTL-treated HCT 116 and SW 480 cell lines, most tumor cells were blocked in the G2/M phase, indicating that the inhibitory effect of this compound was effective. In subsequent mechanistic studies, researchers discovered that PTL, after binding to USP7, modifies several amino acid residues of USP7, significantly inhibiting USP7’s deubiquitination of β-catenin, thus indirectly affecting the activation of the Wnt pathway [ ]. N-benzylpiperidinol derivatives are optimized products of the known USP7 inhibitor L55. Xing Li et al. found that X36, one of these derivatives, exhibited comparable potency to L55.Upon binding with USP7, X36 not only inhibited the immunosuppressive function of Treg and MDSCs but also enhanced the infiltration capacity of NK, NKT, and CD8+ T cells into the tumor, effectively boosting the anti-tumor immune response [ ].
Similar to USP7, USP4 can also indirectly affect the progression of colorectal cancer through the regulation of β-catenin stability in the Wnt pathway. Neutral red (NR) is a non-competitive inhibitor of USP4. Hoa Hong Nguyen et al. found that NR strongly inhibits USP4’s catalytic activity and effectively promotes β-catenin protein degradation upon binding with USP4. Both in vitro and animal studies have demonstrated that NR effectively suppresses tumor cell proliferation and tumor growth.
Moreover, when NR was combined with 5-FU, the tumor-suppressive effect was enhanced in a dose-dependent manner [ ]. U4-I05 is a small-molecule natural compound, and Fengtian Li et al. found that U4-I05 occupies the cysteine residue at position 311 of USP4, inhibiting USP4’s deubiquitination activity of β-catenin and Twist 1 proteins, thus preventing the activation of the Wnt signaling pathway. Furthermore, in tumor xenograft mouse models, researchers found that U4-I05 could increase the tumor’s sensitivity to oxaliplatin [ ], suggesting its potential to help patients overcome chemotherapy resistance. Curcumin is a diaryl heptanoid compound that has demonstrated inhibitory activity in a range of malignancies. Hai Wei et al. found that curcumin weakens USP4’s positive regulatory effect on the expression of LAMP3, thereby inhibiting the proliferation and migration of colorectal cancer cells and enhancing apoptosis [ ].
HMGA2, as a transcription factor, has been proven to promote carcinogenesis in colorectal cancer, breast cancer, lung cancer, and other malignancies. USP48 prevents the degradation of HMGA2 by the proteasome by removing K6 and K48-linked polyubiquitin chains on the K26 residue of HMGA2. Can Cheng et al. found that through small molecule phenotypic screening, DUB-IN-2 can inhibit USP48’s deubiquitination of HMGA2 [ ]. Cinobufotalin (CBF), a steroidal compound, has been shown to specifically bind to the catalytic domain of USP36. Yongjun Hu et al. found that CBF inhibits USP36-mediated deubiquitination of c-MYC, resulting in reduced c-MYC protein stability, and effectively suppresses colorectal cancer cell proliferation, migration, invasion, chemotherapy resistance, and stemness. USP1 has been shown to play a role in DNA repair mechanisms and cell cycle regulation. Xin Xu et al. found that ML323, a USP1 inhibitor, can enhance the cytotoxic effects of DNA-damaging agents, such as doxorubicin, on HCT 116 and SW 480 cells, effectively inhibiting tumor cell DNA repair and anti-apoptotic abilities [ ]. In addition, like ML323, ML364 is also a USP1 inhibitor. However, ML364 not only affects USP1 expression but also inhibits the expression of USP3, blocking its further regulation of cyclin D1, which leads to the accumulation of tumor cells in the G0/G1 phase, inhibiting proliferation, and increasing apoptosis [ ]. It is noteworthy that psoralen (PTL) not only inhibits USP7 but also affects the deubiquitination activity of USP47. A study by Shaohua Zhang et al. found that the growth of most tumor cells was inhibited when PTL was added to colorectal cancer cell lines overexpressing USP47 [ ]. The broad inhibitory effect of PTL on USP family proteins suggests its potential as an anticancer drug for colorectal cancer. However, this multi-target inhibition by PTL may carry the risk of off-target effects during its antitumor activity. Firstly, while PTL inhibits the expression of USP47 and USP7, it may also affect the expression of other USP family members, potentially disrupting biological mechanisms such as the cell cycle, DNA repair, and apoptosis, which could reduce therapeutic efficacy or even increase drug toxicity. Secondly, PTL may interact with other non-USP protein targets, which could interfere with the normal proliferation, apoptosis, and other biological processes in healthy cells. Therefore, the common issues associated with multi-USP small molecule inhibitors like PTL need to be addressed in future research. Potential solutions include, but are not limited to, redesigning the compounds, optimizing drug dosages, and developing multi-drug combination strategies.
Moreover, it is worth noting that most of the currently developed USP inhibitors are small molecule inhibitors, which have significant limitations in their application range, whether targeting the catalytic domain or the non-catalytic domain. Small molecule inhibitors require the target protein to have a catalytic active site and cannot act on proteins that lack enzymatic activity. Furthermore, due to the reversible binding nature of small molecule inhibitors, the duration of their effect on the target protein is relatively short [ ]. Interestingly, the PROTAC (Proteolysis-Targeting Chimera) technology has effectively addressed these challenges. The compounds designed using this technology can recognize the majority of protein molecules. By simultaneously binding the target protein and an E3 ligase, the compound can induce the ubiquitin-proteasome system to degrade the ubiquitin-labeled target protein. Additionally, unlike the Target Occupancy Effect of small molecule inhibitors, the Catalytic Degradation Mechanism of PROTACs allows for a more sustained binding to the target protein [ , ]. Several USP inhibitors based on PROTAC design have been developed, such as the USP7 inhibitor PU7-1, which has demonstrated good inhibitory effects on USP7 [ ]. Therefore, by screening for USP-related ligands (or ligands of USP downstream target proteins) and E3 ligase ligands, and designing and optimizing linkers, it is theoretically possible to form an effective PROTAC that inhibits the expression of specific USPs by coupling the three components.
In summary, although various USPs inhibitors currently under development have shown good tumor-suppressing effects in preclinical stages, traditional small molecule inhibitors still have certain limitations in clinical treatment. Therefore, based on the latest research in recent years, we propose the concept of developing USP inhibitors using PROTACs technology. In the future, we will gradually refine related experiments based on this theory with the aim of developing ideal USP inhibitors.

## Discussion and prospect
Based on current research, we have found that, compared to normal tissues, most USPs are either overexpressed or downregulated in colorectal cancer tissues. This suggests that USPs may have the potential to serve as biological diagnostic biomarkers for colorectal cancer. Based on the regulatory functions and biological characteristics of different members of the USP family, we can classify the entire USP family into three groups: oncogene, tumor suppressor gene, and dual-function gene. After immunohistochemistry (IHC) on tissues obtained from colonoscopy biopsies, we can analyze the protein expression levels of different USPs in the tissue samples by referencing the aforementioned groups. This analysis can not only predict the occurrence of malignancies but also provide clinical guidance for selecting appropriate targeted drugs. For example, elevated expression of USP47, which stabilizes YAP, could indicate that targeting the Hippo pathway might be a beneficial therapeutic strategy for a specific patient. Conversely, decreased expression of USP44, a tumor suppressor, might suggest a higher risk of disease progression and the need for more aggressive treatment. However, the actual operation of IHC is relatively complex, and its sensitivity is typically low. Therefore, its efficiency in large-scale tumor screening and diagnosis is noticeably inferior to that of serological tests.
Consequently, we believe that serological detection of USP gene and protein expression levels may be more advantageous in future studies. It is noteworthy that, to date, only Mathew A. Sloane and colleagues have discovered that monitoring the methylation level of USP44 in serum can effectively assess the incidence of colorectal cancer. Their innovative experimental results provide new ideas for further research. In the future, we can assess the likelihood of tumor occurrence by analyzing the gene and protein expression levels of USPs in serum, building upon immunohistochemical studies. This could involve developing highly sensitive and specific ELISA assays to quantify the levels of specific USPs or their substrates in serum samples, providing a more convenient and cost-effective approach for large-scale screening.
Moreover, the role of USPs in monitoring tumor malignant progression is also worth our attention. By analyzing existing studies, we have summarized the correlation data between USPs and OS and RFS in colorectal cancer patients. The analysis results show that most overexpressed USPs are associated with better OS and RFS, with only a few USPs, such as USP3, being associated with poor OS and RFS. This indicates that most USPs play a positive regulatory role in the prognosis of colorectal cancer patients. However, it is crucial to acknowledge that the specific substrates and downstream effects of these USPs may vary depending on the context and genetic background of the tumor, leading to complex and sometimes contradictory effects on patient outcomes. By evaluating the expression levels of USPs in serum, we can theoretically predict the tumor prognosis of patients and offer new strategies for personalized treatment. For instance, monitoring the levels of USP15 and its substrate PLOD2 could provide insights into the activation status of the PI3K/Akt/mTOR pathway and potential resistance to targeted therapies, allowing clinicians to tailor treatment strategies based on individual patient profiles.
In conclusion, USPs have the potential to become indicators for early screening and prognosis monitoring of colorectal cancer. Future research can validate their reliability by increasing sample sizes and further investigating their regulatory mechanisms. This includes conducting prospective clinical trials to assess the predictive value of USP expression levels in serum and tumor tissues, as well as developing standardized assays for USP detection to ensure reproducibility and comparability across different studies.
In colorectal cancer cell lines and animal models, multiple USP inhibitors have shown inhibitory effects on tumor malignant progression and, to some extent, increased colorectal cancer cell sensitivity to anti-cancer drugs. However, while current research has demonstrated the promising anti-tumor effects of USP inhibitors, there remain many challenges in several aspects. Issues such as the sensitivity and specificity of USP inhibitors, drug toxicity, the possibility of clinical application, and potential off-target effects need to be further addressed through the exploration of the regulatory mechanisms of relevant USPs. This includes identifying the specific substrates of each USP and understanding how their deubiquitination affects downstream signaling pathways, as well as developing strategies to improve the selectivity and bioavailability of USP inhibitors.
In summary, USPs are involved in regulating multiple signaling pathways and play a significant role in the malignant progression of colorectal cancer. We found that the expression levels of different USPs in colorectal cancer tissues can reflect tumor progression and prognosis to some extent. By detecting DNA, RNA, and protein expression levels in blood, we can provide guidance for clinical screening and prognostic detection. Furthermore, inhibitors designed based on the functional properties of USPs have proven effective in suppressing the biological activities of colorectal cancer and enhancing cellular responsiveness to chemotherapy and other anticancer agents. This provides new insights for personalized treatment plans for colorectal cancer. Future research should focus more on exploring the regulatory mechanisms of USPs and further evaluate the efficacy and safety of USP inhibitors through clinical trials. This requires a multi-faceted approach, including identifying novel USP substrates, developing highly selective inhibitors, conducting preclinical studies to assess their efficacy and toxicity in vivo, and ultimately translating these findings into clinical trials to evaluate their therapeutic potential in colorectal cancer patients. The development of PROTAC-based USP degraders represents a promising avenue for future research, offering the potential to achieve more potent and selective inhibition of USP function.
