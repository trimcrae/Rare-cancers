## Background
Colorectal cancer (CRC) is a leading cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide, with the tumor microenvironment (TME) playing a pivotal role in its progression and therapeutic resistance. The ubiquitin–proteasome system (UPS), a central regulator of intracellular protein degradation, is increasingly recognized for its involvement in cancer pathogenesis, though its specific role in modulating the CRC TME remains to be fully elucidated. This review aims to systematically summarize current evidence on how the UPS influences the immunosuppressive network within the CRC TME and to evaluate its potential as a therapeutic target.

## Methods
We conducted a comprehensive literature search in PubMed, Web of Science, and Scopus databases for original research articles and reviews published between January 2010 and August 2025, using keywords including “ubiquitin–proteasome system,” “colorectal cancer,” “tumor microenvironment,”“immune escape,”and “targeted therapy.” Studies were selected based on their relevance to UPS-mediated regulatory mechanisms in CRC TME remodeling, immune cell function, and treatment response.

## Results
Our analysis of preclinical and clinical evidence reveals that the UPS critically regulates immune evasion in CRC through multiple mechanisms: (1) USP14 stabilizes indoleamine 2,3-dioxygenase 1 (IDO1), enhancing tryptophan catabolism and kynurenine accumulation, which suppresses T-cell activity; (2) E3 ligases including SPOP, C-Cbl, KLHL22, and FBW7 modulate PD-L1/PD-1 protein stability via ubiquitination, thereby influencing immune checkpoint signaling; and (3) ZFP91 facilitates K63-linked ubiquitination of PP2Ac, impairing mTORC1-mediated glycolysis in T cells and reinforcing regulatory T-cell immunosuppression. Additionally, the UPS intersects with key oncogenic pathways such as Wnt/β-catenin, NF-κB, and p53, further shaping the immunosuppressive landscape of CRC.

## Conclusions
Targeting the UPS represents a promising strategy to reverse immunosuppression and overcome therapy resistance in CRC. The primary advantage of this approach lies in its ability to simultaneously disrupt multiple immunosuppressive pathways within the TME, offering a potential solution to the limitations of single-target therapies. Current approaches include proteasome inhibitors, E3 ligase modulators, and deubiquitinating enzyme inhibitors, with combination regimens—such as UPS inhibitors with immune checkpoint blockade—showing synergistic efficacy in preclinical models. Future efforts should focus on enhancing the selectivity of UPS-targeting agents, minimizing off-target effects, and integrating genomic profiling to guide personalized treatment. While current evidence strongly supports the therapeutic potential of UPS targeting, its establishment as a reliable alternative therapy in the clinic will depend on overcoming these challenges and validating efficacy in human trials. This review underscores the UPS as a central regulator of the CRC TME and provides a rational basis for novel therapeutic development.

## Introduction

## Colorectal cancer
Colorectal cancer (CRC) is one of the most prevalent and lethal malignancies globally, with an estimated 1.93 million new cases and nearly 900,000 deaths reported annually, making it the third most commonly diagnosed cancer worldwide [ , ]. Although incidence rates have declined in many western countries due to improved screening programs and lifestyle modifications, they continue to rise steadily in developing nations, particularly Asia. Notably, early-onset CRC among younger populations has shown a markedly increasing trend in recent years [ ]. In addition to genetic predisposition, various metabolic and inflammatory conditions—including obesity, diabetes, nonalcoholic fatty liver disease, dyslipidemia, hypertension, and inflammatory bowel disease—have been identified as major risk factors. These conditions are thought to contribute to CRC onset through mechanisms involving insulin resistance, chronic inflammation, and immune dysregulation [ ]. Accumulating evidence underscores the pivotal role of the tumor microenvironment (TME) in CRC pathogenesis, where it influences tumor progression, metastasis, and immune escape. The CRC TME constitutes a complex and dynamic niche enriched with immunosuppressive cells, including regulatory T cells (Tregs), M2 macrophages, and myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs), which collectively impair antitumor immunity [ , ]. Surgical resection remains the primary treatment for CRC, particularly in early-stage disease, and minimally invasive techniques are increasingly being adopted for their favorable postoperative outcomes [ ]. However, advanced CRC and metastatic CRC (mCRC) require systemic therapies, including chemotherapy, targeted therapies (e.g., antiepidermal growth factor receptor [EGFR], antivascular endothelial growth factor [VEGF], BRAF inhibitors) [ , ], and immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs). Although ICIs have transformed cancer treatment, their efficacy in CRC is largely restricted to a small subset of patients with mismatch repair–deficient (dMMR) or microsatellite instability–high (MSI-H) tumors, which account for approximately 15% of cases. Most CRCs, particularly microsatellite stable (MSS) tumors, exhibit poor immunogenicity and resistance to ICIs, primarily due to the immunosuppressive characteristics of the TME [ ]. Moreover, biomarkers such as programed death ligand 1 (PD-L1) and CD73, together with the accumulation of adenosine and the presence of inhibitory myeloid populations, contribute to immune escape and treatment resistance [ ]. This stark limitation has galvanized the research community to delve deeper into the TME, identifying and targeting its non-redundant molecular regulators as a paramount strategy to reverse immunosuppression and expand the reach of immunotherapy.
The ubiquitin-proteasome system (UPS) has emerged as a master regulator of cellular homeostasis and a critical player in cancer. Its dysregulation is a hallmark of many cancers. Extensive research has been conducted on the dysregulation of UPS in cancer, gradually uncovering its significant role in the occurrence and development of malignant tumors and the shaping of the tumor microenvironment (TME). Studies have shown that specific ubiquitinization enzyme components, such as the deubiquitinating enzyme USP14, can stabilize key immune regulatory proteins, such as IDO1, thereby directly influencing the metabolic profile of the TME and promoting immune escape [ ]. E3 ubiquitin ligases, such as SPOP and FBW7, have been demonstrated to dynamically regulate the stability of immune checkpoints (such as PD-L1 and PD-1), directly affecting the efficacy of immunotherapy [ , ]. Therefore, the targeted development of inhibitors for components of the UPS system (such as E3 ligase inhibitors and deubiquitinating enzyme inhibitors) holds great potential in inhibiting the growth of cancer cells, reversing the immunosuppressive microenvironment of tumors, and enhancing the efficacy of immunotherapy. Currently, a large number of drugs targeting the UPS are in the research stage, with only a few in clinical use. The lack of specificity of drugs is a significant factor limiting their development. Recent cutting-edge research has provided new ideas for the development of UPS-targeted drugs for the TME. For instance, Rosic et al. elaborated on the key role of nanobiomaterials in cancer signaling and treatment, systematically introduced the composition and principles of nanodrug delivery systems, and emphasized the importance of nanobiomaterials in the precise treatment of cancer [ ]. Additionally, Dogan et al. revealed the potential of biogenic silver nanoparticles in cancer treatment, highlighting the broader context of new nanobiomaterials in regulating cancer signaling [ ]. These studies suggest that in the precise treatment of cancer, linking advanced materials science with potential molecular mechanisms through nanobiomaterials for targeted drug delivery can precisely strike the TME while minimizing drug side effects. These pioneering studies not only highlight the potential of nanobiomaterials in cancer therapy but also provide a compelling delivery strategy for future UPS-targeted agents. The integration of such advanced delivery systems could be pivotal in overcoming the specificity issues that currently plague UPS drug development, thereby directly informing the therapeutic approaches discussed in this review. In addition, in the recent cutting-edge research on natural products, the extract of Elaeagnus angustifolia leaves has demonstrated broad-spectrum anti-cancer and anti-bacterial activities [ ]; green tea-synthesized selenium nanoparticles have achieved multiple enzyme inhibition [ ]; and chitosan-based composite films containing the extract of Crataegus orientalis (CR) have confirmed their synergistic enhancement effects [ ]. These achievements provide promising new ideas for the development of natural, multi-functional UPS-targeted drugs.
Currently, there is a lack of comprehensive and systematic research on how the ubiquitin cycle system regulates the immunosuppressive network in the tumor microenvironment (TME) of colorectal cancer (CRC). Many studies have focused on individual components, leading to an insufficient overall perspective. This gap is crucial, and thus, this review focuses on the intricate interplay between the UPS and the CRC TME. We will systematically delineate the mechanisms by which specific UPS components—including deubiquitinating enzymes like USP14 and a repertoire of E3 ubiquitin ligases—reshape the TME by modulating immune cell function, metabolic pathways, and critical signaling axes (e.g., Wnt/β-catenin, NF-κB, p53). Furthermore, we will synthesize current knowledge on UPS-targeting therapeutic strategies, such as proteasome inhibitors, molecular glues, and PROTACs, and explore their potential in combination therapies to reinvigorate the antitumor immune response and overcome resistance in CRC.

## Ubiquitin–proteasome system

## Basic concepts of the ubiquitin–proteasome system
The ubiquitin–proteasome system (UPS) is a highly regulated protein degradation and signaling network that controls key cellular processes, including protein degradation, cell cycle, and signal transduction [ – ]. Ubiquitination of protein substrates plays a central role in modulating the activation of diverse intracellular signaling pathways [ ].

## Molecular mechanism
Ubiquitination is an ATP-dependent enzymatic cascade involving three classes of enzymes—ubiquitin-activating enzymes (E1), ubiquitin-conjugating enzymes (E2), ubiquitin ligases (E3)—that sequentially bind ubiquitin molecules to target substrates [ , ]. As shown in Fig. , the E1 enzyme activates ubiquitin in an ATP-dependent manner and transfers the activated ubiquitin to an E2 enzyme. The E2 enzyme, in cooperation with an E3 ligase, then facilitates covalent binding to specific substrate molecules [ ]. Within this cascade, E3 ligases determine substrate specificity, and the E3 family is highly diverse. In the classical ubiquitination pathway, an isopeptide bond is formed between the C-terminal glycine of the ubiquitin molecule and a lysine residue of the target protein. Conversely, nonclassical ubiquitination, which occurs less frequently, may involve the formation of ester or thioester bonds between the ubiquitin molecule and serine, threonine, or cysteine residues of the substrate protein [ – ]. Based on the number of ubiquitin molecules attached to the substrate protein of lysine residues, ubiquitination can be classified as monoubiquitination (the attachment of a single ubiquitin molecule) or polyubiquitination (the formation of a ubiquitin chain) [ ]. Multiubiquitination occurs when additional ubiquitin molecules covalently attach to one of eight specific amino acid residues on the ubiquitin molecule, thereby forming linear or branched chains. These potential ubiquitination sites include seven lysine residues (K6, K11, K27, K29, K33, K48, K63) and the N-terminal methionine [ , ]. The lysine binding sites of these multiubiquitin chains determine the fate of the substrate protein. For example, K48-linked multiubiquitin chains are most extensively studied and serve as one of the most effective signals for 26S proteasome–mediated degradation. Additionally, variations in the length of K48-linked chains may regulate intracellular signal transduction and transcription processes through different lengths of ubiquitin chains. K11-linked ubiquitination is also associated with proteolysis [ ], while K63-linked chains primarily regulate nonproteasome signal transduction and can mediate substrate degradation through the autophagy–lysosome pathway. These processes include endocytosis, DNA replication, and clearance of damaged organelles [ – ]. Other linkages, such as K6, K27, K29, and K33, generally act as nonproteolytic signals involved in intracellular regulation, most notably in DNA replication and cell signal transduction processes. Monoubiquitinated proteins, however, are widely recognized as regulatory signals in processes such as chromatin remodeling, protein classification, and intracellular transport [ , ]. 
Ubiquitination is a dynamic and reversible process mediated by deubiquitinating enzymes (DUBs). The covalent bond formed between the target protein and the ubiquitin chain can be hydrolyzed by DUBs, thereby preventing protein degradation, improving protein stability, and reversing functional changes induced by ubiquitination within cells. In addition to reversing ubiquitination, DUBs regulate diverse cellular pathways, such as protein transport, chromatin remodeling, cell-cycle progression, and apoptosis. Therefore, DUBs are critically implicated in the pathogenesis of various diseases, particularly cancer.

## Relationship between the ubiquitin–proteasome system and cancer
The UPS encompasses two dynamic processes—ubiquitination and deubiquitination of protein substrates. It functions as a post-translational modification mechanism that regulates the stability, localization, and activity of proteins within cells. Through its regulatory roles, the UPS is widely involved in cell signal transduction, immune responses, and cancer pathogenesis. In the context of tumor immunity, ubiquitination and deubiquitination significantly influence immune escape and the response to immunotherapy by regulating immune cell function, tumor antigen presentation, immune checkpoint molecule expression, and related pathways.

## Tumor microenvironment

## Definition and composition
TME is a dynamic ecosystem composed of tumor cells, stromal cells, immune cells, the extracellular matrix (ECM), the vascular and lymphatic network, neural elements, and various physical factors. Its cellular components include tumor cells, tumor-associated fibroblasts (CAFs), tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs), dendritic cells (DCs), lymphocytes, MDSC, type 2 polarized macrophages (M2), endothelial cells, mesenchymal cells, fibroblasts, and myeloid and lymphoid inflammatory cells. These cellular components undergo distinct changes at different stages of tumor cell progression [ – ]. The noncellular components of the TME include the ECM—comprising collagen, fibronectin, cytokines, lipid mediators, and growth factors—as well as metabolites such as lactic acid and adenosine, in addition to the hypoxic conditions.

## Significance
The TME plays a central role in tumorigenesis, proliferation, metastasis, drug resistance, and immune escape. For example, the immunosuppressive properties of the TME are a major reason for the failure of immunotherapy [ ]. Therefore, an in-depth study of the mechanisms of tumor–TME interactions and the identification of novel therapeutic targets are essential for improving various tumor therapies, particularly those aimed at enhancing host antitumor immune responses.

## Key characteristics
The TME is characterized by hypoxia, which results from the rapid proliferation of tumor cells and the presence of abnormal blood vasculature. Persistent hypoxia activates the intracellular hypoxia-inducible factor (HIF) signaling pathway, leading to the accumulation of HIF-2α and the induction of hundreds of hypoxia-responsive genes, including those involved in angiogenesis and the metabolic shift to anaerobic respiration. Angiogenesis further promotes tumor cell proliferation, invasion, and distant metastasis [ , ]. Simultaneously, hypoxia in tumor cells and the associated shift in metabolic pathways lead to excessive accumulation of the lactic acid in the TME, lowering the PH to approximately from 6.5 to 7.2. This acidic environment suppresses immune cell function by reducing the activity of CD8+ T cells, thereby weakening antitumor immunity and facilitating immune escape [ ]. Additionally, the abnormal proliferation of tumor cells disrupts fluid homeostasis among plasma, tissue fluid, and lymph fluid, resulting in elevated interstitial fluid pressure (IFP) within the TME. Driven by the IFP gradient, interstitial fluid flows from the TME into surrounding normal tissues, promoting the diffusion of growth factors and tumor cells into adjacent tissues and lymphatic vessels. At the same time, this pressure gradient reduces the penetration and retention time of vascular-targeted drugs within the TME and hinders immune cell infiltration, thus promoting tumor invasion and progression [ – ].

## Interaction between cells in the TME
Within the tumor microenvironment (TME), cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) critically modulate immune cell function to foster tumor progression. CAFs secrete factors such as TGF-β, IL-6, CXCL12, VEGF, and IDO, which collectively drive immunosuppression, ECM remodeling, and angiogenesis [ – ].
A key mechanism involves CAF-mediated polarization of tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) toward an M2 phenotype via TGF-β/Smad, STAT3, and NF-κB signaling [ , ]. M2 macrophages, in turn, secrete VEGF, TGF-β, and IL-10, promoting angiogenesis and immune suppression. Single-cell RNA sequencing supports CAF-induced M2-like reprogramming of monocytes, marked by elevated CD163 [ ].
CAFs also impair dendritic cell (DC) function by blocking maturation and inducing a regulatory DC (rDC) phenotype via IL-6/STAT3 signaling [ ]. These rDCs exhibit reduced antigen presentation and secrete IDO, which promotes Treg expansion and T-cell dysfunction [ , ]. Tregs further suppress immunity through inhibitory cytokines (e.g., TGF-β, IL-10) and checkpoint receptors (CTLA-4, LAG-3) [ – ]. In colorectal cancer, CAFs recruit Tregs and sustain them via VEGF-A [ – ].
Additionally, CAFs suppress cytotoxic lymphocytes: TGF-β from CAFs inhibits NK cell activity by downregulating NKG2D and NKp30 via miR-183/DAP12 [ – ]. For CD8+ T cells, CAFs employ multiple strategies—CXCL12-mediated exclusion [ ], ECM remodeling and hypoxic limitation [ ], and secretion of IL-6 and TGF-β [ , ]—to limit infiltration and cytotoxicity. The ECM protein β-H3 from CAFs binds CD61 on T cells, disrupting TCR signaling via HIC-5 and LCK [ ]. CAFs also upregulate immune checkpoint molecules (e.g., PD-1) and activate T-cell apoptosis through PD-1/PD-L1 pathways.
Thus, CAFs orchestrate a broad immunosuppressive network in the TME by modulating macrophages, DCs, Tregs, NK cells, and cytotoxic T lymphocytes [ , , ].

## Interplay between ubiquitin–proteasome system and tumor microenvironment in colorectal cancer
The UPS regulates numerous critical molecular pathways and plays a central role in tumor progression and the formation of the immunosuppressive network within the TME. Therefore, targeting the UPS can promote tumor antigen presentation, increase the expression of immune-activating factors, and reduce immunosuppressive molecules, thereby enhancing immune cell activity and reversing the immunosuppressive effects of the TME. This approach shows strong therapeutic cancers, particularly in CRC. Immunotherapy is currently among the most effective treatment strategies for CRC. Many studies have shown that the UPS influences the activity of immune cells within the TME by regulating the stability and function of key proteins, thereby either promoting or inhibiting the progression of CRC. In this section, we summarize three well-characterized mechanisms through which the UPS regulates immune cell function. Finally, we highlight potential pathways of the interaction between UPS and the TME based on current research priorities. Analyzing and synthesizing the key molecules involved in these pathways may provide insights into the development of more effective targeted therapeutic drugs for patients with CRC.Among the various deubiquitinating enzymes within the UPS, USP14 stands out as a particularly critical regulator in the CRC TME due to its direct involvement in promoting immune suppression through metabolic reprogramming, distinguishing it from other factors like USP21. Specifically, USP14 stabilizes indoleamine 2,3-dioxygenase 1 (IDO1), enhancing tryptophan catabolism into kynurenine, which depletes local tryptophan levels and inhibits effector T cell function while fostering an immunosuppressive environment in CRC. This mechanism has been highlighted in recent studies as a key driver of tumor immune evasion, aligning with current research priorities on targeting metabolic vulnerabilities to enhance immunotherapy efficacy. In comparison, USP21 primarily contributes to CRC progression by deubiquitinating and stabilizing PD-L1 on regulatory T cells (Tregs), thereby reinforcing Treg-mediated suppression and correlating with poorer prognosis and reduced immune cell infiltration in the TME. While USP21 also influences therapy response and TME dynamics, its role appears more generalized toward tumor cell proliferation, migration, and overall immune modulation rather than the specific metabolic immunosuppression mediated by USP14 [ ]. Furthermore, USP14‘s additional contribution to chemotherapy resistance, such as in 5-fluorouracil (5-FU)-resistant CRC models, underscores its broader therapeutic relevance. Focusing on USP14 thus provides a strategic lens for exploring targeted interventions that could more effectively reverse TME immunosuppression in CRC patients compared to other USPs like USP21 [ ].

## Ubiquitin-specific protease 14 stabilizes IDO1 and promotes immunosuppression
In this section, we examine how the ubiquitin-specific protease 14 (USP14)–IDO1 axis contributes to immunosuppression by stabilizing the IDO1 protein, thereby promoting immunoregulatory activity of the tryptophan (Trp) metabolism pathway and facilitating tumor immune escape.

## Mechanism of USP14-mediated stabilization of IDO1
USP14 counteracts TRIM21-mediated degradation of the IDO1 protein through its DUB activity, thereby maintaining IDO1 stability. USP14 is a DUB that prevents proteasomal degradation by removing ubiquitin chains from target proteins [ , ]. Studies have shown that E3 ubiquitin ligases, such as TRIM21, ubiquitinate IDO1 through K48-linked chains, marking it for proteasomal degradation. However, USP14 antagonizes this process by directly binding to IDO1 through its ubiquitin-like (UBL), enabling stable sustained expression of the IDO1 protein [ , ]. The UBL domain of USP14 is critical for this interaction, as a USP14 mutant lacking the UBL domain (USP14ΔUBL) fails to bind IDO1, resulting in a significant decrease in IDO1 protein levels. Furthermore, inhibition of USP14 catalytic activity, such as with the small-molecule inhibitor IU1, significantly reduces IDO1 protein abundance and shortens its half-life. These findings indicate that the UBL domain not only mediates binding to IDO1 but may also affect USP14 deubiquitination activity through conformational regulation [ ].
IDO1-mediated Trp metabolism and its metabolite kynurenine (Kyn) are central mechanisms of immune suppression within the TME. Trp metabolism is a common pathway for cancer immune escape. Three enzymes are involved in the degradation of Trp, including Trp 2,3-dioxygenase (TDO), IDO1 and indoleamine 2,3-dioxygenase 2 (IDO2). Among these, IDO1 is the dominant enzyme [ ], and its elevated protein expression is closely associated with immunosuppression within the TME [ ]. IDO1 functions as a rate-limiting enzyme that catalyzes Trp degradation into metabolites such as Kyn, a process that plays a key role in tumor immune escape [ , ]. High levels of the IDO1 protein deplete Trp within the TME [ , ]. This depletion inhibits the proliferation and function of CD8+ T cells [ ], CD4+ T cells, and NK cells, thereby promoting immunosuppression and facilitating tumor immune escape [ , ]. Studies have revealed that tumor cells transfer Kyn to activate the aromatic hydrocarbon receptor (AhR). Once activated, AhR translocates into the nucleus, dimerizes with ARNT, and induces the expression of downstream genes associated with immunosuppression [ ]. For example, this pathway can induce PD-1 expression on CD8+ T cells, weakening their cytotoxic function against cancer cells. Furthermore, Kyn can directly influence TOX mRNA expression in CD8+ T cells, leading to their functional depletion [ ]. Tumor-regenerating cells (TRCs), IFN-γ stimulation, release high levels of Kyn, which is subsequently transported into adjacent CD8+ T cells through the SLC7A8 and PAT4 transporters. This process activates AhR and upregulates PD-1 expression [ ]. The Kyn–AhR pathway has been validated in mouse tumor models and patients with cancer, and its blockade has been shown to enhance antitumor T-cell activity. Additionally, AhR activation induces IL-10 secretion in DCs and NK cells, thereby enhancing immunosuppression [ ]. Another study further revealed that Trp depletion induces T-cell proliferation arrest through the activation of the GCN2 pathway, significantly impairing the helper function of CD4+ T cells. In NK cells, Kyn induces suppression and apoptosis [ ]. Studies have shown that IDO1 inhibits T-cell responses by depleting Trp and producing immunosuppressive metabolites, including Kyn [ ]. USP14 has been shown to prevent the TRIM21-mediated degradation of IDO1. Moreover, IDO1 expression in CRC cells is regulated by canonical signaling pathways. For example, IFN-γ secreted by T cells upregulates IDO1 expression through the JAK1/2–STAT1 pathway [ ], while the activation of STAT3, NF-κB, and other tumor-associated signaling cascades also contributes to increased IDO1 expression. Collectively, USP14 enhances the IDO1-mediated Trp metabolic pathway by stabilizing the IDO1 protein, to maintain its continuous high expression, resulting in Trp depletion and Kyn accumulation within the TME. This, in turn, inhibits CD8+ effector T-cell proliferation, promotes Treg enrichment, and facilitates immune escape.
The findings highlight the stabilizing effect of USP14 on IDO1 and underscore the central role of IDO1 in tumor immune escape. In the following section, we discuss the specific functions and clinical implications of the USP14–IDO1 axis in CRC progression.

## Role of the USP14–IDO1 axis in the progression of CRC
Extensive studies have shown that the USP14–IDO1 axis plays a crucial role in driving immunosuppression in CRC and is associated with poor prognosis. Targeting this axis, therefore, presents therapeutic potential. A study reported in Nature Communications showed that USP14 overexpression in CRC tissues is significantly correlated with elevated IDO1 protein levels [ ]. Increased USP14 and IDO1 expression is often accompanied by reduced infiltration of CD8+ T cells and increased infiltration of FOXP3 Tregs, and this immune profile is associated with worse patient outcomes, such as shorter overall survival. The study further showed that USP14 expression in patients with CRC positively correlates with tumor invasion and metastasis, suggesting its importance in disease progression [ ]. Additionally, the therapeutic implications of USP14 inhibition have also been explored. The first-generation small-molecule USP14 inhibitor, IU1, markedly reduced IDO1 protein levels in both CRC cells and xenograft tumors. This reduction alleviated IDO1-mediated immunosuppression [ ], reduce Trp metabolism, and restored T-cell function [ ]. Notably [ ], USP14 inhibition did not trigger the common AhR overactivation observed with direct IDO1 enzyme inhibitors [ ]. These suggest that suppressing IDO1 through the USP14 pathway may offer a safer therapeutic strategy than direct enzymatic inhibition of IDO1, as it avoids triggering compensatory immunosuppressive signaling caused by abrupt blockade of the Kyn pathway. A more recent study further revealed that USP14 accelerates CRC cells by stabilizing the ATG7 protein [ ]. Consistently, high IDO1 expression in CRC remains strongly associated with poor clinical outcomes and prognosis [ ].
In a horizontal comparison, the USP14–IDO1 axis functions as a metabolic immune checkpoint that complements the classic PD-1/PD-L1 pathway. The USP14–IDO1 axis inhibits T-cell activity through the Trp metabolic pathway, whereas the PD-1/PD-L1 pathway depletes T cells through receptor–ligand interaction. Both mechanisms contribute to an immunosuppressive TME. Notably, after PD-1 monoclonal antibody treatment, tumors can compensatorily upregulate IDO1 expression, thereby diminishing therapeutic efficacy, which suggests a potential synergistic relationship between these two pathways [ ]. Longitudinal analysis of this mechanism showed a causal chain extending from USP14-mediated regulation of IDO1 protein stability to downstream immune effects. USP14 functions as an upstream molecule, controlling IDO1 protein degradation, and thereby influencing the activation of downstream metabolites, such as Kyn, as well as activation of the AhR pathway. This cascade ultimately determines the extent of tumor-mediated T-cell suppression [ ]. Regarding synergistic mechanisms, inhibition of USP14 markedly reduces IDO1 protein levels, alleviates metabolic inhibition of effector CD8+ T cells, and enhances the efficacy of anti–PD-1 immunotherapy. In animal models, the combined use of a USP14 small-molecule inhibitor with PD-1 blockade produced stronger tumor growth inhibition than either monotherapy [ ]. Collectively, these findings indicate a functional intersection between the USP14–IDO1 axis and the PD-1/PD-L1 pathway, both of which regulate immune escape through ubiquitination and deubiquitination processes. This evidence suggests that simultaneous targeting of USP14 and PD-1 may produce synergistic antitumor immune responses.

## Treatment strategies for USP14 in CRC
Inhibition of USP14 to reduce IDO1 protein expression has shown potential in preclinical models, as it may enhance T-cell function and increase the sensitivity of CRC cells to ICIs such as anti–PD-1 therapy [ ]. Conversely, the direct use of IDO1 inhibitors has yielded limited efficacy. In the ECHO-301/KEYNOTE-252 trial, the IDO inhibitor epacadostat combined with pembrolizumab (anti–PD-1) produced outcomes similar to placebo combined with pembrolizumab, indicating no significant clinical benefit [ , ]. The reason for this failure remains unclear, but it has been speculated that it may involve IDO1-mediated activation of AhR pathway [ ]. Current research on other IDO1 inhibitors, including linrodostat(BMS-986205) and KHK2455, is ongoing. However, USP14 inhibition appears to suppress IDO1 protein expression more effectively and may avoid AhR activation. Notably, IU1, a selective USP14 inhibitor with high specificity, has shown stronger suppression of CRC tumor growth in mouse models when [ ] combined with anti–PD-1 compared to when used alone [ ] (Fig. ). 

## E3 ubiquitin ligases regulate the PD-L1/PD-1 axis in CRC
In this section, we examine how various E3 ubiquitin ligases regulate the stability of PD-L1/PD-1 proteins, thereby influencing tumor immune checkpoint activity and contributing to immune escape. We also explore the potential therapeutic significance of these regulatory mechanisms in CRC.

## Mechanism of E3 ubiquitin ligase–mediated regulation of PD-L1/PD-1
Multiple E3 ubiquitin ligases dynamically regulate the PD-1/PD-L1 axis by recognizing and ubiquitinating PD-L1 or PD-1, thereby influencing the function of T cells. E3 ubiquitin ligase regulates protein levels by attaching ubiquitin molecules to target proteins and marking them for proteasome degradation. Several E3 ubiquitin ligases target PD-L1, induce its ubiquitination, and promote degradation, thereby dynamically regulating the abundance of PD-L1 on the surface of tumor cells. The interaction between PD-1 and its ligand PD-L1 is a central mechanism driving escape in CRC [ ]. PD-L1 is a transmembrane protein primarily expressed in tumor cells, DCs, and endothelial cells, whereas PD-1 is expressed in activated T cells, B cells, and NK cells [ ]. The binding of PD-L1 to PD-1 inhibits T-cell activation and effector function. Multiple signaling pathways, including PI3K/AKT, MAPK, JAK/STAT, WNT, NF-κB, and Hedgehog (Hh), promote the expression of PD-1/PD-L1 axis [ , ]. Activation of these pathways enhances PD-1/PD-L1 signaling, which recruits phosphatases such as SHP-2 and SHP-1. These phosphatases dephosphorylate and inactivate key molecules in T cells, thereby enhancing tumor immune escape. Specifically, SHP-1 inhibits T-cell activation by dephosphorylating key molecules of the TCR signaling pathway, including the TCR-ζ chain and ZAP-70 [ ]. SHP-2 is recruited to PD-1 through its binding to phosphorylated ITSM. Once recruited, SHP-2 dephosphorylates key molecules in the TCR signaling pathway, such as the TCR-ζ chain and ZAP70. This activity significantly reduces TCR-mediated IL-2 production [ ], resulting in reduced T-cell proliferation, decreased cytokine secretion (e.g., IL-2 and IFN-γ), and impaired cytotoxic function [ , ]. Speckle-type POZ protein (SPOP), an E3 ubiquitin ligase, regulates PD-L1 stability. Loss-of-function mutations in SPOP prevent its interaction with the C-terminal eight amino acids of PD-L1 (residues 283–290) [ , ], thereby destroying PD-L1 ubiquitination and degradation and resulting in elevated PD-L1 expression [ , ]. Furthermore, elevated RIG-I expression in CRC cells competitively blocks the SPOP–PD-L1 interaction, reducing PD-L1 ubiquitination and degradation, thus stabilizing PD-L1 and promoting immune escape [ ]. C-Cbl, another E3 ubiquitin ligase, binds to the cytoplasmic tail of PD-1 to promote its degradation. This interaction downregulates PD-1 expression levels in immune cells and blocks PD-1 binding to PD-L1. Notably, C-Cbl–deficient mice showed a two- to threefold increase in PD-1 levels on CD8+ T cells and macrophages [ , ]. KLHL22, an adaptor protein for an E3 ligase complex, specifically recognizes incompletely glycosylated PD-1 and targets it for ubiquitination before it reaches the cell surface, thereby preventing PD-1–PD-L1 binding [ ]. β-TrCP, an F-box protein component of the Skp1–Cullin-1–F-box (SCF) complex, requires glycogen synthase kinase 3β (GSK3β)–mediated phosphorylation of PD-L1 at specific intracellular sites to recognize and ubiquitinate PD-L1 for proteasomal degradation. In the absence of glycosylation, PD-L1 is phosphorylated by GSK3β and subsequently targeted by β-TrCP. Therefore, activation of the PI3K/AKT pathway, which inhibits GSK3β activity, indirectly reduces PD-L1 degradation by β-TrCP and stabilizes PD-L1 expression at the cell surface [ , , ]. Studies have identified the E3 ligase FBW7 as a critical “gatekeeper” of PD-1. FBW7, the F-box protein component of the SCF complex, binds to PD-1 and triggers a K48-linked ubiquitination chain at the Lys233 site when PD-1 is phosphorylated at Ser261 by CDK1. This modification targets the PD-1 protein for proteasomal degradation. FBW7 deficiency leads to PD-1 accumulation in T cells, thereby limiting antitumor activity. Conversely, FBW7 overexpression reduces PD-1 levels and restores the functionality of exhausted T cells [ , ]. Mutations or downregulation of E3 ligases such as FBW7 or KLHL22 can sustain elevated PD-1 expression, driving T cells into a depleted phenotype and creating a “cold” TME that is resistant to immunotherapy [ ]. Detailed analyses of these mechanisms highlight the central role of E3 ligases in regulating the PD-L1/PD-1 axis and promoting tumor immune escape.

## Significance of E3 ligase regulation in the treatment of CRC
Targeted regulation of E3 ligase activity to enhance degradation of the PD-1/PD-L1 axis has emerged as a promising strategy for improving immunotherapy efficacy in CRC. Given the central role of the PD-L1/PD-1 axis in antitumor immunity, regulating its ubiquitination has become a new research focus [ ]. Preclinical studies indicate that interventions modulating specific E3 ligases can improve immune responses. For example, in a nonsmall-cell lung cancer model, upregulation of the PD-1-targeting E3 ligase FBW7 significantly improved the efficacy of anti–PD-1 checkpoint blockade therapy. Activation of FBW7 using the small molecule Oridonin, in combination with anti–PD-1 antibodies, reduced PD-1 levels in tumors, increased CD8+ T-cell infiltration, and produced greater tumor growth inhibition compared to immunotherapy alone [ ]. SPOP, as a substrate recognition subunit of the Cullin3-RING ubiquitin ligase complex, functions as a tumor suppressor in CRC by mediating the ubiquitination and degradation of oncogenic proteins, such as ERα [ ]. Currently, drug therapies do not specifically target the interaction between SPOP and the PD-1/PD-L1 axis. Cbl-b has been shown to increase the sensitivity of gastric cancer cells to Cetuximab. Although Cetuximab can inhibit CRC tumor cell growth [ ], no studies have specifically examined the Cbl-b and PD-1/PD-L1 axis. KLHL22, a member of the Cullin-RING ligase family, regulates T-cell function by promoting PD-1 degradation. Studies indicate that 5-fluorouracil (5-FU) chemotherapy can enhance the immune response to anti–PD-1 therapy by inhibiting KLHL22 transcription, thereby reducing PD-1 degradation. Similarly, regulation of PD-L1 also has clinical significance; for example, the small-molecule agent DEM (nor-magnolol) can promote PD-L1 degradation in CRC cells through the ubiquitin–proteasome pathway, thereby improving T cell–mediated tumor cytotoxicity [ ]. It has also been reported that the combined use of CDK4/6 inhibitors (e.g., Palbociclib) and PD-1/PD-L1 antibodies produced synergistic effects in preclinical models, including breast cancer. This synergy is partly attributed to CDK4/6 inhibition, which improves the stability of SPOP, thereby enhancing PD-L1 degradation. Although CDK4/6 inhibition increases PD-L1 transcription through interference with the Rb-E2F pathway, it also alleviates the inhibitory effect on SPOP, accelerating PD-L1 protein degradation and ultimately promoting immune-mediated tumor cell killing [ ]. Notably, another serine/threonine kinase, CDK5, has the opposite effect: activated CDK5 phosphorylates and destabilizes FBXO22 and FBXW7, resulting in increased PD-L1 and PD-1 levels [ ]. Therefore, CDK5 inhibition can indirectly reduce PD-1/PD-L1 levels and enhance immune responses by relieving the negative regulation of these E3 ligases. This concept has been validated in previous clinical trials, in which CDK5 inhibitors (e.g., roscovitine) increased FBXO22 activity, promoted PD-L1 degradation in NSCLC cells, and improved sensitivity to radiotherapy and immunotherapy [ ]. In general, multiple strategies targeting E3 ubiquitin ligases are advancing into experimental stages. For example, Proteolysis-targeting chimera (PROTAC) technology, which directly induces the degradation of PD-1 or PD-L1 or target oncogenic proteins such as KRAS and β-catenin, is entering preclinical research [ ], offering the potential to target traditionally “nonpharmacological” proteins. Other approaches involve small molecules that modulate upstream and downstream regulatory factors. With an increasing understanding of these ubiquitination regulatory mechanisms, targeting the PD-L1/PD-1 axis through E3 ligases is emerging as a promising approach to improve the efficacy of CRC immunotherapy. E3 ubiquitin ligases play a dual role in CRC treatment by regulating the stability of key oncogenic proteins, serving both as therapeutic targets and as key mediators of resistance. Future studies should focus on elucidating their dynamic regulatory networks and developing precise combination strategies to translate these findings from the laboratory into clinical practice [ ]. Horizontally, E3 ubiquitin ligases regulate PD-1/PD-L1 signaling intensity in multiple ways: by directly promoting protein degradation or by indirectly altering its homeostasis through the regulation of other post-translational modifications, and ultimately determining the degree of activation of immune cells in TME. Vertically, from upstream stimulation by inflammatory factors to the phosphorylation of PD-L1 by intracellular kinases (e.g., GSK3β and CK2), followed by recognition of phosphorylation sites by E3 ligases and subsequent ubiquitination and proteasomal degradation, the pathway operates as a cascade of regulatory axes, each presenting potential nodes for therapeutic intervention [ ]. At the intersection of these regulatory nodes, the protein ubiquitination system forms a common hub, whether through the USP14–IDO1 axis or the various E3–PD-L1 pathways. These mechanisms collectively influence tumor immune escape by altering the ubiquitination status of key immunoregulatory proteins. This highlights the need for designing combination therapeutic strategies that target these shared pathways. For example, USP14 inhibitors have been shown to reduce PD-L1–mediated immune tolerance, while combining E3 ligases that promote PD-L1 degradation (e.g., activating SPOP or mimicking its function) with ICIs may enhance antitumor immune [ ]. The regulatory role of E3 ubiquitin ligases in the PD-1/PD-L1 pathway and metabolic enzymes such as IDO1 are functionally complementary: the former primarily modulates immune checkpoint signaling intensity, whereas the latter primarily influence the extent of inhibition of the metabolic microenvironment. Both processes converge on the regulation of protein homeostasis. Synergistic interventions targeting the intersection of these pathways—such as harnessing ubiquitin-mediated degradation—may help overcome CRC tumor immune tolerance and improve immunotherapy efficacy (Fig. ). 

## ZFP91 promotes CRC immunosuppression through the PP2A–mTOR pathway
In this section, we will explore how the E3 ubiquitin ligase zinc finger protein 91 (ZFP91) regulates T-cell metabolism and function by modulating the PP2A–mTOR signaling pathway, ultimately promoting the immunosuppressive microenvironment in CRC.

## Immunosuppressive effect of ZFP91 in CRC
ZFP91 is an E3 ubiquitin ligase that regulates the ubiquitination of various essential proteins in cells. It has been identified as a potential carcinogenic protein in several tumor types, with carcinogenic activity that includes inhibiting tumor apoptosis, promoting tumor growth, and accelerating tumor metastasis [ – ]. In CRC, ZFP91 promotes the assembly of the protein phosphatase 2A (PP2A) holoenzyme, inhibits the activity of the T-cell mTORC1 signaling pathway, and impairs glycolytic function, thereby weakening the antitumor immune response. Specifically, ZFP91 mediates K63-linked ubiquitination of the PP2A catalytic subunit (PP2Ac). The formation of K63-linked ubiquitin chains serves as a signal for protein–protein interaction [ ], facilitating the formation of the PP2Aa–PP2Ac complex and accelerating PP2A holoenzyme assembly. Through this mechanism, PP2A restricts mammalian target of rapamycin complex 1 (mTORC1)–mediated glycolysis in T cells, thereby inhibiting the activation and proliferation of T cells, ultimately reducing antitumor activity within TME [ ]. Single-cell RNA-Seq analysis of CRC tumor–infiltrating T cells showed that ZFP91 mRNA in T cells had a higher expression level than in other cell types. Experimental validation further confirmed that ZFP91 in CRC is associated with the inhibition of T-cell proliferation and activation [ ].

## Composition and functions of PP2A and its subunits
PP2A is a crucial serine/threonine phosphatase whose enzymatic activity and holoenzyme composition play essential roles in regulating T-cell function and mediating the immunosuppressive effect of Tregs. PP2A is composed of three different subunits: a scaffold A subunit (PP2Aa), a regulatory B subunit (PP2Ab), and a PP2Ac [ , ]. In humans, 15 genes encode B subunits that generate 26 subtypes, which combine to form more than 100 different PP2A holoenzymes, thereby conferring diverse biological activities [ ]. The PP2A core enzyme consists of PP2Aa (also known as A or PR65) and the PP2Ac. Both the scaffold and catalytic subunits α and β isoforms, with the α isoform being approximately 10 times more abundant than the β isoform. The PP2A core enzyme associates with variable PP2Ab to form the complete holoenzyme [ ]. PP2A has been shown to contribute to mTOR signaling pathway inhibition. Reduced PP2A activity sustains
T-cell metabolism and effector function, thereby enhancing their cytotoxic activity against tumor tissues. At the same time, increased PP2A activity plays a key role in maintaining the immunosuppressive function of Tregs. In autoimmune diseases, PP2A activity in Tregs is often downregulated [ , ]. These findings show that reduced PP2A activity in T cells within TME may enhance antitumor immunity and inhibit tumor immune escape. In CRC, PP2A has been shown to support tumor cell growth and survival. Moreover, ZFP91 promotes the assembly of the PP2A holoenzyme, an effect that becomes more significant under TCR activation [ ]. Therefore, targeted inhibition of ZFP91 activity could significantly reduce PP2A activity and, when combined with ICIs, may improve the efficacy of immunotherapy in patients with CRC.
Building on the regulatory role of ZFP91 in facilitating PP2A assembly and activity, we next examine how PP2A modulates the mTOR signaling pathway to inhibit T-cell metabolism and function, ultimately promoting the immunosuppression of CRC (Fig. ). 

## PP2A promotes the development of CRC by modulating the mTOR pathway through ZFP91
PP2A inhibits the mTORC1 pathway through dephosphorylation, thereby disrupting T-cell metabolic reprogramming through glycolysis and simultaneously promoting the immunosuppressive function of Tregs. Within TME, various T-cell subtypes are enriched. In the absence of TCR activation, T cells remain in a resting state and primarily rely on oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) for energy. Upon TCR stimulation by MHC tumor antigens, T cells shift toward glycolytic metabolism to support biosynthesis and promote antitumor immune responses, even under the hypoxic conditions of the TME [ ]. Therefore, inhibition of T-cell glycolysis results in functional impairment and diminished antitumor activity. Glycolytic regulation in T cells is primarily influenced by the mTOR signaling pathway. mTOR is a serine/threonine protein kinase that regulates nutrient and cell growth signal levels [ ]. It is involved in the formation of two protein complexes: rapamycin-sensitive mTORC1, composed of mTOR, Raptor, and mLST8, and the rapamycin-insensitive mTORC2, composed of mTOR, Rictor (mAVO3), SIN1, and mLST8 [ – ]. Within the TME, T-cell metabolic reprogramming is activated, leading to a shift in energy metabolism. Tumor-infiltrating T cells primarily rely on glycolysis for energy. mTORC1 and mTORC2 regulate this process by directly and indirectly controlling the expression of glucose transporters, glycolytic enzymes, and transcription factors, thereby inducing the expression of various glycolytic effector genes [ ]. Evidence indicates that ZFP91 inhibits T-cell glycolysis in an mTORC1-dependent manner [ ]. Specifically, under resting conditions, ZFP91 is primarily localized in the nucleus of T cells. Upon TCR stimulation, ZFP91 rapidly translocates from the nucleus to the cytoplasm, where it interacts with PP2Ac and mediates the K63-linked ubiquitination of PP2Ac. The ubiquitinated PP2Ac binds to PP2Aa to form the PP2A core enzyme, which subsequently binds to PP2Ab to assemble the PP2A holoenzyme. Experimental studies have shown that ZFP91 is essential for PP2A holoenzyme assembly [ ]. Previous findings suggest that PP2A primarily regulates mTORC1 in CRC. By dephosphorylating mTOR, AKT, and AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK), mTORC1 activity is directly or indirectly affected. The specific mechanism is as follows: mTOR, as a core component of mTORC1, is dephosphorylated by PP2A, resulting in mTORC1 inactivation [ ], while AKT functions upstream to activate mTORC1 [ ]. As a GTPase-activating protein for Rheb, tuberous sclerosis complex 2 (TSC2) directly catalyzes the hydrolysis of Rheb-GTP to Rheb-GDP, thereby inactivating the Rheb protein. TSC1 enhances the activity of TSC2 [ ], and the two proteins commonly function as a heterodimeric TSC1–TSC2 complex. Rheb-GTP is the direct and essential activator of mTORC1 [ ]. By binding to mTORC1, Rheb-GTP induces a conformational change that relieves autoinhibition and activates the kinase activity of mTORC1 [ ]. AKT enhances the activation of the mTORC1 pathway by phosphorylating and inhibiting TSC2 [ ]. Therefore, PP2A suppresses this process by dephosphorylating and inactivating AKT, thereby inhibiting the mTORC1 pathway. AMPK inhibits mTORC1 activity through a dual mechanism: phosphorylation-mediated activation of TSC2 [ ], and phosphorylation-dependent inhibition of Raptor, a regulatory subunit of the mTORC1 complex [ ]. Therefore, dephosphorylation-mediated inhibition of AMPK by PP2A enhances the expression of the mTORC1 pathway. Once mTORC1 is activated, it phosphorylates the downstream effectors p70S6K and 4E-binding protein 1 (4E-BP1). Activated p70S6K regulates ribosomal translation by phosphorylating initiation proteins, thereby facilitating the translation of hypoxia-inducible factor 1α (HIF-1α) mRNA [ ]. Concurrently, phosphorylation of 4E-BP1 causes its release from eukaryotic initiation factor 4E (eIF4E), lifting translational inhibition and further enhancing the synthesis of HIF-1α mRNA [ ]. Additionally, mTORC1 directly promotes phosphorylation of STAT3 at Ser727, thereby increasing HIF-1α mRNA transcription [ ]. Studies have shown that mTORC1 upregulates the transcription of HIF-1α mRNA by regulating Forkhead box protein K1 (FOXK1). FOXK1 is an essential transcription factor of HIF-1α that binds upstream promoter regions of HIF-1α in the nucleus to promote transcription. The activity of FOXK1 is regulated by GSK3, which phosphorylates FOXK1 at the S402 site. This phosphorylation leads to FOXK1 nuclear export and binding to 14-3-3 proteins (a known phosphopeptide-binding protein), reducing its ability to promote HIF-1α transcription. Given that mTORC1 negatively regulates GSK3, it inhibits FOXK1 phosphorylation, resulting in FOXK1 accumulation in the nucleus and binding to DNA to promote the transcription of HIF-1α mRNA [ ]. In addition to HIF-1α, mTORC1 promotes the translation of Myc mRNA through the coordinated actions of p70S6K and 4E-BP1 [ ]. HIF-1α and Myc are key transcription factors regulating the transcription of glycolytic enzyme genes. Together, they enhance the expression of glucose transporter 1 (GLUT1) and glycolytic enzymes such as hexokinase and pyruvate kinase M2 [ – ]. The upregulation of these factors enhances the glycolytic pathway, thereby promoting tumor cell proliferation, invasion, and metastasis. In the TME of patients with CRC, the expression of ZFP91 mRNA in T cells is elevated. ZFP91 facilitates the assembly of the PP2A holoenzyme, which directly or indirectly inhibits the mTORC1 pathway. This inhibition reduces glycolytic activity in T cells within the TME, leading to impaired energy production and dysregulated proliferation of CD8+ T cells, thereby weakening their cytotoxic activity against tumor cells. Concurrently, secretion of tumor-suppressive cytokines in CD4+ T cells is also inhibited. It has been shown that elevated PP2A activity plays a key role in maintaining the immunosuppressive function of Tregs. Current evidence indicates that Tregs primarily rely on OXPHOS for energy. Conversely, CD4+ effector T cells (Teff) rely more on glycolysis, and excessive glycolytic activity in Tregs has been shown to cause instability and functional impairment [ – ]. Moreover, ZFP91, expressed in Tregs, mediates the K63-linked ubiquitination of the BECN1 protein, thereby promoting BECN1–PIK3C3 binding to enhance TCR-induced autophagy and related downstream pathways. The formation of autophagosomes in Tregs can inhibit mTORC1 activity and suppress c-Myc expression, thereby preventing excessive apoptosis and limiting glycolytic metabolism [ , ]. In the TME of CRC, most cells, including tumor cells, shift to glycolysis under hypoxic conditions. This shift gives Tregs a competitive advantage in utilizing available oxygen, enabling them to sustain mitochondrial-dependent OXPHOS, which is essential for their stability and normal function. Under the influence of ZFP91, the synergistic activity of the BECN1–PIK3C3 pathway and PP2A contributes to the inhibition of the mTORC1 pathway, thereby inhibiting glycolysis and enhancing the immunosuppressive function of Tregs. Together, these processes promote the formation of immunosuppressive TME.
To this point, we have detailed the mechanisms through which the ZFP91–PP2A–mTOR pathway inhibits the metabolism of Teff and enhances the function of Tregs, thereby promoting the immunosuppression of CRC. Thus, targeting ZFP91 inhibition may represent a potential therapeutic strategy to reverse this immunosuppressive microenvironment.

## Targeted inhibition of ZFP91 as a strategy to reverse the immune microenvironment of CRC tumors
In summary, ZFP91 is highly expressed in T cells infiltrating CRC. As an E3 ubiquitin ligase, ZFP91 limits mTORC1-mediated glycolytic metabolism in Teff by mediating the K63-linked ubiquitination of PP2Ac. In Tregs, ZFP91 also induces K63-linked ubiquitination of Beclin1, thereby inhibiting mTORC1-dependent glycolysis and promoting mitochondrial OXPHOS. This shift supports Treg stability and promotes their immunosuppressive function, ultimately facilitating CRC immune tolerance. Therefore, targeted inhibition of ZFP91 may help improve the immunosuppressive TME of CRC, offering a promising therapeutic strategy for improving patient prognosis. At present, however, no clinically available ZFP91-targeted inhibitors exist. Advances in small-molecule drug development could enable the design of such compounds by analyzing the zinc finger domain or the E3 ubiquitin ligase binding interface of ZFP91. Nonetheless, given that ZFP91 enhances Treg-mediated immunosuppression, inhibiting ZFP91 expression could impair Treg function and increase the risk of autoimmune diseases. To mitigate this risk, ZFP91 inhibitors should be combined with the corresponding ligand proteins specifically expressed on T cells infiltrating the CRC TME, thereby enabling targeted delivery of ZFP91 inhibitors to the tumor site while minimizing systemic effects on Tregs in other tissues (Fig. ). 

## Other potential pathways
We have discussed the roles of the USP14–IDO1 axis, the PD-L1/PD-1 axis, and the ZFP91–PP2A–mTOR pathway in mediating immunosuppression in CRC. Beyond these established pathways, several potential pathways may play a role in the interactions between the CRC TME and the UPS. In this section, we provide a brief overview of these potential pathways and explore their associations with CRC.

## Upsregulation of Wnt/β-catenin pathway in the CRC TME
This section examines how the UPS regulates the Wnt/β-catenin pathway and, in turn, influences tumor cell behavior and immune cell function within the TME of CRC. The UPS regulates the stability and degradation of key components of the β-catenin complex, thereby profoundly influencing the activity of the Wnt/β-catenin pathway, which plays a central role in CRC onset and progression. Wnt is a cysteine-rich secreted protein produced by multiple cell types and plays a central role in intercellular communication. Dysregulated secretion is associated with cancer onset. The Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway is an evolutionarily conserved cascade involved in the regulation of embryonic development, tissue homeostasis, and cellular metabolism [ ]. Under normal physiological conditions, Wnt signaling maintains the self-renewal capacity of intestinal stem cells by stabilizing β-catenin, activating downstream genes such as LGR5 and AXIN2, and coordinating the balance between differentiation and proliferation. β-catenin induces transcription of c-Myc and Cyclin D1 (CCND1), thereby promoting the proliferation of crypt basal cells. Concurrently, it inhibits the expression of proapoptotic factors such as Bax, contributing to the regulation of cell proliferation and apoptosis [ – ]. β-catenin degradation is regulated by an intracellular phosphorylated degradation complex composed of the colorectal APC tumor suppressor protein, Axin, casein kinase 1α, and GSK3. This complex promotes its β-catenin ubiquitination and subsequent proteasomal degradation through phosphorylation [ ]. Conversely, Wnt protein binding to its coiled-coil protein receptor (a seven-transmembrane protein) and the low-density lipoprotein receptor–related protein 5/6 coreceptor, inhibits β-catenin phosphorylation by the destruction complex, thereby stabilizing β-catenin [ ]. Studies have shown that β-catenin degradation is impaired in more than 90% of patients with CRC [ ]. Approximately 80% of patients with CRC harbor loss-of-function mutations in the APC gene, which disrupts the phosphorylation-dependent degradation complex composed of APC, AXIN, and GSK3β. Consequently, β-catenin accumulates in the cytoplasm and nucleus, where it persistently activates downstream gene transcription [ – , , ]. Approximately 10% of patients with CRC have activation mutations in the β-catenin–encoding gene CTNNB1 (e.g., S45F), enabling β-catenin to evade ubiquitination-mediated degradation and further enhance Wnt/β-catenin pathway activity [ ]. The nuclear translocation of β-catenin promotes the transcription of the downstream target genes. For example, overexpression of c-Myc and CCND1 accelerates cell-cycle progression, disrupts apoptotic signaling (e.g., through dysregulation of the Bcl-2 family), and enhances tumor cell proliferation and resistance to apoptosis [ – , – ]. High expression of downstream genes such as LGR5 and ASCL2 supports tumor stem cell–like properties, contributing to chemotherapy resistance and CRC recurrence [ , ]. Furthermore, the activation of transcription factors such as SNAIL and TWIST enhances the migratory and invasive capacities of cancer cells [ ]. Given that β-catenin accumulation is a common event in CRC, therapeutic strategies targeting ubiquitination to inhibit Wnt/β-catenin pathway activity have shown good inhibitory effects in preclinical studies. For example, NEURL, a novel tumor suppressor, functions as an E3 ubiquitin ligase that directly binds to β-catenin and induces its ubiquitination-mediated degradation independently of GSK3β and β-TrCP, thereby inhibiting Wnt/β-catenin signaling. Clinical evidence indicates that NEURL expression is significantly downregulated in CRC [ ]. Similarly, BTBD3 inhibits Wnt/β-catenin pathway activity by directly ubiquitinating β-catenin and promoting its degradation, thereby reducing CRC cell migration and invasion. Animal experiments have shown that BTBD3 overexpression significantly inhibits tumor growth and lung metastasis [ ]. As a tumor suppressor E3 ligase, RNF128 inhibits Wnt pathway activity by enhancing the ubiquitination of β-catenin, thereby inhibiting the proliferation and metastasis of CRC cells. In clinical samples, RNF128 expression has been shown to negatively correlate with CRC malignancy [ ]. These findings indicate that enhancing the ubiquitination-mediated regulation of the Wnt/β-catenin pathway through UPS can inhibit CRC progression. Current mechanistic hypotheses suggest that the Wnt/β-catenin pathway may also regulate immune cell function in the TME through the UPS, thereby contributing to the formation of the tumor immunosuppressive microenvironment. For example, the Wnt pathway may inhibit antitumor immune responses by activating specific E3 ligases (e.g., β-TrCP or members of the RNF family), which ubiquitinate and degrade immune-stimulatory molecules (e.g., MHC-I or CD80/86), or stabilize inhibitory molecules (e.g., PD-L1). Furthermore, Wnt-activated tumor cells may transmit E3 ligases or ubiquitinated substrates through exosomes, directly modifying signaling proteins (e.g., STAT or NF-κB) in T cells or macrophages, and disrupting their functions. However, the precise effects of these mechanisms remain to be confirmed in future studies. Next, we explore the NF-κB pathway, which plays a significant role in regulating immune responses in CRC, and analyze how the UPS is involved in its regulation.

## Role of the NF-κB pathway and the ups in the immune regulation of CRC
The NF-κB pathway plays a critical role in the onset and progression of CRC. Members of the NF-κB include RelA (p65), RelB, c-Rel, p50 (encoded by NF-κB1), and p52 (encoded by NF-κB2), which generally function as homodimers or heterodimers (e.g., p65/p50). The inhibitor proteins IκB bind to NF-κB, preventing its translocation into the nucleus. The primary members of this family are IκBα, IκBβ, and IκBε [ ]. Once NF-κB enters the nucleus, it activates downstream genes, including those encoding antiapoptotic proteins such as Bcl-2 and Bcl-xL, thereby promoting cell survival [ ]. NF-κB also induces the expression of proinflammatory cytokines (TNF-α, IL-6, IL-1β) and chemokines (CXCL8, CXCL10), contributing to the regulation of inflammatory responses [ ]. Additionally, NF-κB is involved in T-cell activation, B-cell development, and antigen presentation [ ]. The activation of the NF-κB pathway is classified into classical and nonclassical pathways. In the classical pathway, stimuli such as TNF-α, IL-1β, and LPS activate the pathway by binding to cell surface receptors, including TNFR, IL-1 R, and TLR4. Upon receptor activation, adaptor proteins such as TRADD and MyD88 are recruited to form a signaling complex that activates the IKK complex (composed of IKKα, IKKβ, and NEMO). The IKK complex phosphorylates IκBα, targeting it for ubiquitination and degradation in a process mediated by the E3 ligase β-TrCP. This degradation releases NF-κB, allowing its translocation into the nucleus, where it regulates the expression of inflammation-related genes [ ]. The UPS is involved in the regulation of the NF-κB pathway. For example, the E3 ubiquitin ligase TRAF6 mediates K63-linked ubiquitination of NEMO, promoting IKK complex activation and enhancing the expression of proinflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α and IL-6 [ ]. β-TrCP is another key E3 ligase that mediates the ubiquitination and degradation of IκBα, thereby limiting the NF-κB nuclear translocation [ ]. In CRC, NF-κB activation drives inflammation-related carcinogenesis, including through the COX-2/PGE2 pathway, and promotes angiogenesis and tumor metastasis [ ]. Its overexpression enhances tumor proliferation and metastasis and promotes the formation of immunosuppressive networks within the TME. Therefore, modulation of the NF-κB pathway through targeting USPs may offer an effective strategy for controlling the onset and progression of CRC. Current evidence suggests that USPs may influence the interaction between the NF-κB pathway and the TME. For example, E3 ubiquitin ligases may promote M2 polarization, leading to increased secretion of IL-10 and TGF-β, thereby regulating NF-κB activity in macrophages to form immunosuppressive TME. Additionally, excessive activation of NF-κB signaling may induce T-cell exhaustion by stabilizing PD-L1 through E3 ligase–mediated ubiquitination. These potential mechanisms, however, require further experimental validation.
Beyond the Wnt/β-catenin and NF-κB pathways, the p53 pathway, a classic tumor suppressor pathway, also plays a critical role in CRC. In the following section, we will explore the interactions between the UPS and the p53 pathway within the CRC TME.

## p53 pathway: interaction between the UPS and CRC TME
The p53 pathway is a well-established tumor suppressor whose primary functions include promoting cell-cycle arrest, inhibiting the CDK/cyclin complex, and blocking cell-cycle progression through the activation of genes such as p21 [ , ]. Its proapoptotic effect induces tumor cell death by upregulating proapoptotic proteins such as Bax and PUMA [ , ]. The p53 pathway also contributes to genomic stability by facilitating DNA repair or triggering apoptosis in response to DNA damage, thereby preventing mutation accumulation [ ]. Therefore, mutations in TP53 can lead to malignant transformation. Studies indicate that approximately 50% of patients with CRC harbor TP53 mutations [ ]. In cases with wild-type TP53, post-translational modifications such as ubiquitination often result in p53 inactivation [ , ]. Ubiquitination dynamically regulates p53 stability and activity, primarily through E3 ubiquitin ligases and deubiquitinating enzymes. In CRC, specific molecules play critical roles. First, E3 ubiquitin ligase–mediated p53 degradation: TRIM26 directly binds to the C-terminal region of p53, promoting its ubiquitination and subsequent proteasomal degradation independently of MDM2, a key E3 ubiquitin ligase that regulates p53 stability in human cells. TRIM26 also enhances MDM2-mediated ubiquitination of p53, thereby dually inhibiting p53 function [ ]. RNF126 forms a complex with p53 in TP53 wild-type CRC cells, promoting ubiquitination-mediated degradation and facilitating cell proliferation, migration, and chemotherapy resistance [ ]. As a component of the SCF complex, FBXL8 directly binds p53 and mediates its ubiquitination and degradation, enhancing CRC stem cell characteristics and liver metastasis [ ]. Upon IL-13/IL-13RA2 signaling activation, UBE3C binds p53, induces K48-linked ubiquitination and degradation, and promotes self-renewal of CRC stem cells [ ]. Second, the role of ubiquitin-conjugating enzyme (E2): UBE2T acts synergetically with E3 ligases to promote p53 ubiquitination, downregulating p53 protein levels, thereby accelerating CRC proliferation and metastasis [ ]. UBE2Q1 also ubiquitinates p53, inhibiting its transcriptional activity, reducing the expression of target genes such as MDM2 and BCL2, and promoting tumor progression [ ]. Additionally, p53 stability may depend on the antagonistic activity of deubiquitinating enzymes, such as USP10 or USP7, although their roles in CRC require further study. Collectively, these findings indicate that the UPS plays a key role in regulating p53 activity. The development of small-molecule E3 ubiquitin ligase inhibitors can stabilize p53 and promote tumor cell apoptosis. In CRC, as described above, wild-type p53 normally induces proapoptotic factors and cell-cycle inhibitors to inhibit tumor proliferation, modulates the NF-κB pathway to regulate immune-related factors such as IFN-γ and TGF-β [ ], enhances tumor antigen presentation, including MHC-1 expression, and promotes CD8+ T-cell infiltration, thereby enhancing antitumor immunity within the TME [ ]. Conversely, p53 mutation or inhibition of its ubiquitination can activate CAFs, contributing to the formation of immunosuppressive TME. Therefore, targeted inhibition of the UPS by small-molecule inhibitors to stabilize wild-type p53 activity in CRC may play a key role in preserving immune cell function and maintaining antitumor cytokine homeostasis in the TME (Table ). 

## Synergistic effect
Multiple UPS-related pathways act synergistically, forming an integrated network that collectively drives CRC progression and facilitates immune escape.

## Inhibition of T-cell function
The USP14–IDO1 axis directly inhibits the proliferation and function of CD8+ T cells and promotes Tregs through the depletion of Trp and accumulation of Kyn. E3 ubiquitin ligases regulate the stability of PD-L1/PD-1, dynamically enhancing immune checkpoint signaling and inducing T-cell exhaustion. The ZFP91–PP2A–mTOR pathway further inhibits key metabolic pathways such as glycolysis in CD8+ T cells, thereby impairing their effector functions. Collectively, these pathways comprehensively suppress antitumor T-cell responses at multiple levels—metabolic inhibition, coinhibitory signaling, and intracellular metabolic regulation.

## Promotion of immunosuppressive cell expansion and activation
The NF-κB pathway induces the expression of chemokines and cytokines, including CCL2, CCL22, IL-10, and TGF-β, which recruit and promote the infiltration and activation of M2-type TAMs, MDSC, and Tregs. Concurrently, metabolites generated by IDO1 also promote Treg differentiation and function. Additionally, ZFP91 enhances the metabolic adaptability of Tregs by supporting OXPHOS. Together, these pathways synergistically recruit, expand, and activate immunosuppressive cell populations, thereby establishing an inhibitory network in the TME.

## Reduction of tumor immunogenicity and antigen presentation
Activation of the Wnt/β- catenin pathway is significantly associated with decreased infiltration of CD8+ T cells and Th1 cells into the tumor, resulting in immune exclusion. Inactivation of p53 may diminish the expression of MHC class I molecules on tumor cells and reduce the release of DAMPs, thereby weakening immune recognition. Additionally, excessive activation of NF-κB may, in specific contexts, inhibit DC maturation.
The above discussion comprehensively expounds on the core regulatory role of the UPS within the TME of CRC, highlighting the USP14–IDO1 axis, the regulation of the PD-L1/PD-1 axis by E3 ubiquitin ligases, and the key mechanisms of the ZFP91–PP2A–mTOR pathway in promoting CRC immunosuppression. It further explores the potential interactions between these pathways and the UPS. Key molecular targets within these pathways, such as IDO1, PD-L1/PD-1, ZFP91, β-catenin, NF-κB, and p53, are shown to be essential for the CRC onset and progression as well as immune escape, thereby providing a molecular framework for the development of novel targeted therapeutic strategies.

## Current treatment status
Clinical management of CRC primarily relies on multimodal approaches, with surgical resection as the preferred option for early-stage disease, and radiotherapy, chemotherapy, and targeted therapy for advanced cases [ ]. For mCRC, conventional regimens typically combine chemotherapy containing 5-FU with monoclonal antibodies targeting EGFR or VEGF to improve therapeutic efficacy and patient survival. Despite these interventions, approximately 50% of CRC cases remain ultimately incurable [ ]. In recent years, ICIs have shown significant clinical benefits in some cancer types and have been explored in CRC. Specifically, antibodies against PD-1 have shown excellent efficacy in patients with MSI-H or dMMR CRC [ ]. PD-1 monoclonal antibody can significantly prolong the survival of patients with MSI-H mCRC. However, MSS tumors, which constitute the majority of CRC cases, exhibit poor responses to ICIs [ ]. This limited efficacy is attributable to the “cold tumor” immune microenvironment characteristics of MSS CRC, which are marked by insufficient T-cell infiltration and enrichment of immunosuppressive factors [ ]. Studies have shown that the immune escape in MSS CRC involves elevated expression of immunosuppressive molecules (e.g., PD-L1 and IDO1) and increased infiltration of Tregs. Many of these molecules are tightly regulated by the UPS. For example, IDO1 is a metabolic immune checkpoint molecule that catalyzes Trp, generating an immunosuppressive environment. The deubiquitinase USP14 binds to IDO1 and removes its ubiquitin marker, stabilizing the protein and promoting tumor cells to inhibit T-cell function through Trp metabolism [ ]. High USP14 levels lead to excessive IDO1 protein in CRC, enhancing the inhibitory effect on cytotoxic T cells and weakening the effect of immunotherapy [ ]. In addition to modulating immunotherapy responses, the UPS also plays a key role in the response of CRC to conventional therapies. For example, the E3 ubiquitin ligase FBW7 is inactivated in approximately 15–20% of CRC cases [ ]. Wild-type FBW7 mediates the degradation of multiple oncogenic proteins, such as Cyclin E, c-Myc, and MCL1; its mutation results in excessive accumulation of the antiapoptotic protein MCL1, conferring resistance to multitarget kinase inhibitors such as regorafenib [ ]. Studies have confirmed that CRC cells lacking functional FBW7 exhibit reduced sensitivity to regorafenib, whereas restoring MCL1 degradation or directly inhibiting MCL1 can reverse this drug resistance [ ]. Similarly, the E3 ligase UBR5 is often highly expressed in CRC, where it mediates chemotherapy resistance by inhibiting ferroptosis of tumor cells [ ]. Recent studies have found that the UPS also contributes to the regulation of glycolytic pathways in various cells within the TME, particularly immune cells, thereby directly modulating antitumor immunity. ZFP91 is highly expressed in CRC-infiltrating T cells, where it mediates the assembly of PP2A to inhibit the mTORC1 signaling pathway. This inhibits glycolytic capacity in CD4+ and CD8+ T cells, promotes OXPHOS in Tregs, and contributes to an immunosuppressive TME. Targeted inhibition of ZFP91 can improve the antitumor immunity of patients with CRC. Simultaneously, alterations in the UPS in CRC can impact multiple critical pathways within the TME, such as Wnt/β-catenin, NF-κB, and p53 pathways. These findings indicate that UPS-mediated disruption of protein homeostasis is a key mechanism driving CRC drug resistance, proliferation, metastasis, and immune escape. Building on this understanding, clinical trials are exploring strategies that combine UPS-targeting agents with conventional therapies, such as radiotherapy, to enhance antitumor immune responses. Overall, dysregulation of the UPS permeates all stages of CRC progression and contributes to treatment resistance, highlighting UPS-related pathways as promising targets for improving the efficiency of existing therapeutic approaches [ ].

## UPS targets and inhibitors
As the role of the UPS in CRC development and treatment response has gained increasing attention, a growing number of UPS-related targets have been explored for anticancer therapy [ ]. Although drugs that directly inhibit proteasomes, such as Bortezomib, have shown clinical success in multiple myeloma, their efficacy against solid tumors, including CRC, remains limited [ ]. Therefore, research focus has shifted toward more precise regulation of upstream enzymes of UPS, including E1 activating enzymes, E3 ligases, and DUB. One strategy involves inhibiting the activity of the Cullin-RING E3 ligases. For example, the small molecule MLN4924 (Pevonedistat) blocks the ubiquitin ligase function of Cullin proteins by targeting NEDD8-activating enzymes. Previous studies have shown that MLN4924 induces accumulation of multiple Cullin target proteins in CRC cells, triggers cell-cycle arrest and apoptosis, and enhances CRC sensitivity to radiotherapy [ ]. Due to its promising antitumor activity, MLN4924 has progressed to several clinical trials combined with conventional chemotherapy for advanced CRC, evaluating its safety and efficacy [ ]. Additionally, inhibitors directly targeting E3 ligases are under development. For example, SKP2, an F-box E3 ligase that promotes the degradation of tumor suppressors such as p27, has been targeted using computer-aided drug design and structural virtual screening. The resulting small-molecule SKP2 inhibitors exhibit significant antitumor activity in vivo and enhance the sensitivity of chemotherapy in CRC [ ]. Another example is the MDM2–p53 axis. MDM2 is a RING finger E3 ligase that specifically mediates the degradation of p53. Small molecules targeting MDM2, such as Nutlin-3, can block the MDM2–p53 interaction, thereby restoring p53 function and exhibiting the potential to inhibit tumor growth in p53 wild-type CRC [ , ]. However, because p53 is frequently mutated in most CRCs, the clinical applicability of MDM2 inhibitors in this context is limited [ ]. Conversely, inhibitors of DUB have a broader potential across various tumor types. DUB family members, such as USP1, USP7, and USP14, play key roles in tumor immune escape and drug resistance, making them promising therapeutic targets [ ]. Several DUB inhibitors have progressed to clinical evaluation; for example, USP1 inhibitors are being tested in multiple phase I/II trials to overcome drug resistance [ ]. In CRC, USP14 has emerged as a novel target for immunotherapy due to its role in stabilizing IDO1. The small-molecule USP14 inhibitor IU1 effectively reduces IDO1 protein levels in CRC cells, alleviating IDO1-mediated immunosuppression in preclinical models. Importantly, USP14 inhibition does not trigger the common AhR activation side effects observed with IDO1 small-molecule inhibitors, suggesting a potentially safer therapeutic profile. This finding has prompted the consideration of USP14 inhibition as a strategy to improve CRC immunotherapy. In addition to IU1, selective inhibitors targeting other DUBs, such as USP7 and USP15, have shown tumor growth inhibition or treatment-sensitizing effects in cancer models [ ]. Natural products and small molecules can also exert anti-CRC effects by modulating the UPS. For example, the traditional Chinese medicine component demethylzeylasteral has been reported to promote the ubiquitination and degradation of the PD-L1 protein, reducing PD-L1 expression in CRC cells and enhancing immune cell–mediated cytotoxicity against tumor cells [ ]. Furthermore, the Kelch-like protein KLHL22 is a substrate receptor for the Cullin3 E3 ligase complex. Recent studies indicate that aberrant KLHL22 expression can modulate the mTOR signaling pathway. Specifically, KLHL22 activates mTORC1 by promoting the degradation of the mTOR inhibitor DEPDC5, thereby promoting tumor growth in breast and other cancers [ ]. Its role in CRC has also garnered attention. Studies have shown that KLHL22 expression is downregulated in tumor-infiltrating T cells of patients with CRC, and 5-FU chemotherapy further inhibits KLHL22 in these T cells, leading to upregulation of PD-1 [ ]. These findings suggest that KLHL22 may be involved in chemotherapy-related immunosuppression by regulating PD-1 levels in T cells. Therefore, interventions targeting KLHL22—either increasing its activity in T cells or inhibiting its function in tumor cells—may represent a novel therapeutic approach. Regarding the development of ZPF91-targeted inhibitors, small-molecule inhibitor design strategies can be applied. By analyzing the zinc finger domain of ZFP91 or its E3 ubiquitin ligase binding interface, small-molecule compounds can be designed to target and inhibit infiltrating lymphocytes within the TME of CRC. Currently, numerous candidate drugs targeting the UPS are being examined, including E3 ligase inhibitors, DUB inhibitors, and other small-molecule compounds that can modulate the UPS function. These agents offer a versatile toolkit for overcoming CRC therapy resistance and improving therapeutic efficacy.

## Combined treatment strategy
Given that monotherapy often fails to overcome the complex immunosuppressive network in advanced CRC, combination therapies have emerged as a key approach to improving treatment efficacy. In recent years, combination regimens targeting the UPS regulatory mechanism have shown promising results in preclinical studies. Combined immunotherapy: To address the limited sensitivity of MSS CRC to ICIs, researchers have explored combining UPS-targeting agents with ICIs to activate the tumor im mune microenvironment. A representative example is the combination of USP14 inhibition with the PD-1 antibody. Previous studies have shown that USP14 inhibition can alleviate IDO1-mediated immunosuppression, restore T-cell function, and enhance anti–PD-1 efficacy [ ]. In mouse allograft models, USP14 knockdown or treatment with the USP14 inhibitor IU1, combined with PD-1 antibody, significantly enhanced treatment response and more effectively inhibited tumor growth [ ]. Similarly, direct blocking of both the IDO1 pathway and the PD-1 pathway has been explored. In vivo experiments showed that the combination of the IDO1 inhibitor and PD-1 antibody significantly reduced the growth of MSS-type CRC xenografts and significantly increased the infiltration of CD8+ T cells and M1 macrophages within tumor tissues [ ]. Mechanistically, this combination therapy overcomes the immune “cold” state of MSS tumors by reducing tumor cell tolerance to IFN-γ and inducing macrophage polarization toward a proinflammatory phenotype [ ]. These findings suggest that simultaneous targeting of multiple immunosuppressive nodes, such as the PD-1/PD-L1 axis and the IDO1/Trp metabolic pathway, can produce synergistic antitumor effects in MSS CRC. In addition to IDO1, targeting the Treg pathway represents another strategy for overcoming MSS CRC resistance to ICIs, as the enrichment of FoxP3+ Tregs within tumors inhibits Teff function [ ]. Potential approaches include reducing Treg infiltration or impairing their function, such as by targeting Treg-specific chemotactic receptors such as CCR8 or selectively depleting Tregs with low-dose chemotherapeutic agents [ , ]. Clinical studies have also shown that inhibition of Treg-related p38 MAPK signaling reduces Treg-mediated immunosuppression and enhances the antitumor effect of PD-1 antibodies [ ]. p38 MAPK primarily serves as a core regulator of immune exclusion mechanisms, promoting the formation of a non-T cell inflamed tumor microenvironment, leading to the absence of T cell and dendritic cell activation, thereby causing resistance to immune checkpoint inhibitors (such as PD-1/L1). Specifically, p38 activation is associated with pathways such as Wnt/β-catenin, HIF-1, and IL6, inhibiting T cell infiltration; by inhibiting p38 (e.g., using pexmetinib), it can enhance T cell migration, induce a T cell inflammatory phenotype [ ]. A promising future strategy may involve combining UPS inhibitors with drugs or ICIs that regulate Treg, such as USP14 or USP7 inhibitors combined with anti–PD-1 antibodies and anti-Treg therapies to relieve immunosuppression through multiple complementary mechanisms. UPS inhibitors combined with radiotherapy: Radiotherapy is a cornerstone treatment for locally advanced colorectal cancer (CRC), particularly rectal cancer, where it induces DNA damage through ionizing radiation to promote tumor cell death and is frequently combined with chemotherapy to improve local control and surgical resectability. However, radiotherapy can trigger adaptive resistance mechanisms in CRC cells, such as activation of prosurvival transcription factors like NF-κB in response to radiation-induced stress, which fosters cell survival, DNA repair, and overall radioresistance [ ]. UPS inhibitors are expected to enhance the antitumor effects of chemotherapy by modulating key signaling pathways. For example, Bortezomib, a 26S proteasome inhibitor, prevents the degradation of IκB protein, thereby indirectly inhibiting the activity of the transcription factor NF-κB. NF-κB is activated in CRC cells in response to chemotherapy-induced stress, promoting cell survival and drug resistance; blocking NF-κB activation significantly improves tumor sensitivity to chemotherapeutic agents. Therefore, Bortezomib is considered an effective strategy to overcome drug resistance in CRC by stabilizing IκB and inhibiting NF-κB [ , ], thereby enhancing the efficacy of 5-FU and other chemotherapy drugs [ ]. However, modern research has shown that the response rate of first-generation proteasome inhibitors, such as Bortezomib, as single agents in CRC is relatively low. Conversely, newer reversible and irreversible inhibitors, such as Carfilzomib and Isatuzumab, show greater potential when used synergistically with chemotherapy or targeted therapies. Carfilzomib, a second-generation proteasome inhibitor, exhibits distinct mechanisms of action and pharmacokinetic properties compared with Bortezomib [ ]. Carfilzomib is an irreversible proteasome inhibitor that primarily targets the β5 subunit of the 20S proteasome, thereby interfering with the protein degradation process within the cell and inducing cell-cycle arrest and apoptosis [ ]. Studies have shown that Carfilzomib exerts antitumor activity in various tumor cell types, including those resistant to Bortezomib [ ]. In vitro experiments further show that Carfilzomib promotes endoplasmic reticulum stress and apoptosis in CRC cells resistant to Cetuximab [ ]. Additionally, Carfilzomib has shown preclinical efficacy in BRAF-mutated CRC models [ ]. In these models, Carfilzomib-induced endoplasmic reticulum stress enhances the activation of immune-stimulating danger signals and increases the expression of immunogenic cell death markers [ ]. Moreover, in BRAF-mutated murine CRC, Carfilzomib promotes remodeling of the immune microenvironment by recruiting activated cytotoxic T cells and NK cells, while weakening Tregs [ ]. Despite these promising findings, the clinical application of Carfilzomib in CRC remains challenging. Currently, published clinical trial data on the use of Carfilzomib or Isatuzumab in combination with other drugs for CRC are limited. However, some studies suggest that combining proteasome inhibitors with chemotherapy or targeted therapies may yield synergistic effects. For example, Carfilzomib combined with Cetuximab has been shown to overcome resistance to Cetuximab in CRC cells [ ]. Additionally, Carfilzomib combined with BRAF inhibitors may offer therapeutic benefits in patients with BRAF-mutated CRC. In addition to proteasome inhibitors, new UPS-targeting drugs such as NEDD8-activating enzyme inhibitors, including MLN4924 (Pevonedistat), show promising potential in combination with chemotherapy. MLN4924 blocks E3 ligase–mediated substrate degradation by inhibiting the NEDD8 modification of Cullin-RING E3 ligases, thereby inducing DNA replication stress, extensive DNA damage response (DDR), abnormal cell-cycle arrest, and apoptosis. In CRC cell lines, MLN4924 alone induces a significant DDR response and cell-cycle disruption, further enhancing oxaliplatin-induced DNA damage accumulation, G2-phase arrest, and apoptosis. Mechanistic studies have shown that MLN4924 increases phosphorylation of the checkpoint kinase Chk2, while the levels of the cell-cycle inhibitor p21 and the tumor suppressor protein p53 remain significantly unchanged, indicating that its sensitizing effect is primarily associated with DDR pathway activation [ ]. A study conducted at Fudan University showed that MLN4924 significantly improved the radiosensitivity of CRC cells, with a sensitization ratio of 1.3–1.6 across different cell lines [ ]. Collectively, MLN4924 appears to enhance the sensitivity of CRC cells to oxaliplatin by amplifying DNA damage signaling (particularly through p-Chk2–mediated mechanisms), thereby promoting G2-phase arrest and apoptosis [ ]. These findings suggest that combining MLN4924 with oxaliplatin-based chemotherapy may represent a potential strategy to improve CRC treatment outcomes. In future studies, regulation of the mTORC1, Wnt/β-catenin, NF-κB, and p53 signaling pathways in lymphocytes and tumor cells within the TME through UPS targeting should be explored in patients with CRC. When combined with conventional therapies, gene sequencing can facilitate the development of personalized treatment strategies. In parallel, the advancement of small-molecule inhibitor design is expected to improve drug specificity, minimize adverse effects, and provide promising opportunities to improve treatment outcomes and prognosis in patients with CRC (Table ). 

## Prospects and challenges
A deeper understanding of UPS-related mechanisms has opened new opportunities for CRC treatment, although significant challenges remain. One major advance is the rapid development of targeted protein degradation technologies, which enable indirect targeting of nondrug-resistant carcinogenic proteins. PROTAC is a class of bifunctional small molecules that bind to a target protein at one end and recruit an E3 ligase at the other end, leading to ubiquitination and subsequent proteasomal degradation of the target protein [ ]. By selectively eliminating proteins that cannot be effectively inhibited by conventional compounds, PROTAC represents a revolutionary strategy in drug discovery and development [ ]. In CRC and tumor immunology, PROTAC has been engineered to degrade key immunomodulatory proteins. For example, PROTAC molecules targeting PD-L1 have been reported to induce its degradation in tumor cells, thereby enhancing T-cell activity [ ]. Similarly, nanoparticle-delivered PROTAC has been developed to degrade tumorigenic and drug resistance–related proteins, such as β-catenin and STAT3, with observed inhibition of tumor growth [ ]. Another example is SHP2, a tyrosine phosphatase that serves as a central signaling node in tumor immune escape. Recently, PROTAC targeting SHP2 was reported to successfully modulate multiple pathways such as PD-1/PD-L1 and RAS/MAPK, thereby enhancing antitumor immune responses [ ]. Collectively, these findings highlight the promising role of PROTAC in the comprehensive treatment of CRC. Another emerging approach is the development of “molecular glue” degradation agents. Unlike the bifunctional structure of PROTAC, “molecular glues” are monofunctional small molecules that promote specific protein degradation by stabilizing unconventional interactions between an E3 ligase and its substrate [ ]. A classic example is thalidomide and its derivatives, which act as “molecular glues” by co-opting the E3 ligase CRBN to recruit and degrade the pathogenic transcription factors IKZF1 and IKZF3, thereby exerting efficacy in hematological tumors. “molecular glue” is regarded as a new direction in the “post-PROTAC era” [ , ]. Their relatively simple structure, smaller molecular weights, favorable pharmacokinetic properties, and ability to target protein–protein interfaces that are challenging for PROTAC make them attractive candidates for further development, particularly when supported by artificial intelligence–assisted drug screening. With the development of AI, its application in the discovery of UPS-targeted drugs has become increasingly prominent. Deep learning and virtual screening approaches can accelerate the identification of potential small-molecule modulators. For example, SKP2 E3 ligase inhibitors were identified through structure-based computational screening and subsequently validated in in vivo activity [ ]. AI may also be applied in the future to optimize the linker arm and conformation design of PROTAC, thereby improving cell membrane permeability and degradation efficiency [ , , ]. Overall, AI technology is expected to shorten the development cycle of UPS-targeted drugs and increase the likelihood of identifying effective candidates. Despite these prospects, several challenges remain. The UPS pathway is highly complex, and its effects vary depending on specific substrates and cell types. Direct intervention may result in off-target effects and toxicities [ ]. Improving the selectivity of UPS-targeted drugs while preserving normal protein homeostasis is therefore a critical objective. Furthermore, many UPS inhibitors remain in preclinical or early clinical stages, and their actual efficacy and safety are not yet supported by sufficient experimental evidence [ ]. Acquired tumor tolerance may also emerge; for example, prolonged inhibition of a DUB or E3 ligase may allow tumor cells to adapt through alternative signaling pathways. Therefore, rational drug combination and dynamic monitoring strategies are essential. When developing UPS inhibitors for CRC, their systemic immune function must be carefully considered. Excessive targeting may impair immune cell activity, potentially inducing autoimmune diseases, while also altering metabolic pathways regulated by the UPS in normal tissues and cells. Such events could lead to unintended side effects. With the continuous advancement of gene sequencing and molecular targeting technologies, many of these limitations may eventually be overcome. In summary, the regulation mechanism of the UPS provides a wide range of therapeutic targets and strategies for CRC. These include the use of UPS modulators to enhance the effectiveness of conventional treatments, such as surgery, radiotherapy, and chemotherapy; the combination of UPS-targeted agents with immunotherapy to improve the response rate in patients with MSS; the development of cutting-edge approaches such as PROTAC and “molecular glues,” which show great potential to transform basic mechanism research into clinical application [ , , ]. As more clinical trials are conducted and AI increasingly supports drug discovery and optimization, UPS-targeted therapies are expected to become a key strategy for improving prognosis, overcoming drug resistance, and establishing a central role in the future comprehensive management of tumors.

## Conclusions
This study highlights the critical role of the UPS in shaping the TME of CRC, particularly in immunosuppression and tumor progression. The UPS was shown to influence the immune environment of CRC by regulating the stability and degradation of proteins. Key enzymes, such as the DUB USP14 and several E3 ubiquitin ligases, are central in sustaining the immunosuppressive state of CRC. For example, USP14 accelerates Trp metabolism by stabilizing the enzyme IDO1, thereby enhancing immune tolerance. Similarly, E3 ligases facilitate tumor immune escape by regulating immune checkpoint molecules such as PD-L1. Collectively, these findings underscore the pivotal role of the UPS in coordinating immunosuppressive mechanisms, which remain a major barrier to effective CRC immunotherapy. From a therapeutic perspective, targeting the UPS offers promising opportunities. For example, inhibition of USP14 may destabilize IDO1, thereby reducing immunosuppression and improving the efficacy of ICIs (e.g., anti–PD-1). Similarly, promoting the degradation of PD-L1 through E3 ligase modulation may enhance antitumor immunity. Preclinical studies indicate that combining UPS inhibitors with existing immunotherapies can significantly improve T-cell activity and inhibit tumor growth, offering hope for overcoming current treatment limitations. Looking ahead, it is essential to further explore the complex interaction between the UPS and the TME. This includes identifying additional UPS components involved in immune regulation, validating the clinical efficacy of UPS inhibitors, and designing strategies to integrate these inhibitors into comprehensive treatment regimens. Such efforts are aimed at providing patients with CRC with more effective and personalized treatment options, thereby significantly improving prognosis. In summary, this study not only advances our understanding of the biological mechanisms underlying CRC but also provides valuable insights for the development of innovative treatment methods. Harnessing the regulatory capacity of the UPS within the TME may open novel anticancer pathways and ultimately improve survival outcomes for patients with CRC.
