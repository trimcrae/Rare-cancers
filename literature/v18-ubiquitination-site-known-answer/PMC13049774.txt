## Introduction
The control of chromatin architecture, maintenance of genomic integrity, regulation of gene expression, and regulation of cellular fate are essential for normal physiology and disease prevention. Chromatin-modifying proteins are essential for the coordination of DNA accessibility, gene expression, and the cellular response to stress [ ]. Mortality factor 4-like 1 (MORF4L1, also known as Mortality factor-Related Gene 15 - MRG15) has become an important chromatin-associated protein that connects these processes [ , ]. MORF4L1 was first identified as a gene that reverses the immortal phenotype in human cells [ ]. The discovery established MORF4L1 as a member of a novel family of chromodomain-containing transcription factor-like proteins [ ], thereby paving the way for research on its involvement in chromatin regulation, DNA damage response, and cellular senescence. Despite being a member of the MORF4 protein family and sharing high sequence homology with its paralogs, MORF4L1 exhibits distinct biological activities [ , ]. MORF4L1 deficiency shows embryonic lethality in mice [ ], which is likely due to impaired cell proliferation and delayed development of many organs and tissues. Genome-wide association studies have identified single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) near the MORF4L1 locus that correlate with lipid traits, type 2 diabetes, and coronary artery disease, suggesting a possible genetic regulatory mechanism of MORF4L1 [ , ]. The lethal phenotype of MORF4L1-deficient mice, as well as its association with disease traits, indicates an important role of MORF4L1 in regulating biological processes, pathophysiology, and disease in humans.
This review aims to provide insights into the structure and protein stability of MORF4L1 and to summarize its multiple roles in chromatin remodeling, DNA damage repair, cellular senescence, lipid metabolism, and its emerging relevance in cardiometabolic disease. We propose that MORF4L1’s intricate mechanisms demand a deeper understanding, and MORF4L1 holds promise as a potential therapeutic target for ameliorating vascular and metabolic dysfunction in cardiometabolic diseases.

2.

## MORF4L1 structure and ubiquitination- and acetylationregulated protein stability
2.1.

## MORF4L1 protein isoforms
The dominant protein isoform of both mouse and human MORF4L1 has 323 amino acids ( ). Mouse MORF4L1 has two isoforms resulting from alternative splicing [ ]. The long isoform of mouse MORF4L1 has a 39-amino-acid insertion in the chromodomain, located immediately after the lysine residue at position 51, which may disrupt the chromodomain function [ ]. Indeed, chromatin immunoprecipitation-sequencing revealed that the long isoform of MORF4L1 had a much smaller number of binding sites in the genome, likely reflecting its looser interaction with chromatin [ ]. The short isoform contains 323 amino acids that are 100% identical to the most abundant human MORF4L1 protein isoform. Both the long and short isoforms of mouse MORF4L1 localize to the nucleus of cells, and their expression varies in different mouse tissues (supplementary Fig. 4D in [ ]). Human MORF4L1 has three isoforms ( ). Isoform 1 has 323 amino acids; isoform 2 has 362 amino acids with a 39-amino-acid insertion, the same as the long isoform of mouse MORF4L1; isoform 3 has 235 amino acids, lacking 88 amino acids at the amino-terminus of isoform 1. Transcriptome analysis revealed that the most abundantly expressed transcript variant encodes protein isoform with 323 amino acids in human and mouse aortic endothelial cells and vascular smooth muscle cells ( ). The amino acid sequences of homologs of human MORF4L1 isoform 1 are highly conserved from yeast to mammals [ ].

2.2.

## MORF4L1 structure and functional domains
MORF4L1 protein homologs have two functional domains. For example, human MORF4L1 protein isoform 1 has an N-terminal chromodomain (11–66; FQEGERVLCFHG—WVPESRVLKYV) and a C-terminal MRG domain (151–323; an ATP/GTP binding region followed by a helix-loop-helix and a leucine zipper) ( , ) [ , – ]]; it also has a bipartite nuclear localization signal (96–114; KKTSGLQQKNVEVKTKKNK) flanked by phosphorylation sites, mediating its nuclear localization. The crystal structure of the chromodomain (PDB: 2F5K) of human MORF4L1 was determined at 2.2 Å resolution [ ]. It reveals that the N-terminal chromodomain consists of a β-barrel core and a long α-helix. The β-barrel core contains a hydrophobic pocket formed by three conserved aromatic residues Tyr26, Tyr46, and Trp49 as a potential binding site for a modified histone tail residue [ ]. It recognizes and binds to tri-methylated lysine 36 on histone H3 (H3K36me3) in chromatin, which exhibits selectivity against other methylation sites such as H3K4, H3K9, and H3K27. The chromodomain is crucial for recruiting MORF4L1 to transcribed gene bodies. It is involved in changing chromatin structure and thereby participating in both negative and positive transcriptional control in isogenic K562 cells, and it is also a site for protein-protein interaction [ ]. The MRG domain crystal structures PDB: 2F5J [ ] and PDB: 2AQL [ ], which were determined at 2.20 Å and 2.30 Å resolution, reveal an architecture dominated by six α-helices. The MRG domain has two discrete interaction surfaces: one is plastic, and the other is rigid. The former interaction surface is involved in molecular mimicry, and the latter is engaged in the conserved FxLP motif, a sequence motif shared by most high-affinity interactors [ , ]. This domain mediates protein-protein interactions through a shallow hydrophobic pocket formed by residues Ile160, Leu168, Val169, Trp172, Tyr235, Val268, and Arg269. These residues interact with partner proteins such as PAM14 through primarily hydrophobic interactions, which reveal insights into the potential biological function of MRG15 in transcription regulation and chromatin remodeling [ ]. More recently, the overall Cryo-EM structure of human MORF4L1 long isoform (362 amino acids) has been determined at 3.4Å resolution (PDB: 8C60) as a recombinant version of the human SIN3B complex, composed of the subunits SIN3B isoform 2, HDAC2, PHF12 and MORF4L1, expressed in the baculovirus/insect cell system [ ]. It provides insights into the spatial arrangement of both domains and the flexible linker region. This full-length structure is valuable for understanding how the chromodomain and MRG domain cooperate in mediating MORF4L1’s diverse functions in different cellular contexts. The structural features of MORF4L1, particularly the chromodomain and MRG domain, enable its association with multiple protein complexes involved in diverse cellular processes like chromatin modification, transcriptional regulation, alternative splicing, and DNA repair [ , ]. While MORF4L1 has been implicated in several biological functions, the structural changes that contribute to its specific roles are not well elucidated.

2.3.

## In-silico prediction studies and small molecule targeting
In-silico studies have demonstrated that MORF4L1 possesses binding pockets which could be targeted by small molecules. In a study of epithelial cell death in a mouse model of pneumonia, Zou et al. performed virtual screening of approximately 6000 small molecules from a chemical library against the MORF4L1 crystal structure [ ]. This analysis identified potential sites for chemical binding within the MRG domain, which establishes the theory that MORF4L1 can be targeted by small molecules. Among all the screened 6000 compounds, argatroban, which is an FDA-approved thrombin inhibitor, showed interaction with the binding pocket, with key residues Gly220 and Thr277 within the MRG domain being essential for drug interactions. Argatroban inhibits MORF4L1-associated histone H4 acetylation (H4K12 and H4K16) and reduces MORF4L1-mediated cytotoxicity in cellular models of pneumonia. This finding suggests that MORF4L1 could be targeted by small molecules for protein degradation through well-defined binding pockets which will achieve selective elimination in the treatment of diseases.

2.4.

## MORF4L1 protein homeostasis regulated by ubiquitination and acetylation
MORF4L1 is a short-lived protein with a half-life of ~30 min in lung epithelial cells [ ]. Protein post-translational modifications, including neddylation, ubiquitination, and acetylation, regulate its protein stability. Neddylation inhibition induces MORF4L1 expression in U2OS cells [ ]. Proteasome inhibition also induces MORF4L1 expression in HEK293T cells [ ]. Interestingly, proteasome inhibition drastically decreases the ubiquitylation sites at K104 and K187 in MORF4L1. Emerging evidence indicates that MORF4L1 is ubiquitinated by cullin4-RING E3 ligase (CRL4). MORF4L1 interacts with cullin 4B [ ] and cereblon [ ], the scaffold protein and the substrate receptor of CRL4, respectively. The small-molecule modulator CC-885, a derivative of thalidomide, promotes CRL4-dependent substrate ubiquitination and degradation in cancer cell lines [ ]. CC-885 enhances the MORF4L1-cereblon interaction, inducing dose- and time-dependent degradation of MORF4L1 protein that is proteasome- and neddylation-dependent and blocked by cereblon deficiency. These results demonstrate that CRL4 is a key ubiquitin E3 ligase that mediates MORF4L1 ubiquitination and degradation. While the majority of evidence points to a predominantly nuclear localization of MORF4L1 directed by the bipartite nuclear localization signal, some studies in HeLa cells and hepatocytes have suggested that it may also have cytoplasmic functions [ , ]. Experimental evidence suggests that MORF4L1 is degraded in the cytoplasm because nuclear retention of the protein extends its lifespan in cells [ ]. MORF4L1 can be acetylated in murine lung epithelial cells under normal culture conditions, and HDAC2-dependent deacetylation of MORF4L1 enhances MORF4L1 homodimerization [ ]. MORF4L1 acetylation protects it from Fbxl18-mediated degradation in murine lung epithelium [ ]. Consistent with this result, a study in non-alcoholic steatohepatitis (NASH) conditions in murine NASH models with CRISPR-mediated MORF4L1 knockdown shows that inflammatory cytokines stabilize MORF4L1 levels through increased acetylation [ ]. Because MORF4L1 ubiquitination can be blocked by its acetylation, it is speculated that nutritional status and metabolites can control MORF4L1 protein homeostasis. Mechanistic insights into MORF4L1 protein homeostasis, such as the cellular localization of degradation and the regulation by nutritional status, will improve our understanding of MORF4L1 in pathophysiology and disease.

3.

## MORF4L1 in chromatin remodeling
MORF4L1 regulates chromatin remodeling by forming nuclear protein complexes [ , ]. It is present in the NuA4/TIP60 histone acetyltransferase (HAT) complex [ , ], the Sin3A/Sin3B histone deacetylase (HDAC) complex [ , ] (Sin3B also functions as a demethylase), and the Ash1/ASH1L histone methyltransferase [ – ]. Thus, MORF4L1 controls gene expression by changing histone modification.
3.1.

## MORF4L1 in histone methylation
MORF4L1 is important in histone methylation via interaction with Ash1/ASH1L (Absent, Small, or Homeotic discs 1-Like) histone methyltransferase. MORF4L1 is a subunit of the Ash1 complex in Drosophila [ ]. The Ash1 complex has histone methyltransferase activity that catalyzes mono- and dimethylation of lysine 36 of histone H3 (H3K36). This inhibits trimethylation of lysine 27 on the same histone H3 by the Polycomb repressive complexes and produces a mark of transcriptionally active chromatin. Thus, MORF4L1 stimulates the histone methyltransferase activity of the Ash1 complex and facilitates Ash1-mediated H3K36me2 deposition. The disruption of the interaction between MORF4L1 and the Ash1 complex impairs its function to antagonize gene silencing induced by the Polycomb repressive complexes, leading to developmental problems in Drosophila [ ]. MORF4L1 is also involved in histone demethylation through its interaction with a histone H3K4-specific demethylase (KDM5A, also known as RBP2 or JARID1A) [ ].
While the molecular basis by which ASH1L/Ash1 is activated upon MORF4L1 binding has been studied, the precise mechanism remains unclear. ASH1L, the human homolog of Ash1, contains a hydrophobic motif (FQLP/FxLP) that mediates its binding to the MRG domain of MORF4L1 [ , ]. It was thought that this interaction causes a conformational change of ASH1L that displaces an autoinhibitory loop from the substrate binding pocket of ASH1L, which activates its histone methyltransferase activity. However, a recent study does not support the allosteric mechanism of releasing autoinhibited conformation via interaction with MORF4L1 [ ]. The authors found that MORF4L1-ASH1L interaction has no impact on the conformation of the autoinhibitory loop or the S-adenosylmethionine cofactor binding site of ASH1L in the absence of nucleosome binding mediated by MORF4L1 chromodomain. The study indicates that MORF4L1 enhances ASH1L activity via an uncharacterized mechanism. One possible mechanism is that MORF4L1 functions as an adapter protein linking ASH1L with nucleosomes, thus enhancing its catalytic activity [ ].

3.2.

## MORF4L1 in histone acetylation
MORF4L1 is also important in regulating histone acetylation. Early studies have suggested that MORF4L1 activates or represses gene expression through its interaction with histone acetyltransferase or deacetylase, respectively [ , ]. For example, MORF4L1 was identified as a subunit of NuA4/TIP60-containing histone acetyltransferase complex [ , , ]. Additionally, MORF4L1 overexpression induces CDC2 mRNA expression in HeLa cells by introducing histone H4 acetylation at the CDC2 gene promoter [ ]. Another recent study found that MORF4L1 plays a crucial role in neonatal cardiac regeneration and cardiomyocyte proliferation by recruiting NuA4/TIP60, a histone acetyltransferase, to the enhancer region of Ccnd1. This induces Cyclin D1 expression, progressing the cardiomyocyte from G1 to S phase [ ]. MORF4L1 was also linked to histone deacetylation when its homolog (Alp13) was identified as a protein component of the Sin3 complex in yeast [ ]. In addition, biochemical assays revealed that MORF4L1 interacts with the mammalian histone deacetylase complexes, including Sin3A [ ] and Sin3B [ ]. The MORF4L1-Sin3B complex localizes at genomic loci downstream of the transcription start sites in HEK293T cells and HeLa cells [ ]. Inactivation of the complex promotes gene transcription, suggesting that the complex represses gene transcription. These interactions can fine-tune gene expression downstream of transcription start sites in constitutively active genes [ ]. Genome-wide MORF4L1 binding sites have been revealed by chromatin immunoprecipitation-sequencing in the liver of mice [ ], so an integrative analysis of MORF4L1 genome-wide binding sites and MORF4L1-regulated transcriptome will improve our understanding of MORF4L1 in regulating chromatin remodeling, gene expression, and cellular processes.

4.

## MORF4L1 in DNA damage response and cellular senescence
4.1.

## MORF4L1 in DNA damage response
MORF4L1 is required for effective DNA damage repair. MORF4L1-deficient neural stem cells exhibit severe defects in DNA damage response following ionizing radiation [ ]. This was also observed in mouse embryonic fibroblasts [ ]. In addition, MORF4L1-deficient U2OS and MCF7 cells had reduced efficiency for homology-directed DNA repair of DNA inter-strand crosslinking induced by mitomycin C [ ]. Mechanistically, MORF4L1 knockdown reduces the recruitment of proteins such as BRCA2 and RAD51, important for DNA double-strand break damage repair, to sites of DNA damage [ ]. The recruitment is likely mediated via the interaction between MORF4L1 and a protein named partner and localizer of BRCA2 (PALB2) [ , ]. PALB2 interacts with the MRG domain of MORF4L1 [ ]. PALB2 plays a pivotal role in the repair of DNA double-strand breaks by physically linking BRCA1 and BRCA2 at sites of DNA damage [ – ]. Moreover, PALB2 associates with undamaged genomic loci through its interaction with MORF4L1 to ensure an immediate response to genotoxic and DNA replicative stress during DNA replication in U2OS cells or immortalized fibroblasts [ ]. MORF4L1 is also involved in nucleotide excision repair by activating ASH1L, a histone methyltransferase [ ]. The resultant histone epigenetic mark H3K4me3 primes chromatin for the relocation of a DNA damage sensor (XPC) from native chromatin to damaged DNA sites. In addition, MORF4L1-ASH1L complex recruits histone chaperone proteins to damaged DNA sites, which allows the verification of DNA damage such as cyclobutane pyrimidine dimers for nucleotide excision. Further studies are required to delineate the molecular basis by which MORF4L1 regulates the DNA damage response and maintains genome stability upon exposure to other genotoxic stressors in other cell types or tissues, such as the vascular endothelium.

4.2.

## MORF4L1 in cellular senescence
Several studies have demonstrated that MORF4L1 is important in regulating cellular senescence. MORF4L1 expression is reduced upon cellular senescence or starvation, while increased by serum stimulation in human fibroblast cells [ ]. In addition, loss of MORF4L1 limits cell proliferation by increasing the expression of p21 in neural stem cells [ ]. These findings suggest a role of MORF4L1 in regulating cellular senescence. Indeed, MORF4L1 deficiency accelerates cellular senescence in human mesenchymal stem cells [ ], supported by increased markers of senescence and by integrative analysis of MORF4L1-regulated transcriptome and MORF4L1 genome-wide binding. MORF4L1 forms biomolecular condensates through liquid-liquid phase separation in vitro and in human mesenchymal stem cells, which are required for the protective role of MORF4L1 in cellular senescence [ ] ( ). In contrast, a large-scale CRISPR knockout screening for genes essential for cellular senescence revealed that MORF4L1 deficiency allows human fibroblasts to bypass cellular senescence [ ]. What accounts for the conflicting results remains unknown. A recent study revealed that the expression of the long isoform of mouse MORF4L1 (MORF4L1-L) is increased during replicative senescence in mouse embryonic stem cells [ ]. Interestingly, loss- and gain-of-function studies demonstrate that MORF4L1-L promotes cellular senescence, arresting cells at G2/M phase, while the opposite is true of the short isoform of MORF4L1. MORF4L1 protein isoforms seem to have different roles in cellular senescence, which could potentially explain the above conflicting results. We speculate that MORF4L1 controls cellular senescence by regulating chromatin modification and subsequent gene expression, which warrants further studies.

5.

## MORF4L1 in cardiometabolic disease
5.1.

## MORF4L1 in cardiac development and cardiovascular disease
MORF4L1 is ubiquitously expressed in different cell types of human coronary arteries ( ), and emerging evidence suggests that MORF4L1 plays an important role in cardiovascular disease. MORF4L1 plays a critical role in normal cardiac development through its involvement in chromatin remodeling and transcriptional regulation [ , ], for example, by mediating the role of Ash1. Dimethylation of H3K36 by methyltransferase Ash1 is essential for healthy heart function in Drosophila [ ]. Ash1 silencing results in the disorganization of the cardiac actin filaments, reduced cardiac myofibril density, increased deposition of type IV collagen, and fibrosis in the heart, effects mediated by MORF4L1. In addition, a study in mice has demonstrated that MORF4L1 is critical for proper heart regeneration during embryogenesis and plays an important role in regulating cardiomyocyte proliferation. MORF4L1 forms an activator complex with TIP60, p300, and RNA polymerase II to facilitate histone acetylation at the Ccnd1 enhancer region, and regulatory T cells induce MORF4L1 expression to promote cardiomyocyte proliferation through paracrine signaling [ ]. Alternative splicing of MORF4L1 generates two isoforms with distinct roles in cardiomyocyte proliferation, senescence, and postnatal cardiac repair [ ]. The long isoform with 362 amino acids increases during postnatal cardiac development, which is linked to reduced regenerative capacity. It has reduced affinity for acetylated histone H4, weakens CDK1 regulation, induces G2/M arrest, and promotes cardiomyocyte senescence. Targeted knockout of the long isoform in mice improves cardiac repair and regeneration following myocardial ischemia–reperfusion injury, underscoring the pathogenic role of this isoform in limiting cardiomyocyte cell-cycle re-entry and suggesting that modulation of MORF4L1 splicing may enhance myocardial recovery after infarction [ ].

5.2.

## MORF4L1 in lipid metabolism and fatty-liver disease
MORF4L1 is involved in the regulation of lipid metabolism [ , ] ( ). Analysis of genes associated with genome-wide binding sites of MORF4L1 revealed 180 genes involved in the lipid-biosynthesis process [ ]. Among them, the liver receptor homolog-1 (LRH-1) interacts with MORF4L1 and mediates the recruitment of MORF4L1 to the genomic loci of many genes involved in lipid metabolism. Moreover, CRISPR-mediated MORF4L1 silencing attenuates liver steatosis in mice. The phenotypic changes are associated with the reduction of genes involved in lipid regulation processes, as well as the reduction of triglyceride and cholesterol levels in the blood. These results demonstrate that MORF4L1 plays an important role in lipid metabolism in the liver, suggesting that the pharmacological inhibition of MORF4L1 could serve as a promising therapeutic strategy to treat diet-induced liver metabolic disease [ ].
Further, MORF4L1 contributes to the progression of fatty liver to metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis (MASH) [ ] ( ). MORF4L1 protein expression is elevated in the livers of mice and patients with MASH due to its increased acetylation. MORF4L1 depletion attenuates hepatic steatosis, fibrosis, and inflammation. Mechanistically, MORF4L1 promotes the degradation of TUFM (mitochondrial Tu translation elongation factor), which in turn attenuates mitophagy in fatty livers. Although MORF4L1 is highly expressed in the nucleus, these results demonstrate that MORF4L1 in other cellular compartments is also important in regulating metabolism. Several questions arise from the study. What is the physiological function of such a mechanism in hepatocytes? What enzyme acetylates MORF4L1 in fatty livers? Could it simply result from the reduced activity of deacetylases such as SIRT1 and SIRT3 [ , ]? Is acetylated MORF4L1 highly expressed in the cytosol and associated with the mitochondria? Answers to these questions will improve our understanding of MORF4L1 in lipid metabolism and MASH.

6.

## Summary, perspective, and conclusion
Since MORF4L1 was first identified as a chromatin-associated protein, substantial work has established it as a multifunctional regulator involved in epigenetics [ ], DNA damage response [ , , ], cellular senescence [ , , , , ], lipid metabolism [ , ], and human diseases [ , , ]. Future research may focus on the investigation of MORF4L1’s post-translational regulation, genetic studies to establish disease directionality of MORF4L1 gene variations, and development of therapeutic approaches targeting MORF4L1.
6.1.

## MORF4L1 protein homeostasis
MORF4L1 protein homeostasis is controlled by post-translational modifications, particularly ubiquitination, which may function as a ubiquitin molecular sink affecting protein homeostasis during stress responses. The identity of the E3 ubiquitin ligase(s) targeting MORF4L1, the lysine residues involved in its ubiquitination and degradation, and the specific triggers that prompt its proteasomal turnover are still unknown. It is also unclear whether the ubiquitination machinery differs across cell types, particularly between proliferative and quiescent cells, or between endothelial and non-endothelial cells. As MORF4L1 appears to be continuously degraded under basal conditions, potentially to prevent deleterious effects such as unscheduled chromatin opening, inappropriate cell cycle arrest, or protein degradation, understanding when and where MORF4L1 accumulates in disease states is critical for defining therapeutic windows.

6.2.

## MORF4L1 gene variation and human disease
Genome-wide association studies have identified that MORF4L1 gene variations are associated with cardiovascular disease [ , , , ] ( ). Furthermore, expression quantitative trait loci (eQTL) analysis indicates that the risk alleles (A and C) are associated with increased MORF4L1 expression in aortas ( ), implicating the potential role of MORF4L1 in cardiovascular disease. The SNPs near or within the MORF4L1 locus may influence its expression, potentially contributing to individual susceptibility to cardiovascular disease and metabolic disorders. However, direct evidence of SNP-mediated regulation in humans is still lacking and requires further investigation. The functional direction of these SNPs on MORF4L1 expression and the contribution of cell type remain to be determined. The identification of true loss-of-function or gain-of-function MORF4L1 variants would aid in addressing this knowledge gap.

6.3.

## Development of therapeutic approaches targeting MORF4L1
While there is much yet to discover about MORF4L1, its potential as a therapeutic target is becoming more evident. highlights the variety of approaches that have been used to target MORF4L1 in previous studies. Although MORF4L1 deletion is lethal in mice, several studies suggest that MORF4L1 inhibition or silencing is beneficial under pathological conditions. While small molecule inhibitors have been successfully used to inhibit MORF4L1, therapeutic development using chemically modified antisense oligonucleotides (ASOs) remains extensively unexplored. ASOs are designed to bind specific mRNA, inhibiting the production of proteins that, for example, contribute to atherosclerosis and heart failure. Studies have shown that ASOs are being actively used for cardiovascular diseases, primarily targeting lipid metabolism, such as reducing Lipoprotein(a) and LDL-cholesterol [ ]. Given the clinical success of ASO therapies for other targets and accumulating evidence of MORF4L1’s role in disease pathogenesis, ASO-based strategies need investigation as a viable approach for MORF4L1 inhibition in disease context.
In conclusion, MORF4L1 links chromatin biology, cellular senescence, lipid metabolism, and cardiometabolic pathophysiology. MORF4L1’s intricate mechanisms and regulatory network demand further studies in vascular biology, which will enhance our understanding of vascular homeostasis in cardiometabolic disease. As a regulatory protein at the interface of chromatin biology, cellular senescence, and lipid metabolism, MORF4L1 holds promise as a potential therapeutic target for ameliorating vascular and metabolic dysfunction in cardiometabolic diseases. Future research should be employed on conditional knockout models to establish tissue-specific, isoform-specific, and disease-specific functions in vivo.
