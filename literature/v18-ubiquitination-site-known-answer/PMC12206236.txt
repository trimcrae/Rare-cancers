## Abstract
Essential transcription and cell cycle progression controllers are CDKs, whose dysregulation is a defining trait of many human cancers. CDKs have grown to be very crucial therapeutic targets in cancer. Although traditional CDK inhibitors have demonstrated therapeutic efficacy, they frequently encounter limitations due to resistance mechanisms and off-target effects. Recent developments in targeted protein degradation, like proteolysis-targeting chimeras (PROTACs) and molecular glues, offer creative ways to destroy CDK proteins specifically. These techniques reduce scaffolding activities and slow down kinase activity, hence more completely blocking oncogenic CDK signaling. This paper highlights the clinical and preclinical developments of PROTACs and molecular glues, investigates the current CDK-targeting therapeutic landscape, and studies the molecular basis of CDK dysregulation in cancer. We also address their benefits over conventional inhibitors, current issues, and possibilities for inclusion into precision oncology. These new approaches taken together represent a change in CDK-targeted cancer therapy.

## Background
Comprising consecutive phases—G1, S, G2, and M—the cell cycle is a vital process controlling cell development and division that guarantees exact DNA replication and mitosis. CDKs and their matching cyclin subunits, which are activated by external signals—especially growth factor-initiated signaling pathways influencing particular CDK-cyclin complexes—meticulously control eukaryotic cells’ advancement through these stages 
(Fig. ). Furthermore, DNA damage-induced cell cycle checkpoints could lower CDK activity to maintain genomic integrity .
 Disordered control of the cell cycle is a characteristic of cancer, often caused by genetic abnormalities in CDKs, cyclins, or upstream signaling elements – . This imbalance leads to uncontrolled cell growth and tumor progression. As a result, CDKs have become interesting therapeutic targets, which has prompted the development of several small molecules CDK inhibitors to treat human malignancies .
Creative protein degradation techniques such as proteolysis-targeting chimeras (PROTACs) and molecular glues provide different ways to inhibit CDKs and other oncogenic proteins. Bifunctional compounds called PROTACs bring an E3 ubiquitin ligase to a target protein, causing its destruction using the ubiquitin-proteasome mechanism. Especially in targeting complex proteins like BRD4, they have shown promising efficacy in preclinical cancer models . Small molecules called molecular glues increase protein-protein connections, therefore enabling the focused breakdown of disease-related proteins, such as those interacting with cereblon, represented by lenalidomide . These creative approaches have shown therapeutic promise in neurological diseases, cancer, and autoimmune disorders .
Using a more basic chemical design, molecular glues could use the cell’s natural breakdown mechanisms to solve some PROTAC constraints . Apart from small molecules, non-small molecule strategies such as CRISPR-based gene editing and RNA interference (RNAi) offer precise or transient suppression of CDK activity, hence reducing off-target consequences in cancer models . By enabling the control of previously “undruggable” targets and offering fresh routes for exact cancer therapy, these creative approaches are changing the therapeutic scene.

## Cell cycle proteins and cancer progression

## The role of the CDK4/6-RB pathway and cell cycle proteins in cancer
Most adult tissues consist of G0 phase cells, either in a permanent state from terminal differentiation or senescence, or in a temporary, reversible state known as quiescence. Mitogenic signals can reactivate dormant cells, driving them into the cell cycle. Numerous mitogenic factors commence intracellular signaling cascades that activate CDK4 and CDK6, facilitating the progression from the G0 or G1 phase to the S phase, during which DNA replication transpires 
(Fig. ).
 Though they are well-known controllers of cell cycle progression, CDKs and their cyclin partners also play crucial roles in transcriptional control and homologous recombination (HR)-mediated DNA repair tasks crucial for cancer development and treatment resistance. CDK12 phosphorylates RNA polymerase II and regulates the expression of DNA damage response (DDR) genes, including BRCA1 and FANCD2. Lack of CDK12 or its suppression causes a homologous recombination deficit, which makes cancers more vulnerable to PARP inhibitors and other medications that cause DNA damage. By controlling the expression of oncogenes and DNA damage response-related genes during genotoxic stress, transcriptional CDKs such as CDK7 and CDK9 help cancer cells survive. CDKs’ dual roles in transcription and DNA repair draw attention to the possibility of CDK-targeting PROTACs to block cancer growth and overcome DNA-damaging treatment resistance , .
Apart from their functions in the cell cycle and DNA repair, CDKs—particularly CDK4 and CDK6—also promote cancer development by means of stem cell control and epigenetic reprogramming. Key factors in tumor start, spread, and recurrence, cancer stem cells (CSCs), are influenced in their self-renewal and differentiation by these kinases. By maintaining transcriptional pathways supporting CSC stemness and blocking differentiation, CDKs enable drug resistance and tumor heterogeneity . Furthermore, CDKs affect epigenetic mechanisms like DNA methylation and histone modifications that decrease tumor suppressor genes and promote oncogenes. While CDK12 and CDK13 maintain chromatin structure and encourage DNA repair and gene transcription, CDK7 and CDK9 phosphorylate RNA polymerase II to help oncogene transcriptional elongation , . From a therapeutic standpoint, aiming at these CDKs with techniques like PROTACs could slow cancer development by simultaneously preventing cell cycle progression and fixing oncogenic epigenetic changes . (Table )
 Closely related serine/threonine kinases, CDK4 and CDK6, are characterized by tissue-specific expression and overlapping yet distinct targets . D-type cyclins (D1–D3) increase their activity; INK4 family inhibitors (p16^INK4A, p15^INK4B, p18^INK4C, and p19^INK4D) decrease it . Two main ways cyclin D–CDK4/6 complexes help the cell cycle move forward are: first, they sequester CDK inhibitors p21^CIP1 and p27^KIP1, so allowing the activation of cyclin E–CDK2; second, they phosphorylate retinoblastoma (RB) family proteins—RB1, p107 (RBL1), and p130 (RBL2). RB phosphorylation frees E2F transcription factors, hence starting the transcription of genes required for the G1-S transition, DNA replication, chromatin remodeling, and chromosomal segregation. RB is then phosphorylated by Cyclin E–CDK2 to create a feed-forward loop. Often in cancer, the main tumor suppressors p53 and RB control these pathways. By inhibiting CDK activity and E2F-mediated gene transcription, p53 stimulates CDKN1A (p21), hence preserving cell cycle arrest , .
A common feature in human malignancies is the CDK4/6-RB pathway’s dysregulation. The second most often increased site in several cancer kinds is the CCND1 gene, which codes cyclin D1. Specific changes, such as the R24C mutation in CDK4 seen in melanomas, give INK4 inhibitors resistance. While chromosomal translocations triggering CDK6 are seen in splenic marginal zone lymphomas , CDK4 is elevated in roughly 50% of glioblastomas . Often lost or epigenetically silenced in various malignancies , the CDKN2A gene, which encodes p16^INK4A and p14^ARF, in the end, RB1 loss, common in many cancer types, makes cells independent from cyclin D–CDK4/6 regulation, hence enabling uncontrolled growth .

## CDK2
CDK2 is activated by associating with either A or E-type cyclins. When mitogens are absent, CDK2 complexes are kept dormant by binding p27KIP1 or p21CIP1, two CDK inhibitors. In the latter stages of the G1 phase, CDK2 activity is upregulated by many factors, such as transcription of cyclin E (CCNE) genes driven by E2F, sequestration of p27KIP1 and p21CIP1 mediated by cyclin CDK4/6, and proteolysis of p27KIP1 and p21CIP1 by the ubiquitin/proteasome pathway induced by CDK2-mediated phosphorylation. Notably, WEE1-mediated phosphorylation at Tyr15 inhibits CDK2 function . The cell division cycle 25 (CDC25) family of phosphatases removes this inhibitory phosphorylation . Cyclin E–CDK2 complexes phosphorylate several proteins necessary for centrosome duplication, DNA replication, and cell cycle progression , . After F-box/WD repeat-containing protein 7 (FBXW7)-mediated ubiquitylation, cyclin E is rapidly destroyed during S phase, and CDK2 is associated with newly synthesized cyclin A2 to form active cyclin A–CDK2 complexes , . Human malignancies rarely contain mutations in CDK2, although the catalytic activity of complexes including CDK2 is hyperactivated through various mechanisms. In human cancers, such as ovarian and breast, the CCNE1 locus is often amplified , . The ubiquitin ligase involved in the degradation of cyclin E, FBXW7, has a loss-of-function mutation that leads to cyclin E overexpression in some cancer types , . On the other hand, some tumors express a truncated, hyperactive version of cyclin E1 . Similarly, cyclin A is often overexpressed, due to genomic amplification, as in the cases of breast, colon, and hepatocellular carcinomas – . In some cancers, lower levels of the CDK inhibitor p27KIP lead to increased degradation by S-phase kinase-associated protein 2 (SKP2), which boosts CDK2 activity . However, a variety of tumors are associated with overexpression of CDC25A and CDC25B , . It’s unclear whether CDK2 activity is necessary for tumor initiation or progression. It has been demonstrated that specific human cancer cell lines can grow even when CDK2 activity is inhibited , In melanoma cells, CDK2 deletion inhibited cell cycle progression . Additionally, it was demonstrated in mouse cancer models that CDK2 is essential for the development of breast cancer caused by overexpression of Erbb2 V664E or a shortened form of CCNE1 linked to cancer . Thus, certain cancer types may require CDK2 activity.

## CDK1
Cell cycle progression depends entirely on CDK1, the only CDK. CDK1 is triggered in the G2 phase by its relationship with cyclins A2 and B. At mitotic entry, cyclin A2 degrades; then, by complex formation with B-type cyclins preserves CDK1 kinase activity. Various mitotic steps and the start of mitosis depend on the activation of CDK1 kinase. The anaphase-promoting complex/cyclosome (APC/C) in late mitosis helps destroy B-type cyclins, preventing CDK1 activity and allowing chromosomal segregation and the conclusion of cytokinesis and mitosis . Phosphorylation at Tyr14 and Tyr15 controls CDK1 activity; MYT1 (also known as PKMYT1) and WEE1 help to control this , . CDC25 phosphatases remove these inhibiting phosphorylations.
Although CDK1 activity is usually tightly regulated and seldom dysregulated in cancer, several abnormalities have been observed. Neuroendocrine prostate cancer has been found to exhibit amplifications of the CCNB3 gene . Furthermore, transgenic overexpression of CCNB1 or CCNB2 increased sensitivity to lung and skin cancer, suggesting increased CDK1 activity can promote tumorigenesis . In controlling the transition from the G2 phase to the M phase, the cyclin B family is pivotal, made up of the proteins CCNB1, CCNB2, and CCNB3. Predominantly found in oocytes and testes, cyclin B3 helps govern the G2-M phase in a tissue-specific manner and is involved in meiosis. The G2/M checkpoint and mitotic advancement depend on Cyclin B1, a well-researched regulator of mitotic entry via CDK1 activation. Though functionally similar to B1 in activating CDK1, Cyclin B2 is generated more abundantly in organs, including the ovary and testes . Besides conventional cell cycle control, CDK1 is essential for cancer initiation, development, and progression. Its direct targeting as a treatment approach becomes more difficult, even if it is also crucial for the growth of non-transformed cells – . Especially in MYC-driven liver cancers, mouse lymphomas, and human basal-like triple-negative breast cancer cells, CDK1 suppression has been linked to apoptosis. The findings imply that although causing only reversible cell cycle arrest in normal tissues, CDK1 inhibition might preferentially kill cancer cells. Other studies in animal models have to confirm the therapeutic effectiveness of CDK1 suppression.
CDK1 is the key cell cycle and DNA damage response regulator. While heterozygous deletion has been shown to promote carcinogenesis by partially compromising DNA damage checkpoints, complete homozygous deletion of CDK1 causes embryonic mortality through significant genomic instability and poor DNA repair. This increases genomic instability, which could hasten cancer development – .

## WEE1 and DNA damage checkpoint kinases
Checkpoints cause a cell cycle halt on DNA damage, allowing cells to fix the DNA. Several DNA damage checkpoints affect CDK activity. Depending on the particular type of DNA damage present, either ataxia telangiectasia and Rad3-related (ATR) or ataxia telangiectasia mutant (ATM) protein kinases phosphorylate and activate Checkpoint kinase 1 (CHK1), encoded by the CHEK1 gene. Similarly, ATM can trigger CHK2 (encoded by CHEK2), which helps to turn on p53 , . Transcriptionally induced production of the CDK inhibitor p21^CIP1, controlled by p53 activation, causes G1 arrest and suppresses the cyclin E–CDK2 complex. Phosphorylating and inhibiting CDC25A activates CHK1 and causes a transient S-phase arrest; it also promotes G2 checkpoint arrest by phosphorylating CDC25A, CDC25B, and CDC25C – .
Especially in cancer cells lacking functional p53, which rely on G2 checkpoint activation to block cell division, CHK1 is crucial in the S and G2 stages of DNA damage-induced cell cycle arrest. Originally, CHK1 was seen as a tumor suppressor. Breast and stomach tumors have shown heterozygous loss-of-function mutations in the CHEK1 gene. Still, no homozygous mutations have been found. Supporting these results, heterozygous deletion of Chek1 in mice increased susceptibility to breast cancer when combined with either TRP53 haploinsufficiency or the Wnt1 oncogene – . On the other hand, tissue-specific homozygous deletion of CDK1 in mice protected against cutaneous malignancies caused by carcinogens and reduced mammary carcinogenesis in TRP53-deficient mice.
While CHK1 is vital for the survival of cancer cells by means of DNA damage repair, these results suggest that reduced CHK1 levels brought on by heterozygous CHEK1 deletion might promote carcinogenesis. Unlike CHK1, which is a viable therapeutic target, CHK2 appears to provide a tumor-suppressive role as several CHK2 loss-of-function mouse models show increased cancer susceptibility.
Several cancers, including glioblastoma, melanoma, and hepatocellular carcinoma, show overexpression of WEE1 kinase. While homozygous deletion did not cause tumor development, mice with heterozygous Wee1 deletion in the mammary gland had spontaneous breast cancer – . These results imply that, like results connected to CHK1, a complete reduction of WEE1 activity could not encourage cancer development. Despite conflicting studies, WEE1 is generally considered an oncogene and a new target in cancer therapy.
CHK1 can start cell cycle arrest without active p53. In such cases, it could use p53-independent pathways to activate alternative checkpoint regulators like p27 or stabilize the G2/M checkpoint. Moreover, CHK1 can post-translationally modify proteins to control cell cycle advancement apart from p53 .
The G2/M checkpoint, which predominantly depends on WEE1, ensures DNA repair is finished before mitotic entry. WEE1’s hemizygous deletion can cause mitotic catastrophe and apoptosis, hence hindering tumor growth. In cancers driven by MYC, partial loss of WEE1 may accelerate tumor growth by increasing replication stress and genetic instability, hence restricting its use as a treatment target .
Although WEE1’s main function is to phosphorylate CDK1 to prevent early mitosis, present research points to broader cancerous activities. WEE1 prevents mitotic catastrophe by inhibiting early mitotic entrance into cancer cells under replication stress or DNA damage. Commonly referred to as “Wee1 addiction,” the reliance on WEE1—especially in TP53-mutated tumors—is shown in aggressive cancers, including ovarian cancer, triple-negative breast cancer, and glioblastoma . WEE1 also helps maintain chromosomal integrity by reducing replication stress from oncogenes like RAS and MYC. WEE1 delays mitosis, hence allowing cancer cells to survive potentially lethal DNA damage .
Furthermore, WEE1 changes histone modifications and chromatin accessibility, affecting transcriptional programs linked to cancer and so changing gene expression to support growth and survival . Clinically, WEE1 overexpression is linked with negative prognosis in numerous tumor types. Higher WEE1 levels have been linked to better DNA damage repair, enabling chemotherapy and radiation resistance .

## Polo-like kinases (Plks)
Among the five-member PLK family of serine/threonine kinases, Polo-like kinase 1 (PLK1) is the most extensively studied. Several stages of mitotic formation depend on PLK1’s significance. PLK1 controls the centrosomal placement of Aurora kinase A in the G2 phase, hence enabling centrosome development , . Activating cyclin B–CDK1 complexes is vital; CDC25C phosphatase activity helps to dephosphorylate the inhibitory Tyr15 residue on CDK1, hence activating cyclin B–CDK1 complexes . Moreover, by phosphorylation, PLK1 increases WEE1’s degradation, so reducing CDK1 inhibition at Tyr15 and facilitating mitotic entrance . Apart from mitotic entry, PLK1 is involved in cytokinesis and the start of chromosomal segregation during the metaphase-anaphase shift.
Notwithstanding these obviously defined duties, PLK1’s participation in cancer remains complicated and somewhat perplexing. Many malignancies show higher PLK1 expression, which is usually connected with a poor prognosis, suggesting an oncogenic role. Excessive PLK1 expression could promote cancer development by means of genomic instability and damage to cell cycle checkpoints . Still, studies point to a potential tumor-suppressive effect. Several cancer cell lines have reported mutations that reduce PLK1 stability; heterozygous deletion of Plk1 in mice increased the frequency of spontaneous tumors, implying that a partial loss of PLK1 function could help cancer development .
Adding to the contrast, PLK1 is a fundamental mitotic control needed for correct spindle assembly and chromosomal segregation. Homozygous Plk1 deletion causes mitotic failure and cell death, hence preventing carcinogenesis. Therefore, the effects of heterozygous deletion appear to depend on the tumor environment. In some cancers, tumor cell survival depends on PLK1; its partial lack impairs tumor growth. In others, lower PLK1 expression disturbs mitosis, causing chromosomal instability and aneuploidy that could promote cancer .
Ultimately, PLK1 shows context-dependent actions in cancer, functioning as a key regulator of mitosis and cell cycle progression. Though overexpression is usually linked with tumor progression, certain situations show a tumor-suppressive effect, making PLK1 a complex yet possible target for cancer therapy.

## Aurorae kinases
Mitosis and cytokinesis depend on aurora kinases, serine/threonine protein kinases. During the S phase, when Aurora A localizes to the centrosomes, it is particularly involved in spindle assembly, spindle orientation, and centrosome maturation. Following G2 phase arrest caused by DNA damage checkpoint activation, CDK1 activation and mitotic entry are especially helpful . During mitosis, the interaction between PLK1 and Aurora A creates a reciprocal feedback loop whereby both kinases mutually regulate one another. Aurora A, upstream of PLK1, activates PLK1 by phosphorylating Thr210, a necessary change for PLK1 activation. Associated with PLK1’s co-activator BORA, this phosphorylation is absolutely vital for PLK1’s roles in spindle assembly, mitotic entry, and centrosome maturation . Moreover, PLK1 phosphorylates BORA, which maintains Aurora A activity, so indirectly improving its function; at the same time, it stabilizes Aurora A in the centrosome, enabling correct mitotic progression . Through a positive feedback loop, Aurora A and PLK1 help ensure correct spindle assembly and mitotic entry.
By blocking its proteasomal degradation, Aurora A stabilizes the transcription factor NMYC, therefore encouraging G1-S progression. A member of the Aurora kinase family, Aurora B is a key component of the chromosomal passenger complex (CPC) during mitosis. Aurora B controls chromosomal condensation, orientation, and cytokinesis; its inactivation during mitosis could cause tetraploidy and centrosome amplification . Furthermore, excessive Aurora B expression might cause aneuploidy and chromosomal segregation irregularities – .
With its overexpression linked to numerous cancers, Aurora A and Aurora B have cancerous roles. Though high quantities of Aurora A protein are found in several cancer kinds, the amplification of the Aurora A (AURKA) gene is most often seen in prostates and breast cancers . Though uncommon, Aurora B gene amplification is linked to myelodysplastic syndromes . While AURKB overexpression leads to spontaneous lymphomas in mice, the transgenic expression of AURKA in mouse mammary epithelium produces breast cancer . Especially, heterozygous deletion of AURKA and AURKB in mice increases cancer frequency in several organs, implying a possible tumor-suppressive role in certain tissues despite their oncogenic traits , .
Chromosomal alignment and mitotic spindle formation depend on aurora kinases, including AURKA and AURKB. Homozygous deletion of Aurora kinases causes faulty mitotic progression, which causes cell cycle arrest and prevents tumor formation. Heterozygous deletion of Aurora kinases in some settings can increase chromosomal instability, aneuploidy, and promote cancer transformation . Homozygous versus heterozygous deletion has effects that depend on context, varying by tumor type, genetic background, and the neighboring cellular environment. Depending on the situation, these kinases act as either tumor suppressors or oncogenes; they are complex yet perhaps critical therapeutic targets.

## CDK targeting cancer therapy
Cancer cells usually have dysregulation of cell cycle proteins, which promotes uncontrolled proliferation. As previously mentioned, without significantly affecting normal tissues, genetic ablation of individual cyclins or CDKs or inhibition of cyclin–CDK kinase activity in tumor-bearing mice selectively prevented the onset and progression of specific cancer types driven by particular oncogenic insults.

## Pan-CDK inhibitors
CDK inhibitors are crucial players in the regulation of not just the cell cycle but also apoptosis, transcriptional regulation, cell fate determination, cell migration, and cytoskeletal dynamics. , . Since the majority of early drugs targeting CDKs showed minimal selectivity for specific CDKs, they are known as pan-CDK inhibitors. R-roscovitine, also known as seliciclib, and flavopiridol, also known as alvocidib, are two examples of the first generation of this class of inhibitors.
Flavonol, a semisynthetic flavone that inhibits various CDKs, is the most studied early-generation CDK inhibitor, with about 60 clinical trials started since 1997. It works by causing cell cycle arrest in the G1 and G2 phases , . The administration of flavopiridol caused organ atrophy in a number of mouse tissues by inducing apoptosis. This response was associated with the suppression of cyclin T1–CDK9, or P-TEFb, kinase . While preclinical research suggested that flavopiridol had considerable anti-important activity, clinical phase II studies reported minimal efficacy in solid tumors. However, there was some indication of clinical efficacy in hematological cancers , .
On the contrary, R-roscovitine did not demonstrate antitumor activity in preclinical and clinical trials when used as monotherapy but it is currently under investigation in combination with chemotherapy for solid tumors. First-generation pan-CDK inhibitors had low therapeutic efficacy, which resulted in toxicities at the dosage required to block the targets. Second-generation pan-CDK inhibitors, such as dinaciclib, AT7519, milciclib (also referred to as PHA-848125AC), TG02, CYC065, and RGB-286638, were generated in order to overcome this issue.
Dinaciclib is 100 times more potent than flavopiridol in suppressing RB phosphorylation and has a therapeutic index that is more than ten times greater than flavopiridol. Dinaciclib inhibits tumor cell proliferation in xenograft models of pediatric ALL, pancreatic and ovarian malignancies, and NRAS-mutant melanoma – However, the initial clinical studies across a variety of cancer types did not show much efficacy , . but phase I/II clinical trials showed partial responses in 11% of patients with recurrent multiple myeloma . In addition, a phase I clinical trial demonstrated that 54% of patients with relapsed or refractory chronic lymphocytic leukaemia (CLL) experienced partial remission , .
It’s important to mention that dinaciclib and the AKT inhibitor MK-2206 worked in combination to strongly suppress the tumor growth in pancreatic cancer xenografts by suppressing several KRAS effectors (RAL and PI3K–AKT) . In addition, dinaciclib determined tumor regression and improved survival in mice models, suggesting possible efficacy in treating MYC-overexpressing triple-negative breast cancer and MYC-driven B cell lymphomas , .

## CDK4/6-selective inhibitors
Including palbociclib, ribociclib, and abemaciclib, CDK4/6 inhibitors mark a significant advance in cancer therapy. Developed after promising genetic and preclinical studies, these drugs began Phase II clinical trials in 2009 under palbociclib. By mostly phosphorylating the RB protein, palbociclib suppresses CDK4/6 kinase activity, therefore causing G1 phase cell cycle arrest. Among numerous cancers, including glioblastoma (GBM), colorectal cancer (CRC), rhabdomyosarcoma, multiple myeloma, and acute myeloid leukaemia (AML), it has shown antitumor action. Cancers showing Rb loss, often seen in GBM and some other tumors, may limit the efficacy of palbociclib owing to RB1 gene mutations or deletions – . Loss of RB function is also seen in colorectal cancer (CRC), usually caused by CDK4/6 overactivity, as well as in rhabdomyosarcoma – .
During preclinical trials, palbociclib has shown effectiveness in bypassing the blood-brain barrier and suppressing tumor growth in glioblastoma models . In estrogen receptor-positive (ER + ) breast cancer, it shows selective sensitivity; in HER2+ breast cancer cells, sensitivity rises . Several genetic changes could influence palbociclib sensitivity, including p16INK4A, p15INK4B, or E2F1 deletion, CCNE1 amplification – . Still, the mechanisms fueling CDK4/6 inhibitor resistance are still being studied; changes like RB expression reduction or cyclin D1 overactivation could cause resistance , .
In models of Ewing sarcoma, liposarcoma, rhabdomyosarcoma, and neuroblastoma, selective CDK4/6 inhibitor ribociclib has shown similar anti-tumor activity to palbociclib – . Powerful CDK4/6 inhibitor abemaciclib also targets kinases including PIM1 and CDK9, showing promising results in melanoma, acute myeloid leukaemia, and colorectal cancer xenograft models – . Abemaciclib shows more blood-brain barrier penetration than palbociclib, suggesting it may be useful for treating brain tumors . Still, a major challenge remains CDK4/6 inhibitor resistance. Possible resistance mechanisms include hyperactivation of cyclin E–CDK2 kinases, overexpression of cyclin D1 or CDK4, and decrease of RB expression . Understanding the complex control of the cell cycle in neoplastic cells is absolutely vital despite these challenges. Some cancer cells could offset the inhibition of certain CDKs by increasing the activity of other cyclins or CDKs , . Furthermore, genetic abnormalities changing CDK reliance add more complexity to treatment outcomes – .
Ultimately, especially for cancers with Rb pathway alterations, CDK4/6 inhibitors like palbociclib, ribociclib, and abemaciclib show significant promise in oncological therapy. To maximize their therapeutic use, however, challenges like as resistance mechanisms and the diverse roles of CDKs across several cancer types have to be addressed. Improving the therapeutic efficacy of these inhibitors will depend on ongoing research on their mechanisms of action and strategies to overcome resistance.

## Novel approaches for CDKs targeting (PROTACs and molecular glues)
Especially for cancers showing alterations in the RB pathway, CDK4/6 inhibitors—such as palbociclib, ribociclib, and abemaciclib—mark a major advance in oncological treatment. Particularly palbociclib, these inhibitors work by blocking CDK4/6 kinase activity, hence causing G1-phase cell cycle arrest by RB protein phosphorylation. Clinical studies have shown palbociclib to be effective in various cancers, including glioblastoma, colorectal cancer, and breast cancer . Typically, mutations or deletions in the RB1 gene limit the efficacy of these inhibitors, usually seen in glioblastoma, colorectal cancer, and rhabdomyosarcoma, which causes CDK4/6 inhibition resistance.
Recent studies have looked at the combination of CDK4/6 inhibitors with other treatments to overcome resistance, especially using ribociclib and abemaciclib. While abemaciclib, which blocks CDK4/6 and CDK9, has shown promise in melanoma and colorectal cancer models, ribociclib, like palbociclib, has shown effectiveness in Ewing sarcoma and rhabdomyosarcoma models – . Abemaciclib has superior penetration of the blood-brain barrier compared to palbociclib, indicating its potential for brain tumor treatments . Still, resistance mechanisms include the loss of RB expression and the overactivation of cyclin D1 provide a major difficulty limiting the long-term efficacy of these treatments , , .
Apart from CDK4/6 inhibitors, new targeted treatments such molecular glues and PROTACs (Proteolysis Targeting Chimeras) have emerged as interesting possibilities. Unlike traditional inhibitors, these drugs encourage the degradation of target proteins instead of just blocking their function. Bifunctional molecules called PROTACs draw an E3 ubiquitin ligase to the target protein, hence causing its ubiquitination and following degradation by the proteasome mechanism. This approach lets PROTACs target “undruggable” proteins, including transcription factors and certain CDKs, which are difficult to inhibit with traditional small molecule inhibitors , , .
Compared to conventional small molecule inhibitors, PROTACs provide many advantages including prolonged target suppression, the ability to interact with proteins without clear binding sites, and the possibility to overcome resistance generated by changes in the target protein’s binding site. These benefits make PROTACs an interesting option for combination therapies since they improve the effectiveness of traditional treatments and offer new therapeutic paths for tumors resistant to typical inhibitors , – . A different kind of targeted therapy called molecular glues works by increasing the contact between the target protein and an E3 ligase, hence encouraging its breakdown . With their different modes of action, PROTACs and molecular glues can avoid some resistance mechanisms and aim proteins usually challenging for traditional inhibitors to handle.
Apart from their promise, the development of PROTACs and molecular glues runs into challenges including the choice of suitable E3 ligases, the improvement of selectivity, and the reduction of off-target consequences. To completely exploit the therapeutic potential of these approaches , one must address toxicity issues resulting from the degradation of vital proteins as well as the need for effective cellular transport networks. Signaling a hopeful development in cancer treatment, PROTACs and molecular glues could correct the flaws of traditional CDK inhibitors and offer new treatment options for cancers brought on by dysregulated proteins.
To sum up, while CDK4/6 inhibitors like palbociclib and ribociclib show efficacy in various cancers, their limitations resulting from resistance mechanisms highlight the need for alternative approaches. By using the ubiquitin-proteasome system to destroy target proteins, PROTACs and molecular glues provide a fresh approach to these challenges. Especially for “undruggable” proteins and resistant cancer kinds, these therapies provide a possible future for cancer treatment.

## CDK2 PROTACS
Representing a novel class of therapeutic drugs, PROTACs (Proteolysis Targeting Chimeras) offer a unique approach for protein degradation by using the cell’s ubiquitin-proteasome system rather than just inhibiting protein function. Comprising two halves, these heterobifunctional molecules are one that binds to the protein of interest (POI) and the other that calls an E3 ubiquitin ligase. The resulting ternary complex eliminates the target protein’s ubiquitination, hence solving several issues related to traditional small molecule inhibitors . By selectively destroying CDKs, which are crucial for cell cycle control and often dysregulated in cancer, PROTACs have shown This ability to destroy rather than block proteins has several advantages, including the potential to target “undruggable” proteins and guarantee long-term suppression of the target even after the drug has been removed from the body .
Cancer research has focused mostly on the development of PROTACs targeting CDKs since traditional CDK inhibitors like palbociclib and dinaciclib show little clinical effectiveness because of resistance mechanisms and off-target effects. By means of the degradation of CDKs, particularly those difficult to target with tiny chemicals including CDK2, CDK4/6, and CDK9, PROTACs can help to overcome these obstacles. These PROTACs can perhaps avoid changes granting resistance to traditional inhibitors since they use the ubiquitin-proteasome mechanism to attain lasting target suppression – . One example is a PROTAC targeting CDK2 that uses pomalidomide as the E3 ligase binder, hence demonstrating improved effectiveness in preclinical environments – . Underscoring their possible broad therapeutic uses, several PROTACs—including TMX-2039—have shown the capacity to destroy other CDKs, including CDK2 and CDK5, in ovarian cancer cell lines .
Recent developments in PROTAC technology have focused on increasing the selectivity and bioavailability of these molecules. Changing chemicals like ribociclib helped to reduce oral bioavailability issues, which improved CDK2/4/6 degraders with notable anti-tumor activity in melanoma models , . Moreover, reported are new degraders showing better selectivity for CDK2, including those using the VHL ligase ligand, which showed notable increases in degradation effectiveness . Despite these successes, issues remain including optimizing linker design, improving selectivity within the CDK family, and reducing possible off-target effects and toxicity .
Especially in cancers marked by CDK dysregulation, the PROTAC approach offers a strong substitute for traditional inhibitors. PROTACs are still being developed; more study is required to increase their therapeutic usage effectiveness, selectivity, and pharmacokinetic properties , – .

## CDK4/6 PROTACs
Representing a novel family of tiny chemicals designed to specifically target and destroy particular proteins, PROTACs—PROteolysis TArgeting Chimaeras—offer a substitute for traditional small-molecule inhibitors. By using the ubiquitin-proteasome system to specifically destroy proteins like CDK2 and CDK4/6, PROTACs solve problems connected to conventional inhibitors including resistance and off-target effects.
Targeting CDKs, which control fundamental cell cycle changes, is one of PROTACs main uses. Often dysregulated in cancers, CDK2 is essential for the G1/S phase transition. Problems with selectivity and resistance mechanisms have led conventional CDK2 inhibitors to show little therapeutic efficacy. By totally destroying the protein, PROTACs aimed at CDK2 offer a new approach that may be more efficient and long-lasting than conventional inhibitors. Preliminary studies, including those by Chen et al. (2020), used pomalidomide as an E3 ligase binder with other CDK2 inhibitors to efficiently lower CDK2 . Later studies, notably Grey et al. (2020), showed the effectiveness of several CDK2-targeting PROTACs—TMX-2039 among them—suggesting possible CDK2 and CDK5 degradation . Yang et al. (2020) also offered orally available PROTACs aimed at CDK2/4/6, hence enabling breakdown in melanoma cells .
Later CDK2 PROTACs’ optimization has focused on improving their pharmacokinetic properties and selectivity. Wang et al. presented a particular CDK2 degrader showing improved effectiveness inside the CDK family, hence showing the potential for exact targeting . Aiming at CDK2, Zuo et al. (2021) also designed a separate selective degrader using the VHL E3 ligase in a similar manner . Despite these developments, challenges remain in increasing the capacity and selectivity of CDK2 degraders for more general clinical use , .
Engineered toward CDK4/6, a key G1 phase regulator, PROTACs in the treatment of cancers, especially breast cancer, CDK4/6 inhibitors such as palbociclib have shown efficacy. Still, a major difficulty remains from resistance resulting from mutations in the retinoblastoma (Rb) pathway or the overexpression of other cyclins. Instead of merely inhibiting their function, PROTACs aiming targeting CDK4/6 strive to tackle this problem by completely eliminating the proteins. Many studies have recorded the synthesis of CDK4/6-targeting PROTACs, including those by the Burgess group (2019), who created degraders based on Palbociclib and Ribociclib , – . Jiang et al. (2020) looked more into this approach, producing several PROTACs that particularly aimed at the breakdown of CDK4, CDK6, or both and showed their ability to bypass resistance mechanisms , . Furthermore, medicines such as YX-2-107, developed by De Dominici et al, . displayed considerable CDK6 degradation and reduced tumor growth in murine models . Using different E3 ligase ligands, such DCAF16, has increased the range of available PROTACs for CDK4/6 degradation – .
Developing multi-target degraders is a hopeful path in CDK4/6 PROTACs. Verano et al. (2020) created a CDK4/6/Helios triple-targeted degrader suggesting that the combination of multiple targets could produce synergistic effects . Furthermore, the combination of CDK6 degradation with immunomodulatory drugs (IMiDs) has shown greater effectiveness both in vitro and in vivo , . By aiming at vital proteins for destruction, these investigations highlight the possibility of PROTACs to overcome resistance to conventional medications.
Ultimately, particularly with respect to cell cycle control and cancer therapy, PROTACs offer a strong method for protein breakdown. Progress in PROTAC design, spanning the generation of selective and multi-target degraders, shows promise for better therapeutics, notably in addressing resistance mechanisms that hamper the efficiency of standard small-molecule inhibitors. Still, more research is needed to improve their selectivity, bioavailability, and therapeutic efficacy for clinical use .

## CDK5 PROTACs
Particularly in cancers like breast cancer, the development of PROTACs (proteolysis-targeting chimeras) has generated great interest in targeting CDK4/6, fundamental G1 phase cell cycle regulators. Though resistance to these drugs, either from mutations in the retinoblastoma (Rb) pathway or the overexpression of other cyclins, presents a major challenge, CDK4/6 inhibitors—including palbociclib—have demonstrated therapeutic efficacy. Instead of only reducing their function, PROTACs that eliminate CDK4/6 offer a possible way to overcome this resistance by completely destroying the proteins. In CDK4/6-targeting PROTACs, Cereblon (CRBN) acts as an E3 ligase showing enhanced efficacy and the ability to bypass Rb-pathway alterations, thereby perhaps offering longer therapeutic benefits . The Burgess group (2019) was the first to reveal a PROTAC meant to destroy CDK4/6 using compounds originating from the FDA-approved drugs Palbociclib and Ribociclib . Demonstrating that PROTACs can particularly target different CDKs, these compounds preferentially eliminated either CDK4 or CDK6, or both in certain cases. Jiang et al. expanded on this by creating PROTACs using pomalidomide as the E3 ligase ligand linked with palbociclib, ribociclib, and abemaciclib, which particularly aimed the destruction of CDK4, CDK6, or both. Though the Abemaciclib-based PROTACs were reported to lower CDK9, which could cause a concern, the PROTACs showed promise in combating resistance. Amarnath Natarajan’s team created CDK6 degraders based on Palbociclib, which led to compound 19 showing selective CDK7 degradation , . Examining the influence of linker length and composition on PROTAC effectiveness, the Kronke group (2020) showed that lipophilicity has a major impact on the selectivity of CRBN-based CDK4/6 degraders .
At the same time, De Dominici et al. presented a new CDK6-selective degrader, YX-2-107, which notably reduced tumor growth in a mouse model of Philadelphia-positive acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL), therefore supporting the therapeutic potential of selective CDK6 degradation . The Rui Li group increased the repertoire of E3 ligase ligands for CDK4/6 PROTACs by showing that CDK4/6 can be killed using DCAF16 ligands , . By creating a triple-targeted degrader aimed at CDK4, CDK6, and Helios, Verano et al. pushed the research forward by implying that combining these targets could provide synergistic effects. Moreover, both in vitro and in vivo studies showed indications of the cooperation between IMiDs and PROTACs targeting CDK6 – .
Apart from CDK4/6, CDK5 has also emerged as a major modulator in several diseases including Alzheimer’s. Unlike other CDKs, CDK5 is mostly activated by p35 and p39 rather than by cyclin proteins. Dysregulation of CDK5 in Alzheimer’s disease causes tau hyperphosphorylation and therefore neurodegeneration. These pathological conditions are significantly influenced by the protein p35, which calpain slices to produce the hyper-stable fragment p25 , . Though it is less likely to calpain cleavage, p39, a second activator of CDK5, has different effects on cortical lamination and neuronal activity; its involvement in neurodegeneration is nonetheless important , . By increasing cell motility and giving resistance to treatments including radiation and chemotherapy, CDK5 in cancer promotes tumor growth, especially in glioblastoma and other neoplasms . Studies on CDK5 in neoplasms point to its involvement in both tumor initiation and later spread and growth – . Though they have promise, specific CDK5 inhibitors are scarce. The development of very selective CDK5 degraders stays a field for further study, notwithstanding developments in CDK5 degraders including those found by the Grey group , .
To sum up, although CDK4/6 and CDK5 have different roles in cellular processes, their dysregulation in diseases like cancer and Alzheimer’s makes them attractive targets for therapeutic approaches. The development of PROTACs and degraders targeting CDKs marks a hopeful approach to overcome resistance mechanisms in cancer therapy and to address neurological diseases related to CDK dysregulation.

## CDK8 PROTACs
An essential transcriptional regulator affecting transcriptional elongation and gene expression using the phosphorylation of the C-terminal domain (CTD) of RNA polymerase II (Pol II), CDK8 is a key component of the Mediator complex . Predominantly suppressing transcription, it affects the availability of transcription factors including p53, beta-catenin, and STAT1/3, which engage in cell cycle progression, metabolism, and oncogenesis. By controlling the expression of genes encoding translation initiation factors and ribosomal proteins, CDK8 affects general protein synthesis. In cancer, abnormal CDK8 activity promotes the translation of oncogenic mRNAs, hence supporting tumor growth and therapy resistance. Originally named K35 and DK, CDK8 is closely related to the Mediator complex, made up of MED12, MED13, cyclin C, and CDK8, which supports signal transduction and transcriptional control . Though originally considered transcriptional repressors, CDK8 and the Mediator complex have been shown by recent studies to operate as activators in certain pathways including Wnt/β-catenin, Smad, NOTCH, and JAK-STAT. Often overexpressed in tumors, CDK8 does not affect normal cell survival; hence, it may be a suitable target for cancer treatment – . The considerable similarity between CDK8 and CDK19 (97%), however, has made the creation of specific CDK8 inhibitors difficult .
PROTACs—proteolysis-targeting chimeras—and molecular glues have emerged as possible ways to target CDK8 in reaction to this. Grey’s team published a CDK8 degrader in 2018 using cortistatin A, a naturally occurring substance with an IC50 of 15 nmol/L for CDK8 , . The scientists, therefore, tried to maximize the output since cortistatin A’s complicated manufacturing process (producing between 0.012% and 2%) and poor yield called for 16 to 30 stages. They maintained binding affinity and improved synthesis efficiency by substituting the more simple steroid DHEA for the cortistatin A core. This change led to the creation of compound 32, which included a polyethylene glycol (PEG) connection and allowed CDK8 degradation in Jurkat cells at a concentration of 1µmol/L.
Though the development of selective CDK8 inhibitors has been limited, improvements in PROTAC technology combined with improvements in synthetic methods for natural binders like cortistatin A open the path for more effective CDK8-targeting therapies. These strategies might help not just in cancer treatment but also in diseases linked with CDK8 dysregulation, where focused CDK8 degradation could offer therapeutic benefits without affecting normal cell functions.

## CDK9 PROTACs
A key transcriptional regulator, CDK8, also participates in several signaling pathways including Wnt/β-catenin, Smad, NOTCH, and JAK-STAT . By phosphorylating the C-terminal domain (CTD) of RNA polymerase II (Pol II), CDK8 affects transcriptional elongation and gene expression, hence supporting both transcriptional activation and repression. Crucial for cell cycle progression, metabolism, and oncogenesis, CDK8 controls the availability of transcription factors including p53, β-catenin, and STAT1/3. In cancer, its abnormal activity promotes the translation of oncogenic mRNAs, hence supporting tumor growth and treatment resistance. Often overexpressed in neoplasms, CDK8 is part of the Mediator complex involved in signal transduction – . CDK8 has been proposed as a possible therapeutic target for certain cancers, given its lack of effect on cell survival from CDK8 lowering. The 97% similarity between CDK8 and CDK19 makes creating particular inhibitors more difficult.
PROTACs—proteolysis-targeting chimeras—were first shown in 2018 to target CDK8. However, manufacturing issues led to a changed version with a steroid core , a CDK8 degrader using cortistatin A as a binder showed notable activity. In Jurkat cells, this degrader showed notable CDK8 degradation. Though many CDK8 inhibitors are available, only a few have demonstrated selective inhibition . Creation of compound 32, which effectively activates CDK8 degradation at a concentration of 1 µmol/L , suggests that PROTACs and molecular glues could offer improved targeting of CDK8.
A member of the cyclin-dependent kinase family, CDK9 forms a complex with coactivators T and K . By means of RNA polymerase II phosphorylation, CDK9 controls transcriptional elongation and takes part in vital activities including cell cycle progression and oncogenesis – . PROTACs have successfully targeted CDK9; the first successful degrader was recorded in 2017 . Exemplified by compound 29, PROTAC-based drugs for CDK9 degradation showed selective CDK9 degradation while maintaining other kinases . Later improvements in CDK9 targeting included compound 34, which showed better degradation efficiency at nanomolar levels, hence increasing anti-cancer effectiveness by Bcl-xL suppression, a vital apoptosis regulator , .
Many studies have examined how well PROTACs target CDK9. Highlighting the benefits of targeted protein degradation over traditional inhibition, compound 35 (THAL-SNS-032) showed sub-nanomolar CDK9 degradation and suggested greater cytotoxicity relative to other inhibitors , . Moreover, PROTACs based on wogonin, a natural chemical, have shown a great affinity for CDK9, which may lead to more targeted and efficient therapies for cancers addicted to transcription , .
CDK9-targeting PROTACs have produced promising results in treating triple-negative breast cancer, including tumor suppression in xenograft models . A novel degrader, compound 39 (CD-5), showed substantial CDK9 degradation in vitro; proteome investigations highlighted its selectivity . Including compound 40 (LL-K9-3), more PROTACs showed dual degradation of CDK9 and cyclin T1, hence increasing the method’s selectivity and offering a possible tool for targeting cancers relying on CDK9 for survival .
Moreover, compound 41, developed by Fuchs’s group, showed submicromolar CDK9 degradation, connecting strength to the physicochemical properties of the linkers . A novel degrader, compound 42, also used piperlongumine as an E3 ligase binder, therefore showing promise to increase CDK9 selectivity . Many CDK9-targeting medications have been shown to work in proteomic research, so confirming that CDK9 is a well-known cancer therapeutic target.
By reducing the transcriptional activity of oncogenes like MYC, CDK9 inhibitors show promise in the treatment of hematologic malignancies; nevertheless, issues like poor pharmacokinetics and off-target effects have compromised their clinical effectiveness. Recent developments in PROTACs, which enable E3 ligase recruitment to destroy CDK9, indicate a more efficient and targeted approach that offers better therapeutic benefits for tumors connected to transcriptional dysregulation , . These strategies offer notable promise to overcome the limitations of traditional CDK9 inhibitors and expand treatment options for cancers depending on transcriptional control.

## CDK12 PROTAC
CDK12 PROTAC is crucial for transcriptional and post-transcriptional control to support embryonic development and the DNA-damage response (DDR) , as in fact CDK12 In vivo knockout is embryonic lethal . In physiological conditions, CDK12 and cyclin K form an activator complex that phosphorylates Pol II and contributes to transcriptional elongation. . CDK12 dysregulation is associated with several neoplasia, including ovarian, colorectal, bladder, and esophageal cancers . The strong homology between CDK12 and CDK13 has to be taken into account for targeting approaches and some tumors may benefit from selective CDK12 inhibition. Some characterized CDK12 inhibitors include THZ1 , SR-4835, Dinaciclib , and SY-5609 – .

## CDK13 PROTACs
CDK13 interacts with cyclin K to form a CDK13-cyclin K complex, which phosphorylates Pol II and regulates DDR . There aren’t many studies that have established a functional difference between CDK12 and CDK13, but CDK13 dysregulation and mutations have been linked to a number of diseases, including eating disorders, intellectual disability, developmental delay, craniofacial characteristics, and structural heart problems . For what we know, there isn’t a selective CDK13 degrader currently available, but a dual PROTAC degrader targeting both CDK12 and CDK13 was previously described , .

## PROTACs for CDK1/7/10/11/14/15/16/17/18/19
In this section, we have outlined to the CDK members that PROTACs have partially demonstrated to be degradable. The creation of specific PROTACs that target certain CDK members is highly demanded, even if none of these degraders have been confirmed as of yet. Fischer group published a study in 2020 detailing their approach to quickly build degraders through identifying the degradable kinome . They produced a sizable library of different multi-target PROTACs, including compound 46, and used this library to treat cells in order to use proteomics to detect and measure changes in protein abundance. Even though they did not expressly address CDK member degradation in their study, pan-degrader therapy dramatically reduced CDK abundance. Uncertainty exists regarding whether this reduction is unique or the result of other target perturbations having a second-order influence. According to their research, CDK targets might be degradable, and this could be used in the future to create targeted and selective CDK degraders. To verify the specificity of such degraders, additional research—such as time course and rescue experiments—will be required.

## PROTACs target other CDKs
PROTACs are targeting not just CDK2, CDK4/6, and CDK9, but also additional CDKs implicated in different cellular processes, including CDK1 (cell cycle regulation), CDK7 (transcriptional regulation), and CDK5 (neurodegenerative disorders). For instance, since CDK1 is essential for mitosis, targeting it with PROTACs may offer a unique treatment approach for malignancies. Likewise, using PROTACs to target CDK7 may improve the suppression of transcriptional networks implicated in the survival of cancer cells. Although PROTACs that target these CDKs are still in the early phases of development, the possibility of selectively degrading these proteins offers an intriguing avenue for cancer treatment .

## Therapeutic consequences and challenges
PROTACs that target CDKs have therapeutic potential since they can get around a lot of the drawbacks of traditional CDK inhibitors. Even in the presence of resistance mechanisms like mutations in the CDK-binding pocket or activation of compensatory pathways, PROTACs can produce more comprehensive and long-lasting therapeutic effects by causing the degradation of CDKs rather than just blocking their activity . Furthermore, because of structural or functional limitations, PROTACs provide a method for targeting “undruggable” CDKs that might not be responsive to traditional inhibition. The development of PROTACs that target CDKs is not without difficulties, though. Finding appropriate E3 ligases that can be successfully recruited to the target protein is a significant challenge. Furthermore, reducing off-target effects and guaranteeing efficient in vivo activity require PROTACs’ pharmacokinetics and selectivity to be optimized . Additionally, there are still issues with overcoming possible toxicity associated with the breakdown of vital proteins and effectively delivering PROTACs to the tumor site.
To sum up, new developments in PROTACs that target CDK2, CDK4/6, CDK9, and other CDKs present a viable therapeutic approach to get around the drawbacks of traditional CDK inhibitors. PROTACs offer a more robust and focused method of addressing malignancies caused by dysregulated CDKs by causing the breakdown of these important transcriptional and cell cycle regulators. The potential of PROTACs to target hitherto undruggable proteins and overcome resistance mechanisms makes them a promising path for future cancer therapeutics, even though there are still problems in improving their efficacy and limiting off-target consequences.

## Molecular adhesives in the design of CDK and cyclin degraders
The majority of the molecules covered in this review are made with classical PROTAC design, which is intended to work by creating ternary complexes. Molecular glue is a different method recently discovered, and it is also used for determining protein degradation. . As mentioned previously, CDK9, CDK12, and CDK13 are activated partners of cyclin K. Consequently, cyclin K degradation would prevent Pol II from being phosphorylated, an important step in transcriptional control. The first molecular glue discovered was (R)-CR8, which degraded cyclin K at nanomolar IC50 (half-maximal inhibitory concentration) . This compound was once thought to be an inhibitor that targeted CDK1, CDK2, CDK5, CDK7, CDK9, and CDK12. Despite having a lesser propensity to promote complex formation, the (R)-CR8 analogues did not cause cyclin K degradation, indicating that the modest alteration in phenylpyridine’s orientation may have a substantial impact on degradation. In addition, the binding surface and modes show flexibility and differ in their ability to stabilize the DDB1-CDK12 complex in different circumstances .

## Recent clinical trial updates for CDK-degrading PROTACs and molecular glues
(Fig. ) With the invention of PROTACs (proteolysis-targeting chimeras) and molecular glues that target CDKs, recent advances in targeted protein degradation have opened up new therapeutic options for solid tumors and hematologic malignancies. These novel approaches are presently undergoing clinical testing, offering a translational viewpoint on their possible effectiveness.
 

## PROTACs in hematologic malignancies
Dysregulation of CDKs, especially CDK9, is essential for the development of hematologic malignancies. Developing a PROTAC targeting CDK9 is a noteworthy example; preclinical models of HER2-positive cancers have shown strong anticancer effect. This substance efficiently causes CDK9 to degrade, which suppresses oncogenic transcriptional processes. Clinical trials to evaluate the effectiveness of CDK9-degrading PROTACs in patients with hematologic malignancies have been made possible by these encouraging preclinical findings .

## PROTACs in solid tumors
PROTACs are also used in solid tumors, where CDK dysregulation is prevalent. Dual PROTAC degraders that target CDK12 and CDK13 are extremely effective and selective, according to recent investigations. Preclinical models have demonstrated the effectiveness of these degraders, pointing to possible therapeutic advantages in solid malignancies with abnormal CDK12/13 activation. Important information about these drugs’ translational applicability will be revealed when they advance into clinical studies .

## Molecular glues targeting CDKs
Another interesting strategy for targeted protein degradation is the use of molecular glues. According to a recent study, a molecular glue that facilitates the connection between CDK12 and DDB1 has been found to degrade CDK12 selectively. This approach offers a novel mechanism for targeted protein breakdown by avoiding the requirement for a substrate-specific receptor. Such molecular glues are presently being studied for their therapeutic potential, which could increase the number of cancer treatments available for those caused by CDK dysregulation .

## Clinical translation and future directions
A significant breakthrough in cancer treatment has been made by introducing PROTACs and molecular glues into clinical settings. The first PROTAC molecules went into clinical trials in 2020, offering preliminary evidence of the medicinal approach’s viability. The safety and effectiveness of these chemicals in a variety of cancer types, including solid tumors and hematologic malignancies, are being assessed in ongoing clinical trials. It is expected that the findings of these investigations will guide the creation and improvement of CDK-degrading treatments in the future . In conclusion, the translational potential of PROTACs and molecular glues that target CDKs is shown by their inclusion in clinical studies. Determining their efficacy and safety profiles will require more investigation and clinical testing, which will ultimately help to develop targeted cancer treatments.

## The mechanistic advantages of targeted protein degradation over CDK inhibition
By using the cell’s proteasomal machinery to destroy harmful proteins rather than just inhibiting their activity, targeted protein degradation (TPD) provides clear mechanistic advantages over traditional cyclin-dependent kinase (CDK) inhibition. Conventional small-molecule CDK inhibitors block substrate phosphorylation by occupying the kinase’s active site, but they frequently need large doses for prolonged suppression and can cause compensatory resistance mechanisms. PROTACs, on the other hand, work catalytically by enlisting an E3 ubiquitin ligase to ubiquitinate the target protein, designating it for proteasomal breakdown. Because of their catalytic mechanism of action, PROTACs can achieve extended target depletion at substoichiometric dosages, which lowers the risk of dose-limiting toxicities and off-target effects , . Furthermore, PROTAC-mediated degradation removes CDKs’ enzymatic and scaffolding capabilities, offering a more comprehensive functional blockade than inhibition. Many CDKs are essential for the non-catalytic assembly of protein complexes and the regulation of transcriptional programs; active-site inhibitors only partially impair these processes. PROTACs accomplish a more comprehensive therapeutic impact by completely breaking down the protein, which stops CDKs from performing these alternate functions. Furthermore, because PROTACs’ mode of action does not depend on binding to the ATP pocket of the kinase, they have demonstrated effectiveness against mutant or conformationally changed CDKs that are resistant to traditional inhibitors. With regard to cancer and other proliferative illnesses, these benefits make targeted protein degradation a potent tactic for getting around the drawbacks of conventional kinase inhibitors.

## Challenges and opportunities for CDK-targeting PROTACs and molecular Glues in clinical applications
Molecular glues and CDK-targeting PROTACs are promising therapeutic approaches for cyclin-dependent kinase modulation in cancer and other disorders. Their clinical translation, however, offers both tremendous potential for therapeutic improvement and a number of difficulties.
Degrading particular CDKs selectively without harming closely related kinases is one of the main hurdles in creating CDK-targeting PROTACs. It is challenging to create ligands that specifically engage E3 ligases to destroy only the targeted CDK since many CDKs have highly conserved structural characteristics. Furthermore, since different cell types express different components of the ubiquitin-proteasome system, which may restrict the effectiveness of degradation in particular tissues, the availability of appropriate E3 ligases with widespread tissue expression and effective ubiquitination activity continues to be a constraint.
Because PROTACs and molecular glues frequently have larger molecular weights than conventional small-molecule inhibitors, which affects their cell permeability, bioavailability, and metabolic stability, pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic features also provide challenges. In clinical development, it is still crucial to make sure that these compounds can efficiently reach and interact with their targets at therapeutically relevant concentrations. Strategies to reduce adaptive resistance are also necessary because resistance mechanisms, like modifications to the target protein’s ubiquitination sites or changes in E3 ligase expression, may appear.
Molecular glues and CDK-targeting PROTACs present revolutionary possibilities in precision medicine despite these obstacles. In contrast to traditional inhibitors, PROTACs cause total protein degradation, which gets around problems with temporary inhibition and avoids resistance mechanisms brought on by ATP-binding pocket mutations. In malignancies caused by abnormal CDK activity, where degradation can impair both enzymatic and scaffolding roles essential for tumor growth, this more comprehensive functional blockage is especially beneficial.
Increasing the E3 ligase repertoire to improve tissue selectivity and degradation efficiency presents another potential. New E3 ligases and ligand-based strategies may make it possible to target CDKs specifically in particular cell types, lowering off-target toxicity. Furthermore, there is potential for improving therapeutic efficacy and overcoming drug resistance when PROTACs are used with currently available medicines such checkpoint inhibitors or targeted therapies.
Moreover, an alternate strategy with maybe better pharmacological and specific qualities is provided by molecular glues, which create new protein-protein connections to promote selective disintegration. These tiny compounds may offer novel approaches to targeting CDKs that are presently thought to be “undruggable” with conventional inhibitors since they take advantage of natural ubiquitination processes.
Targeted therapy in oncology and other fields may undergo a revolution as research into CDK-targeting PROTACs and molecular glues improves their delivery, selectivity, and design. This could open up new therapeutic alternatives for diseases with few available treatments.

## Conclusion
In conclusion, CDK proteins play a critical role in controlling the cell cycle, several physiological processes, and the development of diseases. Although the small-molecule inhibitors available today are quite effective, they do not fully meet all of the requirements. These limitations might be removed with the generation of CDK degraders. PROTAC is an effective method for degrading different CDK members, while molecular glue technologies are new methods that are also effective in degrading different CDK members. More CDK-targeting PROTAC and molecular glue compounds are likely to be developed in the future and used as instruments for both clinical therapy and fundamental biological research.
