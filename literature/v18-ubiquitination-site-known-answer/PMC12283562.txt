## Summary
RAS proteins, as the most frequently mutated oncoproteins in human cancers, drive tumor proliferation, metastasis, and therapeutic resistance. Recent studies have revealed that ubiquitination dynamically regulates the stability, membrane localization, and signaling transduction of RAS proteins, profoundly impacting their oncogenic functions. A series of ubiquitination sites, E3 ligases, deubiquitinases, and regulatory proteins are involved in RAS ubiquitination. We also analyze the heterogeneity of ubiquitination patterns across distinct RAS isoforms (KRAS4A, KRAS4B, NRAS, and HRAS) and their functional disparities in cancers. Targeting the ubiquitination pathway offers novel strategies to overcome RAS proteins. Future research should integrate protein structure analysis and high-throughput screening to develop specific ubiquitination modulators and explore combination RAS ubiquitination targeting strategies with RAS inhibitors or immunotherapy, aiming to overcome RAS-driven malignant phenotypes.

## Introduction
The RAS family of GTPases (KRAS4A, KRAS4B, HRAS, and NRAS) represents one of the most frequently mutated oncoproteins in human cancers, with mutations occurring in approximately 19% of all malignancies. These mutations lead to constitutive activation of downstream signaling pathways, such as MAPK and PI3K-AKT, driving uncontrolled cell transformation, proliferation and metastasis. Clinically, tumors harboring mutant RAS are associated with aggressive disease progression, intrinsic resistance to anti-EGFR therapies and significantly poor prognosis. , 
On top of well-characterized oncogenic mutations of RAS genes, the precise regulation of RAS protein emerges as a pivotal mechanism governing cellular homeostasis and malignant transformation. As RAS proteins lack intrinsic catalytic activity, their abundance and interaction with regulatory proteins (e.g., GEFs, GAPs) collectively determine the amplitude and spatiotemporal dynamics of downstream signaling cascades. Post-translational modifications (PTMs) are central to regulating RAS proteins, which dynamically modulate RAS stability, activity, and subcellular localization. , , , , Notably, elevated RAS protein levels is associated with aggressive tumor progression and poor prognosis across diverse malignancies, including hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), , , pancreatic ductal cancer (PDAC), colorectal cancer (CRC), , and breast cancer. , Canonical PTMs, such as palmitoylation, farnesylation, and prenylation facilitate membrane anchoring, , , , methylation and acetylation modulate the recruitment of adaptor proteins to RAS proteins. , Recent studies have illuminated that ubiquitination plays an important role in the regulation of RAS biology, operating through a delicate balance between E3 ubiquitin ligases and deubiquitinating enzymes (DUBs). , , 
Ubiquitination exerts bidirectional control over RAS activity: it can either promote degradation to suppress oncogenic signaling or activate RAS via non-degradative mechanisms such as changing GTP or effector binding and subcellular relocalization, thereby amplifying downstream effector activation. , , This duality is further complicated by the isoform-specific regulation of RAS proteins. Additionally, the genetic context and tumor environment appear to modulate the outcome of ubiquitination. Moreover, dysregulation of this machinery—whether through impaired E3 ligase recognition of mutant RAS isoforms or DUB-mediated stabilization of hyperactive RAS—fuels pathological protein accumulation, driving metastatic progression, and therapy resistance. , These findings underscore the therapeutic potential of restoring ubiquitination homeostasis.
RAS ubiquitination is evolutionarily conserved and exhibits broad relevance across malignancies, highlighting its fundamental role in oncogenic signaling. This review bridges a gap in the RAS oncology field by systematically synthesizing the ubiquitination machinery governing RAS proteins. We dissect the context-dependent antagonism between E3 ligases, deubiquitinating enzymes and other components involved in RAS ubiquitination, which regulate RAS degradation, subcellular trafficking, and activity. We also explore isoform- and mutation-specific regulatory networks and evaluate emerging strategies to exploit the ubiquitination system for RAS-driven cancer therapy.

## The process of RAS protein ubiquitination
Ubiquitination involves the covalent linkage of one or more ubiquitin monomers (monoubiquitination or diubiquitination) or different types of ubiquitin chains (polyubiquitination) to the target protein. In the process of ubiquitination, ubiquitin is enzymatically attached to substrates by E1 Ub-activating enzyme, E2 Ub-conjugating enzyme, and E3 Ub-ligase enzyme. The E3 ligase is the core component in the ubiquitin-mediated proteolysis system (UPS) to cf. specificity to the pathway by recognizing specific degron motifs of the substrate and covalently linking ubiquitin to the target protein. The fate of ubiquitinated proteins is predominantly dictated by the topology of ubiquitin chains. The canonical K48-linked polyubiquitination predominantly marks substrates for proteasomal degradation, whereas K63-linked chains often act as signals for alternative degradation routes, including the autophagy-lysosomal pathway. 

## Ubiquitination sites of RAS proteins
To decipher the ubiquitination landscape of RAS proteins, precise mapping of ubiquitination within their functional domains is essential. Mammalian RAS comprises four isoforms—KRAS4A, KRAS4B, HRAS, and NRAS—encoded by three genes, sharing a conserved G domain but diverging in their hypervariable regions (HVRs) ( A). Functioning as binary molecular switches, RAS oscillates between active GTP-bound and inactive GDP-bound states, regulated by GTPase-activation proteins (GAPs) and guanine nucleotide exchange factors (GEFs). Structurally, the G domain harbors conserved motifs critical for RAS function: GTP binding sites, the phosphate binding loop (P loop) and two switch regions (Switch I/II). The S-II pocket is exposed in the GDP-bound state to accommodate covalent inhibitors. The C-terminal HVR, through PTMs (e.g., prenylation, palmitoylation), directs RAS trafficking between the plasma membrane and endomembranes. , , , 
Crucially, both the G-domain and the HVR contain sites of ubiquitination, directly linking structural features to proteostatic regulation ( B). Lys117 and Lys147 are located in the GTP-binding region of RAS proteins. Lys117 monoubiquitination facilitates nucleotide exchange, hence augmenting RAS activation. Lys147 monoubiquitination hinders GAP-mediated GTP hydrolysis, allows GTP loading and upregulates RAS activity. Most RAS oncogenic mutations drive constitutive activation through three distinct mechanistic categories: (1) impairing GTP hydrolysis (e.g., G12D, Q61L) by inducing steric hindrance or disrupting catalytic networks; (2) accelerating nucleotide exchange (e.g., G13D, A59G) via enhanced GEF interaction or destabilized GDP binding; and (3) rewiring conformational states (e.g., A146T) to modulate effector selectivity through allosteric communication pathways. , , However, the influence of distinct site-specific ubiquitination on mutant RAS remains unknown. Lys104 and Lys128 are spatially distant from the GTP or effector binding region, yet both could create a new docking site to promote or interfere with GEF or GAP. Lys104 monoubiquitination of KRAS has been demonstrated to promote the binding with GEF and activate RAS signaling, whereas Lys128 monoubiquitination facilitates RAS binding to GAP and promotes GAP-mediated GTP hydrolysis, , thus restricting RAS activation. Moreover, ubiquitin conjugation to Lys170 stabilized the anchor segment of the HVR by isolating the farnesyl and palmitoyl moieties, thereby impairing the association of HRAS with the membrane. , The ubiquitination of RAS proteins has an impact on their activation and location in a site-specific manner. Discovering new ubiquitination sites and clarifying the effects of site-specific RAS ubiquitination would be intriguing.

## E3 ligases of RAS protein
As aforementioned , the E3 ligase is the key factor in the ubiquitination cascade. The E3 ligase recognizes specific degron motifs of RAS proteins and allows the transfer of activated ubiquitin from E2 enzymes to RAS proteins. Up to now, four primary types of ubiquitin ligase E3 have been recognized: really interesting new gene (RING), homologous to E6-APC terminus (HECT), U-box and Cullin-RING ligases (CRL). Several E3 ubiquitin ligases have been demonstrated to be involved in modulating the stability and function of RAS ( ; ). Apart from these known E3 ligases, a number of other E3 ligases predicted by Ubibrowser software 2.0 based on structure characteristics are also involved in the process of RAS ubiquitination including SYTL4, FBXO5/28, PML, RBSN, UTCH, FBXL15/18, RING1, WWP1/2, RBX1, NEDD4L, KDM2A, CCNF, BARD1, MNAT1, XIAP, CBL, TOPORS, ZMYND11, FANCA, HSPA8, LITAF, and ZEB2. 

## NEDD4-1
NEDD4-1 (Neural precursor cell expressed developmentally downregulated 4-1), a pivotal HECT-domain E3 ubiquitin ligase, can mediate the degradable polyubiquitination of RAS. , Interestingly, RAS can increase the transcription of NEDD4-1, thereby establishing a negative feedback loop to regulate the abundance of RAS. Notably, NEDD4-1 could independently target tumor regulators. NEDD4-1 promotes proteasomal degradation of tumor suppressor PTEN. , NEDD4-1 could also polyubiquitinate CNrasGEF’s CDC25 domain, triggering its degradation. , This suppresses CNrasGEF’s dual functions: cAMP-dependent activation of Rap1 and RAS interaction via its CDC25 domain. Critically, reduced Rap1 activity attenuates its inhibition of MAPK signaling, elevating p-ERK levels and driving glioma proliferation and invasion. What’s more, activated RAS, whether through oncogenic mutations or signaling pathways, can evade NEDD4-1-mediated degradation, enabling RAS-driven tumorigenesis through dysregulation of both PTEN and CNrasGEF. , This mechanism constitutes a key driver of uncontrolled RAS activation and may underlie the initiation of tumorigenesis. Interestingly, transmembrane proteins PRRG1 (proline-rich Gla protein 1) spatially restrict NEDD4-1 activity at the plasma membrane, shielding RAS from degradation in PDAC. Further research into the function of NEDD4-1 could focus on the mechanisms underlying its dual role as either an oncogene or a tumor suppressor in response to the cellular environment. , , , Besides, the precise site and the specific ubiquitin chain utilized by NEDD4-1 to regulate the RAS protein remain ambiguous.

## Rabex-5
Rabex-5 (also referred to as RABGEF1), a dual-function protein, serves not only as a guanine nucleotide exchange factor (GEF) for Rab5 to regulate early endosomal fusion but also as an E3 ubiquitin ligase targeting RAS proteins. , In Drosophila, which possesses a single RAS homolog (RAS85D), Rabex-5 catalyzes mono-/di-ubiquitination of RAS85D, dampening MAPK signaling by reducing GTP loading and promoting endosomal sequestration. , In mammals, however, Rabex-5 exhibits isoform specificity: it ubiquitinates HRAS and NRAS but not KRAS likely due to structural divergence. , Mechanistically, Rabex-5-mediated RAS ubiquitination reduces GDP-GTP exchange, increases GTP hydrolysis rate and promotes endosomal localization in HEK293T cells. Notably, several mechanisms have been found to regulate Rabex-5-mediated ubiquitination of RAS. RAS and RAB interactor 1 (RIN1), an RAS effector, enhances the ubiquitination of RAS by activating Rabex-5 and inhibits MAPK signaling. Specifically, RIN1 performs as a GEF for Rab-5 to promote the recruitment of Rabex-5 to endosomal sites, where RAS ubiquitination takes place. Y4 phosphorylation is required to facilitate RAS interaction with Rabex-5 and increase Rabex-5 mediated ubiquitination. Some oncogenes, such as JAK2, SRC, and EGFR, could promote RAS Y4 phosphorylation and thus maintain appropriate restriction of RAS activity. Despite its tumor-suppressive RAS regulation, Rabex-5 is paradoxically overexpressed in colorectal, breast, and prostate cancers, correlating with poor prognosis. , , This duality may stem from its oncogenic potential that arises from Rab5 GEF activity. Studies have shown that Rabex-5 mutants with impaired E3 activity but intact Rab5 GEF function enhance RAS signaling. Moreover, Rabex5-mediated ubiquitination suppresses RAS proteins, unlike the activating ubiquitination processes at Lys117 and Lys147. , One possible explanation is that those studies were mostly based on the investigation of Lys117 or Lys147 mutants, as amino acid alterations within the GDP/GTP-binding pocket can modify the pocket’s flexibility. The identification of E3 ubiquitin ligases capable of ubiquitinating RAS proteins precisely at Lys117 or Lys147 will contribute to resolving this apparent paradox.

## SCF-βTrCP
The SCF-βTrCP E3 ligase complex (Cullin, Skp1, Rbx1, ring-finger, and β-TrCP) recognizes phosphorylated RAS. This recognition occurs via a conserved phosphodegron motif centered on Thr-144 and Thr-148, which are phosphorylated by GSK3β. These phosphorylation sites are evolutionarily conserved across H-, N-, and KRAS isoforms, enabling β-TrCP to bind via its DSG(X)2S motif and mediate K48-linked polyubiquitination, thereby targeting RAS for proteasomal degradation. , , , , Loss of β-TrCP causes the accumulation of RAS proteins and drives tumor progression. In the basal state (Wnt-off), the APC/Axin scaffold assembles the destruction complex (GSK3β/CK1α), which coordinately phosphorylates both β-catenin and RAS. , , , Activation of the Wnt pathway triggers dissociation of this complex, leading to dual oncogenic effects: β-catenin escapes degradation and drives target gene transcription, while impaired GSK3β-mediated phosphorylation at Thr-144/Thr-148 prevents β-TrCP recruitment, stabilizing RAS. , , 
The regulatory dynamics of this system exhibit significant context dependency across cancer types. In CRC, where Wnt signaling is hyperactivated due to APC loss (∼90% of cases), this degradation axis is disrupted, disruption of the destruction complex stabilizes oncogenic RAS. , , , Paradoxically, GSK3β exhibits tissue-specific roles—while its inhibition exacerbates RAS-driven tumorigenesis in CRC, it reduces RAS levels and induces apoptosis in pancreatic cancer. , This dichotomy may arise from GSK3β′s involvement in alternative regulatory pathways, such as LZTR1-mediated RAS stability (as discussed further). β-TrCP also exhibits a dual function. While it suppresses RAS and β-catenin in normal contexts, in pancreatic cancer, β-TrCP promotes Smad4 degradation, exerting oncogenic effects. The role of Axin and CK1α in RAS regulation remains enigmatic, with limited evidence linking them directly to RAS stability. , , , 
The RAS-MAPK and Wnt/β-catenin pathways exhibit context-dependent synergy or antagonism. Apart from the degradation pathways of RAS and β-catenin have in common, β-catenin could directly interact with RAS, thereby preventing GSK3β-dependent phosphorylation and degradation. Intriguingly, in melanoma, enhanced MAPK signaling stabilizes axin, inhibiting Wnt signaling and creating a negative crosstalk between the two pathways. Conversely, chronic treatment with sotorasib (a KRAS inhibitor) in melanoma reactivates Wnt signaling as a resistance mechanism, highlighting compensatory rewiring. Critically, RAS employs the tumor suppressor NORE1A to recruit the SCF-β-TrCP E3 ligase, selectively degrading β-catenin to suppress Wnt signaling which is disrupted in NORE1A-deficient tumors. This duality explains β-TrCP’s context-dependent roles and underscores ubiquitination as a pivotal node for therapeutic intervention in pathway-addicted cancers.

## SMURF2
The HECT-type E3 ligase SMURF2 exhibits a paradoxical dual functionality in RAS protein homeostasis, acting through distinct mechanisms depending on its substrate. Conversely, SMURF2 could act as a direct E3 ligase for KRAS. In the cytosol, SMURF2 binds to prenylation-inactive KRAS and catalyzes its K48-linked polyubiquitination, targeting it for proteasomal degradation. This tumor-suppressive activity is, however, counteracted by the scaffold protein AIMP2-DX2, which sterically blocks SMURF2-KRAS interaction by occupying the hypervariable region (HVR) and G-domain of KRAS. Thus, SMURF2’s ability to degrade KRAS is context-dependent, requiring the absence of competitive inhibitors like AIMP2-DX2. Conversely, SMURF2 indirectly promotes RAS stabilization by targeting the SCF-β-TrCP complex, an E3 ligase responsible for RAS degradation as mentioned previously. Mechanically, SMURF2 monoubiquitinates UBCH5 at K144, forming an active SMURF2:UBCH5 complex. This complex subsequently polyubiquitinates β-TrCP, triggering its proteasomal degradation and thereby attenuating SCF-β-TrCP-mediated RAS ubiquitination. 

## LZTR1
LZTR1, an adaptor for the CUL3 E3 ligase complex, governs RAS protein stability and subcellular localization through site-specific ubiquitination. LZTR1 catalyzes mono-/di-ubiquitination of HRAS at Lys170, attenuating its plasma membrane association and downstream signaling. , Recent studies further reveal its role in polyubiquitination-mediated degradation of RAS isoforms (HRAS, KRAS4A, and NRAS) via the proteasome, targeting lysine residues in HVRs (HRAS Lys170, KRAS4A Lys186) and G-domain (KRAS, HRAS, NRAS, and MRAS Lys117/Lys127). , Pathologically, mutations in LZTR1 were observed in multiple cancers and RASopathies, where impaired ubiquitination drives hyperactivation of the MAPK pathway. , , , , , , , In HCC, KCTD17, an adaptor for the Cul3 ubiquitin ligase complex, could promote RAS stabilization by promoting LZTR1 degradation. Intriguingly, oncogenic RAS mutants (e.g., KRAS G12D/V) evade LZTR1-mediated degradation, likely due to conformational masking of ubiquitination sites or reduced binding affinity, thereby enhancing mutant protein stability and tumor progression. LZTR1 modulates RAS trafficking in an isoform-specific manner. This isoform specificity is likely attributed to the differential localization of RAS isoforms within specialized membrane microdomains, facilitating their selective engagement with effector proteins and regulatory partners. , Notably, LZTR1 may play a pivotal role in organizing the spatial partitioning of distinct RAS isoforms into separate membrane compartments, thus establishing a mechanistic basis for preserving their functional specificity.

## WDR76
WDR76, a CUL4-DDB ubiquitin E3 ligase-interacting protein, is potentially involved in apoptosis, cell cycle regulation, DNA damage repair, and the modulation of gene expression. WDR76 degrades RAS regardless of its mutational status via polyubiquitination-dependent proteasomal degradation. , WDR76-dependent RAS degradation impedes the proliferation, transformation, metastasis, and invasion of HCC. WDR76 also suppresses cancer stem cells and causes cell-cycle arrest in the G0/G1 phase in CRC. , 

## FBXL6
F box and leucine-rich repeat 6 (FBXL6), belonging to the F box family, are substantially upregulated in most human cancers and participate in cell development and differentiation. , Both wild-type KRAS and its mutant variant, KRAS G12D, could be activated by FBXL6 through K63-linked polyubiquitination at the Lys128. This process promotes KRAS binding to Raf, followed by initiating MEK/ERK/mTOR signaling. The synergistic effect of elevated FBXL6 with KRAS G12D triggers hepatocarcinogenesis and lung metastasis. Furthermore, HCC driven by FBXL6 and KRAS mutations was effectively inhibited by concurrent inhibition of MEK and mTOR. How FBXL6-mediated ubiquitination affects the bind of RAS to Raf, as well as whether there is a difference in affinity to PI3K and Raf, are questions that need to be answered.

## Ubiquitin-like modifications of RAS proteins
Beyond canonical ubiquitination, ubiquitin-like modifiers (UbIs) such as ISG15 (IFN-stimulated gene 15) and SUMO (small ubiquitin-like) modifiers employ analogous E1-E2-E3 enzymatic cascades to covalently conjugate to target proteins. Up to now, eight ubiquitin-like proteins (UBLs)—ISG15, SUMO1, ATG8, FAT10, NEDD8, UFM1, URM1, and ATG12—have been identified in humans. , Unlike the main proteolytic roles of ubiquitin, UbIs predominantly fine-tune protein-protein interactions, enzymatic activity, or compartmentalization. It is anticipated that more ubiquitin-like modifications of RAS proteins could be identified.

## ISGylation
ISG15 was discovered as an antiviral ubiquitin-like protein. RAS could undergo ISG15 modification (ISGylation), which modulates their biological functions associated with malignant phenotypes, such as proliferation, migration, and epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT). ISGylation is carried out by a series of enzymes, including E1 UBE1L, E2 UBCH8, and certain E3 ligases, and ISG15-specific proteases such as USP18, along with certain viral proteins. , , , ISGylation protects KRAS proteins against lysosomal degradation, thereby maintaining their stability. Inhibition of ISGylation has the potential to reverse the malignant phenotypes observed in breast cancer cells harboring oncogenic KRAS. Recent studies reveal an unexpected interplay between ISG15 and the UPS: ISG15 modifies ubiquitin itself at Lysine 29, forming ISG15-ubiquitin mixed chains on ubiquitinated substrates. Notably, these hybrid chains do not function as degradation signals but instead impair the proteasomal turnover of ubiquitinated proteins. ISG15-binding lysines are located in the HVR domain of KRAS, which is crucial in anchoring to the plasma membrane. KRAS ISGylation probably mislocalizes KRAS from the plasma membrane. Intriguingly, oncogenic KRAS regulates the ISG15 pathway expression (both free ISG15 and conjugated form of ISG15). Notably, ISGylation of NEDD4 inhibits its E3 ubiquitin ligase activity, potentially preventing the ubiquitination of RAS proteins through modulation of NEDD4 function. , 

## SUMOylation
SUMOylation is facilitated by E1 SAE1/UBA2, E2 UBC9, and E3 ligases. RAS proteins are targets of SUMOylation and RAS SUMOylation activates the MAPK signaling pathway, thus promoting cell growth, migration, invasion and tumorigenesis. , Lysine residues involved in SUMO binding differ from those targeted for ubiquitination. In HRAS, NRAS, and KRAS4B, Lys42 serves as the primary site for SUMO3-mediated SUMOylation. The SUMO moiety is transferred to RAS proteins via the SUMO E3 ligase PIASγ. SUMO-specific peptidase 1/2 (SENP1/2) could remove PIASγ-mediated SUMOylation from KRAS. The necessity of sumoylation in KRAS-driven tumorigenesis is not unexpected. KRAS-mutant colon cancer cell growth is attenuated by inhibition of the SUMO pathway through the depletion of ubiquitin-conjugating enzyme 9 (UCB9). SUMOylation and ubiquitination dynamically interact by modifying shared lysine residues on substrates, either competitively inhibiting or cooperatively promoting ubiquitination, thereby regulating proteasomal degradation and contributing to cancer progression through dysregulated crosstalk. , , 

## The deubiquitinases of RAS protein
Deubiquitinases (DUBs) reverse ubiquitination by cleaving the isopeptide bond between ubiquitin and substrates to recycle monoubiquitin. , To date, five families of deubiquitinating enzymes have been identified: ubiquitin-specific proteases (USPs), ovarian tumor proteases (OTUs), ubiquitin C-terminal hydrolases (UCHs), Josephine and JAB1/MPN/MOV34 metalloproteases (JAMMs). Several deubiquitinases involved in the regulation of RAS functions have been identified ( ). 

## USP17
USP17, a subfamily of cytokine-inducible DUBs, could regulate the growth and differentiation of leukocytes. USP17 constrains RAS protein activities by disrupting their membrane localization and post-translational processing. , Specifically, RAS needs the three C-terminal amino acids -AAX to be removed by Ras-converting enzyme 1 (RCE1) to ensure proper localization and function. USP17 blocks RCE1 activity by deubiquitinating RCE1, leading to the mislocation of RAS. In addition to RAS proteins, USP17 has been demonstrated to block subcellular relocalization of other GTPases. USP17 inhibition leads to inappropriate GTPase activation and increased levels of the downstream cyclin-dependent kinase inhibitors P21 and P27, which arrest the cell cycle in the G1/S phase and block cell proliferation. USP17 blocks subcellular relocalization of GTPase essential for cell motility (such as Cdc42, Rac, and RhoA), which could promote cancer metastasis. Moreover, DUB CYLD (cylindromatosis gene) has recently been shown to govern cell migration via the GTPase Rac1. It is still unclear, however, how CYLD regulates RAS activity.

## OTUB1
OTUB1, a member of the OUT (ovarian tumor protease) family, exhibits complex and context-dependent roles in cancer progression. It is frequently overexpressed in non-small cell lung cancers (NSCLCs), prostate, colorectal, and breast cancers, correlated with poor survival outcomes, elevated metastatic potential and resistance to chemotherapeutic agents. , , , In KRAS wild-type tumors such as NSCLC and CRC, OTUB1 blocks mono-/di-ubiquitination of RAS through a non-catalytic mechanism, anchoring it at the plasma membrane to sustain MAPK pathway activation and drive tumor progression. , Analysis of TCGA data reveals a significant mutual exclusivity between OTUB1 overexpression and KRAS mutations. This observation may indicate a functional compensation mechanism, wherein OTUB1-mediated maintenance of RAS membrane localization substitutes for the effects of oncogenic KRAS mutations. As such, OTUB1 is likely to exhibit preferential oncogenic activity in KRAS wild-type cellular contexts. In addition to RAS, OTUB1 was reported to stabilize tumor suppressors such as p53. This duality underscores the importance of cellular context—such as RAS mutation status and coexisting genetic alterations—in determining OTUB1’s functional output.

## USP9X/NDRG3
USP9X collaborates with scaffolding proteins (e.g., NDRG3) to spatially coordinate KRAS deubiquitination, offering a mechanistic contrast to direct DUB-substrate interactions observed in other RAS regulatory pathways. The scaffolding protein NDRG3 bridges KRAS and the DUB USP9X, enabling KRAS deubiquitination and stabilization. This interaction occurs via the C-terminal serine/threonine-rich region of NDRG3, which binds KRAS while recruiting USP9X to the complex. By exploiting the spatial orchestration of deubiquitination, therapeutic disruption of this complex offers a mutation-selective approach to destabilize KRAS—a strategy with the potential to circumvent resistance to catalytic DUB inhibitors. 

## USP7
USP7, a prominent deubiquitinating enzyme (DUB), regulates protein stability and oncogenic signaling through its catalytic removal of ubiquitin chains from substrates. Structurally, USP7 contains TRAF and UBL domains, which enable interactions with diverse targets. Emerging evidence highlights USP7 as a critical regulator of RAS proteins, particularly KRAS, a major driver of NSCLC. USP7 directly interacts with KRAS via its TRAF domain, catalyzing the removal of K48-linked polyubiquitin chains at lysine residue 147 (K147). This deubiquitination stabilizes both wild-type and mutant KRAS isoforms, enhancing RAS-driven therapy resistance. Clinically, elevated USP7 expression correlates with KRAS levels in NSCLC and predicts poorer patient survival. Importantly, USP7 inhibitors suppress NSCLC, even in KRAS-G12C inhibitor-resistance models, positioning USP as a strategic therapeutic node to circumvent KRAS pathway addiction. Beyond KRAS regulation, USP7 modulates other cancer-related pathways. For instance, it stabilizes MDM2, an E3 ligase that promotes p53 degradation, indirectly influencing p53 tumor-suppressor activity. Notably, USP-7 targeting strategies, such as the PROteolysis-targeting chimera (PROTAC) molecule U7D-1, have shown promise in selectively degrading USP7 and inhibiting growth in P53-mutant cancers, further emphasizing its therapeutic potential. 

## USP18
Deubiquitinase USP18, primarily responsible for ISG15 removal from substrate proteins, promotes the stability of KRAS in lung cancer. , , Loss of USP18 leads to KRAS expression and changes KRAS subcellular compartmentalization by mislocating KRAS from the plasma membrane. This finding highlights that the effects of KRAS ISGylation are likely tissue- and cancer-specific. Independent of its ISG15 deconjugation activity, USP18 could also negatively regulate IFN signaling. Of great interest, many proteins regulated by ISG15 and USP18 are involved in the KRAS signaling transduction. Simultaneously destabilizing KRAS and other USP18-regulated proteins downstream of KRAS may have a potential role in reducing the occurrence of KRAS-mutant lung cancers.

## Exploring RAS ubiquitination for cancer therapy
The aberrant stabilization and constitutive activation of RAS proteins drive tumorigenesis by hyperactivating downstream oncogenic signaling pathways. The clinical success of mutant RAS selective inhibitors, such as KRASG12C inhibitors sotorasib and adagrasib has shed light on targeting RAS-driven cancers. , , , Apart from selective inhibitors, the Pan-KRAS inhibitor removes the covalent warhead from the KRAS G12C-selective inhibitor. Multi-selective inhibitors RMC-6236 and RMC-7977, non-covalently targeting RAS protein, could inhibit WT RAS and various RAS variant proteins. , Despite significant progress in developing RAS-directed inhibitors, these therapeutics are subject to certain limitations: (1) dominant therapies target the G12C mutation (∼13% of KRAS mutations), leaving prevalent G12D (∼40%) and G12V (∼25%) variants in pancreatic and colorectal cancers untreatable due to the absence of targetable switch II pockets or reactive cysteine residues. , (2) Epigenetic rewiring or bypass signaling (e.g., NRAS amplification, EGFR feedback) fosters resistance. , , , , (3) Poor cell permeability and off-target effects limit the efficacy of certain compounds. Thus, more alternative treatments are urgently needed to target RAS addiction cancer. The UPS dynamically regulates RAS stability through E3 ligases and deubiquitinases. 

## PROTACs
Mutant KRAS (e.g., G12D) frequently evades proteasomal degradation due to impaired recognition by E3 ubiquitin ligases (e.g., LZTR1), which are critical for tagging oncoproteins with degradation signals. To address this limitation, recent advances in targeted protein degradation (TPD) technologies—particularly PROTACs—have expanded therapeutic options. PROTACs are bifunctional molecules that artificially hijack the UPS by recruiting E3 ligases to substrates. Specifically, RAS-directed PROTACs bind mutant KRAS via a warhead (e.g., KRASG12D inhibitor) while engaging an E3 ligase (e.g., VHL or CRBN) through a linker, forming a ternary complex that triggers substrate-specific ubiquitination and proteasomal degradation. , A summary of PROTACs of RAS is in . PROTACs leverage endogenous E3 ligases (e.g., VHL, CRBN) to ubiquitinate RAS, directing proteasomal degradation ( ). For instance, LC-2, a KRAS G12C-directed PROTAC, induces sustained degradation by covalently binding the mutant protein and recruiting VHL. Clinical trials, such as the phase I study of ASP3082 (NCT05382559), are evaluating KRAS G12D-specific degraders ( ). Although there are over 600 E3 ubiquitin ligases, only four—VHL, MDM2, IAPs, and CRBN—are currently utilized in PROTAC. , Systematic screening platforms like AdPROM (affinity-directed protein missile) are uncovering novel E3s for RAS degradation. The AdPROM system recruits endogenous E3 ligases via high-affinity binders (e.g., monobodies) to degrade RAS. , To bypass endogenous E3 ligase deficiencies, chemical PROTACs exploit conserved PTMs like prenylation to covalently tag RAS and directly engage the proteasome, enabling mutation-agnostic degradation. Moreover, E3-embedded degraders, RC-U, an engineered E3 ligase fusing a KRAS-binding motif with the U-box domain of CHIP, significantly reduce KRAS levels. DARPin-VHL/NS1 Fusion, a macromolecular degrader combining KRAS-targeting DARPins with VHL or NS1 monobodies, inhibits KRAS-driven cancer cell proliferation. By engineering exogenous E3 ligases to enforce ubiquitination of recalcitrant mutants like KRASG12V, dTAG System not only suppresses tumor-intrinsic signaling but also reprograms the immunosuppressive microenvironmental dual mechanism highlighting the immunological benefits of ubiquitination-based therapies. Moreover, Pan-RAS degradation systems, a U-box E3 fusion linked to a pan-RAS intracellular single-domain antibody (iDAb), degrades all RAS isoforms regardless of mutations. In addition to RAS proteins, PROTACs targeting various components of the RAS signaling pathway, including MEK, PDEδ, TBK1, and SHP2, have effectively degraded these corresponding targets and inhibited RAS signaling in vitro or in vivo. , , PROTAC has expanded the scope of USP-based therapies of RAS proteins. 
Strikingly, proof-of-concept KLHDC2-recruiting PROTACs successfully degraded tagged proteins, offering a blueprint for developing RAS-directed degraders. Unlike traditional PROTACs that rely on E3 ligase, these novel degraders directly recruit E2 ubiquitin-conjugating enzymes to KRAS, bypassing E3 dependency to induce ubiquitination and proteasomal degradation. This E3-independent approach complements existing strategies (e.g., KLHDC2-PROTAC) and broadens targetability to E3-resistant RAS mutants. Since ubiquitination regulates the transports, transcription activity and protein-protein interaction of RAS proteins, potential PROTAC usage based on non-degradable ubiquitination is worth further exploration. , Moreover, PROTAC molecules, such as ACBI3 and ASP3082 face limitations in terms of administration routes (e.g., subcutaneous, intraperitoneal, or intravenous delivery), which diminishes their suitability for chronic treatment compared to orally bioavailable KRAS inhibitors like adagrasib. Their large size also limits oral absorption and tumor penetration. Additionally, the potential off-target effects and risks related to immune modulation remain inadequately defined. Furthermore, Pan-RAS degraders may disrupt normal RAS functions, emphasizing the need for isoform- and mutation-specific designs to balance efficacy and toxicity.

## Directly targeting components of the UPS
The UPS provides multiple therapeutic targets for modulating RAS-driven tumorigenesis, with E3 ubiquitin ligases being particularly promising. As primary mediators of substrate-specific ubiquitination, E3 ligases regulate RAS protein stability and activity. However, their therapeutic use faces challenges: most E3 ligases act on diverse substrates beyond RAS proteins, and their catalytic mechanisms are incompletely understood. A deeper understanding of E3 enzymatic dynamics, including substrate recognition and ubiquitin chain topology, is essential for developing selective RAS-targeted therapies. In parallel, deubiquitinase (DUB) inhibitors offer complementary strategies. By blocking DUB-mediated deubiquitination, these compounds enhance RAS protein degradation. Preclinical studies show that DUB inhibitors destabilize oncogenic RAS variants and suppress tumor growth. Moreover, targeting E3 ligases involved in PTMs such as ISGylation and SUMOylation may expand therapeutic options. These pathways influence RAS membrane localization and signaling crosstalk, but their functional redundancy and tissue-specific roles necessitate further validation before clinical translation. Combining the proteasome inhibitor carfilzomib with HRS-4642 (a non-covalent KRASG12D inhibitor) synergistically enhanced tumor suppression and promoted immune-permissive microenvironment remodeling, suggesting dual targeting of KRAS signaling and protein stability as a viable therapeutic approach. 

## Indirectly targeting components of the UPS
Several small molecules have been identified to indirectly induce RAS instability. Previous studies have demonstrated that RAS and β-catenin oncogenic mutations act synergistically to yield tumor transformation. , , As mentioned previously, the co-stabilization of β-catenin and RAS driven by Wnt/β-catenin pathway hyperactivation (e.g., via APC or β-catenin mutations) represents a critical oncogenic vulnerability. Targeting this axis through dual degradation of both proteins has emerged as a promising therapeutic strategy. Small molecules such as KY1220 and KY1022 bind the Axin RGS domain to enhance β-catenin destruction complex assembly. , Their optimized derivative, KYA1797K, exhibits broader efficacy by promoting GSK3β-mediated phosphorylation of β-catenin (S33/S37/T41) and KRAS (T144/T148). , These phosphorylation events recruit the β-TrCP E3 ligase, driving polyubiquitination and proteasomal degradation of both targets. Notably, KYA1797K degrades all RAS isoforms (HRAS, NRAS, and KRAS) regardless of mutational status, bypassing mutation-dependent resistance through their conserved GSK3β phosphorylation motif. Destabilization of both β-catenin and RAS through KYA1797K could be an effective therapeutic strategy for breast cancer and CRC. , , WNT974 (also known as LGK974) is a porcupine inhibitor that interferes Wnt signaling pathway. WNT974 and artesunate combination treatment increases E3 ligases ANAPC2 and β-TrCP and the level of GSK-3β, as a result, enhances RAS protein degradation via the ubiquitin-proteasome pathway. Another small molecule, KY7749, has been demonstrated to degrade RAS independently of the Wnt pathway and suppress the transformation and proliferation of CRC cells. 

## Discussion
The ubiquitination of RAS proteins exerts multifaceted control over their stability activity and subcellular localization, with profound implications for oncogenic signaling. A feature of RAS ubiquitination is its site-specificity, which governs not only proteasomal targeting but also conformational states and signaling outputs. Lysine residues on RAS act as multifaceted hubs for diverse PTMs, including ubiquitination, acetylation, methylation, and SUMOylation, collectively forming a dynamic “PTM code” that fine-tunes oncogenic potential. For instance, methylation of KRAS at Lys182/Lys184 by SETD7 licenses subsequent K48-linked polyubiquitination by RABGEF1, suppressing NSCLC tumorigenesis. However, certain ubiquitin sites on RAS proteins also serve as substrates for competing PTMs. Lys104 and Lys147 have been shown to undergo both ubiquitination and acetylation. , , As mono-ubiquitination and acetylation are mutually exclusive, this antagonism suggests that ubiquitination at Lys104 or Lys147 may spatially or temporally restrict acetylation—or vice versa—thereby modulating RAS activity through a “PTM switch”. , Such crosstalk underscores the need to resolve how site-specific modifications allosterically regulate RAS conformation, GTP-binding kinetics, and effector interactions. Systematic mapping of PTM hotspots (e.g., Lys104, Lys147) and their combinatorial logic could reveal novel therapeutic strategies to disrupt oncogenic RAS signaling by hijacking its PTM code.
Critically, achieving such precise disruption requires more accurate identification of ubiquitination sites, ubiquitin chain types and the underlying molecular mechanisms. Consequently, diverse detection methodologies have been developed to resolve these features. Traditional biochemical techniques, such as chain-specific antibodies and tandem ubiquitin-binding entities, , remain essential for linkage validation. Real-time approaches, including Ub-FRET biosensors, enable live-cell monitoring of deubiquitinase activity, exemplified in mitochondrial autophagy studies. , High-throughput innovations like nanopore sequencing now profile non-canonical chains without antibodies, while quantitative mass spectrometry (DiGly/AQUA) offers absolute site-specific quantification in proteome-wide screens. , Functional validation via CRISPR-mediated lysine mutagenesis (e.g., KRAS K128R) bridges detection to mechanistic insights. 
Ubiquitination-dependent spatial regulation shapes the functional divergence among RAS isoforms. While ubiquitinated H- and N-RAS are recognized by the ESCRT-III complex for endosomal recycling—a process hijacked to amplify oncogenic signaling—KRAS lacks this regulatory axis, relying instead on alternative trafficking routes. This isoform-specific regulation extends to splice variants: KRAS4A, but not KRAS4B, engages the Golgi-localized E2/E3 ligase BIRC6, which selectively ubiquitinates KRAS4A to sustain its pro-tumorigenic activity. Such regulatory dichotomy underscores the importance of isoform- and subcompartment-specific studies when designing RAS-targeted therapies. The landscape is further complicated by tissue-specific roles of RAS isoforms and their ubiquitination dynamics. KRAS mutations dominate in pancreatic, lung, and colorectal cancers, whereas NRAS and HRAS are prevalent in melanoma and bladder cancers, respectively. , This selectivity arises from isoform-specific expression levels, differential signaling potency, DNA repair efficiency, codon usage biases or mutational crosstalk within the RAS pathway. Adding another layer of complexity is the context-dependent role of wild-type (WT) RAS. In CRC, WT RAS promotes tumorigenesis through mutant-induced autocrine growth factor loops or by competing for GAPs. , , Conversely, in lung cancer, WT RAS suppresses mutant RAS-driven tumors via mutant-WT heterodimerization. , Segregated nanoclustering prevents cross-isoform suppression, as evidenced in endometrial cancer where WT KRAS acts tumor-suppressively but WT HRAS does not. , , Ubiquitination modulates this duality—LZTR1-Cullin3 degrades both WT and mutant RAS, yet tissue-specific allelic imbalances (e.g., WT KRAS loss in endometrial cancer) can amplify oncogenic signaling. Consequently, therapeutic strategies targeting this plasticity must account for isoform- and tissue-specific contexts to avoid paradoxical effects on tumor-suppressive functions of WT RAS.
Beyond canonical ubiquitination, emerging regulators like RNF141 redefine how E3 ligase family members control RAS activity. By orchestrating KRAS membrane localization rather than its ubiquitination status, RNF141 exemplifies the functional versatility of RING proteins in cancer biology, offering new therapeutic entry points for RAS-driven malignancies. USP39 is a non-catalytic member of the USP deubiquitinases family that is thought to play a crucial role in pre-mRNA splicing. Growing evidence indicates the involvement of USP39 in the progression of cancers. , Particularly, there is a strong correlation between USP39 expression and KRAS levels in both lung and colon cancer. The postulated synthetic lethal link between USP39 and KRAS in cancer may arise from the splicing-related activity of USP, indicating that tumors with active KRAS would particularly depend on efficient pre-mRNA splicing. These findings collectively expand the regulatory landscape of RAS, positioning ubiquitin system components as multifaceted modifiers of RAS biology—whether through direct ubiquitination, spatial regulation or splicing-mediated expression control.
As showed in , RAS functional outputs are governed by a delicate balance between ubiquitination, deubiquitination, and degradation. In normal cells, inactive RAS-GDP undergoes K48-linked polyubiquitination via the coordinated actions of E1 ubiquitin-activating, E2 ubiquitin-conjugating, and E3 ligase enzymes, marking it for proteasomal degradation. DUBs modulate this process by trimming ubiquitin chains to prevent excessive degradation and facilitate signal recovery. Complementing this system, the autophagy-lysosome pathway selectively eliminates misfolded RAS aggregates through recognition of K63-linked polyubiquitin tags, functioning as a fail-safe quality control mechanism. Collectively, these processes maintain the half-life of KRAS (∼24 h) and enable pulsatile RAS-ERK signaling oscillations essential for tissue homeostasis and stimulus-responsive proliferation or differentiation. , However, malignant cells may disrupt this equilibrium through multifaceted dysregulation. Hypomorphic E1 mutations in Drosophila induce cell-autonomous RAS-ERK hyperactivation and ectopic proliferation, , while Loss of the E3 ligase Smurf2 destabilizes mutant KRAS, decreasing its half-life from >12 h to approximately 2.5 h. This mechanism extends beyond canonical RAS subtypes (KRAS, NRAS, and HRAS). Studies demonstrate that LZTR1-Cullin3-mediated dysregulation of non-canonical RAS proteins (e.g., RIT1, MRAS) contributes to leukemia drug resistance and hematopoietic stem cell transformation. Tumor cells exploit the overexpression of DUBs, such as USP7 in NSCLC, to stabilize KRAS and evade degradation mediated by PROTACs. , Paradoxically, the 26S proteasome is frequently hyperactive in cancers, driving the degradation of tumor suppressors and cell cycle regulators. , This dichotomy is therapeutically exploited through the use of proteasome inhibitors, such as bortezomib, which selectively target hematologic malignancies that are dependent on proteotoxic stress tolerance for survival. Autophagic degradation of polyubiquitinated RAS also plays a crucial role in cancer. 4-hydroxytamoxifen (OHT) induces autophagic clearance of KRAS in estrogen receptor-negative malignancies, while flunarizine (FLN) selectively degrades NRAS via autophagy in basal-like breast cancer. , This cross-talk between UPS and autophagy allows dynamic rewiring of RAS homeostasis, underscoring the adaptability of cancer cells to proteostatic stress.

## Conclusion and future perspectives
The ubiquitination of RAS proteins represents a critical regulatory mechanism in tumorigenesis, modulating RAS stability, subcellular localization, and downstream signaling output. While wide-type RAS undergoes tightly controlled ubiquitin-dependent degradation to prevent oncogenic signaling, oncogenic RAS mutants frequently evade ubiquitin-mediated turnover due to impaired interactions with E3 ligases or altered recognition by ubiquitination machinery. This dysregulation leads to aberrant RAS accumulation, hyperactivation of MAPK and PI3K-AKT pathways and sustained tumor growth. Emerging evidence highlights the therapeutic potential of targeting RAS ubiquitination, including strategies to restore E3 ligase activity, develop PROTACs to degrade mutant RAS or inhibit DUBs that stabilize oncogenic RAS isoforms. However, challenges remain, including the tissue-specificity of ubiquitination regulators, compensatory crosstalk between RAS isoforms and the dynamic interplay between ubiquitination and other PTMs. Future studies should prioritize several aspects. Firstly, resolving the structural architecture of RAS-ubiquitin ligase complexes is essential for identifying druggable interfaces that govern ubiquitination-dependent oncogenic signaling. Secondly, therapies tailored to tissue-specific ubiquitination networks require further study. Thirdly, future efforts should focus on exploiting crosstalk between ubiquitination and other RAS modifications for combinatorial interventions. Fourthly, it has great potential to validate ubiquitination-related biomarkers (e.g., ubiquitinated RAS levels) to predict therapeutic response. Last but not the least, restoring RAS dynamics balance, rather than unilateral inhibition, may define the next generation of RAS-targeted therapies
