## Summary

## Background
Ovarian cancer (OC) is the most aggressive and lethal gynaecological cancer and is characterised by abnormal lipid metabolism. Enhancer of zeste homologue 2 (EZH2) is the catalytic subunit of polycomb repressive complex 2 (PRC2). It functions as a tumour promoter through both canonical H3K27me3 modification and noncanonical mechanisms. Although EZH2 has been developed as a potential anticancer target, the therapeutic effects of EZH2 inhibitors in solid tumours are still limited.

## Methods
The levels of fatty acid and tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle-related metabolites were measured via high-performance liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (HPLC-MS/MS). EZH2-knockout cells, ChIP‒qPCR, qPCR, and western blotting were used to investigate the regulatory effect of EZH2 on FADS2. The expression of FADS2 transcript variants and their correlations with prognosis in clinical OC tissues were analysed via the GEPIA 2 database. Various OC cell lines, patient-derived organoids and tumour-bearing models have been used to test the therapeutic effects of EZH2 and FADS2 inhibitors. 4D-DIA proteomics, the Seahorse assay and flow cytometry-based detection of mitochondrial function and protein synthesis were performed to explore the mechanism of combined therapy.

## Findings
EZH2 knockout increased FADS2 variant 1 (v1) and decreased variant 2/3 (v2/3) at both the mRNA and protein levels. EZH2 occupied the promoters of FADS2 v1 and v2/3, but the enrichment of H3K27me3 at the v2/v3 promoter was significantly lower than that at the v1 promoter. Furthermore, EZH2 inhibitors increased full-length FADS2 v1 expression and polyunsaturated fatty acid abundance in OC cells. A high FADS2 v1/v2 ratio was associated with poor prognosis in patients with OC. Targeting FADS2 with shRNAs or inhibitors reduced EZH2 levels and sensitised cancer cells to EZH2 inhibitors. The combined inhibition of EZH2 and FADS2 induced mitochondrial dysfunction and energy stress and synergised to reduce tumour growth in vitro and in vivo.

## Interpretation
EZH2 inhibition increases the proportion of the full FADS2 isoform through differential regulation, which suggests a poor prognosis in OC patients. Simultaneous inhibition of EZH2 and FADS2 causes mitochondrial dysfunction and energy stress in OC, resulting in a more pronounced antitumour effect. Our study reveals a mechanistic connection between EZH2 and FADS2 and provides a potential therapeutic strategy for OC.

## Research in context

## Evidence before this study
EZH2 promotes tumour progression through multiple mechanisms. EZH2 can inhibit gene transcription through histone methylation and act as a coactivator to promote transcription. EZH2 inhibitors increase lipid synthesis in cancer cells, which weakens the therapeutic effect on tumours. FADS2 is the key enzyme in long-chain polyunsaturated fatty acid synthesis. Increased expression of FADS2 is associated with ovarian cancer metastasis. FADS2 transcript variant 1 encodes the full-length isoform, and isoforms encoded by transcript variants 2 and 3 are predicted to lack desaturase activity.

## Added value of this study
We demonstrated that EZH2 differentially regulates the transcription of FADS2 transcript variants 1 and 2/3. A high FADS2 variant ratio (variant 1/2) suggests a poor prognosis in patients with ovarian cancer. These results reveal the regulatory mechanism and clinical significance of FADS2 transcripts. FADS2 silencing reduces the level of EZH2 and increases vulnerability to EZH2 inhibition. The combination treatment of EZH2 and FADS2 inhibitors synergistically inhibited Notch signalling and mitochondrial ATP production, leading to more promising therapeutic effects both in vitro and in vivo. This study reveals the mechanism by which FADS2 promotes tumour growth, especially under EZH2 inhibitor treatment.

## Implications of all the available evidence
The differential regulation of FADS2 by EZH2 provides a combined therapeutic target for ovarian cancer. The expression, function and regulation of different FADS2 transcripts in various tumours deserve further investigation. Our findings provide a better understanding of the crosstalk between epigenetic modifications and fatty acid metabolism and offer a promising therapeutic strategy for ovarian cancer.

## Introduction
Ovarian cancer (OC) is the most aggressive and lethal gynaecological cancer. The majority of patients have advanced disease and a poor prognosis. Although most patients initially respond to platinum-based chemotherapy, the high frequency of drug resistance and recurrence hinders the success of anticancer treatment. Therefore, there is an urgent need to develop new therapeutic targets and strategies.
Enhancer of zeste homologue 2 (EZH2) is the catalytic subunit of polycomb repressive complex 2 (PRC2). It functions as a histone methyltransferase and catalyses the trimethylation of histone H3K27 (H3K27me3) to mediate transcriptional silencing. The overexpression of EZH2 has been found in OC and other human malignancies and is often associated with cancer progression and poor prognosis. Several EZH2 inhibitors have been developed as potential anticancer therapeutic agents, of which EPZ6438 has been approved by the FDA. However, the clinical efficacy of EZH2 inhibitors remains unsatisfactory and is limited to certain malignancies. Recent studies have revealed that EZH2 has noncanonical functions as a transcriptional coactivator or methylating nonhistone substrates. Thus, targeting EZH2 histone methyltransferase activity is not sufficient to completely block its oncogenic functions. , 
The reprogramming of lipid metabolism has been recognised as a metabolic feature of cancer cells. Genetically elevated polyunsaturated fatty acid (PUFA) desaturase activity was associated with higher risk of several types of tumours. Our previous studies developed a model based on genes involved in PUFA metabolism to predict the prognosis of patients with OC. PUFAs are bioactive components of membrane phospholipids and are involved in membrane physiology and cell signalling. The biosynthesis of long-chain PUFAs (LCPUFAs) from dietary precursors requires the addition of double bonds and chain elongation. These reactions are catalysed by fatty acid desaturases (FADS2, FADS1) and fatty acid elongases (ELOVL5, ELOVL2). FADS2 catalyses the initial Δ6 desaturation step, which is the rate-limiting step for LCPUFA biosynthesis. Aberrant expression of FADS2 is associated with the tumour aggressiveness of ascites-derived OC cells, and FADS2 inhibitor SC26196 synergistically represses OC with cisplatin treatment. How FADS2 functions in tumour cells is not well understood. Our previous studies revealed that EZH2 inhibition upregulated lipid biosynthesis genes in melanoma and liver cancer cells. There are no reports on the regulatory relationship between EZH2 and FADS2.
There are three unique transcripts of the human FADS2 gene (NCBI Gene Reference ID:9415). Transcript variant 1 encodes the full-length FADS2 isoform, which contains a cytochrome b5-haem binding domain with a 4-amino acid motif (HPGG) that is essential for haem binding and desaturase activity. , Transcript variant 2 and variant 3 encode proteins that share close to 90% sequence identity with variant 1, differing only in the N-terminal region. Both variants 2 and 3 lack the HPGG motif and the cytochrome b5-haem binding domain and are thought to lack desaturase activity. Variant 2 is expressed at significantly higher levels than variant 1 in breast cancer cells and negatively regulates FADS2 activity when co-transfected with variant 1. The mechanisms regulating the expression of different FADS2 variants remain elusive.
In this study, we found that EZH2 inhibition in OC differentially regulates the expression of FADS2 variants, leading to an increased abundance of PUFAs. Targeting FADS2 decreased EZH2 levels and sensitised cancer cells to EZH2 inhibitors. Importantly, the combined inhibition of EZH2 and FADS2 led to mitochondrial dysfunction and activated the sensor of energy stress, AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK), leading to enhanced inhibition of OC growth. These findings suggest a functional link between EZH2 and FADS2 and provide a potential therapeutic strategy for OC.

## Methods
Detailed procedures for proteomics and metabolite analysis are provided in the .

## Cell culture
All the cell lines were obtained from the Cell Resources Center of Peking Union Medical College (Beijing, China). OVCAR8 (RRID: CVCL_1629), HEY (RRID: CVCL_0297), and A2780 (RRID: CVCL_0134) cells were maintained in RPMI 1640 medium (HyClone), SKOV3 (RRID: CVCL_0532) cells were grown in F12 medium (HyClone), and ID8 (RRID: CVCL_IU14) cells were cultured in DMEM (HyClone) supplemented with 10% foetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco), 50 IU/mL penicillin, and 50 mg/mL streptomycin (GIBCO). The cells were cultured at 37 °C in a humidified incubator in the presence of 5% CO2. All of the cell lines used in the current study were tested for mycoplasma contamination.

## Organoid preparation and culture
Surgical samples of high-grade serous ovarian cancer from patients (n = 5) were collected for organoid culture. The clinical information of the patients is provided in the .
OC tissues were washed with PBS, sectioned in Gentle Cell Dissociation Reagent (Stem Cell), and digested for 30 min at room temperature. After centrifugation for 5 min at 300×g, the pellets were resuspended in 1 mL of DMEM (with 1% BSA) and filtered through a 70-μm cell strainer. After centrifuging again, the pellets were resuspended in DMEM, mixed with Matrigel at a 1:1 ratio, and added to 24-well plates (50 μL per well). Finally, the organoids were cultured with OC organoid medium (Precedo, China) in 5% CO2 at 37 °C.

## Organoid chemosensitivity assay
After preparation, the organoids were cultured for one week and subjected to a chemosensitivity assay. The organoids were placed into a 96-well plate (20 μL per well). After four days of culture, the EZH2 inhibitor or FADS2 inhibitor was added, and the mixture was incubated for an additional three days. Organoids were examined and photographed via a Leica DMi1 microscope and LAS X software (Leica). Organoid viability was evaluated via the CellTiter-Glo 3D Cell Viability Assay (Promega) and the GloMax Luminometer (Promega).

## Compounds
The EZH2 inhibitors GSK126 and EPZ6438 were obtained from Selleck. The FADS2 inhibitor SC26196, the PROTAC EZH2 degrader-1, the AMPK inhibitor compound C (dorsomorphin), the γ-secretase inhibitor nirogacestat, MG132, cycloheximide, and oligomycin were purchased from MedChemExpress.

## Real-time quantitative PCR (real-time qPCR)
Real-time qPCR was performed using SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme) and the Applied Biosystems QuantStudio 5 system (Applied Biosystems, CA, USA). For mRNA level detection, total RNA was extracted from cells with TRIzol (Invitrogen) and reverse transcribed into cDNA (Vazyme). Beta-actin (ACTB) was used as a housekeeping gene control to normalise the expression levels.
For mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) copy number detection, total cell DNA was extracted (Zymo) and quantified. Then, mtDNA and nuclear DNA (nucDNA) were measured via quantitative PCR. The mtDNA content was normalised to the nucDNA content.
The primers utilised for qPCR are shown in the .

## RNA interference
The negative control (shNC), FADS2 (shFADS2), and ELOVL2 (shELOVL2) shRNA plasmids were purchased from GeneChem (Shanghai, China). To obtain stable cell lines, the lentiviral expression vector (shFADS2 or shNC) and packaging vector (pMD2. G and psPAX2) were co-transfected into HEK293T cells to produce the virus. The supernatants containing the virus were harvested after 48 h. The OC cells were then infected with the harvested virus and selected with puromycin.

## Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) assay
SKOV3 EZH2-KO and WT cells were plated in 10 cm dishes. The cells were fixed with 1% formaldehyde for 10 min at room temperature. Glycine was added to a final concentration of 0.125 M at room temperature for 5 min to stop the reaction. The cells were scraped into cold PBS supplemented with proteinase inhibitor and collected in a 15 mL tube. The ChIP assay was performed via the SimpleChIP Plus Enzymatic Chromatin IP Kit (Magnetic Beads) (Cell Signalling Technology, #9005), anti-EZH2 (Cell Signalling Technology, #5246), and anti-histone H3K27me3 (Cell Signalling Technology, #9733) according to the procedures provided by the manufacturer. The final ChIP DNA samples were then subjected to qPCR analysis. The sequences of the primers are shown in the .

## Western blot
Western blotting was performed using the cell lysate following standard protocols. Briefly, proteins were separated via 4–20% precast PAGE Gel (KeyGen BioTech, China, Cat#: KGC4716-2), transferred to PVDF membranes, and subjected to immunoblotting with the indicated primary antibodies. The membranes were incubated with appropriate secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. LI-COR IRDye-linked antibodies (Lincoln, Cat# 925-68071, RRID: AB_2721181, Cat# 925-68072, RRID: AB_2814912) were used to detect β-actin, α-tubulin and S6, and the membranes were visualised via an Odyssey Imager (LICOR Biosciences). HRP-linked antibodies (Cat#: ZB-2301, ZB-2305, ZSGB-Bio) were used for other target proteins, and the results were obtained with enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) reagent (Cat#: P1050-100, Applygen). All the antibodies were validated by the commercial vendor. The primary antibodies used are described in the .

## Cell proliferation analysis
The cells were seeded in 96-well plates (approximately 5000 cells per well) and treated with the indicated drugs after 24 h. The experiment was performed in quintuplicate. The cells were imaged (phase contrast) via the IncuCyte S3 platform (Sartorius, Göttingen, Germany). Four sets of phase contrast images from distinct regions within each well were taken at intervals of 3 h via a 10X objective. The IncuCyte S3 image analysis software was used to detect the edges of the cells and determine their confluence. Cellular ATP levels were detected via the CellTiter-Glo 3D Cell Viability Assay (Promega) and normalised via cell imaging counting.
Cell viability was determined by a CCK-8 assay. After being cultured in 96-well plates and treated with drugs for 48 or 72 h, 10 μL of CCK-8 (Dojindo Laboratories, Rockville, MA, USA) was added to each well, and the absorbance was measured at 450 nm 1 h later via a multimode microplate reader (Tecan Spark). The combination index (CI) was calculated via CompuSyn software. A CI < 1 indicates a synergistic effect.

## Wound healing assay
The cells were seeded in 96-well plates. When the cells reached 90–100% confluence, the IncuCyte® WoundMaker tool was used to create scratches within a cell monolayer. When cell invasion was detected, Matrigel (Corning) was added to the wells. The medium was changed to serum-free medium. Then, the cells were imaged (phase contrast) via the IncuCyte S3 platform. Two sets of phase contrast images from distinct regions within each well were taken at intervals of 3 h via a 10X objective. The scratched area was measured via IncuCyte S3 image analysis software.

## Seahorse assays
SKOV3 cells were plated onto XF96 cell culture microplates (5000 cells per well) (Agilent Technologies). Then, the cells were treated with GSK126 (10 μM), EPZ6438 (40 μM), SC26196 (30 μM) or their combination for 24 h. The oxygen consumption rates (OCRs) were measured on an XFe96 extracellular flux analyser (Agilent Technologies). OCR traces were recorded in response to oligomycin (1.5 μM), FCCP (1 μM), rotenone and antimycin (0.5 μM each) according to the instructions of the XF Cell Mito Stress Test Kit (Agilent). The data were normalised via cell imaging counting via an Operetta CLS high-content cell imaging system (PerkinElmer).

## Flow cytometry
For cell cycle detection, OC cells were harvested with trypsin and washed with PBS. The cells were fixed in 70% ethanol. Fixed cells were then stained via the Cell Cycle Detection Kit (KeyGen BioTech, China) following the manufacturer's protocol. The flow cytometry data were obtained with a CytoFLEX S (Beckman Coulter) and processed via Kaluza software (Beckman Coulter) to determine the distribution of cells in different phases of the cell cycle.
MitoSOX Red (Invitrogen) and TMRE (MedChemExpress) were used to detect mitochondrial superoxide and the membrane potential. OC cells were treated with EZH2 and FADS2 inhibitors for 48 h, harvested with trypsin and washed with PBS. The cells were subsequently stained with MitoSOX Red or TMRE for 30 min at 37 °C. The cells were washed and analysed with a CytoFLEX S (Beckman Coulter). C11-BODIPY (Invitrogen) was used to evaluate lipid peroxidation. Apoptosis was detected with an Annexin V-FITC/PI Apoptosis Detection Kit (KeyGen BioTech, China) following the manufacturer's protocol.
The puromycin incorporation assay was performed to assess translation activity. OC cells were treated with EZH2 and FADS2 inhibitors for 48 h, and puromycin (final concentration of 10 mg/mL) was added during the last 30 min of inhibitor treatment. The cells were subsequently harvested with trypsin and washed with PBS. After being washed, the cells were fixed and permeabilized via the Foxp3/Transcription Factor Staining Buffer Set Kit (Thermo Fisher eBioscience) following the manufacturer's instructions. An anti-puromycin-Alexa Fluor™ 647 antibody (Sigma–Aldrich, Cat#: MABE343-AF647, RRID: AB_2736876) was used for intracellular staining with puromycin for 30 min. The cells were washed and analysed with a CytoFLEX S (Beckman Coulter).

## Tumour inoculation and treatment in mice
The therapeutic effect of the combination treatment was assessed in vivo in healthy C57BL/6 mice (6–8 weeks of age, female), which were purchased from Vitalriver (Beijing, China) and housed under specific pathogen-free (SPF)-level conditions. Mice (20–22 g) were subcutaneously injected in the right flank with 5 × 105 ID8 cells in 50 μL of PBS. When the tumours reached an average volume of 30 mm3, the mice were randomly allocated to experimental groups (n = 5 or 6 mice/group) and treated with vehicle or drugs. SC26196 (30 mg/kg) was injected intraperitoneally once every 3 days. GSK126 (50 mg/kg) was injected intraperitoneally twice every 3 days. The combination treatment group received equal doses of SC26196 (30 mg/kg) and GSK126 (50 mg/kg). Tumour volume was monitored every two days via a digital calliper. Tumour volume = length∗width∗ width/2. The cage positions were rotated regularly. After two weeks of tumour development, the mice were euthanised. The experiment was repeated twice. The tumour volume was measured by an observer who was blinded to the treatment. No animals or data were excluded.

## Statistical analysis
All the data are expressed as the means ± standard errors of the means (S.E.M.). Two-tailed Student's t tests were used to compare two groups of continuous variables. One-way ANOVA with Tukey's multiple comparison post hoc tests were used when more than two groups of samples were compared concurrently. Data analysis with two-factor treatment was performed via two-way ANOVA. Significance levels were indicated as suggested by Prism Software: P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

## Results

## Inhibition of EZH2 increases the transcription of PUFA synthesis genes and increases PUFA levels in OC cells
In an effort to improve the efficacy of EZH2 inhibitors in tumour treatment, our previous studies revealed that the mechanism of resistance in melanoma cells is related to metabolism. To determine the metabolic impact of EZH2 inhibition in OC cells, we evaluated the levels of fatty acids and metabolites related to the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle. EPZ6438 (EPZ) treatment increased the levels of unsaturated fatty acids and altered the levels of metabolites involved in the TCA cycle ( a and b). The upregulation of fatty acids and the downregulation of citrate suggest increased fatty acid synthesis. This finding is in line with our previous metabolomics findings in melanoma cells subjected to GSK126 (GSK) treatment. A comparison of the total amount of saturated and unsaturated fatty acids revealed that monounsaturated fatty acid (MUFA) and PUFA levels were increased by EPZ treatment, whereas saturated fatty acid (SFA) levels did not change significantly ( c). These data indicate that EZH2 inhibition enhances the desaturation activity of fatty acids in OC. To explore the mechanisms underlying the alteration in PUFAs, we investigated the expression of PUFA synthesis genes, including fatty acid desaturases (FADS2, FADS1) and elongases (ELOVL5, ELOVL2), upon treatment with the EZH2 inhibitors GSK and EPZ ( d). We found that the mRNA levels of FADS2 and ELOVL2 were increased by EZH2 inhibitor treatment ( e). These results indicated that EZH2 inhibitors promoted PUFA synthesis by increasing the expression of related genes. 
To further explore the regulatory mechanism of EZH2 on PUFA synthesis genes, we analysed RNA-seq and ChIP-seq data (GSE145692) obtained in our previous studies using SKOV3 EZH2 knockout (EZH2-KO) and control (EZH2-WT) cells. Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) revealed that the unsaturated fatty acid biosynthesis pathway was upregulated in EZH2-KO cells ( f). We found that the levels of ELOVL2 mRNA and protein were significantly increased in EZH2-KO cells ( g, ). ChIP-seq and ChIP‒qPCR analysis revealed that H3K27me3 and EZH2 were enriched at the ELOVL2 promoter and were significantly decreased in EZH2-KO cells ( ). Surprisingly, the mRNA levels of FADS1 and FADS2 were decreased in EZH2-KO cells ( g). This phenomenon suggests that the regulatory effects of EZH2 on FADS1 and FADS2 might not rely entirely on the classical H3K27me3 paradigm. Consequently, a more meticulous exploration and in-depth investigation are warranted.

## EZH2 differentially regulates FADS2 transcript variants
There are three transcript variants encoding different isoforms of FADS2. The transcriptional start sites (TSSs) for FADS2 variant 2 (v2) and variant 3 (v3) are located approximately 12 kb upstream of variant 1 (v1), which is close to the TSS of the FADS1 gene (on the complementary strand) ( a). This suggests that the regulatory mechanism of FADS2 v1 may be separate from that of FADS2 v2/3 and FADS1. Therefore, we analysed the mRNA levels of the three variants in EZH2-KO cells. Our results revealed that EZH2 knockout increased the expression of variant 1 (v1) but decreased the expression of variants 2 (v2) and 3 (v3) ( b). This resulted in a decrease in the overall level of FADS2 mRNA in EZH2 KO cells ( g). 
To further evaluate the regulatory effect of EZH2 on FADS2, we measured the protein level of FADS2 in the context of EZH2 deficiency. The level of the full-length FADS2 protein (v1, 45 kD) was significantly greater in EZH2-KO cells than in EZH2-WT cells. FADS2 variants 2 and 3 (v2/3, 40 kD) were highly expressed in EZH2-WT cells but decreased in EZH2-KO cells ( c). Treatment with the proteasome inhibitor MG132 increased the level of FADS2 v1 but did not eliminate the effects of EZH2 KO. These findings further indicated that EZH2 differentially regulates FADS2 variants at the transcriptional level ( c). PROTAC EZH2 degrader-1 treatment had the same effect on FADS2 transcript variant expression in A2780 cells. EZH2 degradation increased the levels of ELOVL2 and FADS2 v1 and decreased the levels of FADS2 v2/3 ( d and e). These data indicate that EZH2 differentially regulates the transcription of FADS2 variants, leading to alterations in the protein patterns of FADS2 isoforms.
EZH2 not only occupies genomic loci marked by H3K27me3 but also occupies promoters independent of PRC2 marked by H3K4me3 and promotes transcription in OC. To explore the mechanism by which EZH2 differentially regulates the transcription of FADS2 variants, we analysed the binding of EZH2 to the FADS2 promoter region via GSE211307 data. Co-enrichment of EZH2 and SUZ12 and H3K27me3 was detected after the TSS of FADS2 v1, whereas co-enrichment of EZH2 and H3K4me3 was detected near the TSSs of FADS2 v2 and v3 (as well as near the TSS of FADS1), with a low level of H3K27me3 ( f). The ChIP‒qPCR results also revealed that EZH2 occupied the promoters of FADS2 v1 and v2/v3, but the enrichment of H3K27me3 at the v2/v3 promoter was lower than that at the v1 promoter ( g and h). These data suggest that EZH2 inhibits FADS2 v1 transcription by catalysing H3K27me3 and promotes FADS2 v2/v3 transcription in a different manner.

## Inhibition of EZH2 leads to upregulation of the full-length FADS2 isoform
Although the FADS2 v1 mRNA level was comparable to that of v2/3, the protein level of v1 was much lower in SKOV3 cells ( b and c). Therefore, we analysed the expression patterns of three FADS2 variants in other OC cell lines and tissues. FADS2 v1 was highly expressed in A2780 and OVCAR8 cells at the mRNA level, but it was much less expressed in HEY cells than v2/3, and the transcription of the three variants was comparable in SKOV3 cells ( a). However, FADS2 v2/3 was dominant at the protein level in most of the tested cell lines ( b). We next tested FADS2 expression in clinical OC tissues. The mRNA level of FADS2 v1 was much greater than that of v2/3 in OC samples, whereas the protein levels of FADS2 variants were similar ( c and d). These results suggest the advantage of the protein stability of FADS2 v2/3. We used MG132 and cycloheximide (CHX) to inhibit proteasome and protein synthesis and found that FADS2 v2/3 was more stable than full-length FADS2 v1 ( e and f). Sequence alignment revealed that FADS2 v1 contained several possible ubiquitination sites (K3, K28, K42, and K48) in the N-terminal region but not in v2 or v3, which may account for the observed difference in protein stability ( g). 
Since we detected increased PUFA contents in EPZ-treated cells ( a and c), we explored the effects of EZH2 inhibitors on FADS2 variants. The addition of EZH2 inhibitors increased the levels of FADS2 v1 mRNA and protein in OC cells. However, in the majority of the cell lines, the levels of v2/3 did not increase in response to EZH2 inhibition. Consequently, the proportion of v1 among the three variants significantly increased ( a and b). The alteration of the FADS2 expression pattern induced by EZH2 inhibition may increase FADS2 activity in cancer cells.
To explore the significance of increased FADS2 v1 expression, we analysed the expression of FADS2 variants and their correlation with the prognosis of OC patients via the GEPIA 2 database. Among the three variants, the expression of FADS2 v2 was the highest, followed by that of v1 ( h). Compared with those in normal tissues, FADS2 v1 and v3 were overexpressed in OC tissues, but the v2 and total FADS2 levels did not change significantly ( i, ). There was no significant correlation between total FADS2 level or each variant level and prognosis ( ). Notably, a high FADS2 v1/v2 ratio was significantly associated with a poor prognosis in patients with OC ( j). These data indicate that elevated FADS2 v1 expression may protect cancer cells under EZH2 inhibitor treatment.

## Silencing FADS2 sensitises OC cells to EZH2 inhibition
To investigate the role of upregulated PUFA synthesis in EZH2-deficient cells, we knocked down FADS2 in EZH2-WT and EZH2-KO SKOV3 cells ( a). FADS2 knockdown led to altered morphology and inhibited cell proliferation. The effect of FADS2 knockdown was more pronounced in EZH2-KO cells than in control cells ( b–d). Cell cycle analysis revealed that FADS2 knockdown led to G0/G1 phase arrest ( e), which was consistent with the upregulation of p21 protein in EZH2-KO cells ( a). The anticancer effect of FADS2 knockdown was corroborated in A2780 cell line ( f–h). 
Unexpectedly, we found that the EZH2 level was decreased by FADS2 knockdown, suggesting that EZH2 and FADS2 can regulate each other ( a, f, and g). We further showed that FADS2 expression was positively correlated with EZH2 expression in OC via The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) ( i). FADS2 knockdown improved the inhibitory effects of EZH2 inhibitors on cell proliferation and cell cycle progression in OC cells ( j–n, ). FADS2 knockdown did not significantly affect the proliferation of HEY cells but increased their sensitivity to the EZH2 inhibitor GSK126 ( j and m).
To investigate whether increased ELOVL2 expression also affects sensitivity to EZH2 inhibition, we knocked down ELOVL2 in OC cells. ELOVL2 knockdown inhibited the proliferation, migration and invasion of OC cells ( ). These findings suggest that ELOVL2 may be involved in promoting tumour progression. However, simultaneous knockdown/KO of ELOVL2 and EZH2 did not have an additive inhibitory effect on tumours ( ). ELOVL2 knockdown also did not affect the sensitivity of cancer cells to EZH2 inhibitors ( ). These results suggest that FADS2 plays a more important role than ELOVL2 in supporting the proliferation of EZH2-inhibited cancer cells.

## Combined targeting of EZH2 and FADS2 enhances the inhibitory effect on OC
To determine whether the FADS2 inhibitor could have the same effect as gene silencing, we treated OC cells with the FADS2 inhibitor SC26196 and assessed cell viability. The IC50 of SC26196 in OC cells varies from 23 to 124 μM ( ) and is negatively correlated with the expression level of FADS2 v1 and the ratio of v1/(v2+v3) ( a and b, ). SC26196 had a stronger inhibitory effect on the proliferation of EZH2-KO cells than on that of EZH2-WT cells ( c). These results suggest that elevated levels of FADS2 v1 in EZH2 KO cells may affect the response to the FADS2 inhibitor SC26196. The combination of the SC26196 and EZH2 inhibitors had a stronger inhibitory effect on cell viability ( d and e, ). SC26196 treatment lowered the IC50 of the EZH2 inhibitors ( e), and the combined treatment had synergistic effects (combination index < 1) ( ). 
Next, we tested the synergistic inhibitory effect of EZH2 and FADS2 inhibitors in patient-derived organoids and a tumour-bearing mouse model. The combined approach had a more potent inhibitory effect on the growth of organoids derived from 5 different patients ( f and g). Similarly, this strategy also had a better therapeutic effect on ID8-derived tumour growth in vitro and in vivo ( h–k). Taken together, these data indicate that simultaneous targeting of FADS2 and EZH2 could be a valuable therapeutic approach in patients with OC.

## Combined inhibition of EZH2 and FADS2 impairs mitochondrial function in OC cells
To investigate the molecular mechanism underlying the effect of the combination treatment, we first detected fatty acids to validate the effect of SC26196. The ratios of FA 22:6/FA 22:5 (w-3), FA 22:6/FA 18:3 (w-3), and FA 24:6/FA 24:5 were elevated in the presence of EPZ. These ratios decreased when both EPZ and SC26196 were added ( a). These findings indicate that FADS2 activity was increased by EPZ and suppressed by SC26196. 
Since we found that EZH2 expression was decreased by FADS2 knockdown ( a and f), we further measured EZH2 and H3K27me3 levels in the presence of SC26196. Our results revealed that not only EZH2 inhibitors but also SC26196 inhibited H3K27me3 levels in OC cells, which may be due to reduced EZH2 levels after SC26196 treatment ( b). These results suggest that SC26196 may suppress OC by reducing the EZH2 protein level.
To comprehensively determine the changes in protein expression, we performed a proteomics analysis of SKOV3 cells treated with EPZ and SC26196 ( ). The combined targeting of EZH2 and FADS2 caused extensive changes in proteomics. We annotated the proteins downregulated in the combined group with KEGG pathways (Clusters 3 and 4). Our results revealed that multiple pathways associated with cell proliferation were enriched (ribosome, RNA polymerase, DNA replication and cell cycle, etc.) ( c and d). Proteins in the oxidative phosphorylation pathway, including SDHD, NDUFAF6, and MT-CO2, were downregulated ( c–e). Volcano plots revealed that SDHD (succinate dehydrogenase complex subunit D) was one of the most downregulated proteins in the combined treatment group compared with the control or EPZ groups. SDHD is one of the four subunits of succinate dehydrogenase (SDH), which is also known as complex II of the mitochondrial respiratory chain ( f). SLC31A1, a copper transporter, was also significantly decreased when both EPZ and SC26196 were present ( f). This result suggests that copper ion homoeostasis is dysregulated in OC cells, which impacts the activity of MT-CO1 and MT-CO2 in the mitochondrial respiratory chain. Taken together, our results suggest that the combined treatment suppressed mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation and ATP production. As a consequence, the AMPK signalling pathway was upregulated by the combined treatment ( g). EZH2 and ribosome subunit proteins were decreased in response to energy stress ( h).
We further compared our proteomics data with the RNA-seq results of FADS2-KO OC cells (GSE168720) to determine the overlapping genes downregulated by FADS2 inhibition. A Venn diagram revealed that EZH2, SDHD, and several mitochondrial ribosome proteins were decreased in SC26196-treated and FADS2-deficient OC cells ( i). These data indicate that combined targeting of FADS2 and EZH2 impairs mitochondrial function and induces energy stress in OC cells.
To validate the proteomics data, we performed Western blot and mitochondrial functional assays using OC cells. We found that the SDHD level was significantly reduced in the presence of both SC26196 and EZH2 inhibitors ( a). EZH2 inhibitors suppressed AMPK activity in SKOV3 and A2780 cells. AMPK activity was increased when SC26196 was added in addition to EZH2 inhibitors ( a). AMPK activation significantly increased Raptor phosphorylation, thereby suppressing mTOR signalling-mediated S6 phosphorylation ( a and b), which is consistent with the findings of a previous study. Conversely, treatment with an AMPK inhibitor (Compound C) increased S6 phosphorylation ( c). 
These results suggest that the reduction in SDHD impairs mitochondrial ATP production, thereby activating AMPK signalling. To verify this, we assessed mitochondrial function and the oxygen consumption rate (OCR) in OC cells treated with SC26196 and EZH2 inhibitors. The combined treatment increased mitochondrial superoxide ( d), suppressed the basal and maximal OCRs and ATP production related to mitochondrial respiration ( e and f), and decreased the cellular ATP level ( ). The combined treatment also decreased the mitochondrial membrane potential and mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) content ( g and h). Therefore, mitochondrial function was suppressed upon the inhibition of both EZH2 and FASD2. Activation of the AMPK pathway could lead to the inhibition of protein synthesis. This finding was confirmed by dephosphorylation of the S6 protein and puromycin incorporation assays ( a and i). Cell cycle analysis revealed that the number of cells in the G2/M phase was significantly lower ( j) and that the p21 level was elevated when both EZH2 and FASD2 were inhibited ( a). Although the combined treatment promoted the apoptosis of OC cells, the proportion of apoptotic cells remained relatively low ( ), and it did not significantly affect lipid peroxidation ( ). These findings indicate that dual inhibition of EZH2 and FADS2 causes mitochondrial dysfunction, leading to therapeutic efficacy through the suppression of protein synthesis and cell proliferation rather than through direct regulation of apoptosis or ferroptosis.

## Downregulation of the Notch pathway is involved in the activation of AMPK signalling
In addition to oxidative phosphorylation, the Notch signalling pathway was also downregulated in SKOV3 cells treated with EPZ6438 and SC26196 ( c). The Notch receptors are cleaved twice by proteases (metallopeptidases, γ-secretases) and activated to release the Notch intracellular domain (NICD), which can function as a transcription factor. The combined treatment significantly reduced Notch1 intracellular domain (NICD1) levels in ovarian cancer cells ( a and b), as did FADS2 knockdown ( c). Subsequent analysis of NICD target genes, such as HES1 and c-Myc, revealed their downregulation following co-inhibition of EZH2 and FADS2 in OVCAR8 and HEY cells ( a) but not in SKOV3 and A2780 cells ( b), although they were downregulated by FADS2 knockdown ( c). These findings suggested that the Notch-HES1/c-Myc pathway did not mediate the therapeutic effects of the combined treatment. To elucidate the impact of Notch inactivation and its relevance to mitochondrial dysfunction, we employed the γ–secretase inhibitor Nirogacestat to inhibit Notch and oligomycin to block oxidative phosphorylation and ATP production. Notch inactivation reduced SDHD expression, activated AMPK signalling and suppressed S6 phosphorylation. Similarly, oligomycin–induced ATP depletion activated AMPK while inhibiting Notch signalling activation. The combined treatment synergistically enhanced SDHD suppression and AMPK signalling activation ( d). Oligomycin treatment, but not nirogacestat treatment, decreased EZH2 expression ( e), and blocking AMPK signalling with compound C reversed the EZH2 downregulation induced by FADS2 inhibition ( f). These results suggested that SC26196-mediated EZH2 reduction was due mainly to impaired mitochondrial ATP production rather than to Notch signalling inhibition. Taken together, these findings indicate that dual inhibition of EZH2 and FADS2 synergistically suppresses both Notch signalling and mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation ( g). 

## Discussion
EZH2 is well known to be overexpressed in most OC cases; therefore, it may serve as an effective therapeutic target. Although EZH2 inhibitors have been developed and tested in clinical trials, their therapeutic effects on solid tumours are still limited. , Inhibition of EZH2 enzymatic activity could lead to unwanted expression of genes that contribute to drug resistance. We previously reported that EZH2 inhibitors upregulate USP22 transcription and further stabilise PD-L1, leading to tumour immune evasion. EZH2 inhibitors also increase the expression of lipid synthesis genes, such as SCD1 and SQLE, resulting in decreased sensitivity to treatment. , In addition, increasing evidence indicates that EZH2 has H3K27me3-independent functions in cancer, such as acting as a transcriptional coactivator or protein posttranslational modifier. , , Hence, inhibiting EZH2 methyltransferase activity alone may not be sufficient to suppress EZH2-mediated tumour progression.
In this study, we found that EZH2 inhibitors upregulated FADS2 v1 expression and PUFA abundance in OC. Targeting FADS2 reduces EZH2 expression, leading to increased sensitivity of cancer cells to EZH2 inhibitors in vivo and in vitro. These findings suggest that combination with a FADS2 inhibitor not only suppresses EZH2 inhibitor-induced PUFA synthesis but also may weaken the noncanonical function of EZH2 by reducing its protein level.
Currently, the regulatory mechanism of FADS2, especially the regulation of different transcript variants and protein stability, is not well understood. Previous studies have revealed that FADS2 expression is activated by the transcription factors SREBP1/2 and PPARα, but it is not clear whether these factors have the same effect on different transcripts. We found that EZH2 occupied the promoters of FADS2 v1 and v2/3. EZH2 deficiency led to increased FADS2 v1 and decreased v2/3 levels, but EZH2 inhibitors did not have the same effects on FADS2 v2/3 expression. These findings indicate that EZH2 may promote FADS2 v2/3 expression in noncanonical ways. Previous studies have shown that EZH2 binds to transcription factors such as c-Myc and RelB at non-PRC2 targets and promotes gene expression. The regulatory mechanism by which EZH2 affects the expression of FADS2 v2/3 remains to be further studied.
The elevated FADS2 v1 ratio in the variants induced by EZH2 inhibition may increase FADS2 activity in cancer cells because FADS2 v2/3 are deficient in enzymatic activity and may inhibit the enzymatic activity of FADS2 v1. This is supported by the increase in PUFA abundance upon EZH2 inhibition in cancer cells. Our data revealed that a high FADS2 v1/v2 ratio predicts a poor prognosis in patients with OC. These findings indicate that the relative expression of the full isoform and short isoform of FADS2 is important for promoting cancer progression. We found that FADS2 v2/3 proteins are more stable than v1 proteins under MG132 and CHX treatment, which may be due to differences in amino acid sequences and ubiquitination modifications. This led to higher FADS2 v2/3 protein levels than v1 protein levels in most cells. Our study revealed that the inhibition of EZH2 increased the proportion of FADS2 v1 among the variants. This increases PUFA synthesis and sensitises cancer cells to FADS2 inhibitors.
FADS2 is aberrantly expressed in many types of cancers and is correlated with tumour proliferation, migration, invasion, ferroptosis, resistance to radiotherapy, and prognosis. However, its role is controversial, and the mechanisms are not fully understood. PUFAs incorporated into the plasma membrane are vulnerable to lipid peroxidation and can increase ferroptosis sensitivity. It has been reported that increased expression of FADS1/2 leads to increased PUFA availability and that targeting FADS1/2 prevents ferroptosis induction in aggressive triple-negative breast cancer. Another study revealed that SCD1/FADS2 and unsaturated fatty acid levels are aberrantly upregulated in metastatic OC cells. The inhibition of SCD1 and FADS2 downregulates GPX4, disrupting the cellular/mitochondrial redox balance and leading to lipid peroxidation. In our study, FADS2 inhibition alone did not affect lipid peroxidation, which is consistent with previous findings. Moreover, simultaneously inhibiting EZH2 and FADS2 similarly failed to induce lipid peroxidation, indicating that the anticancer effects of the combination treatment operate through ferroptosis-independent mechanisms.
PUFAs are important lipid components of mitochondrial membranes; therefore, changes in PUFA abundance may influence mitochondrial physiology and reduce the mitochondrial membrane potential. Inhibition of FADS2 by SC26196 or genetic knockout results in reprogramming of the mitochondrial lipidome, reducing the abundance of free PUFAs and phospholipids containing PUFA acyl chains in mitochondria. , Mitochondrial complex II (SDH) is important for maintaining the mitochondrial membrane potential and is associated with cell stemness. , Our proteomic analysis revealed that FADS2 inhibition decreased the levels of components of the respiratory chain (SDHD, MT-CO2, etc.). Consequently, these changes inhibit mitochondrial ATP production and activate AMPK signalling, leading to the inhibition of protein synthesis and cell cycle arrest. These results provide evidence for the role of FADS2 in maintaining mitochondrial function. We found that the inhibition of the Notch signalling pathway induced by the combined treatment contributed to the regulation of SDHD and AMPK signalling. The recruitment of NICD1 to nuclear and mitochondrial genes encoding respiratory chain components has been reported in macrophages. Further investigations are needed to determine whether FADS2 modulates SDHD expression through Notch signalling and direct NICD1 binding to the gene promoter. The activation of AMPK signalling can promote EZH2 degradation and inhibit its methyltransferase activity by phosphorylating EZH2. EZH2 expression is also known to decrease in response to glucose deprivation in colorectal cancer cells. This finding is consistent with our finding that EZH2 is downregulated by FADS2 inhibition-induced mitochondrial dysfunction.
In conclusion, we demonstrated that EZH2 differentially regulates FADS2 transcript variants. EZH2 inhibitors increase the proportion of the full FADS2 isoform, which is correlated with poor prognosis in OC patients. Our findings indicate that the expression levels of different FADS2 isoforms in tumours can potentially predict the prognosis of patients with OC and their sensitivity to FADS2 inhibitors. The combination of FADS2 and EZH2 sensitises OCs to EZH2 inhibitors, leading to synergistic inhibition of Notch signalling and mitochondrial function and a stronger inhibitory effect on tumour growth. This study indicates that dual inhibition of EZH2 and FADS2 may be a promising therapeutic strategy for ovarian cancer treatment. It functions by orchestrating both epigenetic and metabolic mechanisms, potentially opening avenues for more effective interventions.

## Limitations of the study
First, the mechanisms underlying the EZH2-mediated differential regulation of FADS2 transcripts require further investigation. Second, the enzymatic activities of different FADS2 variants need to be verified both intracellularly and extracellularly. Third, future studies need to clarify the potential role of sequence variations in determining ubiquitination patterns and protein stability among FADS2 variants. Fourth, the mechanism by which FADS2 regulates mitochondrial complexes and the Notch pathway warrants further clarification. FADS2 may interact with the γ–secretase subunit PSEN1 to regulate the Notch pathway, but this interaction still needs to be verified. Fifth, the use of a subcutaneous tumour-bearing mouse model in this study has limitations, and more experimental models (such as in situ tumours) are needed to verify the combined therapeutic effect. The therapeutic effects on other tumours also remain to be studied.
