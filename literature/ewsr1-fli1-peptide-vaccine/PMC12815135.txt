Treatment of localized sarcomas comprises surgical resection, radiotherapy and systemic therapies, often in a multimodal approach. In the advanced setting, chemotherapy, tyrosine kinase inhibitors and immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) are commonly used, with radiotherapy applied selectively for palliation or local control. The prognosis of advanced sarcomas remains poor with 3-year overall survival (OS) rates generally below 30%. Despite some favorable outcomes from the use of ICIs in some sarcoma subtypes such as alveolar soft part sarcoma, angiosarcoma and undifferentiated pleomorphic sarcoma, the majority are considered as immunologically “cold” and display primary resistance. 
Cell therapies directly harness the ability of the immune system to target and destroy cancer cells and have shown promising results in the treatment of hematological malignancies. However, their clinical application beyond B-cell malignancies is hampered by numerous hurdles, including treatment resistance, immune escape mechanisms, and a hostile tumor microenvironment (TME). Recent advances in cellular engineering and strategies to modulate TME may expand the applicability of these therapies to a wider range of cancers, creating novel treatment options. In this context, sarcomas represent a compelling target for cell therapies due to their limited responsiveness to standard systemic treatments and the unmet need for novel therapeutic strategies.
This review provides an overview of cell therapy in sarcomas, detailing current strategies such as genetically engineered T-cell therapies, including those based on engineered T-cell receptor T-cell therapy (TCR-T) and chimeric antigen receptor T-cell therapy (CAR-T), tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs), NK cells, and mesenchymal stromal cells (MSC). Combination approaches aimed at overcoming tumor-mediated immune evasion and enhancing therapeutic efficacy are also discussed. The integration of these innovative therapies into the sarcoma treatment paradigm presents both opportunities to improve patients outcomes, as well as challenges related to logistics and cost.

## Tumor microenvironment of sarcoma
Most subtypes of sarcoma are considered “immunologically cold”, characterized by reduced T-cell infiltration, low tumor mutational burden, and abundant immunosuppressive myeloid cells such as M2-polarized tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) and FOXP3+ regulatory T cells (Tregs). TAMs and Tregs secrete inhibitory cytokines and express checkpoint ligands, creating a tolerogenic niche that permits immune escape. 
Additionally, aberrant tumor vasculature and dense extracellular matrix components restrict immune cell trafficking, while hypoxia, glucose deprivation, and accumulation of immunosuppressive metabolites such as lactate and kynurenine further impair effector T-cell persistence and proliferation. Clinically, these TME features contribute to the limited success of ICIs in unselected sarcoma cohorts.
Recent multiomic studies have delineated distinct immunological subtypes within sarcomas, providing a framework for more precise therapeutic approaches. Notably, transcriptomic profiling by Petitprez et al described five immune-based classifications of soft tissue sarcoma (STS), each associated with different extent and quality of immune infiltration. Their findings highlighted tertiary lymphoid structure (TLS)-rich tumors associated with better clinical outcomes and increased response to ICIs. Indeed, in the biomarker-informed PEMBROSARC phase 2 trial, patients with TLS had a 30% response to ICIs. The randomized phase 2 CONgRAtS trial, conducted by the same group, was designed to prospectively validate TLS as a predictive biomarker in STS immunotherapy. Although relatlimab did not improve outcomes, both treatment arms exceeded historical benchmarks. Preliminary analyses indicate distinct immune architectures between responders and non-responders, and ongoing high-dimensional profiling aims to elucidate mechanisms of response and guide future biomarker-driven strategies. 
Proteomic and single-cell analyses have also identified immunosuppressive mediators such as macrophage migration inhibitory factor, which drives the polarization of macrophages toward a protumor phenotype via the CD74 receptor. Inhibiting this axis in osteosarcoma models restored antigen presentation capacity and improved immune activity. Furthermore, dysregulated tryptophan metabolism via indoleamine 2,3-dioxygenase expression contributes to accumulation of kynurenine and suppression of T-effector function. These biomarkers, including TLS, macrophage signatures, and cytokine-driven suppression, are shaping a more nuanced immune classification of sarcomas that informs prognosis and therapeutic strategies.
Advances in TME profiling would be critical to shaping the development of cell therapies in sarcoma. In particular, understanding chemokine gradients such as CXCL9, CXCL10, and CCL5 that regulate T-cell trafficking is crucial for optimizing CAR-T and TCR-T cell infiltration. Immune-inflamed tumors enriched with high tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes or organized TLS may represent more permissive contexts for T cell-based strategies, whereas immune-deserted or myeloid-enriched phenotypes may necessitate combination approaches to modulate the suppressive milieu. These emerging insights into the immunological landscape of sarcoma are key to informing rational cell therapy development in this difficult-to-treat disease.

## General principles of cell therapy
Cell therapy offers a potential solution for the intrinsic lack of intratumoral tumor-killing immune cells. The key principle of cell therapy involves the administration of live immune effector cells with the capacity to recognize and eliminate malignant cells through targeted immune mechanisms. Autologous products originate from the patient and thus avoid rejection, whereas allogeneic products are derived from healthy donors, providing an off-the-shelf resource but introducing a risk of immune incompatibility. Typically, these therapies involve isolating, modifying, or expanding immune cells ex vivo to enhance their antitumor activity before reinfusing them into patients ( ). 
 The manufacturing process for TCR-T or CAR-T cell therapies typically begins with apheresis, followed by ex vivo engineering and expansion of the selected cell population. Prior to cell infusion, patients usually receive non-myeloablative lymphodepleting chemotherapy to promote engraftment and expansion of the infused cells. Unlike traditional treatments, cell-based therapies offer the potential for durable responses by harnessing the specificity and adaptability of living immune cells. However, these potent cell therapies can also trigger significant adverse events such as cytokine release syndrome (CRS) and immune effector cell-associated neurotoxicity syndrome (ICANS), as well as rare but serious adverse events including hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis and macrophage activation syndrome. 
The first US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) approval of a T-cell therapy was granted in 2017 for tisagenlecleucel (CAR-T targeting CD19) in relapsed or refractory B-cell acute lymphoblastic leukemia. Since then, multiple cell therapy products have been approved, with the majority targeting hematological malignancies. In recent years, the pipeline of cell therapies has expanded to include solid tumors. In February 2024, lifileucel, the autologous TIL therapy for advanced melanoma, became the first cell therapy approved by the FDA for any solid tumor. This was followed by the FDA approval of afamitresgene autoleucel, TCR-T targeting the cancer testis antigen MAGE-A4, in September 2024 for metastatic synovial sarcoma. Despite the recent important progress in the application of these cell therapies for solid tumors, they remain a relatively small fraction of all current sarcoma trials, the majority of which are in early phases of development ( ).
 

## Engineered T-cell receptor T-cell therapy
TCR-T employs autologous T lymphocytes that are genetically modified to express high-affinity T-cell receptors capable of recognizing tumor-derived peptides presented in the context of specific human leukocyte antigen (HLA) molecules ( ). Therefore, TCR-T can target both surface proteins on cancer cells, as well as intracellular proteins by recognizing their processed peptide fragments bound to HLA. Of relevance, several sarcomas express higher than normal levels of cancer testis antigens (CTAs), particularly synovial sarcoma and myxoid round cell liposarcoma. CTAs are tumor-associated antigens (TAAs) typically expressed during fetal development (in the placenta and embryo) or in immune-privileged sites lacking HLA class I molecules, such as the testes. Common CTAs identified in sarcomas include the MAGE, NY-ESO-1 and GAGE families, as well as fetal acetylcholine receptors. MAGE and NY-ESO-1 families are intracellular antigens that can be processed and presented by major histocompatibility complex (MHC) molecules. In synovial sarcoma, PRAME, MAGE-A4, and NY-ESO1 are highly expressed in 86%, 82%, and 61% of tumors, respectively, with 96% of tumors expressing at least one of these three CTAs. 
The first-in-human trials of TCR-T in sarcoma were conducted at the US National Institutes of Health in 2011, using autologous T cells transduced with an HLA-A*02:01–restricted TCR specific for NY-ESO-1. In patients with refractory metastatic synovial sarcoma, adoptive transfer of NY-ESO-1-reactive TCR-T (accompanied by high-dose interleukin (IL)-2) led to objective responses in four of six patients. Subsequent expansion of this trial led to a reported overall response rate (ORR) of 61% (11 of 18 patients with synovial sarcoma), including at least one complete response observed, with some responses lasting beyond 1 year. The activity of the TCR-T was independent of the anti-NY-ESO-1 antibody titers and treatment was well-tolerated other than transient cytopenias. These results were remarkable for a disease characterized by poor long-term outcomes and non-durable responses in advanced disease, despite initial chemosensitivity, and also validated CTAs as viable targets in sarcoma. However, the transition from investigator-initiated early-phase trials to multicenter, late-phase trials was markedly delayed, reflecting limited industry engagement and structural funding challenges in sarcoma research.
Following this, an affinity-enhanced TCR targeting NY-ESO-1, subsequently called letetresgene autoleucel (NY-ESO-1c259 SPEAR T cells, or lete-cel), was developed ( ). In a phase I study reported in 2018, administration of lete-cel following lymphodepleting chemotherapy resulted in an ORR of 50% (n=6/12) in patients with advanced synovial sarcoma, with one patient achieving complete response. The engineered T cells expanded in vivo and showed polyfunctional cytokine production that persisted for at least 6 months in responders. Beyond ORR, these studies provided key translational data, showing that durable benefit correlates with in vivo expansion kinetics and cytokine polyfunctionality rather than baseline antigen levels alone. More recently, the IGNYTE-ESO phase II trial (NCT03967223) provided an updated evaluation of lete-cel in an international, multicenter setting. Among the patients with synovial sarcoma, the ORR was 39% (n=9/23), while the ORR in the patients with myxoid round cell liposarcoma was 41% (n=9/22). The median duration of response was 10.6 months, although follow-up data were still immature at the time of the reported analysis. Treatment-related adverse events were generally manageable; however, grade 3 CRS occurred in nine patients (12%), and one patient experienced grade 5 neutropenia. Nevertheless, these findings confirm the therapeutic potential of NY-ESO-1 targeting TCR-T cell therapy in advanced synovial sarcoma and myxoid round cell liposarcoma ( ).
 In addition, in a recent pilot study of lete-cel in advanced or metastatic NY-ESO-1 expressing myxoid round cell liposarcoma, D’Angelo et al evaluated two cohorts treated with either reduced or standard-dose lymphodepleting chemotherapy regimens. In this study of n=20 patients, the investigator-assessed ORR was 20% (n=2/10) for the reduced-dose cohort and 40% (n=4/10) for the standard-dose cohort, with median duration of response of 5.3 and 7.5 months, respectively. Importantly, disease control rates (DCR) were 70% (n=7/10) and 80% (n=8/10) in the two groups, respectively, and T-cell expansion and persistence were associated with clinical benefit. These findings suggest that a higher intensity lymphodepletion regimen may enhance the efficacy of lete-cel by promoting greater T-cell engraftment and proliferation.
Another promising CTA target, MAGE-A4, has recently gained significant attention with the development of afamitresgene autoleucel (afami-cel, formerly ADP-A2M4) ( ). Afami-cel is a TCR-T product directed against an HLA-A2-restricted MAGE-A4 peptide. In the initial phase I trial investigating afami-cel across a range of previously treated advanced solid tumors, including synovial sarcoma and myxoid round cell liposarcoma, afami-cel achieved clinically meaningful response, with particularly notable efficacy in synovial sarcoma. Median progression-free survival (PFS) was 12.3 weeks overall and 20.4 weeks in synovial sarcoma, while the median OS was 42.9 weeks in the entire cohort and 58.1 weeks for patients with synovial sarcoma. Following this, the pivotal international phase II SPEARHEAD-1 trial evaluated afami-cel in HLA-A02-positive patients with advanced synovial sarcoma or myxoid round cell liposarcoma who had previously received anthracycline-based or ifosfamide-based chemotherapy. Among the n=52 patients who received afami-cel, including n=44 patients with synovial sarcoma, the confirmed ORR was 37% (n=19/52), with responses observed in 39% (n=17/44) of patients with synovial sarcoma. The median PFS was 3.7 months (95% CI 2.8 to 5.6) in the overall population, 3.8 months (95% CI 2.8 to 6.4) among patients with synovial sarcoma, and 2.4 months (95% CI 0.9 to 7.4) in those with myxoid round cell liposarcoma. The median OS was 15.4 months (95% CI 10.9 to 28.7), with a survival probability of 60% at 12 months. Notably, afami-cel also showed efficacy in heavily pretreated patients with high disease burden, including those with target lesions exceeding 100 mm. Although CRS was common, occurring in 71% of patients (n=37/52), events were predominantly grade 1 or 2 and responsive to immunomodulatory agents such as tocilizumab. SPEARHEAD-1 met its primary endpoint and established proof-of-concept for afami-cel as the first engineered TCR-T approved for a solid tumor indication. This resulted in the US FDA granting approval for use in adults with unresectable or metastatic synovial sarcoma who have received prior chemotherapy, are HLA-A02-positive, and whose tumor expresses the MAGE-A4 antigen. These results highlight the potential of afami-cel to expand the therapeutic landscape for synovial sarcoma and support exploration in earlier lines of therapy and combinatorial approaches to improve durability and breadth of response.
Emerging data from transcriptomic and immunogenomic studies are helping to unravel the mechanisms underlying both response and resistance to TCR-T. In the IGNYTE-ESO study, higher lymphodepletion regimens (fludarabine 120 mg/m² + cyclophosphamide 2,700–3,600 mg/m²) correlated with greater peak T-cell expansion and improved clinical outcomes. Elevated serum IL-15 levels post-infusion were also associated with durable responses, likely reflecting effective homeostatic proliferation of transferred T cells. In the SPEARHEAD-1 trial, patients with high MAGE-A4 expression (H-scores ≥200) exhibited significantly higher response rates. Additionally, lower baseline tumor burden and absence of bridging therapy before lymphodepletion were associated with better outcomes, potentially suggesting that immune pressure in a less immunosuppressive tumor milieu enhances efficacy. Despite promising responses, resistance remains a major challenge. Primary resistance may stem from insufficient T-cell expansion, inadequate antigen presentation, or highly immunosuppressive TME. Transcriptomic analyses have shown that tumors with reduced expression of HLA class I molecules or antigen-processing machineries (eg, TAP1, β2-microglobulin) exhibit resistance despite persistent NY-ESO-1 expression. Acquired resistance mechanisms include loss of HLA-A*02 expression, alterations in the interferon signaling pathway, and immune checkpoint upregulation such as programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) and lymphocyte-activation gene 3 (LAG-3) expression on tumor and infiltrating immune cells. Analysis of the TME after treatment showed variability in immune cell infiltration. In some responders, increased leukocyte infiltration was observed along with sustained NY-ESO-1 expression and upregulation of PD-L1 and LAG-3 up to 900 days after infusion. This suggests a dynamic interaction between tumor and transferred T cells, with potential benefit from immune checkpoint blockade combinations in patients with emerging resistance markers.
Most recently, Wermke et al reported the first-in-human results of a novel TCR-T cell therapy targeting PReferentially expressed Antigen in MElanoma (PRAME), a CTA also widely expressed in sarcomas and other solid tumors. In the phase I IMA203-101 study, n=40 patients with advanced PRAME-positive solid tumors, predominantly melanoma (n=16), were treated. This cohort also included n=8 patients with synovial sarcoma who received autologous T cells engineered to express an HLA-A*02:01–restricted PRAME-specific TCR. Among seven evaluable patients, two achieved confirmed partial response, three experienced unconfirmed partial response, and stable disease was observed in two patients. Median PFS and OS were not separately reported for patients with synovial sarcoma; however, individual PFS ranged from 2.4 to over 24 months. Notably, treatment was well tolerated across the overall patient cohort, with no grade ≥3 CRS or ICANS events reported; most adverse events consisted of grade 1–2 CRS and cytopenias. Importantly, translational analyses revealed that higher PRAME expression and deeper intratumoral T-cell infiltration correlated with improved tumor regression and longer PFS, suggesting antigen density and T-cell trafficking as key determinants of efficacy. Moreover, IMA203 T cells exhibited rapid engraftment, long-term persistence, and no evidence of peripheral exhaustion, indicating favorable T-cell fitness even in heavily pretreated patients. These promising findings highlight PRAME as a potential immunotherapeutic target in synovial sarcoma and warrant further evaluation in sarcoma-specific trials.

## Chimeric antigen receptor T-cell therapy
CAR-T cell therapy involves the genetic modification of autologous T lymphocytes to express synthetic receptors that recognize TAAs present on the surface of cancer cells ( ). The CAR construct combines an antibody-derived antigen binding domain with intracellular signaling motifs that activate the T cell on antigen engagement. This approach allows T cells to bypass HLA restriction and target cell surface proteins directly, making CAR-T cells suitable for a broad patient population. Currently, seven CAR-T products are FDA-approved for hematological malignancies: five CD19-targeted agents—tisagenlecleucel, axicabtagene ciloleucel, brexucabtagene autoleucel, lisocabtagene maraleucel, obecabtagene autoleucel ; and two B-cell maturation antigen (BCMA)-targeted agents for multiple myeloma—idecabtagene vicleucel and ciltacabtagene autoleucel. These therapies achieve remission rates of 40–90% in refractory patients but face challenges in solid tumors due to antigenic heterogeneity, immunosuppressive microenvironment, and on-target, off-tumor toxicities. 
There are currently no CAR-T cell therapies approved for sarcoma. Several CAR-T cell trials have been conducted in sarcoma exploring a variety of surface antigens as target, with human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2) being the earliest and most studied. A phase I/II clinical trial using HER2-specific CAR-T cells primarily involving n=19 patients with refractory or recurrent metastatic HER2-positive sarcoma (n=16 osteosarcomas, one Ewing sarcoma, one primitive neuroectodermal tumor, and one desmoplastic small round cell tumor) revealed a median OS of 10.3 months and was well tolerated, with no cardiopulmonary toxicity observed. Further evaluation of this therapy in n=10 patients with HER2+sarcomas (n=5 osteosarcoma, n=3 rhabdomyosarcoma, one Ewing sarcoma, one synovial sarcoma) produced an OS of 60% at 1 year with one complete response in osteosarcoma and one in rhabdomyosarcoma. A subsequent phase I, dose-escalation study evaluated the safety and activity of autologous HER2 CAR-T cell infusion following lymphodepletion with fludarabine alone or in combination with cyclophosphamide. In this study, n=13 patients with progressive, metastatic, or recurrent HER2+ sarcoma (osteosarcoma, rhabdomyosarcoma, synovial sarcoma) were treated across three cohorts, with varying lymphodepletion regimens and HER2-directed CAR-T cells. CRS occurred in n=11 patients, including two with grade 3–4 events. By week 6, 21% achieved complete response, 29% had stable disease, and 50% progressed; notably, one patient with rhabdomyosarcoma maintained CR for over 6 years, and another with osteosarcoma relapsed after 42 months of complete response. Median PFS and OS were 2.4 months and 8.2 months, respectively. Taken together, HER2-targeted CAR-T cells demonstrate manageable safety and encouraging antitumor activity, providing a promising model for future CAR-based approaches in sarcoma.
Beyond HER2, several other targets are under investigation for CAR-T cell therapy in sarcoma. GD2, a disialoganglioside that is highly expressed in neuroblastoma, is also present on tumors such as osteosarcoma and some STS. GD2-CAR-T cells have shown success in neuroblastoma and are now being tested in osteosarcoma (NCT03721068). The epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) is overexpressed in certain sarcomas (like osteosarcoma and EGFR-positive rhabdoid tumors) and an ongoing trial (STRIvE-01, NCT03618381) is evaluating an EGFR-targeted CAR-T cell in relapsed sarcomas, with preliminary evidence of mixed tumor responses at low doses. Other novel targets include IGF-1 receptor, which is important in Ewing sarcoma biology; ROR1, an embryonic antigen expressed in some sarcomas; B7-H3 (CD276), a checkpoint molecule widely expressed in sarcomas; and NKG2D ligand (via a CAR that uses NKG2D receptor to target stress ligands on tumors). 
CAR-T cell therapy faces several significant challenges before it can be adopted into clinical practice. In addition to target selection, CAR-T cells in solid tumors face physical barriers to infiltration, a hostile microenvironment leading to T-cell exhaustion, and potential toxicities if the target antigen is not truly cancer-specific. On-target, off-tumor toxicity is a prime concern; for example, HER2 is expressed at low levels in the lung and heart, requiring caution and careful dose escalation in trials. 

## Tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes therapy
TIL therapy uses naturally occurring T cells that have infiltrated the TME. These lymphocytes are isolated from resected tumor specimens, expanded ex vivo, and reinfused into the patient after lymphodepleting conditioning, often administered together with immune-stimulatory agents such as IL-2 ( ). Notably, while early TIL studies primarily relied on high-dose IL-2, recent trials are increasingly investigating synergistic strategies combining TIL therapy with anti-programmed cell death protein-1 (PD-1) blockade, which may further enhance T-cell persistence and tumor infiltration. TILs are polyclonal and recognize a broad array of antigens, including patient-specific neoantigens. TIL therapy is not genetically engineered and potentially offers a personalized, antigen-diverse strategy based on the patient’s intrinsic immune response to their tumor. This contrasts with the antigen-specific activity of engineered TCRs and CAR-T cells. 
TIL therapy, specifically lifileucel, is now FDA-approved for advanced melanoma in the second-line setting. Promising results have also been reported in cervical cancer, lung cancer, ovarian cancer, colorectal cancer, and breast cancer. Mullinax et al demonstrated the feasibility of expanding TILs from n=70 STS specimens, the majority of which generated CD3+ TILs with autologous tumor reactivity. Similarly, Ko et al provided preclinical evidence that certain sarcoma subtypes are amenable to TIL expansion. Zhou et al conducted a retrospective study of n=60 patients with chemotherapy-resistant metastatic osteosarcoma treated with TILs combined with nivolumab. The ORR was 36.7%, including two patients achieving complete response and n=20 with partial response (PR), with an overall DCR of 80% (n=48/60). The median PFS was 5.8 months and responders had a significantly longer OS compared with non-responders (23.7 vs 8.7 months, p<0.0001). Higher CD8+ TIL percentages (≥60%), lower CD4+/FoxP3+ TILs (<20%), and higher total number of TILs infused were significantly associated with better outcomes. 
In a retrospective analysis by Shi et al, the efficacy of the addition of TIL therapy to adjuvant chemotherapy was evaluated in patients with osteosarcoma who had a poor histological response to neoadjuvant chemotherapy. 80 patients with primary high-grade intramedullary osteosarcoma were evaluated: n=40 received adjuvant chemotherapy alone, and n=40 received adjuvant chemotherapy and TIL therapy. Both groups received MAP (methotrexate, doxorubicin, cisplatin) as their systemic regimen. The combination group showed improved outcomes, with a median disease-free survival of 65.3 months versus 55.5 months and a median OS of 95.8 months versus 80.4 months. Multivariate analyses identified a higher number of TIL infusions as an independent prognostic factor for both PFS and OS. Additionally, Wang et al compared nivolumab monotherapy with combination therapy using TILs and nivolumab in n=60 patients with metastatic osteosarcoma. The ORR was significantly higher in the combination group (33.3% vs 6.67%), with improved median PFS (5.4 vs 3.8 months) and OS (15.2 vs 6.6 months). Similarly, overall outcomes were better in patients receiving higher TIL numbers and CD8+ TIL infusions, and worse with increased proportion of CD8+PD-1+ or CD4+FoxP3+ TILs. 
Despite encouraging retrospective and preclinical evidence, TIL therapy in sarcoma remains investigational and has not yet been evaluated in prospective clinical trials. However, ongoing trials, including a phase I study in STS (NCT04052334) and a phase II basket trial with cohort for STS (NCT03935893), aim to further explore its therapeutic potential. Challenges such as immunosuppressive TME and the inherently “cold” nature of many sarcoma subtypes need to be addressed to optimize outcomes.

## Natural killer cell therapy
NK cell therapy harnesses the innate cytotoxic potential of NK cells, which can eliminate tumor cells without prior sensitization or the need for HLA-mediated antigen presentation. NK cells respond to stress-induced ligands or downregulation of HLA class I molecules, features often observed in tumor cells. They can be derived from peripheral blood, cord blood, induced pluripotent stem cells, or established cell lines. NK cell therapies can also be modified to express CAR constructs, combining innate recognition with antigen specificity. As they are not HLA restricted, they may be used in allogeneic settings. NK cells mediate tumor cell killing via two primary mechanisms: release of perforin and granzyme granules that induce apoptosis in target cells, and engagement of death receptor pathways via tumor necrosis factor family ligands. Unlike T cells, NK cells detect stress-induced ligands and downregulated HLA class I on target cells through a balance of activating and inhibitory receptors. This allows NK cells to attack tumor cells that have evaded T-cell surveillance by losing HLA expression.
Early clinical studies with NK cell therapy in solid tumors, including sarcomas, have faced challenges. One limitation has been the ability of NK cells to traffic into the tumor and remain functional within the suppressive TME. Immunosuppressive cytokines and factors in sarcoma tissue can inhibit NK cell activity at the tumor site. Nevertheless, several strategies to improve NK cell efficacy are currently being tested. Autologous NK cells can be expanded ex vivo and activated to enhance their cytotoxicity. For instance, SNK01 is an autologous, non-genetically modified NK cell product with >90% expression of activating markers (CD16, NKG2D, NKp46, DNAM-1) and potent cytotoxicity that can be produced even from heavily pretreated patients. Clinical case reports have documented remarkable responses in patients with sarcoma treated with the combination of NK cells and ICIs. Two patients with advanced chemo refractory sarcomas, one with desmoplastic small round cell tumor and the other with pleomorphic liposarcoma, achieved partial responses to SNK01 NK cells and pembrolizumab, with one patient attaining complete remission following local therapy. These individual cases suggest that NK cells may contribute to meaningful antitumor activity in combination with T-cell directed therapies, even in heavily pretreated metastatic sarcomas.
Although NK-based cell therapies are also gaining traction due to their MHC-independent activity and favorable safety profile, their role in sarcoma remains unexplored in late-phase trials. A phase I trial (NCT03941262) is evaluating SNK01 in combination with pembrolizumab or avelumab in patients with advanced refractory sarcoma. Meanwhile, trials such as NCT06117878 (NK510) and NCT05634369 are examining NK cells alone or in combination with chemotherapy. Hybrid strategies such as TCR-NK cells (NCT06083883) aim to combine the innate cytotoxicity of NK cells with the antigen specificity of TCRs. Other avenues for improving NK cell therapy include the development of CAR-NK cells, where NK cells are engineered with CARs targeting sarcoma-associated antigens, and the use of allogeneic “off-the-shelf” NK cell lines or stem cell-derived NK cells. Off-the-shelf NK cells could allow for repeat dosing and immediate availability, an advantage over autologous approaches. A first-of-a-kind multi-antigen targeted off-the-shelf CAR-NK therapy (FT596) was recently developed and demonstrated promising results in a phase 1 therapy with B-cell lymphomas. 

## Mesenchymal stromal cell therapy
MSC therapy involves the use of multipotent stromal cells with immunomodulatory and tumor-homing properties. MSCs are typically derived from the bone marrow, adipose tissue, or umbilical cord and are not inherently cytotoxic. Instead, they serve as carriers for therapeutic agents including cytokines, proapoptotic molecules or oncolytic viruses. MSCs do not rely on antigen recognition or HLA matching and are not engineered to exert direct immune effects. Their primary utility is to modify the TME and facilitate targeted delivery of therapeutic agents. Injected MSCs can migrate to tumor sites in response to inflammatory signals in the TME and engraft within tumor stroma. Therefore, MSCs have emerged as a novel cell-based therapeutic approach in sarcomas. 
Although MSC therapy for cancer remains largely experimental, genetically engineered MSCs expressing specific proteins may offer new treatment options. For example, MSCs engineered to express tumor necrosis factor-related apoptosis-inducing ligand have shown the ability to trigger extrinsic apoptotic pathways. Another approach uses MSCs to deliver osteoprotegerin, a decoy receptor for RANKL, in osteosarcoma. Osteosarcoma often causes bone destruction through RANKL-mediated osteoclast activation; MSC-produced osteoprotegerin can sequester RANKL, thereby inhibiting osteoclast activity and the cycle of bone resorption and tumor growth. Other approaches involve MSCs engineered to deliver cytosine deaminase-5-fluorouracil, a prodrug capable of converting cytosine to uracil, together with a cytotoxic drug to induce tumor cell apoptosis. Additionally, local administration of IL-12 has been shown to upregulate Fas expression in tumors and promote apoptotic cell death. 

## Future perspectives
Historically, sarcomas have posed significant therapeutic challenges due to their low immunogenicity, heterogeneity and limited responsiveness to ICIs. However, the continued advances in cell-based therapies may transform the therapeutic landscape of sarcoma. There is, as outlined in , a growing number of early phase clinical trials investigating a variety of cell therapy strategies.
 CAR-T cell therapies continue to dominate the clinical development pipeline with multiple antigenic targets, including GD2, B7-H3, and HER2 across multiple histologies. Notably, NCT04995003 is evaluating a novel combination of HER2-directed CAR-T cells with ICIs in HER2-positive sarcomas, reflecting the increasing interest in combination strategies to enhance durability and efficacy. Similarly, NCT03635632 and NCT05312411 are investigating GD2-directed and fluorescein-directed CAR-T cells, respectively, in relapsed sarcomas. Next-generation engineering strategies are also under active investigation to overcome the immunosuppressive TME of sarcomas. These include “armored” CAR-T cells that co-express cytokines like IL-12 or IL-18 to sustain local immune activation, and bispecific or tandem CAR constructs that recognize multiple tumor antigens simultaneously, thereby reducing the risk of antigen escape. Moreover, further advances aim to improve infiltration and metabolic resilience. In preclinical models, chemokine receptor engineering has shown remarkable potential to enhance CAR-T cell trafficking into sarcoma tissues. CXCR2-expressing or CXCR6-expressing B7-H3-directed CAR-T cells demonstrated improved tumor homing, deeper intratumoral persistence, and prolonged survival in osteosarcoma models. A recent preclinical study demonstrated that co-expression of CXCR5 and IL-7 within an NKG2D-based CAR backbone enhanced trafficking, proliferation and persistence of CAR-T cells in osteosarcoma cell lines. Genetic modifications to enhance trafficking (eg, heparanase expression for extracellular matrix (ECM) degradation), knock out inhibitory pathways (eg, PD-1), or co-opt additional co-stimulatory domains are also under investigation. Another promising strategy leverages intrinsic characteristics of the TME, such as hypoxia and acidic pH, to selectively activate CAR-T cells, thereby minimizing off-tumor activation in normal tissues. For example, a CAR construct incorporating an oxygen degradation domain was designed to stabilize CAR expression under hypoxic conditions but undergo degradation at normal oxygen levels. In murine tumor models, this approach achieved effective tumor suppression without notable systemic toxicity. These advances have the potential to dramatically improve the penetration and efficacy of CAR-T cells in dense, immunosuppressive sarcoma tumors.
Beyond CAR-T and TCR-T cell-based approaches, cytokine-induced killer (CIK) cells have emerged as an alternative adoptive platform with translational potential in sarcoma. CIK cells represent a heterogeneous population of polyclonal CD3+CD56+ T cells with functional properties of NK cells, and robust antitumor activity independent of MHC. When engineered with CAR constructs, they combine innate and antigen-specific activity, offering a versatile backbone for solid tumor targeting. Leuci et al demonstrated that CD44v6-directed CAR-CIK cells effectively suppressed high-grade STS growth in vitro and in vivo, while a subsequent CSPG4-targeted CAR-CIK model achieved potent and durable activity across multiple sarcoma subtypes. These findings highlight CIK cells as a next-generation effector platform warranting further investigation.
The observed activity of T-cell therapies, particularly those targeting CTAs, makes this a promising area for future research in sarcoma. Similar to these shared CTAs, sarcoma-specific fusion proteins such as SS18-SSX in synovial sarcoma or EWS-FLI1 in Ewing sarcoma offer unique, tumor-restricted neoantigens. Advances in neoantigen prediction algorithms, epitope validation, and high-throughput TCR discovery may soon facilitate the development of personalized TCR-T cell therapies tailored to individual tumor mutanomes. Future TCR-T studies include expanding targetable antigens and optimizing HLA-restricted approaches. TIL-based therapies, including the next-generation attIL12-TIL (NCT06474676), offer a personalized and potentially potent strategy by leveraging autologous T cells harvested from within the TME. Although technically challenging, these approaches are being refined with cytokine modulation (eg, IL-2 or membrane-bound IL-12) to enhance T-cell function and post-infusion survival. 
Efforts are also underway to enhance the immunogenicity of sarcoma using therapeutic cancer vaccines and oncolytic viruses. Although vaccine monotherapies have historically yielded limited benefit, their integration with adoptive T-cell therapies holds promise. For example, dendritic cell vaccines may help sustain the function of antigen-specific TCR-T, while oncolytic viruses such as talimogene laherparepvec (T-VEC) may inflame the TME and enhance immune cell trafficking. A phase II study combining T-VEC with pembrolizumab in STS has already demonstrated encouraging response rates. Beyond viral vectors, oncolytic peptides like LTX-315 have emerged as potent inducers of TME remodeling, capable of triggering immunogenic cell death, recruiting dendritic cells, and downregulating PD-L1 via inhibition of the ATP11B/CMTM6 axis. In a recent trial involving six patients with metastatic STS, intratumoral administration of LTX-315 followed by adoptive transfer of TILs was shown to be safe and feasible, leading to increased T-cell infiltration and the generation of de novo tumor-specific T-cell clones. The best response was durable stable disease in one patient, accompanied by circulating tumor-reactive T cells. Future trials may incorporate TIL or CAR-T cell infusions following viral priming to capitalize on tumor inflammation.

## Barriers and potential solutions in clinical implementation
Despite these advances, significant barriers remain, including antigen heterogeneity and escape, immunosuppressive TME, and manufacturing scalability. Notably, a recent phase 2 study demonstrated that combining neoantigen-selected TILs with PD-1 blockade (pembrolizumab) achieved a markedly higher response rate compared with TILs alone in patients with treatment-refractory gastrointestinal cancers. Future strategies are likely to involve combination regimens (eg, checkpoint blockade, cytokine support, or TME-targeting agents), biomarker-driven patient selection and synthetic biology approaches to improve targeting, durability and safety. Integration with earlier lines of therapy, identification of synergistic immunomodulatory agents and international collaborations will be pivotal to accelerate clinical translation and optimize patient outcomes.
In addition, there is still a need to optimize the toxicity management considering at least 10% of the patients had grade 3 or higher CRS with the cell therapies. Furthermore, cost issues could be a significant problem limiting access as demonstrated with CAR-T cell therapies in hematologic malignancies. There should be an ongoing multistakeholder effort to improve access to potentially life-prolonging cell therapies in these hard-to-treat tumors, like supporting local manufacturing, pooled funding and support systems to mobilize the patients to trial and treatment sites. Lastly, off-the-shelf production of the cell therapies also holds promise for improving stability and access. An important study with NY-ESO-1 TCR/IL-15 NK in synovial sarcoma and myxoid round cell liposarcoma (NCT06083883) was recently granted investigational new drug approval from FDA and similar studies with off-the-shelf products are eagerly expected in the cell therapies for sarcoma.
Despite the promising efficacy of engineered TCR-T cell therapies such as afami-cel and lete-cel, their applicability remains limited by immunogenetic and biological constraints. Notably, these therapies are restricted to patients with the HLA-A*02 allele, which is expressed in less than 50% of patients. When combined with the requirement for tumor expression of specific CTAs such as MAGE-A4 or NY-ESO-1, the eligible patient population becomes substantially reduced. This presents a key challenge in ensuring broader applicability of these innovative therapies. Future strategies aimed at expanding the HLA coverage or developing HLA-independent approaches will be essential to overcome these barriers and maximize the clinical impact of TCR-T cell therapy in sarcomas.
Finally, a major translational research gap remains in defining predictive biomarkers of response and toxicity. Beyond antigen expression and HLA restriction, dynamic markers such as circulating tumor DNA kinetics, T-cell subset composition (effector vs central memory), and serum cytokine profiles may guide personalized therapy optimization. Additionally, some investigators have explored biomarker strategies based on tracking CAR-T cells engineered to recognize soluble antigens; for example, reductions in soluble transforming growth factor-β (TGF)-β have been shown to correlate with indirect CAR-T effector activity in preclinical and early clinical studies. Similarly, soluble MUC16 and HER2 have been leveraged as surrogate indicators of CAR-T mediated engagement and function. Incorporating such immune monitoring frameworks into ongoing sarcoma trials will be essential to improve patient selection and mechanistic understanding.

## Conclusion
Adoptive cell therapy offers a promising new treatment option for patients with sarcoma. The 2024 US FDA approval of the first genetically-engineered TCR-T, afamitresgene autoleucel for patients with synovial sarcoma represents an important milestone for cell therapy in sarcoma. While early signals of efficacy are encouraging in this poor-prognosis population, further validation is needed across the heterogeneous spectrum of sarcoma subtypes. Despite ongoing challenges, including immune evasion and the complex TME, advances in cellular engineering and antigen discovery are steadily expanding the therapeutic potential of these approaches. In parallel, the effective integration of cell therapies into clinical practice will require the establishment of specialized centers and multidisciplinary teams trained to deliver them safely and efficiently.
