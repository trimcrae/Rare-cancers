## Abstract
Lurbinectedin is a selective inhibitor of oncogenic transcription approved for the treatment of adult patients with metastatic small cell lung cancer with disease progression on or after platinum-based chemotherapy. Preclinical data provide evidence for lurbinectedin exerting its actions in a unique manner that involves oncogenic transcription inhibition, DNA damage, reshaping of the tumor microenvironment, and inducing anticancer immunity. Understanding the mechanism of action has facilitated the rational combination of lurbinectedin and anticancer therapies with complementary modes of action, in order to obtain synergistic effects that could potentially lead to improved efficacy. This review evaluates the mechanism of action for lurbinectedin and provides an overview of the therapeutic landscape with regards to lurbinectedin combination therapies for the treatment of small cell lung cancer based on data from preclinical and clinical studies.

## Introduction
Small cell lung cancer (SCLC) is a lethal malignancy characterized by rapid growth and high propensity to metastasize ( ). Two thirds of patients have tumor dissemination beyond the chest at initial diagnosis ( ), and the 5-year survival rate is less than 10% ( ). SCLC accounts for ∼15% of lung cancers and has been described as a “recalcitrant cancer” because of the lack of actionable targets that, for decades, precluded the development of effective treatments ( , ).
In contrast with most non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) subtypes that are defined by oncogenic mutations ( , ), the genetic drivers in SCLC are tumor suppressor genes, with near-universal TP53 and RB1 inactivation ( , – ), alongside other loci ( , ). SCLC is underpinned by amplification and fusion of genes such as MYC, MYCL1, and SOX2 ( , ). Signaling pathways recurrently affected in SCLC are involved in cell cycle and transcriptional regulation, as well as Notch signaling and neuroendocrine differentiation ( ). Definition of SCLC subtypes is converging on the differential expression of four key transcription factors: ASCL1, NEUROD1, POU2F3, and YAP1, named SCLC-A, SCLC-N, SCLC-P, and SCLC-Y, respectively ( ). The SCLC-A and SCLC-N subtypes are defined by expression of ASCL1 and NEUROD1, respectively, which relate to neuroendocrine features and are the most common ( , ). SCLC-Y has been replaced by SCLC-I (for “inflamed”), as YAP1 expression does not exclusively define a single subtype and shows immune activation and low levels of ASCL1, NEUROD1, and POU2F3 ( , , ). Although RB1 inactivation is a feature of most SCLC tumors, a subset of YAP1-high neuroendocrine tumors has recently been shown to express RB1 ( ). Each molecular subtype of SCLC has been predicted to have distinct vulnerabilities, including to immunotherapy (SCLC-I) and either to PARP (SCLC-P), Aurora kinase (SCLC-N), B-cell leukemia/lymphoma 2 protein inhibitors (SCLC-A), or cyclin-dependent kinase 4/6 inhibitors (RB1-positive; refs. , ). However, further clinical validation of these subtype-specific vulnerabilities is necessary; moreover, classification is complicated by variability within tumors, which could lead to resistance to treatment ( , ). In a study of SCLC tumor samples from patients receiving chemoimmunotherapy, clinical benefit was associated with an enhanced capacity for antigen presentation but not subtype classification ( ). In addition, it has been shown that NSCLC with EGFR mutations may histologically transform into SCLC after treatment failure or the development of EGFR-tyrosine kinase inhibitor or immunotherapy resistance, although the underpinning mechanisms remain unclear ( ). Retrospective analysis indicates the median time to transformation is 16 to 18 months and mutations in BRAF, RB1, TP53, and PIK3CA are commonly identified ( , ). In these cases, therapeutic options may be limited to platinum-etoposide and taxanes ( , ).
As DNA processing mechanisms, including transcription and replication, are intimately involved when dysregulated by pathogenesis, they are among the most straightforward targets for disrupting cancer evolution ( ). Oncogene addiction (due to high transcriptional activity) may be counteracted by targeting specific components including transcription factors and/or gene-specific DNA sequences ( , ).
Lurbinectedin, a synthetic, marine-derived agent and analog of trabectedin, is a selective inhibitor of oncogenic transcription ( , ) that received accelerated FDA approval for the treatment of adults with metastatic SCLC and disease progression on or after platinum-based chemotherapy. This approval was based on the results of a phase II basket trial (NCT02454972) that demonstrated an overall response rate (ORR) of 35.2% and a median duration of response of 5.3 months observed in 105 patients with relapsed SCLC after platinum-based chemotherapy ( – ).
SCLC cells subjected to intense transcriptional activation due to overexpressed ASCL1 and NEUROD1 activators (a transcription addiction effect) require a continuously open chromatin state to allow reading of the gene by RNA polymerase II (Pol II; ref. ). Such intense transcription activity that maintains and increases tumorigenesis also leads to a vulnerability in the accessibility of identified CG-rich regions (CpG islands; refs. , ). In this review, we will discuss how lurbinectedin takes advantage of this situation that represents the “Achilles’ heel” of SCLC cells by inhibiting oncogenic transcriptional programs. Lurbinectedin’s other actions include DNA damage, reshaping the tumor microenvironment, and inducing anticancer immunity ( ). Furthermore, potential combinations of lurbinectedin with other therapies that may offer synergistic treatment endeavors will be discussed.

## SCLC Sensitivity to Lurbinectedin

In vitro efficacy of lurbinectedin in SCLC cells occurs at very low nanomolar concentrations and has been demonstrated in cell lines from all four SCLC subtypes (SCLC-A, SCLC-N, SCLC-P, and SCLC-I); in particular, SCLC cell lines expressing high levels of POU2F3 are especially sensitive to lurbinectedin ( – ). Another study of four platinum-resistant secondary carcinoma cell lines found no cross resistance to lurbinectedin in three, with the fourth showing increased sensitivity to lurbinectedin compared with parental cells ( ). In a recent study of human SCLC cell lines and patient-derived xenograft (PDX) models of SCLC-A, SCLC-N, SCLC-P, and SCLC-I, lurbinectedin reduced cell viability in all four subtypes ( ). It has to be pointed out that in vitro data indicated that SCLC-A cell lines were less sensitive to lurbinectedin in comparison with SCLC-P cell lines; however, the in vivo PDX models indicated that the SCLC-A subtype was more sensitive to lurbinectedin than SCLC-P and SCLC-N subtypes, highlighting the need for both in vitro and in vivo testing ( ).

## Lurbinectedin-Mediated Inhibition of Oncogenic Transcription

## DNA binding
Lung cancers are often associated with an inherent genomic instability and their genomic landscape has been shown to exhibit widespread alterations such as DNA methylation, point mutations, copy number variation, aneuploidy, and chromosomal translocations ( , ). Lurbinectedin is a synthetic alkaloid containing a pentacyclic skeleton composed of two fused tetrahydroisoquinoline rings and a tetrahydro β-carboline unit ( ) that recognizes the minor groove of DNA and binds through the hydroxyl of its hemiaminal group to the central guanine of CGG, AGC, AGG, and TGG DNA triplets ( ). Further chem-seq studies using antibodies directed to the biotin moiety of the lurbinectedin structural analog have identified CpG islands downstream to the transcription start site as main key targets for the drug ( , ). DNase I footprinting assays have shown that tetrahydroisoquinoline alkaloids, such as lurbinectedin, covalently bind to one DNA strand and establish noncovalent bonds with the opposite strand, thereby preventing strand separation as well as transcription on both DNA strands ( , ). Molecular modeling and molecular dynamic simulations have provided additional insights into noncovalent interactions, such as hydrogen bonds and Van der Waals interactions between lurbinectedin and both DNA strands ( , ).
 

## Transcriptional blockade and targeted genes
In SCLC, the transcription addiction process is mainly directed by dysregulated expression of DNA-binding proteins including ASCL1 and NEUROD1, which continuously activate transcription of their responsive genes as demonstrated by the presence of elongating RNA Pol II ( ). Analysis by the assay for transposase-accessible chromatin using sequencing, a method for determining chromatin accessibility across the genome, reveals that the promoters of activated ASCL-1- and NEUROD1-responsive genes are maintained in an open chromatin structure, making DNA fully accessible to DNA binders ( ). Binding of lurbinectedin to CpG-rich islands downstream of transcription start sites irreversibly stalls elongating RNA Pol II, which triggers its subsequent ubiquitination and eventual proteasomal degradation ( ). As a consequence, SCLC cells are driven to apoptosis ( ). Interestingly, other RNA polymerases, including Pol I and Pol III, were not degraded upon lurbinectedin treatment ( ).

In vitro chem-seq, chromatin immunoprecipitation sequencing, and assay for transposase-accessible chromatin using sequencing studies have demonstrated that lurbinectedin preferentially targets certain genomic loci, in particular, open chromatin regions including the promoter of activated ASCL1- and NEUROD1-responsive genes ( ). In DMS53 SCLC cells, after a 4-hour treatment, lurbinectedin targeted more than half of ASCL1 and NEUROD1-responsive genes that were being actively transcribed (62% and 71% of genes, respectively). Overall, 76% of genes downregulated after lurbinectedin treatment were targets of ASCL1 or NEUROD1. Crucially, lurbinectedin downregulated mRNA expression of ASCL1 and NEUROD1 themselves, as well as BCL2, INSM1, MYC, MYB, AURKA, and CDK7, most of which are ASCL1- and NEUROD1-responsive genes implicated in the tumorigenesis and pathophysiology of SCLC, thus representing potential therapeutic targets. By blocking overactive ASCL1- and NEUROD1-mediated transcriptional activity in SCLC-A and SCLC-N subtypes, and downregulating several genes frequently overexpressed in SCLC, lurbinectedin exploits the tumorigenic properties of SCLC cells to eventually trigger their apoptosis ( ; ref. ).
In human SCLC cell lines, lurbinectedin treatment suppressed POU2F3 and upregulated NEUROD1 in the SCLC-P cell line and suppressed ASCL1 and upregulated NEUROD1 and NCAM1 in the SCLC-A cell line ( ). Lurbinectedin treatment also led to upregulation of several tumor progression genes including POU5F1, ICFBP2, POLR2L, and NANOG in both SCLC-P and SCLC-A cell lines, demonstrating that lurbinectedin alters neuroendocrine genes and tumor progression in vitro ( ). In PDX in vivo models, lurbinectedin treatment delayed tumor growth and was associated with dysregulation of WNT/β-catenin, AKT–PI3K–mTORC1, and EMT signaling ( ).

## DNA damage
The formation of DNA breaks in the vicinity of lurbinectedin binding (particularly at the promoter regions) increases the efficacy of this drug in arresting the tumorigenesis process ( , , ). It has been postulated that these DNA breaks may result from an incorrect elimination of DNA damage ( ). For example, lurbinectedin can interfere with nucleotide excision repair machinery and delay, although not completely inhibit, the repair process ( ). In fact, cells proficient in transcription-coupled nucleotide excision DNA repair mechanisms (TCR) show increased sensitivity to lurbinectedin compared with TCR-deficient cells ( ), and the incorrect DNA repair mediated by TCR triggers the occurrence of double-strand breaks (DSB; ref. ). In contrast, cell lines defective in homologous recombination showed a >270-fold increased sensitivity to lurbinectedin compared with control cell lines due to inefficient DSB elimination, indicating the persistence of unrepaired DSB as a key mechanism of action induced by the drug ( ). Lurbinectedin has also been found to induce activation of ATR [ataxia-telangiectasia mutated (ATM) and RAD3-related], ATM, and DNA-protein kinase (primary kinases that regulate DNA repair), and increase expression of H2AX and PARP1 (involved in the DNA damage response pathway; refs. , ). Lurbinectedin induces DNA damage in an ERCC5/XPG-dependent manner, which can lead to cell cycle arrest either in G1- (CDKN1A/p21-dependent) or S-phase (ATR-dependent manner), leading to cell death ( ). In addition to DNA breaks, lurbinectedin treatment also results in the degradation of elongating RNA Pol II, leading to transcription arrest, which further drives cells to apoptosis ( ; ref. ).

## Challenges of the SCLC Tumor Microenvironment
Aside from tumor cells, the tumor microenvironment comprises a complex ecosystem of immune and scaffold components, both cellular and noncellular, including tumor-associated macrophages and neutrophils, cytotoxic and regulatory T cells, B cells, NK cells, blood vessel endothelial cells, chemokines, cytokines, and growth factors ( , ). Within the SCLC tumor microenvironment, several factors contribute to the extremely low immunogenicity of SCLC, including poor antigen presentation and low immune cell infiltration, which may limit response to immunotherapy in SCLC ( ).
Cells use MHC-I to display antigens to cytotoxic CD8+ T cells, which is key to efficient killing of tumor cells by these cells ( ). SCLC tumors typically express low levels of MHC-I and have been described as “immunologically cold;” however, an analysis of patient tumor samples demonstrated that although most (81%) patient tumors did not express MHC-I, a small subset of tumors expressed high or heterogeneous levels of MHC-I ( ). Genetically-engineered mouse models and patient tissue samples indicate that the SCLC tumor microenvironment has a smaller population of innate immune cells than NSCLC, and the population is more lymphocyte predominant with low levels of leukocytes ( ). Single-cell sequencing of SCLC tumor samples demonstrated that neuroendocrine tumors were more likely to be “immunologically cold” with low levels of immune infiltrates, whereas non-neuroendocrine SCLC tumors tended to be well-infiltrated by immune and stromal cells, which is associated with improved outcomes ( , ). In another study analyzing clinical samples, SCLC-A tumors were the most heterogeneous in terms of immunogenicity (as assessed by NK and T-cell scores) and SCLC-P tumors were the most immunogenic ( ). NK cells have been shown to be important for limiting the spread of SCLC in mouse models ( ). However, SCLC cells also express lower levels of ligands for NK cells, contributing to a reduction in immunogenicity ( ). Interestingly, CD8+ T cells were demonstrated to be insignificant in reducing SCLC metastasis in a mouse model ( ), although higher levels of MHC-I expression and CD8+ T cells in patient samples were associated with an improved response to immune checkpoint blockade ( ).

## Lurbinectedin reshapes the tumor microenvironment and induces anticancer immunity
Localized niches within the tumor microenvironment support tumor immune evasion; however, the tumor microenvironment also offers additional targets for anticancer therapies ( ). Lurbinectedin and its analog trabectedin have a unique ability to affect the tumor microenvironment ( , ). For example, lurbinectedin has demonstrated in vivo activity against a drug-resistant tumor variant generated in vitro by increasing drug concentrations ( , ), providing evidence that it can reduce levels of circulating monocytes and tumor-associated macrophages and reduce tumor angiogenesis by directly and indirectly affecting the tumor microenvironment ( ; ref. ). Lurbinectedin may also be able to induce immunogenic cancer cell death, a form of regulated cell death that can be triggered by cellular stressors such as oncolytic viruses and chemotherapeutic drugs ( , ). Dying tumor cells release damage-associated molecular patterns, which subsequently stimulate pattern recognition receptors and activate T cells and tumor-specific antitumor immune responses ( ; ref. ).
Data from a real-world prospective cohort of patients with previously treated SCLC suggested that lurbinectedin might have immune-modulatory functions by promoting proliferation and phenotype shifting of antitumor cell populations. Lurbinectedin increased the proliferation of circulating CD4+ and CD8+ T cells, NK cells, and NK T cells without affecting their ratio within the lymphocyte compartment ( ). This increased proliferation was proposed to result from secondary cytotoxic effects, likely via increased release of tumor-derived damage-associated molecular patterns, rather than direct lurbinectedin activity on the immune cells. Although this lurbinectedin-induced increase in proliferation was independent of clinical response, patients with a higher-than-median proportion of proliferating CD8+ cells prior to treatment had longer progression-free survival (PFS) than those with a lower-than-median proportion (median PFS 4.7 vs. 2.1 months; P = 0.04). A small but significant increase in co-expression of CD28 on CD4+ and CD8+ cells indicated T-cell activation by lurbinectedin. This upregulation of the T-cell stimulatory receptor was accompanied by upregulation of the inhibitor receptor TIM-3, reflecting a counterbalance by the simultaneous upregulation of both co-stimulatory receptors and co-inhibitory markers. Although these findings are based on peripheral blood and may not reflect the tumor microenvironment, they suggest a potential benefit for the combination of lurbinectedin with immunotherapy ( ).

## Rationale for the Combination of Lurbinectedin with Other Therapies
The combination of lurbinectedin with other anticancer therapies targeting complementary mechanisms of action has the potential to promote additive or synergistic effects via multiple molecular pathways and cellular effects. Additional details of the clinical studies of lurbinectedin discussed in the following sections are provided in .
 

## Combination with immune checkpoint inhibitors
In SCLC, responses to immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICI) alone are substantially lower than those in NSCLC, perhaps due, in part, to low infiltration by cytotoxic T cells ( ). Of the four SCLC subtypes, SCLC-I (accounting for ∼18% of SCLC) is thought to be most susceptible to immunotherapy and is enriched for higher expression of checkpoint molecules, HLA, and, IFN signatures ( ). Notably, a recent report demonstrates that none of the subtypes (SCLC-A, SCLC-N, SCLC-P, and SCLC-Y) nor intratumoral heterogeneity was consistently associated with chemoimmunotherapy benefit in patients with SCLC; however, an IFN-γ-dominated transcriptomic profile, including enhanced capacity for antigen presentation, was associated with long-term benefit ( ). Data from mouse models have shown that lurbinectedin induces multimodal immune modulation and augments the antitumor immune response in SCLC ( ). Although lurbinectedin alone delayed tumor growth, when used in combination with anti–PD-L1, six of 10 mice had a complete response (100% reduction) and four had 90% tumor regression, providing a strong rationale for the combination with PD-L1 pathway inhibitors ( ). In patients with relapsed SCLC, initial responses of ∼20% were documented with co-administered anti–PD-1 and anti–CTLA-4 antibodies nivolumab and ipilimumab, and with single-agent anti–PD-1 pembrolizumab in the open-label, phase I/II trials CheckMate 032 ( ) and KEYNOTE-028 and KEYNOTE-158 trials ( ), respectively. Nonetheless, a lack in ICI monotherapy effectiveness was observed in a phase II study of the anti–PD-L1 antibody atezolizumab and a phase III study of nivolumab as monotherapy in patients with relapsed SCLC, neither of which showed significant efficacy versus chemotherapy when used in unselected populations ( , ). However, a survival benefit was observed with ICIs combined with chemotherapy in treatment-naïve patients with SCLC. Both atezolizumab and durvalumab improved overall survival (OS) compared with chemotherapy alone in large, randomized phase III trials of patients receiving first-line therapy for SCLC ( , ), indicating that appropriate combinations with other anticancer therapies are likely required for effective immunotherapy in SCLC.
An optimal combination therapy with immunotherapy in a highly aggressive disease such as SCLC should be cytotoxic for the tumor cell, reinforce tumor cell immunogenicity, alleviate immune suppression, and enhance T-cell cytotoxic activity. Given that lurbinectedin has been shown to attenuate immunosuppression, induce immunogenic cell death, and increase T-cell proliferation ( , , ), and given the positive findings in vivo ( ), its potential to sensitize tumors toward ICIs represents a plausible strategy ( ). Moreover, lurbinectedin is a directly active antitumor agent that may prevent patients from early tumor progression, allowing time to mount an adaptive immune response against SCLC ( ).
This combination of lurbinectedin with dual inhibition of PD-1 and CTLA-4 using nivolumab and ipilimumab was investigated in a phase I/II study (NCT04610658), although it was discontinued because of dose-limiting toxicities ( ). Another PD-1 inhibitor, pembrolizumab, is being investigated in combination with lurbinectedin in the phase I/II LUPER study in patients with relapsed SCLC (NCT04358237). The combination of lurbinectedin and pembrolizumab was found to be feasible, and the recommended phase II dose for lurbinectedin was 3.2 mg/m2 ( ). The phase II part of the study met the prespecified threshold of efficacy, with a confirmed 46.4% ORR, including two complete responses and two durable responses >12 months ( ). Atezolizumab in combination with lurbinectedin is under investigation for previously treated SCLC in the phase I/II 2SMALL study (NCT04253145) and as maintenance therapy in the phase III IMforte study (NCT05091567). Preliminary data for the 2SMALL study show evidence of antitumor activity, with more than 50% of the 26 patients having a complete or partial response and a disease control rate (DCR) of 85% ( ). Phase II studies of durvalumab with lurbinectedin are also ongoing (NCT04607954) or planned (NCT05572476; ).

## Combination with chemotherapy: DNA binding, antimetabolites, and tubulin-targeting agents
A synergistic effect of lurbinectedin and cisplatin has been observed both in cisplatin-sensitive ovarian A2780 carcinoma and cisplatin-resistant A2780/CP70 cells ( ). Furthermore, evidence of synergistic tumor reduction upon lurbinectedin and cisplatin treatment was observed in a cisplatin-resistant epithelial ovarian cancer mouse model ( ). Unfortunately, a phase I study of lurbinectedin in combination with cisplatin in patients with advanced solid tumors reported its unfeasibility at therapeutic dosage due to hematologic toxicity ( ; ref. ). In in vivo mouse models with gastric, ovarian, NSCLC, breast, or prostate tumors, lurbinectedin in combination with paclitaxel or docetaxel (microtubule-stabilizing drugs) resulted in more antitumor activity than lurbinectedin alone ( , ). A phase I study has investigated lurbinectedin combination with paclitaxel in patients with advanced solid tumors and in the seven patients with SCLC, resulting in an ORR of 71% (n = 5/7) across dose levels ( ; ref. ). Three patients had a better response with the recommended dose of lurbinectedin than with their prior therapy. The clinical benefit lasted >12 months in two patients and antitumor activity as well as response rates with combination therapy were noted to be higher than those previously reported for lurbinectedin monotherapy, providing further support for the development of a lurbinectedin–paclitaxel combination for the treatment of SCLC ( ). Lurbinectedin was shown to act synergistically with the nucleoside analog gemcitabine in a pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma xenograft model ( , ), and in a phase I study, treatment with lurbinectedin in combination with gemcitabine in patients with advanced solid tumors resulted in an ORR of 24% (n = 9/38; ; ref. ).

## Combination with DNA topoisomerase I and II inhibitors
Tetrahydroquinoline alkaloids such as lurbinectedin generate DNA adducts that trap topoisomerase I ( , ). Topoisomerase I generates nicks in the DNA to remove supercoils formed during the transcriptional process ( ). Irinotecan inhibits the re-sealing activity of topoisomerase I, but not its nicking activity ( ). The combination of lurbinectedin and irinotecan could perpetuate the generation of DNA breaks in the tumor cell. The combination of lurbinectedin with the topoisomerase I inhibitor irinotecan has been investigated in xenograft murine models of Ewing sarcoma ( ). Although both single agents delayed tumor growth, the combination led to sustained tumor regression and improved survival with significantly low toxicity. Interestingly, the effects were maintained more than 90 days beyond the end of treatment. Furthermore, preclinical studies of lurbinectedin in combination with SN-38, the active metabolite of irinotecan, resulted in synergistic suppression of EWS-FLI1 expression in TC32 Ewing sarcoma cells ( ) and significant antiproliferative effects in ovarian clear-cell carcinoma cell lines ( ). In vivo tumor growth was also significantly reduced upon treatment with lurbinectedin and SN-38, with an 85.1% smaller xenograft tumor volume compared with control and with either agent alone ( ). Considering the potential clinical applicability of these results alongside clinically feasible concentrations, a phase I/II study of the lurbinectedin/irinotecan combination in patients with solid tumors, including SCLC, is ongoing (NCT02611024; ). Preliminary results from the study expansion stage, which comprised 21 patients with advanced SCLC with no more than two prior lines of therapies, reported an ORR of 62% and ongoing median PFS of 6.2 months ( ). Remarkably, the combination was active in both platinum-sensitive [chemotherapy-free interval (CFI) ≥90 days] and platinum-resistant (CFI <90 days) SCLC, with ORRs of 69% and 50%, respectively. The ongoing phase III LAGOON study is evaluating lurbinectedin with or without irinotecan against the investigator’s choice of topotecan or irinotecan in patients with relapsed SCLC and previously treated with one platinum-containing therapy in the presence or absence of anti–PD-1 and anti–PD-L1 treatment (NCT05153239) with a primary endpoint of OS ( ).
The combination of lurbinectedin and the topoisomerase II inhibitor doxorubicin has shown an additive, but not synergistic, effect (combination index ∼1) on cell proliferation in an in vitro study of ovarian clear-cell carcinoma cell lines ( , ) and additive and synergistic antitumor effects in xenografted SCLC tumors in mice ( ). Furthermore, the combination has shown clinical response and antitumor activity in a phase I study in patients with relapsed SCLC at a recommended dose of doxorubicin 50 mg/m2 and lurbinectedin 4.0 mg flat dose on day 1 every 3 weeks ( ; ref. ). The ORR (n = 26) was 57.7% [95% confidence interval (CI), 36.9%–76.6%], DCR was 69.2% (95% CI, 48.2%–85.6%), and tumor shrinkage was observed in 20/26 patients (76.9%), including all 12 patients with sensitive disease and 7/9 patients with resistant disease who were receiving second-line therapy. The overall median PFS was 4.1 months, which increased to 5.8 months for patients with sensitive disease who were receiving second-line therapy. Transient and reversible myelosuppression (including neutropenia, thrombocytopenia, and febrile neutropenia) represented the main toxicity ( ). In a subsequent evaluation of 28 patients with SCLC receiving second-line therapy with a lower doxorubicin dose (40 mg/m2) and lurbinectedin at 2.0 mg/m2, the ORR was 36% (95% CI, 18.6%–55.9%), DCR was 72% (95% CI, 51.3%–86.8%), and tumor shrinkage was observed in 18/26 (69%) patients ( ). This combination was subsequently evaluated in the randomized phase III ATLANTIS trial of lurbinectedin/doxorubicin versus standard of care (topotecan or cyclophosphamide/doxorubicin/vincristine) in patients with SCLC who received a prior platinum-containing treatment ( ). However, this trial did not meet the prespecified primary endpoint of median OS [HR = 0.97 (95% CI, 0.82–1.15), P = 0.70], with comparable activity for both arms ( ). It was noted, however, that lurbinectedin in combination with doxorubicin demonstrated activity in relapsed SCLC and was better tolerated than topotecan or cyclophosphamide, doxorubicin, and vincristine ( ). Possible reasons for the negative trial results in the ATLANTIS trial include the lower dose of lurbinectedin (2 mg/m2) used in combination with doxorubicin compared with that of lurbinectedin monotherapy (3.2 mg/m2) in the basket trial and the inclusion of patients with limited central nervous system involvement ( ). A post hoc exposure–response analysis has predicted significantly longer survival for single-agent lurbinectedin at 3.2 mg/m2 versus the control arm ( ). In fact, patients assigned to the experimental arm who switched to lurbinectedin monotherapy at 3.2 mg/m2 after completing 10 cycles of lurbinectedin in combination with doxorubicin tended to maintain or improve their tumor response (including an increase in complete responses), with favorable OS and duration of response ( , ). Moreover, a post hoc analysis of patients with SCLC with a CFI ≥30 days and no central nervous system metastases treated with lurbinectedin (3.2 mg/m2) from the basket trial matched with the topotecan-treated patients from ATLANTIS found a positive benefit to risk ratio with lurbinectedin, superior to that observed with topotecan ( ).

## Combination with dual DNA damage response inhibitors
There is potential to combine lurbinectedin with dual inhibitors of the ATM/checkpoint kinase 2 and ATR/checkpoint kinase 1 DNA damage response pathways, which are activated by lurbinectedin treatment ( ). In a PDX mouse model of SCLC, treatment with the combination of lurbinectedin and the ATR inhibitor berzosertib resulted in significant toxicity with repeated doses ( ). Additional investigations revealed an increase in lurbinectedin-induced DNA DSBs upon dual ATM and ATR inhibition compared with lurbinectedin or dual inhibition alone, and an increase in the mitotic cell fraction indicating a G2/M checkpoint arrest ( ). These preclinical findings provide a mechanistic rationale to support future clinical investigations of lurbinectedin with ATM/ATR dual inhibitors; indeed, a phase I/II study of berzosertib and lurbinectedin is underway (NCT04802174; ).

## Other potential combinations
The adequate patient selection based on molecular SCLC subtypes may guide combinatorial regimens to explore clinical activity in smaller subsets of patients. It has been postulated that the SCLC-P subgroup displays a unique vulnerability to PARP inhibitors, and expression of SLFN11 in SCLC-A is predictive of response to PARP inhibitors ( ). The PARP inhibitor talazoparib combined with atezolizumab was found to improve PFS in SLFN11-selected patients with SCLC ( ). The PARP inhibitor olaparib has been safely combined with lurbinectedin in phase I and II trials in patients with advanced solid and gynecologic tumors ( , ), supporting further investigation of this combination in SCLC.
Finally, the combination of lurbinectedin with ONC201, a small-molecule compound that induces apoptosis via TRAIL and DR5 pathways and caspase-8 activation, revealed a synergistic toxicity toward patient-derived SCLC cells in vitro, while not affecting lung epithelial cells ( ). Western blot analysis revealed a concentration-dependent increase in expression of phosphorylated checkpoint kinase 1, ATF4, and ClpP upon treatment with both agents, indicating the presence of DNA damage and stress-induced apoptosis. These in vitro findings support further investigation of this combination.

## The Safety Profile of Lurbinectedin
Adverse events (AE) are common with lurbinectedin treatment and include leukopenia, lymphopenia, fatigue, anemia, neutropenia, and thrombocytopenia ( ). In a safety analysis of lurbinectedin monotherapy, 34% of patients experienced serious AEs that included pneumonia, febrile neutropenia, neutropenia, respiratory tract infection, anemia, dyspnea, and thrombocytopenia ( ). Less than 2% of patients experienced AEs leading to permanent discontinuation (including peripheral neuropathy and myelosuppression). AEs led to dose interruption and reduction in 30.5% and 25% patients, respectively ( ). An analysis of safety data pooled from 554 patients with advanced solid tumors (primarily ovarian, SCLC, and endometrial carcinoma) found lurbinectedin to have a manageable safety profile; the most common toxicity was temporary and reversible myelosuppression ( ).
A trial evaluating the combination of lurbinectedin with nivolumab and ipilimumab (NCT04610658) was discontinued because of dose-limiting toxicities and dose-limiting toxicities prevented therapeutic dosing in NCT01980667, which evaluated the combination of lurbinectedin with cisplatin in patients with advanced solid tumors ( ; ref. ). The most frequent grade ≥3 AEs were neutropenia, lymphopenia, leukopenia, thrombocytopenia, fatigue, and nausea. Dose-limiting toxicities included three patients with grade 4 thrombocytopenia, one patient with grade 4 febrile neutropenia, two patients with grade 3 asthenia ≥3 days, one patient with grade 4 neutropenia >5 days, and one patient with grade 4 acute renal failure ( ).

## Conclusions and Future Directions
Due to its specificity in targeting DNA sequences such as CG-rich motifs found in promoter regions of activated ASCL1 and NEUROD1-responsive genes, lurbinectedin represents a significant advancement in the treatment of SCLC. Lurbinectedin’s unique mode of action, which encompasses DNA binding, transcriptional blockade, DNA damage, effects on the tumor microenvironment, and immunogenic responses, as well as its manageable safety profile, opens the door to it being a potential foundational therapy in combination therapy. Results from preclinical and clinical studies support the investigation and development of lurbinectedin combinations with a range of drugs that utilize complementary, and ideally synergistic, strategies. Ongoing clinical trials will shed further light on the clinical application of lurbinectedin in combination with other therapies. Further investigations would be needed to find a drug much more specific than lurbinectedin, which targets CpG islands found in a large number of genes. A hypothetical biomarker could target the enhancers of specific genes, thus preventing the recruitment of their cognate factors and/or further DNA-binding enzymatic processes.
