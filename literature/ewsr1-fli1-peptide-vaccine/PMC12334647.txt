## Abstract
We recently demonstrated that GGAA promoters (GGAAprom) can be used to drive the expression of therapeutic genes (i.e., Cas9) in vitro and in vivo in a highly specific way in Ewing sarcoma cells. In this study, we take advantage of this strategy to express the suicide gene thymidine kinase from herpes simplex virus (HSV-TK), a suicide gene frequently used as an antitumoral therapy in clinical settings. The expression of HSV-TK in Ewing sarcoma cells under the control of the GGAA promoter resulted in cell death in the presence of ganciclovir both in vitro and in vivo. Interestingly, in vitro studies revealed a strong bystander effect. In preclinical models, the tumor volume of Ewing sarcoma xenografts expressing HSV-TK was dramatically reduced upon valganciclovir treatment. When adenoviral vectors were used as suitable therapeutics for clinical translation, HSV-TK expression in Ewing sarcoma cells reduced cell viability in vitro and reduced tumor growth in vivo. Interestingly, the death of Ewing sarcoma cells was accompanied by massive infiltration of the residual tumor by immune cells. Our results demonstrate that suicide genes can be delivered in a specific way to Ewing sarcoma cells by combining Ewing-specific GGAA promoters and adenoviral vectors, paving the way for the development of cancer gene therapies based on the selective expression of genes with therapeutic potential.

## Introduction
Ewing sarcoma is a rare bone sarcoma affecting children and young adults. Despite international collaborative initiatives to improve the prognosis of these patients, no significant improvements have been achieved in the last two decades, and as a consequence, the therapeutic regimens of these treatments have essentially been the same for decades. Thus, the gold standard for treating of these patients is standard chemotherapy/radiotherapy and surgery, which results in a 3-year overall survival rate of 82% , . However, the prognosis for certain groups of patients, such as, those with metastatic disease at diagnosis or those with unresectable primary tumors, which are located mainly in the axial skeleton, remains particularly poor, as second-line treatments are largely ineffective , . Furthermore, despite international research efforts – , no targeted therapies for Ewing sarcoma have reached the clinical setting, contrary to what has fortunately happened in adult cancers . Thus, Ewing sarcoma is a prototypical orphan disease, not only because of its low incidence but also because of the absence of specific treatments. Since conventional treatments with chemotherapy and radiotherapy have likely reached their maximum potential to cure these patients, the development of new disruptive therapeutic approaches that truly revolutionize the treatment of this tumor is urgently needed , , .
From a molecular point of view, Ewing sarcomas are characterized by the expression of chimeric aberrant transcription factors that arise from reciprocal chromosomal translocations . These chimeric transcription factors are formed by the N-terminal region of a member of the FET family of RNA-binding proteins , more frequently EWSR1, and the C-terminal region of a member of the ETS family of transcription factors , more frequently FLI1, which contains the DNA-binding domain , . One of the more intriguing properties of EWSR1::FLI1 fusion proteins is their ability to bind GGAA microsatellites (that is, sequences formed by the concatenation of a variable number of GGAA repeats) and activate the expression of genes located near these DNA elements , . Interestingly, the ability to bind GGAA concatenated repeats and activate gene transcription is exclusive to EWSR1::FLI1, since native FLI1 is not able to transactivate these GGAA-repeat sequences despite sharing the same DNA-binding domain as EWSR1::FLI1 . These findings indicate that GGAA repeat sequences can act as genuine Ewing sarcoma-specific promoters and thus drive therapeutic genes exclusively in Ewing sarcoma cells.
Accordingly, we recently demonstrated that a synthetic promoter consisting of GGAA repeats and a consensus TATA box (hereafter referred to as GGAAprom) can be used to express a gene of interest specifically in Ewing sarcoma cells . In particular, we demonstrated that this strategy was extraordinarily specific in vivo since intravenous administration of an adenovirus harboring a reporter gene (luciferase) under the control of GGAAprom to mice did not result in detectable expression in any normal tissues. To evaluate the therapeutic potential of this strategy, we used GGAAprom to drive the expression of the Cas9 nuclease in combination with a guide RNA (gRNA) directed to inactivate EWSR1::FLI1 and demonstrated that it effectively blocks Ewing sarcoma cell proliferation in vitro and tumor growth in vivo via adenovirus delivery. However, although this therapeutic strategy using Cas9/gRNA has shown effectiveness in vivo, it has several drawbacks, the most important of which is that the vehicle must reach all tumor cells to be truly effective. Thus, other gene therapy strategies that can induce cell death more broadly are needed. For this reason, we explored whether a suicide gene under the control of GGAAprom could be more effective at eliminating Ewing sarcoma cells than the genetic inactivation of the EWSR1::FLI1 gene.
One of the suicide genes that can best meet this objective is the thymidine kinase gene derived from the herpes simplex virus (HSV-TK) – . HSV-TK phosphorylates the nucleotide analog ganciclovir (GCV) (and other chemically similar molecules), giving rise to ganciclovir monophosphate, which is then phosphorylated by intracellular kinases present in mammalian cells to give rise to GCV-diphosphate and GCV-triphosphate. GCV-triphosphate is a potent inhibitor of DNA polymerases and blocks DNA replication by incorporating into the nascent strand, resulting in the termination of chain elongation and ultimately triggering cell apoptosis . Because the thymidine kinases present in mammalian cells cannot phosphorylate GCV, it is only modestly toxic to mammalian cells. Thus, only cells infected with herpes simplex virus or expressing thymidine kinase from the herpes simplex virus because of targeted gene therapy are susceptible to death in response to GCV treatment. One interesting finding of this strategy is that GCV-triphosphate produced by cells expressing HSV-TK can reach neighboring cells that do not express HSV-TK and induce cell death – . This phenomenon, known as the bystander effect, allows the infection of a few cells to be sufficient to kill a larger number of noninfected cells. Therefore, this phenomenon is especially relevant in cancer gene therapy.
Strategies based on the use of HSV-TK as a suicide gene have been successfully used to kill tumor cells of different origins, such as breast cancer , glioblastoma , , colorectal cancer , prostate cancer among others, both in vitro and in vivo. More importantly, several HSV-TK-based strategies are currently available on the market, and clinical trials have shown that they are safe and have variable grades of efficacy – .
In this work, we used the Ewing-specific promoter GGAAprom to drive the specific expression of HSV-TK in Ewing sarcoma cells. We first demonstrated that Ewing sarcoma cells expressing HSV-TK are sensitive to GCV in vitro at IC50 values in the nanomolar range and that these cells exhibit potent bystander effects in vitro. More interestingly, HSV-TK therapy can be delivered to Ewing sarcoma cells via adenoviral vectors both in vitro and in vivo. In addition, in vivo experiments revealed that HSV-TK adenoviral therapy was highly effective at reducing tumor growth. Interestingly, immunohistochemistry of HSV-TK-expressing tumors treated with GCV revealed remarkable infiltration of CD45 + immune cells, which transformed typical cold tumors such as Ewing sarcoma into hot tumors. The efficacy results shown in this work, together with the high specificity of the therapy previously demonstrated, strongly justify additional preclinical research to advance toward a first-in-human clinical trial.

## Results

## Ewing sarcoma cells are sensitive to HSV-TK/GCV-mediated toxicity
To achieve EWSR1::FLI1-specific expression of the HSV-TK suicide gene in Ewing sarcoma cells, we designed lentiviral vectors harboring the SR39h mutant of HSV-TK, an optimized and humanized form of this enzyme , , downstream of the Ewing-specific promoter GGAAprom (GGAA > TK). As previously shown, this promoter displays exquisite specificity for Ewing sarcoma cells both in vitro and in vivo; thus, it is an excellent approach to drive gene therapies for Ewing sarcoma . In addition, we designed a lentiviral vector harboring HSV-TK (SR39h) under the control of the constitutive EF1A promoter as a positive control (EF1A > TK). The constitutive expression of EGFP was also included as a control for lentiviral transduction. We first infected A673 and MHH-ES1 Ewing sarcoma cells with both vectors and analyzed the expression of HSV-TK with an anti-HA antibody. As shown in Fig.  a, HSV-TK(SR39h) protein expression was detected in Ewing sarcoma cell lines transduced with the EF1A > TK (positive control) and GGAA > TK (Ewing-specific promoter) lentiviral vectors. The expression of the EGFP reporter gene was similar in both established cell lines (Supplementary Figure ).
 Next, we performed a combined time-course and dose escalation experiment to determine the effect of ganciclovir (GCV) treatment on Ewing cell lines stably expressing HSV-TK(SR39h). As shown in Supplementary Figure S3, the effect of GCV on cell growth was detected as soon as 48 h and was maximal at 120–144 h of treatment in Ewing sarcoma cells transduced with the EF1A > TK or GGAA > TK lentivirus. No toxic effect of GCV was observed on parental cells at any tested concentration. To obtain more accurate IC50 values, we performed resazurin growth assays with each cell line (Fig.  b). As expected, both EF1A > TK and GGAA > TK Ewing sarcoma cells were susceptible to GCV-mediated cell death, with IC50 values for ganciclovir in the nM range (GGAA > TK: A673 IC50 = 24 nM (95% CI: 7 to 84 nM); MHH-ES1 IC50 = 54 nM (95% CI 46 to 63 nM)).

## HSV-TK/GCV-mediated bystander effect in Ewing sarcoma cells
One of the main advantages of using HSV-TK-based suicide gene therapies is that the toxic metabolite derived from GCV can reach cells that do not express HSV-TK and kill them. This characteristic is known as the bystander effect. To determine and quantify the bystander effect in Ewing sarcoma cells, we cocultured cells expressing HSV-TK under GGAAprom with cells expressing a luciferase reporter gene at various ratios. As shown in Fig.  a, a bystander effect was clearly observed in A673 and MHH-ES1 Ewing sarcoma cell lines. For example, in both cell lines, 6% of HSV-TK-expressing cells were able to kill approximately 20% of the cells that did not express HSV-TK. Figure  b shows representative images of MHH-ES1 cells expressing HSV-TK and EGFP cocultured with parental MHH-ES1 cells expressing mCherry taken at different times after GCV treatment. Representative videos of these experiments can be found in the supplementary material (Supplementary Video V1).
 

## HSV-TK/GCV therapy reduces tumor growth and induces immune cell infiltration in vivo
Next, we analyzed the effects of HSV-TK expression and GCV treatment in vivo. Athymic nude mice were injected s.c. with A673 GGAA > TK cells, and when the tumors reached a mean volume of approximately 200 mm3, the animals were divided into two groups: a control group and a treatment group in which valganciclovir (VGCV) was added to the drinking water (Fig.  a). VGCV was used in in vivo experiments instead of ganciclovir because of its excellent solubility in water and bioavailability after being processed to ganciclovir by hepatic enzymes. As shown in Fig.  b and c, VGCV treatment dramatically reduced the growth of well-established tumors. Immunohistochemistry analysis of the tumor tissues revealed several interesting findings (Fig.  d). While the control group displayed the typical macro- and microscopic aspects of classical Ewing sarcoma (that is, homogeneous sheets of compacted round cells), the treated group presented few, if any, Ewing sarcoma cells and a notable accumulation of apparently non-Ewing sarcoma cells. We hypothesized that these cells could be of immune origin, as athymic nude mice have functional B lymphocyte, monocytes and neutrophils but not functional T cells. Interestingly, the tumors were massively infiltrated by cells strongly positive for the pan-leukocyte marker CD45, confirming their immune origin. No CD45 + cells were observed in the tumor bulk of the control group, which is consistent with the low immune burden described for Ewing sarcoma tumors. Interestingly, the accumulation of some CD45 + cells was observed in the periphery of the untreated tumors, “waiting to enter” into the tumor.
 

## Ad-GGAA > TK plus GCV induces cell death in Ewing sarcoma cells but not in non-Ewing sarcoma cells
The generation of cell models using transduction with lentiviral vectors was useful for demonstrating that the strategy based on the expression of HSV-TK under the control of the Ewing specific promoter was efficient at killing Ewing sarcoma cells. However, the use of a lentivirus for gene therapy in vivo is not suitable for clinical development because the lentivirus genome is inserted into host cells, among other drawbacks. Thus, we explored the use of adenovirus as a suitable alternative for delivering gene therapy to Ewing sarcoma cells. Although the use of adenoviruses as a vehicle for gene therapy has several disadvantages (i.e., extensive liver clearance, high immunogenicity and others), adenoviruses are still among the most commonly used viral vectors in cancer gene therapy, particularly for localized treatments , . Furthermore, we and others have recently demonstrated that Ewing sarcoma cells can be efficiently infected by adenovirus , and that adenovirus can be used to express a protein of interest (for example, the reporter gene luciferase or the therapeutic gene Cas9) upon the control of the Ewing sarcoma-specific promoter .
Thus, we analyzed the effects of the adenovirus Ad-GGAA > TK on Ewing sarcoma cells (A673, SK-N-MC, MHH-ES1, SK-ES1 and A4573) and non-Ewing sarcoma cells (HT1080, fibrosarcoma; Saos-2, U2-OS, osteosarcoma). The Ewing sarcoma cells selected represent the three most common EWSR1::FLI1 fusions observed in Ewing sarcoma tumors; in this way, we were able to analyze how Ewing sarcoma cells with different variants could be affected by HSV-TK/GCV treatment. The cells were then infected with Ad-GGAA > TK at an MOI of 25 and incubated without or with GCV (10 µM) for three or six days. Preliminary experiments showed that higher MOIs (i.e., 100 and 400) had nonspecific cytotoxic effects on the cells (data not shown); thus, we used a concentration at which no toxic effects were observed in the absence of GCV. In addition, the same cells were infected with adenovirus harboring a GGAA > ORF Stuffer cassette with an irrelevant open reading frame sequence upon the control of the GGAA promoter, which was used as a negative control. As shown in Fig.  a, Ewing sarcoma cells infected with Ad-GGAA > TK and incubated with GCV exhibited a severe reduction in cell viability. No effect was observed in control cells not treated with GCV (vehicle, DMSO) or in cells infected with Ad- GGAA > ORF. Notably, more than 90% of Ewing sarcoma cells infected at an MOI of 25 were killed when incubated concomitantly with GCV. In contrast, no effects on cell viability were observed in non-Ewing sarcoma cells (HT-1080, Saos-2, or U2-OS) despite whether they were infected with GGAA > TK or GGAA > ORF or treated or not with GCV. Figure  b shows representative micrographs of Ewing and non-Ewing sarcoma cells after 6 days of HSV-TK/GCV treatment, revealing the severe cell death observed in Ewing sarcoma cells upon treatment.
 

## The combination of Ad-GGAA > TK and VGCV reduces tumor growth and induces immune cell infiltration in vivo
Next, we analyzed the effect of Ad-GGAA > TK/GCV on in vivo tumor growth. Nude mice were subcutaneously injected with A673, and when the tumors reached a mean tumor volume of approximately 200 mm3, the mice were randomly distributed into three groups (Fig.  a): (1) A control group in which tumors were treated with PBS; (2) A control group in which tumors were treated with a control adenovirus carrying an irrelevant protein fragment under the control of the GGAA promoter (Ad-GGAA > ORF, see Figure Supplementary for details); and (3) A group treated with the therapeutic adenovirus Ad-GGAA > TK. In all cases, PBS and adenoviruses were administered intratumorally. All groups received VGCV dissolved in drinking water. Figure  b shows the evolution of tumor volume normalized with respect to the start of treatment for each group and for each animal. There is a significant reduction in tumor size in the Ad-GGAA > TK treated group (mean ± s.e.m. all animals, 194.5 ± 79.2 mm3 compared to control groups treated with the Ad-GGAA > ORF control adenovirus (630.0 ± 162.3 mm3 or PBS (911.8 ± 227.1 mm3. Although there was an apparent reduction in the Ad-GGAA > ORF control group compared to the PBS control group, this was not statistically significant (2-way ANOVA). Figure  c confirms the differences in tumor volume observed at the end of the experiment. Histological and immunohistochemical analyses confirmed the massive loss of tumor cells in tumor treated with Ad-GGAA > TK (Fig.  d). Interestingly, Ad-GGAA > TK/VGCV-treated tumors were massively infiltrated by CD45 + immune cells surrounding the few remaining nests of tumor cells at the end of the experiment. However, no CD45 + cells were detected in the tumor bulk of tumors from both control groups, with only some “ready to enter” CD45 + cells observed at the periphery of the tumors. This result further confirms the specificity of Ad-GGAA > TK in its ability to reduce and even stop tumor growth and that this ability is dependent on TK expression. Comparable results were obtained in a similar experiment using MHH-ES1 Ewing sarcoma cells (Figure S4 Supplementary).
 

## Discussion
Ewing sarcoma is a rare, aggressive childhood and adolescent tumor with a poor prognosis. Approximately 30% of children and young adults diagnosed with this type of tumor will succumb to the disease, and the situation is especially dramatic in patients with disseminated disease at the time of diagnosis or with recurrent disease, for whom survival rates are less than 20%. It is therefore clear that it is necessary to move toward the development of new therapies for these groups of patients with very poor prognosis, since current therapies, based on chemotherapy, radiotherapy, and surgery, have likely reached their maximum possible effectiveness.
Ewing sarcoma has molecular characteristics that make it truly unique and allow it to be differentiated from other types of sarcomas and other tumors in general. The exclusive molecular characteristic of these tumors is the occurrence of chromosomal translocations that give rise to chimeric transcription factors formed by the EWSR1 gene and the FLI1 transcription factor. The second unique characteristic of these tumors is the way in which EWSR1::FLI1 regulates the expression of its target genes. Thus, EWSR1::FLI1 binds to regions in the DNA formed by concatenated repeats of GGAA sequences known as microsatellites. EWSR1::FLI1 binds to these regions, forming aggregates and recruiting the gene transcription machinery, ultimately leading to the induction of the expression of the corresponding target gene. One of the most interesting findings is that these regions are not transactivated by the native FLI1 protein, despite sharing the same DNA binding domain. Therefore, the genes that are activated by EWSR1::FLI1 are not activated by native FLI1. Consequently, the sequences formed by GGAA repeats are actually EWSR1::FLI1-specific response elements.
Given the specificity of EWSR1::FLI1 for these sequences, it is possible to use them to express therapeutic genes specifically in Ewing sarcoma. We recently used this unique property of EWSR1::FLI1 to drive the expression of the Cas9 nuclease in Ewing sarcoma cells to induce genetic inactivation of the EWSR1::FLI1 gene. Thus, using a promoter formed by GGAA repeats and a consensus TATA box, Cas9 can be expressed in Ewing sarcoma cells (A673, MHH-ES1, A4573) but not in non-Ewing sarcoma cells (HT1080, U2-OS and Saos-2). Even more interesting, intravenous injection of adenoviruses carrying a luciferase gene under the control of the Ewing-specific promoter did not detect any expression in the 12 tissues analyzed, demonstrating the high specificity of this promoter . However, while the genetic inactivation of EWSR1::FLI1 using the CRISPR/Cas9 system has been shown to be effective both in vitro and in vivo, this approach has several drawbacks. This type of gene therapy, which relies on EWSR1::FLI1 gene inactivation, should effectively reach all tumor cells, as any cells escaping the therapy could continue to proliferate and reestablish the tumor. Since no delivery system can guarantee that all tumor cells are reached, alternative strategies are needed to locally amplify the toxic effects of the therapy.
One of the strategies that can be used to amplify the toxic effect of a therapy at the local level is the use of systems formed by suicide genes and prodrugs. These systems consist of an enzyme that is capable of processing a nontoxic prodrug into a toxic metabolite that induces cell death. One of the most commonly used systems is the one involving the thymidine kinase of the herpes simplex virus and the prodrug ganciclovir (HSV-TK/GCV). One of the advantages of this system is that it has been widely used in clinical settings, demonstrating that it is a safe system. Therefore, we used this strategy to express the HSV-TK gene specifically in Ewing sarcoma cells and then induce tumor cell death after GCV administration.
First, we generated two Ewing sarcoma cell lines (A673 and MHH-ES1) constitutively expressing of HSV-TK to confirm that HSV-TK could be efficiently expressed in Ewing sarcoma cells downstream of the GGAA promoter. This approach allowed us to confirm the expression and calculate the sensitivity of the cells to ganciclovir-mediated killing at concentrations in the nanomolar range. A key aspect of HSV-TK/GCV therapy is the ability of toxic metabolites to spread to adjacent non transduced cells. These cells were also instrumental in demonstrating the bystander effect mediated by the production of the toxic metabolite. In our study, 6% of the cells expressing HSV-TK were able to kill 20% of the parental cells within just 72 h of treatment. We observed quantitative differences between the two Ewing cell lines analyzed (A673 and MHH-ES1), with MHH-ES1 cells apparently being less sensitive to the bystander effect than A673 cells; however, in both cases, the bystander effect was evident (Fig.  and Supplementary Video V1). The bystander effect of HSV-TK/GCV therapies appears to be associated with the presence of gap junctions between cells, through which the toxic metabolites are transported , . Interestingly, Ewing sarcoma cell lines express high to moderate levels of connexin 43 (depmap portal, https://depmap.org/portal/), a major component of gap functions whose expression has been experimentally associated with the bystander effect .
Another notable feature of our approach is its high therapeutic index, i.e., the concentration of drug required to kill a tumor cell compared with that needed to kill a normal cell. We have shown that this therapy is exquisitely specific for killing Ewing sarcoma cells but has no effect on non-Ewing sarcoma cells, such as HT1080 (fibrosarcoma), Saos-2 and U2OS (osteosarcoma) cells. The lack of effect on HT1080 fibrosarcoma cells is particularly relevant because these cells express the native FLI1 protein, which further supports the specificity of the therapy . Furthermore, Ad-GGAA > TK/GCV treatment efficiently promoted the death of Ewing sarcoma cells harboring three different types of EWSR1::FLI1 fusions, which represent more than 85% of the tumors. Given the functional similarity among fusion types, the Ad-GGAA > TK/GCV approach may be effective for treating all Ewing sarcomas, regardless of the type of fusion.
We used direct intratumoral administration of Ad-GGAA > TK to deliver the therapy to the tumors. Currently, the preferred route of administration for the delivery of therapeutic viruses remains intratumoral. In fact, 80% of sarcoma patients treated with oncolytic virotherapy receive therapy through intratumoral administration . In addition to the massive killing of Ewing sarcoma cells upon treatment with Ad-GGAA-TK/GCV in tumor xenografts, we also observed intense infiltration of immune cells expressing the pan-leucocyte marker CD45, even though we used an immunodeficient mouse model with impaired cytotoxic T lymphocyte function. The role of the immune system in the effectiveness of these therapies is critical, as evidenced by the increased efficacy of these therapies in immune-competent models compared with immune-deficient models in early studies , . The death of tumor cells can liberate tumor antigens, leading to local inflammation and immune-mediated death of nontransduced cells, contributing to the success of therapy in vivo. In this sense, a significant increase in T cells has been reported upon treatment of tumors with HSV-TK/GCV, both in syngeneic animal tumors (for example, esophageal carcinoma, glioblastoma, and hepatocellular carcinoma models) – and in human tumors (for example, pancreatic cancer, malignant glioma, and prostate cancer) – . Thus, treatment with HSV-TK/GCV can convert so-called cold tumor microenvironments into immune-active or hot microenvironments. This can be particularly important in the case of “cold” Ewing sarcoma, in which immunotherapies have proven to be ineffective – . As has been recently shown, this difference seems to be due to the repressive effect that Ewing sarcoma cells exert on the immune system itself, limiting the capacity for infiltration and activation of immune effector cells , . Since it has not been possible to develop syngeneic models of Ewing sarcoma, the only way to study the role of the immune system in HSV-TK/GCV therapy in Ewing sarcoma will necessarily involve the use of humanized mouse models , . We anticipate that when this therapy is administered to Ewing sarcoma tumors implanted in mice with a reconstituted human immune system, the antitumor effect of Ad-GGAA > TK/GCV will be even greater. Interestingly, many studies have demonstrated that although HSV-TK therapy is administered intratumorally, it is able to activate the immune system systemically. For example, intratumoral administration of HSV-TK/GCV therapies protected animals from tumor rechallenge, resulting in durable memory antitumor immunity . It will be of utmost importance to demonstrate whether local administration of Ad-GGAA > TK is capable of activating the immune system systemically and contributing to the elimination of disseminated disease in humanized mouse models.
Interestingly, some studies have demonstrated that the combination of HSV-TK/GCV therapy with immunotherapy, such as anti-PD-L1 therapy, in syngeneic models of glioblastoma improves long-term survival . HSV-TK/GCV has also been used in combination with radiotherapy or chemotherapy , . These studies open the door to establishing combined therapies for Ewing sarcoma that could combine HSV-TK/GCV therapy with chemotherapy, radiotherapy, or immunotherapy, contributing to improving patient outcomes.
Another advantage of HSV-TK/GCV therapeutic approaches is that they have good safety profiles and encouraging efficacy results. Many phase I/II clinical trials have been carried out in glioma , , , , lung adenocarcinoma , malignant pleural effusion , pancreatic adenocarcinoma , prostate adenocarcinoma , , or ovarian cancer patients. Accordingly, most adverse events attributed to viruses in more than 3 000 patients across 20 years were local reactions associated with the injection sites and low-grade adverse effects .
Recently, Hölting and coworkers described a lentiviral-based strategy for delivering HSV-TK to Ewing sarcoma cells . Lentivirus-based in vivo therapies have serious drawbacks due to their ability to integrate into the genome; therefore, lentivirus-based therapy has to overcome many important problems in the clinic. In contrast, we have used a strategy based on the use of adenoviruses to deliver therapy to tumor cells, which, as mentioned above, is a widely used vehicle in clinical settings with solid safety data from clinical trials and, therefore, with direct transfer to the clinic.
In summary, in this work, we report a gene therapy approach in which the suicide gene HSV-TK is expressed in Ewing sarcoma cells in a highly specific way using an Ewing-specific promoter. Intratumoral administration of this therapy (Ad-GGAA > TK) in combination with VGCV dramatically reduced tumor growth in vivo and, more interestingly, promoted massive immune cell infiltration into the tumor. To our knowledge, no other HSV-TK/GCV adenovirus-based approach has been shown to be effective in reducing tumor growth in preclinical models. We believe the results shown here support the use of this strategy to treat Ewing sarcoma and justify continuing its preclinical development.

## Methods

## Cell lines
The Ewing sarcoma cell line A673 (CRL-1598), the fibrosarcoma cell line HT1080 (CCL-121) and the osteosarcoma cell lines U2-OS (HTB-96) and Saos-2 (HTB-85) were purchased from the American Type Culture Collection (Manassas, VA, USA). The Ewing sarcoma cell lines MHH-ES1 (ACC 167), SK-ES-1 (ACC 518) and SK-N-MC (ACC 203) were purchased from the DSMZ-German Collection of Microorganisms and Cell Cultures (Braunschweig, Germany). The Ewing sarcoma cell line A4573 was generously provided by Dr. S. Navarro (University of Valencia, Spain). The A673 and HT1080 cell lines were maintained in DMEM; the SK-N-MC cell line was maintained in DMEM supplemented with 1x MEM nonessential amino acids. The MHH-ES1, A4573, and U2-OS cell lines were maintained in RPMI 1640, and the Saos-2 and SK-ES-1 cell lines were maintained in McCoy’s medium. All media were supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum, 50 U/mL penicillin and 50 µg/mL streptomycin (Corning, Glendale, AZ, USA). The cell lines were checked via short tandem repeats (STR) profiling and periodically tested for mycoplasma contamination (Mycoalert mycoplasma detection kit, Lonza, Rockland, ME, USA).

## Establishment of cell lines expressing HSV-TK(SR39h) under the control of the GGAA promoter
The Ewing sarcoma cell lines A673 and MHH-ES1 were infected with a lentivirus encoding the herpes simplex virus 1 (HSV) thymidine kinase (TK) SR39h mutant , , downstream of an Ewing-specific promoter (GGAAprom) or an EF1A constitutive promoter. To improve HSV-TK(SR39h) protein detection by western-blotting, an HA tag was added at the C-terminus. An EGFP: T2A: Bsd cassette under the control of the CMV promoter was also incorporated into the plasmids for cell selection and monitoring of transfection (Supplementary Figure ). Lentiviral plasmids and lentivirus particles were produced by Vector Builder (Chicago, IL, USA). The cells were infected at an MOI of 0.5 and selected with blasticidin (50 µg/mL). To confirm similar levels of lentiviral transduction, the percentage and intensity of GFP-positive cells were quantified with flow cytometry with a MACS Quant Analyzer flow cytometer (Miltenyi Biotec, Cologne, Germany) and FlowJo™ software (v.10.7.1; De Novo Software, Pasadena, CA, USA).

## Western blot
Western blotting was carried out as described previously . The primary antibodies used and their dilutions were as follows: anti-HA-Tag, 1:250 (#26183, Invitrogen, Basel, Switzerland); anti-FLI1, 1:500 (#133485, Abcam); and anti-β tubulin, HRP-conjugated, 1:10 000 (#ab185057, Abcam). Anti-mouse IgG-HRP (#sc-516102) and anti-rabbit IgG-HRP (#sc-2357) (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Dallas, TX, USA) were used as secondary antibodies.

## GCV sensitivity of Ewing sarcoma cells expressing HSV-TK(SR39h) in vitro
Cells stably transduced with the lentiviral vectors LV-GGAAprom > HSV-TK(SR39h), LV-EF1A > HSV-TK(SR39h) or parental control cells were seeded into 96-well plates at a density of 1 000–3 000 cells/well. Ganciclovir (GCV) (#SML2346; Sigma‒Aldrich, Saint Louis, MO, USA) was added at the indicated concentrations 24- and 96-hours postseeding. After six days, cell viability was quantified via a resazurin metabolic assay. Briefly, 20 µL of resazurin solution (15 mg/mL) (#R7017, Sigma‒Aldrich) was added to each well, and the plate was incubated for 5 h at 37 °C. Fluorescence was measured in an Infinite M200 microplate reader (Tecan, Männedorf, Switzerland) using a filter set of 560-nm excitation/590-nm emission. Viability was expressed as the percentage of viable cells vs. control cells (treated with the DMSO vehicle).
In other experiments, continuous monitoring of cell viability was performed using a live-cell analysis system. Briefly, cells were seeded onto 96-well plates at a density of 3 000 cells/well, and cell growth was monitored for six days in an IncuCyte SX5 live-cell imaging device (Sartorius, Göttingen, Germany). Five images of each well were recorded every 6 h for a total of 144 h. After 24 and 96 h, the cells were treated with various concentrations of GCV or 0.1% DMSO (vehicle). The percentage of confluent cells at each time point was quantified using IncuCyte 2022B Rev2 software and compared with that of control cells.

## Analysis of the bystander effect in vitro
A673 and MHH-ES1 Ewing sarcoma cells stably transduced with the lentiviral vector LV-GGAAprom > HSV-TK(SR39h)/EGFP were cocultured at different ratios with A673 cells stably expressing the reporter genes luciferase and EGFP (A673/LUC/EGFP) or with MHH-ES1 cells stably expressing luciferase and mCherry (MHH-ES1/LUC/mCherry). The next day, 10 µM GCV was added to each well, and after 72 h, luciferase activity was quantified via the Bright-Glo™ Luciferase Assay System (#E2620, Promega Corp., Fitchburg, WI, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, 100 µL of Bright-Glo™ reagent was added directly to each well containing 100 µL of culture cell media and incubated for 10 min at room temperature with agitation. Luciferase activity was measured with an Infinite M200 microplate reader (Tecan).
In other experiments, MHH-ES1/GGAAprom > HSV-TK(SR39h)/EGFP and MHH-ES1/LUC/mCherry were cocultured (3 000 cells each) in µ-slide 8-well plates (80807, Ibidi, Gräfelfing, Germany) in 300 µL of RPMI without phenol red (#BE12-918 F, Lonza, Basel, Switzerland) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and 2 mM glutamine. The next day, GCV (10 µM) or vehicle (0.1% DMS0) was added to the corresponding wells. The cells were monitored for up to 93 h with a Thunder imaging system (Leica, Wetzlar, Germany) set to an excitation wavelength of 475 nm (GFP) or 555 nm (mCherry) and emission filter ranges of 506–532 nm and 578–610 nm, respectively. The cells were imaged at 2-hour intervals. The images were analyzed via ImageJ software (v 1.52, Wayne Rasband, National Institute of Health, USA) to make a video, and then iMovie software (v 10.4.3, Apple Inc., USA) was used to make a composite video.

## Sensitivity to the HSV-TK(SR39h) adenovirus (therapeutic adenovirus) in vitro
The adenoviral vectors (serotype 5, replication defective) Ad-GGAAprom > HSV-TK(SR39h) and Ad-GGAAprom > ORF Stuffer (which harbor an irrelevant ORF) were synthesized via Vector Builder. Adenoviruses were produced by the Viral Production Unit (VPU) of the Universitat Autonoma de Barcelona (UAB). The cells were seeded in triplicate in 96-well plates (6 000 and 3 000 cells per well for 3 and 6 days, respectively) and infected the next day with the aforementioned adenovirus at an MOI of 25. GCV (10 µM) or 0.1% DMSO (control) was added to the corresponding wells. In the experiments performed at 6 days, GCV and DMSO were added once again after 3 days. Cell viability was quantified using a resazurin metabolic assay and is expressed as a percentage of viable cells relative to control cells (noninfected cells).

## Effect of HSV-TK(SR39h)/GCV treatment on Ewing sarcoma tumor growth in vivo
All the experiments involving animals were performed in accordance with animal welfare guidelines and were approved by the Animal Experimentation Ethics Committee of the Instituto de Salud Carlos III (CBA 25_2020 and CBA 24_2022) and the Agriculture Department of the Madrid Region (PROEX 253.8/20 and PROEX 302.3/22). Animal studies are reported in accordance with ARRIVE guidelines.
To evaluate the effect of GCV treatment on Ewing sarcoma tumors stably expressing HSV-TK(SR39h), 8- to 10-week-old athymic female nude mice (CR ATH HO strain, Charles River, France) were inoculated subcutaneously with 5 × 106 A673/GGAAprom > HSV-TK(SR39h) cells, and when the tumors reached a volume of approximately 200 mm3, the animals were randomly distributed into two groups. In the treatment group (n = 8), valganciclovir (VGCV) (#S4050 Selleckchem, Cologne, Germany) was administered in the drinking water at a concentration of 10 mg/mL supplemented with 5% sucrose. The control animals (n = 6) received drinking water supplemented with 5% sucrose. The drinking water containing the corresponding drug was replaced every 48–72 h.
To evaluate the effects of the therapeutic adenovirus, the mice were inoculated subcutaneously with 5 × 106 A673 or MHH-ES1 Ewing sarcoma cells, and when the tumors reached a volume of approximately 200 mm3, they were randomly distributed into two groups. The animals in the treatment group were treated with intratumoral injections of Ad-GGAAprom > HSV-TK(SR39h) (2 × 1010 VPs in 100 µL) at 48–72-hour intervals (4 doses) (A673, n = 7; MHH-ES1, n = 5). The control animals received PBS (100 µL) at the same time points (A673, n = 7; MHH-ES1, n = 5). In the experiments performed in A673 xenografts, an additional group consisting of tumors treated with intratumoral injection of an adenovirus expressing an irrelevant protein under the control of GGAA promoter (Ad-GGAAprom > ORF Stuffer, Figure Supplementary) was also included as an additional control (n = 6). All groups received VGCV in the drinking water as indicated above, starting the day after initiating intratumoral adenovirus treatment.
To distribute the animals equally between the different groups and avoid possible biases, the animals were first ordered from largest to smallest tumor volume and then randomly distributed to one group or the other. Tumor growth was monitored every 2‒3 days with a caliper, and the tumor volume was calculated as V=((length × width2) × π)/6. At the end of the experiment, the animals were sacrificed via carbon dioxide inhalation. Tumors were excised, fixed in formalin and embedded in paraffin for histological and immunohistochemical analysis.

## Immunohistochemistry
Tissue section (3 μm) from paraffin-embedded tumors were deparaffinized, treated with HIER2 buffer for antigen retrieval (Leica Biosystems, Nussloch, Germany), and stained with an anti-mouse CD45 antibody (clone 30-F11) (dil 1/100, #14-0451-82; Invitrogen, Waltham, MA, USA). After DAB development, the samples were counterstained with hematoxylin. All steps were performed in a Bond RX automatic stainer (Leica Biosystems). Stained tissues were scanned at 40x in a NanoZoomer-SQ digital slide scanner (Hamamatsu, Shizuoka, Japan).

## Statistical analysis
The number of independent experiments performed for each experiment is indicated in the corresponding figure legend. A minimum of five animals per group were used, and the exact number used per group is also indicated in the corresponding figure legend. Significant differences between means were calculated with one-way or two-way ANOVA or Student’s t-test, as appropriate. All the statistical tests were two-sided and adjusted for multiple comparisons as appropriate. All the statistical analyses were carried out with GraphPad software, version 9. P values < 0.05 were considered to indicate statistical significance.
