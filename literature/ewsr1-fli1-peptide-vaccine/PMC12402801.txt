## Abstract

## Purpose:
Ewing sarcoma, in contrast to some adult sarcoma subtypes, generally does not respond to single-agent immunotherapy targeting PD1. The features of Ewing sarcoma that preclude the effectiveness of immunotherapy remain largely unknown. To address this question, we utilized biopsies from patients with Ewing sarcoma obtained before and after pembrolizumab (anti-PD1) therapy from the phase II clinical trial SARC028 to interrogate the Ewing tumor microenvironment and features associated with resistance to checkpoint inhibition.

## Experimental Design:
We utilized multiplexed immunofluorescence, spatial proteomics, and spatial transcriptomics to analyze paired pretreatment and 8-week posttreatment biopsy specimens from patients with Ewing sarcoma enrolled in SARC028.

## Results:
Pembrolizumab therapy did not alter the quantity of immune cell infiltration in Ewing tumor biopsies. Analysis of tumor-associated protein markers revealed increased immunoregulatory markers after pembrolizumab. Spatial transcriptomics identified 10 cellular neighborhoods (CN) across patients consisting of specific cell subsets. CN10 was consistently observed across patients with a poor response. This CN was enriched for a tumor subpopulation with a high TGF-β response, low IFN response, and low HLA class I expression. IFN response, HLA class I expression, and overall immune infiltration were correlated.

## Conclusions:
Analyses of paired Ewing sarcoma tumor samples from SARC028 reveal an immunosuppressive triad, the disruption of which should be pursued to improve antitumor immunity. This work highlights the unique insight that can be gained from the analysis of paired patient Ewing sarcoma tumor biopsy samples from clinical trials.

## Translational Relevance
Single-agent checkpoint inhibitors have demonstrated limited clinical benefit in bone sarcomas, including Ewing sarcoma. Although in vitro and limited patient tumor analyses have suggested mechanisms of immunotherapy resistance in Ewing sarcoma, tumor biopsies following checkpoint inhibitor administration have not been examined. SARC028 was a phase II trial investigating the blockade of PD1 in patients with relapsed/refractory sarcomas, including Ewing sarcoma. Tumor biopsies were collected before and 8 weeks after anti-PD1 therapy. In this study, we leverage a unique opportunity to understand Ewing sarcoma immunotherapy resistance through the analysis of paired tumor samples from SARC028. Using a multiomics approach, we identify features of Ewing sarcoma that contribute to primary resistance to anti-PD1 therapy, including the presence of TGF-β signaling, reduced IFN signaling, and low HLA class I expression. This study provides an improved understanding of tumor characteristics driving poor response to immunotherapy in relapsed Ewing sarcoma and will guide the development of future trials to modulate this immunosuppressive axis.

## Introduction
Ewing sarcoma is an aggressive primary bone tumor that primarily occurs in adolescents and young adults ( ). Ewing sarcoma is molecularly characterized by a translocation between members of the FET and ETS families of proteins, most commonly EWS::FLI1 or EWS::ERG ( ). Patients with localized Ewing sarcoma have a 5-year overall survival of more than 80%, based on the most recent cooperative group trials ( ). However, patients with metastatic or relapsed Ewing sarcoma continue to have very poor outcomes (<30% survival rate) despite clinical trials testing various chemotherapy combinations, surgery, and/or radiotherapy ( ). Outcomes for patients with upfront metastatic or relapsed Ewing sarcoma have not improved in decades, and novel strategies and approaches for the treatment of these patients are urgently needed.
Ewing tumor immunobiology and the effect of immunomodulatory therapies on the Ewing sarcoma tumor microenvironment (TME) remain relatively unstudied due to the low incidence of Ewing sarcoma and the corresponding paucity of patient tumor samples. Despite these challenges, it has recently been shown that the prevalence of immune subpopulations in the Ewing sarcoma TME may be predictive of survival ( ). Our group previously interrogated immune infiltrates in Ewing sarcoma at baseline and at the time of disease relapse ( ). We demonstrated that Ewing sarcoma immune cell infiltration in relapsed tumors is higher compared with immune cell infiltration at initial diagnosis, and intracellular communication analyses identified CD14+CD16+ macrophages as drivers of immune infiltration ( ). This suggests a potential role for immunomodulation in the treatment of Ewing sarcoma. In fact, a current trial investigating the effect of priming NK cells to resist the immunosuppressive cytokine TGF-β in Ewing sarcoma is ongoing (NCT05634369; refs. , ). Further preclinical work is needed to identify and prioritize new immunotherapeutic strategies to disrupt the immunosuppressive TME of relapsed Ewing sarcoma.
Immunomodulatory agents have been widely tested in clinical trials across many cancer types ( , ). SARC028 (NCT02301039) was a phase II clinical trial of patients 16 years and older investigating the use of single-agent pembrolizumab, a PD1 checkpoint inhibitor, in relapsed/recurrent sarcomas ( ), including a cohort of patients with bone sarcomas. Previous quantitative analysis of immune cell populations and PD1/PDL1 expression identified that the density of tumor-associated immune cells was predictive of objective response (by RECIST criteria) across sarcoma subtypes that achieved an objective response ( ). In undifferentiated pleomorphic sarcoma, dedifferentiated liposarcoma, and leiomyosarcoma, a sarcoma immune classification has been developed and is predictive of response to pembrolizumab therapy ( ). However, most sarcoma subtypes included in SARC028 demonstrated no objective response to single-agent pembrolizumab therapy, including patients with relapsed Ewing sarcoma. Given the varied response across tumors to checkpoint inhibition, there is a clear need to better understand the mechanisms underlying either response or resistance to immunotherapy ( , ).
SARC028 included 13 patients with relapsed/refractory Ewing sarcoma. Importantly, patients on this trial consented to undergo tumor biopsy both before and after (8 weeks ± 1 week) treatment. On-therapy tumor biopsy specimens such as these are a rare and invaluable tool to improve our understanding of the Ewing sarcoma TME. In this study, we performed multiplex immunofluorescence imaging, spatial proteomics, and spatial transcriptomics on Ewing sarcoma biopsy specimens obtained both before and after pembrolizumab therapy on the SARC028 clinical trial to investigate the longitudinal effect of checkpoint inhibition on the Ewing sarcoma TME. We found that treatment with anti-PD1 led to upregulation of immunoregulatory proteins after treatment, suggesting a pharmacodynamic effect and demonstrating that anti-PD1 therapy is not inert in Ewing sarcoma. We also identify distinct spatially resolved cellular neighborhoods (CN), one of which is consistently found across patients with progressive disease. This immunosuppressed CN harbored a subpopulation of tumor cells correlated with reduced immune cell infiltration and demonstrated low levels of IFN (type I and II) signaling, low levels of human leukocyte antigen class I expression, and enhanced TGF-β signaling. Overall, we have identified an immunosuppressive triad in relapsed Ewing sarcoma that is associated with primary resistance to anti-PD1 therapy. This finding highlights potential combinatorial therapeutic opportunities worthy of future preclinical investigation to modulate this immunosuppressive triad in Ewing sarcoma. In addition, given the small number of patients with Ewing sarcoma enrolled in SARC028, this immunosuppressive triad should be further investigated in future clinical trials.

## Materials and Methods

## Patient demographics and sample accrual
Institutional Review Board approval was obtained from the University of Pittsburgh (STUDY23040147), and a data use agreement was established with the Sarcoma Alliance for Research through Collaboration (DUA00004275). The SARC028 clinical trial (NCT02301039) was a phase II nonrandomized trial to assess the safety and efficacy of pembrolizumab (anti-PD1 antibody) monotherapy in patients with advanced sarcomas. All participants gave written informed consent based on the Declaration of Helsinki principles. Tumor biopsies were obtained before starting therapy and after 8 weeks (±1 week) on therapy. Pembrolizumab (200 mg IV) was administered every 3 weeks. Thirteen patients diagnosed with Ewing sarcoma were enrolled in the trial. Additional optional biopsies were permitted at the time of disease progression; no patients with Ewing sarcoma submitted additional optional biopsy material.

## Generation of tissue microarray
A tissue microarray (TMA) was constructed by the University of Pittsburgh Health Sciences Core Research Facility Pitt Biospecimen Core by expert research histologists utilizing a Beecher MTA-1 Manual Tissue Arrayer (Estigen), with a 1-mm-diameter coring needle. The TMA was designed with 37 cores of deidentified paraffin-embedded tissues placed into a new blank paraffin block. This included 21 pretreatment core specimens from 12 patients and 16 posttreatment core specimens from nine patients. In general, the biopsy specimens obtained were very small, single cores. To maximize available tissue for analysis on the TMA, more than one core punch was included from patient samples with sufficient material. Imperfections in the TMA, such as tears or artifacts, were rare. Following construction, the TMA block was incubated overnight at 40°C to ensure adequate adherence of the cores. The TMA was then sectioned utilizing a standard histology microtome, following a 4-μm histology sectioning protocol.

## Multiplexed immunofluorescence staining of tumor specimens and image acquisition
Multiplexed immunofluorescence analysis was performed as described previously ( ). Briefly, slide-mounted formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded tumor tissue was deparaffinized. AR6 (Akoya Biosciences, catalog No. AR600250ML) was used for heat-induced antigen retrieval in serial cycles to stain tissues for CD68 (1:800, Cell Signaling Technology, catalog No. 76437, RRID:AB_2799882) and FOXP3 (1:250, Cell Signaling Technology, catalog No. 12653, RRID:AB_2797979), and AR9 (Akoya Biosciences AR900250ML) was used for heat-induced antigen retrieval in serial cycles to stain tissues for CD4 (prediluted, Biocare Medical LLC, catalog No. API3209AA), CD8 (1:200, Biocare Medical LLC, catalog No. ACI3160A), and CD20 (1:200, Leica Biosystems, catalog No. NCL-L-CD20-L26, RRID:AB_563521), using Opal detection fluorophores (Akoya Biosciences, catalog No. NEL811001KT) at the following concentrations: Opal-520 diluted 1:100, Opal-570 diluted 1:150, Opal-540 diluted 1:100, Opal-690 diluted 1:100, Opal-620 diluted 1:100, and Opal-650 diluted 1:150. Cell nuclei were also stained with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI). Immunofluorescence images were captured on the Vectra (PerkinElmer) platform and analyzed using Phenochart and inForm software.

## Profiling with GeoMx protein assay
The TMA slide was prepared following Bruker (formerly NanoString) protocols without modification (Manual 10150-04 Manual Slide Preparation). Standard IHC protocols were used to visualize morphologic markers with SYTO13 for DNA and anti-NKX2.2, anti-CD68, and anti-CD8 antibodies for cells. Quantitative protein detection used antibodies with a UV-photocleavable fluorescent barcode containing a unique molecular identifier. The TMA was scanned using the GeoMx four-laser system to generate images at single-cell resolution. Regions of interest (ROI) with a maximum size of 600 × 600 μm were generated to include tumors based on NKX2.2 positivity (Supplementary Fig. S1). Forty-eight distinct ROI were created in strategic locations with the goal of capturing as much of the sample material as possible. Though rare, areas of imperfection in the TMA (e.g., tears) were avoided when possible during ROI selection. Individual segments were identified by CD68 and CD8 positivity, as well as nonpositive stromal areas, and collected separately. Protein probes were sequentially collected by photocleaving the oligonucleotides from the tissue and capturing probes in separate wells of a 96-well plate. Collected probes were barcoded, processed, and counted on the Bruker/NanoString nCounter system (Manual 10089-08 nCounter Readout) without modification. Count files were downloaded from GeoMx and subsequentially indexed to the ROI from the tissue scans for analysis.

## Processing of GeoMx proteomic data and identification of differentially expressed proteins
To normalize the GeoMx data, we established an approach to control for the different ROI sizes and correspondingly different numbers of cells within these regions. To achieve this, we first normalized the GeoMx protein data per region imaged by dividing each analyte expression level per region by the sum of the analytes in that region and then multiplying by a scale factor of 1,000. We then log-transformed (using the natural log) these normalized counts and used them for downstream analyses. To identify differentially expressed proteins, we performed Wilcoxon rank-sum tests, and a two-sided α of less than 5% was considered significant. These workflows are documented in our code repository for this study (https://github.com/CilloLaboratory/sarc028_ewing).

## Provenance and processing of single-cell transcriptomic data
Two single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) datasets from the TME of human Ewing sarcoma tumors were aggregated to construct a reference atlas. The first Ewing sarcoma dataset was enriched for CD45+ immune cells sorted from the TME ( ), whereas the second dataset consisted of all cells from the TME (GSE261693). Together, these datasets provide an in-depth single-cell atlas of the Ewing sarcoma TME. To process these data, we combined the count matrices from all samples into a unified Seurat object. Next, we performed sctransform-based normalization of the highly variable genes followed by reciprocal principal component analysis (PCA)–guided data integration to control for potential technical confounding variables between individual datasets ( , ). Next, we performed graph-based clustering on the principal components from the integrated datasets as well as Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection for Dimension Reduction–based dimensionality reduction from the same principal components for visualization ( ). Principal components were heuristically selected based on the cumulative variance explained by each principal component. These workflows are documented in our code repository for this study (https://github.com/CilloLaboratory/sarc028_ewing).

## Profiling with CosMx Cancer Transcriptome Atlas
We performed imaging-based spatial transcriptomics analyses of a TMA constructed from Ewing sarcoma biopsy samples derived from the SARC028 clinical trial ( ) using the CosMx Human Universal Cell Characterization RNA Panel (1,000-plex, Bruker/NanoString). We also included B2M/CD298, pan-CK, CD45, CD3, and DAPI as morphologic markers. Following the generation of data, cells were segmented, and transcripts were allocated to cells from images and quantified using the manufacturer’s standardized pipeline. We next read the output files into Seurat with the LoadNanoString function. After reading data into Seurat, we jointly embedded our Ewing sarcoma scRNA-seq atlas and the CosMx spatial transcriptomics. This was achieved by first filtering the scRNA-seq data to include only genes that were also present in the CosMx Human Universal Cell Characterization RNA Panel and then performing sctransform and reciprocal PCA-based integration between the cells derived from spatial transcriptomics and those derived from scRNA-seq. After jointly embedding these two types of data, we then performed graph-based clustering of the combined scRNA-seq and CosMx datasets. As we had previously annotated cells in the Ewing scRNA-seq atlas, we were then able to perform a label transfer approach to infer cell types in the CosMx data from the scRNA-seq reference dataset, thus efficiently and robustly labeling the cell types in the CosMx dataset. Briefly, cells from the CosMx data that coclustered with known cell types from the scRNA-seq data were considered to be the same cell type, and the inferred label from the scRNA-seq cells was transferred to the CosMx cells. These workflows are documented in our code repository for this study (https://github.com/CilloLaboratory/sarc028_ewing).

## CN analysis of CosMx spatial transcriptomic data
We sought to identify, in an unsupervised manner, the organizational patterns of cells in space in each biopsy from the SARC028 TMA. To achieve this, we leveraged the annotated cell types and their physical locations within tissues and then created a proximity vector consisting of the proportion of cells present within a given radius of the cell of interest. Specifically, we chose a diameter of 300 units as the distance with which to enumerate neighboring cell types. We performed these measurements across all cells in the dataset and created a matrix of the vector of cells in the region surrounding each individual cell. Using this matrix, we performed k-means clustering to identify recurrent neighborhoods of cells based on the derived proximity vectors. These inferred spatial CN were then used for downstream analyses. This workflow is documented in our code repository for this study (https://github.com/CilloLaboratory/sarc028_ewing).

## Statistical analysis
For differentially expressed proteins in the GeoMx platform and differentially expressed genes from the CosMx platform, we used Wilcoxon rank-sum tests. Nominal two-sided type I error rates of less than 5% were considered significant for the protein data, whereas an FDR of 5% was considered statistically significant for the CosMx transcriptomic data. Given the small size of this patient tumor cohort (Ewing sarcoma) in the SARC028 clinical trial, this study is not statistically powered to conduct formal comparisons between immune signatures and treatment response.

## Data availability
scRNA-seq data are derived from publicly available data deposited in the Gene Expression Omnibus (GSE198896 and GSE261693). The GeoMx data, CosMx data, and all other data files necessary for analyses are available on Zenodo (https://zenodo.org/records/15022777). Code is publicly available for this study at https://github.com/CilloLaboratory/sarc028_ewing.

## Results

## Multiplex immunofluorescence analysis demonstrates that pembrolizumab therapy does not alter the density of Ewing immune cell infiltrates
Due to the rarity of Ewing tumor specimens, we sought to maximize the output from the limited tissue obtained from patients with Ewing sarcoma treated on SARC028. A TMA of biopsy specimens from 13 patients ( ) was generated. In total, 37 tissue sections were included on the TMA. Eight patients had paired pre- and posttherapy (8 weeks ± 1 week on therapy) tissue samples available, and of these patients, seven had available disease response data. No cases of pseudoprogression were observed in patients with Ewing sarcoma. Utilizing this TMA, we conducted analysis with multiplex immunofluorescence, spatial proteomics, and single-cell imaging-based spatial transcriptomics to determine changes in the quantity of immune cell infiltration and immunoregulatory protein expression and to define CN ( ).
 Previous work has shown that the degree of tumor immune cell infiltration at baseline can predict response to pembrolizumab therapy ( ), and tumors that respond to pembrolizumab demonstrate an increase in immune infiltration after therapy ( ). To determine if pembrolizumab altered the quantity of immune cell infiltration in Ewing sarcoma tumor biopsy specimens, the TMA was analyzed using multiplex immunofluorescence for CD4 and CD8 (T-cell markers), CD68 (macrophage marker), CD20 (B-cell marker), FoxP3 (regulatory T-cell marker), CD45 (pan-leukocyte marker), and DAPI (nuclear stain; ). Analyses show that pembrolizumab therapy did not significantly alter the quantity of total immune cell infiltration in Ewing sarcoma tumors when comparing all pretherapy samples with all posttherapy samples ( ). However, on an individual patient level, we did observe both increases and decreases in the quantity of tumor immune cell infiltration following pembrolizumab therapy ( ). The effect of pembrolizumab on the density of immune cell subtypes, including CD4+ T cells, CD8+ T cells, regulatory T cells (Foxp3+), B cells (CD20+), and macrophage populations (CD68+), was also determined. Pembrolizumab therapy did not significantly alter the total quantity of these immune cell subpopulations in Ewing sarcoma biopsy specimens following pembrolizumab therapy (Supplementary Fig. S2).
 We next sought to investigate whether an increase or decrease in the quantity of total immune cell infiltration following pembrolizumab therapy correlated with stable disease or the degree of tumor progression in individual patients. In the seven patients with available paired pre- and posttherapy biopsy samples and tumor response data, analyses did not demonstrate a correlation between the change in total immune cell density (all CD45+ cell types) and the percentage change in tumor size (Supplementary Fig. S3). We did observe a positive correlation between the percentage change in tumor size and the fold change in both CD68+ cell density and CD4+ cell density (Supplementary Fig. S3). However, given the overall limited number of patient samples, no definitive results about tumor response and changes in Ewing tumor immune cell infiltration can be concluded. These findings do suggest that a potential reason for the inefficacy of pembrolizumab as a single agent for this patient population was the inability of pembrolizumab alone to increase the quantity of immune cell infiltration in Ewing sarcoma (vs. overall low immune cell infiltration resulting in low efficacy of pembrolizumab).

## Spatial proteomics of Ewing sarcoma tumors following pembrolizumab therapy reveals upregulation of immunosuppressive proteins
Next, we sought to investigate changes in highly multiplexed protein expression on specific cell subsets following immunotherapy with pembrolizumab. Specifically, we investigated the expression of 68 proteins contained in the combined Bruker/NanoString GeoMx immuno-oncology panels (Supplementary Table S1) on a slide consecutive to the one used for the multiplexed immunofluorescence (Vectra) analysis. The GeoMx spatial proteomics platform enables the isolation of proteins from full ROI, specific morphologic regions, or individual cell populations. We examined protein expression in full ROI, stromal regions, and isolated CD8+ T cells and CD68+ macrophages ( ). Multiple ROI were selected per tumor sample on the TMA to maximize the material captured and included in the analysis. From the 68 proteins across these regions and cell types, we performed PCA to evaluate whether there were consistent changes in protein levels before and after treatment with pembrolizumab ( ). This revealed that there is not a strong separation between before and after treatment across any of the regions analyzed although there were some distinct regions enriched for pre- versus posttreatment samples from the stromal region and the full ROI.
 We next evaluated whether there were significantly differentially expressed proteins between baseline and after treatment in the stromal region ( ). There was no clear separation between baseline and posttreatment samples by hierarchical clustering of gene expression profiles, but we did identify nine proteins that were differentially expressed in this region between baseline and after treatment ( ). Many of these proteins are immune-related, such as CD20, GITR, GZMA, and PD1, and had lower expression in the stromal region after treatment, suggesting that pembrolizumab may alter immune composition or immune cell states in the stroma (vs. exposure to pembrolizumab resulting in modulation of stromal-immune interactions). We also interrogated protein levels in full ROI at baseline and after treatment from the Ewing sarcoma TME ( ). There was some hierarchical clustering between pre- and posttreatment samples by protein expression in full ROI ( ). This clustering pattern was at least partially driven by higher expression of immune regulatory proteins after treatment, including CD163 ( ), LAG3, FOXP3, CTLA4, and IDO1 ( ). We further determined that the expression of an immunosuppressive protein set (PDL1, PD1, CTLA4, PDL2, CD66b, FOXP3, CD163, Tim-3, LAG3, and IDO1) was enriched following pembrolizumab therapy (Supplementary Fig. S4). We also interrogated whether there were correlations between protein abundance and percentage change in tumor size across the stromal regions and the full ROI at either baseline or after treatment. This analysis revealed that several features present in the stromal ROI at baseline were significantly correlated with the percentage change in tumor size. However, the limited number of evaluable patients precludes robust and generalizable conclusions (Supplementary Fig. S5). Overall, there were some changes in protein levels for immune-related features in the stromal region and full ROI following treatment with pembrolizumab, suggesting that there are some pharmacodynamic responses to treatment despite the absence of clinical benefit.

## Joint integration approach maps cell types from scRNA-seq to CosMx
We next evaluated single-cell expression profiles and physical locations of cells within tumors both before and after treatment with pembrolizumab. To achieve this, we used the Bruker/NanoString CosMx Human Universal Cell Characterization RNA Panel (Supplementary Table S2) to perform in situ single-cell spatial transcriptomics on our TMA ( ). After data processing and cell segmentation, we constructed gene expression profiles associated with individual cells (see “Materials and Methods”). Next, we leveraged a scRNA-seq atlas of the Ewing sarcoma TME (including both treatment-naïve and relapsed tumor specimens; see “Materials and Methods”) to create a reference atlas to serve as a robust map for the inference of cell types in the CosMx spatial transcriptomics data. To achieve this, we first identified a shared latent space between these two heterogeneous datasets (see “Materials and Methods”). Briefly, we performed sctransform-based integration and reciprocal PCA to jointly embed the scRNA-seq and CosMx data in the same space, facilitating label transfer from the fully annotated scRNA-seq datasets to the CosMx dataset based on coclustering ( ; see “Materials and Methods”). This joint integration approach led to the robust annotation of single cells from the spatial transcriptomics dataset ( and ).
 

## Spatially resolved CN are differentially associated with response to immunotherapy in Ewing sarcoma
After inferring cell types within our spatial transcriptomic dataset, we next evaluated whether there were recurrent spatial organizational patterns of cells in the Ewing sarcoma TME. To achieve this, we developed and implemented an unsupervised approach to identify spatially resolved CN ( ). Briefly, we identified the proportion of each cell type present within a given radius of a central cell (see “Materials and Methods”). We repeated the process for all cells in the dataset and aggregated the vectors for each individual cell into a matrix. We then grouped these individual CN vectors into clusters by performing k-means clustering. We note two important points about this approach: (i) It identifies recurrent patterns present across patients, and (ii) it is not reliant on any underlying distributional assumptions, which allows it to uncover abstract and irregularly shaped neighborhoods.
 When we performed this analysis, we identified a total of 10 spatially resolved CN ( ). These CN were enriched for different cell types. CN1, CN2, CN3, CN6, CN7, CN9, and CN10 predominantly consisted of tumor cell subsets. Specifically, CN3, CN6, CN7, and CN10 contained mixtures of tumor subtypes, whereas CN1, CN2, and CN9 consisted mostly of individual tumor cell subtypes. Conversely, CN4, CN5, and CN8 consisted of tumor cell types and nontumor cell types, including osteoclasts and immune cell subsets. CN4 and CN5 were hierarchically related and were enriched for osteoclasts, CD8+ T cells, and CD4+ T conventional cells. CN8 was enriched for a diverse array of immune cells, including the CD8+ and CD4+ T conventional cells present in CN4 and CN5, as well as other B lineage cells, myeloid cells, and NK cells. We also identified differentially expressed genes across each of these spatially resolved CN. To achieve this, we leveraged the CN identities and performed a Wilcoxon rank-sum test to compare neighborhoods in a one-versus-all–based approach (see “Materials and Methods”). This analysis revealed that these neighborhoods represent distinct biological states, reflected by the differentially expressed genes associated with each ( ). In summary, our approach for unsupervised identification of spatial patterns of cells in the Ewing sarcoma TME reveals specific neighborhoods of tumor cells, as well as some neighborhoods that were enriched for either T-cell or more diverse immune infiltrates.
We next questioned whether any CN were consistently present across multiple patients. To achieve this, we performed hierarchical clustering of the proportion of cells in each patient that belonged to the CN defined in . When we visualized the resulting heatmap, we also included the time point and response status for each patient, although we note that only one patient had stable disease, and the remaining patients had progressive disease ( ). This analysis showed that CN4 and CN5 were present at both baseline and after treatment in patient 9, the only patient who had stable disease. CN4 and CN5 were associated with T-cell infiltration and tumor cell subpopulation c5 ( ). Conversely, we found that CN10 was present in six patients who had progressive disease, suggesting that this neighborhood may be involved in resistance to anti-PD1 therapy. These results were further corroborated by PCA (Supplementary Fig. S6A and S6B), which demonstrated that CN10 was a strong driver of separation along PC1. CN10 also constituted more than 50% of the cells in the tumor in patients who had high enrichment for CN10 (Supplementary Fig. S6A). CN10 was also consistently observed before and after treatment. CN10 mostly consisted of tumor cell subpopulations c1 and c4, suggesting that these tumor cell subtypes may have features that limit the response to immunotherapy. We note that the small sample size precludes appropriately powered statistical analyses of these comparisons. Taken together, our CN approach revealed recurrent patterns of spatially resolved CN and a specific neighborhood (CN10) that was present across multiple patients with progressive disease.

## Dissection of tumor-specific neighborhoods reveals determinants of primary resistance to immunotherapy in Ewing sarcoma
After identifying that CN10 was associated with primary resistance to immunotherapy, we sought to better understand the features of this neighborhood that contribute to this resistance. To begin to understand the features associated with resistance to immunotherapy in Ewing sarcoma, we visualized the spatially resolved CN in patient 9 (a patient with stable disease; ) and patient 2 (a representative patient with progressive disease and a high proportion of CN10; ). This revealed, as anticipated, a high proportion of CN4 and CN5 in patient 9 ( ) and a high proportion of CN9 and CN10 in patient 2 ( ). We also visualized the spatially resolved CN across all patients and found that CN10 was significantly enriched across multiple patients (Supplementary Fig. S7). There were significant levels of immune infiltrate present in patient 9 and little immune infiltrate in patient 2 based on these CN. We reasoned that the tumor cell subpopulations may be differentially contributing to the immunogenicity of the TME, so we sought to determine whether there were differentially expressed genes across these tumor cell subpopulations (Supplementary Fig. S8). Tumor cell subpopulations c1 and c4 (enriched in CN10) were closely related by hierarchical clustering (Supplementary Fig. S8).
 From here, we sought to determine whether any gene sets were differentially expressed between the tumor subpopulations present in CN10 versus CN4 and CN5. We first interrogated targets of EWS::FLI1 and found that tumor cell subpopulations c4, and to a lesser extent c1 (both enriched in CN10), had higher enrichment of EWS::FLI1 targets versus tumor cell subpopulation c5 (enriched in CN4 and CN5; ). We also found that tumor cell subpopulation c4, and to a lesser extent c1, had significantly higher levels of TGF-β signaling versus tumor cell subpopulation c5 ( ), demonstrating that this immunosuppressive cytokine may also play a role in promoting the poor immunogenicity associated with CN10.
Given that antitumor immunity by CD8+ T cells is predicated on the expression of HLA class I molecules on the surface of tumor cells, we next interrogated the expression of HLA-A, HLA-B, and HLA-C across the tumor subpopulations present in CN4, CN5, and CN10. Surprisingly, this analysis revealed that tumor cell subpopulations c1 and c4 had dramatically lower expression of HLA class I molecules compared with tumor cell subpopulation c5 (which was enriched in the patient with stable disease following pembrolizumab; ). Based on the low levels of HLA class I expression, we next sought to further assess whether this was potentially due to either the absence of IFN responsiveness or other genomic defects. Correspondingly, we found that IFN-γ response signatures were higher in tumor cell subpopulation c5 versus c1 and c4 ( ). We additionally found a significant correlation between HLA class I expression and immune cell infiltration and HLA class I expression and IFN-γ response signatures ( and ). These findings are consistent with a model in which HLA class I expression is low due to impaired IFN response rather than genomic lesions that downregulate HLA expression. However, it has been shown that HLA class I expression can be upregulated in Ewing sarcoma cells in vitro ( ). To this end, we interrogated HLA class I expression on three Ewing sarcoma cell lines in vitro following treatment with IFN-γ or control (Supplementary Fig. S9). Consistent with previous work, we find that HLA class I expression can be modified and upregulated in Ewing sarcoma. Overall, we identified several features of tumor subpopulations associated with CN10 that likely contribute to reduced immune infiltration and the inability to respond to immunotherapy.

## Discussion
To date, our understanding of the Ewing sarcoma TME and the factors in this microenvironment that predict immune infiltration of these tumors has been limited. In this study, we leveraged a unique opportunity to examine immune cell density, spatial proteomics, and single-cell spatial transcriptomics in Ewing tumor biopsy samples both before and after (8 weeks ± 1 week) pembrolizumab therapy. We identified consistent CN present in Ewing tumors associated with poor prognosis, as well as several potentially modifiable immunosuppressive features of these tumors that may contribute to the overall poor response to single-agent checkpoint inhibition in this sarcoma subtype.
In patients enrolled in SARC028 with objective responses (those with dedifferentiated liposarcoma and undifferentiated pleomorphic sarcoma), there was an increase in immune cell populations following pembrolizumab therapy ( ). Our finding confirms that in Ewing sarcoma, there is no effect on overall immune cell density between pre- and posttreatment biopsy samples. Tumor-associated macrophages have been previously identified as a poor prognostic marker in Ewing tumors ( ) and have increasingly been recognized to lead to CD8+ T-cell inactivation. Interestingly, our analysis of differential protein expression following pembrolizumab therapy revealed upregulation of CD163, a marker of an immunosuppressive macrophage population ( – ). We also identified increased expression of FOXP3, a marker of regulatory T cells, which, when present, are also known to blunt the activity of effector T cells ( ). We hypothesize that the increased recruitment of these immunosuppressive cell populations to the Ewing sarcoma TME following pembrolizumab therapy may contribute to the lack of clinical response. Future preclinical investigation of combinatorial therapies aimed at abrogating the immunosuppressive impact of macrophages and regulatory T cells in the Ewing sarcoma TME is warranted.
Mechanisms driving the net immunosuppression noted in relapsed Ewing sarcoma are still poorly understood. Recent scRNA-seq studies of relapsed Ewing sarcoma, including our own work, have highlighted multiple mechanisms of immunosuppression in the Ewing sarcoma TME ( , ). In our current study of paired patient samples from SARC028, we identify the triad of increased TGF-β signaling, reduced IFN response, and low expression of HLA type I as features of Ewing tumor cell populations that predominate in biopsies from patients with disease progression while receiving pembrolizumab. Previous work has shown the important role of TGF-β signaling in Ewing sarcoma biology ( , ). Inhibition of TGF-β has been clinically tested in patients with Ewing sarcoma, including the use of TGF-β–imprinted NK cellular therapy (NCT05634369) and as a component of Vigil therapy, which blocks TGF-β activation through a single mechanism by reducing furin expression (NCT01061840). Our recent in vivo studies in humanized mouse models of Ewing sarcoma have also demonstrated increased immune cell infiltration in Ewing sarcoma tumors following the combination of radiotherapy and inhibition of TGF-β signaling via a ligand trap (bioRxiv 2024.05.07.592974). TGF-β inhibition is worthy of continued preclinical investigation in Ewing sarcoma.
Ewing sarcoma tumor cells have significant heterogeneity in response to cytokines in the TME, such as IFN and TNF-α ( , ). When considering interventions to “boost” or “prime” IFN responses in Ewing sarcoma, an area of active investigation in our laboratory, it is important to understand the mechanisms to induce IFN production in the Ewing sarcoma TME and to understand which Ewing tumors may be less responsive to IFN at baseline. The IFN response is critical given the ability of IFN stimulation to upregulate the expression of key factors regulating immunotherapy response, such as tumor cell HLA class I expression ( ). HLA class I expression in Ewing sarcoma is thought to be modifiable ( ) in cells that retain robust responsiveness to inflammatory signaling. As previously suggested ( ), a path forward for immunotherapy in the treatment of Ewing sarcoma is a focus on interventions that do not rely on HLA class I expression for efficacy, such as targeted chimeric antigen receptor T-cell therapy ( ). As immunosuppression in Ewing sarcoma is multifactorial, an alternative or complementary approach is to utilize treatment combinations to block immunosuppression while promoting inflammation. Radiotherapy, a treatment modality commonly used to treat relapsed Ewing sarcoma, has the potential to both increase HLA class I expression and IFN response ( – ). Interventions aimed at blocking immunosuppression during radiotherapy in Ewing sarcoma warrant further investigation.
Lastly, this study highlights the importance of obtaining on-therapy biopsy samples as a component of clinical trials when feasible, especially for patients with rare tumor types, such as Ewing sarcoma. Given the limited number of patients with Ewing sarcoma enrolled in SARC028, our current study could not determine differences in findings based on age, sex, or the status of biological factors such as STAG2 or TP53 mutation. It is increasingly clear that understanding tumor response to therapy, and in particular tumor characteristics that promote therapy resistance, is critical to designing new therapeutic approaches. Tumor heterogeneity is increasingly recognized as a critical pathway for tumor resistance to therapy ( ). Our use of single-cell spatial transcriptomic data to identify CN, with unique gene signatures and biological states, highlights the heterogeneity of tumor cell populations and corresponding immune cell infiltration in Ewing sarcoma. Increased collection of Ewing biopsy samples in the setting of therapeutic trials or disease relapse is critical to advancing our understanding of the biology of this rare, heterogeneous, and aggressive cancer.
