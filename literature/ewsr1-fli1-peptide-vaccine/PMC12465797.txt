## Abstract
Ewing’s sarcoma (ES) is exceptionally rare in the gastrointestinal tract, with only a few cases documented in the literature. We present a case of duodenal Ewing’s sarcoma in a 44-year-old woman. The patient presented with severe abdominal pain, vomiting, and loss of appetite. Upper gastrointestinal endoscopy revealed a huge bulge in the first part of the duodenum (D1) wall, resulting in luminal narrowing in D1 and D2. A contrast-enhanced computed tomography scan corroborated the findings with no evidence of any distal metastasis. The patient is a known hypothyroid and had undergone hysterectomy five years back and cholecystectomy one year back. The patient underwent a Whipple procedure along with lymph node dissection. During surgery, a 4.5 cm mass was discovered in the D1 extending to the D2 segment of the duodenum. Histological examination showed a small round cell tumor in the submucosal region. The morphological differentials considered were gastrointestinal stromal tumor (GIST), poorly differentiated carcinoma, ES, neuroendocrine carcinoma (NEC) and lymphoma. Immunohistochemically the tumor cells tested positive for vimentin, NKX2.2, and CD99 (MIC2), and negative for markers of epithelial malignancy (AE1/AE3), lymphoma (CD45), melanoma (HMB45), NEC (INMS1,synaptophysin, chromogranin) and GIST (c-kit). One of the peri-duodenal lymph nodes showed a metastatic deposit. The case was diagnosed as Ewing’s sarcoma (extra skeletal), TNM stage pT1 pN1. It was further ratified through fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH), which showed a EWSR1 (22q12.1) gene rearrangement. A post-surgery PET scan indicated no residual disease. The patient had been receiving post-surgical adjuvant therapy and has completed four cycles of VAC (vincristine dactinomycin cyclophosphamide) and two cycles of VIME (Vincristine, Ifosfamide, Mesna, and Etoposide). The patient is disease-free with one-year follow-up. Follow up with PET scan is being performed at 3 months interval for the first year. The prognosis for Ewing’s sarcoma in the gastrointestinal tract is not well-documented; however, a recent review of cases involving the small intestine suggests a poor prognosis. A follow up PET scanning every three months initially would help to target early recurrences and the interval can be increased after 2 years. Also emerging techniques like liquid biopsies is becoming increasingly relevant for detection of recurrences and metastasis.

## Introduction
Ewing’s sarcoma (ES) is a rare and highly aggressive malignant small round cell tumor. It is characterized by varying degrees of neuroectodermal differentiation and the presence of an FET-ETS family fusion genes most common being EWSR1-FLI1 fusion (85–90%) [ ]. While it is predominantly found in the long bones, pelvis, and chest wall, its occurrence in the gastrointestinal tract is extremely rare, with only a handful of cases documented in the duodenum. We report a rare instance of ES of the duodenum, discussing the clinical presentation, diagnostic challenges, and treatment strategies, and emphasizing the importance of considering this diagnosis in atypical locations for early and effective intervention.

## Case presentation
A 44-year-old woman presented with severe abdominal pain, vomiting, and loss of appetite, with no significant past medical history. Upper gastrointestinal endoscopy showed a circumferential large bulge in the first part of the duodenum (D1), resulting in luminal narrowing, and a biopsy was taken. The biopsy was reported as adenocarcinoma, poorly differentiated in an outside centre, and she was referred to tertiary cancer centre for further management. Biopsy review showed scant malignant small round blue cells in the lamina propria. On immunohistochemistry, tumor cells were positive for vimentin and negative for and negative for markers of epithelial malignancy (AE1/AE3), lymphoma (CD45), melanoma (HMB45), NEC (INMS1,synaptophysin, chromogranin) and GIST (c-kit). Simultaneously in view of gastric outlet obstruction, upfront Whipple’s surgery with regional lymph node dissection was performed. The gross characteristics of the mass included a focal ulceroinfiltrative, solid grey-white firm tumor measuring 4.5 × 3.5 × 1.1 cm, involving the first and second part of the duodenum, infiltrating from the submucosa to the muscularis without haemorrhage or necrosis (Fig. ). The pancreas and bile duct were grossly unremarkable.
 Microscopically, the tumor was arranged in nests, lobules, and cords. The cells were largely monomorphic with round to oval hyperchromatic nuclei, inconspicuous nucleoli, and a scant to moderate amount of clear cytoplasm (Fig. A-E). Mitoses were infrequent. No necrosis, lympho-vascular, or perineural invasion were noted. All margins were free of the tumor. On immunohistochemistry, tumor cells were positive for Vimentin (Fig. F), NKX2.2 (Fig. H), CD99, and Cyclin D1 (focal and strong), while negative for BCOR. Cytokeratin was negative. (Fig. G). One peri-duodenal lymph node showed metastasis (1/22) without extra-nodal extension or soft tissue deposits.
 Fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) analysis of the EWSR1 gene rearrangement at chromosome 22q12, using the ZytoLight SPEC EWSR1 Dual Color Break Apart Probe (ZytoVision, GmbH), showed split signals in 84% of the tumor cells (Fig. ).
 A post-operative PET scan revealed no residual viable tumor. Adjuvant treatment planned was to give total four cycles of VAC (vincristine dactinomycin cyclophosphamide) and four cycles of VIME (Vincristine, Ifosfamide, Mesna, and Etoposide) thrice weekly (VAC alternately with VIME). A total of 8 cycles was planned. However, the patient could complete four cycles of VAC and two cycles of VIME. VIME was omitted in view of poor tolerance. The patient is disease-free with a one-year follow-up. Follow up PET has been advised every three months.

## Discussion
ES rarely manifests as a primary duodenal tumor. The peak incidence of skeletal ES occurs during the second decade of life with overall male predilection (M: F ratio: 3:2) [ ]. In the present review of duodenal Ewing’s sarcoma (DES), however a female predilection has been noted (M: F ratio: 1:1.5).The clinical symptoms are generally nonspecific and can include abdominal pain, vomiting, loss of appetite, and gastric outlet obstruction, as observed in the present case. To the best of our knowledge, only fourteen other cases of duodenal involvement have been documented (Table ).
 Endoscopic findings may reveal a circumferential or localized mass with mild mucosal irregularities and possible obstruction, similar to the presentation of other sarcomas, particularly gastrointestinal stromal tumor (GIST), which raises the difficulty of accurately diagnosing DES preoperatively especially in small biopsies without the use of ancillary techniques. Mostly, the diagnosis is made on a postoperative resection specimen. In the present case too biopsy diagnosis was difficult and the architectural pattern in simultaneous resection specimen helped in correct diagnosis. Histologically, ES is composed of uniform small round cells. The major differential diagnoses of this morphology are discussed in Table .
 These poorly differentiated small round cell tumors as illustrated in table share overlapping morphological features but have different prognoses, making precise diagnosis notoriously difficult but essential. In the context of gastrointestinal tract the most common mesenchymal tumor would be a GIST, however these tumors are positive for CD117 and DOG1 which were negative in the present case. Also a diffuse large B cell lymphoma can come in the differential diagnosis, however would show a diffuse CD45 positivity. Desmoplastic small round cell tumor (DSRCT) can also come in the differentials with this morphology, However, it is a tumor of abdominal cavity, organ specific occurrence is very uncommon. In the present tumor, though desmoplasia was noted, but the tumor cells were diffusely positive for NKX2.2 and CD99 which is not found in DSRTCT. Moreover CK was negative which usually shows a dot like positivity in DSCRCT. Strong, diffuse membranous expression of CD99 is evident in about 95% of ES. However, NKX2.2 is considered to have higher specificity than CD99.[ ] Studies also shown that Cyclin D1 is a diagnostic marker for ES and could be targeted for therapeutic strategies.[ ]
ES is characterized by structural rearrangements between the FET and ETS family of proteins leading to the formation of FET-ETS fusion genes. These fusions involve the combination of a FET (FUS, EWSR1, or TAF15) family RNA-binding protein (proteins involved in various aspects of gene regulation, including transcription, RNA processing, and transport) with an ETS (erythroblast transformation-specific) family transcription factor (regulate gene expression). The most common fusion is EWSR1-FLI1 (85-90%), but other fusions like EWSR1-ERG, EWSR1-FEV, and FUS-ETV4 have also been identified.[ ]
Additionally, mutations in STAG2 (15–22%), CDKN2A (12%), and TP53 (7%) may also be present.[ ]ERG and FLI1 IHC may be used as surrogate markers for underlying gene fusions, particularly in the context of Ewing sarcoma. However, IHC does not directly demonstrate the presence of a fusion gene. Rather, it reflects overexpression of the FLI1 or ERG protein, which may be upregulated due to EWSR1-FLI1 or EWSR1-ERG gene rearrangements. While this immunophenotypic expression supports the diagnosis, it is not specific and may also be observed in other neoplasms, such as vascular tumors and certain lymphomas or prostate carcinomas. Therefore, confirmation of the fusion gene requires molecular diagnostic techniques such as fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH), reverse transcriptase polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR), or next-generation sequencing (NGS). Recent advances in molecular diagnostics have significantly improved the identification and classification of ES, particularly in cases with atypical morphology or uncommon gene fusions encompassing round cell sarcoma (RCS) with non-ETS fusions like EWSR1-NFATC2, FUS-NFATC2,and EWSR1-PATZ1 and genetically and clinically distinct entities an like CIC (Capicua Transcriptional Repressor)-rearranged sarcoma and sarcoma with BCOR (BCL6 corepressor) genetic alterations which exhibit deceptive and overlapping histomorphologic features, but show a different clinical behaviour. While conventional techniques such as fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) and reverse transcriptase PCR (RT-PCR) remain gold standards due to their accessibility, speed, and minimal tissue requirements, they possess key limitations. FISH, although widely available, can miss cryptic intrachromosomal rearrangements, particularly involving EWSR1-ERG, and repeated probing for multiple gene partners may lead to tissue exhaustion, especially in limited samples. [ ]RT-PCR is limited by the need for prior knowledge of both fusion partners and cannot detect novel or rare transcripts. [ ]
To overcome these issues, the NanoString nCounter platform has emerged as a reliable intermediate diagnostic tool. This hybridization-based assay allows multiplexed detection of known fusion transcripts in formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tissue with high specificity and reduced turnaround time. Studies by Chang et al. and Sheth et al. have demonstrated 100% concordance between NanoString and RT-PCR, and even identified fusions missed by conventional assays, with no false positives. [ , ]However, false negatives remain possible when the target fusions are absent from the panel or novel in nature.
For comprehensive detection, targeted RNA sequencing using Anchored Multiplex PCR (AMP) such as the Archer FusionPlex Sarcoma assay—offers superior sensitivity and broader coverage. AMP-based sequencing does not require prior knowledge of fusion partners and can detect rare, complex, or atypical gene fusions, making it especially valuable in diagnostically ambiguous cases. It also enables detection of multiple gene fusion variants and emerging fusion subtypes, as demonstrated in studies by Krystel-Whittemore et al. and Brcic et al. [ , ]However, AMP is more resource-intensive in terms of cost, analysis, and turnaround time.
Given these strengths and limitations, a tiered diagnostic algorithm is now considered optimal: beginning with histology and immunohistochemistry (e.g., CD99, FLI1), followed by EWSR1 FISH in CD99-positive cases. When FISH is inconclusive or CD99 expression is weak, NanoString serves as an efficient second-line assay. If NanoString results are negative or non-informative, AMP-based RNA sequencing is recommended as the final diagnostic modality.[ ] This approach enhances diagnostic accuracy, conserves tissue, and allows detection of novel gene fusions, which is critical given the therapeutic and prognostic relevance of specific molecular subtypes in ES and related round cell sarcomas.
Ewing sarcoma usually occurs due to somatic alteration characterized by above mentioned translocations. Potential role for germline (inherited) variants in DNA damage repair genes may be present/increase the risk in a subset of cases. The present patient did not have any familial association.
Management of DES involves a multimodal approach, including surgical resection and adjuvant chemotherapy. [ – ]Complete surgical resection with clear margins is crucial in managing ES to prevent local recurrence. The status of lymphovascular or perineural invasion further guide the postoperative management. The chemotherapy regimen used, consisting of VAC and VIDE/VIME aligns with protocols for ES in other anatomical locations. EWSR1-FLI1, the oncogenic fusion gene is explored for different targeted therapies like Splicing inhibitors (Pladienolide B), minor groove-binding agents (Lurbinectedin), CDK7/12/13 inhibitors which has conferred some promising results in clinical trials. Poly (ADP-ribose) polymerase 1 (PARP1) inhibitor Olaparib did not show significant response. Different clinical trials on programmed cell death receptor 1 (PD-1) and its associated ligand (PD-L1) are currently also ongoing for ES, however being a ‘cold tumor’ with low immunogenicity, very low tumor mutation burden, the possibility of generating CAR-T recognizing neo-antigens is limited. [ ]
Surveillance of patients with Ewing sarcoma should include a physical examination, complete blood count (CBC), contrast-enhanced MRI with or without CT, and plain radiographs of the primary site. Chest imaging (x-ray or CT) is recommended every 3 months. Head-to-toe FDG-PET/CT or bone scan can be considered as appropriate. Surveillance intervals should be increased after 2 years. Long-term surveillance should be performed annually after 5 years, and as clinically indicated (indefinitely). [ ]
In the recent years liquid biopsies have become increasingly relevant in cancer diagnosis and monitoring. Variations of the circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) level of EWSR1 fusion have been correlated with response to treatment, ctDNA being reduced after chemotherapy. Early relapse, metastasis and minimal residual disease can be detected by an increment on the ctDNA levels in patients and this biopmarker is more sensitive than the current standard method of PET/SCAN. [ ]Also being a non-invasive technique repeat biopsies can be avoided though tissue biopsy is still the gold standard.
Given the aggressive nature of ES and its tendency for early metastasis, the prognosis remains guarded, particularly in rare and atypical locations like the duodenum. Long-term follow-up is essential to monitor for recurrence and manage any late effects of treatment.

## Conclusion
This case underscores the critical need for thorough diagnostic workups, including histopathological and molecular analyses, to differentiate rare presentations of ES from other malignancies. It also emphasizes the importance of a multidisciplinary approach in managing such complex cases, incorporating surgical intervention and systemic chemotherapy to achieve optimal patient outcomes.
