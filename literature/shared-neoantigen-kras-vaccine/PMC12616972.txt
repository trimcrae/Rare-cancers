## Introduction
As demonstrated by statistical data obtained from 2024, ovarian cancer (OC) represents the second most prevalent cause of mortality among female patients diagnosed with gynaecological malignancies and the sixth most common cause of cancer-related fatalities [ ]. As a consequence of the presence of only subtle symptoms and an absence of specific detection markers, approximately 75% of patients are diagnosed with stage III-IV disease [ ], thus leading to the five-year survival rate of about 50% [ ]. The standard first-line treatment regimen for ovarian cancer consists of tumor cytoreduction in combination with platinum-based chemotherapy. This approach has been observed to prolong patient survival to a certain extent [ ]; however, recurrence remains inevitable in the majority of cases.In addition, as the population ages, it is predicted that the disease burden of ovarian cancer will rise [ ].
The early detection of ovarian cancer has been demonstrated to have significant effects on mortality rates. A variety of noninvasive screening tests have been the subjects of large-scale prospective trials, including the monitoring of serum CA-125 [ ] and HE4 levels [ ], as well as transvaginal ultrasound [ ]. In addition, a variety of clinical prediction models have been developed. However, the combined prediction model of screening efficacy has failed to meet expectations. Moreover, a long-term prospective study including more than 200,000 volunteers (UKCTOCS) demonstrated that among women who are routinely tested with tumor markers or ultrasound, the mortality rate for high-grade serous ovarian cancer (HGSOC) was reduced by only 15% (even when a multimodal screening strategy was used) [ ]. It was concluded that the limited predictive power of most conventional biomarkers at 6–12 months prior to diagnosis may be due to the difficulty in identifying early-stage, insidious lesions of HGSOC. Existing blood biomarkers and tumor imaging methods may be inadequate for the detection of early-stage ovarian tumors. Therefore, it is necessary to pinpoint early diagnostic and prognostic factors for ovarian cancer patients.
Immunotherapy is an emerging treatment strategy. For example, polyadenosine diphosphate ribose polymerase (PARP) inhibitors and antiangiogenic drugs, like vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) inhibitors, have been employed as maintenance therapies, with improvements in overall survival being observed in clinical researches, which has provided hope for treatments of OC [ – ]. Immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) have been used either as monotherapy or in conjunction with chemotherapy and other clinically applied regimens [ – ]. However, the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment associated with ovarian cancer exhibits the capacity to reduce the efficacy of ICIs, resulting in restricted T-cell infiltration and a paucity of anti-tumor reactive T-cells [ ], which results in the objective remission rate to ICIs of less than 20% [ ]. Research into personalized immunotherapy approaches and early detection methods based on alterations in the tumor microenvironment represents a novel therapeutic avenue [ ]. Many studies have been conducted on tumor vaccines [ ], cytokines [ ] and cellular immunotherapies, including engineered T-cells [ ] and other immunotherapies. Theoretically, TCR-engineered T cells (TCR-T cells) targeting tumor-associated antigens (TAAs) and tumor-specific antigens (TSAs) have the potential to yield superior outcomes. As the starting point of the T-cell effect, the TCR is capable of specifically recognising tumor antigens, thereby initiating T-cell activation, proliferation, and killing; moreover, its unique antigen recognition property offers novel prospects for the precise diagnosis and treatment of ovarian cancer. The monitoring of alterations in the TCR repertoire within the human body is hypothesised to facilitate the early identification of tumor development, the assessment of patients' individual responses to immunotherapy, and the exploration of increased benefits from targeted therapy for ovarian cancer. Further research on TCR-based cellular immunotherapy is anticipated to lead to the development of novel concepts and methodologies for the immunotherapy of ovarian cancer.
The objectives of this review are to systematically elucidate the structural characteristics and antigen recognition mechanism of the TCR and explore the translational value of constructing TCR repertoires via sequencing technology for the early prediction of ovarian cancer and assessments of immune efficacy and immunotherapy, with the goal of identifying new ideas to overcome the bottleneck of current clinical treatments.

## Structure and function of T-cell receptors
TCRs are cell surface heterodimers composed of disulfide-linked α and β chains or γ and δ chains. The αβ TCR, which is composed of α and β chains, predominates in peripheral blood T cells and is the most common type of TCR involved in the body's immune response [ ]. The α and β chains of the classical TCR are composed of the following structures: a variable (V) region, a constant (C) region, a transmembrane region, and an intracellular region (which is unique to the β chain) [ ]. The variable region constitutes the pivotal component of the TCR that facilitates antigen recognition, with the complementarity-determining region (CDR) located within the variable region directly engaging with antigens at relatively low affinities (~ 1–100 μM), which allows for direct interaction with the antigenic peptide-major histocompatibility complex (pMHC) molecule [ ]. Variable regions are formed through a process of gene rearrangement, which is a complex mechanism whereby multiple gene fragments combine with each other. This process is referred to as V(D)J rearrangement. During the process of embryonic development, specific V, D (which is unique to the β chain), and J gene fragments within the genome of T-cell precursor cells undergo rearrangement, thus resulting in a substantial number of TCR variable region genes with diverse sequences. Theoretically, this process can generate approximately 1015 distinct TCR structures [ , ], thereby enabling the human body to recognise a vast array of antigens. Moreover, CD8 + and CD4 + αβΤ cells are capable of recognising short peptides presented by MHC class I and II molecules [ ], respectively. A mere 5–10% of peripheral blood T cells and a proportion of T cells found in mucosa-associated lymphoid tissues express the γδ TCR [ ], which is comprised of γ and δ chains. The γδ TCRs exhibit structural similarity to the αβ TCRs; however, they demonstrate a different mechanism and specificity in their antigen recognition. For example, the activation of these receptors is not restricted by the MHC and can be initiated by both T-cell receptor (TCR)-dependent and TCR-independent pathways [ ]. γδ T cells primarily recognise nonpeptide antigens, such as heat shock proteins and phosphorylated antigens [ ]. These antigens function as a bridge linking the body's innate and adaptive immune systems, thereby playing a crucial role in the process of early immune defence. It has been hypothesised that γδ T cells are associated with favourable prognoses in cancers such as colon cancer and breast cancer [ , ].
The variable regions of the α and β chains (or γ and δ chains) consist of three complementary decision regions (CDR1, CDR2 and CDR3). The CDR1 and CDR2 regions primarily bind to MHC molecules, thereby facilitating the stable binding of the TCR to the pMHC complex. The CDR3 region, which is characterized by its high degree of variability, is formed by the insertion or deletion of nucleotides during the process of rearrangement of the V(D)J gene. This region exhibits a high degree of diversity in terms of length and amino acid composition. Moreover, it serves as the primary contact site for recognition and binding specificity with antigens, whereby it directly interacts with the antigen and contributes to binding specificity [ , ]. This process enables the TCR to specifically recognise a variety of peptide-major histocompatibility complex(pMHC specifically.
The primary function of the TCR is to recognise the antigenic pMHC molecule on the surfaces of antigen-presenting cells (APCs). The TCR binds to the pMHC molecule with relatively low affinity but very high specificity [ , ], and effective binding can only occur if the CDR1, CDR2, and CDR3 regions of the TCR precisely match the spatial structure and amino acid sequence of the pMHC molecule. Upon binding of the TCR of a T-cell to a specific pMHC molecule, activation of the leukocyte-specific tyrosine kinase (LCK) and tyrosine-protein kinase Fyn (Fyn) of the Src family of kinases occurs [ ], thus resulting in the phosphorylation of the immunoreceptor tyrosine-based activation motif (ITAM). The phosphorylated ITAM has been observed to recruit and activate the Syk family of kinases, including zeta-activating protein of 70 kDa (ZAP70) [ ], which correspondingly elicits proximal TCR signalling. ZAP70 is responsible for the phosphorylation of client proteins such as linkers for the activation of T cells (LATs), SH2-domain-containing leukocyte protein of 76 kDa (SLP-76) and IL-2-inducible tyrosine kinase (ITK). The activated molecules form a signalling body scaffold, which activates distal signalling cascades, as well as protein kinase C (PKC), nuclear factor of activated T cells (NFAT), mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK), and nuclear factor-kappa B (NF-κB) signalling pathways, in activated T cells. These changes subsequently lead to the secretion of cytokines, as well as changes in cell motility and cell proliferation [ ]. Consequently, the binding of TCRs to the pMHC molecule represents the most significant step in T-cell activation, differentiation and proliferation.

## T-cell immune repertoire and sequencing
The T-cell immune repertoire is defined as the collection of unique T-cell receptors expressed by all T cells in an individual. The T-cell immune repertoire is characterised by its constant state of flux. A hallmark feature of the immune system's response to disease is the loss of diversity from a primitive polyclonal state, with a concomitant focus on the expansion of specific monoclonal states [ ]. This evolutionary process facilitates the rapid development of specialized T cells and antibodies that are specific to particular pathological antigens, thereby resulting in the expeditious clearance of these antigens. The substantial quantity of TCRαβ heterodimers results in the rare occurrence of identical sequences being generated twice in an individual. It is hypothesised that the detection of TCR diversity, the frequency of use of V(D)J genes, and T-cell clonal populations will provide a more precise method of monitoring immune alterations in the tumor microenvironment, which is expected to provide strong immunological support in monitoring disease progression, predicting recurrence, and evaluating therapeutic efficacy [ ].
The current material source for TCR sequencing is either DNA or RNA. DNA provides information about the potential TCR gene fragments that are present in the genome, thereby revealing genetic susceptibility to TCR diversity and the potential repertoire of TCRs that can be produced by an individual or a population [ ]. However, the use of DNA for sequencing does not directly indicate which TCRs are being actively used by T cells. Therefore, it is mainly used to identify germline gene fragments and potential rearrangements. Additionally, it is commonly used to investigate the genetic susceptibility and potential diversity of TCRs in individuals or populations [ ]. The prevalence of this method is attributable to the stability of the copy number per cell of DNA, which facilitates direct quantification of clonotype frequency. However, it should be noted that the methods employed for detecting DNA are less sensitive and unable to exclude alleles, which is a factor that often results in biased diversity. Conversely, RNA sequencing focuses on the mRNA transcripts encoding TCRs, thus reflecting the diversity of actively utilized TCR libraries and the diversity of T cells responding to specific antigens or conditions [ , ]. RNA sequencing is a common analytical technique that is used to investigate functional TCR libraries under specific conditions, such as disease states, immune responses, or approaches to subsequent treatments, including immunotherapy. RNA sequencing is superior to DNA sequencing in terms of sensitivity; moreover, it can overcome challenges such as allele ambiguity and can rectify amplification and sequencing errors by utilising unique molecular identifiers (UMIs). However, RNA is comparatively less stable, and the expression levels can vary between cells, which can have an adverse effect on TCR quantification.
The management of TCR library diversity is predominantly predicated on next-generation sequencing. A variety of high-throughput TCR sequencing (TCR-Seq) assays have been developed, encompassing batch TCR assays and single-cell TCR sequencing (scTCR-seq). The batch TCR assays include multiplex PCR, 5′-RACE PCR and TCR amplification following gene capture. Among these methods, multiplex PCR is the most frequently employed methodology, however, it introduces amplification bias due to differences in primer efficiency [ ]. The 5′-RACE PCR technique is constrained by the efficiency of the template switch, which introduces 5′ junctions to a mere 20–60% of RNA molecules. This results in diminished quantitative accuracy. Compared with multiplex PCR, 5′-RACE PCR exhibits reduced sensitivity and reproducibility for the detection of low-frequency TCRs [ ]. Therefore, it is imperative that the accuracy and sensitivity of TCR detection library methods be increased. The development of scTCR-seq has enabled the acquisition of more precise information about individual clones. Early multiplex PCR combined with Sanger sequencing or high-throughput sequencing, followed by physical single-cell isolation, has been observed to be capable of obtaining paired TCRαβ sequences. However, this method is time-consuming and exhibits low efficiency and throughput [ , ]. Han proposed a cell separation method based on fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS) technology, which involves the utilisation of a single-cell barcoding strategy. This strategy has the potential to enhance the scalability of sequenced cells by both identifying two TCR chain bindings and sequencing genes that are specifically related to T-cell function [ ]. However, this method only allows for the sequencing of a few hundred to a few thousand cells, which is inadequate for the number and depth of analyses required for clinical monitoring.
Another concept for single-cell sequencing involves microfluidics, which is a field that has undergone substantial advancements regarding the throughput of scTCR-Seq [ ]. This has been achieved by the emulsification of water-in-oil droplets containing individual cells, which have been captured by a minimal amount of reagents. This development has consequently enabled the multiplexing of the number of analysed cells, as demonstrated by Turchaninova [ ]. The integration of droplet-based microfluidic technology with droplet overlap extension reverse transcription PCR (OE-RT-PCR) facilitates the natural αβ-strand pairing process, thereby enabling the acquisition of transcriptome information. Subsequent technological iterations have enabled the analysis of TCR sequences from millions of cells with absolute precision. This method has resulted in the most extensive collection of paired TCR αβ sequencing data to date [ ]. The present commercialisation of this technology by 10 × Genomics further enhances its clinical applicability, with the advantage of being able to achieve unprecedented assay sizes (15,000–20,000 cells per run) for simultaneous TCR sequencing and single-cell transcriptomics [ ]. This is a widely utilized technique in the field of immune cell analysis. Its applications include the characterisation of immune populations following Programmed Death-1 (PD-1) treatment and the monitoring of clonal dynamics within the tumor microenvironment [ ]. However, this platform is characterised by a limited number of assays, elevated costs and constraints imposed by the microfluidic technology itself when compared with conventional scRNA-seq methods.

## TCR repertoires and disease prediction
The most commonly employed metrics for describing the diversity [ ] of TCR libraries in published studies are the Shannon and Simpson [ ] diversity indices. The obtained scores are consequently observed between the two limits. The limit of maximum clonality is characterised by the condition in which a single clone occupies the entirety of the library, whereas at the other extreme, the limit of maximum homogeneity is characterised by a state of complete uniformity (where all of the clones are identical). Another common metric that is used to describe TCR similarities or differences is the Morisita-Horn index [ ]. This term is employed to denote the quantity and prevalence of duplicate TCRs between two groups, with a scale of measurement ranging from 0 (no overlap) to 1 (all of the clones overlap at a similar frequency) [ ]. The seminal study by Yu established RFUs (group functional units) as risk markers and compared the differences in TCR pools between blood samples of patients diagnosed with high-grade plasmacytoid cancers of the ovary and benign patients. This was accomplished by evaluating the differences in TCR diversity and constructing an OVRFU score [ ]. The study revealed that the TCR pool of ovarian cancer patients exhibited significant dynamic alterations over the 5 years preceding diagnosis, which was characterised by rapid escalation followed by a gradual decline between 4 and 2 years prior to diagnosis, with an area under the curve (AUC) value of 0.77 being detected. This change was more pronounced in patients with stage I high-grade plasma carcinoma of the ovary, wherein an AUC value of 0.81 was observed. The financial outlay required for the generation of TCR-seq data for OVRFU score inference was demonstrated to be approximately £200 per patient, and this figure approximates the cost of the standard serum biomarker test evaluated by the UK Collaborative Trial of Ovarian Cancer Screening (UKCTOCS) [ ]. Nevertheless, when utilized as a screening biomarker, the RFU score exhibits significantly lower levels of sensitivity and specificity than is necessary to attain the stipulated 10% positive predictive value. Research by Wang indicates that [ ], in comparison with patients with benign ovarian tumors, those with malignant ovarian tumors exhibit significantly reduced peripheral blood immune repertoire diversity (measured by TCR and B-cell receptor(BCR)) and increased TCR heterogeneity. A TCR prediction model constructed using 16 pairs of V-J genes achieved an average area under the curve (AUC) of 0.93. Emerging computational technologies, particularly AI-driven TCR repertoire analysis, are enhancing the diagnosis of ovarian cancer directly by revealing tumor-specific immune signatures and enabling non-invasive detection. Integrating multi-omics and AI models further improves diagnostic precision by providing a more comprehensive molecular and immunological profile. The present state of affairs with regard to the detection of early ovarian cancer by means of TCR repertoires is that progress is still in its exploratory phase. This is due to limitations inherent in the research, such as the small size of the sample and the inability to incorporate human leukocyte antigen (HLA) status and genotyping.
Despite the advancements in immunotherapy, there is an urgent need to identify predictive biomarkers that can uniformly reflect the responsiveness of patients to immunotherapy. This scenario is due to the immunogenic nature of ovarian cancer, which leads to poor immunotherapy outcomes. TCRs have been proposed as potential biomarker candidates. A number of studies have sought to predict antigen-specific T-cell responses by examining TCR characteristics, including diversity, clonality, and concordance scores. A previous study by Riaz et al. posited that the TCR repertoire undergoes alterations as a result of the implementation of immune checkpoint blockade [ ]. In contrast, Tumeh et al. proposed that patients who demonstrate efficacy of PD-1 therapy exhibit increased TCR clonality [ ]. Conversely, Johnson's findings suggested that TCR clonality lacks the capacity to predict patient responsiveness to PD-1 therapy [ ]. The blockade of Cytotoxic T-Lymphocyte-Associated Protein 4 (CTLA4) has been demonstrated to result in the diversification of the peripheral T-cell pool; however, there is no consensus regarding its utilisation as an indicator of response. Robert [ ] demonstrated that CTLA-4 blockade increased T-cell diversity in the blood while preserving clonality. Hopkins and Postow reported that patients with elevated pretreatment blood TCR diversity scores exhibited increased clinical benefits and increased survival rates with anti-CTLA-4 monotherapy [ , ]. Hogan suggested a correlation between increased TCR clonality and a poorer response to anti-CTLA-4 therapy, while demonstrating a better response to anti-PD-1 treatment and a longer progression-free survival (PFS). [ ]. Additionally, Foord et al. investigated the presence of γδ T-cell infiltration by comparing TCR changes in the peripheral blood, ascites and metastatic tissues from patients with advanced ovarian cancer [ ]. This approach demonstrated alterations in the immune environment of ovarian cancer. Subsequent analyses of a single-arm phase II clinical trial employing niraparib monotherapy in patients with advanced ovarian cancer who were Homologous Recombination Deficiency (HRD) positive (the NANT trial) revealed a substantial decrease in tumor-restricted TCR diversity and an increase in clonality [ ]. Table provides a comprehensive comparison of studies predicting immune reactivity using TCR. Further research in this area that is specifically focused on the characterisation of alterations within the TCR repertoire across diverse anatomical regions in ovarian cancer patients has the potential to enhance our comprehension of the disease process and facilitate the identification of individuals who are more susceptible to immunotherapy. 
The integration of TCR structure and detection methods pose significant challenges to the clinical application of TCR in tumor prediction and immune response. TCR repertoires exhibit extreme diversity, which is dynamically influenced by factors such as age, infection, and immunotherapy. This complicates the process of determining whether TCR changes are attributable to disease, physiological states, or external interventions. Moreover, TCR sequences are unable to directly predict antigen specificity, thereby complicating the identification and validation of disease-associated signals. Consequently, the identification of TCR biomarkers that are universally applicable, specific, and sensitive remains challenging. Moreover, in clinical translation, the absence of standardization in sample processing, sequencing platforms, and bioinformatics analysis workflows gives rise to suboptimal data comparability. Incomplete TCR-antigen correspondence databases limit functional interpretation, while existing studies suffer from small sample sizes and a lack of multicenter validation. Collectively, these factors constrain the clinical application and widespread adoption of TCR repertoire biomarkers.

## T-cell receptor-based immunotherapy strategies for ovarian cancer
Adoptive cell therapies (ACT) are a form of treatment that aims to target tumors by enhancing, reconstituting, or amplifying the immune system [ ]. This encompasses a range of cellular therapies, including CAR-T and TCR-T. CAR-T denotes chimeric antigen receptor T-cell immunotherapy. This technique employs gene editing to introduce chimeric antigen receptors (CARs) into T cells, thereby enabling them to recognize and activate against tumor cells. These CARs facilitate T cell specificity in identifying particular antigens on tumor cell surfaces, including glycoproteins and glycolipids, thus enabling precise targeting and eradication oftumorr cells. It has been established that CARs are capable of binding to specific surface antigens in the absence of HLA molecules [ ], thus obviating the necessity for activation of T cells and the subsequent exertion of their cytotoxic function. The capacity of CARs to function independently of CD3 or co-stimulatory molecules renders them suitable for genetic transduction into immune cells, including macrophages and natural killer (NK) cells [ ]. Nevertheless, given the limited surface antigen selection and low specificity, adverse effects and damage to normal tissues are frequently observed.
The TCR-T cell therapy procedure involves the extraction of the patient's T cells, followed by the introduction of the TCR gene (which expresses genes that target known, characteristic tumor-associated antigens) via in vitro genetic engineering techniques. The patient's body is subsequently infused with the modified TCR-T cells [ ]. Due to its inherent characteristics, the TCR facilitates the recognition of antigens, such as TSA and TAAs, which are derived from membrane-bound and intracellular proteins presented by MHC molecules.A notable aspect of TCR-T-cell activation is its requirement for significantly lower epitope densities, which is a feature that enables the detection of low-abundance antigens and enhances the sensitivity of recognition. The concurrent activity of the TCR with respect to its targets is a distinguishing feature that enables each TCR-T cell to recognise and eradicate multiple antigen-presenting tumor cells, thereby amplifying the efficacy of the immune response against these cells. Nevertheless, the antigen recognition process performed by TCR-T cells is confined to the human leukocyte antigen allele of the presenting epitope, which restricts the number of patients who can receive benefits from a specific TCR-T-cell therapy [ , ].
Currently, TCR-T cell therapy is primarily focused on three categories: TSA and TAAs are defined as antigens that are highly expressed by tumor cells but absent or weakly expressed in normal cells. Examples of TSA and TAAs include members of the melanoma-associated antigen (MAGE-A) family of proteins and the New York esophageal squamous cell carcinoma-1 (NY-ESO-1) antigen. However, clinical trials have revealed that TSA and TAAs expression cannot be entirely excluded in non-tumor tissues, often resulting in intolerable toxicity. A number of trials are currently underway that are screening PRAME and CTCFL as potential TCR targets in ovarian cancer [ ]. However, these trials have not yet progressed to the stage of clinical application. It has been established that tumor viral antigens, including HPV16 E6 and E7 [ ]proteins linked to vulvar, vaginal, and cervical cancers, can be expressed on the cell surface, thereby rendering them effective tumor-specific antigens. Nevertheless, given that human cancers caused by tumor viruses account for only 12% of cases [ ], the applicability of such antigens is limited. Thirdly, Mutant neoantigens are the consequence of nonsynonymous mutations associated with cancer-initiating genetic events or overall genetic instability [ ]. Research has indicated a strong correlation between KRAS (G12V) and KRAS (G12D) [ ]mutations and the development of pancreatic and colorectal cancers. However, further screening and development are required for the application of neoantigens in solid tumors. With the advancement of artificial intelligence technology, tools such as NetMHCIIpan, MixMHC2pred, DeepSeqPanII, and DeepNeoAG [ – ]have now been established for computing and predicting TCR epitopes and neoantigens. For instance, Zhao developed a novel model known as UniPMT for predicting the binding between antigenic peptides and MHC (P-M), between antigenic peptides and TCR (P–T), and between pMHC and TCR (P-M-T) [ ]. The aim of this study was to identify the key sites involved in pMHC and TCR binding.
CAR-T therapy has recently demonstrated considerable success in the treatment of hematological malignancies [ ]. However, the extant literature suggests that CAR-T cell therapy exhibits a comparatively diminished response rate in solid tumors in contrast to its efficacious remission rates in hematological cancers. The primary focus of ongoing clinical research is on gliomas and colorectal, cervical, pancreatic, and lung cancers, all of which are currently in Phase I or II clinical trial stages [ ]. Published data indicate that TCR-T therapy holds greater promise in the treatment of solid tumors due to its capacity to elicit responses against intracellular tumor antigens, which exhibit the highest specificity among those expressed by cancer cells.The majority of contemporary TCR-T-cell clinical trials for ovarian cancer have focused on MAGE-A4 and NY-ESO-1 antigen.The TCR-T-cell therapy known as afamitresgene autoleucel, which targets the antigen MAGE-A4, has received FDA approval for the treatment of synovial cell sarcoma subsequent to prior chemotherapy treatment [ ]. In contrast, in the ADP-A2M4 CD8 + targeted MAGE-A4 mid-ovarian cancer cohort, the overall remission rate (ORR) was observed to be 40%, with a median duration of remission of 17 weeks also being detected. Notably, a patient with plasmacytoid ovarian cancer achieved complete remission after the 8th week of treatment with ADP-A2M4 CD8 + SPEART cells [ ]. Importantly, Expression of NY-ESO-1 is typically limited to testicular germ cells and placental trophoblast cells. The absence or underexpression of this gene has been observed in normal adult somatic cells. The re-expression of the gene has been observed in many in many types of cancer, including ovarian cancer. Therefore, it is considered to be a potential therapeutic target for ovarian cancer [ ]. Indeed, this gene has now been demonstrated to be effectively targeted in clinical trials in patients with metastatic synovial cell sarcoma and melanoma. Furthermore, clinical trials are currently being performed that target mutation-associated neoantigens. Mutant neoantigens have yielded encouraging outcomes in clinical trials for solid cancers, including pancreatic cancer [ ]. Matsuda screened neoantigen-specific TCRs in ovarian cancer cell lines in vitro; however, they did not attempt clinical application [ ]. Both patients showed signs of clinical benefit from Hung's research, which examined the potential of personalized NeoAg TCR therapy for the treatment of ovarian cancer Nevertheless, the sample size is small, which restricts the conclusions that can be made [ ]. These outcomes have demonstrated the clinical importance of the targeting of neoantigens that mutate cancer drivers using TCR-T-cell therapy. Studies have shown that it is feasible to use Clustered Regularly Interspaced Short Palindromic Repeats (CRISPR) gene editing to create personalized TCR-T cells, thus avoiding the autoimmune risks associated with mismatches between endogenous and exogenous TCRs [ ]. The efficient introduction of engineered TCR sequences into T cells via non-viral methods—such as electroporation or the Sleeping Beauty transposon system—also avoids the potential insertion mutation risks inherent in viral vectors [ ]. The integration of CRISPR gene editing with non-viral TCR delivery methods has been shown to significantly enhance the safety and scalability of TCR-T cell therapies. Furthermore, ongoing clinical trials are investigating the targeting of cancer neoantigens and the application of personalized TCR-T-cell therapy in the context of ovarian cancer, such as NCT03412877、NCT05124743.
Currently, TCR-T cells are being studied in 17 ongoing clinical trials for patients with ovarian cancer. (Table  ). Notably, all of these trials are designated as phase I or phase I/II studies. The primary focus of the major findings involves safety and treatment response rates. Importantly, 14 of these results have not yet been published. Three principal outcomes with published results have been reported on the adverse effects and dose-limiting toxicity of TCR-T-cell therapy. Moreover, the adverse effects primarily encompass haematological and lymphatic disorders, including neutropenic fever, anaemia, and thrombocytopenia, as well as gastrointestinal reactions, such as abdominal pain and ascites. Other notable effects include immune system disorders (such as cytokine release syndrome) and infections. Compared with CAR-T therapy, TCR-T-cell therapy has a lower incidence of adverse reactions [ ]. The present study demonstrated the effectiveness of TCR-T-cell therapy in solid tumors, including those cancers that are unresponsive to current immunotherapies. For example, sarcomas have been observed to be particularly amenable to this treatment approach. The effectiveness of TCR-T-cell therapy in ovarian cancer is also expected to become realized. Moreover, the intricacy and deleterious consequences of this therapeutic strategy represent further challenges. 

## Challenges of TCR-T cell therapy in ovarian cancer
Although TCR-T cell therapy has shown promising results in clinical trials for certain solid tumors, such as melanoma, synovial sarcoma, and colorectal cancer, its application in ovarian cancer faces significant challenges. These challenges stem from a complex interplay of tumor-intrinsic factors (e.g.,antigen heterogeneity and immune evasion), the tumor microenvironment (TME), and limitations in T cell functionality. Together, these factors have led to slower-than-anticipated progress in preclinical research and the development of treatments [ ].
A hallmark of ovarian cancer is its pronounced intratumoral heterogeneity, wherein distinct malignant clones within the same tumor exhibit substantial variability in genetic mutations, transcriptional profiles, and antigen expression patterns [ ]. As a result, some tumor subclones may express high levels of specific TAAs or neoantigens derived from somatic mutations, whereas others within the same lesion may show minimal or no expression of these targets [ ]. This heterogeneity complicates the identification of universally applicable TCR targets. When the intended antigen is either not expressed or not properly presented by tumor cells, TCR-T cells fail to recognize and eliminate the malignancy [ ]. Consequently, TCR-T therapies targeting a single antigen often achieve efficacy only against a subset of tumor cells, leaving antigen-negative or low-expressing clones unaffected. These surviving clones can evade immune surveillance, proliferate, and ultimately drive treatment failure, tumor recurrence, and acquired resistance.While multi-antigen targeting strategies may mitigate these effects [ ], even broadly expressed antigens are susceptible to immune editing under the selective pressure exerted by TCR-T cells. Tumor cells can evade immune detection through various mechanisms, including downregulation or complete loss of MHC class I molecules, disruption of antigen processing machinery (e.g., TAP or β2-microglobulin deficiency), or genetic and epigenetic silencing of the target antigen itself [ ]. This phenomenon, commonly referred to as antigen escape, represents a major barrier to durable therapeutic responses.Encouragingly, certain TCR-T target antigens are modulated by epigenetic mechanisms. Treatment with DNA methyltransferase inhibitors or histone deacetylase (HDAC) inhibitors has been shown to restore the expression of such antigens, potentially enhancing T cell recognition and therapeutic efficacy [ , ]. Nevertheless, many tumor-specific neoantigens are highly patient-specific, necessitating personalized TCR design based on individual tumor genomic profiles. This requirement poses a formidable challenge to the scalability and broad applicability of TCR-T therapy in a clinical setting.
The therapeutic potency of TCR-T cells is fundamentally dictated by the affinity, specificity, and stability of the engineered TCR for its cognate pMHC complex. Suboptimally engineered TCRs—characterized by inadequate binding affinity or unintended off-target reactivity—can result in diminished antitumor activity and, in some cases, severe on-target, off-tumor toxicities [ ]. For instance, a landmark 2009 study by Johnson demonstrated objective responses in 30% and 19% of melanoma patients treated with MART-1 and gp100 targeted TCR-T cells, respectively. However, these therapies also elicited collateral damage by attacking normal melanocytes in the skin, eyes and ears [ ]. Similarly, in a 2013 trial reported by Morgan, TCR-T cells targeting the MAGE-A3 epitope KVAELVHFL cross-reacted with the structurally similar MAGE-A12 epitope (KMAELVHFL), which is expressed in neural tissues, leading to fatal outcomes in two patients [ ]. These cases underscore the critical need to identify “ideal targets”or “immunodominant neoantigens” that are not only broadly expressed and immunogenic but also indispensable for tumor cell survival. Ideally, such targets would engage TCRs with high affinity, exquisite specificity, and minimal off-target risk. While recent advances in high-throughput TCR screening, single-cell sequencing, and AI-driven neoantigen prediction hold promise for the discovery of such candidates, their clinical utility in ovarian cancer remains to be fully established [ ].
Ovarian cancer is widely recognized as a prototypical immunologically 'cold' tumor, characterized by a suppressed TME that significantly hinders T cell infiltration, activation, and effector function [ ]. Key features of the ovarian TME include low levels of chemokines that attract T cells, such as CXCL9 and CXCL10 [ ]; reduced tumor mutational burden (TMB); sparse infiltration of cytotoxic T lymphocytes; and an increased presence of immunosuppressive cell populations, including regulatory T cells (Tregs) and myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) [ ]. Tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) in the ovarian TME are often polarized towards an M2-like phenotype, facilitating immune evasion and tumor progression by secreting anti-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-10 and TGF-β [ ]. Furthermore, the overexpression of immune checkpoint molecules such as programmed death ligand 1 (PD-L1) on both tumor cells and infiltrating immune cells can engage inhibitory receptors (e.g.,PD-1 and CTLA-4) on TCR-T cells, leading to T cell exhaustion, impaired proliferation, reduced cytokine production, and, ultimately, diminished cytotoxic capacity [ ]. Together, these factors mean that single-agent TCR-T therapy is not effective enough to achieve meaningful clinical responses in ovarian cancer. Combinatorial approaches, such as co-administration with immune checkpoint inhibitors, costimulatory agonists, cytokine therapies or cancer vaccines, may help to reprogram the TME, enhance T cell trafficking and activation, and improve overall therapeutic outcomes [ – ].
The persistence and resistance to exhaustion of TCR-T cells in vivo are critical factors in determining their long-term anti-tumor efficacy. However, within the ovarian TME, TCR-T cells are susceptible to functional exhaustion and poor durability. Persistent antigens have been demonstrated to push T cells toward a terminally differentiated [ ], worn-out state when combined with immunosuppressive cytokines and metabolic stressors such as hypoxia, lactate buildup, and glucose deprivation. [ ]. This is characterized by the upregulation of inhibitory receptors such as PD-1 [ ], Lymphocyte activation gene3(LAG-3) [ ] and T cell immune receptor with Ig and ITIM domains(TIGIT) [ ], impaired memory formation, and reduced proliferative and cytotoxic capacities. Exhausted TCR-T cells secrete fewer effector cytokines (e.g., IFN-γ and TNF-α), proliferate less, and have a reduced ability to kill tumors, all of which are closely associated with early tumor relapse and treatment failure [ ]. Furthermore, the formation of long-lived, self-renewing memory T cell subsets, particularly stem cell-like memory T cells (Tscm) [ ], is often impaired in the hostile ovarian TME. This further limits the establishment of durable immune surveillance.To enhance the persistence and anti-exhaustion properties of TCR-T cells in ovarian cancer, several strategies are under active investigation.1) Cytokine support: IL-2, IL-7 and IL-15 have each been shown to promote T cell survival, expansion, and memory differentiation. IL-15 is particularly effective in supporting the generation and maintenance of Tscm and central memory T cells (Tcm), which are associated with superior persistence and anti-tumor efficacy [ , ].2) Genetic engineering: Knockout or inhibition of inhibitory receptors (e.g.,PD-1 and LAG-3) and incorporation of costimulatory domains (e.g., 4-1BB and CD28) can delay T cell exhaustion and enhance effector function [ , , ]. 3) T Cell Subset Optimization: Adopting less differentiated T cell subsets, such as naïve or Tscm populations, for transfer may improve engraftment, persistence and recall responses.
As shown in Table  , the clinical application of TCR therapy for ovarian cancer is constrained by multiple complex factors, including tumor heterogeneity, low expression of target antigens, antigen escape, an immunosuppressive microenvironment, and T-cell persistence and exhaustion. Overcoming these barriers will likely require a coordinated, multidimensional strategy integrating patient-specific target selection, microenvironment modulation, advanced T cell engineering, and rational combinatorial approaches. 

## Conclusion and outlook
In recent years, advances in T-cell receptor (TCR) sequencing technology have enabled the high-resolution profiling of TCR repertoires, establishing them as promising predictive biomarkers for ovarian cancer. TCR repertoires can dynamically reflect changes in the immune status of ovarian cancer patients and offer insights into their responsiveness to immunotherapy. Based on the observed alterations in T-cell infiltration within the ascites of patients with advanced tumors (as determined via TCR assays), there is potential for the creation of new therapeutic approaches for extensive peritoneal metastases or peritoneal tumors in advanced ovarian cancer.
Despite its promise, however, TCR sequencing faces several practical limitations that hinder its clinical translation. These include the absence of large-scale, ovarian cancer-specific databases; high technical requirements; cost barriers; and challenges in achieving reproducibility. Future clinical utility will depend on the establishment of comprehensive data networks and the broader commercialization of detection technologies.
In parallel, progress in TCR-based cell therapy for ovarian cancer has demonstrated its feasibility. Clinical trials targeting known tumor antigens (e.g., MAGE-A4 and NY-ESO-1) have validated this approach, and personalized strategies using neoantigen prediction to expand cognate T cells have shown promising results. The transition of TCR-T therapy from the laboratory to clinical application is still facing numerous challenges. These include the immunological characteristics of ovarian cancer itself, technical issues surrounding the widespread application of TCRs, and the high degree of variation between patients.
Future research must adopt multidimensional, personalized, and systematic strategies that integrate TCR prediction with TCR-T therapeutic protocols. This should involve exploring biomarker-based patient stratification schemes to identify those most likely to benefit from TCR-T therapy. Research efforts should focus on precision immunological interventions tailored to tumor characteristics specific to each patient, the development of novel T-cell subsets, and innovative clinical translation approaches. Ultimately, this will enhance treatment efficacy and survival outcomes for ovarian cancer patients.
