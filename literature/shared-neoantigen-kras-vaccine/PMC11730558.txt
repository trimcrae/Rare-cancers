## Abstract

## Background
TG02 is a peptide-based cancer vaccine eliciting immune responses to oncogenic codon 12/13 RAS mutations. This phase 1 clinical trial (NCT02933944) assessed the safety and immunological efficacy of TG02 adjuvanted by GM-CSF in patients with KRAS-mutant colorectal cancer.

## Methods
In the interval between completing CRT and pelvic exenteration, patients with resectable KRAS mutation-positive, locally advanced primary or current colorectal cancer, received 5–6 doses of TG02/GM-CSF. Immune response was defined as a positive delayed-type hypersensitivity or positive T cell proliferation assay response. Tumour biopsies were analysed for tumour-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs) and blood for CEA and ctDNA. TILs and tumouroids were cultured, characterised and tested for their killing efficacy.

## Results
Six patients with rectal cancer were recruited to evaluate TG02. Three patients experienced a total of 16 treatment-related adverse events; all grade 1. Four of the 6 patients (66.7 %) had at least one vaccine-induced TG02 immune response. Flow cytometry analysis showed high proportion of PD-1-expressing TILs in 2 of 3 patient specimens’ post-treatment. A partial to near complete pathological response was reported in 4 of 6 patients.

## Conclusions
This study demonstrated that TG02/GM-CSF was well tolerated and induced a vaccine specific systemic immune response in the majority of patients. Low numbers limit conclusive clinical outcome reporting. High PD-1 expression on post-treatment TILs encourages the addition of an immune checkpoint inhibitor to TG02 and potentially other studies of peptide vaccines in future studies.

## Introduction
Colorectal cancer (CRC) is the 3rd most common cancer and the 2nd leading cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide. It is estimated that nearly 2 million people will be diagnosed with colorectal cancer every year [ ]. Rectal cancer contributes 30 % of the total number of CRC; of those 60–70 % will be classified locally advanced rectal cancer (LARC), defined as stage II (T3-4, N0) or stage III (Tx N1-2) disease [ , , ].
Although the paradigm is changing, a current standard of care for patients presenting with LARC is neoadjuvant (radiotherapy ± chemotherapy) treatment (NAT) prior to definitive surgical resection. Using this approach, NAT can be administered as either long course chemoradiation (LCCRT), followed by an 8-12-week interval before surgery or short course radiotherapy (SCRT), followed by a short interval to surgery. The response rate to this version of NAT varies, with 15–20 % of patients achieving a complete pathological response (pCR), whilst the remainder of patients may achieve a partial response or no response at all [ , ]. NAT reduces the risk of local recurrence and disease-free survival but has not demonstrated improvements in overall survival (OS) [ ]. However, NAT followed by total mesorectal excision (TME) has dramatically reduced rates of local recurrence to less than 5 %. The 5-year OS of LARC patients undergoing NAT and TME ranges from 75 to 85 % [ ]. Despite these advances, systemic recurrence occurs in 20–30 % of LARC patients, i.e. distant failure [ , ]. This is likely due to systemic micrometastases which need to be eradicated to increase cure rates.
RAS mutations are one of the most frequent oncogenic alterations in human cancers, affecting 30–50 % of all CRC patients.
Despite being one of the most common oncogenes, KRAS was considered undruggable for a long time. Recent advances have led to the development of numerous small-molecule inhibitors, PROTACs, TCRs, and vaccines targeting KRAS G12D and G12V as well as pan-KRAS and pan-RAS inhibitors [ ]. While the advances in selective KRAS inhibitors are promising, the development of drugs that target several KRAS mutations are desirable to offer one treatment to all patients with KRAS mutations and not only the ones with a specific mutation. In contrast to inhibitors, KRAS vaccines offer the benefit of elucidating an immunological memory response that if successful, mediate a long-lasting protection without the need of ongoing medication.
TG02 is a novel cancer vaccine that targets malignancies harbouring the 8 most common RAS (codon 12/13, exon 2) oncogenic mutant proteins in CRC which are seen in approximately 30 % of LARC patients [ , , ]. A similar vaccine, TG01 targeting 7 RAS mutations, was studied in patients with resected RAS mutant pancreatic adenocarcinoma in combination with gemcitabine as adjuvant therapy. This combination therapy was well tolerated, with high levels of immune activation. OS and DFS compare favourably with published data for adjuvant gemcitabine monotherapy suggesting an additive effect of TG01 [ ].
RAS mutations in LARC have been correlated with increased tumour invasion, metastasis, resistance to the epidermal growth factor (EGFR) tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs), impaired anti-tumour immune response and low survival rate following NAT [ , , , , ]. When local recurrences occur after more than a 6–12 month treatment interval, NAT is often repeated although in this case using primarily LCCRT.
In these cases, the 8-12-week window between completion of chemoradiotherapy and salvage surgery offers an excellent opportunity to evaluate novel therapies without compromising standard of care. For this trial, TG02/GM-CSF was administered using this ‘window of opportunity’ trial design.
The aim of this study was to assess safety and immune response in a study designed to target KRAS mutant micrometastases and thereby reduce both local and distant recurrence. The design and results of this ‘window of opportunity’ study, including challenges and considerations for future trial designs, are discussed.

2.

## Participants and methods
2.1.

## Study design
This was a non-randomised open label exploratory study to investigate the safety of and induction of immune responses to TG02/GM-CSF vaccination. Participants received TG02/GM-CSF treatment in the time interval between the completion of CRT prior to surgery ( A). There was no delay to participants receiving the standard of care surgery.
 Participant flow, study timelines and interventions are illustrated in ; the 3 phases of the trial were as follows: (i) Screening Phase, (ii) Treatment Phase: participants underwent trial-related therapy over an eight-week period, during the interval prior to surgery at the end of week 8. Where surgery was delayed to up to week 14 due to logistic issues, treatment could extend to week 10, and (iii) Safety follow-up, until 4–6 weeks post-surgery.
The study was initially planned to have two parts: Part I was designed to investigate TG02/GM-CSF-treatment as monotherapy, and Part II was to evaluate TG02/GM-CSF-treatment in combination with pembrolizumab. Part I and Part II were planned to enrol approximately 10 participants each. With subsequent amendments, Part I was changed to recruit 6 participants but due to slow recruitment a decision was made not to initiate Part II. The study was opened across 5 high volume sites capable of pelvic exenteration across Australia (3 sites) and New Zealand (2 sites). All sites received institutional ethics approval.

2.2.

## Outcome measures
There were 2 primary outcomes: safety of the TG02/GM-CSF treatment and evaluation of the systemic TG02-specific immune responses and T cell infiltration within tumour specimens. The following secondary endpoints were examined: changes in immunological and pathological markers in tumour tissue, changes in fluorodeoxyglucose (FDG) positron emission tomography-computed tomography (PET-CT) images,[ ] changes in carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA) [ ]. Furthermore, changes in ctDNA and functionality of T cells in tumour tissue [ ] and peripheral blood were analysed as exploratory objectives.

2.3.

## Participants
Participants at least 18 years of age with locally recurrent or advanced colorectal adenocarcinoma, confirmed oncogenic KRAS exon 2, codon 12 or 13 mutations and suitable for radical resection (R0) were eligible for the study. Participants had to have acceptable laboratory test results and an Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) performance status (PS) of 0 or 1.
Patients who had previously received an anti-cancer vaccine or immune checkpoint inhibitor or an agent with known effect on the immune system were excluded. Patients with any other malignancies within the previous 3 years (except for adequately treated carcinoma of basal or squamous cell skin carcinoma or localised low-grade tumours considered cured and not requiring systemic therapy) or with a history of adverse reactions to peptide vaccines were also excluded.
More detailed patient inclusion, exclusion criteria and evaluations can be found in the Supplementary Section and in the Clinical Study Protocol [ ].

2.4.

## Chemoradiotherapy (CRT)
Participants underwent chemoradiation therapy ≤50.4 Gy in 28 daily fractions over 5 weeks, with concurrent 5FU 200 mg/m2/day, continuous infusion over 5.5 weeks or Capecitabine 825 mg/m2 bid, 5 days per week-on the days of the radiotherapy prior to consenting to this study.

2.5.

## Investigational product - TG02/GM-CSF
TG02 is a RAS-neoantigen peptide vaccine adjuvanted GM-CSF targeting the eight most frequent codon 12–13 RAS mutations where glycine in either codon 12 or 13 has been substituted as follows 12A, 12C, 12D, 12R, 12S, 12V and 13D. TG02 was manufactured according to Good Manufacturing Practice (GMP) standard, as a sterile dry white solid powder consisting of the eight peptides in equal amounts (weight net peptide) (Baccinex SA, Courroux, Switzerland/Bachem Distribution Services, GmbH). Detailed information on each peptide is provided in the Sup. . Recombinant human GM-CSF expressed in E. coli (Amoytop Biotech Co, Ltd, Xiamen, China) was used as an adjuvant immunomodulator.
 
2.6.

## TG02/GM-CSF preparation and administration
GM-CSF (0.03 mg) was intradermally administered in the upper arm at weeks 1, 2, 3, 4, and 6. After 15–30 min TG02 (total 0.8 mg; 0.1 mg/peptide) was administered intradermally in the same area. If surgery was delayed beyond 8 weeks, a further TG02/GM-CSF-treatment was given on week 10 (if surgery was scheduled after week 10) ( b).
The TG02 dosing and the administration time points were based on the studies of the precursor vaccine TG01 that determined that 0.1 mg of each peptide (0.7 mg total per dose) was appropriate to generate an immune response and that mutation-specific T cell responses were induced in cancer patients after 4–6 weeks of vaccination [ , ].

2.7.

## TG02 delayed-type hypersensitivity (DTH) skin test
As a DTH test, 0.8 mg TG02 was administered intradermally (without prior administration of GM-CSF) in the lower area of the contralateral arm to evaluate the immunological response at weeks 1, 4, 6, 8 and if required at week 10. The TG02 DTH dose was administered 30 min before the TG02-treatment and patients were observed for at least 30 min after all TG02-treatments [ ].

2.8.

## Primary efficacy variables
A systemic immune response was defined as having a positive in vivo DTH and/or a positive in vitro T cell proliferation response at least once during the treatment period. The change in intra-tumour T cells in resected tumour tissue at surgery compared to the pre TG02-treatment core biopsy was also evaluated [ ].
(a)Delayed Type Hypersensitivity (DTH) skin reaction: DTH tests were performed in order to demonstrate whether a TG02 KRAS mutation-specific immune response had been elicited. The DTH skin reaction assessment was performed 48 h ( ± 4 h) after each TG02 DTH administration, and recorded by the patient in a study-specific diary. Patients were instructed by the study staff on how to record and document the DTH response. The DTH test was considered positive if the area of the skin reaction had an average diameter of ≥5 mm at the 48 h ( ± 4 h) assessment.
(b)T cell Analyses: 48 mL blood was collected in BD Vacutainer® CPT™ Cell Preparation tubes with Sodium Citrate at baseline (week 1) and week 8. If surgery was delayed to after week 10, a third sample was taken as close as possible up to the date of surgery. All samples were processed and stored under appropriate conditions until analysed in batches to minimise day to day assay variability. The PBMCs were analysed for the presence of TG02-specific T cells using a proliferation assay and vaccine-specific peptides as described previously [ ]. T cell responses were considered positive if the stimulation index (SI) was ≥2, indicating an increase in proliferation of TG02-specific T cells after stimulation with TG02 compared with unstimulated cells.
(c)Tumour biopsy assessment: Tumour samples for assessment of immune response were taken at the time points defined above. The tissue samples were fixed in 10 % neutral buffered formalin post collection and processed for histological review as per the respective site's standard procedure. These samples were used for pathological review, multiplex immunohistochemistry (MIHC) analysis of various immune cell infiltrates.

2.11.

## Statistical power consideration and determination of sample size
As this was an exploratory study, no formal sample size calculation has been performed. However, it was estimated that a sample size of 10 subjects was pragmatic and sufficient to explore the potential effect of TG02/GM-CSF to induce immune responses and to assess safety [ ]. Patients’ disease and survival status were determined by a review of the medical record.

2.12.

## Analysis of safety
Safety was assessed by incidence and severity of adverse events as per NCI Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE) (v4.03: June 14, 2010), and by changes from baseline in laboratory variables, physical examination, vital signs and ECOG performance status. A treatment-emergent adverse event (TEAE) was defined as an AE with start date/time on or after the first administration of investigational product [ ].

3.

## Results
3.1.

## Recruitment and trial progress
The first 3 patients were enrolled in a sequential manner with a minimum lag time of 4 weeks between dosing of the first 3 subjects to ensure an acceptable safety profile [ ]. The safety data collected during the first 4 weeks for these first 3 patients were reviewed by a Safety Steering Committee (SSC) and, based on no incidence of unacceptable toxicities, it was recommended that further patients could be enrolled. On completion of Part I (6 patients), further recruitment was ceased due to slow accrual.

3.2.

## Patient demographics
The patient demographics are summarised in . Six rectal cancer patients were recruited in Part I across three centres. The majority of patients were male, with recurrent disease. Nodal sites were the predominant site of metastases.

3.3.

## Treatment exposure
Treatment exposure is summarised in Sup. . Approximately 13 weeks passed between the commencement of the TG02/GM-CSF vaccination and surgical resection. Patients received between 5 and 6 injections of TG02/GM-CSF and no patients had their dose discontinued or delayed due to toxicity. Median duration of exposure to TG02/GM-CSF was 64 days.
 
3.4.

## Toxicity
All patients experienced at least 1 TEAE during the study. A total of 32 TEAEs were reported; the majority (28 of 32) were Grade 1, 2 were Grade 2 and 2 were Grade 3 in severity. The 2 Grade 3 TEAEs were reported as SAEs; these were urinary tract infection and post-operative anastomotic leak, both were considered unrelated to the TG02-treatment. No patients experienced Grade 4/life-threatening or greater TEAEs. No TEAEs led to discontinuation of treatment, withdrawal from study, or death. Three patients experienced a total of 16 TEAEs that were considered definitely related to the study treatment ( ); 12 of the total 16 TEAEs, all Grade 1, were considered related to TG02 and GM-CSF and were reported in 2 patients (33.3 %) and 4 of the 16 treatment-related TEAEs were considered related to TG02 only. There were no clinically significant (≥ Grade 3) haematology or biochemical laboratory-based TEAEs.

3.5.

## Pathological responses and patient disposition
Overall all 6 patients went to surgery. A partial to near complete pathological response was reported in 4 patients ( ). In terms of outcomes, 3 patients are alive to the last point of contact, 1 with disease. Of the 2 patients without disease 1 patient had recurrent isolated disease, which was removed by surgery. The other 3 patients expired with disease.
 
3.6.

## Systemic vaccine-specific immune response
Four of the 6 patients (66.7 %) had at least 1 vaccine-induced TG02 immune response (defined as a patient who had at least 1 positive Delayed-Type Hypersensitivity (DTH) and/or at least 1 positive T cell response).
In terms of the individual components.
(a)DTH skin test: Four patients (66.7 %) had at least 1 positive DTH skin reaction over the entire study period. Of these, 1 patient had a positive DTH response at baseline, but not at post-vaccination time points, 1 patient had no response at baseline but at one post-vaccination time point and 2 patients a positive DTH skin reaction at baseline and at all 3 post-vaccination time points ( ).
(b)Presence of TG02-specific T cells in peripheral blood: 3 of 6 patients (50.0 %) had at least 1 positive T cell response over the entire study period. Of these, 1 patient had 1 positive response at week 8 (Stimulation Index (SI) 42.8) but not at baseline and week 10–14, 1 patient had 2 positive responses at week 8 (SI 36.6) and 10–14 (SI 31.4) but not at baseline and 1 patient had no response at baseline but a very strong response (SI 262.8) at week 8 ( ).
 
3.7.

## Intratumour immune responses
Due to tumour location, baseline biopsies could only be collected from 2 patients (#2 and #6). Because of the poor quality of the baseline biopsy (patient #2) and lack of tumour cells in the post-vaccination sample (patient #6) no further analysis comparing baseline and post-vaccination intra-tumour lymphocyte infiltration and activation was conducted. However, post-vaccination tumour tissues of 3 patients (Peter Mac site) were analysed for different immune subtypes by flow cytometry. No baseline samples were analysed, as collection was not possible or limited tissue was available. In patient #1 and #2 samples high proportions of cytotoxic CD8+ T cells, expressing high levels of immune checkpoint protein PD-1 (75.6 and 92.6 %) and memory marker CD45RO (88.1 % and 90.3 %) were detected ( ). Moreover, the same two patients had high levels of immunosuppressive myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSC) ranging from 12.3 to 51.1 % of all infiltrating immune cells ( ).
 
3.8.

## Cytokine analysis of plasma samples
Patient plasma samples were collected at baseline (week 1), week 8 and if relevant at week 10–14 and were analysed for IL-5, IL-13, IL-2, IL-6, IL-9, IL-10, IFN-γ, TNF-α, IL-17A, IL-17F, IL-4, IL-21 and IL-22. No clinically relevant differences were observed (data not shown).

3.9.

## Characterisation of PBMC by flow cytometry
PBMCs of 4 patients (Peter Mac site) were isolated at baseline (week 1), week 8 and if relevant at week 10–14 and were characterised by flow cytometry. No clinically relevant differences were observed (data not shown).

3.10.

## Changes in FDG-PET uptake
Over time, no significant changes in FDG-PET uptake within the tumour sites from baseline to pre-surgery were observed, implying a lack of treatment effect on metabolic uptake of 18F-FDG (data not shown).

3.11.

## Changes in CEA values over time
Overall 4 patients CEA values were below 5 ng/mL and 2 patients below 10 ng/mL. The changes in CEA values relative to the baseline were small ranging from a mean percentage change of −6.37 % (N = 6) at 8 weeks and +13.75 % (N = 3) at 10–14 weeks (data not shown).

3.12.

## Circulating tumour DNA (ctDNA)
ctDNA was isolated from the patient plasma and analysed for wild type (wt) and mutant (mut) KRAS. The observed concentrations and fractional abundance of ctDNA are summarised in Sup. . Four patients (66.7 %) had an increase, 1 patient (#6) had a decrease and 1 patient (#3) had no relevant change in mutant KRAS ctDNA levels post-vaccination.

3.13.

## KRAS status tumouroids
DNA was isolated from post-treatment #1 and #2 tumouroids. In both patients tumouroids mutant KRAS copies were detected. In patient #1 tumouroids, the copy ratio of KRAS mutant to wild type was approximately 2, and for patient #2 the ratio equal to 1 (data not shown).

3.14.

## MHC class 1 and PD-L1 expression of tumouroids
PD-L1 and MHC class 1 (HLA-ABC) expression of cultivated tumouroids with and without IFN-y stimulation were determined by flow cytometry (Sup. A). The majority of tumouroid cells of patient #1 and #2 expressed MHC class I, but not PD-L1. After stimulation with IFN-y both tumouroid cultures showed an increased expression of MHC class 1 and the majority of cells expressed high levels of PD-L1 (Sup. A).

3.15.

## Functionality of T cells in tumour tissue
Overall 3 patients post-treatment tumour biopsies (Peter Mac site only) were used to culture tumouroids as well as tumour-infiltrating-lymphocytes (TIL), which was successful in 2 patients. Tumouroids and TILs were co-cultured to access their killing efficacy. Due to limited amount of baseline tumour material, no comparison analysis regarding killing efficacy was performed. TILs isolated from patient #1 post-vaccine tumour tissue were extremely efficient in killing the tumouroids. Patient #2 TILs were able to kill tumouroids but to a lesser extent than observed in patient #1 (Sup. B).
FACS analysis of the cultured TILs of patient #1 and #2 revealed decreased immune checkpoint PD-1 expression on cultured cytotoxic CD8+ T cells and as expected, MDSC were markedly reduced after in vitro expansion (Sup. ). Moreover, less Tregs were observed in both cultures. For patient #2 CD4+ TILs became the dominant cell population after in vitro expansion, a phenomenon which we observe if TILs required longer than 2-week in vitro expansion (unpublished data).

4.

## Discussion
In this study, the cancer vaccine TG02 (combined with adjuvant GM-CSF), covering the 8 most common RAS codon 12/13 exon 2, oncogenic mutant proteins, was evaluated in patients with recurrent rectal cancer in a ‘window of opportunity study’ between the completion of CRT and resection.
Because of the low patient numbers, it must be noted that the results of this study are descriptive and hypothesis generating in nature. However, we demonstrated, that the planned TG02/GM-CSF vaccinations (5–6 per patient) were deliverable without delay or discontinuation of the treatment due to toxicity. The TEAEs related to the vaccination were not unexpected and all Grade 1 in severity, which resolved with no intervention. Our study proves that it is practical to assess novel therapies between the completion of CRT and surgical resection, but also highlights the importance of improving patient selection criteria to ensure achievement of set recruitment numbers and access to biopsies for translational studies.
Previous studies have shown that ctDNA levels post neoadjuvant CRT are strong predictors of pathological response and recurrence in LARC patients [ , ]. In our study KRAS ctDNA changes between baseline (post CRT, before TG02 vaccination) and last analysis before surgery (post TG02-vaccination) correlated with pathological response and time to recurrence. Thus, ctDNA analysis proved to be a good indicator of clinical response and may be worth considering in future trial designs.
As observed in the trial conducted in pancreatic cancer patients (92–95 %), de novo KRAS-specific T cell responses were observed in the majority of patients (4 of the 6 patients, 67 %) [ ]. Mutant KRAS-specific T cells have been identified in patients but also healthy individuals and may explain the baseline DTH responses, which were detected in 3 out of 6 patients in this study [ , , ].
The study was designed to assess a baseline and post-vaccination biopsy for comparison of immune cell infiltration and activation. The small patient cohort in addition to the lack of tumour cells in the post-vaccination specimen made comparisons difficult. Hence, due to these limitations we were unable to include a meaningful analysis about changes in immune cell infiltration in this study. Our experience highlights the importance of selecting patients with biopsy-accessible tumours and collecting post-vaccination biopsies at an earlier time point.
Flow cytometric analysis of post-vaccine specimens revealed high infiltration of CD8+ T cells, high expression of PD-1 in 2 out of 3 patients analysed and the presence of immunosuppressive MDSC, which are a hallmark of KRAS mutant cancers [ , , ]. The high proportion of cytotoxic (CD8+) T cells to T helper cells (CD4+FoxP3-) is notable. KRAS mutations in CRC have been associated with suppressed immune pathways and immune infiltration, in particular a lower cytotoxic CD8+ T cell response [ ]. The high CD8+ T cell infiltration in patient #1 and #2 may imply a treatment-induced recruitment of CD8+ cytotoxic T cells. However, it is unclear if the infiltration of cytotoxic T cells and the expression of PD-1 was induced by CRT or the TG02 vaccine because of baseline specimens were not available for FACS analysis.
Post-treatment cultivated tumouroids of those patients carried the KRAS mutation, were proficient in antigen presentation and in vitro expanded post-treatment TILs were able to kill matched tumouroids efficiently. However, they were not able to eliminate the cancer cells in vivo in the two corresponding patients. We hypothesise that the suppressive tumour microenvironment in vivo (MDSC and high expression of PD-1 on T cells), which was not present in our in vitro assay, suppressed cytotoxicity in the 2 patients. Our results indicate that co-therapy with an immune checkpoint inhibitor and MDSC targeting therapies may present potential approaches to increase clinical response rates.

5.

## Conclusion
This study demonstrated that TG02/GM-CSF vaccination is safe to use between CRT and surgery and induces an appropriate immune response in RAS mutant rectal cancer. The ‘window of opportunity’ between the completion of CRT and surgical resection has the potential to assess novel therapies for rectal cancer supported by translational studies from tumour and peripheral tissues. Participant numbers, selection and recruitment into this type of ‘window of opportunity’ study and biopsy collection time points require optimisation.
Circio (formerly known as Targovax ASA) is aiming to combine the power of mutRAS vaccination and oncolysis by using their oncolytic adenovirus platform ONCOS-102 as a carrier for their mutRAS peptides. Future studies in larger cohorts of patients will show if this strategy will be successful in raising a clinically relevant immune response in RAS mutant cancer patients.

## Clinical practice points

•This study revealed that the novel ‘window of opportunity’ trial design is feasible and that the vaccination administered is safe.
•Whether the TG02/GM-CSF vaccine alone confers a survival benefit remains unclear and requires analysis in a larger cohort.
•The high expression of PD-1 on CD8+ TILs encourages the combination of TG02/GM-CSF with an immune checkpoint inhibitor in future studies.
•The presence of immunosuppressive MDSC in the tumour microenvironment will require attention and may be targeted by chemotherapy or targeted therapy.
