Distinct sets of molecular characteristics define tumor-rejecting neoantigens
".

## Abstract

## Background
Challenges in predicting which neoantigens mediate tumor rejection limit the efficacy of neoantigen vaccines to treat cancers, especially for cancers with a high mutational burden like cutaneous squamous cell carcinoma (cSCC). Only a small portion of neoantigens prioritized by current methods elicit effective T cell responses, demonstrating the critical need for improved criteria for the prediction of tumor-rejecting neoantigens.

## Methods
Publicly available human cSCC datasets were used to assess the frequency of shared mutations between patients. A transplantable ultraviolet light-induced mouse model of cSCC was generated. The mutational signature and driver mutations in the mouse model were compared with human tumors. Neoantigens were prioritized in the mouse model, and tumor-rejecting neoantigens were identified through (enzyme-linked immunosorbent spot (ELISpot) and in vivo prophylactic vaccination. Binding of the neoantigens and corresponding wild-type peptides to major histocompatibility complex (MHC) class I was determined. Structural modeling of peptide:MHC complexes was performed to assess for changes in structural characteristics of the neoantigens relative to the wild-type peptides.

## Results
A minority of human cSCC tumors shared neoantigens. The mouse cSCC model recapitulated the mutational signature and driver mutations found in human disease and was constrained by CD8 T cells. Two MHC class I neoantigens were identified in the mouse model that constrained cSCC growth. One tumor-rejecting neoantigen exhibited improved MHC binding, and the other had increased solvent accessibility of the mutated residue, compared with wild-type. Across known neoantigens that do not impact MHC binding, increased exposure of the mutated residue distinguished tumor-rejecting from non-immunogenic neoantigens.

## Conclusions
Given the paucity of shared mutations, this work supports the need for personalized neoantigen vaccines in cSCC. To facilitate further discovery, we provide a clinically relevant mouse cSCC model with two defined neoantigens that mediate tumor rejection. Structural changes in the exposure of features that promote T cell receptor recognition defined tumor-rejecting neoantigens. Incorporation of structural modeling to predict changes in T cell receptor accessibility is anticipated to improve the selection of neoantigens for inclusion in personalized cancer vaccines.

## WHAT IS ALREADY KNOWN ON THIS TOPIC
Neoantigen-based immunotherapies hold promise in the treatment of cancer, but accurately predicting which neoantigens mediate tumor rejection, especially in high-burden cancers like cutaneous squamous cell carcinoma (cSCC), remains challenging.

## WHAT THIS STUDY ADDS
We evaluated the neoantigen landscape in human cSCC and found that shared neoantigens were rare, highlighting the need for personalized neoantigen-based immunotherapies.
We created a transplantable, ultraviolet light-induced cSCC mouse model which recapitulates the mutational signature and driver mutations found in human disease.
In this cSCC mouse model, we identified two tumor-rejecting neoantigens. One neoantigen improved major histocompatibility complex (MHC) binding compared with the wild-type peptide. For the other neoantigen, the predicted structure presented to the T cell receptor differed.
Increased solvent accessibility of the mutated neoantigen residue in the predicted neoantigen:MHC structure distinguished known tumor-rejecting neoantigens from non-immunogenic neoantigens.

## HOW THIS STUDY MIGHT AFFECT RESEARCH, PRACTICE OR POLICY
Incorporation of structural modeling to predict T cell receptor recognition is anticipated to improve the selection of neoantigens for inclusion in personalized cancer vaccines.

## Background
 Neoantigens are tumor-specific mutated peptides with the potential to elicit T cell-mediated tumor destruction. Neoantigen vaccines can improve clinical outcomes in patients with solid malignancies. , However, the efficacy of neoantigen vaccines is limited by the challenge of identifying neoantigen(s) that mediate tumor rejection. Selection of immunogenic neoantigens is especially difficult in tumors with a high mutational burden, such as skin cancers which contain thousands of missense mutations. In high mutational burden tumors, neoantigens are often prioritized based on the prediction of improved major histocompatibility complex (MHC) presentation, for example, binding affinity and stability of the neoantigen:MHC complex and neoantigen expression. While MHC binding affinity and stability of neoantigens can be predicted with reasonable accuracy, predicting immunogenicity remains much less accurate, as only a small percentage of prioritized neoantigens generate T cell responses. , Furthermore, only a portion of the neoantigens that elicit T cell responses mediate tumor rejection. , Thus, there is a need to better define characteristics of neoantigens which mediate tumor rejection.
Cutaneous squamous cell carcinoma (cSCC) is a high mutational burden tumor and thus an ideal cancer to identify principles to improve the selection of neoantigens to target in neoantigen-based immunotherapies. In this study, we developed a transplantable cSCC murine model from solar ultraviolet (UV) light-induced tumors. This immunogenic model recapitulates the high burden of UV signature mutations and shares driver genes with human disease. Among the predicted neoantigens, two tumor-rejecting neoantigens were identified. Compared with the wild-type peptides, one tumor-rejecting neoantigen exhibits improved MHC binding, and the other has an increase in the predicted solvent accessibility of the mutated residue as determined by high-resolution three-dimensional structural modeling. For neoantigens that do not alter MHC binding, a change in solvent accessibility of the mutated residue predicted by structural modeling distinguishes tumor-rejecting neoantigens from non-immunogenic neoantigens across MHC alleles and cancer models. This study highlights the importance of structural and physical features anticipated to facilitate T cell receptor (TCR) recognition as an added criterion in the prioritization of vaccine candidates in cSCC and other cancers.

## Methods
Additional methods are provided in .

## Mice
BALB/cAnNCrl wild-type and BALB/c CAnN.Cg-Foxn1nu/Crl athymic mice were purchased from Charles River Laboratories or bred for in vivo experiments. Mice were maintained in microisolator cages in the vivarium facility at the University of Arizona College of Medicine - Phoenix in accordance with local and national guidelines.

## Cell lines
Solar UV light-induced tumors were generated in BALB/c mice as described. The central portion of each tumor was used for histological diagnosis, and the remaining portions were used to create cell lines. Tumors were digested with 2 mg/mL collagenase IV (Worthington Biochemical Corporation) by shaking for 20 min at 37°C, then ground through a strainer. The remaining tissue on the strainer, as well as the single cell flowthrough, was separately adapted to cell culture. The cSCC cell lines derived from primary tumors were then cloned via limiting dilution. To generate cSCC1.1.1, a wild-type BALB/c mouse was injected with 5×106 cSCC1.1 cells; the tumor was harvested, and cells were adapted to culture and cloned via limiting dilution.
T2-Dd and T2-Ld cell lines were provided by Dr Peter Cresswell; T2-Kd cell lines were provided by Dr Joyce Solheim. 

## Identification of shared mutations in human cSCC
Somatic variants from 88 cSCC tumors across 10 datasets were obtained from Chang and Shain. Somatic variants from an additional 61 intermediate to high-risk cSCC tumors were obtained from Nassir et al. These somatic variants were identified using the methods described in the Genome_GPA v5.0.3 algorithm (formerly called TREAT). Analyses of whole exome sequencing (WES) and RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) data were performed with the High Performance Computing resources provided by the University of Arizona and Arizona State University (https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3569951.3597573). To ensure high-fidelity calls, the somatic variants from Nassir et al were filtered to include only those identified by a consensus of GATK Mutect2 and Strelka2 as described. Shared mutations were defined as any mutations resulting in the same residue change, regardless of the specific nucleotide change to account for codon degeneracy, and detected in both datasets. Putative driver mutations were annotated using cBioPortal. 

## Binding predictions for shared mutations in human cSCC
Human leukocyte antigen (HLA) types were identified for each tumor from the Nassir et al dataset using Polysolver. Binding was predicted with NetMHCpan4.0 for patient-specific HLA alleles for the Nassir et al dataset. Binding mutations were defined as those with at least one 9mer with a predicted binding affinity <500 nM. For patients for whom we could not determine the HLA types, binding was calculated for the two most common HLA-A, HLA-B, and HLA-C alleles for European individuals, identified from the BeTheMatch registry (https://network.nmdp.org/services-support/bioinformatics-immunobiology/haplotype-frequencies/high-resolution-hla-alleles-and-haplotypes-in-the-us-populations-as-of-2006, access date: 23 August 2023). 

## WES and RNA-seq
WES at 80× coverage and RNA-seq with 20 million read pairs were performed at the Yale University Center for Genome Analysis. Libraries were prepared using the KAPA Hyper kit (Roche Diagnostics) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Exome enrichment was done using SureSelect XT Mouse All Exon kit (Agilent) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Library concentration and quality control were performed using quantitative PCR (Roche Diagnostics) and Tapestation (Agilent). Samples were sequenced in a 2×100 bp paired-end setting on a NovaSeq 6000 system (Illumina) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. WES and RNA-seq FASTQ files were visualized for quality using FastQC (https://www.bioinformatics.babraham.ac.uk/projects/fastqc/) and trimming was performed with bbduk (sourceforge.net/projects/bbmap/).

## Comparison of mutational profile in human and murine cSCC
To ensure that any differences in the human and murine cSCC profile were not attributable to differences in the pipeline, only human data analyzed with a comparable pipeline was used for comparison to the murine cell line. The human data included was from Nassir et al (GSE284467 and PRJNA1202062), Ji et al (GSE144237), and Chitsazzadeh et al (obtained directly from researchers). 

## Comparison of the driver mutations in human and murine cSCC
A list of known driver genes in cSCC was obtained from Chang and Shain. The percentage of human tumors containing a missense or nonsense mutation in a driver mutation was calculated across all data from Nassir et al and Chang et al. The murine cell lines were queried for missense or nonsense mutations in this set of driver genes.

## Tumor transplantation
To establish tumors, 100 µL of cells were injected intradermally into the right flank of 5–10 week old wild-type or athymic BALB/c mice. The number of cells injected is indicated in figures or figure legends. Tumor volume was calculated using the formula volume = (L×W×H×π)/6. Tumor volume (mean±SEM) over time is displayed using last observation carried forward.

## Flow cytometry
Flow cytometry was performed on the LSRII (BD Biosciences), Fortessa (BD Biosciences), or NovoCyte Quanteon (Agilent) and analysis was performed on FlowJo V.10.7.1 (BD Biosciences) or NovoExpress software (Agilent).

## Antibody depletions
Mice were intraperitoneally injected with 10 mg/kg of anti-CD8α (clone 2.43; Bio X Cell) and/or anti-CD4 (clone GK1.5; Bio X Cell), to deplete T cells, or rat IgG2b isotype control (clone LTF-2; Bio X Cell). Unless stated otherwise, mice were treated with depleting antibodies on days −1 and +3 relative to tumor challenge, and weekly thereafter.

## Vaccinations
For prophylactic vaccination with irradiated tumor cells, cells were irradiated with 10–80 Gy using an X-RAD 320 irradiator (Precision X-Ray). After irradiation, cells were intradermally injected into the left flank of mice at 14 and 7 days, or 7 days, prior to tumor challenge. The tumor cells that were irradiated and the number of irradiated cells that were injected are indicated in the figures or figure legends. For prophylactic vaccination with peptides, mice were intradermally injected with 50 µg of peptide and/or 100 µg high molecular weight VacciGrade polyinosinic-polycytidylic acid (poly(I:C)) (Invivogen) 14 and 7 days prior to tumor challenge.

## Neoantigen prioritization in murine cSCC cell lines
Somatic variants were first confirmed by presence in at least one RNA-seq replicate using GATK ASEReadCounter. Neoantigens were then prioritized based on predicted MHC binding, using the NetMHCpan4.1 suite, and variant allele-specific mRNA expression. Neoantigens were prioritized with a combination of binding affinity per cent rank, which predicts the interaction between MHC alleles and neoantigens using IC50 data, and the eluted ligand per cent rank, which predicts the likelihood of neoantigens from being eluted from HLA alleles using mass spectrometry. Neoantigens were filtered for a binding affinity per cent rank and eluted ligand per cent rank under 0.5% for MHC class I alleles. RNA-seq data underwent transcriptome assembly and read count quantifications with Salmon V.0.11.3, using the GRCm38 reference transcriptome. mRNA expression was quantified in units of transcripts per million and averaged across all replicates for each sample. Variant allele-specific expression was quantified by multiplying transcript-level expression by the ratio of variant reads to total reads as quantified by GATK ASEReadCounter.

## Enzyme-linked immunosorbent spot
Tumors and tumor-draining lymph nodes (TDLNs) were harvested from mice 10 days after tumor challenge, and splenocytes were harvested from age-matched naïve mice to serve as antigen presenting cells (APCs). Live cells from tumors, TDLNs, and naïve lymph nodes (LNs) were first isolated using the negative selection Annexin V Dead Cell Removal Kit (STEMCELL). Then, CD4 T cells were isolated using the Mouse CD4 Positive Selection Kit II (STEMCELL), and the flowthrough was used to isolate CD8 T cells using the Mouse CD8α Positive Selection Kit II (STEMCELL). All EasySep isolation kits were used according to the manufacturer’s instructions. T cells were co-cultured with individual peptides and APCs for 48 hours in mouse interferon (IFN)-γ pretreated enzyme-linked immunosorbent spot (ELISpot) plates. Mycobacterium tuberculosis H2-Dd-binding peptide (GGPHAVYLL); influenza hemagglutinin H2-Kd-binding peptide (IYSTVASSL); and β-galactosidase H2-Ld-binding peptide (TPHPARIGL), were used as controls. ELISpot plates were developed following the manufacturer’s protocol for the ELISpot Plus: Mouse IFN-γ (ALP) kit (Mabtech). The plates were dried overnight. Spots were quantified using an AID Classic ELISpot reader (Autoimmun Diagnostika).

## MHC class I stabilization and relative binding affinity using T2 cells
The stabilization of BALB/c-specific MHC class I alleles due to peptide binding was determined using T2 cells, as described. In brief, T2 cells were harvested, plated at 2×105 cells per well, and incubated for 24 hours at 26°C. After 24 hours, cells were treated with 10 µg/mL brefeldin A (Medchemexpress) and 100 µg/mL of peptide. Plates were then incubated at 37°C for 1, 2, or 4 hour(s); cells at the zero-hour time point were not incubated with brefeldin A or peptide. To determine the relative peptide:MHC binding affinities, similar to above, T2 cells were incubated for 24 hours at 26°C and then treated with brefeldin A and a peptide titration for 4 hours at 37°C. In both cases, cells were kept on ice following the completion of the 37°C incubation. Cells were stained for cell surface MHC class I expression.

## Sequence-based and structural modeling approaches for TCR recognition
Tumor-rejecting neoantigens were identified from existing mouse models and included in the analysis if the precise epitope had been individually tested for the ability to elicit tumor destruction. All non-immunogenic neoantigens from this study were used as the comparison group. Peptides were annotated as either having mutations in anchor or non-anchor residues. Anchor residues were defined as the locations with the most conserved residues from the NetMHCpan binding motifs. The proportion of tumor-rejecting and non-immunogenic neoantigens with a mutation to a phenylalanine, tryptophan, tyrosine, leucine, or isoleucine was calculated for all neoantigens with a mutation in-between the annotated anchor residues. The cross-reactivity distance was calculated as described. Since the cross-reactivity distance was trained on human data with HLA alleles that may have different anchor positions and only on 9mer peptides, we employed the amino acid substitution matrix which considers both the size and hydrophobicity of the mutated and wild-type residues and omitted the amino acid position factor. Structural models of peptide:MHC complexes for all neoantigens were determined using TFold, a peptide:MHC-focused implementation of Alphafold2, as described. The three-dimensional conformations of modeled peptides were scored as an average of the predicted local-distance difference test across peptide residues and subtracted from 100. The lowest scores for each output were used to identify models most suitable for subsequent analysis. Overall, confidence scores ranged from 4.01 to 20.66 and 3.67 to 36.54 for negative and positive classes, respectively. We observed that for a subset of the models, the C-terminal residue of the peptide (pΩ) did not properly occupy the pocket of the MHC molecule. Therefore, all models were visually inspected, and those models with pΩ extruding from the groove were excluded from analysis. Solvent accessible surface areas (SASA) of the peptide in the MHC binding groove were calculated using the Mol* Viewer using a probe radius of 1.4 Å. Neoantigens were split into groups based on their differential agretopic index (DAI), which was calculated as the ratio of the predicted wild-type and mutant peptide dissociation constants. The difference between the SASA of the mutant and corresponding wild-type residues was compared between tumor-rejecting neoantigens and non-immunogenic neoantigens in both categories using a Wilcoxon rank-sum test. The optimal DAI cut point was selected by comparing a range of cut points between a DAI of 0.6 and 2.5 and selecting the cut point that minimized the p value for the comparison of tumor-rejecting and non-immunogenic neoantigens in the low DAI category. Evaluation of the root mean square deviation of the Picalm.2 epitopes was performed by superimposing all common peptide atoms using Discovery Studio 2025 (Dassault Systèmes, BIOVIA).

## Statistical analysis
Statistical analysis was performed with R software V.4.1.0 (R Core Team) or Prism V.9.5.1 (528) software (GraphPad). For data compared with Student’s t tests, normality and equal variances were first verified. If the data were not Gaussian, the non-parametric Mann-Whitney test was performed; if the samples were Gaussian but variances were not equal, Welch’s correction was used. The Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons was applied as a p value adjustment where indicated in the figure legends. Tumor growth was compared using a linear mixed effects model, and time to reach tumor volume endpoint was compared using a Cox proportional hazards model.

## Results

## Most mutations in human cSCC are unique to individual tumors
To evaluate whether cSCC tumors harbor shared neoantigens that could serve as broadly applicable therapeutic targets, we analyzed the overlap of missense mutations in 149 human cSCC tumors from two datasets. Compared with 139 276 mutations unique to a single tumor, there were only 27 mutations shared across four or more tumors and present in both datasets ( ). As expected, since driver mutations are under positive selection in cancer, 8 of the 27 shared mutations have been previously identified as putative driver mutations ( ). The presence of shared mutations was not associated with sex or immune status ( ). Compared with tumors without shared mutations, tumors with shared mutations were from older patients ( ), patients less likely to have recessive dystrophic epidermolysis bullosa (RDEB), an inherited disease with increased risk of cSCC ( ), and had a higher mutational burden ( ). Tumors from patients with RDEB occurred at a younger age and had a lower mutational burden compared with tumors from patients without RDEB ( ). On multivariate analysis, mutational burden was marginally associated with shared mutations (p=0.058), and neither age nor RDEB status was associated with shared mutations. Overall, the two most frequently occurring mutations were each found in only 7 out of the 149 tumors (4.7%), and only 74 out of 149 (49.7%) tumors contained at least one of the 27 shared mutations ( ). Thus, a therapeutic approach targeting the 27 shared mutations is expected to cover at most half of the patients with cSCC.
 While approximately half of the tumors contain a mutation shared with four or more tumors, the potential for these mutations to elicit a T cell response depends on the ability of the resulting peptides to bind MHC class I. Therefore, we predicted the MHC class I binding of each of the shared mutations. We first predicted binding for the subset of patients for which patient-specific HLA alleles could be determined. Consistent with previous work showing an inverse relationship between oncogenicity and immunogenicity, the proportion of predicted shared binding neoantigens out of all shared mutations was lower for previously identified driver mutations compared with non-driver mutations ( ). To predict the total number of tumors with shared binding neoantigens, we predicted the binding for the subset of patients without annotated HLA alleles using the most common European HLA alleles ( ). European alleles were selected, as the prevalence of cSCC is highest in individuals of European descent. Overall, 24/149 tumors (16.1%) contain a shared mutation predicted to bind well to MHC class I ( ). Furthermore, binding predictions alone overestimate the rate of immunogenicity, as previous work has demonstrated that <15% of prioritized neoantigens elicit an immune response, and it is unknown what percentage of these can induce immune responses that mediate tumor control. , 

## Mouse cSCC model recapitulates human disease
To develop a transplantable, physiologic model to evaluate the characteristics of neoantigens that mediate tumor control, we derived cSCC cell lines from three solar UV light-induced invasive cSCC tumors in BALB/c mice ( ). The number of total and missense mutations in the murine cSCC cell lines is within the range observed in human cSCC ( and ). Human tumors and the murine cell lines demonstrate a consistently high UV-induced C>T mutational signature with no significant difference in the percentage of C>T mutations for human cSCC compared with the murine cell lines ( ). Genes that contain known driver mutations in human disease also show frequent mutations in the murine cSCC cell lines ( ). In particular, each of the three murine cSCC cell lines contains a Trp53 mutation (mouse homolog of human TP53) in the same location as in human disease ( ). cSCC3 contains a truncating mutation (Q98* p53), shared with a human tumor (Q104* p53), that eliminates the binding domain. cSCC1 and cSCC2 contain a missense mutation in the DNA binding domain (R267C p53) at the same location as in a human tumor (R273H p53). R273 is a site of protein-DNA contact and, thus, important for the function of p53. Overall, the mouse model recapitulates both the mutational signature and driver mutations of human disease.
 

## CD8 T cells constrain the growth of cSCC tumors
To demonstrate that the mouse cSCC cell lines contain antigens that elicit T cell-mediated tumor control, we evaluated the ability of T cells to constrain the growth of seven clonal cSCC cell lines, including cSCC1.1.1, which was generated by passage of cSCC1.1 in a wild-type mouse ( ). Clonal cell lines had high constitutive in vitro expression of MHC class I ( and ), and, thus, are subject to CD8 T cell-mediated killing. cSCC1.1.1 had faster in vivo and in vitro growth ( ) and formed tumors in a greater percentage of mice compared with cSCC1.1 ( ). To assess the extent to which T cells constrain the in vivo growth of the cSCC cell lines, we compared tumor growth between athymic mice and wild-type mice. Each of the cSCC cell lines had a greater rate of tumor growth and more quickly reached the tumor volume endpoint in athymic mice compared with wild-type mice ( and ). These data demonstrate that T cells constrain in vivo cSCC growth. Next, we determined the extent to which CD4 and CD8 T cells constrain in vivo cSCC1.1.1. growth, using depleting antibodies prior to tumor challenge with a low cell number ( ). Depletion of the targeted T cell population(s) was confirmed in the blood, spleens, and tumors ( ). Mice depleted of CD8, but not CD4, T cells, had a substantial increase in tumor growth compared with mice treated with isotype control ( ), demonstrating that CD8 T cells constrain cSCC growth.
 

## Irradiated tumor vaccine elicits tumor-specific CD8 T cell response
We next evaluated whether tumor-specific neoantigens in the cSCC model elicit protective immunity by determining if vaccination with irradiated cSCC cells constrained tumor growth. Mice vaccinated with irradiated cSCC1.1.1 cells were completely protected from challenge with cSCC1.1.1 cells ( ). Additionally, mice were protected from re-challenge with cSCC1.1.1 cells ( ), demonstrating that vaccination resulted in memory. These results demonstrate that the cSCC1.1.1 cells contain antigens which elicit an adaptive immune response.
To investigate the antigens responsible, we first considered the mutations in the panel of clonal cSCC cell lines. The mutational burden and mutational profile of the clonal cSCC cell lines ( ) are similar to the cSCC cell lines ( ). The majority of the missense mutations ( ) and MHC class I binding neoantigens ( ) were unique to the three cSCC tumors that were used to generate the clonal cSCC cell lines. Therefore, clonal cSCC cell lines from distinct tumors could be used to interrogate the antigens responsible for response to vaccination. Vaccination with irradiated cSCC2.1 cells did not protect mice from tumor challenge with cSCC1.1.1 ( ), supporting that tumor-associated antigens or antigens deriving from four shared mutations between cSCC2.1 and cSCC1.1.1 cells, including a putative driver mutation p53 R267C ( , ), are not responsible for the response to vaccination. Vaccination with irradiated cSCC3.1 cells did not protect from challenge with cSCC1.1.1 ( ), supporting that the two predicted binding mutations shared between cSCC3.1 and cSCC1.1.1 ( ) are not responsible for the protection.
 To further evaluate whether the response to vaccination is specific to antigens unique to the tumor that the cells are derived from, mice were vaccinated with irradiated cSCC1.1 or cSCC1.1.1 cells and then challenged with either cSCC1.1 or cSCC1.1.1 ( ). Vaccination with either clonal cell line completely protected all mice from tumor challenge with either clonal cell line. These data support that the antigens responsible for protection from tumor growth are unique to the tumor and that in vivo passage of cSCC1.1 to generate cSCC1.1.1 did not result in the loss of the immunogenic antigens that elicit the response to vaccination.
Next, we determined the contribution of CD8 and CD4 T cells to the response to vaccination with irradiated cSCC1.1.1 cells. To evaluate the priming phase, mice were depleted of CD8 and/or CD4 T cells prior to vaccination with irradiated cSCC1.1.1 cells ( ). The effector phase of the response to vaccination was evaluated by vaccinating mice with irradiated cSCC1.1.1 cells prior to T cell depletion ( ). Both the priming and effector phases of the response to vaccination were dependent on CD8 T cells ( ; ). Together, these data suggest that protection is mediated by CD8 T cell responses to tumor-specific neoantigens.

## Vaccination with mutant Kars and Picalm.2 constrains tumor growth
Given the predominant CD8 T cell-mediated tumor constraint without vaccination ( ; ) and with vaccination ( ; ), we focused on identifying the MHC class I neoantigens in cSCC1.1.1. In cSCC1.1.1, 5010 missense mutations were identified ( ). Of these, 2341 mutations were confirmed in the transcriptome. Since prior work from our group and others has shown the importance of the neoantigen:MHC interaction and expression in the prediction of immunogenic neoantigens, we prioritized mutations with high MHC binding affinity (binding affinity % rank <0.5%), MHC presentation (eluted ligand % rank <0.5%), and expression (top 5% of variant allele-specific expression). These criteria resulted in 28 prioritized mutations, which were validated via Sanger sequencing and resulted in 30 predicted immunogenic 9mer MHC class I neoantigens ( ).
 Of the 30 predicted immunogenic MHC class I neoantigens, two neoantigens, mKars and mPicalm.2, elicited IFN-γ+ CD8 T cell responses by ELISpot. Both mKars and mPicalm.2 induced more IFN-γ+ producing CD8 T cells derived from cSCC1.1.1 tumors ( ) and TDLNs ( ) compared with negative controls. To assess whether Kars-specific CD8 T cells and Picalm.2-specific CD8 T cells selectively recognize the mutant peptide sequences, the mutant and wild-type peptides were compared in ELISpot. Compared with wtKars, mKars elicited more IFN-γ+ CD8 T cells in both tumors and TDLNs, and mPicalm.2 induced more IFN-γ+ CD8 T cells in TDLNs compared with wtPicalm.2. The IFN-γ+ CD8 T cell responses to mKars and mPicalm.2 were not observed in CD8 T cells isolated from naïve LNs ( ). Additionally, none of the neoantigens elicited IFN-γ+ CD4 T cell responses in tumors, TDLNs, or naïve LNs ( ). This supports that both mKars and mPicalm.2 are immunogenic neoantigens.
As mKars and mPicalm.2 elicited IFN-γ+ CD8 T cell responses, we tested whether vaccination with mKars or mPicalm.2 could protect against cSCC1.1.1 tumor outgrowth. Mice prophylactically vaccinated with either 9mer or 21mer mKars with poly(I:C) had a slower rate of tumor growth compared with negative controls ( ). Although some murine studies combine peptide vaccines with immune checkpoint inhibitors, we demonstrate that these tumor-rejecting neoantigens constrain tumor growth in the absence of immune checkpoint inhibitors. The similar response to vaccination with 9mer or 21mer mKars supports that the 9mer mKars peptide represents the immunogenic T cell epitope and that the response is primarily mediated by CD8 T cells. Progressively growing tumors in mKars-vaccinated mice were evaluated for mechanisms that may have contributed to tumor escape. In all tumors evaluated from mKars-vaccinated and poly(I:C)-vaccinated mice, the heterozygous Kars mutation was retained ( ), and Kars mRNA expression was not significantly different ( ); finally, there was no significant decrease in H2-Kd expression, the predicted binding allele for mKars, on tumor cells ( ). These data show that tumor escape was not due to genomic loss of the Kars mutation, loss of Kars RNA expression, or loss of MHC class I; tumor escape may be due to other immunosuppressive changes in the microenvironment, such as T cell exhaustion. Prophylactic vaccination with 9mer mPicalm.2 together with poly(I:C) resulted in a small, but significant, delay in tumor growth compared with negative controls ( ). The constraint of tumor growth was specific to the neoantigen, as vaccination with mKars and mPicalm.2 constrained tumor growth relative to the wild-type peptides ( ). These data demonstrate that mKars and mPicalm.2 are tumor-rejecting neoantigens, and that mKars is a superior tumor-rejecting neoantigen.

## Tumor-rejecting neoantigens differ in MHC binding or structure
Next, we evaluated the interactions of the tumor-rejecting neoantigens, mKars and mPicalm.2, with MHC class I alleles to gain insights into the mechanisms underlying the ability to mediate tumor rejection. We first experimentally determined the ability of the tumor-rejecting neoantigens to stabilize MHC class I alleles on the cell surface of transporter associated with antigen processing-deficient T2 cells. Both mKars and wtKars strongly stabilized H2-Kd ( ), but not H2-Ld ( ) or H2-Dd ( ). The mKars, wtKars, and Kd binding control peptides had similar binding affinities for H2-Kd ( ). These data demonstrate that mKars and wtKars peptides equivalently bind H2-Kd. mPicalm.2 strongly stabilized H2-Ld, whereas wtPicalm.2 only moderately stabilized H2-Ld ( ). The relative binding affinity of mPicalm.2 to H2-Ld was greater than wtPicalm.2 ( ). These data demonstrate that mPicalm.2 peptide binds H2-Ld better than wtPicalm.2. Both mPicalm.2 and wtPicalm.2 similarly stabilized H2-Dd ( ) and had a similar binding affinity to H2-Dd ( ). The experimentally observed levels of binding of mPicalm.2 and wtPicalm.2 to H2-Dd were not predicted ( ). These data demonstrate that, unexpectedly, both mPicalm.2 and wtPicalm.2 similarly bind H2-Dd.
 To examine predicted interactions of the tumor-rejecting neoantigens with MHC and the TCR as well as structural correlates with tumor rejection, we modeled the three-dimensional structure of the neoantigens bound to their presenting MHC class I proteins using TFold, a peptide:MHC-specific variant of AlphaFold2, as described. Since the tumor rejection mediated by mKars is not explained by improved binding to H2-Kd compared with wtKars, we assessed possible differences in the conformation of mKars and wtKars bound to H2-Kd. Both mKars and wtKars share the position two (p2) phenylalanine and p9 isoleucine ( ), which serve as primary anchors for H2-Kd ( ). The model of mKars bound to H2-Kd showed a bulged conformation typical of a nonamer ( , left). The mutated p6 phenylalanine extends up and away from the groove, leaving the side chain exposed for potential engagement by TCRs. We also modeled the wtKars peptide to evaluate any differences resulting from the mutation of serine to phenylalanine at p6. The wtKars peptide is predicted to adopt the same conformation as the neoantigen, except for the wild-type serine at p6, whose side chain points down to the base of the H2-Kd α2 helix. The difference in the structure and chemistry of the p6 side chain results in mKars exposing significantly more hydrophobic and aromatic surface than wtKars ( , right). Notably, exposed aromatic side chains are associated with strong TCR binding and peptide immunogenicity. , Thus, the ability of mKars presented on H2-Kd to mediate tumor rejection is predicted to be due to improved TCR recognition of mKars compared with wtKars.
We next used modeling to evaluate the interaction of mPicalm.2 bound to H2-Ld and H2-Dd. The p2 proline in mPicalm.2 is a preferred primary anchor for H2-Ld, explaining the improved binding of mPicalm.2 compared with wtPicalm.2. The H2-Ld model of mPicalm.2 showed a typical, bulged, nonameric conformation ( ), with the p2 proline and p9 leucine occupying the H2-Ld B and F pockets, respectively. These data suggest that the ability of mPicalm.2 presented by H2-Ld to mediate tumor rejection is most likely due to improved affinity for H2-Ld compared with wtPicalm.2. The models were helpful in understanding the binding of both mPicalm.2 and wtPicalm.2 to H2-Dd. The model of mPicalm.2 bound to H2-Dd revealed that, rather than adopting a traditional nonameric binding geometry, the peptide was predicted to adopt a decameric conformation, resulting in the mutant p2 proline occupying the H2-Dd C pocket and the p1 alanine in the B pocket ( ). This leaves the A pocket empty but allows the peptide to match the H2-Dd peptide binding motif, which shows a strong preference for a proline for the C pocket, usually found in peptide p3, but found at p2 in mPicalm.2 ( ). The shifted conformation for mPicalm.2 readily explained its binding to H2-Dd despite its classification as a nonbinder by NetMHCpan. The wtPicalm.2 peptide adopted a nonameric binding geometry, as expected, with the p1 alanine in the A pocket and the p2 serine in the B pocket ( ). Comparing the peptides shows substantial conformational differences from the N-terminus through to the p7 phenylalanine, resulting in the presentation of a significantly altered peptide configuration from p3 to p6 in mPicalm.2 compared with wtPicalm.2 ( ). This mutation-induced conformational change in the mPicalm.2 epitope is quantified by a peptide atom root mean square deviation from wild-type of 3.6 Å, a large value that is well above our prior determination of 1.1 Å as a point of significance for comparing structures of related peptides in MHC binding grooves. Thus, mPicalm.2 presented by H2-Dd may mediate tumor rejection due to TCR recognition of a distinct peptide conformation that differentiates it from wtPicalm.2.
Next, we determined the proportion and phenotype of CD8+ T cells that respond to mKars and mPicalm.2, as well as the presenting allele for mPicalm.2 that promotes T cell expansion, using tetramer staining. CD8+ T cells specific for mKars:H2-Kd and mPicalm.2:H2-Ld were detected in tumors ( ). Tetramer staining for mPicalm.2:H2-Dd-specific T cells was not clearly detected ( ), suggesting that either CD8+ T cells were not expanded in response to mPicalm.2 presented on H2-Dd or had a binding affinity below the sensitivity of the tetramer-based assay. CD8+ T cells recognizing the mPicalm.2:H2-Dd tetramer were not analyzed further, due to the paucity of cells. To evaluate the functionality of mKars and mPicalm.2 tetramer+ cells in the tumor, we analyzed the expression of programmed cell death protein 1 (PD1), lymphocyte-activation gene 3 (LAG3), and granzyme B (GZMB). A higher proportion of H2-Kd:mKars tetramer+ cells had a PD1+LAG3+ phenotype compared with H2-Ld:mPicalm.2 tetramer+ or total CD8+ T cells, while a higher proportion of PD1−LAG3− cells was detected within H2-Ld:Picalm.2 tetramer+ cells compared with H2-Kd:mKars tetramer+ cells ( ). GZMB, a marker of cytotoxicity, was expressed in a higher proportion of CD8+ tetramer+ cells recognizing mKars and mPicalm.2 compared with GZMB expression in the total CD8+ T cell population ( ). Collectively, these data demonstrate that CD8+ T cells recognizing mKars:H2-Kd and mPicalm.2:H2-Ld are readily detected in tumor, and that tetramer+ cells have been activated and produce cytolytic enzymes.

## Tumor-rejecting neoantigens have structural changes from wild-type
Consistent with the increase in the hydrophobic and aromatic surface area in mKars compared with wtKars, previous work has demonstrated that T cell epitopes have an increased frequency of aromatic and large hydrophobic residues in regions of the peptide likely to influence TCR recognition. , However, it remains unclear whether sequence-based predictions sufficiently encompass the changes in peptides that allow for TCR recognition. Therefore, we compared the efficacy of two sequence-based approaches to structural-based approaches in distinguishing tumor-rejecting neoantigens from non-immunogenic neoantigens. For this analysis, we combined all non-immunogenic neoantigens from this study with a unified set of the tumor-rejecting neoantigens identified in this study and previously identified across mouse cancer models (cSCC, head and neck SCC, colon cancer, and three chemically-induced sarcomas), MHC alleles (H2-Kd, H2-Ld, H2-Dd, H2-Kb, H2-Db, and H2-Kk), and neoantigen prioritization methods ( ). From this study, we included mKars:H2-Kd and mPicalm.2:H2-Ld as tumor-rejecting neoantigens, and excluded mPicalm.2:H2-Dd, because T cells recognizing this epitope were not detected ( ). First, we evaluated whether there was an increase in the proportion of tumor-rejecting neoantigens with a new hydrophobic or aromatic residue in a region of the peptide likely to be recognized by the TCR. There was no difference in the proportion of new aromatic or large hydrophobic residues identified in tumor-rejecting neoantigens compared with non-immunogenic peptides ( ). Next, we applied a modified version of the cross-reactivity distance from Łuksza et al. A higher cross-reactivity distance suggests an increased likelihood that the mutant peptide will be recognized as foreign by T cells due to greater dissimilarity between the wild-type and mutant amino acids. While tumor-rejecting neoantigens with a mutation in an anchor residue had significantly higher cross-reactivity distance, tumor-rejecting neoantigens with a mutation in a non-anchor residue had no difference in cross-reactivity distance ( ). Together, these results demonstrate that additional features, beyond the peptide sequence, contribute to TCR recognition.
 We next examined the extent to which structural features distinguish tumor-rejecting neoantigens from non-immunogenic neoantigens. Using structural modeling, the SASA was determined for the mutated residue and corresponding wild-type residue for each neoantigen. Tumor-rejecting neoantigens with mutations in between the anchor residues had a greater difference in SASA between the mutated and wild-type residue than the three other groups ( ). Since this approach requires identifying the anchor residues for each allele, we next split the neoantigens by the predicted DAI into low and high categories ( ). A high DAI means that the neoantigen is predicted to bind MHC better than the wild-type peptide. Tumor-rejecting neoantigens with a low DAI (ie, smaller differences in MHC binding affinity) had a higher difference in SASA between the mutated and wild-type residue than the three other groups ( ). While a change in the SASA of the mutated residue distinguished the tumor-rejecting neoantigens from the non-immunogenic neoantigens in the low DAI category, there was no difference in the SASA between tumor-rejecting and non-immunogenic neoantigens in the high DAI category, as expected. Comparing the mKars peptide to the non-immunogenic mCkap5 peptide demonstrates how structural models can better predict TCR accessibility of residues compared with sequence-based approaches. While both mutated peptides have a change from a serine to a phenylalanine in a similar position, the phenylalanine from the mCkap5 peptide points into the MHC class I binding groove while the phenylalanine from mKars extends upward, significantly increasing the SASA ( ). Overall, these results indicate that stronger MHC binding of the neoantigen compared with wild-type peptide is not necessarily required for immunogenicity and highlights the potential of structural features in prioritizing candidate tumor-rejecting neoantigens.

## Discussion
Human cSCC tumors are constrained by T cells, as about one-half of patients with advanced cSCC respond to immune checkpoint inhibitors, and immunosuppressed solid organ transplant recipients have a 65–250 fold greater risk of developing cSCC. However, the lack of response to immune checkpoint inhibition in about one-half of patients, the undesirable immune-related adverse events, and the relative contraindication for use of immune checkpoint inhibition in transplant recipients highlight the need for additional immunotherapeutic approaches for cSCC. Recent work in other cancers has demonstrated the potential for shared neoantigen vaccines. However, in human cSCC, the vast majority of mutations are unique to each tumor, with the most common mutation shared in only 4.7% of tumors. A theoretical neoantigen vaccine targeting shared neoantigens predicted to bind MHC with a dissociation constant <500 nM would cover less than 15% of patients. As only a small percentage of neoantigens predicted to bind MHC stimulate T cell responses, , and an even smaller percentage of neoantigens mediate tumor destruction, , it is likely that significantly less than 15% of patients would demonstrate an appreciable response to a shared neoantigen vaccine in cSCC. The low rate of shared neoantigens and scarcity of immunogenic neoantigens support the need for personalized immunotherapeutic approaches for the treatment of, and prevention of recurrence in, advanced cSCC.
For personalized neoantigen-based immunotherapies to be effective, tumor-rejecting neoantigens need to be identified and targeted. This challenge is greatest in high mutational burden tumors, like cSCC. Recent clinical studies with personalized mRNA cancer vaccines demonstrate the feasibility of the implementation of patient-specific vaccines and clinical benefit. However, the precise tumor-rejecting neoantigens responsible for improved outcomes observed in a portion of patients are unknown, as pools of neoantigens and long peptides/mRNA have been targeted. , Thus, discrete tumor-rejecting neoepitopes have not yet been identified in humans. Furthermore, most studies evaluating neoantigen immunogenicity and datasets used to develop neoantigen prediction tools define immunogenic neoantigens based on ex vivo methods such as ELISpot, ELISA, and multimer staining. These ex vivo methods do not take into account the complex in vivo interactions in the tumor microenvironment needed for T cell-mediated control, and immunogenicity demonstrated by ex vivo methods does not necessarily correspond to tumor control. Thus, preclinical mouse cancer models are needed to identify the precise T cell epitopes derived from neoantigens that mediate tumor rejection and the neoantigen characteristics that predict immunogenicity. To date, only a small number of discrete tumor-rejecting neoepitopes have been identified in mouse cancer models. To identify characteristics of tumor-rejecting neoantigens, we generated a transplantable solar UV-induced cSCC model. This cSCC model recapitulates human disease with a high mutational burden, a high proportion of UV-signature mutations, shared driver mutations with human disease, and constraint by CD8 T cells.
Within this cSCC model, two tumor-rejecting neoantigens were identified with distinct sets of characteristics. The first, mPicalm.2, better stabilized H2-Ld and had a higher relative binding affinity compared with wtPicalm.2, resulting in a high DAI. By contrast, both mKars and wtKars stabilized and had similar binding affinities to H2-Kd; however, mKars had significantly more hydrophobic and aromatic surface area exposed for TCR recognition than wtKars. This finding exemplifies two alternative pathways to T cell recognition. Prior studies have identified improved MHC binding of the neoantigen relative to the wild-type peptide (DAI) as a characteristic of tumor-rejecting neoantigens, leading to more effective antigen presentation and increasing the likelihood of T cell recognition. However, DAI is not a consistent characteristic across all tumor-rejecting neoantigens in this and other studies. For neoantigens where mutations are not in an anchor residue and do not alter MHC binding, distinction from the wild-type peptide is required to overcome T cell tolerance. Prior studies of T cell epitopes (primarily viral, but also some neoantigens) emphasized that aromatic and hydrophobic residues, particularly in residues not related to MHC binding, are associated with improved T cell recognition and immunogenicity. , While studies have suggested that exposed surface area or conformation in the groove can structurally differentiate the neoantigen from the wild-type peptide and facilitate TCR recognition, these studies are limited to isolated examples. To evaluate these possibilities more comprehensively, we assessed characteristics associated with T cell recognition across all known tumor-rejecting neoantigens, spanning cancer models and MHC alleles. Across the dataset assembled here, the simple presence of aromatic or hydrophobic residues alone does not distinguish tumor-rejecting neoantigens from non-immunogenic neoantigens, possibly highlighting differences between T cell-driven tumor rejection and other functions such as virus control. When considering the structural conformation, though, we found that an increase in the solvent accessibility of the mutated residue relative to the wild-type residue distinguished tumor-rejecting from non-immunogenic neoantigens in the low DAI group. Overall, these results suggest that, across all known tumor-rejecting neoantigens, immune recognition was distinguished by either improved MHC binding or a structural difference of the mutated peptide relative to the wild-type peptide.
Limitations of this study include that the study is restricted to a small number of neoantigens and does not take into account the diversity of the TCR repertoire among individuals. Further studies are needed to demonstrate broad applicability of these findings and determine the effect of TCR diversity on immunogenicity. To enhance the translational potential of these findings, future studies should also address optimizing therapeutic vaccination strategies. Effective therapeutic vaccination with neoantigens may require complementary approaches, such as combining with immune checkpoint inhibitors. Additionally, it is an open question whether a combined mKars and mPicalm.2 peptide vaccine would have superior efficacy compared with the single peptide vaccination.
Improving the clinical efficacy of personalized neoantigen-based immunotherapies requires accurate selection of neoantigens that mediate tumor rejection. This work characterizes the neoantigen landscape in human cSCC, presents a clinically relevant cSCC mouse model, and extends the limited number of validated precise neoepitopes with the ability to mediate tumor rejection. This is the first study, to our knowledge, to demonstrate that increased exposure of the mutated residue to the TCR distinguishes known tumor-rejecting neoantigens from non-immunogenic neoantigens across mouse cancer models, MHC alleles, and neoantigen prioritization methods. Furthermore, sequence-based prediction of TCR recognition based on aromatic and hydrophobic residues in regions of the peptide likely to influence TCR recognition did not distinguish tumor-rejecting from non-immunogenic neoantigens. Given the speed of structural modeling, which takes minutes to hours per peptide with the algorithms used here, predicted structural differences have the potential to be easily implemented in clinical workflows to help select neoantigens for inclusion in personalized cancer vaccines. While accuracy and its assessment are critical, structural modeling is rapidly advancing and improving. Deep learning-based methods provide assessments of model quality, which combined with the rich and growing database of experimental peptide:MHC structures, permits assessment of accuracy. Application of structural-based TCR recognition tools is anticipated to improve the selection of neoantigens for inclusion in vaccines for cSCC and other cancers.
