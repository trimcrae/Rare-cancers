## Abstract
Neoantigens, tumor-specific peptides originating from somatic mutations, are pivotal targets for personalized cancer immunotherapy. This review synthesizes current neoantigen research, encompassing its molecular origins, discovery methodologies, validation strategies and translational applications. We emphasize integrated genomics, mass spectrometry-based immunopeptidomics and machine learning-enhanced bioinformatics pipelines that are concurrently used to predict neoantigen processing, major histocompatibility complex (MHC)-binding affinity, and immunogenicity. Neoantigen-targeted vaccines demonstrate promising efficacy in solid tumors while combinatorial approaches integrating neoantigen-directed therapies with other immunotherapeutics show significant potential to overcome tumor immune evasion mechanisms and improve treatment outcomes. These new progresses underscore the critical role of neoantigens in advancing precision cancer immunotherapy.
Keywords: Neoantigen, Cancer immunotherapy, Immunopeptidomics, Neoantigen prediction and validation, Machine learning-enhanced bioinformatics, mRNA vaccine

## Introduction
Cancer remains a major global health challenge, with an estimated 20 million new cases and 9.7 million deaths worldwide in 2022 [ ]. Among modern treatments, immunotherapy, which activates the immune system against cancer cells, shows significant clinical promise [ , ]. A key element in personalized immunotherapy is neoantigens, tumor-specific peptide antigens arising from somatic mutations [ , ]. As these neoantigens are absent from normal tissues, they bypass immune tolerance and serve as unique targets for cancer vaccines and T-cell therapies [ , ]. Furthermore, research into neoantigens may provide insights into autoimmunity, in which the immune system mistakenly attacks healthy cells [ ].
Mass spectrometry (MS) and bioinformatics are now indispensable tools in neoantigen research [ , , ]. Advances in high-resolution MS enable highly sensitive identification of complex peptide variants and direct sequencing of tumor-derived peptides [ ]. Concurrently, sophisticated bioinformatic pipelines integrate genomic, transcriptomic and proteomic data to predict neoantigen presentation and immunogenicity [ ]. Together, these technologies allow researchers to prioritize tumor alterations with the highest potential to trigger T-cell responses. However, significant challenges remain, including technical limitations in reliably identifying all immunogenic neoantigens and the complexity of translating these findings into effective therapies [ ].
This review aims to summarize the current progress of neoantigen research by adopting an integrative perspective, spanning the molecular mechanisms of neoantigen generation, their immunogenic properties, the influence of the tumor microenvironment (TME), and the evolution of neoantigen profiles under immune selection. Some of the core concepts used in this article are listed in . We highlight recent advances in MS-based neoantigen discovery, including how high-sensitivity liquid chromatography with tandem MS (LC-MS/MS) and proteogenomics are redefining druggable epitopes, alongside improvements in predictive modeling through next-generation algorithms and AI-driven computational prediction, critically evaluating their translational impact beyond mere benchmark performance. Furthermore, we directly link these discovery technologies to emerging clinical platforms, exploring translational progress in personalized neoantigen vaccines—such as individualized mRNA vaccines and semi-personalized public neoantigen strategies—and adoptive T-cell therapies, thereby bridging innovative research with clinical applications. By framing MS, computational modeling, and vaccine engineering as a unified pipeline from antigen discovery to bedside implementation, and by also considering applications in autoimmunity and antigen-specific tolerance, this work provides a technology-integrated roadmap for the systematic prioritization and clinical exploitation of neoantigens to transform cancer immunotherapy and potentially inform novel treatments for autoimmune disorders.

## Molecular origins and presentation of neoantigens
2.1.

## Genesis and mutational profile of neoantigens
Neoantigens originate from tumor-specific genetic alterations that generate aberrant protein sequences recognizable as non-self by the immune system [ , ]. As summarized in , tumor-specific genetic alterations both drive malignant transformation and generate mutant peptides that are processed and presented by MHC molecules, linking oncogenesis directly to neoantigen formation as the basis for tumor-selective immune targeting. Single nucleotide variants (SNVs), particularly missense mutations inducing single amino acid substitutions, constitute the predominant source of neoantigens [ , , ]. In malignancies exhibiting high mutational burden, such as lung cancer, missense-derived neoepitopes are frequently immunogenic and elicit T-cell responses [ ]. Mitochondrial DNA mutations further contribute peptides that enhance tumor immunogenicity [ ]. Insertions and deletions (indels), especially frameshift variants, represent another significant origin [ , ]. Frameshift mutations create novel open reading frames, yielding entirely aberrant peptides. Tumors with MSI-H phenotypes, driven by mismatch repair deficiency, display pronounced immunogenicity from frameshift neoantigens, correlating with robust T-cell infiltration [ , ]. In MSI-positive colorectal cancers, frameshift peptides activate CD8⁺ T cells and associate with improved survival [ ].
 Structural variations, including gene fusions arising from chromosomal rearrangements, generate chimeric proteins with unique junctional sequences that may function as neoantigens [ ]. Fusion-derived neoantigens are particularly relevant in malignancies with low TMB [ ]. For instance, EML4–ALK fusion peptides in lung adenocarcinoma present non-self epitopes capable of stimulating cytotoxic T lymphocytes (CTLs) [ ]. Recent studies have demonstrated therapy-induced neoantigens resulting from covalent modification of tumor proteins by targeted agents [ ]. Covalent inhibitors (e.g., ARS1620) bound to mutant KRASᴳ¹²ᶜ create haptenated peptides that are processed and presented by MHC class I (MHC-I), enabling T-cell recognition [ ]. This dual mechanism, direct oncoprotein inhibition and de novo immunogen generation, suggests synergistic therapeutic potential, especially in tumors with sparse endogenous neoantigens [ ].
Beyond DNA mutations, tumor-specific alterations generate “neoantigen-like” peptides. Aberrant splicing produces neoepitopes derived from tumor-exclusive exon junctions [ ], while oncogene overexpression (e.g., HER2/MYC amplification) unveils cryptic peptides via proteomic saturation [ ]. Though derived from wild-type sequences, their aberrant expression or context confers immunogenic foreignness. Thus, “neoantigen” may encompass any peptide absent from the normal peptidome. Building upon this broad definition, which encompasses peptides absent from the normal peptidome, it is critical to distinguish bona fide neoantigens from the wider set of gene mutation–derived proteins that lack immunogenicity [ ]. Somatic mutations frequently generate altered protein sequences; however, only a fraction of the resulting peptides is processed, loaded onto MHC molecules, and recognized by TCRs. Throughout this review, “neoantigen” refers specifically to mutation-derived peptides that are absent from the normal peptidome, are naturally processed and presented by tumor MHC molecules, and can prime or boost specific T-cell responses in vivo [ ]. In contrast, mutant proteins that are not expressed, are not efficiently processed or presented, or fail to elicit functional T-cell responses are referred to here as non-immunogenic mutation-derived proteins or candidate neoantigens. This conceptual distinction underlies current discovery pipelines that aim to refine broad lists of predicted mutant peptides into smaller sets of experimentally validated immunogenic neoepitopes for clinical application [ ].

2.2.

## Structural determinants of neoantigen immunogenicity
Neoantigens acquire distinct structural properties through amino acid sequence alterations, critically influencing their proteasomal processing, MHC presentation, and ultimate recognition by T lymphocytes [ ]. Following translation, mutant proteins undergo proteasomal degradation into peptide fragments. Peptides of defined length bind MHC molecules and are transported to the cell surface as pMHC complexes for T-cell surveillance [ ]. The chemical architecture of the peptide—including mutation-induced alterations and PTMs, governs its affinity for the MHC binding groove and compatibility with TCR engagement [ ].
2.2.1.

## Molecular structure of neoantigens
Neoantigenic peptides typically harbor unique sequence motifs arising from tumor-specific mutations, which critically modulate their binding affinity to MHC molecules [ ]. Driver mutations (e.g., KRAS or TP53) frequently alter anchor residues that enhance peptide affinity for specific HLA alleles [ ]. Alternatively, mutations may generate de novo peptide sequences exhibiting fortuitous structural complementarity with the MHC binding groove [ ]. Consequently, many neoantigens demonstrate enhanced MHC binding stability compared to their wild-type counterparts, promoting efficient cell-surface presentation [ ].
At a structural level, the enhanced stability of mutant pMHC complexes is often mediated by amino acid substitutions at primary anchor positions—typically position 2 and the C-terminus for class I molecules—that replace a suboptimal self-residue with one preferred by the relevant HLA pocket [ ]. These anchor-optimizing mutations enhance complex stability by increasing buried hydrophobic surface area, forming additional hydrogen bonds or salt bridges with the MHC groove, and reducing peptide flexibility [ ]. Collectively, these effects slow dissociation kinetics and prolong pMHC cell-surface display. Crystallographic and biophysical studies of recurrent driver neoantigens, including KRAS^G12D and PIK3CA^H1047L, directly illustrate this principle, showing that introduction of an optimal anchor residue leads to tighter packing of the mutant side chain within the B and/or F pockets and confers greater thermal and kinetic stability on the mutant complex relative to its wild-type counterpart [ ]. By contrast, structural analyses of the shared p53^R175H neoantigen demonstrate that when the mutation resides at a solvent-exposed, TCR-facing position, MHC binding mode and stability remain highly similar to the wild-type peptide, and immunogenicity arises predominantly from the altered chemistry and topology of the TCR interaction surface [ ]. More broadly, neoantigens with mutations confined to solvent-exposed positions generally exhibit MHC-binding stability comparable to that of the wild-type peptide, with T cell responses driven by gain of TCR reactivity rather than stronger MHC association [ ]. Collectively, these observations underscore that while enhanced MHC-binding stability is a common consequence of mutations that optimize anchor-residue interactions, it is not an absolute requirement for immunogenicity, and a complete interpretation of neoantigen structure–function relationships must integrate both MHC complex stability and TCR recognition [ ].
MHC-I-presented neoantigens primarily activate CD8⁺ CTLs, whereas MHC class II (MHC-II) presentation enables recognition by CD4⁺ helper T cells, or in tumors exhibiting aberrant MHC-II expression, by cytotoxic CD4⁺ T cells [ , , ]. Crucially, the structural novelty of neoantigens signifies an absence of thymic central tolerance induction, distinguishing them from self-peptides. This lack of pre-existing immunological tolerance constitutes a fundamental determinant of their heightened immunogenicity relative to conventional self-antigens.

2.2.2.

## Determinants of immunogenicity
Only a minority of neoantigens prove immunogenic in patients, highlighting the complex requirements for eliciting effective T-cell responses [ ]. Three primary determinants dictate their immunogenic potential.
MHC binding affinity and stability: High-affinity binding to patient-specific HLA alleles is essential for achieving sufficient pMHC complex density on the cell surface to activate T cells [ ]. Immunogenic neoantigens consistently exhibit significantly stronger MHC binding compared to non-immunogenic variants. The binding affinity and stability of neoantigen-MHC complexes are governed by a confluence of peptide-intrinsic and HLA-intrinsic properties [ ]. For MHC class I molecules, dominant determinants are the identity and chemistry of primary anchor residues, typically P2 and the C-terminus, where mutations introducing preferred amino acids for specific HLA pockets can markedly enhance affinity, while disfavored or disruptive residues diminish binding and complex half-life [ ]. Secondary anchor and auxiliary residues along the peptide backbone further fine-tune affinity by contributing additional van der Waals contacts, hydrogen bonds, and electrostatic interactions within the groove [ ]. Furthermore, peptide length and register must conform to structural constraints, as suboptimal lengths for class I (8–11-mer) or a misaligned core register for class II (typically a 9-mer core within 13–25-mer peptides) significantly compromise stability. For MHC class II, core positions P1, P4, P6 and P9 serve as critical anchors [ ]. Beyond canonical sequence motifs, factors such as peptide flexibility and post-translational modifications (PTMs) (e.g., phosphorylation) can directly alter dissociation kinetics [ ]. Ultimately, the cell surface display of stable pMHC complexes is also shaped by extrinsic cellular factors, including HLA polymorphism, the editing functions of peptide loading chaperones like tapasin and HLA-DM, and the competitive landscape of the endogenous peptidome [ ].
TCR recognition and engagement: Effective MHC binding is necessary but insufficient. Productive immunogenicity requires TCR recognition of the neoantigen-MHC complex. A mutant peptide may bind MHC efficiently yet fail to activate T cells if its structural epitopes are not perceived as adequately “non-self” or if the available TCR repertoire lacks clones capable of productive interaction with the complex [ ]. The structural complementarity between the neoantigen-MHC complex and the TCR constitutes a critical additional criterion [ ]. Current predictive algorithms, which predominantly focus on MHC binding affinity, struggle to model this complex TCR-pMHC interaction [ ]. Factors including TCR repertoire diversity, TCR-peptide contact geometry, and the requirement for adequate co-stimulatory signals mean that even optimally presented neoantigens may not elicit a response if TCR engagement or downstream T-cell activation signals are insufficient [ ].
Antigen processing and presentation efficiency: Mutations generating optimal T-cell epitopes remain immunologically silent if the altered protein region is inefficiently processed by the proteasome or transported into the endoplasmic reticulum [ ]. Dendritic cells (DCs) can overcome this tumor-intrinsic limitation through cross-presentation: internalizing tumor-derived material and presenting processed neoantigens on their MHC-I molecules, thereby priming CD8⁺ T-cell responses against epitopes poorly presented by the tumor cells themselves [ ]. This mechanism is crucial for generating immunity against tumors exhibiting defective antigen processing machinery or insufficient MHC-I expression [ ]. The interplay of MHC binding affinity, TCR recognition capacity, and antigen processing efficiency ultimately determines whether a neoantigen can break immunological tolerance and trigger a productive anti-tumor T-cell response. Neoantigens offer inherent therapeutic potential due to the absence of pre-existing immunological tolerance [ ]. During thymic selection, T cells reactive to self-peptides undergo central deletion or anergy, whereas T cells specific for genuine tumor neoantigens escape these tolerance mechanisms [ ]. Consequently, the endogenous T-cell repertoire targeting neoantigens remains largely intact. This enables robust immune responses against appropriately presented neoantigens without the significant risk of autoimmunity associated with targeting self-antigens [ ]. This fundamental immunological distinction underpins the development of novel therapeutic strategies, including neoantigen vaccines and TCR-T therapies.

2.2.3.

## Multi-dimensional prediction of neoantigen immunogenicity
Accurately predicting neoantigens with high immunogenic potential remains challenging due to multifaceted biological variables [ ]. Advances in NGS and MS-based immunopeptidomics have proven instrumental in addressing this complexity [ ]. High-throughput exome sequencing comprehensively catalogs tumor mutational landscapes, while immunopeptidomics empirically identifies MHC-presented mutant peptides [ , ]. Integration of these datasets enables computational frameworks that prioritize candidate neoantigens through multi-parametric assessment, including predicted MHC binding affinity, proteasomal processing likelihood, mutation clonality (ubiquity across tumor cells) and structural dissimilarity to self-peptides (quantifying foreignness) [ ].
Machine learning algorithms trained on validated immunogenic/non-immunogenic neoantigens have significantly refined prediction accuracy [ ]. Recent work leveraging aggregated immunogenicity datasets demonstrates that incorporating additional variables, particularly transcript-level expression abundance and thymic presentation of wild-type counterparts (thymoproteome data), improves prediction performance by approximately 30%, markedly enhancing vaccine target selection [ , ]. These evolving predictive pipelines facilitate evidence-based prioritization of neoantigens requiring experimental validation, accelerating their translation into personalized immunotherapeutic regimens.

2.3.

## Neoantigens arising from primary oncogenic factors
High-prevalence driver mutations in cancer not only promote oncogenesis but also generate therapeutically actionable neoantigens. Recurrent alterations in genes such as KRAS, TP53, EGFR and BRAF produce tumor-exclusive peptides essential for malignant viability, rendering them compelling immunotherapy targets [ , , , ].
KRAS-derived neoantigens: KRAS ranks among the most frequently mutated oncogenes (e.g., G12D, G12V variants) in pancreatic, colorectal, and lung carcinomas [ ]. Missense mutations alter KRAS amino acid sequences, generating neoantigens distinct from wild-type peptides [ ]. These typically clonal, functionally indispensable mutations enable T cells targeting KRAS neoantigens to theoretically eliminate all tumor cells while sparing healthy tissues [ ]. Multiple KRAS-mutant neoepitopes binding prevalent HLA alleles and eliciting patient T-cell responses have been identified, accelerating development of KRAS-directed vaccines and TCR therapies [ ]. Notably, KRAS mutations may further modulate immunogenicity through cytokine-mediated microenvironmental remodeling [ ].
TP53-derived neoantigens: The tumor suppressor TP53 is mutated in ∼50% of human cancers [ ]. Numerous mutations stabilize dysfunctional mutant p53 proteins that accumulate abnormally in tumor cells [ ]. This aberrant persistence, combined with mutation-altered amino acid sequences, generates immunogenic neoantigens [ , ]. As normal tissues exhibit negligible or transient wild-type p53 expression, mutant p53 peptides demonstrate high tumor specificity. Documented immune responses against these neoantigens support ongoing clinical development of vaccines targeting recurrent TP53 mutations [ ].
EGFR mutations: In non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), activating EGFR mutations (e.g., L858R, exon 19 deletions) drive oncogenic signaling while generating mutant-specific peptides [ ]. These altered peptides are presented by HLA molecules and can elicit T-cell responses in subsets of patients [ ]. Vaccination with EGFR L858R -derived peptides induces mutation-specific T-cell reactivity, demonstrating its immunogenic potential [ ]. Given EGFR’s essential role in tumor survival, targeting these neoantigens represents a strategy for selective tumor eradication with minimal on-target toxicity.
BRAF V600E: The BRAF V600E mutation, a valine-to-glutamic acid substitution at residue 600 prevalent in melanoma and colorectal cancer, generates a novel immunogenic epitope [ ]. Beyond its validated role as a target of kinase inhibitors, this clonal driver mutation functions as a neoantigen [ ]. T cells recognizing BRAF V600E -MHC complexes have been isolated, driving the development of peptide vaccines and TCR-T therapies [ ]. Its ubiquitous expression in tumor cells enables potent selective pressure when targeted immunologically [ ].
IDH1 R132H: The isocitrate dehydrogenase-1 (IDH1) mutation in gliomas and leukemias confers neomorphic activity and a tumor-exclusive peptide sequence [ ]. Mutant IDH1 peptides bind MHC-II, activating CD4⁺ T cells in preclinical models. An IDH1 R132H -targeted peptide vaccine demonstrated immunogenicity and preliminary efficacy in early-phase glioma trials, representing among the first clinically tested mutation-specific vaccines [ ].
ALK fusion neoantigens: Chromosomal rearrangements (e.g., *EML4-ALK* in NSCLC, *NPM1-ALK*in lymphoma) generate chimeric proteins with junctional neoepitopes [ ]. These fusion-derived peptides exhibit high immunogenicity due to absolute sequence novelty. Combining ALK tyrosine kinase inhibitors with junction-targeted vaccines may overcome therapeutic resistance by engaging immune surveillance against fusion neoantigens [ , ].
HER2/MYC amplification-derived peptides: Though not mutation-derived, oncogene amplification (e.g., *ERBB2/HER2* in breast cancer, MYC in diverse malignancies) induces aberrant proteostasis. This unveils cryptic peptides, normally undetectable in homeostatic contexts, through proteasomal saturation or altered processing [ ]. In HER2-overexpressing tumors, such peptides breach immune tolerance and become T-cell targets [ ]. Similarly, MYC dysregulation exposes immunogenic cryptic epitopes [ ]. These phenomena blur conventional distinctions between neoantigens and tumor-associated antigens, highlighting how quantitative and contextual shifts in antigen presentation enable immune targeting.
Across tumor types, driver mutation–derived neoantigens consistently enhance T-cell immunity across diverse tumor histology, although their clinical benefit is modulated by the disease setting and the TME [ ]. For instance, mutant KRAS epitopes, including G12D, G12V and G12R, elicit robust CD8⁺ T-cell responses in pancreatic, colorectal and lung cancers, with objective regressions reported in metastatic colorectal and pancreatic adenocarcinoma following adoptive transfer of TCR-engineered cells targeting the public KRAS G12D epitope, confirming its cross-histological activity [ ]. Supporting this, early-phase trials of the lymph node–targeted amphiphile vaccine ELI-002 in resected KRAS-mutant pancreatic and colorectal cancer induced KRAS-specific T-cell responses in approximately 80%–85% of patients, and higher-magnitude responses correlated with improved relapse-free survival [ ]. Similarly, vaccination against the IDH1 R132H mutation in glioma patients induced mutation-specific CD4⁺ T-helper responses in more than 90% of patients and was associated with encouraging long-term survival outcomes [ ]. Furthermore, TP53 hotspot neoantigens represent another widely shared class: TP53-mutant–reactive T cells have been isolated from ovarian, breast, lung and colorectal tumors, and initial TCR-based cell-therapy studies targeting TP53 hotspot mutations, including R175H, have yielded objective responses in diverse epithelial cancers [ ]. Collectively, these findings support the feasibility of targeting a single driver-derived neoepitope to augment antitumor immunity in multiple cancer types, while underscoring the critical modifying roles of the TME and disease burden.

2.4.

## Immune response to neoantigens and therapeutic implications
Neoantigens represent unique tumor-specific antigens arising from somatic mutations and serve as critical targets in tumor immunology. Presented on the surface of cancer cells via MHC molecules, they enable T cell recognition while evading central tolerance mechanisms. This circumvention expands the repertoire of potentially reactive T cell clones [ ]. Neoantigens exhibiting high binding affinity for MHC molecules demonstrate superior immunogenicity, potently activating cognate TCRs [ ]. The stability of the neoantigen pMHC complex directly influences the probability of TCR engagement and subsequent T cell activation.
A robust correlation exists between tumor neoantigen burden, often incorporating qualitative metrics such as MHC binding affinity and expression, and the presence of TILs [ , , ]. Tumors enriched in high-quality neoantigens, notably MSI-H colorectal cancers and melanoma, frequently exhibit pronounced TIL infiltration. Neoantigen-reactive T cells can be directly isolated from such tumors, and studies of adoptive TIL therapy confirm that a significant proportion of tumor-reactive T cells recognize patient-specific neoantigens [ ].
Priming of neoantigen-specific T cells occurs predominantly within tumor-draining lymph nodes [ ]. Antigen-presenting cells (APCs), particularly DCs, phagocytose tumor-derived material, process neoantigen proteins, and migrate to lymphoid organs. There, they present neoantigen peptides on MHC molecules to naïve T lymphocytes, initiating clonal expansion. This process activates both neoantigen-specific CD8⁺ CTLs and CD4⁺ helper T cells, which subsequently traffic to the tumor site to eliminate antigen-expressing cancer cells [ ]. The non-self nature of neoantigens bypasses central tolerance; consequently, neoantigen-specific T cells exhibit minimal intrinsic inhibition and undergo robust clonal proliferation upon activation [ ]. CD4⁺ T cells further augment the anti-tumor response by providing critical cytokine signaling and co-stimulation to CD8⁺ T cells and B cells [ ]. Optimally, this cascade drives significant effector T cell accumulation within the TME and mediates immune-mediated tumor control.
Diverse immunotherapeutic strategies leverage neoantigen-directed immunity. Personalized neoantigen vaccines aim to induce or amplify T cell responses against a patient’s unique mutational profile. Administration of peptides, or nucleic acids encoding predicted neoantigens derived from tumor sequencing, can expand neoantigen-specific T cell populations in vivo. Early-phase clinical trials in melanoma have demonstrated the safety and capacity of such vaccines to elicit multi-specific T cell responses, with evidence of durable clinical benefit in some patients [ ]. By targeting mutations absent in germline DNA, these vaccines are specifically engineered to minimize off-target toxicity against healthy tissues [ ].
ACT approaches involve the ex vivo expansion or genetic modification of neoantigen-reactive T cells prior to patient reinfusion [ ]. TIL therapy, a form of ACT, entails isolating T cells from resected tumor tissue, expanding in vitro (enriching for neoantigen-reactive clones where possible), and reinfusing the expanded population [ ]. Neoantigen-specific TILs are frequently implicated in mediating tumor regression in responding patients. Alternatively, T cells can be genetically engineered to express TCRs or chimeric antigen receptors (CARs) targeting specific neoantigens. Preclinical development includes TCR-transgenic T cells directed against common driver mutations, such as TP53 hotspot mutations or the EGFRvIII variant [ , , ]. ACT modalities can induce potent anti-tumor immunity as the infused T cells are intrinsically targeted to cancer cells. Focusing ACT on neoantigens derived from critical driver mutations (e.g., KRAS, TP53) offers the potential for precise tumor cell eradication, given the absence of target antigen expression in normal cells.

3.

## MS as the cornerstone technique for empirical neoantigen discovery and characterization
Recent advancements in MS have revolutionized neoantigen discovery through immunopeptidomics, a comprehensive analytical approach to identify peptides presented by MHC molecules on cell surfaces [ , ]. This approach provides direct, unbiased insight into the actual antigenic landscape of tumors. Unlike purely computational predictions (e.g., NetMHC, NetMHCpan), which infer potential presentation based on binding affinity, MS-based immunopeptidomics delivers conclusive biochemical evidence of specific mutant peptides bound to MHC-I or MHC-II molecules on tumor cells [ ]. This empirical verification transforms in silico candidates into high-confidence therapeutic targets. By enabling high-fidelity sequencing of the tumor HLA peptidome, MS excels at identifying neoantigens arising from non-canonical processes often missed by predictive algorithms, including unexpected splicing events (e.g., proteasome-generated spliced peptides), idiosyncratic PTMs, non-synonymous SNVs (nsSNVs), indels, gene fusions, and endogenous retroviral elements (ERVs) [ ]. Consequently, MS-based immunopeptidomics bridges the critical gap between genomic aberration and functional immunogenicity, solidifying its role as the gold standard methodology for neoantigen identification and validation. schematically outlines the MS-based immunopeptidomics workflow, emphasizing that direct isolation and sequencing of MHC-bound peptides from tumors provides empirical evidence of which mutant epitopes are naturally presented and therefore represent high-priority neoantigen targets for therapeutic development.
 3.1.

## Advanced MS technologies for immunopeptidome interrogation
The core MS workflow for neoantigen identification involves isolation of MHC-peptide complexes from tumor tissue, peptide elution, chromatographic separation, ionization, mass analysis, and tandem MS sequencing. Ionization, a critical step converting peptides into gas-phase ions, relies primarily on two techniques. Electrospray Ionization (ESI) generates ions directly from liquid solutions, making it ideal for online coupling with nano-liquid chromatography (nanoLC) in the predominant nanoLC-ESI-MS/MS platform [ ]. ESI produces multiply charged ions, enabling informative fragmentation essential for deciphering complex tumor immunopeptidomes. Conversely, Matrix-Assisted Laser Desorption/Ionization (MALDI) involves co-crystallizing peptides with a matrix, which typically is an organic acid, followed by laser ablation and ion detection via Q-TOF analyzers [ ]. While MALDI-MS offers speed and was pivotal in early studies [ , ], it typically provides lower coverage for complex samples than LC-ESI-MS/MS. Thus, its contemporary use is largely confined to specialized applications like MALDI imaging or rapid screening, with LC-ESI-MS/MS remaining the benchmark for comprehensive discovery of neoantigens.
For immunopeptidomic profiling of limited biopsy samples, nanoLC–ESI–MS/MS is generally the preferred platform because it combines high chromatographic resolution, sensitivity, and dynamic range, which are critical for detecting low-abundance neoantigens [ ]. In contrast, MALDI-MS offers distinct operational advantages for rapid, high-throughput analyses and spatially resolved peptide imaging directly from tissue sections; however, its utility for unbiased neoantigen discovery is limited by a shallower precursor sampling depth, reduced quantitative accuracy, and a strong dependence on carefully optimized matrix deposition protocols [ ].
Mass analyzer evolution has been revolutionary in the past two decades. High-resolution, accurate-mass (HRAM) instruments like Orbitraps and advanced QTOFs with high speed/sensitivity enable discrimination of isobaric peptides and detection of single-amino-acid mass shifts [ , ]. The critical role of MS/MS in peptide identification stems from its ability to fragment isolated precursor ions, generating sequence-informative product ions. The fragmentation pattern, typically characterized by b-ions (N-terminal) and y-ions (C-terminal), reveals the peptide sequence [ , ]. Dominant techniques include Collision-Induced Dissociation (CID)/Higher-Energy Collisional Dissociation (HCD), yielding rich b/y ion series [ ], and Electron-Transfer Dissociation (ETD), which preserves labile PTMs and benefits longer/modified peptides despite slower acquisition [ , ]. Hybrid approaches like EThcD (ETD + HCD) can enhance the coverage.
Recent innovations dramatically boost sensitivity and throughput. Ion Mobility Spectrometry-MS (IMS-MS) adds a separation dimension based on ion collisional cross-section (CCS), improving resolution of complex mixtures [ ]. Integration of Trapped Ion Mobility Spectrometry (TIMS) with TOF-MS (e.g., timsTOF Pro with PASEF) enables near-complete precursor ion sampling within short LC gradients. This technology achieves deep immunopeptidome coverage (>10,000 unique peptides) from minimal tumor material rapidly, facilitating large-scale reference “benign peptidome” libraries [ , ]. Concurrently, ultra-high sensitivity instrumentation now sequences peptides at the attomole-zeptomole level, enabling neoantigen discovery from microgram inputs or single biopsies [ , ], and robustly correlating immunopeptidomes with genomic/transcriptomic data.

3.2.

## Methodological strategies for isolation of MHC-peptide complexes
Efficient extraction of MHC-bound peptides is a foundational step prior to MS-based analysis. Two principal methods, often used complementarily, dominate in the application. Mild acid elution (MAE) exploits pH sensitivity of MHC-I structure. Brief exposure of viable cells to mild acid (pH ∼3) dissociates MHC-I from β₂-microglobulin, releasing bound peptides while preserving cellular integrity [ , ]. The supernatant is neutralized, purified (e.g., C18 solid phase extraction), and analyzed by MS. MAE is straightforward, antibody-free, and captures the surface peptidome, making it suitable for extraction from abundant materials [ , ]. However, it yields fewer peptides than immunoprecipitation (IP). MAE is primarily effective for extraction of MHC-I bound peptides, as MHC-II complexes exhibit greater acid stability [ ].
IP is the current gold standard due to superior yield, specificity, and applicability to both MHC classes [ ]. Tumor lysates are prepared with mild detergents preserving complexes [ ]) and then incubated with antibody-coated beads. MHC-I is typically isolated using the pan-HLA-I antibody W6/32, targeting a conserved epitope on HLA-A/B/C heavy chains [ ], whereas MHC-II uses antibodies against HLA-DR/DP/DQ (e.g., L243) [ ]. After stringent washing, peptides are eluted via acid/denaturation [ ]. IP yields significantly more unique peptides than MAE [ ] but demands high-quality antibodies and substantial amount of starting material (e.g., millions of cells), posing challenges for small biopsies [ ]. Advances in micro-scale prep and ultra-sensitive MS are mitigating this limitation [ ].
MAE and IP are increasingly recognized as complementary. MAE captures surface peptides from intact cells, while IP isolates both surface and intracellular complexes from the entire cellular pool post-lysis [ , , ]. Post-elution, peptides typically undergo fractionation/separation (e.g., micro/nano HPLC) to reduce complexity prior to MS analysis [ ]. Identified peptides are mapped to the patient’s tumor exome/transcriptome to pinpoint the genomic origin [ ]. Further validation requires: (1) MS/MS spectral match - synthetic peptides confirm retention time and fragmentation spectrum congruence [ , ] and (2) immunogenicity assessment - Activation of autologous T cells in assays like IFN-γ ELISpot or cytotoxicity tests [ , ]. Peptides fulfilling both criteria are high-confidence neoantigens for potential therapies.

3.3.

## Identification of neoantigens with MS-based immunopeptidomics
Translating raw MS/MS spectra into confident neoantigen identifications requires sophisticated bioinformatics. Patient-specific customized databases are essential, constructed using the tumor exome to include all possible mutant peptides (nsSNVs, indels, fusions) and matched germline sequences for tumor-specificity confirmation [ , ]. Search engines (e.g., MaxQuant, DIA-NN) match experimental spectra against theoretical in silico spectra, employing scoring algorithms (e.g., XCorr, Andromeda score) [ , ].
A major challenge is distinguishing true positives from false identifications. Rigorous statistical control is applied via the target-decoy strategy, estimating false discovery rates (FDR, typically ≤1%) using reversed/randomized sequences [ , ]. Synthetic peptide validation, confirming co-elution and spectral identity, remains the biochemical gold standard [ , ]. Integrative computational tools (e.g., MS²Rescore, Prosit) leverage additional evidence like predicted retention times, fragment ion intensities, ion mobility CCS values, and replicate consistency to boost confidence [ , ]. Refinements in MS instrumentation (Orbitrap/QTOF), fragmentation techniques (HCD, ETD, EThcD), separation dimensions (TIMS-PASEF) and computational pipelines have established MS-based immunopeptidomics as indispensable for neoantigen discovery. It provides irrefutable empirical validation of tumor-presented neoantigens, overcoming the high false-positive rates and presentation uncertainty inherent in purely in silico predictions [ ]. This allows high-confidence verification of neoantigens for therapies [ ].
Critically, MS addresses fundamental limitations of genomics-only approaches. It empirically reveals instances where predicted “strong binders” are absent from the tumor surface (likely due to antigen processing defects) while uncovering unforeseen neoantigen classes like proteasome-spliced peptides or peptides with unique tumor-associated PTMs, invisible to sequencing alone [ ]. By delivering direct, quantitative insight into the actual antigenic landscape, MS based immunopeptidomics is vital for developing effective personalized immunotherapies (e.g., vaccines, T-cell therapies) [ ]. Future directions include single-cell immunopeptidomics, spatial mapping within the TME, further sensitivity/throughput improvements, and in-depth multiomics integration to fully elucidate neoantigen landscape.

4.

## Integrative bioinformatics pipeline in neoantigen discovery and application
Bioinformatics constitutes an indispensable tool to experimental methodologies within neoantigen research. As depicted in , integrative genomics and bioinformatics link tumor–normal sequencing, HLA typing, and in silico prediction to experimental validation and vaccine design, forming a continuous pipeline from somatic mutation discovery to clinically actionable neoantigen-based immunotherapies. It facilitates the systematic in silico prediction, rigorous analysis, and strategic prioritization of candidate neoantigens derived from the complex and heterogeneous landscape of tumor somatic mutations. This computational approach is fundamental for informing the rational design and development of personalized immunotherapies, including bespoke vaccines and adoptive T cell therapies [ , ].
 By establishing critical linkages between comprehensive genomic, transcriptomic, and increasingly, proteomic and immunopeptidomic datasets, bioinformatics bridges raw molecular data with prospective therapeutic targets, transforming multi-dimensional information into actionable insights [ , ]. Bioinformatics is integral to the neoantigen discovery pipeline, encompassing somatic mutation calling and validation, prediction of pMHC binding affinity, assessment of TCR recognition potential and immunogenicity, and integration of multi-omics data. These computational approaches are essential for improving the precision, efficacy, and clinical translation of neoantigen-based immunotherapies [ ].
The evolution of bioinformatic tools, driven by advances in machine learning and the availability of larger, more diverse datasets, continues to refine the accuracy and robustness of neoantigen prediction. This addresses inherent biological complexities such as tumor heterogeneity, HLA diversity, and variable T cell repertoire availability [ ].
4.1.

## Mutation identification and validation
The foundational and arguably most critical bioinformatic phase in neoantigen discovery entails the comprehensive and accurate identification of tumor-specific somatic mutations [ ]. While high-throughput next-generation sequencing (NGS) technologies have revolutionized and largely standardized the cataloging of somatic alterations across cancer genomes, the subsequent computational challenge demands sophisticated algorithms and multi-step analytical pipelines [ , , , ]. This challenge involves distinguishing genuine driver and passenger mutations from sequencing artifacts, and crucially, identifying which mutations are transcribed and translated into potentially immunogenic peptides.
A standard integrated pipeline initiates with parallel DNA sequencing (whole exome sequencing, WES, or whole genome sequencing, WGS) and RNA-Seq of tumor tissue. This is invariably coupled with sequencing of matched normal tissue (e.g., peripheral blood mononuclear cells, PBMCs) to establish a germline reference [ , , ]. This is succeeded by a computationally intensive multi-step process involving raw data processing (quality control, adapter trimming, alignment), variant calling, extensive filtration, annotation, and validation to generate a high-confidence set of expressed somatic variants suitable for downstream neoantigen prediction.
4.1.1.

## High-throughput sequencing technologies
The selection of sequencing strategy profoundly influences the scope and nature of detectable mutations. WES targets approximately 1%–2% of the genome encompassing protein-coding exons, offering a cost-effective method to focus on regions most likely to generate neoantigens derived from altered proteins [ , ]. In contrast, WGS provides an unbiased, comprehensive perspective of the entire genome, including non-coding regulatory regions (promoters, enhancers, introns, untranslated regions - UTRs), structural variants (SVs), and viral integration sites [ ]. While WGS can identify neoantigens originating from non-canonical sources (e.g., frameshifts in non-coding RNAs, alternative open reading frames, ERVs), its higher cost and greater data complexity often render WES the preferred initial approach for focused neoantigen discovery pipelines [ ]. Both WES and WGS primarily detect SNVs and small indels, which constitute the majority of predicted neoantigens.
Crucially, somatic mutation identification relies on comparing tumor sequencing data (WES/WGS) directly with data from the patient’s matched normal tissue. This comparative analysis enables the computational subtraction of germline polymorphisms, inherited variants present in all cells, to isolate true somatic mutations acquired in the tumor lineage [ ]. Concurrently, RNA-seq of the tumor transcriptome is performed not only to quantify gene expression but, critically, to determine whether identified DNA-level somatic mutations are actually transcribed into mRNA [ ].
Verification of RNA expression is non-negligible. Mutations present in tumor DNA but undetectable in RNA, owing to transcriptional inactivation, epigenetic regulation (e.g., promoter hypermethylation), or location in transcriptionally inert regions, fail to generate neoantigenic peptides. The combined application of WES/WGS for mutation detection and RNA-seq for expression confirmation generates a foundational repertoire of expressed mutant protein sequences that serve as the primary source pool for neoantigen candidates upon translation and potential MHC presentation [ ].
Emerging long-read sequencing technologies (e.g., PacBio, Oxford Nanopore) offer potential advantages in resolving complex genomic regions, phasing mutations, and detecting certain SVs or fusion transcripts that might yield novel neoantigens. However, their integration into standard neoantigen pipelines is still evolving.

4.1.2.

## Bioinformatics for mutation calling
Precise identification of somatic mutations from the massive datasets generated by NGS is highly dependent on specialized bioinformatic tools employing sophisticated statistical models and machine learning algorithms. Variant callers such as MuTect2 (part of the GATK suite), Strelka2, and VarScan2 represent state-of-the-art software designed to differentiate authentic, often low-allele-frequency somatic mutations from the pervasive background noise of sequencing errors, alignment artifacts, and germline polymorphisms not fully filtered by the matched normal comparison [ , ].
In somatic variant detection, the performance characteristics of commonly used callers depend strongly on the experimental design and genomic context [ ]. MuTect2 is optimized for tumor–normal whole-exome or whole-genome sequencing and uses local de novo assembly to achieve high sensitivity for low-allele-frequency single-nucleotide variants and small insertions/deletions, even in samples with moderate coverage and heterogeneous tumor purity; however, its specificity is notably reduced in tumor-only mode or within regions of poor mapping quality [ ]. In contrast, Strelka2 emphasizes computational efficiency and a low false-positive rate, demonstrating robust performance on high-coverage datasets [ ]. VarScan2 remains advantageous for deeply sequenced targeted panels but shows diminishing sensitivity at very low variant-allele fractions [ ]. Consequently, to ensure stringent variant prioritization, particularly for clinical-grade neoantigen selection, many analytical pipelines employ a combination of these callers or adopt consensus-based strategies to leverage their complementary strengths [ ].
These tools address critical challenges inherent in tumor sequencing: tumor purity (cancer cell fraction), subclonality (mutations restricted to tumor subpopulations), ploidy variations, and stromal cell contamination. MuTect2 utilizes a sophisticated Bayesian classifier framework that rigorously compares aligned sequencing reads from tumor and normal samples at each genomic position, incorporating base quality scores, mapping quality, and local sequence context to calculate the probability that an observed variant is somatic. It excels at detecting low-frequency SNVs and effectively eliminates most germline single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) and systematic artifacts [ ].
Strelka2 employs a different, highly sensitive probabilistic model based on continuous approximation of allele frequency distributions, demonstrating particular efficacy in detecting small indels and maintaining sensitivity even in samples with low tumor purity or high ploidy [ ]. VarScan2 employs a heuristic and statistical approach, comparing tumor and normal read counts and applying Fisher’s exact test to identify statistically significant differences indicative of somatic variants [ ]. Given that no single caller is optimal for all variant types or tumor contexts, best practices involve employing multiple complementary callers and integrating their results, e.g., taking the intersection for high stringency or union for increased sensitivity followed by rigorous filtering, to generate a final high-confidence somatic mutation call set [ ].
Following variant calling, extensive bioinformatic annotation is performed using tools like ANNOVAR, VEP (Variant Effect Predictor), or SnpEff. This step overlays each mutation with critical biological information: genomic location (exon, intron, UTR), functional consequence on the protein (missense, nonsense, frameshift, splice site, synonymous), population allele frequency (e.g., gnomAD), presence in cancer mutation databases (COSMIC, TCGA), predicted pathogenicity scores (e.g., SIFT, PolyPhen-2, CADD), and association with known cancer driver genes (e.g., via OncoKB) [ ]. This rich annotation layer is indispensable for subsequent neoantigen prioritization. Neoantigens derived from driver mutations or those with highly predicted functional impact may be considered biologically more significant targets.

4.1.3.

## Variant validation and characterization
The process of variant validation extends beyond initial calling to confirm the accuracy and biological relevance of the identified somatic mutations, specifically their expression and potential to generate neoantigens. A primary bioinformatic validation step leverages the tumor RNA-seq data discussed earlier. Computational tools (e.g., those within GATK, or specialized RNA variant callers like RVboost) are used to verify the presence of mutant alleles within the expressed transcriptome [ ]. Detection of a mutation at the DNA level without corresponding evidence at the RNA level (i.e., absence of mutant reads in RNA-seq) strongly suggests either a false positive DNA call (despite initial filtering) or, more commonly, that the mutation resides within a genomic region not actively transcribed in the specific tumor sample or cell type [ ]. Such unexpressed mutations are biologically inert for neoantigen generation and are typically deprioritized or excluded. Conversely, the confident detection of mutant RNA transcripts, as supported by sufficient read depth and quality, provides strong evidence that the mutation is transcribed, significantly increasing the likelihood that the corresponding mutant protein sequence exists and could potentially be processed and presented by the MHC machinery [ ]. Tools explicitly designed for neoantigen workflows, such as NeoFlow and NeoFox, integrate DNA and RNA-seq data seamlessly, automating the identification of somatic mutations that are not only called with high confidence in DNA but also demonstrate adequate RNA expression levels (e.g., mutant allele RNA expression, MAR) to warrant inclusion in neoantigen prediction steps [ ].
Neoantigen pipelines further stratify mutations by clonality, inferred from purity/ploidy-adjusted variant allele frequency (VAF) using tools like PyClone, SciClone or EXPANDS. Clonal mutations (ubiquitous in tumor populations) are prioritized over subclonal variants as they represent targets conserved across metastases [ ]. Functional consequence type (e.g., missense, frameshift indel, splicing) is equally critical. Frameshift indels produce novel open reading frames with high neoantigenicity, whereas synonymous mutations typically lack immunogenic potential unless disrupting splicing [ ]. This integrated assessment of expression, clonality, and functional consequence constitutes an essential filtering and prioritization step before resource-intensive neoantigen prediction.

4.1.4.

## Proteogenomic validation
Definitive validation of a mutation’s relevance may require proteogenomic evidence of the mutant protein or peptide [ ]. However, resource constraints often limit this approach. MS-based approaches, such as proteomic detection of mutant peptides in tumor lysates or immunopeptidomics identification of MHC-presented peptides, provide definitive validation of neoantigens [ ]. Bioinformatics enables this by generating patient-specific search databases containing tumor-derived mutant sequences for MS spectral matching [ , ]. Proteogenomic validation is increasingly recognized as essential in rigorous neoantigen pipelines [ ]. Integrating comprehensive genomic sequencing with targeted proteogenomic analysis establishes a robust foundation for neoantigen prediction.

4.2.

## Predicting neoantigen-MHC binding affinity
Another essential step in neoantigen identification is the computational prediction of the binding affinity between candidate mutant peptides and the patient’s specific set of MHC molecules, also known as HLAs in humans [ ]. Stable binding to MHC molecules is obligatory because only peptides forming persistent pMHC complexes achieve cell surface presentation, enabling recognition by T lymphocytes via their TCRs [ ].
Bioinformatics provides indispensable, robust methodologies for in silico prediction of pMHC binding affinity. These algorithms drastically reduce the vast number of potential mutant peptides derived from mutation identification (often thousands per tumor) down to a manageable subset (typically hundreds or less) possessing the fundamental prerequisite for immunological relevance: the ability to be presented [ ]. The accuracy and efficiency of these predictions are paramount for the feasibility and success of personalized neoantigen therapies.
4.2.1.

## Importance of MHC binding prediction
The extreme polymorphism of the HLA genes (particularly HLA-A, HLA-B, HLA-C for class I; HLA-DP, HLA-DQ, HLA-DR for class II) results in each individual possessing a unique combination of MHC alleles encoding distinct peptide-binding grooves [ ]. Consequently, the repertoire of peptides that can be presented is highly patient-specific. Neoantigen discovery must therefore be intrinsically tailored to the patient’s unique HLA haplotype, determined through high-resolution HLA genotyping from sequencing data using tools like OptiType (class I), HLA-HD, HLA*LA or Kourami [ ].
The computational objective is to predict which short peptide segments encompassing the mutated amino acid (typically 8–11 amino acids in length for MHC-I molecules, which present peptides to CD8⁺ cytotoxic T cells, and longer peptides of 13–25 amino acids for MHC-II molecules, which present peptides to CD4⁺ helper T cells) will exhibit sufficient binding affinity – often defined operationally as half maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) values in the sub-micromolar range (e.g., <500 nM) or high percentile rank scores – to at least one of the patient’s expressed MHC molecules.
The computational objective is to predict mutant peptide segments with high binding affinity to patient-specific MHC molecules. MHC-I presents peptides of 8–11 amino acids in length to CD8⁺ T cells whereas MHC-II presents peptides of 13–25 amino acids to CD4⁺ T helper cells. Sufficient binding is operationally defined by IC50 < 500 nM or top-percentile rank scores [ ]. Peptides predicted to bind with low affinity or not at all to any of the patient’s MHC alleles are biologically undetectable by T cells and therefore can be confidently excluded from further consideration as potential neoantigens [ ].
The stability of the pMHC complex is not merely a binary requirement; it critically influences the strength and duration of the ensuing T cell response [ ]. Peptides forming stable, long-lived pMHC complexes provide sustained signaling to T cells, typically eliciting stronger and more persistent T cell responses compared to peptides forming transient complexes [ ]. Therefore, binding affinity prediction serves as the primary, essential filter defining the candidate pool for subsequent immunogenicity assessment.

4.2.2.

## Computational tools for predicting pMHC affinity
Diverse computational tools leverage large datasets of pMHC interactions for affinity prediction. Key instruments include NetMHCpan (class I) and NetMHCIIpan (class II), which employ neural networks trained pan-allelically to predict binding across diverse HLA types, including poorly characterized alleles [ , ]. These tools generate affinity scores or percentile ranks; lower values indicate higher predicted affinity [ ]. Neoantigen pipelines typically generate all possible mutant peptides encompassing altered residues and employ NetMHCpan/NetMHCIIpan to identify mutants with superior binding relative to wild-type or exceeding affinity thresholds [ , ].
For pMHC affinity prediction, the NetMHCpan and NetMHCIIpan suites represent benchmark pan-allelic tools and demonstrate high accuracy for common HLA alleles and canonical peptide lengths [ ]. However, their predictive performance diminishes for rare alleles with limited training data, atypical peptide lengths, and non-canonical peptides derived from events such as alternative splicing or PTMs, in part due to biases in available training datasets [ ]. A critical limitation is that these algorithms primarily estimate intrinsic peptide–MHC binding affinity (or related presentation likelihood scores) rather than the fully integrated processes of antigen processing and presentation or subsequent T-cell immunogenicity [ ]. Consequently, even high-affinity predictions yield a substantial fraction of false positives, necessitating cautious interpretation and integration with experimental validation, mass-spectrometry-based immunopeptidomics data, and orthogonal computational methods for reliable inference [ ].
Complementary deep learning tools (e.g., MHCflurry, MHCnuggets) enhance prediction accuracy and computational efficiency through neural network architectures [ ], demonstrating superior performance over matrix-based methods for certain HLA alleles [ ]. Consensus approaches and predictive tools are accessible via the Immune Epitope Database (IEDB) [ ]. MHC-I prediction maturity exceeds that of MHC-II due to structural constraints: MHC-I features a closed binding groove favoring defined 8–11-mer peptides, whereas MHC-II’s open-ended groove accommodates variable-length peptides (13–25-mers), complicating binding motif identification [ ]. Refining MHC-II prediction remains critical given CD4⁺ T cells’ essential role in sustaining cytotoxic responses and immunological memory [ ].
Advanced algorithms now incorporate upstream biological events [ ]. Tools like NetCTL synergize MHC-I binding affinity with proteasomal cleavage likelihood and transporter associated with antigen processing (TAP) efficiency metrics [ ], generating integrated presentation scores that better reflect physiological epitope display and offer a more holistic view of peptide presentation likelihood.

4.3.

## Predicting the immunogenicity of candidate neoantigens
The successful prediction of strong MHC binding affinity identifies peptides capable of being presented. However, the pivotal subsequent question is whether these presented mutant peptides can actually trigger a functional, tumor-reactive T cell response in vivo – their immunogenicity. It is well-established that only a small fraction of MHC-presented peptides, even those binding with high affinity, are naturally immunogenic in a given patient [ ]. Predicting immunogenicity computationally represents a significantly more complex challenge than binding affinity prediction but is absolutely essential for prioritizing the most promising candidates for downstream experimental validation (e.g., peptide synthesis, T cell assays) and development of personalized vaccines or T cell therapies [ ]. The goal is to identify neoantigens most likely to expand pre-existing tumor-reactive T cell clones or elicit potent de novo T cell responses upon vaccination.
4.3.1.

## Rationale for immunogenicity prediction
While numerous tumor peptides exhibit high MHC affinity, few possess cognate T cells within the patient’s repertoire capable of functional recognition, potentially due to anergy or immunosuppression [ ]. Experimental immunogenicity assessment (e.g., peptide synthesis, T cell assays) is resource-intensive, especially when a large number of peptides need to be assessed. Computational predictors aim to enrich for neoantigens with higher T cell response probabilities [ ], enhancing the efficacy of therapeutic products [ ]. Accurate neoantigen prediction is essential to identify cognate CD8⁺ CTLs and CD4⁺ helper T cell subsets, while pinpointing helper epitopes that amplify CTL-mediated tumor killing through cytokine/co-stimulatory signaling [ ].

4.3.2.

## Tools and methods for immunogenicity prediction
Bioinformatics tools augment MHC binding predictions with additional parameters. NetCTL provides composite scores integrating MHC binding, proteasomal cleavage, and TAP transport for class I peptides [ ]. Other tools such as those from the IEDB predict T cell epitope efficacy based on affinity, TCR-facing residues, and immunogenic motifs [ ].
Machine learning models trained on immunogenicity datasets (e.g., DeepHLApan, NeoPred) incorporate factors like MHC binding stability, resemblance to microbial epitopes (implying potential cross-reactivity), TCR-facing residue properties, and gene expression levels [ ]. Random forest classifiers integrating peptide processing scores, thymic presentation of wild-type sequences, and driver gene status have improved the prediction of T cell-stimulating neoantigens [ ]. These multi-parametric models offer improved discrimination between rare immunogenic neoantigens and abundant non-immunogenic variants [ ]. Immunogenicity assessment should encompass both T cell subsets. While focus has been on CD8⁺ T cell (class I) neoantigens, CD4⁺ T cell (class II) neoantigens are equally critical [ ]. Tools like NetMHCIIpan and MARIA predict class II peptide binding and presentation [ ], enabling identification of helper T cell antigens vital for enhancing CD8⁺ responses in vaccines. Glioblastoma (GBM) vaccine trials incorporating predicted MHC II neoantigens elicited CD4⁺ responses correlated with improved outcomes [ , , ].
A growing ecosystem of publicly accessible computational tools has been developed to enhance neoantigen prioritization, moving beyond MHC-binding affinity alone to predict immunogenic potential. Platforms such as the IEDB immunogenicity predictors use sequence-based statistical models trained on curated epitope datasets to generate probability-like scores for T-cell activation [ ]. More advanced deep-learning architectures are increasingly being applied; for instance, DeepHLApan utilizes recurrent neural networks trained on human cancer and pathogen-derived neoepitopes to jointly model MHC-I binding and immunogenicity, whereas DeepImmuno-CNN employs convolutional networks coupled with a beta–binomial scoring scheme for epitope prediction across viral and tumor contexts [ , ]. Further integrating broader immunological processes, tools such as PRIME and PRIME2.0 combine features of antigen presentation and TCR recognition to estimate the likelihood of CD8⁺ T-cell engagement with peptide–MHC-I complexes [ ]. The field continues to evolve with subsequent tools, including DeepNeo and NeoaPred, which incorporate structural and surface features of the pMHC complex to refine predictions [ , ]. Collectively, these open-source platforms provide a critical resource for the efficient pre-filtering of extensive candidate lists and the prioritization of the most promising neoantigens for downstream experimental validation and clinical translation [ , ].

4.3.3.

## Applications in oncology and autoimmunity
Computational immunogenicity prediction directs the design of personalized cancer immunotherapies. Neoantigen discovery pipelines typically generate ∼100 high-affinity neoantigen candidates per patient [ ], which undergo sequential filtering by binding affinity, tumor expression, antigen processing likelihood (proteasomal cleavage/TAP transport scores) and predicted immunogenicity, yielding ∼20 prioritized epitopes. These candidates undergo experimental validations via co-culture with autologous peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) as well as confirmed T cell reactivity (e.g., IFNγ ELISpot, activation-induced marker expression, or expansion in flow cytometry) [ ]. Algorithmic accuracy is demonstrated by concordance between predicted and experimentally confirmed neoantigens. Validated neoantigens are prioritized for vaccine development or monitoring tools (e.g., pMHC tetramers). Melanoma vaccine trials relying solely on computational predictions have successfully elicited de novo T cell responses post-vaccination, demonstrating the predictive utility of these pipelines [ ].
In autoimmune disorders, computational pipelines analogous to cancer neoantigen discovery identify pathogenic neoepitopes that circumvent central tolerance. In type 1 diabetes (T1D), predicted neoepitopes from post-translationally modified beta-cell antigens (e.g., insulin) can elicit autoreactive T cells [ ]. These predicted, modified peptides bind to disease-associated HLA alleles (e.g., HLA-DQ8) and validate T cell reactivity. Integrating HLA binding prediction (e.g., for T1D-risk HLA-DQ alleles) with experimental validation of T cell reactivity (tetramer staining, IFNγ ELISpot) pinpoints epitopes driving autoimmune pathogenesis [ ]. A paradigmatic example is citrullinated antigens in rheumatoid arthritis (RA). Peptidylarginine deiminase (PAD)-mediated conversion of arginine to citrulline generates neoepitopes presented by HLA-DR4. These epitopes drive T/B cell responses and stimulate the production of anti-citrullinated protein antibody (ACPA) [ ]. The pathogenesis of RA involves a breach of immunological tolerance to citrullinated proteins, a process facilitated by specific genetic and mechanistic checkpoints [ ]. HLA-DRB1*04 alleles bearing the shared-epitope motif exhibit higher binding affinity for citrullinated peptides than for their native arginine-containing counterparts [ ]. Consequently, PAD–generated neoepitopes are efficiently presented within the inflamed synovium but are relatively under-represented in the thymic peptidome, allowing citrullinated peptide–reactive T cells to persist [ ]. This discrepancy enables autoreactive CD4⁺ T cells specific for citrullinated antigens such as fibrinogen and vimentin to escape central deletion and expand in the periphery [ ]. Upon receiving additional activation signals from environmental triggers such as cigarette smoking, these T cells provide cognate help to ACPA–positive B cells [ ]. The combination of altered HLA binding, defective clonal deletion, and productive T–B cell collaboration ultimately promotes epitope spreading within the ACPA response [ ]. This progression from a limited set of citrullinated neoepitopes to a broad, diverse autoantibody repertoire breaks immune tolerance and sustains chronic joint inflammation. Immunogenicity prediction algorithms identify immunodominant citrullination sites, enabling targeted antigen-specific tolerance induction therapies.
In both cancer and autoimmunity, precise immunogenicity assessment enables resource prioritization toward therapeutically actionable targets while deprioritizing non-immunogenic candidates [ ]. Mounting clinical validation datasets from therapeutic trials coupled with high-resolution TCR repertoire profiling will iteratively refine predictive models, potentially incorporating patient-specific immunological parameters such as TCR clonality and diversity metrics, checkpoint molecule expression landscapes (e.g., PD-1, LAG-3) and T cell exhaustion signatures.

5.

## Composite immunotherapeutic strategies employing neoantigens and immune modulators
ICIs, specifically monoclonal antibodies targeting cytotoxic T-lymphocyte-associated protein 4 (CTLA-4) and programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1) or its ligand (PD-L1), represent a transformative advancement in oncology by augmenting T cell-mediated anti-tumor immunity [ , ]. Neoantigens, arising from tumor-specific somatic mutations, are critically implicated in ICI efficacy, serving as essential “non-self” epitopes that enable immune discrimination between malignant and healthy cells [ , ]. This section examines the influence of neoantigen burden on checkpoint blockade response, methodologies to enhance neoantigen presentation for improved ICI outcomes, and combinatorial therapeutic approaches integrating neoantigen-targeted strategies with other immunomodulatory modalities. As outlined in A, the major neoantigen-oriented modalities—checkpoint blockade, vaccines, engineered TCR, CAR T and TIL therapies—are positioned along a spectrum from systemic immune reinvigoration to highly personalized cell-based strategies targeting tumor-restricted epitopes.
 5.1.

## Neoantigens and immune checkpoint inhibition
Tumors exploit immune checkpoint pathways to evade T cell-mediated destruction. Checkpoint receptors, including CTLA-4 and PD-1, attenuate T cell activation upon engagement, thereby constraining excessive immune responses [ , , ]. While ICI therapy can reinvigorate anti-tumor T cells, clinical efficacy frequently correlates with tumor neoantigen load [ ]. Following checkpoint blockade, neoantigens provide the requisite targets for reactivated T cells to identify and eliminate cancer cells. Representative tumor types with relatively high versus low TMB observed in large clinical sequencing cohorts are summarized in .
 CTLA-4 primarily modulates T cell activation during the priming phase within lymph nodes. Therapeutic blockade (e.g., ipilimumab) expands the repertoire of tumor antigen-targeting T cells [ ]. Enhanced clinical outcomes following CTLA-4 inhibition are observed in patients with elevated TMB and correspondingly increased neoantigen load, attributed to a greater pool of neoantigen-specific T cells susceptible to activation upon suppression removal [ ]. For instance, melanoma patients achieving long-term survival with CTLA-4 blockade exhibited tumors enriched for T cell-recognized neoantigens [ ]. Notably, recurrent “public” neoantigens, derived from common hotspot mutations, are frequently targeted during CTLA-4 inhibition [ ]. While the correlation between TMB/neoantigen burden and response is not absolute—responses occur in some patients with low TMB and are absent in others with high TMB [ ]—neoantigen burden remains a favorable predictive biomarker for CTLA-4 therapy efficacy.
The PD-1/PD-L1 axis suppresses T cell function predominantly within the TME [ ]. Elevated PD-L1 expression often correlates with an immune-infiltrated ("inflamed") TME, where tumors upregulate PD-L1 as an adaptive resistance mechanism [ ]. Blocking PD-1 or PD-L1 can restore functionality in exhausted TILs, facilitating their proliferation and cytotoxic activity [ ]. TMB is a recognized biomarker for PD-1/PD-L1 blockade response across multiple malignancies [ ]. Cancers characterized by high TMB, such as NSCLC and melanoma, demonstrate pronounced sensitivity to PD-1 inhibitors [ ]. Conversely, tumors with low TMB, including pancreatic and prostate cancers, typically exhibit poor responses, likely due to insufficient neoantigen targets for reactivated T cells [ ]. The FDA has approved PD-1 inhibitors for all solid tumors exhibiting high MSI-H, utilizing the resultant high neoantigen load from frameshift mutations as a selection criterion [ , ]. Post-treatment analyses reveal clonal expansion of neoantigen-specific T cells in circulation among responders, indicating that checkpoint inhibition removes constraints on these cells, enabling their proliferation [ ].
Following the success of PD-1 and CTLA-4 blockade, research has expanded to target additional inhibitory receptors, such as lymphocyte-activation gene 3 (LAG-3), T-cell immunoglobulin and mucin-domain containing-3 (TIM-3), and T cell immunoreceptor with Ig and ITIM domains (TIGIT) [ ]. These receptors are often upregulated on TILs, particularly post-PD-1 therapy, as compensatory tolerance mechanisms [ ]. Clinical trials are currently evaluating their blockade.
Although the relationship between these emerging checkpoints and neoantigens requires further elucidation, the hypothesis posits that inhibiting these checkpoints may unleash or amplify constrained neoantigen-specific T cell responses. LAG-3 binds MHC-II molecules and delivers inhibitory signals; tumors evoking neoantigen-specific CD4⁺ T cell responses may derive particular benefit from LAG-3 blockade [ ]. Early clinical data combining anti-LAG-3 with anti-PD-1 demonstrated improved outcomes in melanoma [ ]. TIGIT inhibition, frequently combined with anti-PD-1, may enhance NK cell and CD8⁺ T cell cytotoxicity against neoantigen-expressing tumor cells otherwise protected by TIGIT-ligand interactions on tumor or myeloid cells [ ]. As next-generation ICIs emerge, ongoing research focuses on defining whether distinct neoantigen signatures correlate with response or if neoantigen loss underlies resistance mechanisms.
In summary, while ICIs do not directly engage neoantigens, neoantigens constitute the essential targets enabling the T cell activation triggered by checkpoint blockade [ ]. Tumors with high neoantigen load provide the necessary substrate for a robust immune response upon checkpoint inhibition. Conversely, tumors with low neoantigen burden offer limited targets, constraining T cell efficacy even after checkpoint removal [ ]. This understanding has catalyzed the development of combination strategies integrating ICIs with neoantigen-targeted therapies (e.g., vaccines, adoptive T cell transfer) to supply the requisite antigenic targets in malignancies inherently deficient in immunogenic neoantigens [ , , ].

5.2.

## Strategies to enhance neoantigen presentation and optimize ICI efficacy
Given the pivotal role of neoantigens in ICI response, strategies aimed at augmenting neoantigen presentation and activating neoantigen-specific T cells represent a logical approach to potentiate ICI efficacy. Several combinatorial modalities are under investigation to address this need.
Neoantigen vaccines and ICIs: Combining personalized neoantigen vaccines to prime T cells with subsequent or concurrent ICI administration aims to sustain and amplify T cell function within the TME [ , , ]. Preclinical models demonstrate additive or synergistic effects: vaccines expand the pool and diversity of tumor-specific T cells, whereas ICIs enhance their effector function and alleviate exhaustion, collectively increasing the likelihood of durable tumor control. Conceptually, such combinations can convert immunologically “cold” tumors into “hot” lesions populated by neoantigen-recognizing effector T cells and thereby sensitize otherwise refractory tumors to checkpoint blockade.
Oncolytic viruses: Oncolytic viruses selectively replicate within and lyse tumor cells, inducing immunogenic cell death [ ]. This process releases tumor antigens, including neoantigens, within a context of acute inflammation (viral infection), effectively delivering in situ vaccination [ ]. Furthermore, viral infection can amplify interferon responses, upregulating MHC expression on tumor cells [ ], thereby improving neoantigen recognition. Combining oncolytic viruses (e.g., talimogene laherparepvec, T-VEC) with CTLA-4 or PD-1 inhibitors in melanoma yields enhanced response rates compared to monotherapy [ ], facilitating the conversion of immunologically “cold” tumors into T cell-inflamed microenvironments [ ]. Analyses reveal diversified T cell responses post-combination therapy, including reactivity to previously unrecognized neoantigens—indicating epitope spreading.
Radiation therapy (RT): Radiation can induce systemic anti-tumor effects (abscopal effect), potentially immune-mediated. RT kills tumor cells, releasing neoantigens and damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs; e.g., HMGB1, calreticulin) that enhance DCs activation [ ]. Combining RT with ICIs has converted non-responders into responders in multiple instances [ ]. For example, localized RT in a PD-1 inhibitor-refractory metastatic lung cancer patient led to regression of non-irradiated lesions, correlating with augmented T cell responses to tumor antigens [ , ]. RT can also increase tumor MHC-I expression via localized IFN-γ production. Ongoing clinical trials combining PD-1 inhibitors with RT suggest RT may enhance immunotherapy efficacy by promoting neoantigen release and TIL infiltration [ ].
Targeted and chemotherapeutic agents: Conventional cytotoxic chemotherapies and targeted agents may modulate tumor immunogenicity. Certain DNA-damaging chemotherapies or poly(ADP-ribose) polymerase (PARP) inhibitors may increase mutational load (and thus neoantigen burden) or induce cytosolic DNA sensing pathways triggering interferon responses, enhancing immunogenicity [ , ]. Cancer treatment increasingly seeks regimens that eliminate tumor cells while enhancing neoantigen presentation [ ]. A key trend in cancer therapy is the development of combination strategies focused on both eliminating tumor cells and boosting neoantigen presentation [ ]. For instance, covalent KRAS-G12C inhibitors not only inhibit their target oncoprotein but also create drug-induced haptenated neopeptides [ , ]. Combining such agents with vaccines targeting the drug-induced neoantigen represents a promising strategy [ ]. Additionally, low-dose chemotherapy or specific targeted agents may be employed to induce a more immunogenic tumor phenotype (e.g., via viral mimicry pathway activation, MHC upregulation) enhancing the efficacy of subsequent neoantigen vaccination or T cell therapy [ , ].
Cytokine therapies: Cytokines can amplify neoantigen-driven immune responses [ ]. Co-administration of interleukin-2 (IL-2) or interferon-alpha (IFN-α) with neoantigen vaccines enhances the proliferation and activation of primed T cells [ ]. Exogenous IFN-γ (or agents stimulating its production) can elevate MHC expression on tumor cells, including inducing MHC-II in some contexts, improving T cell recognition [ ]. Cytokine adjuvants (e.g., granulocyte-macrophage colony-stimulating factor (GM-CSF), moderate-dose IL-2, novel IL-2 agonists) are commonly incorporated into vaccine protocols to optimize the expansion and differentiation of neoantigen-specific T cells into functional effectors [ , ]. GM-CSF is sometimes co-delivered with peptides to facilitate DC activation at the vaccination site.
Neoantigen-targeted immunotherapies embody the principles of personalized medicine, tailoring treatment to individual tumor genetics and immune context. While current approaches focus predominantly on patient-specific neoantigens, research continues to explore the feasibility of “universal” neoantigen vaccines targeting recurrent epitopes, such as shared frameshift neoantigens prevalent in MSI-H malignancies [ ]. Identification of common neoantigens derived from recurrent oncogenic mutations or viral antigens in virus-associated cancers offers potential targets for broader patient populations [ ]. Regardless of the approach—personalized or shared—neoantigen targeting represents a potent strategy to enhance immune specificity against malignant cells.

6.

## Applications of neoantigen-targeted immunotherapy
The clinical implementation of neoantigen-directed therapies marks a paradigm shift in precision medicine, bridging fundamental research with therapeutic applications across oncology, autoimmunity, and preventive healthcare [ ]. These strategies exploit tumor-specific somatic mutations or pathological epitopes to achieve immunological precision unmatched by conventional approaches. The transition from theoretical frameworks to clinical validation underscores their potential to redefine therapeutic paradigms, with ongoing trials demonstrating efficacy in historically treatment-resistant conditions while revealing critical challenges in scalability and predictive biomarker development [ ].
6.1.

## Personalized neoantigen vaccines in clinical oncology
Personalized neoantigen vaccines exemplify the convergence of genomic medicine and immunotherapy, utilizing patient-exclusive somatic mutations delivered via synthetic long peptides (25–30 amino acids) or mRNA constructs to induce tumor-specific T cell responses. As illustrated in , lipid nanoparticle (LNP)-encapsulated neoantigen mRNA is taken up by DCs, translated and processed into peptides that are presented on MHC-I and MHC-II, thereby activating coordinated CD4⁺ and CD8⁺ T-cell responses that mediate tumor-specific killing.
 Multiple vaccine formulation platforms have been engineered for the clinical delivery of neoantigens, each with distinct advantages and limitations [ ]. Peptide-based approaches, which utilize pools of short or synthetic long peptides, often emulsified in depot-forming adjuvants and combined with immunostimulatory agents, are designed to enhance DCs uptake and cross-presentation [ ]. Nucleic acid platforms offer additional benefits. DNA vaccines, which encode concatenated neoepitopes and are frequently delivered with electroporation, enable in vivo antigen expression and have proven feasible and immunogenic in early clinical trials [ ]. RNA vaccines, particularly LNP–encapsulated mRNA formulations, allow rapid manufacturing, provide intrinsic immune activation, and can induce polyfunctional, clinically active T-cell responses [ ]. Viral vector-based systems, including adenoviral or poxviral vectors, provide high-level antigen expression and potent CD8⁺ T-cell priming, although their efficacy can be limited by pre-existing anti-vector immunity [ ]. Furthermore, emerging nanoparticle and scaffold technologies are being developed to optimize the co-delivery of antigens and adjuvants, specifically targeting lymphoid tissues and APC [ ]. In practice, platform selection requires balancing manufacturing scalability, the potency and quality of the induced T-cell response, and logistical considerations for repeated administration in chronic cancer settings [ ].
The workflow integrates whole-exome/transcriptome sequencing, HLA genotyping, bioinformatic immunogenicity prediction, and rapid Good Manufacturing Practice production—a process now achievable within 6–8 weeks in optimized pipelines [ , , ]. PDAC, characterized by its immunosuppressive stroma and low TMB (median 1 mutation/Mb), has proven particularly refractory to single-agent immunotherapies. A pivotal 2023 study addressed this limitation through combinatorial RNA neoantigen vaccination and anti-PD-1 therapy in resected PDAC patients, eliciting measurable neoantigen-specific T cell responses in 52% of patients and significantly prolonged recurrence-free survival in responders (median 18.2 months vs. 8.7 months; HR 0.32) [ , , , ].
The landmark NeoVax trial (NCT01970358) in high-risk melanoma further validated this paradigm, where synthetic long peptides encoding predicted class I/II epitopes induced durable polyfunctional T cell responses. Four of six patients remained recurrence-free > 4 years post-vaccination, while two relapsing patients achieved complete responses to subsequent anti-PD-1 therapy, suggesting vaccine-mediated immunological sensitization [ ]. Current phase II/III trials in GBM, renal cell carcinoma, and NSCLC consistently demonstrate vaccine-induced T cell activation, though heterogeneous survival benefits reveal persistent hurdles. Key challenges include optimal epitope selection balancing immunogenicity and clonality, strategies for TME modulation, and achieving scalable manufacturing for global accessibility [ , , , ]. Crucially, the absence of grade ≥3 toxicities across these trials underscores the favorable safety profile of mutation-targeted vaccines, facilitating its potential integration into mainstream cancer care.

6.2.

## Adoptive T cell therapies focusing on neoantigens
Neoantigen-targeted ACT has evolved from TIL therapy toward engineered precision platforms [ ]. The seminal 2014 cholangiocarcinoma case established clinical proof-of-concept, where expanded ERBB2IP mutation-reactive CD8⁺ TILs mediated complete regression of metastatic lung lesions lasting 13 months [ ]. This demonstrated that rare neoantigen-specific clones (<0.5% of TILs) can mediate durable responses when effectively expanded, establishing clonal neoantigens as ideal therapeutic targets.
Recent advances leverage aberrant MHC-II expression in epithelial cancers (observed in 10%–20% of PDAC and triple-negative breast tumors), enabling direct tumor recognition by CD4⁺ T cells. Mechanistic studies confirm MHC-II⁺ PDAC cells present endogenous neoantigens to CD4⁺ T cells, which exhibit granzyme B/perforin-mediated cytotoxicity and Fas/FasL-induced apoptosis [ ]. Clinical translation employs dual strategies: neoantigen vaccines incorporating helper epitopes to amplify endogenous responses, and adoptive transfer of ex vivo-expanded neoantigen-reactive CD4⁺ clones (“killer CD4” cells) that directly eliminate tumor cells while licensing APCs through IFN-γ secretion [ ].
TCR-engineered T cell platforms targeting shared neoantigens represent the next frontier, offering off-the-shelf potential for recurrent hotspot mutations like KRAS G12D and TP53 R175H [ , ]. Affinity-optimized TCRs overcome low pMHC stability, while combinatorial targeting mitigates antigen escape, which are advantages currently being evaluated in clinical trials (NCT03745326, NCT03970382) with preliminary data showing enhanced T cell persistence and reduced exhaustion phenotypes compared to first-generation constructs. 6.3.

## Neoantigens in autoimmunity and tolerance
A strategic inversion of the neoantigen paradigm is emerging for autoimmune diseases, shifting from immune activation to tolerance induction by targeting pathogenic, post-translationally modified self-epitopes. This approach focuses on disease-specific targets: Citrullinated fibrinogen/vimentin peptides presented by HLA-DRB1*04:01 in RA, deamidated insulin B-chain epitopes restricted by HLA-DQ8 in T1D, and oxidized myelin oligodendrocyte glycoprotein peptides associated with HLA-DR15 in multiple sclerosis [ ]. Phase I trials are deploying nanoparticle platforms to co-deliver these “auto-neoantigens” and tolerogenic adjuvants, specifically, citrullinated peptides/rapamycin in poly(lactic-co-glycolic acid) nanoparticles for RA, deamidated insulin/vitamin D3 in liposomes for T1D, and oxidized myelin proteins/TGF-β conjugated to gold nanoparticles for multiple sclerosis [ ].
Mechanistically, post-translationally modified epitopes can function as “auto-neoantigens” due to their scarcity in the thymus, which permits the escape of high-affinity T cells from central deletion. These autoreactive clones subsequently become candidates for selective tolerization in the periphery. Nanoparticle-based strategies exploit this principle through anatomical targeting; following intravenous or subcutaneous administration, formulations such as those composed of PLGA, liposomal bilayers, or gold are preferentially taken up by phagocytic cells in the spleen and liver. Within these compartments, the nanoparticles reprogram DCs and macrophages toward a tolerogenic phenotype characterized by high PD-L1 expression, IL-10 production, and limited costimulatory signals. This localized action minimizes systemic exposure to encapsulated tolerogenic agents like rapamycin, vitamin D3 or TGF-β [ ].
The translational potential of this approach is supported by its progression to early-stage clinical trials. In seropositive RA (ACPA⁺), a randomized phase I trial demonstrated that liposomes co-loaded with pools of citrullinated peptides and calcitriol were safe, reduced peptide-specific Th1/Th17 responses, and showed signals of clinical improvement (DAS28), thereby supporting the feasibility of epitope-focused tolerization in established disease [ ]. Similarly, in multiple sclerosis, strategies using either autologous peripheral blood cells chemically coupled with myelin peptides or CD206-targeted mannosylated liposomes presenting myelin epitopes induced dose-dependent reductions in antigen-specific T cell proliferation and proinflammatory cytokine production, without inducing broad immunosuppression [ ].
These formulations promote three complementary tolerance mechanisms: clonal anergy induction in autoreactive T cells, expansion of antigen-specific regulatory T cells, and epitope-specific B cell deletion. The multiple sclerosis trial demonstrated 60% reduction in antigen-reactive T cells and decreased gadolinium-enhancing MRI lesions, providing clinical validation without provoking generalized immunosuppression [ ]. This approach represents a paradigm shift toward precision immunomodulation, where nanoparticle engineering enables targeted delivery to specific lymphoid compartments, potentially revolutionizing management of chronic autoimmune conditions through epitope-specific suppression rather than broad immunosuppression. Selected clinical trials leveraging neoantigen/autoantigen-directed tolerance strategies in autoimmune diseases are summarized in .
 In contrast to conventional pan-immunosuppressants like methotrexate or corticosteroids, which non-specifically elevate risks of infection and malignancy, neoantigen-specific tolerance strategies aim to selectively re-educate autoreactive clones without compromising global immunity [ ]. Preclinical evidence substantiates this approach; in murine models of collagen-induced or citrullination-driven arthritis, nanoparticles co-delivering disease-specific peptides with tolerogenic agents like rapamycin reduced pathology and autoantibodies while maintaining immunocompetence [ , ]. Similarly, in NOD mice, nanoparticles encapsulating proinsulin or other diabetogenic antigens expanded antigen-specific FoxP3⁺ Tregs, delayed diabetes onset, and preserved β-cell function, with effects persisting post-treatment [ ]. Collectively, these findings validate the principle that epitope-focused tolerance induction can promote durable remission with a superior safety profile compared to broad immunosuppression [ ].

6.4.

## Prophylactic and early intervention vaccines
Neoantigen vaccines are being reconceptualized for cancer interception in high-risk populations, representing a fundamental shift from therapeutic to preventive oncology. Lynch syndrome carriers (germline MLH1/MSH2 mutations) universally develop frameshift neoantigens in microsatellite-unstable genes: TGFBR2 (85% incidence), ACVR2A (78%), and ASTE1 (67%), providing ideal targets for population-based vaccines [ , ]. A phase I prevention trial vaccinated cancer-free Lynch carriers with frameshift peptides, inducing T cells against multiple vaccine epitopes in all participants. Endoscopic surveillance revealed significantly reduced aberrant crypt foci versus unvaccinated controls, suggesting biological efficacy in intercepting early carcinogenesis [ , ].
Minimal residual disease eradication represents another interceptive strategy, leveraging post-resection vaccination when tumor burden is minimal and lymphatic function intact. Circulating tumor DNA-guided vaccines in colorectal cancer and adjuvant mRNA vaccines in melanoma exploit this therapeutic window, capitalizing on diminished immunosuppressive factors [ , , ]. For smoking-associated lung cancer prevention, high-risk individuals with premalignant atypical adenomatous hyperplasia represent candidates for vaccines targeting recurrent mutations: KRAS G12C/V (28% prevalence), TP53 R248Q/R273H (42%), and EGFR L858R (15% in never-smokers) [ ]. Ongoing biomarker studies are defining immunological eligibility criteria for prevention trials, potentially enabling preemptive strike against oncogenesis before clinical manifestation.
The clinical evolution of neoantigen therapies reveals three transformative trends. First, the field is transitioning from resource-intensive personalization toward standardized platforms targeting public neoantigens, recurrent driver mutations, viral oncoproteins, and frameshift peptide pools for MSI-H cancers, enabling off-the-shelf solutions accessible beyond specialized centers [ , ]. Second, therapeutic efficacy requires mechanistic hybridization: neoantigen vaccines combined with ICIs sustain effector T cell function, ACT integrated with epigenetic modulators counteracts exhaustion, and tolerogenic nanoparticles coupled with cytokine engineering selectively expand regulatory compartments. Third, applications now span the disease continuum: prophylactic vaccines for genetic cancer syndromes, interceptive strategies for minimal residual disease, and established disease management in oncology, mirrored by pre-symptomatic tolerization in autoimmunity. Persistent challenges include reducing manufacturing costs below $50,000/dose and production timelines under 4 weeks, overcoming antigen escape through clonal evolution, and ensuring global access equity [ , , ]. The integration of single-cell multi-omics, spatial transcriptomics, and artificial intelligence-based immunogenicity prediction will address these limitations, potentially enabling curative precision immunomodulation across the pathological spectrum as these innovations mature into clinical practice.
Despite demonstrating safety and consistent immunogenicity in early-phase trials, personalized neoantigen vaccines—delivered as peptide, DNA, or mRNA platforms—have thus far produced objective response rates and survival outcomes that are often comparable to checkpoint inhibitor monotherapy in similar, heavily pretreated metastatic populations [ , , ]. This disconnect between robust T-cell priming and modest clinical efficacy is multifactorial [ , ]. Contributing challenges include suboptimal neoantigen prediction pipelines that select non-immunogenic or subclonal targets, profoundly immunosuppressive TMEs in advanced disease that impair T-cell function and trafficking, and the emergence of antigen-loss variants that can outpace static vaccine formulations [ , ]. In addition, protracted manufacturing timelines can delay vaccine administration beyond a potential therapeutic window [ ]. Consequently, future success hinges on refining neoantigen selection, combining vaccines with potent TME–modulating agents, and optimizing patient selection and treatment timing to translate immunogenicity into durable clinical efficacy.

7.

## Challenges and future perspectives
Despite substantial progress in neoantigen-targeted immunotherapy, persistent limitations constrain its broad clinical efficacy. Correspondingly, B schematically links tumor heterogeneity, HLA diversity, antigen loss, diminished MHC-I expression and prolonged manufacturing timelines as interrelated bottlenecks that collectively constrain the scalability, equity and durability of neoantigen-based immunotherapies. The dynamic co-evolution of tumors and host immunity presents inherent complexities that remain incompletely captured in therapeutic design [ ]. This section delineates key challenges and emerging counter strategies.
7.1.

## Precision in neoantigen validation
A fundamental challenge lies in definitively establishing the immunogenicity of tumor-derived mutations. Current computational pipelines generate numerous putative neoantigens, yet only a minority elicit robust in vivo T-cell responses [ ]. This uncertainty complicates vaccine design. Inclusion of excessive peptides increases manufacturing complexity and may dilute immunogenicity, while overly restrictive selection risks omitting critical antigens. Experimental validation, such as patient T-cell screening against candidate peptides, faces practical constraints due to the urgency of therapeutic initiation [ ]. Consequently, this elevates the risk of “vaccine miss”—the administration of vaccines lacking therapeutically relevant epitopes. Refinement of predictive algorithms and development of rapid, high-throughput T-cell assays are critical research priorities.
Conventional exome sequencing often fails to detect non-canonical neoantigens originating from gene fusions, non-coding regions, or atypical translation events (e.g., alternate reading frames in 5′ UTRs or non-coding RNAs) [ ]. Advanced methodologies like ribosome profiling (Ribo-seq) and proteogenomics offer promise for identifying these variants but currently lack standardization. Furthermore, significant overestimation of functional neoantigens occurs, as many predicted MHC binders lack demonstrable immunogenicity [ ]. Rigorous validation incorporating MS-based immunopeptidomics and confirmation of T-cell reactivity enhances specificity but drastically reduces candidate numbers [ ]. Optimizing the sensitivity-specificity balance in neoantigen identification thus remains a paramount challenge for the field.

7.2.

## Addressing tumor heterogeneity and evolution
Tumors exhibit significant spatial and temporal heterogeneity, typically comprising multiple subclones with distinct mutational profiles [ ]. Targeting subclonal neoantigens risks therapeutic failure, as clones lacking the targeted antigen may proliferate unchecked, driving relapse—a scenario analogous to partial tumor targeting [ ]. Immunoediting under selective pressure, whether from natural immune surveillance or therapeutic intervention, further complicates this dynamic: immunogenic clones are eliminated, favoring the expansion of antigen-loss escape variants. This phenomenon is observed clinically. T-cell-inflamed tumors frequently show evidence of neoantigen editing, while immunologically “cold” tumors may retain high-quality neoantigens due to reduced immune pressure that would otherwise eliminate antigen-bearing clones [ ]. The targetable neoantigen repertoire itself can evolve temporally or differ substantially across metastatic sites. Biopsies provide only a temporal snapshot; by the time therapies (vaccines, engineered T cells) are manufactured and administered, the dominant tumor clone or antigen landscape may have shifted significantly. Targeting neoantigen “A” identified in an initial biopsy may prove ineffective against a metastasis dominated by a subclone expressing neoantigen “B”.
Strategies to mitigate heterogeneity and evolution include multi-site tumor sampling when feasible and prioritizing truncal neoantigens present in all tumor cells to minimize the likelihood of immune escape [ ]. Reflecting this, current clinical trials often incorporate vaccines targeting up to 20 neoantigens to broaden coverage. Serial monitoring via liquid biopsy (analysis of circulating tumor DNA) offers significant potential for real-time adaptation, enabling the detection of emerging resistance mutations or the loss of targeted epitopes (which may signify effective immune pressure). Although not yet standard clinical practice, this monitoring capability could theoretically enable dynamic modification of immunotherapy (e.g., altering vaccine composition or T-cell products) as the tumor evolves.

7.3.

## Overcoming low mutational burden and immunosuppressive TME
Tumors characterized by low TMB, including many pediatric cancers, prostate adenocarcinoma, and PDAC, present a critical challenge due to the scarcity of endogenous immunogenic neoantigens. This scarcity largely explains the frequently inadequate responses of such malignancies to ICIs [ ]. Effective strategies necessitate combination approaches, such as employing radiotherapy or oncolytic viruses to enhance antigen release and induce inflammatory changes. Investigational approaches explore introducing exogenous immunogenic antigens via viral vectors to create synthetic neoantigen-like targets and initiate an immune response capable of spreading to target the tumor itself.
The problem is frequently compounded by the presence of an immunosuppressive TME, which can prevent robust T-cell responses even against validated neoantigens [ ]. These “cold” tumors exhibit poor T-cell infiltration and function due to deficient chemokine expression, physical barriers (e.g., dense stroma or desmoplasia), and/or an abundance of immunosuppressive cells (Tregs, myeloid-derived suppressor cells - MDSCs). Reduced baseline immune pressure in these settings paradoxically may preserve high-quality neoantigens. Effective therapy thus requires a dual approach: introducing neoantigens and fundamentally modifying the inhibitory TME.
Strategies under investigation include combining neoantigen vaccines with agents designed to modulate the TME. Examples include TGF-β inhibitors or CSF1R inhibitors to deplete tumor-promoting macrophages. Radiotherapy can be employed to induce immunogenic cell death, stimulate chemokine production, and upregulate MHC expression, potentially converting “immune deserts” into “immune-active” sites. Oncolytic viruses or specific chemotherapies may also promote inflammation and enhance the recruitment and activity of TILs. Low intrinsic neoantigen burden and an inhibitory TME represent interconnected barriers: the absence of suitable targets or the inability of immune effectors to engage them effectively within the tumor. A successful treatment requires addressing both facets concurrently.

7.4.

## Managing interpatient immunological heterogeneity
Significant interpatient variability exists in immunological competence and the inherent capacity to generate effective anti-tumor T-cell immunity. Factors including age, genetic background, comorbidities, gut microbiome composition, and concurrent immunosuppressive therapies can profoundly impair T-cell priming, expansion, or effector function, irrespective of neoantigen validity. Furthermore, the TME itself can vary drastically in its ability to aggressively exclude or suppress infiltrating immune cells. This heterogeneity means a neoantigen vaccine inducing regression in one patient may fail in another, not due to antigen invalidity, but because the recipient’s immune system cannot successfully engage it.
Neoantigens themselves also exhibit intrinsic variability in immunogenicity. Those derived from driver mutations (ubiquitous and often essential for tumor survival) frequently elicit stronger and more durable T-cell responses compared to those arising from passenger mutations in genes with low expression [ ]. Identifying neoantigens that are both effectively presented by tumor MHC and crucial for tumor fitness is advantageous, as the tumor cannot readily downregulate them without incurring a fitness penalty.
Counter strategies focus on overcoming this variability. Combination therapies are central, employing systemic immune stimulants administered post-vaccination, such as IL-2 agonists or agonistic antibodies targeting co-stimulatory receptors (e.g., OX40, CD40), to boost the magnitude and quality of T-cell responses across diverse patients. Careful patient selection is also critical; personalized vaccines may demonstrate maximal efficacy in settings of minimal residual disease (e.g., post-surgical resection) or in patients exhibiting pre-existing, albeit suboptimal, immune responses to the tumor, rather than in those with advanced, bulky disease and profound immunosuppression. In the context of adoptive T-cell therapies, lymphodepleting chemotherapy prior to infusion can enhance engraftment and efficacy by eliminating regulatory cells and inhibitory factors, while also creating lymphoid space and increasing availability of homeostatic cytokines for the infused cells.

7.5.

## Streamlining manufacturing and logistics
The inherent complexity and cost associated with developing truly individualized therapies for each patient pose significant translational hurdles. Neoantigen vaccine production necessitates a sequential multi-step process: tumor tissue acquisition and sequencing, bioinformatic analysis for neoantigen prediction and prioritization, bespoke manufacturing (peptide synthesis or mRNA production), and subsequent clinical administration—all ideally completed while the patient’s disease remains stable [ ]. Similarly, personalized TIL or TCR-engineered T-cell (TCR-T) therapies involve intricate, time-sensitive cell processing steps including expansion, genetic modification, and rigorous quality control [ ]. These logistical obstacles currently restrict access primarily to specialized tertiary care centers, often only within the confines of clinical trials. Protracted manufacturing timelines, often spanning several weeks, further render these approaches impractical for patients with rapidly progressive disease who require urgent intervention. To reconcile the need for tumor-specific targeting with practical feasibility, semi-personalized strategies are being actively developed.
Key approaches include the creation of “off-the-shelf” allogeneic TCR-engineered T cells designed to target public neoantigens (shared mutations occurring in specific HLA contexts) within defined patient populations. These can be pre-manufactured and banked, allowing eligible patients to receive treatment promptly. Similarly, vaccines can be developed for cohorts of patients sharing a common oncogenic driver hotspot mutation and HLA allele, such as a KRAS-G12D vaccine for individuals expressing HLA-A*11:01 [ ]. This semi-personalized strategy significantly enhances scalability, reduces costs, and shortens time-to-treatment, while still maintaining alignment with the patient’s tumor genetics. Significant regulatory and quality control challenges persist, however, as each patient receives a unique or semi-unique biological product. Standardizing manufacturing methodologies and establishing robust, consistent analytical release criteria are critical ongoing efforts within the field.
Beyond technical manufacturing hurdles, the real-world application of personalized neoantigen therapies is currently constrained by several health-system–level operational barriers beyond the underlying technical challenges. The end-to-end workflow—encompassing sample acquisition, sequencing, bioinformatic analysis, GMP manufacturing, and treatment administration—remains poorly integrated across institutions, resulting in fragmented responsibilities, redundant quality-control measures, and inconsistent turnaround times. Furthermore, the limited availability of specialized GMP and bioinformatics infrastructure restricts access to a small number of centers, exacerbating geographic and socioeconomic disparities. Existing regulatory and reimbursement frameworks, designed for conventional, static biologics, are ill-suited to highly customized and rapidly iterated products, creating complex approval pathways and uncertain payer coverage. An additional critical rate-limiting factor is the scarcity of a highly specialized workforce proficient in molecular pathology, bioinformatics, cell-therapy technology, and regulatory affairs. Overcoming these multifaceted bottlenecks will require the development of standardized, interoperable digital pipelines; modular and scalable GMP platforms; harmonized regulatory guidelines for individualized therapeutics; and sustainable reimbursement models that acknowledge the distinct cost structure and societal value of personalized neoantigen therapies.

7.6.

## Innovative directions and future perspective
Robust research momentum in neoantigen therapies continues, driven by preclinical and early clinical insights shaping novel solutions. To overcome immunoediting and antigen loss evasion, contemporary vaccine trials increasingly employ a dual strategy: clonal neoantigens target the core tumor population, while selected subclonal neoantigens aim to preempt the emergence of distinct escape mutants. Furthermore, to address interpatient variability in immune competence, many trials integrate neoantigen vaccines with immune modulators like IL-2 agonists or co-stimulatory antibodies (e.g., targeting 4–1BB, OX40, CD40), enhancing the post-vaccination immune milieu.
Refining the optimal timing of neoantigen-targeted therapy is critical. Current data indicate that personalized vaccines yield maximal efficacy in adjuvant/minimal residual disease settings (e.g., post-resection or early-stage cancer), leveraging low tumor burden and a less compromised immune system. This contrasts sharply with limited efficacy in advanced, bulky disease. Unifying these insights, the field is progressing toward adaptive methodologies, ultimately aiming for real-time, dynamically personalized immunotherapy. This paradigm would entail comprehensive, longitudinal profiling of the patient’s tumor (genomics, transcriptomics, proteomics) and immune status (HLA type, TCR repertoire, immune cell subsets, soluble factors). The integrated dataset would then guide a customized, multi-modal strategy. For example, an “integrative neoantigen therapy” regimen could combine surgical debulking to reduce tumor mass, a neoantigen vaccine to prime and activate T cells against residual disease, immune checkpoint blockade to overcome inhibitory signals and sustain the immune response, and subsequent adoptive T-cell transfer to further eliminate residual cells, all meticulously coordinated within a systematic treatment plan.
Machine learning and artificial intelligence are poised to play a pivotal role in personalized, neoantigen-targeted therapy [ , ]. Advanced algorithms can analyze a patient’s immunogenomic profile to predict optimal neoantigen-immunomodulatory drug combinations; identify non-responders due to TCR repertoire constraints; and recommend alternative targets or adjunctive therapies. Large-scale collaborative data pooling across institutions may accelerate progress by uncovering statistically significant predictive patterns.
Scalable personalization requires advanced industrial infrastructure. To support growing clinical adoption of personalized immunotherapies, adaptable manufacturing pipelines must manage multiple bespoke products concurrently. Cost innovations include hospital-based rapid mRNA production units or centralized facilities processing hundreds of patient-specific TCR-T batches simultaneously. Semi-personalized approaches targeting shared neoantigens will serve as critical stepping stones, enhancing accessibility while bridging fully customized and standardized therapies.
The foundational science and technologies of neoantigen research are expanding significantly beyond oncology. In infectious diseases, pathogens generate targetable “quasi-neoantigens” through host genome integration or mutant proteins, enabling vaccine or adoptive T-cell strategies. Similarly, technologies and algorithms (e.g., immunopeptidomics), initially applied to cancer neoantigen discovery, now deciphers autoimmune pathogenesis, identifying disease-driving MHC-presented self-antigens. Precise mapping of these autoantigens enables novel tolerogenic therapies designed to selectively suppress pathogenic immune responses without systemic immunosuppression.

8.

## Conclusions
The burgeoning field of neoantigen research has unequivocally established tumor-specific neoantigens, arising from somatic mutations absent in normal tissues, as fundamental and actionable targets for precision immunotherapy. Their unique origin confers the critical advantage of enabling highly specific immune responses against malignant cells while circumventing the significant risk of autoimmunity associated with targeting shared tumor antigens. This intrinsic specificity provides the essential biological rationale underpinning the development of personalized therapeutic strategies, most notably neoantigen-directed vaccines and adoptive T-cell therapies, designed to selectively harness the patient’s immune system against their individual cancer.
A transformative paradigm shift has been driven by the synergistic integration of advanced discovery platforms. High-sensitivity MS-based immunopeptidomics now directly identifies peptides presented by MHC molecules on tumor cells. This powerful technique is increasingly coupled with comprehensive multi-omics analyses – encompassing genomics to define mutations, transcriptomics to assess expression, and proteomics to confirm translation – creating a holistic view of the tumor’s antigenic landscape. Furthermore, sophisticated machine learning algorithms are being integrated into bioinformatics pipelines, significantly enhancing the accuracy of predicting critical steps like MHC binding affinity, antigen processing efficiency, and ultimately, immunogenicity. This convergence of cutting-edge technologies is crucial for reliably distinguishing therapeutically relevant neoantigens from the vast background of passenger mutations within complex tumor genomes, thereby substantially improving the feasibility and precision of designing truly personalized immunotherapies.
Clinically, the validation of the neoantigen concept is increasingly evident, particularly with advancements in vaccine delivery platforms. mRNA-based neoantigen vaccines stand out, demonstrating promising efficacy signals in early-phase clinical trials for solid tumors. These results not only validate the core principle of targeting patient-specific neoantigens but also establish a tangible and viable clinical pathway for personalized immunotherapy, moving the field decisively beyond initial proof-of-concept studies. However, the inherent challenges of tumor heterogeneity, antigen loss variants, and immunosuppressive mechanisms mean monotherapeutic neoantigen-targeting approaches often face limitations in achieving deep and durable responses. Consequently, combinatorial strategies are emerging as essential. Integrating neoantigen-directed interventions, whether vaccines or adoptive T-cell transfer, with modalities designed to overcome immune resistance – such as immune checkpoint blockade to reverse T-cell exhaustion, co-stimulatory agonists to amplify activation, or agents targeting the immunosuppressive TME – holds significant potential for enhancing therapeutic efficacy and overcoming escape mechanisms.
Despite substantial progress, significant challenges define the critical path forward. These include the ongoing need to reliably identify all immunogenic neoantigens, especially those presented at low abundance or derived from non-canonical sources; effectively managing the dynamic processes of tumor heterogeneity and immunoediting that drive resistance; streamlining the complex, time-sensitive manufacturing processes to improve scalability, accessibility, and cost-effectiveness; and crucially, extending the demonstrable efficacy of neoantigen therapies to immunologically "cold" tumors with low mutational burden and to a broader spectrum of cancer types beyond the current successes. Addressing these multifaceted hurdles is paramount for broadening patient eligibility, maximizing clinical benefit, and ensuring equitable access to these personalized treatments.
In conclusion, the rapid evolution of neoantigen research underscores its profound transformative potential for precision oncology. By providing a mechanism to specifically engage the patient’s own immune system against truly tumor-restricted targets, neoantigen-based strategies represent a compelling path towards highly effective and individualized cancer treatments, potentially offering lower toxicity profiles compared to conventional cytotoxic therapies. Moreover, the sophisticated methodologies developed in this pursuit – integrated multi-omics, advanced MS, and machine learning-driven prediction – are not only accelerating immunotherapy development but also yielding fundamental insights into the intricate biology of antigen presentation and immune recognition. Successfully navigating the remaining translational and clinical challenges holds the promise of establishing neoantigen-targeted immunotherapy as a cornerstone of future cancer care. Furthermore, the deep insights gained into orchestrating antigen-specific immune responses and modulating tolerance boundaries may ultimately inform novel therapeutic approaches for autoimmune diseases.
