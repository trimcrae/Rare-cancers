## Introduction
Kirsten rat sarcoma viral (KRAS) is a highly prevalent oncogene with the highest mutation frequency in various cancer types, such as colorectal cancer (CRC), non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), and pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) [ ]. For over three decades, KRAS has been a well-validated therapeutic target, yet its unique structural features have rendered it largely “undruggable”. The primary challenges are the absence of distinct, high-affinity small-molecule binding pockets and the protein’s exceptionally strong affinity for intracellular guanosine triphosphate (GTP), posing a substantial competitive challenge for inhibitor design. Consequently, the development of specific inhibitors to block KRAS-driven oncogenesis remains a formidable hurdle [ ]. However, recent advancements in therapeutic modeling, medicinal chemistry, and genomic sequencing have led to groundbreaking progress in KRAS-targeted therapy.
The development of KRAS-specific inhibitors has garnered significant attention. In 2013, Ostrem, Shokat, and colleagues utilized a novel disulfide tethering technique to generate a new class of direct KRAS[G12C] inhibitors. These compounds target an allosteric pocket adjacent to the cysteine residue, known as the switch II pocket, enabling the direct targeting of mutant KRAS while preserving the wild-type KRAS protein [ , ]. Sotorasib and adagrasib, the first FDA-approved KRAS-targeted agents, exhibit notable antitumor activity against various KRAS[G12C]-mutant malignancies, representing a significant advancement in targeted therapies for tumors with KRAS mutations. Despite promising initial outcomes with KRAS[G12C]-specific inhibitors, substantial challenges need to be addressed to optimize the therapeutic impact of KRAS-targeted treatments. Recent clinical data suggest that resistance to KRAS[G12C] inhibitors can emerge through diverse mechanisms. The increasing complexity of resistance mechanisms highlights the importance of combination strategies to enhance patient response rates to KRAS inhibitors. These strategies entail combining KRAS inhibitors with other targeted therapies such as SHP2 or EGFR inhibitors [ ], emphasizing the critical need to comprehend KRAS inhibitor resistance mechanisms and develop effective strategies to enhance clinical efficacy.
The MYC family has been deemed “undruggable” for many years and ranks among the most extensively studied families of oncogenes, posing threats to many cancer patients [ , ]. Amplification, translocation, and overexpression of MYC frequently occur, whereas mutations in the MYC gene itself are rare in cancer [ ]. Analysis of approximately 9,000 human tumor samples across a diverse range of cancer types revealed that 28% of human malignancies exhibit amplification of at least one MYC gene. c-MYC amplification was most prevalent in breast (30%), squamous cell lung (37.2%), esophageal (45.3%), and ovarian cancers (64%) [ , ]. MYC has also been considered an “undruggable” target due to the lack of enzymatically active sites and a hydrophobic pocket, its high-affinity interaction with the obligate partner MYC associated factor X (MAX), and its location in the nucleus, hindering its binding with low-molecular-weight compounds and posing an ongoing obstacle for the development of effective MYC inhibitors [ , ].
Numerous studies have indicated that the interaction between KRAS and MYC serves as a model for oncogene cooperation. The activation of KRAS and MYC often contributes to tumorigenesis. In vitro assays have demonstrated that individual oncogenes cannot easily transform cultured cells into tumorigenic cells; however, the combined activation of KRAS and MYC can convert normal primary rat embryonic fibroblasts into tumorigenic cells [ ]. Recent reports have suggested that KRAS and MYC collaborate by regulating intracellular processes more precisely and by influencing the host tumor microenvironment and immune system [ ]. Therefore, the specific interdependence between these two oncogenes requires an investigation into the exact mechanisms through which KRAS and MYC cooperate in the complex processes underlying oncogenesis. In this review, we discuss recent advances in understanding the collaborative effects of KRAS and MYC on metabolic reprogramming and epigenetic regulation. We further examine the role of these interactions in shaping the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment through interactions with immune cell populations. We also summarize recent therapeutic advances in anti-KRAS treatment strategies and the challenges of treatment resistance to KRAS-specific inhibitors, particularly in relation to MYC dysregulation. Understanding the relationship between MYC dysregulation and resistance to KRAS-specific inhibitors provides valuable insights for developing more effective combination treatment strategies for KRAS-driven cancers.

## KRAS and MYC: from independent drive to synergistic carcinogenesis
KRAS and MYC are among the most frequently altered oncogenes that are prevalent in numerous malignancies. These two oncogenes, acting either independently or cooperatively, modulate multiple cancer hallmarks, including proliferation, metastasis, apoptosis, and self-renewal. Emerging evidence has demonstrated their synergistic roles in regulating tumor immune escape. Herein, we highlight the distinct individual carcinogenic mechanisms of KRAS and MYC, as well as their cooperative networks that drive malignant progression.

## Carcinogenic mechanisms of activating KRAS in cancer
The KRAS gene encodes a GTP/guanosine diphosphate (GDP)-binding protein belonging to the guanosine triphosphatase (GTPase) RAS family. KRAS is a pivotal cellular “molecular switch” that integrates upstream signals from receptor tyrosine kinases (RTKs) and transmits them into multiple downstream effector pathways, thereby promoting cell survival and proliferation [ , ]. The KRAS protein cycles between an inactive GDP-bound state and an active GTP-bound state [ , ]. This interconversion is tightly regulated by guanine nucleotide exchange factors (GEFs) and GTPase-activating proteins (GAPs). GAPs promote GTP hydrolysis to maintain KRAS in its inactive state [ ]. In contrast, the active state is induced by GEFs, such as the son of sevenless (SOS) proteins. GEFs bind to KRAS and induce conformational rearrangements in the Switch I and Switch II regions, thereby disrupting the high-affinity GDP-binding pocket and promoting its release. This results in a transient, nucleotide-free KRAS-GEF complex, from which the GEF subsequently dissociates. The high intracellular concentration of GTP then enables its binding, leading to KRAS activation [ ]. Upon GTP binding, KRAS undergoes a conformational switch that allows it to engage and activate downstream effectors, including the rapidly accelerated fibrosarcoma (RAF)-mitogen-activated protein kinase (MEK)–extracellular signal-regulated kinase (ERK) and phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase (PI3K)-protein kinase B (AKT)-mechanistic target of rapamycin (mTOR) pathways. These pathways support cell proliferation and survival. Conversely, GDP-bound KRAS is inactive and inhibits continuous signal activation [ ].
However, KRAS mutations stabilize the active state of the GTP-bound form, thereby promoting uncontrolled cell proliferation and survival. KRAS ranks among the most frequently mutated oncogenes, with hotspot mutations predominantly occurring at codon 12 (e.g., KRAS G12C, G12V, and G12D) across numerous malignancies, including NSCLC, CRC, and PDAC [ ]. This persistent activation contributes to the continuous stimulation of downstream signaling pathways, thereby promoting tumor cell growth and survival. Therefore, KRAS mutations correlate with poor tumor differentiation, advanced disease stage, inferior survival rates, and the presence of distant metastasis in patients [ ]. Emerging research has examined the role of KRAS mutations in reshaping the tumor microenvironment as well as the metabolic and epigenetic changes caused by numerous molecular activities. The underlying mechanisms are discussed in subsequent sections. Collectively, we elaborated on the molecular function of KRAS, signaling pathways, and the KRAS mutations as a key carcinogenic trigger in tumor progression.

## Carcinogenic mechanisms of activating MYC in cancer
MYC is one of the most extensively studied oncogenes and is implicated in the initiation, maintenance, and progression of various cancer types [ ]. The MYC gene family comprises three members: c-MYC, L-MYC, and N-MYC [ ]. Dysregulation of MYC gene expression has been implicated in up to 70% of human tumors [ , ]. Given its extensive dysregulation and pivotal role in driving cancer initiation and progression, targeting MYC represents a critical strategy for cancer therapy. However, MYC has long been considered an “undruggable” target. Whereas KRAS oncogenes are typically activated by mutations, MYC dysregulation is driven by a variety of mechanisms, including gene amplification, chromosomal translocation, retroviral promoter insertion, enhanced cellular signaling, and modified protein degradation [ , ]. Among these mechanisms, gene amplification appears to be one of the most prevalent means of MYC activation in solid tumors. Recent research conducted by The Cancer Genome Atlas network, which analyzed approximately 9,000 samples across 33 tumor categories, revealed that 28% of human cancers exhibit amplification of at least one MYC gene. c-MYC amplification was observed at the highest frequency in ovarian cancer (64%), followed by esophageal cancer (45.3%), squamous cell lung cancer (37.2%), and breast cancer (30%) [ ]. In contrast to the frequently amplified c-MYC, amplifications of N-MYC and L-MYC are rare events in cancers, occurring at an overall frequency of less than 7% [ ]. Despite this low overall incidence, N-MYC and L-MYC amplification is highly enriched in specific tumor types. For instance, amplification of N-MYC has been identified in tumors with neuroendocrine characteristics, such as retinoblastoma and medulloblastoma [ ]. On the other hand, L-MYC has been reported to be overexpressed in a limited subset of small-cell lung cancers [ ]. In summary, extensive research over the past several decades has established a causal role of MYC dysregulation in cancer development. A unified model for MYC activity was proposed by seminal findings by Lin et al. [ ] and Nie et al. [ ], which challenged the widely held belief that MYC acts by specifically activating or suppressing distinct groups of target genes. Instead, both studies showed that MYC occupies the promoters of all actively transcribed genes in a concentration-dependent manner, as demonstrated using inducible MYC expression systems together with genome-wide ChIP-seq and transcriptome analysis. Importantly, the binding density of MYC on the promoter is linearly correlated with the gene expression level. The main function of MYC is to amplify the transcriptional output of existing gene expression programs rather than to turn genes on or off. Mechanistically, MYC promotes the release of RNA polymerase II from the proximal paused state at the promoter by recruiting P-TEFb, enabling it to enter the effective transcription elongation stage, which provides a unified explanation for the multifaceted effects of MYC in cancer, including cell growth, proliferation, metabolism and apoptosis [ ]. These results suggest that targeting the transcription elongation process may be a reasonable strategy for combating MYC-driven malignant tumors.

## Synergistic carcinogenesis of KRAS and MYC in solid tumors
KRAS and MYC represent a paradigm of oncogene cooperation, a concept firmly established since their interdependence was first demonstrated in 1983 and further evidenced by recent findings on their joint role in immune suppression [ ]. Researchers have demonstrated that the transformation from primary embryo fibroblasts to neoplastic cells by a human RAS oncogene alone is insufficient, but tumorigenic conversion can be achieved by the co-introduction of RAS with a second oncogene, such as MYC [ , ]. Given the synergistic carcinogenesis of KRAS and MYC, understanding the crosstalk between these pathways is crucial for revealing the fundamental mechanisms of tumor maintenance and developing novel and effective therapies for KRAS-driven cancers.

## KRAS: the key upstream activator of MYC
The KRAS mutation is a highly effective mechanism for activating MYC, significantly enhancing its oncogenic role at the transcriptional, translational, and protein stability levels through the MAPK and PI3K pathways. KRAS influences MYC transcriptional activity via the MAPK-ERK pathway. Hollander D, et al. reported that mutant KRAS triggers the MAPK pathway, leading to overexpression of ELK1, which in turn drives MYC transcription, ultimately leading to the accumulation of MYC [ ]. Furthermore, a study utilizing a KRAS-mutant mouse model emphasized that KRAS promotes c-MYC mRNA translation by activating mTORC, a downstream effector of KRAS, ultimately increasing c-MYC expression. Inhibiting both pathways reduces c-MYC protein levels, thereby decreasing cell proliferation and providing a highly effective c-MYC-dependent co-targeting strategy in various KRAS-mutant animal models [ ]. Another study demonstrated that the RAS/RAF/ERK pathway extends the half-life of MYC, thereby enhancing its stability. KRAS-driven ERK signaling initiates a two-step regulatory cascade by first phosphorylating MYC at Ser 62, which stabilizes the MYC protein and increases its carcinogenic potential. This modification primes MYC for subsequent GSK-3-mediated phosphorylation at Thr 58. Phosphorylation at Thr 58 then serves as a binding site for the FBXW7 E3 ubiquitin ligase, ultimately leading to MYC ubiquitination and proteasomal degradation [ ]. A recent study revealed that KRAS-dependent activation of PITPNC1 stabilizes the MYC protein by activating MEK1/2 and JNK1/2. This signaling cascade controls mTOR localization to suppress autophagy, thereby promoting tumor growth, cell cycle progression, and cell proliferation in lung and pancreatic cancers [ ]. Collectively, mutant KRAS enhances MYC oncogenic activity and prevents MYC proteasomal degradation through downstream effectors, including PI3K and MAPK, ultimately facilitating MYC protein accumulation and stabilization.

## MYC: the core executor of the downstream effects of KRAS
MYC actively engages in and enhances oncogenic KRAS signals rather than serving as a passive downstream effector. Recent studies have highlighted the crucial role of MYC as a stress adaptation factor in KRAS-driven cancers, particularly under high metabolic demands driven by KRAS mutations [ ]. Knocking down MYC significantly impairs KRAS-driven tumor growth. Moreover, MYC can transcriptionally upregulate multiple RTKs (such as EGFR) and their ligands. Simultaneously, it inhibits the expression of miRNAs like let-7, a negative regulator of KRAS, indirectly enhancing RAS/MAPK signaling. This mechanism creates a robust positive feedback loop that sustains and amplifies oncogenic signals [ ].
Taken together, KRAS strongly drives MYC function at the transcriptional, translational, and protein stability levels through the MAPK and PI3K pathways. Conversely, MYC amplifies KRAS signaling by reprogramming transcription, forming a stable positive feedback loop. This loop jointly propels tumor proliferation, metabolic reprogramming, immune evasion, and treatment resistance.

## Interplay between KRAS and MYC in the tumor immune microenvironment
The collaborative roles of KRAS and MYC in tumorigenesis have been extensively researched since their discovery. By synergistically impacting all cancer hallmarks, these oncogenes coordinate the fundamental mechanisms of the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment. They play a role in immunosuppression and the recruitment of immunosuppressive cells to solid tumors [ ]. Despite this established interaction, a key question persists: How precisely do KRAS and MYC jointly regulate the tumor immune microenvironment?
The tumor microenvironment (TME) in solid malignancies is usually marked by the infiltration of various immune cells that are nurtured in the TME and eventually exhibit immunosuppressive characteristics. KRAS mutations and MYC dysregulation separately induce significant restructuring of the tumor immune microenvironment (TIME), fostering an immunosuppressive condition that facilitates immune evasion. However, numerous aspects of the interplay between KRAS and MYC in TIME are still unclear. The subsequent sections delineate the specific mechanisms governing the separate and collaborative interactions of KRAS and MYC with immune cell populations that facilitate cancer immune evasion (Fig.  ).

 

## Recruitment of myeloid-derived suppressor cells regulated by KRAS and MYC in cancer progression
Myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) constitute a heterogeneous population of immature myeloid cells with potent immunosuppressive functions. These cells promote tumor progression by facilitating metastasis and suppressing anti-tumor immune responses [ ]. In KRAS[G12D]-induced pancreatic malignancies, elevated interleukin 6 (IL-6) triggers the activation of the signal transducer and activator of transcription 3 (STAT3) -Socs3 signaling pathway, facilitating the recruitment of MDSCs to adjacent metastatic lesions, thereby promoting PDAC development [ ]. In addition, KRAS[G12D]-driven preinvasive pancreatic neoplasia upregulates functional IL-17 receptors, attracting IL-17-secreting immune cells to the pancreatic stroma. Disruption of IL-17 signaling impedes tumor proliferation and hinders MDSC infiltration [ ]. Moreover, mutations in KRAS[G12V] and KRAS[G12D] enhance MDSC accumulation in the TME through distinct mechanisms. In PDAC and CRC, active KRAS upregulates granulocyte-macrophage colony-stimulating factor (GM-CSF) levels, facilitating immune evasion [ ]. Furthermore, KRAS[G12D] suppresses interferon regulatory factor 2 (IRF2)-mediated interferon signaling, leading to increased CXCL3 secretion. This chemokine interacts with CXCR2 on MDSCs, promoting their migration to tumor sites [ ]. As observed for oncogenic KRAS, MYC amplification specifically recruits MDSCs in various cancer types. For instance, YTHDF2 effectively suppresses MYC expression, thereby reducing the recruitment of the immunosuppressive MDSCs and significantly inhibiting tumor growth by restoring T cell-mediated anti-tumor immune responses [ ]. A transgenic mouse model of MYC-driven multiple myeloma exhibited resistance to PD-1 immune checkpoint blockade therapy but displayed enhanced sensitivity after MDSC depletion, highlighting the potential of MDSCs to inhibit the activation of T cells [ ]. Secretion of GM-CSF and recruitment of MDSCs create an immunosuppressive environment, while the secretion of sonic hedgehog and TGFβ induces fibroblastic characteristics typical of PDAC [ ]. KRAS mutation and MYC overexpression can drive an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment through the recruitment of MDSCs, which suppress T cell activity and thereby enable tumor cells to evade immune surveillance.

## KRAS- and MYC-mediated recruitment of tumor-associated macrophages
Tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) represent one of the most abundant immunosuppressive populations within TME. High TAM infiltration correlates with poor prognosis in various cancers [ ]. Oncogenic RAS signaling upregulates CD47 expression in tumor cells, which binds to SIRPα on macrophages, transmitting a ‘don’t eat me’ signal that hinders macrophage-mediated phagocytosis of tumor cells. Consequently, KRAS-mutant cells evade the immune system through this macrophage checkpoint mechanism [ ]. Genomic and transcriptomic analysis indicated that gene amplification of MYC promotes tumor metastasis by recruiting TAMs [ , ]. In mouse models of hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), MYC and TWIST1 cooperate to release CCL2 and IL-13, promoting the recruitment and polarization of M2 macrophages [ ]. In a KRAS[G12D]-driven lung adenoma model, the interaction between KRAS and MYC transforms dormant KRAS-driven cancers into highly proliferative and invasive inflammatory adenocarcinomas. Mechanistically, MYC hyperactivation induces the secretion of CCL9, which recruits immunosuppressive macrophages and IL-23, leading to the exclusion of B cells, adaptive T cells, and innate immune NK cells. Simultaneous blocking of IL-23 and CCL9 effectively inhibits MYC-induced tumor progression [ ].

## KRAS- and MYC-regulated infiltration of tumor-associated neutrophils promoting cancer metastasis
Tumor-associated neutrophils (TANs) are classified into anti-tumor N1 and pro-tumor N2 subtypes, similar to the M1/M2 macrophage polarization [ ]. Previous research suggested that interleukin-8, an inflammatory mediator, is a transcriptional target of RAS signaling, initiating tumor-associated inflammation and angiogenesis [ ]. In CRC, mutant KRAS activation enhances IL-8 expression, leading to neutrophil recruitment and neutrophil extracellular traps formation, supporting CRC cell proliferation [ ]. Moreover, preclinical KRAS-mutant cancer models exhibit elevated expression of multiple CXCR2 ligands, such as CXCL1-3 and CXCL5 [ ]. These factors promote the infiltration of immunosuppressive myeloid cells, including monocytes and neutrophils, thereby dampening anti-tumor immune responses in the TME.

## KRAS- and MYC-mediated reduction of CD8+ T cell infiltration and promotion of immune escape
KRAS signaling in tumor cells affects the TME, suppressing tumor-infiltrating T cell function through various mechanisms. In KRAS-mutant lung tumors, elevated IL-6 production promotes regulatory T cell (Treg) infiltration and supports pro-tumorigenic activities [ ]. However, studies in pancreatic cancer have revealed a more complex role for IL-6. Recent evidence suggests that high concentrations of IL-6 can promote T cell-mediated anti-tumor immunity in the specific therapeutic context of TGFβ vaccination combined with immune checkpoint inhibitors and radiotherapy [ ]. Moreover, mutant KRAS boosts IL-10 and TGF-β secretion via the activated MEK-ERK-AP1 axis. This process drives the transformation of anti-tumorigenic CD4+FoxP3− T cells into immunosuppressive CD4+FoxP3+ Tregs in preclinical models of KRAS mutant NSCLC, PDAC, and CRC. These findings highlight the vital role of KRAS signaling in shaping an immunosuppressive TME. Intrinsic interferon (IFN) signaling within tumors enhances immune surveillance by increasing major histocompatibility complex (MHC) class I antigen presentation and promoting the secretion of T cell chemoattractants in the TME. Oncogenic KRAS suppresses IFN signaling in various cancer mouse models [ , , ]. Apart from diminishing MHC-I molecules, KRAS-induced immunosuppression can be initiated through PD-L1 mRNA stabilization, which directly inhibits cytotoxic T cell responses. Mechanistically, MEK signaling downstream of KRAS regulates the kinase MK2, leading to the phosphorylation and inhibition of tristetraprolin, a negative regulator of PD-L1 expression, promoting tumor immune evasion [ ]. In NSCLC, the KRAS[G12D] variant is associated with a distinct tumor immune profile compared to other KRAS mutation subtypes, characterized by a reduced tumor mutational burden, lower PD-L1 expression, and diminished CD8+ T cell infiltration within TME [ ]. Similarly, MYC induces immune escape by modulating T cells and reducing the expression of MHC-I molecules. For example, activated MYC disrupts antigen presentation by suppressing MHC class I antigen expression, correlating with decreased infiltration of highly cytotoxic CD8+T cells [ , ]. Additionally, MYC amplification downregulates T cell surveillance by inducing molecules such as CD276 and TGF-β [ , ]. Despite extensive research on the effects of KRAS and MYC on the immunosuppressive microenvironment, further investigation is needed to elucidate the mechanisms underlying their collaboration.
In preclinical lung cancer models, oncogenic KRAS-mediated MYC upregulation inhibits IFN signaling, concurrently promoting immunosuppressive cell infiltration while reducing cytotoxic T cell recruitment, activation, and antigen presentation [ ]. Deregulated MYC expression and KRAS[G12D] are conditionally activated, promoting the secretion of CCL9, which mediates the recruitment of immunosuppressive macrophages, and IL-23, which orchestrates the exclusion of B cells, adaptive T cells, and innate immune NK cells [ ]. This reduction limits the recruitment of T and NK cells while promoting increased infiltration of CD8+ T cells, thereby facilitating tumor immune evasion in numerous mouse preclinical models [ ]. Furthermore, PD-L1 and CD47, as immune checkpoints, act as key intermediaries in the cooperation between KRAS and MYC to facilitate immune evasion. Preclinical studies have demonstrated that the KRAS[G12D] mutation fosters an immunosuppressive TIME through two parallel mechanisms: it diminishes CD8+ T cell infiltration by downregulating CXCL10/CXCL11 via HMGA2, while concurrently suppressing PD-L1 expression through the PI3K/AKT/P70S6K signaling axis [ ]. In contrast, MYC directly upregulates the expression of nuclear Aurora kinase A (AURKA), inducing the expression of PD-L1 and CD47, which shield them from T cell attack [ , ]. In combination with a KRAS[G12C] inhibitor, innate immune surveillance is restored [ , ]. In general, the interconnection among KRAS mutations, MYC amplification, and PD-L1 or CD47 overexpression is a highly promising target for immunotherapy in various cancer types.
In summary, the collaboration between KRAS mutations and MYC amplification coordinates the immunosuppressive TME through various mechanisms. On the one hand, KRAS and MYC facilitate communication between distinct immune cell subsets and tumor cells in the TME by enhancing the influx of immune-suppressing cells and avoiding detection by cytotoxic cells. When KRAS and MYC are activated simultaneously, they promote immune evasion by jointly reducing MHC-I expression, increasing immune checkpoint levels, and releasing particular cytokines and chemokines, thus shielding tumor cells from cytotoxic T cells.

## Synergistic regulation of epigenetic modification in cancer by KRAS and MYC
Epigenetic alterations, such as DNA methylation [ ], histone modifications [ ], chromatin remodeling [ ], and regulatory non-coding RNAs [ ], play a pivotal role in cancer development. KRAS mutations induce substantial alterations in the epigenetic profile of cancer cells while also activating oncogenic signaling [ ]. However, the precise mechanisms by which KRAS and MYC synergistically influence epigenetic modifications are not fully understood. Here, we explore the synergistic effect of KRAS and MYC in shaping the TME by influencing epigenetic modification (Fig.  ).

 

As a fundamental epigenetic modification, DNA methylation is essential for controlling gene expression and preserving genomic stability [ ]. Mutant KRAS promotes cancer progression by creating abnormal DNA methylation patterns that silence tumor suppressors and activate oncogenes [ ]. Moreover, N6-methyladenosine (m6A) in mRNA has emerged as a prevalent epigenetic modification affecting cancer progression [ ]. A recent study elucidated a mechanism by which mutant KRAS induces m6A methylation of genes related to DNA damage repair. This modification stabilizes their mRNA, enhances DNA damage repair capacity in tumor cells, and contributes to drug resistance [ ]. In KRAS-mutated lung cancer, the absence of LKB1 promotes the transcription of AlkB family member 5 (ALKBH5), an m6A demethylase, through m6A modifications to the ALKBH5 promoter, upregulating ALKBH5 expression. Consequently, this process leads to the demethylation of m6A modifications on MYC mRNA, thereby enhancing MYC stability and ultimately promoting tumor aggressiveness in KRAS-mutant lung cancer. These findings were validated in clinical studies involving KRAS-mutated lung cancer patients, suggesting a novel therapeutic approach for tumorigenesis in cases of dual KRAS mutation and LKB1 loss [ ]. However, further clinical investigations are required to explore the combined treatment of KRAS mutations and MYC amplification.
Over the past two decades, numerous studies have highlighted the pivotal roles of non-coding RNAs (ncRNAs) in various cancer types. ncRNAs constitute a distinct class of RNA molecules that do not encode proteins but are essential for regulating gene expression and diverse cellular processes [ , ]. Among ncRNAs, long noncoding RNAs (lncRNAs) are predominantly found in the nucleus, where they are involved in chromatin remodeling, transcriptional regulation, and the formation of distinct nuclear condensates [ ]. Specifically, the lncRNA HIF1A-As2 was found to be upregulated in KRAS mutant NSCLC. Mechanistically, KRAS triggers the expression of MYC, leading to the transcriptional activation and upregulation of HIF1A-As2. HIF1A-As2 subsequently interacts directly with the RNA-binding protein DHX9, thereby promoting MYC transcription. In essence, HIF1A-As2, a KRAS-induced lncRNA, cooperates with MYC to establish a bidirectional regulatory loop that enhances tumor metastasis and proliferation. The substantial reduction in tumor growth achieved by small molecules targeting HIF1A-As2 and MYC highlights the potent synergistic impact of HIF1A-As2, KRAS, and MYC, revealing a critical clinical vulnerability in KRAS-driven NSCLC [ ].
Histone modifications, such as methylation, ubiquitination, acetylation, lactylation, and phosphorylation, act as epigenetic markers involved in transcription, DNA repair, and the stable upkeep of repressive chromatin [ ]. In a transgenic mouse expressing a mutated histone H3 lysine 27 (H3K27M), H3.3K27M promotes tumorigenesis through significant transcriptome reprogramming and epigenetic activation of the RAS/MYC signaling axis. These findings suggest that H3.3K27M-driven transcriptome restructuring is vital for cancer therapy [ ].
The co-mutation of oncogenic KRAS and TP53 occurs frequently and is a lethal cancer characteristic across various types of tumors. Sabe et al. indicated that KRAS promotes MYC gene expression and enhances the translation of ARF6e- and eIF4A-dependent MYC mRNA. Their relationship may be enhanced when TP53 is mutated; TP53 mutations elevate AMAP1 mRNA levels, improve the activation of ARF6, and promote G4 translation by improving energy production, all of which drive cancer cell invasion, metastasis, acidosis, and immune evasion [ ]. In summary, considering the interaction between KRAS and MYC in epigenetic mechanisms, solely targeting KRAS might not be adequate. It may be essential to interfere with the epigenetic mechanisms supporting the expression of MYC and KRAS for long-lasting responses in KRAS-driven cancers.

## Cooperative effect regulated by KRAS and MYC in the transcriptome of cancer cells
A transcriptome typically refers to the complete set of transcripts generated by a cell under physiological conditions. Carcinogenic transcription factors (TFs) promote transcriptional reprogramming and tumorigenesis. The dysregulation of TFs is a common event during cancer development [ ] (Fig.  ). MYC plays a crucial role in modulating the transcriptomic adaptation to stress. In CRC, mutant KRAS promotes MYC translation, stimulating MYC-dependent transcriptomic adaptation to stress with four elevated genes (GADD45B, DDIT3, HSPA1L and CRYBA4) and driving mutant KRAS CRC progression [ ]. A recent study indicated that MYC-associated zinc finger protein (MAZ), a member of the MYC family of TFs, is highly expressed in malignant specimens and promotes tumor progression by driving transcriptional reprogramming. Mechanistically, TF MAZ can significantly bind to the promoters of BRAF and KRAS, increasing their transcription and expression, thereby activating MAPK signaling and promoting tumor progression [ ]. Primary liver cancer (PLC) comprises two common and deadly tumor types: HCC and intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma (iCCA). A recent study demonstrated that in KRAS-driven PLC, the TF MYC was identified as a switch factor in the lineage commitment between HCC and iCCA. The study provided a novel explanation that, through integrative bioinformatics analyses of transcriptomics, MYC modulates the transcriptional activity of the forkhead family transcription factors FOXA1, FOXA2, and ETS1. This regulatory mechanism helps explain why common liver-damaging risk factors, such as alcoholic or non-alcoholic steatohepatitis, can lead to either HCC or iCCA [ ]. In PDAC, activated KRAS promotes c-MYC-mediated IscU2 transcription, promoting mitochondrial functions and tumor growth [ ]. In KRAS- and MYC-driven PDAC xenografts, bromodomain-containing protein 4 (BRD4) and topoisomerase 1 (TOP1) cooperate to accelerate tumor progression by promoting promoter release and recruiting transcription termination factors, underscoring the vulnerability of these tumors to transcriptional disruption. Furthermore, in KRAS mutant NSCLC cells, upregulation of MUC1-C activates the WNT/β-catenin pathway and facilitates the formation of MUC1-C/β-catenin/TCF4 complexes on the MYC promoter. MUC1-C also recruits the p300 histone acetyltransferase, leading to histone H3 acetylation and subsequent transcriptional activation of the MYC gene, thereby promoting malignant tumor growth [ ]. Additionally, another study revealed that in KRAS mutant PDAC mouse models, MYC induces ductal and neuroendocrine lineage heterogeneity associated with poor survival and chemoresistance [ ]. In this section, we examine the collaborative mechanisms between KRAS and MYC in the transcriptome that support tumor maintenance in KRAS mutant cancers. However, the specific functions of KRAS and MYC are not yet fully understood.

 

## Interaction between KRAS and MYC in the metabolic reprogramming of cancer cells
Metabolic reprogramming, a significant hallmark of cancer, strongly contributes to tumor progression due to the high frequency of metabolic stress in various tumor types [ , ]. These significant metabolic changes include increased glycolysis, glutamine metabolism, lipid metabolism, and mitochondrial metabolism. Together, these changes support rapid tumor proliferation, growth, and survival while also contributing to immune evasion by shaping an immunosuppressive TME [ ]. KRAS mutations drive tumor growth by inducing metabolic reprogramming to meet elevated energy requirements and adapt to metabolic stress [ ]. In this context, MYC also induces metabolic rewiring to produce bioenergetic substrates crucial for tumor proliferation [ ]. However, the precise mechanisms by which KRAS-MYC synergistically regulate cancer metabolism remain unclear. Here, we summarize several essential types of metabolic reprogramming facilitated by the collaboration between oncogenic KRAS and MYC, which play a crucial role in reshaping the TME (Fig.  ).

 

## Synergistic regulation of glycolysis by KRAS and MYC to maintain the survival of cancer cells
Glycolysis, a well-established metabolic hallmark, involves the breakdown of glucose to generate energy [ ]. KRAS-mutant tumors predominantly rely on this process to meet their metabolic demands, even in nutrient-replete conditions, a phenomenon known as the Warburg effect or aerobic glycolysis. Although the Warburg effect yields less ATP per glucose molecule compared to oxidative phosphorylation, it operates at a much faster rate, thereby supporting rapid energy production and compensating for its lower efficiency [ ]. This accelerated energy supply is crucial for sustaining the invasive behavior and uncontrolled proliferation of tumor cells, particularly under the high metabolic demands imposed by KRAS mutations [ ]. Multiple studies have identified MYC as a key stress adaptation factor in KRAS-driven cancers. For example, in an inducible KRAS[G12D] PDAC model, mutant KRAS upregulates the expression of IL-4Rα and IL-13Rα, which recognize IL-4 and IL-13 secreted by tumor-infiltrating Th2 cells. This interaction in turn activates the JAK/STAT6 pathway, leading to MYC upregulation and subsequent metabolic reprogramming. Metabolomic analyses further support the role of MYC as a glycolytic regulator downstream of activated KRAS/JAK1/STAT6, effectively rewiring glycolysis in KRAS-driven cancers [ ]. In PDAC, KRAS[G12D] also modulates glycolysis through the MAPK and MYC pathways, enhancing glucose uptake and directing glucose intermediates into the hexosamine biosynthetic pathway and the pentose phosphate pathway [ ]. Similarly, in KRAS mutant CRC, elevated MYG1 expression promotes PKM2 phosphorylation, which transcriptionally activating MYC and drives MYC-dependent glycolysis, a feature associated with poor prognosis in KRAS-mutant CRC patients [ ]. In summary, the oncogenes KRAS and MYC synergistically reprogram glycolysis through multiple molecular mechanisms, most notably MYC-mediated transcriptional activation of glycolytic genes (LDHA, HK2, PKM2). This metabolic reprogramming enables tumor cells to sustain survival and proliferation under metabolic stress. These evidences suggest that targeting these oncogene-driven metabolic pathways may represent a promising therapeutic strategy for KRAS-driven cancers.

## Synergistic regulation of lipid metabolism by KRAS and MYC in tumor growth
Tumors reprogram lipid metabolism by altering fatty acid oxidation, lipid synthesis, and lipid droplet formation to meet their energy demands [ ]. KRAS-driven cancers favor anabolic processes critical for rapid proliferation, attracting significant attention [ ]. However, further investigation is needed to understand the roles of MYC and KRAS in lipid metabolism. A recent study proposed a new molecular mechanism for MYC protein regulation by the KRAS oncogene, suggesting that the phospholipid transporter PITPNC1 mediates the link between KRAS and MYC in LUAD and PDAC. Mechanistically, KRAS upregulates PITPNC1 through JNK1/2 and MEK1/2, controlling mTOR localization, stabilizing MYC protein to inhibit autophagy, and promoting tumor proliferation and metastasis [ ]. Some studies have shown that mutant KRAS is associated with elevated MNK/P-eIF4E signaling, leading to altered translational regulation. This activation increases c-MYC expression, which promotes tumor proliferation by reprogramming the transcription of metabolic pathways, including oxidative phosphorylation and arachidonic acid metabolism [ ]. In line with this, a study reported that Wnt/MYC activity, in a RAS-dependent manner, increases unsaturated fatty acyl groups in phospholipids and promotes the conversion of triacylglycerol to phospholipids. This finding highlights fatty acid desaturation as a critical therapeutic target to counteract the combined effects of RAS and Wnt/MYC signaling pathways in HCC [ ]. In addition, another study demonstrated MYC and KRAS cooperation in a lung adenocarcinoma mouse model with both KRAS mutations and MYC overexpression. Surface lipid profiling confirmed that MYC activity enhances lipid metabolism by promoting the accumulation of CE-rich lipid droplets and increasing cholesterol influx and storage within lung tumors [ ].

## Role of KRAS and MYC in the glutamine metabolism of cancer cells
KRAS-driven tumors undergo metabolic modifications, particularly in glutamine utilization. KRAS mutations reshape cancer cell metabolism by enhancing glutamine uptake and converting glutamine to glutamate, which then directly enters the TCA cycle [ ]. Activation of the RAS/PI3K/ERK pathway stabilizes c-MYC by inhibiting ubiquitin-mediated protein degradation, leading to increased argininosuccinate synthetase levels and melanoma cell resistance to arginine deiminase [ ]. Mitochondrial biosynthesis involves oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS), producing reactive oxygen species (ROS) closely associated with KRAS mutations and MYC protein overexpression [ ]. A recent study has shown that mutant KRAS promotes MYC gene expression, and enhances the translation of both MYC and ARF6 G4 mRNA by upregulating eIF4A activity. MYC promotes mitochondrial biogenesis and OXPHOS, while ARF6 protects mitochondria from oxidative damage by facilitating their anterograde trafficking, converting growth stimulation into malignancy and immune evasion. These findings suggest that KRAS activates MYC gene expression and that mitochondria are the primary targets of KRAS and MYC in cancer [ ].

## Development of direct KRAS inhibitors
KRAS has long been considered an “undruggable” target due to its strong affinity for GTP and the absence of suitable binding sites for therapeutic drugs. Early approaches to target KRAS were primarily indirect. For almost four decades, drug discovery efforts have heavily concentrated on KRAS, aiming not only at the protein itself but also at its post-translational modifications, membrane localization, interactions with other proteins, and downstream signaling pathways. However, most of these strategies have not succeeded in clinical applications [ ]. Initially, a promising approach involved farnesyltransferase inhibitors to hinder RAS membrane localization and signal transduction. However, this strategy failed in clinical trials for cancers with KRAS or NRAS mutations because geranylgeranyl transferases could compensate for the inhibition. Consequently, their therapeutic efficacy is limited to less common HRAS-driven malignancies [ ].
However, a significant advancement occurred in 2013 with the discovery of allele-specific inhibitors by Ostrem, Shokat, and colleagues, targeting the mutant KRAS[G12C] [ ]. These inhibitors can selectively bind to the mutant KRAS while sparing the wild-type form, owing to a newly identified pocket beneath the switch-II region of the KRAS[G12C] protein. By utilizing a thiol-reactive warhead absent in the wild-type protein, these inhibitors exploit their chemical reactivity to target this pocket, resulting in the protein being irreversibly locked into an inactive, GDP-bound state [ , ]. KRAS mutations represent the most prevalent genomic drivers of NSCLCs, accounting for approximately 30% of all cases [ ]. Initial success was observed with KRAS[G12C] inhibitors in patients with advanced NSCLC. Consequently, sotorasib and adagrasib received expedited approval from the US Food and Drug Administration in 2021 and 2022, respectively, for NSCLC treatment. However, short-lived drug responses and drug resistance have emerged in the majority of patients. Further trials are imperative to address concerns regarding the efficacy of sotorasib, given the rapid development of drug resistance, particularly in NSCLC. Recent data indicate frequent co-mutations of several oncogenes with KRAS, such as KEAP1, TP53, and STK11, which interact synergistically with KRAS, resulting in poorer clinical outcomes and the emergence of resistance to sotorasib [ , – ]. These co-mutations necessitate further investigation due to their potential impact on the efficacy and safety of KRAS-directed treatments. Therefore, when managing KRAS-driven cancers, other gene mutations and individualized treatment must be considered. Moreover, KRAS[G12C] inhibitors as monotherapy have demonstrated limited efficacy in CRC compared to NSCLC. The reactivation of EGFR, which enables tumor cells to sustain growth and proliferation, poses a significant challenge to overcoming resistance to KRAS inhibitors [ ].
Despite these advancements, allele-specific inhibitors present significant challenges due to the rapid emergence of acquired resistance. Recent clinical data suggest that resistance to KRAS[G12C] inhibitors can develop through various mechanisms. Analysis of adagrasib-resistant cases revealed frequent reactivation of effector pathways in tumors due to secondary KRAS mutations, MAPK pathway adaptations, genomic rearrangements, and histological transformations [ , – ]. As discussed earlier, it seems unlikely that solely inhibiting the oncogenic mutant KRAS would be sufficient to treat advanced KRAS mutant tumors [ , ]. Therefore, it is crucial to explore the potential benefits of targeting additional cellular components in combination to enhance the effectiveness of KRAS inhibition.

## MYC dysregulation and resistance to KRAS inhibitors
Understanding the mechanisms behind resistance to KRAS inhibitor therapy is essential for developing effective treatment approaches. As mentioned earlier, MYC often shows mutual dependence and functional overlap with the KRAS signaling pathway. Studies have shown that MYC overexpression or dysregulation is linked to resistance to various cancer treatments, such as targeted therapy, chemotherapy, and immunotherapy. MYC amplification contributes to several cancer characteristics, including proliferation, self-renewal, cell survival, and genomic instability, all of which are associated with resistance to therapy. Additionally, MYC modifies the TME by creating conditions that impede drug delivery and immune responses, thus promoting therapeutic resistance. Understanding the interaction between MYC dysregulation and resistance to KRAS inhibitors provides insights into co-targeting strategies to improve treatment effectiveness in KRAS-driven cancers.

## MYC-driven genetic intrinsic resistance to KRAS inhibitors
KRAS mutations may have various co-mutations that can impact resistance to KRAS-targeted therapies and tumor development. In a comprehensive clinical genomic analysis of 424 patients with NSCLC, co-occurring mutations in CDKN2A, SMARCA4, and KEAP1 were significant independent predictors of poorer clinical outcomes following monotherapy with a KRAS[G12C] inhibitor. Alterations in the PI3K/AKT/MTOR pathway and additional modifications in the baseline RAS gene also contribute to unfavorable outcomes when utilizing the KRAS[G12C] inhibitor [ ]. Approximately 15%–20% of lung cancers with KRAS mutations exhibit MYC gene amplification, which acts as a resistance mechanism to both MEK1/2 and KRAS[G12C] inhibitors [ ]. Moreover, a recent pan-cancer whole-genome analysis revealed a crucial genomic characteristic showing that the frequency of KRAS mutations was correlated with high MYC mRNA expression across various tumor types.
The genomic features were derived from the pan-cancer analysis of whole genome (PCAWG) dataset through cBioPortal, which encompasses 2,658 whole-cancer genomes spanning 38 tumor types. This indicates that co-occurring mutations, which can predict treatment response, may function as biomarkers for patient stratification and therapy intensification in randomized clinical trials [ ]. In general, the biological mechanisms responsible for mutation-mediated drug resistance are not well-investigated (Fig.  ).

 

## MYC-driven genetic and non-genetic mechanisms of acquired resistance to KRAS inhibitors
Acquired resistance to KRAS inhibitors through genetic mechanisms involves secondary KRAS mutations at codons 12, 13 and 61, as well as alterations in the switch II binding pocket, including residues R68, H95 and Y96 [ ]. Resistance can also arise from activating mutations in NRAS or HRAS, amplifications or mutations in upstream RTKs such as EGFR and FGFR [ ]. In addition, activating mutations in downstream components of the PI3K and MAPK pathways (such as PIK3CA, mTOR, MEK, or MYC) can effectively bypass KRAS[G12C] and can be further exacerbated by increased RTK feedback [ , ]. Therefore, there is an urgent need for effective inhibitors that can target multiple secondary alterations simultaneously, such as pan-RAS and pan-KRAS inhibitors, RAS degraders, and RAS-on inhibitors. These inhibitors rely on binding outside the switch II pocket to overcome acquired resistance.
In a significant proportion of patients, acquired resistance does not stem from gene mutations but from non-genetic mechanisms. Treatment with KRAS[G12C] (OFF-state) inhibitors suppressed the MAPK and PI3K pathways [ ]. This, in turn, triggers a loss of feedback inhibition and rapid rebound activation of upstream signals mediated by RTKs, wild-type KRAS, or their downstream effectors, thereby limiting drug treatment efficacy. In KRAS[G12C]-mutant CRC models, inhibition of the RAS/RAF/MEK system upregulates EGFR-mediated signaling, reactivating a cascade that has emerged as a prominent resistance mechanism to KRAS[G12C] inhibition [ , ]. However, the role of the MYC oncoprotein, as the core executor of the downstream effects of the PI3K and MAPK pathways, in resistance to KRAS inhibition remains to be investigated. A recent study proposed a novel therapeutic strategy centered on “dual-node blockade of KRAS-MYC” to overcome resistance to KRAS[G12D] inhibitors. Mechanistically, KRAS[G12D] inhibitors suppress HDAC5 expression. HDAC5 normally deacetylates c-MYC at lysine 148, which targets it for degradation. Therefore, the loss of HDAC5 leads to the stabilization of c-MYC. This in turn reactivates MAPK signaling and promotes cell growth, even when the KRAS-MEK-ERK axis is inhibited by the same KRAS[G12D] inhibitors [ ]. Furthermore, in ERK inhibitor-resistant PDAC models, mutant KRAS, which is EGFR-dependent, enhances MYC protein stability by activating ERK1/2 and ERK5, and phosphorylating MYC at S62. Inhibition of ERK1/2 and ERK5 synergistically decreases MYC levels and PDAC growth [ ]. Another study suggested that suppressing MYC, induced by the hyperactivated KRAS/MAPK/ERK pathway, could be an effective therapeutic strategy to increase cancer cell vulnerability to cytotoxic drugs in KRAS mutant tumors [ ]. A recent study highlighted MYC overexpression as a critical resistance mechanism to KRAS[G12C] and MEK1/2 inhibitors in KRAS-mutated lung cancer models, indicating that combining MEK1/2 and KRAS[G12C] inhibitors might overcome resistance mechanisms in lung cancer [ ]. Similarly, the phospholipid transporter PITPNC1, a novel molecular target linking KRAS to MYC to prevent autophagy, showed significant anti-tumor effects when its inhibitors were combined with KRAS[G12C] inhibitors. Mechanistically, KRAS induces high PITPNC1 expression through MEK1/2 and JNK1/2, enhancing MYC protein stability to prevent autophagy, thereby limiting the response rate to KRAS[G12C] inhibitors in lung and pancreatic cancer models [ ].
In addition to being a downstream effector of the KRAS-MAPK pathway, MYC also acts as a downstream mediator of other cancer-related signaling pathways, such as the Wnt and Hippo pathways. For instance, a recent study revealed that USP47, a regulator of signaling pathways associated with cancer (including Wnt, Hippo, and p53), stabilizes c-MYC by inhibiting its proteasomal degradation through de-ubiquitination. This stabilization promotes the growth of NSCLC cells. The USP47 inhibitor, K-552, enhances the effectiveness of sotorasib, indicating a potential combination therapy with KRAS[G12C] inhibitors to address these limitations [ ]. Moreover, as a compensatory mechanism, KRAS inhibition often triggers the activation of the Hippo signaling pathway effector YAP/TAZ, which transcriptionally upregulates MYC and cooperates with it to promote drug resistance. In KRAS mutant PDACs models, mutant KRAS upregulates the eIF5A-PEAK1-YAP-TAZ-MYC signaling, thereby enhancing tumorsphere growth. Similarly, another study suggested that YAP1-TAZ-TEAD signaling compromises the anti-tumor effects of KRAS[G12C] inhibitors. TEAD stimulates the transcription of genes like MYC to drive progression, indicating that TEAD-MYC plays a critical role in acquired resistance to KRAS inhibition. This supports the use of TEAD inhibitors in combination with KRAS[G12C] inhibitors to enhance anti-tumor efficacy in KRAS-driven cancers [ ]. Furthermore, both MYC and ST8SIA6-AS1, a long noncoding RNA, are significantly elevated in KRAS[G12C]-mutant cancer cells with resistance to KRAS[G12C] inhibitors. Simultaneous inhibition of KRAS and the ST8SIA6-AS1/PLK1 axis synergistically downregulates MYC expression, leading to cell death, tumor regression, and reduced resistance to KRAS[G12C] inhibitors. These findings provide further insight into the functional role of MYC in mediating resistance to KRAS-targeted therapies [ ].
In summary, although MYC has emerged as a significant regulator of resistance to KRAS inhibitors in recent preclinical studies, the combined treatment targeting MYC and KRAS is indirect. Further clinical trials are needed to explore direct MYC inhibitors in combination with KRAS inhibitors to assess whether these combinations can enhance therapeutic responses and overcome inhibitor resistance in KRAS-driven cancers.

## MYC-driven histologic and cell-state transformation in KRAS inhibitor resistance
Recent evidence indicates that cell state or lineage transitions contribute to resistance to KRAS inhibition in lung adenocarcinomas. For instance, in genetically engineered mouse models, the KRAS[G12D] inhibitor, MRTX1133, induces a shift to a quiescent alveolar type-1-like state in lung adenocarcinoma tumors with KRAS[G12D] mutations. Targeting alveolar type-1-like cells improves their response to KRAS inhibition, suggesting a potential therapeutic strategy to overcome resistance [ ]. In lung adenocarcinoma, resistance to KRAS inhibitors is linked to the development of squamous cell or small-cell neuroendocrine carcinoma, often driven by MYC amplification or increased activity. The transition from an epithelial-to-mesenchymal state plays an important role in resistance to KRAS inhibition [ ]. A detailed genetic analysis from the autopsy of a patient with KRAS[G12C]-mutant NSCLC, who experienced relapse while on sotorasib treatment, revealed that EMT activation was the primary mechanism of resistance to direct KRAS[G12C] inhibition [ , ].
In summary, dysregulation of MYC not only serves as a central event in KRAS-driven tumorigenesis but also acts as a pivotal node mediating resistance to targeted therapies. Drug-resistant cells employ various adaptive mechanisms to circumvent KRAS inhibition and reinstate MYC activation, thereby maintaining proliferation, survival, and cellular plasticity. Given that drug-resistant cells bypass KRAS inhibition and re-establish MYC activation to sustain cell growth and survival, targeting these processes may represent a future direction of combination-based interventions for KRAS-driven cancers.

## Novel therapeutic strategies targeting KRAS-mutant cancers
Numerous patients developing resistance to KRAS[G12C] inhibitors exhibit multiple resistance mechanisms, highlighting the persistent clinical challenge of overcoming treatment resistance [ , ]. To effectively overcome acquired resistance, therapeutic agents capable of simultaneously targeting multiple secondary alterations, such as pan-KRAS inhibitors, KRAS degraders and RAS-ON inhibitors, are required (Table  ). In addition, this review provides a systematic overview of the molecular mechanisms underlying resistance to KRAS inhibitors, with particular emphasis on the critical role of MYC. Overcoming the “undruggability” of MYC while countering KRAS resistance represents a key future direction for dual targeting of KRAS and MYC.

 

## RNA interference (RNAi)
mRNA targeting through RNAi has evolved as a potent alternative to traditional protein inhibition strategies. A phase II study (NCT01676259) examined the effectiveness, safety, and tolerability of siG12D-LODER in combination with chemotherapy in patients with advanced pancreatic cancer, revealing its safety and tolerability [ ]. Further exploration of RNAi therapy is warranted in diverse patient cohorts with varying KRAS mutations [ ]. Interestingly, MYC appears to be just as important as KRAS in tumorigenesis, yet no direct inhibitor of MYC currently exists. A recent preclinical study revealed that an “inverted” RNAi molecule can simultaneously silence mutated KRAS and overexpressed MYC. Compared with single-gene silencing using siRNA alone, this combined targeting enhanced the suppression of cancer cell survival by 40-fold [ ]. This innovative approach not only provides a strategy to co-target KRAS and MYC in cancers, but also establishes a modular platform that could, in principle, be applied to any pair of target genes, a finding with broad therapeutic implications.

## KRAS degraders
The development of proteolysis-targeting chimeras (PROTAC)-based KRAS degraders represents an emerging strategy for targeting KRAS-mutant cancers. These degraders have the potential to facilitate the degradation of prevalent oncogenic KRAS alleles by binding to an E3 ubiquitin ligase. This interaction leads to ubiquitination, subsequent degradation, and more substantial and enduring inhibition [ , ]. ASP3082, a bifunctional PROTAC degrader designed to target KRAS[G12D], is currently undergoing a phase I clinical trial in patients with advanced solid tumors such as PDAC and CRC (NCT05382559). Additionally, drugs that target various KRAS mutations have been under evaluation in preclinical studies. While further evidence is necessary, the initial safety, efficacy, and pharmacokinetic data from these studies are emerging, indicating promising activity of protein degraders in cancer treatment [ ]. However, compared to small-molecule inhibitors, PROTAC-based degraders present both advantages and disadvantages. The substantial molecular size and structure of these compounds may restrict their oral bioavailability, leading to predominantly intravenous administration to date.
Similar to KRAS, MYC has been deemed “undruggable”. However, strategies such as PROTACs and related protein degraders have made some progress in targeting MYC via the ubiquitin-proteasome system. A recent study highlights a promising lncRNA-mediated degradation platform that directs ubiquitination and proteasomal destruction of key oncoproteins-including KRAS, c-MYC, and EGFR, which offers a synergistic approach to co-targeting dual oncogenic drivers [ ]. Additionally, TAK-981, an inhibitor of the SUMO-activating enzyme E, suppresses MYC expression by promoting its proteasomal degradation. Dual inhibition of SUMOylation and the KRAS downstream pathway enhances therapeutic efficacy in MYC-driven KRAS-mutant cancers across diverse tissue types [ ].
KRAS (ON) inhibitors. “KRAS-on” inhibitors, which specifically bind to the active state of KRAS, offer novel therapeutic strategies [ ]. RMC-6291 targets active KRAS by forming a tri-complex with cyclophilin A and KRAS[G12C] to hinder RAS effector binding, thereby inhibiting downstream pathway activation. This compound is currently under evaluation in phase I studies (NCT05462717) and has shown superior efficacy compared to “KRAS-off” drugs in patients with KRASG13C mutations in NSCLC and CRC, suggesting a potential strategy to combat resistance [ , ]. Another “KRAS-on” or tri-complex inhibitor, RM-018, has demonstrated the ability to overcome treatment resistance by binding to KRAS[G12C]/Y96D in KRAS-mutant NSCLC cases with secondary mutations such as KRASY96D [ ]. In conclusion, these cutting-edge mutation-selective inhibitors are expected to exhibit high therapeutic efficacy and favorable tolerability by targeting a single mutant gene or protein.

## Pan-KRAS and multi-KRAS inhibitors
Pan-KRAS and multi-KRAS inhibitors, in contrast to allele-selective KRAS inhibitors, inhibit various KRAS mutations and the wild-type KRAS protein. RMC-6236, a multi-selective RAS ‘on’ inhibitor, has also shown significant preclinical efficacy in KRAS-mutant tumors that have acquired resistance to first-generation KRAS[G12C] inhibitors, particularly those with secondary NRAS mutations or wild-type KRAS amplification [ , , ]. Another innovative pan-KRAS inhibitor is BI-2865, which interacts with wild-type KRAS and various GDP-bound mutant KRAS proteins, excluding wild-type HRAS or NRAS [ ]. A phase I trial of BI-3,706,674, a compound akin to BI-2865 targeting multiple mutants and wild-type KRAS, is presently undergoing clinical assessment (NCT06056024). Furthermore, for patients with KRAS[G12D] solid tumors, QTX3034, a noncovalent multi-KRAS inhibitor that targets KRAS[G12D] and KRAS[G12V], is in clinical trials as monotherapy or in combination with cetuximab (NCT06227377). In general, pan-KRAS inhibitors offer a higher potential to combat resistance but also pose significant risks, such as on-target toxicity and potential impairment of immune responses to tumors due to the crucial functions of wild-type RAS in normal tissues [ ]. Therefore, further research is imperative to enhance our understanding of the roles of these agents.

## Vaccination against mutant KRAS
Multiple attempts have been made to target KRAS using vaccines and T cell treatments. The mutant KRAS protein serves as an attractive tumor neoantigen that T cells can target. In a preclinical study, mRNA-5671/V941, a lipid nanoparticle-based mRNA vaccine, enhanced CD8+ T cell immune responses against KRAS neoantigens [ ]. Recently, a phase I clinical trial (NCT03948763) was initiated to evaluate the safety and efficacy of mRNA-5671/V941 with or without anti-PD-1 therapy in patients with advanced or metastatic NSCLC, PDAC, or CRC with various KRAS mutations (G12D, G12V, G12C, or G13D) [ ]. In addition, mDC3/8-KRAS, a mature dendritic cell vaccine customized to patient-specific mutant KRAS peptides, is being evaluated for its ability to induce T cell responses to delay disease recurrence in vaccinated individuals (NCT03592888) [ ]. The AMPLIFY 201 trial (NCT04853017) investigated ELI-002 2P, a lymph node-targeted KRAS[G12D]/G12R amphiphile vaccine directed against KRAS[G12D] and [G12R] mutations in patients with PDAC and CRC [ , ]. The vaccine enhances lymph node delivery and immune responses by combining the amphiphile modification of G12D and G12R mutant KRAS peptides with an immunostimulatory amphiphile-modified CpG oligonucleotide adjuvant [ ]. Furthermore, the safety and tolerability of a multi-epitope shared neoantigen vaccine called SLATEv1, encoding 20 shared neoantigens derived from specific common oncogenic driver mutations, was evaluated in patients with advanced or metastatic solid tumors (NCT03953235). The vaccine was well tolerated when combined with the immune checkpoint inhibitors ipilimumab and nivolumab [ ]. However, its clinical efficacy was limited as none of the patients exhibited an objective response. Additionally, vaccine-induced T cell responses tend to focus on TP53 neoantigens rather than KRAS neoantigens, indicating a hierarchy of neoantigen immunogenicity that may hinder vaccines from effectively eliciting robust immune responses against key tumor mutations. Overall, many questions remain unanswered, such as the effectiveness of mutant KRAS vaccines in clinical settings. Future studies on mutant KRAS vaccines should explore combining these strategies with other immunotherapies or KRAS inhibitors.

## Targeting mutant KRAS using therapies based on T cell receptors (TCRs)
An increasing number of studies have identified human leukocyte antigen (HLA) class I-restricted KRAS epitopes and the TCRs that recognize these epitopes. In preclinical animal models, T cells engineered to express these TCRs exhibit potential therapeutic efficacy. For instance, in immunodeficient mouse models implanted with PDAC cells carrying human HLA-A*11:01 and the KRAS[G12D] mutation, human T cells transduced with KRAS[G12D]-reactive TCR showed reduced tumor growth and increased survival [ ]. Furthermore, a phase I clinical trial (NCT05438667) is currently evaluating the safety and preliminary efficacy of autologous T cell therapy engineered to express TCRs that specifically recognize KRAS[G12D] and [G12V] neoantigens presented by human HLA-A*11:01 in patients with advanced pancreatic cancer. In general, mutant KRAS neoantigens are present in common HLA class I alleles such as HLA-A*03:01 and HLA-A*11:01. Therefore, many other HLA class I molecules remain insufficiently investigated for their ability to present these mutant KRAS epitopes [ – ]. Identifying additional mutant KRAS epitopes, HLA class I peptidomes, and TCRs that identify these neoantigens will be essential for expanding the use of mutant KRAS T cell-based therapies.

## Synergistic targeting of MYC and KRAS: the emerging role of OMO‑103
Directly targeting MYC while addressing KRAS resistance represents a key future direction for dual inhibition of KRAS and MYC. OMOMYC was originally designed as a 91-amino-acid dominant‑negative MYC inhibitor derived from the bHLHZ domain of MYC. It incorporates four amino acid substitutions (E61T, E68I, R74Q, R75N), allowing it to form transcriptionally inactive complexes with MAX and compete with endogenous MYC-MAX heterodimers for E-box binding sites [ ]. The first direct MYC inhibitor to enter clinical trial is OMO-103, a cell-penetrating peptide derived from OMOMYC and developed by Peptomyc. In a phase I clinical trial involving 22 patients with heavily pretreated advanced malignant solid tumors, OMO-103 was well tolerated. Early signs of anti-tumor efficacy were observed: among 12 evaluable patients, 8 achieved stable disease, and one experienced a durable response lasting over 24 months [ ]. The majority of adverse events were grade 1 infusion-related responses. OMO‑103 is currently being further evaluated in ongoing clinical trials, including a phase Ib study in metastatic pancreatic cancer (NCT06059001) and investigations of its combination with immunotherapy in KRAS-driven cancers, where MYC plays a critical role in tumor maintenance and immune evasion under the MYCOMBIO project. Collectively, these advances establish OMOMYC/OMO‑103 as the first direct MYC inhibitor to reach clinical development and provide a strong rationale for combining MYC-directed strategies with KRAS-targeted therapies in KRAS‑MYC co‑driven malignancies. Although OMO-103, as the first direct MYC inhibitor, has demonstrated safety and preliminary efficacy in phase I clinical trials, its combination with KRAS-targeted drugs is still in the preclinical stage. The MYCiMAX project (101142260, ERC-2023-ADG), funded by the European Commission, is led by Laura Soucek’s team, which has been investigating the combination of OMOMYC with KRAS inhibitors. Their work aims to elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying RAS-MYC cooperation and advance this strategy toward clinical translation. The strong scientific rationale for this combination is further supported by a recently patent covering the use of OMOMYC with KRAS inhibitors in cancer treatment. Collectively, the combination of OMO-103 and KRAS inhibitors represents a promising future strategy to overcome resistance and improve outcomes in KRAS-driven malignancies.
Taken together, several alternative anti-KRAS strategies, including pan-KRAS inhibitors, KRAS degraders, RAS-ON inhibitors, immunotherapy-based approaches, and PROTACs, broaden their target scope to other KRAS alleles. These strategies aim to improve therapeutic effectiveness, reduce toxicity, delay resistance development, and have undergone clinical evaluation in KRAS mutant tumors, yielding limited prominent outcomes. Furthermore, in pursuit of overcoming the “undruggable” nature of KRAS and MYC, research efforts have begun to explore targeted protein degradation as a strategy to simultaneously inhibit both oncoproteins. Nonetheless, this remains a highly nascent field, with the overwhelming majority of studies still at early preclinical or proof-of-concept stages (Table  ). To date, no drug candidates have progressed into clinical trials.

 

## Conclusions and perspectives
Significant progress has been achieved in directly targeting KRAS, revolutionizing KRAS-targeted therapy and eliminating the “undruggable” status. A class of specific small-molecule inhibitors targeting KRAS[G12C] has been identified and has shown promising results in clinical trials. Despite these advancements, challenges persist, including drug resistance and toxicity, emphasizing the necessity for continued research on patient-centric trial designs. To effectively address acquired resistance, therapeutic agents capable of simultaneously targeting multiple secondary alterations, such as pan-KRAS inhibitors, KRAS degraders, RAS-ON inhibitors, and PROTACs, may be essential. However, these approaches introduce new challenges in drug delivery due to their large molecular sizes, and further investigation is needed to determine if they lead to new drug resistance issues. In addition, a combination of targeting various KRAS-related molecules is essential to maximize the impact of KRAS inhibition in overcoming drug resistance. The MYC oncoprotein is upregulated by RAS signaling, establishing a robust partnership in metabolic reprogramming, epigenetic modification, and the formation of the tumor immunosuppressive microenvironment. MYC dysregulation not only drives KRAS-mediated tumorigenesis but also functions as a central signaling hub in the acquisition of resistance to targeted therapies. Resistant cells exploit a range of adaptive strategies to circumvent KRAS inhibition and sustain MYC activity, thereby maintaining proliferation, survival, and cellular plasticity. Targeting these adaptive escape mechanisms represents a promising and indirect therapeutic avenue in KRAS-driven cancers. Concurrently, future clinical trials should focus on evaluating direct MYC inhibitors in combination with KRAS inhibitors to determine whether these regimens can effectively overcome acquired resistance in KRAS-driven malignancies. This interaction between MYC dysregulation and KRAS inhibitor resistance offers insights for co-targeting strategies that could enhance therapeutic outcomes in KRAS-driven cancers and highlights areas for further exploration.
First, the development of KRAS inhibitors should be pursued in parallel with MYC inhibitors in both preclinical and clinical settings. Similar to KRAS, MYC has historically been considered “undruggable”. However, significant progress has shifted this paradigm from impossible to merely challenging. Some stapled peptides and mini-proteins, such as OMO-103 and IDP-121, which disrupt MYC-MAX dimerization, have successfully entered the early-phase clinical trials. In addition, a notable breakthrough in direct-targeting therapy is the emergence of PROTACs and related protein degraders, leveraging the ubiquitin-proteasome system to degrade MYC. Similarly, targeting KRAS with PROTACs has significantly enhanced the capacity to overcome acquired resistance to KRAS-specific inhibitors. Therefore, the development of PROTACs or analogous protein degraders that simultaneously target both KRAS and MYC represents a compelling therapeutic strategy. Additional approaches, such as SUMOylation or lncRNA-mediated protein degradation, offer effective means for dual inhibition of KRAS and MYC. Furthermore, RNA interference molecules capable of concurrently silencing mutated KRAS and overexpressed MYC present an innovative treatment avenue. Despite these promising prospects, the developing PROTACs or other dual degraders targeting both KRAS and MYC continue to face substantial hurdles. First, molecular design is exceptionally complex, requiring not only high-affinity binding to both targets but also sufficient stability and cellular permeability of the degrader molecule itself. Second, delivery remains a critical challenge: the efficient and selective transport of these macromolecular agents into tumor cells persists as a technical bottleneck in the broader PROTAC field. Third, potential on-target toxicity cannot be overlooked. Since KRAS and MYC are involved in certain normal physiological processes, their complete inhibition or degradation may lead to adverse effects. Fourth, although an increasing number of combination strategies targeting KRAS and MYC have emerged, including degraders, lncRNA-mediated silencing, and siRNA-based approaches, none have yet advanced to clinical trials, despite demonstrated efficacy in preventing tumorigenesis in preclinical models. These co-targeting strategies highlight key areas for further investigation.
Second, therapeutic strategies should consider co-targeting the metabolic adaptations driven by KRAS and MYC, as well as their role in shaping the tumor immunosuppressive microenvironment. Growing evidence points to a functional interplay between MYC dysregulation and resistance to KRAS inhibitors, underscoring the rationale for combining MYC-directed therapies with KRAS inhibition. In addition to direct MYC inhibitors, combining KRAS inhibition with indirect MYC-targeting strategies may offer synergistic benefits. Given that KRAS and MYC cooperatively regulate metabolic processes to support the elevated energetic demands of cancer cells, targeting these metabolic nodes presents a promising approach for managing KRAS-driven malignancies. Moreover, the collaboration between KRAS and MYC contributes to the establishment of an immunosuppressive microenvironment. Combining KRAS inhibitors with immune checkpoint inhibitors can suppress MYC and enhance antitumor immune responses, suggesting the potential of a triple therapy (KRAS inhibitors, MYC pathway inhibitors, and immunotherapy). Additionally, drug-resistant cancer cells can evade KRAS inhibition by reactivating MAPK signaling through upstream feedback mechanisms mediated by RTKs (such as EGFR). This reactivation sustains MYC expression and transcriptional activity, which in turn reinforces upstream feedback loops. Consequently, combining KRAS inhibitors with RTK inhibitors offers an indirect strategy to suppress MYC and overcome resistance. In summary, KRAS and MYC establish a robust partnership across multiple oncogenic processes, including metabolic reprogramming, epigenetic modification, and modulation of the immunosuppressive TME. Integrating KRAS inhibitors with therapies targeting convergent downstream vulnerabilities shared by KRAS and MYC represents a compelling strategy to overcome resistance in KRAS-driven cancers.
Finally, the design of effective combination strategies must account for tumor heterogeneity, patient selection biomarkers, and potential toxicity. Given the substantial heterogeneity among KRAS-driven cancers, encompassing diverse KRAS mutations and distinct metabolic profiles across tumor types, developing broadly effective therapies remains a major challenge. Different KRAS mutations engage distinct downstream signaling pathways and are associated with variable clinical outcomes, which may also influence their degree of functional cooperation with MYC. These considerations underscore the importance of stratifying patients based on their specific KRAS mutation subtype when designing and interpreting co-targeting trials. Equally critical is the need to manage potential synergistic toxicities arising from combined inhibition of KRAS and MYC, given their roles in normal cellular homeostasis. To maximize therapeutic benefit, robust predictive biomarkers are needed to identify patients most likely to respond to KRAS-MYC co-targeting. Emerging evidence suggests that sensitivity to such combinations may be modulated by co-occurring genetic alterations (e.g., in TP53, KEAP1, or STK11), as well as tumor microenvironmental features such as immune cell infiltration and interferon signaling activity. Integrating these multidimensional biomarkers into a composite predictive model could enable more precise patient stratification in clinical trials, ultimately improving outcomes for individuals with KRAS-driven cancers.
