## Abstract
Outcomes in pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) remain poor due to a variety of biological, clinical, and societal factors. However, in recent years, PDAC has seen 1) increased precision of initial evaluation, 2) increased emphasis on supportive care, 3) deeper understanding of the translation biology of PDAC, especially as pertains to genomic alterations, and 4) foundational combination chemotherapy clinical trials across all disease stages. These advances have led to a wide range of new approaches to drug therapy for PDAC. Currently available drugs are showing added benefit, both by resequencing them with each other and also with respect to other therapeutic modalities. Molecular strategies are being developed to predict response to known therapeutic agents and to identify others. Additionally, a wide range of new drugs for PDAC are under development, including drugs which inhibit critical molecular pathways, drugs which attempt to capitalize on homologous repair deficiencies, immunotherapeutic approaches, antimetabolic agents, and drugs which attack the extracellular matrix which supports PDAC growth. These new approaches offer the promise of improved survival for future PDAC patients.
Keywords: drug therapy, pancreatic cancer, clinical trials

## Introduction
Pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) affects over 450 000 people annually worldwide, with a death rate (57.7 per million in 2017) that nearly equals its incidence . Both incidence and mortality rates are rising by ~2.2% annually. In the United States alone, PDAC now affects over 64 000 individuals and is currently the third (soon to be second) leading cause of cancer death in that country . Over 50% of individuals with pancreatic cancer present with metastatic disease . Median survival for this patient cohort is less than 6 months; approximately 25% of patient live for 1-year and 5-year overall survival (OS) is currently 12% .
The dismal survival statistics that characterize PDAC are explained by the significant biologic and clinical obstacles that exist to successful treatment. Biologically, the disease is genetically complex, has great biological heterogeneity, exists symbiotically with a protective surrounding stroma, is immunologically quiescent, metastasizes at an early point in time and has frequent, immense systemic impact on patients, often limiting both their quality and quantity of life. Clinically, patients are typically older (the current average age at PDAC diagnosis is ~70 years old) with extensive comorbidity and little tolerance for treatment, diagnosed with advanced disease, lack optimum supportive care, and are treated with systemic agents of limited activity.
Most importantly, there is often a lack of awareness and pervasive sense of nihilism among medical professionals, the general public, and patients themselves with respect to the problem that PDAC poses. This often results in a lack of motivation, resources, and expertise. It is with these factors as a backdrop that therapeutic progress in PDAC must be made.

## Initial evaluation and current management

## Initial evaluation
Initial evaluation for PDAC has been well outlined in several recent publications . Several points deserve emphasis. Staging laparoscopy, even with modern imaging, remains an important staging tool for patients with radiographic localized disease, detecting occult disease in ~20% of patients . Appropriate consultations (e.g. palliative care, nutrition, and oncology social work) are also of value at care outset .
Along with tissue confirmation, both genetic testing for inherited mutations (germline mutations) and molecular profiling of tumor tissue (somatic mutations) are now a standard of care. Both germline and somatic mutation analyses are done as results, while overlapping, are nonidentical . All patients are tested (as opposed to those just with familial PDAC) as over 40% of germline alterations are found in patients without familial PDAC . The major purposes of germline testing are: 1) to identify homologous repair deficiencies (e.g. BRCA1/2) along with other potentially inherited genetic risks and 2) to identify deficient mismatch repair genes (e.g. MLH1) potentially exploitable by immunotherapy. The major current uses of somatic profiling are: 1) KRAS targeting, 2) identification of other potential actionable mutations (e.g. FGFR2 fusion, NTRK 1, etc.), and 3) additional consideration of immunotherapy (e.g. tumor mutational burden (TMB) score). Having this information at the outset of therapy is of great utility in optimizing the planning and efficiency of the sequence of therapies throughout a patient’s clinical course.
Supportive care is of paramount importance in managing PDAC, perhaps even more important than the choice of initial therapy. In addition to the frequent comorbidities associated with patients of the age range typical for PDAC, seven groups of complications – pain, depression, weight loss, diabetes, gastric dysmotility, hepatobiliary obstruction/infection, and hemostatic complications (thrombosis, bleeding)- occur with unfortunate regularity. These complications must be actively searched for and aggressively managed.

## Current chemotherapy - metastatic disease
No standard systemic therapy for metastatic (mPDAC) existed until 1997 (Table ). That year, Burris et al. , in a phase III trial of 126 patients with treatment-naïve mPDAC, were able to display a modest median overall survival (mOS) advantage for gemcitabine (gem) versus 5-FU (5.65 vs. 4.41 months, P=0.0025), thus establishing the former as a standard of treatment for this disorder. This study also introduced the concept of quality of life parameters as an important aspect of drug trials in PDAC. A subsequent study in 2007 by Moore et al. added erlotinib to gemcitabine and produced a very modest improvement in mOS for the combination therapy (6.2 vs. 5.9 months, P=0.038). This study demonstrated the ‘proof-of-concept’ of targeted therapies being able to enhance (albeit modestly) the therapeutic effect of chemotherapy in mPDAC.
 Superiority of multiagent-chemotherapy regimens over monotherapy for 1st line mPDAC for good performance status patients was established in the decade following. In 2011, two large phase III clinical trials, the PRODIGE 4 trial and the MPACT trial , showed a clear superiority of combination chemotherapy (FOLFIRINOX (FFX) and gemcitabine/Nab-paclitaxel (GnP), respectively, to single-agent gemcitabine. Recently, a third chemotherapy trial raising the question of the use of three-drug versus two-drug chemotherapy regimens was reported in the same patient population. This trial employed the nal-IRIFOX regimen and compared it to GnP in 770 chemo-naïve patients (NAPOLI-3 trial) . A modest survival advantage was produced by nal-IRIFOX versus GnP (11.1 months vs. 9.2 months, P=0.84). Cytopenias, diarrhea, and peripheral neuropathy remain key toxicities for all three regimens. The nal-IRIFOX regimen has recently received FDA approval for first-line use in mPDAC.
The choice of initial therapy in mPDAC is complex, especially as no definitive direct comparison of FFX to GnP or nal-IRIFOX has yet been completed. Many factors influence optimum treatment selection: including those that are 1) patient-related (age, performance status, comorbidity, travel, and logistics), 2) disease-related (metastatic sites/manifestations, rate of progression, results of molecular profiling, presence of stents), and 3) therapy-related (expense/reimbursement, IV access requirements, anticancer activity drug toxicities, clinical trial availability). Initial therapeutic choice is best made with close collaboration between the patient and provider – there is no standard ‘right answer’.

## Chemotherapy - localized disease
Three clinical trials provide the foundation to our current understanding of adjuvant therapy in localized PDAC using combination chemotherapy (Table ). The ESPAC-4 trial randomized 732 patients to gemcitabine and capecitabine versus gemcitabine only. Median OS was 28 months (95% CI: 23.3–31.2 months) for the combination arm and 25.5 months (95% CI: 22.7–28.4 months) for the control arm, 5-year OS was 28% (95% CI: 23–33%) and 20% (95% CI: 16–25%) for the two arms, respectively (HR 0.84, P=0.049).
 The second, the PRODIGE 24 trial , compared FFX to gemcitabine in 493 patients. Median OS was 54.4 months with FFX versus 35 months with gemcitabine (HR 0.64, P=0.003). Additionally, Tempero and colleagues compared GnP to gemcitabine (APACT trial) in 866 patients with localized PDAC. Although this was adjudged to be a negative trial based on failure to meet the primary endpoint of median disease-free survival (DFS) (16.6 vs. 13.7 months), both median (41.8 vs. 37.7 months) and 5-year OS (38 vs. 31%) favored the combination arm.
Over the past decade, neoadjuvant therapy has gained popularity in the treatment of localized PDAC for a variety of reasons. These include: 1) immediacy of micrometastatic disease treatment, 2) improvement in ability of patients to receive and tolerate chemotherapy, 3) ability to identify patients with rapidly progressive disease as nonsurgical candidates, 4) enhancement of (complete) surgical resection, and 5) assessment of in vivo chemotherapeutic sensitivity. Randomized trials to date do not clearly display a clear superiority of neoadjuvant versus adjuvant therapy across all patient groups, especially for patients who receive all intended chemotherapy.
Clinical trials by Unno and colleagues (the Prep-2 JSAP-5 trial) , Versteijne et al. (PREOPANC trial) , and Ghaneh et al. (ESPAC-5 trial) represent key examples of the potential of the neoadjuvant approach in localized PDAC. In general, in a given patient, the more difficult the ability to receive achieve an R0 resection or the more difficult the ability to administer a complete course of chemotherapy, the more important neoadjuvant treatment may become. However, the specific chemotherapy utilized may be less important. This is illustrated by the SWOG 1505 trial (Sohal et al.) in which 147 patients (of whom only 102 were eligible) with de novo resectable PDAC were randomized between 12 weeks of neoadjuvant FFX and GnP followed by an additional 12 weeks of adjuvant chemotherapy in a ‘pick the winner’ design. Toxicities were as expected. There were no significant differences in completion of preoperative therapy (84 vs. 85%), resection rate (73 vs. 70%), completion of all therapy (49 vs. 40%), median DFS (10.9 months vs. 14.2 months), median OS (22.4 months 95% CI: 17.6–45.9 months vs. 23.6 months, 95% CI: 17.8–31.7 months) or 2-year OS (42%, 95% CI: 31–61%, vs. 49%, 95% CI: 31–63%).

## Drug therapy in PDAC – new approaches

## Novel chemotherapy applications
Given the critical importance of response to initial therapy in mPDAC and the complexity of medical decision-making in choosing the initial therapy, several strategies have been developed to aid in this process (Table ). The first is the use of alternating chemotherapy regimens. This approach has several potential advantages. First, toxicities may be reduced by limiting continuous exposure to agents with cumulative effects (e.g. platinols–neuropathy). Second, therapeutic response may be enhanced by earlier tumor exposure to a wider variety of agents – in effect, the regimen ‘anticipates’ rather than ‘reacts’ to treatment resistance. Finally, the use of alternating regimens may produce greater flexibility in employing investigational agents.
 Promise for this approach came from several phase II trials including GnP alternating with FFX (France) and GnP alternating with FOLFIRI (US) . In a randomized phase II trial conducted in Spain (the SEQUENCE trial) ,Carrato and colleagues randomized GnP alternating with FOLFOX to GnP; mOS was 13.2 months (95% CI: 10.1–16.2 months) versus 9.7 months (95% CI: 7.5 vs. 12.0 months), HR 0.68, P=0.023. Toxicity was similar, except for grade ≥3 neutropenia and thrombocytopenia, both higher in the experimental arm (46 vs. 24%, P=0.004 and 24 vs. 8%, P=0.007).
The second strategy relies on ‘molecular navigation’ to choose the preferred chemotherapy regimen. An example is the COMPASS trial performed in Canada . Using whole genome and RNA sequencing, 63 patients were assigned either a classical or basal PDAC subtype at a median of 35 days from biopsy. GATA6 expression in tumor was found to be a robust surrogate biomarker for distinguishing classical versus basal PDAC subtypes. Median OS was superior for patients with classical (10.4 months, 95% CI: 8.3-not reached) than basal (6.3 months, 95% CI: 4.0-not reached). As for the choice of regimen, patients with classical phenotype appeared to respond somewhat better to FFX, whereas patients with basal expression fared better with GnP. These observations are being confirmed in a larger clinical trial (NeoPanc One, NCT04472910).

## Novel systemic agents – pancreatic cancer
According to the database of investigational agents maintained by the Pancreatic Cancer Action Network (PANCAN), ~500 new drugs are in some stage of clinical development worldwide that are potentially applicable to pancreatic cancer . Nevala-Plagemann et al. coined the mnemonic, PRIME (Pathway inhibition, homologous Repair deficiency, Immunotherapy, Metabolism, Extracellular matrix) to assist with structuring the various investigational approaches (Fig. ). Each of these approaches has recently been tested in clinical trials (Table ).
 

## Pathway inhibition
The ‘holy grail’ of targeted therapy is the various KRAS mutations characteristic of PDAC (Fig. ). 1). KRAS is mutated in over 90% of PDAC patients, almost exclusively involving the G12 codon . Long considered ‘undruggable’, a breakthrough occurred in the last several years with respect to KRAS 12C mutations. Although G12C is an uncommon mutation in PDAC, present in only 2% of patients, two drugs, adagrasib and sodograsib, have recently been found to lock G12C into its inactive form. The former was tested in 21 evaluable patients in the recently updated KRYSTAL-1 trial . At a dose of 600 mg twice daily, adgrasib produced a disease control rate (DCR) of 81%, an overall response rate (ORR) of 33% (7/21 patients) and a median progression-free survival (mPFS) of ~6 months in heavily pretreated patients. Toxicity was manageable: most common grade 3 toxicities were fatigue and QT prolongation on EKG. Follow-up is ongoing. Sotorasib, initially approved for the KRAS G12C mutant in nonsmall cell lung cancer, was tested in 38 patients as part of the phase I/II Codebreak K 100 trial . DCR was 84%, ORR 21%, and mPFS was ~4 months.
 This initial success in targeting KRAS in mPDAC has led to a rapid expansion of other agents and concepts. These include: 1) mutation-specific direct inhibitors (e.g. G12D – NCT05737706, G12V, and G12R), 2) mutation-specific oligonucleotides, 3) ‘pan RAS’ inhibitors (NCT05379985), 4) SOS1/SHP2 inhibitors, 5) mutation-specific immunotherapy (e.g. vaccines, adoptive T cell therapy, 6) developing drug combinations (e.g. EGFR inhibitors – NCT04862780, immune checkpoint inhibitors) to augment KRAS inhibition, and 7) targeting the downstream KRAS pathway (e.g. MEK/ERK inhibition) and in particular, pairing them with autophagy inhibitors to exploit the metabolic requirements of PDAC cells (FRAME trial NCT0385820). ## DNA damage repair inhibition
The POLO trial led to the approval of olaparib (a poly-ADP ribose polymerase inhibitor, which retards DNA damage repair) as an approved therapy in the maintenance position for mPDAC following response to a platinum-containing regimen. This trial was a phase III study of 154 patients who harbored BRCA1 or 2 mutations and were progression-free after a minimum of 16 weeks of therapy using a first-line platinum-containing regimen. The patients were randomized to either olaparib (92 patients) or placebo (62 patients). The mPFS in the olaparib group was significantly longer than in the placebo group (7.4 months vs. 3.8 months; HR: 0.53; 95% CI: 0.35–0.82; P=0.004). However, the final mOS was similar between the two arms: 19 months in the olaparib arm versus 19.2 months in the placebo arm, P=0.35. This trial established ‘proof-of-principle’ for the use of targeted therapies for pancreatic cancer as well as sparking interest in the concept of ‘consolidation’ or ‘continuation’ therapy for patients with advanced PDAC to enable them to maintain disease responsiveness while avoiding the inconvenience and side effects of indefinite chemotherapy .
Patients with somatic mutations of homologous recombination repair have heightened sensitivity to chemotherapy, especially platinum-based . Reiss et al. showed superiority for platinum-containing versus nonplatinum-containing regimens in patients with mPDAC. Efforts are underway to standardize the definition of homologous recombination deficiency in PDAC, testing advanced generation PARP inhibitors (e.g. rucaparib) and the impact of combination therapies (e.g. PARP inhibitor plus checkpoint inhibitor – NCT 04666740).

## Immunotherapy
The only immunotherapy currently FDA-approved for use in mPDAC is pembrolizumab (patients with TMB ≥10 or MSI-high patients). Unlike some other cancers, PD-L-1 expression fails to predict response to pembrolizumab in PDAC . These criteria only apply to 1–2% of patients with mPDAC: response rates are also disappointingly low (18% substantially lower than other MSI-high patients , even in patients who meet these criteria).
Given these results, and remembering with immunotherapy that ‘the T cell is the drug’, a wide variety of other immunotherapeutic approaches is currently being investigated, including other immune checkpoint inhibitors (NCT 03175842), bispecific antibodies, TIGIT (T cell immunoreceptor with immunoglobulin and ITIM domain) blockade in an attempt to prevent T cell ‘exhaustion’ (NCT05419479), oncolytic viruses (NCT02705196), cellular therapies (TIL cells (NCT01174121), neoantigen TCR cell therapy , CAR-T cells (NCT01583686), PDAC vaccination strategies, and antibodies directed against a variety of other targets influencing the immune system among them. Numerous combination immunotherapeutic strategies are also under development.
CD40 agonists are one type of immunotherapy that has attracted particular interest. CD40 agonists have a variety of effects in the immunological milieu of mPDAC, including enhancement of antigen presentation by dendritic cells and transformation of tumor immunosuppressive macrophages into tumoricidal macrophages. The PRINCE trial was a three-arm phase Ib/II trial in which GnP was studied both with and without the CD40 agonist soltigalimab and with and without the immune checkpoint inhibitor nivolumab in treatment-naïve mPDAC. A total of 105 patients were randomized to the three arms; only one (the GnP/nivolumab arm) achieved success with regard to the primary study endpoint – statistical significance of 1-year OS versus a historical control 1-year OS of 35% (1-year OS 57.7%, lower boundary, 95% CI: 41.7%, P=0.006, median OS 16.7 months (95% CI: 9.8–18.4 months). The other two study arms, GnP/sotigalimab and GnP/sotigalimab/nivolumab, failed to achieve the primary endpoint (1-year OS 48.1%, P=0.062, median OS 11.4 months, 95% CI: 7.2–20.1 months and 1-year OS 41.3%, P=0.233, median OS 10.1 months (95% CI: 7.9–20.1 months).
The study was critiqued with respect to 1) the absence of a concomitant control arm, 2) the failure of immune checkpoint inhibitors to enhance response to GnP in prior studies, and 3) the failure of the combination immunotherapy arm to achieve statistical significance. However, the study did show that longer survival was associated with particular gene expression signatures and a variety of immunological effects. As such, a search for biomarkers predicting response to the agents in this study is underway.
For a more detailed review of immunotherapeutic approaches in PDAC, the reader is referred to the work of Ullman et al. .

## Cancer metabolism
PDAC has significant metabolic requirements. Despite existing in a relatively hypoxic and nutrient-poor environment, PDAC gains metabolic advantage mainly in three ways: 1) reprogramming intracellular energy metabolism, 2) improving nutrient acquisition by scavenging and recycling (autophagy), and 3) conducting metabolic crosstalk with other components within the microenvironment (see Fig. ).
Each of these methods of metabolic advantage has been targeted in recent clinical trials. An example of the first is CPI-613 (devimistat) a mitochondrial inhibitor of the tricarboxylic cycle. A randomized phase III study comparing FFX with and without devimistat was conducted in 528 patients worldwide (AVENGER trial) . Unfortunately, this was a negative study (median OS 11.1 vs. 11.7 months, P=0.655).
An example of the second is combining a downstream KRAS inhibitor (e.g. MEK, which increases PDAC dependence on autophagy for nutritional requirements) with an autophagy inhibitor. Although targeting MEK alone has not produced significant therapeutic success in PDAC, the potential of this approach was demonstrated using trametinib and hydroxychloroquine (the latter as an autophagy inhibitor) from a case report in a heavily pretreated patient at the University of Utah . Additional studies testing this concept are currently underway (NCT03825289).
The final approach is illustrated by targeting of cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) (Fig. ). CAFs have a variety of metabolic impacts, including the ability to secrete high-energy metabolites such as pyruvate and lactate. CAFs also enrich the microenvironment by releasing exosomes containing tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle intermediates, amino acids and lipids, along with many other protumor effects related to extracellular stromal deposition and immunosuppression. A variety of drugs (e.g. CXCR4/12 inhibitors, vitamin D – NCT04543071) can inhibit CAF activity , .
 For a more detailed review of PDAC metabolism and potential metabolic targets, the reader is referred to the article by Qin et al. .

## Extracellular matrix
mPDAC exists in a complex symbiosis with its surrounding stroma. Characterized by both cellular and noncellular elements, the stroma serves as a barrier to effective drug therapy, both in terms of drug delivery and immunogenicity.
Antistromal therapeutics in mPDAC may focus on either its cellular and/or noncellular constituents. In addition to CAFs cited above, another example of cellular-directed antistromal therapy is agents directed against myeloid-derived stem cells (MDSCs), an important aspect of the immunosuppressive environment that characterizes mPDAC. MDSCs can be targeted in a variety of ways, including drugs that block MDSC recruitment (e.g. CXCR2 inhibitors – NCT 04477343).
An example of an agent directed against the noncellular component of the extracellular matrix is PEGPH20, which decreases the amount of hyaluronic acid in the PDAC stroma and improves survival in mouse models , presumably by increasing drug delivery via decreased interstitial fluid pressure limiting tumor perfusion. Unfortunately, in a 500-patient trial (HALO 301 trial) that compared GnP with and without PEGPH20, no difference was seen in OS between the two arms (mOS 11.2 vs. 11.5 months, HR=1.00, P=0.096).
For a more detailed review of the role of the extracellular matrix in PDAC, the reader is referred to the articles by Hingorani and Perez et al. , .

## Drug therapy in PDAC – new approaches applied to localized disease

## Resequencing standard therapies
The primary determinant of overall survival in localized PDAC is control of systemic disease. To this end, current clinical trials in localized PDAC often explore a comparison of standard drug regimens or sequence of integration with local therapeutic modalities (surgery, radiotherapy). Important examples in this regard include 1) the PREOPANC 2 (EudraCT 2017-002036-17) trial in resectable or borderline resectable PDAC, which looks to extend the observations from PREOPANC 1 by comparing neoadjuvant FFX×8 versus gemcitabine/RT followed by gemcitabine×4 as used in PREOPANC-1, 2) PREOPANC-3 (NCT04927780) and Alliance A021806(NCT0430141) trials in resectable PDAC, both of which compare neoadjuvant FFX×8, followed by FFX×4 postoperatively with all 12 cycles of FFX given in the adjuvant position, and 3) the SOFT (NCT03704662) trial in all potentially operable (resectable, borderline resectable, and locally advanced type A) PDAC patients with neoadjuvant chemotherapy per investigator followed randomization to either gemcitabine-based chemoradiation or SBRT.

## Novel drug approaches - neoadjuvant therapy
Another approach to improving clinical outcomes using extant chemotherapy is to employ alternate drug sequencing strategies such as those cited previously. Current research trials include: 1) alternating chemotherapy regimens (NCT03703063) which will utilize GnP alternating with the NAPOLI-1 regimen in 70 patients with resectable and borderline resectable PDAC, 2) switching between chemotherapy regimens (NCT04539808) which will study in 60 potentially operable patients of any PDAC stage a switch from FFX to GnP based on progression or toxicity, and 3) analyzing resected tissue to determine the best chemotherapy regimen going forward. An example of the last is the NeoPanc One study (NCT 03531125) in Canada, a randomized phase II trial to include 84 patients in which DFS is to be analyzed in resected PDAC using perioperative prophylaxis according to baseline GATA6 levels.
Neoadjuvant treatment requires consideration of the biology of both macrometastatic and micrometastatic disease sites. Application of novel drugs during neoadjuvant therapy offers the advantages of 1) avoidance of toxicity engendered by other therapeutic modalities (or time) and 2) the ability to molecularly interrogate the tumor following treatment to analyze both local and systemic disease response/progression. An example new drug application in localized (locally advanced PDAC) in the neoadjuvant space is pamrevlumab. An investigational agent broadly directed at the extracellular matrix, pamrevlumab inhibits connective tissue growth factor (CTGF-1), a central mediator of fibrosis in a wide variety of disease states and is a central component of the molecular crosstalk between mPDAC and the surrounding stroma (see Fig. ).
 Pamrevlumab was found to have significant anti-PDAC activity in clinical models and was utilized in 24 patients with locally advanced PDAC. Both trials produced an excellent safety profile and encouraging therapeutic results. As a result, pamrevlumab is being tested in a phase III registration trial in locally advanced PDAC (the LAPIS trial NCT03941093). In this trial, patients may receive either GnP or FFX and are then randomized to either pamrevlumab or placebo infusion. After six cycles of therapy, patients are evaluated for surgical resection. The primary endpoint is OS; frequency of surgical resection (as expressed through the concept of event-free survival) is a secondary endpoint. This trial is unique with respect to inclusion of standardized response criteria for surgery and external review of surgical decision. This trial of over 280 patients has now completed enrollment and is awaiting final results. Pamrevlumab is also being tested in a randomized phase III trial as part of PanCAN’s Precision Promise adoptive trial platform (US NCT04229004) for mPDAC.

## Novel drug approaches - combined with local therapies
Novel drugs employed in conjunction with surgery or radiotherapy are employed with the mindset of 1) capitalizing on a therapeutic opportunity provided by the treatment sequence or 2) enhancing the effect of the local modality. An example of the former is a phase I/II trial (NCT 02451982) exploring the use of GVAX, a granulocyte-macrophage colony-stimulating factor (GM-CSF)-secreting allogenic pancreatic cancer vaccine) used with or without a PD-1 blocking antibody (nivolumab) and CD137 (a T cell stimulator) agonist antibody (urelimab) along with chemoradiotherapy as part of neoadjuvant and adjuvant treatment for resected PDAC. The primary outcome of this study is to analyze median IL17A expression in vaccine-induced lymphoid aggregates found in resection specimens.
Concerning the latter, numerous efforts have focused on immunotherapy not only as a form of radiosensitization but also as an ‘accelerant’ of immunological response in PDAC. This occurs via the creation of a kind of in situ vaccination by killing cells, thereby triggering an enhanced systemic immune response, even killing cancer cells at distant sites (abscopal effect). The integration of immunotherapy with radiation was tested initially with radiotherapy using interferon-alpha ; it is now being tested clinically in a variety of ways especially in conjunction with checkpoint inhibition (e.g. Rudloff et al. NCI NCT04327986).

## Novel drugs approaches - adjuvant therapy
PDAC in the adjuvant setting has a particular emphasis on micrometastatic disease and without some of the issues caused by the primary tumor. With respect to optimization of standard drug use, Tsai and colleagues in a trial of 130 patients with resectable and borderline resectable PDAC, found 92 (71%) of patients were able to receive proteomically-directed therapy which was obtained within 5 business days of request. Seventy-four (80%) received fluoropyrimidine-based therapy; 18 (20%) received gemcitabine-based therapy. Of the 130 patients, 107 (82%) completed all intended neoadjuvant therapy and underwent surgery. With median follow-up of 25 months, mOS for the entire patient cohort was 38 months (41 months de novo resectable and 33 months borderline resectable, P=0.99).
As for incorporation of novel drugs, potentially almost any of them could be tested in the adjuvant setting. One example is the use of durvalumab (a PDL-1 inhibitor) and olecumab (a CD73 inhibitor) along with GnP as part of a platform trial in resectable/borderline resectable PDAC (NCT04940286).
In many ways, novel agents employed at the conclusion of all other therapy for localized PDAC may offer the greatest advantages. Effects of other therapies can be avoided, residual disease is minimal, and a particular biologic characteristic of the tumor can be targeted or exploited. Using currently available agents, the APOLLO trial (following the results of the POLO trial, NCT04858334) provides a model. This trial, intended to recruit 152 patients, is a phase II trial intending to randomize patients with BRCA1, 2, or PALB-2 mutations to either olaparib or placebo. Recruitment to this trial is ongoing.
This treatment space offers particular advantages to immunologically-based approaches, such as those that target KRAS. An example is a recently launched phase Ib/II trial (KISIMA-02 trial NCT04046445) in which a heterologous prime boost vaccine directed against KRAS G12D or G12V mutant cells (ATP150/152) and also genetically engineered for cell penetration and immune stimulation is given with an oncolytic viral vector (VSV-GP154) combined with a PD-1 inhibitor (ezabenlimab) for testing in PDAC, ultimately as an adjuvant at the conclusion of other therapy (Fig. ).
 Another example of an immunological approach targeting KRAS is the AMPLIFY 201 trial . In this trial, ELI-002 2P, a vaccine comprised of lymph node-targeted Amphiphile (Amph)-modified G12D and G12R mutant KRAS peptides along with an Amph-modified CpG oligonucleotide adjuvant designed to expand polyfunctional mutant KRAS-specific T cells is administered following locoregional therapy. Twenty-two patients with resected PDAC or colorectal cancer with either elevated CA 19-9 or elevated circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) and KRA/NRAS mutation were treated via this method according to a dose-escalation schema as part of a phase I trial. Tolerance was good; biomarker reduction was observed in 15/19 (79%) and in 5/5 patients at highest doses; clearance of minimal residual disease was observed in 4/19 (21%, n=2 pancreas; n=2 colorectal). Polyfunctional KRAS-specific T cell responses were observed in 80% of patients (n=12/15), with both CD8+and CD4+T cell responses for most and CD3+T cells observed on biopsies. A phase I/randomized phase II study is planned.
Finally, additional enthusiasm for cancer vaccination in resected PDAC was also generated from a recently reported study . In a preliminary report of a phase I/II trial, 16 resected PDAC patients received an individualized mRNA neoantigen vaccine derived from the patient’s surgical specimen along with atezolizumab (a PD-L1 inhibitor) FFX. At 18-month follow-up, 8/16 patients experienced significant T cell expansion in their peripheral blood: this was correlated with an improvement in DFS compared with nonresponders (not reached vs. 13.4 months, HR 0.08, P=0.003). Although preliminary, these results produce an exciting ‘proof-of-principle’ for this approach, both in terms of preclinical and clinical science.
Success using neoantigen T cell receptor gene therapy was also described in a case report .

## Conclusion
Treatment advance in pancreatic cancer remains formidable; treatment results remain unsatisfactory. Increased global awareness of PDAC, greater sophistication of staging and supportive care and a 25-year foundation of drug trials provide a foundation upon which therapeutic progress can be built. Our new understanding of the translational biology of PDAC presents a wide range of new drug therapies for every stage of the disease. As such, a rising tide of optimism exists for future pancreatic cancer patients worldwide.
