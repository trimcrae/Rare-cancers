## Abstract
The Kirsten rat sarcoma viral oncogene homolog (KRAS) mutation is one of the most prevalent activating alterations in cancer. It indicates a poor overall prognosis due to its highly invasive nature. Although several KRAS inhibitors have been developed in recent years, a significant clinical challenge has emerged as a substantial proportion of patients eventually develop resistance to these therapies. Therefore, identifying determinants of drug resistance is critical for guiding treatment strategies. This review provides a comprehensive overview of the mutation landscape and molecular mechanisms of KRAS activity in various cancers. Meanwhile, it summaries the progress and prospects of small molecule KRAS inhibitors undergoing clinical trials. Furthemore, this review explores potential strategies to overcome drug resistance, with the ultimate goal of steering toward patient-centric precision oncology in the foreseeable future.

## Introduction
The virus-coding gene, rat sarcoma viral oncogene homolog (RAS), was initially identified by Harvey in the 1960s.[ ] Harvey RAS (HRAS), neuroblastoma RAS (NRAS), and Kirsten RAS (KRAS) are the three members of the RAS gene family[ ] and are extensively involved in various biological processes, including apoptosis, migration, differentiation, and proliferation.[ , ]
KRAS mutations are genetic drivers of multiple cancer types and are exhibited by approximately one in seven human cancers,[ ] such as non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC), and colorectal cancer (CRC).[ ]
KRAS is a membrane-bound protein with intrinsic guanosine triphosphatase (GTPase) activity.[ ] When activated by upstream signaling molecules such as the epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR), it can transduce extracellular signals to a range of downstream effector proteins that ultimately participate in cell growth, differentiation, transformation, apoptosis, and other physiological processes.[ ]
KRAS mutations have long been considered “undruggable”.[ , ] On the one hand, KRAS has a high affinity for GTP in the picomolar range, whereas the concentration of GTP in cells can reach 0.5 micromols. As a result, developing effective competitive inhibitors of GTP is difficult. On the other hand, KRAS proteins have a near-spherical structure, which is uncharacterized, lack deep hydrophobic pockets, and do not have binding sites that are suitable for small molecule allosteric modulators.
A breakthrough in KRAS-targeted therapy emerged in 2021. Sotorasib (AMG510, Amgen, Thousand Oaks, California, USA), a KRAS allele-specific covalent inhibitor, was approved in 2021 to treat patients with NSCLC and other solid cancers harboring KRASG12C mutations. In a variety of tumor types, Sotorasib and other KRAS inhibitors have demonstrated notable therapeutic success.[ ] However, the prominent single-drug resistance has emerged as an obstacle to the treatment of KRAS-mutant cancer. In this review, we present a brief overview of the most recent advances in KRAS inhibitors, with a particular emphasis on the mechanisms by which resistance develops and possible strategies to overcome it.

## KRAS Biology

## Structure and functions
The KRAS gene is located on human chromosome 12 and is a subtype of the RAS gene family.[ ] It encodes KRAS4 protein, which is also called P12 protein because of its 12,000 Da molecular mass.[ ] KRAS is a membrane-bound regulatory protein that binds guanine nucleotides and belongs to the GTPases family (G protein).[ ] KRAS protein exists as two splice isoforms,[ ] KRAS4A and KRAS4B, with KRAS4B predominating in human cells.[ ] The KRAS protein consists of six beta chains and five alpha helices organized into two domains: an N-terminal G domain and a C-terminal membrane targeting region.[ , ] The G domain, also called the catalytic domain, is a highly conserved region.[ ] It consists of the P-loop, Switch I, and Switch II regions and contributes to the promotion of GTP-guanosine diphosphate (GDP) exchange.[ , ] When GTP binds to KRAS, it leads to conformational changes in the Switch I and II regions and promotes downstream signaling pathways.[ ]
By binding to GTP and GDP, respectively, the KRAS protein functions as a molecular switch changing between active and inactive states.[ ] The GTP-bound conformation represents the “on” state, whereas the GDP-bound conformation represents the “off” state.[ ] When stimulated by relevant signaling molecules, such as EGFR, the capacity of KRAS protein to bind GDP is diminished. With the promotion of guanine nucleotide exchange factor (GEF), GTP replaces GDP to bind to KRAS protein, thereby activating KRAS protein to bind to downstream signal transduction molecules in the form of monomer or dimer.[ , ] Then, the intrinsic GTPase activity of KRAS protein is boosted in response to GTPase activating protein (GAP) stimulation, enabling GTP to be hydrolyzed to GDP and KRAS to revert to the inactive state.[ ]
KRAS activation is tightly regulated by different positive and negative regulators, ensuring a rapid response to various signals.[ ] Positive regulators known as GEFs facilitate the release of bound GDP, which results in the formation of active KRAS-GTP complexes in response to upstream signals. Son of sevenless (SOS), RAS-guanine nucleotide-releasing factor (GRF), and RAS-GRP are the three main families of RAS-GEF.[ ] In contrast, GAP is a negative regulator, mainly consisting of neurofibromin 1 (NF1) and p120GAP. GAP can enhance the intrinsic GTP enzyme activity of KRAS and promote rapid GTP hydrolysis to GDP.[ ] More recently, it was discovered that the membrane location and tyrosine phosphorylation of KRAS are negatively regulated by protein-tyrosine phosphatase non-receptor type 2 (PTPN2).[ , ] Activated KRAS proteins are engaged in numerous physiological activities such as cell survival, growth, proliferation, migration, and differentiation. The balance between nucleotide hydrolysis and exchange determines the level of activated KRAS in the cell.[ ]
KRAS mutations, on the other hand, disrupt the guanine cycling cycle, causing KRAS to become “locked” in the GTP-bound active form, hence constantly boosting downstream signaling pathways and ultimately resulting in the malignant transformation of cells.[ ]

## Upstream and downstream signaling
The KRAS protein cycles between the activated and inactivated conformations to function as a finely regulated molecular switch that affects several signaling cascades [Figure , Table ].[ ] Additionally, KRAS activation is a multi-step process controlled by upstream signaling molecules, including growth factors (epidermal growth factor, platelet-derived growth factor, and fibroblast growth factor), human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2), cytokines, integrins, chemokines, Ca2+, receptor tyrosine kinases (RTKs), and so on.[ ]
 Numerous growth factors can activate RTKs, which in turn activate the KRAS protein via intermediary molecules. For instance, epidermal growth factor (EGF) and EGFR interaction induces EGFR dimerization and autophosphorylation, phosphorylated EGFR binds to growth factor receptor-binding protein 2 (GRB2) via the respective Src homology 2 domain (SH2). Then, GRB2 activates KRAS by activating SOS1.[ ] Similarly, other upstream molecules also regulate KRAS activation and inactivation mainly through GEF and GAP.[ ] When a chemokine binds to its cognate chemokine receptors on the cell membrane, cAMP is produced, which then activates protein kinase A. In turn, activated protein kinase A promotes the activation of KRAS through phosphorylation of RAS-specific guanine nucleotide-releasing factor 1 (RAS-GRF1, a GEF that is mainly expressed in the brain).[ ] In addition, as a protein tyrosine phosphatase (PTP), SH2-containing PTP 2 (SHP2) can mediate multiple RTK to KRAS-extracellular regulated protein kinase (ERK) signaling cascades.[ ] Furthermore, SHP2 is capable of dephosphorylating p120, thereby weakening the negative regulatory effect of GAP.[ ] Tulpule et al[ ] recently demonstrated that some RTK-containing fusion proteins, such as echinoderm microtubule-associated protein-like 4 (EML4)–anaplastic lymphoma kinase (ALK), can bind to GRB2 and SOS to generate membrane-free cytoplasmic protein granules, which is a novel, membrane-independent mechanism of KRAS activation.
The activated GTP-bound KRAS protein undergoes significant conformational changes at Switch I and II, especially at the Switch II region.[ ] These two regions subsequently form a surface that can interact with downstream effectors and activate multiple signaling pathways, including the rapidly accelerated fibrosarcoma (RAF)-mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) kinase (MEK)-ERK signaling pathway, phosphoinositide 3-kinase (PI3K)-serine/threonine-protein kinase B (AKT)-mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR) signaling pathway.[ , ] Survival, proliferation, differentiation, apoptosis, metabolism, cell cycle, and other cellular life activities are affected by these pathways, which transduce signals from the cell surface to the nucleus.[ ]
The RAS-RAF-MEK-ERK signaling pathway is the canonical downstream target of KRAS.[ ] GTP-loaded KRAS promotes RAF recruitment, dimerization, and phosphorylation at the cell membrane.[ ] The RAF family (serine/threonine kinase) is the most characterized RAS effector. Activated RAF phosphorylates and activates MEK1 or MEK2, which in turn can further activate ERK1 or ERK2.[ ] After translocating to the nucleus, ERK may subsequently regulate cell proliferation, differentiation, and migration by phosphorylating a variety of cytoplasmic and nuclear proteins, including myelocytomatosis viral oncogene homolog (MYC), serum response factor (SRF), ribosomal S6 kinase (RSK), transcription factors, and cytoskeletal protein.[ , , ]
KRAS is also engaged in the PI3K-AKT-mTOR pathway,[ ] which is crucial for cellular functions such as cell proliferation, differentiation, apoptosis, glucose transport, and tumor drug resistance.[ ] GTP-bound KRAS initially attaches to the p110s site of PI3K to activate it, and active PI3K can in turn facilitate the conversion of phosphatidylinositol 4,5-bisphosphate (PIP2) to phosphatidylinositol 3,4,5-trisphosphate (PIP3).[ , ] This stepwise phosphorylation process is negatively regulated by phosphatases such as tumor suppressor phosphatase and tensin homolog deleted on chromosome 10 (PTEN).[ ] PIP3 promotes phosphorylation of phosphatidylinositol-dependent protein kinase 1 (PDK1) to AKT, which then activates the mTOR pathway.[ ] And mTOR target proteins have critical roles in cell proliferation, survival, metabolism, protein synthesis, and transcription.[ ] Moreover, the mTOR complex can phosphorylate AKT at its serine phosphorylation site,[ ] resulting in full AKT activation, which activates the B-cell lymphoma/leukemia (Bcl-XL/Bcl-2) associated death promoter (BADs),[ ] stimulates BADs binding to chaperone proteins, and suppresses apoptosis.[ ]
Other prominent KRAS effectors include Ras-like protein (RAL) guanine nucleotide dissociation stimulator (RAL-GDS) and T lymphoma invasion and metastasis (TIAM1).[ , ] The downstream effectors of RAL-GDS proteins can promote cell migration and KRAS-dependent tumor growth. They include phospholipase D (PLD) related to endocytosis, TRAF family member associated NF-κB activator(TANK) binding kinase 1 (TBK1) related to viral immunity, and cell division cycle 42 (CDC42) related to cell migration.[ ] Additionally, RAL-GDS triggers the c-Jun N-terminal kinase (JNK) pathway, leading to transcription and cell cycle progression.[ ] TIAM1-RAC is a GEF that leads to the phosphorylation of p21-activated kinase (PAK) and is involved in RAS-related C3 botulinum toxin substrate 1 (RAC1) signaling in cytoskeletal rearrangement, cell migration, endocytosis, and membrane trafficking.[ , ] KRAS-induced cell transformation in human cancers requires the expression of Yes-associated protein 1 (YAP1) expression.[ ] Epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) is modulated by the convergence of YAP1 and KRAS on the transcription factor FGF-inducible immediate-early response protein (FOS).[ ] Additionally, KRAS contributes to metabolic pathways that enhance the nutritional flux of tumor cells, including glutamine hydrolysis, glucose absorption, glycolysis, tumor metabolism, redox homeostasis, and lipid metabolism.[ , , ]
Therefore, KRAS is a key link connecting various cellular life activities, and its mutations can lead to tumorigenesis through the activation of a variety of downstream pathways.[ , ]

## KRAS Mutations in Cancers

## KRAS mutation-driven cancers
Mutation or amplification of the RAS gene is one of the most common abnormalities in human malignancies.[ ] Approximately a quarter of human cancers carry RAS mutations, which can result in up to 1 million mortalities worldwide annually.[ ]
KRAS is most frequently mutated, especially in solid tumors.[ ]
KRAS accounts for 80% of RAS-related malignancies and is considered one of the most prevalent oncogene drivers in human cancers.[ ]
NRAS mutations have been identified in melanoma and many hematological cancers, whereas HRAS mutations are more common in bladder, thyroid, cervical, and head and neck cancers.[ ]
The KRAS gene uses exon four sequences to encode two splice variants KRAS4A and KRAS4B, both of which are associated with tumor formation.[ ]
KRAS mutation or KRAS wild-type (KRASwt) amplification is most common in PDAC, accounting for approximately 91.21% of the patients (data acquired from cBioPortal.org).[ , ]
KRAS mutations frequency were also high in patients with CRC and NSCLC, with incidences of 42.09% and 22.45%, respectively. Besides, KRAS mutations are mainly found in cholangiocarcinoma, appendiceal, endometrial, gastroesophageal, and ovarian cancers [Figure A], with some variations among racial groups and nationalities.[ ]
 The majority of KRAS mutations are single-base missense mutations, which mainly contain 21 missense alterations and typically occur at codon 12 (G12), codon 13 (G13), or codon 61 (Q61). Other uncommon KRAS mutation sites, involve codons 2, 3, 59, 117, and 146 also contribute to enhancing the oncogenic potential.[ ] Approximately 83% of the mutations occur at G12, causing the substitution of glycine by aspartic acid (G12D, 41%), valine (G12V, 28%), cysteine (G12C, 14%), and other amino acids. These missense mutations prevent GTP hydrolysis, which significantly raises the baseline RAS–GTP: RAS–GDP ratio and results in hyperactivating KRAS and its downstream effectors.[ , ]
Notably, significant differences in the distribution of KRAS mutant subgroups among tumor types have been described [Figure B]. KRASG12C is the most prevalent type of KRAS mutation in lung cancer, accounting for about 43.60% of all KRAS mutations, and is strongly associated with smoking, whereas the KRASG12D mutation is the most common in nonsmokers.[ , ] In pancreatic cancer, KRASG12D and KRASG12V are the predominant mutations, accounting for 40.70% and 31.46% of all KRAS mutations, respectively.[ ] As with pancreatic cancer, G12D and G12V are the most frequent mutations in CRC. It should be pointed out that G13 mutations are detected in approximately 18% of KRAS alterations in CRC but are extremely rarely seen in lung and pancreatic cancers.[ ] Only CRC was found to harbor mutations at codons 117 (K117N/R) and 146 (A146T/V).[ ]
Distinct KRAS mutations trigger specific downstream pathways that confer different phenotypic characteristics on tumors and may have a strong association with tumor type.[ ] The KRASG12V mutation phosphorylates ERK via RAF1 in mouse tumors, but it does not activate AKT. In contrast, G12D markedly increases AKT activation.[ ] In a mouse model, lung adenocarcinomas driven by KRASG12C mutation rely more on RAL pathways to stimulate cell growth than do KRASwt, and the KRASG12C mutation induces greater ERK1/2 phosphorylation and responds better to MEK inhibition than cells with the KRASG12D mutation.[ ] Therefore, identifying which downstream KRAS pathway is activated primarily in a given tumor plays a crucial role in deciding on therapeutic strategies for different patients in the clinic and could potentially guide the evolution of more targeted drugs.[ , ]

## Impact of KRAS mutations on prognosis
KRAS mutations have a strong impact on the prognosis of cancer patients, albeit the results of different studies have shown some inconsistencies, which may be related to specific mutation subtypes and tumor types.[ ]
In PDAC, most studies tend to consider KRAS mutation as a predictor of poor prognosis. Itonaga et al[ ] analyzed 110 patients with PDAC with confirmed pathological diagnosis and found that patients with the KRASwt gene had longer progression-free survival (PFS) and overall survival (OS) than those with KRAS mutation (6.9/5.3 months [P = 0.044] and 19.9/11.8 months [P = 0.037], respectively). A study of 39 PDAC patients indicated that patients with KRAS mutations, particularly those with the G12D subtype, had significantly lower OS and disease-free survival (DFS).[ ] Since PDAC are highly heterogeneous,[ ] and the fraction of malignant cells in the tumor varies from patient to patient, the development of next-generation sequencing (NGS) technology has made it possible to assess the mutation frequency of alleles in tumor samples. Nauheim et al[ ] analyzed microanatomical samples from 144 patients with PDAC, 121 of whom (84%) contained KRAS mutations. According to their research, patients with higher KRAS mutation frequency (≥20%) have larger tumors, higher rates of distal recurrence after surgery, and shorter postoperative DFS than those with lower KRAS mutation frequency (<20%). G12D mutation is also an independent risk factor for poor prognosis in patients with unresectable PDAC, whereas the Q61 subtype predicts better survival, which may be attributed to subtle variations in downstream effector proteins and pathways of different KRAS mutation subtypes.[ ] Additionally, PDAC patients with detectable KRAS mutations in plasma before treatment have a significantly poorer survival rate (16.8 months vs. not reached, P <0.05), and mutations are an independent predictor of early recurrence after surgery.[ ] However, in a study of 219 patients with locally advanced or metastatic PDAC, Bournet et al[ ] reported no difference in survival between KRAS mutant and KRASwt tumors.
Similar to PDAC, KRAS mutations have a negative impact on prognosis and survival in patients with CRC.[ , ] For instance, a study evaluating patients with stage IV CRC indicated that KRAS mutant patients had a shorter OS than KRASwt patients (P = 0.004).[ ] In two studies using EGFR inhibitors with third-line chemotherapy and receiving rectal resection with neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy (NCRT), respectively, KRAS mutation proved to be an independent influencing factor for shorter PFS and poorer prognosis in patients with advanced CRC.[ , ] Furthermore, regression of the KRAS mutation was associated with a better prognosis and oligometastatic status in metastatic CRC patients.[ ] Nevertheless, another study using the National Cancer Database reported that the KRAS mutation was associated with a poorer OS than the KRASwt in patients with CRC under the age of 70 years, whereas no significant association was identified in patients older than 70 years.[ ]
In the investigations of NSCLC, the prognostic impact of KRAS mutations is controversial. According to Pan et al[ ], an analysis of 41 studies encompassing 13,103 patients with early resected NSCLC, KRAS mutations were related to worse OS (hazard ratio [HR]: 1.56 [95% confidence interval [CI]: 1.39–1.76]) and DFS (HR: 1.57 [95% CI: 1.17–2.09]). Modest et al[ ] found that patients with KRAS mutation or p21 expression had poorer survival outcomes than those without these abnormalities (HR: 1.35 [95% CI: 1.16–1.56]). In a trial of patients with refractory metastatic NSCLC treated with targeted therapy, patients with KRASG12C or KRASG12V mutations had a shorter PFS than those with KRASwt or other KRAS mutations.[ ] However, several other studies have shown that KRAS mutations or KRASwt do not statistically significantly improve OS in NSCLC patients.[ , ]
In conclusion, the impact of KRAS mutations differs depending on the specific mutation type and tumor type.[ ] This may partly explain the conflicting results in different studies. Further investigation is required to define and evaluate the true prognostic and predictive utility of KRAS subtype mutations in cancers.[ ]

## KRAS Inhibitors

## Direct inhibitors
Directly targeting KRAS relies on exploring the structural motifs on the protein surface to facilitate specific ligands, which has historically been considered challenging.[ ] Over the past decade, significant milestones have been achieved in targeting KRAS, as scientists have gained more insights into the structure of KRAS and the intricate interactions involved in the RAS signaling protein family [Figure ].[ ]Table lists the currently ongoing clinical trials involving KRAS inhibitors.
 

## Selective KRAS inhibitors
In 2013, Shokat et al[ ] introduced a mutation-specific, innovative approach targeting the Switch I and II structures of KRAS. Through modifications at cysteine 12 and targeting the nucleotide pocket binding site, small molecule drugs were able to irreversibly maintain the KRAS in its GDP-bound state thereby disrupting the RAF or other downstream pathways.[ ] On May 28, 2021, Sotorasib (AMG510) was approved by the United States Food and Drug Administration (FDA) as a tyrosine kinase inhibitor (TKI) targeting KRASG12C,[ ] offering a treatment option for adult patients with NSCLC carrying the KRASG12C mutation who have undergone at least one systemic therapy. In the CodeBreaK 100 phase II monotherapy trial (No. NCT03600883), Sotorasib showed an objective response rate (ORR) of 37%, a median PFS of 6.8 months, and a median OS of 12.3 months.[ ] Sotorasib has received approval in over 45 countries to date. In patients with rare KRASG12C mutations, such as those with PDAC and CRC, Sotorasib has also demonstrated some encouraging clinical benefits. In the CodeBreak 100 trial, Sotorasib-treated ORRs were 9.7% for patients with CRC and 21.0% for patients with PDAC patients.[ ] The most recent findings from the CodeBreak 200 phase III clinical trial (No. NCT04303780) show that when Sotorasib is taken as a treatment for advanced NSCLC with KRASG12C mutation, the PFS is 5.6 months, which offers a moderately beneficial alternative to treatment with docetaxel (a paclitaxel drug with a PFS of 4.5 months). However, no improvement occurs in terms of OS for patients with previously treated metastatic NSCLC.[ ]
Similar to Sotorasib, Adagrasib (MRTX849, Mirati Therapeutics, California, USA) is a small-molecule inhibitor of KRASG12C. Adagrasib binds irreversibly to the mutant protein in its GDP-bound state, maintaining KRAS inactive. The major advantages of Adagrasib include its long half-life (5 hours vs. 23 hours for Sotorasib), excellent oral bioavailability, broad tissue distribution, and central nervous system penetration.[ , ] Adagrasib exhibited promising results in the phase I/II Krystal-1 trial (No. NCT03785249) involving 51 NSCLC patients, showcasing an ORR of 42.9%, a PFS of 6.5 months, a median OS of 12.6 months, and the disease control rate (DCR) was 96%.[ , , ] Additionally, Adagrasib achieves clinical effectiveness in treating digestive system tumors such as pancreatic cancer and cholangiocarcinoma as well as non-digestive system tumors such as ovarian and endometrial cancer.[ , ] In December 2022, the FDA expedited the approval of Adagrasib for the treatment of patients with locally advanced or metastatic NSCLC considering its excellent safety and efficacy profile.[ ]
On August 24, 2023, a KRASG12C inhibitor, Divarasib (GDC-6036, Genentech, South San Francisco, California, USA), was reported to achieve promising outcomes in its initial human trial (No. NCT04449874) with a confirmed ORR among NSCLC patients of 53.4%, one case of complete response (CR), and a median PFS duration of 13.1 months, which was numerically superior to Sotorasib or Adagasib.[ ] Furthermore, patients with CRC showed an ORR of 35.9%, and a median PFS of 6.9 months, and patients with PDAC (only seven cases) showed an ORR of 43%.[ ] Divarasib has the potential as an additional very selective and effective treatment option for patients with solid tumors harboring the KRASG12C mutation.[ , ]
These achievements have sparked the development of novel KRASG12C inhibitors, which have revealed encouraging outcomes. For example, Opnurasib (JDQ443, Novartis) is a selective covalent KRASG12C inhibitor with a unique binding site.[ ] In patients with NSCLC, Opnurasib achieved a DCR of 93% and an ORR of 42% (No. NCT04699188).[ ] Similarly, Olomorasib (LY3537982, Eli Lilly and Company, Indianapolis, Indiana, USA),[ ] Glecarisib (JAB-21822, Jacobio, Beijing, China),[ ] Garsorasib,[ ] D-1553,[ ] and MK-1084[ ] are also undergoing corresponding clinical trials.
The successful development of KRASG12C inhibitors hinges on the covalent inhibition at cysteine and the notably high GTPase activity specific to KRASG12C, traits not entirely present in other KRAS mutation variants. Therefore, new strategies are needed to target other common KRAS mutations. MRTX1133 has been identified as an effective, selective, non-covalent KRASG12D inhibitor that binds to Switch II pockets with a picomolar binding affinity.[ ] It prevents proteins from interacting with effector RAF and inhibits nucleotide exchange.[ ] Preclinical studies demonstrate that MRTX1133 exhibits strong anti-cancer properties in KRASG12D-driven lung, pancreatic, and CRC homograft and xenograft models.[ ] Solid tumors are currently undergoing a phase 1 clinical trial (No. NCT05737706). HRS-4642, a highly selective KRASG12D inhibitor, is undergoing phase I clinical trials in China (No. NCT05533463), with preliminary results showing 33% ORR and 90% DCR in patients with NSCLC and CRC (n = 10).[ ] Another KRASG12D inhibitor called INCB161734 is in the early stages of clinical investigation for treating advanced and metastatic solid cancers with KRASG12D mutations, particularly PDAC, CRC, and NSCLC (No. NCT06179160).
As allele-specific KRAS inhibitors have been introduced, additional clinical data in comparison studies are required to determine individualized therapy options for patients with various tumor types.[ ]

## Pan-KRAS inhibitors
Although directly targeting specific KRAS mutations has shown promising therapeutic effects, selective inhibitors may encounter substantial limitations within heterogeneous and drug-resistant tumors. Recently, the discovery of pan-KRAS inhibitors and targeted chimeric pan-KRAS protein degraders (also known as proteolysis-targeting chimeras [PROTACs]) has been reported,[ ] utilizing KRAS fragment screening and structure-based drug design.[ ] These compounds demonstrate efficacy against a spectrum of cell lines propelled by KRAS mutations or harboring KRAS amplifications.[ ] In particular, the noncovalent KRAS inhibitor compound BI-2865 targets the majority of wild-type and mutant KRAS isoforms by binding to the inactivated KRAS–GDP state with high affinity and prevents nucleotide exchange, while maintaining NRAS and HRAS activity.[ ] In a preclinical study, BI-2865 substantially diminished tumor growth in KRAS-mutant mice.[ ] Similarly, BI-3706674, a pan-KRAS OFF-state inhibitor, is currently in phase I clinical trials involving patients with advanced solid tumors (No. NCT06056024).
Targeting a broad coverage of KRAS mutations seems feasible through diverse strategies for generating pan-RAS inhibitors that block three RAS isoforms (KRAS, NRAS, and HRAS). The small molecule compound RMC-6236 is a non-selective RAS inhibitor that disrupts interactions of multiple downstream effectors by forming a RAS-GTP-GEF ternary complex, thus blocking RAS in its “on” state.[ ] Encouraging and sustained activity of RMC-6236 was observed in studies involving mouse models with KRASG12X mutant cell lines or xenografts of lung, pancreatic, and colon tumors.[ ] Additionally, combining RMC-6236 with anti-PD1 immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) exhibited synergistic effects in vivo, and phase I clinical trials for RMC-6236 are presently underway (No. NCT5379985). RSC-1255 is another small molecule inhibitor showcasing wide-ranging efficacy against both mutant and wild-type RAS.[ ]
Pan-KRAS inhibitors and degraders might emerge as preferred options for patients with KRASwt-driven tumors, harboring multiple mutations, undetermined mutation subtypes, or showing resistance to selective KRAS inhibitors.[ ] Examples include cancers driven by KRASwt amplifications or NF1 tumor suppressor loss, as well as Type 1 neurofibromatosis or its associated malignancies.[ ] Nevertheless, drug tolerance that targets all RAS proteins remains an unresolved issue.

## Indirect inhibitors

## Targeting upstream pathways

## SOS inhibitors
The upstream signaling cascade of KRAS necessitates GEF activation, culminating in the “on” state of KRAS bound to GTP. Among the RAS–EGF family, SOS is widely expressed and involved in the downstream signaling of RTKs.[ ] Consequently, inhibiting SOS1 effectively attenuates KRAS-mediated signaling by hampering the binding of KRAS with GTP. BAY-293 represents the pioneering SOS1 inhibitor demonstrating diminished RAS-mediated signaling and anti-proliferative effects in both KRASwt and KRASG12C mutant cell lines.[ ] Hofmann et al[ ] introduced BI-3406, an orally bioavailable, highly effective, and selectively targeted small molecule SOS1 inhibitor that binds to the catalytic domain of SOS1, thereby disrupting its interaction with KRAS. BI-3406 demonstrates enhanced efficacy against KRAS-driven tumors both in vitro and in vivo and synergizes with the MEK inhibitor Trametinib, resulting in tumor regression and adequate tolerability. BI-1701963, an improved version of BI-3406, is undergoing clinical trials either as a monotherapy or in combination with Trametinib (No. NCT04111458) or Adagrasib (No. NCT04975256) for the treatment of advanced KRAS mutant tumors, and overall tolerance seems adequate at present.[ ] Further research findings are expected on several other SOS1 inhibitors that have been developed, including SDGR5 (Schrödinger), RMC-5845 (Revolution Medicines), GH52 (Genhouse Bio), and MRTX0902.[ , ]

## SHP2 inhibitors
SHP2 functions as a non-receptor protein tyrosine phosphatase, enhancing RAS nucleotide exchange via its interaction with GRB2 and SOS1.[ ] Full activation of the RAS–MAPK pathway requires SHP2, and its inhibition or deletion can decelerate tumor progression. Presently, SHP2 inhibitors such as JAB-3068 (Nos. NCT03518554, NCT04721223), Batoprotafib (TNO-155) (No. NCT03114319),[ , ] and RMC-4630 (No. NCT03634982),[ ] are undergoing clinical trials. They selectively bind to regions outside the GTP catalytic pocket, effectively restraining cell proliferation and impeding tumor growth. Given that both SOS1 and SHP2 inhibitors shift KRAS equilibrium towards GDP-binding status, the rationale for combining these indirect KRAS modulators with mutation-specific KRAS inhibitors is compelling.[ ] Preclinical studies highlight the synergistic anti-proliferative effects and augmented anti-tumor efficacy of SHP2 inhibitors in combination with KRASG12C inhibitors,[ ] as well as SOS1 inhibitors paired with a KRASG12C inhibitor.[ ] Furthermore, the combined inhibition of SHP2 with MEK inhibitors thwarts MAPK signaling rebound, culminating in more potent anti-tumor effects.[ ]

## EGFR inhibitors
As a RTK, EGFR assumes a pivotal role in both cell proliferation and migration, and phosphorylated EGFR can initiate KRAS activation. EGFR-targeted compounds, including Cetuximab, Gefitinib, and TKIs, have demonstrated notable anti-tumor efficacy.[ ] With the ongoing development of KRAS inhibitors, research attention has shifted towards the combined therapy involving KRAS inhibitors and EGFR inhibitors.[ ] For instance, in the treatment of 32 patients with CRC and KRASG12C mutation, the use of Adagrasib alongside Cetuximab exhibited a remarkable ORR of 43% and achieved a DCR of 100%.[ ] However, the tolerability, efficacy, and prognostic value of EGFR inhibitors in patients with KRAS-mutated cancers still require further evaluation.

## Targeting downstream pathways

## MAPK pathway inhibitors
Targeting downstream components of the RAS-MAPK pathway, such as RAF, MEK, and ERK, represents crucial targets in KRAS inhibition.[ ] However, their efficacy in early clinical trials has been underwhelming, which is attributed to MAPK pathway reactivation due to upstream feedback release and the limited efficacy of MAPK pathway inhibitors at maximum tolerated doses. As the first RAF inhibitor to enter clinical trials, Vemurafenib was approved by the FDA in 2011 for the treatment of metastatic melanoma.[ , ] Sorafenib is one of the first drugs used to target the RAF/MEK/ERK pathway in KRAS-mutated NSCLC but was proven to be ineffective.[ ] Preclinical research indicates that Lifirafenib (BGB-283), a reversible inhibitor of RAF and EGFR, enhances the antitumor performance of MEK inhibitors in KRAS-mutant tumors,[ ] and is currently being tested in clinical trials (No. NCT02610361).[ ] In tumor cells with KRAS mutations, RAF/MEK inhibitor combos reported synergistic effects.[ ]
MEK inhibitors, such as Trametinib, Cobimetinib, and Binimetinib, have been approved for the treatment of patients with RAF-mutated advanced melanoma.[ , ] For RAS mutant melanoma, Binimetinib has proven treatment efficacy.[ , ] However, Trametinib did not improve survival in patients with advanced NSCLC harboring KRAS mutations.[ , ] Guo et al[ ] reported VS-6766, a dual RAF-MEK inhibitor displaying reduced feedback-driven pathway rebound and potentially higher therapeutic efficacy compared to other MEK inhibitors. Phase I trials in solid malignancies carrying mutations in the RAS-RAF-MEK pathway or multiple myeloma showcased VS-6766’s safety and efficacy.[ ] Phase I and II studies involving VS-6766 in combination with Defactinib (Nos. NCT04620330 and NCT04625270), Cetuximab (No. NCT05200442), Adagrasib (No. NCT05375994), and Sotorasib (No. NCT05074810) are planned or ongoing, targeting various malignancies encompassing lung, ovarian, and colon. SCH772984 is an ERK-specific inhibitor that has shown potent efficacy in RAS-mutant cancer cells.[ , ] Ulixertinib, also known as BVD-523, is a reversible ATP-competitive ERK inhibitor that displays dose-dependent tumor growth inhibition properties.[ , ] Dabrafenib,[ , ] Belvarafenib,[ , ] Naporafenib (LXH-254),[ , , ] and others are inhibitors of this pathway.
To achieve notable anti-tumor effects, emerging therapeutic approaches have concentrated on combination therapy to block the compensatory mechanism of downstream signals.[ ]

## PI3K pathway inhibitors
The PI3K-AKT-mTOR pathway is another mature downstream axis of RAS. Alpelisib (BYL719, Novartis, Basel, Switzerland) is a p110α-specific inhibitor tested in phase I trials for patients with late-stage solid tumors with PI3K alteration.[ , ] The pan-PI3K inhibitor Pictilisib in combination with first-line therapy of NSCLC exhibited promising anticancer effects and safety in phase I trials.[ ] In hormone receptor-positive breast cancer patients, the AKT inhibitor MK2206 demonstrated an increase in the rate of pathologic CR.[ ] Everolimus (RAD001), a mTOR inhibitor derived from rapamycin, has gained FDA approval for treating several cancers, notably advanced renal cell carcinoma (RCC).[ ] In the phase III RECORD1 trial, Everolimus was associated with prolonged PFS in patients with advanced RCC.[ ] The combination of the second-generation KRASG12C inhibitor ARS1620 with a PI3K inhibitor induced tumor regression in KRASG12C mutant NSCLC both in vitro and in vivo.[ ] However, safety concerns regarding the on-target and off-target toxicities of PI3K inhibitors, which include hyperglycemia, rash, and diarrhea, remain a critical consideration.[ ]

## Other mechanisms

## Disrupting KRAS membrane localization
Only membrane-bound KRAS can activate downstream signaling pathways, with farnesyltransferase (FTase) playing a pivotal role in KRAS localization.[ ] Thus, blocking this process seems a rational approach to inhibiting KRAS activity. FTase inhibitors (FTIs), such as Tipifarnib and Lonafarnib, have shown significant effects in mouse models and early trials, but they did not demonstrate notable anti-tumor efficacy in phase III clinical trials.[ ] This may be attributed to the presence of an alternative geranylgeranyltransferase-I (GGTase-I) bypass pathway. Salirasib, a second-generation drug inhibiting membrane localization of all activated RAS subtypes, also failed to exhibit any meaningful activity in a phase II clinical trial, with no responses observed in all 33 patients.[ ] Since many other proteins also require farnesylation, more research is needed regarding the clinical utility of FTIs.

## Gene therapy
Gene therapy offers a promising approach to treating diverse pathological conditions by delivering specific genetic materials—such as small interfering RNAs (siRNAs), microRNAs (miRNAs), and clustered regularly interspaced short palindromic repeats and CRISPR-CRISPR-associated protein 9 (CRISPR-Cas9) systems—to modify gene expression or alter cellular behavior.[ ] For instance, the siRNA-based strategy, siG12D-LODER, targeting oncogenic KRAS mutations has demonstrated significant therapeutic potential and is in clinical trials.[ ] Similarly, the cyclic RGD peptide-modified polymersomes loaded with siRNA (cRGD-BCP-siKRAS), designed to target the KRASG12D mutation, achieved marked tumor growth suppression in mice, with 40% achieving complete regression.[ ] AZD4785 selectively and effectively reduces KRAS mRNA in cells, thereby inhibiting downstream pathways and exerting anti-proliferative effects in KRAS mutant cells.[ ] Beyond siRNAs, miRNAs such as let-7, miR-18a-3p, miR-29b-3p, miR-30b, miR-126-5p, miR-143-3p, and miR-155-5p have been identified as key regulators of KRAS signaling, showing efficacy in preclinical models of KRAS-driven tumors.[ ] Additionally, CRISPR-Cas9 genome editing enables precise correction of cancer-associated mutations. Viral vector-delivered CRISPR systems, including SpCas9 and dCas9-KRAS, have exhibited robust antitumor activity in KRASG12C-mutant NSCLC mouse models, underscoring their therapeutic potential.[ , ]
The research on KRAS inhibitors has entered a new era with the development of small molecule inhibitors that can directly target KRAS mutants, particularly the KRASG12C mutation. This breakthrough has shifted the paradigm from considering KRAS as “undruggable” to a promising target for cancer therapy. The focus has expanded beyond KRASG12C to include other mutations and the development of pan-KRAS inhibitors. The development of inhibitors that can target a broader spectrum of KRAS mutations could potentially increase the number of patients who could benefit from this type of therapy.[ ]

## Drug Resistance
Despite the promising initial clinical outcomes seen with KRAS inhibitors, their efficacy has been constrained by the emerging resistance in expanding patient cohorts [Figure ]. The latest results from the phase III trial (CodeBreak 200 study, No. NCT04303780) indicate that patients treated with Sotorasib for KRASG12C-mutated NSCLC showed early progression (PFS <3 months) in 62 of 172 patients (36%) with a median PFS of <3 months, the ORR was 28.1% and the 12-month PFS was 24.8% (n = 40).[ ] Nevertheless, no notable improvement in OS was observed throughout this trial, indicating a limited duration of effectiveness for these medications among the majority of patients. Rapid acquisition of resistance subsequent to an encouraging initial response remains a common challenge in monotherapy treatments, attributed to factors such as tumor heterogeneity, genetic instability, and plasticity.
 

## Tumor genetic heterogeneity
Tumor genetic diversity presents a theoretical foundation for the resistance observed in the treatment of KRAS inhibitors, potentially stemming from mutational variations within the tumor or the presence of specific co-mutations.[ ] A recent retrospective analysis involving a substantial cohort of NSCLC patients revealed that 8% of those with KRASG12C mutations simultaneously harbored mutations at other KRAS sites. The most common concurrent mutations were KRASG12F and KRASG12V.[ ] Zhao et al[ ] reported that 27 of 43 (63%) patients with Sotorasib treatment were observed to undergo genetic alterations attributable to the therapy, which included a spectrum of genetic amplifications, mutations, and deletions, notably involving KRAS. Distinct secondary KRAS mutations demonstrated variable responses to different drugs; for example, mutation sites such as G13D, R68M, A59S, and A59T were associated with resistance to Sotorasib but sensitivity to Adagrasib, whereas the Q99L mutation displayed the opposite pattern.[ ] Awad et al[ ] established BA/F3 cell lines with dual mutations such as G12C/R68S, G12C/H95D, G12C/H95Q, G12C/H95R, and G12C/Y96C alleles, which exhibited marked resistance in these cell lines to Adagrasib.
Furthermore, as KRASG12C covalent inhibitors render KRASG12C inactive by trapping it in a GDP-bound state, there is a possibility of secondary mutations occurring in KRASG12C, resulting in a loss of GTPase activity or reduced affinity for GDP. Preclinical findings indicate that upon administration of KRASG12C inhibitors, a subset of cancer cells generate novel KRASG12C mutated proteins, maintaining themselves in an active GTP-bound and elusive drug state through activation of EGFR or Aurora kinase A (AURKA).[ ] Furthermore, the activation of RTKs promotes resistance by upregulating RASwt subtypes that are insensitive to KRASG12C inhibitors.[ ] Recently, a novel KRASY96D mutation has been identified, impacting the Switch II pocket and diminishing the hydrogen bond between the Y96 residue and Adagrasib. Consequently, KRASY96D confers resistance to KRASG12C inhibitors in patient-derived xenografts (PDX) models.[ ]
Mutations in other concurrent genes may contribute to resistance against KRAS inhibition. Recently, functional mutations and deletions in TP53 (41%), STK11 (28%), KEAP1/NFE2L2 (27%), and Kelch-like ECH-associated protein 1 (KEAP1) (24%) genes were found to be significantly enriched in patients with KRASG12C mutant NSCLC undergoing Sotorasib and Adagrasib treatments.[ ] Co-mutations in KEAP1, SWI/SNF related BAF chromatin remodeling complex subunit ATPase 4 (SMARCA4), cyclin-dependent kinase inhibitor 2A (CDKN2A), and other cell cycle regulatory factors have been recognized as independent predictors of deteriorated PFS and OS.[ , ] Recently, Dilly et al[ ] reported that acquired resistance to Adagrasib or Sotorasib in patients with KRASG12C-mutant PDAC was triggered by mutation or amplification of PIK3, CDK6, MYC, MET, and EGFR. While MRTX1133 resistance is linked to the expression of the genes YAP1, MYC, and CDK6 as well as the deletion of tumor suppressor genes such as TP53, PTEN, NF1, and RB1.[ ]

## Restoration of vertical signaling pathway
Some tumor cells may manifest lower dependency on KRAS signaling for their growth and survival due to the reactivation of downstream pathways. In CRISPR-Cas9-mediated KRAS knockout PDAC cells, PI3K-driven MAPK activity has been shown to promote tumorigenesis independently of KRAS deficiency, with similar findings observed in lung cancer cell lines.[ ]
When activated by RTK signals, KRAS triggers multiple proliferative signaling cascades, including the MAPK and PI3K pathways, resulting in cell growth, division, and differentiation. Stimulation of cells treated with ARS1620 by EGF results in the reactivation of KRAS within the cells, strongly suggesting EGFR-mediated adaptive resistance to KRASG12C inhibitors.[ ] In an in vivo study, KRASG12C mutant CRC models exhibited higher baseline RTK activation compared to NSCLC cell lines, especially in EGFR signaling. Therefore, rebound phosphorylation of ERK induced by KRASG12C inhibition is more pronounced in CRC than in NSCLC patients, leading to a generally diminished response to KRASG12C inhibitors.[ ] Ryan et al[ ] reinforced this perspective and hypothesized that the resistance mechanism entails the rapid activation of PTK-mediated adaptive KRAS feedback pathways by RASwt. Notably, KRASG12C inhibitors lack the specificity to selectively inhibit these pathways, as evidenced by their response following in vitro treatment with ARS1620 and Sotorasib.
Moreover, in the absence of RAS, MET amplification can elevate AKT activation, inducing resistance to KRAS inhibitors in NSCLC patients. Thus, simultaneous inhibition of MET and KRASG12C can counteract this resistance.[ ] In addition to the aforementioned molecular pathways, negative regulatory factors should also be considered. RNA sequencing demonstrates a notable suppression of negative regulators (dual specificity phosphatase, sprouty RTK signaling antagonist, pleckstrin homology like domain family A gene families) of the MAPK pathway upon KRASG12C inhibition.[ ] The oncogenic KRAS is implicated in the NF1/RSK1 feedback loop suppressing RASwt signaling. Consequently, inhibiting oncogenic KRAS disrupts this negative feedback pathway, activating RASwt and triggering adaptive resistance.[ ] Another study found the activation of HRASwt and NRAS through various RTK feedback as an alternative mechanism for adaptive resistance of CRC cells to KRASG12C inhibitors.[ , , ]

## Histological transformation
Histological transformation refers to the transformation of cancer cells into types with more aggressive tumor behavior and resistance to therapy.[ ] EMT, adeno-to-squamous transition,[ ] focal adhesion kinase (FAK)-YAP signaling, and mucinous histological characteristics may represent additional potential mechanisms leading to resistance to KRAS inhibitors.[ ] During the EMT process, there is a downregulation of epithelial genes and an upregulation of mesenchymal genes, fostering heightened cellular mobility and invasiveness.[ ] The induction of EMT seems to attenuate KRAS dependency, imparting resistance to tumors. In EMT-induced cells, Adachi et al[ ] demonstrated the sustained activity of PI3K in the presence of KRASG12C inhibitors was primarily regulated by the IGFR-IRS1 pathway. Supporting this perspective, it has been proposed that epithelial cells compensate through the ERBB2/3 signaling pathway, whereas mesenchymal cells display an elevated baseline and feedback activation of FGFR subsequent to the inhibition of ERK and AKT signaling pathways with ARS-1620.[ ]
Under therapeutic pressure, tumor cells undergo transformation into different histological subtypes, resulting in the loss of dependence on the original oncogenic drivers as a mechanism for tumors to elude targeted dependencies. Squamous differentiation has been described as the mechanism of acquired resistance to EGFR TKI treatment in lung adenocarcinoma. Awad et al[ ] observed a noteworthy phenomenon during Adagrasib treatment, whereby tumor cells underwent a transition from adenocarcinoma to squamous cell carcinoma in 2 of 10 patients (comprising 9 NSCLC cases and 1 CRC case), and no other identifiable genomic-level resistance mechanisms were detected. Given the infrequency of KRAS mutations in squamous cell carcinoma, it is plausible to speculate that this histological transformation serves as an alternative strategy to evade KRAS inhibitor blockade.[ ] The optimal treatment approach for patients undergoing squamous cell transformation has yet to be determined. The integration of single-cell sequencing technologies will offer valuable insights into tackling this challenge by delineating the histological subtypes of tumor cells based on their molecular characteristics.

## Immunological mechanisms
Alterations in the tumor immune microenvironment can contribute to the development of drug resistance. Specifically, modifications induced by the histone deacetylase 5 (HDAC5) may facilitate a bypass mechanism that recruits macrophages to the tumor microenvironment (TME), resulting in tumor recurrence even after the eradication of the KRASG12D mutation, and the combined use of inhibitors targeting the HDAC5-SMAD4 pathway along with KRASG12D inhibitors demonstrates a synergistic anti-tumor effect in PDAC model.[ ] HDAC5, through inhibiting the suppressor of cytokine signaling (SOCS3), fosters the upregulation of chemokine ligand 2 (CCL2), thereby recruiting CCR2+ macrophages.[ ] These tumor-associated macrophages (TAM) supply transforming growth factor β (TGF-β) and promote cancer cell growth in a KRAS-independent mechanism after targeted inhibition of KRAS mutation. KRAS can induce immunosuppressive TME by inhibiting T cells, expanding immunosuppressive bone marrow-derived suppressor cells, and directly repressing macrophage phagocytosis.[ ] It additionally stimulates the granulocyte-macrophage colony-stimulating factor (GM-CSF) by promoting the generation of the checkpoint protein CD47 in tumor cells.[ , ] Studies indicate that tumors resistant to Sotorasib significantly reduce the population of adaptive immune cells, leading to an “immunologically cold” TME with dynamic and diverse remodeling patterns, including changes in angiogenesis, coagulation pathways, and fatty acid metabolism.[ ] Moreover, the “immune cold” TME prevents the immune system activation necessary for proper ICIs activity, which leads to a poor response to ICIs.[ ] Canon et al[ ] proposed that Sotorasib treatment leads to increased T cell activation and antigen recognition, promoting the establishment of a long-term T cell response. Combining Sotorasib with anti-programmed death receptor-1 (PD-1) or anti-programmed death ligand-1 (PD-L1) therapy enhances T cell infiltration and achieves a longer-lasting therapeutic effect.[ , ]

## Other mechanisms
CDKN2A serves as a pivotal cell cycle regulator and tumor suppressor, orchestrating the phosphorylation of RB in conjunction with CDK4/6 and influencing cellular proliferation.[ ] Hallin et al[ ] pointed out that genetic alterations in cell cycle regulators lead to cell cycle dysregulation and changes in the allelic frequency of KRAS mutations, thereby determining additional factors that could potentially weaken the therapeutic response to Adagrasib. In xenograft models treated with Adagrasib, the inhibition of cell cycle genes further enhances the suppression of tumor growth. Indeed, in a KRASG12V mouse model, CDK4 inactivation not only diminishes tumor development but also triggers senescence.[ ]
Additionally, dysregulation of FAK-YAP signaling and fibrosis formation can contribute to a compromised therapeutic response.[ ] A potential function of FAK exhibits a potential role in regulating the Hippo signaling pathway through YAP1, emerging as a plausible co-target for KRASG12C inhibition.[ ] Notably, the loss of ubiquitin-specific protease 21 (USP21) has been identified as a factor leading to resistance against KRAS-targeted therapy in PDAC.[ ]
In summary, the identification of different resistance mechanisms presents numerous potential therapeutic targets and pathways for future research, some of which remain unexplored in clinical trials. For instance, the transcriptional enhancer associated domain (TEAD) inhibitor VT3989 has entered clinical trials (No. NCT04665206), offering the prospect of innovative treatment approaches for patients.[ ]
In general, resistance to KRAS inhibitors is multifaceted, which can limit the clinical efficacy of these drugs. Understanding the genetic and molecular underpinnings of resistance in individual tumors could pave the way for personalized medicine strategies, where treatment is tailored to the specific resistance mechanisms present in the patient tumor tissue. This could involve the application of precision medicine approaches, such as genomic sequencing, to identify and target specific resistance mechanisms active in each patient.

## Strategies to Combat KRAS Resistance
Given the widespread emergence of resistance, there is a pressing need to overcome existing resistance, improve therapeutic efficacy, and improve outcomes for patients with various KRAS mutations [Figure ].
 

## Combination therapy
Combination therapy stands out as a prioritized strategy to enhance efficacy, mitigate adverse effects, and defer the onset of acquired resistance. Both preclinical and preliminary clinical data indicate that combination treatments can augment the anti-tumor effects of KRAS inhibitors. For instance, the combination therapy of Sotorasib with standard treatments such as Cisplatin, Pemetrexed, and Docetaxel, is currently undergoing phase II clinical trials (No. NCT04185883). Importantly, combination therapy enables the use of relatively lower doses of each drug, thus minimizing potential clinical toxicities.

## Combining targeting of signaling molecules
The reactivation of KRAS and its downstream effectors induced by RTK represents a potential resistance mechanism. Activated upstream EGFR and other ERBB family members may accumulate as a consequence of KRASG12C inhibitors.[ ] Disrupting nucleotide exchange mediated by RTK can retain KRAS in a drug-sensitive KRAS-GDP state,[ ] thereby enhancing therapeutic effectiveness.[ ] Research suggests that RTK inhibitors can directly facilitate the binding capability of the KRASG12C inhibitor.[ ] The synergistic effects observed with several RTK inhibitors, including targeting EGFR, and HER2, in combination with ARS-1620, underscore robust anti-tumor activity.[ ] The combined targeting of EGFR inhibitor Cetuximab and Sotorasib proves highly effective and is capable of restoring resistance in CRC cells and xenografts derived from patients.[ , ] In the CodeBreaK 101 trial (No. NCT04185883), Sotorasib is undergoing evaluation in combination with the EGFR/HER2 TKI Afatinib or the anti-EGFR monoclonal antibody Panitumumab (with or without Folinic acid + Fluorouracil + Irinotecan (FOLFIRI) chemotherapy).[ , ] Divarasib is also being tested in combination with Cetuximab and the EGFR TKI erlotinib (No. NCT04449874). In a clinical cohort evaluating Adagasib in combination with Cetuximab, the ORR of patients increased to 46%, with a median PFS of 6.9 months.[ , ] In December 2022, the FDA approved Adagasib combined with Cetuximab as a treatment option for CRC patients with KRASG12C mutation based on these studies.
As a converging node downstream of multiple RTKs, SHP2 mediates the adaptive reactivation of KRAS. Studies indicate that the combination of ARS-1620 with SHP-099 (an SHP2 inhibitor) significantly reduces tumor volume in KRASG12C tumor models.[ ] This superiority is primarily attributed to the capacity of the SHP2 inhibitor to impede RAS reactivation mediated by multiple RTKs after KRASG12C inhibition.[ ] CRISPR-Cas9 detection indicates that targeting EGFR, PI3K, or SHP2 together can work in concert with KRASG12D inhibitors and is a therapeutic approach to boost MRTX1133 activity.[ ] Based on these promising preclinical data, several clinical trials are currently assessing the combination of SHP inhibitors with KRAS inhibitors, such as TNO155 with Adagrasib (No. NCT04330664),[ ] Sotorasib (No. NCT04185883), and JDQ443 (No. NCT04699188). Analogous to SHP2 inhibitors, the combination of SOS inhibitors with KRAS inhibitors represents a potential strategy against resistance. The SOS1 inhibitor BAY-293 has demonstrated a synergistic effect when used in conjunction with ARS-853.[ ] The combination of BI3406 with Trametinib proves effective in overcoming acquired resistance caused by secondary Y96D and Y96S mutations in KRASG12C.[ ]
In addition to focusing on upstream molecules, synergistic targeting of downstream molecules can also inhibit tumor resistance. It has been reported that MEK inhibitors can amplify the anticancer effects of Sotorasib. For instance, Crizotinib reinstates sensitivity to Sotorasib by disrupting the MAPK and PI3K signaling pathways.[ ] Similarly, the combination of Adagrasib with drugs targeting mTOR has shown prolonged responses and significant tumor regression in various tumor models.[ ]

## Combination with immunotherapy
KRAS and its co-mutations exert an influence on the tumor immunosuppressive microenvironment.[ ] In a mouse model of KRASG12C mutant tumors treated with Sotorasib, pro-inflammatory changes occurred within the TME, and combined with ICIs resulted in synergistic tumor cell killing.[ ] Briere et al[ ] corroborated that Adagrasib induces major histocompatibility complex class I (MHC I) expression in human tumor xenografts bearing KRASG12C mutations, fostering a pro-inflammatory milieu. Additionally, in a syngeneic mouse model of a subcutaneous transplanted tumor, Adagrasib depleted myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) within tumors while increasing the infiltration of cytotoxic immune cells.[ ] The combination therapy with anti-PD-1 yielded sustained anti-tumor responses and evidence of immune memory responses.[ ] However, KRAS inhibition failed to sensitize non-immunogenic mouse models to immunotherapy.[ ] This preclinical insight may guide the patient selection process for combined clinical trials. Combination therapy with KRAS inhibitors and ICIs is being investigated as frontline therapy in patients with refractory NSCLC (Nos. NCT04613596, NCT04185883, and NCT03600883). In mouse models, SHP2 inhibitors enhanced tumor cell sensitivity to anti-PD-1 treatment, exhibiting features of CD8+ T cell recruitment, reduction of MDSC, and enhancement of anti-tumor immune responses.[ ] Further clinical studies are needed to validate the potential benefit of ICI combination therapy in highly immunosuppressed patients such as those with pancreatic cancer.[ ]

## Combination with cell cycle checkpoint inhibition
Cell cycle inhibition provides another potential synergistic strategy with KRAS inhibitors. The combination of Adagrasib and the CDK4/6 inhibitor Palbociclib has demonstrated antiproliferative effects in Adagrasib-resistant models, attributed to the suppression of RB1/E2F transcription factor signaling.[ ] Ongoing clinical trials investigate the synergistic utilization of diverse CDK4/6 inhibitors (Nos. NCT04165031 and NCT04000529). Further maximizing cell cycle blockade may enhance the therapeutic efficacy of KRASG12C inhibitors and prolong the duration of tolerance.
AURKA also plays a crucial role in the cell cycle and is implicated in malignant tumors harboring KRAS mutations.[ ] The activity of AURKA appears to be a mechanism of intrinsic and acquired resistance to KRAS inhibition, as evidenced by the increased expression of AURKA in Sotorasib-resistant cell lines.[ ] AURKA inhibitors can suppress the reactivation of active KRAS status during KRASG12C inhibitor treatment.[ ] Selective AURKA inhibitors, such as VIC-1911, demonstrate synergistic activity in NSCLC and CRC cell lines with intrinsic resistance to Sotorasib.[ ] The mitotic checkpoint kinase WEE1 is another critical cell cycle regulatory factor and a potential cancer treatment target, that may represent a feasible strategy to overcome resistance to KRAS inhibitors.[ ]

## Other combinatorial approaches
FAK is a non-receptor kinase that plays a role in regulating cell growth, signal transduction, and tumor cell invasion.[ ] Diverse FAK inhibitors, such as IN10018, demonstrate effective anticancer properties and can significantly impede the progression of KRAS-mutant cancers.[ ] The combined treatment of KRAS inhibitors and FAK inhibitors consistently exhibits synergistic benefits and anti-resistance effects across cell line-derived xenograft models (CDX) and PDX models.[ ]
Many studies have indicated that KRAS mutations can lead to tumor-specific metabolic changes, thereby orchestrating oncogenic signaling networks and promoting tumor progression.[ ] Preclinical investigations testing the combined effects of various metabolic pathway inhibitors with KRAS inhibitors have yielded promising results.[ ] Furthermore, large-scale gene screenings have unveiled susceptible genes specific to KRAS-mutant tumors, such as RAF1 and Soc-2 suppressor of clear homolog (SHOC2, encoding CRAF), which emerge as ideal synthetic lethal gene loci.[ ] The clinical benefit of KRAS inhibitors can also be facilitated by combination therapy approaches that target the hippocampal (YAP/TAZ) signaling pathway,[ ] which is also a major contributor to resistance to KRASG12C inhibitor resistance.[ , ]

## Optimizing KRAS inhibitors
Currently, most KRAS inhibitors are designed to target the GDP-bound state.[ ] In contrast to KRAS-OFF inhibitors, KRAS-ON inhibitors can form a ternary complex with the active GTP-bound form of KRAS and intracellular tetracycline A, independently of intrinsic GTPase activity.[ ] This interaction disrupts the binding between mutated KRAS GTP-bound conformers and RAS effector proteins, resulting in signal blockade.[ ] Both RMC-6236 and RMC6291 are KRAS-ON inhibitors and have been shown to effectively inhibit the activation of the RAS pathway in vivo, resulting in sustained tumor regression in CDX and PDX models.[ ] In a KRASG12C NSCLC model, RMC-6291 and Adagrasib exhibited superior response rates and durability compared with Sotorasib.[ ] For patients with advanced solid tumors, several KRAS-ON inhibitors are undergoing phase I clinical trials, including BPO-8520 (No. NCT06244771), RMC-6236 (No. NCT05379985), RMC-9805 (No. NCT06040541), and RMC-6291 (No. NCT05462717).[ ] Additionally, researchers have reported a novel and functionally unique tri-complex KRAS active-state inhibitor, RM018, capable of inhibiting KRASG12C/KRASY96Din vitro.[ ] Importantly, the activity of these inhibitors remains unaffected by RTK activation, and the duration of cell proliferation inhibition is significantly prolonged.[ ] Therefore, such KRAS-GTP inhibitors hold promise in overcoming adaptive resistance that arises after inhibiting aberrant RAS-MAPK pathway activation.
Diverging from the conventional focus on the Switch II pocket, through drug fragment screening, crystallography experiments, and nuclear magnetic resonance methods, researchers have delved into ligand interactions and have unexpectedly discovered a novel binding region induced by Switch I.[ ] Representatively, the small molecule BI-2852 developed by Kessler et al[ ] can induce non-functional dimerization of KRAS, sharing advantageous features with MRTX1133, and simultaneously targeting both the GTP- and GDP-bound states of KRAS. Additionally, at the genetic level, the G-quadruplex (G4) sequence within the KRAS promoter region plays a regulatory role in modulating KRAS transcription. The G4 structure proves high sensitivity to changes in the nuclear microenvironment of tumor cells.[ ] The unique structural configuration of G4 may open up diverse opportunities for targeting KRAS.[ ]

## Proteoly­sis-targeting chimera (PROTAC) technology
A noteworthy category of innovative drugs is PROTACs,[ , ] which facilitate the proteasomal degradation of disease-related proteins by orchestrating the binding of the target protein and E3 ubiquitin ligase.[ ] Bond et al[ ] reported the first degrader, LC-2, capable of degrading endogenous KRASG12C. In cancer cell lines with KRAS mutation, LC-2 covalently binds to KRAS, recruits E3 ligase Von hippel-lindau tumor suppressor(VHL), rapidly induces the degradation of KRAS, and suppresses the MAPK signaling. Similar designs are being applied to the development of Pan-KRAS and other mutation-specific KRAS degraders currently in preclinical development.[ ] In principle, PROTACs harbor the potential for quicker and more sustained pathway blockade. Furthermore, the incorporation of tissue selectivity into PROTAC design is achievable by selecting an appropriate E3 ligase. Notably, KRAS PROTACs with cellular potency and selectivity, such as BI-KRASdegrader1, exhibit the ability to degrade all major circulating KRAS mutants while preserving NRAS and HRAS.[ ] Successful targeting with degraders relies on the development of high-affinity ligands for binding KRAS. Phase I clinical trials for ASP3082 (Astellas), a PROTAC targeting KRASG12D, are currently proceeding in patients with advanced solid tumors (No. NCT05382559). Additionally, the effective delivery of PROTAC degraders containing protein ligands to target tumor cells represents a critical step for their future clinical application.[ , ] The spatial barrier formed by molecular gels may facilitate the binding of target proteins to downstream molecules.[ ]
An alternative approach for KRAS targeting involves the use of chimeric toxins, exemplified by RAS/RAP1-specific endopeptidase (RRSP-DTB), which comprises the RRSP from vibrio vulnificus and the protein delivery machinery of diphtheria toxin (DTB) from diphtheria toxin.[ ] Vidimar et al[ ] demonstrated that this drug inhibits ERK signaling by inactivating RAS in a KRAS-mutant PDAC model, resulting in ≥95% tumor regression without evidence of resistance.

## Immunotherapy targeting KRAS
The unique expression of new antigens by tumor cells makes immunotherapy based on neoantigens a promising tumor-specific treatment approach, with minimal off-target effects.[ ] In 2016, Tran et al[ ] pioneered the description of an endogenous immune response against KRAS-mutant cancer cells. Their work identified T cell receptors (TCRs) recognizing CD8+ tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs) that target novel epitopes derived from KRASG12D. Notably, in a CRC patient harboring a KRASG12D mutation, combination therapy involving these specific TILs resulted in the regression of all seven lung metastases isolated from the patient. Subsequently, the identification of other MHC I-restricted mutant KRAS epitopes has presented opportunities for adoptive cell therapy (ACT) in the treatment of KRAS mutant tumors. Genetically engineered CD8+ T cells equipped with specific TCRs have demonstrated efficacy in xenograft models of metastatic KRASG12V-mutated NSCLC.[ ] TCR-T expressing two KRASG12D-targeted treatments reduced lung metastases in a patient with pancreatic cancer by 62% and 72%, respectively, after 1 and 6 months.[ ] ACT with TCR-engineered T cells targeting KRASG12V mutant cells has progressed to clinical trial stages, which underscores the evolving landscape of precision immunotherapy for KRAS-driven cancers.[ ]
Therapeutic cancer vaccines represent another immune-based strategy for addressing KRAS-mutant tumors. mRNA-5671/V941, a liquid-nanoparticle-formulated mRNA neoantigen vaccine designed to target KRASG12D, KRASG12V, KRASG13D, and KRASG12C variants, has been developed with a robust mechanistic foundation.[ ] In phase II clinical trials, the nucleic acid-based KRAS vaccine GI-4000 exhibited an impressive antigen-specific immune response along with acceptable tolerance.[ ] A dendritic cell vaccine focusing on novel epitopes of pan-KRAS mutation is currently undergoing phase I trials (No. NCT03592888). ELI-002 2P, an innovative vaccine composed of lipid amphiphile (Amph)-modified KRASG12D and KRASG12R mutant KRAS peptides along with the immunostimulatory Amph-modified CpG oligonucleotide adjuvant (Amph-CpG-7909), has been shown to enhance mutant KRAS-specific cytotoxic T cell activity.[ ] Additionally, in CRC, the hydra actinoporin-like toxin (HALT) immunotoxin has been developed for treating CRC patients with KRASG12V mutations, exhibiting cytotoxic effects in KRAS-mutant CRC cells.[ ] Meanwhile, ongoing efforts are being devoted to tumor vaccines targeting KRAS mutations and adoptive cell therapy with antibodies against KRAS-MHC I peptide complexes.[ ] Recently, a study discovered that ARS-1620-mediated covalent modification of KRAS-G12C generates novel antigens delivered to cancer cells by MHC I. Antibodies recognizing these new epitopes have been developed, demonstrating immunotherapeutic effects in KRAS mutation tumor cell lines.[ ] Concurrently, antibodies directed at the KRAS-MHC I complex have been developed, selectively eradicating KRASG12C lung cancer cells displaying intrinsic resistance to Sotorasib treatment.[ ] Statin drugs can stimulate CD8+ T cell immune responses against KRAS-mutant tumors, and their combination with oxaliplatin can enhance sensitivity to anti-PD-1 therapy.[ ] It is noteworthy that, in all these immunotherapeutic approaches, overcoming the immunosuppressive TME induced by mutant KRAS is indispensable for achieving their efficacy.

## Precision treatment facilitated by artificial intelligence (AI)
AI offers an opportunity to bridge the gap between KRAS resistance mechanisms and innovative treatment strategies. For instance, AI leverages the core structures (molecular scaffolds) of known inhibitors for structural diversification, leading to the generation of distinct and more potent inhibitors.[ ] Some algorithms have been transformed into online tools, significantly enhancing their accessibility, such as “AIScaffold”.[ ] AI-based scaffold hopping tools can generate a large number of molecular structures, filter them based on scores, and return the optimal results to users within minutes.[ ] Therefore, AI technology holds the potential to expedite the discovery of novel KRAS inhibitors and identify promising therapeutic molecules tailored to current resistance mutations. For instance, a study utilizes AI to identify potential inhibitors for the KRASG12C protein in NSCLC.[ ] It predicts FDA-approved drugs such as Afatinib, Neratinib, and Zanubrutinib as promising candidates for KRASG12C inhibitors.[ ] Furthermore, AI holds the potential to forecast tumor resistance pathways using advanced deep learning algorithms, which can be augmented with datasets from metabolomics and imaging omics such as magnetic resonance imaging (MRI).[ , ] AI-induced emission is not a static model system; it is dynamic and requires integration with ever-evolving datasets. Presently, ongoing developments in deep learning are aimed at integrating clinical data to make personalized medicine a reality and to refine the decision-making process.[ ]

## Conclusions
Due to the high prevalence of KRAS mutations and their pivotal role in initiating and sustaining tumor growth, targeting KRAS represents an appealing therapeutic strategy. The advent of KRASG12C inhibitors signifies a milestone in this field, yet we are still in the early stages of KRAS drug therapy. The primary challenge lies in gaining a more nuanced understanding of allele-specific effects on tumor biology, precisely matching patients with the most appropriate treatments, and accurately predicting individualized treatment responses. Novel platforms utilizing AI and machine learning strategies for large-scale virtual screening are revolutionizing the drug discovery workflow. Additionally, a growing number of clinical trials contribute to further elucidating resistance mechanisms and rational anti-resistance strategies.
As highlighted in this review, future research efforts should prioritize the development of next-generation inhibitors capable of targeting novel KRAS mutations and pan-KRAS isoforms, offering broader applicability across diverse patient populations. Leveraging cutting-edge technologies such as proteomics and single-cell sequencing will be critical for elucidating tumor heterogeneity and the mechanisms underlying adaptive resistance, thereby informing the design of more effective treatments. Rational drug combinations hold a promising strategy for enhancing therapeutic efficacy and durability, particularly through synergistic interactions with immunotherapies and metabolic modulators. Additionally, investigating how KRAS affects the TME may open new possibilities for overcoming resistance and enhancing patient outcomes.
Emerging areas including targeting the microbiome and KRAS-driven tumorigenesis to achieve therapeutic responses, as well as the potential of epigenetic regulatory strategies to modulate chromatin accessibility and gene expression, are also worth exploring. These innovative objectives underscore the complexity and multifaceted nature of KRAS-driven cancers and highlight the need for interdisciplinary collaboration in addressing these challenges. By integrating these strategies, researchers and clinicians can work towards a more personalized and effective treatment strategy, ultimately improving outcomes for patients with KRAS-mutant cancers. We hope this review serves as a valuable resource and inspiration for ongoing and future research to conquer key issues of oncology therapy.
