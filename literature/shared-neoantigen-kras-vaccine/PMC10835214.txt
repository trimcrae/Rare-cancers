## Abstract
T-cell receptor (TCR)–modified T-cell therapies have shown promise against solid tumors, but overall therapeutic benefits have been modest due in part to suboptimal T-cell persistence and activation in vivo, alongside potential tumor antigen escape. In this study, we demonstrate an approach to enhance the in vivo persistence and function of TCR T cells through combination with Amphiphile (AMP) vaccination including cognate TCR T peptides. AMP modification improves lymph node targeting of conjugated tumor immunogens and adjuvants, thereby coordinating a robust T cell–activating endogenous immune response. AMP vaccine combination with TCR T-cell therapy led to complete eradication and durable responses against established murine solid tumors refractory to TCR T-cell monotherapy. Enhanced antitumor efficacy was correlated with simultaneous in vivo invigoration of adoptively transferred TCR T cells and in situ expansion of the endogenous antitumor T-cell repertoire. Long-term protection against tumor recurrence in AMP-vaccinated mice was associated with antigen spreading to additional tumor-associated antigens not targeted by vaccination. AMP vaccination further correlated with pro-inflammatory lymph node transcriptional reprogramming and increased antigen presenting–cell maturation, resulting in TCR T-cell expansion and functional enhancement in lymph nodes and solid tumor parenchyma without lymphodepletion. In vitro evaluation of AMP peptides with matched human TCR T cells targeting NY-ESO-1, mutant KRAS, and HPV16 E7 illustrated the clinical potential of AMP vaccination to enhance human TCR T-cell proliferation, activation, and antitumor activity. Taken together, these studies provide rationale and evidence to support clinical evaluation of combining AMP vaccination with TCR T-cell therapies to augment antitumor activity.

## Introduction
Recent advancement of engineered T cells genetically modified to express tumor-specific T-cell receptors (TCR) has demonstrated promise for treatment of a variety of cancers ( ). In contrast to chimeric-antigen receptor (CAR) T cells, TCR-modified T cells (TCR T cells) have been developed to target a variety of intracellular and extracellular tumor antigens suggesting the potential for wide-ranging therapeutic benefit resulting from tumor recognition with attractive fidelity and sensitivity ( ). However, although recent clinical trial results have provided important proof-of-concept, the overall benefit to patients has been modest, particularly against solid tumors, where low response rates and poor durability remain challenges. Cell-intrinsic strategies to improve efficacy have included TCR affinity enhancement, which may improve activity but can lead to adverse safety effects ( ). More broadly, treatment failures may be attributed to tumor-intrinsic factors, including loss of antigen expression, or suboptimal T-cell activity, including deficiency of in vivo activation, poor initial engraftment, limited persistence, and weaknesses in T-cell functionality. Therefore, strategies to promote the delivery of comprehensive stimulatory signals to TCR T cells in vivo may be critical to enabling further clinical success ( ).
Numerous methods have been reported for enhancing T-cell function in vivo, including combination of adoptive T-cell transfer with pro-inflammatory molecules ( ). Although these strategies have demonstrated potential to improve efficacy, they rarely supplement the multifactored biology underlying effective T-cell activation and antitumor function. Alternatively, concerted promotion of these mechanisms in vivo, including stimulation of the TCR signaling complex, delivery of costimulation, and cytokine support, can readily be achieved through vaccination, which potently engages the innate immune response to simultaneously deliver the signals needed to activate and support robust T-cell activation. Administration of TCR-specific epitope-based vaccines is highly attractive for this purpose, offering simple, TCR-informed design of cognate peptide immunogens. However, as a result of their small molecular size, conventional peptides are poorly immunogenic due to ineffective accumulation and retention in lymph nodes where adaptive immunity is orchestrated by resident antigen-presenting cells (APC; refs. ). Previous studies have demonstrated that Amphiphile (AMP)-conjugated vaccines promote increased stability and specific trafficking and retention of vaccine payloads into lymph nodes ( ). With this strategy, both peptide antigens and molecular adjuvants can be coupled to lipophilic, albumin-binding domains that noncovalently associate with endogenous tissue albumin at the injection site after administration (Supplementary Fig. S1). These AMP–albumin complexes restrict entry of vaccine components to the systemic blood circulation, preferentially retargeting albumin-bound agents to the lymph nodes. Subsequent uptake by resident APCs can initiate concerted presentation of cognate peptides and costimulatory ligands with cytokine secretion to potently activate T cells in vivo ( ).
Given the efficacy of AMP vaccines to potently expand endogenous effector T-cell responses, we theorized that a similar approach could enhance TCR T-cell therapy against established solid tumors. Using this approach, conventional collection, activation, genetic programming, and re-infusion of tumor-specific TCR T cells can be combined with AMP vaccination including cognate peptide and adjuvant (Supplementary Fig. S1). Following administration, TCR T cells can colocalize in the lymph node with a population of highly costimulatory, cognate peptide–presenting APCs. Through AMP vaccination, delivery of a TCR stimulus with endogenous costimulation and cytokine support can effectively promote TCR T-cell activation, expansion, and reinforcement of antitumor effector function. This simultaneously allows establishment of a supportive lymphatic niche for potential induction of antigen spreading to diversify immune recognition of tumor-specific targets to promote long-term protection. Herein, we utilize multiple TCR T-cell models to demonstrate the enhancement provided by effective lymph node–targeted AMP vaccination, including effects on APCs, to support TCR T-cell activation and expansion, enhanced tumor infiltration, and eradication of established solid tumors. Subsequently, we demonstrate the potential for AMP peptides to enhance the function of human TCR T cells specific for clinically relevant tumor targets indicating the potential for this strategy to improve the therapeutic activity of TCR T cells upon clinical translation.

## Materials and Methods

## Vaccine components
Immunogenic peptides containing cognate minimal TCR epitopes were designed with ectopic terminal cysteines where needed to facilitate chemical modification. Peptides were prepared by solid-phase synthesis (PreludeX automated peptide synthesizer) on a 0.25 mmol scale using preloaded Wang polystyrene resins. Oxyma/DIC chemistry was employed for couplings. 1.2 mmol of Fmoc-amino acid building block and coupling reagents were used for each coupling with each residue double coupled as standard. Capping was carried out using 0.5M acetic anhydride in DMF and Fmoc deprotection was carried out with 20% v/v piperidine in DMF. On completion of the synthesis, the resin was washed (DMF/DCM) and dried in vacuo and then subjected to cleavage and deprotection with a TFA/TIS/H2O/EDT/DMS (87.5/2.5/5/2.5/2.5) cocktail for 4 hours at room temperature. The resin was removed by filtration and the crude peptide isolated by precipitation with diethyl ether. The crude peptide was purified by preparative RP-HPLC using a Luna C18 ( ) 10μ 100Å column (250 × 21.2 mm) and an acetonitrile/water gradient with 0.1% TFA as the modifier. Fractions containing the desired product at sufficient purity were pooled and lyophilized to afford the final material. Purified peptides were characterized by HPLC analysis for purity and MS for identity before conjugation as previously reported ( ) with DSPE-PEG-Maleimide or N-methylmaleimide to generate the desired AMP peptides and N-methyl-succinimide (NMS) capped soluble analogs, respectively. The resulting AMP peptides and soluble NMS-capped peptides were analyzed by HPLC and MS to confirm purity (>95%) and identity. The amino-acid sequences of the peptides were as follows:AMP-gp100 C(AMP)AVGALEGPRNQDWLGVPRQL;

Soluble CpG 1826 (SOL-CpG) was obtained from InvivoGen (cat. no. TLRL1826) and AMP-CpG 1826 (AMP-CpG) was synthesized as reported previously ( ).
gp100 peptide vaccines consisted of 10 μg of gp100 peptide in its nonconjugated, soluble (SOL-gp100) or AMP-conjugated (AMP-gp100) form combined with 1 nmol SOL-CpG or AMP-CpG, respectively. Mock treatment groups were treated with PBS alone.
mKRAS peptide vaccines consisted of 20 μg of mKRAS-G12D or -G12V peptides in soluble (SOL-mKRAS-G12D or SOL-mKRAS-G12V) or AMP-conjugated (AMP-mKRAS-G12D or AMP-mKRAS-G12V) form combined with 5 nmol SOL-CpG or AMP-CpG, respectively. Mock treatment groups were treated with PBS alone.

## Animals
Female, 6- to 8-week-old C57BL/6, B6.Cg-Thy1a/CyTg(TcraTcrb)8Rest/J (Pmel-1), and NOD.Cg-Prkdcscid Il2rgtm1Wjl/SzJ (NSG) mice were purchased from The Jackson Laboratory. Female, 6- to 8-week-old CB6F1-Tg (HLA-A*1101/H2-Kb) A11.01 (HLA A*11:01) were purchased from Taconic. All animal studies were carried out with approval from the Charles River Accelerator and Development Lab–approved Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) protocol and conducted following federal, state, and local guidelines for the care and use of animals.
Mice challenged with tumor (see In Vivo Antitumor Therapy by AMP Vaccination of TCR T Cells) were measured with calipers to confirm equal tumor load and randomized to different treatment groups 1 day before treatment. Mice receiving vaccinations were injected with the indicated concentrations of SOL-gp100, AMP-gp100, SOL-mKRAS-G12V, AMP-mKRAS-G12V, SOL-mKRAS-G12D, or AMP-mKRAS-G12D in combination with SOL-CpG or AMP-CpG admixed with PBS. Injections (100 μL) were administered subcutaneously at the base of the tail (50 μL bilaterally) on indicated days. Whole blood samples were obtained utilizing retro-orbital collection method on indicated days. Inguinal lymph nodes, spleens, and tumors were collected at indicated endpoints for downstream analysis.

## Retroviral constructs, cell isolation, and retroviral transduction
Murine gp100-specific TCR T cells expressing mCherry were generated by transducing pmel-1 T cells with plasmids encoding the mCherry construct in the MSGV-retroviral vector. Retroviral vectors were ordered from GenScript and used to transfect Phoenix Eco cells using the Lipofectamine 3000 Transfection Reagent and protocol (Thermo Fisher Scientific, cat. no. L3000075). Pmel-1 or HLA A*11:01 mice were euthanized, and their spleens were harvested. Following mechanical tissue dissociation and red blood cell lysis with ACK Lysis buffer (Quality Biological, cat. no. 118–156–101CS), T cells were isolated using EasySep protocol (STEMCELL, cat. no. 19851) and activated with Dynabeads (Gibco, cat. no. 11453D). Cells were expanded in vitro by culturing in T-cell media (RPMI1640 supplemented with 10% heat-inactivated FBS, nonessential amino acids, 1 mmol/L sodium pyruvate, 25 mmol/L HEPES, 2 mmol/L l-glutamine, 1% penicillin/streptomycin, 11 mmol/L glucose, 2 μmol/L 2-mercaptoethanol, and 50 IU/mL of recombinant human IL2 for human T-cell applications and 100 IU/mL for murine T-cell applications; Peprotech, cat. nos. 200–02 and 212–12). 24 and 48 hours after initial expansion, mouse T cells were spinoculated on RetroNectin-coated plates with viral supernatant collected from Phoenix-ECO ( ).
To generate the HPV16 E7-, NY-ESO-1-, and MART-1-specific human TCR T cells, plasmids including mCherry and/or the specified TCR alpha and beta chains, as described in Jin and colleagues (HPV16 E7; ref. ) and Drakes and colleagues (NY-ESO-1, 1G4, and MART-1, DMF5; ref. ), were ordered from GenScript in the MSGV-retroviral vector ( ). Plasmids encoding the mKRAS-G12D (701, 4095) and mKRAS-G12V (700) targeted TCRs were kindly provided by Dr. James Yang at the NCI, Surgery Branch. Phoenix AMPHO cells were transfected with MSGV retroviral vectors using the Lipofectamine 3000 Transfection Reagent and protocol (Thermo Fisher Scientific, cat. no. L3000075). Peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMC) were isolated from leukopaks (STEMEXPRESS) from either HLA A*11:01, HLA A*02:01, or HLA C*08:02 donors. Human T cells were isolated using EasySep Human Kit (STEMCELL, cat. no. 17951), and cultured in RPMI1640 supplemented with 10% heat-inactivated FBS, 2 mmol/L l-glutamine, and 1% penicillin/streptomycin for 1 day prior to transduction and supplemented with 50 IU/mL of IL2. Human T cells were activated with human CD3/CD28 Dynabeads (Thermo Fisher Scientific, cat. no. 11132D), at a bead:cell ratio of 2:1 and transduced using supernatant collected from Phoenix-AMPHO cells. 24 and 48 hours after isolation, murine and human T cells were transduced by centrifugation on RetroNectin-coated plates. Autologous human dendritic cells (DC) were isolated from PBMCs and matured in parallel with the human TCR-T cell transduction using a Monocyte EasySep Kit and then matured with differentiation and maturation kits (STEMCELL, cat. nos. 19359, 10988, 10989) prior to antigen presentation to human TCR T cells.

## 
In vitro AMP activation of TCR T cells
For experiments assessing in vitro activation of human TCR T cells with the AMP vaccine, mature autologous human DCs (prepared as in Retroviral Constructs, Cell Isolation, and Retroviral Transduction) were pulsed with PBS or 10 μmol/L of SOL or AMP peptides (mKRAS, HPV16 E7, or NY-ESO-1) for 24 hours. For peptide stability and mock in vivo studies, SOL or AMP peptides were incubated at 37°C for 24 hours in RPMI1640 containing 10% human serum prior to DC pulsing. DCs were washed three times and cultured at a 1:2 DC:TCR T-cell ratio with mKRAS-, HPV16 E7-, or NY-ESO-1-specific human TCR T cells that had been previously de-beaded and rested for 5 days to return to a baseline activation status. On days 1 and 7 after culture, supernatant fluid was collected from the cultures for Luminex cytokine analysis (see Luminex cytokine production analysis) and cells were utilized for killing assays against target cells (see Cytotoxicity Assay). In addition, cells were analyzed for activation and phenotype by flow cytometry on days 1, 4, and 7 after coculture (see Flow Cytometry). ## 
In vivo antitumor therapy by AMP vaccination of TCR T cells
C57BL/6 mice were implanted subcutaneously with 5 × 105 B16F10 GFP/LUC cells 10 days prior to adoptive transfer of 5 × 106 mCherry transduced gp100-specific TCR T cells. Mice were then vaccinated subcutaneously at the tail base with the indicated concentrations of either PBS (n = 10), SOL-gp100/SOL-CpG (n = 20), or AMP-gp100/AMP-CpG (n = 24) on days −1, 3, 7, 10, 14. Mice were followed for survival, and tumors were measured three times per week. To track and assess functionality of circulating TCR T cells, blood was collected on days 5 and 19 for flow cytometry and intracellular cytokine staining (ICS) analysis. For rechallenge experiments, mice were rechallenged with 5 × 105 B16F10 GFP/LUC cells on day 75 after T-cell transfer with or without intraperitoneal injection of 250 μg anti-Thy1.1 (Days −7, 0, 7, 14, 21, 28, 35; BioXCell, cat. no. BE214) to deplete adoptively transferred pmel-1 T cells that are Thy1.1+. Depletion of TCR T cells was confirmed by flow cytometry analysis of processed PBMCs to ensure absence of Thy1.1+ cells. For murine mKRAS AMP vaccination experiments, 3 × 106 mKRAS-G12D or -G12V-specific murine TCR T cells were adoptively transferred by intravenous injection to HLA A*11:01 mice. Mice were vaccinated with the indicated concentrations of either PBS, SOL-mKRAS-G12D/SOL-CpG, AMP-mKRAS-G12D/AMP-CpG, SOL-mKRAS-G12V/SOL-CpG, or AMP-mKRAS-G12V/AMP-CpG and circulating TCR T cells were tracked weekly through flow cytometry analysis of blood samples. End-point harvest analysis was performed by collection and processing of blood, spleen, inguinal lymph nodes, lungs, and tumors (for B16F10-bearing mice) on days 7 or 14 for B16F10 experiments and day 15 for murine HLA A*11:01 mKRAS-G12V and -G12D experiments.
For the human TCR T cell in vivo tumor model, NSG mice were implanted subcutaneously with 1 × 106 Panc-1 cells 10 days prior to the TCR T-cell transfer. Human HLA A*11:01 T cells transduced with a mKRAS-G12D-specific TCR or mCherry control construct were activated in vitro by coculturing at 1:2 ratio with previously AMP peptide pulsed autologous DCs for 1 day. TCR T cells were isolated using a negative T-cell Isolation Kit (STEMCELL, cat. no. 17951) and 1 × 106 TCR T cells were injected intravenously into the tumor-bearing mice. Tumors were collected 35 days after tumor implantation and analyzed for T-cell infiltration, activation, and phenotype.

## Cell isolation for ex vivo analyses
Tissues (spleen, lymph nodes, lungs, tumor) from vaccinated and TCR T-cell-treated mice were excised, mechanically dissociated using a gentleMACS Dissociator (Miltenyi Biotec), then passed through a 70 μm cell strainer. Dissociated cells were centrifuged at 1,500 RPM for 5 minutes at 4°C. The pelleted cells were counted, and an aliquot was resuspended either by cell count (spleen, lymph nodes, and lung) or by mass (tumor) and used for downstream applications.
Peripheral blood was collected from study mice at indicated days post T-cell treatment and used to assess T-cell populations and functionality by flow cytometry. One hundred microliters of peripheral blood was processed in ACK lysis buffer (Quality Biological, cat. no. 118–156–101CS), PBMCs were counted and then resuspended in either 2.5% FBS/PBS prior to antibody staining for flow cytometric analysis of T-cell populations or in RPMI1640 medium with 10% FBS, indicated cell stimulation peptide, and 1× Golgi plug (BD Biosciences, cat. no. 555029) for ICS analysis. The following peptides were used for T-cell stimulation in ICS assays: Trp1 (NTPQFENL, GenScript), Trp2 (SVYDFFVWL, GenScript), or a cocktail of B16F10 tumor-associated antigens (B16TAA) (M30-VDWENVSPELNSTD, Tyrp-1 175–182-NTPQFENL, p15E 604–611-KSPWFTTL, M27-LCPGNKYEM, M47-GRGHLLGRLAAIVG, M48-DLAVIPAGVVHNWD, all synthesized by GenScript). Cells were transferred to a 96-well U-bottom plate and cultured at 37°C for 18 hours. As a positive control, extra PBMCs from mice receiving TCR T cells were combined and cultured with 1× Golgi plug and PMA/ionomycin (Sigma-Aldrich, cat. no. P8139, I0634) for 18 hours. Cells were pelleted, washed once with PBS, and stained with live/dead fixable violet (Thermo Fisher Scientific, cat. no. L34964) for 20 minutes in the dark at 4°C. Cells were pelleted again and surface stained for CD3, CD4, CD8, and Thy1.1 for 20 minutes on ice. Cells were then washed and permeabilized with the BD Fix/Permeabilization Kit, according to manufacturer instructions (BD Biosciences, cat. no. 554714) and stained with IFNγ and TNFα antibodies. Cells were washed and resuspended in flow cytometry buffer for analysis immediately or kept at 4°C for analysis on a CytoFLEX flow cytometer the next day. Antibodies used are listed in Supplementary Table S1. Pmel TCR T cells were defined as lymphocytes/single cells /live/CD3+Thy1.1+ (Supplementary Fig. S2).

## Stimulation of TCR T cells ex vivo with AMP-vaccinated lymph nodes
C57BL/6 mice were injected with mock, SOL-gp100/SOL-CpG, or AMP-gp100/AMP-CpG vaccines; 2 days later, inguinal lymph nodes were collected and mechanically dissociated to generate single-cell suspensions. mCherry-transduced gp100-specific TCR T cells were rested for 3 days prior to culture with lymph node single-cell suspensions from either mock, SOL-gp100, or AMP-gp100 vaccinated mice at a 1:1 ratio supplemented with 50 IU/mL of IL2. On days 1 and 7 after culture, supernatant fluid was collected from the cultures for Luminex cytokine analysis and cocultures containing TCR T cells were utilized for killing assays against target cells. In addition, cells were counted and analyzed for activation and phenotype by flow cytometry on days 1, 4, and 7 after coculture.

## Cytotoxicity assay
Mouse or human TCR T effector cells were plated in T-cell media (RPMI1640 supplemented with 10% heat-inactivated FBS, nonessential amino acids, 1 mmol/L sodium pyruvate, 25 mmol/L HEPES, 2 mmol/L l-glutamine, 1% penicillin/streptomycin, 11 mmol/L glucose, 2 μmol/L 2-mercaptoethanol, and 50 IU/mL of recombinant human IL2 for human T-cell applications and 100 IU/mL for murine T-cell applications) at various effector-to-target ratios as specified (20:1, 10:1, 4:1, 2:1, 1:1, 1:2, 1:4, 1:10, or 1:20) against 5 × 105 antigen-positive target cells expressing GFP/LUC (B16F10, Cos7-mKRAS-G12D/V, Panc-1, CaSki, A375) in 200 μL of total volume in a black-welled 96-well plate. Groups were plated in technical replicates, and additional wells were plated with target only as a background control to determine baseline luminescence. Twenty-four hours after incubation, supernatant was removed and collected for future cytokine analysis. Pelleted cells were incubated with ONE-Glo reagent for 30 minutes at room temperature following the ONE-Glo Luciferase Assay System protocol from Promega (cat. no. E6130). Luminescence was quantified by the Synergy H1 Hybrid reader (BioTek) using the Gen5 Microplate Reader and Imager Software. ## Luminex cytokine production analysis
Cell culture supernatant or serum from mouse gp100-targeting TCR T-cell experiments were collected and stored at −80°C for future cytokine analysis. The mouse Th17 9-plex Milliplex Map Kit (EMD Millipore, cat. no. MTH17MAG-47k) was used to quantify murine TNFα, IFNγ, GM-CSF, IL12p70, IL21, CD40L, IL10, IL2, and IL6 levels. Supernatant from human mKRAS- or HPV16 E7-targeted TCR T–cell, or NY-ESO-1-targeted TCR T–cell experiments were collected and stored at −80°C. The human Th17 8-plex Milliplex Map Kit (EMD Millipore, cat. no. HTH17MAG-14K) was used to quantify secreted human TNFα, IFNγ, GM-CSF, IL12p70, IL21, IL10, IL2, and IL6 levels. Samples were run on a Luminex LX200 machine using xMAP Technology Software.

## Nanostring gene expression analysis
Lymph nodes and tumors from mice were extracted and mechanically dissociated to create a single cell suspension at indicated timepoints. Cells were counted and resuspended by vortex in 75 μL Qiagen Buffer RLT (cat. no. 79216) per 1.5 × 105 cells. Seventy-five microliters of sample was stored in a cryovial and sent to Canopy Biosciences to perform nCounter nanostring RNA analysis utilizing Mouse Immunology panel (561 genes). Data were analyzed using ROSALIND software (ROSALIND, Inc.), using the Standard Differential Expression Analysis module. Results were organized on the basis of gene ontology pathway classification.

## Flow cytometry
Flow cytometric analyses were performed using the 13-color CytoFLEX S (Beckman Coulter) instrument to acquire data. Data were analyzed using FlowJo v10.8 (Becton Dickenson and Company). DAPI (0.5 mg/mL, Thermo Fisher Scientific, cat. no. D1306) or a live/dead fixable violet (Thermo Fisher Scientific, cat. no. L34964) was used to exclude dead cells in all experiments. Antibodies to the murine and human proteins used for flow cytometry are listed in Supplementary Table S1.

## IVIS imaging
C57Bl/6 mice were immunized with 20 μg AMP-mKRAS-G12D or SOL-mKRAS-G12D conjugated with fluorescein carboxylic acid (FITC) or unlabeled AMP-mKRAS-G12D. Vaccines were adjuvanted with 5 nmol AMP-CpG or SOL-CpG, respectively. Twenty-four hours after injection, inguinal and axillary lymph nodes were harvested and imaged ex vivo using the IVIS Spectrum (PerkinElmer).

## Elispot
Spleens were collected from the murine HLA A*11:01 TCR T-cell experiment 14 days after TCR T-cell adoptive transfer. IFNγ ELISPOT was performed using the Mouse IFNγ ELISPOTPLUS Kit (MABTECH product no. 3321–4HPW-10). ELISPOT plates were analyzed using ImmunoSpot CTL plate reader (CTL ImmunoSpot S6 Entry Analyzer) and ImmunoSpot software (ImmunoSpot Pro 7.0.23.3 Professional DC).

## Statistical analysis
All data were plotted, and all statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 8 software. All graphs display mean values, and the error bars represent the SD. No samples or animals were excluded from the analyses. Animals were randomized for the studies, and experiments were blinded to the investigator. Statistical comparisons between groups were conducted using one-way ANOVA, two-way ANOVA, or Mann–Whitney tests as indicated. Data were considered statistically significant if the P value was less than 0.05.

## Results

## AMP vaccination enhances TCR T-cell therapy and eradicates established solid tumors
AMP-modification of peptide antigens and molecular adjuvants is known to promote enhanced lymphatic delivery and retention through albumin “hitchhiking,” leading to potent expansion of cognate endogenous T-cell responses ( ). Therefore, we designed a lymph node–targeted vaccination regimen for adoptively transferred cognate TCR T-cell therapy to enhance therapeutic efficacy against established solid tumors. Wild-type (WT) C57BL/6 mice were implanted subcutaneously with 5 × 105 B16F10 melanoma tumor cells for 10 days, resulting in tumor sizes of 40 to 70 mm3. Activated, tumor-specific, Pmel-1 TCR T cells specific for gp10025–33 (5 × 106) were then administered on day 10 without any prior conditioning or exogenous cytokine supplementation (irradiation preconditioning had previously failed to enhance efficacy in this tumor model, Supplementary Fig. S3). TCR T-cell therapy was administered alone or in combination with a multidose vaccine boosting regimen consisting of cognate gp100 long peptide and CpG adjuvant ( ). To determine the importance of lymphatic targeting for effective vaccination of TCR T cells, unmodified soluble (SOL) peptide and adjuvant were compared with AMP-modified vaccine components. Tumor growth in mice treated with AMP vaccination in concert with TCR T-cell infusion was significantly delayed or completely eradicated compared with therapy with TCR T cells alone or in combination with SOL vaccination ( and ); consequently, AMP-vaccinated mice had significantly enhanced long-term survival without signs of distress, such as weight loss or significantly elevated systemic levels of inflammatory cytokines indicative of potential for cytokine release syndrome ( ; Supplementary Fig. S4). Soluble or AMP vaccination alone was not effective (Supplementary Fig. S5). Treatment with AMP vaccine in combination with a lower dose of TCR T cells (1 × 106) produced similar results with tumor growth delay, leading to durable responses in a subset of treated animals (Supplementary Fig. S6). Relative to TCR T monotherapy, enhanced antitumor efficacy in animals treated with the AMP vaccine and TCR T cells was associated with increased numbers and persistence of TCR T cells in the peripheral blood of treated animals for up to 96+ days, following adoptive transfer ( ; Supplementary Fig. S4).
 To evaluate the potential for protection against relapse, previously treated mice that achieved long-term survival were challenged 75 days after T-cell treatment alongside a naïve control group. All long-term surviving mice exhibited continued TCR T-cell persistence (Supplementary Fig. S4) and prevented growth of the secondary B16F10 tumor ( and ). In another experiment, combination TCR T cell and AMP vaccine treatment of B16F10 tumors established for only 7 days, as compared with 10 days, resulted in further improved antitumor efficacy (Supplementary Fig. S7).

## Exposure to AMP-vaccinated lymph nodes ex vivo enhances TCR T-cell activity
Given the enhanced in vivo efficacy of TCR T cells upon combination with AMP vaccine, we used ex vivo analysis of explanted lymph nodes to mechanistically analyze this combination effect. After dosing tumor-naïve mice with either mock, SOL, or AMP vaccines including gp100 peptide, inguinal lymph nodes were harvested and resulting single-cell suspensions were cocultured with previously activated, transduced, and rested TCR T cells ( ) before assessment of TCR T-cell activation, cytotoxicity, and effector function. Significantly increased pro-inflammatory cytokine levels, including IFNγ and TNFα, were detected in supernatant of TCR T cells cultured with lymph node single-cell suspension from AMP-vaccinated mice relative to comparator conditions ( ; Supplementary Fig. S8). Activation status ( ), proliferation ( ; Supplementary Fig. S8), and tumor-specific lysis ( ) were also significantly enhanced for TCR T cells cultured with AMP-vaccinated lymph nodes. Lymph node single-cell suspension from SOL-vaccinated comparators did not enhance TCR T-cell activation, cytotoxicity, and effector function ( – ), consistent with prior studies demonstrating poor lymphatic accumulation of soluble peptide immunogens and molecular adjuvants ( ). Parallel assessment of endogenous T cells in lymph node cell suspensions showed a rapid decrease in the number of these cells compared with TCR T cells (Supplementary Fig. S8), suggesting that the measured killing activity and cytokine responses could be predominantly attributed to TCR T cells rather than priming of endogenous gp100-specific T cells. To assess the potential for priming of naïve gp100-specific T cells, additional studies were conducted with unactivated TCR T cells (Supplementary Fig. S9). These naïve TCR T cells showed similar potential for AMP-vaccinated lymph nodes to stimulate proliferation, cytokine secretion, and tumor-specific lytic function (Supplementary Fig. S9). Moreover, increased TCR T-cell responses were observed in circulating blood following combination of AMP vaccination with adoptive transfer of naïve TCR T cells to tumor-bearing hosts (Supplementary Fig. S9). Taken together, these results suggest that AMP vaccination can promote activation of both naïve and previously activated T cells including TCR T cells.
 

## AMP vaccination induces DC activation and inflammation in lymph nodes
Given the stimulatory effect of AMP-vaccinated lymph node single-cell suspensions on TCR T cells ex vivo, we investigated the effect of AMP vaccination upon both TCR T cells and APCs within lymph nodes in vivo. As before, WT mice were implanted subcutaneously with B16F10 tumor cells for 10 days before treatment with activated, transduced, tumor-specific TCR T cells alone or with SOL- or AMP vaccination followed by lymph node collection 7 days later ( ). Although SOL-vaccination had no effect, AMP vaccination induced significant increases in the number of lymph node–resident TCR T cells ( ) and total CD8+ T cells secreting pro-inflammatory cytokines in response to stimulation ex vivo with cognate gp100 peptide ( ). These findings were consistent with our prior ex vivo observations and the known relative lymphatic targeting efficiency of SOL-immunogen versus AMP-immunogen, demonstrating that AMP vaccination has similar effects upon adoptively transferred T cells in lymph nodes in vivo to those observed ex vivo.
 Previous studies have shown efficient AMP peptide and adjuvant trafficking to lymph nodes ( ), and we hypothesized that concerted activation and antigen presentation by APCs in the lymph nodes could be a critical mechanism contributing to enhanced cell expansion and effector function. We observed that AMP vaccination induced significant increases in the total number of CD11c+ DCs, as well as the number of activated DCs expressing costimulatory or antigen-presentation markers—CD40, CD80, CD86, and MHC Class II—in lymph nodes one week after initiating TCR T-cell therapy ( – ). Lymph nodes collected from SOL-vaccinated mice exhibited elevated but significantly lower numbers of total and activated DCs. These findings correlate the potent induction of lymph node DC activation to elevated TCR T-cell numbers and function associated with AMP vaccine enhancement of TCR T-cell therapy.
To broadly assess the differential activation status of the lymph node compartment and the relative contribution of TCR T-cell therapy and SOL- or AMP vaccination, RNA was extracted from lymph nodes collected as before and unbiased transcriptome analysis was performed ( ; Supplementary Fig. S10). Compared with SOL-vaccination with and without TCR T-cell infusion, AMP vaccination elevated numerous gene transcripts for pathways associated with inflammation, immune activation, and antigen presentation, whereas gene signatures associated with T-cell anergy or dysfunction were not observed. Upregulated transcripts included those encoding costimulatory molecules Cd40 (2.2x) and Cd86 (3.9x), inflammatory molecules Il12b (4.8x), Ifng (4.6x), and Gzmb (10.6x), as well as Tap1 (2.5x) and Tapbp (2.4x), which are associated with antigen presentation (Supplementary Fig. S11). Transcripts commonly implicated in immune regulation, and T-cell anergy or exhaustion (e.g., FoxP3, Ctla4, and Ceacam1) were not upregulated ( ). AMP vaccination induced similar effects on the lymph node transcriptome regardless of the presence or absence of TCR T cells, suggesting that AMP vaccination including AMP-CpG was the primary driver of broad lymph node activation. Together these data reinforce our observations that AMP vaccination is effective for inducing numerous and comprehensive immune mechanisms important for supporting T-cell activation within lymph nodes.

## AMP vaccination enhances intratumoral TCR T- and endogenous T-cell function
We have shown that AMP vaccination enhances antitumor responses in TCR T cell–treated tumor-bearing mice. This effect correlated with improved in vivo lymph node activation mediated by efficient AMP peptide and adjuvant accumulation in draining lymph nodes in contrast to soluble comparators ( ). To assess the connection between the observed lymph node responses and antitumor immune activity within the tumor itself, we analyzed characteristics of adoptively transferred TCR T cells and endogenous T cells isolated from the tumor parenchyma. Following the previous regimen for treating B16F10 tumor bearing mice with TCR T-cell therapy with or without SOL- or AMP vaccination, tumors were collected 7 days after TCR T-cell transfer ( ). Analysis of tumor samples demonstrated that TCR T-cell therapy combined with AMP vaccination induced significantly higher infiltration of adoptively transferred TCR T cells into the tumor in comparison to mice given mock- or SOL-vaccination in combination with TCR T cells ( ). In addition, total tumor-infiltrating T cells (including endogenous and transferred) from AMP-vaccinated animals were more activated, proliferative, and functional, as illustrated by CD25, Ki67, and IFNγ and granzyme B expression ( – ). Despite the prior observation that SOL-vaccination can drive moderate improvements in lymph node DC numbers and activation status, these did not translate into appreciable improvements in tumor-infiltrating T-cell responses. Promotion of enhanced infiltration, and overall fitness of tumoral T cells by AMP vaccine was not associated with T-cell exhaustion; upregulation of the PD-1 activation marker was not accompanied by increases in PD-1/TIM-3/LAG-3 markers that would indicate T-cell dysfunction (Supplementary Fig. S12).
 We then conducted an unbiased transcriptome analysis of the tumor to examine changes associated with TCR T-cell therapy alone versus combination with AMP vaccination ( ; Supplementary Fig. S11). Although these data do not allow for comparison with vaccination alone, or TCR-T combination with soluble vaccination, comparison of tumors from animals treated with TCR T-cell monotherapy and TCR T-cell combination with AMP vaccine showed upregulation in numerous gene transcripts indicative of immune activation. Specifically, upregulation of transcripts associated with T-cell activation (Tnfrsf9: 3.2x, Cd80:3x), T-cell homing (Cxcl10: 2.5x, Cxcl9:13.6x), IFN signaling (Ifng:46x), T-cell proliferation (Il18:2.2x, Il15Ra:7x), and antigen presentation were observed (Supplementary Fig. S11). As in the associated lymph node analyses, we found that upregulation of immune activating and inflammatory transcripts, including Pdcd1 (which encodes PD-1), was not accompanied by increases in regulatory transcripts associated with T-cell anergy, dysfunction, or tumor promotion such as FoxP3, Ceacam1, or Bcl6. Considered together with the observation of AMP vaccine–dependent induction of T-cell infiltration and improved fitness, these results indicate a comprehensive shift in the local tumor immune microenvironment associated with AMP vaccine–driven lymphatic activation.
We further hypothesized that the effects of AMP vaccination might simultaneously promote endogenous T-cell responses specific to other untargeted tumor-associated or neoantigen epitopes. To investigate this, tumor samples from the various treatment groups were stimulated with a panel of peptides previously reported to be present in the B16F10 mutanome ( ), and the presence of cognate T cells was assessed by detection of effector cytokine production. Tumors from mice treated with TCR T cells combined with AMP vaccination exhibited significantly more infiltrating, tumor antigen-specific T cells relative to comparators receiving TCR T-cell monotherapy or TCR T-cell therapy in combination with SOL-vaccination ( – ). Corresponding responses were observed in draining lymph nodes with levels of endogenous tumor-specific T cells in lymph nodes showing a correlation to those detected in tumor (Supplementary Fig. S13). This increase in AMP vaccine–induced tumor infiltration by endogenous tumor-specific T cells suggests a potential role for this mechanism to promote broad antitumor efficacy and durable protection from tumor progression driven by antigen-loss or heterogeneity. To determine the contribution of endogenous T cells specific for tumor-associated- and neo-antigens to the antitumor effect, mice that had previously eliminated a B16F10 subcutaneous tumor following combination TCR T-cell and AMP vaccination therapy were depleted of adoptively transferred Thy1.1+ TCR T cells prior to secondary B16F10 tumor challenge (Supplementary Fig. S14). Consistent with prior observations of complete rejection of secondary challenge by host animals with a replete TCR T memory cell response, a subset of host mice depleted of adoptively transferred TCR T cells showed complete tumor rejection, however others displayed delayed, yet progressive tumor growth ( and ). This effect illustrates both the contribution of epitope spread within the endogenous T-cell repertoire to promoting overall antitumor activity and the potential of AMP vaccination to support this mechanism in a syngeneic murine tumor model.

## AMP vaccination enhances mutant KRAS-specific TCR T-cell responses in WT and HLA-expressing mice
To assess the potential of AMP vaccination to enhance TCR T cells representative of translational candidates for treating human disease, we conducted studies of mutant KRAS (mKRAS)-specific TCR T cells in WT or transgenic mice capable of TCR T-cell activation through matched human HLA-TCR engagement. We first explored lymphatic targeting in WT mice treated with vaccines including FITC-labeled, SOL- or AMP-mKRAS-G12D peptide immunogens. Lymph nodes were harvested 24 hours after treatment and imaged ex vivo for peptide-associated fluorescent signal ( ; Supplementary Fig. S15). AMP-vaccinated animals exhibited enhanced peptide accumulation in local and distal draining lymph nodes highlighting the ability of AMP-modification to induce robust lymphatic delivery targeting multiple regionally distinct lymph node groups ( ). AMP-modification enhanced the fraction of lymph node–resident DCs and macrophages observed to simultaneously acquire FITC-labelled peptide and exhibit markers of APC-activation, CD80 and CD40, compared with SOL controls ( – ). These data confirmed that clinically relevant AMP peptides traffic to draining lymph nodes more efficiently than conventional soluble peptides and led us to investigate whether these peptides induced TCR T-cell engraftment, activation, and effector function in an HLA-expressing, clinically-relevant murine system.
 HLA A*11:01+ donor murine T cells were engineered to express TCRs specific for clinically-relevant mKRAS-G12D or -G12V peptide epitopes and infused into naïve HLA A*11:01+ host mice in combination with either mock-, SOL-, or AMP vaccination regimens containing cognate peptide immunogens (Supplementary Fig. S16). As observed previously, only administration of AMP vaccine led to increased lymph node APC activation with elevated numbers of CD80/CD86+ and MHC Class II+ activated DCs ( ; Supplementary Fig. S16). AMP vaccination also induced a significant increase in the number of total and activated CD25+/CD69+ TCR T cells within the lymph nodes and the peripheral blood ( – ), consistent with lymph node TCR T-cell expansion and activation leading to more circulating TCR T cells. Combination with AMP vaccination further induced elevated numbers of TCR T cells within the lungs, a tissue of high clinical interest for TCR T-cell therapy in human mKRAS-driven disease ( ).
In addition to enhanced persistence and activation of TCR T cells, combination of TCR T-cell therapy with AMP vaccination augmented various axes of TCR T-cell effector function; combination with AMP vaccine produced significantly higher numbers of IFNγ-secreting splenocytes relative to mock- or SOL-vaccinated comparators ( ). Finally, animals treated with TCR T cells and AMP vaccination exhibited substantially higher levels of in vivo specific lysis against mKRAS peptide–pulsed targets, illustrating the potential of AMP vaccination to enhance the efficacy of clinically relevant TCR T cells in vivo ( ).

## Clinically relevant human TCR T cells are activated by AMP peptide pulsed autologous DCs in vitro
To explore the translational relevance of AMP vaccination to boost TCR T-cell function, we devised an in vitro system in which peptide-pulsed, autologous human DCs were used to stimulate activated, transduced, and rested human TCR T cells across multiple clinically relevant tumor targets and HLA haplotypes. Initially, NY-ESO-1–specific TCRs were transduced into human HLA A*02:01+ T cells and, following a rest period, were cocultured with autologous matured HLA A*02:01+ DCs previously pulsed with soluble or AMP-NY-ESO-1 peptide or vehicle only (Supplementary Fig. S17). In comparison with T cells left untreated or exposed to DCs with or without soluble peptide, exposure to AMP peptide–pulsed DCs resulted in significantly enhanced activation of human TCR T cells ( ) and secretion of significantly higher levels of pro-inflammatory IFNγ, TNFα, and IL2 cytokines ( ; Supplementary Fig. S17). Longitudinal analysis of total TCR T-cell numbers demonstrated significant AMP peptide–dependent expansion of TCR T cells with increased memory phenotype ( ; Supplementary Fig. S17). Finally, TCR T cells exhibited enhanced target lysis following activation with AMP peptide-pulsed DCs indicating improved per-cell cytotoxic potential against targets expressing relevant levels of antigen presentation components and NY-ESO-1 protein ( ). Similar results were observed for analogous evaluations of HPV16 E7-specific (Supplementary Fig. S18) or mKRAS-G12D/V-specific TCR T cells ( – ; Supplementary Fig. S18), indicating the versatility of AMP peptides to enhance TCR T-cell activity independent of antigen specificity.
 Sensitivity to rapid degradation in vivo can limit the activity of soluble peptide immunogens. However, AMP-conjugation is known to enhance peptide stability to ensure intact immunogen delivery to APCs ( ). To investigate the contribution of enhanced stability to AMP peptide stimulation of TCR T-cell activation, AMP peptides and SOL comparators were exposed to mock in vivo conditions (human serum, 37°C) prior to pulsing autologous DCs for subsequent coculture with TCR T cells (Supplementary Fig. S19). Under these conditions, AMP-mKRAS peptides maintained their ability to induce TCR T-cell activation, cytokine secretion, and specific target lysis ( – ; Supplementary Fig. S19). The activity of SOL-peptide comparators was significantly reduced or completely ablated, consistent with the known sensitivity of these agents to passive or enzymatic processes of degradation abundant in tissues. These results highlight another challenge limiting the activity of conventional peptide immunogens and demonstrate that AMP-modification can overcome this limitation to enhance TCR T-cell stimulation and effector function in vivo.

## AMP peptide exposure enhances human TCR T-cell tumor infiltration, activation, and antitumor activity in vivo
To investigate whether the in vitro enhancement conferred to clinically relevant mKRAS-specific TCR T cells led to enhanced in vivo antitumor effect, we first cocultured human TCR T cells with AMP peptide- or mock-pulsed autologous DCs in vitro prior to treating Panc-1 (HLA A*11:01+, mKRAS-G12D+) tumor-bearing NSG mice by adoptive transfer (Supplementary Fig. S209). As NSG mice lack an intact and competent immune system, in particular functioning lymph nodes essential for AMP vaccine mechanisms of action, we activated TCR T cells in vitro prior to infusion. Ten-day Panc-1 tumor-bearing NSG mice were treated and on day 35 after tumor implantation, the tumors were excised and analyzed for tumor mass, and TCR T-cell infiltration and activation. Only the combination of mKRAS-G12D-specific TCR T cells with AMP peptide-pulsed DCs led to a significant relative reduction in endpoint tumor mass ( ). Corresponding analysis of tumor samples showed that the TCR T cells exposed to AMP peptide–pulsed DCs in vitro also exhibited significantly higher tumor infiltration ( ), higher levels of T-cell activation ( ), and a lack of exhaustion phenotype (Supplementary Fig. S20). These data illustrate that DC-mediated AMP peptide exposure promotes activation and solid tumor infiltration by clinically relevant TCR T cells associated with enhanced antitumor responses.
 

## Discussion
Despite modest overall response rates and limited durability, the therapeutic potential of TCR T-cell therapy suggests that optimization of these modalities could have wide-ranging therapeutic benefit in many previously intractable solid tumors ( ). Among other correlates, clinical failures of TCR T-cell therapies have been associated with poor TCR T-cell engraftment and persistence, which can potentially be attributed to a lack of in vivo T-cell activating signals ( ). We have shown that a combination of TCR T-cell therapy with cognate peptide AMP vaccination provides substantial enhancement of antitumor effect, including long-term non-progression and resistance to secondary tumor challenge. These favorable outcomes only occurred for AMP vaccine-treated animals and were associated with persistently elevated levels of circulating TCR T cells, detectable throughout the initial follow-up period and after secondary tumor rechallenge, resulting in complete protection. Elevated TCR T-cell numbers and persistence further correlated with increased tumor infiltration, and function of TCR T cells, consistent with previous preclinical and clinical reports associating functional T-cell dynamics with better overall survival ( ).
Multiple strategies have been developed to enhance the curative potential of TCR T-cell therapies. Pro-inflammatory cytokines or other costimulatory molecules can be included in TCR expression cassettes, generating “armored” TCR T cells with enhanced survival and antitumor capabilities ( ). Alternatively, TCR T-cell engraftment and persistence can be enhanced by exogenous provision of cytokines in combination regimens or through direct T-cell modification with cytokine “backpacks” ( ). Although these therapeutic modalities have great promise, challenges, and limitations have also been reported. Exogenous cytokine supplementation is a nonspecific therapy with potential for unwanted toxicity ( ), often further complicated in practice by short serum half-lives and inadequate therapeutic indices. Alternatively, constitutive expression of pro-inflammatory molecules by armored TCR T cells seeks to restrict potential toxicities associated with systemic exposure; however, the potential for adverse side effects remains and often requires additional genetic modifications to ensure safety ( ). In contrast, immunization has the benefit of repeat dosing potential for safe, on-demand augmentation of engineered T cells in vivo, providing an opportunity for support of long-term T-cell functional persistence ( ). In mice bearing syngeneic tumors, repeat dosing of AMP vaccines was well tolerated in combination with TCR T-cell therapy. Prior evaluation has shown that AMP-directed delivery to lymph nodes can reduce potentially toxic systemic inflammation otherwise observed for potent adjuvants due to primary clearance from the injection site through the blood ( ). In the setting of TCR T-cell combination, restriction of TCR T engagement to lymph nodes provides an additional measure of safety, preventing widespread exposure to inflammatory cytokines associated with potential toxicity. In addition, combination of TCR T-cell therapy with AMP vaccination provided significant improvements in antitumor efficacy without the need for traditional preconditioning regimens or exogenous cytokine supplementation, suggesting the potential for AMP vaccination to supplant these elements of standard practice which are associated with potential safety concerns and limit clinical practice to patients able to undergo preconditioning therapy ( ). Although potent immune activation like that associated with AMP vaccination could theoretically contribute to the development of bystander autoreactive T-cell responses, assessment of body weight, serum cytokine levels, and gross clinical observations indicated no adverse effects either during therapy, or follow-up for long-term non-progressors for as many as 135 days.
Collectively, next-generation genetic engineering strategies or combination regimens have focused on selected single agents with powerful but potentially narrow biological impact. Although these strategies have demonstrated promise, they lack the ability to engage the full breadth of activating mechanisms employed during natural T-cell priming by APCs. More comprehensive T-cell activation can be achieved through cognate peptide vaccination to expand and enhance the function of T cells orchestrated by APCs. AMP vaccination induced significant increases in activated lymph node–resident DCs. Exposure to these cells ex vivo resulted in significant enhancement of TCR T-cell activation, proliferation, effector function, and per-cell tumor killing capacity. Parallel benefits were observed in TCR T cells in blood, lymph nodes, and tumor parenchyma corresponding to enhanced overall antitumor effect. Evaluation of lymph node transcriptomics further revealed the breadth of immune modulating effects driven by AMP vaccination; pronounced enhancement of numerous genes and pathways associated with T-cell activation, costimulation, antigen presentation, and cytokine signaling indicate the comprehensive suite of pro-inflammatory signaling induced by AMP vaccination and accessible to TCR T cells within the vaccinated lymph node microenvironment. Future evaluation of lymph node and tumor by immunofluorescence and/or spatial transcriptomics will be necessary to provide additional mechanistic insights into the primary cellular interactions and functions underlying these findings.
Although antitumor responses have been observed in clinical studies of TCR T-cell therapy, acquisition of tumor-intrinsic escape mechanisms, such as antigen-loss or the downregulation of antigen-presentation components can lead to tumor progression even in patients achieving successful engraftment of adoptively transferred TCR T cells ( ). Therefore, strategies capable of improving TCR T-cell engraftment and functional persistence are attractive, but potentially limited if not adopted in parallel with efforts to overcome mechanisms of tumor immune escape. Tumor antigens shed through the course of tumorigenic progression or released in response to therapy are known to accumulate in draining lymph nodes. These represent an important source of information for directing mutanome-specific adaptive immunity if captured and presented properly by the immune response ( ). Here, AMP vaccination resulting in activation of the endogenous APC compartment offers the opportunity to stimulate tumor-specific endogenous T cells alongside adoptively transferred cells, thus expanding the repertoire of effector cells available for antitumor effect. APC activation within lymph nodes may support de novo priming of tumor-specific T cells, expansion of preexisting memory responses, or a combination of these mechanisms. In addition to the effect on the adoptively transferred TCR T-cell population, we found that AMP vaccination supported the expansion of cytokine-secreting endogenous tumor-infiltrating T cells specific for nontargeted, but tumor-associated antigens. Induction of antigen-spreading in mice treated with TCR T cells and AMP vaccination was further observed to confer partial protection from secondary tumor rechallenge in mice that were depleted of adoptively transferred TCR T cells. These observations illustrate the benefit of AMP vaccination–induced antigen-spreading to diversify the antitumor immune response providing protection against tumor immune escape through mutational antigen-loss or downregulation. A recent report demonstrated similar benefits in the setting of AMP vaccine combination with CAR T-cell therapy ( ). In that study, IFNγ- and IL12-dependent crosstalk between T cells and APCs in the lymph nodes and tumor were critical for stimulating antigen-spreading to induce endogenous T cells. In our study, IL12 and IFNγ were among the most highly upregulated transcripts observed in lymph nodes and tumor following TCR T-cell combination with AMP vaccine, suggesting that similar complimentary immune mechanisms in these two key sites may contribute to the analogous antigen-spreading observed after TCR T-cell AMP vaccine combination. Although the ability of AMP vaccination to promote improved breadth of immune-specificity through antigen-spreading is promising, further opportunities to improve response could be made through synergistic combinations; immune checkpoint inhibition or other immune-modulating regimens may further enhance tumor-directed T cells, or interfere with common mechanisms of tumor immune evasion.
Although vaccine combinations offer the promise to address many of the issues thought to limit TCR T-cell therapeutic efficacy, vaccine components including unmodified molecular adjuvants and conventional peptides fail to successfully traffic to and stimulate immune cells in the lymph nodes where APC:T-cell interactions are orchestrated ( ). Prior studies have reported activation of TCR T cells by tumor-resident DCs ( ), demonstrating the importance of other sites and mechanisms for T-cell invigoration. However, without the ability to efficiently access and prime APCs within the lymph node, candidate vaccines are not able to effectively engage adoptively transferred or endogenous T cells in this potent immunologic site. Throughout these studies, comparison between unmodified (soluble) and AMP vaccine components demonstrated the importance of effective lymph node delivery and the inherent deficiency of conventional adjuvants and immunogens for lymph node targeting. Furthermore, the observed sensitivity of soluble peptides to degradation when exposed to simulated in vivo conditions represents a significant additional challenge for their utility in vivo, whereas AMP-modification imparted complete resistance to peptide degradation. The combined evidence demonstrates that achieving potent TCR T-cell activation in the lymph nodes though AMP-directed vaccine delivery was essential to improving TCR T-cell and endogenous T-cell activity, resulting in greatly enhanced therapeutic efficacy.
Following evaluation in syngeneic murine systems, we demonstrated that this therapeutic modality could be easily applied to enhance the activity of clinically relevant TCR T-cell therapies. HLA-transgenic mice treated with TCR T cells and AMP vaccination maintained substantially higher levels of persistent TCR T-cell populations and exhibited enhanced in vivo cytotoxic function, illustrating the potential of AMP vaccination to enhance the efficacy of clinically relevant TCR T cells. In vitro, AMP peptides were shown to enhance numerous critical T-cell characteristics including activation, proliferation, pro-inflammatory cytokine secretion, and tumor lytic function for human TCR T cells targeting NY-ESO-1, mKRAS, and HPV16 E7, illustrating the flexibility of this approach to enhance TCR T cells of various specificity. Further, the ability to rapidly design and simply manufacture matched AMP peptides informed by knowledge of TCR cognate peptide sequences makes application of this approach feasible for clinical evaluations in combination with existing or progressing TCR T-cell therapies without the need for any modification to existing manufacturing processes.
Prior studies of vaccine combination with adoptive cell therapy have indicated the promise of this approach for improving efficacy against solid tumors ( ). Transduction of endogenous antiviral T cells with tumor-specific CARs offers the potential to enhance the activity of these cells in vivo through immunization against the orthogonal viral antigen ( ). Although these approaches strongly confirm the potential for vaccination to potently enhance tumor-redirected T-cell therapy, they are often dependent on complicated and bespoke multi-cistronic genetic reprogramming for each potential new application. Alternatively, direct vaccine-boosting of CAR T cells through the chimeric receptor offers an effective and streamlined approach ( ). In these prior studies, vaccine-induced activation of APCs in secondary lymphoid tissues was correlated to enhanced CAR T-cell activity and induction of endogenous antigen-spreading responses capable of improving responses against solid tumors with heterogenous antigen expression; this is consistent with our observations demonstrating the importance of lymph node APCs for enhancing TCR T-cell and endogenous T-cell activity. Considered together, the prior evidence for the potential of vaccine combinations with T-cell therapy, the reported importance of effective lymphoid APC involvement, and the relative practical advantages of vaccination directly targeting the tumor-specific TCR suggest that AMP vaccination is a promising combination strategy for TCR T-cell therapy.
In summary, combination of TCR T-cell therapy with AMP-vaccination promotes comprehensive lymph node immune activation to enhance TCR T-cell activity and promote endogenous antitumor T-cell responses resulting in improved solid tumor eradication and durable protection against recurrence. Ongoing clinical trials evaluating ELI-002 (NCT04853017 and NCT05726864), an mKRAS-specific AMP peptide vaccine targeting G12D/R/V/C/A/S and G13D mutations, have recently reported promising safety and activity in patients with adjuvant stage pancreatic and colorectal cancer with minimal residual disease following standard locoregional treatment ( ). Assessment of immunogenicity and antitumor activity demonstrated the induction of ex vivo mKRAS-specific T-cell responses, and reduction or clearance of tumor biomarkers previously associated with clinical outcome. Parallel clinical assessment of mKRAS-specific TCR T cells targeting cognate epitopes present in ELI-002 have recently demonstrated antitumor activity ( ), raising the potential for rapid translation of an AMP vaccine combination strategy to further enhance the therapeutic benefit of mKRAS-targeted TCR T-cell monotherapy. Moreover, simple AMP-modification of TCR-cognate peptide sequences provides an attractive potential for broad and rapid application to clinical TCR T-cell programs to enhance TCR T-cell function and promote more durable and comprehensive antitumor immune responses.
