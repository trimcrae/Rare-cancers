## Abstract

## Purpose:
Minimal residual disease (MRD) after first-line treatment of advanced-stage ovarian cancer remains a long-standing barrier to cure. We investigated the prognostic and translational value of MRD detection by second-look laparoscopy (SLL) and ctDNA at the completion of first-line therapy.

## Experimental Design:
Patients with high-grade epithelial ovarian cancer who had a complete clinical response to first-line therapy and underwent SLL and plasma collection for ctDNA were included. Progression-free survival (PFS) and overall survival (OS) were estimated based on MRD and clinicopathologic status. Spatial transcriptomics (GeoMx and Visium) and proteomics (CODEX) profiling were performed on serial samples from select patients.

## Results:
Forty of 95 (42.1%) patients had surgically detected MRD, which was associated with worse PFS (median PFS 7.4 vs. 23.8 months; P < 0.001) and OS (median OS 33.9 vs. not reached; P < 0.001). SLL positivity was an independent negative prognostic factor for OS (HR, 4.40; 95% confidence interval, 1.37–14.21; P = 0.013) in multivariable analysis. Among 44 patients who underwent SLL and had ctDNA testing, 34% (15/44) were ctDNA-positive, which was associated with worse PFS (6.4 vs. 28.1 months; P < 0.001) and OS (32.4 months vs. not reached; P = 0.008). We demonstrated the feasibility of spatial multiomics in studying MRD and their ability to provide hypothesis-generating observations, implicating the upregulation of the hypoxia signaling pathway, expression of multiple druggable targets (CDK6, GLS, MSLN, ERBB2), and immune exclusion in MRD lesions.

## Conclusions:
Approximately half of patients in clinical remission after first-line therapy have assessable MRD, which can inform prognosis, therapeutic target discovery, and clinical trials.

## Translational Relevance
Cure rates for most patients with advanced-stage ovarian cancer have remained stagnant for over four decades. This is due to minimal residual disease (MRD) that persists after first-line treatment. Although maintenance therapies provide benefits in select populations, they are agnostic to mechanisms of chemoresistance and other adaptive biological features that result in MRD. The lack of reliable clinical detection and poor understanding of the biological underpinnings of MRD represent significant barriers to improving outcomes. In this study, we used second-look laparoscopy and ctDNA to identify MRD and provide proof of principle for the prognostic and translational value of detecting MRD in ovarian cancer. The poor prognosis of MRD-positive patients, along with the identification of multiple druggable targets in MRD tumor regions, provides the rationale for MRD-targeted investigational therapies.

## Introduction
Minimal residual disease (MRD) represents a small volume of cancer that persists in a patient after treatment but remains clinically undetectable ( ). In advanced ovarian cancer, the vast majority of patients’ disease burden drops to levels below the limit of clinical detection (as measured by CT scan and CA-125 tumor marker) after first-line surgery and chemotherapy; however, less than 20% of patients are cured owing to occult, chemoresistant residual disease ( , ). Although maintenance therapies such as PARP inhibitors and bevacizumab are currently utilized during this phase to delay progression ( – ), the vast majority of women eventually develop clinically detectable progression and incurable disease. The current use of maintenance therapies is agnostic to the biological features and mechanisms of chemoresistance harbored by MRD, largely due to a lack of ability to detect and perform detailed biological analyses of MRD. This significant knowledge gap represents a substantial technical barrier to the development of personalized, novel therapies designed to eradicate MRD and ultimately increase cure rates in ovarian cancer.
In many solid tumors, detection of MRD is synonymous with circulating tumor cells or ctDNA ( , – ). However, ovarian cancer is unique in this regard because of the historical precedence for the use of “second-look” surgeries to detect surgical MRD (sMRD), which were originally used to determine the duration of adjuvant therapy and were later adopted for prognostication in the 1970s to 1990s. At that time, second-look surgeries were performed via a large abdominal incision (laparotomy), and although studies found that the result of second-look surgery is prognostic for progression-free survival (PFS), these procedures fell out of practice given their morbidity and the fact that extending the duration of the limited chemotherapy options available at the time did not seem to improve survival ( ). However, given the increasing availability of new targeted and biological therapeutics and the routine use of minimally invasive surgery, second-look laparoscopy (SLL) has now become a significantly less morbid way for surgical assessment of residual disease. Furthermore, because SLL involves obtaining multiple peritoneal biopsies, it provides a gateway to understanding the tumor microenvironment (TME) and cancer cell phenotypic states in this previously inaccessible sMRD phase of ovarian cancer.
As noted above, other methods to assess residual disease in solid tumors have emerged over the past decade, most notably the use of ctDNA, which can be detected via PCR-based or next-generation sequencing (NGS) assays targeting tumor-specific mutations, structural variants, copy-number alterations, and epigenetic features. Tumor-informed ctDNA assessment can be used to detect molecular MRD (molMRD) after upfront therapy, monitor response to therapy, and assess clonal evolution of cancer cells over time ( ). This minimally invasive, blood-based biomarker has been demonstrated to be a prognostic marker for recurrence across multiple solid tumors with high sensitivity ( – ). However, there are minimal data to address the role of ctDNA in ovarian cancer. One study did find ctDNA positivity after surgery to be associated with shorter recurrence-free survival; however, the number of patients with positive ctDNA was limited, and almost half of the cohort had early-stage disease, making it difficult to apply these findings broadly ( ).
The objectives of this study were to evaluate the detection rates and prognostic value of sMRD detection by SLL and molMRD by ctDNA at the completion of first-line therapy in advanced ovarian cancer. Furthermore, we sought to demonstrate the feasibility of applying cutting-edge spatial transcriptomic and proteomic assays to sMRD biopsies collected during SLL and to provide biological insights into mechanisms leading to disease persistence in the sMRD stage.

## Materials and Methods

## Study approval and cohort selection
This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the University of Texas MD Anderson Cancer Center (Institutional Review Board–approved protocols 2019-0944 and PA18-0721) and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. This observational, single-center study included patients with high-grade epithelial ovarian cancer who achieved complete radiologic response with no clinical evidence of disease and normalization of the CA-125 tumor marker after first-line treatment and who consented to undergo SLL after appropriate counseling. Written informed consent was obtained from each patient before the SLL procedure. Patients could have received neoadjuvant chemotherapy (NACT) or undergone primary tumor reductive surgery. Patients who underwent SLL from March 2018 to September 2024 were included in the primary analysis ( ).
 

## SLL procedure
SLL is an outpatient, laparoscopic procedure typically performed 6 to 8 weeks after the final cycle of adjuvant chemotherapy. Patients undergoing ileostomy or colostomy takedown may have concurrent SLL or second-look laparotomy at the treating surgeon’s discretion. After laparoscopic entry, “washings” are collected from the peritoneal cavity for cytology evaluation, and peritoneal surfaces are examined. Areas of obvious tumor, suspicious lesions, or adhesions are biopsied. Subsequently, systematic biopsies are obtained, ideally from the following peritoneal surfaces: right and left pelvis, cul-de-sac, vesical peritoneum, paracolic gutters, any residual omentum and/or falciform ligament, and right diaphragm peritoneum. Histopathologic diagnosis of tumor in any of the biopsies or positive cytology from the washings constitutes a “positive” SLL and presence of sMRD.

## Personalized ctDNA assay
Patients with sufficient tumor tissue collected for whole-exome sequencing during cytoreductive surgery were included in the ctDNA analysis. Plasma samples were collected and banked at the time of SLL. A clinically validated, personalized, tumor-informed 16-plex mPCR-NGS assay (Signatera, Natera, Inc.) was retrospectively utilized for the detection and quantification of ctDNA, as previously described ( , , ). Whole-exome sequencing was performed on extracted DNA from archived formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tissue and matched normal blood samples from each patient. Up to 16 patient-specific, somatic, single-nucleotide variants (SNV) were selected for mPCR-NGS testing in the plasma cell–free DNA of the respective patient. Detection of two or more SNVs above a predefined statistical algorithm confidence threshold was considered ctDNA-positive. ctDNA concentration was calculated and reported as mean tumor molecules per milliliter of plasma. The ctDNA analysis was performed blinded to clinical outcomes.

## Clinical data collection and analysis
Demographic and clinicopathologic information were abstracted from the medical record. Summary statistics (frequency and percentage for categorical variables; mean and 95% confidence interval (CI) for continuous variables) were used to summarize the demographic and clinical characteristics of patients. MRD status was considered a binary variable and was determined in two different ways: based on SLL pathologic outcomes and based on ctDNA outcomes. Two clinical cohorts were examined: all patients who underwent SLL assessment and those who had both SLL and ctDNA collected at the time of SLL. χ2 and Fisher’s exact tests were used to compare categorical variables depending on the underlying distribution of the data. Logistic regression was used to estimate the OR with a 95% CI of having MRD detected at the time of SLL. The reference group in the model consists of patients who had negative SLLs. The final model controlled for first-line treatment (binary; primary surgery vs. NACT), homologous recombination deficiency (HRD) status (binary; HRD negative vs. HRD positive), and the status of initial cytoreduction (categorical; R0, R1, and R2).
PFS and overall survival (OS) from the time of SLL were estimated using the Kaplan–Meier method and compared using the log-rank test and Cox proportional hazards regression. PFS was measured from the date of second-look surgery to the earliest date of progression or death. For patients alive without progression or death at the time of data cutoff, survival times were censored at their last clinical assessment. A similar analysis was conducted for OS, which was calculated from the time of SLL to the earliest date of death or last follow-up. The data cutoff was October 21, 2024. P values < 0.05 were considered significant. All statistical analyses were performed using Stata/MP version 18.0.

## GeoMx digital spatial profiling
FFPE whole tissue sections were mounted on positively charged slides and processed according to Bruker Spatial Biology guidelines. Slides were deparaffinized and rehydrated preceding antigen retrieval in 1× Tris-EDTA (pH 9.0, Novus Biologicals, Cat. # NB900-62085) for 20 minutes at 99°C. RNA targets were exposed by incubating slides in proteinase K solution (1 μg/mL, Life Technologies, Cat. # AM2546) for 15 minutes at 37°C and fixed with 10% neutral buffered formalin (VWR, Cat. # 16004-128). The slides were incubated overnight with the 18,000 gene human Whole Transcriptome Atlas probe set (Bruker Spatial Biology, Cat. # 121401102). Alexa 488 SYTO 13 (1:10,000, Invitrogen, Cat. # S7575), Alexa 532 PanCK (1:50, Novus Biologicals, Cat. # NBP2-33200AF532, RRID: AB_2924722), Alexa 594 CD45 (1:100, Novus Biologicals, Cat. # NB100-77417AF594, RRID: AB_3172631), and Alexa 647 CD3 (1:100, Novus Biologicals, Cat. # NBP2-54392AF647, RRID: AB_3095415) were used as immunofluorescent morphology markers to visualize nuclei, epithelium, immune cells, and CD3+ T cells, respectively. Representative regions of interest were selected based on CD45 expression. Regions of interest were segmented into tumor [pan-Cytokeratin (panCK)+] or stroma (panCK−) when feasible and collected separately, resulting in a total of 184 areas of illumination. Oligo barcodes were cleaved using the NanoString GeoMx Digital Spatial Profiler (Bruker Spatial Biology, Inc., RRID: SCR_021660) and sequenced using the NovaSeq 6000 sequencer (Illumina, RRID: SCR_016387) on the S1 flow cell.

## GeoMx data analysis
GeoMx data preprocessing was performed with the R package GeomxTools (version 3.5.0, RRID: SCR_023424), which read in the digital count conversion and probe kit configuration files, containing the gene count matrix and probe set information, respectively. Raw counts were first subjected to sequencing quality checks, including segment-, probe-, and gene-level quality control based on the recommended criteria from NanoString. Then, Q3 normalization was applied using the upper quartile of the counts in each segment. Following that, differential gene expression analysis was performed using the linear mixed-effect model within the GeomxTools package, implemented with the function mixedModelDE, which took into account the slides’ information when comparing differentially expressed genes across different clinical subgroups. Then, the differentially expressed gene list was ranked by log2 fold change and subjected to downstream gene set enrichment analysis using the R software fgsea (version 1.2.28, RRID: SCR_020938) against the hallmark gene sets in the Molecular Signature Database ( – ), with P values adjusted using the Benjamini–Hochberg method. For the comparison of gene expression levels across different time points, we applied two approaches. First, a one-way ANOVA test was performed on the log-transformed normalized gene expression data of each area of illumination, and the Benjamini–Hochberg method was used for P value adjustment. Second, we applied a Bayesian ordinal regression model to analyze the trend of gene expression along time points, implemented with the R package brms (version 2.22.0). We scaled the log-normalized gene expression data to a mean of 0 and a variance of 1 to allow for comparability among genes. Then we modeled gene expression with sampling time points as a monotonic variable and included slide information as a random effect. As such, the estimated effect of time point on gene expression was obtained. Estimates with 95% credible intervals that lay entirely above or below 0 were considered meaningful. Gene expression data were also overlaid onto the tissue images and visualized using the R software SpatialOmicsOverlay (version 1.2.1).

## Visium spatial transcriptomics library preparation and sequencing
Visium spatial transcriptomics profiling was conducted in accordance with the 10× Genomics Visium CytAssist Spatial Gene Expression protocol. The FFPE samples were first deparaffinized using xylene and ethanol solutions in gradient concentrations. Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining was then performed, followed by obtaining high-resolution tissue images. Subsequently, the tissue sections were destained by incubating with 0.1 N HCl solutions at 42°C for 15 minutes. Next, de-cross-linking was carried out by incubating the samples in 1× de-cross-linking buffer (PN: 2000566, 10× Genomics) at 95°C for 60 minutes. Subsequently, probe hybridization was performed using the Human Whole Transcriptome Probe version 2 (PN: 2000657/2000658, 10× Genomics) at 50°C overnight, followed by probe ligation (ligation enzyme PN: 2000425, 10× Genomics) at 37°C for 60 minutes. The tissue slides were then restained with 10% eosin, and probe release was subsequently conducted using the Visium CytAssist system (10× Genomics, RRID: SCR_024570), with the probe release mix (containing RNase enzyme, PN: 3000593; tissue removal enzyme, PN: 3000387, 10× Genomics). Following a successful Visium CytAssist experiment run, probe extension was carried out (probe extension enzyme PN: 2000389, 10× Genomics) at 45°C for 15 minutes. The ligation products were then eluted with 0.08 mol/L KOH solution and underwent preamplification for 10 cycles using the Amp Mix B (PN: 2000567, 10× Genomics). Following this, purification and target enrichment were carried out with the Beckman Coulter SPRIselect reagent. We performed qPCR experiments to determine the optimal number of library amplification cycles. Library amplification was then performed using the Amplification Master Mix (containing Amp Mix B, PN: 2000567, 10× Genomics), with samples indexed with the Dual Index TS Set A (PN: 3000511, 10× Genomics). Following the amplification step, the library cleanup was again conducted with the Beckman Coulter SPRIselect reagent. The barcoded libraries for each sample were pooled together, with the concentrations proportional to the approximate capture area covered by tissue sections. The pooled libraries were then sequenced on the NovaSeq 6000 platform with S1-100 flow cells (Illumina, RRID: SCR_016387). All samples followed the same version of kits and protocol throughout the library construction process.

## Visium spatial transcriptomic data analysis
The raw sequencing files (FASTQ files) first underwent preprocessing using the 10× Genomics Space Ranger pipeline (version 2.0.0, RRID: SCR_025848), including demultiplexing, sequence alignment, and generating a spot-level gene count matrix. For sequence alignment, the human reference genome GRCh38 was used. Then, spot-level pathology annotations were carried out by an experienced pathologist. Downstream analysis of Visium data was performed using the R package Seurat (version 5.1.0, RRID: SCR_007322; ref. ). Spots with unique molecular identifier counts of less than 100, gene counts of less than 50, or a mitochondrial gene percentage greater than 25% were filtered out. Furthermore, spots that did not cover any tissue were also removed. Then, gene counts were normalized by library size and log-transformed. For visualization of gene expression on tissue slides, the function SpatialFeaturePlot within the package Seurat was applied ( ). Additionally, to complement the information loss due to the spot-level resolution of Visium data, super-resolution gene expression information was imputed with the iSTAR algorithm ( ) in Python (version 3.8.18), implemented with the wrapped script impute.py, which generated pixel-level gene expression data by taking into account spot-level gene expression data along with tissue morphologic features. Then, the scaled pixel-level gene expression data were visualized with the wrapped script plot_imputed.py within iSTAR.

## CODEX imaging
A 36-marker panel was applied for the CODEX assay (see Supplementary Table S1 for the antibody panel and corresponding RRIDs). Sections were processed for CODEX according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, FFPE sections underwent antigen retrieval using Dako target retrieval solution (Agilent, S236784-2) for 10 minutes in an Instant Pot in “High” pressure mode. Sections were bleached in 4.5% wt/vol H2O2 and 20 mmol/L NaOH in PBS for 60 minutes while exposed to white light. The CODEX robot was connected to a ZEISS Axio Observer 7 microscope (Carl Zeiss Microscopy, RRID: SCR_023694), equipped with a Colibri 5 light source, definite focus, and Prime BSI camera (Photometrics). A Plan-Apochromat 20×/0.8 NA M27 objective was used to collect 16-bit four-channel images. We used a 10 millisecond exposure time for DAPI and 300 milliseconds for ATTO 550, AF647, and AF750.

## CODEX image processing and data analysis
Cell segmentation was performed with PathML (https://github.com/DFCI-CODEX-group/pathml), which is based on Mesmer ( ). The DAPI channels were used for nuclear segmentation. Quantification of the raw mean fluorescence intensity signal was computed using the nuclear segmentation boundaries. Mean fluorescence intensity was z-normalized within each sample before aggregating all samples and performing Leiden clustering and Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection for Dimension Reduction. Annotations were validated by overlaying cell labels over the original images in TissUUmaps ( ).

## Results

## Patient/clinical characteristics and associations with residual disease
The SLL cohort included 95 patients with high-grade epithelial ovarian cancer ( ). Demographic and clinical characteristics for the entire SLL cohort and stratification based on SLL status are presented in . The majority of patients were White (82.1%) and non-Hispanic (90.5%) and had high-grade serous histology (87.4%). Approximately 92% of patients had confirmed advanced-stage disease (stage III or IV), whereas eight patients (8.4%) were either incompletely staged or had early-stage disease. The cohort was balanced with respect to first-line treatment (56.8% NACT vs. 43.2% primary surgery). Almost all patients (95.8%) had optimal cytoreductive surgeries [no gross residual disease (R0 cytoreduction) or less than 1 cm of gross residual disease (R1 cytoreduction)]. Most patients had HRD-negative tumors (58.9%) and lacked germline or somatic BRCA mutations (BRCA1/2 wild type; 80.0%). The majority of patients (n = 61; 64.2%) received maintenance therapy after first-line treatment, with 27 of 40 (67.5%) SLL-positive patients enrolling in a clinical trial; most of these patients (n = 25) received single-agent bevacizumab until progression (NCT02884648), whereas two patients received combination pembrolizumab, oral cyclophosphamide, and bevacizumab (NCT06083844; Supplementary Fig. S1).
 At the time of SLL, 42.1% of patients (40/95) had sMRD, with a median of 9.0 biopsies per case (range 3–18) and a median biopsy positivity rate of 0.24. On univariate analyses, there were significant differences between SLL-positive and SLL-negative patients with respect to first-line treatment (27.5% of SLL+ patients underwent primary surgery vs. 54.6% of SLL− patients; P = 0.012), HRD status (72.5% of SLL+ patients had HRD-negative tumors vs. 49.1% of SLL− patients; P = 0.048), and treatment after SLL (P < 0.001); all SLL+ patients received maintenance therapy compared with only 38.2% of SLL− patients, almost all (85.7%; 18/21) of whom had HRD-positive or BRCA-mutated tumors and received PARP inhibitors per standard of care. There were no significant differences between the two groups based on race, ethnicity, histology, stage, cytoreduction status, and BRCA mutation status ( ). On multivariable logistic regression, patients who received NACT compared with primary cytoreductive surgery for upfront treatment had higher odds of being SLL-positive (OR, 4.53; 95% CI, 1.65–12.44; P = 0.003). Patients with HRD-negative tumors were also more likely to be SLL-positive (OR, 3.55; 95% CI, 1.23–10.24; P = 0.019). Conversely, the status of initial cytoreduction was not found to be an independent predictor of sMRD ( ) although, notably, the majority of patients in the overall cohort (73.7%) had R0 cytoreductions.
 Among the cohort of 44 patients who underwent SLL and had ctDNA testing at the same time point, 90.9% of patients had high-grade serous histology, and 90.9% had confirmed advanced-stage disease. Additional patient and clinical characteristics for this cohort are summarized in Supplementary Table S2. Among these 44 patients, 50% of patients (22/44) had sMRD, whereas 34% (15/44) were ctDNA-positive. A significant association was observed between surgical and ctDNA-based residual disease status (P = 0.026). Among the 22 patients who were SLL-positive, 11 patients (50%) were also ctDNA-positive. Of the 22 patients who were SLL-negative, four patients (18%) were ctDNA-positive. Patients who were ctDNA-positive significantly differed from ctDNA-negative patients on univariate analysis with respect to first-line treatment (26.7% vs. 65.6% primary surgery; P = 0.025), BRCA mutation status (0% vs. 34.4% germline or somatic BRCA-mutated; P = 0.031), and treatment after SLL (P = 0.034; Supplementary Table S2).

## Association of residual disease with patient outcomes
In the overall SLL cohort (n = 95), detectable sMRD was associated with worse PFS (HR, 2.9; 95% CI, 1.7–4.9; median PFS, 7.4 vs. 23.8 months; P < 0.001) and OS (HR, 7.7; 95% CI, 2.8–20.9; median OS, 33.9 vs. not reached; P < 0.001; and ). It is important to highlight that the observed negative prognostic value associated with MRD positivity was despite the fact that 100% of MRD+ patients and only 38% of MRD− patients received additional maintenance therapy. Multivariable analysis of prognostic factors for PFS demonstrated HRD positivity as a favorable prognostic factor (HR, 0.27; 95% CI, 0.13–0.56; P < 0.001) and receipt of NACT as a poor prognostic factor (HR, 2.62; 95% CI, 1.34–5.13; P = 0.005; Supplementary Table S3). HRD positivity was associated with improved OS (HR, 0.11; 95% CI, 0.03–0.47; P = 0.002). Notably, SLL positivity was also found to be an independent prognostic factor for OS (HR, 4.40; 95% CI, 1.37–14.21; P = 0.013), indicating a 4.4 times higher risk of death for those with residual disease detected at the time of SLL ( ). Median follow-up for all patients was 23.7 months (range, 0.1–76.7 months).
 In the smaller cohort of patients who underwent SLL and had ctDNA testing at the same time (n = 44), sMRD was associated with worse PFS (HR, 3.1; 95% CI, 1.4–6.9; median PFS, 9.2 vs. 28.1 months; P = 0.003) and OS (HR, 9.9; 95% CI, 2.2–44.7; median OS, 29.0 months vs. not reached; P < 0.001). Furthermore, molMRD by ctDNA positivity in this group was associated with worse PFS (HR, 5.3; 95% CI, 2.3–12.4; 6.4 vs. 28.1 months; P < 0.001) and OS (HR, 3.6; 95% CI, 1.3–10.0; 32.4 months vs. not reached; P = 0.008; and ). Analysis of the four possible combinations of surgery and ctDNA outcomes, although limited by small numbers, demonstrates that patients who are SLL- and ctDNA-negative have the longest median PFS of 36.4 months, whereas those who are positive by both detection methods have a median PFS of only 6.4 months ( ).

## Feasibility and safety
The median time from the end of adjuvant chemotherapy to SLL was 43 days (range, 23–118). Patients had a median of 9.0 intraoperative biopsies (range, 3–18) obtained during SLL. There were two intraoperative complications out of the 95 surgeries (2.1% of cases), both of which were bowel injuries requiring conversion to laparotomy. The first case involved a thermal serosal bowel injury, recognized at the time of SLL, that required a partial transverse colectomy with reanastomosis. The second case was a 3 mm enterotomy in the transverse colon on laparoscopic entry in a patient with dense adhesive disease that was closed primarily. An additional patient required conversion to a laparotomy for a small bowel resection to remove gross disease identified on laparoscopy.

## SLL samples enable analysis of the tumor microenvironment and cell states in MRD lesions
In parallel with retrospective analyses of the SLL clinical cohort, we performed high-parameter spatial analyses of ovarian tumors to elucidate potential adaptations to first-line therapy that contribute to sMRD. We collected samples from seven patients (three sMRD-negative cases and four sMRD-positive cases) at three different time points: pre-NACT biopsies, interval debulking surgical (IDS) samples, and SLL biopsies (Supplementary Table S4). These samples were analyzed using GeoMx digital spatial profiling (DSP), Visium spatial transcriptomics, and CODEX spatial proteomic profiling on adjacent tissue sections ( ). This pilot feasibility investigation tested a complex workflow that required the acquisition of tumor samples from seven patients across three time points and four serial sections per patient, resulting in a total of 84 individual tissue sections for analysis. It also required pathology reviews (at multiple steps), quality control, and successful implementation of protocols for the three spatial platforms, including sequencing/imaging, data analysis, interplatform comparisons, and data integration. Ultimately, 81 of the 84 tissue sections (96.4%) were evaluable, demonstrating the overall feasibility of performing spatially resolved multiomics analyses on the precious, often small samples obtained during SLL.
 First, we analyzed the cancer phenotypic states of the four SLL biopsies. In one case, Pt-10, SLL was performed 4 months after IDS and 6 weeks after the last dose of chemotherapy ( ). Adjacent sections could be aligned with high fidelity using the tumor and stroma areas, as determined by the pathology annotation of the H&E images ( ), pre-GeoMx immunofluorescence ( ), gene expression of the tumor marker KRT8 via Visium ( ), and panCK protein expression via CODEX ( ). In addition, the H&E section showed reactive peritumoral fibrosis and scattered sites of vascular invasion in the surrounding normal connective tissue ( ). Furthermore, cancer cells were highly proliferative, as highlighted by the detection of Ki67 expression from both Visium and CODEX ( and ).
To assess the changes in phenotypic states of cancer cells over the course of treatment, we compared the transcriptomic profiles of tumor compartments between sMRD-positive SLL and IDS samples using GeoMx (Supplementary Fig. S2A and S2B). Notably, hypoxia signaling was enriched in SLL samples ( ; Supplementary Fig. S2A and S2B); this finding was further supported by the detection of hypoxia-related genes and transcription factors, such as HIF1A, VEGFA/B, NFKB1/2, and RELA, exclusively in the tumor region of SLL samples ( ; Supplementary Fig. S3A), as well as the expression of CA9 protein detected in the adjacent CODEX section ( ). In addition, we examined genes that show progressive changes across three time points in the tumor compartment of sMRD-positive cases using GeoMx data (Supplementary Table S5). As ATP-binding cassette (ABC) family proteins are known mediators of multidrug resistance in various cancers ( ), including ovarian cancer ( , ), we specifically examined their expression along the treatment course. In line with our hypothesis, multiple ABC family genes, including ABCA1, ABCB1, and ABCG1, were progressively upregulated and highly expressed in sMRD lesions ( ). This suggests a possible mechanism contributing to disease persistence and the formation of sMRD in high-grade epithelial ovarian cancer. These findings were corroborated by Visium spatial profiling ( ; Supplementary Fig. S3A). Additionally, we identified the expression of multiple other druggable targets in MRD tumor cells, including molecules involved in epigenetic modifications (DNMT1/3A, HDAC1/6), cell-cycle progression (CDK6), cellular growth (ERBB2, FGFR1, MYC), cell-cell adhesion (MSLN), and metabolic reprogramming (GLS, GPX4; Supplementary Fig. S3A and S3B). In summary, these analyses demonstrate the feasibility of performing complex spatial assays on tumor specimens obtained from SLL to identify mechanisms of chemoresistance and potential therapeutic targets.

## Tumor immune exclusion and immune suppression as potential mechanisms of disease persistence in MRD lesions
Next, we explored interactions among malignant and immune cells in sMRD lesions. In another representative case, Pt-19 (Supplementary Fig. S3A), SLL was performed approximately 4 months after IDS. Analysis of a peritoneal biopsy from GeoMx, Visium, and CODEX revealed significant intratumoral heterogeneity ( ). CODEX analysis revealed that CD8+ T cells were excluded from tumor islands in regions 1 and 2, but in region 2, a higher frequency of CD8+ T cells expressed PD-1, consistent with activation or exhaustion ( , columns II–III). In addition, peritumoral stroma in region 1 showed lower expression of fibroblast activation protein and the IFN-stimulated genes HLA-ABC and ISG15 ( , columns IV–V). Lastly, cancer cells in region 1 expressed lower levels of E-cadherin and higher levels of N-cadherin and SLUG, indicating that they were undergoing epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT; , column VI). Indeed, IFN signaling can inhibit the EMT process yet has also been linked to T-cell exhaustion in recent studies (p1; refs. – ).
 These findings were validated with the other spatial platforms. In an adjacent Visium slide, CD3D and CD8A signals were enriched around the EPCAM+ tumor region but did not infiltrate. These signals overlapped with the expression of multiple immune checkpoint genes, such as PDCD1 (PD-1), HAVCR2 (TIM-3), and TIGIT ( ). Although T-cell genes colocalize with EPCAM in region 1 in the Visium slide, the higher resolution CODEX data show that the tumor areas are small and interspersed with, but not infiltrated by, T cells ( , column II). Additionally, cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAF), as characterized by the expression of fibroblast activation protein, ACTA2, and TGFB1, were found to overlap significantly with tumor regions and closely surrounded the tumor core in region 2 ( ). CAFs have been shown to facilitate immune exclusion ( , ). Specifically, TGF-β, among all CAF-derived factors, plays a pivotal role in the process, and inhibiting TGF-β has demonstrated efficacy in reshaping the TME and enhancing antitumoral immune responses in preclinical studies ( – ). Furthermore, intense IFN signals were observed in region 2, which overlapped with CDH1, yet were mutually exclusive with CDH2 and SNAI2 signals ( ). These observations were corroborated with GeoMx. We profiled two regions with similar locations to those identified in CODEX and observed significantly higher expression of IFN-stimulated genes in region 2 than in region 1 ( ). Thus, the intratumoral heterogeneity in MRD lesions indicates that immune exclusion, EMT, and IFN signaling may be concurrent mechanisms of disease persistence.
In another case, Pt-67, a small intestinal metastasis was analyzed that was collected during SLL. With CODEX, abundant CD8+ T cells were observed in the tumor margin (region 1), but they were sparse in the tumor body (region 2). Similar to Pt-19, CD8+ T cells in region 1 also expressed high levels of PD-1 and IFN-stimulating genes (Supplementary Fig. S4A). The exclusion of CD8+ T cells from the tumor body and the exhausted state of T cells residing in the tumor margin were also demonstrated by Visium (Supplementary Fig. S4B). In sum, the microenvironment of MRD lesions from the pilot cohort was highly immune-excluded and immune-suppressive. These interactions among tumor and diverse nonmalignant cell subsets may be key determinants of cancer cell survival. Nevertheless, a larger cohort and further mechanistic studies are needed to fully elucidate the molecular mechanisms driving these interactions and the coevolution of cancer and TME cells during the sMRD disease phase.

## Discussion
The ability to assess a patient’s residual disease status at the end of therapy has numerous important clinical implications. Approximately half of patients with high-grade epithelial ovarian cancer who would otherwise be classified as being in clinical remission by imaging and CA-125 after initial treatment were found to have sMRD, an independent predictor of OS. This proportion was even higher for patients who were HRD-negative or who received NACT. Hence, our current counseling of patients with advanced-stage ovarian cancer who are in clinical remission after completion of first-line therapy is significantly limited by the low sensitivity of currently utilized clinical criteria. Surgical or molecular MRD status can be used, along with other important clinical markers such as BRCA/HRD status and receipt of NACT (which is largely a surrogate for significant disease burden at diagnosis), to counsel patients about their prognosis and inform the next steps of therapy, including participation in MRD-directed clinical trials.
Previous studies have examined the clinical implications of second-look laparotomy. A comparison of outcomes for women who underwent second-look surgery (laparotomy) on an adjuvant chemotherapy trial (GOG 158) for optimally resected advanced ovarian cancer showed that the performance of second-look surgery did not improve survival ( ). The current study is the first to reevaluate the utility of SLL, using a minimally invasive surgical approach, in the modern management of ovarian cancer at a time when there are many more and ever-increasing novel treatment options available. Previous studies have also been performed that aimed to characterize the clinical significance of ctDNA positivity in ovarian cancer. One study found postsurgery ctDNA positivity to be significantly associated with reduced recurrence-free survival; however, this study is limited by the inclusion of patients with early-stage disease, as well as the limited number of ctDNA-positive patients (n = 5; ref. ). A more recent study demonstrated that assessment of serial ctDNA at 3-month intervals for patients with ovarian cancer can detect disease progression earlier than conventional monitoring tools, such as CT imaging or CA-125 assessment; however, the median follow-up for this study was only 12 months, limiting our ability to correlate ctDNA with longer-term outcomes ( ).
Moreover, there are advantages associated with trials designed to target the MRD phase of ovarian cancer, including that patients only have microscopic or molecular disease burden, have received only one prior line of therapy, and there is at least the theoretical possibility of eradicating residual disease to increase cures. Our group has pioneered this approach by embarking on two such trials: The first (NCT02884648) investigates bevacizumab alone (administered until progression rather than a set number of maintenance cycles), and the second is a follow-up investigator-initiated trial (NCT06083844) that investigates the combination of bevacizumab, pembrolizumab, and low-dose metronomic cyclophosphamide, given its favorable outcomes in recurrent platinum-resistant ovarian cancer ( ).
Although not directly addressed by our study, another application of MRD status at the end of first-line therapy is its use as an early surrogate efficacy endpoint to derisk and accelerate first-line trials. Current reliance on PFS as the sole primary endpoint for first-line ovarian cancer trials makes these studies large, time-consuming, and extremely expensive, which in turn stifles innovation in the novel first-line therapy setting. As proof of the feasibility of using sMRD status as a surrogate endpoint, a randomized trial of NACT with or without intraperitoneally administered IMNN001 (NCT05739981) is currently enrolling and was the first trial designed to use the rate of SLL positivity as its primary clinical endpoint.
Beyond the use of surgical and molecular MRD as prognostic markers for more accurate patient counseling and risk stratification for additional therapies, the availability of sMRD biopsies from SLL provides a unique opportunity to study the biology of these persistent and chemoresistant cells to better understand how to therapeutically target and eradicate this residual disease. In this study, we observed the upregulation of the hypoxia signaling pathway in sMRD lesions and detected the expression of VEGF in MRD tumors from multiple cases. These observations suggest that although anti-VEGF drugs such as bevacizumab may be a useful backbone for maintenance therapy in the sMRD phase of disease ( , , , ), additional rational combinations and/or optimal duration of therapy may improve benefits for patients. In this vein, we identified multiple other druggable targets expressed in sMRD tumor regions, such as CDK6, GLS, MSLN, and ERBB2. Though multiple targeted drugs and chimeric antigen receptor T-cell therapies have been developed against these targets ( – ), their potential application in ovarian cancer warrants further exploration through clinical trials in the MRD setting. Furthermore, our results from the pilot cohort also allude to the fact that immune exclusion, as observed in sMRD samples ( ; Supplementary Fig. S4), may contribute to disease persistence. Therefore, modulating the TME could potentially provide another avenue for disease control in the sMRD disease phase. In line with these goals, our study demonstrates that SLL provides an unparalleled opportunity to gain access to sMRD tissue that may enable the development of personalized, biomarker-driven targeted therapies during a critical window when the cancer cell burden is at its lowest. Finally, based on our data, 50% of sMRD+ cases were molMRD−, indicating that tumor-intrinsic features may influence ctDNA shedding. Thus, comparing cancer cell phenotypic states in MRD lesions from molMRD+ and molMRD− cases based on larger cohorts may allow for a deeper understanding of MRD biology in the future.
In this study, we applied three different yet technically complementary spatial platforms, including GeoMx DSP, Visium, and CODEX, on adjacent tissue sections so that key findings from one data modality can be validated by the others. For instance, GeoMx DSP data provide a suitable avenue for directly comparing gene expression profiles in tumor or stromal compartments longitudinally or cross-sectionally, whereas Visium data have the unique strength of visualizing spatial distributions of features, as well as studying spatial interactions among cellular compartments. CODEX data, on the other hand, provide the highest spatial resolution and can be leveraged for validations of key markers at the protein level. The data we present here demonstrate the feasibility of applying the spatial multiomics approach in the ovarian cancer MRD setting, though larger cohort-level studies are needed to gain deeper mechanistic insights.
Although both SLL and ctDNA identified patients with low-volume residual disease and provided highly prognostic information, each offers unique strengths and limitations. SLL identified more patients with residual disease and provided access to tissue samples at this critical and previously undetectable time point when changes related to adaptive therapy resistance are most likely to be identifiable. However, SLL is an invasive surgical intervention that is impractical for serial assessment of MRD. Additionally, treatment decisions were guided by SLL status and could have affected its prognostic value. In contrast, ctDNA provided a less invasive means of molMRD detection with greater prognostic value for PFS. However, ctDNA was only positive in 50% of patients who had sMRD, indicating that a negative test should be interpreted with caution and should not be viewed as grounds for de-escalation of therapy in patients with advanced-stage ovarian cancer. Another advantage of ctDNA testing is that it can provide a quantitative assessment of disease burden longitudinally (bioRxiv 2024.08.21.609031). This opens the door for designing novel clinical trials in high-risk patients who have not yet developed measurable disease but have detectable ctDNA. An example of such an approach was the recently published AMPLIFY-201 study in patients with KRAS-mutated pancreatic and colorectal cancers who had completed first-line locoregional therapy but were positive for molMRD or an elevated tumor marker ( ). Although the follow-up for efficacy outcomes is limited in the initial publication for this trial, it demonstrates the feasibility and application of using ctDNA levels and clearance rates to monitor patients in clinical trials, which has now also been applied to trials in both muscle-invasive bladder cancer (NCT04660344) and high-risk breast cancer (NCT04567420; ref. ).
Limitations of the clinical portion of this study include a single time point for ctDNA analysis, a small sample size, and a relatively homogeneous patient population, predominantly composed of White, non-Hispanic, and insured patients. Additionally, the translational data in this study are limited by different sampling sites across time points, as well as the influence of interpatient heterogeneity. Despite these limitations, this study represents a highly innovative investigation and is the first to explore sMRD in the modern era of ovarian cancer therapy. We report, for the first time, the prognostic value of both sMRD and molMRD status in ovarian cancer, as well as the unique cancer cell phenotypic states and TME features in sMRD lesions.
These findings require further validation but hold the potential to guide future clinical trials and translational investigations in ovarian cancer. We envision a future in which MRD is not only detected but also targeted in a personalized manner, with the ultimate goal of eradicating it to improve survival rates for patients with advanced-stage ovarian cancer.
