It is puzzling to many observers that a complication of cancer and its treatment, known since at least the nineteenth century, continues to plague current cancer settings [ ]. Yet such is the case with venous thromboembolism (VTE)—including deep venous thrombosis (DVT) and pulmonary embolism—which remains both highly prevalent and highly consequential for contemporary individuals with cancer. VTE can be either a harbinger preceding cancer by months or a presenting symptom or sign of cancer. It can occur in inpatient or outpatient treatment settings and can complicate both surgical and medical treatments [ ]. In a systematic review of population cohort studies published in the last decade-and-a-half, the 12-month incidence of cancer-associated VTE among patients with unselected cancers was approximately 3% to 5%, and this represented a 9-fold increase in comparison with a matched noncancer population [ ]. The risk increased even further to 6% to 8% when evaluating only patients with advanced cancers requiring systemic therapy, representing a 20-fold increase over a matched noncancer population.
Even the latest, state-of-the-art treatments for cancer are not impervious to this age-old complication. The paradigm shift in cancer treatment away from chemotherapy toward immune checkpoint inhibitor therapy still exposes cancer patients to high rates of VTE (and its companion, arterial thromboembolism) [ , ]. Some targeted therapies, such as amivantamab-lazertinib directed against epidermal growth factor receptor–mutated advanced non–small cell lung cancer, are associated with an extraordinarily high VTE rate of 40% [ ]. When it occurs, VTE carries significant consequences for patients with cancer: it contributes to both short- and long-term mortality risk, can delay or interrupt cancer treatment, can lead to urgent care visits and hospitalizations, and overall adds substantially to the burden of illness in a population already carrying a significant such burden. The costs of managing this complication as borne by health care systems are also quite substantial. Despite this, there are no nationwide or even statewide registries of VTE. Epidemiologic studies therefore rely on a hodgepodge of reporting from retrospective cohort studies or nonstandardized data from prospective clinical trials [ , ].
Given this context, Maiocco et al. [ ] provide welcome and important new information regarding the risk and consequences of VTE in a contemporary nationwide study. The authors evaluated the codiagnosis of VTE and cancer in data from the Nationwide Inpatient Sample for the year 2021, comprising 28.4 million adult US hospitalizations; 2.9 million (10.2%) had cancer as a codiagnosis. Regarding the dataset, the Nationwide Inpatient Sample is an all-payer 48-state deidentified inpatient database, and it approximates a 20% stratified sample of inpatient discharges from over 5000 hospitals, covering 97% of the US population. Determination of cancer and VTE diagnoses are based on administrative coding methods; these have good but imperfect sensitivity and specificity for the actual diagnoses of cancer or VTE. The database does not allow for differentiating between VTE as a reason for admission vs VTE acquired as a complication of hospitalization. (Having VTE listed as a primary admission diagnosis is an imperfect surrogate for the former.)
With these caveats in mind, the findings reported by Maiocco et al. [ ] are as close to an analysis of a national inpatient registry as we can get in the United States. Of the 2.9 million cancer-related hospitalizations, as many as 234,090 (8.1%) had a codiagnosis of VTE. For context, the general rule-of-thumb is that 1% of all hospital admissions and 4% of all cancer-related admissions are complicated by VTE. Of the events reported in this analysis, 16.7% had VTE as the primary diagnosis suggesting (but not proving) that the large majority were hospital-acquired. Subpopulations of cancer suffered disproportionately from VTE. Confirming prior analyses, Maiocco et al. [ ] found that people with gastrointestinal, genitourinary, and gynecologic cancers had the highest rates of VTE (ranging from 11.4% to 17.5% per admission). Admissions of individuals identified as Black were also at greater risk for VTE (adjusted odds ratio [aOR], 1.29), although—reflecting US population demographics—two-thirds of those with cancer and VTE were White. Perhaps most concerning was confirmation in this contemporary cohort that VTE continued to be associated with worsened outcomes: admissions with cancer and VTE had higher all-cause mortality compared with those with cancer but without VTE (aOR, 1.61). The worse the clot, the worse the outcome: aOR for mortality was 1.41 for DVT and 1.88 for pulmonary embolism. All of these consequences were reflected in the bottom line as well: admissions with cancer and VTE codiagnoses were longer (median of 6 vs 4 days) and much more expensive ($70,416 vs $55,887 per admission; P < .001 for all comparisons) than those for cancer without VTE.
This brings us to the most puzzling part of the whole problem: prevention or the lack thereof. We have known for decades that VTE is preventable in high-risk settings [ ]. Yet, the only existing evidence supporting inpatient thromboprophylaxis in the cancer population are derived from subgroup analyses of randomized studies in the medical inpatient setting, and even, these are inconclusive [ ]. There has not been a single large randomized trial that demonstrates benefit of inpatient thromboprophylaxis in medical inpatients with cancer. A single pilot randomized study raises questions about the appropriate dose of inpatient thromboprophylaxis for cancer patients [ ]. In this study, patients were randomized to fixed-dose enoxaparin (40 mg daily) vs weight-adjusted enoxaparin (1 mg/kg daily) during hospitalization followed by screening bilateral lower extremity ultrasound. In the fixed-dose enoxaparin arm (the current standard), cumulative incidence of DVT at screening was 22% (90% CI, 0%-51.3%) This study highlights the ongoing uncertainty about inpatient thromboprophylaxis in hospitalized cancer patients and the urgent need for better evidence.
There is, thankfully, a much larger randomized trial–based evidence base in the ambulatory cancer setting where patients at high risk for VTE (as determined by a risk assessment tool) benefit from daily prophylaxis with rivaroxaban, apixaban, or a low-molecular-weight heparin for up to 6 months [ , , , ]. Widespread use of such thromboprophylaxis would have likely at least impacted the patients in the current analysis who presented with VTE at time of admission. However, there is a current failure of implementation science in this setting, driven by several factors. First, no current antithrombotic has regulatory approval for outpatient prophylaxis. Second, there is concern about bleeding, which is already high in the cancer population although the number needed to harm associated with outpatient thromboprophylaxis in randomized trials is low [ ]. Third, clinical workflows need to be changed to incorporate identification of at-risk patients and discussion of thromboprophylaxis in busy oncology clinics [ ].
Some of the concerns regarding collection of VTE data and preventing VTE are being addressed by current and planned projects ( ). Advocacy by the National Blood Clot Alliance in the United States and others has led to at least 1 state, Florida, passing legislation, which will create a statewide registry of hospital-acquired VTE. This could be a model for other states and, perhaps, even for a national registry in the future. In terms of prevention, lipid-lowering agents have been associated with reduced risk of VTE without concern for increasing bleeding risk [ ]. A planned National Institutes of Health–funded randomized trial, Statins to Prevent Cancer Associated Venous Thromboembolism (STAT-CAT), is evaluating the use of rosuvastatin 20 mg daily for up to 12 months in risk-adapted primary prevention of cancer-associated VTE (UG3HL176627). Factor XI is emerging as a safer, hemostasis-sparing target for anticoagulants. Factor XI inhibitors are currently being tested in a variety of settings, including the primary and secondary prevention of cancer-associated VTE [ ]. Successful infrastructure and workflow models are being developed in ambulatory cancer centers to enhance guideline implementation of thromboprophylaxis [ ].
 Much work has been done in the field of cancer-associated thrombosis in the past 2 decades but—as this article by Maiocco et al. [ ] highlights—much work remains to be done to reduce the public health impact of this complication of cancer and its treatments.
