The drug treatment of patients on dialysis is complex because the levels of scientific evidence are low; these individuals are systematically excluded from large, randomized controlled trials of cardiovascular therapy [ ].
The main indications for oral anticoagulation with vitamin K antagonists (VKAs) and direct oral anticoagulants (DOACs) are atrial fibrillation, venous thromboembolism and (for VKAs only) valvular heart disease [ , ]—all of which are highly prevalent among patients on dialysis. Indeed, in clinical trials, VKAs and DOACs have been compared in patients with atrial fibrillation [ ] and acute venous thromboembolism [ ].
In chronic kidney disease (CKD), a major thromboembolic risk is paradoxically associated with a major haemorrhagic risk [ ]. Hence, the prescription of oral anticoagulants in CKD patients is challenging, and the quest for a ‘perfect’ oral anticoagulant continues. Although patients on dialysis were excluded from the pivotal Phase III trials of DOACs, the results of three recent, small, multicentre, randomized controlled trials in dialysis patients with atrial fibrillation were published recently [ ]. Two of these three trials compared apixaban with VKAs: one was inconclusive (due to inadequate power) [ ], and the other (with 97 patients) did not find any differences in safety or efficacy outcomes [ ]. In the third trial, the composite outcome of fatal and nonfatal cardiovascular events and major bleeding complications was significantly less frequent among patients on rivaroxaban, relative to patients on VKAs [ ]. No clinical trials have been performed for the other indications.
Most of the retrospective cohort studies comparing these two drug classes were performed in the USA, where, in contrast to Europe, apixaban has been approved for the treatment of non-valvular fibrillation in patients on haemodialysis [ , ]. However, recent reports suggest that in Europe, DOACs are used off-label in patients on dialysis [ ]. Hence, it is important to collect data on the safety and effectiveness of these off-label prescriptions [ ]. In the absence of strong evidence from clinical trials, observational studies of routine clinical practice can provide insights into the effectiveness and safety of medications.
In the present comparative study, we sought to assess the thrombotic and bleeding risks associated with the approved use of VKAs vs the off-label use of DOACs in patients on dialysis. We took an opportunity to use data from the French Renal Epidemiology and Information Network (REIN) registry, which is linked to the French national healthcare system database (Système National des Données de Santé, SNDS) [ ].

## MATERIALS AND METHODS

## Data source
We used data from the REIN registry, which includes all patients on kidney replacement therapy living in France (including French overseas territories). Patients with a diagnosis of acute renal failure are not included in the registry. The REIN registry's structure and quality control procedures have been described elsewhere [ ].
Using an iterative, deterministic algorithm [ ], the REIN registry was linked to the SNDS database—a medical administrative database that covers 98.8% of the French population and contains anonymized data from two main sources: hospital activity (outpatient and inpatient hospital stays) and the reimbursed outpatient care procedures (such as drug prescriptions and consultations with physicians and other healthcare professionals) [ , ]. In France, patients undergoing dialysis treatment are fully reimbursed for health costs related to their kidney disease, including the drugs prescribed in this context, limiting some representativeness biases. The anonymous patient identifiers enable the linkage of information from a reimbursement database, a hospital discharge database [coded using the International Statistical Classification of Diseases and Related Health Problems, 10th Revision (ICD-10)] and the national death registry. The administrative data include sociodemographic information, such as the year of birth, sex and date of death. The pharmacy data comprise anonymous pharmacy identifiers and exhaustive claims for all reimbursed medications (including oral anticoagulants) dispensed in pharmacies (the substances, the quantities supplied, the prescription dates and the fulfilment dates).

## Study design and study population
We emulated a pragmatic clinical trial comparing the effect of initiating VKA vs initiating DOAC in dialysis patients aged 18 years or over. Explicit emulation of a target trial prevents common biases in pharmacoepidemiology studies, such as immortal time bias and prevalent user bias [ ]. The protocol of the target trial and its emulation are described in . Eligible individuals were dialysis patients aged 18 years or over who initiated a DOAC or a VKA between 1 January 2012 and 31 December 2020. The index date was the date of first reimbursement for an oral anticoagulant (a DOAC or a VKA). We excluded patients who were not matched or were mismatched with the SNDS, those with a reimbursement for an oral anticoagulant in the 3-year period prior to dialysis initiation, those lost to follow-up or who discontinued dialysis before the first reimbursement for an oral anticoagulant, those whose characteristics were not registered in the REIN registry before the first reimbursement for an oral anticoagulant and those under 18 years of age (Fig.  ). Therefore, new users of oral anticoagulants were defined as patients lacking a reimbursement for an oral anticoagulant prescription in the 3 years prior to dialysis initiation.
 

## Data collection and definitions
The baseline information from REIN registry included age, sex, dialysis modality, duration of dialysis, body mass index, primary kidney disease, serum albumin level, comorbidities (such as diabetes, heart failure, coronary artery disease, myocardial infarction, aortic aneurysm, stroke and/or transient ischaemic attack, cardiac rhythm disorder, liver cirrhosis, peripheral arterial disease, active cancer and respiratory failure) and smoking habits [ ]. Comorbidities, body mass index and laboratory test results are updated annually. We therefore analysed the last values to be entered in the REIN registry's database before the initiation of the oral anticoagulant. With regard to demographic variables, it should be noted that the French legislation prevents ethnicity from being recorded.
The baseline information extracted from SNDS included concomitant reimbursements for agents acting on the renin–angiotensin system, antihypertensive agents, beta-blockers, calcium channel blockers, diuretics, lipid-modifying agents, antiplatelet agents, proton pump inhibitors, selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors and heparin ( ). Data on a history of atrial fibrillation, valvular heart disease, pulmonary embolism and/or thrombophlebitis, and bleeding were collected by searching the patients’ ICD-10 codes recorded prior to the index date ( ).
A history of atrial fibrillation was defined as a history of cardiac rhythm disorder (according to the REIN registry), together with a history of atrial fibrillation (according to the SNDS). A history of thromboembolism was defined as both a history of stroke and/or transient ischaemic attack (according to the REIN registry), together with a history of pulmonary embolism and/or thrombophlebitis (according to the SNDS).
The follow-up data included (if applicable) the date of transplantation and the date of death (according to the REIN registry). The effectiveness and safety data were based on the hospital ICD-10 codes and reimbursement for oral anticoagulant (according to the SNDS).

## Study outcomes and follow-up
The primary effectiveness outcome was the occurrence of embolism (such as ischaemic stroke, systemic arterial embolism, systemic venous embolism, myocardial infarction and unstable angina), as determined from the relevant ICD-10-coded primary or associated diagnosis on discharge from hospital ( ). The primary safety outcome was the occurrence of major bleeding, which was defined as bleeding that required hospitalization and was determined from searching for haemorrhage-related primary or associated ICD-10 codes ( ). Bleeding events were then categorized as gastro-intestinal bleeding, haemorrhagic stroke, urogenital, intraocular, intra-articular, intracranial or pericardial bleeding, and other types of bleeding.
Patients were evaluated until the earliest of the following events: the date of the outcome of interest, death, kidney transplantation, discontinuation of dialysis or loss to follow-up. For the intention-to-treat (ITT) analysis, we classified the patient as having been exposed to the first drug initiated for the whole duration of follow-up, regardless of whether they discontinued or switched medications.

## Statistical analysis
Baseline variables were summarized as the median [interquartile range (IQR)] or the mean [standard deviation (SD)] for continuous variables and as the number (percentage) for categorical variables. The data were summarized for the study population as a whole and by the type of oral anticoagulant.
We first estimated the total number of study outcome events and incidence rates with their 95% confidence intervals (CIs). We used inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW) to control for baseline confounding. For each outcome, we estimated the probability of receiving a DOAC vs VKA as function of the baseline covariates by using a multivariate logistic regression model. Each model was adjusted for relevant demographic variables and baseline comorbidities that had been preselected after a review of the literature. Weighting was considered appropriate if the standardized mean difference (SMD) between treatment groups was <0.1. We assessed associations between outcomes and DOAC use vs VKA use by constructing IPTW-weighted, cause-specific Cox proportional hazards regression models.
In primary analyses, we adopted an ITT approach and analysed patients according to their initially assigned treatment group irrespective of discontinuation or switching. We performed predefined subgroup analyses with regard to age (<65 vs ≥65 years of age), the presence or absence of a concomitant reimbursement for antiplatelet agents, and the type of dialysis (haemodialysis vs peritoneal). Secondary analyses were performed in a subset of patients (i) with a history of non-valvular atrial fibrillation (n = 2501), (ii) treated with a VKA or with apixaban (n = 8769) and (iii) treated with a VKA or with rivaroxaban (n = 8637). We performed several sensitivity analyses. First, we restricted the thromboembolic outcome to ischaemic stroke and systemic embolism only. Secondly, for each outcome studied, we used cause-specific Cox models to estimate the crude and conventionally adjusted hazard ratios (HRs) (95% CI) associated with oral anticoagulant treatment. These models were adjusted for relevant demographic variables and baseline comorbidities. For all Cox models, the proportional hazard hypothesis was evaluated graphically using Schoenfeld's residuals. Thirdly, we performed an ‘as-treated’ analysis in which data were further censored at the time of oral anticoagulant discontinuation (i.e. no reimbursement for the oral anticoagulant of interest within 60 days of the estimated last day of pill supply from the previous drug reimbursement) and treatment switch, by repeating the primary analyses. This analysis mimicked the censoring that can occur in a per-protocol analysis of clinical trial data. Fourthly, we performed 1:1 propensity score matching by using the nearest neighbour (calliper = 0.1) method to adjust for confounding factors (SMD <0.1). Lastly, we restricted the main analysis to the latest available period of 3 years (i.e. between 2018 and 2020).
Multiple imputation with chained equations was performed to impute missing data on smoking habit, body mass index, serum albumin, diabetes, respiratory failure, coronary artery disease and/or myocardial infarction, aortic aneurysm, peripheral artery disease, history of thromboembolism, active cancer and history of cirrhosis (Table  ). All variables present in the final models were included in the imputation models (i.e. treatment, confounding variables, outcomes, and interaction terms between treatment and confounders). Predictive mean matching was used to impute the data. Thirty databases were created, and the number of iterations was set to 20.
 Our reporting complied with the STROBE (STrengthening the Reporting of OBservational studies in Epidemiology) statement [ ]. Statistical analyses were performed with SAS® software (version 8.3, SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA) and R software (version 4.0.5, Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

## RESULTS

## Characteristics of the study cohort
Among 95 617 patients initiating dialysis between 2012 and 2020, 74 002 were efficiently matched in the SNDS. Of these, we found that 59 055 patients had not made a reimbursement claim for an oral anticoagulant before dialysis initiation. However, 49 668 of these patients had not initiated oral anticoagulants between dialysis initiation and the end of follow-up. Hence, the final cohort consisted of 8954 patients: 8471 who initiated VKAs and 483 who initiated DOACs (Fig.  ). The proportion of DOAC reimbursements rose over the study period and reached a value of 12.2% in 2020 (Fig.  ).
 The patients’ baseline characteristics are shown overall and by type of oral anticoagulant in Table  . The median age was 73 years, and 63% of the patients were males. In comparison with patients who initiated VKAs, patients who initiated DOACs were older, had a higher BMI and a longer period on dialysis, and were less likely to be treated with antiplatelet agents and proton pump inhibitors. Furthermore, patients who initiated DOACs experienced fewer hospital stays during the year preceding the initiation of oral anticoagulant and were prescribed fewer medications. Regarding the history of cardiovascular disease, patients initiating a VKA were more likely to have atrial fibrillation (61% vs 56%, respectively), even though the frequencies of a history of bleeding were similar in the two groups (23% vs 20%, respectively).

## Comparative effectiveness and safety of DOAC vs VKAs
Over a median (IQR) follow-up period of 1.7 (0.8–3.2) years, 2567 patients presented a first thromboembolic event (systemic arterial embolism, 46%; systemic venous embolism, 34%; myocardial infarction, 9%; unstable angina, 6%; ischaemic/undefined stroke, 5%; ) and 1254 patients had a bleeding event (gastrointestinal bleeding, 36%; urogenital bleeding, 10%; haemorrhagic stroke, 7%; ). The overall incidence rates (95% CI) per 100 patient-years were 17.5 (15.3; 20.0) for thromboembolism and 7.3 (5.9; 8.9) for major bleeding (Fig.  ).
 After propensity score adjustment, the risk of a thromboembolic event was significantly lower in patients treated with a DOAC than in patients treated with a VKA [weighted hazard ratio (wHR) (95% CI) 0.66 (0.46; 0.94)] (Fig.  , ). A non-significant trend toward a lower risk of major bleeding events was found in DOAC-treated patients, relative to VKA-treated patients [wHR (95% CI) 0.68 (0.41; 1.12)] (Fig.  , ).

## Subgroup and sensitivity analyses
In general, we did not observe any major differences in wHR estimates between subgroups. However, the results of the subgroup analyses suggested somewhat greater effectiveness for DOACs in older patients and in those on haemodialysis (Fig.  ).
 The results of most of the secondary analyses were robust. Indeed, when comparing patients initiating apixaban (n = 298) vs patients initiating VKA (n = 8471), the wHR (95% CI) was 0.49 (0.30; 0.78) for the thrombotic risk and 0.50 (0.24; 1.04) for the bleeding risk (Table  ). When the analysis was restricted to patients with non-valvular atrial fibrillation, DOAC initiation was associated with a wHR (95% CI) (vs VKA initiation) of 0.36 (0.16; 0.85) for the thrombotic risk and a wHR (95% CI) of 0.50 (0.25; 0.99) for the bleeding risk (Table  ).
 When the analysis of the thromboembolic outcome was restricted to ischaemic stroke and systemic embolism only, DOAC initiation was associated with a wHR (95% CI) (vs VKA initiation) of 0.81 (0.58; 1.13) ( ). In the as-treated analysis comparing DOAC initiation with VKA initiation and during a median (IQR) follow-up period of 0.6 (0.3–1.3) years, 3657 of the 8471 patients on VKAs discontinued oral anticoagulation (43%) and 92 switched to a DOAC (1%). Of the 483 patients who initiated a DOAC, 178 (37%) discontinued oral anticoagulation and 62 switched to a VKA (13%). wHRs (95% CI) of 0.65 (0.39; 1.07) and 0.72 (0.36; 1.44) were observed, respectively, for the thrombotic and bleeding risks ( ). A propensity score–matching analysis gave the same results ( ). Similar but non-significantly lower risks of thrombosis and bleeding were observed when the analysis was confined to the period between 2018 and 2020 ( ). Multivariable adjustments gave similar trends, with an adjusted HR (95% CI) of 0.51 (0.39; 0.67) for the thrombotic risk and 0.60 (0.43; 0.85) for the bleeding risk (data not shown).

## DISCUSSION
The fact that patients on dialysis are excluded from large, cardiovascular disease–focused randomized controlled trials (such as those with DOACs) significantly limits the evidence needed to make clinical decisions. While awaiting evidence from trials, careful analysis of data from large observational cohorts might improve our understanding of effectiveness and safety outcomes. In the present study of a large, nationwide, incident cohort of new oral anticoagulant users on maintenance dialysis, the initiation of DOAC therapy (compared with the initiation of VKA therapy) was associated with a significantly lower risk of thromboembolism and a non-significantly lower risk of major bleeding. Our real-world study of patients on dialysis is one of the first of its type to have assessed the effectiveness and safety of off-label DOAC prescription (relative to VKA prescription) in Europe.
At the start of the study period, DOACs accounted for 5.4% of the oral anticoagulant treatments in France. This proportion rose to 12% by 2020, even though none of the four DOACs is currently approved by the European Medicines Agency for use in patients on dialysis. However, the US Food and Drug Administration approved the use of apixaban in these patients in late 2012, and so most of the observational studies in this population were performed in the USA. Indeed, Siontis et al.’s retrospective cohort study of Medicare recipients registered in the US Renal Data System found that DOACs were increasingly prescribed to patients with atrial fibrillation on dialysis and that 26.6% of new anticoagulation prescriptions in 2015 were for apixaban [ ].
For decades, VKAs have formed the mainstay of thromboembolic event prevention in patients on dialysis and who are eligible for oral anticoagulation. However, the thromboembolism risk reduction associated with VKAs was lower for patients on dialysis than for patients without end-stage kidney disease (ESKD), and there was even a potentially greater risk of bleeding because of VKA-associated platelet dysfunction [ , ]. According to data from clinical trials and from real-world studies, anticoagulation in VKA-treated patients on dialysis is suboptimal and is associated with a significantly greater risk of adverse clinical outcomes [ ]. Indeed, in the RENAL-AF trials of apixaban vs warfarin, the median time in the therapeutic range was only 44% in warfarin-treated patients; the latter were three times more likely to be subtherapeutic [international normalized ratio (INR) <2.0] than supratherapeutic (INR >3.0) [ ]. The difficulty of obtaining effective anticoagulation with VKA might explain our observation of a lower thromboembolism risk with DOACs than with VKAs.
The present safety analysis provides reassurance regarding the off-label use of DOACs in people on dialysis. Specifically, we observed a non-significantly lower risk of bleeding in the DOACs group, relative to the VKA group. The results of the major clinical trials that paved the way for the regulatory approval of DOACs in patients with atrial fibrillation (such as Randomized Evaluation of Long Term Anticoagulant Therapy (RE-LY) [ ], Apixaban for Reduction in Stroke and Other Thromboembolic Events in Atrial Fibrillation (ARISTOTLE) [ ], Effective Anticoagulation with Factor Xa Next Generation in Atrial Fibrillation–Thrombolysis in Myocardial Infarction (ENGAGE AF-TIMI) [ ]) show that DOACs were associated with lesser bleeding events. Furthermore, the trend towards the greater safety of DOACs has also been observed in various large cohort studies of medical-administrative databases [ , ]. Clinical trials of apixaban vs warfarin in patients with ESKD on dialysis failed to show differences but those was probably due to a lack of power [ , , ]. However, a retrospective analysis of a US healthcare reimbursement database indicated that apixaban was associated with a lower risk (vs warfarin) of major bleeding in patients on dialysis [ , ].
Given the potentially favourable risk–benefit ratio in dialysis patients, DOAC might be a good alternative treatment to VKAs. The burden of taking a VKA is not negligible for a patient on dialysis; the treatment is complicated by potential drug–diet interactions, the need for more frequent blood sampling for therapeutic monitoring, and an elevated risk of cardiovascular calcification and calciphylaxis [ ].

## Strengths and limitations
The strengths of our study included its large sample size, the representativeness of the population of patients on dialysis in France, its thorough design, and its inclusion of a variety of subgroup and sensitivity analyses. In contrast to previous real-world studies, and given that our objective was to evaluate the effectiveness and safety of off-label DOAC use, we did not restrict our analysis to patients with atrial fibrillation and addressed all the indications for oral anticoagulants. However, our analysis gave the same results when it was restricted to patients with non-valvular atrial fibrillation.
Our study had a number of limitations. First, and despite the use of an active comparator, a ‘new user’ study design and propensity score adjustment for a large number of baseline characteristics, we cannot rule out the presence of residual confounding factors. Secondly, our use of an administrative database prevented from knowing the exact indication for oral anticoagulants for each patient; however, by combining information from the SNDS and the REIN registry, we were able to define patients with atrial fibrillation and performed a sensitivity analysis on this population. Lastly, it is possible that our analysis lacked the statistical power required to demonstrate a significant difference between VKA-treated patients and DOAC-treated patients for the safety endpoint and in subgroup analyses.
In a study of a large group of patients with ESKD on dialysis initiating oral anticoagulation, we found that DOACs were associated with a significantly lower risk of thromboembolic events and a non-significantly lower risk of major bleeding, relative to VKAs. These results are reassuring with regard to the off-label use of DOACs and might facilitate the establishment of an expert consensus and guidelines on antithrombotic therapy in patients with ESKD.
