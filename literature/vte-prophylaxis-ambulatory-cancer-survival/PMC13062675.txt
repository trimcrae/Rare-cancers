Combination therapy is not routinely advised in guidelines on nonvalvular atrial fibrillation (AF) and for the management of venous thromboembolic disease.
 
 
Not only is bleeding risk higher, but patients on concomitant warfarin-aspirin have higher healthcare utilization than similar patients on warfarin therapy alone.
 

The six-center Michigan Anticoagulation Quality Improvement Initiative (MAQI
2
) has collected registry data on patients anticoagulated with warfarin since 2009. As detailed in a prior publication, between 2017 and 2018, each site of the MAQI
2
implemented an antithrombotic stewardship intervention that identified patients on combined warfarin and aspirin therapy.
 
The intervention focused on patients where there was substantial agreement at that time that aspirin would not be routinely indicated. When aspirin use without an apparent indication was identified, the managing physician was contacted and asked to determine if ongoing aspirin use was advised; the anticoagulation clinic at that site would assist with aspirin deprescribing if recommended by the managing provider. Data assessing the impact of this intervention was collected through December 2019 and analyzed in June of 2021.
 
The study results were published in September of 2022 and showed that after approximately 24 months postintervention, the MAQI
2
intervention was associated with a nearly 50% decline in aspirin use, a significant decrease in major bleeding events, and a nonsignificant decline in nonmajor bleeding events.
 

The financial impact associated with these clinical benefits has not been previously defined. In the current study, we sought to determine the insurance payments associated with episodes of major bleeding, nonmajor bleeding, and thrombosis for our study cohort. We then estimated the reduction in insurance payments associated with aspirin deprescribing through this intervention.

## Methods

## Patient Population and Stewardship Intervention

The MAQI
2
is fully described in previous studies.
 
 
Briefly, MAQI
2
consists of six outpatient anticoagulation clinics throughout Michigan, including academic and community practices. It includes both rural and urban sites with patient censuses in the hundreds to thousands. The registry consists of a random sample of warfarin-treated patients selected for inclusion. All patients entering the registry are new warfarin users. Monthly, each site receives a list of all new warfarin-initiating patients being followed by their anticoagulation management clinic. They randomly select patients to be entered into the MAQI
2
registry and followed longitudinally. Since 2009, the registry has followed over 10,000 unique patients with over 300,000 follow-up interactions.

For the antithrombotic stewardship intervention, each clinic site used an individualized screening process based on its own population and practice patterns to identify adults receiving warfarin for stroke prevention in AF and/or management of venous thromboembolism (VTE) who were also receiving aspirin without a clear indication. Specifically, the intervention was limited to adults without a history of coronary artery disease, myocardial infarction, any percutaneous coronary intervention, coronary artery bypass grafting, peripheral arterial disease, mechanical valve replacement, or use of left ventricular assist devices, taking warfarin for AF and/or VTE. Site-specific adaptation of the quality improvement initiative was allowed. For example, some sites further excluded patients with a history of stroke, heart transplant, or antiphospholipid syndrome. When potential aspirin use without a clear indication was identified, anticoagulant prescribers were contacted to determine if ongoing aspirin use was indicated, and if not, the anticoagulation clinic communicated this recommendation to the patient.

## Data Collection

At the time of MAQI
2
registry enrollment, patient demographics, co-morbidities, bleeding and thrombosis risk factors, history of bleeding or thrombosis, and concomitant medications were collected. Charts were then abstracted at approximately 3-month intervals to record changes in baseline data or adverse events until the time of patient discharge from the anticoagulation clinic, loss to follow-up, death, or the end of the study period. For this analysis, data were collected between January 1, 2010, and December 31, 2019. Data are analyzed by site based on site-specific intervention start dates, which were from 2017 to 2018. Patients both entered and left this registry cohort over time, creating a dynamic, real-world cohort. Given variable enrollment throughout the course of the study, monthly aggregated data were used as the unit of analysis.

From the previous study data,
 
event rates and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for major bleeding, nonmajor bleeding, and thrombosis were calculated preintervention and postintervention.

## Payment Data
For each included patient, the Michigan Value Collaborative (MVC) claims database was queried to determine if insurance claim data were available. The MVC is a Blue Cross Blue Shield of Michigan (a health insurance company) funded collaborative quality initiative that includes over 100 hospitals and 33 physician organizations across the State. MVC provides Michigan hospitals and physician organizations with payment and utilization data for an episode of care from paid, adjudicated claims. For this analysis, MVC provided us with insurance claims data from the following payer sources: BCBSM Commercial PPO, BCBSM PPO Medicare Advantage, Blue Care Network (BCN) HMO, BCN Medicare Advantage, and Medicare Fee for Service.

Insurance claims data for adverse clinical events over the course of the previous study
 
for MAQI
2
patients were identified by a perfect match based on clinical event admission date, discharge date, date of birth, and gender from the electronic health record (EHR). In addition, cost data from additional MAQI
2
patients, including those on warfarin for indications other than AF or VTE and patients with a potential indication for aspirin use (whether or not they were taking aspirin), were identified from the MVC database through the same methods (
 
). All reimbursed facility and professional payments associated with inpatient or emergency department (ED) claims during the study period were aggregated. Data for patients who sought medical attention at their primary care office, urgent care centers, or other facilities were not available and therefore not included. Patients who did not experience adverse outcomes and those with adverse outcomes that could not be conclusively matched to insurance payment data did not contribute to the payment data. Outcome categories with fewer than 10 events were also excluded from analysis to preserve patient confidentiality.

Median and interquartile range (IQR) payments were calculated for the following: bleed severity, location, and inpatient or ED status. Major bleeding was defined based on the International Society on Thrombosis and Haemostasis definition that requires a significant drop in hemoglobin, blood product transfusion, intervention to treat the bleeding event, or bleeding in a critical organ site.
 
All major bleeding events were assumed to receive inpatient treatment. Nonmajor bleed severity was variable, and therefore, the MAQI
2
registry was analyzed to determine the typical location of care for nonmajor bleeding events. This analysis revealed that for nonmajor bleeding events, 25% of patients receive inpatient care, 37% receive ED care, and 38% receive care elsewhere (outpatient care or patients do not seek medical attention; unpublished data). Patients who were seen in the ED and then admitted were counted in the inpatient care group. For the current analysis, nonmajor bleeding events were assumed to have received a similar proportion of inpatient care, ED care, or care at another site or self-care. Again, payments were only considered for inpatient and ED care based on data availability.

## Payment-Savings Estimates
To estimate the total payment savings per 100 patient-years associated with the aspirin deprescription intervention, we calculated the product sum of (1) the median payment per adverse clinical event, and (2) the estimated change in event rate per 100 patient years pre/postintervention. This was performed for major bleeding events, which were assumed to all receive inpatient treatment, nonmajor bleeding events, which were assumed to receive care at locations as described above, and thrombosis, which were assumed to have received inpatient care.

## Discussion

In this study, we report real-world payment data for bleeding and thrombotic events occurring in a cohort of patients on warfarin followed by six anticoagulation clinics in the state of Michigan. Adverse clinical events were associated with significant payments of approximately $13,661 for a major bleeding event, $11,678 for an inpatient nonmajor bleeding event, and $457 for an ED nonmajor bleeding event. Thrombotic events were associated with a cost of approximately $10,265 for inpatient-treated events and $1,758 for ED events. Taking into account savings for all types of bleeding and thrombotic events, our aspirin de-prescribing intervention was associated with an estimated total payment savings of $44,898 per 100-patient years. Extrapolating to the nearly 15,000 patients followed by MAQI
2
, savings annualized postintervention could be as high as $6,734,700 for these six anticoagulation sites. Presumably, these cost savings can be maintained over time with continued support of appropriate aspirin prescribing.

Other studies reporting cost data associated with aspirin-related bleeding in patients not taking anticoagulation are sparse. A U.S. study reported aspirin had higher per-patient-per-month clinical event costs ($381) compared with rivaroxaban use ($243 for therapeutic dose, $123 for prophylactic dose) in patients using either medication alone for secondary VTE prevention.
 
However, total health-care costs were higher with rivaroxaban due to the higher drug costs.
 
A small study from Australia in 22 patients on primary prevention aspirin projected $570,000 in annual state costs and $8,120,000 in annual national costs for admission for major gastrointestinal bleeding specifically attributed to aspirin use.
 
Patients in the UK taking aspirin without concomitant anticoagulation for secondary prevention of transient ischemic attack, stroke, and myocardial infarction, the average cost of first-time major bleeding events was $13,093.
 
This figure is very similar to the median cost of $13,661 for major bleeding events that our study yielded.

Further efforts should be made to understand the costs of adverse events among patients on anticoagulation and the potential cost savings of antithrombotic stewardship interventions. Such data can support the development and maintenance of these programs. Further studies could help understand what patient or management factors are associated with lower healthcare costs for bleeding or thrombosis. Our data also emphasizes the significantly lower healthcare costs associated with outpatient versus inpatient care. Further work to better stratify which patients can be safely managed outpatient for lower-risk episodes of bleeding or thrombosis could also reduce healthcare costs.

Multiple tools are available for aspirin monitoring, including the EHR, which has been found to be 85% accurate in representing patient use of aspirin,
 
and pharmacist-administered questionnaires related to antithrombotic medication use, which has been found to be feasible to administer in up to 70 to 80% of pharmacy patients.
 
In addition to this MAQI
2
initiative, several pharmacy-driven quality improvement initiatives have been successful in discontinuing aspirin in different populations, and these interventions often utilize a combination of EHR review by pharmacists and/or pharmacy students, patient interview, review of patient medical history, communication between pharmacists and patient care teams, and patient and prescriber education.
 
 
 
 
 
These studies reported inappropriate aspirin use rates in up to 42% of patients on direct oral anticoagulants (DOACs) or warfarin for VTE or AF, approximately 23% in patients on dual or triple anti-thrombotic therapy, 18.4% in patients on combined antithrombotic therapy, and up to 24% of U.S. Department of Veterans Affairs primary care patients over age 70. Furthermore, such interventions were able to achieve de-prescription rates of aspirin in as many as 45% of patients who were inappropriately on aspirin and anticoagulation,
 
30% for patients on dual or triple anti-thrombotic therapy,
 
and 76% of patients on potentially inappropriate dual aspirin and anti-platelet therapy.
 
Given the high potential risks of misapplied antithrombotic therapy (wrong drug, wrong dose, wrong duration, or a failure to optimize bleeding risk), there are numerous opportunities to improve care among patients on antithrombotic drugs. This is especially true given that bleeding risk prediction and stratification remain suboptimal.
 
Without being able to reliably predict the highest bleeding risk patients or those most likely to be harmed by aspirin with concomitant application, interventions will need to consider deprescribing for the entire population based on existing guidelines.

As of 2011, it has been estimated that up to 800,000 patients in North America are receiving dual therapy with warfarin and aspirin.
 
Based on our prior study and the work of others,
 
 
we would estimate that hundreds of thousands of patients in North America are likely on warfarin with aspirin without a clear indication for concomitant aspirin therapy.
 
The benefits of aspirin deprescribing would depend significantly on the patients being studied, with the magnitude of benefit being highest among patients at higher bleeding risk and without any apparent indication for aspirin. However, extrapolating from this study and considering that over one million patients are on warfarin in the United States,
 
aspirin de-prescribing efforts have resulted in significant cost savings while potentially improving patient outcomes. These results support investing in antithrombotic stewardship programs and efforts to optimize the prescribing, monitoring, and follow-up of patients on antithrombotic drugs. Efforts to improve the care of patients who suffer adverse bleeding or thrombotic events on antithrombotic therapy are also warranted.

Strengths of the study include chart abstracted data, robust follow-up by trained data abstractors, actual insurance reimbursement data from included patients, and the definition for “inappropriate” aspirin use was conservative, emphasizing a patient population more likely to benefit from aspirin de-prescribing. In the future, sites will be encouraged to expand patients targeted by the intervention to include other groups likely to benefit from aspirin deprescribing, like patients with peripheral artery disease and chronic coronary syndromes; we will consider aspirin deprescribing for DOAC-treated patients.

Our study has several limitations. By limiting the intervention to the patients most likely to benefit from aspirin deprescribing, it is not clear if similar payment savings would be realized by emphasizing guideline-concordant aspirin use for a broader population. While our intervention did impact a significant number of patients on guideline discordant aspirin, future studies should address the economic impact of an intervention that addresses all guideline discordant aspirin. Given that the intervention showed improved outcomes and reduced costs, we did not conduct a formal predictive cost analysis model. Therefore, we only provide an informal estimate of payment savings. Additional studies utilizing formal analytic models are needed to more precisely elucidate the true financial benefits of the practice of aspirin deprescription. We did not have payment data for all patients or clinical events, adding to the imprecision of the reported payment estimates. The matched patients were more likely to be male, older, more likely to be former tobacco users, and had a higher comorbidity index, potentially reflecting a higher cost population compared with patients who did not have matched payment data. In addition, it is unclear if payments for aspirin-treated patients could be different compared with patients not on aspirin. Determining this would likely require a larger study, potentially with randomized aspirin use. We also included payments from patients that would have been excluded from the original deprescription study.
 
This mostly included patients with a potential indication for aspirin and a lesser number of patients on warfarin for other indications. While including these patients likely improved the accuracy of the payment estimates with improved power, it is unclear if payments could be distinct for this group. As noted, we were not able to accurately account for the anticoagulation clinic's costs of the development, implementation, or maintenance of the intervention. These costs would likely vary significantly by site based on variations in the use of technology and personnel involved with the intervention. Overall, we anticipate that the personnel costs were low, as assessing aspirin use and deprescribing if appropriate added a limited amount of time to routine patient care. To accurately estimate payment savings, the costs of the intervention would need to be subtracted from the potential savings estimates. While our intervention was associated with reductions in insurance reimbursements and aspirin reduction, we cannot be confident about causation without randomization. The patients in this cohort were all followed by experienced anticoagulation clinics in Michigan that regularly participate in quality improvement activities and external funding, which limits generalizability. Furthermore, we only studied warfarin and not DOAC patients. Data on myocardial infarction were not well captured throughout the study period. Finally, the duration of follow-up of the study was relatively short (24 months postintervention), making it unclear about the long-term maintenance of the intervention. Overall, it would seem appropriate to confirm these findings in other populations.

Neither patients nor prescribing clinicians receive financial incentives for de-prescribing aspirin. However, Blue Cross Blue Shield of Michigan supported the stewardship intervention financially, most substantially through an audit and feedback mechanism facilitated by the MAQI
2
. The personnel payments for the initiation and maintenance of this intervention could not be calculated, as site-specific data on the added time spent on the intervention were not recorded. It is anticipated that personnel payments varied by site based on the different use of nurses, pharmacists, trainees, physicians, or other staff to implement the intervention.

## Conclusion
Reducing potentially guideline discordant or high-risk aspirin use in patients on warfarin in a six-center quality improvement initiative was previously associated with reduced bleeding outcomes without an increase in thrombotic events. The savings associated with the decrease in major bleeding events could be over $40,000 per 100 years of patient follow-up. This estimate suggests that improved antithrombotic stewardship is likely to result in substantial financial gain to the healthcare system; further research utilizing formal cost analysis methods is needed to more precisely quantify this expected benefit.

What Is Known About This Topic?

About one-third of patients on warfarin for atrial fibrillation and/or venous thromboembolic disease are on concomitant aspirin therapy, often without a strong indication.

The Michigan Anticoagulation Quality Improvement Initiative (MAQI
2
) implemented a multicenter quality improvement intervention in 2017 that significantly decreased aspirin use and was associated with reduced bleeding rates.

What Does This Paper Add?

We determined the pre- and postintervention event rates from our previous cohort study. We utilized insurance claims data linked to this cohort to estimate bleeding, major bleeding, nonmajor bleeding, and thrombosis payments.
This event rate data and payment data suggest that aspirin deprescribing reduces healthcare system payments while improving patient outcomes.
