## Highlights
•Post-operative direct oral anticoagulant (DOAC) use following laparotomy is safe and effective for VTE prevention.
•DOACs were highly acceptable to patients compared to low molecular weight heparin (LMWH).
•Patients taking DOACs reported less difficulty, required less help, and lower costs compared to LMWH.
Keywords: Venous thromboembolism, Gynecologic oncology, Gynecologic cancers, Postoperative, Surgical, Direct oral anticoagulants

## Abstract

## Background
Extended venous thromboembolism (VTE) prophylaxis is recommended for patients undergoing laparotomy for gynecologic cancers. The primary objective of this study was to evaluate patient satisfaction while our program transitioned from low molecular weight heparin (LMWH) to direct oral anticoagulant (DOAC) apixaban for extended VTE prophylaxis.

## Methods
Adult patients undergoing laparotomy for gynecologic malignancy in Alberta, Canada between April – December 2024 were considered for inclusion. The primary outcome was patient satisfaction, measured by the modified Anti-Clot Treatment Scale survey. Secondary outcomes included VTE incidence, major bleeding, and medication adherence.

## Results
111 patients were considered for inclusion; 50 patients completed the survey, and 40 patients had both survey and corresponding clinical data. All patients received pre-operative anticoagulation. Upon hospital discharge, 9 patients received LMWH, and 41 received DOAC. No patients experienced a VTE, and 1 patient on DOAC experienced a major bleed. Patients taking DOAC for extended VTE prophylaxis reported significantly less difficulty following anticoagulation treatment, less concern regarding cost of medication, and required less help compared to LMWH.

## Conclusion
The use of DOAC for extended VTE prophylaxis was associated with high patient satisfaction, further supporting its use in this context.

1.

## Introduction
Post-operative venous thromboembolism (VTE) is a significant cause of patient morbidity and mortality ( , , , ). Studies have demonstrated that post-operative VTE is a significant cause of mortality, and accounts for up to half of peri-operative deaths ( , ). In historical studies, the incidence of post-operative VTE in patients with gynecologic cancers was over 20%, with significant benefit of pharmacologic VTE prophylaxis in reducing the incidence of VTE ( ). The introduction of routine post-operative VTE prophylaxis and extended VTE prophylaxis (use of anticoagulation beyond admission following surgery) have further reduced the incidence of post-operative VTE in this population ( , ).
Multiple society guidelines including American Society of Clinical Oncology (ASCO) ( ), CHEST ( ), and Enhanced Recovery After Surgery (ERAS) ( , , ) recommend use of extended VTE prophylaxis for 28 days in patients undergoing major abdominal surgery for malignancy. Historically, this has been with injectable low molecular heparin (LMWH) preparations. LMWH can be associated with side effects of pain, bruising, and difficulties with injections; all of these can reduce compliance to extended VTE prophylaxis ( , ). One extended prophylaxis quality improvement (QI) initiative identified that 3 out of 4 patients receiving LMWH reported pain and bruising, and half of all patients reported that it negatively impacted their life ( ).
Direct oral anticoagulants (DOAC) are a class of oral medications that have emerged as an alternative to LMWH and Vitamin K antagonists ( ). In the context of post-operative VTE prophylaxis, DOACs were initially demonstrated to be safe and effective in the orthopedic surgical population ( , ). A randomized controlled trial compared the DOAC apixaban to LMWH (enoxaparin) for extended post-operative prophylaxis in patients with gynecologic cancers undergoing abdominal surgery ( ). The trial of 400 patients did not demonstrate any significant differences in rate of VTE (0.5%) or major bleeding (1–1.5%) between groups ( ).
Further retrospective studies and QI initiatives have also demonstrated the safety and efficacy of apixaban for extended VTE prophylaxis. In their QI initiative, Stewart et al. found no difference in rate of VTE or major bleeding between apixaban vs. LMWH for extended VTE prophylaxis ( ). They also demonstrated that apixaban was associated with less pain and bruising, which are important measures of quality of life. Health-related quality of life is a critical measure of high quality, person-centered care. The incorporation of patient reported outcome measurements through using validated tools is recommended in clinical practice as well as research by multiple organizations, including the Society of Gynecologic Oncology ( , ). However, there remains limited evidence on patient satisfaction and patient reported outcomes with DOAC use for extended post-operative VTE prophylaxis.
Given the growing body of evidence supporting the use of DOACs for post-operative extended VTE prophylaxis in the gynecologic oncology population ( , , , ) our centers elected to initiate a transition from LMWH (tinzaparin) to DOAC (apixaban). Prior to this transition there was low adherence to post-operative prescribed anticoagulation, usually in the form of LMWH, with patients not completing the full 28 days. This was brought to our attention by our pharmacy team. When further discussing with patients at their post-operative visit, they reported discomfort and difficulty with the daily injections, pain at the injection site, and cost of the medication being the top reasons for discontinuing the drug. The purpose of this QI initiative was to monitor the transition from LMWH (tinzaparin) to DOAC (apixaban) for patient satisfaction, safety, efficacy, and medication adherence in our local gynecologic oncology patient population.

2.

## Materials and methods
2.1.

## Study design and setting
This was a multi-centre quality improvement (QI) initiative using a post-operative survey design to identify gaps in patient satisfaction and care regarding post-operative anticoagulation as we transitioned from the use of LMWH to a DOAC (apixaban). The project was conducted at two tertiary care centres located in Edmonton and Calgary, Alberta, Canada. Both centres have high volume Gynecologic Oncology services and are ERAS® Centres of Excellence.
Baseline review of our institutional data demonstrated a high rate of discontinuation of the LMWH due to patient discomfort, difficulty with self-administration of the injections, patient cost, and patient understanding. Prior to initiation of the current QI initiative, all patients undergoing laparotomy for gynecologic cancer received pre-operative VTE prophylaxis with mechanical and/or pharmacologic prophylaxis. Furthermore, patients were discharged from hospital with a total of 28 days of anticoagulation with LMWH in alignment with ERAS and ASCO guidelines ( , , , ). Tinzaparin was the LMWH used in our institutions and was dispensed in pre-filled syringes. The aim of our QI initiative was to assess patient perspectives and preference regarding postoperative anticoagulation medication administration to address previously identified low adherence rates and suboptimal patient satisfaction, as measured by patient-reported survey results, during the transition from LMWH to DOAC. This manuscript adheres to the SQUIRE guidelines for QI publication ( ).
Adult (18 years + ) patients undergoing laparotomy for suspected or confirmed gynecologic malignancy between April 1 − Dec 31, 2024 were considered for inclusion. Patients were excluded if they had any contraindication to prophylactic anticoagulation, were receiving therapeutic anticoagulation for any reason, were at high risk of bleeding (including bleeding disorders, recent stroke/intra-cranial hemorrhage, platelets < 150 109/L, active or recent GI bleed, surgical bleeding), weighed > 125 kg or had a BMI > 50 mg/m2, or per provider discretion. The exclusion criteria were derived from prior studies of DOAC use in the post-operative setting ( , ); full exclusion criteria are included in Appendix A.

2.2.

## Intervention and implementation process
Prior to project launch, the gynecologic oncology providers and care team were informed of the initiative during a divisional meeting. Education was provided by the study team regarding the project aim, outcomes of interest, eligibility criteria, and workflow. The interventions involved in this QI process were two-fold. Firstly, a change in prescribing practices was initiated to facilitate DOAC prescriptions (rather than LMWH) for postoperative VTE prophylaxis. This was achieved following appropriate provider education regarding DOAC use in this setting, as well as engagement and buy-in into the value of the initiative. The second intervention was the distribution and collection of a patient survey designed to capture patient perceptions of the post-operative VTE prophylaxis process upon transition to DOAC use as standard of care.
In terms of the post-operative anticoagulation workflow, patients received tinzaparin for VTE prophylaxis starting 12 h after surgery and continuing until day of discharge, or at the discretion of the provider, as per prior local standards of care. Beginning April 1, 2024–the defined timepoint for transition to the use of a DOAC for extended postoperative anticoagulation–patients were prescribed apixaban 2.5 mg oral twice daily at the time of discharge for a total duration of 28 days of anticoagulation. To ensure adherence to the QI workflow, resident physicians, who are involved in direct post-operative patient care and medication orders, were given instructions on how to prescribe apixaban and to whom. The division pharmacists, as on-the-ground champions for this QI initiative, served to monitor and facilitate DOAC prescriptions, where appropriate.
The patient survey was developed by the multidisciplinary study team, including surgeons, residents and pharmacists, based on a modified Anti-Clot Treatment Scale (ACTS). The ACTS is a 15-item patient-reported outcome instrument of satisfaction with anticoagulation that has been validated in multiple patient populations and languages ( ). Each question is scored using a 5-point Likert scale with 1 being the lowest (not at all) and 5 being the highest (extremely) in response to each question. While the ACTS has not been previously used to evaluate post-operative prophylactic anticoagulation, this instrument was chosen because of its widespread applicability and attention to both the burdens and benefits of anticoagulation. The ACTS was modified to remove questions not applicable to the post-operative population; for example, removing the question of not participating in vigorous physical activity due to anticoagulation, as participants would not be doing this due to post-operative restrictions. We added a question about requiring help with anticoagulation, as we hypothesized (based on baseline assessments) that participants taking apixaban would require less help than those on tinzaparin. The patient survey also included 4 open-ended questions to elicit information on what aspects of anticoagulation treatment were most bothersome, medication adherence, and side effects. The survey is listed in Appendix B. Patients were provided with the paper survey at the time of discharge or at their post-operative follow-up appointment. Participation was voluntary with identifiable responses, linking survey responses to patient charts for assessment.
The division pharmacists, as part of the study team with a regular presence on the post-operative ward, assisted in tracking patient accrual and ensured eligible patients were approached consistently. An in-service was provided to the post-operative ward nurses regarding the distribution of the patient survey at the time of discharge teaching. Additionally, an in-service was provided to the outpatient nursing team to ensure collection of completed patient surveys at post-operative clinic visits. A running log of eligible patients and completed surveys was maintained to monitor participation rates. Ongoing evaluation of project implementation occurred on a bi-weekly basis. The study team reviewed: the number of surveys completed, response rates, barriers to survey administration and provider adherence to the workflow.

2.3.

## Outcomes
The primary outcome was patient satisfaction as measured by the modified Anti-Clot Treatment Scale survey. Secondary outcomes (process measures) included proportion of patients experiencing a VTE, proportion of patients with major bleeding after discharge from hospital (using the International Thrombosis and Hemostasis definition ( ), and patient-reported medication adherence.

2.4.

## Data collection
Patient data was abstracted from the electronic medical record ConnectCare (Epic ©) and included patient characteristics (age, BMI, weight (kg), smoking status, comorbidities, prior VTE), cancer diagnosis (cervical, uterine, ovarian/fallopian tube/peritoneal, vulvar/vaginal, other), stage, operative details (length, estimated blood loss, pre-operative anticoagulation, procedures performed, anticoagulation prescribed at discharge, major bleeding, and post-operative VTE. Charlson Comorbidity Index was calculated based on comorbidities reported at initial consultation ( ), and the Aletti Surgical Complexity Score was calculated based on the operative reports ( ). For patients who completed the survey anonymously, the information on anticoagulation prescribed at discharge was self-reported, and for patients with corresponding clinical data this was confirmed in the electronic medical record.

2.5.

## Data analysis
All data from this QI initiative were reported in aggregate, to protect patient confidentiality. Descriptive statistics were performed and data presented as proportions, means with standard deviation for normally distributed variables, and medians with interquartile ranges for non-normally distributed variables. Median patient satisfaction scores were compared using Exact Wilcoxon Rank Sum test. Statistical analysis was performed by SAS software (Version 9.4). The patient responses to the open ended questions from the survey were summarized. Ethics approval was received from the Health Research Ethics Board of Alberta – Cancer Committee (HREBA.CC-23–0385).

3.

## Results
Between Apr 1- Dec 31, 2024, 111 eligible patients were considered for inclusion. Of those, 50 patients completed the patient-reported survey, for a survey response rate of 45%. For 10 of the patients, the survey results were anonymous and the corresponding clinical data could not be identified, leaving 40 patients with both complete clinical and survey data.
Patient characteristics and clinical factors are described in for the 40 patients with both clinical and survey data. The mean age was 59.8 years (SD 12.2 years), median BMI of 28.6 mg/m2 (IQR 25.3–33.6), and mean weight of 75.1 kg (SD 21.8 kg). The majority of patients had ovarian/fallopian tube/primary peritoneal cancer (n = 23), followed by uterine cancer (n = 8), and cervical cancer (n = 4). Most patients were ASA Class 2 (n= 18) or 3 (n= 16). The cohort had a median Charlson Comorbidity Index score of 5.4. The most common comorbidities were hypertension (n = 12), diabetes (n = 4), and being a current smoker (n = 5). Three patients had a prior history of VTE.
 All patients underwent laparotomy. Surgical characteristics are outlined in . The mean operative duration was 129.05 min. The most common estimated blood loss was < 500 mL, with 22 patients (55%) ranging between 100–500 mL, and 11 patients (27.5%) with < 100 mL. Only 3 patients had > 1000 mL of estimated blood loss. All patients (n = 40) received pre-operative anticoagulation with LMWH or unfractionated heparin, and all patients used intra-operative sequential compression devices on the legs for mechanical prophylaxis. Ten patients had Aletti surgical complexity of intermediate/high complexity with a score of 4 or greater.
 Including the anonymous surveys with self-reported data, upon discharge from hospital 9 patients received LMWH (tinzaparin) and 41 patients received a DOAC (apixaban) for extended VTE prophylaxis. No patients were diagnosed with a VTE between the date of surgery and their post-operative follow up, 4–6 weeks following their surgery. One patient on apixaban experienced a major bleed, with a drop in hemoglobin of > 20 points (g/L) after discharge from hospital and prior to post-operative follow up. Upon further chart review, this patient did not require hospitalization or treatment as a result of the drop in hemoglobin.
The patient satisfaction survey results are outlined in . Most patients reported that taking anticoagulation was not time consuming (median score of 1), nor a burden (median score of 1). There were significant differences between the DOAC and LMWH groups with respect to difficulty following anticoagulation treatment (median score of 1 for DOAC vs. 2 for LMWH, p = 0.0184), cost of medication (median score of 1 for DOAC vs. 2 for LMWH, p = 0.0157), and how much of a hassle/inconvenience the anticoagulation was (median score of 1 for apixaban vs. 2 for tinzaparin, p = 0.0253). The most significant differences between groups were in how much help patients required with administering anticoagulation; the median score for the DOAC group was 1, while the median score of the LMWH group was 4 (p < 0.0001).
 Four patients (7.8%) reported missing one or more doses of medication, all of which were due to forgetting. All 4 patients were prescribed DOAC, with 2/4 commenting on difficulties remembering to take the second pill each day. Five patients reported side effects from their medication; nose bleed (1), bruising (1), headache (1), reflux (1), and not specified (1).

4.

## Discussion
In this study, the use of DOAC (apixaban) for post-operative extended VTE prophylaxis was highly acceptable to patients. Patients using DOAC required less help with anticoagulation, reported less difficulty and hassle, and lower medication costs compared to LMWH (tinzaparin). No patients experienced a VTE, and only one patient experienced a decrease in hemoglobin, not requiring hospital admission or intervention.
To our knowledge, this is one of few studies contributing to the literature on patient satisfaction and patient-reported outcomes in the context of DOAC use for extended VTE prophylaxis in gynecologic oncology. Guntupalli et al. reported patient satisfaction as a secondary outcome of their randomized controlled trial of DOAC (apixaban) vs. LMWH (enoxaparin) ( ). In addition to completing the SF-8 quality of life health survey, they asked patients three satisfaction questions: was the medication easy to take, did the patient have difficulty remembering to take the medication, and was there any pain associated with taking the medication. Similar to our findings, they reported greater ease of taking medication for the apixaban group, compared to enoxaparin. Furthermore, patients taking apixaban reported less pain compared to enoxaparin. Stewart et al. describe lower patient-reported pain and bruising with apixaban compared to LMWH in their QI initiative ( ). Improved patient satisfaction with DOAC compared to LMWH is well described in other areas including VTE treatment, further supporting its use for extended prophylaxis ( ).
An additional benefit of using apixaban for extended VTE prophylaxis is cost savings for patients. A 2020 study reported that apixaban was more cost-effective compared to enoxaparin, even when accounting for variations in published VTE event rates and medication adherence ( ). In the Canadian context, Stewart et al. reported a mean out-of-pocket savings of $136-per-patient ( ). Given that medication costs and coverage are highly variable, further Canadian cost effectiveness data are needed. While overall apixaban appears to be more cost-effective than LMWH, in our practice patients who do not have insurance coverage must pay entirely out-of-pocket for medication expenses can sometimes be eligible for a compassionate supply of LMWH. This could indicate potential inequities in post-operative VTE prophylaxis depending on socioeconomic status, however further research in this area is needed.
Our study has several strengths. This was a province-wide initiative, as the cancer centres in Edmonton and Calgary provide gynecologic oncology care to the entire province of Alberta (as well as parts of the Northwest Territories and British Columbia), serving a diverse patient population. This study included a fairly heterogenous patient population, including patients with ovarian, uterine, and cervical cancers, as well as ¼ of the population undergoing intermediate to high complexity surgery. These results are likely highly generalizable. Because of the province-wide electronic medical record system in Alberta, it is unlikely that any VTE or major bleeding events were not captured in our data set.
Limitations of this study include the small sample size of patients included. Patients who were ineligible for post-operative DOAC were not tracked, so we do not have data on what proportion of all laparotomy patients received a DOAC. Furthermore, 9 patients during the QI implementation period received LMWH as opposed to DOAC; the reasons for this could include non-adherence during the transition to DOAC, patient or provider preference, or cost of medication. It is possible that differences between patients who were prescribed LMWH and those prescribed DOAC may have biased the survey responses.
Our survey response rate was 45%, increasing the risk of participation bias. Patients who are not represented in the sample may have systematically differing views or satisfaction with their anticoagulation, compared to those who participated in the survey. The surveys were completed at the patient post-operative appointment, typically scheduled between 4–6 weeks following surgery. There could be concerns with recall of details including missed medications. Self-reported medication adherence has disadvantages including risks of recall bias and inaccurate reporting due to social desirability; therefore there is a risk that patients may have under-reported poor medication adherence ( ). The study by Stewart et al. reported a medication adherence of greater than 90% for patients taking apixaban post-operatively; however they defined medication adherence as missing less than 2 days of medication ( ). If we applied this definition to the current study, our adherence was 100%, as all patients reported missing 1–2 doses only. While the small sample size limits the ability to draw significant conclusions about DOAC and medication adherence in the post-operative setting, it is worth noting that all patients in our study who reported missing a dose of medication were prescribed apixaban. The most common reason for missing medication was reported as missing the second dose of the day, as apixaban is twice daily dosing. Future research should consider investigating whether a once daily DOAC would be preferable to patients.

5.

## Conclusions
This provincial QI initiative demonstrated that transitioning to DOAC (apixaban) for extended VTE prophylaxis for patients undergoing laparotomy for gynecologic cancer surgery is feasible, effective, and acceptable to patients. Patients receiving DOAC reported requiring less help with medication administration, less difficulty following the medication regimen, reduced cost burden of medication, and that the medication was more convenient compared to LMWH.
