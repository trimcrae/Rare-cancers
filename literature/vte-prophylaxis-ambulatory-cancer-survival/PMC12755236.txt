## Abstract

## Background
Venous thromboembolism (VTE) affects >1.2 million Americans annually. Although the clinical outcomes and economic burdens of VTE have been well described, the impact of VTE on patients' health status has yet to be summarized. This systematic review summarizes how patient‐reported outcome measures (PROMs) have been used in VTE to date.

## Methods and Results
PubMed/MEDLINE was queried for literature published through March 2023 using PROMs in a population of patients with VTE. Studies were excluded if the reference was an editorial, review, or case report, or if the study included patients with conditions other than VTE. Qualitative analyses were performed. After screening and exclusion, 136 references were identified; 5 described PROM development, 20 focused on PROM validation, and 111 used PROMs in outcomes research. The most used generic PROMs were the 36‐item Short‐Form Health Survey and EuroQol 5‐dimensional questionnaire, and the most common disease‐specific PROMs were the Venous Insufficiency Epidemiological and Economic Study–Quality of Life/Symptoms and the Pulmonary Embolism Quality of Life Questionnaire. PROMs were used to quantify the changes in health status after diagnosis, characterize the trajectory of subsequent improvement, and identify drivers of continued impairments in health status like postthrombotic syndrome and postpulmonary embolism syndrome. PROMs were also used to investigate the impact of novel treatment modalities on quality of life.

## Conclusions
This review demonstrates the many benefits of PROM use, including quantifying changes in health status with treatment, capturing patients' experiences with the treatment itself, and identifying complications of VTE. Incorporating PROMs into VTE care will be an essential component of evaluating the effectiveness of novel therapies and should lead to improved shared decision‐making for patients with VTE.

## Research Perspective

## What Is New?

To the authors' knowledge, this is the first comprehensive systematic review to describe the generic and disease‐specific patient‐reported outcome measures available for use in venous thromboembolism (eg, the 36‐item Short‐Form Health Survey, EuroQol 5‐dimensional questionnaire, Venous Insufficiency Epidemiological and Economic Study–Quality of Life/Symptoms, and Pulmonary Embolism Quality of Life Questionnaire) and to evaluate how they have been used to in the venous thromboembolism literature to date.
Using these data, this review demonstrates many of the important benefits to the use of patient‐reported outcomes including quantifying the changes in health status patients experience after initial diagnosis, examining the trajectory of subsequent improvement, and identifying drivers of continued impairments in health status, setting the stage for how we can use patient‐reported outcomes in both clinical practice and research moving forward.

## What Questions Should Be Addressed Next?

Future research is needed to expand the validation of the available disease‐specific patient‐reported outcome measures to additional languages and cultures and further delineate what constitutes a minimum clinically important difference in score for each of the tools available.

Venous thromboembolism (VTE), which encompasses both deep vein thrombosis (DVT) and pulmonary embolism (PE), affects >1.2 million Americans annually and is a leading cause of cardiovascular morbidity and mortality worldwide. The adverse clinical outcomes and economic burdens of this disease have been well described, with 30‐day all‐cause mortality rates of up to 10% and associated health care costs reaching $10 billion in the United States each year alone. , , What is less well described so far is the impact of VTE on patients' health status, including changes in their functional capacity, symptoms, and quality of life (QoL).
At the time of their initial presentation, patients with DVT experience varying levels of lower extremity erythema, pain, and swelling associated with reductions in venous outflow from the leg. In those with PE, shortness of breath and pleurisy are also often present. These symptoms result in reductions in mobility and exercise tolerance and limit patients' ability to carry out their activities of daily living. After the initial presentation and management with anticoagulation, limitations frequently persist. In addition to clinical complications of bleeding and VTE recurrence, up to 50% of patients will experience some form of postthrombotic syndrome (PTS) or post‐PE syndrome (PPES), and a smaller proportion will develop chronic thromboembolic pulmonary hypertension (CTEPH). , , , , , Many patients will also develop complications relating to their mental health including anxiety, depression, and posttraumatic stress disorder. These complications can lead to functional limitations or reductions in QoL that persist for years after the initial event. Patient‐reported outcomes (PROs), which are increasingly used in other domains of cardiovascular medicine, provide a framework for capturing these important domains of health by incorporating direct input from patients regarding their health status. , The use of these tools represents an important opportunity to holistically evaluate outcomes after PE and will be a critical component in assessing the value of different PE interventions in the future. , 

The goal of this article is to review the patient‐reported outcome measures (PROMs) available for use in VTE and to describe how they have been used in the literature thus far. This information will be used to set the stage for how PROs can be incorporated into clinical practice and research in VTE moving forwards.

## A Brief Primer on PROs and Relevant Terminology
PROs were first introduced into outcomes assessment in the United States in the early 1980s and refer to any information regarding a health outcome obtained directly from the patient that has not been modified by the health care provider. In the case of VTE, this may refer to self‐reported leg pain or shortness of breath or more generalized symptoms such as fatigue, anxiety, or depression. PROMs refer to the instruments used to evaluate the status of a patient's health condition. These instruments commonly take the form of a questionnaire and are administered in a standardized manner to facilitate comparison not only for a single patient over time but also across patients and populations. PROMs can either be generic or specific to the disease or treatment. Generic PROMs assess health status measures that are applicable to patients across a wide range of conditions and settings, such as mobility or the ability to carry out one's usual daily activities. Disease‐specific PROMs, on the other hand, focus on items that are particularly relevant to a specific condition, such as the severity of leg pain or swelling in a patient with a chronic venous disorder of the lower extremity. These PROMs tend to be more sensitive to change over time and generally provide more actionable results. As PROMs have increased in prevalence in other specialties of medicine, their use as performance measures has also increased. When employed in this setting, they are referred to as PRO performance measures. 

## Methods

## Search Strategy
This review was performed according to Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Review and Meta‐Analysis guidelines and was registered with the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (ID: 427124). , The institutional review board of Columbia University Irving Medical Center exempted this study from the requirement for approval or informed consent.
The primary search was conducted using PubMed/MEDLINE including literature published through March 14, 2023. Previous research has shown that this database has sufficient coverage and functionality to serve as a principal database for systematic reviews. The following search strategy was used: (Venous Thromboembolism or VTE or Deep Vein Thrombosis or DVT or Pulmonary Embolism or Pulmonary Embolus or PE) AND (Patient‐reported outcome or Patient‐Reported Outcome Measure or Patient‐reported outcome performance measure). The initial search was intended to be broad and capture various forms of VTE research that incorporate PROMs. Abstracts were then screened to identify the individual PROMs being used. Separate PROM‐specific searches were then conducted to identify additional relevant references by replacing the text (Patient‐reported outcome or Patient‐Reported Outcome Measure or Patient‐reported outcome performance measure) with the specific name of each PROM and any common abbreviations (eg, Pulmonary Embolism‐QoL or PEmb‐QoL).

## Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria
Inclusion criteria were prespecified before screening and included (1) a study population of patients with DVT, PE, or both and (2) 1 or more PROMs used in outcomes assessment. Studies describing PROM development or validation were also included. Studies were excluded if (1) the reference was an editorial, commentary, systematic review, study protocol, case report, or case series; (2) the study population included patients with conditions other than VTE; (3) the paper was not available in English; or (4) the paper was retracted. Review articles were used for reference identification but were not included in the analysis.
As depicted in the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Review and Meta‐Analysis diagram ( ), 140 records were identified with the initial search strategy and exported to EndNote; 558 additional records were added after PROM‐specific searches. After duplicates were removed (n=123), 575 records were available for screening. Abstracts and titles were screened independently by 2 authors (D.S. and R.Z.). Arbitration of disagreements was performed by a third author (S.S.). Following the screening, 199 records remained for full‐text assessment. During the full‐text assessment, 63 records were excluded, leaving 136 references for data extraction.
 

## Data Collection
Two investigators (D.S. and R.Z.) independently extracted the data from each reference. Arbitration by a third author (S.S.) was available but not required. For studies regarding the development or validation of each PROM, the following data were collected: instrument name, PROM type (eg, generic or disease specific), domains assessed, response format, number of items, scoring format, mode of administration, completion time, and frequency of use. For studies using PROMs in outcomes assessment, the following variables were extracted: primary author, trial name, trial design, PROM used, study population, comparison or association of interest, follow‐up period, and overall outcome.

## Statistical Analysis
Given the descriptive nature of this study, qualitative rather than quantitative analyses were used. A risk of bias assessment was deferred given the results of the study were primarily descriptive.

## Generic PROMs

The 2 most commonly used generic PROMs were the 36‐Item Short‐Form Survey (SF‐36) and the EuroQol 5‐dimensional questionnaire (EQ‐5D; see Table  ). The SF‐36 is a well‐validated 36‐item self‐completed questionnaire that evaluates health status in 8 different domains: vitality, bodily pain, general health perceptions, mental health, physical functioning, emotional role functioning, social role functioning, and physical role functioning. , It is available in 29 different languages and has a shorter 12‐question format (the SF‐12) that replicates the results of the SF‐36 well while taking less than half the time to complete. The EQ‐5D is a shorter 6‐item self‐completed questionnaire that evaluates health status in 5 dimensions: mobility, self‐care, usual activities, pain and discomfort, and anxiety and depression. , The survey is available in 3 different versions (eg, the EQ‐5D‐5 Level, the EQ‐5D‐3 Level, and the EQ‐5D‐Youth), >170 languages, and has been validated for use in VTE. , , The Patient‐Reported Outcomes Measurement Information System (PROMIS) physical function 6b was the only other generic PROM used. Although the PROMIS suite is used commonly in other conditions, to date, it has been used in only 1 study of VTE. 

 

## Disease‐Specific PROMs

The 2 most commonly used disease‐specific PROMs were the Venous Insufficiency Epidemiological and Economic Study–Quality of Life/Symptoms (VEINES‐QoL/Sym) and the Chronic Venous Disease Quality of Life Questionnaire (CIVIQ‐20; see Table  ). These PROMs were originally developed for chronic venous disorders of the lower extremity. The VEINES‐QOL/Sym questionnaire was first introduced in 2003 and was used in 48 of the 111 outcomes studies. It includes 26 items that evaluate health status in 4 dimensions: symptoms, psychological impact, limitations of daily activities, and change over time. Two scores are produced: a summary score based on 25 questions (eg, the VEINES‐QOL score) and a symptoms score based on 10 questions (eg, the VEINES‐Sym score). Although initially developed for chronic venous disorders, the VEINES‐QOL/Sym PROM was subsequently validated for use in DVT and has a version validated for pediatric patients as well. , , , The CIVIQ‐20, despite its introduction 7 years earlier, was less frequently used than VEINES‐QOL/Sym and was included in only 8 of 111 outcomes studies. It evaluates health status in 4 domains: physical, psychological, social, and pain. A short‐form version, the CIVIQ‐14, that combines the social and physical dimensions is available and has removed 6 questions that did not perform well in terms of construct validity. Unlike the VEINES‐QoL/Sym, the CIVIQ‐20 has not been validated for use in DVT. , 

Several disease‐specific PROMs have been created specifically to evaluate health status in VTE: the Pulmonary Embolism‐QoL (PEmb‐QoL), the Venous Thrombosis‐QoL (VT‐QoL), the Deep Venous Thrombosis‐QoL, and the Post‐VTE Functional Status (see Table  ). The PEmb‐QoL was the first disease‐specific PROM to be developed specifically for PE and is still the most widely used in PE‐specific outcomes studies. The questionnaire consists of 40 items and evaluates health status in 6 domains: complaint frequency, complaint intensity, limitations in activities of daily living, work‐related limitations, social limitations, and emotional complaints. It has been cross‐culturally validated for use in 6 different languages and has a defined minimum clinically important difference of 15 points. The VT‐QoL and Deep Venous Thrombosis‐QoL surveys were both developed in 2004 and are validated as disease‐specific outcome measures for patients with DVT. The VT‐QoL consists of a 25‐item questionnaire that assesses health status in 4 dimensions: physical function, social function, general mental health, and thrombosis repercussions. The Deep Venous Thrombosis‐QoL is slightly longer and contains 29 items that assess health status in 6 dimensions: symptoms, limitations in physical activity, emotional distress, hassle with monitoring, dietary problems, and sleep disturbances. Despite specific development for use in DVT, these 2 PROMs have not been used outside of their original studies to date. The Post‐VTE Functional Status was recently developed in 2019 to address gaps in evaluating the impact of VTE on functional status and is a short questionnaire consisting of 4 yes/no statements. It has been validated for use in VTE but has been used in only 1 outcome study to date. , 

Several disease‐specific PROMs were also created to identify complications of VTE, namely PTS and CTEPH (see Table  ). The patient‐reported Villalta (PRV) scale was developed in 2016 and is based on the original Villalta scale used to diagnose PTS. Whereas the original Villalta scale combined a 6‐question clinician assessment of the signs of PTS (eg, edema, redness, hyperpigmentation) with a 5‐question patient assessment of symptoms (eg, pain, cramping, paresthesia, pruritis, and heaviness), the PRV scale allows for the entire survey to be completed by the patient. Despite being well validated against the original Villalta score, the PRV has been used in only 3 studies outside of the initial development and validation studies. , , Two PROMs were developed to evaluate patients' health status with pulmonary hypertension, an important component of PPES. The Cambridge Pulmonary Hypertension Outcome Review score was specifically developed using a cohort of pulmonary hypertension patients that included patients with CTEPH. The instrument includes 65 items that evaluate patients' symptoms, ability to complete daily activities, and quality of life (QoL) and is cross‐culturally validated for use in Colombia. , It has been used in 6 outcomes studies to date. , , , , , The EmPHasis‐10 PROM was developed from a cohort of patients who primarily were diagnosed with pulmonary arterial hypertension; only 2 of the patients involved in item generation for the PROM had CTEPH. For this reason, it is not recommended for use outside pulmonary arterial hypertension and was used in only 1 study outside of the initial validation study. 

## Treatment‐Specific and Adherence‐Based PROMs
Two PROMs have been used to characterize patients' experience with or adherence to anticoagulation treatment: the Anti‐Clot Treatment Scale and the 8‐item Morisky Medication Adherence Scale‐8 (see Table  ). The Anti‐Clot Treatment Scale is a 15‐item PROM that evaluates patients' perceptions of the burdens and benefits of anticoagulation treatment. It produces a Burdens score based on 12 questions and a Benefits score based on 3 questions. It has been validated specifically for use in VTE and has been used in at least 7 outcomes studies to date. The Morisky Medication Adherence Scale‐8 was developed for use with any medication and uses 8 questions to assess a patient's medication adherence. Although not specifically designed or validated for use in VTE, it has been used in 2 of the 111 outcomes studies in this review.

## Use of PROMs in the VTE Literature
Seven studies in the VTE literature used PROMs for comparative effectiveness research (see Table  ). The primary goal of these studies was to determine if differences in health status and treatment‐specific experience and adherence exist between patients taking direct oral anticoagulants (DOACs) and vitamin K antagonists (VKAs). By incorporating the EQ‐5D, SF‐36, VEINES‐QOL/Sym, PRV, Anti‐Clot Treatment Scale, or Morisky Medication Adherence Scale‐8, authors were able to demonstrate that patients with VTE treated with DOACs experienced higher generic QoL, perceived both greater benefits and lower treatment burdens, and had higher treatment adherence than those taking VKAs. , , , , , , 

 Twelve descriptive studies focusing on VTE used PROMs in outcomes assessment: 7 focused on characterizing generic and disease‐specific health status after VTE, and 4 focused on analyzing the impact of various patient‐level factors on health status and experience with treatment (see Table  ). Using PROMs, authors were able to quantify the reductions in health status patients with VTE experience after initial diagnosis, to demonstrate the timeline over which health status improves, and to identify significant drivers of continued impairments in health status including bleeding, recurrence, and PTS. , , , , In addition, investigators identified that the presence of comorbidities and both fatigue and psychological distress were associated with lower general health status after VTE and that low health literacy was associated with both higher perceived treatment burden and lower perceived benefit of treatment. , , 

## Use of PROMs in DVT‐Specific Literature
In the DVT literature, 23 studies have used PROMs for comparative effectiveness research (see Table  ). These studies leveraged the use of PROMs to determine if any improvements in health status could be obtained by switching patients to DOACs, by using catheter‐based interventions (eg, catheter‐directed thrombolysis [CDT] or thrombectomy), or by applying different noninvasive adjunctive therapies (eg, elastic compression stockings or lymphedema therapy). Using PROMs, authors were able to demonstrate that patients with DVT treated with rivaroxaban instead of low molecular weight heparin/VKA had better generic and disease‐specific QoL and had more positive experiences with their treatment (eg, lower perceived burden and higher perceived benefit). , Investigators were also able to demonstrate that patients treated with CDT experienced faster improvements in generic and disease‐specific QoL than those treated with anticoagulation alone and may have a lower likelihood of developing PTS. , , 

 Twenty‐eight descriptive studies focusing on DVT used PROMs for outcomes assessment (see Table  ): 4 focused on characterizing health status after DVT, 4 focused on analyzing the impact of various factors on health status, and 20 focused on the identification and treatment of PTS. By incorporating PROMs, authors were able to quantify the impact of both DVT and resulting PTS on generic and disease‐specific QoL, identify the time course expected for symptom improvement, and explore the efficacy of different treatment modalities (eg, venoplasty, vein recanalization and stenting, or CDT) on improving health status after DVT or PTS.  , , , , , , In addition, authors were able to identify factors such as physical activity level and the extent of residual thrombus that may be linked to the level of improvement in health status after treatment. , 

 

## Use of PROMs in PE‐Specific Literature
In the PE‐specific literature, 6 studies have incorporated PROMs for comparative effectiveness research (see Table  ). These studies used PROMs to evaluate if a transition to DOACs or the use of noninvasive or catheter‐based interventions affected health status after PE. Using PROMs, investigators demonstrated that patients treated with rivaroxaban instead of low molecular weight heparin/VKA had more positive experiences with treatment but had similar long‐term generic and disease‐specific QoL. Investigators were also able to demonstrate the positive impact of noninvasive therapies such as pulmonary rehabilitation as well as catheter‐based therapies such as CDT and ultrasound‐assisted thrombolysis on health status after PE. , , , 

 Eighteen descriptive studies in the PE literature have used PROMs for outcomes assessment: 9 of these focused on characterizing health status after PE, 7 focused on the impact of different treatment modalities on health status, and 2 focused on the identification and treatment of PPES (see Table  ). Using PROMs, authors were able to quantify the impact of both PE and resulting PPES on patients' health status, characterize symptom trajectory, and demonstrate the benefits of noninvasive therapies such as exercise therapy as well as invasive therapies such as ultrasound‐assisted thrombolysis and surgical embolectomy on QoL after PE. , , , , , , In addition, authors were able to identify specific factors that were associated with continued impaired health status after PE including higher body mass index, female sex, and cardiopulmonary disease. 

## Use of PROMs in CTEPH

To date, PROM use in CTEPH has been limited to 16 descriptive studies (see Table  ). These studies have focused on characterizing QoL in patients affected by CTEPH and analyzing the impact of both noninvasive and invasive treatment modalities on patients' health status. Using PROMs, investigators were able to quantify the reductions in generic QoL in those affected by CTEPH and quantify improvements in health status associated with alternative therapeutic approaches. , , , Several variables were identified that correlate with improvements in health status, including 6‐minute walk distance, mean pulmonary artery pressure, and requirement for home oxygen. 

 

## Discussion
The field of VTE care has seen many advances in the past 10 years, with the advent of the pulmonary embolism response team model of care, updated sets of guidelines from the European Society of Cardiology and American Heart Association, and rapid development of new catheter‐based therapies to treat both DVT and PE. , , , As the management of patients with VTE evolves, it is important to take the opportunity to reassess how outcomes are measured and to ensure that new management strategies are evaluated in the most patient‐centered way possible. Although traditional clinical outcomes such as mortality, readmission, and length of stay are easily quantifiable, they fail to capture the entirety of the patient's experience or the impact, both immediate and lasting, that VTE has on a patient's life. PROs, on the other hand, provide the opportunity to incorporate direct patient input into outcomes assessment and to quantify the impact of different VTE interventions on patients' health status. This review showcases how PROs have been used in VTE to date with the goal of demonstrating the benefits of PROM use, appraising the current challenges being faced, and highlighting potential solutions to encourage the introduction of PROMs into both clinical practice and future research in VTE.

## The Benefits of Incorporating PROMs Into VTE Care
Although the use of PROMs is still relatively new in VTE, this review highlights many of the important benefits of incorporating PROMs into outcomes assessment. The most obvious benefit lies in gaining a better understanding of the impact a disease process has on a patient's life. This was exemplified in the work by Marvig et al and Van Es et al, who showed how both generic and disease‐specific PROMs could be used to evaluate the severity of patients' VTE‐related symptoms (eg, leg swelling and pain) and to determine how these symptoms affected their ability to function from both a physical and psychosocial perspective. , After diagnosis, PROMs enable the quantification of changes in patients' health status with treatment. In this review, investigators demonstrated how PROMs could be used to evaluate changes in health status with new catheter‐based therapies and compare changes between patients treated with different anticoagulation classes (eg, Hokusai‐VTE) and between patients treated with catheter‐directed therapies versus anticoagulation alone (eg, CAVA [Ultrasound‐Accelerated Catheter‐Directed Thrombolysis Versus Anticoagulation for the Prevention of Post‐thrombotic Syndrome] and CaVenT [Catheter‐Directed Venous Thrombolysis in Acute Iliofemoral Vein Thrombosis]). , , 

PROMs can also capture patients' experiences with the treatment itself. This was exemplified in the EINSTEIN DVT and EINSTEIN PE trials, where treatment‐specific PROMs were used to compare patients' perceptions of the burdens and benefits of treatment with DOACs as opposed to VKAs. , With longitudinal measurement, PROMs can also capture the trajectory of improvement in health status with various therapies. For example, in the ATTRACT (Acute Venous Thrombosis: Thrombus Removal With Adjunctive Catheter‐Directed Thrombolysis) trial, PROM measurement at prespecified follow‐up intervals was used to evaluate if patients treated with pharmacomechanical CDT experienced faster improvements in health status than those treated with anticoagulation alone. Finally, PROMs can be used to identify the presence and severity of complications of VTE. For example, Wik et al and Tavoly et al demonstrated how the PRV, PEmb‐QoL, and Cambridge Pulmonary Hypertension Outcome Review scores could be used to identify patients with PTS and PPES and to evaluate for improvement in health status with new treatments like balloon pulmonary angioplasty. , , Each of these examples demonstrates how PROMs can help us not only better understand a patient's condition and its impact on their life but also how each treatment available will affect their QoL and speed of recovery, both of which will be critical components of evaluating new treatments and engaging patients in the shared‐decision making process moving forwards.
Although PROMs are clearly useful in the context of clinical practice, shifts in payment models are also likely to bring PRO collection to the forefront of VTE care in the near future. Over the past 10 years, the United States has started to transition from traditional payment models that reimburse for the service provided (eg, fee‐for‐service) to alternative payment models that focus on performance and require demonstration of the health outcomes achieved to justify health care dollars spent (eg, value‐based systems). In these systems, PROs comprise a critical component of health outcomes assessment and often function as performance measures within the system (eg, PRO‐performance measures). Other fields of medicine have already started to embrace this transition. In orthopedics, for example, the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services' bundled payment model for total joint replacement has been in place since 2016 and requires the collection of baseline and postintervention PROMs to demonstrate performance. Within vascular medicine, the American Heart Association has recently focused on advancing PROM collection and developing PRO‐performance measures for peripheral artery disease in preparation for these changes, and it can only be expected that this shift will soon make its way to VTE as well. 

## Current Challenges
There are a number of challenges that stand in the way of PROM collection and use in VTE. The first challenge lies with the tools themselves. Currently, there is substantial variation in the existing PROMs, with 9 different disease‐specific questionnaires available for use in VTE (see Table  ), each of which has undergone varying levels of validation. Although the VEINES‐QoL/Sym, Post‐VTE Functional Status, VT‐QoL, Deep Venous Thrombosis‐QoL, PEmb‐QoL, PRV, and Cambridge Pulmonary Hypertension Outcome Review have all been validated for use in VTE, the CIVIQ‐20 and EmPHasis‐10, despite being used in 10% of outcomes studies in this review, have not been validated for use in VTE. Each tool also assesses slightly different domains and has a different area of focus, whether it be DVT, PE, PTS, or CTEPH. From the patient's perspective, this creates a scenario whereby a patient suffering from a DVT that has partially embolized to the lungs may have to complete several different PROMs to fully capture their experience with DVT (eg, the VEINES‐QoL/Sym), PE (eg, the PEmb‐QoL), and potential resulting PTS (eg, the PRV) or PPES/CTEPH (eg, Cambridge Pulmonary Hypertension Outcome Review). The burden this creates is likely to negatively affect completion rates. From the institution's perspective, this also creates a more complex process for data collection and requires additional resource dedication for data aggregation and interpretation. Finally, from a research perspective, this variation in the PROMs available leads to wide variation in use across the field, thus limiting the ability to provide useful benchmarks or meaningful cross‐study comparisons.
The second major challenge lies in the actual collection of the PRO data. At an institutional level, substantial financial buy‐in is required to successfully implement PROM collection systems that can reliably capture data and aggregate the data in a way that is useful to patients, providers, and the health system as a whole. This is a time‐intensive, iterative process and requires dedicated staff and infrastructure to support data collection, management, and use. , At the department level, given that PROM collection is a relatively new development, providers and care teams will need substantial education to explain why PROM collection is an important component of VTE care. Workflow integration, time requirements, and concerns about overburdening already busy staff are frequently cited as potential barriers to PROM adoption and will be important to address as systems are implemented. , , At the patient level, substantial challenges exist in ensuring that PROMs are accessible to all individuals an institution serves. Although electronic PROs are available that can be distributed and administered via email or smartphone, collection systems need to account for the fact that patients may have varying levels of access to and proficiency with technology, a challenge that is only compounded by the requirement for several different PROMs as discussed here. Although generic PROMs like the SF‐36 and EQ‐5D have been widely validated, the validation of many of the disease‐specific and treatment‐specific PROMs mentioned in this review has been largely limited to populations in the United States and Western Europe. As the use of PROs expands, future research validating these tools in additional languages and cultures will become especially important to ensure that populations from outside of these areas are not systematically excluded from PROM use.
Once PRO data are collected, the next challenge becomes how to make meaningful use of the data in both a clinical and research setting. Previous studies have demonstrated that for PROM data to be useful to providers in clinical practice, they must be integrated into the existing electronic medical record, immediately available, and able to be trended over time. , , , This requires a substantial upfront investment in infrastructure to achieve the level of integration required for meaningful use. Once integrated, the data provided must be easy to interpret, as a raw score of 10 on the PRV is unlikely to be meaningful to most clinicians. Although a simple clinical correlate is currently available (eg, a PRV score of 10 signifies moderate PTS), additional research is needed to help providers understand the prognostic importance of PROM scores and to determine what magnitude of change constitutes a clinically important difference (eg, a minimum clinically important difference). From a research perspective, there is also a need for standardization across how PROMs are used and reported in outcomes studies in the literature; without this, comparability and generalizability will both be hindered.

## Potential Solutions and Ways Forward
In the fall of 2022, a major step was taken to address the heterogeneity in outcome measurements with the development of an international standard set of outcome measures for patients with VTE. This set of outcome measures, created by the International Consortium for Health Outcomes Measurement, was developed using a rigorous literature review in conjunction with input from an international group of VTE experts and patient representatives. Using the PROMIS short form Global Health, PEmb‐QoL, VEINES/QoL, and Post‐VTE Functional Status along with questions on treatment satisfaction and life view, this standardized set provides insight into health status in all the following domains: general QoL, disease‐specific QoL, functional limitations, pain, dyspnea, treatment satisfaction, psychosocial well‐being (eg, anxiety, depression, and postthrombotic pain syndrome), and changes in life view. The survey is designed with adaptive testing to maximize efficiency, with cascade‐style options that add questions from the PROMIS Short Form Pain Intensity, PROMIS Short Form Dyspnea Severity, Patient Health Questionnaire‐9, Generalized Anxiety Disorder‐7, and Anti‐Clot Treatment Scale if patients indicate the presence of pain, dyspnea, depression, anxiety, or treatment dissatisfaction. This outcomes set represents a great step toward standardization of PROM collection and use in both clinical practice and research in VTE, and is available online along with information to assist with implementation. The International Society on Thrombosis and Haemostasis has also set forth a set of common data elements to improve the quality and consistency of clinical research data in VTE, which is publicly available at http://isth.breakthrough.healthcare and includes many of the PRO assessments described in this review. We encourage those engaging in research in VTE to consider following these guidelines and using the International Consortium for Health Outcomes Measurement outcomes set as it will facilitate comparability across trials.
With a standardized set of outcome measures now available, the next major challenge to address becomes facilitating data collection. Fortunately, we have the benefit of observing what has occurred in other fields during PROM implementation and learning from their successes and failures. At the institutional level, others have found success with the identification of clinical champions outside of the administration who act as departmental leads and take responsibility for collection performance. Given substantial concerns about increased workload and overburdening of staff, upfront investment in efficient technological infrastructure (eg, medical record integration) and administrators to oversee PROM collection has also been associated with improved collection rates. , At the provider and care team level, careful attention to workflow integration has been key; although some have demonstrated success using PROM collection systems like Research Electronic Data Capture that can be completed electronically by patients on a phone or computer on their own time, this has the potential to systematically exclude patients who are not proficient with technology, are visually disabled, or do not have access to a phone or computer. Other fields have demonstrated success with the administration of PROMs on a tablet or kiosk during the clinical encounter. Although this requires additional staff and resources, it ensures that patients complete PROMs at a time when members of the care team are available to assist those who need it and avoids their being excluded from data collection. As natural language processing tools develop, this technology may offer the capability to populate PROMs using data extracted from free text, further minimizing the time requirement necessary for completion. Staff education is also critically important; in a prior study of >200 clinics in Massachusetts, staff training in PROs was associated with 20% higher PROM collection rates at the clinic level. At the patient level, ensuring availability in different languages is imperative. Although this is possible with paper‐and‐pen surveys, it is much easier to toggle between survey languages using an electronic device. Attention to minimizing survey length and completion time is also important, whether it be through the use of short‐form surveys (eg, the SF‐12 instead of the SF‐36) or the use of adaptive testing strategies. , With the considerable time commitment required for completion, patients must also receive education regarding how completing PROMs will contribute to their care.
Once PROM collection is addressed, there will be a substantial opportunity to incorporate PROM data into clinical practice and research moving forward. In the clinic, viewing a patient's PROM scores will offer providers the opportunity to quickly understand their patient's symptom burden and how it affects their life. When compared with others across a population, this information can be useful to help patients contextualize their disease. Distribution of PROMs outside of patient visits also has the potential to help providers monitor their patients' health status remotely and identify complications of VTE like PTS or PPES. The insights provided may even be actionable; in the case of PTS, it could be used to identify patients that would benefit from elastic compression stockings and allows providers to track their improvement with treatment. Most important, once PROMs are better incorporated into comparative effectiveness research in VTE, these data will be a critical component of the shared decision‐making process and will enable providers to most appropriately counsel patients on the treatment options available to them.
Finally, in the research setting, PROMs have an opportunity to evaluate how new treatments affect health status for patients with VTE and to compare the efficacy between various invasive and noninvasive treatment modalities. The principal investigators of HI‐PEITHO (Ultrasound‐Facilitated, Catheter‐Directed, Thrombolysis in Intermediate‐High Risk Pulmonary Embolism) and PEERLESS have both led the way with the incorporation of both generic and disease‐specific PROMs as trial end points, and it will be important for future trialists in the space to do the same. Incorporation of PROMs into registries like PREFER in VTE will also be critical in helping us better understand how VTE affects QoL on a population level. Finally, PROMs will also enable further understanding of the complications of VTE; although there is robust work being done in PTS, additional work is still needed to enhance our understanding of PPES.

## Limitations
There are several limitations of this review that must be acknowledged. First, the literature search in this review was limited to 1 database. Although previous research has demonstrated that PubMed/MEDLINE has sufficient coverage to act as a principal database for systematic reviews, it is possible that certain references contained in other commonly used databases (eg, EMBASE, Cochrane) were excluded. Second, given the broad nature of this topic, the examination of each reference was limited to qualitative analyses. Future systematic reviews should consider narrowing the scope of the review by pathology (eg, DVT or PE) or trial design (eg, randomized controlled trials), as this will facilitate more granular, quantitative analyses. Given that this study was primarily descriptive, a risk of bias assessment was not performed for each of the studies included. It is important to note that many of the trials included were small, nonrandomized, often retrospective cohort studies without prespecified end points or analyses, which are inherently subject to a high risk of bias. Finally, this review was limited to studies published in English, which may limit the generalizability of these results to other countries.

## Conclusions
As VTE management evolves, and the breadth of treatment options increases, it is important to step back and reappraise how we approach outcomes measurement. Although traditional outcome measures still have a place, incorporating PROMs into outcome measurement will help us gain a better understanding of a patient's condition and its impact on their QoL and physical, social, and psychological function. Although there are many challenges that will be faced, this integration will be an essential component of evaluating the effectiveness of new treatments and being able to engage VTE patients in the shared decision‐making process in the future, helping to pave the way for more patient‐centered care for patients with VTE moving forward.
