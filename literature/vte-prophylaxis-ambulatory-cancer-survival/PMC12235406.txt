## Abstract

## Background
Apixaban and rivaroxaban are commonly used direct oral anticoagulants for atrial fibrillation (AF) and venous thromboembolism (VTE). Both have been compared to warfarin, but there are insufficient comparative outcome data.

## Objectives
The purpose of this study was to assess outcomes of apixaban, rivaroxaban, and warfarin.

## Methods
This is a registry-based cohort study with data from 6 centers in Michigan, 2009 to 2023. Patients were adults with AF and/or VTE with at least 3 months of follow-up. Outcomes included rates of bleeding, thrombosis, healthcare utilization, and death.

## Results
A total of 13,435 patients met the study inclusion criteria (average age 66.7 years, 58.0% on anticoagulation for AF, average follow-up 28.2 months). After matching, 2 groups of 3,527 patients on apixaban and warfarin were compared. Any bleeding was similar between groups, but major bleeding was less with apixaban. Thrombotic event rates were higher with apixaban. Mortality, rates of emergency room visits, and hospitalizations for bleeding were higher with warfarin. After matching, 1,395 patients on rivaroxaban were compared to 4,185 patients on warfarin. Any bleeding and major bleeding were higher with rivaroxaban. Thrombotic event rates were similar, aside from a higher rate of “other” thrombosis with rivaroxaban. After matching, 2 groups of 1,395 patients on apixaban and rivaroxaban were compared. Any bleeding, major bleeding, and mortality were higher with rivaroxaban. Thrombotic event rates were similar.

## Conclusions
For patients with AF and/or VTE, we observed that bleeding was highest with rivaroxaban, followed by warfarin, and then apixaban. Rates of thrombosis were higher with apixaban than with warfarin, largely driven by “other” thrombotic events.

Key words: atrial fibrillation, factor Xa inhibitors, hemorrhage, outcomes assessment, venous thromboembolism, warfarin

## Central Illustration

Apixaban, rivaroxaban, and warfarin are some of the most commonly used oral anticoagulants for nonvalvular atrial fibrillation (AF) and venous thromboembolic disease (VTE). , , Apixaban and rivaroxaban are among the direct oral anticoagulants (DOACs) that are suggested as first-line treatment for management of these conditions. , Their endorsement comes in part from landmark clinical trials that demonstrated superiority or noninferiority of these agents compared to warfarin, including a lower risk of intracranial hemorrhage. , , , , , , Overall, randomized clinical trial evidence suggests that DOACs have similar efficacy and safety compared to warfarin for VTE ; DOACs seem to be better than warfarin for AF in terms of both efficacy and safety. Comparative efficacy data among the specific DOACs remain inadequate, especially in a nontrial setting.
While guidelines for VTE and nonvalvular AF generally suggest DOACs collectively over warfarin for nonpregnant adults with adequate renal and hepatic function, they offer limited guidance on choosing a specific factor Xa inhibitor or oral direct thrombin inhibitor. Anticoagulant selection logically may be influenced by the dosing schedule, side effect profile (eg dyspepsia with dabigatran), the pharmacology of the drug, drug interactions, dietary considerations, local practice patterns, medication access, the need for treatment with a parenteral anticoagulant before starting the DOAC, patient preferences, indication, and more. While a randomized controlled trial comparing the efficacy of the DOACs would be desirable, such data are not currently available.
The Blue Cross Blue Shield of Michigan-sponsored Michigan Anticoagulation Quality Improvement Initiative (MAQI ) is a diverse collaborative of 6 outpatient anticoagulation clinics in the state of Michigan that contribute data on warfarin-treated patients and 4 clinics that contribute data on DOACs. These clinics include academic and community practices; they are in both urban and rural areas. All types of healthcare insurance are included. Within this registry, approximately 50% to 60% of DOAC-treated patients are on apixaban, <10% are on dabigatran, <1% are on edoxaban, and 30% to 40% are on rivaroxaban. We sought to compare the patient characteristics and outcomes among the most commonly used anticoagulants at our centers.

## Methods
We used MAQI registry data from January 2009 to June of 2023. Data analysis was done in late 2023. Registry data were collected using trained data abstractors, on predefined forms, and random chart audits were done at the coordinating center (the University of Michigan) to confirm the accuracy of the chart abstracted data. Data are reported following the STROBE (Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology) reporting guidelines. This study was approved by the institutional review board at each site before data collection. Because of the nature of the study, a waiver of informed consent was granted as a quality-improvement project.

## Study design and participants
Included patients were at least 18 years of age, were started or switched between an oral anticoagulant for the indications of AF and/or VTE, and had at least 3 months of follow-up data available to ensure patients had adequate follow-up to allow for abstraction of relevant outcomes. Patients were excluded if they had valvular AF, were treated with another anticoagulant, or had <3 months of follow-up.
Data were abstracted from the time of study enrollment until patients were lost to follow-up, discharged from the anticoagulation clinic, the end of the study period (June 2023), or death. Collected data at study enrollment included patient demographics, comorbidities, bleeding and thrombotic risk factors, past medical history of bleeding or thrombosis, and medications. Charts were then abstracted at 3- to 6-month intervals to assess for clinical outcomes.
The primary outcome was any new bleeding event; we anticipated our study to be underpowered to evaluate thrombotic outcomes. New bleeding is a composite outcome that includes major bleeding, as defined by the International Society on Thrombosis and Hemostasis, nonmajor bleeding, and a small number of bleeds that could not be classified as either major or nonmajor. Secondary bleeding outcomes included major bleeding, major bleeding subtypes (fatal, central nervous system, and life threatening), emergency room (ER) visits for bleeding, hospitalizations for bleeding, and transfusions. Secondary thrombotic outcomes included ischemic or embolic stroke, transient ischemic attack, deep vein thrombosis, pulmonary embolism, and other thrombosis (which includes myocardial infarction, intracardiac thrombosis, peripheral embolism, and more). A HAS-BLED score, modified to exclude labile international normalized ratio, and a Charlson Comorbidity Index score were calculated for each patient at enrollment.

## Statistical analysis
Propensity-matched cohorts (apixaban vs warfarin [1:1], rivaroxaban vs warfarin [1:3], and apixaban vs rivaroxaban [1:1]) of patients were analyzed based on DOAC use at study enrollment, using 1:1-1:3 matching ratios. , , Three separate propensity-matched comparisons were done, comparing 2 anticoagulants with each analysis: apixaban and warfarin, rivaroxaban and warfarin, and apixaban and warfarin. Each propensity match comparison used the same covariates and outcomes from the MAQI registry, which was specifically designed to evaluate for factors relevant to anticoagulation selection, management, and outcomes. Patients were matched based on numerous covariates including demographics, social history, comorbidities, medications, bleeding/thrombotic history, indication for anticoagulation, and follow-up (see for a full list of variables used for matching). The matching variables are similar to those used in other MAQI studies and include variables anticipated to impact the study outcomes based on literature review and expert consensus. Propensity scores were generated as a probability of receiving a given oral anticoagulant through a logistic regression model. The optimal matching method was used; optimal matching selects all matches simultaneously and without replacement to minimize the total absolute difference in propensity scores across all matches. A standardized difference of <0.1 was used to indicate a negligible difference in the covariates between the groups. Covariates that maintained residual differences between the groups were included in subsequent Poisson models.
After each of the sets of the matched cohorts were created, we compared event rates between the 2 distinct oral anticoagulants using Poisson regression. These analyses were performed for each of the study outcomes.
For the primary study, we included both standard and reduced-dose DOACs. Reduced-dose DOAC was defined as a total maintenance daily dose of apixaban <10 mg or rivaroxaban <20 mg. For this analysis, we did not assess the reason for dose reduction. For our first sensitivity analysis, we excluded patients on reduced-dose DOACs to only compare patients on full dosing of oral anticoagulants.
While we included both AF and VTE in the primary analysis to maximize study power, we then conducted a second sensitivity analysis evaluating each indication separately. A similar analytic strategy was used as above.
A 2-tailed P < 0.05 was used to indicate statistical significance. All statistical analyses were carried out using SAS 9.4 X64 and R was X64 4.1.3.

## Results
Of 13,435 patients on OAC who met the study inclusion criteria (3,536 on apixaban, 1,395 on rivaroxaban, and 8,504 on warfarin), the average age was 66.7 ± 14.9 years, 51.1% identified through the electronic health record as male, most (58.0%) were on anticoagulation for AF, and the average follow-up duration was 28.2 months (SD 30.7 months). In the primary study cohort, 2,888 patients (81.7%) of patients on apixaban were on doses ≥10 mg/day, and 1,257 (90.0%) of patients on rivaroxaban were on doses ≥20 mg/day. Warfarin-treated patients had a mean time in the therapeutic range of 60% ± 20%) ( ). Before the propensity match, 741 patients (15%) had changed warfarin to a DOAC, representing a small portion of the study population. Approximately 1,061 patients (12.5%) had switched from a DOAC to warfarin. Evaluating our group after matching, 262 patients (18.8%) had changed from warfarin to rivaroxaban. A total of 533 (12.7%) changed from rivaroxaban to warfarin. For apixaban, 470 patients (13.3%) changed from warfarin to apixaban, and 517 patients (14.7%) changed from apixaban to warfarin. After propensity matching, balanced covariates were achieved, with all standardized differences being <0.1 ( ).
 After propensity matching, 3,527 patients on apixaban were compared to 3,527 patients on warfarin. Any bleeding was similar between apixaban and warfarin with 28.6 with vs 27.6 bleeds per 100 patient-years (P = 0.26). Major bleeding was higher with warfarin (3.4 vs 4.7 major bleeds/100 patient-years, P < 0.001, 95% CI: 3.0-3.8 vs 4.2-5.2). All major bleeding subtypes (fatal, intracranial or intraspinal, and life threatening) were significantly higher with warfarin ( ). Thrombotic event rates were higher with apixaban (2.6 vs 2.1 events/100 patient-years, P = 0.026, 95% CI: 2.3-3.0 vs 1.8-2.4), including the thrombotic subtype of other thrombosis (1.0 vs 0.5 events/100 patient-years, P < 0.001, 95% CI: 0.8-1.3 vs 0.4-0.7). Rates of ischemic stroke, transient ischemic attack, PE, and deep vein thrombosis were not significantly different. Rates of ER visits and hospitalizations for bleeding were higher with warfarin (11.5 vs 13.4 ER visits per 100 patient-years, P < 0.001, 95% CI: 10.8-12.3 vs 12.6-14.3; 6.2 vs 7.4 hospitalizations per 100 patient-years, P = 0.005, 95% CI: 5.7-6.8 vs 6.8-8.0). Mortality was higher with warfarin (3.7 vs 4.4 deaths/100 patient-years, P = 0.03, 95% CI: 3.3-4.2 vs 4.0-4.9) ( , ).
 After propensity matching, 1,395 patients on rivaroxaban were compared to 4,185 patients on warfarin. Any bleeding and major bleeding were higher with rivaroxaban (37.9 vs 24.9 bleeds/100 patient-years, P < 0.001, 95% CI: 35.7-40.1 vs 23.9-25.9; 4.7 vs 3.6 major bleeds/100 patient-years, P = 0.04, 95% CI: 3.9-5.5 vs 3.2-4.0). Intracranial or intraspinal bleeds were higher with warfarin, but other major bleed subtypes (fatal and life threatening) were statistically similar between groups ( ). Thrombotic event rates were similar, aside from a higher rate of the thrombotic subtype of other thrombosis with rivaroxaban (1.0 vs 0.3 events/100 patient-years, P = 0.002, 95% CI: 0.7-1.4 vs 0.2-0.5). ER visits, hospitalizations, and mortality were similar between rivaroxaban and warfarin ( , ).
 After propensity matching, 1,395 patients on apixaban were compared to 1,395 patients on rivaroxaban. Any bleeding and major bleeding were higher with rivaroxaban (37.9 vs 25.7 bleeds/100 patient-years, P < 0.001, 95% CI: 35.7-40.1 vs 23.9-27.6; 4.7 vs 2.6 major bleeds/100 patient-years, P < 0.001, 95% CI: 3.9-5.5 vs 2.0-3.2). Major bleeding subtypes were not significantly different between groups. Thrombotic event rates were similar. ER visits occurred more frequently on rivaroxaban (12.8 vs 10.1 events/100 patient-years, P = 0.003, 95% CI: 11.5-14.1 vs 9.0-11.3) as did patient mortality (3.5 vs 2.6 deaths/100 patient-years, P = 0.047, 95% CI: 2.9-4.3 vs 2.1-3.3). Hospitalizations were similar between groups ( , ).
 

## Sensitivity analyses
For our first sensitivity analysis, excluding patients on reduced DOAC dosing, we had 12,648 patients in the baseline study cohort. We were able to achieve balanced groups after propensity matching once again, with no residual difference over 0.1 ( ). Comparing apixaban to warfarin (n = 2,887 in each group), similar results were seen. However, there was no longer an increased rate of thrombosis among patients on apixaban compared to warfarin ( ). The other outcomes were unchanged ( ). Comparing rivaroxaban (n = 1,256) to warfarin (n = 3,768), findings were similar ( ). Comparing apixaban to rivaroxaban (n = 1,256 in each group), results were similar except mortality was no longer significantly different (3.1 vs 2.8 deaths/100 patient-years, P = 0.54) ( ). We did not assess thrombotic subtypes in these analyses ( ).
For our second sensitivity analysis, we included patients with AF. Accordingly, we excluded patients anticoagulated for the indication of VTE or for AF with VTE. We included standard and reduced DOAC dosing. We had 7,623 eligible patients. Propensity matching was again robust, but there were some residual differences in HAS-BLED scores for the warfarin comparisons; this was therefore included in the subsequent Poison regression models ( ). Comparing apixaban to warfarin (n = 2,471 in each group), results were similar ( ). However, nonmajor bleeding was now significantly higher with apixaban (24.0 vs 21.1 bleeds per 100 patient-years, P = 0.007). Hospitalizations for bleeding were no longer significantly increased with warfarin. Comparing rivaroxaban (n = 665) to warfarin (n = 1,995), major bleeding exhibited a similar trend but was no longer statistically significantly increased with rivaroxaban (P = 0.07). Bleeding was otherwise similar in outcomes. Thrombosis was now statistically significantly higher for rivaroxaban with 2.6 vs 1.4 thrombotic events/100 patient-years (P = 0.01). ER visits showed a similar trend but now were statistically significantly higher with rivaroxaban (12.7 vs 10.7 visits/100 patient-years, P = 0.03) ( ). Comparing apixaban to rivaroxaban (n = 665 in each group), outcomes were similar. However, hospitalizations for bleeding were significantly higher with rivaroxaban (6.1 hospitalizations vs 4.2, P = 0.008), and mortality was similar (3.3 for rivaroxaban vs 2.7 for apixaban deaths/100 patient-years, P = 0.26) ( ).
For the second part of our second sensitivity analysis, we included patients with VTE. Accordingly, we excluded patients with AF or VTE and AF. This left a total of 5,646 eligible patients. A robust propensity match was achieved, but there were residual differences in rates of estrogen/progesterone use and chronic liver disease for the rivaroxaban vs warfarin comparison that were included in the subsequent Poisson regression models ( ). Comparing apixaban and warfarin (n = 1,027 in each group), bleeding outcomes were overall similar, but rates were numerically higher in the warfarin group. Thrombosis outcomes showed a similar trend, but the difference was no longer statistically significant (P = 0.51). Mortality showed the opposite trend but was not statistically different (4.4 with apixaban vs 3.1 with warfarin deaths/100 patient-years, P = 0.06) ( ). Comparing rivaroxaban (n = 713) and warfarin (n = 2,139), results were similar to the primary analysis ( ). Comparing rivaroxaban and apixaban (n = 713 in each group), bleeding trends were similar, but major bleeding was no longer statistically significant (4.4 vs 2.9 major bleeds/100 patient-years, P = 0.06). ER visits were also no longer significantly different, nor was mortality ( ).

## Discussion
In this large, registry-based cohort study of propensity-matched patients on oral anticoagulation for AF and/or VTE, we found differences in outcomes based on the specific oral anticoagulant used. Of our baseline registry, warfarin was the most commonly used anticoagulant, followed by apixaban, and then rivaroxaban. Approximately one-third were on aspirin at enrollment, around 5% were on a nonaspirin antiplatelet drug, and about one-third were on some form of gastroprotection. Bleeding outcomes were better with apixaban compared to warfarin (less major bleeding, similar overall bleeding) and compared to rivaroxaban (less major and overall bleeding). Bleeding outcomes were better with warfarin than with rivaroxaban (less nonmajor bleeding and overall bleeding). Thrombotic outcomes were worse with apixaban (more “other” thromboses and overall thrombosis) and rivaroxaban (more “other” thromboses but similar overall) compared to warfarin. Thrombotic outcomes were similar between apixaban and rivaroxaban. Surprisingly, mortality was better with apixaban relative to warfarin or rivaroxaban ( ). These findings could have implications for anticoagulation selection while awaiting more definitive randomized controlled trials.
 Reduced major bleeding was similarly seen when apixaban was compared to warfarin in the ARISTOTLE trial (AF), but any bleeding was overall similar; reduced major bleeding was also seen relative to warfarin when apixaban was studied in VTE. While randomized clinical trial data are not available comparing apixaban and rivaroxaban, available data are consistent with our study findings showing reduced bleeding with apixaban relative to the other oral anticoagulants studied, , , , , , , , , , , although this has not been consistent in all studies. , , This study adds to the existing literature in that it is one of the largest, real-world chart abstraction studies that are able to more thoroughly adjust for potential confounders. Other studies are often based on insurance claims data which may be more subject to misclassification, especially with outcomes. Furthermore, confounding may be more likely in these studies. These various studies with different study methodologies do suggest lower bleeding with apixaban. This could have implications for anticoagulant selection.
Thrombotic outcomes were higher with the DOACs studied compared to warfarin, with the overall thrombotic rate being increased just for apixaban. This was largely driven by “other” thromboses that includes myocardial infarction. This has not been seen in other studies, , but there are limited studies of similar duration and methodology. Given that thrombotic event rates are low for anticoagulated patients, this is also a composite outcome that includes a variety of thrombotic outcomes. The magnitude of difference is also relatively small. While this is hypothesis generating, the significance of it is uncertain at this point. One consideration could be that the short half-lives of the DOACs relative to warfarin could put patients at greater risk if they were to miss doses, but this would need further study. Notably the ATLAS ACS 2-TIMI 51 trial showed reduced risk of the composite end point of death from cardiovascular causes, myocardial infarction, or stroke with rivaroxaban compared to placebo in patients with acute coronary syndrome ; the APPRAISE-2 trial did not show a reduction in ischemic events with apixaban compared to placebo in a similar population. Further exploration of cardiovascular outcome differences may be warranted.
The mechanism for and reason for the observed clinical outcome differences merit further investigation. One possibility is that the twice-daily dosing of apixaban offers more steady levels of anticoagulation than the daily dosing of rivaroxaban, that could influence bleeding outcomes. However, this would not explain the observed differences in thrombotic outcomes, mostly driven by “other” thrombotic events. It is possible that there is confounding in that patients who select a once-daily drug are different from those that select a twice-daily drug. Medication adherence was not addressed in this study, but other studies suggest better adherence with daily drugs compared to twice daily. Overall, given the similar mechanism of action between apixaban and rivaroxaban, observed differences are presumably related to the pharmacokinetics of the drugs themselves.

## Strengths and limitations
Strengths of this study include the extensive propensity match, the use of prospectively collected, abstracted data, and the use of a real-world, diverse patient population. Patients also had a long duration of follow-up and conducted sensitivity analyses that support the findings of the primary study design.
Limitations include those inherent to the observational design, including the potential for unadjusted confounding. We do not adjust for the severity of patient comorbidities or medication adherence. Patients seeking medical care outside of our collaborative may not have been well captured, and data on myocardial infarction may not have been well captured for the duration of the study. We did include patients starting and switching to anticoagulants. We do not think that significantly impacted the study results considering that patients were generally followed up for over 2 years, rates of switching were also similar between anticoagulant classes, and most (>80%) of the patients had newly started anticoagulation rather than switching from warfarin to a DOAC or from a DOAC to warfarin; future research could assess reasons for switching and any outcome differences based on switching patterns. While we conducted a sensitivity analysis excluding patients on reduced-dose DOACs, we did not evaluate the rationale for DOAC dose reductions in our cohort. Dose reductions could be for extended secondary prevention of VTE, for organ dysfunction with AF, or inappropriate/off-label. Furthermore, we do not know if patients were inappropriately on full-dose anticoagulation. However, given that all DOAC prescriptions were monitored over the study period by our anticoagulation group for inappropriate/off-label dosing, we anticipate that most DOAC dosing was consistent with current guideline recommendations, but this is uncertain. The mean time in the therapeutic range on warfarin averaged 60%, which is reasonable for the study population. Given sample size limitations, we were unable to evaluate if outcomes significantly differed based on the quality of warfarin management. This is something that should be considered for future study. Medication use was well captured at registry enrollment, but changing medications, especially aspirin that does not require a prescription, may not be well captured. Patients were all followed up through anticoagulation clinics that regularly participate in quality-improvement work. This may limit the generalizability of the study, as does the geographically limited patient population. The included patient population is diverse, and it largely reflects the patient population of Michigan and may not be representative of other areas. Lastly, event rates were low, potentially limiting our power to detect differences in some event types or subtypes.

## Conclusions
For patients on oral anticoagulation for AF and/or VTE, we observed that bleeding was highest with rivaroxaban, followed by warfarin, and then apixaban. Rates of thrombosis were higher with apixaban than with warfarin, seemingly largely driven by “other” thrombotic events. Thrombotic event rates were otherwise similar between apixaban, rivaroxaban, and warfarin. We observed apixaban to be associated with lower mortality than rivaroxaban and warfarin. While these findings should be confirmed with randomized studies, they may have implications for anticoagulant selection.
