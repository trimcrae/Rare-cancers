## Abstract

## Background
Among patients with cancer-associated VTE (Ca-VTE), anticoagulant choice may impact outcomes such as hospitalized bleeding and death, with potential differences across different cancer sites.

## Objectives
This study aimed to evaluate the risks of hospitalized bleeding and death by oral anticoagulant (OAC) prescribed (ie, apixaban, rivaroxaban, and warfarin), overall and by cancer site.

## Methods
Patients with Ca-VTE aged ≥65 years were identified from the U.S. Medicare 20% sample databases for years 2011 to 2019. Algorithms based on International Classification of Diseases codes were used to define venous thromboembolism, cancer site, comorbidities, and hospitalized bleeding. Cox regression was used, with follow-up restricted to 6 months.

## Results
Among the 30,342 patients with Ca-VTE who filled prescriptions for OACs (apixaban: 6,546; rivaroxaban: 7,936; warfarin: 15,860), 634 experienced incident hospitalized bleeding and 5,977 died within 6 months of incident venous thromboembolism. Overall, the hospitalized bleeding HR (95% CI) for apixaban vs rivaroxaban was 0.68 (0.56-0.83). This pattern was present for most cancer sites (ie, breast, colon, lung, and prostate), though precision was poor and associations were not statistically significant. No clear pattern emerged when comparing apixaban or rivaroxaban to warfarin. For mortality there was no association overall (HR apixaban vs rivaroxaban: 0.99 [0.91-1.08]). Results were generally similar in analyses stratified by age category, sex, prevalent kidney disease status, and metastatic cancer status.

## Conclusions
Among patients with Ca-VTE, apixaban may be associated with lower risk of hospitalized bleeding than rivaroxaban. Additional research is needed to understand associations of OAC prescribed with all-cause mortality among patients with Ca-VTE.

Key words: apixaban, bleeding, cancer, oral anticoagulation, rivaroxaban, venous thromboembolism

## Central Illustration

Cancer is an established risk factor for venous thromboembolism (VTE). The mechanism of thrombosis in patients with cancer relates to the cancer itself as well as cancer treatments and complications of cancer treatment. Factors, such as cancer site, stage, treatments, central catheters, and surgical procedures, all contribute to the risk of VTE. , A recent analysis of Danish medical registries found the 12-month cumulative incidence of VTE after cancer diagnosis to be 3%, which is 9-fold higher than in the general population. However, VTE risk varies by cancer site. The 12-month cumulative incidence of VTE (with death as a competing risk) ranged from 1.0% for breast cancer to 5.5% for pancreatic cancer. Patients with cancer-associated VTE (Ca-VTE) with VTE also experience higher rates of VTE recurrence and bleeding complications during VTE treatment. , , , , After the cancer itself, thrombosis is the next leading cause of death in people with cancer. Therefore, optimizing management of patients with Ca-VTE is paramount.
Anticoagulation is the mainstay for treatment of Ca-VTE and non–Ca-VTE. , Historically, low-molecular-weight heparins (LMWHs) were the treatment of choice for Ca-VTE due to the reduced risk of VTE recurrence and easier dosing vs the vitamin K antagonist warfarin. But, they required patients to give themselves subcutaneous injections and had greater expense. With the development of direct oral anticoagulants (DOACs) (dabigatran, rivaroxaban, apixaban, and edoxaban), alternative oral anticoagulant (OAC) therapies to treat Ca-VTE became available with at least equal efficacy to LMWH, but with potentially increased bleeding for some cancer sites, such as gastrointestinal (GI) and genitourinary. , There are no randomized controlled trials (RCTs) directly comparing OACs for the management of Ca-VTE, leaving clinicians with little firm data on how to best manage Ca-VTE.
Evaluations of real-world data using pharmacoepidemiologic approaches are increasingly being used by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration to support health care decisions, and may provide important insights into the best practices for treatment of Ca-VTE. Real-world data are more generalizable than data from RCTs, , since they provide information on people who would not have been enrolled in RCTs, whether due to their geographic location, socioeconomic status, or organ function. Few such observational studies of OACs in Ca-VTE have been conducted, and fewer still have stratified by underlying cancer site. Doing so may be important since the clinical course of VTE is known to differ according to cancer site. 
Using data from the U.S. Medicare 20% sample, we first identified patients with VTE associated with cancer. Then, using a “new user” active-comparator design we compared the risks of death and hospitalized bleeding by OAC prescribed. Analyses were conducted for all cancers combined, and separately for the following cancer sites: breast, colon/rectum, lung, prostate, upper GI system, and genitourinary systems. These cancers were selected because they are the 4 most common (ie, breast, colorectal, lung, and prostate) or were noted in American Society of Clinical Oncology (ASCO) guidelines as being associated with increased bleeding risk (ie, GI and genitourinary). , Our primary analysis involved a head-to-head comparison of apixaban and rivaroxaban. These drugs should have similar prescription patterns and in 2020 approximately 90% of patients with VTE filled a prescription for either apixaban or rivaroxaban (data were for VTE overall, not specifically Ca-VTE). Secondarily, we also evaluated head-to-head comparisons of apixaban and rivaroxaban with warfarin. We hypothesized that, though absolute incidence rates would vary by site, across all cancer sites DOACs would be associated with lower risk of bleeding than warfarin, but similar rates of death.

## Methods

## Data source
Medicare is a federal health insurance program that covers >93% of U.S. citizens aged ≥65 years. The present analysis used beneficiaries in the Medicare 20% sample, aged ≥65 years with fee-for-service coverage of parts A (hospital insurance), B (medical insurance), and D (prescription pharmacy coverage). The Medicare 20% sample data sets contain detailed inpatient and outpatient medical claims, which are linked to outpatient prescription drug claims and person-level enrollment information including geographic location (Zone Improvement Plan (ZIP) code) and self-reported race/ethnicity.

## Study population
Among enrollees included in the Medicare 20% sample and enrolled in fee-for-service coverage of Medicare parts A, B, and D in years 2011 to 2019, we further required evidence of comorbid cancer and VTE. Individuals were required to be enrolled for at least 6 months before their incident VTE to allow for ascertainment of prevalent VTE cases and comorbidities.
Incident VTE was defined as 1 inpatient International Classification of Diseases (ICD) code or 2 outpatient ICD codes (7-184 days apart) indicating VTE in any position, and a new anticoagulant prescription within 31 days of the initial diagnosis. The ICD codes used to define VTE are provided in . In a validation study using a similar definition, by Sanfilippo et al, this algorithm’s specificity was 99%, sensitivity was 72% and its positive predictive value was 91%.
The initial sample included 402,852 individuals aged 65 to 99 years with 1 inpatient or 2 outpatient ICD codes indicating VTE ( ). We then further restricted the sample to those with a prescription fill for an anticoagulant within 31 days of the incident VTE date (N = 171,147). After excluding those with their first anticoagulant prescription outside of their enrollment period (N = 883) and those without a 6-month run-in period (N = 41,942), there were 128,322 enrollees with incident VTE.
Finally, cancer was defined by the presence of an ICD code, in any position, indicating malignant or in situ neoplasm on inpatient, outpatient, or carrier claim files within a year prior to the incident VTE diagnosis. The final analytic data set included 30,342 Medicare enrollees with Ca-VTE. Cancer was further classified by site (ie, breast [female only], prostate [men only], lung, colorectal, upper GI, and nonprostate genitourinary cancers) based on the Chronic Conditions Warehouse definitions. 
ICD codes used to define cancer are documented in .

## Oral anticoagulants
To emulate the target trial we compared OACs. Prescriptions for OACs were identified from outpatient pharmaceutical claims data, which includes information on the National Drug Code, the prescription fill date, and the number of days supplied. Validity of warfarin claims in administrative databases is excellent (sensitivity: 94%, positive predictive value: 99%). Validation studies of DOAC claims have not yet been conducted. We did not include dabigatran or edoxaban users in this analysis due to low numbers. Additionally, we did not include LMWH since it has a different mode of administration.
The analysis focused on “new users” of OACs, meaning that the patients with VTE and cancer did not have any exposure to OACs prior to the time period under observation. Patients were categorized according to the first OAC prescription filled, and remained in this initial category for the entire analysis, in order to mimic the intent-to-treat approach of a RCT. , 

## Outcome ascertainment
Hospitalized bleeding events were defined according to ICD-9 and ICD-10 codes on inpatient claims after the start of follow-up. We defined hospitalized bleeding events based on the Cunningham algorithm. It consisted of intracranial hemorrhage (from a primary discharge diagnosis on an inpatient claim), GI bleeding, or other major bleeding events (from primary and secondary diagnoses on inpatient claims and the presence of transfusion codes). This algorithm has a positive predictive value of 86% for identifying serious bleeding events and is comparable to other peer-reviewed algorithms. , 
Death information was available from the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services through linkage of Medicare claims to data from the U.S. Social Security Administration and the Railroad Retirement Board, with revisions submitted by family members. Overall, 99% of death days have been validated. 

## Statistical analysis
Descriptive characteristics are provided, overall and by OAC type. Our primary analyses involved head-to-head comparisons of apixaban vs rivaroxaban for the management of Ca- VTE, and 6-month risk of bleeding and mortality. Analyses were done for the whole study population, and stratified by cancer site. Secondary analyses compared the 2 DOACs to warfarin. An intent-to-treat approach was used to mimic a RCT. Start of follow-up was defined as an enrollee’s first anticoagulant prescription date, hereby referred to as the “index date.” There were no missing data, because of the nature of the data source and covariate creation (ie, if a code was present for a comorbidity it was classified as “yes,” whereby if there was no code for a comorbidity it was classified as “no”). Though there were no apparent missing data, it is important to be mindful of misclassification, despite our use of validated algorithms when possible.
Analyses were performed using Cox regression to estimate adjusted HRs and corresponding 95% CIs for the associations of anticoagulant choice on hospitalized bleeding and mortality within 6 months. Follow-up was measured from the index date to either hospitalized bleeding or mortality date, loss of follow-up, censoring after 6 months, or December 31, 2019, whichever occurred first. Model 1 was adjusted for age, sex, race, and year. Model 2 adjusted for model 1 covariates and additionally for the Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI) which has been shown to demarcate diagnostic and prognostic differences among subgroups of patients sharing the same medical diagnosis. Comorbidities were assessed by compiling inpatient, outpatient, and carrier records that occurred from the date of Medicare enrollment to the index date. The list of comorbid conditions included in the CCI is listed in , and the ICD-9/10-CM codes used to define them can be found in . For analyses of cancer sites combined, we also adjusted for cancer site (categorial variable with 7 groups; the 6 cancers sites evaluated separately plus an “other” category). For the primary analysis (apixaban vs rivaroxaban), using cross-product terms we assessed whether there was multiplicative interaction by age category (65-74 vs 75+), sex (except for breast cancer which was only evaluated in women, and prostate cancer which was only evaluated in men), presence of kidney disease, and whether the ICD codes indicated the cancer was metastatic. Analyses were run using SAS v9.4 (SAS Institute Inc).

Interactions were not tested for upper GI cancers due to low numbers.
 

## Combined cancer
A total of 634 incident hospitalized bleeding events and 5,977 deaths were observed within 6 months of incident VTE. Hospitalized bleeding risk was lower among users of apixaban than users of rivaroxaban; in the fully adjusted model, the HR (95% CI) was 0.68 (0.56-0.83). When comparing the DOACs to warfarin, no statistically significant associations were observed in the fully adjusted model: apixaban vs warfarin HR: 0.88 (95% CI: 0.70-1.11), rivaroxaban vs warfarin HR: 1.12 (95% CI: 0.92-1.36). For the mortality outcome, HRs for all comparisons neared 1 (eg, HR apixaban vs rivaroxaban: 0.99 [95% CI: 0.91-1.08]).

## Breast cancer
A total of 5,980 patients with breast cancer and VTE were included. Within the 6 months after VTE, 126 patients had a hospitalized bleeding event and 827 had died. For hospitalized bleeding, there was suggestion of lower risk with apixaban vs rivaroxaban in the fully adjusted model, though precision was poor and the estimate was not statistically significant (HR [95% CI]: 0.72 [0.46-1.13]). For mortality, there were no statistically significant associations when comparing the OACs.

## Lung cancer
The 4,809 lung cancer patients with comorbid VTE included in the present analysis experienced 101 hospitalized bleeding events and 1,862 deaths by 6 months post-VTE. Though not statistically significant, there was indication that apixaban was associated with lower hospitalized bleeding risk than rivaroxaban, after accounting for confounders (HR [95% CI]: 0.63 [0.37-1.08]). When comparing the DOACs to warfarin, the HRs were 0.60 (0.33-1.10) for apixaban vs warfarin and 0.68 (0.42-1.11) for rivaroxaban vs warfarin, respectively. There was no evidence that risk of death varied by OAC prescribed (eg, HR apixaban vs rivaroxaban: 0.92 [0.79-1.06]).

## Prostate cancer
Among the 5,782 patients with prostate cancer and VTE included in this analysis, 115 experienced a hospitalized bleeding event and 722 died within 6 months of the incident VTE. For hospitalized bleeding risk, the fully adjusted HR (95% CI) was 0.76 (0.48-1.22) comparing apixaban to rivaroxaban. There were also no significant differences in bleeding risk when comparing the DOACs to warfarin. The HRs for risk of death were 1.26 (0.98-1.61) for patients prescribed apixaban vs rivaroxaban, while the HRs comparing the DOACs to warfarin were near 1.00.

## Colorectal cancer
A total of 3,470 patients with colorectal cancer and incident VTE were included in the analysis. Over 6 months of follow-up, 77 had an incident hospitalized bleeding event and 680 died. OAC prescribed was not significantly associated with risk of hospitalized bleeding, though the fully adjusted HR was 0.63 (0.35-1.15) comparing apixaban to rivaroxaban. When comparing the associations of DOACs to warfarin, there were no differences in bleeding risk. For mortality, the HR (95% CI) was 1.23 (0.95-1.59) among participants prescribed apixaban vs rivaroxaban, 1.30 (0.96-1.77) for apixaban vs warfarin, and 1.15 (0.93-1.43) for rivaroxaban vs warfarin.

## Genitourinary cancer
Among the 5,344 patients with genitourinary cancer included in our sample, 124 went on to have a hospitalized bleeding event and 1,152 died within 6 months. The HR (95% CI) for bleeding risk for apixaban vs rivaroxaban was 0.70 (0.44-1.12). Associations comparing DOACs to warfarin were not statistically significant. For death, HRs were near 1, across all comparisons.

## Upper GI cancer
Our analytic sample included 1,102 patients with upper GI cancer. These individuals went on to experience 21 bleeding events and 353 deaths. The HR for bleeding risk comparing apixaban vs rivaroxaban was 1.18 (0.42-3.32). Risk of death did not differ significantly by OAC used, across any of the comparisons.

## Discussion
In this observational analysis of 30,342 patients with Ca-VTE, aged 65 years and older, we assessed whether risk of hospitalized bleeding and death during the 6 months following the VTE event varied according to OAC prescribed. For our combined cancer outcome, apixaban was associated with approximately 32% lower bleeding risk than rivaroxaban ( ). Precision was poorer when stratifying by cancer site. For most cancer sites (ie, breast, colorectal, lung, and prostate) the association was in the direction of lower bleeding risk with apixaban than with rivaroxaban, though CIs were wide. In our secondary analyses, no clear patterns for bleeding risk emerged when comparing the DOACs to warfarin. For the mortality outcome, there was no overall association between anticoagulant choice and risk of death at 6 months.
 Novel aspects of this paper include the head-to-head comparisons of DOACs in a real-world setting, and stratification of analyses by cancer site. Additionally, we provide information on OAC choice and safety outcomes in the context of GI and genitourinary cancers.

## Bleeding
In the present analysis, we found approximately 32% lower bleeding risk with apixaban vs rivaroxaban in the first 6 months following a Ca-VTE event. When looking separately by cancer site, for most cancers explored (ie, breast, colorectal, lung, and prostate) the association was in the direction of lower bleeding risk comparing apixaban to rivaroxaban, though CIs were wide and results were not statistically significant. We also explicitly evaluated GI and genitourinary cancers, which are the 2 cancer sites that current ASCO guidelines noted may have increased major bleeding risk associated with use of DOACs. We did not find evidence of increased bleeding risk in genitourinary cancer patients using DOACs. For upper GI cancer patients with VTE, CIs were too wide to make meaningful conclusions.
While apixaban and rivaroxaban are both oral factor Xa inhibitors and have many similarities, there are differences which may have contributed to our findings. For instance, conventionally dosed rivaroxaban has been shown to have a greater reduction of thrombin generation than apixaban, suggesting a potential mechanism for increased bleeding. , Importantly, though in many ways apixaban and rivaroxaban are ideal to consider as active comparators, our analysis was not randomized and there may be differences in prescribing patterns which could explain our findings, despite our attempts to control for confounding.
RCTs of DOACs in the context of Ca-VTE have been conducted, including with major bleeding as a secondary outcome. In all instances, however, LMWH was the comparator. As noted in the Methods, we did not consider LMWH herein since LMWH has a different mode of administration. Nonetheless, a RCT of apixaban vs LMWH found that risk of major bleeding was similar between the 2 anticoagulants (HR apixaban vs LMWH: 0.82 [95% CI: 0.40-1.69]). Likewise, risk of major bleeding was similar when comparing edoxaban to LMHW. A trial was also conducted comparing rivaroxaban to LMWH on risk of bleeding, but the number of patients was insufficient and CIs were too wide to make meaningful interpretations. Lastly, the CANVAS (Cancer Related VTE Anticoagulation Strategies) trial randomized participants to a DOAC (could be dabigatran, rivaroxaban, apixaban, or edoxaban) vs LMWH. Major bleeding occurred in 5.2% of participants in the DOAC group and 5.6% in the LMWH group, and did not meet the noninferiority criterion.
Though RCTs among patients with Ca-VTE have not directly compared management with DOACs to warfarin, or conducted head-to-head evaluations of DOACs, a network meta-analysis of RCTs has been conducted. The network meta-analysis concluded that among the DOACs, apixaban had the lowest risk of major bleeding (indirect comparison risk ratio for apixaban vs rivaroxaban: 0.43 [95% CI: 0.22-0.85]). Results from this indirect comparison of RCTs aligns with our observational finding that apixaban is associated with the lowest bleeding risk.
Looking to the observational literature, a few studies have compared OACs in the context of Ca-VTE. In a study of 750 Ca-VTE patients from the Mayo Thrombophilia Clinic, the HR for major bleeding comparing apixaban vs rivaroxaban was 0.73 (95% CI: 0.32-1.66). Warfarin was not considered. A later analysis of Mayo Thrombophilia Clinic data specifically evaluated both GI and nongastrointesional cancer. The authors found that treatment with apixaban was associated with a numerically higher rate of major bleeding; however, the difference did not reach statistical significance (P = 0.1). Another study, which used data from 4 U.S. Commercial Claims databases, reported a lower risk of bleeding with apixaban vs warfarin (HR: 0.73; 95% CI: 0.53-1.00). Rivaroxaban was not considered as a comparator in that analysis. In the context of VTE events not provoked by cancer, observational studies have typically shown apixaban to have a lower risk of major bleeding events than rivaroxaban. , , 

## Mortality
In the present analysis, there was no overall association between OAC choice and risk of all-cause mortality. However, when stratifying by cancer site, there was some suggestion (albeit nonsignificant) that use of apixaban vs rivaroxaban was associated with greater mortality among patients with prostate or colorectal cancer and VTE. The directionality of these nonsignificant findings was not expected and warrants evaluation in independent samples.
To date, RCTs of comparing apixaban and rivaroxaban to each other, or to warfarin, have not been conducted among patients with Ca-VTE with mortality as an endpoint. However, there have been RCTs comparing DOACs to LMWH for the management of Ca-VTE. CIs were wide in all instances, and no statistically significant associations were observed. , , The RCTs that initially demonstrated the safety and efficacy of DOACs vs warfarin included very few participants with cancer. In the EINSTEIN RCT, rivaroxaban was shown to have noninferior efficacy compared to warfarin with respect to total deaths through the intended treatment period (HR: 0.67; 95% CI: 0.44-1.02). The AMPLIFY (Apixaban for the Initial Management of Pulmonary Embolism and Deep-Vein Thrombosis as First-Line Therapy) RCT, which compared the safety and efficacy of apixaban to warfarin, also showed noninferiority for the DOAC when all-cause mortality was the outcome (RR: 0.79; 95% CI: 0.53-1.19). 
In the previously mentioned observational analysis of 750 consecutive patients with Ca-VTE treated at the Mayo Thrombophilia Clinic, apixaban was associated with greater risk of death (HR: 1.67 [1.20-2.33]) relative to rivaroxaban. Few other studies have evaluated risk of death by OAC prescribed in patients with Ca-VTE. Our study is also unique because we conducted analyses according to cancer site.
The comparative safety of DOACs on mortality among non–Ca-VTE patients has also been evaluated in observational settings. An analysis of Medicare data that included >47,000 individuals found that risk of death was similar by OAC prescribed, though there was some suggestion of lower mortality with rivaroxaban. Another study of non–CA-VTE patients, which used claims data from OptumLab Data Warehouse and 6-month mortality as the outcome, found no difference between rivaroxaban vs warfarin and apixaban vs warfarin. That study did not evaluate apixaban vs rivaroxaban.

## Clinical implications
There are several clinical implications of these findings. First, they report on the safety of DOACs vs warfarin for management of Ca-VTE in terms of bleeding risk. This pattern of safety was also observed across cancer sites, including in genitourinary cancers where current ASCO guidelines suggest there may be greater bleeding risk with DOACs. Unfortunately, precision was too poor in our analyses to make meaningful conclusions about the risk of bleeding with apixaban or rivaroxaban vs warfarin among patients with upper GI cancer. Second, our head-to-head DOAC comparisons suggest that apixaban may be associated with lower bleeding risk than rivaroxaban. This pattern was observed overall, and with most individual cancers studied. It is possible that in the absence of other decisional factors, apixaban may be preferred for patients at high risk of bleeding. Lastly, there was no overall association between OAC prescribed and risk of death, which suggests that factors other than overall mortality should be considered most prominently when deciding between OACs.

## Strengths and limitations
A major strength of this analysis is its breadth; 3 OAC VTE treatment options were evaluated with risk of hospitalized bleeding and death among patients with any cancer, and cancer at 6 different sites. The study population spans the United States and is largely representative of American adults aged 65 years and older. However, our results may not be generalizable to younger patients with Ca-VTE. Misclassification is an important consideration when utilizing administrative claims data. When defining cancer, VTE, comorbidities, and hospitalized bleeding we used validated algorithms whenever possible. Nonetheless, some misclassification certainly occurred. The validity of death in these data is excellent, given linkage to the Social Security Administration. A limitation of these analyses is that, for the bleeding outcome, we only considered events that were associated with inpatient hospitalization, in accord with the validated Cunningham alogrithm. Bleeding events that were managed on an outpatient basis or did not receive clinical attention were not considered, due to concerns about the validity of an outpatient-based definition, and lack of information about nonclinical events. Another limitation is that we did not consider recurrent VTE as an outcome, due to our concern about the validity of defining recurrent VTE using ICD codes. Confounding and indication bias are also important threats to validity; to mitigate these we adjusted for numerous comorbidities via the CCI. However, it is possible that our efforts were inadequate. We also tried to limit various forms of selection bias by using a new user active comparator design. The design, however, is observational. RCTs are needed to make definitive causal claims about the safety and efficacy of these OACs in the management of Ca-VTE. Lastly, our analysis was underpowered for some associations, particularly those involving VTE associated with upper GI cancers or when assessing interactions.

## Conclusions
VTE is an important complication of cancer, and improving its outcomes is imperative to reducing morbidity and mortality among patients with cancer. Our findings from this real-world analysis provide reassurance about the bleeding profile associated with DOACs in adults aged 65 years and older with Ca-VTE. Though CIs were wide, DOACs also appeared safe across various cancer sites. Apixaban use was associated with lower risk of bleeding than rivaroxaban use in our analysis. We found no association overall between OAC prescribed and risk of all-cause mortality in this population. Ideally in the future, a RCT of OAC options to treat CA-VTE should be powered for subgroup analyses by cancer site.
