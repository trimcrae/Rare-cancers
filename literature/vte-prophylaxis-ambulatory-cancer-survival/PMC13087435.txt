## ABSTRACT

## Background
Direct oral anticoagulants (DOACs) have demonstrated non‐inferiority to low‐molecular‐weight heparin (LMWH) for preventing recurrent venous thromboembolism (VTE) in cancer‐associated thrombosis. However, patients with gastrointestinal (GI) cancer receiving DOACs have reported increased major bleeding events. Hereby, we aim to compare the risks of recurrent thromboembolism and bleeding between those treated with DOACs and LMWH.

## Methods
Using Taiwan's National Health Insurance Research Database, we retrospectively identified GI cancer patients with newly diagnosed VTE from 2011 to 2020. Outcomes were recurrent VTE and major bleeding. Covariate balancing was performed using inverse probability weighting, and Cox proportional hazards models compared risks, with a competing risk model for death. Follow‐up was 12 months.

## Results
Among 1895 patients (1038 on DOACs, 857 on enoxaparin), recurrent VTE occurred in 20.71% of DOAC and 19.25% of enoxaparin patients. Competing risk analysis showed relatively higher recurrent VTE risk with DOACs (subdistribution HR, 1.27; 95% CI, 1.09–1.48; p < 0.01). Regarding composite bleeding risk, DOACs were similar compared with LMWH (subdistribution HR, 0.99; 95% CI, 0.68–1.45; p = 0.97). However, DOACs presented with an increased GI bleeding risk (subdistribution HR, 2.59; 95% CI, 1.48–4.54; p < 0.01) but a reduced intracranial hemorrhage risk (subdistribution HR, 0.35; 95% CI, 0.16–0.78; p = 0.01).

## Conclusions
Compared with DOACs, LMWH expressed a lower risk of recurrent VTE in GI cancer patients. However, DOAC presented with a lower intracranial hemorrhage risk but a higher GI bleeding risk. Further research is needed to confirm these findings.

## Efficacy and Safety Outcomes Comparing DOACs and LMWH Therapy with 1 Year Follow‐Up Duration
Among 1895 patients (1038 on DOACs and 857 on enoxaparin), recurrent VTE occurred in 20.7% of DOAC users and 19.3% of enoxaparin users. Figure  shows the unadjusted Kaplan–Meier curve for VTE risk, indicating no significant difference in survival probability between the two groups during 1 year of follow‐up. Consistently, the hazard ratio (HR) for recurrent VTE did not differ significantly between DOAC and LMWH users (HR, 0.92; 95% CI, 0.79–1.07; p = 0.29) (Table  ). However, when accounting for death as a competing risk, the sHR for recurrent VTE was significantly higher among DOAC users compared with LMWH users (sHR, 1.27; 95% CI, 1.09–1.48; p < 0.01) (Table  ).
 With respect to bleeding outcomes, DOACs were associated with a lower composite bleeding risk than LMWH (HR, 0.62; 95% CI, 0.42–0.90; p = 0.01) (Table  ), although the unadjusted Kaplan–Meier curve for composite bleeding showed no significant difference between the groups (Figure  ). Similarly, after accounting for death as a competing risk, the risk of composite bleeding did not differ between DOAC and LMWH users (sHR, 0.99; 95% CI, 0.68–1.45; p = 0.97) (Table  ). In further analyses, DOACs were associated with a higher risk of gastrointestinal bleeding (HR, 2.59; 95% CI, 1.48–4.54; p < 0.01), but a lower risk of intracranial hemorrhage (HR, 0.35; 95% CI, 0.16–0.78; p = 0.01). Additionally, DOACs reduced the risk of other critical‐site bleedings (sHR, 0.10; 95% CI, 0.02–0.54; p = 0.01) compared with LMWH users (Table  ).

## Efficacy and Safety Outcomes Comparing DOACs and LMWH Therapy with the Maximal Follow‐Up Duration
Furthermore, we extended the follow‐up period to the maximum duration permitted by the database to evaluate the long‐term effects of DOAC therapy compared with LMWH in patients with GI cancer. The extended follow‐up results were consistent with our initial findings, showing no significant difference in VTE outcomes between the two groups (HR, 1.00; 95% CI, 0.86–1.16; p = 0.96) (Table  ). However, when accounting for death as a competing risk, DOAC users demonstrated a higher risk of recurrent VTE than LMWH users (sHR, 1.45; 95% CI, 1.25–1.67; p < 0.01) (Table  ). In contrast, while DOAC use was associated with a reduced risk of other critical‐site bleeding (sHR, 0.17; 95% CI, 0.05–0.58; p < 0.01), it was linked to an increased risk of gastrointestinal bleeding (sHR, 3.00; 95% CI, 1.94–4.64; p < 0.01), alongside a nonsignificant trend toward lower intracranial hemorrhage risk (sHR, 0.79; 95% CI, 0.47–1.33; p = 0.37) compared with LMWH use.

## Subgroup Analyses Focusing on Recurrent VTE and Bleeding
As illustrated in Figure  , our subgroup analysis of recurrent VTE associated with GI cancer highlights that patients under 65 years of age demonstrated a reduced risk of VTE when treated with DOACs. Similarly, patients with more advanced cancer (grades 3 and 4) also showed a statistically significant benefit from treatment with DOACs. Moreover, a notably significant improvement was observed in patients with a history of antiplatelet agent use. Figure  presents the K‐M curve for VTE risk, illustrating no statistically significant differences in VTE occurrence between patients treated with different anticoagulants over the study period (p = 0.17).
 As illustrated in Figure  , the subgroup analysis of composite bleeding in patients with GI cancer presents that those with chronic kidney disease benefited more from DOAC use. Patients with a history of bleeding showed a stronger response to DOAC treatment. Moreover, the subgroup of patients without a history of antiplatelet agent use also experienced a lowered risk when treated with DOACs. Figure  depicts the K‐M curve for composite bleeding events, illustrating the cumulative incidence of bleeding complications across different anticoagulants without any statistically significant differences observed during the study period (p = 0.20).

## Discussion
In this study, we observed that among 1895 patients with GI cancer–associated VTE, one‐year outcomes showed no significant difference in recurrent VTE between DOAC and LMWH users (HR, 0.92; p = 0.29). However, when accounting for death as a competing risk, DOACs were associated with higher VTE recurrence (sHR, 1.27; p < 0.01). Regarding safety, although DOACs showed no significant difference in composite bleeding risks compared with LMWH (sHR, 0.99; p = 0.97), further analyses revealed increased gastrointestinal bleeding but reduced intracranial and other critical‐site bleeding with DOACs. Extended follow‐up confirmed these findings with similar directional effects. The major findings were summarized in Figure  .
 Although currently, guidelines from the American Society of Clinical Oncology (ASCO) and the International Society on Thrombosis and Hemostasis (ISTH) have gradually shifted toward accepting DOAC use as an alternative to LMWH, this trend also underscores the importance of individual patient assessment, particularly considering factors like bleeding risk and life expectancy [ , , ]. In contrast to previous reports [ , , , ], we observed that DOACs may carry a higher risk of GI bleeding while relatively reducing the risk of intracranial hemorrhage. Of note, our subgroup analysis of younger patients (aged under 65 years) and those with advanced cancer (grades 3 and 4) showed a reduced risk of recurrent VTE with DOAC treatment. The observed reduced VTE risk in younger patients aligns with the previous body of evidence, reinforcing the idea that age is a critical factor in anticoagulant therapy, and younger patients with cancer may derive a greater benefit from DOAC use with lower associated risks. In terms of advanced‐stage cancer, which is often associated with a hypercoagulable state [ ], leading to higher rates of VTE, the reduction in VTE risk in patients with advanced GI cancer treated with DOACs suggests that these medications may offer a protective effect even in more aggressive disease stages, which is a positive finding given the challenging nature of managing thrombosis in this patient cohort. Our present results may help refine patient selection for DOAC therapy, suggesting that these subgroups could be prioritized for DOAC treatment to maximize benefit and reduce VTE recurrence. However, our data also underscore the need to continue monitoring for bleeding complications, especially in patients with GI cancers.
Among LMWHs, tinzaparin has been extensively evaluated in cancer patients and offers several practical and pharmacological advantages, including once‐daily dosing, predictable pharmacokinetics, and a lower potential for drug–drug interactions [ , ]. Importantly, tinzaparin has demonstrated favorable safety and efficacy profiles across diverse oncology settings, making it particularly relevant for patients with renal insufficiency, polypharmacy, or heightened gastrointestinal bleeding risk. While DOACs provide convenience and are increasingly used in routine practice, LMWHs such as tinzaparin remain crucial for individualized anticoagulation strategies in complex cancer populations [ , ]. Nevertheless, our study did not specifically evaluate different LMWH agents, including tinzaparin, and therefore cannot directly compare their relative effectiveness or safety. Future studies incorporating agent‐level analyses are warranted to better define optimal anticoagulant selection in oncology patients.
Our study differs from prior randomized trials and meta‐analyses that suggested DOACs reduce the risk of recurrent VTE in cancer patients [ , , , , ]. In our GI cancer cohort, we observed no reduction in recurrent VTE with DOACs compared with LMWH, and competing risk analysis revealed a relatively higher risk of recurrence among DOAC users. Several factors may explain these discrepant findings. First, the patient population is an important distinction. RCTs typically enrolled heterogeneous cancer cohorts with relatively preserved functional status, whereas our study focused on GI cancers, which are strongly prothrombotic and carry a higher risk of both recurrence and bleeding [ , , ]. Second, mortality was high in our cohort, with nearly half of patients dying within 1 year [ ]. HR from Cox models estimate relative risk under the assumption of independent censoring [ ]. In cancer populations with high competing mortality, this assumption is violated, as death precludes subsequent events such as recurrent VTE or bleeding [ ]. sHRs from Fine–Gray competing risk models incorporate death as a competing event, providing risk estimates more reflective of real‐world patient trajectories [ , ]. In our analysis, while HRs suggested no significant difference in VTE recurrence between DOACs and LMWH, sHRs revealed a higher recurrence risk with DOACs, underscoring the importance of competing risk adjustment in oncology populations. Finally, real‐world factors including older, frailer patients, comorbidities, and variability in adherence may further contribute to outcome differences [ ]. Clinicians should carefully individualize anticoagulant choice in GI cancer patients, balancing recurrence and bleeding risks.

## Limitations
This study has several limitations. First, retrospective studies are subject to inherent biases, such as selection bias, information bias, and potential misclassification of exposure and outcomes [ ]. Our findings may have been influenced by factors not fully captured or controlled for in the database, such as variations in cancer severity, differences in concurrent treatments, and patient adherence to prescribed therapies. Second, the study is based on a Taiwanese population, and this may limit the generalizability of the results to other populations with different ethnic backgrounds, healthcare systems, and treatment practices. Third, although our study used stabilized IPTW to balance baseline covariates, there may still be unmeasured confounding factors that could affect the outcomes, such as detailed cancer staging, specific tumor characteristics, nutritional status, and the presence of other comorbidities. Fourth, although we carefully selected ICD codes based on prior literature and validated coding accuracy, misclassification remains a limitation in real‐world database analyses [ , ]. To note, our study demonstrated a recurrent VTE rate of 21.3%, which is substantially higher than the recurrence rates reported in pivotal RCTs of NOACs in cancer‐associated thrombosis (ranging from 0.7% to 7.9%) [ , , ]. This discrepancy likely reflects differences between trial populations and real‐world practice, including more advanced cancer stages, higher comorbidity burden, and less stringent follow‐up in routine care. For example, Chen et al. reported recurrent VTE or major bleeding in 14.1% of NOAC users [ ]. These findings underscore the need for careful patient selection and vigilant monitoring. Fifth, the CARAVAGGIO trial found that all GI bleeding in GI cancer patients occurred in those with unresected tumors but in our study, we could not determine tumor resectability [ , ]. The presence of unresected or advanced tumors may sustain hypercoagulability and diminish the protective effect of DOACs observed in broader populations [ , ]. Also, hereby bleeding complications were restricted to ICH, GI, and other bleeding, whereas important sites such as genitourinary bleeding, a common source of bleeding in this population, were not captured. Last, for anticoagulant drugs ATC codes are required to ensure precision [ ]. In contrast, for chemotherapy or radiotherapy, ICD codes such as Z51.11 (antineoplastic chemotherapy) and Z51.0 (radiotherapy) are generally sufficient, as they capture treatment encounters across both inpatient and outpatient settings, consistent with previous studies [ , ]. However, when distinguishing between specific chemotherapeutic agents (e.g., cisplatin vs. oxaliplatin), ATC codes provide greater specificity and are therefore more appropriate.

6

## Conclusions
In this large real‐world cohort of patients with gastrointestinal cancer–associated VTE, DOAC use was not associated with a lower risk of recurrent VTE compared with LMWH when accounting for death as a competing risk. Meanwhile, DOACs were associated with increased gastrointestinal bleeding but reduced intracranial and other critical‐site bleeding compared with LMWH. However, due to the study design and other limitations, further prospective studies or randomized controlled trials are needed to validate these findings and better inform clinical practice.
