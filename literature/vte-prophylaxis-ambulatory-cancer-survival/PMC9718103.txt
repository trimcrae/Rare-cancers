## Abstract

## Objective
To assess the extent and trends in registration of Orthopaedic randomized clinical trials (RCTs) between 2015 and 2020.

## Design
Epidemiological study. Primary publications of RCTs published in top Orthopaedic journals (ISI Journal Citation Reports 2019 rankings) between 2015 and 2020 were included in this meta-epidemiological study with no restrictions on patient population, intervention/control groups or outcome type. Independent reviewers in pairs were involved in RCT selection and data extraction. The proportion of RCTs published that were registered (prospectively or retrospectively) or not registered were reported using counts and percentages stratified by years for each journal.

## Results
A total of 474 primary RCTs were considered eligible. We identified 157 out of 474 RCTs (33% of RCTs across journals) that were reported to have been registered prospectively.
The proportion of prospective RCT registrations had increased by 40% (10%–50%) between 2015 and 2020. On the other hand, the proportion of RCTs with no registrations were reduced by 29% (50%–21%) between 2015 and 2020.

## Conclusion
Prospective RCT registration in the past 5 years in the field of orthopaedic has increased, but 2/3 of published RCTs still failed to report prospective registration.

Keywords: Randomised clinical trials, Transparency, State of evidence, Orthopaedics

1.

## Introduction
Large randomized clinical trials (RCTs), conducted properly, are considered the most rigorous method of establishing cause-effect relationship between treatment and outcome(s). An RCT is placed second on the hierarchy of evidence, only to systematic reviews of such RCTs [ , , ].
In theory, RCTs guard against systematic error (bias), however, it is of paramount importance that authors ensure adequate reporting of RCT design, maintain high level of transparency and rigor in RCT conduct and to ensure that trials are reproducible [ , , ]. The International Committee of Medical Journal Editors (ICMJE) 2005 recommendations, the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) 2010 statement, and the Standard Protocol Items: Recommendations for Interventional Trials (SPIRIT) 2013 Statement and that of n-of-1 trials, have all been published to enhance the reporting, transparency, rigor and reproducibility of RCTs [ , , , , ].
The ICMJE recommendations support medical journals, editors and authors/researchers to create and publish accurate, transparent and reproducible studies/trials [ , , ]. The ICMJE mandates have been put forth for use by RCT authors who intend to publish in ICMJE member journals [ , , ]. Furthermore, non-ICMJE member journals have also voluntarily and explicitly begun to adhere to such (ICMJE) recommendations in their “Instructions for Authors” sections of their respective journals [ , , ]. The ICMJE “requires, and recommends that all medical journal editors require, registration of clinical trials in a public trials registry at or before the time of first patient enrollment as a condition of consideration for publication” [ , , ]. This ICMJE trial registration mandate is aimed to a large extent to minimize selective publication and reporting of outcome biases. In addition, it attempts to potentially prevent the conduct of similar studies and to set up a platform to fascinate and enable patients to enroll in current or future trials of interest [ , , ].
A recent review indicated that the publication of RCTs in the field of Orthopaedic have increased steadily over the last few years [ ]. However, it necessary to evaluate the degree to which proper trial registration in public trials registries is being used as an indicator of the quality of emerging RCTs in this field. Rongen 2016 review indicated that between years 2010–2014, only 3% (12 out of the 362 RCTs) in the field of Orthopaedics were prospectively registered (registration prior to first patient enrollment) [ ].
Rationale and Objectives:
Considering that a) all of the top journals in the field of Orthopaedics explicitly state in their “Instructions for Authors” the “required” need to adhere to ICMJE mandates regarding RCT registration, b) there has been a steady increase in number of RCTs published in the field of Orthopaedics; c) RCTs play a critical role in producing high quality evidence; and d) that there is a paucity of evidence regarding the adherence to ICMJE recommendations for RCTs in the field of Orthopaedics beyond year 2014, we aimed:
1.to examine the proportion of RCTs published that were reported to have been registered (prospectively or retrospective) or not registered between 2015 and 2020 in top Orthopaedic journals.
2.to assess the association between RCT registration and Orthopaedic journals, that is, which journals are more likely to publish greater number of RCTs that were adequately registered?

2.

## Methods
2.1.

## Protocol registration
We followed the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines [ , ].

2.2.

## Eligibility criteria
Primary publications of RCTs published between September 9, 2015 and September 9, 2020 in top Orthopedics journals were included in this systematic review with no restrictions on patient population (condition/disease), intervention/control groups or outcome type (i.e. continuous, dichotomous outcome or time to event). We defined an RCT as a comparative study reporting a random assignment of patients/participants (reporting “random,” “randomized”, “randomised”, “randomization”, randomization”) anywhere in the report [ , ]. We excluded duplicate, protocol, systematic review, (network) meta-analysis, analysis plans, feasibility, pilot or phase 1 publications. In addition, we excluded subgroup, exploratory, interim, pooled, post hoc, secondary outcome or economic/cost-effectiveness analyses, preliminary results/studies, ancillary/sub-studies, laboratory studies, measurement studies, proof-of-concept studies or mechanism research [ , , ].

2.3.

## Information sources
PubMed was systematically searched on September 10, 2020, to identify primary publications of RCTs published between September 9, 2015 and September 9, 2020. Specifically, the primary publications of RCTs published in the ten highest impact factor Orthopedics journals, as reported by ISI Journal Citation Reports 2019 [ ].
Orthopaedic journals rankings according to ISI Journal Citation Reports 2019 were as follows [ ]:
1.American Journal of Sports Medicine
2.Journal of Physiotherapy
3.Osteoarthritis and Cartilage
4.Journal of Bone and Joint Surgery-American volume
5.Clinical Orthopaedics and Related Research
6.Arthroscopy-The Journal of Arthroscopic and Related Surgery
7.Bone & Joint Journal
8.Journal of Orthopaedic Translation
9.Cartilage
10.Journal of Orthopaedic & Sports Physical Therapy

2.5.

## Study selection
Study selection was performed using Covidence (https://www.covidence.org, Melbourne VIC 3000, Australia). One review author performed the systematic electronic search in PubMed and retrieved all the publications. The retrieved studies were then uploaded to the Covidence and the software automatically removed the duplicates. Two review authors then applied the inclusion/exclusion criteria and screened studies based upon titles and abstracts. In the event of disagreement, the opinion of a third reviewer was requested. Retrieved studies were then classified as ‘yes’ or ‘maybe’. The articles of the “yes” and “maybe” records were moved through Covidence for full-text screening by the same two independent reviewers. In the event of disagreement concerning inclusion or exclusion of full text studies, the opinion of a third reviewer was requested (i.e. a third reviewer resolved the conflicts). The Journal of Physiotherapy was listed under “rehabilitation” subcategory; therefore, its published RCTs were not considered.

2.6.

## Data collection process
Two independent reviewers performed data extraction from the included RCTs and identified (if available) the corresponding trial registration. First one reviewer individually extracted data for all the included RCTs. Then, to further ensure accurate data extraction, the primary author (GN) independently performed duplicate data extraction from all included RCTs. Extracted data was then cross checked for accuracy and consistency. For every primary publication of published RCT we extracted the (authors, year, study title, citation, journal, type of registration, if any). For registered information, that is, from the trial registries (if available), we extracted (authors, year, name of registry/registration number, registration date, start date/start of patient enrollment date, end date/end of patient enrollment date, current status).

2.7.

## Data items
Identifying trial registries: For each published RCT, we identified the registration number/name of registry via trial registries (International Clinical Trials Registry Platform (ICTRP), ClinicalTrials.gov, Health Canada Clinical Trial Database, EU Clinical Trials Register, German Clinical Trials Register, Netherlands Trial Register (Dutch), Swiss National Clinical Trials Portal, ISRCTN, Australian New Zealand Clinical Trials Registry, Chinese Clinical Trial Registry, Clinical Trials Registry – India, Iranian Registry of Clinical Trials, Japan Primary Registries Network, Clinical Research Information Service, Australian New Zealand Clinical Trials Registry, Philippine Health Research Registry, Sri Lanka Clinical Trials Registry, Thai Clinical Trials Registry, Brazilian Clinical Trials Registry, Public Cuban Registry of Clinical Trials, Peruvian Registry of Clinical Trials, Pan African Clinical Trials Registry, South African National Clinical Trials Register, Tanzania Clinical Trial Registry).
If a registration number with no corresponding trial registry name, or the trial registry name with no registration number were reported, we performed a manual search to identify the corresponding trial registry name or registration number, respectively. If we were unable to identify any registration number or trial registry name, the published RCT was considered not registered [ , , ].
Journals “Instructions for Authors”: The primary author assessed the “Instructions for Authors” for each journal and checked whether each journal clearly requested 1) the registration of an RCT (and as of when, if applicable) before submission of an RCT and 2) if the journal referred authors to the ICMJE guidelines about RCT registration.

2.8.

## Summary measures/synthesis of results
The proportion of RCTs published that were registered (prospectively or retrospectively) or not registered were reported using counts and percentages, stratified by years. This proportion statistic was therefore interpreted as “at least x% of RCTs were prospectively or retrospectively registered or not registered”. We followed ICMJE recommendations concerning RCT registrations and categorized RCTs as follows:
1)the final set of included RCTs without trial registrations were categorized as “RCTs not registered”.
2)the RCTs that were registered after RCT commencement (i.e. trial registration date was after the start of first patient enrollment date) were categorized as “RCTs registered retrospectively”.
3)the RCTs that were registered prior to RCT commencement (i.e. trial registration date was prior to the start of first patient enrollment date) were categorized as “RCTs registered prospectively”.
The categorization of types of registrations (prospectively or retrospectively or no registration) were performed by 2 reviewers independently. The two reviewers each individually categorized all the included RCTs (in duplicate). The agreements between the types of categorization of registrations (prospectively or retrospectively or no registration) were assessed using Kappa (κ) coefficient between the two reviewers.
To further examine the association between RCT registration and Orthopaedic journals, we combined the categories (RCTs not registered and RCTs registered retrospectively) into the category of RCTs with “Inadequate registration”. The RCTs registered prospectively category was not altered and was kept as it was. For each journal comparison, we tabulated all information in a 2x2 table relating the type of trial registration (prospective registration vs inadequate registration/total RCTs) to journal pairs (for example, journal A vs journal B). An odds ratio (M ​− ​H, random effect) was then calculated from the 2x2 table for every journal pair. Odds ratios greater than 1.0 meant that a specific journal had higher odds of publishing a prospectively registered RCT compared with another given journal. Odds ratios less than 1.0 meant that a specific journal had higher odds of publishing an inadequately registered (retrospectively or not registered) RCT compared with another given journal.

5.

## Discussion
5.1.

## Summary of evidence
Our meta-epidemiological study found that approximately one-third of the RCTs published between 2015 and 2020 were prospectively registered and the remaining two-thirds were inadequately registered (retrospective or no registration). The proportion of prospectively registered RCTs had increased by 40% and percentage of RCTs with no trial registration had decreased by 29%, between 2015 and 2020 in the field of Orthopaedic.

5.2.

## Agreements/disagreements with other reviews
The ICJME guidelines for RCT registration was introduced in 2005. This initiative mandated that RCT authors register trials prior to first patient enrollment (prior to commencement of the RCT – prospectively), in order to enhance RCT transparency and reproducibility, author accountability, and public access [ , , , ]. Rongen 2016 review indicated that between years 2010–2014, only 3% (12 out of the 362 RCTs) in the field of Orthopaedics were prospectively registered (registration prior to first patient enrollment) [ ]. Our review indicated that between years 2015–2020, 33% of RCTs (157/474 RCTs) were reported to have been prospectively registered (registration prior to first patient) in the field of Orthopaedics. This is was a considerable (30%) increase in prospective RCT registrations in the field of Orthopaedics. Furthermore, our estimate of 33% (157/474 RCTs with prospective registration) corresponded well with Mathieu 2009. The report by Mathieu 2009 indicated that 45% (147 out of the 323) of RCTs from fields of cardiology, gastroenterology, and rheumatology were adequately registered [ ]. Upon further investigation of Mathieu 2009 review, the “adequately registered” category referred to RCTs that were registered before the end of the trial, and not necessarily (or primarily) prior to first patient enrollment [ ]. This is an important distinction, and had a more stringent classification of “adequate registration” been defined (i.e. prior to first patient enrollment) a lower proportion of RCTs with prospective registrations would have been identified. The lack of any RCT registration (no registrations) based on our current work was 33% (157/474 RCTs). Similarly, this proportion corresponded well with that of Mathieu 2009 review of 28% (90 out of 323 RCTs in cardiology, gastroenterology, rheumatology) [ ].
There is a concerning gap between the target of 100% registration implied by author instructions in all of the reviewed journals which was intended to enact the ICMJE statement that “requires, and recommends that all medical journal editors require, registration of clinical trials in a public trials registry at or before the time of first patient enrollment as a condition of consideration for publication”. There are some reasons why prospective registration was not feasible in the early days of the recommendation implementation since some RCTs would have started before the recommendation came out. That is why journals allowed a discretionary period where registration was either not required or allowed to be retrospectively performed. Retrospective registration supports the importance of documenting the trial intentions but does not protect against bias as intended. However, at this point trials coming forward should have had adequate opportunity to prospectively register. Although the trend has been for improvement in the proportion of RCTs registered, the lag in full implementation suggests inadequate attention to the importance of registration by both researchers and journal editors.
There are multiple points of contact where RCT registration can be enforced. Funders could refuse to release funds until registration proofs and ethics boards can refuse to approve protocols before registration is completed. This is likely to be more effective than placing the burden on journal editors since enforcement should occur when it can change the outcome. Research training should include training in RCT best practices and instill the importance of registration. Adherence to registration should be checked prior to submitting RCTs to review, and only unique exceptions should be made. For example, publication of trials that started prior to the guideline or where the potential for public good exceeds the potential for harm due to bias e.g. pandemic trials that may not have registered should be rare exceptions.
We identified all the RCTs published from top Orthopaedic journals between 2015 and 2020. All the top Orthopaedic journals explicitly stated in their “Instructions for Authors” sections the need for prospective RCT registration and adherence to ICMJE guidelines. Despite this, the proportion of RCTs with retrospective registration (34%) or no registrations (33%) were still high. However, our results also indicated that the overall trends in prospective RCT registrations in Orthopaedics have been greatly improving since 2010. Possible explanations for such improving trajectory may be due to journal editors' carefully assessment of registration details upon manuscript submission (by authors) and/or RCT authors’ strict adherence to ICMJE mandates and pre-planning.
We would also like to highlight some caveats in our meta-epidemiological study. First, we primarily focused on and included published RCTs and compared it with records in registries and did not take into consideration the proportion of prospectively registered Orthopaedics trials that were discontinued due to lack of funding, never published due to unfavorable findings, and/or rejected due to lack of novelty. Second, we only examined the proportion of published RCTs that were registered or not registered in the top Orthopaedics journals. This proportion was not examined in other respected Orthopaedics journals. It is possible that our indicated proportions be over- or under-estimates of the estimates for the entire field. However, we expected that higher impact journals were more likely to have the most rigorous editorial procedures and be likely to attract top RCTs. Lastly, we considered and categorized all the RCTs with no registration number and/or no name of registry in the published manuscript as “not registered”. It may be possible that some of the RCTs might have been prospectively registered, but the author(s) simply failed to indicate the corresponding registration number and/or name of registry in the published manuscript. However, we considered this scenario to be very unlikely mainly due to the fact that RCT registration mandates (i.e. the ICMJE guidelines) were first introduced 15 years ago, and that since 2005, multiple reporting guidelines for RCTs (CONSORT 2010, SPIRIT 2013, and their extension-versions) have all been published and endorsed by numerous journals further emphasizing the need for appropriate RCT registration (registration prior to first patient enrollment). Further any protocol that is not accessible and known to have been published prior to RCT commencement does not allow people to assess the extent to which the RCT adhered to its a priori protocol.

6.

## Implications for research
Journal editors and reviewers, funders and ethics boards should check trial registration prior to subjecting RCTs to peer review process to further enforce adherence to the requirement for RCTs to be registered prior to first patient enrollment in the field of Orthopaedics.

7.

## Conclusions
Fifteen years after the introduction of standards for RCT registration, only one-third of the RCTs in the field of Orthopaedics were prospectively registered (2015–2020). The remaining two-thirds were inadequately registered (retrospectively or no registration). Further steps are necessary to enforce this important mechanism for enhancing the integrity of RCTs in the field of Orthopaedics.
