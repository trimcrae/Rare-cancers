## Abstract

## Background
The risk of major bleeding from anticoagulant treatment is influenced by both the treatment and the patient's baseline risk, which is often disregarded.

## Objectives
To determine the excess bleeding risk in venous thromboembolism (VTE) cases during initial (0 to 6 months) and extended (6 months to 5 years) treatment compared to matched population controls without VTE or anticoagulant treatment, overall, and stratified by sex and age.

## Methods
Cancer‐free patients with VTE treated with direct oral anticoagulants from 2014 to 2020, along with propensity score–matched controls, were identified from nationwide Swedish registers. Excess risk of major bleeding was assessed using the incidence rate difference (IRD) calculated by subtracting the control bleeding rate from the case bleeding rate.

## Results
The matched cohort comprised 36,115 VTE cases and 36,115 controls. During initial treatment, 388 VTE cases (1.07%) and 103 controls (0.29%) experienced bleeding, IRD: 2.19 (95% confidence interval 1.89–2.49) per 100 person‐years. Following rematching at 6 months, 139 cases (0.70%) and 214 controls (1.08%) experienced bleeding, IRD: 0.70 (0.52–0.89). During initial treatment, females had a higher excess bleeding risk than males, with male IRD: 1.73 (1.34–2.12) and female IRD: 2.69 (2.23–3.15). Excess bleeding risk was highest in the oldest patient population. In extended treatment, excess bleeding was not dependent on sex—male IRD: 0.60 (0.35–0.85), female IRD: 0.81 (0.54–1.08)—and did not increase with age.

## Conclusion
The excess bleeding risk from anticoagulant treatment was high during initial treatment, particularly among females and the elderly, but lower and not influenced by sex or age during extended treatment.

## Comorbidities and concomitant medications
The following comorbidities were captured from 7 years before and up to the date of the VTE diagnosis: liver disease, renal failure, inflammatory bowel disease, dementia, heart failure, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), ischemic stroke, peripheral artery disease, ischemic heart disease, diabetes, hypertension, systemic connective tissue disorders, hemorrhagic stroke, and atrial fibrillation (for ICD codes, see Table ). Previous bleeding events were recorded if they occurred within 7 years before the VTE diagnosis, excluding those that occurred on the same date as the VTE diagnosis. The following concomitant medications were recorded if a prescription was filled at least once during follow‐up periods (0–6 months or 6 months to 5 years): proton pump inhibitors (PPIs), statins, and selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs).

## Outcomes
Major bleeding was defined as a principal/first‐position inpatient diagnosis of bleeding (intracranial, gastrointestinal, hematuria, airway bleeding, anemia due to bleeding/iron deficiency, hemopericardium, and gynecological bleeding) or bleeding as an underlying or contributing cause of death in the Cause of Death Register. Fatal bleeding was defined as a diagnosis of bleeding as an underlying or contributing cause of death in the Cause of Death Register or primary inpatient diagnosis and death within 30 days. The ICD codes for bleeding are provided in Table .
Cases and controls were followed until major bleeding, death, diagnosis of cancer, start of anticoagulant treatment for controls, end of anticoagulant treatment, or switch to another anticoagulant medication for cases, 5 years after diagnosis of VTE, or December 31, 2020. Controls suffering a VTE were censored as controls and became cases on the date of admission to the hospital. The duration of DOAC treatment, used to determine follow‐up time, was calculated by dividing the number of tablets dispensed for each anticoagulant medication by the daily tablet usage. This calculation included a maximum allowable gap of 14 days between the date the tablets were depleted and the subsequent refill.

## Statistical analysis
Continuous baseline characteristics variables were described by median and interquartile range (IQR), and categorical variables by frequencies and percentages.
To facilitate a comparison between VTE cases and controls, each VTE case was matched with one control person (1:1 propensity score matching) without replacement with a 2.5% caliper both at baseline and after 6 months of treatment. The following matching variables were included: sex, date of VTE, documented history of hospitalization for bleeding, liver disease, renal failure, inflammatory bowel disease, dementia, heart failure, COPD, ischemic stroke, peripheral artery disease, ischemic heart disease, diabetes, hypertension, systemic connective tissue disorders, hemorrhagic stroke, atrial fibrillation antiplatelet treatment, PPI, statins, and SSRI. Cumulative incidence curves for pairwise propensity score 1:1 matched groups of cases and controls were calculated and presented with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). These curves were generated both with and without accounting for death as a competing risk, revealing no significant differences between the two methods. The curves presented herein exclude competing risks. Incidence rates were determined by dividing the number of events by the total follow‐up years for each respective study group, and these rates were expressed per 100 person‐years. The incidence rate difference (IRD) was calculated by subtracting the incidence rate of bleeding for controls from that of cases and was used to estimate the excess bleeding incidence due to DOACs.
Cases and controls were further compared to estimate the excess relative risk with incidence risk ratios, IRRs, calculated by dividing the incidence rate for cases by the incidence rate of controls. Incidence rates and incidence rate ratios, along with corresponding 95% CIs, were estimated using a log‐linked Poisson model for count data. Differences in incidence rates were calculated using SAS macro %NLMeans.
All statistical tests were two‐tailed and performed at a 5% significance level.
All statistical analyses were performed in SAS version 9.4, and graphs were drawn in RStudio and R 4.3.3.

## Results

## Baseline characteristics
A total of 36,115 VTE cases were identified and propensity score matched with 36,115 controls. The median age was 66 years (IQR 52; 76), with females comprising 48% of the cases and 51% of the controls. Among cases, 50% presented with DVT. Prior to study inclusion, 2% of cases and controls had a documented history of major bleeding. The most common comorbidities among cases and controls included hypertension, diabetes, atrial fibrillation, and heart failure. In addition, 30% of cases and controls used PPI, 17% used statins, and 13% used SSRIs. Despite propensity score matching, residual differences in baseline characteristics were observed, particularly regarding sex, heart failure, renal disease, ischemic heart disease, ischemic stroke, hypertension, and atrial fibrillation. In contrast, the use of concomitant medications was well balanced between the matched cohorts (Table  ). Baseline characteristics of cases and controls before propensity score matching are provided in Table .
 After 6 months, 19,765 VTE cases with ongoing DOAC treatment were rematched with 19,765 controls. The new baseline characteristics of this cohort are presented in Table . Overall, the characteristics of the cohort matched at 6 months were consistent with those of the propensity score–matched cohort at the start of the study. However, some notable differences were observed, particularly in terms of fewer subjects with preexisting bleeding, heart failure, and renal disease. Conversely, there was a higher prevalence of hypertension and atrial fibrillation, as well as increased use of PPIs, statins, and SSRIs.

## Major bleeding during initial and extended treatment
During the initial treatment period (0–6 months), major bleeding events were observed in 388 VTE cases (1.07% of patients who initiated treatment) and 103 controls (0.29%) (Fig.  , Table  ). The incidence rates were 2.79 per 100 person‐years for cases and 0.60 for controls. The excess risk of major bleeding was 2.19 bleedings per 100 person‐years, with an IRR of 4.67 (95% CI, 3.76–5.80) (Fig.  ). Detailed information on bleeding sites and the number of fatal bleeding events is provided in Table  . The case‐fatality rate was 16% (64 of 388) among cases and 24% (25 of 103) among controls.
 During the extended treatment period (6 months up to 5 years), 139 cases (0.70% of patients still on treatment at 6 months) and 214 controls (1.08%) experienced major bleeding (Fig.  , Table  ), with incidence rates of 1.07 per 100 person‐years for cases and 0.37 for controls. The excess risk of major bleeding during this period was 0.70 hemorrhages per 100 person‐years, whereas the IRR was 2.91 (95% CI, 2.35–3.61) (Fig.  ). The bleeding sites and number of fatal bleeding episodes are displayed in Table  . The case fatality rate was 19% (27 of 139) in cases and 26% (56 of 214) in controls.
 

## Males and females
During the initial treatment period (0–6 months), major bleeding events were observed in 169 (0.90%) male cases and 219 (1.26%) female cases. Among the female cases, 31 experienced gynecological bleeding, with 17 occurring in women aged 18–45 years. The incidence rate of major bleeding was 2.34 per 100 person‐years for males and 3.27 for females. In contrast, no sex differences were observed among controls, with incidence rates of 0.61 per 100 person‐years for males versus 0.58 for females (Fig.  , Table  ). The excess risk of major bleeding was 1.73 hemorrhages per 100 person‐years for males and 2.69 for females. The IRR for cases compared to controls was 3.82 (95% CI, 2.80–5.21) for males and 5.62 (95% CI, 4.15–7.63) for females (Fig.  ).
During the extended treatment period (6 months up to 5 years), major bleeding occurred in 66 (0.65%) male cases and 73 (0.76%) female cases (Fig.  , Table  ). The incidence rate was 1.01 per 100 person‐years for male cases and 1.13 for female cases, whereas the corresponding incidence rates for controls were 0.41 for males and 0.33 for females. The excess risk of major bleeding was 0.60 per 100 person‐years for males and 0.81 for females. The IRR for major bleeding in cases versus controls was 2.47 (95% CI, 1.83–3.34) for males and 3.48 (95% CI, 2.56–4.73) for females (Fig.  ).

## Age groups
During the initial treatment period (0–6 months), major bleeding occurred in 17 (0.47%) cases aged 18 to <40 years and in 155 (2.44%) cases aged 80+ years. The incidence rate increased with age for cases, from 1.26 per 100 person‐years in the 18 to <40 years age group to 6.58 in those aged 80+ years. A similar trend regarding increased bleeding events with age was observed in the control group (Fig.  , Table  ). Except for the 70–80 years age group, the excess risk of bleeding escalated with age, from 1.26 per 100 person‐years for those aged 18 to <40 years to 4.44 in the 80+ years age group. In contrast, the IRR for cases versus controls indicated a higher relative risk in younger age groups, which could be attributed to their lower baseline risk (Fig.  ).
During the extended treatment period (6 months up to 5 years), 4 (0.25%) cases aged 18 to <40 years and 47 (1.37%) cases aged 80+ years experienced major bleedings, corresponding to an incidence rate of 0.68 per 100 person‐years for cases aged 18 to <40 years and 1.79 for cases aged 80+ years. The baseline risk in controls also increased with age, from 0.10 per 100 person‐years in those aged 18 to <40 years to 1.18 in those aged 80+ years (Fig.  , Table  ). The excess risk of bleeding did not increase progressively with age but varied among age groups, with the lowest risk for individuals aged 40 to <60 years, 0.16 per 100 person‐years. Due to a low number of events, the IRRs were uncertain with wide CIs, particularly in younger age groups (Fig.  ).

## DVT and PE
During the initial treatment period (0–6 months), 169 (0.94%) DVT cases and 219 (1.21%) PE cases experienced major bleeding events, corresponding to an incidence rate of 2.54 per 100 person‐years for DVT cases and 3.01 for PE cases. In contrast, the corresponding incidence rates for the control group were 0.51 and 0.69, respectively (Fig.  , Table  ). The excess risk of major bleeding was 2.03 events per 100 person‐years for DVT and 2.33 for PE.
During the extended treatment period (6 months up to 5 years), a similar trend was observed, with a higher incidence of major bleedings in PE cases (0.94 per 100 person‐years for DVT and 1.13 for PE) compared to controls (0.30 for DVT and 0.42 for PE) (Fig.  , Table  ). The excess risk of major bleeding was 0.65 events per 100 person‐years for DVT and 0.71 for PE.

## Discussion
The present study explored the excess risk of bleeding associated with DOAC use during initial (0–6 months) and extended treatment (6 months to 5 years) in patients diagnosed with VTE, compared to matched controls. During the initial treatment period, the excess risk of bleeding due to DOACs exceeded 2 per 100 person‐years. However, it was considerably lower during the extended treatment period, at approximately 0.7 per 100 person‐years. Female sex indicated a higher excess risk of bleeding compared to male sex during the initial but not during the extended treatment period. Moreover, older patients demonstrated a heightened excess risk of bleeding during the initial treatment compared to younger patients. Although advancing age was associated with an increased overall risk of bleeding during the extended treatment period, a consistent progression in excess bleeding with age was not evident.
During initial treatment, 1.07% of cases suffered major bleeding. Several previous studies have assessed the total risk of bleeding during anticoagulant treatment with DOACs for VTE [ – , ]. The pooled bleeding incidence in DOAC‐treated patients from Phase 3 trials has been reported to be 1.1% for major bleeding events [ ]. In clinical practice studies based on registers and medical claims data, major bleeding has ranged from 0.83% to 1.76% [ , – ]. Thus, our results align with prior data despite variations in the definition of major bleeding. RCTs typically employ stricter definitions such as, or similar to, the International Society on Thrombosis and Haemostasis criteria [ ], whereas register studies such as ours have defined major bleeding as a principal inpatient diagnosis of bleeding [ , – ], occasionally supplemented by a procedure code for the treatment of bleeding [ ].
During extended treatment up to 5 years, 0.70% of cases suffered major bleeding. Pooled data from RCTs and prospective cohort studies have shown an incidence of major bleeding during extended treatment of 0.55%; however, a very limited number of patients were followed beyond 1 year of extended treatment [ ]. Data from clinical practice are limited, in particular when excluding cancer patients, but one previous study showed a bleeding incidence from 6 months to 5 years of 0.88% [ ].
We report a case‐fatality of 16% during initial treatment and 19% during extended treatment, both considerably higher than previously reported pooled data, which indicated case‐fatality rates of 5.3% for initial treatment from RCTs [13] and 9.7% for extended treatment from RCTs and prospective cohort studies [ ]. We found no previous register data reporting case‐fatality separately during initial and extended treatment. The higher case‐fatality in the present study could be due to the inclusion of many patients that were excluded from RCTs, such as the elderly, those with severe renal failure, and patients terminally ill with other diseases [ ].
Case‐fatality rates have previously been reported to be higher in patients on anticoagulation experiencing bleeding [ ] than in patients experiencing recurrent VTEs after stopping treatment [ ], indicating that bleeding is a more serious complication. Therefore, it has been proposed that comparing the risk of fatal bleeding from anticoagulation with the risk of fatal VTE after stopping treatment could better inform risk‐benefit assessments for extended treatment [ ]. In our cohort, the incidence rate of major bleeding during extended treatment was 1.07 per 100 person‐years, with a case‐fatality rate of 19%. Thus, the annual risk of death due to bleeding while on treatment was 0.20%. Matched controls had a major bleeding rate of 0.37 per 100 person‐years, with a case‐fatality rate of 24%, resulting in an annual risk of death due to bleeding without treatment of 0.09%. The resulting difference in the yearly excess risk of death from bleeding due to DOAC use was approximately 0.11%, suggesting that many patients might benefit from extended treatment without any added risk of bleeding compared to the background population.
In line with previous studies, we found that the excess bleeding risk on anticoagulation was highest during the initial treatment period, which may suggest an inherent predisposition for bleeding [ ]. However, the present study demonstrated that the difference in cumulative incidence of bleeding between cases and controls continued to increase during the extended treatment, indicating that the risk of bleeding seems to persist throughout the treatment period. This supports the importance of regular follow‐up for patients on anticoagulation for VTE to identify factors that may increase bleeding risk, as recommended by current guidelines [ , ].
Female sex was identified as a risk factor for bleeding during the initial treatment phase, with females exhibiting a higher excess bleeding risk than males, a finding supported by existing bleeding scores [ , ]. Gynecological bleeding accounted for some, but not all, of this difference. However, during extended treatment, no apparent difference in excess bleeding rates was observed between males and females. One possible explanation could be that females with a predisposition for bleeding, such as heavy menstrual bleeding, may seek medical attention early if the bleeding is severe enough to require inpatient treatment.
Age has previously been established as a risk factor for bleeding during both initial and extended anticoagulant treatment following VTE,2 and advanced age is frequently included in bleeding risk stratification schemes for VTE management [ , , , ]. This study confirms the elevated excess risk of bleeding associated with DOAC use in elderly patients, particularly during the initial treatment. However, during the extended treatment period, the bleeding risk among the oldest patients appears to be primarily attributed to the baseline risk in the population rather than the excess risk posed by DOACs. In a clinical context, this suggests that the excess risk of bleeding due to DOACs in the very elderly, who have not experienced bleeding during the initial treatment period, may be low.

## Strengths and limitations
Among the strengths of our study is the use of national registers with a near‐complete coverage of all hospital‐based care in Sweden. Sweden offers publicly available healthcare to all citizens, and the registers, therefore, include patients from all socio‐economic backgrounds, avoiding potential selection bias related to data from private health care insurance databases. Another strength is the exclusion of cancer patients. Patients with cancer have a high bleeding risk [ ], making data on mixed populations of both cancer and non‐cancer patients difficult to interpret in a clinical context.
This study also has some limitations. The baseline bleeding risk was not directly measured in VTE patients but was estimated using propensity score–matched controls. Although propensity score matching reduced the differences among the groups, it did not eliminate them; accordingly, any residual differences may still influence the results. Additionally, our estimates might differ from the true risk due to residual confounding factors, such as diseases typically managed in primary care settings (e.g., hypertension, diabetes, and depression) because primary care is not included in the National Patient Register. In addition, over‐the‐counter medications, such as non‐steroidal anti‐inflammatory drugs, could not be accounted for, as these are not recorded in the available registers. It is also unclear how many cases received a reduced DOAC dose and if this influenced bleeding rates. Furthermore, given that the study only included patients treated with DOACs, the findings may not be generalizable to patients treated with warfarin or low molecular weight heparin for VTE. Moreover, we cannot exclude that patients with early bleeding complications or other adverse events were more likely to stop treatment than to continue with extended treatment, which could result in an underestimation of the excess bleeding risk with the current study design.
The estimation of bleeding risk scores, for example, VTE‐BLEED [ ], ACCP [ ], and RIETE [ ], could have enabled standardized assessment of bleeding risk. However, due to the unavailability of data—including creatinine clearance, alcohol abuse, and uncontrolled hypertension—these scores could not be calculated. Register studies also have a possibility of coding errors. Still, the National Patient Register has previously demonstrated high overall validity for registered diagnoses [ ], and previous validation studies focusing on bleeding in patients with atrial fibrillation have shown excellent accuracy when combining data on hospitalization and fatal bleeding [ ].

## Conclusion
The excess risk of bleeding due to DOACs was high during the initial treatment period. In patients who did not suffer bleeding during the initial treatment period, the excess bleeding risk during extended treatment was low.
Females had a higher excess bleeding risk due to DOACs than males during initial, but not extended, treatment. For the oldest patients, initial treatment carried a high excess bleeding risk due to DOACs, but the risk of extending anticoagulant treatment in patients who did not suffer bleeding during initial treatment was low.
