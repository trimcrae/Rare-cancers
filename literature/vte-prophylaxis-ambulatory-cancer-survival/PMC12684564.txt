## Background
The relationship between dyslipidemia, a significant contributor to atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease, and atrial fibrillation remains unclear. This study aims to investigate the impact of remnant cholesterol (RC) on the prognosis of patients with atrial fibrillation on oral anticoagulants.

## Methods
A total of 23 267 patients with atrial fibrillation on oral anticoagulants from the Tianjin Health Medical Data Platform in China were identified for the analysis. We categorized participants into RC tertiles and used a multivariate Cox model to evaluate outcomes. Subgroup and sensitivity analyses were used to evaluate the association between RC tertiles and all‐cause death. The study analyzed the combined effect of RC and low‐density lipoprotein cholesterol on outcomes on the basis of the median levels of both lipids.

## Results
The top RC tertile (tertile 3) was associated with a 24% increased risk of all‐cause death (hazard ratio [HR], 1.24 [95% CI, 1.11–1.38]), 18% increased risk of ischemic stroke (HR, 1.18 [95% CI, 1.00–1.38]), and 35% increased risk of acute myocardial infarction (HR, 1.35 [95% CI, 1.07–1.71]) compared with the bottom RC tertile (tertile 1). A sex‐specific interaction was noted for RC and death, with the association appearing more evidently in men. The results remained consistent throughout various sensitivity analyses. This association with RC appeared to persist even when low‐density lipoprotein cholesterol levels were relatively low (below the median of 102 mg/dL), showing a 17% increased risk of composite ischemic outcomes (HR, 1.17 [95% CI, 1.01–1.37]).

## Conclusions
Higher RC levels in patients with atrial fibrillation receiving oral anticoagulation therapy were associated with an increased risk of death, acute myocardial infarction, and ischemic stroke.

## Combined Effect of RC and LDL‐C on Study Outcomes
To further examine the combined effect of RC and LDL‐C on study outcomes, we conducted a concordance/discordance analysis based on the median values of RC (16 mg/dL) and LDL‐C (102 mg/L) as cutoff points. Groups were classified as “high” or “low” on the basis of whether their RC and LDL‐C levels were above or below the respective median values. Our findings indicate that, compared with the low–LDL‐C/low‐RC group, the composite ischemic outcome risk was significantly higher in the low–LDL‐C/high‐RC group (HR, 1.17 [95% CI, 1.01–1.37]), and in the high–LDL‐C/high‐RC group (HR, 1.37 [95% CI, 1.18–1.59]). For IS, compared with the low–LDL‐C/low‐RC group, risks were significantly higher in the high–LDL‐C/low‐RC (HR, 1.23 [95% CI, 1.01–1.49]) and high–LDL‐C/high‐RC (HR, 1.35 [95% CI, 1.12–1.61]) groups, while the low–LDL‐C/high‐RC group showed no statistically significant increase. For AMI, the risk in the high‐LDL/high‐RC group was also significantly higher than that in the low‐LDL/low‐RC group (HR, 1.45 [95% CI, 1.12–1.89]), whereas the low‐LDL/high‐RC group showed no statistical significance (HR, 1.23 [95% CI, 0.94–1.60]) (Table  ). After full adjustment, no significant differences in all‐cause death or hemorrhage‐related outcomes (composite hemorrhagic outcome, ICH, GIB) were observed among any groups compared with the low–LDL‐C/low‐RC group. To offer further clinical context, a supplementary concordance/discordance analysis was conducted. This analysis used an LDL‐C threshold of 70 mg/dL, reflecting a key ASCVD treatment target, , , while RC categorization remained on the basis of its median value. ## Subgroup Analysis
After full adjustment, the findings were generally consistent with the main effects across all stratified groups, except for sex. The association between higher RC tertiles and increased all‐cause death appeared more evident in men than women (P for interaction=0.016) (Table  ). In men, the HR for all‐cause death was 1.16 (95% CI, 1.00–1.34) in tertile 2 compared with tertile 1, and 1.43 (95% CI, 1.23–1.66) in tertile 3, suggesting that higher RC levels are associated with an increased mortality risk. In women, the HR was 0.90 (95% CI, 0.76–1.07) for tertile 2 compared with tertile 1, and 1.03 (95% CI, 0.88–1.21) for tertile 3, indicating that changes in RC levels do not significantly affect mortality risk, as these HRs were not statistically significant. Results for other subgroups are provided in Table  . Baseline medication use, including statins (29.5% in men versus 27.0% in women; P<0.001), differed slightly by sex (Table  ). To further assess this sex interaction’s robustness, a sensitivity analysis in participants not receiving statins at baseline indicated a persistent, statistically significant interaction (Table  ).
 

## Sensitivity Analysis
After excluding individuals on statin therapy at baseline, the association between the high RC tertile on all‐cause death and IS remained consistent with the main findings and was statistically significant. The risk associated with the high RC tertile on the composite ischemic outcome showed a marginally increased trend, while the effect on AMI generally aligned with the main findings but did not reach statistical significance (Table  ). The robustness of our primary findings was confirmed through sensitivity analyses after excluding individuals with conditions potentially carrying a high mortality risk (Tables  through ). Further analysis of 5222 individuals with paroxysmal AF (22.4% of the total population) showed that, after full adjustment, RC was significantly associated with AMI (Table  ).

## Discussion
Using a large electronic medical record database from THMDP in China, the study found that among patients with AF on OAC therapy, higher levels of RC were associated with an increased risk of all‐cause death, AMI, and ischemic stroke. Specifically, the observed association between RC and all‐cause death seemed more apparent in male patients. Additionally, the study demonstrated that even with relatively low LDL‐C levels, the risk of composite ischemic outcomes associated with RC still persisted.
Our study on AF prognosis should be contextualized within the conflicting literature on RC and incident AF. Notably, large studies from the UK Biobank found an inverse RC–AF association (lower RC, higher risk), , whereas a US ARIC (Atherosclerosis Risk in Communities) cohort study reported a positive link. A recent editorial suggests that this “dramatic reversal” may arise from critical differences in population ethnicity, covariate adjustment, and untested proportional hazard assumptions in the models. While our secondary prevention cohort is distinct, this conundrum is instructive. Our finding that higher RC predicts mortality and ischemic outcomes aligns with RC’s established proatherogenic role. This suggests the biological mechanisms linking RC to the initiation of AF may differ fundamentally from those driving its prognostic impact in established disease, a distinction warranting further research.
Traditionally, it has been widely believed that dyslipidemia and death are related, with higher levels of HDL‐C and lower levels of LDL‐C associated with a reduced risk of death. , , However, this straightforward relationship appears increasingly complex. Recent studies in the general population suggest that the association between LDL‐C and death, including cardiovascular death, may follow a U‐shaped pattern. , , For instance, the recent ChinaHeart (Health Evaluation and Risk Reduction Through Nationwide Teamwork) study, which used risk stratification for the general population, revealed that both lower and higher levels of LDL‐C in low‐risk and primary prevention cohorts were associated with an increased risk of death. Conversely, the secondary prevention cohort exhibited a J‐shaped relationship. Additionally, recent research regarding HDL‐C suggests that elevated levels may paradoxically be associated with a higher risk of death and other adverse outcomes. , This raises questions about the clinical significance of high HDL‐C levels and whether they should be targeted for intervention, warranting further investigation. There is currently a notable research gap in understanding the relationship between dyslipidemia and death among patients with AF undergoing oral anticoagulant therapy, particularly regarding the emerging lipid marker: RC. A prior post hoc analysis of the ARISTOTLE (Apixaban for Reduction in Stroke and Other Thromboembolic Events in Atrial Fibrillation) trial primarily examined apolipoprotein A and apolipoprotein B (apo B) within the oral anticoagulant‐treated AF population, finding higher apolipoprotein A associated with a lower mortality rate, yet paradoxically, also higher total apo B. Our study on RC offers a targeted perspective on atherogenic lipoproteins. RC, the cholesterol within apo B–containing triglyceride‐rich lipoprotein remnants, consistently showed a positive association with all‐cause death and ischemic events in our cohort. This contrasts with the ARISTOTLE substudy’s total apo B findings and suggests that RC may specifically flag the risk from these particularly harmful remnant apo B particles, a risk potentially obscured when assessing total apo B in complex AF populations. Moreover, elevated RC often aligns with lower apolipoprotein A/HDL‐C, further indicating an adverse lipid profile. While we did not measure apolipoproteins, these relationships help frame RC’s prognostic importance. The ARISTOTLE subanalysis also faced limitations such as a smaller sample size and less detailed baseline data, potentially impacting confounder adjustment and the apo B interpretation. Moreover, real‐world evidence suggests that the benefits of targeting apolipoproteins for lipid‐lowering remain insufficient, and similar studies focusing on the AF population are scarce. In contrast, the prevailing approach continues to focus on cholesterol management to reduce the risk of ASCVD. Consequently, our study further investigates the prognostic significance of RC in the OAC‐treated AF population. A key finding from our research revealed that higher levels of RC were associated with an increased risk of all‐cause death, with this trend showing a linear increase in subsequent continuous analyses. Notably, after excluding various baseline populations that could influence the mortality rate, the results remained consistent with the primary findings.
Previous studies indicate that statin therapies can reduce cardiovascular events, , potentially confounding RC associations. While our main models adjusted for baseline statin use, we further explored this by excluding baseline statin users. In this nonstatin subgroup, the associations of higher RC with all‐cause death and IS generally persisted, although the link with AMI did not reach statistical significance, possibly due to reduced statistical power. Particularly noteworthy within this nonstatin user subgroup was the continued statistical significance of the sex interaction for RC and all‐cause death. This persistence, independent of baseline statin use, suggests that the observed sex‐specific pattern warrants further consideration beyond statin effects. Potential contributors to such sex differences are multifactorial. Biologically, , , sex hormones can distinctively modulate lipid metabolism and inflammatory responses and sex‐specific variations in immune function. Our findings suggest that sex may be an important factor in RC‐related risk in this population, but these observations from a subgroup analysis require cautious interpretation and further investigation to elucidate underlying mechanisms and confirm consistency across diverse cohorts.
Previous clinical studies have demonstrated that RC is associated with an elevated risk of subsequent cardiovascular events and ischemic incidents. , , Notably, a study derived from the PREDIMED (Prevention With Mediterranean Diet) clinical trial found that among individuals at high cardiovascular risk adhering to a Mediterranean diet, RC was linked to an increased risk of cardiovascular outcomes, particularly major adverse cardiovascular events, independent of lifestyle and cardiovascular risk factors. Similarly, a large Danish clinical study found that elevated RC levels were associated with a higher risk of ischemic events, including myocardial infarction, stroke, and peripheral vascular disease. As a key cholesterol component of triglyceride‐rich lipoproteins, RC can readily penetrate the arterial endothelium, accumulate in the intima, and be readily taken up by macrophages without extensive oxidative modification, leading to foam cell formation and the development of atherosclerotic plaques. Beyond direct plaque formation, RC and triglyceride‐rich lipoproteins can also promote endothelial dysfunction, induce proinflammatory states and contribute to adverse vascular remodeling, all of which are integral to the progression of ASCVD. These processes can culminate in ischemic events such as AMI and IS, which are major drivers of cardiovascular death and contribute significantly to all‐cause death. While these mechanistic pathways are well established in the broader context of ASCVD, it is important to state that their specific contribution and interplay in driving death and cardiovascular risk within our particular cohort of patients with AF receiving OACs remain to be fully elucidated by dedicated mechanistic studies. Our observational findings support an association, but the underlying mechanisms in this population are inferred from existing biological understanding and remain speculative in the context of our study design. Consequently, the increased risk of ischemic events, including IS and AMI, and death among individuals in the higher tertile of RC in our study is biologically plausible on the basis of these pathways.
In real‐world practice, traditional lipid‐lowering strategies primarily focus on LDL‐C, with the mantra “Lower is better” firmly established. However, related studies suggest that well‐controlled LDL‐C levels, or even extremely low LDL‐C levels, may be associated with adverse outcomes, including death, bleeding, and dementia. , , The issue of residual risk in these populations warrants further investigation. A Mendelian randomization study conducted in a large‐scale, multiethnic population suggested that RC, independent of LDL‐C, increases the risks of coronary heart disease, myocardial infarction, and IS by 51%, 57%, and 23%, respectively, for each additional SD. Clinical studies have also demonstrated that RC can influence cardiovascular prognosis even when LDL‐C levels are strictly controlled. Recent analysis of discordance between RC and LDL‐C has shown that in populations with relatively low LDL‐C levels, higher RC is associated with an increased risk of subsequent ASCVD. Consequently, our study analyzed the combined effects of RC and LDL‐C and reached a significant conclusion: In patients with relatively low LDL‐C levels, higher RC is associated with an increased risk of subsequent ischemic outcomes, further supporting its atherogenic role in ASCVD. Accordingly, current guidelines should place greater emphasis on the pathogenic role and clinical significance of RC.
The underestimation of the incidence of paroxysmal AF in real‐world settings is a significant concern, as many patients are diagnosed only during an AF episode, complicating detection and diagnosis. Asymptomatic paroxysmal AF often necessitates diagnosis through ambulatory ECG monitoring. Previous studies have reported a wide range of prevalence rates for paroxysmal AF, with our study indicating a proportion of ≈22.4%. Each episode of paroxysmal AF can be considered an acute event, reflecting a deterioration in the patient’s overall disease state. Current research and clinical guidelines do not clearly differentiate between the management of paroxysmal AF and persistent, permanent, or chronic AF, resulting in confusion and underscoring the need for further distinction in the management strategies for acute and chronic AF states. A secondary analysis from the ENTRUST‐AF PCI (Edoxaban‐Based Versus Vitamin K Antagonist‐Based Antithrombotic Regimen After Successful Coronary Stenting in Patients With Atrial Fibrillation) trial suggests that patients with paroxysmal AF who undergo percutaneous coronary intervention experience a higher incidence of myocardial infarction compared with those with nonparoxysmal AF (4.87% versus 2.01%; P=0.0024). Similarly, a secondary analysis of a clinical trial comparing edoxaban with warfarin in patients with AF found that following electrical cardioversion, those with paroxysmal AF had a higher incidence of myocardial infarction compared with patients with persistent AF (1.0% versus 0.1%). A recent retrospective clinical study using the TriNetX database also indicates that among patients with AF on OACs, those with paroxysmal AF have a higher risk of myocardial infarction compared with patients with nonparoxysmal AF (HR, 1.65 [95% CI, 1.35–2.01]), primarily driven by non–ST‐segment–elevation myocardial infarction. Given that paroxysmal AF is slightly more prevalent in the general population compared with other forms of AF, and that episodes in patients with paroxysmal AF tend to resemble acute events, distinct from the persistent AF observed in other types, it raises the question of whether their management should be approached similarly. Our study, which examines patients with paroxysmal AF from a lipid management perspective, identified a significant correlation between elevated levels of RC and an increased risk of subsequent AMI in patients with paroxysmal AF undergoing oral anticoagulation. The relationship between paroxysmal AF and AMI may be elucidated by several mechanisms, including the influence of paroxysmal episodes on ventricular function at the microvascular level, abnormal beat‐to‐beat flow variability induced by rapid heart rates, and oxidative stress driven by angiotensin receptor 1. , Our findings indicate that lipids may also play a mediating role in this association. Given that this population can be viewed as being in an acute state, it may be both effective and feasible to incorporate strategies aimed at controlling ASCVD risk factors into the existing AF management guidelines, which include the use of lipid‐lowering agents such as statins. However, the specific effects of lipids on patients with AF receiving OACs and the prognostic implications of statin therapy warrant further investigation through additional randomized controlled trials and clinical studies.
Our study has some limitations. First, observational design demonstrates associations, not causation. Second, despite multivariable adjustment, residual confounding from unmeasured factors (eg, lifestyle, medication adherence, socioeconomic status) may remain. Third, observed sex‐specific associations should be interpreted cautiously, as sex might represent unmeasured lifestyle or other effect modifiers differing between men and women, beyond purely biological sex differences. Fourth, reliance on ICD‐10 codes and electronic health records for diagnoses, comorbidities, medication use, and outcomes introduces potential for information bias and misclassification due to their inherent limitations in sensitivity and specificity. Furthermore, RC exposure could be misclassified due to variations in preanalytical conditions, notably the nonuniform documentation of fasting status for lipid sample collection in this real‐world data set. Nonfasting samples can yield higher calculated RC levels, introducing potential variability, although RC from such samples has still demonstrated prognostic utility in other studies. , Fifth, selection bias may affect generalizability. Our cohort from THMDP users meeting specific criteria, predominantly a Chinese population, may not represent all patients with AF or other ethnicities.

## Conclusions
In a Chinese population of AF receiving OACs, elevated levels of RC are associated with a heightened risk of all‐cause death, AMI, and IS. Furthermore, an increased risk of subsequent ischemic events was found to be associated with higher RC, even when LDL‐C levels were relatively low. However, given the observational nature of our study, further research is imperative. Specifically, future efforts should focus on large‐scale, prospective, multicenter cohort studies to confirm these prognostic associations in varied AF cohorts. Concurrently, mechanistic investigations are needed to fully elucidate the biological pathways connecting RC to cardiovascular events in this population. Critically, randomized controlled trials will be essential to determine if interventions aimed at lowering RC can translate into tangible clinical benefits for these patients, thereby informing future treatment guidelines.
