## Abstract

## Background
Anticoagulation for isolated distal deep vein thrombosis (IDDVT) in critically ill patients remains controversial. The aim of our study was to assess whether anticoagulation could benefit critically ill patients with IDDVT.

## Methods
We identified critically ill patients with IDDVT diagnosed by ultrasound from June 2022 to June 2023 and divided them into anticoagulation and non-anticoagulation groups retrospectively. The primary outcome was thrombus propagation, defined as a composite of pulmonary embolism (PE) and proximal deep vein thrombosis (PDVT). The secondary outcomes included PE, PDVT, thrombus resolution, major bleeding, clinically relevant non-major bleeding and all-cause mortality. The follow-up period was from the day of IDDVT diagnosis to the time of discharge/death. After propensity score matching (PSM), the incidence of outcomes was compared and risk factors for thrombus propagation/bleeding were analyzed.

## Results
A total of 261 patients were included for analysis, 115 in the non-anticoagulation group and 146 in the anticoagulation group. After PSM, 53 pairs of patients were well-matched. The incidence of thrombus propagation was significantly lower in the anticoagulation group than in the non-anticoagulation group (5.7% vs. 18.9%, P = 0.038). However, there was no statistical difference in the incidence of secondary outcomes between the two groups. Subgroup analysis revealed that over 70% of patients received low-dose rather than standard-dose anticoagulation, particularly those with coagulopathy and deep vein catheterization, and no significant differences were observed in any outcomes between the two subgroups. Finally, surgery (OR 3.959, 95% CI 1.101–14.233, P = 0.035) was an independent risk factor for thrombus propagation, while early anticoagulation (OR 0.243, 95% CI 0.061–0.966, P = 0.045) was a protective factor. Active malignant tumor (OR 10.257, 95% CI 1.883–55.870, P = 0.007), trauma (OR 9.766, 95% CI 1.193–79.948, P = 0.034), and an IMPROVE score ≥ 7 (OR 5.279, 95% CI 1.146–24.321, P = 0.033) were all independent risk factors for bleeding.

## Conclusions
Early low-dose anticoagulation can reduce the risk of thrombus propagation in critically ill patients with IDDVT without increasing bleeding risk, but caution should be exercised in those with active malignant tumor, trauma or an IMPROVE score ≥ 7, given their inherently high bleeding risk.

Keywords: Isolated distal deep vein thrombosis, Pulmonary embolism, Proximal deep vein thrombosis, Critically ill patients, Intensive care unit, Anticoagulation

## Background
Isolated distal deep vein thrombosis (IDDVT) of the legs affects the infrapopliteal veins and accounts for 30–50% of all deep vein thrombosis (DVT) [ ]. The 9th edition of American College of Chest Physicians (ACCP) antithrombotic guidelines [ ] state that about 15% of untreated IDDVT are expected to subsequently extend into the popliteal vein and may cause pulmonary embolism (PE). The European Society for Vascular Surgery (ESVS) 2021 guidelines [ ] mention that the rate of propagation of IDDVT to the popliteal vein or above to be around 9% and the rate of PE to be around 1.5%. Therefore, it is not acceptable to neither anticoagulate nor do surveillance to detect proximal propagation of thrombus (hereinafter referred to as “thrombus propagation”). Unlike proximal deep vein thrombosis (PDVT), whether and how to administer anticoagulation in patients with IDDVT remains widely controversial due to conflicting findings among available studies [ – ].
Compared with outpatients and general inpatients, critically ill patients not only have a higher risk of thrombosis, but also a higher risk of bleeding [ – ]. How to weigh the risk of thrombus propagation and bleeding exacerbation is the focus of prevention and treatment of in-hospital venous thromboembolism (VTE).
The 9th edition of ACCP antithrombotic guidelines [ ] recommend anticoagulation for hospitalized patients with IDDVT, but emphasize that this recommendation is only based on low-quality evidence (2 C). However, there is a lack of research and guidelines related to anticoagulation for IDDVT in critically ill patients. Therefore, we conducted this study with the main objective of investigating whether anticoagulation can improve the clinical benefit of critically ill patients with IDDVT.

## Methods
This study was registered with the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (https://www.chictr.org.cn/). Registration number: ChiCTR2400091716; Registration date: November 1, 2024.

## Study design and patients
A single-center, retrospective observational cohort design was employed to analyze data from the First Affiliated Hospital of Chongqing Medical University. We retrospectively included consecutive eligible patients from June 2022 to June 2023. The present study complied with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the ethics committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Chongqing Medical University (Number: K2023-464). Written consents were waived for its retrospective design by the committee. The inclusion criteria were: (1) admission to intensive care unit (ICU), including Emergency ICU, Surgical ICU, Cardiovascular ICU, Respiratory ICU, Neurology ICU, Neurosurgery ICU, and Thoracic ICU; (2) age ≥ 18 years; (3) diagnosis of IDDVT by ultrasound. The exclusion criteria were: (1) hospitalization in the ICU < 72 h; (2) initiation of anticoagulation within 3–14 days after IDDVT diagnosis; (3) no ultrasound follow-up after IDDVT diagnosis; (4) comorbidities of PDVT or PE; (5) incomplete data.
Previous studies [ , ] have identified the first 14 days after IDDVT diagnosis as a high-risk window for thrombus propagation. Our study aims to evaluate the efficacy and safety of early anticoagulation in such patients. Therefore, the patients were categorized into two groups for better comparison. The “anticoagulation group” was defined as the initiation of anticoagulation (both standard-dose and low-dose) within 3 days after IDDVT diagnosis, whereas the “non-anticoagulation group” was identified as no anticoagulation within 14 days after IDDVT diagnosis. The definition of standard-dose is shown in Table  , while low-dose refers to any anticoagulation regimen that does not meet the standard-dose criteria.
 

## Diagnosis of IDDVT
The proximal deep veins of the lower extremities, including the common femoral vein, superficial femoral vein, deep femoral vein, and popliteal vein, were first examined by ultrasound. Next, the distal deep veins were examined, including the axial veins (posterior tibial vein, peroneal vein, anterior tibial vein) and muscular veins (gastrocnemius vein, soleus vein). If the thrombus was located only in the distal deep vein and there was no evidence of PDVT or PE, the diagnosis of IDDVT was made.

## Baseline characteristics
For each patient, the demographics (age, gender, smoking history), comorbidities (hypertension, diabetes mellitus, renal dysfunction, hepatic dysfunction, coagulopathy, sepsis, atrial fibrillation, acute myocardial infarction, heart failure, respiratory failure, ischemic stroke, hemorrhagic stroke, active malignant tumor, history of VTE, autoimmune disease, pregnancy, trauma), medical interventions (surgery, deep vein catheterization, mechanical ventilation, glucocorticoid, blood purification, initiation time and regimen of anticoagulation), examination results (white blood cell count, neutrophil count, lymphocyte count, hemoglobin, platelet count, D-dimer, date of IDDVT diagnosis, location of thrombus), and clinical assessments (APACHE II score, risk of PE, risk of bleeding) were collected from the electronic medical record system. The risk of PE was assessed using the simplified Wells (sWells) score, with a score > 1 indicating high risk and ≤ 1 indicating low risk. The risk of bleeding was evaluated by the IMPROVE score, where a score ≥ 7 defined high risk and < 7 denoted low risk.
Renal dysfunction was characterized by serum creatinine > 200 µmol/L or estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) < 60 mL/min/1.73 m². Hepatic dysfunction was defined as the presence of cirrhosis, total bilirubin > 2 times the upper limit of normal (ULN), or ALT/AST > 3×ULN. Coagulopathy was identified as INR > 1.5 or APTT > 1.2×ULN (without anticoagulant use). Blood purification referred to extracorporeal techniques for pathogenic substance removal and homeostatic regulation, encompassing hemodialysis, hemofiltration, hemoperfusion, plasma exchange, and continuous renal replacement therapy (CRRT) in this study.

## Study outcomes
The follow-up period of the study was from the day of IDDVT diagnosis to the time of discharge/death in hospital. The primary outcome was thrombus propagation, defined as a composite of PE and PDVT. PE was defined as patients presenting with clinical manifestations (e.g., dyspnea, decreased oxygenation, etc.) associated with acute PE and confirmed by CT pulmonary angiography, ventilation/perfusion scan, or pulmonary angiography. PDVT was defined as the presence of relevant clinical manifestations (e.g., lower limb edema, increased leg circumference, changes in skin temperature/color, limb pain, etc.) accompanied by typical thromboembolic manifestations in the popliteal, femoral, iliac, and vena cava veins confirmed by ultrasound or CT venography. The secondary outcomes included: (1) PE; (2) PDVT; (3) thrombus resolution; (4) major bleeding; (5) clinically relevant non-major bleeding (CRNMB); (6) all-cause mortality. Thrombus resolution was defined as the absence of filling defects in each venous segment by ultrasound. Major bleeding and CRNMB was diagnosed according to guidelines [ , ] published by the International Society on Thrombosis and Hemostasis (Table  ).
 

## Statistical analysis
A descriptive analysis was performed to describe the baseline characteristics and study outcomes of the cohort. For continuous variables that meet the criteria of normal distribution, they were expressed as means ± standard deviations, and for the remaining continuous variables, the medians (interquartile ranges) were calculated. Comparisons between two samples were performed using t-tests or Mann-Whitney U tests. For the categorical variables, counts (percentages) were calculated, and comparisons between groups were performed by χ2 or Fisher’s exact tests. A P value < 0.05 indicated significant differences between measurements.
In addition, propensity score matching (PSM) was used to control the confounding bias between the two groups. Most of the baseline variables (age, gender, smoking history, hypertension, diabetes mellitus, renal dysfunction, hepatic dysfunction, coagulopathy, sepsis, atrial fibrillation, acute myocardial infarction, heart failure, respiratory failure, ischemic stroke, hemorrhagic stroke, active malignant tumor, autoimmune disease, trauma, surgery, deep vein catheterization, mechanical ventilation, glucocorticoid, blood purification, white blood cell count, neutrophil count, lymphocyte count, hemoglobin, platelet count, D-dimer, APACHE II score, risk of PE, risk of bleeding) were included, and the anticoagulation group was matched with the non-anticoagulation group in a 1:1 ratio based on the propensity scores using the Nearest Neighbor Matching algorithm with a caliper of 0.02. A P value > 0.05 indicated well matching between the two groups.
Subsequently, descriptive and comparative analyses were performed on study outcomes in the post-PSM cohort. Thereafter, subgroup analysis was conducted based on the initial anticoagulation dose after IDDVT diagnosis, with comprehensive comparisons made between the standard-dose and low-dose subgroups regarding both baseline characteristics and study outcomes.
Moreover, univariate and multivariate Logistic regression were performed in the matched cohort to calculate the odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) in order to explore the factors associated with thrombus propagation and bleeding. Variables showed statistical significance in the univariate analysis (P value < 0.10) were included in the multivariate analysis. A P value < 0.05 indicated significant differences.
Statistical analysis was performed with SPSS for Windows, version 27 (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA) and RStudio for Windows, version 2024.04.2.

## Results

## Baseline characteristics
A total of 341 patients were considered for this study, of which 80 were excluded for hospitalization in the ICU < 72 h (n = 20), initiation of anticoagulation within 3–14 days after IDDVT diagnosis (n = 32), no ultrasound follow-up after IDDVT diagnosis (n = 17), comorbidities of PDVT or PE (n = 8), incomplete data (n = 3). Finally, 261 patients were included for analysis, 115 in the non-anticoagulation group and 146 in the anticoagulation group (Fig.  ).
 Overall, there were significant differences in baseline characteristics between the two groups (Table  ). The anticoagulation group demonstrated significantly higher median age (72 years vs. 67 years, P = 0.013), hemoglobin levels (104 g/L vs. 93 g/L, P = 0.001), and proportion of heart failure cases (26.7% vs. 10.4%, P < 0.001) compared to the non-anticoagulation group. Conversely, significantly lower rates were observed in the anticoagulation group for hemorrhagic stroke (11.0% vs. 42.6%, P < 0.001), surgical history (33.6% vs. 50.4%, P = 0.006), and high bleeding risk (64.4% vs. 80.9%, P = 0.003). After PSM, 53 pairs of patients were well-matched. Analysis of the post-PSM cohort revealed no statistically significant differences in baseline characteristics between the two groups (Table  ).
 

## Details of anticoagulation
In the anticoagulation group before PSM, 19 patients (13.0%) received prophylactic anticoagulation after hospitalization, with only 1 case being escalated to standard-dose anticoagulation following IDDVT diagnosis. Regarding anticoagulant agents, 90.4% initially treated with low-molecular-weight heparin (LMWH), 6.2% treated with direct oral anticoagulants (DOACs), 2.1% treated with unfractionated heparin (UFH), and 1.4% treated with warfarin. In terms of dosing strategies, 31.5% initially received standard-dose anticoagulation and 68.5% received low-dose anticoagulation. The median duration of anticoagulation was 13 (8–21) days (Table  ).
 After PSM, 3 patients (5.7%) received prophylactic anticoagulation after hospitalization, including 1 case being escalated to standard-dose anticoagulation after IDDVT diagnosis. Regarding anticoagulant agents, 94.3% initially treated with LMWH, 3.8% treated with DOACs, 1.9% treated with UFH, and no patients were prescribed warfarin. In terms of dosing strategies, 26.4% initially received standard-dose anticoagulation and 73.6% received low-dose anticoagulation. The median duration of anticoagulation was 13 (8–17) days (Table  ).

## Follow-up and study outcomes
For the pre-PSM cohort, the median follow-up time was significantly shorter in the anticoagulation group compared with the non-anticoagulation group (18 d vs. 23 d, P = 0.027). The incidence of thrombus propagation was significantly lower in the anticoagulation group than in the non-anticoagulation group (5.5% vs. 20.9%, P < 0.001). For secondary outcomes, the rates of PE (1.4% vs. 10.4%, P = 0.001), PDVT (4.8% vs. 12.2%, P = 0.030), major bleeding (2.1% vs. 12.2%, P = 0.001), and CRNMB (19.9% vs. 35.7%, P = 0.004) were significantly lower in the anticoagulation group, and there were no significant differences in the rates of thrombus resolution and all-cause mortality between the two groups (Table  ).
 After PSM, there was no statistical difference in median follow-up time between the two groups (17 d vs. 22 d, P = 0.198). Similar to the pre-PSM cohort, the incidence of thrombus propagation remained significantly lower in the anticoagulation group (5.7% vs. 18.9%, P = 0.038). However, there was no statistical difference in the incidence of secondary outcomes between the two groups after PSM (Table  ).

## Subgroup analysis: standard-dose vs. low-dose
In terms of baseline characteristics, the low-dose subgroup had significantly higher proportions of patients with coagulopathy (38.5% vs. 7.1%, P = 0.041) and deep vein catheterization (66.7% vs. 35.7%, P = 0.044), as well as significantly lower hemoglobin levels (99.26 g/L vs. 119.07 g/L, P = 0.001) compared to the standard-dose subgroup after PSM. No other significant differences were observed in baseline characteristics between the two groups.
The median follow-up time was 16 days in the standard-dose subgroup and 17 days in the low-dose subgroup (P = 0.793), while the median duration of anticoagulation was 15 days and 13 days (P = 0.879), respectively. No statistically significant differences were observed between the two subgroups in either primary or secondary outcomes (Table  ).
 

## Factors associated with thrombus propagation
The details of patients who underwent thrombus propagation during follow-up were summarized in Table  . A total of 13 patients (10 in the non-anticoagulation group and 3 in the anticoagulation group) experienced thrombus propagation, of which 9 (69.2%) occurred within 14 days after IDDVT diagnosis and 11 (84.6%) occurred within 30 days.
 After univariate Logistic regression, surgery and early anticoagulation were included in the multivariate analysis. Finally, surgery (OR 3.959, 95% CI 1.101–14.233, P = 0.035) was confirmed as independent risk factor for thrombus propagation, and early anticoagulation (OR 0.243, 95% CI 0.061–0.966, P = 0.045) was considered to be an independent protective factor (Table  ).
 

## Factors associated with bleeding
The univariate Logistic regression confirmed that hepatic dysfunction, coagulopathy, active malignant tumor, trauma, deep vein catheterization, hemoglobin level, APACHE II score, and IMPROVE score ≥ 7 had statistically significant effects on the bleeding (including major bleeding and CRNMB). After multivariate Logistic regression, active malignant tumor (OR 10.257, 95% CI 1.883–55.870, P = 0.007), trauma (OR 9.766, 95% CI 1.193–79.948, P = 0.034), and IMPROVE score ≥ 7 (OR 5.279, 95% CI 1.146–24.321, P = 0.033) were confirmed as independent risk factors for bleeding (Table  ).
 

## Discussion
This study had two major findings. First, the incidence of thrombus propagation was significantly lower in the anticoagulation group than in the non-anticoagulation group, while there was no significant difference in the rates of major bleeding and CRNMB between the two groups. Second, surgery and no early anticoagulation were independent risk factors for thrombus propagation in these patients, while active malignant tumor, trauma, and an IMPROVE score ≥ 7 were independent risk factors for bleeding.
In our study, the incidence of thrombus propagation was 18.9% in the non-anticoagulation group and 5.7% in the anticoagulation group, which was similar to the findings of Elmi et al. [ ], who also included a large number of patients with high bleeding risk. Unfortunately, there was a lack of research data on critically ill patients with IDDVT. For outpatients with IDDVT, thrombus propagation occurred in 5%−11% of patients who did not receive anticoagulation, compared with 0–3% of patients who received anticoagulation [ , ]. For inpatients with IDDVT, thrombus propagation occurred in 5%−25% of patients who did not receive anticoagulation, compared with 3%−13% of patients who received anticoagulation [ , – ]. Overall, the incidence of thrombus propagation in our study was higher than the average of existing studies.
For clinicians, the decision whether to perform anticoagulation in patients with IDDVT mainly depends on weighing the risk of thrombus propagation and bleeding exacerbation. However, no studies have revealed the role of anticoagulation in critically ill patients with IDDVT, and the lack of guidelines and consensus put clinicians in a dilemma. Our study showed that the risk of thrombus propagation was significantly lower in the anticoagulation group than in the non-anticoagulation group regardless of PSM. However, contrary to clinical practice, the incidence of major bleeding and CRNMB was significantly lower in the anticoagulation group before PSM. This may be explained by the significant difference in baseline characteristics between the two groups—the proportion of patients at high bleeding risk being much lower in the anticoagulation group compared to the non-anticoagulation group. After PSM, there was no longer a significant difference in the incidence of major bleeding and CRNMB between the two groups. The ACT study [ ] was a randomized controlled trial (RCT) that included 70 ambulatory patients with IDDVT. This study showed that the incidence of thrombus propagation was higher in the non-anticoagulation group than in the anticoagulation group during 3-month follow-up, but the difference did not reach statistical significance (11.4% vs. 0, P = 0.11). Additionally, the ACT study noted that there were no major bleeding events in either group. Two retrospective studies [ , ] reached a consistent conclusion that anticoagulation can significantly reduce the risk of thrombus propagation without increasing the risk of bleeding. However, two RCTs took the different view. The CACTUS study [ ] found no significant difference in the incidence of thrombus propagation (3% vs. 5%, P = 0.54) between the anticoagulation and placebo groups, but the risk of bleeding (4% vs. 0, P = 0.03) was significantly higher in the anticoagulation group. Another RCT [ ] showed no significant difference between the anticoagulation and non-anticoagulation groups in terms of thrombus propagation (3.7% vs. 3.8%, P = 0.99) and major bleeding (0 vs. 0, P = 1.00). It is noteworthy that almost all patients included in the above two RCTs were outpatients, which may directly contribute to the lower incidence of thrombus propagation and bleeding. In addition to the above RCTs, several observational studies [ , , , ] have shown that anticoagulation not only failed to reduce the risk of thrombus propagation, but also increased the risk of bleeding.
In the anticoagulation group after PSM, over 70% of patients initially received low-dose rather than standard-dose anticoagulation after IDDVT diagnosis. Subgroup analysis revealed that no significant differences were observed in any study outcomes between the standard-dose and low-dose subgroups, which aligns with findings from a retrospective study [ ] of 33 postoperative patients with IDDVT. Although no significant differences in any outcomes between the two subgroups in our study, it should be noted that baseline characteristics were imbalanced. Specifically, the low-dose subgroup had significantly higher proportions of patients with coagulopathy and deep vein catheterization compared to the standard-dose subgroup, which may have inherently increased bleeding risk. Regrettably, the limited sample size in both subgroups precluded additional PSM. We therefore postulate that low-dose anticoagulation may potentially offset the elevated bleeding risk associated with baseline characteristics when compared to standard-dose anticoagulation.
Our study demonstrated that nearly 70% of thrombus propagation cases occurred within 2 weeks after IDDVT diagnosis, while over 80% occurred within the first month. A prospective study [ ] showed that more than 90% of thrombus propagation occurred within 2 weeks after IDDVT diagnosis. In addition, the 9th edition of ACCP antithrombotic guidelines [ ] recommend serial imaging of the deep veins for 2 weeks in patients who do not receive anticoagulation, which suggests that the initial 2-week period following IDDVT diagnosis constitutes a critical high-risk window for thrombus propagation. Based on the previous studies and our current findings, early monitoring for PDVT as well as PE may be essential in critically ill patients with IDDVT.
In our study, surgery and no early anticoagulation were considered as independent risk factors for thrombus propagation in critically ill patients. Previous studies [ , , , – ] have shown that advanced age, male sex, larger thrombus, higher D-dimer level, active cancer, prolonged immobilization, inpatient status, orthopedic surgery, end-stage renal disease, stroke, and history of VTE were all risk factors for thrombus propagation in patients with IDDVT. In addition, a recent meta-analysis [ ] showed a higher risk of thrombus propagation in patients with axial IDDVT compared to those with muscular IDDVT. Most of the patients included in our study had the above risk factors, such as advanced age, high D-dimer level, prolonged immobilization, and inpatient status. However, there were no relevant studies revealing risk factors for thrombus propagation specific to critically ill patients with IDDVT. Patients undergoing surgical procedures develop pathophysiological responses such as vascular endothelial injury, postoperative immobilization, systemic inflammation, and hypercoagulability, which may be associated with thrombus propagation. Whether anticoagulation is a protective factor for thrombus propagation remains controversial [ , , , ]. In our study, the role of anticoagulation may be amplified by the multiple risk factors for thrombus propagation in critically ill patients, such as severe mobility impairments and high D-dimer levels.
The incidence of both major bleeding and CRNMB in our study was higher than that reported in previous studies [ – , , ], which may be explained by differences in patient selection—over 70% of the patients in our study had a high bleeding risk, whereas most prior studies focused on low-risk outpatients or general inpatients. This study identified active malignancy, trauma, and an IMPROVE score ≥ 7 as risk factors for bleeding in these patients, which has been confirmed in previous researches [ , ]. Therefore, for critically ill patients with active malignancy, trauma, or an IMPROVE score ≥ 7, clinicians should adopt a more cautious approach to anticoagulation, as these patients inherently carry a significantly elevated bleeding risk.
The incidence of thrombus resolution in patients with IDDVT receiving anticoagulation was 50–70% [ , , ]. In our study, the incidence of thrombus resolution was 39.6% and 28.3% (P = 0.218) in the anticoagulation and non-anticoagulation groups, respectively, which was lower than above studies, and this may be due to some factors such as prolonged immobilization, inadequate anticoagulation, and insufficient follow-up duration. Sule et al. [ ] found no statistically significant difference in the incidence of thrombus resolution between patients with IDDVT who received anticoagulation and those who did not. Similar conclusions were given by a previous RCT [ ], which showed that the incidence of thrombus resolution was 66.6% in patients with standard-dose anticoagulation and 60.4% in those who did not receive anticoagulation (P = 0.23). However, while another retrospective study [ ] showed that the incidence of thrombus resolution was significantly higher in patients receiving standard-dose anticoagulation than in those receiving low-dose or no anticoagulation (61.2% vs. 40.0% vs. 41.0%, P = 0.003), our subgroup analysis failed to replicate these findings.
In general, patients with IDDVT had a lower risk of mortality than patients with PDVT [ – ]. Consistent with the findings of most previous studies [ , , , , , , ], our study showed no effect of anticoagulation on all-cause mortality in critically ill patients with IDDVT regardless of PSM. However, a retrospective study [ ] found that standard-dose anticoagulation significantly reduced all-cause mortality (OR = 0.37, 95% CI 0.17–0.80, P = 0.02). It is worth noting that this study categorized patients into standard therapy group and non-standard therapy group by anticoagulant dosage, however, our subgroup analysis demonstrated that standard-dose anticoagulation failed to reduce all-cause mortality.

## Limitations
This study has several limitations. First, it should be considered that a retrospective study has inherent weaknesses, owing to the confounding bias in baseline characteristics between the two groups. In response, we performed PSM to control the bias, but lost a large number of samples as a result. Second, CT pulmonary angiography was not performed in all included patients at baseline to assess for combined PE, which may have overestimated the incidence of thrombus propagation. Third, ultrasound at baseline failed to provide detailed data about the IDDVT, such as diameter and length of thrombus, which have been identified as risk factors for thrombus propagation. Fourth, this study had a short follow-up period compared with the routine 3-month follow-up period, which may underestimate the risk of thrombus propagation. In addition, the patients failed to undergo ultrasound or CT pulmonary angiography regularly during follow-up, which may also lead to an underestimation of the incidence of thrombus propagation. Finally, while our study conducted subgroup analysis by anticoagulant dosage, the limited sample size in each subgroup substantially reduced statistical power. Therefore, multicenter, large-scale studies are warranted to determine the optimal anticoagulation regimen for this patient population in the future.

## Conclusion
In our study, critically ill patients with IDDVT were at high risk for short-term thrombus propagation, and early anticoagulation can significantly reduce this risk without additionally increasing bleeding events. However, caution should be exercised in patients with active malignant tumor, trauma, or an IMPROVE score ≥ 7, as they were inherently at high bleeding risk. In addition, although most patients in our study received low-dose anticoagulation, the role of this regimen in such population warrants further comprehensive evaluation.
