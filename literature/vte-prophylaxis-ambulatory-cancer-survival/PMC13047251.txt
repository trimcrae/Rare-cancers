## Abstract
Cancer patients have an increased risk of thromboembolic events. Consequently, anticoagulants such as rivaroxaban are often prescribed. With the increasing use of targeted therapies, there is a growing concern about potential drug–drug interactions, as these therapies can affect metabolic enzymes and transporters (i.e., cytochrome P450 3A and P‐glycoprotein). When used in combination with rivaroxaban, these interactions may elevate a patient's risk of thromboembolic events or bleeding. Our primary objective was to investigate the effect of sotorasib on the pharmacokinetics of rivaroxaban and determine the safety of this combination in healthy volunteers. In an open label, single‐sequence, pharmacokinetic drug–drug interaction study in healthy subjects, the influence of a once‐daily 960 mg dose sotorasib for a period of 14 days was evaluated on the pharmacokinetics of a single 20 mg dose rivaroxaban. This clinical study followed the FDA guidance on drug interaction studies. A clinically relevant pharmacokinetic interaction was considered absent if the 90% confidence intervals (CIs) of the geometric mean ratios for C
max and AUC0–∞ were within the no‐effect boundaries of 0.70–1.43. A total of 20 healthy subjects completed the study. The geometric mean ratios 1.00 (90% CI 0.90–1.12) for C
max and 0.79 (90% CI 0.73–0.86) for AUC0–∞ fell inside the predefined criteria, indicating no clinically relevant pharmacokinetic interaction. In addition, rivaroxaban and sotorasib were well tolerated by the healthy subjects. These results suggest that rivaroxaban can be safely co‐administered without the need for switching to another treatment or dose adjustments in patients treated with sotorasib.

What's new?

Venous thromboembolism (VTE) is common in cancer patients, with direct oral anticoagulants, including rivaroxaban, recommended as first‐line therapy. However, oral anticoagulant use is limited due to the growing use of targeted therapy and concerns about drug‐drug interactions. Here, the authors assessed possible pharmacokinetic interactions between rivaroxaban and the targeted KRAS inhibitor sotorasib in healthy volunteers. No clinically relevant changes in rivaroxaban exposure were observed following co‐administration of rivaroxaban and sotorasib. Both drugs were also well‐tolerated. The findings suggest that rivaroxaban may be safely used alongside sotorasib, without the need for dose modification or switching to other treatments.

## Blood sampling
For each participant, blood samples were collected over a period of 48 h to construct pharmacokinetic curves of both rivaroxaban and sotorasib. Blood samples to assess rivaroxaban concentrations were collected at: t = 0 (pre‐dose), 0.5, 1, 1.5, 2, 3, 4, 6, 8, 12, 24, and 48 h post dose on day 1 and day 16. For sotorasib, samples were collected at the same time points (except at t = 48 h) on day 16 to confirm drug intake, compare exposure with reported patient data, and account for variability. All blood samples were centrifuged at 1900 G for 5 min at room temperature within 1.5 h after collection. Rivaroxaban and sotorasib plasma samples were stored at −80 and −40°C, respectively.

2.3

## Safety and tolerability
Safety was evaluated by monitoring treatment‐emergent adverse events. Adverse events were graded with the most recent version (version 6) of the Common Toxicity Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE). Incidence, severity, and relationship of adverse events to the study drugs were recorded. Additional testing of liver values (ALAT, ASAT, GGT, ALP, and total bilirubin) and serum electrolytes (sodium, potassium, chloride, calcium, phosphate, and magnesium) were performed on the days of pharmacokinetic sampling, which took place before the start of sotorasib and 14 days later (in accordance with the recommendations in the Summary of Product Characteristics [SmPC] ). All adverse events were followed to a satisfactory resolution or clinically stable endpoint.

2.4

## Sample size calculation
Based on a previous pharmacokinetic drug–drug interaction study with rivaroxaban, an intra‐individual coefficient of variation (CV) of 20% in C
max was assumed for sample size calculation. This CV is considered a worst‐case scenario, as the observed intra‐individual CV for rivaroxaban C
max and AUC in this previous study was 16.5% and 11.3%, respectively. A total sample size of 20 subjects was considered sufficient to show that the 90% confidence interval of the ratio of the log‐transformed AUC or C
max is within the no‐effect boundaries of 0.70–1.43, with a power of 90% and a significance level of 5%. Our goal was to include a total of 22 subjects to account for dropouts.

2.5

## Analysis
Concentrations of rivaroxaban and sotorasib in plasma were quantified using ultra‐performance liquid chromatography with targeted tandem mass spectrometric (UPLC‐MS/MS) assays using stable isotope‐labeled internal standards. Both assays were validated in accordance with regulatory bioanalytical method validation guidelines. The lower limits of quantification in these assays are 2.0 and 100.0 μg/L for rivaroxaban and sotorasib, respectively. Concentrations above the lower limit of quantification were determined with a precision (%CV) of less than 15% and an accuracy within 85–115% for both rivaroxaban and sotorasib. Additionally, both assays are subjected to external quality control.
A non‐compartmental pharmacokinetic analysis was performed using Phoenix WinNonlin version 8.4 (Certara, Durham, NC). To determine the area under the plasma concentration–time curve from time 0 to infinity (AUC0–∞), the linear up/log down trapezoidal method was used. Additionally, C
max, time to maximum plasma concentration (T
max), and elimination half‐life (t
1/2) were determined. For the first measurement, concentration values reported as below the lower limit of quantification (LLOQ) were replaced by 0.5*LLOQ for the pharmacokinetic analysis. For subsequent measurements, concentration values reported below the LLOQ were replaced by 0 μg/L.
Geometric mean ratios (GMR) and 90% confidence intervals (CIs) of the test treatment (rivaroxaban and sotorasib combined) to the reference treatment (rivaroxaban alone) were determined using the method described in the FDA guidance on Drug Interaction Studies. To establish whether there is a potential, clinically relevant pharmacokinetic interaction, the geometric mean ratios of AUC0–∞ and C
max should lie within the no‐effect boundaries of rivaroxaban. The following prespecified acceptance criteria were used: if after a single dose, the 90% CIs of the geometric mean ratios of AUC0–∞ and C
max are within the range of 0.70–1.43, there is no clinically relevant pharmacokinetic interaction. The 0.70–1.43 boundaries were selected based on a previous drug–drug interaction study with rivaroxaban, under the assumption that a ~30% change in AUC is not clinically relevant, as this lies within the range of inter‐individual variability typically observed in patients. , To assess the potential relation between systemic exposure to sotorasib (AUC0–24h) and the relative change in rivaroxaban exposure (AUCafter/AUCbefore), Spearman's rank correlation was used.

## Impact of sotorasib on the pharmacokinetics of rivaroxaban
To assess the influence of sotorasib on the pharmacokinetics of rivaroxaban, the mean plasma concentration–time profiles of rivaroxaban in the absence and presence of sotorasib were determined (Figure  [linear scale]; Figure  [semi‐logarithmic scale]). Additionally, a non‐compartmental pharmacokinetic analysis was performed to determine the pharmacokinetic parameters of rivaroxaban (Table  ). In the presence of sotorasib, C
max was unchanged (GMR 1.00; 90% CI 0.90–1.12). However, rivaroxaban AUC0–∞ was significantly lower as the whole 90% CI was below 1.00 (GMR 0.79; 90% CI 0.73–0.86), but it was not considered clinically relevant as the whole 90% CI was within predefined acceptance criteria of 0.70–1.43.

## Pharmacokinetics of sotorasib
To assess the pharmacokinetics of sotorasib, the geometric mean plasma concentration‐time profile was determined (Figure  [linear scale]; Figure  [semi‐logarithmic scale]) and a non‐compartmental pharmacokinetic analysis was performed (Table  ). To assess the potential relationship between systemic exposure to sotorasib (AUC0–24h) and the relative change in rivaroxaban exposure (AUCafter/AUCbefore), Spearman's rank correlation was used (Figure  ). No significant association was observed (ρ = 0.026, p = .915).
 
3.4

## Safety and tolerability
There were no changes in liver (ALAT, ASAT, GGT, ALP, and total bilirubin) or serum electrolytes values (sodium, potassium, chloride, calcium, phosphate, and magnesium) after the administration of sotorasib. None of the results that were outside of the normal range were clinically significant (Table  ).
In total, 51 adverse events of grade 1 and 2 were observed and reported during the study. No grade 3 or 4 adverse events were observed. The grade 2 adverse events (allergic reaction to food and dyspnea) were judged not to be related to the study medication. The most commonly observed adverse events included diarrhea, headache, and nausea. Among all adverse events, 15 were deemed unrelated to the study medication, 6 were considered unlikely to be related, 12 were possibly related, and 18 were probably related (Table  ). None of the drop‐outs in the study were related to adverse events.

## DISCUSSION
Contrary to prior guidance, our study showed no clinically relevant pharmacokinetic interaction between rivaroxaban and sotorasib, suggesting for the first time that it is safe to use these drugs concomitantly. As we followed the FDA guidance on drug interaction studies, the results can be directly implemented in clinical care.
Drug–drug interactions between targeted oncological therapy and DOACs are an important concern. Vitamin‐K‐antagonists (VKAs) are often the oral anticoagulant of choice because their anticoagulant effect can be monitored, and doses are easily adjusted. Nevertheless, VKAs are not the optimal treatment option for cancer patients, as studies have shown that LMWHs and DOACs are more effective in preventing recurrent venous thromboembolism (VTE) while carrying a similar risk of bleeding. As a result, low‐molecular‐weight heparins (LMWHs) are typically the first choice for patients on targeted therapy who require anticoagulant therapy, due to their lack of drug–drug interactions with these therapies. However, LMWH therapy has notable drawbacks, including lower patient satisfaction, reduced quality of life, reduced adherence, plastic waste and higher costs, which often lead to a switch to oral anticoagulants. Although DOACs are the preferred choice for cancer patients, they are frequently not prescribed to patients who are treated with targeted therapy due to concerns regarding potential drug–drug interactions. Our study demonstrated that there is no clinically relevant pharmacokinetic interaction between rivaroxaban and sotorasib when concomitantly administered. The combination of CYP and/or P‐gp inhibitors and/or inducers with their substrates is often avoided, even though, as we have demonstrated here for the first time with the KRAS G12C inhibitor sotorasib and rivaroxaban, the interaction may not necessarily be clinically relevant. Therefore, we would like to encourage further research on drug–drug interaction studies between DOACs and small‐molecule inhibitors.
The timing of administration may be critical for the observed interaction. Sotorasib has been suggested to be a P‐gp inhibitor, based on the increased exposure of digoxin when simultaneously administered in a single dose. Co‐administration of sotorasib during the absorption phase of rivaroxaban may therefore lead to transient higher intestinal and/or hepatic P‐gp inhibition impacting rivaroxaban pharmacokinetics. In this study, rivaroxaban and sotorasib were administered simultaneously; therefore, we are cautious with extrapolation of our findings to scenarios where the drugs are taken at separate times. This timing may also partly explain why the magnitude of our observed drug–drug interaction differed somewhat from predictions based on prior clinical interaction data with midazolam and digoxin, and from findings in previous reports, where a 59.1% decrease in rivaroxaban C
max was observed following sotorasib initiation. That study, however, was limited by its small sample size (n = 1). In contrast, our sample size is based on the intra‐individual variability of rivaroxaban and is considered sufficient to assess whether a clinically significant interaction occurs.
We found rivaroxaban pharmacokinetics (C
max 332 μg/L; AUC0–∞ 3393 h*μg/L) in the same order of magnitude as in previous studies in healthy subjects (C
max 173–280 μg/L; AUC0–∞ 1612–2425 h*μg/L) and in patients (C
max 270 μg/L; AUC24 2814 h*μg/L). , , , , , , , The observed intersubject variability was moderate (29% for C
max and 27% for AUC0–∞) for rivaroxaban, which is consistent with previous studies , , , , and its summary of product characteristics. 

The observed sotorasib AUC0–24h of 16,632 h*μg/L in our study correlated well with the predicted AUC of 14,226 h*μg/L for healthy subjects at steady‐state, based on the population pharmacokinetic study reported by the license holder. 

Rivaroxaban and sotorasib were generally well‐tolerated by the participants, since all adverse events with estimated relation to study medication were grade 1. These findings align with adverse events previously reported in single‐dose trials of rivaroxaban in healthy subjects. , , , , , Similarly, this outcome was anticipated for sotorasib, given the drug's high selectivity for the KRAS G12C mutation, which is exclusively present in tumor cells of individuals with KRAS G12C‐mutated cancer. The observed adverse events were consistent with those observed with sotorasib in previous healthy volunteer studies. , , , 

It could be argued that conducting our study in healthy subjects using a single‐dose design is a limitation. However, this approach is consistent with regulatory guidance for drug–drug interaction studies when the victim drug demonstrates clear linear and time‐independent pharmacokinetics, as is well established for rivaroxaban. A single‐dose design permits a reliable assessment of exposure changes while minimizing unnecessary bleeding risk in healthy subjects that would be associated with multiple dosing. It should also be noted that single‐dose pharmacokinetics studies in healthy subjects are widely used and considered the gold standard for evaluating drug–drug interactions with DOACs. A 20 mg rivaroxaban dose was used because long‐term therapy for cancer‐associated VTE typically consists of a 20 mg once‐daily maintenance dose, making it the most clinically relevant for this population. Moreover, because rivaroxaban exhibits linear pharmacokinetics across its therapeutic range, the relative effects observed with 20 mg can be extrapolated to other clinical doses. For sotorasib, the registered therapeutic dose of 960 mg once daily was selected, corresponding to the approved clinical regimen for patients. , Prior to our study, exposing vulnerable patients with an indication for treatment with sotorasib to rivaroxaban, without knowing if there is a clinically significant interaction, would have been unethical due to the potential bleeding risk. Since we observed no clinically significant pharmacokinetic interaction between rivaroxaban and sotorasib, one can infer that rivaroxaban can also be prescribed in patients who are treated with sotorasib. We, nonetheless, advise any prescriber to take all concurrent medications and comorbidities into account when considering combining sotorasib and rivaroxaban.

5

## CONCLUSION
Co‐administration of sotorasib with rivaroxaban does not cause a clinically relevant pharmacokinetic drug–drug interaction. These results suggest that rivaroxaban can be safely co‐administered without the need for switching to another treatment or dose adjustments in patients treated with sotorasib.
