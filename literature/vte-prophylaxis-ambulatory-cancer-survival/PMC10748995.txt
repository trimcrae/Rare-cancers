## Abstract

## Objectives
Compared with ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI) patients, non-STEMI (NSTEMI) patients have more comorbidities and extensive coronary artery disease. Contemporary comparative data on the long-term prognosis of stable post-myocardial infarction subtypes are needed.

## Design
Long-Term rIsk, clinical manaGement and healthcare Resource utilisation of stable coronary artery dISease (TIGRIS) was a multinational, observational and longitudinal cohort study.

## Setting
Patients were enrolled from 350 centres, with >95% coming from cardiology practices across 24 countries, from 19 June 2013 to 31 March 2017.

## Participants
This study enrolled 8277 stable patients 1–3 years after myocardial infarction with ≥1 additional risk factor.

## Outcome measures
Over a 2 year follow-up, cardiovascular events and deaths and self-reported health using the EuroQol 5-dimension questionnaire score were recorded. Relative risk of clinical events and health resource utilisation in STEMI and NSTEMI patients were compared using multivariable Poisson regression models, adjusting for prognostically relevant patient factors.

## Results
Of 7752 patients with known myocardial infarction type, 46% had NSTEMI; NSTEMI patients were older with more comorbidities than STEMI patients. NSTEMI patients had significantly poorer self-reported health and lower prevalence of dual antiplatelet therapy at hospital discharge and at enrolment 1–3 years later. NSTEMI patients had a higher incidence of combined myocardial infarction, stroke and cardiovascular death (5.6% vs 3.9%, p<0.001) and higher all-cause mortality (4.2% vs 2.6%, p<0.001) compared with STEMI patients. Risks were attenuated after adjusting for other patient characteristics. Health resource utilisation was higher in NSTEMI patients, although STEMI patients had more cardiologist visits.

## Conclusions
Post-NSTEMI chronic coronary syndrome patients had a less favourable risk factor profile, poorer self-reported health and more adverse cardiovascular events during long-term follow-up than individuals post STEMI. Efforts are needed to recognise the risks of stable patients after NSTEMI and optimise secondary prevention and care.

## STRENGTHS AND LIMITATIONS OF THIS STUDY
This longitudinal cohort study used data from a large, multinational (24 countries), multicentre, prospective observational registry (TIGRIS, long-Term rIsk, clinical manaGement and healthcare Resource utilisation of stable coronary artery dISease); thus, the results are potentially generalisable among ethnically and racially diverse populations.
A unique contribution from the study was the assessment of self-reported quality of life during long-term follow-up on cardiovascular outcomes in stable patients identified 1–3 years post-MI.
The TIGRIS registry was the first to analyse whether ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI) and non-STEMI patients received specialised care during outpatient follow-up.
TIGRIS was not based on a specific a priori hypothesis, thus the findings cannot be considered conclusive.
Lack of a control population, limited details on extent of coronary disease, treatment patterns and targets for comorbidities and cardiovascular risk factors and under-reporting of outcomes for patients who left the registry was a further limitation.

## Introduction
Significant advances in the recognition and treatment of ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI) and non-STEMI (NSTEMI) have led to more favourable short-term and long-term clinical outcomes, with similar improvements in 30-day mortality in both myocardial infarction (MI) subtypes. However, studies have suggested that 1 year mortality rates for NSTEMI are significantly higher compared with those for STEMI. In addition to these studies demonstrating increased comorbidities in NSTEMI patients at baseline, NSTEMI patients also received suboptimal care, with a lower use of percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) and guideline-recommended medications at discharge.
Long-Term rIsk, clinical manaGement and healthcare Resource utilisation of stable coronary artery dISease (TIGRIS) is a global registry of patients with stable coronary artery disease (CAD) who were followed up after an MI 1–3 years prior to enrolment. TIGRIS included a ‘real-world’ patient population that spanned multiple countries and, therefore, has provided an opportunity to examine the characteristics, management, health resource utilisation, quality of life and long-term outcomes of a diverse cohort of MI patients. In our recent study, which was also based on the TIGRIS registry, we evaluated the associations between health-related quality of life (HRQoL) and patient profile, resource use, cardiovascular (CV) events and mortality in stable patients post-MI. This previous study demonstrated that poor HRQoL was associated with an increased risk of hospitalisations, major CV events and death. However, it did not assess the differences in outcomes between NSTEMI and STEMI patients, two distinct subtypes of MI. In addition, there is a lack of more contemporary data on the clinical course of chronic coronary syndrome patients who survive 12 months or more after MI. Therefore, the current study aimed to compare the long-term prognosis of CV outcomes between STEMI and NSTEMI patients.

## Methods

## Study design and patients
The TIGRIS registry is a multinational, observational, longitudinal cohort study that enrolled 8277 patients in 350 centres, with >95% of the patients coming from cardiology practices across 24 countries ( ), from 19 June 2013 to 31 March 2017. The study centres that participated in this study are listed in . The study was intended to provide contemporary data on late outcomes of post-MI patients. Details of the TIGRIS registry have been published. Briefly, patients were enrolled if they had stable CAD and were 50 years or older 1–3 years after the index MI, which included STEMI, NSTEMI or unknown. All patients had at least one additional risk factor, including age ≥65 years, diabetes mellitus requiring medication, second prior MI, multivessel CAD or chronic non–end-stage (creatinine clearance (by Cockcroft-Gault equation) 15 mL/min to <60 mL/min) chronic kidney disease. Exclusion criteria were the presence of serious comorbidities that may limit life expectancy to <1 year, active participation in a blinded randomised clinical trial, presence of any condition/circumstance that could limit complete patient follow-up and receiving ticagrelor beyond 12 months after the index MI or for off-label use.

Data were collected using standardised electronic case report forms, which included demographics, comorbidities, variables from routine examination (at baseline), medication use, quality of life, health resource utilisation and clinical events during follow-up. Index event data were collected retrospectively, as patients were enrolled in the outpatient setting 1–3 years after their MI. However, baseline and follow-up data were collected prospectively.
TIGRIS was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, the International Conference on Harmonisation Good Clinical Practice guidelines and applicable legislation on non-interventional studies. All study patients provided written informed consent. The study protocol and informed consent were reviewed by the corresponding health authorities and ethics boards of all participating study sites ( ).

## Patient and public involvement
Patients and the public were not involved in the research, study design or the dissemination of study results.

## Outcome measures
For this analysis, the primary outcome measure included a composite of MI, stroke and death from any cause as well as health resource utilisation. Secondary outcome measures were ischaemic events and bleeding events that required medical attention. Over the 2 year follow-up from 1 to 3 years after MI, patients were contacted every 6 months either by telephone or hospital visit. The incidence of CV events and deaths was recorded along with self-reported health using the EuroQol Foundation survey instrument, which was completed by study patients at each visit. The instrument measures health status in five dimensions (EQ-5D), including mobility, self-care, usual activities, pain/discomfort and anxiety/depression, with three levels of severity (EQ-5D-3L) categorised as none, moderate and severe; a visual analogue scale (VAS), where patients self-rate their health status from 0 (worst possible) to 100 (perfect health) and the UK-weighted index combining all five domains into a single score, which provides a widely accepted summary of the overall health status. A score of 1 indicates ‘no problems’ on all five items, and a score of <1 indicates increasingly poor self-rated health status, down to a rare score of 0, indicating ‘as bad as being dead’. 
Patients underwent clinical assessments and received standard medical care as determined by the treating physician without any experimental intervention or experimental treatment.

## Statistical analysis
Continuous variables are presented as means (SD) or median (IQR) and compared using Student’s t-test or Mann-Whitney U test where appropriate. Discrete variables are presented as frequencies with their respective percentages and compared using the χ2 test of independence.
The 2 year cumulative incidence (risk %) of each clinical outcome was calculated for STEMI and NSTEMI patients using Kaplan-Meier estimates, and univariable comparisons were made using log-rank tests. Multivariable Poisson regression models were used to compare STEMI and NSTEMI patients for their relative risk of events, adjusting for prognostically relevant patient factors.
The number of hospitalisations for any cause and CV cause, and the number of emergency room (ER), general practitioner (GP), cardiologist and other specialist visits over the 2 year follow-up period were summarised in 7415 patients with health resource utilisation data at every visit and compared with the data of NSTEMI versus STEMI patients using a two-sample t-test.
To estimate the association between type of index MI and self-reported quality of life, the EQ-5D UK-weighted index score (categorised as <0.65, 0.65–0.69, 0.70–0.74, 0.75–0.99 and 1) and EQ-5D VAS score (categorised as <60, 60–69, 70–79, 80–89 and 90–100) were compared between NSTEMI and STEMI patients using the χ2 test for trend, and individual items (mobility, self-care, usual activities, pain/discomfort and anxiety/depression categorised as ‘no problems’ and ‘moderate or severe problems’) were compared using the χ2 test of independence.
A p value<0.05 was considered statistically significant. Stata V.15.1 was used for all statistical analyses.

## Results
Of the 8277 patients enrolled, 525 had MI of unknown type and were excluded from this analysis. Hence, this report is based on 7752 patients, of whom 4149 (53.5%) had STEMI and 3603 (46.5%) had NSTEMI ( ). The median follow-up was 2 years.
Patient characteristics at enrolment by type of index MI are presented in . Differences in baseline characteristics were modest. NSTEMI patients were marginally older, more likely female and had a higher body mass index. Diabetes and other CV conditions were also more common among NSTEMI patients. STEMI patients were more likely to be treated with PCI for their index MI, whereas NSTEMI patients had higher rates of coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG) and medical management only.
 Patients’ self-reported EQ-5D health status for mobility, usual activities and pain showed a significantly higher proportion of NSTEMI patients reporting problems compared with STEMI patients ( ). The EQ-5D UK-weighted index score showed that compared with STEMI patients, NSTEMI patients had a highly significant tendency for poorer self-rated health at enrolment 1–3 years after the index MI. The EQ-5D VAS showed similar findings in patients with NSTEMI compared to patients with STEMI.
 shows patient enrolment by region and by country and the corresponding percentage with NSTEMI. There were significant interregion and intercountry variations (both p<0.001). North America and Asia/Australia enrolled the lowest and highest percentage of NSTEMI patients, respectively. Among countries, Japan and Romania enrolled the highest percentage of NSTEMI patients, whereas Norway and the USA enrolled the lowest.
The uses of antithrombotic treatments, both at discharge after the index MI and at enrolment, are shown by the type of index MI in . STEMI patients underwent PCI more often (87.8% vs 71.2%) and were more likely to receive dual antiplatelet therapy (DAPT) at discharge (81.6% vs 72.9%) compared with NSTEMI patients. At enrolment (1–3 years after the index event), DAPT use had reduced markedly, but more STEMI patients were still on DAPT (27.1% vs 21.8%) compared with NSTEMI patients. In addition, more NSTEMI patients were on diuretics and antidepressants, reflecting their numerous comorbidities compared with STEMI patients.
The incidence of CV events and deaths by the type of index MI over the 2 year follow-up is shown in . NSTEMI patients had a higher incidence of the composite of MI, stroke and CV death compared with STEMI patients (5.6% vs 3.9%, p<0.001). This was mainly driven by an excess of CV deaths in NSTEMI patients (2.5% vs 1.5%, p<0.001), which in turn led to an excess of all-cause deaths (4.2% vs 2.6%, p<0.001). displays a steady divergence over 2 years in the cumulative incidence of (a) the composite of CV death, MI and stroke and (b) all-cause death.
 The unadjusted incidence rate ratios for NSTEMI versus STEMI for the composite of CV death, MI and stroke was 1.46 (95% CI: 1.19 to 1.80; p<0.001). After adjustment for imbalances in baseline variables at enrolment, including age, diabetes, second prior MI, quality of life and peripheral arterial disease as well as geographical variation, the risk ratio (RR) was 1.23 (95% CI: 0.99 to 1.53; p=0.06).
All-cause death was significantly higher among NSTEMI patients with a RR of 1.62 (95% CI: 1.26 to 2.07; p<0.001). The excess risk was attenuated after adjustment but remained statistically higher with an RR of 1.34 (95% CI: 1.04 to 1.73; p=0.02).
 shows the comparison of STEMI and NSTEMI patients across six different aspects of health resource utilisation over the 2 year follow-up period, including all-cause and CV hospitalisations; visits to ERs, GPs and cardiologists; and visits to other specialists. NSTEMI patients had significantly higher health resource utilisation than STEMI patients. NSTEMI patients had 26% more hospitalisations in total, 24% more CV hospitalisations, 52% more ER visits and 6% more visits to specialists other than cardiologists. However, NSTEMI patients had 28% fewer visits to cardiologists.
 

## Discussion
Our analysis of a large, multinational, multicentre, prospective, observational study showed that stable patients with prior NSTEMI had more risk factors and comorbidities, higher incidence of CV events, poorer quality of life and higher health resource utilisation than STEMI patients. In contrast to prior single-country studies, this analysis was based on data from 24 countries with relatively few exclusion criteria; thus, the results are potentially more generalisable among ethnically and racially diverse populations. In addition, this study provided a view of long-term outcomes in stable patients who survived 1–3 years after the index MI, a group of patients who have not been analysed and systematically studied previously.
In TIGRIS, the incidence of the composite of CV death, MI and stroke over 2 years was higher among NSTEMI patients compared with STEMI patients. In a pooled analysis of five randomised trials examining the efficacy of drug-eluting stent technologies, Pilgrim et al assessed the outcomes of 3364 patients with non-ST-elevation acute coronary syndromes (NSTEACS) and 1952 patients with STEMI. Their study showed that NSTEACS patients had a less favourable risk factor profile compared with STEMI patients. In contrast with our results, the 2 year mortality in their study was higher among STEMI compared with NSTEACS patients (OR: 1.40; 95% CI: 1.09 to 1.78; p=0.007). However, a landmark analysis showed that after 30 days, adverse cardiac events continued to occur steadily in NSTEACS patients at a significantly higher rate than in STEMI patients, who typically experienced a higher upfront risk during the first 30 days after the index event. It is important to keep in mind that this landmark analysis study only included a selected group of patients meeting stringent eligibility criteria for a clinical trial and that all patients were revascularised with PCI. Similar findings were observed in the GUSTO-IIb trial that enrolled 12 142 patients across the spectrum of acute coronary syndromes (ACS), from unstable angina to NSTEMI and STEMI. Patients with NSTEMI had a significantly higher incidence of late reinfarction and mortality than STEMI patients. The difference in event rate was attributed to higher residual ischaemia among NSTEMI patients, as STEMI patients were more likely to be completely revascularised. The Global Registry of Acute Coronary Events (GRACE) also assessed outcomes of 46 829 patients with ACS according to the presentation with ST-segment elevation, ST-segment depression or no ST deviation. In STEMI patients, 43% of deaths occurred within the first 4 days, whereas this proportion was 26% for patients with ST-segment deviation and 22% for those without ST-segment deviation. For patients who survived the first 2 weeks, the risk inverted, and the 180-day mortality was the greatest in the ST-segment depression group, exceeding that of STEMI patients (HR: 0.85; 95% CI: 0.75 to 0.97; p=0.02). Patients without ST deviation had the lowest mortality. Prospective follow-up of hospital survivors to 2 years in a subset of 19 122 patients in GRACE, including 35% with STEMI, 37% with NSTEMI and 28% with unstable angina, demonstrated lower age-adjusted and sex-adjusted rates of death, recurrent MI, heart failure and unscheduled CV rehospitalisation in the STEMI group versus the NSTEMI group. 
Our TIGRIS analysis extends previous findings to stable patients at 1–3 years after their index event, showing that NSTEMI patients continue to experience a linear increase in adverse ischaemic events and also experienced higher number of overall hospitalisations due to their larger burden of comorbidities. Specifically, diabetes, atrial fibrillation (AF) and peripheral arterial disease were more common in the NSTEMI group. Our previous study reported that stable post-MI patients with diabetes mellitus (especially insulin treated) had poorer EQ-5D scores, higher hospitalisation rates and length of stay, and worse clinical outcomes versus those without diabetes mellitus. In addition, a significantly higher all-cause mortality have been reported in patients with NSTEMI compared with patients with STEMI. In stable post-MI patients, the presence of AF is associated with poorer quality of life and increased risk of clinical outcomes than in patients without AF. Moreover, studies have shown that AF was associated with an increased risk of NSTEMI but not STEMI. Another study also reported that patients with peripheral arterial disease experience more NSTEMI than STEMI. These findings are consistent with those reported in our study and highlight the need for continued aggressive secondary prevention measures in this population. In addition, adherence to these secondary treatment strategies are also crucial as non-adherence is associated with increased risk of all-cause and CV mortality, revascularisation procedures, hospitalisation and higher healthcare cost. Therefore, interventions to ensure adherence to these secondary treatment strategies are crucial for optimising the outcomes of patients diagnosed with CAD. 
A unique contribution from our study was the assessment of self-reported quality of life during long-term follow-up. Most previous studies did not assess the impact of quality of life on CV outcomes in stable patients initially treated for ACS. A Korean multicentre registry used the EQ-5D scoring in a 30-day span after what they termed ‘optimal PCI’ for STEMI and NSTEMI patients. They found that after percutaneous revascularisation, the general health status of patients treated for NSTEMI was worse than that of those treated for STEMI. The former group experienced angina relief but did not improve in the general domains of quality of life compared with the latter group. The authors suggested that a more systematic and holistic care with an emphasis on rehabilitation and social/psychological support is warranted for NSTEMI patients, as opposed to only simple angina-specific therapy. Our analysis from TIGRIS confirmed these findings and extended them to a population identified at 1–3 years after MI and followed them for an additional 2 years. Ischaemia-guided management may not be the only key to reducing the mortality of these patients; the management of the underlying atherosclerotic disease and other factors that impact the quality of life, including frailty, may also be paramount.
An interesting finding from our study was that NSTEMI patients were substantially less likely to be seen by a cardiologist during follow-up despite higher rates of hospitalisations and ER visits. Previous studies did not analyse whether patients received specialised care during outpatient follow-up. As NSTEMI tends to occur in higher-risk patients with less favourable outcomes and requires higher health resource utilisation, patients require careful clinical monitoring and aggressive secondary prevention measures.

## Limitations
Our analysis has several limitations, including those that are inherent to registry-based cohort studies. As the study was not based on a specific a priori hypothesis, the findings cannot be considered conclusive. Other limitations include a lack of a control population, limited availability of treatment data and under-reporting of outcomes if a patient left the registry. Of note, only 3.5% (n=273) of patients were lost to follow-up within 2 years. The frequency of 3 year events after MI was also notably low in the STEMI and NSTEMI populations in our study. Additionally, details regarding the extent of coronary disease (beyond qualitatively identifying those with multivessel involvement) and left ventricular function evaluation (eg, estimated ejection fraction) were not collected. This longitudinal study included patients from multiple centres, countries and regions with variable and potentially diverse treatment patterns and styles. For example, patients who undergo revascularisation procedures may be better medically managed and more likely to be followed by cardiologists than those not undergoing these procedures. Although certain statistical methods aim to control such geographic and intercentre variability, the considerable number of study sites involved raises concerns regarding the effectiveness and robustness of such analyses, and whether it would have any significant impact on the findings of this study. Moreover, we acknowledge the presence of potential unmeasured confounders that may have an effect on the relationship between the type of MI and outcomes. Although sensitivity analyses, such as instrumental variable analyses, can partially address this problem, they may not be able to resolve this issue entirely. As such, we have not performed such complex analyses in this study. Nonetheless, our analysis offers a comprehensive description of a contemporary cohort of patients with stable ischaemic heart disease 1 year after an acute coronary event, which provides clinicians with important insights into the outcomes and prognosis of these patients.
Young STEMI patients (<50 years old) with single-vessel disease were excluded from the study, which could explain why the baseline differences between groups (eg, age, number of comorbidities and smoking history) were not as large as expected.
Treatment targets for comorbidities and CV risk factors, such as blood pressure and lipid levels, were not recorded. In addition, patients who were receiving ticagrelor for more than 12 months were excluded from study participation as this antiplatelet therapy was not approved for use beyond 12 months after ACS at the time of patient enrolment in TIGRIS. However, more recent studies have shown benefits with prolonged DAPT (or the combination of aspirin and low-dose rivaroxaban) in high-risk CAD cases, a phenotype that may overlap with some patients in the NSTEMI cohort who have multivessel CAD. 

## Conclusions
In this large registry of chronic coronary syndrome patients post-MI, those who suffered NSTEMI had a less favourable baseline risk factor profile with more comorbid conditions and had poorer self-reported health and more adverse CV events during long-term follow-up compared with STEMI patients. Post-NSTEMI patients had higher hospitalisation rates and health resource utilisation and were less likely to be cared for by a cardiologist during long-term follow-up. These findings suggest the need for continued long-term efforts to optimise secondary prevention and overall medical care among NSTEMI patients.
