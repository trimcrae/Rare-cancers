## Abstract

## Background and Objective
Venous thromboembolism (VTE) remains a leading cause of preventable morbidity and mortality in surgical patients, with individuals undergoing abdominal-based free flap breast reconstruction representing a particularly high-risk group. Despite the widespread use of risk assessment tools such as the Caprini Risk Assessment Model (RAM), prophylaxis strategies remain inconsistent across institutions. This literature review aims to synthesize current literature on VTE incidence, risk stratification, and chemoprophylaxis in abdominal-based free flap breast reconstruction to identify best practices and areas for future research.

## Methods
A narrative review of the literature was performed, focusing on studies evaluating VTE incidence, prevention strategies, and outcomes in patients undergoing abdominal-based autologous breast reconstruction. Emphasis was placed on risk stratification models, pharmacologic and mechanical prophylaxis, and the timing, dosing, and duration of anticoagulation regimens.

## Key Content and Findings
Validated tools such as the Caprini RAM effectively stratify surgical patients by VTE risk; however, specific recommendations for abdominal-based reconstruction are lacking. Extended-duration prophylaxis appears beneficial in high-risk patients, with recent studies supporting preoperative initiation. Despite concerns about flap loss or hematoma, evidence suggests that appropriately dosed chemoprophylaxis does not significantly increase complications. Oral agents like apixaban show promise for improving adherence without increased bleeding risk. The majority of VTE events occur after discharge, supporting the rationale for outpatient prophylaxis. Future directions include machine learning-enhanced risk prediction and multicenter trials to standardize prophylaxis.

## Conclusions
Patients undergoing abdominal-based breast reconstruction face substantial VTE risk, and tailored chemoprophylaxis is essential. While current evidence supports individualized risk-based strategies, practice variability underscores the need for a standardized chemoprophylaxis algorithm. Future prospective studies are critical to establish optimal prophylaxis regimens, timing, and duration in this high-risk surgical population.

Keywords: Venous thromboembolism (VTE), breast reconstruction, autologous reconstruction, abdominal-based breast reconstruction, deep inferior epigastric perforator flap (DIEP flap)

## Introduction
Venous thromboembolism (VTE), encompassing deep vein thrombosis (DVT) and pulmonary embolism (PE), is one of the leading preventable causes of perioperative morbidity and mortality in the United States ( , ). Among plastic surgery patient populations, patients undergoing abdominal-based breast reconstruction, such as those undergoing deep inferior epigastric perforator (DIEP) flap, represent a uniquely high-risk subgroup ( , ). These patients frequently have elevated Caprini risk scores due to long operative times, decreased postoperative mobility, and comorbidities such as obesity and a history of malignancy ( , ). Despite this well-established risk, there is no universally adopted standard for VTE chemoprophylaxis in abdominal-based breast reconstruction ( ). Guidelines from the American Society of Plastic Surgeons (ASPS) emphasize risk stratification but offer limited procedure-specific recommendations ( ). In practice, chemoprophylactic strategies for abdominal-based breast reconstruction—including timing, dosing, and duration—vary widely.
This review synthesizes current evidence on VTE in patients undergoing abdominal-based breast reconstruction, focusing on risk assessment tools, chemoprophylaxis strategies, and associated clinical outcomes. By providing a comprehensive, evidence-based summary, this review seeks to inform best practices and highlight opportunities for standardization and future research.
In particular, we aim to address the following key clinical questions:
What is the incidence of VTE in patients undergoing abdominal-based breast reconstruction, and which patient or procedural factors are associated with thrombotic events?
How effective are existing risk stratification tools—such as the 2005 Caprini Risk Assessment Model (RAM)—in predicting VTE risk in this surgical population? What are the recommended chemoprophylaxis regimens based on Caprini RAM score?
What are the comparative benefits and risks of preoperative versus postoperative initiation of chemoprophylaxis?
Does extended-duration outpatient chemoprophylaxis meaningfully reduce VTE rates?
Should weight-based dosing be incorporated into routine practice for optimizing chemoprophylaxis in high-risk patients?
Can oral anticoagulants such as apixaban offer a safe and effective alternative to low-molecular-weight heparin in this patient population?
We present this article in accordance with the Narrative Review reporting checklist (available at https://gs.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/gs-2025-242/rc).

## Methods
A comprehensive literature search was conducted to identify studies related to VTE risk, prevention, and chemoprophylaxis in patients undergoing abdominal-based breast reconstruction (see ). The search included English-language articles published between January 1995 and May 2025. Studies were identified through PubMed, Embase, and Cochrane Library databases using combinations of the following search terms: “venous thromboembolism”, “deep vein thrombosis”, “pulmonary embolism”, “breast reconstruction”, “autologous reconstruction”, “abdominal-based breast reconstruction”, “DIEP flap”, “chemoprophylaxis”, and “anticoagulation”. Priority was given to randomized controlled trials, cohort studies, meta-analyses, and systematic reviews. Additional references were identified by manually screening the bibliographies of included articles and relevant clinical guidelines. Studies that addressed non-abdominal-based flap reconstruction or were not focused on VTE prevention strategies were excluded. Both published and ahead-of-print studies were considered to ensure inclusion of the most up-to-date evidence.
 

## Epidemiology and incidence of VTE
VTE remains a leading cause of preventable morbidity and mortality in surgical patients, responsible for over 100,000 deaths annually in the United States ( ). Among patients presenting with symptomatic PE, there is a 10% chance of mortality within the first hour, and approximately 5% develop chronic right heart dysfunction ( , ). Even patients with isolated DVT are at risk for post thrombotic syndrome and a lifelong DVT recurrence rate of 3% per year ( ). Risk factors contributing to venous thromboembolic events include elevated body mass index (BMI), prolonged operative time, general anesthesia, history of malignancy, use of oral contraceptives or hormone replacement therapy, smoking, advanced age, and inherited thrombophilias ( , ). Women undergoing abdominal-based microsurgical breast reconstruction often possess multiple risk factors for VTE, including advanced age, history of malignancy, estrogen therapy, limited postoperative mobility, and prolonged operative duration ( , - ). While the overall incidence of VTE in plastic surgery inpatients is estimated at 1.69%, this masks substantial variability in risk ( ). Certain subpopulations—such as those undergoing free flap breast reconstruction—experience VTE rates as high as 2.2% to 3.4% ( , ).

## Preoperative optimization
Effective VTE prevention in abdominal-based breast reconstruction begins before the day of surgery. Preoperative optimization includes risk modification strategies and, in select cases, formal evaluation for underlying hypercoagulable states. Identifying and addressing modifiable risk factors—such as smoking cessation, weight loss, optimization of mobility, and management of hormone therapy—can meaningfully reduce thrombotic risk ( ). Patients with a personal or family history of VTE, recurrent pregnancy loss, or known thrombophilia warrant further investigation with a hypercoagulable workup ( , ). Laboratory testing may include screening for Factor V Leiden mutation, prothrombin gene mutation, protein C/S deficiency, antithrombin III deficiency, and antiphospholipid antibodies. In these high-risk individuals, referral to hematology is often appropriate to determine the need for extended prophylaxis. Studies by Zarb et al. and Sezgin et al. further underscore the importance of identifying and managing hypercoagulable conditions in this population. Zarb et al. demonstrated that microsurgical breast reconstruction can be performed safely in patients with primary hypercoagulable disorders when appropriate perioperative management is employed ( ). Sezgin et al. proposed an algorithmic approach to risk assessment and anticoagulation in hypercoagulable patients, reinforcing the need for individualized strategies and multidisciplinary coordination ( ).

## Risk stratification and chemoprophylaxis
Risk stratification forms the cornerstone of VTE prevention, particularly in high-risk surgical populations such as patients undergoing abdominal-based breast reconstruction. The 2005 Caprini RAM is a validated scoring system that has gained widespread use for this purpose ( ). It integrates various perioperative risk factors—including patient age, obesity, active cancer, mobility status, and personal or family history of thromboembolism—to stratify patients into risk categories. In plastic surgery patients, particularly those undergoing microsurgical breast reconstruction with abdominal-based flaps, Caprini scores typically range from 5 to 10, indicating a high-risk cohort. A 2011 study by Pannucci et al. validated the Caprini RAM in plastic surgery patients by analyzing outcomes in 1,126 individuals who underwent plastic surgery procedures under general anesthesia without chemoprophylaxis ( ). They found a clear, stepwise increase in VTE risk with higher Caprini scores: patients with a score >8 experienced an 11.3% incidence of postoperative VTE, and those in the 7–8 range also faced significantly elevated risk. The study validated the Caprini RAM as an effective tool for VTE risk stratification and underscored the need for continued focus on high-risk patients.
Recognizing the importance of standardized risk assessment in surgical patients, the ASPS convened a dedicated VTE Task Force in 2009, building upon the 2008 Surgeon General’s Call to Action to Prevent DVT and PE ( ). In 2012, the Task Force released plastic surgery-specific recommendations that reinforced the use of the Caprini RAM for risk stratification ( ). These guidelines recommend using the Caprini RAM for all plastic surgery inpatients receiving general anesthesia and suggest its application in outpatient settings. For patients with Caprini scores ≥7 undergoing elective procedures, general risk-reduction strategies such as minimizing operative time, early ambulation, weight loss, and cessation of hormone therapy are advised. In major surgeries lasting over 60 minutes—such as abdominoplasty, breast reconstruction, or extensive extremity and head and neck reconstruction—postoperative prophylaxis is tailored to risk level: mechanical prophylaxis for scores ≥3, low molecular weight heparin (LMWH) or unfractionated heparin (UFH) for scores of 3–6, and extended LMWH prophylaxis for scores ≥7.
However, more recent studies have updated recommendations for chemoprophylaxis based on Caprini RAM stratification. In a large meta-analysis, Pannucci et al. demonstrated that surgical patients with Caprini scores of 7 to 8 and greater than 8 experienced significant reductions in postoperative VTE risk with chemoprophylaxis, while those with scores ≤6—who comprise 75% of the surgical population—did not derive benefit ( ). Notably, this study was not specific to plastic surgery patients or to abdominal-based breast reconstruction patients. Another recent multicenter retrospective study, specific to plastic surgery population, also found that patients with Caprini scores of 7–8 and greater than 8 were found to have significant risk reduction of VTE with postoperative enoxaparin ( ). These more recent studies depart from the ASPS Task Force’s recommendation to administer chemoprophylaxis to patients with Caprini scores of 3–6, and instead emphasize its use in high-risk patients with scores greater than 7. This risk-based approach aims to maximize clinical benefit while reducing unnecessary bleeding risk in lower-risk individuals.
Building on these efforts to improve preoperative risk stratification, a 2014 study by Pannucci et al. developed and validated a simplified VTE risk model for surgical patients ( ). Unlike the Caprini RAM, which includes a broader and more complex array of factors, this seven-item model relies solely on preoperative variables and was designed to be practical for routine use. The included factors are current cancer, personal history of VTE, sepsis, age ≥60 years, BMI ≥40 kg/m2, male sex, and family history of VTE. Based on data from more than 10,000 patients, the model demonstrated strong predictive ability, with area under the receiver operator curve values of 0.72 and 0.70 in the derivation and validation cohorts, respectively. However, it is important to note that these risk stratification models and chemoprophylaxis protocols are not specific to abdominal-based breast reconstruction, but were developed for broader surgical or general plastic surgery populations. ## VTE mechanical and chemoprophylaxis strategies

## Mechanical devices
Recent consensus guidelines from the American Association of Plastic Surgeons (AAPS) recommend sequential compression devices (SCDs) over elastic stockings for two main reasons ( ). First, SCDs target two components of Virchow’s triad—stasis and hypercoagulability—by simulating the calf muscle pump to reduce venous stasis and by activating the body’s natural fibrinolytic pathways. In contrast, elastic compression stockings address only venous stasis by redirecting blood from superficial to deep veins. Second, a recent meta-analysis found that SCDs are more effective than elastic stockings in reducing VTE risk ( ). Given their dual mechanism of action and superior clinical efficacy, SCDs are considered the preferred modality for mechanical VTE prophylaxis in plastic surgery patients ( ).

## Timing of chemoprophylaxis initiation
The optimal timing of chemoprophylaxis in autologous breast reconstruction remains a subject of ongoing debate. While postoperative initiation is more common in plastic surgery, other surgical disciplines—such as gynecologic oncology and bariatric surgery—have adopted protocols that start anticoagulation preoperatively. Studies in these fields have demonstrated both the safety and efficacy of early administration ( , ). Notably, a meta-analysis by Leonardi et al. found that preoperative anticoagulation resulted in a threefold reduction in VTE among cancer patients undergoing non-orthopedic surgery ( ). These findings have prompted growing interest in preoperative strategies within plastic surgery, particularly given concerns about intraoperative venous stasis and early thrombus formation. Recent evidence supports the safety and potential benefits of preoperative chemoprophylaxis in microsurgical breast reconstruction. A study by Bassiri-Tehrani et al. compared preoperative versus postoperative enoxaparin (40 mg) in 196 patients and found a lower hematoma rate in the preoperative administration group (1.0% vs. 4.4%), with similar transfusion rates (2.9% vs. 4.4%) ( ). The authors suggest that administering LMWH preoperatively allows adequate time for intraoperative hemostasis while reducing the risk of delayed postoperative bleeding. Initiating anticoagulation prior to anesthesia induction mirrors established practices in other surgical fields, where preoperative prophylaxis has been shown to reduce thrombotic risk without increasing bleeding complications.

## Role of extended-duration chemoprophylaxis
Extended-duration VTE prophylaxis—defined as continuing chemoprophylaxis after discharge—is gaining traction in the microsurgical community. Momeni et al. conducted a prospective study evaluating lower extremity venous stasis in abdominal-based breast reconstruction patients ( ). Duplex ultrasound revealed persistent increases in femoral vein diameter (~18–19%) and area (~46%), along with decreased flow velocity (~11%) from postoperative day one through discharge. These findings suggest that inpatient-only prophylaxis may be inadequate. Similarly, a study by Rochlin et al. reviewed 12,778 microsurgical breast reconstruction patients and found that a majority of the VTE events (67.1%) occurred following discharge ( ).
 Recent studies support extended-duration chemoprophylaxis in high-risk patients, particularly those with BMI ≥30 kg/m2, active cancer, or prolonged operative time. A study by Pittelkow et al. compared patients receiving extended enoxaparin for 14 days post-discharge to those receiving inpatient-only prophylaxis ( ). Although not statistically significant, the VTE rate was lower in the extended group (1.5% vs. 2.8%), and there was no increase in bleeding. Tuaño et al. found that a standardized two-week postoperative enoxaparin protocol significantly reduced VTE incidence in DIEP flap breast reconstruction patients (0% vs. 3.7%, P=0.03) without increasing post-discharge hematoma risk ( ). Similarly, Graziano et al. demonstrated that abdominal-based breast reconstruction patients receiving extended prophylaxis within an enhanced recovery after surgery (ERAS) protocol maintained low VTE and hematoma rates, supporting the safety and potential benefit of these extended duration regimens ( ).
However, a recent systematic review and meta-analysis challenges this assumption ( ). It compared short-term (STC) versus long-term chemoprophylaxis (LTC) in 1,114 DIEP flap patients across four studies. Baseline characteristics, including Caprini scores, were similar between groups. VTE incidence was 2.19% in the STC group compared to 1.33% in the LTC group [risk ratio (RR) 1.42; P=0.62], and hematoma rates were 8.32% versus 9.02%, respectively (RR 0.78; P=0.25). The authors conclude that extended prophylaxis did not significantly reduce VTE risk and may not justify the potential for increased bleeding.
Many plastic surgeons remain hesitant to adopt extended regimens, citing the lack of prospective randomized trials. Nonetheless, in patients undergoing abdominal-based breast reconstruction, the persistent lower extremity venous stasis and population-based data showing that most VTE events occur after discharge support the rationale for extended prophylaxis in the outpatient setting. The balance between VTE prevention and bleeding risk continues to be a subject of ongoing debate, underscoring the need for further high-quality evidence to guide practice.

## Optimizing enoxaparin dosing: weight-based strategies
Growing evidence highlights the limitations of a fixed-dose approach to enoxaparin prophylaxis, particularly in surgical populations with wide variability in body weight and metabolism. In many cases, a standard 40 mg BID dose results in subtherapeutic or supratherapeutic anti-factor Xa levels, which correlates with reduced efficacy or increased bleeding, respectively. The FIxed or Variable Enoxaparin (FIVE) trial addressed this by comparing fixed versus weight-based dosing (0.5 mg/kg BID) ( ). Weight-based dosing demonstrated superior pharmacokinetic results: higher rates of target anti-factor Xa levels, fewer under- or over-anticoagulated patients, and no significant difference in bleeding or VTE incidence. However, while statistical significance was achieved for the trial’s primary endpoints—reflecting confidence in the pharmacodynamic findings—the study was not powered to detect differences in 90-day VTE or bleeding, which were not significantly different between groups. Similarly, a study by Bertolaccini et al. found that weight-based heparin infusions (10 units/kg/hour) combined with anti-factor Xa monitoring significantly improved time to therapeutic anticoagulation in microsurgical patients compared to fixed dosing (1.5 vs. 2.5 days, P=0.045) ( ). While only 20% reached target levels at the initial dose, 65% achieved adequate anticoagulation with dose adjustments. No VTEs occurred, and the 90-day bleeding rate was 15%.
These studies suggest the value of monitoring pharmacodynamics in high-risk populations. Implementing weight-based prophylaxis may improve VTE prevention while avoiding unnecessary bleeding. However, larger studies are needed to see clinical reduction in VTE or bleeding events. Further, some centers have explored real-time anti-factor Xa monitoring as a tool to adjust dosing perioperatively, though logistical barriers and cost limit widespread adoption.

## Enoxaparin vs. apixaban for VTE prophylaxis
Although enoxaparin is commonly used for chemoprophylaxis in abdominal-based breast reconstruction, it has several limitations, including the potential for weight-based dosing, monitoring of anti-factor Xa levels, and administration via subcutaneous injection—all of which may hinder optimal dosing, duration, and patient adherence. In contrast, apixaban offers practical advantages as a fixed-dose oral agent that does not require monitoring, improving ease of use for both patients and providers. A randomized controlled trial by Momeni et al. evaluated the safety and efficacy of apixaban versus enoxaparin for VTE chemoprophylaxis in high-risk patients undergoing autologous breast reconstruction ( ). Seventy-nine patients with Caprini scores ≥7 were randomized to receive either fixed-dose oral apixaban or subcutaneous enoxaparin postoperatively. Both groups had similar baseline characteristics, and the incidence of bleeding events was similar (3% in each group). No symptomatic VTE events were reported in either arm. This study demonstrated that apixaban is a safe and well-tolerated alternative to enoxaparin, with comparable outcomes, representing a promising paradigm shift in VTE prophylaxis for abdominal-based breast reconstruction patients.

## Safety considerations: bleeding and flap loss
Safety remains a concern when implementing VTE chemoprophylaxis. Critics of preoperative or extended chemoprophylaxis cite risks of hematoma, flap loss, and the potential need for reoperation. However, evidence continues to accumulate that these risks are not significantly elevated when enoxaparin is appropriately dosed and timed. Graziano et al. found that flap loss was reduced in patients who received preoperative enoxaparin along with an extended postoperative course, as part of an ERAS protocol ( ). The rate decreased from 2.1% in the pre-ERAS cohort to 0.7% in the ERAS group. Hematoma and reoperation rates remained similar, suggesting that preoperative and extended chemoprophylaxis did not significantly compromise reconstructive outcomes. Bassiri-Tehrani et al. compared pre- and postoperative enoxaparin administration in autologous breast reconstruction patients and found a reduction in hematoma and transfusion rates with preoperative dosing ( ). Similarly, a study by Kroll et al. found that low-dose heparin does not increase the risk of hematomas in free flap reconstruction, while also noting no relationship between anticoagulation and flap loss ( ).
A multicenter retrospective cohort study by Pannucci et al. found that there were no clinically or statistically significant differences in 60-day hematoma rates between patients who received enoxaparin and those who did not. However, this study identified breast surgery and microsurgical procedures as independent predictors of re-operative hematoma ( ). The authors suggest this finding may reflect surgeons’ tendency to re-explore out of concern for a compromised vascular pedicle rather than for a progressively enlarging hematoma. It is important to note that the ‘microsurgery’ cohort in this study includes a wide range of procedures—such as head and neck and extremity reconstruction—which limits the applicability of these findings to abdominal-based breast reconstruction.

## Moving beyond the Caprini RAM
While the 2005 Caprini RAM remains a valuable tool for assessing VTE risk in surgery patients, it should serve as a starting point—not a standalone determinant—for anticoagulation decisions. Surgeons should use the score to tailor chemoprophylaxis based on individual risk profiles, integrating clinical judgment. Several relevant patient and procedural risk factors fall outside the scope of the Caprini RAM. For example, these include the type of anesthesia, operative duration, degree of obesity, and procedure characteristics (e.g., plication in abdominal-based breast reconstruction). Therefore, while stratification can be an important part of the chemoprophylaxis decision-making process, it should not completely replace surgeon judgement.

## Our institutional chemoprophylaxis algorithm
All patients at our institution, regardless of attending surgeon, undergoing breast reconstruction with abdominal-based free flaps received enoxaparin 40 mg daily, with the first dose administered preoperatively. Postoperatively, patients with a BMI <30 kg/m2 received a total of 7 days of anticoagulation, while those with BMI >30 kg/m2 received a total of 30 days. Patients were treated with enoxaparin during their hospital stay and were discharged on oral rivaroxaban 10 mg daily to complete the designated anticoagulation course. Our chemoprophylaxis algorithm addresses several key contributors to VTE risk. Given the established association between obesity and VTE, patients with higher BMI receive extended anticoagulation ( , ). As most VTE events occur after discharge, prophylaxis is designed to extend beyond the typical inpatient stay (1 day for unilateral and 2 days for bilateral DIEP flaps) ( , ). Unlike traditional plastic surgery protocols that begin anticoagulation postoperatively, ours initiates enoxaparin preoperatively, aligning with evidence from gynecologic oncology and bariatric surgery showing reduced VTE risk with preoperative dosing ( , ). Enoxaparin was selected over UFH based on data demonstrating superior efficacy in VTE prevention ( , ).
Although many studies advocate for individualized risk stratification using tools such as the Caprini RAM, applying such models to every patient can be time-consuming and impractical in real-world practice. As a result, our institution has adopted a streamlined, protocolized approach. Importantly, we have previously demonstrated that this approach results in low rates of both VTE and bleeding complications in our abdominal-based breast reconstruction population ( ). Until large, well-powered prospective or randomized controlled trials specific to this surgical cohort are available, we continue to support the use of our chemoprophylaxis algorithm.

## Future directions
Despite improved protocols, significant gaps remain in VTE prevention for abdominal-based breast reconstruction. Large, prospective, multicenter trials are needed to define optimal chemoprophylaxis regimens for abdominal-based breast reconstruction, in order to move beyond data extrapolated from other populations. Research should continue to explore the use of novel oral anticoagulants, such as apixaban, which may improve adherence and ease of use post-discharge. Lastly, the Caprini RAM lacks specificity in the abdominal-based breast reconstruction population, and future models incorporating machine learning and intraoperative data could enhance accuracy and guide tailored chemoprophylaxis.

## Conclusions
VTE is a significant but preventable risk in patients undergoing abdominal-based breast reconstruction. As this review highlights, many of these patients fall into high-risk categories based on validated tools such as the 2005 Caprini RAM. While the majority of studies support the use of sequential compression devices, individualized risk stratification, and chemoprophylaxis for high-risk patients (Caprini score ≥7), optimal chemoprophylaxis strategies remain a subject of debate. Questions persist regarding the timing (preoperative vs. postoperative administration) and duration (inpatient vs. extended) of chemoprophylaxis. Despite concerns, the use of chemoprophylaxis has not been strongly associated with increased rates of complications such as hematoma or flap loss in abdominal-based breast reconstruction populations. Institutional variability in VTE prevention strategies underscores the need for standardized, evidence-based guidelines in abdominal-based breast reconstruction. Moving forward, multicenter prospective studies are critical to define the optimal chemoprophylaxis regimen, timing, and duration.
