## Abstract
Thromboembolic diseases have long been a leading cause of morbidity and mortality, necessitating advances in anticoagulant drugs. Heparins, vitamin K inhibitors, and direct oral anticoagulants (DOACs) are well-established drug classes that help prevent thromboembolic complications. While effective, they pose significant risks during long-term therapy, including bleeding, osteoporosis, heparin-induced thrombocytopenia, and the need for frequent monitoring and dose adjustments. Factor XI (FXI) inhibitors represent an innovative approach in anticoagulation therapy, aiming to balance thromboembolic events with the risk of bleeding complications. They include: a) orally administered small molecule inhibitors such as milvexian and asundexian; b) monoclonal antibodies such as abelacimab, osocimab, and xisomab, which specifically bind and inactivate FXI; c) FXI-antisense oligonucleotide (FXI-ASO), which downregulate FXI synthesis at the mRNA level and reduce plasma FXI concentrations. Available data indicate that FXI inhibitors decrease the risk of thromboembolic events and are associated with a lower incidence of major bleeding than current gold standard methods. Hence, FXI inhibitors may become the preferred anticoagulant class, especially for patients with elevated bleeding risk. Their development is an important step in the history of anticoagulant therapy, striving to find a balance between preventing thromboembolism and reducing bleeding risk, ultimately improving patient outcomes. In this context, a discussion on the characteristics of FXI inhibitors, a summary on data regarding the efficacy and safety of FXI inhibitors based on preclinical and clinical studies, and an outline of future perspectives regarding therapeutic strategies of FXI inhibition in venous thrombosis are presented in this study.
Keywords: factor XI, anticoagulants, embolism, thrombosis, hemostasis

## Introduction
Thromboembolic conditions remain a significant global health concern, contributing to approximately one-quarter of all deaths worldwide in 2010 and persistently being one of the leading causes of mortality [ ]. Venous thromboembolism (VTE) affects a substantial number of individuals annually, with an estimated incidence of 7–14 million cases worldwide. Similarly, arterial thrombosis (AT) exerts a profound impact on public health, accounting for more than 10 million deaths annually.
Thrombosis involves the formation of an obstructive blood clot, known as a thrombus, within a blood vessel, thereby impeding the normal flow of blood throughout the circulatory system [ ]. It may occur in veins, resulting in VTE, mostly manifesting as deep vein thrombosis (DVT) and pulmonary embolism (PE), or in AT, comprising mainly ischemic heart disease and ischemic stroke [ ]. The mechanisms driving these conditions differ: platelet activation and aggregation on atherosclerotic plaques are the main mechanisms of AT formation. Hence, the current standard of care for AT treatment includes three classes of antiplatelet agents: acetylsalicylic acid (ASA), P2Y12 inhibitors, and glycoprotein IIb-IIIa antagonists [ ]. However, VTE is a common condition with pathophysiology explained by Virchow’s triad, including blood stasis, hypercoagulability, and endothelial injury, necessitating anticoagulant therapy rather than antiplatelet treatment [ ]. While antiplatelet and anticoagulation therapies effectively prevent AT and VTE, respectively, their use is inevitably associated with an elevated risk of bleeding complications, such as intracranial, gastrointestinal, and retroperitoneal bleeding [ ]. Bleeding complications often lead to antiplatelet and anticoagulation therapy or the inappropriate use of low-dose treatment regimens [ ]. Therefore, this highlights an urgent need for novel therapeutic approaches with a lower risk of bleeding.
 In this context, recent reports documenting the successful use of coagulation FXI inhibitors in clinical practice have yielded potential clinical significance. Evidence suggests that FXI inhibitors may be more effective than the hitherto administered antiplatelet and anticoagulation agents for preventing and treating both AT and VTE, resulting in less bleeding than conventional anticoagulants [ ].
Currently, various types of FXI inhibitors have been developed, including i) orally administered small molecule inhibitors (milvexian, asundexian), ii) monoclonal antibodies (abelacimab, osocimab, xisomab), iii) FXI-antisense oligonucleotide, and iv) natural inhibitors (isolated from living organisms such as bats and snakes) [ , ]. Thus, this study presents the history of coagulation factors inhibitors development, discusses the characteristics of FXI inhibitors, summarizes data regarding the efficacy and safety of FXI inhibitors based on preclinical and clinical studies, and outlines future perspectives regarding therapeutic strategies of FXI inhibition in arterial and venous thrombosis.

## Role of coagulation FXI in coagulation cascade
Factor XI is a vital component of the coagulation cascade, initially characterized in 1953 by Rosenthal et al. at the Beth Israel Hospital in New York [ ]. Also known as plasma thromboplastin, FXI is a liver zymogen, i.e., an inactive form of FXI [ ]. It is composed of two identical 80-kDa polypeptide homodimers connected by a disulfide bond. It consists of four homologous domains at the N terminus and a serine protease at the C terminus [ ]. The conversion of FXI to its activated form, factor XIa (FXIa), is mediated by factor XIIa ( ). Factor XIa activates factor IX and then factor X, which is a common point of the intrinsic and extrinsic coagulation pathways [ ].
 Factor XII activation occurs, among others, after exposure to negative charge accumulation on medical devices without a layer of endothelial cells serving as protection, including mechanical heart valves, vascular grafts, stents, catheters, and dialysis circuits [ ]. Factor XI plays a significant role in the intrinsic coagulation pathway, where it amplifies thrombin-forming pathological clots. Following vascular injury or plaque rupture, initial thrombin activated by the extrinsic pathway is insufficient to sustain thrombosis. Once activated, FXI boosts thrombin production through feedback activation, strengthening and propagating the clot. This amplification by FXI is essential in thrombosis but less critical in normal hemostasis, which relies primarily on the extrinsic pathway’s initial thrombin generation [ ].
Human FXI is encoded by the F11 gene located on the long arm of chromosome 4, comprising 15 exons [ ]. Mutation in this gene results in either quantitative deficiency or a qualitative abnormality of FXI, leading to the development of hemophilia C, also known as Rosenthal syndrome [ ] ( ). It is an autosomal coagulation disorder mostly inherited as a recessive trait, although some cases of dominant transmission have been documented [ ]. Notably, the disease exhibits a predilection for the Ashkenazi Jewish population, with a disease frequency approaching 5%. In this population, four distinct mutations within the F11 gene are predominantly related to FXI deficiency [ ]. It has been observed that patients with congenital FXI deficiency have a reduced incidence of VTE and ischemic stroke compared to those with sufficient levels of properly structured FXI. Unlike hemophilia A and B, hemophilia C is associated with the relatively infrequent occurrence of spontaneous bleeding events [ ]. Experimental data suggests that severe FXI deficiency protects against arterial and venous thrombosis without compromising hemostasis in animal models. Research has also shown that the incidence of DVT is significantly lower in a group of patients with severe FXI deficiency compared to the general population [ ].
Factor XI inhibitors differ in their mechanism of action. Orally administered small-molecule inhibitors (milvexian and asundexian) inhibit FXI’s active form. Monoclonal antibodies (abelacimab, osocimab, and xisomab) specifically bind and inactivate FXI or FXIa. Furthermore, FXI-ASO downregulates FXI synthesis at the mRNA level and reduces plasma FXI concentrations. Natural inhibitors, derived from nematodes, snakes, bats, and ticks, are serine proteases that inactivate FXIa (e.g., Ixodes ricinus contact phase inhibitor – the only dual FXIIa and FXIa inhibitor in a clinical phase of investigation) [ , ]. The four currently available classes, along with their corresponding mechanisms of action, are summarized in the . The fifth class of FXI inhibitors, aptamers, which are single-stranded DNA or RNA sequences that act as noncompetitive inhibitors of FXI, FXIa, or both, are currently in the preclinical development phase [ ].

## Milvexian
Milvexian is a reversible FXIa inhibitor [ ]. Along with asundexian, it belongs to the chemically synthesized small-molecule inhibitors. Concerning its chemical construction, biotechnological changes in milvexian’s structure improved its characteristics by reducing plasma protein binding. Milvexian contains a pyrazole P2′ moiety with a half-life ranging from 8.3 to 13.8 h [ ]. The P2′ moiety is especially interesting in terms of the anticoagulants’ structure, as it has proven to be a better substitute than the previously used series of anticoagulants consisting of a methyl phenyl carbamate P2′ [ ]. Milvexian distinguishes itself from other FXI inhibitors by its high affinity for FXIa [ ] and its oral route of administration, which is possible only for small molecules. Preclinical studies have demonstrated the antithrombotic activity of milvexian, administered along with aspirin, without disrupting hemostasis in animal models of arterial and venous thrombosis [ , , ].
Given that small molecules are metabolized by renal excretion or CYP450 enzymes in the liver, earlier studies focused on their safety in patients with liver or renal impairment. In a preliminary study involving patients with mild (Child-Pugh class A; n = 9) and moderate (Child-Pugh class B; n = 8) hepatic impairment, milvexian was safe and well-tolerated [ ]. Similarly, a single dose of milvexian 60 mg was safe and well-tolerated in participants with normal renal function, as well as with moderate and severe renal impairment (eGFR < 30 mL/min/1.73 m2), despite an increase in milvexian exposure in the latter group [ ]. These results suggest that dosage reduction may not be necessary in patients with liver or renal impairment, though further studies are required to confirm these preliminary observations.
Administration of milvexian alone or combined with aspirin, clopidogrel, or both in healthy volunteers was safe and well-tolerated without an increased risk of bleeding (NCT03698513) [ ]. The first phase 2, a randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled, dose-finding trial focused on the use of milvexian added to dual antiplatelet therapy (DAPT) in secondary stroke prevention (NCT03766581) and VTE prevention after total knee arthroplasty (NCT03891524), confirming its safety [ , ].
The ongoing LIBREXIA study focuses on the evaluation of orally administered milvexian in three indications: ischemic stroke (LIBREXIA STROKE), acute coronary syndrome (LIBREXIA ACS), and atrial fibrillation (LIBREXIA AF). LIBREXIA STROKE (NCT05702034) is a randomized, double-blind, parallel-group, placebo-controlled study. It aims to present the safety and efficacy of milvexian in addition to single antiplatelet therapy (SAPT) or DAPT for stroke prevention after an acute ischemic stroke or high-risk transient ischemic attack (TIA). LIBREXIA ACS (NCT05754957) is a randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled study aiming to investigate the safety and efficacy of milvexian, administered twice daily, after a recent ACS in patients’ SAPT or DAPT. LIBREXIA AF (NCT05757869) is a randomized, double-blind, double-dummy, parallel-group, active-controlled study designed to determine the safety and efficacy of milvexian compared to apixaban in patients with AF. Notably, the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) provided the LIBREXIA program with a Fast Track Designation, proving the importance of milvexian research.
Currently, milvexian has not yet received approval for usage in any indication. The ongoing trials will provide evidence of whether milvexian-based management strategies are safe and efficient for patients with numerous thromboembolic disorders, including ACS, AF, and recurrent stroke.

## Asundexian
Asundexian is a novel, oral, particularly bioavailable small molecule that inhibits FXIa [ ]. It has a mean half-life of 14 to 17 h. Research has shown that Asundexian reduces thrombus formation without concomitant prolongation of bleeding time in vitro and in animal models [ ]. It did not increase bleeding time or blood loss, whether administered alone or in combination with DAPT (aspirin and ticagrelor) after intravenous or oral administration [ ].
Asundexian prompts new hopes in patients suffering from AF or acute myocardial infarction (AMI) due to its ability to lower the risk of bleeding compared to standard DOAC therapy [ ]. In the PACIFIC-AF study (NCT04218266), asundexian was compared to apixaban for stroke prevention and safety in patients with AF. Asundexian at doses of 20 mg and 50 mg once daily was associated with over 50% reduction in bleeding compared to standard doses of apixaban. Patients treated with asundexian had lower bleeding risks compared to those treated with apixaban (hazard risk [HR] 0.42, 95% confidence interval [CI] 0.26–0.67) [ ]. The tolerability of asundexian was comparable to that of apixaban.
The double-blind, placebo-controlled, phase 2, dose-ranging PACIFIC-AMI (NCT04304534) trial assessed the pharmacodynamics, safety, and efficacy of asundexian (10 mg, 20 mg, and 50 mg) in conjunction with DAPT, compared to DAPT only in patients post-AMI for 6 to 12 months [ ]. Asundexian resulted in the effective inhibition of FXI activity without an increase in bleeding events. Surprisingly, patients not treated with asundexian had higher bleeding risk compared to the groups receiving asundexian (10 mg or 20 mg). However, it should be acknowledged that many patients were excluded from the study due to their elevated bleeding risk, highlighting the need for further investigation to substantiate asundexian’s efficacy and safety.
Asundexian was also evaluated in the OCEANIC program, which included OCEANIC-AF (NCT05643573), OCEANIC-STROKE (NCT05686070), and OCEANIC-AFINA trials. OCEANIC-AF was a multicentre, international, randomized, double-blind, active comparator-controlled trial, which included 14,810 patients observed for a median of 155 days. In this study, the efficacy and tolerability of asundexian (20 mg or 50 mg orally once daily) were compared to apixaban (5 mg or 2.5 mg orally twice a day) in patients with AF at risk for stroke. Despite obtaining favorable results of asundexian in phase 2 of the OCEANIC-AF trial [ ], the phase 3 trial was discontinued after less than 6 months based on the Independent Data Monitoring Committee’s recommendation due to asundexian’s inferior antithrombotic efficacy for stroke prevention compared to apixaban. Patients treated with asundexian had a 3.8-fold higher incidence of systemic embolism or stroke (1.3% vs. 0.4%), although the risk of major bleeding was lower (0.2% vs. 0.7%) [ , ]. The inferior antithrombotic efficacy of asundexian in the phase 3 study might reflect the inadequate pharmacodynamic activity of the studied dose. Thus, it remains unclear whether FXIa inhibition is a reasonable alternative to current oral anticoagulant options in atrial fibrillation. Considering the reduced incidence of bleeding events in patients treated with asundexian and in individuals with FXI deficiency, it might be a viable option for patients at low thromboembolic but high bleeding risk. However, further investigation into novel therapies acting on the XIa inhibition pathway is needed before firm conclusions can be made [ , ].
OCEANIC-STROKE is a multicentre, international, randomized, placebo-controlled, double-blind trial that will include 12,300 patients observed for up to 31 months. It investigates the efficacy and safety of asundexian as an addition to standard therapy (aspirin alone or in combination with clopidogrel) for the prevention of ischemic stroke in patients who were recently diagnosed with a non-cardioembolic ischemic stroke or a temporary high-risk TIA [ ]. The findings of phase 3 are expected to be published in 2025.
OCEANIC-AFINA is a multicentre, international, randomized, placebo-controlled, double-blind trial that will compare asundexian vs. placebo in patients with AF over 65 years of age and at high risk for stroke or systemic embolism. In contrast to other studies, patients recruited into the study group are deemed ineligible for currently used oral anticoagulant treatment. Phase 3 of this study has not yet started [ ].

## Abelacimab
Abelacimab is a highly selective, human monoclonal antibody that binds to FXI and renders it in the non-active precursor form [ ]. Monoclonal antibodies are catabolized by phagocytic cells, such as macrophages and monocytes, eliminating the participation of the kidney or liver [ ]. This characteristic may be an important factor in further research on the usage of monoclonal antibodies in patients with renal or hepatic impairment. The anticoagulant effect of abelacimab was observed in mice in 2005 [ ]. In animal models, abelacimab demonstrated superior efficacy compared to DOACs. In contrast to FXa or thrombin inhibitors, a reversal agent might not be needed, although it might still be desired to mitigate any residual bleeding risk [ ].
Current trials focus on the potential utility of subcutaneous (s.c.) administration of abelacimab to prevent VTE in patients undergoing total knee arthroplasty. FXIa may be involved not only in the development of thrombosis but also in its initiation. In the context of postoperative VTE, inhibiting FXI holds potential benefits for patients undergoing orthopedic surgeries. Enoxaparin is a widely used agent for thromboprophylaxis following lower extremity orthopedic procedures despite the inherent risk of bleeding and associated side effects. A recent prospective, randomized, parallel-group trial indicated that abelacimab was associated with a dose-dependent risk reduction of postoperative VTE without an increase in bleeding complications after knee arthroplasty compared with enoxaparin. In this trial, patients were randomly assigned to one of four groups (98 to 102 patients in each group) receiving abelacimab (30 mg, 75 mg, or 150 mg) or enoxaparin (40 mg). Abelacimab was administered in a single intravenous infusion over 30 to 60 minutes, starting 4 to 8 hours postoperatively [ ]. A 30 mg dose of abelacimab administered postoperatively was non-inferior to enoxaparin, and doses of 75 mg and 150 mg of abelacimab were more efficient in preventing postoperative VTE than 40 mg enoxaparin. Moreover, abelacimab exhibited a more favorable risk-benefit profile when compared to enoxaparin. However, other researchers highlighted certain limitations associated with this study, including its relatively small sample size (a total of 412 patients) and the open-label design [ ]. In the AZALEA Trial (NCT05712200), 1,287 patients were assigned to a regimen of abelacimab 150 mg s.c. monthly, abelacimab 90 mg s.c. monthly, or rivaroxaban 20 mg p.o. daily. Abelacimab, at both doses, was superior to rivaroxaban, causing substantial reductions in both major and clinically relevant non-major bleeding. These exceptional results led to the premature termination of the trial.
The ongoing studies on abelacimab focus on patients with AF (TIMI trial) and cancer-related VTE (ASTER and MAGNOLIA trials). These and further studies are required to establish the efficacy and safety of abelacimab as monotherapy or in combination with other drugs.

## Osocimab
Osocimab is another human monoclonal antibody inhibiting FXI. In the phase 2 randomized FOXTROT trial, which compared different dose regimens of osocimab, enoxaparin, and apixaban in 813 patients undergoing knee arthroplasty, single postoperative intravenous administration of osocimab (0.6 mg/kg, 1.2 mg/kg, and 1.8 mg/kg) was non-inferior compared with enoxaparin. In contrast, the preoperative dose of 1.8 mg/kg was superior to enoxaparin in preventing VTE 10 to 13 days postoperation [ ]. Further studies are needed to establish the efficacy and safety of osocimab relative to standard thromboprophylaxis.

## Xisomab
Xisomab, also known as gruticibart (AB023), is a recombinant, humanized monoclonal anti-FXI antibody [ ]. It mostly binds FXI, preventing its activation by XIIa. Clinical trials on xisomab are now in progress, including one that aims to provide information on catheter-associated thrombosis (CAT) in ambulatory cancer patients receiving chemotherapy (NCT04465760). Preliminary data showed that administration of xisomab over 1 minute through a central line was well-tolerated [ ]. During the study, CAT occurred in 1 out of 11 patients in the xisomab study and 4 out of 11 patients in the parallel, noninterventional study used as a comparator. Xisomab alleviated the increase in C-reactive protein level following central line placement (7.3 ± 2.1 μg/mL before central line placement and 6.5 ± 1.7 μg/mL at the time of follow-up visit) compared to the noninterventional study. The latter is particularly important when administering immunoglobulins due to the possibility of hypersensitivity reactions, including anaphylactic shock. However, only 22 patients were enrolled in the study, which is a major limitation. Overall, data on the efficacy, tolerability, or safety of xisomab are very limited, highlighting the need for further research.

## FXI-ASO
FXI-second-generation ASO is another promising FXI inhibitor. The principle of action of FXI-ASOs is their specific binding in the liver to messenger RNA (mRNA) for FXI. This interaction results in the formation of non-physiological DNA/RNA double strands, which leads to the degradation of the mRNA that comprises them. Consequently, transcription and translation processes are disrupted, gene expression for FXI is downregulated, and the synthesis of FXI in the liver is reduced [ , ]. FXI-ASO exhibits a delayed onset of action, with its full efficacy manifesting up to a month following subcutaneous administration. It also prolongs activated partial thromboplastin time (aPTT) while demonstrating no effect on prothrombin time (PT) or international normalized ratio (INR) [ ]. In pursuit of a novel approach to address bleeding complications following total knee arthroplasty, Büller et al. conducted a study investigating the efficacy of FXI-ASO, comparing two different doses of the inhibitor (200 mg and 300 mg) to a single dose of enoxaparin [ ]. Subcutaneous administration of FXI-ASO was initiated 36 days prior to surgery and continued for up to three days postoperatively, confirming that FXI-ASO reduces the VTE risk without a concomitant increase in bleeding risk. While FXI-ASO may not be suitable for emergencies due to the delayed mode of action, it may be beneficial in chronic thrombotic conditions. Nevertheless, there is a need for a deeper understanding of FXI-ASO before its adoption in clinical practice. Admittedly, while the safety of FXI-ASO is promising, a further comparison of efficacy with contemporary anticoagulation regimens is necessary.

## Concerns and future perspectives
Increasing research indicates a significant need for novel approaches to thrombosis prevention. As such, new strategies for balancing the risk of bleeding and thrombotic events are the subject of extensive research. Although FXI inhibitors remain in the phase of clinical trials and are not yet available in routine clinical practice, their promising features might change the future landscape of anticoagulation therapy. Asundexian, milvexian, and abelacimab, which specifically inhibit FXI, have advanced the field and offered new treatment options for thrombosis in patients with various conditions, including VTE prevention after knee arthroplasty [ , , ], AF [ ] cancer-associated VTE [ ], AMI [ ] and secondary stroke prevention [ ]. Further developments are still needed to fully realize the clinical potential of FXI inhibitors. The use of FXI inhibitors has limitations, including a delayed onset of action, the need for parental administration [ ], and the extended elimination time of some of them. For example, FXI-ASO implies sustained inhibition of FXIa for up to a month following the cessation of drug administration, potentially impacting thromboembolic events and bleeding homeostasis [ ]. Future research should focus on improving the routes of administration and pharmacokinetic profiles of FXI inhibitors. For example, finding ways to administer the drugs orally or through non-invasive methods could improve patient compliance and make them more accessible to a broader population. Moreover, the cost of these agents should be reduced, as it plays a significant role in the probability of widespread implementation, considering the low cost and high availability of the already established antithrombotic medications. Future research should also focus on the combination of FXI inhibitors with other antithrombotic therapies, which could potentially reduce the risk of thrombosis and improve treatment outcomes. It is also important to consider the use of FXI inhibitors in different patient populations, particularly those with renal impairment [ , ], or liver dysfunction [ , ].
There are also concerns regarding the potential risks and limitations of FXI inhibitors. For example, individuals with systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) or hemophilia demonstrate increased vulnerability to FXI inhibition [ , ]. The activity of SLE appears to incite the generation of antibodies specifically targeting FXI [ ]. This may affect the clotting cascade, resulting in coagulopathies and bleeding events. As for hemophilia, although the majority of cases are known to be idiopathic (50%), it can also be associated with autoimmune diseases [ ]. In such instances, the presence of antibodies against clotting factors interferes with the intrinsic pathway of coagulation, which may lead to life-threatening bleeding.
In conclusion, the development of FXI inhibitors represents a promising area of research that can improve the management of thrombotic disorders. While further studies are needed to fully realize the clinical potential of these agents, the existing data, at the time of publication of this paper, is encouraging and might offer insights into future therapeutic approaches.
