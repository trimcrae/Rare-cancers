## Abstract
The COVID-19 pandemic led to millions of deaths worldwide after its emergence in 2020. The SARS-CoV-2 virus primarily affects respiratory function, but immune dysregulation leading to systemic inflammation, endothelial dysfunction, and coagulopathy can predispose to systemic complications including hematologic and vascular complications. Treatment strategies for patients with COVID-19 have rapidly evolved and the effectiveness and safety of antithrombotic agents have been evaluated in multiple clinical trials. The findings have spurred interest in the prevention and treatment of the hematologic and vascular complications of non-COVID-19 respiratory infections. This review is focused on hematological and vascular complications of COVID-19, including their pathophysiology, clinical manifestations, and management. Because of the perpetually changing nature of the disease, the review places previous data in temporal contexts and outlines potential next steps for future research in COVID-19 and other severe respiratory infections.

## Résumé
La pandémie de COVID-19 a entraîné des millions de décès dans le monde après son apparition en 2020. Le virus SRAS-CoV-2 qui en est responsable affecte principalement la fonction respiratoire, mais des dérèglements immunitaires menant à une inflammation généralisée, à une dysfonction endothéliale et à une coagulopathie peuvent prédisposer certaines personnes à des complications systémiques, notamment des complications hématologiques et vasculaires. Les stratégies de traitement pour les patients atteints de la COVID-19 ont évolué rapidement, et de nombreux essais cliniques ont porté sur l’efficacité et l’innocuité des agents antithrombotiques. Les conclusions ont suscité un intérêt envers la prévention et le traitement des complications hématologiques et vasculaires attribuables aux infections respiratoires autres que la COVID-19. Cette synthèse relève les complications hématologiques et vasculaires de la COVID-19, de même que leur physiopathologie, leurs manifestations cliniques et leur prise en charge. En raison de l’évolution continue de la maladie, les données précédentes sont placées dans leur contexte temporel, et des axes de recherche sur la COVID-19 et d’autres infections respiratoires graves sont proposés.

Since the emergence of the COVID-19 pandemic in March 2020, the SARS-CoV-2 virus has contributed to millions of deaths globally. Although SARS-CoV-2 gains entry to the host through the respiratory system, the clinical manifestations of COVID-19 can be systemic and can include a range of hematological and vascular complications. Over the past 3 years, an understanding of these mechanisms of disease and their treatment strategies has rapidly evolved. This review is focused on the pathophysiological basis for the hematological and vascular complications of COVID-19 and their management. An important preamble to this review is that although we attempt to summarize the best available understanding of the vascular complications of the disease, COVID-19 has also evolved over the past several years, and there is an incomplete understanding of the potentially changing nature of its pathophysiology and treatment in view of these temporal trends. In this article we highlight the temporal context for available data, but readers should bear such potential temporal changes in mind when considering the available evidence. Finally, this review offers forward-looking perspective on how the learnings from COVID-19 might affect care of patients with other, non-COVID-19 respiratory infections.

## Hematologic Derangements and Vascular Pathophysiology
Endothelial dysfunction contributes to the pathogenesis and clinical course of COVID-19. 
,
 COVID-19 manifests as a spectrum of phenotypes and illness severity, which might reflect the degree of endothelial injury, immune activation, and coagulopathy. 
,
 SARS-CoV-2 gains entry to human cells via interaction of its spike protein with the angiotensin-converting enzyme 2 receptor, which is abundantly expressed in the respiratory epithelium and vascular endothelium, as well as in the abdominal adipose tissue and many other organs. 
,
 
,
 
,
 Diverse pathways contribute to the host response to SARS-CoV-2, including macrophage and complement system activation, tissue factor expression, and leucocyte recruitment driven by adhesion molecules and chemokines. 
,
 
,
 

Endothelial cells exposed to inflammatory stimuli might inadequately regulate hemostasis. Disruption in vascular integrity leads to excessive permeability and vascular tone with consequent vascular leakage and vasoconstriction that induce ischemia, apoptosis, and end organ damage. 
,
 
,
 Amplification loop of the inflammatory response with overproduction of cytokines and immune mediators, including interleukin-1 and -6, and tumour necrosis factor α, can contribute to excessive immune activation and cytokine storm. The maladapted immune response propagates systemic inflammation and endothelial dysfunction in multiple vascular beds and adversely affects diverse organ systems including the kidneys, brain, heart, and intestines. 
,
 
,
 

At the time of endothelial injury, exposure of a thrombogenic basement membrane can trigger coagulation pathways with the potential development of disseminated intravascular coagulation. 
,
 Activation of the clotting cascade involves recruitment of platelets and release of trophic cytokines and procoagulant mediators, which are further enhanced by the expression of adhesion proteins (including P-selectin, von Willebrand factor, and fibrinogen), increase in tissue factor activity, and suppression of fibrinolysis and thrombomodulin. 
,
 
,
 Downregulation of angiotensin-converting enzyme 2 activity and related accumulation of angiotensin-II and marked reduction in pericytes through apoptosis of infected cells might also aggravate this thromboinflammatory process. 
,
 
,
 
,
 
,
 As a result, elevated levels of inflammatory markers and hematological derangements such as thrombocytopenia and high levels of D-dimer and other fibrin breakdown products were observed early in the pandemic to correlate with greater mortality risk and adverse outcomes. 
,
 
,
 
,
 

Patients are at high risk of developing deep vein thrombosis (DVT), pulmonary embolism (PE), myocardial infarction, and ischemic stroke. 
,
 Interestingly, as in sepsis, 
,
 this excess hazard might persist even after hospital discharge. Apart from direct cardiac injury and metabolic supply-demand mismatch risking type II myocardial infarction, patients are also at increased risk of type I myocardial infarction—as in other pneumonia syndromes. Inciting mechanisms include atherosclerotic plaque destabilization triggered by circulating cytokines, reactive oxygen species, intraplaque inflammatory cells (eg, metalloproteinases and peptidases), and superimposed coronary thrombosis owing to the prothrombotic state and production of neutrophil extracellular traps. Microvascular thrombosis and small-vessel vasculitis in several organs have also been reported in autopsy studies of patients infected with SARS-CoV-2.

## Arterial and Venous Thrombosis
Since the initial waves of the COVID-19 pandemic, studies have shown an increased risk of venous thromboembolism (VTE), which was most evident in patients with severe disease and patients with additional risk factors for VTE. 
,
 VTE diagnosis and elevated D-dimer levels during acute COVID-19 correlate with disease severity and prognosis. Furthermore, patients with severe COVID-19 remained at high risk for VTE even when prophylactic anticoagulation was administered. In a large meta-analysis published in 2020, the overall estimated prevalence of VTE during acute COVID-19 infection was 14.1%, with greater prevalence in trials that implemented VTE screening protocols and in intensive care unit (ICU) patients (VTE prevalence was 22.7% in ICU patients vs 7.9% in non-ICU patients). Notably, although increased risk of vascular thrombotic events has been observed in COVID-19 patients, the association is present in patients with other pneumonia syndromes; in other pneumonia syndromes, arterial thrombosis might be more common than venous thrombosis. 

The risk of DVT and PE associated with COVID-19 was evaluated in a Swedish nationwide self-controlled cases series and matched cohort study. The registry included 1,057,174 subjects who tested positive for SARS-CoV-2 between February 2020, and May 2021. A total of 94.5% of all patients had mild COVID-19, 4.8% were hospitalized, and 0.7% were admitted to the ICU. During days 1-30 after COVID-19 diagnosis, the adjusted risk ratio was 4.98 (95% confidence interval [CI], 4.96-5) for DVT and 33 (95% CI, 32.8-33.3) for PE. In the subgroups of hospitalized and ICU patients, the adjusted risk ratios for PE were substantially greater. VTE was associated with increased mortality. In a meta-analysis published in November 2021 by Kollias et al., the pooled mortality odds ratio was 2.1 (95% CI, 1.2-3.6) for COVID-19 patients with evidence of VTE compared with patients with no evidence of VTE.
As noted, the increased risk for VTE also extends to the posthospital discharge period. The published estimated rates for postdischarge VTE diagnosis range from 0.5% to 2%. 
,
 The CORE-19 Registry was a prospective study designed to evaluate the rate of a thromboembolic event in the 90 days after hospital discharge. Of a total of 4, COVID-19 patients included in the study between March 1 and May 31, 2020, postdischarge VTE was diagnosed in 76 patients (1.55%), including 44 DVTs (0.90%) and 42 PEs (0.85%). Age older than 75 years, cardiovascular risk factors, chronic kidney disease, International Medical Prevention Registry on Venous Thromboembolism D-Dimer (IMPROVE-DD) VTE Score ≥ 4, and ICU stay were all significantly associated with major thromboembolic events. Postdischarge thromboprophylaxis was administered in 12.7% of the patients and was reported to reduce the composite outcome of all thromboembolic events and all-cause mortality by 46%; however, only a relatively small subgroup of patients received thromboprophylaxis.
COVID-19 is associated with arterial thrombosis including acute myocardial infarction (AMI) and ischemic stroke. The reported incidence of acute arterial events related to COVID-19 range widely in the literature. In a study published during the early period of the pandemic on 3334 COVID-19 patients hospitalized in New York City in March and April, 2020, the cumulative incidence of arterial thrombotic events was 11.1% (1.6% ischemic stroke, 8.9% AMI). Risk factors for arterial thrombosis included older age, male sex, Hispanic ethnicity, prevalent coronary artery disease, and higher D-dimer levels at presentation. Venous and arterial thrombosis were significantly associated with mortality (adjusted hazard ratios [HRs] of 1.37 and 1.99, respectively). In a population-wide cohort study in which data from 48 million adults in England and Wales in 2020 were analyzed, the adjusted HR for a first arterial thrombotic event after COVID-19 diagnosis compared with no COVID-19 diagnosis was 21.7 (95% CI, 21.0-22.4) in the first week after COVID-19 diagnosis and declined to 1.34 (95% CI, 1.21-1.48) during weeks 27-49 after COVID-19 diagnosis.
COVID-19 was previously reported to be associated with an increased risk of ischemic stroke. In the American Heart Association COVID-19 Cardiovascular Disease Registry, the authors reported that among 21,073 patients admitted to a hospital with COVID-19 diagnosis between January and November 2020, 160 patients (0.75%) experienced an acute ischemic stroke or transient ischemic attack. More than half of the events occurred within 2 weeks of COVID-19 symptom onset. In multivariable-adjusted models, hypertension, atrial fibrillation, ICU admission, and mechanical ventilation were significantly associated with an increased risk of acute ischemic stroke or transient ischemic attack.
In 2 large nationwide registries of inpatients and outpatients with COVID-19 in Sweden and Denmark the incidence of newly diagnosed AMI during the first 14 days after COVID-19 diagnosis relative to a control interval was reviewed (Sweden: between February and September 2020; Denmark: all patients diagnosed at Danish hospitals up to July 2020). 
,
 The incidence rates for newly diagnosed AMI significantly increased in the first 2 weeks after COVID-19, ranging from 3 to 8 times higher in the Swedish registry and 5 times higher in the Danish registry. The absolute risk of AMI was low in both registries (24/83,913 [0.03%] in the Swedish and 17/5,119 [0.3%] in the Danish registry). COVID-19 was also associated with increased risk for other systemic arterial thrombotic events, including acute limb ischemia, mesenteric ischemia, 
,
 and microvascular thrombosis. 

In addition to the vascular and hematologic complications of COVID-19, vaccine-induced immune thrombotic thrombocytopenia has been reported as a very rare complication of some COVID-19 vaccines. 
,
 These complications might possibly be related to the generation of antibodies that cross-react with platelet factor 4, leading to platelet activation and thrombosis. 
,
 The most common presentation is of thrombosis (including cerebral vein thrombosis)—treatment of these events focused on anticoagulation with adjunctive measures worthy of specialist consideration. Additional research is needed to fully understand the pathogenesis and incidence of these complications and to identify strategies for their prevention and management.
Vaccination, changing variants, and patient demographic characteristics, and concurrent treatment have appreciably affected the disease course. Contemporary data are needed regarding the residual risk of vascular thrombosis in patients with COVID-19 in view of changing disease manifestations and patient characteristics as well as with the use of evidence-based antithrombotic dosing.
Non-COVID-19 respiratory infections and sepsis are known to be associated with an increased risk of thrombotic complications such as venous VTE and arterial thrombosis. During the acute phase of the disease, sepsis-induced coagulopathy is a common finding that can lead to VTE and arterial thrombosis. 
,
 A prospective multicentre study showed that in patients with severe sepsis or septic shock who were receiving guideline-recommended thromboprophylaxis, the incidence of VTE was 37.2% (95% CI, 28.3-46.8). In a nationwide analysis of hospitalizations for viral pneumonias, thrombosis risk was lower than that observed in patients with COVID-19; the proportion of hospitalizations complicated by thrombosis was lower in patients with viral respiratory illness in 2002-2014 than in patients with COVID-19 in 2020 (5% vs 16%; P < 0.001). In an ongoing trial in patients with non-COVID-19 pneumonia optimal antithrombotic dosing is being evaluated (remapcap.org).
Apart from the acute phase of the disease, respiratory infections and sepsis have also been associated with long-term thrombotic complications, particularly VTE. A study showed that the incidence rate of VTE was significantly higher in patients with a previous respiratory infection (9.1 per 1000 person-years) compared with those without a previous respiratory infection (5.7 per 1000 person-years), with the risk remaining elevated for up to 12 months after the infection. Similarly, another study showed that hospitalization for respiratory infection was associated with increased risks of thrombosis that were highest in the first 30 days after discharge and declined over time. A recent population-based matched cohort study showed that sepsis survivors experienced an increased hazard of stroke, myocardial infarction, or cardiovascular death compared with nonsepsis survivors (HR, 1.30; 95% CI, 1.27-1.32). Sepsis survivors also faced an increased hazard of VTE (HR, 1.61; 95% CI, 1.55-1.67) and all-cause death (HR, 1.26; 95% CI, 1.25-1.27). 

The exact mechanisms of how respiratory infections and sepsis lead to long-term thrombotic risk are not fully understood, but it might relate to persistent inflammation, endothelial dysfunction, and changes in coagulation and fibrinolysis systems.

## Bleeding Complications
Although COVID-19 is mainly associated with hypercoagulability, patients might also be at increased risk for bleeding, especially those receiving anticoagulation therapy. 
,
 Although antithrombotic therapy inherently increases bleeding risk, the etiology of bleeding in patients with COVID-19 is multifactorial and can be attributed to: the intensity of anticoagulation therapy; bleeding risks associated with critical illness and patients’ comorbidities; iatrogenic causes, which are more common in ICU patients including extracorporeal membrane oxygenation; SARS-CoV-2-associated thrombocytopenia; and coagulopathy. In the randomized clinical trials to date, bleeding rates have consistently been low.
Various randomized controlled trials (RCTs) that evaluated the role of anticoagulation in patients with COVID-19 reported bleeding rates. In the Antithrombotic Therapy to Ameliorate Complications of COVID-19 (ATTACC)/Accelerating COVID-19 Therapeutic Interventions and Vaccines 4 ACUTE (ACTIV-4A)/Randomized, Embedded, Multifactorial Adaptive Platform Trial for Community-Acquired Pneumonia (REMAP-CAP) multiplatform trial therapeutic anticoagulation with heparin in critically and noncritically ill patients with COVID-19 was evaluated. Among noncritically ill patients, major bleeding occurred in 1.9% of the patients receiving therapeutic-dose anticoagulation and in 0.9% of those receiving thromboprophylaxis. Among critically ill patients, major bleeding occurred in 3.8% of the patients receiving therapeutic-dose anticoagulation and in 2.3% of those receiving thromboprophylaxis. Other trials in which anticoagulation strategies were evaluated in noncritically ill patients with COVID-19 reported major bleeding events; in the RAPID trial, major bleeding occurred in 2 patients (0.9%) assigned to therapeutic heparin and 4 (1.7%) assigned to prophylactic heparin (odds ratio, 0.52 [95% CI, 0.09-2.85]; P = 0.69). In the FREEDOM COVID trial in-hospital major bleeding was infrequent; it occurred in 0.1% and 0.4% of patients in the prophylactic-dose and therapeutic-dose anticoagulation groups, respectively (incidence rate ratio 2.36 [95% CI, 0.81-6.88]; P = 0.18). The HEP-COVID trial included critically ill and noncritically ill patients with COVID-19 with D-dimer levels more than 4 times the upper limit of normal. The incidence of major bleeding was 1.6% with standard-dose vs 4.7% with therapeutic-dose heparins (relative risk, 2.88 [95% CI, 0.59-14.0]; P = 0.17). The Anticoagulation Coronavirus (ACTION) trial randomized patients hospitalized with COVID-19 and elevated D-dimer levels to therapeutic anticoagulation (with either rivaroxaban, enoxaparin, or unfractionated heparin) or prophylactic-dose anticoagulation. Major or clinically relevant nonmajor bleeding occurred in 26 (8%) patients assigned therapeutic anticoagulation and 7 (2%) were assigned prophylactic anticoagulation (relative risk, 3.64 [95% CI, 1.61-8.27]; P = 0.001). In Prevention of Arteriovenous Thrombotic Events in Critically-Ill COVID-19 Patients Trial (COVID-PACT), anticoagulation and antiplatelet therapy in critically ill patients with COVID-19 was evaluated. Fatal or life-threatening bleeding occurred in 2.1% of patients receiving therapeutic-dose anticoagulation vs 0.5% of patients receiving a prophylactic dose (P = 0.19). Moderate to severe bleeding was more frequent in patients receiving therapeutic-dose anticoagulation (7.9% vs 0.5%; P = 0.002). In the Randomised Evaluation of COVID-19 Therapy (RECOVERY) platform trial hospitalized patients with COVID-19 were randomized to aspirin 150 mg once daily vs usual care. The incidence of major bleeding events was higher in the aspirin group (1.6% vs 1.0%; absolute difference 0.6%, standard error 0.2%). 

In a multicentre retrospective study of 400 patients hospitalized in March and April 2020 with COVID-19 (144 critically ill), who primarily received standard-dose prophylactic anticoagulation, the observed rate for overall bleeding was 4.8% (7.6% in the critically ill). The rate for major bleeding (World Health Organization grade 3 or 4) was 2.3% (5.6% in the critically ill). Thrombocytopenia (platelet count < 150 × 109/L) and D-dimer > 2500 ng/mL at initial presentation were reported to be predictive of bleeding complications in a multivariable analysis. In an observational analysis of a nationwide cohort including hospitalized COVID-19 patients in Japan between April and September 2021, it was reported that among the 2882 patients included in the study, 57 (2.0%) had major bleeding. The bleeding risk correlated with disease severity with incidence rates of 0.5%, 2.3%, and 12.3% among patients with mild, moderate, and severe COVID-19 infection, respectively. The most common bleeding sites were the gastrointestinal tract (25/57; 44%), followed by surgery-related/iatrogenic (11/57; 19%), and intracranial bleeding (4/57; 7%). COVID-19 severity, history of major bleeding, and use of anticoagulants were independently associated with an increased risk of bleeding. Moreover, bleeding events were associated with a longer duration of hospitalization and higher mortality rates; the adjusted odds ratio for mortality was 2.27 (95% CI, 1.10-4.93) compared with COVID-19 patients with no evidence of bleeding. In a Swedish nationwide registry of COVID-19 patients, the risk ratio for a first bleeding event during days 1-30 post COVID-19 diagnosis was 1.88 (95% CI, 1.71-2.07). The study showed that a statistically significant increase in bleeding rate was only found in patients admitted to the hospital. Greater risk of bleeding was significantly associated with cancer diagnosis, surgery, and long-term anticoagulation treatment (risk ratio of 2.37 for long-term anticoagulation).

## Management and Treatments
Varying dose intensities of anticoagulation (prophylactic, intermediate, and therapeutic doses) as well as antiplatelet therapy across varying severities of illness have been investigated in multiple RCTs. A summary of evidence from major RCTs in which the use of empirical antithrombotic therapy in COVID-19 patients was evaluated is shown in 
. Recommendations on antithrombotic therapy among patients with COVID-19 are provided by the American Society of Hematology and others.
 Provided there are no contraindications, all hospitalized patients with COVID-19 should receive at minimum pharmacological thromboprophylaxis. Many clinical practice statements indicate that empirical therapeutic anticoagulation might be considered in noncritically ill hospitalized patients, particularly those with a high thrombosis risk (as evidenced by elevated D-dimer levels) and low bleeding risk. 
,
 Two RCTs showed a benefit of therapeutic anticoagulation in noncritically ill hospitalized patients. 
,
 A third RCT showed that compared with prophylactic dose enoxaparin, therapeutic anticoagulation (with either enoxaparin or apixaban) did not significantly reduce a 30-day composite end point that included all-cause mortality, ICU care, systemic thromboembolism, or ischemic stroke. However, therapeutic anticoagulation significantly reduced all-cause mortality and intubation compared with prophylactic anticoagulation. Two other RCTs were stopped early for operational reasons limiting inference; in both of these trials, there was nevertheless a trend toward benefit with full- or intermediate-dose heparin in hospitalized patients. 
,
 In contrast, in a trial of rivaroxaban, a factor Xa inhibitor, no benefit was observed in hospitalized patients, suggesting that heparin might have beneficial effects beyond Xa inhibitors, which might include anti-inflammatory and antiviral effects. In contrast, among critically ill patients intermediate- and therapeutic-dose heparin are nonbeneficial. 
,
 
,
 An important trial design feature of many trials was stratification of patients according to illness severity, which proved to be an important determinant of heterogeneity of treatment effect. , , The role of extended thromboprophylaxis for postdischarge patients is uncertain. There is a suggestion that patients at elevated risk of VTE might benefit from postdischarge pharmacological thromboprophylaxis, 
,
 paralleling uncertainty among patients hospitalized for other reasons. Recent guidelines differ somewhat in their recommendation on postdischarge thromboprophylaxis mainly because of treatment efficacy and safety uncertainties in light of the existing evidence. The American Society of Hematology guidelines suggest that routine outpatient anticoagulant thromboprophylaxis not be used for COVID-19 patients after their hospital discharge unless there is a specific indication for therapy (conditional recommendation on the basis of very low certainty in the evidence about effects). In contrast, the International Society on Thrombosis and Hemostasis guidelines suggest that in select COVID-19 patients, who are discharged from the hospital and have a high risk of VTE, postdischarge treatment with prophylactic-dose rivaroxaban may be considered (class 2B recommendation). Our practice resembles the recommendations of the American Society of Hematology guidelines, because we recommend anticoagulant thromboprophylaxis on the basis of specific indications and not for postacute COVID-19 infection per se, using a patient-specific consideration of thrombotic risk compared with the bleeding risk.
The efficacy of antithrombotic therapy for symptomatic COVID-19 outpatients was studied in the Early Thromboprophylaxis in COVID-19 (ETHIC) trial using prophylactic low molecular weight heparin vs standard of care (no low molecular weight heparin) for at-risk symptomatic outpatients, and also in the Accelerating COVID-19 Therapeutic Interventions and Vaccines 4B (ACTIV-4B) trial in which symptomatic outpatients were randomized to 4 groups receiving either aspirin (81 mg once daily), prophylactic-dose apixaban (2.5 mg twice per day), therapeutic-dose apixaban (5 mg twice per day), or placebo. The thrombotic event rates in the total study population were very low in both trials (eg, only 3 VTE events were diagnosed in ETHIC and 1 in ACTIV-4B). The ETHIC trial was terminated early because of a lower than expected enrollment rate and a lower than expected event rate. The ACTIV-4B trial was terminated early because of a lower than expected event rate. Both trials showed no statistically significant difference in their primary outcomes or the rate of VTE events in either treatment group, but the low event rates and premature termination of both studies limits the ability to draw firm conclusions.
Unlike therapeutic anticoagulation, empirical antiplatelet therapy does not clearly confer benefit in patients with COVID-19. 
,
 , , The RECOVERY trial of aspirin vs usual care in 14,892 hospitalized patients did not observe clear benefit. The REMAP-CAP Antiplatelet Domain trial observed that antiplatelet therapy (aspirin or a P2Y12 inhibitor) in critically ill patients with COVID-19 reduced 90-day mortality; however, this was a secondary end point and the overall trial primary outcome was neutral. The ACTIV-4A trial showed that in noncritically ill patients hospitalized with COVID-19 there was no benefit in antiplatelet therapy, when used in addition to a therapeutic heparin dose, compared with heparin only. Accordingly, additional trials are being conducted to address this question. Because of the contribution of inflammation to COVID-19, anti-inflammatory and anti-viral treatments might theoretically attenuate benefit of escalated antithrombotic strategies, although this is speculative and individualized treatment decision-making is indicated.
A number of other treatments are indicated for the treatment of COVID-19 at various stages of illness severity, which might have early and late benefits. 
,
 These treatments target the inflammatory activation accompanying the virus, and therefore in some ways act upstream of thromboinflammatory cascades, thus having the potential to indirectly reduce the incidence of vascular thrombosis. However, there are limited trial data to evaluated effects of such treatments on vascular events. Such data are needed.
Controversies and uncertainties regarding vascular complications of COVID-19 and their management require further research. First, with the emergence of new viral variants, improved herd immunity, vaccination, and effective treatments, it is unclear whether the pathophysiology and epidemiology of vascular complications will occur with the same frequency and severity. Further studies are needed to understand the effect of the new variants on the vasculature and to determine if any changes in management or treatment are necessary. Second, there is a need to investigate the differences in vascular complications of vaccinated and unvaccinated patients with COVID-19. The role of vaccination in reducing the incidence and severity of vascular complications is an important area of research, particularly as vaccination rates increase globally. Third, although antiviral and anti-inflammatory treatments have shown promise in managing COVID-19, their specific effect on vascular complications is not yet fully understood. Further studies are needed to determine the efficacy of these treatments in reducing vascular complications in COVID-19 patients. Finally, there is a need to further investigate the management of vascular complications in the postacute phase of COVID-19 infection. It is unclear how long vascular complications persist and how best to manage them in the long term.

## Conclusions
Patients with COVID-19 are at elevated risk for vascular complications. It is uncertain how the incidence and severity of vascular events complicating COVID-19 infection might have changed over time. Notably, early and late vascular complications also occur with non-COVID-19 pneumonias. A range of treatments strategies are available. These treatments are generally safe concerning vascular complications, but attention should be paid to the balance between thrombosis and bleeding when choosing an antithrombotic strategy. As the SARS-CoV-2 virus continues to infect patients globally, treatment strategies might need to be adjusted in response to the emergence of new strains and varying levels of immunity in the population—there remains utility to ongoing data in response to the changing disease.
