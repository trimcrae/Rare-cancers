## Abstract
Asparaginase is a cornerstone in the treatment of acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL) and its variant, T-cell lymphoblastic lymphoma (T-LBL), particularly in adolescents and young adults (AYA). However, its use is associated with a significant risk of thrombotic complications due to its profound effects on coagulation pathways. We report a catastrophic case of asparaginase-induced cerebral and systemic thrombosis in a previously healthy 20-year-old female with T-LBL. Despite prophylactic anticoagulation, the patient developed extensive cerebral venous sinus thrombosis, deep vein thrombosis, and pulmonary embolism, necessitating mechanical thrombectomy, Argatroban therapy, and long-term anticoagulation. This case underscores the multifactorial pathophysiology of asparaginase-associated thrombosis and highlights the need for individualized risk assessment, vigilant monitoring, and dynamic anticoagulation strategies. A comprehensive literature review is provided to contextualize the epidemiology, mechanisms, risk factors, prevention, and management of this life-threatening complication, with practical recommendations for optimizing care in high-risk patients.

## Introduction
Acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL) is the most common childhood malignancy and a leading cause of cancer-related mortality in adolescents and young adults (AYA). Therapeutic advances over the past decades—particularly the incorporation of asparaginase into multi-agent chemotherapy regimens—have significantly improved survival rates, with cure rates exceeding 90 % in pediatric populations and showing marked improvement in AYA groups treated with pediatric-inspired protocols[ , ]. Asparaginase, an enzyme that depletes extracellular asparagine essential for lymphoblast survival, plays a central role in induction and consolidation phases of treatment. However, despite its clinical efficacy, asparaginase is associated with a unique toxicity profile that can disrupt treatment continuity and pose life-threatening risks [ ].
Among the most serious complications of asparaginase is venous thromboembolism (VTE), which includes deep vein thrombosis, pulmonary embolism, and cerebral sinus venous thrombosis. The pathogenesis of asparaginase-induced thrombosis is multifactorial, driven by reduced hepatic synthesis of anticoagulant proteins (notably ant thrombin III, protein C, and protein S), endothelial dysfunction, and impaired fibrinolysis. These alterations lead to a profound but transient hypercoagulable state, particularly in adult and AYA patients, where thrombotic risk may exceed 25 % in some cohorts [ , ].
This concern is magnified in patients with T-cell lymphoblastic lymphoma (T-LBL), a clinical variant of ALL with overlapping therapeutic strategies but frequently higher tumor burdens and mediastinal involvement. The prothrombotic milieu in these patients may be further exacerbated by additional risk factors such as corticosteroid use, central venous catheter placement, infections, and individual thrombophilic predispositions [ , ]. Despite growing awareness, there remains a lack of consensus regarding optimal thromboprophylaxis strategies, monitoring protocols, and management of thrombotic complications in asparaginase-treated patients, particularly within the AYA population.
In this context, we present a case of catastrophic asparaginase-induced cerebral and systemic thrombosis in a previously healthy 20-year-old female with T-LBL. The case highlights diagnostic and therapeutic complexities, including resistance to standard anticoagulation, the need for mechanical thrombectomy, and prolonged recovery requiring individualized antithrombotic strategies. We further integrate current literature to provide a comprehensive review of the epidemiology, pathophysiology, risk factors, prevention, and management of asparaginase-associated thrombosis, with a particular focus on practical recommendations for high-risk ALL and T-LBL patients.
1.1.

## Representative case
A 20-year-old previously healthy female presented with progressive shortness of breath and systemic B symptoms, including fever, night sweats, and unintended weight loss. Chest imaging revealed a large anterior mediastinal mass. Histopathologic examination of a biopsy specimen confirmed a diagnosis of T-LBL.
Initial management included cyclophosphamide, vincristine, and prednisone (CVP), pending confirmation of the final histologic diagnosis. Upon confirmation of T-LBL, she was transitioned to a pediatric-inspired chemotherapy protocol and received induction phase that included 1 dose of pegylated asparaginase (PEG-Asp) on the 4th day of induction.
During induction, the patient developed signs of thyrotoxicosis—palpitations, tremors, and heat intolerance. Thyroid function tests (TFTs) were abnormal, and PET-CT revealed increased uptake in the thyroid gland. She was managed initially with a loading dose of propylthiouracil, followed by methimazole for maintenance once thyroid function stabilized. After 7 days of PEG-Asp administration and despite use of prophylactic low-molecular-weight heparin (enoxaparin), the patient developed severe headaches and focal neurological deficits. MRI and MR venography revealed extensive thrombosis of the superior sagittal sinus. The patient was admitted to intensive care unit (ICU). A continuous intravenous heparin infusion was initiated. Due to progression on imaging, the patient underwent multiple mechanical thrombectomies with stent placement. Persistently low ATIII levels rendered heparin less effective and for that anticoagulation was switched to Argatroban under a low-risk protocol. Subsequent imaging demonstrated progression of Cerebral Venous Sinus Thrombosis (CVST), involving the occipital sinuses and bilateral jugular bulbs. The patient concurrently developed a DVT involving the right common femoral and external iliac veins and Imaging confirmed a pulmonary embolism. An inferior vena cava (IVC) filter was placed and anticoagulation with Argatroban was intensified, with the activated partial thromboplastin time (aPTT) target increased to a ratio of 2.5–3.0. By day 21 post-PEG-Asp, ATIII and fibrinogen levels normalized. The patient’s clinical status improved significantly, and she was transitioned from the ICU to the general ward. Argatroban was replaced with therapeutic enoxaparin. Subsequently, the patient underwent successful allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT). Post-transplant monitoring revealed complete donor chimerism with no signs of acute graft-versus-host disease. Enoxaparin was later transitioned to apixaban for long-term anticoagulation, and IVC filter removal was scheduled in 6–9 months post-placement.

1.2.

## Epidemiology
The reported incidence of asparaginase-associated thrombosis varies widely across studies. In pediatric populations, symptomatic venous thromboembolism (VTE) occurs in approximately 2 % to 14 % of patients, while rates in adults may reach 25 % to 30 % among those receiving asparaginase-containing regimens [ ]. Common thrombotic manifestations include central venous catheter (CVC)-associated thrombosis, deep vein thrombosis (DVT), pulmonary embolism (PE), and cerebral sinus venous thrombosis (CSVT). Notably, in a retrospective comparison of VTE incidence in adult ALL patients treated with pegylated asparaginase (PEG-ASP) versus native l-asparaginase (L-ASP), VTE occurred in 28.6 % of PEG-ASP recipients during induction, compared to 10.3 % in the l-ASP group. During intensification, these rates increased to 34.4 % with PEG-ASP and 13.6 % with l-ASP, highlighting a potentially higher thrombotic risk associated with the pegylated formulation [ ].

1.3.

## Pathophysiology
The thrombotic complications associated with asparaginase therapy arise from a complex interplay of coagulation system disturbances, endothelial dysfunction, and impaired fibrinolysis. Asparaginase-induced hepatic dysfunction leads to a marked reduction in the synthesis of several critical anticoagulant proteins, including antithrombin III (ATIII), protein C, and protein S. The depletion of these natural inhibitors results in unchecked activation of coagulation cascades, favoring thrombus formation [ ].
Additionally, asparaginase therapy triggers endothelial cell activation, characterized by elevated circulating levels of von Willebrand factor, soluble thrombomodulin, and P-selectin. These changes contribute to a prothrombotic endothelial phenotype, promoting platelet adhesion and aggregation at the vessel wall [ ]. Furthermore, asparaginase is associated with decreased fibrinolytic activity through the reduction of plasminogen levels and increased expression of plasminogen activator inhibitor-1 (PAI-1), leading to impaired clot dissolution [ ].
Hypofibrinogenemia, another consequence of asparaginase therapy, alters clot architecture, resulting in the formation of denser and more fibrin-rich thrombi. Although hypofibrinogenemia is traditionally considered a risk factor for bleeding, paradoxically, it can contribute to thrombotic risk under specific clinical contexts by promoting abnormal fibrin deposition [ ].
The cumulative effect of these alterations is a hypercoagulable state that significantly elevates the risk of venous and, less commonly, arterial thrombosis. Importantly, recent evidence suggests that the magnitude of coagulation imbalance varies among individuals, influenced by factors such as baseline endothelial function, genetic thrombophilic predispositions, and concurrent inflammatory conditions, highlighting the need for personalized risk assessment and management strategies during asparaginase therapy [ ].

## Risk factors
Age above 10 years remains one of the strongest independent predictors of thrombosis. High-risk ALL subtypes, including T-cell phenotype and hyperleukocytosis, are associated with an elevated incidence of VTE [ ]. The presence of central venous catheters, particularly peripherally inserted central catheters, significantly increases the risk of upper extremity thrombosis [ ]. Recent evidence suggests that prednisone use, compared to dexamethasone, is linked with a higher risk of CNS thrombosis during induction therapy [ ]. Although the contribution of inherited thrombophilias, such as Factor V Leiden and prothrombin gene mutations, remains controversial, a slight increase in risk has been reported [ ]

## Prevention strategies
Maintaining antithrombin activity above 70 % during asparaginase exposure has been advocated to mitigate VTE risk. A multicenter study demonstrated that targeted antithrombin supplementation during induction therapy reduced thrombosis incidence without a corresponding increase in bleeding complications [ ]
Pharmacologic prophylaxis, particularly using low-molecular-weight heparin (LMWH), has been shown to effectively reduce VTE events in ALL patients without significantly increasing bleeding risk [ ]. Emerging evidence also highlights the potential role of direct oral anticoagulants (DOACs), such as rivaroxaban and apixaban, due to their antithrombin-independent mechanisms. Early-phase studies indicate that prophylactic rivaroxaban is feasible and effective in high-risk hematologic malignancy patients [ ].
Optimal CVC management practices, including preference for internal jugular over peripheral insertions and minimizing catheter dwell time, have been recommended to further reduce catheter-associated thrombosis risk.

1.6.

## Management of asparaginase-associated thrombosis
The management of thrombotic events in the setting of asparaginase therapy requires a multidisciplinary approach that balances the risks of thrombosis against the critical need to continue leukemia-directed therapy. The cornerstone of treatment for asparaginase-associated thrombosis is therapeutic anticoagulation, most commonly with low molecular weight heparin (LMWH). LMWH is preferred due to its predictable pharmacokinetics, ease of administration, and lower risk of heparin-induced thrombocytopenia compared to unfractionated heparin.
Monitoring anti-Xa levels during LMWH therapy is advised, particularly in patients with fluctuating renal function, obesity, or ongoing asparaginase exposure, to ensure therapeutic anticoagulation. In cases where antithrombin levels are critically low, supplementation with antithrombin concentrates may be considered to restore heparin efficacy.
Emerging evidence supports the cautious use of direct oral anticoagulants (DOACs) such as rivaroxaban and apixaban for thrombosis management in patients with ALL. Preliminary studies suggest that DOACs may offer a convenient alternative to LMWH, particularly during maintenance therapy phases or in the outpatient setting. However, robust randomized data specific to the asparaginase-treated population are still awaited, and DOACs should be used with careful consideration of bleeding risk, drug interactions, and patient-specific factors.
Regarding continuation of asparaginase therapy after a thrombotic event, growing evidence supports the resumption of asparaginase under therapeutic anticoagulation. Several studies have demonstrated that re-challenging patients with asparaginase does not necessarily lead to recurrent thrombosis when concurrent anticoagulation is maintained. Decisions regarding rechallenge should be individualized based on the severity of the initial thrombotic event, ongoing anticoagulation status, and the overall risk-benefit assessment .
Mechanical interventions, such as thrombectomy or catheter-directed thrombolysis, may be necessary in life-threatening thromboses, particularly those involving the cerebral venous sinuses or massive pulmonary embolism. These interventions require close coordination between hematology, interventional radiology, neurology, and critical care teams.
Long-term anticoagulation is typically recommended for at least the duration of asparaginase therapy and for a minimum of 3–6 months following the thrombotic event. Extended anticoagulation beyond this period may be considered in patients with persistent risk factors, ongoing exposure to prothrombotic chemotherapy agents, or in the context of allogeneic stem cell transplantation .
Finally, comprehensive patient education regarding signs and symptoms of thrombosis, adherence to anticoagulation therapy, and prompt reporting of new neurologic or respiratory symptoms is essential to optimize outcomes in this high-risk population.

2.

## Conclusion
Thrombosis remains a significant complication of asparaginase therapy in ALL, especially in older children and adults. The inclusion of a real-world catastrophic thrombosis case adds urgency to efforts toward individualized risk stratification, early monitoring, and aggressive intervention. Advances over the past decade support prophylactic LMWH, consideration of DOACs, and multidisciplinary management to reduce thrombotic morbidity without compromising leukemia treatment. The presented case of a young female with T-LBL who developed simultaneous cerebral venous sinus thrombosis, pulmonary embolism, and lower limb deep vein thrombosis despite prophylaxis underscores the multifactorial nature of asparaginase-associated thrombosis and the need for dynamic, patient-specific anticoagulation strategies. It further illustrates the clinical relevance of ongoing research in optimizing prevention, monitoring, and treatment protocols to balance thrombosis control with uninterrupted leukemia therapy.
