## Abstract

## Background:
There was controversy surrounding the optimal thromboprophylaxis strategy for coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) patients. This included debates on the dosage of anticoagulants for thromboembolism prophylaxis, the requirement for additional antiplatelet therapy, and the necessity of prophylaxis for outpatients and postdischarge. To explore this, the authors performed a meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials.

## Methods:
PubMed, Cochrane Library, Embase, and Web of Science were last searched on 26 July 2023 for studies comparing the effect of different dose of anticoagulation, additional antiplatelet, and postdischarge prophylaxis for COVID-19 patients. The results of eligible studies were analyzed in terms of thromboembolism events, major bleeding and all-cause mortality during follow-up.

## Results:
Our study included a total of 25 randomized controlled trials, involving 17 911 patients. Our results revealed that, compared to prophylactic dose, therapeutic dose showed lower thrombotic risk (RR, 0.66; 95% CI: 0.45–0.96) but had similar major bleeding risk for critically ill patients with COVID-19. On the other hand, intermediate dose and prophylactic dose demonstrated similar thromboembolism risk and major bleeding risk. For noncritically ill patients with COVID-19, therapeutic dose of anticoagulants was associated with lower thrombotic risk (RR, 0.50; 95% CI: 0.34–0.72) but, at the same time, increased the risk of major bleeding (RR, 2.01; 95% CI: 1.22–3.33). However, intermediate dose showed lower thromboembolism risk (RR, 0.38; 95% CI: 0.21–0.69) while maintaining a similar major bleeding risk. In critically ill patients, additional antiplatelet therapy showed similar thromboembolism, major bleeding risk, and mortality when compared to no treatment. For outpatients, additional prophylactic anticoagulation showed similar thromboembolism, major bleeding risk, and mortality when compared to no treatment. For postdischarge patients, postdischarge prophylaxis reduced thromboembolism risk (RR, 0.49; 95% CI: 0.31–0.76) but increased major bleeding risk (RR, 2.63; 95% CI: 1.13–6.14).

## Conclusion:
For noncritically ill patients, therapeutic dose prophylactic anticoagulation significantly reduced venous thromboembolism but increases major bleeding risk. Intermediate dose effectively lowered venous thromboembolism without raising major bleeding risk. The optimal dose and need for additional antiplatelet therapy in critically ill patients, as well as the necessity of prophylactic anticoagulation in outpatient and postdischarge patients, required further investigation and confirmation through rigorous evidence studies.

Keywords: additional antiplatelet, anticoagulation dose, COVID-19, outpatient prophylaxis, postdischarge prophylaxis

## Introduction
Highlights
For noncritically ill patients, prophylactic anticoagulation of therapeutic dose can reduce the incidence of venous thromboembolism, but it also poses an increased risk of major bleeding.
For noncritically ill patients, prophylactic anticoagulation with an intermediate dose can reduce the occurrence of venous thromboembolism without significantly increasing the risk of major bleeding.
For noncritically ill patients, the use of an intermediate dose for prophylactic anticoagulation is more recommended.
The pathogen responsible for severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2), commonly referred to as coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19), primarily spreads through modes of transmission such as ‘contact’, ‘droplet’, and ‘airborne’ . It has triggered a global pandemic that transcends geographical borders and puts the lives of billions at risk. Thrombotic events are frequently observed in COVID-19 patients during hospitalization, even with the administration of standard thromboprophylaxis, particularly among those who are critically ill – . Therefore, it is recommended to implement thromboprophylaxis for hospitalized COVID-19 patients – . However, there is inconsistent guidance among different guidelines regarding specific thromboprophylaxis strategies.
Firstly, regarding the use of prophylactic anticoagulation, guidelines from American Society of Hematology (ASH), American College of Chest Physicians (CHEST), International Institutes of Health (ISTH), National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE,) and National Institutes of Health (NIH) suggest using therapeutic doses for noncritically ill patients with COVID-19, while WHO recommends standard doses – . For critically ill patients with COVID-19, guidelines from ASH, CHEST, NIH, and WHO advocate for standard doses, while ISTH and NIH guidelines do not recommend intermediate and therapeutic doses – . However, recently, several randomized controlled trials (RCTs) have shown that intermediate and therapeutic doses are associated with lower thromboembolism risk , . Secondly, regarding the need for additional antiplatelet therapy in critically ill patients, many studies suggest that adding antiplatelet treatment on top of prophylactic anticoagulation does not provide additional benefits , . However, a meta-analysis incorporating retrospective studies suggests that antiplatelet therapy can improve clinical outcomes . Thirdly, regarding the need for postdischarge prophylaxis in discharged patients, the ASH and NIH guidelines do not recommend the use of anticoagulation , . However, the CHEST, ISTH, and NICE guidelines recommend the use of postdischarge prophylaxis for patients after being discharged , – .
Fourthly, there is currently no guideline addressing the question of whether prophylactic anticoagulation is necessary for outpatient individuals with COVID-19. Therefore, four key unresolved issues persist: the appropriate dosage of anticoagulants for COVID-19 patients, remains unclear; the necessity of additional antiplatelet therapy for critically ill patients is uncertain; whether prophylactic anticoagulation should be continued for postdischarge patients; and the need for prophylactic anticoagulation for outpatient individuals remains undetermined.
Recently, there have been several RCTs , – published regarding thromboembolism prevention in COVID-19 patients, but they have not been incorporated into the latest guidelines or subjected to a meta-analysis. In order to provide better guidance for clinical practice, we conducted a meta-analysis of RCTs to explore the appropriate dose for thromboprophylaxis in hospitalized COVID-19 patients, the necessity of using antiplatelet therapy, and the need for outpatient and postdischarge prophylaxis.

## Methods
This meta-analysis was conducted in accordance with the PRISMA 2020 statement (Supplemental Digital Content 1, http://links.lww.com/JS9/C275). Our study has also been cross-checked with the AMSTAR 2 checklist (Supplemental Digital Content 2, http://links.lww.com/JS9/C276) and exhibits a high level of consistency with the AMSTAR 2 criteria . The study protocol was registered on PROSPERO.

## Search strategy and selection criteria
Comprehensive literature search for studies exploring thromboprophylaxis for patients with COVID-19 was conducted (last search on 26 July 2023) in the electronic databases PubMed, Cochrane Library, Embase, and Web of Science. After eliminating duplicate articles, four reviewers independently conducted a thorough screening of the titles and abstracts to identify eligible RCTs. Additionally, the same group of four reviewers independently assessed the full texts of the previously identified articles. The inclusion criteria were: COVID-19 patients; critically ill patients referring to those who were admitted to the ICU or received intensive care-level support while hospitalized; noncritically ill patients referring to those hospitalized patients who did not require intensive care-level support; outpatients referring to patients who did not require hospitalization. Postcharge patients referring to those who were discharged after being hospitalized for COVID-19; comparison of the preventive effects of different doses (prophylactic/standard, intermediate, or therapeutic dose) of anticoagulant drugs for hospitalized patients, or comparison of the preventive effects of additional antiplatelet therapy for critically ill hospitalized patients, or comparison of the preventive effects of postdischarge prophylaxis. The specific medication administration and dosage follow the descriptions provided in studies such as Randomized, Embedded, Multifactorial Adaptive Platform Trial for Community-Acquired Pneumonia (REMAP-CAP) . The definitions for therapeutic dose, intermediate dose, and prophylactic dose, as well as the definition of additional dose and prophylactic anticoagulation, were provided in the supplement (eTable 1 in Supplement, Supplemental Digital Content 3, http://links.lww.com/JS9/C277). The exclusion criteria were: patients with cancer, atrial fibrillation, or antiphospholipid syndrome; those not receiving concomitant antiplatelet therapy with anticoagulation; non-RCTs ; and inaccessible full-text articles.

## Data extraction
The same four reviewers independently extracted the relevant data from the included studies using a standardized form, which included the following information: first author, year of publication, country, number of included patients, patient characteristics, follow-up period, the incidence of thromboembolism events, major bleeding, and mortality. In instances where data was missing, we proactively reached out to the authors in an endeavor to obtain the original data. Any discrepancies that emerged were resolved through thorough discussions.

## Risk of bias and quality of evidence
The same four reviewers independently evaluated the quality of the included RCTs using Rob2, a revised tool for assessing the risk of bias in randomized trials . Rob2 evaluates studies across five crucial domains, which include the randomization process, deviations from intended interventions, missing outcome data, the measurement of the outcome, and the selection of the reported results. Within each domain, bias was assessed and categorized as either low risk, some concerns, or high risk, based on the specific circumstances and relevant evidence. Ultimately, the overall bias of each study was classified as either low risk, some concerns, or high risk, based on the comprehensive assessment of bias across the five domains.
The quality of evidence was evaluated using the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development, and Evaluation (GRADE) approach .

## Primary and secondary outcomes
The primary outcome is the incidence rate of thromboembolism event, including venous thromboembolism, ischemia stroke, myocardial ischemia and limb’s ischemia. Secondary outcomes include major bleeding and mortality during follow-up. Major bleeding as defined by International Society on Thrombosis and Hemostasis (ISTH) criteria .

## Statistically analysis
The dichotomous variables were extracted and presented as the event number and total number. The Mantel–Haenszel method was used to pool the dichotomous variables and display the results as risk ratios with their 95% CI. Random-effect models were used for our analysis. The statistical heterogeneity was assessed using I2 and χ2. All analyses were performed using R (version 4.3.1) and Review Manager (Version5.4).

## Result

## Literature search
On 26 June 2023, a total of 8025 articles were retrieved, with 3081 identified as duplicates. Following the screening of titles and abstracts, 4904 studies were excluded. Subsequently, 40 studies underwent a full-text review, and 15 were eliminated based on the inclusion and exclusion criteria. Finally, 25 RCTs were included in the final analysis. eFigure 1 (Supplemental Digital Content 3, http://links.lww.com/JS9/C277).

## Study characteristic
A total of 25 studies – , – , , – involving, 17 911 patients were included. There were 18 studies comparing different doses of anticoagulants on hospitalized patients. Among these, two studies compared therapeutic doses with intermediate or prophylactic doses for critically ill patients. Additionally, three studies compared intermediate doses with prophylactic doses for critically ill patients. Furthermore, nine studies compared therapeutic doses with prophylactic doses for noncritically ill patients. In most of the studies, Low-Molecular-Weight Heparin (LMWH) or heparin was used as the anticoagulant, but in two studies, the intervention group received a therapeutic dose of rivaroxaban instead. There were also four studies comparing intermediate doses with prophylactic doses for noncritically ill patients. Moreover, three studies investigated the use of additional antiplatelet therapy, primarily P2Y12 agonists, with one study also including aspirin in the treatment. Furthermore, three studies , , explored postdischarge prophylaxis and four studies , , , explored prophylactic anticoagulation for outpatients, with one study using LMWH (Table ).
 

## Quality assessment
Nineteen studies had some concerns of bias, mainly because all these studies were open-label. Two studies , had some concerns about the selection of the reported results because they did not report major bleeding or mortality. Only six studies , , , , , were assessed as low risk across all domains. Efigure2 (Supplemental Digital Content 3, http://links.lww.com/JS9/C277).

## Therapeutic, intermediate, or prophylactic dose
In studies , investigating the therapeutic dose for critically ill patients, the therapeutic dose exhibited a statistically significant lower risk of thromboembolism events compared to intermediate/prophylactic doses (RR, 0.66; 95% CI: 0.45–0.96) (Fig. A). However, when it comes to major bleeding risk (RR, 1.63; 95% CI: 0.82–3.25) (Fig. B) and mortality (RR, 0.84; 95% CI: 0.37–1.87) (Fig. C), there were no statistically significant differences observed between therapeutic dose and intermediate/prophylactic doses.
 In studies , , investigating intermediate dose for critically ill patients, no statistically significant differences were found between intermediate dose and prophylactic dose in terms of thromboembolism events (RR, 0.84; 95% CI: 0.56–1.27) (Fig. A), major bleeding (RR, 1.82; 95% CI: 0.72–4.62) (Fig. B), and mortality (RR, 1.04; 95% CI: 0.87–1.23) (Fig. C).
 In studies , , – investigating the therapeutic dose for noncritically ill patients, the therapeutic dose showed a statistically significant lower risk of thromboembolism events compared to the prophylactic dose (RR, 0.50; 95% CI: 0.34–0.72) (Fig. A). However, the therapeutic dose also showed a statistically significant higher risk of major bleeding than the prophylactic dose (RR, 2.01; 95% CI: 1.22–3.33) (Fig. B). There was no statistically significant difference in mortality between the two groups (RR, 0.85; 95% CI: 0.65–1.10) (Fig. C).
 In studies , , , investigating the use of intermediate doses for noncritically ill patients, the intermediate dose showed a statistically significant lower risk of thromboembolism events compared to the prophylactic dose (RR, 0.38; 95% CI: 0.21–0.69) (Fig. A). However, there were no statistically significant differences in terms of major bleeding (RR, 1.01; 95% CI: 0.42–2.42) (Fig. B) and mortality (RR, 0.96; 95% CI: 0.63–1.47) (Fig. C) between the two groups.
 There were also two studies , comparing the therapeutic dose and intermediate dose for noncritically ill patients. The pooled results showed that there was no statistically significant difference in thromboembolism events (RR, 0.97; 95% CI: 0.37–2.53) (eFigure 3A in Supplement, Supplemental Digital Content 3, http://links.lww.com/JS9/C277) and mortality (RR 1.07; 95% CI: 0.55–2.08) (eFigure 3B in Supplement, Supplemental Digital Content 3, http://links.lww.com/JS9/C277).

## Additional antiplatelet
In studies , , investigating the use of additional antiplatelets for critically ill patients, there was no statistically significant difference in thromboembolism events (RR, 0.91; 95% CI: 0.69–1.20) (Fig. A), major bleeding (RR, 1.68; 95% CI: 0.54–5.18) (Fig. B), and mortality (RR, 0.89; 95% CI: 0.77–1.03) (Fig. C) between those who received additional antiplatelet therapy and those who did not receive any antiplatelet treatment.
 

## Outpatient and postcharge prophylaxis
Four studies , , , compared the use of prophylactic anticoagulation or no anticoagulation for outpatient. The results showed no statistically significant difference in thromboembolism events (RR, 0.50; 95% CI: 0.19–1.20) (Fig. A), major bleeding (RR, 3.02; 95% CI: 0.31–28.96) (Fig. B), and mortality (RR, 0.73; 95% CI: 0.30–1.81) (Fig. C) between those who received prophylactic anticoagulation and those who did not receive prophylactic anticoagulation.
 In studies , , investigating the postdischarge prophylaxis for postdischarge patients, postdischarge prophylaxis demonstrated a statistically significant lower risk of venous thromboembolism compared to no prophylaxis (RR, 0.49; 95% CI: 0.31–0.76) (Fig. A). However, postdischarge prophylaxis also showed a statistically significant higher risk of major bleeding than no prophylaxis (RR, 2.63; 95% CI: 1.13–6.14) (Fig. B). There was no statistically significant difference in mortality between the two groups (RR, 0.82; 95% CI: 0.33–2.02) (Fig. C).
 

## Sensitivity analysis
We conducted sensitivity analyses for each outcome using the one-at-a-time removal method, and overall, the results showed stability (eFigure 4 in Supplement, Supplemental Digital Content 3, http://links.lww.com/JS9/C277).

## Publication bias
Considering the limited number of included studies, we only performed publication bias analysis for therapeutic dose for noncritically ill patients (eFigure 5 in Supplement, Supplemental Digital Content 3, http://links.lww.com/JS9/C277). The funnel plots showed a symmetrical distribution of the included studies. Moreover, statistical tests Egger’s test did not reveal any significant evidence of publication bias (t=−1.55, P=0.1651).

## Grade ratings
GRADE ratings for above outcomes were assessed. The quality of evidence ranged from low to moderate. Only in noncritically ill patients, the outcome of intermediate dose versus prophylactic dose was considered moderate, while the quality of evidence for the remaining outcomes was categorized as low (Table ).
 

## Discussion
Our study findings revealed that for critically ill patients, therapeutic dose was associated with a lower risk of thromboembolism compared to intermediate and prophylactic doses, while the risks of major bleeding and mortality were similar. On the other hand, using an intermediate dose showed similar thromboembolism, major bleeding, and mortality risks as the prophylactic dose. Our meta-analysis results indicated that compared to the prophylactic dose, the therapeutic dose has advantages in terms of venous thromboembolism prevention and safety. In the 2021 ASH guidelines, it is suggested that prophylactic dose is preferred over intermediate dose for critically ill patients with low certainty . According to the 2020 Chest guidelines, an ungraded consensus-based statement recommends prophylactic dose anticoagulant thromboprophylaxis over intermediate or therapeutic dose anticoagulation . These two guidelines were based on early pandemic experience and limited research results. However, it is important to note that our inclusion of only two studies for the therapeutic dose results in a lower level of evidence, necessitating more high-quality evidence to support this recommendation. Guidelines recommend administering therapeutic doses to noncritically ill patients . However, our meta-analysis of newest RCTs showed that for noncritically ill patients, therapeutic dose, although associated with a lower thromboembolism risk, significantly increases the risk of major bleeding and the intermediate dose shows a lower thromboembolism risk compared to the prophylactic dose while maintaining a similar level of major bleeding risk. Therefore, compared to the prophylactic dose, both intermediate dose and therapeutic dose could significantly lower the risk of venous thromboembolism. However, the therapeutic dose carried a higher risk of major bleeding. There are relatively few studies that directly compare therapeutic dose and intermediate dose. Our conclusion is primarily drawn indirectly by comparing therapeutic dose and intermediate dose to the prophylactic dose. Therefore, caution is warranted when interpreting the results. Valeriani et al. meta-analysis indicates that in hospitalized patients with COVID-19, high-dose thromboprophylaxis is more effective than low-dose in preventing venous thromboembolism (VTE) but comes with an increased risk of major bleeding. However, their meta-analysis combined therapeutic dose and intermediate dose into the high-dose category and did not conduct further analysis based on the severity of patients’ conditions. As a result, it may not accurately reflect specific clinical practices. In contrast, our meta-analysis takes a separate and comprehensive approach, analyzing both critically ill and noncritically ill patients while considering therapeutic, intermediate, and prophylactic doses. This method offers more precise guidance for clinical practices.
In critically ill patients, the use of additional antiplatelet therapy showed similar effects to no antiplatelet therapy in terms of thromboembolism, major bleeding, and mortality. The guidelines indicate multiple trials in moderate COVID-19 patients showed no benefit of adding antiplatelet therapy to anticoagulants in improving clinical outcomes and suggest using antiplatelet agents only for the setting of a clinical trial for hospitalized patients with COVID-19 , . The meta-analysis conducted by Zong et al. on observational studies indicates that antiplatelet therapy is beneficial in reducing patient mortality. However, their meta-analysis only analyzed observational studies and did not conduct an analysis based on the severity of patients’ conditions. In contrast, our meta-analysis focused on RCTs and revealed that the use of additional antiplatelet therapy for critically ill patients did not improve patient outcomes.
For outpatients, although prophylactic anticoagulation did not significantly increase major bleeding risk or mortality, it also did not significantly reduce the risk of thromboembolism. For postcharge patients, postcharge prophylaxis was associated with a lower risk of thromboembolism compared to no postcharge prophylaxis, but it was also accompanied by a higher risk of major bleeding. The guidelines have different opinions regarding the necessity of postdischarge prophylaxis. Some suggest not using postdischarge prophylaxis, some recommend continuing postdischarge prophylaxis, and others suggest that for patients who are at high risk for VTE and low risk of bleeding, extended VTE prophylaxis can be considered . In Dai et al. meta-analysis, it was found that prophylactic use of anticoagulation in postcharge patients can reduce thrombosis without increasing the risk of major bleeding. However, their meta-analysis included both observational studies and RCTs and did not incorporate the latest published RCTs. In contrast, our meta-analysis includes all relevant RCTs and suggests that the decision to administer postcharge prophylaxis should be based on individual patient characteristics. The inflammation profile, elevated D-dimer levels, illness severity, ICU treatment, and invasive mechanical ventilation treatment may be thromboembolism risk factors , . Our results showed that for discharged patients, it is necessary to balance the risks of bleeding and thrombosis based on the patient’s characteristics to determine whether postcharge prophylaxis is warranted.
Our study delved into the controversies surrounding COVID-19 thromboprophylaxis over the 4 years of the pandemic, addressing key issues such as dosage, antiplatelet therapy, outpatient, and postdischarge prophylaxis. On the one hand, we conducted an analysis and summarized the debates that unfolded during this period. On the other hand, our research has the potential to offer valuable strategies for thromboprophylaxis in the event of future, unforeseen resurgences of respiratory infectious diseases. However, our study has some limitations. Firstly, for the thromboprophylaxis efficacy of therapeutic and intermediate doses in noncritically ill patients, we included 11 studies, providing reliable evidence of high-quality. However, the number of studies included for critically ill patients, additional antiplatelet therapy, as well as outpatient and postdischarge patients, is limited, with the quality of evidence being constrained. Further research is needed in these areas. Secondly, our analysis exhibits a certain degree of heterogeneity. This heterogeneity may arise from virus mutations, variations in medical capabilities among different institutions, and differences in patient characteristics across various studies. Thirdly, the majority of the studies we included were open-label, which introduces potential bias in the implementation of patient blinding. However, the outcomes we included were mainly objective assessments of thrombosis, major bleeding, and mortality. Fourthly, the question of whether additional antiplatelet therapy is needed on the basis of anticoagulation applies only to critically ill patients, as there are no RCTs available for noncritically ill patients. Therefore, this aspect requires further research in the future. Fifthly, for noncritically ill patients, due to the lack of direct comparisons between therapeutic dose and intermediate dose, our results are drawn indirectly by comparing them to the prophylactic dose, which reduces the strength of the evidence. Sixthly, Due to limited information on SARS-CoV-2 variants in the included studies, we were unable to conduct a subgroup analysis for SARS-CoV-2 variants. Lastly, due to limited data, we were unable to perform further analyses for specific patient groups to explore factors such as thrombosis risk and the impact of different types of diseases on thromboprophylaxis. More high-quality research is needed to further investigate thromboembolism-related issues in COVID-19 patients.

## Conclusion
For noncritically ill patients, compared to the prophylactic dose, the therapeutic dose had significantly lower risks of venous thromboembolism, higher risks of major bleeding, and comparable mortality risks; the intermediate dose had lower risks of venous thromboembolism with comparable risks of major bleeding and mortality. Therefore, our data supported a moderate recommendation in favor of intermediate dose against therapeutic dose for thromboprophylaxis for noncritically ill patients.
For critically ill patients, compared to the prophylactic dose, the therapeutic dose had a lower risk of venous thromboembolism with comparable risks of major bleeding and mortality; the intermediate dose presented comparable risks of venous thromboembolism, major bleeding, and mortality. In critically ill patients, additional antiplatelet therapy had comparable risks of venous thromboembolism, major bleeding, and mortality compared to no antiplatelet therapy. For outpatients, prophylactic anticoagulation had comparable risks of venous thromboembolism, major bleeding, and mortality compared to no anticoagulation. For postcharge patients, prophylactic anticoagulation had comparable risks of venous thromboembolism and mortality but higher risks of major bleeding. The quality of evidence for these outcomes was low, and high-quality research was needed in future for further exploration.
