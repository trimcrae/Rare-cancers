## Abstract
Cancer-associated thrombosis (CAT), especially venous thromboembolism (VTE), is the second highest cause of mortality in cancer patients, following the cancer itself. Notably, among patients with thrombosis, cancer is the most common underlying cause of death, highlighting the strong interrelationship between these conditions. Tumour cells primarily promote a prothrombotic environment through activation of procoagulant properties of the host cells. Additionally, chemotherapy, radiotherapy and hormone treatments may amplify the thrombotic risk in cancer patients. Consequently, both arterial thromboses, including myocardial infarction (MI) and ischemic stroke (IS), and venous thrombosis, such as deep vein thrombosis (DVT) and pulmonary embolism (PE), are significantly more prevalent among patients with malignancies. This review will discuss the association between cancer and thrombosis, illustrate the most common risk factors and discuss the direct and indirect molecular mechanisms involved.

## Background
The relationship between malignancy and hypercoagulability was first described by Armand Trousseau in 1865 [ ]. Since then, thrombosis has been identified in multiple studies as the second leading cause of death in cancer patients [ ]. Cancer-associated thrombosis (CAT) can be either venous thromboembolism (VTE) or arterial thromboembolism (ATE). Patients can present with CAT, most commonly VTE, before cancer has been diagnosed and it could be the first sign of underlying malignancy [ ]. Compared to healthy people, cancer patients have a 4 to sevenfold increased risk of VTE which includes deep vein thrombosis (DVT) or pulmonary embolism (PE) [ ]. For example, in the United Kingdom, the hazard ratio (HR) of VTE between cancer patients and healthy individuals was 4.7% and the incidence rate was 13.9/1000 per year [ ]. Whilst arterial thrombosis, which includes myocardial infarction (MI) and cerebrovascular event (CVA) accounts for mortality in around 5.6% of cancer patients receiving outpatient chemotherapy [ ]. Treatment of cancer patients with thrombosis is more complicated increasing the financial burden compared to patients without thrombosis [ ].
Although the association between cancer and thrombosis has been well established, the underlying mechanisms are complex and varied [ ]. In general, patients with cancers have the components of Virchow's triad which consists of stasis of blood flow, endothelial injury and hypercoagulability. However, the underlying molecular mechanisms of these abnormalities have not been fully elucidated [ ].
Improvements have been made in the understanding and management of CAT including the use of direct oral anticoagulants (DOACs) [ ]. In addition, several societies such as the International Society on Thrombosis and Haemostasis (ISTH) [ ], International Initiative on Thrombosis and Cancer (IITC) and European Society of Medical Oncology (ESMO) [ ] have recently provided guidelines with several recommendations for management of CAT.

## Types of Cancer-Associated Thrombosis

## Venous Thromboembolism (VTE)
VTE is highly prevalent and accounts for approximately 3 million deaths per year globally [ ]. DVT is most commonly initiated in the valve sinus making this the site most prone to thrombosis. Subsequently, the blood flow and shear stress decrease, leading to hypoxia and endothelial dysfunction [ ]. These conditions trigger the activation of platelets and leukocytes, particularly neutrophils, which play a critical role in the formation of thrombi [ ]. In malignancy, the blood flow inside these compressing veins can reduce due to tumours resulting in venous stasis which propagates thrombus formation [ ]. Patients with active cancer also at an increased risk of thrombosis in unusual sites. Upper extremity thrombosis is 18 times more common due to use of a central venous catheter [ ]. Visceral vein thrombosis (VVT) is mostly associated with gastrointestinal (GI) malignancy [ ].
In approximately 10% of patients with an unprovoked VTE, an occult cancer will be detected [ ]. Therefore, screening after any unprovoked VTE event has been recommended for early prognosis and to reduce costs [ ]. The ISTH recommendations for screening after an unprovoked VTE include medical examination, chest radiography and laboratory tests including blood counts and liver function tests [ ].
Certain patients, such as those with recurrent VTE and bilateral DVT may require more comprehensive cancer screening tests because they are at a higher risk for occult cancer. Risk factors for occult cancer in unprovoked VTE patients include, age > 50 years, male gender, high leukocytes and platelets counts [ ].

## Arterial Thromboembolism (ATE)
There are fewer studies into the relationship between arterial thrombosis and cancer compared to those investigating VTE [ ]. Nonetheless, the evidence does support a relationship between arterial thrombosis and cancer [ – ]. One study found that 4.7% of cancer patients had developed ATE at 6 months compared to 2.2% in non-malignant individuals [ ]. The prevalence of the two most common types of ATE, acute MI and ischemic stroke, are estimated as 139.9 and 114.3 per 100.000 people respectively, which is around 0.25% overall incidence rate in individuals [ ]. The increased risk of ATE has been demonstrated in different types of cancer including kidney [ ] and lung cancer [ ].
Rupture or erosion of atherosclerotic plaques is often the initiating event of ATE. A thrombus can develop either over a ruptured plaque or over an unprovoked plaque that has experienced superficial endothelial erosion. Plaques with a high concentration of lipids in the core and a thin layer of fibrous tissue covering them are the most unstable and therefore likely to trigger thrombosis [ – ]. Activated platelets promote thrombus formation through release of procoagulant molecules within the plaque core [ ]. High shear rates in the compressed arteries can further stimulate platelets which also promotes thrombus formation [ ]. In addition, tissue factor (TF) present within atherosclerotic plaque promotes thrombus formation [ ]. However, in cancer patients, ATE can occur in the absence of atherosclerotic plaque rupture due to tumour cell release of TF and vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) which supports thrombin generation and activation of platelets [ ].

## Risk Factors for Cancer-Associated Thrombosis
There are several other risk factors that have been linked to an increased risk of thrombosis in cancer patients. These risk factors can be divided into three connected categories, including patients-related, cancer-related, and treatment-related factors [ ] which are discussed below with a focus on VTE due to the higher prevalence compared to ATE.

## Patient-Related Risk Factors
In the general population, the prevalence of thrombosis is correlated with patient age. Compared to those in the 45–54 age range, patients 85 years of age or beyond have a tenfold increased chance of developing VTE [ ]. A number of retrospective studies have found that ageing increases the likelihood of VTE in cancer patients [ ]. For example, elderly cancer patients (65 years or older) have a higher risk of developing a VTE compared to younger patients [ ]. Furthermore, older cancer patients are approximately twice as likely as younger patients to develop VTE after chemotherapy or surgery [ ]. Gender of cancer patients has also been suggested to affect the probability of developing a VTE [ ]. However, the evidence of this is not clear. Some studies have indicated that women with cancer have a higher risk of developing VTE than men [ ], with men being more likely to develop arterial thrombosis [ ]. However, others have not found an association between gender and CAT [ ]. Multiple studies have revealed that ethnicity is another risk factor for CAT. Khorana et al. found that there was a substantial difference between the prevalence of VTE in black patients (5.1%) compared to white ones (4%) [ ]. Whilst the risk in Asian patients of developing VTE was found to be low (3.3%) [ ] consistent with the previous study [ ]. No appreciable variations in PE and DVT incidence rates between black and white patients have been identified [ ]. Comorbidities such as renal failure, pulmonary disease, and acute infection also enhance the risk of thrombosis in cancer patients [ , ]. Thrombophilia genes, such as factor V Leiden, have been associated with gastrointestinal cancer, indicating these patients may be particularly predisposed to develop CAT [ ]. Furthermore, 2.2–3.7-fold increased risk of ATE was linked to diseases requiring therapy with lipid-lowering or antiplatelet medications, smoking status, history of cardiovascular disease, and hypertension [ ]. These characteristics matched the results from the RIETE cohort of VTE-afflicted cancer patients [ ]. CAT incidents are also linked to other genetic risk factors at an earlier age, including non-O blood types, protein C and protein S deficiencies, and antithrombin (AT) deficiency [ , ].

## Cancer-Associated Risk Factors
Cancer-associated risk factors encompass various aspects such as the location, stage, subtypes of cancer, and the duration of time since diagnosis [ , ]. Certain cancers are thought to pose a significant risk for developing VTE, such as kidney, lung, stomach, and pancreatic cancer [ , ]. A meta-analysis identified pancreatic cancer as having the highest incidence rate of VTE [ ]. However, contrasting findings from other studies have suggested that the gastrointestinal tract may have the highest incidence of VTE [ ]. Conversely, the chance of developing VTE could be connected to the incidence of specific cancer subtypes. There is a strong association between VTE and the highly prevalent ovarian and breast cancer in women, and prostate and colorectal cancer in men. These observed variances in risk may indicate the existence of distinct pathways associated with thrombus formation in different forms of cancer [ ].
The stage of cancer also impacts the risk as a substantial rise in the risk of VTE is observed during advanced stages of cancer [ ]. Metastatic cancer is correlated with a higher likelihood of VTE in comparison to non-metastatic cancer [ ]. According to a cohort study conducted in the Danish population, the risk of thrombosis in cancer patients exhibited an escalation from 2.9 in stage I to 17.1 in stage IV, respectively [ ].
Moreover, certain histological subtypes of cancer may be associated with an elevated risk of CAT [ ]. In the context of lung cancer, the incidence of VTE is higher in adenocarcinoma subtypes as compared to squamous cell carcinoma [ ]. In contrast, a substantial difference in the risk of thrombosis was not observed across histological subtypes of breast [ ] and colon cancer [ ].
The period following the diagnosis of cancer is an additional risk factor for the development of thrombosis [ ]. Several studies have proposed that the risk is increased in the initial year following a confirmed cancer diagnosis, while others have specifically identified the first 3 to 6 months as the period of highest susceptibility [ ]. During this period, treatment of the cancer is initiated, which may lead to an elevated risk of VTE [ ].
Another significant risk factor for thrombosis is immobility in patients with cancer which is clinically evaluated by the performance status. Patients with cancer that have a poor performance status had a greater risk of VTE [ ]. Prolonged bed rest (more than 3 days) following cancer surgery is linked to noticeably increased rates of VTE [ ] thought to be due to reduced venous flow [ ]. In comparison to cancer patients lacking a history of VTE, those with a past VTE exhibit an approximately sevenfold increased risk of recurrent VTE [ ].

## Treatment-Related Risk Factors
Anticancer medicines and surgical interventions frequently induce a prothrombotic state [ ]. One study has reported a 2- to threefold increase in the risk of VTE and PE among post-operative cancer patients compared to non-cancer patients undergoing similar operations [ ]. Patients undergoing pelvic, abdominal, and orthopedic procedures are observed to have the largest risk of VTE [ ]. Nevertheless, advancements in surgical techniques and preventive measures such as increased mobility and improved health care services after surgery have contributed to a decrease in the occurrence [ ].
Chemotherapy is widely recognized as a significant risk factor for CAT [ , ]. This association, referred to as chemotherapy-associated thrombosis, can elevate the risk of VTE by around 6 to 7 times [ ]. Patients diagnosed with pancreatic, or stomach cancer have been identified as a high-risk population for chemotherapy-induced thrombosis in comparison to individuals with other types of cancer [ ]. Khorana et al. (2013) revealed a significant increase in the incidence of VTE among cancer patients who had undergone chemotherapy treatment for a duration of 12 months, as compared to individuals without cancer [ ]. Furthermore, it should be noted that certain angiogenesis inhibitors, like bevacizumab, have the potential to interact with the circulating vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), hence potentially elevating the risk of ATE in individuals diagnosed with cancer [ ]. Immunomodulatory drugs (thalidomide, lenalidomide, pomalidomide) markedly increase venous thromboembolism (VTE), especially when combined with corticosteroids or chemotherapy [ ]. Cytotoxic agents such as cisplatin and L-asparaginase are also implicated—cisplatin via endothelial injury and release of procoagulant factors, and L-asparaginase through depletion of antithrombin III [ ]. Hormonal therapies (e.g., tamoxifen) further contribute to VTE risk [ ].
The administration of red blood cell transfusions, platelets, and erythropoiesis-stimulating medications has been observed to potentially contribute to the development of VTE in patients undergoing cancer treatment [ ]. The hospitalization, hormonal therapy, central venous catheters, endocrine therapy, immunotherapy, protein kinase inhibitors, targeted therapies and immune checkpoint inhibitors, which all have been linked to VTE [ , – ].

## Molecular Mechanisms Underlying CAT
The precise molecular pathways behind the hypercoagulable state observed in individuals with cancer are currently the subject of ongoing research. Multiple studies have revealed two pathways which are categorized as direct and indirect molecular mechanisms. Both mechanisms have been found to raise the risk of thromboembolic status in patients with oncological conditions.

## Direct Molecular Mechanism of CAT
The haemostatic pathways observed in cancer patients exhibit significant differences compared to those observed in non-cancer individuals (Fig.  ) [ ].
 

## Tissue Factor (TF)
TF is a protein that contributes to both the growth of cancer and the development of thrombosis in individuals affected by cancer [ ]. TF is an integral membrane protein with a molecular weight of 47 kDa, which is predominantly found in high quantities within subendothelial cells, specifically fibroblasts and vascular smooth muscle cells [ ]. Under normal circumstances, the expression of TF is not observed in the circulating blood due to its separation by the endothelial membrane. It is a high affinity receptor and acts as a cofactor for conversions of factor VII to VIIa [ ]. The formation of the TF/FVIIa complex leads to the activation of the extrinsic coagulation pathway, resulting in the conversion of FIX to FIXa and FX to FXa [ ]. In the context of malignancy, tumour cells and endothelial cells exhibit abnormal production of TF [ ]. For example, in breast and pancreatic cancer, TF is highly expressed and correlated with the grade and tumour progression [ , ]. However, the correlation of TF expression and the risk of developing VTE has only been demonstrated in pancreatic and ovarian cancer [ – ].

## Microparticles (MPs)
MPs, which are small vesicles released from tumour cells, have been found to contribute to the prothrombotic response observed in individuals with cancer [ ]. The MPs are derived from active normal cells, apoptotic cells, or quiescent malignant cells [ ]. The release of procoagulant MPs, both in vivo and in vitro, was identified at an early stage in breast and hepatocarcinoma cell lines [ , ]. MPs have been observed to have the ability to enhance thrombus development in vivo across all types of malignancies [ ].

## Podoplanin (PDPN) and Tumour-Derived Platelet Agonists
Malignant cells have the ability to induce platelet activation and aggregation via C-type lectin receptor 2 (CLEC-2) stimulation, facilitated by the production of podoplanin (PDPN) [ ]. Additionally, tumour cells release two significant agonists, namely Adenosine Diphosphate (ADP) [ ] and thrombin [ ], which promote further platelet activation by P2Y1, P2Y12, PAR-1, and PAR-4 platelet receptors. Reduction of thrombosis was observed in a DVT model using CLEC-2 platelet receptor knock-out mice transfused with wild-type platelets [ ]. Interestingly, it is not only malignant cells that express PDPN, tumour-derived MPs expressing PDPN have also been identified in the bloodstream of individuals diagnosed with pancreatic or colorectal cancer [ ]. The presence of platelet agonists originating from tumours was observed in the plasma of individuals diagnosed with pancreatic cancer, providing evidence of their connection to platelet activation and the formation of blood clots [ ].

## Plasminogen Activator Inhibitor-1 (PAI-1)
The risk of thrombosis in cancer patients is heightened by the suppression of fibrinolysis [ ]. Plasminogen activator inhibitor-1 (PAI-1), a prominent inhibitor of fibrinolysis, has been elevated in various types of cancer [ ]. Expression of PAI-1 has been associated with the occurrence of thrombosis in individuals diagnosed with pancreatic cancer [ ]. In other cancer types such as breast, gastric and ovarian, elevation of PAI-1 was correlated with poor patient outcomes [ – ]. Plasma PAI-1 has been suggested as a potential prognostic tool for different types of cancer such as breast cancer [ ]. The investigation and comprehensive understanding of the role and particular mechanisms of PAI-1 in cancer-associated thrombosis remain incomplete, necessitating further research and study.

## Cancer Procoagulant (CP)
The stimulation of the coagulation cascade by cancer procoagulant (CP) also contributes to an increased risk of thrombosis in individuals with malignancy. CP was initially obtained from malignant tissue in rabbits and subsequently from human cancer [ ]. It has been observed that CP is capable of directly initiating the coagulation cascade by activating FX to FXa, without requiring the activation of FVII [ ]. In previous studies, the presence of novel factor X activating proteins were proposed [ ]. However, subsequent investigations indicated the possibility that procoagulant activity in CP preparations were due to contamination with TF-FVIIa complex [ ]. Although these proteins could be recognised with anti-TF antibodies they differed in their molecular weight and amino acid sequences [ ]. Further research is still needed to determine the ability and mechanism of CP in activating FX.

## Indirect Molecular Mechanism of CAT:

## Tissue Factor-Positive Microparticles from other Cells than Cancer Cells
Platelets, leukocytes, and endothelial cells are the primary cellular components that have been implicated in the pathogenesis of thrombosis in individuals with cancer [ ]. This section will discuss the indirect process by which cancer-associated thrombosis can be augmented (Fig.  ).
 As discussed above, tumour cells are a source of TF-Positive MPs, however, they can also be released by activated endothelial cells and monocytes in response to malignancy [ ]. The production of inflammatory cytokines including tumour necrosis factor alpha (TNF-α) and interleukin-1β (IL-1β) by tumour cells can enhance the release of TF-positive MPs and promote thrombosis [ ]. The complex relationship between primary cells involved in thrombosis and TF-positive MPs still needs further investigation.

## Inflammatory Cytokines
During malignancy, TNF-α and IL-1β may enhance the synthesis of TF and von -Willebrand factor (VWF) by vascular endothelial cells [ ] and downregulate the natural anticoagulant thrombomodulin [ ]. Both TNF-α and IL-1β have the ability to induce the synthesis of PAI-1 from endothelial cells, thereby impeding the process of fibrinolysis [ ]. Additionally, it can suppress the release of nitric oxide and prostacyclin from endothelial cells, leading to an augmentation of platelet adhesion and aggregation [ ].

## Adhesion Molecules
The procoagulant response is enhanced by tumour cells' ability to produce special adhesive molecules that enable them to adhere to the endothelial cells lining the arterial wall [ , ]. As an illustration, the HT-29 M colon cancer cell line exhibits expression of the adhesion molecule E-Selectin, facilitating their attachment to the vascular A 375 M cells and leading to the formation of thrombi and subsequent artery occlusion [ ]. In addition, cancer cells can interact with platelets through P-selectin which is expressed by activated platelets and endothelial cells. Also, platelet integrin αIIb/β3 and sulphatides which are expressed by some cancer types can enhance the platelet and tumour cell interactions promoting thrombus formation [ ].

## Neutrophil Extracellular Traps (NETs)
NETs refer to the release of proteases and histones as antimicrobial mechanisms in response to infection [ ]. However, NETs have been found to have a significant role in the facilitation of thrombosis in a murine model [ ]. Release of histones promotes activation of endothelial cells, leading to an increase in the concentration of VWF which facilitates platelet adhesion, aggregation and subsequently the formation of thrombi [ ]. Inhibition of NETs decreases thrombus stability in a murine model implicating their involvement in thrombus persistence [ ]. The presence of NETs has been identified in individuals diagnosed with pancreatic and gastric cancer [ , ].

## Endothelial Dysfunction
Endothelial dysfunction was identified in cancer patients and leads to an increase in the hypoxic microenvironment created by tumour cells [ ]. Levels of platelet-activating factor (PAF) and prostaglandins are elevated, which facilitate the activation of neutrophils and promote their adhesion to endothelial cells [ ]. Hypoxia promotes the upregulation of ADP and VWF, leading to activation and increased binding of platelets, hence enhancing thrombus formation by an indirect route [ ]. Moreover, damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) are release by tumour cells through cell stress pathways [ ]. DAMPs encompass a variety of protein types, including histones, High Mobility Group Box 1 (HMGB1), S100 proteins, and heat shock proteins [ ].
Furthermore, alongside the procoagulant mechanisms, a considerable drop in coagulation inhibitors, including protein C, free protein S, thrombomodulin, and antithrombin III have been observed in pancreatic cancer patients [ ].

## Prophylaxis and Treatment of Cancer-Associated Thrombosis

## CAT Prophylaxis
Extensive research has been undertaken in the clinical domain about the prevention of thrombosis in cancer patients. A range of risk factors, including as hospitalization, surgical interventions, and variables relating to the tumour, patient, and cancer treatment, must be meticulously assessed when formulating a preventive approach.
A comprehensive analysis of randomized trials assessed the efficacy of anticoagulation in hospitalized cancer patients, comparing low molecular weight heparin or fondaparinux with no anticoagulation. In the cohort of 307 cancer patients involved in these trials, the incidence of VTE was not significantly diminished, as evidenced by a pooled relative risk of 0.91 (95% CI 0.21–4.0) with anticoagulation [ ].
In studies including hospitalized cancer patients, low molecular weight (LMW) heparin or fondaparinux proved advantageous for the prevention of VTE in high-risk individuals [ ]. LMW heparin has demonstrated a reduced incidence of hemorrhage relative to DOACs in clinical trials and observational studies including cancer patients [ – ].
The duration of thromboprophylaxis must be determined in conjunction with the selection of an anticoagulant. A meta-analysis involving 3655 cancer patients assessed the best period of thromboprophylaxis. Prolonged prophylaxis did not markedly decrease the risk of VTE compared to conventional duration thromboprophylaxis. The incidence of clinically significant bleeding was twice as high with prolonged duration [ ].
Up to 40% of patients have postoperative VTE, which is more common in cancer patients than in the general population [ – ]. Consequently, persons with cancer should be regarded as at elevated risk for the development of postoperative VTE.
It is advisable to provide perioperative VTE prevention with an anticoagulant for the majority of cancer patients undergoing surgery. A few exceptions are people having minor surgery or people who shouldn't be receiving mechanical thromboprophylaxis [ ].
A meta-analysis of 16 randomized trials including perioperative cancer patients and VTE indicated that LMW heparin, unfractionated heparin, and fondaparinux had comparable efficacy in preventing perioperative VTE [ ].
Three distinct trials examined the advantages of extended postoperative anticoagulation, revealing a substantial decrease in the incidence of VTE after four weeks of enoxaparin or dalteparin in contrast to one week. None of the trials demonstrated elevated bleeding in the extended anticoagulation cohort [ – ].

## CAT Treatment
The main strategy for treating CAT is anticoagulation therapy. When treating cancer-associated VTE, oral Factor Xa inhibitors have demonstrated superior efficacy over warfarin and LMW heparin, the previous standard of therapy. Additionally, the use of Factor Xa inhibitors in these patients—such as apixaban, edoxaban, and rivaroxaban—has grown popular among clinicians. Warfarin, unfractionated heparin (UFH), and DOACs have all been compared to LMW heparin (Table  ).
 For patients with cancer-associated VTE, LMW heparin has long been known to be an effective monotherapy. Different LMW heparins have not been directly compared with one another in any study. Nonetheless, the most often utilized medications in clinical practice are enoxaparin or dateparin (Table  ).
 

## Risk Assessment and Biomarkers of CAT
Several risk assessment models (RAMs) have been developed for identifying cancer patients with a high risk of thrombosis [ ]. RAMs include different variables and are divided into several scores [ ]. The first score is the Khorana, the first assessment for VTE in cancer patients. In this score, five variables include cancer types, leukocyte count, platelet count, haemoglobin level, and body mass index. These variables should be assessed before starting the chemotherapy treatment for malignant patients [ ]. The Khorana score is the most common tool recommended by several guidelines because it was established after retro and prospective studies for more than 35,000 patients [ ]. The Vienna score encompasses the five variables in the Khorana score along with D-dimer and soluble P-selectin levels which were added to increase the positive predictive values (PPV) for CAT [ ]. The Prophylaxis Thromboembolic Events Chemotherapy (PROTECHT) uses the original five variables plus consideration of the use of platinum-based or gemcitabine-based chemotherapy [ ]. These two RAMs scores were not validated in clinical practice; however, it is a beneficial tool for the early detection of VTE among cancer patients [ ].
As well as these RAMs, complete blood count (CBC) can be of use in assessing risk. elevated leukocytes and platelets count with a reduction in the haemoglobin levels before starting the chemotherapy treatment are markers of association of VTE in cancer patients [ ]. It is a cost-effective prognostic method because the complete blood count (CBC) test is available in most general practitioner clinics [ ]. In addition, the elevated fibrin degradation product level detected by the D-dimer test is also a marker for the high risk of VTE in malignant patients [ ]. Some studies suggest that the P-selectin and tissue factor-derived microparticles are also markers for predicting VTE in cancer patients [ ].

## Conclusion
The correlation between cancer and thrombosis is well recognized. Patients are susceptible to both arterial and venous thrombosis. However, VTE is more prevalent and may frequently manifest before the diagnosis of malignancy. Age, gender, ethnicity, thrombophilia genes, and other genetic disorders are the primary risk factors for CAT patients. The location, stage, and subtypes of cancer are also regarded as risk factors. CAT increases the difficulty of managing these individuals, necessitating hospitalization, surgery, and treatment, thereby increasing the overall cost of care. Following cancer, VTE and ATE constitute the second leading cause of mortality in cancer patients, underscoring the need to investigate their correlation. Despite advancements in our comprehension of CAT, significant aspects of the underlying mechanisms, particularly cancer-type-specific changes, remain to be elucidated. The processes PAI-1 in cancer-related thrombosis, CP in activating FX, and the interplay between primary cells implicated in thrombosis and TF-positive MPs are poorly understood.
Clinical practice is evolving swiftly in the prevention and management of CAT, with numerous therapeutic alternatives emerging for both prevention and treatment in cancer patients. The universal approach centered on LMWH is being supplanted by personalized decision-making because of new evidence regarding the efficacy and safety of DOACs. The advice for primary prevention using DOACs is a novel development supported by most major guidelines, signifying a paradigm shift in this context. Nonetheless, this also entails increased complexity and novel obstacles. Physicians must meticulously assess the optimal antithrombotic medication, the risks of bleeding and recurrence, possible drug interactions, and patient preferences to establish the most effective strategy for each individual. Furthermore, enhancements in risk stratification are necessary; continuing research is focused on biomarkers, such as genetic profiles. It is essential to translate the clinical trial and research outcomes into health system-based practice approaches to maximize the benefits of all these findings from previous research and reduce the public health burden of VTE linked to cancer.
