## Abstract

## Aim
Venous thromboembolic events (VTEs) are relatively common adverse surgical complications. Extended VTE prophylaxis for 4 weeks is recommended after colorectal cancer surgery, but its use in inflammatory bowel disease surgery lacks high‐quality evidence. This retrospective study aimed to assess and characterize VTEs within the first 30 days after ileal pouch–anal anastomosis (IPAA) procedures and subtotal colectomies (STCs) for ulcerative colitis (UC).

## Methods
All patients who underwent IPAA for UC between 1 January 2017 and 31 December 2021 were included. VTE rates after IPAA, in‐hospital or at‐home occurrences, utilization of in‐hospital thromboprophylaxis, and prescribed anticoagulant treatment were evaluated. Retrospectively, the same variables were analysed if patients of the cohort underwent STC before the IPAA construction.

## Results
In all, 204 patients underwent IPAA (61.8% men, 73% laparoscopic), with an average hospital stay of 6.8 days. Among them, 116 patients underwent STC prior to IPAA. Thirteen patients (6.3%) experienced VTEs after IPAA, with 76.9% (10/13) of cases occurring during hospitalization and under adequate thromboprophylaxis. The VTE rate after STC was 10.3% (12/116), with 58.2% (7/12) occurring in hospital and under appropriate thromboprophylaxis. No reoperations or mortality were attributed to thrombotic events. The type and duration of anticoagulant treatment varied considerably.

## Conclusion
The VTE rate after IPAA for UC was 6.3%, with the majority of events occurring in hospital and under adequate thromboprophylaxis. These findings suggest that routine use of extended VTE prophylaxis in our cohort may not be supported. Further research is needed to clarify the optimal VTE prophylaxis strategy for inflammatory bowel disease surgery.

## What does this paper add to the literature?
This paper contributes to the literature by examining the use of extended venous thromboembolism prophylaxis after inflammatory bowel disease surgery. Based on a large sample of ileal pouch–anal anastomosis procedures from a high‐volume inflammatory bowel disease centre, the study suggests that routine use of extended venous thromboembolism prophylaxis may not be necessary as most events occur in hospital.

## INTRODUCTION
Venous thromboembolic events (VTEs), including portomesenteric vein thrombosis (PMVT), deep venous thrombosis (DVT) and pulmonary embolism (PE), represent a significant postoperative morbidity and mortality risk following major abdominal surgery [ , ]. Various factors contribute to VTE occurrence, including surgical trauma, systemic inflammation in inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), dysregulation in inflammatory cytokines, tumour necrosis factor activity and elevated prothrombotic agent levels [ , ].
The reported VTE rates after colorectal surgery within the first 4 weeks range from 0.9% to 9% [ , , ]. To mitigate this risk, in‐hospital thromboprophylaxis with unfractionated heparin or low molecular weight heparin is recommended [ , ]. Extended VTE (eVTE) prophylaxis, particularly with low molecular weight heparin for 30 days postoperatively, has gained attention as a preventive measure [ ].
While eVTE prophylaxis strongly recommended for colorectal cancer (CRC) surgery, the recommendations for IBD surgery have been conflicting despite the higher incidence of VTEs in this population [ , , ]. The American Society of Colon and Rectal Surgeons guidelines strongly advocate eVTE prophylaxis for 4 weeks following CRC surgery, but the recommendation for IBD surgery lacks high‐quality evidence [ ].
In the light of being a high‐volume IBD centre, we conducted a retrospective study within our ulcerative colitis (UC) patient cohort undergoing pelvic pouch surgery. The study aimed to determine the rate of VTEs, their presentation (in hospital or at home) and the administered thromboprophylaxis. These findings will inform our institutional policy on routine eVTE prophylaxis in this specific population.
By analysing these data as part of a quality improvement project, we intend to contribute valuable insights into the necessity of routine eVTE prophylaxis for UC patients undergoing pelvic pouch surgery. The results will aid in optimizing our institutional practices and enhancing patient care.

## METHODS

## Study design and population
We conducted a retrospective review of a prospectively maintained institutional IBD database. Prior to the start of the study, the protocol was approved by the Research Ethics Board.
All patients diagnosed with UC or indeterminate colitis (IC) who underwent an ileal pouch–anal anastomosis (IPAA) construction between 1 January 2017 and 31 December 2021 were included in the study. Patients with familial adenomatous polyposis or other polyposis syndromes were excluded.
Patients who underwent IPAA construction were categorized into two groups: (1) those who underwent a total proctocolectomy (TPC) with IPAA construction and (2) those who had previously had a subtotal colectomy (STC) followed by completion of proctectomy (CP) with IPAA construction; within this last subgroup of patients (CP + IPAA), patients were included if the STC was performed at either our own centre or an outside centre and subsequently referred to our centre for IPAA construction.

## Primary and secondary outcomes
The primary outcome of this study was to determine the proportion of patients who had a VTE following IPAA construction for UC or IC over the last 5 years at our centre. For this outcome, VTE was defined and recorded as per the National Surgical Quality Improvement Program (NSQIP) criteria for VTE, which include a new diagnosis of a new venous thrombosis (superficial or deep), confirmed by a duplex, venogram, CT scan or any other definitive imaging modality (including direct pathology examination such as autopsy) with anticoagulation therapy prescribed, if this therapy was not contraindicated, within 30 days after the primary procedure [ ].
The secondary outcomes were to determine the proportion of patients who had a VTE following STC at our centre, the proportion of all patients receiving appropriate VTE prophylaxis while in hospital, the number of VTEs occurring while in hospital or at home within the 30 days following surgery, the number of patients treated for VTEs and the type and duration of anticoagulation treatment for VTEs.

## Data collection
Demographic, clinical and surgical variables were collected from the institutional IBD database and the patient's electronic medical record. These data were abstracted into a study database specifically designed for the study.
Demographic, clinical and surgical variables are shown in Table  . Specific variables related to VTEs were history of VTEs before initial surgery (i.e., STC or TPC), number of postoperative days to the time of diagnosis, whether the VTE occurred as an inpatient or outpatient, if the VTE was treated and the type and duration of anticoagulation treatment received.
 

## Statistical analysis
Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the results. Quantitative variables were summarized as mean/median with the corresponding standard deviation (SD) or range. Categorical variables were summarized as frequency or percentage. A specific database with all our variables was built for the purpose of this study, and data were analysed with IBM SPSS Statistics (version 28).

## RESULTS
Over the study period, 232 patients underwent an IPAA construction at our centre. Twenty‐eight patients were excluded (22 familial adenomatous polyposis and six with incorrect or missing information). The final cohort included 204 patients: 27 underwent a TPC + IPAA and 177 underwent a CP + IPAA. Within this last subgroup, 61 patients underwent STC at an outside institution (34.4%) and 116 underwent STC at our institution. Of these 116 patients, 44% (51 patients) underwent emergency surgery due to fulminant colitis refractory to medical treatment or toxic megacolon. Among 27 patients who underwent TPC + IPAA, 20 underwent surgery due to dysplasia and seven had a confirmed preoperative cancer diagnosis. In total, the number of resectional surgeries related to UC/IC analysed was 320 procedures (116 STC + 204 IPAA construction, Figure  ).
 Demographic analysis showed that 61.8% (126/204) were men, and the mean age of diagnosis was 27.2 years (SD 13.07 years). The mean age at the time of STC and IPAA construction was 33.2 years (SD 13.2 years) and 35.8 years (SD 13.3 years), respectively. Nine per cent (19/204) had a diagnosis of primary sclerosing cholangitis and three patients a previous liver transplant, and 5.9% (12/204) had other extraintestinal manifestations of their UC/IC. Thirty‐one per cent of the cohort (63/204) had at least one comorbidity unrelated to UC (Table  ).
The IPAA construction was laparoscopically assisted in 73% of the cases (149 patients), and 9.8% (20 patients) were converted to open surgery (conversion rate of 11.8%). The remaining 35 patients (17.2%) underwent an open procedure. A combined laparoscopic and transanal approach was performed in 18.6% (38 patients) of the cohort. A protective loop ileostomy was performed in 93.6%, in 164 patients after CP + IPAA (92.7%) and 27 patients after TPC + IPAA (100%). The morbidity rate after IPAA construction, according to the Clavien–Dindo classification, was 48% (98 patients) in the entire cohort, 83/177 patients after CP + IPAA (46.8%) and 15/27 patients after TPC + IPAA (55.5%). Most of these events were Clavien–Dindo Class I and II (36.2%), with fewer Class IIIa (3.9%), IIIb (5.9%) and IV (2%). No mortalities were observed. In total, the median length of stay was 6 days (range 48, 2–50 days) after IPAA construction, 5 days after CP + IPAA (range 48, 2–50 days) and 7 days after TPC and IPAA (range 22, 3–25 days). The median length of stay after STC was 6 days (range 35, 3–38 days). The reoperation and re‐hospitalization rates were 6.9% and 16.7% after IPAA construction and 6.9% and 11.2% after STC (Table  ).
 

## Venous thromboembolic events after IPAA construction
Of 204 patients who underwent an IPAA construction, 13 (6.3%) developed a VTE during their first 30 days after surgery. Eight of these 13 patients met the two criteria as per the NSQIP guideline for VTEs (new VTE and anticoagulation prescribed) and five met just one (new VTE). Thirty per cent (62 patients) of the cohort required postoperative imaging studies within 30 days after surgery (61 CT scan of the abdomen and pelvis with intravenous contrast +/− oral and pouch contrast, seven CT PE chest and eight Doppler ultrasound). The reasons for these studies are shown in Table  and most commonly were done for suspected leak/collection or prolonged ileus. A VTE was diagnosed in 13 patients (VTE rate after IPAA 6.3%), of which 12 patients had a PMVT and one had a DVT (Figure  ). None of the patients in the cohort was diagnosed with PE. Of the VTEs, 76.9% (10/13) occurred while the patient was in hospital and 92.3% (12/13) received appropriate pharmacological thromboprophylaxis (Table  ). None of the patients who had a VTE required surgery related to this event or died. As can be seen in Table  , 61.5% of patients diagnosed with VTE received anticoagulation treatment (8/13). The type and duration of this treatment were highly variable. Among untreated patients with VTEs, there were no complications or unfavourable outcomes related to the progression of their VTE.
 

## Venous thromboembolic events after STC
Of 116 patients who underwent an STC at Mount Sinai Hospital, Toronto, 12 patients (10.3%) developed a VTE during their first 30 days after surgery. Ten of these 12 patients met the two criteria as per the NSQIP guideline for VTE (new VTE and anticoagulation prescribed) and two met just one (new VTE). Thirty‐five per cent (41 patients) required postoperative imaging while in hospital (40 CT scan of the abdomen and pelvis with intravenous contrast +/− oral and rectal contrast, eight CT PE chest and four Doppler ultrasound). The most common reasons for these imaging studies were for suspected rectal stump leak and prolonged ileus (Table  ). A VTE was diagnosed in 12 patients (VTE rate after STC 10.3%), of which seven patients had a PMVT, three had a PE and two had a DVT (Figure  ). Of the VTEs, 58.2% (7/12) occurred while the patient was in hospital and 100% (12/12) received appropriate pharmacological thromboprophylaxis while in hospital (Table  ). None of the patients who had a VTE required surgery related to this event. As can be seen in Table  , 83.3% of patients diagnosed with VTEs received anticoagulant treatment (10/12), and the type and duration of this treatment were variable. Among patients not receiving treatment, there were no complications related to the progression of their VTE.

## Venous thromboembolic events before any surgical procedure (STC, TPC + IPPA construction or CP + IPPA construction)
Five per cent (11/204) of the cohort had a history of at least one VTE, either before STC or before TPC + IPAA. None of these patients had a new VTE after their STC or IPAA construction (Figure  ).

## DISCUSSION
This study evaluated the VTE rates in patients undergoing IPAA or STC due to UC. Our findings revealed a VTE rate of 6.3% (13/204) and 10.3% (12/116) within the first 30 days post‐surgery in patients undergoing IPAA construction and STC respectively at Mount Sinai Hospital, Toronto. Following IPAA construction, almost all VTEs were PMVT (92%), and most of these events occurred while in hospital and receiving appropriate VTE prophylaxis, suggesting that additional factors beyond the duration of prophylaxis may contribute to VTE development. The study highlights the variability in the type and duration of anticoagulation treatment prescribed for VTEs. This suggests that there may be a lack of standardized guidelines for VTE management in UC patients undergoing surgery. Developing evidence‐based recommendations for VTE prophylaxis and treatment in this patient population is crucial to optimize outcomes and reduce the risk of complications.
Our study has similar results in terms of VTE rates after UC‐related surgeries compared to other publications in previous years. During the last decade, several retrospective studies and a few systematic reviews assessed the risk and rate of VTEs in IBD and CRC surgeries. In one of the last systematic reviews, McKechnie et al. found 30 studies that describe VTE rates after colorectal surgeries in IBD patients ranging from 0.6% to 8.9%, with a median follow‐up of 1 month [ ]. These studies show the most significant risk of VTEs during the first two postoperative weeks [ ]. In 2014, Allaix et al. published 1069 laparoscopic resections in non‐metastatic CRC and IBD patients. This study found 37 patients with symptomatic PMVT (3.5%) after surgery, and multivariate analysis showed that TPC and postoperative intra‐abdominal septic complications were independent predictor factors of PMVT and led to increased length of stay (14.8 vs. 7.4 days, P < 0.001) [ ]. Gu et al. published their results on 521 patients who completed surgical treatment for UC and found a symptomatic PMVT after surgery in 7% of their patients that led to an increased hospital stay with multivariate analysis showing that TPC + IPAA construction was associated with a significantly greater risk of PMVT than CP + IPAA construction (OR 4.9, P = 0.003) or total abdominal colectomy (OR 3.5, P = 0.011) and the use of preoperative steroids as an independent risk factor until 1 month before surgery (OR 5.8, P = 0.006) [ ]. More recently, while eVTE prophylaxis in patients undergoing surgery for CRC has been strongly recommended and has been widely adopted into clinical practice as part of the standard of care, recommendations for eVTE prophylaxis following surgery for IBD are much less clear and, as a result, eVTE prophylaxis following IBD surgery has not been widely adopted into clinical practice. This is mostly due to the lack of high‐quality evidence limited by small sample sizes and heterogeneous IBD populations. Our study seems to support these findings as the majority of VTEs in patients who underwent an IPAA construction occurred while in hospital prior to discharge.
Another important finding of this study was the variation in the prescribed treatment for VTEs. Given this variation, guidelines to treat VTEs following IBD surgery are probably warranted given the long‐term impact of a post‐thrombotic syndrome after DVT or the potential worsening functional outcomes with IPAA construction following PMVT that significantly affect the quality of life.
Several factors could potentially explain the occurrence of VTEs despite adequate thromboprophylaxis. First, individual patient characteristics, such as comorbidities, genetic predisposition and immobility, may play a significant role in VTE pathogenesis. These factors could contribute to a higher baseline risk of developing VTEs, making patients more susceptible even with appropriate preventive measures in place.
Furthermore, the complexity and dynamic nature of VTE formation necessitates a multifaceted approach to prevention. Although thromboprophylaxis is undoubtedly crucial, eVTE prophylaxis in IBD surgery is still a question with no right answer, especially if we add to our equation the costs and medical risks of this approach. Extended thromboprophylaxis should be considered as part of a broader strategy encompassing risk stratification, early identification of high‐risk patients and implementation of comprehensive measures to promote mobility and minimize immobility‐associated risks.
This study has several limitations. First, while we used the NSQIP algorithm and criteria to detect VTEs, the majority of events were identified based on CT scans that were done for other reasons. Since CT scans and Doppler ultrasound were not done on all patients, it is impossible to know the true incidence of VTEs following pelvic pouch procedure; however, it is most likely that fewer VTEs would be found in asymptomatic patients, and therefore the actual VTE rate is probably higher than we have reported. This finding would be unlikely to change our results that the risk of VTEs is relatively low following IPAA and suggests that eVTE prophylaxis may not be necessary. Another limitation is the study's retrospective design, which may have introduced selection bias and limitations in data collection. Additionally, the study was conducted at a single centre, which may limit the generalizability of the findings.
Efforts should be directed towards identifying high‐risk patients, implementing strategies to promote mobility, and exploring additional modifiable risk factors that can be targeted to reduce the incidence of VTEs. By adopting a multifaceted approach, healthcare providers can effectively address the persistent challenge of VTE prevention during hospitalization. Further prospective studies involving larger, multicentre cohorts are needed to validate these results and provide more robust evidence.
Finally, a total of 320 IBD surgeries were performed in the study period, comprising 204 IPAA procedures and 116 STCs among these patients. Notably, of these 320 IBD surgeries, only eight patients (2.5%) experienced a VTE after discharge within the initial 30 postoperative days in a non‐hospital setting. This finding may underscore the remarkably high number needed to treat with eVTE prophylaxis to prevent a single VTE episode occurring outside the hospital environment. The observation that only a small proportion of patients developed VTEs at home following discharge further highlights the challenges associated with implementing routine eVTE prophylaxis.
This study has significant implications for our clinical practice, suggesting that the routine use of eVTE prophylaxis may not be justified, given the low incidence of VTEs observed in the post‐discharge period. The clinical decision‐making process regarding the implementation of eVTE prophylaxis should consider the potential benefits weighed against the considerable number of patients who would need to undergo such treatment to prevent a single VTE episode occurring outside the hospital.

## CONCLUSION
The result of this study showed a VTE rate of 10.3% and 6.3% following STC and IPAA construction, respectively. Most of these VTEs were PMVT and occurred in hospital before discharge while receiving appropriate venous thromboprophylaxis. These findings may suggest that eVTE prophylaxis may not be necessary following pelvic pouch surgery or even following STC. These findings have important implications for our clinical practice and guideline development. Future randomized controlled trials to assess the effectiveness of eVTE prophylaxis following IBD surgery and guidelines for treating VTEs and PMVT following IPAA surgery are warranted.
