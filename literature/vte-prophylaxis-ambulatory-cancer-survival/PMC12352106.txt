## Abstract

## Background
Transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR) thrombosis is a known complication, but its association with vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) inhibitors has not been previously reported.

## Case summary
A 69-year-old male with previous TAVR developed new-onset prosthetic valve dysfunction after receiving bevacizumab for age-related macular degeneration. Echocardiography revealed moderate aortic stenosis with significantly elevated gradients compared to baseline. Following discontinuation of bevacizumab and initiation of systemic anticoagulation, valve function normalized at three-month follow-up.

## Discussion
This first reported case of TAVR thrombosis potentially linked to bevacizumab therapy demonstrates a temporal relationship between drug administration and valve dysfunction, with subsequent resolution upon drug discontinuation and anticoagulation. Clinicians should be aware of potential thrombotic complications when prescribing bevacizumab to patients with bioprosthetic valves and maintain regular echocardiographic surveillance. Prompt anticoagulation and medication adjustment may reverse valve dysfunction. Further studies are needed to determine a causal relationship between TAVR thrombosis and VEGF inhibitors.

## Introduction
Transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR) treats severe aortic stenosis, but data on long-term durability, including valve degeneration and thrombosis, remain limited. Prosthetic valve thrombosis is influenced by anatomic, procedural, and pharmacological factors, per Virchow’s triad. Bevacizumab, a vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) inhibitor, used in cancers and retinal diseases, is associated with arterial and venous thrombosis but has not been linked to TAVR thrombosis.

## Case presentation
A 69-year-old male presented for a routine follow-up in August 2023. He had a history of surgical aortic valve replacement (#25 Carpentier-Edwards tissue valve), left atrial appendage ligation, and single vessel coronary artery bypass grafting in 2009 and was lost to follow-up ( ). Ten years later, he presented with palpitations and dyspnoea. A transthoracic echocardiogram (TTE) revealed severe bioprosthetic aortic valve stenosis [area 0.6 cm2, mean pressure gradient (PG) 56.7 mmHg, maximum velocity (Vmax) 4.67 m/s, dimensionless index 0.19]. Left heart catheterization showed intermediate stenosis of the mid-left anterior descending coronary artery [not significant by fractional flow reserve (FFR)/instantaneous wave-free ratio (IFR)] and patent vein graft to an occluded right coronary artery (RCA). Deemed high risk for surgery due to high STS operative mortality risk, hostile mediastinum from previous sternotomy, and frailty, he underwent TAVR using Medtronic Core Valve (29R) in 2019. Transthoracic echocardiogram after 1 month ( , , ) showed improved valve function (area 1.73 cm2, mean PG 10 mmHg, Vmax 2.05 m/s, and dimensionless index 0.49), which remained stable for three years.
 At his 4-year outpatient follow-up, he was asymptomatic, but examination revealed a 4/6 systolic murmur at the second right intercostal space. The only recent medication change was bevacizumab eye injections for age-related macular degeneration within the past 30 days. Transthoracic echocardiogram ( , , and ) showed moderate aortic stenosis (area 1.3 cm2, mean PG 28.3 mmHg, and Vmax 3.28 m/s), significantly changed from the prior year’s TTE. Differential diagnoses included structural valve deterioration, non-structural valve deterioration, prosthetic valve thrombosis, and prosthetic valve endocarditis, but his clinical picture was consistent with thrombosis.
 He was then admitted to the hospital. Laboratory tests were unremarkable. A transoesophageal echocardiogram (TEE) showed thickened aortic valve leaflets without restriction ( , , and ), elevated mean gradient of 21.2 mmHg, and a Vmax of 2.95 m/s, suggesting microthrombi but no large thrombus or regurgitation. He was continued on aspirin and started on intravenous heparin, then transitioned to warfarin with subcutaneous low molecular weight heparin bridging, and discharged home. Bevacizumab was discontinued.
 Three months later, TTE ( , , and ) showed normalized valve function (mean PG 6.8 mmHg and Vmax 1.68 m/s). The patient was treated with warfarin and aspirin for six months.
 The patient initially chose to discontinue bevacizumab, but resumed it after vision worsened. Once his valve stabilized, he was switched to apixaban to allow easier interruption of anticoagulation for eye injections if needed. Lifelong anticoagulation was deemed necessary due to the high thrombosis risk with a valve-in-valve and that in the event of valve deterioration, the only treatment option would be a high-risk surgical valve replacement. Although aspirin isn’t routinely recommended alongside anticoagulation for TAVR thrombosis, it was continued with apixaban due to the patient’s coronary artery disease/TAVR history, low bleeding risk, and decision to continue bevacizumab. He has had no further thrombotic events.

## Discussion
Valve thrombosis can be classified by timing as early (<3 months), late (3 months–1 year), or very late (>1 year). Thrombo-embolic risk is highest in the first three months after TAVR as new intimal coverage of the valve frame leaflets requires adequate time. Other classification systems divide it into subclinical or clinical based on symptomatology and echocardiographic parameters.
The incidence of clinical valve thrombosis (CVT) following TAVR is ∼1.2%. Adrichem et al. recommend oral anticoagulation in patients who developed CVT on antiplatelet therapy, and switching to vitamin K antagonists if the patient is already on a non-vitamin K oral anticoagulant (NOAC). There are no head-to-head comparisons to support switching between NOACs. In cases with significant haemodynamic changes, parenteral unfractionated or low molecular weight heparin is reasonable. Lifelong anticoagulation is recommended, particularly for valve-in-valve cases, given the elevated risk of thrombosis recurrence after anticoagulation interruption. , 
Our patient, after starting bevacizumab, developed new-onset dysfunction in a previously stable valve. Imaging showed an elevated gradient over 50% above baseline and thickened valve leaflets. He had clinically significant valve thrombosis with stage 2 moderate haemodynamic valve deterioration as evidenced by an increase in mean transvalvular gradient ≥ 10 mmHg (from 11.2 to 28 mmHg) with a concomitant decrease in valve area ≥ 0.3 cm2 (from 1.77 to 1.3 cm2) per the Valve Academic Research Consortium criteria. Following three months of warfarin, aspirin and stopping bevacizumab, the valve function normalized on repeat echocardiogram.
According to Dangas et al., prosthesis thrombosis can be diagnosed with clinical, imaging, or pathological evidence. A key imaging criterion is a ≥10 mmHg mean gradient regression after anticoagulation, supporting our diagnosis of very late TAVR thrombosis. While this could have been spontaneous bioprosthetic valve thrombosis per Arnold et al,. the fact that valve thrombosis occurred within a month of starting bevacizumab in a 3-year stable valve supports bevacizumab-associated thrombosis.
The exact mechanism of bevacizumab-induced TAVR thrombosis and prosthetic dysfunction remains unclear. Bevacizumab reduces nitric oxide and prostaglandin I2 production, inducing endothelial cell death, exposing procoagulant phospholipids and potentially leading to thrombosis. 
Bevacizumab has been associated with major cardiovascular events and arterial and venous thrombosis. A study on cancer patients showed hypertension as the most common adverse event, followed by vascular thrombo-embolic events. There is also a higher risk of arterial thromboembolism, in particular myocardial ischaemia and stroke. In non-cancer patients treated for diabetic macular oedema, bevacizumab increases all-cause mortality, especially with frequent injections. Thus, minimizing injection frequency or considering alternative treatments is advisable, especially for patients with stroke or myocardial infarction history.
A meta-analysis of 9557 patients found that bevacizumab increased venous thromboembolism compared to ranibizumab without increasing overall cardiovascular mortality. Suzuki et al. reported a lung cancer patient on bevacizumab, who developed an abdominal aortic thrombus, which resolved after anticoagulation and discontinuing the drug.
Our report’s strength lies in documenting the first known instance of bevacizumab-associated TAVR thrombosis in a bioprosthetic aortic valve, supported by clinical timeline, imaging, and gradient improvement after drug withdrawal.
However, the single-case nature limits generalizability and the lack of randomized controlled data makes it difficult to establish definitive causality between bevacizumab and TAVR thrombosis. Additionally, limited follow-up restricts insight into long-term outcomes, and it remains unclear whether improvement was due to bevacizumab withdrawal, anticoagulation, or both. Further studies are needed to clarify whether one or both interventions were responsible for the observed improvement in valve function. Lastly, the lack of similar cases makes comparing and validating findings challenging.

## Conclusion
This case highlights a potential link between bevacizumab and TAVR thrombosis, as valve function improved after discontinuing bevacizumab and starting anticoagulation. It underscores the importance of annual TTE follow-ups post-TAVR and bevacizumab’s possible complications in patients with bioprosthetic valves.
