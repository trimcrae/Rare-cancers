## Abstract

## Aims
The incidence of arterial thromboembolism (ATE) among ambulatory cancer patients varies by primary tumour site. However, it is unclear whether this alters the benefit-to-harm profile of prophylactic anticoagulation for ATE prevention. Therefore, we systematically evaluated the efficacy and safety of anticoagulants for ATE prevention among ambulatory cancer patients according to the primary tumour site.

## Methods and results
We conducted a systematic review using Medline, Embase, SCOPUS, and CENTRAL, and included randomized trials comparing prophylactic anticoagulation to no anticoagulation among ambulatory cancer patients who initiated tumour-directed systemic therapy. The incidence of symptomatic ATE (acute ischaemic stroke, acute myocardial infarction, or peripheral artery occlusion) and major bleeding, as well as risk differences (RDs) attributable to anticoagulation, were meta-analysed by primary tumour site using random-effects modelling. We included 10 randomized controlled trials with 9875 patients with follow-up ranging from 3.3 to 68 (median 6.6) months. While prophylactic anticoagulation did not reduce ATE risks overall (RD −0.49%; 95% CI −0.49% to 0.01%; I2 = 0%), it conferred a protective effect among pancreatic cancer patients (RD −3.2%; 95%CI −5.7% to −0.8%; I2 = 0%) without a detectable increase in major bleeding (RD −1.4%; 95% CI −4.6% to 1.8%; I2 = 0%). Prophylactic anticoagulation was not associated with ATE risk reduction in other tumour sites.

## Conclusion
Based on available evidence, prophylactic anticoagulation did not reduce ATE risk among ambulatory cancer patients overall. However, we observed a lower incidence of ATE among pancreatic cancer patients randomized to receive anticoagulation. Prophylactic anticoagulant use to reduce ATEs in pancreatic cancer should be evaluated in future research.

## Introduction
Thrombosis is a leading cause of death among ambulatory patients with cancer, with an attributable risk that is second only to progression of cancer itself. While malignancy is a well-established risk factor for venous thromboembolism, its impact on arterial thromboembolism (ATE) remains less characterized. In a recent systematic review and meta-analysis of 11 randomized controlled trials, prophylactic use of anticoagulation was not associated with a statistically significant reduction in ATE risk but an increased risk of major and non-major bleeding. However, the incidence of cancer-associated ATE is heterogeneous by the site of primary malignancy, and it is unknown whether tumour-related factors alter the benefit-to-harm profile of anticoagulants for ATE prevention. Therefore, we performed an updated systematic review and meta-analysis to characterize the efficacy and safety of anticoagulants on ATE prevention among ambulatory patients with cancer with stratification by primary tumour sites.

## Methods
This review is reported in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis (PRISMA) Statement. The protocol was registered in PROSPERO (CRD42022315125), and the present analysis was a pre-specified secondary objective of this project.

## Eligibility
The methods have been reportedly in full previously. In brief, a systematic review of published manuscripts, meeting abstracts, and trial registrations was performed using Medline, Embase, SCOPUS, and the Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials (CENTRAL) from inception to 22 April 2024, with input from an information specialist. We included randomized controlled trials comparing oral or parenteral anticoagulants at any dosing to no anticoagulant treatment among ambulatory patients with solid tumours or lymphoma who were receiving or initiating tumour-directed systemic therapy. Studies were eligible for inclusion if they reported symptomatic ATEs, defined as acute ischaemic stroke, acute myocardial infarction, or peripheral artery occlusion, as an efficacy outcome or adverse event with stratification by primary tumour sites.
We excluded studies with any of the following: (i) observational studies, reviews, abstracts, trial registries, protocols, conference proceedings, and unpublished studies; (ii) haematologic malignancies (except lymphoma) and primary brain tumours in ≥5% of the trial population, as these patients have different risks of thromboembolism or anticoagulant-associated bleeding; , (iii) studies comparing anticoagulants to antiplatelet agents (however, studies that enrolled individuals on antiplatelet agents at baseline were eligible); (iv) studies with duration of follow-up less than 3 months; and (v) patients on anticoagulation at enrolment (to exclude studies on secondary prevention of cancer-associated thrombosis).

## Study selection, data extraction, and quality assessment
Abstract and full text screening were performed in duplicate by three authors working independently, with discrepancy adjudicated by initial discussion and an additional author if needed. Search results, including abstract and full text publications were uploaded into Covidence (Alfred Hospital, Melbourne, Australia). Data were extracted in duplicate using a pre-piloted form in Excel 2016. We contacted study authors for unpublished data or individual patient data if potentially eligible publications did not provide tumour site-specific results on ATE or major bleeding. For two included trials, we obtained access to individual patient data through data contributors Sanofi and GSK through Vivli, Inc. and performed subgroup analyses. Two reviewers independently used the Cochrane Risk of Bias (RoB 2) tool to complete the risk of bias assessment at the individual study level for ATE and major bleeding, and discrepancies were resolved by consensus.

## Study outcomes
The primary outcome was ATE, consisting of acute ischaemic stroke, acute myocardial infarction, or peripheral arterial occlusion as defined by the individual studies. The secondary outcome was major bleeding as defined by the individual studies. All outcomes were extracted using the intention-to-treat population, with the exception of major bleeding, for which we used the modified intention-to-treat or per-protocol population, as reported by the study.

## Data analysis
First, we described the incidence of tumour site-specific ATE and major bleeding over the follow-up duration of each study. The baseline incidence of ATE and major bleeding were determined for patients randomized to the non-anticoagulated, control arms of eligible studies. For sites of primary malignancy with ≥1 randomized trial, we performed transformation of proportions via the Stuart and Ord method, followed by inverse-variance weighted random effects meta-analysis using the DerSimonian and Laird model.
Next, we generated a risk difference (RD) for all outcomes with anticoagulant use. Given clinical heterogeneity across included studies (e.g. anticoagulant type, intensity, and treatment duration), we used the DerSimonian and Laird random-effects model to determine weighted averages for measures of effect, with calculation of 95% confidence intervals (CI). Statistical heterogeneity was assessed through visual inspection of effect size estimates and 95% CI and via calculation of the I2 statistic. Two-tailed α of <0.05 was considered statistically significant. Where included trials contained more than one treatment group (e.g. different anticoagulant dosages), these were collapsed into a single interventional arm. We conducted a priori sensitivity analyses focusing on studies without high risk of bias based on the RoB 2 tool.
We used StatsDirect Version 3 (Cheshire, UK) to perform meta-analysis of ATE and major bleeding incidence and Review Manager (RevMan version 5.4.1, The Cochrane Collaboration, 2020) to conduct the meta-analysis of RDs.

## Results

## Search results
Of 10 130 records identified by the literature search, 7162 were screened by title and abstract following removal of duplicates. Of these, 6984 were excluded as irrelevant based on title and abstract screening, with 178 records proceeding to full-text review. Of these, 168 records were excluded primarily due to the absence of reporting on ATEs and conference abstracts only (Figure  ). These exclusions included five studies that reported ATE events in the overall trial without disaggregated data stratified by the primary site of malignancy, despite attempts to contact the study authors. Ten records were therefore eligible for inclusion in the systematic review and meta-analysis.
 

## Characteristics of included studies
Of the 10 articles identified with 9875 participants, the median follow-up duration was 6.6 months (range: 3.3–68;  ). Five studies collected ATEs in a passive fashion (e.g. as an adverse event) rather than as primary or secondary outcomes. , , Five of the studies were open-label, , , and the remaining five were blinded. , , Prior ATEs were captured in three studies. , , One study reported baseline use of antiplatelet agents in 23% of randomized patients. Definitions of ATE and major bleeding reported by the included studies are shown in , .
 Five of the 10 studies were judged to be at high risk of bias for the outcome of ATE using the RoB 2 tool (   , ), , , primarily in the outcome measurement domain. Specifically, these studies reported VTE or all-cause mortality as their efficacy outcomes, with passive collection of ATEs as adverse events. On the other hand, only one study was identified as being high-risk of bias for the outcome of major bleeding ( , ), which was specifically collected as a safety endpoint in all included studies.

## Site of primary malignancy
Eight studies, including a total of 8460 patients, reported ATEs with stratification by the site of primary malignancy. , Meanwhile, all 10 studies included in the systematic review reported major bleeding events with stratification by tumour site. The incidence of ATE among patients not receiving prophylactic anticoagulation was highest in pancreatic, prostate, lung, and stomach/gastroesophageal junction tumours ( ). Prophylactic anticoagulant use was not associated with lower ATE risk compared to no anticoagulation in the overall population (RD −0.49%; 95% CI −0.49% to 0.01%; I2 = 0%). However, anticoagulant use was associated with a significant risk reduction in ATE among patients with pancreatic cancer (RD −3.20; 95% CI −5.65 to −0.76, P = 0.01, I2 = 0%,  and  ), when compared with no anticoagulation. There was no statistically significant reduction in ATE risk with anticoagulant use in other tumour sites. The risk of major bleeding was not increased in any of the tumour sites assessed with anticoagulant use ( and  ), including patients with pancreatic cancer (RD −1.40%, 95% CI −4.59 to 1.79, P = 0.31, I2 = 0%). Heterogeneity in effect size was low among pooled estimates with two or more studies ( ). Testing for interaction between the primary site of malignancy and ATE demonstrated a significant effect for pancreatic and non-pancreatic cancers (χ2 = 4.93, P-value for interaction = 0.03), whereas no statistically significant interaction was detected for major bleeding (χ2 = 2.12, P-value for interaction = 0.15).

Effect size estimates were consistent when we restricted the meta-analysis of the primary outcome to five studies at low or moderate risk of bias ( , ).

## Discussion
In this systematic review and meta-analysis of 10 randomized controlled trials with over 9000 participants, we observed a significant variation in the incidence of ATE and major bleeding among ambulatory patients with cancer undergoing chemotherapy depending on the primary tumour site. Patients with pancreatic cancer derived a 3.2% absolute risk reduction in ATEs with use of anticoagulation, without an increase in major bleeding. However, we did not observe similar risk reductions in ATE among patients with other tumour sites.
While our previous study-level systematic review and meta-analysis did not demonstrate a benefit to anticoagulant use for prevention of ATE in the overall population of ambulatory patients with cancer, the analysis was limited by the absence of specific cancer-related subgroup data to identify patients who are most likely to benefit. In the current study, we identified patients with pancreatic cancer as an important subgroup in whom anticoagulation might be effective in ATE prevention. Increased ATE risk among patients with pancreatic cancer is attributable to haemostatic changes observed in this condition, including circulation of tissue factor-containing macrovesicles, activation of factor VII, and release of plasminogen activator inhibitor 1. The 4.2% risk of ATE in the absence of anticoagulation seen in our study is consistent with results of a large administrative database study based in the United States that reported an ATE incidence of 5.7% among patients with pancreatic cancer following the first 6 months of its diagnosis. Among patients with pancreatic cancer-associated VTEs, a prospective multi-centre cohort in Norway reported a 36% cumulative incidence of breakthrough ATEs within first 6 months of therapeutic apixaban treatment, primarily manifest as acute ischaemic strokes. On the other hand, we did not detect an excess risk of major bleeding with prophylactic anticoagulant use in this population. Taken together, patients with pancreatic cancer appear to be a key population at high risk of cancer-associated ATE (in addition to the known VTE risk), in whom the benefit-to-harm ratio of prophylactic anticoagulant use specifically for ATE prevention should be evaluated in large prospective studies.
International guidelines recommend the use of low-dose anticoagulants among outpatients receiving systemic anticancer therapy at high risk of developing VTE. , However, there is a paucity of systematic data syntheses on the prevention of cancer-associated ATE. Establishing the effect of anticoagulants on ATEs is crucial, as formalized risk assessment and appropriate pharmacologic prophylaxis for VTE prevention remains underutilized in ambulatory patients with cancer. For example, structured tools for VTE risk assessment are used in only 5–13% of ambulatory patients receiving chemotherapy, , with even lower uptake of outpatient anticoagulant use among high-risk patients. Therefore, identifying specific patient subgroups in whom anticoagulants confer protection against cancer-associated ATEs, in addition to VTEs, would further clarify the net clinical benefit of primary pharmacologic thromboprophylaxis.

## Limitations
To our knowledge, this is the first systematic review and meta-analysis to evaluate the impact of anticoagulants on ATE prevention among ambulatory patients with cancer, with a specific focus on cancer sites. By restricting our search and synthesis to randomized controlled trials, we aimed to reduce the risk of confounding. However, our study is subjective to several limitations. First, five randomized controlled trials that assessed the efficacy of anticoagulation on ATE could not be included in our meta-analysis as they did not provide tumour-specific subgroup data. However, these studies reported low numbers of events overall, and are therefore unlikely to have influenced results significantly. Second, several studies involved passive capture of ATE (often as adverse events) rather than an active surveillance for this as an outcome at each study visit. This is relevant, as prior studies had shown discordant rates of thrombotic complications between those actively captured as an efficacy outcome compared to an adverse event. However, this was accounted for in the risk of bias tool, and a sensitivity analysis removing studies with high risk of bias demonstrated consistent results. Nonetheless, the open-label design used in half of included studies increases the risk of ascertainment bias between anticoagulant and control groups, while lack of individual patient data introduces the risk of ecological fallacy associated with study-level meta-analyses. Third, there is an inherent risk of type 1 error in the context of multiple comparisons between anticoagulant and no anticoagulant groups across primary sites of malignancy. However, the effect estimates of studies within the pancreatic cancer subgroup were consistent with each other, with low heterogeneity overall. Fourth, we did not restrict our analysis based on anticoagulant type (parenteral and oral), acknowledging that there is paucity of data around use of LMWH for ATE prevention compared to DOACs or warfarin outside of malignancy. However, LMWH was tested against warfarin in the ACE trial that randomized 496 patients with atrial fibrillation undergoing transesophageal echocardiogram-guided cardioversion to enoxaparin or warfarin. Compared to warfarin, patients randomized to the enoxaparin arm had numerical but non-significant reductions in thromboembolic events and major bleeding. Furthermore, the 2022 European Society of Cardiology guidelines on cardio-oncology recommend the use of LMWH as second-line agent among patients with active cancer and atrial fibrillation for ATE prevention. Nevertheless, additional research on the interaction between anticoagulant type and primary site of malignancy is warranted. Finally, despite the efforts of a meta-analysis, it may remain underpowered to evaluate whether anticoagulants are effective to prevent ATEs in several high-risk tumour sites, such as prostate and gastric/gastroesophageal junction tumors. While the median follow-up duration of 6 months observed in our meta-analysis is typically sufficient to detect benefits and risks of anticoagulant use on VTE outcomes in cancer, this may be too brief to accrue differences in ATE events. Adequately powered studies to determine the efficacy and safety of anticoagulation for ATE prevention in these specific tumour sites are therefore an urgent area of future research direction.

## Conclusions
In summary, our systematic review and meta-analysis of randomized trials with over 9000 participants revealed that anticoagulant use did not reduce the risk of ATEs in the overall ambulatory cancer population. However, anticoagulant use prevented ATEs without excess major bleeds in patients with pancreatic cancer. Given the heterogeneity in trial characteristics and types of anticoagulants used, the efficacy of anticoagulation to reduce the excess ATE burden among ambulatory patients with cancer at low bleeding risk should be further evaluated in adequately powered, prospective trials.
