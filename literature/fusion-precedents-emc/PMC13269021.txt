## Abstract

## Background
Superficial ALK-rearranged soft tissue tumors are rare mesenchymal neoplasms showing heterogenous morphology and clinical behavior. They usually present as slowly enlarging nodules in young adults and can mimic other spindle cell tumors. While some ALK-altered mesenchymal neoplasms can be classified into defined WHO categories (e.g., inflammatory myofibroblastic tumor, epithelioid fibrous histiocytoma, and a few others), the nosology of many tumors has not yet been delineated, and they are currently reported descriptively. A case of a young man with a three-year follow-up is presented, providing prognostic insights into this entity.

## Case description
A healthy 23-year-old male presented with a purplish mass on the plantar left foot that had enlarged over one year. MRI showed a well-defined lesion without bone involvement. Histopathology revealed sheets of bland round to oval cells within myxohyaline stroma containing focal pseudorosette-like hyalinized nodules and increased mitotic activity. Immunohistochemistry demonstrated strong cytoplasmic ALK expression with heterogeneous expression for CD34, and S100. Focal EMA and pancytokeratin expression were noted. Next-generation sequencing confirmed a MYH10::ALK fusion. After excision with positive margins, re-excision revealed residual tumor as well. Further surgery was declined, and the patient developed metastatic disease during follow-up.

## Discussion
This case broadens the spectrum of acral ALK-rearranged unclassified mesenchymal tumors and identifies MYH10 as a potential fusion partner in metastatic disease. Although these tumors are typically indolent, features such as increased mitotic activity may indicate more aggressive behavior. Diagnosis requires integrated histologic, immunophenotypic, and molecular assessment. This case highlights the metastatic potential of ALK-rearranged unclassified mesenchymal neoplasms, particularly those exhibiting a tyrosine kinase phenotype (co-expression of S100 and CD34), supporting their classification within the intermediate, rarely metastasizing category according to soft tissue tumor stratification principles. It further emphasizes the need for heightened clinical vigilance and thorough surgical management, which may influence long-term outcomes.

## Introduction
Soft tissue tumors with ALK gene rearrangements represent a recently recognized and heterogeneous group of mesenchymal neoplasms, with emerging evidence delineating their clinicopathologic and molecular spectrum ( – ). These tumors may present in young adults as slowly enlarging, often purplish nodules or masses, and can be challenging to diagnose due to their rarity and overlapping features with other spindle cell neoplasms ( , ).
Histopathologically, ALK-rearranged tumors may either fit within a defined WHO category (e.g., inflammatory myofibroblastic tumor, epithelioid fibrous histiocytoma) or represent an unclassified, highly heterogeneous group of cutaneous and subcutaneous tumors. These tumors frequently display a myxoid or myxohyaline stroma with spindle to ovoid cell morphology, or epithelioid features with or without high-grade cytology. These tumors have been referred to by variable and often vaguely defined terms: some have been grouped with epithelioid fibrous histiocytomas, others described as superficial ALK-altered myxoid spindle cell neoplasms (SAMS), and many additional cases have been reported descriptively as exhibiting epithelioid or myxoid morphology. Immunophenotypically, they are characterized by predominantly cytoplasmic ALK expression without additional lineage-specific marker expression. However, a subset of tumors shows co-expression of CD34 and S100 protein, analogous to other tyrosine kinase fusion–driven neoplasms, such as those harboring NTRK fusions ( , ). The biological behavior of these tumors remains incompletely defined. Limited follow-up data suggests a generally indolent course; however, occasional recurrences and metastases have been reported, underscoring the need for careful clinicopathologic correlation and long-term surveillance. This case contributes to the growing body of literature on ALK-fused mesenchymal neoplasms, particularly those arising in acral locations, with several years of follow-up offering valuable insight into long-term behavior and clinical outcomes. ## Case description
A healthy 23-year-old male with no relevant medical history presented with a 2.5 x 2.5 cm tumor on the left foot. The sharply demarcated, purplish mass had a firm, rubbery consistency and was located on the plantar aspect of the first metatarsophalangeal joint ( ). The patient first noticed the mass approximately one year ago, and it had progressively enlarged, causing increasing discomfort while walking. The delay in seeking medical attention was attributed to the patient’s apprehension about visiting healthcare professionals. MRI revealed no immediate contact between the mass and the underlying bone ( ). Surgical resection was performed, followed by histopathological examination ( , ).

## Diagnostics and therapy
Microscopic examination demonstrated tumor involvement of the dermis, subcutaneous tissue, and fascia, including focal invasion into adjacent skeletal muscle. Microscopic involvement of the surgical margins was noted. The lesion exhibited densely cellular proliferation of round to oval cells with large vesicular nuclei and poorly delineated cytoplasm. Repeated cell condensation around small blood vessels was identified, with no evidence of multinucleated cell forms. Mitotic activity was substantially increased, with up to 7 mitoses per 2 mm ( ) and small areas of necrosis were present. Additionally, sclerotic stromal areas containing smaller epithelioid cells were observed. Molecular analysis was performed using, among others, next generation sequencing, identifying an in-frame MYH10::ALK fusion gene. Subsequently an immunohistochemical examination was carried out, which demonstrated a strong cytoplasmic positivity for ALK (clone 1A4), in line with the detected ALK gene fusion. Furthermore, the tumor cells were positive for S100 and CD34 with focal expression of pancytokeratin and with detectable vessel invasion.
The biological behavior and prognostic implications of the tumor were uncertain, and after interdisciplinary discussion, a recommendation for a repeat surgical resection was made, to which the patient consented following multiple consultations. The procedure took place three months after the initial operation, uncovering a small, residual tumor adhering to the dermis during intraoperative examination. Histopathology once again identified microscopic residual tumor medially, laterally and dorsally. Despite thorough consultation, the patient decided against further resection, opting instead for a wait-and-see approach under the supervision of an oncologist with periodic MRI exams of the foot. Following an initially unremarkable clinical and radiological course with no evidence of macroscopic tumor recurrence, the patient presented twelve months later with new-onset left inguinal lymphadenopathy. Imaging revealed suspicious lymph nodes in the groin, as well as lesions of the hip involving the piriformis and iliopsoas muscles. Staging further demonstrated likely malignant pulmonary lesions in the left upper lobe and the right lower lobe. Subsequent inguinal lymphadenectomy confirmed lymph node metastasis of the tumor (pT1 pN1 cM1). The patient declined surgical removal of the other lesions, including those in the hip musculature, and systemic therapy with crizotinib 500 mg daily was initiated, followed by scheduled follow-up examinations. After an initial phase of stable disease, imaging at 10 months revealed progression of the hip musculature lesions, prompting treatment with locoregional radiotherapy. Recent imaging demonstrates a substantial reduction in the size of these lesions, with no further signs of disease progression. shows the timeline of clinical events.

## Discussion
The present case of a 23-year-old male with an acral soft tissue tumor harboring a MYH10::ALK fusion exemplifies the evolving clinicopathologic spectrum and the biological potential of ALK-rearranged mesenchymal neoplasms. These tumors are increasingly recognized in superficial and acral locations, typically presenting in young to middle-aged adults as slowly enlarging, well-demarcated masses with variable symptoms. They may be misdiagnosed due to their rarity and overlapping features with other entities, such as spindle cell neoplasms ( – , , ). Approximately 71% of superficial tumors arise in the lower extremities, followed by the upper extremities (14%), with less frequent involvement of the trunk and head and neck regions ( ). presents a range of differential diagnoses based on clinical and histopathological features that warrant consideration and exclusion.
 Histologically, ALK-rearranged tumors exhibit a broad morphologic spectrum, ranging from well-defined entities (e.g., inflammatory myofibroblastic tumor, epithelioid fibrous histiocytoma) to unclassified, heterogeneous neoplasms characterized by spindle to epithelioid cell patterns, myxoid or myxohyaline stroma, and variable cytologic features, spanning from predominantly bland-appearing to, in rare cases, frankly malignant cytology. The latter may show increased mitotic activity and focal necrosis, as seen in this case. Pediatric cases of fibrosarcoma-like spindle cell tumors of the soft tissue and kidney harboring ALK fusions further support the concept that ALK-rearranged spindle cell neoplasms constitute a broader, molecularly defined entity with overlapping clinicopathologic features across age groups and anatomic sites ( ). Immunophenotypically, co-expression of ALK, CD34, and S100, as seen in this case, is characteristic of a subgroup of unclassified ALK-rearranged tumors, that are part of the emerging family of tyrosine kinase fusion-positive mesenchymal neoplasms. They occur across all age groups (infancy to elderly) and can involve superficial and deep soft tissue as well as viscera ( , ). MYH10 is a recurrent partner in ALK-rearranged tumors and preserves the tyrosine kinase domain, which is relevant for both diagnosis and potential targeted therapy ( , , ). The identification of a MYH10::ALK fusion by next-generation sequencing is a notable finding in this case. To date, only three tumors harboring this fusion have been reported across two prior series; none occurred at an acral site, and follow-up was limited to less than 10 months in two cases without reported adverse events. No recurrences or metastases have been documented specifically for superficial tumors with MYH10::ALK fusion in the available literature ( , ).
Molecular characterization is important for precise diagnosis and prognostication in ALK-associated neoplasms, particularly in recurrent or refractory cases ( – ). ALK immunohistochemistry alone cannot reliably distinguish true ALK fusion-driven tumors from morphologic or immunophenotypic mimics. Overly sensitive ALK immunohistochemistry protocols may yield false-positive or equivocal results due to background staining in non-ALK-rearranged tumors, and aberrant ALK expression has been increasingly recognized in neoplasms carrying non-ALK gene fusions, necessitating confirmatory molecular testing to accurately identify ALK gene rearrangements ( ). Next-generation sequencing enables precise identification of fusion partners, which may have therapeutic implications ( ). In this context, ALK inhibitors, such as crizotinib and alectinib, have demonstrated efficacy in select cases of ALK-rearranged mesenchymal tumors, particularly in advanced or metastatic disease ( ).
Prognostically, most superficial ALK-rearranged tumors appear to follow an indolent course, with low rates of recurrence and metastasis, particularly in cases lacking high-grade features or deep tissue involvement. However, cases with increased mitotic activity, necrosis, or deep extension may exhibit more aggressive behavior, underscoring the need for careful histopathologic assessment and long-term follow-up. The presence of vascular invasion in this case was a further risk factor, as documented in select reports ( , , ).
In summary, this case highlights the importance of integrating clinicopathologic, immunohistochemical, and molecular data in the diagnosis and management of acral ALK-rearranged soft tissue tumors. Ongoing surveillance and interdisciplinary discussion are warranted, given the potential for aggressive behavior, as illustrated in this case.

## Translated from German
“A few years ago, I noticed a slowly growing lump under my foot, which I initially tried to ignore. I was afraid of seeing a doctor and kept hoping it would disappear on its own. Even though I felt that something was not right, I continued with my daily life until I could no longer fit properly into my shoe and every step became painful.
After the tumor was removed, I hoped for quick reassurance, but instead I was faced with a lot of uncertainty. I was told that the findings were unusual and that specialists needed more time to understand what this meant. Waiting for answers was not easy, as I did not know whether the tumor was harmless or something that could become dangerous. This made it difficult to feel at ease, even though I felt physically well and the wound was healing without problems.
Following a second operation, I was told that further tissue might still need to be removed. This was overwhelming. The idea of potentially facing who-knows-how-many surgeries was frightening, especially since I was recovering well and was hoping to get back to work. I decided for close monitoring instead and tried to focus on returning to a normal routine. For some time, things were indeed stable, and I was working full-time again.
Later, I noticed new symptoms, and suddenly the situation felt more serious. The possibility that the disease had spread terrified me, but making decisions about further treatment still was not easy, particularly when surgery was discussed again, which I did not want to pursue. I am currently taking medication and have undergone radiotherapy for the spots in my hip, which have since become much smaller. Although uncertainty remains, I do not want this condition to control my life. At present, the situation feels manageable, and I am confident that if new challenges arise in the future, we will be able to deal with them as well.
