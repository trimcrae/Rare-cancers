xmlns:xlink="http://www.w3.org/1999/xlink" content-type="pdf" xlink:href="10.1177_10915818261417235.pdf"/><abstract><p>Oligonucleotide therapeutics (ONTs) are a growing class of nucleic acid–based therapies with the potential to treat a variety of diseases through several different mechanisms of action (MoA). The most prevalent MoA entails the inhibition of therapeutically relevant levels of protein expression via mRNA degradation, exemplified by numerous approved ONTs. ONTs have unique physicochemical properties, pharmacokinetic characteristics, and MoAs that are distinct from other drug classes. The result can be a disconnect between the administered dose, plasma drug concentrations, pharmacodynamics, and toxicity. Thus, ONT development poses unique scientific and regulatory challenges that are not fully addressed by current International Council for Harmonisation (ICH) guidelines. The complexity of ONT development has recently been recognized with the acceptance of the new ICH topic proposal “ICH S13—Nonclinical Safety Evaluation of Oligonucleotide-based Therapeutics.” In preparation for the ICH S13 Step 2 public consultation period, the International Consortium for Innovation and Quality in Pharmaceutical Development (IQ) DruSafe ONT Working Group convened to identify and discuss key considerations for ONT development, and to communicate areas where regulatory clarity and harmonization are most needed. Specifically, these include strategies to assess off-target toxicities, safety pharmacology, general toxicity studies, First-In-Human dose selection, genotoxicity studies, reproductive and developmental toxicity studies, carcinogenicity assessments, and the appropriate use of a surrogate molecule in development. The intended goals of this paper are to provide a means for communicating input towards the ICH process and to provide a resource for assisting with the public review of the draft ICH S13 Guidance.</p></abstract><kwd-group><kwd>oligonucleotide therapeutics</kwd><kwd>nonclinical id="sec1-10915818261417235"><title>Introduction</title><p>Oligonucleotide therapeutics (ONTs) are a mature class of drugs that enable highly specific pharmacologic activity. ONTs are composed of synthetic single- or double-strands of nucleic acids designed to target specific genetic sequences, thereby modulating gene expression and/or function to treat various diseases, including but not limited to genetic disorders, cancers, viral infections, cardiovascular disease, and neurological conditions. The authors considered the development of the following ONTs while crafting the recommendations encompassed in this manuscript: antisense oligonucleotides (ASOs), small interfering (si)RNAs, antagomirs, RNA editing oligos, miRNA mimics, and small activating (sa) RNAs. In general, the authors did not consider ONTs such as (g)RNA for DNA editing, DNA/RNA vaccines, and coding mRNAs during development of this manuscript due to the differences in mode of action.</p><p>Through ONT interaction with messenger RNA (mRNA), pre-mRNA, or other molecular targets, ONTs alter the expression of a specific disease-relevant protein target(s) or variants, thus enabling highly targeted treatment approaches.<sup><xref rid="bibr1-10915818261417235" ref-type="bibr">1</xref>,<xref rid="bibr2-10915818261417235" ref-type="bibr">2</xref></sup> In general, ONTs are cleared rapidly from systemic circulation, yet have long pharmacodynamic (PD) effects in tissue.<sup><xref rid="bibr3-10915818261417235" ref-type="bibr">3</xref>-<xref rid="bibr5-10915818261417235" ref-type="bibr">5</xref></sup> The physicochemical properties of ONTs may require localized administration, such as intrathecal injection for central nervous system (CNS) disorders, intravitreal injection for ocular disease, or inhalation for lung-targeted therapies. When administered systemically (e.g., subcutaneous or intravenous injection), specialized delivery strategies are often utilized to enhance ONT uptake into target tissue and cell type; these strategies include small molecule (e.g., N-acetyl galactosamine (GalNAc)), large molecule (e.g., transferrin receptor 1 (TfR1)-targeting antibody) conjugation, or the use of lipid nanoparticle encapsulation.<sup><xref rid="bibr6-10915818261417235" ref-type="bibr">6</xref>,<xref rid="bibr7-10915818261417235" ref-type="bibr">7</xref></sup></p><p>Unlike conventional pharmaceuticals, ONTs do not fall neatly into small or large molecule drug categories. ONTs mostly are similar to biologics as they have long lasting PD and mirror naturally occurring biological mechanisms that are highly specific. However, ONTs are synthetically manufactured and typically include chemical modifications to enhance their stability, potency, and/or durability.<sup>
<xref rid="bibr8-10915818261417235" ref-type="bibr">8</xref>
</sup> An industry survey sponsored by the European Federation of Pharmaceutical Industries and Associations (EFPIA) concluded that the industry largely develops ONTs in accordance with the International Council for Harmonisation (ICH) M3(R2) guidance<sup>
<xref rid="bibr9-10915818261417235" ref-type="bibr">9</xref>
</sup> while also referencing ICH S6(R1)<sup>
<xref rid="bibr10-10915818261417235" ref-type="bibr">10</xref>
</sup> guidance where appropriate.<sup>
<xref rid="bibr11-10915818261417235" ref-type="bibr">11</xref>
</sup> The regulatory landscape for ONTs remains complex with differences in regional expectations, requiring careful nonclinical assessment and strategic interactions with health authorities. With advancements in the field, including the clinical approval of numerous ASOs and siRNAs, ONTs represent a promising frontier in precision medicine, offering hope for patients with previously untreatable or difficult-to-manage conditions.<sup><xref rid="bibr12-10915818261417235" ref-type="bibr">12</xref>,<xref rid="bibr13-10915818261417235" ref-type="bibr">13</xref></sup> To date, there is no internationally harmonized regulatory guidance specific to ONTs; however, the Japanese Pharmaceuticals and Medical Devices Agency (PMDA) issued a guideline in 2020 for ONTs.<sup>
<xref rid="bibr14-10915818261417235" ref-type="bibr">14</xref>
</sup> Largely, the development of ONTs is based upon current industry practices, published data from industry sponsors, white papers from cross-industry working groups (e.g., Oligonucleotide Safety Working Group (OSWG) and EFPIA Oligonucleotide Nonclinical Working Group), and consideration of publicly available regulatory review of approved molecules.</p><p>Recognizing the maturation of ONTs as a therapeutic modality, and considering the unique aspects to their nonclinical development, the ICH has initiated a new safety topic “ICH S13—Nonclinical Safety Evaluation of Oligonucleotide-based Therapeutics” in early 2024, with the final concept paper endorsed in November 2024.<sup>
<xref rid="bibr15-10915818261417235" ref-type="bibr">15</xref>
</sup> Also in November 2024, the FDA published their draft guidance “Nonclinical Safety Assessment of Oligonucleotide-Based Therapeutics Guidance for Industry.”<sup>
<xref rid="bibr16-10915818261417235" ref-type="bibr">16</xref>
</sup> To provide industry perspectives on the development of ICH S13, the International Consortium for Innovation and Quality in Pharmaceutical Development (IQ) DruSafe ONT Working Group (ONT WG) established a dedicated workstream. This initiative was designed to identify key considerations for ONT development and communicate areas where regulatory clarity and harmonization are most needed. Members of the ONT WG met frequently to discuss topics and recommendations related to the nonclinical safety evaluation of ONTs.</p><p>In this manuscript, the ONT WG utilized diverse industry experiences and expertise from WG members, existing regulatory guidelines, scientific publications, and industry white papers to develop key recommendations and considerations related to nonclinical safety evaluation and development of ONTs (<xref rid="fig1-10915818261417235" ref-type="fig">Figure 1</xref>). Note that ONTs for advanced cancer indications are considered outside the scope of this manuscript and would be addressed by ICH S9.<sup>
<xref rid="bibr17-10915818261417235" ref-type="bibr">17</xref>
</sup> This manuscript is not intended as a review of ONT toxicities, class effects, platform findings, or industry best practices. The authors direct readers to the following comprehensive reviews on these subjects<sup><xref rid="bibr1-10915818261417235" ref-type="bibr">1</xref>,<xref rid="bibr7-10915818261417235" ref-type="bibr">7</xref>,<xref rid="bibr18-10915818261417235" ref-type="bibr">18</xref>,<xref rid="bibr19-10915818261417235" ref-type="bibr">19</xref></sup> and other key references within this manuscript. The intended goals of this paper are to provide a means for communicating input towards the ICH process and to provide a resource for assisting with the public review of the draft ICH S13 Guidance.<fig position="float" fig-type="figure" id="fig1-10915818261417235" orientation="portrait"><label>Figure 1.</label><caption><p>The IQ DruSafe ONT Working Group (ONT WG) utilized diverse industry experience and expertise from its members, existing regulatory guidelines, scientific publications, and industry white papers to develop key recommendations and considerations related to nonclinical safety development of ONTs. These recommendations and considerations aim to focus ICH S13 authoring on topics where regulatory guidance and consistency are most needed.</p></caption><graphic xmlns:xlink="http://www.w3.org/1999/xlink" position="float" orientation="portrait" xlink:href="10.1177_10915818261417235-fig1.jpg"><?image-name 10.1177_10915818261417235-fig1.jpg?><?image-size 99384?><?image-md5 483534c804911b5274ebbe017af1f14b?><?image-image-server-status LOAD_COMPLETED?><?image-original-height 2912?><?image-original-width 2899?><?image-scaled-height 727?><?image-scaled-width 724?><?image-cloudpmc-urn urn:cdn:blobs/3991/13053874/483534c80491/10.1177_10915818261417235-fig1.jpg?><?thumb-name 10.1177_10915818261417235-fig1.gif?><?thumb-size 11167?><?thumb-md5 36125cc887a01a6ec7265b13f8d4582a?><?thumb-image-server-status NEVER_LOAD?><?thumb-scaled-height 100?><?thumb-scaled-width 100?><?thumb-cloudpmc-urn urn:cdn:blobs/3991/13053874/36125cc887a0/10.1177_10915818261417235-fig1.gif?></graphic></fig></p></sec><sec id="sec2-10915818261417235"><title>Key Considerations and Recommendations</title><p>The development of ONTs presents an opportunity to promote a scientifically driven risk assessment approach and the application of the 3Rs (replacement, reduction, and refinement of animal use in research) principles. The ONT WG encourages the ICH S13 expert working group (EWG) to consider these principles while drafting the guidance. Existing guidance should be leveraged where possible, and opportunities for Q&amp;A or addendums to existing guidance should be considered where appropriate. One important factor to be considered for the ICH S13 guidance is the heterogeneity among ONTs such as siRNAs, ASOs, micro (mi)RNAs, and aptamers that may impact safety assessment with regard to: mechanisms of action (MoA); delivery system; dosing schedule and formulation; and applicability, or lack thereof, of <italic toggle="yes">in vitro</italic> methodologies conducted routinely with small molecule therapeutics. Additionally, given the potential for lack of ONT sequence homology and/or conjugated targeting moiety cross reactivity between humans and toxicology test species (particularly in rodents), the design, characterization, and applicability of pharmacologically active surrogate molecules for nonclinical development should be addressed. Specific considerations and recommendations by the ONT WG for the conduct of off-target assessment, safety pharmacology, general toxicology, genotoxicity assessment, developmental and reproductive toxicity, carcinogenicity, and the appropriate use of a surrogate molecule are described below.</p><sec id="sec3-10915818261417235"><title>Hybridization-Dependent Off-Target Analysis</title><p>One potential safety risk for ONTs is the off-target hybridization-dependent effects, that is, ONT-induced modulation of unintended transcripts. With the long duration of PD for most current ONTs (weeks to months), identification and avoidance of relevant off-target effects is a key area for nonclinical safety assessment of ONTs. A subcommittee of the OSWG (Oligonucleotide Safety Working Group) recently published updated industry recommendations for off-target identification and risk assessment.<sup>
<xref rid="bibr20-10915818261417235" ref-type="bibr">20</xref>
</sup> The steps proposed in the white paper are<list list-type="simple" id="list1-10915818261417235"><list-item><p>1. Identification of potential off-target interactions via <italic toggle="yes">in silico</italic> and transcriptomics analyses.</p></list-item><list-item><p>2. Focus analysis on cell types where relevant activity of the ONT can be demonstrated.</p></list-item><list-item><p>3. Experimentally confirm <italic toggle="yes">in silico</italic> predicted off-targets in human cell models and establish <italic toggle="yes">in vitro</italic> margins to the <italic toggle="yes">In vitro</italic> on-target activity.</p></list-item><list-item><p>4. Assess potential consequences of hitting off-target transcript(s) of concern.</p></list-item></list></p><p>The IQ DruSafe WG encourages the ICH S13 EWG to consider both the recommendations outlined in the OSWG white paper<sup>
<xref rid="bibr20-10915818261417235" ref-type="bibr">20</xref>
</sup> and to include information on the appropriate timing of these assessments in relation to development.</p></sec><sec id="sec4-10915818261417235"><title>Use of Animal Active Surrogate Molecules</title><p>Where no pharmacologically relevant species is available for a candidate ONT, the use of a rodent surrogate oligonucleotide (active against the ortholog target) can be employed to assess the effects of exaggerated pharmacology on a given endpoint (e.g., general toxicology or developmental toxicity). Surrogates may also be considered for reproductive testing as a method to reduce NHP use. The IQ DruSafe WG encourages ICH S13 EWG to consider the following points for use of a surrogate. If a surrogate molecule with activity in a rodent model is used to assess potential pharmacology-mediated toxicities, then it should only be used for hazard identification due to potential limitations that the surrogate presents such as differences in hybridization-dependent off-targets. The surrogate should be administered at a minimal dose level and dose frequency to demonstrate pharmacological activity that is predicted for the clinical candidate in humans. Dosing at suprapharmacological dose levels could result in species-specific off-target hybridization and other toxicities that may be associated with the surrogate ONT and have no clinical relevance. Assessing pharmacokinetics and toxicokinetics of surrogates may be unnecessary if PD is demonstrated in toxicology studies. The ONT WG recommends that the surrogate molecule should have the same chemistry and modifications, and a similar sequence length as the clinical candidate ONT, to maximize its relevance and that the surrogate molecule should be active both in terms of ONT sequence and the targeting moiety (e.g., ligand).</p></sec><sec id="sec5-10915818261417235"><title>Safety Pharmacology</title><p>The ONT WG recommends that the safety pharmacology assessment be integrated into the general toxicology studies when feasible as outlined in ICH S7A and S7B<sup><xref rid="bibr21-10915818261417235" ref-type="bibr">21</xref>,<xref rid="bibr22-10915818261417235" ref-type="bibr">22</xref></sup> and ICH S6(R1).<sup>
<xref rid="bibr10-10915818261417235" ref-type="bibr">10</xref>
</sup> Additionally, the ONT WG agrees that safety pharmacology testing should occur prior to initiating clinical studies and incorporate recommendations from the Safety Pharmacology Subcommittee of the Oligonucleotide Safety Working Group.<sup>
<xref rid="bibr23-10915818261417235" ref-type="bibr">23</xref>
</sup> Given the long PD effects of ONTs, plasma T<sub>max</sub> and maximal PD effects should be considered when selecting timepoints for safety pharmacology assessments in animal studies. Additionally, the ONT WG suggests an <italic toggle="yes">in vitro</italic> human ether-a-go-go-related gene (hERG) assay is not warranted, even for TQT waiver, based on known physiochemical properties of ONTs, a low-risk profile based on available data,<sup><xref rid="bibr24-10915818261417235" ref-type="bibr">24</xref>-<xref rid="bibr26-10915818261417235" ref-type="bibr">26</xref></sup> and in alignment with the PMDA Oligonucleotide guidance.<sup>
<xref rid="bibr14-10915818261417235" ref-type="bibr">14</xref>
</sup></p><p>The ONT WG aligns that a stand-alone <italic toggle="yes">in vivo</italic> cardiovascular assessment study may be needed in a pharmacologically relevant species if a specific risk is known with the target/pathway or if there is demonstrated targeting to the cardiovascular system. The ONT WG does find value in an <italic toggle="yes">in vitro</italic> hERG assay if the target biology (or confirmed off-target activity) suggests the potential for affecting hERG expression or function, or if there were observed QTc prolongation detected during the <italic toggle="yes">in vivo</italic> cardiovascular telemetry study. If an <italic toggle="yes">in vitro</italic> hERG assay is performed, the ONT WG is opposed to determining intracellular drug concentrations as part of this assay due to feasibility limitations, the inability to distinguish between intracellular, membrane-bound, or ion channel–bound drug, and limited interpretability due to a lack of clinical cardiac tissue drug level data.</p><p>In addition, the ONT WG does not recommend testing ONTs in other <italic toggle="yes">in vitro</italic> ion channel or other secondary pharmacology assays (e.g., G-protein-coupled receptors, transporters, and enzymes) as these assays were designed and optimized for low molecular weight compounds. On the other hand the ONT WG sees utility in adding appropriate <italic toggle="yes">in vitro</italic> assays retrospectively to investigate specific safety signals identified in the <italic toggle="yes">in vivo</italic> safety studies.</p></sec><sec id="sec6-10915818261417235"><title>General Toxicology</title><p>The main considerations in designing repeat dose toxicology studies are species selection, route of administration, dose selection, dosing frequency and duration, and recovery period. ONTs are specially designed to target human sequences and therefore could have limited pharmacological activities in some nonclinical species. While many ONTs are administered systemically, local delivery such as intrathecal or intravitreal is relatively common. In general, ONTs are cleared rapidly from systemic circulation, yet have long tissue half-life and associated duration of PD effects.<sup><xref rid="bibr27-10915818261417235" ref-type="bibr">27</xref>-<xref rid="bibr29-10915818261417235" ref-type="bibr">29</xref></sup> Such characteristics warrant different considerations than a standard small molecule or biologics approach. The ONT WG would like to make the following recommendations and highlight areas for consideration for the ICH S13 WG.</p><sec id="sec7-10915818261417235"><title>Species Selection</title><p>ONTs are typically evaluated in a rodent and a non-rodent species, even though ONTs are often not pharmacologically active in rodents.<sup>
<xref rid="bibr11-10915818261417235" ref-type="bibr">11</xref>
</sup> For 3R considerations and leveraging the principles outlined in ICH S6(R1), the ONT WG encourages the ICH S13 EWG to consider scenarios where a single species could be adequate. Some examples include<list list-type="simple" id="list2-10915818261417235"><list-item><p>• Use of a single species in First-In-Human (FIH)-enabling and/or chronic toxicology studies when there is no pharmacologically relevant species and no surrogate is available (e.g., exon skipper).</p></list-item><list-item><p>• Use of a single species in chronic toxicology studies when the sub-chronic studies do not identify adverse toxicity or when the animal toxicities typical for a given class of ONTs are of limited relevance for human risk assessment (e.g., complement activation observed in NHP with phosphorothioate (PS) backbone ASOs).</p></list-item><list-item><p>• Use of a single species when the ONT is conjugated to a targeting moiety (i.e., monoclonal antibody) that binds only a single nonclinical species, is only cross reactive to human, or when the ONT and targeting moiety do not share a cross-reactive species (i.e., there is no single pharmacologically relevant species, as above).</p></list-item></list></p></sec><sec id="sec8-10915818261417235"><title>Dose Selection</title><p>The current industry standard for repeat dose toxicology study design of ONTs is a combination between the ICH M3(R2) and ICH S6(R1) guidances.<sup>
<xref rid="bibr11-10915818261417235" ref-type="bibr">11</xref>
</sup> When setting the top dose in a nonclinical toxicology study, we encourage the ICH S13 EWG to consider the disconnect between systemic pharmacokinetics (PK) and tissue PD. ONTs are generally cleared quickly from the blood compartment and have an extended duration of tissue exposure and prolonged PD effect in tissues.<sup><xref rid="bibr29-10915818261417235" ref-type="bibr">29</xref>-<xref rid="bibr31-10915818261417235" ref-type="bibr">31</xref></sup> Therefore, using a maximum feasible dose or limit dose based on ICH M3(R2) seems inappropriate for this class of drugs and ONT WG encourages the ICH S13 EWG to consider ICH S6(R1) for high dose selection to enable FIH-enabling studies. For ONT therapeutics, high dose selection in repeat dose toxicity studies should follow ICH S6(R1) rather than ICH M3(R2). S6(R1) directs sponsors to use PK/PD to set the high dose as the higher of (i) a dose achieving maximal intended pharmacology in the toxicology species or (ii) a dose providing an approximately 10-fold exposure multiple over the maximum clinical exposure; S6(R1) also notes that further escalation above this range rarely adds useful information. In contrast, M3(R2) was written for small molecules and allows a 50-fold AUC-based margin as an adequacy criterion for high dose. For ONTs, whose pharmacology and toxicity are determined by tissue exposure and intracellular mechanisms with rapid plasma clearance and prolonged PD, AUC-based 50× targets are not mechanistically informative, can be unachievable due to saturable uptake, and increase the likelihood of species-specific artifacts (e.g., NHP-specific complement activation), thereby diluting clinical relevance and increasing animal use. Therefore, S6(R1)’s ∼ 10×/max PD framework provides a scientifically sound and 3Rs-aligned basis for ONT high dose selection, with M3(R2) elements considered only as needed for off-target or chemistry-specific risks.</p><p>The ONT WG has conducted an industry-wide survey on current practices for nonclinical high dose selection, safety margin setting, and HED scaling with plans to publish the results with best practice considerations in 2026. The ONT WG encourages the ICH S13 EWG to consider these data when available.</p></sec><sec id="sec9-10915818261417235"><title>Dosing Frequency and Duration</title><p>ONTs are often dosed less frequently in the clinic compared to other classes of drugs (e.g., once monthly, quarterly, or less frequently).<sup><xref rid="bibr32-10915818261417235" ref-type="bibr">32</xref>,<xref rid="bibr33-10915818261417235" ref-type="bibr">33</xref></sup> Given this unique aspect of many ONTs, the ONT WG encourages the ICH S13 EWG to consider how the dose frequency in repeat dose toxicity studies and the nonclinical dose regimen are used to support prolonged dosing intervals in humans. In addition, the duration of PD effect for some ONTs is extended compared to other drug classes (e.g., ≥ 6 months for some ONTs)<sup><xref rid="bibr34-10915818261417235" ref-type="bibr">34</xref>,<xref rid="bibr35-10915818261417235" ref-type="bibr">35</xref></sup>; therefore, the ONT WG encourages the ICH S13 EWG to consider the need for recovery groups and their duration periods for ONTs in repeat dose toxicology studies. Due to the extended half-life of ONTs in tissue, the ONT WG recommends that the washout of drug from tissues or return to baseline for PD of the target gene is not necessary at the end of the recovery phase. The duration of the recovery phase should be adequate to demonstrate partial recovery of reversible on- or off-target related findings. We further propose that demonstrating recovery may not be necessary for well characterized ONT class effects previously demonstrated to be reversible.</p><p>The ONT WG suggests that total duration of pharmacological activity observed during dosing and recovery phase could be combined to support initiation of longer clinical trials or open label extensions.</p></sec></sec><sec id="sec10-10915818261417235"><title>Combination Toxicology Studies</title><p>The ONT WG recommends that the ICH S13 EWG include guidance for when two or more ONTs are being codeveloped as a combination product. Consistent with ICH guidance M3(R2),<sup>
<xref rid="bibr9-10915818261417235" ref-type="bibr">9</xref>
</sup> in the case where clinical and nonclinical data and pharmacology indicate that additive, unpredicted, or synergistic toxicity are unlikely to occur with the combination, consideration for waiver of a combination toxicity study is warranted. In the case that a combination toxicity study is required, a 90-day repeat dose toxicity study in a single relevant species should be sufficient to support late-stage clinical trials and/or market application of the combination. The primary objective of such a study is to investigate if combination of the ONTs results in unanticipated toxicity at relevant doses. Pharmacological relevance of the individual ONTs in the toxicology species should be considered with respect to the utility of a combination toxicology study. If the ONT is not pharmacologically active in a toxicological species, a pharmacologically active surrogate may be considered to be tested in a combination toxicity study. Pharmacologic response along with relevant human dose and exposure for each individual ONT should be considered when selecting dose(s) and dosing frequency in the combination toxicity study.</p></sec><sec id="sec11-10915818261417235"><title>Genetic Toxicology</title><p>Based on the results of an industry-wide survey conducted by the EFPIA Oligonucleotide Nonclinical Working Group<sup>
<xref rid="bibr36-10915818261417235" ref-type="bibr">36</xref>
</sup> and the work of others,<sup><xref rid="bibr37-10915818261417235" ref-type="bibr">37</xref>-<xref rid="bibr40-10915818261417235" ref-type="bibr">40</xref></sup> there is a lack of positive findings in the core battery of genotoxicity studies with a range of ONT classes and chemistries. ONTs are not DNA-reactive, and classes and chemistries tested to date do not induce mutagenicity or chromosome breaks.<sup><xref rid="bibr36-10915818261417235" ref-type="bibr">36</xref>,<xref rid="bibr38-10915818261417235" ref-type="bibr">38</xref></sup> ONT-mediated genotoxicity could potentially arise if the therapeutic target, or an off-target mRNA, affects DNA repair or cell cycle control. However, this risk can typically be mitigated through target biology and selectivity assessments.<sup>
<xref rid="bibr36-10915818261417235" ref-type="bibr">36</xref>
</sup> Thus, the ONT WG considers the overall genotoxicity risk for the oligonucleotide modality to be very low. It is the recommendation of the ONT WG that for ONT candidates containing only combinations of modifications that have been well characterized for genotoxicity, and without evidence for on-target or potential off-target mechanisms of genotoxicity, the assessment should be informed by prior knowledge and <italic toggle="yes">in vitro</italic> and <italic toggle="yes">in vivo</italic> testing omitted. Where novel modifications are being used within an ONT candidate, the standard battery of tests defined in ICH S2(R1) are considered appropriate to characterize risk.<sup>
<xref rid="bibr41-10915818261417235" ref-type="bibr">41</xref>
</sup> These recommendations are consistent with those presented previously by other industry groups focused on the genotoxicity assessment of ONTs.<sup><xref rid="bibr36-10915818261417235" ref-type="bibr">36</xref>,<xref rid="bibr38-10915818261417235" ref-type="bibr">38</xref></sup> Further, considering the very low genotoxicity risk for the ONT modality, and aligning with the principles of a 3Rs approach, where genotoxicity testing is appropriate, the ONT WG recommend the <italic toggle="yes">in vivo</italic> assessment be incorporated into repeat dose toxicity studies, using the bone marrow micronucleus test. Due to the prolonged tissue half-life of ONTs, it is scientifically justified to perform <italic toggle="yes">in vivo</italic> erythrocyte micronucleus assessments with the dosing regimen used in the repeat dose studies, and daily dosing is not required. Given that ICH S2(R1) limit doses are not typically reached in repeat dose toxicity studies, and the more limited distribution to bone marrow of many ONT types (relative to other organs, such as the liver and kidney), guidance on acceptable dose levels may be required in ICH S13.</p></sec><sec id="sec12-10915818261417235"><title>Developmental and Reproductive Toxicity</title><p>The Health and Environmental Sciences Institute (HESI) Developmental and Reproductive Toxicology (DART) Committee hosted an ONT workshop in 2023 outlining the industry and regulators recommendations for development and reproductive toxicology studies.<sup>
<xref rid="bibr42-10915818261417235" ref-type="bibr">42</xref>
</sup> There was broad consensus in the workshop, across regulators and industry, that sexually mature monkeys should be a model of last resort for assessing DART endpoints with ONTs. This is consistent with previous industry recommendations<sup>
<xref rid="bibr43-10915818261417235" ref-type="bibr">43</xref>
</sup> and the views of the ONT WG. The ONT WG also agrees with the HESI DART Committee that 2 species (rodent and rabbit) should be used during the embryo fetal development study, with pharmacologic activity only needed in a single species as detailed in ICH S5(R3).<sup>
<xref rid="bibr44-10915818261417235" ref-type="bibr">44</xref>
</sup> The ONT WG recommends if the ONT is not pharmacologically active either in a rodent or the rabbit, an active surrogate should be used in addition to the clinical candidate in one or more of the required DART studies. Alternatively, the use of a rodent model expressing the human target may be considered. In the case an ONT is not pharmacologically active in either the rodent or rabbit, and a surrogate is not appropriate, then two species should still be used for the embryo fetal development assessments. As with general toxicity studies, the ONT WG encourages the ICH S13 EWG to consider how the dose levels and dose frequency in DART studies may be used to support the proposed clinical regimen.</p></sec><sec id="sec13-10915818261417235"><title>Carcinogenicity Assessment</title><p>To date, the risk of genetic toxicity and carcinogenicity of ONTs has been low. The ONT WG recommends the carcinogenicity risk assessment of ONTs be conducted in accordance with ICH M3(R2), S1A, and S1B(R1).<sup><xref rid="bibr9-10915818261417235" ref-type="bibr">9</xref>,<xref rid="bibr45-10915818261417235" ref-type="bibr">45</xref>,<xref rid="bibr46-10915818261417235" ref-type="bibr">46</xref></sup> When applicable, the ONT WG highly encourages the use of the ICH S1B(R1)<sup><xref rid="bibr45-10915818261417235" ref-type="bibr">45</xref>,<xref rid="bibr46-10915818261417235" ref-type="bibr">46</xref></sup> addendum weight of evidence approach, particularly for ONTs not pharmacologically active in rats or mice. The ONT WG generally discourages the use of rodent surrogates in carcinogenicity studies based upon the same justification provided in ICH S6(R1)<sup>
<xref rid="bibr10-10915818261417235" ref-type="bibr">10</xref>
</sup> for biotechnology-derived pharmaceuticals, that the use of homologous products in carcinogenicity studies is generally of limited value. Consistent with ICH S1B(R1),<sup>
<xref rid="bibr45-10915818261417235" ref-type="bibr">45</xref>
</sup> potential on-target carcinogenicity risk cannot fully be de-risked with a carcinogenicity evaluation. Thus, a WoE approach is recommended, rather than additional studies with a species-specific surrogate ONT. If a surrogate ONT is deemed necessary to assess carcinogenic potential, the study should be used as hazard identification only and designed to limit the potential for off-target findings (i.e., rodent surrogate–specific off-target gene modulations) not relevant to the lead candidate in humans. Lastly, the ONT WG considers the WoE approach acceptable if there are findings in previously conducted studies that are considered rodent-specific and may result in carcinogenicity signals not relevant to human safety (e.g., injection site–associated tumors, hemangiosarcomas in mice, and liver tumors resulting from chronic hepatocellular injury that has not been observed clinically).</p></sec><sec id="sec14-10915818261417235"><title>Immunotoxicity and Hematologic Effects</title><p>Some ONTs have exhibited immunotoxicity risks through various mechanisms. PS-modified ONTs have shown complement factor H inhibition which drives and activates the alternative complement pathway in both humans and NHPs. However, human factor H is ∼ 3-fold more potent at inhibiting complement activation than NHP factor H. Although complement activation is considered a class effect of this ONT type (and has been observed primarily with ASOs), it has exhibited some sequence specificity.<sup>
<xref rid="bibr47-10915818261417235" ref-type="bibr">47</xref>
</sup> In addition, ONTs can activate toll-like receptors resulting in proinflammatory responses and release of cytokines. The immunostimulatory potential of ONTs has also been shown to be sequence dependent.<sup><xref rid="bibr7-10915818261417235" ref-type="bibr">7</xref>,<xref rid="bibr37-10915818261417235" ref-type="bibr">37</xref>,<xref rid="bibr48-10915818261417235" ref-type="bibr">48</xref>-<xref rid="bibr51-10915818261417235" ref-type="bibr">51</xref></sup> Recent ONT designs typically avoid immunostimulatory motifs and/or include modifications that reduce the potential for immunotoxicity, yet many companies are still performing extensive analyses <italic toggle="yes">in vitro</italic> and <italic toggle="yes">in vivo</italic> that may not be necessary. Given the sequence specificity of many of these effects, it is the recommendation of the ONT WG that immunotoxicity assays be streamlined by focusing on the specific risks associated with the ONT class/type.</p><p>Thrombocytopenia and alterations in coagulation parameters have been noted occasionally in preclinical species and clinical studies administered ONTs, particularly ASOs with PS backbones. The exact mechanism for thrombocytopenia is not fully elucidated, although both immune and non-immune mechanisms have been suggested.<sup>
<xref rid="bibr7-10915818261417235" ref-type="bibr">7</xref>
</sup> Nonetheless, both risks can be characterized in NHP studies by measuring the appropriate parameters (e.g., platelets, fibrinogen, prothrombin time, and activated partial thromboplastin time), although immune-mediated thrombocytopenia can only be fully discharged in later stage clinical trials due to its rarity. The recommendation is that <italic toggle="yes">in vitro</italic> assays are not conducted as a default, but when warranted based on emerging data, in line with the FDA draft guidance.<sup>
<xref rid="bibr16-10915818261417235" ref-type="bibr">16</xref>
</sup></p><p>The ONT WG also suggests that the ICH S13 EWG consider when an anti-drug antibody (ADA) assessment is needed during repeat dose toxicology studies for ONTs. Measurement for the presence of ADA should be considered when significant changes in toxicokinetic profile, PD response, or toxicity are observed. To facilitate these assessments, samples may be proactively collected and stored.</p></sec></sec><sec sec-type="conclusion" id="sec15-10915818261417235"><title>Conclusion</title><p>In conclusion, the key aspects of nonclinical safety evaluations for ONTs have been highlighted, and the ONT WG recommends these to be considered by the ICH S13 EWG while drafting the guidance. The ONT WG recognizes the need for ongoing discussion and refinement of each aspect, acknowledging that there may be other relevant topics not covered within this manuscript. Our goal is that this manuscript will stimulate dialogue, data sharing, and publication of examples from the ONT community so the guidance can be developed using robust and recent data. The ONT WG is committed to ensuring a robust and harmonized safety assessment framework for ONT development and looks forward to submitting comments on the ICH S13 draft guidance during the ICH Step 2 public consultation period.</p></sec></body><back><ack><title>Acknowledgements</title><p>The authors would like to thank the DruSafe LG members and the DruSafe secretariat for their sponsorship, advice, and guidance.
