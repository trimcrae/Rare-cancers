Regional Lymph Node Involvement Is Associated With Poorer
Survivorship in Patients With Chondrosarcoma: A SEER Analysis

Abstract
Background Regional lymph node involvement is thought
to be rare in patients with chondrosarcoma, but its actual
prevalence is unclear. Additionally, it is often not consid-
ered when prognostic factors are analyzed in patients with
chondrosarcoma. However, it has been well established
that lymph node involvement is a poor prognostic marker
in patients with many types of bone and soft tissue sar-
coma, including rhabdomyosarcoma, osteosarcoma, and
Ewing’s sarcoma. Although lymph node metastases are
rare among all sarcoma types, it is important to consider
whether lymph node metastases should be assessed in
patients with chondrosarcoma because these metastases
may impact survival.
Questions/purposes (1) What is the reported prevalence of
regional lymph node involvement in patients with chon-
drosarcoma? (2) Do patients who have chondrosarcomas
with regional lymph node involvement have different
clinicopathologic presentations and survival than patients
without regional lymph node involvement? (3) Is regional
lymph node involvement independently associated with
prognosis in patients with chondrosarcoma?
Methods The data of patients with chondrosarcoma regis-
tered in the Surveillance Epidemiology and End Results
database (SEER) (1988-2015) were analyzed for the repor-
ted prevalence of regional lymph node involvement and its
relationship with clinicopathologic features and the 5-year
overall survival rate. From 1988 to 2015, 5528 patients with
chondrosarcoma were registered in the SEER database.
After screening by the inclusion criterion— chondrosarcoma
as the ﬁrst primary tumor, diagnosis with histology con ﬁr-
mation, patients with active followup and available in-
formation about regional node status— 3374 patients met the
inclusion criteria and were analyzed. Demographics and
clinicopathologic data were compared using chi-square or
Fisher’s exact tests. Logistic regression analysis was used to
assess the adjusted odds ratio. The overall survival rate was
estimated with Kaplan-Meier curves and log-rank tests.
Univariate and multivariate analyses of overall survival were
performed with Cox proportional hazard models. In

Results Forty-four patients (1.3%) were recorded in the
database as having regional lymph node involvement at the
time of the primary diagnosis. Lymph node metastases
were more likely to be reported in an extraskeletal primary
site (3% [13 of 426] versus 1% [31 of 2948], adjusted odds
ratio [OR] = 2.9, 95% CI, 1.5 –5.8; p = 0.003) for bone
primary sites and tumors with maximum diameter $ 8c m
(2% [26 of 1045] versus 1% [10 of 1075], adjusted OR =
2.9, 95% CI, 1.3–6.3; p = 0.008) and poorer differentiation
(4% [24 of 608] versus 1% [14 of 2308], adjusted OR = 4.0,
95% CI, 2.0 –8.2; p < 0.001), and in those with distant
metastases (7% [14 of 203] versus 1% [30 of 3148], ad-
justed OR = 3.5, 95% CI, 1.7 –7.1, p = 0.001). The 5-year
overall survival rates of patients with and without regional
lymph node involvement were 28% (95% CI, 15–42%) and
77% (95% CI, 75 –78%), respectively (p < 0.001). After
controlling for age, sex, race, grade, metastatic status, size,
and histologic subtype, the presence of regional lymph
node involvement was associated with poorer survival
(hazard ratio, 2.20; 95% CI, 1.50 –3.24; p < 0.001); this
ﬁnding was con ﬁrmed in several sensitivity analyses.
Conclusion The prevalence of regional lymph node in-
volvement in patients with chondrosarcoma was 1.3% in the
SEER database. Although chondrosarcomas are rare, patients
with chondrosarcomas who have regional node metastases
have a poorer prognosis than those who have not reported to
have them. This may underrepresent the true proportion of
patients with lymph node metastases given the inaccuracies
of reporting in this database, but we believe these ﬁndings
indicate that clinicians should examine patients more care-
fully for chondrosarcoma with lymph node metastases. Fu-
ture studies are needed to assess potential treatment strategies
to improve the prognosis of these patients.

Introduction
After osteosarcoma, chondrosarcoma is the second most
prevalent type of primary malignant bone sarcoma, ac-
counting for approximately 20% to 30% of all skeletal
sarcomas [ 3, 26 ]. Effective clinical treatment is primarily
restricted to surgical resection because this disease is not
sensitive to radiation or chemotherapy [ 11, 16 ], partially
because it has a low mitotic index and poor vascularity [ 4,
11, 35].
Identifying prognostic factors is valuable for clinical
decision making. Metastatic patterns in bone malignancies
are typically hematogenous, with the lungs and bone being
the most prevalent metastatic sites [ 19, 23, 25 ]. Distant
metastases have been demonstrated to be independently
associated with poorer survival in patients with chon-
drosarcoma [21, 35]. Regional lymph node involvement is
thought to be rare in chondrosarcoma, although the prev-
alence of regional lymph node involvement has not been
well de ﬁned in previous studies, which were mainly case
reports [13, 18] and a single-institution retrospective study
with a small sample size (49 patients) [ 9] or were focused
on chondrosarcoma arising in a speci ﬁc site (larynx) [ 7].
Likewise, the effect of regional lymph node involvement
on the survival of patients with chondrosarcoma has not
been systematically evaluated. However, although lymph
node metastases are also rare in other bone and soft tissue
sarcomas, the status of regional lymph nodes has been used
to stratify risk in patients with rhabdomyosarcoma,
Ewing’s sarcoma, and osteosarcoma [1, 24, 29, 36]. Lymph
node metastases in patients with chondrosarcoma may also
be associated with poorer survival, and this may be im-
portant to consider when planning treatment or advising
patients about their condition. However, currently, the
proportion of patients with nodal metastases at the time of
diagnosis and the impact of having these metastases is not
well understood.
With this in mind, we asked: (1) What is the reported
prevalence of regional lymph node involvement in patients
with chondrosarcoma? (2) Do patients who have chon-
drosarcomas with regional lymph node involvement have
different clinicopathologic presentations and survival than
patients without regional lymph node involvement? (3) Is
regional lymph node involvement independently associ-
ated with prognosis in patients with chondrosarcoma?
Patients and Methods
Patient Selection
To answer these questions, we evaluated the clinical
characteristics and outcomes of patients with chon-
drosarcoma with or without regional lymph node in-
volvement using a large population with adequate length of
followup. Data were obtained from the US National Cancer
Institute’s Surveillance Epidemiology and End Results
(SEER) database, which consists of patient-level data de-
rived from 18 cancer registries and covers approximately
28% of the US population [ 20]. Patients with chon-
drosarcoma who were diagnosed between 1988 and 2015
and who were enrolled in the SEER database with their
corresponding details were extracted using the SEER*Stat
version 8.3.5 software (National Cancer Institute,
Bethesda, MD, USA). Two authors (LW, CT) in-
dependently extracted and reviewed the collected data to
ensure adequate quality (Fig. 1), when there was a dis-
crepancy, these two authors cross-reviewed and reached an
agreement before conducting the analyses.

We chose the SEER database because it is the only
comprehensive source of population-based information in
the United States that contains stage (tumor size, lymph
node involvement, and distant metastasis) of cancer at the
time of diagnosis and patient survival data. Currently,
SEER covers approximately 30% of the US population,
thus encompassing a substantial sample size that would not
otherwise be possible with any single or multi-institutional
experiences. Although the SEER database has several
shortcomings, including a limited number of variables
available and loss of several outcomes of interest, such as
local or distant recurrence, we did not feel those were
disqualifying for our purposes because most of the varia-
bles that may affect the patient prognosis, such as age,
tumor size, grade, and metastatic status as well as the
outcome of interest (5-year survival status) in this study
were all included.
The inclusion criteria were a diagnosis of chon-
drosarcoma that was made in a living patient, not from
autopsy or a death certi ﬁcate only; a diagnosis con ﬁrmed
with microscopy, not with radiology or a clinical diagnosis
only; patients with chondrosarcoma as the ﬁrst primary
malignancy; and patients with available information about
regional lymph node involvement. We excluded 404
patients with extraskeletal myxoid chondrosarcoma
because it is a misnomer to call it a real chondrosarcoma;
we also excluded approximately one-ﬁfth of the potentially
eligible patients (899 of 4273) because the lymph node
status was not reported (Fig. 1).
The International Classi ﬁcation of Diseases for Oncol-
ogy morphology codes for patients with chondrosarcoma
who were eligible for inclusion in this study were 9220/3,
chondrosarcoma not otherwise speci ﬁed; 9240/3, mesen-
chymal chondrosarcoma; 9221/3, juxtacortical chon-
drosarcoma; 9231/3, myxoid chondrosarcoma; 9242/3,
clear cell chondrosarcoma; and 9243/3, dedifferentiated
chondrosarcoma. Patients with chondrosarcoma of bone
and soft tissue were also included in this analysis, skeletal
(axial bone, extremity bone, and bone [not other speciﬁed])
and extraskeletal (arising in site other than bone) chon-
drosarcoma were classi ﬁed based on the International
Classiﬁcation of Diseases for Oncology topography codes
in the SEER database. From 1988 to 2015, 5528 patients
with chondrosarcoma were enrolled in the SEER database.
A total of 3374 patients met the inclusion criteria and were
included in this study (Table 1).

Covariates
We evaluated patient characteristics and survival outcomes
based on the presence or absence of regional lymph node
involvement. We regarded patients with distant lymph
node metastasis but without regional node status record as
having unknown regional node status. Demographic vari-
ables in the analyses included age, sex, race, and year of
diagnosis. Clinical characteristics in the analyses included
tumor site, grade, metastatic status, size, and histologic
subtype. Distant metastasis refers to a primary tumor that
has spread to distant organs or distant lymph nodes. The
SEER database also provides data on the length of fol-
lowup and patient’s vital status at the time of last followup,
enabling us to calculate the overall survival rate.
Statistical Analyses
We separated patients into two groups: those with and
those without regional lymph node involvement. For cat-
egorical variables, we used the chi-square test or Fisher ’s
exact test, and we used two sample t-tests for continuous
variables to determine the differences in demographic and
tumor characteristics between these two groups. Overall
survival was estimated using the Kaplan-Meier curve,
and a log-rank test was applied to assess for a difference in
survival between the groups. The median followup time of
the research group was 108 months (interquartile range,
57-166 months), 22% (728 of 3374) patients lost to fol-
lowup before 5 years and regarded as censored patients.
We used a Cox proportional hazard regression analysis to
determine the hazard ratio (HR) with 95% con ﬁdence
intervals (CIs). Univariate and multivariate Cox regression
analyses were performed using 5-year overall survival as
the outcome measure. The choice of variables in the mul-
tivariate analysis was based on the p value (< 0.1) of the
variable under consideration in the univariate analysis
(see Table; Supplemental Digital Content 1, http://
links.lww.com/CORR/A201 ). Proportional hazards
assumption was evaluated with time-dependent cova-
riates. SEER*Stat version 8.3.5 (National Cancer In-
stitute, Bethesda, MD, US A) was used to access the
SEER database. All statistical analyses were performed
using STATA, version 14.0 (Stata Corp, College Sta-
tion, TX, USA).
Sensitivity Analyses
Along with our main analyses, we performed ﬁve sensi-
tivity analyses to test the robustness of the Cox pro-
portional hazard model. In the ﬁrst analysis, we excluded
patients with unknown tumor size to reduce the potential
bias caused by this group of patients. The second analysis
involved only patients with skeletal chondrosarcoma,
which was a more typical origin of this malignancy. To
diminish the potential impact of improved treatment within
years, the third sensitivity analysis was restricted to patients
diagnosed from 2010 to 2015. Next, we also conducted an
analysis of patients with grade III or IV chondrosarcoma.
The ﬁnal sensitivity analysis only included patients with
pathologically con ﬁrmed regional node status to avoid
potential bias from the diagnosis method of lymph node
involvement.

Chondrosarcoma Is Rare
We found that 44 of 3374 (1.3%) patients were reported
to have regional lymph node involvement at the time of
the initial diagnosis (see Ta ble; Supplemental Digital

Patient Characteristics Differed Between Patients with
and without Regional Node Involvement
All demographic features, including age, sex, race, and
year of diagnosis, were similar in patients with and without
regional lymph node involvement (Table 1). With respect
to tumor characteristics, patients with regional lymph node
involvement were more likely to have extraskeletal tumors
than those without (3% [13 of 426] versus 1% [31 of 2948],
adjusted OR (odds ratio) = 2.9, 95% CI, 1.5 –5.8; p =
0.003), poorer differentiation (Grade III or IV: 4% [24 of
608] versus 1% [14 of 2308], adjusted OR = 4.0, 95% CI,
2.0–8.2; p < 0.001), distant metastatic disease (7% [14 of
203] versus 1% [30 of 3148], adjusted OR = 3.5, 95% CI,
1.7–7.1; p = 0.001), and tumor dimensions $ 8 cm (2% [26
of 1045] versus 1% [10 of 1075], adjusted OR = 2.9, 95%
CI, 1.3–6.3; p = 0.008).

Discussion
Chondrosarcoma is a heterogeneous group of aggressive
tumors characterized by the production of a cartilaginous
matrix by malignant chondrocytes [ 29]. Factors associated
with poorer survival in patients with chondrosarcoma in-
clude older age at the time of diagnosis, the presence of
distant metastasis, and larger or higher-grade tumors [ 5, 8,
12, 17, 27 ]. Regional lymph node metastasis is thought to
be rare in patients with chondrosarcoma, although the
prevalence of regional lymph node involvement was
poorly deﬁned in existing reports [ 7, 9, 13, 18 ]. Likewise,
the impact of regional lymph node involvement on chon-
drosarcoma is also poorly understood, and we couldﬁnd no
reports on the survival of patients with and without lymph
node metastasis. In this study, we con ﬁrmed that regional
lymph node involvement is rare in patients with chon-
drosarcoma, with a prevalence of 1.3%. We also found
patients with regional node metastasis had different tumor
characteristics compared with patients without regional
node metastasis. Most importantly, we identi ﬁed that re-
gional lymph node involvement independently indicated
poorer survival in patients with chondrosarcoma; this may
be important to consider when planning treatment or ad-
vising patients about their condition.
There were several inherent limitations to the use of the
SEER registry. The SEER database allowed us to
examine a rare subgroup of patients who had chon-
drosarcoma within a relatively large cohort, but the SEER
database did not provide detailed information about the
patients’ treatments. For example, records on chemother-
apy plans, detailed surgical methods, and management of
regional lymph nodes with radiation or surgical resection
were not available. Therefore, it was impossible to evaluate
the effect of different management approaches in these
patients. We think that knowledge about the regional
lymph node status is important in guiding future thera-
peutic regimens and informing patients about their

Our results regarding lymph node involvement were sim-
ilar to those of two previous studies. One study [8]r e p o r t e d
that one of 46 patients (2.2%) with nonosseous chon-
drosarcoma and another [7] found that ﬁve of 150 patients
(3.3%) with larynx chondrosarcoma had regional lymph node
involvement. This suggests that when a diagnosis of extra-
skeletal chondrosarcoma is made, the regional lymph nodes
should be examined carefully. Our rate is similar to that
reported in other studies on osteosarcoma (0.8%-2.7%) [2, 31,
33]. The rarity of regional lymph node metastasis in patients
with bone sarcoma may be partially attributed to the paucity of
lymphatic channels in bone tissue. Edwards et al. [6]r e p o r t e d
that lymphatic vessels were lacking in either normal bones or
benign and malignant bone tumors, but that they could be
found in tumors that have extended into the periosteum and
surrounding soft tissues, suggesting that extraskeletal chon-
drosarcoma tends to be involved in the regional lymph nodes,
and a chondrosarcoma with regional lymph node involvement
is more likely to have a larger tumor diameter.
More importantly, our study is the ﬁrst we know of to
indicate that patients with chondrosarcoma and regional
lymph node involvement have inferior 5-year survival
outcomes. Previously, the paucity evidence about the im-
pact of regional node involvement on the prognosis of
chondrosarcoma might just result from its rare prevalence.
However, several reports provide hints for the potential
adverse effects of regional lymph node involvement on
chondrosarcoma [7, 12, 34]. Giuffrida et al. [12] reported a
large sample size study that included 2890 patients with
chondrosarcoma to identify its prognostic characteristics,
although regional node status was not a covariate their
analysis model. They found that chondrosarcoma at the
regional stage, meaning that the tumor had extended to the
regional nodes and/or adjacent tissues, had inferior survival
compared with chondrosarcoma patients with local disease
only. In a review of 150 patients with larynx chon-
drosarcoma, Ferlito et al. [ 7] found that three of ﬁve
patients with cervical node involvement died within 3 years
after surgery, and the other two patients did not have fol-
lowup data. This suggested that regional node involvement
had a negative impact on larynx chondrosarcoma patients
given that patients with chondrosarcoma in the larynx
without lymph node metastases exhibited excellent long-
term survival after surgery [ 22, 32]. In patients with oste-
osarcoma, regional nodal metastasis, whether alone or
coexistent with other metastatic disease was associated
with poorer survival [ 34], indicating that regional node
involvement might facilitate tumor progression; however,
the exact mechanism remains elusive.

was an independent and inferior indicator of prognosis in
patients with chondrosarcoma. Distant metastatic disease has
been demonstrated to be the most important predictor of poor
survival in chondrosarcoma [5, 10, 30 ]; however, we found
evidence that regional node involvement was also associated
with poorer survival even in patients without metastatic dis-
ease at initial presentation. This may be in part because the
invaded regional lymph nodes serve as a transfer station and
initial “selection” sites where tumor cells with metastatic
competence seed, expand, and are selected for increasingly
immunosuppressive variants which then spread to other sites
[14, 37]. This is similar to the regional progression and risk of
recurrence that has been observed in patients with bladder and
breast cancer who have regional lymph node disease [15, 28].
However, this relationship cannot be assessed in the SEER
database because only the survival status of the last followup
was recorded. Given the strong adverse prognostic impact of
regional lymph node metastasis, we recommend that suspi-
cious regional lymph nodes found during a physical exami-
nation or imaging undergo a biopsy to con ﬁrm the regional
lymph node’s status and accurately diagnose this rare sub-
group. This will help clinicians identify patients with chon-
drosarcoma who have a higher survival risk, particularly
among patients without distant metastatic lesions. We could
not assess whether or not a lymph node resection will affect
survival in patients with involved regional nodes.
In conclusion, our study provided evidence that there
were differences in tumor characteristics and survival among
patients who had chondrosarcomas, with or without regional
lymph node involvement. The prevalence of lymph node
metastasis in the patients from the SEER database is likely is
an underestimate, but it indicates that more careful assess-
ment might be important both for advising patients about
their prognosis and for developing better treatment options
for patients who have lymph node metastases. In light of the
independent association between regional lymph node in-
volvement and poorer survival, patients with chon-
drosarcomas who present with lymph node involvement may
be reasonable candidates for novel therapeutic strategies and
clinical trials that are currently applied to patients with distant
metastases and poorly differentiated chondrosarcomas. The
biological mechanisms underlying tumor dissemination via
the regional lymph nodes, and the association of thisﬁnding
with a greater risk of death, both merit further research.
