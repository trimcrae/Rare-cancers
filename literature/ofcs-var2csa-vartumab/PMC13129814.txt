## Methods

## Identification of T. vaginalis and P. falciparum IMRS Primers
We utilized the Identical
Multi-repeat Sequence (IMRS) algorithm with modified input constraints
to identify primer pairs that would enable isothermal amplification. ). Reaction
temperature increments from 55 to 65 °C were tested, with amplification
only observed within the 59–62 °C range. Primer concentrations
were also optimized: P. falciparum primers above
2.4 μM led to significant nonspecific amplification, while T. vaginalis primers below 2 μM inhibited amplification,
leading to false negatives. Increasing the T. vaginalis forward primer to 3.2 μM improved band visibility on the lateral
flow strips. MgSO4 supplementation was tested but had no
beneficial effect or appeared to cause nonspecific amplification.
The addition of DMSO substantially improved amplification. Bst 2.0 polymerase concentrations below 640 U/mL produced
no amplification, while 800 U/mL significantly enhanced T.
vaginalis amplification. Substitution with Bst XT polymerase for Bst 2.0 was attempted but was
unsuccessful. Ficoll 400 was critical for amplification, with concentrations
below 3% failing to support amplification and above 4% resulting in
nonspecific bands. Finally, betaine addition markedly improved reproducibility.
 Serially diluted T. vaginalis gDNA
suspended in

Tagged reaction products were visualized on both LFAs and a 12% polyacrylamide

To evaluate the analytical
sensitivity of the T. vaginalis iso-IMRS singleplex
assay in artficial urine, real-time amplification
experiments were performed using a 10-fold serial dilution of gDNA,
with eight replicates (n = 8) conducted at each concentration
point. Following amplification, probit regression and analysis were
performed. The analytical sensitivity was calculated as the concentration
at which T. vaginalis gDNA can be detected with 95%
confidence. The iso-IMRS assay for T. vaginalis had
an analytical sensitivity of 0.62 copies/μL ( ).

 The iso-IMRS assay demonstrated
strong target specificity
for both T. vaginalis and P. falciparum ( ). When
evaluated
against a comprehensive panel of off-target genomic materials from
pathogens commonly detected in urine, including P. vivax, C. trachomatis, N. gonorrhoeae, N. meningitidis, S. agalactiae, and deidentified HPV-positive cervical swabs and serum spiked with
inactivated HIV virions, no amplification was observed using either
the T. vaginalis- or P. falciparum-specific IMRS primer sets. Distinct amplicon bands were observed
exclusively in reactions containing target DNA, as visualized by 12%
polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis. Similarly when testing off-target
effects using the multiplexed iso-IMRS assay, distinct amplicons were
only seen in reactions containing target DNA (Figure S6) Together, these findings confirm the potential
for high analytical specificity of the iso-IMRS assay and demonstrate
its ability to discriminate target pathogens from a diverse range
of nontarget genomic backgrounds under the tested conditions. Of course,
before the assay is used clinically, a large survey of all potential
confounders would be run.
 

## Discussion
We investigated the sensitivity
of a novel
multiplexed isothermal
assay for T. vaginalis and P. falciparum in urine. The multiplexed iso-IMRS test was compared against existing
and emerging diagnostic assays. Three primary factors were considered
in this comparison: analytical sensitivity (limit of detection), time
to result, and the required instrumentation and technical expertise.
For each assay, the evaluation focused on whether it demonstrated
sufficient sensitivity when used on a urine matrix, whether its turnaround
time was suitable for clinical applications, and whether the required
instrumentation was compatible with POC or near-POC clinical settings.
Microscopy-based detection of T. vaginalis is
largely impractical for POC use, as it requires either a wet mount
examined by a trained microscopist or a culture device that can take
up to 5 days to yield a positive result.
 below.
 Here, we used human urine
as the test matrix, which
is a noninvasive
sample and allows for the collection of a large sample volume. In
future work, we aim to reduce total assay runtime by eliminating urine
processing steps. Initial results using spiked pooled human urine
suggest this is feasible. However, if proteinuriacommonly
seen in urine from patients with P. falciparum infectionproves
inhibitory, we will explore strategies such as incorporating a sacrificial
protein (e.g., bovine serum albumin) into the amplification mix to
mitigate the effect.
−

 Sacrificial proteins act as decoys to bind nonspecific
or interfering molecules, thereby protecting the DNA polymerase from
degradation, loss, or nonspecific adsorption. Other technical limitations include the absence of a sample adequacy
control, such as a constitutively present human genomic target, which
was not incorporated at this stage. For future clinical applications,
we plan to incorporate a spatially separated human DNA control to
verify sample quality. As with any kit-based approach, there remains
some risk of contamination or material loss during transfer from the
reaction volume to the strip. In addition, clinical samples may contain
both lysed and intact organisms or cells, in which case the inclusion
of a lysis step could further enhance sensitivity. Along with further
optimization to streamline urine processing in the sample collection
cup or operate in unprocessed urine, ongoing studies aim to confirm
efficacy in real patient samples collected near Kenyan testing sites.

## Conclusion
In this study, we demonstrate the feasibility
of a multiplexed
iso-IMRS assay for the simultaneous detection of Plasmodium
falciparum and Trichomonas vaginalis using
human urine as a noninvasive clinical matrix. By leveraging a streamlined
primer design strategy and isothermal amplification chemistry, the
assay achieves sensitivity comparable to PCR while minimizing equipment
requirements and sample preparation. We present the use of simple
desalting columns upstream of isothermal amplification, a strategy
not previously reported. Downstream integration with lateral flow
readout further supports its suitability for point-of-care applications,
particularly in low-resource settings where both pathogens contribute
significantly to maternal and reproductive health burdens, and are
tests recommended as part of routine antenatal care.
,
 The workflow enables complete sample processing, amplification,
and result readout in under 1 h. Sample preparation requires approximately
5 min, amplification is completed within 30 min, and the lateral flow
readout is obtained in approximately 10 min. Using this workflow,
analytical sensitivities of 0.571 copies/μL for P. falciparum and 1.027 copies/μL for T. vaginalis are
reported. These findings highlight the potential of iso-IMRS as a
flexible, field-ready molecular diagnostic platform. Ongoing work
will focus on reducing preprocessing steps, validation in clinical
patient samples, and expanding multiplexing capabilities for broader
infectious disease surveillance.
