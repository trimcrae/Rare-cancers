## Abstract
The Duffy‐binding protein (DBP) is a promising antigen for a malaria vaccine that would protect against clinical symptoms caused by Plasmodium vivax infection. Region II of DBP (DBP‐II) contains the receptor‐binding domain that engages host red blood cells, but DBP‐II vaccines elicit many non‐neutralizing antibodies that bind distal to the receptor‐binding surface. Here, we engineered a truncated DBP‐II immunogen that focuses the immune response to the receptor‐binding surface. This immunogen contains the receptor‐binding subdomain S1S2 and lacks the immunodominant subdomain S3. Structure‐based computational design of S1S2 identified combinatorial amino acid changes that stabilized the isolated S1S2 without perturbing neutralizing epitopes. This immunogen elicited DBP‐II‐specific antibodies in immunized mice that were significantly enriched for blocking activity compared to the native DBP‐II antigen. This generalizable design process successfully stabilized an integral core fragment of a protein and focused the immune response to desired epitopes to create a promising new antigen for malaria vaccine development.

10.13039/100000060 DBP‐II has been investigated in preclinical and clinical trials but has not elicited a robust antibody response targeting the receptor‐binding surface. Immunization of animals with DBP‐II elicits receptor‐blocking antibodies, but these antibodies are largely strain‐specific (Cassan et al.,  ; Michon et al.,  ; Ntumngia et al.,  ; Wiley et al.,  ). Characterization of monoclonal antibodies from these studies revealed none that bind to the receptor‐binding surface; instead, the mAbs bind to the S3 subdomain and likely block receptor interaction through indirect steric clash (Chen et al.,  ; George et al.,  ). Antibodies isolated from immunized humans mirror those from animals. Three out of ten isolated mAbs efficiently block parasite invasion, and only one is strain transcending, also binding the S3 subdomain rather than S1S2 (Rawlinson et al.,  ). In controlled human infection trials, only a highly immunogenic DBP‐II vaccine (S1–3) slowed parasite growth, but not enough to eliminate the need for drug treatment (Hou et al.,  ). These results suggest that the S3 subdomain is immunodominant and that focusing the antibody response to the receptor‐binding surface could improve vaccine efficacy.
Here, we created an isolated S1S2 immunogen that focused the immune response to the receptor‐binding surface. The native S1S2 sequence could not be produced in isolation, likely due to the large surface exposed upon removal of the S3 subdomain. We used a previously reported computational design process called SPEEDesign to identify amino acid changes that stabilize S1S2 in isolation (Dickey et al.,  ; Dickey, Gupta, et al.,  ; Dickey, Ma, et al.,  ). Stabilized immunogens were at least as thermostable as DBP‐II and bound neutralizing mAbs that recognize the receptor‐binding surface. Immunization of mice with an S1S2 immunogen elicited DBP‐II‐binding antibodies that were highly enriched for blocking activity.

## RESULTS
2.1

## Isolated S1S2 immunogens produced by SPEEDesign
We used SPEEDesign to create an isolated DBP‐S1S2 immunogen (Figure  ). SPEEDesign requires a functional definition for each amino acid in the protein and consequently its mutability during the design process (Figure  ). The DARC interface and DBP‐dimer interface were held constant during the design process, as were the epitopes of the neutralizing human antibodies (nAbs) 053054 and 092096 (Batchelor et al.,  ; Urusova et al.,  ). The residues comprising the sulfated tyrosine binding site were not fixed, as this information was not available at the time of design. Residues exposed upon removal of the S3 subdomain were thoroughly searched during the design process to identify changes that would stabilize the isolated S1S2. All the other amino acids were allowed to sample an intermediate sequence space defined by energetic and evolutionary constraints.
SPEEDesign uses four different ROSETTA design strategies, each of which generates 10,000 decoys. These computational decoys were sampled in a sparse matrix fashion by clustering sequences by identity and testing the expression of 26 divergent sequences in Escherichia coli (Figure  ). Unmodified S1S2 (WT S1S2) was expressed at low levels, and 18 immunogens were expressed at greater levels. These 18 immunogens and WT S1S2 were purified from inclusion bodies and refolded by rapid dilution. Seven of these immunogens could be correctly refolded, as determined by enzyme‐linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) with the 092096 nAb, that binds to a conformationally dependent epitope, while WT S1S2 exhibited no binding to 092096 (Figure ). These seven immunogens were further purified by size‐exclusion chromatography and screened by differential scanning fluorimetry (DSF). Three lead candidates could be purified as thermostable monomeric proteins and were moved forward for further characterization (Figure  ). All the SPEEDesign strategies produced immunogens that bound to 092096 to some extent, and the three lead antigens were all derived from strategy 4, which samples a moderate sequence space. As with previous applications of SPEEDesign, the three lead immunogens did not possess the best ROSETTA scores, further emphasizing the utility of the screening process.

2.2

## Stabilized S1S2 immunogens present well‐folded neutralizing epitopes
Immunogens 15, 17, and 18 could be highly purified as monodisperse thermostable proteins (Figure  ). These S1S2 immunogens eluted from a size‐exclusion column at a volume consistent with their theoretical molecular weights of 21 kDa, suggesting that they were properly folded, globular, and monomeric proteins (Figure  ). The proteins were highly pure with no visible degradation, and they exhibited very little intermolecular disulfide cross‐linking (Figure  ). The melting temperature of each immunogen was better than or equal to that of DBP‐II, as measured by DSF (Figure  ). Immunogen 15 (S1S2‐15) had the highest T
m of 59°C, which was 19°C higher than that of DBP‐II, indicating that SPEEDesign successfully improved immunogen thermostability.
 We used biolayer interferometry (BLI) to probe the integrity of the neutralizing epitopes on S1S2‐15. We measured the binding of the immunogen to the human nAbs 053054 and 092096 (Table  and Figure  ). S1S2‐15 binds both antibodies with sub‐nanomolar dissociation constants (K
D) of 0.56 and 0.37 nM, respectively. These affinities are within 2‐fold of the K
D for DBP‐II, and well within the 1000‐fold range of binding affinities (~0.01–10 nM) typically seen for nAbs that bind DBP and other antigens (Alanine et al.,  ; Patel et al.,  ; Ragotte et al.,  ; Rawlinson et al.,  ; Tang et al.,  ; Urusova et al.,  ). This indicates that the neutralizing epitopes are retained and unperturbed on S1S2‐15. S1S2‐15 also binds DARC, validating that the receptor‐interaction surface is largely intact (Figure  ). S1S2‐15 has slightly lower DARC‐binding activity than DBP‐II, which may be due to mutations in the extended DARC interface that was undiscovered and therefore not fixed at the initiation of our design process. Together, these data suggest that S1S2‐15 retains the required epitopes and surfaces to elicit DBP/DARC‐blocking antibodies upon immunization.
 The predicted structure of S1S2‐15 reveals diverse mechanisms of antigen stabilization (Figure  ). S1S2‐15 contains 31 amino acid changes that are predominantly located on the surface exposed by removal of S3 (Figure  ). The extensive interface between WT S1S2 and S3 is a mixture of complementary charged and hydrophobic patches, and the removal of S3 exposes a 1126 Å2 surface that must be stabilized. Several large hydrophobic residues at the interface are mutated to smaller hydrophobic or polar residues (W228P, W305A, G308R, M319Q, I374K, V381K), reducing the surface hydrophobicity (Figure  ). There are also highly charged patches exposed upon the removal of S3, and the mutation of charged residues (R223S, R224N, D227P, D240P, R242Q, K297E, K301E, G308R, T317R, K347E, K378D, and V381K) results in a more balanced charge distribution (Figure  ). Part of this redistribution of charged residues includes the introduction of a salt bridge between T317R and K347E (Figure  ). Finally, there are three mutations to proline: D240P likely stabilizes an adjacent α‐helix, and D227P and W228P likely stabilize a kinked backbone (Figure  ). The diversity and complexity of the stabilizing mutations underscores the utility of a combinatorial design approach like SPEEDesign, where multiple mutations are simultaneously introduced.
 
2.3

## S1S2‐15 elicits antibodies focused on DBP/DARC‐blocking epitopes
We immunized mice with S1S2‐15 to characterize the antibody response elicited by the stabilized S1S2 immunogen (Figure  ). Mice were immunized with DBP‐II as a comparator, and both antigens were administered in a prime‐boost regimen adjuvanted with alhydrogel. S1S2‐15 elicited antibodies that bind DBP‐II, and the titers were significantly lower than those elicited by DBP‐II, consistent with the removal of the immunodominant S3 subdomain (Figure  ). Conversely, DBP‐II elicited antibodies that bind S1S2‐15, demonstrating that S1S2‐15 largely recapitulates the DBP‐II S1S2 domain (Figure  ). The S1S2‐15 titers elicited by S1S2‐15 were higher than those elicited by DBP‐II suggesting that some antibodies are raised to the newly designed surface on S1S2‐15. However, the difference in anti‐S1S2‐15 geometric mean titer (GMT) is only 5.2‐fold while the difference in anti‐DBP‐II GMT is 38.4‐fold, which suggests that the antibody response to the S3 domain is immunodominant.
 S1S2‐15 also elicited functional antibodies that blocked the binding of DBP to DARC (Figure  ). This blocking activity supports the conclusion that S1S2‐15 is properly folded and accurately presents the receptor‐binding surface and neutralizing epitopes upon immunization. The blocking antibody titers elicited by S1S2‐15, however, were lower than those elicited by DBP‐II. While the quantity of blocking antibodies elicited by S1S2‐15 was lower than DBP‐II, the anti‐DBP‐II antibodies elicited by S1S2‐15 were enriched in blocking activity (Figure  ). We measured the antibody quality elicited by each antigen by calculating the ratio of the blocking titer to the DBP‐II binding titer (Figure  ). We also measured antibody quality by purifying anti‐DBI‐II antibodies from pooled serum and measuring their blocking activity (Figure  ). Indeed, S1S2‐15 elicited significantly higher‐quality antibodies than did DBP‐II, indicating that S1S2‐15 successfully focused the immune response to functional epitopes on DBP.

## DISCUSSION
DBP is a prime vaccine candidate for P. vivax malaria. Naturally acquired immunity to P. vivax can protect against disease and is exemplified by naturally acquired human antibodies that bind the DARC‐binding site in DBP located in S1 and S2 (Carias et al.,  ; Urusova et al.,  ). In contrast, immunization with DBP‐II does not appear to confer strong inhibitory activity, and antibody generation appears to be skewed toward S3 (Chen et al.,  ; George et al.,  ; Payne et al.,  ; Rawlinson et al.,  ; Singh et al.,  ). Immunization with S1S2 could circumvent these problems, but S1S2 is unstable and cannot be expressed. S1S2 forms an integral core domain that interacts extensively with S3 precluding the ability to extract S1S2 from S3 and DBP‐II. This has hampered vaccine design for P. vivax and DBP‐based interventions.
We created a stabilized DBP‐S1S2 immunogen that focuses the antibody response to key receptor‐blocking epitopes on DBP‐II. Removal of the S3 subdomain eliminates a large immunodominant surface that contains predominantly non‐blocking or weakly blocking epitopes. Upon immunization with S1S2‐15, the removal of S3 decreased the overall antibody titer to DBP‐II, and the DBP‐II antibodies that were elicited were enriched in blocking activity. It is also possible that S1S2‐15 elicits a higher quality antibody response due to changes in other antibody characteristics like binding kinetics and avidity. Regardless of the mechanism, S1S2‐15 elicits DBP‐II antibodies that have significantly improved blocking activity.
While the quality of antibodies elicited by S1S2‐15 is greater than that elicited by DBP‐II, the quantity of blocking antibodies (i.e., blocking antibody titer) is lower. This could occur for several reasons, including reduced immunogenicity of S1S2‐15 due to its smaller size, immunodominance of the exposed S3 interface, decreased antigen stability during formulation or in vivo, loss of indirect blocking epitopes on S3, or mutation of unknown blocking epitopes on S1S2 during the design process. All these potential issues can now be investigated given the proof of concept that an S1S2 immunogen can be generated. Future work on next‐generation subdomain vaccines based on S1S2‐15 could circumvent these issues by using nanoparticle display, conjugation, adjuvant optimization, glycan masking, or reversion of mutated residues, including the tyrosine sulfate binding residues. These sulfate‐binding residues were undiscovered at the time of our design process, and consequently, S1S2‐15 has mutations at the interface that reduce affinity to sulfated DARC. While no known neutralizing antibodies target the sulfate‐binding sites, the restoration of these sites may improve the blocking antibody response. The addition of the S3 domain may also be required to achieve the most potent blocking antibody response. Removing the S3 domain eliminates immunodominant non‐blocking epitopes, but it also removes some blocking epitopes. Masking the immunodominant non‐blocking epitopes on S3 while retaining the blocking epitopes may achieve the most potent blocking antibody response. A complete antibody epitope map, like those for other malaria antigens, would help achieve this goal (Alanine et al.,  ; Ivanochko et al.,  ; McLeod et al.,  ; Patel et al.,  ; Ragotte et al.,  ; Shukla et al.,  ; Tang et al.,  ).
A next‐generation DBP immunogen may also be able to circumvent the strain‐specific neutralizing response often elicited by infection and vaccination (Chootong et al.,  ; Cole‐Tobian et al.,  ; Michon et al.,  ; Ntumngia et al.,  ; Rawlinson et al.,  ). The receptor‐binding interface on S1S2 is well conserved while flanking regions in S1S2 as well as regions of the immunodominant S3 are polymorphic (Guy et al.,  ; VanBuskirk et al.,  ). Focusing the immune response to the receptor‐binding surface may result in improved breadth, in addition to potency, due to the conserved nature of this interface and the S1S2 design.
The creation of an S1S2 immunogen extends the applications of SPEEDesign to highly unstable antigens and new production systems. We previously demonstrated that SPEEDesign can improve isolated domains when unmodified versions of these domains can be produced to some extent (Dickey et al.,  ; Dickey, Gupta, et al.,  ; Dickey, Ma, et al.,  ). DBP‐II and S1S2 pose greater challenges because both suffer from poor expression and poor purification yields. WT S1S2, in particular, had no reactivity in our ELISA screen (Figure  ), suggesting that properly folded protein could not be produced. This is likely due to the large surface exposed upon removal of S3, which results in a highly unstable target antigen. The complex nature of this exposed surface requires a diverse set of mutations to stabilize the antigen including several residues that interact or are proximal to one another. Such a complex network of changes would not be identifiable through human‐guided design or easily achieved by making single amino acid changes. The combinatorial power and sampling strength of SPEEDesign identifies synergistic amino acid changes that can achieve a solution like S1S2‐15. Additionally, this is the first application of SPEEDesign toward a protein refolded from E. coli, demonstrating that antigen design principles are consistent across production platforms.
The ability to extract and stabilize a core subdomain will also be applicable to other antigen and pathogen targets. DBL domains are found in numerous Plasmodium protein vaccine candidates, including VAR2CSA, EBA‐175, and EBA‐140. Furthermore, vaccine antigens for other pathogens exhibit similar phenomena in which the key neutralizing epitopes are located in core domains integral to a larger protein. Human‐guided structure‐based design efforts have improved numerous antigen targets, but the extent of these modifications is typically minor (Ma et al.,  ; McLellan et al.,  ; Pallesen et al.,  ; Patel et al.,  ). Epitope scaffolding, another computational approach, can isolate and display individual epitopes, but these epitopes are often insufficient for achieving potent neutralization (Correia et al.,  ; Sesterhenn et al.,  ). The isolation and stabilization of integral domains enabled by SPEEDesign offers a middle ground that focuses the response toward key functional surfaces and could be a valuable method for improving vaccine antigens in general.

## MATERIALS AND METHODS
4.1

## SPEEDesign
The general SPEEDesign process was performed as described previously (Dickey et al.,  ). SPEEDesign requires each amino acid to be defined as fixed, intermediate, or deep search to determine the amino acids sampled at each position. The S1S2 residues that form the interface with the N‐terminal DARC helix or that constitute the DBP dimerization interface were defined as fixed (PDB: 4NUV) (Batchelor et al.,  ). The residues that form the interface with the neutralizing antibodies 053054 and 092096 were also defined as fixed (PDB: 6OAO and 6OAN), ensuring that neutralizing epitopes would be retained during the design process (Urusova et al.,  ). Interface residues were defined as those that have a >1.0 Å2 change in solvent‐accessible surface area, as measured in PyMOL (DeLano,  ).
S1S2 residues that form the interface with the S3 subdomain were defined as deep search, such that they would sample a diverse sequence space. These residues are also buried in homologous proteins, meaning that evolutionary‐based design principles may not be sufficient to stabilize the isolated S1S2 domain. All the other residues were defined as intermediate, which were allowed to vary based on evolutionary and energetic constraints.
Four different strategies were used to define the amino acids sampled by the FastDesign module in ROSETTA, as previously described (Dickey et al.,  ). Each strategy generated 10,000 decoys, and the sequences with scores in the 95th percentile were clustered using CD‐HIT (Li & Godzik,  ). The number of clusters generated was based on the sequence diversity produced by each computational strategy.

4.2

## Immunogen cloning, expression, purification, and refolding ELISA screen
The top‐scoring sequence in each cluster was codon optimized for mammalian expression, synthesized, and cloned into a customized pET‐28a vector without tags (Genscript). Plasmids were transformed into Rosetta 2(DE3) competent cells (Sigma Aldrich), which were subsequently grown overnight in Luria Broth supplemented with 30 μg/mL kanamycin and 34 μg/mL chloramphenicol. After overnight growth, the culture was diluted 1:500 in ZYM‐5052 autoinduction media and grown overnight at 37°C (Studier,  ). Cells were harvested by centrifugation, and protein expression was analyzed via SDS–PAGE.
Inclusion bodies were purified from cells with high levels of immunogen expression and solubilized in 6 M guanidinium hydrochloride. Immunogens were refolded by rapid dilution into 400 mM l‐arginine, 50 mM Na‐Tris (pH 8.0), 10 mM Na‐EDTA, 0.1 mM PMSF, 2 mM reduced glutathione, and 0.2 mM oxidized glutathione, followed by incubation at 4°C with gentle mixing for 24 h.
Nunc Maxisorp plates (Thermo Fisher Scientific) were coated with 20 μL of refolding reaction diluted in 80 μL of 50 mM sodium carbonate (pH 9.5). The plates were incubated overnight at 4°C and then washed three times with phosphate‐buffered saline (PBS) + 0.05% Tween‐20 (PBST). The plates were blocked for 1 h at room temperature with 2% bovine serum albumin (BSA) in PBST and then washed three times with PBST. The neutralizing mAb 053054 was diluted to 250 ng/mL in PBST + 2% BSA, and 200 μL was added to each well (Urusova et al.,  ). After incubating for 1 h at room temperature, the plates were washed three times with PBST, and 200 μL of 1:5000 peroxidase‐conjugated anti‐human IgG was added (Jackson ImmunoResearch Laboratories, Inc., Cat. # 109‐035‐098). The plates were incubated for 30 min at room temperature and washed three times with PBST. Finally, 70 μL of tetramethylbenzidine (TMB) (Millipore Sigma) was added, and the mixture was incubated for 30 min at room temperature before quenching with 70 μL of 0.16 M H2SO4. The absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a BioTek Synergy H1 plate reader.

4.3

## Immunogen purification and thermostability screen
Refolding reactions with reactivity to 053054 were concentrated and purified by size‐exclusion chromatography using a Superdex 75 10/300 GL column (Cytiva) equilibrated in 1× PBS. Peak fractions were collected and analyzed via SDS–PAGE to determine purity.
Thermostability was measured by differential scanning fluorimetry (DSF) using the Protein Thermal Shift Dye Kit according to manufacturer instructions (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The final reactions contained 0.8 mg/mL purified immunogen, 1× Protein Thermal Shift Buffer, 1× Thermal Shift Dye, and 0.63× PBS. Fluorescence was monitored using a 7500 Fast Real‐Time PCR system (Thermo Fisher Scientific) as the temperature was increased from 25°C to 95°C at a ramp rate of 1%. The melting temperature (T
m) was calculated using the Boltzmann method. DSF reactions were performed in technical quadruplicate on each plate and in biological triplicate.

4.4

## Large‐scale purification of DBP‐II and immunogen leads
Refolding reactions for DBP‐II and the three lead immunogens (S1S2‐15, ‐17, and ‐18) were scaled up and incubated at 4°C for 48 h with gentle stirring. Antigen was captured using SP Sepharose Fast Flow resin, eluted, and stored at −80°C. Protein was thawed the day before use and further purified by size exclusion chromatography using a Superdex 75 10/300 GL column (Cytiva) for the S1S2 immunogens and a Superdex 200 10/300 GL column (Cytiva) for DBP‐II equilibrated in 1× PBS.

4.5

## Antibody cloning, expression, and purification
For IgG production, plasmids encoding the 092096 IgG1 and IgL chains were cotransfected into human expi293F cells, which were subsequently grown according to manufacturer instructions (Thermo Fisher Scientific). After 4 days of expression, the cell‐free supernatant was harvested by centrifugation. The cell‐free supernatant was diluted 1:1 with IgG binding buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and batch incubated with protein A resin (GoldBio) for 1 h at 4°C. The resin was collected and washed with 20 column volumes (CV) of IgG binding buffer before elution with 10 CV IgG elution buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) into 1 CV 1 M Tris (pH 9.0). Protein was buffer exchanged into 1× PBS, concentrated, snap‐frozen, and stored at −80°C.
For Fab production, the 053054 and 092096 heavy chain variable regions were genetically fused with the IgHG*01 CH1 domain and a 6× histidine tag and cloned into a modified pHL‐sec vector. pHL‐sec was a gift from Edith Yvonne Jones (Addgene plasmid # 99845; http://n2t.net/addgene:99845; RRID:Addgene_99845) (Aricescu et al.,  ). The plasmid was cotransfected with the IgL plasmid into human Expi293F cells, which were subsequently grown according to manufacturer instructions (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The cell‐free supernatant was harvested by centrifugation after 4 days and purified by gravity chromatography using Ni Sepharose Excel resin according to manufacturer instructions (Cytiva). Fab was further purified by size‐exclusion chromatography using a Superdex 200 10/300 GL column (Cytiva) equilibrated in 1× PBS. Peak fractions were pooled, concentrated, snap‐frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at −80°C.

4.6

## Biolayer interferometry (BLI)
Biotinylated antigens were produced and used for BLI. Biotin ligase (BirA) recognition sequences (AviTag) were added to DBP‐II and S1S2‐15 and proteins were expressed and purified as described above. The purified protein was thawed and added to a reaction mixture containing 30 μM antigen, 50 mM bicene (pH 8.3), 10 mM ATP, 10 mM magnesium acetate, 50 μM d‐biotin, and 83 μg/mL purified BirA. The reactions were incubated at room temperature for 30 min, diluted 4:1 with HBS‐E buffer (10 mM Na‐HEPES pH 7.4, 150 mM NaCl, 3 mM EDTA), and purified by size exclusion chromatography using a Superdex 75 10/300 GL column (S1S2‐15) or Superdex 200 10/300 GL column (DBP‐II) equilibrated in HBS‐E buffer. Fractions corresponding to biotinylated protein were pooled and used for BLI.
Binding affinities were measured using a kinetic BLI assay on an Octet‐Red96e (Sartorius). Biotinylated antigens were diluted in HBS‐EP buffer (HBS‐E + 0.005% [v/v] P20 surfactant) and loaded onto Octet SA biosensors (Sartorius) until a signal of 0.6–1 nm was reached. The biosensors were then immersed in HBS‐EP solution containing 092096 or 053054 Fab ranging from 10 to 0.156 nM in a 2‐fold dilution series. After 300 s, the biosensors were immersed in HBS‐EP solution to measure the dissociation of Fab. The association rate (k
a), dissociation rate (k
dis), and dissociation constant (K
D) were globally fit using a 1:1 binding model in Data Analysis 12.0 (Sartorius).

4.7

## Mouse immunizations
Mouse immunogenicity studies were performed in an AAALAC‐International accredited facility under the guidelines and approval of the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) at the National Institutes of Health. Antigens were purified by size‐exclusion chromatography using columns equilibrated in 1× PBS the day before immunization and stored at 4°C overnight. On the day of immunization, the protein was mixed 1:9 with Alhydrogel adjuvant 2% (Invivogen) and stored at 4°C until immunization. Animals were immunized by subcutaneous injection of 100 μL of solution containing 10 μg of antigen and 0.2% Alhydrogel. Immunizations were performed on days 0 and 21. Blood was collected on day 35, and the serum was separated and stored at −80°C. Mice were randomly assigned to each group.

4.8

## Serum antibody titer ELISAs
Nunc MaxiSorp plates (Thermo Fisher Scientific) were coated with 100 μL of 0.005 mg/mL DBP‐II or S1S2‐15 diluted in 50 mM Na‐carbonate (pH 9.5). The plates were incubated overnight at 4°C and then washed with 1× PBS + 0.05% Tween 20 (PBST). The plates were blocked with 200 μL of PBST + 2% bovine serum albumin (BSA) for 1 h at room temperature and washed with PBST. Mouse serum was diluted in PBST + BSA, added to the blocked plates, and incubated for 1 h at room temperature. The plates were washed with PBST, and 200 μL of 1:5000 peroxidase‐conjugated anti‐mouse IgG was added (Jackson ImmunoResearch Laboratories Inc., catalog no. 115‐035‐164). The plates were incubated for 30 min at room temperature and washed with PBST. Finally, 70 μL of tetramethylbenzidine (TMB) (Millipore Sigma) was added, and the mixture was incubated for 20 min at room temperature before quenching with 70 μL of 0.16 M H2SO4. The absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a Biotek Synergy H1 plate reader.
The absorbance values were converted to antibody units (AU) using a standard curve run in triplicate on each plate. The standard reference was created by pooling serum from high‐titer mice and assigning it a defined and constant AU value. This value corresponds to the dilution level required to achieve an Abs450 value of 1.0 in a calibration experiment. Each assay plate contained standard serum diluted in a two‐fold series in triplicate starting at 4 AU. Abs450 values for these standards were fit to a 4‐parameter logistic curve to create a standard curve. Serum from individual animals was diluted to produce an Abs450 value within the standard curve between 0.15 and 3.0, and AU values were interpolated using the standard curve. The AU values for each animal were measured in triplicate on separate plates, and the average is reported.

4.9

## DBP/DARC binding assay
The DBP‐binding region of DARC was produced by fusing it to the constant region of human IgG1. The DARC sequence contains amino acids 1–60 of the Fy‐b antigen with a C51A mutation. This sequence was genetically fused to the N‐terminus of the constant region of human IGHG*01, followed by a 6× His tag. DNA coding for this fusion protein (DARC‐Fc) was codon optimized for expression in human cells and inserted into a modified pHL‐sec vector. The plasmid was transfected into Expi293F cells, which were subsequently grown according to manufacturer instructions (Thermo Fisher Scientific). DARC‐Fc was purified from cell‐free supernatant by gravity chromatography using Ni Sepharose Excel resin according to manufacturer instructions (Cytiva) and further purified by size‐exclusion chromatography using a Superdex 200 10/300 GL column (Cytiva) equilibrated in 1× PBS. The peak fractions were pooled, concentrated, snap‐frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at −80°C.
Nunc MaxiSorp plates (Thermo Fisher Scientific) were coated with 100 μL of 0.004 mg/mL DARC‐Fc diluted in 50 mM Na‐carbonate (pH 9.5). The plates were incubated overnight at 4°C and then washed with 1× PBS + 0.05% Tween 20 (PBST). The plates were blocked with 200 μL of PBST + 2% bovine serum albumin (BSA) for 1 h at room temperature and washed with PBST. Biotinylated DBP‐II or S1S2‐15 were diluted to 28 nM in PBST+BSA and serially diluted in a 3‐fold dilution series. Fifty microliters was added to each well in technical triplicate and incubated at room temperature for 30 min. The plates were washed with PBST before adding 200 μL of 1:10,000 streptavidin‐HRP (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The plates were incubated for 30 min at room temperature and washed with PBST. Finally, 70 μL of tetramethylbenzidine (TMB) (Millipore Sigma) was added, and the mixture was incubated for 20 min at room temperature before quenching with 70 μL of 0.16 M H2SO4. The absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a Biotek Synergy H1 plate reader.

4.10

## Purification of DBP‐II IgG from serum
Two hundred and fifty microliters serum from each mouse in a group was pooled and IgG was purified using protein G Sepharose resin. Pooled serum was diluted 4‐fold with protein G IgG binding puffer (Pierce cat. #21011) and incubated with equilibrated protein G sepharose resin at room temperature for 45 min. Resin was washed with 10 column volumes of binding buffer and eluted with 10 column volumes IgG elution buffer (Pierce cat. #21009) into 1 CV 1 M Tris–HCl pH 9.0. Purified IgG was buffer exchanged three times into PBS using a 50,000 MWCO amicon ultra centrifugal filter and stored at 4°C overnight.
DBP‐II specific antibodies were purified using NHS‐activated Sepharose 4 Fast Flow beads coupled to purified DBP‐II. DBP‐II SP Sepharose Fast Flow eluant (see large‐scale purification method above) was thawed and purified by size exclusion chromatography using a Superdex 200 10/300 GL column (Cytiva) equilibrated in 1× PBS pH 8.0. Peak fractions were pooled to yield 2.8 mL 0.4 mg/mL protein and coupled to 5 mL NHS‐activated Sepharose 4 Fast Flow beads (Cytiva) according to manufacturer instructions. Coupled beads were equilibrated in 1× PBS pH 7.4 and purified IgG from each group was passed over a 1 mL column by gravity flow. Each column was washed with 5 column volumes PBS and eluted with 5 column volumes IgG elution buffer into 1 column volume 1 M Tris–HCl pH 9.0. Purified DBP‐II‐binding IgG was buffer exchanged three times into PBS using a 50,000 MWCO amicon ultra centrifugal filter and stored at 4°C overnight before use in a DBP/DARC blocking assay.

4.11

## DBP/DARC blocking assay
Nunc MaxiSorp plates were coated with DARC‐Fc and blocked with PBST + BSA, as described above for the DBP/DARC binding assay. Mouse serum was diluted in PBST+BSA in a 3‐fold series from 1:10 to 1:21,870, and 50 μL of diluted serum was mixed with 10 μL of 0.9 nM biotinylated DBP‐II. For experiments with purified IgG, affinity purified IgG was diluted in a 2‐fold series from 7.8 to 0.0019 μg/mL before adding biotinylated DBP‐II. The serum or IgG/DBP‐II mixture was incubated at room temperature for 30 min before 50 μL was added to the blocked plates. The plates were incubated for 1 h at room temperature and washed with PBST before adding 200 μL of 1:10,000 streptavidin‐HRP (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The plates were incubated for 30 min at room temperature and washed with PBST. Finally, 70 μL of tetramethylbenzidine (TMB) (Millipore Sigma) was added, and the mixture was incubated for 20 min at room temperature before quenching with 70 μL of 0.16 M H2SO4. The absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a Biotek Synergy H1 plate reader.
The average Abs450 values of background controls lacking biotinylated DBP‐II were first subtracted from all other wells. The maximum signal was calculated from the average of four wells without serum, and inhibition was calculated using the following formula:
%inhibition=100×1−Xmax,
where X is the Abs450 of a well after background subtraction and max is the average of the four maximum signal samples without serum after background subtraction.
The % inhibition values were measured for each serum dilution in triplicate on different plates, and average values were plotted in GraphPad Prism 9. The data were fit using a normalized dose–response curve with a variable slope:
Y=100/1+ID50XHillSlope,
where X is the serum dilution, Y is the % inhibition, and HillSlope and ID50 are calculated parameters corresponding to the slope of the curve and the dilution at which 50% inhibition occurs, respectively.
