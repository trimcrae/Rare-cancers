## Abstract

## Background
The Plasmodium falciparum phenotypic characteristics (cytoadherence and rosetting), and the concentration of Histidine Rich Protein 2 (HRP2) in serum are associated with severe malaria in regions like Africa and Asia. Also, HRP2 has recently been associated as a P. falciparum virulence factor. Parasites lacking pfhrp2 gene were first reported in the Peruvian Amazon, however, there are not yet reports of the study of their phenotypes. For this research we decided to characterize the phenotype and genotype of P. falciparum field isolates from the Peruvian Amazon with different pfhrp2 patterns (positive or negative) cultivated in vitro.

## Methods
P. falciparum field isolates (n = 5) from the Peruvian Amazon region were isolated and cultured in vitro. Three isolates had a positive result or pfhrp2 positive pattern and two of them were negatives (pfhrp2 negative pattern) by HRP2 Rapid diagnostic test. Then, these results were confirmed by conventional PCR. The following phenotypic characterization assays were tested: (i) Cytoadherence capacity (evaluated against Chondroitin Sulfate A (CSA) protein and Human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVEC cells)), and (ii) rosetting formation. Genotypic characterization assays were carried out by PCR (gene detection) and qPCR assays for pfhrp2 and pfhrp3 gene expression. Additionally, we evaluate the pfhrp2/3 genes and flanking genes stability using long-term cultures of these field isolates during a year.

## Results
Two of the three isolates with pfhrp2 positive pattern showed rosetting formation (R + , CS-, Hu-) and cytoadherence to CSA protein (R-, CS + , Hu-), on the other hand the two field isolates with pfhrp2 negative pattern showed adhesion to HUVEC cells (R-, CS-, Hu +). 3D7 strain was used for normalization during qPCR assays. We identified that pfhrp2 gene expression levels were higher in the field isolates with pfhrp2 positive pattern, while pfhrp3 gene expression levels were similar or lower. All cultured field isolates showed genomic stability of pfhrp2 gene and flanking genes during the long-term in vitro culture. However, one field isolate showed two pfhrp3 patterns during the culture due to the presence of two parasite genotypes populations, as corroborated by microsatellite markers.

## Conclusions
P. falciparum Peruvian field isolates with pfhrp2 positive pattern showed cytoadherence to Chondroitin sulfate A protein (R-, CS + , Hu-) or positive characteristics for rosetting (R + , CS-, Hu-); pfhrp2 negative parasites showed cytoadherence to HUVEC cells (R-, CS-, Hu +). In this small set of Peruvian field isolates, a clear cytoadherence and resolution profile was observed for the different pfhrp2 patterns. These findings should be interpreted as preliminary and require further verification in larger isolated panels.

## Background
Phenotypic characteristics of Plasmodium falciparum as cytoadherence and rosetting phenotypes contribute to the obstruction of blood flow in the microcapillaries – and are associated with severe forms of malaria [ , ]. Duffy binding-like domain-α, has been shown to mediate rosetting and endothelial binding of Plasmodium falciparum-infected erythrocytes (iRBC) through heparin sulfate, blood group A antigen, and complement receptor 1 (CR1) [ – ]. PfEMP1, RIFIN and STEVOR are other proteins expressed at the surface of the parasitized red blood cell that are postulated to mediated rosetting and sequestration [ ]. Cellular adhesion assays have been developed to evaluate the cytoadherence/rosseting profile of P. falciparum parasites in vitro conditions such as: (i) employing Human Umbilical Vein Endothelial Cells (HUVECs) [ ], (ii) rosseting assays [ , ], and (iii) using Chondroitin Sulfate A (CSA) protein attached to a solid substrate [ ].
pfhrp2 and pfhrp3 gene deletion was reported in parasites from the Peruvian Amazon [ – ], these deletions leads to misdiagnosis and false-negative results by HRP2-based Rapid Diagnostics Tests (RDTs), a point of care alternative method to microscopy diagnosis in rural areas [ ]. The histidine-rich protein 2 (HRP2), is a 60–105 kDa protein excreted by all erythrocytic stages of the parasite and it is characterized by a high antigenic capacity, it is believed that it might be involved in the pro-coagulation phase, impeding a constant blood flow, allowing rosettes generation and cytoadherence of iRBC to other cell types and therefore contributing to the pathogenicity of malaria [ - ].
Compared to Africa, severe malaria cases in the Amazonian region of Peru are relatively rare [ ], probably reflecting a combination of phenotypic and genotypic differences in parasites and host genetic and immunological factors, which together may contribute to differences in cytoadherence and rosette profiles, however, in the Amazon region there are no phenotype studies from Pf parasites and its relation with pfhrp2 gene deletion. The aim of this study was to characterize the phenotype and genotype of Pf isolates from the Peruvian Amazon with different patterns of pfhrp2 expression (based on a positive or negative detection of pfhrp2 gene by PCR assays and RDT results); additionally, we performed molecular assays to assess the frequency of deletions of pfhrp2 and pfhrp3 genes and flanking genes in long-term in vitro cultures and characterize pfhrp2/pfhrp3 gene expression.

## Methods

## Collection and culture of field isolates
P. falciparum field isolates were collected from blood from Pf infected individuals (diagnosed by microscopy) living in peri-urban and rural/riverine communities near Iquitos city, capital of Loreto region in the Peruvian Amazon region, between 2008 and 2009 (Table  ). After participants signed the informed consent form, 10 mL of heparinized whole blood and two filter paper were collected by venipuncture; in parallel, one HRP2 rapid diagnostic test (ICT Malaria Combo Cassette Test, Lot number: 31902) was performed. The first generation of parasites adapted to in vitro cultures were cryopreserved and then used for the different assays, to maintain proximity to the original parasite line, in total six samples were isolated and adapted and we assigned them as F04, F06, F13, F23 and F32 (Fig.  ).

 

## Parasites, cell lines, proteins, and culture
Biological resources were employed for the assays. The ITG parasite line was obtained through BEI Resources Repository, NIAID, NIH (MRA-327, MR4/BEI Resources, VA, USA), and then the CS2, FCR3, Dd2, and 3D7 parasite lines were obtained from the Center for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) (Atlanta, GA, USA). The Chondroitin sulfate A protein (CSA) from the human placenta tissue and Human Umbilical Vein Endothelial Cells (HUVEC cells) were obtained from MR4/BEI Resources. The AFR line was isolated from a traveler coming back from Ivory Coast in 2007 and was cultivated and cryopreserved at Malaria Laboratory at Universidad Peruana Cayetano Heredia (Lima, Peru) following all ethical institutional regulations.
All parasite lines and Peruvian field isolates were maintained in O + human RBCs at 5% hematocrit in RPMI-HEPES supplemented with 10% human pool serum AB +, according to standardized methods [ , , ]. Cultures were maintained in synchronized stages using the sorbitol methodology [ , ]. HUVEC cells were adapted to the laboratory, according MR4 protocols [ ].

## Long—term in vitro culture of P. falciparum
Peruvian field isolates that achieved parasitemia between 4 and 10% (in vitro conditions) were included in this assay and were cultivated for one year (365 days) obtaining a total of 182.5 parasite generations (PG) where one “PG” was considered for every 2 cultivate days. In order to avoid the formation of gametocytes, parasite cultures were maintained under standard in vitro conditions with regular replacement of the medium and monitoring of the cultures. The term “sub-cultivation” refers to the dilution or partitioning of the sample, which was carried out approximately 3 to 6 times a month depending on the growth rate of the parasite. And for each subculture 500 µL of parasite culture (at 5% hematocrit) was stored at −80ºC for molecular assays.

## Phenotypic characterization

## Rosetting assays
Cultures were synchronized using sorbitol to obtain mature trophozoites and schizonts at 5% parasitaemia. Afterwards, 10 µL of parasite was mixed gently with 7µL of 5%fetal bovine serum (SigmaAldrich, St, Louis, MO, USA), then 4 µL of 1 mg/mL acridine orange was added (SigmaAldrich) and 10 µL of this suspension was placed on a glass slide [ , ]. The slides were observed a 100X in immunofluorescence microscopy (Olympus BX61, Shinjuku, Tokyo, Japan). Rosetting assays were performed as previously described [ – ]. 100 iRBCs were counted by microscope and the number of iRBCs forming rosettes was determined when one, two or more uninfected RBCs surrounded an iRBC, these were counted and expressed as percentages, and the field isolates that showed this characteristic were named as R +. Uninfected RBCs and AFR parasite lines were considered as negative and positive control, respectively for this assay.

## Cytoadherence assays of P. falciparum iRBCs to cell line HUVEC
Cell-culture treated coverslips (Corning, Glendale, AZ, USA) were placed inside of each well of 24-well cell culture plate (SigmaAldrich), then 1 mL of M199 complete medium (SigmaAldrich) was added to each well and incubated at 37º C for 1 h. Then, 1 mL containing 5 × 104 HUVEC cells were added per each well. To let cell-cytoadherence to the coverslip, the plate was incubated overnight at 37º C at 5% CO2. Cytoadherence pattern was evaluated when the cells reached 80% confluence. Subsequently, 0.5 mL of parasite culture at 5% of parasitaemia (8PG) was centrifugated at 100 × g for 5 min at room temperature. iRBCs were washed three times and resuspended with the same medium until reach 2% of parasitaemia. Next, 500 µL of culture suspension (iRBC at 2% parasitemia) was added to each coverslip and incubated at 37º C for 60 min, manually moving the plate gently every 15 min. After that, the coverslips were carefully submerged in Petri plates (Corning) with RPMI 1640 medium (SigmaAldrich) to remove free erythrocytes. The coverslips were fixed with 1% glutaraldehyde (SigmaAldrich) for 60 min, washed with distilled water, and stained with 1% Giemsa (SigmaAldrich). Then, the coverslips were mounted on glass slides and examined by light microscopy. All cytoadherence HUVEC assays were performed as previously described [ , ]. 100 HUVEC cells were counted and the number of iRBCs bounded was calculated, the field isolates that showed this characteristic were named Hu +. ITG parasite line was employed as a positive control of these assays, as it has been reported that this parasite line binds strongly to ICAM-1 [ ].

## Adherence of P. falciparum-infected RBCs to CSA protein
CSA protein solution at 2µ/mL (PBS, pH 7.2) was placed in a spot (~ 4 mm diameter) within a Petri plate and left overnight at 4 °C. Then, it was blocked with 20 µL of 2% BSA for 2 h at room temperature. After aspirating the BSA, 15 µL of 2% of the parasite culture suspension (at 8PG) in RPMI medium was added (SigmaAldrich). After 40 min. of incubation, the plates were washed three times with PBS (pH 7.2). The attached iRBCs were fixed with 2% glutaraldehyde (SigmaAldrich), stained with 1% Giemsa for 10 min and counted by light microscopy. All assays were performed as previously [ ] 
The values of adherence to CSA protein were expressed by number of iRBC per mm2, and the field isolates that showed adherence to CSA were named as CS +. CS2 parasite line was used as positive control, since it binds to CSA and hyaluronic acid receptors and not to ICAM-1 [ ]. For all described assays, uninfected RBCs were employed as a negative control.

## Criteria for phenotypic classification
Rosetting and cytoadherence assays have been performed according to previously established protocols [ – , ]. For rosette testing, at least 100 infected red blood cells (iRBCs) were counted, and rosettes were defined as iRBCs that were bound to one or more non-infected erythrocytes. Field isolates were classified as rosette-positive (R +) if rosettes were consistently observed in at least three independent biological replicates above background levels; isolates that did not show detectable rosettes were classified as R −.
For the cytoadhesion test on HUVEC cells, the binding was quantified as iRBCs per endothelial cell per 100 endothelial cells. Isolates were classified as HUVEC-positive (Hu +) if iRBC adhesion was detected above the negative control level in all biological replicates; isolates without detectable adhesion were classified as Hu − 
Isolates were classified as CSA-binding positive (CS +) if specific adhesion (iRBCs per mm2) to CSA-coated surfaces was reproducibly detected above the background levels observed under control conditions, and non-detectable adhesion isolates were classified as CS-.

## Genotypic characterization

## DNA extraction
At the first time of sample collection (0 PG), whole blood from the participant was impregnated in filter paper for later DNA extraction and determination of P. falciparum genotype, following a previous reported protocol [ ]. Genomic DNA was extracted at trophozoite stage cultures (~ 5% of parasitemia) using the phenol–chloroform-isoamyl alcohol method [ ]. The precipitate was dried at room temperature and was resuspended in TE Buffer (10 mM Tris–HCl (pH 8.0), 1 mM EDTA (pH 8.0)). The samples were quantified using Qubit™ dsDNA HS Assay Kit (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) and were stored at −20 °C to further molecular assays.

## PCR assays
PCR was performed to test genomic stability of pfhrp2, pfhrp3 genes in long-term in vitro cultures for the identification of genotype variations (deletions of the pfhrp2, pfhrp3, and their flanking genes), utilizing previously described [ ]. All PCR products were separated and analyzed at 2% agarose gel (Fig.  ). It should be noted that conventional PCR presence and absence tests are qualitative assays and will primarily detect the predominant population of parasites in a sample. Therefore, this approach does not allow the quantification of subpopulations with a low frequency of gene deletion in mixed infections.
 

## Microsatellites markers applied to P. falciparum long-term cultures.
A protocol described by Manrique et al. 2015 [ ] was used, which employs 14 microsatellites for Pf genotype characterization [ ]. The labeled PCR products were then separated on ABI PRISM 3100 Genetic Analyzer system electrophoresis (Thermo Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). The GeneMapper v3.0 software was used for microsatellite characterization. Peaks with more than 50 RFU (Relative fluorescence units) in the electropherogram were interpreted as alleles, and if an additional peak of at least one-third the height of the predominant peak was seen it was also scored as allele (Fig.  ).

## qPCR assays
Pf field isolates were cultured up at 8 PG (≥ 2% parasitemia at ring stage), before synchronization for harvesting RNA for further assays. The parasite cultures were transferred to 15-mL tubes and centrifuged at 1800 rpm for 5 min. The supernatants were removed and two volumes of 0.25% (P/V) saponin (SigmaAldrich, St, Louis, MO, USA) were added to the iRBC pellets, which were then mixed by pipetting 10 times. After incubation at room temperature for 2 min., 10 mL of chill PBS was added; the mix was resuspended and then centrifuged at 5000 rpm for 20 min. The supernatant was discarded; and a parasite pellet was resuspended in 100 µL PBS and 400 µL of Binding—lysis buffer (Roche Molecular Systems, Inc., Pleasanton, CA, EE. UU), mixed by immersion and vortexed and then stored at − 80 °C until further use in RNA assays (Fig.  ).
Total RNA was extracted using HP RNA Isolation Kit following the manufacturer's instructions (Roche Molecular Systems, Inc., Pleasanton, CA, EE. UU). Total RNA (~ 500 ng) was transcribed into cDNA using Transcriptor First Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (Roche Molecular Systems) with random hexamer primers. qPCR assays for pfhrp2 and pfhrp3 genes and analysis were performed according to Baker J. et al. [ ].
The strain P. falciparum 3D7 was used as a calibration for the relative gene expression analysis since it carries the unchanged genes for pfhrp2 and pfhrp3 and is the standard strain used for reference. In addition, RNA from laboratory-adapted parasite strains CS2, ITG and Dd2 were included as controls for the expression of the marker. Field isolates lacking pfhrp2 served as biological surrogates for parasites negative for HRP2. A standardized laboratory-adapted reference strain, which is recognized as a limitation, was not included in this study (Fig. ).
 The raw qPCR data obtained from the LightCycler 480 II system was analyzed with LinQPCR 11.1 (https://www.gear-genomics.com/rdml-tools/) to calculate the individual PCR efficiency of each reaction. Relative expression levels were determined by the Ct corrected method, where Ct of each gene in the sample was compared with Ct of the same gene in the highest expression level. These calculations were performed before normalization.
GenEx enterprise 5.0 Analysis Software (www.genex.gene-quantification.info/) was used to determine the four most stable genes with values of M < 1, which were subsequently used to determine the normalization factor for each sample, according to Vandesompele et al. [ ]. The relative (normalized) expression levels were obtained by dividing the expression quantities by the respective normalization factor, that is, the levels of expression of the other genes were normalized using the normalization factor of the most stable genes in this study, while these last genes were normalized among each other. The reference genes used for all normalization steps were PF140758, PFB106c, pfseryl and pfbactin1.
To control for differences in parasite input between samples, the transcription levels of pfhrp2 and pfhrp3 were first normalized in each sample using a geometric mean factor (M < 1) derived from the most stable parasite reference genes (geNorm algorithm). The relative expression levels were then calculated by dividing the efficiency-corrected target gene expression values by this standardization factor (Fig. ).
 Finally, all (normalized) expression levels of the different samples/genes were divided by the level of expression of the control strain 3D7, which was analyzed in this experiment. This method is known as rescaling. Thus, the 3D7 strain was given a predefined expression level of "1", and other samples could then be compared to determine if a gene was over-expressed (value > 1) or under-expressed (value < 1) compared to the 3D7 parasite strain. Two-fold increases or decreases in the levels of gene expression were considered as significant differences (p ≤ 0.05) for comparison purposes between samples.

## Data analysis
To compare medians and standard deviations between the phenotypic characteristic of the cultivated parasites GraphPad PRISM software (GraphPad Software, La Jolla USA) was used (t-student). All performed tests, involved at least three biological replicates and the representative results were used for analysis.

## Results
Based on RDT test results and PCR assays for pfhrp2/3 genes detection, three P. falciparum field isolates (F04, F06, and F13) were classified as pfhrp2 positive pattern and two field isolates (F23 and F32) as pfhrp2 negative pattern (Table  ). The five Peruvian field isolates were adapted to in vitro conditions and four (except F04) of them were long term cultured for one year to determine their phenotypic and genotypic characteristics.

 

## Phenotypic characterization
At the phenotypic level, the field isolates showed specific characteristics and have limited adhesion capabilities for rosetting with non-infected RBC (R +), adhesion to HUVEC cells (Hu +), or CSA protein (CS +) (Table  ). When the capacity of forming rosettes in Peruvian field isolates was evaluated, only F06 (pfhrp2 positive pattern) showed positive a rosetting activity (10.0 ± 2%) however, its capacity was three—times lower in comparison of AFR parasite line (33.2% ± 2%). Chondroitin Sulfate A (CSA) protein was used for the cytoadherence assay, only F13 field isolate (pfhrp2 positive pattern) showed adherence to this protein of 1033 ± 14 iRBC per mm2. Nevertheless, this value was low in comparison to the positive strain control CS2 (5366 ± 15 iRBC per mm2). HUVEC cells express the I-CAM1 receptor in their surfaces, these receptors are abundant in the endothelium of venules and interacts with the iRBC by members of PfEMP1 family [ ]. The F32 field isolate (pfhrp2 negative pattern) showed a 2 ± 1 Hu + value, and the F23 field isolate (pfhrp2 negative pattern) showed 122 ± 7 Hu +, both values were lower in comparison with the positive control ITG parasite line (697 ± 38 Hu +).

## Genotypic characterization

## Pfhrp2 and pfhrp3 genomic stability
The Peruvian parasite lines (F06, F13, F23 and F32) were evaluated for 182.5 PG (one year), for determination of pfhrp2 and pfhrp3 genes and flanking genes stability during long-term in vitro culture conditions (Fig.  ). Interestingly, during one year of in vitro culture, conventional PCR did not detect any change in the detectable presence or absence of pfhrp2, pfhrp3 or flanking genes in the field isolates analyzed. (see Supplementary Table S1). F04 was excluded because it never reached parasitemia higher than 4%.
The pattern of pfhrp3 showed discrepant results between the 0 PG (filter paper) samples, specifically F06 (pfhrp3-) and F32 (pfhrp3 +), compared to those observed during the long-term in vitro culture (182.5 PG). The detected pfhrp3 pattern during the long-term in vitro culture was reversed, F06 displayed a pfhrp3 + pattern and F32 displayed a pfhrp3- pattern. (Table  ). The apparent differences in pfhrp3 detection between genotypes determined at 0 PG and those observed in long-term culture are probably due to the presence of a mixed population of parasites in the initial sampling and to the selection of the dominant genotype during in vitro adaptation, rather than to a de novo gene deletion event.

## Microsatellites for P. falciparum
Fourteen microsatellites were used to corroborate clonal nature and the presence of sub-clones in the Peruvian field isolates. F13, F23 and F32 were clonal during the long-term culture (one year). However, F06 field isolated (pfhrp3- at 0PG) had different alleles between 92nd and 93rd PG (Table  ),this might explain the detection of pfhrp3 gene during the long-term culture assays (pfhrp3- → pfhrp3 +).

 Microsatellite profiles were evaluated at separate time points during the long-term culture. Mixed haplotypes were not detected above a predefined score threshold before or after observed F06 haplotype switching. However, since microsatellite typing based on conventional PCR and capillary electrophoresis primarily detects dominant alleles, the presence of minor subpopulations of the parasite with alternative haplotypes below the detection limit cannot be excluded.

## Relative expression of pfhrp2/pfhrp3 genes in field isolates
A high expression of Pfhrp2 gene expression was observed in CS2 and ITG strains in comparison to 3D7 parasite line (p < 0.05). F04 and F13 also showed high Pfhrp2 gene expression, confirming their pfhrp2 positive pattern found by RDT and PCR. A pfhrp2 low expression level was observed in F06 field isolate and as expected there was no detectable pfhrp2 mRNA in F23 and F32 field isolates (both pfhrp2 negative pattern). A positive pfhrp3 gene expression was observed for F06 and F13, and no pfhrp3 gene expression in F04, F32, and F23 field isolates.

## Descriptive analysis: phenotypic profiles observed in isolates with different pfhrp2 patterns.
Pfhrp2 positive pattern field isolates (F04, F06, and F13) showed variable levels of mRNA expression (Low and High) for pfhrp2 gene in contrast to pfhrp3 gene that showed low levels of expression during the long-term in vitro cultures. Within pfhrp2 positive pattern, one pfhrp2 + /pfhrp3 + strain (F13) was characterized only as CS binding strain (R-,CS +, Hu-), however, within the others field isolates (pfhrp2 + /pfhrp3-), one of them (F06) presented rosetting capacity (R +, CS-,Hu-) and the other (F04) did not show any particular phenotype (R-,CS-,Hu-) (Table  ). Pfhrp2 negative pattern field isolates (F23 and F32) showed no mRNA expression to pfhrp3 gene. F23 and F32 were typified as pfhrp2-/pfhrp3- and pfhrp2-/pfhrp3 + , respectively. Both field isolates were characterized only as HUVEC binding strains (R-, CS-, Hu +) (Table  ).

## Discussion

## Phenotype characterization of pfhrp2 positive/negative pattern
Rosetting is the phenomenon during which the iRBC binds to uninfected RBCs, engaging parasite ligands and RBC surface receptors [ ]. This phenotype (R +) has been directly linked to the ability of P. falciparum to contribute to microvascular obstruction, cerebral sequestration and the incrementation of parasite biomass generating complicated disease and severe infections [ ]. Studies about phenotypes of P. falciparum field isolates have been limited in South America. Wahlgren et al. [ ] used P. falciparum field isolates from 75 patients with severe malaria cases from Africa and South America (Colombia), and found that rosetting formation capacity was present in 49% of all isolates. Also, rosetting frequency was higher in Africa (55%) than South America isolates (38%); moreover, isolates from Africa showed 11% rosetting activity while the ones from the South America, presented a higher value of 16.2%. In our study, only F06 field isolated showing a pfhrp2 positive pattern presented 10% of rosetting activity, a close value to those found for isolates from Africa.
The rosetting capacity (R +) gives the parasite, the advantage to establish an effective infection by merozoites [ ]. However, according to our results Peruvian strains, independently of their pfhrp2 pattern, would not be taking advantage of forming rosettes because of their low value of rosetting capacity or the low frequency of this phenotype (one Peruvian field strain was R + and the others four were R-), which lead us to think that these parasites might display other novel invasive mechanisms [ ].
An important characteristic of malaria during pregnancy is the kidnapping of iRBC in the placenta intervillous spaces [ ]. The P. falciparum parasites that can attach to CSA protein, presented a specific type of PfEMP1, named VAR2CSA trough the ID1-DBL2Xb region. In the present study, only one field isolated (F13, pfhrp2 positive pattern) featured adherence to CSA protein (CS +). In the Peruvian Amazon region the cases of malaria during pregnancy is a serious public health problem around Iquitos [ ]; and further surveillance studies are necessary to estimate the real proportion of this CS + parasites that are circulating in the Amazon Region.
The adhesion of iRBCs to endothelial cells (e.g. HUVEC cells) leads to increased expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines and transfers from iRBC into the endothelial cells different products, such as membrane components and cytoplasmic malaria antigens such as aldolase, and HRP2 protein [ ], contradictory and according to our results, only two field isolates (F23 and F32), both pfhrp2 negative pattern, showed association with HUVEC cells (Hu +) in vitro conditions. Recently, Prya et al. [ ], demonstrated that the HRP2 protein increase the expression of ICAM-1 and VCAM-1 in Blood–Brain Barrier hCMEC/D3 Cell Line. One possible advantage for the P. falciparum is that triggering an inflammatory pathway leads to increased expression of cytoadherence molecules on the endothelial surface. However, the capacity to bind HUVEC cells of the Peruvian P. falciparum strains is intriguing because parasites that do not express HRP2 protein would not require the expression of adhesion receptors over endothelial cells and might produce another way to escape the clearance in the spleen.

## Genotype characterization of pfhrp2 positive/negative pattern
The location of pfhrp2 and pfhrp3 genes in the sub telomeric regions makes them very susceptible to rapid evolution by the non-condensed state of chromatin during clonal mitosis in an infected human red blood cell (asexual phase), which contribute to structural breaks that favor the loss or duplication events of certain genes [ ]. In this study, pfhrp2 and pfhrp3 genes stability and their flanking genes were assessed in long-term cultures over 182.5 PG (one year), in four Peruvian field isolates with different pfhrp2 and pfhrp3 genotypes combinations. We additionally hypothesize that spontaneous deletion or loss events of pfhrp2/pfhrp3 genes could occur during this period (one year), due to the characteristic chromosomal instability of sub-telomeric regions during mitosis. The above was termed the ‘breakage and healing model’, which explained the existence of heterogenous patterns of telomeric repeats after their eventual recovery [ ]. The four Peruvian field isolates showed genomic stability of these genes during one-year culture and any spontaneous deletion was detected in pfhrp2. However, further studies extending the period of culture for 2–5 years are necessary to determine the real stability of these genes.
The use of microsatellite markers has been useful to discriminate P. falciparum population for epidemiological studies in Peru [ ]. In the present study, this tool allowed us to identify two parasite populations with different pattern to pfhrp3 gene inside of F06 field isolate: one population with pfhrp3 + genotype and a 240pb allele for MS14 was observed since 0 to 92 PG, and another population pfhrp3- genotype and a 237pb allele for MS14 was detected from 93 to 183 PG. The difference of these alleles could be explaining the difference in pfhrp3 pattern in F06 field isolate.
F32 field isolate showed a discrepancy between the 0 PG and its molecular characterization, because the filter paper PCR results presented a pfhrp3 + gene and then at 8PG it changed to pfhrp3- genotype, despite this fact, microsatellite markers results showed a clonal population during all the long-term culture. These results suggest the event that some parasites may have growth advantages over other ones and the loss of parasite sub-populations during culture adaptation [ ].
A previous study reported that pfhrp2 gene expression can be variable between strains from different parts of the world, including South American strains which can present different low patterns of this gene expression [ ]. The field isolates and strains used in the present study, were normalized against the 3D7 strain (pfhrp2 + /pfhrp3 +). For the samples with pfhrp2 + genotype, F04 and F13 field isolates presented a high level of expression of pfhrp2 in contrast to F06 that showed lower expression. The assessment of the levels of pfhrp2 gene expression could be a useful tool to evaluate the classification of field strains by RDT or ELISA for HRP2 protein detection [ ].
The genetics of pfhrp2-deletion in malaria parasites, and the implications of this gene lost in their phenotype, and epidemiological characteristics are elusive and need to be studied in detail [ ]. In sub-Saharan Africa, the key drivers of pfhrp2-deletion selection are low malaria transmission and a high frequency of people seeking treatment and being correctly treated on the basis of diagnosis with a PfHRP2-based RDT being this hypothesis one example of the emergence of resistance of a pathogen to a diagnostic test [ ].
This study has some important limitations that need to be recognized. First, the number of field isolates analysed was low (n = 5) and included only two parasites that were negative for pfhrp2. Therefore, phenotypic differences observed between isolates with different pfhrp2 patterns should be interpreted strictly as preliminary observations and not as evidence of a phenotypic-genotypic association. Secondly, since only a limited number of isolates from a single geographical area have been studied, the findings may not be representative for the populations of P. falciparum circulating in other endemic regions. Third, because we have not generated genome-wide or breakpoint resolution data to determine the size and extent of deletion in chromosome 8 in these isolates. In Peru, deletion of pfhrp2 is often part of larger subtelomeric deletions, which may involve other loci; any phenotypic differences observed in this population may reflect wider structural differences rather than the state of pfhrp2 itself. Future work involving WGS (or targeted breakpoint mapping) will be necessary to assess whether specific deletion structures correlate with cytoadherence and rosette phenotypes.
One methodological consideration is that the evaluation of the long-term culture deletion of pfhrp2 and pfhrp3 was based on conventional PCR and microsatellite typing, which are qualitative approaches. These methods cannot detect or quantify subpopulations with low gene-deletion frequencies when mixed with non-deleted parasites. Therefore, although these approaches did not detect any changes in deletion patterns during the culture period, it cannot be excluded that minor subpopulations carrying deletions may have appeared or fluctuated below the detection threshold. Quantitative approaches such as digital PCR or deep sequencing would be necessary to resolve the dynamics of mixed populations of parasites in vitro over a long period of culture.
Another limitation of the transcriptional analysis is the lack of a laboratory-adapted negative control pfhrp2 reference strain. Although field isolates negative for pfhrp2 have been included as biological comparators, future studies with well characterised HRP2 negative reference lines will further enhance comparative transcript analysis.
Finally, while our results provide a first descriptive description of cytoadherence and rosette profiles in Peruvian field isolates, larger studies with expanded isolate panels and higher-resolution genomic approaches will be needed to determine whether these phenotypes are consistent with the deletion of pfhrp2.

## Conclusion
In this study, we conducted a descriptive phenotypic and genotypic characterization of field isolates of Plasmodium falciparum from the Peruvian Amazon region that exhibit a variable pfhrp2 pattern. Using in vitro assays, we observed heterogeneous cytoadherence and rosette profiles for the analyzed isolates. However, due to the limited number of field isolates evaluated, these findings should be interpreted as preliminary. No definitive association between the pfhrp2 status and the phenotype of the parasite can be derived from this data set.
Despite these limitations, this study provides useful in vitro observations of the biological consequences of the deletion of pfhrp2. Larger studies with expanded isolates collections and higher resolution genomic approaches will be needed to determine whether the phenotypic models described here are reproducible and biologically meaningful.
