## Abstract
Gliomas, including astrocytoma, oligodendrogliomas, and glioblastomas, represent the most common type of primary malignant brain tumors in adults and a significant burden in pediatric neuro-oncology. Despite being traditionally diagnosed according to histopathological features, the classification of gliomas has evolved significantly with the integration of molecular biomarkers, now essential components of the World Health Organization Central Nervous System 5 (WHO CNS5) classification. This review synthesizes key established and emerging molecular markers that define glioma subtypes and impact diagnosis, prognosis, and therapeutic decisions. Core biomarkers include IDH1/2 mutations, which are hallmarks of lower-grade gliomas and confer improved prognosis; 1p/19q codeletion, pathognomonic for oligodendrogliomas; and TERT promoter mutations, often associated with poor outcomes. Additional markers such as TP53, ATRX, and H3K27M mutations provide crucial subtype and grade-specific information, particularly in astrocytic and diffuse midline gliomas. Novel alterations, including BRAF V600E, EGFRvIII, and MET, NTRK, or ALK gene fusions, are increasingly recognized for their therapeutic relevance in targeted treatment approaches. Epigenetic profiling, including DNA methylation signatures, has further refined glioma taxonomy and classification. Emerging markers such as MYCN/MYC amplifications, PIK3CA/PTEN pathway alterations, and ZFTA::RELA fusions, although more prominent in other CNS tumors, may offer insights into tumor biology or therapeutic resistance in gliomas. This review provides an updated evidence-based overview of classical and novel biomarkers, highlighting their biological roles and clinical implications, and guiding future directions for precision neuro-oncology.

## Introduction
Brain and other central nervous system (CNS) tumors are the most common and most lethal types of cancer in children aged 0–14 and the 2nd most prevalent tumor in adolescents aged 15–19 (Ostrom et al. ). In adults over 40 years of age, brain and CNS tumors represent the 8th most common type of cancer (9th among men and 5th among females) with an average annual age-adjusted incidence rate of 42.85 per 100,000 inhabitants (Ostrom et al. ). In adults, the 5-year survival of brain cancer is approximately 36% (Schaff and Mellinghoff ).
Brain and CNS tumors constitute a heterogeneous group of neoplasms that vary widely in terms of incidence, biological behavior, and clinical outcomes. Traditionally, the classification and diagnosis of these tumors have relied heavily on histopathological examination, which involves assessing tissue architecture, cellular morphology, and staining patterns. However, with advancements in molecular biology and genomics, the field of neuro-oncology has undergone a paradigm shift (Barbaro et al. ). The integration of molecular biomarkers into tumor classification has not only improved diagnostic accuracy but also facilitated a more personalized approach to treatment (Barden and Omuro ).
The WHO CNS5 classification categorizes CNS tumors into several main types based on histological and molecular characteristics (Louis et al. ). These include, among others, gliomas (e.g., astrocytomas, oligodendrogliomas, glioblastomas), glioneuronal and neuronal tumors, ependymal tumors, choroid plexus tumors, embryonal tumors like medulloblastomas, and other variants such as meningiomas, mesenchymal tumors, melanocytic tumors, hematolymphoid tumors, and metastatic tumors. The most prevalent brain and CNS tumors are intracranial metastases from systemic cancers, meningiomas, and gliomas (McFaline-Figueroa and Lee ). Histopathological examination has long been the cornerstone for diagnosing brain and CNS tumors, performed by the microscopic evaluation of tissue samples obtained through biopsy or surgical resection. The process typically starts with the preparation of tissue sections that are stained using standard techniques, such as Hematoxylin and Eosin (H&E), to evaluate cell morphology and tissue architecture. This method allows pathologists to classify tumors based on their cellular appearance, growth patterns, and tissue organization. Although histopathology remains a critical tool in diagnosing and classifying brain tumors, its limitations are apparent, especially when tumors exhibit overlapping histological features or when atypical presentations arise (Shetty et al. ). The rapid growth of digital tools and artificial intelligence (AI) approaches nowadays is facilitating the diagnosis by using these techniques (Borah et al. ; Zhu et al. ); however, these systems are neither infallible, evidencing the need for integrative and multidisciplinary approaches. In this sense, the analysis of the molecular profile of brain and CNS tumors, particularly through immunohistochemical (IHC) analysis, represents a critical tool for understanding biological and clinical features of these tumors. IHC involves the application of antibodies to tumor tissue sections to detect the expression of particular proteins, which can provide valuable diagnostic and prognostic information. In comparison to previous versions, the WHO CNS5 places an unprecedented emphasis not only on histopathology but also on molecular diagnostics (Louis et al. ). This shift acknowledges the limitations of morphology-based classification and recognizes that certain tumors with similar histological appearances may exhibit vastly different genetic profiles, prognoses, and therapeutic responses (Garrett et al. ). Beyond well-established biomarkers, emerging genomic and epigenetic markers continue to refine CNS tumor classification, shaping the next frontier in neuro-oncology (Chan et al. ; Boots-Sprenger et al. ; Reitman et al. ; Yoshida et al. ; Jaunecker et al. ; Chung et al. ). These discoveries have not only improved classification but also paved the way for new therapeutic targets, fostering the development of precision medicine strategies.
This article aims to provide a comprehensive overview of the histopathological and molecular landscape of gliomas, from classical diagnostic markers to emerging biomarkers that are reshaping the field. By decoding the intricate interplay between histopathology and molecular pathology, we seek to highlight how these advancements are transforming the diagnosis, prognosis, and treatment of brain tumors. Understanding these developments is crucial for clinicians, pathologists, and researchers as they navigate the rapidly evolving landscape of CNS tumor classification and personalized medicine (Fig.  ).

 

## Classification and Characteristics of Gliomas
The classification of brain and CNS tumors is a complex process that integrates histopathological, molecular, and genetic information to improve diagnostic accuracy and treatment strategies. CNS tumors are broadly categorized into several major types based on their histological and molecular characteristics, as outlined by the WHO CNS5 classification (Louis et al. ). According to this, gliomas are included as a first group, designed as “gliomas, glioneuronal, and neuronal tumors”. Herein, several tumor groups formerly called gliomas, diffuse astrocytomas and oligodendrogliomas, other astrocytic tumors, other gliomas, neuronal and glioneuronal tumors, and ependymomas (Mlika et al. ) are included.
Within this new category, gliomas are classified based on their circumscribed or diffuse nature, typical age of occurrence, and aggressiveness. Gliomas are the most common primary brain tumors, which can affect all areas of the brain (Mesfin et al. ). These tumors are thought to develop from neural stem or progenitor cells that carry tumor-initiating genetic alterations. The WHO grading system for gliomas is based on a range of histological characteristics, including cellular atypia, proliferative patterns, and the presence of necrosis, allowing its classification into grade 1 (least aggressive and benign) to grade 4 (most aggressive and malignant) (Toader et al. ). Three main groups of diffuse gliomas have been defined: one for adult gliomas (which can be grade 2, 3, or 4) and two pediatric subtypes (either low-grade or high-grade (Mlika et al. ). In adults, diffusely infiltrating gliomas can be categorized into three distinct types, each differing in disease progression, treatment response, and prognosis (Weller et al. ). Oligodendrogliomas have the most favorable prognosis; astrocytomas exhibit an intermediate outcome, while glioblastomas (GBMs) are associated with poor survival rates. Oligodendrogliomas are categorized as low grade (grade II) or high grade/anaplastic (grade III). Grade II tumors may evolve into grade III tumors over time. According to the CNS 5, oligodendrogliomas are identified as isocitrate dehydrogenase (IDH)-mutant and 1p/19-codeleted (Torp et al. ). Astrocytomas are identified as IDH mutant (grades II, III, IV), whereas GBMs are characterized as IDH wild type (grade IV). GBMs, in particular, account for approximately 50% of malignant brain tumors in adults (Schaff and Mellinghoff ) and are histologically defined as grade IV diffuse astrocytoma characterized by high cellularity, significant mitotic activity, and prominent microvascular proliferation and necrosis (Mikkelsen et al. ). Primary GBMs develop rapidly de novo in elderly patients, without clinical or histologic evidence of a malignant precursor lesion; meanwhile, secondary GBMs appear in younger subjects and progress from low-grade diffuse astrocytoma or anaplastic astrocytoma (Ohgaki and Kleihues ). Despite a few histological differences that can be recognized, each subtype exhibits differential genetic and epigenetic profiles.
Pediatric diffuse gliomas are classified into low-grade gliomas (LGG) and high-grade gliomas (HGG). In this population, pilocytic astrocytoma is the most frequent LGG, exhibiting well-circumscribed growth and favorable survival. Diffuse pediatric HGG often demonstrates aggressive clinical behavior (Weller et al. ).
The fifth edition of the WHO Classification of CNS tumors further refines glial, glioneuronal, and neuronal neoplasms into six principal divisions, integrating histopathology with molecular signatures: (1) adult-type diffuse gliomas (e.g., GBM, IDH-wildtype); (2) pediatric-type diffuse LGG (indolent phenotype); (3) pediatric-type diffuse HGG (aggressive biology); (4) circumscribed astrocytic gliomas (localized growth boundaries); (5) Glioneuronal and neuronal tumors (a diverse group of tumors, featuring neuronal differentiation); and (6) ependymal tumors (subclassified by anatomic site and genetic/epigenetic alterations) (Louis et al. ). Choroid plexus tumors are excluded from this grouping due to their epithelial lineage. DNA methylation profiling visualized via t-distributed stochastic neighbor embedding (t-SNE) confirms distinct epigenetic clusters among these groups (Jurmeister et al. ).
Several novel entities have been recognized in this novel classification: diffuse astrocytoma, MYB-1 or MYLB1-altered, polymorphous low-grade neuroepithelial tumor of the young; MAPK pathway-altered diffuse LGG; H3 G34-mutant diffuse hemispheric glioma; H3- and IDH-wildtype pediatric HGG; infant-type hemispheric glioma; high-grade astrocytoma with piloid features; provisional diffuse glioneuronal tumors with oligodendroglioma-like features; myxoid glioneuronal tumor; and multinodular and vacuolating neuronal tumor (Rigsby et al. ).
Diagnosis of certain subtypes, such as MAPK-driven LGG and pediatric HGGs lacking classical H3 or IDH mutations, requires a layered approach combining histology with defined molecular alterations (Patel et al. ). Nomenclatural refinements include redesignating diffuse midline gliomas as H3K27-altered and astroblastoma as MN1-altered, following CIMPACT update 6 recommendations. These principal updates to diffuse glioma taxonomy are (1) formal separation of adult-type versus pediatric-type diffuse gliomas to reflect distinct age distributions and molecular drivers; (2) consolidation of adult diffuse gliomas into astrocytoma, IDH-mutant, oligodendroglioma, IDH-mutant and 1p/19q-codeleted, and GBM, IDH-wildtype, leveraging NOS/NEC designations and intra-type grading; and (3) unified grading of IDH-mutant astrocytic tumors (WHO grades 2–4), with CDKN2A/B homozygous deletion serving as an automatic grade 4 criterion, while IDH-wildtype astrocytic tumors harboring TERT promoter mutation, EGFR amplification, or IDH-wildtype (WHO grade 4) (Louis et al. ) (Table  ).

 

## Relevant Biomarkers for Glioma
As we have previously reported, the molecular landscape of gliomas has undergone a profound transformation in recent years, redefining how these tumors are diagnosed, classified, and treated. Once characterized primarily by histopathological features, gliomas are now understood through a complex interplay of genetic, epigenetic, transcriptomic, and immunological alterations. Biomarkers such as IDH mutations, 1p/19q codeletion, and H3K27M have become cornerstones in modern diagnostic criteria, while others like PD-L1 expression, TERT promoter mutations, and diverse gene fusions highlight key pathways in tumor progression, immune evasion, and therapeutic resistance. The integration of these biomarkers not only enables refined classification and prognostication but also opens avenues for targeted therapies, personalized treatment strategies, and minimally invasive monitoring. In this section, we explore the major classes of glioma biomarkers, organized by their biological functions and translational relevance, to provide a comprehensive overview of their current and emerging roles in neuro-oncology.

## Biomarkers with Diagnostic and/or Molecular Classification

## IDH1/2 Mutations
IDH is an enzyme with two major isoforms, a cytoplasmic and a mitochondrial variant, named IDH1 and IDH2, respectively (Table  ). Physiologically, IDH mainly drives the conversion of isocitrate to α-ketoglutarate (α-KG). Other metabolites like D-2-hydroxyglutarate (D2HG) can also be produced due to residual actions of IDH and other enzymes; however, the accumulation of this product in non-pathological conditions is limited because of the activity of an endogenous D2HG dehydrogenase (D2HGDH), which catalyzes the conversion of D2HG to α-KG (Waitkus et al. ). In cancer, the mutated IDH gains a novel function, leading to the accumulation of D2HG, which influences both tumor cells and their microenvironment (Pirozzi and Yan ). The accumulation of this compound leads to significant epigenetic alterations (changing methylation patterns of DNA and histones), metabolic reprogramming, and impairs cell differentiation, thus explaining the carcinogenic role of IDH1/2 mutations (Waitkus et al. ).
Notably, mIDH2 inhibitors have also shown clinical promise in other malignancies. SH1573, a novel selective mIDH2 R140Q inhibitor, demonstrated potent suppression of 2-hydroxyglutarate production, favorable pharmacological properties, and safety in preclinical AML models, leading to its approval for clinical trials (CTR20200247) (Wang et al. ).
IDH mutations are a major signature of LGG (grade II and III oligodendrogliomas) (Matsutani ), and it is also present in 70%–80% of grades II–III astrocytomas and secondary GBMs (Konovalov et al. ). However, whereas oligodendrogliomas frequently exhibit 1p/19q codeletion with IDH1 mutations, astrocytomas are commonly accompanied by mutations in the TP53 and ATRX (Konovalov et al. ). Conversely, primary GBMs are mostly identified as IDH wild type, as these mutations rarely appear in these tumors (between 3 and 6% of recognized cases) (Labussiere et al. ; Han et al. ), defining this biomarker as a valuable tool for distinguishing between primary and secondary GBMs. Despite this, Hata et al. ( ) reported that primary GBMs arising from the insular cortex region commonly carry this mutation and present a similar biological signature to secondary GBMs. Overall, more than 90% of IDH mutations are IDH1 mutations, and within these, R132H represents more than 90% of cases (Xia et al. ).
IDH mutations are well-established prognostic biomarkers in gliomas, significantly influencing both overall survival (OS) and progression-free survival (PFS). As mutations of IDH1/2 are inversely associated with grade in diffuse glial tumors, the presence of IDH mutations is generally associated with a favorable prognosis (Labussiere et al. ; Sasaki et al. ). In more detail, a comprehensive meta-analysis of 55 studies involving 9,487 patients demonstrated that individuals with IDH1/2 mutations experienced a 61% reduction in mortality risk (HR = 0.39, 95% CI 0.34–0.45) and a 58% decrease in progression risk (HR = 0.42, 95% CI 0.35–0.51) compared to those with wild-type IDH (Xia et al. ). This meta-analysis confirmed these results, considering the WHO grade, showing that both patients with grade III or II-III had better OFS and PFS outcomes. On the other hand, different studies have failed to replicate this prognostic value of IDH1 mutations in secondary GBM and grade IV gliomas. Even a negative association between IDH mutations with OS and PFS has been described in these groups (Juratli et al. ; Alimohamadi et al. ).
Management of IDH-mutant gliomas remains largely guided by clinical trials predating the molecular classification of these tumors, with standard treatment typically involving surgical resection followed by radiation and chemotherapy (Lally et al. ). While these approaches remain foundational, emerging insights into the molecular complexity and heterogeneity of IDH-mutant gliomas are driving a shift toward more personalized strategies. Novel therapeutic avenues—such as mutant IDH (mIDH) inhibitors, PARP inhibitors targeting DNA repair pathways, and immunotherapies including checkpoint inhibitors and peptide vaccines—are currently under investigation (Alshiekh Nasany and de la Fuente ). Ongoing clinical trials aim to refine the role of these targeted treatments, with the goal of improving survival outcomes while minimizing long-term toxicity. Among the most studied agents are Ivosidenib, a selective IDH1 inhibitor, and Vorasidenib, a dual IDH1/2 inhibitor with CNS penetration, the latter of which showed promising results in the phase 3 INDIGO trial by delaying disease progression and deferring additional treatments (Mellinghoff et al. ). Beyond direct IDH inhibitors, epigenetic therapies such as HDAC inhibitors (e.g., Panobinostat) and Jumonji demethylase inhibitors (e.g., KDM5-targeting agents) are under investigation, exploiting the hypermethylated chromatin state induced by D-2-HG (Choate et al. ). While some agents like Panobinostat have faced regulatory setbacks, the pursuit of targeted therapies for IDH-mutant gliomas remains active, with multiple strategies advancing in both preclinical and clinical development (Choate et al. ). Recent findings (Hou et al. ) have also linked gut microbiota composition to temozolomide (TMZ) efficacy in glioma, revealing that microbial-driven immune modulation can influence chemotherapy sensitivity. This enhances the complex interaction between systemic metabolism, immune regulation, and molecular determinants such as IDH status in glioma therapy (Hou et al. ).
Collectively, these findings underscore the critical role of IDH mutation status in prognostication and therapeutic decision-making across the spectrum of glioma grades.

## 1p/19q Codeletion
The combined loss of chromosome arms 1p and 19q is a canonical genetic signature of oligodendroglial tumors (Table  ). Mechanistically, this codeletion arises from an unbalanced translocation t (1;19) (q10; p10) that deletes one copy of 1p and 19q and corresponds to loss of the tumor-suppressor genes CIC (on 19q) and FUBP1 (on 1p) (Jiao et al. ).
In clinical practice, 1p/19q codeletion occurs essentially only in IDH-mutant gliomas: it defines oligodendroglioma under the 2021 WHO CNS classification (oligodendroglioma is by definition IDH-mutant and 1p/19q-codeleted (Louis et al. ). It is much less common in mixed oligoastrocytomas (about 20–50%) (Jiao et al. ) and is essentially absent in pure astrocytomas. The codeletion is associated with a favorable prognosis: oligodendrogliomas with 1p/19q loss have substantially longer survival than non-codeleted gliomas (Li et al. ) and respond especially well to alkylating chemotherapy (PCV regimen), improving overall survival in grade II and III oligodendrogliomas (Hu et al. ; Sasaki et al. ). In fact, landmark trials (e.g., EORTC 26951/RTOG 9402) showed that first-line radiation plus PCV greatly prolongs survival in 1p/19q–codeleted oligodendrogliomas (Lassman et al. ).
Regarding the biological effects of 1p/19q codeletion, Lv et al. ( ) suggested that the 1p/19q codeletion status was strongly linked to an immunosuppressive microenvironment in LGGs, characterized by reduced immune cell infiltration and lower expression of immune checkpoint genes, potentially due to the loss of immune-related gene copies. In a similar line, 1p/19q codeletion was related to a reduced acidic environment, aiding in explaining their favorable benefits with overall survival (Yao et al. ). Finally, Kamoun et al. ( ) distinguished three distinct subgroups within 1p/19q co-deleted tumors: oligodendrocyte precursor cells (OPC) and neuronal-like, where the OPC-like group exhibits more aggressive molecular features, including MYC activation, and correlates with poorer prognosis independently of histological grade. Likewise, by using magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), Naeem et al. ( ) demonstrated an existing correlation between the shape of oligodendroglioma tumors, specifically irregular and poorly defined edges, and the presence of 1p/19q co-deletion.

## FUBP1 and CIC
CIC (capicua transcriptional repressor) and FUBP1 (far upstream element-binding protein 1) play critical roles in the molecular pathogenesis of specific CNS tumors, particularly oligodendrogliomas (Table  ) (Hwang et al. ). CIC functions as a transcriptional repressor downstream of receptor tyrosine kinase (RTK) and RAS/MAPK signaling, regulating genes involved in cell proliferation and differentiation (Jiménez et al. ). Loss-of-function mutations in CIC lead to dysregulated expression of ETV family transcription factors (such as ETV1, ETV4, and ETV5), promoting oncogenesis (Wang et al. ). FUBP1, originally characterized for its role in activating MYC transcription, has been increasingly recognized as a potential tumor suppressor in gliomas. It is involved in the regulation of cell cycle progression and apoptosis, interacting with p53 and other nuclear proteins to maintain cellular homeostasis (Debaize and Troadec ).
Both genes are recurrently mutated in IDH-mutant, 1p/19q-codeleted oligodendrogliomas, and their inactivation is now recognized as a hallmark of this tumor subtype under the WHO CNS5 classification. CIC mutations are identified in approximately 50–70% of these tumors, while FUBP1 mutations occur in about 15–30% (Yip et al. ; Jiao et al. ). These alterations are typically mutually exclusive with TP53 mutations and ATRX loss, thereby assisting in distinguishing oligodendrogliomas from astrocytomas at the molecular level (Jiao et al. ). This is particularly critical when histological features are ambiguous or overlap with other glioma subtypes.
From a clinical perspective, CIC and FUBP1 mutations also provide valuable prognostic information. While some studies suggest these mutations may be associated with improved survival outcomes due to the indolent behavior of the tumors in which they occur, the prognostic impact of CIC and FUBP1 individually remains under investigation, with conflicting findings across cohorts (Chan et al. ). Although rare in pediatric cases, CIC alterations have been identified in a subset of pediatric high-grade neuroepithelial tumors, which display a unique DNA methylation profile differing from CIC-rearranged sarcomas, suggesting an intermediate malignancy grade (Hou et al. ). FUBP1, by contrast, has a more limited role in pediatric CNS neoplasms, and further investigation is warranted (Yin et al. ).
Currently, CIC and FUBP1 mutations are not considered actionable therapeutic targets. However, their downstream pathways, such as RTK/RAS/MAPK signaling in CIC-deficient tumors or MYC dysregulation in FUBP1-mutant gliomas, may offer future opportunities for targeted interventions (Bettegowda et al. ; Bahar et al. ). Continued investigation into these pathways could pave the way for precision medicine approaches in well-defined CNS tumor subsets (Ippen et al. ).

## TERT Promoter Mutations
Telomerase reverse transcriptase (TERT) promoter mutations exhibit a significant role in tumorigenesis, particularly in the case of gliomas, meningiomas, and medulloblastomas, whereas TERT promoter hypermethylation has been observed in the case of ependymomas and pediatric brain tumors (Table  ) (Murugan et al. ). In more detail, GBM has a high frequency of mutations (80%) - often associated with co-mutations in epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) and CDKN2A/B- meanwhile WHO grade II and III lesions (oligodendrogliomas), have a relatively lower frequency of TERT mutations (60–70%), and WHO grade I tumors, such as astrocytomas, have the lowest frequency of TERT mutations (30–40%) (Killela et al. ; Patel et al. ). A similar trend with meningiomas was reported, with 1.7% of WHO grade I expressing these mutations, 5.7% for WHO grade II, and 20% for WHO grade III (Patel et al. ). TERT promoter mutations (C228T and C250T) induce a novel ETS1 binding site, which increases the expression of the telomerase enzyme, directly contributing to tumorigenesis (Bell et al. ).
Both the mutation and hypermethylation of the TERT promoter lead to an enhanced activity of this enzyme, enabling cellular immortality by maintaining telomeric length (Bell et al. ).
TERT promoter mutations (C228T and C250T) are commonly associated with poor survival in gliomas (Powter et al. ). However, You et al. ( ) reported that their clinical implications varied across histological and molecular subtypes. For instance, they observed that TERT mutations often co-occur with IDH1/2 mutations and 1p/19q codeletion in LGG, correlating with improved survival. In contrast, when observed in mesenchymal subtype or co-occurring with TP53 mutations or EGFR alterations in tumors like GBM, TERT appears to be linked to poorer outcomes. Additionally, distinct hotspot mutations (C228T and C250T) may have differing biological impacts, with C250T potentially playing a protective role against recurrence (You et al. ). As it will be subsequently discussed, TERT mutations and other clinically relevant biomarkers can be easily detected with high precision and sensitivity in liquid biopsies, facilitating longitudinal analysis throughout therapy and aid in cancer patient management (Powter et al. ).
Finally, TERT promoter alterations can represent an attractive therapeutic target, according to authors like Olympios et al. ( ). They observed that although no telomerase inhibitors are currently approved for clinical use, various approaches are under investigation, including small-molecule inhibitors like imetelstat and BIBR1532, immunotherapies, and therapeutic vaccines. Imetelstat has shown preclinical efficacy but raised safety concerns in pediatric trials. Alternative strategies such as targeting GABPβ1L, a key activator of mutant TERTp, or using eribulin, which inhibits both microtubules and TERT’s RNA-dependent RNA polymerase function, have shown promise in preclinical models (Thompson et al. ). Immunotherapeutic approaches using dendritic cell vaccines and the novel UCPVax telomerase-derived peptide vaccine are also under clinical evaluation, particularly in TERTp-mutated GBM (Carpentier et al. , ). Collectively, these developments underscore the potential of telomerase inhibition in personalized GBM treatment, especially when combined with molecular profiling of TERT and IDH1/2 status (Mansour et al. ).

## Mutations in H3: H3K27M and H3G34R/V
The H3K27M alteration is a lysine (K)-to-methionine (M) substitution at residue 27 of histone H3 variants -most commonly H3F3A (H3.3) and HIST1H3B/C (H3.1)- that profoundly disrupts chromatin regulation (Lewis et al. ). Mutant H3K27M proteins sequester the Polycomb repressive complex 2 (PRC2), causing a global loss of H3K27 trimethylation and aberrant gene expression (Fang et al. ; Kfoury-Beaumont et al. ). In vitro, H3K27M accelerates stem cell growth and impairs neural differentiation, maintaining cells in a stem-like, tumorigenic state (Kfoury-Beaumont et al. ) (Table  ).
This mutation is a defining driver of diffuse midline gliomas (DMGs). It is found in the majority of diffuse intrinsic pontine gliomas (DIPG)—roughly 60–84% of cases (Dufour et al. ; Vallero et al. )—and in other pediatric midline HGG (thalamic, spinal, etc.). By definition, DMGs with this mutation form a distinct 2021 WHO entity (DMG, H3K27-altered” (Vallero et al. ). Thus, H3K27M status is diagnostic of DMG; immunohistochemistry or sequencing for H3K27M is now standard in suspected midline gliomas (Wang et al. ). Loss of H3K27 trimethylation on IHC also serves as a surrogate marker of the mutation is also observed in a subset of adult midline gliomas, albeit less frequently. In general, tumors harboring H3K27M are uniformly WHO-grade 4 and carry an especially poor prognosis (median survival ≈ 11 months) (Dufour et al. ; Vallero et al. ). H3.3 (H3F3A) K27M gliomas have worse survival than the rarer H3.1 K27M variants, as observed in DIPG overall survival (~ 11 m vs ~ 15 m) (Mackay et al. ).
The mutant epitope is immunogenic, and H3K27 M-directed vaccines are under investigation. A recent trial of an H3K27M peptide vaccine (H3K27 M-vac) in recurrent midline glioma induced mutation-specific T-cell responses and prolonged survival in one patient (complete remission > 31 mo) (Grassl et al. ). H3K27M, thus can also offer a mutation-specific therapeutic target.
On the other hand, the H3G34R/V histone mutation was first identified in 2012 in tumors initially classified as pediatric GBMs. Since then, H3G34-mutant gliomas have been recognized as a distinct diagnostic entity due to their unique molecular, histopathological, and clinical characteristics (Korshunov et al. ). In the 2021 WHO CNS-5 classification, a new category named “diffuse hemispheric glioma (DHG), H3G34-mutant” was delineated in the pediatric-type diffuse HGG category (LaBelle et al. ). It typically affects adolescents, with a median diagnosis age of 15–16 years and a median overall survival of 18–22 months. However, DHG can also occur in adult patients, distinguishing it from classic GBM in both age distribution and clinical behavior (Nguyen et al. ). Tang et al. ( ) performed a scoping review of DHG H3G34-mutant, considering their epigenetic and molecular profiles, clinicopathology, and treatment opportunities. They remarked that this tumor is characterized by astrocytic infiltration, GBM-like histology, and variable expression of glial markers. It often presents TP53 and ATRX mutations, PDGFRA alterations, and impaired DNA repair mechanisms, contributing to genomic instability and immune activation via the cGAS/STING pathway (Lin et al. ). Compared to DMG, H3 K27-altered, DHG G34-mutant shows better respectability and distinct epigenetic and molecular features. Despite its aggressive nature and treatment resistance, clinical trials are limited, with emerging strategies focusing on targeted therapy, epigenetic modulation, immune-based treatments, and novel agents like CDK4/6 and EGFR inhibitors (Tang et al. ).

## Chromosome 7 Gain and Chromosome 10 Loss (7+/10−) Signature
The co-occurrence of chromosome 10 loss and chromosome 7 gain in gliomas represents the most frequent loss-gain co-aneuploidy pair in human cancers (Table  ) (Nair et al. ). This chromosomal pattern is predominantly observed in IDH-wildtype GBM and has been investigated for more than 40 years. In more detail, one study demonstrated that approximately 79% of GBMs are defined as 7+/10−, with the remaining 21% showing partial or atypical 7+/10− alterations, such as 7+/10q−, 7p/10−, and 7q+/10.
(Deimling) and has been investigated since the late 1980s. In more detail, one study demonstrated that 81/136 GBMs (59.55%) had partial alterations and 123/136 (90.44%) had combined complete 7 gain and 10 losses, with only 11/136 (8.08%) GBMs showing neither chromosome 7 gains nor 10 losses (Reuss et al. ).
Analyses of tumor aneuploidy data suggest that the most likely order of events is chromosome 10 loss followed by chromosome 7 gain. This sequential pattern has functional significance, as extensive genomic and transcriptomic data from both patients and cell lines suggest that chromosome 7 gain functionally compensates for the detrimental consequences arising from chromosome 10 loss (Nair et al. ). The gain of chromosome 7 may present as trisomy, tetrasomy, or even higher polysomy, though of remains unclear whether the extent of polysomy impacts prognosis. Kaplan-Meier survival analysis demonstrates lower survival rates in patients with monosomy 10, trisomy 7, and the combination of both anomalies (Quan et al. ). This chromosomal signature is increasingly being incorporated into prognostic stratification models for gliomas (Deimling).
Finaly, the combination of chromosome 7 gain and chromosome 10 loss is often accompanied by other genetic alterations. For instance, amplification of the EGR gene, frequently reported in GBM, is associated with trisomy/polysomy 7 in 44% of cases and with monosomy 10 in 36% of cases (Quan et al. ). All three alterations (trisomy 7, monosomy 10, and EGFR amplification) co-occur in 28% of cases (Quan et al. ). Notably, the gain of chromosome 7 is more frequent than EGFR amplification in GBM, IDH-wildtype (Stichel et al. ).
The therapeutic landscape for 7+/10− gliomas is complicated by the presence of multiple oncogenes amplified on chromosome 7, particularly EGFR and PDGFRA. Traditional EGFR tyrosine kinase inhibitors have shown limited clinical efficacy despite high expression levels. This resistance stems from the unique nature of EGFR mutations in GBM, which primarily affect the extracellular domain rather than the intracellular kinase domain targeted by conventional inhibitors (Stichel et al. ). Moreover, intratumoral heterogeneity creates distinct subpopulations with different RTK amplifications, where up to 43% of PDGFRA phosphorylation at critical tyrosine sites Y720, Y849, and Y742, even in cells with low EGFR expression. This cross-inhibition suggests significant direct interactions between EGFR and PDGFRA in coamplified cells, but importantly, gefitinib does not abolish imatinib-sensitive AKT activation when PDGFRA is activated (Szerlip et al. ). These findings indicate that effective treatment of 7+/10− gliomas requires simultaneous inhibition of multiple RTKs, as single-agent approaches fail to completely abrogate downstream PI3K pathway activity. Cell lines derived from coamplified tumors demonstrate genotype selection under ligand stimulation or pharmacologic inhibition, with PDGFB ligand specifically selecting for PDGFRA amplified genotypes over time.

## ATRX
ATRX (alpha-thalassemia/mental retardation syndrome X-linked) is a chromatin-remodeling protein that helps incorporate histone H3.3 into telomeric and pericentromeric chromatin. It plays a key role in telomere maintenance and genomic stability (Table  ) (Wong et al. ). Loss-of-function mutations in ATRX disrupt normal chromatin at telomeres, leading to alternative lengthening of telomeres (ALT)—a telomerase-independent telomere maintenance mechanism (Li et al. ). In tumors, ATRX mutations often co-occur with TP53 and indicate an ALT phenotype, promoting continued proliferation without telomere erosion (Akter et al. ).
In CNS tumors, ATRX mutations are most common in infiltrating astrocytomas. Strikingly, approximately 82% of IDH-mutant astrocytomas show ATRX inactivation (Lee et al. ). By immunohistochemistry, ATRX nuclear staining is lost in these tumors. In contrast, ATRX mutations are virtually absent in 1p/19q-codeleted oligodendrogliomas (Lee et al. ). Thus, ATRX status is a reliable marker distinguishing astrocytoma from oligodendroglioma lineage. ATRX mutations are rarely found in adult HGG (6–7.1%) but are frequent in grade II and III gliomas (33.2–71%) and secondary GBM (53–57%) (Haase et al. ), in which ATRX mutations are associated with IDH1 and TP53 mutations. ATRX loss also appears in many pediatric HGG (e.g., some H3 G34-mutant gliomas) (Voon and Wong ).
ATRX loss is significantly associated with better OS in HGG patients (Houssaini et al. ). However, because ATRX-mutant astrocytomas typically use ALT, some studies have explored ATRX as a therapeutic target. ALT-positive tumors (ATRX-deficient) may be vulnerable to specific strategies (e.g., ATR kinase or DNA repair inhibitors), but such approaches are experimental (Haase et al. ; Qin et al. ). Overall, ATRX’s role in chromatin and telomeres makes it a critical marker of astrocytic tumors: its loss confirms astrocytoma lineage and implicates ALT telomere maintenance (Lee et al. ).

## CDKN2A/B Deletion
The CDKN2A/B locus on chromosome 9p21 encodes the tumor-suppressors p16^INK4a and p14^ARF (p15^INK4b from CDKN2B) ([CSL STYLE ERROR: reference with no printed form.]) (Table  ). These proteins are master regulators of the cell cycle, with p16^INK4a implicated in the inhibition of CDK4/6 (maintaining Rb-mediated G1 arrest) and p14^ARF involved in the stabilization of p53 by inhibiting MDM2 (LaPak and Burd ). Homozygous deletion of CDKN2A/B is a potent oncogenic event, abrogating these key checkpoints. This deletion is particularly enriched in high-grade astrocytic gliomas. In unselected cohorts, about 23.8% of diffuse gliomas harbor homozygous CDKN2A/B loss (Noack et al. ). Its prevalence varies by subtype and grade: it is very common in IDH-wildtype tumors (over half of IDH-wildtype gliomas), but much less frequent in IDH-mutant gliomas (~ 10%) (Noack et al. ). Among IDH-mutant tumors, homozygous deletion is especially seen in anaplastic astrocytomas (WHO grade 3–4): for example, reports have found ~ 0% of grade II astrocytomas, ~ 14% of grade III, and ~ 27% of grade IV gliomas with this loss. Overall, astrocytomas exhibit the highest CDKN2A deletion rates (on the order of 50–55% in many series), whereas oligodendrogliomas show much lower rates (~ 20–25%) (Noack et al. ). In pediatric gliomas, CDKN2A/B loss is less frequent than in adults but still notable: a recent study found 12.9% of 510 pediatric gliomas had any CDKN2A/B deletion (7% of pediatric low-grade, 29% of pediatric high-grade) (homozygous in 3.5% of pediatric LGG and 18.8% of pediatric HGG) (Power et al. ).
Biologically, CDKN2A/B deletion removes potent brakes on the cell cycle, and clinically, it is a negative prognostic marker. Pooled analyses show that CDKN2A/B-deleted tumors have significantly shorter survival (Zhang et al. ). For instance, one meta-analysis reported median OS of 13 months versus 18 months (HR ≈ 1.5) in IDH-wildtype gliomas with versus without deletion, and 40 months versus 92 months (HR ≈ 3.2) in IDH-mutant gliomas (Power et al. ). The 2021 WHO CNS guidelines now consider an IDH-mutant astrocytoma with homozygous CDKN2A deletion as WHO grade IV (Ghosh et al. ).
In practice, CDKN2A/B status informs prognosis and can guide therapeutic approaches. Homozygous deletions of CDKN2A/B have emerged as significant negative prognostic indicators for OS in both IDH-mutant and IDH-wildtype gliomas (Noack et al. ). P16 immunohistochemistry presents a potential surrogate marker for identifying these deletions, though its accuracy varies depending on tumor type and scoring thresholds (Vij et al. ). Likewise, Ghosh et al. suggest that hemizygous CDKN2A/B loss predicts shorter survival in IDH1/2-mutant astrocytoma (Ghosh et al. ). Conversely, Ippen et al. ( ) reported that hemizygous CDKN2A/B deletions do not significantly worsen OS or PFS in IDH-mutant astrocytomas and oligodendrogliomas (CNS WHO grades 2 and 3). Finally, previous works claim that IDH-mutant gliomas with deletion of CDKN2A/B are sensitized to CDK4/6 inhibitors (e.g., palbociclib, abemaciclib) (Nasser et al. ), though clinical benefit remains under study.

## MYB/MYBL1 Fusions
MYB and MYBL1 are closely related proto-oncogenic transcription factors involved in cell differentiation and proliferation (Anand et al. ). Rearrangements that fuse MYB or MYBL1 to partner genes lead to dysregulated MYB-family activity, driving oncogenesis. In CNS tumors, MYB/MYBL1 gene fusions occur characteristically in indolent pediatric LGG (Suh et al. ). A prototypical example is angiocentric glioma, an uncommon childhood glioma. Virtually all angiocentric gliomas harbor an MYB–QKI fusion (usually MYB exon 9 fused to QKI) (Bandopadhayay et al. ). This fusion induces truncation of MYB and translocation of enhancer elements, potently activating MYB signaling. Similarly, a subset of diffuse (WHO grade I–II) pediatric astrocytomas carries fusions or internal rearrangements of MYB or its paralog MYBL1 (Table  ) (Bandopadhayay et al. ). In many reported series, pediatric diffuse astrocytomas (IDH-wildtype) with MYB/MYBL1 alterations appear to have bland morphology and favorable behavior (Moreira et al. ). Collectively, such tumors are now classified as “pediatric-type diffuse LGG, MYB- or MYBL1-altered.”
These MYB/MYBL1–fusion gliomas are rare. Angiocentric gliomas account for only a few percent of pediatric gliomas, and other MYB-altered diffuse gliomas are even less common. They almost always arise in children and young adults, often in the temporal lobes (Chung et al. ). Other CNS tumor types do not characteristically harbor MYB/MYBL1 fusions (e.g., adult GBMs or metastases do not). The frequency of MYB/MYBL1 fusions among all pediatric LGGs is low (an institutional study of 249 pediatric LGGs found only 19 angiocentric cases, all with MYB-QKI) (Bandopadhayay et al. ).
Clinically, MYB/MYBL1 fusions serve mainly as diagnostic/prognostic markers. Detection of a MYB-QKI or MYBL1 fusion helps confirm a diagnosis of angiocentric glioma or MYB-altered glioma when histology is equivocal. These tumors are WHO grade I (indolent); patients often present with seizures, and gross total resection can be curative (Suh et al. ). Accordingly, MYB/MYBL1-altered gliomas generally have an excellent prognosis. The presence of these fusions indicates a biologically benign tumor that typically does not require aggressive therapy beyond surgery (Wefers et al. ). At present, there are no specific targeted therapies for MYB/MYBL1 fusions in CNS tumors, but given their low-grade nature, this has not been a focus (Manoharan et al. ). In summary, MYB and MYBL1 fusions mark a distinct subgroup of pediatric LGGs, and their identification guides a favorable prognostic outcome.

## Biomarkers Related to Oncogenic Alterations and Emerging Signaling Pathways

## MAPK Signaling and BRAF V600E
The MAPK pathway is hyperactivated in 47.6% of gliomas, with 127 genes overexpressed compared to normal brain tissue (Liu and Tang ). This overexpression enables stratification into two molecular subtypes: C1 (poor prognosis, higher cancer stemness, immune checkpoint activation) and C2 (better survival, enriched in IDH-mutant tumors) (Liu and Tang ). Patients with C1 tumors exhibit significantly shorter overall survival (HR = 2.1, p < 0.001) and lower predicted response to immune checkpoint blockade (44.4% vs. 57.9% in C2) (Liu and Tang ). MAPK/ERK signaling modulates glioma immunogenicity by enhancing interferon responses and antigen presentation. Preclinical studies show that ERK phosphorylation increases CD8 + T-cell infiltration and synergizes with anti-PD-1/CTLA-4 therapies, leading to durable remission in murine models (Kim et al. ). Paradoxically, MAPK activation in recurrent GBM is associated with immunosuppressive microglia interactions, highlighting context-dependent roles (Kim et al. ). In IDH-wildtype GBM, MAPK-altered subtypes exhibit marginally better survival than classical GBM (median OS: 24 vs. 15 months, p = 0.044), suggesting a distinct molecular entity (Sievers et al. ).
BRAF encodes a serine/threonine kinase critically implicated in the dysregulation of the MAPK pathway (Table  ). Under normal conditions, it transduces growth signals from receptor tyrosine kinases to promote cell proliferation and survival (Srinivasa et al. ). The V600E mutation substitutes glutamate for valine at residue 600, mimicking the phosphorylated active state and uncoupling BRAF from upstream regulation (Srinivasa et al. ). This mutation renders BRAF constitutively active, driving oncogenic MAPK signaling even in the absence of ligand or RAS activation (Srinivasa et al. ). In gliomas, BRAF V600E thus acts as a potent oncogenic driver.
BRAF V600E is strongly enriched in certain adult and pediatric CNS tumors. In case of adult tumors, it appears in 80–95% of papillary craniopharyngioma, ~ 60% of pleomorphic xanthoastrocytomas (PXAs), 38% of astrocytomas, and to a lesser extent in LGG (2–5%) and GBM (1–2%) (Di Nunno et al. ). Regarding pediatric tumors, it appears in 30–80% of dysembryoplastic neuroepithelial tumor; 20–60% of gangliogliomas, and 45% of desmoplastic infantile ganglioglioma whereas other pediatric tumors, such as diffuse leptomeningeal glioneuronal tumor or pilocytic astrocytoma, commonly exhibit the fusion BRAF-KIAA1549 (~ 70% of cases) (Di Nunno et al. ). Diffuse astrocytomas IDH-wildtype in adults carry V600E in < 1% of cases, whereas pediatric diffuse astrocytomas often harbor BRAF alterations (estimates ~ 8–43% V600E in children) (Di Nunno et al. ). Overall, BRAF V600E defines a subset of glial and glioneuronal tumors (and is nearly ubiquitous in papillary craniopharyngioma) (Srinivasa et al. ; Di Nunno et al. ). It is essentially absent from medulloblastoma and oligodendroglioma and is uncommon in ependymoma or IDH-mutant astrocytoma.
The translational success of BRAF-targeted therapy has been most evident in patients with BRAF V600E-mutant gliomas, where clinical trials have demonstrated remarkable objective response rates ranging from 47% to 69% when using combination BRAF/MEK inhibitor therapy (Schaff et al. ). These responses have been particularly striking in pediatric low-grade gliomas, where the combination of dabrafenib and trametinib has shown superior efficacy compared to traditional chemotherapy regimens, with overall response rates of 47% versus 11% for vincristine/carboplatin, and significantly prolonged progression-free survival of 20.1 months versus 7.4 months (Schaff et al. ).
The clinical development of MEK inhibitors represents another significant translational advance derived from MAPK pathway research in gliomas. Selumetinib, binimetinib, trametinib, and cobimetinib have all demonstrated clinical activity in BRAF-rearranged gliomas, with response rates ranging from 39% to 50% in pediatric populations (Schaff et al. ). The recent approval and clinical success of tovorafenib, a type II RAF inhibitor specifically designed for enhanced central nervous system penetration, represents a major milestone in translational glioma research (Kilburn et al. ). In the FIREFLY-1 trial, tovorafenib demonstrated an objective response rate exceeding 50% using multiple response assessment criteria, with particularly notable efficacy in patients who had previously received MAPK inhibitor therapy, suggesting the potential for sequential targeting strategies within this pathway (Kilburn et al. ).
Beyond direct tumor cell targeting, recent translational research has revealed that MAPK signaling plays a crucial role in modulating tumor immunogenicity and response to immunotherapy (Wu et al. ). Large-scale CRISPR/Cas9 screens have identified the RAF-MEK-ERK pathway as the most critical modulator of glioma susceptibility to CD8 + T cells and anti-PD-1 therapy across all kinases (Kim et al. ). This discovery has profound translational implications, as it suggests that MAPK activation status could serve as a predictive biomarker for immunotherapy response in GBM patients. Clinical validation of this concept has been demonstrated in patients with elevated phospho-ERK levels, who showed improved overall survival following anti-PD-1 and anti-CTLA-4 combination therapy (Kim et al. ). The mechanistic understanding that MAPK activation enhances interferon responses, antigen presentation, and T cell infiltration provides a rational foundation for combination immunotherapy approaches in gliomas with activated MAPK signaling (Xing et al. ).
The translational application of MAPK pathway targeting has also revealed important insights into resistance mechanisms and optimal treatment sequencing. Clinical experience has demonstrated that BRAF/MEK inhibitors show greater efficacy in non-enhancing, early-stage disease compared to enhancing, more advanced gliomas (Villanueva et al. ). This finding has influenced clinical trial design and treatment recommendations, emphasizing the importance of early intervention in BRAF-mutant gliomas. Additionally, the observation that first-generation BRAF inhibitors are ineffective for BRAF rearrangements and may paradoxically promote MAPK reactivation has led to the development of MEK inhibitor monotherapy strategies for these genetic subtypes (Johnson and Kopetz ). The translational understanding of these differential responses has enabled more precise patient stratification and treatment selection based on specific BRAF alteration types.
Combination strategies represent an evolving area of research. The dual inhibition of BRAF and MEK, originally developed for melanoma, has shown enhanced durability of response in gliomas compared to monotherapy (Johnson and Kopetz ). Moreover, recent findings indicate that MAPK activation promotes neuronal differentiation in GBM cells through upregulation of lineage-specific transcription factors such as ASCL1, DLL3, and NeuroD4 (Khan et al. ). This has opened new avenues for differentiation therapy, suggesting that MEK inhibitors might also induce tumor cell maturation alongside their antiproliferative effects, potentially leading to more durable therapeutic outcomes.

## PI3K/Akt/mTOR Signaling
The PI3K/Akt/mTOR signaling axis is aberrantly activated in approximately 90% of GBM, making it one of the most dysregulated pathways in this malignancy (Table  ) (Langhans et al. ). Key genetic alterations driving this activation include gain-of-function mutations in PI3KCA, which encodes the PI3Kα catalytic subunit, and loss-of-function mutations or deletions in PTEN, a tumor suppressor that serves as a negative regulator of the pathway (Tanaka et al. ). Activating hotspot mutations in PI3KCA are observed in 6–15% of GBM cases, and are typically associated with younger patient age, increased invasiveness, and poorer clinical outcomes, including reduced progression-free and overall survival (Tanaka et al. ).
PTEN inactivation, either by deletion or mutation, occurs in an estimated 5–40% of gliomas (Han et al. ). This alteration leads to persistent, unregulated signaling through the PI3K pathway and is consistently linked to unfavorable prognoses, as confirmed by multiple meta-analyses demonstrating significantly shortened survival in PTEN-mutant glioma patients. Similar patterns of PI3K/Akt upregulation are also seen in other central nervous system tumors (Han et al. ). For example, a subset of Sonic Hedgehog (SHH)-driven medulloblastomas, especially adult-onset cases, harbor mutations in either PI3KCA or PTEN, indicating a broader relevance of this pathway beyond gliomas (Menyhárt and Győrffy ).
In terms of therapeutic development, pharmacologic inhibitors targeting the PI3K pathway have been designed to act at various levels of the cascade, from pan-PI3K inhibitors to more selective agents that target mTOR (Castel et al. ). Each approach has its own therapeutic advantages and limitations. Buparlisib (BKM120), a pan-class I PI3K inhibitor, has demonstrated adequate CNS penetration and on-target activity, with reduced levels of phosphor-Akt observed in tumor tissue from approximately 67% of treated patients (Wen et al. ). However, monotherapy with buparlisib has yielded only modest clinical benefit, with a median progression-free survival of just 1.8 months in recurrent GBM, highlighting the challenge of disrupting this complex signaling network in isolation (Singh et al. ).
Efforts to develop next-generation PI3K inhibitors have aimed at specificity and minimizing adverse effects without compromising efficacy. Compounds such as GDC-0941 (Pictilisib) have demonstrated superior activity compared to the mTOR inhibitor rapamycin, particularly in reducing cell motility and enhancing apoptosis in response to temozolomide (Langhans et al. ). Preclinical studies have shown that GDC-0941 significantly suppresses tumor growth and prolongs survival in orthotopic human GBM xenograft models. These findings underscore the importance of targeting not only proliferation but also other tumor—promoting processes, such as invasion and migration (Huijts et al. ).
Recent studies have also highlighted the context-dependent and cell state-specific roles of PI3K/Akt signaling within the heterogeneous GBM tumor microenvironment (TME) (Yang et al. ). For instance, in genetically identical glioma cell populations with differentiating states, the PI3K pathway was found to mediate chemoresistance in stem-like cells, while regulating motility in more differentiated cells (Borlongan and Wang ). These insights suggest that the effectiveness of PI3K-targeted therapies may be dependent on the differentiation status of the tumor and support the need for personalized combination strategies (LoRusso ).
The approval of Tumor Treating Fields (TTFields) for GBM has introduced new dimensions in understanding PI3K/Akt pathway dynamics (Jones et al. ). TTFields therapy has been shown to activate this signaling cascade via focal adhesion kinase and N-cadherin-mediated mechanisms, potentially contributing to therapeutic resistance (Klein-Goldberg et al. ). However, the concurrent use of PI3K inhibitors has been shown to sensitize GBM cells to both in vitro and in vivo (Klein-Goldberg et al. ). Moreover, genomic analyses have identified activating PI3KCA mutations as potential biomarkers of reduced responsiveness to TTFields, paving the way for patient stratification and rational combination therapy approaches (Klein-Goldberg et al. ).
Comprehensive genomic profiling has further emphasized the complexity of PI3K pathway alteration in gliomas (Crespo et al. ). In recurrent GBM, PI3KCA mutations have been detected in 10% of patients, PI3K3R1 mutations in 5%, and PTEN mutations in up to 33% (Tomás and Pojo ). Notably, patients harboring both PTEN loss and PI3KCA mutations often exhibit worse outcomes following PI3K inhibitor monotherapy, an observation that underscores the inadequacy of using single-gene mutations as sole predictors of therapeutic response (Klemm and Joyce ). These findings suggest that co-occurring mutations and the broader TME context must be considered.
As a result, current translational efforts are focused on designing combination strategies that target multiple nodes within the PI3K/Akt/mTOR pathway or that simultaneously inhibit other oncogenic signaling axes (Ibanez et al. ). Dual PI3K/mTOR inhibitor and combinations with MAPK pathway inhibitors are under investigation as potential strategies to overcome resistance and enhance therapeutic efficacy in this molecularly complex and adaptive cancer.

## TSC1/TSC2
The TSC1–TSC2 complex serves as a critical negative regulator of the mechanistic target of rapamycin complex 1 (mTORC1), a central hub for integrating growth factor, nutrients, and energy signals to drive cell proliferation and metabolism (Table  ) (Huang and Manning ). Functionally, TSC2 acts as a GTPase-activating protein (GAP) for the small GTPase Rheb, converting it to its GDP-bound inactive state, thereby suppressing mTORC1 activity. Loss-of-function mutations or deletions in TSC1 or TSC2 result in constitutive Rheb-GTP accumulation, hyperactivating mTORC1, and promoting uncontrolled anabolic processes such as protein synthesis and lipid biosynthesis (Inoki et al. ). In gliomas, mTORC1 hyperactivity is further amplified by upstream oncogenic signals, including EGFR amplification and PTEN loss, creating a permissive microenvironment for tumor growth and therapeutic resistance. Beyond mTORC1, emerging evidence suggests the TSC1-TSC2 complex also modulates mTORC2, which phosphorylates Akt at Ser473 to sustain survival signals, though this interaction remains less well-characterized in glioma pathogenesis (Li et al. ; Huang and Manning ).
Somatic mutations in TSC1 or TSC2 occur in approximately 1.4% of solid tumors, including gliomas, with TSC2 alterations being marginally more frequent than TSC1 (Rosset et al. ; Li et al. ). In GBM, homozygous deletions of TSC1 or TSC2 are rare, but biallelic inactivation via loss of heterozygosity (LOH) or epigenetic silencing has been observed in subsets of patients. Preclinical models demonstrate that Tsc1 deletion in neural stem/progenitor cells (NSPCs) synergizes with oncogenic EGFRvIII to accelerate gliomagenesis, enhancing tumor vascularity and invasive potential (Kobayashi et al. ; Yamada et al. ). Notably, TSC1/2-deficient gliomas exhibit elevated phospho-S6 and phospho-4E-BP1 levels, biomarkers of mTORC1 activation, which correlate with mesenchymal molecular subtypes and poorer survival outcomes (McBride et al. ; Liu et al. ). While TSC1/2 mutations are not mutually exclusive with IDH1/2 mutations, they are enriched in IDH-wildtype tumors, suggesting divergent pathogenic mechanisms between glioma subtypes (Yamada et al. ; Azriel et al. ).
Constitutive mTORC1 has provided a strong rationale for the use of mTOR inhibitors—particularly the rapalogs (rapamycin, everolimus, temsirolimus)—in gliomas with TSC1/TSC2 dysfunction (Zhou et al. ). Clinical studies in patients with tuberous sclerosis, a genetic disorder defined by germline TSC1 or TSC2 mutations, have demonstrated that rapalogs can induce significant regression of subependymal giant cell astrocytomas (SEGAs), a form of LGG, with improvements in seizure control and tumor size reduction (Wander et al. ). These results provide robust proof of concept that mTOR inhibition is effective in tumors driven by TSC1/TSC2 loss, and have led to regulatory approval of everolimus for SEGAs in TSC patients.
In sporadic gliomas, and particularly in HGG and GBM, clinical trials of rapalogs have shown only modest single-agent activity, with limited progression-free survival benefit (Reardon et al. ). For example, phase II studies of temsirolimus in recurrent GBM reported radiographic improvement in a subset of patients and a median progression-free survival of 2.3 months, with higher response rates in tumors exhibiting elevated phosphorylated p70S6 kinase, a downstream target of mTORC1 (Galanis et al. ). However, the overall impact of rapalogs in unselected glioma populations has been disappointing, likely due to pathway redundancy, feedback activation of AKT via mTORC2, and the molecular heterogeneity of gliomas (Galanis et al. ). These limitations have prompted the development and clinical evaluation of dual mTORC1/mTORC2 inhibitors (such as AZD8055, vistusertib, and sapanisertib), which more comprehensively block mTOR signaling and may overcome resistance mechanisms associated with rapalogs (Panwar et al. ). Preclinical studies have shown that dual mTORC1/2 inhibition with agents like AZD8055 dramatically reduces the viability of glioma stem-like cells and is highly effective in vivo, both as monotherapy and in combination with temozolomide, the standard chemotherapeutic agent for GBM ([CSL STYLE ERROR: reference with no printed form.]; Luchman et al. ). Early-phase clinical trials have demonstrated that these dual inhibitors are generally well tolerated and can achieve disease stabilization in a subset of patients, though objective response rates remain low, and further studies are needed to identify predictive biomarkers and optimal combination regimens (Banerji et al. ; Lapointe et al. ).
Another promising translational avenue stems from the intersection of TSC2/mTOR signaling and immune modulation (Glaviano et al. ). Recent research has revealed that TSC2-deficient tumor cells exhibit increased expression of TRAIL receptors, rendering them more susceptible to immune-mediated cytotoxicity via TRAIL signaling (Lin et al. ). This suggests that combining mTOR pathway modulation with TRAIL agonists could sensitize glioma cells to T-cell-mediated killing, potentially bypassing the immunosuppressive effects of mTOR inhibitors on immune effector cells (Lin et al. ). Such combination strategies are under preclinical and early clinical investigation, with the aim of harnessing both direct tumoricidal and immunomodulatory effects (Hushmandi et al. ).
Despite these advances, several challenges remain. The blood-brain barrier limits the penetration of many mTOR inhibitors, and the cytoprotective effects of mTOR inhibition, such as upregulation of DNA repair enzymes and reactive oxygen species detoxification, may antagonize the efficacy of standard chemotherapies like temozolomide (Sauer et al. ). As a result, careful consideration of drug combinations, dosing schedules, and patient selection is essential to maximize therapeutic benefit and minimize unintended resistance mechanisms (Leekha et al. ). In summary, the translational landscape for TSC1/TSC2 alterations in gliomas is rapidly evolving, with mTOR pathway inhibition representing a validated therapeutic approach in genetically defined subsets, and novel strategies—including dual mTORC1/2 blockade, immunomodulation, and rational combinations—offering hope for improved outcomes in this challenging disease.

## TP53 (p53)
The TP53 gene, encoding the p53 tumor suppressor, plays a central role in maintaining genomic stability and cellular homeostasis. Often called the “guardian of the genome” (Table  ), Wild-type p53 orchestrates protective responses to genotoxic and oncogenic stress through transcriptional activation of genes involved in cell cycle arrest (e.g., p21), apoptosis (BAX), and senescence (Hernández Borrero and El-Deiry ). TP53 is the most frequently altered gene across human cancer, with alterations predominantly affecting its DNA-binding domain, leading to impaired transcriptional regulation and loss of checkpoint control, thereby promoting genomic instability and tumorigenesis (Tornesello ). Pathologically, strong nuclear accumulation of p53 protein, detectable via immunohistochemistry, often correlates with missense mutations that stabilize the otherwise labile protein, serving as a surrogate marker of TP53 mutation (Ahmadi et al. ).
In the context of CNS tumors, TP53 displays a distinct tumor-specific distribution. They are highly prevalent in infiltrating astrocytic gliomas, particularly those harboring IDH mutations, IDH-mutant astrocytomas. Large-scale studies report that approximately 86% of IDH-mutant astrocytomas carry TP53 mutations, underscoring their critical role in astrocytic tumorigenesis (Lee et al. ). In contrast, oligodendrogliomas characterized by IDH-mutant, 1p/19q-codeleted, rarely harbor TP53 alterations. Indeed, TP53 alterations are virtually mutually exclusive with 1p/19q codeletion (Wesseling et al. ). In GBM, TP53 is mutated in a minority of primary cases (~ 20–30%) but is more frequent in secondary GBMs (60–70%) (Thakkar et al. ). Among pediatric HGGs, TP53 mutations are commonly found in DMGs with H3K27M mutations, as well as in diffuse hemispheric gliomas DHGs harboring H3G34 mutations, implicating p53 dysfunction in histone-mutant gliomagenesis (Vallero et al. ).
TP53 alterations are also relevant in pediatric embryonal tumors, particularly in medulloblastoma, where mutation frequencies vary by molecular subgroup. Approximately 61% of WNT-activated and 21% of SHH-driven medulloblastomas harbor TP53 mutations (Zhukova et al. ). This stratification has prognostic significance, especially within the SHH subgroup, where TP53 mutation status correlates with high-risk disease and poorer outcomes (Taher et al. ; Kite et al. ). Additionally, rare CNS tumors, such as anaplastic ependymomas and HGGs in patients with Li-Fraumeni syndrome, often involve germline or somatic TP53 mutations. In brain metastases, TP53 states typically mirror that of the primary tumor, especially in metastases from breast and lung carcinomas, although TP53 mutation itself does not have a diagnostic role in determining tumor origin (Lo Nigro et al. ; Mathiot et al. ).
From a clinical perspective, TP53 status contributes both diagnostically and prognostically. In neuropathology evaluation, diffuse and intense nuclear p53 immunoreactivity in an IDH-mutant glioma favors an astrocytic phenotype, consistent with the high prevalence of TP53 mutations in this context (Lee et al. ). Conversely, lack of p53 overexpression supports an oligodendroglial diagnosis, given the rarity of TP53 alterations in 1p/1q-codeleted tumors. In SHH-subgroup medulloblastomas, TP53 mutation status is now incorporated into risk stratification schemes, where SHH/TP53-mutant cases are classified as high-risk, regardless of histologic features, due to markedly unfavorable prognosis (Zhukova et al. ).
Despite its biological significance, TP53 mutation is not currently a predictive biomarker for targeted therapy in routine clinical practice. Nevertheless, therapeutic strategies aiming to reactivate p53 signaling, such as MDM2 inhibitors, which prevent p53 degradation, are under investigation in TP53-wildtype GBM (Pellot Ortiz et al. ). TO date, however, no targeted agents directed at mutant p53 have been approved for clinical use.

## Angiogenic Markers
In gliomas, neovascularization primarily arises through angiogenesis, the process by which new blood vessels sprout from pre-existing vasculature. Although vasculogenesis, involving bone marrow-derived endothelial progenitor cells, also contributes to tumor vascularization, and arteriogenesis plays a minor role, angiogenesis remains the dominant mechanism fueling the vascular expansion required for tumor growth and maintenance (Aghi and Chiocca ) (Table  ).
This angiogenic cascade typically initiates under hypoxic conditions, where upregulation of angiopoietin-2 (Ang-2), a functional antagonist of the Tie-2 receptor, destabilizes existing vessels. Subsequent degradation of the extracellular matrix (ECM) by matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs) facilitates exposure and activation of potent pro-angiogenic signals, particularly vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) and fibroblast growth factor (FGF), both of which promote endothelial proliferation and migration (Handsley and Edwards ).
VEGF signaling, particularly mediated by VEGF-A, is a central driver of angiogenesis in gliomas and especially in GBM, one of the most vascularized human tumors. A systematic review and meta-analysis by Seyedmirzaei et al. ( ) demonstrated that serum and tissue VEGF levels are significantly elevated in glioma patients compared to healthy individuals, although no clear distinction in VEGF expression was found between low-grade and high-grade gliomas (Nowacka et al. ). In GBM, overexpression of VEGF-A is strongly associated with tumor aggressiveness and reduced overall survival, underscoring its value as a prognostic biomarker (Chen et al. ).
Among VEGF isoforms, VEGF-A predominates in the hypoxic and necrotic microenvironments characteristic of GBM, exerting its effects primarily through VEGFR-2, with additional modulation by co-receptors such as neuropilin-1 and neuropilin-2, as well as heparan sulfate proteoglycans. In contrast, VEGF-C and VEGF-D promote lymphangiogenesis via VEGFR-3 (Nowacka et al. ). The tumor-associated vasculature is structurally abnormal, leading to elevated interstitial fluid pressure, inefficient drug delivery, and persistent hypoxia, which in turn fuels further angiogenesis, creating a vicious cycle of vascular dysfunction. Given the pivotal role, the VEGF/VEGFR axis has been a major target for therapeutic intervention. Bevacizumab, a monoclonal antibody targeting VEGF-A, has shown the ability to improve imaging response and PFS in GBM patients, though no improvement in OS has been demonstrated (Alves et al. ). This limited efficacy is likely to reflect the redundancy and adaptability of angiogenic pathways, where VEGF inhibition can trigger compensatory pro-angiogenic signaling, diminishing therapeutic impact (Cook and Figg ).
Other anti-angiogenic agents have been investigated, including aflibercept, axitinib, and cediranib, which target various components of the VEGF pathways. While some clinical benefit has been observed, their use is often hindered by adverse effects, limited penetration of the blood-brain barrier, and absence of OS advantage (Grobbelaar et al. ). Similarly, dovitinib and pazopanib had minimal impact, though molecular profiling efforts suggest that biomarker-driven strategies may help identify responsive subgroups in the future. Trials involving ramucirumab and sunitinib have also yielded modest results, largely due to suboptimal delivery and insufficient intratumoral drug concentrations (Nowacka et al. ).
In conclusion, VEGF-driven angiogenesis is a hallmark of GBM pathophysiology, and while anti-angiogenic therapies have been incorporated into clinical management, their benefits remain limited. Overcoming resistance mechanisms and improving delivery across the blood-brain barrier represent key challenges. Ongoing research focuses on next-generation angiogenesis inhibitors, combination regimens, and precision medicine approaches to enhance efficacy in glioma patients.

## MYC/MYCN Amplification
MYCN overexpression and amplification have been frequently associated with GBM, occurring in approximately 40% of tumor samples, while MYC amplification shows variable frequencies depending on the specific glioma subtype (Table  ) (Borgenvik et al. ). In pediatric high-grade gliomas, MYCN amplifications are particularly prevalent in specific molecular subgroups, with H3.3 G34 subtype pHGGs showing upregulated MYCN expression and representing one of the most biologically aggressive subtypes (Borgenvik et al. ).
Functionally, MYC proteins act as master regulators of transcription, controlling proliferation, cell cycle progression, metabolic reprogramming, and cancer stem cell maintenance (Wang et al. ; Annibali et al. ). In glioma stem-like cells, MYC modulates key transcription factors such as OLIG2, SOX2, and POU3F2, maintaining tumorigenic potential (Kozono et al. ). Furthermore, MYC-driven tumors exhibit a metabolic shift toward glycolysis, creating dependencies exploitable by glycolytic inhibitors like NAMPT inhibitors (Tateishi et al. ). Additional oncogenic pathways, such as the MYC/PNO1/THBS1/FAK/Akt axis, contribute to invasion and proliferation, broadening the therapeutic landscape beyond direct MYC inhibition.
MYCN amplification serves as a reliable indicator of poor prognosis in various tumor types, including gliomas, where it correlates with increased tumor aggressiveness and reduced patient survival (Gonzales-Céspedes and Navarro ). In the context of GBM, MYC expression levels correlate with tumor grade and malignancy, with low expression observed in grade I and II tumors and high expression characteristic of grade III and IV tumors (Herms et al. ). This correlation extends to survival outcomes, where patients with MYC/MYCN amplified tumors typically experience shorter overall survival and higher rates of tumor recurrence. However, discrepancies between gene amplification and protein expression complicate both prognosis and therapeutic targeting, emphasizing the need for integrated molecular diagnostics (Gonzales-Céspedes and Navarro ). In this sense, recent advances in molecular profiling have led to the development of clinically relevant prognostic indices that incorporate MYC/MYCN amplification status along with other molecular markers. A novel clinical index based on the expression of genes associated with glioma-associated mesenchymal stem cells has been established, which predicts survival and treatment response independent of traditional prognostic factors, including radiotherapy, temozolomide treatment, or MGMT methylation status (Herms et al. ). This index maintained its prognostic value across patient groups stratified according to WHO grading, except for grade II patients, and reflected the immunological composition of tumors as well as the expression levels of immunotherapy targets. Such integrated molecular approaches demonstrate the clinical utility of incorporating MYC/MYCN amplification status into comprehensive prognostic frameworks (Stepanenko et al. ).
Therapeutically, recent developments have transformed MYC/MYCN amplification from an “undruggable” target to a vulnerable Achilles’ heel via indirect and combinatorial strategies. Inhibitory tools like Omomyc and siRNA-loaded engineered exosomes (iExo-Myc) have shown efficacy in preclinical GBM models, reducing tumor growth and shifting cell phenotypes toward less aggressive states (Annibali et al. ; Haltom et al. ). Drug screens in MYCN-driven pediatric gliomas have identified chemotherapeutic agents such as Doxorubicin and Irinotecan as promising candidates (Schoof et al. ), while metabolic inhibitors demonstrate selectivity for MYC/MYCN amplified tumor spheres (Tateishi et al. ). Moreover, clinical trials of downstream pathway inhibitors like FAK inhibitors (PF562271, VS-4718) suggest a feasible path to targeting MYC-dependent oncogenic signaling networks (Tang et al. ). Together, these translational insights establish a foundation for tailored therapeutic regimens based on MYC/MYCN status in glioma patients.

## RB1 Alterations in GBM
RB1 gene alterations are more commonly associated with high-grade gliomas (Urbschat et al. ). Approximately 30% of malignant astrocytomas exhibit loss of heterozygosity (LOH) at the RB1 locus, while such alterations are absent in low-grade gliomas, suggesting that RB1 disruption is more relevant to tumor progression rather than initiation (Table  ). In glioblastoma specifically, RB1 is altered in about 14% of cases, typically through mutations or LOH, leading to impaired regulation of the G1 to S phase cell cycle checkpoint. One of the predominant mechanisms responsible for rB1 silencing is promoter hypermethylation, a process observed more frequently in secondary GBMs than in their primary counterparts, as reported by Nakamura and colleagues (Nakamura et al. ).
Interestingly, in IDH-wildtype GBM, RB1 mutations are associated with improved clinical outcomes. In one cohort, patients harboring RB1 exhibited a median OS of 23.5 months compared to 17.7 months in those with wild-type RB1, with a hazard ratio (HR) ranging from 0.24 to 0.49 (p ≤ 0.016) (Dono et al. ). These mutations often co-occur with TP53 and SETD2 alterations but are typically mutually exclusive with CDKN2A/B deletions and EGFR amplifications, suggesting a distinct molecular group within IDH-wildtype.
At the mechanistic level, RB1 loss results in deregulated E2F transcription factor activity, which facilitates uncontrolled cellular proliferation. From a translational standpoint, therapeutic strategies have focused on the CDK4/6-RB1 axis. In RB1-proficient gliomas, CDK4/6 inhibitors such as palbociclib have demonstrated preclinical efficacy, inducing G1 phase arrest and apoptosis (Schröder and McDonald ; Whittaker et al. ).
However, resistance to CDK4/6 inhibition can develop, often through loss of functional RB1, as observed in breast cancer models, highlighting the need for combination treatments or alternative approaches to overcome therapeutic resistance. These findings underscore the potential of integrating RB1 status into both prognostic frameworks and targeted treatment strategies in GBM (Condorelli et al. ; Safonov et al. ).

## TRAF7 Mutations in Gliomas
Tumor necrosis factor receptor-associated factors (TRAFs) are a group of cytoplasmic signaling proteins. Most TRAFs also function as E3 ubiquitin ligases, triggering downstream signaling pathways that lead to the activation of nuclear factor-κB (NF-κB), mitogen-activated protein kinases (MAPKs), or interferon regulatory factors (IRFs) (Xie ). Altered TRAF7 expression has been associated with the development and progression of brain tumors like meningiomas (Najm et al. ) (Table  ). The role of this marker in the context of gliomas has been less established; however, a recent study performed by Chen et al. ( ) has demonstrated that high TRAF7 expression is closely associated with a higher recurrence rate and poorer overall survival in glioma patients. In vitro, they demonstrated that TRAF7 knockdown significantly inhibits glioma cell proliferation, invasion, and migration (Chen et al. ) and, through RNA-seq analysis, they revealed that TRAF7 inhibition activates pathways related to cellular senescence and cell cycle arrest. In more detail, they reported that TRAF7 interacts with KLF4, and rescue assays demonstrated that KLF4 overexpression can reverse the effects of TRAF7 depletion on proliferation and cellular senescence. Interestingly, the combination of TRAF7 knockdown and lomustine treatment has shown enhanced therapeutic efficacy both in vitro and in patient-derived glioma stem cell (GSC) assays (Chen et al. ). These findings suggest that TRAF7 could serve as a prognostic and a potential therapeutic target in the management of glioma progression and recurrence. However, further clinical studies are warranted to verify the potential applications of these discoveries.

## Gene Fusions and Tyrosine Kinases

## EGFR Alterations
EGFR amplification occurs in 40–50% of IDH-wildtype GBM, often co-occurring with chromosome 7 gain/10 loss (Lassman et al. ; Wang et al. ). The EGFR variant III (EGFRvIII) is a tumor-specific mutant of the epidermal growth factor receptor (a receptor tyrosine kinase) detected in approximately 20–30% of GBM cases (Wang et al. ). It is rare or absent in LGG and other CNS tumors. Unlike many adult GBMs, pediatric GBMs rarely show EGFRvIII (EGFR alterations in children often involve PDGFRA or H3K27 mutations instead) (Table  ).
EGFRvIII arises from an in-frame genomic deletion of exons 2–7 in the EGFR gene (Chistiakov et al. ). This deletion removes a large part of the extracellular ligand-binding domain; the resulting protein is incapable of binding normal ligands but is constitutively dimerized and autophosphorylated (Chistiakov et al. ). In this way, EGFRvIII is a permanently active form of EGFR, driving downstream signaling (MAPK, PI3K/AKT) independent of growth factors. It is also inefficiently downregulated, leading to sustained pathway activation (Chistiakov et al. ).
Clinically, EGFRvIII has been explored as a therapeutic target. This variant confers radioresistance via constitutive PI3K/AKT activation (Mukherjee et al. ). Its tumor-specific expression (not found in normal brain) makes it attractive for targeted immunotherapy. Contrary to historical data, EGFRvIII expression correlates with prolonged survival in temozolomide-treated patients (HR = 0.42, p = 0.0023), particularly when combined with methylated MGMT and intact PTEN (Montano et al. ). Regional variations exist, with lower EGFR amplification rates in Asian populations (35% vs. 56% globally) (Lassman et al. ). EGFRvIII-specific monoclonal antibodies and vaccines (e.g., rindopepimut/CDX-110) have been developed and tested in trials (Paff et al. ). Unfortunately, so far, outcomes have been mixed: EGFRvIII GBMs tend to be aggressive, and trials of rindopepimut plus standard therapy failed to improve overall survival. EGFRvIII expression is also associated with resistance to conventional EGFR tyrosine kinase inhibitors, since the mutation alters drug binding (Huang and Fu ).
Prognostically, studies differ; some meta-analyses suggest EGFRvIII does not clearly change survival in GBM, whereas other data indicate it often correlates with more invasive tumors and worse outcomes (Chen et al. ). In practice, EGFRvIII status may have prognostic significance but is not used alone for risk stratification (Mizoguchi et al. ). It does remain an important predictive marker for experimental therapies and a diagnostic indicator of classical GBM biology.

## MET Gene Fusions in CNS Tumors
The MET gene encodes c-MET, the receptor tyrosine kinase for hepatocyte growth factor (HGF). c-MET activation normally stimulates MAPK and PI3K pathways to promote growth and survival. Pathogenic MET fusions involve joining the MET kinase domain (often exon 14 or downstream exons) to a 5′ partner (e.g., TPM3, TFG, or others) that drives constitutive dimerization (Zuckermann et al. ; Febres-Aldana et al. ). The result is ligand-independent MET kinase activity and enhanced MAPK/PI3K signaling (Table  ).
MET fusions in the brain are rare but most characteristically define a subset of pediatric high-grade gliomas. A recently identified entity, infant-type hemispheric glioma (IHG), often harbors MET fusions, as well as ALK, NTRK, and ROS1 fusions (Febres-Aldana et al. ). In one series, MET fusions were found in up to 12% of pediatric high-grade gliomas in children older than 3 years (Zuckermann et al. ). MET fusions have also been reported in adult disease: for example, up to 15% of secondary GBMs may contain MET fusions (Bao et al. ). Outside of these settings, MET fusions are uncommon in CNS tumors; routine surveys show no significant MET fusions in LGGs or medulloblastoma. By contrast, MET amplification or exon 14 skipping mutations occur in systemic cancers and occasionally in high-grade gliomas, but pure gene fusions are mostly confined to the infant/childhood tumors above.
Therapeutically, MET fusions are actionable: several MET inhibitors have CNS activity. Crizotinib (a multi-kinase inhibitor) was the first FDA-approved MET inhibitor; it showed transient benefit in a MET-fusion glioma patient but was not durable (Bender et al. ). More recently, the highly selective MET inhibitor capmatinib has shown promising intracranial responses in MET-driven tumors (Gainor et al. ; Wu et al. ). Because of this, identifying a MET fusion can guide targeted therapy in children: capmatinib or other MET inhibitors are considered off-label options. As with other RTK fusions, patients with MET fusion gliomas may be enrolled in clinical trials or given broad-spectrum therapies for MET alterations. Currently, there is no established targeted regimen proven in phase III trials, but preclinical and early clinical evidence support the concept of MET-directed therapy for MET-rearranged CNS tumors (Sattler and Salgia ; Nakazawa et al. ). In summary, MET fusions serve as predictive markers for MET inhibitor therapy in pediatric gliomas (Gorodezki et al. ). They also define a molecular subgroup, infant hemispheric glioma, with somewhat better prognosis than other pediatric HGGs.

## NTRK Fusions in CNS Tumors
The NTRK1, NTRK2, and NTRK3 genes encode the TRK A, B, and C receptor tyrosine kinases for neurotrophins. Oncogenic NTRK fusions juxtapose an N-terminal partner gene, often one that promotes oligomerization, to the C-terminal kinase domain of TRK, creating a chimeric TRK protein that signals constitutively (Wu et al. ). These fusion proteins drive cell proliferation via MAPK and PI3K pathways. NTRK fusions are oncogenic “actionable” drivers; they are potent inducers of tumor growth yet are exquisitely sensitive to TRK inhibitors (Cocco et al. ) (Table  ).
In the CNS, NTRK fusions are most commonly seen in pediatric tumors. Up to 40% of DHG in children under age 3 harbor an NTRK fusion (Wu et al. ). Signature examples include ETV6–NTRK3, TPM3–NTRK1, and various NTRK2 fusions (Cetinbas et al. ). Older children and adults have NTRK fusions only rarely in gliomas. NTRK rearrangements are generally not found in classic GBM or most other brain tumors, except for some rare pediatric gliomas. There are reports of NTRK fusions in LGGs (e.g., pilocytic astrocytoma has ~ 3% NTRK2 fusions) and are extremely occasional in other glioneuronal tumors, but by far the highest yield is in infant HGG (Hargrave et al. ). NTRK fusions are essentially absent in embryonal tumors like medulloblastoma.
Clinically, the importance of NTRK fusions lies in targeted therapy. Larotrectinib and entrectinib are small-molecule TRK inhibitors approved in a tumor-agnostic manner for NTRK fusion–positive cancers (Ziegler et al. ; Fuerst ; Mayr et al. ). Pediatric CNS case reports and trials show dramatic responses of NTRK-fusion gliomas to these drugs (Ziegler et al. ). For example, relapsed infant gliomas with NTRK fusions have exhibited complete or near-complete regression on larotrectinib. Thus, NTRK fusion status is a predictive biomarker; it directly informs the use of TRK inhibitors (Santi et al. ). These fusions are also prognostically favorable in the sense that effective therapy is available; historically, without targeted agents, NTRK fusion HGG would be high-risk. In WHO CNS5, tumors with NTRK fusions and other RTK fusions are grouped under “infant-type hemispheric glioma,” reflecting their distinct biology and relatively better outcomes (Louis et al. ).

## ALK Fusions in CNS Tumors
The ALK gene encodes a receptor tyrosine kinase normally active in development. ALK fusion oncogenes couple the ALK kinase domain to various partner genes (e.g., NPM1, EML4, or others), leading to constitutive ALK signaling. In the brain, ALK fusions are exceedingly rare and almost exclusively reported in infants. They form part of the spectrum of “infant-type hemispheric gliomas” along with NTRK, ROS1, and MET fusions (Guerreiro Stucklin et al. ). Infant gliomas harboring ALK fusions tend to present in the first few months of life (mean age ~ 1.6 months) and are often high-grade histologically. Outside of this group, ALK is not known to be altered in major CNS tumors (no role in adult GBM, medulloblastoma, etc.) (Table  ).
ALK fusions are clinically important as therapeutic targets. Potent ALK inhibitors have shown striking efficacy in ALK-driven tumors. Case reports illustrate that ALK-positive infant glioma patients who received ALK inhibitors (ceritinib, crizotinib, or the CNS-penetrant lorlatinib) achieved rapid tumor regression (Guerreiro Stucklin et al. ). Notably, a single case reported by Bagchi et al. described an infant with ALK-fusion glioma who had a near-complete response to lorlatinib (Bagchi et al. ). Accordingly, any child’s glioma found to carry an ALK fusion is considered for ALK inhibitor therapy. In the 2021 WHO classification, ALK fusion gliomas are included in the infant-type hemispheric glioma category, which, similar to NTRK cases, has a relatively favorable 5-year survival, around 50–60%, compared to other pediatric HGG (Guerreiro Stucklin et al. ). In summary, ALK fusions in CNS tumors serve as an actionable predictive marker; they identify a unique tumor subgroup treatable with ALK inhibitors. They also carry diagnostic weight (defining infantile hemispheric glioma) and imply a potentially better prognosis if targeted therapy is applied (Bagchi et al. ).

## ROS1
ROS1, a receptor tyrosine kinase closely related to ALK, is an emerging but rare oncogene in CNS tumors. Oncogenic ROS1 fusions (gene rearrangements that fuse ROS1’s kinase domain to various partners) are well-known in lung cancer and inflammatory myofibroblastic tumors; they have also been identified sporadically in gliomas and glioneuronal tumors. Recent genomic surveys have found ROS1 fusion events in a small subset of pediatric and adult gliomas (Meredith et al. ). The prototypical fusion in CNS tumors is GOPC–ROS1 (also known as FIG–ROS1), which constitutively activates ROS1 kinase (Charest et al. ). Unlike ALK fusions, GOPC–ROS1 signals via Golgi localization and leads to robust activation of downstream RAS/MAPK and PI3K/AKT pathways. Although rare, these ROS1 fusions are diagnostically useful because they are targetable, next-generation ROS1 inhibitors such as entrectinib have demonstrated potency against ROS1 fusion proteins and achieve good CNS penetration (Drilon et al. ; Fischer et al. ). Early reports document significant responses to ROS1-directed TKIs in brain tumor patients harboring ROS1 fusions. Thus, identification of ROS1 rearrangements in brain tumors, via sequencing or RNA panels, has clear translational relevance, opening the door to repurposing ROS1 inhibitors (like lorlatinib or entrectinib) for these patients (Charest et al. ) (Table  ).

## FGFR1 Mutations and Fusions
FGFR1 mutations and fusions are significant oncogenic drivers in gliomas, particularly in pediatric LGGs and glioneuronal tumors, with a prevalence of 3–12% across all glioma subtypes (Engelhardt et al. ). Recurrent FGFR1 tyrosine kinase domain mutations (e.g., N546K, K656E) and fusions (e.g., FGFR1-TACC1, MYO5A-FGFR1) induce constitutive activation of MAPK and PI3K/mTOR pathways, promoting proliferation, metabolic reprogramming, and therapy resistance (Bale ; Galvin et al. ). These alterations are enriched in pediatric cohorts and glioneuronal histologies, such as dysembryoplastic neuroepithelial tumors (DNTs) and rosette-forming glioneuronal tumors (RGNTs) (Linzey et al. ). FGFR1-driven tumors often exhibit intratumoral heterogeneity, with spatial variations in hypoxia and immune infiltration influencing aggressiveness (Egbivwie et al. ). While FGFR1 mutations may co-occur with other MAPK pathway alterations, fusions like FGFR1-TACC1 are more common in high-grade gliomas (HGGs) and associated with chromosomal instability (Di Stefano et al. ).
Prognostically, FGFR1 mutations in LGGs correlate with favorable outcomes compared to fusion-driven tumors, which often display aggressive behavior (Schüller et al. ). In diffuse midline gliomas with H3K27M mutations, coexisting FGFR1 mutations are linked to improved survival (median 24 vs. 12 months in wild-type cases), suggesting a mitigating effect on H3K27M-driven malignancy (Schüller et al. ). Conversely, FGFR1 overexpression in GBM predicts temozolomide resistance and shorter survival (12.7 vs. 21.7 months) (Shirai and Chakravarti ). Diagnostically, FGFR1 alterations aid in classifying molecular subtypes, such as supratentorial pilocytic astrocytomas with MYO5A–FGFR1 fusions, and are integrated into the 2021 WHO guidelines to resolve histologically ambiguous cases (Galvin et al. ). Methylation profiling further refines classification, with FGFR1–TACC1 fusions defining a subset of extraventricular neurocytomas (Sievers et al. ).
FGFR3 alterations, particularly FGFR3-TACC3 fusions, occur in 3–5% of adult IDH-wildtype GBMs, driving oxidative phosphorylation and chromosomal instability (Gött and Uhl ). These tumors exhibit mutual exclusivity with EGFR amplifications and retain the fusion at recurrence, making them actionable targets for FGFR inhibitors like erdafitinib (response rate: 10–44%) (Gong et al. ). FGFR2 fusions (e.g., FGFR2-INA) define a rare epigenetic subgroup of low-grade glioneuronal tumors, often lacking IDH1/2 or BRAF mutations (Gupta et al. ). While FGFR2 amplifications are rare in gliomas, they are prevalent in gastrointestinal cancers, highlighting tissue-specific oncogenic roles (Ratti et al. ). FGFR3-driven GBMs show mesenchymal transition and immune evasion, whereas FGFR1-altered tumors activate neuronal transcriptional programs, influencing therapeutic vulnerability (Li et al. ).
Therapeutically, selective FGFR inhibitors (e.g., infigratinib) show limited efficacy as monotherapies (6-month progression-free survival: 16%), but durable responses occur in tumors with activating mutations (e.g., K656E) or fusions (Lassman et al. ). Preclinical models demonstrate synergy between FGFR1 inhibition and radiation or MEK/PI3K inhibitors, reversing metabolic dependencies and radiosensitivity (Haagensen et al. ). Emerging strategies include nanoparticle-encapsulated FGFR1-siRNAs and engineered exosomes to enhance blood–brain barrier penetration (Mehdizadeh et al. ). Challenges include resistance via gatekeeper mutations (e.g., V561M) and intratumoral heterogeneity, necessitating combinatorial approaches (Smyth et al. ). Clinical trials (NCT04166409, NCT04576117) are evaluating MEK inhibitors in FGFR1-altered pLGGs, while liquid biopsies monitoring FGFR1 fusions in cerebrospinal fluid offer non-invasive response assessment (Manoharan et al. ; Charifa et al. ).
In conclusion, FGFR1 alterations define a molecularly distinct glioma subset with age-specific prevalence and therapeutic vulnerabilities. While prognostic implications vary by mutation type and context, biomarker-driven trials and combination therapies targeting MAPK/PI3K pathways hold promise. Standardized molecular profiling and novel delivery systems are critical to overcoming resistance and improving outcomes for patients with FGFR1-driven gliomas (Table  ).

## ZNF395
Finally, ZNF395 is a lesser-known marker among these. It encodes a zinc-finger protein, a class that comprises a large fraction (~ 5%) of human genes and includes many transcription factors and chromatin regulators (Charifa et al. ). By analogy, ZNF395 might influence gene expression or genome stability in cancer, but its role in CNS tumors has not been clearly defined. Large-scale genomic studies of gliomas have occasionally listed ZNF395 among genes with mutations or altered expression, but no peer-reviewed report has yet elucidated its function or clinical significance in brain tumors (Xiao et al. ). Thus, ZNF395 remains a candidate biomarker requiring further investigation. In general, uncharacterized zinc-finger proteins like ZNF395 could modulate oncogenic pathways, but specific data in glioma, medulloblastoma, or ependymoma are lacking (Table  ).

## Epigenetic Biomarkers

## DNA Methylation Profiling
DNA methylation plays a central role in glioma biology, with the glioma CpG island methylator phenotype (G-CIMP) emerging as a defining feature of IDH-mutant tumors. Approximately 40–50% of IDH-mutant gliomas exhibit G-CIMP, characterized by genome-wide CpG island hypermethylation driven by the oncometabolite 2-hydroxyglutarate (2-HG), which inhibits DNA demethylases (Noushmehr et al. ; Christensen et al. ). This epigenetic reprogramming silences tumor suppressor genes (e.g., CDKN2A) and activates pro-survival pathways, while G-CIMP stratification (high vs. low) correlates with prognosis—G-CIMP-high tumors show prolonged survival (median 7.2 years) compared to G-CIMP-low (2.7 years) and IDH-wildtype gliomas (1.2 years) (Malta et al. ). Notably, partial DNA demethylation during malignant progression, often involving Wnt pathway genes, is linked to accelerated cell division and therapy resistance (Nomura et al. ). MGMT promoter methylation, observed in 30–45% of GBMs, remains a critical biomarker, predicting improved response to temozolomide and longer survival (median 21.7 vs. 12.7 months in unmethylated cases) (Hegi et al. ; Pandith et al. ). However, discordance between methylation status and protein expression occurs in 23–26% of cases, necessitating multimodal assessment.
Therapeutically, epigenetic targeting shows mixed results. DNMT inhibitors like azacitidine demonstrate limited efficacy in IDH-mutant gliomas despite preclinical promise, though combination approaches with temozolomide or venetoclax are under investigation (Yamashita et al. ; Federici et al. ; Sun and Turcan ). EZH2 inhibitors (e.g., tazemetostat) reduce proliferation and induce differentiation in GBM stem cells by reversing PRC2-mediated repression of neurogenesis genes, particularly in mesenchymal subtypes (Julia and Salles ). Challenges include blood-brain barrier penetration and identifying optimal patient subgroups, as MGMT-methylated tumors with smaller volumes (< 32 cm3) show better survival (560.5 vs. 329 days) (Szylberg et al. ). Emerging strategies include nanoparticle-encapsulated agents and CRISPR/dCas9-based editors to enhance specificity (Givens et al. ) (Table  ).
In conclusion, DNA methylation profiling has revolutionized glioma management, enabling precise molecular stratification and biomarker-driven therapies. While MGMT and G-CIMP statuses guide prognosis and treatment response, clinical translation of epigenetic therapies requires overcoming drug delivery limitations and heterogeneity. Future integration of multi-omics data, blood-based methylation biomarkers (e.g., circulating tumor DNA), and combinatorial regimens targeting genetic-epigenetic vulnerabilities may transform gliomas into chronically manageable diseases. Standardized assays and cost-effective platforms will be critical to global implementation, particularly in resource-limited settings.

## DICER1 Mutations
DICER1 mutations or chromosomal loss occur in diverse CNS tumors, including high-grade gliomas, where they define a subset with unique clinicopathological features (Liu et al. ). In a retrospective cohort study of 98 patients with primary CNS tumors containing DICER1 mutations or loss of DICER1 on chromosome 14q, the most common malignancies were grade 3/4 glioma (61%), grade 1/2 glioma (17%), and CNS sarcoma (6%) (Liu et al. ). These alterations disrupt microRNA (miRNA) biogenesis, leading to global miRNA dysregulation that enhances tumorigenesis and stem cell maintenance (Mansouri et al. ). For instance, DICER1 loss in glioma stem cells (GSCs) increases tumor aggressiveness and radiation resistance by altering miRNAs such as let-7b and miR-21, which normally suppress oncogenes like SOX2 and STAT3 (Foshay and Gallicano ; Mansouri et al. ). Synthetic lethality studies reveal that DICER1 inactivation, combined with RB1 and TP53 loss, accelerates carcinogenesis, highlighting interconnected pathways in tumor suppression (Huang et al. ). Clinically, DICER1-altered gliomas exhibit molecular heterogeneity, with biallelic mutations common in pediatric sarcomas and pineal tumors (Antoniades et al. ). Translational strategies aim to exploit miRNA dysregulation, using inhibitors of oncogenic miRNAs (e.g., miR-17-92 cluster) or replacements for tumor-suppressive miRNAs, which have shown promise in preclinical models (Hong et al. ). Additionally, targeting downstream effectors like CCNE1 or EGFR, which are upregulated in DICER1-deficient cells, may offer therapeutic leverage (An et al. ; Ji et al. ) (Table  ).

## EZH Inhibitory Protein
EZH inhibitory protein (EZHIP, a.k.a. CXorf67) is a recently discovered epigenetic oncoprotein in pediatric CNS tumors (Nambirajan et al. ). EZHIP is normally expressed during development but is aberrantly overexpressed in nearly all posterior fossa ependymomas group A (PFA-EPN) and in a subset of DMG that lack H3K27M mutation (Jain et al. ; Antin et al. ). EZHIP binds to the SET domain of EZH2 (the catalytic subunit of PRC2) and inhibits its methyltransferase activity, mimicking the effect of H3K27M mutations (Jenseit et al. ). The net result is global loss of H3K27 trimethylation in the tumor—a defining feature of PFA ependymoma chromatin and H3-wildtype DMG chromatin. Immunohistochemically, strong nuclear EZHIP staining is seen in essentially 100% of tested PFA-EPN and in H3-wildtype diffuse midline gliomas with H3K27me3 loss (Antin et al. ). Biologically, EZHIP-driven tumors behave aggressively: PFA-EPN has a dismal outcome (often in very young children) similar to H3K27M DIPG, and the newly recognized EZHIP-positive midline gliomas are likewise high-grade (Pajtler et al. ). Therapeutically, EZHIP poses challenges: because it directly blocks PRC2 activity, classic epigenetic therapies (like EZH2 inhibitors) may not be effective. Some preclinical work is exploring whether targeting co-factors (e.g. EZH1, or modulating H3K27 methylation indirectly) could be effective in EZHIP-driven tumors (Jain et al. ). At minimum, EZHIP serves as a specific diagnostic and prognostic biomarker: its presence identifies a distinct molecular subtype (PFA-EPN or EZHIP-DMG) with poor prognosis (Castel et al. ) (Table  ).

## Biomarkers Linked To the Immune System

## PD-L1
PD-L1 expression is prevalent in gliomas, with rates increasing with tumor grade. Across studies, PD-L1 is expressed in 51.1% of gliomas overall, rising from 49.2% in grade II to 68.8% in GBM (Zeng et al. ). IDH wild-type gliomas exhibit significantly higher PD-L1 expression (32%) compared to IDH-mutant tumors (6%), highlighting its association with aggressive biology (Hölzl et al. ). Lower PD-L1 levels are observed in proneural and glioma G-CIMP subtypes, while higher PD-L1 expression is detected in the mesenchymal subtype (Xue et al. ). This relationship is partly explained by the higher methylation level of the PD-L1 promoter in IDH-mutant gliomas compared to IDH-wildtype gliomas, with PD-L1 expression negatively correlating with promoter methylation levels (Chen et al. ). Elevated PD-L1 also correlates with higher Ki67 proliferation indices, reinforcing its link to aggressive phenotypes (Kumar et al. ) (Table  ).
Spatial heterogeneity is notable, with PD-L1 often enriched at tumor edges, forming a molecular shield to evade immune surveillance (Xue et al. ). Biologically, PD-L1 upregulation is driven by oncogenic pathways (e.g., PTEN loss activating PI3K-Akt signaling), hypoxia, and cytokines like IFN-γ, which promote immune evasion by binding PD-1 on T-cells, suppressing antitumor responses (Cha et al. ; Ortega et al. ). Additionally, PD-L1 exerts cell-intrinsic oncogenic effects, enhancing glioma proliferation and invasion via MAPK pathways (Ma et al. ).
The relationship between PD-L1 expression and patient outcomes in gliomas has been extensively studied, though results remain somewhat controversial. Prognostically, high PD-L1 correlates with worse overall survival (OS) in gliomas (HR = 1.30) and GBM (HR = 1.40) (Xue et al. ). Multivariate analysis suggests that low PD-L1 expression remains predictive of longer OS even after accounting for other demographic and clinical factors (Nduom et al. ). In a similar way, pooled analysis indicated that PD-L1 expression in tumor tissues was significantly related to poor OS in GBM (HR = 1.63, 95% CI 1.19–2.24, P = 0.003), but interestingly, in subgroup analyses, PD-L1 positivity was significantly associated with worse OS for patients of American and Asian regions, but not for European regions (Hao et al. ). Likewise, in recurrent gliomas, PD-L1 positivity post-therapy paradoxically associates with improved OS, possibly reflecting immune activation and therapeutic success (Weathers et al. ).
Therapeutically, anti-PD-1/PD-L1 monotherapy has shown limited efficacy in GBM due to the immunosuppressive microenvironment dominated by tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) and regulatory T-cells (Tregs) (Schonfeld et al. ). Combination strategies—pairing checkpoint inhibitors with antiangiogenics (e.g., bevacizumab), chemotherapy, or myeloid-targeting agents—demonstrate preliminary promise in preclinical and early clinical trials (Schonfeld et al. ). Novel approaches, such as nanoparticle-encapsulated siRNA targeting PD-L1 or engineered exosomes, aim to enhance blood-brain barrier penetration and reduce systemic toxicity (Wu and Wang ). Challenges include identifying predictive biomarkers (e.g., MHC class II expression in TAMs) and addressing resistance mechanisms like compensatory MET or ERK activation (Yakubov et al. ).

## CTLA4
CTLA4 is an important immune checkpoint molecule involved in glioma-mediated immunosuppression. CTLA4 expression is increased in higher-grade gliomas, particularly IDH-wildtype and mesenchymal subtypes, with a prevalence increasing from 49.2% in grade II to 68.8% in GBM (Liu et al. ). It correlates with infiltration of immunosuppressive cells, including regulatory T cells (Tregs) and tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs), and shows strong associations with PD-1, TIGIT, and chemokine pathways (Liu et al. ). Biologically, CTLA4 inhibits T-cell priming by binding CD80/CD86 on antigen-presenting cells and promotes tumor immune evasion through interactions with myeloid cells in the TME (Vandenborre et al. ). In GBM, CTLA4 expression is linked to JAK/STAT and MAPK pathway activation, enhancing tumor proliferation and mesenchymal transition (Ghouzlani et al. ).
High CTLA4 expression acts as a negative prognostic marker for OS in gliomas (HR = 1.7), particularly in IDH-wildtype and recurrent tumors (Liu et al. ; Guan et al. ). Moreover, soluble CTLA4 (sCTLA4) can be measured in serum as a noninvasive biomarker in glioma patients, with higher levels (> 189.84 pg/mL) correlating with advanced tumor grade and independently predicting PFS in multivariate analysis (Liu et al. ). Diagnostic stratification using CTLA4 levels helps identify mesenchymal subtypes, which exhibit higher immune cell infiltration and resistance to conventional therapies (Guan et al. ). In serum, sCTLA4 levels > 220.43 pg/mL distinguish high-grade gliomas (sensitivity 73.1%, specificity 79.2%) and independently predict PFS in multivariate analysis (Liu et al. ).
From a therapeutic perspective, anti-CTLA4 monotherapy (e.g., ipilimumab) shows limited efficacy in GBM, possibly due to compensatory upregulation of PD-1 and blood-brain barrier (BBB) limitations (Bianconi et al. ). However, combination strategies demonstrate promise: Fc-enhanced anti-CTLA4 antibodies (e.g., botensilimab) selectively deplete intratumoral Tregs via macrophage-mediated phagocytosis and synergize with anti-PD-1 and doxorubicin, achieving 90% cure rates in preclinical models (Kim et al. ; Chand et al. ). Nanoparticle-delivered CTLA4 inhibitors and engineered exosomes improve BBB penetration, while dual checkpoint blockade (e.g., CTLA4 + PD-1) is currently under clinical evaluation (NCT05273762, NCT05864534) (Beavis et al. ). Some of the remaining challenges include resistance driven by MET/ERK pathway reactivation and myeloid-mediated immunosuppression (Table  ).

## TIGIT
TIGIT (T-cell immunoreceptor with Ig and ITIM domains) is a critical immune checkpoint in gliomas, particularly GBM, where it is expressed on 25–60% of tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs), predominantly CD8 + T cells and regulatory T cells (Tregs) (Vincze et al. ). Its ligand, CD155, is overexpressed on glioma cells, perivascular monocytes, and neurons, creating an immunosuppressive niche by inhibiting cytotoxic T-cell activity and promoting T-cell exhaustion (Lucca et al. ; Lupo et al. ). TIGIT expression is significantly higher in GBM compared to non-neoplastic CNS conditions like multiple sclerosis, with co-expression of PD-1 observed in TILs, suggesting synergistic immune evasion mechanisms (Lucca et al. ). Preclinical studies reveal that TIGIT + CD8 + T cells in GBM exhibit reduced functionality, and peripheral TIGIT levels in patients correlate with systemic immunosuppression, highlighting its role in both local and systemic immune dysregulation (Liu et al. ) (Table  ).
Prognostically, high TIGIT expression is associated with shorter OS (HR = 1.53) and PFS in GBM, particularly in IDH-wildtype and mesenchymal subtypes (Hung et al. ; Raphael et al. ). It correlates with unmethylated MGMT promoters and resistance to temozolomide, underscoring its diagnostic value in identifying aggressive tumor subsets (Kitange et al. ). In pediatric high-grade gliomas (pHGGs), TIGIT mRNA levels above specific thresholds predict poorer outcomes, supporting its role as a biomarker for immune evasion and therapeutic targeting (Frederico et al. ). Spatial transcriptomic analyses further link TIGIT-rich niches to hypoxic regions and myeloid cell infiltration, which drive adaptive resistance (Hung et al. ).
Therapeutically, dual TIGIT/PD-1 blockade demonstrates preclinical efficacy, reducing myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) and enhancing CD8+T-cell infiltration in murine GBM models (Hung et al. ). Clinical trials, such as NCT05273762, are evaluating anti-TIGIT antibodies (e.g., domvanalimab) combined with PD-1 inhibitors, with early-phase studies showing manageable toxicity (Rodriguez-Abreu et al. ). However, challenges persist, including blood-brain barrier penetration and compensatory MET/ERK pathway activation (Ratnam et al. ). Innovative strategies like 89Zr-DFO-TIGIT immunoPET imaging enable non-invasive monitoring of TIGIT expression, while nanoparticle-delivered siRNAs aim to improve CNS-specific targeting (Hettich et al. ). Additionally, TIGIT blockade reprograms immunosuppressive myeloid cells into pro-inflammatory phenotypes, offering combinatorial opportunities with radiotherapy or metabolic inhibitors (Hung et al. ).

## B7-H3
B7-H3 (CD276) is overexpressed in 58–68.8% of gliomas, with prevalence increasing with tumor grade and peaking in GBM compared to LGG and normal brain tissue (Babič et al. ). Its expression is strongly associated with IDH-wildtype status and mesenchymal molecular subtypes, correlating with aggressive biological behaviors such as enhanced proliferation, invasion, and epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) (Wang et al. ). Mechanistically, B7-H3 activates the JAK2/STAT3/Slug pathway, downregulating E-cadherin while upregulating matrix metalloproteinases (MMP-2/-9), thereby promoting tumor cell migration and invasion (Zhong et al. ; Babič et al. ). Two splicing variants, 2IgB7-H3 and 4IgB7-H3, exhibit distinct roles: 4IgB7-H3 is enriched in primary GBM and serves as a therapeutic target, whereas 2IgB7-H3 is linked to temozolomide resistance and tumor recurrence (Zhong et al. ).
Prognostically, high B7-H3 expression predicts shorter overall survival in both LGG (55.5 vs. 136.1 months) and GBM (12.7 vs. 21.7 months), independent of IDH mutation or MGMT promoter methylation status (Tang et al. ). It also correlates with immunosuppressive features, including increased regulatory T-cell infiltration and myeloid-derived suppressor cell activity, contributing to immune evasion (Li et al. ). Diagnostically, B7-H3 helps differentiate gliomas from non-neoplastic brain tissue and identifies mesenchymal subtypes, with its perivascular expression in recurrent tumors serving as a potential biomarker for monitoring progression (Zhang et al. ).
T cells with chimeric antigen receptors (CAR) targeting B7-H3 have demonstrated preclinical efficacy, inducing tumor regression in orthotopic models of GBM and prolonging survival (Vitanza et al. ). Monoclonal antibodies such as MGA271 and 8H9 inhibit glioma growth through antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity (ADCC) and are currently undergoing clinical evaluation (Ahmed et al. ). The combination of STAT3 inhibitors (e.g., NAP) with temozolomide suppresses JAK2/STAT3 signaling, reducing tumor invasion and improving survival in murine models (Nguyen et al. ). Challenges include overcoming resistance via MET/ERK pathway reactivation and optimizing blood-brain barrier penetration for systemic therapies (Cui et al. ) (Table  ).

## TIM-3
TIM-3 (T-cell immunoglobulin and mucin domain-containing protein 3) is overexpressed in gliomas, particularly in GBM and IDH-wildtype tumors, with prevalence increasing with tumor grade. Studies report TIM-3 expression in 73.8% of GBM cases, notably enriched in mesenchymal molecular subtypes and IDH-wildtype gliomas compared to lower-grade or IDH-mutant tumors (Duong et al. ). Biologically, TIM-3 interacts with ligands like galectin-9 to drive immunosuppression by inducing T-cell exhaustion and promoting myeloid cell-mediated immune evasion. In glioma cells, TIM-3 activates NF-κB signaling, increasing interleukin-6 (IL-6) secretion, which recruits pro-tumorigenic macrophages and enhances tumor stemness (Yang et al. ). This immune checkpoint also correlates with inflammatory pathways akin to PD-L1, contributing to adaptive resistance in recurrent tumors (Table  ).
Prognostically, high TIM-3 expression is linked to poorer outcomes, especially in IDH-wildtype GBM with unmethylated MGMT promoters, where median survival drops to 7.6 months compared to 21.8 months in TIM-3-low/MGMT-methylated cases (Zhang et al. ). While some studies paradoxically associate TIM-3 with better survival in specific contexts (e.g., IDH-mutant gliomas), its role in mesenchymal subtypes and recurrent disease underscores its value as a biomarker for aggressive behavior and treatment resistance (Wu et al. ). Diagnostic stratification using TIM-3 levels helps identify immunosuppressive niches and predict temozolomide resistance, particularly when combined with MGMT status (Pu et al. ).
TIM-3 blockade shows promise in preclinical models. Dual inhibition with anti-PD-1 and radiotherapy achieved 100% survival in murine GBM, while anti-TIM-3 monotherapy reprogrammed myeloid cells into pro-inflammatory phenotypes in pediatric LGGs (Kim et al. ). In clinical trials, such as NCT05273762, TIM-3 inhibitors (e.g., MBG453) in combination therapies, though challenges like blood-brain barrier penetration and compensatory MET/ERK activation persist ([CSL STYLE ERROR: reference with no printed form.]). Innovative approaches, such as inhibitors delivered via nanoparticle and CAR-T cells targeting TIM-3 + myeloid cells, aim to enhance efficacy.

## NKG2C (KLRC2) in Gliomas
NKG2C is a C-type lectin-like receptor that forms heterodimers with CD94 to recognize HLA-E complexes presenting pathogen- or tumor-derived peptides (Kaiser et al. ). In gliomas, NKG2C can modulate immune responses by interacting with HLA-E on myeloid cells, leading to a reduction in immunosuppressive myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) and enhancing the infiltration of cytotoxic lymphocytes (Pearson et al. ). This shift contributes to the transformation of the tumor milieu into an immunologically “hot” environment, potentially increasing the effectiveness of immune checkpoint inhibitors.
In addition to its immune-related functions, KLRC2 expression within GBM cells is associated with more invasive tumor phenotypes. Single-cell RNA sequencing data show KLRC2 enrichment, particularly at the infiltrative margins of tumors, suggesting its involvement in processes like migration and angiogenesis (de Dios et al. ). Spatial expression patterns reveal that KLRC2 is overexpressed in approximately 30–40% of IDH-wildtype GBMs, with the highest expression observed at the tumor’s invasive edge. In contrast, its expression is rare in LGGs (de Dios et al. ). Furthermore, KLRC2 expression is significantly associated with mesenchymal GBM subtypes and those exhibiting EGFR amplification. It is notably absent in tumors harboring IDH1/2 mutations, indicating its role in distinct oncogenic pathways (Segura-Collar et al. ) (Table  ).
From a translational perspective, KLRC2 has potential as both a biomarker and a therapeutic target. In phase II clinical trials, GBM patients with high KLRC2 expression in tumors demonstrated extended overall survival (median OS: 18.2 months vs. 10.1 months) when treated with anti-PD-1 antibodies ([CSL STYLE ERROR: reference with no printed form.]). These findings are supported by preclinical studies in GL261 murine glioma models, where KLRC2 overexpression enhanced CD8 + T-cell infiltration and reduced MDSC presence following therapy (Cejalvo et al. ). Therapeutically, antibody-drug conjugates targeting NKG2C are currently under development, aiming to harness NK cell-mediated cytotoxicity against glioma cells. Additionally, KLRC2 detection in liquid biopsies, such as circulating tumor DNA, offers a non-invasive approach to identify gliomas that may be responsive to immunotherapy (Kellner et al. ; de Dios et al. ).

## MMR Deficiency
Mismatch repair (MMR) deficiency in gliomas drives genomic instability through impaired correction of DNA replication errors, leading to hypermutation (≥ 10 mutations/Mb) and microsatellite instability (MSI). While rare in adult GBMs (3.7%), primary MMR deficiency (MMRD) affects 12.4% of pediatric high-grade gliomas, particularly in IDH-wildtype tumors with TP53 pathogenic variants (Venetis et al. ). Loss of MMR proteins (MLH1, PMS2, MSH2, MSH6) detected via immunohistochemistry (IHC) or sequencing correlates with temozolomide resistance due to failed apoptosis induction via futile repair cycles (Friker et al. ).
Despite not being directly related to the immune system, MMRD gliomas have a higher neoantigen burden, making them candidates for anti-PD-1 immunotherapy, though intratumoral heterogeneity, where 50% retain functional MMR subclones, limits response durability (Xie et al. ). New liquid biopsy methods detect MMR-related mutations in circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA), enabling noninvasive monitoring. In young patients (< 40 years) with GBM, systematic MMR screening is critical, as 12.5% of IDH-wildtype cases under 40 harbor MMRD, often linked to Lynch syndrome (germline MSH2/MLH1 mutations) (Ronvaux et al. ). Recent studies highlight durable responses to immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) in hypermutated gliomas, particularly those with germline MMR defects, though post-temozolomide secondary MMRD tumors show limited immunogenicity due to clonal heterogeneity (Johanns et al. ). Standardized next-generation sequencing (NGS) panels outperform IHC for detecting MMRD, identifying 15% of cases missed by traditional methods, underscoring the need for molecular stratification in immunotherapy trials (Guo et al. ) (Table  ).

## STAT3 Activation
Constitutive STAT3 activation, marked by Tyr705/Ser727 phosphorylation, occurs in 53% of GBMs and correlates with mesenchymal transition, immunosuppression, and poor survival (Zhang et al. ). Tumor-intrinsic STAT3 upregulates anti-apoptotic (Bcl-2), pro-invasive (MMP-9, FAK), and angiogenic (VEGF) factors, while polarizing microglia/macrophages to immunosuppressive M2 phenotypes via IL-10/TGF-β secretion (Wierenga et al. ). Phosphorylated STAT3 (p-STAT3) levels in exosomes or plasma serve as liquid biomarkers (AUC: 0.79) for monitoring therapeutic response (Table  ). Despite preclinical success, clinical trials of STAT3 inhibitors (e.g., WP1066) show limited efficacy (median PFS: 2.3 months), partly due to compensatory IGF-1R signaling in STAT3-low tumors (Groot et al. ). Combinatorial approaches—such as JSI-124 (STAT3 inhibitor) with temozolomide—reduce viability (IC50: 5–10 µM) and enhance radiation sensitivity in STAT3-high models (Lo et al. ). A 32-gene STAT3 signature stratifies patients for targeted therapy, with mesenchymal subtypes showing heightened dependency on STAT3-NF-κB crosstalk. Emerging strategies focus on disrupting STAT3-DNA binding with decoy oligonucleotides or targeting upstream JAK kinases, though blood-brain barrier penetration remains a challenge. Longitudinal analysis of STAT3 activity in cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) may refine patient selection for adaptive immunotherapy regimens (Tan et al. ; Verhoeven et al. ).

## Complementary Immunohistochemical Biomarkers

## Ki-67
Ki-67 is a nuclear protein expressed in all active phases of the cell cycle (G1, S, G2, M) but absent in quiescent (G0) cells (do Nascimento Santos et al. ). Its precise molecular role is not fully understood, but Ki-67 is thought to organize chromatin and ribosomal RNA synthesis during mitosis. In practice, Ki-67 immunohistochemistry (often using the MIB-1 antibody) provides a labelling index (LI), i.e. the percentage of tumor cells staining positive, serving as a direct measure of proliferative fraction (Bullwinkel et al. ). A high Ki-67 LI indicates a high growth fraction; meta-analyses show that Ki-67 correlates with tumor cell proliferation and aggressiveness across cancers (Ubezio and Cameron ). For example, higher Ki-67 in gliomas independently predicts shorter progression-free and overall survival (Li et al. ). Thus, Ki-67 reflects the cell-division machinery activity and is a broadly used proliferation marker in pathology.
Ki-67 is universally expressed in proliferating tumor cells, so it appears in virtually all CNS neoplasms, with levels generally rising in higher-grade lesions. Diffuse astrocytomas (WHO grade II) typically show low Ki-67 indices (often < 4%), whereas anaplastic astrocytomas (grade III) and GBMs (grade IV) show progressively higher indices (WHO reports ~ 5–10% for anaplastic astrocytomas and ~ 15–20% for GBM) (Soliman and Yussif ). In practice, LGGs often have Ki-67 LIs in the single digits, while GBMs frequently show double-digit or higher Lis (Deacu et al. ).
Clinically, Ki-67 is used as a diagnostic and prognostic marker in CNS tumors. In diagnostics, Ki-67 index assists grading (e.g., distinguishing diffuse astrocytoma from anaplastic astrocytoma) and can help separate meningioma grades or assess recurrence risk. High Ki-67 indicates a more aggressive tumor phenotype (Shivaprasad et al. ). Numerous glioma studies show that high Ki-67 LIs are associated with poorer outcomes. For example, one glioma cohort found Ki–67–positive tumors had significantly shorter survival (median OS) and were independent predictors of death (HR ≈ 2–3) (Dwianingsih et al. ). Ki-67 is not a predictive marker for specific therapies (no drug targets Ki-67 directly), but its prognostic value influences treatment intensity (e.g., recommending adjuvant therapy for high-LI tumors). In summary, Ki-67’s biological function as a proliferation marker makes it a widely used index in grading and prognostication of CNS neoplasms (Sahebjam et al. ) (Table  ).

## Glial Fibrillary Acidic Protein (GFAP)
Glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP) is a type III intermediate filament protein predominantly expressed in the cytoskeleton of astrocytes. It plays a key structural role and is notably upregulated in the context of reactive gliosis (Leipp et al. ). It provides structural support and is upregulated in reactive gliosis. GFAP undergoes post-translational modifications (e.g. phosphorylation) and polymerizes to form astrocytic filaments. Several isoforms of GFAP exist, including GFAPα and GFAPδ, which may influence filament dynamics and stability. GFAPα is the dominant isoform in mature astrocytes, while GFAPδ is enriched in regions containing neural stem or progenitor cells (Yang and Wang ). Notably, an increased GFAPδ/GFAPα ratio has been associated with greater malignancy and invasiveness in astrocytomas (Radu et al. ) (Table  ).
GFAP is strongly expressed in astrocytic tumors and serves as a highly specific marker of astrocyte lineage. It is consistently present in nearly all pilocytic astrocytomas, diffuse astrocytomas, and GBMs. GFAP is also seen in other glial neoplasms such as ependymomas and PXA (Ono et al. ). By contrast, pure neuronal tumors (e.g. oligodendroglioma, central neurocytoma) usually lack GFAP. In metastases to the brain, GFAP may be absent (helping distinguish metastasis from primary astrocytoma).
GFAP immunohistochemistry is routinely employed to confirm astrocytic differentiation. A positive GFAP stain supports a diagnosis of astrocytoma or GBM, while helping to rule out entities such as metastatic carcinoma or lymphoma (van Bodegraven et al. ). Although less commonly assessed in biofluids, GFAP positivity in cerebrospinal fluid or serum may reflect glial injury or the presence of a glial tumor. Elevated blood GFAP is an FDA-approved biomarker for traumatic brain injury and is currently under investigation for its potential role in detecting CNS metastases at early stages (Kulbe and Geddes ).
Clinically, GFAP expression correlates with the degree of tumor differentiation. Higher expression is generally indicative of more differentiated, and therefore less aggressive, tumor phenotypes. In GBM, uniform GFAP staining has been significantly more frequent among long-term survivors (94%) compared to short-term survivors (~ 62%), suggesting that GFAP expression may be a favorable prognostic factor (Sommerlath et al. ). Conversely, loss of GFAP (common in high-grade, dedifferentiated gliomas) is linked to poorer outcome. Emerging research is evaluating circulating GFAP isoforms and fragments as non-invasive biomarkers (Wilhelmsson et al. ). Serum GFAP levels may help differentiate high-grade gliomas from lower-grade tumors or metastases, while isoform-specific analysis, particularly of GFAPδ, may refine prognostic stratification. GFAP itself is not targetable, but its regulation intersects pathways of tumor invasion (Brommeland et al. ).

## OLIG2 (Oligodendrocyte Lineage Transcription Factor 2)
OLIG2 is a basic helix–loop–helix transcription factor critical for the development of oligodendrocytes and motor neurons (Chen et al. ). In the adult central nervous system, OLIG2 is expressed in oligodendrocyte precursor cells and to some extent in astrocytes. It promotes the proliferation of glial progenitor cells and regulates transcriptional programs associated with cell cycle control and differentiation. In gliomas, OLIG2 maintains a stem-like program and confers resistance to therapy (Myers et al. ).
OLIG2 is broadly expressed in diffuse gliomas. Nearly all oligodendrogliomas demonstrate strong nuclear staining, with prevalence reported as high as 93% (Ligon et al. ).
Astrocytomas and GBMs also commonly express OLIG2, though often with reduced intensity or in a patchier distribution. Pediatric diffuse gliomas (including DIPG) typically exhibit OLIG2 positivity (Liao et al. ). Non-glial tumors, such as embryonal neoplasms, generally lack OLIG2 expression, making it a useful lineage marker (Otero et al. ).
In clinical practice, OLIG2 immunostaining is widely used to identify glial tumors and to differentiate them from non-glial neoplasms such as metastatic carcinoma, which lacks OLIG2 expression (Alturkustani et al. ). While OLIG2 is expressed in both astrocytic and oligodendroglial tumors, limiting its utility in distinguishing between these two, it serves to confirm glial origin and is valuable when differentiating from neuronal tumors like central neurocytoma, which is OLIG2-negative (Ligon et al. ).
The prognostic relevance of OLIG2 expression appears to depend on tumor subtype. In diffuse cerebellar GBM, for instance, high OLIG2 expression has been correlated with improved survival outcomes (Zhou et al. ). More generally, OLIG2 has been associated with glial “stemness” and proliferative capacity, and its expression may identify tumors with dependency on oligodendroglial developmental programs, potentially predicting responsiveness to certain therapeutic regimens (Ilkanizadeh et al. ).
OLIG2 is also being explored as a therapeutic target. Preclinical studies have demonstrated that OLIG2 silencing can impair glioma growth, underscoring its functional importance in tumor maintenance (Lu et al. ; Kosty et al. ). Although not yet integrated into clinical decision-making algorithms, OLIG2 is part of the molecular classification framework for gliomas. While classification is guided primarily by IDH mutation and 1p/19q co-deletion status, OLIG2 immunopositivity supports the diagnosis of glial lineage and remains an essential tool in neuropathological diagnostics (Ocampo-Navia et al. ) (Table  ).

## Vimentin
Vimentin is an intermediate filament protein normally expressed in mesenchymal cells and immature glia. It provides structural integrity and supports cell adhesion and migration. In neoplasia, vimentin is upregulated during epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT) and invasion (Sjöqvist et al. ). In CNS tumors, vimentin expression often reflects the lineage or phenotype of the tumor cells. For example, most astrocytic tumors (astrocytomas, GBMs) express vimentin, since vimentin is a normal component of developing astrocytes. Similarly, ependymomas frequently express vimentin (Yung et al. ). By contrast, oligodendrogliomas and embryonal tumors typically do not express vimentin. Thus, vimentin is used in immunohistochemistry to help characterize glial versus neuronal/primitive phenotypes (Chen et al. ) (Table  ).
Studies show that astrocytomas of all grades stain strongly for vimentin (often diffusely), whereas medulloblastomas and oligodendrogliomas are generally vimentin-negative. In GBM, vimentin is markedly upregulated compared to normal brain or LGGs (Felix et al. ). Indeed, one recent study reported that GBM tissues had significantly higher vimentin mRNA levels than lower-grade gliomas or non-neoplastic brain. Conversely, meningiomas often show focal vimentin positivity (reflecting meningeal fibroblastic components) but lower GFAP (Zottel et al. ). Brain metastases exhibit variable vimentin: many sarcomatous or melanoma metastases are vimentin-positive, whereas carcinoma metastases (breast, colon) are usually vimentin-negative. Overall, vimentin is a sensitive marker for astrocytic lineage but lacks specificity (it is also expressed in reactive astrocytes and mesenchymal tumors) (Fischer et al. ).
Clinically, vimentin is chiefly a diagnostic adjunct, not a stand-alone biomarker. In practice, a panel including GFAP and other markers is used to confirm glial differentiation (Paetau ). Vimentin can help identify a glial (especially astrocytic) tumor when GFAP is weak or negative. It also highlights gliosarcoma components (the sarcomatous elements are vimentin-positive) (Reifenberger et al. ). As a prognostic marker, vimentin expression correlates with the mesenchymal phenotype in GBM, a subtype associated with worse outcomes, but it is not used quantitatively for prognosis (Zhang et al. ). Importantly, vimentin is being explored as a therapeutic target in glioma: for instance, an anti-vimentin nanobody (Nb79) significantly inhibited GBM cell invasion in vitro and in vivo, suggesting that targeting vimentin-mediated motility could limit glioma spread (Zhao et al. ). However, this is experimental; no anti-vimentin therapy is approved. In summary, vimentin’s role as a cytoskeletal protein makes it a broad marker of astrocytic and mesenchymal differentiation in CNS tumors (Zhao et al. ).

## Circulating Biomarkers
The management of gliomas traditionally relies on neuroimaging and invasive tissue biopsies, both of which have significant limitations. Imaging techniques cannot always differentiate between true tumor progression and treatment-related changes (pseudoprogression), while tissue biopsies are invasive, carry risks, and cannot be routinely performed to monitor disease evolution (Müller Bark et al. ). These limitations have driven interest in liquid biopsies, which involve analyzing tumor-derived components in bodily fluids such as blood or cerebrospinal fluid (CSF). Liquid biopsies present some important advantages, such as being non-invasive or minimally invasive, while potentially facilitating serial monitoring and real-time assessment of tumor dynamics or heterogeneity and providing complementary molecular information to imaging (Soffietti et al. ). In this section, we will discuss the role of circulating tumor cells (CTCs), cell-free nucleic acids, and extracellular vesicles (EVs), which are under study for diagnosis and monitoring.

## Circulating Tumor Cells
CTCs are tumor cells that exist in the circulatory system and have three typical characteristics: (1) They originate from the primary tumor or metastases, (2) appear in blood vessels, and (3) participate in blood circulation (Zhang et al. ). Although gliomas rarely metastasize outside the central nervous system, CTCs have been detected in the peripheral blood of glioma patients (Gao et al. ; Zhang et al. ), challenging the traditional view that the blood-brain barrier (BBB) prevents glioma cells from entering circulation. Several approaches have been developed for isolating glioma CTCs: Reported detection rates vary widely (20–70%) depending on the methodology (Soffietti et al. ). For example, density-gradient isolation found CTCs in ≈ 20% of GBM patients, whereas more sensitive microfluidic or enrichment methods found CTCs in 40–77% of cases. A novel high-throughput microfluidic device can deplete hematopoietic cells through high-flow channels and force-amplifying magnetic lenses, enabling the analysis of large blood volumes and yielding high CTC counts (Mishra et al. ). Despite many efforts yet to be made, these types of techniques can aid in the establishment of CTCs as relevant biomarkers in gliomas.
The biological landscape of circulating tumor cells (CTCs) in gliomas reveals profound insights into tumor behavior, challenging traditional views of blood-brain barrier impermeability. Glioma CTCs exhibit stem cell-like properties, marked by elevated expression of SOX2, OCT4, and NANOG, which confer resistance to radiotherapy and chemotherapy while enabling “self-seeding” back to primary tumors, driving local recurrence (Liu et al. ). This stemness is further amplified by Wnt pathway activation, as shown through single-cell RNA sequencing, which promotes chemoresistance and survival in circulation. Epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition (EMT) allows these cells to detach from tumors, evade immune detection, and survive shear stress in blood vessels, with mesenchymal CTCs showing enhanced aggressiveness and resistance to EpCAM-based detection methods (Chen et al. ; Zhang et al. ; Deng et al. ). Molecularly, CTCs mirror primary tumor genetics—including IDH1 mutations, 1p/19q co-deletions, and MGMT promoter methylation—but also display unique features like ALK and ERBB4 variants, suggesting evolutionary divergence during dissemination (Krol et al. ; Qi et al. ). Paradoxically, despite systemic circulation, extracranial metastases remain rare in gliomas, likely due to the brain’s unique microenvironment rich in neural growth factors and immune privilege, which favors intracranial reseeding over distant colonization (Gao et al. ; Qi et al. ).
Translational advances in CTC detection technologies are reshaping glioma management. The CTC-iChip platform achieves 42% detection rates in GBM by combining immunomagnetic depletion with glioma-specific antibodies, while SE-iFISH targeting chromosome 8 polyploidy reaches 77% sensitivity across glioma subtypes (Gao et al. ; Li et al. ). These platforms enable dynamic monitoring of treatment response, with CTC levels outperforming MRI in distinguishing tumor recurrence from pseudoprogression—a critical diagnostic challenge where imaging often fails (Gao et al. ; Qi et al. ). Postoperative surges in CTC counts, observed in 30% of patients, correlate with shorter survival, highlighting surgical dissemination risks and the need for timed blood sampling (Gao et al. ). Prognostically, circulating tumor microemboli (CTMs)—clusters of 2–23 cells—serve as independent predictors of poor outcomes, validated by Cox models showing 85% accuracy in survival prediction (Qi et al. ). Emerging methods like rVAR2-based capture, which targets cancer-specific glycosaminoglycans, and lateral filter arrays with 90% efficiency, circumvent limitations of epithelial markers, offering glioma-specific detection (Bang-Christensen et al. ; Edd et al. ).
Future directions demand harmonization of biological insights with technological innovation. Standardizing detection protocols across platforms like CellSearch® and MiSelect R II could enable CTC thresholds for clinical decision-making, while microfluidic devices processing 30 mL/hour blood volumes may soon facilitate real-time monitoring (Edd et al. ; Barber et al. ; Mishra et al. ). Prospective trials validating CTC-driven algorithms for early recurrence detection (AUC 0.91 in preliminary studies) and targeting self-seeding mechanisms with Wnt inhibitors represent transformative opportunities (Liu et al. ). As these advancements converge, CTC-based liquid biopsies promise to complement imaging and histopathology, ushering in an era of precision neuro-oncology where dynamic tumor evolution guides therapeutic strategies in real time.

## Circulating Non-coding RNAs
Non-coding RNAs, including microRNAs (miRNAs), long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs), and circular RNAs (circRNAs), have emerged as promising liquid biopsy biomarkers for gliomas. These molecules can be detected in biofluids either freely circulating or packaged within extracellular vesicles.

## MicroRNAs
miRNAs are small non-coding RNAs that regulate gene expression post-transcriptionally. Importantly, the relevance of miRNAs in cancer has been demonstrated widely (Ortega et al. ; Smolarz et al. ; Pekarek et al. ) Circulating miRNAs in gliomas function as master regulators of tumorigenesis, influencing proliferation, invasion, and therapy resistance. Oncogenic miRNAs such as miR-21 and miR-155 are consistently upregulated in GBM, where they suppress apoptosis by targeting pro-death genes like Bax and PTEN, while concurrently activating NF-κB and STAT3 pathways to promote treatment resistance (Ma et al. ; Mahinfar et al. ). Conversely, tumor-suppressive miRNAs like miR-181a/b and miR-124-3p are downregulated, leading to derepressing of oncogenes such as MET and EZH2, which drive AKT signaling and DNA repair mechanisms (Wu et al. ; Sadeghipour et al. ). Exosomal miRNAs derived from glioma stem-like cells (GSCs) play a pivotal role in shaping the tumor microenvironment. For instance, GSC-derived miR-155-5p suppresses ACOT12 in recipient astrocytes, inducing mesenchymal transition and fostering an immunosuppressive niche (Aili et al. ; Bao et al. ). Similarly, hypoxic glioma cells release EVs enriched in miR-1246, which reprograms macrophages toward an M2 phenotype by inhibiting DUSP3 and TERF2IP, thereby promoting angiogenesis and immune evasion (Peng et al. ; Zhao et al. ).
The stemness of glioma cells is tightly regulated by circulating miRNAs. miR-10b, overexpressed in GBM CSF, enhances self-renewal of GSCs by targeting HOXD10 and p53, while miR-128-3p downregulation in plasma correlates with SOX2 and OCT4 overexpression, driving therapy-resistant phenotypes (Suvarnapathaki et al. ; Dwianingsih et al. ). Single-cell sequencing studies reveal that mesenchymal subtype gliomas preferentially release miR-21 and miR-221/222 via exosomes, which activate EMT programs in neighboring cells by suppressing RECK and TIMP3, facilitating invasion (Rooj et al. ). These miRNAs also cross the BBB, as demonstrated by elevated serum levels of miR-182-5p and miR-340-5p in patients with leptomeningeal spread, where they promote cytoskeletal remodeling through RND3 and RAB11A targeting (Pushkin et al. ; Dwianingsih et al. ).
The diagnostic potential of circulating miRNAs is underscored by meta-analyses showing pooled sensitivities of 83–88% and specificities of 85–96% for distinguishing gliomas from controls, outperforming conventional imaging in detecting early recurrence (Wang et al. ; He et al. ). CSF-based miRNA panels, particularly miR-21 and miR-222, achieve AUC values of 0.96 for differentiating pseudoprogression from true progression, addressing a critical clinical challenge (Beylerli et al. ; Suvarnapathaki et al. ). Blood tests leveraging miR-214 and miR-196a stratify patients by IDH mutation status and MGMT promoter methylation with 85–91% accuracy, enabling non-invasive molecular subtyping. For LGG, a plasma signature of miR-518b, miR-1271-3p, and miR-598-3p distinguishes WHO grade II from IV tumors with AUCs exceeding 0.90, facilitating risk-adapted monitoring (Dwianingsih et al. ).
Therapeutically, miRNA modulation strategies are advancing toward clinical application. Nanoparticle-delivered miR-138 and miR-218 restore TMZ sensitivity in resistant gliomas by targeting MGMT and P-glycoprotein, respectively, while antagomirs against miR-21 and miR-10b enhance radiation efficacy in preclinical models (Ma et al. ; Sadeghipour et al. ). EV-based delivery systems show promise, with engineered exosomes carrying miR-302c reducing tumor volume by 70% in murine gliomas through CDK6 and BMI1 inhibition. Combinatorial approaches, such as miR-34a mimics with PD-1 inhibitors, reverse immunosuppression by downregulating PD-L1 on GSCs and repolarizing macrophages toward an M1 phenotype (Aili et al. ; Zhao et al. ).

## Long Non-coding RNA and Circular RNA
lncRNAs are transcripts longer than 200 nucleotides that, despite not coding for proteins, play crucial roles in gene regulation. In GBM, the lncRNAs LINC00565 and LINC00641 are significantly upregulated compared to controls, and their elevated levels are associated with worse clinical performance, larger tumor size (≥ 5 cm), and reduced PFS and OS, highlighting their potential as diagnostic and prognostic biomarkers (Amer et al. ). Furthermore, their detection in blood samples supports their application in liquid biopsies, providing a minimally invasive tool for GBM patient stratification (Amer et al. ). HOXA10-AS, an antisense transcript to the HOXA10 gene, is overexpressed in high-grade gliomas and drives proliferation by stabilizing HOXA10 mRNA—a transcriptional activator linked to poor prognosis (Dong et al. ; Pokorná et al. ; Chen et al. ). Knockdown of HOXA10-AS induces glioma cell apoptosis via caspase-3 activation and Bcl-2 suppression, while its exosomal transfer to astrocytes activates pro-tumorigenic pathways such as TGF-β signaling, fostering a permissive TME (Dong et al. ; Bian et al. ; Pokorná et al. ). Beyond HOXA10-AS, lncRNAs like HOTAIR and NEAT1 modulate epigenetic states by recruiting Polycomb repressive complexes (PRC2) or acting as competing endogenous RNAs (ceRNAs) for oncogenic miRNAs. For example, HOTAIR silences tumor suppressors by binding EZH2, whereas NEAT1 sponges miR-139-5p to enhance Wnt pathway activity (Wang and He ; Wu et al. ; Eraky et al. ). Liquid biopsy studies detect elevated levels of HOTAIR and MALAT1 in glioma patient serum, with expression levels correlating with IDH mutation status and MGMT promoter methylation—key determinants of therapeutic response (Soffietti et al. ; Eraky et al. ; Pokorná et al. ).
circRNAs are a class of non-coding RNAs characterized by a stable cyclic structure that confers resistance to degradation, making them promising candidates as biomarkers. In gliomas, circRNAs such as circZNF652 and circ-0001445 promote tumor invasion and angiogenesis by sponging miRNAs like miR-127-5p and miR-4686, thereby upregulating pro-metastatic targets such as SNX5 and downregulating tumor suppressors (Marangon and Lecca ; Tang et al. ; Miao and Zhang ). Exosomal circRNAs, packaged within extracellular vesicles (EVs), cross the blood-brain barrier (BBB) and mediate intercellular communication between glioma cells and the tumor microenvironment (TME). For instance, glioma stem cell-derived exosomes enriched in miR-155-5p suppress ACOT12 in recipient cells, driving mesenchymal transition and therapy resistance (Marangon and Lecca ; Miao and Zhang ).Clinically, circRNA profiles in serum or cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) demonstrate diagnostic sensitivity exceeding 80% for distinguishing GBM from lower-grade gliomas, with dynamic changes post-radiotherapy correlating with treatment response (Marangon and Lecca ). The translational potential of circRNAs extends to real-time monitoring of recurrence, as elevated levels of circSMARCA5 and circHIPK3 in plasma predict radiographic progression months before MRI detection (Tang et al. ; Miao and Zhang ).

## Circulating Tumor DNA (ctDNA)
CtDNA are circulating free DNA (cfDNA) fragments derived from tumor cells. They offer a minimally invasive biomarker for glioma, yet its detection in plasma is hampered by the BBB, which restricts tumor DNA release and yields a plasma tumor fraction of only 0.1–5% of total cfDNA (Carpenter and Bagley ; Jones et al. ). As a result, plasma ctDNA assays often achieve sensitivities below 50% (Jones et al. ), whereas CSF analysis attains superior diagnostic performance (AUC = 0.947 vs. 0.741 in plasma; difference not statistically significant) (Lu et al. ).
To overcome these limitations, highly sensitive techniques have been developed: digital droplet PCR (ddPCR) for hotspot mutations (e.g., IDH1, TERTp-C228T, EGFRvIII) in plasma (Jones et al. ); targeted next-generation sequencing (NGS) panels, which outperform broader genomic approaches (Carpenter and Bagley ); and cfDNA methylation profiling, capable of distinguishing IDH-mutant glioma, low-grade glial-neuronal tumors, and IDH wild-type gliomas (Carpenter and Bagley ).
Numerous studies have evidenced the relevance of ctDNA in the auxiliary diagnosis of gliomas, with blood-based approaches showing moderate sensitivity for mutation detection (e.g., TERT promoter mutations at 62.5%) but greater diagnostic accuracy when leveraging cfDNA methylation profiling, such as the GeLB score or cfMeDIP-seq, which can distinguish gliomas from other conditions with high sensitivity and specificity (up to AUC = 0.99) (Lu et al. ). Beyond detection, plasma ctDNA levels correlate with tumor grade and burden. Bagley et al. ( ) showed that GBM patients had higher plasma cfDNA concentration than age-matched healthy controls (mean 13.4 vs. 6.7 ng/mL). Besides, plasma ctDNA concentration strongly correlated with tumor burden on post-radiation MRI (ρ = 0.77, p = 0.003) and often increased before or during radiographic progression. Elevated preoperative cfDNA levels (> 13.4 ng/mL) and detection of somatic mutations in plasma were associated with shorter PFS and OS, though some associations did not reach statistical significance (Bagley et al. ).
Several ongoing clinical trials are evaluating the role of ctDNA in the auxiliary diagnosis and prognosis of gliomas, including its use in CSF and plasma to detect tumor-specific mutations, monitor disease progression, and guide treatment decisions (e.g., NCT05934630, NCT04539431, NCT05964153). These studies aim to validate new molecular assays, correlate ctDNA findings with imaging and clinical outcomes, and assess ctDNA as a prognostic biomarker in GBM and other glioma subtypes. However, some important challenges still need to be overcome due to low ctDNA levels and technical limitations. Future research should focus on improving detection sensitivity (particularly in plasma and CSF) through technical advances and combined sampling strategies, while large-scale prospective studies are needed to validate current findings (Lu et al. ).

## Extracellular Vesicles (EVs) and Exosomes
Extracellular vesicles, including exosomes, are small membrane-bound particles released by cells into the extracellular environment. They carry a diverse array of bioactive molecules, including proteins, lipids, and nucleic acids (Ortega et al. ). Glioma-derived exosomes (GDEs) significantly influence glioma development by transporting bioactive molecules (like the aforementioned ncRNAs) that shape the tumor microenvironment, promote angiogenesis, enhance tumor proliferation and invasion, induce treatment resistance, and modulate immune responses (Yang et al. ). However, other studies suggest that spherosomes (recently discovered ER-Golgi-derived EVs that aggregate under the cell membrane) may be more abundant and important in the context of LGG than GDEs (Baselga et al. ), although further studies are warranted.
However, GDEs have emerged as promising non-invasive biomarkers due to their ability to reflect tumor status through their cargo, including miRNAs, circRNAs, lncRNAs, and proteins. Exosomal miRNAs like miR-21 and miR-181, as well as circRNAs such as circNEIL3 and circARID1A, show strong potential in glioma diagnosis and prognosis. Similarly, exosomal lncRNAs and proteins, including EGFRvIII, contribute valuable insights into tumor behavior and treatment resistance (Yang et al. ). With high stability, tissue specificity, and ease of sampling, exosomes support early diagnosis, therapeutic monitoring, and prognosis in GBM patients. Future research should focus on validating these biomarkers in larger cohorts and optimizing liquid biopsy protocols to improve diagnostic precision and clinical utility.
Therapeutic engineering of exosomes is revolutionizing glioma treatment. PTX-loaded GDEs demonstrate 90% capture efficiency in microfluidic devices, delivering chemotherapeutics across the BBB with sustained release profiles (Rana et al. ). Selumetinib-loaded exosomes induce apoptosis in GBM cells by downregulating Bcl2 and activating PARP, reducing tumor volume by 70% in preclinical models (Mastantuono et al. ). Immunotherapy strategies leverage exosomes conjugated with α-galactosylceramide to reprogram TAMs, enhancing dendritic cell activation and cytotoxic T cell infiltration (Luo et al. ). Emerging strategies combine exosomal analysis with ctDNA and protein biomarkers (e.g., GFAP) to achieve > 95% diagnostic accuracy, potentially replacing invasive biopsies. Clinical trials (NCT04945680) are evaluating exosome-based vaccines encoding tumor-specific circRNAs to stimulate anti-glioma immunity (Wu et al. ). Some investigators are engineering exosomes to deliver siRNAs or small molecules to brain tumors (Khatami et al. ). Moreover, blocking harmful EV-mediated signaling (e.g., EV PD-L1) is under consideration. As these innovations mature, exosomes promise to transform glioma management, bridging molecular insights with personalized therapies to improve outcomes in this devastating disease (Table  ).
Despite progress, clinical translation faces important challenges. Variability in EV isolation methods (e.g., ultracentrifugation vs. microfluidic chips) affects detection sensitivity (20–88%), necessitating standardized protocols (Yang et al. ). The low abundance of tumor-specific exosomes in peripheral blood requires high-volume processing technologies, such as lateral filter arrays capable of analyzing 30 mL/hour. Future research must address exosomal heterogeneity across glioma subtypes and validate biomarkers in multi-institutional cohorts.

 

## Conclusion and Future Paths
The landscape of glioma diagnostics and management has undergone a profound transformation with the integration of molecular and circulating biomarkers into clinical practice. Moving beyond traditional histopathological assessment, a wide array of biomarkers now offer enhanced precision in tumor classification, prognosis, and therapeutic stratification. From canonical markers such as IDH1/2 mutations and 1p/19q codeletion to emerging alterations involving cell signaling pathways, gene fusions, epigenetic modifications, immune-related biomarkers and circulating components, the biomarker repertoire continues to expand. This evolution reflects a broader shift toward precision neuro-oncology, where molecular characterization informs individualized treatment strategies. As evidence continues to accumulate, the integration of novel biomarkers—particularly those involved in immune modulation, signal transduction, and liquid biopsy—holds promise for improving patient outcomes and overcoming therapeutic resistance. Ongoing research and validation in clinical settings will be essential to fully realize the diagnostic and prognostic potential of these biomarkers across the glioma spectrum.
