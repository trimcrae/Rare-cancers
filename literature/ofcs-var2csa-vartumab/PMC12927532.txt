## ABSTRACT
As the leading cause of maternal mortality, the pathogenesis of postpartum hemorrhage (PPH), with 60%–70% cases of uterine atony, remains unclear due to a fundamental lack of animal models for studying. Our study develops a novel placenta‐targeted nanodelivery system, termed HN@DC NPs, which is formulated with DSPC (1,2‐distearoyl‐sn‐glycero‐3‐phosphocholine), DSPE‐PEG2000 (1,2‐distearoyl‐sn‐glycero‐3‐phosphoethanolamine‐N‐[methoxy (polyethylene glycol)‐2000]), and DSPE‐PEG‐CSA (DSPE‐PEG2000‐chondroitine sulfate A). This system is designed to co‐deliver key inflammatory mediators—nucleotide‐binding oligomerization domain, leucine‐rich repeat, and pyrin domain‐containing 3 (NLRP3) agonist nigericin and high mobility group box‐1 protein (HMGB1), which are identified from pregnant women with atonic PPH to establish a pathophysiologically relevant animal model. The HN@DC NPs achieve precise enrichment in placental trophoblasts and effective diffusion into decidua with good short‐term biocompatibility. The pregnant rats model constructed by HN@DC NPs shows prolonged labor duration and weakened uterine smooth muscle contractility with the activation of inflammatory markers in the placental decidua. The new insight into a reproducible construction strategy of the atonic PPH animal model in this study promotes the mechanism research and clinical therapeutic development of atonic PPH.

This study develops a placenta‐targeted nanodelivery system co‐loading HMGB1 protein and the NLRP3 agonist nigericin to establish an animal model of atonic postpartum hemorrhage. The model accurately recapitulates clinical phenotypes, including prolonged labor and uterine contractility dysfunction, while revealing inflammatory activation in placental decidua, providing a new platform for mechanistic insights and therapeutic development.

JQ22024L232087 For fabricating HN@DC, five components were needed, including DSPC, DSPE‐PEG2000, DSPE‐PEG2000‐CSA, HMGB1 protein, and Nigericin (NLRP3 agonist). HMGB1 and Nigericin could induce an inflammatory response; DSPE‐PEG2000‐CSA has the function of targeting placental tissue (Figure  ). These amphiphilic molecules and drugs were then mixed and dispersed in water, resulting in self‐assembly into liposomal nanoparticles (NPs). HMGB1 and Nigericin were then loaded into the lipid bilayer to obtain the HN@DC NPs. To simultaneously achieve the best combination of various functions, formulations of 1,2‐distearoyl‐sn‐glycero‐3‐phosphocholine (DSPC) / 1,2‐distearoyl‐sn‐glycero‐3‐phosphoethanolamine‐N‐[methoxy (polyethylene glycol)‐2000] (DSPE‐PEG2000)/ DSPE‐PEG2000‐chondroitine sulfate A (CSA) with different lipid molar ratios of 20:10:70, 45:10:45, 70:10:20 were investigated, and named as HN@DC‐1, HN@DC‐2, and HN@DC‐3, respectively. Particle size and zeta potential were then investigated. It was found that as the DSPC ratio increases, the particle size gradually increases, and the negative potential value also increases. The particle size of HN@DC‐3 reached 440 nanometers. This stepwise increase in particle size may also be related to the reduction in hydrophobic DSPE‐PEG2000‐CSA proportion, which affected the self‐assembly stability of nanoparticles (Table ). A larger particle size is not conducive to penetrating the placental tissue. In contrast, the NPs sizes of HN@DC‐1 and HN@DC‐2 were both less than 200 nanometers and are negatively charged (≈−30 mV), which was beneficial for extending the circulation time in the body (Figure  ). The encapsulation rate and drug loading of HMGB1 and Nigericin varied among the three formulations. Formulation 2 demonstrated the highest encapsulation efficiency for both substances, with drug loadings of 0.84±0.001% for HMGB1 and 0.99±0.001% for Nigericin. Although formulation 3 had a lower HMGB1 drug loading (0.15±0.01%), it achieved the highest Nigericin drug loading (1.98±0.001%) among the three, this may be due to the increase in drug loading caused by the increase in particle size (Table ). Considering the enhancement of targeting function, the formula of HN@DC‐2 was ultimately chosen, and it will be referred to as HN@DC hereafter. The transmission electron microscopy (TEM) image also proved the successful fabrication of HN@DC, as exhibited in Figure  . It showed that the prepared NPs displayed the uniform spherical liposome morphology.
To evaluate the long‐term stability of nanoparticles, we investigated their dispersion behavior in water, PBS, serum, and culture medium. DC and HN@DC dispersed well in different media (Figure ), under the physiological simulation environment of 37°C, the dynamic monitoring of the particle size and charge revealed that the particle size and zeta potential of DC and HN@DC changed slightly (Figure ). The above results indicated that nanoparticles had good stability and could maintain structural uniformity in the physiological environment, providing a physicochemical basis for subsequent applications.

## HN@DC NPs Mediated Cytotoxicity Profiles at the Cellular Level
The cytotoxicity of HN@DC NPs to HTR‐8/SVneo cells was first investigated by the cell counting kit‐8 (CCK‐8) assay. After incubation for 24 h, the cell viabilities were more than 70% at tested concentrations of 25 and 50 µg/mL in the nanoparticles loaded with Nigericin (N@DC NPs) group (25 µg/mL: 74.53±7.90%; 50 µg/mL: 76.86±9.97%) (Figure  ) and the nanoparticles loaded with HMGB1 (H@DC NPs) group (25 µg/mL: 82.81±11.24%; 50 µg/mL: 73.39±8.42%) (Figure  ), suggesting relatively little cytotoxicity in lower concentrations. Obviously, concentration‐dependent cytotoxicity was exhibited in the N@DC NPs group (Figure  ) and the H@DC NPs group (Figure  ). The biocompatibility of different drug‐loading systems at the cellular level was systematically evaluated via Live/Dead staining experiments. At a concentration of 25–50 µg/mL, fluorescence staining of the H@DC NPs group with single‐loaded HMGB1 showed that over 82.4% of cells were viable (Figure  ). At a concentration of 25–100 µg/mL, the survival rate of the N@DC NPs group with single‐loaded Nigericin exceeded 80% (Figure  ). To ensure the relative safety of the HTR‐8‐SVneo cells, we chose 25 and 50 µg/mL for the next step of verifying the uptake ability. However, in the H@DC NPs and N@DC NPs (HN@DC NPs) group, higher cell safety was demonstrated, with cell viabilities were more than 80% at all tested concentrations in the CCK‐8 assay (83.70±5.08% to 95.87±5.73%) (Figure  ). In contrast, in the double‐loaded HN@DC NPs group at 100 µg/mL, sporadic red fluorescence points emerged, while the overall survival rate still reached 77.4±3.0% (Figure  ). These results were also consistent with CCK‐8 outcomes. Therefore, in subsequent studies, a concentration below 50 µg/mL was selected to ensure the safety and effectiveness of the particles.

## The Targeting Efficacy of HN@DC NPs
In order to test whether nanoparticles containing chondroitin sulfate could be taken up more by trophoblasts, flow cytometry and high content screening were used to monitor the Cy 5.5 fluorescence intensity in HTR‐8/SVneo cells (Figure  ). The results of flow cytometry showed that the fluorescence values of the H@DC NPs group, N@DC NPs group, and HN@DC NPs group were stronger than those of the control group at 0.5 to 16 h after injection of nanoparticles (Figure  ). Similarly, the high‐content screening results showed that HTR‐8/SVneo cells in the H@DC NPs group, N@DC NPs group, and HN@DC NPs group had statistically higher uptake of Cy 5.5‐containing nanoparticles than the control group at 4 h after injection of nanoparticles (Figure  ). The above results suggested that after loading chondroitin sulfate, trophoblasts take up more nanoparticles.

## In vivo Placenta Targeting of HN@DC NPs
To investigate the placenta‐targeting ability, Cy 5.5‐labeled nanoparticles were used to explore the in vivo biodistribution of CTRL, H@DC, N@DC, and HN@DC NPs via the in vivo imaging systems (IVIS). After being intravenously administrated with Cy 5.5‐labeled CTRL, H@DC, N@DC, and HN@DC NPs, the tissue distributions and localization of the nanoparticles were monitored. Cy 5.5 signal from the uterus region was observed as early as 30 min after injection, and a strong signal was maintained up to 36 h (Figure  ). However, non‐targeted nanoparticles (CTRL NPs) showed whole‐body imaging after injection into rats, rather than being concentrated in the uterus (Figure  ). Moreover, the signal from the CTRL, H@DC, N@DC, and HN@DC NPs could be detected in the uterus up to 36 h after injection based on ex vivo examination (Figure  ). Notably, this signal was significantly higher in placentas loaded with H@DC, N@DC, and HN@DC NPs than in those loaded with CTRL NPs. Furthermore, quantitative ex vivo fluorescence imaging revealed no significant accumulation of nanoparticles in the fetuses (Figure  ).

## Evaluation of Placenta in Pregnant Rat Model with Uterine Atony
We constructed a pregnant rat model of uterine atony by intravenous injection of nanoparticles and evaluated the placental function (Figure  ). Inflammation infiltration was observed in the decidua and uterine muscle stimulated with H@DC, N@DC, and HN@DC NPs, compared with the control group (Figure  ), while the other tissues displayed no structural changes and toxicity (Figures  and ). We determined the expression of the HMGB1‐related markers (HMGB1 and nuclear factor kappa‐B [p‐NF‐κB(p65)]) and Nigericin‐related markers (NLRP3, Caspase‐1, and gasdermin D (GSDMD)) in the placentas of the induced and non‐induced labor groups. The results depicted that after intravenously injected H@DC and HN@DC NPs, HMGB1, and NF‐κB showed an increase in the placenta (Figures  and ). Similarly, after intravenously injected N@DC and HN@DC NPs, NLRP3, Caspase‐1, and GSDMD showed a significant increase in the placenta (Figures  and ). However, the expression of these indicators in the heart, liver, spleen, lungs, and kidneys of each group showed no significant differences compared to the control group (Figure  ). We further conducted transcriptome analysis on each group's placenta. The results showed that in the induced (Figure  ) and non‐induced labor groups (Figure ), the significantly differentially expressed genes in the placenta of the H@DC, N@DC, and HN@DC NPs stimulation group were enriched in immune‐inflammatory pathways and coagulation‐related pathways compared with the control group. Significantly, the differentially expressed genes in the H@DC NPs group were enriched in pathways such as NF‐κB, leukocyte migration, and cytokine function, while the differentially expressed genes in the N@DC NPs and HN@DC NPs groups were found to be enriched in pathways such as coagulation and fibrinolysis that affect bleeding.

## Evaluation of Uterine Smooth Muscle Function in a Pregnant Rat Model with Uterine Atony
The uterine smooth muscle contraction function of the pregnant rat model was also evaluated. In the induced and non‐induced labor groups, the delivery time were mildly prolonged (induced labor ‐H@DC NPs: 42.00±4.88 vs CTRL: 38.91±2.91, P = 0.108; induced labor ‐N@DC NPs: 39.25±4.34 vs CTRL: 38.91±2.91, P = 0.861; induced labor ‐HN@DC NPs: 39.94±4.54 vs CTRL: 38.91±2.91, P = 0.584; non‐induced labor ‐H@DC NPs: 61.67±6.26 vs CTRL: 58.33±7.31, P = 0.362; non‐induced labor ‐N@DC NPs: 63.50±6.63 vs CTRL: 58.33±7.31, P = 0.183; non‐induced labor ‐HN@DC NPs: 60.06±4.90 vs CTRL: 58.33±7.31, P = 0.611) (Figures  and ) and uterine muscle contractility were significantly reduced (induced labor ‐H@DC NPs: 1.79±1.37 vs CTRL: 3.81±3.11, P = 0.025; induced labor ‐N@DC NPs: 1.98±1.14 vs CTRL: 3.81±3.11, P = 0.047; induced labor ‐HN@DC NPs: 1.24±0.43 vs CTRL: 3.81±3.11, P = 0.005; non‐induced labor ‐H@DC NPs: 2.69±1.35 vs CTRL: 5.49±2.79, P = 0.004; non‐induced labor ‐N@DC NPs: 1.49±0.46 vs CTRL: 5.49±2.79, P<0.001; non‐induced labor ‐HN@DC NPs: 1.41±0.58 vs CTRL: 5.49±2.79, P<0.001) (Figures  and ) after injected H@DC, N@DC, and HN@DC NPs. We determined the expression of uterine smooth muscle contraction associated proteins (CAPs) (oxytocin receptor [OTR], transgelin [TAGLN], matrix metallopeptidase 2 [MMP2], connexin 43 [GJA1], and α‐smooth muscle actin [α‐SMA]), indicating lower expression of OTR, TAGLN, MMP2, GJA1, and α‐SMA in the uterine smooth muscle after stimulated with H@DC, N@DC, and HN@DC NPs, compared with the control group (Figures  and ). We further conducted transcriptome analysis on each group's uterine muscle layers. The results showed that in the uterine smooth muscle of the induced (Figure  ) and non‐induced (Figure ) labor groups, the significantly differentially expressed genes were enriched in pathways affecting uterine smooth muscle contraction, such as the regulation of smooth muscle contraction, sodium and calcium ion signaling pathway, skeletal muscle tissue growth, and energy metabolism.

## Discussion
Maternal‐fetal interface immune homeostasis critically influences pregnancy complications, yet current models cannot directly modulate this interface. Using atonic PPH as a model, we developed a targeted delivery system to address this gap. We propose that an ideal animal model for studying PPH pathogenesis should meet two key criteria: (1) specific activation of immune‐inflammatory responses at the maternal‐fetal interface, and (2) stable and reproducible phenotypic recapitulation of the disorder. Currently, there are several methods for inducing a PPH‐like condition in animal models [ ]:①surgical approaches mechanically disrupting uterine tissue or employed lacerations to uterine arteries, renal arteries, ovarian arteries, the abdominal aorta or the myometrium to generate substantial bleeding, along with femoral artery blood withdrawal to establish postpartum hypotension models; ②pharmacological induction of bleeding, uterine contraction prevention, or preterm labor induction, which has been used to validate progesterone's contraction‐inhibiting effects, NSAID‐mediated prostaglandin suppression, and contraction‐promoting effects of prostaglandins, dexamethasone, and acetaminophen [ , , , ]; ③infection induction to increase PPH risk [ ]; and ④genetic models such as comparing prostaglandin F2α (PGF2α) effects in wild‐type versus prostaglandin F receptor (Ptgfr−/−), thromboxane prostanoid receptor (Tbxa2r−/−), and/or prostaglandin E receptor‐3 (Ptger3−/−) deficient mice. These existing models face limitations, including an inability to establish pure atonic uterine models, poor stability, low hemorrhage reproducibility, and limited mechanistic exploration capacity. Therefore, this study pioneers the application of placenta‐targeted delivery systems to construct an atonic PPH animal model to effectively address the problems mentioned above.
Our targeted drugs—HN@DC represented a novel placenta‐targeted drug delivery system, with its approximately 200 nm nanoparticle size enabling effective penetration through the capillary barrier in placental villous spaces. Through surface modification with chondroitin sulfate targeting ligands, it achieved precise enrichment in placental trophoblasts via receptor‐mediated active targeting mechanisms. These characteristics have been validated by both Cy 5.5 fluorescent uptake experiments in HTR‐8‐SVneo cells and in vivo imaging in pregnant rats. Furthermore, HN@DC NPs demonstrated effective diffusion into decidual regions (directly contacting myometrium) closely associated with trophoblasts, as evidenced by the activation of inflammatory markers in placental decidua following HN@DC NPs administration. Notably, the dynamic changes in the placental microenvironment significantly influence drug release behavior. The local weakly acidic (pH 6.5–7.0) and oxidative stress conditions in the placenta during pregnancy can trigger alterations in liposome membrane permeability, leading to membrane destabilization and subsequent release of encapsulated drugs [ , ]. The unique advantages of this delivery system include its small particle size and negative charge reduce nonspecific protein adsorption through electrostatic repulsion, thereby prolonging circulation time, and efficient uptake by placental trophoblasts via endocytic pathways, with the low pH environment in endosomes/lysosomes further promoting liposome membrane fusion to achieve precise intracellular drug release.
Furthermore, while the in vitro drug release profile under physiological and acidic conditions is a standard characterization for nanocarriers, the drug loading content of HN@DC was deliberately optimized to be exceptionally low (HMGB1: 0.84% ± 0.001; Nigericin: 0.99% ± 0.001), reflecting the high potency of the cargoes, which are biologically active at microgram levels. Consequently, the absolute mass of drugs available for release in a standard in vitro setup falls below the reliable detection limit of conventional analytical instruments (e.g., High Performance Liquid Chromatography [HPLC]), rendering accurate kinetic quantification impractical. Therefore, we turned to the most biologically relevant system to evaluate the functional release and efficacy of our platform. The potent and specific pathological phenotype of atonic postpartum hemorrhage induced by HN@DC in our animal model provides definitive evidence that both nigericin and HMGB1 were not only successfully delivered to the target tissue but were also released in a bioactive form and in a therapeutically relevant manner. We contend that this compelling in vivo efficacy serves as the ultimate validation of successful drug release and activity, thereby overcoming the limitations of in vitro methodology for evaluating such a low‐dose, high‐potency formulation.
In vitro experiments demonstrated that at concentrations ≤50 µg/mL, H@DC, N@DC, and HN@DC NPs maintained trophoblast cell viability above 70%. Animal studies showed no significant structural damage in various organs, indicating good short‐term biocompatibility. No acute toxic reactions were observed at a single dose of 1 mg/kg, and no significant increase in fetal malformation rates was observed, enabling mechanistic investigation with low toxicity.
HMGB1, also called amphoterin or HMG, serves as a mediator of inflammatory, immune, and metabolic responses [ , , ]. During infection or tissue injury, it can reach the extracellular space through active secretion or passive release, thereby mediating inflammatory and immune responses [ , ]. Currently, HMGB1 has been identified to be useful for the detection, diagnosis, risk assessment, and treatment of various immune‐inflammatory diseases [ , , ]. In pregnancy‐related disorders, HMGB1 was closely associated with preeclampsia [ ], unexplained recurrent pregnancy loss [ ], and gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) [ ]. Our previous proteomic analysis comparing vaginal blood samples from patients with atonic PPH and normal deliveries revealed that elevated HMGB1 expression was associated with atonic PPH. Similarly, as a perturbation detector for cellular stress, the NLRP3 inflammasome mediates caspase‐1 activation and the secretion of proinflammatory cytokines interleukin (IL)‐1β/IL‐18 in response to microbial infection and cellular damage [ , ]. NLRP3 is closely associated with apoptosis and a hyperinflammatory phenotype, serving as a target for drug development [ , ]. NLRP3 has also been implicated in immune‐inflammatory activation in pregnancy‐related disorders, including GDM [ ], preterm labor [ ], and preeclampsia [ ]. Our previous single‐cell transcriptomic analysis of vaginal blood samples demonstrated NLRP3 activation in atonic PPH cases, leading us to employ Nigericin, an NLRP3 inflammasome agonist, for animal model construction. In this study, in the double‐loaded HN@DC NPs group at 100 µg/mL, sporadic red fluorescence points emerged, while the overall survival rate still reached 77.4±3.0%, potentially associated with pyroptosis triggered by the synergistic effect of the drugs. As a damage‐associated molecular pattern (DAMPs) molecule, HMGB1 activated the toll‐like receptor 4 (TLR4)/ myeloid differentiation primary response protein 88 (MyD88) pathway, while Nigericin, as an NLRP3 inflammasome agonist, in its combined action might lead to excessive activation of caspase‐1, inducing pyroptosis. However, the precise upstream‐downstream relationship between HMGB1 and NLRP3 activation remains elusive, representing a key direction for our immediate future research.
For animal model construction, rats were selected based on several considerations. Rats have shorter gestation periods compared to humans, which facilitates accelerated pregnancy and birth‐related research [ ]. Rats have similar oxytocin receptor–ligand interactions compared to humans [ , ], making them suitable for studying pharmacological effects on oxytocin receptors within the myometrium [ ]. Our observations indicated that rat decidual tissue more closely resembled human placental decidua compared to mice, while offering better experimental consistency and lower drug resistance than rabbits, with lower inter‐individual variability. Furthermore, rats were more accessible and cost‐effective than larger animals, with reduced batch‐to‐batch variation. Our studies showed that placenta‐targeted delivery of HMGB1 and Nigericin in pregnant rats resulted in prolonged labor duration and weakened uterine smooth muscle contractility, supported by transcriptomic evidence of abnormal uterine contraction pathways—mirroring clinical observations in some atonic PPH cases. Further investigation revealed broad effects on contraction‐associated proteins (MMP2, TAGLN, α‐SMA, GJA1) and oxytocin receptor expression, partially explaining the poor response to oxytocin‐based therapies in refractory PPH cases.
The principal advantage of our model lies in its broad applicability. We anticipate that it will serve as a valuable platform for investigating a range of pregnancy complications, including preterm birth and preeclampsia. Moreover, the model exhibits robust reproducibility across experimental replicates, further enhancing its utility for translational research. The placenta‐targeted nanoparticles can deliver a diverse array of mechanistic molecules associated with atonic PPH and other pregnancy‐related complications, enabling in vivo mechanistic investigations. Unlike models directly inhibiting specific uterine contraction receptors or proteins, HN@DC NPs simulated the inflammatory state at the maternal‐fetal interface during delivery. While reliably establishing the atonic phenotype, it enabled downstream investigation of multiple contraction mechanisms, including contraction‐associated protein expression changes, calcium signaling alterations, and energy metabolism modifications. Additionally, we established a stable induced labor model in which the induced labor group received 0.5 mL of oxytocin and chloroprostol sodium via intramuscular injection at E20, which advanced delivery by approximately one day with relatively consistent phenotype, showing promise for labor induction research.
However, limitations remain in this study. While this study successfully established a new animal model of atonic PPH using a placenta‐targeted nanoplatform, the experimental design does not include a control group with non‐targeted nanoparticles or free drugs. While this allows for a precise characterization of the contribution of active targeting, the primary goal of this work is to construct and validate pathophysiologically relevant models. Systematic comparisons of targeted versus non‐targeted delivery and the efficacy of free drugs are critical and reasonable next steps. Future work will directly address this issue, evaluating our platform's targeting efficiency, biodistribution, and therapeutic window against these controls to comprehensively assess its translational potential. Besides, rats approximate some but not all anatomical and physiological features of human pregnancy or postpartum conditions. For example, the placental villus structure of rats still differs from that of humans; the safety of repeated administrations requires further optimization of dosing regimens through pharmacokinetic modeling to avoid cumulative toxicity. Based on current evidence, HN@DC NPs demonstrated acceptable short‐term safety at single therapeutic doses, but their potential effects on placental endocrine function and transgenerational consequences require further validation. What's more, most drug‐loaded nanoparticles undergo significant intake and rapid clearance by non‐target organs, such as the liver and spleen, during systemic circulation, with only a fraction reaching target sites. Liposome formulations also tend to aggregate, and minor parameter variations during scale‐up can cause batch quality inconsistencies, requiring more precise manufacturing techniques. In addition, to improve drug uptake and lysosomal escape efficiency, future development could focus on smart‐responsive liposome systems incorporating placenta‐specific protease, such as matrix metalloproteinase, sensitivity or pH‐responsive membrane structures, as well as core–shell liposomes with sonosensitive phospholipid outer layers enabling spatiotemporally precise ultrasound‐triggered drug release at placental sites.

4

## Conclusions
In conclusion, this study established the first stable pregnant rat model of atonic PPH using placenta‐targeted drug delivery, addressing a global gap in atonic PPH animal models. Future applications could incorporate diverse atonic PPH‐related molecules and develop versatile targeted delivery systems for mechanistic exploration and clinical translation research, providing effective solutions for this important public‐health challenge associated with high morbidity and mortality. This model exhibits remarkable extensibility and holds substantial promise for elucidating the mechanistic basis of other pregnancy complications driven by immune dysregulation at the maternal‐fetal interface. Nevertheless, further validation studies are necessary to confirm its broader applicability.

## Methods
5.1

## Collection of Human Vaginal Blood Samples
Inclusion criteria: over 18 years old, naturally conceived, singleton pregnancy, full‐term vaginal delivery, regular prenatal check‐ups in a fixed hospital during the first trimester, postpartum hemorrhage volume greater than 500 mL, and the sole cause of hemorrhage being uterine atony.
Exclusion criteria: Multiple pregnancies, cesarean section delivery, pregnancy complicated with autoimmune diseases, various pregnancy comorbidities and complications, refusal to be enrolled Postpartum hemorrhage caused by other reasons such as placental factors (including retained placenta, placental adhesion or implantation, and partial retention of placental membranes, etc.), laceration of the soft birth canal (including injuries to the perineum, vagina, cervix and lower uterine segment, etc.) or coagulation dysfunction (including primary or secondary coagulation dysfunction).
Biological sample collection: Immediately after the placenta was delivered at the end of the third stage of labor, 5 mL of vaginal blood was collected and placed in EDTA anticoagulant tubes. Mononuclear cells (including lymphocytes and monocytes, without granulocytes) in vaginal blood were isolated by the Ficoll method.
This study has been reviewed and approved by the Medical Research Ethics Committee of Peking University Third Hospital (2021‐013‐01). This study adheres to the STROBE specification requirements and the Declaration of Helsinki. Each subject was included in this study after obtaining full informed consent and signing the informed consent form.

## Materials
Ethanol and dimethyl sulfoxide were obtained from Macklin (China, E801077).1,2‐distearoyl‐sn‐glycero‐3‐phosphoethanolamine‐N‐[methoxy (polyethylene glycol)‐2000] (DSPE‐PEG2000)(China, R‐1028‐2K), 1,2‐distearoyl‐sn‐glycero‐3‐phosphocholine (DSPC)(China, LP‐R4‐076), and DSPE‐PEG2000‐CSA (China, R‐GS‐028) were obtained from Ruixibio. Nigericin was purchased from InvivoGen (France, tlrl‐nig). HMGB1 was purchased from Acro (China, HM1‐H5220). Deionized water was prepared using the Milli‐Q Gradient purification system.

5.3

## Synthesis of CSA Nanoparticles
HN@DC‐1, HN@DC‐2 and HN@DC‐3, DSPC/ DSPE‐PEG2000/ DSPE‐PEG2000‐CSA were prepared according to the molar ratios of 20:10:70, 45:10:45, 70:10:20, respectively. HMGB1 and Nigericin were incorporated at a drug‐to‐lipid ratio of 1:1000 and 1:100, respectively. The above formulation was dissolved in 100 µL of ethanol and dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO), then injected into 0.8 mL of water using a syringe while stirring magnetically at 800 rpm for 30 min. This process ensured complete dispersion of the organic phase, resulting in a stable and homogeneous suspension. The nanoparticles were then filtered through a 0.2 µm membrane to eliminate bacteria and stored in a refrigerator at 4°C. The H@DC and N@DC were prepared in the same way as HN@DC. For the control group, nanoparticles were prepared by injecting DSPC: DSPE‐PEG2000 at a molar ratio of 9:1 through an ethanol injection method. DSPE‐Cy 5.5 was mixed into the phospholipids at a 3% mass ratio, and then self‐assembled into fluorescently labeled nanoparticles in accordance with the same method described above.

5.4

## Characterization of the Nanoparticles
The morphology of the nanoparticles following staining was examined using a TEM (model JEM‐1200 EX, Japan) at an accelerating voltage of 200 kV. Additionally, the samples were adjusted to a specific concentration for the measurement of their size and zeta potential through Malvern dynamic light scattering (DLS) (Particle Sizing Systems, USA).
The stability of the nanoparticles was evaluated by monitoring changes in hydrodynamic diameter and zeta potential when stored at 37°C over 7 days (with measurements taken on days 1, 2, 3, 5, and 7). Additionally, the dispersibility was assessed by incubating the nanoparticles in ddH2O, PBS, FBS, and cell culture medium.

5.5

## Biocompatibility
The HTR‐8/SVneo cell line, purchased from Procella, China (CL‐0765), was cultured in dulbecco's modified eagle medium (DMEM) (Gibco, CAT: C11995500BT) containing 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 0.5% antibiotic–antimycotic. To investigate the biocompatibility of NPs at different concentrations, HTR‐8/SVneo cells were seeded into 96‐well plates at a density of 1 × 104 cells per well and cultured overnight. Subsequently, the culture medium was replaced with fresh medium containing CTRL, H@DC, N@DC, and HN@DC NPs at different concentrations (25, 50, 100, 200, and 300 µg/mL). After a further 24 h of incubation, the wells were washed three times with PBS, and medium supplemented with 10% CCK‐8 solution was added for a 2‐h incubation period. Finally, absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader to assess the viability in each well.
For a visual assessment of cell viability and death, Calcein‐AM and PI staining were conducted following 24 h of incubation with nanoparticles at different concentrations. Calcein‐AM/PI Double Staining Kit was purchased from Beyotime Biotechnology (China, C2015M). The stained cells were then examined under a fluorescence microscope.

5.6

## Flow Cytometry
HTR‐8/SVneo cells were collected into 12‐well plates at 3 × 106 cells per well for 24 h. Subsequently, the culture medium was replaced with fresh medium containing CTRL, H@DC, N@DC, and HN@DC NPs. Cells were cultured at 37°C and 5% CO2 for 0.5, 2, 4, 6, and 16 h and washed three times with phosphate buffer saline (PBS). After digestion in the 20 ng/µ L trypsin for 2 min, 1 mL DMEM medium was added, and then centrifuged to remove the supernatant. The 200ul PBS was added to 12‐well plates. A SONY ID7000 full‐spectrum flow cytometer was used to detect fluorescence.

5.7

## High Content Screening
HTR‐8/SVneo cells were collected into confocal culture dishes and cultured for 24 h. After discarding the culture medium, HTR‐8‐Svneo cells were washed three times with PBS, and culture medium containing CTRL, H@DC, N@DC, and HN@DC NPs was added for 4 h. After the culture medium was removed, and cells were washed three times with PBS, and Hoechst 33342 was added for staining for 30 min, and then washed three times. The High Content Analysis System (PerkinElmer, USA) was used to detect fluorescence, and then fluorescence values were analyzed through Harmony.

5.8

## Animal Model
SD rats (9 weeks of age) were obtained from Beijing Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China) and caged in a pathogen‐free animal room with a 14 h light/10 h dark cycle. Gestational ages were determined by monitoring the formation of vaginal plugs (E0.5 = vaginal plug day). SD rats, including induced labor and non‐induced labor groups, were injected intravenously in the angular vein with each group of NPs at a dose of 1 mg/kg at E19. The induced labor group received 0.5 mL of oxytocin and chloroprostol sodium via intramuscular injection at E20, while the non‐induced labor and non‐pregnant groups did not receive induction drugs.
Each group was further divided into four subgroups randomly according to nanoparticles, namely ① Control (CTRL, n = 11), ② H@DC NPs (n = 9), ③ N@DC NPs (n = 8), and ④ HN@DC NPs (n = 9). The delivery process of pregnant rats was monitored after drug administration. The calculation method for delivery time was the time interval between E20 (time for injection of induction drugs) and delivery. After rats were sacrificed by inhalation drug method (carbon dioxide), placental, decidual, and uterine tissues were collected and placed in 10% formaldehyde fixative or stored at −80°C. An in vitro muscle strip contraction test was conducted to determine the range of uterine smooth muscle contractions in the pregnant rats. Technical replicates were performed at least three times for each experiment.

5.9

## IVIS (In vivo Imaging systems)
To evaluate targeting by H@DC, N@DC, and HN@DC NPs, pregnant rats at E18 were administered CTRL, H@DC, N@DC, and HN@DC NPs (1 mg/mL, 250 µL) in the angular veins. The rats were then scanned using an IVIS spectrum instrument (Perkin Elmer 2000). Cy5.5 fluorescence signals in the uterus area at different time points (0, 0.5, 1, 2, 5, 8, 12, 24, and 36 h) were collected by whole‐body fluorescence imaging on the IVIS Spectrum In Vivo Imaging System (PerkinElmer). At 36 h after injection, the rats were sacrificed, and the main organs, uterus, placenta, and embryo were collected for fluorescence imaging.
Data analysis was performed using Living Image 4.0 Software (Caliper Life Sciences, CA).

5.10

## Histological Staining
Tissue collection and processing for histological analysis were performed following a standard protocol [ ]. Briefly, tissue samples from placentas, myometrium, livers, hearts, spleens, lungs, and kidneys were collected and fixed in 10% neutral‐buffered formalin at 4°C for 48 h. Subsequently, the tissues were infiltrated with and embedded in paraffin, sectioned (5 µm), and stained with hematoxylin and 1% eosin. Slice scanning was performed using a Hamamatsu Digital Pathology Panorama Scanner (NanoZoomer A10).

5.11

## Immunohistochemistry
Rat tissue sections (5 µm) were deparaffinized and rehydrated. After microwave thermal repair using pH 9.0 ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA), rabbit HMGB1 mAb (Abcam, CAT: ab79823, dilution 1:600), and rabbit NF‐kB p65 (phospho S536) mAb (Abcam, CAT: ab86299, dilution 1:300) primary antibody working solutions were added to the tissues and incubated at 25°C for 2 h. After microwave thermal repair using pH 6.0 citric acid, rabbit NLRP3 mAb (Immunoway, CAT: YM8024, dilution 1:200), Caspase‐1 polyclonal antibody (Immunoway, CAT: YT5743, dilution 1:200), and rabbit GSDMD mAb (Immunoway, CAT: YT7991, dilution 1:300) primary antibody working solutions were dropped onto the tissue at 37°C for 1 h. 50–100 µL of secondary antibody goat anti‐ rabbit or mouse (ZSGB‐Bio, CAT: PV‐6001 or PV‐6002) was added onto the tissue, incubated at 25°C for 30 min, and washed in PBS. Diaminobenzidine was used as the working fluid for color development. Stained sections were then panoramic imaged using a Hamamatsu Digital Pathology Panorama Scanner (NanoZoomer A10) and analyzed.

5.12

## Transcriptome of Rat Tissue
Total RNA was isolated using the RNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen). Paired‐end libraries were synthesized by using the TruSeq RNA Sample Preparation Kit (Illumina, USA) following the TruSeq RNA Sample Preparation Guide. Briefly, the poly‐A‐containing mRNA molecules were purified using poly‐T oligo‐attached magnetic beads. Following purification, the mRNA was fragmented into small pieces using divalent cations at 94°C for 8 min. The cleaved RNA fragments were copied into first‐strand cDNA using reverse transcriptase and random primers. This was followed by the second strand cDNA synthesis using DNA Polymerase I and RNase H. These cDNA fragments then go through an end repair process, the addition of a single ‘A’ base, and then ligation of the adapters. The products were then purified and enriched with PCR to create the final cDNA library. Purified libraries were quantified by Qubit 2.0 Fluorometer (Life Technologies, USA) and validated by Agilent 2100 bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies, USA) to confirm the insert size and calculate the mole concentration. Cluster was generated by cBot with the library diluted to 10 pm and then was sequenced on the Illumina HiSeq Xten (Illumina, USA). The library construction and sequencing were performed at Shanghai Biotechnology Corporation.
Sequencing raw reads was preprocessed by filtering out rRNA reads, sequencing adapters, short‐fragment reads, and other low‐quality reads. The raw reads were filtered by Seqtk (version: 1.0‐r82‐dirty) before mapping to the Rnor_6.0 genome using Hisat2 (version:2.0.4). Gene expression was standardized to fragments per kilobase of per million mapped readings (FPKM). Significantly differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified as those with a False Discovery Rate (FDR) value above the threshold (Q< 0.05) and fold‐change >2 using edgeR software. Gene ontology (GO) analysis and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) analysis were performed for function annotation of differential genes.

5.13

## Western Blotting
We prepared 12.5% and 5% lower‐layer separation and upper‐layer concentrated gels, respectively. The lysates were resolved using sodium dodecyl sulfate‐polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis, followed by 250 mA electro‐transferring onto the nitrocellulose membrane, with a loading amount of 20 µg of protein. Sealing was performed for 1 h, followed by incubation with specific anti‐MMP2 (Abcam, ab92536, 1:1000), anti‐α‐SMA (Abcam, ab124964, 1:1000), anti‐TAGLN (Abcam, ab170902, 1:1000), anti‐OTR (Abcam, ab300443, 1:1000), anti‐GJA1 (Abcam, ab314908, 1:1000), and anti‐β‐tubulin (abcam, ab133543, 1:3000) overnight at 4°C. Subsequently, the membrane was incubated with horseradish peroxidase‐labeled secondary antibodies (1:3000) at 25°C for 1 h. Next, the membrane was washed four times with 0.05% TBST. The Tianneng system was used for visualization.

5.14

## Uterine Smooth Muscle Tension Detection
The Constant Temperature Smooth Muscle Trough (ZS‐SX) and Madlab bioinformatics medical signal acquisition and processing system (Madlab‐4C/5H) were purchased from Beijing Zhongshi Dichuang Technology Development Co., Ltd. Uterine smooth muscle tissue (20 mm) was collected and placed in Kerb's solution at 37°C. A mixture of 95% and 5% O2 and CO2, respectively, was continuously administered. One end of the tissue was fixed to a tension transducer, and the contraction range of each group of uterine smooth muscles was measured (contraction maximum muscle tension–minimum muscle tension) [ ].

## Statistics
The distribution of continuous variables was assessed by the Shapiro‐Wilk test. Those following a normal distribution (p > 0.05) were analyzed with Student's t‐test (for two groups) or one‐way ANOVA (for three or more groups), and the results were presented as mean ± SD. Variables that were not normally distributed (p < 0.05) were analyzed with the Mann‐Whitney U test or the Kruskal‐Wallis H test, with results expressed as medians with interquartile ranges. Statistical analyses were conducted with SPSS (version 26.0; SPSS Inc., Chicago, Illinois, USA). Statistical significance was set at P<0.05.
