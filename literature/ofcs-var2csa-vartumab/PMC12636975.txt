## Abstract

## Background
While CD19-targeting chimeric antigen receptor T-cell (CAR-T) therapies have shown efficacy in B-cell hematological malignancies, treatment of antigen-negative or relapsed tumors remains challenging. The recombinant malaria protein VAR2CSA (rVAR2) binds specifically to placental-like chondroitin sulfate A (pl-CSA), which is expressed on many cancer types, enabling its use in various tumor-targeting platforms. However, rVAR2-based CAR-T strategies are limited by poor protein thermostability, short serum half-life, and safety concerns related to irreversible CAR activation. To address these limitations, we developed a navigable CAR-T cell system that uses an rVAR2-based navigator to direct CAR-T cells to pl-CSA+ tumors.

## Methods
We overexpressed and purified rVAR2 and three catenation proteins in Escherichia coli, then produced and epitope-mapped the anti-rVAR2 monoclonal antibody 5H4VAR2omab following murine immunization. Using its single-chain fragment variable, we constructed a third-generation CAR-T with CD28 and 4-1BB co-stimulatory domains. An interlocking rVAR2 catenane, AXVB, was identified via orthogonal screening and employed as a navigator for directing CAR-T cells to pl-CSA+ tumors. The antitumor efficacy of this navigable CAR-T cell system was evaluated in vitro and in hematologic cancer cell line-derived xenograft models.

## Results
We developed an orthogonal CAR-T cell system that employs a navigator protein (rVAR2 or AXVB) to target pl-CSA+ tumors. Through a reversible switch mechanism, this system enables indirect tumor recognition by CAR-T cells, thereby improving the safety profile of conventional CAR-T therapies. Compared with the rVAR2 monomer, the AXVB multimer exhibited enhanced thermostability and target affinity. Both in vitro and in vivo, AXVB-(navigation)-CAR T cells potently eliminated CD19-positive and CD19-negative pl-CSA+ tumor cells and significantly prolonged survival in tumor-bearing mice.

## Conclusions
Protein catenation improved the thermostability and functional activity of rVAR2. In a murine model of hematologic malignancy, CAR-T cells utilizing the rVAR2 catenane (AXVB) as a targeting module exhibited superior antitumor activity.

## WHAT IS ALREADY KNOWN ON THIS TOPIC
The recombinant malaria protein VAR2CSA (rVAR2) protein exhibits broad-spectrum targeting capacity against at least 106 different types of human tumors by binding to placental-like chondroitin sulfate A (pl-CSA) present on the tumor cell plasma membrane and in the extracellular matrix. Beyond its use in drug conjugates and bispecific immune cell engagers, rVAR2 has also been applied to chimeric antigen receptor (CAR)-T cells to treat pl-CSA+ bladder cancer. However, current rVAR2-based CAR-T cell platforms rely on an irreversible covalent bond between rVAR2-SpyTag protein and SpyCatcher-CAR on T cells. Moreover, the short serum half-life of rVAR2 (<10 min) limits the in vivo assembly of such rVAR2-armed CAR-T cells.

## WHAT THIS STUDY ADDS
We developed an rVAR2-based cyclized catenation protein, designated AXVB. Compared with the rVAR2 monomer, AXVB forms an interlocking multimer with superior thermostability, enhanced CAR affinity, increased tumor-binding activity, and an extended serum half-life. AXVB-directed CAR-T cells exhibit tunable controllability and demonstrate potent, broad-spectrum antitumor activity against hematological malignancies in vitro and in vivo.

## HOW THIS STUDY MIGHT AFFECT RESEARCH, PRACTICE OR POLICY
Our findings support the clinical potential of this rVAR2-based navigable CAR-T cell system for treating pl-CSA-expressing, heterogeneous, and therapy-resistant tumors refractory to current CAR-T therapies.

## Introduction
 Leukemia and lymphoma are the most prevalent hematological malignancies, with 1.1 million new cases in 2020, accounting for 5.7% of global cancer incidences. Among these, B-cell lymphoma and leukemia are the most common subtypes. CD19, specifically expressed on most normal and malignant B cells, serves as a target for chimeric antigen receptor (CAR)-T cell therapy in treating B-cell malignancies. Indeed, CAR-T cell technology has achieved remarkable success in treating CD19-positive relapsed and refractory B-cell malignancies in recent years. However, challenges persist in achieving consistent and sustained efficacy while mitigating CAR-T-associated adverse events (AEs). 
Despite high initial response rates, only approximately 40% of patients achieved complete and durable remission. , In contrast, 10%–20% of patients with B-cell lymphoma or leukemia fail to respond to anti-CD19 CAR-T therapy in clinical trials, primarily due to antigen escape. Current strategies to address antigen escape include the development of bispecific CAR-T cells targeting CD19 in tandem with CD20, CD22, or CD123 via single-chain fragment variable (scFv) antibodies, , as well as combination therapies employing CD19-targeted CAR-T cells with CD20-targeted or CD22-targeted CAR-T cells. , Notably, Schneider et al developed trispecific CD19-CD20-CD22-targeting duoCAR-T cells, which demonstrated robust efficacy in eliminating antigen-heterogeneous B-cell tumors in preclinical models. 
Safety concerns, particularly the inability to control CAR-T cells post-infusion, pose significant challenges. CAR-T-associated AEs, such as cytokine release syndrome (CRS), immune effector cell-associated neurotoxicity syndrome, macrophage activation syndrome, hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis, and on-target off-tumor toxicity-induced long-term B-cell aplasia, continue to threaten patient lives. , Severe AEs, often resulting from uncontrolled CAR-T cell activation, further limit clinical application. Prolonged persistence of CAR-T cells in the body increases the risk of long-term side effects, even after tumor clearance. These issues may be ameliorated by “safety switch” systems, including: (1) antibody-based switches incorporating small molecules or peptide tags , ; (2) antibody-based suicide gene systems using pharmaceutical antibodies for transduced T-cell detection and selection ; and (3) small molecule-induced chemical genetic control systems. , A notable example was the iCasp9 safety switch, which successfully controlled CRS in an early clinical study of interleukin-15-armored GPC3 CAR-T cells for solid tumors. 
However, due to tumor heterogeneity, current CAR-T systems struggle to simultaneously target multiple tumor antigens and integrate compatible safety switches. Thus, there is a need for novel, controllable CAR-T cell systems that extend beyond conventional designs. To this end, we developed a switchable CAR-T cell system using recombinant malaria protein VAR2CSA (rVAR2) as a navigation molecule, termed navigable CAR-T cells, to address both antigen escape and CAR-T cell control challenges. The rVAR2 protein, derived from the malaria parasite Plasmodium falciparum, uniquely binds placental-like chondroitin sulfate A (pl-CSA), an oncofetal chondroitin sulfate (ofCS). The pl-CSA is abundantly expressed on proteoglycans in various human cancer cells, allowing rVAR2 to bind cancer cells via interactions with pl-CSA epitopes anchored to multiple tumor-associated antigens (TAAs), such as BCAN, CSPG4, CD44, SDC1, and TGFBR3. This makes rVAR2 an attractive navigator for tumor cell targeting. Previous studies have used rVAR2 in drug conjugates (VDC886) or bispecific immune cell engagers (V-aCD3) demonstrating antitumor efficacy in mouse models. Oo et al pioneered VAR2-armed CAR-T cells for bladder cancer treatment, while Khazamipour et al (2024) developed transient VAR2-(SpyTag/SpyCatcher)-CAR T-cell therapy for pl-CSA-expressing solid tumors. However, both studies relied on irreversible conjugation to construct rVAR2-integrated CARs, limiting their controllability.
To overcome these limitations, we developed navigable CAR-T cell systems using monomeric rVAR2 and its catenation variant AXVB as navigators. This work evaluates the broad-spectrum immunotherapeutic potential of pl-CSA-targeted navigable CAR-T cells, including rVAR2-(navigation)-CAR T and AXVB-(navigation)-CAR T cells, as a promising strategy for treating pl-CSA-positive hematologic malignancies.

## Results

## Design of a navigator-based orthogonal CAR-T cell system for targeting pl-CSA-positive tumors
We engineered an ON/OFF switch actuated by CAR activation through the rVAR2 protein ( ). In this design, rVAR2, derived from the minimal CSA-binding region (ID1-DBL2X-ID2α) of P. falciparum 3D7 VAR2CSA (amino acid residues 385–1016; ), exhibits high affinity for pl-CSA. , This “navigator” guides CAR-T cells to indirectly target cancer cells, unlike conventional CD19-targeted CAR-T cells, which use an scFv derived from the murine monoclonal antibody (mAb) FMC63 to directly bind cancer antigens ( ).
 To construct the (navigation)-CAR (anti-rVAR2 CAR; ), we selected a high-affinity scFv derived from the mouse anti-VAR2CSA mAb 5H4VAR2omab (5H4 scFv; ) as the ectodomain. This scFv targets the ID2a-P10 epitope (CDDRKEYMNQWSCGS) within the ID2a domain ( , ). The specificity of the 5H4 scFv-based CAR for rVAR2 epitopes was confirmed indirectly via peptide neutralization assays ( ). For comparison, a CD19-directed CAR incorporating the FMC63 scFv ectodomain was used ( ). Both CARs feature a CD8a hinge region, a CD28 transmembrane domain, and an intracellular signaling module comprising tandem CD28 and 4-1BB co-stimulatory domains followed by the CD3ζ endodomain ( ). Each construct also includes an N-terminal CD8a leader sequence and a C-terminal enhanced Green Fluorescent Protein (eGFP) reporter, separated by a P2A self-cleaving peptide ( ). Structural analysis showed that the ID2a-P10 epitope is located ≥11.0 Å from the key “WIW-motif” (amino acid residues 555–562 in PF3D7_1200600: NKNWIWKK) essential for rVAR2 binding to pl-CSA ( ). Additionally, epitope searches confirmed the absence of the CAR recognition sequence in the human proteome ( ), allowing for orthogonal interaction between CAR-T cells and the navigator, thereby enhancing tumor-targeting precision.
CD4+ and CD8+ T cells were isolated from healthy donor peripheral blood using magnetic beads, mixed at a 1:1 ratio, and transduced with lentiviral vectors to generate CD19-CAR T cells and (navigation)-CAR T cells. High CAR expression was confirmed by flow cytometry ( ) and western blotting ( ).
We assessed the binding of rVAR2 to various cancer cell lines. The rVAR2 protein exhibited reactivity with both CD19+ and CD19− human hematopoietic cancer cell lines, as well as the choriocarcinoma cell line BeWo (ATCC# CCL-98). In contrast, it showed minimal binding to healthy donor peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) and no detectable binding to primary human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs) (ATCC# PCS-100–010) ( ). The binding of rVAR2 to cancer cells was concentration-dependent and could be competitively inhibited by BT-CSA, a soluble bovine trachea chondroitin sulfate A containing 18.36% C6S (Sigma, Cat.# C9819). BT-CSA shares structural features with the cancer-associated mono-sulfated chondroitin sulfate (CS) component, and inhibition occurred in a dose-dependent manner ( ). These results are consistent with previous studies showing that rVAR2 recognizes and binds to a broad range of cancer cells via interactions with pl-CSA and had limited off-target binding to non-malignant cells. 

## Protein catenation enhances thermostability and tumor-targeting capability of rVAR2
Protein catenation improves the thermostability and antitumor activity of folded proteins without altering their native sequences or functional domains. , Catenane formation relies on the synergistic assembly of the p53 dimerization domain (p53dim) and the SpyTag-SpyCatcher system under cellular conditions. In this study, we designed three catenation cassettes by inserting rVAR2 between the p53dim, SpyTag, and SpyCatcher domains ( ). These constructs facilitated the formation of rVAR2 catenanes ( ) and their integration into a navigable CAR-T cell system ( ). Codon-optimized genes with C-terminal Strep-tag II or Twin-Strep tags were synthesized by Tsingke Biotechnology ( ) and cloned into the pET-28a (+) vector (Novagen, USA). Proteins were expressed in Escherichia coli BL21 (DE3) and purified using Strep-Tactin XT resin (IBA Lifesciences, GmbH). AXVB exhibited the highest yield (100–150 mg/L) compared with AVXVB and VAXVB (10–30 mg/L). Importantly, the AXVB strain produced a high portion of trimer protein catenanes, which were at around 58% ( ).
 Since scFv proteins retain the same specificity and affinity for their antigens as monoclonal antibodies, we indirectly compared the affinities of rVAR2 and its catenanes (AXVB, AVXVB, and VAXVB) to the 5H4 scFv by measuring their binding to 5H4VAR2omab. Using the ELISA assay, we found that AXVB exhibited higher affinity to 5H4VAR2omab than that of rVAR2, AVXVB, and VAXVB ( ), suggesting that AXVB also had higher affinity to 5H4 scFv compared with the other proteins. To further analyze the binding kinetics, we performed biolayer interferometry (BLI) by immobilizing 5H4VAR2omab on an AMQ biosensor and saturating it with either rVAR2 or AXVB. AXVB showed higher affinity to 5H4VAR2omab ( ), with a KD of 5.2±0.16 nM compared with 11.7±0.19 nM for rVAR2. This difference is likely due to the slower off-rate of AXVB (1.2×10−4 s−1 vs 3.1×10−4 s−1 for rVAR2), reflecting its prolonged dissociation from the ligand.
We next assessed the thermostability of rVAR2 and AXVB navigation proteins by dynamically monitoring their tumor cell binding activity. AXVB showed significantly higher binding to Raji cells than rVAR2 at elevated temperatures and prolonged incubation times ( ). Notably, rVAR2 lost all binding activity after 48 hours at 42°C, whereas AXVB retained its activity. Similar trends were observed with K562 cells ( ).
To quantify thermostability, we calculated relative residual activity (%) as (mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) of experimental samples/MFI of positive controls)×100, comparing AXVB and rVAR2 binding to Raji ( ) and K562 ( ) cells after temperature treatment. AXVB consistently displayed higher thermostability and maintained greater tumor cell binding activity than rVAR2 at temperatures ≥37°C ( ). Interestingly, AXVB treated at higher temperatures (≥30°C) showed enhanced activity compared with samples thawed at 4°C ( ). These results indicate that catenation enhances both the thermostability and tumor cell binding activity of folded rVAR2 proteins.

## Protein catenation of rVAR2 retained the antitumor activities of navigable CAR-T cells in vitro
To determine whether navigable CAR-T cells activate on engagement with pl-CSA-positive cancer cells, cytokine release levels were measured using a cytometric bead array (CBA) approach and compared with conventional CD19-CAR T cells. In CD19+ Raji cells, CD19-CAR T cells induced significantly higher levels of interleukin (IL)-2, IL-4, IL-6, IL-10, tumor necrosis factor (TNF), and interferon (IFN)-γ than either rVAR2- or AXVB-(navigation)-CAR T cells, while IL-17A levels were similar across all groups ( ). In contrast, this cytokine profile was reversed in CD19− K562 cells treated with CD19-CAR T cells ( ), with only IL-4 released at comparable levels in both target cell groups. Particularly, rVAR2- or AXVB-(navigation)-CAR T cells predominantly elicited a Th1 cytokine response (IL-2, TNF, and IFN-γ), rather than a Th2 response (IL-4, IL-6, and IL-10), with IL-6 and IL-10 nearly undetectable ( ). IL-17A levels remained stable at~35 pg/mL across all groups, consistent with the phenotype of T cells from healthy donors. AXVB-(navigation)-CAR T cells released significantly higher levels of IL-2 and IL-4 than rVAR2-(navigation)-CAR T cells when targeting both CD19+ Raji (p<0.05 for IL-2, p<0.01 for IL-4; ) and CD19− K562 cells (p<0.05 for both IL-2 and IL-4; ). Pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-6, TNF, and IFN-γ) were significantly reduced in rVAR2- or AXVB-(navigation)-CAR T cells (p<0.01; ). Compared with CD19-CAR T cells, rVAR2- or AXVB-(navigation)-CAR T cells secreted lower levels of IL-4 and negligible IL-10, suggesting a reduced risk of CRS.
 Next, we examined the relationship between navigable CAR-T cell activation and target cell cytotoxicity using a luciferase-based assay. After 24 hours of co-culture, both CD19-CAR T and navigable CAR-T cells effectively lysed CD19+ Raji cells at an effector-to-target (E:T) ratio of 4:1, while non-transduced T cells (NT) showed no cytotoxicity ( ). Navigable CAR-T cells also lysed CD19− K562 cells, while CD19-CAR T and NT cells had no such activity ( ). Among navigable CAR-T cells, AXVB-(navigation)-CAR T cells demonstrated cytotoxicity equivalent to rVAR2-(navigation)-CAR T cells in all tested conditions ( ). Both cytokine secretion ( ) and cytotoxicity ( ) required the co-presence of 100 nM navigation proteins and (navigation)-CAR T cells, demonstrating that (navigation)-CAR T-cell function could be regulated by adding or removing the navigation proteins.
To further investigate this possibility and evaluate the system’s efficacy against other tumor types, we tested whether navigation proteins could modulate (navigation)-CAR T-cell activity against CD19-negative solid tumor cells. Given the reported overexpression of pl-CSA (ofCS) in non-small cell lung cancer, we used the pl-CSA+ H460-RFLuc lung carcinoma cell line (derived from NCI-H460; ATCC# HTB-177; ) to evaluate cytotoxicity and cytokine secretion under varying AXVB concentrations ( ).
AXVB-(navigation)-CAR T-cell cytotoxicity against H460-RFLuc cells was proportional to AXVB concentration, unlike non-transduced or CD19-CAR T cells ( ). Similarly, cytokine (IL-2, IFN-γ, and TNF-α) secretion levels were significantly higher in AXVB-(navigation)-CAR T cells and directly correlated with AXVB concentration ( ). These findings indicate that both cytotoxicity and cytokine release in the AXVB-(navigation)-CAR T-cell system can be precisely regulated by AXVB levels.
Additionally, rVAR2- or AXVB-(navigation)-CAR T cells underwent clonal expansion (green cells) on binding to target cells (red cells), effectively eliminating both CD19+ Raji-RFLuc and CD19− K562-RFLuc cells ( ). CD19-CAR T cells expanded only in the presence of CD19+ Raji-RFLuc cells, while NT cells remained inactive. Live-cell imaging revealed that rVAR2- or AXVB-(navigation)-CAR T cells exhibited delayed cytotoxicity compared with CD19-CAR T cells, with a 50% versus 85% reduction in CD19+ Raji-RFLuc cell viability at 24 hours ( ), consistent with their lower cytokine release ( ). CD19-CAR T cells showed limited cytotoxicity against CD19− K562 cells ( ), accompanied by IFN-γ release ( ) and minimal clonal expansion ( ), consistent with manufacturing-related activation effects. In contrast, rVAR2- or AXVB-(navigation)-CAR T cells demonstrated significantly higher cytotoxicity against CD19− K562 cells than CD19-CAR T cells ( ).

## Immunogenicity profiling of navigable CAR-T cell system components
As a living cell therapy, the immunogenicity of the CAR T-cell system must be addressed. In addition to murine scFv, foreign navigation proteins could induce T cell and/or humoral immune responses. To mitigate T-cell immunogenicity, we analyzed the rVAR2/AXVB navigation proteins, 5H4 scFv, linker, and flanking sequences in the CAR using NetMHC-4.0 and NetMHCIIpan-4.0 to predict human major histocompatibility complex class I (MHC-I) and MHC-II binding peptides. Both NetMHC methods classify peptides as strong MHC binders (SBs; %Rank <0.5% for MHC-I, <1% for MHC-II) or weak MHC binders (%Rank <2% for MHC-I, <5% for MHC-II). Given the predominance of 9-mer peptide-MHC interactions, we focused on this length. Only one high-affinity peptide has been identified: AMVDTLSGL (%Rank=0.30), an MHC-I-binding sequence derived from the SpyCatcher N-terminal domain in AXVB. No other peptides show strong binding to common MHC-I/II alleles ( ).
To address humoral immunogenicity, we immunized BALB/c mice with 200 nmol/kg rVAR2 or AXVB protein solution in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) seven times over 14 days but did not observe high antibody titers against these proteins. In contrast, co-administration of the navigator protein with Complete Freund’s Adjuvant (Sigma, Cat.# F5881) induced high-titer polyclonal antibody responses. From these, a panel of highly specific monoclonal antibodies was isolated, including 5H4VAR2omab, which was used in this study.

## Protein catenation of the rVAR2 enhances navigable CAR-T cell activity in vivo
To evaluate the antitumor efficacy of rVAR2- or AXVB-(navigation)-CAR T cells in vivo, we established cell line-derived xenograft models using human hematologic cancer cell lines. Raji lymphoma or K562 leukemia cells were injected intravenously via the tail vein into immunodeficient B-NDG mice (NOD.CB17-PrkdcscidIl2rgtm1/Bcgen, Beijing Biocytogen, Cat.#110586) ( ). Navigation protein injections began 30 min before T-cell administration and were repeated every 2 days for a total of 10 doses ( ). As anticipated, rVAR2-(navigation)-CAR T cells ( ), CD19-CAR T cells ( ), and AXVB-(navigation)-CAR T cells ( ) consistently inhibited CD19+ Raji tumor growth from day 0 to day 28 post-infusion ( ). Mice treated with these CAR-T cells showed significantly prolonged survival ( ). Notably, mice treated with AXVB-(navigation)-CAR T cells exhibited the longest median survival of 79 days, compared with 44 days for PBS, 49 days for CD19-CAR T cells, and 56 days for rVAR2-(navigation)-CAR T cells.
 In the CD19− K562 model, rVAR2-(navigation)-CAR T and AXVB-(navigation)-CAR T cells were as effective as CD19-CAR T cells in suppressing tumor growth during the initial treatment phase (day 0 to day 14) compared with PBS controls ( ). However, tumor growth rebounded in the CD19-CAR T group after 14 days, reaching levels comparable to the PBS group ( ). AXVB-(navigation)-CAR T-cell-treated mice showed a median survival of 76 days, which was significantly longer than that of the PBS control group (p<0.05, ). In contrast, median survival for PBS-treated, CD19-CAR T-cell-treated, and rVAR2-(navigation)-CAR T-cell-treated mice was 38, 47, and 61 days, respectively.
 To assess the in vivo half-life of the rVAR2 protein coupler, we referenced Salanti et al, who reported that a near-infrared (NIR) fluorescent probe-conjugated rVAR2 protein (rVAR2-NIR) localized rapidly to tumors, with detectable signals 48 hours post-injection. The strongest signal was observed 1 hour after injection, followed by ≥25% attenuation within 2 hours. We also evaluated the circulatory half-life of rVAR2-NanoLuc and AXVB-NanoLuc in B-NDG mice, finding that both exceeded 24 hours ( ). Interestingly, AXVB-NanoLuc exhibited a stronger NanoLuc luciferase-catalyzed reaction signal at equimolar concentrations compared with rVAR2-NanoLuc ( ), suggesting higher drug concentration retention in the bloodstream, which may enhance (navigation)-CAR T-cell exposure. The weaker rVAR2-NanoLuc signal aligns with findings by Khazamipour et al (2024), who reported a serum half-life of less than 10 min for rVAR2 protein. 
To monitor CAR-T cell persistence in mice, we developed an ex vivo flow cytometric assay. At 2 weeks after the final infusion (day 28; ), eGFP+ CAR-T cells remained detectable in the peripheral blood of Raji-RFLuc xenograft B-NDG mice by flow cytometry ( ).
In summary, AXVB-(navigation)-CAR T and rVAR2-(navigation)-CAR T cells demonstrated broader antitumor efficacy against both CD19+ and CD19− B cell malignancies compared with CD19-CAR T cells. AXVB-(navigation)-CAR T cells, in particular, exhibited superior in vivo antitumor activity.

## Discussion
Antigen escape and AEs limit the clinical utility of CAR-T cell therapies for hematological malignancies. Multipronged T cells capable of simultaneously recognizing multiple TAAs have greater access to cognate ligands on cancer cell surface compared with T cells lacking this capacity. This implies that cancer cells are less likely to evade multipronged CAR-T cells than conventional single antigen-targeting CAR-T cells. Thus, multispecific CAR-T cells represent a promising strategy to address antigen escape. However, severe AEs, primarily due to uncontrolled CAR-T cell activation, continue to hinder the clinical application of this approach. To mitigate these AEs, incorporating a functional switch into CAR-T cell therapy could be a viable solution. Nevertheless, no reported CAR-T cell strategy combines multitargeting capabilities with modulatory functions. We propose the development of a broad-spectrum navigation module equipped with a safety switch to prevent tumor antigen escape and control CAR-T-associated AEs. To achieve this, we developed rVAR2-based navigable CAR-T cells ( , ).
Hyperthermia associated with CRS is the most common side effect of CAR-T cell therapy, often presenting as a body temperature ≥38°C or, in severe cases, a high fever >39°C lasting over 10 hours. The thermostability of proteins under such conditions may directly impact the efficacy of protein-navigated CAR-T cell systems in patients with cancer. To enhance the thermostability and antitumor activity of the multispecific and navigable CAR-T cell system, we engineered rVAR2 catenation variants as CAR-T cell navigators. We found that the cyclized catenation protein AXVB, constructed by integrating SpyTag and SpyCatcher domains at the N-termini and C-termini of a fusion protein comprising the p53dim domain and rVAR2 monomer ( ), exhibited higher yields of interlocking multimers and greater binding affinity to (navigation)-CAR T cells compared with rVAR2 monomer and other rVAR2 catenation proteins ( and ). AXVB also showed superior thermostability, maintaining tumor cell binding activity at elevated temperatures (≥37°C) where rVAR2 lost activity ( ). This enhanced in vitro thermostability may improve the transportability of protein-based therapeutics and extend their in vivo half-life. Additionally, the tumor cell binding activity of rVAR2 and AXVB varied across cell lines ( ), suggesting that different tumor cells may express proteoglycans with varying molecular densities of pl-CSA-modified membrane glycosaminoglycans.
Next, we evaluated the cytotoxicity and cytokine release of navigable CAR-T cells and CD19-CAR T cells against pl-CSA+CD19+ Raji cells, pl-CSA+CD19− K562 cells, and pl-CSA+CD19− H460 cells in vitro. We found that rVAR2- or AXVB-(navigation)-CAR T cells effectively targeted and killed pl-CSA-positive tumor cells across different lineages ( and ). However, cytokine release from rVAR2- or AXVB-(navigation)-CAR T cells was relatively lower than that of CD19-CAR T cells when targeting pl-CSA+CD19+ Raji cells ( ). In terms of CD19+ Raji cell killing, rVAR2- or AXVB-(navigation)-CAR T cells exhibited slower tumor cell killing compared with CD19-CAR T cells ( ), possibly due to the time required for functional navigable CAR-T cell formation. Furthermore, rVAR2- or AXVB-(navigation)-CAR T cells secreted significantly lower levels of IL-4 compared with CD19-CAR T cells after co-incubation with Raji cells ( ). All CD19-CAR T cells, rVAR2- or AXVB-(navigation)-CAR T cells secreted low levels of IL-10 after co-incubation with target cells, with rVAR2- or AXVB-(navigation)-CAR T cells producing nearly undetectable levels ( ).
Recent studies by Zhao et al demonstrated that interleukin (IL)-10-producing CAR-T cells retain metabolic fitness, resist exhaustion, and exhibit potent antitumor activity, as evidenced by high cure rates and durable responses in syngeneic and xenograft models of hematological and solid tumors. Similarly, Bai et al and Feng et al found that Fc-IL-4 treatment enhances glycolytic metabolism and prolongs CD8+ effector T-cell survival, improving CAR-T cell persistence and antitumor efficacy. We hypothesize that the antitumor function of navigable CAR-T cells with reduced Th2-type activity could be enhanced by co-expressing IL-10, adding IL-4 during manufacturing, or pretreating CAR-T cells with IL-4 before infusion. However, recent studies suggest that IL-4 signaling drives exhaustion in CAR-T cells with CD28 co-stimulation, indicating that Th2-type cytokine effects may vary depending on CAR design.
The cytotoxicity and cytokine release of the (navigation)-CAR T cells against pl-CSA+CD19− H460 lung cancer cells were proportional to the concentration of AXVB available to the (navigation)-CAR ( ), offering a potential safety switch to improve the safety profile of navigable CAR-T cells. This also indicates that the rVAR2- or AXVB-(navigation)-CAR T-cell system holds promise for targeting pl-CSA-positive solid tumors. However, further in vivo validation is required. Compared with conventional CD19-CAR T cells, which target only CD19-positive cells, the rVAR2- or AXVB-(navigation)-CAR T-cell system demonstrated efficient antitumor activity against various hematological tumor cells, including CD19+ Raji cells and CD19− K562 cells ( ), and significantly prolonged the survival of tumor-bearing mice ( ). Interestingly, CD19-CAR T cells also exhibited early antitumor responses in a mouse model harboring CD19− K562 cells ( ), potentially due to activation during the manufacturing process. That is, early in the infusion of CD19-CAR-T cells, these activated CAR-T cells may induce limited non-specific antitumor activity ( ).
Importantly, AXVB-(navigation)-CAR T cells exhibited stronger antitumor activity than rVAR2-(navigation)-CAR T cells, likely due to AXVB’s higher affinity for (navigation)-CAR and prolonged in vivo half-life ( ).
The immunogenicity of exogenous biologics remains an inherent challenge in CAR-T cell therapy. Bioinformatics analysis of human MHC-binding peptides ( ) offers insights for reducing T-cell immunogenicity in the navigable CAR-T system used here. The weak humoral response to navigation proteins is consistent with the low natural antibody levels against rVAR2 observed during P. falciparum infection in malaria-endemic areas, despite rVAR2 being a top placental malaria vaccine candidate. An early phase clinical trial for the development of a pregnancy-associated malaria vaccine demonstrated that rVAR2 is less immunogenic in humans without adjuvants but exhibits an acceptable safety profile. The low immunogenicity of VAR2CSA proteins may result from sequence diversification and evolution to evade immune recognition while maintaining pl-CSA binding affinity. Another critical consideration for rVAR2’s potential clinical utility is its off-target binding profile. Previous preclinical studies demonstrated that rVAR2 exhibits no off-target binding to normal human tissues in extensive immunohistochemical screens. Instead, rVAR2 efficiently captures circulating tumor cells from patient blood samples and enables pan-cancer detection, highlighting its translational potential.
Unlike conventional CAR designs that are genetically fused to VAR2CSA (VAR2-CAR), both VAR2-armed CAR T cells and our navigable CAR-T cell system guided by rVAR2 or AXVB protein offer a safer alternative for female patients. Directly targeting pl-CSA with CAR-T cell therapy poses a significant risk of miscarriage, even after a normal pregnancy post-cancer recovery. This is because CAR-T cells are living drugs that persist long-term and cannot be withdrawn once administered, potentially leading to placental attack in pregnant patients due to shared pl-CSA (ofCS) expression. In contrast, transient VAR2-armed CAR T cells and navigable CAR-T cells circumvent this risk, since cytotoxicity toward the placenta can be prevented by halting VAR2-(SpyT) (rVAR2-SpyTag) or navigator protein delivery.
Our results demonstrate that multispecific navigable CAR-T cells outperform non-switchable CAR-T cells targeting single antigens. However, a key limitation of this system is the short in vivo persistence of navigation proteins, which requires multiple doses to sustain antitumor responses. Future research should prioritize developing navigation proteins with extended half-lives. Alternatively, traditional CAR-T cell formats incorporating recently developed anti-pl-CSA (or anti-oncofetal CS) scFvs may offer greater clinical practicality, as they eliminate the need for additional VAR2-armed CAR-T cell reassembly or rVAR2-derived navigator infusion. Nevertheless, their safety profiles remain a concern. Persistence of CAR-T cells is another concern. Conventional protocols often use sustained human IL-2 supplementation (1 µg/mouse, approximately 50 µg/kg every 3 days) to support CAR-T expansion in immunodeficient models. In contrast, we administered 3 weekly infusions of 1×10⁷ T cells (60–80% CAR+; ) to maintain sufficient cell numbers while avoiding potential effects of high-dose IL-2. Although eGFP+ CAR-T cells remained detectable 2 weeks after the final infusion (day 28; ), their frequency among PBMCs had declined relative to day 3 post-initial infusion. The AXVB-(navigation)-CAR T-cell group exhibited the greatest reduction in the proportion of eGFP+ CAR-T cells, with a 163.17-fold decrease (from 9.79% to 0.06%). This suggests limited clonal expansion or rapid depletion in vivo, possibly due to the absence of cytokine support such as human IL-2 or IL-15 in these mice. Moreover, unlike other CAR-T systems, the AXVB protein simultaneously engages multiple pl-CSA molecules and provides multiple binding sites (≥2) for (navigation)-CARs ( ). The lowest peripheral CAR-T cell levels observed after AXVB-(navigation)-CAR T-cell treatment in the B-NDG model ( ) suggest possible fratricide among T cells in vivo.
In conclusion, we present a navigable CAR-T cell system directed by either the rVAR2 protein or its catenation variant, AXVB. The AXVB-directed system demonstrates enhanced navigator thermostability and superior antitumor efficacy against pl-CSA-positive hematological malignancies. Theoretical mitigation of serious AEs may be achieved through navigator dose reduction or discontinuation on onset. This navigable CAR-T strategy establishes pl-CSA as a compelling immunotherapeutic target and exhibits strong translational potential for treating hematological malignancies.

## Materials and methods

## Recombinant malarial VAR2CSA protein design and purification
The rVAR2 protein used in this study comprises the ID1, DBL2X, and truncated ID2a domains of the full-length malarial VAR2CSA protein from P. falciparum 3D7 (Pf3D7_1200600, AA385-1016). This protein binds to pl-CSA with high specificity and affinity (KD~7.6 nM). Plasmids of pET-28a (+)-rVAR2, containing an E. coli codon-optimized strep-tagged rVAR2 expression cassette, were transformed into the E. coli BL21(DE3) cells. The rVAR2 proteins were overexpressed and extracted using Strep-Tactin Sepharose resin (Cat.# 2-1201-025, IBA GmbH), followed by purification via ion exchange chromatography using DEAE Sepharose resin (Cat.# 17070901, Cytiva, USA) to remove endotoxins. The buffer of the purified recombinant protein solution was exchanged with endotoxin-free PBS using a PD10 desalting column (Cat.# 17085101, Cytiva, USA). Finally, sterile filtration was performed using a 0.22 µm filter unit (Cat.# SLGPR33RB, Merck Millipore). The prepared rVAR2 proteins were stored at −20°C and characterized by sodium dodecyl sulphate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE), gel filtration chromatography and Bradford protein assay before use.

## Cellular synthesis of rVAR2 protein catenanes
The method used the p53 dimerization domain (p53dim) to guide protein chain intertwining and genetically encoded SpyTag-SpyCatcher chemistry for efficient cyclization. The rVAR2 protein-coding gene was cis-spliced with p53dim, SpyTag, and SpyCatcher fragments in fusion gene expression cassettes. All E. coli codon-optimized genes for rVAR2 protein catenanes were synthesized by GENEWIZ. The rVAR2 catenane variants were cloned into the pET-28a (+) for protein expression, extraction, purification, endotoxin removal, buffer exchange, filter sterilization and characterization as described above. Yields of protein catenanes were at least 10 mg/100 mL terrific broth medium for AXVB.

## scFv generation and selection
An anti-rVAR2 mAb was generated by immunizing BALB/c mice with purified rVAR2 proteins emulsified with Complete Freund’s adjuvant (Sigma, Cat.# F5881). Spleen cells from immunized mice were fused with mouse myeloma SP2/0 cells, and hybridoma supernatants were screened by ELISA against a bovine serum albumin (BSA)-conjugated rVAR2 proteins and by flow cytometry for cancer cells. Among the positive hybridomas, an mAb-secreting cell line, 5H4, was selected. This line secreted an IgG antibody with high affinity to rVAR2 and no binding activity to human cell lines. RNA extraction was performed, and the variable region coding sequences of the heavy and light chains of the murine 5H4 mAb (5H4VAR2omab) were analyzed by Reverse Transcription-Polymerase Chain Reaction (RT-PCR) using specific primers from GENEWIZ. The amino acid sequences of the variable regions and complementary determining regions of the heavy and light chains of 5H4 are shown in . To confirm the non-human specificity of 5H4VAR2omab, a two-step assay was performed. First, the epitope was preliminarily identified by antigen-specific ELISA using three major antigenic peptides (ID1, DBL2X, and ID2a). Only the ID2a antigen peptide-coated wells showed results consistent with rVAR2 protein-coated wells, indicating the epitope was located on ID2a. Second, an overlapping peptide library ( ) was synthesized by GenScript to identify the specific epitope in the ID2a domain. One peptide, ID2a-P10, showed a positive signal ( ) in a peptide neutralization assay. The specific epitope of ID2a-P10 (CDDRKEYMNQWSCGS) was confirmed. Peptide search was used to analyze whether this epitope was present in the human proteome.

## SDS-PAGE and western blotting
The target cells were homogenized and lysed in radio immunoprecipitation assay (RIPA) lysis buffer (Cat.# KGP702-100, KeyGene) containing protease inhibitor cocktail (Cat.# P1010, Beyotime) and phosphatase inhibitor cocktail D (Cat.# P1096, Beyotime) for 30 min on ice. Equal volumes of 4×Protein SDS-PAGE Loading Buffer (Cat.# 9173, Takara) were added, and samples were boiled for ≤5 min to denature proteins. After boiling, samples were stored at −80°C before loading. Protein samples were separated by SDS-PAGE (Cat.# M00665, GenScript SurePAGE) and transferred to polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes (Cat.# ISEQ00010, Millipore) for western blotting. Membranes were incubated with an anti-CD3ζ primary antibody (Cat.# sc-1239, Santa Cruz Biotechnology) and a horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG H&L secondary antibody (Cat.# ab205719, Abcam). After washing with phosphate buffered saline (PBS) solution with 0.05% Tween 20 (PBS-T) buffer, bands were detected with enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) reagents (Cat.# WBULS0500, Millipore) and visualized with a chemiluminescent image analyzer (Tanon 5200, Tanon Science and Technology). Densitometry was quantified using Image Studio Lite (LI-COR Biosciences), and results were presented as the ratio of target protein densitometry.

## Flow cytometry
Cells were grown to 70%–80% confluence, harvested in PBS containing 2% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 2 mM EDTA, and incubated with protein (25–100 nM) or primary antibody (recommended dosage) in PBS containing 2% FBS and 0.01% w/v NaN3 for 45 min at 4°C. Binding was analyzed using a BD Accuri C6 (BD Biosciences) or Cytek Aurora (Cytek Biosciences) flow cytometer after secondary incubation with a fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC)-labeled goat anti-mouse antibody, unless stated otherwise. Antibodies used for flow cytometry on other freshly isolated blood cells are detailed in the main text or . For inhibition studies, proteins were co-incubated with CSA (BT-CSA; Sigma, Cat.# C9819). Data were analyzed with FlowJo software (BD Biosciences).

## Affinity/scFv binding assessment
The equilibrium dissociation constants (KD) of the mouse anti-VAR2CSA 5H4 mAb ligand (5H4VAR2omab) for rVAR2 and its catenation proteins (AXVB, AVXVB, and VAXVB) were determined by ELISA. Briefly, rVAR2 or its catenation proteins were coated onto a 96-well ELISA plate (JET BIOFIL, Cat.# FEP101896). After blocking with PBS-T containing 5% BSA, plates were incubated with 5H4VAR2omab gradient solutions and then with an HRP-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG (H+L) antibody (Invitrogen, Cat.# 31430). The 3,3′,5,5′-tetramethylbenzidine (TMB) substrate was added for color development. After terminating the reaction with 2M sulfuric acid, absorbance (OD450) was measured using a BioTek Synergy H1 microplate reader. Data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism software.
Binding kinetics of the anti-VAR2CSA scFv were assessed by BLI on an Octet RED96e System (Pall Forté Bio/Sartorius). The 5H4VAR2omab was immobilized on anti-mouse capture biosensors (AMQ) at a target density of 10 response units. Protein analytes (rVAR2 or AXVB) at varying concentrations were tested in 96-well microplates (Greiner, Cat.# 655209). Experiments were conducted at 25°C in 1×PBS buffer containing 0.1% BSA and 0.02% Tween 20 (pH 7.4). Data were analyzed using Octet Data Analysis High Throughput software (Pall Forté Bio/Sartorius), and kinetic rate constants were calculated using a 1:1 binding model.

## CSA competitive binding assays
Bovine tracheal CSA (BT-CSA; Sigma, Cat.# C9819) was used to disrupt rVAR2 and cancer cell interactions. High amounts (100–400 µg/mL) of BT-CSA were co-incubated with 25 nM rVAR2 protein in PBS containing 2% FBS for 30 min at 4°C. The treated rVAR2 protein solutions were then added to 2.0 mL Eppendorf tubes with 1×106 cells per tube and incubated at 4°C for 45 min. Cells were washed twice with PBS containing 2% FBS, followed by sequential addition of 5H4VAR2omab and FITC-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG H&L secondary antibody (Abcam, Cat.# ab6785). After incubation and washing, cells were analyzed by flow cytometry. Data were processed using GraphPad Prism software, and fold changes in MFI were calculated as MFI of experimental samples divided by MFI of unstained controls.

## CAR-T cell production
PBMCs were isolated from healthy donor blood using Lymphoprep (STEMCELL Technologies). CD4+ and CD8+ T cells were purified using Dynabeads CD4 and CD8 Positive Isolation Kits (Invitrogen, Cat.# 11331D and 11333D) and stimulated with Dynabeads Human T-Activator CD3/CD28 (Gibco) in X-VIVO 15 serum-free medium (Lonza, Cat.# BE02-060F) supplemented with 210 IU/mL recombinant human interleukin-2 (rhIL-2, R&D Systems, Cat.# 202-IL-050) for 18–24 hours. The activated T cells were transduced with recombinant lentiviral vectors for 24 hours. The lentiviral transfer plasmid, under the EF1α promoter, encoded the following components in sequence: CD8 signal peptide, 5H4 ScFv, CD8a hinge region, CD28 transmembrane domain, CD28 and CD137 (4-1BB) co-stimulatory domains, and CD3ζ signaling domain ( ). Transduction efficiency was assessed 72 hours post-transduction by measuring eGFP expression using Cytek Aurora spectral flow cytometry (Cytek Biosciences). Transduced CAR-T cells were expanded for 10–14 days in X-VIVO 15 medium supplemented with 5% autologous human serum or 5% heat-inactivated FBS (Biological Industries, Cat.# 04-001-1ACS) and 210 IU/mL rhIL-2.

## Thermostability assessment of navigation proteins
The thermostability of AXVB and rVAR2 proteins was evaluated by measuring their residual activity after incubation at 30°C, 37°C, and 42°C for 30 min, 2 hours, 24 hours, and 48 hours. Proteins were purified to ≥98% purity, thawed on ice, and incubated at the specified temperatures. Samples were collected periodically and stored at 4°C. Baseline activity was determined using a control sample stored at −80°C. Raji or K562 cells were incubated with protein samples, followed by sequential addition of 5H4VAR2omab and Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG (Abcam, Cat.# ab150113). Binding was analyzed by Cytek Aurora spectral flow cytometry. Relative residual reactivity (%) was calculated as (MFI of experimental samples/MFI of positive control)×100.

## Cellular cytotoxicity assay
The human B-cell lymphoma cell line Raji (ATCC# CCL-86) and the chronic myeloid leukemia cell line K562 (ATCC# CCL-243) were obtained from ATCC (Manassas, Virginia, USA). These cells were transduced with a lentiviral vector encoding mCherry and a firefly luciferase reporter gene (mCherry-FFLuc, RFLuc) linked by an F2A peptide, generating CD19+ Raji-RFLuc and CD19− K562-RFLuc cell lines.
A luciferase-based cytotoxicity assay was used to evaluate the in vitro cytotoxicity of rVAR2- or AXVB-(navigation)-CAR T cells. Briefly, RFLuc-expressing Raji and K562 cells were used as target cells. Effector CAR-T cells (E) and target cells (T) were co-cultured in triplicate at specified E:T ratios in black-walled 96-well plates. Each well contained 5×103 target cells in 100 µL Roswell Park Memorial Institute (RPMI)-1640 medium (Gibco, Cat.# 11875093) supplemented with 5% heat-inactivated FBS (Biological Industries, Cat.# 04-001-1ACS) and 100 nM rVAR2 or AXVB navigation protein. Target cells alone were plated to determine maximum luciferase activity (measured in relative light units, RLUmax). After 24 hours, 100 µL Bright-Glo luciferase substrate (Promega) was added, and luminescence was measured using a BioTek Synergy H1 plate reader. Per cent lysis was calculated as (1 − (RLUsample / RLUmax)) × 100. Data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism software.
To assess the role of the AXVB navigation protein in the navigable CAR-T cell system, the same cytotoxicity assay was performed using the human lung cancer cell line H460 (ATCC# HTB-177), transduced with RFLuc (H460-RFLuc), at varying molar concentrations of AXVB.

## Cytokine measurement assay
Cytokine secretion by CAR-T cells co-cultured with CD19+ Raji or CD19− K562 cells was quantified using a Human Th1/Th2/Th17 CBA Kit (BD Biosciences). Target cells (5×103/well) were plated onto a 96-well plate in 100 µL X-VIVO 15 serum-free medium (Lonza, Cat.# BE02-060F) containing 100 nM rVAR2 or AXVB. After incubation at 37°C for 30 min, CAR-T or NT were added at an E:T ratio of 4:1 in a final volume of 200 µL. Following 24 hours of co-culture, supernatants were collected and analyzed for IL-2, IL-4, IL-6, IL-10, TNF, IFN-γ, and IL-17A using Cytek Aurora spectral flow cytometry. Cytokine concentrations were determined from standard curves using Cytek SpectroFlo and GraphPad Prism software.
For H460-RFLuc cells, cytokine levels (IL-2, TNF-α, IFN-γ) were measured using R&D ELISA kits (R&D Systems, Cat.# D2050, DTA00D, and DIF50). Briefly, H460-RFLuc cells (1.25×105/well) were cultured in RPMI-1640 medium supplemented with 5% heat-inactivated FBS and navigation proteins at varying concentrations. After 6 hours, supernatants were replaced with fresh medium, and CAR-T or NT were added at an E:T ratio of 4:1. After 24 hours, supernatants were collected and analyzed according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Data were processed using GraphPad Prism software.

## Live cell imaging of CAR-T cell cytotoxicity
The killing dynamics of CAR-T cells were monitored using an IncuCyte S3 live-cell imaging system (Sartorius, USA). rVAR2- or AXVB-(navigation)-CAR T cells and CD19-CAR T cells were co-cultured with Raji-RFLuc or K562-RFLuc cells at an E:T ratio of 4:1, in the presence or absence of 200 nM navigation protein. NT served as a negative control. The cells were imaged every 2 hours over a 48-hour period.

## Xenograft mouse model experiments
Female immunodeficient B-NDG (NOD.CB17-PrkdcscidIl2rgtm1/Bcgen) mice (8 weeks old, Beijing Biocytogen, Cat.# 110586) were intravenously injected with 5×105 Raji-RFLuc or K562-RFLuc cells on day −3. Following verification of tumor engraftment via bioluminescence imaging using an In Vivo Imaging System (IVIS) Spectrum (Caliper Life Sciences, Hopkinton, Massachusetts, USA) on day −1, mice were randomly assigned to four groups (n=5 per group). On days 0, 8 and 14, mice received intravenous administration of 1×10⁷ T cells (60–80% CAR+) 15 min after pretreatment with 200 nmol/kg of either rVAR2 or AXVB protein. Additional supplemental doses of 200 nmol/kg rVAR2 or AXVB were administered intravenously every 48 hours for a total of 10 doses. Bioluminescence imaging was performed every 7 days for a total of five time points. Prior to imaging, mice were anesthetized with 2% isoflurane and injected intraperitoneally with 150 mg/kg D-luciferin (Yeasen Biotechnology, Cat.# 40 902ES03). Imaging was conducted 12 min after luciferin injection.
To measure the in vivo half-life of navigation proteins, tumor-free B-NDG mice were injected intravenously with 100 nmol/kg rVAR2-NanoLuc or AXVB-NanoLuc on day 15. Luciferase activity was monitored using the IVIS Spectrum system after intraperitoneal injection of Nano-Glo Luciferase Assay Substrate (Promega, Cat.# N113). Data were analyzed using Living Image software (Caliper Life Sciences).
Mice were housed under specific pathogen-free conditions, five per cage. Body weight was measured two times per week. Animals exhibiting ≥20% body weight loss or ulcerated tumors were humanely euthanized. All procedures were performed in accordance with the “3R” principles of animal welfare.

## Statistical analysis
Statistical significance and median survival time were calculated using GraphPad Prism software. Unless specified otherwise, in vitro data were analyzed using a one-tailed Student’s t-test, and in vivo data were analyzed using the log-rank (Mantel-Cox) test. Data from in vitro assays with experimental replicates are presented as mean±SD, while data from in vitro or in vivo assays with biological replicates are presented as mean±SEM. Significance levels are denoted as follows: *p<0.05, **p<0.01, and ***p<0.001.
