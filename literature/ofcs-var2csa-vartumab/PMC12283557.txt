## Summary
Placenta-targeting nano-drugs have emerged as a safe and effective treatment option for preeclampsia (PE). Downregulation of peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor γ (PPARγ) induces dysfunctional placental trophoblasts and facilitates placental reactive oxygen species, which weakens the PGC1α/UCP2 oxidative stress pathway, causing PE symptoms. Herein, rosiglitazone (RGZ), as an effective PPARγ agonist, exhibited great potential in PE treatment. However, low solubility and nonspecific effect limit the clinical application. Therefore, this study presents CGKRK-modified and RGZ-loaded lipid nanoparticles (CRNPs), based on the specific binding of CGKRK to calreticulin highly expressed in human and mouse trophoblasts. CRNP alleviates excessive oxidative stress and improves placental development and fetal growth in L-NAME-induced PE mice. However, it does not cause maternal side effects or fetal malformation in pregnant mice injected with a high dose of CRNP through the tail vein. Consequently, the safe and effective delivery of CRNP optimizes the placental-targeting therapeutic strategy.

## Introduction
Preeclampsia (PE), a unique pregnancy syndrome that affects approximately 4 million maternal pregnancies worldwide, is estimated to cause at least 70,000 maternal and 500,000 infant deaths annually, posing a serious threat to maternal and child health. , According to the widely accepted two-stage theory, in the first stage, weakened invasion ability of placental trophoblasts and a defect in spiral artery remodeling cause placental ischemia and hypoxia ; in the second stage, the dysfunctional placenta produces numerous active substances, including reactive oxygen species (ROS), which enter maternal circulation and cause maternal vascular endothelial dysfunction, leading to PE systemic vasospasm. Maternal systemic vasospasm is the primary cause of excessive oxidative stress in PE. , Existing clinical drug control measures cannot effectively improve placental narrow spiral arteries and maternal systemic vasospasm, including the non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drug aspirin (ASP) for PE prevention and the anti-hypertensive drug labetalol (LBT) for PE therapy. , However, high ROS levels caused by constant vasospasm may be an antioxidant target for PE treatment.
Peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor γ (PPARγ) is highly expressed in adipose tissues and normal placental syncytiotrophoblasts (STBs). , , , , , Normal PPARγ levels promote placental trophoblast cell development and alleviate PE damage in cell and mouse models. , Additionally, increased PPARγ can significantly reduce intracellular ROS levels in PE explants. , However, patients with PE have significantly lower levels of PPARγ in their placental villi, which is related to PE symptoms. , , , Down-regulation of PPARγ can weaken the bond between PPARγ and peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor γ-coactivator 1α (PGC1α), destroying placental mitochondrial functions. , Low PPARγ-PGC1α complex cannot effectively activate mitochondrial uncoupling protein 2 (UCP2) promoters through E-Box elements, leading to increased mitochondrial proton pump gradient and ROS production in placental villi of patients with PE. , A previous study reported that paternal hyperglycemia may induce epigenetic modification of PPARα in the liver of an offspring, causing impaired lipid metabolism, indicating that PPARs are essential in pregnancy-related disorders. However, targeted treatment for PPARγ in PE is rare, and the systemic activation of PPARγ can simultaneously affect other organs and reduce drug concentration. Consequently, developing a nano-drug delivery system for rosiglitazone (RGZ), a PPARγ activator, to target the placenta and alleviate PE oxidative stress is imperative.
Recently, nanoparticle drugs have effectively loaded therapeutic molecules, such as organic drug molecules, to provide a more effective and safe therapeutic effect on several diseases. , , , , , , , Among these, engineered nano-medicine has great potential for treating placental diseases due to its high biocompatibility, specific targeting, and slow drug release. , This application of mitochondrial antioxidant (MitoQ) nanoparticles to reduce placental oxidative stress and improve fetal outcomes for a placenta-targeted therapeutic strategy in a rat model of prenatal hypoxia is supported by Ganguly et al. This observation that PEG5k-PLA8k nanoparticles containing Nrf2 siRNA and sFLT siRNA could mediate simultaneous down-regulation of placental Nrf2 and sFlt1 and improve maternal and fetal outcomes in a PE mouse mode is also reported by Li L et al. And the fact of different ionizable lipid nanoparticles containing mRNA of placental growth factor (PLGF) and vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) for placental gene therapy is also confirmed by Young et al. and Geisler et al. , Although some nanoparticles can effectively treat diseases of the placenta, most nanoparticle researches focus on expensive placental gene delivery, lack chemical drugs used in pregnancy, including RGZ, and utilize the same placenta-targeting polypeptide plCSA. RGZ is cheaper and more readily available than gene therapy. A previous study reported that a tumor-targeting peptide, CGKRK can specifically target the placental tissues of pregnant women and mice without any significant effect on fetal mice. Accordingly, we hypothesized that CGKRK-targeting RGZ-loaded lipid nanoparticles (CRNPs) may be a better placental nano-delivery for the prevention and treatment of progressive PE. We demonstrated that CRNP effectively accumulated in the placentas of NG-Nitro-L-arginine Methyl Ester,Hydrochloride (L-NAME)-induced pregnant Institute of Cancer Research (ICR) mice, alleviated the excessive ROS level, improved the pregnant outcomes, and exhibited no side effects on pregnant and fetal mice.

## Results

## Characterizations of NP, RNP, and CRNP
As a lipid nanocarrier, placenta-targeting and RGZ-loaded CRNP NP were designed and injected into ICR PE mice. Effective drug RGZ and placenta-targeting peptide CGKRK were used to prepare null-loading lipid nanoparticles (NPs), RGZ-loaded lipid nanoparticles (RNPs), and DSPE-PEG2000-CGKRK, and different lipid materials. The hollow lipid nanoparticles was sequentially synthesized through the self-assembly of lipid components as indicated in ( A–S1C). Dynamic light scattering (DLS) analysis revealed that the particle size of NP, RNP, and CRNP were 117.62 ± 3.00, 115.19 ± 1.78, and 124.65 ± 1.54 nm, respectively ( A–1D). The surface zeta potential was −29.36 ± (−0.61), −24.37 ± (−0.68), and −26.56 ± (−0.23) mV, respectively ( D). The polydispersity index (PDI) values were 0.19 ± 0.00, 0.16 ± 0.00, and 0.18 ± 0.00, respectively ( ). Chemical coupling analysis using an ultraviolet spectrograph (UVS) revealed that the amine group on CGKRK was conjugated with the carboxyl group of DSPE-PEG2000 on RNP with the disappearance of 200–380 nm characteristic peaks of the carboxyl group ( E and 1F). Images using a transmission electron microscope (TEM) displayed that RNP and CRNP were hollow and spherical-like liposomes ( G and 1H). Stability analysis using DLS revealed that RNP and CRNP were stable in double distilled water (DDW), 1X phosphate buffer solution (PBS), fetal bovine serum (FBS), and RPMI 1640 medium (RPMI) for 24 h incubation ( I and 1J). Drug loading measurement using an ultraviolet spectrophotometer indicated that the drug loading rates of RNP and CRNP were 7.8% and 7.6%, respectively. 

## Cytotoxicity, cell uptake, and ROS alleviation
We examined the reductive effect of RNP on ROS levels in HTR-8/Svneo and HUVEC cell models, compared to the prophylactic drug ASP and the therapeutic drug LBT for clinical use. We used cell counting kit-8 assays (CCK-8) to ensure the suitable concentration of different reagents to treat these cell models, including 50 μM/100 μM H2O2, 1% DMSO, and 30 μg/mL RGZ/ASP/LBT ( A–S1D and A–S2F) and 400 μg/mL RNP ( A and 2B), respectively. We used Cy5-labeled RNP (Cy5-RNP, 400 μg/mL) and flow cytometry (FCM) to prove the increasing uptake efficiency of HTR-8/Svneo and HUVEC cells from 0 to 8 h ( C and 2D), revealing that these cells could uptake RNPs autonomously. These H2O2-induced models and the DCFH-DA probe are commonly used to examine ROS production in different research. FCM revealed that RNP and RGZ could effectively reduce ROS levels of HTR-8/Svneo ( E, 2F, 2I, and 2J) and HUVEC ( G, 2H, 2K, and 2L) cell models to prevent H2O2 damage and rescue cells, despite the fact they exist in different forms in vitro. Analogously, ASP can achieve the same mitigation effect of RNP in HTR-8/Svneo and HUVEC cell models as a clinical prophylactic for PE. However, LBT as a clinical treatment for PE can effectively reduce the ROS level in the HUVEC cell model but not in HTR-8/Svneo and is less effective in two-cell models. Additionally, DMSO/NP could not reduce this ROS production ( A–S3H). These results strongly confirmed the effective anti-oxidative effect of RNP in PE pathologically related placental trophoblast and endothelial cells. 

## Improvement of cell functions
After ROS remission was observed in the cell model, several cell functions were examined using different cell experiments. Cell scratch assays revealed that treatment with RNP and RGZ significantly enhanced the same migratory area in HTR-8/Svneo ( A and 3I) and HUVEC ( C and 3J) cell models for prevention. Moreover, the migration ability generated from ASP is more effective than that of RNP. Additionally, LBT revealed similar boosting effects in cell rescue experiments of HTR-8/Svneo ( B and 3K) and HUVEC ( D and 3L) cell models. However, LBT played a worse role in the migratory improvement of HUVEC cell models. Transwell chamber experiments revealed that RNP and RGZ exhibited the same effect on promoting HTR-8/Svneo cell invasion and were more effective than ASP and LBT ( E, 3F, 3M, and 3N). Tube formation experiments revealed that RNP and RGZ exhibited the same effect for prevention but not for rescue ( G, 3H, 3O, and 3P). Differently, RGZ was less effective than RNP for rescue. ASP and LBT revealed a worse condition than RNP. Except that NP accidentally stimulated the tubular formation of HUVEC but was much weaker than RNP, DMSO/NP did not exhibit any improvement in different functional experiments as aforementioned ( A–S4H and A–S5H). These results revealed that RNP could improve cell functions by alleviating ROS levels. 

## Metabolism, distribution, and placenta targeting
For metabolism, 1X PBS, Cy5-RNP, and Cy5-CRNP were injected into these normal pregnant mice at G16.5d through tail vein injection (VI) ( A and 4B), respectively. In vivo fluorescence imaging revealed that the Cy5 fluorescence intensity in blood circulation was significantly decreased and disappeared at 24 h ( C), indicating that these lipid nanoparticles could be metabolized within 24 h. For distribution, Cy5-RNP and Cy5-CRNP exhibited low enrichment in nonspecific organs ( D). For specific placenta targeting, the placenta-targeting CRNP was significantly lodged in the placentas of pregnant uteruses and the labyrinthine of placentas but not in the fetuses ( E). The placental structure between mice and humans is different, and the placental diagram of ICR mice was drawn rationally according to this published review. Consequently, these results strongly demonstrated that CRNP can specifically target and accumulate in the placentas without traversing them to affect fetal development. 

## Alleviation of hypertension and urine albumin
We investigated the appropriate dosage of L-NAME (0–200 mg/kg) in normal pregnant ICR mice through abdominal subcutaneous injection (SI) for 7 days ( A). Preliminary experiments strongly revealed that the severity of hypertension, urinary protein levels, and glomerular atrophy become more severe with increasing dosage, with 100 mg/kg L-NAME identified as the appropriate dose for subsequent in vivo experiments ( B–S6D). Subsequently, in vivo efficacies of RNP and CRNP were assessed in ICR PE models ( A). Compared to the non-treatment, RGZ administered through gavage and RNP administered through tail VI significantly diminished hypertension, urinary protein levels, and glomerular atrophy, thereby preventing PE progression ( B–5H and 5J). Placenta-targeting CRNP exhibited better effects than non-targeted RGZ and RNP. ASP as a prophylactic agent for PE did not demonstrate superior efficacy compared to CRNP. Similarly, in the treatment of ICR PE models, RGZ and RNP could effectively alleviate the symptoms, as previously mentioned. Consistently, placenta-targeting CRNP demonstrated the best therapeutic effects except for the relief of systolic blood pressure ( E–5I and 5K). Consequently, as a clinical anti-hypertensive drug for PE, LBT exhibited the best anti-hypertensive effect. However, PBS/BO and NP could not significantly improve these symptoms as illustrated previously ( A–S7D, A–S8D, A, and A). These results strongly displayed that placenta-targeting CRNP could effectively alleviate PE-like symptoms in mice models. 

## Improvement of gestational outcomes
Subsequently, the gestational outcomes of RNP and CRNP were evaluated in ICR PE models. Treatment with RNP and RGZ could significantly increase fetal weight and length to prevent PE progression and restore damaged placental zones but exhibited no effect on placental weight and diameter ( A, 6C–6G, and 6M–6O). Although RGZ and RNP treatment did not demonstrate a statistically significant difference in fetal absorbance rate (AR), a declining trend was still observed. The greatest increase in fetal weight and length occurred with placenta-targeting CRNP treatment. ASP did not indicate a better effect than CRNP. Correspondingly, a similar improvement in fetal weight, fetal length, and placental damage was exhibited in the therapeutic models ( B, 6H–6L, and 6N–6P). CRNP exhibited the best therapeutic effect. However, some effective improvement was not observed in PBS/BO and NP, except for lower fetal and placental weight and shorter fetal length ( A–S9F, A–S10F, B, S11C, B, and S12C). These results strongly demonstrated that placenta-targeting CRNP can effectively improve gestational outcomes of ICR PE models. 

## eNOS, PPARγ, PGC1α, and UCP2 in PE placentas from clinical and mice samples
The placenta-targeting therapy of CRNP exhibited potential pharmacological effects for PE; however, the aforementioned results focused on phenotypic changes in pregnant mouse models. Herein, we examined the protein expression of different proteins, including eNOS, PPARγ, PGC1α, and UCP2, through tissue chip fluorescence staining. Fluorescence images from clinical samples revealed that eNOS and PPARγ dysregulation in PE groups was more significant than those in the normal pregnancy (NP) groups ( A–S13D and A–S14D). Although dysregulation grade was observed, PGC1α expression was non-significant, and the UCP2 expression exhibited no significant change ( A–S15D and A–S16D). These results were due to few clinical samples and substantial heterogeneity from human samples. Besides, fluorescence staining from mouse placentas demonstrated that RGZ, RNP, CRNP, ASP, and LBT can significantly increase the protein expression in ICR PE models, and placenta-targeting CRNP exhibited the best effects ( A–7J). However, PBS/BO and NP revealed no effects on these proteins ( A–S17D, A–S18D, A–S19D, and A–S20D). These results revealed that CRNP can partly increase the eNOS level and alleviate the PE statement through the PPARγ/PGC1α/UCP2 pathway. 

## Toxicity on the blood system and different organs and fetuses
The toxicity of RNP and CRNP was assessed in pregnant mice treated with a high dose of RNP and CRNP (400 mg/kg, 10-fold higher than the normal treatment dose) through VI ( A). During the injection, all pregnant mice behaved normally and did not have preterm or obstructed labor. Subsequently, at G17.5d, the blood and organs were obtained, tested using a blood detector, and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H/E) staining. Subsequently, blood tests revealed that no significant change in blood composition, blood biochemistry, coagulation function, or pregnant hormone in high-dose groups was observed compared to normal groups ( B–8M). H/E staining revealed that no abnormal structure and deformities were observed in these organs and fetuses when compared to NP groups ( N–8P). Therefore, these experiments revealed that different RNP and CRNP treatments administered through VI to pregnant mice models were safe, and no obvious side effects were observed in pregnant mice or their fetuses. 

## Discussion
Recent clinical treatment primarily aims to alleviate PE symptoms but is ineffective in enhancing placental narrow spiral arteries and maternal systemic vasospasm. Excessive ROS production induced by maternal endothelial damage and systemic vasospasm are distinct features of PE. , As a result, antioxidant medications that target excessive oxidative stress may be a potential therapeutic strategy for PE. While RGZ, a PPARγ agonist, has been reported to reduce ROS levels in PE explants, its in vivo antioxidant efficacy remains unclear, and its clinical application is limited by its strong polarity and non-targeting. Furthermore, conventional oral administration complicates the assurance of localized drug concentration. The tumor-targeting peptide CGKRK can selectively target placental tissues in humans and mice, providing a robust option for placenta targeting. Based on the effective drug delivery system, we developed placenta-targeting RGZ-loaded lipid nanoparticles CRNP using the modified method. These nanoparticles especially accumulate in placentas affected by PE without affecting other maternal organs and fetuses, and they effectively mitigate the elevated ROS level, thereby improving pregnant outcomes in ICR PE models.
Currently, PPARs are essential for placental trophoblasts, and PPARs dysfunctions can cause several pregnancy diseases, including gestational diabetes mellitus, PE, and intrauterine growth restriction. Our previous study focused on the association between PPARα and lipid metabolism induced by paternal hyperglycemia in the liver of offspring. The therapeutic effect of PPARγ activated by RGZ in PE is unknown and may be a potential target for PE. In our previous experiments, a significant reduction in PPARγ expression in PE placentas was significantly observed through the tissue chip immunofluorescence staining. This observation that reduced placental PPARγ is negatively correlated with 24 h urinary protein, serum creatinine, and systolic and diastolic blood pressure of patients with PE while exhibiting a significantly positive correlation with fetal size based on an examination of placental tissues from 90 patients with PE and 60 healthy pregnant women, is reported by Yang Y et al. And the study result that the administration of PPARγ antagonist (T0070907) in healthy pregnant mice inhibited the expression of PPARγ in the placenta, leading to PE-like symptoms, including hypertension, proteinuria, intrauterine growth restriction, vascular dysfunction, and increased platelet aggregation in pregnant mice, is also displayed by McCarthy FP et al. The prepared RNP and CRNP can deliver an effective nano-antioxidant treatment in preeclamptic cell models and ICR mice. Our study demonstrated that RGZ-loaded RNP and CRNP are essential in mitigating PE-related oxidative stress. In vitro, RNP positively influenced ROS and enhanced cellular functions in HTR-8/Svneo and HUVEC cells induced by H2O2, without significant cytotoxicity observed at elevated concentrations. In vivo, we demonstrated that L-NAME-induced pregnant mice can inhibit eNOS expression, reduce nitric oxide production, and elicit PE-like symptoms. , , Calreticulin derived from placental tissues is an effective target for the tumor-targeting peptide CGKRK. CRNP can specifically bind to placental tissues, increase the PPARγ expression, diminish ROS levels, alleviate PE symptoms, and enhance pregnant outcomes without side effects on the maternal organs circulatory system, placental function, or fetal growth and development.
PPARγ is a fatty acid-activated nuclear receptor highly expressed in adipose tissue. Additionally, it is strongly expressed in healthy human placental STBs and extracellular trophoblasts, playing a crucial role in embryo implantation, endometrial remodeling, trophoblast physiological changes, and oxidation balance. , , In addition, the low expression of placental PPARγ can cause a decrease in the antioxidant capacity of various antioxidant enzyme systems, subsequently resulting in heightened oxidative stress in PE. PGC1α binds to PPARγ, forming a PPARγ-PGC1α complex that enhances mitochondrial transcription factor A expression. Furthermore, mitochondrial DNA assembly, stability, and replication functions are regulated. , PGC1α mRNA expression was also significantly reduced in the placental villi of patients with PE. The PPARγ-PGC1α complex can directly activate mitochondrial UCP2 promoters through E-Box elements, thereby diminishing the mitochondrial proton pump gradient, local oxygen consumption, and ROS production. , , , 
In vitro studies on placental villi from patients with PE. Reduced expression of the placental PPARγ/UCP2 pathway can significantly induce mitochondrial dysfunction and oxidative stress damage in placental trophoblasts. Consequently, given the significant role of PPARγ on the antioxidant oxidase function of placental tissue, PPARγ may serve as an effective antioxidant therapeutic target, essential for the prevention and treatment of progressive PE.
Various nanoparticles were synthesized for PE-targeted treatment in previous studies. , , , Most of them encapsulated efficient nucleic acid molecules in nanoparticles, which exhibit reduced stability in solution, increased synthesis complexity, and higher cost than chemical drug molecules. RGZ, one of PPARγ agonists, is stable, cheap, and commonly used in other PE research. However, the hydrophobicity and non-targeting of RGZ require resolution. As a consequence, we rationally encapsulated RGZ into CRNP, which effectively activated placental PPARγ and enhanced PE pregnancy outcomes in L-NAME-induced ICR mice as described in the aforementioned experiments. Besides, placental chondroitin sulfate A (plCSA) was altered on various nanoparticles for targeted delivery to the placenta in other studies. The safety of this peptide in fetuses has not been experimentally investigated. However, the placenta-targeting peptide CGKRK on CRNP was detected in pregnant mice without any discernible effects on the fetal mice. Our nano-drug may have unknown limitations, including long-term side effects on offspring and possible off-target effects on placentas. Therefore, additional long-term experiments are required to address these concerns before clinical applications. Furthermore, all PE mouse models fail to simulate real disease conditions, including the L-NAME-induced PE model. Despite their closer relevance to patient pathogenesis, humanized models, including the tissue organoid model and monkey model, are constrained in their application of these models in basic and clinical research due to their high costs and complex experimental procedures.
In conclusion, a new placenta-targeting treatment utilizing CRNP lipid nanoparticles has been developed to enhance the prevention and treatment of progressive PE. Initially, we prepared placenta-targeting lipid nanoparticles by binding CGKRK peptide to the carboxyl groups of DSPE-PEG2000. We demonstrated that CRNP mitigated placental excessive oxidative stress-induced high ROS levels and improved maternal and fetal outcomes in L-NAME-induced pregnant ICR mice by simultaneously up-regulating PPARγ, PGC1α, and UCP2. The administration of RGZ in CRNP binding to placental trophoblasts is safe for maternal and fetal health and is expected to be a new strategy for PE drug control.

## Limitations of the study
Long-term experiments of CRNP for maternal and fetal influence in pregnant mice are difficult to get scientific research funding support for the time being. And PE mouse models induced by L-NAME fail completely to simulate real disease conditions. What’s worse, the cost of humanized models, including the tissue organoid model and monkey model, is too high for our research group to maintain.

## Experimental model and subject details

## Placental villus tissues from normal and preeclampsic pregnant women
3 normal pregnant women and 3 preeclampsic pregnant women were recruited within an age of 25–30, the average age was 27.3 years. All volunteers of this study were female and Han Chinese from Chong, China. All placental villus tissues (1 × 1 × 1cm3) were collected under ethics approved by the First Affiliated Hospital of Chongqing Medical University (No. 2020 − 41), and written informed consent for participation was gained from all participants. All placental samples were used for immunofluorescence staining with eNOS, PPARγ, PGC1α and UCP2 antibodies. Normal samples and preeclampsic samples were used for negative contral and positive experiment.

## Animal
Female ICR mice (female and male, 8–10weeks old) were purchased from Ensiweier, Chongqing, China and housed with free access to standard water and food. All mice experiments were approved by the Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Chongqing Medical University (Approval number: 2021-645).

## Method details

## Preparation and characterizations of different lipid nanoparticles
The film and extrusion method was used to prepare null-loaded lipid nanoparticles (NPs), RGZ-loaded lipid nanoparticles (RNPs), CGKRK-targeting RGZ-loaded lipid nanoparticles (CRNPs), and Cy5-RNP/CRNP. Briefly, 200 mg DSPE-PEG2000 (or 220 mg DSPE-PEG2000 CGKRK), 150 mg SL, and 50 mg CL (or 20 mg RGZ or 2 mg Cy5) were completely dissolved in 20 mL CF to prepare basic NP, RNP, CRNP, and Cy5-RNP/CRNP. Subsequently, a dynamic light scattering analyzer (DLS) detected the particle size and potential of NP, RNP, and CRNP. A transmission electron microscope (TME) was used to examine the lipid vesicle structure of RNP and CRNP. An ultraviolet spectrograph (UVS) was used to confirm the chemical linkage between DSPE-PEG2000 and CGKRK on CRNP. An ultraviolet spectrophotometer was used to determine the drug loading capacity of RGZ in RNP and CRNP. Subsequently, RNP and CRNP were kept in double distilled water (DDW), 1× phosphate buffer solution (PBS), fetal bovine serum (FBS), and RPMI1640 medium for 0–24 h, and a dynamic light scattering analyzer was used to detect the stability of RNP and CRNP.

## Cell culture, cytotoxicity, and cellular uptake ability
The human placental trophoblast cell line (HTR-8/Svneo) and human umbilical vein endothelial cell line (HUVEC) were obtained from the Chongqing Key Laboratory of Maternal-fetal Medicine, the First Affiliated Hospital of Chongqing Medical University. These cells were cultured in complete RPMI 1640 medium (10% FBS and 1% P/S) at 37°C and 5% CO2. For cytotoxicity, HTR-8/Svneo or HUVEC (1 × 105/well) seeded in 96-well plates were treated with different H2O2 (0–1024 μM), DMSO (0%–100%), RGZ (0–1024 μg/mL), ASP (0–1024 μg/mL), LBT (0–1024 μg/mL), and RNP (0–1024 μg/mL) RPMI 1640 complete medium for 24 h incubation. Subsequently, the CCK-8 agent was added and incubated for 1 h. A microplate reader was used to detect the cell viability. For cellular uptake, HTR-8/Svneo or HUVEC (3 × 105/well) seeded in 24-well plates were treated with Cy5-RNP (400 μg/mL) complete medium and incubated for 8, 6, 4, 2, 1, 0.5, and 0 h. These cells were digested with 0.25% trypsin and collected to detect the fluorescence intensity using a BD flow cytometer (FCM).

## Cellular ROS level
HTR-8/Svneo or HUVEC (3 × 105/well) seeded in 24-well plates were treated with DMSO (1%), RGZ (30 μg/mL, 1% DMSO), NP (400 μg/mL), RNP (400 μg/mL), and ASP (30 μg/mL, 1% DMSO) complete medium for 24 h. These cells were treated with 50 or 100 μM H2O2 in complete medium for 24 h to detect ROS levels for prevention. HTR-8/Svneo or HUVEC (3 × 105/well) seeded in 24-well plates were treated with 50 or 100 μM H2O2 complete medium. These cells were treated with DMSO (1%), RGZ (30 μg/mL, 1% DMSO), NP (400 μg/mL), RNP (400 μg/mL), and LBT (30 μg/mL, 1% DMSO) in complete medium for 24 h to detect ROS levels for rescue.

## Cell migration
HTR-8/Svneo or HUVEC (1 × 104/well) seeded in migratory plug-ins were treated with DMSO (1%), RGZ (30 μg/mL, 1% DMSO), NP (400 μg/mL), RNP (400 μg/mL), and ASP (30 μg/mL, 1% DMSO) complete medium for 24 h. After removing these plug-ins, these cells were treated with 50 or 100 μM H2O2 in complete medium for 24 h to detect migratory area for prevention. HTR-8/Svneo or HUVEC (1 × 104/well) migratory plug-ins were treated with 50 or 100 μM H2O2 in complete medium for 24 h. Subsequently, these cells were treated with DMSO (1%), RGZ (30 μg/mL, 1% DMSO), NP (400 μg/mL), RNP (400 μg/mL), and LBT (30 μg/mL, 1% DMSO) in complete medium for 24 h to detect migratory area for rescue.

## Cell invasion
HTR-8/Svneo (4 × 104/well) seeded in 24-well plates were treated with DMSO (1%), RGZ (30 μg/mL, 1% DMSO), NP (400 μg/mL), RNP (400 μg/mL), and ASP (30 μg/mL, 1% DMSO) complete medium for 24 h. These cells were transferred into a transwell with 50 μL of Matrigel (1:9) and treated with 50 or 100 μM H2O2 in complete medium for 24 h to detect invasive cell numbers for prevention. HTR-8/Svneo (4 × 104/well) seeded in 24-well plates were treated with 50 or 100 μM H2O2 in complete medium for 24 h. These cells were transferred into a transwell with 50 μL of Matrigel (1:9) and treated with DMSO (1%), RGZ (30 μg/mL, 1% DMSO), NP (400 μg/mL), RNP (400 μg/mL), and ASP (30 μg/mL, 1% DMSO) for 24 h to detect invasive cell numbers for rescue.

## Tube formation
HUVEC (1 × 105/well) seeded in 24-well plates were treated with DMSO (1%), RGZ (30 μg/mL, 1% DMSO), NP (400 μg/mL), RNP (400 μg/mL), and ASP (30 μg/mL, 1% DMSO) complete medium for 24 h. These cells were transferred into a 96-well plates with 50 μL primary Matrigel and treated with 50 or 100 μM H2O2 in complete medium for 24 h to detect tube formation for prevention. HUVEC (1 × 105/well) seeded in 24-well plates were treated with 50 or 100 μM H2O2 in complete medium for 24 h. These cells were transferred into a 96-well plates with 50 μL primary Matrigel and treated with DMSO (1%), RGZ (30 μg/mL, 1% DMSO), NP (400 μg/mL), RNP (400 μg/mL), and ASP (30 μg/mL, 1% DMSO) for 24 h to detect tube formation for rescue.

## Animal feeding and L-NAME-induced non-pregnant ICR mice
The local ethical committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Chongqing Medical University approved all animal studies. All ICR mice were procured from Ensiweier, Chongqing. After 3 days of acclimatization, male (10 weeks) and female (8 weeks) ICR mice were used for copulation (F/M ratio 3:2) in the same cage at noon (G0.5d). On the following day, vaginal plugs were observed to identify fertilized female mice. Subsequently, non-pregnant female ICR mice were continuously injected with 0, 50, 100, and 200 mg/kg L-NAME liquid (1× PBS) through subcutaneous injection (SI) for 7 days. Each mouse was housed alone in a metabolic cage for urine collection and only given standard water for 24 h. The urine was centrifuged at 4,000 g at 4°C for 15 min. The upper urine was stored at −80°C for subsequent urine albumin measurement using a BCA protein kit and a microplate reader. All mice were measured using a blood pressure 2000 system for systolic pressure detection. After all mice were sacrificed through cervical dislocation, their kidneys were carefully harvested, fixed in 4% PFA, and stained with the H/E staining. Inverted fluorescence microscope (IFM) was used to photograph all the H/E images.

## Placenta-targeting experiment of RNP and CRNP in NP ICR mice
1× PBS, Cy5-RNP (40 mg/kg), and Cy5-CRNP (40 mg/kg) were injected through tail vein injection (VI) into non-pregnant mice at G16.5d. At 0, 2, 4, 6, 8, and 24 h, the blood was collected through tail truncation under 2% isoflurane. Subsequently, all mice were sacrificed through cervical dislocation, and their hearts, livers, spleens, lungs, and kidneys were obtained for fluorescence pictures using a Living body imager. Finally, all placentas were fixed in optimal cutting temperature compound, sliced for 5 μm, and pictures were captured using IFM.

## Safety assessment of RNP and CRNP in NP ICR mice
1× PBS, 10× RNP (400 mg/kg), and 10× CRNP (400 mg/kg) were injected into normal pregnant ICR mice at G8.5d, G10.5d, G12.5d, G14.5d, and G16.5d, respectively. During the injection, the behavior of these injected pregnant mice was observed. At G17.5d, the blood was collected in EDTA anticoagulant tubes, sodium citrate anticoagulant tubes, and non-anti-coagulant tubes through eyeball extraction under 2% isoflurane and the levels of blood routine, blood biochemical, procoagulant and hormone were detected. These pregnant mice were sacrificed through cervical dislocation, and their hearts, livers, spleens, lungs, kidneys, small intestines, unimplanted uteruses, ovaries, brains, placentas, and fetuses were obtained completely, fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA), and stained with H/E, and their pictures were captured using IFM.

## Administration of RNP and CRNP in L-NAME-induced PE models
All pregnant mice were injected with 100 mg/kg L-NAME liquid (1× PBS) through the abdominal SI at G7.5d-G13.5d. For preventing and treating progressive PE, at G11.5d-G13.5d, detailed groups and administration were as follows: NP group; model group; model + PBS/BO group, VI/gavage; model + RGZ group, 3 mg/kg/day, benne oil (BO)/gavage; model + NP group, 40 mg/kg/d, VI; model + RNP, 40 mg/kg/d, VI; model + CRNP, 40 mg/kg/d, VI; model + ASP, 3 mg/kg/d, BO/gavage; n = 4, each group. For therapy, at G14.5d-G16.5d, detailed groups and administration were as follows: NP group; model group; model + PBS/BO group, VI/gavage; model + RGZ group, 3 mg/kg/d, BO/gavage; model + NP group, 40 mg/kg/d, IV; model + RNP, 40 mg/kg/d, VI; model + CRNP, 40 mg/kg/d, VI; model + LBT, 3 mg/kg/d, BO/gavage; n = 4, each group.

## Urine albumin, blood pressure, placental and fetal growth measurement
At G16.5d, each pregnant mouse in these groups was housed in a metabolic cage alone and only given standard water for 24 h for urine collection. The collected urine was centrifuged at 4,000 g at 4°C for 15 min, and the upper urine was stored at −80°C for subsequent urine albumin measurement using the BCA protein kit and the microplate reader. At G17.5d, the systolic, diastolic, and mean arterial pressure of these pregnant mice were measured using the blood pressure 2000 system. At G17.5d, all pregnant mice were euthanized through cervical dislocation. All placentas and fetuses were obtained, washed with 1× PBS, dried with gauze, lined up on A4 paper, and photographed using a shadowless lamp box. The weight, length, diameter, and absorption rate of these placentas and fetuses were counted, respectively. The absorbance rate (AR) formula was: AR = alive fetuses number/Total fetuses number X 100%.

## HE and fluorescent staining of different organs from ICR PE models
Fresh kidneys, placentas, and fetuses were fixed in 4% PFA, embedded in paraffin, sliced into 5 μm sections, stained using H/E staining, and photographed using FSIM. Different placental sections were dewaxed, repaired, membrane-ruptured, sealed, and incubated with eNOS (anti-mouse, 1:300), PPARγ (anti-rabbit, 1:300), PGC1α (anti-mouse, 1:300) and UCP2 (anti-rabbit, 1:300) antibodies and FITC-secondary antibodies (anti-mouse/rabbit, 1:2000) and photographed using IFM.

## HE and fluorescent staining of human placental tissues from clinical PE samples
After informed consent was obtained, all clinical placental tissues were obtained from normal pregnant (NP) women (37–42 weeks) and patients with PE (early stage: 31–34 weeks; late stage: 34–37 weeks) booked for cesarean in the Department of Obstetrics, the First Clinical College Affiliated of Chongqing Medical University. PE inclusion criteria included: 1. Patients with systolic blood pressure ≥140 mmHg or diastolic blood pressure ≥110 mmHg after 20 weeks of gestation, measured thrice; patients with 24-h urine protein >300 mg, or urine protein/urine creatinine ≥0.3, or routine urine protein ≥3 ++, or other manifestations, such as platelet count <100 × 109/L, serum creatinine >97 μmol/L, ALT >2 times, pulmonary edema. The exclusion criteria included patients with gestational infection, gestational diabetes, chronic hypertension, immune diseases, and other pregnancy complications. Subsequently, these placental tissues were washed in 1× PBS, carefully selected, dried at 4°C, fixed in 4% PFA, made into tissue chips, stained with eNOS (anti-mouse, 1:300), PPARγ (anti-rabbit, 1:300), PGC1α (anti-mouse, 1:300), and UCP2 (anti-mouse, 1:300), and green/orange/red/pink IF-secondary antibodies (anti-goat, 1:1000) and photographed using IFM.

## Quantification and statistical analysis
All experiments were repeated at least three times. Statistical Package for the Social Sciences software (version 22.0) was used for data analysis. Data are presented as mean ± SEM and independent-sample t-tests were used for group comparisons. Statistical significance is also reported in Figure Legends and Supplementary Information Legends. (∗p < 0.05, ∗∗p < 0.01, and ∗∗∗p < 0.001 were considered statistically significant; ns: no significance.)
