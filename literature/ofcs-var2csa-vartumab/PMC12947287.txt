Liquid biopsy has emerged as a transformative alternative, offering a minimally invasive approach for acquiring tumor-related information through blood samples. This technique can be performed repeatedly during treatment, enabling continuous monitoring of tumor progression. Liquid biopsies evaluate key biomarkers, including circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA), extracellular vesicles (EVs), and CTCs, for early diagnosis, prognostic assessment, and therapeutic monitoring [ ]. ctDNA, a subset of cell-free DNA (cfDNA), is released by tumors into the bloodstream and reflects tumor-specific genetic alterations [ ]. Its ability to provide comprehensive genetic information makes it an invaluable tool for tracking tumor evolution and resistance mechanisms. Ranging in size from 30 to 2,000 nm, EVs are broadly categorized into two groups: extracellular bodies and exosomes. They carry molecular cargo, including proteins, lipids, and nucleic acids, and provide valuable insights into cellular communication and tumor progression [ ].
CTCs are intact cancer cells shed from primary or metastatic tumors into the bloodstream [ ]. These nucleated cells, typically larger than 4 μm, are distinguished by their lack of leukocyte-specific antigen CD45 and their expression of epithelial markers such as epithelial cell adhesion molecule (EpCAM) and cytokeratins 8, 18, and 19 [ ]. However, the study of CTCs is challenging due to their extreme rarity, with only one CTC typically found per 109 blood cells in patients with metastatic cancer [ ]. Despite these challenges, CTCs are emerging as robust biomarkers, offering a minimally invasive approach for early tumor detection, metastasis assessment, prognostic evaluation, tumor heterogeneity characterization, and personalized treatment [ ], By leveraging these biomarkers, liquid biopsy represents a paradigm shift in oncology, offering dynamic, real-time insights into tumor biology while reducing the risks associated with traditional invasive diagnostic techniques. Notably, as intact cellular entities, CTCs provide a dynamic and comprehensive window into tumor biology. They enable not only real-time monitoring of disease progression and response to therapy but also serve as direct targets for detailed molecular phenotyping and functional characterization. Consequently, this review specifically aims to elucidate how Raman imaging technology can critically empower CTCs research by enhancing detection sensitivity, resolving cellular heterogeneity, and enabling non-destructive molecular analysis. We posit that this synergistic approach is pivotal for translating liquid biopsy from a promising concept into a robust tool for precision oncology, ultimately guiding personalized therapeutic strategies.
The Raman effect arises from the inelastic scattering of light, enabling the direct detection of vibrational and rotational states within molecules and materials [ ]. This technique facilitates the acquisition of detailed chemical and biological information from both living and stationary samples [ ]. Raman spectroscopy (RS) is a label-free, chemoselective hyperspectral technique, offering exceptional sensitivity and specificity in molecular characterization. The advancement of RS has had a profound impact on biological and medical research, particularly in areas such as oncology and molecular diagnostics [ ]. As a reliable and non-invasive method, Raman spectroscopy holds significant promise for applications in liquid biopsy, providing high-resolution molecular insights that support cancer detection and monitoring [ ].

2.

## CTCs: from molecular mechanisms to clinical applications
CTCs are defined as malignant cells that intravasate into the circulatory system following detachment from a primary tumor or metastatic deposit. Their subsequent survival, transit through the vasculature, and potential to seed distant sites constitute a multifaceted process—often termed the metastatic cascade—which is characterized by intricate biological adaptations and profound inter- and intra-patient heterogeneity.
2.1.

## Origin, survival, and migration mechanisms of CTCs
In 1869, Thomas Ashworth, an Australian physician, first identified CTCs in the blood of patients with metastatic cancer. These cells exhibited morphological similarities to primary tumor cells [ ]. CTCs can exist in various forms, including single cells, homotypic clusters (aggregates of cancer cells), or heterotypic clusters (aggregates of cancer cells with non-tumor cells, such as platelets, neutrophils, and fibroblasts) [ ]. Circulating tumor cells face multiple stresses in the circulatory system, including blood flow shear stress, apoptosis due to loss of anchoring, and immune clearance. Less than 0.01% survive to metastasize [ , , ]. However, a fraction of CTCs evade apoptosis by upregulating several survival pathways, such as increasing growth factor secretion, downregulating death receptors, and overexpressing anti-apoptotic ligands [ ].
During the progression and invasion of malignant tumors into neighboring tissues, tumor cells secrete matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs), which degrade the basement membrane. This degradation facilitates the entry of tumor cells into the bloodstream or lymphatic vessels, enabling them to circulate as CTCs until they reach and invade distant tissues [ ]. The metastatic process involves a cascade of endocytosis, translocation, exocytosis, and colonization, allowing cancer cells to migrate and establish secondary tumors in distant organs [ ]. During metastasis, circulating tumor cells undergo epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT), acquiring migratory and invasive capabilities. They achieve extravasation and implantation through interactions with endothelial cells [ ]. Szczerba et al. analyzed blood samples from 70 patients with invasive breast cancer and observed the presence of CTC-white blood cell (WBC) clusters across all mouse models tested (including immunodeficient mice). These clusters accounted for 0.05–61% of the total CTCs population and were characterized by elevated levels of Ki67 expression in CTCs associated with neutrophils. This finding suggests that neutrophils directly interact with CTCs to support cell cycle progression in circulation and enhance metastatic seeding [ ].
Hypoxia-inducible factor 1α (HIF1α) plays a critical role in this process by upregulating adhesion molecules such as L1 cell adhesion molecule (L1CAM) and CXC-chemokine receptor 4 (CXCR4). These molecules enhance CTC-endothelial binding and endocytosis, with L1CAM signaling mediated through angiopoietin-like protein 4 (ANGPTL4) [ ].
Studies have also revealed that CC chemokine ligand 5 (CCL5) is overexpressed in CTCs, and its expression is transcriptionally regulated by p38-MAX signaling. This pathway promotes immune evasion by recruiting regulatory T cells (Tregs), thereby facilitating metastatic seeding and tumor progression [ ]. Furthermore, transcriptome analyses indicate a significant downregulation of key oncogenes in CTCs, including MKI67 (Ki-67), MYC (c-Myc), and CTNNB1 (β-catenin). Similarly, epithelial markers such as EpCAM are downregulated during EMT, underscoring the plasticity of CTCs during the metastatic cascade [ ].

2.2.

## Phenotypic and molecular heterogeneity of CTCs
CTCs can be phenotypically classified into epithelial-type (ECTC), mesenchymal-type (MCTC), and epithelial-mesenchymal mixed-type (EMCTC) [ ], exhibiting high heterogeneity in surface marker expression, transcriptome, and epigenetic characteristics [ ]. This heterogeneity is closely associated with tumor progression, treatment resistance, and prognosis. For instance, mesenchymal-type CTCs may correlate with stronger metastatic potential, while epithelial-type CTCs hold greater prognostic value in certain cancer types [ ]. Sun et al. performed single-cell RNA sequencing of CTCs from multiple vascular sites in hepatocellular carcinoma patients, revealing significant intravascular and intervascular heterogeneity at the transcriptomic level among CTCs of different vascular origins [ ]. DNA methylation plays a crucial role in immune surveillance and metastasis of CTCs [ ]. Concurrently, genome-wide DNA methylation analysis also indicates that CTCs exhibit epigenetic heterogeneity [ ]. Furthermore, Su et al. traced copy number alterations (CNAs) in allele-specific individual CTCs isolated at different time points during chemotherapy and found that CNA heterogeneity may arise from the loss of the original CNA allele [ ].

2.3.

## Current status of CTCs enrichment and detection technologies
Positive enrichment techniques for CTCs can be broadly categorized into two types: label-free enrichment (independent of marker expression) and marker-dependent enrichment (targeting specific cell surface markers) [ , ]. These enrichment strategies leverage either the physical properties of CTCs—such as size, density, charge, and deformability—or their biological characteristics ( ), such as the expression of surface markers like EpCAM (used for positive selection) and CD45 (used for negative selection) [ , , , ].
 The Isolation by Size of Epithelial Tumor Cells (ISET) system was the first assay to enable the direct filtration of peripheral blood for CTCs enrichment. This method employs a cylindrical pore polycarbonate filter membrane with a pore diameter of 8 μm to separate CTCs from smaller blood cells, facilitating their detection and analysis [ ]. Another commonly used technique is the OncoQuick system, which utilizes density gradient centrifugation to enhance the enrichment of tumor cells while depleting mononuclear cells (MNCs) from the blood. This method optimizes the detection of disseminated tumor cells and improves the sensitivity of CTCs isolation [ ].
The CellSearch® system is the first and only FDA-approved system for CTCs detection, with a reported sensitivity of ≥85% [ ]. This system isolates CTCs by leveraging anti-EpCAM antibody-coupled ferromagnetic fluids and rare-earth magnets. CTCs are defined as anti-cytokeratin-positive and anti-CD45-negative cells. Once isolated, the cells are washed and incubated with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) and fluorescently labeled anti-cytokeratin and anti-CD45 antibodies. The prepared samples are then transferred to a viewing chamber within the CellTracks magnetic probe bar, where CTCs are counted using a semi-automated fluorescence microscope [ ].
Despite its utility, the CellSearch system may have limitations in cases where EpCAM expression is low, potentially reducing detection sensitivity. To address this, the MagSweeper system incorporates EpCAM-modified immunomagnetic beads, enabling the isolation of circulating endothelial progenitor cells (CEpCs) with low to moderate EpCAM expression [ ]. However, CTCs detection in pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) remains particularly challenging due to the extremely low concentration of CTCs in circulation. To improve sensitivity in this context, Stoecklein et al. employed diagnostic leukapheresis (DLA), which significantly enhanced the identification of patients with high-risk PDAC [ ].

2.4.

## Clinical Application of CTCs in tumor diagnosis and treatment
2.4.1.

## Early diagnosis of tumors
Early cancer detection is crucial for reducing patient mortality. CTCs have recently emerged as reliable biomarkers for the early diagnosis of various cancers. Studies have demonstrated that CTCs counts are significantly correlated with the standard Barcelona Clinical Liver Cancer (BCLC) stage and serum alpha-fetoprotein levels, highlighting their utility in the early detection of hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) [ ]. Another study showed that liquid biopsies based on CTCs analysis enabled both early diagnosis of HCC and tracking of molecular changes within the tumor [ ]. Furthermore, Tsai et al. demonstrated the efficacy of the CellMax test, which successfully detected precancerous adenomas (both advanced and non-advanced) as well as colorectal cancer (CRC) across stages I–IV, with high sensitivity and specificity [ ].

2.4.2.

## Tumor prognosis assessment
There is growing evidence that CTCs serve as effective prognostic markers in various cancers. For instance, CTCs testing has been shown to predict prognosis in lung cancer patients [ ]. In pancreatic cancer, prospective studies revealed that CTCs stratification could accurately predict overall survival (OS), progression-free survival (PFS) and identify patients likely to benefit from neoadjuvant therapy [ ]. Meta-analyses of patients with metastatic breast cancer (MBC) who received neoadjuvant chemotherapy (NCT) [ ] or first-line chemotherapy [ , ] found that the presence of CTCs was consistently associated with poor OS and PFS. Similarly, Radovich et al. conducted a study involving patients with early-stage triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) undergoing neoadjuvant chemotherapy. They found that combining CTCs analysis with ctDNA sequencing provided a robust assessment of recurrence risk [ ]. In metastatic castration-resistant prostate cancer (mCRPC), findings from the MAINSAIL phase III clinical trial suggested that elevated CTCs counts were linked to a poor prognosis in patients treated with docetaxel and prednisone, either with or without lenalidomide [ ].

2.4.3.

## Drug development and individualized therapy
CTCs play a pivotal role in the development of individualized tumor therapies. In a GOG-240 phase III randomized trial, CTCs counts were identified as a predictive biomarker for guiding antiangiogenic therapy in patients with advanced cervical cancer [ ]. Similarly, Pearl et al. demonstrated that invasive CTCs (iCTCs) were more sensitive than CA125 in assessing treatment efficacy in patients with epithelial ovarian cancer (EOC) undergoing standard paclitaxel/carboplatin therapy [ ]. CTC-derived cell lines have also been instrumental in understanding chemotherapy resistance. Grillet et al. established three CTCs cell lines (CTC41, CTC44, and CTC45) from patients with metastatic CRC. They found that these CTCs lines exhibited greater resistance to the standard chemotherapy combination of 5-fluorouracil (5-FU) and SN-38 (the active metabolite of irinotecan) compared to primary and metastatic tumor-derived cells grown under identical conditions [ ].
A phase III randomized clinical trial further demonstrated the prognostic value of CTCs counts in hormone receptor-positive, ERBB2-negative metastatic breast cancer. Patients in the CTC-stratified group showed a significant improvement in median progression-free survival (mPFS) compared to the standard group (15.5 months, 95% CI: 12.7–17.3 vs. 13.9 months, 95% CI: 12.2–16.3), suggesting that CTCs counts may serve as a reliable biomarker for selecting chemotherapy or single-agent endocrine therapy as first-line treatment [ ]. Lian et al. explored the use of a novel methodology targeting mouse breast cancer (4T1) cells to simultaneously block CD47 (“don’t eat me”) and CD274 (“don’t find me”) signaling pathways in CTCs. This specific antibody approach resulted in significant inhibition of tumor growth and metastasis in CTCs [ ]. Meng et al. provided additional evidence of CTCs plasticity, demonstrating that breast cancer CTCs from HER2-negative primary tumors could acquire HER2 gene amplification during disease progression [ ]. These findings were further supported by the DETECT III clinical study, which showed that patients with HER2-negative MBC harboring HER2-positive CTCs could benefit from HER2-targeted therapy. This underscores the utility of phenotypic analysis of CTCs for stratifying MBC patients to appropriate treatments [ ].
A novel concept, known as tumor “self-nesting”, has also emerged. This phenomenon involves the recruitment of CTCs back to previously resected primary tumor sites, leading to tumor recurrence, as well as their migration to identified metastatic lesions [ ]. Bioluminescence imaging (BLI) has demonstrated that engineered “self-nesting” CTCs, which co-express imaging reporter genes and therapeutic transgenes, can serve as innovative cellular vehicles for treating both primary tumors and disseminated metastases in preclinical breast cancer models [ ].

2.4.4.

## Micrometastasis monitoring
EMT is a complex biological process that facilitates increased motility and invasiveness of tumor cells. This involves disruption of cell-cell junctions, loss of epithelial cell morphology, and alterations in cell adhesion and polarity. EMT plays a pivotal role in the dissemination of cancer cells by enabling the release of CTCs from primary tumors and distant metastatic sites [ ]. During circulation, CTCs dynamically activate the EMT program, which enhances their survival and metastatic potential [ ]. Tumor cells establish tight interactions with the host organism via the bloodstream, lymphatic system, and nerve pathways. Through these connections, tumors release mediators that perturb systemic homeostasis and promote changes in distant tissues, contributing to the formation of pre-metastatic niches [ ].
Key cytokines, such as interleukin-6 (IL-6) and interleukin-8 (IL-8), have been implicated in tumorigenic processes, including cancer cell chemotaxis. Kim et al. demonstrated in mouse models that IL-6 and IL-8 secreted by tumor cells act as strong inducers of CTCs formation in breast cancer. Furthermore, the poor prognostic marker MMP1/collagenase-1 and the actin cytoskeleton protein fascin-1 were identified as mediators of CTCs infiltration into breast tumors [ ]. A prospective study in breast cancer patients identified five key markers associated with EMT in CTCs: Snail, N-cadherin, vimentin, EpCAM, and CK7. These findings suggest that a majority of heterogeneous CTCs phenotypes (22 of 24 detectable) exhibit significant epithelial-mesenchymal plasticity, reflecting their ability to adapt to various microenvironments during metastasis [ ].

## Raman imaging technology: Principles, frontiers, and comparative positioning
3.1.

## Fundamental principles of raman imaging: from spectra to imaging
Raman imaging is defined as a spatially resolved analytical technique that extends conventional Raman spectroscopy. It operates by acquiring spectra at each pixel across a sample, which are then compiled to generate pixelated chemical maps. This process allows for the label-free visualization and mapping of biochemical constituents based on their intrinsic molecular vibrations [ ]. The physical basis of this technology is the Raman scattering effect. When monochromatic laser light interacts with sample molecules, the vast majority of photons undergo elastic scattering (Rayleigh scattering), while approximately one in ten million photons undergo inelastic scattering, known as Raman scattering [ ]. During this process, photons exchange energy with the vibrational energy levels of molecules [ , ], resulting in a characteristic frequency shift of the scattered light. This shift corresponds one-to-one with the specific chemical bonds and vibrational modes of the molecule, thereby forming a unique “molecular fingerprint” spectrum [ ]. Various key biomolecules in biological samples, such as proteins (characteristic peaks like the amide I band and amide III band), nucleic acids (purine and pyrimidine ring vibrations), lipids (C-H stretching and bending vibrations), and carbohydrates, all exhibit characteristic peaks in Raman spectra. These peaks provide the basis for label-free chemical identification [ ].
Raman imaging is a spatially resolved chemical analysis technique derived from Raman spectroscopy. It advances beyond single-point analysis by systematically mapping a sample through point-by-point or line-scanning. The collected spectra are computationally reconstructed to generate two- or three-dimensional maps of chemical components, enabling the label-free visualization of a sample’s intrinsic biochemical composition and spatial heterogeneity [ ]. Therefore, Raman imaging merges the molecular fingerprinting capability of Raman spectroscopy with microscopic spatial resolution [ ]. This provides a powerful platform for directly revealing the chemical heterogeneity of biological specimens without the need for exogenous labels or extensive sample preparation. This unique capability confers a distinct advantage in liquid biopsy. For example, Raman imaging allows for the direct identification and spatial metabolic phenotyping of rare cells (e.g. circulating tumor cells) in blood based on their intrinsic biochemical fingerprints, providing a powerful tool for precise oncology research and diagnosis. With ongoing advancements, Raman imaging is establishing itself as a critical bridge linking molecular diagnostics with morphological analysis, showcasing significant potential in both preclinical and translational medicine [ ].

Notably, significant differences exist between normal and cancerous tissues, which can be optically detected through variations in Raman signal intensities, peak positions, and peak widths [ ]. These distinctions provide critical insights into the biochemical and structural changes associated with malignancies, making Raman spectroscopy a powerful tool for cancer diagnosis and analysis.
 
3.2.

## Typical Raman instrument configurations and parameter considerations
The unique capabilities of Raman imaging stem from its integration of the molecular fingerprinting inherent to Raman spectroscopy with high-resolution spatial visualization. To harness this potential effectively, careful instrumental configuration and optimization of key parameters are essential, as these factors directly govern spectral quality, spatial resolution, and practical applicability. A standard system for Raman spectral acquisition comprises several core components: an excitation laser source, a spectrometer, a sensitive detector (such as a CCD or CMOS camera), and specialized optics for coupling and filtering [ ]. The excitation source in Raman spectroscopy is typically a high-power laser selected for its chromatic purity. The choice of wavelength involves a critical balance between optimizing Raman scattering efficiency, minimizing autofluorescence background, and avoiding photothermal damage to the sample. For example, visible wavelengths such as 532 nm are often used for inorganic or non-fluorescing materials, whereas near-infrared lasers (e.g. 785 or 1064 nm) are preferred for biological specimens due to their superior ability to reduce fluorescence interference and lower photodamage risk [ ]. The core of a spectrometer is its dispersive element. The line density and focal length of the grating determine the system’s spectral resolution and coverage range [ ]. In terms of detectors, back-illuminated deep-depleted CCDs have become the mainstream choice due to their high quantum efficiency and low dark noise across the visible to near-infrared spectrum, while EMCCDs are suitable for detecting extremely faint signals [ ]. A confocal optical configuration is frequently employed to enhance axial resolution and reject out-of-focus background through the use of a spatial pinhole, albeit with an inherent trade-off in reduced signal collection efficiency. For optimal performance, a rigorous empirical optimization of system parameters is required. Acquisition settings (e.g. laser power, integration time) and optical conditions (e.g. objective numerical aperture, spectral calibration) must be jointly adjusted based on sample properties to balance the critical metrics of signal strength, signal-to-noise ratio, and spatial fidelity [ ].

3.3.

## Preprocessing and background subtraction methods for Raman spectra
Acquired Raman spectra are frequently contaminated by background artifacts arising from sample autofluorescence, instrumental noise (e.g. dark current), and cosmic ray events. Effective preprocessing is therefore essential to isolate and extract the intrinsic Raman bands for reliable analysis [ ]. Background subtraction is a critical preprocessing step. Among the most common techniques is polynomial fitting, which estimates the broad fluorescence background by fitting a low-order polynomial to the raw spectrum and subsequently subtracts this modeled baseline [ ]. As an advanced nonparametric technique, the airPLS algorithm performs background subtraction through adaptive iteration. Its core mechanism re-weights data points to progressively separate signal from background, concurrently applying a penalty term to ensure baseline smoothness. This makes it highly effective for processing Raman spectra with challenging fluorescence profiles [ ]. Additionally, the wavelet transform effectively separates high-frequency Raman signals from low-frequency background/noise through multiscale decomposition [ ]. Machine learning-based approaches are gaining widespread adoption, leveraging models such as neural networks to learn underlying patterns from large datasets and perform predictive deductions [ ]. After background subtraction, spectral smoothing is often performed to suppress random noise, followed by normalization (e.g. setting the total spectral area or specific peak intensity to 1) to facilitate comparisons between different spectra [ ].

3.4.

## Comparative analysis of raman imaging with other single-cell analysis techniques
Single-cell Raman spectroscopy constitutes the “data unit” of Raman imaging. Cells are the fundamental units of life, and studying individual differences at the single-cell level is essential for understanding cell differentiation, development, gene expression, and cellular functions. Single-cell analysis is particularly significant in cancer research, as it provides insights into tumor heterogeneity and therapeutic responses [ ]. Among single-cell technologies, single-cell Raman spectroscopy (SCRS) stands out as a non-invasive, label-free technique that allows for in vivo and multiparameter analysis of living cells. SCRS enables the detection of cellular composition, structure, and metabolic status with high spatial resolution [ ].
Raman-activated cell sorting (RACS) extends the utility of SCRS by characterizing phenotypic changes, physiological states, and functional properties of cells [ , ]. RACS can isolate rare cancer cells from heterogeneous populations, such as CTCs in blood samples. This facilitates early cancer detection and metastasis monitoring [ ].
Schie et al. proposed a high-throughput screening Raman spectroscopy (HTS-RS) platform that combines automated imaging microscopy with Raman spectroscopy. This platform can identify CTCs in a mixture of EpCAM-positive pancreatic cancer cells (Panc-1) and leukocytes ( ) and estimate the proportions of each cell type within the sample [ ].
 The central challenge in CTC research is the multidimensional characterization of inherently rare, heterogeneous, and dynamic live cells. Current single-cell technologies reflect diverse technological philosophies: high-throughput screening to locate these “needles in a haystack,” deep molecular profiling to elucidate mechanisms, or functional live-cell analysis to capture dynamics. Anchoring our analysis to the specific problem of “deep functional phenotyping of rare CTCs,” this section systematically compares Raman imaging with mainstream techniques (e.g. flow cytometry, single-cell sequencing). This comparison clarifies the multidimensional, complementary, and often synergistic role of Raman imaging within the existing technological ecosystem. ( ).
 

4.

## Raman spectroscopy for CTC heterogeneity and functional analysis
The heterogeneity of CTCs is a key factor driving tumor metastasis, drug resistance, and recurrence. Traditional detection technologies primarily rely on surface markers or gene expression profiles, making it challenging to achieve functional phenotyping analysis of live cells in a non-invasive, real-time manner. Leveraging its label-free nature, high chemical specificity, and spatial resolution, Raman imaging is emerging as a powerful tool for decoding CTCs heterogeneity and functional states. This section systematically reviews the latest advances and in-depth applications of Raman imaging in CTCs phenotype correlation, metabolic function, drug response, and dynamic monitoring.
4.1.

## Correlation between Raman spectral features and specific CTC phenotypes
Different CTCs phenotypes (epithelial, mesenchymal, and epithelial-mesenchymal mixed) exhibit significant biochemical composition differences. Raman spectroscopy, as a label-free tool, demonstrates potential in distinguishing CTCs with distinct EMT phenotypes. In Raman spectroscopic analysis, the signature bands for protein secondary structures are predominantly observed within the amide I (∼1650 cm−1) and amide III (∼1250 cm−1) spectral regions. Concurrently, nucleic acids exhibit characteristic vibrational modes, notably from the purine and pyrimidine ring structures, which are frequently detected near ∼785 cm−1 [ ]. Epithelial-type CTCs display marked heterogeneity in proliferative activity. Specific subpopulations—often characterized by a high nuclear-to-cytoplasmic ratio and Ki-67 positivity—possess elevated proliferative potential and are linked to an increased risk of tumor metastasis [ ]. Raman studies have confirmed that the signal intensity of nucleic acid characteristic peaks in the Raman spectra of highly proliferative cells is significantly enhanced, directly reflecting their more active nucleic acid synthesis state [ ]. Consequently, epithelial-type CTCs are expected to yield more intense nucleic acid-specific Raman bands compared to their mesenchymal or hybrid counterparts. This inference establishes a critical molecular foundation for achieving label-free phenotypic discrimination of CTCs via Raman imaging. In contrast, mesenchymal-type circulating tumor cells (M-CTCs) undergo epithelial-mesenchymal transition, characterized by a fundamental reorganization of the cytoskeletal and adhesion molecule expression profiles (e.g. downregulation of epithelial markers such as E-cadherin and keratin and upregulation of mesenchymal markers such as vimentin) [ ]. The Raman spectral profile of such cells is likely to display enhanced signatures associated with lipids. These include bands corresponding to CH2 bending and C = O stretching vibrations, which may reflect intracellular lipid droplet accumulation or alterations in membrane fluidity [ ]. The spectra of mixed-type CTCs may exhibit transitional or concurrent characteristics as described above, such as simultaneously displaying identifiable nucleic acid peaks alongside specific lipid peaks. The inherent spectral complexity itself may serve as an indicator for identifying this mixed phenotype.

4.2.

## Decoding biochemical features via Raman spectroscopy to characterize CTCs functional heterogeneity
Raman spectroscopy not only identifies phenotypic characteristics but also provides deeper insights into functional intracellular biochemical states. The specific molecular signatures it reveals are closely associated with key functional attributes of CTCs, including metastasis, stemness, and drug resistance. To begin with, high lipid content (characterized by peaks such as the CH2 deformation vibration at ∼1440 cm−1 and the C = C stretching vibration at ∼1654 cm−1) [ ] is a hallmark of more invasive and migratory CTCs. This is associated with increased cell membrane fluidity and energy reserves, which promote cell migration and colonization [ ]. The stemness marker CD133, which serves as a hallmark of metastasis-initiating circulating cancer stem cells, can be specifically targeted with SERS nanoprobes to generate a robust spectral peak at 985 cm−1. This forms the basis of a precise “spectral barcode,” thereby allowing for the accurate optical identification of this critical cell subpopulation [ ]. Additionally, changes in Raman fingerprint peaks of metabolites like glutamine and lactate serve as a proxy for the metabolic reprogramming (e.g. glycolysis [ ], glutamine [ ] metabolism) that fuels CTCs stemness and therapy resistance. This capability transforms Raman from a compositional tool into one that generates a dynamic biochemical portrait, thereby mapping molecular features directly onto metastatic phenotype and informing prognostic and therapeutic precision.

4.3.

## Application of single-cell Raman spectroscopy in dynamic monitoring of circulating tumor cells
Single-cell Raman spectroscopy typically contains over 1,000 Raman bands, which provide rich intrinsic information about the cell (e.g. nucleic acids, proteins, carbohydrates, and lipids), reflecting its genotype, phenotype, and physiological state [ ]. Berry et al. utilized single-cell Raman microspectroscopy coupled with deuterium isotope probing to enable in situ, label-free quantification of macromolecular anabolism—specifically of lipids, proteins, and nucleic acids—within individual cells. This approach establishes a fundamental methodological platform for real-time metabolic monitoring at the single-cell level [ ]. In a lung cancer model, the pro-invasive phenotype and associated glycolytic shift induced by hyperglycemia produce a detectable Raman spectral signature—marked by enhanced nucleic acid signals, altered protein conformation, and reshaped lipid profiles—which correlates significantly with increased proliferation and migration, offering a non-invasive metric for functional phenotyping [ ]. This suggests that without additional labeling, analyzing single-cell Raman spectra from CTCs alone may enable the identification of subpopulations exhibiting high glycolytic activity and strong metastatic potential due to their high-sugar microenvironment or intrinsic metabolic abnormalities. Furthermore, as a label-free optical technique, single-cell Raman spectroscopy has been directly applied to the precise assessment of in vitro anticancer drug sensitivity. Research indicates that combining single-cell Raman spectroscopy with machine learning can effectively identify drug-resistant phenotypes by decoding their distinct biochemical signatures. The sensitivity of this method allows it to detect the initial biochemical adaptations induced by low-dose treatments, which precede overt phenotypic changes [ ]. Circulating tumor cell clusters, which function as oligoclonal constellations of metastatic seeds, exhibit markedly greater metastatic competency than single CTCs and constitute a critical determinant of cancer prognosis [ ]. Stimulated Raman scattering (SRS) microscopy distinguishes itself among label-free techniques by enabling both real-time observation of cellular activities in live models and the detection of histopathological features like nuclear atypia in fixed tissues, all while delivering histopathological-grade structural detail [ ]. These characteristics suggest that Raman spectroscopic imaging holds substantial promise as a research and translational tool, thereby supporting studies in single-cell metabolic phenotyping, cell cycle status, cluster biology, and metastatic potential evaluation in oncology.

5.

## Applications of Raman imaging in the immune microenvironment
Raman imaging permits the label-free discrimination of diverse immune cells (e.g. T cells, B cells, NK cells, and neutrophils) along with their activation dynamics. This discriminatory capability is instrumental for elucidating the interactions between circulating tumor cells and the tumor immune microenvironment.
Leukocytes, or white blood cells, are essential components of the human immune system, playing a critical role in recognizing and combating infections. Different immune cells perform specialized functions, such as pathogen elimination through phagocytosis or the release of mediators [ ]. The lymphatic system supports this immune function by transporting lymphatic fluid from peripheral tissues to the circulatory system, maintaining tissue fluid homeostasis [ ]. Lymphocytes, a subset of leukocytes, represent the most abundant cell population within peripheral blood mononuclear cells (SRS), comprising 70–90% of this group. Lymphocytes are further classified into T-lymphocytes (40–70%), B-lymphocytes (5–15%), and natural killer (NK) cells (5–10%), based on their migration patterns and functional roles [ ]. T- and B-lymphocytes are morphologically similar but form the cornerstone of adaptive immune responses by recognizing pathogens and initiating chemical signaling cascades [ ].
Conventional methods for assessing lymphocyte activation, such as cell staining (flow cytometry) or cell lysis (mRNA analysis), are destructive to cells [ ]. In contrast, Raman imaging offers a non-destructive, label-free alternative capable of visualizing morphological features comparable to standard staining methods [ ]. RS has been successfully employed to analyze immune cells, revealing intrinsic biochemical properties such as DNA, lipid, and protein content [ ]. This technique has shown promise in identifying all leukocyte subtypes, including T and B lymphocytes, granulocytes, monocytes, and NK cells [ ].
Raman spectroscopy enables precise identification of leukocyte subtypes and lymphocyte activation in response to pathogens [ ]. For instance, Raman imaging can differentiate between resting and activated mature T cells at the molecular level. Activated T cells exhibit irregular shapes, increased size, and changes in cellular components such as elevated protein and lipid content, which are detectable by RS [ ]. Paidi et al. developed a label-free method combining diffraction phase imaging and Raman microscopy to phenotype individual B cells and identify different stages of leukemia cells [ ].
Raman imaging also provides detailed biochemical information on lymphocyte lineages, enabling distinction between T and B cells [ ]. McReynolds et al. combined Raman spectroscopy with digital holograms of single cells, achieving high sensitivity (86.8–100%) and specificity (85.4–100%) in identifying and differentiating immune cell populations, including CD4+ T cells, CD8+ T cells, B cells, and monocytes [ ]. Additionally, Borek-Dorosz et al. identified specific Raman markers for distinguishing B cells from T cells, such as carotenoids and nucleic acid bands. Notably, β-carotene was found to be the main carotenoid present in T cells, highlighting the molecular uniqueness of these immune subtypes [ ].
Raman imaging has also been utilized for the differentiation of immune cells. Ramoji et al. applied unsupervised statistical methods, including principal component analysis (PCA) and hierarchical cluster analysis, to isolate the Raman spectra of neutrophils and lymphocytes, with a particular focus on CD4+ T-lymphocytes [ ]. This non-invasive approach provided a detailed biochemical fingerprint for distinguishing these immune cell types. In the context of leukemia phenotyping, Leszczenko et al. demonstrated that label-free Raman imaging combined with chemometric techniques allowed for the phenotyping and subtype classification of acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL) [ ]. Similarly, Vanna et al. investigated 19 cases across nine leukemia subtypes, including six acute myeloid leukemia (AML) subtypes (AML M0, M1, M2, M3, M5a, and M6) and three ALL subtypes (ALL B Ph-, ALL B Ph+, and ALL T). Using Raman microscopy and imaging, the study identified trends in the maturation and differentiation of leukemia subtypes, particularly by analyzing myeloperoxidase (MPO), carotenoid, and hemoglobin (HB) expression. These findings provided insights into the biochemical and functional variations across leukemia subtypes [ ].
Raman spectroscopy holds significant promise not only for basic immune cell classification but also for the label-free evaluation of tumor-related immune status. This capability offers a powerful approach for interrogating immune evasion mechanisms of CTCs and for informing novel immunotherapeutic strategies.

6.

## Advances in SERS for CTC detection and imaging
In liquid biopsy, SERS technology fulfills distinct yet complementary roles stemming from its signal-enhancing properties. On the spectroscopic front, it serves as a platform for ultra-sensitive CTC enumeration and molecular typing. On the imaging front, SERS-active nanoprobes enable the visualization of biochemical composition within individual CTCs at high spatial resolution. Recent developments in both areas will be examined herein.
6.1.

## Advances in SERS for CTC detection and analysis
Detection of CTCs is a critical tool for the early diagnosis of cancer, prognosis, evaluation of antitumor drug efficacy, and selection of treatment regimens. SERS, discovered by Fleischmann et al. and Van Duyne et al. in the 1970s, is widely employed in bioanalysis due to its rich spectral information and high sensitivity enabled by plasmon-enhanced scattering [ ]. SERS, recognized as an ultra-sensitive spectroscopic technique, surpasses traditional methods like fluorescence by offering a narrow bandwidth of 0.1 nm, compared to fluorescence’s 20–80 nm. This enables multiplexed detection of multiple targets within the same sample during a single analysis [ ]. Typically, two platforms serve as SERS-active substrates: periodic two-dimensional nanostructures and colloidal nanoparticles (NPs) in solution [ ]. Biomedical applications of SERS leverage two primary approaches: label-free detection and indirect detection using SERS tags. In label-free SERS, spectral signals arise from analyte adsorption onto the substrate, while in SERS-label-based assays, reporter molecules on the SERS tags generate the spectral signals [ ]. SERS offers numerous advantages, including high sensitivity, rapid analysis, fingerprint-level specificity, label-free detection, and non-destructive testing [ ]. SERS operates on the fundamental principle of localized surface plasmon resonance in noble metal nanostructures, achieving signal enhancement by several orders of magnitude and thereby overcoming the intrinsic weakness of conventional Raman scattering. Its technical approaches are broadly categorized into two strategies. Label-free SERS detection relies on the direct adsorption of cellular components onto enhancing substrates for signal acquisition. In contrast, labeled SERS detection employs functionalized nanoprobes conjugated with specific targeting ligands and Raman reporter molecules, enabling both specific cellular recognition and multiplexed biomarker analysis.

6.2.

## Application of SERS as an ultra-sensitive detection technique in CTCs counting and subtyping
The synergistic integration of SERS with advanced enrichment platforms—including microfluidics and immunomagnetic separation—has collectively pushed the detection limit for CTCs down to a few cells per milliliter of blood. This advancement now permits multi-parameter molecular profiling at the single-cell level, achieving an unprecedented level of analytical detail.
Representative advancements include: Multiplexed Phenotypic Profiling: Wilson et al. engineered an integrated chip platform combining immunomagnetic capture with multicolor SERS nanotags. This system enables the simultaneous, multiplexed quantitation of four protein biomarkers (EpCAM, HER2, CD44, IGF1R) on individual CTCs, addressing the limitation of traditional epithelial-marker-dependent approaches and providing a robust tool for dissecting CTCs heterogeneity [ ]. High-Sensitivity Detection Platforms: To improve detection fidelity, Wang et al. engineered a dual-mode platform integrating SERS and fluorescence readouts, which utilizes both aptamer and antibody recognition. This platform achieved a low limit of detection (LOD) of 5 cells mL−1, with the cross-verification capability between the two modalities effectively minimizing false-positive rates [ ]. In a parallel development, Wu et al. designed a folate-targeted composite SERS nanoprobe that also attained an LOD of 5 cells mL−1, demonstrating high signal intensity and target specificity [ ]. Advances in Nanomaterials and Amplification Methodologies: Progress in this area spans novel materials and ingenious amplification schemes. Material innovations include octagonal silver oxide nanoparticles with an impressive LOD of 1 cell mL−1 [ ], and hybrid SERS probes combining plasmonic nanoprisms with magnetic particles for improved performance [ ]. In parallel, novel signal amplification strategies have emerged, such as the ratiometric SERS sensor based on DNA walker cascades by Xiong et al. [ ] which significantly enhances detection reliability at the single-cell level. Strategies for Biomarker-Independent Analysis: Research has pursued biomarker-free methods. This is exemplified by the use of the VAR2CSA protein for wide-spectrum CTCs capture coupled with Raman detection [ ]. In a related vein, Niciński et al. [ ] reported a label-free SERS method using a photovoltaically enhanced substrate, which distinguished cancerous cells in blood with ∼89% accuracy, demonstrating promising clinical translation.

6.3.

## Application of SERS imaging in CTCs morphological and chemical spatial analysis
SERS evolves from a detection technique into a powerful spatial analysis tool when integrated with microscopic imaging systems. It enables high-resolution visualization of CTCs surface marker distribution, spatial heterogeneity in biochemical composition, and interactions with the tumor microenvironment.
Major imaging applications include: Targeted Probe-Based Cellular Imaging: Studies confirm that polyethylene glycol-modified gold-core SERS probes can specifically recognize and distinguish multiple cancer cell lines from normal cells under confocal Raman microscopy. This enables targeted cellular imaging based on SERS signals, providing specific optical contrast for morphological identification of CTCs [ ]. Image-Guided CTCs Capture and Identification: The combination of aptamer-modified magnetic microspheres with SERS imaging allows for efficient CTCs capture alongside direct in situ imaging analysis and visual identification, achieving an integrated capture-and-characterization workflow [ ].
In summary, through continuous innovation in nanomaterials and optimization of detection strategies, SERS technology has achieved significant progress in the ultrasensitive detection, multiparametric phenotyping, and spatial imaging analysis of CTCs. These advances offer versatile solutions for liquid biopsy, moving beyond simple enumeration toward in-depth molecular and functional characterization, thereby demonstrating considerable potential for clinical translation.

## Current challenges and limitations
Despite its demonstrated promise in CTCs analysis, the clinical translation of Raman imaging technology faces a series of formidable yet distinct challenges spanning technical, biological, and translational domains.
Technically, the intrinsically weak Raman signal constitutes the primary limitation. The extremely small spontaneous Raman scattering cross-section leads to low signal-to-noise ratios and prolonged acquisition times for single-cell imaging, hindering its suitability for high-throughput clinical workflows. Furthermore, within the complex blood matrix, background interference from erythrocytes, leukocytes, and other components significantly complicates the detection of exceedingly rare CTCs (typically 1–10 cells/mL). While SERS technology can dramatically improve sensitivity by leveraging plasmonic effects, its performance is critically dependent on the homogeneity, stability, and reproducibility of the nanoparticle substrates. Currently, batch-to-batch variations in nanoprobe synthesis and signal inconsistencies arising from the uneven distribution of electromagnetic “hot spots” remain core bottlenecks, impeding high-precision quantitative analysis and cross-platform reproducibility.
Biologically, the inherent characteristics of CTCs present fundamental obstacles. Their extreme rarity in peripheral blood demands exceptionally efficient and robust enrichment techniques. Moreover, procuring sufficient cell numbers for statistically robust Raman analysis remains a significant practical hurdle. CTCs exhibit marked phenotypic and molecular heterogeneity, encompassing diverse epithelial-mesenchymal transition states, metabolic profiles, and genomic landscapes. This complexity challenges the comprehensiveness of any single spectral or imaging strategy, demanding that detection platforms possess broad capture and accurate subtyping capabilities. The current difficulty in stably expanding CTCs populations in vitro further constrains longitudinal functional studies, drug response assays, and the generation of large-scale, high-quality Raman spectral datasets for robust model training.
From a translational perspective, a notable gap persists between promising laboratory proofs-of-concept and routine clinical application. Most studies to date have relied on cell line models or limited patient cohorts. There is a critical paucity of large-scale, prospective clinical trials conducting direct, head-to-head comparisons of Raman/SERS-based detection against established clinical standards like CellSearch®. Consequently, the definitive clinical sensitivity, specificity, and prognostic value of these technologies remain unvalidated. Furthermore, high capital costs for advanced systems (e.g. confocal Raman and SRS microscopes) and the expense of specialized nanoprobes present barriers to widespread adoption in standard clinical laboratories. Finally, the lack of standardized protocols and quality control frameworks across the entire workflow—from sample collection and processing to data acquisition and analysis—undermines reproducibility and complicates regulatory approval pathways. Addressing these interconnected systemic challenges is imperative for the successful translation and standardization of Raman-based liquid biopsy.

8.

## Conclusion
CTCs serve as minimally invasive markers for liquid biopsies and can be enriched based on their physical and biological properties. CTCs play a critical role in cancer diagnosis, metastasis monitoring, prognosis assessment, and individualized treatment. However, CTCs from different cancer types exhibit significant heterogeneity, posing challenges for their detection and characterization. Raman imaging and CTCs analysis share overlapping roles in oncology ( ), but despite significant advancements in CTCs monitoring technologies, there remain issues with sensitivity, specificity, cost, and the accurate identification of CTCs. Moreover, the scarcity of CTCs in blood and the difficulty in their in vitro culture and expansion highlight the urgent need for stable and efficient CTCs capture methods, as well as simpler, more reliable approaches for phenotyping and single-cell biocharacterization. These advancements are crucial for basic cancer biology research [ ]. We hypothesize that the integration of Raman imaging with CTCs detection could significantly enhance the precision and scope of cancer diagnostics and treatment, opening new possibilities for high-resolution, real-time tumor characterization.
 Unlike conventional monitoring techniques, single-cell Raman imaging offers a non-destructive, high-resolution, and label-free spectral analysis platform. This technology is already capable of imaging blood, lymphatics, and leukocytes. Additionally, RS has been applied to EVs in liquid biopsies, enabling the detection and characterization of biomolecular components such as proteins, lipids, and nucleic acids [ ]. For Raman imaging to reach clinical applications, it is essential to further develop highly sensitive, easy-to-operate, cost-effective, and accurate techniques. Currently, enhanced Raman spectroscopy techniques, particularly SERS, have substantially advanced both the sensitivity and specificity of CTCs detection. By integrating these platforms with microfluidics for efficient sample handling and artificial intelligence for robust data analysis, the field is being propelled toward liquid biopsy solutions of greater precision, practicality, and clinical utility.
Raman imaging technology, recognized as an advanced analytical tool, is capable of single-cell detection and holds significant potential for detecting CTCs in theory. Recent studies have explored the integration of Raman imaging and SERS technology in CTCs detection, demonstrating advancements in sensitivity and specificity for identifying rare CTCs. These investigations build on the current state of research, highlighting the promise of SERS-enhanced Raman imaging in improving the detection and characterization of rare cancer cells. Given the wide range of clinical applications for CTCs in cancer diagnosis and treatment, integrating Raman imaging with CTCs detection holds the promise of significantly improving detection precision. Furthermore, the application of Raman imaging to CTCs analysis may enhance the accuracy of personalized treatment strategies and the evaluation of drug efficacy. Beyond these potential applications, Raman imaging has proven effective for lymphoma typing and could be expanded to include the diagnosis and typing of solid tumors. We envision a future where the integration of Raman imaging and CTCs technologies becomes a cornerstone for advancing tumor diagnostics and therapeutic precision. Looking ahead, the evolution of Raman imaging in CTCs research is anticipated to converge along four strategic pathways. First, the development of high-throughput platforms that integrate microfluidic enrichment with automated spectral acquisition and analysis will be crucial for improving detection efficiency and clinical workflow compatibility. Second, advancing the multimodal fusion of Raman with complementary techniques, such as fluorescence and quantitative phase imaging, will enable correlative analysis of CTCs morphology, molecular markers, and intrinsic biochemical composition. Third, a deeper focus on the functional profiling and longitudinal monitoring of live CTCs in vitro will be essential to decipher their metabolic dynamics, drug response, and resistance mechanisms, thereby generating real-time insights for personalized therapeutic strategies. Fourth, conducting large-scale, prospective clinical validations with direct comparison against established diagnostic standards is imperative to define performance benchmarks and establish standardized operational and analytical protocols.
Collectively, these efforts will accelerate the translation of Raman imaging from a research tool to a clinically integrated solution, transforming CTCs analysis from simple enumeration to comprehensive functional characterization. The continued convergence of Raman-based imaging with CTCs biology is poised to provide transformative tools for early cancer detection, tailored therapy selection, and dynamic prognosis assessment, ultimately advancing liquid biopsy into an era of precise cellular visualization.
