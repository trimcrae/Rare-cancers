## ABSTRACT
Pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) poses a significant challenge for early diagnosis due to the lack of sensitive and specific biomarkers. This encouraged us to explore the diagnostic value of cancer‐derived small extracellular vesicles (sEVs) as early detection biomarkers. We previously showed that the recombinant malaria protein VAR2CSA (rVAR2) selectively binds to oncofetal chondroitin sulfate (ofCS) on the surfaces of cancer cells, which might be useful for identifying cancer‐derived sEVs. Indeed, flow cytometry revealed strong ofCS expression in PDAC cell‐derived sEVs, as evidenced by the presence of mutant KRAS, a common genetic alteration in PDAC. Plasma from PDAC patients showed significantly higher ofCS+ sEV levels compared to healthy donors and patients with benign gastrointestinal diseases. ROC analysis for ofCS+ sEVs revealed an AUC of 0.9049 for the detection of all‐stage and 0.9222 for early‐stage PDAC. Notably, mutant KRAS was also detected in these patient‐derived sEVs. Most intriguingly, combining ofCS+ sEVs and CA19‐9 resulted in an AUC of 0.9707 for the detection of early PDAC. Our study demonstrates that rVAR2 is suitable for detecting ofCS+ cancer‐derived sEVs in plasma, thereby providing high efficiency for identifying PDAC patients among a diverse population. These findings suggest that rVAR2‐based sEV detection could serve as a powerful diagnostic tool to improve patient survival through early detection.

Zhao et al. explored the utility of small extracellular vesicles (sEVs) for early detection of pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) using oncofetal chondroitin sulfate (ofCS) as a specific marker for cancer cell‐derived sEVs. For detecting early‐stage PDAC among healthy individuals and patients with non‐malignant disease, ROC analysis revealed an AUC of 0.8475 for CA19‐9 alone versus 0.9707 for the addition of ofCS+ sEVs. These findings support ofCS+ sEVs as a promising biomarker to improve early diagnosis of PDAC.

## Isolation of PDAC Cell‐Derived sEVs
Next, we explored the rVAR2‐based isolation and purification of PDAC cell‐derived sEVs. Characterisation and quantification of the sEVs obtained through differential ultracentrifugation were performed (Figure  ). NTA revealed a predominant particle size distribution ranging from 30 to 150 nm, with the expected peak at approximately 111 nm (Figure  ). Transmission electron microscopy displayed typical cup‐shaped vesicle structures (Figure  ). Western blot analysis confirmed the presence of the exosome markers CD63, CD9, Syntenin‐1 (SDCBP) and TSG101 in PDAC cell‐derived sEVs, while Calnexin (CANX) as a marker for the endoplasmic reticulum was negative (Figure  ). Flow cytometry analysis of sEVs bound to latex beads further validated the expression of exosome markers, with CD9 exhibiting the highest positive rates (Figure  ).
 
3.3

## Detection of ofCS+ sEVs by Flow Cytometry
To next ascertain the expression of ofCS on sEVs released from PDAC cultures, a latex bead‐based flow cytometry experiment was conducted. Results indicated high ofCS expression on PDAC cell‐derived sEVs, suggesting the potential utility of rVAR2 protein for sEV detection in PDAC patients (Figure  ). To further validate the specific binding of the rVAR2 protein to PDAC‐derived sEVs, we employed Chondroitinase ABC (ChABC) treatment to remove ofCS from the sEV surface. Remarkably, after the ChABC treatment, there was virtually complete abrogation of the signal, strongly supporting the notion that rVAR2 specifically binds to ofCS on the surface of sEVs derived from primary PDAC cultures (Figure  ).

3.4

## Detection of KRAS Mutations in ofCS+ PDAC Cell‐Derived sEVs
To further validate the cancer origin of the sEVs, we investigated the presence of mutant KRAS, which is frequently found in PDAC. DNA was extracted from sEV samples derived from primary PDAC cells. Fluorometric quantification revealed the existence of double‐stranded DNA in PDAC cell‐derived sEVs. Subsequent ddPCR analysis unveiled the presence of KRAS mutations in sEV‐derived DNA for all three PDAC models (SiC002, SiC003 and SiC005), with SiC002 exhibiting the highest number of copies per µL (Figure  ). Notably, while the PDAC models SiC003 and SiC005 also exhibited wild‐type KRAS, no wild‐type KRAS was detected in sEVs derived from the PDAC model SiC002, suggesting biallelic KRAS mutations consistent with the high levels of KRAS mutations detected (Figure  ). Positive and negative controls were established using 10 ng of DNA from the parental PDAC cells and 10 ng of DNA from wild‐type KRAS HEK293T cells, respectively.

3.5

## Detection of Cancer‐Derived sEVs in Human Plasma
For the detection and quantification of sEVs by flow cytometry, we used latex beads, which bind sEVs irrespective of their origin. rVAR2 staining was subsequently applied to enable specific detection of cancer‐derived sEVs. As the proportion of cancer‐derived sEVs associated with each latex bead increases, the fluorescent signal from that bead becomes stronger.  
Using the same workflow for isolating PDAC cell‐derived sEVs as described above, we first validated the characteristics of sEVs derived from the plasma of healthy donors and PDAC patients (Figure  and Table  ).
 NTA (Figure  ) was used to examine the particle concentration and diameter distribution of sEVs, revealing that sEVs from both pancreatic cancer patients and healthy donors predominantly ranged from 30 to 150 nm. The peak size distribution for sEVs from healthy donors was approximately 98 nm, while sEVs from PDAC patients were slightly larger with approximately 108 nm. Transmission electron microscopy confirmed the distinct round or elliptical saucer‐shaped structures of purified sEVs (Figure  ). To assess the molecular signature of sEVs, we performed Western Blot experiments demonstrating the presence of CD63 and CD81 in sEVs enriched for exosomes obtained through differential ultracentrifugation, while the ER marker Calnexin (CANX) remained negative (Figure  ).
To evaluate the potential presence of mutant KRAS in PDAC plasma‐derived sEVs, DNA was extracted and quantified from sEVs of four PDAC patients, one from each stage (I, II, III and IV), as well as from sEVs of one healthy donor. Droplet Digital PCR confirmed the presence of mutant KRAS in sEVs from all four PDAC patients (Figure  ). The quantification of KRAS mutations in DNA derived from sEVs of these patients showed varying concentrations. Expectedly, wild‐type KRAS was also detected in these samples. To further validate the specific detection of cancer‐derived sEVs, we pretreated the sEVs derived from PDAC patients with ChABC. This pretreatment efficiently prevented rVAR2 binding, thereby confirming the specificity of our rVAR2 staining (Figure  ). Together, these data suggest that our platform is suitable for detecting cancer‐derived sEVs.

3.6

## Specificity of rVAR2 Binding for Detecting Cancer‐Derived sEVs in Plasma
To further assess the signal‐to‐noise ratio of our platform, we systematically analysed plasma samples from various control populations: (1) healthy donors, (2) patients with non‐malignant gastrointestinal diseases and (3) patients with non‐malignant pancreatic diseases. Although healthy donors (HD) were slightly younger than PDAC patients, our non‐malignant gastrointestinal disease (GID) patient population was age‐matched (average age: 62.8 years) and had the same female‐to‐male ratio of 40:60 as PDAC patients (Table  ). Both HD and non‐malignant GID patients showed very low levels of ofCS expression in sEVs (Figure  ).
 Next, we studied the plasma of patients with non‐malignant pancreatic diseases (non‐malignant PD; Figure  and Table  ). This group included patients with pancreatic pseudocysts, solid pseudopapillary tumours, chronic pancreatitis, mucinous cystic neoplasms, intraductal papillary mucinous neoplasms (IPMNs) and non‐invasive low‐grade (G1) neuroendocrine tumours. Here, we noted two outliers with higher ofCS staining: one patient with a pancreatic pseudocyst and one patient with an IPMN (Figure  ). Pancreatic pseudocysts represent a common non‐cancerous complication of pancreatitis. The IPMN lesions were classified as low‐grade based on clinical imaging results. These patients were enrolled in 2024, and we are continuing to monitor them for any occurrences of malignant transformation.

3.7

## Diagnostic Performance of ofCS+ sEVs for Detecting PDAC
We next enrolled a large panel of PDAC patients across all stages, including early PDAC, and found consistently enhanced rVAR2 binding compared to non‐PDAC controls (Table  and Figure  ). Specifically, we compared ofCS expression on sEVs from PDAC patients with those from the control groups, including HD, non‐malignant GID and non‐malignant PD, and found significantly increased ofCS expression in PDAC cases (Figure  ). Most notably, despite two outliers in the non‐malignant PD group, the difference for the expression of ofCS on the surface of sEVs was still highly significant compared to PDAC patients (Figure  ).
 These findings were further validated through AUC analyses. Detecting early‐stage PDAC among all control samples yielded an excellent AUC of 0.9222, with a sensitivity of 86.67% and specificity of 90.91% (Figure  ). The addition of ofCS+ sEVs significantly raised the CA19‐9 AUC from 0.8475 to an impressive 0.9707 (95% confidence interval 0.9370–1.000), demonstrating the potential of this combination for early detection of PDAC. Intriguingly, similar results were obtained when the analysis was performed on all‐stage PDAC, achieving an AUC of 0.9801 (95% confidence interval 0.9587–1.000) (Figure ). Of note, for CA19‐9, we used a cut‐off value of 58.13 U/mL rather than the standard cut‐off value of 37 U/mL, which resulted in improved outcomes.
As we cannot, at this early stage of follow‐up, completely rule out any malignant transformation for the non‐malignant PD samples, we also calculated the results for non‐PD controls (healthy donors and those with non‐malignant GID). For ofCS+ sEVs and early‐stage PDAC, we obtained an AUC of 0.9684, with a sensitivity of 90.0% and specificity of 94.74% (Figure ). Corresponding results for CA19‐9 were 0.8667, with a sensitivity of 70.0% and specificity of 100.00%. The combination of these two biomarkers resulted in an AUC of 0.9912 (95% confidence interval 0.9737–1.000). Performing the analysis on all‐stage PDAC revealed similar results with an AUC of 0.9936 (95% confidence interval 0.9820–1.000) (Figure ).
In contrast, detecting the most prevalent KRAS mutations (G12A, G12C, G12D, G12R, G12S, G12V and G13D) in the collected sEVs did not further enhance the predictive value of ofCS+ sEVs, as trace amounts of mutant KRAS could already be observed in 8 out of 14 patients with non‐malignant pancreatic diseases (Figure ). These findings align with previous reports where low‐level KRAS mutations have also been detected in non‐malignant pancreatic diseases (Cohen et al. ; Kanda et al. ) and, in some instances, even apparently healthy individuals (Allenson et al. ). Specifically, our ROC analysis for the detection of early‐stage PDAC shows that addition of mutant KRAS resulted in an AUC that was almost identical (ofCS+ sEVs alone: 0.9238 vs. combination: 0.9289). The slightly improved specificity, increasing from 87.5% to 91.7%, was offset by a reduced sensitivity for PDAC detection, dropping from 92.7% to 87.8%. Since the primary objective of our study is to develop an early detection strategy for PDAC, we consider sensitivity to be of utmost importance.
Finally, when testing the impact of the level of ofCS+ sEVs on the outcome of PDAC patients, we did not observe significant differences between patients with low versus high ofCS expression on their respective sEVs (Figure ). Results were similar for patients receiving no chemotherapy during follow‐up. Surprisingly, for patients treated with chemotherapy, the trend was reversed, with higher risk for patients with lower ofCS+ sEVs levels. We speculate that higher levels of ofCS+ sEVs might reflect a biologically more active or immunogenic tumour, which responds better to chemotherapy leading to improved outcome. Taken together, the present data strongly support the hypothesis that rVAR2 protein is highly suitable for the detection of cancer‐derived sEVs in the plasma of PDAC patients, whereas the prognostic impact of the amount of circulating ofCS+ sEVs in patients with diagnosed PDAC requires further investigation.

## Discussion
The prognosis of PDAC is inherently poor due to its typically late diagnosis at an advanced stage, when treatment options and efficacy are extremely limited. This underscores the critical importance of early detection, allowing for surgical intervention, currently the only potentially curative approach with a significant impact on patients’ long‐term survival. State‐of‐the‐art diagnostic techniques include computed tomography (CT), magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography (MRCP), Positron Emission Tomography‐Computed Tomography (PET‐CT) and Endoscopic Ultrasonography Guided Fine Needle Aspiration (EUS‐FNA). EUS‐FNA, particularly noted for its precision, has varying accuracy and potential risks, such as bleeding and cancer cell dissemination (Li et al. ).
To supplement EUS‐FNA, blood tests including biomarkers, such as CA19‐9, CA125, CA242 and CEA, have been integrated into the diagnostic panel for assessing tumour burden, monitoring treatment response and prognostication (Dong et al. ; Liu et al. ; Zhang et al. ). However, CA19‐9 not only lacks specificity because it is elevated in various benign conditions, but it also provides limited sensitivity for the detection of PDAC. Approximately 12% of all‐stage PDAC patients have been reported to have CA19‐9 levels below the upper limit of normal due to the absence of the Lewis antigen, which leads to a lack of CA19‐9 secretion (Liu et al. ). In our current study, 32.3% of early‐stage PDAC patients were negative for CA19‐9 levels, in line with a previous report where more than half of early‐stage PDAC patients were negative for CA19‐9 (Tang et al. ). Even for the late‐stage PDAC patients enrolled in the present study, we found a considerable fraction of 12.1% still negative for CA19‐9. These findings underscore the urgent need for more reliable early detection methods.
Liquid biopsy, including circulating tumour DNA (ctDNA), CTCs and extracellular vesicles (EVs), may offer a more effective non‐invasive approach for comprehensive disease detection and monitoring. Initial studies have already demonstrated their potential, but these biomarkers still require further refinement to improve sensitivity and specificity (Cheng et al. ; Cohen et al. ; Hadano et al. ; Luchini et al. ; Melo et al. ; Pang et al. ; Takai et al. ; Xie et al. ). Indeed, the sensitivity of ctDNA in early‐stage pancreatic cancer varies, and its detection is complicated by dilution effects in peripheral blood (Cohen et al. ; Hadano et al. ; Takai et al. ). Although CTCs show high specificity, their isolation is challenging owing to diverse and complex isolation techniques, CTC scarcity, leukocyte contamination and high costs (Pang et al. ; Xie et al. ). CTCs present variable detection rates across studies, ranging from 5% to 100%, with high specificity up to 100% (Pang et al. ). CtDNA, while potentially suitable for monitoring minimal residual disease or early recurrence, is markedly diluted in circulating blood, limiting its sensitivity for early PDAC detection. The sensitivity of ctDNA for early‐stage PDAC varies between 30% and 62% (Cohen et al. ; Hadano et al. ; Lee et al. ), while combined ctDNA and CTCs exhibit generally high specificity of 86% (Luchini et al. ).
The present study now explored the potential of cancer‐derived sEVs as biomarkers for early PDAC detection. These vesicles carry rich molecular information, and their surface proteins are closely linked to their cellular origin. Indeed, cancer‐derived sEVs, marked by cancer‐specific surface proteins, hold diagnostic value for early PDAC detection. However, the identification of optimal surface markers remains a critical step in developing sEV‐based biomarkers. Malarial VAR2CSA protein, which binds to ofCS in various cancer types, has previously been explored for its utility in isolating CTCs in various cancers, including PDAC, and vastly surpassing the efficacy of current CTC isolation methods (Agerbaek et al. ). Exploring the potential of recombinant VAR2 for detecting cancer‐derived sEVs, we found that rVAR2 protein has strong capabilities to specifically bind to ofCS on the surface of sEVs derived from PDAC cells.
Further analysis revealed the presence of KRAS mutations in the DNA of sEVs derived from PDAC cells, supporting the contention that these sEVs indeed originated from PDAC cells. Moreover, sEVs isolated from the plasma of PDAC patients exhibited significantly higher levels of ofCS+ sEVs compared to those from healthy donors. Importantly, treating these sEV samples with the ChABC enzyme resulted in a marked reduction in ofCS+ sEV levels, reinforcing the specific binding of rVAR2 to ofCS on cancer‐derived sEVs. Our approach, based on rVAR2‐mediated detection of ofCS+ sEVs, demonstrates high sensitivity, as evidenced by significantly elevated ofCS+ sEVs in PDAC patients, along with the excellent specificity of rVAR2, resulting in an AUC of 0.9222 for early‐stage PDAC and 0.9049 for all‐stage PDAC. This performance vastly exceeds the results of a recent study focusing on ofCS plasma levels, where the AUC was only 0.73 for all stages and 0.69 for early‐stage PDAC (Zhang et al. ). These findings underscore the advantages of utilising sEVs for ofCS detection over unprocessed plasma. Even more importantly, the combined assessment of ofCS+ sEVs and CA19‐9 produced an impressive AUC of 0.9707 for early stage PDAC and 0.9801 for all‐stage PDAC. These data suggest that the supplementation of current CA19‐9 measurements by ofCS+ sEV analysis could markedly enhance our capability for earlier detection of malignant transformation in the pancreas, and probably other organs as well.
A limitation of our study lies in the smaller sample size, which clearly requires further validation in a large and diverse population of gastrointestinal patients. Such study should include at least 54 early PDAC patients versus 54 gastrointestinal control patients in order to reliably validate the diagnostic value of the rVAR2‐based sEV platform for the detection of early pancreatic cancer. It will also be of interest to explore potential changes in cancer cell‐derived sEV levels in response to treatment. Nevertheless, our study lays the groundwork for such future investigations into the diagnostic potential of ofCS+ sEVs. Further research should expand the diversity of the study cohort and include head‐to‐head comparisons of the diagnostic performance with other PDAC biomarkers. In the meantime, our study supports the notion that rVAR2 is a powerful tool for detecting and isolating cancer‐derived sEVs, presenting exciting opportunities for the early diagnosis of pancreatic cancer and the development of targeted therapeutic interventions.
