In this study, we used an enrichment method based on recombinant Plasmodium falciparum VAR2CSA protein to enable the capture of rare circulating tumor cells (CTCs) from a single blood draw in early-stage (AJCC I-II) melanoma patients. CTCs were subsequently identified using fluorescent antibodies targeting tumor initiating and melanoma specific cell markers. In parallel, we investigated circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) in these patients. Among 92 early-stage melanoma patients, CTCs were detected in 21 patients (22.8%) at time of initial diagnosis. The presence of CTCs was significantly associated with an unfavorable clinical outcome, such as disease progression or death related to melanoma disease, with a median observation time of 30 months (IQR 24.0–36.0) (p = 0.043). Furthermore, when combining CTC with ctDNA detection, a highly significant correlation with disease progression was observed (p = 0.014) underlining the role of early tumor cell dissemination in melanoma disease. These findings suggest that CTC assessment, particularly when combined with ctDNA analysis, may represent a valuable biomarker to facilitate risk stratification of early-stage melanoma patients to improve personalized treatment approaches.

## Introduction
Melanoma is the most malignant form of skin cancer. Most melanoma patients do not show clinically manifested metastases at time of initial diagnosis. Nevertheless, early diagnosis and appropriate treatment are crucial to reduce risk of disease progression and formation of metastases. Despite surgical resection of the primary tumor, metastases develop within 5 years after initial diagnosis in 10% of all patients , . This suggests that shedding of circulating tumor cells (CTCs), which is associated with an unfavorable outcome, occurs early in the course of the disease , . Liquid biopsies have emerged as a minimally invasive approach to depict metastatic potential as well as tumor heterogeneity. While ctDNA is already well established in metastatic melanoma, the role of ctDNA and CTCs in early-stage melanoma disease remains unclear .
A number of difficulties limit the clinical utility of liquid biopsy in melanoma patients. Melanoma cells are a phenotypically very heterogeneous cell population of non-epithelial origin, which precludes the use of size exclusion and of EpCAM/CK-based detection methods . To overcome these challenges and to represent the heterogeneity, methods such as PCR-based assays, microfluidic chips technologies or approaches based on physical properties like cell plasticity and size have been developed to improve sensitivity. Most typically, isolation of circulating melanoma cells is achieved using magnetic beads coupled with antibodies targeting melanoma-specific markers . For this approach, it is particularly important to use a broad diversity of markers to account for the cellular heterogeneity . While the majority of the research conducted has focused on advanced stages III-IV of melanoma there is limited data that specifically addresses early-stage melanoma – .
Previous studies showed increased expression of oncofetal chondroitin sulfate (ofCS) in malignant tissues , . Oncofetal chondroitin sulfate is displayed as secondary modification to a broad panel of proteins and demonstrated to play a role in tumor cell motility, growth and immune evasion, which suggests ofCS as a novel therapeutic cancer target , . Additionally, ofCS can be used to enrich for CTCs and separate them from the abundance of white blood cells in peripheral blood samples . Lastly, ofCS has recently been demonstrated as a detection marker for CTCs in late-stage melanoma patients .
As ofCS expression is independent on tumor tissue origin, we investigated an anti-ofCS-based approach to detect CTCs in blood samples from melanoma patients at initial diagnosis, with a focus on early-stage disease (AJCC I-II) and melanoma cell-free sentinel lymph node biopsies. Furthermore, we performed ctDNA sequencing and correlated both the image-based CTC findings and the ctDNA results with the clinical outcome.

## Results
Early-stage melanoma patients were monitored using a liquid biopsy sample, which was employed for quantifying CTCs and subjected to ctDNA sequencing. We developed a fast and robust method using the recombinant ofCS binding rVAR2 protein to isolate CTCs, which were subsequently detected by immunofluorescence. ctDNA characterization was performed using a next generation sequencing panel covering 29 known oncogenes and tumor suppressor genes.

## Sensitivity and specificity
The sensitivity and specificity of CTC detection and ctDNA analysis when applied in combination are 71.4% and 72.6%.The corresponding false positive rate and false negative rate are 28.6% and 27.4%.

## Spike-in controls for recovery
To ensure rVAR2 stability throughout the enrichment and staining process, recovery rates of 100 prestained cells of the cultured colon cell line COLO205 spiked into 4 mL of whole blood were validated on a routine basis. Spike-in cells were counted as double positive cells for Celltracker and DAPI. We acquired a median recovery rate of 77.7% with the internal spike-in control (IQR 65.2–87.2).

## Early-stage melanoma patient cohort
In total, a cohort of 92 melanoma patients with early-stage disease (AJCC I-II) at initial diagnosis and a negative sentinel lymph node (SLN) biopsy result were followed within this study. They were enrolled with a single blood draw procedure at time of initial diagnosis or within six weeks after initial diagnosis in case of re-excision and sentinel lymph node (SLN) biopsy (Fig.  ). 
Their median age was 64 years (IQR 54.0–69.3) with most patients between 51 and 70 years (60.9%). The gender distribution was even with 45 female (48.9%) and 47 male (51.1%) patients. At diagnosis, tumor thicknesses varied from 0.38 mm to 14 mm. All patients showed normal S100 serum levels at time of initial diagnosis (≤ 0.1 µg/L) (Table and Fig.  ). 

## Marker expression in CTCs in early-stage melanoma patients
Blood samples from early-stage melanoma patients (AJCC I-II) were tested by rVAR2-based cell enrichment and downstream immunofluorescence staining for cells expressing NG2, PD-L1 and the previously identified CTC markers CD146, CD271, RANK and ABCB5 , , . Results were classified as CTC positive, CTC negative or – when only weak fluorescence signals were detected or cell morphology was inconclusive – as CTC unclear. Among SLN negative patients, 21 out of 92 presented with CTCs (22.8%), 58 patients (63%) without CTCs and 13 patients (14.1%) were classified as unclear (Fig.  ). Figure  presents four images of CTCs as they show green staining by melanoma and tumor-initiating markers, no leukocyte counterstain in red, and appropriate cell morphology and shape of the cell nuclei. 
The clinicopathological characteristics of the CTC positive and CTC negative cohorts were comparable, particularly regarding age, sex, Breslow thickness and ulceration (Table ).
Patients with unclear CTC detection were excluded from the subsequent statistical analysis. Distribution of tumor thicknesses was identical regardless of positive or negative CTC detection (p = 0.535, Wilcoxon rank sum test) (Fig.  A). 

## ctDNA sequencing results in early-stage melanoma patients at time of diagnosis
Of the 92 melanoma patients initially diagnosed with early stage (AJCC I-II) and with a negative sentinel lymph node biopsy result, ctDNA sequencing was successful in 83 cases (90.2%). Pathogenic or likely pathogenic gene alterations were detected in the ctDNA at time of initial diagnosis in six cases (7.23%), which were subsequently classified as ctDNA positive. All detected gene variants in ctDNA positive patients were added to the appendix. ctDNA detection did not correlate with the tumor depth (p = 0.149, Wilcoxon rank sum test) (Fig.  B).

## Prognostic significance of CTC and ctDNA detection
A worse clinical outcome was considered as tumor progression or cause-specific death. In this evaluation only patients with a minimum of ≥ 6 months of clinical follow-up (median 30 months, IQR 24.0–36.0) were included and local recurrences were not considered as disease progression as they do not indicate a systemic event. In these cases, ≥ 1 CTC in a blood sample of AJCC I-II melanoma patients at time of diagnosis was predictive of a worse clinical outcome (p = 0.043, Log rank test) (Fig.  A). 
When considering at least 6 months of clinical follow-up (n = 80) in these early-stage melanoma patients, a pathological or likely pathological ctDNA sequencing result from the blood at time of initial diagnosis had no significant correlation with a worse clinical outcome (p = 0.117, Log rank test) (Fig.  B). When combining CTC and ctDNA results at time of initial diagnosis in early-stage melanoma, a statistically significant correlation was observed with reduced cause-specific progression-free survival (p = 0.014, Log rank test) (Fig.  C) and disease progression (p = 0.023, Fisher´s exact Test) (Fig.  D). Sensitivity analyses excluding the small subset of seven patients presenting with second primary malignancies (evenly distributed across the CTC positive and CTC negative cohort) yielded results consistent with the primary analyses, suggesting that the observed association between CTC detection and reduced progression-free survival was not materially altered.
The association of CTC and/or ctDNA detection with reduced progression-free survival remained significant when Cox proportional hazard ratios were calculated in a multivariate model, adjusting for age, sex, ulceration and tumor depth (Breslow thickness) (p = 0.035) (Fig.  ). 
Altogether, the data suggests that CTCs exist even in early-stage I-II melanoma patients with negative sentinel lymph node and that they are associated with a poor clinical outcome.

## Discussion
Although circulating melanoma cells were first reported 30 years ago, their relevance in malignant melanoma remains debated . While previous studies were focused on advanced melanoma stages, AJCC stage III or IV, the role of CTCs in early-stage melanoma and the underlying molecular mechanisms behind metastasis are mainly unknown.
In a large study, Lucci et al. analyzed CTCs utilizing the CELLSEARCH® Circulating Melanoma Cell Assay in patients with AJCC stage III melanoma at their first clinical visit, finding that the detection of ≥ 1 CTC was associated with an increased risk of relapse . In the same year, Edgar et al. investigated photoacoustic flow cytometry for CTC detection to predict metastasis in a cohort of AJCC stage I-III melanoma patients . Despite valuable insights, their research was limited by a relatively small study cohort mainly consisting of patients with advanced AJCC stage III disease.
In another study including 84 patients across all AJCC stages, predominantly those with advanced melanoma, the authors similarly demonstrated that the presence of CTCs could predict a worse clinical outcome . Recently Guetter et al. showed a correlation between shorter survival and the presence of cells positive for melanoma-associated chondroitin sulfate proteoglycan (MCSP) and three selected melanoma transcripts in SLN of non-metastatic melanoma patients with AJCC stage I-III .
The conflicting findings concerning CTC detection and disease prognosis in early-stage melanoma underlined the difficulties in identifying these cells and the need for more precise and sensitive detection techniques.
Quantification and characterization of cfDNA is more commonly investigated and already used to monitor therapy response and/or even detect tumoral diseases in multiple tumor entities. However, ctDNA characterization in melanoma focuses also almost exclusively on advanced tumor stages – .
To address the known intratumoral heterogeneity of melanoma , we utilized a novel approach for CTC enrichment based on the specific binding of the recombinant malaria protein rVAR2 to oncofetal chondroitin sulfate (ofCS), which is expressed on both mesenchymal and epithelial tumor cells . Additionally, the following immunofluorescence stain for cell detection included not only known melanoma-specific cell markers , , but also tumor-initiating markers. These aspects enabled us to address a broader diversity of tumor cells and thus also detect CTCs in early-stage melanoma patients.
It was notable that 22.8% of patients with early-stage melanoma presented with one or more tumor cells in peripheral blood when using a prospective single blood draw procedure at time of initial diagnosis. Furthermore, an association with an unfavorable clinical outcome was observed when considering ≥ 6 months of clinical follow-up. This was the first observation of a statistically significant correlation between CTC detection in early-stage melanoma and unfavorable clinical outcome, thereby indicating that melanoma may be a systemic disease even at this early stage. Furthermore, the rate of CTC positive cases might be increased in a monitoring setting with serial blood draws.
Interestingly, no correlation was found between CTC detection and Breslow thickness, which is one of the most common biomarkers for progression. Fourdrain et al. came to a similar result in their study, which included all AJCC stages . However, there are also studies that prove the opposite. Nevertheless, these studies mainly focus on late stage melanoma – . The correlation between CTC detection and Breslow thickness is therefore controversial and, in our case, also limited by the cohort, which is restricted to AJCC I-II patients.
When using ctDNA as a biomarker in early-stage melanoma, sensitivity of detection is a limiting factor. The low tumor burden in these stages results in lower ctDNA concentrations. To address this limitation, we used a method based on massively parallel sequencing, which allowed higher sensitivity in detecting ctDNA even at low concentrations. Additionally, tumor heterogeneity may be challenging. Due to the intratumoral heterogeneity of melanomas, mutations detected in the primary tumor may not necessarily reflect those present in ctDNA or metastases.
Although a highly sensitive ctDNA sequencing method was employed, no correlation was identified between a pathological or likely pathological ctDNA sequencing result and either reduced cause-specific relapse-free survival or tumor thickness in our early-stage melanoma cohort. At this point it must be noted that the cohort of ctDNA positive patients is very small, which in turn is due to the early stages of the patients. In general ctDNA detection rates increase with higher stages and higher tumor burden .
Our findings indicated that the combined approach of CTC detection and ctDNA sequencing in early-stage melanoma provides statistically significant prediction of recurrence and disease progression within three years, whereas ctDNA detection alone does not. Similarly, Gorges et al. highlighted the benefit of combining ctDNA and CTC detection due to the intra-patient heterogeneity in melanoma patients with advanced tumor stages . Our results demonstrated that melanoma may be a systemic disease at the very beginning and the process of metastasis starts even before histological sentinel lymph node examinations yield positive results.
Another important benefit of early detection of CTCs may be the identification of distinct subsets of melanoma patients, which would benefit from a higher follow-up frequency, innovative targeted and adjuvant therapy strategies.
However, several limitations must be considered. Detecting CTCs based on microscopy presents challenges in standardization and reproducibility. Further, standardized protocols for broader clinical application need to be established, enabling larger-scale studies to validate our findings. Additionally, the presence of second primary malignancies in melanoma patients warrants consideration. Sensitivity analyses excluding this small subset of patients yielded comparable results, suggesting that second primary tumors did not substantially influence the observed association between CTC detection and reduced progression-free survival. Despite these challenges, the non-invasive nature of the CTC detection and circulating tumor DNA analysis offers significant advantages for providing additional information besides traditional invasive diagnostic methods, such as tissue biopsies.
In conclusion, our study presents combining CTC detection with ctDNA analysis as a promising tool to successfully predict an unfavorable outcome of AJCC I-II melanoma, even in the absence of metastatic signs. Thereby indicating considerable potential for risk stratification, predicting recurrence, and selecting personalized treatment options. Furthermore, by employing an enhanced staining cocktail that accounts for melanoma heterogeneity, we successfully detected CTCs in early-stage melanoma patients, highlighting the clinical significance of these cells in disease progression. While challenges related to sensitivity, standardization, and optical assessment remain, the non-invasive and cellular size independent nature of this technique renders it a compelling alternative to conventional diagnostic methods.

## Methods
In this study, CTCs were enriched from patient blood samples using the recombinant malaria Plasmodium falciparum VAR2CSA protein (rVAR2) followed by immunofluorescent detection by melanoma specific staining. Further, ctDNA was analyzed by next generation sequencing.

## Sample collection and patient cohort
The study protocol was approved by the Ethical Committee of Johannes Kepler University Linz #EK 1174/2018 and all recipients provided written informed consent for research. Therefore, the work conducted was in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.
In this study a single blood draw procedure was performed. Therefore whole blood samples from 162 patients receiving medical care at the Department of Dermatology (Ordensklinikum Linz Elisabethinen, Austria), were prospectively collected at time of initial diagnosis or within six weeks after initial diagnosis in case of re-excision and sentinel lymph node (SLN) biopsy from July 2020 to April 2023. Tumor staging for malignant melanoma was carried out according to AJCC guideline 2018 : (i) 78 patients with stage I, (ii) 53 patients with stage II, (iii) 13 patients with stage III, (iv) 15 patients with stage IV and (v) three with stage 0 (Fig.  ). In this cohort, 93 patients presented with a negative sentinel lymph node result at time of initial diagnosis. One patient received adjuvant therapy and was subsequently excluded from the analysis. Among them, 88 patients considering ≥ 6 months of follow-up (median 30 months, IQR 24.0–36.0). The follow-up regimen was defined based on the clinical setting and the local guidelines applied in the hospital. The course of the disease was monitored during regular routine check-ups. Observation time was defined as time from initial diagnosis to last clinical check-up.
In this study newly diagnosed metastases and the growth of existing metastases were classified as disease progression. Local recurrences were excluded, as it is not a systemic event. A small subset of seven patients developed second primary malignancies during the observation period. These include basal cell carcinoma (n = 3), B-cell chronic lymphocytic leukemia (n = 1), and early-stage adenocarcinoma of the lung, colon or prostate (n = 3). Sensitivity analysis excluding these patients were performed for progression-free survival analyses to assess robustness of findings.

## rVAR2 cell enrichment
Whole blood samples were collected in 9 mL K2EDTA VACUETTE® tubes (455,045, Greiner Bio-One, Kremsmünster, Austria) and processed within 2 h of collection. We used an indirect approach for rVAR2-based bead retrieval from Sand et al. to enrich rVAR2 expressing CTCs . We obtained the rVAR2 protein directly from Centre of translational Medicine and Parasitology at Department of Immunology and Microbiology, University of Copenhagen and Department of Infectious Diseases, Copenhagen University Hospital, 2200 Copenhagen, Denmark.
In order to biotinylate rVAR2, it was mixed with the biotinylated SpyC in a 1:1.2 ratio and incubated for 1 h at room temperature . The Sera-Mag™ streptavidin blocked speedbeads (GE21152104010150, Merck, Darmstadt, Germany) were washed twice with 900 µL PierceTM Protein-Free Blocking Buffer (PF-Buffer, 37,572, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, United States of America) and finally resolved in PF-Buffer to obtain a final concentration of 1.67 µg/µL.
For the enrichment, 4 mL K2EDTA blood were used and an erythrocyte lysis was performed for 12 min using a lysis buffer resulting in a final concentration of 0.155 M NH4Cl, 0.01 M KHCO3 and 0.1 mM EDTA (Ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid). After washing the samples with DPBS (Dulbecco´s Phosphate-Buffered Saline), they were incubated in DPBS with 5% FBS and 1 mM EDTA with 100 nM biotinylated rVAR2 SpyC mix for 30 min at 4 °C with slow rotation. Another incubation in DPBS with 0.1% BSA and 2 mM EDTA and 26 µg washed beads per samples for another 30 min at 4 °C with slow rotation followed. Bead-bound cells were fixed using 100 µL cold 4% PFA (paraformaldehyde, J61899.AK, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, United States of America) for 4 min and then resuspended in DPBS with 10% PF buffer and 1 mM EDTA.

## Immunofluorescence staining and quantification
To identify circulating tumor cells, the enriched sample was stained with Alexa Fluor 488 labelled antibodies against CD146 (342,008, BioLegend, San Diego, United States of America; 1:10), NG2 (53–6504-82, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, United States of America; 1:25), CD271 (53–9400-42, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, United States of America; 1:30), RANK (NBP2-24702AF488, Novus Biologicals, 1:30), ABCB5 (NBP1-77689AF488, Novus Biologicals, 1:30) and PD-L1 (25048S, Cell Signaling, 1:20). Furthermore, remaining white blood cells were identified used Alexa Fluor 594 labelled mouse antibodies against CD45 (304,060, BioLegend, San Diego, United States of America; 1:55), CD16 (ELA-E-AB-F1236P, Biozol, 1:75) and CD11b (301,340, BioLegend, San Diego, United States of America; 1:65). Nuclear staining was performed using DAPI (D1306, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, United States of America; 1:1000). Quantification of tumor cells was carried out manually using the MetaSystems image analysis system (MetaSystems, Altlussheim, Germany) with an AXIO Imager Z2 (Zeiss, Jena, Germany) microscope. CTCs were identified as FITC+, TRITC- and DAPI+ cells, whereas WBCs were identified as FITC-, TRITC+ and DAPI+ cells. Microscopic evaluation was conducted independently by four highly experienced laboratory scientists and the results were categorized as CTC positive, CTC negative or CTC unclear (Fig.  ).

## ctDNA analysis
Whole blood samples were collected in 10 mL Cell-Free DNA BCT® CE tubes (218,997, Streck, La Vista, United States of America) and processed within 72 h of collection. CfDNA was isolated using the Chemagen 360 protocol. To measure the DNA concentration of the purified DNA, the Quantus™ Fluorometer (Promega, Madison, Wisconsin) was used. The purified and quantified DNA samples were analyzed using the Archer® LiquidPlex™ Universal Solid Tumor Panel for Illumina® (Integrated DNA Technologies, Coralville, United States of America) for targeted next generation sequencing of circulating cell-free tumor DNA (ccfDNA/cfDNA/ctDNA) from 29 genes commonly associated with solid tumor type cancers including most frequently altered genes in melanoma (BRAF, NRAS, TP53). The amplified and labeled DNA is quantified using the KAPA library quantification Kit for Illumina® platforms (Roche, Basel, Switzerland) and prepared for the sequencing on the Illumina NextSeq500® platform (Illumina®, San Diego, United States of America). The raw sequencing data was analyzed with Archer Analysis pipeline (version 6.2.7; Integrated DNA Technologies, Coralville, United States of America). Archer® LiquidPlex™ was able to detect variants at 0.5% allele frequency.

## Interpretation of identified sequence variants
Variant classification was performed according to the guidelines for the interpretation of sequence variants by the American College of Medical Genetics (ACMG) and the Association for Molecular Pathology (AMP) . Variants were categorized as either pathogenic, likely pathogenic, variance of unknown significance (VUS), or benign according to their effect on DNA splicing, on protein function, their population frequency, etc. . The classification was done using multiple databases (ClinVar, VarSome, COSMIC—Catalogue Of Somatic Mutations In Cancer, Jackson Laboratory Clinical Knowledgebase, Genome Aggregation Database, SpliceAI lookup). In this work, all gene alterations were considered to ensure that patterns in the occurrence of non-pathogenic variants were also evaluated. However, for better clarity only pathogenic and likely pathogenic mutations were included in the statistical evaluation and creation of figures.

## Cell culture
Cultured tumor cells were used as an internal spike-in control to determine the CTC recovery during the enrichment and staining process. Therefore COLO-205 (ATCC®) cells were cultured in RPMI 1640 with 10% FBS, 1% penicillin and streptomycin. For recovery determination, COLO-205 cells were prestained with 0.75 mg CellTracker™ Deep Red (C34565, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, United States of America) or CellTracker™ Green CMFDA (5-chloromethylfluoresceindiacetate) (C7025, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, United States of America) per liter RPMI 1640 without any additives. Alternatively to the prestain, the spiked-in cells were stained using the Alexa Fluor 488 labelled C-11 cytokeratin Pan monoclonal antibody (MA1-10,325, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, United States of America; 1:10) additionally to the melanoma staining cocktail.

## Statistical analysis
For survival analysis cause-specific progression-free survival was taken into account with either progress or death related to melanoma disease marking an event. Log rank test and cox regression analysis were applied to establish associations. For conducting statistical analyses and creating figures, we utilized GraphPad Prism (version 10.4.1; https://www.graphpad.com) and R (version 4.2.1; R Core Team; https://www.r-project.org), accessed via RStudio (version 2022.07.0), using the ggplot2, survival and survminer packages. Statistical significance was defined by p-values less than 0.05, with values below 0.01 considered as highly significant. P-values ranging from 0.05 to 0.1 were interpreted as weak evidence for a statistically significant relationship.
