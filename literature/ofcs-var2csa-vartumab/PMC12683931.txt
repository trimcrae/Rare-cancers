## Background
Placental malaria (PM) is among the leading public health problems associated with adverse outcomes for the mother, the foetus, and the newborn, particularly in sub-Saharan Africa (SSA). Although there are many studies published on PM in SSA, comprehensive evidence for targeted interventions is limited. Thus, this study aimed to synthesize evidence on the prevalence, predictors, and adverse perinatal outcomes of PM in SSA.

## Methods
Pertinent studies online searching, selection, data extraction, and quality assessment were conducted. Data were extracted using Microsoft Excel and analyzed by STATA Version 17.0. Pooled estimates were determined by random-effect meta-analyses. Sub-group analysis was conducted to account for sources of heterogeneity.

## Results
Overall, 178 observational studies published from 2000 to 2024 involving 93,809 parturient women from 23 countries were included. The overall pooled prevalence of PM in SSA was 25.1% (95% CI: 22.0–28.3), which was 16.9% (95% CI: 12.3–21.6), 19.1% (95% CI: 17.4–20.9), 24.8% (95% CI: 20.9–28.8), and 34.8% (95% CI: 29.3–40.2) by RDT, microscopy, PCR, and histopathology, respectively. Predictors of PM were: younger age (OR = 2.50 95% CI: 1.89–3.12), primigravidae (OR = 2.65 95% CI: 2.12–3.18), primiparae (OR = 1.62 95% CI: 1.23–2.01), not using insecticide-treated nets (OR = 2.11 95% CI: 1.34–2.89), not using ANC (OR = 7.43 95% CI: 2.47–12.40), not using intermittent preventive treatment during pregnancy (OR = 1.98 95% CI: 1.42–2.54), MiP history (OR = 3.05 95% CI: 1.85–4.25), HIV infection (OR = 2.67 95% CI: 1.22–4.62), and delivering during rainy season (OR = 2.24 95% CI: 1.05–4.07). The pooled prevalence of gestational malaria and congenital malaria was 22.1% (95% CI: 19.8–24.5) and 7.6% (95% CI: 6.5–8.8), respectively. The pooled prevalence of LBW, maternal anaemia, preterm delivery, small-for-gestational age, stillbirth and neonatal malaria was 12.7% (95% CI: 11.1–14.3), 39.0% (95% CI: 34.1–44.0), 11.2% (95% CI: 9.0–13.4), 17.9% (95% CI: 13.7–22.1), 3.4% (95% CI: 2.0–5.1), and 6.9% (95% CI: 3.9–9.8), respectively. PM increased the odds of maternal anaemia (OR = 2.18 95% CI: 1.73–2.63) and LBW (OR = 1.71 95% CI: 1.26–2.16).

## Conclusion
Placental malaria was a multifactorial and multifaceted significant health problem with many adverse perinatal outcomes in SSA. Concerted efforts targeting the identified predictors through multidimensional approaches are needed.
The protocol of this systematic review and meta-analysis was registered on PROSPERO with the code number: CRD42024528199.

## Background
Malaria mainly affects pregnant women and young children, particularly in SSA. It remained among the major causes of child and maternal morbidity and mortality [ – ]. The pathophysiology of MiP is partly due to the presence of the placenta suitable for parasite sequestration and development, and the downregulated immunity, rendering the pregnant woman susceptible to the infection and disease complications [ , ]. Malaria in pregnancy (MiP), especially with P. falciparum, often involves PM since Plasmodium-infected red blood cells (iRBCs) expressing a unique variant surface antigen chondroitin sulfate A (VAR2CSA) sequester in the placenta through binding to CSA receptors on the syncytiotrophoblast surface [ – ]. Although most inflammatory responses and infiltration of the intervillous space with the parasite and haemozoin pigment are mainly associated with P. falciparum, PM can also be caused by other species of Plasmodium, including P. vivax [ , – ].
The pathological mechanism behind the damaging outcomes of PM is largely associated with the activation of local inflammation due to the maternal immune response to the sequestration. This inflammatory response is characterized by the accumulation of activated maternal mononuclear cells, such as monocytes and macrophages, within the intervillous space. These activated immune cells contribute to placental pathologies through increased secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines, chemokines, and complement factors, together with the decline in key angiogenic and placental growth factors [ , ]. The infiltration of the intervillous space with the parasite, pigment, and immune cells leads to hypertrophy and fibrinoid necrosis of the villi, which can interfere with the nutrient, oxygen, and waste exchange between the mother and the foetus. Besides, the heightened inflammation may interrupt the normal development of the placenta, especially if the infection occurs during early gestation, potentially impairing the formation of the artery and its permeability [ , ]. These events impair and damage the development, structure, and function of the placenta, which can lead to several adverse pregnancy outcomes involving the mother, foetus and the child [ – , – ]. Structural damage to the placenta includes broken syncytiotrophoblast, syncytial knotting, thickening of the basement membrane, narrowed intervillous space, widespread fibrinoid necrosis, and dysregulated trophoblast apoptosis. This compromises the capacity of the placenta, causing placental insufficiency to support foetal wellbeing and growth, thereby increasing the risk of intrauterine foetal growth restriction (IUGR), preterm delivery (PTD), stillbirth, low birth weight (LBW), and neonatal mortality [ , , , ]. Therefore, the dysregulated cytokine level, infiltration of the intervillous space, and damage to the placenta mediate the pathophysiology and adverse consequences of PM [ , ].
Malaria in pregnancy, particularly PM, results in a multitude of adverse outcomes at an individual and community level [ , , , – ]. The type of adverse outcomes occurring to the mother, the foetus, and the newborn varies by the intensity of malaria transmission [ , ]. Multigravidae women living in high transmission settings are less susceptible to PM, whereas all women are susceptible in low transmission settings [ , , ]. Complications of PM in primigravidae are due to a lack of placental parasite-specific immunity that develops during subsequent pregnancies [ , ]. Often, PM is associated with a high risk of miscarriage, stillbirth, neonatal and infant mortality. By impairing the adequate supply of nutrients to the foetus, PM can result in PTD and IUGR, which in turn leads to LBW [ , , , ]. Besides, intensified cytokine release and heightened inflammatory response to PM can also lead to prematurity and poor development, ultimately resulting in LBW [ , , , ]. Moreover, a child born to a mother with PM is at higher risk of congenital malaria (CM), neonatal malaria (NM) and neonatal anaemia, which collectively lead to poor growth, impaired cognitive development, and short stature [ , – ]. It is also documented that children born to mothers with PM have an increased risk of malaria during the first year of life [ , – ], in addition to increased susceptibility to other febrile diseases [ , , ]. On the other hand, chronic PM with the accumulation of pigmented monocytes that release inflammatory cytokines is associated with maternal anaemia through bone marrow suppression [ ]. Taken together, the negative impact of PM goes beyond the mother to impact her offspring starting from the foetus [ , , , , ].
The global prevalence of PM varies across different parts of the world, mainly due to variation in malaria endemicity, intensity of prevention activity, diagnostic tools, and specimen type involved in the study, and other reasons [ – ]. Its prevalence ranges from 4.4% in Papua New Guinea [ ] to 10.3% in India [ ], and 11.0% in Colombia [ ], as well as 9.0% worldwide [ ]. Various factors determine the risk of PM [ , – ]. These include: socio-demographic factors such as age and place of residence [ , ], obstetric factors such as gravidity and parity [ ], health intervention factors such as ANC and vector control interventions [ – ], as well as history of malaria [ – ].
To avert the adverse outcome of MiP, accurate and timely data are critical. Effective mitigation of MiP depends upon a piece of timely and comprehensive evidence generated through systematic selection and synthesis of data from relevant studies [ , – ]. In the last 100 years, several primary studies have been conducted on PM in different parts of SSA. However, these studies vary in their study period, design, sample size, specimen type, and laboratory tests used to diagnose PM. Besides, to the best of our knowledge, there is no synthesized and meta-analyzed data showing the pooled prevalence, predictors, and adverse outcomes of PM in SSA, which carries the largest burden of MiP and suffers from its deleterious outcomes [ , ]. This makes effective mitigation of this ever-evolving parasitic disease difficult and complicates its negative impact. As a result, systematically synthesized, optimally comprehensive, and timely data are needed to generate robust evidence that can guide designing a targeted intervention for mitigating PM through the prevention of MiP. Such data enable designing an intervention tailored to the geographical setting, time, and the dynamic characteristics of the parasite, people, and vector [ , , ]. Therefore, this systematic review and meta-analysis (SRMA) was aimed at determining the pooled prevalence, predictors, and adverse perinatal outcomes (APOs) of PM in SSA. The results of this study can help develop an effective mitigation strategy accounting for different factors of PM. In addition, this study will fill gaps in the body of knowledge on the Epidemiology, diagnosis, prevention, and control of PM in SSA and beyond, ultimately contributing to improving maternal and child health.

## Methods

## Study design and protocol registration
This SRMA was conducted to assess the pooled prevalence and predictors of PM and its associated APOs in SSA. This SRMA was conducted according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analysis (The PRISMA 2020 statement) guideline [ ] (Supplement Table 2). The protocol of this SRMA was registered on the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) with identification code: CRD42024528199 and made publicly available from: https://www.crd.york.ac.uk/prospero/display_record.php?ID=CRD42024528199.

## Data source and search strategy
Studies reporting the prevalence, predictors, and adverse outcomes of PM published up to December 31, 2024, were searched online from major global databases and search engines. The major online sources searched were MEDLINE/PubMed, Malaria in Pregnancy Library, African Journal Online, and Google Scholar. The search strategy for this SRMA was developed based on the CoCoPop (Context, Condition, and Population) framework of the research question. The search terms included: study population (parturient or delivering women); condition (prevalence, predictors, and adverse outcomes of PM by P. falciparum or P. vivax or P. ovale or P. malariae); context (In SSA; diagnosed from placental blood using RDT or microscopy or PCR or from placental biopsy using histopathology; published in English; up to December 2024). To develop a robust search strategy, we used controlled descriptors such as MeSH terms and Boolean operators, in addition to identified keywords. Two authors (AA and SB) independently searched the articles. In the case of a lack of the needed data and/or any vague concerns in the article, the corresponding author was contacted via email. The full search strategy conducted for this SRMA using PubMed can be found in Supplement Text 1.

## Eligibility

## Inclusion criteria
Studies meeting the following inclusion criteria were included in this SRMA: observational studies (such as descriptive and analytical cross-sectional studies, cohort, and case–control studies) conducted in SSA and published in English reporting PM detected by RDT and/or microscopy and/or PCR and/or Histopathology from placental blood and/or biopsy.

## Exclusion criteria
Studies were excluded from this SRMA if they met at least one of the following exclusion criteria: (a) case reports, case series, opinion, letter to the editor, commentary, and review articles; (b) studies that took data from the previously published study without including a new data; (c) studies with no available full-text document; (d) retrospective cohort and case–control studies in which the exposure of interest was PM cases, and the cases were PM patients.

## Study selection
All articles retrieved through the search strategy were imported to and managed by EndNote Version 21.0 (Thomson Reuters, NY, USA). After excluding duplicates, titles, and abstracts were independently screened for inclusion into full-text review by AA and SB. Discrepancies between these two authors were resolved through discussion. In case of disagreement between these two authors’ reports, the final decision was made by the second group of authors (AZ, DY, GT, and JB). For articles that are considered relevant, their full text was reviewed for inclusion in this SRMA. Reasons for exclusion of articles after full-text review were documented in the PRISMA flowchart that shows the process of inclusion and exclusion of studies [ , ].

## Data extraction
Two authors (AA and SB) independently extracted the data. When there were differences between these two authors concerning the extracted data, the discrepancy was resolved through discussion. If the issue was not resolved through discussion, the second group of authors (AZ, DY, GT, and JB) made the final decision. Data including the name of the first author, publication year, study period, study setting, country, study design, sample size, number of positives, type of specimen, laboratory test, predictors and adverse outcomes of PM with their effect measure, and other pertinent data were extracted and recorded in a Microsoft Excel spreadsheet. When authors found multiple publications from the same dataset, the article reporting the relevant data in extractable form was used. Repeated cross-sectional studies that reported the relevant data during baseline study were used for this SRMA.

## Quality appraisal
Two reviewers (AA and SB) independently assessed and scored the quality of the selected studies according to the Newcastle–Ottawa scale (NOS) [ ]. This scale is used for observational studies, where studies are scored between one and nine stars for nine questions, that cover three thematic areas: selection (representativeness of the sample, sample size, non-response rate, ascertainment of the exposure), comparability (the subjects in different outcome groups are comparable, based on the study design or analysis; and confounding factors are controlled), and outcome (assessment of the outcome and statistical test). A study was given one star for each item within the selection and outcome categories. For comparability (if comparison was made by sub-group analysis or multivariable analysis whenever required), a maximum of two stars was awarded. Inconsistencies in the findings were resolved by discussion between the two authors (AA and SB), and arbitration was made by the second group of authors (AZ, DY, GT, and JB).

## Data analysis and synthesis
The extracted data from the included studies were entered into Microsoft Excel and exported into STATA version 17.0 (Stata Corp., Texas, USA) for meta-analysis. The pooled prevalence (together with its 95% CIs) of PM among the overall population from all studies and sub-groups was estimated using a random-effects model. Additionally, the prevalence of PM was estimated as the ratio of the number of parturient women with PM (numerator) to the sample size (denominator). Potential publication bias was assessed by visually observing the symmetry of funnel plots as well as using Egger’s test. The degree of heterogeneity across included studies was quantified by using the I2 statistics. An I2 cutoff values of 25.0%, 50.0%, and 75.0% were assumed to represent low, medium, and high heterogeneity, respectively [ ]. A Chi-square test based on Cochran’s Q statistic was used to statistically test for heterogeneity and generate a p-value. A p-value less than 0.10 was considered evidence of heterogeneity. The effect size expressed as OR and its corresponding 95% CI was analyzed to estimate a pooled OR to determine a significant association between PM and its predictors. Sensitivity analyses were carried out to assess heterogeneity between the included studies due to outlier studies. Sub-group analyses were carried out to look for potential explanations for the heterogeneity, considering region, status of MiP, laboratory tests, specimen type, sample size, and species of the Plasmodium as grouping variables. Results of this SRMA are presented in narration, tables, and figures by forest plot.

## Outcome of the study
The outcomes of this SRMA are the prevalence, predictors, and adverse outcomes of PM in SSA.

## Operational definitions

➢ Adverse perinatal outcome: Pregnancy outcomes after 28th week of gestation, including stillbirth, LBW, PTD, SGA, maternal anaemia, and NM.
➢ Congenital malaria: Detection of Plasmodium infection in umbilical cord blood by RDT and/or microscopy and/or PCR.
➢ Gestational malaria: Detection of Plasmodium infection in peripheral blood of parturient women by RDT and/or microscopy and/or PCR.
➢ LBW: Weight of a newborn less than 2500 g.
➢ Maternal anaemia: Hb concentration below 11.0 g/dl in peripheral blood of parturient women.
➢ Neonatal malaria: Detection of Plasmodium parasite in the peripheral blood of a newborn (within seven days of birth).
➢ Parasitaemia: Presence of asexual stage parasites in thick and/or thin placental blood films.
➢ Placental malaria: Detection of iRBCs and/or malaria pigment in inflammatory immune cells and/or in fibrin by Histopathology (in placental biopsy) among parturient women. And/or, detection of Plasmodium infection by RDT and/or microscopy and/or PCR (in placental blood) among parturient women.
➢ Preterm delivery: Birth before the 37 completed weeks of gestational age.
➢ Stillbirth: Death of a baby after the 28th week of gestation.

## Result

## Search history and process of study selection
Initially, a total of 18,942 records were retrieved mainly from PubMed and the MiP Library. Of these records, 6,843, 3,901, and 8,942 were removed at duplicate removal, title and abstract screening, and full-text screening levels, respectively. Finally, 72 and 178 studies were included for qualitative synthesis and quantitative meta-analysis, respectively (Fig.  ). 

## Characteristics of the included studies
Totally, 208 studies were included in the descriptive part of this SRMA work. These studies were published from 1925 to 2024 involving 113,348 parturient women across 24 countries in SSA. The pooled prevalence of PM in SSA from 1925 to 1999 was 33.6% (95% CI: 27.8–39.6). Sierra Leone and Nigeria accounted for the oldest and the most publications on PM in SSA, respectively. The overall pooled data from those studies showed declining pattern of PM prevalence in SSA along the time from 1925 to 2024. Details of those studies published before 2000 are provided in supplements files (Supplement Table 3, Supplement Figs. and ).
Only 178 studies that were published in the twenty-first century were included in the detail statistical analysis of this SRMA. These studies were published from 2000 to 2024 involving 93,809 parturient women across 23 countries in SSA. The number of study participants per the included study ranged from 21 to 4,831 participants with the mean of 527 (± 533.14). The majority of the included studies were published from Western region of SSA (45.5%) followed by the Eastern region (27.5%). About one- fifth (18.5%) of these articles were published from Nigeria (Fig.  and Table  ). 
The median number of studies published per year in SSA was seven, ranging from two to 12 studies per year. The minimum number of studies published per year was two in 2001 and the maximum was 12 in 2012 and 2022 (Fig.  and Table ). 
Microscopy and placental blood were the most frequently used type of diagnostic tool and specimen, respectively, to diagnose PM in SSA. The majority of the included studies used only microscopy (41.0%), and only placental blood (61.2%) to diagnose PM. The minimum number of diagnostic tools used in a single study was one, and the maximum was four. Six studies used a combination of four diagnostic tools, whereas 114 studies used a single tool (Fig.  and Table  ). 
Nearly a quarter [ ] of the included studies have simultaneously assessed the Plasmodium infection in the three (peripheral, placental, and cord) blood compartments of the parturient women. Nineteen, 24, and nine studies did not specifically report the species of Plasmodium detected from peripheral, placental, and cord blood compartments, respectively. Plasmodium falciparum single-species-infection was the most commonly reported type of the Plasmodium infection in all blood compartments. Except few participants found to have PM in the absence of P. falciparum within a study (such as a study done in Benin by Doritchamou et al. 2018) [ ], no study reported the overall prevalence of PM with a single species of Plasmodium other than P. falciparum. Other species of Plasmodium were found to be a mixed-species infection. All studies involving placental blood tests reported the presence of P. falciparum in all PM cases, both as a single or mixed-species infection. Double and triple species-mixed-infections were reported in peripheral and placental blood compartments. The four well-known human malaria-causing species of Plasmodium were reported from peripheral and placental blood. None of the included studies reported P. ovale and triple species mixed-infection in cord blood (Fig.  and Table  ). 

## Quality assessment results of the included studies
The mean cumulative quality score of the included studies was 6.7 (± 1.23) out of nine, with the corresponding minimum and maximum cumulative scores of two and nine, respectively. The cumulative quality score of 105 studies was above the mean. At least 75 studies tried to prevent selection bias. One hundred sixty-eight studies tried to provide a comparable result by generating a disaggregated report for sub-groups and/or conducting multivariable statistical analyses. An appropriate statistical test was conducted in 156 studies based on the reported aim of that particular study. Only 76 studies provided a complete report according to the type of the specimen and the laboratory test involved, and the species of the Plasmodium detected (Table  and Supplement Table 3) (Table ). 

## Publication bias and small-study effect
We assessed for publication bias by conducting a qualitative and quantitative analysis. The observed asymmetry in visual inspection of the funnel plot and statistically significant finding in a Regression-based Egger test (p < 0.001) depicted the potential presence of a small study effect (Fig.  ). 

## Prevalence of placental malaria in Sub-Saharan Africa
The overall pooled prevalence of PM in SSA was 25.1% (95% CI: 22.0–28.3). The prevalence ranged from 0.7% (Tanzania in 2014) [ ] to 86.8% (Burkina Faso in 2022) [ ]. The pooled prevalence of PM by RDT, Microscopy, PCR, and Histopathology was 16.9% (95% CI: 12.3–21.6), 19.1% (95% CI: 17.4–20.9), 24.8% (95% CI: 20.9–28.8), and 34.8% (95% CI: 29.3–40.2), respectively (Figs.  and ). 
The prevalence of PM detected from only blood, only biopsy, and combination of both specimens was 20.9% (95% CI: 17.6–24.5), 37.0% (95% CI: 26.3–48.2), and 29.0% (95% CI: 23.1–35.3), respectively. Disregarding the 24 studies that did not specifically report the Plasmodium species detected in the placental blood, 7.1% (9/127) of the PM was a mixed-species infection. A P. falciparum and P. vivax mixed-species infection was the most common (55.6% = 5/9) type of mixed-species PM (Fig.  ).
The prevalence of PM in SSA was heterogeneous, prompting the need to conduct sub-group analysis. According to the result of the sub-group analyses, the pooled prevalence of PM was higher in the studies published from the Western region of SSA (28.5%), with sample size below 100 (36.2%), involving only HIV-positive parturient women (32.4%) and those with MiP infection (69.2%), as well as those infected with P. falciparum species (23.7%), compared to their corresponding counterparts. Besides, a higher pooled prevalence of PM was recorded by studies conducted using only placental biopsy specimen (37.0%) and by using a combination of diagnostic tools (28.2%) (Supplement Figs. 3 - 13).
According to the time-trend analysis, the mean annual prevalence of PM in SSA ranged from 14.2% (in 2023) to 45.1% (in 2001). The overall time trend of this pooled data showed a slightly declining pattern of PM prevalence in SSA from 2000 to 2024 (Fig.  and Supplement Fig. ). 

## Predictors of placental malaria in sub-Saharan Africa
Thirty-seven studies that reported 10 predictors of PM (OR together with its 95% CI) were included in this SRMA to estimate a pooled OR of each predictor variable. The odds of PM was higher among a younger (OR = 2.50 95% CI: 1.89–3.12 p < 0.001), primigravidae (OR = 2.65 95% CI: 2.12–3.18 p < 0.001), primiparae (OR = 1.62 95% CI: 1.23–2.01 p < 0.001), rainy season delivered (OR = 2.24 95% CI: 1.05–4.07 p = 0.012), and HIV infected (OR = 2.67 95% CI: 1.22–4.62 p = 0.006) parturient women as well as those with history of malaria (OR = 3.05 95% CI: 1.85–4.25 p < 0.001) than their respective counterparts in SSA. The odds of PM was higher among those with no and/or inadequate utilization of ITN (OR = 2.11 95% CI: 1.34–2.89 p < 0.001), ANC (OR = 7.43 95% CI: 2.47–12.40 p < 0.001), and IPTp (OR = 1.98 95% CI: 1.42–2.54 p < 0.001). Using at least one dose of IPTp reduced the odds of PM by 69.8% (OR = 0.30 95% CI: 0.18–0.41 p < 0.001) (Fig.  and Supplement Table 4). 

## Prevalence of gestational malaria and congenital malaria in sub-Saharan Africa
Based on the data from 99 included studies that reported GM data, the overall pooled prevalence of GM among parturient women with PM in SSA was 22.1% (95% CI: 19.8–24.5). The prevalence of GM ranged from 0.0% in Sudan in 2024 [ ] to 81.6% in Uganda in 2017 [ ]. Plasmodium falciparum was the most frequently detected species among majority of GM cases (Fig.  Panel A). 
Based on the data from 49 included studies that reported the CM data, the overall pooled prevalence of CM among parturient women with PM in SSA was 7.6% (95% CI: 6.5–8.8). The prevalence of CM ranged from 0.0% in Sudan in 2024 [ ] to 54.2% in Nigeria in 2005 [ ]. Plasmodium falciparum was the most frequently detected species among majority of CM cases (Fig.  Panel B).

## Adverse perinatal outcomes of placental malaria in sub-Saharan Africa
Eighty-five of the included studies reported at least one APO of PM together with PM data in SSA. The least and the most frequently reported APOs were SGA (6 studies) and LBW (65 studies), respectively (Fig.  ). 

## Low birth weight
Sixty-five studies reported the prevalence of LBW together with PM. The pooled prevalence of LBW newborn among parturient women with PM in SSA was 12.7% (95% CI: 11.1–14.3). The lowest prevalence (0.8%) was recorded in Kenya in 2004 [ ], and the highest prevalence (53.0%) was recorded in Sudan in 2021 [ ] (Fig.  Panel A). 

## Maternal anaemia
Fifty-four studies assessed the prevalence of maternal anaemia together with PM. The pooled prevalence of maternal anaemia among parturient women with PM in SSA was 39.0% (95% CI: 34.1–44.0). The lowest prevalence was 6.2% (in Nigeria in 2011) [ ] and the highest was 82.8% (in Sudan in 2013) [ ] (Fig.  Panel B).

## Preterm delivery
Thirty-one studies reported the prevalence of PTD together with PM. The pooled prevalence of PTD among parturient women with PM in SSA was 11.2% (95% CI: 9.0–13.4). The lowest (0.6% in Malawi) [ ] and the highest (26.5% in Ghana) [ ] prevalence were recorded in 2013 and 2010, respectively (Fig.  Panel C).

## Small for gestational age
Six studies reported the prevalence of small for its completed gestational age together with PM. The pooled prevalence of SGA among parturient women with PM in SSA was 17.9% (95% CI: 13.7–22.1). The lowest (11.0% in Uganda) [ ] and highest (23.3% in Malawi) [ ] prevalence was recorded in 2020 and 2013, respectively (Fig.  Panel D).

## Stillbirth
Eight studies reported the prevalence of stillbirth together with PM. The pooled prevalence of stillbirth among parturient women with PM in SSA was 3.4% (95% CI: 2.0–5.1). The lowest (1.1% in Mozambique) [ ] and the highest (12.7% in Ghana) [ ] prevalence was recorded in 2018 and 2015, respectively (Fig.  Panel E).

## Neonatal malaria
Seven studies reported the prevalence of NM together with PM. The pooled prevalence of NM among parturient women with PM in SSA was 6.9% (95% CI: 3.9–9.8). The lowest (0.0%) [ ] and the highest (46.7%) [ ] prevalence was recorded in Nigeria in 2022 and in Uganda in 2005, respectively (Fig.  Panel F).

## Association of adverse perinatal outcomes with placental malaria in s ub-Saharan Africa
Ten studies reported the association of PM with at least one APO using odds ratio. Based on five studies reports, PM was significantly associated with higher odds of maternal anaemia (OR = 2.18 95% CI: 1.73–2.63 p < 0.001). Based on six studies reports, PM was significantly associated with higher odds of LBW (OR = 1.71 95% CI: 1.26–2.16 p < 0.001). However, based on two studies reports, PM showed a borderline association with the odds of PTD (OR = 1.43 95% CI: 0.88–1.97 p = 0.041) (Fig.  ). 

## Discussion
Malaria in pregnancy remains a significant public health problem, particularly in SSA. The disease poses a threat to pregnant women, foetuses, newborns, children, and beyond. It also poses an economic threat not only to the family, but also to the overall health system and economic development of this African region [ , ]. Placental malaria involves various pathological changes that affect the normal development, structure, and function of the placenta, resulting in a multitude of APOs such as anaemia, IUGR, prematurity, and LBW [ , ]. Although there are many primary studies conducted in SSA, there is a paucity of comprehensive data that can guide designing a targeted intervention to mitigate the burden and adverse outcomes of PM. To fill this gap in evidence, this comprehensive SRMA revealed the pooled prevalence, predictors, and APOs of PM in SSA by systematically reviewing and synthesizing data from 178 original articles published in English during the last 25 years.
The overall pooled prevalence of PM in SSA was 25.1% (95% CI: 22.0–28.3). This prevalence varied by country, the status of HIV and MiP infection for inclusion, the type of specimen and diagnostic tool used, and the species of the parasite detected. The dominant species found was P. falciparum. The pooled estimate reflects the substantial magnitude of PM found in SSA with many APOs with potential clinical and public health importance thereby suggesting the need to strengthen efforts for timely and effectively mitigation of MiP [ , , ]. Considering the observed significant prevalence of PM and its APOs together with the detection of P. falciparum in all PM cases in SSA, where P. falciparum predominates signals the underlying high burden of PM that has high potential to sustain or even escalate if no effective and timely intervention is taken. This finding was consistent with previous SRMA reports that identified P. falciparum as the major species causing PM. The expression of VAR2CSA on the surface of P. falciparum iRBCs orchestrates parasite sequestration in the placenta [ – ]. However, the recorded overall prevalence of PM was higher than similar SRMAs that reported the pooled prevalence of (microscopic = 3.5% versus sub-microscopic = 9.0%) and (microscopic = 5.1% versus sub-microscopic = 15.4%) in the world and Africa, respectively [ ]. The discrepancy might be due to the use of placental biopsy, histopathological assay, and the inclusion of very old studies in the current SRMA, unlike that SRMA published in 2023 pooled data generated by the detection of PM from placental blood using microscopy and PCR [ ]. In fact, it is plausible that the inclusion of studies that were conducted by involving placental biopsy histopathology into the SRMA will generate a higher pooled estimate of PM, partly owing to the high sensitivity of histopathology [ , – ]. This notion was further strengthened by the higher pooled prevalence of PM detected by histopathology (34.8%) than qPCR (24.8%) than microscopy (19.1%) than RDT (16.9%) in the current SRMA. This variation in the prevalence by different diagnostic tools reflects the limited sensitivity of the routinely used conventional diagnostic tools, hence implication for policy, practice and research to strengthen the use of highly sensitive diagnostic tools to show the true prevalence of PM [ , , – ].
The current SRMA identified 10 factors independently predicting the odds of PM in SSA. These predictors were: being young, primigravidae, and primiparae; lack and/or inadequate utilization of ITN, IPTp, and ANC; having childbirth during the rainy season; as well as having HIV and MiP. Accordingly, these predictive factors are discussed below. Based on the findings of the current SRMA, being young increased the odds of PM by 2.5-fold among parturient women in SSA. A consistent finding was reported by recently published SRMAs that identified higher odds of malaria among younger women [ , ]. Because age often goes in line with gravidity (from the perspective of immunity to malaria), along which immunity develops, might explain the reason for the association with lowered risk of PM with increasing age, which has potentially likely conferred the development of a gravidity-dependent, partially acquired immunity to PM [ ]. The current SRMA finding showed that being primigravidae and primiparae increased the odds of PM by 2.6-fold and 1.6-fold, respectively. A consistent finding was reported from an SRMA on asymptomatic malaria in pregnancy (AMiP) in SSA in which primigravidae were found to have a higher odds of AMiP [ ]. This might be because acquired immunity developed during successive pregnancies through repeated exposure helps multigravidae women produce anti-adhesion antibody that prevents the sequestration of Plasmodium-iRBCs in the placenta [ , , ]. Given acquired immunity to PM is attained through the progressive frequency of pregnancy, obstetric factors such as gravidity [ , , , ], and parity [ , ] determine the risk of malaria among pregnant and parturient women. According to the results of the current SRMA, lack and/or inadequate utilization of ITN (OR = 2.1) and ANC (OR = 7.4) increased the odds of PM. On the other hand, utilization of IPTp and ANC reduced the odds of PM in SSA. Utilization of IPTP reduced the odds of PM by 68%. Moreover, this finding was further consolidated by almost two-fold increased odds of PM among parturient women with no and/or inadequate utilization of IPTp recorded in this SRMA. An appropriate use of IPTp reduces the odds of PM through the prevention of MiP or the timely clearance of parasitaemia. By recognizing its indispensable benefit, the WHO recommended the use of an IPTp-SP as a vital tool to mitigate MiP and its complications to PM [ , , , , ]. A consistent finding was reported by a recently published SRMA that stated the use of anti-malarial drugs during pregnancy reduced the odds of having MiP [ ]. Health interventions such as ANC service, health information, and vector control measures (such as ITN) play vital roles in the prevention of MiP, particularly PM [ , , , , ]. Conversely, it is conceivable that lack and/or poor utilization of basic ANC services and anti-malarial interventions such as ITN, IPTp, and IRS raises the likelihood of PM infection [ , , , ]. Reports from studies conducted across various places in the world strengthened this concept [ , , ]. Moreover, recently published SRMAs highlighted the important role of ANC service utilization plays in reducing the odds of having MiP [ , ]. The current SRMA showed that having a history of MiP increased the odds of PM by about 3.1-fold. This finding was supported by consistent reports from studies conducted in Papua New Guinea [ ], and Colombia [ ]. It is plausible that recurrent infection increases the chance of PM though frequent inflammation compromising the integrity of the placenta and other immune response in peripheral circulation forcing the parasite to sequester in the less strong immune response area-the placenta. This has clinical, operational and policy implication suggesting early diagnosis and treatment of MiP cases to reduce risk of relapse and recrudescence, and strengthen vector control activities to prevent re-infection [ , , ]. In addition to this, having an HIV infection and child delivery during the rainy season raised the odds of PM by about 2.6-fold and 2.4-fold, respectively. Basically, it is plausible that HIV infection and rainy season render pregnant women susceptible to MiP (which in turn leads to PM through placental sequestration from peripheral parasitaemia) by compromising maternal immunity, including placental integrity, and providing a conducive breeding environment for the mosquito population, respectively [ , , , ]. These findings reflect that having a history of MiP increases the chance of having PM, thereby prompting the importance of active screening and treatment of pregnant women in their early gestation to prevent PM and its adverse consequences for the mother and the foetus [ , , , ]. Overall, identification of these important risk factors translates into the potential contribution of these factors to the burden of PM, and hence the need for designing strategies tailored to these factors for targeted intervention to mitigate the burden of PM in SSA [ , , ].
About half (85/178) of the included studies reported at least one of the six APOs of PM discussed in this SRMA. The pooled prevalence of maternal anaemia among parturient women with PM in SSA was 39.0%. Besides, PM was found to be significantly associated with raising the odds of maternal anaemia by over twofold in SSA. A consistent finding of this result was reported from a study that found pregnant women with Plasmodium infection had by 2.4-fold increased odds of having maternal anaemia [ ]. It is haematologically plausible that PM can be associated with the development of maternal anaemia potentially through decreased erythropoiesis through cytokine-mediated bone marrow suppression and/or increased haemolysis ultimately leading to a decreased RBC count and/or Hb concentration [ – , ]. In fact, supportive evidence indicating the decreasing magnitude of maternal anaemia by prevention of malaria using IPTp was reported by Desai et al. [ ]. The recorded association of PM with increased risk of maternal anaemia reflects the impact of malaria on the haematological aspects of pregnancy (which is very critical for the health pregnancy and its outcome) urging the need for focusing on prevention and control of MiP, including PM, particularly in SSA where P. falciparum (which the major species associated with anaemia and PM) is highly prevalent [ – ]. The pooled prevalence of LBW newborn among parturient women with PM in SSA was 12.7%. Besides, PM was found to be statistically significantly associated with increased odds of giving birth to an LBW newborn by 1.7-fold among parturient women with PM in SSA. It is conceivable that the heightened inflammation in the intervillous space of the infected placenta by damaging the structure, development, and function of the placenta, renders placental insufficiency that disrupts the normal delivery of nutrients and oxygen to the foetus. This compromises foetal growth, thereby ultimately resulting in LBW [ – ]. Similar to the current finding, pregnant women with Plasmodium infection were found to have increased odds of giving birth to an LBW infant in some studies [ , , , ]. The pooled prevalence of PTD, SGA, and stillbirth among parturient women with PM in SSA was 11.2%, 17.9%, and 3.4%, respectively. A concordant finding was reported from a similar SRMA by Satapathy et al. 2024 [ ]. The recorded prevalence and association of maternal anaemia, PTD, LBW, and SGA together indicate the negative impact of MiP in creating a big problem by resulting in small babies and anemic mothers, and hence implication to the overall health, and socio-economic aspects of an individual and the community at large [ – , ]. Furthermore, the overall pooled prevalence of CM and NM in SSA was 11.2% and 6.9%, respectively. Consistent with the current SRMA finding, the prevalence of CM in SSA ranges from 0% to 54.2%, depending upon malaria endemicity, geographic area, study design, and the type of diagnostic test used in the study. This pooled prevalence of CM and NM in the current SRMA was lower than the findings from other SRMA that reported 39.5% and 12.1%, respectively [ ]. This discrepancy might be due to the time of publication, laboratory test, and type of specimen used to diagnose CM among the included studies [ , – , , ]. Congenital malaria is the presence of the Plasmodium parasite in the umbilical cord and/or peripheral blood of a newborn during the first week of life. It often results from the transplacental transfer of the parasite, which can result in NM characterized by the detection Plasmodium parasite in the peripheral blood of a newborn [ , ]. This congenital transmission of malaria calls for strengthening the mitigation of MiP [ – ]. In a nutshell, the breadth and depth of APOs reported together with PM in the included studies and the magnitude of these problems, as shown by the pooled estimates (prevalence and association), reflect the sustained multifaceted high burden of PM in SSA. This calls for an urgent need to strengthen mitigation of MiP through integration of anti-malarial intervention with antenatal care to avert these preventable APOs of PM, optimize efforts to eliminate malaria, and improve maternal and child health [ – , ].
Generally, this vast and comprehensive SRMA revealed the overall and disaggregated pooled prevalence of PM in SSA. Although there is a slightly declining trend of PM prevalence, the magnitude of the problem, the diversity and severity of its APOs are considerable in SSA. Pregnant and parturient women continued to be the major disproportionately affected and less protected segment of the general population despite a preventable adverse outcome of MiP to themselves, the foetus, newborn, and the general population. The observed significant magnitude of PM has a multidimension implication to the clinical practice concerned with foetal, neonatal and maternal health, to the healthcare system that is continuously striving to eliminate malaria and improve maternal and child health, cognitive capacity along with its educational achievement of the children, and to the economic aspect of SSA already suffering from the severe burden of poverty and tropical diseases. Therefore, this significant magnitude and multifaceted multifactorial complex nature PM calls for thoughtful, evidence-based, collaborative, and urgent interventions designed in integrated and multidimensional implementation approaches targeting the identified predictors and sources of heterogeneity [ , , – ].

## Strengths and limitations of this systematic review and meta-analysis
To the best of our knowledge, this is the first comprehensive SRMA simultaneously reporting the pooled prevalence, predictors, and APOs of PM in SSA. Besides, the inclusion of over 170 studies in determining the prevalence of PM (overall and disaggregated by many sub-groups) makes this SRMA a major source of reliable, comprehensive data for future studies, evidence-based intervention, and policy-making. The extensive sub-group analysis conducted by considering more than 10 grouping variables provided a stratified prevalence of PM that is essential for designing a targeted intervention.
On the other hand, there are some limitations to consider in this SRMA. Due to the limited number of studies assessing risk factors and adverse outcomes of PM, it was difficult to assess publication bias on pooled estimates of their OR and proportion, respectively. It was difficult to generate a pooled estimate of OR for assessing the association of SGA, stillbirth, and NM with PM due to the limited number of articles that directly reported multivariable analysis results. Since the majority of the included studies were cross-sectional, it is difficult to establish cause-and-effect relationship based on this SRMA finding. The result of histopathology, followed by PCR, followed by microscopy, followed by RDT, was used to determine the overall prevalence of PM whenever disaggregated data at the diagnostic test level were available for those studies that did not provide the overall prevalence despite using more than one diagnostic test. Besides, the specimen-level and diagnostic test-level pooled prevalence of PM was determined. We did this based on the existing scientific evidence concerning the diagnostic performance of the currently available PM detection tools, to facilitate the result of this SRMA comparable by the type of specimen and diagnostic test utilized in the included studies; and to provide a comprehensive evidence relevant for policy-level decision-making with special interest on optimizing case detection. However, variation in the specific type of RDT and PCR used in some of the included studies might still be a potential source of heterogeneity that should be considered. Furthermore, there was a significant amount of heterogeneity (though sub-group analyses were run to account for the variability) in the pooled estimate of PM prevalence, emphasizing the need for caution in generalizing and interpreting this finding.
In the prospect of clinical and public health applicability of this finding, the significant heterogeneity observed among the included studies (mainly due to diagnostic tool, specimen type, publication time, and geographical setting). Thus, researchers, public health experts, clinicians, policymakers, and other potential users of this pooled estimate data should be aware of the limitations of this SRMA and consider the potential sources of variability that prompt designing tailored interventions to efficiently mitigate the burden of MiP in SSA. Furthermore, this variability underscores the importance of continued research across different transmission settings by involving a large sample size, and longitudinal study design (from preconception up to postpartum periods to assess the stratified effect of malaria infection during preconception, early, mid, and late-gestation periods on maternal, foetal, neonatal, infant and child health), and more sensitive diagnostic tool to better characterize the burden of MiP and monitor the effectiveness of its diagnostic and therapeutic interventions in the continually changing characteristics of the parasite, the vector and the human. This will improve the understanding and mitigation of MiP, in particular, and optimize the productivity of interventions to eliminate malaria, in general, ultimately attaining a malaria-free world.

## Conclusion and recommendation
With its high magnitude and many APOs, PM was found to be a critical public health problem in SSA. Its prevalence varied by the type of specimen and diagnostic tool used, geographical location, and publication time. Being a younger age, primigravidae, primiparae, having a history of MiP and HIV co-infection, and inadequate utilization of IPTp, ITN, and ANC services were predictors of PM. The diversity and prevalence of APOs of PM were considerable.
Concerted mitigation efforts involving a multidimensional approach tailored to the identified factors and heterogeneity are needed to mitigate PM and its APOs in SSA. Further studies (mainly longitudinal) aimed at characterizing the Epidemiology of PM using more than one specimen and diagnostic tool type are needed.
