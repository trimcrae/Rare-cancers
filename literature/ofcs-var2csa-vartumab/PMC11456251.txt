## Introduction
 The narrow therapeutic window of chemotherapy drugs represents a significant constraint in oncological treatments, as highlighted by recent studies [ – ]. The challenge often lies in balancing antitumor efficacy with patient safety, a compromise that frequently leads to the development of drug resistance in patients [ ]. Nanodrug delivery systems have shown promise in enhancing drug bioavailability by concentrating drugs at the tumor site, leveraging either passive enhanced permeability and retention (EPR) effects or active targeting mechanisms [ ]. However, the application of synthetic nanoparticles (NPs) is hindered by their rapid clearance by the reticuloendothelial system and the increased potential toxicity resulting from repeated administrations.
Biomimetic nanocarriers, which are crafted from the cell membrane or extracellular vehicles (EVs) of natural cells, vary in size and type—including exosomes (EXOs), microvesicles (MVs), apoptotic bodies, and large oncosomes. These nanocarriers possess intrinsic proteins on or within their structure that inherit the functional attributes of the source cells, altering the behavior of receptor cells [ ]. This property prevents their clearance by the immune system, facilitating specific aggregation at the intended site of action. For instance, red blood cells can circulate in the body for 120 h without being cleared, which is related to the presence of phospholipids, proteins, cholesterol, and other substances on the red blood cell membrane. The cluster of differentiation 47 (CD47) is an integrin related transmembrane protein that has a high affinity for thromboreactive protein (TSP) and signal regulatory protein-α (SIRP-α) on the macrophage membrane. It can induce phosphorylation of the cytoplasmic tail of SIRP-α and block phagocytosis by inhibiting the accumulation of the motor protein myosin IIA at the phagocytic synapse [ ]. Additionally, tumor-derived EVs have been implicated in promoting tumor growth through the delivery of MicroRNA (miR)-630 [ ], and in stimulating tumor angiogenesis via the transport of miR-210, miR-9, miR-135b, and various long noncoding RNAs [ ]; and downregulate phosphatase and tensin homolog (PTEN) expression and activate the phosphatidyl-inositol 3-kinase/serine-threonine kinase (PI3K/AKT) signaling pathway to induce polarization of M2 macrophages by delivering miR-934 [ ]. EVs derived from M1 macrophages facilitate the transfer of biomolecules to recipient cells, inducing the repolarization of M2 macrophages back to a pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype [ ]. The effect of EVs on tumors can be altered by loading drugs and external modifications; for example, cancer cell-derived EXOs loaded with anticancer drugs exert anticancer effects [ ] and synergize with immune activation [ ]. Thus, harnessing the inherent communicative capabilities of EVs, alongside advanced engineering modifications, presents a promising avenue for developing superior therapeutic carriers.
Despite the extensive literature on biomimetic nanocarriers, most reviews focus predominantly on their roles as drug delivery vehicles, often overlooking a comprehensive analysis of their inherent communicative functions with tumor cells and the tumor microenvironment (TME). This paper aims to meticulously explore the various characteristics of biomimetic nanocarriers derived from different cell types, their interaction mechanisms with tumor cells and the TME, and their application in tumor therapy. In addition, this paper reviews the modification of biomimetic nanocarriers by ligands to achieve the ability of tumor cells to target and respond to the TME. The development and potential advancements in biomimetic nanocarrier technology are also critically reviewed.

## Biomimetic nanocarriers derived from erythrocytes
Erythrocytes, the most abundant cell type in human blood with an average density of 5 billion cells per milliliter, serve as a valuable resource for drug carrier functionalization due to their abundance and historical safety in clinical transfusions spanning the past century [ ]. Their disc-shaped, highly flexible structure allows them to adopt a cup or spherical shape in response to decreases in osmotic pressure, enabling them to traverse the narrowest capillaries smoothly. This unique ability is crucial for erythrocytes to transport drugs and other therapeutic compounds effectively [ ]. Furthermore, mature erythrocytes lack a nucleus and various organelles, simplifying their extraction and purification processes.

## Response to the hypoxic microenvironment
Hemoglobin, the primary oxygen-carrying protein in erythrocytes, is frequently leveraged for hypoxic treatment of TME. Initial research on oxygenation therapy in cancer treatment focused on enhancing the delivery of hemoglobin to the TME to elevate oxygen levels, thereby improving the efficacy of chemotherapy and radiotherapy [ ]. However, conventional cancer treatments like surgery, chemotherapy, and radiotherapy often fall short in meeting clinical demands due to their significant side effects and limited efficacy. In contrast, photodynamic therapy (PDT) [ ] and sonodynamic therapy (SDT) [ ] employ light and sound-sensitive agents, respectively, to transfer energy to surrounding oxygen or water molecules, thereby generating reactive oxygen species (ROS) capable of eradicating cancer cells. But the hypoxic conditions of TME limit the generation of ROS. Hemoglobin, as an endogenous protein in biology, is widely used as an oxygen carrier due to its superior oxygen carrying properties. Gao et al. [ ]used a layer by layer method to prepare red blood cell (RBC) shaped magnetic nanoparticles by encapsulating photosensitizer indocyanine green (ICG), hemoglobin, and Fe3O4 nanoparticles in red blood cell membranes (RBCMs). The experiment confirmed the oxygen loading and release ability of hemoglobin. The authors pointed out that the high oxygen affinity of RBCM micromotors was due to the quaternary state of hemoglobin, and the ultrasound treatment during the preparation process did not significantly affect the oxygen carrying capacity of hemoglobin in RBCM. The antiphagocytic ability of magnetic nanoparticles loaded with red blood cell membrane was significantly lower than that of magnetic nanoparticles without red blood cell membrane loading, and more than 75% of HeLa cells could be killed by RBCMs, significantly higher than the group without hemoglobin cores. Liu et al. [ ] constructed a red blood cell-derived vehicle (RDV) rich in hemoglobin and encapsulated the photosensitizer chlorin e6 (Ce6) and iron death promoter sorafenib (SRF). Hemoglobin can provide both oxygen supply and Fe2+ simultaneously, and in vitro and in vivo experiments have shown that the carrier has a good synergistic inhibitory effect on tumors. The subcutaneous tumor model shows that the anti-tumor effect of the Ce6@SRF@RDV group is significantly better than that of the Ce6@RDV + laser group and SRF@RDV group. Yuan et al. [ ] utilized zeolitic imidazolate framework 8 (ZIF-8)-encapsulated hemoglobin for melanoma treatment. On the one hand, hemoglobin acts as an oxygen donor to experiment with the release of oxygen when it reaches the hypoxic TME, and on the other hand, hemoglobin acts as an acoustic sensitizer to generate reactive oxygen species and then induce mitochondrial damage to inhibit the growth of tumor cells.

## Prolonged circulation in the body
Upon entering a biological system, nanoparticles encounter various biological barriers that significantly impede their delivery efficacy. Disc-shaped particles are particularly advantageous because they exhibit margination under blood flow, which substantially extends their circulation time, improves biodistribution, and enhances cellular interactions [ ]. Oval-shaped erythrocytes exhibit high oxygen permeability, on-demand cargo release, and the ability to traverse the extracellular matrix (ECM) of tumor cells, thereby achieving superior tumor permeability [ ]. Additionally, their deformability allows them to pass through narrow blood vessels smaller than their own size, and under unidirectional force, they can stretch to almost twice their original diameter [ ]. As erythrocytes age, their intracellular hemoglobin concentration increases while their deformability diminishes; their membranes thicken and harden, leading to eventual clearance by macrophages due to the adsorption of immunoglobulin and complement proteins [ ]. The stiffness and deformability of erythrocytes significantly influence their lifespan and vascular fluidity. Erythrocyte membranes were coated on polyethylene glycol diacrylate (PEGDA) hydrogel nanoparticles with different degrees of cross-linking to obtain soft RBC membrane-derived nanovesicles (RBC-SNVs) and elastic and hard nucleus-shell nanoparticles (RBC-ENPs and RBC-HNPs) to mimic diseased, dynamic, and senescent erythrocytes, respectively. The results showed that RBC-ENPs had less adsorption of immune response modulators (immunoglobulins, fibrinogen, and complement proteins) in the blood and led to a longer blood circulation time. In addition, RBC-ENPs with moderate deformability were highly mobile within the tumor ECM, which helped the drug penetrate deeper into the tumor tissue [ ].

## Biomimetic nanocarriers derived from platelets
Platelets, which are non-nucleated, disc-shaped cell fragments derived from the cytoplasm of megakaryocytes within the bone marrow, circulate in the bloodstream for approximately 7–10 days [ ]. Tumor cells activate circulating platelets, which subsequently foster tumor growth and viability by forming a protective barrier around the tumor cells [ ]. In primary tumors, platelets are recruited to the TME, where they release growth factors such as proangiogenic and mitogenic proteins. In the scenario of metastatic tumors, the interaction between platelets and tumor cells leads to the formation of cancer thrombi, which are instrumental in facilitating tumor cell metastasis [ ]. Moreover, platelets have the capacity to bind directly to tumors and the TME, a process during which they release both growth factors and exosomal particles and exhibit specialized wound-targeting capabilities. This unique functionality of platelets underscores their potential as a foundation for the development of platelet-based biomimetic nanocarriers, aiming to enhance targeted therapeutic delivery and efficacy in oncological treatments.

## Tumor cell adhesion
Platelets play an essential role in attachment and communication with tumor cells through the presence of cell adhesion molecules (CAMs) on their membrane surfaces. These CAMs are predominantly classified into four principal protein families: integrins, selectins, the immunoglobulin superfamilies (Ig SF), and calmodulin [ ]. Integrins and selectins are particularly critical for facilitating platelet adherence to cancer cells and for the transmission of cellular signals.
The α6β1 integrin on platelets interacts with tumor cells expressing a disintegrin and a metalloprotease 9 (ADAM9), facilitating lung metastasis [ ]. Conversely, αvβ3 integrin binds to fibrinogen and CD97 on cancer cell surfaces, enhancing cross-linking between platelets and cancer cells and thus creating a robust platelet shield [ ]. αIIbβ3, the major integrin on platelets, undergoes a conformational change in its extracellular structural domain in response to agonist stimulation, and this change greatly enhances the affinity of αIIbβ3 for fibrinogen on the surface of cancer cells, mediating irreversible and stable platelet adhesion, spreading and aggregation and facilitating the growth of thrombi and spread of cancer cells [ ].
Glycoprotein VI (GPVI), a member of the Ig-superfamily (Ig SF) family, is only expressed in platelets and their precursor megakaryocytes, where it mediates platelet adhesion and anchoring. GPVI initiates a signaling cascade by binding to collagen-related peptides, leading to cellular activation and initial adhesion, followed by αIIbβ3-dependent stable adhesion and recruitment of additional platelets [ ]. Galectin-3, a β-galactose-binding lectin, possesses a collagen-like structural domain and is highly expressed on the extracellular surface of tumor cells. Platelet GPVI can be activated by galactin-3 to form a disulfide homodimer with the immunoreceptor tyrosine-based activation motif (ITAM) sequence, which increases platelet adhesion. Calcium dependent lectin like receptor 2(CLEC-2) is a type II transmembrane protein expressed on the surface of platelets. Podoplanin (PDPN), the only known endogenous ligand for this receptor, is expressed in squamous cell carcinoma, brain tumors, and osteosarcoma, and it is associated with a poor prognosis in cancer patients [ ]. p-Selectin is a platelet-specific ligand that targets the CD44 receptor on cancer cells [ ], promoting the adherence of circulating tumor cells (CTCs) to blood vessels and facilitating targeted drug delivery (Fig.  A) [ ].
Poly(lactic-co-glycolic acid) (PLGA) loaded with the photosensitizer ICG and coated with a platelet membrane can effectively deliver drugs to the tumor site [ ]. Platelet membranes can coat small molecule immunomodulators, allowing for prolonged presence in the TME and the ability to combat tumors at small doses [ ]. Uslu et al. [ ] The encapsulation of doxorubicin (DOX) in human platelet EXOs significantly reduced the activity of MDA-MB-231 breast cancer cells within 24 h, whereas the application of platelet-EXOs alone increased cell viability, suggesting that the combined application of EXOs and drugs can modify the effect of EXOs on tumors.
Platelet-derived microvesicles (P-MVs) are the predominant type of EV found in human blood, making up more than 50% of the total. In addition to the markers of platelet EXOs, p-selectin and GPIIb/IIIa (CD41/CD61 or integrin αIIb/β3) are also expressed on the surface of these MVs [ ]. As a result, P-MVs can adhere to tumor cells. Biomimetic nanocarriers derived from platelet membranes or EVs can be used for tumor targeting without the need for further ligand modification.

## Vascular targeting
Tumor cells acquire oxygen and nutrients from blood vessels and metastasize [ ]. Nanomedicines generally accumulate passively in tumor cells through the EPR effect rather than in the endothelial cells of the tumor vasculature, which results in a diminished destructive effect on the vasculature. GPIb/V/IX, GPVI, and GP IIb/IIIa receptors on platelet membranes and EVs can interact with wound collagen and fibrin so that platelets can adhere to damaged blood vessels [ ]. In addition, platelets can target thrombi [ ]. Platelets and their derivatives can increase medicine delivery to the wound site by affecting signals in the coagulation cascade. This can help lower the cancer recurrence rate after surgery.
As a coagulation activator, tissue factor (TF) initiates the exogenous coagulation pathway, changes plasminogen to thrombin, and draws platelets together to form thrombi. On tumor cells and tumor neovascular endothelial cells, the binding of TF to the αvβ3 integrin receptor (RGD) initiates the coagulation signal cascade at the tumor vascular site, and platelets are released to form thrombi. Subsequently, anti-PD-1 antibody-conjugated platelets (P-aPD-1) were injected in response to the clotting signal, actively involved in thrombosis, and enriched at the tumor site. The clotting cascade triggers platelet activation and releases platelet-derived microparticles (PMPs), enabling aPD-1 antibodies to be delivered to the tumor site, stimulating T cells, and boosting the immune response. This technique addresses the issue of nontumour specificity in receptors, minimizing off-target effects and systemic toxicity (Fig.  B) [ ]. Ji et al. [ ] showed that administering platelet membrane-coated nanoparticles containing DOX (PM-HDOX) before surgery in a glioma mouse model improved the penetration of the blood‒brain barrier and the effectiveness of targeting surgical margins. In the preoperative treatment group, the intensity of PM-HDOX at the surgical periphery was twice as high as that in the postoperative treatment group.

 

## Biomimetic nanocarriers derived from adipocytes
Many types of tumor cells rely on adipocytes as their main source of energy to satisfy their infinite value-added energy demand by catabolizing stored lipids and long-chain fatty acids [ ]. As central players in lipid metabolism, adipocytes engage in extensive interactions with cancer cells, supporting tumor proliferation and metastasis by contributing to membrane synthesis, cellular signaling, and inflammatory processes [ ]. Consequently, adipocytes and their lipid droplets serve as critical energy reservoirs that facilitate intercellular communication within cancer environments.
In an innovative approach, Zhao et al. [ ] employed mature adipocytes coated with palmitate-modified triptolide (the two components are linked by a disulfide bond) and the photosensitizer Ce6 for melanoma treatment. Drug-carrying adipocytes can be recruited into melanoma cell tissues, where tumor cells facilitated the release of the encapsulated drug through the degradation of adipocytes. Palmitic acid-modified triptolide, which serves as an energy source, was absorbed by tumor cells. Under the influence of high concentrations of glutathione (GSH), disulfide bonds are broken, leading to the release of triptolide. The author inoculated A375 cells subcutaneously on the right side of BALB/C nude mice to establish a melanoma model, and administered the drug by peritumoral injection. The tumor inhibition rate reached 88.18 ± 7.42%. Wen et al. [ ] utilized adipocytes loaded with telratolimod (TAAs), a lipid-conjugated Toll-like receptor 7/8 agonist, to promote M1 polarization of tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) and reverse the immunosuppressive microenvironment caused by M2-like TAMs. Adipocytes loaded with TAAs can promote the recruitment of macrophages within the TME, after which tumor cells release TAA by activating fatty acid binding protein 4 (FABP4) in adipocytes to further induce M1 polarization of TAMs. Using fat cells or lipid droplets as carriers can promote anticancer therapy through metabolic intervention.
Lipid droplets, as organelles widely present within adipocytes, are composed of a lipid core surrounded by a phospholipid monolayer. Due to their high lipid content, they provide an ideal reservoir for lipophilic drugs [ ]. Lipid droplets loaded with lipid-conjugated photosensitizers (pyrolipid) exhibit excellent stability and biocompatibility and can be engulfed by cancer cells and accumulate in the mitochondria and lysosomal compartments within cells. Furthermore, when freeze-dried into powder, lipid droplets can be stably stored, indicating the potential for commercial production [ ]. With their wide availability and ease of sourcing from adipocytes, they hold great promise as biomimetic nanocarriers.

## Biomimetic nanocarriers derived from tumor cells
The tumor cell membrane carries a complete set of proteins and surface antigens, enabling them to stimulate immune responses and be utilized as a cancer vaccine. miR-9 in EXOs from nasopharyngeal carcinoma cells can target midkine (MDK), regulate the 3-phosphoinositide-dependent protein kinase-1/protein kinase B (PDK/AKT) signaling pathway, and prevent endothelial vessel formation and migration [ ]. The clinical application of tumor-derived EVs as carriers for cancer therapy drugs carries certain risks due to their involvement in tumor recurrence and metastasis. Therefore, designing and constructing drug delivery systems that maximize the value of tumor-derived EVs is an urgent challenge. Furthermore, the ability of tumor cells to multiply quickly, the lack of need for plasma from living donors, and the ease of growing large numbers of cells in a laboratory setting all make it possible to make biomimetic nanocarriers from cancer cells on a large scale.

## Homologous targeting
The remarkable homing ability of tumor cells represents a significant advantage of biomimetic nanocarriers derived from cancer cells. The aberrant expression of CAMs and intercellular molecules is the primary cause of the robust and specific homotypic adhesion of cancer cells [ ]. Cadherins, galectin-3, selectin, integrin, the immunoglobulin superfamily (Ig-SF), and lymphocyte homing receptors (such as CD44) jointly promote adhesion interactions between cancer cells and vascular endothelial cells, homologous cancer cells, and other cell types [ , ]. Zhu et al. [ ] demonstrated for the first time the homologous targeting ability of tumor cell membranes by encapsulating the cell membranes of UM-SCC-7 and HeLa cells on magnetic iron oxide and DOX (MNP@DOX) surfaces, respectively. Subsequently, homotypic targeting of tumor cell membranes has been verified in various cancer treatments, including breast cancer MCF-7 cells [ ], 4T1 cells [ ], osteosarcoma HOS cells [ ], bladder cancer T24 cells [ ], and colon cancer CT26 cells [ ]. However, direct evidence supporting homotypic targeting between specific proteins on cancer cell membranes and cancer cell membrane-coated nanoparticles is currently limited. Xie et al. [ ] conducted the first experimental study demonstrating the crucial role of the highly expressed integrin αvβ3 on cancer cell membranes in selective recognition by tumor cells. This study sheds light on the integrin-dependent regulation of cell-membrane-camouflaged nanoparticles (CMC-NPs) in future biomedical applications. Zhang et al. [ ] prepared a TME responsive nanomedicine (MAuGLs) using pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) membrane coated gold nanocages (AuNCs) for combined delivery of gemcitabine (GEM) and nitric oxide (NO) donor (L-Arg). The drug distribution experiment in vivo showed that the tumor temperature of mice treated with MAuGLs could increase to 54.2 ± 0.7 ℃, much higher than that of AuGLs (41.9 ± 1.6) ℃, indicating that the homologous targeting of PDAC membrane can promote the aggregation of nanoparticles to the tumor site (Fig.  A).

## Immune activation
Cancer cell-derived EVs often activate tumor immune cells, facilitating immune-mediated cytotoxicity [ ]. Tumor-derived EXOs have a discrete proteome, including major histocompatibility complex class I and II (MHC-I and MHC-II), phosphatidylserine, cadherin (MFGE8), Rab7, lysosome-associated membrane protein 1 (LAMP1), CD9, CD81, membrane-associated proteins II, CD54, and CD63. These proteomes facilitate the uptake of EXOs by associated ligands on dendritic cells (DCs). Moreover, tumor-derived EVs transfer tumor-associated antigens to DCs, which subsequently initiate tumor-specific cytotoxic T lymphocytes (CTLs) and induce effective antitumor immune responses [ ]. Tumor cell membranes and tumor cell-derived EVs play dual roles as drug carriers and immune activators. Tumor cell membranes contain a high proportion of antigens, making them suitable for tumor immune activation. Li et al. [ ] prepared RuPOP@CM by repeatedly extruding MDA-MB-231 and K562 cell membranes with ruthenium polypyridyl complexes (RuPOP). The releases of tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), interleukin (IL)-6, and IL-12 in the serum of Balb/c mice injected with RuPOP@CM were higher than those of free RuPOP group, which was associated with macrophage inflammatory responses triggered by antigens and tumor adhesion molecules on the membrane surface of tumor cells (Fig.  B). However, studies have shown that the immunogenicity of membrane antigens is weak, and immunosuppressive molecules on the cell surface hinder recognition by the immune system. Therefore, antigenic epitopes are often processed and modified on tumor cell membranes or hybridized with bacterial membranes to enhance immunogenicity [ ].
Cholesterol-modified CpG (cholo-CpG) with enhanced antigen presentation and induced a TH1-type immune response, and immunoadjuvant toll-like receptor 9 (TLR9) agonists and cholesterol-modified DC-specific intercellular adhesion molecule (ICAM)-3 grabbing nonintegrin (DC-SIGN) aptamers with macrophage-targeting effects were inserted into cancer-derived cell membranes [ ]. The surface modification of CpG can effectively enhance the immune-stimulating properties of tumor cell membranes and promote the maturation of macrophages. At the same time, aptamers can enhance the binding of macrophages to biomimetic nanocarriers, improve their delivery to lymph nodes, and promote the generation of specific T cells [ ]. Cell line-derived cancer cell membranes (CLMs) are not good for use as antigens in cancer vaccines because the antigenic components of cancer cell membranes vary from person to person. Li et al. [ ] were the first to use surgery-derived cancer cell membranes (SCMs) as tumor-associated antigens, showing that they were more suitable and specific (Fig.  C).
In tumor immunotherapy, the complex process of antigen presentation requires sophisticated strategies that may be difficult to translate. We can skip the role of antigen-presenting cells when making biomimetic nanocarriers for direct stimulation of T cells. Jiang [ ] first transfected B16-F10 cells with a plasmid encoding CD80 to generate the B16-CD80 cell line. The supernatant from virus-infected cells expressing ovalbumins (OVA) was mixed with that from B16-CD80 cells to generate B16-CD80/OVA cells. After the B16-CD80/OVA cell membrane was isolated, it was coated onto the surface of the nanoparticles. Additionally, B16-CD80 cells have MHC-I on their own, which allows them to express endogenous CD8 + T-cell antigens in the form of peptide epitopes. By combining MHC-I and CD80, the biomimetic nanocarriers can send antigens directly to T cells that are specifically aimed at cancer treatment. In vitro experiments have shown that B16-CD80/OVA cells can enhance the activation of CD8 + T cells in the spleen of OT-I transgenic mice. Moreover, they increased the proportion of CD8 + T cells with the CD44high CD62Lhigh central memory phenotype.

 

## Biomimetic nanocarriers derived from macrophages
Based on their functions, macrophages can be divided into two major categories: classically activated or inflammatory macrophages (M1) and alternatively activated or anti-inflammatory macrophages (M2) [ ]. M1 macrophages express α4 and β1 integrins on their surface, which can specifically target tumor tissues by binding to vascular cell adhesion molecule-1 (VCAM-1) on cancer cells and phagocytosing tumor cells [ ]. The use of live macrophages, macrophage-derived cell membranes, and EVs as vehicles can retain tumor-targeting ability.

## Phagocytosis
Common biomimetic nanocarrier delivery strategies involve coupling the drug to the surface or using external stimuli to encapsulate the drug but may cause cell membrane damage, affecting the expression of proteins on the surface of the carrier and the functions of the carrier, such as adhesion and migration. Macrophages are differentiated cells of the innate immune system that can phagocytose foreign substances and can be used as carriers for the uptake of adequate amounts of therapeutic drugs, both time- and concentration-dependently [ ]. To avoid the deleterious effects of high doses of therapeutic drugs on macrophages, drugs can be loaded into liposomes or nanoparticles to enhance their stability, and DOX-loaded PLGA nanoparticles [ ] and 7-ethyl-10-hydroxycamptothecin (SN38) nanoparticles [ ] delivered by macrophages have shown good antitumor effects.

## Tumor tissue targeting
Macrophage surface α4 and β1 integrins can bind to vascular cell adhesion molecule-1 (VCAM-1) on the surface of cancer cells, giving macrophages a natural targeting ability for metastatic tumors [ ]. Zuo et al. [ ] employed hollow mesoporous iron oxide (HMFe) nanocatalysts loaded with 4-(2-aminoethyl) benzenesulfonyl fluoride, and coated with a macrophage membrane to achieve the accumulation of macrophages at the tumor site through the α4/VCAM-1 interaction. Macrophages can also be recruited into tumor tissues by CC chemokine ligand 2 (CCL2) and chemokine receptor 4 (CXCR4) [ ]. Zhao et al. [ ] utilized macrophage membrane-encapsulated quercetin-loaded bismuth selenide (M@BS-QE) NPs to inhibit the metastasis of both primary and lung metastatic breast cancer. The SeBi nanoparticles had a good photothermal therapeutic effect. Heat shock protein 70 (HSP70) levels decreased when quercetin was present, which increased the death of tumor cells caused by high temperatures. Furthermore, it inhibits tumor invasion and metastasis by downregulating the p-serine-threonine kinase/Matrix Metalloproteinase-9 (p-Akt/MMP-9) signaling pathway. Through its CCL2/C-C Motif Chemokine Receptor 2 (CCR2) recruitment capacity and α4/VCAM-1 recognition capability, the macrophage membrane achieved dual targeting of breast cancer cells. The author established Balb/c lung metastasis model to evaluate the anti-cancer effect of M@BS-QE. The average number of metastatic of M@BS-QE group accounted for only 17% compared to PBS group.

## Macrophage polarization
M1 macrophage-derived EVs enriched with RNA and proteins from parent cells, including miR-27a, miR-125b, miR-155, and miR-199a, promoted phenotypic switching from M2-like TAMs to M1-type macrophages, and reprogrammed M1 macrophages had a protein expression profile similar to that of classically activated M1 macrophages, showing strong phagocytic and antigen cross-presentation capacity [ ].
Nanovesicles (NVs) produced by rapidly extruded M1 macrophages have a similar cell membrane composition and size to EXOs but a greater protein and RNA content than EXOs. These NVs can be efficiently taken up by M2 macrophages, leading to their repolarization into M1 macrophages and a reduction in the expression of the metastasis factor CCL-18 in M2 macrophages [ ].
Nie et al. [ ] bound M1 EXOs to dibenzocyclooctyne-modified antibodies against CD47 (aCD4) and SIRPα (aSIRPα) via pH-sensitive junctions. A responsive EXO bioconjugate was constructed for the treatment of mouse breast cancer. aCD4 can target overexpressed CD47 on the surface of tumor cells, increasing its accumulation in tumor tissues. The slightly acidic TME triggers the cleavage of imine bonds, leading to the release of aSIRPα and aCD4, which bind to the inhibitory receptor SIRPα on macrophages and the CD47 receptor on tumor cells, respectively. This effectively eliminated the “do not eat me” signal from tumor cells, enhancing the phagocytic capability of M1 macrophages. Furthermore, M1 EXOs effectively changed M2 macrophages in the tumor tissue to the M1 phenotype, which improved tumor cell engulfment. The synergistic effect between macrophage polarization and antibody-mediated intervention significantly improved anticancer efficacy. Wang et al. [ ] incubated M1-like macrophages with the chemical drug banoxantrone (AQ4N) and then modified the chemical trigger agent (CPPO) and the photosensitized agent Ce6 on the EV membranes of M1 macrophages to obtain CCA-M1-EVs. The synergistic effect between macrophage polarization and antibody-mediated intervention significantly improved anticancer efficacy. H2O2 can react with CPPO to generate chemical energy for the activation of Ce6, leading to a subsequent abundance of ROS. This process consumes copious amounts of oxygen, exacerbates hypoxia in the tumor region, and facilitates the conversion of AQ4N to the toxic AQ4, augmenting tumor chemotherapy. CCA-M1-EVs can promote the M1 polarization of macrophages in both cell-derived xenograft model and PDX model, increase the ROS level and reduce the oxygenation level in the tumor area.

## Biomimetic nanocarriers derived from NK cells
NK cells are cytotoxic lymphocytes of the innate immune system that avoid attacking healthy cells by utilizing inhibitory receptors that interact with their own MHC I-like molecules. Conversely, cancer cells reduce MHC I expression, enabling NK cells to eliminate tumor cells through a “missing self” mechanism [ ]. Activated NK cells are capable of eliminating tumor cells through direct cytotoxicity and/or the production of proinflammatory cytokines, mediating apoptotic pathways through membrane receptor interactions [ ], and modulating the immune response of other cells through the secretion of cytokines such as interferon-γ (IFN-γ) and granulocyte-macrophage colony-stimulating factor (GM-CSF) [ ].

## Killing tumor cells
Upon interaction with MHC I-downregulated cancer cells, NK cells are activated and subsequently bind to tumor cells through activating receptors such as NKG2D, NKp30, NKp44, NKp46, and CD16. Activated NK cells induce apoptosis in tumor cells by releasing perforin-containing particles [ ]; expressing molecules such as TNF-α, Fas ligand (FasL), and TNF-related apoptosis-inducing ligand (TRAIL) [ ]; producing cytokines such as IFN-γ; and secreting EXOs containing death ligands and perforin molecules [ ]. Expanded natural killer cells (eNK-EXOs) express classical proteins of natural killer cells (e.g., CD6, CD81, and Alix) [ ] and cytotoxic proteins (e.g., perforin, granzyme B, FASL, and TRALL) and can be taken up by tumor cells to induce tumor cytotoxicity. NK-EVs exert antitumor effects by enhancing the activation of specific apoptotic markers in hepatocellular carcinoma cells (HCC) (Hep3B) through the inhibition of serine/threonine protein kinase phosphorylation (Fig.  A) [ ]. The tumor-suppressive microRNA 186 carried by NK-EXOs exhibits cytotoxicity against neuroblastoma [ ]. NK-EXOs carrying miR-30 C enhanced the cytotoxicity of lung cancer cells by reducing polypeptide N-acetylgalactosaminyltransferase 7 (GALNT7) and blocking the PI3K/AKT pathway [ ]. Additionally, miR-3607-3p [ ], microRNA let-7b-5 [ ], miR-30e-3p [ ], miR-221-5p, and miR-186-5p [ ] found in NK cell-derived EXOs demonstrate antitumor effects in pancreatic cancer, human esophageal squamous cell carcinoma, and bladder cancer, respectively. NK-EXOs enhance antitumor efficacy by expressing specific ligands or loading active therapeutic agents. NK-EXOs were loaded with paclitaxel ((PTX)-NK-EXOs)) for the treatment of breast cancer, and PTX-NK-EXOs significantly reduced the migration rate of MCF-7 cells and increased apoptosis [ ]. The loading of BCL-2 siRNA in NK-EXOs targeted apoptotic breast cancer cells, and the activity of NK-EXOs loaded with siRNA was not affected [ ].

## Regulating the immune system
In addition to recognizing and killing senescent, malignantly transformed, and pathogenic infected cells, NK cells also exhibit regulatory functions in immune responses, particularly in modulating T-cell activity. NK-EVs improved the cytotoxicity of CTLs toward tumor cells and facilitated the infiltration of CTLs into tumor tissues (Fig.  B) [ ]. High expression of T-cell-responsive miRNAs, such as miR-10b-5p, miR-155-5p, and miR-92a-3p, in NK EVs enhanced Th2-like responses and T-cell activation by increasing the production of IFN-γ and IL-1. Additionally, they induce the expression of MHC-II and CD86 in DCs, enhancing the antigen-presenting and costimulatory functions of monocyte-derived DCs (Fig.  C) [ ]. NK-EXOs also contain substances that target the transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β) pathway, thus alleviating immune suppression within tumors [ ]. Nanoparticles loaded with photosensitizers (TCPPs) encapsulated within NK cell membranes have been utilized to eliminate primary tumors and inhibit distant tumor metastasis. The NK cell membrane imparts tumor-targeting properties to nanoparticles and can induce M1 polarization in macrophages, thereby promoting antitumor immune responses [ ].

 

## Biomimetic nanocarriers derived from DCs
In the context of cancer, DCs can present tumor antigens (TAs) to CTLs through direct presentation and cross-presentation. Direct antigen presentation involves TAs being internalized by DCs and processed by lysosomal proteases into short peptides, which are subsequently loaded onto MHC II molecules, forming complexes that are transported to the plasma membrane for presentation, ultimately leading to the activation of CD4 + T cells [ ]. DCs can also present TAs on MHC I molecules through a process known as cross-presentation, which facilitates the priming of naive CD8 + T cells [ ]. DCs establish a link between innate and adaptive immune responses. Based on the characteristics of DCs, designing nanoscale complexes of antigens and immune stimulators that activate DCs is an important research direction [ ].
Genetically engineered DC EXOs expressing alpha-fetoprotein (AFP) on their surface can be utilized for the treatment of hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC). The modification of AFP promotes the maturation of DC EXOs, resulting in the upregulation of MHC-I and the costimulatory molecules CD80 and CD86 in DC EXOs [ ]. Ma et al. [ ] utilized activated mature dendritic cell membrane (aDCM) wrapped with rapamycin (RAPA)-loaded polylactic acid-hydroxyacetic acid copolymer (PLGA) nanoparticles to prepare aDCM@ PLGA/RAPA. In vitro experiments demonstrated that aDCMs could be recognized and taken up by immature DCs, which then develop into mature DCs and present antigens as APCs. In the Orthotopic C6 tumor model, the percentage of CD3 + CD8 + T cells in the aDCM@PLGA/RAPA group was about 42.32%, which was 2.2 times higher than that in the saline group; the percentage of CD3-CD161 + NK cells was about 41.96%, which was 2.8 times higher than that in the saline group. The results showed an increase in tumor-infiltrating immune-stimulating cells. The survival curve indicated that 50% of the mice in the aDCM@PLGA-treated group survived for a long time (Fig.  A). Fan et al. [ ] employed anti-CD3 and anti-(endothelial growth factor receptor) EGFR modifications on tumor antigen-stimulated DC-derived EXOs (tDC-EXOs) as a “bridge” between tumor cells and activated T cells. The surface of tDC-EXOs contains major histocompatibility (MHC)-antigen complexes and CD86 costimulatory molecules, which act as necessary signals for T-cell activation and promote T-cell proliferation and differentiation. Anti-CD3 and anti-EGFR can promote the binding of T cells to cancer cells, and the nano preparation has good therapeutic activity. The C57BL/6J lung metastasis mouse model shows that the nano formulation can effectively prevent postoperative tumor recurrence and prevent tumor lung metastasis by enhancing T cell infiltration in lung tissue (Fig.  B). Despite advances in EXO-based cancer immunotherapy, clinical applications have been hampered by production constraints, such as low yields and a lack of scalable production. Harvey [ ] devised an innovative membrane disruption method to induce the fusion or aggregation of endogenous nanovesicles in cells. Researchers have employed nitrogen cavitation to disrupt the cell membrane, resulting in the abundant production of cell-derived nanovesicles (CDNVs) from IFN-γ-stimulated DCs. These CDNVs have similar properties to dendritic EXOs and can drive antigen-specific CD8 + T-cell activation through direct and indirect mechanisms under minimal treatment conditions.

 

## Biomimetic nanocarriers derived from neutrophils
Neutrophils, the most abundant granulocytes in human blood, play a dominant role in the immune system’s response to various types of solid tumors. Neutrophil populations within the TME present antitumor N1 and protumor N2 phenotypes due to hypoxia, are heterogeneous and functionally diverse, and play dual roles in tumor progression. Among them, N1 EXOs carrying miR-223 promote immune cell recruitment upon arrival at the TME [ , ]. Circulating neutrophils in the blood reach the TME and can develop into tumor-associated neutrophils (TANs), leading to cancer progression and drug resistance [ ]. Therefore, administering untreated neutrophils directly to tumor patients may pose an additional risk. Combining chimeric antigen receptor engineering with highly motile neutrophils can maintain the anti-tumor N1 phenotype of neutrophils [ ]. In addition, using neutrophil derived exosomes or cell membranes instead of neutrophils themselves can eliminate the potential risk of N1 to N2 conversion during tumor progression while preserving the unique functions of neutrophils [ ]. Additionally, the specific CAMs associated with the membrane of neutrophils confer the ability to target CTCs and the TME.
A chronic inflammatory environment is an important feature of the TME. Neutrophils can be recruited to the tumor inflammatory microenvironment and form neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs) to promote tumor growth [ ]. VCAM-1 on CTCs can bind to integrins on neutrophils to form neutrophil-CTC clusters [ ]. Therefore, neutrophils have a natural ability to target tumor sites. The degree of neutrophil extravasation depends on the degree of tight adhesion to the vascular endothelium and the intensity of inflammatory factors. Zhang et al. [ ] utilized neutrophil phagocytosis of nanoparticle albumin-bound paclitaxel (nab-PTX) and anchored vascular endothelial growth factor receptor 2 (VEGFR2)-targeting peptide K237 to neutrophil cytopharmacuticals (PNEs) to obtain K237-PNEs. K237 can be recognized by VEGFR2 on tumor vascular endothelial cells, and the vascular endothelial targeting and further adhesion of K237-PNES are promoted. After entering the TME, K237 peptide can be released from K237-PNES to inhibit angiogenesis, and PNEs can form NETs to release loaded nab-PTX to kill tumor cells (Fig.  A). The quinone-modified monomethyl auristatin E (MMAE) dimeric prodrug (HQ-MMAE2), which is sensitive to hypoxic conditions, was encapsulated within PLGA NPs and added to neutrophil membranes for the treatment of late-stage breast cancer. These biomimetic nanocarriers, upon intravenous administration, can be recruited to the site of an inflamed tumor, enhancing tumor accumulation and minimizing leakage of MMAE into the systemic circulation. Furthermore, in the profoundly hypoxic environment of advanced breast cancer, hQ-MMAE2 degrades, facilitating the release of MMAE. Additionally, these biomimetic nanocarriers efficiently compete with neutrophils, interactively impeding neutrophil-CTC cluster formation and thus inhibiting tumor metastasis [ ]. Wang et al. [ ] prepared NEs-Exos/DOX by loading doxorubicin into Neutrophil EXOs by ultrasound, and subsequently established a mouse model of LPS-induced brain inflammation to evaluate the chemotactic ability of NEs-Exos. The results of immunofluorescence images showed that the fluorescence intensity of NEs-Exos in the LPS-treated group increased 5.08-fold compared with that in the saline group, indicating the validated chemotaxis of Neutrophil EXOs. The pharmacodynamic experiment showed that NEs-Exos/ DOX could prolong the survival time of C6 glioma-bearing nude mice from 19 days to 27 days with a good anti-glioma effect (Fig.  B).
Neutrophil-derived neutrophil elastase (ELANE) or its analog, porcine pancreatic elastase (PPE), selectively eliminates various types of cancer cells while preserving noncancerous cells. Zhang et al. [ ] first prepared an Fe-porphyrin metal-organic framework (MOF) and grafted a nuclear localization signal (NLS) on the surface of the synthesized MOF to obtain an NLS-grafted Fe-porphyrin MOF (FK). Biomimetic nanodevice (FKPN) NPs were obtained by loading PPE on the surface of the FK and wrapping the NK cell membrane in the outer layer. Upregulation of CD44 and ICAM-1 on the surface of MDA-MB-231 and 4T1 cells enhanced neutrophil attachment. The camouflage of the neutrophil membrane enhanced the tumor-targeting effect of the FKPN NPs. Synthetic MOF entered tumor cells and released porphyrin-NLS and PPE in response to intracellular GSH. PPE releases the CD95 death domain (DD) through protein hydrolysis, induces DNA damage, and triggers histone H1 isoform nucleocytoplasmic translocation. Porphyrin-NLs target the nucleus and produce 1O2 upon laser irradiation, further promoting the release of histone H1 isoforms. The binding of translocated histone H1 to CD95 DD initiated the mitochondrial apoptotic pathway to kill tumor cells and led to the release of cancer-specific antigens, triggering an adaptive immune response in vivo. In the subcutaneous tumor mouse model, the level of mature DCs in the FKPN+ laser irradiation group increased from 40.7 to 60.0%, the level of CD8 + T cells increased from 23.6 to 52.4%, the percentage of CD4 + T cells increased from 23 to 48.2%, and 50% of the tumors were eradicated (Fig.  C). Zhang et al. discovered that neutrophil-derived EXOs containing FasL, granzyme A, granzyme B, and perforin could exert antitumor effects by activating the caspase signaling pathway [ ].

 

## Biomimetic nanocarriers derived from T cells
T cells are pluripotent stem cells derived from bone marrow and are composed of a group of heterogeneous lymphocytes with different functions, such as CD4 + T cells, CD8 + T cells, γδ T cells, and Tregs. CD4 + T cells, also known as helper T cells, can assist CD8 + T cells in cellular immune killing, clearing tumor cells, and assisting B cells in humoral immunity [ ]. CD4 + helper T cells can activate CD8 + T cells and DCs by secreting cytokines. CD8 + T cells recognize APC-presented MHC I molecules and are converted to cytotoxic effector CD8 + T cells called cytotoxic CTLs. Subsequently, CTLs are transported to the TME, where they directly kill tumor cells through granule exocytosis and the FasL-mediated apoptotic pathway [ ]. Additionally, CD8 + T cells can also exert their effects on tumor immunity through the release of cytotoxic EVs [ ]. T cells can be classified into αβ T cells and γδ T cells based on their T-cell receptors (TCRs). γδT cells primarily recognize tumor antigens through two mechanisms: T-cell receptor (TCR)-dependent and natural killer receptor (NKR)-dependent pathways [ ]. Upon recognition, γδT cells induce tumor cell apoptosis through the release of perforin-granzyme complexes or via the FasL and TRAIL pathways. Additionally, they can exert cytotoxic effects on tumor cells through antibody-dependent cell-mediated cytotoxicity (ADCC) and the secretion of cytokines such as IFN-γ and TNF-α [ ]. Furthermore, they can indirectly contribute to antitumor activity by influencing the function of other immune cells, such as enhancing the antigen-presenting capacity of DCs and augmenting the cytolytic activity of cytotoxic T cells against tumor cells.

## Activation of CD8 + T cells
IL-2, which is primarily secreted by CD4 + T cells, is a multifunctional cytokine that can activate various immune cells, such as T cells, B cells, monocytes, and NK cells [ ], and it also stimulates Tregs, which promote antitumor immunity in the TME [ ]. EVs derived from IL-2-stimulated CD4 + T cells containing miR-25-3p, miR-155-5p, miR-215-5p, and miR-375 activate CD8 + T cells to kill tumor cells without affecting Tregs, thus overcoming the limitations of IL-2 immunotherapy [ ]. IL2 was attached to the transmembrane structural domain (TM) of the platelet-derived growth factor receptor (PDGFR) via a flexible linker, and the gene was then integrated into a lentivirus to infect Jurkat T cells. The flexible linker induced an autocrine effect, causing infected Jurkat T cells to secrete IL2-sEVs. The interaction between membrane-bound IL-2 and T cells induces a self-stimulatory effect that modifies the microRNA (miRNA) profile of sEVs. This alteration increased the release of miR-181a-3p and miR-223-3p, which significantly decreased the amount of PD-L1 protein in melanoma cells. Furthermore, miR-181a-3p enhances the activity of CD8 + T cells while suppressing the activity of Treg cells, thereby promoting the cytotoxic effects of CD8 + T cells [ ].

## Killing of tumor cells
CD8 + CTLs that are fully activated by tumor antigens can enhance the activation of low-affinity CD8 + T cells through the secretion of EXOs, thereby participating in the process of tumor killing [ ]. CD45RO-CD8 + T-cell EXOs contain more miR-765, which regulates the estrogen receptor β (Erβ)/miR-765/PLP2 (proteolipid protein 2)/Notch axis, than CD45RO-CD8 + T cells and has a role in limiting the development of estrogen-driven endometrial cancer (UCEC) [ ]. Peripheral γδ-T cells typically express Vδ2 and can be activated by phosphoantigens, leading to rapid expansion [ ]. EXOs derived from γδ T cells carry FasL and TRAIL, which activate the NKG2D receptor on NK cells, the immunostimulatory ligands CD80 and CD86, and the MHC I and II molecules, which can be selectively targeted to clear Epstein–Barr virus-associated tumor cells [ ]. EXOs derived from activated T cells can interact remotely with PD-L1 on the surface of cancer cells by secreting PD-1, which can trigger clathrin-mediated endocytosis, leading to the internalization of PD-L1 in cancer cells. Ultimately, this mechanism attenuates the inhibitory effect of PD-L1 on tumor-specific cytotoxic T-cell activity [ ]. The study above provides a biomimetic nanocarrier preparation strategy by modifying the surface of activated T-cell-derived EXOs with membrane-bound inhibitory immune checkpoint receptors to weaken the inhibitory tumor immune microenvironment.
EXOs derived from γδ-T cells retain the cytotoxic profile of γδ-T cells and exhibit positive expression of NKG2D, FasL, tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-a), interferon-gamma (IFN-g), and perforin, which have direct antitumor effects and stimulate antitumor immune responses. miR-138, which can directly kill tumors and boost the immune system, is delivered by γδ-T-EXOs to oral squamous cell carcinoma (OSCC) cells to produce an inhibitory effect. The γδ-T-EXOs also transferred miR-138 to CD8 + T cells and decreased the levels of PD-1 and CTLA-4. Both γδ-T-EXOs and miR-138 showed synergistic inhibitory effects on oral squamous cell carcinoma both in vitro and in vivo [ ].

## Biomimetic nanocarriers derived from mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs)
MSCs are pluripotent stem cells that can be derived from bone marrow, adipose tissue, the brain, the lung, and the pancreas and can self-renew and differentiate into multilineage cells [ ]. Due to their tumor tendency, cell membranes and EVs from MSCs are often used to prepare biomimetic nanocarriers.
MSCs can migrate toward tumor tissues within the body, although this migration is nonspecific. MSCs accomplish this migration toward tumor sites through the interaction of cell factors/receptors such as stromal cell-derived factor-1 (SDF)-1/CXCR4, stem cell factor (SCF)/c-Kit, hepatocyte growth factor (HGF)/mesenchymal-epithelial transition factor (c-Met), vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF)/VEGFR, PDGF/PDGFr, monocyte chemoattractant protein-1 (MCP-1)/CCR2, high mobility group box 1 (HMGB1)/receptor for advanced glycation end products (RAGE), and CAMs [ ]. Adipose mesenchymal stem cell-derived EXOs [ ], human bone marrow mesenchymal stem cell-derived EXOs [ ], and human umbilical cord mesenchymal stem cell-derived EXOs [ ] can all be used as RNA or drug delivery carriers for cancer therapy. Xie et al. [ ] used human umbilical cord-derived MSC (hUC-MSC) membranes encapsulated on the surface of hollow manganese dioxide nanoparticles loaded with PTX and modified with transcriptional activator protein (TAT) on the membrane surface. The intact surface proteins of the hUC-MSCs in this formulation maintained the “homing” properties of the tumor, the TAT peptide promoted the localization of the nucleus, and the nanoparticles effectively promoted the maturation of DCs and recruited effector T cells into the tumor, resulting in a promising therapeutic effect on lung cancer. In the unilateral tumor-bearing C57BL/6 mice model, the tumor volume of the HMnO2-MSC-TAT@PTX group was significantly reduced to 14.8% of that of the PBS group after 21 days of tail vein administration. The effects of MSCs on the TME are mainly related to the transfer of soluble molecules and other factors via EVs [ , ]. The source of MSCs (collection site, donor age), culture conditions (oxygen supply, culture temperature, chemotherapy drugs), number of passages, and cancer type all have an impact on the effect of MSC EXOs on cancer [ ].

## Modified biomimetic nanocarriers are endowed with additional communication capabilities

## Biomimetic nanocarriers surface modification strategy
The commonly used biomimetic nanocarrier surface modification strategies include lipid insertion [ ], membrane fusion technique [ ], genetic engineering [ ], covalent modification [ ], etc. The lipid insertion method uses phospholipids that can bind to the cell membrane phospholipid bilayer on one end and coupled with drugs on the other end to modify the cell membrane surface [ ]. The membrane fusion technique combines a cell membrane with targeting ability with another cell membrane with other characteristics, enabling multifunctional biomimetic nanosystems to perform complex tasks in dynamic biological environments. Cells can obtain cell membrane fragments after treatment with hypotonic solution, ultrasound or homogenization [ ]. Co incubation of membranes of different cell types and combination with ultrasound can promote membrane fusion, and finally, uniform sized hybrid vesicles can be obtained by extrusion method. Genetic engineering uses plasmid vectors to genetically engineer cells that produce extracellular vesicles to establish cell lines that stably express ligand proteins [ ]. Finally, extracellular vesicles that express ligand proteins can be obtained by high-speed centrifugation. Covalent modification involves selecting appropriate chemical reactions to allow the functional groups on the ligand molecule to interact with the surface proteins of the biomimetic carrier, including click chemistry reactions, protein ligases such as Sortase A and OaAEP1 ligases, which can form strong and persistent chemical bonds between the ligand molecule and the biomimetic carrier [ ].

## Increased tumor cell targeting
Some cell membranes or EVs do not have tumor cell targeting properties, and the modification of ligands such as organic small molecules, proteins, and nucleic acid molecules on the surface of biomimetic vectors can confer precise targeting capabilities (Fig.  ; Table  ).

## Small molecules and polysaccharides
Compared to large-molecule antibodies, small-molecule ligands offer advantages such as lower cost, greater stability, and easier characterization. Vitamin analogs and carbohydrates are commonly used small-molecule ligands.
The mannose receptor (MR), also known as CD206, is a highly glycosylated type I transmembrane protein primarily expressed on the surface of macrophages and DCs and is an important pattern recognition receptor and endocytosis receptor in the innate immune system. On the one hand, MRs can take up antigens by mediating endocytosis and carry out antigen processing and presentation [ , ]. Mannose-modified lipids were prepared by ultrasonic membrane dispersion, fused with the erythrocyte membrane by ultrasonic extrusion, and coated with induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs) protein to prepare nanovaccines (iPSC@RBC-Mlipo). The modification of mannose promoted the absorption of iPSC@RBC-Mlipo by macrophages [ ]. On the other hand, MRs can target M2-type TAMs and play a role in immune microenvironment regulation by repolarizing into an M1-type phenotype [ ].

## Peptides
Compared with small molecular ligands with poor affinity, peptides show significant potential for targeted therapy of tumor cells at a lower biological cost. Peptides can be obtained by a variety of methods, such as phage display libraries or targeted antibody reference libraries [ ]. Peptide-based components are limited by their low molecular weight and rapid clearance from the body. To overcome these challenges and enhance their targeting specificity and therapeutic efficacy, their modification allows for attachment onto the surface of biomimetic nanocarriers, thereby improving their circulation time in vivo.

## Antibodies
Antibodies are glycoproteins consisting of multiple chains that are generated by the immune system in response to antigens. They are among the most commonly used natural ligands for surface modification of biomimetic nanocarriers [ ]. Antibodies exhibit high affinity and specificity for antigens, ensuring targeted delivery of therapeutic drugs and thereby minimizing side effects on healthy tissue. Antibodies and chemotherapy drugs have synergistic therapeutic effects and can kill cancer cells through two different mechanisms. On the one hand, ligands contribute to the internalization of NPs into cancer cells, and on the other hand, antibody receptor binding leads to simultaneous therapeutic effects (anti-inflammatory, antiapoptotic-based anticancer effects, etc.) [ ].

## Aptamers
Aptamers are a class of single-stranded DNA or RNA molecules with specific targeting functions that can be folded into three-dimensional structures to bind their targets with high affinity and strong specificity. Aptamers are often used as targeting ligands because of their low molecular weight, easy modification, batch stability, low toxicity, low immunogenicity, and ease of mass production. DNA aptamers such as AS1411 [ ], anti-human epidermal growth factor receptor-2 (HER2) aptamer (HApt) [ ], and transmembrane glycoprotein mucin 1 (MUC1) [ ] have been used in various tumor targeting methods and have broad application prospects.

 

 

## Increased environmental responsiveness
After internalization, nanocarriers are often trapped within endosomes or lysosomes, which can pose challenges in terms of therapeutic efficacy and drug resistance. Techniques such as membrane fusion or surface modification are utilized to introduce pH, ROS, GSH enzymes, light, and magnetic-sensitive components onto the surface of biomimetic nanocarriers, thereby enhancing drug release capabilities (Fig.  ).

## TME response
Aerobic glycolysis in tumors results in the production of large amounts of lactic acid and the release of H+, and inadequate blood perfusion limits the removal of acidic waste products, ultimately resulting in an extracellular pH between 6.5 and 6.8, which is lower than that of normal tissue [ ]. In addition to the mild acidity of the TME, the pH drop is more pronounced in the lysosomes and endosomal compartments within the tumor cells. After internalization, the biomimetic nanocarriers sequentially passed through early endosomes (pH 6.0–6.5), late endosomes (pH 4.5–5.5), and lysosomes (pH 4.5–5.5), resulting in a pH decrease from 6.5 to 4.5. The acidic nature of the TME and intracellular compartments has inspired the development of pH-responsive biomimetic nanocarriers that can achieve specific drug release, enhance cellular uptake, and facilitate lysosomal escape by recognizing the different pH values of the TME and intracellular compartments [ ].
The fusion of pH-responsive liposomes with cell membranes, or EXOs, is a common method for preparing pH-responsive biomimetic nanocarriers [ ]. Platelet membranes were coextruded with pH-responsive liposomes and then coated on the surface with iron-gallic acid coordination polymer nanodots (FeCNDs). Cluster of differentiation 62 platelet (CD62p) on the platelet membrane can selectively bind to CD44 receptors on tumor cells and be internalized into tumor cells. Under acidic conditions, the pH-responsive hybrid cell membrane ruptures and rapidly releases the internal nanoparticles. A three-dimensional multicellular spheroid model demonstrated that in an acidic environment (pH = 5.0), the tumor tissue penetration rate of FeCNDs was 72.84%, which was greater than that of the non-pH-responsive group (17.77%) [ ].
NPs responsive to GSH encapsulation can deplete intracellular GSH in tumor cells, increasing their sensitivity to oxidative stress. Additionally, utilizing GSH-sensitive chemical linkers to connect drugs can enhance the intracellular release of the drugs [ ].

## External stimulus response
The modification of photoresponsive and magnetically responsive groups on the surface of cell membranes, or EXOs, can make them responsive to external stimuli. One study [ ] utilized spinach-derived thylakoid nanovesicles (NTs) fused with E. coli MG1655-derived outer membrane vesicles (OMVs) to create bacterial-plant hybrid nanovesicles (BPNs). These BPNs consisted of NTs that carry photosensitive chlorophyll and various enzymes capable of converting H2O and H2O2 into O2, effectively inducing photodynamic effects. Upon intravenous injection, BPNs selectively accumulated in tumor tissues and initiated DC maturation. Under laser irradiation, the oxidative enzymes within NTs generate abundant ROS, leading to effective immunogenic cell death (ICD) and the release of tumor-associated antigens (TAAs). Subsequently, TAAs are efficiently presented by DCs, inducing a significant antigen-specific immune response.
Another approach is to load PSs [ ] and acoustic sensitizers [ ] into biomimetic nanocarriers. In photothermal therapy, photosensitizers kill tumor cells by absorbing light energy and converting it into heat, effectively promoting the release of cellular contents and inflammatory factors and inducing immunogenic cell death. In photodynamic therapy, photosensitizers deliver light energy to surrounding molecules, generating cytotoxic ROS for cancer treatment. Furthermore, drugs can be connected to photosensitizers through ROS-responsive linkers, thereby facilitating drug release. Sonodynamic therapy has better tissue penetration than photodynamic therapy and kills tumor cells by producing ROS. To promote better release of drugs, researchers usually combine multiple responsive materials to prepare biomimetic nanocarriers with multiple responses [ ].

 

## Challenges and perspectives
The TME is a complex network in which not only tumor cells communicate with each other but also interact with immune cells and stromal cells. Protein or RNA molecules on the surface or inside biomimetic nanocarriers enable them to act as communication mediators, and tumor cells respond to the physical conditions of the microenvironment, thus adapting to metabolic changes accordingly. The proteins on the surface of the biomimetic nanocarriers cannot be removed by communicating with the immune clearance system, enabling long-term circulation within the body. Derived from various cell types, these nanocarriers inherit attributes from their source cells, including the prolonged circulatory capability of erythrocytes, the oxygen transport functionality of hemoglobin, the wound-targeting properties of platelets, the homing capabilities of cancer cells, and the immunomodulatory functions of immune cells. These inherited properties enable the nanocarriers to effectively interact within tumor tissues. Finally, the lipid bilayer structure of cell membranes, or EXOs, offers opportunities for further modifications. By incorporating ligands on their surfaces, the targeting capability of biomimetic nanocarriers is enhanced, endowing them with specific tumor-homing properties. Additionally, such modifications can improve the heterogeneity of biomimetic nanocarriers. For example, while EXOs derived from cancer cells or platelets may inherently promote tumor progression, this challenge can be addressed through strategic engineering of the EXOs. Through surface modification with responsive ligands tailored to the characteristics of the TME, communication between biomimetic nanocarriers and the microenvironment can be achieved, enabling reshaping of the TME.
Currently, numerous clinical trials are underway to utilize EXOs, or cell membrane-based drug delivery systems, for cancer treatment, yielding a series of promising results (Table  ). However, several challenges remain to be overcome to achieve widespread clinical application of EXOs and cell membrane-based drug delivery systems. The first challenge lies in obtaining biomimetic nanocarriers. In EXO production, the culture medium used to generate EXOs is crucial. EXOs isolated from serum-containing media may contain exosomal impurities derived from serum. On the other hand, serum-free conditions can impose significant stress on cells, leading to changes in EXO production. Hence, researchers need to predeplete the serum from EXOs before utilizing them for cell culture or developing serum-free media. The current methods for extracting EXOs include ultracentrifugation, ultrafiltration, polymer precipitation, immunoaffinity chromatography, and immunomagnetic bead capture [ ]. However, these existing separation methods have notable limitations. Low-cost methods such as ultracentrifugation and ultrafiltration have lower production costs but may result in the deformation of EXOs, require time-intensive procedures, and yield lower sample purity. On the other hand, immunoaffinity chromatography and immunomagnetic bead capture have high purity and efficiency, but they often yield a lower quantity of EXOs, and the cost of antibodies is high. Therefore, further exploration is necessary to discover improved extraction methods for EXOs to increase both extraction efficiency and purity. In addition, the safety of biomimetic nanocarriers is limited by the presence of various functional and harmful substances in EXOs derived from cancer cells, platelets, and mesenchymal stem cells in clinical applications; therefore, methods that can effectively remove or inactivate harmful substances in biomimetic nanocarriers to ensure the safety of EXO therapy are needed.
The drug-carrying efficacy of biomimetic nanocarriers is affected by a variety of engineering approaches with relatively low drug-carrying capacity. For instance, a diffusion-based, noninvasive method that involves incubating cargo with purified EXOs or cell membranes is a simple approach. However, this method has a lower cargo loading efficiency. Transfection methods, which require specific DNA plasmids and transfection reagents, can lead to chemical contamination. Physical treatments such as ultrasound, electroporation, and freeze‒thaw cycles provide relatively easy operation and high cargo loading efficiency, but they can potentially damage the membranes and biological macromolecules of the cargo. Hade et al. conjugated miR-21-5p with a CPP (CPP YARA) for cargo loading, resulting in an 18.6-fold increase in cargo loading capacity. This approach is noninvasive and avoids issues such as chemical contamination, making it potentially promising for clinical applications [ ]. Currently, biomimetic nanocarriers are primarily produced in laboratory settings. To achieve clinical applications and commercial development, it is necessary to establish industrial processes. Continuous flow processes hold greater appeal for the industry, but this remains an unexplored field that requires further development [ ]. The development of biomimetic nanocarriers requires a multidisciplinary team. Combining the endogenous characteristics and functions of biomimetic nanocarriers with the controllability and quantification of production is the key problem that biomimetic nanocarriers need to solve.
