## Significance
The parasites that cause malaria avoid destruction by the human immune response by systematically changing the antigens they expose on the infected cell surface. This process, called antigenic variation, is responsible for the chronic nature and virulence of the disease. The mechanisms enabling parasites to undergo antigen switching remain unknown; however, it has been assumed that this process occurs stochastically over the course of an infection. Here we show that parasites can sense changes in their environment and undergo antigen switching in response to those changes. This implies a much more sophisticated interaction between host and parasite than was previously appreciated and suggests that parasites have evolved to undergo antigenic switching when needed, for example in response to immune recognition.
Keywords: methylation, metabolism, gene expression, var genes, immune evasion

## Abstract
The primary antigenic and virulence determinant of the human malaria parasite Plasmodium
falciparum is a variant surface protein called PfEMP1. Different forms of PfEMP1 are encoded by a multicopy gene family called var, and switching between active genes enables the parasites to evade the antibody response of their human hosts. var gene switching is key for the maintenance of chronic infections; however, what controls switching is unknown, although it has been suggested to occur at a constant frequency with little or no environmental influence. var gene transcription is controlled epigenetically through the activity of histone methyltransferases (HMTs). Studies in model systems have shown that metabolism and epigenetic control of gene expression are linked through the availability of intracellular S-adenosylmethionine (SAM), the principal methyl donor in biological methylation modifications, which can fluctuate based on nutrient availability. To determine whether environmental conditions and changes in metabolism can influence var gene expression, P. falciparum was cultured in media with altered concentrations of nutrients involved in SAM metabolism. We found that conditions that influence lipid metabolism induce var gene switching, indicating that parasites can respond to changes in their environment by altering var gene expression patterns. Genetic modifications that directly modified expression of the enzymes that control SAM levels similarly led to profound changes in var gene expression, confirming that changes in SAM availability modulate var gene switching. These observations directly challenge the paradigm that antigenic variation in P. falciparum follows an intrinsic, programed switching rate, which operates independently of any external stimuli.

Malaria parasites cause disease through invasion and replication within the circulating red blood cells of their vertebrate hosts. A defining feature contributing to the pathogenicity of Plasmodium falciparum is its ability to express a series of cytoadherence proteins on the surface of the iRBC. P. falciparum erythrocyte membrane protein 1 (PfEMP1) is anchored in knob-like structures within the iRBC membrane, extending into the extracellular space and facilitating cytoadherence to vascular endothelial receptors, thereby allowing the parasite to sequester within the microvasculature of various organs ( . Variant forms of PfEMP1 are encoded by a large, multicopy gene family called var ( – ). Each P. falciparum genome possesses about 60 var genes, located primarily within subtelomeric regions of the chromosomes ( , ).
var genes are expressed in a mutually exclusive manner such that each parasite only expresses a single gene over many cycles of asexual replication ( ). Over the course of an infection, the host’s immune system mounts an antibody response to the predominately expressed variant of PfEMP1, drastically reducing the circulating parasite population, often to undetectable levels. However, subpopulations of parasites expressing an alternative var gene can expand to high parasitemia, thereby leading to recurrent waves of parasites and enabling the infection to persist for a year or more ( , ). The transcriptional activation and silencing of var genes are controlled epigenetically with transcriptionally silent var genes found in a state of condensed heterochromatin, whereas the single active var gene is maintained in a transcriptionally permissive euchromatic state ( – ). Three marks have been found to be of primary importance in var gene regulation. Nucleosomes spanning the promoter region of the single active var gene are acetylated at the H3K9 position (Histone 3, Lysine 9; H3K9ac), while those at silent var genes are tri-methylated (H3K9me3). Conversely, the H3K36me3 mark is found at both active and silent var genes ( , ). Di- and tri-methylation of H3K4 are enriched at actively transcribed genes in many organisms and similarly are found around the single var promoter marked for activation ( ). Methyl groups at each of these positions are deposited by specific histone methyltransferases (HMTs), which in P. falciparum are largely SET-domain-containing proteins. PfSET2 and PfSET3 deposit the H3K36me3 and H3K9me3 marks, respectively ( – ). In addition, PfSET1 and PfSET10 were found to deposit the H3K4me3 mark ( , ).
var2csa (Pf3D7_1200600) is a unique member of the var family that encodes a form of PfEMP1 that exclusively binds to a placental-specific ligand, chondroitin sulfate A (CSA) ( , ). Unlike the hypervariable nature of the rest of the var gene family, var2csa is universally conserved in all P. falciparum isolates ( ). The upstream regulatory regions of var genes can be organized into three basic types, called UpsA, B, and C; however, var2csa has a unique promoter called UpsE. In contrast to other var genes, var2csa can be translationally repressed even when transcriptionally active due to the presence of a unique upstream open-reading frame (uORF) ( , ), and gene transcripts can be detected in nonpregnant individuals ( – ), suggesting that it might serve a separate function apart from encoding PfEMP1. Recent work has implied that this additional role relates to coordinating var expression switching. Specifically, long-term in vitro culture of parasites results in a high activation rate of var2csa, suggesting that it might represent a default state for var gene switching ( ). Similarly, we previously showed that altering the activity of HMTs governing var gene mutually exclusive expression resulted in activation of var2csa ( , ), and deletion of var2csa from the parasite’s genome has a profound effect on switching rates ( ). Because of its unique nature and its rapid activation when var switching is induced, expression levels of var2csa can be used as a sensitive tool to monitor changes in var gene expression within a population of parasites.
The methionine arm of one carbon metabolism links it to histone methylation ( ), and thus the epigenetic processes of the cell, through the intermediate metabolites S-adenosylmethionine (SAM) and S-adenosylhomocysteine (SAH). SAM, generated through the folate and methionine cycles, is the universal methyl donor for many cellular functions, including histone methylation, yielding SAH which is then hydrolyzed to homocysteine. HMT activity depends on the concentration of SAM, and SAH is a known inhibitor of HMTs, and thus the SAM/SAH ratio, also called the methylation index, plays a role in regulating HMT activity ( , ). HMT Km values lie in the range of intracellular SAM concentrations, suggesting that even small fluctuations in SAM levels can significantly affect HMT activity, increasing or decreasing histone methylation and thus providing a direct link between it and cellular metabolism through SAM ( – ). Recent work in several systems has supported the importance of this link ( – ).
Several recent reports have begun to explore the capacity of Plasmodium species to alter gene expression in response to changes in the local environment ( – ). With respect to var gene switching, it has been assumed that parasites evolved an intrinsic rate of switching that corresponds to the time required for their host to generate an effective antibody response, thus enabled them to maintain a chronic infection. However, here we challenge this paradigm and provide evidence of var gene expression switching in response to altered environmental conditions. We found that manipulation of specific components of one-carbon metabolic pathways that alter the intracellular ratio of SAM/SAH, either environmentally or genetically, induces changes in var expression. Specifically, we observed a coordinated switch to var2csa expression across independent parasite populations grown under conditions designed to increase SAM/SAH. These results phenocopy previous observations in which the activity of specific HMTs was altered, further supporting a central role for var2csa within a var switching network. We propose a model whereby P. falciparum parasites can respond to changes in their environment through altered SAM/SAH levels, leading to altered antigen (var gene) expression.

## Results

## Altering Environmental Levels of Lipid Metabolic Precursors Induces var Transcriptional Switching.
Previous work showed that altering the activity of the HMTs PfSET2 and PfSET3 induced dramatic var expression changes, specifically activation of var2csa ( , ). Given recent evidence supporting a link between SAM levels and HMT activity ( – , , ), we decided to test whether changes in the levels of environmental precursors of one-carbon metabolic pathways would similarly alter var gene expression.
Parasite growth and replication require the synthesis of large amounts of new membranes, increasing the total phospholipid concentration of the iRBC by as much as sixfold ( , ). Similar to most eukaryotes, the primary components of Plasmodium membranes are phospholipids, with phosphatidylcholine (PtdCho) and phosphatidylethanolamine (PtdEtn) accounting for 40 to 55% and 30 to 40% of total plasma membrane phospholipid content, respectively ( – ). The synthesis of the primary membrane lipid PtdCho requires the initial synthesis of phosphocholine, which can take place via the serine decarboxylase phosphoethanolamine methyltransferase (SDPM) pathway or directly through the first steps of the cytidine diphosphate (CDP)-choline pathway (also known as the Kennedy pathway) ( ) ( , , ). The de novo CDP-choline pathway uses choline and fatty acids from host serum as precursors, while the SDPM pathway uses serine and fatty acids. In the SDPM pathway, serine must first be decarboxylated to ethanolamine then converted to phosphoethanolamine (p-Etn) before joining the CDP-choline pathway as phosphocholine (p-Cho) through the action of a unique enzyme, phosphoethanolamine N-methyltransferase (PfPMT) ( , ). PfPMT methylates p-Etn to p-Cho, consuming three molecules of SAM. Thus, for the parasite to synthesize PtdCho from serine it must consume SAM. The addition of excess choline in culture, allowing parasites to use the CDP-choline pathway to synthesize PtdCho, was observed to lower PfPMT transcript and protein levels, suggesting the capability of P. falciparum to adjust its use of these pathways based on the nutrient conditions of its host ( ). We therefore hypothesized that growing parasites in culture under varied levels of choline and serine would alter the parasites’ consumption of SAM and consequently change intracellular SAM/SAH. This in turn could alter var gene expression over time through the intersection of these lipid synthesis pathways with SAM pools.
 To test this hypothesis, we manipulated the concentrations of serine and choline available to parasites grown in culture and determined the effects on growth rates and var gene expression patterns. We used a subcloned population of the 3D7 reference line that was known to be deficient in the production of gametocytes due to a mutation in pfap2-g, a gene required for sexual commitment ( , ). This enabled us to focus on changes in var gene expression without the complication of changes in gene expression due to sexual commitment, which has been shown to be influenced by lipid precursors ( ). This line was cultured in a custom-made “modified” RPMI base medium lacking choline and serine, where differing amounts of either were supplemented depending on the condition being tested (SI Appendix, Tables S1 and S2). For comparison, control parasites were cultured in parallel in standard RPMI 1640. Since serine is present in hemoglobin, removal from the media is likely to deplete but not eliminate the availability of this amino acid. Total RNA was extracted from each culture after 2 and 4 wk and analysis of var expression was performed using a standardized, qRT-PCR that determines the expression level of each individual var gene ( ). In addition, intracellular metabolites were extracted from parasites and measured by LC-MS as previously described ( ). This enabled us to directly determine if the changes in the concentrations of serine and choline in the media were reflected by changes of these nutrients within the parasites and if the changes affected SAM levels. As hypothesized, alterations in the levels of choline and serine in the culture medium greatly accelerated var switching in comparison to parasites cultured under standard nutrient conditions ( and Supplementary data). Specifically, depletion of serine led to an increase in intracellular SAM and accelerated activation of var2csa expression. This effect was greatly enhanced when combined with addition of excess choline. Depletion of both choline and serine, or excess serine did not result in activation of var2csa and instead mimicked the expression pattern observed in parasites grown in standard media. No significant growth phenotype was observed when culturing parasites under these modified conditions (SI Appendix, Fig. S2).
 The conditions that induced changes in var gene expression, excess choline and/or depleted serine, are both predicted to shift lipid synthesis away from the SDPM pathway and toward the CDP pathway ( ), thus reducing SAM consumption and potentially increasing the availability of SAM for HMT activity. This in turn could destabilize epigenetic regulation of var expression and trigger a transcriptional switch over time. These results phenocopy previous results in which the activity of specific HMTs was directly manipulated ( , ), which similarly let to a coordinated switch to var2csa. While the shifts in var gene expression to var2csa were dramatic, the metabolomic analysis indicates that the changes in SAM and SAH levels were subtle although reproducible ( ), consistent with the hypothesis that even small fluctuations in SAM/SAH levels can have significant effects on HMT activity and gene expression ( – ).

## Excess Methionine Induces var Transcriptional Switching.
As shown in , the balance between the CDP and SDPM pathways can be influenced by the availability of either choline or serine, which in turn can affect SAM pools via SAM consumption by PfPMT. In contrast, methionine, taken up by P. falciparum either from human serum or the digestion of hemoglobin, can be used directly by the parasite to synthesize SAM ( ). If the changes in var gene expression induced by depletion of serine and/or excess choline are in fact due to an increase in SAM pools ( ), we hypothesized that conditions of excess methionine would have a similar effect. To test this hypothesis, we grew parasites in culture media in which the availability of methionine was manipulated (SI Appendix, Table S1). Similar to serine, methionine is present in hemoglobin; therefore, it can only be depleted in the growth medium and cannot be completely eliminated from the culture. var expression patterns were analyzed by Q-RT-PCR after 2 and 4 wk of growth in the modified media and intracellular metabolites were measured using LC-MS. Under conditions of depleted methionine, var expression closely resembled that observed in parasites grown in standard media ( ). In contrast, growth in the presence of excess methionine led to a measurable increase in SAM Levels and a coordinated switch to var2csa, similar to the conditions of excess choline (  and replicate experiment shown in Supplemental Data). No significant growth phenotype was observed when culturing parasites under these modified conditions (SI Appendix, Fig. S2). These experiments, coupled with those in which the levels of choline and serine were altered, further suggest that conditions that elevate SAM pools accelerate var gene switching, specifically activation of var2csa.
 

## Increasing Expression of PfSAMS Induces var Switching.
The changes in var gene expression observed upon altering the availability of choline, serine or methionine correlated with changes in intracellular SAM levels, although other possible effects of nutrient availability were also possible. We therefore sought to more directly test how changes in intracellular SAM can affect var gene expression. In eukaryotes, SAM synthetase (SAMS) catalyzes the formation of SAM from methionine and ATP, while SAH hydrolase catalyzes the reversible hydrolysis of SAH to adenosine and homocysteine ( ). Homocysteine is then secreted from the infected cell. The activity of SAMS can directly raise the methylation index (SAM/SAH ratio) by catalyzing the production of SAM, and this enzyme is conserved in Plasmodium and encoded by a single copy gene (PfSAMS: PF3D7_0922200). To more directly test the hypothesis that var switching is affected by the levels of intracellular SAM, we altered the expression of PfSAMS through the use of an overexpression construct, applying the RNA aptamer system adapted for Plasmodium by Ganesan et al. ( ). This system enables regulated expression of an episomally encoded protein through the placement of Tet repressor protein (TetR) aptamers in the 3′ untranslated region (UTR) of the transcript. In the presence of anhydrotetracycline (Atc), the transcript is stabilized, enabling overexpression of the protein of interest (in addition to normal expression from the chromosomal copy) ( ) ( ). The specific vector used here (pMG68 supplied by Dr Jacquin Niles) expresses an epitope-tagged (HA) protein. The PfSAMS-HA overexpression construct (OE) was transfected into NF54 parasites and overexpression verified by Western blot probed with anti-SAMS antibodies. This enabled detection of both the wild-type and HA-tagged proteins ( ). This analysis showed leaky expression of SAMS-HA coming from the plasmid prior to induction, but significantly greater expression in the presence of Atc. Quantification of total SAMS expression (WT + SAMS-HA) showed a nearly 2.5-fold increase in SAMS expression in the presence of Atc ( ). To determine whether PfSAMS overexpression leads to an increase in intracellular SAM, metabolites were once again extracted from parasites and measured by LC-MS ( ). This analysis showed that SAMS overexpression induces a significant increase in intracellular SAM, as expected, while levels of SAH did not change significantly ( ).
 To determine whether an increase in intracellular SAM leads to changes in var gene expression, the PfSAMS-HA overexpression construct was transfected into the same 3D7 parent clone described above and the parasites assayed for var expression both “on” and “off” Atc. A line transfected with an empty vector (EV) served as a control. Remarkably, the overexpression line displayed profound changes in var expression compared to the parent population or the empty vector control ( ). Specifically, the culture had switched to expression of var2csa at the initial timepoint, as soon as the culture grew to a sufficient parasitemia to recover RNA. Switching to var2csa was near absolute, with very little expression of alternative var genes detectable in the population. This switch occurred even in the absence of induction by Atc, suggesting that even leaky expression from the episome was enough to robustly alter the epigenetic control of var genes. The EV control line showed no difference in var expression before or after application of Atc ( ). An additional replicate is shown in Supplementary Data. It is noteworthy that the change in intracellular SAM observed here was much greater than what resulted from altering choline, serine or methionine levels in the media and that the shift to var2csa was also much more profound, providing additional evidence that it is the changes in SAM that are responsible for alterations in var gene expression.
Across three replicates, the PfSAMS OE did not grow at a visibly different rate than untransfected WT parasites or the EV transfected line (SI Appendix, Fig. S2). An addition of 0.5 mM of Atc also did not detectably alter growth. These data further support the hypothesis that conditions that increase the ratio of SAM/SAH alter var expression, and that this switch is uniquely to var2csa.

## PfSAMS Overexpression Lines Revert to Wild-Type upon Loss of Transgene Expression.
The PfSAMS overexpression plasmid carries the blasticidin S deaminase gene ( ), allowing for selection and maintenance of the episome through the inclusion of the antibiotic blasticidin S in the culture media. Transfected episomes in P. falciparum require continuous selection for maintenance, and therefore, the growth in the absence of selection pressure leads to rapid shedding of the plasmid by the parasites. The PfSAMS overexpression line displayed a profound switch to expression of var2csa immediately after transfection ( ), likely due to leaky expression of the transgene from the episome, even in the absence of Atc ( ). To ensure that switching to var2csa was a result of PfSAMS overexpression and not due to some other aspect of the transfection process, this line was grown in the absence of blasticidin S selection pressure for 2 mo and the var expression profile determined. Representative results are shown in (and an additional replicate is shown in the Supplemental Data). The line displayed a shift in var expression away from var2csa when drug selection was removed and Q-RT-PCR analysis showed that PfSAMS mRNA had returned to the levels found in the empty vector control ( ), indicating that the stable var2csa expression phenotype observed in the transfected line was indeed a result of the increased PfSAMS expression.

## Reducing PfSAMS Expression Does Not Induce var Switching.
To assess whether a decrease in the levels of SAM/SAH could affect var gene expression, we assayed var expression profiles in three clones of a PfSAMS-glmS line previously described by Harris et al (bioRxiv 2022.01.18.476397) in which the level of PfSAMS expression can be knocked down, leading to a reduction in SAM levels by ~60%. This line was generated through integration of the glmS ribozyme into the 3′ UTR of the PfSAMS locus, allowing for post-transcriptional regulation of gene expression as previously described by Prommana et al. ( ). When glucosamine (GlcN) is added, the ribozyme is cleaved leading to degradation of the mRNA ( ) and a corresponding reduction in protein expression. In three clones, expression of PfSAMS was found to be reduced by about half after application of GlcN (2.5 mM) relative to those in the absence of GlcN (0 mM) ( ). var expression profiles were determined for each line, in both the presence and the absence of GlcN. Representative results for one PfSAMS-glms clone are shown in with data from additional clones provided in the Supplemental Data. Growth over a 4-wk period either on or off GlcN did not induce a switch to var2csa across any of the three clones. To ensure that the addition of GlcN alone does not alter var expression, an untransfected (WT) 3D7 line was grown in parallel on and off the same concentration of GlcN ( ). No significant change in var expression was observed. Intriguingly, all three PfSAMS-glms clones displayed virtually identical, heterogenous var expression patterns (high expression levels of six var genes: PF3D7_1000100, PF3D7_0412400, PF3D7_0412700, PF3D7_0632500, PF3D7_0809100, and PF3D7_0600200) at all time points (  and Supplemental Data) suggesting that var switching rates might be reduced in these lines, and the overall level of var expression was much greater when compared to wild-type parasites (SI Appendix, Fig. S3). Together with the nutrient manipulation experiments and the PfSAMS overexpression studies, these results indicate that conditions that increased the intracellular level of SAM/SAH are uniquely capable of inducing switching to var2csa.
 Growth assay results for two PfSAMS-glms clones are shown in SI Appendix, Fig. S2. Interestingly, the PfSAMS-glms lines appear to have a positive growth phenotype, growing at a slightly faster rate than an untransfected WT line. An addition of 2.5 mM GlcN caused a slight reduction in growth rate for both the WT and PfSAMS-glms lines.

## Changes in SAM Metabolism Result in Alterations in Histone Methylation.
Our manipulation of nutrient availability or PfSAMS expression altered levels of SAM, the methyl donor for the histone methyltransferases involved in epigenetic regulation of gene expression. To determine whether manipulating SAM levels resulted in changes in histone methylation, we performed whole genome “cut and run” analysis ( ) to examine levels of H3K9me3 and H3K4me3, histone methylation marks directly implicated in var gene regulation ( , ). Specifically, we compared parasites grown under conditions that increase SAM availability (excess choline) to parasites grown under conditions of depleted choline and methionine. These experiments were performed 26 h after the parasites were exposed to the altered media conditions, prior to any detectable changes in var gene expression, thus enabling us to observe changes in histone modifications that are a direct response to altered nutrient availability rather than any adaptive or long-term changes that might occur over extended growth under these conditions. Whole genome analysis identified distinct increases of histone methylation at regions throughout the genome known to be enriched in heterochromatin. Four representative examples that display a variety of gene families are shown in , including a subtelomeric region of chromosome 2, an internal var tandem array from chromosome 4, the clag3.1/clag3.2 locus from chromosome 3 and the subtelomeric region of chromosome 12 where var2csa resides. The increases in histone methylation extended into regions containing members of other clonally variant gene families, including, rif, stevor, phist and acs. In all cases, we detected a consistent and distinct increase in H3K9me3 at the variant antigen encoding genes while H3K4me3 was much less responsive ( ). The parasite line used was expressing var2csa, and this locus displayed little change in H3K9me3 or H3K4me3, consistent with the effect being more pronounced at silent regions of the genome that are enriched in H3K9me3. The differential effect on levels of the two histone modifications likely reflects different Km values of the specific methyltransferases that deposit the marks, although this will require experiments with the purified enzymes to verify. These results are consistent with a model in which increases in SAM availability leads to corresponding increases in histone methyltransferase activity and histone methylation.
 

## Overall Gene Expression Changes in Response to Alterations in SAM Metabolism.
Culture conditions that increased SAM levels (excess choline/depleted serine, excess methionine, and overexpression of PfSAMS) resulted in a switch in var gene expression to var2csa while knockdown of PfSAMS did not ( – ). To determine whether these changes had additional effects on expression of genes other than var genes, parasites reared under conditions of increased choline or methionine supplementation or after PfSAMS overexpression or knockdown were subjected to whole transcriptome analysis by RNA-seq. Differential gene expression analysis was performed by comparing the transcriptomes derived from each of these conditions to that obtained from wild-type parasites grown in standard media. Using relatively stringent criteria [at least 10,000 basemean (mean of normalized counts of all samples) and a log2FoldChange of at least 1.95], we identified relatively few genes that were responsive to our manipulations (changes in gene expression for all conditions are summarized in Dataset S1). This was not unexpected given that these conditions had little or no effect on parasite growth. We did observe an expected decrease in PfPMT expression in trophozoites grown in excess choline, as was previously described ( ). We also observed a decrease in expression of a large cohort of genes expressed very late in the replicative cycle in the PfSAMS knockdown line. This is consistent with a small delay in cell cycle progression that was previously observed when parasites were grown in the presence of low doses of a histone methyltransferase inhibitor ( ). It is logical that reduced SAM availability would similarly reduce methyltransferase activity and produce a similar phenotype. Interestingly, in neither case is the overall parasite growth rate reduced, indicating that the cell cycle delay is transient and that the parasites complete the overall replicative cycle on time.
As a more sensitive way to identify any genes that are responsive to changes in SAM availability, we performed differential gene expression analysis comparing the two manipulations that led to the largest changes in SAM levels, the PfSAMS overexpression line to the PfSAMS knockdown line (SI Appendix, Table. S4). Using the same stringency criteria, in ring-stages, we observed an upregulation of eight genes in the PfSAMS overexpression line, including the gene encoding ornithine aminotransferase (OAT), which was previously shown to be responsive to changes in SAM metabolism ( ), as well as the gene encoding Plasmodium transcription enhancer factor (PTEF), a protein previously shown to be involved in translation of the var2csa mRNA ( ). We also observed upregulation of transcripts encoding two AP2 transcription factors (AP2-FG and AP2-I), the significance of which is not clear. Comparison of the trophozoite transcriptomes only detected changes in expression of the very late-stage specific genes mentioned above that are downregulated in the PfSAMS knockdown line. These are primarily genes encoding proteins involved in erozoitee formation and their reduced expression is consistent with slowed entry into the last phase of the replicative cycle of the PfSAMS knockdown line.
We were particularly interested in any changes in expression of genes known to be subject to clonally variant expression and therefore likely to be responsive to changes in histone methylation. Specifically, we compare the PfSAMS overexpression and knockdown lines separately to wild-type parasites and examined changes in mRNA abundance of the genes and gene families known to be linked to the histone modification H3K9me3, including var, rifin, stevor, Pfmc-2TM, clag, phist, hyp, acs and others ( ). To increase the likelihood of detecting meaningful changes in genes that are often expressed in only a subset of parasites within a population, we reduced the threshold level of expression to a basemean of 500 for this analysis. As shown in , the vast majority of these genes displayed a reduction in transcript levels when PfSAMS was overexpressed and a corresponding increase in expression when PfSAMS was knocked down. This likely reflects the changes in the abundance of the silencing mark H3K9me3 shown in . We also observed that var genes appear to be more responsive to changes in SAM levels that the other gene families. Interestingly, from this large group of genes, only four defied the general trend in response to both genetic manipulations, becoming more highly expressed in the PfSAMS overexpressing line and repressed in the PfSAMS knockdown line. These included one member each of the PHISTa, PHISTb, and PHISTc families (Pf3D7_0832200, Pf3D7_1201000 and Pf3D7_0424000, respectively). The PHIST (Plasmodium helical interspersed subtelomeric) proteins are exported into the host RBC where some members have been proposed to play a role in PfEMP1 trafficking and display on the RBC surface ( – ). Pf3D7_0424000 was previously shown to be transcriptionally upregulated with var2csa in clinical isolates ( , ), providing additional evidence that these genes might be coregulated with var2csa.
 To ensure that any changes in gene expression that we observed were in fact the result of changes in SAM metabolism rather than unexpected mutations in the parasite’s genome, whole genome sequencing was performed on the PfSAMS overexpressing and knockdown transgenic parasite lines as well as parasites grown for 4 wk under culture conditions of excess choline/depleted serine or excess methionine. We identified no mutations shared by parasites displaying a shift toward var2csa expression or in genes thought to be involved in lipid or SAM/SAH metabolism, chromatin modification or transcription. Therefore, we conclude that the gene expression changes we observed are unlikely to be due to genetic mutations.

## Subcloning of Transfected Parasite Lines Gives Additional Insight into their var Switching Patterns.
The propensity of parasites to undergo var gene switching can also be readily detected by generating subcloned parasite lines by limiting dilution. For example, subcloned populations are typically recovered that either express a single dominant var gene or alternatively express a very heterogenous mix of var genes ( ), with the latter parasites presumably reflecting a population that arose from a single parasite that was actively undergoing var gene switching at or near the moment that it was isolated. We applied this method to examine var gene switching patterns for parasites in which we had earlier altered PfSAMS expression. Specifically, we subcloned populations of the empty vector control, PfSAMS overexpression and PfSAMS knockdown lines, maintaining a selection pressure of 1 μg/mL blastidicin S to facilitate maintenance of the episomal expression construct. We then examined the var expression pattern in each of the subcloned lines.
For the empty vector (EV) control line, we analyzed eight individual subclones and the var expression profiles are pictured as pie charts in . Out of the eight screened EV clones, one had a unique var expression profile from the parent clone, manifesting as a heterogenous expression profile and indicative of active switching. It is notable that each of the seven remaining clones had maintained high expression of the dominant “on” var, and none had switched to var2csa.
 For the PfSAMS overexpression line, we anticipated that all would be found to continue expressing var2csa at very high levels. We isolated a total of 14 individual subclones and found that 12 continued to express var2csa as the dominant var gene, as expected ( ). Two clones, however, had switched to expressing a heterogenous mix of var genes at low levels, indicative of active switching. Given that overexpression of PfSAMS resulted in virtually complete switching to var2csa ( ), we expected these lines to express almost exclusively var2csa as long as PfSAMS overexpression was maintained, therefore we were surprised to observe clones that displayed active switching. Consequently, we more closely examined PfSAMS expression in these two lines using Q-RT-PCR. Surprisingly, PfSAMS expression was greatly reduced ( , clones H9 and C7), indicating that this population was no longer overexpressing PfSAMS and providing an explanation for why the parasites had stopped exclusively expressing var2csa. Expression of PfSAMS in these two clones was found to be even lower than an untransfected WT line. The reason for this loss of PfSAMS overexpression is not known, but likely is the result of an alteration in the activity of the episomal promoter driving expression of the PfSAMS overexpression construct. To determine if the var2csa expression phenotype could be rescued by increasing PfSAMS expression from the transfected episome, the concentration of blasticidin S in the culture media was increased slightly to 2 μg/mL, a condition that is known to increase transgene expression ( , ). As anticipated, these cultures reverted to var2csa expression after 3 wk ( ), further supporting that increased PfSAMS expression is responsible for var2csa transcriptional activation.
The clones obtained from the PfSAMS knockdown line grown in the presence of glucosamine are pictured as pie charts in . Interestingly, all clones maintained generally the same heterogenous var expression pattern as the parent population. If these parasites were undergoing normal or rapid switching, we would expect the heterogenous profiles to differ between each clone. However, the patterns were very similar, with the most highly expressed genes shared between all eight clones. This is surprising and indicates either that switching has become skewed toward this subset of highly expressed genes, or alternatively, that mutually exclusive expression has been altered and that individual parasites are now expressing all these genes. The overall increase in total var gene expression in the knockdown lines is consistent with the latter hypothesis (SI Appendix, Fig. S3). Interestingly, some of the genes that are expressed at a low level differ between the clones, suggesting that the original population maintains the ability to switch var genes to some extent under conditions of lower PfSAMS expression. Together, results from manipulation of PfSAMS expression further support a model in which higher levels of SAM/SAH push parasite populations to express var2csa.

## Discussion
How parasites avoid rapid exhaustion of their var repertoire has been a topic of speculation ever since the var gene family was first described. Several hypotheses have been proposed to address this question, including that parasites have evolved an inherent switching frequency that matches the time required for the host to mount an effective antibody response against the expressed form of PfEMP1 (reviewed in ref.  ). Alternatively, it has been suggested that parasites might be able to sense increasing titers of antibodies that recognize the infected RBC surface and respond by undergoing var gene switching. Experimental data supporting the existence of a sensing mechanism have been presented for the monkey parasite Plasmodium knowlesi ( ); however, no similar data have been reported for P. falciparum or any other malaria parasite species. The data we present here however indicate that P. falciparum can in fact sense environmental changes and undergo var gene switching in response. Specifically, conditions that alter SAM metabolism can lead to robust changes in var gene expression. These results parallel recent observations that changes in the availability of certain environmental metabolites can drastically alter rates of sexual differentiation ( ) and rates of multiplication ( ). We have performed similar experiments in a gametocyte-producing line of P. falciparum and found that conditions that induce var gene switching (excess choline or methionine) suppress pfap2-g expression and sexual commitment (Harris et al, bioRxiv 2022.01.18.476397), indicating that these two phenomena are responding to similar environmental stimuli, but in opposite directions.
If parasites can respond to environmental changes by undergoing var gene switching, what conditions might elicit this response? If parasites evolved to be able to undergo changes in var gene expression in response to immune recognition, rising antibody titers targeting PfEMP1 and immune cell activation by immune complexes could be key to triggering var gene switching. For example, it is known that activation of various antigen presenting cells (dendritic cells, macrophages, B cells) can cause dramatic changes in local availability of various amino acids, including arginine and tryptophan, as well as local concentrations of polyamines, numerous cytokines and reactive oxygen species (reviewed by refs.   and  ). Such changes can result in downstream effects on lipid metabolism and SAM availability, which are known to cause profound changes in epigenetic marks associated with heterochromatin in model systems ( , , ). Thus, the immune response stimulated by antibodies against the expressed form of PfEMP1 could result in drastic changes in local nutrient availability, thereby stimulating var gene switching through the pathways described here. A similar model was previously proposed for regulating sexual commitment based on the observation that levels of LysoPC can change dramatically in response to immune activation or infection by various pathogens ( , – ). var gene switching could be similarly modulated, albeit separately from sexual commitment.
In the experiments described here, manipulations that increased SAM levels resulted in a corresponding increase in histone methylation throughout the heterochromatic regions of the genome ( ). Transcriptomic analysis detected very little changes in overall gene expression, suggesting that the increased histone methylation reinforced heterochromatic gene silencing that was already in place in these regions. With respect to var genes, promoter competition has frequently been cited as a model for mutually exclusive gene expression ( – ), with a single gene outcompeting other members of a family to become the transcriptionally active copy. We hypothesize that increased silencing efficiency at UpsA/B/C var loci shifts competition to var2csa, leading to its activation under conditions of increased SAM. Interestingly, decreased SAM levels appear to loosen competition, leading to activation of several var genes and an overall increase in total var transcript levels (  and  and SI Appendix, Fig. S3). These data suggest a model in which the unique var2csa promoter becomes selectively activated under conditions of increased heterochromatic silencing, enabling it to serve as a switching node ( ). While the precise nature of the competition between var promoters is not understood, histone methylation levels appear to strongly influence the interactions. Interestingly, we did not detect activation of specific members of the rifin, stevor or Pfmc-2TM gene families in response to our manipulations, suggesting that expression of these gene families is regulated differently. The co-induction of PF3D7_0424000, a gene previously detected as being upregulated along with var2csa in clinical isolates ( , ), and the gene encoding PTEF, a protein implicated in overcoming the repressive effect of the var2csa uORF ( ), reinforces a possible regulatory link between these genes and var2csa.
While we observed changes in histone modifications at heterochromatic regions throughout the genome, it is also possible that SAM availability might not influence the entire genome equally. For example, while our genetic and metabolic manipulations led to profound changes in var gene expression, we observed little to no change in parasite growth and the overall transcriptome changed very little, indicating that major effects on gene expression are likely limited to a relatively narrow portion of the genome. In a recent paper ( ), Heinberg and colleagues provided evidence for how, through the formation of R-loops, var antisense lncRNAs serve to recruit specific protein complexes to the active var locus, thereby facilitating the localized assembly of transcriptionally active chromatin within the subnuclear var expression site. Remarkably, among the recruited proteins are metabolic enzymes, including thioredoxin peroxidase (PfTPx-1), which was previously shown to interact directly with both PfSAMS and PfSAHH ( ), thus linking SAM metabolism to chromatin assembly at the active var gene. These authors showed that manipulation of expression of either PfTPx-1 or PfSAMS alters var gene expression switching, specifically activating var2csa transcription, further implicating these proteins in a transcriptional response pathway. Together with the observations described here, this work defines a molecular mechanism that enables parasites to sense alterations to their environment and respond through epigenetic changes in gene expression. In addition, by employing lncRNAs to anchor protein complexes to specific loci, parasites are able to target epigenetic changes to individual genes, thus differentiating the assembly of active versus silent chromatin and contributing to mutually exclusive var gene expression.
