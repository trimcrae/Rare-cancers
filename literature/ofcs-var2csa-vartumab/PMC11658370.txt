## Background
Placental malaria (PM) is characterized by Plasmodium parasite sequestration in the placenta. It is responsible for various adverse pregnancy outcomes, including maternal anaemia and low birth weight (LBW). This study aimed to assess prevalence and risk factors of PM, and gestational malaria (GM), together with the prevalence of congenital malaria (CM), maternal anaemia, and LBW among parturient women attending delivery ward of Metti Health Centre (Metti HC) in Majang Zone of Gambella Region, Southwest Ethiopia.

## Methods
A cross-sectional study involving 180 parturient women attending delivery ward of Metti HC was conducted from November 2022-March 2023. Sociodemographic, obstetric, and anti-malarial intervention data were collected. Capillary, placental and cord blood, and placental biopsy were collected to diagnose malaria using rapid diagnostic test (RDT), microscopy, quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR), and histopathology. Haemoglobin concentration and blood group of the mother and weight of the newborn were determined. Statistical analyses were done by SPSS Version 26.0. Multivariable logistic regression analysis and Chi-square test were done to identify risk factors. Results were presented in text, tables and graphs.

## Results
The prevalence of GM, PM, CM, maternal anaemia, and LBW was 24.4% (95% CI 18.1–30.1), 34.4% (95% CI 27.4–41.4), 5.0% (95% CI 2.4–8.8), 41.7% (95% CI 34.6–49.0) and 27.8% (95% CI 21.6–34.6), respectively. Risk factors of GM were: presence of malaria history within the previous year (AOR: 5.10; 95% CI 1.64–15.83), lack of indoor residual spray (IRS) within the previous year (AOR: 2.98; 95% CI 1.05–8.45), and lack of antenatal care (ANC) contact during the index pregnancy (AOR: 3.96; 95% CI 1.44–10.87). Risk factors of PM were: presence of malaria history within the previous year (AOR: 2.98; 95% CI 1.05–8.45), and lack of ANC contact during the index pregnancy (AOR: 4.83; 95% CI 1.91–12.18). The risk of CM (p < 0.001), maternal anaemia (p < 0.001) and LBW (p < 0.001) increased with GM and PM.

## Conclusion
There is high prevalence of GM, PM, maternal anaemia, and LBW in the study area. The presence of GM and PM increased the risk of maternal anaemia, CM, and LBW. The identified risk factors should be considered to mitigate malaria among parturient women and its adverse outcomes.

## Background
Malaria in pregnancy (MiP) continues to be a significant public health problem, particularly in sub-Saharan Africa. It poses a threat to pregnant women, fetuses, newborns, children, and beyond [ , ]. The pathophysiology of MiP is mainly due to the presence of a placenta suitable for parasite sequestration and altered immunity, which renders the woman incapable of resisting the invading parasite [ , ]. Malaria in pregnancy, especially falciparum malaria in high transmission settings, often involves PM, which is characterized by the sequestration of Plasmodium-infected red blood cells (iRBCs) in the intervillous spaces of the placenta mediated by the unique variant surface antigen chondroitin sulfate A (VAR2CSA) [ , ]. Placental malaria involves various pathological processes and changes, including inflammation, tissue necrosis, and infiltration of the intervillous space with immune cells, haemozoin pigment, and fibrin deposit. These alterations collectively affect the normal development, structure, and function of the placenta, including the impaired exchange of nutrients, oxygen, and waste, resulting in a multitude of adverse pregnancy outcomes, such as anaemia, intrauterine growth retardation (IUGR), prematurity, and LBW [ , ]. Although Plasmodium vivax and Plasmodium falciparum are reported to cause PM, the majority of the deleterious inflammatory responses and infiltration of the intervillous space are due to P. falciparum infection [ , , ].
The global prevalence of GM in parturiency ranged from 3.5% in Papua New Guinea [ ], 15.6% in five vivax malaria-endemic countries [ ] to 16.5% in Thailand [ ], and 35.8% in Colombia [ ]. Gestational malaria is a daunting public health problem in sub-Saharan Africa, where 14.2%, 30.8%, 37.8%, and 59.2% prevalence rates were reported from Mozambique [ ], Congo Brazzaville [ ], Sudan [ ], and Nigeria [ ], respectively. The prevalence of GM at delivery in Ethiopia ranged from 6.4% [ ] to 15.2% [ ]. The prevalence of PM varies across different parts of the world, mainly due to variations in malaria endemicity, intensity of prevention activity, diagnostic tools, and specimen types involved in the studies [ , , ]. Its prevalence was 2.3% (sub-microscopic) in Laos [ ], 4.4% in Papua New Guinea [ ], 10.3% in India [ ], 12.4% in five vivax malaria-endemic countries [ ], and 20.9% in Colombia [ ]. The prevalence of placental malaria has remained unacceptably high in many parts of sub-Saharan Africa [ , , – ]. Although there is granularity of data due to the type of sample and diagnostic tool used for the diagnosis of PM, sample size, length of study period, inclusion criteria, and other methodological issues, the prevalence and adverse consequences of PM are dreadful in this area, which accounts the largest burden of malaria [ – , – ]. There are few studies that reported the prevalence of PM in Ethiopia such as 2.3% in Kuch HC [ ], 10.9% in Jawi District [ ], and 6.5% in Gambella General Hospital [ ]. However, these data are not adequate to show the true prevalence of PM in the country since none of these studies used the histopathology technique, which is the gold standard [ , , ].
Various factors determine the risk of GM and PM. Health interventions such as ANC service and vector control strategies play a pivotal role in the prevention and control of MiP [ , , , ]. Accordingly, the lack and/or poor utilization of anti-malarial interventions such as insecticide-treated bed nets (ITNs) [ , ] and IRS [ – ] have been reported to increase the risk of GM and PM. A lack of or poor adherence to ANC increases the risk of MiP by rendering pregnant women miss basic ANC service and anti-malarial interventions [ , , ]. Reports from studies conducted across various places in the world strengthened this notion [ , , , ]. A history of MiP or during the preconception period is an important factor contributing to the risk of PM. This underscores for the importance of active screening and treatment of all pregnant women in early gestation for fetomaternal health. A significant association between a history of malaria and the risk of PM was reported from studies conducted in Papua New Guinea [ ], Ethiopia [ ], Uganda [ ], and Colombia [ ]. Furthermore, given that acquired immunity to PM is attained through a higher frequency of pregnancy, obstetric factors, such as gravidity [ , , , ] and parity [ , ] determine the risk of malaria among pregnant and parturient women.
According to a recent systematic review and meta-analysis (SRMA), the global pooled prevalence of CM is 6.9% [ ]. However, a higher prevalence than this pooled estimate was reported from studies conducted in Colombia (12.4%) [ ], Sudan (29.7%) [ ], and Nigeria (16.2%) [ ]. The prevalence of CM in Ethiopia ranged from 0.5% [ ] to 3.7% [ ]. Furthermore, a 1.6% prevalence of CM was reported by Newman et al. [ ] from Gambella.
The impaired development, structure, and function of the placenta can lead to several adverse pregnancy outcomes. This compromises the capacity of the placenta to undertake its normal function, which contributes to an increased risk of IUGR, prematurity, and LBW [ , ]. Malaria in pregnancy is associated with the risk of maternal anaemia and LBW infants [ ]. Chronic PM, characterized by the accumulation of pigmented monocytes that release inflammatory cytokines, is associated with maternal anaemia through bone marrow suppression [ ]. A consistent finding was reported by Das et al. [ ] who recorded by 2.4 and 1.99 increased odds of anaemia and LBW, respectively among those with MiP than those with negative results. Microscopically detectable P. falciparum infection at ANC enrollment was associated with maternal anaemia at delivery [ ]. A study conducted in Blue Nile State, Sudan, reported a 68.6% prevalence of anaemia among parturient women [ ]. Peripheral blood microscopic P. falciparum infection at delivery was associated with LBW [ ]. Similarly, a 53.0% prevalence of LBW was found in another study conducted in Sudan [ ]. Furthermore, a recent evidence shows that MiP can affect the neurocognitive development of an infant, resulting in poor educational attainment [ ]. Taken together, these findings indicate that MiP, particularly PM, disrupts the haematological and nutritional parameters of the mother, leading to anaemia, which in turn renders the woman unable to meet the nutritional requirements of the developing fetus. Moreover, the inadequate supply of this scarce resource is worsened by the presence of PM, which compromises the structure and function of the placenta. Through these mechanisms, MiP results in a multitude of adverse pregnancy outcomes, including maternal anaemia and LBW [ , ].
To avert the deleterious outcome of MiP, appropriate prevention combined with early diagnosis and effective treatment of cases is fundamental [ ]. The diagnosis of Plasmodium infection during pregnancy, and parturiency can be done by RDT, microscopy, and PCR from peripheral blood. However, the diagnosis of PM is only possible after delivery by RDT, microscopy, and PCR, using placental blood, and by histopathology from placental biopsy [ , , ]. The effectiveness of any intervention to mitigate the burden and adverse outcomes of MiP depends upon a robust data on the problem and strong prevention and control activities. To this end, a comprehensive data on the prevalence and predictors of malaria at parturiency supplemented by maternal anaemia and LBW are central to show a wide-ranging picture of the problem, which in turn can guide intervention. However, there is a paucity of data on the prevalence and predictors of GM, PM, and CM together with their effects on the outcome of the pregnancy in Ethiopia, in general, and in the current study area, in particular. Moreover, the recently reported high prevalence of asymptomatic malaria in pregnancy (AMiP) in the current study area triggers to assess the status of PM, and its subsequent adverse pregnancy outcome [ ]. Hence, this study aimed to assess the prevalence and risk factors of GM, PM, and CM together with their effects on maternal anaemia and LBW newborn among parturient women attending the delivery ward of Metti HC in Majang Zone of Gambella Region, Southwest Ethiopia.

## Methods

## Study area and period
This study was conducted from November 2022 to March 2023 in Metti HC found in Metti Town. Metti Town, located 600 km southwest of Addis Ababa, is the capital of the Majang Zone in the Gambella Peoples’ National Regional State of Ethiopia. This zone is bordered to the south and east by the Southwest Ethiopia Peoples' Regional State, to the west by the Anywa Zone, and to the north by the Oromia Regional State of Ethiopia. The altitude of the zone ranges from 750 to 1800 m above sea level, with a mean annual temperature ranging from 17.6 °C to 27.5 °C and a mean annual rainfall ranging from 1400 to 1800 mm [ , ]. The major livelihood activity of the zone is agriculture, which mainly involves the production of grains and cash crops, such as coffee, as well as beekeeping [ , ]. The Majang Zone is among the malaria-endemic areas where P. falciparum and P. vivax are co-endemic. The Zone has one hospital, four health centres, and approximately 30 health posts [ , ]. Metti HC, with the largest patient flow, provides preventive and curative health services, including delivery services, to over 87,374 people in the Zone [ ].

## Study design and population
A facility-based cross-sectional study was conducted among parturient women attending the delivery ward of Metti HC.

## Sample size determination
The required sample size was calculated using the single population proportion formula as described below. After considering a 95% confidence level, 3% margin of error, and a 3.9% prevalence of PM, a finite population correction (annual number of parturient women attending the delivery ward of Metti HC: 1095), and a 10% non-response rate, the final sample size was calculated to be 154 [ ] (Eq. 1). 

## Sampling technique
Parturient women who came to the delivery ward of the Metti HC during the data collection period, consented to participate and met the inclusion criteria were consecutively included in the study until the final sample size was attained.

## Inclusion and exclusion criteria
All singleton parturient women who lived in the Zone for at least six months and gave birth at the delivery ward of the Metti HC during the study period were included. Whereas, parturient women with severe illness and serious birth complications were excluded from this study. In addition, those with twin pregnancies, those who took anti-malarial drugs within 14 days before the date of enrollment, and those referred to other health institutions were excluded.

## Study variables
The dependent variables assessed in this study were GM, PM, CM, maternal anaemia, and LBW. The independent variables were sociodemographic (such as age, residence, family size, marital, and educational status), obstetric (such as parity, and gravidity), anti-malarial intervention (such as ITN, and IRS), number of ANC contact, ABO blood group, and a history of malaria.

## Sociodemographic, obstetric, and anti-malarial intervention data collection
Data on the sociodemographic, obstetric, anti-malarial intervention, and ANC service utilization factors of the delivering women were collected using a pre-tested, semi-structured, and interviewer-administered questionnaire. The questionnaire was adapted from the Ethiopian Demographic Health Survey (EDHS 2016), the Malaria Indicator Survey (MIS-2015), and various related sources, and it was initially written in English, then translated into Amharic and finally translated back to English for consistency checkup. Two trained midwifery professionals conducted the interviews in Amharic, and recorded the response to every question on the questionnaire [ , ].

## Blood and biopsy specimen collection
Within one hour of delivery, capillary blood, placental blood, and placental biopsy specimens were collected from the parturient women at Metti HC. The fresh full placenta was placed in a small plastic bucket containing a clean and clear disposable plastic bag and was taken to the Metti HC laboratory department. Then, the samples were placed on a table covered with a biohazard bag for gross analysis and cleaned with 0.85% physiological saline. Following thorough cleaning of the maternal side of the placenta, placental blood was collected by aspirating it with a Pasteur pipette from the two lakes formed after the removal of placental biopsies with a surgical blade. Capillary blood (also referred to as peripheral blood in this paper) was collected by finger-prick from the parturient woman using a sterile lancet. In addition, cord blood was collected from the umbilical cord blood vessels using a syringe. Then, the capillary, placental, and cord blood specimens were used to perform malaria RDT, prepare thick and thin blood films, and prepare dried blood spots (DBSs) for the qPCR assay to diagnose malaria. The DBSs were prepared by spotting four independent drops of blood (50 µl each) on Whatman filter paper and left to air dry overnight. Then, each DBS was separately packed in a plastic bag containing desiccant and kept in a refrigerator at − 20 °C until it was transported to the molecular laboratory of Tropical and Infectious Diseases Research Centre (TIDRC) at Sokoru, Southwest Ethiopia, for the qPCR assay [ – ]. Besides, the haemoglobin (Hb) concentration and blood group of the woman were determined from the capillary blood (Fig.  ). 
Briefly, from a total of 180 parturient women, 180 placental biopsies were collected for histopathological analysis. Additionally, a triplet of blood samples (i.e. peripheral, placental and cord blood) were collected from each parturient woman to have 540 blood samples of the 180 women. These blood samples were used to make 540 DBSs, 540 blood films and 540 RDTs. Finally, all the BFs, and RDTs were analysed; however, due to logistic reason constraint, a randomly selected 180 DBSs (only a third of the 180 DBSs from each blood compartment, i.e. 60 peripheral, 60 placental, and 60 cord blood) were used for the PCR analysis.

## Laboratory analysis

## Malaria rapid diagnostic test from peripheral, placental, and cord blood
Malaria RDT was performed by Bioline™ Malaria Ag P.f/Pan RDT (catalog number: 05FK60, Abbott Diagnostics, Republic of Korea) following the manufacturer’s instructions. This RDT is a rapid, qualitative, and three-band differential lateral-flow immunochromatographic Ag detection cassette test that detects HRP2 Ag of P. falciparum and pLDH Ag of all human-infecting Plasmodium species in the blood based on the capture of a labelled-antibody-antigen complex forming a visible line on the result window of the cassette. The procedure involved addition of a 5 µl blood and four drops of assay buffer into sample and buffer well, respectively, followed by reading the result at 15 min [ , ].

## Malaria microscopy from peripheral, placental, and cord blood
For each participant, thick (6 µl) and thin (2 µl) blood films were prepared on two glass slides and air-dried. After the thin films were fixed in absolute methanol for 10–15 s, the films were stained with 3% Giemsa for 30–40 min and examined under ×1000 magnification using a light microscope by a trained medical laboratory technologist at Metti HC. The microscopic examination involved assessing the smear for the presence of the Plasmodium parasite, identifying the species, and determining its developmental stage and density. The density of the asexual and sexual stage Plasmodium parasite was determined by counting the parasite until 200 and 500 white blood cells (WBCs) are counted, respectively, and converting the total number into parasite/µl (p/µl) assuming 8000 WBCs/µl of blood. A blood film was declared negative if no parasites were detected after examination of 200 fields [ – ].

## Malaria qPCR assay from peripheral, placental, and cord blood
The molecular detection and identification of the Plasmodium species involved genomic deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA) extraction from DBSs by the Chelex-100 method and target amplification by qPCR in a molecular laboratory at the TIDRC of Jimma University, Sokoru. The extraction involved cutting and placing approximately 3 mm diameter DBS pieces into 1.5 ml Eppendorf tubes, adding 5% Chelex-100 alkaline solution, and then incubating overnight, followed by a series of centrifugation and thermal vortexing steps, resulting in the release of the parasite DNA and the transfer of 0.5 ml of the pure DNA into a new Eppendorf tube for storage at − 20 °C until the PCR assay. The amplification of the species-specific 18S rRNA gene in the extracted DNA was performed by qPCR using a QuantStudio™ 3 System instrument (Applied Biosystems). The process involved the use of pairs of primers and probes specifically designed for simultaneous detection and identification of both P. falciparum and P. vivax [ , ]. The PCR amplification was performed in a total volume of 12 µl of PCR mixture contained in a 96-well PCR plate. The mixture was prepared by adding 6 µl (× 2) of PerfeCTa master mix [PerfeCTa® qPCR ToughMix® Low ROX™, (Quantabio)], 0.5 µl (× 2) of each of the Pf-Fam and Pv-NED probes, 0.4 µl (× 4) of each of the forward and reverse primers, 2 µl of template DNA and 1.4 µl of PCR-grade water into each well. The thermal cycling conditions involved a hold stage at 50 °C for 2 min and initial denaturation at 95 °C for 2 min, followed by 45 cycles of amplification at 95 °C for 3 s and 60 °C for 30 s, resulting in a total run-time of 65 min. Plasmodium falciparum DNA (MR4 BEI) and PCR-grade water were used as positive and negative controls, respectively [ ].

## Diagnosis of placental malaria by histopathological assay from placental biopsy
The histopathological assay for diagnosing PM involved microscopic examination of a 5 µm thick section of a neutral-buffered formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (NBFFPE) placental biopsy smear stained with H&E stain by a pathologist and a haematologist [ , – ]. The overall process of histopathological diagnosis of PM involved collecting 2 cm3 tissue biopsy from two places at the middle of the distance between the umbilical cord insertion to the edge of the placenta on its maternal side → fixing it with 10% NBF → processing it (through fixing, dehydrating, clearing and impregnating) → embedding it in a paraffin wax → sectioning it into 5 µm slices with a microtome → attaching/smearing it onto a glass slide → staining it with H&E stain → microscopic examination for the presence of Plasmodium parasite iRBCs and/or haemozoin pigment and/or inflammatory cells and/or fibrin deposit [ – ].

## Placental tissue processing, embedding, and sectioning
Placental tissues were processed using a Leica TP1020 tissue processor (Leica Biosystems, Germany) at Jimma University Medical Centre Pathology Laboratory. Briefly, a group of 2 cm3 placental biopsies from each participant was independently placed into a labeled tissue cassette and fixed by placing them in 10% NBF for approximately five hours. Then, the biopsies were dehydrated to remove residual water by placing them in an ascending grade of alcohol (70%, 80%, 90% 100%, and 100%) for 1 h each. This alcohol solution was removed and cleared from the biopsies by placing them into two containers of xylene for 1 h each. Then, the biopsies were infiltrated by placing them into two containers of paraffin wax at 56 °C for 1 h each [ , – ].
The processed placental biopsies were embedded into a tissue block in paraffin wax by a Leica EG1150 Paraffin Embedding Station (Leica Biosystems, Germany). Each biopsy sample was removed from its cassette and placed on an appropriate tissue mold filled with a molten paraffin wax. Then, the cassette was replaced, and the mold carrying the cassette-tissue-wax was placed on a refrigerated surface for solidification for approximately 30 min. Finally, the cassette containing its tissue block was detached from the mold and transferred for sectioning [ , – ].
Placental tissue blocks were sectioned into small slices by using a Leica RM2235 (Leica Biosystems, Germany) rotary microtome. Briefly, the blocks were cleaned from excess wax and debris by clean forceps and gauze. The thickness of the microtome blade was adjusted to 20 µm, and the tissue block was placed on it to trim-out excess wax until the appropriate thickness was reached. Then, the microtome was adjusted to 5 µm to section the tissue into 5 µm thick ribbons. The resulting ribbons were floated onto a water bath to avoid the formation of wrinkles and to facilitate the attachment of the ribbons to the glass slide as a single layer. At least three floating ribbons from each placental tissue block were mounted onto two labeled-grease-free frosted-end glass slides. Excess wax from the tissue smear was removed by placing the smears in an incubator for approximately 30 min. Subsequently, the slides were air-dried and transferred for staining [ , – ].

## Staining the placental tissue by Haematoxylin and Eosin staining technique
The 5 µm sections of the placental biopsy were subjected to hematoxylin and eosin staining procedure that involved staining the nucleus and cytoplasm with contrasting colors to readily differentiate the cellular components of the tissue and the sequestered parasite. The basic principle underlying the H&E staining technique is the chemical reaction/attraction between the tissue and the dye. Haematoxylin, a basic dye, produces a blue‒purple contrast on acidic or nuclear components (primarily those containing nucleic acid moieties such as chromatin, ribosomes, and cytoplasmic regions rich in RNA). On the other hand, eosin, an acidic dye, counterstains basic or cytoplasmic components such as RBCs, cytoplasm, muscle, and collagen at varying intensities (pink, orange, and red). The H&E staining procedure used in this particular study involved dewaxing in xylene (for 5 min) → rehydration in descending concentrations of alcohol (for 4 min) → washing (for 1 min) → nuclear staining in haematoxylin (for 7 min) → differentiation (for 5 s in weak acid) → washing (for 1 min) → cytoplasmic staining in eosin (for 4 min) → dehydration in ascending concentrations of alcohol (for 4 min) → clearing in xylene (for 2 min) → mounting the cover glass with DPX [ , – ].

## Microscopic examination of the stained placental biopsy
When the H&E-stained placental biopsy film was completely air-dried and mounted with DPX, the staining quality and overall appearance of cells were grossly examined using 40× magnification (i.e., 4× objective and 10× eyepiece) under a light microscope. Then, the slides were examined under a microscope using 400× magnification (i.e., 40× objective and 10× eyepiece) by a pathologist and a haematologist while paying particular attention to the sequestered iRBCs in the intervillous spaces of the placenta. Besides, the examination involved searching for inflammatory cells, haemozoin, fibrin deposits, and syncytial knotting. Plasmodium iRBCs appear red/pink with black/brown circular dot at their centre in the intervillous space and/or inside macrophages and/or attached to the syncytiotrophoblast. Haemozoin appears dark brown/golden brown/black and can be focally present as coarse granular material or can be found in iRBCs/monocytes/macrophages/ trophoblasts/freely inside fibrin. Finally, the finding of the examination was reported as negative or positive. A brief description of result reporting is shown in the table below. [ , – ] (Table  ). 

## Diagnosis of low birth weight
The birthweight of the newborn was measured by putting a naked newborn on a digital weight scale that was calibrated to give the weight in grams to the nearest 100. The LBW was declared if the weight of a newborn was less than 2500 g [ , ].

## Determination of maternal haemoglobin concentration and the ABO blood group
The Hb concentration of the parturient women was determined using a HemoCue® Hb 301 Analyzer (HemoCue® Hb 301 Analyzer AB/Kuvettgatan, Ängelholm, Sweden). Approximately 10 µl of capillary blood was drawn into the microcuvette cavity by capillary action, followed by swift insertion of the microcuvette into the analyzer that delivers the Hb concentration in g/dl within 10–20 s. after measuring the absorbance [ , ]. Besides, the ABO blood grouping was performed by slide haemagglutination method using ForSure monoclonal IgM anti-sera (Setia Scientific Solution, Selangor, Malaysia). This blood grouping method involved observing the presence or absence of agglutination after mixing one drop of each anti-serum (anti-A, anti-B, and anti-D) with one drop (10 µl) whole blood added onto each anti-serum on the glass slide [ ].

## Data management and statistical analysis
Before data collection, training on how to collect blood specimens, perform the diagnostic tests, and complete the questionnaire was given by the research team to the data collectors. The questionnaire was pretested at Gambella General Hospital. The research team supervised the data collection process. All laboratory tests were performed strictly following standard operating procedures, job aids, and manufacturers’ instructions. Controls were run and passed before reporting the results. Two trained laboratory technologists independently examined the blood films. Whenever there were discrepant results in the microscopy readings, a third (senior laboratory technologist) examiner resolved it. The collected data were appropriately cleaned, coded, entered into Epidata version 3.1 and exported into Statistical Package for Social Science Version 26 Software for Windows (SPSS, Chicago, IL, USA). After checking for completeness and consistency, statistical analyses were performed. Descriptive statistics, chi-square tests, Fisher’s exact tests, and binary logistic regression analyses were conducted to summarize the data and identify the risk factors. In univariate binary logistic regression analysis, factors with p < 0.2 were considered candidates for multivariable binary logistic regression analysis, from which those with p < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. An AORs were obtained along with their corresponding 95% CIs. The findings were illustrated in text, tables, and graphs.

## Operational definitions

MiP: Detection of Plasmodium parasite in a pregnant or a parturient woman by a parasitological and/or, molecular and/or histopathological tests [ , , ].
GM: Detection of Plasmodium parasite in peripheral blood of a parturient woman by a parasitological and/or molecular tests [ ].
PM: Detection of Plasmodium parasite in placental blood by parasitological and/or molecular tests and/or Plasmodium parasite iRBCs and/or haemozoin pigments in monocytes and/or fibrin deposits in placental biopsies by histopathological tests [ – ].
CM: Detection of Plasmodium parasite in the umbilical cord blood by a parasitological and/or, molecular tests [ ][ – ].
Low parasitaemia: < 1000 asexual-stage parasites/µl [ , ].
Moderate parasitaemia: 1000–9999 asexual-stage parasites/µl [ , ].
High parasitaemia: ≥ 10,000 asexual-stage parasites/µl [ , ].
Maternal anaemia: [Hb] < 11.0 g/dl [ ].
Mild anaemia: [Hb] 10.0–10.9 g/dl [ ].
Moderate anaemia: [Hb] 7.0–9.9 g/dl [ ].
Severe anaemia: [Hb] < 7.0 g/dl [ ].
Low birth weight: The weight of a newborn less than 2500 g [ , ].

## Results

## Characteristics of the parturient women
One hundred eighty parturient women participated in this study. The median age of the participants was 26 years (ranged from 18 to 42). The majority of them attended formal education (75.6%), were housewives (65.6%), and were urban residents (59.4%). The median number of people living per household was three, with a minimum and maximum of one and nine, respectively. The majority (73.9%) of the participants had at least one ANC contact, with a median and maximum of two and eight, respectively. The majority of the participants owned at least one ITN (72.8%), but approximately 30.0% of the participants never utilized an ITN within the previous week and year. The axial body temperature of the parturient women ranged from 35.8 °C to 38.4 °C with a mean of 37.13 (± 0.46). The majority (63.4%) of them had asymptomatic malaria (Table  ). 

## Overall prevalence of malaria among parturient women
The overall prevalence of GM was 24.4% (95% CI 18.1–30.1), PM was 34.4% (95% CI 27.4–41.4), and CM was 5.0% (95% CI 2.4–8.8) at delivery ward of Metti HC in Majang Zone of Gambella Region. Approximately, 36.1% (65/180) of the parturient women have Plasmodium infection in at least one of their three compartments (peripheral, placental, and umbilical cord).

## Prevalence and risk factors of gestational malaria

## Prevalence of gestational malaria
The overall prevalence of GM among parturient women at Metti HC was 24.4% (44/180; 95% CI 18.1–30.1). The prevalence was 17.8% (32/180; 95% CI 12.1–23.4) by RDT, 21.2% (38/180; 95% CI 15.1–27.1) by light microscopy, and 28.3% (17/60; 95% CI 16.6–40.1) by qPCR (p < 0.001). Plasmodium falciparum and moderate parasitaemia accounted for 61.4% (27) and 50.0% (18) of the overall prevalence and density of the parasite, respectively. The asexual stage Plasmodium parasite density in peripheral blood ranged from 480 p/µl to 114,600 p/µl, with a geometric mean density of 6,790.2 p/µl (95% CI 4508.7–10,226.1). Moreover, the sexual stage Plasmodium parasite density in peripheral blood ranged from 160 p/µl to 12,634 p/µl, with a geometric mean density of 789.42 p/µl (95% CI 412.5–1510.5) (Fig.  ). 

## Risk factors of gestational malaria
Sociodemographic, obstetric, anti-malarial intervention and other variables were initially analyzed by univariate binary logistic regression to assess for a possible association with GM. Ten variables met the inclusion criteria (p < 0.2) for multivariable binary logistic regression analysis, in which only three variables were found to be statistically significantly associated with GM. These were having a history of malaria within the previous year [AOR: 5.10; 95% CI 1.64–15.83 (p: 0.005)], lack of IRS spraying within the previous year [AOR: 2.98; 95% CI 1.05–8.45 (p: 0.040)], and lack of ANC contact during the index pregnancy [AOR: 3.96; 95% CI 1.44–10.87 (p: 0.007)] (Table  ).

## Prevalence and risk factors of placental malaria

## Prevalence of placental malaria
The overall prevalence of PM at Metti HC was 34.4% (62/180; 95% CI 27.4–41.4). The prevalence rates were 16.1% (29/180; 95% CI 11.4–22.3) by RDT, 17.8% (32/180; 95% CI 12.8–24.1) by microscopy, 26.7% (16/60; 95% CI 16.7–39.6) by qPCR, and 32.8% (59/180; 95% CI 26.2–40.0) by histopathology (p < 0.001). Active infection (acute = 30, and chronic = 14) accounted for 74.5% of the PM detected by histopathology. Approximately 56.4% (35/62) of the PM was detected in both blood and biopsy specimens. Whereas, 38.7% (24) of the PM was detected in biopsy only. Plasmodium falciparum and moderate parasitaemia accounted for 65.8% (25) and 44.0% (14) of the overall prevalence and density of the parasite, respectively. The asexual stage Plasmodium parasite density in the placental blood ranged from 192 p/µl to 102,840 p/µl, with a geometric mean density of 5007.2 p/µl (95% CI 2927.5–8564.5). Besides, the sexual stage Plasmodium parasite density in the placental blood ranged from 800 p/µl to 14,800 p/µl, with a geometric mean density of 3882.8 p/µl (95% CI 369.4–4799.1) (Fig.  ). 

## Risk factors of placental malaria
Sociodemographic, obstetric, anti-malarial intervention and other variables were initially analyzed using univariate binary logistic regression for possible associations with PM. Ten variables met the inclusion criteria (p < 0.2) for multivariable binary logistic regression analysis, in which only two variables were found to be statistically significantly associated with PM. These were having a history of malaria within the previous year [AOR: 2.98; 95% CI 1.05–8.45 (p: 0.006)] and lack of ANC contact during the index pregnancy [AOR: 4.83; 95% CI 1.91–12.18 (p: 0.001)]. The peripheral blood parasite density strongly positively correlated with the placental blood parasite density (r: 0.91, p < 0.001) (Table  ). 

## Prevalence and risk factors of congenital malaria
The overall prevalence of CM at Metti HC was 5.0% (9/180; 95% CI 2.4–8.8). The prevalence was 2.8% (5/180; 95% CI 1.1–6.0) by RDT, 3.9% (7/180; 95% CI 1.7–7.4) by microscopy, and 5.0% (3/60; 95% CI 1.3–12.5) by qPCR (p < 0.001). Plasmodium falciparum and mild parasitaemia accounted for 88.9% and 57.1% of the overall prevalence and parasite density, respectively. The asexual stage Plasmodium parasite density in the umbilical cord blood ranged from 192 p/µl to 4684 p/µl, with a geometric mean density of 837.8 p/µl (95% CI 272.3–2577.1). There is no sexual stage of the Plasmodium parasite detected on the umbilical cord blood. The prevalence of CM was higher among parturient women with GM (20.5%) than among those negative for GM (0.0%; p < 0.001). Similarly, the prevalence of CM was higher among parturient women with PM (14.5%) than among those negative for PM (0.0%; p < 0.001) (Fig.  ). 

## Prevalence and risk factors of maternal anaemia

## Prevalence of maternal anaemia
The mean Hb concentration of the parturient women was 10.8 g/dl (± 2.17), with minimum and maximum values of 4.8 g/dl and 17.2 g/dl, respectively. The prevalence of anaemia among parturient women at Metti HC was 41.7% (75/180; 95% CI 34.6–49.0). About half (48.0%) of the anaemia cases were mild type (Fig.  ). 

## Risk factors of maternal anaemia
The Hb concentration decreased with an increase in the density of the asexual stage Plasmodium parasite in the peripheral (r = -0.741; p < 0.001), placental (r = − 0.792; p < 0.001) and cord (r = − 0.488; p: 0.267) blood reflecting the negative moderate linear correlation between the density of the parasite and the Hb concentration of the mother. However, the correlation between the density of the asexual stage Plasmodium parasite in the cord blood and the Hb concentration was not statistically significant (Fig.  ). 
The prevalence of maternal anaemia (92.6%) was higher (X2: 51.09; p < 0.001) among parturient women with GM caused by P. falciparum infection than among those with negative results (27.2%). Similarly, the prevalence of maternal anaemia (92.6%) was higher (p < 0.001) among parturient women with PM caused by P. falciparum infection than among those with negative results (27.7%). Similarly, the prevalence of maternal anaemia (100.0%) was higher (X2: 52.7; p < 0.001) among parturient women with CM caused by P. falciparum infection than among those with no CM (38.6%). Likewise, the risk of maternal anaemia increased in the presence of GM (X2: 47.8, p < 0.001) and PM (X2: 50.4, p < 0.001). The prevalence of anaemia among the malaria-positive parturient women was higher (56.9%) than among their counterparts (22.5%; p < 0.001). Over half of the malaria-positive parturient women were anaemic (Fig.  ). 

## Prevalence and risk factors of low birth weight newborns

## Prevalence of low birth weight newborn
The mean birth weight of the newborns was 2831.5 g (± 527), with a minimum and maximum of 2000 g and 4200 g, respectively. The overall prevalence of LBW among newborns was 27.8% (95% CI 21.6–34.6) (Fig.  ). 

## Risk factors of low birth weight newborn
The birth weight decreased with an increase in the density of the asexual stage Plasmodium parasite in the peripheral (r = − 0.724; p < 0.001), placental (r = − 0.711; p < 0.001) and cord (r = − 0.083; p: 0.859) blood reflecting the negative moderate linear correlation between the density of the parasite and the birth weight of the newborn. However, the correlation between the density of the asexual stage Plasmodium parasite in the cord blood and the birth weight was not statistically significant (Fig.  ). 
The prevalence of LBW was higher among parturient women with GM (65.9%; X2: 42.4; p < 0.001), PM (78.9%; X2: 62.8; p < 0.001), and CM (77.8%; X2: 11.8; p: 0.002) than among their corresponding counterparts (Fig.  ). 
The birth weight of the newborn increased with an increase in the concentration of the maternal Hb (r: 0.528, p < 0.001) reflecting the positive fairly linear correlation between them, which is statistically significant. The risk of LBW increased with maternal anaemia (X2: 42.2; p < 0.001), GM (X2: 42.2; p < 0.001), PM (X2: 58.1; p < 0.001), and CM (X2: 11.8; p: 0.002) (Fig.  ). 
In summary, 22.7% (41/180) of the parturient women had both GM, and PM. Likewise, five percent (9/180) of the parturient women had malaria in their peripheral, placental, and cord blood. About 3.8% (7) and 0.5% (1) parturient women have Plasmodium infection only in their peripheral and placental blood, respectively. All the Plasmodium infections found in the cord blood were from those with Plasmodium infection in their peripheral and placental blood. About 11.2% (20) of the parturient women have malaria only detected by histopathology. Moreover, approximately 3.8% (7/180) of the parturient women simultaneously had maternal anaemia, LBW newborns, and malaria in their peripheral, placental, and cord blood. Approximately, 22.7 (41/180) have anaemia and gave birth to LBW newborn (Fig.  ). 
Generally, the presence of Plasmodium infection in any sample has shown a moderately positive agreement with maternal anaemia (K: 0.503, p < 0.001) and LBW newborn (K: 0.558, p < 0.001). Similarly, the presence of Plasmodium infection in any blood sample has shown a moderately positive agreement with maternal anaemia (K: 0.510, p < 0.001) and LBW newborn (K: 0.524, p < 0.001) (Fig.  ). 

## Discussion
This study aimed to assess the prevalence and risk factors of GM, PM, and CM together with their effects on maternal anaemia and LBW newborns as adverse pregnancy outcome among parturient women attending the delivery ward of Metti HC in Majang Zone of Gambella Region, Southwest Ethiopia. This is the first report of a histopathological, haematological, parasitological, and molecular-based cross-sectional study that generated a comprehensive data on the prevalence and associated risk factors of GM, PM, and CM together with adverse pregnancy outcomes of MiP, such as maternal anaemia and LBW, in a high transmission area of Ethiopia. There is a high prevalence of GM, PM, maternal anaemia, and LBW in the current study area.
The overall prevalence of GM among parturient women at Metti HC was 24.4%. The prevalence was 17.8% by RDT, 21.2% by light microscopy, and 28.3% by qPCR. Plasmodium falciparum and moderate parasitaemia accounted for 61.4% and 50.0% of the overall prevalence and parasite density, respectively, in the GM. The prevalence found in the current study is consistent with the reports from those of studies conducted in Nigeria [ ] and the Republic of Congo Brazzaville [ ], where 30.7%, and 30.8% overall prevalence of GM, respectively, was found in the peripheral blood of parturient women at delivery. However, the prevalence of GM found in the current study area is higher than the prevalence reported from studies conducted in Mozambique [ ], Malawi [ ], and Central [ ] and Northwest [ ] Ethiopia. This discrepancy might be due to the use of qPCR, the inclusion of both symptomatic and asymptomatic as well as HIV-positive and HIV-negative parturient women in the current study unlike to those studies that did not use qPCR [ , ], excluded symptomatic [ , ], and HIV-positive [ ] parturient women. It is conceivable, from diagnostic parasitology point of view, that a higher prevalence of malaria can be found in a study that involved symptomatic participants and PCR assay, which has good detectability and sensitivity, respectively, than their respective counterparts [ , , ]. Besides, HIV increases the risk of malaria and its detectability by impairing immune integrity, which predisposes pregnant women to the risk of Plasmodium infection with high parasitaemia [ , , ]. On the other hand, the prevalence of GM in the current study area is lower than the reported prevalence of 35.8% from Colombia [ ] and 59.2% from Nigeria [ ]. The involvement of a larger sample size [ , ], longer study period, and more than one health facility [ ] might have contributed to the higher prevalence found in those studies than in this study.
In the current study, having a history of malaria within the previous year, a lack of IRS spraying within the previous year, and a lack of ANC contact during the index pregnancy were found to be significantly associated with the increased odds of GM. In this study, parturient women with a history of malaria within the previous year were five times more likely to have GM compared to their corresponding counterparts. This finding is in line with similar reports from previous studies performed in Papua New Guinea [ ], Colombia [ ], and Ethiopia [ , ]. In these areas, having a history of malaria within the previous year was reported to increase the odds of GM among parturient women. Parasitologically, it is conceivable that having a history of malaria could increase the risk of GM, particularly in areas where P. falciparum and P. vivax are co-endemic through recurrence from either species or both [ , ]. In this study, parturient women who lacked ANC contact during index pregnancy were approximately four times more likely to have GM compared to their respective counterparts. Similar findings were reported from studies conducted in Ethiopia [ ], Ghana [ ], and Sudan [ ]. Actually, it is epidemiologically and immunologically conceivable that pregnant women with better ANC contact could receive health care services such as advice, iron, intermittent preventive treatment in pregnancy (IPTp), and other anti-malarial interventions that collectively help prevent malaria and improve pregnancy health [ , , ]. Furthermore, the odds of having GM was approximately three times among those whose home was not spayed with an IRS within the previous year. This findings is concordant with previous reports by Gemechu et al. [ ], and Tilahun et al. [ ], and Alemayehu et al. [ ]. Similar to the current finding, these authors reported the higher odds of GM malaria among those whose house was not IRS sprayed within the previous year compared to their counterparts. In fact, this finding is entomologically plausible since spraying the home wall with IRS reduces the risk of malaria by killing or repelling the mosquito that transmits malaria [ ].
The overall prevalence of PM at Metti HC was 34.4%. The prevalence was 16.1% by RDT, 17.8% by microscopy, 26.7% by qPCR, and 32.8% by histopathology. Active infection accounted for 74.5% of the PM detected by histopathology. Above half (56.5%) of the PM was detected from both blood and biopsy specimens. Plasmodium falciparum and moderate parasitaemia accounted for 65.8% and 44.0% of the overall prevalence and parasite density, respectively. These findings are consistent with the findings of those of studies conducted in Ghana [ ], Sudan [ ], and Nigeria [ ], from where a 37.4%, 34.5%, and 33.9% prevalence of PM, respectively, was detected. However, the overall prevalence of PM found in the current study area is higher than the reported prevalence of 12.4% in five vivax malaria-endemic countries [ ], 20.9% in Colombia [ ], 19.0% in Nigeria [ ], 14.2% in Mozambique [ ], 7.0% in Ghana [ ], and 2.3% to 10.9% in Ethiopia [ , , , ]. The observed higher prevalence in the current study setting might be explained by the use of multiple diagnostic tools, particularly histopathological and PCR assays, the co-endemicity of P. falciparum and P. vivax in the current study area, and the inclusion of both symptomatic and asymptomatic as well as HIV-positive and HIV-negative parturient women. Unlike to the current study, studies those did not use histopathological assays [ , , , , , , , ], and qPCR assays [ , , , ] reported a lower prevalence. The use of diagnostic tools with relatively very good sensitivity might have allowed the current study to detect higher prevalence of PM. The use of PCR and histopathological assays for malaria diagnosis from placental blood and biopsy, respectively, in a study improves the detection of PM compared to RDT and microscopy, thereby increasing the overall prevalence of PM in a particular study area [ , , ]. Furthermore, the inclusion of HIV-infected pregnant women living in P. falciparum-dominated areas (both infections known to increase the risk of PM, and transplacental transmission of one another) might explain the higher prevalence of PM than those studies conducted in P. vivax-endemic areas [ ], and those studies excluded HIV-positive [ , ], and malaria-symptomatic [ , ] parturient women. On the other hand, the prevalence of PM found from the current study area is lower than the reported prevalence of 86.8% from Burkina Faso [ ], 59.3% from Sudan [ ], 44.8% from Nigeria [ ], and 44.3% from Uganda [ ]. The involvement of a larger sample size [ , ], longer study period [ , ], and limiting the study to parturient women with Plasmodium [ , ] and HIV [ ] infected only might have contributed to the higher prevalence recorded in those studies than the current study. It is plausible that infection with HIV and Plasmodium, particularly P. falciparum, increases the risk of PM by impairing immune integrity and adhering to chondroitin sulfate A receptor, respectively [ , , ].
In the current study, having a history of malaria within the previous year and a lack of ANC contact during the index pregnancy were found to be significantly associated with increased odds of PM. As the present study results show, the odds of having PM was 5.1 among parturient women with a history of malaria within a year than those with no history. This finding was substantiated by consistent reports from studies done in Papua New Guinea [ ], Ethiopia [ , ], and Uganda [ ]. Given the ineligibility of pregnant women to radical-cure anti-malarial drugs such as PQ, it is physiologically and epidemiologically plausible that participants with a history of malaria within the previous year living in P. falciparum and P. vivax co-endemic areas could have a higher risk of recurrence, such as reinfection and relapse [ ]. This finding underscores the importance of active screening and treatment of pregnant women during their early gestation for the wellbeing of both the mother and her fetus [ , ]. Furthermore, a lack of ANC contact during the index pregnancy was found to increase the odds of having PM by approximately fivefold. This finding is consistent with the findings of similar studies conducted in Sudan [ ], Northwest Ethiopia [ ], and Ghana [ ]. In fact, it is epidemiologically and immunologically conceivable that strengthening ANC contact, especially starting early in the first trimester, can reduce the risk of PM by providing an opportunity for malaria prevention through optimized availability and utilization of interventions such as ITN and malaria control through early detection and prompt treatment of cases [ , ]. Moreover, pregnant and parturient women with a higher frequency of ANC contact could have received ANC service, anti-malarial interventions, and other healthcare services critical to improving pregnancy health and reducing the risk of Plasmodium infection [ , ].
The overall prevalence of CM at Metti HC was 5.0%. The prevalence was 2.8% by RDT, 3.9% by microscopy, and 5.0% by qPCR. Plasmodium falciparum and mild parasitaemia accounted for 88.9% and 57.1% of the overall prevalence and parasite density, respectively. This finding is concordant reported prevalence of 3.7% in Ethiopia [ ] and 6.9% pooled prevalence in the world [ ]. Nevertheless, the prevalence of CM recorded in the current study area is higher than the prevalence of 1.5% and 0.5% reported by Newman et al. from Gambella General Hospital [ ] and Limenih et al. from Kuch HC [ ], respectively. The use of qPCR in the current study might explain this discrepancy. On the other hand, the prevalence found in the current study is lower than the reported prevalence of 29.7% from Sudan [ ], 16.9% from Nigeria [ ], and 12.4% from Colombia [ ]. The involvement of a larger sample size and longer study period in those studies might explain the observed disparity [ , ]. The prevalence of CM was higher among parturient women with GM (20.5%) than among those negative for GM (0.0%) (p < 0.001). Similarly, the prevalence of CM was higher among parturient women with PM (14.5%) than among those negative for PM (0.0%) (p < 0.001). This indicates the presence of an important association between GM, and PM with the risk of CM. Similar finding was reported from a study conducted in Malawi [ ].
The prevalence of anaemia among parturient women at Metti HC was 41.7%. Approximately half (48.0%) of the anaemia prevalence was mild type. However, this prevalence was lower than the 68.6% prevalence reported in a study conducted in the Blue Nile State of Sudan. The higher prevalence of malaria in that study than in this study might have contributed to the higher prevalence of maternal anaemia [ ]. The risk of maternal anaemia increased with the presence of GM (p < 0.001) and PM (p < 0.001). The prevalence of anaemia among malaria-positive parturient women was greater (56.9%) than that was found among their counterparts (22.5%; p < 0.001). Over half of the malaria-positive parturient women were anaemic. The prevalence of maternal anaemia was higher (p < 0.001) among parturient women infected with gestational falciparum malaria (92.6%) than among those with negative results (27.2%). Similarly, the prevalence of maternal anaemia was higher (p < 0.001) among parturient women with placental P. falciparum infection (92.6%) than among those with negative results (27.7%). This finding is in agreement with the report by Das et al. [ ] from a SRMA in which the odds of having maternal anaemia was 2.4 times higher in pregnant women with malaria. Furthermore, a comprehensive review of MiP underpinned this finding by revealing its association with the risk of maternal anaemia [ ]. In fact, it is haematologically plausible that malaria increases the risk of anaemia by suppression of erythropoiesis and/or increased haemolysis [ , , ].
The overall prevalence of LBW newborns was 27.8%. However, this prevalence is lower than the 53.0% prevalence reported from a study conducted in the Blue Nile State of Sudan. The higher prevalence of malaria in the Sudanese study might explain the observed discrepancy [ ]. The prevalence of LBW was higher among parturient women with GM (65.9%; p < 0.001), PM (78.9%; p < 0.001), and CM (77.8%; p: 0.002) than among their corresponding counterparts. The risk of LBW increased in the presence of maternal anaemia (p < 0.001), GM (p < 0.001), PM (p < 0.001), and CM (p: 0.002). Consistent with findings in this study, peripheral malaria, particularly falciparum malaria, positivity at delivery was significantly associated with the risk of LBW in a study conducted in Papua New Guinea [ ], and India [ ]. Furthermore, according to the report of Limenih et al. [ ], GM, and PM at delivery were statistically significantly associated with LBW. According to the SRMA report by Das et al. [ ], pregnant women with malaria have approximately twofold increased odds of having LBW infants. A supportive conclusion was drawn from a comprehensive review that reported the association of MiP with the risk of LBW [ ]. By compromising the development, structure, and function of the placenta, it is reasonable that MiP, particularly the PM, impairs the normal delivery of nutrients and oxygen to the fetus rendering sub-optimal development that eventually results in the LBW newborn [ , , ].
In summary, 22.7% of the parturient women had both GM and PM. Similarly, five percent of the parturient women had GM, PM, and CM at a time. Moreover, approximately 3.8% of the parturient women simultaneously had GM, PM, CM, LBW, and maternal anaemia. In addition to this, the presence of Plasmodium infection in any sample was moderately positively concordant with the presence of maternal anaemia (K: 0.503, p < 0.001) and LBW newborn (K: 0.558, p < 0.001). Similarly, the presence of Plasmodium infection in any blood sample was fairly positively concordant with maternal anaemia (K: 0.510, p < 0.001) and LBW newborn (K: 0.524, p < 0.001). These findings indicate the underlying relationships among each other, and the possible negative impact of MiP on maternal Hb concentration and birth weight of the newborn. These results contribute to the understanding of the dynamics of Plasmodium parasites in blood sample types during pregnancy and their impact on adverse pregnancy outcomes, thereby informing public health experts about the epidemiology of malaria among this high-risk, but neglected segment of the general population. Furthermore, the fact that majority of the parturient women with PM were asymptomatic, and more than 5.0% of the peripheral and placental malaria was sub-microscopic, it is conceivable that these population might serve as a potential human infectious reservoir of transmission [ , , ].

## Strengths and limitations of the current study
The strength of this study includes the use of at least three diagnostic tools for the detection of malaria in a matched peripheral, placental, and cord blood samples; the use of histopathological assay for PM detection; and the comprehensiveness of the overall data it generated. The diagnosis of malaria from the three blood compartments and biopsy by using many diagnostic tools improves the detection of malaria. Besides, the maternal anaemia and LBW data shows the adverse outcome of MiP on the mother and the newborn, respectively. Generally, such a comprehensive data on the burden, adverse outcome, and associated risk factors of malaria among parturient women could assist in designing a targeted intervention to mitigate malaria. On the other hand, due to the cross-sectional nature of the study, it was difficult to follow the participants from pregnancy until delivery to establish cause and effect relationship, as well as assess the prevalence variation across different transmission seasons.

## Conclusion and recommendation
Malaria among parturient women and its adverse pregnancy outcome remained a formidable challenge in the Majang Zone. There is a high prevalence of GM, PM, maternal anaemia, and LBW in the current study area. Having a history of malaria within the previous year, a lack of IRS utilization within the previous year, and a lack of ANC contact during the index pregnancy were risk factors associated with malaria among parturient women. The risk of maternal anaemia, CM, and LBW increased in the presence of GM and PM.
Strengthening ANC contact could help lower the risk of GM and PM together with their adverse outcomes. Malaria during pregnancy prevention interventions, such as IRS and ITN, should be strengthened to reduce the risk of MiP and hence the ensuing GM and PM at parturiency. Targeting the identified associated risk factors could assist programme managers and other stakeholders in designing a targeted intervention together with efficient implementation strategies mitigate MiP and its deleterious outcomes, such as maternal anaemia and LBW. Future studies involving larger sample size with a longitudinal study design by following from early in the pregnancy up to delivery should be done to further reveal the adverse outcome of MiP.
