## Significance
We report an advance in antibody discovery, by identifying naturally acquired broadly inhibitory human monoclonal antibodies against Plasmodium falciparum malaria parasites directly from plasma using mass spectrometry. This represents a significant technical advance in profiling antibodies from blood and has the potential to transform how we study naturally acquired immunity. The antibodies were isolated from a 9-y-old Tanzanian child with prior malaria exposure and, like previously described broadly inhibitory antibodies, target a limited set of conserved P. falciparum erythrocyte membrane protein 1 (PfEMP1) residues essential for receptor binding, although through a distinct binding mode. By enabling direct analysis of circulating antibodies, this approach provides a powerful strategy for uncovering naturally acquired immunity and guiding next-generation malaria vaccine design.

Plasmodium falciparum pathology is driven by the accumulation of parasite-infected erythrocytes in blood capillaries. This sequestration process is mediated by the parasite’s P. falciparum erythrocyte membrane protein 1 (PfEMP1) adhesins, which bind select endothelial cell receptors. A subset of PfEMP1 binding human endothelial protein C receptor (EPCR) through their cysteine-rich interdomain region alpha 1 (CIDRα1) domains drives the pathogenesis to severe malaria. Despite high sequence diversity among CIDRα1 domains, individuals living in malaria-endemic regions become immune to severe disease in part through acquisition of antibodies inhibiting the PfEMP1–EPCR interaction. Here, we demonstrate an approach to identify pathogen-specific human monoclonal antibodies from plasma, combining mass spectrometry analysis of antigen-purified polyclonal plasma IgG and Ig transcript sequencing. We identified a clonal family of broadly reactive and EPCR binding-inhibitory human monoclonal antibodies against CIDRα1. The antibodies, isolated from a 9-y-old child, exhibited potent inhibition of EPCR binding broadly across CIDRα1 domains as well as binding of infected erythrocytes to EPCR. Structural analysis of one antibody variant complexed with CIDRα1 revealed a shared epitope of the clonal antibody family overlapping the EPCR binding site and the epitopes of two previously identified monoclonal antibodies, C7 and C74, with similar functional patterns. However, although C7, C74, and 110-3 antibodies all depend on the same few residues conserved in CIDRα1 to retain EPCR binding, the 110-3 antibodies contact additional variable residues, reducing their breadth of reactivity across the CIDRα1 family. These data bolster the hypothesis that broadly inhibitory antibodies against severe malaria-associated PfEMP1 target similar epitopes and are commonly developed in malaria-exposed individuals.

Each year, malaria claims ~600,000 lives, predominantly among young children ( ). Malaria pathology is driven by the sequestration of P. falciparum–infected erythrocytes in the microvasculature of vital organs, leading to reduced blood flow, inflammation, and tissue damage. In severe cases, this process can lead to organ failure and death ( ). Sequestration is mediated by P. falciparum erythrocyte membrane protein 1 (PfEMP1) expressed on the surface of infected erythrocytes ( ). PfEMP1 enables the parasite to evade splenic clearance by adhering to endothelial receptors in the microvasculature. Among the diverse PfEMP1 variants, those containing cysteine-rich interdomain region alpha 1 (CIDRα1) domains bind the endothelial protein C receptor (EPCR) ( , ) and severe malaria in children and adults across Africa and Asia is associated with parasites relying on EPCR-binding PfEMP1 ( ). Immunity to severe malaria develops with exposure and is associated with CIDRα1-specific antibodies that block EPCR-mediated sequestration ( ). CIDRα1 domains have diversified under immune selection but retain their overall fold and EPCR-binding site to ensure high-affinity interaction ( , ). The first broadly EPCR binding-inhibitory monoclonal antibodies (bIAbs) against CIDRα1 were recently identified ( ). Two distinct bIAbs from malaria-immune adults were shown to bind most variants from five of six CIDRα1 subclasses (CIDRα1.4 to 8, but not CIDRα1.1) through a similar mechanism relying on interactions with a few conserved residues in the EPCR-binding site of CIDRα1. Serological studies suggest that such antibodies are likely to be common and arise early in life among people in malaria-endemic areas ( ). Indeed, we previously isolated IgG pools with broad binding-inhibitory activity from Tanzanian children’s plasma using affinity purification with a peptide spanning the EPCR-binding site of a single CIDRα1 variant ( ).
Previous approaches to identify human monoclonal antibodies have largely relied on isolation of memory B cells or plasmablasts, limiting direct analysis of the circulating antibody repertoire. In this study, we applied a methodology that enables the identification of immunoglobulins directly from plasma through integrated mass spectrometry (MS) and transcriptomic analysis ( ). Using this strategy, we identified single species of circulating bIAbs in the plasma of a Tanzanian child. Cryogenic electron microscopy (Cryo-EM) structural analyses show that these bIAbs bind at the CIDRα1s EPCR-binding site via a mechanism related to, but distinct from, previously reported bIAbs.

## Results

## Sample Selection.
To select samples for the purification and analysis of antigen-specific plasma antibodies, we assessed IgG reactivity to a panel of CIDRα1 domains in plasma from eight 8- to 18-y-old Tanzanian individuals living under low to moderate malaria transmission ( ). These donors were previously found to possess antibodies against three distinct CIDRα1 domains when sampled 16 mo earlier ( ). From this group, we selected the two plasma samples exhibiting the broadest and highest reactivity across the panel ( ). This peptide had previously been used for the affinity purification of IgG pools containing broadly inhibitory antibodies (bIAbs) ( ). The two selected samples, 110 and 136, obtained from 9- and 18-y-old individuals, respectively, were used to purify IgG with affinity for a 63-mer peptide spanning the EPCR-binding (EB) and EPCR-binding supporting (EBS) helices of the HB3VAR03 CIDRα1.4 domain. Enrichment of IgG reactivity and EPCR-binding neutralization broadly across CIDRα1.4-8 variants was confirmed ( ), indicative of the presence of bIAbs. The affinity-purified IgG samples were then analyzed by de novo MS sequencing, which combined with Ig-sequencing data obtained on the matching B cell samples, was used to identify putative monoclonal IgG specimens ( ) ( ).
 

## Antibody Screening.
The analysis of the affinity-purified IgG indicated particularly strong enrichment of peptides matching Ig-sequences representing three and seven distinct complementarity-determining region 3 (CDR3 regions) in sample 110 and 136, respectively. One of these, 110-3, represented a cluster of eight IGHV variants with high sequence conservation within their CDR3 region ( ). The three different heavy chain sequences from sample 110 were paired with three light chains sequences (two lambda and one kappa chain) and the seven different heavy chains from 136 were paired with six light chains (two lambda and four kappa chains), based on peptide coverage and most likely pairing, to produce three 110-derived and nine 136-derived monoclonal IgG antibodies. Only one mAb, derived from on one 110-3 subvariant (mAb 110-3.4), exhibited high reactivity across most (13 of 19) CIDRα1.4-8 variants and no reactivity to CIDRα1.1 variants.
 An additional 34 mAbs derived from 110 and 31 from 136 were generated by repairing nonreactive heavy and light chains with four additional heavy and four additional light chain sequences from 110, identified with lower peptide coverage. None of these alternative pairings yielded antigen-reactive mAbs.

## Functional Characterization of 110-3 Antibodies.
Sequence analysis ( ) of the eight 110-3 heavy chain variants revealed a shared V gene origin and sporadic variation, predominantly in the CDR2 and FR3 regions, suggesting they represent somatic variants of a clonally expanded IgG clonotype with similar antigen specificity. To assess this, recombinant IgG was produced for each 110-3 heavy chain sequence variant using the same 110-3 light chain. These mAbs exhibited similar broad reactivity across CIDRα1.4-8 variants (13 of 19 variants; ). Assessment of the EPCR-binding neutralization showed a similar broad activity by all eight 110-3 mAbs, mirroring their reactivity pattern. Strong inhibition (>50%) was observed for 13 out of 19 CIDRα1 variants, representing CIDRα1.4 to 8 subtypes but not CIDRα1.1. All 110-3 mAbs bound and showed blocking of EPCR binding by recombinant multidomain and full-length ectodomain PfEMP1 proteins, albeit less efficiently than the previously described bIAbs C74 and C7 ( ) (SI Appendix, Fig. S2A). Binding affinities to a broad panel of CIDRα1 domains were determined using biolayer interferometry (BLI) for mAbs 110-3.4 and 110-3.6, which represent an Alanine to Proline dichotomy in the 110-3 CDR3 sequences ( and SI Appendix, Fig. S3B). The two mAbs showed similar kinetics across the antigen panel characterized by a similar variation in on-rates and very low off rates across reactive antigen, preventing accurate KD determination to the single CIDRα1 domains due to the exceptionally slow dissociation. To determine the potency of neutralization, titration of EPCR binding neutralization was performed for mAbs 110-3.4 and 110-3.6 ( and SI Appendix, Fig. S2C). This confirmed broad albeit varying neutralization across the bound CIDRα1.4 to 8 variants with a potency (geometric mean IC50 for inhibited CIDRα1 variants) of 0.79 μg/mL (±3.2-fold) and 0.34 μg/mL (±11.0-fold) for 110-3.4 and 110-3.6, respectively. For comparison, the potency of the previously published bIAbs, C7, and C74 was 0.1 µg/mL and 0.38 µg/mL. Finally, we verified mAb 110-3.4’s ability to inhibit EPCR binding by parasite-infected erythrocytes expressing the CIDRα1.4-containing HB3VAR03 and CIDRα1.6-containing IT4VAR18 PfEMP1 ( ). These data establish that the 110-3 antibody variants constitute a group of functionally similar bIAbs.

## Structural Characterization of 110-3 Binding to CIDRα1.
To understand the molecular basis of 110-3 antibodies’ binding to CIDRα1, the structure of 110-3.4 in complex with the three-domain IT4VAR22 PfEMP1 protein (CIDRα1.7) was determined by cryo-EM ( ). Focused refinement was performed on the Fab fragment of 110-3.4 and the CIDRα1.7 domain of IT4VAR22, achieving a resolution of 3.4 Å based on the GS-FSC at 0.143 (SI Appendix, Fig. S1). This showed that the Fab binds to the EPCR-binding and supporting helices (EB and EBS helices) of CIDRα1 ( ), overlapping the position of EPCR when bound to the CIDRα1 domain ( ). The DBLα and DBLβ domains flanking CIDRα1.7 were unresolved due to flexibility. Thus, as observed for EPCR and mAbs C7 and C74, the 110-3.4 mAb displaces DBLα from its interaction with CIDRα1 and induces an open conformation of the PfEMP1 N-terminal domain complex ( , ).
 At the core of the CIDRα1–EPCR interaction lies a hydrophobic patch centered on a conserved double-phenylalanine motif (F661 and F662 in CIDRα1.7 from IT4VAR22) located within the EB helix. The second phenylalanine in this motif inserts directly into a hydrophobic pocket on EPCR. The 110-3.4 heavy chain complementarity-determining regions H-CDR3 and H-CDR1 loops form a similar CIDRα1-complementary hydrophobic crevice to interact with the FF motif region ( and SI Appendix, Fig. S3A). This is reminiscent of the binding mode of the C7 and C74 mAbs, which engage the CIDRα1 FF motif through π-stacking interactions by aromatic residues placed similarly in the C7 and C74 paratopes ( ). However, in contrast to C7 and C74, the 110-3.4 mAb binds the FF motif without relying on aromatic residues. Like C7 and C74, the 110-3.4 mAb interacts with the EBS helix N-terminal glutamic acid, conserved among CIDRα1.4-8 variants (E671 in IT4VAR22 CIDRα1.7) ( ). While both C7 and C74 interact with the glutamic acid through a pair of serine and tyrosine residues in their H-CDRs, the 110-3-4 mAb likely forms hydrogen bond interactions through residues of both the heavy and light chain CDRs (H-CDR3 S100b; L-CDR R50 & N51).
The 110-3 mAbs divide into two groups having either an asparagine-X-threonine (NXT) motif or an N-proline-T (NPT) sequence within their heavy chain CDR3 (H-CDR3) ( ). An NXT/S motif, -particularly when found in loop or turn conformations, as observed in the H-CDR3 of 110-3.4 is a consensus sequence for N-linked glycosylation, with the exception that X cannot be a proline ( ). The cryo-EM data confirm that N97 in the H-CDR3 of 110-3.4 is glycosylated (SI Appendix, Fig. S3B), with the glycan oriented away from the CIDRα1-binding interface. Substitution of the glycosylated NAT motif in mAbs 110-3.1 to 110-3.4 with the predicted nonglycosylated NPT motif in mAbs 110-3.5 to 110-3.8 does not result in significant differences in affinity or binding breadth ( ). Thus, it is unclear whether the two 110-3 CDR3 sequence types arose by chance, with mutations agnostic to binding, or whether they reflect a selective pressure optimizing stability or antigen binding.
These interactions likely underlie the specificity of 110-3 mAbs for CIDRα1.4 to 1.8 variants. Similar to C7 and C74, the 110-3 antibodies do not bind CIDRα1.1 variants, which primarily differ from CIDRα1.4 to 1.8 in key residues of the EB helix (the FF motif) and EBS helix (the N-terminal glutamic acid). Mutating either the double phenylalanine motif or the glutamic acid residue to alanine in CIDRα1.4, CIDRα1.5, or CIDRα1.8 abrogated binding by both 110-3.4 and 110-3.6 ( ). Conversely, introducing a double phenylalanine motif in the EB helix and a glutamic acid residue in the EBS helix into a CIDRα1.1 domain (IT4VAR20) conferred reactivity with 110-3.4 and 110-3.6.
Like C74—but not C7—110-3.4 interacts with residue E581 (in IT4VAR22 CIDRα1.7) via H-CDR1 residue R30 (salt bridge) ( ). This glutamic acid is highly conserved across CIDRα1.4 to 1.8 variants and contributes to the domain’s secondary EPCR-binding site. However, unlike C7 and C74, 110-3.4 forms additional contacts with the N-terminal region of the EBS helix, specifically through hydrogen bonds between CIDRα1.7 N669 and N675 and 110-3.4 H-CDR3 V100 and L-CDR N30. These interactions, along with the heavy and light chain orientations flipped and a more tilted angle of approach toward the EBS helix at 69° angular difference compared to C74 (SI Appendix, Fig. S3C), appear to distinguish the binding mode of 110-3.4 from that of C74. Sequence conservation analysis of CIDRα1 variants, either recognized or not recognized by 110-3.4 and C74, further supports, that interactions with the N-terminal region of the EBS helix—particularly at residue N675—contribute to the narrower binding breadth of 110-3.4 relative to C74 ( ). Overall, these findings suggest a shared molecular basis of the 110-3 mAbs binding to CIDRα1, with their binding breadth limited by a dependence on interactions with variable residues in the EBS helix of CIDRα1.

## Discussion
The recent identification of two broadly inhibitory monoclonal antibodies targeting the EPCR-binding site of severe malaria-associated PfEMP1, along with serological evidence suggesting that similar antibodies are abundant and common among malaria-exposed individuals ( ), prompted us to explore whether we could identify such single antibody specimens circulating in plasma. Following a recently developed protocol for combined MS and Ig-sequencing analysis of antigen-enriched IgG and bulk B cell sequencing ( ), we identified one clonal bIAb family from an initial screening of 3 and 9 putative mAbs derived from two different samples, respectively. Expanding the screen to include an additional 65 putative CIDRα1-specific mAbs did not result in the identification of more reactive mAbs.
Multiple technical factors, including sample volume, immunization history, antigen size, and the efficiency of antigen-specific IgG purification, may influence the success of antibody discovery through the MS and Ig-sequencing protocol. A limitation of this approach is the lack of definitive pairing of heavy and light chains. While the approach has improved accuracy compared to random pairing, it lacks the definitive precision offered by single B cell sequencing. In our study, we identified eight heavy-chain variants of the 110-3 antibodies sharing a common light chain. It is unlikely that all variants used the exact same light chain; however, we found no clonal variants to test. Given the high affinity and specific antigen interactions involving the light chain, it is likely that the identified pairing was correct or closely related for at least one antibody variant.
Compared to other antibody discovery methods, our approach offers distinct advantages. B cell sequencing captures large, often undifferentiated repertoires and may not reflect circulating, functionally matured IgG. Importantly, most circulating antibodies, the key effectors of humoral immunity, are secreted by long-lived plasma cells in the bone marrow, not by circulating B cells, which represent only ~2% of the total B cell population. This can lead to a disconnect between B cell–derived mAbs and the dominant plasma antibody responses. While phage display enables high-throughput mAb generation, it often relies on artificial chain pairing and selection conditions that may not recapitulate the physiological antibody repertoire. In contrast, our approach directly interrogates the final, affinity-matured product of the humoral response, the circulating IgG, providing a more direct window into functionally relevant humoral immunity.
In a prior study applying this method to COVID-19 vaccine responses ( ), ~190 μg of SARS-CoV-2 spike protein receptor-binding domain (RBD)-enriched IgG was extracted from ~22 mg of total IgG from a 49-y-old female, 60 d after her third vaccination with the Moderna Spikevax COVID-19 vaccine. This led to the identification of three unique RBD-binding and inhibitory mAbs. In the present study, the 110-3 antibodies were identified from ~120 μg of antigen-affinity-purified IgG extracted from 12 mg of total IgG from a 9-y-old naturally immunized child. While we failed to identify functional mAbs from the second donor with similar IgG input, this demonstrates the method’s applicability to antibody discovery from naturally infected individuals. Thus, the MS-based approach used here offers an alternative to B cell–based methods for characterizing functional antibody responses in infectious diseases, particularly useful in sample-limited settings, studies using paired archived plasma and PBMCs compatible with Ig sequencing, and sample collection situations where advanced cell processing is challenging.
Structural and sequence analysis provided insights into a shared binding mechanism of the 110-3 bIAbs, and their narrower coverage of the CIDRα1 protein family compared to the C7 and C74 mAbs. Despite dichotomizing their H-CDR3 being glycosylated or not, key interacting residues and overall paratope structure were conserved among 110-3 variants. Like C74, the 110-3 antibodies bind CIDRα1.4-8 but not CIDRα1.1 variants through a structurally complementary hydrophobic canopy spanning the central FF motif of the CIDRα1 EPCR-binding site. In C7 and C74, the hydrophobic interaction is mediated by structurally similar aromatic paratope residues, whereas aromatic residues are absent in 110-3 s hydrophobic paratope. As observed in C74, the 110-3 antibodies anchor at the CIDRα1 core helix 1 N-terminal as well as at the EBS helix N-terminal glutamic acid conserved in CIDRα1.4-8 domains. However, 110-3.4 tilts more toward the EBS helix and exhibits multiple hydrogen bond interactions with semiconserved residues on the helix compared to the more broadly reactive C74. This dependency on interaction with residues that CIDRα1 domains can vary while retaining EPCR binding, may explain the reduced inhibitory breadth of 110-3 mAbs compared to C74.
Although C7 and C74 share the same VH gene and binding mode, their sequences differ significantly. In contrast, 110-3 utilizes a different VH gene. All three antibodies have undergone affinity maturation, illustrating that multiple developmental pathways can give rise to bIAbs targeting CIDRα1. While different routes may lead to broad inhibitory reactivity against EPCR-binding PfEMP1, bIAbs likely converge on common molecular features, including a central hydrophobic patch supported by a minimal number of anchoring interactions. To what extent these principles apply to antibody responses against CIDRα1.1 variants remains to be determined.
It is not possible to determine precisely when and how the 110-3 antibodies were induced, or their exact abundance in the 9-y-old child. However, the discovery of broadly inhibitory plasma antibodies in a younger individual from a different geographical region than those in previous reports supports the notion that such antibodies are commonly developed in malaria-exposed individuals and can reach concentrations comparable to those against viral virulence proteins ( , ). This supports the notion that bIAbs are important components of the protective antibody shield in malaria-exposed individuals. We speculate that most individuals in malaria-endemic areas must develop similar bIAbs to avoid severe malaria, a finding that may inform development of preventive interventions against severe malaria pathogenesis.

## Materials and Methods

## Sample Collection and Preparation.
In a malariometric survey conducted in July 2021 in north-eastern Tanzania, levels of plasma IgG reactive to three CIDRα1 domain variants (CIDRα1.1, CIDRα1.4, and CIDRα1.5) were assessed by ELISA in 501 participants aged 0 to 19 y ( ). In a follow-up sampling conducted in 2023 ( ), blood samples were collected from eight individuals over 8 y old who exhibited CIDRα1-reactive IgG levels in the top >90th percentile. Plasma was separated from blood cells by centrifugation, and PBMCs were transferred directly and stored in RNAprotect Cell Reagent. The study was approved by the Tanzania Medical Research Coordinating Committee of the National Institute for Medical Research, and informed consent or assent was obtained from the donors. CIDRα1-reactive antibodies were purified from total IgG by elution on an NHS column coupled with peptide: H-LFTNKHDIPKKYYLNINDLFDSFFFQVIYKFNEGEAKWNELKENLKKQIASSKANNGTKDSEA-OH as previously described ( ). Eluted IgG was concentrated and screened at 1:10 dilution along with total IgG and the run-through IgG adjusted to the same IgG concentration, for reactivity and EPCR-binding neutralization to the panel of 19 EPCR-binding CIDRα1 domains and two CD36-binding CIDRα2/5 domains as in ref. .

## Antibody and B Cell Receptor Sequencing.
Affinity-purified IgG (110 aff IgG: ~120 μg; 136 aff IgG: ~99 μg) and PBMCs from the same donor were analyzed using an integrated proteogenomics, combining de novo MS sequencing of IgG with expressed B cell receptor sequencing, as described in ref. .
Each IgG sample was initially concentrated using vacuum centrifugation in a low-pressure centrifuge (i.e., Speedvac) and then reduced with dithiothreitol (DTT) to a final concentration of 30 mM for 15 min at 95 °C. The sample was then divided into two tubes for additional treatment. In the initial fraction (5/7 of the sample), alkylation with Iodoacetamide (IAA) was performed at a final concentration of 50 mM for 30 min. After the IAA treatment, the sample underwent precipitation with three volumes of acetone, followed by incubation at −20 °C, centrifugation at 23,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C, and the pellet was dried in a low-pressure centrifuge. The pellet was rehydrated in 4 M urea and incubated at 37 °C for 10 min. An additional equivalent volume of water was added, and the sample was partitioned into five tubes, with four tubes receiving 30 µL of 50 mM ammonium bicarbonate for overnight digestion using Trypsin, Lys-C, Asp-N, and Chymotrypsin at a 1:20 ratio. In the fifth tube, Pepsin digestion was conducted by adding 30 µL of HPLC-grade water, 2 µL of 1 N HCl, and Pepsin in a 1:20 ratio. The mixture was then digested at 37 °C for 15 min and subsequently inactivated at 95 °C for 3 min. The residual fraction (2/7) was treated with 0.5 M 2-bromoethylamine hydrobromide (BEA from Sigma) in a 100 mM tris buffer at pH 8 for 4 h at 25 °C. BEA converts cysteine into a lysine analogue, establishing it as a potential protease cleavage site for both Trypsin and Lys-C. The BEA-labeled samples were precipitated by adding trichloroacetic acid to achieve a final concentration of 20% (w/v). The precipitated sample was washed with acetone, dried, reconstituted, and digested overnight with Trypsin and Lys-C at a 1:20 ratio in 30 mM ammonium bicarbonate. All protease digests were dried under reduced pressure, reconstituted in 40 µL of 0.1% formic acid, and loaded into an Evotips following a procedure as in ref. , subsequently analyzed using HCD mode on the Evosep-Exploris 240 (Thermo-Fisher). All used proteases employed in the polyclonal digestion were obtained from Promega.
Gel-based separation was conducted using isoelectric focusing (IEF) and “Nonreducing Room Temperature” (NRT) gels with and without IdeS treatment as described in ref. . IEF gel: IEF polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (IEF-PAGE) was performed using Novex™ pH 3 to 10 IEF Protein Gels (1.0 mm, 12-well, 5% polyacrylamide; pI range 3.5 to 8.0). Fifteen micrograms of polyclonal antibody samples were mixed with 2× IEF sample buffer and loaded per well. IEF marker (Bio-Rad) was included as a pI reference. Electrophoresis was conducted using Novex™ IEF cathode and anode buffers in a Bio-Rad PowerPac HV system. Following separation, gels were fixed for 30 min at room temperature, washed with deionized water (2 × 5 min), and stained with Bio-Safe™ Coomassie (Bio-Rad) for 1 h. Gels were destained in deionized water until optimal contrast was achieved.
Nonreducing SDS-PAGE was performed at room temperature to analyze polyclonal antibody conformational states. Samples (10 µg/lane) were mixed with Laemmli buffer without heat denaturation and separated on 7.5% Mini-PROTEAN TGX precast gels. Electrophoresis was carried out at 150 V for 60 min using Tris/Glycine/SDS running buffer. Protein bands were visualized by Coomassie brilliant blue staining (30 min) followed by overnight destaining. All electrophoresis reagents and materials were from Bio-Rad.
In gel digestion was performed following modified protocols from ref. . Excised gel bands underwent sequential washing with HPLC-grade water (2 × 200 µL) and dehydration with 1:1 mixture of 100 mM TEAB:acetonitrile (200 µL). Proteins were reduced using 5 mM DTT in 100 mM TEAB (56 °C, 30 min) and alkylated with 55 mM IAA (room temperature, 30 min). After washing with water (2 × 400 µL), bands were dehydrated again using TEAB:acetonitrile (1:1). Proteins were digested overnight at 37 °C with trypsin (6 ng/µL in 100 mM TEAB, 100 µL per sample). Peptides were extracted from the gel matrix in two steps: first collecting the digestion supernatant and then performing an additional extraction with 60% acetonitrile/0.1% formic acid (100 µL). Combined extracts were dried under vacuum and reconstituted in 0.1% formic acid (40 µL). The entire sample was loaded onto Evotips according to the manufacturer’s specifications. Peptide analysis was performed using HCD fragmentation on an Evosep-Exploris 240 mass spectrometer under conditions detailed in the MS section.
MS analysis: Mass spectrometric analyses were performed on an Orbitrap Exploris 240 for HCD experiments (Thermo-Fisher). The samples were separated on a 15 cm PepSep column (Bruker Daltonics) with 30 samples analyzed per day. Data acquisition parameters included precursor scanning at 60,000 resolutions over m/z 400 to 2,000, automatic AGC target and injection time, 2.5e4 intensity threshold, and charge state selection of 2 to 8. Fragment ions were generated using 30% normalized collision energy and detected at 7,500 resolutions with 15-s dynamic exclusion.
Ig sequencing. Paired-end Illumina MiSeq sequencing of heavy, kappa, and lambda variable regions was performed using the TakaraBio SMARTer Human BCR IgG IgM H/K/L Profiling Kit on cDNA synthesized from total RNA extracted from RNAprotect (sigma)-preserved buffy coat of same blood sample used for plasma IgG preparation. Read number outcomes (H: heavy chain, K: kappa light chain, L: Lamda light chain): Donor 110: H replicate 1: 3,479,990; H replicate 2: 3,792,515; K: 3,216,281; L: 4,061,192. Donor 136: H replicate 1: 1,808,608; H replicate 2: 2,099,722; K: 1,811,290; L: 2,413,368.
For proteogenomic analysis, Ig-sequence data were processed to obtain a custom protein database. Raw reads (R1 and R2) were translated to amino acid sequences using the IMGT human germline reference database, followed by consolidation of redundant protein entries. Peptide mass spectra were searched against this database via Novor.Cloud platform. Database matches were prioritized using two criteria: peptide coverage across CDRs and overall sequence coverage. Additional sequence refinement involved CDR3-based clustering. The de novo sequencing workflow comprised three sequential steps: initial contig generation, assembly of CDRs within individual chains, and final pairing of heavy and light chains. Heavy and light chain pairing was accomplished by analyzing peptide coelution patterns across IEF and NRT gel fractions. Quantitative profiles were generated using MaxQuant (Ver2.1.3), and chain pairs were established based on peptide correlation coefficients between different fractions. Detailed methodology for the heavy-light chain pairing approach used is described in ref. .

## Recombinant mAb and Fab Production and Screening.
Recombinant monoclonal IgG1 antibodies (mAbs) were produced by Biointron Biological Inc. Breadth and potency of mAb reactivity and EPCR-binding neutralization were tested using the panel of 19 EPCR-binding CIDRα1 domains using customized Luminex bead assay as well as on a panel of mutated HB3VAR03 CIDRα1.4 and IT4VAR20 CIDRα1.1 recombinant proteins by ELISA, as described in ref. . Recombinant Fab fragments of 110-3.4 and 110-3.6 mAbs were expressed and produced by Genscript USA Inc. with C-terminal HIS-tag on the heavy chain of the variable domains using their TurboCHO2.0 system. Fabs were buffer-exchanged to 20 mM Tris/HCl and 150 mM NaCl, pH 7.5, and aliquoted for long-term storage at −80 °C.

## BLI Analysis.
Fab kinetics analysis was performed in ForteBio Octet96 Red using Fab2G sensors (Sartorius). Fabs were diluted to 10 µg mL in kinex buffer [1× PBS, 0.01% Tween-20, 0.01% BSA, and 0.005% pH.7.4]. Analytes, the single domain CIDRα1 variants had 4× dilution concentrations from 200 nM to 3.125 nM and, the multidomain HB3VAR03 had 2× dilution series from 500 nM to 3.9 nM. Fabs were loaded onto the sensor until the threshold reached 1 nm. After loading sensors were dipped into the kinex buffer to read the baseline. Association was measured for 300 s, and dissociation was measured for 300 s for CIDRα1 variants. For the multidomain HB3VAR03 analyte, the association was measured 500 s, and dissociation was measured 600 s. A background signal from a biosensor loaded with Fab but dipped in buffer (no analyte) was subtracted from the analyte signal. Kinetics analyses were performed twice with independently prepared analyte dilution series. Curve fitting was done at 1:1 binding model. On-rate and off-rate were calculated by averaging binding curves with R2 > 0.95.

## Cryo-EM Sample Preparation.
110-3.4 Fab was complexed with IT4VAR22 three-domain protein at 3:1 Fab to antigen molar ratio and incubated for 1 h at room temperature. The complex was then purified using size exclusion chromatography in S200 increase 10/300 GL column in Tris-buffered saline pH 7.5. Fractions corresponding to the complex were pooled and concentrated to 1 mg/mL. 3 µL of the concentrated complex was applied to UltrAuFoil holey R1.2/1.3 300 mesh glow-discharged grid and plunge frozen in Vitrobot Mark IV (TFS). The grid was then stored in liquid nitrogen for data collection.

## Cryo-EM Data Collection and Processing.
Cryo-EM data of 110-3.4:IT4VAR22 3D protein were collected on a 200kEV Glacios (ThermoFisher) microscope with Falcon IV direct electron detector. Movies were collected at a nominal magnification of 190,000×, resulting in 0.725 Å pixel size. The defocus range of −1 to −2 µm was applied. Movies with a total of 40 electron event representation fractions were collected at a total electron dose of 49 e/Å2. EPU v3.5.1 software was used for data collection. A total of 6,604 movies were collected and dose weighted in Cryosparc LIVE™ and contrast transfer functions were estimated correspondingly. Initial particle picking was performed using blob picker function in cryosparc v4.5.3 ( ). Initial 2D templates were generated after multiple iterations of 2D classification and those templates were used to repick particles using template picker. 1,162,714 particles were extracted and were subjected to several iterations of reference-free 2D classifications. 530,312 particles corresponding to good classes were then selected and subjected to ab initio and heterogeneous refinements to generate initial 3D maps. 394,495 particles corresponding to good classes were then used for nonuniform refinement with one of the best heterogenous class map. A local mask was generated in chimera to mask the CIDRα1.7 domain and the variable domain of the fab as shown in SI Appendix, Fig. S1 to perform local refinement on the NU-refined map. Postprocessing of the local refined map was performed by applying B-factor sharpening with a value of −120 Å2. Final resolution of 3.2 Å was estimated using gold-standard Fourier shell correlation (GS-FSC) at FSC 0.143. Local resolution of the map is shown in SI Appendix, Fig. S1.

## Cryo-EM Structure Modeling and Refinement.
Initial coordinates of CIDRα1.7 were used from PDBid: 8VDG, and the model for variable domain of 110-3.4 was generated using ABodyBuilder2 ( ). The in silico models were docked into the local refined map using chimera, and initial rigid body fitting was performed in COOT v0.9.8 ( ). Full minimization refinement was performed using real-space refinement in PHENIX v1.21.5 ( ). Interface analysis was performed by calculating buried surface area in PDBePISA ( ). Data collection parameters, refinement statistics, PDB, and EMDB entry IDs are reported in SI Appendix, Table S1. Structural figures were made in ChimeraX ( ).

## P. falciparum Culture and EPCR Binding Assay.
P. falciparum parasites were cultured and selected for PfEMP1 variant (HB3VAR03 or IT4VAR18) expression as described previously ( , ). Parasites were maintained at 4% hematocrit in O+ erythrocytes in RPMI-1640 medium supplemented with 5 g/L AlbuMAX II, 25 mM HEPES, 4 mM L-glutamine, 0.02 g/L hypoxanthine, and 25 μg/mL gentamicin. Erythrocytes expressing HB3VAR03 and IT4VAR18 PfEMP1 variants were enriched via selection for binding to recombinant EPCR and anti-IT4VAR18 CIDRα1.6 IgG (elicited in rats), respectively. Expression phenotypes were confirmed by flow cytometry with variant-specific antibodies and by RT-qPCR using primers specific for each var gene in respective HB3 and IT4 clones. EPCR binding inhibition assays were performed as previously ( ). Briefly, twenty microliters of 20 μg/mL recombinant EPCR was spotted radially on Petri dishes. After a 2-h incubation at 37 °C, unbound EPCR was removed, and spots were blocked with PBS/2% BSA for 30 min. Late-stage HB3VAR03 or IT4VAR18-expressing parasites, purified by magnetic-aided cell sorting, were preincubated for 15 min with 50 μg/mL of anti-VAR2CSA PAM1.4 mAb, 110-3.4 mAb, recombinant EPCR, or RPMI alone. Block solution was removed, and 20 μL parasite (3 × 106/mL) suspensions were added to the spots and incubated for 1 h at 37 °C. Dishes were washed with PBS/2% BSA, and adhered erythrocytes were fixed with 1.5% glutaraldehyde, stained with Giemsa, and imaged under a 10× objective. Bound parasites were enumerated by Fiji software, and binding percentages were calculated relative to the RPMI-only.
