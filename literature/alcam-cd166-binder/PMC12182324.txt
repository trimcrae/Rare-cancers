## Summary
The concept of intravascular immunity is reshaping our understanding of immune surveillance, challenging the traditional view that immune responses are confined to either tissue parenchyma or the bloodstream. Our pilot study utilized intravascular staining (ivs) in rhesus macaques (RhM) to spatially distinguish pulmonary vasculature-associated (ivs+) CD8+ T cells from interstitial (ivs-) T cells. Single-cell RNA sequencing and flow cytometry revealed that ivs+ T cells are not passive blood contaminants but rather a distinct, “resident-like” population enriched in cytotoxic effectors, with elevated expression of transcripts associated with tissue residency, cell adhesion, and vascular/platelet interactions, while ivs- T cells exhibited a classic TRM signature. The presence of SIV-specific tetramer+ ivs+ CD8+ T cells in infected RhM suggests a role in antiviral defense within the lung vasculature. These findings provide new insight into intravascular immunity, highlighting a unique population of CD8+ T cells as potential pulmonary vascular sentinels poised for rapid immune responses.

## Introduction
Mounting an effective immune defense against invading pathogens relies not only on circulating T cells but also on their strategic positioning within peripheral tissues. Traditionally, T cells have been thought to reside mainly in the inner layers, or parenchyma, of non-lymphoid organs. However, emerging evidence shows that the vasculature itself—the vast network of blood vessels winding through tissues—may play an active role in tissue-level immunity. , , , , , Immune cells, acting as sentinels, have been observed patrolling the microvasculature of various organs, a concept known as “intravascular immunity,” which expands our understanding of where and how immune cells function within tissues. In mice, CD8+ tissue-resident memory T cells (TRM) patrol the hepatic sinusoids, where they scan for pathogens and protect the liver against infection. , Similarly, cells of the innate immune response, such as natural killer T (NKT) cells and non-classical patrolling monocytes (NCPM), , survey the microvasculature of various organs. Like CD8+ TRM, NKT cells can be found patrolling the hepatic sinusoids, , while NCPMs have been observed surveying the microvasculature of diverse organs, including the small veins and arteries in the retina, brain, lungs, skin, and mesentery. , , , , 
Intravascular staining (ivs) is a powerful tool in immunology that has transformed our understanding of immune cell localization by enabling the spatial distinction of lymphocytes within the vasculature from tissue-resident populations, proving particularly valuable in highly vascularized organs with complex vascular beds such as the lung. , , , , Initially developed in murine models, this technique was adapted for rhesus macaques (RhM) by Kauffman et al. in 2018 and has been further refined in more recent work, including the use of serial ivs (SIVS) to study leukocyte trafficking by Potter et al. , , Ivs involves intravital infusion of fluorochrome-labeled monoclonal antibodies against immune cell markers, such as CD45, prior to necropsy. Cells within the vasculature, including those in the pulmonary vasculature, are labeled by the infused antibody (ivs+), whereas tissue-resident cells remain unlabeled (ivs−). Initially considered as “blood contaminants,” ivs+ cells were later found to exhibit unique characteristics warranting further investigation. , , 
Studies utilizing ivs have identified notable differences between lung ivs+ T cells and circulating T cells in both mice and RhMs, , suggesting that these cells are not simply “blood contaminants.” In SIVS experiments, the vast majority of ivs+ T cells in the lung co-label with antibodies from previous infusions, indicating a sustained “resident-like” state in which they persist within the pulmonary vasculature for a prolonged period of time. , These findings parallel murine studies of liver CD8+ TRM, wherein intravascular antibody infusions stain the majority of these cells, yet they remain localized to the liver, as demonstrated by parabiosis experiments. , , Challenging the traditional dichotomy between circulating and tissue-resident T cells, these studies suggest the potential existence of a distinct T cell population that may contribute to intravascular immunity within the lung and emphasize the importance of developing translational animal models to understand their significance in conferring protection and maintaining immune homeostasis.
The lung, constantly exposed to environmental factors, serves as a portal of entry for pathogens and, as such, serves as a critical immunological effector site. , , , With its complex vascular system, comprising bronchial and pulmonary arteries, as well as perivascular capillary beds, the lung offers multiple routes for leukocytes to migrate, establish residency, and potentially mount intravascular immune responses. , Rather than merely serving as a site of transit, the lung’s vast capillary network could be a strategic location for T cells to patrol and respond to threats. , 
Here, in this pilot study, we hypothesized that the lung vasculature harbors a unique population of intravascular T cells, distinct from both tissue-resident and circulating T cells that contribute to pulmonary immune surveillance. To explore this, we performed ivs and utilized flow cytometry along with single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNAseq) to comprehensively characterize ivs+ and ivs- T cells in the lungs of RhMs. Nonhuman primates (NHPs) serve as an ideal animal model for investigating pulmonary immunology due to their close similarity to humans in both immune system function and lung anatomy, compared to other animal models, providing more accurate and translatable insights into human immunology. To our knowledge, ours is the first study to combine scRNAseq and ivs in order to characterize pulmonary ivs+ and ivs- T cells in RhMs. Our findings show that ivs- CD8+ T cells exhibit a core TRM signature, whereas ivs+ CD8+ T cells in the pulmonary vasculature display a unique transcriptional profile enriched in cytotoxic and tissue-residency markers, as well as genes associated with vascular and platelet interactions. These findings suggest that CD8+ T cells may act as intravascular sentinels, poised to detect and respond to pathogens within the lung’s microvasculature, shedding light on a previously undervalued layer of immune defense.

## Results

## ivs in rhesus macaques reveals differential tissue distribution
We performed ivs in RhM by administering anti-CD45-AL647 antibodies via the saphenous vein 5 min prior to necropsy. Following euthanasia, cells were isolated from various tissues and analyzed by flow cytometry ( A). We found that the frequencies of ivs+ cells varied across tissues ( B–1D). In blood, virtually all CD45+ cells were ivs+, confirming that the ivs antibody was effectively administered in sufficient quantities to label >99% of intravascular cells. The vast majority of lymphocytes in both lung and liver were tagged by the infusion antibody, with approximately 75% and 80% identified as ivs+, respectively. In contrast, <1% of cells in BAL and gut were ivs+ ( C), indicating minimal antibody spillover into these tissue compartments. 
CD69 serves a dual function in T cell phenotyping. While constitutive CD69 expression is a hallmark of TRM, it is not a perfect marker as not all resident memory T cells express CD69. Adding to this, CD69 also acts as a marker of recent activation in effector T cells (TEFF), which upregulate CD69 upon antigen recognition, subsequently preventing their egress from tissues. , , Here, we observed minimal to no expression of CD69 among CD4+ and CD8+ T cells in the blood ( D and 1E). Conversely, in the lung, CD69 was expressed by approximately 50% of ivs- CD4+ T cells and 70% of ivs- CD8+ T cells, compatible with TRM or TEFF that had migrated into the lung prior to infusion. Notably, only a small fraction (∼5%) of pulmonary ivs+ CD4+ T cells expressed CD69, whereas a considerable proportion (∼30%) of ivs+ CD8+ T cells were CD69 positive. A similar pattern was observed in the liver, where CD69 expression was markedly higher on ivs+ CD8+ T cells (∼75%) compared to ivs+ CD4+ T cells (∼20%) (SFig 1A). Interestingly, among the examined tissues, CD69 expression on ivs+ CD8+ T cells was detected only in the lung, liver, and spleen, whereas CD69 was exclusively expressed by ivs- CD8+ T cells in the BAL, nasal mucosa, jejunum, and colon ( D).
We next analyzed the distribution of CD4+ and CD8+ T cells across lung and blood samples, revealing significant differences between these compartments ( F). Previous studies in NHPs have demonstrated a typical CD4:CD8 ratio of approximately 2:1 in peripheral blood, which aligns with our findings. , However, within the pulmonary ivs+ subset, this ratio was dramatically lower (∼0.22, +/− 0.13), indicating a predominance of CD8+ T cells within the lung vasculature. Notably, the CD4:CD8 ratio in ivs+ cells more closely resembled that of the lung’s ivs-population than peripheral blood, highlighting a distinct enrichment of CD8+ T cells in both lung compartments, a result similar to the findings of Potter et al. , Further characterization of CD8+ T cells within the pulmonary vasculature (ivs+) revealed distinct characteristics compared to interstitial (ivs-) and circulating counterparts. These ivs+ CD8+ T cells exhibited markedly elevated expression of the cytolytic molecule granzyme B (GZMB) ( G) and predominantly displayed an effector phenotype ( H).
Building on these findings, we further characterized the phenotypes of CD8+ T cells across different compartments using flow cytometry. We found distinct expression profiles among vasculature-associated (ivs+), interstitial (ivs-), and blood-derived CD8+ T cells in the lung ( I and 1J, SFig 1B). Specifically, lung ivs+ cells were characterized by high expression of CD45RA and GZMB, intermediate expression of CD69, and low expression of CD28 and CD95 ( I). In contrast, lung ivs-cells showed high expression of CD69, CD95, and CD49a, with minimal CD45RA expression, indicative of a tissue-resident phenotype. Peripheral CD8+ T cells showed elevated levels of CCR7, CD62L, and CD45RA. Interestingly, liver ivs+ and ivs- CD8+ T cells exhibited largely similar tissue-resident profiles, differing only in their expression of CD45RA ( J, and B). These findings suggest that lung ivs+ CD8+ T cells represent a distinct subset differing from both tissue-resident (ivs-) and circulating T cells, while liver vascular-associated T cells more closely resemble their ivs-interstitial counterparts.

## Single-cell RNA sequencing and PCA analysis highlight distinct clustering of CD8+ T cells across tissues
To further explore the unique characteristics of pulmonary T cell subsets identified by flow cytometry, we then performed single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNAseq). Using fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS), we isolated CD3+ ivs+ and CD3+ ivs- T cells from the lung, along with CD3+ T cells from peripheral blood (all ivs+) and bronchial lymph nodes (BLN, all ivs-) ( A). These populations were confirmed by flow cytometry prior to sequencing ( C). 
To characterize transcriptional differences between CD8+ T cells across tissues, we performed principal-component analysis (PCA) ( B–2F). PCA revealed a distinct clustering of CD8+ T cells isolated from lung vasculature (Lung ivs_Pos, orange), lung interstitium (Lung ivs_Neg, turquoise), peripheral blood (Periph, blue), and BLN (Bronch LN, green) ( B). Notably, lung ivs+ and ivs- CD8+ T cells clustered separately but relatively closer, indicating shared characteristics unique to lung-resident cells. There was a clear separation between peripheral and BLN CD8+ T cells from lung ivs+ cells, further supporting the idea that pulmonary ivs+ CD8+ T cells are not simply blood contaminants but rather represent a unique population.
To quantify the separation between lung ivs+ CD8+ T cells and the other subsets, we calculated the centroid distance ( C). For PC1 and PC2, the centroid distances were 7.19 for lung ivs-, 9.94 for blood, and 12.29 for BLN, demonstrating increasing divergence from the ivs+ population. In the PCA plot of PC2 and PC3 ( B), lung ivs+ and ivs- CD8+ T cells showed closer proximity (centroid distance: 4.58), while peripheral blood (9.92), and BLN (8.79) T cells remained more distinct. Additionally, we identified genes that contributed to the separation of these CD8+ T cell populations ( D–2F). Among the top genes driving the divergence in PCA were GZMB, IL7R, SPOCK2, RAP1GAP2, ITGA1, and AKT3 ( D–2F).

## UMAP analysis reveals a heterogeneous distribution of CD8+ T cell subsets
To further explore the heterogeneity of CD8+ T cells across tissues, we applied uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) analysis, which revealed distinct CD8+ T cell populations distributed across the lung vasculature (ivs+), lung interstitium (ivs-), peripheral blood, and BLN ( G–2I). Within the lung vasculature (ivs+), we identified a prominent cluster of CD8+ effector/effector memory T cells (TEFF/TEM) highly enriched (31%) that were distinguished by elevated expression of GZMB and the fractalkine/CX3CL1 receptor CX3CR1. The lung interstitium (ivs-) was enriched with CD8+ TRM, making up 34% of the T cell population. This population exhibited elevated expression of canonical TRM markers, including CD69, ITGAE (CD103), ITGA1 (CD49a), and CD44. , , Naive/resting CD4+ T cells, characterized by expression of CCR7, SELL, TCF7, and PECAM, were highly enriched in the BLN, making up 39% of the T cell subset. , , Although CD4+ T cells predominated in the BLN, we also found a notable presence of CD8+ CCR7+ central memory T cells (TCM) with high expression of IL7R. The peripheral blood displayed a more heterogeneous mix of T cell phenotypes without any single population dominating. Overall, these findings highlight the tissue-specific distribution of T cell subsets, with CD8+ T cells more highly prevalent in the lung (both ivs- and ivs+), while CD4+ T cells dominated the BLN.

## Differential transcriptomic signatures among CD8+ T cells across tissues indicate pulmonary vascular-associated cells maintain an enhanced cytotoxic state
Building on our PCA and UMAP findings, we investigated the transcriptomic landscape of CD8+ T cells across lung and peripheral blood ( A–3C). Comparative analysis of lung-associated CD8+ T cell populations (ivs- and ivs+) versus peripheral CD8+ T cells identified 121 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) ( A). Of these, 45 genes were shared by both lung subsets, while 26 were uniquely expressed in the lung vasculature (ivs+), and 50 were specific to the interstitial tissue (ivs-). A direct comparison between lung ivs+ and ivs- CD8+ T cells identified 54 DEGs, with 24 genes expressed in the ivs+ subset and 30 in the ivs-population ( B). Notably, the DEG profiles suggest an equivalent level of separation between lung ivs+ and ivs-populations as there is between lung ivs+ and peripheral blood CD8+ T cells, indicating that these three groups are transcriptionally distinct based on DEGs. 
Top DEGs distinguishing lung ivs+ CD8+ T cells included genes linked to cytotoxicity (GZMB), cell adhesion and migration (ITGAV, MEGF9, HEG1, ZEB2, and RAPGEF2), immune regulation and signaling (TGFB1, CREM, TNFRSF1B), membrane trafficking (SYTL3), and transcriptional regulation (SKI, and BAZ1A) , , ( C and 3D). We also noted upregulation of a number of heat shock proteins (HSP), namely HSP90A1, HSP90B1, HSPH1, HSPD1, and HSPA8 within the ivs+ CD8+ T cell subset.
Lung interstitial (ivs-) CD8+ T cells were enriched for tissue-resident makers, such as ITGA1 (CD49a), AHR, and ICOS, consistent with their residency in the lung parenchyma ( C and 3D). CD8+ T cells localized in the BLN expressed lymph node homing genes SELL and PECAM1, along with elevated levels of JUN, TCF7, and LEF1, suggesting a predominantly naive or recently activated phenotype , , ( C and 3D). In contrast, CD8+ T cells in the peripheral blood showed marked downregulation of these tissue-specific genes, reflecting their less differentiated state ( C and 3D).
Through PCA and DEG analysis, we found that GZMB was a key driver of transcriptional divergence among CD8+ T cell populations ( F, 3C, and 3D). Looking specifically at GZMB and other genes associated with cytotoxicity, we found that lung ivs+ CD8+ T cells demonstrated enhanced cytotoxic potential, evidenced by significantly higher expression of genes coding for cytolytic molecules (GZMB and PRFN1) as well as RAB27A which is essential for secretion of cytolytic molecules , , ( E). This is consistent with our flow cytometry data which showed elevated protein expression of GZMB in the lung ivs+ subset ( G and 1I). Together, these findings suggest that CD8+ T cells within the pulmonary vasculature maintain a heightened cytotoxic state, possibly enabling rapid responses to blood-borne pathogens.

## Single-cell analysis of CD8+ T cells across tissues reveals upregulation of transcripts associated with tissue residence in pulmonary ivs+ T cells
To further delineate the unique transcriptional profiles of CD8+ T cells across tissue compartments, we focused on specific genes related to tissue residency, cell adhesion, trafficking, and T cell differentiation ( ). CD8+ T cells isolated from lung parenchyma (ivs-) exhibited the highest expression of classic tissue-resident markers, including CD69, ITGAE (CD103), ITGA1 (CD49a), CD44, RUNX3, and BHLHE40 , , , ( A). CD8+ T cells in the lung vasculature (ivs+) showed a notable enrichment in residency markers CD69 (compared to peripheral T cells) and CD44, as well as upregulation of tissue-residency promoting transcription factors RUNX3 and BHLHE4 (compared to both peripheral and BLN T cells). We found minimal expression of tissue egress/lymph node homing markers CCR7 and SELL (CD62L) among CD8+ T cells in the lung (both ivs+ and ivs-) and blood, whereas these markers were significantly elevated in CD8+ T cells isolated from BLN ( A). 
To gain additional insight into the residency status of lung CD8+ T cells, we scored cells based on previously published core TRM and circulating gene signatures. Consistent with our findings above, both ivs+ and ivs- CD8+ T cells displayed significantly higher TRM signature scores compared to CD8+ T cells in blood and BLN ( B–4E). Notably, the ivs-subset exhibited the lowest circulating signature score, indicative of a distinct tissue-resident phenotype. However, ivs+ CD8+ T cells also demonstrated a significantly lower circulating signature score compared to blood, suggesting that while they retain some features of circulating cells, they are enriched for tissue-resident characteristics ( C and 4E). PCA analysis using these TRM and circulating gene sets further highlighted the divergence between tissue-localized and circulating T cells. This analysis showed a clear separation of ivs+ and ivs- CD8+ T cells from their counterparts in peripheral blood and BLN, underscoring the distinct molecular profiles associated with tissue residency in the lung ( D).
Given the critical role of transforming growth factor β (TGF-β) signaling in establishing and maintaining a tissue-resident phenotype, we explored TGF-β expression across the CD8+ T cell subsets. , , Interestingly, both lung CD8+ T cell subsets (ivs+ and ivs-) showed high expression of TGFB1 and TGF-β receptor 2 (TGFBR2), with the ivs+ subset also exhibiting high levels of TGFBR3 ( F). To assess the functional impact, we evaluated TGF-β signaling using the Seurat AddModuleScore function, which scored the cells based on expression of a previously published TGF-β-signaling gene signature derived from TGF-β treated CD8+ T cells ( F). This confirmed high TGF-β signaling in both ivs+ and ivs- CD8+ T cell subsets, with significantly higher TGF-β signature scores compared to cells in blood and BLN CD8+ T cells. These findings suggest that significantly elevated TGF-β signaling likely contributes to a “resident-like” phenotype in lung ivs+ CD8+ T cells within the pulmonary vasculature.

## Key transcription factors define pulmonary CD8+ T cell subsets
Both lung ivs+ and ivs- CD8+ T cell subsets exhibited elevated expression of transcription factors associated with TRM, resulting in distinct clustering when compared to blood and BLN ( G). Specifically, NR4A1, EGR2, IRF4, BHLHE40, RUNX3, RBPJ, and AHR were highly expressed in both lung subsets relative to circulating CD8+ T cells , , ( G, A, and S2B). Though not statistically significant, we also observed elevated levels of PRDM1 (BLIMP1), a transcription factor implicated in TRM development in mice. , Consistent with prior studies demonstrating downregulation of EOMES and KLF2 in TRM, both lung subsets demonstrated significant downregulation of these transcripts, supporting their tissue-resident phenotype. , , Interestingly, NOTCH1, STAT3, TBX21, and ZEB2 expression distinguished ivs+ from ivs-pulmonary CD8+ T cells, with higher levels observed in the ivs+ subset. Together, these findings reveal distinct transcriptional profiles for pulmonary ivs+ and ivs- CD8+ T cells. The ivs+ cells express markers of both TEFF/TEM (TBX21 and ZEB2) and TRM (STAT3, NR4A1, IRF4, RUNX3, RBPJ, and AHR), while the ivs-subset aligns closely with canonical TRM signatures (FOX01, BCL6, NR4A1, EGR2, IRF4, RUNX3, and AHR), reflecting a tissue-resident phenotype analogous to human and murine TRM cells. 

## Regulators of T cell trafficking and transendothelial migration
Analysis of gene expression patterns associated with cell adhesion and transendothelial migration showed clear distinctions among CD8+ T cells from lung vasculature (ivs+), lung tissue (ivs-), blood, and BLNs ( H and ). Notably, several integrins were upregulated in the lung ivs+ CD8+ T cell subset compared to both lung ivs- and peripheral CD8+ T cells, including integrin alpha L (ITGAL), ITGAV, integrin beta 1 (ITGB1), and ITGB2 ( H, A, and S3C). Together, ITGAL and ITGB2 form the integrin LFA-1, which plays a pivotal role in leukocyte adhesion by establishing high-affinity interactions with endothelial intercellular adhesion molecule-1 (ICAM-1), facilitating firm arrest and attachment of cells to the vascular endothelium. , , , We also found significantly higher expression of ITGAV in the lung ivs+ CD8+ T cells subset, with 45% of these cells expressing ITGAV, compared to just 10%–15% of CD8+ T cells isolated from lung tissue (ivs-), blood, and BLN ( H, A, and S3C). Activated leukocyte cell adhesion molecule (ALCAM), CD6, and cell migration-inducing hyaluronan-binding protein 2 (CEMIP2) were also enriched in the lung ivs+ subset (compared to both lung ivs- and peripheral CD8+ T cells) as well as CD99 (compared to peripheral CD8+ T cells). These genes encode proteins involved in cell adhesion and migration, facilitating T cell interactions with endothelial cells and their extravasation into tissues during immune responses. , Additionally, lung vasculature-associated CD8+ T cells (ivs+) exhibited elevated expression of chemokine receptors CX3CR1 and CXCR4, as well as transcripts involved in RAP1 signaling (RAP1A, RAP1B, RASSF5, and RAPGEF2). Notably, RAP1 signaling has been shown to activate integrins, in particular, LFA-1, enhancing cell adhesion and migration. , 
Lung interstitial CD8+ T cells (ivs-) displayed elevated expression of transcripts associated with cell adhesion (ITGA1 (CD49a), CD6, CD99, CEMIP2) and RAP1 signaling (RAP1A, RAP1B, RASSF5) as well as chemokine receptors CXCR3 and CXCR4, relative to CD8+ T cells in the blood ( H and B). CXCR4 expression was significantly higher in both lung ivs- and ivs+ CD8+ T cells subsets compared to blood and BLN-derived cells, suggesting a potential role for CXCR4/CXCL12 interactions in regulating T cell localization to the lung in RhM. BLN-derived CD8+ T cells shared similarities to CD8+ T cells in the lung vasculature (ITGAL and ADGRE5), lung tissue (ITGA1 and CXCR3), and blood (PECAM and ITGA4).

## Pathway enrichment analysis suggests ivs+ CD8+ T cells have an enhanced ability to interact with endothelial cells
To further characterize lung vascular-associated CD8+ T cells (ivs+), we performed pathway enrichment analysis (PEA) to identify pathways uniquely enriched in this subset ( A; ). Our analysis revealed significant upregulation of several reactome pathways related to vascular interactions, including “cell surface interactions at the vascular wall,” “platelet activation signaling and aggregation,” and “hemostasis” ( A, 5C, and 5D). The upregulated genes contributing to these pathways are detailed in . These findings suggest that the ivs+ CD8+ T cells have an enhanced ability to interact with endothelial cells lining the pulmonary vasculature, potentially facilitating their retention or migration within this niche. These interactions, particularly those related to vascular adhesion and immune surveillance, merit further investigation to elucidate their functional implications. 

## Enrichment of diverse signaling pathways highlights the functional complexity of lung ivs+ CD8+ T cells
In addition to vascular interaction pathways, PEA also identified enrichment in a number of signaling pathways in the ivs+ CD8+ T cell subset, most notably “TGF-β receptor signaling activates SMADS” ( A and 5B). This finding is consistent with our earlier analysis of TGF-β signaling, in which we observed upregulation of TGF-β stimulated genes in CD8+ T cells, further supporting the role of this pathway in modulating the phenotype of lung vasculature-associated T cells. Other signaling pathways enriched within this population (ivs+) included “signaling by GPCR,” “G alpha S signaling events,” “G alpha I signaling events,” “signaling by FGFR1,” “signaling by FGFR3,” “signaling by interleukins,” “signaling in immune system,” “signaling by VEGF,” “signaling by receptor tyrosine kinases,” “signaling by Met,” and “signaling by SCF Kit” ( B). The broad enrichment of these pathways highlights the multifaceted roles of ivs+ CD8+ T cells in modulating immune responses, tissue repair, and homeostasis within the pulmonary vasculature. These findings underscore the unique functional capabilities of ivs+ CD8+ T cells, warranting further exploration of their contributions to pulmonary immune regulation.

## Phenotypic and functional analysis of pulmonary CD8+ T cells (ivs+ and ivs-) in SIV-infected RhMs reveals distinct spatial signatures
We then investigated the phenotype and functional capabilities of pulmonary ivs+ and ivs- CD8+ T cells in SIV-infected RhM. Four RhM were exposed to SIVmac239 and placed on antiretroviral treatment (ART) two weeks following infection. ART treatment continued for approximately one year, after which the animals were euthanized four weeks following the cessation of ART ( A). Phenotypic analysis of pulmonary (ivs- and ivs+) and peripheral T cells at necropsy revealed patterns similar to those of the naive animals ( –1H and B–6D). While SIV infection resulted in a decrease in CD4+ T cells and thus a lower peripheral CD4:CD8 ratio, the distribution of T cells was similar to that of the naive animals with greater proportions of CD8+ T cells in both the lung tissue and lung vasculature as compared to the blood ( B). We found that the ivs-cells had notably higher expression of the memory marker CD95 compared to ivs+ cells, supporting the predominance of TEFF cells in the pulmonary vasculature ( C). Similar to the naive animals, pulmonary interstitial (ivs-) CD8+ memory T cells had significantly higher expression of CD69, and although not statistically significant, the ivs+ CD8+ memory T cell compartment exhibited higher CD69 expression compared to those in the blood ( D). In our investigation into the functional profile of memory CD8+ T cells within the lung, we uncovered distinct differences in T cells localized to the lung vasculature (ivs+) compared to interstitial-associated T cells ( E). Specifically, we observed a significantly higher proportion of memory (CD95+) CD8+ T cells expressing GZMB, IFN-γ, and TNF-⍺ in the pulmonary vasculature (ivs+), whereas IL-2-producing CD8+ T cells were predominantly found in the lung interstitium (ivs-) ( E). 

## Identification of SIV-specific CD8+ T cells suggests pulmonary vascular-associated T cells have a role in antiviral immunity
Next, we characterized SIV-specific CD8+ T cells through MHC Class-I tetramer staining (A01-CM9+) ( F–6J). Four weeks post-ART interruption (week 58), we observed an expansion of SIV-specific memory CD8+ T cells in both blood (rising from 1.58% to 7.61%) and BAL (rising from 1.00% to 5.56%) ( G). At necropsy, we identified SIV-specific CD8+ T cells in both the lung vasculature (ivs+) and lung interstitium (ivs-) ( H). Comparing these pulmonary tissue-resident and vasculature-associated cells, we found a higher proportion of SIV-specific CD8+ T cells in the pulmonary tissues (ranging from 4% to 8% of the total CD8+ T cell population within the ivs-subset) than in the pulmonary vasculature (ranging between 2% and 3% of CD8+ T cells within the ivs+ subset) ( I).
Characterization of the phenotypic differences between the ivs+, ivs-, and circulating SIV-specific CD8+ T cells revealed that ivs-cells in the lower respiratory tract expressed markers indicative of TRM ( J). The ivs- SIV-specific CD8+ T cells exhibited elevated expression of retention markers (CD69, CD49a), reduced expression of the tissue egress marker CD62L, higher expression of chemokine receptors associated with tissue residence (CXCR3, CCR5), and evidence of memory (CD95, CD45RA-). Lack of Ki-67 and CD38 expression, combined with expression of PD-1, suggests that the CD69 expression on ivs-cells is not a consequence of recent activation but rather signifies tissue residency. In contrast, the pulmonary ivs+ SIV-specific CD8+ T cells displayed characteristics consistent with a recently activated effector phenotype, evidenced by elevated Ki-67 and CD38 expression. Compared to their circulating counterparts in blood, the pulmonary ivs+ cells showed reduced expression of CD62L and elevated CD103 expression, reflecting their distinct localization within the pulmonary vasculature. Reduced expression of CD95 among ivs+ CD8+ T cells supports a predominance of effector-like cells, distinguishing them from both TRM and peripheral virus-specific T cells.

## Discussion
Our study highlights a fascinating yet underexplored aspect of pulmonary immunity: the role of intravascular immune surveillance within the lung microvasculature. While intravascular immunity has been well-documented in organs such as the liver, , the pulmonary vasculature—especially in highly translatable animal models such as rhesus macaques (RhM)—remains largely unexplored. Here, we present evidence of a specialized subset of CD8+ T cells uniquely localized within the lung vasculature, challenging the traditional views of pulmonary immunity and tissue residency.
Pulmonary ivs+ T cells were initially thought to be mere “blood contaminants,” transient cells trapped in the vasculature of organs at the time of necropsy. , However, our findings challenge this view, aligning with and expanding upon previous observations of distinct differences between pulmonary ivs+ T cells and circulating T cells. , , Notably, we found significant enrichment of effector CD8+ T cells in the lung vasculature (ivs+), far exceeding their frequency in the blood, suggesting a specialized role for these cells. Through flow cytometry and scRNAseq, we demonstrated that ivs+ CD8+ T cells possess enhanced cytotoxic potential, characterized by high expression of both cytolytic proteins and genes. These cells also expressed elevated levels of cell adhesion markers, further distinguishing them from CD8+ T cells in peripheral blood. Additionally, ivs- CD8+ T cells displayed a transcriptional profile that closely aligns with tissue-resident memory T cells (TRMs), underscoring the utility of ivs staining in identifying bona fide tissue-resident cells in NHPs.
Our PCA analyses further emphasized the distinct transcriptional profile of pulmonary ivs+ CD8+ T cells, showing that vascular-associated T cells (ivs+) cluster separately from CD8+ T cells in blood and bronchial lymph node (BLN). Interestingly, the ivs- CD8+ T cell population had the shortest centroid distance to the ivs+ CD8+ T cells, indicating a closer phenotypic relationship between the vascular-associated (ivs+) and tissue-resident (ivs-) subsets compared to circulating CD8+ T cells. Notably, pulmonary ivs+ CD8+ T cells exhibited a significantly higher TRM gene signature score compared to blood and BLN T cells (albeit lower than ivs-lung cells), suggesting a unique “resident-like” behavior within the vascular niche. This was further supported by elevated expression of tissue-residency-associated transcripts in the ivs+ subset, including CD69, CD44, RUNX3, and BHLHE40. , , , , Additionally, the considerably lower circulating gene signature score alongside low expression of egress-promoting genes, CCR7, SELL, and KLF2, reinforced the notion that the ivs+ CD8+ T cells represent a specialized population rather than simply being circulating cells entrapped at the time of necropsy. , , , , 
We detected high expression of TGF-β-related genes (TGFB1 and TGF-β receptors 2 and 3 (TGFBR2, TGFBR3)), upregulation of TGF-β stimulated genes, as well as enrichment in the TGF-β signaling pathway. Given the role of TGF-β signaling in TRM differentiation and survival, its enrichment within the ivs+ CD8+ T cell subset suggests a potential mechanism by which these cells adopt a “resident-like” state within the pulmonary vasculature. , , , , , , , Despite the lack of CD103 (integrin ⍺E) expression, which is traditionally linked to TGF-β-driven TRM phenotypes, , , , , our data indicate that TGF-β signaling may drive alternative pathways of tissue residency in the lung.
The concept of intravascular immunity, in which immune cells within the vasculature contribute directly to local immune surveillance, is supported by studies of liver-resident CD8+ T cells, which patrol sinusoidal vessels while maintaining a “resident” status. , , Despite their classification as tissue-resident, a significant proportion of liver TRMs are exposed to in vivo-infused antibodies, signifying their presence within the vasculature. , , This phenomenon suggests that residency is not solely confined to tissue parenchyma but can also extend to the vascular compartment. Similar to hepatic TRM, pulmonary ivs+ CD8+ T cells exhibit high expression of LFA-1 (CD11a/ITGAL + CD18/ITGB2), an integrin essential for interactions with endothelial cells and for establishing residency. Both lung ivs+ and ivs- CD8+ T cell subsets also showed elevated expression of genes associated with RAP1 signaling, which has been shown to activate integrins, particularly LFA-1. , Given the pivotal role of integrin-mediated adhesion in T cell trafficking, the upregulation of RAP1 signaling genes may enhance the adhesive properties of these CD8+ T cells, facilitating their selective recruitment and retention within the pulmonary vasculature. Another interesting finding was the differential expression of the integrin ITGAV, which was identified as one of the top DEGs in the ivs+ CD8+ T cell subset. Interestingly, while previous studies have reported upregulation of ITGAV in CD8+ TRM within human lung interstitium, we observed minimal expression of this integrin in the ivs- CD8+ T cell subset, suggesting that prior studies may have inadvertently included T cells originating from the lung vasculature or highlight potential differences between human and RhM TRM. Further studies are needed to elucidate the role of ITGAV in lung vasculature-associated CD8+ T cells, as it may represent a lung-specific trafficking marker or a potential identifier for the ivs+ subset.
The lung’s extensive capillary network may serve as a specialized niche for CD8+ T cells, enabling them to take on the role of vascular sentinels, much like liver-resident CD8+ T cells. The enrichment of pathways such as “cell surface interactions at the vascular wall,” “platelet activation signaling and aggregation,” and “hemostasis” in ivs+ CD8+ T cells suggests a specialized ability to interact with endothelial cells and other vascular components. Notably, interactions between CD44 on CD8+ T cells and platelets anchored to the endothelium facilitate the initial trapping of CD8+ T cells in the liver, which contrasts with the traditional selectin-mediated rolling observed in other tissues. The high expression of CD44 by lung ivs+ cells, coupled with enrichment of transcripts associated with the “platelet activation signaling and aggregation” pathway, suggests parallels between the localization of CD8+ T cells in the lung and liver vasculature. Just as the liver’s unique sinusoidal structure supports T cell retention and surveillance, the lung’s vast capillary network may also serve as a protective interface, capturing, and neutralizing pathogens before infiltrating deeper into tissues.
Our findings have important implications for understanding pulmonary immunity in respiratory infections and inflammatory lung diseases, highlighting the potential role of vascular-associated (ivs+) T cells as frontline defenders. The heightened effector responses within the ivs+ subset during SIV infection, characterized by increased proportions of GZMB, IFN-γ, and TNF-⍺-producing CD8+ T cells, suggests that these cells may play a critical role in antiviral immune defense, complementing the functions of CD8+ T cells localized to the lung interstitium. Moreover, the presence of SIV-specific tetramer+ CD8+ T cells in both ivs+ and ivs-compartments indicates that these distinct populations may contribute differently to the overall antiviral response in the lung.
Our findings lend additional evidence to the hypothesis that specific subsets of cytotoxic CD8+ T cells are sequestered within the vascular spaces to minimize immune-mediated tissue damage. Buggert et al. described highly cytotoxic CD8+ T cell populations found in human and NHP blood samples that were largely absent in thoracic duct lymph (TDL), hypothesizing that the vasculature may house these potent cells so as to shield tissues from immune-mediated damage. Our ivs+ subset further supports this protective hypothesis, as these cells exhibited increased molecular transcripts (GZMB, PRFN1, and RAB27A) and protein expression (GZM-B) of cytotoxic markers compared not only to CD8+ T cells in the lung parenchyma (ivs-) but also the blood. This increased cytotoxic profile aligns with a role in intravascular immune defense that mitigates potential immunopathology within delicate lung tissues.
Collectively, our study reveals a previously underappreciated vascular niche in the lung that harbors a distinct subset of CD8+ T cells, bridging the gap between circulating and tissue-resident immunity. Moving forward, it will be essential to identify the molecular cues that govern the retention and activation of ivs+ T cells within the pulmonary vasculature. Investigating the impact of integrin-mediated adhesion and TGF-β signaling on their behavior could open new therapeutic avenues targeting vascular immunity. The identification of these specialized pulmonary T cells not only advances our understanding of the lung immunological landscape but also highlights their potential as targets for modulating immune responses in respiratory infection and inflammatory lung disease.

## Limitations of the study
While we provide compelling evidence for a unique subset of CD8+ T cells within the pulmonary vasculature, our pilot study is observational and does not directly demonstrate the functional capacity of these cells in vivo. Future studies utilizing functional assays and in vivo models of infection, with larger sample sizes, as well as a comparison of male and female RhMs, are needed to fully elucidate their role in host defense and tissue homeostasis. Additionally, the reliance on necropsy samples limits our ability to investigate the dynamics of pulmonary vascular-associated T cells during active infection or inflammation. Longitudinal studies utilizing SIVS or intravital imaging techniques could provide a deeper understanding of their role during disease progression.

## Experimental model and study participant details
The two female and four male, specific pathogen-free, rhesus macaques used in this study were purpose-bred at the Tulane National Primate Research Center (TNPRC). Macaques were housed in compliance with the NRC Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals and the Animal Welfare Act. Experiments involving animals were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Tulane University. The TNPRC is fully accredited by AAALAC International. Animal Welfare Assurance No. A3180-01. Animals were socially housed indoors in climate-controlled conditions with a 12/12-light/dark cycle. All animals in this study were monitored twice daily to ensure their welfare. Any abnormalities, including those of appetite, stool, and behavior, were recorded and reported to a veterinarian. The animals were fed commercially prepared monkey diet twice daily. Supplemental foods were provided in the form of fruits, vegetables, and foraging treats as part of the TNPRC environmental enrichment program. Water was available ad lib through an automatic watering system. The TNPRC environmental enrichment program is reviewed and approved by the IACUC semi-annually. Veterinarians at the TNPRC Division of Veterinary Medicine have established procedures to minimize pain and distress through several means. Monkeys were anesthetized with ketamine-HCl (10 mg/kg) or tiletamine/zolazepam (3–8 mg/kg) prior to all procedures. Preemptive and post-procedural analgesia (buprenorphine 0.03 mg/kg IM or buprenorphine sustained-release 0.02 mg/kg SQ) was required for procedures that would likely cause more than momentary pain or distress in humans undergoing the same procedures. The animals were euthanized at the end of the study using methods consistent with recommendations of the American Veterinary Medical Association (AVMA) Panel on euthanasia and per the recommendations of the IACUC. Specifically, the animals were anesthetized with tiletamine/zolazepam (8 mg/kg IM) and given buprenorphine (0.01 mg/kg IM), followed by an overdose of pentobarbital sodium. Death was confirmed by cessation of heartbeat and respiration, pupillary dilation, and lack of corneal reflex. The TNPRC policy for early euthanasia/humane endpoint was included in the protocol in case those circumstances arose. Four male RhM were assigned to the SIV experimental group and two female RhM to the naïve group. Animals were assigned based on animal availability.

## Method details

## Intravascular staining
Six Rhesus macaques received a single injection of 30 μg/kg Anti-CD45 [ITS_rhCD45]-AF647 (CD45-ivs) antibody (Nonhuman Primate Reagent Resource (NHPRR), RRID:AB_2910539) diluted in five mL sterile phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) administered through a catheter placed in the saphenous vein 5 min prior to necropsy.

## Immune cell isolation
PBMCs were isolated from EDTA whole blood using SepMate-50 Isolation tubes (Stem Cell Technologies) following the manufacturer’s recommended protocol. BAL samples were centrifuged at 1800 rpm for 5 min at room temperature (RT). BAL supernatant was aspirated and stored at −80°C. All tissue samples were cut into small pieces (∼0.5 cm) using scissors or surgical scalpels. Lung tissue was digested using type IV collagenase (Worthington) and deoxyribonuclease (DNase) I (Roche) at 37°C at 300 rpm for 45 min. Cold stop media (RPMI supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% anti-anti) was added to the digested lung tissue. Clumps were broken up using scissors, and the tissue was plunged ∼10 times using a 50 mL serological pipette. Samples were then filtered through a 100 μm cell strainer and centrifuged at 400 g for 10 min at 4°C. Cells were resuspended in 30% Percoll and centrifuged for 12 min at 780 g at RT. Cells were then washed in PBS with 2% FBS. Samples were incubated on ice for 5 min in 1x ACK lysis buffer (Gibco), to lyse red blood cells, followed by washing with PBS containing 2% FBS. Nasal mucosa was digested in Liberase TL (MilliporeSigma) and DNase I for 45 min at 37°C while shaking at 250 rpm. Cells were filtered over a 70 μm filter, washed in RPMI supplemented with 20% FBS (R20), and centrifuged at 400g for 10 min. If needed, red blood cells were lysed using 1x ACK buffer, and samples were again washed in R20. Lymph node, spleen, and liver samples were physically dissociated and filtered using a 70 μm cell strainer, washed in R10, and centrifuged at 1800 rpm for 5 min. Jejunum and colon tissues were digested first with Hanks Balanced Salt Solution (HBSS, Corning) supplemented with 1mM EDTA (Invitrogen) for 30 min at 37 °C at 400 rpm. The supernatant was aspirated, and this process was repeated a second time. The tissue was then digested in Collagenase II (Sigma-Aldrich) for 30 min at 37 °C at 400 rpm. Cells were filtered over a metal strainer, and the process was repeated for any remaining tissue. Cells were filtered over a metal strainer and washed in RPMI. Cells were either stained immediately for flow cytometry or cryopreserved in Bambanker cell freezing media (GC Lymphotec) at 1 × 107 cells/mL and stored at −80°C.

## Flow cytometry
Whole blood, freshly isolated mononuclear cells from blood, lung, nasal, gut, and lymph nodes, or thawed cryopreserved PBMCs, bronchial lymph node (BLN), liver, and lung cells were washed in FACS buffer (PBS supplemented with 2% FBS) and resuspended in live/dead stain cocktail (50 μL PBS with 0.5 μL live/dead stain) (Fixable Aqua Dead Cell Stain Kit, Invitrogen) in the dark for 20 min at RT. Cells were washed in FACS buffer, resuspended in a chemokine stain cocktail containing Brilliant Stain Buffer (BD Biosciences), and incubated at 37°C for 15 min. Surface staining cocktail was then added with additional Brilliant Stain Buffer (for a total of 50 μL Brilliant Stain Buffer), samples were incubated in the dark for an additional 20 min at RT. Cells were washed in FACS buffer and incubated in Cytofix/Cytoperm Buffer (BD Biosciences) to fix the cells and allow for intracellular staining. Cells were washed in 1x BD Perm/Wash Buffer (BD Biosciences), resuspended in an intracellular stain cocktail, and incubated at RT for 30 min. For SIV peptide stimulation experiments, cells were first stained with 5 μL anti-CD69 antibody in 50 μL Brilliant Stain Buffer (BD Biosciences) for 20 min at RT. Cells were washed and resuspended in 1 mL R10 with either DMSO or SIV Gag peptides along with co-stimulatory antibodies (CD28 and CD49 d at one μL/mL) and Brefeldin A (one μL/mL, BioLegend) and incubated overnight at 37°C, 5% CO2. In the relevant experiments, MHC Class-I tetramer (Mamu-A∗01 SIV Gag 181–189, CM9, NIH Tetramer Core Facility) was added for 45 min at RT immediately before the chemokine stain. Cells were washed and fixed in 1x BD Stabilizing Fixative (BD Biosciences). Samples were analyzed using the BD FACSymphony A5 Cell Analyzer (BD Biosciences) and FlowJo 10 for Mac OS (Tree Star).
For sorting experiments, thawed PBMC, lung, and bronchial lymph node (BLN) cells were stained in 15 mL conical tubes with live/dead and surface cocktails as described above but with 2x the typical antibody concentration. Cells were washed in FACS buffer and resuspended in RPMI-1640 without phenol red supplemented with 10% FBS. Cells were sorted using fluorescence-activated sorting (FACS) to isolate CD3+ T cells from blood and BLN as well as CD3+ ivs- and CD3+ ivs+ cells from lung using the BD FACSAria (BD Biosciences) or MACSQuant Tyto (Miltenyi Biotec).

## Single-cell RNA sequencing
CD3+ ivs- and CD3+ ivs+ cells from lung, as well as CD3+ T cells from blood and BLN, were processed for single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNAseq) following the 10x Genomics® Single Cell Protocols – Cell Preparation Guide for the “preparation of limited samples.” 10x libraries were generated using the Chromium Next GEM Single Cell 3′ LT Reagent Kits v3.1 (Dual Index) kits following the manufacturer protocol. 10x Genomics Cell Ranger 3.1.0 pipeline was used to process raw data and generate separate barcodes, features, and matrix files for each sample, referencing GTF annotations for the Rhesus macaque genome (Macaca mulatta, GCA_003339765.3). The Seurat (v5.1.0) package in R was used to perform data analysis, as previously described. Briefly, individual Seurat objects were generated for each sample, and the raw count matrices were merged using the merge command. Cells with more than 5% mitochondrial genes or more than 2500 genes were excluded from further analysis. The standard Seurat workflow for downstream analyses comprised normalization, scaling, graph-based clustering using shared nearest-neighbor, differential gene expression (DEG), pathway enrichment analysis (PEA), and data visualization. The Harmony algorithm was applied to account for batch effects and biological variability. 

## Differential gene expression (DEG) analysis
DEG analysis was conducted by comparing T cells across tissues using Seurat’s FindMarkers and FindAllMarkers functions. A heatmap was generated in R to illustrate DEGs upregulated in Lung ivs+, Lung ivs-, and both Lung ivs+ and Lung ivs- CD8+ T cells compared to peripheral CD8+ T cells ( A–3C). ## Pathway enrichment analysis (PEA)
Pathway enrichment analysis of ivs+ CD8+ T cells was performed using ClusterProfiler in R. DEGs with an adjusted p-value ≤0.05 were included in the analysis. We performed PEA using the Reactome gene collection from The Broad Institute Molecular Signature Database (MSigDB), , which was filtered to contain only genes present in our dataset in order to avoid bias. The enrichplot package in R was used to illustrate Reactome pathways enriched in our gene set ( A–5C).

## SIV infection
Four RhM were inoculated intravenously with SIVmac239 (n = 2) or SIVmac239 harboring a single nonsynonymous mutation in the nef gene (n = 2). There were no differences in viral or immune dynamics in RhM receiving either virus, so they are treated together throughout the manuscript. All four RhM were placed on antiretroviral therapy (ART) two weeks post-inoculation consisting of tenofovir disoproxil fumarate (TDF; 5 mg/kg), emtricitabine (FTC; 40 mg/kg; both from Gilead, Foster City, CA), and dolutegravir (DTG; 2.5 mg/kg; ViiV Healthcare, London, UK). They were monitored for approximately one year, after which ART was discontinued. Four weeks post-ART-interruption, the animals were euthanized. Pre- and post-infection sampling included blood and bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL), which continued throughout the course of the study.

## Quantification and statistical analysis
Statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.4.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) and GraphPad Prism 10 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). For E, A, and 4F, violin plots were generated using the VlnPlot function from the Seurat package (v5.1.0), with customization applied using the ggplot2 package (v3.5.1). Pairwise comparisons were added using the stat_compare_means function from the ggpubr package (v0.6.0), using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test with Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified through the FindMarkers and FindAllMarkers functions in Seurat using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test, with Bonferroni correction applied to adjust p-values for multiple comparisons. Genes were considered significant based on an adjusted p-value ≤0.05 and a log2 fold change (log2fc) threshold of ≥1 (heatmap, C) or ≥0.5 or ≤ −0.5 (volcano plots, D). Pathway enrichment analysis (PEA) was conducted on upregulated DEGs in the lung ivs+ CD8+ T cell subset using the ClusterProfiler package (v4.12.6) and the Reactome database. Pathways were considered enriched based on an adjusted p-value ≤0.05. The enrichplot package (v1.24.4) was used to visualize the results ( A–5C). For E, ANOVA was conducted using the base R function aov to compare scores across sample groups, followed by Tukey’s Honest Significant Difference (HSD) test for post-hoc comparisons where applicable.
For and , the Wilcoxon rank-sum test was used to determine significance using the wilcox.test function in R.
