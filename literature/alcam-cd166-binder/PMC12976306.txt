## Abstract
Adipose-derived mesenchymal stromal cells (AD-MSCs), also known as mesenchymal stem cells, hold great promise in regenerative medicine due to their pleiotropic effects, including the secretion of cytokines, chemokines, and growth factors. These cells can promote tissue repair, enhance angiogenesis, and modulate inflammation. However, understanding neoadjuvant chemotherapy’s impacts on AD-MSC functionality, immunophenotype, cytokine secretion, and their interactions with fibroblasts remains insufficient. This study aimed to investigate the effects of chemotherapy on AD-MSCs and their ability to influence fibroblast function in vitro. Human, autologous AD-MSCs and skin fibroblasts were isolated from two patient cohorts - those without and those after neoadjuvant chemotherapy, from subcutaneous adipose tissue obtained during surgical procedures. AD-MSCs identity was confirmed by flow cytometry, positive and negative markers, and by induction of adipogenic, osteogenic, and chondrogenic differentiation. Autologous fibroblasts were co-cultured with AD-MSCs to assess fibroblast chemotaxis, proliferation, migration, cytokine secretion, cell-cycle distribution, collagen deposition, and gene expression. Flow cytometric analysis of surface markers, secreted cytokines, transcriptomic analysis, and the cell cycle revealed no significant differences between AD-MSCs from patients who either received or did not receive chemotherapy. A slight increase in mitochondrial activity was observed in cells from chemotherapy-treated patients. Analysis of dermal fibroblasts revealed significant alterations in their biological activity post-chemotherapy. Their chemotactic activity, migration, and collagen production were weakened, and their cytokine secretion profile was altered. Co-culture of autologous fibroblasts and AD-MSCs showed that AD-MSCs stimulated fibroblast chemotactic activity in both groups. However, AD-MSCs stimulated fibroblast migration and proliferation only in patients without previous chemotherapy. This study shows that AD-MSCs maintain their functionality after chemotherapy and can influence fibroblast activity. These findings add to understanding the chemotherapy effects on skin fibroblasts and AD-MSCs and support further research to optimize the use of AD-MSCs in regenerative medicine for oncology patients, particularly in wound healing and tissue repair post-cancer treatments.

## Introduction
 Adipose-derived mesenchymal stromal cells (AD-MSCs) are increasingly valued for their potential in clinical applications, due to their easy availability, multipotency, paracrine activity, and immunomodulatory properties , . AD-MSCs secrete anti-inflammatory cytokines that reduce inflammation, promote tissue regeneration by enhancing angiogenesis, modulate immune responses by influencing T-lymphocyte and macrophage activity, and support the survival and proliferation of surrounding cells – . These pleiotropic effects of AD-MSCs can be modulated by additional factors such as obesity, cancer, or neoadjuvant treatment , . Adjuvant therapy is additional treatment given after primary treatment (usually surgery) to eliminate any remaining cancer cells and reduce the risk of recurrence. Neoadjuvant therapy is systemic treatment - chemotherapy or radiotherapy given before primary treatment to shrink the tumor, facilitate surgery, and improve outcomes . Although AD-MSCs from chemotherapy-treated patients exhibit some differences compared to those from healthy individuals, available evidence indicates that their isolation efficiency and differentiation potential are largely preserved , . By contrast, AD-MSCs isolated from patients after radiotherapy demonstrate an altered ability to differentiate into endothelial-like cells . However, some studies suggest that exposure to cytotoxic agents does not significantly affect the function and regenerative capacity of AD-MSCs , . Therefore, the effects of neoadjuvant treatment on AD-MSCs remain insufficiently characterized.
Although clinical trials using autologous AD-MSC stem cells are being conducted for the treatment of injuries, wound healing or autoimmune disorders, none of the clinical trials involve cells after neoadjuvant treatment , . Cancer patients frequently need regenerative or reconstructive treatments, especially after surgical procedures, therefore, the effectiveness of AD-MSC autologous therapy after cancer treatment remains an open question.
This study aims to investigate whether neoadjuvant chemotherapy affects the phenotype and regenerative potential of AD-MSCs, as well as their interactions with fibroblasts, in vitro.
Interactions between AD-MSCs and surrounding cells, such as fibroblasts, play a crucial role in tissue regeneration and wound healing , . The therapeutic properties of MSCs are closely related to their potential for differentiation, secretion of trophic factors, and immunomodulation , .
Available literature does not provide a clear answer on how AD-MSCs change after chemotherapy, nor whether the neoadjuvant treatment impairs the effect of AD-MSCs on fibroblasts. This question is important in the context of applications in regenerative medicine of stem cells from chemotherapy-treated donors, especially for patients after chemotherapy.

## Materials and methods

## Clinical material
Skin and subcutaneous adipose tissue samples were obtained from the Department of Oncological Surgery and Plastic Surgery during routine surgical procedures. The obtained samples were excess tissue excised as part of the standard surgical field, mostly from the abdominal area (e.g., abdominoplasty or oncological surgery, Suppl. Table   S). The tissues were classified as medical waste, for which patient consent was obtained, along with approval from the Bioethics Committee for Scientific Research of the Medical University of Gdańsk, Poland (consent no. NKBBN/746/2019–2020; NKBBN/746 − 31/2024). Samples from 66 patients were used in this study, including 40 patients without chemotherapy and 26 patients after chemotherapy (Suppl. Table   S). The mean age of patients without chemotherapy was 58 ± 17 years, while those who had undergone chemotherapy (CHT) were 68 ± 11 years old. Both sexes were included. Tissue collection took place between 2019 and 2024 (Table  ). The control group consists of patients without chemo and radiotherapy (in situ cancer) and plastic surgery patients, while the study group consists of patients exposed to chemotherapy. In our laboratory, isolated cells are routinely tested for mycoplasma and bacterial (microscopic observation) purity, only cells with negative results are taken for further analysis.
 

## Isolation of human, autologous fibroblasts and adipose-derived stromal cells

## Isolation of human adipose-derived stromal cells
AD-MSCs were isolated from subcutaneous adipose tissue using mechanical and enzymatic digestion with type I collagenase (0.8%, Sigma-Aldrich, cat. no. C2674), based on a standard protocol . Multiple washes and erythrocyte lysis were performed to obtain the stromal vascular fraction (SVF). The cells were then seeded into Primaria flasks (Corning, USA) at a density of 375 × 103 cells/75 cm2 in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium Low Glucose (DMEM LG, 1 mg/mL glucose; Sigma-Aldrich, cat. no. D6046) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Sigma-Aldrich, cat. no. F9665) and antibiotics (penicillin 1000 U/mL, streptomycin 1000 U/mL; Sigma-Aldrich, cat. no. P0781). Cultures were maintained at 37 °C with 5% CO₂, and the medium was changed every 2–3 days. Subsequent passages were performed at 70–80% cell confluence, and cells were trypsinized with 0.25% trypsin/EDTA solution (Sigma-Aldrich, Saint Louis, Missouri, USA), with a 7-day interval between passages.

## Fibroblast isolation
Human skin fragments were collected and washed in PBS with penicillin/streptomycin (100 units/ ml penicillin and 100 µg/ml streptomycin). Next, skin samples were cut into small fragments and digested with dispase (6 units/ml) overnight in 4 °C to remove the epidermis. Obtained skin explants were placed in 6-well plates in DMEM HG medium (high glucose 4,5 g/L) supplemented with 10% FBS and penicillin/streptomycin (100 units/ ml penicillin and 100 µg/ml streptomycin). After three weeks of culture, the retrieved fibroblasts were passaged (1 × 105 per T25 cell culture flask). The medium was replaced every 2–3 days.

## Confirmation of mesenchymal phenotype of cells isolated from adipose tissue

## Phenotypic confirmation of AD-MSCs by flow cytometry
Following the guidelines of the International Society for Cell and Gene Therapy AD-MSC phenotype was confirmed by immunophenotyping at the second passage – . In addition to the recommended surface markers, the cells were stained with a much wider range of fluorescently labelled antibodies: HLADR, CD31, CD29, CXCR7 – FITC; CD73, CD49d, CD166 – PE; CD14-ECD; CD13, CD10 – PC5.5; CD105, CXCR4- PC7; CD34, CD26 – APC; CD19, CD44 – APC-A700; CD90- APC-A750; DAPI, CD73 – PB450, and CD45 – KO525 (diluted 1:100 in PBS, Suppl. Table S). In three, separate samples, 1 × 105 AD-MSCs were suspended in PBS with antibodies for 0.5 h, in the dark. Control cells were not stained. Flow cytometric analysis was performed using a flow cytometer Cytoflex (Beckman Coulter).

## Differentiation potential
AD-MSC cells at passage two (p2) were seeded into 96-well plates at 5 × 103 per well in four replicates and cultured for 14 days in dedicated differentiation media for adipocytes (StemPro™ Adipogenesis Differentiation Kit, cat. no.A1007001, Thermo Fisher; composition: high-glucose DMEM/F12-based basal medium, L-glutamine, sodium pyruvate, sodium bicarbonate, calcium and sodium phosphate, insulin, dexamethasone, indomethacin, 3-Isobutyl-1-methylxanthine (IBMX), and osteocytes (Gibco™ StemPro™ Osteogenesis Differentiation Kit, cat. no. A1007201; composition: basal medium (DMEM or α-MEM), 10% FBS, dexamethasone (glucocorticoid), β-Glycerophosphate (phosphate source), ascorbic acid (vitamin C, for collagen synthesis), L-glutamine and antibiotics). Cells for chondrocytes were seeded at 8 × 104 per well. In each experiment, a non-differentiating medium (Mesen Pro, MesenPRO RS™ Medium, Gibco, cat. no.12746012; composition: basal medium (DMEM/F12-based), 2% FBS, recombinant human growth factors (e.g., FGF-2), insulin, transferrin, selenium, antibiotics) was used as the control. Subsequently, cells were fixed with 4% PFA (paraformaldehyde) and stained with Red Oilfor adipocytes , and Alizarin Red for osteocytes . For quantitative analysis, the dye was eluted from the stained cells.
From adipocytes, the dye was eluted with isopropanol and read at OD490 nm. Stained osteocytes were treated with 10% HCl, then placed in a thermoblock at 85 °C/10 min, and successively cooled on ice. They were centrifuged, 10% ammonia water was added to 100 µl of filtrate, and absorbance was read at OD 450 nm .
The differentiated chondrocytes formed a micropellet, stained using 1% Alcian blue (30-minute incubation), which highlighted the glycosaminoglycans secreted by the chondrocytes. The dye was eluted by the addition of guanidine hydrochloride for six hours (with shaking) and read with a Synergy LX spectrophotometer at 620 nm .

## AD-MSCs and fibroblasts co-culture
To assess the effect of autologous AD-MSCs on fibroblasts, both cell types were placed in co-culture (Fig. l ). Fibroblasts were placed at the bottom of the culture well (Falcon cat. no. 353504 or 353046), while autologous AD-MSCs was placed on an insert (with a pore size 0.4 μm; Falcon, cat. no. 353495 ). Only for chemotaxis cells were replaced. The co-culture allows to analyze cells` interactions without direct contact between them. Cells were cultured in DMEM low glucose medium (Sigma-Aldrich, cat. no. D6046) with 3% FBS and antibiotics, for 24 h. Control were only fibroblasts (without AD-MSC presence). Fibroblasts were detached from the bottom of the culture well by trypsinization and then analyzed.

 

## Cell viability (XTT test)
The XTT assay (Cell Proliferation Kit II (XTT), Merck, cat. no. 11465015001) was used to assess fibroblast, AD-MSCs, and fibroblasts after coculture with AD-MSCs proliferation (based on mitochondrial activity). A freshly prepared XTT mixture was added to each well with cells and placed in a CO2 incubator for 4 h. After this period, the samples were read using a Synergy LX spectrophotometer at a 450 nm wavelength. The results were presented as a percentage of the control.

## EdU test
100,000 AD-MSCs (passage 2) were cultured in DMEM LG medium for 1 day, then 5 µl of EdU (BaseClick, cat. no. BCK-TCell-FC488) was added and incubated for the next 24 h. Next, cells were trypsinized, centrifuged, and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde solution for 15 min in the dark, at room temperature. Cells were centrifuged, supernatant was removed, and cells were permeabilized (in PBS-saponin solution) in the dark for 20 min. An EdU cocktail (containing dye – 6-FAM Azide) was added to the cells and incubated in the dark for 30 min. Finally, the cells were washed and analyzed by flow cytometry.

## Chemotaxis assay
To examine the effect of AD-MSCs on the chemotaxis of fibroblasts, fibroblasts were placed in inserts (ThinCert™ 8 μm, cat. no. 662 638, Greiner Bio-One Gm) in serum-free medium for 24 h before the experiment. Simultaneously, AD-MSCs were seeded separately on a 24-well plate (BD Falcon, cat. no. 353047) in DMEM low glucose, 10% FBS with antibiotics. The next day, inserts with fibroblasts were placed in wells with autologous AD-MSCs, in DMEM LG with 3% FBS, and antibiotics. Cells in co-culture were incubated for 24 h, at 37 °C and 5% CO2, in DMEM LG medium. After this time, the inserts were transferred to a new well, and the cells were stained with 8 µM Calcein-AM solution (Thermo Fisher Scientific, cat. no.C3099) for 45 min. Cells that had migrated through the membrane were detached with trypsin (500 µl for 10 min). Then, 200 µl of trypsin from each well was transferred to a 96-well plate, and fluorometric reading at 485/520 nm was performed using a Synergy LX spectrophotometer (Green Filter Cube). Control cells were fibroblasts with no AD-MSCs on the bottom of the culture plate.

## Migration assays
For migration studies, cells were seeded in inserts (Ibidi, Culture-Insert 4 Well in µ-Dish 35 mm, high, cat. no. 80466) in DMEM LG medium supplemented with 10% FBS at a density of 2 × 104 per insert chamber and incubated under standard conditions, approx. 24 h until appropriate confluence was achieved. Then, the inserts were removed using anatomical forceps, leaving defined cell-free gaps on the culture dish surface, and the cells were incubated for 2 h in medium supplemented with 5 µg/ml mitomycin C to block proliferation. After this time, the medium was removed into a conditioned (DMEM LG with 3% FBS, and antibiotics enriched with biologically active factors secreted by AD-MSCs during their growth in that medium for 48 h) or regular (fresh DMEM LG with 3% FBS, and antibiotics) medium for control. Cells were incubated under standard conditions (37 °C and 5% CO2) for 24 h. After the incubation time, the medium was removed, the cells were washed with PBS, and fixed for 30 min with 3.7% paraformaldehyde (PFA, ChemCruz, cat. no.: sc-281692). The cells were stained for 15 min with 0.05% crystal violet solution. The cells were analyzed under an inverted light microscope, where the surface area of the scratch (places where cells did not migrate) was outlined. The results were presented as the average of 4 independent measurements of the obtained surface areas.

## Cell cycle (PI staining) analysis
Cell cycle analysis was performed to assess the distribution of cells across different phases of the cell cycle. AD-MSCs isolated from healthy donors and from patients who had undergone chemotherapy were routinely passaged every 7 days. A total of 5 × 10⁵ cells at passage 2 were harvested and fixed in chilled 70% ethanol. Cells were incubated at -20 °C until flow cytometry analysis. On the analysis day, cells were washed in PBS and centrifuged to remove residual ethanol. The cell pellet was resuspended in PI (Propidium Iodide) - RNase dye for 0.5 h in the darkness. In the next step, the staining solution was removed by washing in PBS, and flow cytometric analysis was performed.

## Cytokines secretion (Luminex)
Luminex xMap technology (bead-based immunoassay) was used to evaluate the levels of cytokines and growth factors in the culture medium. MILLIPLEX® Human Cytokine/Chemokine/Growth Factor Panel A (HCYTA-60 K, Merck) kit, enabling simultaneous evaluation of EGF, FGF-2, GM-CSF, IL-10, IL-6, IL-8, PDGF-BB, TNF-alpha, and VEGF-A was used. After co-culture experiments, the culture medium was collected, centrifuged, and kept at -80˚C until the analysis. The analysis was performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions. It included overnight sample incubation with antibody-immobilized beads, followed by 1 h incubation with detection antibodies and 30 min incubation with streptavidin and phycoerythrin. The samples were acquired on MAGPIX® analyzer using Luminex xPONENT software. The concentration of analytes was calculated with MILLIPLEX analyst and expressed as pg/ml.

## Collagen synthesis
Second passage cells were seeded in a 96-well plate at 3 × 103 per well and cultured in standard DMEM LG medium with 2% FBS and antibiotics for 14 days. Then, cells were fixed with Bouin’s fluid, washed, and stained with Direct Red 80 (Sirius Red). They were shaken for 1 h and photographed, and next washed with 0.01 N HCl and the dye was eluted with 0.1 N NaCl for 30 min. It was examined spectrophotometrically at OD 550 nm.

## Isolation of RNA
Fibroblasts isolated from 10 patients undergoing chemotherapy and from 9 control patients (without neoadjuvant treatment) were analyzed. AD-MSCs isolated from 5 patients undergoing chemotherapy and from 6 control patients (without neoadjuvant treatment) were also analyzed. Total RNA was extracted using an RNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen, Cat No. 74104) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

## cDNA synthesis
The templates to estimate transcript levels were obtained by cDNA synthesis performed in a reaction mix containing (80–120 ng of RNA), 100 picomoles of oligo dT20, 4 µl of 5x reaction buffer (250 mM Tris-HCl, 375 mM KCl, 15 mM MgCl2, 50 mM DTT), and 200 units of Maxima Reverse Transcriptase (ThermoScientific, Cat.No. EP0742) in a final volume of 20 µl.

## Quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction
Real-time PCR reactions were carried out in a final volume of 10 µl containing 5 µl of FastStart Essential DNA Green Master (Roche, Cat. No. 06402712001), 2 µl of cDNA, and 0.25 µl each of forward and reverse primers (10 µM) on a LightCycler LC96 (Roche). The transcript levels were calculated using the 2− dCt method relative to housekeeping genes: RPLP0 and RPS18. In the 2− dCt formula, dCt is the difference between the cycle in which the amplification enters the exponential phase between the analyzed transcript and the reference gene. The primer sequences are listed in Supplemental Information (Suppl. Table   S). qPCR reactions were performed in triplicate.

## Statistical analysis
Data are presented as mean ± SD. Statistical analysis was performed using the Mann-Whitney U test, and * p < 0.05 was considered significant (Statistica 13.3). Statistical significance was determined assuming a two-tailed distribution. Graphical visualization of the data was performed using GraphPad Prism 10 software.

## Results

## Analysis of the mesenchymal phenotype of cells isolated from adipose tissue
AD-MSCs from all patients exhibited a mesenchymal phenotype, as confirmed by their ability to differentiate into osteocytes, chondrocytes, and adipocytes, their adhesion to the substrate, and the presence of characteristic surface markers (Suppl. Figure 1 S). All AD-MSCs complied with the International Society for Cell and Gene Therapy (ISCT) criteria .

## Impact of neoadjuvant chemotherapy on AD-MSCs activity

## Surface markers expression
Immunophenotypic analysis was performed to identify potential differences in surface marker expression between stromal cells isolated from adipose tissue of patients who had not undergone chemotherapy and those who had received neoadjuvant chemotherapy. The analysis of a broad panel of surface antigens (Suppl.Table S), showed no significant differences between stromal cells isolated from adipose tissue of control patients and those who had undergone chemotherapy. The surface marker CD14, an inflammatory response modulator, showed a statistically significant increase (p < 0.028) in patients after chemotherapy .

## Viability assay
The XTT assay was performed to assess the effect of chemotherapy on AD-MSCs cell viability (Fig.  A).
 AD-MSCs isolated from tissue obtained from patients who received neoadjuvant chemotherapy showed no difference in mitochondrial activity compared to cells derived from CHT unexposed donors at day 1 (similar results were obtained in EdU assay - Suppl. Figure 2 S). However, mitochondrial activity increased more in the cells from donors undergoing chemotherapy at later time points, reaching a significant difference at day 3 (Fig.  A).

## Cell cycle
Cell cycle analysis aimed at quantifying the percentage of cells in different phases (resting (G0/G1), replicating (S), dividing (G2/M), and decaying (subG0)) between both groups.
Cell cycle analysis of AD-MSCs from patients unexposed to CHT and from patients after neoadjuvant chemotherapy revealed no biologically meaningful differences in the distribution of cells across cell-cycle phases (Fig.  B, D). Although a statistically significant difference was observed in the sub-G0 fraction, the magnitude of this change was very small and is unlikely to be of clinical or functional relevance.

## Cytokines secretion
Mesenchymal stem cells are known for their paracrine function. These cells secrete many growth factors, cytokines, and chemokines, contributing to immune modulation, tissue remodeling, and cellular homeostasis during regeneration . As demonstrated in Fig. C, chemotherapy did not alter the secretory profile of AD-MSCs.

## Gene expression changes
To evaluate the impact of neoadjuvant chemotherapy on the activity and transcriptomic profiles of AD-MSCs, the expression level of selected genes related to wound healing and skin tissue regeneration, such as those of growth factors, growth factor receptors, pluripotency, and proliferative markers, was analyzed (Fig.  ).
 This analysis showed that chemotherapy causes only a trend in decreasing the expression of some growth factor receptors, including CSF2RA, EGFR, PDGFRA, the pluripotency markers NANOG, and SOX2, and TP63 (Fig.  ). Overall, chemotherapy does not appear to have significant effects on the expression of the selected genes in AD-MSCs.

## Impact of chemotherapy on fibroblasts` activity

## Surface markers expression
We profiled fibroblasts from 16 patients, and showed that chemotherapy mostly did not alter the expression of their surface markers, as indicated by the absence of significant differences in either the percentages of positive cells or mean fluorescence intensity (Suppl. Table   S).

## Viability assay
The mitochondrial activity of fibroblasts isolated from the skin of patients who had undergone neoadjuvant chemotherapy was also analyzed, and a statistically significant increase was observed (Fig.  A).
 

## Fibroblast cytokine secretion
A previous studies have shown that chemotherapy can alter the secretory profile of fibroblasts – , we assessed the expression levels of a few factors involved in wound healing responses (Fig. B). We found that chemotherapy alters the secretory profile of fibroblasts, as the levels of IL-6, IL-10, TNFalpha, FGF-2 tended to increase, while EGF, GM-CSF, IL-8 tended to decrease, although these differences are not statistically significant (Fig. B).

## Migration, chemotaxis, and collagen production
The ability of cells to move is an important process in recovery, especially in wound healing and regeneration , .
The chemotaxis test (Boyden chamber) allows us to assess whether co-culture with AD-MSCs and the presence of compounds secreted into the medium stimulate the directed migration of fibroblasts. Obtained results showed that chemotactic capacity of fibroblasts isolated from patients after chemotherapy was reduced, but without statistical significance (Fig.  C). By performing scratch assays, we showed that the unclosed wound area was significantly larger in patients exposed to chemotherapy compared to those without chemotherapy, which suggests an impaired migration capacity of fibroblasts after neoadjuvant chemotherapy (Fig.  D).
Fibroblasts can be potentially altered by chemotherapy, so we decided to perform a chemotaxis assay to evaluate how/if their ability of has been changed .
As fibroblasts are the main cells responsible for collagen synthesis, we investigated whether collagen production is modulated by exposure to chemotherapy. Collagen production by fibroblasts from patients after chemotherapy was decreased compared to the control group, but the difference was not statistically significant (Fig.  E).
Analysis of gene expression changes showed that chemotherapy causes a trend in decreasing the expression of some growth factors, including GMCSF, EGF, PDGFB; growth factor receptors, including CSF2RA, EGFR, PDGFRA, PDGFRB; the pluripotency markers NANOG, POU5F1 (OCT4) and SOX2; markers of proliferative potential TP63 and ITGB1 (CD29) (Fig.  ).
 

## Impact of AD-MSCs on fibroblast activity in a co-culture system

## Viability assay
In fibroblasts obtained from donors without chemotherapy, the co-culture with autologous AD-MSCs, had a stimulatory effect, as observed by an increase in total mitochondrial fibroblast activity in cell culture. In samples after chemotherapy, the co-culture showed an inhibitory effect on fibroblasts (Fig.  A). Considering the altered mitochondrial activity of fibroblasts by neoadjuvant chemotherapy, the result suggests an additional protective effect of AD-MSCs.
 

## Migration, Chemotaxis, and collagen production
The wound area in the presence of AD-MSCs slightly decreased, indicating a stimulating effect of stromal cells on fibroblasts` migration. In contrast, the opposite effect was obtained for fibroblasts isolated from patients after chemotherapy (Fig.  B). The increased wound area suggests a reduced ability of fibroblasts to migrate as a result of chemotherapy.
We also evaluated whether a two-day co-culture with autologous AD-MSC affected the chemotactic capacity of fibroblasts. Fibroblasts co-cultured with AD-MSCs showed a slight increase in chemotactic activity in both groups (Fig.  C).
AD-MSCs slightly impaired the ability of fibroblasts to produce collagen in neoadjuvant chemotherapy treated patients only, but the effect was not statistically significant (Fig.  D).

## Flow cytometric analysis of fibroblasts’ surface markers after co-culture with AD-MSCs
Flow cytometric analysis of fibroblasts was performed to assess the effect of 48 h co-culture with autologous AD-MSCs. The presence of AD-MSCs in culture did not alter the expression of surface markers regardless of the analyzed group (Table  ).
 

## Gene expression changes
To assess the in vitro impact of adipose-derived stromal cells (AD-MSCs) on the activity and transcriptomic profile of skin fibroblasts, gene expression was analyzed in fibroblasts isolated from oncological patients undergoing neoadjuvant chemotherapy, and after co-culture with autologous AD-MSCs (Fig.  ). The control group consisted of fibroblasts and AD-MSCs collected from patients without neoadjuvant treatment, and their autologous co-culture.
 The expression level of most of the analyzed markers was reduced in fibroblasts with chemotherapy, both in standard cultures and in co-cultures with AD-MSCs. BNC1 and MYC, which showed reverse responses (increase) only in fibroblast culture, in co-culture there was also a reduction in both genes. (Fig.  ). Lower expression levels of growth factors GMCSF (GM-CSF2), PDGFB (statistically significant), EGF, PDGFA, growth factor receptors and pluripotency markers NANOG, SOX2, POU5F1 (OCT4), markers of proliferative potential ITGB1 (CD29) (statistically significant), ITGA6 (CD49f), TP63 were found in fibroblasts from patients after chemotherapy (Fig.  ).
The introduction of AD-MSCs into fibroblast cultures from patients without chemotherapy resulted in increased expression of GM-CSF and EGF, accompanied by decreased expression of PDGFB. In contrast, fibroblasts obtained after chemotherapy exhibited reduced VEGFA expression in co-culture. In fibroblasts collected from patients without chemotherapy, under the influence of AD-MSC cells, an increase in the expression of growth factors GMCSF (GM-CSF2), VEGFA, EGF, PDGFA (Fig.  ), SCF2RA, EGFR, PDGFRB, SOX2, POU5F1 (OCT4), ITGA6 (CD49f), TP63 (Fig.  ) was observed. This indicates that chemotherapy reduces the potential of AD-MSC to stimulate transcripts associated with wound healing and tissue regeneration.

## Discussion and conclusions
Chemotherapy is a commonly employed treatment modality in oncology. Despite their clinical efficacy, chemotherapeutics are associated with numerous side effects, including skin toxicity . This study aimed to investigate how oncological treatments affect adipose-derived stromal cells and whether the application of these cells can positively influence skin cells.
A comparison of AD-MSCs isolated from patients not exposed and exposed to chemotherapy demonstrated that stromal cells are resistant to the effects of neoadjuvant treatment. Prior chemotherapy did not significantly affect the expression of surface markers (Suppl. Data, Table S), cell cycle (Fig. B, D), or cytokine secretion profile (Fig. C) of AD-MSCs. The broad panel of surface markers remained unchanged under treatment, indicating the stability of all stemness markers. These findings are consistent with the still limited number of existing studies addressing this issue .
AD-MSCs from chemotherapy-treated patients exhibited faster cell growth rates (Fig. A) compared to the control group. AD-MSCs from patients treated with chemotherapy showed increased mitochondrial activity (Fig. A) compared to controls. Similar results were obtained by Komoto et al.. in fibroblasts after radiotherapy and by Moss et al. in tumor cells. Metabolic reprogramming - increased biogenesis and mitochondrial function - can occur after chemotherapy, leading to chemotherapy resistance by promoting oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) and cellular energy requirements . This is an important aspect in determining the potential of stem cells for reconstruction or stem cell-based therapy in cancer patients.
Molecular studies analyzing the expression levels of genes involved in the wound-healing process, such as growth factors, their receptors, pluripotency markers, and markers of proliferative potential, showed that chemotherapy does not significantly impact the expression of these factors in AD-MSCs (Fig.  ). Only a slight, statistically insignificant decrease in the expression of growth factor receptors (CSF2RA, EGFR, PDGFRA), pluripotency markers (NANOG, SOX2, TP63), and markers of proliferative potential was observed after chemotherapy treatment. These findings suggest that AD-MSCs may exhibit resistance to the detrimental effects of chemotherapy agents.
The resistance of AD-MSC to chemotherapy has not yet been explained due to insufficient research on this phenomenon. However, it is believed that this process is influenced by the microenvironment created by the cells. It is thought that the paracrine properties of the cells enhance DNA repair and upregulate multidrug resistance (MDR) transporters . In addition, the release of polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs) to remove reactive oxygen species (ROS) protects cells from cytotoxic damage. Additionally, AD-MSCs stiffen the extracellular matrix (ECM) via metalloproteinases (MMPs), creating physical barriers that hinder drug penetration .
Adipose-derived stromal cells appear to retain their important characteristics and are a promising source of autologous stem cells for fat grafting and reconstruction, in oncology patients. The above findings highlight that AD-MSCs are promising cells for regenerative medicine even after chemotherapy, which does not reduce their properties.
As demonstrated in numerous studies, neoadjuvant treatment does affect fibroblast activity. Cytotoxic antitumor effects act not only on cancer cells, but also on fibroblasts , . The harmful effects of chemotherapy result in delayed wound healing. Chemotherapeutics can inhibit cell metabolism, cell division, angiogenesis, can lead to nerve damage, interfere with cell migration into the wound, reduce extracellular matrix production, and alter cell function at the molecular level (interfering with replication, transcription, translation, and cell signaling) . Studies showed that fibroblasts can retain long-term memory of cytotoxic exposure in the form of epigenetic modifications, which impair their tissue repair capacity even years after treatment .
Our study showed the detrimental effect of chemotherapy on fibroblasts, which had reduced most of analyzed biological activities. Chemotherapy significantly reduced the migratory capacity of cells (Fig. D). The chemotactic capacity of fibroblasts and collagen production were also impaired without significance (Fig. E). These functional deficiencies contribute to the wound healing difficulties commonly observed in patients undergoing oncological treatment. Similar results were observed by other researchers , .
Flow cytometric analysis of the surface markers showed their stability after chemotherapy. The only exception was the CD26 (dipeptidylpeptidase IV) antigen, which was downregulated by 4.5% after chemotherapy (Suppl. Data, Table S). The function of CD26 in the skin has not been fully elucidated, but previous work suggests its association with secreted cytokines. Sorrel, Caplan et al. showed that IL-6, TNFα, and TGFβ decrease CD26 expression . Our results are similar - we noted a parallel increase in TNFα and Il-6 (additionally in IL-10) cytokine secretion by fibroblasts after chemotherapy and a concomitant decrease in CD26. According to other researchers, CD26 expression on fibroblasts is associated with a subpopulation of fibroblasts associated with a higher capacity for collagen production (high COL1A1 levels) . Our observation of a reduction in collagen production capacity and a concomitant decrease in CD26 in fibroblasts isolated from chemotherapy patients seems to support this theory.
Fibroblasts after chemotherapy showed reduced secretion of some cytokines (EGF, GM-CSF, IL-8, and VEGF-A) (Fig. B), which further may affect wound healing – . Previous studies have indicated that chemotherapy may induce changes in healthy fibroblasts, such as increased secretion of inflammatory cytokines (e.g. IL-6) and enhanced glycolytic activity . The metabolic transformation of stromal fibroblasts into cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAF) alters the tumor microenvironment, promoting tumor growth and metastasis , .
Chemotherapy-induced changes in fibroblasts demonstrate a significant impairment of the biological properties of the cells, resulting in delayed wound healing. Fibroblasts taken from chemotherapy patients have numerous limitations in clinical use.
Having demonstrated that AD-MSC cells, in contrast to fibroblasts, are more stable upon chemotherapy, we wanted to find out how the presence of autologous stem cells affects fibroblasts and what the difference is between co-cultures of CHT unexposed cells and those after chemotherapy.
Autologous co-culture of AD-MSCs and fibroblasts from control donors stimulated fibroblast activity compared to fibroblasts alone. The presence of stem cells stimulates fibroblast proliferation (Fig.  A), migration, and chemotaxis (Fig.  B, C), but does not affect collagen (Fig.  D) production. The co-culture, regardless of the donor’s health status, is neutral to the expression of surface markers (Table  ). In the light of the obtained results, the presence of CHT unexposed AD-MSCs exerts a stimulating, pro-proliferative, and supportive effect on fibroblasts. For a clinical perspective, information indicating AD-MSCs as a profitable factor in co-culture with fibroblasts is crucial. Moreover, autologous tissue transplants supported by AD-MSCs do not pose a risk, instead, they are expected to promote regeneration.
There are still a limited number of publications addressing the effect of AD-MSC co-culture on fibroblasts, and conclusions are often inconsistent. Successful studies using AD-MSCs to treat post-chemotherapy lesions are emerging . Other studies suggest that the conditioned media derived from AD-MSC cultures may be more effective than direct co-culture in stimulating fibroblast activity .
Co-culture with AD-MSCs resulted in a (statistically insignificant) increase in the transcription levels of GM-CSF (GM-CSF2), PDGFA, and PDGFB in fibroblasts from chemotherapy-treated samples (Fig.  ). In fibroblasts from patients without chemotherapy, co-culture with AD-MSCs led to an increase in the expression of growth factors (GM-CSF [GM-CSF2], VEGFA, EGF, PDGFA), receptors (SCF2RA, EGFR, PDGFRB), pluripotency markers (SOX2, POU5F1 [OCT4]), and markers of proliferative potential (ITGA6 [CD49f], TP63) (Fig.  ).
In autologous co-cultures of fibroblasts and AD-MSCs obtained from patients after chemotherapy, most of the analyzed biological activities of fibroblasts (viability, migration, collagen production) were impaired. These results were unexpected and undoubtedly require further insights.
As in earlier analyses, no effect of the presence of AD-MSCs on surface markers was demonstrated (Table  ).
In our study, a decrease was also observed in the expression of growth factors GM-CSF (GM-CSF2), PDGFB, EGF, PDGFA, and all growth factor receptors in post-chemotherapy fibroblasts. A statistically significant decrease was observed in the expression of PDGFB and PDGFRB. Additionally, the expression of pluripotency markers (NANOG, SOX2, POU5F1 [OCT4]), as well as markers of proliferative potential (ITGB1 [CD29], ITGA6 [CD49f], TP63) was reduced in post-chemotherapy fibroblasts in co-culture.
This study demonstrates that AD-MSCs retain their functional properties after chemotherapy and are relatively resistant to its effects. Our in vitro studies showed only limited effects of AD-MSCs on fibroblasts, likely due to the differences between in vitro and in vivo environments, the short time of interaction, and possibly the insufficient release of paracrine mediators such as exosomes.
However, some limitations should be acknowledged. The findings are based solely on in vitro experiments, and their translational relevance requires further validation in vivo. In addition, the experimental setup does not fully reflect the complexity of the tissue microenvironment. More advanced models, such as organoid-based systems, and co-cultures including immune cells, may provide a more comprehensive assessment of AD-MSCs- mediated effects.
In conclusion, this study enhances our understanding of mesenchymal stromal stem cells at both the immunophenotypic and molecular levels, providing a framework for future mechanistic and translational studies.
