## Significance
The capsular polysaccharide (CPS) is the vaccine target for pathogens like Streptococcus pneumoniae and Haemophilus influenzae. The prevailing view is that CPSs are overall negatively charged, thereby repelling mucus and promoting colonization. As some of them are neutral or zwitterionic, we propose these surface glycans function beyond their electronegativity. Here, glycan motifs important for bacterial adhesion and survival on human airway epithelial cells were systematically identified using a large collection of capsule-switch mutants. We also demonstrated that the CPS reduces cytokine and chemokine release from epithelial cells. Elucidating critical glycan motifs in the CPS will inform vaccine development and help explain the usage bias of sugar residues and linkages in prokaryotic glycans.

Virtually all living cells are encased in glycans. They perform key cellular functions such as immunomodulation and cell–cell recognition. Yet, how their composition and configuration affect their functions remains enigmatic. Here, we constructed isogenic capsule-switch mutants harboring 84 types of capsular polysaccharides (CPSs) in Streptococcus pneumoniae. This collection enables us to systematically measure the affinity of structurally related CPSs to primary human nasal and bronchial epithelial cells. Contrary to the paradigm, the surface charge does not appreciably affect epithelial cell binding. Factors that affect adhesion to respiratory cells include the number of rhamnose residues and the presence of human-like glycomotifs in CPS. Besides, pneumococcal colonization stimulated the production of interleukin 6 (IL-6), granulocyte-macrophage colony-stimulating factor (GM-CSF), and monocyte chemoattractantprotein-1 (MCP-1) in nasal epithelial cells, which also appears to be dependent on the serotype. Together, our results reveal glycomotifs of surface polysaccharides that are likely to be important for colonization and survival in the human airway.

It is a leading cause of community-acquired pneumonia that results in nearly a million children's deaths worldwide annually ( ). The most susceptible cohorts to invasive pneumococcal diseases (IPDs) are infants, immunocompromised individuals, and the elderly. IPDs are thought to begin with asymptomatic colonization of S. pneumoniae at the nasopharynx ( , ). After influenza infections ( ), or other events that may compromise the host’s immunity, S. pneumoniae can migrate to the lower respiratory tract and cause pneumonia, septicemia, and meningitis ( , ). Colonization is also a major driver of transmission ( , ), which can last for months in some individuals ( , , ). It was estimated that up to 40% of infants and 10% of adults have S. pneumoniae found in their nasopharynx at any given time ( ). These figures are likely an underestimation, as the advancement of technology continues to push the detection limit of pneumococci colonized in the upper respiratory tract ( ).
One way to reduce colonization and transmission of S. pneumoniae is to administer pneumococcal conjugate vaccines (PCVs) ( , ). These vaccines target the capsular polysaccharide (CPS) on the bacterial surface and presumably promote clearance by agglutination ( ). Given S. pneumoniae can produce over 100 types of serologically distinct CPS ( , ), PCVs are only effective against the serotypes included in the formulation, or the “vaccine types” ( ). Consequently, serotype replacement ( ) and capsule-switching ( ) slowly undermine the efficacies of PCVs. Efforts to expand the coverage of PCVs include the development of PCV-15 ( ) and PCV-20 ( ). Yet, identifying the next problematic serotypes is challenging because the abundance of pneumococcal serotypes is geographically and temporally highly dynamic ( ). The decision of which serotypes to be included in the formulation is mostly based on their prevalence in Western countries and their antibiotic-resistant profiles ( ). An improved understanding of the structure–function relationship of CPS will therefore guide vaccine development by revealing glycan patterns that are associated with colonization and IPDs.
Epidemiology studies have identified a strong correlation between serotype and invasiveness ( , , ). In general, negatively charged CPS with fewer carbon and high energy bonds are considered favorable, as they are associated with increased prevalence and the likelihood of IPDs ( ). Yet, exactly how sugar composition and glycosidic linkages contribute to colonization remains unclear. Mechanistic studies to link CPS structures and pathogenicity were mostly performed with clinical isolates or a relatively small set of isogenic capsule-switch mutants ( ). While they provided useful insights into the interplay between CPS and the host, interpretation of the results can be difficult because the pneumococcal genome varies significantly among clinical isolates ( , , ). So far, there is no systematic investigation into the composition of CPS that affects attachment to human respiratory epithelial cells. To address this question, we generated 258 isogenic capsule-switch mutants that display one of the 84 capsule types, covering most of the 101 known serotypes in S. pneumoniae. The mutants were then molecular barcoded to facilitate rapid identification. Next, their ability to bind human nasal epithelial cells (hNECs) and bronchial epithelial cells (hBECs) was assessed. Our systematic approach enabled us to study glycans with very subtle differences, subsequently revealing glycan motifs that are important for colonization and survival on primary epithelial cells. In addition, we observed CPS modestly masks the innate response of hNECs. Together, our work may help explain the bias of sugar residue and glycosidic linkage usage in surface glycans of pathogenic bacteria.

## Results

## Establishing a Platform to Investigate Pneumococcal Adhesion on Human Airway Epithelial Cells.
Many studies on host–pathogen interaction in pneumococcus were conducted using cancer cell lines like A549 and NCI-H292 (reviewed in ref.  ). Although they are fast-growing and easy to handle, they require a fully submerged condition to survive and propagate, rendering them fundamentally different from the human respiratory lining ( ). Moreover, to establish stable colonization on NCI-H292 cells, pneumococci need to be “preconditioned” by passaging them on a layer of formalin-fixed cells, or else they would eradicate the cell line shortly after coculturing ( , ). The preconditioning step likely affects the physiology of the bacteria, as there is only ~25% of the capsule that remains attached to the pneumococci afterward ( ). We reasoned that hNECs and hBECs models may circumvent these problems ( ) because when arranged in transwells, these cells could be differentiated into a multilayer, pseudostratified epithelium that comprised beating cilia and a mucus layer ( ). These cells also formed an air-liquid interface (ALI) that closely resembles nasal and bronchial environments ( , ). hBECs are similar to hNECs, except they produced bronchiole-like intrusions, some of which are located underneath the ciliated cells. These characteristics were confirmed by immunofluorescence microscopy using cell type-specific antibodies ( ). To collect progenitor cells, we performed airway biopsies from ≈100 healthy individuals and fractionated the airway stem/progenitor cells for differentiation. Unlike NCI-H292 and A549 cells, hNECs and hBECs tolerated S. pneumoniae colonization for at least 24 h without preconditioning, as judged by their viability in the coculture ( and SI Appendix, Fig. S1). The nasal epithelial cells could survive longer if the basal medium was changed more frequently (SI Appendix, Fig. S1). In addition, pneumococci remained viable on hNECs for at least 48 h post-infection ( ). Thus, the hNECs and hBECs models enable long-term colonization experiments of pneumococci on epithelial cells.
 As a proof of principle, we monitored the colonization of three isogenic mutants expressing serotype 2, 3, and 19A capsules on hNECs and compared it with the unencapsulated parent. Pneumococci were overlain on hNECs at the ALI and the coculture was incubated for ~20 h. Bacteria loosely attached were collected by rinsing the transwells with buffer, and the tightly bound pneumococci were then harvested by scraping the cells. As expected, the Δcps mutant strongly adhered to hNECs, likely because CPS occludes most adhesins on the cell surface ( , ). A notable exception is pili, which is absent in the serotype 2 strain D39 ( ). Cells expressing serotype 2 and 3 CPS exhibited weaker binding than serotype 19A, indicating that the capsule type might affect their affinity towards hNECs. Similar results were obtained when strains were mixed before infection on the epithelial cells ( ).

## Construction of Isogenic CPS-Switch Mutants and Molecular Barcoding.
Next, we investigated whether changes in the CPS structure would affect pneumococcal colonization on hNECs. To do this, we constructed isogenic CPS-switch mutants by allelic exchange ( ). Apart from serotype 37, cps genes in S. pneumoniae are organized into a single operon located between dexB and aliA ( , ). Thus, switching the cps locus is sufficient to convert the serotype ( ). We generated strain NUS0114 [rpsL1
∆cps::P-sacB-kan-rpsL+] by replacing the cps locus with a Sweet Janus cassette ( ). Next, we transformed the strain with purified genomic DNA of the clinical isolates harboring different capsule types to introduce a noncognate cps locus. The presence of the noncognate cps locus was validated by PCR and sequencing, and the production of the engineered CPS was confirmed by immunostaining (SI Appendix, Fig. S2). To eliminate unwanted genetic fragments that might be introduced into the strains by rare cotransformation events, we purified genomic DNA from the CPS-switch mutants and used it to transform NUS0114 again (i.e., “backcrossing”). This process removed potential genetic contamination because the cps locus is the only genetic element under the selection ( ). A sampling of three CPS-switch mutant genomes confirmed that there was no additional genetic alteration other than the engineered cps locus (accession number PRJNA841444 for strains NUS1130, NUS0276, and NUS0282, respectively).
 To facilitate multiplex detection and quantitation of CPS-switch mutants, we engineered a unique nine base pairs barcode downstream of a kan-rpsL+ cassette at the ectopic locus (CEP::P-kan-rpsL+-barcode) (SI Appendix, Fig. S3). The barcodes introduced were confirmed by Sanger sequencing. We showed that the number of the barcode reads recovered faithfully reported the number of bacteria in the mixture ( ) and the measurements were highly reproducible ( ) like other studies using Bar-seq ( ). It has been known that capsule production reduces the growth rate ( ). Consistently, several serotypes such as serotypes 2, 6C, and 34 grew slower (SI Appendix, Fig. S4 and Table S3). Nevertheless, the barcode abundance of many CPS-switch mutants is within twofold after passaging for multiple generations (SI Appendix, Fig. S4), suggesting that they likely have similar growth rates compared with the ∆cps parent. The barcode sequencing results were validated by measuring the doubling time of 13 isogenic CPS-switch mutants and the unencapsulated parent strain (SI Appendix, Table S3). Interestingly, serotypes 3 and 13 grew slightly faster than other strains in the library (SI Appendix, Fig. S4 and Table S3). Their marginally faster growth rates could be recapitulated in 14ml culture tubes in 5% CO2, but not in 96-well plates (SI Appendix, Fig. S4 and Table S3), possibly due to differences in oxygen and CO2 levels. In conclusion, the barcodes can be used as an estimate of the relative abundance of CPS-switch mutants.

## The CPS Determines the Cell Surface Charge of Pneumococcus.
We then examined the surface charge of the CPS-switch mutants by measuring their zeta potentials. The zeta potential is an estimate of the electrophoretic mobility of particles in solution under an electric field, which serves as an indicator of the surface electronegativity ( ). Pneumococcal cells were inactivated, washed, and loaded into a flow cell, where their electrophoretic light scattering was determined ( ). When available, our zeta potential measurements generally agree with the values reported by the laboratory of Marc Lipsitch ( ). This implies that noncapsule elements do not appreciably interfere with zeta potential measurements, as the strains from the two studies are from different genetic backgrounds. Also, the chain lengths did not appear to affect zeta-potential measurements (SI Appendix, Fig. S5). Most of the CPS-switch mutants have a negative zeta potential ranging from −10 to −20 mV. As expected, cells that display neutral capsules (serotypes 7F, 7A, 14, 33F, and 33A) have higher zeta potentials. The serotype 16F CPS has two phosphate groups per repeating unit and half of the serotype 3 CPS consists of negatively charged glucuronic acid. Consistently, they are more electronegative ( ). Intriguingly, the zeta potential of the ∆cps strain is similar to most of the encapsulated strain. This is likely because of the negatively charged phosphocholine residues in teichoic acids ( ). A negatively charged cell surface is thought to be associated with increased prevalence and invasiveness of the strain ( ). However, we did not observe a strong correlation between these factors (SI Appendix, Fig. S6). A plausible explanation is that the expression of certain types of CPS may lead to slower growth. They may interfere with the synthesis of teichoic acids or peptidoglycan as they share the same lipid carrier (SI Appendix, Fig. S4 and Table S3). Nevertheless, some capsule types did not impair growth, indicating that the capsule composition may play a role in adhesion and pathogenesis that goes beyond altering the overall charge of the cell surface.
 

## Structural Features of the Cell Surface Glycans that Modulate Binding to the Human Respiratory Cells.
To investigate how the CPS affects adhesion, barcoded CPS-switch mutants were pooled and inoculated at the ALI of hNECs and hBECs. We observed little binding within the first hour of coculture (SI Appendix, Fig. S7). Thus, we collected the loosely and tightly adhered bacteria after 20 h of incubation, similar to the pilot experiments ( ). Genomic DNA was purified from each fraction and the barcodes were amplified and sequenced. Reads were normalized and converted to the number of colony-forming units (CFUs), followed by comparing their relative abundance ( ). While there are subtle differences in the binding affinity of pneumococcus to hNECs and hBECs, the two datasets strongly correlate with each other ( ; ρ = 0.7575 and P < 0.0001).
 As expected, the unencapsulated mutant bound tighter to both hNECs and hBECs. Consistent with our pilot experiment ( ), the serotype 19A mutant showed a stronger affinity to respiratory cells than serotype 2 and 3 strains ( ). Most CPS-switch mutants have similar binding affinities. Distinguished from a typical capsule are serotypes 2, 8, and 31, which bound epithelial cells weaker. On the contrary, serotype 5, 11F, 14, and 15C capsules are associated with tighter adhesion. Binding to hNECs and hBECs does not seem to directly correlate with prevalence, the number of carbon per repeating unit, and the number of high energy bonds per repeating unit (SI Appendix, Fig. S6). In addition, the differences in binding cannot be explained by the variation in CPS amount (SI Appendix, Fig. S8) or cell chaining (SI Appendix, Fig. S5), indicating the CPS structure (or chemical composition) is more likely to be causative. Interestingly, serotype 34 and ∆cps cells survived better on hNECs and hBECs (SI Appendix, Fig. S9). On the other hand, several serotypes were underrepresented after coculturing (SI Appendix, Fig. S9). Some of them were not due to growth defects in brain heart infusion (BHI) broth or in the same medium for growing hNECs and hBECs (Pneumacult) (SI Appendix, Figs. S4 and S10 and Table S3), suggesting they may be more susceptible to innate responses like antimicrobial peptides. Alternatively, the respiratory cells may somehow create an environment that causes these strains to lyse. More experiments will need to be done to test these hypotheses. Yet, survival on hNECs and hBECs only affected adhesion modestly (SI Appendix, Fig. S10), indicating that these two processes are not linked. In conclusion, we showed that the capsule in general reduces bacterial adhesion. This is likely independent of the CPS amount. However, some of the CPSs are more favorable in binding to epithelial cells, perhaps because they have specialized structures.
Intriguingly, serotypes 2 and 31 CPS are rich in rhamnose. This prompted us to investigate whether rhamnose residues correlate with adhesion. Indeed, the ability to bind epithelial cells is inversely proportional to the number of rhamnose residues ( ). On the other hand, capsules that are associated with stronger binding strikingly resemble human glycans. For example, the serotype 11F CPS contains an isoglobotriosylceramide motif [iGb3; Gal(α1-3)Gal(β1-4)Glc], which is the precursor of iGb4 (GlyGen 000083). iGb4 is thought to play an important role in the metastasis of cancer cells in the lung ( ). The milk sugar core [GlyGen 001029; Gal(β1-4)Glc] is also frequently found at the reducing end of human glycans, such as galactosylgloboside Gb5 and type 4 blood group H antigen. Moreover, the serotype 14 CPS is nearly identical to lacto-N-neotetraose (paragloboside; nLC4) ( ). The same glycan motif can be found in serotype 15C, suggesting nLC4 may facilitate respiratory cells binding. In contrast, despite the serotype 8 CPS comprising sugar residues commonly found in the human glycome (Glc, Gal, and GlcUA), we could not detect any human-like glycan motif after searching it against the GlyTouCan server ( ) and it binds weaker to epithelial cells. Thus, we propose several human-like glycan motifs facilitate adhesion, whereas rhamnose residues that are unique to bacteria are likely disadvantageous.
 To validate the results, we attempted to block bacterial adhesion with anti-capsule antisera. Nevertheless, the antisera inhibited adhesion nonspecifically and we were unable to resolve this issue (SI Appendix, Fig. S11). Thus, we individually inoculated nine CPS-switch mutants to hNECs and hBECs. We included serotype 1 here because it was underrepresented in the pool of capsule-switch mutants. The data from the validation experiment generally reproduced the Bar-seq results with a correlation coefficient of 0.85 and 0.73 for hNECs and hBECs, respectively ( ). Consistently, there is a correlation between hNECs and hBECs binding (SI Appendix, Fig. S12). We also confirmed that the serotype 14 CPS adhered stronger than serotype 2 on hNECs, yet the effect was less pronounced on hBECs. Finally, serotype 1 CPS is associated with tighter binding to hNECs and hBECs compared with serotype 2, 47F, and 22F. Together, our results indicate that capsule types affect pneumococcal colonization on airway epithelial cells, likely through glycan–glycan or glycan–protein interactions.
 

## CPS Dampens the Innate Immunity of Nasal Epithelial Cells.
Monocytes and macrophages respond to S. pneumoniae through the Iκβζ pathway by secreting interleukin 6 (IL-6) and granulocyte-macrophage colony-stimulating factor (GM-CSF) ( ). Yet, in hBECs Iκβζ was not induced during pneumococcal colonization, implying this may be a cell type-specific response ( ). We wondered whether S. pneumoniae colonization could trigger innate responses in hNECs, as they are presumably the first line of defense at the nasopharynx. Cytokine and chemokine levels were profiled after hNECs were exposed to unencapsulated, serotype 2, and serotype 14 pneumococci. Unlike hBECs ( ), IL-6, GM-CSF, and monocyte chemoattractant protein-1 (MCP-1/Chemokine (C-C motif) ligand 2; CCL2) were readily detected in the basal medium of hNECs after colonization of the ∆cps mutant ( and SI Appendix, Fig. S13). The capsule blocked IL-6 and MCP-1 responses, whereas the serotype 14 CPS seems to have a weaker effect. To validate the results of cytokine profiling, we measured the levels of IL-6, MCP-1, and GM-CSF in the basal medium by Enzyme-Linked Immunosorbent Assay (ELISA) ( ). While the overall trend corroborates the speculation that the CPS blocks cytokine release, the differences are not statistically significant. There was however a mild reduction of MCP-1 levels when comparing ∆cps cells and cells expressing serotype 1, 2, 5, and 14 CPS, highlighting a potential role of CPS in modulating the innate immunity of hNECs.
 

## Discussion
As the outermost layer of the cell, the capsule serves as the interface between pneumococcus and the host. This layer facilitates immune evasion by blocking opsonophagocytosis and complement deposition ( , , ). Compelling epidemiological and empirical evidence supports the notion that the CPS modulates adhesion to the respiratory lining ( , , , , , ), thereby shaping nasopharynx colonization of pneumococcus. However, exactly what structural features in the glycan affect host cell adhesion remain elusive. In this study, we sought to identify the “glycoletter” and “glycowords” ( ) that govern attachment to the human airway epithelial cells.

## Glycan Motifs that Affect Epithelial Cell Binding.
We found that glycans containing an nLC4 motif, such as the serotype 14 CPS, adhered tighter to human respiratory cells. nLC4 promotes adhesion likely because it interacts with Gb4 [GalNAc(β1-3)Gal(α1-4)Gal(β1-4)Glc] globoside, which is an abundant glycolipid on erythrocytes (96 µg/mL) ( , ). Gb4 can also be found in the endothelium of alveolar capillaries and kidney proximal tubules ( ). It is plausible that S. pneumoniae can exploit this glycan–glycan interaction to promote attachment to the respiratory lining. Producing an nLC4-like glycan is not a unique feat of S. pneumoniae. Neisseria gonorrhoeae produces paraglobosyl lipooligosaccharide when it reaches the urethra ( ). This molecule is important for virulence, supporting the hypothesis that the nLC4 motif is important for host–pathogen interaction. Other glycan motifs that may facilitate epithelial cell binding are also uncovered in this study. How they function requires further investigation. For example, the serotype 11C CPS contains an iGb3 glycan motif that interacts with CD1d ( ). Although this interaction is weak, it may contribute to the adhesion ( ). Another avenue we have yet to investigate is the interaction between different serotypes and the competition between unencapsulated and encapsulated cells, as the results from the pooled mutant and individual binding experiments are consistent ( ). Contrary to the prevailing view, we were unable to detect any correlation between the electronegativity of the capsule and epithelial cell binding. We speculate the neutral CPS may have already adapted to compensate for the loss of a negative charge, such as including a human-like glycan motif. Notably, more negatively charged glycans are found in prokaryotes compared with plants, mammals, and fungi ( ). Whether this bias is a coincidence or has some functional consequences is yet to be determined.
In addition, we found that rhamnose-rich CPS tends to have a weaker binding affinity to the airway epithelial cells ( ). As these glycans are thought to be immunogenic ( ), we postulate the innate response from the epithelial cells may antagonize attachment, perhaps by increasing the production of cytokine, mucus, and antimicrobial peptides. We have not yet elucidated the immune defense of hNECs against rhamnose, because it is likely not included in the 42 cytokines we profiled. Nevertheless, since rhamnose is commonly found in bacteria, it is perceivable that the epithelial cells can sense and respond to rhamnose-rich glycans. Consistently, rhamnose-specific lectins have been reported in human cells ( ). Moreover, anti-rhamnose antibodies are abundant in the bloodstream and have been exploited in anticancer vaccines and therapies ( ). Another possibility is that rhamnose may directly interact with bacterial adhesins on the cell surface. However, there is no known rhamnose-binding adhesin in S. pneumoniae. Also, the D39W strain lacks pili ( ) or PsrP ( ) that may bind carbohydrates. In addition, pilot biofilm experiments with a panel of adhesin knockout mutants do not support this view (SI Appendix, Fig. S14), perhaps because many of these adhesins are buried underneath the capsule layer ( ). While we have not fully excluded this possibility, we favor the model that rhamnose-rich glycans trigger host immune responses that prevent bacterial attachment.

## Glycan Motifs that Affect the Survival of Bacteria on Epithelial Cells.
Survival in the human airway requires mechanisms to evade phagocytosis, neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs) ( , ), and killing by antimicrobial peptides ( ). We found no detectable correlation between epithelial cell binding and prevalence, perhaps because initial attachment to the epithelium is necessary but not sufficient for colonization. Intriguingly, several CPS-switch strains were unable to colonize respiratory cells without any growth defect in the liquid medium. We postulate that these strains produce glycans that are recognized and attacked by galectins and collectins ( ), as they have an increased frequency of α-linked galactose and N-acetylgalactosamine (SI Appendix, Fig. S9). Several pneumococcal capsules have also been implicated in sensitizing bacteria to defensins ( ). In addition, surface glycans are likely to play a more important role in the host because specialized macrophages like Kupffer cells preferentially engulf bacteria that display specific types of glycans ( ). Besides, the capsule also shields pathogen-associated molecular patterns such as lipoteichoic acids ( ). Consistently, we demonstrated that when introduced to epithelial cells, encapsulated pneumococci stimulated less cytokine production compared with unencapsulated cells. Ongoing work is underway to elucidate the mechanism to sense and respond to these bacterial surface polysaccharides and to uncover the underlying glycan motifs.
Finally, the barcoded, isogenic CPS-switch mutants presented here can serve as a “cellular glycan array” that allows us to investigate carbohydrate functions in a high-throughput manner. Similar arrays have been constructed in eukaryotes ( ), which demonstrated distinct advantages over traditional glycan arrays. It is primarily because the glycans are positioned on their native scaffold ( ), for this study, the pneumococcal cell. We propose this approach can be extended to other genetically tractable pathogens to decipher the role of surface glycans in host–pathogen interaction.

## Materials and Methods

## Bacteria Strains and Culture Conditions.
The strains used in this study are listed in SI Appendix, Table S1. Clinical isolates of S. pneumoniae expressing 84 CPS types were collected from hospitals and research institutes ( ). Unless otherwise specified, S. pneumoniae was grown in brain heart infusion broth (BHI; ThermoFisher Scientific CM1135B) or on tryptic soy agar-II plates supplemented with 5% (v/v) sheep blood (blood agar; Biomed Diagnostics, 221261) at 37 °C in 5% CO2. Antibiotics were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich and used at final concentrations of 250 mg/mL for kanamycin (Kan) and 300 mg/mL for streptomycin.

## Growth Curves.
Overnight cultures of the indicated strains were grown in BHI broth to an optical density at 600 nm (OD600) of 0.1 to 0.3. Cultures were diluted in BHI broth or Pneumacult culture media to an OD600 of 0.01 in 17-mm-diameter polystyrene tubes (Falcon, 352057) or 96-well plates. Strains in tubes were grown at 37 °C in 5% CO2 for 7 h, and the OD600 readings were measured every 1 h using the GeneSys 30 spectrophotometer (Thermo). Strains in 96-well plates were grown at 37 °C for 7 h inside a Tecan Spark 10 M multimode plate reader, and OD600 readings were taken every 10 min after 30 s of shaking. Doubling times of strains grown in BHI were quantified using a python script, which is available at https://github.com/jadechun.

## Strain Construction.
S. pneumoniae strains were constructed by transforming genomic DNA, isothermal assembly products, or PCR amplicons after inducing natural competence ( , ). Primers used for the synthesis of PCR fragments are tabulated in SI Appendix, Table S2. In general, PCR products were synthesized with Phusion DNA polymerase (New England Biolabs, M0530S) and purified using the QIAquick PCR Purification Kit (Qiagen, 28106). Transformants were suspended in warm, molten 0.7% (w/v) Difco nutrient agar (Biomed Diagnostics, 213000) or 0.7% (w/v) Difco nutrient agar supplemented with 40% (w/v) sucrose (Fisher Scientific, S8600/60) and poured on blood agar plates with appropriate antibiotics. Colonies were streaked twice on blood agar with the indicated antibiotics. The engineered cassettes were validated by diagnostic PCR using GoTaq DNA polymerase (Promega, M712) or KAPA2G Fast DNA polymerase ready mix (Sigma-Aldrich, KK5103) and Sanger sequencing. Isogenic capsule-switch mutants were constructed by allelic replacement ( ). Briefly, strain NUS0114 (D39 rpsL1 Δcps::P-sacB-kan-rpsL+) was constructed by transforming strain IU1781 (D39 rpsL1) with the Sweet Janus cassette ( ) to replace the serotype 2 cps locus. Next, NUS0114 was transformed with genomic DNA purified from clinical isolates using the DNeasy blood and tissue kit (Qiagen, 69606) ( ) and selected for sucrose and Str-resistant transformants. Once the introduced noncognate cps locus was confirmed by PCR, genomic DNA was purified from the capsule-switch mutants and used to transform NUS0114 again to remove unwanted genetic contamination ( ). The “backcrossed” isogenic capsule-switch mutants were validated by PCR ( ) and immunostaining (i.e., Quellung reaction) ( ) using antisera purchased from Statens Serum Institute. Whole-genome sequencing was performed by Genewiz, and the sequences were analyzed by CLC genomic workbench (QIAGEN) ( ). Strain NUS0114 was used as a negative control. Next, cassettes containing a kanamycin-resistant gene and a unique 9-nucleotide barcode were synthesized by PCR and constructed by Gibson’s assembly ( ) using primers listed in SI Appendix, Table S2. They were introduced into the backcrossed CPS-switch mutants, and the insertion of barcodes was confirmed by Sanger sequencing.

## Measurement of Zeta Potentials.
Measurement of zeta potentials was performed as described in Li et al. with slight modifications ( ). Briefly, the indicated cultures were grown to an OD600 of ~0.2 in BHI broth, and cells from 1 ml culture were pelleted at 3,000 × g for 5 min at room temperature. The cells were washed twice with 1 mL 1× phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) (137 mM NaCl, 2.7 mM KCl, 1 mM CaCl2, 0.5 mM MgCl2, 10 mM Na2HPO4, and 1.8 mM KH2PO4, pH 7.4) and resuspended in the same volume of PBS. Bacteria were heat-inactivated by incubation at 65 °C for 45 min and stored at −80 °C. The suspension was normalized with PBS to an OD600 of 0.1 (~107 CFU/mL). Zeta potentials were measured at 25 °C using Zetasizer Ultra (Malvern Panalytical) according to the manufacturer's protocol.

## Growth and Differentiation of Human Nasal Epithelial Stem/Progenitor Cells (hNESPCs) to Produce hNECs.
Collection and culturing hNECPCs and hNECs were approved by the National Healthcare Group Domain-Specific Board of Singapore (protocol D/11/228) and the institutional review board of the National University of Singapore (protocol 13-509). hNESPCs were collected from healthy donors who underwent septal plastic surgery at National University Hospital (NUH), Singapore. Informed consent was obtained for tissue collection and the samples were deidentified prior to the experiments. No glucocorticoids or antibiotics were administered within 3 mo prior to surgery.
The differentiation of hNECs from hNESPCs was conducted as described previously ( ). Harvested patient tissues were resuspended in prechilled Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) (Hyclone, high glucose) with 100 IU/mL antibiotic-antimycotic solution (Gibco-Invitrogen, Grand Island, NY) and transferred to the laboratory within 1 h. Overnight digestion using Dispase II (Sigma) was performed at 4 °C, followed by trypsinization at 37 °C for 15 min. NIH/3T3 feeder layer cells (ATCC, Manassas, VA) were treated with mitomycin-C (Sigma) in 6-well plates. Subsequently, single-cell suspensions were seeded onto the NIH/3T3 feeder layer cells at 1 to 1.5 × 104 epithelial cells/cm2. hNESPCs were cultured in serum-free medium 3 (DMEM/Nutrient Mixture F-12 (DMEM/F12; Gibco-Invitrogen); 10 ng/mL human epithelial growth factor (Gibco-Invitrogen); 5 μg/mL insulin (Sigma); 0.1 nM cholera toxin (Sigma); 0.5 μg/mL hydrocortisone (Sigma); 2 nM/mL 3,3′,5-triiodo-l-thyronine (T3; Sigma); 10 μL/mL N-2 supplement (Gibco-Invitrogen); 100 IU/mL Antibiotic-Antimycotic) for 3 to 4 d at 37 °C.
At the exponential growth phase, the hNESPCs were passaged twice, then harvested at passage 1 by Accutase (Gibco, #A1110501) dissociation. The hNESPCs were seeded at a cell density of 3 × 104 onto 6.5-mm diameter Transwell® inserts with a 0.4-µM polyester membrane (Corning, #3470) under submerged conditions for 3 d using serum-free medium 3. Subsequently, the medium was changed to the Pneumacult-ALI Basal Media [includes Pneumacult-ALI 10× Supplement and Pneumacult-ALI Maintenance Supplement (100×)] (all provided under Stemcell Technologies, #5001), supplemented with 1 mL 0.2% heparin sodium sulfate (Stemcell Technologies, #07980) and 2.5 mL hydrocortisone (200×) solution (Stemcell Technologies #7925). Cells were washed every 48 h using Dulbecco’s phosphate-buffered saline (dPBS) without calcium chloride and magnesium chloride (Gibco; 14200075), followed by a change of differentiation medium. Fully differentiated hNECs with beating ciliated cells and mucus-producing goblet cells were obtained after 32 to 35 d of ALI culture.

## Derivation of Human Epithelial Stem Cells (hESCs) and In Vitro Differentiation of hBECs.
For the derivation of hBECs differentiated from H9 epithelial stem cells (ESCs), the following procedures were adopted with modifications from others ( ). Briefly, H9 ESCs underwent stepwise differentiation to definitive endoderm and anterior foregut endoderm phases to obtain lung progenitors. The definitive endoderm phase was induced in the chemically defined medium supplemented with 100 ng/mL Activin A (Peprotech 120-14P), 80 ng/mL recombinant human basic fibroblast growth factor (rhFGF2, Peprotech 00-18B), 10 ng/mL recombinant human bone morphogenetic protein-4 (rhBMP4, Peprotech 120-05), 3 µM CHIR99021 (Tocris, 4423), and 10 µM LY294002 (Promega, V1201) for 3 d, as previously described ( ). To pattern cells toward anterior foregut endoderm and subsequently lung progenitors according to previously established protocols ( , ), cells were grown in complete serum-free differentiation medium (cSFDM) containing 10 µM SB431542 (Tocris, 1614) and 2 µM Dorsomorphin (Tocris, 3093) for 3 d followed by cSFDM containing 3 µM CHIR99021, 10 ng/mL rhFGF10 (Peprotech 100-26), 10 ng/mL recombinant human keratinocyte growth factor (KGF, Peprotech 100-19), 10 ng/mL rhBMP4, and 50 nM retinoid acid (RA, Sigma, R2625) for 7 d.
Lung progenitors were then sorted for CD166pos (activated leukocyte cell adhesion molecule, ALCAM, R&D Systems, FAB6561P) to enrich for lung progenitor cells, followed by seeding onto Matrigel-coated Costar 6.5-mm diameter Transwells® inserts with 0.4 µM polyester membrane (Stemcell Technologies, 38024) at 70,000 cells per Transwell® ( ) under submerged conditions for 7 to 9 d in cSFDM containing 250 ng/mL FGF2, 100 ng/mL FGF10, 50 nM dexamethasone (Sigma-Aldrich, D4902), 0.1 mM 8-bromoadenosine 30,50-cyclic monophosphate sodium salt (cAMP, Sigma-Aldrich, B5386) and 0.1 mM 3-Isobutyl-1-methylxanthine (IBMX, Sigma-Aldrich, I7018). Cells were washed every week with Dulbecco’s phosphate-buffered saline without calcium chloride and magnesium chloride (Hyclone, SH30028) prior to adding Pneumacult-ALI™ differentiation medium (StemCell Technologies, 05001) containing 10 µM DAPT (Sigma, D5942) only to the basal chamber ( ). Fully differentiated hBECs with beating ciliated cells and mucus-producing goblet cells were obtained after 14 d of ALI culture.

## Live Dead Assay of hNECs.
The live dead staining was performed using the LIVE/DEAD™ Viability/Cytotoxicity Kit for mammalian cells (ThermoFisher Scientific, L3224) as per the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, the staining solution was made by mixing calcein AM (component A) and ethidium homodimer-1 (component B) with dPBS. The staining solution was added directly to the hNECs and incubated for 30 min at room temperature. Images were captured by fluorescence microscopy.

## Immunofluorescence Microscopy of hNECs and hBECs.
hNECs and hBECs were characterized by immunofluorescence staining of ciliated and goblet cell markers (i.e., β-tubulin IV and MUC5AC) as described before ( ). Briefly, transwells containing the cultured cells were fixed for 60 min with 4% (w/v) paraformaldehyde and embedded in paraffin blocks. Slides sectioned from the paraffin blocks were subjected to antigen retrieval (pH 6.0) and then blocked for 30 min with 10% (v/v) goat serum at room temperature. Cells were then incubated with a primary antibody solution overnight at 4 °C. The primary antibody solution consisted of mouse anti-human p63 monoclonal antibody ( ), mouse anti-human β-tubulin IV monoclonal antibody (Clone ONS.1A6; Abcam), and mouse monoclonal antibody against Mucin 5AC (Clone 2-11M1; Abcam) prepared in dilutions of 1:80, 1:800, and 1:400, respectively. Cells were then incubated for 1 h at room temperature with a secondary antibody solution. The secondary antibody solution consisted of goat anti-mouse Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated secondary antibodies (Invitrogen, #A11029), and goat anti-rabbit Alexa Fluor 549-conjugated secondary antibodies (Invitrogen, #A11037), both at 1:500 dilution. Finally, sections were subjected to DAPI staining. Images were captured using confocal microscopy.

## Pooling of the Capsule-Switch Mutants.
The molecular-barcoded isogenic strains were streaked on blood agar and incubated overnight at 37 °C in 5% CO2. Colonies on each plate were scraped, resuspended in 10 mL BHI broth, and incubated at 37 °C in 5% CO2. After concentrating and normalizing cultures to an OD600 of 0.8, equal volumes of each strain were pooled and mixed thoroughly by pipetting. Glycerol was supplemented to a final concentration of 15% (v/v). The mixture was aliquoted and stored at −80 °C.

## Barcode Sequencing and Quantification.
Genomic DNA was extracted by the DNeasy blood and tissue kit (Qiagen, 69606). The genetic locus containing the barcode was amplified by the KAPA HiFi HotStart Library Amplification Kit (Roche, KK2611) using primers listed in SI Appendix, Table S2. These primers contain a unique barcode sequence for multiplexing. Up to seven libraries were pooled and sequenced using the Novaseq PE250 platform (2 million reads per sample) with PhiX spike-in and quality control analysis. Data were demultiplexed, and the barcode reads were enumerated with the CLC Genomics Workbench (Qiagen) and exported to Microsoft Excel for further processing.
To ensure that the number of barcodes correspond to the number of colony-forming units (CFUs), the library was spiked with a known number of CFUs of a barcoded serotype 2 strain. First, strain NUS0590 [rpsL1 CPS2 ΔCEP::P-kan-rpsL-TCGTATAAT] was grown in BHI broth at 37 °C in 5% CO2 until OD600 reached 0.2. Cells were pelleted at 10,000 × g for 2 min at room temperature and resuspended in BHI broth, serial diluted, and introduced to the pooled CPS-switch mutant library. The serial diluted NUS0590 suspension was plated to enumerate the number of CFUs. Genomic DNA from the mixture of bacteria was extracted and the barcodes were sequenced. The number of barcode reads was normalized to reads per one million reads (RPM). Next, RPM was converted to the number of CFUs based on the plate counting of the barcoded library and the serial diluted NUS0590 culture.
To measure the change of barcodes over time, pooled capsule-switch mutants were inoculated into BHI broth and incubated at 37 °C in 5% CO2. At the indicated time points, samples were harvested for genomic DNA extraction and barcode sequencing. Cultures were diluted to an OD600 of 0.05 when OD600 reached 0.8. Reads were normalized RPM and compared, and depicted as heat maps using GraphPad Prism.

## Pneumococcal Infection of hNECs or hBECs.
Before infection, epithelial cells were washed with 1× Dulbecco’s phosphate-buffered saline (dPBS; 2.6 mM KCl, 14.7 mM KH2PO4, 137.8 mM NaCl, 8 mM Na2HPO4) (ThermoFisher Scientific, 14200-075). After incubation at 37 °C in 5% CO2 for 10 min, dPBS was aspirated from the wells. Fresh PneumaCult™-ALI Medium with inducer supplements (STEMCELL Technologies Inc.) was added to the basal chambers. To prepare the inoculum, an aliquot of the barcode library or the respective pneumococcal strains was thawed, inoculated in BHI, then incubated at 37 °C in 5% CO2. When the OD600 of the culture reached ~0.1, bacteria were pelleted by centrifugation at 3,000 × g for 5 min at room temperature, then resuspended in fresh differentiation media [PneumaCult™-ALI Basal Medium (Stemcell Technologies, 5001), PneumaCult™-ALI 1× Supplements (Stemcell Technologies, 5001), hydrocortisone solution (Stemcell Technologies, 7925), heparin sodium salt in phosphate-buffered saline (Stemcell Technologies, 7980), and PneumaCult™-ALI Maintenance Supplements (Stemcell Technologies, 5001)]. Bacteria inoculum in 10 µL was added to the hNECs or hBECs at a multiplicity of infection of 1. Where indicated, anti-CPS antisera was mixed with the inoculum at a 1:10 dilution. An aliquot of the inoculum was collected for plate counting and barcode sequencing. The cocultures were then incubated for 20 h at 37 °C in 5% CO2.
To collect the unbound fraction, 100 µL dPBS was added to the hNECs or hBECs and incubated for 10 min at 37 °C in 5% CO2. The loosely bound bacteria (or unbound fractions) were released by pipetting gently for five times before aspiration. The tightly bound bacteria were collected by adding 100 µL dPBS at the ALI and scraping the epithelial cells. The suspension was homogenized by pipetting five times before it was transferred to a new microcentrifuge tube. Bound and unbound fractions were serial diluted in PBS and plated on blood agar for enumeration. Genomic DNA extraction and barcode sequencing were performed as described above.

## Cytokine Profiling.
Cytokine profiling was performed using the human cytokine antibody array (membrane, 42 targets) (Abcam, ab133997) according to the manufacturer’s protocol using the buffers provided in the kit. Briefly, membranes were blocked with 1× blocking buffer at room temperature for 30 min and aspirated. Basal media from three biological replicates of infected hNECs were pooled in equal volumes to a final volume of 1 mL before applying to the blocked membrane. After overnight incubation at 4 °C, the membrane was washed with 20 mL wash buffer I and incubated at room temperature for 30 min with gentle shaking. Membranes were washed thrice with 1× wash buffer I and twice with 1× wash buffer II. After washing, 1× biotin-conjugated anti-cytokine antibodies were added to the membranes and incubated for 2 h at room temperature. After three washes with 1× wash buffer I and two washes with 1× wash buffer II, 1× horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated streptavidin was added and incubated for 2 h at room temperature. The membrane was washed thrice with 1× wash buffer I and twice with 1× wash buffer II and detected by buffers C and D provided. The blot was imaged using a ChemiDoc™ MP Imaging System (Bio-rad).

## Enzyme-Linked Immunosorbent Assay (ELISA).
To measure the amount of CPS, anti-CPS antisera were cross-adsorbed as detailed in refs.   and  . Briefly, unencapsulated strain NUS1181 [D39 rpsL1 ΔcpsE CEP::P-kan-rpsL-TGAATCCAA] was grown in 200 mL BHI broth overnight at 37 °C in 5% CO2. Cells were pelleted, washed with 1× PBS, then resuspended in 20 mL 1× PBS. After heat-inactivation at 65 °C for 1 h, cells were washed, resuspended in equal volumes of 1× PBS, and stored at −80 °C. Anti-CPS antisera (Statens Serum Institute, Denmark) were diluted at 1:1,000 (anti-CPS2, anti-CPS6, anti-CPS9, anti-CPS22, anti-CPS23, and anti-CPS39) or 1:4,000 (anti-CPS19) in blocking buffer (1% (w/v) bovine serum albumin in 1× PBS). To cross-adsorb, 35 µL of heat-killed cells was added to 1 mL of the diluted antisera. The mixture was incubated overnight at 4 °C. After centrifugation at 16,100 × g for 1 min at 4 °C, the supernatant fraction was filtered through a 0.22-µm pore-size membrane (Corning Costar Spin-X centrifuge tube; Sigma-Aldrich CLS8160). The cross-adsorbed antisera were kept at 4 °C until use. Next, the indicated strains were grown in BHI broth at 37 °C in 5% CO2 to an OD600 of ~0.4. Cells from 1 mL culture were pelleted at 16,100 × g for 1 min at room temperature, then resuspended in an equal volume of 1× PBS. 25 µL proteinase K (Qiagen, 19133) was added to the cells and incubated at 56 °C for 1 h. Cells were then pelleted and resuspended in 1 mL 50 mM carbonate/bicarbonate buffer (Sigma-Aldrich, C3041). The suspension was serial diluted and added onto ELISA MaxiSorp plates (ThermoFisher Scientific, 442404) and incubated overnight at 4 °C. Serial diluted purified CPS (Statens Serum Institut Diagnostica) was used as an internal standard. After removing the liquid from the ELISA plates, wells were blocked with the blocking buffer for 1 h at room temperature. Plates were washed four times with 1× PBS with 0.05% (v/v) Tween 20, followed by the addition of the cross-adsorbed anti-CPS antiserum. After incubation overnight at 4 °C, plates were washed four times, and the goat anti-rabbit HRP antibodies (ThermoFisher, A16110) were added at a 1:1,000 dilution in blocking buffer. After four washes, antibodies were detected using o-phenylenediamine dihydrochloride (Sigma, P9187-50SET), according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The optical density at OD450 was recorded using a Tecan Spark 10 M multimode plate reader.
GM-CSF, IL-6, and MCP-1 levels were quantified using the human GM-CSF (Abcam, ab174448), IL-6 (Abcam, ab178013), and MCP-1 (Abcam, ab179886) ELISA kit following the manufacturer’s protocol using the buffers provided in the kit. Briefly, basal media from infected hNECs were thawed and diluted using Sample Diluent NS. The samples, the standards, and the antibody cocktail were added to the wells, sealed, and incubated for 1.5 h at room temperature with shaking. Wells were washed thrice with 1× wash buffer PT, followed by the addition of TMB development solution. After incubation for about 15 min in the dark with shaking, the stop solution was added, and the OD450 was recorded using a Tecan Spark 10 M multimode plate reader.

## Biofilm Assay.
The formation of biofilm was measured as described in ref.   with slight modifications. Cultures were grown in BHI broth at 37 °C in 5% CO2 in 17-mm-diameter polystyrene tubes (Falcon, 352057) to an OD620 of 0.5 to 0.7. The culture was discarded, and the biofilm formed on the side of the tube was washed twice gently with 500 µL PBS. Next, 500 µL 0.15% (w/v) crystal violet dissolved in 8.2% (v/v) ethanol and 0.4% (v/v) methanol was added and incubated for 20 min at room temperature. The staining solution was decanted, and the biofilm was rinsed four times with 1 mL PBS. Retained crystal violet was solubilized with 2 mL 95% (v/v) ethanol for 20 min. Samples were diluted twofold in 95% (v/v) ethanol. The A550 of the dissolved crystal violet was measured in 96-well plates using a Tecan Spark 10 M multimode plate reader and normalized to the OD620 of the culture.

## Immunofluorescence Microscopy (IFM).
IFM was performed essentially as described in ref.  . Cells of strain NUS1181 [D39 rpsL1 ΔcpsE CEP::P-kan-rpsL-TGAATCCAA] were prepared for cross-adsorption as described in the ELISA section. Briefly, 1 µL anti-serotype 34 antiserum (SSI) was added to 299 µL 1× PBS and 300 µL heat-killed NUS1181 cells. The mixture was incubated overnight at 4 °C. After adsorption, antibodies were separated from heat-killed cells by centrifugation and sterilized by filtration. Strain NUS1152 [rpsL1 CPS34 ΔCEP::P-kan-rpsL-TCCAAAAAG] was grown in BHI broth at 37 °C in 5% CO2 to an OD600 of 0.4. Cells were inactivated at 65 °C for 45 min and collected by centrifugation at 20,000 × g for 2 min at room temperature. Bacteria were washed once with 1 mL 1× PBS, and 100 µL cross-adsorbed anti-serotype 34 CPS antiserum was added. The mixture was incubated on ice for 5 min. Cells were then pelleted by centrifugation, washed twice with 1× PBS, then resuspended in 100 µL 1× PBS. CPS was detected by adding goat anti-rabbit IgG conjugated with Alexa Fluor 488 (Thermo Fisher, A11034) at a 1:100 dilution and incubated on ice for 5 min. Labeled cells were washed with 1 mL 1× PBS and resuspended in 5 µL 1× PBS. Cells were mounted on a glass slide and imaged using an IX81 phase-contrast microscope (Olympus) with a 100× oil immersion objective and a complementary metal oxide semiconductor camera (Hamamatsu, C11440).

## Data and Statistical Analyses.
The prevalence of pneumococcal serotypes was obtained from ref.  . The number of carbons per repeat unit and the number of high energy bonds per repeat unit were retrieved from ref.  . For enumerating the number of rhamnose per CPS repeating unit, structures of CPS were retrieved from the Carbohydrate Structure database ( ). Human glycans similar to serotype 5, 14, 11F, and 15C CPS were retrieved from the GlyTouCan glycan repository ( ). Unless otherwise stated, all statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism V9.0 software. For example, nonparametric Spearman correlation was used to evaluate correlation in scatterplots. Student's t tests or nonparametric Mann–Whitney U tests were used to calculate P values.
