## Abstract

## Background
Brain metastases (BrM) in non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) present a significant challenge due to poor prognosis. While immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) have been standard treatments for NSCLC, their efficacy in BrM is variable, emphasizing the urgent need for predictive biomarkers and fundamental mechanisms.

## Methods
Patients with NSCLC and BrM planning to receive ICI therapy were enrolled in the prospective cohort, dynamic cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) samples (n=20) were collected through lumbar puncture and used for single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq), and BrM tumors were collected if they have the indications of surgery, with integrating scRNA-seq data from external database (total n=20). Three independent cohorts underwent flow cytometry (n=8), proteomics analysis (n=31), and multiplex immunohistochemistry (n=25) for validation.

## Results
Our study provided a high-resolution atlas of cellular dynamics in the CSF and BrM during ICI therapy in patients with NSCLC and BrM. Notably, we identified a key immune cell subset, CD4+PDCD1+CXCR6+ T cells, as a positive predictor of ICI intracranial tumor responses, which presented highly functional and transcriptomic similarities in both CSF and BrM tumor environment. Moreover, CXCR6 could serve as a specific marker for CD4+PDCD1+ T cells linked to ICI response. Further, we revealed that the novel cluster of CD4+PDCD1+CXCR6+ T cells was closely associated with lymphocyte activation and aggregation in CSF and BrM of ICI responders, and classical dendritic cells of ICI responders interacted with CD4+PDCD1+CXCR6+ T cells for enhanced antigen presentation and inflammatory activation.

## Conclusion
Our findings revealed critical insights into the immune landscape of NSCLC BrM under ICI therapy, highlighting CD4+PDCD1+CXCR6+ T cells in CSF as a promising biomarker and illuminating fundamental mechanisms underlying ICI efficacy.

## WHAT IS ALREADY KNOWN ON THIS TOPIC
Brain metastases (BrM) in non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) presented a significant challenge due to the poor prognosis. Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) could reflect the immune microenvironment of BrM; however, previous studies have been constrained by small sample sizes and limited to leptomeningeal metastases in pan-cancer cohorts. The efficacy of immunotherapy in BrM is variable, emphasizing the urgent need for predictive biomarkers and fundamental mechanisms.

## WHAT THIS STUDY ADDS
Our study, with the largest cohort to date, comprehensively delineated the immune cell landscape of BrM, characterized the cellular dynamics in CSF during immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) therapy, and more importantly, identified intracranial response-associated CD4+PDCD1+CXCR6+ T cells in CSF and BrM. Cross-tumor analyses uncovered active interactions of CD4+PDCD1+CXCR6+ T cells with antigen-presenting cells and cytotoxic cells, enhancing activation and enrichment of lymphocytes in both CSF and BrM tumors of responders.

## HOW THIS STUDY MIGHT AFFECT RESEARCH, PRACTICE OR POLICY
Our study provided critical insights into the cellular dynamics in CSF and BrM under ICIs therapy and identified CD4+PDCD1+CXCR6+ T cell as a predictive immune cell subset for ICIs intracranial response, uncovering the immunological mechanisms of ICI strategies. We pioneered the use of CSF as a liquid biopsy medium in BrM, making CSF a reliable surrogate for tracking immunotherapy response and guiding personalized treatment for BrM of NSCLC.

## Introduction
 Brain metastases (BrM), with dismal prognosis, increasingly occur in advanced non-small-cell lung cancers (NSCLC), highlighting the urgent need for new therapeutic strategies. Immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) have been the standard therapy for advanced NSCLC without driver genes. For those with BrM, ICIs showed preliminary efficacy with intracranial objective control rates ranging from 16.4% to 52.5% in several retrospective studies or phase II studies. , ICIs single agent or combination therapy have become important treatment options for BrM, and the combination of ICIs with radiotherapy has garnered considerable attention due to its potential synergistic effects. However, it remains challenging to improve the clinical outcomes of BrM owing to their highly heterogeneous intracranial response to ICIs, and only a minority could achieve complete response, underscoring the urgent need for predictive biomarkers of ICIs.
Due to the inherent heterogeneity, BrM harbored distinct genomic and transcriptomic characteristics, and extracranial tumors showed limited utility to predict intracranial responses to systemic treatments. Obtaining tissue of BrM poses risks and challenges, making cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) a valuable liquid biopsy for assessing the biological profiles of BrM. Our previous studies, along with others, have shown that CSF can be used to reveal the genetic and cellular profiles of intracranial tumors from NSCLC. Besides, several studies also used CSF to reflect the tumor immune cell infiltration and characteristics of microenvironment of BrM. Nonetheless, these studies focused on BrM of solid tumors but included very limited number of lung cancers. Although the immune cells in the CSF of leptomeningeal metastases (LM) could indicate cellular features associated with ICIs response, these results are less representative of BrM because functional and phenotypic heterogeneity of immune cells existed between LM and BrM. One study showed that immunological cytokines in CSF could be used to characterize the responders following ICIs in BrM, indicating the promising potential of CSF as a surrogate for investigating ICI response for intracranial tumors. However, this study had a limited sample size and only focused on cytokines, which lacked insights for understanding the cellular behavioral dynamics for immune responses to ICIs for BrM of NSCLC. Therefore, many questions around ICI response in BrM and the usage of CSF as a liquid biopsy surrogate remain unanswered. Furthermore, the concordance of CSF and BrM and the temporal changes of CSF under ICI treatment for patients with NSCLC are also elusive.
Here, we performed single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq), proteomics, flow cytometry and multiplex immunohistochemistry (mIHC) analyses on CSF and BrM tumor of NSCLC, aiming to comprehensively delineate the immune cell landscape of BrM, characterize the cellular dynamics in CSF during ICIs therapy, and more importantly, identify potential predictors of intracranial tumor ICI responses. Our study provided critical insights into the cellular dynamics in CSF and BrM under ICIs therapy and identified CD4+PDCD1+CXCR6+ T cell as a potentially predictive immune cell subset for ICIs intracranial response, uncovering the immunological mechanisms of ICI strategies.

## Results

## Single-cell profiling of NSCLC BrM under ICI therapy
In this study, we prospectively collected twenty CSF samples and four tumor tissue samples from 18 patients with NSCLC and BrM for scRNA-seq analyses, supplemented by the integration of 16 published scRNA-seq data of tumor tissues ( , ). The procedure of CSF collection was well-tolerated, with no complications observed during the 24-hour post-procedure period. Based on ICI treatment status and efficacy, including anti-programmed cell death protein-1 (PD-1) and anti-programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1), samples were categorized into five groups ( and ; Methods). In total, the 20 CSF samples for scRNA-seq analysis included 3 CR-pre (“confirmed responders” before ICI treatment), 3 PR-pre (“potential responders” before ICI treatment), 1 PR-post (“potential responders” after ICI treatment), 4 NR-pre (“non-responders” before ICI treatment), 5 NR-post (“non-responders” after ICI treatment) and 4 controls without ICI.
 Following quality control of the scRNA-seq data ( ), transcriptome data of 100,051 single cells were retained for analysis. After data normalization, batch effect removal, dimensionality reduction, and unsupervised clustering, 11 major immune cell types were eventually identified based on their respective marker genes ( ). These included CD4+ T cells, CD8+ T cells, regulatory T cells (Tregs), B cells, natural killer (NK) cells, monocytes, macrophages, classical dendritic cells (cDCs), plasmacytoid DCs (pDCs), neutrophils, and a minor population of cycling cells ( and ). Each CSF sample contained approximately 3,000–6,000 single cells, with CD4+ T cells being the most abundant immune cells in the CSF ( ). An increased number of macrophages and decreased abundance of CD4+ T and B cells were observed in the CR-pre group compared with the NR-pre group ( and ). Consistent with previous reports, an increased proportion of CD8+ T cells was observed post-ICI treatment compared with pretreatment samples. More importantly, we observed obvious differences in the density distribution of T cells between sample groups, as shown in the t-distributed stochastic neighbor embedding (t-SNE) visualization, hinting distinct functional distribution of T cells in each group ( ). Subsequently, we performed additional clustering on CD4+ T, CD8+ T, NK, B, and myeloid cells and ultimately identified 46 fine-grained cell subtypes, after excluding two potential doublet clusters and merging two plasma cell clusters into one ( and ). Compared with previous reports , we constructed a comprehensive single-cell atlas of the CSF in NSCLC BrM with more refined cellular subtypes and integrated ICI treatment information.

## CD4_Trm_CXCR6 cells were enriched in CSF of ICI responders
We divided CD4+ T cells into nine subtypes, including naïve T cells (Tn) CD4_Tn_CCR7, five subtypes of memory T cells (Tm), tissue-resident memory T cells (Trm) CD4_Trm_CXCR6, terminally differentiated effector memory cells (Temra) CD4_ Temra_EOMES, and Tregs ( ). The five Tm subtypes included CD4_Tm_IFI6, CD4_Tm_CCR6, CD4_Tm_BHLHE40, CD4_Tm_FOS, and CD4_Tm_Nos without specific gene expression enrichment ( ).
In the NR-pre group, CD4+ T cells were predominantly CD4_Tn_CCR7 and CD4_Tm_Nos ( and ). While in the CR_pre group, the CD4_Trm_CXCR6 population was significantly increased compared with the NR_pre group ( and ). Comparing to NR-pre, the distribution of CD4+ T-cell subtypes of the PR-pre and NR-post more closely resembled the CR-pre group, with a higher abundance of CD4_Trm_CXCR6 ( and ). Using proteomics analysis, we found that most of the characteristic proteins of CD4_Trm_CXCR6 were highly expressed in the CR-pre group in an independent validation cohort, for example, BLC (CXCL13) and TIM-3 (HAVCR2) ( and ). Additionally, using a custom-developed relative rank-enrichment algorithm to score these characteristic proteins, the proteomics data inferred that CD4_Trm_CXCR6 were significantly increased in CR-pre compared with NR-pre ( and ; Methods). Collectively, the CD4_Trm_CXCR6 population is positively correlated to ICI response.
 To further explore the differences in CD4+ T-cell subsets between sample groups, we used the partition-based graph abstraction (PAGA) analysis, which identified four transition trajectories from CD4_Tn_CCR7 towards CD4_Treg, CD4_Tm_FOS, CD4_Trm_CXCR6, and CD4_Temra_EOMES, respectively ( ). High connectivity between CD4_Tm_Nos and CD4_Trm_CXCR6 cells, as well as between CD4_Tm_BHLHE40 and CD4_Tm_FOS cells in the CR-pre group were observed ( and ), potentially implying close functional characteristics between these subtypes in CR-pre. Conversely, connectivity between CD4_Tm_Nos and CD4_Trm_CXCR6 cells decreased in NR-pre samples. In addition, CellChat analysis revealed that both overall and CD4+ T cell-specific interactions were higher in the CR group compared to the NR groups ( ). Specifically, the CD86-CD28 co-stimulation signaling involving CD4+ T cells was enriched in CR-pre, PR-pre, and NR-post in comparison to NR-pre ( and ). Furthermore, the CD6-ALCAM co-stimulation pathway activity, known to enhance inflammation, activation, proliferation, and migration of CD4+ T cells, , was only enriched in CR-pre and PR-pre, with the CD6 gene significantly higher expressed in the CD4_Trm_CXCR6 cells ( and ). In addition, the proteomics data also revealed that the CSF of the CR-pre group were enriched for proteins related to immune checkpoint signaling and co-stimulation signaling pathways ( ). These findings collectively suggest that CD4+ T cells in the CR-pre group were more likely to transit into CD4_Trm_CXCR6 T cells, whereas CD4+ T cells in the NR-pre group, where co-stimulation signals were low, tended to stagnate as CD4_Tn_CCR7 and CD4_Tm_Nos cell, further supporting the correlation of CD4_Trm_CXCR6 to ICI responses.

## Similarity of CD4+PDCD1+CXCR6+ T cells in CSF and BrM tumors of responders
Next, we investigated the transcriptome profile and functional characteristics of the ICI response-related CD4_Trm_CXCR6 subset. CD4_Trm_CXCR6 cells exhibited high expression of genes such as ITGAE, ITGA1, CXCR6, IFNG, CXCR3, PRDM1, and GZMA, with the highest Trm and Th1 functional scores, and cytotoxicity functional score second only to CD4_Temra_EOMES ( ). CD4_Trm_CXCR6 cells also showed elevated expression of cytokines (XCL1, IL21 and CXCL13) and exhaustion-related genes (LAG3, HAVCR2 and CTLA4), and were enriched for gene expression related to T-cell activation, differentiation, and cell adhesion ( , ). In addition, flow cytometry analysis independently verified the existence of CD4_Trm_CXCR6 cells in the CSF of intracranial metastases from NSCLC, with upregulated cytokines or exhaustion-related markers including PD-1 (encoded by PDCD1) and interferon (IFN)-γ, which further confirmed the findings from scRNA-seq data ( ).
PDCD1 and other exhaustion-related genes, known to co-express on exhausted T cells
(Tex), are closely linked to responses to immunotherapy. We integrated scRNA-seq data of 20 NSCLC BrM tumor samples ( and ) and identified three Tex cell subtypes: CD4_Tex_CXCR6, CD8_Tex_CD69, and CD8_Tex_CXCL13 ( ). Notably, CD4_Tex_CXCR6 cells in BrM tumor were transcriptomic similar to the CD4_Trm_CXCR6 in CSF and also exhibited high expression of PDCD1, CXCL13, CXCR6, and PRDM1 ( ). We further compared CD4+ T cells in BrM tumor and CSF, and found significant overlap between CD4_Tex_CXCR6 and CD4_Trm_CXCR6 in the single-cell dimensionality reduction space, due to high correlation in overall gene expression profile ( ). Similar to the CD4_Trm_CXCR6 cells in CSF, the outgoing signaling with the highest relative strength from the CD4_Tex_CXCR6 cells in BrM tumor was mediated by CD6 ( ). Additionally, the CD4_Tex_CXCR6 cells also exhibited relatively strong outgoing CD40 signaling, which was also upregulated in CD4+ T cells from the CR-pre CSF samples ( ). Together, CD4_Trm_CXCR6 T cells were identified in the CSF and CD4_Tex_CXCR6 T cells were detected in BrM tumors, with both subsets exhibiting highly similar function and transcriptional profiles characterized by elevated CXCR6 and PDCD1 expression, and collectively defined as CD4+PDCD1+CXCR6+ T cells.
 

## CD4+PDCD1+CXCR6+ T cells contributed to lymphocyte activation and aggregation in ICI responders
Regulation of immune cells, either by activating or suppressing, is a key function of CD4+ T cells in tumor immunity. We further explored the functional correlations and interactions of CD4+PDCD1+CXCR6+ T cells with other immune cells and their association with immunotherapy outcome. In BrM tumors, the CD4_Tex_CXCR6 cells exhibited strong outgoing signals towards CD8+ T-cell subsets and were positively correlated with lymphocyte population sizes, including CD4+ T and CD8+ T-cell subsets, B cells, and NK_ITGAE cells ( left). The tumor-infiltrating CD8_Tex_CXCL13 subset, which was reported as a tumor-reactive and immunotherapy-responsive cytotoxic T-cell subtype, highly expressed PDCD1, CXCL13, CXCR6, and GZMB , and showed the highest positive correlation in abundance with the CD4_Tex_CXCR6 cells ( ). Similarly, in the CSF, the abundance of CD4_Trm_CXCR6 cells was positively correlated with the population sizes of activated immune cell subsets, such as CD8_Temra_GZMB, NK_GZMB, and B_Memory cells, but was negatively correlated with the abundance of unmatured lymphocytes (eg, CD4_Tm_Nos, CD4_Tn_CCR7 and B_Naive) and myeloid cell subsets (eg, cDC2_CD1E, Mac_MRC1 and Mac_PADI2) ( right). Furthermore, the Mac_SPP1 subset in tumors with high expression of SPP1 and GPNMB, and associated with tumor angiogenesis, immune suppression, and poor prognosis across various cancers, exhibited the strongest negative correlation in abundance with both CD8_Tex_CXCL13 and CD4_Tex_CXCR6 cells ( left and ). Meanwhile, SPP1 signaling coming from macrophages in the CSF was significantly enriched in the PR-pre and NR group (both pretreatment and post-treatment) compared with responders ( ), suggesting that SPP1 might be a potential target for combination therapies with ICI. 
Further analysis of ligand-receptor interactions from CD4_Tex_CXCR6 in BrM tumor to selected lymphocyte subsets, which had high positive correlation in abundance with CD4_Tex_CXCR6 ( , G). We also found potential CXCL13-CXCR3 interaction between CD4_Tex_CXCR6 and selected lymphocyte subsets ( ). CXCL13 (also known as BCA-1) could potentially induce chemotaxis of activated T cells via CXCR3 and recruit B lymphocytes through CXCR5, which is specifically expressed in B cells of tumor samples ( ). Additionally, the antitumor cytokine interleukin (IL)-21, which is known to enhance and sustain cytotoxic cell activity as well as induce B-cell proliferation, differentiation, and immunoglobulin production, was exclusively expressed in the CD4_Tex_CXCR6 cells within tumors ( ). Similar to our findings, CD4_Tex_CXCR6-like phenotypes, akin to CD4+PDCD1+CXCL13+ T-cell subsets, have been reported to activate tumor-associated B cells and correlate with favorable prognosis across diverse cancers. Similarly, proteomic analysis of the CSF also revealed an enrichment of B-cell and T-cell activation-related pathways in CR-pre compared with NR-pre ( ). Specifically, at the cell subpopulation level, B_Memory and CD8_Tem_TCF7 cells, characterized by stemness-related gene expression, showed increased abundance in the CR-pre compared with NR-pre group ( ). Additionally, the cell development trajectory analysis revealed that the CSF CD8+ T cells were more inclined towards transiting into CD8+ Temra cells in responders and post-ICI patients ( ).
 

## CXCR6 served as a specific marker for CD4+PDCD1+ T cells linked to ICI response
Next, mIHC/IF (multiplex immunohistochemistry/immunofluorescence) assays revealed that BrM tumor tissues from the CR-pre group contained significantly more CD4+PDCD1+CXCR6+ T cells ( ). In contrast, no significant differences were observed among other cell subsets labeled with PD-1, CD4, CD8, and CD3 ( ). This suggests that the CD4+PDCD1+CXCR6+ T-cell subset might serve as a predictive marker for ICI response in BrM tumors, consistent with findings from the CSF of responder groups. We further integrated multiple published datasets to explore the distribution of CD4+PDCD1+CXCR6+ T-cell subsets in different sites of patients with lung cancer who received ICI treatment ( ). We found that this T-cell subset was nearly absent in peripheral blood, relatively scarce in normal lung tissue, but more abundant in primary lung cancer tumors and BrM tumors ( ).
Moreover, we integrated two single-cell sequencing datasets of enriched CD4+ T cells from patients with lung cancer, which contained both scRNA-seq and single-cell TCR sequencing (scTCR-seq) data (total of 63,956 cells), to further explore the characteristics of CD4+PDCD1+CXCR6+ T cells ( ). We found that PDCD1+CD4+ T cells were clearly divided into two subsets: CXCR6+ and CXCR6- (cluster 10 and 5, respectively; ). The abundance distribution of these subsets across different sites in patients was consistent with the previous results ( ), and there was overlap in TCR clonotypes, implying possible state transition between these two subsets ( ). Although both subsets highly expressed CXCL13 and IL21, only CD4+PDCD1+CXCR6+ T cells exhibited higher expression of IFNG, PRF1, and GNLY ( ). More importantly, we observed that, after ICI treatment, CD4+PDCD1+CXCR6+ T cells showed significantly greater TCR clonal expansion in ICI responders at the primary lung tumor site compared with non-responders, while no statistical difference was found in the CD4+PDCD1+CXCR6− T cells ( ). These results suggested that CXCR6 could serve as a more accurate and detailed marker for defining immune therapy responsive CD4+ T-cell subsets (PDCD1+ and/or CXCL13+), which was not reported by previous studies. 

## cDCs of ICI responders interacted with CD4+PDCD1+CXCR6+ T cells for enhanced antigen presentation and inflammatory activation
CSF serves as a niche for central nervous system (CNS) immune cells. Under pathological conditions, antigen-presenting cells (APCs), particularly mature DCs, migrate through the CSF into lymphatic vessels and then to deep cervical lymph nodes, where they activate and recruit T cells to the CNS for immune responses. Among the signals received by the CD4_Tex_CXCR6 cells, MHC-II stands out with the strongest relative strength ( ), and APCs, including cDCs, monocytes, macrophages, and B cells, were the strongest source cells of incoming signals towards CD4_Tex_CXCR6 cells ( ). Similar to the CD4_Tex_CXCR6 in tumors, the CD4+ T cells in the ICI-responsive group received more MHC-II signals from APCs, particularly cDCs ( and ). In addition, the main types of APCs—cDCs, pDCs, monocytes, macrophages, and B cells—demonstrated higher antigen processing and presentation functional scores in the responders (CR_pre), whereas no such increase was observed in APCs from PR-pre ( ). Furthermore, proteomics assays independently verified the CSF samples of ICI responders (CR-pre) were enriched for proteins related to antigen presentation signaling via MHC-II, in comparison to NR-pre ( ).
 cDCs in the CSF were primarily divided into two classical subsets: cDC1 and cDC2, along with a mature/migratory DC (migDC) subset ( ). Using CytoTRACE, we found that cDC2 and migDC in responders had higher cell stemness ( and ). Further analysis revealed that immune-suppressive genes AREG and VSIG4 were more strongly expressed in the cDC2 of non-responders ( ), and the relatively lower-stemness cDC2_IFI6 subset is more prevalent in NR-pre compared with CR-pre ( ). Additionally, previously reported immune-suppressive characteristic genes of the migDC subset—including CD274, PDCD1LG2, CD200, EBI3, IDO1, SOCS1, SOCS2, and LGALS9, as well as cytokines CCL17 and CCL19—were highly expressed in the NR group ( ). Recent studies have reported an increase in LAMP3 protein, a key marker of migDC, in the CSF of ICI responding patients. We also observed upregulation of the signature of migDC (LAMP3+ DC) subset in CR-pre by proteomics ( ; Methods), suggesting that CSF of responders might possess more “early-stage” LAMP3+ DCs with weaker immune-suppressive functions, which may be favorable for recruiting and activating T cells in the lymph nodes.
We further explored the different differentiation programs of cDCs between sample groups ( ; Methods). CR-pre and NR-pre had 261 and 399 unique significant genes associated with differentiation from cDC2 to migDC, respectively, along with 388 shared genes ( ). The shared genes were enriched in functions consistent with previous studies, including myeloid cell migration, differentiation, and immune regulation ( ). Early in the trajectory from cDC2 to migDC in CR-pre, the cells were enriched for genes related to pathways such as IL-1-mediated signaling, collagen catabolic processes, and cell-matrix adhesion ( ). At the end of the trajectory, CR-pre group-specific genes were enriched in lymphocyte activation involved in immune response ( ), whereas NR-pre specific genes were enriched for cytoplasmic translation ( ). Further Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway activity analysis also confirmed that migration and inflammation-related pathways in cDC2 were more active in CR-pre ( ). Consistently, the IL1B-IL1R2 inflammatory signaling pathway in cDCs was not enriched in NR-pre ( and ).

## Pro-inflammatory activation in CSF of ICI responders
Next, we further validated the inflammatory activation in the CSF of CR-pre compared with NR-pre through pathway enrichment analysis (KEGG and REACTOME databases) using the proteomics data ( ). Given the pro-inflammatory activation observed in the CR-pre group, we investigated potential pro-inflammatory cell subsets in the CSF and found that monocytes exhibited significantly higher inflammatory activity in the CR-pre group ( ). Further, inflammatory signaling pathways, including the tumor necrosis factor and NF-kappa B pathways, were more active in monocytes from the CR-pre group compared with NR-pre ( ). Among monocyte subsets, Mono_SELL cells increased in both CR-pre and NR-post compared with NR-pre ( ). A similar Mono_SELL subset was also found in brain tumor tissue ( ), and the monocytes with high expression of SELL and CFP have been reported as predictors of immunotherapy response in primary NSCLC. In the CSF, Mono_SELL cells were enriched for expression of genes related to migration, chemotaxis, and cytokine production ( ) and showed high activity in the activator protein 1-related transcription factor network (JUN, FOS, FOSB and FOSL2) ( ). Moreover, the trajectories of monocyte-macrophage showed that Mono_COLEC12 was at the root of transition, with trajectories leading to two main directions, Mono_SELL cells and macrophages ( ). These results suggest that Mono_SELL represented a transition direction independent of macrophages in both BrM tumor tissue and CSF, and was enriched in the CSF of ICI responders and post-treatment patients.

## Discussion
Our study provided a high-resolution depiction of cellular dynamics during ICI treatment in CSF and BrM tumors of NSCLC, identifying CD4+PDCD1+CXCR6+ T cells as a positive predictor of intracranial tumor responses and uncovering their potential mechanisms in influencing ICI efficacy ( ).
 Only a minority of NSCLC with untreated BrM benefits from ICIs, highlighting the importance to develop valuable biomarkers for better patient selection. In our study, CD4+PDCD1+CXCR6+ T cells in CSF were significantly increased in ICI responders, characterized as both features of Th1 and Trm cells. CXCR6 was part of the tissue-residency gene program and contributed to CD8+ Trm maintenance in tissue, and it was also reported to be the core of cytotoxic T cells to survive in the tumor microenvironment and critical for maximizing antitumor immune responses. CXCR6 further possessed tumor antigen specificity and enhanced the efficacy of anti-PD-1 blockade, providing the basis for ICI treatments. Moreover, with subdividing the baseline treatment response group of CSF into CR-pre (without radiotherapy) and PR-pre (with radiotherapy), we found that CD4+PDCD1+CXCR6+ T cells were increased in both groups, indicating that this cell subset uniquely predicts immune therapy response efficacy, independent of brain radiotherapy. CD4+PDCD1+CXCR6+ T cells could modulate the activation of immune response with increasing cytotoxic cytokines in hepatocellular carcinoma development in previous publications, together supporting our finding of CD4+PDCD1+CXCR6+ T cells as the positive predictor of ICI intracranial response.
Furthermore, CD4+PDCD1+CXCR6+ T cells presented highly functional and transcriptomic similarities in both CSF and BrM tumor environments in our study, displaying an exhausted phenotype within tumor tissues. Consistent with findings in the CSF, our independent mIHC/IF cohort also revealed an increase in CD4+PDCD1+CXCR6+ T cells in BrM tumor of ICI responders. These findings provided evidence supporting the potential use of the CSF immune microenvironment as a predictor of immunotherapy outcomes for brain metastases, and further validation is needed. The reason for the similarity may be that CXCR6 signaling emerged as a potential mechanism facilitating antigen-specific T-cell migration into the brain via the CSF. This pathway also plays a critical role in linking microglia and T cells, supporting the maintenance of forebrain Trm cells as well as the activation of microglia and astrocytes in the CNS, suggesting the potential migration of CD4+PDCD1+CXCR6+ T cells between the CSF and BrM. Similar to previous reports, this cell population within the tissue exhibited high expression of CXCL13 and IL-21 and engaged in close interactions with tumor-specific CD8+ T cells and B cells. The close correlation of CD4+PDCD1+CXCR6+ T cells and active lymphocytes in CSF and BrM tumor would help shape the immune environment to better promote the antitumor function of ICIs therapy in BrM. In addition, we further identified that CXCR6 can serve as a potentially more precise marker for the defined immune therapy-predictive and responsive T-cell subsets (CD4+PDCD1+ and/or CXCL13+ T cells).
The brain was not an immune-privileged organ as in traditional perspective; nevertheless, the CNS lymphatic system, consist of functional lymphatic vessels lining the dural sinuses and deep cervical lymph nodes, allowed immune cells from the CSF to communicate with peripheral immune system. One key factor enabling CNS lesions to respond to ICIs is the activation of antigen-presenting cells, which facilitates T-cell entry into the intracranial system via the peripheral lymphatic system. Our study found that CD4+PDCD1+CXCR6+ T cells closely interact with cDCs in the tumor microenvironment, and that cDCs and other APCs in the CSF of ICI responders exhibited enhanced antigen presentation, potentially bridging the BrM and peripheral immune systems. In primary NSCLC, a recent study reported that tumor antigen-specific CXCL13+PDCD1+CD4+ T cells closely interact with DCs in the tumor microenvironment, potentially sustaining antigen presentation, supporting the presence of similar CD4+ T cell-DC interactions in our cohort. Despite the lack of functional upregulation of antigen-presenting cells in the PR_pre group receiving ICI combined with brain radiation, these patients still responded to treatment. This suggests that radiotherapy may enhance immunotherapy responsiveness through mechanisms other than boosting antigen presentation. Preclinical studies have demonstrated that radiotherapy synergizes with ICI by promoting T-cell infiltration and activating endogenous antigen-specific immune responses. Moreover, our clinical trial, along with others, has provided preliminary evidence of both improved progression-free survival (PFS) and intracranial PFS observed in a group of ICI plus brain radiotherapy. Further exploration is needed to elucidate the role of CD4+PDCD1+CXCR6+ T cells and APCs in the response to combined treatments, such as immunotherapy and chemo-radiotherapy.
The present study had certain limitations. One limitation in this study was a cohort with restricted samples for scRNA-seq analysis, so we validated our results by proteomics, mIHC/IF assay and flow cytometry in multiple independent cohorts and integrated data from published datasets. Additionally, under ICI therapy, both the migration of T lymphocytes among brain tissue, CSF, and the peripheral immune system, and the underlying mechanisms of CD4+PDCD1+CXCR6+ T cells warrant further investigation. Although matched peripheral blood samples were not collected due to prior evidence that plasma biomarkers were less representative than CSF, we will explore the role of peripheral blood in future studies. Lastly, due to the complex nature of real-world study, prospective researches are needed.
In summary, we performed a comprehensive single-cell immune landscape analysis for CSF and BrM tumors of NSCLC following ICI therapy for different response statuses, which advanced the current understanding of the CNS immunity in BrM under ICIs. Importantly, given that CSF provides a minimally invasive alternative to high-risk BrM tumor biopsies and facilitates the profiling of immune cells in BrM and the CNS, highlighting CD4+PDCD1+CXCR6+ T cells in CSF as predictive markers of intracranial ICI response. Our findings underscored the potential use of CSF in individualized treatment and dynamic monitoring of ICI efficacy.

## Methods

## Patient enrollment and sample collection
Patients with NSCLC and BrM planning to receive ICI treatment were enrolled in the prospective cohort, and dynamic CSF samples (n=20) before ICI and during ICI therapy were collected through lumbar puncture and used for cytological examination and scRNA-seq analysis. BrM tumors (n=4) were also collected if they have the indications of surgery, with the integrated scRNA-seq datasets of BrM tumor (n=16) from external database ( ). The “confirmed responders” (CR) group included samples from patients showing effective response without brain radiotherapy, including partial response of BrM lesions or stable disease of intracranial lesions more than 6 months using RANO-BM assessment. Pre-ICI (before ICI treatment) samples were collected from CR patients due to clinical sampling constraints. The “potential responders” (PR) group contained samples from patients showing intracranial response with ICI plus brain radiotherapy using RANO-BM assessment, and both pre-ICI and post-ICI (after ICI treatment) samples were collected. Patients with no response or disease progression post-ICI were classified as the “non-responders” (NR) group, and both pre-ICI and post-ICI samples were collected. Additionally, samples from patients not treated with ICI were used as background controls. The cohort of CSF for scRNA-seq included three CR-pre, three PR-pre, one PR-post, four NR-pre, five NR-post and four controls ( ). CSF from NSCLC with CNS patients (n=8) was collected for validation of CD4+PDCD1+CXCR6+ T cells by flow cytometry analysis in another cohort ( ). Moreover, CSF samples for proteomics analysis (n=31) and BrM samples (n=25) for immunofluorescence colocalization, collected before and after ICI treatment, were obtained from the specimen repository to validate findings in an independent cohort ( ).

## scRNA-seq data generation
Fresh CSF was centrifuged at 300×g for 5 min to separate the cells from the supernatant. After removing the supernatant, the cells were resuspended in sample buffer and centrifuged again at 300×g for 5 min. The cells from the CSF were then stored in new sample buffer. BrM tumors were placed in MACS Tissue Storage Solution (Miltenyi Biotec) after surgery. Single-cell isolation was processed as following methods: after washing with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), tissues were minced into small pieces on ice, then enzymatically digested with 250 U/mL collagenase I (Gibco), 100 U/mL collagenase IV (Gibco), and 30 U/mL DNase I (Worthington) for 45 min at 37°C, with agitation. Next, samples were passed through a 70 µm cell strainer and centrifuged at 300×g for 5 min. After removing the supernatant, cells were suspended in Red Blood Cell Lysis Buffer (Miltenyi Biotec). After washing with PBS containing 0.04% bovine serum albumin (BSA), cell pellets were resuspended in PBS containing 0.04% BSA and passed through a 35 µm cell strainer. The cell viability and activity of the sample were assessed using the BD Rhapsody Scanner to ensure the suitability of the cells for subsequent single-cell sequencing. Then, we used the BD Rhapsody Single-Cell Analysis System (BD, USA) for the preparation of single-cell libraries. The libraries were generated following the manufacturer’s protocol. Subsequently, the libraries were subjected to sequencing using the Illumina NextSeq platform.

## scRNA-seq data processing and cell type annotation
scRNA-seq data analysis was performed by NovelBio Bio-Pharm Technology with NovelBrain Cloud Analysis Platform. We applied fastp (https://github.com/OpenGene/fastp) with default parameter for filtering the adaptor sequence and removed the low-quality reads to obtain high-quality data. UMI-tools (https://github.com/CGATOxford/UMI-tools) was applied for single cell transcriptome analysis to identify the cell barcode whitelist. The UMI-based clean data was mapped to the human genome (Ensemble V.100) using STAR (https://github.com/alexdobin/STAR/) with customized parameter from UMI-tools standard pipeline to obtain the UMI counts of each sample. To filter out low-quality cells, cells with inadequate gene detection (less than 500 or more than 7,000 genes) and mitochondria UMI rate over 25% were removed.
Raw count data were normalized, and the highly variable genes were selected per sample by R package Seurat (https://satijalab.org/seurat/). Then, integrating all samples through harmonizing “anchors” to remove batch effect. Number of reads, number of features, and percent of mitochondrial reads were regressed out in the data scaling step. PCA (principal component analysis) was constructed based on the scaled data with top 2,000 high variable genes. The first 30 selected principal components were used for t-SNE (t-distributed stochastic neighbor embedding) or UMAP (Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection) dimension reduction and cell clustering. Clustering was done using the Louvain Modularity algorithm. Differentially-expressed gene (DEG) analysis with multiple testing correction was performed to identify genes under the following criteria between cell subtypes: Log2FC>0.25; padj value<0.05; min.pct>0.10. These DEGs, along with known marker genes, were used to annotate and classify the cell populations. In addition, those expressing signature genes of two or more immune cell types were defined as doublets.

## Collection and analysis of public datasets
We collected three scRNA-seq datasets of NSCLC BrM tumor samples from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database: GSE131907, GSE143423, and GSE186344. After quality control, the public and four novel generated scRNA-seq data analysis followed the same analysis methods as previously described. Additionally, we collected three datasets from patients with lung cancer across different sites to explore the distribution of CD4+PDCD1+CXCR6+ T cells, including GSE131907 (normal lung tissue and early-stage lung cancer), GSE148071 (advanced-stage lung cancer), and GSE242780 (peripheral blood). All re-analyzed scRNA-seq datasets of GEO were listed on .
Given the large number of integrated cells in these three datasets, we performed integration using Scanpy (https://scanpy.readthedocs.io/en/stable/) and batch effect removal with BBKNN (https://github.com/Teichlab/bbknn). Finally, we further collected CD4+ T cells from GSE99254 (peripheral blood, normal lung tissue, and lung cancer) and GSE179994 (primary lung cancer with immunotherapy group information), which contain both scRNA-seq and scTCR-seq data. The scRNA-seq data were integrated and processed using Seurat as previously described, while the scTCR-seq data analysis was conducted using the R package STARTRAC (https://github.com/Japrin/STARTRAC) to quantify TCR clonal expansion and assess cell state transition potential for T-cell clusters.

## Calculation of functional gene module score
To evaluate the potential functions of interest for cell clusters and sample groups, the enrichment scores of functional gene modules were calculated by using R package AUCell (https://www.bioconductor.org/packages/release/bioc/html/AUCell.html). AUCell is ranking-based, and is independent of the gene expression units and the normalization procedure. Ranked expression matrix was built by using the AUCell_buildRankings function, and then AUC value was calculated by using the AUCell_calcAUC function.

## GO and KEGG pathway analysis
Gene Ontology (GO) biological process (BP) terms enrichment analysis for DEGs was conducted using R package clusterProfiler (http://bioconductor.org/packages/release/bioc/html/clusterProfiler.html). Fisher’s exact test was applied to identify the significant GO categories, and Benjamini-Hochberg correction was used to correct the p values for multi-testing. KEGG pathway activity estimates were obtained using the GSVA (https://github.com/rcastelo/GSVA). The GSVA algorithm calculates the pathway enrichment score of gene expression matrix. Then the lmFit function was used to analysis of R package limma (https://bioconductor.org/packages/release/bioc/html/limma.html) to obtain the significantly different pathways.

## Single-cell trajectory inference
For CD4+ and CD8+ T-cell subtypes, PAGA analysis in python package Scanpy was used to reconstruct cell development trajectories. PAGA (https://github.com/theislab/paga) was used to characterize connections between cell subpopulations and infer transitions between different T-cell subsets. In addition, Naïve T cell was set as the root cell, then tl.dpt and tl.draw_graph function in Scanpy were used for the calculation of pseudo-time and visualization of graph, respectively. For the trajectory inference of monocyte and macrophage subtypes, RNA velocity analysis was conducted by using Python package velocyto (http://velocyto.org/velocyto.py/index.html) and scVelo (https://scvelo.readthedocs.io/en/stable/). The spliced and unspliced UMIs for each gene in each cell were counted using velocyto, while for single-cell RNA velocity inference, the dynamical model of scVelo was applied. Following the results of RNA velocity analysis, PAGA is extended by velocity-inferred directionality. The velocity graph was then used to embed RNA velocities in the UMAP map in the form of streamlines. Finally, we reconstructed the developmental trajectories of cDCs with top 300 signature genes calculated by differentialGeneTest function in Monocle2 (https://cole-trapnell-lab.github.io/monocle-release/). CytoTRACE (https://cytotrace.stanford.edu/) analysis was also performed, an algorithm that predicts differentiation states from scRNA-seq data based on the simple yet robust observation that transcriptional diversity decreases during differentiation, to complement the trajectory analysis from Monocle2.

## SCENIC analysis
To assess transcription factor regulation activity, we applied the Single-cell regulatory network inference and clustering (SCENIC) workflow (https://github.com/aertslab/pySCENIC). The SCENIC analysis included four steps: the single-cell gene expression count table for cells from each subtype of macrophage was first fed into SCENIC with a list of 1,390 known human transcription factors (https://scenic.aertslab.org/), and sets of genes that are co-expressed (either positively or negatively) with TFs were identified by random forest models ; putative target genes in each co-expressed module were then subjected to cis-regulatory motif discovery analysis, and only modules with significant motif enrichment in the ±500 bp or ±10 kb region around the TSS for the corresponding TF were kept for further analysis ; the AUCell algorithm of SCENIC was used to score the activity of each regulon in each cell, and regulons with average AUC score (ie, regulon activities) ≥0.05 were retained; and calculating regulon specificity score for monocyte subtype.

## Inference and analysis of cell–cell communication
We evaluated the intercellular communication between different cell types using the R package CellChat (https://github.com/sqjin/CellChat). CellChat takes gene expression data as input and models the probability of intercellular communication by integrating gene expression with known databases of interacting ligands, receptors, and their cofactors. Analysis of intercellular communication between different groups was conducted by using the default workflow.

## Correlation analysis
We calculated the Pearson correlation coefficient (PCC) for the proportion of each cell subset in CSF and tumor tissue. Considering the sparsity of scRNA-seq data, the top 2,000 highly variable genes were selected to calculate the Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient of cell subsets in tumor tissue and CSF.

## Flow cytometry analysis
CSF samples of CNS metastases from patients with NSCLC were collected by lumbar puncture, and cells in CSF were obtained by centrifuging at 300×g for 5 min. Cells were first stimulated in BD Pharmingen Leukocyte Activation Cocktail, with BD GolgiPlug at 37°C for 4 hours. Cells were then stained with antibody against CXCR3 for 10 min at room temperature, then with a mixture of antibodies in Brilliant Stain Buffer Plus (BD Biosciences) for 20 min on ice followed by washing with PBS containing 0.2% BSA. Cells were then fixed and permeabilized with a Cytofix/Cytoperm Fixation/Permeabilization Kit (BD Biosciences, San Jose, California, USA) and then stained with an antibody mix against intracellular cytokines. Antibodies used in flow cytometry included anti-human CD45-BV510, anti-human CD3-BUV805, anti-human CD4-BUV661, anti-human CD8-BV650, anti-human CD279 (PD-1)-BV786, anti-human CD366 (TIM-3) APC/Cyanine7, IFN-γ PE-Cy7, anti-human CXCR6- BV421, anti-human CD49a-PE and anti-human CXCR3-PE-CF594. The gating strategies for cell populations were shown in . All data were acquired using a BD FACSymphony S6 Cell Sorter and analyzed with FlowJo V.10.4 software (BD Biosciences). The cohort of CSF included one NR-pre, one NR-post and six controls ( ).

## Multiplex immunohistochemistry/immunofluorescence assays
mIHC/IF was used to validate the findings of scRNA-seq, including the status of T cells, B cells and immune checkpoint expression. Formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded NSCLC BrM tumors were obtained from specimen repository (n=25). Antibodies used in mIHC/IF included Rabbit anti-Human CXCR6, Rabbit anti-Human CD3, Mouse anti-Human CD4, Mouse anti-Human CD8, Rabbit anti-Human PD-1 and Rabbit anti-Human CD20. We analyzed the relative protein expression in four 5 μm thick slides from each patient, according to the manufacturer’s instructions. PANO7-plex IHC kit (#0004100100; Panovue, Beijing, China) was used for multiplex immunofluorescence staining. Different primary antibodies were applied, horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibody incubation and tyramide signal amplification (TSA) were performed, followed by microwave after TSA operation. After all the antigens were labeled, nuclei were stained with 4’,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, Missouri, USA). Mantra instrument (PerkinElmer, Waltham, Massachusetts, USA) was used for scanning the stained slides and fluorescence spectra were captured, and individual scanned images were combined to construct the image stack. The autofluorescence spectra of the tissue and each fluorescein were extracted into images for analysis. Whole-slide scanning fluorescent images was performed using an Olympus vs200 (Olympus Germany), in conjunction with Olympus UPLXAPO 20× objective lens. Whole slide fluorescence images were analyzed with QuPath software (https://qupath.github.io/). The cohort of BrM tumors included seven CR-pre, two PR-pre, five NR-pre, six NR-post and five controls ( ).

## Proteomics data generation and analysis
A human antibody array (a combination of Human Antibody Array detecting 8000 Human Proteins, RayBiotech) was performed on CSF of 31 patients with NSCLC with BrM, according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Each CSF sample was hybridized to the arrays overnight at 4°C. All slides were scanned using a Mapix V.310 Microarray Scanner and analyzed using Mapix V.8.1 software. Data visualization was performed using Q-Analyzer Software (RayBiotech, Peachtree Corners, Georgia, USA). Median pixel intensities of the local background were subtracted from the median pixel intensities of individual array spots. The average spot intensity across quadruplicate spots was calculated. Inter-slide and intra-slide signals were normalized using positive control spots.
For the GSEA analysis between CR-pre and NR-pre of GO BP terms, KEGG and REACTOM pathways were performed using the R package fgsea, and normalized enrichment score was calculated for each tested term and pathway. All results from the three databases are presented in . For the characteristic set of LAMP3+ DCs, we identified differential genes specific to migDCs compared with other cDCs (log2FC>1). Then these genes were converted to protein names and intersected with the 8,000 proteins available in the array. For the characteristic protein set of CD4_Trm_CXCR6, we selected the intersection of its differential genes within CD4+ T cells and all cells (log2FC>0.25), converted them to protein names, and intersected them with the array proteins, ultimately extracting proteins potentially related to T-cell function ( ). These differential genes are listed in .
Since this protein array only allows relative quantification across samples, existing deconvolution algorithms are not applicable to this data. Therefore, we modified a previously developed rank-enrichment (mean expression rank) algorithm (https://github.com/aapupu/ImmunoRankAge), naming the new approach as “relative rank-enrichment” for inferring the abundance of cells expressing a given set of genes. In detail, for a feature gene set , where are genes also present in the expression matrix, the enrichment score for sample on is calculated as:
 ESGj=rankA,j+rankB,j+rankC,j3∙Nwhere represents the expression rank of gene in sample , and is the number of samples.
We evaluated the performance of the relative rank-enrichment approach and found that it effectively enriched pseudo-bulk expression profiles aggregated from single-cell transcriptomes of CD4+ T-cell subsets. The enrichment scores correlated significantly with the proportions of these CD4+ T-cell subsets ( ), with the exception of the CD4_Tm_FOS subset, which had a very low abundance. Consequently, we applied this relative rank-enrichment to the protein array data to calculate enrichment scores for CD4_Trm_CXCR6, ultimately performing a deconvolution analysis. The cohort of CSF for proteomics included 5 CR-pre, 5 PR-pre, 1 PR-post, 10 NR-pre and 10 NR-post ( ).

## Statistics and reproducibility
No statistical method was used to predetermine sample size. No data were excluded from the analyses. The experiments were not randomized. The investigators were not blinded to allocation during experiments and outcome assessment. Data collection and analysis were not performed blind to the conditions of the experiments.
Statistical analysis was performed in R V.4.1.0, including PCC, Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient, t-test, Wilcoxon rank-sum test, analysis of variance test, and Kruskal-Wallis test. PCC and Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient were calculated the correlation between proportion of each cell subset in CSF and transcriptome similarity of cell subsets in brain tissue and CSF, respectively. Comparisons of numerical variables between two groups were carried out using either the Wilcoxon rank-sum test or t-test, depending on whether the data met the assumptions of normality and equal variances. When using the latter, normality and equal variances were tested and the data distribution was found to be normal.
Single-cell sequencing for each CSF and brain tissue biopsy was performed in one experimental run. This is typical for human studies because cell counts from a given biopsy sample are usually low and cells cannot be analyzed more than once. For the analysis of scRNA-seq and proteomics data, at least three samples were used in each group.
