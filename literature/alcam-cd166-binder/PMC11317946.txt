Four categories of study were used to classify cell cultures: 1) Standard cells cultured in complete media without neuronal trans-differentiation inducers and PBM treatment; 2) Control cells cultured in neuronal trans-differentiation media but not treated with PBM; 3) Cells treated with neuronal trans-differentiation media and NIR irradiation at 825 nm and 5 J/cm2; and 4) Cells treated with neuronal trans-differentiation media and G irradiation at 525 nm and 5 J/cm2.

## Characterization of induced neuronal trans-differentiation of immortalized adipose-derived stem cells

## Flow cytometry and immunofluorescence
Secondary antibody conjugation was used to determine the presence of a cluster of differentiation (CD) and neuronal markers through flow cytometry (BD Accuri Flow Cytometer, BD Biosciences, Johannesburg, South Africa) and immunofluorescence using an inverted fluorescence microscope (Axio Observer Z1, ZEISS, Johannesburg, South Africa). Stem cell markers: CD44 (SAB1405590, Sigma-Aldrich), CD90 (SAB4200497, Sigma-Aldrich), and CD166 (SAB1306488, Sigma-Aldrich) were screened according to the ATCC® guidelines. Following treatment, the decrease in stemness was also determined with these CD markers. Immortalized ADSCs were furthermore screened for early neuron-specific enolase (NSE) (SAB4200571, Sigma-Aldrich) and neuronal nuclear protein (NeuN) (MAB377, Sigma-Aldrich), and late microtubule-associated protein-2 (MAP2) (M9942, Sigma-Aldrich) and Tau (T9450, Sigma-Aldrich, Johannesburg, South Africa) neuronal markers 7 days following PBM treatment. Cells were seeded at 1 × 105 and centrifuged in ice-cold washing buffer (0.01 % w/v sodium azide [S8032, Sigma-Aldrich], phosphate buffer solution [PBS], and 0.1% w/v bovine serum albumin (BSA; A2153, Sigma-Aldrich). The cells were gently resuspended in blocking buffer (10% w/v BSA, PBS) and incubated on ice for 30 minutes. The washing step was repeated three times. The trans-differentiated immortalized ADSCs were re-suspended with primary mouse and/or rabbit anti-human antibodies in working buffer (0.01% w/v sodium azide, PBS, 0.1% w/v BSA, and 2% FBS) and incubated on ice for 30 minutes. The immortalized ADSCs were washed three times by centrifugation. On ice, immortalized ADSCs were incubated for 30 minutes with secondary fluorescent antibodies: fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC) goat anti-rabbit (NB720-F, Novus Biologicals, Cape Town, South Africa) for CD166 cell samples and FITC goat anti-mouse (NB720-F, Novus Biologicals) for the remaining cell samples. Lastly, stained cells were washed by centrifugation at 4000 × g for 5 minutes three times. Immortalized ADSCs were detected through antigenic flow cytometry and immunofluorescence by the conjugated fluorescent markers to determine the presence or absence of the CD and neuronal markers.

## Morphology: inverted light microscopy
Morphological observations were observed and studied 24 hours, 48 hours, and 7 days following laser treatment by inverted light microscopy (CKX41, OLYMPUS, Cape Town, South Africa). These observations were photographed with a digital camera connected to the microscope (SC30, OLYMPUS) which makes use of the cellSens Imaging Software (OLYMPUS). Data obtained from morphology was quantitatively analyzed using ImageJ, a public Java domain image processing system (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA).

## Biochemical analysis

## Cell viability: Trypan Blue exclusion assay
The Trypan Blue Staining technique (0.4%) (T10282, Thermo Fisher Scientific) was used to perform a color exclusion assay to determine the cell survival rate. The Countess Automated Cell Counter (AMQAX1000, Thermo Fisher Scientific) was used to graphically display cells and in turn to measure the percentage viability and cell concentration.

## Proliferation: ATP luminescence assay
The proliferation of cells was determined with the CellTiter-Glo® 2.0 (G9241, Promega, Johannesburg, South Africa) adenosine triphosphate (ATP) luminescence assay. The Victor NIVO Multilabel Plate Counter (HH3522019094, PerkinElmer, Johannesburg, South Africa) determined luminescent intensity by measuring relative light units (RLUs).

## Membrane permeability: lactate dehydrogenase
The CytoTox96® non-radioactive cytotoxicity assay (G1780, Promega) was used to determine the quantity of lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), as LDH leaks into the media because of cell membrane damage. This study, however, compared the results to a positive control to establish if the LDH was caused by detrimental membrane damage or by membrane permeability owing to trans-differentiation. This was a colorimetric assay with a red by-product, where the reactivity is dependent on NADH, and was measured at a wavelength of 490 nm by a Victor NIVO Multilabel Plate Counter.

## MMP
Mito Red (53271, Sigma-Aldrich), a rhodamine-based dye, was utilized to measure cellular membrane potential because it binds to mitochondria. Cells were incubated with 20 nM Mito Red buffer at a seeding density of 5 × 104 cells/mL for 30 minutes at 37°C. The dye emission was seen using a fluorescence microscope fitted with a rhodamine filter (Axio Observer Z1, ZEISS, Johannesburg, South Africa).

## Protein detection

## Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay
Early, NSE, and late, ciliary neurotrophic factor (CNTF) neuronal markers were detected using the NSE in vitro SimpleStep ELISA® (ab217778, Abcam, Cambridgeshire, UK) and CNTF in vitro SimpleStep ELISA® (ab264608, Abcam) kits, respectively. Following protein extraction, a standard curve was generated with varying concentrations of BSA, and this was used to determine the total protein concentration in each sample. The cell culture supernatant was used to identify early, NSE, and late, CNTF, neuronal markers within samples. All reagents, samples, and standards were prepared at room temperature. To each well on the provided 96-well plate, 50 µL of standard or sample then 50 µL of antibody cocktail was added. The samples were incubated at room temperature for 1 hour. The mixture was aspirated from the wells, and the plate was washed three times with 350 µL washing buffer. After washing, 100 µL of development solution was added to each well. After 10 minutes of incubation, 100 µL of stop solution was added and the optical density was measured at 450 nm.

## Statistical analysis
Biochemical assays were conducted in n = 4. Spectrophotometry studies were performed using a blank sample taken from the relevant data gathered. Statistical analyses were conducted on the datasets using unpaired t-test to compare two groups and one-way analysis of variance followed by Tukey’s post hoc test for comparisons involving more than two groups with a single variable. These analyses were performed using SigmaPlot program v.12 (Systat Software Inc., San Jose, CA, USA). All data were expressed as mean ± SE. Differences were considered significant at P < 0.05.

## Results

## Neuronal marker expression
The immortalized ADSCs were characterized with flow cytometry and immunofluorescence following neuronal induction and PBM treatment to establish loss or maintenance of stemness and the presence of neuronal markers indicative of cell trans-differentiation. The CD markers; CD44, CD90, and CD166 are established indicators of immortalized ADSC maintenance. The early, NSE and NeuN, and late, MAP2 and Tau, neuronal markers determined the rate of trans-differentiation into neuronal-like cells. A decrease in CD44, CD90, and CD166 stem cell expression was observed in immortalized ADSCs exposed to IT media, further, a statistically significant decrease was observed in cells also treated with PBM ( A). Following data analysis, cells exposed to IT 1 neuronal media, NIR PBM, and G PBM showed significant (P < 0.001) decreases in CD44 and CD166. Immortalized ADSCs treated with IT 2 neuronal media showed a significant (P < 0.001) decrease in G PBM CD44, and NIR PBM CD44, CD90, and CD166. Cells treated with IT 3 neuronal media also showed a significant (P < 0.001) decrease in stemness in the G PBM CD44, CD90, and CD166 and NIR PBM CD90 and CD166. Notably, a significant (P < 0.001) increase in CD44 expression in IT 3 neuronal media and NIR PBM was observed, however, this observation was expected as CD44 is a marker commonly expressed in neural cell types thus being suggestive of immortalized ADSC trans-differentiation in neuronal-like cells (Sawada et al., 2020).
 Additionally, flow cytometry was used to detect early and late neuronal marker expression to determine whether cells had trans-differentiated into neuronal-like cells following trans-differentiation media incubation and PBM treatment ( A). Immortalized ADSCs treated with IT 1 neuronal medium and G PBM treatment presented a significant (P < 0.001) increase in NeuN and Tau compared to the control group. Cells treated with IT 1 neuronal medium and NIR PBM treatment revealed a significant (P < 0.001) increase in NeuN expression compared to that of the control group. Post-PBM treatment, immortalized ADSCs treated with IT 2 neuronal medium and NIR PBM irradiation indicated significant (P < 0.001) increases in MAP2 expression compared to the control. The control cells treated only with IT 3 neuronal medium showed a significant (P < 0.001) increase in Tau. Cells treated with IT 3 neuronal media and G PBM irradiation indicated significant (P < 0.001) increases in comparison to the control group in NeuN and MAP2 expression. Immortalized ADSCs treated with IT 3 neuronal media and NIR PBM showed a significant (P < 0.001) increase in NeuN and NSE expression compared to the control group.
 Immortalized ADSCs were qualitatively analyzed by immunofluorescence for CD44, CD90, CD166, and neuronal markers NeuN, NSE, MAP2, and Tau ( B and 2B). Fluorescein isothiocyanate, a green fluorescence visualized protein expression; and DAPI, a blue fluorescence visualized the nucleus, filter channels were utilized. A stable maintenance of immortalized ADSC CD marker expression was observed amongst all IT 2 neuronal media experimental groups ( B-ii)). B-ii showed an increased expression of NeuN, NSE, MAP2, and Tau when treated with IT 2 neuronal medium and NIR PBM and G PBM. With reference to B-iii, MAP2 and Tau expressions were elevated by the combinational treatment of IT 3 neuronal media and NIR PBM, while G PBM treatment had increased Tau expression only.

## Morphological observation
Morphological observations revealed that immortalized ADSCs exposed to IT 1 neuronal media and PBM treatment became gradually confluent over time with little morphological changes compared to the control group ( A). Cells trans-differentiated with IT 2 neuronal media ( B) and PBM irradiation showed some cytoplasmic extension morphological changes with limited confluency. Immortalized ADSCs exposed to IT 3 neuronal media similarly lacked cell confluency, however, the cells exposed to G PBM treatment displayed the characteristic shrinking of the cytoplasm towards the nucleus and elongated extensions indicative of a neuronal cell type in comparison to the control group at 7 days post-PBM irradiation ( C).
 

## Evaluation of molecular and cellular components

## Examination of the viability and proliferation of trans-differentiated cells
The trypan blue exclusion assay was used to measure the effect of neuronal induction media and PBM treatment on immortalized ADSCs. Viable cells have intact cellular membranes preventing the dye from penetrating the cells; the demolished membrane of non-viable cells cannot prevent the dye from entering the cell thus allowing distinctions to be made. Following viability measurements, no significant increases or decreases were observed compared to the standard on cells exposed to IT 1, IT 2, or IT 3 neuronal media post-PBM treatment ( A). Viability was maintained throughout the investigative period.
 The ATP Luminescence Assay was used to establish the IT neuronal media and PBM irradiation effects on immortalized ADSC proliferation and its interdependent relationship with metabolism. This assay produces a luminescent signal through the luciferase enzyme that is equivalent to the concentration of ATP in a sample. The ATP concentration is proportional to the rate of proliferation. An upregulation in ATP indicates an upregulation in the stimulation of the mitochondria and cellular proliferation, conversely. Cellular proliferation was measured at 24 hours, 48 hours, and 7 days post-PBM irradiation ( B). Results indicated that the IT 1 neuronal medium and G PBM irradiation, IT 2 neuronal medium control, and IT 3 neuronal medium control, NIR, and G PBM significantly (P < 0.05) proliferated at 24 hours post-PBM treatment; IT 3 neuronal media and G PBM treatment had the most significant (P < 0.05) increase in proliferation compared with the IT 3 control NIR PBM experimental group. It was observed that the IT 2 and IT 3 control, and IT2 G PBM irradiated groups significantly (P < 0.05) increased proliferation at 48 hours post-PBM treatment in comparison with the standard. The NIR and G PBM groups treated with IT 3 neuronal media significantly (P < 0.001) increased ATP production compared with the standard group and the IT 3 control (P < 0.01) at 48 hours post-PBM treatment. At 7 days post-PBM irradiation, the IT 1 control, NIR, and G PBM groups showed significant (P < 0.05) increases in proliferation compared to the standard group. The IT 2 control and NIR PBM groups, IT 3 NIR and G PBM groups (P < 0.001), and IT 2 G PBM group and IT 3 control group (P < 0.01) indicated significant decreases in ATP production compared to the standard group at 7 days post-PBM treatment.

## Membrane permeability
Membrane permeability was measured with the NADH-dependent CytoTox96® Non-Radioactive Cytotoxicity Assay. If the membrane of a cell is damaged, LDH leaks into the media, and this can be an indication of cytotoxicity or membrane permeability damage. At 24 and 48 hours post-PBM irradiation, all treatment groups showed a significant (P < 0.05) increase in LDH leakage compared with the standard group ( C). Seven days post-PBM treatment, IT 1 (P < 0.05), IT 2 (P < 0.001), and IT 3 (P < 0.05) exhibited significant increases in LDH activity compared with the standard group. In comparison with the other experimental groups, the IT 2 treated immortalized ADSC control group revealed the most significant (P < 0.05) increase in LDH activity at 7 days post-PBM treatment. While these increases were significant in comparison to the standard group and control group, these increases were not indicative of detrimental concentrations when compared with the experimental positive control representing 100% toxicity. Thus, it can be concluded that the identified LDH was due to membrane permeability and not cell cytotoxicity.

## MMP
Cytochrome c Oxidase (CcO) specifically absorbs light within the mitochondria of mammalian cells (Hamblin, 2018). Light absorption results in nitric oxide dissociating from CcO to restore the electron transport chain, stimulating the MMP (de Freitas and Hamblin, 2016). In the following experiment, the effect of IT neuronal media, NIR PBM, and G PBM on the MMP was determined through immunofluorescence microscopy at 48 hours and 7 days post-PBM treatment. Due to limited resources, the effect of PBM after 24 hours was not explored. The images were analyzed using ImageJ to determine the mean fluorescence intensity. Results indicated that cells maintained their MMP over time after IT treatment and irradiation ( B, D, and F). The analysis demonstrated that both the IT 1 control group (P < 0.01) and the PBM groups exhibited a significant stimulation of MMP at 48 hours and 7 days post-PBM irradiation, in comparison to the standard group ( A). Further analysis revealed that IT 1 PBM treatment significantly stimulated MMP (P < 0.01) compared with the control group at both 48 hours and 7 days post-PBM treatment ( A). Similarly, the analysis indicated that both the IT 2 NIR PBM and G PBM significantly (P < 0.05) stimulated MMP at 48 hours post-PBM exposure compared with the standard and control groups ( C). Furthermore, after 7 days following PBM irradiation, MMP was significantly (P < 0.05) stimulated by IT 2 NIR PBM treatment ( C). In the case of IT 3 treatment, analysis revealed a significant (P < 0.01) stimulation of MMP at 48 hours post-PBM irradiation exposure compared with the standard group ( E). At 7 days post-PBM treatment, the MMP was significantly (P < 0.001) stimulated by all IT 3 PBM groups compared with the standard and control groups ( E).
 

## Neuronal protein marker levels
The early and late neuronal markers, NSE and CNTF respectively, proteins were detected through ELISA ( ). When observing the results of NSE, there were no significant differences in expression amongst any IT or irradiation group ( A). The late neuronal marker, CNTF, increased significantly compared to the control group where IT 3 NIR PBM and IT 3 G PBM irradiation had the most significant increase (P < 0.05; B).
 

## Discussion
Multiple studies have considered the effect of inducing immortalized ADSCs into neuronal cells (Kingham et al., 2007; Urrutia et al., 2019; Wong et al., 2020), and the subsequent effect of applying PBM (George et al., 2020). However, these studies did not consider the use of G PBM to improve trans-differentiation for neuronal cells (George et al., 2020; Crous et al., 2022). Photobiomodulation appears to have a variety of favorable outcomes at the molecular, cellular, and tissular levels based on observations made in prior findings, though, the specific biochemical mechanism through which PBM exerts its therapeutic effects is still under investigation (Chung et al., 2012). The following hypothetical mechanisms include the cellular photo-acceptors that have been suggested most frequently as mediating the biological effects in NIR PBM (780–940 nm) and G PBM (495–570 nm). The movement of inhibitory nitric oxide induced by red and NIR light has been hypothesized to stimulate CcO, consequently enhancing MMP, and assisting the production of mitochondrial ATP for cell proliferation (Chung et al., 2012). The widely acknowledged speculative mechanism of G light PBM is that it initiates opsin signaling and stimulates cellular differentiation (Wang et al., 2016; Serrage et al., 2019). Transient receptor potential channels are suggested to be one downstream target of opsins. The G light stimulation causes the transient receptor potential channel to open, allowing a flood of calcium (Ca2+) ions to enter the cell’s cytoplasm. The Ca2+ ions then stimulate calcium/calmodulin-dependent kinase II activity, which in turn stimulates the phosphorylation of the nuclear transcription factor cAMP response element-binding protein. Sequentially, cAMP response element-binding protein causes various alterations in gene transcription that in turn cause the advantageous effects identified in PBM application (Samanta et al., 2018).
Previous studies have confirmed the presence of stem cell markers prior to trans-differentiation with IT 1, IT 2, and IT 3 without PBM, however, they did not comment on stem cell expression following trans-differentiation media treatment (Ashjian et al., 2003; Cardozo et al., 2010; Jang et al., 2010; Zemel’ko et al., 2013). Ashjian et al. (2003) who formulated IT 1, identified that isolated ADSCs lost stemness as hemopoietic CD11 and CD45 showed a decrease. Compared with the control, IT 2 NIR PBM and G PBM CD44, IT 2 NIR PBM CD90, and IT 2 NIR PBM CD166 showed the most significant decline in expression. Furthermore, significant decreases in expression of IT 3 G CD44, IT 3 CD90 NIR PBM and G PBM, and IT 3 NIR PBM and G PBM in comparison to the control group were identified. The neuronal markers, NeuN, NSE, and MAP2, were screened by Ashjian et al. (2003) following trans-differentiation with IT 1. An increase in NSE was detected, whereas NeuN and late neuronal marker expression were not detected (Ashjian et al., 2003). A previous study that used IT 2 without PBM indicated that early neuronal markers, synaptophysin, TUB-III, GFAP, and Nestin were expressed, but not late neuronal markers (Cardozo et al., 2010). Furthermore, studies that have used components of novel IT 3 to trans-differentiate ADSCs have shown an increase in NeuN, NSE, and MAP2 expression (Ashjian et al., 2003; Jang et al., 2010; Zemel’ko et al., 2013). Immunoreactivity with NeuN, NSE, and MAP2 was significantly increased in cells exposed to IT 3 NIR and G PBM. Early neuronal marker detection is not uncommon in trans-differentiation studies; however, each IT with PBM had a significant increase in early markers compared to most corresponding control groups (Ashjian et al., 2003; Cardozo et al., 2010). The detection of MAP2 and Tau neuronal late markers in this study differs from the results of previous studies that employed these induction types without PBM and did not detect any late neuronal marker expression (Ashjian et al., 2003; Cardozo et al., 2010). This study shows that PBM exposure to IT-treated cells, specifically, IT 3 NIR PBM- and IT 3 G PBM-treated immortalized ADSCs, encourages not only the neurogenesis process but results in more mature neuronal-like cells in comparison to immortalized ADSCs treated with IT media without PBM irradiation. Flow cytometry and immunofluorescent microscopy confirmed that IT 3 NIR PBM and G PBM trans-differentiation was the most efficient of all the experimental groups.
Morphological studies identified typical spindle-shaped morphology characteristics of immortalized ADSCs in the untreated standard group (Crous et al., 2022). The significant variations in morphology seen in the IT 3 G PBM treated group showed similar morphological changes previously observed by trans-differentiation studies; including branching extensions and a shrinking of the nucleus toward itself with some refraction noticed (Ashjian et al., 2003; Cardozo et al., 2010). Immortalized ADSCs treated with IT 3 G PBM exhibited a definitive change in morphology.
The viability of the treated cells was not compromised following any experimental treatments. A high viability was maintained in all cells demonstrating that these trans-differentiation protocols are not detrimental to cell health in vitro. Studies where NIR PBM and G PBM were used on MSCs showed no significant effects on viability (Lee et al., 2017; Nurković et al., 2017; Crous et al., 2022). A proliferation study revealed a significant increase in the IT 3 NIR and G PBM treated group after 48 hours post-PBM treatment. After 7 days, a significant decline in IT 2 and IT 3 experimental groups was observed which may be attributed to a biphasic dose response (Huang et al., 2011). Unfortunately, the studies that specifically used these IT combinations did not report on proliferative effects (Ashjian et al., 2003; Cardozo et al., 2010). Olivos-Cisneros et al. (2021) observed a decrease in proliferation suggestive of cell trans-differentiation and preservation of their neurogenic potential. Moreover, they concluded that the results might suggest that proliferation control is independent of the trans-differentiation mechanism. Tani et al. (2018) studied the effects of red at 635 nm, blue at 405 nm, and NIR at 808 nm PBM on osteoblast differentiation. They observed a decrease in proliferation for NIR PBM treated cells, and no effect on proliferation for red and blue PBM treated cells. It should be noted that they used a very low fluence of 0.4 J/cm2 (Tani et al., 2018). When immortalized ADSCs were treated with NIR or G PBM (825 and 525 nm at 5 J/cm2) PBM, without IT media, a significant increase in proliferation was observed (Anwer et al., 2012; Nurković et al., 2017; Crous et al., 2022). An increase in cellular proliferation was observed when metabolic activity was stimulated, and excessive ATP was generated as seen in cells exposed to IT 1 media and PBM. The decrease in ATP seen in IT 2 exposed cells may be attributed to energy being moved to other signaling pathways to enhance and prime for trans-differentiation; proliferation decreases as trans-differentiation increases (Wang, 2021).
The effect of IT media and PBM on MMP was determined, and results indicated that immortalized ADSCs treated with IT 3 media and NIR PBM displayed a significantly higher fluorescence intensity than the standard and other experimental groups. After 7 days, the MMP was significantly stimulated by IT 3 NIR PBM. Previous studies show a dependent relationship between an increase in the rate of MMP to that of trans-differentiation (Zhang et al., 2017). Photobiomodulation treated cells showed an increase in fluorescence intensity in comparison to the standard, which correlates to the work of Wang et al. (2017b) and Huang et al. (2011) that reported the stimulation of MMP at 810 nm NIR PBM. This increase in MMP was indicative of the priming of cells for trans-differentiation (Li et al., 2017), as trans-differentiation and lineage commitment were observed in protein detection and morphology experiments. Compared with the control group, MMP was more stimulated by PBM than without it, although this was not significant. In this study, cells treated with NIR PBM had a significant overall increase in MMP, showing that NIR PBM affects endogenous MMP modulations. MMP modulation and hyperpolarization have been shown in several studies to act as a trigger for trans-differentiation (Konig et al., 2004; Sundelacruz et al., 2009). Li et al. (2020) trans-differentiated neurogenic stem cells with trans-differentiation media containing EGF (10 ng/mL) and FGF (10 ng/mL), observed an accumulation of MMP, following treatment without PBM. Zhao et al. (2004) saw a significant increase in MMP following MSC trans-differentiation with media that included RA (0.5 M) and BHA (200 M). A previous study trans-differentiated MSCs with induction media containing FGF (4 ng/mL) and observed an increase in MMP activity (Zhao et al., 2004). Previous studies have shown that trans-differentiation media containing RA or indomethacin, components of IT 3, resulted in significant increases in MMP compared to untreated cells (Mareschi et al., 2006; Zhang et al., 2011; Li et al., 2020). Jang et al. (2010) trans-differentiated human ADSCs into functional neurons with trans-differentiation media containing forskolin, of unknown concentrations, and observed significant stimulation of MMP.
The presence of CNTF was detected with ELISA. Cells treated with PBM showed a significant increase in CNTF expression compared to the control group. Additionally, IT 3 NIR PBM and IT 3 G PBM treatment showed the most significant increase in CNTF expression. Significantly, previous studies performed without PBM often reported minimal to no detection of late neuronal markers (Cardozo et al., 2010; Flachsbarth et al., 2018; George et al., 2020).
In conclusion, photobiomodulation improved the ability of immortalized ADSCs to trans-differentiate into neuronal-like cells. Morphology investigations of the trans-differentiated immortalized ADSCs indicated structural alterations in the IT 3 neural medium and G PBM treatment group. Based on protein identification, NIR PBM and G PBM and IT 3 neuronal medium were the most effective at influencing trans-differentiation. The immortalized ADSCs were trans-differentiated and optimized using IT 3 neuronal media and NIR PBM and IT 3 neuronal media and G PBM treatments. The study can be enhanced by using multiple fluences and examining the in vitro effects within a three-dimensional cell culture environment. This approach aims to better optimize the differentiation and proliferation of immortalized ADSCs. The incorporation of three-dimensional cell culture techniques is seen as a potentially beneficial strategy, bridging the gap between in vitro and in vivo studies. This may expedite the translation of findings into clinical trials, particularly in the context of using PBM in stem cell therapy.
