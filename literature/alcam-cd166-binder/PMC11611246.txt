## Abstract
Leukocyte immunoglobulin-like receptor (LILR) B4 (also known as ILT3/CD85k) is an immune checkpoint protein that is highly expressed in solid tumors and hematological malignancies and plays a significant role in the pathophysiology of cancer. LILRB4 is highly expressed in acute myeloid leukemia (AML), and this phenotype is associated with adverse patient outcomes. Its differential expression in tumors compared to normal tissues, its presence in tumor stem cells, and its multifaceted roles in tumorigenesis position it as a promising therapeutic target in AML. Currently, several immunotherapies targeting LILRB4 are undergoing clinical trials. This review summarizes advancements made in the study of LILRB4 in AML, focusing on its structure, ligands, expression, and significance in normal tissues and AML; its protumorigenic effects and mechanisms in AML; and the application of LILRB4-targeted therapies in AML. These insights highlight the potential advantages of LILRB4 as an immunotherapeutic target in the context of AML.
Keywords: LILRB4; Leukemia, myeloid, acute; Immune checkpoint; Tumor microenvironment; Immunotherapy

## Introduction
The leukocyte immunoglobulin-like receptor (LILR) family is a group of receptors expressed on human hematopoietic cells, which are vital for blood cell formation. This family encompasses 13 members, consisting of 6 activating receptors (LILRA1–6), which have immunoreceptor tyrosine-based activation motifs (ITAMs) in their cytoplasmic tails, and 5 inhibitory receptors (LILRB1–5), which contain immunoreceptor tyrosine-based inhibition motifs (ITIMs) in their cytoplasmic tails.[ , ] In addition to LILRAs and LILRBs, there are two related pseudogenes that are non-functional copies of genes. The LILR family plays a critical role in regulating immune responses, ensuring that the immune system responds effectively while avoiding excessive reactions.
Acute myeloid leukemia (AML) is a hematological malignancy originating from hematopoietic stem progenitor cells in the blood. Numerous studies have confirmed the overexpression of LILRB family members in AML cell lines and AML patients.[ , ] In 1997, LILRB4, also known for its various other aliases, such as cluster of differentiation 85k (CD85K), leukocyte immunoglobulin-like receptor-5 (LIR5) and immunoglobulin-like transcript 3 (ILT3), was initially identified as an inhibitory receptor primarily found on monocytes. It shares homology with the mouse glycoprotein (gp49B),[ , ] highlighting its importance in immune regulation. LILRB4 has emerged as a novel immune checkpoint molecule that is significantly overexpressed in various solid tumors and hematological malignancies.[ , , ] Its close association with the biological characteristics of tumors and multiple prognostic indicators has been well established.
This article provides a comprehensive summary of the structural attributes, functional roles, expression patterns, and the significance of LILRB4 in the context of AML. Moreover, potential therapeutic strategies targeting LILRB4 in AML treatment were explored, shedding light on the potential of LILRB4 as a novel target in the field of AML immunotherapy.

## Basic Properties of LILRB4

## Structure of LILRB4
LILRB4 comprises two extracellular immunoglobulin (Ig)-like domains (designated D1 and D2), a transmembrane domain, and three intracellular ITIMs. While the structure of D2 resembles the D4 domain found in other LILRBs, D2 is distinguished by the presence of unique 310 helical turns that limit its interaction with D1. This distinctive feature results in the formation of a D1–D2 interface characterized by an obtuse angle, rendering it both conformationally and electrostatically unsuitable for binding to major histocompatibility complex I (MHC I), unlike the Ig-like domains observed in LILRB1 and LILRB2.[ ] Specific surface regions of D1 and the D1–D2 hinge region facilitate the binding of LILRB4 to unconventional non-MHC class I ligands, such as apolipoprotein E (APOE)[ ] and fibronectin (FN). Within the LILR family, whose genes are located on human chromosome 19q13.4 within the leukocyte receptor complex,[ , ] LILRB4 is one of the most notable polymorphic receptor-encoding genes, exhibiting a minimum of 15 documented single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs).[ , ] Despite these polymorphisms, the precise implications and significance of these genetic variations in LILRB4 function remain unclear.

## Ligands of LILRB4
The specific ligands of LILRB4 have not been fully elucidated. Notably, LILRB4 does not appear to be structurally or electrostatically capable of interactions with the major histocompatibility complex (MHC) molecules,[ ] hormones, or virus products. Hence, it may not be directly involved in the regulation of MHC-mediated antigen-specific T cell activation[ ] or in the cooperative suppression of phagocytosis via CD47,[ ] the activation of natural killer (NK) cells,[ ] immune responses to specific pathogens,[ ] or the maintenance of hematopoietic stem-cell stemness.[ ]
Previous studies have suggested that the activated leukocyte cell adhesion molecule (ALCAM/CD166/MEMD) and APOE serve as ligands for LILRB4. The interaction of ALCAM with LILRB4 has been associated with the promoting of tumor cell growth, while the interaction of APOE with LILRB4 has been linked to the development of AML.[ , ] Recently, the discovery of galectin-8 as a LILRB4 ligand that drives monocytic myeloid-derived suppressor cells (M-MDSCs) defined a class of antibodies that fight solid tumors. However, the complete range of LILRB4 ligands and their implications for various physiological and pathological processes are areas of ongoing investigation.

## ALCAM/CD166
CD166, also known as ALCAM, is a cell-surface protein that consists of five extracellular immunoglobulin (Ig) domains (V1, V2, C1, C2, and C3), in addition to a transmembrane domain and a short cytoplasmic tail.[ , ] CD166 plays a regulatory role by mediating adhesion through its connection to the cytoskeleton, which is facilitated by a supramolecular complex that includes proteins such as ezrin and syntenin.[ ] ILT3 and CD166 are members of the Ig superfamily.
CD166 is expressed on the surfaces of activated T cells, myeloid progenitors, hematopoietic and mesenchymal stem cells, and various types of tumors and has been identified as a ligand of LILRB4. Studies have indicated that the recombinant ILT3.Fc protein has the potential to suppress the growth of tumor cells. This effect is believed to be mediated by interference with homophilic interactions between CD166 molecules on malignant cells.[ ] This highlights the importance of CD166 in cell−cell adhesion and its potential as a target for therapeutic interventions in cancer.

## APOE
Mutagenesis experiments have revealed the significance of specific regions in both APOE and LILRB4 for APOE-induced activation of LILRB4. The critical regions include the N-terminal domain of APOE, as well as the residues P35 and W106 in the first Ig domain and Y121 in the linker region connecting the two Ig domains of LILRB4.[ ]

## Galectin-8
Galectins constitute a family of proteins with evolutionary conservation, demonstrating an affinity for glycans and implicating potential roles in cancer-related processes such as cell survival, angiogenesis, metastasis, and immune modulation.[ , , , , ] Although the specific function of galectins in immune regulation remains a subject of debate,[ ] their involvement in cancer-related mechanisms has gained increasing recognition.[ ] Studies suggest that Gal-8 activates quiescent immune cells while exerting inhibitory effects on activated immune cells.[ ] Within the tumor microenvironment (TME), Gal-8 is associated with T cell dysfunction, as delineated via the tumor immune dysfunction and exclusion (TIDE) model,[ ] underscoring the need for critical mediators at inflammatory sites. Notably, Gal-8 has been shown to bind both soluble and membrane-bound LILRB4, triggering the in vivo expansion of myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) by activating the signal transducer and activator of transcription 3 (STAT3) and inhibiting the nuclear factor κB (NF-κB) through Src homology 2 domain-containing protein tyrosine phosphatase 1 (SHP-1).[ ] The link between NF-kB inhibition and STAT3 activation involves the ADAM17-IL6R pathway. The collaborative interplay of the STAT3 and NF-kB pathways is presumed to promote cancer progression by regulating communication between cancer cells and inflammatory cells.[ ]In vivo experiments have indicated that the interaction between Gal-8 and LILRB4 promotes tumor growth, and this growth can be inhibited by antibodies that block galectin-8 and LILRB4.[ ]
Empirical evidence supporting the direct suppression of T lymphocyte function by a soluble form of ILT3 strongly suggests the presence of a functionally relevant ligand situated on the surface of T cells.[ , ] LILRB4, an inhibitory receptor, exerts its influence on T cell proliferation by inducing unresponsiveness (anergy) in CD4+ helper T cells and fostering the differentiation of T regulatory cells. This finding suggests the likely presence of LILRB4 ligands or the expression of counter-receptors on the surface of T cells.[ ]

## LILRB4 expression, localization, and functions in homeostasis
LILRB4 is expressed on various immune cell types, including monocytes, dendritic cells (DCs), macrophages, plasma cells, activated T cells, NK cells, and osteoclasts.[ , ]
LILRB4 contains ITIM motifs that weaken the cross-linking-dependent activation of receptors containing ITAMs. ITIMs are phosphorylated by the Src family of tyrosine kinases, which in turn enable these phosphorylated ITIMs to effectively recruit Src homology 2 (SH2) domain-containing phosphatases. These phosphatases include proteins such as SHP-1 or SHP-2, as well as the SH2-containing inositol phosphatase (SHIP-1). This leads to the direct inhibition of Syk and the activation of phosphoinositide 3-kinase (PI3K) signaling, ultimately resulting in the termination of the activation signals that were initially induced by ITAM-containing receptors.[ ] FcγRI is a high-affinity IgG receptor found on the surfaces of monocytes and macrophages. It is composed of a α chain that binds ligands and a γ chain containing an ITAM motif. Upon cross-linking of the receptor complex and initiation of downstream signaling events, the ITAM on FcRγ becomes phosphorylated by Src kinases. Subsequently, it recruits Syk, thereby inducing cell activation through the phospholipase C gamma 1 (PLCγ) and PI3K signaling pathways. PLCγ plays a critical role in hydrolyzing PI(4,5)P2 into IP3 and diacylglycerol (DAG). Release of IP3 leads to calcium mobilization, while the activation of PKC by DAG triggers the downstream signaling pathways of NF-κB, NFAT, MAPK, and JNK. This cascade promotes effector functions, maturation, and cytokine release. Additionally, PI3K binds to Ras to activate Ras/Raf signaling and associates with pleckstrin homology domains. This activation step, in turn, leads to the stimulation of Btk and Akt on the cell membrane, resulting in immune cell proliferation and survival and enhanced motility [Figure  ].[ , ] Coexpression of LILRB4 and FcγRI significantly inhibits the FcγRI-induced proinflammatory cytokine TNF-α production by reducing the phosphorylation of key signaling molecules, including Syk, Lck, ERK, LAT, and c-Cbl, and also inhibits cytokine production. Additionally, LILRB4 suppresses the processes of endocytosis and phagocytosis, which are dependent on FcγRI, by facilitating the SHP-dependent dephosphorylation of key molecules, including Syk, clathrin, and the E3 ubiquitin protein ligase Cbl.[ ]
 In monocytes and macrophages, the interaction of LILRB4 with FcγRIII results in the recruitment of SHP-1, leading to the inhibition of downstream signaling and Ca2+ mobilization mediated by FcγRIII.[ ] In various other cell types, LILRB4 plays a pivotal role in suppressing the T cell activation. For instance, it facilitates the conversion of alloreactive CD4+CD45RO+CD25+ T cells into regulatory T cells (Tregs), which subsequently promotes DC-mediated immune tolerance.[ ] Notably, research conducted in humanized mice has provided evidence that LILRB4 effectively suppresses the release of proinflammatory cytokines such as interleukin-1α (IL-1α), IL-6, and IFN-I, as well as the chemokines C-X-C motif chemokine ligand 10 (CXCL10) and CXCL11. These cytokines and chemokines are crucial contributors to the recruitment of effector T cells.[ ]

## Role of LILRB4 in the TME
Prior studies have shown that LILRB4 expression is evident on MDSCs,[ ] DCs exhibiting tolerogenic properties,[ ] and tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs).[ , ] These cell populations contribute to the establishment of a tumorigenesis-permissive and immunosuppressive microenvironment, thereby shielding cancer cells from recognition and elimination by the immune system.[ , , ] Targeting these protective cellular components within the TME may be a strategy for controlling tumor progression.[ ]

## Inhibiting the cytotoxicity of NK cells
FN is a vital component of the extracellular matrix and is notably abundant in the TME. FN has crucial functions in facilitating tumor metastasis and evading immune responses.[ ] Canonical FN receptors include a group of integrins, such as α5β1 and αvβ3, which are ubiquitously expressed on cells, including leukocytes.[ ] Tumor cells that express these integrins contribute to the advancement and spread of tumors by enhancing various aspects, including tumor cell migration, invasion, proliferation, and survival. Interaction of integrins with the extracellular matrix provides the necessary traction for tumor cell invasion.[ ]
Upon binding to FN, these integrins trigger the spleen tyrosine kinase (Syk)-mediated proinflammatory cascade in macrophages.[ ] Syk activation has been identified as an early and requisite signaling event in the development of natural cytotoxicity directed against a variety of cellular targets.[ ]
gp49B, the murine counterpart of ILT3/LILRB4, is an inhibitory receptor identified on activated NK cells and T cells,[ , ] as well as on myeloid cells[ , , , , , , , ] in mice. Very recently, integrins were found to co-tether with FN and gp49B on tissue-resident macrophages.[ ] Integrins and gp49B can also simultaneously bind immobilized FN in close proximity.[ , ] The resulting trimeric gp49B–FN–integrin complex was suggested to be an important focal-adhesion regulator for the inhibition of Syk activation in macrophages[ ] and for bone marrow-derived DCs.[ ] Researchers have shown that LILRB4 is capable of binding to FN on target cells in a trans manner in coordination with integrins in a cis configuration [Figure  ]. This interaction results in the inhibition of Syk activation within NK cells, ultimately leading to a weak NK cell response.
 Consequently, tumor cells expressing FN may evade NK cell attacks, underscoring the potential roles of LILRB4 and FN as attenuators of NK cell cytotoxicity within the TME.[ ]
The interplay between ILT3 and FN serves as a critical “matrix checkpoint” through which the extracellular matrix actively exerts inhibitory control over myeloid cells. When this interaction is interrupted, myeloid cells within the TME might transition toward a stimulatory phenotype, potentially leading to heightened antitumor T cell responses.[ ]

## Inducing MDSCs towards inhibitory phenotype differentiation
MDSCs are a subset of immunosuppressive cells that strongly dampen T cell-mediated antitumor immune responses.[ ] They also contribute to the promotion of angiogenesis and tumor metastasis by orchestrating changes within the TME.[ , , , ] In non-small cell lung cancer, LILRB4 is expressed on both polymorphonuclear-myeloid-derived suppressor cells (PMN-MDSCs) and M-MDSCs. Notably, in PMN-MDSCs, the presence of LILRB4 is associated with adverse outcomes in patients diagnosed with non-small cell lung cancer.[ ]
MDSCs can undergo polarization into either a classically activated (M1) or an alternatively activated (M2) phenotype. M1 MDSCs actively engage in antitumor activities through the secretion of TNF-α and nitric oxide (NO). Conversely, M2-polarized MDSCs play a role in suppressing effector T cells (Teffs), while concurrently activating Tregs. This functional transition is facilitated by the expression of IL-10, transforming growth factor (TGF-β), and arginase-1 (ARG1) [Figure  ].[ , ] Notably, the immunosuppressive effects of MDSCs limit the efficacy of cancer-immunotherapy drugs, which include those targeting programmed death receptor-1 (PD-1), programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1), and cytotoxic T lymphocyte antigen 4 (CTLA4).[ , ] Hence, addressing the presence of tumor-infiltrating MDSCs represents a critical facet of cancer therapy.
Notably, LILRB4 is expressed on MDSCs and is correlated with survival in lung cancer patients.[ ] Research findings have indicated that LILRB4/gp49B contributes to the promotion of MDSC-mediated tumor metastasis. This process involves the modulation of M2 polarization of MDSCs and the suppression of the production of antitumor exosomal miRNAs, which ultimately facilitate tumor migration and invasion.[ ]
Compared with monocytes, M-MDSCs generated through the coculture of human peripheral blood mononuclear cells and SK-MEL-5 cancer cells in vitro exhibit elevated surface expression levels of LILRB4 and LILRB2. Effective reprogramming of tumor-associated M-MDSCs from an immunosuppressive phenotype to an immunostimulatory phenotype has been demonstrated by ILT3 blockade. This transformation leads to a diminished T cell inhibitory capacity imposed by M-MDSCs.[ ] When combined with programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1) blockade, which exerts antagonistic effects, ILT3 blockade synergistically enhances in vitro T cell activation, as indicated by increased IFNγ secretion. This combined approach significantly contributes to improving the TME.[ ]
Moreover, FN expressed by stromal cells in the TME interacts with and activates LILRB4 on MDSCs. FN–LILRB4 interaction recruits SHP-1, inhibiting Syk-mediated FcgR signaling and the immunosuppressive activities of MDSCs.[ ] Taken together, these findings suggest that MDSCs may impede T cell activity through the involvement of LILRB4.

## Inducing DCs tolerance
LILRB4 expressed on DCs plays a crucial role in facilitating the conversion of alloreactive CD4+CD45RO+CD25+ T cells into Tregs. These Tregs, in turn, contribute to the induction of a state of tolerance in DCs.[ ] Interestingly, the presence of IL-10 and/or interferon IFN-α induces the expression of ILT3 and ILT4 in DCs and endothelial cells, increasing their capacity to inhibit allogeneic T cell proliferation.[ ]
Alloantigen-specific T suppressor (Ts) cells of the CD8+ CD28– subtype lead to the upregulation of ILT3 and ILT4 on monocytes and DCs, thereby rendering these antigen presenting cells (APCs) tolerogenic. Tolerogenic APCs exhibit reduced expression of costimulatory molecules and are capable of inducing antigen-specific unresponsiveness in CD4+ T helper cells. Studies on human heart transplant recipients have revealed that patients who experience rejection-free outcomes possess circulating Ts cells. These Ts cells trigger the upregulation of ILT3 and ILT4 in donor APCs,[ ] potentially contributing to the promotion of tolerance.

## Inducing T cell exhaustion
In a study on the impact of an anti-LILRB4 antibody on T cells via cytometry by time of flight (CyTOF), researchers focused on tumor-infiltrating CD3+ T cells and identified 12 clusters with a relative frequency exceeding 0.5%. These clusters included three Treg cell clusters, two CD4+ effector (Teff) cell clusters, three CD8+ T cell clusters, two NKT cell clusters, and one γδ T cell cluster.[ ]
Within the two CD4+ Teff cell clusters, higher LILRB4 expression was noted in PD-1high LAG3high CD4+ Teff cells. Among the three CD8+ T cell clusters, LILRB4 expression was exclusive to the PD-1high LAG3high Tim-3high cluster,[ ] reinforcing the correlation between LILRB4 and inhibitory receptors such as PD-1 and LAG-3. CD4+ and CD8+ T cells expressing these receptors are identified as exhausted T cells due to chronic stimulation, resulting in a compromised response against viruses or tumors. Thus, the coexpression of LILRB4 with other inhibitory receptors implies a potential role for LILRB4 in regulating CD8+ T cell exhaustion.[ ]
Notably, the antitumor effect of anti-LILRB4 antibodies was nullified upon CD8+ T cell depletion, emphasizing the reliance of LILRB4 on suppressing host T cells to facilitate tumor growth.[ ]
Among CD3+ T cells, LILRB4 is expressed at the highest level on Treg cells, followed by CD4+ T cells, and its expression increases with tumor progression.[ ] This expression pattern holds true across various murine tumor models, including B16/F10, mT5 pancreatic tumor, MC38 colon carcinoma, and MB49 bladder cancer models.[ ] Importantly, LILRB4 expression is limited to tumor-infiltrating immune cells and absent in splenic immune cells, highlighting LILRB4 as a promising target for tumor immunotherapy.

## Blocking LILRB4 reduces Treg cell inhibition
In addition to MDSCs, circulating monocytes can be recruited to the TME and further differentiate into TAMs.[ ] Functioning akin to alternatively activated macrophages (M2), TAMs exhibit diminished phagocytotic activities, actively facilitating tumor cell evasion from immune surveillance and promoting metastasis to various tissues and organs.[ ] Extensive investigations have demonstrated the expression of LILRB4 on TAMs in human lung cancer, melanoma, colon carcinoma, and pancreatic carcinoma, as well as in mouse melanoma and colon cancer models.[ , , ] Inhibition of LILRB4, either through antibody blockade or gp49b deficiency, promotes the infiltration of antitumor immune cells into the TME. Moreover, it mitigates the inhibitory effect of Treg cells by modulating the production of IL-1b and iNOS by TAMs.[ ]

## Expression and Function of LILRB4 in AML

## Expression
LILRB4 is expressed at higher levels in primary human AML cells than in their normal counterparts.[ , ] Dobrowolska et al[ ] demonstrated that LILRB4 is expressed at different maturation stages in all M4 and M5 AML cases (characterized by monocytic differentiation) but is not expressed in M1/M2 and M3 AML cases. Furthermore, it is coexpressed with the leukemia stem cell markers CD34 and CD117 in 39% and 50% of cases, respectively. These findings suggest that LILRB4 is a highly sensitive and specific marker that enables the differentiation of AML with monocytic differentiation from other AML subtypes. This distinction is particularly crucial in the realm of differential diagnosis, as AML with monocytic differentiation and microgranular acute promyelocytic leukemia (APL) necessitate distinct treatment strategies.[ , ]
Moreover, the expression of LILRBs and specific ITIM-containing receptors is elevated in Philadelphia chromosome-positive acute lymphoblastic leukemia (PhC b-ALL) cells compared to normal precursor B cells.[ ] Notably, while LILRB4 is not expressed in normal B cells, it was detected in 23 out of 47 B-cell chronic lymphocytic leukemia (CLL) cases.[ ]
In the context of solid tumors, LILRBs are selectively expressed or upregulated in pancreatic cancer cells, breast cancer cells, and lung cancer cells.[ , , , , , ] Notably, Zhang et al[ ] reported a moderate level of LILRB4 expression in certain gastric cancer cells and tissues, where in conjunction with LILRB1, LILRB4 may exert inhibitory effects on NK cell-mediated cytotoxicity directed against gastric cancer cells. Furthermore, it is worth noting that more than 40% of patients diagnosed with specific solid organ tumors, including colorectal cancer, pancreatic cancer, and melanoma, have detectable levels of soluble LILRB4. This soluble form of LILRB4 has the potential to suppress T cell immunity in vitro.[ ]

## LILRB4 signaling in AML
Within the realm of AML, LILRB4 serves as a mediator of T-cell suppression and facilitates the infiltration of leukemia cells through intracellular mechanisms. In an established human T-cell-reconstituted AML mouse model, which involves subcutaneous implantation of THP-1 cells (human monocyte leukemia cells), blockade of LILRB4 was effective at reducing tumor burden and extending overall survival.
Previous studies have suggested the immunosuppressive properties of APOE,[ , ] which binds to LILRB4 and recruits SHP-2 to the two distal intracellular ITIMs of LILRB4.[ ] At these sites, tyrosines at residues 412 and 442 undergo phosphorylation, further activating the NFκB signaling pathway.[ , , ] The urokinase plasminogen activator receptor (uPAR) is a target of NF-κB and actively promotes leukemia cell infiltration by facilitating processes such as cancer invasion, metastasis, survival, and angiogenesis.[ , ] It also hinders T-cell activation through ARG-1 [Figure  ].[ ]
One hallmark feature of monocytic AML is the heightened extramedullary infiltration of tumor cells.[ ] Research has shown that LILRB4+ AML cells tend to migrate to tissues and organs that feature immune privilege, including sites such as the bone marrow, liver, and brain.[ , , ]
The extracellular domain of LILRB4 plays a pivotal role in inhibiting the maturation of T helper cells and cytotoxic cells, which promotes regulatory/suppressive T cell differentiation and facilitates long-term tolerance maintenance.[ ]

## Key amino acid sites of LILRB4 function in AML
In the intracellular domain of LILRB4, 3 ITIMs are located at positions 360, 412, and 442,[ , , ] and feature tyrosine residues as sites for phosphorylation. Both experimental data from in vitro studies and in vivo observational data point toward the critical role of Y412 and Y442 in the capacity of LILRB4 to suppress T cells, whereas Y360 is dispensable for this particular function. It should be noted that all three ITIMs are vital for enabling leukemia cell infiltration.[ ] The infiltration of leukemia cells is impacted by all three ITIMs of LILRB4. Notably, patients diagnosed with monocytic AML exhibit a greater propensity for extramedullary infiltration than individuals diagnosed with other types of leukemia.[ , ] The signaling mediated by LILRB4 actively supports the infiltration of monocytic AML cells.[ ]

## Epigenetic regulation of LILRB4
Robust enrichment of LILRB4 was detected in mixed lineage leukemia (MLL)-rearranged AML, specifically in the monocyte-like MLL subtype, as well as in acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL) associated with KMT2A. This observation aligns with prior research indicating a correlation between LILRB4 expression and MLL-rearranged leukemia.[ , ] Studies have revealed that genes targeted by MLL fusions often exhibit hypomethylation.[ , ] Similarly, the expression levels of LILRB2, LILRB3, and LILRB4 are negatively correlated with promoter methylation, with LILRB4 exhibiting the strongest association. Examination of chromatin immunoprecipitation sequencing (ChIP-seq) data for MLL fusion proteins (H3K79me2, H3K27ac, and H3K4me3) in the MV4-11 (MLL-AF4) and THP-1 (MLL-AF9) cell lines revealed significant enrichment of MLL-N proteins in the gene promoter of LILRB4. This consistent pattern was corroborated by analysis of five additional ChIP-seq datasets, which suggested that MLL fusion proteins play a direct regulatory role in modulating LILRB4 expression through epigenetic mechanisms.[ ]

## Clinical Significance of LILRB4 in AML
High expression of LILRBs is associated with distinct clinical features in patients. A greater percentage of patients with the FAB M4/M5 AML subtype exhibit elevated LILRB expression, while a lower proportion present with the FAB M2/M3 subtype. Additionally, patients with high LILRB expression tend to be older than 60 years and are less likely to exhibit favorable cytogenetic characteristics. In addition, changes in LILRB expression have been linked to mutations in AML patients; elevated LILRB4 expression correlates with RUNX1 mutations,[ ] which predict a favorable outcome.[ ] High LILRB expression in AML patients is also associated with recurrent amplification at loci crucial for AML pathogenesis, including KMT2A and ERG, consistent with previous findings of an association between LILRB4 and MLL-rearranged leukemia.[ , ]
In-depth examination of LILRB4 expression was conducted across 15 leukemia cell lines sourced from the Cancer Cell Line Encyclopedia (CCLE) database, with a focus on those with or without MLL rearrangements according to RNA-seq or Affymetrix microarray analysis. This investigation provided robust confirmation that cell lines expressing MLL fusion genes exhibit markedly elevated levels of LILRB4 expression.[ ] Notably, patients with elevated LILRB4 expression predominantly fell into immune-enriched subcategories characterized by abundant lymphocytes, elevated PD-L1 expression, and amplification of the PD-L1 and PD-L2 genes.[ ] These findings point toward the presence of an immune-rich microenvironment, suggesting potential suitability for immunotherapeutic interventions.
In the context of AML, monocytic differentiation is consistently associated with distinct clinical characteristics that contribute to an unfavorable prognosis.[ , , , , ] These features indicate a greater risk of extramedullary disease,[ ] elevated white blood cell counts,[ ] and coagulation abnormalities.[ , ] Approximately 2% of newly diagnosed AML patients present with extramedullary disease, often coinciding with bone marrow involvement, particularly in cases of monoblastic or monocytic differentiation.[ ] An analysis of the TCGA database identified LILRB4 as a potential predictive marker for overall survival and central nervous system (CNS) involvement in AML,[ ] consistent with its role in AML progression and infiltration.[ , ] In a cohort comprising 56 AML patients spanning both pediatric and adult populations, 20% of the cases exhibited CNS invasion,[ ] and LILRB4 was identified as a significant predictor of CNS involvement. Experimental inhibition of LILRB4 signaling, achieved through knockout or treatment with antagonistic antibodies, led to reduced invasion into internal organs, including the brain.[ ] Additionally, clinical investigations have revealed that, in contrast to non-monocytic AML subtypes, the FAB M4/M5 leukemia subtype is predictive of relapse or extramedullary relapse following allogeneic stem cell transplantation.[ ] The 3-year disease-free survival rate for AML-M5 patients is approximately 25%.[ ]
AML patient risk stratification is based predominantly on cytogenetic and molecular parameters;[ , , , ] however, there is a pressing need for new biomarkers, particularly for cytogenetically normal AML. In this context, ILT3-positive (ILT3+) AML patients frequently exhibit abnormalities linked to an intermediate prognosis, including trisomy 8, t(4;11), and t(9;11).[ , ]
Moreover, ILT3 has been investigated as a potential marker for assessing posttreatment response in monocytic AML patients. Notably, surface ILT3 expression remains detectable in leukemic cells from relapsed or refractory (R/R) AML-M4/M5 patients following treatment.[ ]
In the context of CLL, a study conducted by Colovai et al[ ] revealed the presence of LILRB4 on the B cells of patients diagnosed with CLL. Interestingly, B cells from individuals without CLL displayed no signs of LILRB4 expression. Interestingly, the proportion of patients with LILRB4 positivity was notably greater among CLL patients with extensive lymphoid tissue involvement than among CLL patients without lymphoid tissue involvement. These findings led the investigators to propose that the presence of LILRB4 may be a prognostic factor for predicting lymph node infiltration in CLL patients.[ ]
Within the context of colorectal cancer, there was a notable increase in ILT3 expression in tumor specimens compared to adjacent normal tissues. This heightened expression was further linked to unfavorable clinical features, including positive lymph node metastasis and advanced disease stage, and to decreased overall survival. Notably, high ILT3 expression was found to be negatively correlated with CD45RO+ T cell density, suggesting its potential role in promoting tumor progression by influencing the TME.[ ]

## Advantages and Potential of LILRB4 as a Target for AML Treatment
At present, the primary treatment for most AML patients is chemotherapy. Although the initial induction response rates are promising, disease relapse occurs within five years in most patients, often leading to fatal outcomes. In the search for potential curative treatments for AML, allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT) has gained prominence.[ ] However, posttransplant relapse remains a formidable obstacle to successful treatment, and its occurrence rate varies widely, ranging from 30% to 70%.[ , , , ] The specific factors influencing relapse rates are multifaceted and include variables such as disease status at the time of transplantation, donor source, conditioning regimens, and the T cell composition of the graft.[ , ] Among the factors predictive of extramedullary relapse posttransplantation are the presence of prior extramedullary disease, classification under the French–American–British M4/M5 subtype of leukemia, high-risk cytogenetics, and advanced disease status during transplantation.[ ] In addition, a significant portion of AML patients, particularly elderly patients, face challenges associated with the risks of transplant procedures.[ , , ]
Recently, the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) has approved several novel medications designed to target AML. Notably, venetoclax, a B-cell lymphoma 2 (BCL-2) inhibitor, has emerged as a therapeutic gold standard, particularly for patients aged 65 and older or those who have underlying health conditions that make intensive chemotherapy impractical.[ ] However, recent findings have shed light on a concerning issue: the M5b subtype, characterized as acute monocytic leukemia, has displayed resistance to venetoclax.[ ] Notably, the IC50 value of venetoclax in acute monocytic leukemia significantly exceeds that in other AML subtypes. Genetic mutations are strongly associated with AML-M5.[ ] However, mutations in NPM1, FLT3, and NRAS are not universally present in AML-M5 patients,[ , , ] limiting the applicability of genetic mutation-targeted therapies.
Hence, there is a pressing clinical need for the development of effective treatment strategies tailored to monocytic AML, particularly the M4 and M5 subtypes, which are characterized by increased risk of post-stem cell transplant relapse and resistance to existing therapeutic approaches.
It is important to note that most of the presently explored targets are expressed not only in LSCs and leukemic cells but also in normal hematopoietic progenitor cells, mature cells, and non-hematopoietic cells.[ ] Treatments targeting these widely expressed factors can result in significant bone marrow toxicity and extramedullary organ toxicity, adding to the treatment challenges in AML.
Considering the current treatment landscape in AML, directing therapeutic efforts toward targeting LILRB4 offers several distinct advantages and potential:
LILRB4-targeted therapy has potent and specific cytotoxicity against LILRB4+ AML cells, while previous strategies targeting AML antigens such as CD123 and CD33 led to severe myelotoxicity.[ , ]
Since LILRB4 is expressed in the majority of M4/M5 AML patients, LILRB4-targeted therapy has broader applicability than therapies that specifically target genetic mutations.
LILRB4 is expressed on CD34+/c-Kit+ AML stem cells and plays a crucial role in relapsing or refractory AML.[ , ] LILRB4-targeted agents can effectively target this subset of leukemic cells and have significant implications for managing R/R leukemia.
LILRB4 expression is associated with a poor prognosis,[ , , , , , ] and blocking LILRB4 expression in AML has been shown to suppress tumor progression,[ ] enhance the immune response of T cells,[ ] and improve patient outcomes.
LILRB4 has broad interactions with immune cells, the vasculature, and the stroma within the TME.[ , , , , ] Therefore, LILRB4-targeted therapy can disrupt the immunosuppressive TME and contribute to the inhibition of angiogenesis and stromal remodeling.

## Therapeutic Strategies Targeting LILRB4 and Their Effects on AML
In recent years, the landscape of leukemia treatment has evolved from traditional chemotherapeutic methods to targeted and immunotherapeutic strategies.[ ] In this context, we provide an overview of targeted therapeutic approaches for addressing LILRB4 [Table  ].
 

## Autologous cell therapy
Two autologous cell therapies, specifically, anti-LILRB4 CAR-T cell therapy and anti-LILRB4 synthetic T cell antigen receptor T cell therapy (STAR T cell therapy), have advanced to phase I clinical trials. These trials are being conducted in patients with R/R M4 or M5 AML and R/R AML, respectively.

## Anti-LILRB4 CAR-T cells
Anti-LILRB4 CAR-T cell therapy has demonstrated significant cytotoxicity against AML cells in both MV4-11 cell line cultures and murine xenograft models. In the MV4-11 cell line culture, these CAR-T cells exhibited robust in vitro cytotoxicity against all the AML cell lines expressing LILRB4, leading to a substantial increase in the release of effector cytokines such as IFN-γ and TNF-α. In xenograft models, mice treated with anti-LILRB4 CAR-T cells exhibited a marked reduction in leukemia burden, resulting in extended survival. Notably, among mice sacrificed between days 28 and 37, nearly complete eradication of leukemia was observed.[ ] Importantly, these anti-LILRB4 CAR-T cells demonstrated no toxicity toward CD34+ hematopoietic stem and progenitor cells (HSPCs), as healthy HSPCs do not express LILRB4. Moreover, no toxicity was observed in a humanized hematopoietic reconstitution mouse model. This study underscores the potential of anti-LILRB4 CAR-T cells to improve the prognosis of R/R monocytic AML patients.[ ]
While anti-LILRB4 CAR-T cell therapy shows promise, it is important to note that this therapy may lead to the depletion of healthy monocyte populations. However, this loss can be partially compensated for by neutrophils, although prolonged monocytopenia may increase the susceptibility to infections, such as atypical mycobacteria infections, in specific patient groups, as observed in MonoMAC syndrome. Notably, patients with MonoMAC syndrome often exhibit additional cytopenias in the B and NK cell lineages due to lineage-specific GATA2 mutations.[ ]
Clinical investigations related to anti-LILRB4 CAR-T cells have progressed to phase I clinical trials (NCT04803929), with the primary objective of evaluating the safety and effectiveness of this innovative CAR-T cell therapy targeting LILRB4 in patients with R/R AML (M4/M5).
In addition to its expression in AML cells of the monocyte lineage, LILRB4 is also expressed in other hematological malignancies, including approximately 50% of B-cell CLL cases, where its expression may serve as a prognostic indicator.[ ] Furthermore, research and data analysis have indicated the presence of LILRB4 expression in select patients with multiple myeloma and precursor B-cell ALL, including high-risk patients harboring MLL gene mutations.[ ] As a result, anti-LILRB4 CAR-T cell therapy has potential application value in the treatment of these malignant neoplasms.

## LILRB4 STAR-T cells
We developed nanobody-based STAR-T cells targeting LILRB4 with the aim of introducing an innovative treatment strategy to improve the outcomes of patients with monocytic AML. Our in vitro and in vivo cytotoxicity assessments have consistently shown that all LILRB4-specific STAR-T cells exhibit robust cytotoxic activity against AML cell lines expressing LILRB4. Furthermore, among STAR-T cell types, LILRB4 biparatopic STAR-T cells have demonstrated the highest antileukemic efficacy and the highest rate of in vivo expansion.[ ]
Currently, there are two separate single-center, single-arm, open-label phase I clinical trials supported by Hebei Yanda Ludaopei Hospital (NCT05518357) and Peking University People’s Hospital (NCT05548088) related to this topic. These trials aim to evaluate the safety and effectiveness of LILRB4-specific STAR-T cells in individuals with R/R AML. Additionally, a single-center, single-arm, open-label phase I clinical study sponsored by the Institute of Hematology & Blood Diseases Hospital (NCT05739409) is underway to assess the safety and effectiveness of LILRB4-specific STAR-T cells in individuals with monocytic leukemia.

## Efficacy of the humanized anti-LILRB4 antibody
Rabbits were systematically immunized with the extracellular domain (ECD) protein of LILRB4. Following immunization, the rabbit with the highest serum titer for binding to LILRB4 was identified via Enzyme-Linked Immunosorbent Assay (ELISA). Subsequently, a single memory B cell was isolated and cultured, ultimately leading to the selection of the mAb 128-3 as the primary monoclonal antibody. This selection was based on its superior LILRB4 binding activity, as demonstrated by ELISA; its enhanced affinity for LILRB4, as determined via the Octet RED96 system; and its ability to potently block LILRB4/APOE interactions. Notably, h128-3 exhibited remarkable efficacy in inhibiting the progression of monocytic AML in diverse models, including patient-derived xenograft (PDX) mice and syngeneic immunocompetent AML mice. Furthermore, the h128-3 mAb significantly increased the effectiveness of chemotherapy treatment against AML by promoting the mobilization of leukemia cells. Mechanistic investigations revealed four concerted mechanisms underlying the anti-AML activity of h128-3: (1) reversal of T cell suppression; (2) inhibition of tissue infiltration by monocytic AML cells; (3) induction of antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity (ADCC); and (4) induction of antibody-dependent cellular phagocytosis (ADCP).[ ]
Presently, there are three distinct mAbs targeting LILRB4 undergoing clinical trials. Notably, two of these antibodies, IO-202 and MK-0482, are being evaluated in AML patients. The first LILRB4-targeted antibody approved for clinical use in AML treatment is IO202, an IgG1 monoclonal antibody currently undergoing phase I evaluation in patients with hematologic malignancies (NCT04372433).[ ] IO-202 has received orphan drug designation for the treatment of AML and fast track designation for the treatment of recurrent/refractory AML by the FDA. Although clinical-efficacy data are yet to be disclosed, preclinical studies have demonstrated the efficacy of IO-202 in hLILRB4-transgenic and xenograft mouse models of AML, with favorable tolerability in cynomolgus monkeys.[ ] MK-0482, an IgG4 anti-LILRB4 antibody, is also in phase I clinical trials for patients with recurrent/refractory AML (NCT05038800).[ ]

## The LILRB4 antagonist antibody ATG-034
LILRB4 is upregulated on tolerogenic dendritic cells (tolDCs),[ ] characterized by low expression levels of costimulatory molecules and resistance to DC maturation. Targeting LILRB4 to reprogram tolDCs has emerged as a promising strategy for cancer treatment.
Preliminary investigations of ATG-034 have demonstrated its potential in this context. ATG-034 strongly binds to the LILRB4 protein, with binding occurring in the single-digit nanomolar range. Notably, it effectively counteracts the FN-mediated inhibition of FcR-driven TNF-α cytokine production. ATG-034 successfully induces the transformation of tolDCs into immunogenic DCs, resulting in significant increases in the expression of human leukocyte antigen-DR (HLA-DR), human leukocyte antigen-ABC (HLA-ABC), and CD86 while simultaneously reducing CD206 expression. Moreover, ATG-034 significantly reinvigorated tolDCs, promoting T cell activation, as demonstrated by increased CD25 expression and enhanced IFN-γ secretion, ultimately strengthening the antitumor immune response. The potential of ATG-034 as an innovative cancer treatment strategy is evident.[ ]

## ADCs
The monoclonal antibody h128-3, which has the N297A and N297Q mutations, is efficiently internalized by AML cells that express LILRB4, a crucial initial step in target-specific cytotoxicity for ADCs. To gain a more comprehensive understanding of the dynamics of LILRB4-mediated endocytosis and potential alterations in expression levels during extended anti-LILRB4 monoclonal antibody treatment, further investigations are needed.
Researchers have successfully developed ADCs using a humanized anti-LILRB4 monoclonal antibody and the antimitotic payload monomethyl auristatin F (MMAF). These conjugates were thoroughly characterized and evaluated for their specific cytotoxicity against LILRB4-expressing AML cells, potential toxicity to progenitor cells, pharmacokinetics, and therapeutic effectiveness. Leveraging the ADC linker technology platform has enabled the creation of uniform anti-LILRB4 ADCs with a well-defined ratio of drug antibodies. These homogeneous anti-LILRB4 ADCs can mediate LILRB4 internalization, possess suitable physicochemical properties, and exhibit potent killing activity against LILRB4+ AML cells. Importantly, the data suggest that these ADCs spare normal progenitor cells. Notably, one of these homogeneous conjugates demonstrated significant therapeutic efficacy in a xenograft mouse model of disseminated human AML without causing substantial toxicity.[ ]

## Treatments targeting LILRB4 at the transcriptional, posttranscriptional, and epigenetic levels in AML
LILRB4 expression is regulated at multiple levels, including transcriptional, posttranscriptional, and epigenetic levels, suggesting a potential array of therapeutic avenues.
FTO, which functions as an RNA N6-methyladenosine (m6A) demethylase, has been implicated in oncogenic processes across various cancer types. FTO exerts a positive influence on the expression of LILRB4 in monocytic AML cells by preventing the degradation of LILRB4 mRNA, a process mediated by YTH N6-methyladenosine RNA-binding protein 2 (YTHDF2). This, in turn, leads to the self-renewal of leukemia stem/initiating cells and the reprogramming of the immune response. Research has shown that inhibitors that specifically target FTO can reduce the expression of LILRB4 and PD-L1/2 and notably increase the sensitivity of AML cells to cytotoxicity induced by activated T cells [Figure  ].[ ]
 Protein arginine methyltransferase 5 (PRMT5) is an enzyme responsible for catalyzing the symmetric dimethylation of protein arginine residues and is found in both the cytoplasm and nucleus.[ , ] PRMT5 plays a role in the upregulation of LILRB4 expression in AML cells [Figure  ]. LILRB4 upregulation subsequently activates the mTOR pathway, thereby increasing the invasive capabilities of AML cells.[ ]

## Other Targets of the LILRB Family
Preclinical investigations have unveiled the potential utility of targeting other members of the LILRB receptor family for AML therapy. However, the application of agents targeting these factors in AML has been hindered by the intricate signaling patterns within the AML immune TME and by the limited knowledge regarding their biological characteristics. Consequently, antibody-based therapeutics designed to target these receptors have not progressed to the clinical stage of AML treatment.
Nevertheless, crucial insights have emerged from AML xenograft studies involving antibodies that block LILRB1[ ] or LILRB3.[ ] Notably, these studies showed that inhibiting LILRB signaling leads to increased cell death in AML and enhances the effector functions of NK cells and cytotoxic T lymphocytes (CTLs). Moreover, it suppressed the infiltration of anti-inflammatory MDSCs and Tregs into the AML immune TME. These findings underscore the potential of these alternative receptors within the LILRB family as targets for improving AML therapeutic outcomes.

## Limitations of LILRB4 as an AML Treatment Target
Due to the limited understanding of the detailed mechanisms of LILRB4 in the pathogenesis of AML and the recognition of the human LILRB4 receptor, research on the application of LILRB4 blockade therapy in AML treatment is scarce. Furthermore, the development of therapies targeting LILRB4 in hematological malignancies is not as advanced as that in solid tumors, and most of these studies are preclinical in nature. At present, various therapeutic approaches, including humanized mAbs targeting LILRB4,[ ] CAR-T cells engineered against LILRB4,[ ] ADCs specifically designed to target LILRB4,[ ] and biomimetic inhibitors, are actively under investigation.[ ] Among these, anti-LILRB4 CAR-T cells, LILRB4 STAR-T cells, and mAbs directed against LILRB4 have progressed into phase I clinical trials, but preliminary research findings have yet to be publicly disclosed. Positive outcomes from these clinical trials are highly anticipated.

## Summary
In conclusion, an extensive body of literature underscores the overexpression of LILRB4 in AML. This overexpression phenotype has been implicated in various processes, such as tumorigenesis, proliferation, metastasis, drug resistance, and relapse, not only in AML, but also in several other malignancies. Although the specific pathogenic mechanisms of LILRB4 in AML are yet to be fully elucidated, delineating the impact of transcriptional and posttranslational modifications on its pathogenicity and refining our understanding of the signaling pathways through which it functions in AML will advance the understanding of LILRB4.
LILRB4 is a promising prognostic biomarker in AML and a novel therapeutic target. Preliminary investigations of LILRB4-targeted therapies have shown promising efficacy and safety profiles. Further in-depth research into the aforementioned aspects is warranted to provide novel insights and directions for the clinical application of LILRB4-targeted therapies in AML and beyond.
