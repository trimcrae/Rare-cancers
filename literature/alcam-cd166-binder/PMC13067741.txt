The prognosis for GC is generally poor, with a five-year survival rate below 30%, primarily due to pronounced molecular and cellular heterogeneity, delayed clinical detection, and limited effectiveness of current therapeutic strategies [ ]. Molecular classification categorizes GC into four subtypes—Epstein-Barr virus (EBV)-positive, microsatellite instability-high (MSI-high), genomically stable, and chromosomal instability—each associated with distinct biological features and clinical trajectories [ ]. Despite progress in molecular subtyping, conventional treatment modalities, including surgery, chemotherapy, and targeted therapies, often fail to produce sustained clinical responses in advanced-stage disease. This is largely attributable to therapeutic resistance and the absence of robust biomarkers to guide patient stratification [ ]. For example, while HER2 has been adopted as a biomarker for targeted therapy, its clinical application is constrained by limited sensitivity and specificity [ ]. These challenges underscore the urgent need for innovative approaches to identify reliable biomarkers and therapeutic targets to improve clinical outcomes in GC.
Recent advances in genomic technologies—particularly single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) and spatial transcriptomics (ST)—have substantially enhanced our capacity to interrogate the tumor microenvironment (TME) of GC with unprecedented resolution. scRNA-seq enables high-throughput profiling of gene expression at the single-cell level, revealing the extensive cellular heterogeneity, rare subpopulations, and complex intercellular communications within tumors [ ]. In GC, scRNA-seq has been instrumental in characterizing the diversity of immune and stromal components, identifying novel cellular states, and uncovering potential therapeutic vulnerabilities [ , ]. For instance, Sathe et al. [ ] identified distinct fibroblast subsets in GC using scRNA-seq, implicating them in immune regulation. However, scRNA-seq inherently lacks spatial context, limiting its utility in understanding the physical organization and interactions of cells within the TME. Conversely, ST preserves spatial localization, enabling the mapping of gene expression directly onto tissue architecture [ ]. In GC, Kim et al. [ ] applied spatial transcriptomics to reveal spatially distinct remodeling of the TME in diffuse-type GC, linking invasion patterns to localized microenvironmental adaptations. Nevertheless, most current spatial transcriptomics platforms lack true single-cell resolution (such as 10X Visium, 1 ~ 10 cells per spot) and often yield data at the multicellular spot level [ ]. Although high-resolution technologies such as 10 × Xenium and NanoString CosMx have emerged, their transcript detection efficiency remains limited, often resulting in sparse expression matrices.
Integrating scRNA-seq with ST leverages the strengths of both modalities, enabling single-cell–level reconstruction of tissue architecture and facilitating a more comprehensive understanding of tumor biology [ ]. In other malignancies, such integrative strategies have been transformative. For instance, Ji et al. [ ] combined scRNA-seq with spatial data to uncover spatially localized immunosuppressive niches in pancreatic cancer. In the context of GC—where the TME critically influences tumor progression, immune escape, and therapeutic resistance—such integration is particularly valuable [ ]. Moreover, these approaches enable the identification of spatially resolved cellular interactions and signaling pathways, offering new avenues for the development of targeted interventions.
In this study, we integrated scRNA-seq and ST datasets to dissect the cellular and spatial complexity of the GC TME. The scRNA-seq datasets included GSE167297 (n = 30,365 cells) [ ] and OMIX001073 (n = 142,398 cells) [ ], while ST data from 10 patients were obtained from GSE251950 [ ]. By combining the high-resolution cellular information from scRNA-seq with the spatial context provided by spatial transcriptomics, we reconstructed the spatial landscape of the GC TME at single-cell resolution. This analysis uncovered key intercellular communication axes and signaling pathways associated with prognosis, offering novel insights into candidate biomarkers and therapeutic targets for the precision treatment of GC.

## Materials and methods

## Data acquisition
Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) data for gastric cancer (GC) tumor and paratumor tissues were obtained from public repositories. The OMIX001073 [ ] (n = 10 patients) dataset was retrieved from the Open Archive for Miscellaneous Omics data (OMIX) database, containing raw count matrices and metadata in TSV format. The GSE167297 [ ] (n = 14 patients) dataset was downloaded from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database. Spatial transcriptomic (ST) data of GC patients were acquired from GSE251950 (n = 10 patients), provided as 10X Visium spatial gene expression files, including count matrices, spatial coordinates, and histological images. Bulk RNA-seq data of GC patients for survival and deconvolution analyses, including “The Cancer Genome Atlas” – Stomach Adenocarcinoma (TCGA-STAD) and GEO datasets (GSE23739 (n = 80 samples) [ ], GSE62254 (n = 300 patients) [ ], GSE13861 (n = 90 patients) [ – ], GSE26942 (n = 217 patients) [ ], GSE66229 (n = 400 patients) [ ]), were accessed via GEOquery (version 2.72.0) R package, with gene expression matrices log2-transformed and annotated with gene symbols using platform-specific annotation files.

## Single-cell data, preprocessing, integration, and clustering
scRNA-seq data from GC tumor and paratumor tissues (OMIX001073 and GSE167297) were processed using Seurat (v4.1.3) [ ]. Raw gene expression matrices (UMI counts) were loaded with `Read10X`. Two Seurat objects were created, normalized using `NormalizeData` (log1p transformation), and merged after aligning overlapping genes. Mitochondrial (`^MT-`) and ribosomal (`^RP[SL]`) gene percentages were calculated with `PercentageFeatureSet`, and cell cycle phases were assigned using `CellCycleScoring`. To integrate single-cell data from two sources, the merged object was split by sample, and each subset was independently normalized and used to identify the top 3,000 variable genes using the “vst” method. Immunoglobulin and T-cell receptor genes, mitochondrial and ribosomal protein genes, as well as cell cycle– and heat shock–related genes were excluded. Genes’ variable in at least two-thirds of samples were retained and integrated using Harmony (`runHarmony`) on 50 principal components (PCs), which were reduced to 30 [ ]. Cells were clustered via the Shared Nearest Neighbor (SNN) algorithm using `FindNeighbors` (k = 20) and `FindClusters` (resolution = 0.1). Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified with `FindAllMarkers` (min.pct = 0.25; logfc.threshold = 0.25; Wilcoxon test, adjusted p-values < 0.01), defining ten major cell types: Epithelial (EPCAM, KRT19), T cells (CD3D, CD3E), B cells (CD79A, MS4A1), Plasma (MZB1, XBP1, TNFRSF17), Myeloid cells (CD68, CD14), Mast (TPSAB1, CPA3, KIT), cancer-associated Fibroblasts (CAFs) (COL1A1, FAP), Endothelial cells (PECAM1, VWF), Endocrine cells (CHGA, SCG5, CPE), and Mito cells (MT-CO1, MT-CO3).
T cells, B cells, CAFs, and myeloid cells were iteratively subclustered by repeated normalization, batch correction using Harmony, and clustering. The resulting subpopulations were annotated according to established marker genes as follows: T cells were subdivided into multiple subsets, including CD4 + T naïve cells (CD4T_Naive; CCR7, LEF1, SELL), CD4 + T naïve resting (CD4T_Naive-resting; CCR7, LTB, FTH1), CD4 + memory T cells (CD4_Tmem; IL7R, CD40LG, CCR6), CD4-EGR1|FOS (EGR1, FOS, TNF), regulatory T cells (CD4_Treg; FOXP3, CTLA4, IL2RA), CXCL13⁺ TOX2⁺ CD4 + T cells (CD4T-CXCL13|TOX2; CXCL13, TOX2, CTLA4), CD8 + T naive cells (CD8T_Naive: CCR7, LEF1, SELL), effector memory CD8 + T cells (CD8_Tem-GZMK; GZMK, CCL5, CST7), LGALS1⁺ CD8 + T cells (CD8T-LGALS1; GZMB, NKG7, CCL5), IL17A⁺ CD8 + T cells (CD8T-IL17A; IL17A, KLRB1, RORA), tissue-resident memory CD8 + T cells (CD8_Trm-ZNF683; ZNF683, ITGA1, XCL1), intraepithelial lymphocytes (CD8T_IEL; CD160, KLRC1, ITGAE), GNLY⁺ cytotoxic CD8 + T cells (CD8T-GNLY; GNLY, KLRD1, PRF1), IFI6⁺ interferon-activated CD8 T cells (CD8T-IFI6; IFI6, MX1, ISG15), exhausted CD8 + T cells (CD8_Tex; CTLA4, LAG3, HAVCR2), MAIT cells (CD8T_MAIT; KLRB1, NCR3, SLC4A10), and natural killer cells (NK; GNLY, NKG7, PRF1). B cells were subdivided into B-CD79A|FCER2 (CD79A, MS4A1, FCER2), B-MS4A1|CD74 (MS4A1, CD74, BANK1), B-CD27 (CD79A, TNFRSF13B, BLK), B_EGR1_CD69 (CD83, EGR1, CD69), germinal center B cells (GCB; TCL1A, RGS13, LRMP). CAFs cells were subdivided into fibroblasts (Fibroblasts; DCN, COL1A1, POSTN), smooth muscle cells (SMCs; TAGLN, ACTA2, MYH11), and mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs; DCN, LUM, FBLN1). Myeloid cells were subdivided into conventional dendritic cells (cDC) CLEC9A subtype (cDC-CLEC9A; CLEC9A, DNASE1L3, IDO1), cDC-FCER1A subtype (FCER1G, CLEC10A, CD1C), cDC-LAMP3 subtype (LAMP3, CCL22, CCR7), plasmacytoid dendritic cells (pDC; LILRA4, IRF7, PLD4), macrophages (Macro; CD68, C1QA, APOE), monocytes (Mono; S100A9, IL1B, FCN1), and mast cells (TPSAB1, CPA3, KIT).

## Spatial transcriptomic (ST) data processing, and clustering
ST data from GSE251950 (including 10 patients) were loaded using `Load10X_Spatial` and normalized with `NormalizeData`. Mitochondrial and ribosomal gene percentages were calculated as described above, and cell cycle effects were regressed out. Batch effects were corrected using Harmony (version 1.2.0) [ ] on 30 PCs, followed by clustering at resolution 0.2 with `FindClusters`. DEGs were identified with `FindAllMarkers` (min.pct = 0.25, logfc.threshold = 0.25, adjusted p-values < 0.01).

## Spatial deconvolution and mapping of single cells to spatial coordinates
Cell type deconvolution of ST data was performed using the CARD package (version 1.1) [ ]. A custom `runCARD` function integrated scRNA-seq and spatial data. Single-cell counts and metadata were extracted from a downsampled Seurat object using `GetAssayData`. Spatial counts and coordinates were obtained from spatial Seurat objects. A CARD object was initialized with `createCARDObject`, specifying cell types (`ct.select`), sample identifiers (`sample.varname`), and filtering thresholds (minCountGene = 100, minCountSpot = 5). Deconvolution was performed with `CARD_deconvolution`, and single-cell mapping was conducted using `CARD_SCMapping` (max 10 cells per spot). Results were saved and visualized with `CARD.visualize.pie`.

## Gene ontology (GO) and pathway enrichment analysis

## Tertiary lymphoid structure (TLS) identification
Tertiary lymphoid structures (TLS) were identified by scoring TLS-associated gene signatures (Table S3) [ ] with `AddModuleScore` from Seurat. Spots with scores exceeding mean plus 3 standard deviations were classified as TLS. Enrichment was visualized with `SpatialFeaturePlot`.

## Cell–cell communication analysis
Cell–cell interactions were inferred using the CellChat (version 1.6.1) package [ ] to compare tumor and paratumor conditions. Cell types were defined based on metadata annotations, and the human ligand–receptor interaction database (`CellChatDB.human`) was used. Overexpressed genes and ligand–receptor pairs were identified using `identifyOverExpressedGenes` with thresholds of “thresh.fc > 0.5, thresh.pc > 0.5, and thresh.p < 0.01”. Interactions were projected onto the human protein–protein interaction network, and communication probabilities were estimated using raw expression data. Only interactions supported by at least five cells were retained. Subsequently, signaling pathways were inferred, aggregated into networks, and centrality scores were calculated to assess the relative influence of each cell type. Differential communication patterns between tumor and paratumor samples were visualized using `netVisual_diffInteraction`, and significantly ligand–receptor pairs involving major cell types were highlighted with `netVisual_bubble`.

## Survival analysis
Overall survival (OS) analysis was conducted on TCGA-STAD data using survival (version 0.4.9) [ ] and survminer (version 3.7–0) packages. Signature scores for gene pairs (e.g., OSM-OSMR, ITGB2-ICAM1) were calculated using `AddModuleScore` from Seurat package. Clinical metadata included vital data. Survival times were defined as days to death (deceased) or last follow-up (censored). Optimal cutoffs for signature scores were determined with `surv_cutpoint`, stratifying patients into high/low-risk groups. Kaplan–Meier curves were generated with `survfit` and visualized using `ggsurvplot` (log-rank p-values).
Additionally, OSM and ITGB2 expression stratified patients into four groups (OSMhighITGB2high, OSMhighITGB2low, OSMlowITGB2high, OSMhowITGB2low) across quantile thresholds (set to 0.75), with survival differences assessed and plotted.

## Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT–PCR)
Total RNA was extracted from GC tissues and matched adjacent normal tissues using the RNAprep Pure Polysaccharide/Polyphenol-Rich Plant Total RNA Kit (Tiangen, DP441). RNA concentration and purity were determined with a DeNovix micro-volume spectrophotometer. cDNA was synthesized from 1 μg of total RNA using the HiScript III RT SuperMix for qPCR (+ gDNA wiper) kit (Vazyme, R323-01) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. qRT–PCR was performed on a Roche LightCycler 480 II system using 2 × Taq Pro Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme, Q712-03) in a 20 μL reaction containing 10 μL of master mix, 0.4 μL each of forward and reverse primers (10 μM), 1 μL of cDNA, and nuclease-free water. The thermal cycling conditions were 95 °C for 3 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 10 s and 60 °C for 30 s, with a subsequent melting curve analysis. Relative mRNA expression levels of ITGB2, ICAM1, OSM, and OSMR were calculated using the 2–ΔΔCt method. Each gene was measured in three biological replicates, with paired tumor and adjacent normal tissues analyzed on the same qPCR plate. Primer sequences were: ITGB2, forward 5′-ATGACGGCTTCCATTTCG-3′, reverse 5′-GTCGTAGGTGACTTTCAGGGT-3′; ICAM1, forward 5′-TCTTCCTCGGCCTTCCCATA-3′, reverse 5′-AGGTACCATGGCCCCAAATG-3′; OSM, forward 5′-AGTACCGCGTGCTCCTTG-3′, reverse 5′-CAGCCGTGTCTGAGTTGTCC-3′; and OSMR, forward 5′-GAGGCAAGTCAAGGAAAT-3′, reverse 5′-TGGGCTGTGACAACTCTA-3′. Gene expression levels were normalized to GAPDH and calculated using the 2⁻ΔΔCt method (see Table S7).

## Multiplex immunofluorescence assays
Multiplex staining was performed using the AlphaXTSA® 7-color fluorescent staining kit (AOGMA, AOGMA2681) based on the tyramide signal amplification (TSA) technique. Tissue sections from GC samples were sequentially incubated with primary antibodies against CD68 (AOGMA, AOGMA2681; macrophage marker), VIM (Hua’an, HA721174; Fibroblast’s marker, clone PDH0-01), and ACTA2 (smooth muscle cell marker), followed by corresponding fluorophore-conjugated tyramide reagents according to the manufacturer’s instructions. After staining, nuclei were counterstained with DAPI, and the slides were mounted using an anti-fluorescence quenching mounting medium. Whole-slide fluorescence imaging was acquired using a fluorescence scanning system, and signal colocalization of the target proteins within GC tissue sections was analyzed using HALO image analysis software.

## Regions of interest (ROI)-based analysis of SMC–Macro coexistence
Using multiplex immunofluorescence images, multiple representative regions of interest (ROIs) were selected per section. Within each ROI, cells were segmented based on DAPI nuclear staining and classified as SMCs or Mac according to marker positivity. For each ROI, SMC and Mac abundance was quantified as the percentage of SMCs (ACTA2⁺) or Mac (CD68⁺) cells among total DAPI⁺ nuclei. Coexistence between SMCs and Macro cell types was then assessed by calculating Pearson correlation coefficients across ROIs using the ROI-level percentages of SMCs and Macs.

## Cell culture and siRNA transfection
Human macrophages (CP-H264) and human aortic vascular smooth muscle cells (HA-VSMCs) were obtained from authenticated cell banks and cultured according to the manufacturers’ instructions. Macrophages were maintained in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), while HA-VSMCs were cultured in DMEM containing 10% FBS. All cells were incubated at 37℃ in a humidified atmosphere with 5% CO2.
Small interfering RNAs targeting OSM or ITGB2, as well as a non-targeting negative control siRNA (NC), were synthesized commercially. CP-H264 were transfected with siRNA using Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, cells were seeded at 60–70% confluence and transfected with siRNA at a final concentration of 50nM. After 48 h, cells were harvested for qPCR validation or used for subsequent co-culture experiments.

## Transwell co-culture and proliferation assay
To assess the effect of Macro-derived signals on SMC proliferation, a transwell co-culture system was established using inserts with a 0.4-μm pore size membrane in serum-free medium. Transfected CP-H264 (si-NC, si-OSM, or si-ITGB2) were seeded in the upper chamber, while HA-VSMCs were cultured in the lower chamber, allowing paracrine interaction without direct cell mixing. After 48 h of co-culture, HA-VSMC proliferation was evaluated using the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) assay according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

## Transwell migration assay
The effect of macrophage-derived OSM and ITGB2 on SMC migration was examined using transwell migration assays with 8-μm pore size inserts. HA-VSMCs (2 × 104 cells) were seeded in the upper chamber in serum-free medium, while transfected macrophages were placed in the lower chamber containing complete medium. After 24 h, non-migrated cells on the upper surface of the membrane were removed. Migrated cells on the lower surface were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, stained with crystal violet, and quantified under a light microscope in randomly selected fields.

## Deconvolution of bulk expression data
LinDeconSeq (version 0.1) [ ] was applied to estimate cell type proportions in bulk expression datasets (TCGA-STAD, GSE23739 [ ], GSE62254 [ ], GSE13861 [ – ], GSE26942 [ ], GSE66229 [ ]), using a reference matrix constructed from the average expression from our integrated GC single-cell data, where signature genes defined as variable genes identified from the same dataset.

## Statistical analysis
All statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.3.2. Differential expression used Wilcoxon tests. Survival differences were assessed with log-rank tests. Cell type proportions were compared with t-tests or Wilcoxon tests. Correlations used Spearman’s method. Visualizations were generated with ggplot2 (version 2_3.5.1) [ ], ggpubr (version 0.6.0) [ ], and ComplexHeatmap (version 2.20.0) [ ].

## Results

## Single-cell atlas of tumor and paratumor tissues reveals comprehensive cellular heterogeneity in gastric cancer (GC)
We constructed a detailed single-cell atlas of the GC microenvironment by integrating single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) data from two datasets: GSE167297 (n = 30,365 cells) [ ] and OMIX001073 (n = 142,398 cells) [ ], yielding a total of 172,763 single cells from GC patients. Initial clustering using Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) revealed the overall cellular landscape, identifying ten major cell types: T cells (CD3D, CD3E), B cells (CD19, MS4A1), Plasma cells (MZB1, XBP1), Endothelial cells (PECAM1, PLVAP), Endocrine cells (CHGA, SCG5), cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAF) cells (COL1A1, DCN), Myeloid cells (CD14), Epithelial cells (EPCAM, TFF1), Mast cells (TPSAB1, CPA3), and mitochondrial (Mito) cells (MT-CO1) (Fig.  A; see Materials and Methods). Paired UMAP plots showed the distribution of 61,952 paratumor cells and 110,811 tumor cells, indicating that all major cell types, except Mito cells, were present in both compartments (Fig.  B). Further analysis indicated that these Mito cells were primarily derived from the GSE167297 cohort, reflecting a dataset-specific contribution (Fig.  C). 
Next, we performed fine-grained subclustering of B cells, Myeloid cells, CAFs, and T cells (see Materials and Methods). UMAP visualizations revealed distinct subsets: B cells (n = 18,188 cells) were divided into B-EGR1|CD69, B-CD7, B-MS4A1|CD74, B-CD79A|FCER2, and a germinal center (GC)-specific cluster (Fig.  D; Table S1); Myeloid cells (n = 7,407 cells) into cDC-CLEC9A, cDC-LAMP3, cDC-FCER1A, monocytes (Mono), macrophages (Macro), and plasmacytoid dendritic cells (pDC) (Fig.  E; Table S1); CAFs (n = 5,149 cells) into mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs), Fibroblasts, and smooth muscle cells (SMCs) (Fig.  F; Table S2); and T cells (n = 93,075 cells) into natural killer cells (NK), CD8 + T, CD4 + T, and T-MALAT1 (Fig.  G). Further resolution revealed that T-MALAT1 cells (n = 4,791) clustered into CD4T_Naive-resting and CD8_Tem-GZMK populations (Fig. H-J). CD4 + T cells (n = 34,470) were subdivided into CD4T_Naive, CD4_Tmem, CD4T-EGR1|FOS, CD4T-CXCL13|TOX2, and CD4_Treg, while CD8 + T cells (n = 48,038) comprised CD8T_Naive, CD8_Trm-ZNF683, CD8_Tem-GZMK, CD8T-IL17A, CD8T_MAIT, CD8T_IEL, CD8T-GNLY, CD8T-IFI6, and CD8T-LGALS1, CD8_Tex (Fig. K and L; Table S1). Given the exclusive presence of Mito cells, we assessed their quality by examining mitochondrial gene expression. Violin plots showed that Mito cells had an average mitochondrial gene expression (percent.mt) exceeding 90%, indicating potential low-quality or apoptotic cells (Fig.  M). These cells were excluded from downstream analyses. A dot plot of marker gene expression across all identified cell subtypes shown in Fig.  N, facilitating reliable cell type annotation (Fig.  N; Table S1).
We further investigated cell type enrichment across paratumor and tumor samples (see Materials and Methods), revealing distinct cellular compositions (Fig.  O; Table S2). Paratumor tissues were enriched in stromal components, including Epithelial cells, Fibroblasts, MSCs, and Endocrine cells, suggesting preserved tissue architecture potentially involved in limiting tumor invasion or facilitating repair. In contrast, tumors exhibited elevated levels of Endothelial and SMCs, consistent with active angiogenesis and structural remodeling to support tumor growth. Most myeloid subsets, except Mast cells, were more abundant in tumors, indicative of immunosuppressive mechanisms within TME. Notably, Mast cells were enriched in paratumor tissue, possibly reflecting early inflammatory responses [ ]. Among B cell subsets, B-CD27 and B-EGR1|CD69 cells were enriched in paratumor tissue, suggesting active immune surveillance, while germinal center B cells were more prevalent in tumors, likely contributing to intratumoral antibody responses (Fig.  O). T and NK cell subsets were predominantly enriched in tumors, reflecting immune infiltration [ , ]. However, several subsets—including CD4T-EGR1|FOS, CD4T-CXCL13|TOX2, CD8T_Naive, and CD8_Trm-ZNF683—were specifically enriched in paratumor tissue, suggesting roles in early immune activation or tissue-protective responses (Fig.  O). Notably, our findings revealed a previously underappreciated functional dichotomy between paratumor and tumor regions, with paratumor tissue favoring immune surveillance and tissue maintenance, in contrast to the immunosuppressive and structurally supportive environment within tumors. Importantly, we identified paratumor-specific enrichment of CD4T-CXCL13|TOX2 and CD8_Trm-ZNF683 cells—subsets previously reported within tumors [ , ]—highlighting potential novel roles in peripheral immune regulation and tumor restraint.

## Integration of spatial transcriptomic data reveals distinct functional regions in gastric cancer
Given the importance of tissue context in shaping T-cell states, we examined the spatial distribution and context-associated features of CD4-CXCL13|TOX2 and CD8_Trm-ZNF683 T cells in GC. Across independent cohorts, both populations were consistently more abundant in paratumor tissues. Specifically, in the TCGA-STAD and GSE23739 [ ] datasets, CD4T-CXCL13|TOX2 and CD8_Trm-ZNF683 cells were present at significantly higher proportions in normal samples than in tumor tissues, supporting preferential localization outside tumor regions (Figs. O and A; p-value < 0.05). We next compared the transcriptional profiles of these cell populations between tumor and paratumor regions using the integrated single-cell data and identified differentially expressed genes. Paratumor-derived CD4T-CXCL13|TOX2 and CD8_Trm-ZNF683 T cells exhibited higher expression of PGA3 and PGC than their tumor-associated counterparts (Fig.  B). As PGA3 and PGC are associated with gastric epithelial and glandular lineages, their elevated expression is consistent with the surrounding paratumor tissue environment rather than T-cell–intrinsic transcriptional programs. At the pathway level, gene set enrichment analysis revealed that protein folding, translation, and proteostasis-related programs were more prominent in tumor-associated CD4-CXCL13|TOX2 and CD8_Trm-ZNF683 T cells (Fig.  C). We also assessed cell–cell communication patterns (see Materials and Methods). For CD8_Trm-ZNF683 T cells, interaction strength was broadly reduced in tumor tissues relative to paratumor regions across nearly all interacting cell types (Fig.  D). In contrast, CD4-CXCL13|TOX2 T cells showed a more selective pattern: interactions with myeloid populations and B-cell subsets were stronger in paratumor tissues, whereas interactions with other T-cell subsets were comparatively weaker (Fig.  D). These observations indicate that CD4-CXCL13|TOX2 and CD8_Trm-ZNF683 T cells exhibit tissue context–associated differences in abundance, transcriptional programs, and intercellular communication between tumor and paratumor regions. 
Next, considering that ST data provide spatial coordinates for cells, we utilized the GSE251950 [ ] spatial dataset (10X Visium platform), comprising 10 GC samples, with spot counts per slice ranging from 1,882 to 4,274 (median 3,267). Using the Seurat [ ] spatial workflow, we integrated these ST samples with Harmony [ ] batch correction on the top 30 principal components (see Materials and Methods), followed by clustering at a resolution of 0.2, yielding six spatial clusters (C0–C5) (Fig.  E). Given the critical role of tertiary lymphoid structures (TLS) in tumor progression, we applied a TLS gene signature from Tang et al. [ ] to score spatial spots using the AddModuleScore function from Seurat, identifying potential TLS regions where scores exceeded the mean plus three standard deviations (Fig.  E; Table S3; see Materials and Methods). To resolve cell type composition, we also employed the CARD deconvolution tool [ ] using our structured GC single-cell data as reference (Fig.  E; see Materials and Methods). The spatial distribution of cell types across clusters revealed pronounced heterogeneity (Fig.  F; Table S4). Spatial clusters C3 and C4 were dominated by Epithelial cells, while C5 was enriched for SMCs, MSCs, Fibroblasts, and Endothelial cells. C0 was primarily composed of Macro, B cell subsets (such as GCB, B-CD27, Plasma) and T cell subsets, and C2 showed a mixed immune-stromal profile enriched in CD4T-EGR1|FOS, Endothelial, Mast, CD8_Trm-ZNF683, NK, B-EGR1|CD69, pDC, Mono, and CD4_Tmem cells. Cluster C1 was specifically enriched in cDC subtypes (including cDC-FCER1A, cDC-CLEC9A, and cDC-LAMP3), and contained a high proportion of Macro cells (Fig.  F). The specificity score analysis showed that C3 and C4 had the highest specificity, whereas C0 had the lowest, consistent with the broad enrichment of diverse immune cell types in C0 (Fig.  G; see Materials and Methods). These patterns suggest distinct spatial niches: C0 as an immune-active zone, C1 as a cDC-rich immunoregulatory niche, C2 as an immune–stromal interface, C3/C4 as epithelial-dominated tumor regions, and C5 as a stromal-supportive compartment. Notably, the presence of CD8_Trm-ZNF683 and CD4T-EGR1|FOS in C2 suggest potential modulation at tumor-stroma boundaries.
Functional enrichment analysis of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) across clusters further supported these interpretations (Fig. H and I; Table S5; see Materials and Methods). Cluster C0 was enriched for “leukocyte-mediated immunity,” C1 for “negative regulation of cell–cell adhesion”; C2 for “lipid transport” and “organic hydroxy compound transport”; Clusters C3 and C4 showed enrichment for “digestion” and “epithelial structure maintenance”; while C5 was associated with “muscle contraction” and “muscle system process”. These gene ontology (GO) terms aligned well with the corresponding cell type enrichments (Fig.  H). Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analysis revealed that C0 was involved in “leukocyte transendothelial migration” and “viral protein interaction with cytokine and cytokine receptor”; C1 in “gastric cancer,” “lipid and atherosclerosis,” and “TNF signaling pathway”; and C2 in “collecting duct acid secretion” and “efferocytosis.” C3 and C4 were associated with “gastric acid secretion,” “tyrosine metabolism,” and “thyroid hormone synthesis,” while C5 was linked to “vascular smooth muscle contraction” (Fig.  I). Notably, the enrichment of “efferocytosis” in C2, in conjunction with Mast cell presence, suggests a potentially novel immune-regulatory mechanism in the GC stroma.
TLSs are ectopic lymphoid aggregates that form in non-lymphoid tissues under conditions of chronic inflammation or tumorigenesis, closely resembling secondary lymphoid organs in both structure and function [ ]. In GC, emerging evidence positions TLSs as potential local hubs for adaptive immune activation, possibly contributing to tumor control [ , ]. Comparison of cell type composition reveals distinct differences between TLS and non-TLS regions: TLS regions exhibit significant enrichment of B cells—particularly GCB cells—and key T cell subsets, such as CD8_Tex, CD8_ Tem-GZMK, and CD4_Treg, while Epithelial cells predominate outside these structures (Fig.  J). This accumulation of B and T cells aligns with their established roles in lymphoid neogenesis [ ]. These observations highlight the potential prognostic and therapeutic relevance of TLSs in gastric cancer, warranting further investigation.

## Reconstruction of cellular architecture at single-cell resolution in gastric cancer
To investigate the spatial architecture and cellular organization of the GC microenvironment, we utilized the GSE251950 [ ] ST dataset as a reference and mapped single cells to spatial coordinates across 10 tissue sections. By applying the CARD tool [ ], GC single-cell data from OMIX001073 [ ] and GSE167297 [ ] were projected onto spatial context, enabling the reconstruction of tissue architecture at single-cell resolution (Fig.  A; see Materials and Methods). Comparison of CARD-based spot-level deconvolution with the reconstructed single-cell spatial architecture showed near-perfect concordance in cellular composition across the entire tissue (R = 0.99 at the spot level; R = 1 across sections; Fig.  B). The results demonstrate the robustness and accuracy of our spatial inference strategy for reconstructing tissue-level cellular organization. 
In regions identified as putative TLSs, we observed high densities of GCB cells, alongside prominent co-expression of CR2 and CXCL13—two markers indicative of TLS maturation [ ] (Figs. E and C). CXCL13, a chemokine critical for B cell recruitment, and CR2, a complement receptor associated with immune activation, were both elevated in these areas, as visualized using a blue-to-red expression gradient, confirming the presence of a mature immune environment (Fig.  C). Further analysis of GCB cell abundance across six spatial clusters (C0–C5) revealed heterogeneity, with TLS enrichment most prominent in cluster C0, suggesting this region represents an immune-active zone (Fig.  D).
We next investigated GCB cell interactions differences within TLS and non-TLS regions. In TLS areas, GCB cells exhibited strong spatial co-localization (R > 0.45) with several immune subsets, including B-CD27, B-CD79A|FCER2, B-MS4A1|CD74, and CD8T_Naive, reinforcing the role of TLSs as immune-active hubs (Fig.  E). Interestingly, the negative correlation between cDC-LAMP3 and GCB cells suggests that cDC-LAMP3 cells localize to the TLS periphery, potentially serving as a bridge between tumor antigens and immune activation, and reflecting a delivery–activation zonation within TLSs (Fig.  E). In contrast, in non-TLS regions, GCB cells were more closely associated with CD4T_naive, CD4T_Naive-resting, CD4T-CXCL13|TOX2, and CD4_Treg cells, reflecting distinct interaction patterns. Additionally, consistent with established tumor immune architecture, we observed strong positive correlations between T cells and myeloid cells across spatial sections (Fig. E and F). Notably, a robust and previously unrecognized association was observed between Macro and SMCs, as well as with Fibroblasts and Endothelial cells, in both TLS and non-TLS regions. This Macro–SMC correlation was particularly prominent in TLSs, suggesting a coordinated stromal–immune interaction potentially involved in TLS formation or function. While prior studies have discussed the roles of Macro, Fibroblasts, and Endothelial cells in TLS biology [ , ], the involvement of SMCs in this cellular network has not been reported. Our findings reveal a potential novel stromal–immune axis in the GC microenvironment that may contribute to TLS organization and merits further investigation.

## Remodeling of intercellular signaling and spatial Interactions in gastric cancer
We used CellChat [ ] to compare cell–cell communication between tumor and paratumor GC tissues (see Materials and Methods). The tumor tissues showed significantly increased outgoing signaling from SMCs, Endothelial cells, and Fibroblasts, coupled with substantially enhanced incoming signals targeting Macro (Fig.  A). These patterns highlight an intensified and intricate signaling network in the tumor microenvironment, with Macro and SMCs playing pivotal roles in orchestrating these interactions. Further breakdown of sender–receiver relationships reveals that although overall sender strength is higher in paratumor regions, key subsets such as SMCs, Macro, Endothelial cells, Fibroblasts, MSCs, and Mono exhibit stronger outgoing signals in tumor tissues. On the receiving end, Macro and Endothelial cells display increased responsiveness, alongside Mast cells, B-CD27, and CD4_Treg (Fig.  B). This broad enhancement of receiver activity suggests dynamic remodeling of immune and stromal cell crosstalk within tumors. 
To quantify the magnitude of the aforementioned alterations in cell–cell communication, we calculated the log₂ fold change in interaction weights between tumor and paratumor states for both sender and receiver cell types. As shown in Fig.  C, SMCs exhibited the greatest increase in outgoing signaling, followed by Endothelial cells, Mono, Fibroblasts, and Macro. On the receiving end, Epithelial cells, Endothelial cells, and Fibroblasts showed the most pronounced elevations, indicating a tumor-specific reorganization of the intercellular signaling network (Fig.  C). These differential patterns highlight Endothelial cells as a central signaling modulator, in line with its well-established role in tumor-associated stroma [ ]. Notably, the dominant signaling activity initiated by SMCs reveals an underappreciated dimension of GC biology, suggesting that their functional role in tumor progression warrants further investigation. Moreover, analysis of cell types exhibiting concurrent increases in both sending and receiving activity within the tumor state identified Endothelial cells, Epithelial cells, Fibroblasts, GCB, Macro, Mono, and SMCs as key participants (Fig.  D).
A central question is whether the observed alterations in cell–cell communication correspond to changes in cellular abundance between tumor and paratumor states. Comparative analysis revealed that, among all bidirectional interactors, only Macro exhibited a statistically significant increase in abundance in the tumor state, underscoring their sensitivity to tumor-associated microenvironmental remodeling (Fig.  E; p-value < 0.05). To further explore the role of Macro, differential interaction analysis showed widespread enhancement of Macro-mediated communication with most cell types, except for pDCs, cDCs, and CD8_Trm-ZNF683 (Fig.  F; Table S6; see Materials and Methods). The most pronounced increase was observed in interactions with CD4_Tem, suggesting selective engagement of effector T cell responses. Strong interactions were also observed between Macro and Fibroblasts, as well as SMCs, forming a robust Macro–Fib–SMC axis potentially involved in tumor structural reprogramming. Given that spatial proximity enhances the likelihood of direct cellular communication, we further investigated the spatial distribution of Macro relative to other cell types. Co-localization indices revealed the highest spatial associations with Epithelial cells, MSCs, and SMCs, with particularly strong co-localization observed with Fibroblasts and SMCs (Fig.  G; see Materials and Methods). Spatial validation across the representative histological sections confirmed the prominent co-occurrence of Macro with Fibroblasts and SMCs (Fig.  H; see Materials and Methods). In contrast, despite high interaction weights, Macro exhibited limited spatial proximity to CD4_Tem and CD4T-EGR1|FOS indicating that elevated interaction signals do not always reflect spatial adjacency. The consistent spatial and functional convergence of the Macro–Fib–SMC axis represents a previously underrecognized structural motif in the GC microenvironment, potentially driving fibrosis, vascular remodeling, and matrix stiffening—hallmarks of tumor progression in GC.

## Macrophage–stromal interactions remodel the tumor microenvironment
Given the spatial co-localization of Macro with Fibroblasts and SMCs, and the enhanced intercellular communication between these cell types and CD4_Tem as well as CD4T-EGR1|FOS cells in tumor regions compared to adjacent non-tumor tissues, we investigated ligand-receptor (L-R) gene pairs potentially mediating interactions between Macro and key cellular components within the GC microenvironment. Focusing on the tumor context, we identified significant alterations in ligand-receptor interactions involving Macro and CD4_Tem, CD4T-EGR1|FOS, Fibroblasts, and SMCs (Fig.  A; see Materials and Methods). Specifically, the CD80-CD28 and ALCAM-CD6 pairs were enriched in tumors, suggesting facilitation of communication between Macro and CD4_Tem/CD4T-EGR1|FOS cells. In contrast, OSM-OSMR and GAS6-AXL selectively mediated interactions between Macro and Fibroblasts/SMCs. Although the ITGB2-ICAM1 pair was detected in both tumor and adjacent tissues, its signaling intensity was markedly elevated in tumors, indicating increased activity of this axis within the tumor microenvironment (Fig.  A). To further characterize ligand expression dynamics, we compared the expression levels of five Macro ligands—OSM, GAS6, ITGB2, CD80, and ALCAM—in tumor versus adjacent tissues. All five ligands exhibited significant upregulation in Macro cells from tumor samples (Fig.  B; Wilcoxon rank-sum test, p-value < 0.05). Validation using the TCGA-STAD cohort confirmed significantly higher expression of OSM, ITGB2, and CD80 in tumor tissues relative to “solid tissue normal” controls (Fig.  C). Corresponding receptors OSMR, ICAM1, and CD28 were also upregulated. ALCAM, GAS6 and AXL expression remained unchanged in the TCGA dataset. 
Considering the clinical focus on ligand-receptor pairs with prognostic and therapeutic potential, we selected three pairs—OSM-OSMR, ITGB2-ICAM1, and CD80-CD28—with pronounced expression and signaling changes for further clinical association analysis. Based on their combined expression levels (i.e., signature score inferred using `AddModuleScore` from Seurat package), TCGA-STAD patients were stratified accordingly. Kaplan–Meier survival analysis revealed that high expression of OSM-OSMR and ITGB2-ICAM1 significantly with poorer overall survival (Fig.  D; log-rank test, p-value < 0.05). Although high CD80-CD28 expression showed a similar trend, it did not reach statistical significance. Additionally, spatial architecture demonstrated that ITGB2-ICAM1 exhibited broad and robust signal distribution across tissue sections, implying a widespread role within the tumor microenvironment, whereas CD80-CD28 expression was more spatially restricted (Fig.  E). Importantly, OSM-OSMR and ITGB2-ICAM1 displayed substantial spatial overlap, suggesting potential interaction or co-regulation. Prior studies have established that the OSM-OSMR pathway modulates inflammatory cytokine secretion, influencing the local microenvironment and disease progression. The functional role of ITGB2-ICAM1 in GC remains uncharacterized; however, its spatial and prognostic convergence with OSM–OSMR implies a cooperative role in modulating the stromal niche. Immunofluorescence staining supported the spatial and transcriptional findings, showing clear co-localization of SMCs (ACTA2⁺), Fibroblasts (VIM⁺), and Macro (CD68⁺) in tumor regions (Fig.  F). Quantitative assessment across seven representative fields of view was performed to evaluate cellular co-occurrence. Given the broad distribution of Fibroblasts throughout the tissue, the analysis centered on SMCs and Macro. A strong positive correlation was observed between these two cell types (R = 0.64), indicating frequent co-localization, and coordinated accumulation within tumor regions (Fig. G, H, and S1; see Materials and Methods). Consistently, qPCR analysis of paired tumor and adjacent normal samples revealed elevated expression of OSM, OSMR, ITGB2, and ICAM1 in tumors (Fig.  I; Table S7), supporting the transcriptional upregulation detected in both single-cell and bulk datasets. Notably, these differences did not reach statistical significance (p-value > 0.05), likely due to the limited sample size.
Using TCGA-STAD data, we examined the relationship between OSM and ITGB2 expression and cellular infiltration (Fig.  A). High OSM expression correlated with significantly increased infiltration of Fibroblasts and SMCs, while elevated ITGB2 expression was associated with increased SMC abundance, though this did not reach significance. A strong positive correlation was observed between OSM and ITGB2 expression (Fig.  B), suggesting cooperative involvement of ITGB2-ICAM1 and OSM-OSMR in stromal remodeling. Further survival analysis indicated that patients with concurrent high expression of both OSM and ITGB2 (ITGB2highOSMhigh) exhibited the shortest overall survival, whereas those with high ITGB2 but low OSM expression (ITGB2highOSMlow) had comparatively better outcomes (Fig.  C; log-rank test, p-value < 0.05). This suggests that co-overexpression of OSM and ITGB2 may exacerbate stromal alterations, contributing to poorer prognosis. Additionally, bulk deconvolution using LinDeconSeq across multiple independent cohorts—including TCGA-STAD, GSE62254, GSE13861, GSE26942, and GSE66229—revealed a significant increase in SMC proportions within the ITGB2highOSMhigh subgroup (Wilcoxon test, p-value < 0.05), supporting the hypothesis that enhanced ITGB2-ICAM1 signaling under high OSM expression promotes SMC proliferation (Fig.  D). Quantitative real-time PCR confirmed effective knockdown of ITGB2 and OSM in macrophages (Fig.  E-F). In transwell co-culture assays, silencing ITGB2 or OSM in macrophages significantly reduced SMCs proliferation at 48 h, as measured by CCK-8 (Fig.  G). Moreover, Macro-specific knockdown of ITGB2 or OSM markedly attenuated SMCs migration in transwell assays (Fig.  H-I), indicating that Macro-derived ITGB2 and OSM promote smooth muscle cell proliferation and migration. 
Collectively, the ITGB2-ICAM1 axis mainly affects SMCs, while the OSM-OSMR axis targets Fibroblasts. Their synergistic activity may promote stromal remodeling and tumor progression in gastric cancer. High co-expression of ITGB2 and OSM is associated with increased proliferation and migration of SMCs and Fibroblasts, indicating collaborative regulation of stromal cell growth and tumor microenvironment dynamics.

## ICAM1⁺ SMCs cells exhibit pro-tumorigenic characteristics in gastric cancer
Given our observation that the synergistic activation of ITGB2–ICAM1 signaling (Macro–SMCs interaction) and OSM–OSMR signaling (Macro–Fibroblast interaction) promotes SMCs proliferation, we further investigated the association between SMCs abundance, clinical prognosis, and pathway activation in GC. Using data from the TCGA-STAD cohort, we estimated the proportion of SMCs via the LinDeconSeq algorithm [ ]. Survival analysis indicated that patients with a higher proportion of SMCs exhibited significantly poorer outcomes (p-value < 0.05) and showed elevated ITGB2–ICAM1 signaling activity, identifying SMC infiltration as a negative prognostic factor in GC (Fig. A and B; see Materials and Methods). Clinical association investigation revealed that the proportion of SMCs was relatively lower in stage I and IV tumors, and higher in stage II and III, although these differences were not statistically significant (Fig.  C). Notably, methylation-based clustering defined by Witte et al. demonstrated the lowest SMC abundance in cluster C2, with progressively higher levels observed in clusters C1, C3, and C4 (Fig.  D). This suggests a potential role for epigenetic modifications in regulating SMC recruitment and activation within the tumor microenvironment. 
To elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying SMC proliferation, we extracted SMC populations from integrated single-cell datasets and stratified them into ICAM1+ and ICAM1− groups based on ICAM1 expression. Differential expression analysis showed that ICAM1+ SMCs exhibited markedly elevated expression of key genes associated with extracellular matrix (ECM) remodeling (e.g., FN1, COL1A1, PCOLCE), cell adhesion (e.g., VCAM1), and inflammatory responses (e.g., IL32, CXCL2) (Fig.  E). These expression patterns suggest that ICAM1+ SMCs represent an activated, tumor-promoting phenotype. KEGG pathway enrichment analysis further demonstrated significant enrichment of ECM organization and PI3K/Akt signaling pathways in ICAM1+ SMCs (Fig.  F). To quantitatively assess pathway activity, we applied curated gene sets from the literature [ ] for pathway scoring. ICAM1+ SMCs exhibited significantly higher activity scores in both ECM remodeling and PI3K/Akt pathways (Fig.  G). ECM remodeling activity implies enhanced matrix degradation and tissue restructuring capacity, while PI3K/Akt activation reflects increased proliferative and survival signaling—both contributing to tumor progression and metastasis. Moreover, leveraging the reconstructed single-cell–resolution spatial architecture of gastric cancer tissues, we examined the spatial distribution of ITGB2⁺ Macro and ICAM1⁺ SMCs across tissue sections. These two cell types exhibited pronounced spatial co-localization, providing further evidence for a potential functional interaction between Macro and activated SMCs (Fig.  H).
Collectively, these findings support a model in which Macro engage in functional crosstalk with SMCs through direct ITGB2–ICAM1 interaction and the indirect OSM–OSMR signaling axis (Fig.  I). The synergistic activation of these pathways exacerbates poor clinical outcomes by promoting SMC proliferation. Co-elevated expression of ITGB2 and OSM not only predicts worse prognosis but also reinforces SMC expansion via a positive feedback loop, with increased SMC abundance further correlating with unfavorable outcomes. At the molecular level, ICAM1+ SMCs exhibit a more aggressive, tumor-promoting phenotype characterized by activation of PI3K/Akt and ECM remodeling pathways. This integrative model highlights how macrophage-mediated signaling networks converge to drive SMC activation and expansion, ultimately contributing to gastric cancer progression and adverse clinical outcomes. These findings offer novel insights into the complexity of intercellular communication within the tumor microenvironment.

## Discussion
GC presents a prototypical case of tumor ecosystems shaped by extensive spatial and cellular heterogeneity [ , ]. Through the integration of single-cell and spatial transcriptomic data, our study reconstructed the GC architecture at single-cell resolution and revealed spatially organized immune and stromal programs that drive tumor progression. Previous systematic analyses of the tumor secretome and membrane proteome have facilitated the identification of intercellular signaling pathways in cancer by cataloging candidate ligands and receptors with potential clinical relevance, including recurrent cytokine–cytokine receptor interaction networks in gastrointestinal adenocarcinomas [ , ]. Building upon these frameworks, our study extended secretome-based insights by incorporating cellular and spatial resolution to define the cellular sources and anatomical contexts of intercellular signaling within the GC microenvironment. Initially, we showed that the immune architecture was compartmentalized between tumor and paratumor regions. Paratumor tissues were selectively enriched for CD4⁺CXCL13⁺TOX2⁺ and CD8_Trm-ZNF683⁺ T cells—subsets with tissue-resident and follicular-helper–like characteristics that have traditionally been associated with intratumoral exhaustion or anti-tumor activity. Their preferential localization outside the tumor suggested spatial restriction of effector programs, likely reflecting differential microenvironmental cues or physical exclusion by stromal barriers [ ].
Beyond immune segregation, spatial clustering of transcriptomic profiles reveals functionally specialized tumor niches that are not apparent from morphological assessment. Among the six spatial clusters identified, one niche enriched for mast cells exhibits gene signatures associated with lipid metabolism and efferocytosis, suggesting a unique immune-regulatory microenvironment that may influence macrophage polarization or lymphocyte recruitment. Another niche dominated by conventional dendritic cells engages in TNF signaling and lipid transport, potentially acting as a local immunoregulatory hub. Their discovery exemplifies the power of spatial transcriptomics to uncover cryptic tissue architectures with functional and clinical significance. Reconstruction of the spatial organization of tertiary lymphoid structures (TLSs) further illustrates the ability of our integrative approach to resolve complex immune compartments. In GC tissues, TLSs are enriched for GCB cells and activated CD8⁺ and CD4⁺ T cells. Notably, LAMP3⁺ DC cells exhibit spatial segregation from GCB cells, suggesting localized antigen-presenting interactions and reflecting a delivery–activation zonation within TLSs. Given the emerging recognition of TLSs as immune activation hubs and favorable prognostic indicators in solid tumors [ , ], these findings point to a structural and functional role for dendritic cell–B cell crosstalk in sustaining TLS integrity and activity in GC.
Perhaps the most distinctive feature of the GC microenvironment revealed in our analysis is a triadic signaling unit composed of macrophages, fibroblasts, and smooth muscle cells (SMCs). These three cell types were strongly co-localized in tumor regions and displayed mutually reinforcing signaling interactions. While Macro–Fibroblasts communication is well documented in fibrotic and neoplastic stroma, the coordinated inclusion of SMCs in this stromal axis has not been described in GC. Given the roles of SMCs in matrix deposition, vascular tone regulation, and mechanical stiffness, the presence of this triad suggested a structurally integrated stromal program that may promote tumor progression by reshaping the physical and signaling landscape of the TME. This hypothesis was further supported by the identification of two ligand–receptor pairs—ITGB2–ICAM1 and OSM–OSMR—that are selectively upregulated in tumors and enriched at sites of Macro–SMC–Fibroblasts interaction. Both signaling axes were associated with poor prognosis and increased SMC abundance. OSM–OSMR is known to drive fibroblast proliferation in other solid tumors and fibrotic diseases [ , ], but its potential synergy with ITGB2–ICAM1 signaling in regulating SMC proliferation and stromal expansion has not been previously reported. The co-expression of OSM and ITGB2 correlates with the worst clinical outcomes, suggesting that these pathways may act cooperatively to orchestrate an aggressive, vascularized, and fibrotic tumor stroma. These findings extended our understanding of stromal–vascular communication in solid tumors and suggested actionable molecular targets for disrupting pro-tumorigenic niches in GC.
Despite these insights, our study has several limitations that warrant consideration. First, the integration of scRNA-seq and ST data, while powerful, relies on computational deconvolution to map single-cell profiles to spatial coordinates. This approach may introduce inaccuracies due to assumptions about cell type proportions and spatial distributions, particularly in regions with high cellular heterogeneity. Second, the ST data from the 10X Visium platform lack true single-cell resolution, capturing multiple cells per spot. This limitation may obscure fine-grained cellular interactions and necessitate validation with higher-resolution platforms like 10 × Xenium or NanoString CosMx, which were not used here due to their limited transcript detection efficiency. Finally, while we identify ITGB2-ICAM1 and OSM-OSMR as key signaling axes, their functional roles in SMC proliferation and stromal remodeling require experimental validation through in vivo models to confirm causality and therapeutic potential.
Collectively, our study established a comprehensive architecture for understanding the cellular and spatial complexity of the GC microenvironment. The identification of immune exclusion in peritumoral compartments, the recognition of functionally distinct spatial niches, and the elucidation of a Macro–Fibroblasts–SMCs signaling axis driven by ITGB2–ICAM1 and OSM–OSMR signaling offered new mechanistic insights with clear translational potential, and provided a resource for the development of spatially informed therapeutic strategies and prognostic tools.

## Conclusion
This study reconstructs the GC spatial architecture at single-cell resolution and identifies a novel stromal activation mechanism driven by macrophage-derived signaling. ICAM1⁺ smooth muscle cells play a central role, and the ITGB2–ICAM1 and OSM–OSMR pathways offer potential targets for stromal-directed therapies to improve GC outcomes.
