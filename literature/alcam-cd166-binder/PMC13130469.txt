## Abstract
The early and accurate diagnosis of acute myocardial infarction (AMI) remains a significant clinical challenge. To this end, we profiled the surface proteome of individual plasma extracellular vesicles (EVs) from AMI patients using single-vesicle sequencing, aiming to identify disease-associated alterations with diagnostic and therapeutic potential. Profiling the EV surface proteome across healthy controls (HC), coronary artery stenosis (CAS), and AMI revealed 21 differentially expressed proteins (DEPs), 11 of which were uniquely associated with AMI compared to HC. Notably, these included elevated levels of DSCAML1, CR1, ACE2, FN1, CDH15, and C5b‑9. EVs were subsequently stratified into 17 subpopulations, with clusters 1, 8, and 9 characterized by DSCAML1, ALCAM, and CR1, respectively, and showing the highest enrichment in AMI. We further demonstrated that plasma EVs from AMI patients (AMI-EVs) promote cardiomyocyte proliferation and endothelial cell activity in vitro, followed by the finding that the DSCAML1-enriched subpopulation (DSCAML1-EVs) enhances myocardial repair and angiogenesis both in vitro and in vivo, with mechanistic studies implicating the EREG/ERK pathway in these effects. In summary, DSCAML1-positive EVs show dual potential as both a diagnostic biomarker for AMI and a therapeutic target for improving post‑infarction prognosis, providing insight into the translational potential of EV‑based strategies in precision cardiology.

## Introduction
Myocardial infarction (MI) results from the abrupt occlusion of a coronary artery, leading to ischemic necrosis of myocardial tissue [ ]. Timely and accurate diagnosis is essential for preserving cardiac function and improving patient prognosis. Current diagnostic strategies primarily rely on clinical presentation, such as ischemic chest pain, characteristic electrocardiographic (ECG) alterations, and elevated circulating levels of high-sensitivity cardiac troponin [ , ]. Although these approaches have improved diagnostic accuracy, identifying novel biomarkers from diverse research perspectives remains a critical clinical research challenge for achieving more precise diagnosis of AMI and ensuring favorable prognostic outcomes.
EVs have garnered significant attention as emerging biomarkers for cardiovascular disease [ , ]. Recent evidence indicates that specific microRNAs, including miR-21, enriched within plasma-derived EVs, exhibit strong potential for distinguishing acute coronary syndrome (ACS) from stable coronary artery disease, with miR-499 demonstrating a notable correlation between concentration gradients and the extent of myocardial injury [ , ]. Furthermore, a population-based study revealed differential expression of acute-phase proteins, such as ceruloplasmin, in EVs from patients with AMI compared to those with chronic coronary syndrome (CCS) [ ]. Another investigation revealed that LIPCAR levels were significantly elevated in EVs derived from AMI patients who subsequently developed left ventricular remodeling (LVR), compared to those who did not [ ]. Although these studies highlight the clinical relevance of EV cargo in predicting cardiovascular risk, it’s important to note that changes in circulating EVs under pathological conditions may mainly reflect shifts in specific EV subpopulations from diseased tissues. These subpopulations, though low in abundance, could serve as highly specific biomarkers for cardiovascular pathology.
Indeed, EVs not only serve as predictors of diseases but also participate in their pathogenesis. In a clinical study, Li et al. demonstrated that plasma-derived ITGB3 + EVs promote the invasion capability of colorectal cancer in mice [ ]. Likewise, several studies in the cardiovascular field have underscored the pathogenic and regulatory roles of plasma-derived EVs. For instance, cardiac-derived EVs present in the plasma of animals with chronic heart failure were found to mediate heart-brain crosstalk [ ]. In the context of MI, one of the most important strategies for cardiac repair after MI is the promotion of angiogenesis in the infarct border zone, which can effectively prolong the survival of damaged myocardium and improve ventricular remodeling and cardiac function [ , ]. Given the well-established function of EVs in mediating intercellular communication and the recognized therapeutic promise of angiogenesis following MI, it remains unclear whether post-MI angiogenesis is mediated by circulating EVs.
In this study, we used proximity barcoding assay (PBA) technology to accomplish single‑vesicle sequencing, which enables the capture of EVs followed by single-round rolling circle amplification and DNA barcode sequencing for proteomic profiling at the single-EV level, to delineate the heterogeneity of plasma-derived EVs in patients with AMI. We identified six proteins that were significantly elevated in AMI patients compared with the HC group. Additionally, we discovered a distinct EV subpopulation characterized by DSCAML1 expression, which demonstrated strong discriminatory power for AMI. Furthermore, DSCAML1-EVs exerted pro-repair and pro-angiogenic effects both in vitro and in vivo. Taken together, our findings establish DSCAML1-positive EVs as both a robust biomarker for AMI and a potential therapeutic mediator for promoting myocardial repair and neovascularization in ischemic heart disease (Scheme ).
 

## Materials and methods

## Study sample
 All participants were recruited from the Department of Cardiovascular Medicine at Jinan Central Hospital between August and December 2023. Study participants were divided into three groups: healthy controls (HC; n = 13) with no angiographic evidence of arterial stenosis, patients with coronary atherosclerosis (CAS; n = 13) exhibiting approximately 50% coronary stenosis, and patients diagnosed with acute myocardial infarction (AMI; n = 13) characterized by plaque rupture and complete coronary occlusion (100% coronary stenosis). Patient inclusion was based on the Fourth Universal Definition of Myocardial Infarction criteria for diagnosing acute myocardial infarction (AMI) [ ] and the American Heart Association (AHA) guidelines for diagnosing chronic coronary artery disease (CAS) [ ]. For exclusion, patients with symptoms or a history of infection in the past three months, such as autoimmune diseases, AIDS, cancer, and other related conditions, were excluded. This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the Ethics Committee of Jinan Central Hospital (Approval No. R202306190133; NCT No. NCT05699629). All research procedures followed relevant ethical guidelines and regulations. Informed consent was obtained from all participants and/or their legal guardians. All AMI patients presented to the emergency department with cardiac symptoms. Blood samples were collected within 4 h of symptom onset, immediately upon arrival at the emergency department, and prior to any reperfusion therapy. All enrolled subjects in the HC and CAS groups were patients hospitalized due to cardiac symptoms. Individuals in the HC group were confirmed to be free of cardiac disease and exhibited no coronary stenosis or plaque following systematic examinations. All CAS patients had stable lesions with stenosis severity between 40% and 60%. Their blood samples were also collected upon initial hospitalization. Coronary angiography findings of all enrolled participants are presented in Table S1.

## Preparation and characterization of EVs
EVs from human plasma: Peripheral blood (5 mL) was collected from each patient and placed into anticoagulant tubes containing ethylene diamine tetraacetic acid (EDTA). The blood was centrifuged at 3,000 rpm for 15 min. To remove cellular debris, the plasma was then centrifuged at 10,000 g for 15 min. For the isolation of plasma-derived EVs, samples were processed using size exclusion chromatography (SEC, IZON, New Zealand) and concentrated using a 100 kDa filter (Merck Millipore, Germany). Plasma EVs were collected and stored for subsequent analysis.
EVs from cell culture medium: HEK293T cells were cultured in complete DMEM until they reached 70% confluence. The complete medium was then replaced with DMEM supplemented with 10% EV-free fetal bovine serum (FBS). After 48 h, the supernatants were collected and sequentially centrifuged at 300 g for 15 min, 3,000 g for 15 min, and 10,000 g for 45 min at 4 °C to remove cell debris and other impurities. The supernatant was then ultracentrifuged at 120,000 g for 70 min (CP110NX, Hitachi Ltd., Tokyo, Japan) at 4 °C to pellet the EVs. The supernatant was discarded, and the pellet was resuspended in PBS. The resuspended sample was subjected to another round of ultracentrifugation at 120,000 g for 70 min. The final EV pellet was collected and transferred to an ultrafiltration tube for further concentration. The ultrafiltration concentrate was passed through a 0.22-µm membrane filter for sterilization. It was then aliquoted and stored temporarily at −80 °C to avoid repeated freeze-thaw cycles. To meet the quantity requirements for in vivo experiments, EVs from different preparation batches were mixed. The total particle concentration of the pooled suspension was determined using nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA). Based on this measurement, the suspension was aliquoted in a standardized manner ensure dose consistency across experiments.
The size distribution and particle concentration of the isolated EVs were measured using NTA on a NS300 system (Malvern Instruments Ltd., UK). EV protein markers, including CD63 (Ab134045, Abcam, USA), Alix (67715-1-Ig, Proteintech, China), HSP70 (Ab181606, Abcam, USA), Calnexin (Ab133615, Abcam, USA), and Syntenin-1 (22399-1-AP, Proteintech, China), were detected by Western blotting. The purified EVs were visualized using transmission electron microscopy (TEM, HT7800, Hitachi Ltd., Tokyo, Japan) at an accelerating voltage of 90 kV.

## Proximity barcoding assay
EV samples from patient plasma were analyzed using PBA according to the established protocol [ , ]. A panel of 261 antibodies was conjugated with oligonucleotides containing unique protein tags, molecular tags, and a universal primer-binding sequence, enabling the detection of biomarkers at the single-EV level. One microliter of plasma was mixed with 1 µL of PBA buffer and incubated with 1 µL of antibody mix for 2 h at room temperature. To capture the EVs, biotinylated cholera toxin subunit B (biotin-CTB, C34779, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) was added to each well of a 96-well plate pre-coated with streptavidin (PCR0STF-SA5/100, Biomat, Italy). The plate was incubated at room temperature for 20 min and washed three times with PBS containing Tween 20. Plasma EVs/antibody mixes were diluted to a final volume of 20 µL with PBA buffer and transferred into each well of the CTB-coated plate, where EVs were captured via the interaction between biotinylated CTB and GM1 ganglioside, which is enriched in the lipid membrane of EVs.
Following capture, the oligonucleotides on each individual EV received a unique EV barcode during the extension reaction after binding to EV tag templates. Sample indexing and sequencing adapters were added via designed primers during PCR amplification. The resulting library was sequenced using an Illumina NextSeq system (CN500, Illumina, USA) with a single-end 75-bp sequencing reagent kit (20024906, Illumina, USA). After sequencing, data for each sample were obtained as bcl files. The PBA experiments were conducted at SecreTech (Shenzhen, China). In this study, EV samples from each individual patient underwent independent proteomic sequencing.

## Mouse MI model and treatment
To generate an MI model, 8-week-old male C57BL/6 mice were purchased from Jinan Xingkang Biotechnology (Jinan, China) and underwent permanent left anterior descending (LAD) ligation. Mice were first anesthetized with isoflurane and ventilated using an ABS-type small animal gas anesthesia machine (Shanghai Yuyan Instruments, China). A thoracotomy was then performed on the left side of the mouse, and the LAD was ligated with a 7 − 0 prolene suture. The sham group underwent the same surgical procedure, except that the LAD was not ligated. After LAD ligation, immediate myocardial ischemia was confirmed under the ligation site, indicating a successful MI model. Additionally, electrocardiogram (ECG) was performed to further confirm the successful establishment of the MI model. MI mice were randomly assigned to five groups: PBS group, Empty-EVs group, DSCAML1-EVs group, CR1-EVs group, and ACE2-EVs group. For MI mice, 200 µL of EVs was injected via the tail vein 2 h after MI surgery. All animal procedures were approved by the Ethics Committee for Animal Experiments of Shandong First Medical University & Shandong Academy of Medical Sciences (Approval No. JNCH2024-037).

## In vivo biodistribution
To investigate the biodistribution of EVs in MI mice, Cy5 dye (Cyanine 5 NHS ester, Lumiprobe, 43020) was used to label the EVs, which were then administered via intravenous injection. Mice were anesthetized and imaged at 6 h using the IVIS Spectrum In Vivo Imaging System (IVIS, PerkinElmer, Boston, USA). Following imaging, the hearts were collected and imaged again using IVIS.

## Echocardiography
At the specified time points (7 and 14 days) following the establishment of the MI model, the mice were anesthetized with isoflurane and subsequently evaluated using the Vinno 6LAB imaging system. Key parameters, including left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF), left ventricular fractional shortening (LVFS), left ventricular internal diameter at end-diastole (LVIDd), and left ventricular internal diameter at end-systole (LVIDs), were measured over at least three consecutive cardiac cycles in both long-axis and short-axis views [ ]. The echocardiographer was blinded to the experimental treatments of the mice.

## Infarct size measurement
To assess myocardial infarct size, hearts were stained with 2,3,5-triphenyl-2 H-tetrazolium chloride (TTC, Beyotime, China). One week after MI induction, mice were euthanized, and their hearts were excised and rinsed with PBS. The hearts were then sectioned into 1-mm thick slices, cut perpendicularly from apex to base. The slices were incubated in 1% TTC solution in the dark at 37 °C for 15 min. After incubation, the slices were washed with PBS and imaged digitally. The infarcted areas, which appeared pale, were measured to determine the infarct size.

## Histological staining
On day 14 post-MI, mice were euthanized, and heart tissues were harvested, fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned at 4 μm thickness. Prior to staining, paraffin sections were dewaxed according to standard protocols. Sections were then subjected to Masson’s trichrome staining (C0189S, Beyotime, China) and hematoxylin-eosin (HE) staining (C0105S, Beyotime, China) and imaged using an optical microscope (SLIDEVIEW VS200 slide scanner, Olympus, Japan). Apoptotic cells in the heart tissue were detected using TUNEL staining (Vazyme, China), and cardiomyocyte nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (P0131, Beyotime, China). Quantification of apoptosis was performed by calculating the percentage of TUNEL-positive cells. For immunohistochemistry, sections were incubated with primary antibodies against CD31 (1:300, GB11063-2, Servicebio) and α-SMA (1:1000, GB121364, Servicebio) for 12 h, followed by incubation with species-specific HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies. After DAB chromogenic development and nuclear counterstaining, images were captured under bright-field microscopy.

## Cell culture
Rat cardiomyocyte H9C2 cells and human embryonic kidney 293 T cells were purchased from Procell (Wuhan, China). Human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs) were obtained from the Cell Bank of the Chinese Academy of Sciences (Shanghai, China). In addition, the MycAway™ Plus-Color One-Step Mycoplasma Detection Kit (40612ES25, Yeasen, China) was used to confirm that all cells were free from mycoplasma contamination. H9C2 and 293 T cells were maintained in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM, Gibco, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco, USA) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin. HUVECs were cultured in Endothelial Cell Medium (ECM, Sciencell, USA) supplemented with 10% FBS, 1% endothelial cell growth supplement, and 1% penicillin/streptomycin. All cells were incubated at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2.
To prepare EV-depleted FBS, FBS was ultracentrifuged at 120,000 × g for 16 h. The supernatant was collected and filtered through a 0.22-µm membrane (Millipore, SLGP033RB) prior to use in cell culture.

## Lentiviral transduction
HEK293T cells were used for lentivirus packaging. Lentiviral packaging plasmids pCMV-VSV-G, pMG2.G, and pRSV-Rev were co-transfected with either the overexpression plasmid (pLV3-CMV-DSCAML1[human]−3×FLAG-Puro, pLV3-CMV-CR1[human-repeat-opt]−3×FLAG-Puro, or pLV3-CMV-ACE2[human]−3×FLAG-Puro) or the empty vector control (pLV3-CMV-MCS-3×FLAG-Puro) at a 1: 1: 1: 2 ratio into HEK293T cells. After 4–6 h, the transfection medium was replaced with fresh medium and the cells were cultured for 48 h. The culture supernatant containing viral particles was collected and centrifuged at 3,000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C to remove cell debris. The supernatant was transferred to a new tube, avoiding any precipitate, and filtered through a 0.45 μm membrane. Polyethylene glycol 6000 (PEG6000) was added at a ratio of 1: 4 (virus solution: 4× PEG6000), and the mixture was gently shaken overnight at 4 °C to precipitate the virus. The virus was then collected by centrifugation at 7,000 rpm for 20 min at 4 °C, the supernatant was discarded, and the viral pellet was resuspended in 200 µL of serum-containing culture medium.
For transduction, target cells were seeded and allowed to adhere, then 200 µL of the virus solution was added to 1.8 mL of culture medium. After 24 h, cells were passaged, and infection was repeated three times to increase transduction efficiency. Following triple infection, cells were subjected to puromycin selection at a final concentration of 10 µg/mL (1: 1000 dilution) to enrich for stably transduced cells. Only virus-infected cells expressing puromycin resistance survived, while untransduced cells were eliminated. This selection process was repeated three times to establish 293 T cells stably overexpressing DSCAML1, CR1, ACE2, or empty vector controls. Expression of the target proteins was confirmed by Western blotting.

## Cell migration assay
To evaluate cell migration, HUVECs were seeded into 12-well plates and cultured until they reached approximately 80% confluence. A linear scratch was created in the monolayer using a 200 µL pipette tip, and cells were incubated for an additional 12 h. Images of the wound area were captured at 0 and 12 h using an inverted phase-contrast microscope (Olympus, Japan). The migration rate was calculated using the formula: Migration rate (%) = (A0 – A12)/A0 × 100%. Where A0 is the initial wound width and A12 is the wound width after 12 h.
Cell migration was further assessed using transwell assays (8.0 μm pore size, BD Falcon). HUVECs were resuspended in 200 µL of serum-free medium and seeded into the upper chamber, while the lower chamber was filled with 400 µL of complete medium containing either different EV preparations or PBS. After 12 h of incubation, cells that had migrated to the lower surface of the membrane were fixed and stained with crystal violet (C0121, Beyotime, China). Five randomly selected fields per membrane were imaged using an inverted microscope (TS2, Nikon, Japan), and the number of migrated cells was quantified using Image J software (Version 1.4.6).

## Vascular tube formation
The ability of HUVECs to form vascular-like structures was assessed using Matrigel basement membrane matrix (356227, Corning, USA). Briefly, 96-well plates were coated with Matrigel and allowed to polymerize. HUVECs (3 × 104 cells per well), pre-incubated with EVs for 24 h, were seeded onto the Matrigel-coated wells and incubated for 6 h. The cells were then stained with Calcein AM (C2012, Beyotime, China) for 30 min. Five randomly selected fields per well were imaged using an inverted fluorescence microscope (IX73-DP74, Olympus, Japan), and the number of vascular tubes formed was quantified using Image J software.

## Uptake of EVs
To evaluate the internalization of EVs by H9C2 cardiomyocytes and HUVECs, EVs were labeled with either PKH26 (Red Fluorescent Cell Linker Mini Kit, Sigma-Aldrich, MINI26) or Dil (Red Fluorescent Dye, Beyotime, C1036). For labeling, 1 µL of Dil (100 µmol/L in DMSO, stored at −20 °C) was incubated with 1 × 10¹⁰ EVs for 1 h in the dark. Similarly, 1 µL of PKH26 was incubated with 1 × 10¹⁰ EVs for 30 min under light-protected conditions. Excess dye was removed by ultrafiltration at 3,000 × g for 30 min. H9C2 or HUVECs were seeded in 24-well plates and cultured until approximately 60% confluence, after which they were incubated with Dil- or PKH26-labeled EVs for 24 h. Following incubation, cells were washed with PBS, fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 20 min, and counterstained with DAPI (Beyotime, C1005) for 10 min. Fluorescent signals were visualized using an inverted fluorescence microscope (IX73-DP74, Olympus, Japan) or a confocal microscope (FV12-IXCOV, Olympus, Japan).

## Quantitative real-time PCR
Total RNA from heart tissues and cultured cells was isolated using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, 15596018CN). RNA concentration and purity were determined with a NanoDrop spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). For mRNA analysis, complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesized using the HiScript II 1 st Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (Vazyme, R223-01), followed by quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) with ChamQ SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme, Q711-02/03). GAPDH served as the internal reference. The forward primer for EREG is CAGATGGAAGACGATCCCCG, the reverse primer is CTCACATCGCAGACCAGTGT. The forward primer for GAPDH is TGATGGGTGTGAACCACGAG, and the reverse primer is AGTGATGGCATGGACTGTGG. Relative gene expression was calculated using the −2ΔΔCT method.

## CCK-8 assay
Cell viability was assessed using the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8, Biosharp, BS350A). Cells were seeded into 96-well plates and cultured for 24 h until reaching ~50% confluence. Subsequently, cells were treated with either EVs (108 particles) or PBS as control. After 24 h of incubation, the culture medium was replaced with 10 µL of CCK-8 reagent per well and incubated for 2 h at 37 °C. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader.

## EdU assay
Cell proliferation was evaluated using the EdU Cell Proliferation Kit (Ribobio, China) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. After staining, five random fields were imaged using an inverted fluorescence microscope. The number of EdU-positive cells and total nuclei were quantified with ImageJ software.

## Western blotting
Total protein was extracted from cells or heart tissues using RIPA lysis buffer (P0013, Beyotime, Shanghai, China). Protein concentrations were determined using a BCA protein assay kit (P0011, Beyotime, China). Equal amounts of protein were denatured at 100 °C for 5 min in loading buffer, separated by 8–12% SDS-PAGE, and transferred onto PVDF membranes. Membranes were blocked with 5% skim milk in TBST for 1 h at room temperature and subsequently incubated overnight at 4 °C with the following primary antibodies: DSCAML1 (AF3311, 1:1000, R&D Systems, USA), CR1 (ab133293, 1:1000, Abcam, UK), ACE2 (ab108252, 1:1000, Abcam, UK), EREG (abs106776, Absin, China), ERK (11257-1-AP, Proteintech, China), p38 (14064-1-AP, Proteintech, China), JNK (24164-1-AP, Proteintech, China), phospho-ERK (28733-1-AP, Proteintech, China), phospho-p38 (28796-1-AP, Proteintech, China), phospho-JNK (80024-1-RR, Proteintech, China), VEGFR (13245, Research Cloud Biology), and VEGFA (19003-1-AP, Proteintech, China). After washing, membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies against mouse (SA00001-1, Proteintech, China) or rabbit (SA00001-2, Proteintech, China) for 1 h at room temperature. Protein bands were visualized using enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) and quantified with Image J software.

## Immunofluorescence
HUVECs were cultured on glass coverslips and treated with DSCAML-EVs or Empty-EVs for 24 h. Cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min, permeabilized with 0.5% Triton X-100, and blocked with 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA; Zhongshan Jinqiao Biotechnology, Beijing, China) for 30 min at 37 °C. Samples were then incubated overnight at 4 °C with anti-EREG antibody (1:300, abs106776, Absin, China), followed by Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG (A-11034, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) for 45 min at 37 °C. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI. Fluorescence signals were captured using a fluorescence microscope (Olympus, Japan), with more than three randomly selected fields analyzed per slide across nine slides.

## ELISA
Blood was collected from anesthetized mice via retro-orbital puncture and allowed to clot at room temperature for 20 min. Samples were then centrifuged at 3,000 rpm for 15 min, and the serum supernatant was collected. The concentration of NT-proBNP in serum was quantified using a commercial ELISA kit (Nanjing Jiancheng, China). Absorbance was measured at 450 nm with a microplate reader.

## RNA-sequencing
Total RNA was extracted from HUVECs using the RNeasy Mini Kit (QIAGEN) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA quality and concentration were evaluated with a NanoDrop spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and an Agilent Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies). Sequencing libraries were generated using the Illumina TruSeq RNA Sample Preparation Kit, and high-throughput sequencing was performed on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform (Illumina, USA). Raw sequencing data were deposited in the NCBI Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database. Sequencing reads were aligned to the reference genome using STAR or HISAT2, and alignment quality was assessed by average read length, proportion of uniquely and multi-mapped reads, and splice junction coverage. Differentially expressed genes were identified using DESeq2, with statistical significance defined as an adjusted p-value (FDR) < 0.05. Functional enrichment of differentially expressed genes was performed using DAVID Bioinformatics Resources, including Gene Ontology (GO) categories and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathways.

## Statistical analysis
Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). For two-group comparisons, Student’s t-test was used when data were normally distributed with equal variances; otherwise, Welch’s t-test or Mann–Whitney U test was applied. For comparisons among multiple groups, one-way or two-way ANOVA followed by Duncan’s test was performed under normality and homogeneity assumptions, while the Kruskal-Wallis test with pairwise Wilcoxon tests was used otherwise. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

## Results

## Baseline characteristics
To identify potential biomarkers for acute myocardial infarction (AMI), we employed a stratified grouping strategy based on coronary angiographic findings, thereby capturing the continuum of coronary artery stenosis severity. The baseline demographic and clinical characteristics of the patients across these groups, including age, heart rate, body mass index, LVEF, blood pressure, and relevant medical history (e.g., pre-infarction angina and smoking), were presented in Table  . Compared with HC and CAS groups, AMI patients demonstrated a significantly lower left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) (p < 0.05) and elevated plasma levels of troponin I (TNI) (p < 0.05), aspartate aminotransferase (AST) (p < 0.05), and alanine aminotransferase (ALT) (p < 0.05), reflecting myocardial injury. Other clinical parameters, such as blood pressure, body mass index, blood routine, blood lipids, blood ions, and renal function, were comparable across groups.
 

## PBA-based single EV proteomics for biomarker discovery
To characterize the composition of plasma EVs, samples from HC, CAS, and AMI patients were subjected to PBA analysis (Figure S1A). The median number of EVs detected by PBA, quantified through EV tags, was approximately 1.56 × 10^5 per sample (Fig.  A). The median number of detected proteins, quantified through molecule tags, was 1.14 × 10^6 (Fig.  B), corresponding to an average of 6.76 proteins per EV (protein/EV) (Fig.  C). Although the AMI group exhibited a higher number of EVs, no significant differences were observed among the HC, CAS, and AMI groups. A total of 261 proteins were detected under investigation, which were previously reported in literatures, especially in cardiovascular study. Next, the expression data were normalized using the trimmed mean of M values (TMM) algorithm. A dot plot summarizing the differential expression of all 261 detected proteins is provided in Figure S1B. A total of 21 DEPs were identified among the sample groups and visualized in a heatmap (Fig.  D). Further comparison between the two groups delineated their specific expression changes. Relative to the HC group, the CAS group showed upregulation of proteins such as MCAM and downregulation of CDH2 (Fig.  E). Compared with HC, the AMI group exhibited elevated levels of proteins including DSCAML1 and reduced levels of CXCR4 (Fig.  F). Furthermore, a direct comparison between AMI and CAS revealed an increase in proteins like ACE2, while proteins such as CD8A were decreased (Fig.  G). This suggests that the protein profile of plasma EVs undergoes dynamic alterations across the spectrum of coronary artery stenosis severity. Subsequently, a focused analysis of DEPs between the HC and AMI groups was conducted in the search for myocardial infarction biomarkers. Notably, DSCAML1, CR1, ACE2, FN1, CDH15, and C5b-9 were found to be significantly upregulated in AMI patients. A similar upward trend was noted in the CAS group compared to HC, but it was not statistically significant (Fig.  H). ROC curve analyses revealed that in distinguishing AMI patients from healthy controls, the area under the curve (AUC) values were 0.7692 for DSCAML1, 0.7751 for CR1, 0.8343 for ACE2, 0.8343 for FN1, 0.8402 for CDH15, and 0.8106 for C5b-9 (Fig.  I). Collectively, these proteins were identified as potential EV-based biomarkers for AMI.
 

## EV subpopulation alteration in AMI patients
To cluster EV subpopulations based on proteomic characteristics identified by PBA technology, we applied the unsupervised machine learning algorithm FlowSOM [ ], which enables dimensionality reduction and clustering of EVs. As shown in Fig.  A, EV subpopulations were visualized using t-distributed stochastic neighbor embedding (t-SNE) [ ]. FlowSOM analysis characterized a total of 17 distinct EV clusters, with the top-ranked protein in each cluster serving as a molecular feature to define its proteomic signature. Representative examples include DSCAML1, CR1, ALCAM, and DSC1, which highlighted the distinct protein signatures of specific clusters (Fig.  B, Figure S2A). We then analyzed the phenotypic similarities and differences among AMI, CAS, and HC groups. The distribution of EVs across subpopulations was quantified and displayed using t-SNE, revealing significant compositional differences between the groups (Fig.  C), with a comprehensive sample-level distribution shown in Fig.  D.
 Among all identified subpopulations, cluster 1 represented the largest proportion of total EVs, accounting for 36.12%, and exhibited a progressive increase from 16.75% in HC to 19.08% in CAS and 64.18% in AMI. Cluster 8 and 9, while minor in overall size, were dominated by AMI-derived EVs, which made up 55.23% and 53.68% of these clusters, respectively (Fig.  E). Single-EV proteomic analysis revealed distinct subpopulations with characteristic protein signatures. Cluster 1 was characterized by the universal presence of DSCAML1, while Clusters 8 and 9 were defined by the ubiquitous expression of ALCAM and CR1 on every EV, respectively (Fig.  F, Figure S2B). In summary, cluster 1, 8, and 9 were identified as AMI-associated, characterized by their distinct distribution across the HC, CAS, and AMI groups and their highest proportion in AMI samples.

## The pleiotropic effects of AMI-EVs on the endothelial cells and cardiomyocytes
Given the observed alterations in EV protein composition and subpopulations in the plasma of CAS and AMI patients compared with healthy controls, we hypothesized that these EVs may exert distinct functional roles and contribute to AMI progression. To test this, plasma EVs were isolated and characterized by nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA), transmission electron microscopy (TEM), and Western blotting. NTA revealed that plasma EVs had an average diameter of approximately 60 nm (Fig.  A). TEM showed that EVs from HC, CAS, and AMI groups (HC-EVs, CAS-EVs, and AMI-EVs) exhibited the typical elliptical or spherical morphology with a bilayer membrane (Fig.  B). In addition, Western blot analysis confirmed the presence of the EV markers CD63, syntenin-1, and Alix, while calnexin was absent, consistent with successful EV isolation (Fig.  C) [ ].
 We next investigated the functional effects of EVs from HC, CAS, and AMI donors on human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs), the predominant non-cardiomyocyte cell type involved in MI pathophysiology [ , ]. PKH26-labeled EVs were detected within the cytoplasm of HUVECs, indicating efficient endocytosis (Fig.  D). Functionally, cell viability and proliferation, measured by CCK-8 assay and EdU staining, were significantly increased in HUVECs treated with AMI-EVs or CAS-EVs compared with PBS controls (Fig.  E, F). To further evaluate their pro-migratory activity, scratch wound healing and transwell assays were performed. AMI-EVs treatment substantially enhanced endothelial migration compared with CAS-EVs and HC-EVs (Fig.  G, H). In addition, tube formation assays demonstrated that HUVECs exposed to AMI-EVs or CAS-EVs for 6 h displayed a significant increase in total tube length relative to PBS-treated cells, with AMI-EVs exerting a more potent effect than CAS-EVs, whereas HC-EVs showed no significant difference from PBS controls (Fig.  I). Taken together, these results indicate that AMI-EVs and CAS-EVs enhance endothelial cell proliferation, migration, and angiogenesis, with AMI-EVs exerting superior pro-neovascularization activity.
To determine whether AMI-EVs exert comparable effects on cardiomyocyte, we next assessed their uptake and function in H9C2 cells. Confocal microscopy demonstrated that EVs were predominantly localized in the cytoplasm, indicating efficient internalization by H9C2 cells (Figure S3A). Functionally, AMI-EVs markedly enhanced H9C2 viability and proliferation compared with PBS control, as assessed by CCK-8 and EdU incorporation assays. CAS-EVs exerted a similar but less pronounced effect (Figure S3B, C). Collectively, these findings indicate that plasma-derived EVs from AMI and CAS patients promote cardiomyocyte survival and proliferation in vitro.

## DSCAML1-EVs promote HUVECs angiogenesis in vitro
To investigate whether the pro-proliferative effects of AMI-EVs on endothelial cells and cardiomyocytes result from specific, highly expressed proteins, we selected key candidates (DSCAML1, CR1, and ACE2) for subsequent validation. First, we generated EVs to achieve high expression of these target proteins for subsequent experiments. Stable HEK293T cell lines overexpressing DSCAML1, CR1, ACE2 or empty vector were generated via lentiviral transduction [ ], and overexpression was validated by western blotting (Fig.  A). The EVs secreted by these engineered cells were designated as DSCAML1-EVs, CR1-EVs, ACE2-EVs, and Empty-EVs. Then these EVs were characterized by TEM, NTA, and western blotting. These vesicles exhibited the typical cup-shaped morphology, expressed canonical EV markers Alix and HSP70, and displayed a predominant diameter of ~80 nm (Fig.  B-D).
 Given that DSCAML1, CR1, and ACE2 are functionally expressed in endothelial cells and have recognized roles in endothelial biology, we subsequently focused our analyses on the effects of these marker-positive EV subpopulations in endothelial cells [ – ]. Uptake assays confirmed efficient internalization of all four EV types by endothelial cells HUVECs (Fig.  E). We next evaluated their effects on HUVECs viability using the CCK8 assay. As shown in Fig.  F, both DSCAML1-EVs and ACE2-EVs significantly increased cell viability compared with Empty-EVs, whereas CR1-EVs showed no obvious effect. Consistently, EdU staining demonstrated that DSCAML1-EVs, CR1-EVs, and ACE2-EVs all promoted HUVECs proliferation (Fig.  G). To assess the effect of these EV subpopulations on endothelial migration, wound healing and transwell assays were performed. All three EV types enhanced HUVEC migration (Fig.  H-K), with quantitative analysis revealing that DSCAML1-EVs exerted the strongest migration-promoting effect. Furthermore, tube formation assays showed that treatment with DSCAML1-EVs, CR1-EVs, or ACE2-EVs markedly increased vascular network formation compared with Empty-EVs, with DSCAML1-EVs displaying the most robust pro-angiogenic activity (Fig.  L-M). For comparison, efficient uptake of all EV subpopulations was also observed in H9C2 cardiomyocytes (Figure S4A). Functionally, DSCAML1-EVs enhanced cardiomyocyte viability and proliferation, whereas CR1-EVs and ACE2-EVs did not exert comparable effects (Figure S4B-C). Collectively, these results demonstrate that DSCAML1-EVs, CR1-EVs, and ACE2-EVs promote multiple core features of angiogenesis, including enhanced endothelial viability, proliferation, migration, and tube formation, with DSCAML1-EVs showing the most robust effects, while also exerting selective pro-survival and pro-proliferative influences on cardiomyocytes.

## DSCAML1-EVs mediates myocardial repair and angiogenesis in vivo
To investigate the in vivo effects of DSCAML1-EVs, CR1-EVs, and ACE2-EVs on post-infarction cardiac repair, a mouse model of MI was established by ligating the left anterior descending coronary artery (Fig.  A). Successful induction of MI was confirmed by ST-segment elevation on ECG and comparable infarct sizes across groups (Figure S5A). Prior to evaluating therapeutic effects, we assessed the biodistribution of EVs following intravenous administration. All types of EVs were labeled with the near-infrared dye Cy5 and administered to MI mice. Hearts harvested at 6 h post-injection showed detectable Cy5 signals, indicating cardiac accumulation of EVs under ischemic conditions (Fig.  B). To investigate the cellular distribution of EVs in cardiac tissue, we performed immunofluorescence colocalization analysis on frozen cardiac sections. As shown in Figure S5B, Dil-labeled EVs preferentially accumulated in the infarct region, consistent with the enhanced permeability and retention effect characteristic of ischemic tissues. Higher-magnification immunofluorescence analysis further revealed that EV signals were predominantly distributed around vascular structures. Co-immunostaining for CD31 and cardiac troponin T demonstrated that EVs were detectable in both endothelial cells and cardiomyocytes (Figure S5C-E). Histological analysis using TTC and Masson’s trichrome staining at 14 days post-MI showed that administration of DSCAML1-EVs or ACE2-EVs significantly increased left ventricular wall thickness and reduced myocardial fibrosis and infarct size compared with Empty-EVs (Fig.  C-D). In addition, H&E staining indicated that both DSCAML1-EVs and ACE2-EVs alleviated inflammatory cell infiltration in the infarcted myocardium (Fig.  E). Echocardiography performed on day 7 and day 14 further demonstrated significant improvements in cardiac function in the DSCAML1-EVs and ACE2-EVs groups, with DSCAML1-EVs producing more pronounced benefits in both systolic (LVEF, LVFS, LVIDs) and diastolic (LVIDd) function (Fig.  F; Figure S5F). By contrast, CR1-EVs showed no appreciable effects compared with Empty-EVs. Consistently, serum levels of N-terminal pro-B-type natriuretic peptide (NT-proBNP) in mice further confirmed that both DSCAML1-EVs and ACE2-EVs improved post-infarction cardiac function, with DSCAML1-EVs exhibiting the greatest efficacy (Fig. S5G). These findings indicate that DSCAML1-EVs and ACE2-EVs, but not CR1-EVs, protect the infarcted heart against adverse remodeling and improve post-MI cardiac function.
 Cardiomyocyte apoptosis is a major determinant of post-infarction ventricular remodeling. To assess whether these EVs exert anti-apoptotic effects in vivo, we performed TUNEL staining in the peri-infarct region at day 14. Compared with Empty-EVs, DSCAML1-EVs, CR1-EVs, and ACE2-EVs all significantly reduced the number of TUNEL-positive cells, with DSCAML1-EVs showing the strongest anti-apoptotic effect (Fig.  G). Consistent with this, caspase-3 staining confirmed markedly lower apoptotic activity in the DSCAML1-EVs group relative to Empty-EVs (Figure S5H). Given that angiogenesis also contributes to limiting cardiomyocyte loss and fibrosis after MI [ ], we next examined neovascularization in the infarcted hearts. Immunostaining for CD31 and α-SMA demonstrated significantly increased vessel density in both DSCAML1-EVs and ACE2-EVs groups compared with PBS or Empty-EVs, with DSCAML1-EVs again showing superior pro-angiogenic activity (Fig.  H and I). Western blot analysis of cardiac tissues further revealed elevated expression of pro-angiogenic proteins VEGFA and VEGFR in the DSCAML1-EVs, CR1-EVs, and ACE2-EVs groups (Figure S5I). Taken together, these results demonstrate that DSCAML1-EVs and ACE2-EVs enhance post-MI cardiac repair by attenuating apoptosis, suppressing inflammation, and promoting angiogenesis, thereby improving cardiac functional recovery, with DSCAML1-EVs exhibiting the most robust therapeutic effects.

## DSCAML1-EVs promotes angiogenesis by regulating the EREG/ERK pathway
Given that our experimental data identified DSCAML1-EVs as improving myocardial infarction via angiogenesis by boosting endothelial cell function, we proceeded to dissect the specific mechanism of DSCAML1-EVs on endothelial cells. We compared the mRNA expression profiles of DSCAML1-EVs and Empty-EVs by RNA sequencing. HUVECs were co-cultured with EVs for 24 h, followed by RNA extraction and sequencing. Differential expression analysis revealed 509 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in the DSCAML1-EVs group relative to the Empty-EVs group, with 212 upregulated and 297 downregulated (Fig.  A). KEGG pathway enrichment analysis of these DEGs highlighted the mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) signaling pathway as a key regulator (Fig.  B). Within this pathway, epiregulin (EREG), a factor closely associated with angiogenesis [ ], was identified as a critical gene (Fig.  C).
 To validate the sequencing results, qRT-PCR was performed, which confirmed the upregulation of EREG in the DSCAML1-EVs group (Fig.  D). At the protein level, DSCAML1-EVs significantly enhanced EREG expression in HUVECs compared with Empty-EVs (Fig.  E, F). Previous studies have shown that EREG, an EGFR ligand, activates downstream pathways such as MEK/ERK upon binding to EGFR/ERBB, thereby promoting angiogenesis, proliferation, and stress response [ , ]. Consistent with this mechanism, DSCAML1-EVs markedly increased the phosphorylation of ERK in HUVECs, whereas other MAPK pathways (P38 and JNK) were not significantly affected (Fig.  G; Figure S6). Collectively, these findings suggest that DSCAML1-EVs promote angiogenesis and exert their biological effects by upregulating EREG and selectively activating the ERK signaling pathway.

## Discussion
Acute myocardial infarction (AMI) remains a leading cause of mortality among cardiovascular diseases, and timely intervention is essential to improving clinical outcomes. Although high-sensitivity troponin detects myocardial injury, its lack of disease specificity highlights the need for early and accurate biomarkers for AMI diagnosis. In this study, we employed PBA to characterize the heterogeneous populations of plasma-derived EVs in patients with varying degrees of coronary artery stenosis. Among the identified EVs, DSCAML1-positive EVs emerged as a distinct population with strong diagnostic potential for AMI. Functionally, DSCAML1-EVs promoted myocardial repair and angiogenesis both in vitro and in vivo. Mechanistically, these effects were mediated, at least in part, through upregulation of EREG and subsequent activation of the ERK signaling pathway.
EVs are emerging as promising biomarkers for CVD due to their ability to reflect the state of their parent cells and their potential for early disease detection. Current investigations have primarily focused on miRNAs and proteins encapsulated in plasma-derived EVs as potential biomarkers [ ]. Additionally, Sze et al. identified a panel of six novel plasma-derived EV proteins related to myocardial injury as biomarkers, including C1QA, C5, APOD, APOC3, GP1BA, and PPBP, in an independent cohort [ ]. Despite these advances, disease-associated EVs are often present at low abundance relative to other plasma EVs, which may obscure their diagnostic potential [ ]. To address this challenge, we utilized PBA [ ], an innovative method that enables high-resolution profiling of single EV, thereby permitting the identification of disease-specific EV subpopulations. To our knowledge, this represents the first application of PBA technology for biomarker discovery in CVD. Using this approach, we directly profiled EVs from human plasma and characterized the surface protein combinations of individual vesicles. FlowSOM clustering analysis and t-SNE visualization further enabled the identification of discrete EV subpopulations. Among these, DSCAML1 was predominantly enriched in cluster 1 and displayed the highest expression levels. ROC analysis revealed an AUC of 0.77, supporting the potential of DSCAML1 as a candidate single-vesicle biomarker. Nonetheless, validation in larger, independent cohorts will be essential.
Beyond biomarker discovery, another important finding of this study is the biological activity of disease-associated EVs. Both AMI-EVs and CAS-EVs markedly enhanced endothelial cell proliferation, migration, and angiogenesis, with AMI-EVs exerting stronger effects, suggesting their potent pro-angiogenic and reparative properties. These observations are consistent with prior studies reporting that plasma EVs derived from myocardial infarction patients or animal models can attenuate post-infarction inflammation and improve cardiac function in mouse [ , ]. However, given the heterogeneity of plasma EVs, it has remained uncertain whether these protective effects are mediated by specific EV proteins enriched under pathological conditions. Here, based on the DSCAML1-EVs cluster identified through our PBA screening, we constructed stable HEK293T cell lines overexpressing DSCAML1 via lentiviral plasmid transduction and generated DSCAML1-EVs, which were further assessed for their potential effects. The results showed that DSCAML1-EVs play a pivotal role in promoting cardiac function following myocardial infarction and contribute to key features of angiogenesis after MI, including EC viability, proliferation, migration, and tube formation. Indeed, DSCAMs are a group of immunoglobulin-like transmembrane proteins that is implicated in the neurological and cardiac phenotypes associated with Down’s syndrome [ , ]. DSCAML1 belongs to the immunoglobulin superfamily and can mediate cell adhesion which plays important roles in neural development [ , ]. The impact of DSCAML1 on the cardiovascular system is currently poorly understood and necessitates further study. Additionally, we found that AMI-EVs and DSCAML1-EVs promote cardiomyocyte proliferation in vitro. However, caution is warranted when extrapolating these in vitro findings, as they were obtained using a proliferative cell line, whereas adult cardiomyocytes in vivo are non-proliferative. Currently, this effect on cardiomyocytes is considered a suggestive secondary finding that warrants further investigation. Our study could not distinguish whether the beneficial effects of the EVs are mediated predominantly through uptake by endothelial cells or cardiomyocytes. Given the limited regenerative capacity of adult cardiomyocytes, the direct effect of EVs on cardiomyocytes in vivo could not be definitively established. We hypothesize that EVs may influence multiple cell types, and that promoting angiogenesis (likely via endothelial uptake) may constitute a primary mechanism through which they improve cardiac function, while not excluding possible direct effects on cardiomyocytes.
While our results provide valuable insights, several additional limitations beyond those discussed above deserve further consideration. A significant limitation is that, although PBA technology can detect individual single EV proteins, the available data do not allow us to unequivocally assign a specific cellular origin to these EVs. EVs derived from different cell types may carry distinct cargos and exert different biological effects [ ]. Therefore, we cannot confirm which cell types in the human body are responsible for secreting DSCAML1-containing EVs. The enrichment of DSCAML1 + EVs is most reasonably explained by the activation of the “heart-brain axis” under the intense stress state of AMI [ , ], which leads to overactivation of the sympathetic nervous system or stress responses, thereby prompting neurons or glial cells to release DSCAML1-containing EVs into the circulation. However, direct evidence tracing these EVs back to neuronal cells is currently lacking. Future studies utilizing neuron-specific EV labeling techniques or conditional knockout mouse models are required to confirm the origin and validate the inter-organ communication pathway proposed here. CR1, as a complement receptor, is primarily distributed on erythrocytes and immune cells. Following AMI, Cardiomyocyte necrosis releases abundant intracellular contents and the complement system is strongly activated, generating abundant C3b complement fragments [ ]. Circulating CR1-rich EVs may originate from two sources: one is erythrocytes, which, after binding complement-opsonized immune complexes, may release EVs to regulate complement burden; the other is AMI-activated immune cells (such as monocytes/macrophages), which may exhibit upregulated surface CR1 expression during the clearance of necrotic debris and subsequently release CR1-rich EVs. ACE2 is present in vascular endothelial cells and cardiomyocytes. Following myocardial infarction, both endothelial cells and cardiomyocytes can release ACE2 [ ]. Due to the systemic activation of the renin-angiotensin system (RAS) during AMI, these ACE2 positive EVs may also originate from vascular endothelium in other parts of the body. Additionally, although a uniform early blood collection window was applied, EV release post-AMI is a highly dynamic process. As a cross-sectional analysis with a single time point, this study cannot capture the evolving profile of EVs over the disease course. Future longitudinal cohort studies are critically needed, involving serial blood sampling at fixed time points after AMI onset (e.g., at admission, 24 h, 72 h, 1 week), to dynamically map the evolution of the EV surface proteome. Although we conducted animal experiments to investigate the role of EVs in the MI model, the duration of these experiments was not long enough to assess the long-term impact. Previous studies have shown that ventricular remodeling following myocardial infarction typically takes several months to complete [ , ]. The mouse permanent LAD ligation model used in this study simulates acute coronary occlusion through mechanical obstruction but does not encompass the atherosclerotic pathological basis commonly seen in human acute myocardial infarction. Therefore, this study primarily reveals the function of EVs following acute coronary occlusion. Caution is warranted when extrapolating these findings to clinical scenarios, as the function of EVs in human disease may be additionally influenced by factors such as chronic metabolic disorders and the plaque microenvironment. Moreover, further investigations are needed to explore the mechanisms by which DSCAML1-EVs influence myocardial regeneration and angiogenesis in the context of long-term myocardial injury.
In summary, single-EV proteomic profiling revealed that DSCAML1-positive EVs progressively increase in abundance with worsening coronary artery disease severity, reaching peak levels in AMI, thereby highlighting their potential as a biomarker for AMI. Functional analyses further demonstrated that DSCAML1-EVs exerted protective effects on myocardial repair and angiogenesis both in vitro and in vivo. Mechanistically, these effects were mediated, at least in part, through activation of the EREG/ERK signaling pathway. Collectively, our findings highlight DSCAML1-positive EVs as a promising biomarker and therapeutic target for the diagnosis and treatment of AMI.
