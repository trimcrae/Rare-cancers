## Abstract
Acute kidney injury (AKI) is a serious condition marked by a rapid decline in renal function, often leading to long-term complications. Mesenchymal stromal cells (MSCs) and their derivatives, including conditioned medium (CM) and extracellular vesicles (EVs), show promise as regenerative therapies. However, the comparative efficacy of CM and EVs and the development of clinically translatable interventions remains underexplored. This study systematically compared the renoprotective effects of CM and EVs derived from human umbilical cord MSCs in a murine cisplatin-induced AKI model, using a therapeutically feasible dose. Both treatments improved renal function, reduced histological damage, preserved mitochondrial integrity, energy metabolism, and antioxidant response. Notably, EVs induced the greatest proliferative response in renal tubular cells. To further enhance the regenerative potential of EVs, we engineered MSCs to overexpress nicotinamide phosphoribosyltransferase (NAMPT), a metabolic enzyme that plays a key role in NAD+ biosynthesis. NAMPT-transfected MSCs released NAMPT-enriched EVs, which more effectively enhanced cell viability, reduced apoptosis, and protected mitochondria in cisplatin-damaged tubular cells in vitro compared to EV-GFP. In mice with AKI, NAMPT-enriched EVs improved renal function and repaired damage by enhancing renal NAMPT and NAD+ levels, promoting tubular cell regeneration. Mechanistically, the amelioration of mitochondrial function was related to increased PGC1α and SIRT3 and consequently SOD2 and ATP5i expression. These findings highlight the therapeutic potential of EVs, particularly NAMPT-enriched EVs, in renal repair, supporting their promise as a clinically translatable approach for promoting recovery from AKI and other kidney diseases.

## Introduction
Acute kidney injury (AKI) is a serious condition marked by a rapid decline in renal function, typically indicated by elevated serum creatinine levels and reduced urine output [ ]. Its causes are diverse and include sepsis, renal hypoperfusion, and exposure to nephrotoxic agents such as cisplatin [ ]. AKI affects 10–15% of hospitalized patients and over 50% of those in intensive care units [ ]. It can lead to long-term complications, including fibrosis and nephron loss, which contribute to the development of chronic kidney disease (CKD). Currently, the management for AKI is largely limited to supportive care, underscoring the urgent need for innovative therapeutic strategies.
Cell therapy with mesenchymal stromal cells (MSCs) has emerged as a promising strategy to support renal function and promote kidney regeneration in pre-clinical AKI models [ , ]. MSCs derived from various sources have shown protective effects on renal function by reducing apoptosis and enhancing tubular cell turnover [ – ]. In murine models of cisplatin-induced AKI, MSC administration has been shown to facilitate tubular cell recovery by preserving both the structural and functional integrity of mitochondria [ ]. Despite these encouraging results in experimental settings, clinical studies involving MSCs for kidney diseases remain limited, with most trials to date focusing primarily on safety and tolerability [ – ]. Given that MSCs exert their effects largely through paracrine mechanisms, research has increasingly turned toward MSC-derived products like conditioned medium (CM), which contains bioactive molecules, and extracellular vesicles (EVs), which can replicate the regenerative effects of MSCs in experimental models of renal diseases [ , , – ]. These cell-free strategies may offer the advantage of reducing possible risks like immune rejection, tumor formation, embolism, and infection transmission, while possibly providing a cost-effective, practical alternative that can be mass-produced, stored long-term, and easily made available for clinical use [ , ].
In models of cisplatin-induced AKI, CM from umbilical cord (uc) MSCs has been shown to preserve renal function by maintaining mitochondrial functional integrity [ ]. Notably, MSC-derived CM induced global metabolic reprogramming in cisplatin-injured tubular cells in vitro, upregulating the genes involved in mitochondrial energy production and homeostasis, including adenine monophosphate-activated protein kinase (AMPK) and its downstream target, nicotinamide phosphoribosyltransferase (NAMPT), the rate-limiting enzyme in the nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NAD+) salvage pathway [ ]. NAD+ is essential for mitochondrial function, energy metabolism, and the activity of NAD+ responsive enzymes like SIRT3, which regulates oxidative stress, inflammation, and cellular senescence [ ]. Emerging evidence suggests that boosting NAD+ levels through supplementation with its precursors may offer therapeutic potential for treating a range of diseases [ ]. Nevertheless, a key experimental and clinical limitation of NAD+ precursor supplementation is their low oral bioavailability, primarily due to extensive metabolism in the gastrointestinal tract and liver [ ]. When administered orally, the NAD+ precursors NR, NMN and NAM are primarily degraded by the gut microbiota [ ]. In addition, liver metabolism of NAD+ precursors limits their ability to reach other tissues and reduces their bioavailability [ ], diminishing their therapeutic efficacy. Given these challenges with absorption, strategies that protect NAD+ precursors from degradation could enhance their effectiveness. In this context systemic delivery using microvesicles loaded with NAD+ precursors might offer a promising solution.
The EVs, an important component of the MSC secretome, exhibit bioactive functions and are thought to play a significant role in mediating the protective effects of CM. These natural membranous nanoparticles carry bioactive molecules, including microRNAs, mRNAs, and proteins that facilitate intercellular communication and modulate recipient cell behavior [ ]. Several lines of evidence have demonstrated the renoprotective effect of EVs derived from various cell sources, including human bone marrow MSCs and ucMSCs, in experimental AKI models [ , ]. These renoprotective effects are associated with enhanced tubular cell proliferation, driven by antioxidant and anti-apoptotic mechanisms [ ]. EVs can also be engineered for targeted therapeutic applications, often demonstrating greater efficacy in treating kidney diseases compared to unmodified EVs [ ]. For example, functionalized EVs have been developed to deliver therapeutic siRNAs targeting P65 and Snai1 directly to the injured kidney, reducing tubulointerstitial inflammation and fibrosis [ ]. Likewise, EVs that overexpress indoleamine 2,3-dioxygenase, an enzyme that is critical in modulating immune responses and inflammation, improved renal function in mice with ischemia-reperfusion injury [ ]. Despite these encouraging findings, further research is needed to address certain limitations in the clinical application of EVs. Most pre-clinical studies use very high doses of EVs that are impractical and potentially unsafe for routine clinical use [ , ]. Moreover, EV administration is often preventive, occurring before substantial kidney injury has developed. This approach limits the translational relevance of these findings. Therefore, further research is needed to optimize dosing regimens and delivery timing, and to fully understand the safety and efficacy of EV-based therapies in relevant disease contexts.
To address these critical gaps, this study aims to develop a more clinically translatable EV-based intervention by systematically comparing, in a cisplatin-induced AKI model, the renoprotective effects of CM and its corresponding EV components, administered at a concentration lower than typically reported in the literature. Furthermore, treatment efficacy will be assessed when CM and EVs are given after the onset of renal injury. This approach is designed to better reflect clinical conditions to enhance translational relevance. In addition, based on transcriptomic analysis of MSC-derived CM, which identified the NAD+ pathway as a key mediator of MSC therapeutic activity [ ], we will engineer MSCs to produce EVs that overexpress NAMPT. This strategy enables the EV-mediated delivery of NAMPT, both as mRNA and protein, directly into target cells, thereby supplying the molecular machinery for local NAD⁺ biosynthesis and effectively bypassing the bioavailability limitations associated with oral administration of NAD⁺ precursors. Through comprehensive in vivo and in vitro studies with engineered EVs, we aim to enhance the therapeutic potential of EVs in renal repair processes following acute injury.

## Materials and methods

## Cell cultures
Human umbilical cord mesenchymal stromal cells (ucMSCs), kindly provided by Dr Martino Introna and Dr Chiara Capelli (Center of Cellular Therapy G. Lanzani, ASST Papa Giovanni XXIII, Bergamo, Italy), were obtained in accordance with a Good Manufacturing Practice-compliant protocol, as previously described [ ]. The ucMSC preparation used in this study has been characterized extensively by our group [ , ] in accordance with the ISCT criteria. Immunophenotypic analysis showed that ucMSCs strongly expressed the main MSC markers CD73, CD90, and CD105, as well as CD44 and CD166. Additionally, ucMSCs exhibited a high proliferative capacity, as assessed by colony forming unit (CFU-F) assays and typical differentiation potential into adipogenic, chondrogenic, and osteogenic lineages [ , ]. For the experiments, ucMSCs were grown in culture medium, consisting of αMEM (Invitrogen) enriched with 5% human platelet lysate and 50 mg/mL gentamicin in a humidified atmosphere of 5% CO2 at 37 °C. At 80% confluence, cells were detached, seeded at the concentration of 10,000 cells/cm2 and used for up to a maximum eight passages. Human renal proximal tubular epithelial cells (RPTECs) were purchased from Lonza (CC-2553) and grown in REBM medium (CC-3191, Lonza, Basel, Switzerland) enriched with REGM SingleQuotsTM (CC-4127, Lonza) and 5% fetal calf serum (FCS) in a humidified atmosphere of 5% CO2 at 37 °C. RPTECs were used for the experiments for up to a maximum of eight passages.

## Protocol for isolation of CM and EVs
To prepare CM, ucMSCs at 80% confluence were incubated overnight in medium supplemented with 1% ultracentrifuged FCS (10 ml per flask). The following day, the medium was collected and centrifuged at 2000×g for 20 min at 4 °C to remove cellular debris. Supernatant was then concentrated using Amicon Ultra-15 centrifugal Filter Devise cut off 3 K (Merck Millipore) and centrifuged at 4000xg for 20 min.
EVs were purified from CM using an ultracentrifugation protocol. Cell-free supernatants were transferred into Polypropylene Centrifuge Tubes (Beckman Coulter), ultracentrifuged at 100,000xg for 1 h at 4 °C, washed in PBS, and ultracentrifuged a second time under the same conditions. The EV pellet was then resuspended in 100 µL of PBS, and the protein content was quantified using the Bradford method (BioRad). For in vitro and in vivo experiments, fresh EVs were used.

## Lentivirus-mediated overexpression of NAMPT
GFP Control (PS100093V) and GFP-tagged NAMPT (RC210707L4V) lentiviruses, a standard 3rd generation lentivirus system, which is replication-deficient, were obtained from Origene (https://cdn.origene.com/assets/documents/lentiviral/biosafety-features-of-origenes-lentiviral-system.pdf). When ucMSCs reached 70% confluence, a medium containing lentivirus (24 MOI) and polybrene (8 µg/ml; TR-1003-G; Sigma-Aldrich) was added. After 24-hour incubation, the supernatants were replaced with α-MEM (Gibco) supplemented with 5% human platelet lysate and 50 µg/mL gentamicin, and the cells were cultured for an additional 48 h. Stably infected cells were passaged at least once. The infection efficiency was assessed using fluorescence microscopy. To enrich the transfected cell population, selection with 1 µg/mL puromycin (Gibco) was performed and medium was changed two additional times before the cells were used for the experiments.

## Characterization of EVs derived from transfected and untransfected UcMSCs
For characterization, EVs were isolated from conditioned medium of untransfected ucMSCs, GFP-ucMSCs, and NAMPT-ucMSCs. A total of 10 µL of each EV preparation was adsorbed onto copper grids for 1 min, blotted, and stained with 10 µL uranyl acetate for 1 min. After drying at room temperature, samples were imaged at 80 kV using a Hitachi HT-7700 transmission electron microscope.
EVs suspensions were diluted in PBS and analyzed using Particle Metrix PMX120 to determine particle size distribution and concentration. Measurements were performed on diluted samples following the manufacturer’s settings for exosome-sized particles.
For Western blot analysis, EV preparations were lysed in lysis buffer supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors and incubated on ice for 30 min. After centrifugation at 13,000 g for 10 min at 4 °C, 10 µL of lysate were quantified using a BCA assay (Thermo, #23227) and absorbance was measured at 562 nm using a TECAN Infinite F50 microplate reader. Equal amounts of protein (5 µg) from each exosome lysate were resolved on 8–20% SDS-PAGE gels and transferred onto 0.22 μm PVDF membranes (ISEQ00010, Millipore). Membranes were blocked with 5% skim milk in Tris-buffered saline (TBS) supplemented with 0.1% Tween-20 for 1 h at room temperature and probed for exosomal markers CD9 (1:3000; 20597-1-AP, Proteintech), CD63 (1:1000; ab134045, Abcam), and CD81 (1:2000; 27855-1-AP, Proteintech). The intracellular endoplasmic reticulum marker calnexin (1:1000; ab22595, Abcam) was used as a negative marker to evaluate the purity of EV preparations. The signals were visualized on Bioimaging System (Cytiva, Amersham ImageQuant 800) or on an Odyssey FC Imaging System (LiCor) by chemiluminescent substrate with a secondary goat anti-rabbit IgG H&L (HRP) antibody (1:10,000; ab97051, Abcam).

## Quantitative real-time RT-PCR
Total RNA was extracted from cells and EVs using the Trizol reagent (Thermo Fisher, Life Technologies) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Contaminating genomic DNA was removed with RNase-free DNase (Promega, Madison, WI, USA) for 1 h at 37 °C. The first-strand complementary DNA (cDNA) (1.5 µg) was produced using the SuperScript II cDNA Synthesis Kit (Thermo Fisher, Life Technologies) for 1 h at 42 °C. No enzymes were added for reverse transcriptase-negative controls.
Amplification was performed using the 7300 Real-Time PCR System with the SYBR Green PCR Master Mix (Applied Biosystems) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The thermal cycling conditions were as follows: an initial hold at 50°C for 2 minutes and at 95°C for 10 minutes, followed by 40 cycles of denaturation at 95°C for 15 seconds and annealing/extension at 60°C for 60 seconds. Gene expression levels were quantified using the comparative Ct method, which normalizes the target gene copy number to that of the housekeeping gene 18S rRNA (ΔCt). The relative quantity of amplified molecules was determined using the formula 2−ΔΔCt. The following primers were used: for 18S rRNA (3 µM) forward 5’-ACGGCTACCACATCCAAGGA-3’, reverse 5’-CGGGAGTGGGGTAATTTGCG-3’; and for NAMPT (3 µM) forward 5’-GAACAGATACTGTGGCGGGA-3’, reverse 5’-AAGCCGTTATGGTACTGTGCT-3’.

## In vitro experimental design
RPTECs seeded at 20,000 cells/cm2 were exposed to the nephrotoxic drug cis-diaminedichloroplatinum, cisplatin (25 µM, Teva Srl, Rome, Italy) for 6 h in REBM basal medium (Lonza) supplemented with 2% FCS. Then, cisplatin was removed and cells were maintained for 24 h in REBM basal medium with 2% ultracentrifuged FCS (control medium) alone or in control medium containing 0.06 µg EV-GFP or 0.06 µg EV-NAMPT (see experimental design Fig.  C). This amount of EVs was produced by a number of ucMSCs similar to the number of RPTECs per well. At the end of the incubations, RPTECs were used to assess cell viability and for immunofluorescence analysis.
 Cell viability was evaluated using phase contrast inverted microscope (Primovert, Zeiss, Jena, Germany) and images were taken using a 10x objective. The number of viable cells was counted for each image and data were expressed as the number of viable cells/µm2.
The EV uptake by RPTECs was studied after exposing cells to EV-GFP or EV-NAMPT for 1 h. Then, RPTECs were fixed with 2% paraformaldehyde (PFA, Electron Microscopy Science, Hatfield, PA, USA) and 4% sucrose (Sigma-Aldrich), followed by staining with the DNA dye 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI, Sigma-Aldrich). Cells were examined using a confocal inverted laser microscope (Leica TCS SP8, Leica Microsystems, Wetzlar, Germany).
In selected experiments, RPTECs were incubated with NAMPT-enriched EVs in the presence of the NAMPT inhibitor FK866 (Sigma-Aldrich; Cat# F8557) at a dose of 50 nM, a concentration widely reported as effective for NAMPT inhibition while remaining non-cytotoxic in short-term treatments on normal cells [ , ].

## In vitro Immunofluorescence experiments
After exposing cisplatin-treated RPTECs to medium or EVs, the cells were fixed and permeabilized with 0.3% Triton X-100 (Sigma-Aldrich). Cells were treated with a blocking solution containing 1% FCS, 1% BSA, and 0.2% bovine gelatin in PBS, then incubated with: rabbit anti-cleaved caspase-3 (Asp175) antibody (1:300; 9661; Cell Signaling Technology) or rabbit anti-SOD2 antibody (1:500; 06-984; Merck Millipore), followed by incubation with a goat anti-rabbit Cy3-conjugated secondary antibody (1:80; Jackson ImmunoResearch Laboratories). Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI. Negative controls were incubated with corresponding isotype-matched antibodies. Cells were examined using a confocal inverted laser microscope (Leica TCS SP8). Quantification of the cleaved caspase-3 signal was performed in 8–10 randomly acquired fields per sample using ImageJ software (version 1.54f; WS Rasband, National Institute of Health, USA). A threshold was applied to the total image, and the data were expressed as the percentage of the area occupied by the fluorescent staining, normalized to the number of DAPI-positive nuclei. For SOD2, quantification was performed in 8–10 randomly acquired fields per sample and was based on the percentage of cells that exhibited an altered pattern of SOD2 expression, consisting of a redistribution of the staining in the perinuclear region.

## In vivo experimental design
A total of 76 two-month-old female C57BL6/J wild-type mice were obtained from Charles River Laboratories Italia. Animals were housed at a constant room temperature (22–24 °C) with a 12-hour dark and 12-hour light cycle and fed a standard diet.
To compare the effects of human ucMSC-derived CM or EVs in AKI injury, mice received a subcutaneous injection of cisplatin at the dose of 12.7 mg/kg, as previously described [ , ]. Then, cisplatin-treated mice were randomly assigned to one of three experimental groups and received two intravenous (i.v.) injections, one and two days after cisplatin administration, as follows: Group 1, saline (n = 11); Group 2, ucMSC-derived CM (n = 8); Group 3, ucMSC-derived EVs (n = 7) (see experimental design Fig. A). Fresh CM or EVs for each injection were obtained from 1.5 × 106 ucMSCs. EVs were resuspended in a saline solution (100 µL). Untreated C57BL6/J wild-type mice were used as controls (n = 11). Four days post-cisplatin treatment, mice were anesthetized with isoflurane (4–5% induction, 2–3% maintenance) for renal function determination and then euthanized by CO2 at a flow rate of 30% of the chamber volume per minute (QuietekTM 1, Next Advance, Inc.) to collect organs for subsequent analyses.
 The therapeutic effects of MSC-derived EV-GFP and EV-NAMPT were evaluated in a more severe AKI model induced by a dose of cisplatin of 14.0 mg/kg. Cisplatin-treated mice were randomly assigned to one of three experimental groups and received two i.v. injections, one and two days after cisplatin administration, as follows: Group 1, saline (n = 11); Group 2, EV-GFP (n = 8); and Group 3, EV-NAMPT (n = 7) (see experimental design Fig.  A). Each mouse was injected with fresh EV-GFP or EV-NAMPT resuspended in saline solution at the concentration of 0.7 µg/100 µl (produced by 1.5 × 106 ucMSC). Untreated C57BL6/J wild-type mice were used as controls (n = 10). Four days post-cisplatin treatment, renal function was determined based on BUN and creatinine levels and mice were sacrificed to analyze renal specimens.
 In selected experiments, the toxicity of NAMPT-enriched EVs was evaluated in the kidney, liver and heart by injecting NAMPT-enriched EVs twice, 24 h apart, at the dose of 0.7 µg/100 µl, in control mice that were sacrificed 72 h after the first injection (n = 3). In this group of animals, potential systemic toxicity was also evaluated in serum obtained at sacrifice.
The animals receiving the same treatment were housed in two separate cages to avoid any treatment-related confounding. The researchers performing the procedures on the animals were always aware of the group allocation during all stages of the experiment. For all the experiments, no inclusion or exclusion criteria were used. The researchers conducting all subsequent analyses were blinded to the treatments.

## Laboratory parameters and histology
To evaluate renal function, blood urea nitrogen (BUN) and creatinine levels were measured in controls and all groups of mice with AKI using the Element RC analyzer to assess renal function (SCIL Animal Care Company, Bergamo, Italy). For renal histology, kidney samples were fixed in a Duboscq-Brazil solution and embedded in paraffin. Sections were stained with periodic acid-Schiff’s reagent (PAS) to assess tubular casts and necrosis. Images were acquired using a ×40 objective (Primo Star, Zeiss). The number of casts and necrotic tubular profiles was quantified in at least 10 randomly acquired fields per sample.
To assess potential toxicity, NAMPT-enriched EVs were injected into control mice and plasma levels of alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), alkaline phosphatase (ALP), BUN, creatinine, blood glucose (BG) and cholesterol were measured by Element RC analyzer.
In the same animals, histological analysis of the kidney, liver and heart was performed to evaluate tissue toxicity. Kidney, liver and heart samples were fixed in formalin. Paraffin-embedded Sect. (4-µm thick) were stained with hematoxylin–eosin and assessed by light microscopy (ApoTome Axio Imager Z2; Zeiss).

## Immunohistochemical studies
To perform immunofluorescence analysis on renal tissue, 3-µm PFA-fixed cryosections were washed with PBS. Antigen retrieval was performed and non-specific binding sites were blocked by incubating samples for 1 h with 1% BSA. After blocking, the slides were incubated overnight at 4 °C with the following primary antibodies: rabbit anti-Ki67 (1:200; ab15580, Abcam), rabbit anti-cleaved caspase-3 (Asp175) (1:50; 9661, Cell Signaling Technology), rabbit anti-SOD2 (1:200; 06-984, Merck Millipore) or rabbit anti-KIM-1 (1:50; 30948-1-AP, Proteintech). Then, samples were incubated for 1 h at room temperature with a goat anti-rabbit Cy3-conjugated secondary antibody (1:100; Jackson ImmunoResearch Laboratories). Nuclei were stained with DAPI, and renal structures were labeled with FITC-conjugated wheat germ agglutinin (WGA) lectin (1:400; FL-1021 S, Vector Laboratories). Imaging was performed using confocal inverted laser microscope. Ki67-positive cells were counted in 8–10 randomly acquired fields per mouse, with data expressed as the number of Ki67-positive cells per field. Cleaved caspase-3 staining was quantified in 8–10 randomly acquired fields per mouse using ImageJ software (version 1.54f; WS Rasband, National Institute of Health, USA) and data were expressed as fluorescent area per field. SOD2 expression in tubular structures was evaluated in 8–10 fields per mouse. SOD2 quantification was calculated as percentage of the fluorescent area per field or percentage of tubules with altered SOD2 pattern, normalized to the total number of tubules.
For immunohistochemistry analysis, formalin-fixed, 3-µm paraffin-embedded kidney sections were incubated for 5 min with Peroxidazed 1 (PX968, Biocare Medical) to quench endogenous peroxidase, after antigen retrieval in Rodent Decloaker (RD913, Biocare Medical). After blocking for 30 min with Rodent Block M (RBM961G, Biocare Medical), sections were incubated with the following primary antibodies: rabbit anti-NAMPT (1:100; HPA047776, Sigma), rabbit anti-ATP5i (1:100; HPA035010, Sigma), rabbit anti-PGC1α (1:100; ab54481, Abcam), mouse anti-VDAC (1:400; ab186321, Abcam), and rabbit anti-SIRT3 (1:100; ab189860, Abcam) and rat anti-MAC2 antibody (1:600, CL8942AP, Cederlane). The sections were incubated with rabbit on rodent HRP-Polymer (RMR622H, Biocare Medical) or rat on mouse HRP-Polymer (RT517, Biocare Medical) and the staining was visualized using the betazoid 3,3′diaminobenzidine chromogen kit solutions (BDB2004H, Biocare Medical). Slides were counterstained with Mayer’s hematoxylin (MHS80-2.5 L, Bio Optica) and observed using light microscopy (ApoTome, Axio Imager Z2, Zeiss). Negative controls were included by omitting the primary antibody on adjacent sections. Approximately 8–10 random fields per animal were acquired. The intensity of NAMPT immunostaining was quantified using ImageJ Fiji software with the color-deconvolution plug-in and expressed as the percentage of the positive area per field. ATP5i expression was quantified using ImageJ Fiji software and expressed as the percentage of the positive area per field. Tubular expression of PGC1α was quantified by assigning a score between 0 and 3 (0-signal affecting < 25% of tubuli/field; 1-signal affecting ≥ 25 to 50% of tubuli/field; 2-signal affecting > 50 to 75% of tubuli/field; 3-signal affecting > 75% of tubuli/field). The number of MAC-2-positive monocytes/macrophages in the tubular compartment was quantified in 8–10 fields per sample and expressed as the average number of cells per field. SIRT3 expression was quantified in at least 10 fields per sample and expressed as percentage of positive tubuli.

## NAD+ content assay
Renal tissues were washed twice in a normal 0.9% (w/v) sodium chloride solution, homogenized in extraction buffer for NAD+ evaluation (NAD+/NADH Assay Kit, Abnova, Taipei City, Taiwan) and total protein concentration was determined using the DC™ assay (Bio-Rad Laboratories). Samples were then processed for determination of NAD+ content by the multimode microplate reader TECAN Infinite M200® PRO (Tecan Group Ltd.) at 565 nm under controlled temperature for 15 min according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Results were normalized for total protein concentration of each sample.

## Statistical analysis
Based on an a priori power calculation, the sample size for the in vivo experiments was estimated to be at least n = 7 animals per group to ensure adequate statistical power to detect biologically meaningful differences between experimental conditions. Results are expressed as mean ± SEM. Statistical analysis was performed with GraphPad Prism 9 Software. Comparisons were made using 1-way ANOVA with the Tukey post hoc test. Statistical significance was defined as P < 0.05. See figure legends for details on the number of biological replicates (n) used.

## Results

## Renoprotective effects of human ucMSC-derived CM and EVs in mice with cisplatin-induced AKI
To study and compare the renoprotective effects of CM and EVs derived from human ucMSCs, we used a previously established mouse model of cisplatin-induced AKI [ ]. Briefly, C57BL6/J mice received a single subcutaneous injection of cisplatin following intravenous administrations of saline, human ucMSC-derived CM or EVs, at 24 and 48 h after cisplatin (experimental design, Fig. A), when tubular injury was already present in terms of mitochondrial ultrastructural damage [ ]. Untreated mice served as controls. Each dose of CM and EVs used for injection was derived from 1.5 × 106 ucMSCs, a cell number comparable to those shown to be effective in experimental models of AKI. At day 4, we found that cisplatin injection significantly impaired renal function, as evidenced by elevated BUN and creatinine levels (Fig. B). Administration of CM or EVs significantly improved renal function, as demonstrated by a marked reduction in BUN and creatinine levels in both groups compared to cisplatin mice treated with saline (Fig. B).
To evaluate and compare how CM or EV treatments affect renal structure, we performed histological analysis of PAS-stained kidney sections. Mice with cisplatin-induced AKI had tubular casts and cell alterations, including brush border loss, tubular epithelial denudation, and necrosis (Supplemental Fig. S1). Notably, treatment with CM and even more so with EVs, led to a significant reduction in the number of tubular casts and necrotic tubules compared to saline-treated cisplatin mice (Fig.  C, Supplemental Fig. S1), indicating that both treatments substantially reduced renal tubular damage.

## CM and EVs preserve mitochondrial structural and functional integrity, leading to tubular cell regeneration in mice with cisplatin-induced AKI
We aimed to elucidate the molecular mechanisms through which CM and EV treatments can alleviate tubular injury by investigating their effects on mitochondrial damage. We first used immunohistochemistry to examine the expression of PGC1α, a key regulator of mitochondrial biogenesis [ ], which was constitutively expressed in control mice, primarily within renal tubules (Fig. D, Supplemental Fig. S2). In contrast, cisplatin-treated mice given saline showed a marked decrease in PGC1α expression, which was restored by both CM and EV treatments (Fig. D, Supplemental Fig. S2). Furthermore, the expression of ATP5i, a key component of mitochondrial energy production [ ], and VDAC, a marker of mitochondrial mass [ ], were significantly lower after cisplatin exposure. However, treatment with either CM or EVs brought their levels back down close to those seen in control mice (Fig. E; Supplemental Figs. 3 and 4). In order to study the mitochondrial capacity to mitigate oxidative stress, we investigated the expression of the mitochondrial antioxidant enzyme superoxide dismutase 2 (SOD2) [ ]. We found that SOD2 expression was significantly altered in cisplatin mice receiving saline, with most tubules exhibiting either an altered granular pattern or a reduction in SOD2 levels compared to control mice (Fig. F). Remarkably, both CM and EV treatments re-established widespread SOD2 expression, with EVs having a superior effect (P < 0.01) compared to CM (P < 0.05) (Fig. F). The expression of the mitochondrial protein SIRT3, a deacetylase that regulates the activity of various target proteins, including SOD2 and ATP5i [ , ], decreased markedly in AKI mice given saline compared to controls (Supplemental Fig. 5). In contrast, CM- and EV-treated mice exhibited increased SIRT3 signal similar to that of control kidneys (Supplemental Fig. 5).
Moreover, cisplatin-treated mice showed a significant reduction in proximal tubular cell proliferation, as indicated by decreased Ki67 expression (Fig.  G). In contrast, both CM and EV treatments significantly increased tubular cell proliferation compared to saline-treated cisplatin mice, with EVs having a more pronounced effect (Fig.  G). This suggests that EVs may have greater regenerative potential than CM. The results presented thus far indicate that while both CM and EVs exert renoprotective effects in AKI, EVs appear to have a superior ability to promote antioxidant defense, energy production and pro-regenerative activity in the tubular cell compartment. Based on this evidence, we selected EVs as the preferred tool to further enhance renal tissue regeneration.

## NAMPT-enriched EVs promote cell survival and reduce apoptosis of cultured tubular cells more effectively than EV-GFP by preserving mitochondrial function
Building on our previous transcriptomic analysis that demonstrated that the renoprotective effects of human ucMSCs are largely driven by enhanced NAD+ metabolism [ ], we genetically engineered human ucMSCs to overexpress the rate-limiting enzyme of the NAD+ salvage pathway, NAMPT, in the EVs. First, we conducted a comprehensive characterization of all EV preparations in accordance with the guidelines of the International Society for Extracellular Vesicles [ ] to verify whether the transfection of ucMSCs with GFP or NAMPT alters the fundamental physical or molecular characteristics of the EVs. As shown in Supplemental Fig. S6, TEM analysis showed that EVs derived from GFP- or NAMPT-transfected ucMSCs exhibited exosome-like ultrastructural features consisting of cup-shaped or rounded morphology, similar to that of EVs from untransfected ucMSCs. Similarly, nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA) showed that the average particle size of EVs derived from the three different cell sources was within the exosomal range displaying sharp, single peak distribution, indicative of uniform particle populations (Supplemental Fig. S6). Lastly, Western blot analysis of EVs derived from transfected and untransfected cells confirmed the presence of the typical markers CD63, CD81 and CD9 across all samples (Supplemental Fig. S6), while lack of calnexin, an endoplasmic reticulum marker, confirmed the absence of cellular debris and apoptotic bodies in the EV preparations (Supplemental Fig. S6).
Through real-time RT-PCR we then confirmed a significant upregulation of NAMPT mRNA in the lentivirally-transfected ucMSCs compared to ucMSCs transfected with control GFP and untransfected ucMSCs (Fig.  A, left). Importantly, NAMPT mRNA was successfully incorporated into the EVs derived from NAMPT-overexpressing MSCs (EV-NAMPT), reaching levels 8-fold higher than those found in EVs from GFP-transfected cells (EV-GFP) and untransfected EVs (Fig.  A, right). The uptake of GFP- or NAMPT-enriched EVs were successfully observed in damaged proximal tubular cells (RPTECs), as indicated by the presence of green fluorescence in RPTEC cytoplasm (Fig.  B).
To evaluate the regenerative potential of NAMPT-enriched EVs, we used an in vitro model of cisplatin-induced RPTEC damage (Fig.  C). The analysis of cell viability revealed that, 24 h after cisplatin removal, there was a significant decrease in the number of viable cells in response to a high dose of cisplatin, compared to control cells. In this setting, the addition of NAMPT-enriched EVs preserved cell viability, unlike EV-GFP (Fig.  D). Apoptosis analysis revealed that cleaved caspase-3 expression increased significantly in RPTECs exposed to cisplatin, compared to control cells (Fig.  E). Treatment with EV-GFP or NAMPT-enriched EVs significantly reduced cell apoptosis, with the NAMPT-enriched EVs exhibiting a more pronounced protective effect (Fig.  E).
To assess whether engineered EVs exert their protective effects by mitigating oxidative stress in damaged RPTECs, we evaluated the pattern of expression of SOD2. In control cells, SOD2 exhibited a robust filamentous pattern, indicative of an intact mitochondrial network (Fig.  F). Following cisplatin exposure, there was a dramatic shift in SOD2 localization to the perinuclear region (Fig.  F). Treatment with either EV-GFP or NAMPT-enriched EVs significantly reduced the number of cells with an altered SOD2 pattern (Fig.  F). Notably, NAMPT-overexpressing EVs resulted in a striking restoration of the filamentous SOD2 distribution, highlighting their greater protective capability (Fig.  F). The proof of concept that the protective effect of NAMPT overexpression depends on the restoration of intracellular NAD⁺ levels through activation of the salvage pathway was obtained by evaluating the effect of EV-NAMPT on SOD2 expression in the presence of FK866, a specific NAMPT inhibitor, in cisplatin-treated tubular cells. As shown in Fig.  G, treating cells with FK866 completely abolished the protective effect of EV-NAMPT on the altered SOD2 distribution that appeared to be similar to that observed in cells exposed to cisplatin alone.
These findings possibly suggest that NAMPT-enriched EVs, through restoration of NAD+ levels, confer more efficient mitochondrial protection compared to EV-GFP, and consequently increase cell viability, and restore redox balance, with an improvement in cellular resilience.

## NAMPT-enriched EVs exert a greater renoprotective effect than EV-GFP in mice with cisplatin-induced AKI
The renoprotective effects of NAMPT-enriched EVs were studied in an in vivo setting. First, we ruled out a potential toxic effect of engineered EV-NAMPT per se when injected in control mice by analyzing tissues from the kidney, as well as the liver and heart. Through histological analysis, no structural changes were detected in any of the organs studied (Supplemental Fig. S7) and serological test excluded potential systemic toxicity (Table ).
 Then, we studied the effects of NAMPT-enriched EVs versus EV-GFP in a more severe model of cisplatin-induced AKI (Fig. A) that allowed us to better appreciate the difference across the groups. Compared to controls, cisplatin mice exhibited a significant increase in serum creatinine and BUN levels (Fig. B), which was higher than those observed in mice injected with the lower dose of cisplatin (Fig. B). Mice received an intravenous administration of saline, EV-GFP or NAMPT-enriched EVs, one and two days after cisplatin injection (Fig. A). Treatment with NAMPT-enriched EVs, but not EV-GFP, significantly mitigated renal function impairment, as evidenced by lower serum creatinine levels than those found in cisplatin mice that received saline (Fig. B). Both EV treatments reduced BUN levels, although they did not reach statistical significance (Fig. B). However, significant protection was found in renal structure, as observed through histopathological analysis. Indeed, PAS-stained kidney tissues from cisplatin mice given NAMPT-enriched EVs showed a significant reduction in tubular injury (Fig. C, D). To a lesser extent, EV-GFP provided protective effects by reducing tubular damage (Fig. C, D). These data are supported by the analysis of KIM1, a transmembrane tubular protein that increases in dysfunctional tubuli in various renal diseases [ ]. As shown by the representative images in Supplemental Fig. S8, KIM1 was undetectable in control mice, whereas it increased in cisplatin mice. Treatment with NAMPT-enriched EVs or EV-GFP greatly reduced its expression in tubuli.
To verify whether these greater protective effects could be due to the increased level of NAMPT in renal tissues of AKI mice, we analyzed its protein expression using immunohistochemistry. In control mice, NAMPT protein was predominantly expressed in the cytoplasm of tubular cells, whereas cisplatin significantly reduced its levels (Fig.  A). Treatment with NAMPT-enriched EVs significantly enhanced tubular NAMPT expression, in contrast with EV-GFP, which were not able to modulate NAMPT levels in this model of severe AKI (Fig.  A). When we analyzed renal NAD+ levels, we observed a significant increase in NAD+ content in cisplatin mice that received NAMPT-enriched EVs but not in those that received EV-GFP, compared to cisplatin mice given saline (Fig.  B). Notably, these data show that the effects of NAMPT-enriched EVs on renal NAMPT and NAD+ levels was still present 48 h after treatment (Fig.  A, B).
 Importantly, in cisplatin mice given NAMPT-enriched EVs robust increased tubular cell proliferation was observed, as revealed by a significantly higher number of Ki67-positive tubular cells (Fig.  C). Otherwise, EV-GFP treatment did not induce significant cell proliferation (Fig.  C). In parallel, NAMPT-enriched EVs, but not EV-GFP, effectively limited cell apoptosis induced by cisplatin, as measured through cleaved caspase-3 expression (Fig.  D).
Our results suggest that NAMPT-enriched EVs exert superior renal regenerative effects compared to EV-GFP in mice with AKI.

## NAMPT-overexpressing EVs improve mitochondrial function and reduce inflammatory cell infiltration in mice with cisplatin-induced AKI more effectively than EV-GFP
To better understand the molecular mechanisms driving the effects of NAMPT-enriched EV treatment, we studied the potential protective effect on mitochondria. The marked decrease in PGC1α expression found in cisplatin-treated mice given saline was significantly enhanced by NAMPT-enriched EV administration (Fig.  A). Moreover, treatment with NAMPT-enriched EVs led to a significant increase in ATP5i expression compared to cisplatin mice given saline, while EV-GFP had no significant effect (Fig.  B). When we analyzed SOD2 expression pattern, we found that in cisplatin mice, treatment with NAMPT-enriched EVs reduced the percentage of tubuli with altered SOD2 in contrast to those given saline or EV-GFP (Fig.  C).
 Whether the observed higher ATP5i and improved SOD2 expression could be regulated by SIRT3 was further explored. We found that NAMPT-enriched EVs almost normalized the expression of SIRT3 (Fig.  D) in renal tubuli and this increase was significantly higher than that observed in EV-GFP treatment (Fig.  D).
Finally, to investigate whether NAMPT overexpression regulates inflammation in response to renal tissue damage, we evaluated the infiltration of MAC-2 positive macrophages and found that significantly higher numbers of MAC-2-positive cells infiltrated the tubular compartment in cisplatin mice that received saline compared to control mice (Fig.  E). Treatment with NAMPT-enriched EVs, but not with EV-GFP, reduced the presence of MAC-2-positive cells to levels comparable to the control group (Fig.  E).
Mechanistically, all these in vivo findings suggest that NAMPT-enriched EVs, by enhancing NAMPT-NAD+ levels in damaged tubuli, have potent therapeutic effects as they restore mitochondrial functional integrity (Fig.  ) and induce substantial anti-inflammatory activity, thus translating into remarkable tubular cell recovery from AKI.
 

## Discussion
Our study provides compelling evidence of the renoprotective effects of CM and EVs derived from human ucMSCs in a murine model of cisplatin-induced AKI. While both treatments, administered when injury was already established and with a clinically relevant dose, led to similar improvements in renal function and histological damage, the regenerative response in damaged proximal tubular cells was more effectively promoted in the EV-treated group. Therefore, we aimed to further enhance their therapeutic potential by engineering MSCs to modify the EV cargo via overexpression of NAMPT, a key enzyme in NAD+ metabolism. Our data show that NAMPT-enriched EVs offer superior renoprotective effects in a more severe model of cisplatin-induced nephrotoxicity compared to EV-GFP.
The therapeutic efficacy of EVs in treating experimental AKI has been documented across numerous studies [ ]. However, there are limited data in the literature on comparisons between CM and EVs, as these treatments have never been studied systematically within the same experimental design. This hinders our ability to determine which approach offers greater therapeutic advantages. Our study contributes to addressing this critical issue. Here, we show that CM and EVs derived from the same number of MSCs, have comparable efficacy in treating AKI, supporting the idea that the vesicular component of CM, namely the EVs, is sufficient to produce the observed therapeutic effects. Notably, EVs provided an even greater benefit by promoting tubular cell proliferation.
EVs are a more advantageous tool than CM for therapeutic applications. CM contains a complex and variable mixture of soluble factors and extracellular vesicles, making it more difficult to standardize and characterize. In contrast, isolated EVs are a more defined and concentrated subset of bioactive components, allowing for greater consistency and reproducibility. EVs also exhibit superior stability, as their lipid bilayer protects encapsulated molecules from enzymatic degradation, enhancing their shelf life and functional integrity. Furthermore, EVs can be purified, quantified, and engineered for targeted delivery, which is not feasible with whole conditioned medium. Although a thorough evaluation of their safety and immunogenicity is still needed, along with improvements in production scalability, the properties of EVs make them a more promising and controllable platform for clinical translation [ ]. With regard to dosing, a wide range of EV concentrations has been reported as effective for achieving therapeutic benefit, creating a significant barrier to the reproducibility and comparability of findings [ ]. Most published studies across different experimental AKI models have used very high EV doses, up to 200 µg in some cases [ , , , ]. However, using such high amounts poses challenges for translating pre-clinical results into clinical practice, due to the difficulty of producing EVs at the scale required for therapeutic use. In our in vivo studies, we used a concentration of EVs that is much lower than those commonly used in the literature [ , ] to make our findings more relevant to clinical practice. In addition, unlike the majority of studies that have used EVs as a preventive treatment [ , – ], our approach of administering CM or EVs when renal damage had already been observed, more closely aligns with the timing of intervention in AKI patients.
In our setting, the effectiveness of CM and EV treatments in improving renal function and structure was attributed to their ability to protect mitochondrial homeostasis, ensuring the cellular energy balance was maintained, and re-establishing antioxidant defense in the injured tubular cells of cisplatin mice. In our previous study, genome-wide differential gene expression analysis revealed that cisplatin-damaged tubular cells can be rescued by the paracrine action of MSCs driven by the tubular cell increase of NAMPT gene expression and the consequent NAD+ content, which were markedly decreased in this model [ ]. Building on this, we hypothesized that enhancing NAMPT activity in renal tissues could further improve mitochondrial function and support the tubular regenerative process. In this study, we engineered MSCs to overexpress NAMPT, successfully generating EVs enriched with NAMPT mRNA. This confirms the efficient delivery of the cargo into the vesicles, enhancing their protective effect on cultured injured tubular cells. When engineered EVs were injected into mice with severe AKI, NAMPT expression and consequently NAD+ levels were successfully increased in the tubular compartments, thus promoting renal regeneration and repair, highlighting the therapeutic advantage of NAMPT-enriched EVs over EV-GFP and unmanipulated EVs, as well as its long-lasting effects. These data are supported by recent findings that demonstrate that overexpression of NAMPT preserved the mitochondrial NAD+ pool and, as consequence, promoted cell survival and mitochondrial functional integrity [ ]. Our observations that mitochondrial dysfunction is linked to a decline in renal NAMPT and NAD+ levels in cisplatin mice are in line with this [ ] and with other studies showing that in AKI, NAD+ consumption was dependent on the deficient salvage and biosynthetic pathway [ – ]. A decrease in NAD+ levels may affect the activity of intracellular enzymes that use NAD+ as substrate, including the mitochondrial protein SIRT3, PARPs [ ], as well as ATP synthase [ ]. Several pre-clinical and clinical studies have shown that supplementation with NAD+ precursors can improve kidney function and promote kidney recovery through enhanced mitochondrial function and anti-inflammatory and anti-apoptotic effects [ , , ]. Supplementation with NAD⁺ precursors is a promising strategy for counteracting disease-related declines in NAD⁺ levels. However, it is important to bear in mind that the therapeutic efficacy of this approach is significantly limited by the poor oral bioavailability of these compounds, due to extensive metabolism in the gastrointestinal tract and first-pass clearance in the liver [ ]. Using EVs as delivery vehicles for NAMPT, either in its mRNA or protein form, or for NAD⁺ precursors, may represent a compelling therapeutic alternative. EVs can shield their cargo from enzymatic degradation, enhance cellular uptake, and improve biodistribution. They also enable targeted delivery to sites of tissue damage or inflammation, thereby increasing local NAD⁺ availability and helping to overcome the systemic limitations of conventional precursor administration.
Having established that increased renal NAMPT/NAD⁺ levels in cisplatin-injured tissues translated into robust renoprotective effects following engineered EV therapy, we next investigated their contribution to maintaining mitochondrial functional integrity. Mechanistically, we first examined PGC1α, a NAD+-dependent regulator of mitochondrial biogenesis [ ], and found that NAMPT-enriched EVs restore its expression. Considering that PGC1α also drives SIRT3 transcription, a mitochondrial NAD+-dependent deacetylase that orchestrates adaptive stress responses and energy metabolism [ , ], we examined its expression and observed a clear upregulation following NAMPT-enriched EV treatment. Beyond transcriptional control, SIRT3 activity can be directly modulated through NAMPT-dependent NAD⁺ replenishment [ ]. Our finding that engineered EVs enhance the mitochondrial expression of SOD2 and ATP5i - two canonical SIRT3 targets involved in antioxidant defense and ATP production [ , ], further supports the functional role of NAMPT-NAD⁺-SIRT3 axis in mitochondrial functional recovery induced by NAMPT-enriched EVs (Fig. ).
Concomitantly, an interplay between the preservation of mitochondrial functions and reduced inflammation in response to NAMPT-enriched EVs was demonstrated by our data that renal macrophage infiltrates were markedly reduced in cisplatin-damaged tissues, underscoring another important effect of the NAMPT-NAD+ axis in supporting renal repair. These findings align with recent studies showing that NAD⁺ precursor supplementation can prevent inflammatory cell recruitment during kidney injury [ ].
In conclusion, our findings demonstrate that EVs are the central component of the MSC secretome responsible for the renoprotective effects of CM. Moreover, NAMPT enrichment in EV cargo provides strong proof-of-concept that promoting mitochondrial-mediated regenerative pathways through cell engineering, can deliver a significant therapeutic advantage. These insights pave the way for the development of innovative, EV-based therapies for treating kidney injury and other diseases associated with mitochondrial dysfunction. Nevertheless, while native and engineered EVs offer an increasingly promising and controllable platform for future applications, their clinical translation will benefit from further thorough evaluation of safety, immunogenicity, and production scalability. Moreover, extending investigations beyond the acute phase, through long-term and fibrosis-oriented models, will be essential for determining whether the therapeutic benefits of engineered EVs can also foster reparative processes in chronic kidney disease, representing a promising avenue for future therapies.
