## Significance
Hearing loss (HL) is a common yet poorly understood complication in systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE), an autoimmune disease affecting multiple organs. This study unveils a mechanism behind SLE-related HL using an induced SLE mouse model. We demonstrate that damage to the blood–labyrinth barrier (BLB) in the inner ear, caused by cytotoxic immune cells, leads to hearing impairment. Specifically, we identify granzyme B as a key factor in damaging the barrier’s integrity. Importantly, inhibiting granzyme B significantly improves hearing in SLE mice. These findings not only enhance our understanding of autoimmune-related hearing loss but also suggest a potential therapeutic approach for preserving hearing in SLE patients, with implications for other autoimmune disorders affecting sensory functions.

Hearing loss (HL) in patients with systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) has been widely reported, though the underlying mechanisms remain unclear. In this study, we demonstrate significant HL in an imiquimod-induced mouse model of SLE, accompanied by blood–labyrinth barrier (BLB) damage in the stria vascularis (SV). We found that cytotoxic CD8+ T lymphocytes and natural killer cells mediate BLB disruption, with granzyme b (Gzmb) acting as the primary factor inducing cell death of the cochlear capillary endothelial cells. Remarkably, inhibiting Gzmb by Serpinb9 significantly alleviates the elevations of hearing thresholds in imiquimod-induced SLE model mice, suggesting a therapeutic target for autoimmune-related HL. Our findings provide insights into the pathogenesis of SLE-related HL and propose Gzmb inhibition as a potential therapeutic strategy.

HL is associated with dementia, depression, and mortality and has a negative impact on the psychosocial state, financial burden, and quality of life ( ). HL is anatomically categorized as sensorineural, conductive, and mixed, in which sensorineural hearing loss (SNHL) is the most common type in adults ( ). SNHL may arise from damage to the sensory structures of the inner ear, including the organ of Corti, the stria vascularis (SV), or the spiral ganglion neurons (SGNs) ( ). While SNHL is associated with risk factors such as aging, noise exposure, and ototoxic drugs, autoimmune disorders have also emerged as critical contributors to SNHL. While autoimmune-related SNHL is extensively observed in clinic ( ), the underlying mechanisms of autoimmune-related HL remain poorly understood.
Systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) is an autoimmune disease characterized by the production of nuclear autoantibodies that lead to inflammation and multiorgan damage ( ). SLE primarily affects organs such as the kidneys, skin, and joints, but emerging evidence indicates that the auditory system may also be involved ( ). Several clinical studies have reported a correlation between SLE and HL, with sensorineural HL being the predominant form observed ( ). A meta-analysis found that approximately 27% of SLE patients experience HL, suggesting a possible link between the immune system and cochlear dysfunction ( ). Importantly, SNHL is recognized as the major HL type in SLE patients ( ), indicating that cochlea tissues are the primary targets. Despite these clinical observations, the mechanisms underlying hearing impairment in SLE are not well elucidated, and experimental models are limited.
In this study, we established an SLE mouse model induced by imiquimod (IMQ), which display significant HL and blood–labyrinth barrier (BLB) damage. Our findings revealed that cytotoxic CD8+ T lymphocytes (CTLs) and natural killer (NK) cells are key contributors to BLB damage through the release of granzyme B (Gzmb), which induces cell death in cochlear endothelial cells. We further demonstrated that inhibition of Gzmb effectively rescues HL in IMQ-induced SLE mice. Our study sheds light on the pathogenesis of autoimmune-related HL, particularly in SLE, and highlights the potential for targeting immune-mediated damage in the cochlea as a therapeutic approach.

## Results

## HL in IMQ-Induced SLE Model Mice Occurs During the Midprogression of the Disease.
Multiple mouse models of SLE have been previously reported, including spontaneous models, induced models, and humanized mouse models ( ). To investigate the onset and progression of HL in SLE, we induced SLE in mice using IMQ, a Toll-like receptor 7 (TLR7) agonist, as previously described ( ). To evaluate the manifestations of SLE at different time points (2, 4, 6, 8, and 10 wk after IMQ induction), we detected levels of serum indicators (anti-dsDNA antibody, anti-Sm antibody, and IgG), urine proteins, spleen weight, and renal pathology of the model mice. Spleens were dissected and weighed. Spleen index was calculated as spleen weight/body weight, which was increased significantly at week 4 ( ). Serum anti-dsDNA antibody, anti-Sm antibody, IgG, and urine protein levels were increased significantly at week 4 after induction ( and SI Appendix, Fig. S1A). Kidneys were dissected followed by immunofluorescence and hematoxylin-eosin (H&E) staining, which showed glomerular IgG deposition and histopathology ( and SI Appendix, Fig. S1B). These results indicate the successful establishment of the SLE mouse model induced by IMQ.
 To evaluate the auditory function, thresholds of the auditory brainstem response (ABR) and distortion products otoacoustic emissions (DPOAE) were measured at different time points (weeks 2, 4, 6, and 8). In addition, shifts of ABR threshold were also calculated and contrasted. Elevations of the ABR thresholds began at week 4 and continued to increase from week 6 to 8 after induction ( ). Similarly, DPOAE thresholds were also elevated dramatically at week 4 and were stable at week 6 and 8 ( ), suggesting the potential damage of outer hair cells (OHCs) or SV. ABR thresholds, ABR threshold shifts, and DPOAE thresholds were compared before induction (week 0) and week 2 to 8 after induction (SI Appendix, Fig. S1 C–E), which revealed the onset of HL at week 4 postinduction. In contrast, no significant threshold shifts were observed at week 2, indicating that HL in IMQ-induced SLE model mice begins in the midprogression of the disease. Importantly, this timing coincided with the onset of other SLE manifestations, such as increased serum anti-dsDNA antibody levels and proteinuria, which also emerged at week 4 based on our data ( ) and the previous study ( ). This temporal correlation suggests that HL is associated with the systemic progression of SLE.
Additionally, we also examined the auditory functions of MRL/lpr mice, a popular spontaneous SLE mouse model. We totally established and evaluated two batches of MRL/lpr mice. The results of ABR and DPOAE tests demonstrate that MRL/lpr mice in Batch 1 develop significant HL at age of 20 wk (SI Appendix, Fig. S2 A–C), similar to the imiquimod-induced model. To further verify these findings, we acquired another batch of MRL/lpr mice and evaluated their auditory functions. However, the second batch of MRL/lpr mice aged 21 wk failed to exhibit significant HL (SI Appendix, Fig. S2 D–E), indicating discrepant results and unreliability of this spontaneous SLE model for HL studies. According to the literature, the hearing of the MRL/lpr mice did not always deteriorate ( ). Given the inherent limitations of the MRL/lpr model, we suggest its inadequacy as a robust model for immune-mediated sensorineural HL based on current evidence. Therefore, the IMQ-induced SLE mouse model was selected for further study.

## Single-Cell RNA Sequencing Reveals Capillary Endothelial Cell (CEC) Abnormalities in SV of the IMQ-Induced SLE Mice.
To assess hair cell damage in the cochleae of IMQ-induced SLE mice, we performed immunofluorescence and analyzed OHC densities at cochlear apical, middle, and basal turns (SI Appendix, Fig. S3A). Densities of OHCs were normal in the IMQ-induced SLE mice (SI Appendix, Fig. S3B), suggesting that hair cell damage did not play a major role in SLE-related HL. Additionally, we examined the histological morphology of SV by H&E staining and did not observe SV atrophy in the IMQ-induced SLE mice (SI Appendix, Fig. S3 C and D).
To explore the pathogenic mechanism of SLE-related HL, we performed single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) on cochlear samples from SLE and control mice at week 4 postinduction. Unbiased clustering by uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) analysis identified 16 distinct cell clusters based on their gene expression profiles ( ). Using established cell markers, we classified these cell clusters into eight major cochlear cell types and seven immune cell types ( ). The cochlear cells included hair cells (HC, Osbpl6+, Slc5a3+) ( , ), supporting cells (SC, Sox2+) ( , ), spiral ganglion neuron (SGN, Nefh+)( , ), Schwann cells (Schwann, Pmp22+) ( ), capillary endothelial cell (CEC, Vwf+, Ly6c1+, and Cd34+) ( ), intermediate cell (IC, Dct+ and Met+) ( ), marginal cell (MC, Kcne1+) ( , ) and perivascular-resident macrophage-like melanocytes (PVM/M, Adgre1+, Mrc1+, and Cd68+) ( , ). The immune cells were classified into 7 types according to previous study ( , ), including B cells (B, Cd79a+ and Cd19+), T cells (T, Cd3e+, Cd3g+, and Cd4+), cytotoxic T lymphocytes/natural killer cells (CTL/NK, Cd8a+, Nkg7+, Klrd1+, and Prf1+), macrophages (Mac, Cd68+), monocytes (Mono, Lst1+), mast cells (Mast, Kit+), and granulocytes (Gra, Ly6g+).
 By analyzing the proportion of each cell type in different samples, we observed a distinct reduction in the proportion of CECs in the cochleae of SLE mice compared to controls ( ). This reduction suggests that CEC damage or loss may play a key role in SLE-induced HL.
To explore the intercellular signaling pathways between these cell types, we performed a cell–cell communication analysis using CellChat algorithms. By analyzing the number and strength of differential interactions, we found that the intercellular communications between CECs became weakened ( ). Particularly, the cell–cell junction–related signaling pathways, such as VE-cadherin and JAM signaling, were downregulated in the SLE samples compared to controls ( ). These findings suggest that the tight and adhesion junctions of CECs are disrupted in SLE.
We further examined the expression of critical tight and adhesion junction molecules in CECs and found that levels of VE-cadherin, occludin, ZO-2, JAM-2, and JAM-3 were significantly decreased in SLE samples ( ). This loss of junction proteins indicates reduced cohesion between CECs and may contribute to increased capillary permeability and leakage in the SV, providing a potential mechanism for the HL observed in IMQ-induced SLE model mice.

## BLB Function and Structure Are Impaired in IMQ-Induced SLE Mice.
BLB is a specialized structure in the SV, formed by endothelial cells, pericytes, and PVM/M ( ). This barrier separates the inner ear fluid compartments from bloodstream and plays a crucial role in maintaining the electrochemical composition of the endolymph ( ). To assess the permeability of BLB, we applied several tracers of different molecular weights via intravenous injections. We first injected Evans blue, a nonfluorescent tracer with a molecular weight of 961 Da, and evaluated its presence in the cochlea according to a previously established method ( ). Accumulation of Evans blue was observed in the cochlear lateral wall of the IMQ-induced SLE mice, but not of the control mice ( ). Next, we employed two fluorescent tracers, FITC-dextran (40 KDa) and FITC-IgG (150 KDa), to further evaluate capillary leakage in the cochlea. Immunofluorescent staining revealed substantial leakage of both FITC-dextran and FITC-IgG from the capillaries of IMQ-induced SLE mice but not control mice ( ). Quantifications of FITC-dextran fluorescence ( ) and FITC-IgG fluorescence ( ) confirmed significant tracer leakage in the SLE cochleae, further supporting the disruption of BLB in IMQ-induced SLE mice.
 Previous studies indicated that immune complex deposition played an important role in pathophysiology of inner ear involvement in SLE ( , ). To detect potential IgG and complement depositions in the cochlea, we conducted immunofluorescence assays using specific anti-IgG and anti-C3/C4 antibodies. We observed mild IgG depositions at the cochlear modiolus, while C3 or C4 complement depositions were not detected (SI Appendix, Fig. S4). These IgG molecules may enter cochlear modiolus and form depositions due to the capillary leakage, further supporting the disruption of BLB in IMQ-induced SLE mice. To investigate the molecular basis of this barrier disruption, we examined the expression levels of key junction proteins in the SV tissues by western blot (WB) analysis. Tight junction proteins (occludin, ZO-2, JAM-1, JAM-2, and JAM-3) and the adhesion junction protein VE-cadherin were significantly reduced in the SLE cochleae compared to controls ( ), supporting the findings from the scRNA-seq analysis. Thus, the loss of these crucial junction proteins may have compromised the integrity of BLB, leading to increased capillary leakage. Our results confirm that the BLB is severely impaired in the IMQ-induced SLE mice, with both structural and functional defects contributing to HL.

## Immune-Mediated Cytotoxicity Contributes to BLB Damage.
To investigate the underlying mechanism of BLB damage in IMQ-induced SLE mice, we analyzed the differential expression genes (DEGs) in CECs between SLE and control mice identified by the scRNA-seq. KEGG enrichment of these DEGs revealed that pathways related to leukocyte transendothelial migration, natural killer cell-mediated cytotoxicity and cell adhesion molecules were upregulated in the CECs of SLE mice ( ). Furthermore, gene set variation analysis (GSVA) also revealed that pathways of cell adhesion molecules, natural killer cell-mediated cytotoxicity, and apoptosis were significantly upregulated in the SLE mice ( ). These results suggest that migration, adhesion, and cytotoxicity of immune cells are major contributors to BLB damage, with apoptosis as a likely cause for the reduced numbers of CECs in SLE mice. In addition, GSVA identified increased proliferation and activation of T cells and NK cells in SLE mice ( ), further supporting the pivotal role of immune-mediated cytotoxicity in BLB damage.
 To extend our scRNA-seq findings, we also performed bulk-RNA sequencing analyses on the control and SLE cochleae (SI Appendix, Fig. S5A). After quality control and differential analysis, 143 DEGs were identified (SI Appendix, Fig. S5 B and C). KEGG enrichment analysis of these DEGs also revealed enrichment of pathways related to the cell adhesion molecules, NK cell-mediated cytotoxicity, and leukocyte transendothelial migration in SLE cochleae (SI Appendix, Fig. S5D), consistent with the scRNA-seq results. Reactome annotations further highlighted the roles of immune system in the pathogenesis of BLB damage with DEGs enriched in pathways related to the cell surface interactions at vascular wall (SI Appendix, Fig. S5 E and F), suggesting the potential crosstalk between immune cells and CECs.
To refine our analysis, we employed BayesPrism, a Bayesian method for cell type and gene expression deconvolution, using scRNA-seq as prior information to predict cell proportions from bulk RNA-seq data ( ). This analysis confirmed that the proportion of CECs in the SLE cochleae was significantly lower than that in the control cochleae (SI Appendix, Fig. S6 A and B), consistent with the scRNA-seq data. This abnormal reduction in CECs in IMQ-induced SLE mice further supports the role of immune-mediated cytotoxicity in BLB damage.
To further verify the role of CTLs/NK cells in BLB damage, we examined existence and infiltration of these immune cells in the SV by immunohistochemistry. CTLs (marked by CD8a) and NK cells (marked by NKp46) notably accumulated in the SV of IMQ-induced SLE mice ( ). The abnormal accumulation of CTLs/NK cells suggested that these immune cells may have mediated BLB damage in SLE.

## Granzymes From CTLs/NK Cells Mediate Apoptosis of CECs in the BLB.
Given the significant role of CTLs/NK cells in BLB damage, we next investigated the top 25 DEGs of CTLs/NK cells and CECs between SLE and control cochleae ( and SI Appendix, Fig. S7A). Using DEGs with a log fold change (logFC) > 0.75, we constructed a protein–protein association network based on the STRING database ( ) and identified the hub genes using maximal clique centrality (MCC), a network scoring method in Cytoscape ( ). The analysis revealed that Ifng (IFN-γ), Ccl-5, Gzmb, Nkg7, and Ccl-4 were the top 5 hub genes. Among these, Ifng was known to play essential roles in the pathogenesis of SLE ( ). Ccl-5 and Ccl-4 are involved in the chemotaxis of immune cells ( , ), while Nkg7 mediates the cytotoxic degranulation of NK cells and cytolytic activity of target cells ( ). Gzmb, encoding granzyme B, a cytotoxic serine protease secreted by CTLs and NK cells, was identified as a key gene driving apoptosis in target cells ( ). The presence of Gzmb as a hub gene in CTLs/NK cells from SLE cochleae suggests that it acts as the primary executor of BLB damage.
 To further explore interactions between CTLs/NK cells and CECs, we performed a cell communication analysis using CellChat. In SLE cochleae, 16 interactions were identified between CTLs/NK cells (as source cells) and CECs (as target cells) ( ). The most likely interactions (with a probability > 0.2) included Ccl5-Ccr1, Thy1-(Itgam+Itgb2), Gzma-Pard3, and Cd6-Alcam (SI Appendix, Fig. S7 B–E). These interactions are involved in communication, adhesion, migration, and activation of the immune cells. Notably, Gzma (granzyme A), another serine protease in cytotoxic granules ( ), also emerged as a potential executor of CEC apoptosis ( ).
We further examined the expression levels of Gzma and Gzmb in CTLs/NK cells from SLE and control samples in the scRNA-seq data. Both Gzma and Gzmb were significantly upregulated in the SLE samples ( ). Importantly, the expression levels of proapototic gene Bax and antiapoptotic gene Bcl-2 were upregulated and downregulated in CECs respectively, suggesting elevated apoptosis in CECs from the SLE samples ( ). Protein expression of these genes in SV tissues were then verified by WB. Both Gzma and Gzmb protein levels were increased in the SLE samples compared to the controls ( ). Consistently, we observed increased expression of proapoptotic protein Bax and decreased levels of antiapoptotic Bcl-2 in SLE samples ( ). WB analysis of Caspase-3, a downstream effector of Gzmb-mediated apoptosis, showed increased levels of cleaved Caspase-3 (p17 and p12) and decreased levels of pro-Caspase-3 in the SLE samples ( ).
To quantitatively analyze the death of CECs, we performed flow cytometry with the TUNEL assay to detect dead CECs in SV. The proportion of dead CECs was significantly higher in SLE samples ( ), providing strong evidence that death of CECs in the SV is a key contributor to BLB damage in IMQ-induced SLE animals.

## Inhibition of Gzmb Rescues HL in the IMQ-Induced SLE Mice.
Gzma and Gzmb are the most abundant members in the granzyme family, and both exhibit potent cytotoxic activity ( ). In our study, both Gzma and Gzmb were identified as potential mediators of CEC apoptosis in the BLB. However, it is unclear which granzyme plays a predominant role in this process. To address this issue, we administered specific inhibitors to the IMQ-induced SLE mice, including Serpinb6b for Gzma ( ) and Serpinb9 for Gzmb ( ) ( ). The IMQ-induced SLE mice were divided into four groups: control, SLE, Serpinb6b-treated, and Serpinb9-treated. To ensure the inhibitors reached the BLB, we first detected their serum levels by ELISA after intravenous injections. The serum concentration of inhibitors in Serpinb6b/Serpinb9-treated mice was significantly higher than control mice (SI Appendix, Fig. S8 A and B). We further confirmed that the amount of inhibitors present in the SV tissues in Serpinb6b/Serpinb9-treated mice was much more than that in control mice through Western blot (SI Appendix, Fig. S8 C–F). Auditory functions of these mice were examined at 4 wk postinduction. IMQ-induced SLE mice treated with Serpinb9, but not Serpinb6b, showed significant reductions in both ABR and DPOAE thresholds at 12 to 24 kHz, indicative of improved hearing ( ). These results suggest that Gzmb, rather than Gzma, is the key granzyme responsible for the pathogenesis of SLE-related HL.
 To further investigate the effect of Serpinb9 on apoptosis of CECs in BLB, we examine the protein level of Bax, Bcl-2, and Caspase-3 in the SV tissue samples. In the Serpinb9-treated SLE samples, apoptosis was significantly ameliorated compared to the SLE samples, as evidenced by the increased level of Bcl-2 and decreased level of Bax and cleaved Caspase-3 ( ). These effects were not observed in the Serpinb6b-treated SLE samples ( and SI Appendix, Fig. S9), consistent with the lack of hearing rescue by Serpinb6b treatment ( ).
Additionally, we examined the death of CECs in Serpinb9-treated SLE samples, using CD31 and TUNEL double labeling in flow cytometry. The proportion of dead CECs was significantly reduced in the Serpinb9-treated SLE samples compared to the untreated SLE samples ( ). Overall, these data suggest that Gzmb is the dominant executor of CEC death, playing a critical role in the pathogenesis of SLE-related HL. Therefore, inhibiting Gzmb may present a promising strategy for hearing restoration in the IMQ-induced SLE mice and patients.

## Discussion
This study provides evidence of auditory dysfunction in an induced SLE mouse model, exhibiting HL characteristics that closely align with those observed in SLE patients. Our findings reveal that BLB damage, mediated by cytotoxic lymphocytes through granzyme B release, is the primary mechanism underlying HL in this model. Importantly, inhibiting granzyme B effectively rescues HL in IMQ-induced SLE mice, suggesting a therapeutic target for autoimmune-related HL. These results provide insights into SLE-related HL pathogenesis and propose a potential therapeutic strategy with broader implications for autoimmune disorders.
Auditory dysfunction can be mediated by immune mechanisms, and the association between autoimmune conditions and HL has been explored since 1958 ( ). Auditory involvement in autoimmune disorders, including SLE, has been widely reported ( ). However, experimental animal models have been insufficient for an extended period. We thoroughly analyzed the characteristics of the auditory functions in the induced SLE mouse model and revealed striking similarities in auditory phenotypes between our model and the patients with SLE. Specifically, SNHL, the most common type in patients with SLE ( , , ), was observed in our model mice. The middle and high frequencies (above 4,000 Hz) are most commonly affected in SLE patients ( , , ). Our IMQ-induced SLE mouse model also exhibited similar susceptibility to middle and high frequency HL, suggesting that autoimmunity primarily affected these frequency ranges. Our study also provides insight into the progression of SLE-related HL. While most previous studies involved patients already diagnosed with SLE, leaving the onset of HL unclear, our model reveals that HL emerges during the midprogression of SLE in the mouse model, suggesting HL might be an underrecognized manifestation of SLE progression.
Research on the pathogenesis of HL in SLE remains limited. Previous clinical studies have linked HL in patients with SLE to high systemic lupus international collaborating clinics/ACR damage index (SDI) scores and secondary Sjogren’s syndrome, but not to the SLE Disease Activity Index score ( , ). This indicates that HL is related to cochlear damage rather than overall SLE disease activity. Most findings of pathological changes rely on human samples, such as plasma and temporal bone specimens. Degeneration of the cochlear SV, hair cell and SGN has been reported in patients with SLE ( , ). It is hypothesized that immune complex deposition, direct antibody/antigen reactions, and cytotoxicity may be the underlying mechanisms of SLE-related HL ( ), though experimental evidence is still lacking. Our study demonstrates that SLE-related HL is not attributable to significant hair cell loss or evident degeneration of SV and SGNs. The BLB, comprising CECs, pericytes, PVM/Ms, junctional structures, and basement membrane in SV, forms a barrier structure that regulates exchange of substances between the bloodstream and the cochlea ( ). Dysfunction of BLB is associated with various types of HL, including autoimmune-related HL ( , , ). In the IMQ-induced SLE mouse model, we observed reduced CECs and junction molecules, indicating that BLB disruption plays a major role in the HL. Interestingly, the blood–brain barrier (BBB), a similar structure to BLB, is often compromised in SLE ( ) leading to cognitive impairment in SLE patients ( , ). Mechanistically, multiple effector cells migrate from peripheral circulation to the brain via disrupted BBB due to abnormal activated immune system ( ). Therefore, the BLB disruption is the key pathological feature in IMQ-induced SLE model mice with HL. The leakage of BLB could underlie HL through increase immune cell infiltration and cytotoxicity.
Although HL occurs in approximately 27% of patients with SLE globally ( , ), the underlying pathogenic mechanisms of SLE-related HL are previously unknown. A key finding of our study is that CEC damage, mediated by CD8+ CTLs and NK cells, is the primary mechanism for BLB damage in SLE. CEC, a critical component of BLB, contributes to the integrity and normal permeability of BLB. Endothelial dysfunction or damage is common in patients with SLE ( , ). We found a reduced proportion of CECs in cochlea of IMQ-induced SLE mice, along with increased cell death of CECs, supporting their roles in BLB disruption. Several immune pathways are implicated in vascular damage in SLE, including type I interferons (IFN-I), neutrophils and neutrophil extracellular traps, complement, adaptive immunity, metabolic dysfunction, and immune drivers of systemic hypertension ( ). To thoroughly identify potential cross-talks between CTL/NK cells and parenchymal cells, we further performed cell–cell communication analysis based on our single-cell RNA-seq data. Our analysis demonstrated that CECs in SLE group exhibited upregulated surface expression of multiple receptors and glycoproteins (SI Appendix, Fig. S10), involved in immune cell adhesion, cell–cell interactions, and migration.
Our data highlight CD8+ CTLs and NK cells as critical players in of CEC degeneration, a process driven by the secretion of granzymes. CD8+ CTLs and NK cells are known as cytotoxic immune cells and play an important role in immune clearance through killing target cells ( , ). Intriguingly, a recent study using single-cell transcriptome sequencing analysis reported heterogenous and novel populations of activated cytotoxic CD8+ T cells and NK cells in peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) from patients with SLE ( ), highlighting the roles of CTLs and NK cells in SLE pathogenesis, including HL. Recent studies have confirmed that CD8+ T cells are the predominant infiltrating cell type in renal tissues of lupus nephritis and mediate rapid progression of glomerulonephritis by damaging glomerular podocytes ( , ). Additionally, single-cell RNA sequencing reveals the elevated expression of cytotoxic markers with low levels of exhaustion markers in infiltrating CD8+ T cells, indicating that the CD8+ T cell exhaustion observed in peripheral blood does not extend to SLE-affected tissues ( ). Therefore, these findings suggest the pivotal role of infiltrating CD8+ T cells in the mechanism of tissue damage in SLE, consistent with our findings. Moreover, the cochlear cells are not in direct contact with circulating immune cells due to the presence and integrity of BLB under physiological conditions. Therefore, the CD8+ T cells infiltrating the SV are likely polyclonal T cells recruited by nonspecific inflammation, rather than antigen-specific (monoclonal) T cells targeting cochlear cell antigens, a hypothesis remains to be further addressed.
Through bioinformatic and functional analyses, we revealed that granzymes (Gzma and Gzmb) secreted by CTLs/NK cells induced apoptosis of CECs. Granzymes are the key executors to induce cell death when cytotoxic lymphocytes identify and conjugate to target cells ( ). The granzyme family includes five members in the human and ten in the mouse ( ). Among these, Gzma and Gzmb are the most abundant granzymes and expressed by NK cells, cytotoxic T cells, and some regulatory T cells (Tregs) ( ). Gzmb is the most powerful proapoptotic granzyme and induces rapid and effective apoptosis of target cells through cleaving caspase-3 in the mouse ( ). In contrast, Gzma induces a slower cell death than Gzmb through a novel caspase-independent mechanism termed athetosis, which depends on an intact actin cytoskeleton and presents a writhing morphology of the dying cell ( ). While both Gzma and Gzmb were upregulated in the SV of IMQ-induced SLE mice, only granzyme B inhibition by Serpinb9 effectively prevented HL. This finding is particularly significant given that current treatments for SLE-related HL. Previous studies reported that hydroxychloroquine, the standard treatment for SLE, neither causes ototoxicity nor rescues HL ( , ), while steroid therapy shows limited benefits and substantial side effects ( ). Our findings demonstrate that Gzmb is a critical target for intervention and suggest a promising therapeutic strategy for autoimmune-related HL. In conclusion, this study reveals that HL in IMQ-induced SLE model mice is primarily caused by BLB damage, mediated by CTLs and NK cells through Gzmb-induced death of CECs. Inhibiting Gzmb effectively alleviates HL, highlighting it as a potential therapeutic target for autoimmune-related auditory dysfunction (SI Appendix, Fig. S11). These findings not only enhance our understanding of the pathogenesis of autoimmune-related HL but also suggest a unique approach for preserving hearing in SLE patients, with potential implications for other autoimmune disorders affecting sensory functions.

## Materials and Methods

## Animals.
Wild-type female C57BL/6 J mice were used to establish an induced-SLE mouse model. All procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Nanjing Drum Tower Hospital.

## Auditory Tests.
For ABR measurements, the threshold was defined as the lowest intensity at which reproducible waves were visually identifiable. Recordings were performed on a Tucker-Davis Technologies System (TDT) workstation running the BioSigRZ software. Distortion product otoacoustic emissions (DPOAEs) were measured in response to two primary tones marked as f1 and f2. DPOAE thresholds were defined as the f1 level capable to evoke a response at −10 dB SPL. DPOAE recordings were conducted using EPL Cochlear Function Test Suite software (Mass Eye and Ear, Boston, MA).

## Serpingb6b and Serpbinb9 Administration.
We used Serpinb6b (Sino Biological, China) to inhibit Gzma and Serpinb9 (Sino Biological, China) to inhibit Gzmb. Each mouse was treated with 125 μg/kg Serpinb6b or Serpinb9 through intravenous injection. The inhibitor was administered once every three days accompanied by SLE modeling. See SI Materials and Methods for more detailed information.

## Statistical Analysis.
Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism (Version 9.0). Data were presented as mean ± SD. Differences between two groups were evaluated by the Mann–Whitney or Student’s t test. One-way ANOVA was used to evaluate the statistical significance of one factor among three or more groups followed by the Kruskal–Wallis test or Tukey’s post hoc test. Statistical analysis for two factors among multiple groups was performed using two-way ANOVA. P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.
For full methodological details, see SI Appendix, Materials and Methods.
