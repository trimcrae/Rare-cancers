## Abstract
Migrasomes are newly identified extracellular organelles that form on the retraction fibers of migrating cells. Characterized by their pomegranate-like, membrane-bound structure, they encapsulate diverse molecular cargo, including proteins, lipids, RNA, and even damaged mitochondria. The biogenesis of migrasomes is a highly regulated, multi-stage process that depends on cell migration and requires an interplay between specific molecules and membrane mechanical stresses. Functionally, migrasomes play pivotal roles in intercellular communication by mediating the spatial transfer of signals and components. This review provides an overview of migrasome biology, covering their biogenesis mechanisms, distinctions from other types of vesicles, and their dual roles in physiology and pathology. Furthermore, we discuss the potential of migrasomes in disease diagnosis and therapeutic applications.
Keywords: Migrasomes, Extracellular vesicles, Disease, Biomarker

## Background
Intercellular communication governs physiological and pathological processes in multicellular organisms, mediated through direct cell-cell contact and indirect mediators. Extracellular vesicles (EVs) were described initially as cellular debris but are now established as important vehicles in cell-cell communication. These vesicles comprise small EVs and large EVs, with these subtypes characterized by distinct biogenesis pathways and functions. Cell migration is fundamental to multicellular systems, driving processes including embryonic development, wound healing, immune responses, and cancer metastasis [ ]. During this process, cells leave behind distinct traces known as retraction fibers (RFs) [ ]. Although observed since the mid-20th century, the functional role of RFs remained unknown until the landmark discovery of migrasomes in 2015 [ ]. Migrasomes, a novel class of extracellular organelles, are large, pomegranate-like vesicular structures (PLS) that form on RFs during cell migration. The unique biogenesis of migrasomes depends on cell migration and is regulated by a set of specific molecular factors, including tetraspanins, integrins, Rab35, and phosphatidylinositol (4,5)-bisphosphate [PI(4,5)P₂] [ , ]. Once matured, they are released into the extracellular space through the rupture of RFs, subsequently releasing their contents to mediate intercellular communication; this process is termed migracytosis [ ]. They facilitate targeted signal delivery, lateral mRNA transfer, mitochondrial quality control, and immunomodulation mediated by chemokines and cytokines [ – ]. Although growing evidence implicates migrasomes in disease pathogenesis, key challenges remain in elucidating the mechanisms behind their cargo sorting, spatiotemporal regulation, and physiological-pathological functions. This review synthesizes current knowledge on migrasome biogenesis, composition, and multifunctional roles, and highlights their emerging potential as diagnostic biomarkers and therapeutic targets.

## History of the discovery of migrasomes
The discovery of migrasomes unfolded over several decades through cumulative observations. As early as 1945, Porter et al. observed filamentous structures, described as “jagged points,” left behind by migrating cells [ ]. Later, in 1963, Taylor and Robbins further coined the term “retraction fibrils” to describe retraction-associated filamentous structures [ ]. A significant advancement came in 2012, when Li Yu’s group discovered that migrating cells form PLS at the junctions or tips of RFs. Subsequent studies confirmed that PLS formation was dependent on cell migration. Building upon this foundation, in 2014, Liang Ma et al. defined these pomegranate-like structures as “migrasomes” and identified the migration-dependent cellular contents release mechanism as “migracytosis” [ ]. This nomenclature has since been widely adopted, establishing migrasomes as a distinct class of migration-dependent extracellular vesicular organelles.
Following the identification of migrasomes, attention shifted toward understanding how they form and what roles they play in cellular processes. In 2017, Danni Wu et al. discovered that migrasome formation is determined by specific integrin- extracellular matrix (ECM) pairings [ ]. Subsequently, in 2019, Xiaoxin Zhao et al. identified four migrasome-specific protein markers bifunctional heparan sulfate N-deacetylase/N-sulfotransferase 1 (NDST1), phosphatidylinositol glycan anchor biosynthesis, class K (PIGK), carboxypeptidase Q (CPQ), and EGF domain-specific O-linked N-acetylglucosaminetransferase (EOGT), and successfully detected migrasomes in human serum samples for the first time [ ]. A set of migrasome markers applicable to clinical samples was established in this study, providing valuable tools for future studies on migrasome-related diseases. Concurrently, Lilian Chen et al. identified fluorescently labeled wheat germ agglutinin (WGA) as a rapid, versatile probe for migrasome detection in living and fixed cells [ ], which is now widely recognized as a convenient and reliable approach for migrasome detection. Moreover, Yuwei Huang et al. demonstrated that migrasome formation is driven by increased membrane stiffness in tetraspanin- and cholesterol-enriched macrodomains (TEMAs) [ ]. Advancing the field in 2021, Maciej Gagat et al. discovered that TNFα induces F-actin reorganization into parallel stress fibers, facilitating directed migration and mediating migrasome formation and migracytosis through discontinuous cell-cell contacts and enhanced adhesion [ ]. In the same year, Shogo Saito et al. showed that peptide-modified substrates with the peptide interface comprising cell-penetrating peptides (pVEC and R9) and virus fusion peptide (SIV) boost cell migration and migrasome formation more effectively than fibronectin or bare substrates [ ]. Further expanding the functional scope in 2022, Mengdie Wang et al. found that PD-L1 concentrates at the rear of migrating carcinoma cells, facilitating retraction and leading to the formation of PD-L1-containing RFs and migrasomes [ ]. Additionally, Changyuan Fan et al. identified cell migration persistence and speed as key factors regulating migrasome formation via RFs, with vimentin as a critical modulator [ ].
In 2023, a series of mechanistic studies significantly deepened understanding of this process. Yongbin Ma et al. found that migrasome-derived nanoparticles (MDNPs) express migrasome markers but not EV markers and carry unique miRNAs, revealing a novel intercellular communication mechanism [ ]. Simultaneously, Tianlun Ding et al. discovered that phosphatidylinositol 4-phosphate 5-kinase 1 A (PIP5K1A)-mediated synthesis of PI(4,5)P₂ recruits Rab35 to regulate integrin-dependent migrasome formation [ ]. Meanwhile, Haisha Liang et al. demonstrated that migrasome formation is initiated by the assembly of sphingomyelin synthase 2 (SMS2) foci at the leading edge of migrating cells [ ], while Raviv Dharan et al. revealed that Tetraspanin 4 (TSPAN4) stabilizes membrane swellings and facilitates their maturation into migrasomes [ ]. Most recently, in 2024, Yiyang Han et al. discovered that calcium ions promote migrasome formation through Synaptotagmin-1 (Syt1), which initiates unstable membrane swellings that are subsequently stabilized by tetraspanin recruitment [ ]. In parallel, Yong Li et al. revealed that Rab10-Caveolin-1 (CAV1) vesicles transport the colony-stimulating factor-1 (CSF-1) to migrasomes via the motor protein Myosin Va and adaptor proteins RILPL2 for monocyte differentiation, regulated by the kinase LRRK2 phosphorylation [ ]. In 2025, Ben Zucker et al. revealed that migrasome formation initiates at tubular junctions via membrane tension-driven bulging [ ]. These studies have advanced understanding of migrasome biology by elucidating key signaling molecules, cytoskeletal dynamics, and regulatory factors, providing a foundation for exploring their potential clinical significance.
Concurrently, research methodologies for migrasomes have undergone significant technological advancements. In 2023, Guoxun Zhang et al. developed deep self-supervised learning enhanced microscope (DeepSeMi), a deep learning-based microscopic denoising method that enables half-day migrasome imaging [ ]. This breakthrough allowed researchers to study migrasome formation, mitochondrial release, and interactions with retractosomes during cell migration in unprecedented detail. Building on this progress, Jie Cui et al. introduced TTCPy in 2025, an aggregation-induced emission (AIE)-active near-infrared probe specifically designed for high-contrast migrasome labeling and super-resolution imaging [ ]. Simultaneously, Zhi Lu et al. presented a novel imaging technique called confocal scanning light-field microscopy (csLFM), which achieves higher spatiotemporal resolution with significantly reduced phototoxicity. This advancement enables long-term observation of dynamic migrasome processes that were previously challenging to capture [ ]. Recently, Kongqi Chen et al. developed a novel mechanosensitive fluorescent probe, OT2SQ, which enables real-time monitoring of migrasome morphology, spatiotemporal distribution, and membrane tension dynamics, allowing investigation of the dynamic mechanical properties of migrasomes [ ].
Since their discovery, migrasome research has advanced rapidly, yet key controversies regarding in vivo validation, methodology, and fundamental biological functions persist. These controversies have driven further research into the roles of migrasomes in disease. Accumulating evidence indicates that migrasomes are not merely passive byproducts of cell migration but play active roles in diverse physiological and pathological processes. They have been implicated in intercellular communication by facilitating the transfer of signaling molecules and proteins between cells. In embryonic development, particularly in zebrafish models, migrasomes function as chemoattractant sources to guide the migration and positioning of dorsal forerunner cells (DFCs), thereby ensuring proper organ morphogenesis [ ]. In maintaining homeostasis, studies reveal that neutrophils utilize migrasomes to expel damaged mitochondria, a process crucial for cellular homeostasis and mitochondrial quality control [ ]. Furthermore, migrasomes are increasingly recognized for their significant involvement in various pathological conditions. Enhanced migrasome formation has been observed in highly metastatic tumor cells, where they are suggested to contribute to tumor microenvironment modulation, metastasis, and potentially drug resistance [ ]. Beyond cancer, emerging research explores their functions in cardiovascular diseases [ ], wound healing [ ], and inflammatory disorders [ ].
In summary, studies of migrasomes have progressed from initial observation to deep mechanistic understanding. They have been known as active organelles with important roles in development, homeostasis, and disease progression. Novel imaging and analytical technologies have significantly enhanced the capacity to visualize and investigate them. Future work will focus on their exact functions and their potential as targets for new therapies. ## Biogenesis of migrasomes
Migrasome formation is a tightly regulated, multistage process comprising nucleation, maturation, and expansion, driven by specific lipid-protein interactions (Fig. ). In the early phase, migrasome development relies heavily on sphingomyelin (SM) production, a process driven by SMS2 at the migrasome formation sites (MFSs) [ ]. During cellular movement, SMS2 preferentially concentrates at the leading end of the cell and is then retained and moves into RFs. By converting ceramide into SM, SMS2 thereby drives the growth of migrasomes. Consequently, the early aggregation of SMS2 foci serves as a nucleation event, representing the foundational step in migrasome generation. Once nucleation is initiated, PIP5K1A localizes to MFSs and catalyzes the conversion of phosphatidylinositol 4-phosphate (PI4P) to PI(4,5)P₂ [ ]. This newly synthesized signaling lipid then recruits and interacts with Rab35, which further recruits integrin. Through specific anchorage to ECM, integrins establish stable adhesive structures that provide essential structural stability for migrasomes [ ].
 Integrin recruitment marks the maturation of migrasome formation sites, triggering subsequent accumulation of tetraspanins and cholesterol. This molecular cascade initiates the migrasome expansion phase [ ]. Furthermore, Syt1, a calcium-sensing protein enriched in migrasomes, initiates their biogenesis through calcium binding [ ]. At MFSs, Syt1 triggers these sites to swell into unstable precursors, which are subsequently stabilized by tetraspanin accumulation—thus providing the structural basis for migrasome formation. As the expansion phase proceeds, membrane tension and molecular clustering work in synergy to drive structural maturation. Driven by membrane tension, tetraspanin molecules progressively cluster at these expanding membrane protrusions [ ]. Over time, these structures develop into micrometer-scale tetraspanin-enriched microdomains (TEMs) through continuous accumulation of tetraspanin proteins [ ]. TEMs further coalesce into TEMAs via cooperative clustering of tetraspanins and cholesterol; subsequently, membrane tension-mediated expansion of TEMAs drives their transformation into mature migrasomes, while increased bending rigidity confers mechanical stability to these spherical structures.
In the terminal phase, as sustained cell migration proceeds, RFs progressively fragment, resulting in either the release of associated migrasomes into the extracellular compartment or their internalization by recipient cells proximal to the release site via endocytosis. Through these distinct pathways, migrasomes deliver bioactive cargo via either discharge into the local microenvironment or direct intercellular transfer, thereby fulfilling their essential role as intercellular communication mediators. Taken together, the formation of migrasomes represents a precisely regulated biological process.

## Distinctions between migrasomes and other EVs
EVs represent a class of membrane-enclosed nanostructures that are actively released by nearly all cellular organisms spanning from prokaryotic bacteria to complex multicellular eukaryotes [ ]. These vesicular structures serve as fundamental mediators of intercellular signaling pathways, operating during routine physiological functions as well as under various disease conditions. The EV population demonstrates significant diversity across multiple parameters, including their formation pathways, dimensional attributes with sizes varying between 30 nanometers and several micrometers, and specialized functional capabilities. These vesicles function as sophisticated transport systems for an array of biologically active components such as proteins, various forms of nucleic acids, and lipid molecules. Through these molecular cargos, EVs participate in the regulation of cellular equilibrium, facilitate disease development processes, and hold substantial potential for diagnostic applications. Based on their size, EVs can be broadly classified into two categories: small EVs and large EVs. The small EV population primarily comprises exomeres (< 50 nm) [ ], exosomes (30–150 nm) [ ], and microvesicles (100–1000 nm) [ ]. In contrast, large EVs mainly consist of migrasomes (0.5–3 μm) [ ], apoptotic bodies (1–5 μm) [ ], and large oncosomes (LO, 1–10 μm) [ ]. This size-based classification reflects their distinct biogenetic origins and biological roles in intercellular communication (Fig. ).
 

## Exomeres
EVs constitute a heterogeneous population of membrane-bound and non-membranous nanoparticles released by nearly all cell types, from bacteria to plants and mammals. Among these subsets, exomeres represent a recently identified class of non-membranous nanoparticles that play a crucial role in intercellular communication. Exomeres are distinct from other EVs owing to their extremely small size (< 50 nm) and the absence of a lipid bilayer membrane [ ]. Despite the absence of an external membrane, exomeres are highly abundant and carry a distinctive set of proteins that modulate recipient cell behavior [ ].
Proteomic analyses have revealed that exomeres are enriched in metabolic regulators—including methionine adenosyltransferase 1 A (MAT1A), isocitrate dehydrogenase 1 (IDH1), GDP-mannose pyrophosphorylase B (GMPPB), UDP-glucose pyrophosphorylase 2 (UGP2), exostosin glycosyltransferase 1 (EXT1), and phosphofructokinase, liver type (PFKL)—as well as in glycan-processing enzymes such as mannosidase alpha class 2 A member 1 (MAN2A1), hexosaminidase subunit beta (HEXB), and glucosidase II alpha subunit (GANAB) [ ]. These findings suggest that exomeres may facilitate the targeted delivery of cargo to recipient cells via glycan-mediated recognition, thereby modulating glycosylation patterns within those cells. Notably, exomeres are also enriched in metabolic enzymes associated with glycolysis and the mechanistic target of rapamycin complex 1 (mTORC1) signaling pathway, suggesting a potential role in the reprogramming of cellular metabolism within target cells. Given that glycolysis serves as a central energy-producing pathway in both physiological and pathological contexts, including cancer, exomeres may act as metabolic regulators during disease progression. Additionally, exomeres selectively accumulate proteins associated with both coagulation (e.g., Factor VIII and Factor X) and hypoxia, suggesting their potential involvement in thrombotic and oxygen-responsive signaling pathways.
The discovery of exomeres broadens our understanding of EV-mediated communication, particularly with respect to metabolic regulation. Unlike larger EVs, exomeres lack a surrounding membrane, which may enable more efficient cargo transfer or distinct mechanisms of cellular uptake. Their small size may also facilitate deeper tissue penetration, thereby enhancing their functional impact. Further investigations are warranted to elucidate their biogenesis, mechanisms of secretion, and specific roles in both physiological and pathological contexts. Nevertheless, exomeres constitute a novel and intriguing component of intercellular communication, with potential implications for metabolic disorders, cancer, and therapeutic development.

## Exosomes
Exosomes are nano-sized EVs (30–150 nm in diameter) that play a fundamental role in intercellular communication across diverse biological systems. These cup-shaped, membrane-bound vesicles originate from the inward budding of endosomal membranes, forming intraluminal vesicles within multivesicular bodies (MVBs) that are subsequently released into the extracellular space upon fusion with the plasma membrane [ ]. Characterized by a distinct molecular composition, exosomes carry a complex cargo of proteins, diverse lipids, and nucleic acids such as mRNAs, microRNAs, and mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) [ ].
Exosomes exhibit substantial heterogeneity in their molecular contents, with protein and RNA profiles varying depending on the originating cell type and its physiological or pathological state. They are predominantly enriched in small RNAs (< 200 nt) [ , ]. Canonical exosomal markers include tetraspanins (CD9, CD63, CD81), ALG-2-interacting protein X (Alix), tumor susceptibility gene 101 (TSG101), and heat shock proteins (HSP70, HSP90) [ ].
The biological significance of exosomes spans multiple domains, ranging from maintaining cellular homeostasis through the removal of cellular waste to facilitating disease progression in conditions such as cancer, where tumor-derived exosomes promote metastasis by transferring oncogenic factors. Their ability to traverse biological barriers and selectively target specific cell types has positioned exosomes as promising therapeutic delivery vehicles, fueling advances in precision and personalized medicine.
However, challenges remain in their efficient isolation and characterization. Conventional techniques such as ultracentrifugation often compromise yield and purity, prompting the development of more advanced isolation technologies, such as the ultrafast isolation system (EXODUS), to address these limitations [ ]. Ongoing research continues to reveal the complex biology of exosomes. Their dual roles as disease biomarkers and therapeutic agents highlight their growing importance in biomedicine and their potential for clinical translation in diagnosis and therapy.

## Microvesicles
Microvesicles (also termed ectosomes or microparticles) typically range from 100 to 1000 nm in diameter and are larger than exosomes [ ]. Unlike exosomes, which form within MVBs and are released upon fusion with the plasma membrane, microvesicles arise through direct outward budding and fission of the plasma membrane. This distinct biogenesis pathway enables the rapid formation and release of microvesicles into the extracellular space upon stimulation, facilitating efficient intercellular communication. Typical microvesicle markers include gelatinases, β1-integrin, TSG101, arrestin domain-containing protein 1 (ARRDC1), major histocompatibility complex class I (MHC-I), ADP-ribosylation factor 6 (ARF6), vesicle-associated membrane protein 3 (VAMP3), and membrane type 1 matrix metalloproteinase (MT1-MMP). These molecular features indicate that microvesicles participate in biological processes such as inflammation and tumor metastasis. Owing to their rapid release and diverse molecular cargo, including proteins, lipids, and nucleic acids, microvesicles serve as active mediators of intercellular communication in both physiological and pathological contexts. Further studies on their formation and cargo-sorting mechanisms may improve their potential use in disease diagnosis and targeted therapy.

## Migrasomes
Migrasomes are a distinct type of membrane-bound organelle formed during cell migration. First identified in 2015, migrasomes are closely associated with cell motility and exhibit a characteristic pomegranate-like morphology. These structures are typically 0.5–3 μm in diameter and contain multiple smaller vesicles of 50–100 nm within their oval membranes. Unlike other EVs, migrasomes originate at the tips or intersections of RFs behind migrating cells [ ]. At these sites, specific proteins such as TSPAN4, integrins, and cholesterol accumulate, enhancing membrane rigidity [ ]. After detachment from the cell, migrasomes are released into the extracellular space. Once released, they can function as local signaling hubs or rupture to release their vesicular contents.
Biochemically, migrasomes are enriched in specific proteins such as NDST1, EOGT, PIGK, and CPQ [ ]. These proteins are either absent or present at very low levels in exosomes. Mass spectrometry analyses show that migrasomes share only about 27% of their proteins with exosomes, indicating a distinct molecular identity. Exosomes are formed by the inward budding of multivesicular bodies, whereas migrasomes arise through outward membrane expansion along RFs. This process is closely related to cell motility. The unique formation pathway of migrasomes supports their classification as organelles rather than typical EVs, as they perform specialized functions while still connected to the cell before release.
Functionally, migrasomes serve as dynamic mediators of intercellular communication. They concentrate signaling molecules such as cytokines, growth factors, and chemokines to form localized signaling hubs that influence nearby cells [ ]. In zebrafish embryos, migrasomes regulate organ morphogenesis by controlling the spatial distribution of developmental signals [ ]. Migrasomes also play a role in mitochondrial quality control [ ]. They selectively remove damaged mitochondria under cellular stress, which helps maintain cellular homeostasis. In addition, migrasomes mediate lateral cargo transfer [ ]. Cellular components, including RNAs and proteins, are packaged into migrasomes and later released for uptake by neighboring cells. This process enables communication between cells that do not have direct contact. Tetraspanins such as TSPAN4 and cholesterol are known to stabilize the migrasome membrane. However, the mechanisms underlying selective cargo packaging remain unclear. This issue is particularly important in pathological contexts such as cancer metastasis. Further research is needed to clarify the functions of migrasomes in disease and to evaluate their potential as diagnostic and therapeutic targets.
Technically, current methods for isolating and detecting migrasomes require further refinement. Most existing isolation techniques rely on differential centrifugation, which may not fully preserve the structural integrity or purity of migrasomes. More standardized and reliable approaches are needed to accurately quantify migrasomes and to distinguish them from other types of EVs in complex biological samples.
In summary, migrasomes form an important connection between cell migration, organelle biology, and intercellular communication. Their unique formation process, specific molecular composition, and diverse functions in development, homeostasis, and disease underscore their significance as a distinct cellular structure. Future research will further elucidate the mechanisms of cargo sorting and spatial regulation, and explore their potential therapeutic applications, thereby reinforcing their importance in cell biology and biomedicine.

## Retractosomes
Retractosomes are a newly identified subtype of small EVs with diameters ranging from 50 to 250 nm [ ]. They are specifically produced during cell migration through the fragmentation of RFs. When cells migrate away, the RFs break apart and release bead-like vesicles that remain on the ECM, forming structures now referred to as retractosomes. Although retractosomes fall within the size range of small EVs, they display unique biological and compositional properties that distinguish them from other well-characterized EVs such as exosomes and microvesicles. Retractosomes share strong molecular similarities with migrasomes. Their formation depends on the tetraspanin protein TSPAN4, and they can be specifically labeled using WGA. Proteomic analyses have shown that retractosomes contain several migrasome-associated proteins, including PIGK, EOGT, and propionyl-CoA carboxylase alpha chain (PCCA). In contrast, they show very low or undetectable levels of classical small EV markers such as Tsg101 and CD63. Importantly, retractosomes lack cholesterol, which is a major component of migrasomes.
The isolation procedure for retractosomes is similar to that used for migrasomes, with an additional filtration step through 0.22 μm filters to remove larger migrasomes and enrich the smaller retractosome fraction. The precise biological functions of retractosomes are not yet fully understood. Given their origin from RFs, which mediate communication between migrating cells and their environment, it is possible that retractosomes participate in intercellular signaling, matrix remodeling, or other processes related to cell migration.
In summary, retractosomes represent a unique class of migration-derived small EVs. They share some biogenetic and molecular characteristics with migrasomes but also possess distinct structural and compositional features. Their discovery broadens the known diversity of EVs and highlights the complexity of intercellular communication associated with cell migration.

## Apoptotic bodies
Apoptotic bodies are large membrane-bound vesicles, typically 1–5 μm in diameter, that are released by tumor cells and other cell types during apoptosis, a form of programmed cell death [ ]. Their formation marks the terminal phase of apoptosis and involves plasma membrane blebbing, protrusion, and fragmentation. This disassembly process is characterized by nuclear fragmentation, increased membrane permeability, and exposure of phosphatidylserine (PS) on the outer membrane. Apoptotic bodies contain fragmented proteins, lipids, and nucleic acids derived from dying cells and serve as carriers that convey the final molecular messages of apoptotic cells to the surrounding microenvironment. During the early phase of apoptosis, the enhanced permeability of their membranes facilitates the release of bioactive molecules that can influence neighboring cells. These vesicles display characteristic surface markers, including externalized PS and Annexin V, which mediate recognition and uptake by surrounding cells. Functionally, apoptotic bodies promote efficient clearance of cellular debris by phagocytes, preventing the release of toxic intracellular materials and maintaining tissue homeostasis. The fragmentation of dying cells into smaller vesicles increases the surface area available for phagocytic recognition and engulfment. Beyond waste removal, apoptotic bodies also participate in intercellular communication and immune regulation, influencing whether cell death elicits an inflammatory or immunologically silent response.

## Large oncosomes
Large oncosomes (LOs) are a distinct type of tumor-derived EV with diameters of 1–10 μm [ ]. They are enriched with oncogenic molecules and contribute to cancer progression. Unlike EVs that originate from endosomal compartments, LOs are generated directly from the plasma membrane. Their formation is driven by activation of the small GTPase ARF6 or by the loss of the cytoskeletal regulator diaphanous-related formin 3 (DIAPH3), allowing them to package and transfer oncogenic signals within tissues.
These vesicles are abundant in highly aggressive and motile cancer cells, particularly those with an amoeboid phenotype. Their cargo includes metalloproteinases that enhance invasion, RNAs that modulate gene expression, CAV1 involved in signaling and mechanical stability, and ARF6, which regulates vesicle biogenesis and signaling. Through the transfer of these active molecules, LOs reshape the tumor microenvironment, promote metastasis, and facilitate communication between cancer cells. Together, these features identify LOs as important mediators of tumor progression and metastatic spread.

## Biomarkers of migrasomes
Current proteomic and biochemical analyses have identified a distinct set of molecular markers that define migrasomes (Table ). In 2014, Liang Ma et al. identified TSPAN4 as a definitive marker for migrasomes. Using mass spectrometry, they first characterized proteins present in isolated migrasomes and then tagged TSPAN4 with GFP [ ]. Under fluorescence microscopy, TSPAN4 was observed to accumulate on RFs and migrasomes. This localization was confirmed through correlative light and electron microscopy. Using stable TSPAN4–GFP cell lines, they visualized the entire process of migrasome formation, growth, and release in real time, establishing TSPAN4 as a specific and reliable marker for this newly identified organelle. In 2017, Danni Wu et al. demonstrated that integrins regulate the spatial sites of migrasome formation [ ]. They found that integrin α5β1 is highly enriched in its active state on the basal surface of migrasomes, suggesting that it anchors migrasomes to ECM. Migrasome formation depends on the precise pairing between cellular integrins and their corresponding ECM ligands. For example, cells expressing high levels of integrin α5 produced more migrasomes on fibronectin, whereas cells engineered to express integrin α3 formed migrasomes on laminin, its corresponding ligand. Thus, specific integrin–ECM interactions are key determinants of migrasome biogenesis.
 Building upon these insights, subsequent studies focused on identifying more specific molecular markers. In 2019, Xiaoxin Zhao et al. discovered four new migrasome-specific proteins, namely NDST1, PIGK, CPQ, and EOGT, using quantitative mass spectrometry of purified migrasomes and exosomes [ ]. These proteins were highly enriched in migrasomes but absent in exosomes. Their localization was confirmed by live-cell imaging and immunofluorescence. These specific markers enabled the first biochemical detection of migrasomes in human serum, verified by western blotting and electron microscopy. Unlike earlier markers such as TSPAN4 and integrins, which are also present on exosomes, these four proteins are unique to migrasomes and thus provide robust tools for their identification in clinical samples. In the same year, Lilian Chen et al. identified WGA as a simple and effective fluorescent probe for visualizing migrasomes [ ]. Fluorescently tagged WGA strongly marked migrasomes in live cells, with much higher signal intensity on migrasomes than on RFs. This staining worked optimally in glutaraldehyde-fixed cells and was compatible with live-cell imaging and high-content screening systems. Using this method, they determined the optimal fibronectin concentration for migrasome formation. Unlike protein-based markers, WGA provides a rapid, noninvasive means to visualize migrasomes without genetic modification. These discoveries in 2019 established key methodologies for future migrasome research.
Collectively, these discoveries have established a set of specific molecular markers that allow basic identification, characterization, and detection of migrasomes in both experimental and clinical contexts. However, research on migrasome biomarkers is still constrained by issues such as low specificity, limited in vivo validation, and other challenges. Future work should focus on identifying more specific markers and validating them in vivo and in clinical settings.

## Isolation methods for migrasomes
Several methods have been established for the isolation and purification of migrasomes from both cell cultures and body fluids (Table ). The primary and most frequently cited method was developed by Yu’s laboratory. This standard protocol is a multistep process combining differential centrifugation with density gradient centrifugation. Initially, cells and large debris are removed from the conditioned medium or body fluid through a series of low-speed centrifugation steps, typically at forces such as 1000×g and 4000×g. The resulting supernatant, which contains crude migrasomes, is then subjected to high-speed centrifugation, commonly at 20,000×g, to pellet the migrasomes. For further purification, the pellet is resuspended and layered onto a density gradient medium, such as Optiprep or sucrose, followed by ultracentrifugation at forces around 150,000×g for several hours. Migrasomes are subsequently collected from specific fractions of the gradient. This method yields high-purity samples but is also time-consuming, equipment-intensive, and requires specialized reagents. As research has progressed, alternative and complementary methods have been developed to overcome the limitations of the standard protocol. Yongbin Ma et al. introduced a size-based filtration technique in which the pre-cleared supernatant is passed through a 0.45 μm filter [ ]. Due to their larger size, migrasomes are retained on the filter and then eluted by reversing the filter and flushing it with a buffer solution. The eluate can subsequently be concentrated using ultrafiltration tubes. Although this method is faster and more suitable for high-throughput applications, a major drawback is the potential loss of smaller migrasomes or MDNPs that pass through the filter pores. Chenhui Gu et al. later presented a modified version of Yu’s method [ ]. They used cytochalasin B to stimulate migrasome shedding before performing density gradient centrifugation with a Ficoll separation solution. This modification improves yield but introduces additional specialized reagents and procedural complexity.
 For body fluids such as serum, plasma, and urine, the isolation process is more complex due to sample heterogeneity and the presence of contaminants. The standard ultracentrifugation combined with density gradient centrifugation has been adapted for these samples. However, its procedural complexity and long processing time make it less suitable for rapid clinical applications such as liquid biopsy. A significant innovation for body fluids, specifically urine, was developed by Rong Yang et al. [ ]. This method uses WGA-conjugated magnetic beads and flow cytometry (WBFC). The WBFC method relies on the specific binding of fluorescently labeled WGA to N-acetylglucosamine and sialic acid residues enriched on the surface glycoproteins of migrasomes. This interaction enables sensitive and quantitative detection of WGA-positive migrasomes by flow cytometry, and podocyte origin is confirmed through co-staining with podocyte-specific markers. This affinity-based method is faster, more streamlined, and highly promising for efficient isolation from clinical samples, thereby facilitating downstream molecular analyses. Despite these advantages, its application is mainly limited to body fluids like urine, and changes in migrasome surface features may affect isolation efficiency.
In summary, migrasome isolation methods should be chosen based on study needs. Density gradient centrifugation ensures high purity but is time-consuming and complex, filtration is faster but less pure, and WGA–magnetic bead-based affinity methods are promising for clinical samples but limited by sample type and marker specificity. Future research is needed to further improve and optimize migrasome isolation techniques.

## Functions of migrasomes
Research on migrasome function has progressively revealed their diverse roles in cellular communication and homeostasis (Table ). These functions are closely associated with their structural and mechanistic features. Formed along RFs, migrasomes can selectively package and transport proteins, RNAs, mitochondria, and signaling molecules, with their formation tightly regulated by cytoskeletal dynamics, membrane tension, and signaling pathways. This combination of cargo capacity, dynamic formation, and regulated release contributes to their involvement in intercellular communication, organelle quality control, homeostasis, development, and pathological processes. In 2019, Dong Jiang et al. observed abundant migrasomes in live zebrafish embryos and demonstrated their function as signaling organelles coordinating organ morphogenesis [ ]. Subsequent work by Mingli Zhu et al. in 2020 revealed that these structures facilitate the transfer of functional mRNAs and proteins to recipient cells, directly influencing cellular behavior [ ]. Further expanding their functional spectrum, Haifeng Jiao et al. identified mitocytosis as a key mitochondrial quality-control mechanism, where damaged mitochondria are transported via kinesin family member 5B (KIF5B) to migrasomes for removal, thus maintaining cellular homeostasis [ ]. The physiological significance of migrasomes was further evidenced in 2022, when Cuifang Zhang et al. discovered that monocytes utilize vascular endothelial growth factor A (VEGFA)/ C-X-C motif chemokine ligand 12 (CXCL12)-enriched migrasomes to promote angiogenesis while establishing a self-reinforcing recruitment loop [ ]. However, this communication system can be subverted under pathological conditions, as demonstrated by Na Zhang et al., who showed that Chikungunya virus (CHIKV) hijacks the host PIP5K1A–PI(4,5)P₂ signaling pathway through its nonstructural protein nsP1, promoting migrasome formation and cell migration, thereby facilitating viral replication and dissemination [ ]. Investigations in 2023 continued to illuminate the dual nature of migrasome function. Ilker A. Deniz et al. uncovered a novel bone marrow communication mechanism where mesenchymal stem cell-derived migrasomes containing stromal cell-derived factor 1 (SDF-1)/CD166 chemoattract hematopoietic cells through C-X-C chemokine receptor 4 (CXCR4) [ ]. Meanwhile, Wendy A. Lea et al. revealed that the binding of polycystin-1 (PC1) interacting protein-1 (CU062) to the ectodomain of PC1 within migrasomes regulates mitochondrial function, with disruption of this process potentially explaining mitochondrial defects in autosomal dominant polycystic kidney disease (ADPKD) [ ]. Additional studies by Yalan Liu et al. and Lu Lv et al. independently established migrasomes as vehicles for viral propagation, while Wenbo Zhao et al. demonstrated that vaccinia virus (VACV) utilizes these structures to evade antiviral treatments [ – ]. Recent advances in 2024 have further refined our understanding of migrasome functionality. Dong Jiang et al. reported that neutrophil-derived migrasomes accumulate coagulation factors and adhesion molecules crucial for injury site clotting [ ]. Haifeng Jiao et al. subsequently redefined “migracytosis” as a specialized secretory mechanism in migrating cells, with the same group demonstrating that monocytes deploy cytokine-packed migrasomes to establish localized immune responses at inflammation sites [ ]. Collectively, findings accumulated since 2019 establish the migrasome as a versatile organelle with an increasingly recognized role in both physiological and pathological processes.
 

## Roles of migrasomes in diseases
Migrasomes, as a novel type of EVs, play crucial roles in a wide range of diseases, including neurological, cardiovascular, respiratory, digestive, urinary, reproductive, skeletal, viral, and skin-related conditions. By mediating intercellular transport and signal transmission, they regulate processes such as inflammation, tissue repair, tumor progression, and immune function. In diseases, studying the formation mechanisms, cargo, and functions of migrasomes not only helps to elucidate pathogenic processes but also highlights their potential as early diagnostic biomarkers and therapeutic targets.

## Neurological diseases
Accumulating research indicates that migrasomes contribute significantly to the pathophysiology of neurological diseases. The work by Antje Schmidt-Pogoda et al. in 2018 was pivotal [ ]. It demonstrated that high dietary salt intake exacerbated ischemic brain injury in mice. This study provided the first in vivo evidence linking migrasome formation to a neurological disease. It showed that microglia and macrophages produce abundant migrasomes in the ischemic brain parenchyma after stroke. These migrasomes contained neuronal cytoplasmic material. A strong positive correlation existed between the number of shrunken neurons and the extent of migrasome formation. This suggested migrasomes might incorporate contents from damaged neurons. The study also found migrasomes in human post-mortem stroke brain tissue. This confirmed their clinical relevance. The discovery of migrasomes in these neurological conditions opens new avenues for research. The study by Mengyan Hu et al. in 2023 provided deeper mechanistic insight [ ]. It focused on cerebral amyloid angiopathy (CAA). In CAA, amyloid beta (Aβ) protein accumulates within cerebral blood vessels. Macrophages engulf Aβ40. This triggers them to produce migrasomes enriched with a protein called CD5 antigen-like (CD5L). These migrasomes attach to blood vessels, docking CD5L onto the vessel wall. This local enrichment of CD5L activates the complement system. It leads to complement-dependent cytotoxicity. This process damages the blood–brain barrier. The study showed that migrasome production and complement activation correlated with disease severity in patients and mouse models. Importantly, complement-inhibitory treatment protected against this migrasome-mediated damage in mice. This identified migrasomes as a potential therapeutic target. Their role in intercellular communication and material transfer is crucial. Given that Alzheimer’s disease (AD) is the most common neurodegenerative disorder, characterized by the extracellular deposition of Aβ plaques and intracellular neurofibrillary tangles, and that Aβ accumulation is considered an initiating and central event in AD pathogenesis, it remains to be investigated in the future whether migrasomes also contribute to protein spread and disease progression in AD. In 2025, Zhe Huang et al. demonstrated that glioblastoma (GBM) cells produce migrasomes, with TSPAN4 playing a critical role in their formation [ ]. Elevated TSPAN4 expression was correlated with advanced tumor grade and poorer patient prognosis. Knockdown of TSPAN4 in GBM cells significantly reduced migrasome formation and concurrently impaired cellular migration and invasion. Proteomic analysis revealed that migrasomes are enriched with ECM-associated proteins, particularly p21-activating kinase 4 (PAK4) and laminin alpha 4 (LAMA4), which are released into the extracellular space. Functional rescue experiments confirmed that supplementing TSPAN4-knockdown cells with purified migrasomes restored their migratory and invasive capabilities. However, this effect was abolished when using migrasomes deficient in PAK4 or LAMA4, indicating that these proteins are essential for migrasome-mediated promotion of GBM progression. The study highlights TSPAN4, PAK4, and LAMA4 as potential therapeutic targets in GBM treatment. Following brain injury, research could investigate how migrasomes might influence recovery processes. These structures may potentially modulate inflammatory responses or facilitate repair mechanisms. Determining whether their net effect supports recovery or contributes to damage would be significant. Beyond GBM, other brain tumors might also utilize migrasomes. The existence of similar PAK protein mechanisms represents an interesting possibility. Identifying shared biological pathways could potentially lead to more universal treatment approaches. The relationship between migrasomes and brain immune cells appears particularly important. These areas represent promising research directions. Collectively, current findings open new possibilities for mechanistic exploration and therapeutic development in nervous system diseases.

## Ophthalmic diseases
In 2022, Liangjing Wu et al. provided pivotal insights [ ]. It demonstrated that migrasomes contribute to the development of proliferative vitreoretinopathy (PVR). This study provided compelling evidence linking migrasome formation to retinal disease. It showed that retinal pigmented epithelium (RPE) cells produce migrasomes when activated by TGF-β1. The migrasome marker TSPAN4 was highly expressed in human PVR membranes. These migrasomes were observed along RFs during RPE cell migration. Further analysis confirmed that migrasomes contain specific proteins and can be internalized by other RPE cells. This process increased cell migration and proliferation. Functionally, inhibiting TSPAN4 reduced migrasome formation and impaired PVR development in rabbit models. Mechanistically, TGF-β1 induced TSPAN4 expression through the Smad2/3 signaling pathway. The study also found that the protein Alix facilitates migrasome formation. These findings establish migrasomes as key players in PVR progression. They suggest that targeting TSPAN4 or blocking migrasome formation could be a new therapeutic strategy. This discovery opens new avenues for investigating other ocular diseases involving cell migration.

## Cardiovascular diseases
In cardiovascular diseases, migrasomes have been found to play important yet contrasting roles. In 2025, Zhang et al. found that migrasomes are highly enriched in atherosclerotic tissues, where their levels increase with disease severity [ ]. Endothelial cells, particularly a subtype called Endo1, were identified as the main source. Oxidized low density lipoprotein (ox-LDL) stimulates these cells to produce migrasomes via the PI(4,5)P₂-Rab35-ITGα5 signaling axis. These migrasomes carry amyloid protein precursor (APP), which interacts with cluster of differentiation 74 (CD74) receptors on macrophages, promoting pro-inflammatory M1 polarization and accelerating atherosclerosis. Blocking CD74 disrupts this signaling and slows disease progression, revealing migrasomes as amplifiers of vascular inflammation.
Conversely, a study by Ping Sun et al. demonstrated a protective role for migrasomes in myocardial ischemia-reperfusion injury (MIRI) [ ]. Low-intensity Pulsed Ultrasound (LIPUS) was shown to improve myocardial ischaemia‒reperfusion injury (MIRI) by activating the RhoA/Myosin II pathway, which increases migrasome production. These migrasomes mediate mitocytosis, a process that selectively exports damaged mitochondria, thereby maintaining mitochondrial quality control and reducing cell death. The mechanism involves LIPUS triggering YAP nuclear translocation, which activates transcription of motor proteins KIF5B and Drp1 to transport damaged mitochondria into migrasomes. Blocking migrasome formation abolishes this protection, proving their essential role.
Collectively, these findings reveal migrasomes as double-edged swords in cardiovascular health, capable of either promoting inflammation in atherosclerosis or enabling protection in ischemic injury. They open new research directions for exploring migrasome functions in other cardiovascular conditions and developing organelle-targeted therapies. This area holds significant promise for future cardiovascular medicine.

## Respiratory diseases
Lung fibroblasts, macrophages, and bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells engage in extensive intercellular signaling during respiratory disease. Migrasomes function as vehicles for molecular transfer within these networks. Research by Yawen Peng et al. in 2025 demonstrated that during sepsis, lung fibroblasts release large numbers of migrasomes loaded with mtDNA [ ]. This occurs because lipopolysaccharide (LPS) suppresses peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor gamma coactivator 1-α (PGC-1α), leading to mitochondrial dysfunction and enhanced fibroblast migration, which together promote robust migrasome production. When macrophages take up these mtDNA-containing migrasomes, they undergo macrophage-to-myofibroblast transition (MMT), a key pathological process that contributes to the progression of sepsis-associated pulmonary fibrosis.
Conversely, Tiemei Li et al. revealed that bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells release dermcidin-loaded migrasomes that migrate to the lungs after stroke and enhance pulmonary macrophages’ bacterial clearance through LC3-associated phagocytosis (LAP), thereby preventing post-stroke pneumonia [ ]. These findings raise the possibility that in other infectious lung conditions, migrasomes might also transport immune-active molecules to support macrophage function and aid in inflammation resolution.
Other chronic respiratory diseases, including asthma and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis, may also involve migrasomes in cellular communication during inflammation and tissue repair, a possibility that warrants further investigation. The presence of migrasomes in lung tissue highlights their therapeutic potential. This therapy could involve delivering beneficial molecules or inhibiting detrimental effects. These approaches offer promising avenues for treating respiratory infections and fibrotic diseases, a prospect that warrants further research.

## Digestive system diseases
Emerging studies have identified migrasomes as key mediators in the digestive system, playing roles in both infection and cancer progression, including angiogenesis, tumor invasion, immune regulation, and metabolic reprogramming.
In hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), multiple studies have identified key migrasome-related components. In 2024, Kangnan Zhang et al. discovered that CD151-enriched migrasomes promote HCC invasion and angiogenesis. CD151 is highly expressed in HCC tissues and correlates with poor patient survival [ ]. Experimentally reducing CD151 resulted in decreased migrasome formation and reduced cancer cell invasiveness. These migrasomes were found to carry vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), supporting the formation of new blood vessels for tumor growth. Furthermore, migrasomes from aggressive cancer cells could be taken up by less invasive cells, transferring aggressive traits. Another study by Wanjin Chen et al. in 2025 identified integrin subunit alpha 5 (ITGA5) as a critical migrasome-related gene in HCC [ ]. ITGA5 is highly expressed in tumors, linked to poor prognosis, and involved in pro-tumor pathways such as PI3K–AKT and immune cell infiltration. The study also found that high ITGA5 expression may increase tumor sensitivity to a drug called TGX221. Laboratory tests confirmed that knocking down ITGA5 suppressed cancer cell proliferation, migration, and invasion. Complementing this, a study in 2025 highlighted the role of TSPAN4 in HCC [ ]. TSPAN4 is elevated in tumor tissues and predicts unfavorable outcomes. The study further revealed that TSPAN4 is associated with immune cell regulation and disrupts cellular metabolic pathways, particularly those involving pentose and glucuronate interconversions. Functionally, reducing TSPAN4 had minimal effect on cell proliferation but significantly impaired cell migration. In our recent experiments, we observed clear differences in migrasome production between normal liver cells and HCC cells (Fig. ). Compared with the human normal liver cell line THLE-2, the human HCC cell line HCCLM3, known for its highly migratory phenotype, generated a substantially greater number of migrasomes. Both scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and WGA-488 fluorescence imaging consistently showed increased migrasome formation on the surface of HCCLM3 cells relative to THLE-2 cells. These results indicate a positive association between migrasome production and the enhanced migratory characteristics of malignant hepatocytes, suggesting that migrasomes may contribute to tumor progression in HCC.
 Research on pancreatic cancer has also highlighted the influence of migrasomes. A 2025 study by Qingwen Hu et al. reported that TSPAN4-positive fibroblasts, which are enriched at the invasive tumor edge, exhibit high expression of migrasome-associated genes such as TSPAN4 and ITGA5 [ ]. These fibroblasts engage in extensive communication with macrophages and endothelial cells, promoting ECM remodeling, immunosuppression, and metastatic niche formation. High TSPAN4 expression correlates with impaired immunotherapy response and poorer clinical outcomes. Experimental validation further showed that silencing TSPAN4 in pancreatic cancer cells markedly reduced their proliferation, migration, and invasive capacity. Another pivotal study in 2024 by Ronghua Zhang et al. provided clear evidence of pancreatic cancer cell-derived migrasomes (PCDMs) in both human and mouse models [ ]. These PCDMs have a unique protein composition, rich in specific signaling molecules like CXCL5 and TGF-β1, which differ from their parent cells. Macrophages that take up these PCDMs transform, expressing M2-like markers and secreting factors that promote tumor growth. These educated macrophages help create an immunosuppressive environment by inhibiting T-cell proliferation and activation, partly through the molecule arginase 1 (ARG1). In mouse models, PCDM treatment increased tumor burden, boosted immunosuppressive M2 macrophages, and reduced T cells, identifying migrasomes as a key communication mechanism for immune evasion.
In gastric cancer, a 2025 study by Huiqin Kang et al. identified a signaling pathway involving lysine specific-demethylase 1 (LSD1), estrogen receptor alpha (ERα), and fibronectin 1 (FN1) that drives migrasome formation and cancer cell migration [ ]. LSD1 is highly expressed in gastric cancer tissues, especially in metastatic cases. The study demonstrated that LSD1 forms a complex with ERα and enhances its transcriptional activity. This complex upregulates FN1, a key upstream factor required for migrasome formation. Blocking LSD1, either genetically or pharmacologically, markedly reduced the levels of migrasome markers TSPAN4 and NDST1 and consequently suppressed gastric cancer cell migration. In mouse models, E2-induced activation of this axis promoted lung metastasis, supporting the in vivo relevance of the pathway.
Beyond cancer, migrasomes also play a role in digestive system infections. A 2024 study by Diyin Li et al. found that bacterial toxins such as a variant TcdB (TcdB3) from Clostridioides difficile induce a process termed non-canonical migracytosis in liver and immune cells [ ]. This toxin-driven response forces cells to generate migrasomes even in the absence of migration, leading to the bulk release of inflammatory mediators. While this mechanism acts as an early danger-signaling system to alert the immune response, it also amplifies tissue-damaging inflammation. In genetically modified mice lacking Tspan9, an essential regulator of migrasome formation, toxin exposure resulted in reduced inflammation and improved survival, demonstrating that migrasome production exacerbates tissue injury during severe bacterial infection.
These studies suggest the potential role of migrasomes as central regulators of digestive system pathophysiology. In cancers of the liver, pancreas, and stomach, migrasomes orchestrate tumor invasion, metastatic niche formation, immune suppression, and metabolic reprogramming through key molecules such as CD151, ITGA5, TSPAN4, LSD1, ERα, and FN1. Beyond malignancy, toxin-induced migracytosis in severe infections amplifies inflammation and tissue injury. The detection of migrasomes and their cargo in tissues and body fluids highlights their promise as diagnostic biomarkers. Targeting migrasome formation or their molecular drivers offers a promising therapeutic avenue for treating digestive system cancers and inflammatory disorders. Future research should further define these mechanisms and translate them into clinical applications.

## Urinary system diseases
Noninvasive biomarkers for kidney disease are urgently needed. The foundational work by Ying Liu et al. in 2020 was pivotal [ ]. Their study provided the first evidence that podocytes release migrasomes during injury, linking migrasome formation directly to early podocyte damage in kidney disease. They demonstrated that human podocytes produced significantly more migrasomes when stimulated with LPS, puromycin aminonucleoside (PAN), or high glucose (HG). This increased release was effectively blocked by a Rac1 inhibitor, connecting migrasome production mechanistically to podocyte injury pathways. A key finding from Ying Liu et al. was that an increase in migrasomes in the urine of mice with kidney injury occurred before the onset of proteinuria. This suggested that urinary migrasomes could serve as a more sensitive indicator of early podocyte injury than standard clinical markers. This observation was extended to humans, where patients with diabetic nephropathy (DN) were found to have elevated levels of migrasomes in their urine. These urinary migrasomes carried specific podocyte markers such as podocin, confirming their cellular origin from injured podocytes. The study also noted that other renal cells, such as tubular cells, released far fewer migrasomes, underscoring the specificity of migrasomes as a marker for podocyte injury. Building on this foundation, Rong Yang et al. in 2024 introduced a significant methodological advancement for migrasome detection [ ]. They developed a technique termed WBFC, which combines WGA-coated magnetic beads with flow cytometry to efficiently capture and quantify migrasomes from urine. Using this approach in a clinical cohort, they detected markedly elevated levels of podocyte-derived migrasomes in patients with kidney diseases, including membranous nephropathy (MN), DN, and focal segmental glomerulosclerosis (FSGS), compared with healthy individuals. The diagnostic performance of this migrasome-based assay, measured through the TSPAN4 migrasome signal, exceeded that of conventional markers such as estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) and 24-hour urine protein. Furthermore, the study demonstrated that migrasomes isolated from MN patients contain the phospholipase A2 receptor (PLA2R), enabling these native migrasomes to act as a natural antigen source for detecting circulating PLA2R autoantibodies. This finding suggests a promising noninvasive strategy for diagnosing primary MN and may reduce dependence on invasive kidney biopsies. Qitao Chen et al. recently found that N-terminal truncation of ITM2B in renal cell carcinoma (RCC) cells promotes migrasome formation and mediates active caspase-7 migracytosis, leading to IL-6 secretion from macrophages and accelerated RCC growth. Clinically, ITM2B truncation-enriched migrasomes are elevated in RCC tissues and patient urine, highlighting potential targets for diagnosis and therapy [ ].
These findings open new avenues for noninvasive diagnosis and monitoring of a spectrum of kidney diseases. Future research is poised to explore the precise role of migrasomes in disease progression and their potential as therapeutic targets.

## Gynecological and obstetric diseases
Research on migrasomes in female reproductive health is an emerging field. Their role in both normal pregnancy and pathological conditions is increasingly recognized. In the context of early pregnancy, trophoblast cell migration and invasion are critical for placental development. Defects in this process are a known cause of miscarriage.
A pivotal 2024 study by Shukun Wan et al. provided the first clear evidence that trophoblast cells generate migrasomes during migration and that these structures are essential for proper trophoblast migration and invasion [ ]. The study identified TSPAN4 and NDST1 as key regulators required for migrasome formation in human trophoblasts. Exposure to environmental toxins such as polystyrene nanoplastics markedly suppressed migrasome formation by downregulating TSPAN4 and NDST1, thereby impairing trophoblast migratory and invasive capacities and ultimately leading to defective placental development and pregnancy loss. Mechanistically, the authors showed that nanoplastics induce autophagy, which promotes the degradation of the transcription factor SOX2. Reduced SOX2 expression in turn downregulates ROCK1, a critical mediator of cell migration and migrasome production. This pathogenic cascade was consistently observed in villous tissues from patients with unexplained miscarriages and validated in mouse models. Importantly, restoring SOX2 or ROCK1 expression rescued migrasome formation and significantly reduced miscarriage rates. Notably, recent work by Weina Chen et al. found that TGFβ2-mediated trophoblast migrasome formation is negatively regulated by lnc-HZ05 and is closely associated with unexplained recurrent miscarriage (RM). Serum levels of TGFβ2 and lnc-HZ05 may serve as potential biomarkers for RM risk, offering novel targets for diagnosis and therapeutic intervention [ ]. Research in breast cancer has demonstrated that tumor cells can produce migrasomes [ ]. These migrasomes are enriched in activating transcription factor 6 (ATF6), which disrupts endothelial barriers by inducing endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress and downregulating tight-junction proteins such as zonula occludens-1 (ZO-1) and vascular endothelial cadherin (VE-cadherin). This mechanism compromises the blood–brain barrier and facilitates metastatic dissemination, playing a pivotal role in breast cancer brain metastasis.
Collectively, these studies demonstrate that migrasomes play critical roles in both reproductive biology and oncology. In pregnancy, they are essential for trophoblast migration and placental development, and their disruption by environmental toxins can lead to miscarriage. In cancer, tumor-derived migrasomes, such as ATF6-enriched migrasomes in breast cancer, can impair endothelial barriers and promote metastatic spread. These findings highlight migrasomes as multifunctional organelles whose dysregulation contributes to diverse gynecological and systemic diseases. Future research should investigate their involvement in additional pregnancy disorders, such as preeclampsia and fetal growth restriction, and explore their potential as diagnostic markers and therapeutic targets across reproductive and cancer-related conditions.

## Skeletal system diseases
Evidence indicates that migrasomes play important roles in the pathology of skeletal system diseases. Two key studies published in 2024 and 2025 have revealed their respective roles in primary bone cancer and bone metastasis. In 2025, Wanshun Liu et al. investigated migrasomes in osteosarcoma [ ]. Their research indicated that osteosarcoma cells produce migrasomes, which promote tumor growth and spread. They identified small vesicles within migrasomes, termed migrasome-associated nanoparticles (MANPs). These MANPs carry signals between cancer cells and macrophages. The study identified milk fat globule-EGF factor 8 (MFGE8) as a key protein within these vesicles. MFGE8 enhances macrophage phagocytosis of apoptotic cells and promotes their polarization toward the M2 phenotype. This M2 polarization supports tumor growth. The team found that deletion of MFGE8 from migrasomes prevented M2 macrophage polarization and reduced tumor progression. In 2024, Chenhui Gu et al. explored migrasomes in bone metastasis [ ]. The study showed that migrasome formation requires high extracellular calcium levels, and reducing calcium availability significantly suppressed migrasome production and tumor-associated osteoclast ‘tumasteoclast’ (TAOC) differentiation. Based on these findings, the researchers developed a phosphate-releasing nanoliposome therapy designed to target the early tumor–osteoclast niche. The released phosphate ions react with local calcium to form calcium phosphate microcrystals that inhibit migrasome formation and induce tumor cell death, effectively preventing early bone metastatic colonization.
Both studies highlight migrasomes as crucial communication tools in the bone microenvironment. The 2024 study focused on tumor–osteoclast interactions in metastatic cancer, whereas the 2025 study examined macrophage reprogramming in primary bone cancer. Both research groups identified specific molecular mechanisms through which migrasomes influence disease progression. These discoveries open new research directions for other skeletal disorders. The emerging understanding of migrasomes in bone biology provides new perspectives and potential therapeutic strategies for skeletal diseases.

## Virus
During infection, many viruses take advantage of migrasomes to enhance their spread and transmission. The first evidence came from the work by Na Zhang et al. in 2022, which showed that CHIKV uses its nsP1 protein to promote migrasome formation [ ]. The viral protein recruits the lipid kinase PIP5K1A, increases the level of PI(4,5)P₂, and stimulates actin-dependent migrasome biogenesis. This pathway also supports viral replication, suggesting that CHIKV hijacks host migrasome machinery to enhance infection. Building on this early finding, subsequent research expanded the link between migrasomes and viral spread to other pathogens. In 2023, the work by Yalan Liu et al. and colleagues provided the first direct evidence that herpes simplex virus type 2 uses migrasomes as vehicles for cell-to-cell spread [ ]. The authors found that infected epithelial cells release migrasomes that contain intact virions. These virus-carrying migrasomes can infect new cells, even those that normally do not allow viral entry, which shows that migrasomes act as a new transmission route for herpesvirus. Further supporting the concept of migrasome-mediated dissemination, another study the same year revealed a similar strategy used by poxviruses. Another important study by Lu Lv et al. in 2023 showed that VACV can reside inside migrasomes [ ]. The authors demonstrated that VACV infection generates migrasomes that contain infectious virions, including intracellular mature virus and intracellular enveloped virus. This finding suggests that poxviruses may use migrasomes as an alternative route of dissemination that does not rely solely on the formation of extracellular enveloped virions. The study also showed that elevated levels of TSPAN4 stabilize these migrasomes and enhance their capacity to carry viral proteins. Because intracellular mature virus does not require the F13 protein targeted by tecovirimat, this mechanism may help explain how poxviruses could evade current antiviral treatments. This observation is consistent with later work examining the limitations of existing antiviral therapies. A related study by Wenbo Zhao et al. in 2023 confirmed that tecovirimat, although effective at blocking extracellular virus formation, does not inhibit the migrasomes induced by VACV infection [ ]. Virus-containing migrasomes continued to form even in the presence of the drug, indicating that migrasomes may provide an alternative transmission route that is not blocked by tecovirimat. More recent studies have extended these findings by identifying pharmacological inhibitors that target migrasome-dependent viral spread. The work by Ting Xu et al. in 2025 showed that VACV infection induces abundant migrasomes, which are associated with viral dissemination [ ]. The authors found that the approved drug dasabuvir blocks this process by inhibiting ROCK1 and by interfering with the viral F13 protein, thereby suppressing the production of extracellular enveloped viruses. Their results demonstrated that reducing migrasome formation significantly limits viral spread. Recently, Yao Hou et al. found that zinc ions inhibit virus-induced migrasome formation during VACV and CHIKV infections, likely by targeting SMS2, resulting in reduced migrasome-mediated viral spread and suppressed CHIKV replication, with potential clinical significance as a broad-spectrum antiviral strategy [ ]. Concurrently, a recent study by Linghui Yu et al. found that the ER-resident E3 ligase AMFR negatively regulates migrasome formation via K48-linked ubiquitination of TSPAN4, whereas AMFR O-GlcNAcylation stabilizes TSPAN4 and promotes migrasome biogenesis. Furthermore, viruses such as VSV and HSV-1 exploit this pathway to enhance their spread, highlighting a potential target for antiviral intervention [ ].
Together, these studies reveal a unifying concept. Many viruses can induce or hijack migrasomes, and these structures can carry viral proteins or whole virions to new cells. Migrasomes therefore represent a newly recognized route of viral spread that supports infection even when classical pathways are blocked. These findings identify migrasome biogenesis as a potential antiviral target. Future research should test whether other viruses use similar strategies and determine how blocking migrasome formation can help control viral diseases.

## Regenerative medicine
The work by Yunzi Chen et al. in 2024 was pivotal. It demonstrated that adipose-derived stem cells (ASCs) utilize migrasomes to facilitate soft tissue regeneration [ ]. This study provided clear evidence linking migrasome function to tissue repair. It showed that ASCs produce migrasomes enriched with the chemokine CXCL12. These migrasomes deposit CXCL12 along cell migration paths. This creates a localized chemical signal. The study found that migrasomes recruit stem cells by activating the CXCR4 receptor. This activation triggers RhoA signaling inside cells. RhoA drives cell migration toward the injury site. The recruited stem cells then help regenerate adipose tissue. This process forms a positive feedback loop. More stem cell recruitment leads to better tissue repair. Researchers confirmed this mechanism in mouse models. Injecting isolated migrasomes into poorly healing tissue improved regeneration. Injecting purified migrasomes increased stem cell recruitment, improved adipocyte morphology, and reduced fibrosis. Blocking the CXCR4 receptor with the inhibitor AMD3100 halted this process. This proved that the pathway is essential. The study also showed that migrasomes carry specific proteins. TSPAN4 and integrin β1 are key markers. Integrin helps anchor migrasomes to the ECM. This keeps signals in the right place. This research uncovers a previously unrecognized role for ASCs in tissue healing. They do not just differentiate into new cells. They also release migrasomes that recruit additional stem cells. Together, these findings suggest that ASC-derived migrasomes represent a promising therapeutic strategy for enhancing adipose tissue regeneration. Future studies should explore migrasomes in other regenerative contexts. Their role in wound healing or muscle repair is still unknown. Understanding how to control migrasome production could lead to new therapies. Migrasomes offer a promising direction for enhancing regenerative medicine.

## Skin diseases
Key roles of migrasomes in skin repair and aging highlight their importance in skin diseases. Research by Haoyu Zhou et al. in 2025 confirmed the existence of migrasomes in human skin and oral mucosa, showing they originate from fibroblasts [ ]. Their study demonstrated that these fibroblast-derived migrasomes are internalized by recipient cells such as keratinocytes. In a rat wound model, injection of purified migrasomes significantly accelerated wound closure, enhanced re-epithelialization, and increased granulation tissue thickness. Single-cell RNA sequencing revealed that this treatment upregulated key repair factors in fibroblasts, including VEGFA, IL-6, and COL1A1. A central mechanistic finding was the high enrichment of the chemokine CXCL12 in these migrasomes. Functional experiments showed that depleting CXCL12 from migrasomes impaired their ability to promote cell proliferation, migration, and angiogenesis. Concurrently, research by Hanlin Tu et al. in 2025 revealed a critical link between migrasomes and skin aging [ ]. They found that the ability of fibroblasts to form migrasomes decreases with age, with younger skin containing more migrasomes. When migrasomes from young fibroblasts were injected into wounds of aged mice, they accelerated healing and reduced the expression of senescence markers such as p21 and senescence-associated β-galactosidase (SA-β-gal). In vitro experiments further showed that young fibroblast-derived migrasomes could alleviate senescence in human keratinocytes. This was evidenced by reduced aging markers, improved cell proliferation and migration, and lower levels of reactive oxygen species. These findings suggest a more comprehensive connection between migrasome biogenesis, aging, and disease susceptibility. Aging is a well-known risk factor for cancer, and the cellular and molecular changes associated with senescence may create an environment that tumor cells can exploit. It will be exciting to explore how age-related changes in migrasome production and composition influence cancer risk and progression.
Overall, these studies demonstrate that migrasomes represent a fundamental mechanism in skin physiology, with substantial potential for developing treatments for a wide range of conditions.
In summary, there is growing evidence that migrasomes are critical mediators of intercellular communication driving disease initiation and progression across multiple organ systems (Fig. ). Migrasomes regulate key processes including inflammation, immune modulation, tissue repair, angiogenesis, and tumor invasion by transporting bioactive cargo. As research on migrasomes progresses, further mechanistic and translational studies may elucidate their potential as diagnostic biomarkers and therapeutic targets.
 

## Conclusion and perspective
Migrasomes have emerged as a pivotal and distinctive class of EVs, distinguished by their unique biogenesis on RFs during cell migration and their multifaceted roles in intercellular communication. Unlike conventional EVs such as exosomes and microvesicles, migrasomes exhibit a specialized mode of formation that reflects their dynamic association with cellular motility. This distinctive origin endows them with the capacity to integrate spatial and temporal information, thus making them critical mediators of tissue-level coordination.
Migrasomes serve as powerful hubs of intercellular signaling by facilitating ligand–receptor interactions, the lateral transfer of proteins, lipids, and nucleic acids, and potentially direct contact-mediated communication. Such versatility positions migrasomes not merely as passive carriers of cellular materials, but rather as dynamic organizers capable of orchestrating complex biological processes across multiple systems. Their ability to generate structured and temporally controlled gradients enables cells to convey combinatorial signals with remarkable precision. This property is particularly vital during physiological events such as embryonic morphogenesis [ ], where spatial signaling determines tissue patterning, and immune surveillance [ , , , ], where precise signaling controls immune cell trafficking, and tissue regeneration [ ], where coordinated cues drive repair and remodeling.
In pathological contexts, migrasomes have demonstrated a broad spectrum of functional implications. Accumulating evidence suggests their involvement in tumor metastasis, where migrasome-mediated communication influences the tumor microenvironment, promote angiogenesis, and facilitate pre-metastatic niche formation [ , , , , , , ]. Similarly, their emerging role in viral propagation highlights their potential as vehicles that viruses exploit to enhance viral spread and evade immune detection [ , – , ]. Moreover, in chronic diseases such as fibrosis [ ], neurodegeneration [ , ], and metabolic disorders [ , ], migrasomes may act as conduits for persistent inflammatory or stress-related signals, contributing to disease progression. These findings show that, on one hand, migrasomes are essential for normal cell communication, while on the other hand, they can be taken over and used to promote illness under disease conditions.
Despite these significant advances, several fundamental questions remain unresolved. The molecular mechanisms that govern migrasome formation, cargo sorting, and selective release are still not fully understood. The way in which cytoskeletal dynamics, lipid composition, and TEMs coordinate to regulate migrasome assembly still requires further in-depth investigation. Moreover, little is known about how specific cargoes, such as signaling molecules, RNAs, or organelle fragments, are selectively incorporated into migrasomes and how these cargoes influence recipient cell fate. The lack of standardized isolation methods, reliable quantification techniques, and universally applicable clinical biomarkers continues to present major challenges for reproducibility and translational research.
Future investigations should adopt an integrated and multidisciplinary approach to effectively overcome these challenges. Combining super-resolution and live-cell imaging will be essential for visualizing migrasome dynamics and interactions in vivo. Multi-omics analyses, including proteomics, lipidomics, and transcriptomics, could comprehensively map migrasome cargo composition and identify condition-specific signatures. Moreover, biophysical and computational modeling could provide insight into the forces driving migrasome biogenesis and the diffusion patterns of migrasome-derived signals. From a translational perspective, developing standardized isolation and detection protocols, such as antibody-based capture assays or high-throughput nanoparticle profiling, will be critical for advancing clinical applications.
Looking ahead, migrasomes represent an emerging and exciting frontier in biomedical research. Their unique biology offers opportunities for innovation in diagnostics, such as their use as liquid biopsy biomarkers that reflect dynamic cellular states, and in therapeutics, where engineered migrasomes could serve as targeted delivery systems for drugs or genetic materials. As research advances, a deeper understanding of migrasomes is likely to redefine the paradigms of intercellular communication. By bridging molecular, cellular, and systemic biology, migrasomes hold the promise of inspiring novel therapeutic strategies for a broad spectrum of conditions, from developmental disorders and immune dysregulation to cancer and infectious diseases.
Ultimately, the study of migrasomes extends beyond a single class of vesicles; it reshapes how we perceive the ways in which cells interact, adapt, and coordinate within multicellular systems. Continued exploration of these fascinating structures will not only enrich our fundamental understanding of cell biology but also open transformative avenues in regenerative medicine, disease intervention, and precision health.
