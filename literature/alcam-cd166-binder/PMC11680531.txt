## Introduction
Recently, the American Cancer Society released the report titled “Global Cancer Statistics 2024”. The most commonly diagnosed cancers in 2022 included lung cancer (12.4%), female breast cancer (11.6%), colorectal cancer (9.6%), prostate cancer (7.3%), and stomach cancer (4.9%). Among women, breast cancer, lung cancer, and colorectal cancer are the most prevalent types of cancer, accounting for 51% of all new cancer cases in women, with breast cancer alone accounting for 32% of these cases [ ]. Breast cancer is recognized as a complex disease with significant variations in clinical and pathological features. To ensure accurate diagnosis and tailor treatment options, it is classified based on the expression status of Estrogen receptor (ER) and Progesterone receptor (PR). This allows for categorization into distinct molecular subtypes [ – ]: luminal breast cancers, which are ER or PR positive; Human epidermal growth factor receptor 2-positive (HER2-positive) tumors; and Triple negative breast cancer (TNBC) [ ], which lack expression of ER/PR/HER2 receptors. Within the luminal subtype, further distinctions can be made between categories A and B [ , ]. Breast cancer is among the tumors with the most effective treatments currently available. Progress in various therapeutic strategies, including comprehensive surgery, radiotherapy, chemotherapy, endocrine therapy (ET), targeted therapy, and immunotherapy, has significantly improved its overall prognosis [ ]. However, considering the diversity of affected populations and the presence of recurrent or metastatic patients, it remains vital to further enhance their quality of life and survival duration. Approximately 25–30% of all breast cancer cases are HER2-positive [ – ]. Several anti-HER2 therapies are currently available for treating HER2-positive breast cancer, including monoclonal antibodies (e.g., trastuzumab), tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs, e.g., lapatinib), and ADCs [ – ].
On the other hand, TNBC accounts for approximately 15–20% of all breast cancers. Due to its high degree of malignancy and invasiveness, TNBC has consistently presented a significant treatment challenge. The primary therapeutic approach for patients with triple-negative breast cancer is chemotherapy, often with neoadjuvant chemotherapy administered before surgery. However, recent advancements in immunotherapy and targeted therapy have greatly expanded the treatment options for both early-stage operable and advanced metastatic TNBC [ – ].
ADC-based cancer therapies have shown promising results in treating various cancers, including metastatic breast cancer and TNBC. Research focusing on HER2 and Trop-2 targets continues to be a central area of interest in breast cancer studies. Several specific mAbs have demonstrated clinical efficacy in breast cancer patients. To date, three ADC drugs have received global approval and are commercially available: Trastuzumab Emtansine (T-DM1) [ , ], Trastuzumab Deruxtecan (T-DXd) [ ], and Sacituzumab Govitecan (SG) [ ]. Meanwhile, other ADC drugs are still undergoing clinical trials (Table  ). 
Despite the significant potential of ADC drugs in oncology, their clinical advancement has been hampered by a high failure rate. This can be attributed to adverse reactions from ADC drug therapy, which often limit the allowable dosage to below what is required for effective anti-cancer activity. Adverse drug reactions have become a major hurdle in drug research, with even FDA-approved ADCs for breast cancer treatment being associated with grade 3 or higher adverse reactions during safety evaluations. In many cases, these adverse effects have led to dose reductions of ADCs, treatment interruptions, and unfortunately, patient fatalities.
In the future of tumor therapy, precision-targeted ADC drugs are poised to become a key treatment, yet rising ADC availability brings concerns over drug-related toxic side effects, including interstitial lung disease (ILD). This review explores the etiology and epidemiology of ADC-induced ILD in breast cancer treatments, proposing strategies for precise diagnosis and management based on principles for drug-induced ILD and insights from immunotherapy-induced ILD.

## Mechanism of action of ADCs
After ADC drugs bind to specific antigens on the surface of tumor cells, this process serves as a precise navigation, guiding the drugs into the interior of the tumor cells (Fig.  ). Subsequently, through antigen-mediated endocytosis, the complex of ADC drugs and antigens is ingested and internalized by the tumor cells. Inside the lysosomes of the tumor cells, due to the acidic environment, the linker of the ADC drugs begins to degrade, releasing highly active cytotoxic drugs. 
These cytotoxic drugs are central to the efficacy of ADC drugs. Once released, they rapidly act on the DNA or protein synthesis process of the tumour cells, interfering with and destroying the basic links of these life activities, thus effectively preventing tumour cells from dividing and proliferating, and ultimately triggering the death of the tumour cells [ , ].
However, the efficacy of ADC drugs is not limited to this. It is worth noting that some lipophilic cytotoxic drugs are able to penetrate the cell membrane after killing the tumour cells, spread out from the dead tumour cells and enter into the adjacent tumour cells to continue to play a killing role. This phenomenon is known as the “bystander effect”, which enables ADC drugs to show significant efficacy when targeting tumours with low antigen expression or uneven distribution, thus greatly broadening the scope of application of ADC drugs [ – ].
ADCs target specific tumor antigens to deliver cytotoxic drugs directly into cancer cells, inducing apoptosis or necrosis in these cells. This mode of cell death not only aids in the elimination of tumor cells but may also induce immunogenic cell death (ICD), activating the immune system [ ]. ICD is a unique mechanism of cell death that stimulates and enhances the body’s immune response, leading to the induction of anti-tumor immunity. It not only directly causes the death of tumor cells but also releases specific molecular signals that attract and activate immune cells to eliminate residual tumor cells. The process of ICD is typically accompanied by the release of key molecules such as heat shock proteins (HSP), ATP, and calreticulin [ ], which act as damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) that recruit immune cells through various pathways, thereby initiating an immune response and enhancing anti-tumor immunity [ ]. In some cases, this enhanced immune response may not only target tumor cells but also attack normal tissues, potentially resulting in local inflammatory responses that can damage pulmonary interstitial cells. Additionally, calreticulin serves as a classic "eat me" signal, and ICD facilitates its translocation to the cell surface, attracting macrophages and dendritic cells to phagocytize apoptotic tumor cells [ – ].
Some studies have indicated that ADCs capable of successfully inducing ICD are associated with improved efficacy and patient survival rates [ – ]. ICD enhances anti-tumor immunity by releasing tumor-associated antigens and activating immune responses, allowing T cells and other immune cells to recognize and attack cancer cells [ ]. This surveillance mechanism may persist even after ADC treatment [ , ]. However, ongoing cell death and immune-mediated responses may affect surrounding normal cells and tissues. Furthermore, as a significant proportion of the ADC dosage may not reach the tumor, ADCs can metabolize in other parts of the body, releasing the active payload in undesired locations. High doses of the payload may lead to direct damage to normal tissue cells, thereby triggering related adverse reactions. Accumulation of the payload or the generation of metabolites in non-tumor tissues can also result in cellular injury, potentially causing chronic pulmonary inflammation that may ultimately progress to drug-induced ILD.
In addition to direct killing of tumour cells, the antibody portion of ADC drugs also exerts anti-tumour effects through various other mechanisms. For example, the Fab segment of the antibody can specifically bind to the receptors on the surface of the tumour cells, thus blocking the function of these receptors and inhibiting the growth, invasion and metastasis of the tumour cells. On the other hand, the Fc segment of the antibody can interact with cells or complement molecules in the immune system, triggering a series of immune responses, such as antibody-dependent cytotoxicity (ADCC), complement-dependent cytotoxicity (CDC), and antibody-dependent cellular phagocytosis (ADPC), etc. [ – ]. Together, these immune responses can further clear the tumour cells and enhance the anti-tumour effect of ADC drugs.

## Composition and potential toxicities of ADCs
Although different types of antibody–drug couplings (ADCs) have different structures, they share a similar mechanism: the use of monoclonal antibodies to precisely recognise cancer cell targets, retaining both the cytotoxicity of the cytotoxic load portion of the ADC drug and the antibody-dependent cytotoxic effect (ADCC) of the antibody portion, to achieve highly effective anti-tumour therapy. However, safety concerns remain, such as side effects like lung injury. Current research is working to optimise ADCs to enhance efficacy and reduce adverse effects (Fig.  ). 

## Antibodies and targets
The selection process for ADC targets takes into account several factors. These include high expression of the antigen on tumor cells with minimal or no presence on normal cells [ ]; effective interaction between the antigen and antibodies in the circulatory system, coupled with resistance to displacement; and efficient cellular endocytosis upon specific binding of the ADC to its target, ensuring swift entry of the ADC drug into tumor cells [ ]. Currently, IgG is the preferred choice of antibody due to its structure, which consists of two Fab fragments and one Fc fragment, making it the primary option for researchers. However, it is important to note that ADC binding to Fcγ receptors can potentially lead to adverse effects [ ]. Given toxicity concerns, there is a risk that improperly bound antibodies might target unintended normal cells. Basically, the non-specific binding of ADC antibodies to Fc receptors on normal cells and the subsequent payload release within these cells can induce apoptosis. This phenomenon is commonly termed “off-target toxicity” [ ].

## Linkers and connections
The linker has the ability to attach the antibody to the payload and maintain it in a stable circulating state. Once internalized by the cell, the linker swiftly releases the payload to exert its anti-tumor effect [ ]. While the linker itself does not directly produce toxicity, its stability significantly affects the subsequent toxic release from the payload. Chemical linkers explored to date can be primarily classified into two types: cleavable (e.g., T-DXd) and non-cleavable (e.g., T-DM1) [ , ]. Each type of linker offers its own advantages and disadvantages. Cleavable linkers act quickly but possess reduced stability, leading to a broader scope of potential toxicity. Conversely, non-cleavable linkers are highly stable with fewer off-target effects and have a higher likelihood of producing specific toxicity [ ].

## Payload
Payloads are a specific class of small-molecule cytotoxic drugs with the ability to induce cell-killing effects [ ]. These payloads are essential in enabling the cytotoxic effect of ADCs on tumor cells. Currently, ADC payloads under investigation in clinical trials and research can be divided into two main categories: microtubule inhibitors and DNA alkylating agents [ ]. Microtubule inhibitors work by either causing DNA damage or blocking the synthesis of microtubule proteins, leading to apoptosis. In contrast, DNA alkylating agents create irreversible covalent bonds with electron-containing groups within DNA molecules, disrupting processes like replication and transcription and ultimately causing cell death [ ]. Each type of payload displays specific toxicities at associated sites. For example, MMAE is linked with the induction of peripheral neuropathy and neutropenia, while MMAF often results in thrombocytopenia and ocular toxicity. DM2 predominantly causes gastrointestinal issues, along with platelet and neutrophil Skalimycin use has been associated with the onset of thrombocytopenia and hepatotoxicity [ , ].

## Optimization of drug-to-antibody ratio, bystander effect, and maximum tolerated dose in antibody–drug conjugates (ADCs)
The bystander effect is a phenomenon observed in certain ADCs, where the unique structural characteristics of these drugs lead to cytotoxic effects on non-target tumor cell populations, mediated by both the payload and its metabolites [ ]. This effect can be attributed to various biochemical properties of the linker and payload, including linker stability, the chemical bond between the antibody and linker, and the polarity of the payload, among others. Notably, T-DXd [ ] and SYD985 [ ] are examples of ADCs that exhibit bystander effects. Additionally, it is important to recognize that off-target toxicity from ADCs can also result from the premature release of the payload into the bloodstream before reaching the targeted tumor cells. Several factors contribute to this phenomenon, including the stability of the linker and the coupling method. Cleavable linkers, such as those in T-DXd [ ], act quickly but have reduced stability, leading to a wider spectrum of toxic effects. In contrast, non-cleavable linkers, like those found in T-DM1, offer greater stability, lower off-target risks, and are more likely to produce targeted toxicity.
The coupling mode is bifurcated into two methods: random and site-specific coupling. In the traditional method of random coupling, the process proceeds in an indiscriminate manner, producing a range of ADC variants. This variability can result in a spectrum of ADC kinetic properties, instability, increased potential for antibody aggregation, release of cytotoxic agents, and off-target toxicity, often due to unpredictable aspects such as lysine conjugate coupling that results in 0–8 drug molecules attached to each antibody. However, site-specific coupling techniques can overcome this variability, known as the drug-to-antibody ratio (DAR), by producing consistent and homogeneous ADCs, thereby reducing the likelihood of off-target toxicity. For instance, T-DXd is coupled using a thioether bond to an average of eight cysteine residues, which arise from four pairs of interchain disulfide bonds in the reduced antibody structure. This leads to the creation of a uniform and stable ADC with a DRA of 8 [ , ].
The MTD of ADCs is often limited by off-target platform toxicities that occur outside of tumor tissues, with these toxicities varying depending on the linker-payload types used [ , ]. This form of toxicity, known as off-target or off-tumor toxicity, is currently under extensive investigation. If the ADC dosage exceeds the MTD, it can lead to damage in normal tissues, especially in cells that express the target antigen, even if the drug predominantly accumulates in the tumor site. For patients with solid tumors, the clinical efficacy of ADCs is further complicated by factors such as tissue permeability, which is particularly critical in solid tumors as opposed to hematological malignancies. Poor tumor penetration of ADCs, a challenge observed not only in animal models but potentially more pronounced in humans, has been shown in recent studies to limit efficacy due to perivascular distribution [ ]. For instance, co-administration of unconjugated antibodies with ADCs has been shown to improve ADC tissue penetration, allowing a larger proportion of cells to receive the payload, thus enhancing tumor suppression [ , ].
However, the MTD of ADCs is closely associated with their potential for adverse effects. Even with good target specificity, high doses inevitably expose surrounding normal tissues to the cytotoxic payload, leading to localized toxicity. This challenge contributes to the clinical failure of many ADCs that initially showed promise in preclinical studies due to toxicity and/or limited therapeutic windows. Currently, there is no universally accepted understanding of ADC toxicity. Although evidence suggests that off-target effects mediated by the payload drive most ADC toxicities, binding of ADCs to target antigens in healthy tissues can also lead to significant adverse effects [ ]. For instance, in clinical trials of T-DXd, ILD and pneumonia were identified as adverse events leading to dose adjustments, delays, or discontinuation of treatment, with severe and fatal cases reported [ ]. Similar findings have been observed in patients treated with other anti-HER2 ADCs, such as SYD985 and ARX788 [ , ].
Notably, the same ADC may exhibit different toxicities when treating different cancer types, suggesting that ADC-induced ILD cannot be fully understood through the lens of a single drug [ ]. Its mechanisms are likely linked to the MTD and other factors, such as immune cells in the tumor microenvironment. ## Prospects and cost-effectiveness of combination therapy for solid tumors
Numerous ongoing studies are rigorously investigating the combination of ADCs with various anticancer agents—including chemotherapy, molecularly targeted drugs, and immunotherapies—in both preclinical research and clinical trial settings. However, combination therapy does not always guarantee [ ] controlled trials, improvements in expected survival rates for solid tumors and the underlying biological mechanisms remain challenging to discern.
From current clinical trial summaries [ ], we observe that combining ADCs with chemotherapy often intensifies adverse effects. For instance, a study evaluating T-DM1 combined with docetaxel (with or without pertuzumab) for HER2-positive breast cancer reported dose-limiting toxicity (DLT) and Grade ≥3 adverse events in approximately 80% of patients with metastatic breast cancer [ ]. Similarly, Dato-DXd combined with platinum-based chemotherapy and pembrolizumab [ ], as well as the DESTINY-Gastric03 trial involving T-DXd in combination with 5-fluorouracil (5-FU) or capecitabine [ ], demonstrate an increased toxicity profile.
Conversely, endocrine-based combination regimens appear not to increase toxicity. This finding aligns with the distinct adverse event profiles of each drug when administered independently and with the generally favorable safety profile of most endocrine therapies compared to other systemic cancer treatments. For example, in the Phase III KATHERINE trial, T-DM1 patients with or without endocrine therapy showed no significant differences in any-grade, Grade ≥3 adverse events (26.0 vs. 24.9%), serious adverse events (12.9 vs. 12.2%), or events leading to T-DM1 dose reduction (11.0 vs. 15.0%) [ ]. Similar trends were observed in the TALENT study [ ] and the Phase Ib DESTINY-Breast08 study. Interestingly, the combination of immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) with T-DXd, Dato-DXd [ ], or sacituzumab govitecan [ ] also appears not to increase ADC-associated ILD incidence.

## Epidemiology of drug-induced ILD (the epidemiological description of the original manuscript was summarized and written into a separate subsection)
While the efficacy of ADCs is well-documented, their clinical application can be hindered by adverse reactions, such as pneumonia/ILD, which can compromise a patient’s health. Drug-induced ILD is a commonly observed pulmonary adverse event following ADC administration in clinical settings (Table  ). One analysis showed that the incidence rate for combined grades of pneumonia in patients undergoing ADC monotherapy was approximately 5.08% (95% CI, 2.76–7.96%). In contrast, drug-related pneumonia due to T-DXd had an incidence rate of about 12.75% (95% CI, 8.33–17.88%) across all grades. For T-DM1 treatment, the rate for all grades of pneumonia stood at around 1.24% (95% CI, 0.12–3.10%), and for the SG drug, it was a mere 0.01% (95% CI, 0.00–0.30%). However, the overall incidence rates for all-grade pneumonia remained relatively low, hovering around 3.37% [ ]. Previous research analyzed 28 cases of ILD associated with anti-HER2 ADCs, including T-DM1, T-DXd, RC48, SYD985, ARX788, and various combinations of T-DM1. The overall prevalence rate for all grades of ILDs was 3.7% (n = 291) among 7806 patients, with grade 3 or higher ILDs occurring in only 0.7% (n = 31) [ ]. Notably, when used as a single drug, T-DXd exhibited a stronger association with ILDs compared to other medications. In contrast, combining T-DM1 with other antineoplastic drugs increased the risk of developing ILDs. Based on available data, RC48 does not seem to be associated with the onset of ILDs. While it is important to consider the potential of ARX788-induced ILDs, current evidence regarding its toxicity is too limited to draw definitive conclusions. Existing studies suggest potential correlations between T-DXd and adverse ILD effects, which necessitate close monitoring by healthcare professionals. Moreover, the combination of different ADC drugs might increase the risk of pharmacologically-induced ILDs. 
T-DXd treatment has been associated with a significantly higher incidence of adverse reactions, including severe toxic events. Various clinical studies, such as DESTINY-Breast02, were presented at the SABCS 2022 Congress. In the T-DXd group (n = 404) and the physician-selected chemotherapy group (n = 195), ILD/pneumonia occurred in 10.4 and 0.5% of patients, respectively, with two cases of grade 5 events observed in the T-DXd group [ ]. Researchers have expressed concerns about the potential of T-DXd to induce ILD, given its camptothecin analog content. ADC drug-induced interstitial pneumonitis has led to dose reductions, treatment discontinuation, and life-threatening situations for patients.

## Speculation about ADC-associated pneumonia
The intricate mechanisms underlying pneumonia caused by ADC drugs remain incompletely understood. However, potential explanations for its theoretical pathogenesis might be attributed to either the inherent chemical structure of ADCs or their internalization pathway within organisms. Currently, there is a paucity of interventional studies addressing lung injury associated with antibody-conjugated drugs. Possible factors contributing to lung injury induced by ADCs could include: (1) ADC uptake dependent on the target; (2) ADC uptake by normal cells instead of intended targets; (3) elimination of bystander cells due to the release of free warheads from cancer cells; and (4) generation of circulating free warheads because of incomplete coupling to the intended targets [ ] (Fig.  ). Speculations regarding ILDs associated with ADC usage can be primarily categorized into two areas: toxicity targeting intended sites and toxicity affecting unintended sites. 

## The hypothesis of targeted toxicity
The concept of targeted toxicity refers to the toxicity associated with a specific objective. It is important to note that many objectives are not only highly expressed in tumor cells but also in normal cells and tissues [ ]. Intact ADCs have the remarkable ability to infiltrate normal cells either through non-specific endocytosis or by binding to target antigens or Fc/c-type lectin receptors. Moreover, ADCs efficiently penetrate target-bearing normal cells via antibody-mediated specific recognition pathways, releasing a payload with cytocidal toxicity that induces damage and apoptosis in these otherwise healthy cells [ ]. Initially, concerns regarding the safety of ADCs centered on potential target toxicity in tissues showing varying levels of target antigen expression. However, it was anticipated that differences in target expression between cancerous cells and healthy tissues would significantly influence the therapeutic index of ADCs.However, based on extensive clinical experience with ADCs, it is rare for dose-limiting toxicity (DLT) to be attributed to target expression in healthy tissues. Previous investigations have shown that ADCs with similar linker/loading characteristics often exhibit similar toxicity profiles, occurrences of DLT, and maximum tolerated doses (MTD), regardless of the targeted antigen or the level of antigen expression in healthy tissues [ ].

## The hypothesis of off-target toxicity
Off-target toxicity refers to the absorption of the payload in normal cells unrelated to the target antigen, which can arise through various potential mechanisms. These underlying mechanisms are complex. After administering ADCs, the released payload, often termed the "free" payload, quickly enters the systemic circulation. Exposure of plasma to this free payload is partly due to the premature detachment of the payload in the systemic circulation because of insufficient linker stability [ ]. Thus, uptake pathways independent of the target antigen (i.e., off-target toxicity) might contribute to lung injury. A recent study by Kumagai et al. revealed that administering T-DXd at a dosage exceeding 30 mg/kg led to the onset of ILD in monkeys. The observed histopathological features were similar to those typically seen in ILD during cancer treatment in humans, including widespread lymphocyte infiltration and mild fibrosis. Immunohistochemical analysis showed that T-DXd predominantly localized within alveolar macrophages, with pathological lesions also present in the alveolar interstitial spaces. Notably, HER2 expression was confined exclusively to the bronchial region in monkeys’ lungs [ ].
Examples of such phenomena include T-DM1-induced thrombocytopenia, which is viewed as toxicity unrelated to the intended target, given that platelets and Megakaryocytes (MKs) do not express HER-2. Initially, Uppal et al. proposed that the frequent occurrence of thrombocytopenia associated with trastuzumab emtansine (T-DM1) treatment might arise from interactions between IgG and immune cells, facilitated by the binding of Fc domains to Fc receptors (FcR) on their surfaces. It seems that off-target toxicity is mediated by FcγRIIa, and blocking the interaction between FcγRIIa and T-DM1 prevents its uptake [ ].
However, another study by Zhao et al. determined that the uptake of T-DM1 in differentiated MKs was not dependent on FcγRIIa but was instead mediated through MKs themselves [ ]. Corroborating these findings, a subsequent study by Aoyama et al. demonstrated that blocking FcγRIIa did not alter the cellular uptake and cytotoxicity of monomeric ADCs in the human megakaryoblastic leukemia cell line MEG01-S, which does express FcγRIIa [ ]. Moreover, it was noted that ADC aggregation activated FcγRs, leading to an uptick in uptake and cytotoxicity in cells that express FcγRs but not in cells devoid of FcγRs expression. These results suggest that monoclonal antibody aggregates intensify the activation of FcγRs, and this amplified activation might result in a higher rate of internalization and lysosomal degradation of mAb aggregates by immune cells compared to native mAbs [ , ].
Based on the above-mentioned theoretical evidence, it is unlikely that freely circulating payloads would elicit ILD manifestations comparable to those observed with T-DXd. Furthermore, ADC-associated ILDs are predominantly non-target-related, leading to speculation that off-target effects might be the primary source of toxicity in ADC drugs, specifically through non-target-dependent endocytosis pathways [ ]. While these pathways have been extensively explored in relation to T-DXd, further research is essential for other medications. Our hypothesis suggests that lung macrophages internalize ADCs via a non-target-dependent pathway, which might be the primary mechanism underlying ADC-induced alveolar injury. To better understand the role of FcγR in ADC-induced ILD, studies utilizing ADCs engineered with modified FcγR conjugation could be valuable [ ]. In conclusion, both macroscopic and microscopic processes play roles in the uptake of ADCs in normal cells, potentially leading to off-target toxic effects elicited by these drugs [ ].

## The relationship between macrophage “uptake accumulation” and ADCs-associated interstitial pneumonia
In the human body, macrophages express significant levels of FcγRs, potentially heightening their vulnerability to off-target toxicity in ADC therapy due to Fc-mediated uptake [ ]. Macrophages are essential innate immune cells endowed with the capacity to phagocytose cells and present specific antigens for adaptive immune response activation. Among these, alveolar macrophages (AMs) reside on the lung mucosa surface and act as primary immune defenders within the alveoli [ – ]. AMs, originating from fetal liver monocytes, boast extended lifespans and the ability for self-renewal [ ]. They uphold homeostasis by clearing endogenous proteins such as surfactants and cellular debris and also by mitigating inflammation through the removal of expired cells [ ]. Given the pronounced expression of FcγR in alveolar macrophages and the minimal HER2 expression in respiratory alveoli, there is a theoretical basis to suggest that non-specific uptake via Fc-mediated mechanisms contributes to ADC-induced ILD.
With the continuous iteration and improvement of ADCs therapies, researchers have been working not only on optimizing ADC structures but also on investigating the mechanisms behind various adverse reactions associated with ADC drugs. In recent studies, significant progress has been made in understanding the mechanisms underlying ADC-associated ILD. Existing research has noted that ADC drugs accumulate in alveolar macrophages after entering lung tissues, which is considered to be linked to the adverse event of interstitial pneumonia due to macrophage uptake of ADC drugs or their metabolites. Kumagai et al. demonstrated that HER2-targeted ADC T-DXd is nonspecifically taken up by alveolar macrophages in a cynomolgus monkey model [ ]. However, this study did not explicitly examine the relationship between macrophages and ADC pharmacological properties, though it provided initial evidence that nonspecific macrophage uptake may lead to pulmonary toxicity.
Macrophage phagocytosis can be categorized based on the size of particles involved in different types of endocytosis. These categories include vesicle- and lattice protein-mediated endocytosis, vesicle- and lattice protein-independent endocytosis, and micropinocytosis [ ]. Large-scale endocytosis encompasses both phagocytosis and macropinocytosis. Immune complexes containing ADCs or aggregates of ADCs might also be taken up through this mechanism [ , ]. Upon the introduction of ADC drugs into the body, endocytic events prominently occur on macromolecules. The primary mechanisms involve clathrin-mediated endocytosis (CME) [ ], caveolin-independent fovea-mediated endocytosis, CLIC/GEEC [ ], and giant cytosolic efflux.
In the study conducted by Shigehiro Koganemaru et al. [ ], it was demonstrated that the concentration of TROP2-eribulin (an ADC drug) in lung tissues is independent of tumor target expression and is directly proportional to the administered dose. In animal models, it was observed that deruxtecan without the conjugated structure does not induce drug-induced ILD, suggesting that the pulmonary toxicity of this drug may not be related to HER2 targeting. Additionally, HER2 expression in alveolar tissues is minimal, which does not support the hypothesis that lung damage arises from HER2-targeted drug uptake in lung tissues. Instead, this finding suggests that immune cells might be taking up the drug in a HER2-independent manner, potentially triggering ILD as the primary mechanism underlying this toxicity. This indicates the presence of nonspecific distribution and release of the drug within lung tissues. Immunofluorescence staining results showed that TROP2-eribulin primarily accumulates in CD68+ alveolar macrophages, with a significant increase in ADC concentration in lung tissues correlating with escalating doses. Furthermore, the uptake of ADC by CD68+ alveolar macrophages also exhibited a dose-dependent enhancement. These findings suggest that ADC drugs enter lung tissues via nonspecific uptake by macrophages.
Further experiments utilizing THP-1 cells (which model alveolar macrophages) in vitro revealed that THP-1 cells nonspecifically uptake TROP2-eribulin through Fcγ receptors, subsequently releasing eribulin (the active payload) into the surrounding tissues. Additionally, this study found that treatment with the ADC drug significantly increased the release of TNF-α from THP-1 cells, indicating that nonspecific uptake may lead to an inflammatory response, which is associated with the development of ILD.
This research represents the first in-depth analysis of the distribution of ADCs in lung tissues and their pharmacokinetics of drug payloads, elucidating the pathological mechanisms by which ADCs induce ILD. These findings highlight the critical role of nonspecific uptake and toxic payload release by alveolar macrophages in the pulmonary toxicity associated with ADC therapies.

## Clinical management of ILDs in breast cancer patients treated with ADCs

## NCI grading of drug-induced ILD
ILD induced by ADCs is graded according to the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE) established by the National Cancer Institute (NCI), which aids clinicians in evaluating the severity of drug-induced ILD and determining appropriate management strategies [ , ]. According to the NCI’s adverse event assessment criteria, ILD caused by ADCs is classified into five grades: Grade 1 indicates mild, asymptomatic cases characterized solely by clinical or diagnostic findings that do not require intervention. Grade 2 represents moderate cases with mild symptoms, such as cough or shortness of breath, necessitating intervention (e.g., oxygen support or medication) that impacts instrumental activities of daily living. Grade 3 involves severe symptoms that significantly impair daily activities, requiring hospitalization and potentially more intensive interventions (such as oxygen therapy, particularly when resting SpO2 < 88% or PaO2 ≤ 55 mmHg. Grade 4 denotes life-threatening conditions characterized by severe respiratory distress that may necessitate mechanical ventilation or intensive care, posing significant life-threatening risks. Finally, Grade 5 refers to death directly resulting from ILD [ , , ].

## Clinical presentation, prediction, and assessment
The heterogeneity in symptom presentation and onset time observed in drug-induced ILD aligns with the broad spectrum of ILDs. The results of the DESTINY-Breast01 trial showcased a variable onset of ILD severity, ranging from 63 to 148 days, with treatment-related pulmonary complications typically emerging after an average duration of 6 months [ ]. Therefore, it is prudent to closely monitor patients for any signs or manifestations of respiratory issues and to consult a pulmonologist during T-DXd therapy. Further research is necessary to determine the best approach for follow-up and management of T-DXd-associated ILD. Typical signs of ILDs include exertional dyspnea and persistent cough. However, these symptoms might also be accompanied by dyspnea at rest, chest discomfort, chest tightness, paroxysmal coughing, and fever. In some cases, patients may only present with a cough [ ]. Despite the presence of diffuse lung opacities in imaging studies, some individuals may remain asymptomatic. During pulmonary examinations, crackles are often heard; however, specific physical signs might not always be apparent, as some patients might exhibit “Velcro” sounds or moist rales upon auscultation. Distinguishing drug-induced ILDs from other causes of pulmonary diseases is a significant challenge in clinical practice. The suspicion of drug-induced ILD usually arises from noting the temporal correlation between drug exposure and the onset of clinical symptoms [ ].
In most cases, a conclusive diagnosis of drug-induced ILD remains elusive, even after comprehensive clinical assessment, including lung biopsy. The severity of symptoms associated with drug-induced ILD can range from mild to life-threatening and can escalate rapidly, leading to fatal outcomes. Therefore, a thorough review of the patient’s medical history is vital. Laboratory examinations, such as serological tests and microbiological cultures, may help pinpoint potential infectious or other causes of pulmonary infiltrates in suspected individuals, but they are not definitive for diagnosing drug-induced ILD. Similarly, pulmonary function tests typically reveal a restrictive pattern (with reduced FVC and/or total lung volume) and diminished diffusing capacity characteristic of ILD [ ], assisting in assessing the extent of lung damage but not offering a definitive confirmation of drug-induced ILD.
Additionally, studies suggest that Japanese patients might be more susceptible to developing ILD after treatment with T-DXd.The incidence of drug-related ILD/pneumonia in patients treated with T-DXd was reported to be 15.4%, with a small percentage being severe cases (2.2%). Most of these events were classified as low-grade (grade 1/2, 77.4%) [ ]. Moreover, the authors identified several factors that may increase the risk of drug-associated ILD/pneumonia, such as age below 65 years, Japanese ancestry, existing pulmonary complications, moderate to severely impaired renal function, a time to diagnosis exceeding 4 years, administration of T-DXd at a dose higher than 6.4 mg/kg q5w, and oxygen saturation below 95%. These findings offer potential insights for clinical screening and prevention strategies for ADC-associated ILD prior to initiating treatment with T-DXd. Patients with these risk factors might have a higher likelihood of developing ILD during T-DXd treatment; therefore, healthcare providers should be aware of these factors and take appropriate measures to reduce the risk of ILD during therapy. Another study pooling data from various advanced solid malignancies treated with T-DXd concluded that, among the included cohort of 1193 patients, there were fatalities resulting from adverse events in approximately 10.66% of cases [ ].
In conclusion, the classification of ILD associated with ADC drugs and the analysis of drug administration methods warrant consideration. The incidence of T-DXd-related ILD is significantly higher compared to other ADC drugs, underscoring the need for heightened clinical vigilance on this issue. Clinicians must be cognizant of the risk of drug-induced ILD and employ necessary preventative or intervention measures prior to its manifestation. Factors in patients, such as age, ancestry, and the presence of underlying pathologies or concurrent lung conditions (including history of prior lung surgery, smoking, or pneumonia), may heighten the risk of ADC drug-induced ILD.

## Contribution of imaging in the diagnosis of drug-induced ILD

## Indications for performing CT in patients suspected of having drug-induced ILD
In patients prescribed medications with known potential for pulmonary toxicity, chest CT scans, especially high-resolution thin-section CT scans with section thicknesses ≤2.5 mm, are instrumental in assessing and monitoring pulmonary abnormalities [ ]. It is recommended to promptly initiate CT scanning when there is a confirmed correlation between the patient’s symptoms and drug exposure. These advanced imaging techniques not only facilitate the early detection of reversible drug-induced ILD but also help distinguish other potential causes of the patient’s symptoms. Moreover, CT scanning is invaluable in evaluating non-specific clinical manifestations suggestive of pneumonia, encompassing both community-acquired and healthcare-associated pneumonitis. It further aids in identifying concurrent issues involving common causes of pneumonia, such as community-acquired or healthcare-associated infections.
In two completed single-arm clinical trials, the majority of T-DXd-associated ILD and pneumonia cases were classified as grade 1 or 2, comprising 76.9% (100/130) of the total cohort. A detailed radiological analysis revealed that the most prevalent patterns observed were histopneumonia (63.1% of cases), followed by hypersensitivity pneumonitis (16.9% of cases), and diffuse alveolar damage (DAD) (14.6% of cases). Notably, 11 (8.5%) of these 130 cases resulted in patient mortality due to ILD/pneumonia, with a substantial proportion (8/11, or 72.7%) exhibiting the DAD pattern [ ]. These findings underscore the importance of rigorous monitoring and prompt management in patients receiving T-DXd treatment, and contribute to the growing understanding of the radiological patterns associated with this therapy.
Moreover, patients exhibiting an acute interstitial pneumonitis (AIP) or acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) pattern demonstrated an earlier onset of pneumonia in comparison to other patients. Specifically, the median onset of pneumonia in these patients was 17.9 weeks after treatment, significantly preceding the median onset of 32.7 weeks observed in the general patient population (p = 0.019) [ ]. This noteworthy difference indicates that patients presenting with an AIP/ARDS pattern are at a heightened risk of developing pneumonia, thus necessitating prompt clinical attention and intervention to minimize the potential for severe adverse effects.

## The utilization of HRCT as a significant adjunct in the identification of drug-induced ILD
High-resolution computed tomography (HRCT) is recognized as the primary non-invasive technique for evaluating drug-induced lung disease. The HRCT findings in drug-induced ILD are similar to those seen in other types of ILDs or idiopathic interstitial pneumonia. A common radiographic feature is the presence of bibasilar crackles. Typical patterns include distinct ground-glass or reticulated opacities, airspace consolidation, interlobular septal thickening, a pattern termed honeycombing, and bronchiectasis due to traction forces [ ]. Clinically, HRCT acts as an invaluable diagnostic adjunct for identifying patients with drug-induced ILD. For individuals undergoing cancer treatment, the timely execution of HRCT is essential. It is generally recommended to conduct HRCT before administering antineoplastic drugs to patients, serving as a foundational assessment. Special attention should be given to any respiratory changes in the patient. If there is a deterioration in respiratory health or the emergence of new symptoms, a timely HRCT should be undertaken to rule out other factors contributing to ILD development. In clinical practice, it is advisable to follow standard oncology monitoring protocols and carry out imaging during treatment. For patients post-treatment, adjusting the frequency of HRCT examinations based on the pathological assessment of confirmed drug-induced ILD is clinically recommended.

## The early use of bronchoalveolar lavage with lung biopsy is recommended for suspected ILD patients
Bronchoscopy plays a pivotal role in diagnosing pneumonia and helps identify pneumonia caused by lymphangiocarcinoma, vasculitis, or infectious agents. Conversely, a negative broncho alveolar lavage (BAL) cytology effectively rules out most drug-induced immune reactions [ , ]. Therefore, combining bronchoscopy with alveolar lavage is considered a supplementary diagnostic tool. Studies have shown that drug-induced ILD results in increased lymphocytes, eosinophils, and/or neutrophils in the alveolar lavage fluid, typically characterized by a predominance of CD8+ cells in lymphocytic alveolitis [ ]. Performing an appropriate lung biopsy through bronchoscopy (transbronchial biopsy) or surgical methods can reveal the histopathological pattern of lung injury [ ]. While open lung biopsy remains the gold standard for the histological diagnosis of ILD, it is essential to contemplate the potential early need for this procedure in clinical management due to increased mortality associated with severe respiratory dysfunction and the reliance on mechanical ventilation during a surgical lung biopsy. Given the diverse etiology, intricate pathogenesis, and varied clinical manifestations of ILD, clinicians are recommended to meticulously monitor patients undergoing treatment with ADCs for respiratory abnormalities and to conduct a series of tests as previously described. Some studies have indicated that myeloperoxidases (MPO) levels may be altered during the development of ILD [ , ]. Clinicians should advise follow-up imaging within 1–2 weeks or as clinically indicated and maintain vigilant monitoring of patients until symptoms and/or imaging findings improve. In conclusion, when patients receiving antineoplastic therapy develop new lung lesions temporally associated with drug therapy, the possibility of drug-induced ILD should be considered, along with a range of differential diagnoses.

## Other investigations
Krebs von den Lungen-6 (KL-6), a mucin-like glycoprotein primarily secreted by type II alveolar lung cells and bronchial epithelial cells, plays a pivotal role in the biological response to injury and regeneration associated with ILD [ , ]. A recent scientific investigation has established a robust correlation between the type of lung injury and serum KL-6 levels in patients with drug-induced lung disease. Specifically, the study demonstrated a significant elevation in KL-6 levels among patients whose chest HRCT scans exhibited patterns consistent with DAD or cryptogenic organizing pneumonia (COP). Conversely, in patients presenting with lung injury patterns characteristic of bronchiolitis obliterans organizing pneumonia (BOOP/EP) or hypersensitivity pneumonitis (HP), KL-6 levels did not demonstrate a comparable increase [ ]. These findings indicate that KL-6 may serve as a reliable biomarker for discriminating between specific types of lung injury in DILD, thereby enhancing the scientific accuracy of diagnosis and guiding clinically informed management strategies.

## Treatment and management of drug-induced ILD
For the accurate diagnosis or management of T-DXd-induced ILD/pneumonia, please refer to the FDA guide issued in 2021 [ ]. This guide recommends that in cases of asymptomatic ILD/pneumonia (Grade 1), temporary discontinuation of T-DXd is advised until resolution to Grade 0 is achieved. If resolution occurs within a span of 28 days or less from onset, the dosage remains unchanged; however, if resolution takes longer than 28 days, the dosage should be reduced by one level.
It is noteworthy to mention that, in patients treated with corticosteroids, a significant proportion exhibited a favorable therapeutic response, thereby corroborating the beneficial role of corticosteroids in mitigating the symptoms of this particular type of pneumonia. Crucially, the timing of corticosteroid administration plays a pivotal role in determining its therapeutic outcome, with optimal results typically observed when administered during the early inflammatory stage of the disease. This observation underscores the crucial significance of a timely and strategic approach in utilizing corticosteroids for the management of drug-induced lung disease.
In situations of symptomatic ILD/pneumonia, immediate cessation of T-DXd is recommended, accompanied by the initiation of corticosteroid therapy. For patients with symptomatic/Grade 2 or higher drug-induced ILD, discontinuation of T-DXd is recommended. Consider initiating corticosteroid therapy, such as prednisolone or an equivalent, at a dose of ≥1 mg/kg/day for a minimum duration of 14 days or until complete resolution of clinical and imaging findings. After this period, gradually taper the corticosteroid dosage over at least 4 weeks. If there is no improvement or if clinical indications persist, reevaluate imaging results and consider increasing the corticosteroid dose to 2 mg/kg/day (prednisolone or equivalent) and transitioning to intravenous administration, such as methylprednisolone [ , ].
Management of interstitial lung disease/pneumonitis associated with anti-HER2 ADCs requires immediate discontinuation of ADCs upon confirmed or suspected diagnosis, with grading according to CTCAE 5.0 standards. For mild (Grade 1) cases, ADC therapy should be paused, and low-dose glucocorticoids (e.g., prednisone at 0.5 mg/kg/day) are recommended with reassessment within two weeks. Treatment may resume upon complete resolution; however, if remission takes longer than four weeks, a dose reduction is advised. For moderate (Grade 2) cases, ADCs should be discontinued, and treatment with prednisone at 1 mg/kg/day should be initiated. If symptoms improve within one week, glucocorticoid therapy should be continued until full resolution, with gradual tapering over at least four weeks. Once symptoms are reduced to Grade 0–1, ADC therapy may resume at a reduced dose. For severe (Grade 3–4) cases, ADCs should be permanently discontinued, and patients should be hospitalized with high-dose glucocorticoid therapy (e.g., methylprednisolone 500–1000 mg/day IV for 3 days), followed by maintenance with prednisone at 1 mg/kg/day. Broad-spectrum antibiotics may be considered to prevent infection; if glucocorticoids are ineffective, additional immunosuppressants such as infliximab may be used. Even if symptoms of severe cases resolve completely, re-administration of ADCs is generally not recommended. This management strategy integrates clinical experience and research data from Chinese patients, aiming to minimize ADC-related pulmonary injury while enabling patients to continue benefiting from treatment [ ].

## Conclusion
The prospects for the use of ADCs in breast cancer and other solid tumors are increasingly bright as numerous studies continue to delve into their potential, and a growing array of novel products are being developed. ADCs that specifically target the human epidermal growth factor receptor or Trop-2 show significant promise as therapeutic options for patients diagnosed with breast cancer. The emergence of ADC-based drugs has notably enhanced the treatment options available for breast cancer patients over the past decade. Thus, it can be inferred with considerable certainty that ADCs are poised to establish themselves as an innovative cornerstone in the field of oncology in the coming years. As these exceptional ADC medications continue to gain widespread recognition and acceptance as promising cancer treatments, their efficacy against tumors is likely to eclipse that of conventional therapies. The literature has expanded impressively due to breakthroughs achieved in the last two decades concerning ADC design, development, and research, with a particular focus on breast cancer. These pivotal advancements have reshaped treatment approaches and expanded traditional classifications with the introduction of a potentially revolutionary subtype known as “HER2-low.”
Currently, a plethora of promising ADC drugs are undergoing clinical trials for breast cancer patients. The advancement of cutting-edge techniques enhancing the uptake of ADCs by cancer cells has been instrumental in their development. Despite the impressive efficacy demonstrated by highly potent ADCs, as evidenced by the evolutionary trajectory of these therapeutic agents, there remain significant challenges that warrant careful attention. These challenges include addressing drug resistance and effectively managing treatment-related adverse effects (such as ILDs) during dosage administration. These can potentially hinder therapeutic progress and have a substantial impact on tumor prognosis.
Therefore, the identification of primary lung disease and ILD/pneumonia associated with ADC therapy, along with the prevention and management of corresponding adverse events, requires the formulation of comprehensive diagnostic and management principles. Given the lack of specific clinical indicators for ADC drug-induced ILD, it is often overlooked or misdiagnosed during initial evaluations. Beyond clinical symptoms, a meticulous review of the patient’s medical history is paramount to exclude other potential contributing factors. Enhancing clinical examinations for the early detection and diagnosis of anti-tumor therapy-related ILD is essential in managing ADC-associated ILD. In the absence of standardized guidelines for identifying and treating ADC pulmonary toxicity, clinicians must tailor their approach based on individual circumstances, keeping in mind general ILD guidelines. However, with the growing prevalence of ADC drug therapy, there is an urgent need to expedite the development and refinement of guidelines for diagnosing and managing ADC-associated ILD.
