EgyptoDanielle Christinne https://orcid.org/0000-0003-0657-4825dos Reis-NetoEdgard Torres https://orcid.org/0009-0009-0051-3424CarlessoGuilherme Pereira MouraLuiz Antonio AndradeLuis E C https://orcid.org/0000-0001-5535-3223CalderaroDebora https://orcid.org/0000-0001-8540-014XSatoEmilia Inoue Clinical and lab tests rarely predict lupus nephritis (LN) histology accurately; thus, kidney biopsy—considered the ‘gold standard’—is recommended for LN diagnosis and classification whenever feasible. Treatment decisions are based on biopsy results. European Alliance of Associations for Rheumatology (EULAR) recommends renal biopsy with any sign of renal involvement, such as proteinuria ≥0.5 g/24 hours plus glomerular haematuria or cellular casts. The American College of Rheumatology (ACR) suggests biopsy if renal involvement presents as elevated creatinine without clear cause, proteinuria ≥1.0 g/24 hours or proteinuria ≥0.5 g/24 hours with haematuria and/or casts. The Brazilian Society of Rheumatology (SBR) advises biopsy when 24 hours proteinuria or urinary protein/creatinine rate (UPCR) ≥0.5, unexplained rise in creatinine (>30%) or decrease in glomerular filtration rate (GFR) or glomerular haematuria with proteinuria <0.5 g/24 hours are present. Despite evidence of clinical-histological discordance, definitions of renal response usually omit histological analysis. 
Monitoring LN follows international guidelines, using biomarkers such as anti-dsDNA, serum complement (C3, C4), urinalysis with sediment microscopy, 24-hour proteinuria or UPCR and serum creatinine. While these are practical and accessible, they have limitations in distinguishing active from inactive LN and differentiating LN from comorbidities like diabetes or hypertension in SLE patients. 
Kidney rebiopsy has revealed persistent histological activity despite good clinical/laboratory response in LN. Some studies suggested that rebiopsy should become a standard procedure for defining response after immunosuppressive treatment; identifying patients who will need to prolong or intensify therapy, as well as avoiding treatment beyond what is necessary in those already having a complete histological response. 
EULAR recommends rebiopsy for cases with worsening or refractoriness to standard treatment and in cases with relapses to identify changes or progression of histological classes, changes in Activity Index (AI) and Chronicity Index (CI), assess prognosis and detect other associated pathologies. 
Urinary biomarkers are easily obtained and can reflect renal inflammatory activity. They are better than serum tests in evaluating renal involvement in LN. However, the development of urinary biomarkers has been limited by inconsistent assays, processing differences and lack of standardisation of normal values. Monocyte chemoattractant protein 1 (MCP-1) and TNF-related weak inducer of apoptosis (TWEAK) are biomarkers detected in active LN. The combination of both can be used to differentiate between active and inactive LN, to predict progression to chronic kidney disease and to evaluate therapeutic response. Higher levels of urinary interleukin 16 (IL-16) were described in SLE patients with active LN, especially in proliferative classes, with a decrease after treatment, suggesting it to be a biomarker for evaluating therapeutic response. Urinary lipocalin-associated neutrophil gelatinase (NGAL) levels correlate with LN activity, predict renal flare and response to treatment in lupus patients cohorts. Kidney injury molecule 1 (KIM-1) levels are elevated in patients with active LN and correlate with proteinuria and kidney injury. Activated leucocyte cell adhesion molecule (ALCAM) levels in urine distinguished proliferative from membranous forms of LN. ALCAM and vascular cell adhesion molecule 1 showed a strong correlation with histological activity and may indicate a decline in kidney function. Adiponectin, ceruloplasmin, haemopexin, MCP-1, NGAL and KIM-1 constitute a set of biomarkers of the Renal Activity Index Lupus (RAIL), used to predict LN activity in SLE patients. 
Few studies have assessed biopsy and rebiopsy after induction therapy in LN or their association with serum and urinary biomarkers. When a kidney biopsy is contraindicated, emerging biomarkers that correlate with clinical and histological responses may eventually serve as alternatives.
The main objective is to evaluate the correlation between clinical response and kidney histological response in the second biopsy, after induction therapy, and to correlate serum and urinary biomarkers with clinical, histological response, histological classes, AI and CI.

## Methods
Open, longitudinal, prospective, multicentre study carried out in university hospitals of the Federal Universities of São Paulo, Paraíba and Minas Gerais from 2020 to 2023. SLE patients (Systemic Lupus International Collaborating Clinics 2012 criteria) , aged ≥18 years attended outpatient clinics of university hospitals with kidney involvement and indication of renal biopsy according to EULAR/ACR/SBR criteria were invited to participate. After signing the consent form, urine and blood samples were collected immediately, and the renal biopsy was scheduled. In three cases where patients could not adhere to this schedule due to logistical reasons, urine and blood samples were collected within 1 week after the biopsy. No patients began induction treatment before renal biopsy. Patients who have active LN classes III, IV, V, III+V or IV+V, confirmed through first renal biopsy were included in the study.
Exclusion criteria were pregnancy; long-standing diabetes mellitus (DM) (>10 years of type 1 DM or >20 years of type 2 DM or with target organ damage) or insulin-dependent or contraindication for kidney biopsy.
This project was approved by the research ethics committee (Project number 20008019.2.0000.5505) of the three participating universities.

## Kidney biopsies
Kidney biopsies were conducted by nephrologists or interventional radiologists. The initial biopsy (T1) diagnosed LN, and the second (T2) was performed within 3 months post induction therapy. A single nephropathologist (LAM) evaluated all samples per the International Society of Nephrology and Renal Pathology Society (ISN/RPS) criteria. Specimens underwent standard preparation and were analysed by light microscopy and immunofluorescence. Samples were fixed, embedded in paraffin, sectioned and stained with H&E, periodic acid-Schiff, Masson’s Trichrome and Jones Silver. For immunofluorescence (IF), tissue was frozen, sectioned and incubated with fluorescein-conjugated reagents for detection of immunoglobulins (Ig) (A, G, M), C1q, C3c, fibrinogen and Kappa/Lambda light chains.
Samples with eight or more glomeruli were considered representative. The National Institute of Health (NIH) AI and CI were evaluated in each biopsy. 

## Treatment protocol and assessments
For induction therapy, all patients received pulse therapy with methylprednisolone (500 mg/day–1 g/day for 3 days), followed by oral prednisone at a dose of 0.5 mg/kg/day (creatinine≤1.5 mg/dL) or 1 mg/kg/day (creatinine>1.5 mg/dL), with a decrease until 5–10 mg/day by the sixth month of treatment. Except for those with formal contraindications, hydroxychloroquine (HCQ) was prescribed at a dose of 5 mg/kg up to a maximum of 400 mg/day. Patients with 24-hour proteinuria >1 g received an ACE inhibitor or an angiotensin receptor blocker.
The choice of immunosuppressant for induction treatment was made by the attending physician with: cyclophosphamide (CYC) NIH (0.5–1 g/m2 of body surface, intravenously, monthly, for 6 months); CYC Euro-Lupus (500 mg every 2 weeks, intravenously, for 3 months) or mycophenolate mofetil (MMF) 2–3 g/day, orally, for 6 months. Maintenance therapy was performed with MMF 1–2 g/day or azathioprine (AZA) 2 mg/kg/day and maintained for at least 3 years.
Patients were evaluated monthly until completing induction therapy or biweekly for those with Euro-Lupus schedule. Clinical evaluation included questionnaire with sociodemographic, clinical and laboratory data (blood count, urea, creatinine, estimated creatinine clearance, erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR), C-reactive protein (CRP), urine analysis and 24-hour proteinuria) at each visit. C3, C4, anti-dsDNA, anti-nucleosome, anticardiolipin IgM and IgG antibodies and serum albumin were evaluated at baseline and at the end of induction treatment. At baseline and after induction therapy, serum and urine were also collected for serum (anti-C1q, KIM-1, MCP-1, NGAL) and urine (TWEAK, MCP1, NGAL, KIM-1, adiponectin, ceruloplasmin, haemopexin, IL-16 and ALCAM) biomarkers evaluation by ELISA. After centrifugation and separation of the supernatant, samples were stored at −80 °C and the tests (TWEAK, KIM-1, adiponectin, NGAL and ceruloplasmin (Elabscience) and MCP-1, anti-C1q, haemopexin, IL-16 and ALCAM (AFG Scientific)) were carried out in duplicate at the same time by a biomedical scientist ( ).
Target renal response (TRR) according to II Brazilian Rheumatology Society LN consensus was used to evaluate response to treatment and includes improvement or maintenance of renal function (±10% of baseline serum creatinine) combined with a decrease in 24 hours proteinuria or 24 hours UPCR of 25% at 3 months, 50% at 6 months and proteinuria <0.8 g/24 hours at 12 months. Disease activity was assessed by Systemic Lupus Erythematosus Disease Activity Index-2K (SLEDAI 2K) at baseline and after 6 months. Lupus Low Disease Activity State and remission (Definition of Remission in SLE (DORIS)) were evaluated at the end of the study.

## Statistical analysis
Data were analysed descriptively. Categorical variables are shown as counts and percentages; numerical variables are summarised by mean and SD or median and quartiles. Associations between categorical variables were tested using χ2 or Fisher’s exact tests. The standardised adjusted residual test identified local differences. Data normality was assessed with Kolmogorov-Smirnov, and means were compared via Student t-test or Mann-Whitney test for small groups. Spearman correlation measured associations between numerical variables. R values<0.5 indicated weak, 0.5–<0.7 moderate and ≥0.7 strong correlations. McNemar and Wilcoxon tests compared distributions and means from two assessments. A 5% significance level was accepted. Analyses employed IBM SPSS Statistics V.20.0.

## Results

## Demographic and clinical features
24 patients (21 women, 17 non-white) underwent two kidney biopsies. The main reason for exclusion of patients was the absence of histological activity (16 patients) at T1, despite presenting clinical and/or laboratory changes that justified renal biopsy. Among them, only two patients had proteinuria below 1 g, both with dysmorphic haematuria. The second reason was the absence of representative renal cortex material. Three patients died during the study, one due to complications from COVID-19, one due to SLE activity (alveolar haemorrhage and renal insufficiency) and the other one due to perirenal haematoma and sepsis ( ).
 The mean age was 32.5±8.2 years, and the disease duration was 6.6±6.3 years. Arterial hypertension was the most common comorbidity and in 62.5% it was the first episode of LN (mean disease duration of 59.8 months). Regarding the medications, 87.5% were using HCQ, 43.5% immunosuppressants and 87.5% were using antihypertensives at first biopsy. CYC NIH was used by 66.7% and MMF by 29.2% as induction therapy ( ).
 18 patients (75%) achieved TRR. Three patients did not reach TRR due to the 24-hour proteinuria criterion, two to the creatinine criteria and one patient due to both criteria.
14 patients (58.3%) achieved histological response (AI=0); of these, four (28.5%) did not reach the TRR due to failing creatinine (two cases), proteinuria (one case) or both criteria (one case).
We found 12 discordances between clinical and histological responses. Eight patients achieved TRR without histological response and four patients with histological response without TRR. Among eight patients with TRR without histological response, five presented with haematuria and among four patients with TRR without histological activity, two presented with haematuria at T2 ( ) and ).
 Disease activity assessed by SLEDAI-2K increased in only one patient at T2. Eighteen patients presented a decrease in more than three points and in five patients there was no change in disease activity score. No patient achieved remission criteria (DORIS) in the last month of the study.

## Kidney biopsy
Class IV(±V) was found in 62.5% biopsies, AI ranged from 1 to 10, and CI ranged from 2 to 6 in T1. Change in the histological class from the first to the second biopsy was identified in 58.3% of patients. We observed 21 patients (87.5%) with classes III or IV(±V) in the first biopsy and 12 (50.0%) in the second biopsy. It was also observed that 77.8% of patients decreased the AI and 33.3% increased the CI at T2 ( ).
 At the second renal biopsy, five patients did not exhibit immune deposition as assessed by immunofluorescence. As a result, these patients could not be classified according to the LN classification criteria, and therefore they were not included in . None of them showed any signs of active disease. However, three patients presented varying degrees of chronic morphologic alterations, suggesting previous renal changes. Additionally, one patient showed evidence of chronic nephropathy. Among these five patients, four exhibited some arterial intimal hyperplasia, indicating changes in the arterial walls. Three of five patients presented negative anti-dsDNA. Only one patient showed evidence of complement consumption (low C3 and C4 levels), and this individual was negative for anti-dsDNA antibodies.

## Serum and urinary biomarkers
Regarding the serum and urinary biomarkers, there was a difference in 24-hour proteinuria (p<0.001), C3 (p=0.033) and C4 (p=0.011) between T1 and T2. At T2, 45.8% presented normal urinary sediment and 40.0% showed normalisation of C3 level. An evaluation of treatment outcomes showed that 18/24 (75%) experienced a reduction of at least 50% in their 24-hour urine protein excretion after 6 months of induction therapy. There were differences in means only for the relative variation of 24-hour proteinuria with a greater mean reduction in the group that achieved the TRR (−33.6±58.9 X −80.6±14.5; p=0.004).
20 out of 24 patients (83.3%) presented a reduction of 25% in the 24-hour proteinuria after 3 months, and 18/24 (75%) presented a decrease of 50% in the 24-hour proteinuria after 6 months, compared with the baseline. After 12 months of follow-up, 14/24 (58.3%) presented 24 hours proteinuria<0.8 g. We observed that most patients who achieved the proteinuria criterion of TRR at 12 months had achieved this criterion in 3 months (65%) and in 6 months (66.6%). Only one patient who did not achieve the TRR proteinuria criterion at 3 months and two patients who did not achieve this criterion at 6 months achieved the TRR proteinuria criterion at 12 months. 24 hours proteinuria at 3 months and 6 months was related to lower proteinuria at 12 months in the present study ( ).
Concerning the first biopsy, there was a weak negative correlation between C3 (p=0.045) and C4 (p=0.046) with creatinine. Regarding serum biomarkers, a weak negative correlation was found between MCP-1 and urea (p=0.030) and creatinine (p=0.015); a strong positive correlation between NGAL and urea (p<0.001) and a moderate positive correlation with creatinine (p<0.001) and a moderate positive correlation between KIM-1 and urea (p=0.001) and with creatinine (p=0.002). Urine ceruloplasmin showed a moderate negative correlation with AI (p=0.010) ( , ).
A higher number of patients with low C3 was observed in patients with haematuria compared with those without haematuria (88.9%×33.3%; p=0.018). When comparing patients with and without nephrotic proteinuria, we found a higher frequency of positive anti-dsDNA (76.5%×16.7%; p=0018) and antinucleosome (94.1%×50%; p=0.040) in those with non-nephrotic proteinuria, without difference in relation to other biomarkers.
Regarding biopsy 2, we observed a weak negative correlation between anti-dsDNA and urea (p=0.032) and creatinine (p=0.049); weak positive correlation of serum KIM-1 with creatinine (p=0.030) and moderate with proteinuria (p=0.014) and a weak positive correlation with urinary KIM-1 and proteinuria (p=0.029). Urinary adiponectin showed a moderate negative correlation with urea (p=0.016) and creatinine (p=0.021) and moderate positive correlation with AI (p=0.025). Urinary ceruloplasmin showed a moderate positive correlation with proteinuria (p=0.012) ( , ).
 There were differences in urinary TWEAK means per haematuria in T2. The group with haematuria had a higher urinary TWEAK mean than the group without haematuria (13.8±5.7 vs 7.7±6.2, p=0.043).
There were differences in mean anti-dsDNA and distinct distributions of anti-dsDNA (p=0.018) and antinucleosome (p=0.040) at T1, by variation in proteinuria at T2. Thus, the group with improvement in proteinuria had a lower mean of anti-dsDNA level (p=0.044) and lower percentages of positivity in anti-dsDNA (16.7%×76.5%; p=0.018) and antinucleosome (50.0%×94.1%; p=0.040) compared with the group without improvement.

## Discussion
This study evaluated the correlation between TRR and histological response and between TRR and serum and urinary biomarkers after induction therapy. We found discordance between clinical and histological response, with 50% of patients with good clinical-laboratory response showing histological activity. A lower percentage of patients achieved histological response without TRR. The discrepancy could be higher because of the short interval between the two biopsies.
We also observed a change in histological class between the first and second biopsy in 58.3% of patients. It is known that a change in histological class can occur during the follow-up of LN patients; however, we identified a high percentage of class change in a short interval of time. We also observed that five patients could not be classified using LN classification criteria because they did not present immunofluorescence positivity at T2 biopsy, but they cannot be classified as normal biopsy due to the presence of some chronic alteration.
In 62.5% of patients this was the first episode of LN. The mean disease duration was 59.9 months, which is longer than reported in the literature showing the first episode of LN occurring 6–36 months after diagnosis of the disease in most lupus patients, which may be its initial presentation. 
Most of the discordance that we observed was due to the clinical response without histological response. Our results are in line with those found in studies by Alsuwaida A et al , Zickert A et al , De Rosa M et al and Malvar A et al. 
We observed that five patients among eight (62.5%) who achieved clinical response with histological activity presented haematuria at T2. However, two (50%) patients with histological response without clinical response also presented haematuria at T2. Therefore, haematuria could not reduce discordance.
CYC was the immunosuppressant used for induction therapy in 66.6% of cases. This is justified because in Brazil, mycophenolate was provided free of charge through the public health system only in 2023. Despite this, we observed that 75% of patients had achieved TRR after the induction therapy, even with 70% of patients being non-white.
We found that traditional biomarkers, such as 24-hour proteinuria and complement level, showed significant variations in relation to the two renal biopsies. At T2, we observed a reduction in 24-hour proteinuria and an increase in C3 and C4, while the new serum and urinary biomarkers did not show significant variations in our study. Our results differ from some studies in the literature, which show changes in biomarkers after therapy, presenting them as good predictors of disease activity and response to treatment. However, the kits used to measure these biomarkers were different from those used in some of the studies, and this can be one of the reasons for differences in results. Another factor that could have contributed to the negative finding is the small number of patients in the present study.
We observed that a decrease in 24-hour proteinuria is important for evaluating renal response after induction therapy, reinforcing data from studies showing a decrease in proteinuria as the best predictor of clinical response and renal survival. However, although the reduction in proteinuria is shown to be the best biomarker of renal response, it is not 100% reliable, as it may be altered in acute and chronic lesions in renal tissue. 
When correlating the biomarkers measured at T1 with the variation in haematuria and 24-hour proteinuria at T2, we found that high positivity and high levels of anti-dsDNA and high anti-nucleosome positivity at T1 were correlated with lower improvement of proteinuria after induction therapy. These results suggest a lower chance of achieving the TRR in the presence of high titres of anti-dsDNA and antinucleosome at T1. Our findings differ from those reported by Fava et al, in which higher levels of anti-DNA antibodies were associated with an increased chance of treatment response. This contrast highlights potential differences in patient populations, treatment schedule or methodologies that may influence the relationship between antibody levels and treatment outcomes. 
When we separated patients according to the clinical response and correlated with creatinine, 24-hour proteinuria, urinary sediment abnormality, C3, C4 and the value of new serum and urinary biomarkers, we found significant differences only for the 24-hour proteinuria variation in the subgroup that reached the TRR.
In a longitudinal study, Noa Schwartz et al compared urinary TWEAK with traditional biomarkers such as anti-dsDNA, C3 and C4 in SLE, and identified higher levels of TWEAK in patients with LN than without LN and controls. The authors concluded that urinary TWEAK was higher during renal flare than 4–6 months before or after flare. 
Our study did not confirm the results reported by Moloi et al who found urinary TWEAK and MCP-1 levels reduction in 17 patients who achieved complete or partial renal response after induction therapy. Additionally, this study also did not find an association between these biomarkers and histological findings. However, this study presents some strange results, such as 25% of patients being treated only with steroids as induction treatment, and they reported 85% patients with partial or complete response.
When we performed the correlation of biomarkers with the level of urea, creatinine, 24-hour proteinuria, proliferative and membranous histological classes and AI and CI, we found that serum NGAL showed strong correlation with urea and moderate correlation with creatinine, while serum KIM-1 showed a moderate correlation with urea and creatinine. NGAL seems to be a marker of impaired renal function in LN. In a systematic review and meta-analysis, urinary NGAL demonstrated to be useful for LN diagnosis, for assessing disease activity and for predicting renal flares. However, no correlations of serum NGAL with creatinine and urea have been reported. Studies with a larger number of patients must be carried out to confirm this correlation.
Nozaki et al evaluated the correlation between urinary (u-KIM-1) and tubular KIM-1 (t-KIM-1) and disease activity, histological findings, 24-hour proteinuria value, serum creatinine and estimated GFR in LN and observed patients with active LN had higher KIM urinary and tubular levels than those with inactive LN and healthy controls. u-KIM-1 correlated with proteinuria and tubular damage. 
At T1, we observed that C3 consumption and anti-dsDNA positivity were more frequent in the group with non-nephrotic proteinuria. Antinucleosomes were also found more frequently in patients with non-nephrotic proteinuria. It is well known the association of these autoantibodies with active nephritis, mainly in proliferative classes. The small number of patients in the present study could not confirm the association between autoantibodies and LN class.
There were no differences in the urinary biomarkers that constitute RAIL in T1 by renal clinical response. However, urinary adiponectin showed correlation with AI in T2.
Brunner et al found that the combination of MCP-1, NGAL, TWEAK, adiponectin, ceruloplasmin and haemopexin was the best predictor of LN activity in juvenile lupus. Gulati and collaborators validated the RAIL panel in adults with LN. Brunner et al in a longitudinal study evaluated RAIL biomarkers after 12 months of induction therapy and found that RAIL biomarkers were higher in non-responders than in responders. 
Unfortunately, we did not evaluate sCD163, a promising biomarker for LN described more recently. 
The main limitation of our study is the small number of participants, because of technical problems in performing the renal biopsy, with a considerable number of patients with insufficient and unrepresentative renal cortical material; followed by the exclusion of patients without renal histological activity, although they had laboratory tests suggesting active LN and had indication for a renal biopsy. Another limitation is the absence of sCD163 analysis and the use of commercial kits which are different from those used in some previous studies. This discrepancy can introduce variability in the results, making it challenging to directly compare findings across studies. The choice of kits and their inherent differences may influence assay performance, sensitivity and specificity. These limitations highlight the importance of conducting comparative studies that use kits from various suppliers. Such efforts would facilitate better understanding of kit-to-kit variability and support the development of standardised protocols. Improved standardisation across different commercial kits is essential to enhance the reliability and comparability of biomarker studies.
Traditional biomarkers have proven efficiency in determining active nephritis, mainly 24-hour proteinuria, haematuria, serum complements, anti-dsDNA and anti-nucleosome, and these biomarkers should continue to be used, considering that they are accessible in the routine. A previous study showed anti-C1q and anti-DNA in high levels was associated with the proliferative form of LN. 
In our study, we find that these new biomarkers are technically difficult to measure, have high costs, not all biomarkers’ kits are worldwide available, and the results could be different according to the commercial kits suppliers. Therefore, it is necessary to do more studies with a larger sample size to define the role and importance of these biomarkers for routine use.
Our study found a high rate of discordance between clinical-laboratory and histological responses after induction therapy, highlighting the ongoing importance of renal biopsy for LN diagnosis and monitoring. A decrease in 24-hour proteinuria was linked to TRR, supporting its role as a reliable response marker post therapy. However, the lack of correlation between biomarkers and baseline histological parameters, which is inconsistent with prior studies, renders the biomarker findings inconclusive.
