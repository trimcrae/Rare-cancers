## Abstract

## Background
Mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs)-based and MSCs-derived extracellular vesicle (EV) therapies, have garnered significant attention as potential treatments for severe Coronavirus disease 19 (Covid-19), leading to evaluation in numerous clinical trials. This interest stems from the observed ability of MSCs, and their EVs, to mitigate the hyper-inflammatory state that characterizes severe cases of the disease. However, despite the promising preclinical and early clinical results, the precise mechanisms of action remain unclear, hindering therapy optimization.

## Methods
This study investigated the response of Wharton’s jelly-derived mesenchymal stem cells (WJ-MSCs) to a diverse panel of sera collected from multiple hospitalized Covid-19 patients, aiming to recapitulate in vitro the spectrum of inflammatory conditions MSCs encounter in vivo.

## Results
Exposure to Covid-19 serum activated WJ-MSCs, increasing proliferation and RNA/protein synthesis. Transcriptomic analysis revealed an early and significant downregulation of the inflammation-related long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs) NEAT1 and MALAT1. This downregulation of NEAT1 and MALAT1, triggered by Covid-19 serum, suggests a potential mechanism by which WJ-MSCs may limit excessive inflammation. Importantly, reducing MALAT1 (and to a lesser extent NEAT1) expression in WJ-MSCs enhanced the therapeutic potential of MSC-derived EVs. This suggests that targeting pro-inflammatory lncRNAs in parental cells could be a promising therapeutic strategy for managing Covid-19 and other inflammatory lung diseases.

## Background
Mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) offer promising therapeutic potential for tissue and organ repair across a wide range of clinical conditions, including cardiovascular, neurological, musculoskeletal, and immune diseases [ ]. This efficacy stems primarily from their ability to secrete a diverse array of soluble bioactive molecules and extracellular vesicles (EVs), which facilitate tissue repair and modulate inflammation [ , ]. Notably, EVs, nanoscale particles that encapsulate and deliver therapeutic cargo like growth factors, cytokines, and nucleic acids, including microRNAs, provide a safer, cell-free alternative to MSCs transplantation, mitigating the risks associated with live cell therapy [ ].
Interestingly, MSCs adjust their paracrine responses depending on their local microenvironment, showing an adaptive transcriptomic plasticity that mediates the therapeutic responses of these cells. Inflammatory cytokine pretreatment enhances the immunomodulatory properties of MSCs [ – ]. Moreover, cytokine priming is a useful strategy for harvesting anti-inflammatory MSCs-derived EVs (MSCs-EVs) for clinical applications [ , , ]. In the context of severe Covid-19 disease, characterized by a hyperinflammatory “cytokine storm”, clinical trials have demonstrated the safety and efficacy of MSC and MSCs-EVs infusions in both children and adults [ – ]. However, despite observed clinical benefits, the precise mechanisms underlying the therapeutic efficacy of MSCs and MSCs-EVs in Covid-19 remain largely unclear. This knowledge gap hinders the optimization of these therapies and limits the ability to predict patients’ response.
The in vitro stimulation or priming of MSCs with patient serum —an approach previously established to recreate the systemic pathological microenvironment of a number of diseases, including the Covid-19 disease [ – ] — was adopted in this study to simulate the abnormal signaling WJ-MSCs encounter post-infusion in hospitalized Covid-19 patients. Here, we present the first evidence that Covid-19 serum priming activates WJ-MSCs, leading to a notable downregulation of two pro-inflammatory lncRNAs, NEAT1 and MALAT1. This finding suggests a potential cellular mechanism for restoring homeostasis, which is particularly striking given that high levels of these lncRNAs have previously been detected in Covid-19 patient samples [ , ]. This inverse regulation in MSCs could represent their adaptive response to the inflammatory milieu.
Furthermore, ASO-mediated knockdown of NEAT1 and MALAT1 in WJ-MSCs led to EVs with improved ability to inhibit T-cell activation, indicating increased immunomodulatory potential. These findings contribute to the understanding of the mechanisms by which MSCs modulate inflammation in Covid-19 patients and suggest that lncRNA targeting represents a promising therapeutic approach for MSCs or EVs-based therapies.

## Methods

## Patients and clinical parameters
Serum samples were obtained from patients admitted to the Pulmonology and/or Intensive Care Units (ICU) due to Covid-19 between January and February 2021 who were willing to donate samples. Healthy control serum samples were obtained from individuals prior to the Covid-19 pandemic. No specific exclusion criteria were applied to the patients for this study; detailed patient and healthy control donor information is provided in Supplementary Table 1.

## WJ-MSCs culture
Commercial WJ-MSCs were cultured under standard growth medium: DMEM low glucose supplemented with GlutaMAX and pyruvate (Ref: 21885108; ThermoFisher), fetal bovine serum (FBS) 10% (Ref: A5256701, Gibco) and 100 U/ml of penicillin/streptomycin (p/s) (Ref: 15140122; ThermoFisher).
Physiological and Covid-19 microenvironments were modeled in vitro using standard growth medium supplemented with either 10% Control (for physiological conditions) or Covid-19 patient serum (for Covid-19 conditions) instead of FBS.

## WJ-MSCs proliferation under serum priming
WJ-MSCs were seeded in quadruplicates in white-walled 96 well plates with clear bottoms at 1,000 cells/well in standard growth medium. 24 h after seeding cells were washed with PBS, and standard growth medium supplemented with either Control or Covid-19 patient serum at 10% (instead of FBS) was added to each quadruplicate, along with 2x RealTime-GLO™ MT Cell Viability Assay (Promega). Cells were incubated at 37 °C, 5% CO2 and viability (emitted luminescence) measurements began 1 h after the addition of all components to wells (day 0). The luminescence was then measured every 24 h for a period of 3 days. On day 4, the media was replaced by a new growth medium with the corresponding sera of healthy controls and patients and 2x RealTime-GLO™ MT Cell Viability Assay. Luminescence was measured on the sample plate during 2 additional days. For all experiments, the measurements were done using a GloMax-96 microplate luminometer (Promega, Madison, WI, USA). Luciferase reads of MSCs in starvation medium were used as blank control and the data obtained in day 0 were used to normalize the remaining measurements. The experiment was repeated three times, using each time sera from 16 sera from control donors and 15 sera from Covid-19 affected patients. The results are expressed as the median of these 3 independent experiments.

## Secretome isolation
Wharton’s jelly-derived MSCs (WJ-MSCs) were seeded in 24-well plates at a density of 20,000 cells per well and cultured in complete standard growth medium. After 24 h, the growth medium was removed, and the cells were washed and subsequently primed with either Covid-19 or Control sera samples for a period of 48 h. Following priming, the sera-containing media were removed, and the WJ-MSCs were incubated in serum-free media for 24 h to allow for secretome collection. The collected secretome was then centrifuged at 2,000 x g for 5 min to remove any cellular debris, and the supernatant was aliquoted and stored at −80 °C for further use.

## Protein measurements
Protein content in PBS-diluted secretome obtained after 48 h of priming with either Control sera or Covid-19 sera samples and subsequent overnight starvation, was determined by the Pierce™ BCA protein assay kit (Thermo Fisher) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, working reagent was prepared by mixing Reagent A and Reagent B in a 50:1 ratio. Samples, including starvation media as negative control and protein standards (Bovine Serum Albumin, BSA), were added in duplicates to a 96-well microplate, followed by the prepared working reagent. The plate was then incubated at 37 °C for 30 min, and absorbance was measured at 562 nm using a microplate reader.

## Immunofluorescence
WJ-MSCs were seeded in chamber slides at a density of 20,000 cells per well. The following day, the cells were washed with PBS, and then standard growth medium supplemented with either 10% Control sera or 10% serum from Covid-19 patients was added for 48 h. Then, the cells were fixed for 10 min at room temperature (RT) using 4% paraformaldehyde. Following fixation, the cells were washed three times with PBS and then permeabilized for 30 min at RT with 0.2% Triton X-100. Next, the cells were washed with PBS and blocked with 3% BSA in 0.05% Triton X-100 for 30 min at RT. The cells were incubated with the mouse monoclonal primary antibodies anti-Sec31a (BD, Ref: 612350) diluted 1:50 in blocking buffer overnight at 4 °C. After being washed three times with PBS, cells were incubated with the secondary antibody Alexa Fluor 594 anti-mouse, (Invitrogen) diluted 1:500 in blocking buffer for 1 h at RT. After being washed thrice with PBS, the coverslips were mounted on top of the chamber, adding one drop of mounting medium with DAPI (P36966 Invitrogen) to stain cell nuclei for visualization. Finally, fluorescence images were obtained in a confocal microscopy (ZEISS LSM880 AIRYSCAN - objective 20X).

## Human antibody arrays
The expression profiles of growth factors in pooled secretomes derived from WJ-MSCs primed for 24 h with either Control (n = 5) or Covid-19 (n = 5) sera samples and subsequently subjected to overnight starvation were analyzed. Specifically, a RayBio C series human growth factor antibody array (Ref: AAH-GF), designed for the semi-quantitative detection of 41 human proteins in cell culture media, was used according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Each growth factor was represented in duplicate spots on the array membrane. Briefly, array membranes were blocked in 2 mL of blocking buffer for 1 h. Then, the membranes were incubated with 1 mL of pooled secretomes from Control-primed or Covid-19-primed WJ-MSCs overnight at 4 °C. The samples were then decanted from each container, and the membranes were washed 3 times with 2 mL of wash buffer at RT with shaking. Subsequently, the membranes were incubated with biotinylated detection antibodies (as per manufacturer’s instructions) overnight at 4 °C. Following this incubation, the membranes were subjected to a second wash, 3 times with 2 mL of wash buffer at RT with shaking. Subsequently, the membranes were incubated in a 1:2,000 dilution streptavidin-horseradish peroxidase at RT for 30 min. After thorough washing, the membranes were exposed to a peroxidase substrate prior to imaging.

## RNA sequencing (RNA-seq)
WJ-MSCs were seeded at a density of 200,000 cells in T-25 flasks. The following day WJ-MSCs were primed with Control sera (n = 16) or Covid-19 sera samples (n = 47) for 24 h. It is important to specify that while the Covid-19 sera used for priming the MSCs originated from 37 distinct patients, some of these patients contributed multiple samples collected at different time points (e.g., 2 or 3 samples per patient). This resulted in a total of 47 Covid-19 sera samples utilized to prime WJ-MSCs for RNA-seq analysis. Detailed patient information for both the Control and Covid-19 sera samples, including specifics on longitudinal collections, is provided in Supplementary Table 1. After treatment, cells were detached with Triple Select and centrifuged at 300 x g for 5 min. The resulting cell pellets were then processed for total RNA extraction using AllPrep DNA/RNA/miRNA Universal Kit (QIAGEN) following manufacturer’s instructions. RNA quantity and integrity were subsequently evaluated using an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer, revealing RIN values above 9.4 in all samples. For RNAseq, total RNA (0.5 µg) was processed using the TruSeq stranded total RNA with Ribo-Zero kit in order to perform ribosomic RNA depletion and cDNA library synthesis. The sequencing of the cDNA library was performed in a high throughput platform (Macrogen), targeting 60 million reads per sample. The Illumina sequencer generated raw images and performed base calling through the integrated primary analysis software RTA (Real Time Analysis). The BCL (base calls) binary was converted into FASTQ with the Illumina package bcl2fastq. The FASTQ files were encoded using the standard Sanger Phred + 33 quality scale. Across all the samples, the total number of bases per sample ranged from approximately 9.1 to 13.0 billion, reflecting a high sequencing depth with an average of 74,761,698 reads per sample (range: 60,575,088 to 86,077,002 reads). Adapters were not trimmed from the reads. Raw sequencing reads were subsequently subjected to quality control. The base composition showed balanced GC content, ranging from 49.86% to 53.46%, indicating minimal bias in nucleotide distribution. Sequencing quality was consistently high, with the percentage of bases above Q20 exceeding 98% for all samples (mean Q20 = 98.31%) and the percentage of bases above Q30 averaging 95.27%, reflecting low base-calling error rates. These quality metrics demonstrate that the RNA-seq data are of high fidelity and suitable for downstream transcriptomic analyses. RNA-seq reads were aligned to the human reference genome GRCh38 using HISAT2 [ ]. The aligned reads were assembled into putative transcripts with Cufflinks [ ], and in-house pipeline software was employed to integrate the expression results into a single text file for downstream analysis in MATLAB (MathWorks). The data were log-transformed and quantile-normalized following established practices in transcriptomic analysis [ ].

## Quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR) validation
For mRNA expression analysis, 1 µg of total RNA was reverse-transcribed into complementary cDNA using the SuperScript IV VILO cDNA synthesis kit (Invitrogen) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR) was performed using Brilliant III Ultrafast SYBR Green qPCR master mix (Agilent). Gene expression was normalized relative to glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) mRNA, and the relative expression of each gene of interest was calculated by the 2⁻ΔCtt method. The specific primer sequences utilized for these analyses were: ANGPTL4 (forward: 5’-CACAGCCTGCAGACACAACTC-3’, reverse: 5’-GGAGGCCAAACTGGCTTTGC-3’), NEAT1 (forward: 5’-CGGCACCTAGCATGTTTGAC-3’, reverse: 5’-CGCCAAACCTAGAGAAAAGTCC-3’), MALAT1 (forward: 5’-CTTCCCTAGGGGATTTCAGG-3’, reverse: 5’-GCCCACAGGAACAAGTCCTA-3’), and GAPDH (forward: 5’-CAAGGCTGAGAACGGGAAGC-3’, reverse: 5’-AGGGGGCAGAGATGATGACC-3’).

## Functional enrichment analysis
Significantly dysregulated genes (both up- and down-regulated) were analyzed using IPA software to identify enriched biological processes. GO analysis was also performed using DAVID (https://david.ncifcrf.gov/home.jsp). Biological processes with a P-value < 0.05 were considered significantly enriched.

## GapmeRs design
LncRNA gene knockdown was performed using custom-designed Locked Nucleic Acid Antisense Oligonucleotides (LNA ASOs) purchased from Exiqon (Qiagen). Specifically, LNA GapmeRs, a type of LNA-ASO with enhanced endonuclease resistance, targeting MALAT1 (catalog #339515 LG00000003-DDA) and NEAT1 (catalog #339511 LG00806407-DDA), were employed. As a negative control, Negative Control A LNA GapmeR was used in all the experiments (catalog # 339515 LG00000002-DDA).

## WJ-MSCs transfection
Lipofection was performed on WJ-MSCs at 60–70% confluence using Dharmafect 1 transfection reagent (Dharmacon) following the instructions from the manufacturer. The recommended amount of Dharmafect 1 to transfect WJ-MSCs was mixed with serum free DMEM supplemented with GlutaMAX and pyruvate. On the other hand, to prepare the GapmeR mix, each of the GapmeRs (at 10 nM) or GapmeRs combinations was also mixed with serum-free DMEM supplemented with GlutaMAX and pyruvate. After 5 min of incubation at RT, GapmeR mix was added dropwise on top of the Dharmafect mix, and incubated for 20 min at RT. The mix was then complemented with growth medium and added to the previously seeded WJ-MSCs dropwise. 24 h after transfection, cells were washed once with PBS and fresh culture medium was added during 48 h. To recover secreted EVs, cells were grown in EVs recovery medium: DMEM low glucose supplemented with GlutaMAX and pyruvate, p/s + 10% FBS previously depleted of EVs (Exo-free). We performed three independent knockdown experiments for SC, NEAT1, and NEAT1 + MALAT1 and two for MALAT1.

## EVs isolation
EVs released by knocked-down WJ-MSCs were isolated using size exclusion chromatography (SEC). Briefly, the obtained secretome (≈ 80 mL per sample) was concentrated up to 1 mL using 100 kDa Amicon filters. Then, 500 µL of this concentrated sample was loaded onto a qEV original 35 nm column (IZON Science, USA) and eluted with freshly filtered PBS, collecting 24 fractions of 500 µL each in low protein binding tubes. Following cleaning of the column with 0.5 M NaOH and then PBS, the remaining 500 µL of secretome was processed using the same procedure. Bradford assay analysis indicated that soluble proteins began to elute in fraction 13, with the bulk eluting between fractions 15 and 21. NTA analysis was performed to determine the concentration and size of the isolated EVs in each fraction. Based on this, only fractions 7 through 11 were pooled and subsequently concentrated to 100 µL via ultracentrifugation (100,000 x g, 70 min). EVs samples were aliquoted and stored at −80 °C for subsequent analysis.

## Cryo-electron microscopy (Cryo-EM)
For cryo-EM analysis, pooled samples of isolated EVs from at least 2 independent experiments of knocked-down MSCs were vitrified using glow-discharged holey carbon grids (blotted at 95% humidity) from Quantifoil, and liquid ethane (Vitrobot system; MaastrichtInstruments). After vitrification, samples were imaged using a JEM-2200FS/CR transmission cryo-EM (JEOL) at liquid nitrogen temperature and at an acceleration voltage of 200 kV.

## Western blot
Samples were prepared using Bolt 4X LDS sample buffer (NuPAGE, Life Technologies) and heated at 70 °C for 10 min. EVs (≈ 1 µg/sample, equivalent to ≈ 4 × 109 EVs) and WJ-MSC extracts (100 µg) were loaded onto 4–12% Bis-Tris Precast gels (NuPAGE, Life Technologies) and electrophoresed in 1X MES buffer (NuPAGE, Life Technologies) for 35 min at 200 V. Proteins were transferred to polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes (Immobilon-FL, 0.45 μm, IPFL00010, Millipore) using an iBLOT2 transfer device (Thermo Fisher Scientific) with the P3 program for 7 min at 20 V. Membranes were allowed to air-dry for 1 h at RT prior to blocking, to maximize protein retention on the membrane. Membranes were then blocked for 1 h at RT in LI-COR Intercept Blocking Buffer (927–70001). Subsequently, membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with specific conjugated primary antibodies: ExoBrite™ 680/700 CD81, (Ref #P006-680) and ExoBrite™ 770/800 Calnexin (Ref P007-770) (Biotium), diluted in Intercept Blocking Buffer containing 0.1% Tween-20. After three washes with TBS-T, membranes were scanned using the LI-COR Odyssey M imaging system.

## Nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA)
Nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA) was conducted using the ZetaView® instrument to determine the concentration and size of the isolated EVs according to the manufacturer’s instructions. To ensure optimal particle concentration for analysis, all samples were diluted in particle-free, filtered 10% PBS to a final volume of 1 ml. The manufacturer’s default software settings for EVs were selected accordingly. For each measurement, one cycle was performed by scanning 11 randomly chosen positions of particle distribution and capturing 60 frames in scatter mode per position using the following settings: frame rate: 30 fps; number of frames recorded: 60; sensitivity: 80; exposure: 100; minimum brightness: 30; minimum pixel size: 10; maximum pixel size: 10,000; trace length: 30; temperature: 25 °C.

## Multiplex bead-based flow cytometry analysis
For semi-quantitative analysis of 37 different surface protein markers in EVs samples, we used a commercial multiplex bead-based flow cytometry kit (MACSPlex MSCs-EVs kit, human, Miltenyi Biotec) according to manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, for each sample of EVs, 1 × 10⁹ particles were diluted with MACSPlex Buffer to a final volume of 120 µL and mixed with 15 µL of MACSPlex exosome capture beads (FITC-PE), which contain beads coupled to 37 exosomal surface epitopes and 2 control isotypes. Then, 15 µL of MACSPlexExosome Detection Reagent cocktail of APC fluorescent antibodies against tetraspanins (CD9, CD63, CD81) was added. The mixture was incubated for 1 h at RT with light protection on an orbital shaker (450 rpm). As a control (blank), MACSPlex buffer alone was incubated with the beads and detection antibodies. Following two washing steps (3000 x g, 5 min) with MACSPlex Buffer, a total of 150 µL of the samples were transferred to flow cytometry tubes and acquired using a MACSQuant Analyzer 10. Median Fluorescence Intensity (MFI) in the APC channel was measured.

## T cell activation assay
To measure the proliferation of lymphocytes in the presence of EVs, PBMCs were labeled with 5-(and-6)-carboxyfluorescein diacetate succinimidyl ester (CFSE; Molecular Probes, Thermo Fisher Scientific) at a final concentration of 5 µM. Then, PBMCs were seeded in triplicates for each condition (2 × 105 cells/well) in round-bottomed 96-well plates and cultured for five days in the presence of 2 µg/well of phytohemagglutinin (PHA) and EVs (5 × 1010 part/mL). Cells were collected from each well and transferred to 5-mL FACS tubes for analysis by flow cytometry using the MACSQuant Analyzer 10 (Miltenyi Biotec). Raw data were analyzed using FlowJo.

## Statistics
All results are expressed as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM), with sample size (n) for each group specified in the respective figure legends. The Shapiro-Wilk test was performed to assess the normality of the data distribution for each variable. To compare Covid-19 serum and Control serum groups, statistical significance was determined using a two-tailed Student’s t-test for data that passed the normality test, and Mann-Whitney U test for non-normally distributed data. In the case of RNA-seq data, to identify the differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between two groups of samples, the mean expression values of each probe were computed across all samples in each group. Transcripts with an absolute difference in mean expression below a predefined threshold (θDEG = 2 in log2 scale) were all filtered out. The remaining genes were subjected to statistical analysis using Student’s t-test to determine significant expression. For comparisons involving multiple groups (lncRNAs knockdown and subsequent EVs experiments), a one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed, followed by post-hoc Tukey’s test to account for multiple comparisons and control the risk of Type I error. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05. Data analysis and graph generation were performed using GraphPad Prism.

## Results

## Covid-19 serum priming elicits an enhanced activation and secretory function in WJ-MSCs
To elucidate the impact of the Covid-19 microenvironment on WJ-MSCs, these cells were cultured with serum derived from hospitalized Covid-19 patients exhibiting varying degrees of lung involvement. As a control, WJ-MSCs were also primed with Control serum isolated from healthy individuals, collected prior to the Covid-19 pandemic (Supplementary Tables 1 and Fig. A). Initial morphological features were assessed 24 h post-serum priming, a timepoint where differences were most pronounced and representative. Bright-field microscopy revealed that Control sera-stimulated MSCs exhibited a characteristic elongated, fibroblast-like morphology. In contrast, WJ-MSCs primed with Covid-19 sera appeared rounder with a more granular and less filamentous cytoplasm (Fig. B). To explore this, we studied the proliferation capacity of WJ-MSCs following exposure to Control or Covid-19 sera samples. The data revealed a gradual increase in proliferation for both groups over time; however, Covid-19 serum priming consistently resulted in a significantly higher proliferation index at days three, four and five irrespective of disease severity (Fig. C and Supplementary Fig. 1). Moreover, 24-hour treatment with Covid-19 serum led to a significant rise in total RNA in WJ-MSCs when compared to Control serum-primed WJ-MSCs (Fig. D). This initial boost in cellular activity was further supported by the increased total protein concentration observed in the secretome of Covid-19 serum-primed WJ-MSCs after 48 h of priming when compared to cells stimulated with Control serum (Fig. E). To further investigate these changes in the secretome, we examined the cellular machinery involved in protein trafficking. Immunofluorescence imaging highlighted notable differences in the accumulation and subcellular distribution of Sec31a, a crucial protein for COPII vesicle budding from the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) to Golgi as part of the secretory pathway [ ], between WJ-MSCs primed with Control serum and those treated with Covid-19 serum after 48 h of priming. WJ-MSCs primed with Control serum displayed diffuse cytoplasmic distribution of Sec31a consistent with its typical ER localization. Conversely, WJ-MSCs exposed to Covid-19 serum exhibited a brighter and more punctate Sec31a staining pattern throughout WJ-MSCs cytoplasm (Fig. F). Consistent with these broad changes in protein secretion, antibody arrays further demonstrated a specific increase in Hepatocyte Growth Factor (HGF), a crucial tissue repair growth factor, within the secretome of Covid-19 serum-primed WJ-MSCs (Fig. G).
 

## Global transcriptional profiling reveals significant alterations in WJ-MSCs induced by Covid-19 serum
To examine the early global transcriptional changes induced by Covid-19 serum priming in WJ-MSCs, we performed bulk RNA sequencing on cells primed for 24 h with either Control sera (n = 16) or Covid-19 sera (n = 44). Principal Component Analysis (PCA) (Fig.  A) revealed a clear separation between WJ-MSCs primed with Control serum (blue dots) and those primed with Covid-19 serum (red dots). The two groups exhibited distinct clustering along the first principal component (PC1, 11% variance) and the second principal component (PC2, 4.5% variance). This demonstrated a significant divergence in their gene expression profiles, underscoring a robust and distinct transcriptional response of WJ-MSCs to Covid-19 serum. Contrary to expectations, subsequent PCA analysis utilizing clinical lung affectation to categorize WJ-MSCs responses based on Covid-19 severity, revealed a lack of clear separation between patient groups categorized as mild (green), moderate (yellow/orange), and severe (red) (Fig.  B). This observation implies that a specific molecular signaling related to a Covid-19 infection in the serum was the primary driver of the observed transcriptional changes in WJ-MSCs, regardless of whether the donor had mild, moderate, or severe lung involvement.
 Comparative transcriptomic analysis using volcano plot identified 74 significantly differentially expressed transcripts (p < 0.05; Fold Change > 2) in Covid-19 primed WJ-MSCs relative to the Control-primed WJ-MSCs (Fig.  C). Specifically, 35 transcripts were upregulated, and 39 downregulated. Heatmaps further illustrated these gene expression patterns, with each column representing the transcriptional response to individual serum samples, highlighting the consistency of changes across the different biological replicates (Fig.  D). To validate these RNAseq results, we performed qPCR for the most upregulated gene, ANGPTL4, and the two most downregulated lncRNAs (NEAT1, MALAT1), confirming the consistency of the expression patterns observed (Fig.  E).
Subsequently, to identify enriched biological pathways within the differentially expressed genes (DEGs), we conducted independent analyses of upregulated and downregulated gene lists. Notably, Ingenuity Pathway Analysis (IPA) revealed that priming with Covid-19 serum induced a significant enrichment of pathways associated with lipid metabolism, glycosaminoglycan biosynthesis, and anti-inflammatory signaling in WJ-MSCs (Fig.  A). Conversely, downregulated genes were enriched in categories related to protein ubiquitination, collagen assembly, and cell cycle regulation. To gain deeper insights into the biological processes impacted by Covid-19 serum in WJ-MSCs, Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis was conducted using the DAVID (Database for Annotation, Visualization and Integrated Discovery) platform (Fig.  B). The upregulated genes showed enrichment in GO terms including “lung development,” “response to vitamin D,” “mTOR signaling pathway,” “epithelial cell proliferation” and “decidualization” indicating the potential activation of developmental, immune-modulatory and secretory pathways. Moreover, the enrichment of GO categories related to “mitochondrion” and “reactive oxygen species metabolic process” within upregulated genes indicated changes in cellular energy and stress responses, while “negative regulation of apoptotic process” suggested enhanced cell survival. Downregulated genes exhibited enrichment in GO terms such as “integrin binding” and “collagen-containing extracellular matrix,” which corroborated the IPA-identified downregulation of collagen assembly pathways, implying a modulation of cell-matrix interactions. Notably, the enrichment of the “regulation of inflammatory response” category within downregulated genes aligned with IPA findings, whereas “lncRNA-mediated post-transcriptional gene silencing” category mirrored the observed decrease in NEAT1 and MALAT1, two long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs). Remarkably, the downregulation of these lncRNAs, was uniformly observed across all WJ-MSCs primed with Covid-19 sera, differing significantly from Control sera-primed WJ-MSCs (Fig.  C).
 

## Effects of NEAT1 and/or MALAT1 knockdown on WJ-MSCs secreted EVs
The consistent downregulation of NEAT1 and MALAT1 observed in all Covid-19 serum-primed WJ-MSCs, alongside their known role in promoting inflammation across various pathological contexts [ – ], prompted us to investigate whether mimicking this effect via ASO-mediated knockdown in WJ-MSCs could enhance their immunomodulatory potential, a function largely attributed to their secreted EVs [ ]. Therefore, we aimed to determine the impact of NEAT1 and MALAT1 knockdown on EVs characteristics. To this end, we employed 10 nM locked nucleic acid (LNA)-Gapmer antisense oligonucleotides (ASOs) targeting NEAT1, MALAT1, or both in WJ-MSCs, using a scrambled ASO (SC-ASO) as the experimental control (Fig. A). qPCR analysis 48 h after transfection demonstrated the knockdown efficiency of the ASOs. While NEAT1 expression was slightly reduced in WJ-MSCs, the MALAT1 expression was markedly attenuated following treatment with ASOs (Fig. B). Notably, no cross-regulation was observed between NEAT1 and MALAT1 expression, as silencing one did not affect the other, indicating that NEAT1 and MALAT1 are transcribed and regulated independently (Fig. C). Simultaneous silencing of NEAT1 and MALAT1 exhibited a consistent and slightly synergistic effect (Fig. C).
 We then isolated the EVs present in the secretome of control (EVsSC), NEAT1-knockdown (EVsNEAT1), MALAT1-knockdown (EVsMALAT1) and NEAT1/MALAT1-double knockdown (EVsNEAT1+MALAT1) WJ-MSCs using size exclusion chromatography, followed by an ultracentrifugation step, pooling those SEC fractions enriched with EVs (F7-11) (Fig.  A). Nanoparticle tracking analysis showed that the majority of particles in all conditions exhibited diameters compatible with EVs, ranging from 100 to 200 nm (Fig.  B). Cryo-EM further verified the presence of intact, spherical EVs, with characteristic bilayer lipid membrane and nanoscale dimensions (Fig.  C). Western blot analysis confirmed the enrichment of the established EVs marker CD81 in all EV preparations, while the absence of the endoplasmic reticulum marker Calnexin indicated minimal cellular contamination (Fig.  D).
 The presence of surface markers on EVs was further assessed through semiquantitative bead-based flow cytofluorimetric analysis using the MACSPlex exosome kit (Fig.  A and B). The expression of the tetraspanins CD9, CD63, and CD81, alongside MSCs-associated markers like CD73, CD90, CD105 and CD44 was confirmed in the isolated EVsSC, EVsNEAT1, EVsMALAT1, EVsNEAT1+MALAT1(Fig.  B). Adhesion-related markers (CD49E and CD166) and immunomodulatory markers (CD200, CD59) also showed notable signal (Fig.  B). As expected, hematopoietic markers (CD45, CD79a, CD14, CD19, CD11b) exhibited very low expression, validating the purity of the EV preparations.
 

## NEAT1 or MALAT1 silencing in WJ-MSCs enhanced EVs immunomodulatory potential, which was lost upon double silencing
Considering the reported roles of NEAT1 and MALAT1 in modulating pro-inflammatory signaling [ , ], we investigated whether their knockdown in WJ-MSCs enhanced the immunomodulatory potency of their derived EVs. To this end, carboxyfluorescein succinimidyl ester (CFSE)-stained peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were cultured in the presence of the mitogen phytohemagglutinin (PHA) alone or in presence of PHA plus EVsSC, EVsNEAT1, EVsMALAT1 or EVsNEAT1+MALAT1 for 5 days (Fig. A). Unstimulated PBMCs served as a control to determine the baseline fluorescence of undivided cells. As described, PHA stimulation significantly increased PBMCs proliferation compared to unstimulated cells (Fig. A, B & C). Treatment with EVsSC showed a slight, non-significant reduction in PHA-induced proliferation, which indicated a baseline inhibitory effect of these EVs at the tested concentration (5 × 1010 particles/ml, as determined by NTA analysis). EVsNEAT1 significantly reduced the proliferation of PHA-stimulated PBMCs, exhibiting a more potent inhibitory effect compared to EVsSC. Interestingly, EVs from MALAT1 knockdown cells showed the greatest reduction in the proliferation of PHA-activated PBMCs, surpassing even the effect of EVsNEAT1. However, the combined knockdown of NEAT1 and MALAT1 did not significantly inhibit the proliferation of PHA-stimulated PBMCs compared to PHA alone.
 

## Discussion
The demonstrated capacity of MSCs to adapt their paracrine signaling in response to the microenvironment offers a compelling strategy for the development of enhanced cell-free therapies. It is well established that under inflammatory settings, MSCs shift towards an anti-inflammatory profile [ , ]. Thus, exposing MSCs to proinflammatory disease-relevant signals, such as human serum isolated from systemic lupus erythematosus or graft versus host disease, has been shown to enable the production of EVs with tailored and amplified immunomodulatory properties [ , , , , ].
In this study, we investigated the effect of a brief 24-hour priming duration in the early in vitro transcriptomic response of WJ-MSCs within a Covid-19-like microenvironment, a clinically relevant timeframe informed by in vivo tracking studies in inflammatory acute lung injury models showing peak MSCs signal within the first 24 h post-infusion [ ]. Our findings collectively demonstrate that Covid-19 serum induces a significant reprogramming of WJ-MSCs, characterized by the activation of pathways related to development and metabolism alongside the suppression of those involved in cell-matrix interactions. We observed that in vitro stimulation with Covid-19 sera samples led to a multifaceted activation of WJ-MSCs, evidenced by increased cell proliferation, enhanced protein translation and secretion, and substantial transcriptomic changes.
Key early transcriptomic alterations included the early upregulation of ANGPTL4, an MSC-secreted anti-inflammatory factor [ ] and the downregulation of pro-inflammatory lncRNAs NEAT1 and MALAT1, whose high levels have been detected in Covid-19 patient samples [ , ]. These combined findings strongly suggest an early, adaptive anti-inflammatory response by WJ-MSCs. This aligns with a previous study, which reported that priming WJ and BM-MSCs with sera from critically ill Covid-19 patients enhanced the production of immunoregulatory molecules within the initial 24 h of culture [ ], further supporting an early mechanism by which MSCs may modulate hyperactive immune responses in these patients. Beyond its anti-inflammatory role, ANGPTL4, also plays roles in angiogenesis and tissue remodeling [ ]. This dual function underscores the potential of a WJ-MSCs-based paracrine approach for Covid-19 intervention. Further supporting this, the conditioned medium of Covid-19 primed WJ-MSCs exhibited increased levels of HGF. HGF is a soluble factor secreted by MSCs known for its critical role in attenuating lung injury via mechanisms that facilitate tissue repair, diminish inflammation, and enhance lung function [ , ].
Several host lncRNAs are induced by viruses and drive antiviral responses including cytokine production, which in turn can impact virus invasion and pathogenesis [ ]. The consistent downregulation of NEAT1 and MALAT1 across all tested Covid-19 sera, regardless of patient disease severity, treatment, or comorbidities, suggests a common triggering mechanism in WJ-MSCs, likely related to a baseline level of inflammatory signaling characteristic of hospitalized Covid-19 patients. Supporting this assumption, gene enrichment analyses, utilizing both IPA and DAVID tools, consistently highlighted significant alterations in pathways critical for immune modulation and inflammation responses (e.g., IPA: IL-4/IL-13 signaling; DAVID: response to LPS and regulation of inflammatory response). This convergence strongly suggests that Covid-19 priming profoundly impacts on how WJ-MSCs interact with the immune environment. Moreover, both in silico analyses pointed towards changes in the ECM, indicating a significant remodeling of the cellular microenvironment and cell-matrix interactions, potentially affecting MSCs migration and paracrine signaling. In fact, both tools also highlighted alterations in cellular metabolism. IPA revealed significant enrichment in specific metabolic pathways such as nicotinate metabolism and adipocyte differentiation, while DAVID indicated an increase in ROS metabolism and mitochondria, pointing towards cellular stress. Intriguingly, the DAVID-identified upregulation of genes associated with lung development and epithelial cells proliferation suggests a potential training effect of Covid-19 sera, priming MSCs to secrete factors that could actively contribute to lung repair and regeneration. In addition, our transcriptomic analysis of WJ-MSCs primed with Covid-19 sera identified the concomitant upregulation of both SCT2 and PTGS2 within the significantly enriched decidualization pathway, alongside ANGPTL4. Considering ANGPTL4’s crucial involvement in endometrial angiogenesis and receptivity during decidualization [ , ], these findings point to a potentially coordinated and novel decidual-like reprogramming [ , ] occurring in these WJ-MSCs in response to robust pro-inflammatory signals. Strikingly, the RNA-seq analysis uncovered an unexpected enrichment of lipid metabolism and ROS-related pathways, indicating an early trend towards an altered metabolic demand after Covid-19 priming in WJ-MSCs. This metabolic shift is particularly relevant as it aligns with the understanding that decidualization, the differentiation of endometrial stromal cells into specialized secretory decidual stromal cells involving significant metabolic reprogramming in amino acids and lipids [ , ], is essential for establishing the anti-inflammatory and tolerogenic microenvironment vital for successful pregnancy. Further supporting this molecular observation were morphological changes: the Covid-19 primed WJ-MSCs displayed a flatter, more cuboidal shape and increased granularity, mirroring changes seen during the initial stages of decidualization in endometrial stromal cells [ ]. This intriguing possibility of a decidual-like transition of WJ-MSCs after Covid-19 sera priming warrants dedicated future investigation.
While our study provides valuable insights, it is important to acknowledge a potential limitation regarding the age range of our patient cohort. Our healthy donor group had a relatively narrow age range (41–59 years), whereas the Covid-19 patient serum samples were derived from individuals with a broader age spectrum (40–70 years). Although this variability reflected the real-world demographic diversity of Covid-19 patients, it could introduce age-related factors into the observed WJ-MSC responses. As age can influence immune profiles and cellular functions, potentially impacting MSCs responses to inflammatory stimuli. However, it is notable that despite the inherent variability across samples, including age differences, the transcriptomic downregulation of NEAT1 and MALAT1 was remarkably homogeneous across all Covid-19 serum samples, which strengthens the robustness of this specific finding. Future studies using strictly age-matched cohorts would still be beneficial to precisely delineate the specific effects of the disease state independent of age-related factors.
Using MSC-EVs as therapy addresses the safety concerns of live cell administration, enables efficient tissue penetration due to their nanoscale size, and can bypass microvascular entrapment that often limits cell-based therapy efficacy. Building on the known regulatory functions of NEAT1 and MALAT1 in gene expression, particularly concerning pro-inflammatory cytokines [ ], and their observed therapeutic anti-inflammatory effects when silenced in vivo in preclinical mouse models of lung injury [ – ], we aimed to test whether reducing their expression in WJ-MSCs would enhance the immunomodulatory potential of their secreted EVs.
Individual knockdown of NEAT1 or MALAT1 showed varying efficiencies, with NEAT1 silencing being less efficient. This difference may be attributed to the distinct secondary and tertiary structures of these lncRNAs; NEAT1 forms paraspeckles, nuclear bodies involved in RNA processing and retention, whose complex structures might render NEAT1 less accessible to ASOs compared to MALAT1 [ ]. Interestingly, knockdown of one lncRNA had no effect on the other’s expression, indicating that in WJ-MSCs, NEAT1 and MALAT1 expression is regulated by independent signaling pathways. The enhanced immunomodulatory potential of EVs derived from WJ-MSCs with single NEAT1 or MALAT1 knockdown, particularly being higher in the case of MALAT1 knockdown, underscores the therapeutic potential of targeting these lncRNAs. Future evaluation of the expression levels of key cytokines and NEAT1 and MALAT1-regulated genes in response to EVs treatment would further deepen our understanding of the immunomodulatory mechanisms and therapeutic potential of these EVs.
On the other hand, the lack of an additive effect in the combined NEAT1 and MALAT1 knockdown suggests a complex interplay between these lncRNAs in EV-mediated immunomodulation. This could potentially involve distinct or overlapping signaling pathways where simultaneous knockdown might disrupt a specific pathway crucial for the enhanced immunomodulatory effects observed with individual knockdowns. While our current findings on PBMC activation lay an important foundation, a limitation of our study is the lack of direct quantification of key cytokine expression levels and downstream genes regulated by NEAT1 or MALAT1 in PBMCs following treatment with EVs. Given the critical role of cytokine expression and pro-inflammatory mediators in Covid-19 disease severity, we recognize the value of such data. Future studies will aim to fully elucidate the immunomodulatory mechanisms and therapeutic potential of these EVs.

## Conclusion
This study provides a valuable in vitro model that reflects the complex microenvironment MSCs encounter when infused into Covid-19 patients. The significant transcriptional changes induced by Covid-19 serum, particularly the modulation of pathways related to inflammation, tissue repair, and metabolism, indicate that MSCs undergo a functional reprogramming in response to the Covid-19 microenvironment. Understanding these changes is crucial for optimizing EVs-based therapies, as it could reveal avenues to enhance their therapeutic potential. The observation that EVs from NEAT1- or MALAT1-knockdown MSCs display enhanced immunomodulatory potential holds significant implications for in vivo applications. In conclusion, our findings lay critical groundwork for advancing therapeutic strategies. By specifically targeting lncRNAs in parental WJ-MSCs, we can enhance the immunomodulatory potential of their EVs, offering a promising avenue for the treatment of Covid-19 and other inflammatory conditions.
