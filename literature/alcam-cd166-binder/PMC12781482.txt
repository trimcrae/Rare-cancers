## Abstract

## Background
Alterations in N-glycosylation are frequently observed in breast cancer metastasis but are not well understood. Recently, decreases in expression of the Golgi α-mannosidase I MAN1A1 have been linked to worse patient prognosis in breast cancer.

## Methods
To examine MAN1A1 expression in breast cancer progression, we developed and conducted MAN1A1 immunostaining of breast cancer tissue microarrays (TMAs), which were analyzed using pathology-guided object classifiers, which identified normal breast epithelial cells, primary tumor cells, and metastatic tumor cells.

## Results
MAN1A1 protein expression was significantly increased in primary tumor and local lymph node metastasis for a cohort of patients without distant metastases (N = 48). Changes in MAN1A1 protein expression along the metastatic path were context-dependent, as well as associated with survival for a rare, limited cohort of terminal metastatic breast cancer patients (N = 16). For these patients, an increase in MAN1A1 expression from normal breast to primary tumor was concomitant with decreased survival time (p = 0.032, hazard ratio = 2.68), as was a decrease in MAN1A1 expression from primary tumor to metastasis (p = 0.0018, hazard ratio = 3.45).

## Conclusion
In our pilot study, MAN1A1 protein expression is a significant actor in progression along the metastatic path, supporting further study into MAN1A1 as driver of breast cancer patient survival.

## Introduction
Breast cancer is the most common form of cancer, making up 11.9% of all new cancer diagnoses and 24.5% of new cancer diagnoses in women globally in 2020 [ ]. It was also the fourth leading cause of cancer-related death after lung, liver, and colorectal cancers and the leading cause of cancer-related death in women globally in 2020 [ ]. While 5-year survival rates are > 90% for stages I and II breast cancer in the United States, they drop significantly to 75% for stage III and 29% for stage IV [ ], which represent local and distant metastasis, respectively [ ]. Metastatic disease is difficult to treat for a variety of reasons, chief among them changes to or loss of molecular marker proteins (receptors) or signaling pathways used to target cancer cells with traditional hormone therapy or targeted therapies [ , ]. Furthermore, access to high quality care and other health disparities further decrease survival rates in metastatic breast cancer, with black women having the lowest survival rates for metastatic breast cancer in the United States [ ]. Metastatic breast cancer therefore presents the greatest hurdle in breast cancer treatment and reducing morbidity and mortality due to breast cancer [ ].
To form distant metastases, cancer cells from the primary tumor must invade through the basement membrane, intravasate into blood vessels or lymphatic vessels, and then disseminate to other organs by traveling in these vessels, where they extravasate from the vessel and into the tissue [ – ]. Breast cancer therefore progresses from local disease confined to the breast, to local metastasis in nearby lymph nodes, to growth in distant organs throughout the body (including distant lymph nodes). This metastatic path corresponds to the current TNM staging system, wherein cancer is defined by primary tumor size (T), degree of lymph node involvement (N), and the presence of distant metastases (M) [ ].
While metastasis is widely studied, the molecular mechanisms promoting or disrupting metastasis remain largely unknown [ , , , , ]. One cellular process whose role in metastasis remains uncertain is N-glycosylation, the co-translational addition of a glycan to an asparagine residue, which takes place in the endoplasmic reticulum (EndoR) and Golgi apparatus (Fig.  ) [ , ]. Glycosylation of proteins can alter their signaling, cell–cell adhesion, and cell–matrix adhesion, promoting or disrupting epithelial to mesenchymal transition (EMT) and metastasis [ , ]. For example, studies have shown that aberrant N-glycosylation of E-Cadherin, a critical adherens junction protein involved in maintaining epithelial cell polarity, leads to tumorigenesis [ , – ]. Changes in the N-glycome have been observed in metastasis of several cancers, including colorectal [ , ], endometrial [ ], and breast cancer [ – ]. In breast cancer specifically, it has been shown that N-glycan abundance increases as cancer progresses, with a shift towards more high mannose (also called oligomannose) N-glycans being expressed on cancer cells [ , ]. This suggests decreased trimming of precursor N-glycans, normally initiated by EndoR α-mannosidase and completed by Golgi α-mannosidase I (Fig.  B, C).
 Recent findings of increased abundance of high mannose N-glycans in breast cancer metastasis have suggested a potential role in altered α-mannosidase expression or activity in breast cancer metastasis [ , ]. While many protein modifications are substrate-specific, N-glycosylation follows a set pathway until terminal differentiation of the glycan is finalized in the Golgi (Fig.  A, B), and N-glycosylation enzymes are shared by all N-glycosylated proteins. After an N-glycan is added to a polypeptide and protein folding checks are completed by calnexin and calreticulin in the EndoR, one mannose residue is removed by the EndoR α-mannosidase (MAN1B1), before the glycosylated protein is moved to the Golgi (Fig.  B) [ , ]. Once in the Golgi, Golgi α-mannosidases I remove three α1,2-mannose residues, enabling the continued differentiation of N-glycans into hybrid or complex type N-glycans (Figs. C-E and  A) [ , ].
 While there are three Golgi α-mannosidases I, MAN1A1, MAN1A2, and MAN1A3/MAN1C1, they are thought to differ mainly in expression patterns [ , ]. Recent transcriptomic studies [ ] and tumor protein studies [ ] revealed that low MAN1A1 expression is associated with decreased survival in breast cancer patients. These findings support the hypothesis that decreased MAN1A1 expression may lead to increased abundance of high mannose N-glycans, increased metastasis, and decreased survival in breast cancer. However, to this point, no studies have investigated how MAN1A1 expression changes in metastasis, or within individual metastatic breast cancer patients as their disease progresses. Our study has utilized unique single-patient tissue microarrays (TMAs) from metastatic breast cancer patients [ , , ] to examine MAN1A1 expression in patients’ normal breast epithelial cells, primary tumor cells, and metastatic tumor cells to determine if and how MAN1A1 expression changes as breast cancer progresses.

## Materials and methods

## TCGA data analysis using Xena
The TCGA-BRCA (the Cancer Genome Atlas Breast Invasive Carcinoma) dataset [ ] was accessed using Xena [ ]. The variables queried were MAN1A1 gene expression (genomic variable) and sample type (phenotypic variable), and data were exported to GraphPad Prism (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA) for further analysis. To generate the Kaplan–Meier curve, the Xena-generated Kaplan–Meier curve data containing all subtypes were downloaded, samples were matched by unique sample identifier with the type of sample, and the primary tumor samples were isolated and exported to GraphPad Prism for further analysis, as described below.

## Cell culture
One human breast cancer cell line, MDA-MB-468 (female, American Type Culture Collection/ATCC Cat# HTB-132, Manassas, VA, USA) was used in this study. These cells were purchased from the ATCC, cultured in high-glucose Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) (Thermo Fisher Scientific cat #11965118, Waltham, MA, USA) supplemented with 10% heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum FBS (Corning Cellgro cat #35–011-CV), and maintained in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2 at 37 ºC. Cells were used for less than ten passages after thawing and were passaged using 0.25% trypsin–EDTA solution (Millipore Sigma cat #T4049, Burlington, MA, USA). Cell lines were routinely tested for mycoplasma using a PCR-based (polymerase chain reaction) MycoDtect kit (Greiner Bio-One, Monroe, NC, USA) and authenticated by STR (short tandem repeat) profiling at the Genetic Resources Core Facility of the Johns Hopkins University School of Medicine.

## siRNA knockdown
1 × 106 MDA-MB-468 cells were seeded per well in a 6-well plate or 2 × 107 MDA-MB-468 cells per 175 cm2 flask for cell pellet construction. Lipofectamine RNAiMAX (Invitrogen cat #13778150, Waltham, MA, USA) was mixed according to the manufacturer’s instructions with either siMAN1A1 (siGENOME MAN1A1 SMARTpool, Horizon Discovery, cat #M-012174–00–0010, Cambridge, UK) or siControl (siGENOME Non-Targeting siRNA #2, Horizon Discovery, cat #D-001210–02–05) in Opti-MEM I Reduced-Serum Medium (ThermoFisher Scientific cat #31985070) and incubated for 15 min. Cells were then treated with 20 nM of the appropriate siRNA and transferred to the incubator. Media was changed after 24 h, and cells were collected using 0.25% trypsin–EDTA solution (Millipore Sigma cat #T4049) after 48 h.

## Western blot
Cells were washed with PBS (phosphate buffered saline, Quality Biological cat #119–069–131, Gaithersburg, MD, USA) prior to addition of RIPA buffer (Millipore Sigma cat #R0278-50ML) with Halt protease/phosphatase inhibitor cocktail (Thermo Fisher Scientific cat #1861281). Cells were collected and homogenized by vortexing for 10 s followed by 20 min rest on ice, repeated three times. Samples were centrifuged at 13,100 RPM (revolutions per minute) for 20 min at 4ºC. Protein lysates were isolated from pellets and total protein concentration was measured using a Pierce BCA Assay kit (Thermo Scientific cat #23227) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. 4 × SDS-PAGE (sodium dodecyl-sulfate polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis) loading buffer was prepared by mixing 4 × Laemmli sample buffer (Bio-Rad cat #1610747, Hercules, CA, USA) with 2-mercaptoethanol (Thermo Fisher Scientific cat #21985023) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Protein was diluted in water and mixed 3:1 with 4 × SDS-PAGE loading buffer for a final protein concentration of 2 mg/mL, and this mixture was heated at 95ºC for 10 min to denature soluble proteins. 10 μL of each sample was loaded onto a 4–15% PAGE gel (Bio-Rad cat #4561083) for separation via electrophoresis for 50 min at 120 V and transferred to an Immobilon-P PVDF (polyvinylidene fluoride) membrane (Millipore Sigma cat #IPVH00010) for 45 min at 100 V. Membrane was washed for 5 min in PBS, then incubated for 45 min at room temperature in blocking buffer of 5% skim milk (Millipore Sigma cat #1153630500) in PBS. The membrane was then washed three times in PBST (PBS-tween), followed by overnight incubation at 4ºC with primary antibody in blocking buffer. The membrane was then washed three times with PBST followed by incubation for 45 min at room temperature with horseradish peroxidase conjugated secondary antibody, against the species in which the primary antibody was raised, diluted 1:2,000 in blocking buffer. The membrane was then washed three times in PBST. Chemiluminescence was detected using a ChemiDoc imaging system (Bio-Rad) following incubation using the SuperSignal West Pico chemiluminescent substrate kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific cat #34580) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Antibodies used were anti-alpha 1,2-mannosidase IA/MAN1A1 antibody (1:1,000 dilution; catalog #ab140613, Abcam, Cambridge, UK); anti-GAPDH antibody (1:3,000 dilution, Sigma Aldrich cat #G8795, Burlington, MA, USA); anti-mouse IgG, HRP-linked Antibody (1:2,000 dilution, Cell Signaling Technology cat #7076S, Danvers, MA, USA); anti-rabbit IgG, HRP-linked Antibody (1:2,000 dilution, Cell Signaling Technology cat #7074S).

## Cell pellet construction
First, 100 μL of 2% agarose (Genemate cat #e-3121–125, Radnor, PA, USA) in PBS was pipetted slowly into 0.5 mL centrifuge tubes. Once the agarose had hardened, 200 μL PBS was added, and the agarose plug was stored at 4ºC. One 175 cm2 flask each of siMAN1A1 and siControl cells were collected at 48 h after transfection (described above) by using 0.25% trypsin–EDTA solution (Millipore Sigma cat #T4049). Cells were pelleted by centrifugation at 2,000 RPM for 5 min and washed twice by resuspending cells in cold PBS and pelleting by centrifuging again at 2,000 RPM for 5 min. PBS was removed from the agarose plugs with a pipette. Cells were then resuspended in 200 μL neutral-buffered 10% formalin (Thermo Fisher Scientific cat #245685), and gently pipetted onto an agarose plug. Tubes were then centrifuged for 5 min at 1,000 × g in a swinging bucket centrifuge to ensure a flat pellet. Supernatant was removed with a pipette, and 10% formalin was gently added again to the tubes without disturbing the pellets. Tubes were centrifuged again for 5 min at 1,000 × g and left to fix at room temperature for 48 h. After 48 h, a fresh, sharp razor blade was used to carefully cut off the bottom of the centrifuge tubes containing the fixed cell pellets. Empty labelled tissue cassettes were placed in a shallow container of PBS, and each centrifuge tube was inverted over the appropriate tissue cassette and the pellet was gently extruded into the cassette by gently pushing the agarose plug with the blunt end of a wooden cotton applicator stick. The agarose plug was carefully removed from the cassette using forceps, and the cell pellet was stored in its cassette in PBS at 4ºC until paraffin embedding. This process was repeated for the other cell pellet in a separate tissue cassette. Both cell pellets were embedded into a single paraffin block using a Sakura Tissue-Tek VIP tissue processor (Torrance, CA, USA) in the Oncology Tissue Services Core Facility of the Johns Hopkins School of Medicine (JHSOM).

## TMA construction
The single-patient TMAs used in this study were constructed as previously described [ , ]. Briefly, 16 patients with terminal, widely metastatic breast cancer, as described in Table  , were enrolled in a rapid autopsy program and TMAs from their tissues were constructed as previously described [ , ].
 The control TMA used to optimize the immunostaining was constructed for this study at the Oncology Tissue Services Core of JHSOM. The TMA was constructed using paraffin tissue blocks of normal patient tissues from the rapid autopsy study of metastatic breast cancer patients described above [ , ]. Ten tissue types, including lung, skin, spleen, kidney, colon, breast, liver, brain, pancreas, and small intestine, were used to construct the TMA. For each tissue type, three patients’ normal tissues were used, and three 1.75 mm diameter cores from each patient and each tissue type were placed on the TMA to minimize sampling error.
The multi-patient TMA was purchased from TissueArray.com (formerly Biomax), containing tissues from breast cancer patients from all common molecular subtypes. Primary tumor cores had a matched normal breast core or a matched lymph node metastasis core from the same patient, for a total of 48 patients. Survival data were not available for these patients.
For single-patient TMAs, sixteen TMAs were constructed using biopsy cores from each patient’s archived primary tumor and normal breast tissue obtained at initial diagnosis following mastectomy or lumpectomy, as well as from multiple metastatic tumor sites obtained at autopsy [ , ]. Each TMA consisted of 99 cores, each measuring 1.4 mm in diameter. To minimize sampling error, 4 to 5 cores per tumor sample were placed on each TMA. In total, these 16 TMAs combined contained 105 normal breast cores, 209 primary tumor cores, and 982 metastasis cores derived from 21 different organs, with 2–8 metastatic sites per patient. Analysis of these TMAs is described in detail below. These unique TMAs are a rare resource, in which the progression and dissemination of breast cancer can be robustly studied for a single patient, and studies employing these TMAs have been used to study expression of EpCAM, GATA3, N-glycan abundance, and many other questions in breast cancer metastasis [ , , ].

## Immunostaining
Immunostaining was performed at the Oncology Tissue Services Core of the Johns Hopkins University School of Medicine. Immunolabeling for MAN1A1 was performed on formalin‐fixed, paraffin embedded sections on a Ventana Discovery Ultra autostainer (Roche Diagnostics, Indianapolis, IN, USA). Briefly, following dewaxing and rehydration on board, epitope retrieval was performed using Ventana Ultra CC1 buffer (Roche Diagnostics cat #6414575001) at 96ºC for 64 min. Primary antibody, anti-alpha 1,2-mannosidase IA/MAN1A1 antibody (1:100 dilution; Abcam cat #ab140613) was applied at 37ºC for 60 min. Primary antibodies were detected using an anti-rabbit HQ detection system (Roche Diagnostics cat #7017936001 and #7017812001) followed by Chromomap 3,3′-diaminobenzidine (DAB) immunohistochemistry (IHC) detection kit (Roche Diagnostics cat #5266645001), counterstaining with Mayer’s hematoxylin, dehydration and mounting.

## Image analysis
After staining was completed, slides were scanned at 40X magnification using a Nano Zoomer S210 Hamamatsu slide scanner (Bridgewater, NJ, USA) at the Oncology Tissue Services Core of the Johns Hopkins University School of Medicine and .ndpi image files were uploaded into QuPath (v0.5.1) [ ], with H-DAB (hematoxylin-DAB) as the image type. For each image, the TMA grid was set to 9 by 11 and TMA cores were manually adjusted to ensure that the maximum intact tissue was included in each core, without including damaged tissue, folded tissue, or tissue from adjacent cores. Stain vectors were estimated for each image by circling an area with a wide range of staining intensity and auto-estimating vectors. Finally, simple tissue detection was used on all cores in each image, with a threshold of 229 to 232, depending on the image.
To detect cells, the Stardist deep-learning nuclear segmentation tool [ ] was employed on QuPath [ ]. Using a modified version of a script obtained from Zaidi et al. on GitHub (https://github.com/MarkZaidi/Universal-StarDist-for-QuPath/)32 and the HE Heavy Augment model [ ], cell detection was optimized for each image individually by optimizing the detection threshold, the minimum nuclear intensity, and the lower limit of normalization for each image. The exact code used is available on GitHub (https://github.com/njenkinson/MAN1A1-IHC), and the parameters used for each TMA are available in Table S1 in Supplemental Tables.
To train models to recognize breast cancer cells and normal breast epithelial cells, regions on each TMA (except single-patient cases #4 and #6, which were used to test the model) were then annotated in QuPath, as either “Tumor” or “Other” tissue in primary tumor and metastasis cores, or as either “Normal breast epithelium” or “Other breast” in normal breast cores. These annotations were verified by a board-certified breast pathologist (Dr. Argani). “Other” included a wide array of other cell types on each TMA, including normal functional tissue from various organs, immune cells, fibroblasts and stroma, fat cells, and connective tissue, to help ensure the model did not mistakenly recognize these cells as tumor cells. “Other breast” similarly included a variety of different cell types present in normal breast cores, excluding epithelial cells. One object-based classifier was trained on QuPath from these 15 annotated images to classify cells as “Tumor” or “Other,” and another object-based classifier was trained on these images to classify cells as “Normal breast” or “Breast other.” These models were approved by a board-certified pathologist and run on the appropriate cores to classify cells. Both models are available on GitHub (https://github.com/njenkinson/MAN1A1-IHC).
Since MAN1A1 should be present in the Golgi, rather than the nucleus, a single-measurement classifier measuring mean cytoplasmic DAB intensity was optimized for each image and used to classify cells as either DAB + or DAB-. For each core analyzed, the number of DAB +, DAB-, and total cells were reported. This data was pooled for each replicate core on a single TMA, to determine the overall percentage of DAB positive cells, which was calculated as the total number of DAB + cells on a given TMA for a given tissue type, divided by the total number of cells of that tissue type on that TMA. This calculation produced a single datapoint for each tissue type on each TMA, which was exported to GraphPad Prism for further analysis.

## Statistical analysis
All statistical analyses and graphing were performed on GraphPad Prism (v10.3.0). Depending on the groups being compared, significance was determined with either an Ordinary One-Way ANOVA test or a two-tailed paired t-test. Kaplan–Meier curves were generated on GraphPad Prism, and the significance, hazard ratios, and median ratios listed were estimated using the log-rank method. The log-rank method was used instead of Cox proportional hazards regression because of our limited sample size. Several studies have shown that Cox proportional hazards regressions are unreliable at samples sizes < 10 events per variable [ – ]. Details of the estimation of the hazard ratio are described in the Supplemental Methods section of the Supplemental Information. Statistically significant results were defined as p < 0.05. Figure  was created with ChemDraw (v19.1), Fig.  was created with BioRender.com, and all other figures were made using Microsoft PowerPoint (v16.89.1) and GraphPad Prism (v10.3.0).
 

## Results

## MAN1A1 gene expression data in breast cancer patients
To confirm MAN1A1 as a target for further study, we first queried publicly available mRNA expression data in the TCGA-BRCA (the Cancer Genome Atlas Breast Invasive Carcinoma) database for changes in MAN1A1 mRNA expression using Xena and found that both primary tumor and metastasis samples had significantly lower MAN1A1 mRNA expression than that in normal breast samples (Fig.  B) [ , ]. This matches with previous studies that revealed increased high mannose N-glycans in breast cancer metastasis [ ] and reduced survival in patients with decreased MAN1A1 expression [ , ]. However, there was no significant impact on overall survival between groups with high and low MAN1A1 mRNA expression in primary tumor samples (Fig. S1 in Supplemental Information).

## MAN1A1 immunostaining optimization
To further probe the impact of MAN1A1 expression on breast cancer metastasis and survival, we conducted an immunohistochemistry (IHC) study using breast cancer patient tissues. The specificity of the MAN1A1 antibody to be used was first confirmed by comparing MDA-MB-468 cells treated with MAN1A1 siRNA (siMAN1A1) and those treated with a non-targeting siRNA control (siControl) in both Western blot and IHC staining of cell pellets (Fig.  C, D). A tissue microarray (TMA) was then constructed using patient tissues to test the MAN1A1 immunostaining intensity in a variety of normal tissues. Each tissue type contained three normal tissue cores from three different patients, for a total of nine cores of each tissue type (Fig.  E, Table S2 in Supplemental Tables). The staining intensity matched the expected MAN1A1 expression patterns in the Human Protein Atlas [ , ], with low expression in skin, moderate expression in lung, and high expression in small intestine (Fig.  F-H). From this, we concluded that our IHC staining protocol was optimized for use in breast cancer patient tissues.
Using our optimized MAN1A1 IHC staining protocol, the metastatic breast cancer patient TMAs were all stained at the same MAN1A1 antibody concentration (1:100) and digitally scanned at 40X using a Nano Zoomer S210 Hamamatsu slide scanner (Fig.  A). The scanned MAN1A1-stained images were then uploaded into QuPath [ ], where TMA cores were defined, staining vectors were estimated, and tissue boundaries were detected (Fig.  B). Following these image pre-processing steps, cells were detected using Stardist [ , ], with the detection parameters being optimized for each TMA (Fig.  C). Tissue cores were then annotated, and an object-based classifier was trained to distinguish tumor cells from all other cells in primary tumor and metastasis cores, or to distinguish normal breast epithelial cells from other cells in normal breast cores (Fig.  D). All these annotations and the outputs of the object-based classifiers were approved by a board-certified pathologist. Finally, cells were classified as having either high or low MAN1A1 expression using a threshold level of the 3,3′-diaminobenzidine (DAB) staining intensity (Fig.  E). The results of these classifications for each core were then exported for analysis. In full, these steps allowed us to confidently identify tumor cells and normal breast epithelial cells and to determine the proportion of those cells that had high MAN1A1 expression.

## MAN1A1 expression in multi-patient TMA cohort
We first analyzed a multi-patient TMA, purchased from TissueArray.com (formerly Biomax), consisting of tissues from 48 patients. The tissues on this TMA were from breast cancer patients with different molecular subtypes, including luminal (Lum), HER2-enriched (HER2), and basal-like carcinoma (BLC). The primary tumor cores were classified into molecular subtypes based on the expression data provided by the manufacturer for hormone receptors (HR), the estrogen receptor (ER) and progesterone receptor (PR), and human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2). Primary tumor samples were classified into subtypes according to standard guidelines (luminal = ER +, HER2-enriched = ER-/HER2 +, basal-like carcinoma = HR-/HER2-) [ ]. Each primary tumor (PT) core had either a matched normal breast core (two rows, 12 patients with one primary tumor and one normal breast core each) or a matched lymph node metastasis (LN Met) core (six rows, 36 patients with one primary tumor and one lymph node metastasis core each) from the same patient, for a total of 48 patients (Fig.  A, Table S3 in Supplemental Tables). Survival data were not available for these patients.
 Unpaired analysis of all cores on the multi-patient TMA showed a significant increase in MAN1A1 protein expression in both primary tumor and lymph node metastasis tissue when compared to normal breast, but no difference in expression between primary tumor and lymph node metastasis (Fig.  B). These results were supported by paired analysis, where MAN1A1 expression was compared between normal breast and primary tumor, or between primary tumor and lymph node metastasis tissues from a single patient (Fig. S2A in Supplemental Information). Finally, the samples were categorized by the molecular subtype of the primary tumor, and a pairwise analysis within individual subtypes found no difference in MAN1A1 expression between primary tumor and lymph node metastasis tissues in any subtype (Fig.  C, S2B in Supplemental Information). These findings on MAN1A1 protein expression levels differ from the results from the TCGA analysis, which is based on mRNA levels (Fig.  B).

## Context-dependent MAN1A1 expression is associated with patient survival in single-patient TMA cohort
Although we found no difference in MAN1A1 expression between primary tumor and local lymph node metastasis in the multi-patient TMA cohort, previous studies have detected significant increases in high mannose N-glycans in breast cancer metastasis when compared to primary tumor [ ], which would suggest a decrease in MAN1A1 expression. We therefore sought to examine MAN1A1 expression in distant metastases, and employed a rare cohort of unique, single-patient TMAs for this analysis. These TMAs were constructed as part of a rapid-autopsy program of metastatic breast cancer patients, almost all of whom had received standard surgical, hormone, chemo-, and/or radiation therapies (treatment details for each patient in Table S4 in Supplemental Tables) [ , ]. Each TMA contains 99 cores, including normal breast tissue, primary tumor tissue, metastatic nodule tissues from different metastatic sites, and normal tissues from distant organs from each patient, as well as a column of staining controls (Fig.  A, Tables S5 to S20 in Supplemental Tables). Normal breast and primary tumor tissues were obtained at initial diagnosis following mastectomy or lumpectomy, while distant metastases were obtained during autopsy. These patients’ breast cancers were categorized as either luminal, luminal/loss, HER2-enriched, HER2/loss, or basal-like carcinoma, where “/loss” refers to a loss of the hormone receptor(s) or HER2 expression in metastatic sites (Table  ). Case #10 (HER2/loss) and Case #15 (HER2-enriched) were grouped as “HER2” for analyses based on subtype as there was only one case of each type.
 A pairwise analysis of all 16 patient cases revealed trends of increased MAN1A1 expression in primary tumor (N = 16) compared to normal breast (N = 13), and decreased MAN1A1 expression in metastases (N = 16, all metastatic sites pooled) compared to primary tumor (Fig.  B). However, the differences between these groups were not statistically significant. Based on these results, survival analysis was performed comparing cases with increased MAN1A1 expression in primary tumor (N = 7) versus normal breast to those with unchanged or decreased MAN1A1 (N = 4), which revealed a significant decrease in survival time in the increased MAN1A1 cohort (p = 0.032), with a hazard ratio (HR) of 2.68 (Fig.  C). Median survival time for patients who had increased MAN1A1 expression in their primary tumor was 5 years, compared to a median survival time of 9.5 years in patients without increased MAN1A1 in their primary tumor. Thus, patients who did not have increased MAN1A1 in their primary tumor lived 1.9 times longer than those who did (median ratio = 1.90).
Patient data for each of the single-patient TMAs is provided, with all patients being female, and between the ages of 28–71 years at the time of diagnosis, and between the ages of 37–79 at the time of death. The post-mortem interval represents the time in hours between the patient’s death and their autopsy; all post-mortem intervals were < 12 h and all but one were < or = 4 h. Staging provided is based on TNM staging at the time of diagnosis, and tumor type is either invasive lobular carcinoma (ILC) or invasive ductal carcinoma (IDC). Treatment details are provided in Table S4 in Supplemental Tables.
Similarly, a survival analysis comparing cases with decreased MAN1A1 expression in metastases versus primary tumor (N = 9) to those with unchanged or increased MAN1A1 (N = 6) showed a significant decrease in survival time in the decreased MAN1A1 cohort (p = 0.0018), with a hazard ratio of 3.45 (Fig.  D). Median survival time for patients with decreased MAN1A1 expression in metastasis compared to primary tumor was only 4 years, compared to 9.5 years for patients without decreased MAN1A1 expression in metastasis. Patients therefore lived 2.38 times longer if they did not have decreased MAN1A1 expression in metastasis compared to primary tumor (median ratio = 2.38). Taken together, these two survival analyses demonstrated that the trends observed for MAN1A1 expression increasing from normal breast to primary tumor and MAN1A1 expression decreasing from primary tumor to metastasis are both associated with significantly decreased median survival. These findings support context-dependent protein expression of MAN1A1 in breast tumor growth and metastasis, where MAN1A1 expression is elevated in primary tumor growth, but decreased in metastasized cancer cells (Fig.  ).
 

## MAN1A1 expression across different breast cancer subtypes
We next sought to determine if MAN1A1 expression patterns varied across subtypes and split the cases up into groups of luminal (N = 4), luminal/loss (N = 5), HER2 (N = 2, includes both HER2-enriched and HER2/loss), and basal-like carcinoma cases (N = 5, Fig.  E). Of these subtypes, luminal breast cancer is considered to have the least aggressive course of disease with the best prognosis, with HER2-enriched breast cancer being more aggressive with a worse prognosis, and basal-like carcinoma being the most aggressive with the poorest prognosis [ ].
In analyzing MAN1A1 expression by breast cancer subtype, we found that luminal/loss, HER2, and basal-like carcinoma cases followed the overall patterns observed in Fig.  B of increased MAN1A1 expression in primary tumor versus normal breast and decreased MAN1A1 expression in metastases versus primary tumor. The decrease in MAN1A1 in metastases as compared to primary tumor was more pronounced in more aggressive subtypes, with basal-like carcinoma cases having the largest decreases in MAN1A1 in metastases versus primary tumor (Fig.  E). Interestingly, the difference between luminal cases and all other cases resulted from a lack of increased MAN1A1 expression in primary tumor versus normal breast in luminal cases. Based on these findings, we pooled all luminal/loss, HER2-enriched, and basal-like carcinoma cases and conducted another paired analysis, excluding luminal cases (Fig.  F), which revealed a significant decrease in MAN1A1 expression in metastases as compared to primary tumor (control breast, N = 10; primary tumor, N = 12; metastasis, N = 12). Interestingly, all basal-like and HER2-enriched cases displayed decreased MAN1A1 protein expression in metastasis, while only 40% of luminal/loss and no luminal cases displayed this pattern. This trend of decreasing MAN1A1 protein expression in metastasis in increasingly aggressive breast cancer subtypes (Fig.  E) suggests a possible relationship between MAN1A1 expression and the aggressive phenotype and warrants further investigation.

## MAN1A1 expression in different metastatic sites
Finally, we examined individual sites of metastasis separately. The most common metastatic sites in our patient cohorts were lymph node (N = 12), liver (N = 12), bone (N = 11), lung/pleura (N = 10), adrenal gland (N = 6), and diaphragm (N = 5). Other metastatic sites which occurred on fewer than 5 TMAs were included in pooled analyses of all metastatic sites (Fig.  B-F), and the metastatic sites which occurred on each TMA are listed in Table S21 in Supplemental Tables. We found that with all breast cancer subtypes pooled, all metastatic sites except liver showed decreased MAN1A1 expression compared to primary tumor, with MAN1A1 expression in bone being significantly decreased compared to primary tumor (Fig.  G). Additionally, we analyzed metastatic sites by subtype for luminal (Fig. S3A in Supplemental Information), luminal/loss (Fig. S3B in Supplemental Information), and basal-like carcinoma (Fig. S3C in Supplemental Information) cases, and discovered that bone metastases consistently displayed the lowest expression of MAN1A1 of all sites in all subtypes. HER2 cases were not analyzed by metastatic site due to the low case number (N = 2), and only metastatic sites which occurred on 3 or more TMAs of the same subtype were included in this analysis.

## Discussion
As the first study to examine MAN1A1 protein expression in metastatic nodules and to track the changes in MAN1A1 expression along the metastatic path, our study finds evidence that MAN1A1 expression changes as breast cancer progresses along the metastatic path. These trends were more pronounced in more aggressive breast cancer subtypes, including HER2-enriched and basal-like carcinomas. Furthermore, our study was the first to examine MAN1A1 expression in distant metastases in a rare, limited cohort of terminal metastatic breast cancer patients from the Johns Hopkins rapid autopsy program, whose tissues are arranged on single-patient TMAs [ , , ]. In this limited cohort of 16 patients, we found that decreased MAN1A1 expression in metastasis was associated with decreased survival time in a context dependent manner, where elevated MAN1A1 expression was evident in primary tumor cells, while metastatic cancer cells displayed MAN1A1 downregulation. Comparison of different metastatic sites revealed particularly low MAN1A1 expression in bone metastases, while expression in liver metastases was similarly elevated to that in primary tumor.
The single-patient TMAs used in this study (Fig.  ) were the same as those used in Ščupáková et al., which showed by using mass spectrometry imaging that N-glycan abundance increased along the metastatic path, with higher abundance in primary tumor than normal breast, and higher abundance in metastases than primary tumor [ ]. Many of the individual N-glycan species with the greatest increases in abundance were high-mannose N-glycans, which supported previous studies that revealed increases in high mannose N-glycans as breast cancer progressed [ , ]. Interestingly, our study found increased MAN1A1 protein expression in primary tumor compared to normal breast. This conflicted with our initial hypothesis that MAN1A1 expression would be decreased, leading to increased high mannose N-glycan production. However, MAN1A1 is not the only potential driver of increased high mannose N-glycan production. It is possible that increased high mannose abundance is driven by decreases in the other Golgi alpha mannosidases, MAN1A2 or MAN1A3/MAN1C1, or by decreased expression of GnT1/MGAT1, which adds the first GlcNAc to make a hybrid N-glycan (Fig.  C).
Additionally, because overall N-glycan abundance significantly increased along the metastatic path [ ], we hypothesized that the overall expression of multiple N-glycosylation enzymes increased. The lack of significant increase in MAN1A1 expression may therefore represent a decreased proportion of MAN1A1 expression relative to other MAN1A1 enzymes and total N-glycans. The association of increased MAN1A1 expression in primary tumor and decreased survival time may therefore be a product of increased overall N-glycosylation in primary tumor [ ], rather than increased MAN1A1 activity alone. By contrast, the association of decreased MAN1A1 expression in metastasis and decreased survival time is in opposition to findings of increased N-glycosylation in metastasis in these samples [ ], and is therefore unlikely to be confounded by the overall increase in N-glycan abundance. Further study would be necessary to determine the mechanism underlying the relationship between increased MAN1A1 expression in primary tumor and decreased survival time. Promoter methylation of MAN1A1 has been found in several cancers and is associated with decreased MAN1A1 expression [ , ]. Analysis of MAN1A1 promoter methylation in these TMAs in future studies may provide further insight into the regulation and role of MAN1A1 in breast cancer metastasis.
We found that MAN1A1 protein expression levels in our study (Figs. and ) differed from the results of our analysis of the TCGA-BRCA dataset [ ], which is based on mRNA levels (Fig.  B). Our MAN1A1 data measured protein expression levels exclusively in cancer cells and normal breast epithelial cells, using a careful digital pathology approach which measures MAN1A1 protein levels in cancer cells and normal breast epithelium only (Fig.  ). In contrast, the TCGA data is based on mRNA expression levels from bulk tumor and bulk normal breast tissue, containing contaminations from unrelated cells, which is particularly problematic because breast cancer tissues are known to be very heterogeneous [ , ]. Moreover, it is well known that mRNA levels can differ from the corresponding proteins levels in the same or similar studies because of alternative splicing, mRNA stabilization, and various types of posttranslational regulation, including protein N-glycosylation [ , ]. Alternative splicing of mRNA produces a large variety of differently spliced RNA transcripts that may be translated into diverse proteins [ ]. For example, alternative splicing can reduce protein expression through mechanisms that lead to the production of non-functional, truncated, or unstable proteins, or by causing the transcript to be targeted for degradation by cellular quality control processes such as nonsense-mediated decay (NMD) [ – ]. mRNA stabilization can also lead to decreased protein expression because the stabilization mechanisms can interfere with the efficiency of translation [ , , ]. Posttranslational regulation, for example the addition of N-glycans to the protein, can increase protein stability and therefore the protein's lifespan, leading to higher protein levels than corresponding mRNA levels [ , , ]. Lastly, our study design differs significantly from that in the TCGA data which represent unpaired observations of separate measurements in separate patients on normal breast epithelium, primary tumors, and metastases [ ]. In contrast, our study specifically measured MAN1A1 protein expression changes within the same patient, i.e., in primary tumor versus normal epithelium, and in progression along the metastatic path in the same patient. This approach removes the effects of differences in baseline levels of MAN1A1 protein expression which may exist across patients’ breast epithelia and specifically answers the question of how MAN1A1 is regulated along the metastatic path within each patient.
While the cohort of terminal metastatic breast tumor patients in the single-patient TMAs was limited, consisting of only 16 patients, the cohort of patients on the multi-patient TMA utilized for evaluating MAN1A1 expression levels in paired comparisons of normal epithelium versus primary tumor versus lymph node metastasis consisted of a larger cohort of 48 patients. The analysis of this larger cohort of N = 48 patients revealed significant differences in MAN1A1 protein levels between primary tumor and normal breast epithelial cells, as well as lymph node metastasis versus normal breast epithelial cells. This finding leads us to hypothesize that if there were a significant difference in MAN1A1 protein levels between local lymph node metastases and primary tumor, it would have been picked up in our current analysis of N = 48 patients. Importantly, we performed a carefully designed digital pathology approach which considered MAN1A1 staining in breast epithelial and breast cancer cells only, adding to the rigor to our findings. Breast cancer cells in the primary tumor and local lymph node metastases may be more alike than when compared to normal breast epithelium or breast cancer cells in distant metastases because local lymph node metastases are closer to the primary tumor than are distant metastases when considering the metastatic path in terms of location and timeline. This makes breast cancer cells in local lymph nodes and the primary tumor more likely to be molecularly similar to each other. This is also reflected in our recent study on N-glycans in the cohort of patients with distant metastases from the rapid autopsy program, which shows that N-glycan levels in primary tumor and local lymph node metastases have similar N-glycan levels which are significantly higher than in normal breast epithelium but significantly lower than in distant metastases [ ].
Our study’s finding that increased MAN1A1 expression in primary tumor was significantly associated with decreased survival time (Fig.  C) in our cohort of 16 terminal metastatic breast cancer patients differed from our initial hypothesis, which hypothesized the opposite that increased MAN1A1 would increase survival time. Previous studies in primary tumor mRNA [ ] and protein [ ] both found that decreased MAN1A1 expression in primary tumor compared to normal breast tissue was a strong indicator of decreased survival. We therefore expected that decreased MAN1A1 in primary tumor versus normal breast epithelium would be associated with shorter survival. However, the design of our study was very different than previous studies, which is likely the reason for our different findings. While previous studies compared MAN1A1 expression levels between different patients, our study examined how MAN1A1 expression changed in the primary tumor and then further along the metastatic path as compared to normal breast epithelium within a single patient and did not make any comparisons between patients. Moreover, we analyzed MAN1A1 expression in normal and cancerous breast epithelial cells only, using pathology-guided object classifiers, which identified normal breast epithelial cells, primary tumor cells, and metastatic tumor cells, whereas previous studies used bulk tissue homogenates, mixing in large numbers of other cell types and stroma with the breast cancer/epithelial cells. We subsequently showed that when MAN1A1 expression does change along the metastatic path, these changes are associated with significant effects on patient survival time (Fig.  D). Taken together, our findings support context-dependent protein expression levels of MAN1A1 in breast tumor growth and metastasis, where elevated MAN1A1 expression is observed in primary tumor growth, while breast cancer metastasis displayed MAN1A1 downregulation. We hope that these new findings will contribute to continued exploration of the role of MAN1A1 in breast cancer metastasis.
The patient cohorts for the previous studies of MAN1A1 expression in breast cancer differed significantly from that of the cohort of patients in the single-patient TMAs in our study. Over half of the patients in each previous study (260/394 in Milde-Langosch et al., 65/105 in Legler et al.) [ , ] consisted of patients with lymph node negative disease at the time of sample collection, while our patient cohort was exclusively composed of patients with distant metastases and terminal illness. Except for primary tumor samples and normal breast tissue, which were largely archived from resection earlier in the course the disease, our samples were collected at autopsy, representing the end point of treatment-resistant disease progression. Meanwhile, the samples used in prior studies were collected during initial surgery to resect primary tumors, before administration of any other therapies [ , ]. The results of previous studies also examined likelihood of survival, while ours examined survival time, as all patients died from their illness.
Interestingly, Ščupáková et al. found bone metastases to be an outlier, with decreased N-glycan and specifically high mannose N-glycan abundance when compared to both primary tumor and normal breast [ ]. Our study’s finding of drastically decreased MAN1A1 expression in bone (Fig.  G) may therefore be reflective of decreased expression of all N-glycosylation enzymes, decreased expression of MAN1A1, or both. However, hydrochloric acid-based and long-term formic acid-based bone decalcification procedures have recently been shown to lead to decreased antigenicity and false negatives in IHC [ ]. As these samples were decalcified using long-term formic acid treatment and some were surface decalcified using hydrochloric acid, it is unclear if the findings of decreased N-glycan abundance by Ščupáková et al. and of decreased MAN1A1 expression in this study reflect to some degree consequences of sample processing, rather than true differences in molecular biology when compared to other metastatic sites.
All four common breast cancer subtypes were included in our study, and their occurrence was dictated by the subtypes found in the terminal metastatic breast cancer patients who were enrolled in our rapid autopsy program. We also encountered luminal/loss and HER2/loss cases, in which the respective receptor expression was lost while the cancer cells metastasized along the metastatic path to form distant metastases, which had lost the hormone and HER2 receptor expression. These "/loss" cases indicate that clonal breast cancer cell selection may have occurred along the metastatic path, preferentially selecting for the breast cancer cells without receptor expression to form distant metastases as previously reported [ , ].
There are several links between breast and ovarian cancer, including shared heritability due to BRCA mutations, shared biomarkers, and similar at-risk populations, though they may differ in mechanisms of metastasis [ ]. While breast cancer metastasis is commonly described using the “seed and soil” model involving dissemination of cancer cells to distant tissues via blood vessels, ovarian cancer is thought to most commonly disseminate peritoneally as spheroids [ , , ]. Hamester et al. showed that increased MAN1A1 protein expression in primary ovarian tumor samples was associated with decreased recurrence free survival (RFS) and that MAN1A1 knockout in ovarian cancer cells led to decreased spheroid formation, suggesting a potential mechanism by which MAN1A1 facilitates metastasis in ovarian cancer by encouraging spheroid formation [ ]. Similar future studies into the mechanism of MAN1A1 in breast cancer metastasis could involve migration, invasion, and adhesion assays in MAN1A1 knockout or overexpressing breast cancer cells.
Hamester et al. also found that MAN1A1 expression changed the prognostic role of adhesion molecule ALCAM, with high ALCAM being associated with decreased RFS in the context of high MAN1A1 expression and decreased RFS in the context of low MAN1A1 expression in primary tumors [ ]. These results are interesting regarding the study of our single patient TMAs, where Ščupáková et al. showed that expression of a different adhesion molecule, EpCAM, was significantly associated with the expression of seven distinct N-glycans [ ]. Future studies to determine if there is any relationship between EpCAM, MAN1A1, and survival in the single-patient TMA cohort could shed light on the roles of both proteins in breast cancer metastasis.
Glycans have been an area of interest in biomarker research; Saldova et al. found that free N-glycans predicted the number of circulating tumor cells (CTCs) in breast cancer patient sera [ ], while Wang et al. developed a novel method to identify glycan biomarkers on breast cancer CTCs [ ]. In this and previous studies, MAN1A1 protein and mRNA expression has been shown to be a strong indicator of different measures of breast cancer patient survival [ , ]. To leverage the prognostic value of MAN1A1, we propose further investigation into MAN1A1 as a biomarker on CTCs. Our finding that MAN1A1 expression was decreased in metastasis in most breast cancer subtypes suggests that MAN1A1 expression on CTCs may be useful as a predictor of metastasis and localization of metastases. MAN1A1 on CTCs may also be useful as a general biomarker to help determine patient prognosis, considering the large hazard ratios (2.68–3.45) associated with changes in MAN1A1 expression found in this study.
Our study conclusively shows a relationship between MAN1A1 protein expression at different stages of the metastatic path and patient survival time, but further functional studies are needed to determine if this relationship is causal. Though outside the scope of this study, future functional studies in cell culture and mouse models of metastasis using breast cancer cells with MAN1A1 knockout and MAN1A1 overexpression could shed light on whether a causal relationship exists between MAN1A1 expression and metastasis. Limitations of our study include the limited sample size of the rare cohort of terminal metastatic breast cancer patients enrolled in the Johns Hopkins rapid autopsy program, and possible limitations of bone decalcification procedures potentially affecting IHC results [ ].
In conclusion, our study reveals that distinct patterns of MAN1A1 expression along the metastatic path, i.e., increased MAN1A1 expression in primary tumor versus normal breast and decreased MAN1A1 expression in metastases versus primary tumor, were associated with worse prognosis in a cohort of terminal metastatic breast cancer patients. These findings point toward context-dependent regulation of MAN1A1 expression in breast cancer progression from primary tumor growth to metastatic dissemination to secondary growth at distant metastatic sites (Fig.  ). MAN1A1 expression was also confirmed to be increased in primary tumor compared to normal breast in a larger cohort of breast cancer patients without distant metastases. These findings provide further insight into changes in N-glycosylation along the metastatic path and suggest avenues for future research into the mechanism of MAN1A1 in breast cancer metastasis.
