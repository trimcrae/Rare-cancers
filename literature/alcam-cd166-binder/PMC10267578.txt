However, because of the limited time window available for reperfusion therapy, a large population of stroke patients do not benefit from these treatments. These patients suffer from various sequelae, such as paresis, and receive limited therapy, such as antiplatelet treatment and rehabilitation. Therefore, it is essential to develop alternative therapies for stroke patients with a broader therapeutic window.
Using a mouse ischemic stroke model, we and others have demonstrated that endogenous stem cells with neural differentiation potential are induced within ischemic regions. In addition, we found that they exhibit multipotency and can differentiate into both neural and mesenchymal lineages. , , Using brain samples obtained from stroke patients requiring both partial lobectomy and decompressive craniectomy for massive cerebral infarction, we demonstrated that putative ischemia/injury-induced multipotent stem cells (iSCs) are present within post-stroke human brains and that they can differentiate into electrophysiologically functional neurons in vitro. 
Evidence of iSCs with neuronal differentiation potential within post-stroke human brains indicates the potential of iSCs to regenerate damaged brains following ischemic stroke. In addition, iSCs are stem cells that are specifically induced under pathological conditions, such as ischemic stroke. Therefore, it would be ideal if human-derived iSC (­h-iSC)-based therapy could be used for stroke patients.
In the present study, we transplanted h-iSCs into post-stroke mice. First, we determined the effect of transplanting low (1.0 × 104 cells) or high (5.0 × 104 cells) numbers of h-iSCs by assessing multiple neurological functional tasks. Next, we examined whether h-iSCs contribute to neural replacement by transplanting green fluorescent protein (GFP)-labeled h-iSCs into post-stroke mice. Then, we determined whether h-iSCs activate endogenous NSPCs by transplanting mCherry-labeled h-iSCs into post-stroke Nestin-GFP transgenic mice. And finally, we investigated whether the presence of h-iSCs affects the proliferation and neuronal differentiation of endogenous NSPCs in coculture experiments.

## Materials and Methods

## Ethical Statement
All experimental procedures were approved by the Animal Care Committee of Hyogo Medical University (approval numbers: 16-059; 18-061; 18-074; 22-019; 2019-10-3). Human brain samples from post-stroke areas were obtained from patients receiving decompressive craniectomy and partial lobectomy as part of their treatment, and who satisfied criteria for patient selection as described. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. Human tissue sampling was performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, and the protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of Hyogo Medical University (approval numbers: 1776, 0385).

## Animal Studies
Adult mice (6-10-weeks-old) were used for all experiments [C57BL/6JJcl mice (Clea Japan Inc., Tokyo, Japan); CB-17/Icr-+/+Jcl mice (Clea Japan Inc.); CB-17/Icr-scid/scid Jcl mice (Clea Japan Inc.); and B6.Cg-Tg(Nes-EGFP)1Yamm mice (RIKEN BioResource Research Center, Ibaraki, Japan)]. Nestin-GFP transgenic (TG) mice obtained from the RIKEN BioResource Research Center were generated in association with the National Bioresource Project of the MEXT/AMED, Japan.
For some experiments, B6.Cg-Tg(Nes-EGFP)1Yamm mice [Nestin-GFP TG mice (C57BL/6 background)] were crossed with CB-17/Icr-+/+Jcl mice (CB-17 wild-type mice) as described. , The resulting hybrid (F1; first filial hybrid) mice were continuously backcrossed with CB-17 wild-type mice. By repeating these procedures (more than 6 generations, ≥N6), Nestin-GFP TG mice (CB-17 background) that were reproducible and exhibited high survival rates were produced. , We used this background of mice to trace the fate of nestin+ NSPCs following h-iSC transplantation.

## Induction of Ischemic Stroke
Permanent focal cerebral ischemia was induced in mice by ligating and interrupting the distal portion of the left middle cerebral artery (MCA) as described previously. , , Briefly, under isoflurane anesthesia, a burr hole was made in the skull using a drill (H021 Minimo; Minitor Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan). After opening the dura mater, MCA occlusion (MCAO) was established by electrocoagulation, followed by disconnection of the distal portion of the left MCA. Sham-operated mice received the same surgical procedure up until the dura mater was opened as described. , 

## Cell Transplantation
Transplantation of h-iSCs was performed as described. Briefly, under isoflurane anesthesia, low (0.5 μL; 2.0 × 104 cells/μL) or high (0.5 μL; 1.0 × 105 cells/μL) numbers of h-iSCs were transcranially transplanted into peri-ischemic areas (3 mm lateral from the bregma and 2.5 mm below the dural surface) of CB-17/Icr-scid/scid Jcl mice 6 weeks following MCAO. In other experiments, h-iSCs were first transfected with GFP- or mCherry-expressing lentivirus vectors as described , , , for cell tracing. These GFP+ h-iSCs (0.5 μL; 1.0 × 105 cells/μL) or mCherry+ h-iSCs (0.5 μL; 1.0 × 105 cells/μL) were transplanted into peri-ischemic areas of CB-17/Icr-scid/scid Jcl mice or Nestin-GFP TG mice, respectively, 6 weeks after MCAO. As a control, 0.5 μL of phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) was injected into the peri-ischemic areas of CB-17/Icr-scid/scid Jcl mice or Nestin-GFP TG mice 6 weeks after MCAO.

## Preparation of Brain Samples Following Ischemic Stroke
Mice were treated intraperitoneally with a mixture containing medetomidine (0.3 mg/kg), midazolam (4 mg/kg), and butorphanol (5 mg/kg), , and transcardially perfused with 4% PFA as described. , , , The brains were removed and fixed with 4% PFA for 24 h. The fixed brains were cryoprotected in 30% sucrose, frozen at −80 °C, and sliced into 20-μm coronal sections using a cryostat for immunohistochemical analysis.

## Immunohistochemistry
Immunohistochemistry was performed as described ­previously. , , , , In brief, coronal brain sections (20-μm thick) were immunostained with primary antibodies against GFP [1:1000, rabbit, Abcam (ab6556), Cambridge, UK; 1:2000, chicken, Abcam (ab13970)], mCherry [1:1000, rabbit, Abcam (ab167453), Cambridge, UK], nestin (1:100, Santa Cruz, Biotechnology; Dallas, TX), Tuj1 (1:2000, rabbit, Abcam), and microtubule-associated protein 2 (MAP2; 1:1000, rabbit, Millipore, Billerica, MA, USA), and immunolabeling was visualized using Alexa Fluor 488- or 555-conjugated secondary antibodies (1:500, Molecular Probes, Eugene, OR, USA). Nuclei were counterstained with 4ʹ,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI; 1:500, Kirkegaard & Perry Laboratories, Inc., Gaithersburg, MD, USA). Images were captured using a confocal laser microscope (LSM780; Carl Zeiss AG, Oberkochen, Germany).

## Electron Microscope
h-iSC-derived cell clusters were fixed and examined by electron microscope (EM) as described. , , 

## Cell Culture
h-iSCs were isolated and maintained as described. Briefly, human ischemic tissue samples were mechanically dissociated by passage through 23- and 27-G needles and single-cell suspensions were seeded on 10-cm dishes (Corning, NY) in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium/F12 (DMEM/F12; Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA) containing basic fibroblast growth factor (bFGF; 20 ng/mL; Peprotech, Rocky Hill, NJ), epidermal growth factor (EGF; 20 ng/mL; Peprotech), N2 (1%; Invitrogen), and 2% fetal bovine serum (FBS). Once the cells reached confluence, they were treated with trypsin and reseeded under the same conditions. Putative h-iSCs were used for the experiments in passages 5-10. In some experiments, h-iSCs were immunostained with an antibody against nestin (1:100, Santa Cruz, Biotechnology).
For generation of neurosphere-like clusters, h-iSCs were incubated in DMEM/F12 containing bFGF, EGF, and N2 as described. To promote neuronal differentiation, cell clusters were harvested and plated on poly-L-lysine-coated dishes for 10 days in neurobasal medium (Invitrogen) containing bFGF, B-27 supplement (Invitrogen), and 2% FBS as described. , Differentiated cells were then immunostained with antibodies against Tuj1 (1:2000, rabbit, Abcam), neurofilament (NF; rabbit, Abcam), myelin basic protein (MBP; 1:100, mouse, R & D systems, Minneapolis, MN, USA), and glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP; 1:500, rabbit, Abcam), followed by incubation with Alexa Fluor 488- or 555-conjugated secondary antibodies (1: 500, Molecular Probes). Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (1:500; Kirkegaard & Perry Laboratories, Inc.). Images were captured using a laser scanning microscope (LSM780; Carl Zeiss AG).
Endogenous mouse-derived NSPCs (m-NSPCs) were isolated from ischemic areas as described. To investigate the effect of h-iSCs on the proliferation of m-NSPCs, m-NSPCs were cocultured with h-iSCs. To determine the effect of h-iSCs on endogenous NSPC differentiation, neurospheres derived from m-NSPCs were cocultured with h-iSCs. A detailed explanation of the conditions is provided in the Materials and Methods section of Supporting Information.

## Microarray Analysis
Total RNA was isolated from the h-iSCs of 3 different patients (h-iSCs-1, h-iSCs-2, and h-iSCs-3) using the RNeasy Micro Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) as described. , Total RNA was also extracted from human brain-derived endothelial cells (h-ECs; ACBRI-376, Cell Systems, Kirkland, WA), human brain-derived astrocytes (h-ACs; #1800, ScienCell Research Laboratories, Carlsbad, CA, USA), H9 ES cell-derived human neural stem/progenitor cells (h-NSPCs; SCC003, Millipore), and human bone marrow-derived mesenchymal stem cells (h-MSCs; PT-2501, Lonza, Basil, Switzerland) maintained in media specified by the corresponding supplier. For each cell type, RNA samples were isolated for microarray analysis and results were analyzed using the Affymetrix transcriptome analysis console as described. , , , 

## Single-Cell RNA-Seq Analysis
Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) was performed on h-iSCs and h-MSCs using the ICELL8 System as described by a contract service (Takara Bio, Inc., Shiga, Japan). The resulting data were further analyzed using Surate 4.2.

## Behavioral Tests
Neurological function was assessed in sham-operated mice (Sham group), PBS-injected mice after MCAO (M-PBS group), low-dose (1.0 × 104 cells) h-iSC-administered mice after MCAO [M-iSC (low) group], and high-dose (5.0 × 104 cells) h-iSC-administered mice after MCAO [M-iSC (high) group] for 2-9 weeks after treatment (8-15 weeks post-MCAO or sham surgery). Behavioral tests included the wire hang, basket, open-field, hot plate, Y-maze, passive avoidance-learning, sucrose preference, and tail suspension tasks as described. , Sham-operated mice (Sham groups), PBS-injected mice after MCAO (M-PBS group), and mice administered 5.0 × 104 GFP+ h-iSCs after MCAO were also tested in the Y-maze at 9 weeks post-treatment.

## Statistical analysis
Data are presented as the mean ± SEM. Differences between 2 groups or samples were evaluated statistically using Student’s t test. Behavioral test results were compared among Sham, M-PBS, M-iSC (low), and M-iSC (high) groups by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by multiple comparisons tests, or by repeated-measures ANOVA including factors treatment and time as described. , , P < .05 was considered statistically significant for all tests.

## Results

## h-iSCs Are a Distinctive Novel Stem Cell Population With the Potential to Differentiate Into Neural Lineages
We previously showed that h-iSCs exhibit multipotency and can differentiate into electrophysiologically functional neurons. However, the detailed properties of h-iSCs remain unclear. Therefore, we first examined the traits of h-iSCs by comparing various cell types, including h-ECs, h-ACs, h-NSPCs, and h-MSCs. The results of microarray analysis ( ), including a principal component analysis (PCA) ( ) indicated that h-iSCs are a distinctive cell population. Further scRNA-seq analysis comparing h-iSCs and h-MSCs yielded distinct distributions of cell clusters ( ), confirming that h-iSCs and MSCs are distinctive stem cell populations. Transcriptome analysis ( ) also revealed that h-iSCs expressed both the neural lineage marker nestin and multiple mesenchymal markers (CD44, ENG, ALCAM), so we examined if h-iSCs express these markers at the single-cell level. A violin plot analysis revealed that h-iSCs highly expressed both nestin (NES) ( ) and mesenchymal markers, including CD44 ( ), ENG ( ), and ALCAM ( ). In contrast, the expression of NES was lower in h-MSCs compared with the h-iSCs ( ), whereas high expression of CD44 ( ), ENG ( ), and ALCAM ( ) was also observed in h-MSCs. In addition, scRNA-seq analysis of h-iSCs revealed that 239 of 352 cells (68%) were nestin-positive, and that the majority of these nestin+ cells were simultaneously positive for CD44 (234 of 239 nestin+ cells; 97.9%), ENG (195 of 239 nestin+ cells; 81.6%), and ALCAM (227 of 239 nestin+ cells; 95.0%). These results indicate that most nestin+ cells co-express various mesenchymal markers at the single-cell level, indicating that h-iSCs exhibit features of both neural and mesenchymal lineages.
 Next, we investigated the potential of h-iSCs to differentiate into neural lineages. Consistent with our previous studies, immunohistochemistry showed that putative h-iSCs ( ) expressed the NSPC marker nestin ( ). Then, gene expression patterns of neural markers were evaluated in h-iSCs by comparing them with that of h-NSPCs. A heat map of the microarray results revealed that the astrocytic markers, GFAP, APQ4, and S100B, were weakly expressed in both h-iSCs and h-NSPCs. Although h-NSPCs expressed high levels of neuronal lineage markers including MAP2, TUBB3, NEFL, NEFM, and NEFH, h-iSCs expressed high levels of both neuronal (eg, MAP2, TUBB3, and NEFM) and oligodendrocytic lineage markers (eg, MBP) ( ).
Then, h-iSCs maintained in adherent culture were incubated with floating cultures under conditions that promote the formation of neurosphere-like cell structures ( ). These cell clusters ( ) were then incubated under neural differentiation conditions. Immunohistochemistry revealed that h-iSCs differentiated into Tuj1+ ( ) and NF+ neurons ( ) as well as MBP+ oligodendrocytes ( ) and GFAP+ astrocytes ( ). Moreover, EM revealed myelination formation in clusters after prolonged incubation (up to 3 weeks) under conditions promoting neural differentiation ( ). Thus, h-iSCs have the potential to differentiate into multiple neural lineages.

## h-iSC Transplantation Improves Neurological Function Following Ischemic Stroke
To determine whether h-iSC transplantation affects post-stroke neurological function, mice were divided into 4 groups: (1) sham-operation (Sham group; n = 13), (2) PBS injection after MCAO (M-PBS group; n = 12), (3) low-dose h-iSC administration (1.0 × 104 cells) after MCAO [M-iSC (low) group; n = 13], and (4) high-dose h-iSC administration (5.0 × 104 cells) after MCAO [M-iSC (high) group; n = 13] ( ). Neurological function was assessed in mice at 2-9 weeks following PBS or h-iSC treatment by a behavioral test battery ( ) as described previously. 
 In the wire hang test ( ), latency to fall was significantly shorter in the M-PBS groups compared to the Sham group, indicating reduced motor strength. Further, latency to fall did not differ significantly between M-PBS and M-iSC (low) groups, indicating no substantial improvement. However, latency to fall was significantly longer in the M-iSC (high) group compared to the M-PBS group ( ). In the basket test ( ), latency to reach the floor was significantly longer in M-PBS group compared to the Sham group and did not differ between M-PBS and M-iSC (low) groups, but was significantly reduced in the M-iSC (high) group ( ). Results of the wire hang and basket test indicate that high-dose h-iSC transplantation can improve post-stroke motor function compared post-stroke vehicle treatment (M-PBS group).
Spontaneous locomotor activity was assessed for 3 consecutive days in the open-field test ( ). On day 1, locomotor activity did not differ significantly among Sham, M-PBS, M-iSC (low), and M-iSC (high) groups, but on days 2 and 3, was significantly higher in M-PBS and M-iSC (low) groups compared to the Sham group and significantly lower in the M-iSC (high) group compared to the M-PBS group. In addition, locomotor activity on day 3 was significantly lower in the M-iSC (high) group compared to the M-iSC (low) group ( ), indicating that the higher h-iSC dose was more effective at suppressing stroke-associated hyperactivity.
In the hot plate test ( ) of thermal nociception sensitivity, latency to jump was significantly longer in the M-PBS group than in the Sham group, indicating that stroke-induced thermal hypoalgesia. However, latency to jump was significantly shorter in the M-iSC (high) group than in the M-PBS group and M-iSC (low) group ( ), suggesting that high-dose h-iSC transplantation can reverse thermal hypoalgesia.
In the Y-maze test ( ) of spatial working memory, alternation rate (exploring different arms sequentially without repetition) was significantly reduced in the M-PBS group compared to the Sham group, consistent with a stroke-induced working memory impairment. However, alternation rates were significantly higher in the M-iSC (high) group than in the M-PBS group, indicating that h-iSC transplantation improved this spatial working memory deficit induced by ischemic stroke ( ).
In the passive avoidance-learning test ( ) of long-term associative memory, latency to enter the chamber in which the mouse was previously exposed to an electrical shock was significantly longer in Sham, M-iSC (low), and M-iSC (high) groups compared to corresponding baseline latencies (conditioning), but was significantly shorter in the M-PBS group than in the Sham group on test day, indicating reduced passive avoidance behavior due to an associative memory deficit. Compared to the M-PBS group, latency to enter the shock-paired chamber on the test day was not significantly altered in the M-iSC (low) group, but was significantly prolonged in the high-dose M-iSC group compared to both M-PBS and M-iSC (low) groups ( ), consistent with improved associative memory function.
A diminished capacity to experience pleasure or anhedonia is a core symptom of depression. Moreover, depression is among the most common comorbidities following stroke. Anhedonia was measured in all mouse treatment groups using the sucrose preference test ( ), and results indicated that preference was significantly lower in the M-PBS group than the Sham group. However, sucrose preference rates were significantly higher in both M-iSC (low) and M-iSC (high) groups compared to the M-PBS group ( ), suggesting that h-iSC transplantation mitigated depression-like symptoms.
Depression-like symptoms following ischemic stroke were also evaluated by the time spent immobilized in the tail hang test ( ). Immobility time was significantly longer in the M-PBS group than the Sham group. However, immobility time was significantly shorter in the M-iSC (high) group compared to the M-PBS group ( ). These results indicate that depression-like symptoms were improved by h-iSC transplantation, especially by the higher cell dose.

## Transplanted iSCs Differentiate into Neuronal Cells
Thus far, our data show that h-iSCs can differentiate into neural lineages in vitro and that h-iSC transplantation (in particular high doses of h-iSCs) can improve neurological function after ischemic stroke. Therefore, to elucidate the underlying mechanism, mice were transplanted with high doses of GFP-labeled h-iSCs ( ) 6 weeks after MCAO ( , ).
 On day 3 after transplantation, GFP-labeled h-iSCs were observed around the injection site ( , ). Then, brain sections were subject to immunohistochemistry with an anti-nestin antibody, which reacts with the human protein, but not the mouse. Immunohistochemistry revealed that many of the GFP+ h-iSCs expressed nestin ( , ). However, on day 3 following transplantation, they did not express the neuronal marker Tuj1 or the oligodendrocytic marker MBP or astrocytic marker GFAP (data not shown). Thus, injected cells at this stage retained stem cell status and did not differentiate into mature neural cells.
On day 10 after transplantation, however, some GFP+ h-iSCs exhibited neurite-like formations ( ) that were immunopositive for the mature neuronal markers Tuj1 ( ) and MAP2 ( ). These results indicate that some h-iSCs transplanted into post-stroke mouse brains differentiated into neuronal cells. On day 10 after transplantation, all mice had GFP+ h-iSCs in the brain. However, 7 weeks following transplantation, GFP+ h-iSCs were not observed in 10/23 (43.48%) mice, indicating that transplanted GFP+ h-iSCs do not survive in the post-stroke brain microenvironment for a long time. In contrast, 7 weeks following transplantation, 13/23 (56.52%) mice still retained GFP+ h-iSCs in the brain and some of which co-expressed MAP2 ( ). Of note, some endogenous neurons (GFP−/MAP2+ cells) appear to contact these transplanted h-iSC-derived neurons (GFP+/MAP2+ cells) ( ). Thus, a subpopulation of h-iSC-derived neurons may have integrated into neural circuits with endogenous neurons.
Next, we examined if these differentiated neurons can improve neurological function by comparing Y-maze performance among Sham, PBS-injected, and GFP+ h-iSC-injected mouse groups. Alternation rate was significantly lower in the M-PBS group (n = 20) than the Sham group (n = 24), but was significantly higher in the subpopulation of mice retaining GFP+ h-iSCs (h-iSC GFP(+) group, n = 13) compared to the M-PBS group ( ). Alternation rate was also numerically higher in the M-iSC subpopulation lacking GFP+ h-iSCs (h-iSC GFP(−) group; n = 10) compared to the M-PBS group. This finding suggests that not only neuronal replacement by grafted h-iSCs but also other factors contribute to neurological improvement following h-iSC transplantation.

## Transplanted h-iSCs Activate Endogenous NSPCs
Based on observations that some endogenous neurons (GFP−/MAP2+ cells) appeared to contact transplanted h-iSC-derived neurons (GFP+/MAP2+ cells) in the post-stroke mouse brain ( ), we examined if h-iSC transplantation influences endogenous NSPCs. To investigate the fate of NSPCs exactly, we employed Nestin-GFP transgenic line which we recently developed by a backcrossing technique ( ). , Briefly, these Nestin-GFP mice were transplanted with mCherry-labeled h-iSCs ( ) 6 weeks after MCAO ( , ). On day 3 after transplantation, immunohistochemistry revealed that mCherry-labeled h-iSCs were present around the injection site ( ). Compared to PBS-injected controls ( ), many GFP+ NSPCs were present in proximity to mCherry-labeled h-iSCs ( ), indicating that locally derived endogenous NSPCs are activated by h-iSC transplantation.
 

## h-iSCs Enhance the Proliferation of Endogenous NSPCs In Vitro
To obtain direct evidence that h-iSCs activate endogenous NSPCs, regionally derived NSPCs isolated from mouse brains (m-NSPCs) were cocultured with h-iSCs. Briefly, m-NSPCs were plated on dishes and incubated alone ( ) or with GFP+ h-iSCs ( ). On day 8, cultures were fixed and immunostained with a mouse-specific nestin antibody. Compared to control cultures containing m-NSPCs alone ( , ), cocultures exhibited significantly greater numbers of nestin+ m-NSPCs (GFP−/nestin+ cells) ( , ). Similarly, compared to m-NSPC mono-cultures ( , ), cocultures harbored a significantly greater number of Sox2+ m-NSPCs (GFP−/Sox2+ cells) ( , ). Furthermore, compared to m-NSPC mono-cultures ( , ), the ratio of Ki67+ m-NSPCs (GFP-/Ki67+ cells to GFP-/DAPI+ cells) was significantly greater in cocultures ( , ). These results indicate that h-iSCs can promote the proliferation of m-NSPCs, thereby increasing the number of m-NSPCs in vitro.
 

## h-iSCs Promote the Neuronal Differentiation of Endogenous NSPCs In Vitro
We further examined whether the presence of h-iSCs can promote the differentiation of m-NSPCs into neural cells. Briefly, m-NSPC-derived neurospheres were seeded on dishes alone ( ) or with GFP+ h-iSCs ( ) for 7 days, then fixed and immunostained for GFP, Tuj1, MBP, and GFAP. Both m-NSPCs cultured alone ( , , ) and cocultured with GFP+ h-iSCs ( , , ) differentiated into neurons (GFP−/Tuj1+ cells) ( , ), oligodendrocytes (GFP−/MBP+ cells) ( , ), and astrocytes (GFP−/GFAP+ cells) ( , ). However, the ratio of m-NSPC-derived neurons (GFP−/Tuj1+ cells to GFP−/DAPI+ cells) was significantly higher in cocultures compared to mono-cultures ( ), whereas the ratio of ­m-NSPC-derived oligodendrocytes (GFP−/MBP+ cells to GFP−/DAPI+ cells) ( ) and the ratio of ­m-NSPC-derived astrocytes (GFP−/GFAP+ cells to GFP−/DAPI+ cells) ( ) did not differ between culture conditions. These findings indicate that the presence of h-iSCs promotes the neuronal differentiation of m-NSPCs.
 

## Potential of Neuronal Network Formation Between h-iSC-Derived Neurons and m-NSPC-Derived Neurons
Collectively, the results presented thus far indicated that transplanted h-iSCs can differentiate into neuronal cells and activate NSPCs around the injection site. In addition, coculture experiments showed that the presence of h-iSCs promotes the proliferation and neuronal differentiation of m-NSPCs.
Therefore, we investigated the potential circuit formation between h-iSC-derived neurons and endogenous neurons by co-culturing h-iSCs with m-NSPCs for up to 3 weeks under conditions that promote neural differentiation ( ) and immunostaining for synaptic proteins. Both GFP+ ­h-iSC-derived cells and GFP− m-NSPC-derived cells expressed the mature neuronal marker MAP2 ( ). In addition, both GFP− m-NSPC-derived cells and GFP+ h-iSC-derived cells expressed the presynaptic marker synaptophysin (SYP) ( ). Further, GFP+ h-iSC-derived cells made contact with GFP− m-NSPC-derived cells expressing the postsynaptic marker postsynaptic ­density-95 (PSD-95) ( ).
 

## Discussion
This is the first preclinical study that demonstrates the efficacy of cell therapy using h-iSCs. In the present study, some transplanted h-iSCs survived and differentiated into mature neurons. Further, these neurons likely contributed to improved neurological function as improvements were observed primarily in mice with surviving (GFP+) h-iSCs. In addition, transplanted h-iSCs activated endogenous NSPCs near the injection site. Consistent with these findings, coculture experiments showed that the presence of h-iSCs increased m-NSPC proliferation and neuronal differentiation. Thus, transplanted h-iSCs may improve functional recovery through neuronal replacement by grafted cells as well as neurogenesis by activated endogenous NSPCs. Moreover, we found that some endogenous neurons formed neuronal networks with h-iSC-derived neurons in coculture and likely also in vivo, suggesting that these cells can co-integrate into neural circuits to restore neurological function. The precise contributions of these cells to reparative neuroplasticity require much future study.
Specific regions of the adult mammalian brain such as the SVZ and hippocampal subgranular zone harbor NSPCs throughout life. In addition, we previously reported that iSCs appear at the site of neural damage in mice following ischemia , , and spinal cord injury. These cells may be converted into NSPC-like cells through a process akin to mesenchymal-epithelial transition or by BMP signaling. However, genetic mapping using a Cre-loxP system showed that iSCs are distinct both in origin and genetic phenotype from NSPCs that develop at conventional neurogenic zones such as the SVZ. Consistent with these findings, the present study showed that h-iSCs express different lineage markers and thus may represent an independent cell population participating in neural repair and cell replacement following injury.
Using brain samples obtained from the ischemic areas of stroke patients requiring partial lobectomy and decompressive craniectomy, we recently demonstrated that h-iSCs with neuronal differentiation potentially develop within ischemic areas of the human brain. In addition, we reported that h-iSCs extracted from ischemic areas exhibit high proliferative potential in a culture medium containing the growth factors bFGF and EGF, indicating that a large number of these cells can be produced in vitro for exogenous cell transplantation. Thus, h-iSCs may be a valuable source of cells for stroke therapy.
In the present study, to investigate the efficiency of h-iSCs against ischemic stroke, we performed a preclinical study by administering low or high doses of h-iSCs to post-stroke mouse brains. In addition, we transplanted h-iSCs at chronic periods (6 weeks after MCAO) for the following reasons: (1) a sufficient quantity of h-iSCs can be prepared in vitro for the chronic periods, even in the case of autologous transplantation; (2) there are no efficient therapies for patients with stroke during chronic periods despite the existence of many patients suffering from sequelae. We found that h-iSC transplantation (in particular high doses of h-iSCs) promoted neurological recovery following MCAO. In most cases, these improvements were observed only following injection of the higher cell dose, possibly because only this large quantity of cells was sufficient to replace neurons lost to ischemia. Alternatively, endogenous NSPCs may be activated to a greater extent by higher doses of transplanted h-iSCs. Although the optimal dose of transplanted h-iSCs should be determined in future studies, the present study indicates that autologous transplantation by h-iSCs can be applied to stroke patients during subacute and chronic periods after a satisfactory expansion of h-iSCs in vitro. However, autologous transplantation has several challenges. For example, it may be technically difficult to obtain sufficient h-iSC numbers from patients with small infarct regions (eg, lacunar infarctions). The identification of specific h-iSC markers may enable stock of large numbers of h-iSCs, thereby enabling allogenic transplantation even during the acute post-stroke period by thawing frozen cells. However, several problems, most notably immunological rejection, must be solved before allogenic transplantation of h-iSCs in patients.
In the present study, we transplanted h-iSCs into immunodeficient mice to prevent immunological rejection, but the number of transplanted h-iSCs still decreased gradually over the following weeks. This depletion may be due to cross-species rejection mediated by residual immune cells such as natural killer cells that function normally even in immunodeficient mice (such as conventional SCID mice). Further studies using more severely immunodeficient mice (eg, NOD SCID mice, NOG mice) or studies using immunosuppressants targeting certain immune cell populations may be necessary to identify the reasons for limited h-iSC survival following intracranial transplantation.
Currently, there are several ongoing trials investigating the therapeutic efficacy of h-MSC administration for stroke patients. Although we previously demonstrated that h-iSCs and h-MSCs are both multipotent, single-cell RNA-seq analysis clearly showed that h-iSCs and h-MSCs are distinct stem cell populations. In addition, our recent study showed that h-iSCs, but not h-MSCs, can differentiate into electrophysiologically functional neurons. While the reason for this greater neuronal differentiation potential is unclear, we previously reported that iSCs are likely derived from brain pericytes originating from the neural crest, and so may retain both neural and mesenchymal lineage differentiation potential. In support of this idea, the neural lineage marker nestin was more highly expressed in h-iSCs than h-MSCs. Thus, h-iSC phenotype may be closer to NSPCs than h-MSCs.
Neuroinflammation is a major factor in stroke-related damage, so it is possible that h-iSC transplantation restores neurological function at least in part by regulating inflammation. Whether transplanted h-iSCs exert immunomodulatory effects, as shown for transplanted h-MSCs, must be clarified in future studies, ideally using immunocompetent mice.
Several additional challenges must be addressed before clinical application of h-iSCs. For example, it remains unclear which regions are most appropriate for h-iSC transplantation. In our preliminary study, we found that h-iSCs transplanted within ischemic areas did not survive as long as those transplanted adjacent to ischemic areas. The transplantation protocol also requires optimization. In the present study, we transplanted h-iSCs only once following ischemic stroke; however, greater efficacy may require repeated administration. To mitigate variability in efficacy due to different h-iSC sources, we transplanted h-iSCs obtained from the same patient group. While the basic properties of these h-iSCs (eg, proliferation and differentiation capacity) were almost the same as reported in a previous study, the individual patient source may still influence the therapeutic outcome. Further, it is unclear if there are distinct subpopulations of h-iSCs and if some are more effective as grafted cells than others. Another important issue to resolve is the potential for tumor formation, although we have found no evidence that transplanted h-iSCs form tumors in vivo (data not shown). Nonetheless, the potential for tumorigenesis should be carefully investigated in future studies.
In conclusion, we demonstrate that h-iSC transplantation can improve neurological function in an ischemic stroke model. Although the transplantation protocol needs improvement and many safety issues must be addressed before clinical trials, the present study provides compelling evidence for the therapeutic potential of h-iSC transplantation for stroke patients.
