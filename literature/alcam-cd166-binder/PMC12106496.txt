## Abstract
Soft-tissue sarcomas represent a diverse group of rare malignancies originating from mesenchymal tissue, accounting for less than 1% of adult cancers in the USA. With over 13,000 new cases and around 5350 deaths annually, patients with metastatic soft-tissue sarcomas face limited therapeutic options and an estimated median overall survival of 18 months. While immunotherapy has demonstrated effectiveness in several cancers, its application in soft-tissue sarcomas remains challenging owing to the tumors’ largely “cold” immunological environment, characterized by low levels of tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes and a lack of soft-tissue sarcoma-specific biomarkers. This review examines potential mechanisms underlying immunotherapy resistance in soft-tissue sarcomas, including the complex interplay between innate and adaptive immunity, the tumor microenvironment, and the role of immune-related genes. Despite preliminary findings suggesting correlations between immune profiles and histological subtypes, consistent biomarkers for predicting immunotherapeutic responses across soft-tissue sarcoma types are absent. Emerging strategies focus on converting “cold” tumors to “hot” tumors, enhancing their susceptibility to immunologic activation. While research is ongoing, personalized treatment approaches may offer hope for overcoming the inherent heterogeneity and resistance seen in soft-tissue sarcomas, ultimately aiming to improve outcomes for affected patients.

## Key Points

 

## Introduction
Soft-tissue sarcomas (STSs) are a collection of rare and diverse malignant tumors that develop from the transformation of mesenchymal tissue. These cancers make up less than 1% of all adult malignancies in the USA, with an estimated 13,130 new cases and around 5350 deaths per year [ ]. Over 100 different STS histological and molecular subtypes exist, each with unique clinical, therapeutic, and prognostic features [ ]. Patients who develop metastatic disease have limited therapeutic options and a median overall survival (OS) of about 18 months [ , – ].
Systemic treatment options are very limited and have a low response rate and significant toxicities that often limit treatment dosing and length of therapy [ – ]. The advent of immunotherapy has shown positive results in certain cancers, including melanoma, non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), and renal cell cancer [ – ]; however, its use for STS remains challenging because of the limited response in clinical trials and the lack of STS-specific biomarkers. Immunologically, STS can be described as “cold” [ ]. “Hot” or “inflamed” tumors have a high number of tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs) and tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs), which means they actively modulate the immune system; cold tumors are the opposite [ ]. Studies have found that most STSs are immunologically cold, which could explain why they are less likely to benefit from these immunological therapies compared with their hot tumor counterparts. By combining approaches, studies have looked at potentially modifying these cold tumors to become hot, making them more vulnerable to immunologic responses [ , ].
Several studies have aimed to identify immune-related genes in the complex immune profile of STS. Using CIBERSORT technology, researchers found immune signatures correlated with a histological subtype [ ]. While each immune histotype has a diverse immune profile, no consistent biomarkers were found across all sarcoma types [ ]. Others have found preliminary evidence of immune-related genes associated with the tumor microenvironment (TME), impacting prognosis and OS. This further validates the potential resistance mechanisms of STS owing to inadequate immune responses [ ]. It is premature to draw conclusions from these initial observations; however, research is progressing toward personalized treatment for the resistant and heterogeneous nature of STS. Although many questions remain unanswered, this review focuses on exploring the most recent scientific literature regarding proposed STS mechanisms of immunotherapy resistance.

## The Immune System

## Innate Versus Adaptive Immunity
The innate immune system is often described as the first line of defense, acting quickly and broadly to stop pathogens. It includes the physical skin barrier; chemical barriers like enzymes; cellular defenses like macrophages and neutrophils; proteins; and small molecules like the complement system that circulate in the bloodstream [ ]. Conversely, the adaptive immune system is a second line of defense that is slower to react but highly specific to the pathogen [ ]. Adaptive responses include B cells, which, upon encountering an antigen, proliferate and differentiate into both plasma cells and memory B cells. Plasma cells are responsible for the production of antibodies, and memory cells provide long-term immunity to a specific pathogen. Antibodies target pathogens for destruction by phagocytes, natural killer (NK) cells, cytotoxic T cells, and the complement system. Memory cells persist in the body, allowing for a more rapid and robust response when the same pathogen is reencountered [ ].

## Tumor Microenvironment (TME)
The TME consists of various cell types, vascular structures, signaling molecules, and the extracellular matrix that surrounds tumor cells. It is a complex setting that impedes immune cell functionality and survivability through interwoven and overlapping features.

## Tumor-Infiltrating Lymphocytes (TILs)
Tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes are immune cells that have infiltrated tumor tissues and are pivotal in identifying and potentially targeting cancerous cells for elimination [ ]. Tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes are made up of CD3+ proteins; they can exert specific cytotoxic antitumor activity (CD8+) and promote (CD4+/T helper-1) or limit (CD4+/T helper-2, CD4+/FOXP3+ regulatory T cell [Tregs]) antitumor responses [ ]. They have been identified in many types of solid tumors; when infiltrating the tumor, they correlate with an improved response to cancer treatments [ , ]. Compared with immunoreactive carcinomas, STSs have lower numbers of TILs per gram of tissue and lower CD4/CD8 ratios of TIL infiltration [ , ].
Recent immunotherapeutic approaches have targeted TILs, aiming to harness their therapeutic potential [ ]. Traditionally, TILs are extracted from patient tumors and expanded in culture ex vivo before being infused back into patients [ ]. Tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes have been identified and demonstrated efficacy in melanoma and some solid tumors, including cervical cancer, with promising results in colorectal cancer, cholangiocarcinoma, NSCLC, and breast cancer [ – ]. In general, malignancies with high TILs have a greater response to treatment [ , ]. In a phase III clinical trial, melanoma showed a notable response to TIL therapy, demonstrating an overall response rate of nearly 50% [ ].
Therapeutic approaches using TILs in patients with advanced unresectable STSs have been tested in various settings [ , ]. In 2021, Mullinax et al. successfully generated clinically significant amounts of TILs from resected sarcomas. This groundbreaking publication defined the method of rapid expansion of TIL culture for clinical use [ ]. An active clinical trial conducted by Mullinax and his team is underway evaluating the validity of TIL infusions with an intravenous infusion of interleukin-2, fludarabine, and cyclophosphamide (lymphodepletion plus adoptive TIL therapy) [ClinicalTrials.gov: NCT04052334]. Additionally, a phase II clinical trial is investigating the use of combined TIL therapy and aldesleukin in patients with advanced STS (ClinicalTrials.gov: NCT03935893) [see Table ].
 Tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes have several advantages over other forms of immunotherapy. Because there can be multiple TIL clones, they can recognize a broader array of tumor antigens [ , , – ]. Despite the major limitation of the lack of persistence of transferred autologous mature lymphocytes in humans, TILs, when expanded ex vivo, can be activated without the suppressive TME of Tregs, myeloid-derived suppressor cells, and inhibitory macrophages. This offers a potential solution to the challenges of TIL therapy in STS, providing an ideal environment for continued expansion and engraftment and paving the way for further research and development in this field.
The immunosuppressive nature of STS’s TME could also be a barrier to the effectiveness of TIL therapy [ ]. Soft-tissue sarcoma exhibits a particularly immunosuppressive TME, which can impede the effectiveness of TIL therapy [ , ]. It has been reported that STS can actively change the TME by secreting factors to convert immunoreactive M1 macrophages to immunosuppressive and tumorigenic M2 macrophages [ ].
Myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) are a group of immune cells with unique suppressive capabilities that play crucial roles in various diseases, particularly cancer [ ]. These cells have been linked to poorer clinical outcomes in cancer and are typically rare in healthy individuals; however, they gradually accumulate in the blood and lymphoid organs of individuals with certain tumors [ , ]. Tumors adapt their microenvironment to support rapid proliferation, survival, and differentiation [ ]. In metastatic cancers, MDSCs exhibit spontaneous migratory activity and are continuously recruited to sites of chronic inflammation [ ]. One key mechanism involves the chemokine CCL2, which induces monocytes to adopt an MDSC phenotype and recruits them to the tumor site [ ]. Once there, MDSCs secrete molecules such as transforming growth factor (TGF)-β, inducible nitric oxide synthase, and reactive oxygen species to suppress T-cell antitumor responses [ , ].
Myeloid-derived suppressor cell migration is driven by the chemokine receptor CXCR in response to CSCL 1 and 2 [ ]. Inhibition of CXCR with small-molecule inhibitors has shown promise in improving outcomes in STS and enhancing the activity of programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1) inhibitors [ , ]. Cancers with high levels of MDSCs are associated with significant intratumor immunosuppression, which correlates with poor tumor progression [ , ]. Harnessing the potential of MDSCs and understanding their immunosuppressive mechanisms are paramount in defining their role in immunotherapy.
Soft-tissue sarcomas, in particular, have shown inhibitory environments that impair their ability to elicit effective immune responses [ ]. While therapy targeting the immunosuppressive effects of MDSCs has shown promise in promoting antitumor responses in various cancers, its potential application in sarcoma remains poorly understood [ ]. Myeloid-derived suppressor cells employ several mechanisms to mediate immunosuppression, including the production of reactive oxygen species, which dampens T-cell signaling activation, and the consumption of amino acids required for T-cell function and activation [ ]. Indoleamine 3,4 dioxygenase is a tryptophan catabolic enzyme that is highly upregulated in metastatic cancers and suppresses local CD8 killer T cells while inducing MDSCs and Tregs, making it a potential therapeutic target [ – ].
There is evidence suggesting that MDSCs could also promote tumor progression through their impact on the antitumor activity of NK cells. For example, cell-cell contact between MDSCs and NK cells in mice with tumors has been shown to inhibit NK-cell activation, reducing their ability to target tumor cells [ – ]. A study conducted by Ma et al. verified the cell-cell contact between MDSC and NK cells in mice, proving that tumors can inhibit the interaction and, thus, activation of NK cells, decreasing their ability to attack tumor cells [ ].
While TIL therapy has shown promise in immunogenic cancers such as melanoma, its application in STS remains experimental, with limited data from small-scale studies. The low baseline immune infiltration and immunosuppressive TME of STS create substantial barriers, as these tumors exhibit low CD8+ T-cell infiltration and dominance of immunosuppressive cells like MDSCs and TAMs. Although ex vivo expansion of TILs allows for selective enrichment of tumor-reactive lymphocytes, it does not fully address the hostile immune landscape of STS, where high levels of TGF-β, vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), and other immunosuppressive signals restrict T-cell activity.
Additionally, the durability of TIL responses in STS remains unclear owing to limited long-term follow-up data and a lack of comprehensive studies evaluating relapse mechanisms. Given the heterogeneity within STS, only specific subtypes such as undifferentiated pleomorphic sarcoma (UPS), which displays higher programmed cell death 1 ligand 1 (PD-L1) expression and T-cell infiltration, may benefit. In contrast, immune-cold subtypes like leiomyosarcoma (LMS) and dedifferentiated liposarcoma (DDLPS) may remain resistant. The absence of robust randomized trials with standardized response criteria and biomarker-driven patient selection further complicates efforts to assess the true therapeutic impact of TILs in this setting.
To advance TIL therapy for STS, a multifaceted strategy integrating immune modulation with adoptive cell therapy is essential. Approaches such as targeting TAMs and MDSCs, blocking TGF-β signaling, and disrupting VEGF-mediated immunosuppression could improve TIL infiltration and persistence within the TME. Enhancing TIL metabolic fitness and engineering resistance to inhibitory cytokines may increase response durability. The current reliance on small early-phase trials highlights the need for larger well-controlled studies with histology-specific stratification to determine which STS subtypes may benefit from TIL therapy. Without addressing these barriers, the clinical translation of TIL-based approaches in STS remains uncertain, necessitating a more refined, mechanistically informed approach to overcome resistance mechanisms and optimize outcomes.

## Checkpoint Inhibitors

## Programmed Cell Death Protein 1 (PD-1)/Programmed Cell Death 1 Ligand 1 (PD-L1) Axis
Programmed cell death protein 1 is a protein that is expressed on active immune cells, notably T and B lymphocytes, macrophages, monocytes, and dendritic cells [ ] [see Fig. ]. When PD-1 binds to its ligands, PD-L1/2, both the active and innate immune responses are inhibited [ ]. Cells have been shown to utilize these proteins to maintain peripheral tolerance [ ]. Because of the immunomodulatory function of the PD-1/PD-L1 axis, many malignancies, including renal cell carcinoma, NSCLC, and pancreatic cancer, express PD-L1 as a method of evading the immune system’s innate and adaptive response [ – ].
 Programmed cell death 1 ligand 1 has been expressed in several malignancies, and relative expression of PD-L1 has been found to correlate with poor prognosis in several cancers, including renal cell carcinoma, melanoma, and hepatocellular carcinoma, to name a few [ , – ]. However, its expression is not a biomarker of response in STS.
The SARC028 multicenter phase II trial (NCT02301039) evaluated the anti-PD-1 monoclonal antibody pembrolizumab in patients with advanced STS and osteosarcomas [ ]. Among the 40 patients in the study, seven (18%) demonstrated an objective response as assessed by the Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors (RECIST), with the most favorable outcomes observed in UPS and DDLPS. Although the primary endpoint was not achieved in the STS cohort, the 12-week progression-free survival (PFS) rate of 55% underscores meaningful therapeutic activity. In the follow-up expansion of the SARC028 study, the UPS cohort successfully met its primary endpoint, achieving a 23% response rate [ ]. Patients with alveolar soft part sarcoma (ASPS) have shown remarkable responses to anti-PD-L1-based therapies. In a phase II clinical trial, pembrolizumab combined with the VEGF receptor tyrosine kinase inhibitor (TKI) axitinib demonstrated significant activity in 30 patients with advanced sarcomas. Notably, the greatest efficacy was observed in TKI-naïve patients with progressive ASPS, highlighting the potential of this combination therapy [ ]. In 11 patients with ASPS, six had a partial response, and two had stable disease with a 72.7% 3-month PFS. Additionally, atezolizumab as a single agent showed a 42% objective response rate in eight of 19 patients with advanced ASPS who were naïve to PD-1/PD-L1 treatment [ ]. Response to PD-1/PD-L1 blockade appears to vary by histology, as evidenced by the lack of efficacy in patients with advanced uterine LMS. These patients showed no significant response to nivolumab, with a median PFS (mPFS) of just 1.8 months. Understanding these differences can help guide more effective histology-based treatment strategies in the future for patients with STS [ ] (see Table ).
 Several clinical trials are underway, each with the potential to significantly impact cancer treatment. These include evaluating the efficacy of combined therapy, such as CAB-AXL-ADC in combination with a PD-L1 inhibitor (ClinicalTrials.gov: NCT03425279) and exploring chimeric antigen receptor T-cell (CAR T) therapy in combination with both pembrolizumab and nivolumab (ClinicalTrials.gov: NCT04995003). Another trial is testing a combination of durvalumab and tremelimumab plus radiation for high-risk STS (NEXIS) [ClinicalTrials.gov: NCT03116529], while another is looking at intratumoral injections of TTI-621 in subjects with relapsed and refractory solid tumors and mycosis fungoides (ClinicalTrials.gov: NCT04996004).
A high tumor mutational burden (TMB) has been linked to response to immunotherapy [ ]. Highly mutated tumors have been shown to produce proportionally higher neoantigens, which correlates with a higher TMB [ ]. There is a growing body of evidence supporting the use of the TMB as a prognostic biomarker for immunotherapy, which indicates a better overall response rate, disease control rate, OS, and PFS [ – ].
High TMB levels are infrequent in STS, which may contribute to a limited response to immunotherapeutic agents owing to reduced neoantigen production [ ]. It is important to note that while not classified as a mechanism of resistance, this factor holds significant importance in this context.
Other proposed biomarkers include lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) levels, suggesting that tumors with low serum LDH levels are more likely to respond to checkpoint inhibitor therapy like anti-cytotoxic T-lymphocyte-associated protein 4 (CTLA-4) therapy [ ]. A study looking at the LDH levels of 103 patients demonstrated a connection between high serum LDH levels and higher metastasis [ ]. Although promising, the use of LDH as a biomarker is not common because of its low specificity: it can be elevated in a wide range of conditions, from tissue damage and blood disorders to infections and cancer.

## Cytotoxic T-Lymphocyte-Associated Protein 4 (CTLA-4) Inhibitors
Cytotoxic T-lymphocyte-associated protein 4 is an immune checkpoint receptor (see Fig. ) that suppresses immune responses by interacting with two distinct ligands: CD80 and CD86 [ ]. Ipilimumab, the only US Food and Drug Administration-approved CTLA-4 inhibitor, was tested with the PD-1 inhibitor nivolumab in two randomized phase II trials for unresectable or metastatic STS (Alliance A091401, ClinicalTrials.gov: NCT02500797 [ ] and NCT01643278 [ ]). The Alliance A091401 trial assessed nivolumab alone and in combination with ipilimumab in a non-comparative design. In the single-agent nivolumab arm, only two of 38 patients (5%) achieved a confirmed response, with an mPFS of 1.7 months. This arm failed to meet the primary endpoint of a ≥ 13% response rate and was deemed less effective than existing chemotherapy-based treatments [ ]. The combination of ipilimumab and nivolumab showed efficacy across various sarcoma subtypes, including UPS, LMS, angiosarcoma, and myxofibrosarcoma, achieving the primary endpoint of a confirmed objective response rate. Among 38 chemotherapy-refractory patients treated with this immunotherapy, the response rate was 16%, with an mPFS of 4.1 months [ ].
 Despite extensive research on the PD-1/PD-L1 axis in cancer immunotherapy, its role in STS is not fully understood, with highly variable responses across subtypes. While immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) have been successful in cancers such as melanoma and NSCLC, their impact on STS has been limited, benefiting only a subset of patients. Certain histologies, such as UPS, ASPS, and DDLPS, show greater sensitivity to PD-1 blockade, whereas LMS exhibits intrinsic resistance. Emerging evidence suggests that differences in TME composition, immune cell infiltration, and antigen presentation may explain these disparities. Unlike other tumors where PD-L1 expression correlates with treatment efficacy, its predictive value in STS is not reliable, as most PD-L1-positive sarcomas do not respond to ICIs and few PD-L1-negative sarcomas may respond [ ]. This raises concerns about relying on PD-L1 as a biomarker and highlights the need to explore additional predictors of response, such as immune gene expression profiles and TIL characteristics.
A major challenge in understanding ICI efficacy in STS is the lack of large histology-specific trials. Most studies group diverse STS subtypes together despite their distinct molecular and immune characteristics, leading to low accrual of individual histologies. This limits statistical power and makes it difficult to draw definitive conclusions.
Current evidence suggests that some sarcomas are naturally more immunogenic, showing higher T-cell infiltration and inflammatory signaling. In contrast, others are immune excluded, dominated by suppressive elements like MDSCs and TAMs. These immune-cold tumors often exhibit low TMB, deficient antigen presentation, and a macrophage-enriched TME, creating an inhospitable environment for T-cell-mediated tumor destruction. Moreover, ICI effectiveness is likely influenced by factors beyond the tumor itself, including systemic immune dysfunction and stromal architecture, which can act as barriers to immune infiltration. Without stratifying sarcoma subtypes based on their immunological profiles, it is difficult to determine whether poor response rates stem from inherent resistance mechanisms or trial design limitations.
To optimize immunotherapy in STS, a more refined, biologically informed approach is needed. Rather than broadly applying ICIs across all sarcomas, they should be tailored to histologies that exhibit immune responsiveness; combination strategies should be explored in immune-resistant subtypes. Therapies targeting VEGF-driven immunosuppression, myeloid-derived immune suppression, or defective antigen presentation could help reshape the TME and enhance ICI activity. Additionally, biomarker development must go beyond PD-L1 expression to incorporate multidimensional immune profiling, ensuring treatment is directed toward patients most likely to benefit. Without addressing these fundamental issues, the application of ICIs in STS will remain largely empirical, missing the opportunity to harness the full potential of immunotherapy in this challenging disease.

## Sarcoma Heterogeneity
Soft-tissue sarcomas are highly heterogeneous mesenchymal malignancies with distinct genetic alterations and TME compositions that influence their response to ICIs. Currently, there are no universal biomarkers that predict ICI response. Unlike tumors with a high TMB and strong antigenicity, most STSs exhibit a low TMB, weak neoantigen expression, and an immunosuppressive TME [ , ], likely limiting ICI efficacy [ , ]. However, responses vary significantly across histologies, reflecting differences in the mutational burden, immune infiltration, and stromal composition, underscoring the need for tailored therapeutic strategies.
Undifferentiated pleomorphic sarcoma and ASPS exhibit relatively inflamed immune profiles, making them more responsive to ICIs. Undifferentiated pleomorphic sarcoma often harbors higher PD-L1 expression, increased CD8+ T-cell infiltration, and a higher mutational load, contributing to an immune-active TME [ ]. However, MDSCs, TAMs, and Tregs remain dominant, leading to immune suppression despite the presence of infiltrating T cells [ ]. Despite its typically low TMB, ASPS is highly vascularized owing to the ASPS chromosome region 1-transcription factor E3 (ASPSCR1-TFE3) fusion, which upregulates angiogenesis and potentially immune evasion pathways [ ]. This reliance on VEGF signaling may explain its sensitivity to a VEGF blockade, which can enhance immune activation.
Leiomyosarcoma exhibits intrinsic resistance to ICIs, largely owing to low baseline T-cell infiltration, poor antigen presentation, and an immunosuppressive TME [ , , ]. Leiomyosarcoma displays low PD-L1 expression and a macrophage-dominant TME. Macrophages can transition between an M1-like antitumoral phenotype and an M2-like pro-tumoral phenotype based on the cytokine composition of their microenvironment [ ]. The high M2/M1 ratio is associated with homologous recombination deficiency, copy number alterations, and a poorer prognosis [ , ]. Dedifferentiated liposarcomas are characterized by mouse double minute 2/cyclin-dependent kinase 4 (MDM2/CDK4) amplifications [ , ]. Dedifferentiated liposarcoma and well-differentiated liposarcoma show roughly 10–20% PD-L1 expression in both immunohistochemistry and gene expression studies [ – ]. Compared with other STSs, DDLPSs have higher proportions of TILs and PD-L1 expression, though less infiltration than subtypes like UPS [ , ]. Tumor infiltrating lymphocytes are present in over 50% of cases, including NK cells, B cells, tertiary lymphoid structures, and co-inhibitory checkpoints such as lymphocyte-activation gene 3 (LAG3) and T-cell immunoglobulin and mucin-domain containing-3 (TIM3) [ , ]. However, T-cell infiltration is less oligoclonal than in LMS or UPS [ ]. Dedifferentiated liposarcoma have significant TAMs but also substantial T-cell infiltration, making the TAM/TIL ratio average similar to well-differentiated liposarcoma [ ].
Synovial sarcoma, despite expressing immunogenic cancer-testis antigens such as NY-ESO-1, often lacks sustained immune infiltration because of defective antigen presentation and human leukocyte antigen (HLA) downregulation, resulting in an immune-cold phenotype [ ]. The genetic and microenvironmental diversity among STS subtypes highlights the limitations of a uniform immunotherapy approach. While certain histologies may be intrinsically susceptible to immune activation, others require strategies beyond a PD-1/PD-L1 blockade. Tumor microenvironment-intrinsic resistance mechanisms, including immune exclusion, stromal fibrosis, and angiogenic signaling, remain significant barriers, necessitating approaches that integrate targeted immune modulation, stromal reprogramming, and antigen-directed therapies to overcome histology-specific resistance [ – ].

## Chemotherapy Combinations Using the ICI Backbone
One key challenge in treating sarcomas is their intrinsic heterogeneity, which manifests in the diverse biological behaviors of their subtypes. In general, they remain largely resistant to ICIs, likely owing to an immune-cold TME with low T-cell infiltration and high immunosuppressive signals. Several studies have demonstrated that targeting VEGF-driven angiogenesis alongside a PD-1 blockade can partially overcome these barriers by increasing immune infiltration and modulating the TME.
Wilky et al. conducted a phase II trial that assessed the efficacy of combining axitinib, a VEGF receptor TKI, with pembrolizumab, an anti-PD-1 ICI, in 33 patients with advanced sarcomas, including ASPS. The study aimed to determine if a VEGF blockade could enhance immune responses in sarcomas by counteracting the immunosuppressive TME. With a median follow-up of 14.7 months, the 3-month PFS rate was 65.6% overall and 72.7% in ASPS. Partial responses were seen in 25% of patients, with ASPS showing the highest response rate at 54.5%. The combination therapy had manageable toxicity, with common grade 3/4 adverse events (AEs), including hypertension (15%) and autoimmune toxicities (15%) [ ]. These findings indicate that axitinib plus pembrolizumab shows promising clinical activity, particularly in ASPS, and warrants further investigation in randomized trials. While responses in non-ASPS sarcomas were more modest, a subset of patients experienced significant long-term benefits, highlighting the potential of VEGF and a PD-1 blockade as a therapeutic strategy in sarcomas.
Martin-Broto et al. reported a phase Ib/II multicenter trial that tested the combination of nivolumab (an anti-PD-1 drug) and sunitinib (a VEGF receptor TKI) in 68 patients with advanced STS. The goal was to see if blocking both angiogenesis and immune checkpoints could improve treatment outcomes. The 6-month PFS rate was 48%, well above the 15% threshold, and the median OS was 24 months, indicating better results than standard second-line therapies. About 21% of patients had an objective response, and the treatment was generally well tolerated, with manageable side effects like transaminitis and neutropenia [ ]. These results suggest that combining antiangiogenics and ICIs could inflame the typically immune-cold sarcoma microenvironment and perhaps make it more responsive to ICIs.
Given that certain cytotoxic agents, such as doxorubicin [ , ] and trabectedin [ ], induce immunogenic cell death and alter antigen presentation, combining them with ICIs may convert cold tumors into inflamed ICI-responsive phenotypes. Recently, Pollack et al. published a phase I/II trial that evaluated doxorubicin and pembrolizumab in 37 patients with advanced anthracycline-naïve sarcomas. The ORR was 13% in phase II and 19% overall, with median PFS of 8.1 months and median OS of 27.6 months, suggesting a potential survival benefit over historical controls. The regimen was well tolerated, with manageable toxicities, primarily neutropenia and fatigue. Notably, UPS and DDLPS demonstrated promising responses, indicating subtype-specific sensitivity [ ]. While the trial did not meet its predefined ORR threshold, the favorable PFS and OS support further exploration, which is currently underway in an ongoing phase III trial (NCT06422806).
The TRAMUNE trial [ ], a phase Ib study, evaluated trabectedin and durvalumab in 40 patients with advanced pretreated STS or ovarian carcinoma. Trabectedin 1.2 mg/m2 was established as the recommended phase II dose, with manageable toxicity, including fatigue, nausea, and neutropenia. In STS, 43% of patients experienced tumor shrinkage, but the ORR was modest at 7%, with a 6-month PFS of 28.6%. In ovarian carcinoma, the ORR was higher at 21.4%, and the 6-month PFS reached 42.9%. Notably, PD-L1 expression and CD8+ T-cell infiltration correlated with PFS in ovarian carcinoma. While trabectedin plus durvalumab was well tolerated, its efficacy in STS remains limited.
The SAINT-SOC-1702 phase II trial evaluated the combination of trabectedin, ipilimumab, and nivolumab in 91 previously untreated patients with advanced STS. The regimen showed an ORR of 25% and a disease control rate of 85%, with a median PFS of 7.4 months and a median OS of 32.0 months. As of the 2024 data cut-off, 23 patients (25%) remained alive. Responses were seen across multiple STS subtypes, including LMS, UPS, LPS, synovial sarcoma, and myxofibrosarcoma [ ]. The combination was well tolerated, with grade 3/4 treatment-related AEs that were mainly hematologic and metabolic.
Another phase II trial (NCT03899805) evaluated eribulin and pembrolizumab in 19 patients with previously treated LMS to see if this combination could modify the TME and enhance immunotherapy responses. The 12-week PFS was 42.1%, below the 60% efficacy threshold, with a median PFS of 11.1 weeks and an ORR of 5.3%. The clinical benefit rate was 26.3%, with one partial response and five cases of stable disease at 12 weeks. The median OS was not reached, and the regimen had a 68% rate of grade ≥3 toxicities, including neutropenia, anemia, and gastrointestinal effects, though all were reversible [ ]. Given the LMS cohort did not achieve its efficacy endpoint, a more targeted approach incorporating biomarker-driven strategies and optimized patient selection is crucial to enhance therapeutic outcomes. Taken together, these findings suggest that chemotherapy plays a critical role in reshaping the TME to enhance ICI efficacy, but further histology-specific trials are needed to refine combination strategies and optimize patient outcomes.

## Sarcoma Antigen-Based and Cell Therapy

## Dendritic Cell (DC)-Based Immunotherapy
Dendritic cells (DCs), essential antigen-presenting cells in the immune system, have emerged as a potential basis for immunotherapy owing to their role in both innate and adaptive immune responses. They are highly effective in promoting significant anti-cancer T-cell responses, leading to their use in enhancing the body’s own DC immunocompetence through immunomodulators and DC vaccines [ ].
Various strategies of DC-based immunotherapy have been tested, which include the activation and mobilization of in-vivo DCs, enhancing T-cell co-stimulation, restoring DC function by inhibiting DC inhibitory molecules, delivering tumor-associated antigens (TAAs) directly to DCs, and DC cancer vaccines [ ]. Kang and Lee noted that DC vaccines pulsed with TAAs were effective in several tumor types [ ]. Jneid et al. found that selectively stimulating the DNA-sensing cGAS-STING pathway in DCs promoted T-cell antitumor activity [ ]. STING was preferentially targeted in DCs, and when combined with a tumor Treg-depleting antibody, it led to complete and durable tumor eradication [ ].
The effectiveness of DC immunotherapy in treating STS has been limited because of factors such as low immunogenicity, short lifespan, potential toxicity, poor delivery efficiency, and the immunosuppressive activity of the TME. Soft-tissue sarcomas create an immunosuppressive environment that interferes with antigen presentation, maturation, and the function of DC vaccines, which contributes to their shortcomings [ ]. Furthermore, as discussed previously, STSs are considered cold tumors, which may contribute to their resistance to DC therapy because of a lack of immunogenicity from an improper T-cell status [ ].
In brain tumors, researchers have found that treating the TME with cyclophosphamide to suppress Tregs before DC administration has improved the clinical outcomes of this therapy; a similar effect may be seen in STS [ ]. Advancements in nanotechnology, including different types of nanoparticle DC delivery methods, may improve DC immunotherapy efficacy through higher solubility, bioavailability, and the therapeutic index [ ]. Perez et al. suggest that DCs can display low effectiveness owing to poor migration to the lymph nodes, where they present TAAs to effector T cells [ ]. Upregulation of C-C chemokine receptor 7, which is important for trafficking TAAs to lymph nodes, can improve DC migration toward C-C chemokine receptor 7 ligands [ ].
Additionally, choosing the proper DC subset for an adjuvant or target for vaccination, a quantity of DCs for injection, an injection schedule, and the proper route of injection are other areas that influence the treatment’s effectiveness [ ]. The effectiveness of DC vaccines can also be limited by tumor-specific mechanisms, such as the TME eliminating antigen expression, altering major histocompatibility complex molecules, lack of co-stimulation, expression of inhibitory ligands, induction of Tregs, Indoleamine 2,3-dioxygenase (IDO) expression, or production of immunosuppressive cytokines [ ]. Zhou et al. showed that combining a DC vaccination with cytokine-induced killer cells, which help activate DCs, improved survival for patients diagnosed with stomach cancer or colorectal carcinoma [ ]. Studying these mechanisms in STS will help us uncover the foundations of DC therapy and overcome potential resistance mechanisms.

## Personalized Peptide Vaccines (PPVs)
Personalized peptide vaccines (PPVs) are used for tumors to trigger an immune response against tumor antigens, targeting both tumor-associated and tumor-specific antigens [ ]. Over the past several years, therapeutic trials with PPVs have yielded promising outcomes in several malignancies, including prostate, pancreatic, and colorectal cancers [ – ]. In a phase II study, the feasibility of PPVs was evaluated in 20 patients with refractory bone and STS, encompassing nine different subtypes [ ]. Patients treated with PPV therapy achieved a median OS of 9.6 months, surpassing the OS reported for second-line palliative therapy (8 months) [ ] and supportive care (5.3 months) [ ]. Further investigation is required to evaluate the efficacy of PPVs in patients with refractory STS, as they hold potential for application across a wide range of STS subtypes.
A study investigated the immunogenicity and effectiveness of a long synthetic peptide-based vaccine that targeted the tumor antigen survivin (SVX). The vaccine contained three peptide sequences with several CD4+ and CD8+ T-cell epitopes. When studied in healthy individuals, CD4+ and CD8+ T-cell immunogenicity of SVX peptides was observed. In patients with cancer (head and neck, lung, and renal), researchers detected high levels of spontaneous T-cell precursors specific to the SVX peptide. This signified that patients did not have a tolerance for these peptides. The SVX vaccine also showed effectiveness when used on murine models and even elicited antitumor memory immune responses in the mice, which eliminated secondary tumor growth. The SVX vaccine increases amounts of CD4+ and CD8+ T cells but does not affect PD-1 expression ( ).
A randomized phase II clinical trial by Noguchi et al. was conducted on patients with metastatic castration-resistant prostate cancer [ ]. Scientists studied the effects of a PPV with or without metronomic cyclophosphamide (CPA) on Tregs and MDSCs to analyze the immune response elicited by the treatment. Patients receiving a PPV and CPA showed significant increases in MDSCs after treatment, whereas those with only PPV treatment did not show differences. There was no correlation between percent changes in Tregs or MDSC frequencies and Cytotoxic T Lymphocytes (CTLs) responses.
One possible explanation for the PPV and CPA treatment’s low efficacy could be that increased MDSCs impact immune activity. Several studies have indicated that CPA chemotherapy increases MDSC levels, some of which can induce de novo FOXP3+ Tregs. A direct cell–cell interaction or production of specific soluble factors causes the induction of Tregs by MDSCs, which may be responsible for the treatment’s failure. Another explanation for the treatment’s poor results could be that optimal doses and schedules of CPA have not yet been determined. Animal studies have shown immune responses when CPA was administered within a week before vaccination. Finally, the chosen antigens for the PPV vaccines may not have been appropriate, and the correct antigens still need to be identified [ ].
Another phase II clinical trial by Takahashi et al. evaluated the effectiveness of PPV in 20 patients with refractory bone and STS. A maximum of four HLA-matched peptides were chosen to be administered in the vaccines after they showed higher peptide-specific immunoglobulin G responses in pre-vaccination plasma. The PPV treatment was administered weekly for 6 weeks and biweekly thereafter, which caused a reduction in lung metastasis and long-term stable disease for each patient. The authors concluded that PPV may be feasible for most patients with refractory STS owing to the treatment’s safety and high response rate. They add that a barrier to effective treatment could be the diversity of STS subtypes and HLA types [ ].
A current study (ClinicalTrials.gov: NCT04024878) focused on a new vaccine called “Personalized NeoAntigen Cancer Vaccine” (NeoVax) is being conducted in combination with nivolumab for patients with ovarian cancer. This study is currently in the recruiting stage and will investigate whether a peptide vaccine can be effective and safe when created using characteristics of the patient’s cancer. An active phase I clinical trial (ClinicalTrials.gov: NCT02950766) is currently investigating the safety and ideal dose of NeoVax in combination with ipilimumab to treat patients with renal cell carcinoma. (This study is conducted by the same group of researchers as the ovarian cancer study above, with a different intervention and cancer type.)
Another clinical trial (ClinicalTrials.gov: NCT04930783) will measure the safety and effectiveness of NeoVax in combination with CDX-301 and nivolumab when treating patients with melanoma (with the same group of investigators as above). This study is in the recruiting phase. Studies by this previously mentioned group are also investigating NeoVax in combination with CPA and pembrolizumab in immunoglobulin heavy-chain variable region gene unmutated chronic lymphocytic leukemia (ClinicalTrials.gov: NCT03219450), ipilimumab and nivolumab in advanced melanoma (NCT03929029), radiotherapy and pembrolizumab in newly diagnosed glioblastoma (ClinicalTrials.gov: NCT02287428), pembrolizumab after rituximab in follicular lymphoma (ClinicalTrials.gov: NCT03361852), and NeoVax alone for melanoma (ClinicalTrials.gov: NCT01970358).
There are no current NeoVax trials currently studying STS; however, if this therapy shows promise, it may have implications for future directions in STS therapy. Several PPV studies conducted on other cancers have had positive results, yet it appears that administering these vaccines in combination with adjuvant drugs is beneficial. As the PD-L1 on cancer cells binds to PD-1 on T cells, inactivating them, cancer cells may display some resistance to peptide vaccines. Thus, using an inhibitor of this process, like nivolumab, has improved immune responses [ ]. Hu et al. have demonstrated that personalized neoantigen vaccines were able to induce prolonged T-cell responses with tumor-specific cytotoxicity in melanoma. Nevertheless, they note that these vaccines alone may not be able to sustain antitumor properties as the TME evolves [ ]. For this reason, researchers would likely encounter incomplete antitumor responses when testing these vaccines on STS.
While not a resistance mechanism, it is worth noting that there are other difficulties in creating a successful PPV. Scientists must use a wide variety of datasets and software to create a suitable peptide sequence [ ]. Moreover, high variability when HLA typing to identify major histocompatibility complex molecules presents yet another challenge [ ]. Ideally, the vaccines should target specific somatic mutations that are present in all cancer cells; however, with various subclonal mutations, the dominant one must be utilized as it is found in most of the tissue [ ]. These factors must all be considered when designing an appropriate peptide, which must be done in a timely cost-effective manner; this has hindered the success of such treatments [ ]. Because of the rarity and unpredictability of STS, PPV trials have seldom been conducted.

## T-Cell Targeting Vaccine
T-cell targeting vaccines are T-cell infusions that commonly target the NY-ESO-1 protein owing to its role as the most immunogenic cancer testis antigen. The NY-ESO-1 cancer testis antigen is specific to testicular germ cells and malignancies instead of somatic tissue, which makes it important for STS diagnosis and an attractive target for immunotherapy [ ].
One phase I/II clinical trial aimed to use transduced T cells to stimulate an antitumor response in 12 patients with recurrent or metastatic synovial sarcoma [ ]. The T cells were designed to target NY-ESO-1, which is expressed in approximately 76% of synovial sarcomas yet rarely expressed in other spindle-cell mesenchymal tumors [ , ]. The trial revealed that transduced NY-ESO-1 T cells may be able to self-regenerate and sustain an antitumor response [ ].
A phase I clinical trial treated 12 patients with advanced STS using T-cell receptor (TCR) affinity-enhanced specific T-cell therapy (TAEST16001) and targeted the NY-ESO-1 TCR. The study demonstrated that the autologous T cells engineered to target NY-ESO-1 receptors produced no adverse reactions in patients, and their delivery can be optimized for safety and efficacy [ ].
Another phase I trial consisted of two infusions of NY-ESO-1-specific, TCR gene-modified T-cell therapy into HLA-matched patients with STS expressing the cancer testis antigen. A lymph node-targeting nanoparticulate peptide vaccine containing an epitope recognized by TCR-T cells was administered 1 day before and 7 days after the infusion in the absence of lymphodepletion. It was determined that the combination of a TCR-T-cell infusion and the NY-ESO-1 epitope vaccine was able to induce significant antitumor effects in refractory synovial sarcoma [ ].
T-cell vaccines have been utilized to circumvent instances involving the immune checkpoint blockade being limited to cancers with a specific TMB. Vaccination aims to enable autologous T cells to recognize neoantigens and promote antitumor activity. It was shown that vaccination with CD4+ or CD8+ neoantigens did not provide therapeutic immunity, while vaccines containing therapeutic CD4+/CD8+ T-cell neoantigens modified the TME in a way that increased T-cell levels and overcame resistance to immune checkpoint blockade therapy [ ]. Soft-tissue sarcomas may also become resistant to these treatments because of the loss of NY-ESO-1 expression [ ]. This resistance can manifest as a response to transgenic adoptive cell therapy with DC vaccination and PD-1 blockade, which causes extensive methylation and suppression of the promoter region of NY-ESO-1 [ ].
Gyurdieva et al. studied TCR T-cell resistance mechanisms in synovial sarcoma and discovered that if NY-ESO-1 expression does not change after treatment, a decrease in HLA-A expression and antigen-presenting genes can be responsible for antigen escape [ ]. Furthermore, a loss of heterozygosity among HLA genes can also be responsible for resistance to T-cell therapy [ ].

## Chimeric Antigen Receptor T-Cell (CAR T)
Chimeric antigen receptor T-cell therapy has shown promising results as an immunotherapy owing to rapid development in recent years [ ]. In particular, the therapy has shown unprecedented success in treating hematological malignancies, especially B-cell leukemias and lymphoma [ ]. Chimeric antigen receptor T-cell therapy uses engineered CARs that exhibit antigen specificity and T-cell cytotoxicity based on the malignancy being targeted. chimeric antigen receptor T cells recognize specific TAAs, causing cytokines to be released, which strengthens the immune response [ ].
One of the most common reasons that CAR T fails is because of an antigen escape, in which loss of the target antigen on tumor cells negates the CAR T ability to initiate an immune response. While high response rates may initially be observed in patients, many eventually have malignancies that exhibit a partial or complete loss of the target antigen [ ]. A study by Wilkie et al. showed that to overcome this resistance mechanism in breast cancer, dual targeting of TAAs can be extremely effective. This method relies on the tumor cells expressing two TAAs, namely ErbB2 and MUC1. When CARs engineered to target these two TAAs were administered simultaneously, it was shown that complementary signals were established, leading to enhanced T-cell proliferation [ ]. Ultimately, this resistance mechanism demonstrates a need to optimize the selection of TAAs to strengthen the initial response and prevent relapse [ ].
Another explanation for CAR T’s failure in solid tumors is the immunosuppressive TME, which contains Tregs, TAMs, and CD4+ T helper-2 cells that produce anti-inflammatory cytokines and inhibit an immune response. To bypass this resistance, CAR T have been created with armored CAR accessory molecules to avoid the immunosuppression of the TME [ ]. The expression of immune checkpoints, such as the PD-L1 ligand, along with changes in the metabolic environment of the TME and the influence of suppressive myeloid cells, may play significant roles in resistance to therapies like CAR T. These factors highlight the importance of understanding adaptive resistance, which could guide future strategies to enhance treatment efficacy [ ]. Additional failures in CAR T for solid tumors arise because of physical tumor barriers, such as the tumor stroma, which prevent the CAR T from infiltrating the tumor. Researchers have attempted to circumvent this issue via local injections [ ].
The SPEARHEAD-1 phase II trial evaluated afamitresgene autoleucel (afami-cel), an engineered TCR therapy, in patients with HLA-A*02 and MAGE-A4-expressing advanced synovial sarcoma and myxoid round cell liposarcoma. Conducted across 23 international sites, the study enrolled heavily pretreated patients who had undergone at least one prior chemotherapy regimen. The ORR was 37%, with 39% in synovial sarcoma and 25% in MRCLS, demonstrating promising antitumor activity. The treatment was generally well tolerated, with cytopenia being the most common severe AE and manageable cytokine release syndrome occurring in 71% of patients. No treatment-related deaths were reported [ ]. These findings support the potential of TCR therapy in targeting solid tumors, providing a rationale for further exploration of afami-cel and similar therapies in broader oncological applications.
There are emerging data on CAR T and STS using potential antigen targets (HER2, IFGR1, CD166, EphA2, Gd2+) [ , ]. These targets span various histologies, including osteosarcoma, Ewing sarcoma, synovial sarcoma, and chondrosarcoma. However, they lack specificity to STS or tumor cells alone, as exemplified by CD166, which is also expressed in fibroblasts and neurons. This poses a challenge, as highlighted in studies targeting EphA2 in osteosarcoma cell lines, where EphA2 downregulation led to the emergence of EphA2-negative tumor cells [ ]. This phenomenon is thought to occur through cancer immunoediting. While Ewing cell lines showed enhanced survival with CAR T therapy targeting antigens such as IGF1R and ROR1, challenges remain, including low antigen expression and potential off-target toxicity [ ]. Additional studies are needed to refine and enhance the clinical utility of CAR T therapy in STS.
The study by D’Angelo et al. looked into the clinical potential of letetresgene autoleucel (lete-cel), an adoptive T-cell therapy targeting NY-ESO-1, for advanced/metastatic MRCLS. Given the high expression of NY-ESO-1 in MRCLS and the limited effectiveness of standard treatments, this study aimed to evaluate lete-cel in HLA-A*02-positive patients. The trial enrolled 20 patients, divided into two groups based on different lymphodepletion regimens. The ORR was 20% in the reduced-dose group and 40% in the standard-dose group, with median PFS of 5.4 and 8.7 months, respectively. Despite common side effects like CRS and cytopenias, the safety findings were in line with what is expected for T-cell therapies. Notably, T-cell expansion and persistence were linked to the response, reinforcing the potential of targeted TCR therapy in MRCLS. These findings highlight lete-cel as a promising treatment, warranting further exploration in larger phase II trials. Additionally, this study led to the accelerated Food and Drug Administration approval of lete-cel for treating advanced MRCLS (141) (see Table ).
 The development of antigen-based and cellular therapies for sarcomas is an exciting frontier in cancer immunotherapy, but significant limitations in trial design and mechanistic understanding hinder widespread clinical adoption. Dendritic cell-based vaccines, despite their potential, have struggled to generate durable antitumor responses in STS because of low tumor immunogenicity and an immunosuppressive TME. Preclinical models show that modifying DC function can enhance immune priming, but clinical translation remains challenging because of poor trafficking and limited persistence of activated DCs. Most DC-based trials in STS have been early-phase studies with small sample sizes, lacking the statistical power to determine efficacy across histological subtypes. Without better patient stratification and optimized delivery methods, DC vaccines in STS will likely remain experimental.
Personalized peptide vaccines represent another avenue for harnessing tumor antigenicity, yet their effectiveness in STS is yet to be defined. Phase II trials in other malignancies have shown extended OS and immune activation, but responses in STS have been variable, likely owing to heterogeneous antigen expression and tumor evolution leading to immune escape. Identifying antigens specific to STS and capable of eliciting a strong immune response is challenging, given the low baseline TMB and the absence of highly recurrent mutations in many sarcoma subtypes. Immunosuppressive factors further dampen vaccine efficacy, necessitating combination strategies with checkpoint inhibitors or immune adjuvants. Improvements in antigen selection, delivery methods, and combination regimens will be crucial for advancing PPVs in STS.
Adoptive cellular therapies, particularly CAR T and TCR-engineered T-cell approaches, hold promise in STS but face significant barriers related to antigen specificity, persistence, and immune evasion. NY-ESO-1-targeting TCR therapies have shown some success in synovial sarcoma, but antigen escape, epigenetic downregulation, and loss of HLA presentation limit their durability. CAR T-cell approaches are hindered by the low expression of sarcoma-specific antigens and the presence of stromal and immunosuppressive barriers within the TME. Novel strategies such as dual-antigen targeting, armored CARs resistant to TGF-β suppression, and localized CAR T delivery are being explored, but their clinical application remains in its infancy. Refining antigen selection, addressing tumor heterogeneity, and integrating immune-modulating strategies will be key to overcoming resistance and maximizing the therapeutic potential of cellular therapies in STS. Without these advancements, the current generation of antigen-based and cellular therapies is unlikely to achieve widespread success in sarcomas beyond select histological subtypes.

## Future Directions
Despite advances in understanding TIL composition, MDSC infiltration, and PD-1/PD-L1 signaling, reliable predictive biomarkers are still lacking, hindering patient stratification. Key questions remain about the factors influencing ICI response across different histologies, as some subtypes like UPS and DDLPS show limited responses, while others like LMS are resistant. Moving forward, histology-driven approaches and biomarker-based trial designs are crucial for refining immunotherapy strategies and shedding light on resistance mechanisms.
Efforts to “inflame” cold STS have focused on VEGF blockade, chemo-ICI combinations, and T-cell therapies, but adaptive resistance—through antigen loss, T-cell exhaustion, and stromal reprogramming—continues to limit efficacy. Single-cell transcriptomics and spatial immune profiling are essential for identifying mechanisms that distinguish responders from non-responders, guiding more effective combination strategies. Early-phase trials suggest that chemotherapy may synergize with ICIs, but maintaining response durability remains a challenge, indicating that sustained immune activation needs additional interventions targeting the TME.
Looking ahead, the field must shift toward precision immuno-oncology, integrating TCR-T, neoantigen vaccines, and chemotherapy-ICI pairings to enhance immune priming and persistence. Addressing immune escape mechanisms will require tailored multimodal approaches informed by patient-specific immune signatures. The next phase of STS immunotherapy should focus on personalized histology-specific strategies to bridge the gap between immune recognition and effective tumor clearance.

## Conclusions
Because STSs are rare and heterogeneous, they remain difficult to treat, often because of the cancer becoming resistant to various therapies. While some immunotherapy treatments, such as ICIs, have shown promise for STS, response rates remain low. A better understanding of immune-related genes and their biomarkers is necessary to improve patient outcomes. Further research is also needed to investigate the potential to convert STSs into hot tumors, to search for resistance-related biomarkers, and to create personalized treatments that can effectively treat STS.
