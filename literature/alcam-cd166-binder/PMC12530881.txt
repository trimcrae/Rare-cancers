## Abstract

Background: Joint bone destruction in rheumatoid arthritis (RA) leads to poor prognosis, with current treatments mainly targeting inflammation and limited focus on bone damage. Mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) offer anti-inflammatory and bone repair properties, but their clinical application is hindered by cellular heterogeneity. Induced pluripotent stem cell–derived mesenchymal stem cells (iMSCs) present a promising alternative due to their lower heterogeneity and replicative senescence, although their potential in RA treatment remains underexplored.

Methods: iMSCs were injected intraarticularly in a collagen-induced arthritis (CIA) model. Treatment outcomes, including plantar swelling, joint score, histological and immunohistochemical staining, microCT imaging, and bone loss, were assessed. Single-cell RNA sequencing was employed to study iMSCs’ effects on synovial macrophage subsets.

Results: 
In vivo, iMSCs significantly reduced systemic inflammation and joint bone damage. Analysis of macrophage subpopulations revealed that iMSCs shifted macrophages from a pro-inflammatory CD86hiIL1βhi cluster to an anti-inflammatory CD86hiIL1βlo cluster, leading to reduced inflammation and bone resorption.

Conclusions: iMSCs effectively alleviate inflammation and bone damage in CIA by modulating macrophage phenotypes, demonstrating potential for RA therapy.
Keywords: rheumatoid arthritis, iPSCs, iMSCs, macrophage, cellular therapy

## Introduction
Rheumatoid arthritis (RA) is a systemic autoimmune condition distinguished by synovitis, cartilage degradation, and bone erosion. Epidemiological research indicates that RA is associated with a significant incidence of disability and deformity, resulting in substantial financial strain and societal challenges for affected individuals, their families, and the broader community. , Currently, glucocorticoids, traditional disease-­modifying anti-rheumatic drugs (DMARDs), biological DMARDs, and targeted synthetic DMARDs (such as JAK inhibitors and IL6R inhibitors) are the primary pharmaceutical interventions utilized in the management of RA. While the clinical remission rate with standardized treatment has shown significant improvement, there remains a subset of patients whose remission does not meet established benchmarks. In the context of above therapies, the joint synovial tissue is recognized as a crucial component in mitigating systemic symptoms. Many immune cells within the synovium play a crucial role in the development of RA, with macrophages being particularly central to the onset and progression of chronic inflammation associated with the disease. According to classical classification, M1 macrophages contribute to the inflammatory response in RA and are linked with persistent disease activity. Conversely, the synovial tissue of RA patients in remission is characterized by the presence of M2 macrophages. Targeting the imbalance between M1 and M2 macrophages to favor anti-inflammatory M2 macrophages may offer a therapeutic strategy for restoring tissue homeostasis. Identifying molecules that can influence M1/M2 polarization in RA macrophages represents a valuable and novel therapeutic target. 
Mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) have been shown to release transforming growth factor-1 (TGF-β1), vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), interleukin-6 (IL6), chemokine (MCP-1), and other cytokines, which are crucial in the process of tissue repair and remodeling. MSCs exhibit anti-inflammatory characteristics and have the capacity to regenerate osteochondral tissue, rendering them a promising option for cell therapy in RA, a disease marked by autoimmune responses and previous osteochondral damage. MSC can promote the M1/M2 transformation of macrophages. This claim is substantiated by numerous studies conducted on a global scale. The treatment of RA with MSCs has entered the clinical trial phase. , However, various clinical trials vary in dosages, administration methods, and therapeutic outcomes. A crucial consideration is the diversity in sources of MSCs utilized, leading to heterogeneity within the population of MSCs from the same source. Increasing evidence suggests that while MSCs from different sources may satisfy the criteria set by the International Society for Cell Therapy (ISCT) for identification, there exists significant variability in terms of proliferation, differentiation capacity, and immunomodulatory properties. , Induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs) represent a significant advancement in the field of medical biology due to their resemblance to pluripotent stem cells and their capacity for virtually unlimited proliferation. These cells serve as the foundational material for numerous experiments in cell differentiation. Currently, the techniques and technologies for the differentiation of iPSCs into MSCs have reached a high level of maturity. Consequently, iPSC-­derived mesenchymal stem cells (iMSCs) have the potential to serve as a significant source of MSCs for clinical applications in the future. Several studies have demonstrated that iMSCs exhibit superior characteristics such as enhanced cell viability, uniformity, and proliferation capacity when compared to traditional MSCs derived from homologous tissues. Based on the above advantages, iMSCs may be easier to clinically transform than MSCs.
The objective of this study was to examine the therapeutic potential of iMSCs in the context of RA and to elucidate their therapeutic effects in a collagen-induced arthritis (CIA) mouse model. To assess the impact of iMSCs on the cellular composition of the synovium, we conducted single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) analysis of synovial tissues following treatment. The findings of this investigation provide a theoretical foundation for the clinical application of iMSCs in the treatment of RA.

## Methods

## Induction of iPSCs
Healthy volunteer PBMC was used as a cell source for reprogramming iPSCs. PBMC was isolated from the patient’s whole blood using lymphatic separation medium (Corning, USA). Following isolation, PBMC was cultured for 2-4 days in X-VIVO-15 medium (Lonza, China) supplemented with 1% non-essential amino acids (NEAA) (Thermo, USA), 1 mM Glutamax (Thermo, USA), 0.1 mM β-mercaptoethanol (Sigma, Germany), and cytokines including 20 ng/mL IL-3, 20 ng/mL IL-6, 200 ng/mL GM-CSF, 100 ng/mL FLT3 ligand, and 100 ng/mL SCF (SINO Bio, China). Reprogramming plasmids pEP4 E02S ET2K and pCEP4-MYCL-h302 were introduced into the cells via electroporation (Celetrix, 580 V). The cells were then seeded onto feeder cells and cultured in TeSRTM-E7TM medium (Stemcell, USA) supplemented with 250 μM NaB (Sigma, Germany). iPSC colonies were isolated on day 20 post-transduction, expanded using E8 medium (Thermo, USA), and passaged every 3 to 4 days at a ratio of 1:5.

## Alkaline phosphatase staining
iPSCs exhibit an undifferentiated phenotype marked by elevated Alkaline phosphatase (AP) expression, suggesting a capacity for self-renewal. The AP staining Kit II (Thermo, USA) was employed to assess AP activity in iPSCs, following the manufacturer’s instructions. Imaging was conducted with a Nikon Fluorescence Microscopy system (Nikon, Japan).

## Karyotype analysis
iPSCs were exposed to 0.32 μg/mL of colcemid (DAHUIBIO, China) for 55 min and subsequently digested using Accutase (Sigma, Germany). The cells were then harvested and immersed in a hypotonic solution at 37 °C for 36 min, followed by fixation with Carnoy’s fixative (methanol and acetic acid, 3:1). More than 50 metaphase chromosomes were spread and analyzed using Leica Cytovision software (Leica Biosystems) in accordance with the International System for Human Cytogenetic Nomenclature (ISCN, 2019).

## Immunofluorescence staining
Gently wash the iPSCs cultured on coverslips twice with PBS, then fix them with 4% paraformaldehyde at room temperature for 15-20 min, followed by three PBS washes. Next, permeabilize the cells with 0.1% Triton X-100 in PBS for 10 min to enhance antibody penetration and wash again with PBS. Block the cells with 5% BSA or 10% serum for 1 h. After blocking, incubate with diluted primary antibodies (anti-SOX2 and anti-OCT4; Thermo, USA) at room temperature for 2 h, wash thoroughly with PBS, and then incubate with fluorescence-labeled secondary antibodies in the dark for 1 h at room temperature. Following another wash, stain the nuclei with DAPI for 5 min. Finally, mount the coverslips with an anti-fade mounting medium and observe under a fluorescence microscope.

## Induction and identification of iMSCs
The hPSC-MSC induction differentiation kit (Shownin, China) was utilized for the induction and differentiation of iMSCs. iMSCs were seeded into six-well plates at a density of 4 x 105 cells per well, and differentiation experiments were carried out following the provided protocols, resulting in the acquisition of MSCs after 13-14 days. The surface markers of MSCs were identified using a surface marker identification kit (Oricell, China), with positive cell surface markers including CD29, CD105, CD73, CD166, and CD44 and negative markers consisting of CD45, CD14, CD11b, CD34, and HLA-DR. Flow cytometry was employed for the detection of these markers.

## iMSCs differentiation
The proficiency of iMSCs was validated through differentiation into bone, cartilage, and adipocytes using specific differentiation media: Human Mesenchymal Stem Cell Osteogenic Differentiation Medium (Oricell, China) for osteogenesis, Human Mesenchymal Stem Cell Chondrogenic Differentiation Medium for chondrogenesis (Oricell, China), and Human Mesenchymal Stem Cell Adipogenic Differentiation Medium for adipogenesis (Oricell, China), following the manufacturer’s protocols. The trilineage differentiation potential of iMSCs was assessed via histochemical staining with Alizarin red solution (osteogenesis), Alcian blue (chondrogenesis), and Oil Red O (adipogenesis), respectively.

## CIA mouse model
DBA/1J mice (male, 6-8 weeks old, n = 40) were obtained from GemPharmatech (China) for the CIA experiments. The mice were housed in standard cages under a 12 h light/dark cycle, and they had unrestricted access to water and standard mouse chow. The complete Freund’s adjuvant (Chondrex, USA) was mixed with bovine type II collagen (Chondrex, USA) to prepare the CII emulsion. On day 0, mice were anesthetized by mask inhalation of isoflurane (RWD, China), and the initial immunization was performed by injecting 100 μL of the CII emulsion subcutaneously at the base of the tail and on the back of each mouse. On day 21, the boost immunization was carried out by mixing incomplete Freund’s adjuvant (Chondrex, USA) with bovine type II collagen, and 100 μL of the mixture was injected subcutaneously in each mouse (same as day 0). The normal control group of mice (n = 8) underwent no additional processing and were fed under the same conditions as the experimental model group.
Healthy male SD rats (GemPharmatech, China) aged 6-8 weeks (body weight 180 ± 20 g) were acclimatized for 1 week before experimentation, with housing conditions identical to those of the mice. For model establishment, CII was emulsified with complete Freund’s adjuvant at a 1:1 volume ratio on ice until a stable water-in-oil emulsion was achieved. For primary immunization, rats were anesthetized via inhalation and injected subcutaneously at four dorsal sites with a total of 0.4 mL of the emulsified antigen. A booster immunization was administered 7 days later using CII emulsified with incomplete Freund’s adjuvant via the same method. Arthritis assessment was performed using the same criteria as for CIA mice.

## Treatment and evaluation in CIA
On day 28, mice without ankle swelling were excluded, based on the joint swelling status of the mice, they were randomly divided into four groups: CIA group (n = 6), PBS group (n = 6), UCMSC group (n = 6), and iMSC group (n = 6). Normal control mice (NC) were also randomly selected for matching (n = 6). Each mouse was assigned a unique identification number, and the data handlers were blinded to the group assignments associated with these numbers. Mice were anesthetized by mask inhalation of isoflurane. UCMSC group and iMSC group was injected with 3 × 105 cells per joint with an injection volume of 15 μL PBS through the knee joint, while the PBS group was given volume of 15 μL PBS. The CIA group was left untreated as the positive control. After that, injections were given every 7 days, and treatment ended after five injections. The mice were euthanized by cervical dislocation. Throughout the course of treatment, the mice underwent regular weighing and assessment for arthritis symptoms in accordance with the criteria outlined in . Each paw was individually scored, with the cumulative total of all four scores constituting the Arthritic Index (AI), with a maximum value of 16.

## MicroCT analysis
Following treatment, the knee and ankle joints of mice were immobilized in 4% paraformaldehyde for a period of 3 days. Subsequently, high-resolution small animal MicroCT (Bruker Daltonik Gmbh) was utilized to conduct joint scans and data reconstruction at a resolution of 2.8 μm in order to generate 3D images. Various bone morphological parameters, such as bone volume/tissue volume (BV/TV), were measured. Additionally, quantitative analysis was performed on trabecular number (Tb.N) and trabecular separation (Tb.Sp).

## Histological stains
After the final treatment, the liver, kidney, spleen, and knee and ankle joints were collected and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde. Knee and ankle joints were decalcified with EDTA (pH = 8.0). The decalcified joints were then coated with paraffin and sectioned for H&E, Safranin O-Fast Green Staining.

## Immunohistochemistry
Paraffin-embedded tissue sections were deparaffinized and rehydrated. Antigen retrieval was performed in a microwave oven. The sections were then washed in PBS-T (PBS with 0.1% Tween-20) and blocked with 3% hydrogen peroxide-methanol at room temperature to inhibit endogenous peroxidase activity. Subsequently, the sections were blocked with 3% bovine serum albumin (Servicebio, China) at room temperature for 1 hour, followed by overnight incubation at 4 °C with primary antibody IL1β and TNFα. After washing, the sections were incubated at room temperature for 50 min with HRP-conjugated anti-rabbit secondary antibody and visualized using 3,3-diaminobenzidine (Servicebio, China). The staining results were analyzed with Image-J software (NIH, United States). Semiquantitative analyses were performed by calculating the average optical density (AOD), which was obtained by comparing the integral optical density (IOD) to the area.

## Cytokine assay
At the end of treatment, peripheral blood samples were obtained from mice. The samples were subsequently centrifuged at 3000 rpm at 4̊ C for 15 min to isolate serum. Serum levels of TNFα, IL1β, and IL6 were quantified using an enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kit (AndyGene, China).

## Single-cell RNA sequencing
At the end of therapy, joint tissues were removed (each sample consisted of cells that were isolated from the one knee joints of three animals.), cut into pieces, repeatedly cleaned with PBS, dissociated with a solvent (0.35% collagenase IV, 2 mg/mL papain, 120 U/mL DNase I), reacted in a water bath at 37 °C for 20 min, and then PBS was added to terminate the dissociation. 1 mL of red blood cell lysate was added to wet ice for 2∼10min. After lysis, 100 μL of Dead Cell Removal reagents were added: Dead Cell Removal MicroBeads and Miltenyi® Dead Cell Removal Kit. After the reaction, the reagent is removed by centrifugation. Then, PBS was re-suspended to obtain cell suspension. The CD45+ cells were sorted out. The cell activity was detected by Trypan blue staining, and the cell activity requirement was >85%. The resulting cell suspension was loaded into the 10× Chromium instrument, and cell capture (capture target cell 8000), cDNA amplification, and library construction were performed according to the operation instructions of the official 10× Genomics Chromium Single-Cell 3ʹ kit (V3). Overall, we sequenced 15 015 cells, about 7500 cells per group. cDNA amplification and library preparation were carried out in accordance with standard protocols. The libraries were sequenced by LC-Bio Technology (Hangzhou, China) using an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 sequencing system, utilizing double-end sequencing with a read length of 150 bp and achieving a minimum depth of 20 000 reads per cell.

## scRNA seq data analysis
The sequencing data was parsed and converted into FASTQ files using Illumina bcl2fastq software (version 2.20). Cell Ranger, the official analysis software of 10× Genomics, was used to perform data quality statistics on the original sequencing data and compare the reference genome. The analysis result file of Cell Ranger is imported into Seurat software (version 4.1.0) for data dimensionization reduction, cluster analysis, and expression matrix analysis. Filtering Low-Quality Cells: The thresholds for filtering low-quality cells were set to include only those with more than 500 genes expressed per cell and with mitochondrial genes expressed in fewer than 25% of cells. Cells were then projected into a 2D space using UMAP. Seurat’s FindAllMarkers toolkit analyzes differentially expressed marker genes in different cell populations using bimod likelihood ratio statistical tests. Seurat V4.0 performs further follow-up analysis of the single-cell expression matrix. UMAP clustering algorithm was used to reduce the dimension of cells. Based on the results of cell subset classification, UMAP nonlinear dimensionality reduction method was further used to visualize the classification results of single cell subsets. The differences of genes in different clusters were analyzed. Through these analyses, the characteristics of single cell populations in different groups were compared. SingleCell Data Analysis was performed using the OmicStudio tools created by LC-BIO Co., Ltd (HangZhou, China) at https://www.omicstudio.cn/cell.

## Microarray dataset analysis
The microarray dataset (GSE97779) of macrophages from the GEO database includes peripheral blood macrophages from healthy controls(HC) and RA synovial fluid macrophages. The data was normalized using the preprocessCore package in R software, applying the normalize quantiles function for standardization. Batch effects were removed using the removeBatchEffect function from the limma package in R. Differential gene analysis was performed using the edgeR package, focusing on genetic variations with a fold change (FC) threshold of 4.

## Isolation and culture of macrophages in RA
Monocytes were isolated from peripheral blood of RA patients (with informed consent and ethics approval) and differentiated into macrophages. Briefly, 5 mL of fresh anticoagulated whole blood was diluted 1:1 with PBS, layered over 5 mL of lymphocyte separation medium, and centrifuged (2000 rpm, 20 min, RT). The PBMC layer was collected, lysed with RBC lysis buffer, washed, and resuspended in MACS buffer. CD14+ monocytes were isolated using magnetic microbeads (20 μL/107 cells, 4 °C incubation for 15-30 min), followed by positive selection via MS column (Miltenyi, Germany). After washing, purified CD14+ monocytes were cultured in RPMI 1640 (10% FBS, 1% penicillin/streptomycin) with 50 ng/mL M-CSF (PeproTech, USA) to induce macrophage differentiation.

## Cell culture
Macrophages were cultured in a mixed medium consisting of 70% 1640 medium and 30% RA synovial fluid, supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco), 100 IU/mL penicillin, and 100 μg/mL streptomycin. The cells were maintained at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere with 5% CO2. The synovial fluid was obtained from RA patients and centrifuged at 300 g to remove cellular debris prior to use.

## Co-culture protocol
Macrophages were inoculated into the lower cavity of the Transwell inserter (Corning, USA) at a density of 1 × 106 cells per well with or without lipopolysaccharide for 100 ng/mL for 24 h (LPS, Sigma, Germany). iMSCs were spread on the upper cavity (a membrane with a pore size of 0.4 μm) in a number of 1 × 105 cells/pores. The medium volume was 2 mL in the lower cavity and 2 mL in the upper cavity. After incubation, culture supernatant and macrophage RNA were collected for further experiments. Cells and supernatant were collected and stored at −80°C for future use.

## Quantitative real-time PCR analysis
Extract total RNA from macrophage using TRIzol reagent (Tiangen Biotech, China). Perform reverse transcription (RT) and RT-PCR using the M5 HiPer First Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit and M5 HiPer plus Taq HiFi PCR mix (Mei5 Biotechno­logy, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. β-actin serves as the endogenous control. RT-PCR primers are synthesized by GeneCopoeia (Tiangen Biotech, China). The genes’ primer sequences are outlined in .

## Western blot analysis
To detect the expression and phosphorylation levels of NF-κB (abcam, UK) and IκB (abcam, UK) proteins in macrophages, cells were lysed using RIPA buffer supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors. The total protein concentration in cell extracts was quantified using a BCA assay kit. Equal amounts of protein (25 μg per sample) were separated by 10% SDS-PAGE and transferred onto PVDF membranes. The membranes were blocked with 0.1% bovine serum albumin (BSA) and then incubated with primary antibodies at 4 °C overnight, followed by incubation with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies (1:3000 dilution in BSA) at room temperature for 1 h. Protein bands were visualized using chemiluminescence detection reagents and X-ray film exposure.

## Statistical analysis
Statistical analyses were conducted using Bonferroni Multiple Comparison Test on GraphPad Prism. P <0.05 were considered significant. Average values and standard deviations were ­calculated for each group. A trend was noted for P < 0.05 in one-tailed t-tests. Results were presented as mean ± SD.

## Generation and identification of iPSCs and iMSCs
Healthy volunteer PBMC was used as a cell source for reprogramming iPSCs. The morphological changes during differentiation are shown in . AP staining was used to show the proportion of plasmid-positive cells, indicating pluripotency ( ). Then we identified the karyotype, and the karyotype was normal ( ), and stem cell-related genes (OCT4 and SOX2) were identified ( ). iPSCs were successfully induced. iPSCs were induced into MSCs using the Mesenchymal Progenitor Kit, morphological changes of the cells could be seen during the induction process ( ). Specifically, as most iPSCs adopt the typical MSCs spindle shape on day 10. To provide additional evidence of the multipotent capabilities of iMSCs, osteoblasts, lipoblasts, and chondroblasts were generated through induction by iMSCs ( ). MSCs with surface markers CD73, CD116, CD44, and CD105 were identified by flow cytometry after repassage after 13 days of differentiation and did not express negative markers CD45, CD34, CD11b, CD14, HLA-DR ( ). The above results indicated that iMSCs were successfully induced. Comparative analysis revealed no significant differences in the expression levels of positive surface markers between the iMSCs and umbilical cord mesenchymal stem cells (UCMSCs) ( , see for a color version of this figure).
 

## iMSC treatment alleviate joint inflammation in the CIA model
To investigate the therapeutic effects of iMSCs on RA, this study employed the collagen-induced arthritis (CIA) model as an RA animal model, with PBS and UCMSCs serving as control groups. illustrates the schematic workflow of model establishment and experimental interventions. During the treatment period, both the PBS and CIA groups exhibited weight loss, likely attributable to pain and systemic inflammatory responses that impaired appetite. Notably, the CIA group showed more pronounced weight reduction, possibly due to persistent inflammatory conditions ( ). Statistical analyses of paw swelling and arthritis index throughout the treatment duration were performed. Line graphs demonstrated that both UCMSC and iMSC groups had significantly lower paw swelling and joint scores compared to PBS and CIA groups, though no statistically significant difference was observed between UCMSC and iMSC treatments ( ). Interestingly, all treatment groups displayed transient exacerbation of swelling during the initial phase, peaking at day 42 before gradual improvement in UCMSC and iMSC groups. To further evaluate systemic anti-inflammatory effects, serum levels of inflammatory cytokines (TNFα, IL1β, and IL6) were measured post-treatment. Results showed significantly reduced inflammatory factors in UCMSC and iMSC groups versus CIA group ( ), with no statistical difference between the two MSC treatments (P > 0.05). Histopathological examination (H&E staining) revealed no significant abnormalities in liver, kidney, or spleen morphology, nor evident inflammatory cell infiltration across all groups ( , see for a color version of this figure).
 

## iMSC treatment can alleviate the destruction of bone in CIA
To further investigate the therapeutic efficacy of iMSCs, micro-computed tomography (Micro-CT) and histological analyses were employed to assess joint destruction and synovial inflammation. Micro-CT scans of ankle ( ) and knee joints ( ) revealed: normal mice exhibited smooth bone surfaces, whereas CIA mice showed rough bone surfaces with severe erosion; iMSCs treatment significantly ameliorated bone erosion and improved surface smoothness compared to the PBS group. Notably, no significant difference was observed between UCMSC and iMSC groups in bone erosion repair (P >0.05). Quantitative analysis demonstrated that iMSCs treatment significantly improved bone volume/total volume (BV/TV), trabecular number (Tb.N), and trabecular separation (Tb.Sp) in both ankle ( ) and knee joints ( ) compared to PBS group, reaching levels comparable to normal controls (NC), with no statistically significant difference from UCMSCs treatment.
 H&E staining showed reduced inflammatory cell infiltration and restored synovial structure in iMSC-treated mice. Safranin O-Fast Green staining revealed marked recovery of cartilage formation (red arrows) and joint architecture compared to PBS group, again with comparable outcomes to UCMSCs treatment ( ). Both the iMSC and UCMSC groups demonstrated significantly reduced OARSI scores ( ) and Mankin scores ( ) compared to PBS group. These results demonstrate that iMSCs effectively alleviate inflammation-induced bone loss in both knee and ankle joints, with therapeutic efficacy equivalent to UCMSCs ( -C, see for a color version of this figure). To further validate the generalizability of iMSC treatment in CIA models, we conducted parallel intervention experiments using a rat CIA model. The results demonstrated that iMSC administration similarly alleviated articular cartilage damage and bone loss in this system ( , see for a color version of this figure).
 

## Overall of CD45+ cells in synovial tissue
The impact of iMSCs treatment on the immune cell population within the synovial tissue of the knee joint in mice was investigated through the scRNA-seq of CD45+ cells after treatment. Following quality control and dimensionality reduction analysis, a comprehensive cellular landscape was generated. Subsequently, marker genes specific to each immune cell type were utilized for cell population annotation, resulting in the identification of five predominant immune cell types ( , , see for a color version of this figure). CellPhoneDB was utilized for the analysis of intercellular communication among various cell populations. Based on the ligand-receptor pairing data ( , see for a color version of this figure), protein interaction values were calculated between different cell types, yielding statistically significant results (P <0.05). Heatmap were generated to visually represent these interactions ( ). The findings suggest that macrophages exhibit the highest level of communication with other cell populations, indicating their potential role as key effector cells in iMSCs therapy.
 

## Characteristics of macrophage in synovial tissue
Further examination of distinct macrophage clusters revealed observable differences in macrophage clusters between PBS and iMSC groups ( ). M1 markers (CD80, CD86, and IL1β) and M2 markers (MRC1(CD206), CD163, and ADGRE1(F4/80)) were utilized for macrophage clusters analysis, indicating that macrophages could be categorized into three clusters: M1, M2, and an intermediate macrophage (Mint) which expression of both markers was not obvious ( , see for a color version of this figure). The ratio of M1, M2 and Mint did not change significantly after iMSC treatment ( , see for a color version of this figure). In addition, it was observed that the most obvious cluster difference between PBS and iMSC group appeared in the M1 cluster ( ). Marker gene identification revealed the presence of seven clusters of macrophages, with the major marker genes of these groups displayed in and (see for a color version of this figure). Macrophages exhibiting high levels of CD86 were further categorized into CD86hiIL1βhi and CD86hiIL1βlo (hi: high, lo: low) based on their expression of IL1β, representing the two macrophage clusters with the most significant disparity iMSCs treatment. CD86hiIL1βhi and CD86hiIL1βlo cluster had high expression of interferon-related genes, CCR2 and VCAM, and were a pro-inflammatory macrophage population with strong recycling and chemotactic ability. MRC1+ macrophages are mainly divided into two clusters, MRC1+MerTK+ and MRC1+LY6D+. Stefano Alivernini et al. suggested that MRC1+MerTK+ cluster is associated with remission maintenance. Stephan Culemann et al. found CX3CR1 organization resides of macrophages. In MRC1+MerTK+ cluster, we observed CX3CR1, TREM2, TIMD4 and high expression of APOE, this is consistent with literature reports ( , see for a color version of this figure). In addition, Mint macrophages were divided into three clusters according to their main characteristic genes: CD86loMPO+, CD86loCD48, and CD86loS100A8. These three clusters of macrophages do not have obvious markers for M1 and M2, so they are further defined based on their marker genes. The characteristic feature of the CD86lowS100A8 cluster is the abundance of the inflammatory alarmin proteins S100A8/9. S100A8/9 act as chemoattractants for neutrophils and inducers of TNF and IL6 production by monocytes and fibroblasts, respectively. This cluster may be associated with the persistence of inflammation. Through a comparative analysis of subset proportions, it was determined that there was no statistically significant difference in the proportions of M1, M2, and Mint cluster between PBS and iMSC group. However, notable disparities were observed in the proportions of CD86hiIL1βhi and CD86hiIL1βlo cluster. Specifically, the CD86hiIL1βhi cluster exhibited a significant reduction in iMSC group ( ). Consequently, it is plausible to suggest that the therapeutic efficacy of iMSCs may be associated with the decreased presence of the CD86hiIL1βhi cluster.

## CD86hiIL1βhi macrophages are the main target cell cluster for iMSCs therapy
In order to investigate the distinctions between CD86hiIL1βhi and CD86hiIL1βlo cluster and their developmental trajectories, we employed RNA velocity analysis to assess gene expression dynamics at the single-cell level. This involved quantifying the rates of transcription and degradation of newly transcribed pre-spliced mRNA and mature post-spliced mRNA in a standard scRNA dataset. Utilizing stochastic models within the scVelo framework, we inferred gene-specific transcription, splicing, and degradation rates for distinct macrophage populations, allowing us to reconstruct the temporal sequence of cellular events. The findings indicated that there was minimal variation in shear rate among all clusters ( , see for a color version of this figure), and there was a noticeable shift in macrophage phenotype from PBS to iMSC group, transitioning from M1 to M2, and from CD86hiIL1βhi to CD86hiIL1βlo cluster ( ). Differential genes (DEGs) analysis of the two clusters (CD86hiIL1βhi and CD86hiIL1βlo) showed the expression of inflammation-related factors such as SAA and IL1β decreased significantly in CD86hiIL1βlo cluster ( , , see for a color version of this figure). KEGG analysis was conducted on increased DEGs and decreased DEGs. The up-regulated DEGs were predominantly associated with oxidative phosphorylation and metabolism-related signaling pathways ( , see for a color version of this figure). GO analysis revealed that the upregulated genes were significantly enriched in immune and inflammatory responses, as well as cellular functions related to extracellular exosomes ( , see for a color version of this figure). In contrast, the down-regulated DEGs were enriched in the TNF signaling pathway, NF-κB signaling pathway, and pathways related to RA, suggesting a significant correlation between the observed alterations in this cluster and the pathogenesis of RA. ( ). The analysis of the enriched genes of the three pathways showed that TNF, IL1β, CXCl2, and ICAM1 were the key genes ( , see for a color version of this figure). To further clarify the situation of macrophages in the synovial microenvironment in clinical samples, we performed DEGs analysis on the data of public platform chip in healthy controls (HC) and RA. By DEGs analysis of synovial macrophages of HC and RA, 481 up-regulated and 332 down-regulated DEGs were found ( , see for a color version of this figure). KEGG analysis revealed that up-regulated DEGs in RA were significantly enriched in the TNF signaling pathway, the NF-κB signaling pathway, and pathways associated with RA ( , see for a color version of this figure). This finding aligns with the macrophage-targeted pathways observed in CIA models following treatment with iMSCs ( ). There are 32 overlapped genes of RNA-up (DEGs up-regulated in RA compared with HC) and scRNA-down (DEGs down-regulated in CD86hiIL1βlo compared with CD86hiIL1βhi). The analysis of overlapped genes through PPI network revealed that IL1β and TNF were the hub genes ( ). In the scRNA seq, IL1β and TNF were mainly expressed in macrophages and granulocytes ( , see for a color version of this figure), and the major DEGs were expressed in CD86hiIL1βhi and CD86hiIL1βlo clusters ( ). Through the joint analysis of CIA and RA macrophages, it was found that IL1β, TNF, and related inflammatory signaling pathways (TNF signaling pathway, NF-κB signaling pathway) are crucial therapeutic targets for macrophages in RA, and they are also key therapeutic pathways for iMSCs. Specifically, CD86hiIL1βhi cells are the primary therapeutic target cells for iMSCs.
 

## iMSCs promoting the transformation of CD86hiIL1βhi into CD86hiIL1βlo
We further assessed the expression levels of IL1β and TNFα in clinical samples from HC and RA synovial tissues. It was found that the synovial lining in RA tissues was significantly thicker, with increased levels of IL1β and TNFα ( ). In comparison to CIA and iMSC group within the CIA model, iMSCs were found to reduce the expression levels of IL1β and TNFα in synovial tissue, thus decreasing inflammation levels ( ). In order to further verify the effect of iMSCs on the reduction of IL1β in macrophages, we used RA macrophages for co-culture and detected the genes related to inflammation ( ). After inducing macrophages with LPS and co-culturing them for 24 h. The levels of IL1β and TNFα in the co-culture supernatant were decreased, which corresponded to a reduction in macrophage secretion ( ). The expressions of IL1β, TNFα, iNOS, and MCP1 were all significantly reduced ( ). This suggests that iMSCs can mitigate the inflammatory phenotype of macrophages. Additionally, bioinformatics analysis revealed that the phenotypic transition from CD86hiIL1βhi to CD86hiIL1βlo subsets was significantly associated with downregulation of NF-κB signaling pathway. We subsequently examined phosphorylation levels of key NF-κB pathway components (P65/NF-κB and IκB) in co-culture systems. Notably, iMSCs co-cultured with LPS-prestimulated RA macrophages consistently reduced phosphorylation of both NF-κB and IκB, regardless of persistent LPS stimulation ( ).
 

## Discussion
Preclinical studies on MSC therapy for RA have demonstrated promising therapeutic effects, while reviews of clinical studies investigating the use of MSCs in RA have predominantly reported positive outcomes. However, the heterogeneity of MSCs and aging-related challenges present significant obstacles to further advancements in clinical translation. The large-scale production of heterogeneous products poses quality control challenges and necessitates repeated sampling due to batch-to-batch variability stemming from their aging propensity. Wang et al. conducted an in-depth analysis of the disparities between iMSCs and UCMSCs, revealing that iMSCs exhibit greater similarity to MSCs in the original tissue microenvironment. Joanna Frobel et al. have demonstrated that iMSCs retain donor-derived DNA methylation profiles while eliminating tissue-specific and age-related DNA signatures. Consequently, iMSCs demonstrate superior proliferative capacity, reduced heterogeneity, and enhanced immunosuppressive and osteogenic potential compared to tissue-derived MSCs. Thus, we posit that iMSCs may offer the most effective solution to address these limitations. We have systematically discussed and reviewed the heterogeneity and advantages of iMSCs, so we will avoid delving into broad details here. , 
In contrast to directly tissue-derived MSCs, iMSCs exhibit significant advantages in clinical translation; however, their efficacy in RA treatment necessitates further investigation. Studies on MSC-based RA therapy have elucidated their predominant mechanisms of action, primarily centered on immune response suppression and the attenuation of bone destruction. , Notably, our investigation also discerned the therapeutic potential of iMSCs in a CIA model. Acknowledging that local joint inflammation is a key factor in the perpetuation and exacerbation of RA pathology, this study employed intra-articular injection of iMSCs for therapeutic intervention. Furthermore, extant research on MSCs has underscored that a significant proportion of administered cells become sequestered within the pulmonary vasculature, underscoring the rationale for local injection to enhance MSCs homing to the target site, potentially conferring heightened efficacy in mitigating local inflammation and facilitating tissue regeneration. Encouragingly, our experimental findings substantiated this conjecture. Despite the local administration of iMSCs into the joint cavity, comprehensive evaluation revealed discernible reductions in paw swelling and joint destruction within the CIA model. A comprehensive assessment of systemic inflammation levels further corroborated these observations, manifesting as significant reductions in key inflammatory biomarkers within the CIA model, indicative of ameliorated systemic inflammation in RA. Furthermore, imaging and histological appraisal of the injected knee joint evinced diminished inflammatory cell infiltration and attenuated bone and cartilage degradation. Hence, iMSCs hold promise as agents for immune inflammation suppression and preservation of bone integrity in the context of RA pathology.
Research has demonstrated the therapeutic efficacy of iMSCs in the management of osteoarthritis and other diseases, highlighting their potential in ameliorating arthritis. RA is a systemic condition, underscoring the significance of targeting specific cells for treatment. Utilizing scRNA seq, this study examined CD45+ cells in synovial tissue pre- and post-treatment, elucidating the predominant interaction between iMSCs and macrophages in mitigating the inflammatory milieu of the synovium. The analysis demonstrated that interactions between iMSCs and macrophages are key in attenuating the inflammatory milieu of the synovial microenvironment. Macrophages are pivotal in the pathogenesis of RA and significantly contribute to disease progression. Synovial tissue macrophages originate from two primary cell lineages: bone marrow-derived mononuclear cells and monocytes, which undergo a spectrum of differentiation from scattered to organized during embryonic development. , Monocytes are crucial in the initiation and perpetuation of inflammation in the synovial tissue of RA, while macrophages in the synovium play a significant role in T cell activation and are stimulated by antigens. These cells can also be recruited from the bloodstream to the synovial tissue through chemotaxis, facilitated by interactions with fibroblast-like synoviocytes (FLSs) and other immune cells. The CD86hiIL1βhi and CD86hiIL1βlo clusters identified in this study exhibited characteristics indicative of a monocytic origin. Additionally, a distinct cluster of macrophages known as synovial tissue-resident macrophages was also observed. Stephan Culemann et.al. discovered that the organization of CX3CR1 within a dynamic membrane structure composed of macrophages in the synovial lining forms an internal immune barrier, exhibiting typical characteristics of epithelial cells. Stefano Alivernini et.al. suggested that the MRC1+MerTK+ cluster is linked to remission maintenance. Additionally, MRC1+MerTK+ cluster was identified in the study, with no significant variance in key genes pre- and post-treatment. Further investigation is necessary to explore the impact of iMSCs on this specific cluster.
Macrophages exhibit plasticity, with their dynamic phenotypes and subsets influencing the immune milieu of the microenvironment. The physiological role of macrophages is contingent upon their activation state, which is traditionally classified into the M1 and M2 phenotypes, representing opposing poles of macrophage activation. Research indicates that the equilibrium between TNFα-producing macrophages and IL10-secreting anti-inflammatory M2 macrophages is disrupted in RA compared to HC, with a higher proportion of M1 relative to M2. The activation of the NF-κB signaling pathway in macrophages in RA, induced by TLR4, is responsible for the pro-inflammatory behavior of M1, leading to the ­production and release of IL6. Furthermore, TNFα and IL1β contribute to the exacerbation of inflammation. Research on MSCs and macrophages has shown that MSCs typically possess the ability to inhibit inflammatory M1 and promote the M2 phenotype. The study demonstrated that RA is associated with the promotion of M2 phenotypes. MSCs have been confirmed that in vitro studies can promote the transformation of M1 to M2, but the in vivo effects require further investigation. In our study, there was no significant difference in the ratio of M1 to M2 changes after treatment. However, further analysis of M1 in synovial tissue revealed significant changes in the CD86hiIL1βhi and CD86hiIL1βlo cluster. Treatment with iMSCs resulted in the conversion of CD86hiIL1βhi to CD86hiIL1βlo, which may be a key mechanism of therapy. The pivotal event in the transition of macrophages from a resting state to the M1 phenotype is glycometabolic reprogramming, characterized by a shift from oxidative phosphorylation to aerobic glycolysis. During this process, the oxygen consumed is primarily utilized for the synthesis of reactive oxygen species (ROS), which can enhance the inflammatory response of macrophages through the activation of factors such as NF-κB. Consequently, attenuating the NF-κB signaling pathway in M1 macrophages and shifting their metabolic processes toward oxidative phosphorylation are crucial strategies for inhibiting M1 activity. Upon analyzing the DEGs and pathway alterations between the two cell clusters, it was observed that both the TNF signaling pathway and the NF-κB signaling pathway were significantly down-regulated, whereas the oxidative phosphorylation pathway was up-regulated. We hypothesize that induced iMSCs have the potential to attenuate the NF-κB signaling pathway, reprogram the metabolic profile of CD86hiIL1βhi, and convert them into CD86hiIL1βlo cluster. This transformation may lead to a reduction in the secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines, including TNFα and IL1β, thereby mitigating joint inflammation and bone destruction associated with RA.

## Conclusions
Our study, employing the CIA model of iMSCs therapy, elucidates the potential of iMSCs to attenuate inflammation and bone degradation in the context of rheumatoid arthritis. Further analysis of macrophage subset alterations following treatment indicated that iMSCs facilitated the transition of CD86hiIL1βhi to CD86hiIL1βlo phenotype. This transformation effectively shifted the inflammatory characteristics of macrophages toward a suppressive state, ultimately contributing to the reduction of joint inflammation and bone destruction.
