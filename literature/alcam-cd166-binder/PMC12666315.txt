## Abstract
Colorectal cancer progression is associated with an increase in PROX1+ tumor cells, which exhibit features of colorectal cancer stem cells and contribute to metastasis. Here, we aimed to provide a better understanding of the function of PROX1+ cells in colorectal cancer, investigating their progeny and their role in therapy resistance. PROX1+ cells in intestinal adenomas of ApcMin/+ mice expressed intestinal epithelial and colorectal cancer stem cell markers, and cells with high PROX1 expression could both self-renew tumor stem/progenitor cells and contribute to differentiated tumor cells. Most Prox1 lineage–traced tumor cells were stem/progenitor cells, which can supply cells to multiple intestinal tumor cell lineages, whereas most lineage-traced Lgr5+ tumor cells were enterocytes, indicating that Prox1+ and Lgr5+ tumor stem cells have distinct differentiation programs. Although the PROX1+ tumor cells proliferated slower than PROX1− cells, irradiation increased the proportion of PROX1+ cells in human colorectal cancer cell lines, patient-derived organoids, and tumor xenografts. Furthermore, transcripts related to DNA damage repair (DDR) were enriched in PROX1+ versus PROX1− cells in adenomas and in colorectal cancer tumor cells from patients. Experiments with PROX1 silencing and overexpression indicated that PROX1 expression enhances colorectal cancer cell colony formation following irradiation. PROX1 interacted with DDR proteins, including components of nonhomologous end joining (NHEJ) and base excision repair, and inhibition of NHEJ repair led to a decreased proportion of PROX1+ cells following irradiation. In conclusion, PROX1+ cells are irradiation-resistant tumor stem/progenitor cells capable of self-renewal and differentiation. DDR inhibitors could represent a strategy to target the treatment-resistant PROX1+ tumor stem cells.

## Significance:
Colorectal cancer stem/progenitor cells expressing the PROX1 transcription factor display increased radiation resistance, which may be targeted by small-molecule inhibitors of the nonhomologous end-joining DNA damage repair pathway.

## Introduction
Colorectal cancer is the third most common cancer worldwide ( ). Colorectal cancer treatment includes surgical resection of the primary tumor and lung and liver metastases, chemotherapy, targeted therapies, and immunotherapy. Approximately one third of colorectal cancers are rectal cancers, in which preoperative radiotherapy is also used. Although radiotherapy has led to increased disease-free survival, the overall patient survival has not improved ( ). A better understanding of the molecular mechanisms underlying resistance to rectal cancer treatment should lead to increased survival and decreased recurrence rates.
We have previously shown that the expression of PROX1, a downstream target of the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway that is activated in approximately 90% of patients with colorectal cancer, is increased in intestinal adenomas of ApcMin/+ mice and during human colorectal cancer progression ( – ). PROX1+ colorectal cancer cells have been shown to exhibit tumor stem cell features and increased resistance to cytostatic treatments via increased expression of the cell-cycle inhibitor CDKN1C ( ). Furthermore, previous studies using Prox1-lineage tracing have shown that PROX1+ cells can produce differentiated tumor cells ( ). Lgr5, a marker of healthy intestinal epithelial stem cells, is also expressed in some colorectal cancer stem cells ( ). However, differences between LGR5+ and PROX1+ colorectal cancer cells have not been characterized. Here, we analyzed the transcriptomics of Prox1- or Lgr5-lineage–traced ApcMin/+ tumor cells and found that the progenies of these stem/progenitor cells differ.
As PROX1+ colorectal cancer cells have increased resistance to cytostatic agents via increased expression of CDKN1C, and as the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway promotes radioresistance ( , ), we have here analyzed whether PROX1 expression in tumor stem/progenitor cells is associated with radioresistance. We assessed PROX1 expression in colorectal cancer cell lines, patient samples, and mouse intestinal adenomas. We found that PROX1-expressing tumor stem cells/progenitor cells proliferated slower than PROX1-negative tumor cells, yet they were enriched after exposure to radiation. We also found that several DNA repair pathways were upregulated in PROX1+ tumor cells and nonhomologous end joining (NHEJ) was important for the survival of PROX1+ colorectal cancer cells, as shown by the chemical inhibition of LIG4, one of key NHEJ components. Our results should offer new insights into targeting colorectal cancer resistance to radiotherapy.

## Materials and Methods

## Isolation and culture of patient-derived three-dimensional organoids for single-cell RNA sequencing
The Ethical Committee of the Helsinki University Central Hospital approved the experiments involving patient samples. Written informed consent was obtained from all the patients. This study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Fresh colorectal cancer tissue samples were chopped, washed in cold PBS, and incubated in advanced DMEM/F12 (Gibco, #12634-010) containing 5 μg/mL metronidazole (Sigma, #M-3761), penicillin/streptomycin (Lonza, #DE-17-602E), 4 mg/mL dispase II, 1 mg/mL collagenase I, and 1,000 U/mL benzonase at 37°C for 40 minutes. Tissue fragments were mechanically disrupted, pelleted for 5 minutes at 1,500 RPM in advanced DMEM/F12, dispersed in advanced DMEM/F12 growth medium, and allowed to settle for 1 minute. The supernatant containing the tumor cells was collected and the cycle was repeated 5 to 10 times. The tumor cells were finally combined, centrifuged for 5 minutes at 1,500 RPM, embedded in Geltrex (50 μL per well in a 24-well plate; Gibco, #A14132-02), and cultured in advanced DMEM/F12 growth medium supplemented with B27 (Gibco, #17504-044), N2 (Gibco, #17502-048), 2 mmol/L glutamine (Corning, #25-005-CI), 10 nmol/L gastrin I (Sigma, #G-9020), 10 mmol/L HEPES (Thermo Fisher Scientific, #BP310-1), 1 mmol/L N-acetylcysteine (Sigma, #A-9165), 10 mmol/L nicotinamide (Sigma, #N-0636), 50 ng/mL human recombinant EGF (PeproTech, #AF-100-15), 500 nmol/L A83-01 (Sigma, #SML0788), 10 µmol/L SB202190 (p38/MAP kinase inhibitor, Sigma, #S7067), and 10 µmol/L SB431542 (TFG-βR1 inhibitor, Tocris, #1614; 500 μL growth medium per well). Wnt agonists were not added to the culture medium. Metronidazole (5 μg/mL) was used for 1 week to inhibit anaerobic bacteria. The organoids were passaged once a week. First, Geltrex was manually disrupted using a pipette tip, and the wells were washed thoroughly with PBS to collect all organoids. Organoids were centrifuged and dispersed into fresh Geltrex, which was allowed to polymerize in new wells.
Geltrex was disrupted as described above for dissociation of organoids into single cells, and the samples were centrifuged. After removal of the supernatant, warm trypsin was added, and the organoids were incubated at 37°C for 15 to 20 minutes. Samples were vortexed every 5 minutes during incubation and vigorously pipetted 50 to 100 times at the end of incubation. This was followed by washing with advanced DMEM/F12 and centrifugation at 1,500 RPM for 5 minutes. The single-cell suspension was confirmed using microscopy. From each sample, 10,000 cells with good viability (>70%) were used. The samples were processed with Chromium Next GEM Single Cell 3′ Reagent Kit v3.1 (10x Genomics) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The experiment was repeated once, and organoids from each treatment group were harvested from six wells. Clinical data of the patients with colorectal cancer are listed in Supplementary Table S1.

## Dissociation of tumors into single cells for single-cell RNA sequencing
Macroscopic ApcMin/+ adenomas (RRID: IMSR_JAX:002020) from all different anatomic regions of the small intestine were dissected and stored in high-glucose DMEM (Gibco, #11960-044) on ice. After isolation of all adenomas, the medium was replaced with a cell dissociation buffer containing 4 mg/mL dispase II (Sigma, #04942078001), 1 mg/mL collagenase I (Worthington, #LS004196), 1 mg/mL collagenase H (Roche, #11074032001), and 1,000 U/mL benzonase (ChemCruz, #sc-202391) in Hank’s Balanced Salt Solution (HBSS; Gibco, #14175-053). After 30 minutes of incubation at 37°C, trypsin-EDTA (Thermo Fisher Scientific, #25200056) was added, and incubation was continued for 10 minutes at 37°C. The samples were vortexed regularly during incubation. Trypsin was inactivated in high-glucose DMEM. Samples were filtered through a 40-μm filter, followed by centrifugation for 10 minutes at 150 relative centrifugal force (RCF). For analysis with Chromium X, 10,000 cells in 0.04% BSA in HBSS with good viability (>70%) per sample were targeted.

## Dissociation of intestinal epithelium into single cells for single-cell RNA sequencing
Small intestines from wild-type (WT) mice were incubated in 10 mmol/L EDTA pH 8.0 for 10 minutes on ice. Cells were detached by scraping with a coverslip. After washing with PBS and centrifugation for 5 minutes at 150 RCF, the cells were incubated in 4 mg/mL dispase II (Sigma, #04942078001), 1 mg/mL collagenase I (Worthington, #LS004196), 1 mg/mL collagenase H (Roche, #11074032001), and 1000 U/mL benzonase (ChemCruz, #sc-202391) in HBSS (Gibco, #14175-053) for 30 minutes at 32°C. This was followed by washing with cold DMEM, filtering once with a 100-μm filter and twice with a 40-μm filter, and centrifuging for 10 minutes at 150 RCF. Then, 10,000 cells in 0.04% BSA in HBSS, with good viability per sample, were targeted for 10× Chromium. Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) experiment was repeated three times, and the data were pooled from four mice.

## scRNA-seq analysis
Sample libraries produced with Chromium X (RRID: SCR_024537) were sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 sequencer (RRID: SCR_016387) at the Institute for Molecular Medicine Finland using the Single Cell Analytics unit. Cell Ranger (version 6.1.2, 7.0.0, or 7.0.1, RRID: SCR_017344) was used to align the reads, quantify gene expression, generate custom references, and call the cells. The reads were aligned to the mouse reference genome mm10 (ApcMin/+ experiments) and the human reference genome GRCH38 [patient-derived three-dimensional (3D) organoid experiment]. Data analysis was performed using R Studio (version 4.2.2) following Seurat version 4 and 5 instructions (RRID: SCR_007322; ref. ). SoupX (version 1.6.2, RRID: SCR_019193; ref. ) was used to reduce background RNA contamination, and adjusted counts were then used for the Seurat analysis. Doublets were identified and removed using DoubletFinder (version 2.0.3, RRID: SCR_018771; ref. ). pN was set to 0.25 (as suggested by the method developers), pK was determined using the paramSweep_v3() function, and nExp was calculated based on the 10x recommendation multiplet rate (https://kb.10xgenomics.com/hc/en-us/articles/360001378811-What-is-the-maximum-number-of-cells-that-can-be-profiled) for each sample separately. Cells with fewer than 200 features and genes expressed in fewer than three cells were filtered out. Samples were filtered based on the expression of mitochondrial genes (cutoff 15%–20%) and the number of RNA features (500/2,000–9,000). After filtering, Malat1 and genes representing rRNA contamination and overlapping with the rRNA element Rn45s including Gm42418, Gm26917, and AY036118 were removed from further analysis. Individual samples were normalized using SCTransform with the glmGamPoi method (RRID: SCR_022146; ref. ). The samples were integrated for downstream analysis by identifying common anchors ( ). The first 35 principal components recognized as significant by the ElbowPlot and DimHeatmap were used for the analysis. The FindNeighbours and FindClusters commands were used for cell clustering with a resolution of 0.5. For RNA expression analysis, integrated objects were log-normalized (scale factor = 10,000), and variable features were identified using the FindVariableFeatures command. Data were then scaled. Clusters were identified based on previously published cell markers. Once the clusters were identified, the Epcam+ cells/cluster were subjected to further analysis. After scaling and finding new clusters with the resolution set to 1.0, the cell number in each treatment group was downsampled to 1,600 to match the cell number in each treatment group. Differential gene expression analysis was performed using FindAllMarkers. The Monocle3 package (RRID: SCR_018685; refs. – ) was used for trajectory analysis. Differences in cellular composition across WT and ApcMin/+ tumors were analyzed using the Bayesian method scCODA (version 0.1.9; ref. ). The stem/progenitor 5 cluster was used as the reference cell type for the compositional analysis. The stem/progenitor 5 cluster was automatically selected by the tool (reference_cell_type = “automatic”); however, using stem/progenitor 2 and goblet clusters as reference yielded consistent results, supporting the robustness of our findings. In compliance with the scCODA package default settings, the FDR value of 0.05 was used as the cutoff between credible and noncredible effects.

## RNA velocity and cell fate inference
The spliced and unspliced mRNA transcripts per gene were obtained from Cell Ranger bam files using velocyto (version 0.17.17, RRID: SCR_018167) and merged with the preprocessed count data obtained from Seurat. Gene expression levels were normalized using the standard scanning normalization approach to ensure consistency across cells and mitigate the effect of sequencing biases.
RNA velocity analysis and trajectory inferences were performed using scvelo (version 0.3.2; ref. ). Moments representing first- and second-order gene expression dynamics were computed using scvelo.pp.moments() with default parameters. Velocity vectors, capturing the direction and rate of gene expression changes, were then calculated in dynamic mode using the scvelo.tl.velocity(). Velocity graphs were constructed and plotted using scvelo.tl.velocity_graph() and scvelo.pl.velocity_embedding_stream().
The computed velocities were used as inputs for further analysis using CellRank (version 2.0.4, RRID: SCR_022827; ref. ). kk.compute_transition_matrix() and cellrank.plot_projection() were employed to calculate the cell–cell transition matrix and for visualization. Cell fate estimation and macrostate inference were performed using the GPCCA estimator. Cellrank. plot _macrostates() was utilized to plot the predicted macrostates, including the initial and terminal cell states. The RNA velocity was separately run per sample, and one of the samples was selected for final presentation after confirming the consistency of the results across the whole dataset.

## Differential abundance analysis
The edgeR package (RRID: SCR_012802; ref. ) was used to perform differential abundance analysis to assess the effect of radiation on the depletion or enrichment of cell types expressing specific genes. A count matrix was constructed, in which each entry represented the number of cells in the cell types of interest across the experimental conditions [Ctrl, irradiation (IR) 1 day, IR 2 days, and IR 7 days]. The DGEList object was then created and normalized based on the total number of cells in each sample, a design matrix was formulated to model the experimental conditions, and dispersion estimates were calculated. We then fitted a generalized linear model to the count data and conducted quasi-likelihood F tests (glmQLFTest) for pairwise comparisons of interest. The Benjamini–Hochberg method was applied for multiple testing corrections.

## Reanalysis of colorectal cancer scRNA-seq data
The GSE132465 ( ) and GSE200997 ( ) datasets were reanalyzed. Eight samples were analyzed from GSE132465 data, including 986, 309, 170, 451, 1761, 276, 274, and 261 EpCAM+ cells used for the analysis. A total of 15 samples from GSE200997 data were used for the analysis, and these included 724, 800, 525, 76, 90, 790, 277, 201, 61, 821, 389, 98, 1,004, 287, and 107 (in total 6,250) EpCAM+ cells.

## Culture of tumor cells
All the cell lines were maintained at 37°C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2. DMEM-F12 (Lonza, #BE04-687F/U1) was used for LS174T cells (RRID: CVCL_1384); DMEM with high glucose (Gibco, #11960-044) for HT29 (RRID: CVCL_A8EZ), SW1222 (RRID: CVCL_3886), and HEK293FT cells (Thermo Fisher Scientific, #R70007, RRID: CVCL_6911); and RPMI-1540 (Corning, #15-040-CV) for LoVo (RRID: CVCL_0399) and HCT116 cells (RRID: CVCL_0291). All growth media were supplemented with 2 mmol/L L-glutamine (Corning, #25-005-CI), 100 U/mL penicillin/streptomycin (Lonza, #DE-17-602E), and 10% FBS (Biowest, #S181B-500). The cells were regularly checked for Mycoplasma contamination using DAPI staining (Vector Laboratories, #H-1200, RRID: AB_2336790). Cell lines were not authenticated except for LS174T cells, which were originally authenticated by the ATCC. Colorectal cancer and HEK293FT cells were used for experiments within 5 to 20 passages of thawing. Goat anti–human PROX1 antibodies (R&D Systems, #AF2727, RRID: AB_2170716) were used for immunoprecipitation and subsequent mass spectrometry analysis.

## Irradiation

In vitro and ex vivo cultures, as well as mice, were irradiated with the gamma irradiator OB29/4 (STS, isotype 137Cs) at a dose of 1.4 Gy/minute. Whole-body irradiation was used for in vivo experiments.

## RNA isolation, cDNA synthesis, and qPCR
RNA was extracted from cells using the NucleoSpin RNA II Kit (Macherey-Nagel, #740955) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. For cDNA synthesis, a High-Capacity cDNA Reverse Transcription Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, #4368814) was used according to the manufacturer’s instructions (1,000 ng of RNA per sample). RNA transcript expression analysis was performed using qPCR (CFX96 Real-Time System) with a DyNAmo HS SYBR Green qPCR Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, #F410XL). Primers used are listed in Supplementary Table S1.

## 
In vivo experiments
All in vivo experiments were approved by the Regional State Administrative Agency of Finland (ESAVI/22643/2022, ESAVI/22042/2019, ESAVI/6306/04.10.07/2016, and ESAVI/7945/04.10.07/2017). The mice were housed in monitoring facilities and had access to food and water ad libitum. Mouse genotypes were checked from ear samples twice before the experiments and once from a tail sample collected after euthanization. SCR7 pyrazine (Sigma-Aldrich, #SML1546) was used to inhibit LIG4. Tumor numbers in the small intestines were counted in overnight paraformaldehyde (PFA)-fixed and PBS-washed tissues.
LS174T cells were injected subcutaneously into the NOD/SCID gamma mice (NOD.Cg-Prkdcscid Il2rgtm1/wjl/SzJ, The Jackson Laboratory, #005557, RRID: IMSR_JAX:005557). Once the tumors had reached an average volume of 100 mm3, the mice were randomized into treatment groups according to the volume of the larger tumor (two tumors/mouse). SCR7 pyrazine (20 mg/kg; Sigma, #SML1546) or PBS was injected intraperitoneally every day starting 1 day before radiation. The mice were euthanized 2 days after irradiation. Tumor numbers in the small intestines were counted in overnight PFA-fixed and PBS-washed tissues. Paraffin sections of the xenografts were stained for PROX1, and the percentage of PROX1+ cells was counted. Representative images of the tumors with staining for PROX1 (red) and DNA (DAPI) are shown.
Conditional deletion and tdTomato lineage tracing were induced by the administration of 2 or 4 mg of tamoxifen dissolved in corn oil (100 μL) 1 to 3 times.

## Immunofluorescence analysis of paraffin sections
Tissue sections (4-µm thick) from overnight 4% PFA-fixed, paraffin-embedded intestinal rolls/tumor were de-paraffinized with xylene substrate (Tissue-Tek, Tissue-Clear, SAKURA, #1466, 3 × 5 minutes) and rehydrated with decreasing alcohol series (2 × 100%, 2 × 96%, 1 × 70%, and 1 × 50% for 3 minutes each). This was followed by heat-induced antigen retrieval at high pH, a 30-minute blocking with 0.1 mol/L Tris-HCl, 0.15 mol/L NaCl, and 0.5% TSA-blocking buffer (FP1012, AKOYA BIOSCIENCES) at room temperature, and primary antibody incubation overnight in the same buffer. The primary antibodies used were PROX1 (1:200; R&D Systems, #AF2727, RRID: AB_2170716), Ki67 (1:200; Abcam, #ab16667, RRID: AB_302459), γH2AX (1:200; Abcam, #ab22551, RRID: AB_447150), and β-catenin (1:200; anti-mouse; Abcam, #AB6302, RRID: AB_305407). After TNT washes, the sections were incubated in a TNB–secondary antibody mix for 60 minutes at room temperature, followed by TNT washes, MQ water washes, and labeling of the nuclei with DAPI.

## Immunofluorescence of cultured cells
Cells were fixed with 4% PFA (Sigma, #PG148) for 10 minutes at room temperature, followed by three 5-minute washes in PBS. After 10 minutes of blocking at room temperature in 1% BSA (Biowest, #P6154) and 0.1% Triton-X (Thermo Fisher Scientific, #BP151-500) diluted in PBS, the following primary antibodies were added and incubated at room temperature for 60 minutes (diluted in blocking buffer): PROX1 (1:200; R&D Systems, #AF2727, RRID: AB_2170716), Ki67 (1:200; Abcam, #ab16667, RRID: AB_302459), and γH2AX (1:200; Abcam, #ab22551, RRID: AB_447150). This was followed by three 5-minute washes in PBS and incubation with a secondary antibody (Invitrogen, Alexa Fluor, 1:500) at room temperature for 30 minutes. After three 5-minute washes in PBS, coverslips were quickly dipped in MQ water and DAPI was added for nuclei labeling.

## Imaging
Immunofluorescence imaging was performed using an epifluorescence microscope (Zeiss Axio Imager, RRID: SCR_018876) equipped with a Hamamatsu Orca Flash 4.0 LT camera. The original images were processed and analyzed using Fiji software (RRID: SCR_002285). Analysis and quantification were performed in a blinded manner.

## Colony formation assay
The colorectal cancer cells were seeded into six-well plates or 10-cm dishes as indicated for each experimental setup. Once cell colonies had formed, and the cells were fixed with 4% PFA at room temperature for 10 to 15 minutes, followed by washing with 3× PBS. The cells were then incubated in 0.5% crystal violet (Sigma, #C0775-25G) diluted in H2O for 10 minutes at room temperature. After 3 to 5× PBS washes, colonies were imaged either with GelCount (Oxford Optronix) or a Canon digital camera. Analysis of colony number was performed either directly with GelCount or using Fiji software on digital images.

## Production and use of lentiviral vectors
The 293(F)T cells were grown to 70% confluence in high-glucose DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS, 1% glutamine, and penicillin/streptomycin. The following day, the growth medium was replaced with complexes of jetPEI (Polyplus, #101000020) and DNA, according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The growth medium, including the lentiviruses, was harvested 48 hours thereafter and centrifuged at 4°C, the supernatant was removed, and the pellet was diluted in 200 μL of high-glucose DMEM and stored at −80°C. The plasmids used are listed in Supplementary Table S1.
Cells transduced with lentiviruses were plated on a 6-cm dish and allowed to grow overnight, after which, the growth medium was replenished and 200 μL of concentrated lentivirus was added together with polybrene 8 μg/mL (Sigma, #H-9268). After a 24- to 48-hour incubation, the growth medium was replenished with a fresh medium. The following day, puromycin selection (10 ng/mL, Sigma, #P-8833) was started if the vector used included the puromycin resistance gene. After a 2-day incubation in puromycin-containing growth medium, the medium was replenished with fresh growth medium. PROX1 silencing and overexpression were confirmed by qPCR± immunofluorescence staining of PROX1. To account for potential off-target effects, at least two independently designed hairpins from PROX1 were used in the experiments.

## Isolation and growth of intestinal organoids
To isolate crypts from ApcMin/+ and ApcMin/+;Prox1fl/fl;Villin(Tg1000)Cre (VApcMin/+ P∆/∆) mice, freshly collected small intestines were washed with ice-cold PBS. This was followed by incubation of the intestines in 10 mmol/L EDTA (Sigma, #E5134) at pH 8.0 for 10 minutes. EDTA was removed by washing twice with PBS. The epithelium was scraped off from the intestine using a glass coverslip. After centrifugation at 1,500 RPM for 5 minutes, the supernatant was discarded, and the samples were incubated in digestion buffer containing 1 mg/mL collagenase I, 1 mg/mL collagenase IV, 4 mg/mL dispase II, and 1,000 U/mL benzonase for 40 minutes at 32°C on a gentle shaking plate. Samples were then washed with high-glucose DMEM and filtered once through a 100-μm filter and twice through 40-μm filters. After centrifugation at 1,500 RPM for 5 minutes, the supernatant was removed and the pellet was mixed with Matrigel (Corning, #356231). The Matrigel + crypt mixture (50 μL) was allowed to polymerize on a 24-well plate. Next, 500 μL of fresh growth medium (supplemented with B27, N2, L-glutamine, HEPES, and penicillin–streptomycin) was added to each well. To remove nontumor cells, organoids were passaged two to three times before performing the experiments. The organoids were passaged similarly to the patient-derived organoids. Deletion of Prox1 was confirmed by qPCR of RNA samples isolated from organoids. The organoids were imaged using an EVOS FL inverted epifluorescence microscope (Thermo Fisher Scientific).

## Cell-cycle analysis
To analyze the cell cycle, SW1222 PROX1high and PROX1low cells and LS174T and SW1222 PROX1high cells transduced with shPROX1 or shSCR lentivirus were fixed with 70% ethanol at −20°C at least overnight. After fixation, the cells were washed with PBS supplemented with 2% FBS. This was followed by incubation in 100 μL RNase A (Macherey-Nagel, #740505) at a concentration of 1 mg/mL at 37°C for 30 minutes and staining with propidium iodide (PI; Sigma, #P4170). After a 30-minute incubation with PI, the cells were analyzed with BD Accuri C6 Plus Analyzer with flow under 500 events/second.

## Statistical analysis
The number of technical and biological replicates is indicated in the figure legends. Data are presented as mean ± SEM. GraphPad Prism (version 9, RRID: SCR_002798) was used for statistical analyses. The Mann–Whitney test or unpaired t test was used to test for statistical significance. If not indicated elsewhere, statistical significance is indicated by P values (*, <0.05; **, <0.005; ***, <0.0005; ****, <0.0001) and is presented in the figures.

## Results

## Cells expressing Prox1 and intestinal stem cell markers populate ApcMin/+ tumors
To investigate the cellular composition of ApcMin/+ tumors versus healthy epithelium from the small intestine of WT C57 mice, we compared scRNA-seq data from the two groups. After unsupervised clustering of the cells and subsetting of the Epcam+ cells based on previously published markers, we identified the stem/progenitor, transit-amplifying (TA), Paneth, goblet, enteroendocrine (EEC), and tuft cells, and enterocytes in both datasets ( ; Supplementary Fig. S1A; ref. ). Only a few cells of the stem/progenitor 1, 4, and 6 and TA 1 clusters were present in the WT samples, but they were abundant in the ApcMin/+ tumor samples ( ). In agreement with our previous report, Prox1 expression, which is essential for the maintenance of the Lgr5+ stem cells in the intestinal adenomas of Apcfl/fl mice, was located mainly in the goblet, tuft, and EEC clusters in the WT intestine ( , , , ). In contrast, Prox1+ cells were highly abundant in tumor stem/progenitor clusters 1, 3, 4, and 6, and TA 1, tuft, and EEC clusters ( and ). Most of the Lgr5+ cells in the WT sample were observed in the proliferating stem/progenitor cell cluster, whereas in the tumor samples, the Lgr5+ cells were mainly located in the tumor stem/progenitor and TA clusters ( and ). Trajectory/pseudotime analysis using Monocle3 confirmed that proliferating stem/progenitor cells were the initiating cells in the WT sample (Supplementary Fig. S1B and S1C). Next, we used the proliferating stem/progenitor cell cluster in the WT sample to design healthy intestinal stem cell signatures (hISCS) 1 and 2 based on the average expression or the highest fraction ratio (pct.1/pct.2) of the top 30 transcripts (Supplementary Fig. S1D and S1E). Notably, hISCS 1 includes at least one classical intestinal stem cell marker (Olfm4), whereas three other markers (Ascl2, Lgr5, and Gkn3) were included in hISCS 2 (Supplementary Fig. S1D and S1E). Ribosomal transcripts (17/30) were deleted from hISCS 1 because of their higher expression in other clusters. As shown in Supplementary Fig. S1D and S1E, both hISCSs highlighted the proliferating stem/progenitor cluster in the WT samples, whereas in the ApcMin/+ tumor samples, hISCS 1 was greatly enriched in the proliferating stem/progenitor cluster, and the stem/progenitor and other TA clusters expressed abundant hISCSs 1 and 2 (Supplementary Fig. S1D and S1E). Taken together, these results indicate a significant expansion of Prox1 expressing cells, and concomitantly, ISCS+ cells, in ApcMin/+ tumors.
 

## 
Prox1 is expressed by slow-cycling tumor stem/progenitor cells
To characterize the ApcMin/+ tumor cell–specific transcriptome, we identified the stem/progenitor, TA, Paneth, goblet, and tuft cells, EECs, and enterocytes from the Epcam+ tumor cell subset ( ; Supplementary Fig. S2A–S1H). Cells expressing the intestinal stem cell markers Lgr5, Ascl2, Axin2, Sox9, Prom1, Tnfrsf19, Sox17, Cd44, and Clu were abundant in the stem/progenitor cell clusters. On average, 90%, 82%, 50%, 23%, and 50% of cells in stem/progenitor 1, 2, 3, 4, and proliferating stem/progenitor clusters, respectively, expressed Prox1, whereas Lgr5 was expressed in a significantly lower proportion of cells in these clusters ( and ; Supplementary Fig. S2I). Approximately two thirds of the Prox1+ cells in the stem/progenitor 1 cluster expressed Lgr5, whereas 97% of Lgr5+ tumor stem/progenitor 1 cells expressed Prox1. TA cluster 2, which contained on average 45% Lgr5+ cells, also showed the highest expression of the two hISCSs but almost completely lacked Prox1+ cells ( and ; Supplementary Fig. S2J and S2K). Notably, only a low percentage of stem/progenitor cells, including Prox1+ cells, expressed transcripts that were associated with cell proliferation ( ; Supplementary Fig. S2A and S2C). Bmi1, Hopx, and Lrig1 transcripts characteristic of reserve stem cell capacity were also enriched in the stem/progenitor 1 cluster (Supplementary Fig. S3A; refs. – ). These findings indicated that Prox1 expression is enriched in infrequently proliferating tumor cells that expressed stem/progenitor cell transcripts.
 RNA velocity and pseudotime analyses were performed to identify the tumor-initiating cells. The projection of computed velocities along the Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection revealed a major trend starting from the stem/progenitor clusters, proceeding to TA cells, and converging into either goblet, Paneth, or enterocyte clusters ( ). Additionally, a minor trend was observed, starting and ending within the stem/progenitor 1 cluster ( ). Interestingly, computing the macrostates and classifying them into initial and terminal states revealed that stem/progenitor 2 and 4 clusters are the initial states, whereas goblet 2 and enterocytes 2 and 9 are the terminal states. Notably, the stem/progenitor 1 cluster was classified as both an initial and a terminal state ( ). This finding was further elaborated through random walk presentations starting from each of the initial states ( ) and by examining the expression trends of top lineage-related genes along the pseudotime (Supplementary Fig. S3B–S3F). Furthermore, cells in the stem/progenitor 2 and 4 clusters seemed to give rise to stem/progenitor 1, 3, and 5, proliferating stem/progenitor, TA, Paneth, and goblet cells, plus enterocytes ( , , and ). These results suggested that stem/progenitor 1 cells, of which almost all expressed Prox1, can both regenerate stem/progenitor cells and give rise to differentiated cells ( and ). Furthermore, trajectory and pseudotime analyses suggested that stem/progenitor 1 cells are tumor-initiating cells ( and ). Taken together, these data indicated that the Prox1+ cells in ApcMin/+ tumors are slow-cycling stem/progenitor cells.
To address whether PROX1 expression is associated with colorectal cancer stem/progenitor cells, we reanalyzed published scRNA-seq data from eight well-differentiated colorectal cancer patient samples (GSE132465; ref. 23). PROX1 expression was highest in a small cluster (cluster number 11), including cells that expressed previously identified markers of colorectal cancer stem cells (LGR5, EPHB2, ALCAM, CD44, EPCAM, ASCL2, and SOX9) and, at low levels, proliferation markers (MKI67, OLFM4, CENPE, CENPF, MCM5, MCM6, and PCNA; Supplementary Fig. S4A–S4E; ref. ). These markers were also highly expressed in the ApcMin/+ stem/progenitor and TA clusters (Supplementary Fig. S4F). Taken together, these data suggest that PROX1 is a marker of slow-cycling stem/progenitor cells in adenomas and colorectal cancer.

## 
Prox1 versus Lgr5 lineage–traced cells show distinct tumor cell differentiation programs
To further analyze the differentiation capacity of Prox1+ tumor stem cells and to confirm that Prox1+ stem/progenitor cells function as multipotent stem cells, we performed scRNA-seq analysis of tumors isolated from tamoxifen-treated ApcMin/+;Prox1-CreERT2;Rosa26LSL-tdTomato mice. The expression of tdTomato in Prox1+ cells and their progeny was induced 3 or 7 days prior to scRNA-seq analysis ( ). Because of the previously established multipotential differentiation capacity of Lgr5+ stem cells, we also compared Prox1 and Lgr5 lineage–traced cells in ApcMin/+ tumors 7 days after tamoxifen administration ( – ). scRNA-seq analysis indicated that Prox1- and Lgr5-tdTomato lineage tracers (Prox1-LT and Lgr5-LT, respectively) were found in all tumor cell differentiation lineages, although the proportion of Prox1-LT cells was much smaller than the proportion of Lgr5-LT–positive cells in the tumor cell clusters showing intestinal epithelial differentiation markers, and both were low among Paneth cells, presumably reflecting the slow turnover of Paneth cells ( ; Supplementary Fig. S5A–S5D; Supplementary Table S2). In the day 7 sample, almost half of the Lgr5-LT tdTomato+ cells were enterocytes and less than 10% were stem/progenitor 1 cells ( ; Supplementary Table S2). In comparison, approximately one third of the Prox1-LT cells were enterocytes in day 3 samples and 19% in day 7 sample, and approximately one third of the Prox1-LT cells were stem/progenitor 1 cells in both day 3 and day 7 samples ( ; Supplementary Fig. S5B, Supplementary Table S2). The proportion of Lgr5-LT cells was higher than the proportion of Prox1-LT cells among the enterocytes, TA 1 and 2 cells, goblet, Paneth, and tuft cells, and EECs (Supplementary Table S2). In contrast, stem/progenitor 1, 2, 3, 4, and proliferating stem/progenitor cells had more Prox1-LT than Lgr5-LT cells in the day 7 sample (Supplementary Table S2). This suggests that most of the lineage-traced Lgr5+ cells differentiate into enterocytes, whereas most of the lineage-traced Prox1+ tumor stem/progenitor cells retain features of stem and progenitor cells, as also indicated by the RNA velocity analysis ( ).
 To confirm the expression of the tdTomato lineage tracer in cells displaying various differentiation markers, we stained tumor sections for TDTOMATO (red fluorescent protein) and various intestinal epithelial differentiation markers. Red fluorescent protein–positive Prox1-LT cells also stained for the Paneth cell marker LYSOZYME 1 (LYZ1), goblet cell marker MUCIN2 (MUC2), tuft cell marker DOUBLECORTIN-LIKE KINASE 1 (DCLK1), enterocyte marker INTESTINAL-TYPE ALKALINE PHOSPHATASE (ALPI), and EEC marker CHROMOGRANIN-A (CHRA), indicating their contribution to multiple differentiation lineages ( ). Moreover, the stained Lgr5-LT tumor sections showed TDTOMATO+/ALPI+ enterocytes, supporting the scRNA-seq data ( ). Thus, the Prox1+ tumor stem/progenitor cells have a multipotent differentiation capacity that differs from that of Lgr5+ tumor stem cells.

## Enrichment of PROX1+ tumor cells by radiotherapy
As tumor stem cells are known to be relatively resistant to cancer treatment, we tested the effect of irradiation on PROX1+ versus PROX− tumor cells. We irradiated patient-derived colorectal cancer 3D organoids and subjected them to scRNA-seq analysis 1 day after irradiation ( ; Supplementary Fig. S6A). We found that the percentage of PROX1+ cells was significantly higher in irradiated organoids than in control organoids (control, 15%; IR, 37%; ). The median PROX1 RNA level per cell was also increased after radiation ( ). Furthermore, in three of four patient-derived organoids, PROX1 RNA expression was 2- to 10-fold higher in irradiated than nonirradiated organoids ( ; Supplementary Fig. S6B). LGR5 transcripts in scRNA-seq analysis of both control and irradiated organoids from patient 1 were low (control, 0.4%; IR, 2%; Supplementary Fig. S6C). PROX1 transcript levels were also increased by irradiation in two-dimensional (2D) cultures of SW1222, LS174T, and HT29 colorectal cancer cells ( ; Supplementary Fig. S6D and S6E). Immunofluorescence analysis confirmed that the percentage of PROX1+ cells increased following the irradiation of SW1222 and HT29 colorectal cancer cells ( ; Supplementary Fig. S6F and S6G). However, irradiation did not induce PROX1 expression in 2D cultured PROX1– LoVo and HCT116 colorectal cancer cells or PROX1– colorectal cancer patient-derived 3D organoids (Supplementary Fig. S6H–S6K).
 To evaluate whether PROX1+ cells have a growth/survival advantage after radiation in vivo, we injected LS174T cells subcutaneously into NOD/SCID gamma mice and irradiated tumor-bearing mice with 2 Gy or 2 Gy × 3. Two days after the last irradiation, the mice were euthanized and the tumors were harvested for analysis ( and ). The percentage of PROX1+ tumor cells increased from 11% to 20% with 2 Gy and 35% with 2 Gy × 3 irradiation ( and ). In summary, these results indicate that radiation does not induce PROX1 expression in PROX1– colorectal cancer cells and that the proportion of PROX1+ tumor cells is increased in cells that survive radiation in vitro and in vivo.

## PROX1 expression boosts colorectal cancer colony formation after radiation
To study the ability of PROX1+ tumor cells to initiate tumor regrowth following radiation, we performed in vitro colony formation assays. PROX1high SW1222 and LS174T cells were transduced with either shPROX1 or shSCR lentivirus. The cells were then irradiated with 1 Gy and subjected to colony formation assay for 7 days. We found that both PROX1 silencing and irradiation decreased the number of colonies formed (Supplementary Fig. S7A–S7H). Furthermore, when PROX1 was overexpressed by transfection in 10% to 30% of HCT116 colorectal cancer cells, which were originally PROX1–, the irradiated PROX1-overexpressing cells formed more colonies than the irradiated control cells (Supplementary Fig. S8A–S8G).
To evaluate whether Prox1 deletion enhanced the effect of radiation in vivo, we irradiated ApcMin/+ mice and their Prox1-deleted counterparts (ApcMin/+;Prox1fl/fl;Villin-Cre), in which Prox1 was constitutively deleted, and euthanized the mice 7 days after the last irradiation (Supplementary Fig. S9A). We found that irradiated Prox1-deleted mice had fewer intestinal tumors than the irradiated control mice without Prox1 deletion (Supplementary Fig. S9B–S9E). Conditional deletion of Prox1 starting on the first irradiation day did not induce a strong decrease in the tumor number 3 days after the last irradiation of ApcMin/+;Prox1fl/fl;Villin-CreERT2 mice (Supplementary Fig. S9F and S9G). We also isolated intestinal organoids from ApcMin/+ mice and their constitutively Prox1-deleted counterparts and irradiated them with a single 2 to 10 Gy dose ( ; Supplementary Fig. S9H). Quantification of the nontransparent organoids on the following day indicated that Prox1 deletion decreased the survival of the organoids in a dose-dependent manner following irradiation ( ). Taken together, these data indicate that PROX1 expression supports colony formation by colorectal cancer cells and tumor cell survival in colorectal cancer organoid cultures, as well as the growth of both nonirradiated and irradiated tumor cells in vivo.
 

## Role of cell cycle in the radiation resistance of PROX1+ tumor cells
Shiokawa and colleagues ( ) have shown that PROX1+ colorectal cancer cells have stem cell–like properties and are resistant to chemotherapy because their slow-cycling state is maintained by elevated expression of the CDKN1C cell-cycle inhibitor (p57). However, the precise mechanisms underlying resistance to therapy remain unknown. Analysis of the cell cycle and proliferation markers in our ApcMin/+ scRNA-seq data confirmed that Cdkn1c transcripts were significantly higher in Prox1+ than Prox1− cells, thus confirming the results of Shiokawa and colleagues (Supplementary Fig. S10A; ref. ). However, a greater proportion of Prox1+ than Prox1− adenoma cells expressed Mki67 (Supplementary Fig. S10A). Although cultured PROX1+ SW1222 cells proliferated more slowly than their PROX1− counterparts ( ), immunofluorescence staining of PROX1 and Ki67 in cultured LS174T and SW480 colorectal cancer cells did not indicate PROX1-related differences in proliferation (Supplementary Fig. S10B).
To analyze the effect of PROX1 on the distribution of colorectal cancer cells in the cell cycle, LS174T, PROX1high and PROX1low SW1222 cells were stained with PI with and without PROX1 antibody, followed by flow cytometric analysis. The results showed no PROX1-related differences in the proportion of tumor cells in the S (most radiation-resistant), G2/M (most radiation-sensitive), or G0/G1 cell-cycle phases (Supplementary Fig. S10C and S10D; ref. ). Lentiviral silencing of PROX1 increased apoptosis in LS174T cells but did not increase the proportion of cells in the radiation-sensitive G2/M-phase in LS174T or PROX1high SW1222 cells (Supplementary Fig. S10E–S10G). scRNA-seq analysis of cell proliferation markers in nonirradiated colorectal cancer organoids from patient 1 indicated that MKI67, TOP2A, HMMR, and CENPF transcripts were more abundant in PROX1− than in PROX1+ tumor cells (Supplementary Fig. S10H–S10O). Furthermore, these transcripts were increased in PROX1+ cells after irradiation, suggesting increased entry into the cell cycle, whereas MKI67 expression decreased in PROX1− cells following irradiation (Supplementary Fig. S10H–S10O). These results suggest that a slow proliferation rate can partially explain the increased radiation resistance of PROX1+ cells in patient-derived organoids and ApcMin/+ tumors but not in the tested colorectal cancer cell lines.

## Transcripts involved in DNA damage repair are enriched in Prox1+ tumor cells
As tumor stem cells have been suggested to have enhanced DNA damage repair (DDR) capacity ( ), we analyzed the expression of transcripts related to NHEJ, homologous recombination, and base excision repair (BER) in scRNA-seq data from the ApcMin/+ mice. We found that several DDR transcripts were more frequently expressed in Prox1+ than in Prox1− tumor cells ( and ; Supplementary Fig. S11A). Similar results were obtained from the reanalysis of a previously published scRNA-seq dataset from 15 colorectal cancer patient samples (GSE200997), in which a greater proportion of PROX1+ than PROX1− tumor cells expressed NHEJ- and BER-related transcripts ( and ; Supplementary Fig. S11B). In support of these data, mass spectrometry identified several DDR proteins interacting with PROX1 in SW620 and R18 colorectal cancer cells, including DNA ligase 3 and the DNA repair protein XRCC1, both of which play a role in BER (Supplementary Table S2). Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes pathway analysis of interacting proteins showed that the mismatch repair and BER pathways were enriched in PROX1+ cells (FDR 1.5e−01 and 1.7e−01, respectively). Analysis of SW620 mass spectrometry data showed that PARP1, which is also a component of BER, was among the top 10 proteins interacting with PROX1+ (Supplementary Table S2). However, PROX1 silencing decreased only NHEJ1, but not the other tested DDR transcripts in LS174T cells, suggesting that PROX1 may not directly control these genes (Supplementary Fig. S11C). In addition, we analyzed γH2AX immunostaining, which reflects the amount of unrepaired DNA damage ( ), 1 day after 2 Gy radiation in LS174T xenografts. We found a trend of less γH2AX+ positivity in PROX1+ versus PROX1− cells (39% and 48%, respectively) and a statistically significant difference in the number of nuclear γH2AX foci (2.9 vs. 4.1 foci/γH2AX+ cells; Supplementary Fig. S11D–S11F). We also found that PROX1 silencing in LS174T cells decreased the expression of ID3, whereas PROX1 overexpression in HCT116 cells increased ID1 and ID3 expression ( ). This observation is of interest as ID1 and ID3 are known to maintain colorectal cancer–initiating cell self-renewal by promoting p21 expression, which retards the cell cycle and prevents the accumulation of DNA damage ( ). In summary, our results indicate that DDR was enhanced in PROX1+ cells.
As the NHEJ DDR pathway protein LIG4 mediates Wnt signaling–induced radioresistance in colorectal cancer cells ( ), we tested the small-molecule inhibitor SCR7 pyrazine that inhibits LIG4 in LS174T colorectal cancer cells and xenografts. We found that SCR7 pyrazine monotherapy did not affect the proportion of PROX1+ cells in vitro or in vivo ( and ). However, when combined with radiation, LIG4 inhibition decreased the percentage of PROX1+ cells in the SCR7 pyrazine–treated samples ( and ). Taken together, our results indicate that PROX1 expression in colorectal cancer cells is associated with enhanced DDR transcript expression and that the increase in PROX1+ cells following irradiation can be decreased by inhibiting LIG4.

## 
Prox1
+ tumor stem/progenitor cells in ApcMin/+ tumors are enriched after radiation
To study how radiation affects the transcriptomes of ApcMin/+ tumor cells, we irradiated ApcMin/+ mice with 2 Gy and performed scRNA-seq analysis of the Epcam+ tumor cell fraction at 1, 2, and 7 days after irradiation ( ; Supplementary Fig. S12A). Following irradiation, we found a trend of decreased proportion of Lgr5+ cells in the tumor-initiating stem/progenitor 1 cluster and a decrease in Lgr5 transcripts in stem/progenitor 1 and 2 cells 1 day after irradiation ( ; Supplementary Fig. S12B). In contrast, the proportion of Prox1+ cells in stem/progenitor 1, 2, and 4 clusters did not decrease ( , , and ). Prox1 expression levels were increased 1 day after irradiation in the stem/progenitor 1 and 2 cells, indicating that the Prox1+ cells have a growth/survival advantage following irradiation ( and ).
 To analyze the effect of irradiation on cell-cycle control, we analyzed the expression of Mki67, Cdkn1a, and Cdkn1c. Mki67 expression decreased 1 day after irradiation in stem/progenitor 2 and 4 cells (Supplementary Fig. S12C), and Ki67 staining showed a decreased proportion of proliferating cells in ApcMin/+ tumor sections 1 and 2 days after radiation (Supplementary Fig. S12D). Although CDKN1C was considered to maintain PROX1+ cells in a slow-cycling state ( ), scRNA-seq data did not show increased Cdkn1c expression after radiation in stem/progenitor 1, 2, or 4 cells ( ; Supplementary Fig. S12E), but Cdkn1a expression was strongly increased in all tumor-initiating stem/progenitor cell clusters ( ; Supplementary Fig. S12F). These results suggest that radiation induced cell-cycle arrest in the tumor stem/progenitor cells by increasing Cdkn1a (p21) expression.

## 
Prox1
+ cells contribute to multiple ApcMin/+ tumor cell lineages also after irradiation
As therapy-resistant tumor cells have been implicated in tumor relapse, we analyzed the capacity of Prox1+ cells to give rise to progeny following irradiation. In agreement with the trajectory and lineage-tracing analysis of the ApcMin/+ tumors, trajectory analysis of the irradiated tumors indicated that cells in the Prox1+ stem/progenitor 1 cluster can still function as the cells of origin following irradiation ( ). We also induced lineage tracing in ApcMin/+;Prox1-CreERT2;Rosa26-LSL-tdTomato mice with one dose of tamoxifen, irradiated them the following day with 2 Gy, euthanized the mice 2 or 7 days thereafter, and subjected the tumor cells to scRNA-seq ( ). After subsetting Epcam+ cells, we compared tdTomato expression between irradiated and nonirradiated tumor cells ( ). Based on our analysis, the distribution of tdTomato+ cells among the different cell types was similar in irradiated and nonirradiated samples ( ). Importantly, we found Prox1−/tdTomato+ cells among all cell types, except in stem/progenitor 4 and tuft cells ( ). Taken together, these results indicate that Prox1+ tumor stem/progenitor cells possess a multipotent differentiation capacity also following irradiation.

## Discussion
Our results highlight the role of Prox1+ tumor cells as stem/progenitor cells in intestinal tumors, as they are endowed with critical capacities that allow both self-renewal and production of differentiated progeny. The Prox1 stem/progenitor cells could contribute to all differentiated tumor cell lineages in ApcMin/+ tumors, and their percentage was increased in the irradiated tumors, in which they continued to function as multilineage tumor stem cells. According to our lineage tracing analysis, the Lgr5+ cells also contributed to all different tumor cell lineages, particularly to enterocytes, but less to stem/progenitor cells than the Prox1+ cells. Furthermore, by using irradiation of tumor organoids derived from patients with colorectal cancer and colorectal cancer cell line cells, we demonstrate enrichment of PROX1-expressing cells by irradiation, which suggests that PROX1 confers tumor cell protection from radiation-induced damage. Transcripts encoding components of the DDR pathway were more highly expressed in PROX1+ than in PROX1− cells in tumors from both patients with colorectal cancer and ApcMin/+ mice. Consistent with this, we detected DDR proteins in PROX1 immunoprecipitates from the colorectal cancer cell lines. Moreover, PROX1+ cells showed less DNA damage than PROX1− cells following irradiation, and inhibition of the LIG4 component of the NHEJ pathway led to reduced survival of irradiated PROX1+ colorectal cancer tumor cells in vitro and in vivo.
Previous studies have shown that Prox1 is a critical regulator of the fate of several types of stem and progenitor cells during embryonic development ( ). Although PROX1 is not expressed in healthy intestinal stem cells in adults, PROX1-expressing cells are induced within a few days after deletion of the Apc gene, and they are increased in adenomas of ApcMin/+ mice ( ). Our current scRNA-seq analysis here reveals that the vast majority of Prox1+ cells in ApcMin/+ tumors function as stem/progenitor cells that can self-renew and produce differentiated tumor cells. Furthermore, Prox1+ cells were more frequent than Lgr5+ cells among the tumor stem/progenitor cells. One week after activation of Prox1 lineage tracing, we found a tdTomato lineage tracer in both nonirradiated and irradiated ApcMin/+ tumor cells that featured various intestinal cell lineage markers. The majority of Lgr5+ progeny cells were enterocytes, whereas Prox1+ progeny consisted mainly of stem/progenitor cells, suggesting that Prox1+ and Lgr5+ tumor cells undergo distinct pathways of cell differentiation. Moreover, Prox1 expression persisted in tumor-initiating stem/progenitor 1, 2, and 4 cells after irradiation, suggesting that Prox1 can increase resistance to radiotherapy. In agreement with the results of Shiokawa and colleagues ( ), we showed that Prox1+ stem/progenitor tumor cells both in ApcMin/+ tumors and in tissue samples from patients with colorectal cancer are more slow-cycling than Prox1− cells, a feature that is associated with increased resistance to cancer therapies ( ).
We also showed that PROX1+ cells are enriched following irradiation of 2D/3D colorectal cancer cultures and colorectal cancer xenografts in mice. However, irradiation did not induce PROX1 expression in PROX1– 2D and 3D cultures of colorectal cancer cells. Silencing PROX1 inhibited and overexpression of PROX1 enhanced the colony formation capacity of irradiated colorectal cancer cells, suggesting that PROX1 is critical for clonogenic activity following irradiation. The antitumor effect of Prox1 deletion was evident also on irradiated cells, as evidenced by decreased tumor numbers and decreased organoid survival. Taken together, these results indicate that PROX1 expression is critical in tumor stem cells following irradiation.
DDR following radiation requires inhibition of the cell cycle. Shiokawa and colleagues ( ) have shown a PROX1+ population of slow-cycling stem cells in ApcMin/+ tumors and colorectal cancer organoids. This population expressed the CDKN1C/p57 cell-cycle inhibitor and was responsible for the resistance of PROX1-expressing cells to cytostatic agents ( ). However, after irradiation, instead of Cdkn1c, we found that tumor stem/progenitor cells had elevated expression of the Cdkn1a/p21 cell-cycle inhibitor, which is known to inhibit tumor stem cell proliferation ( ). Moreover, the expression of MKI67 was lower in PROX1+ than in PROX1− cells in colorectal cancer patient samples, suggesting that at least part of the irradiation resistance of PROX1+ cells could be because of their slow-cycling state. However, in 2D cultures of colorectal cancer cells, PROX1 expression did not alter the percentage of cells in the radiation-resistant versus radiation-sensitive cell-cycle phases. These results suggest that differences in the cell cycle are not a major determinant of radiation resistance in established colorectal cancer cell lines, but they may in part explain the increased radiation resistance of PROX1+ cells in colorectal cancer patient samples.
Previous studies have shown that the DNA ligase LIG4, which joins single-strand DNA breaks, mediates Wnt signaling–induced radioresistance in colorectal cancer cells ( ). In our study, LIG4 inhibition in LS174T cells and xenografts decreased the survival of PROX1+ cells following irradiation, both in vitro and in vivo. This suggests that LIG4 is involved in DDR in colorectal cancer. Interestingly, a recent study indicated that PROX1 interacts with the XRCC6 and PRKDC components of NHEJ repair in breast cancer cells, highlighting the importance of NHEJ in PROX1+ cells ( ). Although several preclinical trials have provided promising results from studies combining DDR inhibitors and radiation, none of these therapies have been approved for the treatment of patients with colorectal cancer ( ). Besides the reported off-target effects of such inhibitors, another concern is drug resistance, which is driven by the abundance of proteins involved in DNA repair in tumor cells ( ). However, future studies should address combination treatments, such as radiation plus PARP1 and NHEJ inhibitors, that could optimally target the most radiation-resistant tumor stem/progenitor cells.
In summary, we show that PROX1+ cells possess stem/progenitor cell properties in intestinal adenomas and colorectal cancer and that they express high levels of DDR transcripts. Furthermore, PROX1 interacts directly with proteins involved in DDR, and chemical inhibition of LIG4 in the NHEJ DDR pathway decreases the survival of PROX1+ colorectal cancer cells following irradiation. These results indicate that PROX1 contributes to multilineage tumor cell differentiation and therapy resistance, raising interest in the analysis of PROX1 downstream effectors as possible targets for cancer treatment.
