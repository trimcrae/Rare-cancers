## Background
Brain metastases (BM) remain the leading cause of death in patients with lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) despite the availability of comprehensive therapeutics. This study aimed to systematically identify crucial genes associated with BM in patients with LUAD, and develop a heuristic search algorithm combining information filtering and multiple stepwise regression to construct a multi-layered transcription factor regulatory network.

## Methods
We used the mRNA expression profiles of primary tumours derived from LUAD patients with different organ metastasis and adjacent normal tissue samples to identify differentially expressed genes specific to BM, and validated it in bulk, spatial and single-cell RNA-sequencing data. Furthermore, we collected 110 stage I–III LUAD specimens to validate the correlation of high PSMD12 (proteasome 26 S subunit, non-ATPase 12) protein expression with BM incidence and patient prognosis. We also developed an in vitro blood–brain barrier (BBB) model to investigate the disruption of PSMD12 on the barrier. Mechanistically, we developed an algorithm to construct an upstream regulator network of PSMD12 overexpression.

## Results
Through integrated bioinformatics analyses, we identified PSMD12 as a critical gene associated with BM. The associations of PSMD12 with BM and poor survival were validated in multiple public datasets comprising 706 patients. Immunohistochemical results confirmed that patients exhibiting high PSMD12 protein expression had a significantly higher incidence of BM within 5 years and shorter progression-free survival. Functional experiments revealed that PSMD12 overexpression in A549 cells disrupted BBB integrity and enhanced tumor cell invasiveness, whereas PSMD12 knockdown attenuated these effects. Furthermore, upstream regulator network and knockdown experiments indicated that downregulated EOMES expression would contribute to PSMD12 overexpression.

## Conclusions
PSMD12 emerges as a crucial gene in promoting BM, potentially serving as an early warning indicator of BM in early-stage LUAD. The upstream regulatory network of PSMD12 overexpression provides potential mechanisms for BM in LUAD patients.

## Background
Lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) is the most common type of non-small cell lung cancer, accounting for 80–85% cases of lung cancer [ ]. The brain is the most likely target for distant metastases in LUAD, and approximately 10–40% of patients develop brain metastases (BM) during cancer progression [ ]. Although targeted therapies and immunotherapy have shown clinical benefits, the median 5-year survival rate of patients with BM remains less than 5% [ ]. Currently, cerebrospinal fluid analysis in combination with varied imaging (CT, MRI, and PET-CT) has been noted as the gold standard in the diagnosis of leptomeningeal metastasis, while lack the ability of early warning for BM, which is essential to improve the prognosis of LUAD [ ]. Therefore, the molecular and biological mechanisms by which lung cancer metastasizes to the brain should be elucidated, providing an early warning for BM in advancing clinical goals [ ]. 
Considerable efforts have been devoted to exploring the molecular mechanisms underlying BM. For example, Münsterberg et al. found that elevated ALCAM expression promotes BM in non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) via increased tumour cell dissemination and interaction with brain endothelial cells using cell line and mice experiments [ ]. The advent of transcriptomic technologies has provided a platform for the comprehensive analysis of lung cancer BM [ ]. Many studies have analysed the mechanisms underlying BM using transcriptomic data to identify molecular targets. For example, Kikuchi et al. compared the expression data of 16 brain metastatic loci with 22 primary LUAD samples and found that the genes encoding plasma membrane proteins, cell antigens, and cytoskeletal proteins play crucial roles in enhancing the ability of BM [ ]. In another study, Kamer et al. identified a set of 22 genes which correlated specifically with BM but not with metastasis to other sites by analysing RNA-sequencing data from NSCLC patients with BM and control groups [ ]. However, these studies majorly focused on samples derived from the brain metastatic tissues of patients, and whether these molecular abnormalities could be observed in their primary lung tissues is unclear. Therefore, understanding the molecular abnormalities at the primary site of LUAD patients with BM would contribute to deciphering early warnings of metastasis.
BM is a complex and dynamic pathological process which involves interactions between multiple factors [ ]. Some studies have employed multiple feature selection methods to identify disease-related factors [ – ]. However, they have largely overlooked the regulatory interactions between genes. Investigating the gene regulatory network, a collection of molecular regulators that interact with each other to govern gene expression and function, would facilitate gaining insights into the transcriptional regulation patterns in patients with BM [ ]. Transcription factors (TFs) are important for the regulation of gene expression and form a complex regulatory network together with target genes for oncogenesis [ ]. Several algorithms have been developed and tailored to build multi-layered hierarchical gene regulatory networks (ML-hGRNs) to decipher complex diseases [ ]. For example, the backward elimination random forest algorithm was used to construct an ML-hGRN by determining the TF for the regulatory layer above the pathway layer with a Gaussian mixture model [ ]. However, as the universal co-expression pattern among genes involved in multiple biological processes in cancer samples [ ], some TFs which do not directly regulate the expression of their target genes may be incorrectly identified in a given biological context. Therefore, we hypothesised that the TFs directly responsible for dysregulating the expression of their target genes should act independent of other TFs.
In this study, we aimed to identify crucial genes associated with BM in LUAD patients based on the transcriptomic data of the primary tissues of patients with BM and others, and validated them in multiple public datasets, clinical samples and in vitro blood–brain barrier (BBB) experiment. We also proposed a heuristic search algorithm combining multiple stepwise regression and information filtering (HMSRF) to iteratively construct the upstream TF regulator network of the crucial gene. Our study sheds light on the crucial gene associated with BM and reveals its upstream regulatory relationships, providing an early warning indicator and molecular mechanisms for BM in early-stage LUAD.

## Materials and methods

## Public data sources and pre-processing
Six public LUAD transcriptomic datasets were obtained from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO, http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/), based on the inclusion criteria that comprising specific metastases in brain or the number of the treatment-naïve patients with stages I-III was more than 200. The clinical information is listed in Table  .

 

The GSE11969 dataset was used as discovery data to identify crucial genes involved in BM and construct their ML-hGRN, including five adjacent normal samples and the primary tissue samples of 43 non-metastatic patients (nonM), 39 patients with other metastatic (otherM), and 7 patients with BM. Three datasets, GSE13213, GSE200563, and GSE131907, were used to verify these genes. The GSE13213 dataset included primary tissue samples from 58 nonM, 49 otherM, and 9 BM patients. The spatial RNA-sequencing dataset (GSE200563) contained 29 primary lung cancer samples, 12 lymph nodes metastatic sites (mLN) samples, 26 BM samples, 15 primary lung cancer microenvironments, 27 BM microenvironment samples, and 6 healthy brain samples [ ]. From the single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) dataset (GSE131907), we extracted data of LUAD epithelial cells derived from the primary sites of 11 early-stage and 4 advanced-stage patients, lymph node metastatic sites of 7 mLN patients, brain sites of 10 BM patients, and normal epithelial cells from the distant lung tissues of 11 patients with LUAD [ ]. In the discovery dataset (GSE11969), the survival information of 53 treatment-naïve patients with stage I–III was also extracted and used for survival analysis. Two additional independent transcriptomic datasets (GSE68465 and GSE31210) of 239 and 204 treatment-naïve patients with stage I–III disease, respectively, were selected for survival validation.
For the microarray datasets generated by the Affymetrix platform, a robust multiarray averaging algorithm [ ] was used to pre-process the raw data. Originally processed data (series matrix files) were collected for other datasets generated using the Agilent and Illumina platforms. Probe IDs were mapped to Gene IDs using the corresponding platform files. For each sample, the expression measurements of all probes corresponding to the same Gene ID were averaged to obtain a single measurement. Probes which did not match any Gene ID or matched multiple Gene IDs were deleted. For the mRNA expression data derived from the Illumina HiSeq 2000 sequencing platform, normalized count values processed using the RNA-Seq by expectation-maximization method were extracted and log2-transformed into expression measurements.
Additionally, we downloaded the nonsynonymous mutation data (Illumina Genome Analyser DNA Sequencing GAIIx platform) of LUAD from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) database (https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/) to analyse the genomic alterations of ML-hGRN, comprising 106 stage I–III LUAD samples with paired mRNA profiles.

## Clinical samples
Tissue specimens were obtained from 110 patients with LUAD who were diagnosed at stages I–III and underwent surgery at Harbin Medical University Cancer Hospital (Heilongjiang, China) between 2005 and 2008. None of the patients had received any therapy before surgery. All experiments were performed in accordance with the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki, and all participants provided written informed consent. Clinical factors, including age, sex, smoking status, tumour stage, grade, primary tumour size, metastasis status, and disease-free survival (DFS), were recorded. Among these patients, 11 developed BM, 33 relapsed or developed other metastases (13 relapsed in situ, 1 abdomen, 10 lymph node, 2 liver, and 7 bone metastases), 39 did not progress, and 27 patients were censored within 5 years.

## Immunohistochemistry
Tissue sections were de-paraffinized in xylene and rehydrated in a series of graded alcohol solutions in accordance with standard procedures. Antigen retrieval was performed by placing the slides in citrate buffer (0.01 M, pH 6.0), heating for 10 min, and cooling naturally to enhance immunoreactivity. The slides were then immersed in hydrogen peroxide (3%) for 15 min at room temperature. Next, the specimens were incubated with anti-PSMD12 primary antibody (1:100, rabbit polyclonal; Wanleibio, China) at 4 °C overnight. After incubation with biotin-labelled secondary goat anti-rabbit IgG antibodies (1:200 Beyotime A0277, China) at room temperature for 30 min, the specimens were incubated in horseradish peroxidase-labelled Avidin at 37 °C for 30 min. The slides were stained with 3, 3’-diaminobenzidine and counterstained with haematoxylin. Finally, each section was dehydrated using a series of graded alcohol solutions and covered with a coverslip. The staining results were analysed by two independent experienced pathologists blinded to the clinicopathological data. Staining results were scored according to the sum of the intensity and the proportion of positively stained cells. Staining intensity was classified as follows: 0 (no staining), 1 (light yellow/weak staining), 2 (yellow-brown/moderate staining), or 3 (brown/strong staining). The proportion of positively stained tumour cells was scored as follows: 1 (positive cells, 1–25%), 2 (positive cells, 26–50%), 3 (positive cells, 51–75%), or 4 (positive cells, 75–100%). The samples were divided into a high-PSMD12 group (score 4–7) and a low-PSMD12 group (score 0–3) based on the median value of PSMD12 protein expression.

## Cell culture
Human LUAD cell line (A549), human brain capillary endothelial cell lines (hCMEC/D3), astrocytes, and pericytes were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, Manassas, VA, United States). All culture media were supplemented with 10% FBS (PAN, Biotech GmbH, Germany) and antibiotics. A549 cell lines were cultured in RPMI-1640 (1640, Gibco®, Grand Island, NY, USA) and HCMEC, astrocytes, pericytes cell lines were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s Medium (DMEM, Gibco®, Grand Island, NY, USA) at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2.

## Cell transfection
A549 cells were transfected with PSDM12 plasmid, siRNAs of PSMD12 or EOMES using Lipofectamine™ 2000 (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The siRNAs were obtained from Genecreate (Wuhan, China, see Table ).

## Western blot (WB)
The WB protocol utilized in this study has been described in detail in a previous study [ ]. Proteins were extracted using a Whole Protein Extraction Kit (Wanleibio, Shenyang, China, WLA019). The antibodies employed for the WB analysis are listed in Table , including anti-PSMD12 (Proteintech, 11412-1-AP), anti-EOMES (Proteintech, 28316-1-AP) and anti-β-actin (ZSGB-BIO, TA-09). Protein bands were developed using enhanced chemiluminescence (Wanleibio, China, WLA003), and quantified with ImageJ.

## Real-time quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR)
The PCR technique was performed following the methods. Total RNA was extracted using TRIpure (BioTeke, China, RP1001), cDNA was synthesized using the BeyoRT II M-MLV reverse transcriptase (Beyotime, Jiangsu, China, D7160L), and qRT-PCR was performed using a BIOER TC-96GH(b) PCR instrument (BIOER, Hangzhou, China), with SYBR Green Master (ROX) (Solarbio, Beijing, China, SY1020). The optical density values of the target bands were analysed using gel-pro analyser. Primer details are in Table . Fold-change (FC) in gene expression levels were calculated using the 2-ΔΔCt method.

## Transwell invasion assay
Transwell inserts (Corning, NY, USA, 3422) were coated with 100 µL of Matrigel (Corning, NY, USA) and placed in an incubator overnight at 37 °C. Afterward, 200 µL of a cell suspension was added into the upper chamber, whereas culture medium (700 µL) containing 10% FBS was added into the bottom chamber; the cells were incubated for 24–48 h at 37 °C. After fixing with 4% paraformaldehyde, 0.1% crystal violet (Amresco, USA, 0528) was used to stain the cells that migrated to the bottom chamber. Finally, the invading cells were visualized and counted using an inverted microscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan, IX53). Three visual fields were selected randomly for observation.

## Establishment of in vitro blood-brain barrier (BBB) models
Cell sources: To investigate the ability of tumour cells to cross the BBB, we constructed an in vitro BBB model using normal human pericytes, astrocytes cell and human cerebral microvascular endothelial cell (hCMEC/D3). Cells were obtained from the ATCC (Manassas, VA, United States).
Culture process: Pericytes (2 × 104 cells/cm2) and astrocytes (1 × 105 cells/cm2) were seeded on the bottom side of the Matrigel-coated polyester membranes in Transwell inserts (Corning Life Sciences, USA). These cells were left to adhere firmly overnight, following which hCMEC/D3 (2 × 105 cells/cm2) were seeded on the inside or upper side of the inserts placed in the well of the 12-well culture plates devoid of cells. Culture medium was added to both the inner and the outer chambers of the Transwell plate. The cultures were then maintained for an additional three days.
Functional assessments: liquid level difference is an indicator of the functionality of the BBB model. If the liquid level difference between the inner and outer chamber is smaller, the greater the damage of the blood-brain barrier. The barrier integrity of BBB was measured in units of transendothelial electrical resistance (TEER) using a1600R ECIS system at 24, 48, and 72 h (day 0). The readings were acquired continuously at 4,000 Hz and at 30 min intervals. The final resistance values (Ω) were obtained by subtracting the background resistance from the measured blocking resistance and then multiplying it by the effective surface area of the filter [ ]. High TEER values reflected tight barriers. After 72 h, the resistance reading is stable and the BBB model is successfully constructed.

## In vitro BBB transmigration assay
To study the ability of tumour cells to cross the BBB model, treated A549 cells were labeled with 5 µM of CellTracker Green 5-chloromethylfluorescein diacetate (CMFDA) (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) in serum-free medium for 45 min and were inoculated into the upper chamber of the inserts at a density of 10,000 cells in 200 µl of DMEM medium containing 0% FBS. The lower chamber was filled with 600 µl of DMEM medium containing 10% FBS. Following overnight incubation, the inserts were removed and the number of transmigrated cells that had migrated to the lower chamber was recorded by a positive fluorescence microscope (Motic China Group Co., Ltd., China). The invasive abilities of the treated A549 cells were determined by the number of cells crossing the BBB at the time points of 24 h and 48 h.

## Information filtering and multiple stepwise regression method to construct ML-hGRN
The regulation between TFs and target genes was downloaded from the ChEA database (https://maayanlab.cloud/Harmonizome/), which included 199 TFs and 21 220 target genes. We considered that TFs within coherent regulatory signals could reinforce the regulation of the target expression and that TFs are directly responsible for the observed changes in their target genes independent of other TFs (Figure ). Thus, we proposed a heuristic search algorithm combining multiple stepwise regression and information filtering, HMSRF, to iteratively construct the upstream ML-hGRN which governs an IG. The details of the HMSRF algorithm are as follows (Figure ).

## Step 1: identification of the candidate upstream TFs that affect expression of the target gene
Pearson correlation was performed to estimate the expression correlation of a target gene with all its upstream TFs, and the significantly associated TFs was selected. Initially, IG was first placed as the target gene.

## Step 2: filtration of the upstream TFs inconsistent in the signal flow
A positive correlation indicates the transcriptional activation of the TF to its target gene, while a negative correlation indicates the transcriptional inhibition to its target gene. Multiple stepwise regression was performed to select the directly upstream TF of the target gene, whose expressions remained significantly associated with the expression of the target gene.

## Step 4: construction of a complete upstream TF regulation network for the IG iteratively
Each TF remaining from Step 3 was taken as a target gene, and Steps 1–3 were used to search for the upstream TFs to the target iteratively until there were no eligible upstream TFs. During this process, the TFs that existed in the regulatory network were no longer used for subsequent iterations.

## Building a simulated ‘toys dataset’
A simulated ‘toys dataset’ was generated to validate the efficiency of the algorithm. This dataset simulated the expression values of one IG (y) and 1066 assumed genes [X1, X2,…, X1066]. In these 1066 genes, the first six [X1, X2,…, X6] were considered the true upstream TFs, which regulate the expression of the IG (y). The six TFs were divided into groups 1 [X1, X2, X3] and 2 [X4, X5, X6] with different signal intensities. The next 60 genes [X7, X8,…, X66] correlated with the expression levels of the six upstream TFs of the IG (y), and the other 1000 genes were noise. The settings were as follows:

Xi ~ N (1) for i = 1,2,3.
Xi ~ N (1, 3) for i = 4,5,6.
Xi ~ αj * x1 + N(0, 1) for i = 7–16; for j = 1–10, αj = 0.1*j.
Xi ~ αj * x2 + N(0, 1) for i = 17–26; for j = 1–10, αj = 0.1*j.
Xi ~ αj * x3 + N(0, 1) for i = 27–36; for j = 1–10, αj = 0.1*j.

## Statistics and reproducibility
All statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.2.2 (https://www.r-project.org/). Protein–protein interactions were downloaded from the BioGRID database (https://thebiogrid.org/). Pearson or Spearman correlation analysis was used to estimate the correlation of gene expression with other gene expression and gene mutation statuses.
OS and DFS were defined as the time from the date of initial surgical resection to the date of death, recurrence, or last contact (censored), respectively. Survival curves were fitted using the Kaplan–Meier method and compared using the log-rank test [ ]. HRs and 95% CIs were calculated using the Cox proportional hazards model [ ]. ROC curves were constructed and the AUC for each ROC curve was computed to discriminate patients with and without BM. A multivariate Cox proportional hazards regression model was used to assess the independent association with patient survival after adjusting for clinical parameters, including stage, age, smoking status, grade, and sex.
Pearson correlation was used to estimate the correlation of gene expression and several cancer-related molecular scores, including hypoxia [ ], stemness [ ], proliferation [ ] and immune [ ], which were calculated based on mRNA expression profiles (details in Supplementary Methods). The relative abundance level of infiltrating immune cell subpopulations was calculated using the online tool CIBERSORTx [ ] with 1000 permutations and the prohibited normalization setting (https://cibersortx.stanford.edu/). Functional enrichment analysis was performed by using a cumulative hypergeometric distribution model based on the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes. The GSVA package was used to quantify the pathway enrichment scores of samples [ ]. 
P-values were adjusted using the Benjamini–Hochberg approach to control FDR. Statistical significance was defined as two-sided P < 0.05 or FDR < 0.05 for multiple testing.

## Results

## PSMD12 was identified to be correlated with BM and prognosis of LUAD
In the discovery dataset (GSE11969), we firstly identified 3284 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between primary tissues of seven patients with BM and five normal samples (limma, false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.05; Fig.  a). Owing to the weak signal within tumours, we utilized a 1% P threshold to identify 173 and 48 DEGs of seven patients with BM when comparing with 43 nonM and 39 otherM patients, respectively (Fig.  b and c). The results showed that nine genes overlapped among the three DEG sets with the same dysregulated directions, including seven upregulated genes (COX7A2, MEA1, PSMD12, HS6ST2, RBAK, PEX6, and OGFRL1) and two downregulated genes (FOS and OXER1) in the BM group when compared with the other groups (Fig.  d). Secondly, we constructed a co-expression protein–protein interaction network linking the nine genes to other interacting genes (Pearson correlation, FDR < 0.05; Fig.  e). As a result, PSMD12 (proteasome 26 S subunit, non-ATPase 12) showed the highest degree in the network. In addition, we performed survival analyses for the nine genes in stage I–III patients who did not receive adjuvant therapy after surgery (treatment-naïve) and found that only the PSMD12 expression was associated with overall survival (OS) of patients. The Kaplan-Meier curves showed that patients with high-PSMD12 expression (> median) had significantly shorter OS than low-PSMD12 expression patients (log-rank P = 0.0023, Hazard ratio (HR) = 5.66, 95% confidence interval (CI) = 1.61–19.92; Fig.  f, Table S4). Herein, through the above filters (Figure ), we identified PSMD12 as a critical gene for further analysis. Specifically, the prognostic association of PSMD12 was also observed in patients with stage I disease (log-rank P = 0.0070, HR = 6.25, 95% CI = 1.36–28.60; Fig.  g). The receiver operator characteristic (ROC) curve demonstrated that PSMD12 had a good performance in predicting BM with area under the curve (AUC) of 0.902. Therefore, PSMD12 might be a crucial gene that associated with BM in LUAD patients, and its overexpression in the primary tissues of early-stage patients might indicate a high risk of BM.

 

Subsequently, biological analyses of PSMD12 showed that the mRNA expression of PSMD12 was significantly positively correlated with stemness and proliferation scores and negatively associated with the infiltration of six immune cells, such as activated B cells (Pearson correlation, FDR < 0.05; Fig.  a), demonstrating the molecular and immune characteristics of PSMD12 in facilitating cancer progression [ , ]. We further extracted 2519 genes significantly correlated with the mRNA expression of PSMD12 (Pearson correlation, |r| > 0.35, FDR < 0.05) and found that these genes were significantly enriched in five pathways related to cancer progression, such as ‘Proteasome’, ‘Neuroactive ligand–receptor interaction’, and ‘DNA replication’ pathways (hypergeometric distribution model, FDR < 0.05; Fig.  b). A previous study reported that several nicotinic acetylcholine receptors in the neuroactive ligand–receptor interaction pathway promote BM of lung cancer cells [ ]. Therefore, we additionally estimated the correlation of the ‘Proteasome’ involved with PSMD12 and the ‘Neuroactive ligand–receptor interaction’ pathway and found that the GSVA scores of the two pathways were significantly negatively correlated (Pearson correlation, r = -0.72, P < 0.05; Fig.  c). These results suggested that PSMD12 overexpression in the ‘Proteasome’ pathway might lead to the dysfunction of the ‘Neuroactive ligand–receptor interaction’ pathway through selective degradation of some genes and associate with metastasis of tumour cells to the brain.

 

## The correlations of PSMD12 with BM and poor prognosis were validated in public datasets
To evaluate the correlations between PSMD12 and BM, we collected three independent datasets for validation. In the bulk dataset (GSE13213), we found PSMD12 expression was significantly higher in the primary tissues of the BM patients than in those of the otherM (limma, logFC = 0.40, P = 0.0323; Fig.  a) and nonM patients (limma, logFC = 0.66, P = 9.53e-05; Fig.  a). In the spatial RNA-sequencing dataset (GSE200563), the expression of PSMD12 was the highest in the 26 samples derived from BM sites than other LUAD samples from 12 mLN and 29 nonM patients (limma, BM vs. nonM, logFC = 0.22, P = 0.0438; BM vs. mLN, logFC = 0.07, P = 0.6273; mLN vs. nonM, logFC = 0.15, P = 0.2694; Fig.  b). Additionally, the expression of PSMD12 was low in the samples from lung and BM microenvironments and healthy brain samples (Fig.  b).

 

In a scRNA-seq dataset (GSE131907), we first simulated the scRNA-seq data into pseudo-bulk data using the simulating bulk RNA-seq data (SimBu) method, and showed that PSMD12 expression was the highest in 10 samples derived from BM sites than 7 samples from mLN sites and the samples from primary site of 4 advanced-stage and 11 early-stage patients (limma, all logFC > 0, all P < 0.0001; Fig.  c). Furthermore, we also compared the expression level of PSMD12 in different cell types derived from different sites, and found that PSMD12 expressions were significantly higher in tumour epithelial cells derived from BM sites than that derived from other sites (limma, all logFC > 0, all P < 0.05; Fig.  d), while there was no obvious difference in the expression of PSMD12 at different sites in other cell types (Fig.  d). Additionally, PSMD12 expression showed superior performances in distinguishing BM and non-BM with AUC of 0.737, 0.765 and 0.724 in these three validation datasets, respectively (Fig.  e). These results indicated a comparative stability and superior discriminative capacity of PSMD12 for BM and non-BM.
The correlation between PSMD12 expression and patient prognosis was validated using two independent bulk datasets. Survival analyses showed that the stage I–III patients with high PSMD12 expression (> median) had significantly shorter OS than those with low PSMD12 expression (≤ median) in the GSE31210 (log-rank P = 0.0093, HR = 2.80, 95% CI = 1.25–6.30; Fig.  f) and GSE68465 (log-rank P = 0.0460, HR = 1.56, 95% CI = 1.00–2.42; Fig.  g) datasets. Specifically, the stage I patients with higher PSMD12 expression exhibited significantly shorter OS in the GSE31210 datasets (log-rank P < 0.0001, HR = inf, 95% CI = 0–inf; Fig.  h), while marginally shorter OS in the GSE68465 dataset (log-rank P = 0.1481, HR = 1.42, 95% CI = 0.88–2.28; Fig.  i). These results demonstrated that high PSMD12 expression was associated with BM, leading to a poor prognosis even for patients with early-stage LUAD.

## PSMD12 protein was associated with high incidence of BM in clinical samples
To further confirm the association of PSMD12 protein expression in primary site with incidence of BM and poor prognosis, we collected primary tissues of 110 stage I-III LUAD specimens from the clinic, and detected the PSMD12 protein expression levels using immunohistochemistry staining. In total, 43 and 67 specimens were separated into high- and low-PSMD12 groups, respectively, according to the median PSMD12 protein expression levels across samples. The representative immunohistochemistry staining of the PSMD12 protein in high- and low-PSMD12 samples are represented in Fig.  a. The protein level of PSMD12 was significantly higher in 11 samples that developed BM than in 33 samples that developed otherM or relapsed in situ (limma, logFC = 1.79, P = 0.0400; Fig.  b) and in 39 samples that did not progress within 5 years (limma, logFC = 2.18, P = 0.0048; Fig.  b). The results showed that 73% of the patients who developed BM within 5 years were classified into the high-PSMD12 group, and this percentage was significantly higher than that of the patients who developed otherM or relapsed in situ (48%) and that (31%) of the patients without disease progression (Fisher exact test, P = 0.0359; Fig.  c). ROC curve demonstrated that PSMD12 protein expression had a good performance in predicting BM with AUC of 0.727 (Fig.  d). Survival analysis also validated that the patients in the high-PSMD12 group had significantly shorter DFS than the low-PSMD12 patients (log-rank P = 0.0121, HR = 2.04, 95% CI = 1.16–3.60; Fig.  e). Multivariate Cox analysis showed that the PSMD12 protein expression remained significantly associated with the DFS of the patients with LUAD after adjusting for clinical parameters, including stage, age, smoking status, grade, and sex (P = 0.0246, HR = 1.98, 95% CI = 1.09–3.59; Fig.  f). Even for stage I, the patients in the high-PSMD12 group exhibited significantly shorter DFS than those in the low-PSMD12 group (log-rank P = 0.0464, HR = 2.89, 95% CI = 0.96–8.70; Fig.  g).

 

## PSMD12 enhances the ability of LUAD cells to cross the in vitro BBB model
To explore the impact of PSMD12 on invasion ability of LUAD cells, we overexpressed and knocked down PSMD12 in A549 cells by transfecting PSMD12 (PSMD12 group) and four siRNA-PSMD12 plasmids (si-PSMD12 groups), respectively. The sequences are represented in Table , and the transfection efficiency was validated by WB experiment (Fig.  a and b). We performed transwell invasion assays in these cells and found that PSMD12 group exhibited significantly increased invasive ability relative to corresponding negative control cells (NC cells), whereas si-PSMD12 groups exhibited significantly decreased invasive ability relative to siRNA-NC cells (si-NC cells) (Fig.  c and d).

 

To further investigate the role of PSMD12 on the ability of LUAD cells to cross BBB, we established an in vitro BBB model (see Method) and inoculated the transfected A549 cells alternatively into the upper chamber of the inserts in the model. We first measured the liquid level difference and TEER value to estimate the integrity of BBB, and found they were both significantly reduced in PSMD12 group, whereas significantly increased in four si-PSMD12 groups compared with corresponding control groups (T-test, P < 0.0001; Fig.  e and f). Whereafter, we estimated the number of tumour cells labelled by CMFDA crossing BBB, and found that the number of cells with high PSMD12 expression that passed through the BBB model was significantly higher than that of NC cells (Fig.  g). In contrast, the number of tumour cells that passed through the BBB model was significantly weakened when PSMD12 was knocked down (Fig.  h). These results indicate that high expression of PSMD12 in LUAD cells may compromise BBB integrity for enhancing their ability to pass cross the BBB.

## HMSRF algorithm was developed to reveal an upstream TF regulator network for PSMD12
In TCGA multi-omics dataset with 106 stage I–III LUAD samples, we did not observe genomic (mutation and copy number variation) or epigenomic alterations (DNA methylation and upstream miRNAs) of PSMD12. Here, we inferred that the dysregulations of upstream TFs might be one of the reasons for PSMD12 overexpression. Therefore, we developed a heuristic search algorithm combining multiple stepwise regression and information filtering (HMSRF) algorithm to iteratively construct an upstream multi-layered hierarchical gene regulatory network (ML-hGRN) which governs an interesting gene (IG) and elucidate the molecular mechanisms causing the overexpression of PSMD12 (see Method, Figure ). The method was first tested in a simulated ‘toys dataset’ consisted of 1066 assumed genes [X1, X2,…, X1066] and compared with the backward elimination random forest (BWERF) method [ ]. Here, [X1, X2, X3] and [X4, X5, X6] were considered true upstream TFs regulating the expression of the IG (y) with weak and strong signal intensities, respectively. The next 60 genes [X7, X8,…, X66] correlated with the expression levels of the six true TFs, and the other 1000 genes were noise.
As shown in Table  , both algorithms correctly identified strong regulatory TFs [X4, X5, X6] and weak regulatory TFs [X1, X3]. However, eight genes correlated with six true TFs were misidentified using BWERF, and the importance value of X46 for the IG, which is the related variable of X4 (a stronger regulatory TF), surpassed the importance value of X3 (a weaker regulatory TF). In addition, the computational time of HMSRF was 0.56 s (s), much shorter than that (668.29 s) of BWERF. Overall, the capacity and efficiency of HMSRF were superior to those of BWERF.

 

In view of the above, we applied HMSRF to construct an upstream gene regulatory network of PSMD12 in the discovery set (Table S5), which consisted of 42 genes, 47 edges, and 9 layers of upstream TFs of PSMD12 (Fig.  a). Aside from the ‘Cell cycle’ and ‘Cellular senescence’ pathways, these genes in the network were also enriched in the ‘TGF-beta signalling pathway’ (hypergeometric distribution model, FDR < 0.05; Fig.  b), which could promote the formation of BM from lung cancer [ ]. In this network, TP53, a critical tumour suppressor, functioned as a third-layer TF of PSMD12. Here, TP53 downregulation suppressed ESRRB, which in turn repressed EOMES. The downregulation of EOMES directly induced PSMD12 upregulation. To further validate the regulatory interactions between EOMES with PSMD12, we knocked down EOMES in A549 cells by transfecting siRNA-EOMES plasmids (si-EOMES-1 and si-EOMES-2 groups). The sequences are represented in Table and the transfection efficiencies of si-EOMES in A549 cells were validated at both mRNA and protein levels. The result showed that knockdown of EOMES significantly increased PSMD12 mRNA level and there was an obvious elevation in the protein level (Fig.  c), confirming the negative regulatory relationship of EOMES on PSMD12 in the regulatory network.

 

## Discussion
BM is the leading cause of LUAD mortality. Being able to decipher early warning signals for BM would be an effective strategy to improve patient prognosis. In this study, we identified PSMD12 as a crucial gene whose overexpression in primary tumour tissues is associated with BM and poor prognosis of patients with LUAD, even of those with stage I disease. PSMD12 is a non-ATPase subunit of the 19 S regulator of the 26 S proteasome complex, which regulates cell cycle, apoptosis, and DNA damage by removing misfolded proteins or damaged intracellular substructures [ ]. The results of correlation analysis of PSMD12-enriched pathways suggested that PSMD12 overexpression in the ‘Proteasome’ pathway might lead to the dysfunction of the ‘Neuroactive ligand–receptor interaction’ pathway. A previous study reported that the nicotinic acetylcholine receptors (nAChRs) in the ‘Neuroactive ligand–receptor interaction’ pathway were expressed in a variety of brain cells, and activation of them might reprogram the brain tumour microenvironment for promoting tumour progression [ ]. Another study found that Lynx1 was an endogenous proteins from the Ly6/uPAR family, which could degrade expressions of nAChRs by hydrolysis [ ]. Therefore, in GSE11969 dataset, we analysed the correlation of PSMD12 with LYNX1 expressions, and found significantly negative correlation (Pearson correlation, r = -0.40, P = 1.08e-04, Figure a). Furthermore, we analysed the expression correlations of LYNX1 with 16 nAChRs and found that three nAChRs (CHRNA1, CHRNA3, and CHRNB5) were significantly negatively correlated with LYNX1 expression (Pearson correlation, all r < 0, all P < 0.05, Figure b-d), which were significantly positively correlated with PSMD12 expression (Pearson correlation, all r > 0, all P < 0.05, Figure e-g). These results suggested that PSMD12 overexpression might selectively degrade LYNX1 to induce nAChRs overexpression, promoting metastasis of tumour cells to brain in LUAD.
The correlation of PSMD12 with BM was effectively validated in independent public datasets, clinical samples and in vitro BBB model. In multiple independent datasets, the mRNA expression of PSMD12 was significantly higher in the primary tissues of the BM patients than in those of the otherM and nonM patients. Besides, the significant high expression of PSMD12 was also observed in brain sites of BM patients with LUAD. ROC curves demonstrated a comparative stability and adequate discriminative capacity of PSMD12 for BM and non-BM. Survival analyses showed that PSMD12 overexpression was associated with poor prognosis, even for stage I patients. We evaluated the association of PSMD12 mRNA expression with LUAD subtypes and stages recorded in the TCGA database. However, we did not observe significant correlations between PSMD12 expression and subtypes or stages (Fisher’s Exact Test, both P > 0.05, Table S6). It suggested that PSMD12’s role as a BM indicator was independent of LUAD subtypes and stages, which was consistent across diverse patient populations. To validate the potential of PSMD12 in warning BM, we collected clinical samples from patients with stage I–III LUAD and demonstrated that 73% of patients who developed BM within 5 years exhibited high PSMD12 protein expression, higher than that (48%) in patients with otherM or relapsed in situ and that (31%) in patients who did not metastasis within 5 years. Survival analysis also showed that the stage I patients with high PSMD12 protein expression had significantly shorter DFS than those with low expression. Therefore, PSMD12 might be a biomarker for early diagnosis BM in LUAD patients. Furthermore, using an in vitro BBB model, knockdown and overexpression experiments showed that high expression of PSMD12 led to the destruction of BBB and enhanced the ability of LUAD cells to pass cross BBB.
Since there were no genomic or epigenomic alterations of PSMD12 in TCGA multi-omics dataset, we further developed HMSRF to construct an upstream TF regulatory network acting in a coherent feed-forward loop fashion for PSMD12 overexpression. In this network, downregulation of a tumour suppressing gene TP53 [ ] may have contributed to PSMD12 overexpression by inhibiting ESRRB and EOMES expression. For the first-layer TF EOMES, we have carried out knockdown experiment to validate the negative regulatory interactions between EOMES and PSMD12, supporting the rationality of developed HMSRF method. For the second-layer TF ESRRB, a previous study reported that inactivation of ESRRB stimulates proteasome activation [ ], indicating a potential molecular mechanism wherein TP53 alteration enhance proteasome pathway activity by upregulating PSMD12 and inducing tumour cells to metastasize to the brain.
This study still had some limitations. First, we identified PSMD12 and its molecular mechanisms in the primary tissues that subsequently developed into BM concentrating on upstream TFs regulations. Other molecular regulation modes, such as N6-methyladenosine (m6A), may be responsible for dysregulations of interesting genes, which reflect diverse biological processes. Second, functional analysis indicated that PSMD12 might be associated with BM in LUAD patients by regulating the neuroactive ligand–receptor interaction pathway, which merits further investigation. Third, we used in vitro knockdown experiments to confirm PSMD12’s role in BM of LUAD. Future studies should focus on developing or repurposing drugs targeting PSMD12 or its upstream regulators and evaluating the efficacy in preclinical models to enhance clinical validity.

## Conclusions
This is a study to identify PSMD12 as a crucial gene associated with BM and validate in independent public datasets, clinical samples and in vitro BBB model. It may serve as an early warning indicator of BM in early-stage LUAD. Furthermore, this study proposes the HMSRF method to identify the upstream TFs underlying PSMD12 overexpression, providing insights into the molecular mechanisms of BM.
