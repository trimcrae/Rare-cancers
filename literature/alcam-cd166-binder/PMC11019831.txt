## Abstract
HSCs, the resident pericytes of the liver, have consistently been at the forefront of liver research due to their crucial roles in various hepatic pathological processes. Prior literature often depicted HSCs in a binary framework, categorizing them as either quiescent or activated. However, recent advances in HSC research, particularly the advent of single-cell RNA-sequencing, have revolutionized our understanding of these cells. This sophisticated technique offers an unparalleled, high-resolution insight into HSC populations, uncovering a spectrum of diversity and functional heterogeneity across various physiological states of the liver, ranging from liver development to the liver aging process. The single-cell RNA-sequencing revelations have also highlighted the intrinsic plasticity of HSCs and underscored their complex roles in a myriad of pathophysiological processes, including liver injury, repair, and carcinogenesis. This review aims to integrate and clarify these recent discoveries, focusing on how the inherent plasticity of HSCs is central to their dynamic roles both in maintaining liver homeostasis and orchestrating responses to liver injury. Future research will clarify whether findings from rodent models can be translated to human livers and guide how these insights are harnessed to develop targeted therapeutic interventions.

## INTRODUCTION
HSCs, also known as Ito cells, fat-storing cells, lipocytes, and perisinusoidal cells, typically exist in a nonproliferative (a.k.a, “quiescent”) state in healthy livers. HSCs are activated during liver injury and transdifferentiate into myofibroblastic states with enhanced proliferative, migratory, and/or fibrogenic activities. , This transformation plays a pivotal role in liver defense and healing. However, chronic liver injury leads to sustained activation of HSCs and excessive deposition of fibrous extracellular matrix (ECM), thereby causing progressive replacement of functional hepatic parenchyma by scar that ultimately culminates in cirrhosis. , Although cirrhosis was once thought to be irreversible, growing evidence indicates that liver fibrosis can regress on eliation of the causative factors. The mechanisms that restore effective liver regeneration and remove scars are poorly understood but clearly require shrinking populations of activated HSCs. This seems to occur through 3 main processes: reversion of activated HSCs to an inactivated state, induction of cellular senescence, and eradication of activated HSCs through apoptosis and ferroptosis. , The factors that regulate these responses and whether there is a dominant mechanism that drives the regression of liver fibrosis remain subjects of ongoing research. Progress is now possible thanks to new methodologies that enable phenotypic characterization at the level of individual cells. These approaches confirm that the long-acknowledged heterogeneity of populations with HSC results, in large part, from the tremendous inherent plasticity of HSCs themselves. Importantly, gene markers that differentiate functionally unique HSC subsets are being identified. This knowledge, in turn, is being leveraged to identify tractable mechanisms that configure HSC communities to optimize liver repair. This review aims to summarize and synthesize these emerging findings, highlighting how transitions in HSC communities both maintain liver homeostasis and contribute to liver pathology.

## HSC in liver development
A better understanding of the origin(s), plasticity, and function of HSCs during liver development will facilitate the design of targeted therapies for HSC-driven pathologies in adult livers. However, while much progress has been achieved in past decades, the embryonic origin of HSCs remains a subject of debate , (Figure ). During early embryogenesis, mesodermal posterior 1+ mesoderm differentiates into Wilms tumor 1+ septum transversum mesenchyme. Septum transversum mesenchyme is thought to be a major source of HSCs. Lineage tracing studies in mice demonstrate that Wilms tumor 1+ cells from the mesothelium migrate inward from the liver surface, contributing to the formation of this population with HSC. , Other studies have reported that HSCs from the fetal liver also express neural markers (eg, nestin, glial fibrillary acidic protein, GFAP) and endodermal markers (eg, cytokeratin 8 and 18), suggesting respective neuroectodermal and endodermal origins of HSCs as well. , Delineating the embryological derivation of adult populations with HSC is further confounded by studies reporting that HSCs can be derived from hematopoietic stem cells , or generated from induced pluripotent stem cells, , and may even transdifferentiate from hepatic epithelial cells through epithelial-mesenchymal transition in injured adult livers. , , Thus, it remains unclear to what extent the heterogeneity of adult populations with HSC reflects true lineage-specific differences in their origins versus different transitional states of cells derived from a common HSC precursor. This ongoing controversy highlights the intricate interplay of various cell lineages during liver development and underscores the residual plasticity of their adult progeny.
 Employing single-cell RNA-sequencing (scRNA-seq) techniques, Lotto et al scrutinized 45,334 individual transcriptomes from embryonic day (E)7.5 to E10.5, a critical period encompassing endoderm progenitor specification at E7.5 and emergence of liver parenchymal and nonparenchymal cell lineages by E10.5. The study identified HSCs within the liver as early as E9.5, with HSC progenitor clusters exhibiting notable heterogeneity and expressing mesenchymal markers (Alcam, Mab21l2), HSC lineage markers (Lhx2, Des), and activation-related and migration-related genes (Zeb2, Rspo3) along with ECM proteins, suggesting that HSC activation during liver bud formation plays a pivotal role in sinusoidal organization and basement membrane formation. More recently, Wesley et al conducted scRNA-seq analyses on human fetal livers. HSCs were captured at 5 weeks postconception, and 3 distinct populations with HSC were identified: one expressing mesenchymal markers, another with an endothelial bias, and a third expressing markers for both lineages. Pseudo-time analyses further revealed that the fetal endothelial and HSCs originate from a common stellate-endothelial progenitor population. Moreover, the analysis also unveiled diverse and complex interactions between HSCs, KCs, and endothelial cells, indicating the potential roles of HSCs in ECM organization, hematopoietic development, and innate immunity in the fetal liver.
In the developing liver, VEGF and retinoic acid signaling mediate HSC migration and differentiation, , while Wilms tumor 1, Lhx2, and Wnt/β-catenin signaling prevent aberrant HSC activation. , , , HSCs interact with other hepatic cell types to control hepatic organogenesis. For example, HSCs and endothelial cells closely interact to ensure vascular tube formation and integrity through SDF1α and PDGFβ signaling; , , HSCs recruit hematopoietic stem cells to the liver through SDF1α/CXCR4 signaling; , HSCs also secrete growth factors such as Wnt, FGF, HGF, and retinoic acid to regulate the proliferation and differentiation of hepatoblast progenitor cells into hepatocytes. , , Expression the Notch ligand jagged 1 (Jag1) in HSCs was shown to be critical for the development of biliary cells. , Recently, scRNA-seq of the human fetal liver revealed ligand-receptor interactions between HSC-expressed RSPO3 and hepatoblast-expressed LGR4/5, indicating HSCs’ supportive role in hepatoblast self-renewal and hepatic niche construction.

## HSC in healthy liver
In the healthy adult liver, HSCs constitute approximately 5%–8% of liver cells and function as resident pericytes situated in the space of Disse, a confined area between sinusoidal endothelial cells and the hepatocyte basal surface. These cells possess a distinctive star-like morphology characterized by extended protrusions that emanate from the cell body and wrap around the sinusoids. They are typically nonproliferative (ie, replication-quiescent), and the lipid droplets in the cell body store retinol ester comprising the largest reservoir of vitamin A of the body.
Contrary to the earlier beliefs that HSCs constitute a uniform cell population within the hepatic acinus, recent advances, particularly in scRNA-seq analysis, have revealed a striking spatial zonation of HSCs in the hepatic lobule of healthy livers (Table ). This zonation results in the formation of transcriptome-distinct subpopulations with HSC in different regions of the lobule: portal vein-associated HSCs (PaHSCs) and central vein-associated HSCs (CaHSCs). Among the 81 genes differentially expressed in these zones, 52 are associated with PaHSCs (eg, Ngfr and Itgb3), while 29 are linked to CaHSCs (eg, Adamtsl2 and Rspo3). Brenner group corroborated these findings, and their STRING analysis demonstrated that PaHSCs exhibit stronger interactions with basement membrane–type ECM proteins like laminins and type 4 collagens. In contrast, CaHSC-associated genes show more robust interactions with activator protein 1 components (such as Jun, JunB, and Fos), but not with ECM pathway genes. These transcriptomic differences have functional significance, as CaHSCs were identified as the dominant pathogenic collagen-producing cells in carbon tetrachloride (CCl4)-induced fibrosis, whereas both populations significantly contributed to fibrosis in the western diet-induced metabolic dysfunction–associated steatohepatitis (MASH) model. The variation in zonal contribution correlated with the topographical location of injury, as CCl4 causes primarily centrilobular necrosis and MASH-induced metabolic insult affects the entire acinus, resulting in diffuse fibrosis. As proof of concept, blockade of Lpar1 that expressed in CaHSCs but not PaHSCs greatly inhibits liver fibrosis in centrilobular areas but less so in portal area in a rodent NASH model. 

 In human studies, 2 distinct subpopulations with HSC have been observed as well, although they do not exhibit as pronounced transcriptional differences as PaHSCs and CaHSCs in rodents. Glypican-3–positive HSCs in humans are localized around portal and central veins, while dopamine beta-hydroxylase–positive HSCs are predominantly perisinusoidal. Both subpopulations contribute to ECM production and organization but with differing gene expression profiles: glypican-3–positive HSCs are involved in glycosaminoglycan metabolism, whereas dopamine beta-hydroxylase–positive HSCs show a gene signature akin to antigen-presenting cells. This nuanced understanding of HSC heterogeneity, gleaned from both human and rodent studies, underscores metabolic zonation as a fundamental factor in determining HSC transcriptomic diversity. It remains to be determined which of the subpopulations with HSC in humans or rodents have the most (or least) fibrogenic activity and whether we can specifically target the pathogenic HSC subsets for a more precise and effective treatment for liver fibrosis.

## HSC in injured liver
The transformation of HSCs into myofibroblasts (MFBs), commonly referred to as activation, is a critical process in the liver’s response to various forms of injury. This includes exposure to hepatotoxins, viral hepatitis, and conditions such as alcohol-associated or NASH. During activation, HSCs undergo profound phenotypic alterations, which include cell enlargement and elongation, depletion of lipid droplets, and the acquisition of proliferative, motile, chemotactic, and contractile capabilities. In addition, this transformation is marked by the initiation of gene expression related to ECM proteins. The regulation of HSC activation involves a multitude of mechanisms. Central to these are various signaling molecules and pathways, including the Hippo-YAP, Hedgehog, TGFβ, PDGF, VEGF, connective tissue growth factor (CTGF), and the endocannabinoid system. Furthermore, pathways that regulate carbohydrate, mitochondrial, lipid, and retinoid metabolism, as well as intracellular events and stress responses from cellular compartments (such as autophagy, reactive oxygen species, and endoplasmic reticulum stress), play significant roles. Epigenetic alterations, including changes in microRNAs, DNA methylation, and histone modification, alongside extracellular signals from the ECM and stimuli from resident and infiltrating inflammatory cells (including cytokines and adipokines), also constitute key mediators of HSC activation. These mechanisms are not discussed in detail here since they have been extensively reviewed elsewhere. , , , 

Activated HSCs were perceived conventionally as a uniform population evenly distributed throughout the hepatic acinus, contributing equally to liver fibrosis and unequivocally upregulating the canonical myofibroblastic markers like Acta2 and collagen proteins. While earlier studies had sporadically reported that these canonical markers are variably expressed among the larger population with HSC, , , definitive evidence for such plasticity and heterogeneity was only obtained recently through application of scRNA-seq analysis (Table ). Krenkel et al reported the first scRNA-seq analysis of activated HSCs from CCl4-injured liver. Four different subpopulations were identified on the basis of transcriptomic differences/similarities. The largest cluster MFB expresses the highest levels of the classic myofibroblastic HSC (myHSCs) marker Acta2 and various types of collagens, such as Col1a1, Col3a1, and Col6a3; the second cluster (MFB II) has a reduced expression of ECM genes but demonstrates a high expression of inflammatory genes such as complement factor C3, Cd74, serum amyloid A3 (Saa3) and leukocyte protease inhibitor (Slpi), suggesting the existence of a distinct subset of immunoregulatory HSC; the third cluster (MFB III) was defined as the proliferative HSC subset based on high expression of the components of the AP1 transcription factor (such as Jun, JunB, Fos); the smallest cluster (MFB IV) display a mixed phenotype with a high expression of ECM modulators such as the matrix gla protein (Mgp) and fibulin 1 (Fbln1) and some features for portal fibroblasts (PF). They also studied the heterogeneity of in vitro–activated HSCs using the scRNA-seq approach, and revealed that in vitro–activated HSCs express various marker genes transiently, suggesting that HSCs may transition through these states during in vitro activation. Another in vitro study using scRNA-seq identified a novel HSC state that is enriched with genes related to activation, p53 signaling and cancer, and the authors suggested that HSCs in this state may contribute to cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) in vivo. 

 Zhang et al also conducted scRNA-seq analyses to delve into the plasticity and heterogeneity of HSCs isolated from CCl4-induced fibrotic liver. Rather than characterizing each cluster of activated HSCs in isolation, this study employed differential gene expression, pseudo-time trajectory, velocity, and Monocle analyses to study HSC state transitioning. The unsupervised analysis identified a total of 8 subpopulations with HSC, each displaying a unique transcriptomic profile, although all expressed Acta2, the classic HSC activation marker (Table ). Among these clusters, cluster 1 was felt to represent an early phase activation state that is characterized by genes related to cytokinesis. It served as the root lineage, differentiating into 3 other major subtypes, respectively, localizing in clusters 2–4. Cluster 2 emerged as the most populated subtype, and was designated as the mature myofibroblastic population with HSC due to its high expression of the hallmark gene Acta2, as well as genes related to cellular contraction, platelet activation, and collagen assembly. In contrast, cluster 3 featured the expression of chemokine genes that may promote inflammation, while cluster 4 represented a novel HSC subtype likely involved in multiple processes, including immune reaction, vascular remodeling, and liver repair. The remaining clusters were less enriched and consequently less characterized. Importantly, the observed heterogeneity of HSCs in mouse liver is likely present in cirrhotic human liver, as the HSC cluster-specific markers identified in the mouse liver were also uniquely expressed in subpopulations of HSCs in fibrotic human liver. In a more recent study, Yang et al performed scRNA-seq analysis of HSCs isolated from both chronic CCl4- and bile duct ligation (BDL)-injured mouse livers. They identified 3 distinct stages of HSCs based on the differential gene expression kinetics in the pseudo-time trajectory analysis: stage-1 HSCs exhibited high expression of inflammatory cytokines (eg, Ccl2, CXC chemokine ligand 10 (CXCL), and Ccl7); stage 2 HSCs were characterized by high expression of cell mobility and contractility-associated genes (eg, Acta2, Tpm1, Vim, Tagln, and Tnc); stage-3 HSCs demonstrated extremely high expression of genes related to ECM and tissue remodeling (Col1a1, Lox, and Lum). Surprisingly, only a small fraction of HSCs appeared to be proliferative, with most proliferation occurring in stage 2 subpopulations. The investigation also disclosed that HSCs exclusively gave rise to MFBs in CCl4-treated livers, while PFs emerged as the primary source of MFBs in early cholestatic liver fibrosis. More importantly, nitric oxide receptor soluble guanylate cyclase is exclusively expressed in HSCs, but not in PFs. The soluble guanylate cyclase stimulator riociguat potently reduced liver fibrosis in CCl4-treated livers but showed no therapeutic efficacy in BDL livers.
Rosenthal et al furthered the characterization of activated HSCs using a mouse model of MASH induced by a high-fat western diet in “foz/foz” mice (Table ). They identified four distinct clusters, each defined by a unique gene signature. The first cluster resembled the classic CCl4-induced myHSCs, characterized by high expression of fibrillar collagens, Acta2, and Timp1. Cluster 2 represented an intermediate activated state, displaying weaker expression of classic HSC activation gene markers compared to the first cluster. This intermediate state of HSCs was not observed in the CCl4-induced fibrosis model, possibly due to the gradual and milder nature of the injury insult in MASH compared to CCl4 toxicity. Cluster 3 expressed fewer fibrogenic markers but exhibited inflammatory markers such as Cd36, Ly6c, and CLEC, while cluster 4 co-expressed both fibrogenic markers and proliferative markers such as Cdk1, Cenpa, and Top2a, indicating that only activated HSCs are proliferative in the fibrotic liver. Interestingly, there was a high correlation among classic myHSC markers such as Col1a1, Timp1, and Acta2, suggesting that these genes tend to be expressed at similar levels within the same cells. In contrast to the dominant contribution of CaHSCs in CCl4-induced liver fibrosis, activated HSCs originating from both zonated subpopulations were diffusely distributed in this mouse model of MASH (Table ). This observation suggests that the injury pattern (ie, pericentral hepatotoxicity, periportal cholestatic injury, or diffuse metabolic stress) can change HSC phenotypes and dictate the recruitment and distribution of quiescent HSCs as they undergo activation during liver injury. Consequently, the distinct contributions of zonal HSCs merits careful consideration in the design of targeted antifibrotic therapies tailored to different etiologies. The observation of heterogeneous HSC clusters extends beyond mouse models of liver fibrosis and includes instances of liver regeneration and normal liver repair. Three of the four clusters identified in MASH (classic MFBs, intermediately activated cluster, and a proliferating cluster) were similarly observed in mice following partial hepatectomy (Table ). Depletion of collagen-producing cells using Col-ER-Cre/DTA mice resulted in impaired liver regeneration, confirming earlier studies which had identified an integral role of these diverse HSC clusters in liver homeostasis and response to injury. , , 

Collectively, recent application of scRNA-seq techniques to study HSCs in mouse models of liver fibrosis, MASH, and liver regeneration have revealed that activated HSCs constitute a heterogeneous population comprised of various subsets, each associated with specific functions. Notably, individual cells within this population exhibit plasticity and can transition through multiple states, with their roles varying depending on timing and context. In parallel with mouse studies, scRNA-seq analyses of human fibrotic livers have also indicated that HSCs likely serve as the major source of scar-associated mesenchyme, and related spatial platforms confirm their localization within fibrotic niches. , However, it is important to acknowledge that studies in humans are scarce. Further, the representation of mesenchymal cells in these human studies is low relative to that of various other cell types. These limitations underscore the need for more comprehensive human scRNA-seq data. This will require in-depth analysis of more livers with differing levels of damage and diverse disease etiologies, as well as consideration of patient variables that critically influence disease progression, such as sex, age, and ethnicity.

## HSCs in the resolution of liver diseases
Although liver fibrosis was traditionally considered an irreversible condition, evidence indicates that fibrosis can regress once the underlying liver insult is removed. , , , Because improvement in fibrosis is typically accompanied by the disappearance of activated HSCs, much research has focused on developing strategies to eliminate these cells in order to promote fibrosis resolution. Consistent findings from rodent models of liver fibrosis suggest that activated HSCs may be eliminated through three distinct mechanisms: cell death, senescence, or inactivation (also referred to as “reversion” in the literature) (Figure ).
 

## HSC death by apoptosis
Studies in rodents indicate that apoptosis may account for the elimination of up to 50% of activated HSCs during liver fibrosis regression. , Activated HSCs are characterized by increased expression of death receptors, including Fas (CD95), TRAIL, and TNFR1, which may predispose them to apoptotic signaling. This hypothesis is supported by findings where anti-CD95 antibodies have induced apoptosis in activated HSCs, and systemic administration of PEGylated TRAIL has been effective in reducing liver cirrhosis in rat models. Despite these observations in rodents, translating these findings to human liver fibrosis remains challenging. There is a notable paucity of robust experimental data demonstrating the effectiveness of inducing HSC apoptosis in reducing liver fibrosis and improving liver function in human contexts. Moreover, emerging evidence indicates a potential resistance of human HSCs to apoptosis, , , further complicating this approach. Proinflammatory cytokines such as TNFα and IL­1β have been implicated in promoting the resistance of HSCs to apoptosis through NF­κB activation. Moreover, TIMP1 and TGFβ, which are upregulated in activated HSCs, serve as antiapoptotic signals, promoting the survival of HSCs. Our recent scRNA-seq analysis of HSCs from chronic CCl4 and BDL models has revealed intriguing insights. HSC clusters with high Acta2 expression, indicative of a myofibroblastic phenotype, displayed a low Bcl2/Bax ratio. However, contrary to expectations, these cells remained viable, suggesting that their survival mechanisms extend beyond the classical Bcl2 pathway. This observation aligns with our experimental data, which demonstrated that myHSCs exhibit relative resistance to Bcl2 inhibitors. Furthermore, systemic treatment with a Bcl2 inhibitor in a CCl4-induced fibrosis model unexpectedly aggravated liver fibrosis, highlighting the complexity of targeting HSC apoptosis in therapeutic strategies. Given these findings, the role of HSC apoptosis in liver fibrosis resolution is far from definitive. It appears to be a complex, multifaceted process that may differ significantly between rodent models and human liver disease. Thus, further investigation is required to unravel the intricacies of HSC apoptosis in liver fibrosis, particularly in the context of human pathology.

## HSC death by ferroptosis
Ferroptosis is a newly identified, lipid peroxidation-mediated cell death that is characterized by disturbed iron and redox homeostasis. It is distinct from other forms of cell death morphologically, biochemically, and genetically. Ferroptosis eliminates cells that have become “unfit” and thus, it has beneficial roles in liver development, as well as adult liver homeostasis and regeneration. However, dysregulated ferroptosis has also been implicated in a variety of pathological processes. , , Defining the roles of ferroptosis in HSC biology and liver fibrosis have recently emerged as very active areas of liver research. , Several agents with ferroptosis-inducing properties, such as artemether and artesunate, magnesium isoglycyrrhizinate, berberine, sorafenib, and dihydroartemisinin, have demonstrated efficacy in mitigating liver fibrosis in rodent models through the induction of ferroptosis in HSCs. Concurrently, markers indicative of ferroptotic stress have been observed in human HSCs derived from patients with fibrosis with HCC undergoing sorafenib monotherapy. Key signaling regulators, including the P53 pathway, heme oxygenase-1 (HO-1), m6A methylation, , and RNA-binding proteins (such as ZFP36 and ELAVL1/HuR ), have emerged as pivotal players in orchestrating ferroptosis within HSCs. Recently, our group reported that culture-activated HSCs are highly susceptible to ferroptosis and showed that Hippo-YAP signaling dictates myHSC ferroptosis sensitivity by regulating the P21-GPX4 axis. Consistent with this discovery, our reanalysis of publicly available single-cell data derived from fibrotic liver primary HSCs revealed that the HSC cluster with the lowest YAP_Conserved_Signature was depleted of activated HSC markers (Acta2 and Col1a1) but enriched with markers of ferroptotic stress. However, akin to the outcomes observed with systemic Bcl2 inhibitors, systemic administration of ferroptosis inducers (including cyst(e)inase, erastin, and its derivative imidazole ketone erastin) exacerbated liver injury and fibrosis in mice with either CCl4 or BDL-induced liver fibrosis. These disappointing outcomes suggest that chronic liver injury may activate conserved mechanisms that sensitize diverse cell types to apoptosis or ferroptosis. Consequently, systemic delivery of drugs that induce these death programs run the risk of accentuating liver epithelial injury, amplifying signals derived from injured hepatocytes, and fostering the accumulation of myHSC, thereby perpetuating the cycle of liver fibrosis. 

To date, there is a limited body of research investigating HSC death through other alternative cell death pathways such as necroptosis , or pyroptosis. Consequently, the intricate intracellular mechanisms governing HSC death and death susceptibility remain incompletely understood. A more comprehensive understanding of the delicate equilibrium between HSC death and survival holds the potential to unveil more precise and efficient strategies for antifibrotic therapies.

## HSC senescence
Cellular senescence is a cell state of irreversible growth arrest, yet the cells remain metabolically active. HSC senescence has been observed in various liver diseases in both rodents and humans. , Characterization of senescent HSCs has elucidated some of their key features, including an activated p53/p21 pathway, reduced expression of MFB markers and ECM proteins, enhanced secretion of ECM degrading enzymes, and upregulation of genes involved in immune response. , The impact of HSC senescence on liver pathophysiology is complex and context-dependent. Cheng et al recently reported that HSCs underwent senescence within 2 days after two-thirds partial hepatectomy in young mice. The senescent HSCs secreted IL-6 and CXCR2 ligands as part of their senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP), initiating multiple signaling pathways that stimulated liver regeneration and thus, their elimination impaired liver regeneration. In numerous studies of chronic liver injury models, , , , , , inducing HSC senescence has also been beneficial by limiting fibrosis while disrupting the senescence program has been reported to enhance MFB accumulation, intensify liver fibrosis, and promote HCC development. , , , Interestingly, however, Amor et al identified the urokinase plasminogen activator receptor as a cell surface protein broadly induced in senescent cells, including senescent HSCs. urokinase plasminogen activator receptor–specific CAR T cells efficiently ablate senescent HSCs and suppress both CCl4-induced and NASH-induced liver fibrosis in mice. Additionally, Lujambio and colleagues revealed that senescent HSCs secrete factors that promote M1 macrophage polarization, and these polarized macrophages can eliminate senescent cells and support an antitumor microenvironment. In parallel, several studies , , have also reported that natural killer cells preferentially kill senescent HSCs and facilitate the resolution of liver fibrosis. These findings highlight a possible pathogenic role of senescent HSCs in the resolution of liver fibrosis, and underscore the therapeutic potential of targeting specific HSC subsets (eg, senescent HSCs) using immunotoxin-specific strategies in the treatment of liver fibrosis. Single-cell analytical approaches may help to resolve this paradox by mapping context-dependent differences in signaling and cell-cell interactions.
Despite the importance of HSC senescence in liver pathophysiology, the molecular mechanisms governing this fate transition remain elusive. Our recent investigations highlight the significance of Hippo-YAP signaling in determining whether myHSC maintain activation or undergo senescence. scRNA-seq analyses of HSCs from chronic CCl4 and BDL models further revealed that the HSC cluster with the lowest Yap activity exhibits the most reduced expression of the activated HSC markers Acta2 and Col1a1 but the most elevated levels of the senescent marker Cdkn1a (p21). Yap1−/− HSCs from CCl4-treated mice are cell cycle arrested and display a robust SASP. Remarkably, selective YAP depletion in activated HSCs induces senescence and protects against liver fibrosis in diverse mouse models, including those induced by CCl4, BDL, and a methionine-choline–deficient diet. These findings underscore the rationale for considering the induction of HSC senescence as a promising strategy for the treatment of liver fibrosis. Nevertheless, it’s crucial to note that excessive accumulation of senescent cells can be pathogenic since the persistent secretion of SASP factors by senescent cells may promote paracrine senescence, amplify local inflammation, and disrupt tissue homeostasis, potentially promoting outgrowth of liver cancers. Therefore, strategies limiting senescent HSC accumulation should also be considered to promote disease regression following therapy-induced HSC senescence. Future studies should explore whether the senescent population with HSC represents a simple homogeneous cluster or a heterogeneous phenotype with distinct subsets serving specific functions.

## HSC inactivation (or reversion)
Utilizing a sophisticated array of methodologies, including genetic cell fate tracking, single-cell PCR, and microarray analysis, Kisseleva et al and Troeger et al concurrently demonstrated a gradual decrease in the expression of classic markers of activated HSCs (Col1a1, Timp1, Acta2, and TGFβ) over time during the recovery phase from fibrosis induced by various etiologies, such as chronic CCl4 or thioacetamide administration, intragastric alcohol feeding, or BDL surgery. Intriguingly, while HSCs in livers with regressing fibrosis reacquire several features of quiescence (eg, re-expression of lipogenic genes Adfp, Adipor1, CREBP, and PPAR-γ), they exhibit enhanced reactivity to TGFβ or mitogenic stimulation in culture. Moreover, they more rapidly reactivate into MFBs in response to secondary fibrogenic stimuli and more strongly contribute to liver fibrosis in mice, , suggesting that these HSCs acquire a “intermediate” phenotype akin to, but distinct from, quiescent HSCs. Kisseleva et al also demonstrated that approximately half of the MFBs escape apoptosis, and HSCs undergoing inactivation upregulate the antiapoptotic genes Hspa1a/b. This underscores the notion that the delicate balance between proapoptotic and antiapoptotic signaling may be a pivotal mechanism in determining the fate of inactivated HSCs as liver fibrosis regresses. Microarray profiling of the primary human HSCs reverted in vitro also revealed that these cells display an intermediary phenotype that is distinct from quiescent HSCs or activated HSCs, and characterized by the increased expression of CXC chemokine ligand 1, CXC chemokine ligand 2, and CTSS, genes that were identified as signature genes of in vivo inactivated mouse HSCs. Recently, using the scRNA-seq approach, Brenner’s group further characterized the inactivated HSCs isolated from MASH mouse liver 8 weeks after MASH diets were withdrawn to initiate liver recovery. By comparing the transcriptomics to their counterparts in healthy and injured liver, they revealed that the classic activated HSC markers (Col1a1, Timp1, and Acta2) are low in quiescence, rise with activation, and then return toward baseline with inactivation. Some genes (eg, Bambi, Vipr1, and ApoE) are highly expressed in quiescence, decrease in activation, and return to high levels during inactivation. Notably, there are also some genes that are low in quiescence that remain low in activation but are uniquely induced during inactivation (eg, GABA-A Receptor Subunit Alpha 3, Cxcl1, and Fbln7). These genes potentially play pivotal roles in the transition from activation to inactivation and in reshaping the fibrotic and inflammatory liver microenvironment. GABA-A Receptor Subunit Alpha 3 (Gabra3) is intriguing, though its specific role in HSC is still being explored. The GABA(A) receptor has been identified as an inhibitory signal for hepatic cell proliferation, and GABA(B) receptor agonists have been shown to inhibit HSC activation. Cxcl1, a chemokine known to be primarily released by HSCs, plays a significant role in modulating the immune response or cell migration. Fbln7 (Fibulin 7), an ECM protein, could be involved in the reorganization of the ECM as HSCs transition back to an inactivation state. Further research is needed to elucidate the precise roles of these genes in HSC biology, which could uncover new targets for therapeutic intervention in liver fibrosis.

## HSC in liver carcinogenesis
Globally, liver cancer ranks as the third leading cause of cancer-related mortality, and its incidence is escalating. The predominant forms, HCC and intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma (iCCA), collectively account for 80%–90% and 10%–20%, respectively, of all primary liver cancers. Compounding the gravity of the situation, the liver also serves as the primary site for metastasis in 25% of metastatic gastrointestinal and colon cancers. Primary liver cancers typically arise in the setting of chronic liver diseases with advanced fibrosis. HSCs are well recognized as the major sources of collagen-producing MFB-like cells and cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) in HCC and liver metastasis; contributions from PFs may contribute to fibrosis in iCCA. However, as discussed below, the role of HSC-triggered liver fibrosis in liver carcinogenesis is debated. While most primary liver cancers occur in cirrhotic livers and fibrosis regression correlates with decreased liver cancer risk, , , , , liver cancers have also been diagnosed in nonfibrotic livers of individuals who have had chronic hepatitis B, C, or metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease. , , 

Whether HSC-triggered liver fibrosis is necessary and sufficient for liver carcinogenesis in human remains ambiguous. Filliol et al recently suggested that the controversy in animal models of hepatocarcinogenesis may arise from differences in study designs and the use of Gfap-Cre transgenic mice, as Gfap is not a specific marker of HSCs in fibrotic liver. Lrat serves as more specific marker of HSC. Utilizing Lrat-Cre transgenic mice and genetic approaches across various animal models of liver carcinogenesis, Filliol et al identified a dual role of HSCs: an overall tumor-promoting function and an unexpected tumor-suppressing role that localized within distinct HSC clusters. More specifically, using scRNA-seq analyses in mouse and human HSC, they revealed 2 distinct subpopulations with HSC in fibrotic liver: myHSCs enriched in ECM-related genes and less fibrogenic cytokine-producing HSCs (cyHSCs) enriched in cytokine and growth factor genes. Genetic ablation experiments demonstrated that HGF, enriched in cyHSCs, protects against hepatocyte death and HCC development, while type I collagen, enriched in myHSCs, promotes hepatocyte proliferation and HCC progression. Trajectory analyses indicated that the dynamic transition of cyHSCs to collagen-producing myHSCs during fibrosis and HCC development correlates with an elevated HCC risk in patients, suggesting that targeting specific mediators in subpopulations with HSC to restore this imbalance presents a novel strategy to mitigate HSC-driven HCC risk. Interestingly, the roles of these subpopulations with HSC in iCCA and liver metastasis differ from their effects in HCC. For example, myCAFs were reported to promote tumor growth in iCCA, whereas myCAFs-derived type I collagen suppressed tumor growth in liver metatases. On the other hand, cyHSCs promoted growth of both iCCA and metastases through the mesenchymal epithelial transition axis. , Thus, targeting specific signaling pathways identified in these newly characterized HSC subsets or modulating the immune environment also represent promising therapeutic strategies to mitigate fibrosis and carcinogenesis.
A growing body of literature has also underscored the pivotal contribution of senescent HSCs to the intricate landscape of liver cancer development (Figure ). Krizhanovsky et al firstly used serial section staining to show colocalization of senescence markers p21 and p16 with alpha smooth muscle actin in human cirrhotic livers. By immunofluorescence analysis, Yoshimoto et al also revealed that a series of senescence markers such as DNA damage response markers (γH2AX foci and 53BP1 foci), CDK inhibitors (p16 and p21) and SASP factors (IL-6 and IL-8) were co-stained with αSMA in HCC arising in patients with nonfibrotic NASH. Likewise, Loo et al demonstrated through immunofluorescence that senescence markers like p21 and SASP factors including IL-1β and Cox2 were co-localized with αSMA in similar human HCC cases. The enterohepatic circulation of deoxycholic acid induces an SASP phenotype in HSCs, leading to the secretion of inflammatory and tumor-promoting factors such as IL-6, GROα, and CXC chemokine ligand, thereby facilitating HCC development. Subsequently, Li et al revealed that hepatocytic loss of FBP1 triggers HSC activation, followed by senescence and the acquisition of a proinflammatory SASP. “Senolytic” treatments with dasatinib/quercetin or ABT-263 were reported to deplete senescent HSCs and suppress HCC progression. In support of this, Wakita et al further demonstrated that a newly identified senolytic compound also reduces senescent HSCs and mitigates obesity-associated HCC development in mice. More recently, Yamagishi et al discovered that in patients with NASH-associated HCC, αSMA colocalizes with the SASP factor IL-33 but not with the proliferation marker Ki-67. Gasdermin D–mediated release of IL-33 from senescent HSCs impairs CD8+-mediated antitumor immunity and promotes obesity-associated HCC. Additionally, Zhou et al reported that the senescent HSC secretome promotes MASH malignant transformation through activating morphogenic hedgehog or oncogenic Wnt signaling pathways. However, it is important to note the controversy surrounding the role of HSC senescence in hepatocarcinogenesis. Senolytics used in the above studies are not specific to senescent HSCs, and p53 depletion in HSCs was shown to decrease HSC senescence but increase liver tumorigenesis. On the other hand, epigallocatechin gallate induces HSC senescence and attenuates the development of HCC. Therefore, future definitive studies will be needed to test whether the specific induction or depletion of senescent HSCs affects liver carcinogenesis.

## HSC in aged liver
Aging profoundly impacts the hepatic sinusoid and increases the prevalence and severity of liver diseases, including liver fibrosis, in both humans and rodent models. , , , Thus, there is a critical need to investigate how aging influences HSC biology and function. Warren et al conducted a comprehensive examination of HSCs in livers from rats (3–4 vs. 24–27 months) and mice (2–3 vs. 20–22 months). Their findings revealed that cells expressing the HSC marker desmin doubled in old age in both mice and rats. However, αSMA-positive cells did not show a significant increase and remained only a small fraction of the desmin-positive cells. Electron microscopy demonstrated a substantial increase in the number of larger diameter lipid droplets within HSCs of aged animals, accompanied by a marked increase in HSC protrusion into the sinusoidal lumen in old mice. Maeso-Diaz et al corroborated the presence of lipid accumulation within aged HSCs. Intriguingly, they found that HSCs in aging rats exhibited slight activation, as evidenced by expression of αSMA, PDGFR-β, and collagen I. Additional markers and mediators of HSC state change, such as the senescence marker p16, matrix remodeling enzymes, Timp2, and Mmp9, and intracellular oxidative stress, were also increased. However, no changes were observed in the expressions of the proinflammatory mediators TLR4, NF-κB, or a major fibrogenic factor, TGFβ. , Therefore, the functionality of HSCs in aging livers remains uncertain, as the aged population with HSC exhibits features of quiescent and senescent HSCs, but has also upregulated expression of markers consistent with HSC activation. This unique phenotype suggests that the population with HSC in aged livers has diverged from those in healthy, injured, or fibrosis-regressed younger livers. Whether this age-related diversification reflects biased enrichment/depletion of certain communities with HSCs that populate young livers or the outgrowth of a novel subpopulation with aging-specific HSC is unknown. Future studies can leverage single-cell analytical approaches to decipher the basis for this complexity and elucidate how the heterogeneity contributes to the growing prevalence and severity of aging-related liver diseases.

## Summary, limitations, and future direction
The advent of scRNA-seq has markedly enhanced our understanding of HSC plasticity, heterogeneity, and dynamics, providing crucial insights into their roles in liver physiology and pathology (Figure ). In the developing liver, the embryonic origin(s) of HSC still remain(s) controversial, but studies indicate that septum transversum mesenchyme likely gives rise to most HSCs. In healthy young adult livers, HSCs were originally believed to be a homogenous cell population. However, recent scRNA-seq studies have unveiled transcriptome-distinct subpopulations: portal vein-associated HSCs (PaHSCs) and central vein-associated HSCs (CaHSCs). The functional significance of these subpopulations appears intricately linked to the location of liver injury. In aging livers, HSCs exhibit a distinctive phenotype, characterized by markers of quiescence (eg, lipid accumulation), senescence (eg, p16 expression), and activation (eg, αSMA induction). Liver injury triggers quiescent HSCs to transdifferentiate into MFBs. Contrary to the earlier notion of homogeneity, activated HSCs are now recognized to form distinct transcriptome-based clusters with varying phenotypes, including fibrogenic, intermediate activated, proliferative, inflammatory, vasoactive, contractile, and migratory states. The regulation of this plasticity and its impact on the progression or regression of liver fibrosis remain focal points of ongoing research. Emerging evidence suggests multiple fates for activated HSCs: reversion to an inactivated state, senescence, or elimination through cell death pathways. The functional consequences of these outcomes are diverse and significant. For instance, senescent HSCs, while limiting fibrosis, may inadvertently promote tumorigenesis due to their proinflammatory secretomes. Inactivated HSCs, remaining “primed” for activation, might impede recovery from liver injury. The resolution of liver fibrosis has been associated with both HSC apoptosis and ferroptosis, though the predominant mechanisms during fibrosis regression are yet to be fully elucidated. Recent studies leveraging scRNA-seq analysis have also opened new therapeutic avenues. For instance, targeting specific HSC subsets, such as using immunotoxin-specific strategies (eg, senolytic CAR T cells or natural killer cells against senescent HSCs) or focusing on specific signaling pathways in newly identified HSC subsets (eg, HGF signaling in cyHSCs), has been shown to hold promise for more precise and effective treatment of liver disease.
 While the adoption of single-cell methodologies has been transformative, it is essential to acknowledge certain caveats associated with the interpretation of such data. From a technical standpoint, it’s important to note that the scRNA-seq approach to analyze HSCs and other nonparenchymal cells might underestimate their numbers since the enzymatic digestion of liver tissue and cell isolation techniques favor the recovery of other cell types. In addition, the perceived degree of cellular heterogeneity is notably influenced by arbitrary cutoffs within the informatics pipeline and the actual number of cell type subclusters. Another common limitation in current scRNA-seq studies is the characterization of HSCs at a single time point, which may not fully capture the dynamic transformations occurring throughout their lifespan and during different stages of liver disease progression or regression. Moreover, few studies have delved into the molecular mechanisms driving these dynamic changes, the interactions between different HSC subsets, and their communication with neighboring cell types to influence the local microenvironment. Addressing these questions is crucial for developing targeted interventions that can alter the abundance and phenotype of the entire population with HSC, thereby influencing overall liver pathophysiology. Additionally, the translatability of findings from rodent models to the human liver, both in health and disease, remains an open question.
Advancements in other transcriptomics approaches, such as single-nuclei RNA-sequencing (snRNA-seq) and spatial transcriptomics RNA-seq, are evolving and poised to offer more comprehensive insights into HSC pathobiology. , snRNA-seq bypasses the cell dissociation step, thus improving recovery of nonparenchymal cells, including HSCs. For example, a head-to-head comparison of snRNA-seq and scRNA-seq by Andrews et al demonstrated that snRNA-seq captured 50% more cholangiocytes and hepatic mesenchymal cells than scRNA‐seq. Notably, most of the differentially expressed genes in the snRNA‐seq data were not identified as significant in the scRNA‐seq samples, highlighting the importance of obtaining sufficient numbers of liver nonparenchymal cell transcriptomes to demonstrate mesenchymal cell heterogeneity. Moreover, by integrating scRNA-seq, snRNA-seq, and spatial RNA-seq, this study provided a comprehensive and high-resolution map of multiple cell populations in the healthy human liver. Another advantage of the snRNA-seq approach is that it enables gene expression analysis from archived cryopreserved or formalin-fixed tissues, a capability not achievable with scRNA-seq. However, snRNA-seq also has limitations because analysis is restricted to nuclei-localized RNAs and thus, does not account for changes in gene expression that are mediated by post-transcriptional mechanisms that may occur in other cell compartments. Traditional morphology-based methods, such as immunohistochemistry and fluorescent in situ hybridization can help to validate changes in nuclei transcriptomes but typically focus on a limited number of candidate markers. To address these limitations, spatial transcriptomics RNA-sequencing has been developed using barcoding technology to capture RNA information through in situ sequencing. Recently, Friedman group combined the snRNA-seq approach with spatial localization technologies to unravel intricate autocrine HSC signaling and cell-cell interactions among HSCs, cholangiocytes, and endothelial cells in MASH livers. In both human and murine MASH, novel profibrotic autocrine signaling loops involving numerous receptor-ligand pairs became particularly prominent in HSCs during late-stage disease. The authors showed that disrupting one such autocrine interaction inhibited culture-related activation of human HSCs and reversed liver fibrosis in a murine model of MASH. Their findings provide robust proof of concept that cell profiling and omics analyses can be leveraged to develop novel interventions that precisely target-specific subpopulations with HSC that drive liver disease progression without compromising the functions of other populations with HSC critical for maintaining liver homeostasis or regenerating healthy hepatic parenchyma after injury.
