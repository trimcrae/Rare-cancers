## Abstract

## Background
Mesenchymal stromal cells (MSCs) are a population of undifferentiated, non-hematopoietic fibroblast-like cells isolated from several tissues with multipotent differentiation capacity in vitro. This study focused on the establishment and characterization of a mesenchymal stromal cell line derived from ovine peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs). 

## Methods
MSCs were isolated from ovine blood and used to establish a continuous cell line. Several assays were performed to characterize this cell line and confirm its mesenchymal origin. First, cells were characterized by flow cytometry (FACS) using monoclonal antibodies specific for MSCs and hematopoietic non-stromal cell surface antigens. Second, real-time quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR) was performed using gene primer pairs specific for hematopoietic and mesenchymal cell surface markers. Third, we evaluated the potential of this cell line to differentiate in vitro into other lineages such as osteoblasts and neurons, which was confirmed by specific staining with Alizarin Red (AR), RT-qPCR and immunofluorescence (IFA) assays. Finally, we tested the susceptibility of this ovine cell line to diverse livestock, animal and human viruses. 

## Results
FACS and RT-qPCR analyses revealed that the ovine cell line expressed mesenchymal markers, and was negative for hematopoietic markers. In addition, our ovine cell line was able to differentiate into osteoblasts and neurons, as we observed via quantitative analyses of AR staining, RT-qPCR and IFA. Importantly, the ovine cell line was susceptible to infection by all the viruses tested, as confirmed by IFA and viral growth kinetics assays. 

## Conclusion
In this work, we isolated MSCs from ovine peripheral blood and established a continuous ovine cell line. The thorough characterization conducted as well as the osteogenic and neuronal differentiation capacity of these cells confirmed that the ovine cell line had a mesenchymal stromal origin. Interestingly, the newly established ovine cell line was permissive to all viruses studied in this work, suggesting its potential as a valuable tool for isolating and characterizing viruses and studying virus-host interactions.

## Introduction
Virus isolation is still used as the most specific method for diagnosis to identify the viral agent on infected samples in veterinarian laboratories when results derived from molecular testing or serology are unavailable or equivocal [ ]. Primary cells of bovine, chicken, ovine or porcine origin or continuous cell lines are used as tools to isolate viruses from biological samples or to produce large-scale antigens by diagnostic laboratories and veterinary pharmaceutical industries, respectively [ ]. Primary cell cultures are difficult, expensive and time-consuming to produce, causing a delay in diagnosis. In the case of immortal cell lines, the main disadvantage to consider is their lower susceptibility to infection, especially when their origin species is different from the viral host range, which could lead to underestimation of possible positive cases, increasing the number of sick animals and the risk of an outbreak. These continuous cell lines are also used to generate inactivated virus preparations used for vaccination purposes. Therefore, optimal viral growth is key to reduce costs related to vaccine production. The most common immortalized cell lines used for this purpose are Vero cells, derived from the kidney of an African green monkey; BHK-21 cells, derived from baby hamster kidney fibroblasts; and Madin-Darby Canine Kidney (MDCK) epithelial cells [ , ].
Mesenchymal stromal cells (MSCs) are a population of undifferentiated non-hematopoietic fibroblast-like cells with multipotent differentiation capacity that are isolated in vitro from several tissues (blood, adipose tissue, fetal liver, placenta, bone marrow, and umbilical cord tissue) and from various animal species [ ]. Sheep and goats are commonly used as animal models for bone tissue engineering to test the potential of MSCs for bone regeneration [ ]. When cultured under standard conditions MSCs have the ability to proliferate; adhere to plastic surfaces; express a certain panel of phenotypic markers; and differentiate into osteogenic, chondrogenic and adipogenic lineages and other cell types like neuron-like cells, astrocytes or glial cells and corneal tissues when cultured in specific induction media [ , – ]. Owing to their multidifferentiation potential and proliferative capabilities, they are potential candidates for cartilage and bone tissue engineering and are used in regenerative medicine to generate muscle or bones or as tools to regenerate damaged tissues [ , ]. In addition, immortalized MSCs lines can be helpful for the development of in vitro models for bone disease and regeneration studies [ – ]. Other studies have shown the therapeutic effects of MSCs, which are able to secrete active soluble substances to deliver immunomodulatory, antiapoptotic, angiogenic, and antioxidative effects [ ].
Previous works have reported the isolation of MSCs from the peripheral blood of different species including rabbits, mice, rats, horses, dogs, humans and chickens [ – ]. This procedure has many advantages, including that it is less invasive, painful, and quicker, and avoids other steps such as enzymatic digestion or disaggregation to remove other contaminant tissues prior to the purification of MSCs, allowing growth under standard tissue culture conditions [ ].
Other studies have focused on virus-MSCs interactions, although there is limited information about the use of MSCs in infectious disease research [ – ]. In particular, little is known about the susceptibility of MSCs to viral infection and their antiviral properties, such as the upregulation of their antiviral mechanisms and the effects on cell-based immune response [ ]. In this work, we established a continuous ovine cell line derived from MSCs. Furthermore, we studied the susceptibility of the newly established ovine cell line to a panel of relevant viruses causing infectious diseases.

## Materials and methods

## Animals
All experiments with sheep were performed at INIA-CISA following EC guidelines [Directive 86/609) upon approval by the Ethical Review and Animal Care Committees of INIA and Comunidad de Madrid (authorization decision PROEX 192/17). This study was conducted in accordance with the ARRIVE guidelines 2.0 to ensure the transparency and reproducibility of the animal research.

## MSCs isolation and generation
Peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMC) were obtained from a 9 month-old female lamb (Castellana breed). Blood samples were collected from the jugular vein and immediately processed for PBMC isolation. Briefly, 20 ml of blood was diluted in 1 volume of PBS (phosphate-buffered saline buffer, Sigma), carefully layered over Ficoll-Paque PLUS (GE Healthcare) at a 1:1 ratio and centrifuged at 400xg for 30–40 min at 18–20 °C. The mononuclear fraction was taken and rinsed twice in PBS by centrifugation for 5 min at 1600xg. Finally, the cells were resuspended in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s Medium (DMEM; Lonza, Spain) medium supplemented with 20% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Spain) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (P/S; Gibco) and maintained at 37 °C in 5% CO2.
The cells were initially seeded in 96-well tissue culture plates (Costar) in complete DMEM with 20% FBS at 37 °C with 5% CO2. The medium was replaced every 3–4 days and non-adherent cells were removed. When the cells reached 80% confluence, they were detached with 0.25% Trypsin-EDTA (Sigma) and subcultured at a 1:2 ratio. Once the culture was expanded and the growth rate stabilized, the percentage of FBS was reduced to 10%. The new cell line generated (called GeLo cell line or GeLo cells) was grown in chamber slides (Lab-Tek® Chamber Slide™ System-Nunc), fixed in ice-cold methanol, stained with May-Grünwald Stain and observed under an inverted light microscope.

## Cloning of GeLo cells
To ensure the culture homogeneity of the GeLo cell line, a method for cloning adherent mammalian cells was carried out [ ]. Very briefly, cells were trypsinized, counted and seeded at approximately 10,000 cells in a first well of a 96-well microplate. In the second well, we made a 1/10 dilution to prepare 1000 cells. This methodology was repeated in the following wells of the first row and of the first column of the 96-well plate until one cell per well was obtained. Finally, 100 µl of complete 20%FBS-DMEM were added to the wells and with a multichannel pipet, we took 100 µl of the wells of the first row/column and passed to the wells of second row filled with 100 µl of medium (1/2 dilution). We continue passing the same volume until the last row/column. Finally, we incubated the plate at 37 °C in a CO2 incubator in 8% CO2-in-air with 98% relative humidity and checked the wells under an inverted light microscope to select those wells where there was only one cell. Once the cells of these wells reached 80% confluence, they were detached with 0.25% Trypsin-EDTA (Sigma) and subcultured. We obtained three different clones that were analysed via FACS to determine the presence and percentage of different mesenchymal markers present on their surface. Finally, both the GeLo cell line and ovine bone marrow stromal cells (oBMSCs) were used for morphological, phenotypical and differentiation culture studies.

## Culture of ovine bone marrow stromal cell line
Primary culture of oBMSCs was employed in this study as a control for MSCs cells. oBMSCs were cultured in complete 20%FBS-DMEM at 37° C in a humidified atmosphere with 5% CO2. The culture medium was then replaced every 3 days; when the percentage of oBMSCs reached 80%, subculturing was performed for cell expansion using Trypsin-EDTA as mentioned above.

## Phenotypic characterization via flow cytometry
The GeLo cell line was trypsinized, washed and analysed by FACS. A total of 2 × 105 cells were transferred to FACs polypropylene tubes (NUNCTM, VWR International, Denmark). The optimal amounts of monoclonal antibodies (mAbs) were determined and added to each tube for 40 min at 4 °C in darkness (for more detailed description of the dilutions used in this assay, please see Table  in supplementary material). The antibodies used are characteristic of markers associated with mesenchymal and hematopoietic lineages, some of which are specific to humans and others present cross-reactivity with bovine and ovine lineages and are listed in Table  . A control tube for each of the chromogens used contained equivalent amounts of isotype standards. A minimum of 1 × 104 cell events per assay were acquired using a FACsCalibur flow cytometer (BD Biosciences, Spain). The data were analysed via Cell Quest software (BD Biosciences), and the results are expressed as the means of the positive percentages.
 

## Osteogenic differentiation of GeLo cells and oBMSCs
The GeLo cell line and oBMSCs were differentiated toward the osteoblast lineage. Differentiation experiments were carried out under normoxic conditions (i.e., a humidified atmosphere with 5% CO2 at 37 °C). The GeLo cell line and oBMSCs were detached via trypsin-EDTA and seeded at 2 × 104 cells/well in a chamber slide (BD Falcon, France) for histology. Osteogenesis was induced by culture for 21 days with a StemPro® Osteogenesis Differentiation Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific) following the manufacturer’s instructions.
The culture media was changed every 2–3 days. Osteogenic differentiation was compared to that of a control consisting of oBMSCs cultured for the same period of time in 20%FBS-DMEM. Staining techniques were carried out to confirm differentiation. For this purpose, the cells were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde and stained with Alizarin Red (AR, Sigma). For osteogenesis evaluation, differentiation was analysed via AR staining to assess the presence of calcium deposits.

## Neuronal differentiation of the GeLo cell line
The GeLo cell line was chemically induced to become neuron-like cells in vitro via the addition of 2.4 mg/ml tetramethylpyrazine (TMP, Sigma) in DMEM without FBS. After 24 h of incubation, the cells exhibited morphological changes similar to those of neurons and were visualized via phase-contrast microscopy. A monolayer of differentiated cells was collected to isolate Poli A + mRNA, which was used to amplify specific neurogenic markers (Table  ) expressed by neurons via RT-qPCR following the manufacturer´s instructions (Qiagen).
 

## RNA extraction, reverse transcription and real-time polymerase chain reaction (RTqPCR)
Poli A + mRNA from the GeLo cell line non and differentiated with TMP were isolated from total RNA via the Oligotex Direct mRNA minikit (Qiagen) and were reverse-transcribed into cDNA using Superscript IV reverse transcriptase (according to the manufacturer’s instructions, Invitrogen) in a 2720 Thermal Cycler (Applied System). RTqPCR were performed using 1 µg of cDNA and SsoFast EvaGreen Supermix (Bio-Rad) in the Illumina Eco Real-Time PCR system (PCR-Max Limited, United Kingdom). The specific gene primer pair sequences for mesenchymal and neuronal markers and those specific for hematopoietic markers employed are described in Tables and , respectively. Reference genes such as GAPDH (glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase), β-actin or vimentin, were used to analyse and normalize the expression levels. For differentiated cells, TUBB3 was used to normalize the expression levels. The thermal profile used consisted of heating (95 °C/5 s), followed by a stage of cooling (60 °C/5 s) for 35 cycles and a melting stage of 95 °C/5 s, 65 °C/5 s and 95 °C 5 s. Data analysis was performed in triplicate and relative expression was calculated by comparing the levels of expression of the gene of interest against the levels of expression of an internal control gene, in this case vimentin. The formula used was ΔCT = CT(a target gene) − CT(a reference gene) [ ].
 

## Viral infection assays and viral kinetics
GeLo cells were used to study their susceptibility to infection with several viruses causing disease in ruminants, including epizootic hemorrhagic disease virus (EHDV), which is responsible for a recent outbreak in Spain and Europe [ ], and other viruses that affect animals as well as humans, such as Rift Valley fever virus (RVFV) and West Nile virus (WNV) (Table ). Additionally, we assayed whether the viral vaccine vector modified vaccinia virus Ankara (MVA) [ ] was able to infect the GeLo cell line with the purpose of adapting the viral vector to ovine cells and improving its potential as a vaccine vector in ruminants. For the infection assay, the GeLo cell line was infected at two multiplicity of infection (MOI) 0.1 and 0.01, and incubated for one hour at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 and 95% humidity atmosphere in a cell incubator. After 1 h of viral adsorption, the cells were supplemented with fresh medium containing 2% FBS and then maintained at 37 °C. The cells were observed every 24 h under a phase-contrast inverted microscope to detect the cytopathic effect (CPE). In the case that we observed CPE, the supernatant was collected and titrated; to this end, Vero cells were seeded in 24-well plates and inoculated at ten-fold serial dilutions in DMEM supplemented with 2% FBS. After 1 h of incubation at 37 °C, the inoculum was removed and the cells were overlaid with semi-solid medium (Eagle’s minimum essential medium (EMEM)−1% carboxymethylcellulose) and further incubated for 3 days at 37 °C to allow plaque development. Afterwards, the semi-solid overlay was poured off with water and the monolayer was fixed with 10% formaldehyde and stained with 2% crystal violet. Viral titers were calculated from the plaque numbers obtained for each dilution.
 Once, the viral infection was confirmed with all viruses from Table , a growth kinetics assay was carried out to compare the virus production in the newly established cell line with that in common cell lines used for this purpose, such as Vero cells or BHK-21 cells. For this purpose, cells seeded in 24-well plates at a density of 4 × 105 cells/mL in 2% DMEM the day before were infected with the different viruses mentioned above at MOIs of 0.1 or 0.5, with the exception of the WNV strain New York with a MOI of 0.01 due to its fast growth. A different assay was employed with influenza A virus as described previously [ ]. The supernatants were collected at 24, 48 and 72 h post infection, with the exception of FMDV, since it grows faster than the other viruses do, to determine the infection kinetics of each virus in two cell lines, the traditional growth cell line and GeLo cells. Finally, the titer of each supernatant was assayed in Vero cells via a plaque-assay as described previously.

## Immunofluorescence and confocal microscopy
GeLo cells were infected at an MOI of 1 in glass slides (15 mm diameter, Fisher Scientific). After 24 h, the cells were fixed with 10% paraformaldehyde for 15 min, washed with PBS and blocked with 20% FBS in PBS (blocking and dilution buffer) for 1 hat RT. Next, the cells were incubated with anti-sera specific for each virus obtained from experimentally infected mice, rabbits or sheep and naturally infected sheep in the case of EHDV. After two washes with dilution buffer, the cells were incubated in the same buffer with a secondary antibody conjugated with Alexa-488 specific for mouse or sheep immunoglobulins G (IgG) for 1 h at 37 °C. The slides samples were examined via a confocal microscopy using a Zeiss LSM880 confocal laser microscope (Gmbn).

## Cytokine analysis in GeLo cell cultures
GeLo cell cultures were treated with phytohemagglutinin (PHA Sigma, positive control) or infected with different DNA or RNA viruses for 24 h. Then, the supernatants were collected and used in the assays. Cytokine levels were analysed via a multiplex fluorescent bead immunoassay (MILLIPLEX Mouse Cytokine Kit, Burlington, MA, USA) for the quantitative detection of ovine cytokines (IL-1β, IL-4, IL-6 and IL-8) following the manufacturer’s instructions. The samples were analysed with a MAGPIX system (Luminex Corporation, Austin, TX, USA), and the data were collected via xPonent version 3.1 software. The cytokine concentrations (pg/ml) were calculated using standard curves. Values that fell below the level of detection of the assay were assigned as the lowest detectable concentration.

## Results

## MSCs isolation, culture and morphology
MSCs were selected by plastic adherence as described previously [ ]. The GeLo cell line cultured in vitro, and observed via a phase-contrast inverted microscope (Nikon TMS) presented a homogenous population with a typical spindle-shaped morphology with polygonal shapes. The cells presented an irregular cytoplasm with numerous branches and oval nuclei with prominent nucleoli and fine chromatin characteristic of the MSCs (Fig. a).
 To analyse their morphology in greater detail, GeLo cells were stained with May-Grünwald Giemsa Stain. After staining, we observed a pink nucleus with two nucleoli, the nuclear DNA and chromatin purple, with the cytoplasm stained blue (Fig.  b). The GeLo cell line presented high cellular activity with numerous cells undergoing cellular division (mitosis).

## Phenotypic characterization
Immunophenotypic analysis was carried out to test the expression of mesenchymal and hematopoietic markers in the newly established GeLo cell line. The cells were incubated with a panel of antibodies specific for MSCs (CD29, CD44, CD73, CD90, CD105 and CD166) and hematopoietic cells (CD45). These antibodies are specific for sheep or other species, such as humans, mice or rats, but are cross-reactive with ovine cells (Table  ).
By FACS analysis, we observed that GeLo cells were positive for MSCs markers such as CD29, CD44 and CD166 and negative for CD45, CD73, CD90 and CD105 (Fig.  ). At the same time, the GeLo cell line was cloned, and the percentage of cells positive for different MSCs markers was also assessed. Following cloning, the GeLo cell line presented values between 66% and 97% of the various MSCs surface markers employed, whereas the corresponding values obtained before cloning were between 26% and 28% (Supplementary Fig. 1). These findings confirmed the success of the cloning assay (Fig.  ).
 

## Osteogenic differentiation
To study the potential of the GeLo cell line to differentiate to into other cell lineages, we first analysed its capacity to differentiate into osteoblasts. To that end, we incubated GeLo cells with a StemPro® Osteogenesis Differentiation Kit and evaluated the newly formed calcium deposits by staining with Alizarin Red (AR) at 28 days postdifferentiation. The GeLo cell line exhibited nodule-like aggregations stained in red when maintained in osteogenic medium, whereas the cells maintained in both 20% FBS DMEM and the oBMSCs used as a control (Fig.  A) showed faint staining or no deposits. These results were confirmed via quantitative analyses of AR staining using ImageJ 1.48v (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, USA) (Fig.  B).
 

## Neuronal differentiation and analysis of the expression of gene markers
We also studied the potential of the GeLo cell line to differentiate into neuron-like cells. After 24 h in culture with TMP, the morphology of the cells drastically changed. The neuron-like phenotype was confirmed via: (i) morphological analysis (Fig.  A); (ii) immunofluorescence with a neuronal-specific protein/marker namely β-tubulin III (a microtubule structural marker expressed during the earliest phases of neuronal differentiation), (Fig.  B); and (iii) the expression of different neuronal markers (βTUBIII, NELF, NES andMAP2) by RT-qPCR (Fig.  D).
 The GeLo cell line showed a reduction in size, with a rounded bright nucleus with fine and long multipolar extensions and branching end brands (Fig.  A). To confirm the neuronal differentiation of GeLo cells, an immunofluorescence assay was performed to identify the expression of neural specific markers via the use of specific mAbs to detect β-tubulin III and vimentin (type III intermediate filament protein expressed in mesenchymal cells). Many of the differentiated GeLo cells expressed the neuronal markers β-tubulin III (red) or vimentin (green) and some of them coexpressed both proteins (merged in yellow) (Fig.  B). These results confirmed the potential of the GeLo cell line to differentiate in vitro into neuron‑like cells in the presence of TMP.
We subsequently analysed the expression of gene markers before and after the differentiation of GeLo cells via RT-qPCR of each target gene (Tables  and ). Vimentin was used as the reference gene on the basis of the results obtained by RT-qPCR with different housekeeping genes (HK). This gene showed stable expression compared with the variability or lack of expression of HK genes (GAPDH or β-actin) after several rounds. The results obtained with mesenchymal primers revealed that the GeLo cell line efficiently expressed CD29, CD44, CD73 and CD166, with the CD29 gene being the most highly expressed and the CD166 gene the least highly expressed, confirming the mesenchymal origin/lineage of GeLo cells (Fig.  C). In addition, several markers specific to neuronal cells (βTUBIII, NELF, NES andMAP2) were used by RT-qPCR in the GeLo cell line after TMP differentiation. Compared with undifferentiated GeLo cells, which do not express any of those markers, differentiated GeLo cells expressed the neuronal markers NELF, NES and MAP2 at very similar levels (Fig.  D).

## Detection of viral antigens in GeLo cells by Immunofluorescence
We wondered whether GeLo cells were susceptible to infection with different viruses, including zoonotic viruses or those whose host range is restricted to ruminants (see Table  ). To that end, GeLo cells were infected with RVFV, AkV, BTV-4, MVA, AHSV-4, EHDV-8, TosV, FMDV, SBV, PPRV, WNV and IAV and CPE was monitored. To confirm the presence of the viral antigens in infected cells, indirect immunofluorescence (IFA) assays were performed using specific sera or monoclonal antibodies against each of the viruses tested. We confirmed the efficient expression of viral antigens in the cytoplasm and nucleus of GeLo cells. Protein expression was detectable at 24 h.p.i. (Fig.  ). Taken together, these results confirmed that the GeLo cell line is susceptible to viral infection.
 

## Susceptibility of the GeLo cell line to viral infection
The growth kinetics of various viruses were monitored to confirm infection and their ability to replicate in GeLo cells. GeLo cells were susceptible to infection with all these viruses after 48 h from the first passage. For the Schmallenberg virus, one blind passage was necessary to obtain the infectious virus in the following passage. Syncytium formation during PPRV infection was observed after GeLo cells were cultured for 4 days (Supplementary Fig. 2) confirming the success of viral infection. A growth kinetics assay was also performed to compare virus production in the GeLo cell line with that in other cell lines commonly used to produce viruses, such as Vero cells and BHK-21 cells. The results showed similar curves in most cases, with the GeLo cell production being lower during the first 24 h and obtaining values similar to those of Vero or BHK-21 cells at the next time points, apart from MVA where values were significantly lower in GeLo cells at all-time points. Despite productive infection occurring in GeLo cells, FMDV and SBV virus growth rates were lower than those observed in BHK-21 and Vero cells, respectively (Fig.  ). The variation observed after 72 h of RVFV infection was associated with the state of the infected monolayer after three days in culture.
 

## Cytokine analysis in GeLo cell cultures
As mentioned above, MSCs have anti-inflammatory and immunomodulatory potential since they can regulate the secretion of pro-inflammatory and anti-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-1βand IL-6, respectively. During the beginning of infection, MSCs are activated by viral antigens eliciting a strong immune response by producing pro-inflammatory cytokines that enhance the antiviral properties of immune cells [ ]. Thus, we investigated whether GeLo cells elicit immunomodulatory responses by secreting cytokines with antiviral properties such as IL-1β, IL-4, IL-6 and IL-8. To this end, we performed infection assays with RNA or DNA viruses in GeLo cells. We observed that supernatants from GeLo cells infected with DNA viruses had detectable levels of IL-1β, IL-4, IL-6 and IL-8. In contrast, we did not detect IL-1β in supernatants from GeLo cells infected with RNA viruses. However, detectable levels of IL-4, IL-6 and IL-8 were measured in the supernatants of GeLo cells infected with RNA viruses, although the IL-4 levels were lower than those observed after infection with DNA viruses (Fig. ).
 

## Discussion
MSCs can be isolated from different tissues, such as bone marrow, adipose tissue, the umbilical cord or peripheral blood from different species [ , , ]. Human MSCs are the best characterized, and the International Society for Cellular Therapy has proposed minimal criteria for defining these types of cells [ ]. First, MSCs should adhere to plastic. Second, MSCs should express surface markers of the mesenchymal stromal cells CD105, CD73 and CD90 and should not express CD45, CD34, CD14 or CD11b, CD79a or CD19 and HLA-DR. Third, MSCs should be able to differentiate into osteoblasts, adipocytes and chondroblasts in vitro [ – ]. The GeLo cell line was characterized following the same criteria as those for human MSCs. In this study, fibroblast-like cells were isolated from peripheral blood by adherence to plastic. Several rounds of washing were conducted to remove non-adherent cells while they were maintained in 20% FBS DMEM. Once the culture was stabilized, these cells were expanded, and different assays were performed to characterize them. The expression of mesenchymal markers and the absence of hematopoietic markers were analysed and confirmed by flow cytometry and RT-qPCR. The ability to differentiate into osteoblasts or neurons was carried out by in vitro culture and confirmed by RT-qPCR and immunofluorescence. Moreover, we studied the susceptibility of the newly established ovine mesenchymal stromal cell line to a panel of relevant viruses that cause infectious diseases.
The newly established ovine cell line (called the GeLo cell line) presented some cell type heterogeneity in culture. To address this issue, adherent cells were successfully cloned by limiting dilution [ ]. Several clones were obtained and the immunophenotypic characterization was carried out via FACS. Despite the importance of sheep as a large animal model for many studies, the characterization of ovine MSCs (oMSCs) is still limited. A major limitation is the absence of specific ovine reagents and scarce data on surface markers present in oMSCs. MSCs have principally been isolated from human bone marrow, and are well characterized by the expression of specific surface markers. We used a total of eight mAbs, some of which were specific to humans, and others presented cross-reactivity with bovines and ovines (Table ) [ ]. Notably, the GeLo cell line expressed three MSC markers (CD29, CD44 and CD166) and none of the hematopoietic cell markers. The negative results obtained for several mesenchymal surface markers, such as CD45, CD73, CD90 and CD105, could be due to the absence of reactivity of these mAbs in sheep since many of these antibodies are specifically designed or optimized for humans. Even isolation and analysis methods could influence the detection of these markers. For example, the culture medium or oxygen availability could modify the percentage and type of cells obtained [ , , , , ], and a less sensible analysis method might not detect markers with a low level of expression. Another possibility is the absence of these markers in oMSCs from blood since the expression of these markers can vary depending on the tissue of origin. Furthermore, MSCs are not homogenous, as not all cells within a population uniformly express CD73, CD90 and CD105. A subpopulation of MSCs may present a low expression of these markers or not express them at all. On the other hand, controversy exists among the results because, depending on the species where MSCs have been isolated, they can express or not express the markers described above. Some studies have identified markers in other species that are not characteristic of hMSCs [ , ]; thus, there might be specific proteins that perform these functions in sheep. It is clear that several factors might cause the lack of expression of CD73, CD90 and CD105, which implies that these cells are not necessarily MSCs, as long as a functional evaluation is needed, and that neuronal/osteogenic differentiation is performed as we have conducted for the GeLo cell line. In our experience, we were not able to detect CD73 by FACS but we obtained CD73 transcripts by RT qPCR, which confirmed its expression. A possible explanation for CD73 is that the mAbs used were clones that displayed variable degrees of binding affinity and in our assays, the mAbs used did not work. Another variable is the method used to detach the cells from the tissue culture plastic which can digest some cell-surface markers, leading to their loss or reduced detection during FACS [ , , ].
We also analysed the expression of mesenchymal and hematopoietic markers in GeLo cells by RT-qPCR. We used a list of ten pairs of primers specific for ovine or bovine surface markers. There was no amplification of the hematopoietic markers CD45, CD31 or CD34, which coincides with the results obtained by FACS. We confirmed the expression of CD29, CD44 and CD166 and detected transcripts of CD73 by RT-qPCR in the GeLo cell line. Owing to the poor availability of ovine reagents, RT-qPCR could be an interesting alternative for characterizing the gene expression of surface markers in MSCs [ , ].
The second goal of this work was to carry out osteogenic and neuronal differentiation to demonstrate the ability of the GeLo cell line to differentiate into other lineages typical of MSCs. Differentiation into osteoblasts was demonstrated using AR. Nodule-like aggregations stained in red appeared in the osteogenic media on the 21 st day of culture, indicating that these cultures were mineralized at a late stage. These results are compatible with previously published studies [ , , ].
We also carried out neuronal differentiation in the presence of TMP. After 24 h, the GeLo cell morphology was drastically modified. We confirmed differentiation by RT-qPCR using specific primers for neuronal markers. We detected expression profiles of neurogenic markers (MAP2, NELF, NES and TUBB3) that are consistent with neural differentiation. TUBB3 was used to normalize the results obtained because of the variability obtained with other HK genes. We confirmed the results obtained by RT-qPCR by immunofluorescence. We observed the presence of vimentin and TUBB3 in GeLo cells differentiated into neurons. These results confirmed the potential of the GeLo cell line to differentiate into neuron‑like cells in vitro in the presence of TMP [ ].
Finally, we studied whether the GeLo cell line was susceptible to infection with a panel of relevant DNA and RNA viruses that cause infectious diseases in animals and/or humans. Interestingly, the results obtained by viral inoculation revealed that GeLo cells were susceptible to infection with all the viruses studied in this work. The capacity of these viruses to infect GeLo cells was determined by immunofluorescence to detect viral antigens with specific sera or mAbs (Fig. ), and the success of viral infection was confirmed by a growth kinetics assay (Fig. ). Previous studies have demonstrated that MSCs are susceptible to infection by a wide variety of RNA and DNA viruses both in vitro and in vivo. The capacity of viruses to enter and infect host MSCs may also be host species dependent [ ]. MSCs have many functional surface receptors that potentially enable viral entry. MSCs surface receptor expression and viral tropism may in part explain the susceptibility of MSCs to viral infection. The most common virus receptors are those molecules involved in cellular adhesion, for example, I-CAM1 which MSCs express and use for transmigration and immunomodulation or through the heparan sulfate receptor. MSCs are highly permissive to infection by herpesviruses, including Herpes Simplex-1 (HSV-1), Cytomegalovirus (CMV) and Varicella Zoster virus (VZV) [ ]. There are other factors such as antiviral signaling of cytokines, availability of intracellular host factors which supports viral RNA/DNA synthesis, and activation state of the cell involved in the viral tropism of MSCs. As mentioned previously, some studies have demonstrated the therapeutic effects of MSCs being able to secrete active soluble substances to induce immunomodulatory responses [ , ]. MSCs that are activated by viral antigens elicit strong immune responses by production of pro-inflammatory cytokines which enhance the antiviral properties of immune cells at early stages of infection [ ]. Therefore, we decided to assess whether GeLo cells are capable of inducing immunomodulatory responses by secreting cytokines with antiviral properties, such as IL-1B, IL-4, IL-6 and IL-8. For that purpose, we carried out infection assays with RNA or DNA viruses in GeLo cells.
For Il-6 and IL-8, the results revealed that GeLo cells display a differential profile of cytokines independent of the type of virus (Fig.  ). In both viral types, we observed high levels of IL-6 and IL-8. The increase in IL-6 was expected since MSCs can secrete various cytokines, such as IL-6, that have been reported to directly affect cell death pathways. The increase in IL-6 and IL-8 in the supernatants suggests that, when infected, GeLo cells might be involved in modulating the local and systemic immune response, which may contribute to resolution of the infection. The low or absent expression of IL-1β in the supernatant of DNA and RNA virus-infected GeLo cells may reflect modulation of the immune response, which may be beneficial in some contexts. IL-1β is a key pro-inflammatory cytokine involved in the early activation of the immune response and the induction of inflammation, but its absence or low levels may be beneficial in reducing excessive inflammation. In the case of viral infection, uncontrolled IL-1β production can lead to severe systemic inflammation, increasing the risk of tissue damage and associated complications. Reducing IL-1β could therefore contribute to a more controlled immune response and prevent chronic or harmful inflammatory damage. The higher levels of IL-4 in the supernatants of GeLo cells infected with DNA viruses than in those infected with RNA viruses may be due to differences in the ways in which these two types of viruses activate immune responses. Compared with RNA viruses, DNA viruses are generally recognized by TLR-9 in endosomes, triggering an immune response that may be less pro-inflammatory in its early phase. In addition, DNA virus infection often triggers a more rapid adaptive response, including activation of T and B lymphocytes, and may promote polarization. Thus, increased IL-4 production in cells infected with a DNA virus reflects an immune response that is more focused on activating the humoral response. These findings support the use of these cells as treatments for infectious diseases.
To conclude, one of the possible applications of the GeLo cell line may be related to the adaptation of viral vaccine vectors such as MVA [ ] or adenovirus with the purpose of adapting the viral vectors to ovine cells and improving their potential as vaccine candidates in ruminants. One example could be the MVA virus that we used in previous works to develop vaccines against RVFV and although the results obtained were promising, further optimization should be carried out [ , ]. Other applications include the isolation and characterization of viruses and virus-host interactions.

## Conclusions
In this work, we used isolated MSCs from peripheral blood to establish a new ovine cell line to use as a tool to isolate or produce viruses from the same origin as the host. The characterization and osteogenic and neuronal differentiation confirmed that the GeLo cell line had a mesenchymal stromal origin. In fact, the newly established ovine cell line was permissive to all the viruses studied in this work. The new sheep cell line could be a useful tool for isolating and characterizing viruses and studying virus-host interactions.
