## Abstract
Osteoarthritis (OA) is a challenging degenerative joint disease with limited treatment options. Subchondral bone plays a critical role in maintaining joint homeostasis and influencing OA progression. Here, we investigated the role of senescence in mesenchyme-derived stem/progenitor cells (MDSPCs) during OA progression, aiming to identify potential therapeutic targets. Histopathological evaluations and bioinformatic analyses of OA samples from both humans and mice revealed that EGFR+ MDSPCs and EREG+ macrophages constitute a senescent skeletal unit within the osteoarthritic articular subchondral bone. In vitro and in vivo experiments demonstrated that EREG promotes senescence and excessive osteogenesis in EGFR+ MDSPCs. Moreover, interference with Ereg expression, via adeno-associated virus–mediated Ereg knockdown or genetic knockout in mice, significantly suppressed senescence of EGFR+ MDSPCs in subchondral bone and alleviated both pathological sclerosis and pain in OA mice. Our findings indicate that MDSPC senescence in the subchondral bone is a key event driving OA progression, offering a valuable reference point to develop innovative therapeutic strategies for OA.

The EGFR+ MDSPCs and their associated senescent niche represent potential therapeutic targets for the treatment of osteoarthritis.

## INTRODUCTION
Osteoarthritis (OA) is a globally prevalent chronic degenerative joint disorder for which there are few effective treatments to halt disease progression ( , ). Patients with advanced OA experience limited mobility and reduced quality of life, eventually necessitating joint replacement surgery ( , ). With the global increase in aging populations and joint injuries, the number of individuals affected by OA continues to rise, generating increased demand for prevention and treatment strategies, and imposing notable burdens on public health systems and the global economy ( ). Therefore, a comprehensive understanding of the mechanisms underlying OA progression is essential for developing effective treatment strategies.
The classical pathological features of OA progression include degradation of the cartilage extracellular matrix (ECM), subchondral bone sclerosis, osteophyte formation, and chronic inflammation ( ). However, accumulating evidence suggests that subchondral bone lesions may precede the destruction of articular cartilage ( – ). While previous investigations have predominantly focused on the pathological roles of endothelial cells (ECs), synovial macrophages, synovial fibroblasts, osteoblasts (OBs), and osteoclasts (OCs) in OA pathogenesis ( ), increasing attention has been directed toward the role of mesenchyme-derived stem/progenitor cells (MDSPCs) in the subchondral bone during OA progression. Zhen et al. ( ) demonstrated that elevated levels of transforming growth factor–β1 in subchondral bone during OA progression in mice induce the formation of a Nestin+ mesenchymal stem cell (MSC) subset, whose enhanced osteogenic potential promotes excessive osteogenesis in subchondral bone, leading to subchondral bone marrow lesions. Ilas et al. ( ) reported that the number of NGFR+ MSCs in the subchondral bone of patients with OA significantly increases in regions of pathological bone formation, suggesting a role for NGFR+ MSCs in the abnormal remodeling of subchondral bone in OA. Notably, a previous study also demonstrated that targeted regulation of skeletal stem cells (SSCs) in subchondral bone can promote articular cartilage regeneration ( ), indicating that MDSPCs in the subchondral bone represent a potential therapeutic target for OA treatment. However, there is still a lack of comprehensive studies delineating the full spectrum of pathological alterations in MDSPC subpopulations within subchondral bone during OA progression and elucidating their mechanistic links to key pathological events in OA.
Senescent cells accumulate in mammalian tissues with age, contributing to organ dysfunction ( – ). Various factors, including aging and injury, can induce cellular senescence, thereby promoting OA progression ( – ). For instance, articular chondrocytes in OA become senescent, and these senescent chondrocytes release senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP) factors that promote ECM degradation and disrupt cartilage homeostasis ( ). In addition, SASP factors are also secreted by OBs and synovial fibroblasts during OA progression ( , ). Adult stem cells are among the longest-living proliferative cells in the body, making them more susceptible to accumulating DNA mutations and epigenetic alterations than differentiated cells and, thus, more prone to senescence ( , ). Nevertheless, whether MDSPCs in osteochondral tissues undergo senescence during OA and how these senescent cells contribute to OA progression remain to be fully elucidated.
Moreover, accumulating evidence reveals that cellular senescence exhibits considerable heterogeneity across different tissues ( , ), highlighting the importance of characterizing senescent cell heterogeneity to develop targeted senolytic therapies. Fortunately, advancements in high-throughput technologies such as single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) have enabled the identification of senescent cell heterogeneity at single-cell resolution ( , ), thereby offering an efficient approach to uncover senescent targets in osteoarthritic tissues.
Stem cells and their surrounding niches form unique regenerative units within various tissues that respond to environmental stimuli, including injury, and contribute to either tissue repair or pathological remodeling ( – ). Spatial transcriptomics (ST) has emerged as a powerful tool for characterizing the cellular and spatial heterogeneity of histological regions of interest ( – ) and may facilitate the identification of senescent cell units within osteoarthritic tissues.
In this study, we performed scRNA-seq and ST analyses of articular osteochondral tissues from patients with OA and observed that subchondral bone tissues exhibited more pronounced pathological features and markers of cellular senescence during OA progression. Through cell-cell communication analysis, functional experiments, and knockout (KO) mouse models, we identified a senescent skeletal unit in the subchondral bone consisting predominantly of epidermal growth factor receptor–positive (EGFR+) MDSPCs and epiregulin-positive (EREG+) macrophages. Within this unit, EREG played a pivotal role in driving the senescence of EGFR+ MDSPCs. Inhibition of EREG reduced subchondral bone damage and alleviated OA progression in a murine post-traumatic OA (PTOA) model. In summary, our findings reveal that the senescent skeletal unit and its associated senescence factor EREG constitute promising therapeutic targets for OA, offering a promising strategy for OA intervention.

## RESULTS

## scRNA-seq analysis of human OA samples reveals the major pathological cell population involved in OA progression
To fully elucidate the underlying mechanisms of OA progression, we performed histopathological evaluation and bioinformatic analysis of full-layer tibial plateau tissues [including cartilage, subchondral bone plate (SBP), and subchondral trabecular bone (STB)] from the I~II side (Outerbridge grade I~II) and III~IV side (Outerbridge grade III~IV) of samples from patients with OA ( ). Histopathological staining revealed that osteochondral tissues on the III~IV side exhibited more severe damage, characterized by extensive destruction of the articular cartilage and abnormal subchondral bone structure ( ). Consistently, histopathological scoring of each OA sample demonstrated that samples from the I~II side had lower Mankin scores and milder osteochondral damage, whereas samples from the III~IV side had higher Mankin scores and more severe osteochondral lesions ( ).
 To investigate the cellular subpopulations in the articular osteochondral tissues of patients with OA and to reveal the pathological events associated with OA progression, six osteochondral tissues were collected from the I~II and III~IV sides of tibial plateau samples from three patients with OA for scRNA-seq. In total, 38,991 single cells were obtained after data quality control and single-cell integration analysis, 20,364 from the I~II side tissues and 18,627 from the III~IV side tissues. Global gene expression analysis revealed that the III~IV side tissues expressed a greater number of pathological genes, such as SPARC, FN1, IGFBP5, and SERPINE2 ( ). Quantitative polymerase chain reaction analysis of samples from three patients with OA confirmed that the expression of these differentially expressed genes (DEGs) was significantly higher in the III~IV side tissues compared to that in the I~II side tissues ( ). These findings indicate that DEGs between the I~II and III~IV side tissues robustly reflect OA progression.
Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) visualization identified 12 distinct subpopulations ( ), all of which were present in each sample, albeit with slight variations in their proportions (fig. S1, A and B). MDSPC1 expressed the stromal stem cell marker NGFR and the mesenchymal marker FOXC2 ( , ); MDSPC2 expressed the mesenchymal progenitor marker FGFR1 and the mesenchymal marker EGFR ( , ); and MDSPC3 expressed the mesenchymal stromal cell marker TWIST1 and the stemness marker FZD7 ( , ). OBs expressed ALPL, RUNX2, and CDH11; chondrocytes expressed COL2A1 and ACAN; ECs expressed PECAM1 and CDH5; pericytes expressed RGS5 and ACTA2; T cells expressed CD3D and CD3E; B cells expressed CD79A and MS4A1; monocytes/macrophages expressed CD14; dendritic cells (DCs) expressed CD80 and CD86; and OCs expressed ACP5 and CTSK ( ). Differential gene expression analysis across clusters revealed that MDSPC2 and MDSPC3 exhibited enriched expression of DEGs associated with the III~IV side tissues (i.e., progression-associated OA signature genes) ( ). OA signature gene set scoring further showed that MDSPC2 and MDSPC3 exhibited pronounced pathological features characteristic of OA progression ( ). Moreover, there were significant differences in the pathological characteristics of MDSPC2 and MDSPC3 between the I~II and III~IV side tissues ( ). Together, these findings reveal cellular heterogeneity during OA progression and identify MDSPC2 and MDSPC3 as subpopulations with OA-associated pathological features.

## A senescent subpopulation exists in pathogenic MDSPC during OA progression
To further explore the pathogenic roles of the MDSPC subpopulations during OA progression, we characterized the biological features of the three MDSPC subsets ( ). Differential gene expression analysis revealed notable differences among these subsets ( ). Specifically, MDSPC1 [proliferative MDSPC (pro-MDSPC)] expressed the stemness genes KLF10 and HES1; MDSPC2 [5'-nucleotidase ecto–positive (NT5E+)MSC] expressed NT5E and ENG; and MDSPC3 [podoplanin–positive (PDPN+)SSC] expressed PDPN and CADM1 ( ). Gene Ontology (GO) analysis of DEGs in each cluster revealed that the pro-MDSPC population was associated with cell number homeostasis, mesenchyme development, and mesenchymal cell differentiation. The NT5E+ MSC population was associated with endoplasmic reticulum stress and responses to misfolded proteins, while the PDPN+ SSC population exhibited gene expression signatures related to calcification, mineralization, and ECM degradation ( ).
 We further investigated the relationships between these MDSPC subsets using RNA velocity and partition-based graph abstraction analyses. The results showed that both the NT5E+ MSC and PDPN+ SSC subsets originated from the pro-MDSPC population and were interconnected ( ). Correlation analysis revealed a particularly strong relationship between the NT5E+ MSC and PDPN+ SSC subsets ( ). Gene expression profiling along the RNA velocity trajectory from pro-MDSPC to NT5E+ MSC and PDPN+ SSC revealed that senescence-associated genes (PAPPA, IGFBP6, and SERPINE2) ( ) and osteogenesis-related genes (PRRX1, COL6A3, and COL5A2) were highly expressed at the terminal ends of the differentiation trajectory ( ). Additionally, senescence-related genes such as CTNNB1, PPP1CA, HMGA1, IGFBP4, MMP14, and MMP3 were enriched in both the NT5E+ MSC and PDPN+ SSC subsets ( ). Aging gene set scoring further confirmed that these two subsets exhibited strong senescence characteristics ( ).
Notably, the main senescence feature of the NT5E+ MSC subset was its inflammatory profile, while the PDPN+ SSC subset was primarily characterized by ECM degradation and abnormal collagen metabolism (fig. S2, A and B). Both subsets also showed a capacity for ossification (fig. S2B). Activation of TGF-β and WNT signaling pathways in the PDPN+ SSC population drove ECM remodeling (fig. S3, A to C), whereas interleukin-6 (IL-6) signaling in NT5E+ MSC promoted pro-inflammatory responses and exerted regulatory effects on PDPN+ SSC (fig. S3B). Furthermore, PDPN+ SSC influenced NT5E+ MSC primarily through the pro-osteogenic secretion signal of secreted phosphoprotein 1/osteopontin (SPP1) (fig. S3C). Despite their functional differences, there was no significant difference in aging scoring between the NT5E+ MSC and PDPN+ SSC subsets ( ).
Among the markers assessed, the expression range of EGFR best overlapped with that of both the NT5E+ MSC and PDPN+ SSC subsets ( and fig. S4). Therefore, we collectively designated these two subsets as “EGFR+ senescent MDSPC” ( ). EGFR+ senescent MDSPC from the III~IV side of OA samples exhibited elevated aging scores ( ) and significantly up-regulated expression of classical senescence genes CDKN1A and CDKN2A ( ), confirming that EGFR+ MDSPCs (i.e., the NT5E+ MSC and PDPN+ SSC subsets) undergo senescence during OA progression.

## EGFR+ senescent MDSPC mainly exists in the subchondral bone region during OA progression
Although our scRNA-seq data revealed EGFR+ MDSPCs in articular osteochondral tissues from patients with OA, it remained unclear whether the senescence of this subpopulation was associated with pathological lesions in the articular cartilage or the subchondral bone. Therefore, we explored ST data (GSE254844) of articular osteochondral tissues from healthy individuals and patients with OA ( ). OA signature gene scoring results revealed that the deep zone (DZ) of the articular osteochondral tissues, the region of the articular cartilage’s DZ contiguous with the calcified cartilage layer, exhibited the most pronounced OA-associated pathological features ( ). Additionally, the DZ displayed distinct senescence characteristics, expressing a wide range of senescence-related genes ( ). Notably, only in the DZ was the aging score higher in patients with OA than in healthy individuals ( ).
 Subsequent analysis showed that the EGFR gene was expressed in the articular surface, superficial zone, middle zone, and DZ of the articular osteochondral tissues ( ). However, elevated expression of EGFR in patients with OA compared to that in healthy individuals was observed only in the DZ ( ). Multiplex immunofluorescence staining further revealed a higher abundance of EGFR+ MDSPCs surrounding the STB in the III~IV side tissues, which exhibited a more pronounced senescence phenotype evidenced by increased expression of p16 and p21 ( ). These results indicate that the articular subchondral bone is the region with the most prominent pathological and senescence features during OA progression, wherein EGFR+ MDSPCs undergo senescence.

## EGFR+ senescent MDSPC has enhanced osteogenic ability and decreased self-renewal capacity
To elucidate the role of EGFR+ senescent MDSPC during OA progression, we conducted bioinformatics analysis and cellular functional experiments to examine biological differences in EGFR+ MDSPCs between the I~II side tissues and the III~IV side tissues. Differential gene expression analysis revealed that EGFR+ senescent MDSPC in the III~IV side tissues highly expressed osteogenesis-related genes such as COL5A2, BGN, and HTRA1 ( ) and senescence-associated genes including IGFBP4, TIMP1, and PTGES ( ) ( ). GO and gene set enrichment analysis further indicated that EGFR+ senescent MDSPC in the III~IV side tissues exhibited a senescent phenotype and a greater propensity for ossification ( ). Immunofluorescence staining for RUNX2 and SPP1 confirmed that EGFR+ MDSPCs around the subchondral trabeculae in the III~IV side tissues had an enhanced osteogenic phenotype ( ).
 We then performed adherent culture of MDSPCs (acMDSPCs) from subchondral bone tissue of the I~II and III~IV side OA tissues according to previously published protocols with minor modifications ( ). Flow cytometry analysis showed that both I~II-side and III~IV-side acMDSPC populations expressed markers of the three MDSPC subpopulations [PDPN, NT5E, and activated leukocyte cell adhesion molecule (ALCAM)], along with the senescent MDSPC marker EGFR (fig. S5A). The EGFR+ cell population included both NT5E+PDPN− and NT5E+PDPN+ subpopulations (fig. S5B), indicating that acMDSPCs retain key features of tissue-resident MDSPCs. Notably, a significant increase in the proportion of the EGFR+NT5E+PDPN+ subpopulation was observed in the III~IV side tissues (fig. S5C), suggesting that this subpopulation may play a predominant pathogenic role in advanced OA. Therefore, acMDSPCs isolated from the subchondral bone tissues of patients with OA on the I~II and III~IV sides were used for downstream functional assays. β-Galactosidase staining demonstrated that EGFR+ acMDSPCs from the III~IV side tissues had a more prominent senescence phenotype ( ). In osteogenesis assays, EGFR+ acMDSPCs from the I~II side tissues showed osteogenic differentiation and mineralization capabilities, whereas the EGFR+ acMDSPCs from the III~IV side tissues exhibited markedly enhanced osteogenesis ( ). Furthermore, the colony-forming unit fibroblast (CFU-F) assay indicated that EGFR+ acMDSPCs from the I~II side tissues had strong self-renewal capacity and effectively regulated their cell number to maintain homeostasis. In contrast, EGFR+ acMDSPCs from the III~IV side tissues displayed limited self-renewal ability and failed to form sizable cell colonies ( ). Given that the pathological features of subchondral bone in progressive OA include subchondral sclerosis ( ), these results suggest that EGFR+ senescent MDSPC contributes to OA progression through excessive osteogenesis.

## The senescent skeletal unit is mainly localized within the subchondral bone compartment adjacent to the trabecular bone during OA progression
Stem cells are located within a specific microenvironment called a “niche” that maintains and regulates their biological characteristics ( ). Together, stem cells and their niches constitute the regenerative unit, playing essential roles in tissue development, homeostatic maintenance, regeneration, and aging ( – ). To further investigate the cellular composition of the niche of EGFR+ senescent MDSPC, we performed cell-cell communication analysis, with a particular focus on EGFR+ senescent MDSPC. Based on the expression of the EGFR gene and the high-aging score zone ( ), the 12 cell subpopulations were reclassified, resulting in 11 cell subpopulations (including the EGFR+ senescent MDSPC) ( ). We subsequently analyzed the incoming and outgoing interaction signals for each subpopulation. The results showed that EGFR+ senescent MDSPC exhibited relatively stronger incoming interaction signals ( ).
 To explore the niche signals acting on EGFR+ senescent MDSPC, we analyzed the overall incoming signaling patterns of each subpopulation and found that the main incoming signal for EGFR+ senescent MDSPC was the epidermal growth factor (EGF) signaling pathway ( ). Signaling network analysis further revealed that EGFR+ senescent MDSPC received EGF signals originating from multiple cell types, including OBs, chondrocytes, ECs, MDSPC1 subclusters, DCs, and monocytes/macrophages ( ). Notably, pathway enrichment quantification indicated that monocytes/macrophages were the dominant cellular contributors to EGF pathway activation ( ). This secreted signaling axis identified monocyte/macrophage populations as critical niche components regulating EGFR+ senescent MDSPC via microenvironmental signaling modulation. Further receptor-ligand interaction analysis of the EGF pathway showed that the interaction signal strength of “EREG-EGFR” was greater than that of “heparin-binding EGF-like growth factor (HBEGF)–EGFR,” indicating that “EREG-EGFR” represented the principal receptor-ligand interaction pattern for EGFR+ senescent MDSPC ( ). We next examined the major cellular sources of the receptor EGFR and the ligand EREG in this signaling axis. Signaling pathway network and gene expression data revealed that EGFR was expressed in EGFR+ senescent MDSPC, while EREG was expressed in the monocyte/macrophage cluster, indicating that monocyte/macrophage-derived EREG specifically targeted EGFR+ senescent MDSPC ( ).
In addition, EGFR+ senescent MDSPC exhibited notable interactions with OBs, OCs, and ECs during OA progression. Signaling network analysis showed that EGFR+ senescent MDSPC primarily exerted outgoing signaling toward ECs while receiving incoming signals from OBs and OCs. These interactions were markedly enhanced in the III~IV side OA tissues (fig. S6, A and B). Detailed receptor-ligand pathway analysis revealed that both OBs and OCs secreted SPP1, which acted on EGFR+ senescent MDSPC and enhanced their osteogenic differentiation capacity (fig. S6C). Moreover, EGFR+ senescent MDSPC recruited ECs through CXCL12 signaling, thereby promoting angiogenesis (fig. S6D).
Given the specific role of monocyte/macrophage-derived EREG in regulating EGFR+ senescent MDSPC, we further dissected the cellular heterogeneity of monocyte/macrophage subsets. The monocyte/macrophage cluster was regrouped into four distinct subclusters: macrophage 1 (Ma1), macrophage 2 (Ma2), DCs, and OCs ( ). The DEGs of each cluster were used for functional annotation ( ). Ma1 was characterized by the expression of CD68, CD14, and TLR2; Ma2 expressed C1QA, FGFR1, and FGL2; DCs expressed CD86, CD80, and CCR7; and OCs expressed TRAP1, CTSK, and TNFRSF11A ( ). GO analysis of the DEGs revealed that Ma1 participated in chronic inflammatory responses and promoted the production of inflammatory cytokines such as interferon-γ and tumor necrosis factor; Ma2 primarily functioned in anti-inflammatory processes by inhibiting leukocyte activation and maintaining myeloid cell homeostasis; DCs exhibited robust major histocompatibility complex class II activity and antigen presentation capability; and OCs were involved in bone resorption ( ). Notably, Ma1 displayed biological features related to aging ( ) and specifically expressed senescence-associated genes ( and fig. S7A). The aging score analysis showed that the high-aging score region overlapped with the Ma1 cluster, which also specifically expressed EREG ( ). Thus, we defined Ma1 macrophages as EREG+ senescent macrophages. The senescence features of EREG+ macrophages differed between I~II and III~IV sides, with the III~IV side exhibiting significantly higher expression of the classical senescence gene CDKN1A (p21) (fig. S7B), although no significant difference was observed in CDKN2A (p16) expression (fig. S7C).
 We then validated the presence of the senescent skeletal unit composed of EGFR+ MDSPCs and EREG+ macrophages in the subchondral bone tissues of patients with OA. Multiplex immunofluorescence staining demonstrated significantly increased accumulation of both EGFR+ senescent MDSPC and EREG+ senescent macrophages near bone trabeculae in the III~IV side tissues compared to those in the I~II side tissues ( ). These cellular aggregates formed a distinct “senescent skeletal unit” within the subchondral bone regions of advanced OA, potentially contributing to pathological bone remodeling in osteoarthritic subchondral bone.
To further examine the effects of EREG on EGFR+ MDSPCs, we treated both EGFR+ and EGFR− acMDSPCs isolated from subchondral bone tissues of patients with OA with recombinant human EREG (rhEREG) and conducted functional assays. β-Galactosidase staining revealed that EGFR+ acMDSPCs exhibited significant senescence compared to EGFR− acMDSPCs, and rhEREG treatment further promoted senescence in EGFR+ acMDSPCs (fig. S8, A and B). Western blotting confirmed that rhEREG induced up-regulation of the classical senescence marker p21 and OA-related SASP components [IL-6 and matrix metallopeptidase 13 (MMP13)] in EGFR+ acMDSPCs (fig. S8, C and D). Additionally, osteogenic differentiation experiments demonstrated that EGFR+ acMDSPCs had greater osteogenic potential than EGFR− acMDSPCs, and this ability was further enhanced by rhEREG (fig. S8, E and F). Notably, rhEREG treatment had no significant effect on EGFR− acMDSPCs, indicating that EGFR acts as a specific receptor for EREG in this context.

## EREG is a SASP component associated with the age of patients with OA and osteogenic capacity during OA progression
To fully elucidate the association between EREG and OA progression, we performed bulk RNA sequencing on the articular osteochondral tissues from the I~II side (n = 6) and the III~IV side (n = 6) samples of patients with OA. Differential gene expression analysis revealed that, although there were some individual differences among the patients with OA, the overall gene expression remained notably different between the I~II and III~IV side tissues ( ). The overlapping genes between the DEGs of the III-IV side tissues and the senescent gene list ( ) included EREG, MMP2, MMP9, and PLAU ( ), among which the expression of EREG stood significantly higher than the others ( ), indicating that EREG was both a senescent gene and a characteristic gene of OA progression. Subsequently, peripheral blood serum samples were collected from patients with OA (n = 26) without other diseases, stratified by varying ages and distinct Kellgren and Lawrence grades, followed by EREG protein quantification using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA). The ELISA results revealed that higher serum levels of EREG were positively correlated with the age of patients with OA. In addition, patients with higher OA severity exhibited increased serum EREG concentrations ( ). Moreover, the GO analysis of the DEGs revealed that the III~IV side tissues of OA samples were involved in biological processes related to ossification, collagen fibril organization, and tissue remodeling ( ). Furthermore, protein-protein interaction (PPI) network analysis revealed that EREG regulated osteogenic differentiation through the EGFR and SMAD3 pathways ( ). These results suggest that EREG is one of the SASP components associated with ossification during OA progression.
 

## The senescent skeletal unit in the subchondral bone exhibits human-mouse conservation and mediates subchondral bone lesions and cartilage degeneration in murine PTOA models
To verify the conservation of the senescent skeletal unit consisting of EGFR+ MDSPCs and EREG+ macrophages during murine OA progression, we performed histopathological analysis and scRNA-seq on articular tissues from PTOA model mice, including the control (normal), early-stage PTOA (PTOA 1w), and late-stage PTOA (PTOA 8w) models (fig. S9A). As OA progressed, the articular cartilage was gradually destroyed, accompanied by abnormal subchondral bone remodeling (fig. S9, B and C), indicating that the murine PTOA samples used for scRNA-seq analysis accurately recapitulated the pathological progression of OA. Integrated single-cell analysis of stromal cells from the PTOA mouse samples was performed, and a total of 28,143 cells were used for downstream analysis. UMAP visualization revealed eight distinct subpopulations (fig. S10A). The OB progenitor cells expressed Sp7, Runx2, and Cadm1. Fibrocartilages were characterized by the expression of Col3a1 and Col6a1. Cartilage precursor cells expressed Prg4, Sox9, and Acan. Cartilage cells expressed Sox9 and Acan. Hypertrophic chondrocytes expressed Sox9, Col10a1, and Ibsp. EGFR+ MDSPCs (EGFR) expressed Egfr and Grem1. ECs expressed Pecam1 and Cdh5, and pericytes expressed Acta2 and Rgs5 (fig. S10B). EGFR+ MDSPCs highly expressed senescence genes such as Serping1, Igfbp4, and Igfbp5 and specifically expressed senescence genes such as Angpt1, Pappa, and Fgf7 (fig. S10C). The aging score results also revealed that EGFR+ MDSPCs exhibited clear and specific senescence characteristics (fig. S10D), with the aging score increasing in EGFR+ MDSPCs as OA progressed (fig. S10E).
In addition, we integrated the scRNA-seq dataset of immune cells from the mouse PTOA samples, which were classified into seven subpopulations: two distinct macrophage clusters (Ma1 and Ma2), EREG+ macrophages (EREG), neutrophils (Ne), B cells (B), T cells (T), and red blood cells (RBCs) (fig. S10F). Ma1 expressed Cd14, Il1b, and Cd9. Ma2 expressed Cd14 and Cd68. The EREG+ macrophages were defined by the expression of Cd9 and Ereg. Neutrophils expressed Mpo and Ctsg; B cells expressed Ms4a1 and Mzb1; T cells expressed Cd3d and Cd28; and RBCs expressed Hba-a1 and Hba-a2 (fig. S10G). Furthermore, EREG+ macrophages in PTOA mice also highly expressed many senescence genes (fig. S10H). The aging score results revealed that EREG+ macrophages underwent senescence (fig. S10I), and the aging score increased with OA progression in these cells (fig. S10J). Additionally, p21 gene expression analysis demonstrated that the senescence characteristics of EGFR+ MDSPCs and EREG+ macrophages were closely associated with OA progression (fig. S10, K and L). Notably, consistent with previous results from human OA scRNA-seq, gene co-expression analysis revealed that the Egfr gene was co-expressed with SASP genes (fig. S11A) and the Ereg gene was co-expressed with classical SASP genes (fig. S11B). Together, these findings demonstrate that both EGFR+ senescent MDSPC and EREG+ senescent macrophages are conserved between humans and mice during OA progression.
Subsequently, to investigate the role of the senescent skeletal unit in OA pathological damage and disease progression, the PTOA model was established using Ereg-KO mice. All Ereg-KO mice were born viable and exhibited no overt developmental abnormalities. Multiplex immunofluorescence staining revealed that, compared with EREG+/+ OA mice, EREG−/− OA mice exhibited a significant reduction in the proportions of both EGFR+p21+ cells (EGFR+ senescent MDSPC) and EREG+p21+ cells (EREG+ senescent macrophages) (fig. S12, A and B). Micro–computed tomography (micro-CT) imaging, histopathological, and behavioral analyses further demonstrated that EREG−/− OA mice showed marked amelioration of pathological features, including reduced subchondral bone sclerosis (fig. S12, C and D), enhanced cartilage matrix synthesis (fig. S12, E and F), and alleviated pain (fig. S12, G and H).

## Inhibition of EREG alleviates murine OA progression by preventing the senescence of EGFR+ MDSPCs
To elucidate the pathological linkage between the senescent skeletal unit and aberrant bone remodeling, we performed systematic detection and quantification of the senescent skeletal unit across distinct pathological stages of murine subchondral bone lesions, including the microdamage phase, marrow edema phase, and terminal sclerotic phase. Micro-CT analysis revealed that, 2 weeks post-PTOA modeling, microdamage emerged in the medial subchondral bone of murine joints, characterized by decreased bone volume fraction (BV/TV), reduced bone mineral density, and increased trabecular separation. At 5 weeks post-PTOA modeling, further deterioration of the medial subchondral bone was observed, with significant thinning of the SBP. By 8 weeks post-PTOA modeling, the medial subchondral bone exhibited localized sclerosis and the formation of subchondral bone cysts (fig. S13, A and B).
Notably, multiplex immunofluorescence staining showed that the senescent skeletal unit composed of EGFR+p21+ cells (EGFR+ senescent MDSPC) and EREG+p21+ cells (EREG+ senescent macrophages) was already detectable adjacent to subchondral bone trabeculae during the microdamage phase at 2 weeks post-PTOA induction. The proportions of these cell populations increased significantly as subchondral bone pathology progressed (fig. S13, C to E). Furthermore, multiplex immunofluorescence staining demonstrated that EGFR+NT5E+p21+ cells significantly increased during the early stage (microdamage phase) of OA subchondral bone lesions and persisted into the late stages. In contrast, EGFR+PDPN+p21+ cells markedly increased during the middle-to-late stages (bone marrow edema and sclerosis phases) of subchondral bone pathology (fig. S14, A and B). These findings suggest that distinct EGFR+ senescent MDSPC subpopulations may play phase-specific pathological roles in OA subchondral bone lesion progression.
To further investigate whether EREG intervention could attenuate OA progression in vivo, adeno-associated virus 5 (AAV5)–mediated Ereg knockdown was performed by intra-articular injection at 2 weeks post-PTOA induction (subchondral bone microdamage phase). The maps of the three m-Ereg shRNA plasmids are shown in fig. S15. All AAV vectors efficiently transduced subchondral bone tissues, with minimal transduction observed in articular cartilage (fig. S16). Furthermore, localized delivery of Ereg-knockdown AAV5 produced no adverse effects on murine liver or brain tissues (fig. S17, A and B). Multiplex immunofluorescence staining of EGFR, p21, and p-EGFR demonstrated cell senescence in both articular cartilage and subchondral bone EGFR+ cells during OA progression. However, intra-articular administration of AAV5-Ereg knockdown selectively suppressed cell senescence (evidenced by reduced p21 expression) and phosphorylation activation of EGFR+ cells in the subchondral bone, without affecting EGFR+p21+p-EGFR+ cells in articular cartilage. This indicates that intra-articular AAV5-Ereg suppression specifically targeted EGFR+ senescent MDSPC in the subchondral bone (fig. S18, A and B).
Subsequently, multiplex immunofluorescence analysis revealed that AAV5-mediated Ereg knockdown significantly reduced the proportions of EGFR+p21+ and EREG+p21+ cells adjacent to subchondral trabeculae ( ). Co-staining for EGFR, p21, and SPP1 further demonstrated that AAV5-Ereg intervention markedly attenuated the osteogenic differentiation capacity of EGFR+ senescent MDSPC (fig. S19, A and B). Micro-CT and histopathological analyses showed that AAV5-Ereg inhibition significantly reduced medial subchondral BV/TV and SBP thickness ( ), ameliorated cartilage degeneration ( ), and decreased osteophyte formation ( ), with no significant sex-dependent differences observed between male and female OA mice. Additionally, behavioral analyses revealed that AAV5-mediated Ereg inhibition significantly prolonged thermal response latency and increased mechanical withdrawal thresholds in the hind paws of PTOA mice, indicating effective attenuation of OA-associated pain ( ). Collectively, these results suggest that intra-articular AAV5-mediated Ereg suppression alleviates senescence and excessive osteogenesis of EGFR+ MDSPCs in the subchondral bone, thereby partially improving subchondral bone remodeling and slowing OA progression.
 

## DISCUSSION
In this study, we identified a senescent skeletal unit composed of EGFR+ MDSPCs and EREG+ macrophages in the subchondral bone adjacent to trabecular bone during OA progression. Our findings demonstrated that EREG plays a critical role in inducing the senescence of EGFR+ MDSPCs, which contributes to subchondral bone lesions in OA. Specific inhibition of EREG alleviated subchondral bone destruction and ameliorated pathological progression in a murine PTOA model, highlighting a promising therapeutic strategy to address unmet clinical needs in OA.
The subchondral bone region is critical for maintaining joint tissue homeostasis, working in concert with cartilage to form a functional osteochondral unit that optimizes load distribution and shock absorption ( , ). Subchondral bone abnormalities, such as aberrant remodeling and mineralization with disorganized matrix, may precede cartilage degradation during OA progression ( , , ). A previous study has shown that inhibiting necroptosis in subchondral OBs significantly mitigates OA progression in rats, suggesting that subchondral bone–targeted therapies may be effective in treating OA ( ). Furthermore, emerging evidence from scRNA-seq has revealed functional heterogeneity among terminally differentiated cell types—chondrocytes, OBs, OCs, synovial macrophages, and synovial fibroblasts—and their respective contributions to OA pathogenesis ( , , ). However, the roles of tissue-resident MDSPCs and their niche microenvironments within subchondral bone have remained largely unexplored in the context of OA progression.
In this study, we performed comprehensive scRNA-seq analysis of osteochondral tissues from tibial plateau specimens of patients with OA, stratifying the samples into early-stage (I~II) and advanced-stage (III~IV) disease groups. Our analysis revealed EGFR+ MDSPC subpopulations, including NT5E+ MSC and PDPN+ SSC, exhibiting senescence-associated gene expression and SASP characteristics during OA progression. Moreover, through in-depth analysis of ST data (GSE254844) from healthy controls and patients with OA in public repositories, we identified pathophenotypes associated with advanced OA and senescence hallmarks in the osteochondral DZ (the region adjacent to the calcified cartilage layer), in contrast to the more superficial cartilage zones. Multiplex immunofluorescence staining confirmed that EGFR+ senescent MDSPC was primarily localized to the subchondral bone compartment adjacent to trabeculae. In addition, combined functional assays and multiplex immunofluorescence staining demonstrated that the senescence of EGFR+ MDSPCs was characterized by excessive osteogenesis, mechanistically linking this senescent state to aberrant subchondral bone remodeling in OA pathogenesis. Notably, this phenotype differs from that observed in osteoporotic murine models, in which bone marrow SSCs show reduced osteogenic capacity and enhanced adipogenic differentiation ( ). Such differences in senescence-associated progenitor phenotypes may stem from disease-specific alterations in niche microenvironments that govern skeletal stem/progenitor cell fate determination.
Stem cells reside within specialized microenvironments termed niches, and heterogeneity across disease-specific niches can drive alterations in stem cell biology, including changes in self-renewal and multilineage differentiation capacity, ultimately contributing to disease onset and progression ( ). In this study, intercellular communication network analysis identified the EGFR signaling pathway as the dominant signaling axis regulating EGFR+ senescent MDSPC. The EGFR pathway is known to play a key role in both skeletal homeostasis and the development of bone-related disorders ( ). EGFR can be activated by seven ligands, classified into two groups based on receptor-binding affinity: high-affinity ligands include EGF, TGF-α, betacellulin, and HBEGF; low-affinity ligands include epigen, EREG, and amphiregulin (AREG) ( , ). Among them, high-affinity ligands typically promote cell proliferation, while low-affinity ligands tend to induce differentiation ( ). Furthermore, individual ligands may elicit distinct cellular responses depending on the context of cell type and pathological environment ( ). In mice, Egfr-KO leads to accumulation of hypertrophic cartilage in the growth plate and decreased trabecular bone mass ( ), while Areg knockdown significantly reduces tibial trabecular bone ( ); in contrast, overexpression of Egf results in impaired growth and reduced cortical bone thickness ( ). Thus, the complex role of EGFR signaling in the skeletal system may be attributed to the functional diversity of its ligands.
EREG has been reported to modulate inflammatory processes in the pathogenesis of both gouty arthritis and rheumatoid arthritis ( , ). Furthermore, as a SASP component in the tumor microenvironment, EREG has been shown to promote therapy resistance by reprogramming tumor cell biology ( ). In our study, we found that EREG derived from the monocyte/macrophage subpopulation exerted specific effects on EGFR+ senescent MDSPC. Functional experiments confirmed that rhEREG promoted the senescence of EGFR+ acMDSPCs and enhanced their osteogenic differentiation. We defined the EREG+ senescent macrophage subpopulation based on EREG gene expression and aging score profiling. These macrophages were located in the subchondral bone region adjacent to the EGFR+ senescent MDSPC during OA progression, together forming a unique senescent skeletal unit in the subchondral bone. These findings suggest that EREG, acting as a senescent niche factor for EGFR+ MDSPCs, may exacerbate pathological damage and promote OA progression by inducing senescence and osteogenesis in subchondral bone. However, a prior gene microarray study of patients with OA reported significant down-regulation of Ereg mRNA expression in subchondral bone tissues ( ), which may reflect methodological differences in tissue sampling and platform sensitivity across studies.
Moreover, our data demonstrated that EGFR+ senescent MDSPC and EREG+ senescent macrophages exhibited senescence-associated gene expression and secretory profiles, indicating heterogeneity among senescent cell types. This heterogeneity likely arises from differences in cell subtype, function, and niche context, which, in turn, generate diverse senescent phenotypes ( ).
Given the limited sample size of human OA specimens in our initial scRNA-seq study, we performed additional validation through bulk RNA sequencing and ELISA using an expanded cohort of patients with OA to further investigate the association between EREG and OA progression. We found that higher serum EREG concentrations correlated positively with both disease severity and patient age, suggesting that circulating EREG may serve as a predictive biomarker for OA progression and senescence burden. PPI network analysis implicated SMAD3 pathway activation as the potential mechanism linking EREG to the pathological osteogenic differentiation of EGFR+ MDSPCs in subchondral lesions. Notably, a previous OA study demonstrated that overexpression of HBEGF or exogenous administration of TGF-α alleviated cartilage degeneration via extracellular signal–regulated kinase (ERK) pathway–mediated chondroprogenitor proliferation ( ). This functional divergence underscores ligand-specific differences in EGFR downstream signaling: While EREG preferentially activates SMAD3-mediated pro-osteogenic processes, HBEGF and TGF-α engage ERK-dependent pathways that promote chondrocyte regeneration.
Subsequently, we evaluated the therapeutic potential of EREG inhibition in ameliorating the senescence features of EGFR+ MDSPCs and attenuating OA progression in PTOA mice. Through murine OA scRNA-seq and immunofluorescence analyses, we confirmed the conservation of EGFR+ senescent MDSPC and EREG+ senescent macrophages between patients with OA and mouse OA specimens. Furthermore, by using Ereg-KO mice for OA modeling, we validated in vivo that EREG promotes EGFR+ MDSPC senescence in the subchondral bone region, contributing to osteochondral damage and pain-associated behavioral phenotypes. Notably, systemic EREG ablation exhibited sex-dimorphic effects on subchondral pathology in female versus male mice, potentially attributable to functional cross-talk between EREG and gonadotropins in ovarian regulation ( ). Last, intra-articular delivery of AAV5-mediated Ereg knockdown in OA mice significantly reduced the senescence and osteogenic capacity of EGFR+ MDSPCs within the subchondral bone compartment, concomitant with mitigated osteochondral degeneration and pain behaviors. These therapeutic outcomes showed no sex-dependent differences. These results indicate that intra-articular AAV5-mediated Ereg inhibition alleviates OA disease progression, at least partially, by mitigating the senescence and excessive osteogenesis of EGFR+ MDSPCs in the subchondral bone.
The study has several limitations. First, although we validated our findings from human scRNA-seq data using bulk RNA sequencing analysis, immunohistochemical staining of human OA samples, and functional assays of MDSPC derived from human OA tissues, the scRNA-seq dataset itself included only three patients with OA. Second, due to the challenges in clinical sampling, we did not obtain normal articular osteochondral samples as controls for the scRNA-seq analysis. Third, although we identified macrophages as the source of EREG in subchondral bone through scRNA-seq and immunohistochemical staining, the use of systemic Ereg-KO mice introduced potential confounding effects, as the influence of EREG from other tissues or cell types could not be excluded. Last, the pathogenesis of clinical OA involves complex interactions among inflammation, senescence, and trauma. However, we evaluated the therapeutic effect of EREG inhibition solely in a PTOA murine model. Whether EREG suppression produces comparable benefits in other OA subtypes, including inflammation-dominant collagenase-induced models or senescence/genetic-driven spontaneous models, remains to be systematically investigated.
In brief, this study demonstrates that EGFR+ senescent MDSPC within the subchondral bone compartment and its senescence-associated niche factor EREG play key roles in OA progression by driving dysregulation of subchondral bone homeostasis. These findings highlight the senescent skeletal unit, composed of EGFR+ MDSPCs and EREG+ macrophages, as a potential therapeutic target for OA treatment.

## MATERIALS AND METHODS

## Study design
This study aimed to explore the pathological effects of MDSPC senescence on OA progression, with a particular focus on the contribution of subchondral bone lesions. To achieve this, we conducted the following analyses: (i) delineation of a single-cell atlas of articular osteochondral tissue from patients with OA; (ii) examination of spatial transcriptome data from patients with OA to compare pathological features and senescence characteristics between superficial and DZs of osteochondral tissues; (iii) aging score analysis to identify senescent MDSPC subsets exhibiting OA-associated pathological features during disease progression; (iv) investigation of the senescent niche of EGFR+ senescent MDSPC through cell-cell communication analysis; (v) multiplex immunofluorescence staining to spatially localize the senescent skeletal unit comprising EGFR+ senescent MDSPC and EREG+ senescent macrophages in osteochondral specimens from patients with OA; (vi) validation of the association between EREG expression and OA disease severity and senescence burden through bulk RNA sequencing and serum EREG ELISA in patients with OA; (vii) demonstration of conservation of the senescent skeletal unit between patients with OA and PTOA mice, and investigation of its pathological role in subchondral bone damage through scRNA-seq analyses and PTOA modeling in wild-type and Ereg-KO mice; (viii) multiplex immunofluorescence staining of murine OA joint specimens across distinct stages of subchondral bone lesion progression to establish a spatiotemporal pathological link between the senescent skeletal unit and subchondral bone remodeling abnormalities; and (ix) evaluation of the therapeutic effects of intra-articular AAV5-mediated Ereg knockdown in subchondral bone on OA progression in a PTOA murine model. Sample sizes were determined on the basis of our previous experience with similar experimental models ( ). All experimental data were replicated at least three times. No data were excluded. All human and animal experiments received ethical approval. Data analysis was performed in a blinded manner, and all samples were randomly allocated to experimental groups.

## Human samples
The human OA samples were obtained from the People’s Liberation Army General Hospital. Briefly, knee tibial plateau samples were harvested from patients with OA who underwent total knee arthroplasty. Informed consent was obtained with the approval of the ethics committee of the People’s Liberation Army General Hospital (2023KY029-KS001). The osteochondral tissues were used for subsequent bioinformatics sequencing analysis, cell culture, and histopathological evaluation. Patient information is shown in tables S1 and S2. In addition, peripheral blood was drawn from the patients with OA after they provided informed consent for subsequent ELISA experiments. Patient information is shown in table S3.

## Animal studies
Female (n = 120) and male (n = 120) C57BL/6 mice were purchased from Beijing Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology, and the wild-type (EREG+/+, n = 20) and Ereg-KO (EREG−/−, n = 10) mice (C57BL/6 background) were generated by the Shanghai Research Center of Southern Model Organisms. All animal studies were conducted in accordance with the ethics committee of the Academy of Military Medical Science (IACUC-DWZX-2024-P523). All mice were housed in a specific pathogen–free environment with 54 ± 5% humidity, a temperature of 25 ± 2°C, and a 12-hour light/dark cycle.
To establish a PTOA model in mice, 2- or 3-month-old female and male C57BL/6 mice (including normal, wild-type, and Ereg-KO mice) underwent anterior cruciate ligament transection (ACLT) of the left knee. The age of mice at the time of PTOA modeling is shown in each figure legend. For the sham group, the joint capsule was opened without ACLT. For the AAV treatment groups, three distinct Ereg-knockdown AAV5 vectors and control virus particles were administered via intra-articular injection 2 weeks after ACLT surgery. AAV production was carried out by Hanheng Biotechnology (Shanghai, China), with construction and administration procedures detailed in the Supplementary Text.

## Preparation of human single-cell suspension
Following joint replacement surgery, the I~II and III~IV sides of osteochondral tissues (including cartilage, SBP, and STB) from human OA tibial plateau samples were washed twice with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and dissected into 1-mm3 fragments using sterile surgical scissors. The minced tissues were enzymatically digested with collagenase type II (Sigma-Aldrich, V900892; 2 mg/ml) and deoxyribonuclease I (DNase I; Sigma-Aldrich, DN25-100MG; 10 μg/ml) in a 37°C shaking water bath for 1 hour. The resulting cell suspension was sequentially filtered through a 40-μm cell strainer (MACS SmartStrainer, Miltenyi Biotec, Germany) and centrifuged at 500g for 5 min. After complete aspiration of the supernatant, the cellular pellet was resuspended in 1 ml of PBS supplemented with 0.04% (w/v) bovine serum albumin (BSA). Cell concentration and viability were assessed using an automated cell counter with trypan blue exclusion. The procedure was repeated six to eight times until the osteochondral chips were fully digested into debris.

## Preparation of mouse single-cell suspension
Murine joint samples from each group (n = 10 mice per group) were dissected to remove muscle and ligament tissues, washed twice with PBS, and minced into approximately 1-mm3 fragments using sharp surgical scissors. The fragments were enzymatically digested at 37°C for 1 hour using collagenase type II (Sigma-Aldrich, V900892; 2 mg/ml) and DNase I (Sigma-Aldrich, DN25-100MG; 10 μg/ml). Following digestion, the suspensions were filtered through a 40-μm MACS SmartStrainer (Miltenyi Biotec, Germany), centrifuged at 500g for 5 min, and the supernatant was discarded completely. The cell pellet from each group was resuspended in 1 ml of PBS containing 0.04% (w/v) BSA for scRNA-seq. Cell count and viability were assessed using an automated cell counter.

## Single-cell RNA sequencing
The scRNA-seq library construction and sequencing were performed by Capital Biotechnology Co. Ltd. (Beijing, China). Libraries were prepared using the Single Cell 3′ Reagent Kit v3 (10× Genomics), and sequencing was conducted on an Illumina NovaSeq platform to generate 150–base pair paired-end reads. Quality control and data processing methods are described in the Supplementary Text.

## Cell culture
acMDSPCs from the I~II and III~IV sides of the articular subchondral bone in patients with OA were obtained using previously published protocols with minor modifications ( ). Briefly, tibial plateau samples from patients with OA were thoroughly rinsed with PBS to remove blood and subchondral bone marrow. Subchondral bone tissue (including SBP and trabecular bone) was fragmented with bone rongeurs, then digested with 0.1% type II collagenase in a 5% CO₂ incubator at 37°C for 2 hours. After digestion, bone fragments were washed with PBS and transferred to minimum essential medium (MEM) Alpha supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), penicillin (1,000 U/ml), and streptomycin (100 mg/ml) and incubated in a 5% CO₂ incubator at 37°C. After 15 or more days in culture, cells migrated out from the digested bone fragments. Adherent cells from passages 2 to 4 were used for flow cytometry, CFU-F, Western blotting, osteogenic differentiation, and cell senescence assays, as described in the Supplementary Text.
For EGFR+ MDSPCs isolation, acMDSPCs from passages 2 to 4 were collected and sorted using the Miltenyi MACS bead–based sorting system per the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, MDSPC suspensions were labeled with an anti-EGFR antibody (1:250, Proteintech, 18986-1-AP), followed by anti-rabbit immunoglobulin G microbeads (Miltenyi Biotec). EGFR+ cells were isolated using MS columns (Miltenyi Biotec). Sorted cells were cultured in MEM Alpha medium containing 10% FBS, penicillin (1000 U/ml), and streptomycin (100 mg/ml) in a 5% CO₂ incubator at 37°C. Passages 2 to 3 of EGFR+ acMDSPCs were used for CFU-F, Western blotting, osteogenic differentiation, and cell senescence experiments, as described in the Supplementary Text.

## Histopathological staining
All articular samples from human and mouse OA specimens were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned into 6-μm slices following decalcification. Hematoxylin and eosin staining was performed to evaluate the overall structure of the articular tissue. Safranin O/fast green staining and toluidine blue staining were conducted to assess the distribution of the cartilaginous matrix.

## Multiplex immunofluorescence staining
Target protein labeling was carried out using the tyramide signal amplification (TSA) fluorescence kit (Melady Biosciences) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Tissue sections were baked at 60°C for 1 hour, followed by deparaffinization in xylene and rehydration through graded ethanol concentrations (100, 95, 80, and 70%), and then rinsed in ddH2O. Heat-induced antigen retrieval was performed using microwave treatment in EDTA buffer (pH 9.0). Endogenous peroxidase activity was quenched with 3% hydrogen peroxide. Sections were blocked with blocking solution for 20 min. Primary antibodies were applied and incubated overnight at 4°C. After washing with PBS, species-appropriate horseradish peroxidase–conjugated secondary antibodies were incubated at room temperature for 20 min. Following further PBS washes, fluorescent dyes for TSA staining were applied.
The primary antibodies used included anti-EGFR (1:100; Affinity Biosciences, AF6043), anti-p21 (1:100; Affinity Biosciences, DF6423), anti-p16 (1:100; Abcam, ab108349), anti-EREG (1:100; Invitrogen, PA5–24727), anti-CD68 (1:100; Affinity Biosciences, DF7518), anti-PDPN (1:100; Affinity Biosciences, DF12456), anti-NT5E (1:100; Affinity Biosciences, DF6763), anti-p-EGFR (1:200; Abcam, ab40815), anti-SPP1 (1:100; Proteintech, 22952-1-AP), and anti-RUNX2 (1:100; Proteintech, 20700–1-AP). The TSA staining procedure was repeated sequentially for each antibody target. After completing all stainings, sections were washed with PBS and counterstained with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole. Whole-slide imaging was then performed using a multichannel fluorescence scanner (3DHISTECH, Pannoramic MIDI), and the images were analyzed with SlideViewer software.

## Statistical analysis
All quantitative experiments were conducted with at least three biological replicates. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 8.0. For comparisons between two groups, an unpaired Student’s t test was used; for comparisons among multiple groups, one-way analysis of variance was applied. Data are presented as means ± SDs. A P value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.
