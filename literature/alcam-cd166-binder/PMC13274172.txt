Known for its chronic and relapsing nature, IBD currently lacks curative pharmacological therapies. Herein, we investigated the therapeutic potential of human umbilical cord mesenchymal stem cell-derived exosomes (hucMSC-Ex) in IBD and explored the underlying mechanisms. In vivo and in vitro experiments demonstrated that the administration of hucMSC-Ex significantly attenuated inflammatory levels and ameliorated associated disease symptoms in IBD models. Concurrently, hucMSC-Ex treatment effectively modulated bile acid dysregulation in the IBD condition, contributing to the restoration of intestinal metabolic homeostasis. Mechanistically, the protective effects of hucMSC-Ex were mediated through the upregulation of farnesoid X receptor (FXR) expression. FXR, a critical regulator of intestinal homeostasis, plays a pivotal role, particularly in bile acid metabolism. Elevated FXR expression further suppressed ferroptosis in macrophages, as evidenced by reduced lipid peroxidation, diminished oxidative stress, restored iron metabolism homeostasis, and normalized expression of ferroptosis-related markers (GPX4, ACSL4, MBOAT1). Collectively, our findings indicate that hucMSC-Ex alleviates IBD by activating FXR in macrophages, thereby inhibiting lipid peroxidation and reducing ferroptosis, ultimately mitigating inflammation and ameliorating intestinal damage. This offers the potential of a FXR-targeted therapeutic strategy based on exosomes for the treatment of IBD.

## Introduction
Inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) is a heterogeneous, chronic relapsing gastrointestinal inflammatory disorder [ ]. Recent epidemiology shows a soaring global IBD burden linked to industrialization [ ]. Conventional immunosuppressants and biologics are limited by drug resistance, adverse events and high surgery rates, demanding novel therapies [ ]. Our prior work found human umbilical cord mesenchymal stem cell-derived exosomes (hucMSC-Ex) alleviate murine IBD [ ], with benefits including targeted migration, tissue repair and paracrine immunomodulation [ ]. HucMSCs have ideal clinical accessibility, no ethical constraints and sustainable regenerative capacity [ ]; MSC-based therapies are approved in multiple countries [ ]. As key intercellular messengers delivering bioactive cargo, hucMSC-Ex offer a novel strategy for chronic intestinal inflammation [ – ].
IBD pathogenesis is closely linked to dysregulation of the intestinal microenvironment, with bile acid (BA) metabolism emerging as a critical mediator of host-microbiome crosstalk. BAs modulate immune responses and barrier repair through nuclear receptors such as the farnesoid X receptor (FXR) [ , ]. FXR has recently been implicated in regulating ferroptosis, revealing synergistic therapeutic potential. Mechanistically, FXR activation transcriptionally upregulates key antioxidant genes, bolstering glutathione (GSH) metabolism and redox homeostasis [ ]. FXR suppresses ferroptosis by directly binding promoters of ferroptosis-related genes (Aifm2, Gsta4) to inhibit lipid peroxidation and iron accumulation, while repressing pro-ferroptotic genes via MAFG transcription factor modulation [ ]. In gastric cancer cells, FXR-BACH1 interaction reinforces GSH synthesis through BACH1-mediated transcriptional repression, diminishing ferroptotic susceptibility [ ]. These findings position FXR as a promising therapeutic target for ferroptosis-associated disorders.
Ferroptosis contributes significantly to IBD progression by disrupting intestinal immune homeostasis and epithelial barrier integrity, driving metabolic dysregulation, oxidative stress, and inflammation [ ]. During ferroptosis, macrophages release damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) that amplify inflammatory cascades and impair phagocytic/antigen-presenting functions [ ]. While hucMSC-Ex have demonstrated ferroptosis-modulating effects in intestinal epithelial cells [ ], their impact on macrophage ferroptosis, which serves as a pivotal immune component in IBD, remains unexplored. Elucidating the precise regulatory mechanisms of hucMSC-Ex on macrophage ferroptosis warrants further investigation.

## Materials and methods

## Isolation, culture, and characterization of hucMSCs
The experimental protocol of this study was reviewed and approved by Ethics Committee of Zhenjiang First People’s Hospital (Approval No.: K-20240196-Y) and strictly adhered to the ethical guidelines for the use of human clinical specimens. In a biological safety cabinet, umbilical vessels were dissected, residual blood removed with sterile PBS containing 1% antibiotic-antimycotic, and cords cut into about 1 mm³ fragments. Fragments were adhered to 3.5 cm dishes (Excell), and cultured in α-MEM (Hylcone) with 15% FBS (Vazyme) and 1% antibiotic-antimycotic at 37 ℃, 5% CO₂. Primary fibroblast-like hucMSCs (swirling, dense) emerging around fragments were trypsin-passaged: P1 (passage1)in 15% FBS/α-MEM, subsequent passages in 10% exosome-free FBS/α-MEM.
Characterization included light microscopy (typical fibroblast morphology: slender, low nuclear-cytoplasmic ratio, adherent; passaged at > 80% confluency) and flow cytometry (OriCell). For flow analysis, P3 cells (90% confluent, logarithmic phase) were digested, centrifuged (800 rpm, 5 min, RT), washed twice with pre-cooled PBS, and resuspended to 3 × 10⁶ cells/mL. Aliquots (100 µL, 3 × 10⁵ cells) were incubated with 2 µL negative (CD11B, CD14, CD45, HLA-DR), positive (CD44, CD73, CD166) markers, or isotype control (IgG1) for 30 min (low-temperature, dark, shaking). After washing twice, negative/positive groups were incubated with PE-secondary antibody (1:50, 2 µL) in 100 µL flow buffer for 30 min, rewashed, resuspended in 300–500 µL buffer, and analyzed (≥ 10,000 events).

## Preparation and characterization of hucMSC-derived exosomes
Culture supernatants from well-conditioned P3-P8 hucMSCs (cultured in α-MEM with 10% exosome-free FBS) were collected. All centrifugation steps were performed at 4 °C: sequential centrifugation at 2000×g (30 min) and 10,000×g (30 min) to remove debris, followed by 100 kDa ultrafiltration (2000×g, 30 min). The filtrate was ultracentrifuged at 100,000×g (3 h), the pellet was resuspended in PBS and recentrifuged at 100,000×g (6 h). The final exosome pellet was dissolved in PBS, filtered through a 0.22 μm filter (Millipore), aliquoted, and stored at -80 °C.
Characterization included: TEM of negatively stained (3% phosphotungstic acid, pH 7.0) exosome samples; NTA for size and concentration after dilution in purified water. Protein was extracted from exosomes and hucMSCs using RIPA lysis (followed by centrifugation and processing), protein concentration quantification was carried out via microplate reader at 562 nm using standard curves, and Western blot was performed to detect CD9, CD81, TSG101, and Calnexin (Affnity).

## Targeted bile acid metabolomics detection
The experimental protocol of this study was reviewed and approved by Ethics Committee of Zhenjiang First People’s Hospital (Approval No.: K-20240196-Y) and strictly adhered to the ethical guidelines for the use of human clinical specimens. In targeted bile acid metabolomics research, the detection workflow encompasses sample pretreatment, metabolite extraction, LC-MS/MS analysis, and data processing. For sample processing, 50 µL of serum was transferred into a clean EP tube, followed by the addition of 2.5 µL of 10 µg/mL internal standard mixture working solution and 197.5 µL of 20% methanol-acetonitrile solution. The mixture was vortexed for 10 min and then stored in a -20 °C refrigerator for 10 min. Subsequent centrifugation was performed, and the supernatant was processed through a protein precipitation plate prior to LC-MS/MS analysis.​ Subsequent steps sequentially involved data preprocessing, principal component analysis (PCA), cluster analysis, metabolite identification and annotation using a metabolite database, differential metabolite analysis, and KEGG pathway analysis.

## Immunohistochemistry (IHC)
The experimental protocol of this study was reviewed and approved by Ethics Committee of Zhenjiang First People’s Hospital (Approval No.: K-20240196-Y) and strictly adhered to the ethical guidelines for the use of human clinical specimens. Following dewaxing, paraffin sections were treated with 3% hydrogen peroxide at room temperature for 30 min to block endogenous peroxidase activity, followed by 1× histochemical PBS washes (5 min each, 3 times). For antigen retrieval, sections were immersed in freshly prepared 0.01 M citrate buffer, steamed using an electric stove for 30 min, and then allowed to cool naturally to room temperature, with subsequent 1× histochemical PBS washes (1–2 times). After blocking with 5% BSA at room temperature for 30 min, excess BSA was discarded. Diluted PCNA or P65 antibodies were applied to cover the tissue sections and incubated overnight at 4 °C. Sections were then washed with 1× histochemical PBS (5 min each, 3 times) before incubation with goat anti-mouse secondary antibody at room temperature for 1 h, followed by another round of 1× histochemical PBS washes (5 min each, 3 times). SABC reagent was added dropwise, and sections were incubated at 37 °C for 30 min. Without additional washing, sections were immersed in histochemical PBS pending chromogenic reaction. Meanwhile, an appropriate amount of freshly prepared DAB chromogen solution (BOSTER) was used for color development under microscopic monitoring for optimal duration. Counterstaining was performed with hematoxylin (Solarbio), followed by tap water rinsing for 20–30 min to remove dye precipitates. Sections were dehydrated, mounted, and air-dried. Images were acquired using a pathological slide scanner (Pannoramic MIDI, 3DHISTECH, Hungary).

## Establishment of IBD mouse models
Male BALB/c mice (6–8 weeks old, 20 g) used in this study were purchased from the Animal Research Center of Jiangsu University. This study was reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee and the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Jiangsu University (Approval No.: UJS-IACUC-AP-2024030512), and all experimental animals and materials involved in the study are in compliance with the ethical requirements for experimental animals. All animal-related experiments were approved by the Ethics Committee and the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Jiangsu University. Mice were randomly divided into three groups: negative control group (NC group), dextran sulfate sodium (DSS)-induced colitis group (DSS group), and hucMSC-Ex-treated colitis group (hucMSC-Ex group). The NC group was given autoclaved pure water, while the DSS and hucMSC-Ex groups received autoclaved pure water containing 3% DSS. Additionally, mice in the hucMSC-Ex group were administered hucMSC-Ex (50 mg/kg) via tail vein injection every three days, whereas mice in the DSS group received an equal volume of sterile PBS via tail vein injection. Mice were weighed daily, and their fecal characteristics were recorded. On day 10, all mice were euthanized, and serum, colon, and spleen tissues were collected for subsequent experiments. Kits for detecting MDA and GSH levels were purchased from Nanjing Jiancheng, and kits for Fe²⁺ and serum iron detection were obtained from Solarbio.

## Cell culture
RAW264.7 cells (Procell) were divided into groups for treatment: the negative control group (NC group) received no special treatment; the lipopolysaccharide-treated group (LPS group) was supplemented with 1 µg/mL LPS (Sigma); the exosome intervention group (hucMSC-Ex group) was co-treated with 1 µg/mL LPS and 200 µg/mL hucMSC-Ex. After drug treatment, cultures were terminated at 6 h (for RNA extraction) and 12 h (for protein extraction) post-intervention, and corresponding samples were collected. THP-1 cells (Procell), a suspension cell line, were induced to differentiate into adherent macrophages using phorbol 12-myristate 13-acetate (PMA, Sigma), followed by the same subsequent treatments as RAW264.7 cells. A cell model with enhanced ferroptosis under inflammatory conditions was established by co-treatment with LPS (1 µg/mL) and the ferroptosis inducer erastin (8 µM, Sellck). After overnight culture, the selective FXR agonist GW4064 (10 µM, MCE) and hucMSC-Ex (200 µg/mL) were added. To investigate the regulatory role of FXR, five experimental groups were set up: (1) Negative control group (NC group): no special treatment; (2) Inflammation-ferroptosis enhancement group (L&E group): treated with LPS to induce inflammation and erastin to induce ferroptosis; (3) hucMSC-Ex-treated group (hucMSC-Ex group): L&E group with additional hucMSC-Ex treatment; (4) FXR activation-ferroptosis enhancement group (L&E+GW4064 group): L&E group with additional GW4064 to activate FXR; (5) hucMSC-Ex + FXR activation group (hucMSC-Ex+GW4064 group): hucMSC-Ex group with additional GW4064 to activate FXR.

## Western blot
Proteins were extracted from colon tissues and RAW264.7 cells using RIPA lysis buffer (Solarbio). Protein samples were separated by 10% SDS-PAGE and transferred onto PVDF membranes (Merck Millipore). Membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat milk in TBST buffer for 1 h at room temperature, then incubated with primary antibodies against FXR, GPX4, ACSL4 (Abclonal); MBOAT1 (Bioss); CD9, CD81, TSG101, Calnexin, CD206, iNOS (Affinity); and β-actin (Proteintech) at 4 °C overnight. After washing, membranes were incubated with secondary antibodies (Proteintech) at room temperature for 1 h, followed by visualization and image analysis.

## Quantitative real-time (qRT)-PCR
Total RNA was extracted from mouse colon tissues, RAW264.7 cells, and THP-1 cells using Trizol reagent (Vazyme). Complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesized using a cDNA synthesis kit (Vazyme). β-actin was used as the internal control for detecting the expression of target genes (Vazyme). The primer sequences used are listed in Table  .

 

## Immunofluorescence (IF)
Macrophages (1 × 10⁵ cells/well) were seeded on sterile coverslips in 24-well plates, cultured at 37 °C with 5% CO₂ until adherence, then treated with LPS and hucMSC-Ex. After intervention, cells were rinsed 3 × 5 min with PBS, fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (20 min, RT), rinsed again, and permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 (30 min). Coverslips were blocked with 5% BSA (30 min, RT), incubated with primary antibodies (1:100 in 5% BSA) at 4 °C overnight, then rinsed 3 × 5 min with PBS. FITC-conjugated secondary antibody (1:200, Abcam) was applied (1 h, 37 °C, dark), followed by 3 × 5 min PBS rinses (protected from light). Cells were mounted on slides with DAPI-containing anti-fluorescence quencher (10 min, dark, Absin), and imaged immediately using super-resolution/fluorescence microscopy.

## Imaging of mitochondrial ultrastructure
Mitochondrial ultrastructure of cells was visualized using transmission electron microscopy (TEM). For sample preparation, cells in logarithmic growth phase were rinsed with PBS, then fixed with pre-chilled 2.5% glutaraldehyde at 4 °C for 2 h. This was followed by post-fixation with 1% osmium tetroxide in 0.1 M sodium cacodylate buffer containing 1.5% potassium ferrocyanide for 1.5 h in the dark. Fixatives were removed by centrifugation at 2000 g for 10 min. Dehydration was performed sequentially with 30%, 50%, 70%, 90% ethanol, and anhydrous acetone (each for 10 min). Samples were then subjected to gradient infiltration with epoxy resin, followed by polymerization at 60 °C for 48 h to form hardened embedding blocks. Ultrathin Sect.  (70 nm thickness) were cut using a diamond knife, stained with 2% uranyl acetate in methanol for 30 min in the dark, and counterstained with lead citrate for 5 min to enhance membrane structure contrast. Mitochondria were subsequently imaged using a transmission electron microscope.

## Statistics
Data in this study are presented as mean ± standard deviation (Mean ± SD). Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 8.0. Data were analyzed by one-way analysis of variance (One-way ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s multiple comparison test for post hoc analysis. Statistical significance was defined as *P<0.05, **P<0.01,***P<0.001. Error bars in all figures represent SD values.

## Results

## Preparation and characterization of hucMSCs and their exosomes
Primary hucMSCs exhibited typical spindle-shaped, fibroblast-like morphology and strong plastic adherence (Fig. A). Flow cytometric profiling confirmed their identity, revealing high expression of MSC markers (CD44, CD73, CD166; >90%) and negligible levels of hematopoietic lineage markers (CD45, HLA-DR, CD11b, CD14; <0.5%) (Fig. C). Following isolation, hucMSC-Ex were characterized according to MISEV guidelines [ ]. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) visualized characteristic cup-shaped, double-membrane vesicles (Fig. B), while nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA) confirmed a size distribution of 30–200 nm with a mean concentration of 1.9 × 1011 particles/mL (Fig. D). Western blot further validated the enrichment of exosomal markers (CD9, CD81, TSG101) and the absence of the ER marker Calnexin (Fig. E). These data confirm the successful isolation of high-purity hucMSCs and exosomes.

 

## Differential bile acid metabolism and related molecular expression in IBD patients versus healthy individuals
Targeted serum metabolomics unveiled a distinct remodeling of the bile acid landscape in IBD patients compared to healthy controls, characterized by clear separation in PCA and clustering analyses (Fig.  A, B). Specifically, the following bile acids were significantly decreased in IBD patients compared to healthy individuals: 3β-hydroxychol-5-en-24-oic acid (5-isoLCA); Isoallolithocholic acid (IALCA); Dehydrolithocholic acid (DLCA); Isolithocholic acid (ILCA); 3-Oxocholic acid (3-oxo-CA); Glycolithocholic acid 3-sulfate (GLCA-3 S); Lithocholic acid 3-sulfate (LCA-3 S); Deoxycholic acid (DCA); Glycolithocholic acid (GLCA); 3β-Deoxycholic acid (3β-DCA); Ursodeoxycholic acid 3-sulfate disodium salt (UDCA-3 S); Taurolithocholic acid (TLCA). Conversely, 7-Ketodeoxycholic acid (7-KDCA) andCholic acid (CA) were significantly increased. Among these differentially altered bile acids, 7-KDCA showed the most pronounced increase, while GLCA exhibited the most significant decrease in IBD patients (Fig.  C). Disease association analysis using the Human Metabolome Database (HMDB) further revealed significant links between specific altered bile acids and pathologies: CA was significantly associated with UC and CD; DCA showed marked association with irritable bowel syndrome (IBS) and UC; and 7-KDCA was highly associated with colorectal cancer (CRC) (Fig.  D). At the tissue level, this metabolic dysregulation coincided with a marked suppression of the FXR-ferroptosis axis: IHC staining revealed a coordinated downregulation of both FXR and the anti-ferroptosis guardian GPX4 in IBD colonic mucosa compared to the robust expression seen in controls (Fig.  E, H). Corresponding H&E staining confirmed severe histological deterioration in IBD tissues, characterized by crypt architecture disruption, goblet cell depletion, and dense inflammatory infiltration (Fig.  I).

 

## HucMSC-Ex alleviates DSS-induced murine IBD and restores bile acid homeostasis
HucMSC-Ex treatment significantly attenuated the clinical severity of DSS-induced colitis, evidenced by minimized weight loss and a reduced disease activity index (DAI) (Fig.  A, B). This therapeutic efficacy was corroborated by gross pathological improvements, including preserved colon length and reduced splenomegaly (Fig.  D, F). Histologically, hucMSC-Ex administration restored colonic crypt architecture, normalized splenic lymphoid follicles, and suppressed inflammatory infiltration (Fig.  G, H). Coinciding with these structural improvements, hucMSC-Ex modulated the colonic immune microenvironment, downregulating pro-inflammatory transcripts (IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α) while upregulating the anti-inflammatory cytokine IL-10 (Fig.  C).
Metabolomic data indicated 9 differentially abundant bile acids between the NC and DSS groups, while only 4 differences remained between the NC and hucMSC-Ex groups (Fig.  I). PCA showed significant separation between the hucMSC-Ex and DSS groups in their bile acid metabolic profiles, characterized by large non-overlapping regions, with the hucMSC-Ex group shifting towards the metabolic pattern of the NC group (Fig.  J). Bile acid clustering heatmaps visually demonstrated that hucMSC-Ex reduced the metabolic disorder induced by DSS (Fig.  K, L). Following hucMSC-Ex treatment, serum levels of several secondary bile acids were downregulated in IBD mice, including Cholic acid (CA), α-Muricholic acid (α-MCA), Murideoxycholic acid (MDCA), Cholic acid 7-sulfate (CA-7 S) and so on. Conversely, the taurine-conjugated bile acids such as Taurodeoxycholic acid (TDCA), and Tauro-α-muricholic acid sodium salt (Tα-MCA)were upregulated.
Among these altered bile acids, the cholic acid derivative CA-7 S exhibited the most pronounced downregulation upon hucMSC-Ex treatment, decreasing by approximately 80% compared to the DSS group (Fig. M). These results suggest that hucMSC-Ex may restore bile acid homeostasis by inhibiting both the classical synthesis pathway—mediated by CYP8B1 for CA generation—and the alternative pathway—mediated by CYP27A1 for acidic bile acid production [ , ]. The significant elevation of taurine-conjugated bile acids, such as TUDCA and TDCA, implies a specific activation of the gut microbial 7α-dehydroxylation metabolism of primary bile acids [ ]. This may be linked to hucMSC-Ex ameliorating gut dysbiosis and promoting the metabolic activity of specific anaerobic bacteria [ ]. These findings provide crucial molecular insights into how hucMSC-Ex modulates the bile acid metabolic network to alleviate IBD pathology.

 

## HucMSC-Ex regulates macrophage function under inflammatory conditions
In macrophage inflammation models using RAW264.7 and THP-1 cells, qRT-PCR analysis demonstrated that hucMSC-Ex treatment significantly reduced mRNA expression levels of the pro-inflammatory cytokines IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α compared to the control group. Conversely, mRNA expression of the anti-inflammatory cytokine IL-10 was markedly upregulated (Fig.  A). HucMSC-Ex significantly rescued LPS-induced cytotoxicity, restoring cell viability from 32% to 70.7% (Fig.  B). Further qRT-PCR analysis of M1/M2 polarization marker expression (Fig.  C) revealed that hucMSC-Ex treatment significantly downregulated mRNA expression of the M1 marker iNOS while concurrently upregulating mRNA expression of the M2 marker Arg1. This repolarization was corroborated at the protein level: Western blotting and flow cytometry confirmed the downregulation of iNOS and CD86, with a concurrent, significant expansion of the CD206⁺ M2 population (Fig.  E-G). Functionally, this phenotypic shift was accompanied by reduced motility, as hucMSC-Ex significantly inhibited macrophage migration in wound healing assays (Fig.  D). Collectively, these results demonstrate that hucMSC-Ex, while maintaining macrophage viability, significantly inhibits macrophage migratory behavior and promotes a phenotypic switch towards the anti-inflammatory M2 state.

 

## HucMSC-Ex upregulates FXR and suppresses ferroptosis in murine IBD models
To elucidate the regulatory effects of hucMSC-Ex on FXR expression and ferroptosis in mice, we conducted further mechanistic investigations. Western blot analysis revealed that hucMSC-Ex treatment significantly upregulated colonic FXR protein expression compared to the DSS group. Concurrently, ferroptosis inhibitors GPX4 and MBOAT1 were markedly increased, while the ferroptosis marker ACSL4 was downregulated (Fig.  A). qRT-PCR results (Fig.  B) demonstrated corresponding mRNA expression changes consistent with protein-level observations.Prussian blue staining of colonic sections showed substantial iron granule deposition in DSS-treated mice, whereas hucMSC-Ex intervention effectively attenuated this pathological accumulation (Fig.  C). Immunohistochemical analysis confirmed enhanced FXR positivity and elevated expression of ferroptosis inhibitors GPX4 and MBOAT1 in the hucMSC-Ex group versus DSS controls (Fig.  D-F).Serum oxidative stress assays further delineated hucMSC-Ex-mediated regulation of ferroptosis-related metabolic dysregulation.Malondialdehyde (MDA), a terminal lipid peroxidation product, was significantly reduced, disordered iron metabolism under inflammation was ameliorated, with restoration of serum Fe²⁺ levels and total iron content, andtotal glutathione (GSH) antioxidant capacity increased approximately twofold (Fig.  G-J). Collectively, these data demonstrate that hucMSC-Ex activates the FXR pathway while upregulating key effectors (GPX4/MBOAT1), thereby inhibiting ACSL4-mediated ferroptosis. This mechanism rectifies iron dyshomeostasis and lipid peroxidation, ultimately reestablishing intestinal redox homeostasis to alleviate IBD.

 

## HucMSC-Ex upregulates FXR and suppresses ferroptosis in macrophages
Western blot and qRT-PCR analyses demonstrated that hucMSC-Ex treatment significantly restored the LPS-induced suppression of FXR protein and mRNA expression in RAW264.7 cells. Concurrently, it enhanced expression of the ferroptosis-protective molecules GPX4 and MBOAT1 while markedly inhibiting both protein and mRNA levels of the ferroptosis-promoting factor ACSL4 (Fig.  A, B).Assessment of oxidative stress revealed that hucMSC-Ex effectively reduced intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS) accumulation (Fig.  C) and diminished production of the lipid peroxidation product, malondialdehyde (MDA) (Fig.  D). Analysis of macrophage iron metabolism showed that hucMSC-Ex suppressed abnormal elevations in intracellular ferrous (Fe²⁺) and total iron levels (Fig.  E, F), indicating its role in antagonizing ferroptosis through iron homeostasis regulation.Parallel experiments in THP-1 cells recapitulated these regulatory effects: hucMSC-Ex treatment significantly upregulated FXR and ferroptosis inhibitors (GPX4, MBOAT1) while suppressing ACSL4 expression (Fig.  G, H). Consistently, it reversed pathological manifestations including ROS accumulation, excessive MDA content, and iron overload in THP-1 cells (Fig.  I-L).Immunofluorescence confocal microscopy further validated these findings, demonstrating enhanced intracellular FXR protein expression and intensified GPX4 fluorescence signals in hucMSC-Ex-treated RAW264.7 cells (Fig.  M, N).

 

## HucMSC-Ex attenuates inflammation via FXR-mediated suppression of macrophage ferroptosis
To delineate FXR’s regulatory role in macrophage ferroptosis, we established an enhanced ferroptosis-inflammatory model using co-treatment with the ferroptosis inducer Erastin and LPS (L&E group). Western blot analysis revealed significantly reduced FXR, GPX4, and MBOAT1 protein expression alongside elevated ACSL4 in RAW264.7 and THP-1 cells under L&E conditions versus normal controls (NC). These perturbations were markedly attenuated by either the FXR agonist GW4064 (L&E+GW4064) or hucMSC-Ex. Notably, combined hucMSC-Ex+GW4064 treatment exhibited superior efficacy over monotherapies (Fig.  A, C). IF confirmed diminished FXR nuclear intensity in L&E-treated macrophages, indicating suppressed transcriptional activity (Fig.  B, D). HucMSC-Ex reversed this effect, restoring FXR expression to levels comparable with GW4064 treatment, which demonstrates its potent FXR-activating capacity.​qRT-PCR analysis showed L&E downregulated FXR, GPX4, and MBOAT1 mRNA while upregulating ACSL4 and pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-1β, IL-6, TNF-α), with concomitant IL-10 suppression (Fig.  E, F). HucMSC-Ex counteracted these changes: normalizing ferroptosis-related gene expression, enhancing IL-10, and suppressing pro-inflammatory transcription. Although GW4064 similarly modulated ferroptosis genes, it showed limited efficacy against IL-6 and TNF-α, underscoring hucMSC-Ex’s superior anti-inflammatory properties. The combined intervention (hucMSC-Ex+GW4064) yielded maximal effects, suggesting convergent FXR-targeted pathways.Biochemical assays confirmed L&E-induced elevations in intracellular Fe²⁺ and malondialdehyde (MDA), both attenuated by hucMSC-Ex or GW4064, with maximal suppression in the combination group (Fig.  G, H).Transmission electron microscopy revealed characteristic ferroptotic mitochondria in L&E cells—evidenced by swelling and cristolysis (cristae disintegration)—while hucMSC-Ex restored ultrastructural integrity. GW4064 co-treatment further normalized mitochondrial morphology, indicating FXR activation potentiates exosomal protection against ferroptotic damage (Fig.  I).

 

## Discussion
Exosomes serve not only as valuable diagnostic biomarkers but also as promising therapeutic vectors. In graft-versus-host disease (GVHD), MSC-Ex significantly reduces pro-inflammatory cytokine levels and promote regulatory T cell (Treg) generation, thereby ameliorating clinical symptoms [ ]. Therapeutic efficacy can be further augmented through engineered modifications of exosomal cargo via genetic manipulation or preconditioning strategies [ ]. Notably, hucMSC-Exrepresent a promising therapeutic direction for IBD.
Our integrated analysis of serum bile acid profiles from both IBD patients and hucMSC-Ex-treated DSS-induced mouse IBD models reveals characteristic disruptions in bile acid homeostasis and elucidates regulatory mechanisms underlying hucMSC-Ex intervention. In hucMSC-Ex-treated IBD mice, serum levels of seven bile acids including CA, LCA, and DLCAwere significantly reduced compared to DSS controls, while three taurine-conjugated bile acids(TUDCA, TDCA, and Tα-MCA)showed elevated trends. Crucially, both DSS models and IBD patients displayed consistent CA upregulation compared to healthy groups, which was effectively reversed by hucMSC-Ex treatment.This observation gains significance given Li et al.‘s demonstration that either CA administration or overexpression of CYP8B1 (the key enzyme in hepatic CA synthesis) exacerbates colitis and impairs mucosal barrier repair [ ].Thus, CA downregulation likely constitutes an important mechanism through which hucMSC-Ex alleviates bile acid dysregulation in IBD. Concurrently, we identified significantly reduced expression of the bile acid regulatorFXR in IBD, potentially attributable to inflammation-induced CpG island hypermethylation in the FXR promoter region that silences its expression [ ]. HucMSC-Ex treatment effectively reversed this suppression. Impaired hepatic and intestinal FXR signaling in colitis upregulates CYP8B1 expression, driving excessive production of toxic CA. This bile acid inhibits PPARα-mediated fatty acid oxidation in intestinal stem cells, thereby hindering epithelial regeneration and exacerbating mucosal barrier damage [ ]. Discrepancies in bile acid profiles between human IBD and murine models (e.g., elevated 7-KDCA in clinical specimens but not acute DSS models) may reflect pathophysiological differences between chronic human disease and acute murine injury, limited sample sizes, or variations in disease stages. Indeed, the animal models of IBD (usually induced by DSS or TNBS) mainly simulate acute intestinal damage and inflammation, but cannot fully replicate all the characteristics of chronic, recurrent and immune-disordered features of human Crohn’s disease or ulcerative colitis. Therefore, the long-term efficacy and safety of this therapy in chronic IBD models are still unclear and warrant further longitudinal evaluation. Furthermore, our study has confirmed that hucMSC-Ex can regulate bile acid imbalance and upregulate FXR, and has clearly demonstrated that it can inhibit macrophage ferroptosis through FXR. However, the complex regulatory mechanism between bile acids and FXR has not been fully elucidated, and the causal relationship between the two still needs to be further verified.
HucMSC-Ex treatment attenuated DSS-induced ferroptosis by reducing lipid peroxidation products, correcting iron dysmetabolism, and upregulating ferroptosis inhibitors. While DSS-induced IBD mice exhibited decreased serum iron and Fe²⁺ levels, Prussian blue staining revealed significant iron deposition in colonic tissues. This apparent contradiction may be explained by multiple mechanisms: inflammatory cytokines (e.g.,IL-6, IL-1β) stimulate hepatic hepcidin production [ ], which triggers ferroportin internalization and degradation on enterocytes and macrophages [ ], thereby reducing systemic iron export. Simultaneously, enhanced recruitment and activation of macrophages in inflamed colon tissue promotes phagocytosis of iron-containing cellular debris and bacteria, though impaired iron processing capacity under inflammatory conditions may cause local iron accumulation. Increased intestinal permeability in IBD [ ] further facilitates transmural passage of luminal iron compounds. Additionally, lamina propria macrophages may activate mTORC1 signaling to upregulate serine proteases that degrade transferrin locally, limiting iron transport from enterocytes to circulation [ ]. These interconnected mechanisms collectively explain the observed iron deposition in inflamed tissues alongside systemic iron deficiency.
Regarding the dynamic changes in macrophage subtypes, this study focused on the inhibition of ferroptosis in macrophages, but macrophages undergo a phenotypic transformation from pro-inflammatory (M1) to reparative (M2) during the course of IBD. Whether hucMSC-Ex promotes this phenotypic transformation by inhibiting ferroptosis, or whether the activation of FXR directly affects macrophage polarization, which remains a critical area for future investigation.
While our study elucidates a novel mechanism of hucMSC-Ex-mediated IBD amelioration through FXR-dependent ferroptosis regulation, complete mechanistic understanding requires further investigation. Future directions should include employing FXR-knockout murine models and cell lines to definitively establish FXR’s indispensable role and exclude confounding factors. Furthermore, hucMSC-Ex function as complex vehicles delivering diverse bioactive cargo. Pinpointing the specific molecular determinants (e.g., miRNAs or proteins) responsible for FXR activation represents a critical next step toward developing precise, cell-free therapeutics.

## Conclusion
This study demonstrates that hucMSC-Ex ameliorates IBD pathology through a novel mechanism involving FXR-mediated suppression of macrophage ferroptosis. Clinical specimen analysis revealed characteristic bile acid dysregulation in IBD patients, featuring impaired secondary bile acid synthesis accompanied by significantly reduced FXR expression. Using a DSS-induced murine IBD model, we confirmed that hucMSC-Ex treatment effectively alleviates intestinal inflammation, restores bile acid homeostasis, and ameliorates iron dysmetabolism and lipid peroxidation damage via upregulation of FXR and ferroptosis inhibitors (GPX4, MBOAT1). In vitro experiments further demonstrated that hucMSC-Ex attenuates LPS-induced macrophage inflammation while promoting polarization toward the anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype and reducing pro-inflammatory M1 polarization. Cellular models incorporating ferroptosis inducers and FXR agonists established that hucMSC-Ex exhibits FXR-agonistic properties, potently activating FXR signaling in macrophages. This activation significantly reduces intracellular iron overload and MDA accumulation, ameliorates compromised mitochondrial ultrastructure, and ultimately suppresses macrophage ferroptosis to mitigate inflammation.
