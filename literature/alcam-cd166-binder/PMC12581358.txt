Overall, our study underscores the spatial relationship between CCR1+ macrophages and CD8+ Tex cells in ICB therapy, providing a promising strategy to overcome ICB resistance in melanoma.

## Introduction
Melanoma is a highly aggressive cancer, characterized by early metastasis and poor prognosis [ ]. Due to its high immunogenicity, melanoma has emerged as a prime candidate for immunotherapy. Since the introduction of the first immune checkpoint inhibitors, therapies targeting PD-1, CTLA-4, or LAG-3 have shown durable efficacy in some patients with advanced melanoma [ ]. However, nearly 60% of patients develop resistance to treatment, with some experiencing rapid disease progression [ ]. This underscores the urgent need to investigate the mechanisms underlying melanoma’s resistance to immunotherapy. With the advent of multi-omics technologies, the integration of genomics, transcriptomics, proteomics, and metabolomics data enables deeper exploration of the complex mechanisms driving immunotherapy resistance within the tumor microenvironment (TME) [ , ]. These approaches may provide crucial insights for overcoming current challenges in melanoma immunotherapy.
Increasing evidence suggests that the high heterogeneity, complexity, and interplay within the TME are closely linked to resistance to immunotherapy [ ]. Cytotoxic T cells, specifically CD8+ T cells, are crucial components of the adaptive immune system, and their infiltration and activation are essential for effective antitumor immunity [ ]. Studies have shown that chemokines in the TME, such as CCL3, CCL5, and CXCL10, can recruit CD8+ T cells to infiltrate the tumor core, where they exert cytotoxic effects against tumor cells [ ]. However, continuous exposure to tumor antigens can lead to functional exhaustion of CD8+ T cells, diminishing their ability to kill cancer cells [ ]. Moreover, the cytotoxic activity of CD8+ T cells is regulated and influenced by other immune cell types in the TME, such as tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) [ , ]. TAMs are the predominant immune cell type within the melanoma TME, and their phenotype is shaped by various cells and stimuli present in TME [ , ]. TAMs can differentiate into classically activated M1 macrophages (pro-inflammatory, antitumor) or alternatively activated M2 macrophages (anti-inflammatory, protumor). Beyond the traditional M1/M2 dichotomy, recent research has focused on classifying TAMs based on specific cell surface markers, offering a more nuanced understanding of their functions [ ]. While existing studies emphasize the importance of TAM-CD8+ T cell interactions in the antitumor immune response, the spatiotemporal dynamics of these interactions within the melanoma TME, and their effects on immunotherapy remain poorly understood [ – ].
Given that chemokines in the TME mediate immune cell recruitment, localization, and interaction by binding to specific receptors on target cells [ ], this study first explores the role of chemokine signaling pathways in melanoma immunotherapy. We then investigate chemokine receptor-ligand interactions between immune cell types within the TME. By combining retrospective clinical studies, in vivo animal models, multiplex immunofluorescence staining, and spatial transcriptomics analysis, we aim to elucidate the role of CCR1+ macrophages and CD8+ Tex cell niches in the response to ICB therapy, and to uncover the mechanisms underlying their formation.

## Materials and methods

## Patient samples
Patient tissue samples with a pathological diagnosis of melanoma were included in this study, and none of the patients had received preoperative treatment. All clinical samples were collected at Nanjing Drum Tower Hospital (Nanjing, China). A total of 10 enrolled patients received adjuvant immune checkpoint blockade (ICB) therapy postoperatively. Disease-free survival (DFS) was defined as the time from surgery until disease recurrence or death from any cause. Informed consent was obtained from all patients prior to sample collection, and the use of these samples was approved by the Ethics Committee for Research at Nanjing Drum Tower Hospital (2024-282-01).

## Bulk RNA-seq datasets
Normalized gene expression data for melanoma was downloaded from the TCGA-SKCM data portal (https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/). Gene expression profiles of melanoma patients treated with ICB were obtained from the Gene Expression Omnibus (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/).

## Single-Cell RNA-seq datasets
Single-cell transcriptomics datasets for melanoma patients treated with ICB were obtained from the Gene Expression Omnibus (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/). The raw counts were filtered, normalized, integrated, and scaled using the Seurat R package (v4.2.0). Utilizing two heuristic methods in Seurat (i.e., the modified JackStraw procedure and the ranking variance method), the top 30 principal components were selected for further non-linear dimensional reduction (UMAP) and unsupervised clustering analysis.

## Spatial transcriptomics dataset
Spatial transcriptomics (ST) data for melanoma was downloaded from the 10X Genomics ST Platform. The data was analyzed and visualized using the Seurat package (versions 4.2.1) in R. The dataset, sequenced using the 10X Genomics ST platform “1k,” were processed according to the following filtering criteria: (1) genes found in at least five capture spots with a total count of ≥ 100 were retained; and (2) capture spots containing ≥ 500 total transcripts were retained. Dimensionality reduction and clustering were performed using principal component analysis (PCA) at a resolution of 0.8, considering the first 25 principal components. Spatial feature expression plots were generated using the SpatialFeaturePlot function in Seurat (version 4.2.1).

## Defining the signature score of the KEGG chemokine signaling pathway
Single-sample gene set enrichment analysis (ssGSEA) [ ] was employed to calculate the absolute enrichment score of the KEGG chemokine signaling pathway in single-cell RNA sequencing data. In bulk RNA sequencing data, gene set variation analysis (GSVA) was used to determine the enrichment score for the same pathway. Patients were then classified into two subgroups—high expression and low expression-based on the median score.

## Defining the CCR1+ macrophage and exhausted T cell scores
To quantify the enrichment scores of CCR1+ macrophages and exhausted T cells, GSVA was employed. The analysis employed two predefined gene signatures derived from human gene lists. For CCR1+ macrophages, the signature included CCR1, CD68, CD163, CD206, CSF1R, FCN1, and VCAN, while the signature for exhausted T cells comprised HAVCR2, TIGIT, LAG3, PDCD1, CXCL13, and LAYN.

## Differential expression analysis and GSEA
Differential expression analysis was performed to identify differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between responder (R, defined as partial/complete responder) and non-responder (NR, defined as progressive disease) groups towards ICB treatment in the melanoma dataset using the DESeq2 package (version 1.38.0). DEGs were considered significant if they met the criteria of log2 fold-change >1.5 or < − 1.5, with a false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.05. Gene ranks were generated based on the log2 fold-change values. Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) was then conducted to evaluate the KEGG chemokine signaling pathway using the Molecular Signatures Database (MSigDB, version 7.151) [ ]. Pathways with Benjamini-Hochberg adjusted p-values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

## Immune infiltration analysis
CIBERSORT, a deconvolution algorithm based on linear support vector regression, was used to analyze the immune cell subtype expression matrix. This method leverages RNA-Seq data to estimate the relative abundance of immune cells within a given sample [ ]. The CIBERSORT R package was applied to quantify 22 immune cell types across disease and control samples. Immune cell composition was visualized through boxplots, and differences in immune cell proportions were assessed using the Wilcoxon test. A P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

## Cell-cell interaction analysis
To systematically understand cell-cell communication among immune cell types in the tumor ecosystem, the CellChat package (http://www.cellchat.org/) was employed to explore ligand-receptor interactions between different immune cells in melanoma. For subsequent analysis, we selected the most highly expressed receptors and ligands within specific clusters.

## Developmental trajectory analysis
To explore potential functional changes and lineage differentiation of immune cells, the Monocle algorithm (version 3.03.0) was used to infer the developmental trajectory of CD8+ T cells. Expression data for the indicated cell type, outputted from Seurat, was converted into a CellDataSet object as input for Monocle3. Dimensional reduction and cell ordering were then performed using DDRTree, based on significant differentially expressed genes between subclusters (calculated via the FindAllMarkers function in Seurat). Finally, the differentiation trajectory and pseudotime of the cells were inferred and visualized using the default parameters of Monocle3.

## Analysis of mixed cell population with Deconvolution
To estimate the proportions of various cell types within regional spots of transcriptomic data, we utilized the SPOTlight algorithm. This method leverages spatially resolved transcriptomic data to perform cell-type deconvolution and estimate the cellular composition of distinct tissue regions. For reference, we incorporated single-cell RNA sequencing datasets (GSE120575 and GSE72056), which include annotations for eight major cell types: melanoma cells, B cells, CD4 + T cells, CD8 + T effector (Tex) cells, CD8 + T cells 2, CCR1 + macrophages, cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs), and endothelial cells. Spatial visualizations of the estimated cell type distributions were generated using R software (version 4.2). These visualizations enabled a comprehensive examination of cellular localization patterns within the tissue context. Furthermore, to explore the relationships between cell types, we calculated correlation coefficients for the spot-level cellular abundances. The resulting correlation matrix was visualized using heatmaps, which provided insight into the co-occurrence and potential interactions between different cell populations across spatial regions.

## Immunohistochemistry (IHC) analysis
Human tumor samples were collected from treatment-naive melanoma patients post-surgery and prepared as 5 ㎛ formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded (FFPE) sections. For CCR1 IHC, endogenous peroxidase activity was blocked by incubating the sections in 3% H2O2 methanol solution. Antigen retrieval was performed by heating the slides in 10 mmol/L citrate buffer (pH 6.0) for 15 min. The sections were then washed with deionized water, rinsed in PBST, and blocked with 5% BSA (Beyotime) in PBST for 30 min at room temperature. Following this, the sections were incubated overnight at 4 ℃ with a primary antibody against human CCR1 (Absin, abs133089, 1:200) in a humidified chamber. After washing with PBST, the sections were incubated with a peroxidase-labeled polymer conjugated to monoclonal anti-rabbit IgG for 1 h. The immune reaction was visualized under a ZEISS microscope. Two independent pathologists from Drum Tower Hospital, blinded to the sample groupings and clinical data, evaluated the IHC staining and quantified the number of CCR1-positive cells per high-power field (HPF).

## Multiplexed Immunofluorescence (IF) staining
Multiplex immunofluorescence staining was performed using the PANO 5-plex IHC kit (Panovue, China). Tissue Sect. (5 μm) from formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) specimens were first deparaffinized, followed by sequential incubation with primary antibodies. For human melanoma tissue, the primary antibodies included CCR1 (Absin, abs133089, 1:200), CD68 (Proteintech, 66231-2-Ig, 1:3000), CD8 (Proteintech, 66231-2-Ig, 1:250), and TIM3 (CST, 45208, 1:400). For mouse melanoma tissue, the primary antibodies included CCR1 (Thermo Fisher, PA1-41062, 1:200), F4/80 (CST, 70076, 1:200), CD8 (CST, 98941, 1:200), and TIM3 (CST, 83882, 1:200). This was followed by incubation with secondary antibodies and their corresponding Opal fluorophores. Nuclei were stained with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI). As a negative control, tissue slides were incubated with primary and secondary antibodies without fluorophores to assess autofluorescence. Multiplex-stained slides were scanned using the PanoVIEW VS200 System (Panovue, China) at wavelength intervals of 20 nm, ranging from 440 to 780 nm, with a fixed exposure time. All scans for each slide were superimposed to create a single composite image. Multilayer images were then imported into inForm v.2.4.8 (Akoya Biosciences) for quantitative image analysis. Three randomly selected tumor regions from each patient were analyzed, including the spatial position of each cell in the tissue (x- and y-coordinates of the nuclei) and the corresponding cell type. Spatial relationships between all cells within the TME were examined using the first-nearest neighbor (1-NN) statistic, as implemented in the spatstats package.

## Animal experiment
C57BL/6 mice were purchased from Cavens Laboratory Animal Co., (Changzhou, China). To establish a syngeneic cancer model, B16F10 cells (3 × 10^5), mixed with Matrigel, were injected into the right inguinal fat pad of 8-week-old female C57BL/6 mice (n = 5 per group). Two days after injection, the mice were randomly assigned to treatment groups and administered combinations of the following drugs: subcutaneous injection of 20 mg/kg CCR1 antagonist (BX471, provided by Selleck) every 8 h starting from day 4; intraperitoneal injection of mouse PD-1 antibody (anti-mPD-1, provided by Junshi Biosciences) every 72 h; or IgG2a (isotype control for anti-mPD-1, C1.18.4, Bio X Cell BE0085). Tumor size was measured every 72 h, and mice were euthanized when the ethical endpoint was reached. After treatment, tumors were harvested for flow cytometry analysis. All animals were housed in pathogen-free facilities at Nanjing University Medical School Affiliated Drum Tower Hospital (Nanjing, China). All animal experiments were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Drum Tower Hospital (approval number: 2020AE01064) and conducted in accordance with guidelines approved by MD Anderson’s Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee.

## Flow cytometry analysis
Single-cell suspensions were prepared from tumor tissues immediately upon mouse sacrifice. To detect cell-surface marker expression (CD3, CD8a, CD80, CD163, CD11b, F4/80, CCR1, CD44 and CD69), these suspensions were stained with mouse antibodies for 30 min at 4 °C, then washed twice in PBS. Stained cells were analyzed using the BD Accuri C6 PLUS Flow Cytometer (BD Biosciences). All raw data were processed using FlowJo software (version 10.4, Tree Star). The following antibodies were used: anti-mouse CD3 (APC/Cyanine7, #100222), anti-mouse CD8a (PerCP/Cyanine5.5, #100734), anti-mouse CD80 (PE, #104708), anti-mouse CD163 (APC, #155306), anti-mouse CD11b (Brilliant Violet 605, #101257), anti-mouse F4/80 (PE, #111603 and FITC, #123107), anti-mouse CCR1 (FITC, #152506), anti-mouse CD44 (PE, #103007), and anti-mouse CD69 (Brilliant Violet 421, #104545), all from BioLegend. Isotype control antibodies included anti-mouse IgG1 (PE, #400408), anti-mouse IgG1 (APC, #400412), anti-mouse IgG1 (FITC, #400406), and anti-mouse IgG1 (PerCP/Cyanine5.5, #400410).

## Cell culture
Raw264.7 cell line was purchased from the Cell Bank of Chinese Academy of Science (Shanghai, China). Raw264.7 cells were cultured in RPIM 1640 medium, supplemented with 10% FBS and 100 U/mL penicillin-streptomycin. These cells were cultured in a humidifying incubator at 37 ℃ with 5% CO2.

## Chemotaxis assay of RAW264.7 cells
Chemotactic response assays were performed using Transwell chambers with 8 μm pores (Corning, Cat# 3422). RAW264.7 cells were seeded in the upper chamber, and the indicated medium, supplemented with or without mCCL3 (200 nM, MCE, Cat# P7255) and the CCR1 inhibitor BX471, was carefully added to the lower chamber. The cells were then cultured for 24 h at 37 ℃ and 5% CO2. After incubation, the Transwell insert was removed, and non-migrated cells on the upper surface were gently wiped away using a cotton swab. Migrated cells on the lower surface were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, stained with crystal violet solution (Beyotime, Cat# C0121) for 10 min at room temperature,, washed with ddH2O, and photographed.

## Statistical analysis
R software (version 4.2.0) and GraphPad Prism 7.0 software were used for data analysis and graphic representation. For comparisons between two groups, a two-tailed Student t test was used when data were normally distributed, and the Wilcoxon rank sum test was used when data were not normally distributed. All data are presented as mean ± SD. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. ns, not significant; *, P < 0.05; **, P < 0.01; ***, P < 0.001; ****, P < 0.0001.

## Results

## Correlation of intercellular chemokine signaling intensity between TAMs and CD8+ T Cells with melanoma immunotherapy efficacy
To explore the role of chemokine signaling in immunotherapy resistance and its impact on TIME in melanoma, we analyzed the immunotherapy-related scRNA-seq dataset (GSE120575). Single-cell samples collected prior to immunotherapy (Supplementary Table 1) were selected, and clusters were annotated into seven major cell types based on the average expression of canonical markers (Fig. A, Supplementary Table 2). The chemokine signaling pathway activity, referred to as the “chemokine score,” was calculated for each cell, revealing elevated scores in CD8+ T cells and macrophages (Fig. B). As previously reported [ ], patients were categorized into responder (R) and non-responder (NR) groups based on their immunotherapy response. Comparing the immune cell proportions between the two groups, we found that CD8+ T cells and macrophages were more abundant in the NR group, while CD4+ T cells and B cells were more abundant in the R group (Fig. C). Further analysis confirmed that both CD8+ T cells and macrophages had consistently higher chemokine scores, with significantly elevated scores in the NR group compared to the R group (Fig. D). These findings suggest that chemokine signaling, particularly in CD8+ T cells and macrophages, may play a pivotal role in driving immunotherapy resistance in melanoma.

 

To further characterize the immune response and explore the interactions between chemokine signaling and various immune cell populations, we estimated the abundance of 24 immune cell types, including CD8+ T cells, CD4+ T cell subsets, macrophages, B cells, and others. Melanoma samples with high chemokine scores exhibited higher abundances of CD8+ T cells, M1 macrophages, and regulatory T cells (Tregs), while samples with low chemokine scores had higher abundances of M0 and M2 macrophages (Fig. E). Additionally, we observed a positive correlation between the chemokine score and the abundance of CD8+ T cells, M1 macrophages, and Tregs, while a negative correlation was found with CD4 memory T cells and M0/M2 macrophages. These findings further suggest that chemokine signaling plays a critical role in regulating CD8+ T cells and macrophages within the TIME. To better understand the impact of chemokine signaling on immunotherapy efficacy, we analyzed two melanoma patient cohorts undergoing anti-PD-L1 treatment [ , ]. Our analysis revealed that the KEGG chemokine signaling pathway was enriched in the non-responsive (NR) group (Fig. G). Moreover, samples with high chemokine scores were more prevalent in the NR group (Fig. H). Taken together, the integration of bulk RNA-seq and scRNA-seq data suggest that chemokine signaling modulates CD8+ T cells and macrophages, thereby influencing the response to anti-PD-1 therapy.

## CCR1 is primarily expressed in macrophages
To investigate the role of chemokine signaling across immune cells in the TIME of melanoma, we analyzed differential intercellular communications between the NR and R groups. Compared with the R group, chemokine signaling interactions involving CD8+ T cells and macrophages were significantly increased in the NR group (Supplementary Fig. 1A-B). Notably, CCR1-associated chemokine signaling, including CCR1-CCL3 and CCR1-CCL3L1, played a more prominent role in immunotherapy resistance (Fig.  A). Using the TCGA-SKCM dataset, we further revealed that CCR1 expression was predominantly localized within tumor tissue, in contrast to normal tissue (Supplementary Fig. 2). To identify the immune cell types expressing CCR1 within the melanoma TIME, we analyzed five scRNA-seq and one spatial transcriptomics datasets from publicly available melanoma datasets. Our findings showed that CCR1 expression was primarily enriched in macrophages (Fig.  B-C and Supplementary Fig. 3A-D). Additionally, we confirmed the colocalization of CCR1 with macrophage markers CD68 in human melanoma tissues and F4/80 in mouse melanoma tissues (Fig.  D and Supplementary Fig. 4). Collectively, these results indicate that CCR1 is predominantly expressed in macrophages within the melanoma TIME.

 

## Impact of CCR1+ macrophages on the response to immunotherapy in melanoma
To further explore the impact of CCR1+ macrophages on the efficacy of immunotherapy, we analyzed the RNA-seq dataset of melanoma patients treated with ICB (GSE78220), which included 13 non-responders and 15 responders. We observed that both CCR1 expression and the CCR1+ macrophage score were significantly higher in the NR group compared to the R group (Fig.  A-B). We also assessed CCR1 expression in macrophages from the scRNA-seq dataset (GSE120575) and found that CCR1 expression in macrophages was significantly elevated in the NR group (Fig.  C and Supplementary Fig. 5). Additionally, we collected tumor tissue samples from treatment-naive melanoma patients in our hospital who underwent post-surgical ICB therapy. CCR1-positive cells per HPF were quantified in each sample. Patients were followed for DFS and classified into good responder and poor responder groups based on the median DFS. We found that patients with shorter DFS tended to have a higher number of CCR1-positive cells per HPF (Fig.  D and Supplementary Fig. 6, P = 0.0724). Given the established association of exhausted T cell-related genes, such as PDCD1, HAVCR2 (TIM-3), CTLA4, and LAG3, with the efficacy of ICB therapy in melanoma, we further examined the relationship between CCR1 expression, the CCR1+ macrophage score, and exhausted T cell gene expression. Both CCR1 expression and the CCR1+ macrophage score were significantly positively correlated with the expression of exhausted T cell genes (Supplementary Fig. 7 and Fig.  E). Additionally, the CCR1+ macrophage score showed a significant positive correlation with the exhausted T cell score (Supplementary Fig. 8). In conclusion, our findings suggest that a high CCR1+ macrophage score is associated with resistance to ICB therapy in melanoma. These results highlight a critical role for CCR1+ macrophages in shaping the response to immunotherapy in melanoma, offering a potential target for overcoming treatment resistance.

 

## Combined CCR1 antagonist and PD-1 Blockade suppresses tumor progression in melanoma model
To investigate the role of CCR1+ macrophages in modulating the immune response within the TIME of melanoma, we analyzed B16F10 tumor-bearing mice treated with BX471 (a CCR1 antagonist) [ , ] and ICB in combination or alone (Fig. A). Our results showed that the combination of BX471 and ICB significantly improved therapeutic efficacy compared to the placebo or monotherapy groups (Fig. B and Supplementary Fig. 9). Flow cytometry analysis of the TIME in B16F10 tumor-bearing mice revealed notable differences in macrophage and CD8+ T cell populations. Specifically, the combination therapy resulted in a significant decrease in the percentage of CCR1+ macrophages, whereas BX471 monotherapy did not induce a significant change (Fig. C). Given the established impact of macrophage polarization on ICB response, we further analyzed macrophage subtypes. In the combination treatment group, the percentage of M2 macrophages significantly decreased compared to placebo or monotherapy, while no notable changes were detected in M1 macrophages (Fig. D-E). Interestingly, BX471 monotherapy showed no significant differences in either M1 or M2 macrophage populations compared to placebo (Supplementary Table 3). Previous studies have highlighted the strong correlation between macrophage infiltration, CD8+ T cell exhaustion, and resistance to immunotherapy within the TIME [ , ]. Our earlier findings suggested that chemokine signaling between macrophages and CD8+ T cells impact immunotherapy efficacy. Therefore, we further investigated the status of CD8+ T cells. Flow cytometry analysis revealed a marked reduction in the percentage of TIM3+ or PD-1+ CD8+ T cells in the combination treatment group, indicating a significant alleviation of CD8+ T cell exhaustion (Fig. F-G). However, no notable changes in TIM3+ or PD-1+ CD8+ T cells were observed in the BX471 monotherapy group compared to placebo (Supplementary Table 3). Given that CD69 is a marker of early CD8+ T cell activation [ ] and CD44 is a marker of T-cell activation [ ], we also evaluated these markers on CD8+ T cells. We found a significant decrease in the percentage of CD69+ CD8+ T cells in the combination treatment group compared to BX471 monotherapy (Fig. H). However, no differences were observed in the percentage of CD44+ CD8+ T cells across treatment groups (Supplementary Fig. 10). In summary, the combination of BX471 and ICB led to a marked reduction in CCR1+ macrophages, alleviation of CD8+ T cell exhaustion, and an overall improvement in tumor burden, highlighting the therapeutic potential of targeting CCR1+ macrophages in combination with PD-1 blockade.

 

## Association of CCR1+ macrophages and CD8+ Tex cell niches with resistance to ICB therapy
As previously mentioned, the combination of a CCR1 antagonist and ICB significantly reduces CD8+ T cell exhaustion in melanoma model. To further explore this, we assessed the spatial organization of CCR1+ macrophages and CD8+ exhausted T (Tex) cells by performing spatial transcriptomics (ST) on melanoma tissue using the 10X Genomics ST platform. By scoring tissue spots based on marker genes of CCR1+ macrophages and CD8+ Tex cells from melanoma scRNA-seq data (GSE120575), we identified clusters with CCR1+ macrophages and CD8+ Tex cells co-localized in the same spot (Fig. A and Supplementary Fig. 11A-B). Additionally, immunofluorescent labeling confirmed the close proximity of CCR1+ macrophages and TIM3+ CD8+ Tex cells in human melanoma tissue (Fig. B). Analysis of the nearest distance between cells in the TIME revealed that CCR1+ macrophages were spatially closer to TIM3+ CD8+ T cells compared to CCR1− macrophages or CCR1+ macrophages to TIM3− CD8+ T cells (Fig. C). This suggests a potential recruitment mechanism between these two cell types. Further, we evaluated the spatial relationship between CCR1+ macrophages and CD8+ Tex cells in the B16F10 tumor-bearing mouse model using mIF (Fig. D). In the PD-1 monotherapy group, a substantial accumulation of CCR1+ macrophages was observed around CD8+ Tex cells within the TME. In contrast, in the CCR1 inhibitor monotherapy group, CCR1+ macrophages were markedly reduced around CD8+ Tex cells, although the total number of CCR1+ macrophages in the TME was not significantly different between the two groups. This may be due to macrophage recruitment to the TME via CCR5 signaling [ , ]. When the CCR1 inhibitor was combined with PD-1 blockade, a significant reduction in CD8+ Tex cells was observed in the TME, which was consistent with the flow cytometry results (Fig. F). These findings suggest that disrupting the niche relationship between CCR1+ macrophages and CD8+ Tex cells could enhance the efficacy of ICB therapy.

 

## CCL3-CCR1 axis mediates the formation of CCR1+ macrophage and CD8+ Tex cell niches
To further investigate the mechanisms underlying the formation of CCR1+ macrophage and CD8+ Tex cell niches, we extracted macrophages and CD8+ T cells from the melanoma scRNA-seq dataset (GSE120575), which included 156 macrophages and 2279 CD8+ T cells. Using marker-based annotation, we classified the macrophages into CCR1+ and CCR1− subsets, and the CD8 + T cells into four subsets: CD8+ exhausted T cells (Tex), CD8+ central memory T cells (Tcm), CD8+ effector memory T cells (Tem), and CD8+ naïve T cells (Tn) (Supplementary Tables 2 and Supplementary Fig. 12 A). CD8+ Tex cells and CCR1+ macrophages exhibited increased proportions in the NR group (Supplementary Fig. 12B). In the NR group, CCR1+ macrophages exhibited a higher number and strength of interactions with CD8+ Tex cells (Supplementary Fig. 12 C). Next, we further investigated crosstalk between CCR1+ macrophages and CD8+ Tex cells. CellChat analysis revealed strong interactions between CCR1+ macrophages and CD8+ Tex cells in the NR group, with high activity in the CCR1-CCL3, CCR1-CCL3L1, and CCR5-CCL3 signaling (Fig.  A). Notably, CCL3 was predominantly expressed in CD8+ Tex cells, CCL3L1 in CCR1+ macrophages, and CCR5 in CD8+ Tex/em cells (Fig.  B). To explore the relationship between CCR1 ligands and CD8+ T cell exhaustion, we performed trajectory analysis of CD8+ T cells, aligning them along a differentiation trajectory (Supplementary Fig. 13). We found that, along with T cell exhaustion-related genes (PDCD1, HAVCR2), CCL3 expression progressively increased as CD8+ T cells exhaustion (Fig.  C). However, other CCR1 ligands, such as CCL4 and CCL5, showed no significant changes along the CD8+ T cell exhaustion trajectory (Fig.  D). Moreover, melanoma CCL3 expression was significantly correlated with CD8 + Tex cell exhaustion scores in melanoma (Fig.  E). Additionally, in vitro sorted human CD8+ T cells subjected to exhaustion induction showed a significant increase in CCL3 concentration in their conditioned medium (Fig.  F, Supplementary Fig. 14). To validate these findings, we generated a homozygous sg-CCR1 Raw264.7 cell line and performed an in vitro chemotaxis assay using Transwell chambers (Fig.  G). RAW264.7 cells (2 × 10⁵) were plated in the upper chamber, while recombinant mCCL3 or the CCR1 antagonist BX471 was added to the lower compartment. Migrated cells were stained with crystal violet. Notably, mCCL3 significantly promoted RAW264.7 cell migration, an effect that was markedly suppressed by both BX471 and CCR1 knockout (Fig.  H-I). These results suggest that the CCR1-CCL3 axis is crucial for the recruitment of CCR1+ macrophages to CD8+ Tex cells within the melanoma TME.

 

## Discussion
Chemokines are well-known for shaping the TME and influencing the immune response to tumors by orchestrating immune cell recruitment, spatial positioning, and facilitating cell-cell interactions. However, the inherent complexity of the chemokine system-marked by its apparent redundancy and seemingly contradictory roles in tumor biology-has posed significant challenges to achieving a comprehensive understanding of its multifaceted contributions and clinical implications for cancer patients. In this study, we leveraged an integrated approach, combining bulk, single-cell, and spatial transcriptomics of melanoma, to elucidate the pivotal roles of chemokine signaling in immune regulation, focusing specifically on the CCR1+ macrophage and CD8+ Tex cell niches.
Macrophages are present in the TME throughout all stages of tumor development, where they can give rise to TAMs with a broad range of differentiation states that variably exhibit characteristics of IFNγ-induced classical M1 or IL-4-induced alternative M2 polarization [ ]. TAMs have been reported to promote tumor progression through various mechanisms, including enhancing tumor cell invasion and promoting angiogenesis [ ]. While it is well-established that CCR2 facilitates the recruitment of monocyte-derived macrophages into the TME [ ], recent studies have identified other chemokine receptors, such as CCR1, CXCR1, and CXCR2, as alternative pathways for TAM infiltration into tumors [ ]. Interestingly, monocytes downregulate CCR2 and instead upregulate CCR1 and CCR5 during their differentiation into macrophages in the TME [ ]. In our study, we found that non-responders towards ICB therapy had elevated chemokine signaling activity, and CCR1+ macrophages were associated with immunotherapy response in melanoma. Notably, the combination of the CCR1 antagonist BX471 with ICB significantly reduced tumor burden in mice, which was attributed to the depletion of CD8+ Tex cells and M2 macrophages. Le et al. demonstrated that BX471 monotherapy significantly reduced tumor size in the FC-muMCL1 mouse model, a syngeneic model of mantle cell lymphoma (MCL), by depleting M2 macrophages and increasing the infiltration of cytotoxic CD8+ T cells. Interestingly, CCR1 depletion in a nonalcoholic steatohepatitis (NASH) model was found to reduce hepatic oxidative stress and shift macrophage polarization from the pro-inflammatory M1 to the anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype [ ]. Although these differing results may be attributed to variations in disease types, organ heterogeneity, and treatment approaches, they collectively highlight the critical importance of the crosstalk between CCR1+ macrophages and CD8+ Tex cells in shaping the immunosuppressive TME.
The spatial relationships between immune cells in the TME are closely linked to the efficacy of tumor immunotherapy. Among these, the infiltration and activation state of CD8+ T cells, as well as the polarization of TAMs, play critical roles in antitumor immunity and have thus been the focus of many studies. Nixon et al. found that TAMs promote T cell exhaustion through the transcription factor IRF8 in cancer, but their study did not clarify the spatial interactions between these cells at the single-cell level [ ]. Similarly, Li et al. [ ] identified that MS4A4A is selectively expressed by TAMs, and its inhibition in vivo curbed tumor growth and improved the efficacy of ICB therapy by reducing the infiltration of M2-TAMs and exhausted T cells, though the spatial relationship between these cell types was not further explored. In contrast, Kersten et al. [ ] demonstrated a spatiotemporal co-dependency between macrophages and exhausted CD8+ T cells in cancer, showing that TAMs and CD8+ T cells engage in unique, long-lasting, antigen-specific synaptic interactions that fail to activate T cells but instead prime them for exhaustion, a process that is further accelerated under hypoxic conditions. Similarly, Xun et al. [ ] found that ALCAMhigh macrophages co-localize with exhausted CD8+ T cells in the tumor spatial microenvironment and promote T cell exhaustion around the tumor boundary. However, the mechanisms by which TAMs and exhausted CD8+ T cells recruit and localize near each other in the TME remain unclear. In our study, we found that CCR1+ macrophages and CD8+ Tex cells were spatially proximal in the TME, and the CCR1+ macrophage-CD8+ Tex cell niche was correlated with ICB treatment response. Moreover, CCL3, one of the ligands for CCR1 [ ], exhibited progressively increased expression as CD8+ T cells became exhausted, suggesting that the CCR1-CCL3 chemokine signaling axis may contribute to the formation of CCR1+ macrophages and CD8+ Tex cells niches. Previous research has shown that activated cytotoxic T lymphocytes (CTLs) themselves can become a rich source of CCR5 ligands such as CCL3 and CCL4, thereby recruiting multiple CCR5+ CTLs with the ability to kill cancer cells [ , ]. Our study also found that CCR5 is predominantly expressed in CD8+ Tex/em cells. Additionally, CCR2 and CCR5 are known to facilitate interactions between T cells and monocytes or macrophages in inflamed tissues, regulating antimicrobial activities [ ]. Therefore, CCR5 may also direct CTLs to interact with monocytes and macrophages, enhancing their tumoricidal functions and guiding them to sites of CCL3, CCL4, and CCL5 secretion by other CTLs, potentially resulting in a “swarming” behavior. These findings highlight the possibility that multiple chemokine signaling pathways are involved in the interactions between TAMs and CD8+ Tex cells in the TME, warranting further in-depth investigation.
In conclusion, our study highlights the critical role of CCR1+ macrophage and CD8+ Tex cell niches in the response to ICB therapy in melanoma. Targeting the chemokine signaling between CCR1+ macrophages and CD8+ Tex cells may provide a promising strategy to overcome ICB resistance and improve therapeutic outcomes. However, given the limitations of animal models and the limited specificity of CCR1 antagonists, further studies using genetically engineered mouse models with macrophage-specific CCR1 deletion across multiple melanoma animal models are warranted to validate the spatial interaction between CCR1+ macrophages and CD8+ T cells.
