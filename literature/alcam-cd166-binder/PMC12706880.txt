## Background
COL4A1 defects are known to cause a variety of multisystem disorders with significant vascular dysfunction leading to neuronal damage. Case reports suggest that patients with COL4A1 mutations or extracellular COL4A1 deficiency in the basement membrane may put individuals at increased risk for developing visual deficits with neurotrauma. However, no experimental evidence is available. This study investigated the impact of Col4a1 deficiency on visual dysfunction following mild traumatic brain injury (mTBI) and evaluated the therapeutic efficacy of COL4A1-enriched adipose-derived stem cell-conditioned medium (ASC-CCM) in mitigating associated neurovascular deficits.

## Methods
Using a retina-targeted knockdown approach in C57Bl/6 mice via intravitreal delivery of AAV2-Col4a1 shRNA, followed by a controlled 50-psi air-blast to induce mTBI, we assessed visual performance, retinal histopathology, and gene expression profiles for 4 weeks post-injury. Treatment with ASC-CCM was administered intravitreally post-blast. In-vitro, Col4a1 knockdown in human retinal endothelial cells (HRECs) assessed the therapeutic benefit of COL4A1-enriched ASC-CCM.

## Results
After blast injury, Col4a1-deficient mice displayed significantly greater reductions in visual acuity and contrast sensitivity thresholds compared to control mice, which were substantially restored following ASC-CCM treatment. Histological and molecular analyses revealed marked glial activation, vascular instability, and synaptic disorganization in Col4a1-deficient retinas post-injury, which were attenuated upon ASC-CCM administration. In-vitro assays further confirmed that COL4A1 plays a crucial role in endothelial integrity. After Col4a1 knockdown, HRECs showed impaired cell migration and increased leukocyte transmigration, effects that were reversed by treatment with COL4A1-enriched, but not COL4A1-depleted, ASC-CCM. Moreover, COL4A1-enriched ASC-CCM suppressed inflammatory responses in cytokine-stimulated microglia and stabilized TNF-α-induced endothelial permeability.

## Conclusions
These findings collectively identify COL4A1 deficiency as a sensitizing factor for post-traumatic visual dysfunction and demonstrate that ASC-CCM exerts therapeutic effects by preserving retinal vascular structure and modulating inflammatory responses, positioning it as a promising candidate for treating TBI-related ocular neurovascular injury.

## Introduction
Traumatic brain injury (TBI) is a significant global public health issue, affecting millions annually, leading to a range of neurological and sensory impairments, including vision-related deficits, with eye serving as the window to these pathologies [ ]. According to estimates by the Global Burden of Disease (GBD) 2016 Study, approximately 27.2 million new TBI cases were reported worldwide, with falls identified as the predominant cause in 74% of cases across various regions [ ]. Additionally, sports and recreational injuries contribute to TBIs with ~ 3.42 million emergency department visits during 2001–2012 [ ]. Vulnerable populations, such as the elderly and children, exhibit a higher susceptibility to fall-related TBIs, highlighting the necessity for preventive strategies. Other contributing factors to TBI include pedestrian road accidents (14%), motor vehicle collisions (5%), and conflict-related injuries (2%) [ ]. Vision in TBI is underrepresented, and the number of active clinical trials in this field are sparse [ ]. Frequently, visual problems are overlooked at the time of TBI, often resulting in progressive vision loss, and lengthening and impairing rehabilitation. TBI can be either penetrative or non-penetrative, associated with degeneration of neurons, apoptotic cell death, inflammation, microglial activation, hemorrhage associated with vascular dysfunction; however, precise animal modeling that mimics the extensive visual deficits of TBI pathology remains elusive. Although many approaches have been tested in animal models and human clinical trials [ ], effective therapies for mitigating neurotrauma effects have not been developed. To this end, leveraging an established murine model of mild traumatic brain injury (mTBI) [ , ], we previously showed the beneficial effects of intravitreal administration of adipose tissue-derived concentrated conditioned medium (ASC-CCM) containing both soluble molecules and extracellular vesicles in improving the visual deficits post-injury [ – ].
The basement membrane (BM) provides structural support to the cells and serves as the reservoir of growth factors that direct and fine-tune cellular functions in the retina [ ]. Collagens are essential for cell-cell interactions and cell attachment to the BM. Among the family of more than 28 different kinds of collagen identified thus far, collagen, type IV (COL4, all caps for protein), is one of the nonfibrillar collagens and is a principal constituent of all BMs of vascular endothelium. COL4 makes up about 50% of BM components, with Laminin, Nidogen, and Perlecan contributing to the rest [ , ]. Among the six distinct isoforms of COL4, COL4A1 and COL4A2 predominantly constitute retinal and choroidal vessels [ ]. Both COL4A1 and COL4A2 assemble into heterotrimers in the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) and advance through a secretory pathway to form BM. Mutations in Col4a1 (italics for gene) are known to cause a variety of multisystem disorders with significant vascular dysfunction leading to neuronal damage [ , ]. Most of the mutations in the COL4 gene reside in the collagenous domain that contains a long stretch of Gly-Xaa-Yaa repeats, forming a triple helix, resulting in misfolding and defective secretion of mutant COL4A1, building up in ER resulting in stress [ ]. Although it is unclear if the pathogenicity of such mutation stems from intracellular retention leading to cellular toxicity or from extracellular COL4A1deficiency in BMs, it is well accepted that the pathogenic events are tissue specific [ ]. While intracellular retention is associated with cerebral hemorrhages [ ]; peripheral neuropathy almost certainly involves extracellular deficiency [ ]. Interestingly, human subjects developing cerebral vasculopathy after trauma without underlying significant cardiovascular risk factors have been found to have Col4a1 mutations [ , ] suggesting that patients with Col4a1 mutations or extracellular COL4A1 deficiency in BMs may be at risk for developing increased visual deficits with neurotrauma. A greater understanding of Col4a1 defects as related to neurotrauma must be explored.
Recently a rat model of low-level blast exposure demonstrated loss of COL4, microglial activation and cognitive and behavioral alterations [ ], suggesting a close connection between BM changes and trauma associated neurodegeneration. Serendipitously we found in blast mice that received saline a significant reduction in Col4a1 mRNA in the retina, with no other collagens affected by neurotrauma. Since Col4a1 gene expression is reduced following mTBI injury, and dysfunctional COL4A1 is associated with retinal vascular insults [ ], we reasoned that COL4A1 deficiency might contribute to the injury process in TBI and thus serve as a therapeutic target in neurotrauma. Given the essential role of COL4A1 in maintaining retinal vascular integrity and its reported vulnerability in the setting of TBI, this study further examined whether COL4A1 deficiency contributes to exacerbated visual dysfunction following mTBI. Additionally, we assessed the therapeutic efficacy of COL4A1-enriched ASC-CCM in restoring retinal endothelial dysfunction, microglial activation, and thus improving visual outcomes after injury.

## Materials & methods

## Preparation of mesenchymal stem cell secretome (ASC-CCM)
Adipose tissue-derived mesenchymal stem cells (ASCs) were procured from Lonza (Cat # PT-5006, Walkersville, MD, USA) and cultured in EGM2MV medium within T75 flasks at an initial seeding density of 5.3 × 10³ cells/cm². ASCs were routinely tested for MSC characteristic markers as described by us previously [ ] and were confirmed by the manufacturer to be >90% positive for CD13, CD29, CD44, CD73, CD90, CD105, and CD166 and < 5% positive for CD14, CD31, and CD45. The culture conditions were maintained at 37 °C with 5% CO₂ to ensure optimal cell growth. The generation of ASC-CCM followed a previously established protocol [ ]. In brief, ASCs underwent two sequential washes with Dulbecco’s phosphate-buffered saline (DPBS) before stimulation with 20 ng/mL tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α; R&D Systems) and 10 ng/mL interferon-gamma (IFN-γ; R&D Systems) in basal α-minimum essential medium (MEMα; Thermo Scientific). After a 24-h incubation period, exogenous cytokines were removed via two additional washes with DPBS, followed by media replacement with fresh basal MEMα. The conditioned medium was collected after another 24-h incubation, subjected to ultrafiltration using a 3-kDa molecular weight cutoff Amicon Ultra-15 centrifugal concentrator (Millipore Sigma), and subsequently desalted against DPBS. The final protein concentration of the secretome was quantified utilizing the Qubit Protein Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and used in the animal studies.
To immunodeplete COL4A1 protein from ASC-CCM, the medium was incubated with COL4A1-specific IgG, with isotype-matched IgG serving as a control. Briefly, a total of 100 µL of Dynabeads Protein G (Thermo Fisher Scientific) was suspended in 1 mL of wash buffer containing 50 mM Tris-HCl and 0.1% Triton X-100. Magnetic separation of the beads from the buffer was performed using a DynaMag™-2 system (Thermo Fisher). To facilitate antibody binding, the beads were incubated overnight at 4 °C with 1 µg of either a COL4A1-specific antibody or an isotype control antibody (Rabbit IgG Isotype Control, Thermo Scientific) in a 1 mL volume of wash buffer. After incubation, the beads were thoroughly washed multiple times to eliminate unbound antibody residues. Following antibody coating, the beads were incubated at 4 °C on a rocker with 2 mL of complete ASC-CCM to enable immunodepletion. After incubation, the negative selection fraction depleted of COL4A1 was designated as ASC-CCM-COL4A1, while the IgG-treated fraction retaining COL4A1 was labeled as ASC-CCM+COL4A1. The amount of COL4A1 protein in both ASC-CCM-COL4A1 and ASC-CCM+COL4A1 was quantified with ELISA according to the manufacturer’s instructions (cat # NBP2-75870, Novus Biologicals). These conditioned media samples were subsequently collected for further experimental analysis.

## Col4a1 knockdown and mild traumatic brain injury (mTBI)
Eight to twelve-week-old C57BL/6J mice, sourced from The Jackson Laboratory, were housed in a controlled temperature environment with a 12-h light-dark cycle, having unrestricted access to food and water. To achieve Col4a1 knockdown in the retina, approximately 8-week-old C57BL/6J mice were anesthetized through a subcutaneous injection of ketamine (50 mg/kg) and dexmedetomidine (0.25 mg/kg). Pupil dilation was performed using tropicamide (1%) and proparacaine (0.5%) served as a local anesthetic. To knock down Col4a1, a custom shRNA approach was used utilizing the mouse Col4a1 target sequence “GCAATTACTACGCAAATGCTT” by adding a loop and restriction site followed by synthesizing and cloning it into a rAAV cis vector by SignaGen Laboratories, A Gene Delivery Company. About 1µL of COL4A1 shRNA-CMV-GFP (1 × 1013 VG/ml; AAV Serotype 2; Col4a1 shRNA) or the scrambled-CMV-GFP control shRNA (cat # SL100815; Con shRNA) were intravitreally injected into the left eye of the mice. After 4 weeks, mild traumatic brain injury (mTBI) was induced in accordance with established protocols as described by us previously [ ]. Briefly, the blast injury was induced with a single 50-psi impact to the left cranial region between the eye and ear, while sham animals were exposed to a 0-psi stimulus under anesthesia. To minimize discomfort, acetaminophen (32 mg/mL) was administered in drinking water at a dose of 300 mg/kg/day from 24 h before the blast injury and continued for 48 h post-injury. Within 1–2 h post-blast injury, mice received either 1 µL of saline or 1 µL of ASC-CCM (approximately 200 ng of protein) via intravitreal administration into both eyes, resulting in four groups: sham blast: saline, blast mice: Con shRNA, blast mice: Col4a1 shRNA, and blast mice: Col4a1 shRNA/ASC-CCM. Following the procedure, anesthesia was reversed using atipamezole hydrochloride (0.25 mg/kg) to aid recovery.

## Retinal fundus imaging
To assess retinal tissue integrity after intravitreal injection and to study the integration of GFP tagged AAV2-shRNA, mice were anesthetized through a subcutaneous injection of ketamine (50 mg/kg) and dexmedetomidine (0.25 mg/kg). Pupil dilation was performed using tropicamide (1%) and proparacaine (0.5%) served as a local anesthetic. A Micron IV color fundus system for rodents (Phoenix-Micron, Inc) allowed to noninvasively obtain retinal structure in fine detail, including the nerve fiber layer, inner and outer plexiform layers, the retinal pigment epithelium. The system uses an interchangeable filter that allowed to capture both brightfield and GFP signal. Following the procedure, anesthesia was reversed using atipamezole hydrochloride (0.25 mg/kg) to aid recovery.

## Electroretinography stimulation and recording procedure
To assess retinal function, electroretinograms (ERG) were obtained from both eyes following the experimental procedures after 4 weeks of blast injury. As part of the protocol, animals were dark-adapted overnight before undergoing anesthesia. To facilitate retinal imaging, pupils were dilated using 1% tropicamide. A dual light guide electrode equipped with a built-in stimulator was placed directly on the corneal surface of each eye (Celeris ERG, Diagnosys LLC). Sequential light pulses of 0.01, 0.1, and 1.0 cd-s/m² were applied, and corresponding retinal responses were recorded from both eyes simultaneously. Data acquisition and offline analysis were conducted using specialized software provided by Diagnosys LLC. To ensure accuracy, a minimum of five responses per light intensity were averaged for determining the amplitude of a- and b-waves.

## Optokinetic reflex assessment
To evaluate visual acuity (VA) and contrast sensitivity (CS) thresholds in both eyes, mice underwent optokinetic reflex testing following the experimental timeline (OptoMotry system from Cerebral Mechanics Inc). During the assessment, unrestrained mice were positioned on a stationary platform at the center of the experimental setup, where they freely observed vertically aligned sine wave patterns displayed on a rotating cylinder via an LCD screen. A blinded investigator recorded the reflexive head and neck movements of the animals as they tracked the moving stripes in clockwise and counterclockwise directions. Visual acuity measurements were taken at maximum contrast (100%, black versus white stripes) while systematically adjusting the spatial frequency of the patterns. Contrast sensitivity was examined at a fixed spatial frequency (0.042 cycles per degree) while altering the contrast levels of the stripes.

## Immunohistochemistry
Eyeballs from experimental subjects were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde at 4 °C overnight, followed by careful removal of the cornea and lens. After cryoprotection in sucrose solutions, they were embedded in optimal cutting temperature (OCT) compound and rapidly frozen in liquid nitrogen. Cryosections of 10 μm were prepared, mounted on slides, and stored at − 80 °C until use. Before staining, sections were thawed, air-dried, and blocked with 2% bovine serum albumin (BSA), 5% normal horse serum and 0.5% Triton-X-100 for an hour. Primary antibodies [glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP), 1:1000 dilution; COL4A, 1:1000 dilution; Perlecan, 1:1000 dilution; Synaptophysin, 1:1000 dilution] in PBS containing 0.1% Triton-X-100, 5% normal horse serum and 0.1% BSA, incubating overnight at 4 °C. Secondary fluorescence-conjugated antibodies were used at 1:2000 dilution for an hour at room temperature. After three PBS washes, sections were mounted with an antifade medium containing DAPI and examined by fluorescence microscopy (Zeiss LSM 710) for imaging and analysis. The pixel intensities were quantified with ImageJ software and expressed as mean intensity per 100,000 µm2 of the retina as described previously [ ].

## Retinal flat mount examination
To assess retinal vessel integrity, mice were euthanized, eyes were enucleated, and then fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 1 h. They were subsequently rinsed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). The anterior chamber, lens, and vitreous humor were dissected while maintaining retinal tissue integrity. Retinas were carefully separated from the eyecup and optic nerve, and the posterior segment, including the sclera–choroid complex. The retinal cups were incubated with a 1:200 dilution of Isolectin-B4-Alexafluor 488 (Thermo Fisher) to label the vasculature for 2 h. Tissues were mounted on slides with an appropriate medium and cover-slipped. Confocal fluorescence microscopy (Zeiss LSM 710) was used for visualization, and images were processed using Fiji software for further analysis.

## RT qPCR analysis
Total RNA was extracted from retinal tissue using the NucleoSpin RNA Plus kit (Cat # 740971.250, Macherey-Nagel GmbH & Co KG, Germany), following the manufacturer’s instructions to ensure high-quality RNA isolation. Differential expression of collagen gene expression changes was assessed using NanoString Counter® multiplex analysis with the neuropathology panel, as we previously described [ ]. The expression levels of representative gene transcripts were confirmed by converting the RNA (250 ng) into complementary DNA (cDNA) using the High-Capacity cDNA Reverse Transcription Kit (Cat # 4368814, Thermo Fisher). This followed, qPCR amplification using TaqMan probes, allowing precise quantification of target mRNA levels (Table ). The gene expression was normalized against 18 S ribosomal RNA, which served as an internal reference. The expression levels of target gene transcripts were determined using the 2−ΔΔCt method and normalized to the housekeeping gene control.

 

## Cell culture
Primary human retinal microvascular endothelial cells (Cat # ACBRI-181; HRECs) were sourced from Cell Systems (Kirkland, WA) and cultured under optimized conditions. The culture surface was pre-coated with 2% gelatin (Sigma) to enhance cell adherence and viability. Cells were maintained in EGM-2 basal medium supplemented with EGM2-MV BulletKit (Cat # CC-3202, Lonza, Walkersville, MD), ensuring optimal growth conditions. The mouse microglial cell line, BV2, was a kind gift from Professor Grace Sun, PhD, University of Missouri, Columbia, MO, USA. BV2 cells were routinely grown in 100 mm cell culture dishes in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) with 10% fetal bovine serum and antibiotics. To preserve inherent cellular properties, culture studies were restricted to a maximum of eight passages.

## Col4a1 knockdown in HRECs
HRECs were seeded onto cultureware pre-coated with 2% gelatin and allowed to grow until reaching 80% confluence in complete medium. The small interfering RNA (siRNA) targeting Col4a1 (ON-TARGETplus Human Col4a1 siRNA, Cat # L-011618-00-0005, Horizon Discovery) was applied following the manufacturer’s protocol. A scrambled siRNA sequence (ON-TARGETplus Non-targeting Pool, Cat # D-001810-10-05) was used as a control to validate specificity. Transfection was conducted using Lipofectamine RNAiMAX, strictly adhering to the manufacturer’s instructions to facilitate efficient siRNA delivery into cells. Following transfection, the experimental procedures were continued for an additional 16 h to assess the knockdown efficacy.

## Scratch wound migration assay
An in-vitro scratch assay was conducted to evaluate cell migration as described by us previously [ ] with slight modifications. Briefly, upon reaching 80% confluence, cells were transfected for ~ 16 h with either siRNA targeting the gene of interest or a scrambled siRNA control. A linear wound was then introduced using a sterile P200 pipette tip, followed by the removal of detached cells through washing with EBM-2 medium. Fresh EBM2 medium was then added, with or without ASC-CCM. Images of the wound area were acquired at baseline (0 h) and after 16 h using an inverted microscope (BioTek Lionheart FX Automated Microscope) with a 20X objective. Quantitative analysis of migration was performed by measuring the distance between wound edges over time, expressed in micrometers (µm).

## Transmigration assay
Transendothelial migration of mononuclear leukocytes across the monolayer of HRECs was performed as described previously [ ]. HRECs were cultured on the upper surface of transwell inserts pre-coated with 2% gelatin (Corning Costar, Bodenheim, Germany) until achieving full confluence. At the 6-h mark, cells were transfected with either scrambled siRNA or COL4A1-specific siRNA. Following a 24-h incubation period post-transfection, the inserts were transferred to fresh wells containing serum-free medium. Dil-labeled THP-1 monocytes (ATCC, TIB-202) at a concentration of 1 × 10⁶ cells were introduced into the upper chamber of the transwell system, allowing for cell migration toward the lower compartment. After an additional 24-h incubation, fluorescence intensity was measured in the medium collected from the bottom chamber to quantify the extent of THP-1 cell transmigration.

## Immunocytochemistry
Immunocytochemistry for VE-cadherin in HREC was performed by a method, as described by us previously [ ], with slight modifications. HRECs were cultured on 10 mm round coverslips placed in a 24-well plate at a density of 2 × 10⁵ cells per well. Following designated treatments, cells were fixed using ice-cold 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) to preserve cellular structures. Three washes with 0.1% Triton-X100 in 1X PBS, each lasting 5 min, were performed to ensure thorough removal of residual fixative. To reduce nonspecific antibody binding, blocking was performed at room temperature for 30 min using a solution containing 5% normal goat serum, 1% bovine serum albumin (BSA), and 0.01% Triton-X100. After blocking, cells were incubated with the primary antibody specific for VE-Cadherin at a dilution of 1:500 (Santa Cruz Biotechnology Cat # sc‐9989), allowing sufficient time for binding. Once immunostaining was complete, cells were mounted using an aqueous mounting medium (Lab Vision PermaFluor, Thermo Fisher Scientific) to enhance fluorescence signal stability. Imaging was conducted using a Zeiss LSM 710 laser scanning confocal microscope equipped with a 20X objective to visualize and document VE-Cadherin localization.

## Immunoblotting
The protein concentration was determined using the BCA assay (Pierce BSA). A total of 50 µg of protein was electrophoretically separated on NuPAGE Bis-Tris gels and transferred onto a nitrocellulose membrane. The membrane was blocked with 5% bovine serum albumin in TBST for an hour, followed by incubation with primary antibodies (COL4A1, Cat # MAB6308, R&D Systems; TSG-6, Cat # AF2104, R&D Systems; TIMP-1, Cat # ab211926, Abcam) and subsequent detection using HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies. Protein visualization was performed using an enhanced chemiluminescence kit, with separate blots analyzed for individual protein targets.

## Electrical cell impedance assay
Trans-endothelial electrical resistance (TER) was assessed using an electric cell-substrate impedance sensing device (ECIS Zθ; Applied Biophysics, Inc., Troy, NY, USA) to evaluate changes in cell barrier function as described by us previously [ ]. HRECs were seeded onto gold electrode arrays (8W10E+; Applied Biophysics, Inc.) at a density of 2 × 10⁵ cells/mL in a total volume of 400 µL per well. Cells were allowed to adhere and proliferate, reaching peak resistance after 16 h of incubation. Following initial cell adhesion, cultures were subjected to pre-treatment with ASC-CCM+COL4A1 or ASC-CCM-COL4A1 for 6 h. After pre-treatment, cells were exposed to TNF-α stimulation (20 ng/mL, Cat # 10602-HNAE; Sino Biological US, Inc), and real-time impedance measurements were recorded over a period of 60 h. Changes in resistance were monitored continuously and expressed as normalized Ohm values to quantify alterations in endothelial integrity and barrier function.

## Assessment of microglia activation
BV2 microglial cells were pre-treated with either 1 µg ASC-CCM+COL4A1 or ASC-CCM-COL4A1 for 6 h, with a third group treated with PBS, which served as a control. The microglial cells were stimulated with lipopolysaccharide (LPS, 100 ng/mL) and interferon-gamma (IFN-γ, 10 ng/mL) to induce an inflammatory response as described by us previously [ ]. After 16 h microglial activation was evaluated by measuring nitrite levels using a Griess colorimetric assay kit (Thermo Scientific), per the manufacturer’s protocol. Additionally, the expression levels of activation markers, including CD86 and interleukin-1 beta (IL-1β), were quantified by qRT-PCR.

## Statistical analysis
Data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism software (version 6.0 or later, GraphPad Software, Inc., La Jolla, CA, USA). Gene expression levels obtained from the 2−ΔΔCt qRT-PCR method were log-transformed before statistical comparisons. For NanoString analysis, the signal intensities (arbitrary units) for each target from four to nine individual retina were averaged, and pairwise group comparisons were made. Any expression below 0.5-fold was considered downregulated; 0.5–1.5-fold considered unchanged, and any value above 1.5-fold was considered upregulated. All samples with correlation coefficients > 0.8 within the biological group were included in the study. Scatter plots were generated by unsupervised clustering analyses using Spearman correlation in the nSolver program. One-way ANOVA was performed for multiple group comparisons, followed by Bonferroni post hoc analysis to correct for multiple comparisons for normally distributed data, while non-normal distributions were analyzed via the Kruskal‒Wallis test. Differences between groups were considered statistically significant at p < 0.05.

## Results

## Downregulation of COL4A1 with mTBI in the retina alleviated with intravitreal delivery of ASC-CCM
We previously demonstrated that a single intravitreal delivery of ASC-CCM generated by cytokine stimulation of ASCs restored the visual deficits after mTBI [ ]. Additionally, several neuroinflammatory genes analyzed by the NanoString neuropathology gene panel from retinal tissues demonstrated changes in microglial-related genes [ ]. In this study, using the same dataset, we serendipitously identified a significant differential expression of Col4a1 mRNA in blast mice retina that received saline, with a notable upregulation in blast mice treated with ASC-CCM (Fig. A, B). To further confirm, we quantified the relative gene expression levels of several collagen isoforms by Taqman qPCR assays, including Col1a1, Col3a1, Col4a1, Col5a1, and Col7a1, in mTBI mice. As expected, quantitative PCR analysis revealed that Col4a1 mRNA expression was significantly decreased by approximately 50% following mTBI (p < 0.001, Fig. C). In contrast, intravitreal administration of ASC-CCM resulted in an approximately 10-fold upregulation of COL4A1 expression compared to control groups. Notably, the expression levels of the other collagen variants, Col1a1, Col3a1, Col5a1, and Col7a1, were not significantly altered by either the injury or the treatment interventions. Interestingly, ASC-CCM obtained from three different donors demonstrated substantially higher COL4A1 protein levels compared to ASC conditioned medium (ASC-CM), which was obtained without cytokine stimulation, suggesting that ASC-CCM has enriched COL4A1 levels (Fig. D).

 

## Retinal-specific knockdown of Col4a1 mimics visual deficits after mTBI
To address the hypothesis that downregulation of COL4A1 leads to visual deficits similar to mTBI, an shRNA approach was used. Col4a1 shRNA-CMV-GFP (1 × 1013 VG/ml; AAV Serotype 2) and scrambled-CMV-GFP control shRNA were intravitreally injected into the left eyes of the C57BL/6J mice (Fig.  A). Since the shRNA is GFP tagged, we could confirm the successful integration of shRNA into the retina, as confirmed by fundus imaging at 1-month post-injection (Fig.  B). Colocalization of GFP with COL4A1 protein in the perivascular cells by confocal microscopy in Con shRNA mice suggested potential cell-specific targeting of the shRNA knockdown (Fig.  B). Further confirmation of Col4a1 knockdown was obtained from whole retinal tissues by significant downregulation of Col4a1 mRNA by qPCR (Fig.  C). Interestingly, COL4A1 shRNA but not the scrambled control shRNA intravitreally injected into C57BL/6J mice at 1-month post-injection yielded visual deficits that resembled those with blast injury (Fig.  D). A decrease in visual acuity with a corresponding increase in contrast thresholds was noted only in the left eye with minimal to no changes in the right eye.

 

## Retinal-specific knockdown of Col4a1 exacerbates visual deficits after mTBI are alleviated with intravitreal delivery of ASC-CCM
Having shown that Col4a1 deficiency is associated with visual deficits, we next tested if Col4a1 knockdown in blast mice yields a more severe phenotype than either knockdown or blast alone. Towards this end, we intravitreally injected Col4a1 shRNA or the control shRNA and performed focal cranial blast injury (Fig. A). Blast mice that received control shRNA demonstrated a decrease in visual acuity and an increase in contrast sensitivity thresholds (Fig. B) as described for blast mice previously [ ]. On the other hand, blast mice that received Col4a1 shRNA demonstrated a greater decrease in visual acuity and an increase in contrast sensitivity thresholds (Fig. B). Interestingly, these deficits were significantly alleviated by intravitreal ASC-CCM in the blast mice receiving control shRNA and Col4a1 shRNA (Fig. B). Complementing the visual system injury, Col4a1 shRNA delivery into blast mice demonstrated a decrease in b-wave and a-wave amplitudes compared to control shRNA, with a significant improvement with intravitreal delivery of ASC-CCM (Fig. C).

 

## Retinal-specific knockdown of Col4a1 exacerbates neurodegeneration markers after mTBI are alleviated with intravitreal delivery of ASC-CCM
We next tested if Col4a1 knockdown in blast mice exacerbates neuroinflammatory markers. Previously, we have shown that glial activation ensues from focal cranial blast mTBI [ ]. We thus assessed gene transcripts for glial activation including gfap and CD86 which were significantly elevated in blast mice receiving Col4a1 shRNA as compared to those blast mice with Con shRNA, and were significantly reduced toward normal levels in blast mice that received Col4a1 shRNA and ASC-CCM (Fig. A). The increased gfap expression was confirmed by immunostaining, with a significant reduction in blast mice that received ASC-CCM (Fig. B) compared to those that did not. Previously it was reported that Col4a1 mutant mice develop ER stress [ ], and accordingly, we showed that Col4a1 shRNA treated blast mice showed increased ER stress as revealed by mRNA expression of grp78, Chop, and Xbp-1 with a significant reduction in these ER stress markers with ASC-CCM (Fig. A). In the context of TBI, a synaptic loss can result from diffuse axonal injury secondary to neuroinflammation [ , ]. In accordance with this, synaptophysin immunoreactivity showed a reduction in Col4a1 shRNA treated blast mice compared to sham blast mice. Blast mice receiving ASC-CCM that had been injected intravitreally with Col4a1 shRNA showed a significant recovery, suggesting that enhanced synaptic connectivity was a likely means for the improved functional recovery post TBI (Fig. C), as also observed with other stem cell therapies [ , ].

 

## Retinal-specific knockdown of Col4a1 alters retinal vasculature and increases endothelial dysfunction after mTBI is alleviated with intravitreal delivery of ASC-CCM
We next addressed whether Col4a1 shRNA-treated blast mice show alterations in blood vessel integrity. Towards this end, Isolectin-B4 was used to label endothelial cells, and Perlecan (heparan sulfate proteoglycan) to label BM in retinal flat mount and retinal sections, respectively. As shown in Fig.  A, blast mice that received Col4a1 shRNA showed degenerated vessels and loss of Perlecan immunostaining that was restored to near normal in blast mice with ASC-CCM. Endothelial activation results in upregulation of Icam1, Ccl2 and Vcam1, which were significantly elevated in blast mice receiving Col4a1 shRNA as compared to those blast mice with control shRNA, and were significantly reduced toward normal levels in blast mice that received Col4a1 shRNA and ASC-CCM (Fig.  B).

 

## Col4a1 knockdown in retinal endothelial cells impairs endothelial integrity in-vitro restored with ASC-CCM
To directly link COL4A1 reduction with the vascular dysfunction seen in-vivo, we then performed Col4a1 knockdown via an siRNA approach in human retinal endothelial cells (HREC) with >94% efficiency (Fig. A). Following knockdown, we performed several endothelial cell bioassays with and without ASC-CCM. (1) Transmigration of monocytes across the monolayer of endothelial cells has been correlated with loss of barrier integrity and subsequent tissue inflammation [ , ]. To this end, Col4a1 knockdown significantly increased the transmigration of monocytes across the endothelial monolayer in-vitro (Fig. B), suggesting Col4a1 knockdown causes endothelial cell activation and contributes to tissue inflammation seen in-vivo. (2) Changes in endothelial adherens junction proteins, such as the VE-Cadherin was correlated with endothelial dysfunction [ ]. To this end, an increase in transmigration of monocytes with Col4a1 knockdown in HRECs correlated with a decrease in VE-cadherin expression (Fig. C), suggesting a loss in barrier integrity. (3) Endothelial dysfunction has been correlated with poor rates of migration in a scratch wound healing assay. To this end, Col4a1 knockdown in HRECs correlated with a decrease in wound healing rates (Fig. D). Interestingly, pre-incubation of endothelial cells with ASC-CCM alleviated all these effects of COL4A1 knockdown, suggesting a direct link to a vascular benefit from the presence of COL4A1 in ASC-CCM.

 

## COL4A1 secreted by ASCs is necessary for microglial and endothelial integrity in-vitro
To further study the therapeutic role for COL4A1 secreted by ASC, we then selectively immunodepleted COL4A1 from ASC-CCM with anti-COL4A1 IgG (ASC-CCM−COL4A1) and used non-immunodepleted ASC-CCM that had been treated with isotype-matched normal IgG (ASC-CCM+COL4A1) as a control (Fig. A). Both ELISA and immunoblots confirmed the presence and absence of COL4A1 with no changes to other potential proteins released by ASCs. Following this, we tested the therapeutic benefit of ASC-CCM with and without COL4A1 using cell-based potency assays in microglial and endothelial cells, as described in a recently published study by our group [ , ]. While ASC-CCM+COL4A1 could suppress the production of nitrite by LPS/IFNγ-treated BV2 cells, the ASC-CCM−COL4A1 at the same total protein concentration (1 µg/ml) failed to suppress nitrite release (Fig. B). A similar observation is also made with changes in IL1b and CD86 gene expression, which are known microglial activation markers (Fig. B). Previously, we have shown that ASC-CCM could preserve retinal barrier function in an in-vitro model via measuring the transendothelial electrical resistance (TER) [ ]. Whereas TNFα-treated HREC demonstrated sustained reduction in the barrier integrity as evidenced by decreased TER at the 40-h time point (TNFα, 0.564 ± 0.012 control, 1.0; p < 0.001), only cells pretreated with ASC-CCM+COL4A1, but not ASC-CCM−COL4A1, partially rescued the loss in TER (0.055 ± 0.035, p < 0.05, Fig. C). Taken together with microglia and endothelial biopotency assays, this data suggests that ASC-CCM+COL4A1 could modulate microglial activation, suppress pro-inflammatory cytokine release, and promote a shift toward a neuroprotective (M2-like) phenotype, enhance endothelial barrier function thereby serving as a key indicator of ASC-CCM’s therapeutic potential in retinal or neuroinflammatory conditions.

 

## Discussion
The key findings of our study indicate that COL4A1 plays a crucial role in maintaining retinal vascular integrity, especially in the context of TBI. A deficiency in COL4A1 may worsen neurovascular degeneration in TBI. Our research demonstrated the therapeutic potential of COL4A1-enriched ASC-CCM in restoring retinal endothelial function, reducing microglial activation, and improving visual outcomes after injury. Along with our previous studies showing the benefits of ASC-CCM in visual deficits following mTBI [ – ], this work advances cell-free therapies as safe and effective options for treating vision problems after neurotrauma.
Collagen plays a crucial role in ocular tissues, particularly in maintaining structure and transparency with specific subtypes contributing uniquely to different anatomical regions of the eye. The cornea and lens contain primarily collagen types I and IV, which are essential for clear vision [ ]. Type I collagen, abundant in the cornea and sclera, ensures biomechanical stability [ ], while type II collagen is essential for vitreous body structure and implicated in vitreoretinal disorders such as Stickler syndrome [ ]. Type III collagen plays a key role in the stability of ocular vasculature, and its mutations associated with vascular Ehlers–Danlos Syndrome which can lead to scleral thinning and increased risk of globe rupture [ ]. Type IV collagen, a key component of basement membranes, is crucial for retinal and vascular health; its mutations are linked to retinal hemorrhages, optic nerve anomalies, and congenital cataracts [ , ]. Type V collagen regulates collagen fibril formation, particularly in the cornea, and its dysfunction has been implicated in keratoconus, characterized by corneal thinning and ectasia [ , ]. Type VII, a component of anchoring fibrils, is critical for the adhesion of the basement membrane to the underlying stroma; mutations result in dystrophic epidermolysis bullosa, which can cause recurrent corneal erosions and scarring, impairing vision [ ].
Disruption of collagen subtypes due to genetic mutations or trauma plays a critical role in the pathogenesis of various ocular disorders, emphasizing their importance in maintaining normal visual function. In this study, we evaluated the effects of mTBI on the expression patterns of multiple collagen subtypes and investigated the therapeutic potential of ASC-CCM in restoring their homeostasis. Blast injury significantly altered the gene expression profiles of Col1a1, Col3a1, Col4a1, Col5a1, and Col7a1, with Col1a1, Col3a1, and Col5a1 showing substantial upregulation, while Col4a1 and Col7a1 were markedly downregulated. Among the examined collagen subtypes, Col4a1 exhibited the most marked decrease following blast injury. Notably, therapeutic intervention with ASC-CCM demonstrated a potent ability to enhance Col4a1 gene expression, suggesting its potential role in stabilizing the basement membrane and mitigating blast-induced retinal damage in the mTBI mouse model.
Since we observed significant alterations in the Col4a1 expression in mTBI mice and the therapeutic intervention with ASC-CCM demonstrated a potent ability to enhance Col4a1 expression, we further assessed its efficacy in the Col4a1 knockdown mouse model. The impact of ASC-CCM treatment on retina-specific knockdown of Col4a1 subjected to mTBI was assessed with various analyses. Electroretinogram (ERG) improvements following ASC-CCM treatment suggest functional preservation of retinal neurons, particularly b- and a-waves, which reflect bipolar cell and photoreceptor activity, respectively. The increase in ERG amplitudes aligns with prior studies indicating that mesenchymal stem cell-derived therapies can exert neuroprotective effects by secreting trophic factors that promote neuronal survival and repair. Knockdown of Col4a1 (shRNA) abrogated the protective effect, evidenced by reduced ERG amplitudes. This underscores COL4A1’s pivotal role in mediating the therapeutic benefits of ASC-CCM. The Optokinetic Measurements align with ERG results, which exhibit increased contrast sensitivity thresholds and reduced visual acuity in Col4a1 shRNA mice compared to Control shRNA mice subjected to mTBI. Further treatment with ASC-CCM in Col4a1 shRNA improved contrast sensitivity and demonstrated near-complete restoration of visual acuity.
Reactive gliosis, characterized by the activation of retinal glial cells such as Müller cells and astrocytes, is a hallmark of neuroinflammation and degeneration in the retina. A key indicator of this process is the upregulation of glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP), an intermediate filament protein [ ]. In COL4A1 mutant mice, studies have shown a significant increase in GFAP expression, particularly in hypertrophied Müller cells, extending into the outer plexiform layer. This suggests a pronounced gliotic response associated with COL4A1 mutations [ ]. In agreement with previous observations, mTBI mice with Col4a1 shRNA demonstrated a significant increase in GFAP expression and a significant attenuation with the treatment of ASC-CCM. Further confirmation of this therapeutic benefit is also reflected in gene expression changes of GFAP and CD86, two important glial activation markers that were reduced to sham blast levels. These findings align with previous research demonstrating that ASC-CCM can modulate glial activation and promote retinal repair mechanisms [ ].
TBI disrupts the blood-retinal barrier (BRB), leading to increased permeability and inflammation. Retinal ganglion cells undergo apoptosis, contributing to visual deficits, while retinal pericytes, upon IL-1β stimulation, release inflammatory mediators such as IL-8, CCL2, and CCL5, exacerbating endothelial dysfunction [ ]. Adhesion molecules ICAM-1 and VCAM-1 facilitate leukocyte adhesion and endothelial activation, promoting neovascularization [ ]. Studies highlight a surge in cytokine levels (IL-6, TNFα, IL-10, CCL2, IL-8) within the first 2 days post-TBI, correlating with neuroinflammation, astrogliosis, and axonal damage [ , ]. Extensive research over the years has demonstrated that ER stress plays a significant role in the progression of secondary injury following TBI, influencing various biological pathways [ ]. This interplay between immune activation, ER stress and vascular degeneration underscores the systemic consequences of TBI-induced BRB disruption. In line with these observations, we demonstrate that Col4a1 knockdown exacerbated endothelial dysfunction in mTBI mice via increasing the Icam-1, Vcam-1, and Ccl2 expression and degeneration of capillaries as evidenced by Isolectin-B4 staining with reduced Perlecan immunostaining, whereas treatment with ASC-CCM attenuated these changes in the mTBI mice that received Col4a1 shRNA. Additionally, we demonstrate that Col4a1 knockdown exacerbates ER stress in mTBI mice by increasing the levels of Grp78, Chop, and Xbp-1, with a significant reduction following ASC-CCM treatment. Although it is unclear how Col4a1 knockdown causes neuroinflammation in the retina after TBI, it is likely due to cytokine-mediated BRB dysfunction combined with Col4a1 disruption. This disruption may worsen these effects by weakening basement membrane integrity and inducing ER stress, collectively leading to vascular degeneration, endothelial inflammation, and retinal neuronal loss.
To further delineate the functional role of COL4A1 in regulating retinal endothelial integrity and inflammatory responses, we conducted in-vitro assays using HRECs. Silencing of Col4a1 via siRNA resulted in a significant reduction in endothelial cell migration, as demonstrated by scratch-wound and transwell migration assays, indicating a critical role for COL4A1 in endothelial motility. Furthermore, Col4a1 silencing led to increased endothelial permeability and enhanced leukocyte transmigration, likely due to disruption of adherens junction proteins such as VE-cadherin. This suggests that COL4A1 contributes to endothelial barrier stability, and its downregulation compromises vascular integrity, facilitating inflammatory cell infiltration. These findings align with earlier studies demonstrating that mutations or loss of COL4A1 weaken basement membrane structures, promoting vascular leakage and immune cell recruitment in ocular and cerebral vasculature [ , ]. Importantly, treatment with ASC-CCM restored endothelial function in Col4a1-silenced HRECs. ASC-CCM not only attenuated leukocyte transmigration but also reinforced endothelial junction integrity, as evidenced by increased TER and VE-Cadherin junctions.
Our previous studies using ASC-CCM suggest that the beneficial effects are likely mediated by a variety of paracrine factors, including anti-inflammatory cytokines, extracellular matrix remodeling proteins, and angiogenic mediators known to promote endothelial repair and modulate immune responses [ ]. However, a more direct evidence in this study indicates that COL4A1 in the ASC-CCM is likely responsible for these effects. This evidence was obtained through immunodepletion studies aimed at removing COL4A1 from ASC-CCM. To this end, ASC-CCM enriched with COL4A1 but not the ASC-CCM that was immunodepleted for COL4A1 protein successfully inhibited LPS-induced microglial nitrite release, activated microglial genes, and protected against TNF-α-mediated endothelial permeability in-vitro. A variety of recombinant proteins are widely used in clinical therapy to treat various conditions, such as the use of recombinant Insulin for managing diabetes mellitus, recombinant Factor VIII and Factor IX used to treat hemophilia A and B, among others. COL4 is a large, complex protein that forms a network structure in BMs, making it difficult to produce and deliver in a functional recombinant form. Currently, COL4-related diseases, such as Alport syndrome, are often addressed through gene therapy, small molecule therapies, or supportive treatments rather than direct protein replacement [ ]. Our studies here point to a novel cell-based approach to restore COL4 function that may be applicable beyond TBI.
ASC-CCM therapy is a cell-free regenerative approach that targets neurovascular integrity and inflammation in TBI models. Compared with other emerging strategies for blood–brain barrier (BBB) repair, ASC-CCM emphasizes paracrine signaling mechanisms rather than cell replacement. Our previous research demonstrated that intravitreal ASC-CCM normalized Müller and microglial cell activity, restored glutamate homeostasis, and reversed BRB-associated retinal dysfunctions, indicating a multimodal neurovascular modulation effect through cytokine-mediated signaling rather than direct tissue integration [ – , , ]. Emerging BBB repair strategies, including mesenchymal stem cell (MSC) that cross the endothelial barrier of the BBB [ ], and exosome therapies leveraging vesicle-mediated transfer of miRNA, proteins, and lipids to restore endothelial function [ ], or nanocarriers and drug-loaded nanoparticles, designed to promote BBB regeneration or facilitate targeted delivery of therapeutic molecules [ ] also improve angiogenesis and neuroprotection but face challenges in consistency and delivery. In contrast, ASC-CCM presents a practical, scalable, and low-risk option for BRB and BBB repair compared with cell- or nanoparticle-based methods, though further validation in long-term TBI recovery is required.
We acknowledge some limitations of the current study. Our previous studies have implicated TSG-6 as a viable therapeutic candidate in mTBI [ ]; however, our current studies point to the fact that despite the presence of TSG-6, in the absence of COL4A1, the ASC-CCM failed to rescue endothelial TER, suggesting that a more complex cell signaling might be in play in COL4A1 deficiency conditions. Whether the deletion of both TSG-6 and COL4A1 might have synergistic effects on ASC-CCM therapeutic modality is not known and should be tested in the future. Along the same lines, future studies need to clarify whether COL4A1 functions synergistically with other known protective factors in the MSC secretome, such as TIMP-1/2, HGF, IL-10, ANGPT1, and others, which are known to reinforce endothelial junctions, suppress NF-κB–driven inflammation, and alleviate oxidative or ER stress. Therapies utilizing the secretome of stem cells for the treatment of retinal degenerative diseases show promise; however, their exact mechanism of action and cellular targets remain unclear [ ]. Additionally, the current study evaluated the protective effects of COL4A1 in the ASC-CCM over a 1-month period. However, this timeframe is limited, as previous research indicates that the protective effects of ASC-CCM can last up to 10 months after blast injury. Furthermore, blast-exposed mice treated with ASC-CCM showed significant improvements in visual function even after 10 months [ ]. Building on these findings, we hypothesize that the improvements in BRB function may also be longer-lasting; however, more validation is needed. To better translate these findings, long-term tracking is essential, and intervention strategies should also be considered, especially since current studies administered ASC-CCM immediately after blast injury. Future studies delivering ASC-CCM two to 3 weeks post-injury with long-term tracking are more likely to reflect real-world scenarios and improve clinical relevance. Loss of the BBB in TBI is well documented to induce tissue inflammation and neurodegeneration [ ]. However, it is not known whether a loss in retinal barrier integrity becomes a trigger for visual deficits in TBI as a consequence of changes in BM dynamics. Therefore, future studies seeking to clarify the relationship between endothelial dysfunction and neurodegeneration after Col4a1 knockdown are necessary. For example, a direct permeability assay, such as Evans blue leakage, FITC-dextran extravasation, or IgG penetration, would provide functional confirmation that ASC-CCM effectively preserves retinal barrier integrity after mTBI. miRNAs are an abundant family of small RNAs (~ 22 nucleotides) that act as crucial post-transcriptional regulators of genes involved in such fundamental biological processes as differentiation, proliferation and apoptosis [ , ]. We and others have recently shown that MSC secretome containing exosomes carry a variety of miRNAs and are therapeutic in mTBI [ ]. However, there is a critical knowledge gap that link miRNAs to COL4A1 expression in TBI.

## Conclusions
Our study provides compelling evidence that Col4a1 deficiency exacerbates retinal dysfunction following mTBI, and emphasizes its crucial role in maintaining retinal vascular integrity leading to endothelial cell activation, microglial polarization, synaptic defects, and neurodegeneration culminating in visual dysfunction (Fig.  ). On the other hand, the use of COL4A1-enriched ASC-CCM significantly ameliorated visual impairments in mTBI mice with Col4a1-knockdown, highlighting its therapeutic potential. Restoration of visual acuity and contrast sensitivity thresholds was accompanied by attenuation of gliosis, normalization of endothelial junctions, and suppression of inflammatory gene expression in the retina. In-vitro assays further confirmed that COL4A1 is vital for endothelial cell migration and barrier integrity, and that its loss enhances leukocyte transmigration and microglial activation effects that were reversed by treatment with COL4A1-enriched ASC-CCM, but not with COL4A1-depleted preparations. These findings collectively underscore the therapeutic relevance of COL4A1 as a molecular target and support ASC-CCM as a promising regenerative strategy for mitigating mTBI-induced visual deficits via vascular stabilization and neuroimmune modulation.
