## Abstract
Asthma is a prevalent pulmonary disease that affects more than 300 million people worldwide and imposes a substantial economic burden. While medication can effectively control symptoms in some patients, severe asthma attacks, driven by airway inflammation induced by environmental and infectious exposures, continue to be a major cause of asthma-related mortality. Heterogeneous phenotypes of asthma include type 2 (T2) and non-T2 asthma. Non-T2 asthma is often observed in patients with severe and/or steroid-resistant asthma. This review covers the molecular mechanisms, clinical phenotypes, causes and promising treatments of non-T2 severe asthma. Specifically, we discuss the signalling pathways for non-T2 asthma including the activation of inflammasomes, interferon responses and interleukin-17 pathways, and their contributions to the subtypes, progression and severity of non-T2 asthma. Understanding the molecular mechanisms and genetic determinants underlying non-T2 asthma could form the basis for precision medicine in severe asthma treatment.

## Introduction
Asthma, caused by chronic airway inflammation, affects more than 300 million individuals worldwide, with most cases occurring in low- and middle-income countries [ ]. The prevalence of asthma varies across regions, with rates ranging from 0.9% to 29.0% [ ]. The associated costs of emergency care and severe persistent disease contribute significantly to the overall economic and health burden on patients, families, and society [ ]. Clinical symptoms of asthma include wheezing, shortness of breath, chest tightness, exacerbation tendency, coughing and wheezing. Furthermore, asthma is highly complex and diverse condition, with multiple distinct endotypes that are defined by different underlying pathophysiological mechanisms, natural histories and treatment responses [ ]. The main endotypes of asthma include type 2 (T2) and non-T2 asthma, which may require distinct treatment strategies once we fully understand their underlying mechanisms.

## Allergic, eosinophilic and type 2 asthma
Allergic asthma, the prevailing asthma phenotype, is triggered by certain inhaled allergens and impacts ∼7.3% of the general population [ ]. This subtype of asthma is defined by total serum IgE ≥30 kU·L−1 and one or more positive perennial aeroallergen-specific IgE (≥0.35 kU·L−1) at baseline [ – ]. Eosinophilic asthma, affecting 50% of patients with asthma [ ], is defined by the blood eosinophil counts (high ≥300 cells·µL−1; low cut-off ≥150 cells·µL−1) [ ]. T2-asthma is defined by fractional exhaled nitric oxide (FENO) (high ≥35 ppb; low cut-off ≥25 ppb) [ ]. Post hoc analyses from two adult populations showed that different classification criteria result in overlaps among these endotypes. In a general adult asthma population without specific subtype selection, 78.0% exhibited allergic asthma; among them, 39.5% presented with eosinophilic asthma and 29.5% had T2 asthma, with overlapping subjects [ ]. For example, 75.8% of those with eosinophilic asthma had allergic asthma and 41.3% of those with eosinophilic asthma had T2 asthma. Among those individuals with T2 asthma, 81.1% had allergic asthma and 59.2% had eosinophilic asthma [ ]. In addition, a distinct subtype of asthma, referred to as “non-T2 asthma”, is indicated by being nonallergic, noneosinophilic, “T2-low” asthma [ ].

## Non-T2 asthma

## Definition and natural history
Severe asthma has been defined as “asthma that requires treatment with high-dose inhaled corticosteroids plus a second controller and/or systemic corticosteroids to prevent it from becoming ‘uncontrolled’ or that remains ‘uncontrolled’ despite this therapy” [ ]. It results from persistent bronchospasm, airway hyperresponsiveness (AHR) and mucus hyperproduction due to airway inflammation [ ]. The prevalence of severe asthma was found to be 1.1% among the randomly selected adult population and 9.5% among those with a history of asthma [ ]. In severe asthma, 30–50% of patients present with nonallergic, noneosinophilic, non-T2 asthma [ ].
Diagnosing non-T2 asthma can be challenging, since these patients are often characterised by unresponsiveness to corticosteroid therapy, the absence of T2 asthma biomarkers, making diagnosis difficult. Current anti-inflammatory therapies that target type 2 inflammation or the use of high doses of inhaled or systemic corticosteroids typically do not yield satisfactory results for this type of asthma, indicating the presence of steroid resistance in non-T2 asthma [ , , ]. In addition, these patients tend to have normal eosinophil counts in both sputum and peripheral blood (sputum eosinophil counts ≤1.9% [ ]; blood eosinophil counts ≤5% [ ]), as well as low FENO levels, although the cut-off values for FENO may show slight variations (20 ppb or 35 ppb) based on factors such as age, clinical context or the levels of local air pollution [ , ]. Nevertheless, a growing number of biomarkers not dependent on T2 asthma have been validated in understanding pathophysiology of severe asthma, such as α1-antichymotrypsin, apolipoprotein-E, complement component 9, complement factor I, macrophage inflammatory protein-3, interleukin (IL)-6, sphingomyelin phosphodiesterase 3, tumour necrosis factor (TNF) receptor superfamily member 11a, transforming growth factor-β and glutathione S-transferase [ ]. Additionally, female sex, exposures to particulate matter, gaseous pollutants, smoking and mixed traffic-related air pollution, or even pathogen infection, are correlated with non-T2 asthma [ – ].
The natural history of non-T2 asthma is diverse and challenging to predict on an individual basis. In non-T2 asthma, persistent symptoms are often linked to more severe asthma phenotypes. Clinical cluster analysis from the Severe Asthma Research Program (SARP) indicates that, in cases of non-T2 asthma, the neutrophilic phenotypes tend to associate with higher severity and increased frequency of exacerbation [ , ]. In addition, from a 48-week multicentre randomised controlled clinical study, patients with non-T2 asthma showed 32.4% high dependency unit (HDU)/intensive care unit (ICU) admission [ ]. In contrast, patients with T2 asthma exhibited 17.8% HDU/ICU, indicating that patients with non-T2 asthma are more prone to have a history of admission to HDU/ICU. However, an observational study involving 42 cases of severe asthma showed that individuals with non-T2 asthma had significantly lower frequency of coughing (2.7 versus 12.7 coughs per hour) over a 24-h period [ ], indicating a special mechanism in non-T2 asthma.

## Various subtypes of non-T2 asthma
Non-T2 asthma includes various subtypes, such as neutrophilic and paucigranulocytic asthma, which are distinguished by distinct mechanisms of inflammation [ ]. In the Belgian Severe Asthma Registry, which included 350 severe adult asthmatics as defined by the American Thoracic Society [ ] from nine Belgian centres, cell counts in induced sputum samples from patients with severe asthma indicated that 22% had neutrophilic asthma, while 17% had paucigranulocytic asthma [ ]. In another study, involving bronchoscopy in 126 children with severe asthma, 52% were found to have paucigranulocytic asthma and 16% had isolated airway neutrophilia [ ]. A cross-sectional analysis, conducted on the inflammatory phenotype of 70 individuals with asthma, showed that individuals with neutrophilic asthma demonstrated increased frequency of severe disease (p=0.03), more exacerbations (p=0.02), decreased forced expiratory volume in 1 s (FEV1) response to bronchodilators (ΔFEV1) (p=0.004) compared to non-neutrophilic asthma [ ]. However, the use of neutrophil counts as a criterion to define non-T2 asthma remains controversial, since neutrophil activation, as indicated by neutrophil myeloperoxidase, is not often observed in the airways of individuals with severe asthma [ , ]. Furthermore, patients receiving moderate-to-high doses of corticosteroids showed increased sputum neutrophil counts in comparison to those receiving low-dose corticosteroids [ ]. In addition, in a multicentre randomised double-blind placebo-controlled dose-finding trial, blocking neutrophil recruitment by using a CXCR2 antagonist did not improve asthma [ ], casting doubt on whether neutrophils are the underlying cause of asthma.
In contrast to neutrophilic asthma, paucigranulocytic asthma is characterised by low levels of both neutrophils (<76%) and eosinophils (<3%) in induced sputum samples, along with typical asthmatic features such as airway smooth muscle hypertrophy, reversible airway obstruction and AHR [ , ]. Additionally, mixed granulocytic asthma is defined by high levels of neutrophils (>76%) and eosinophils (>3%) in induced sputum [ ]. Nevertheless, the non-T2 asthma exhibited longitudinal instability and it is hard to effectively predict subsequent events by using those criteria [ ]. A study involving 42 children diagnosed with severe asthma who underwent repeated sputum induction every 3 months showed that only nine out of 42 had a consistent non-T2 asthma pattern (paucigranulocytic n=8; neutrophilic n=1) [ ]. Additionally, a revaluation of sputum cellularity data from two previous prospective studies involving 41 children with asthma showed that none of the children had a consistent neutrophilic phenotype [ ].
Overall, defining non-T2 asthma and looking for additional biomarkers to accurately identify the subtypes of non-T2 asthma is ongoing area of research that will have significant implications for asthma diagnosis and treatment.

## The causes of non-T2 asthma
While the causes of T2 asthma are relatively well studied [ ], the causes of non-T2 asthma are ultimately unclear, and may include genetic factors, environmental pollutants, obesity and pathogen infection. Further research is needed to identify the causes and underlying mechanisms of non-T2 asthma, which may help to develop better diagnosis and treatment strategies for preventing and managing non-T2 asthma.

## Genetic factors
Asthma is clearly caused by the combination of environmental factors and genetic factors that significantly contribute to the individual's asthma predisposition [ ] and which may influence endotypes of asthma [ ]. The estimated heritability of asthma ranges from 36% to 79% [ – ]. Genome-wide association studies (GWAS) have identified numerous reproducible asthma loci [ , – ]. A meta-analysis of childhood asthma and adult asthma from the UK Biobank and the Trans-National Asthma Genetic Consortium has identified 66 novel asthma loci; together with prior studies, the authors identified a total of 212 asthma signals [ ]. Among these, 52 were significant in the childhood-onset asthma GWAS and 19 were significant in adult-onset asthma [ ]. Additional asthma exacerbations GWAS conducted on 2681 children of European descent who were treated with inhaled corticosteroids from eight different studies identified 10 independent loci [ ]. However, no genetic factors specifically associated with non-T2 asthma have been well characterised yet, although studies have identified several potential candidate genes and loci that may play a role in non-T2 asthma development and pathogenesis.
The 17q12 locus is the first and the most reproducible GWAS locus associated with asthma susceptibility [ – ], especially in children who experienced wheezing illnesses caused by human rhinovirus (RV) [ ]. Furthermore, children carrying 17q21 asthma risk alleles were found to have reduced serum sphingolipid levels, frequently seen in children with non-T2 asthma [ ]. Asthma risk alleles at 17q12–21 are associated with increased expression of two susceptibility genes in airway epithelial cells: gasdermin-B (GSDMB) and ORMDL sphingolipid biosynthesis regulator 3 (ORMDL3) that may contribute to asthma pathogenesis independently [ , ]. Increased expression of ORMDL3 may contribute to the development of paucigranulocytic asthma, which is a significant subtype of non-T2 asthma, by inducing the upregulation of mediators involved in airway remodelling rather than inflammation [ ]. Moreover, overexpression of ORMDL3 promoted autophagic cell death in airway epithelial cells, possibly leading to epithelial damage [ ]. In addition, ORMDL3 reduced levels of serum sphingolipids and increased AHR and asthmatic airway remodelling without airway inflammation, suggesting multiple mechanisms by which ORMDL3 may determine susceptibility of asthma [ ]. In contrast, the function of GSDMB is not fully characterised yet. Like other gasdermin family members, GSDMB may promote inflammasome-mediated pyroptosis [ ]. Additionally, expression levels of GSDMB correlated with genes involved in IFN response in airway epithelial cells [ ], and GSDMB contributes to childhood asthma, possibly related to increased IL-17A response to viral infections [ ]. Considering the important roles of IFN response, the inflammasome and IL-17 in the pathogenesis of non-T2 asthma, we speculate that GSDMB may also be involved in the development of non-T2 asthma.
The 19q13.41 locus was identified in a recent GWAS conducted on patients with moderate-to-severe asthma. This locus was found to be specifically associated with non-T2 asthma and has pinpointed kallikrein-related peptidase 5 (KLK5) as the causal gene responsible for this association [ ]. Indeed, overexpression of KLK5 or the lack of an endogenous KLK5 inhibitor, SPINK5, in the airway led to spontaneous airway neutrophilic inflammation [ ]. In addition, single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) near the activated leukocyte cell adhesion molecule (ALCAM) gene region are associated with the nonallergic asthma phenotype in adult asthma in GWAS [ ].
The chromosome 1q21.3 variants, rs2228145 and rs4129267, characterised by a nonsynonymous SNP, are linked to elevated plasma soluble (s)IL-6R levels [ , ], which is more commonly observed in individuals of the neutrophilic and mixed granulocytic subtypes [ ]. Besides, a two-stage case–control design from a GWAS study showed that the variant rs11603634 located within the MUC5AC region correlated with increased expression of MUC5AC mRNA [ ]. This elevated expression of MUC5AC mRNA is probably associated with the severity of asthma and the formation of mucus plugs in severe neutrophilic asthma [ ].
Overall, the genetic factors underlying non-T2 asthma remain largely unknown, which requires further comprehensive investigations especially functional investigation on identified loci associated with non-T2 asthma, which may illuminate the molecular mechanism contributing to the heterogeneous asthma subtypes.

## Pathogen infections
Pathogen infections, including viral, bacterial and fungal infections, can play a diverse role in asthma ( ). They may either exacerbate existing asthma symptoms or play a role in the initial occurrence of disease [ ].
 

## Viral infections
Respiratory virus infections present a notable risk for the development and severity of asthma and exacerbations. A study conducted on 76 adult asthmatics requiring extracorporeal membrane oxygenation for near-fatal asthma found that respiratory viruses were detected in >50% of cases, with human RV being the most common type [ ]. RV is an RNA virus with a single-stranded, positive sense genome that consists of three subtypes: RV-A, RV-B and RV-C. These viruses are closely linked to chronic airway inflammation, leading to recurrent wheezing and childhood asthma [ ]. Recurrent RV1B exposure in mice resulted in significantly increased neutrophils in the bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL), supporting the link between RV infection and neutrophilic asthma [ , ]. In addition to RV, respiratory syncytial virus is also an important cause of severe lower respiratory tract infections, characterised by neutrophilic inflammation frequently seen in infected lungs in infants.

## Bacterial burden
Increased bacterial burden and a distinct composition of microbiota in respiratory tracts [ , ], such as Haemophilus influenzae, Mycoplasma pneumoniae and Chlamydophila pneumoniae, were frequently found in neutrophilic asthma, indicating possible roles of bacteria in the perpetuation of airway inflammation [ – ].

## Fungal infection
Asthma has also been reported to be associated with local fungal dysbiosis in the lungs, supported by increased species of fungi in induced sputum samples in patients with asthma compared to control samples [ ]. Additionally, specific fungi were more abundant in bronchoscopy and BAL samples in children with severe asthma as compared to those without it. However, there was no difference in overall fungal diversity [ ]. Notably, asthmatics sensitised to fungi have worse lung function and atopy, with higher IL-1 receptor antagonist (IL-1RA) levels in BAL, which can promote type 1 and type 17 responses, leading to neutrophilic inflammation and airway hyperreactivity [ ].

## Environmental pollutants
Environmental pollutants, including particulate matter, gaseous pollutants, and mixed traffic-related air pollution ( ), have long been recognised as significant contributors to the development of a variety of respiratory diseases including lung cancer and asthma [ , ].
Particulate matter is classified into several categories based on particle size, including PM10 (<10 µm), PM2.5 (<2.5 µm) and ultrafine particles (<0.1 µm) [ ]. Exposure to PM10 activates the release of neutrophil elastase and myeloperoxidase in neutrophils in vitro and increases cellularity and inflammatory infiltrate in the murine lungs [ ]. In addition, exposure to PM2.5 is associated with increased expression of genes involved in non-T2 inflammatory pathways in human airway epithelial cells [ ], promoting pathogenic T-helper 17 (Th17) differentiation and contributing to the pathogenesis of neutrophilic asthma [ ].
Involving 62 individuals with asthma and nine controls in a cross-sectional analysis, as well as a randomised crossover controlled exposure study of 87 healthy adults, the research revealed that exposure to ozone has been linked to sputum neutrophilia in older healthy volunteers and patients with atopic asthma [ , ]. Furthermore, ozone exposure in mice resulted in airway AHR through the production of IL-17A, a potent cytokine recruiting neutrophils [ ]. However, the mechanism by which ozone exerts its biological effect is not yet fully understood, and may involve its interaction with components of the airway lining fluid, leading to the production of secondary signalling molecules [ ].
Diesel exhaust particles, the primary component of traffic-related air pollution, can recruit a distinctive type of neutrophil that expresses both Ly6G and Siglec-F, and increase the amount of neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs) in the lung [ , ]. Additionally, in a mouse model of house dust mite-induced allergy, exposure to diesel exhaust particles resulted in an increase in IL-17A levels, leading to neutrophilic inflammation and airway remodelling in severe asthma [ , , ].
Cigarette smoke, a complex mixture of chemicals, has been demonstrated to markedly diminish lung function and elevate the risk of wheezing and asthma [ ]. A retrospective cross-sectional study in 833 adults with asthma showed that 60% of smokers with asthma have noneosinophilic airway inflammation (15% neutrophilic and 45% paucigranulocytic) [ ]. Additionally, a prospective cohort study encompassing 94 079 individuals aged 20–100 years from the Copenhagen General Population Study showed that individuals with asthma who also smoke have increased numbers of neutrophils (p=1×10–38), without affecting blood eosinophil (p=0.14) and IgE (p=0.1) levels [ ]. Furthermore, findings from a 12-year follow-up study conducted at a single centre on patients with adult-onset asthma indicated that asthmatics who have a history of smoking with >20 pack-years are at a greater risk of being hospitalised for respiratory-related issues [ ]. One potential explanation is that cigarette smoke elevates the number of activated type 3 innate lymphoid cells, leading to increased circulating neutrophil counts and frequencies of M1 alveolar macrophages, frequently increased in uncontrolled severe asthma [ ].
Electronic cigarettes (e-cigarettes) are often regarded as a harm-reduced substitute for combustible cigarettes [ ]. However, emerging evidence indicates a correlation between e-cigarette use and the prevalence of asthma [ , ]. Given that e-cigarette aerosol triggers the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines [ , ], along with elevated markers of neutrophil activation in the sputum of e-cigarette users [ , ], we hypothesise that e-cigarettes might be associated with non-T2 asthma, even though direct evidence is lacking. Further research is needed to determine the underlying mechanisms and develop focused treatments for individuals with non-T2 asthma who are exposed to cigarette smoke or e-cigarettes.

## Obesity
Obesity, which has undergone a consistent rise in prevalence over the past several decades [ ], is markedly associated with the development of asthma ( ) [ ].
 Emerging evidence has shown that obesity is correlated with poor outcomes in asthma, such as higher rate of frequent use of oral corticosteroids (46.9% versus 23.1%, p=0.028), more frequent dispensing of oral corticosteroids (81.6% versus 56.9%, p=0.014), higher doses of oral corticosteroids (median 1350 (interquartile range 280–3180) mg versus 600 (0–1650) mg prednisolone, p=0.010) and frequent urgent healthcare visits (38.8% versus 16.9%, p=0.033) [ , ]. A study involved 63 obese (body mass index (BMI) ≥30 kg·m−2) and 213 lean (BMI <25 kg·m−2) patients with asthma showed that individuals with obesity-related asthma typically manifest more neutrophils in sputum (52% versus 36%) and blood (4200 cells·μL−1
versus 3400 cells·μL−1) than those patients with asthma who are not obese [ ]. Another study encompassing 96 patients with asthma also showed predominant neutrophilic airway inflammation in obesity-only asthma (neutrophils 59.4%), rather than the obesity–atopy overlap asthma (neutrophils 25.0%) [ ]. Furthermore, in obese asthmatic children, there is observable nonatopic Th1-polarised systemic inflammation, which is associated with impairments in pulmonary function [ ]. Overall, obesity is clearly a significant risk factor for the development and management of asthma.

## Molecular mechanisms of non-T2 asthma
Non-T2 asthma involves activation of various pathways in multiple types of immune cells, including activation of the inflammasome, type I, II and III interferons (IFNs), the IL-17 pathway and the release of cytokines and chemokines in neutrophils, macrophages, mast cells, dendritic cells and epithelial cells. These mechanisms are pivotal in the initiation and amplification of the inflammatory response, ultimately contributing to the development and progression of non-T2 asthma.

## Inflammasome activation
The inflammasome, a protein complex activated upon cellular infection or stress, facilitates the maturation, and secretion of IL-1 family members, as well as the initiation of gasdermin D-mediated inflammatory cell death, pyroptosis [ ]. The inflammasome protein complex includes three major components: an adaptor protein, a scaffold protein and pro-caspase 1. The adaptor protein is ASC (apoptosis-associated speck-like protein containing a CARD), and scaffold proteins include AIM2 (absent in melanoma 2), Nod-like receptors (NLRs) such as NLR family pyrin domain containing 1 (NLRP1), NLRP3, NLRP6 and NLRC4 ( ) [ ]. Dysfunction of the inflammasome contributes to various diseases, such as chronic rhinosinusitis [ ], colorectal cancer [ ], very early onset inflammatory bowel disease [ ] and infectious diseases [ , ]. Importantly, inflammasome activation has recently been found in the development and progression of non-T2 asthma [ ].
A study involving 585 participants with asthma and 527 healthy controls showed that increased expression of inflammasome genes including NLRP3, caspase-1, caspase-4, caspase-5 and IL-1β was detected in nonsmoking, neutrophilic asthma, indicating that the NLRP3-dependent inflammasome activation in neutrophilic asthma may promote inflammation through the production of IL-1β [ ]. Likewise, a transcriptomic and proteomic analysis conducted on sputum samples from severe asthma cohorts (n=265) suggested that the expression of IL-1 and IL-18R family members, as well as the inflammasome genes NLRP1, NLRP3 and NLRC4, were associated with the neutrophilic asthma [ ]. In addition, in a study involving 399 patients diagnosed with asthma from SARP-3, as well as 94 healthy control subjects, the presence of sputum extracellular DNA in asthma was linked to an increase in airway neutrophilic inflammation, a rise in soluble NET components, higher caspase 1 activity and an elevation in IL-1β levels [ ]. Mouse models have provided additional validation regarding the significance of the inflammasome in neutrophilic asthma [ ]. Application of caspase-1 inhibitor (Ac-YVAD-cho) or NLRP3 inhibitor (MCC950) effectively suppressed IL-1β responses and mitigated steroid-resistant features of the non-T2 asthma by targeting inflammasome activation [ ]. Additionally, the immunomodulatory protein macrophage migration inhibitory factor (MIF), which promotes NLRP3 inflammasome assembly and IL-1β secretion in macrophages, can also elicit neutrophil recruitment to the lung and antagonise responses to glucocorticoids [ , ]. Thus, administering the MIF antagonist ISO-1 was found to effectively reduce neutrophilic inflammation and improve glucocorticoid responsiveness in treatment [ ]. Furthermore, obesity induced by a high-fat diet led to AHR that was insensitive to steroids and was associated with heightened NLRP3 inflammasome responses in asthma mouse models [ , ]. Hence, inflammasome may provide a potential target for treating non-T2 asthma.

## Type I and type III IFN response

## Molecular pathways
Type I and type III IFN responses are important mechanisms in the innate immune system which defend against viruses. The MAVS (mitochondrial antiviral-signalling protein)-TBK1 (TANK binding kinase 1) pathway and the cyclic guanosine monophosphate (GMP)-AMP synthase (cGAS)-stimulator of IFN genes (STING) pathway are two major pathways that sense viral infection and activate IFN pathway [ , ]. Upon RNA virus infection, pattern recognition receptors, including retinoic acid-inducible gene I (RIG-I) or melanoma differentiation-associated gene 5 (MDA-5) recognise viral RNA and recruit MAVS to activate phosphorylation of TBK1 and IFN regulatory factor 3 (IRF3) for type 1 and type 3 IFN production [ ]. Aberrant intracellular DNA is sensed by the DNA sensor cGAS, leading to the conversion of guanosine 5′-triphosphate and ATP into the second messenger 2′3′ cyclic GMP-AMP (cGAMP). Subsequently, STING binds to cGAMP and activates TBK1 and IRF3 phosphorylation for downstream type I and type III IFN production [ ]. By means of autocrine and paracrine mechanisms, those type I and type III IFNs bind to IFN receptors, activating the janus kinase/signal transducer and activator of transcription (JAK/STAT) pathway that induces expression of IFN-stimulated genes (ISGs) [ ], limiting the dissemination of viral infections ( ). However, ISGs also induce pro-inflammatory cytokines, thereby promoting inflammation in various lung diseases, such as COPD [ ], idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis [ – ], severe coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) [ ] and long COVID [ ].
 

## Type I and III IFNs and asthma
Emerging evidence has shown that type I and type III IFNs and their downstream ISGs are closely linked to asthma development. For example, higher expression of airway epithelial ISGs was linked to lower FEV1 and increased bronchodilator response, independent of T2 inflammation [ ]. Also, increased production of IFN-β post-RV16 infection in airway epithelial cells led to a significant increase in T2 gene expression in mast cells [ ]. Furthermore, the production of IFNs such as IFN-λ in the serum also increased during the development of asthma and increased expression of IFN-λ receptor was detected in peripheral blood mononuclear cells from primary-school-age children with asthma [ ]. In addition to virus, poly (I:C), a virus mimic, also led to increased induction of IFN-β in patients with atopic asthma compared to nonatopic patients [ ].
IFNs response may be involved in T2 asthma as well. For example, in response to the allergen house dust mite, dendritic cells induce type 2 inflammation through type I IFN [ ]. However, other studies present contradictory observations. Neutrophilic, but not eosinophilic, asthmatics display overexpression of IFN-β, IFN-λ1/IL-29 and ISGs in their sputum samples [ ]. Interestingly, C-C motif chemokine ligand 5, which is a typical ISG, may contribute to both neutrophilic and eosinophilic inflammation observed in asthma [ ]. It is worth noting that transcription-associated clusters (TACs) using hierarchical clustering of differentially expressed genes revealed three distinct molecular phenotypes of asthma. Among them, TAC2 was associated with sputum neutrophilia and characterised by genes related to IFN-α, together with TNF-α and inflammasome [ ].
Although those studies suggest that individuals with asthma display elevated activation of IFN signalling upon respiratory viral infection compared to nonasthmatics, another line of evidence suggests the opposite [ – ]. For example, the expression of IFN-α and IFN-β in bronchoalveolar lavage cells from asthmatic patients were delayed and deficient following RV infection [ ]. Moreover, a noteworthy breakthrough has been achieved in understanding how the asthma risk gene, GSDMB, promotes IFN signalling in response to respiratory virus infection and aberrant release of mitochondrial DNA [ , ]. In addition, in individuals with asthma, the bronchial epithelium exhibited less IFN-α and IFN-β production, both at baseline and post-infection, in comparison to the control subjects [ ]. These apparent inconsistencies could potentially be reconciled: first, deficient IFN signalling at the early stage could impair viral clearance, and second, a sustained IFN signalling at a later stage could exacerbate airway inflammation, both contributing to the development of asthma. Additionally, disparities in factors related to the host, such as age during infection, atopic condition, genetic background, as well as viral factors such as viral species or strain variations, could account for these conflicting results.

## Type II IFN
In addition to type I and type III IFNs, type II IFN (IFN-γ) was also shown to promote asthma pathogenesis, especially for severe asthma, which responds poorly to high doses of systemic corticosteroids [ , – ]. Additionally, following RV infection, asthmatics produced higher levels of IFN-γ in nasal lining fluid [ ], which was required for development of AHR in severe asthma [ , ]. Mechanically, IFN-γ can induce expression of CXC motif chemokine ligand 10 (CXCL10), which recruits Th1 cells via CXCR3 receptor, contributing to severe asthma [ ].

## IL-17 pathway
IL-17 cytokines play a vital role in regulating neutrophil accumulation, mucus secretion, macrophage mobilisation and smooth muscle reactivity in several experimental mouse models of airway diseases [ , , ]. Recent evidence suggests that elevated IL-17 expression, which tends to be more pronounced in adult patients with severe and corticosteroid-insensitive asthma, is associated with some, but not all non-T2 asthma [ ]. Furthermore, the IL-17 pathway facilitates obesity-induced AHR in mouse models [ ]. Hence, IL-17 is a potential contributor to neutrophilic asthma [ ].
IL-17 cytokines promote recruitment of neutrophils to the airways by inducing epithelial-derived chemokines that attract neutrophils, including CXCL1 and CXCL8 [ ]. Indeed, airway samples from individuals with severe neutrophilic asthma showed significant upregulation of those chemokines induced by IL-17 [ ]. Additionally, TRIM33 (tripartite motif containing protein 33), was also reported to regulate function of Th17 cells by inducing IL-17 [ ], and could act as a marker for asthma with the highest BMI, systemic inflammation and highest blood neutrophil count. This highlights the significance of IL-17 in the development of neutrophilic asthma. However, the precise role of IL-17 in asthma remains controversial with either a protective or a detrimental effect on asthma in murine models, depending on the timing of IL-17 blockade [ ]. Moreover, neither blocking IL-17RA [ , ] nor inhibiting IL-23, an inducer of IL-17, by risankizumab, an anti-IL-23 monoclonal antibody, has resulted in any therapeutic benefit for individuals with asthma [ , ]. Thus, further research is needed to fully understand the mechanisms by which IL-17 contributes to non-T2 asthma and to develop effective therapeutic strategies to target IL-17.

## IL-6 signalling pathway
IL-6 is also implicated in severe asthma, regardless of eosinophilic inflammation status [ ], supported by increased IL-6 levels detected in serum samples from asthmatics, especially in severe asthma patients with obesity, metabolic issues or neutrophilic asthma [ ]. Such increased IL-6 levels also significantly correlate with lower baseline percentage predicted FEV1, higher blood neutrophil counts, more recent hospitalisations and systemic corticosteroid use in the past year [ ]. Non-T2 asthmatics with systemic IL-6 inflammation and obesity are more likely to have a shortage of airway cytotoxic CD8+ T-cells, which might indicate T-cell exhaustion, leading to weakened antiviral immune responses and an increased frequency of exacerbations [ ]. Additionally, sIL-6R levels are also elevated in the serum, BAL and sputum of individuals with non-T2 asthma [ , ]. Furthermore, in those cells that cannot express IL-6Rα, sIL-6R engages with IL-6 to trigger IL-6 trans-signalling within epithelial cells, leading to pro-inflammatory effects and ultimately resulting in lung function impairment [ , ]. Given that asthmatic patients who exhibit high airway IL-6 and sIL-6R levels were more prevalent in the neutrophilic and mixed granulocytic subtypes, targeting IL-6 and IL-6 trans-signalling pathway might hold promise in providing advantages to patients with non-T2 asthma [ ].

## Alarmins and epithelial-derived cytokines
Alarmins and epithelial-derived cytokines, including thymic stromal lymphopoietin (TSLP), IL-33 and IL-25, are mediators released during cell degranulation upon injury or cell death [ ]. In addition to driving T2 inflammation in asthma, these alarmins and cytokines may also contribute to non-T2 asthma [ ]. TSLP promotes neutrophilic, non-T2 airway inflammation by enhancing the differentiation of the naïve T-cells to Th17 cells [ ], through recruiting neutrophils to the airways by epithelial-derived chemokines, such as CXCL1 and CXCL8 [ ]. This could potentially explain the observed benefits of anti-TSLP therapy in a broad population of asthma subjects with non-T2 characteristics [ , – ]. IL-33 is another alarmin cytokine that plays vital roles in both T2 and non-T2 inflammation [ ]. It binds to its receptor (ST2; IL-1 receptor-like 1) on parenchymal and inflammatory cells, leading to the activation of Th1 or Th2 cells [ ]. IL-33 also activates mast cells, which associates with neutrophilic asthma [ ]. Thus, targeting the IL-33–ST2 axis may mitigate the severity of an asthma exacerbation by suppressing neutrophil recruitment and neutrophilic inflammation [ , , ]. In addition, in airway epithelium, oxidised IL-33 can promote mucus hypersecretion via ST2-independent receptor for advanced glycation end products/epidermal growth factor receptor signalling complex, suggesting that oxidised IL-33 might represent a prospective target in chronic inflammatory diseases [ ].

## Others
In addition to the mechanisms discussed previously, there are other factors involved in the development of non-T2 asthma, including CXCR2, alkaline phosphatase, tissue-nonspecific isozyme (ALPL), IL-33 and possibly the cytokine TSLP. CXCR2 levels are elevated in neutrophilic asthma and CXCR2 receptor antagonists, such as AZD5069, have been shown to significantly reduce absolute sputum neutrophil counts in bronchiectasis patients [ , ]. ALPL is also strongly related to sputum neutrophil percentage, potentially contributing to neutrophilic inflammation in patients with asthma [ ]. IL-33-activated gene signatures are increased in asthma subtypes characterised by elevated neutrophil counts or a mixture of granulocytes, and this corresponds to the expression of IL1RAP co-receptors [ ]. TSLP, produced by epithelial cells and associated with allergic inflammation, is also potentially involved in non-T2 pathology, as anti-TSLP therapy has shown some efficacy in treating patients with asthma and low blood eosinophil counts [ , – ].

## Mouse models
Multiple mouse models have been developed to trigger airway neutrophilia mimicking non-T2 asthma. For example, when mice are challenged with ovalbumin (OVA) plus lipopolysaccharide (LPS), a marked Th17 response and modest Th2 responses are elicited in lungs, leading to neutrophilia and AHR [ ]. Repetitive administration of house dust mite, five times a week for 3 weeks, led to pulmonary inflammation dominated by activations of various T-cell subtypes: Th17>Th2>Th1 response, accompanied by lung eosinophilia and neutrophil infiltration that resulted in AHR [ ]. Furthermore, evidence from mouse models supports inflammasome [ ], IL-17 [ ] and IL-6 [ ] as therapeutic targets in T2-low asthma. Although mice are extensively employed as experimental models in allergy research, they do not naturally manifest “asthma” on their own [ ]. Consequently, these models come with a variety of limitations that need to be taken into consideration in the future.

## Therapeutic strategies targeting non-T2 asthma
Even though monoclonal antibodies that target IgE, IL-4, IL-13 and IL-5 are successful in treating severe T2 asthma, ∼30–50% of patients diagnosed with non-T2 asthma are not suitable for these treatments in severe asthma clinics [ ]. Hence, there is an urgent need for new targets and treatment strategies ( ).
 

## Pharmacological therapies
In subjects with severe, uncontrolled asthma, ecleralimab and tezepelumab, two antibodies that neutralise the activity of TSLP, have been found to significantly alleviate airway inflammation and even asthma exacerbations in patients with low blood eosinophil counts, indicating that blocking TSLP may provide additional benefits in the management of asthma beyond reducing inflammation in T2 inflammation [ , ]. Excepting aiming at the target of TSLP, directing attention towards IL-33 could also offer benefits for individuals with non-T2 asthma. For example, IL-33 blockade by a neutralising antibody REGN3500 effectively decreases the infiltration of lung neutrophils and the presence of pro-inflammatory cytokines, like IL-6, IL-1β and TNF-α, along with mitigating T2 inflammation [ ]. Furthermore, itepekimab, a novel monoclonal antibody targeting IL-33, leads to a lower occurrence of events signifying compromised asthma management and improved lung function in patients [ ]. Additionally, inhibiting IL-33/ST2-mediated inflammation with astegolimab reduces asthma exacerbation rate in those patients with low eosinophil levels [ ].
IL-1β is another novel target against neutrophil-attractant cytokine release [ ]. The production of lung IL-1β and subsequent neutrophilic airway inflammation was reduced when either a pharmacological NLRP3 inhibitor (MCC950) or caspase-1 inhibitor (Ac-YVAD-cho) was administered [ ]. Additionally, a phase I clinical trial involving 17 healthy volunteers who participated in a double-blind placebo-controlled crossover study displayed that anakinra, an IL-1 receptor antagonist, has also been identified as a possible treatment option based on its ability to reduce sputum neutrophil counts [ ]. Furthermore, tocilizumab, a monoclonal antibody targeting the IL-6 receptor, has been successful in treating two patients with asthma with mixed Th2/Th17 inflammation [ ].
In addition, other alternative strategies for treating non-T2 asthma are still under investigation. For example, LPS-OVA-treated mice developed neutrophilic-like bronchial asthma, the administration of LC28-0126, a scavenger for reactive oxygen species, effectively mitigates the pathophysiology of the condition [ ]. Treatment with azithromycin, an antibiotic employed to treat a wide variety of bacterial infections, correlated with a diminished frequency of exacerbations in asthma as well as in the noneosinophilic and severe asthma subgroups [ – ]. Importantly, add-on azithromycin therapy in poorly controlled asthma proved to be a cost-effective treatment strategy [ ]. Additionally, a study involving 17 patients with severe persistent asthma showed a correlation between noneosinophilic sputum profile and a better response to the recently developed anticholinergic, tiotropium bromide [ ]. Furthermore, data from 2 phase III trials involving symptomatic moderate asthma and 2 phase III trials involving symptomatic severe asthma showed tiotropium bromide is efficacious for treating 6- to 17-year-olds with asthma, independent of T2 phenotype [ ]. However, given that eosinophil count might serve as an incomplete biomarker to separate T2 from non-T2 asthma, additional phenotyping at molecular levels on patients with asthma who derived most benefits from these antibody treatments will facilitate precision medicine for asthmatics in the future [ , – , , ].

## Nonpharmacological therapies
In addition to pharmacological therapies, nonpharmacological therapies for non-type 2 asthma have also been proposed. Airway remodelling, featured as thickened basement membrane, increased epithelial loss and heightened smooth muscle mass, may potentially play a significant role in triggering AHR and symptoms [ , ]. The remodelling features of airway smooth muscle mass showed an elevation in all asthma groups [ ]. In particular, in patients lacking eosinophilic infiltration, structural changes characteristic of asthma develop in asthmatic children in the absence of T2 inflammation [ ], suggesting additional cytokines contributing to airway remodelling in non-T2 asthma, such as the IL-17 family of cytokines IL-1β, IL-33 and IFN [ , , ]. Bronchial thermoplasty represents an invasive bronchoscopic procedure applied to the airway wall to decrease the mass of smooth muscle, or inhibition of airway neurons or inflammatory cells [ , , ]. Patients exhibiting non-T2 asthma and AHR have the potential to be suitable candidates for bronchial thermoplasty [ ]. Clinical studies suggest that applying bronchial thermoplasty in subjects with moderate or severe asthma results in improved asthma management, albeit associated with a short-term increase in asthma-related morbidity among individuals with symptomatic, severe asthma [ – ]. Significantly, in practice, bronchial thermoplasty emerges as a treatment choice for patients diagnosed with severe asthma, particularly among those with non-T2 asthma as demonstrated by the 86% response rate in the non-T2 cohort [ ]. Of note, a study of 32 smokers with asthma showed that individuals who quit smoking demonstrated significant improvement in lung function and a reduction in sputum neutrophil count (reduced by 29.1%) after 6 weeks’ smoking cessation [ ]. A longitudinal study conducted on individuals with both obesity and asthma showed that bariatric surgery can also improve small airway function, reduce systemic inflammation and decrease the count of mast cells within the airways [ ]. Furthermore, a study involving eight non-T2 and five T2-high obese asthmatics showed that weight loss led to a reduced sensitivity to airway closure in non-T2 obese asthmatics, but not T2-high obese asthmatics [ ].

## Conclusion
Our pathogenic understanding and diagnostic criteria for non-T2 asthma have not yet reached consensus, and current treatment options for non-T2 asthma are limited. Therefore, developing more diagnostic biomarkers and exploring additional treatment targets is necessary. Given the involvement of inflammasome activation, IFN response, IL-17 pathway, IL-6 and TSLP in the progression of non-T2 asthma, identifying these potential molecular signatures, including IL-1β, IL-18, IL-17, IL-6, IFNs and TSLP can be practical for diagnosing non-T2 asthma in a clinical context. Also, by applying personalised medicine strategies to strategy patients treated with targeted therapies for genetic determinants for non-T2 asthma, we may enhance health-related quality of life, improve asthma control, and reduce primary care acute visits for individuals with this condition.
