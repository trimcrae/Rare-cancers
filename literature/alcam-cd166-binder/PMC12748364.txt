## Introduction
Cancer remains a formidable global health challenge, characterized by its complex heterogeneity, aggressive progression, and alarming rates of recurrence and therapeutic resistance [ – ]. While conventional treatment modalities, including surgery, chemotherapy, immunotherapy, and radiotherapy, have significantly improved patient outcomes to some extent, they frequently encounter intrinsic limitations [ , ]. These limitations stem from their often broad, non-specific modes of action, leading to cytotoxic effects on healthy tissues, systemic side effects, and the development of multidrug resistance [ – ]. A major contributing factor to treatment failure and tumor relapse is the persistence of a small, highly resilient subpopulation of cells known as cancer stem cells (CSCs) [ , ]. These cells possess unique stem-like properties, including self-renewal capacity and the ability to differentiate into the heterogeneous cell types that constitute the tumor bulk, thereby driving tumor initiation, progression, metastasis, and, crucially, resistance to conventional therapies [ ]. CSCs often reside in a quiescent or slow-cycling state, enabling them to evade therapies that primarily target rapidly dividing cells [ , ]. Paradoxically, conventional treatments can even enrich the CSC fraction within a tumor, paving the way for more aggressive recurrence [ ]. This phenomenon occurs because therapies that kill only the bulk of the more sensitive tumor cells allow more resistant CSCs to evade elimination, potentially leading to CSCs constituting the greatest fraction of remaining or recurrent tumors [ ]. This feedback loop, where conventional therapies enrich the resistant CSC population, highlights the urgent need for new strategies that can eradicate these cells to achieve durable patient responses [ ].
In response to these profound challenges, the fields of nanomedicine and nutraceutical research have emerged as highly promising avenues [ , ]. Nanomedicine, leveraging materials engineered at the nanoscale, offers unprecedented opportunities to revolutionize cancer therapy by enhancing the specificity, efficacy, and tolerability of anticancer agents [ ]. Nanocarriers can encapsulate both hydrophilic and hydrophobic drugs, protect them from degradation, prolong their systemic circulation, improve their bioavailability, and facilitate their targeted delivery to tumor sites, thereby minimizing off-target toxicity and improving therapeutic indices [ ]. Concurrently, nutraceuticals—bioactive compounds derived from natural sources, particularly plants—have garnered significant attention for their diverse pharmacological properties, including antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and direct antitumor effects [ , ]. These compounds demonstrate the ability to modulate various cellular processes and signaling pathways implicated in carcinogenesis and, importantly, in the maintenance of CSC stemness [ ]. However, the clinical translation of many potent nutraceuticals is often hampered by their inherent limitations, such as poor aqueous solubility, rapid metabolism, low systemic bioavailability, and instability under physiological conditions [ ]. This is where the synergy between nanomedicine and nutraceuticals becomes particularly powerful [ ]. Nanocarriers are designed to directly overcome these pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic limitations of nutraceuticals, transforming these promising but bio-limited compounds into clinically viable therapeutic agents [ ]. By enhancing their stability, solubility, and targeted delivery, nanocarriers unlock the full therapeutic potential of nutraceuticals, allowing for more potent and less toxic targeted therapies against CSCs [ , ].
This comprehensive review aims to synthesize the current understanding at the intersection of nanomedicine and nutraceuticals, focusing specifically on their synergistic application for targeted drug delivery to cancer stem cells [ , ]. The discussion will delve into the unique biological characteristics of CSCs that render them elusive targets, explore the principles and platforms of nanomedicine that enable precise drug delivery, and delineate the anti-CSC mechanisms of various promising nutraceuticals [ ]. Subsequently, innovative nanocarrier-based strategies designed to overcome the limitations of nutraceuticals and achieve specific CSC targeting will be highlighted, presenting compelling preclinical evidence [ ]. Finally, the significant translational challenges that must be addressed for these advanced therapies to reach clinical fruition will be critically discussed, and the transformative potential of emerging technologies, including biomimetic systems, microfluidics, and artificial intelligence, in shaping the future of cancer therapy at the "STEM Frontier" will be illuminated [ ].

## Methodology
This narrative review is based on a comprehensive literature search conducted across multiple electronic databases, including PubMed/MEDLINE, Scopus, and Google Scholar, to identify relevant articles published from 2000 up to February 2025. The search strategy combined keywords and MeSH terms related to the core topics. Primary search strings included combinations of: ("cancer stem cell" OR "CSC") AND ("nanomedicine" OR "nanocarrier" OR "nanoparticle") AND ("nutraceutical" OR "phytochemical" OR "natural compound") AND ("targeted delivery" OR "drug delivery"). Additional searches were performed for specific nutraceuticals (e.g., "curcumin," "resveratrol") and emerging technologies (e.g., "biomimetic nanoparticles," "tumor-on-chip," "AI in nanomedicine").
Inclusion criteria for selected articles were: (1) original research (preclinical, in vitro, and in vivo studies), (2) comprehensive review articles, and (3) publications written in English. Conference abstracts, editorials, and non-peer-reviewed sources were excluded to maintain the scientific rigor of the review. Preprint servers were monitored for the latest research, but findings were only included if subsequently published in a peer-reviewed journal. The initial screening was based on titles and abstracts, followed by a full-text assessment for final inclusion by the authors. While this narrative review did not follow a systematic protocol, the initial search yielded approximately 600 articles. Through the described screening process, 151 highly relevant sources were selected and included in this comprehensive synthesis.

## Cancer stem cells: the root of recurrence and resistance
Cancer stem cells (CSCs) represent a distinct and highly critical subpopulation within heterogeneous tumors, recognized as the primary drivers of tumor initiation, progression, metastasis, and, crucially, resistance to conventional cancer therapies [ ]. These cells, often comprising only a small fraction of the total tumor mass, exhibit remarkable self-renewal capacity, allowing them to continuously generate new CSCs while also differentiating into the diverse non-stem cancer cells that form the bulk of the tumor [ ]. This hierarchical organization means that even if the majority of the tumor is eradicated by treatment, the persistence of a few CSCs can lead to aggressive tumor relapse [ ].
Key characteristics contributing to therapeutic resistance are the intrinsic properties of CSCs, including quiescence and plasticity. Many CSCs exist in a dormant or slow-cycling state, which allows them to evade chemotherapies and radiation therapies that primarily target rapidly dividing cells [ , ]. Compounding this, CSCs exhibit significant plasticity, enabling them to dynamically transition between stem-like and differentiated states in response to therapeutic pressure or environmental cues [ ]. This inherent adaptability makes them "moving targets" and underpins their profound resilience, leading to their enrichment following conventional treatments and subsequent tumor recurrence [ ].
Beyond these intrinsic properties, CSCs thrive within a specialized microenvironment known as the CSC niche, which protects them and sustains their stemness [ ]. A critical function of this niche is to provide immune privilege. CSCs possess sophisticated mechanisms to evade immune recognition, including the downregulation of major histocompatibility complex (MHC) molecules, the secretion of immunosuppressive cytokines (e.g., TGF-β), and the active recruitment of regulatory immune cells like T-regulatory cells (Tregs) and myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) [ ]. This immune-privileged status, summarized in Table  , enables them to evade the body's natural defenses and emerging immunotherapies. Consequently, truly effective anti-CSC strategies must be multi-modal, not only aiming for direct elimination but also disrupting their dynamic adaptability and overcoming their sophisticated immune evasion mechanisms [ ]. Understanding these multifaceted roles is essential for developing effective and durable cancer therapies [ , ]. 
The self-renewal and survival of CSCs are intricately regulated by several aberrantly activated cellular signaling pathways [ ]. Key among these are the Wnt/β-catenin, Hedgehog, Notch, and phosphoinositide-3-kinase–Akt–mammalian target of rapamycin (PI3K–Akt–mTOR) pathways [ ]. The Wnt/β-catenin pathway is fundamental for both normal stem cell and CSC self-renewal, with its dysregulation implicated in various cancers [ ]. Aberrant activation, often involving nuclear accumulation of β-catenin, is linked to enhanced self-renewal, higher tumor relapse rates, and resistance to chemotherapy and radiation [ ]. The Hedgehog pathway normally functions in tissue development but is frequently dysregulated in cancer, promoting cellular differentiation and proliferation [ ]. While Hedgehog inhibitors exist, their use can sometimes promote drug-resistant tumors, potentially due to CSC selection [ ]. The PI3K-Akt-mTOR pathway is frequently hyperactivated in malignancies, promoting tumor growth and survival, and its aberrant alterations in CSCs provide crucial molecular targets [ , ]. The Notch pathway is also widely implicated in CSC maintenance and survival [ ]. These altered intracellular pathways, compared to non-stem cancer cells and normal cells, provide molecular candidates for CSC biomarkers [ ].
The identification of CSC-specific surface receptors or biomarkers is paramount for the development of targeted therapies. Several markers have been associated with CSCs, including CD44, CD133, CD166, and ALDH [ , ]. CD133 is a cell-surface glycoprotein conserved across various cancer CSCs (e.g., breast, liver, lung, ovarian) and is involved in Wnt and PI3K-Akt signaling. CD44 is a widely used cell surface adhesion receptor biomarker for breast, colon, and glioblastoma CSCs, often overexpressed and acting as a receptor for hyaluronic acid. ALDH1 (Aldehyde Dehydrogenase 1), particularly the ALDH1A1 isoform, is an intracellular enzyme overexpressed by CSCs in breast, prostate, colon, lung, and ovarian cancers, and has been explored for CSC detection and sorting. However, a central challenge lies in the poor specificity and low abundance of these conventional endogenous markers, as they are often also expressed by non-stem cancer cells or healthy cells, albeit at lower levels. Similarly, intracellular transcription factors are not specific to CSCs as most signaling and metabolic pathways are shared with non-stem cancer cells and normal cells. Relying solely on the current set of conventional CSC surface markers for active targeting strategies may lead to significant off-target effects and compromised therapeutic efficacy due to a lack of true selectivity [ ]. This inherent limitation has spurred efforts to move towards more sophisticated and highly specific targeting approaches, such as the development of artificial biomarkers [ ]. For instance, ALDH1A1-responsive AAMCHO can selectively label CSCs with azido groups for targeted conjugation via click chemistry. This specificity is paramount for successful clinical translation, ensuring precise delivery to CSCs while minimizing collateral damage to healthy tissues [ ] (Table  ). 

## Nanomedicine in cancer therapy: principles and platforms
In the context of this review, the term ‘nanoparticle’ refers to any particle within the 1–1000 nm range, while a ‘nanocarrier’ is a nanoparticle specifically engineered to transport a therapeutic payload.
Nanomedicine represents a paradigm shift in cancer therapeutics, offering sophisticated solutions to the inherent limitations of conventional treatments [ , ]. Its application signals a new epoch, distinguished by significant improvements in the specificity, efficacy, and tolerability of cancer interventions. The fundamental principle behind nanomedicine's advantage lies in its ability to engineer materials at the nanoscale (typically 1–1000 nm), allowing for precise manipulation of drug pharmacokinetics and biodistribution [ , ].
The mechanisms and advantages of nanoparticle-mediated drug delivery are multifaceted [ ]. Nanoparticle-based drug delivery platforms have emerged as superior vehicles for overcoming pharmacokinetic limitations associated with conventional drug formulations, such as poor solubility and rapid clearance. Nanocarriers can encapsulate both hydrophilic and hydrophobic drugs, preventing their rapid blood clearance, supporting sustained drug release, extending plasma circulation half-life, enhancing tumor accumulation, and improving overall bioavailability while mitigating toxic effects on non-target tissues [ , ]. This leads to a substantial improvement in the therapeutic index of anticancer agents. Furthermore, nanomedicine can reduce multidrug resistance, assist in tumor diagnosis and imaging, and achieve steady-state drug levels over extended periods [ , ]. Their nanosized nature facilitates tissue penetration and enhances cellular uptake, ensuring efficient drug delivery and activity at the targeted location [ , ]. Nanoparticles also enable the co-delivery of multiple drugs, which can improve therapeutic efficacy and overcome drug resistance by providing precise control over the spatiotemporal exposure and appropriate ratios of each drug. The integration of therapeutic agents with imaging modalities allows for visualization of drug delivery sites and real-time feedback on in vivo efficacy [ , ].
Nanocarrier-mediated tumor targeting is achieved through two primary strategies: passive and active. Passive targeting leverages the Enhanced Permeability and Retention (EPR) effect, where the leaky vasculature of tumors allows nanocarriers (typically 10–200 nm) to accumulate preferentially in the tumor microenvironment [ , ]. Active targeting enhances this specificity by functionalizing the nanocarrier surface with ligands (e.g., antibodies, peptides) that bind to receptors overexpressed on CSCs. This ligand-receptor interaction promotes selective cellular uptake at the target site [ ].

## Biological barriers to effective nanocarrier delivery
Despite the promise of passive and active targeting, the clinical efficacy of nanocarriers is often limited by a complex series of biological barriers that impede their journey from circulation to the target cancer stem cells. Systemically, nanoparticles are susceptible to opsonization—coating by plasma proteins—which triggers their rapid clearance by the mononuclear phagocyte system (MPS), leading to high accumulation in the liver and spleen and reducing the dose available for the tumor [ ].
Within the tumor itself, the Enhanced Permeability and Retention (EPR) effect, the foundation of passive targeting, is highly heterogeneous. It varies significantly between patients, tumor types, and even within different regions of a single tumor, leading to unpredictable and often insufficient drug accumulation [ – ]. Furthermore, the tumor microenvironment presents formidable physical obstacles, including a dense extracellular matrix (ECM) and high interstitial fluid pressure, which severely limit the deep penetration of nanoparticles. As a result, nanocarriers can become trapped near the vasculature, failing to reach the quiescent and often deeply embedded CSC niches [ , ]. Overcoming these systemic and intratumoral barriers is a central challenge in nanomedicine and the primary motivation for developing more advanced, actively targeted, and biomimetic delivery systems.
A diverse array of nanocarrier types has been developed for cancer drug delivery. Promising vehicles include liposomes, which are lipid-based vesicles extensively investigated for clinical relevance, facilitating the delivery of chemotherapeutic drugs and minimizing harm to non-target tissues [ ]. Examples include liposomal doxorubicin, which has shown reduced cardiotoxicity compared to free drug [ ]. Polymeric nanoparticles and polymeric micelles are also widely studied, offering high drug loading, controlled release, and ideal biocompatibility [ ]. Other notable types include dendrimers, nanoemulsions, nanotubes, and inorganic nanoparticles such as silicon-based nanomaterials and metal nanoparticles [ ]. The physicochemical properties of these nanocarriers can be finely tuned by altering their compositions (organic, inorganic, or hybrid), sizes, shapes (sphere, rod, or cube), and surface properties (surface charge, functional groups, PEGylation, or attachment of targeting moieties) [ ]. For instance, magnetic iron oxide nanoparticles can be utilized for hyperthermia-based cancer treatment, act as contrast agents for MRI, and can be modified with specific coatings or conjugated to drugs, proteins, or antibodies for targeted delivery, often guided by an external magnetic field [ ]. The utility of nanocarriers in cancer therapy is profoundly multi-faceted [ ]. They are not merely inert vehicles for transporting drugs but are active components that can dynamically interact with and modulate the complex tumor microenvironment [ ]. This includes enhancing immune responses, integrating diagnostic capabilities (leading to "theranostics" – combined therapy and diagnosis), and even direct therapeutic action [ – ]. This holistic approach is crucial for addressing the complex and heterogeneous biology of CSCs and the broader tumor landscape, moving beyond simple drug delivery to comprehensive disease management and personalized oncology [ ] (Table  ). 

## Nutraceuticals: natural agents with anti-cancer stem cell potential
Nutraceuticals, defined as food or food components providing health benefits beyond basic nutrition, have garnered significant interest in oncology due to their inherent therapeutic potential and generally favorable safety profiles [ , ]. A diet rich in plant-based foods, such as the Mediterranean Diet, and specific bioactive compounds like bergamot polyphenolic fraction (BPF), cynaropicrin, oleuropein, quercetin, resveratrol, and serotonin, has been associated with reduced cancer risk and improved treatment outcomes [ ]. These compounds can modulate critical biological processes, including oxidative stress, inflammation, the tumor microenvironment, cell cycle progression, and drug resistance [ , ]. While nutritional supplements are widely used by cancer patients who perceive them as anticancer and antitoxicity agents, large-scale randomized trials have shown mixed results, with some notable adverse effects (e.g., beta-carotene increasing lung cancer risk, vitamin E increasing prostate cancer) alongside beneficial ones [ ]. This highlights the importance of precise delivery and understanding their mechanisms of action, especially at high doses where some can exhibit pro-oxidant, anticancer effects [ , ].
The therapeutic efficacy of many phytochemicals against cancer stems from their ability to influence cellular processes and signaling pathways, with beneficial effects observed in both in vivo and in vitro investigations. Crucially, a growing body of evidence suggests that several nutraceuticals possess specific anti-cancer stem cell (anti-CSC) activity, making them attractive candidates for overcoming therapeutic resistance and preventing tumor recurrence [ ].Curcumin A polyphenolic compound derived from Curcuma longa, curcumin is well-known for its pleiotropic activity, modulating multiple molecular targets involved in tumorigenesis, including transcription factors (NF-κB, STAT3), growth factors, inflammatory mediators (TNF-α, IL-6), apoptotic regulators (Bcl-2 family), and key signaling pathways such as PI3K/Akt, MAPK, and Wnt/β-catenin [ ]. Specifically against CSCs, curcumin has been shown to significantly decrease the expression of CSC markers like ALDH, CD44, CD133, and CD166 [ ]. It inhibits STAT3 phosphorylation, suppresses the Wnt/β-catenin pathway by mechanisms such as down-regulating p300 protein, decreasing β-catenin/Tcf-Lef transcriptional activity, and inducing caspase-3-mediated cleavage of β-catenin [ ]. Curcumin also inhibits PI3K/AKT signaling, down-regulates Notch-1, and modulates various microRNAs (e.g., upregulating miR-15a, miR-34a, miR-181b, and inhibiting miR-21) that regulate CSC properties [ ]. A novel derivative, CDF, exhibits superior biological activity and better bioavailability, targeting the EZH2-miRNA regulatory circuit [ ].
Resveratrol (RSV) This phytoalexin, found in red grapes and berries, is a potent antioxidant with anti-inflammatory, pro-apoptotic, and antitumor properties [ ]. Against CSCs, RSV inhibits stem cell characteristics of pancreatic CSCs and induces apoptosis by activating caspases and inhibiting anti-apoptotic proteins like Bcl-2 and XIAP. It suppresses pluripotency-maintaining factors (Nanog, Sox-2, c-Myc, Oct-4) and the drug resistance gene ABCG2. Furthermore, RSV has been shown to inhibit Wnt signaling, contributing to its anti-CSC effects [ ].
Sulforaphane (SFN) Derived from broccoli, SFN exhibits significant chemopreventive and anticancer effects, with a particular focus on its anti-CSC properties [ ]. SFN inhibits the self-renewal capacity of pancreatic CSCs and induces apoptosis. It modulates key CSC signaling pathways, including NF-κB (impairing antiapoptotic signaling), Sonic Hedgehog (SHH) (decreasing components like Smo, Gli1, Gli2 in leukemia and lung cancer CSCs), and Wnt/β-catenin (down-regulating the pathway in breast cancer stem cells). SFN also inhibits epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition (EMT) proteins (e.g., β-catenin, vimentin, twist-1, ZEB1) and down-regulates Notch-1 and c-Rel. Its ability to potentiate the cytotoxicity of conventional anticancer drugs makes it a promising candidate for combination therapy [ ].
Genistein An isoflavone found in legumes, genistein shows inhibitory effects on various tumor cells and has anti-CSC activity [ ]. It inhibits cell growth, clonogenicity, cell migration and invasion, EMT phenotype, and the formation of pancreatospheres. Genistein reduces the expression of CSC surface markers like CD44 and EpCAM. Notably, it decreases breast cancer stem-like cell populations by down-regulating the Hedgehog-Gli1 signaling pathway. It also modulates microRNAs (e.g., miR-200b, miR-221, miR-222) and reduces cell viability and induces apoptosis in prostate cancer cells by down-regulating Notch-1, Akt, and FoxM1 [ ].
Epigallocatechin gallate (EGCG) The main bioactive component of catechins predominantly present in green tea, EGCG is well known for its wide spectrum of biological activities as an anti-oxidative, anti-inflammatory, and anti-tumor agent [ ]. EGCG modulates EGFR and Notch pathways, collectively inhibiting cancer growth. It inhibits Wnt signaling in a dose-dependent manner and acts as an ATP-competitive inhibitor of both PI3K and mTOR, inhibiting cell proliferation and Akt phosphorylation. EGCG also alters miRNA expression (e.g., down-regulation of miR-21 and miR-27) and directly antagonizes androgen action [ ] (Fig.  ).
 

## Nanocarrier-based strategies for targeted nutraceutical delivery to cancer stem cells
Despite their profound anti-CSC potential, the clinical translation of many nutraceuticals is significantly hindered by their inherent pharmacokinetic limitations, including poor aqueous solubility, rapid metabolism, low systemic bioavailability, and instability under physiological conditions [ ]. Nanocarrier-based strategies offer a robust solution to these challenges, effectively enhancing the stability, bioavailability, and targeted delivery of these natural compounds to cancer stem cells [ ]. By encapsulating nutraceuticals within nanoscale delivery systems, their therapeutic efficacy can be dramatically improved, allowing them to exert their anti-CSC mechanisms more effectively [ , ].
Active targeting of CSCs is achieved by functionalizing nanocarrier surfaces with specific ligands that bind to receptors overexpressed on CSCs. Key CSC biomarkers such as CD44, CD133, and ALDH1 serve as attractive targets for these ligand-functionalized nanocarriers. For instance, therapeutic agents, including small molecules and nanoparticles, can be functionalized with corresponding antibodies or other targeting ligands for enhanced binding to CSCs. A CD133 antibody-toxin conjugate has been developed for ovarian cancer treatment in mouse models, and gemcitabine-incorporating nanoparticles functionalized with a CD44 antibody have effectively targeted CD44-positive breast cancer CSCs, improving overall therapeutic efficacy [ ]. This precise targeting minimizes off-target effects and maximizes the therapeutic payload delivered to the resistant CSC population [ , ].
Preclinical studies have demonstrated compelling efficacy of nutraceutical-loaded nanocarriers for CSC targeting:Curcumin Nanocarriers Nanocarrier encapsulation of curcumin aims to overcome its poor bioavailability and enhance its therapeutic efficacy against CSCs by enabling targeted modulation of critical oncogenic pathways [ ]. The preclinical evidence for this approach can be categorized as follows:

In Vitro Evidence Studies using cancer cell lines have demonstrated that nano-formulated curcumin exerts potent anti-CSC effects through multiple mechanisms. For instance, solid lipid curcumin particles (SLCPs) induce stronger apoptosis in glioblastoma (GBM) cells by inhibiting the PI3K-Akt/mTOR pathway [ , – ], while other curcumin-based nanoparticles have been shown to modulate CSC properties by regulating key microRNAs, such as upregulating miR-15a and inhibiting miR-21 [ ].
Clinical Evidence and Cautious Framing It is important to note that while preclinical results are highly promising, clinical trials specifically evaluating CSC-targeted curcumin nanocarriers in human patients are still in early stages or lacking. Therefore, the therapeutic potential, while significant, remains to be validated in a clinical setting.

Resveratrol Nanocarriers To overcome the significant pharmacokinetic challenges of resveratrol (RSV), various nanocarriers, such as advanced hydrogel systems, have been developed to enhance its stability and enable targeted delivery [ ].

Preclinical Evidence In vivo studies using animal models of colorectal cancer and glioblastoma have demonstrated the potential of these platforms. RSV-loaded hydrogels were shown to suppress metastasis, reverse chemoresistance, and target CSCs by inhibiting key signaling pathways like Wnt/β-catenin and utilizing triggers such as reactive oxygen species (ROS) for drug activation [ , ].
Clinical Evidence Despite these promising preclinical results, clinical trials specifically investigating the efficacy of RSV-loaded nanocarriers against CSCs in human patients have not yet been reported. Further research is required to translate these findings to a clinical context.

Sulforaphane Nanocarriers Sulforaphane (SFN) possesses potent anti-CSC activity by modulating critical self-renewal pathways, and its encapsulation in nanocarriers is being explored to enhance its therapeutic delivery and enable combination therapies [ , , ].

In Vitro Evidence In cell culture models, SFN has been shown to potentiate the cytotoxic effects of conventional anticancer drugs against pancreatic and prostate CSCs [ ].
In Vivo Evidence These findings are supported by animal studies, where co-treatment with SFN and other chemotherapeutic agents abolished the tumor-initiating capacity of CSCs in nude mice, demonstrating a powerful synergistic effect [ ].
Clinical Evidence The clinical application of SFN-loaded nanocarriers for targeting CSCs remains an area for future investigation, as human trial data is not yet available.

Genistein Nanocarriers Genistein's anti-CSC activity is well-documented, and advanced nanocarriers are being designed to overcome its inherent limitations, such as poor solubility and low bioavailability.

In Vitro Evidence At the cellular level, free genistein has been shown to exert anti-CSC effects by down-regulating the Hedgehog-Gli1 signaling pathway in breast cancer stem-like cells [ ].
In Vivo Evidence To enhance delivery, biomimetic nanosystems like platelet membrane-cloaked genistein liposomes (PLTM-LPs@Gen) have been developed. In preclinical lung cancer models, these nanocarriers significantly enhanced antitumor efficacy and increased drug accumulation at the tumor site while showing no evident toxicity, leveraging the platelet membrane's immune-evasive properties [ ].
Clinical Evidence While these preclinical platforms show great promise for improving genistein delivery, clinical studies focused on CSC-targeted genistein nanocarriers are needed to validate their therapeutic efficacy in patients.

Epigallocatechin gallate (EGCG) Nanocarriers Nanoencapsulation is a key strategy to overcome the rapid degradation and low bioavailability of Epigallocatechin gallate (EGCG), thereby enhancing its stability and potential for targeted tumor delivery [ ].

In Vitro Evidence In cell-based studies, nano-formulations have demonstrated superior performance. For instance, folic acid-functionalized nanoparticles have been shown to increase EGCG uptake in cancer cells that overexpress folate receptors [ , ]. It has been suggested that EGCG-loaded nanoparticles can specifically target CSCs by reducing the self-renewal capacity and reversing stem-like traits in triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) stem cells [ ].
Clinical Evidence As with other nutraceutical nanocarriers, the translation of these promising in vitro findings into clinical applications for targeting CSCs has yet to be realized, and further research, including in vivo and human studies, is essential [ , ] (Table ).

 

The synergy between nutraceuticals and nanocarriers is evident. Nanocarriers are not just passive transporters; they are indispensable enabling technologies that transform nutraceuticals from promising but bio-limited compounds into clinically viable therapeutic agents [ , ]. This partnership allows for the effective delivery of compounds that possess inherent anti-CSC biological activity, paving the way for more potent and less toxic targeted therapies [ , ]. This combined approach unlocks the full therapeutic potential of nutraceuticals against CSCs, offering a powerful avenue for advanced cancer therapy [ , ] (Fig.  ). 

## Translational challenges and future directions
Despite the significant advancements and promising preclinical data, the translation of nanomedicine and nutraceutical-based nanocarriers for targeted cancer stem cell delivery into widespread clinical practice faces substantial challenges [ , ].
One primary concern revolves around toxicity and biocompatibility. While nanocarriers aim to reduce systemic toxicity, some nanomaterials, particularly metallic nanoparticles like gold and silver, may accumulate in the body and potentially cause long-term toxicity or adverse immune reactions [ ]. There are limited studies on the long-term impacts of nanomaterials on human health, especially concerning chronic toxicity and biodegradability [ ]. Nanoparticles can interact unpredictably with biological systems, for instance, by forming a "protein corona" with bloodstream proteins, which can alter their behavior, targeting ability, and potentially trigger hypersensitivity reactions by activating the complement system [ ]. This protein corona can also mask targeting ligands, reducing efficiency and leading to premature clearance [ ].
Large-scale production presents another formidable hurdle. Scaling up nanoparticle production from laboratory to commercial levels while ensuring consistent quality and safety is a significant challenge guided by Chemistry, Manufacturing, and Controls (CMC) principles. Adhering to a Quality by Design (QbD) approach, manufacturers must strictly control critical quality attributes (CQAs) to ensure batch-to-batch reproducibility. These include maintaining a consistent particle size (e.g., typically below 200 nm), a narrow polydispersity index (PDI < 0.2), stable surface charge, and high drug encapsulation efficiency. Furthermore, the final sterile product must meet stringent safety thresholds, such as acceptable residual solvent limits and low levels of pyrogens, with endotoxin levels typically required to be below 5 EU/kg, making the entire process complex and costly [ , , ].
Regulatory and approval hurdles are complex due to the novel materials and mechanisms involved. Navigating this landscape requires extensive safety, toxicity, and efficacy data that adheres to guidances from regulatory bodies like the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA), European Medicines Agency (EMA), and the International Council for Harmonisation (ICH, e.g., ICH Q8 for pharmaceutical development). The successful clinical translation of pioneering nanomedicines like Doxil® (liposomal doxorubicin) and Abraxane® (nab-paclitaxel) provides a regulatory precedent, yet each new platform faces intense scrutiny. The lack of standardized test methods for assessing aspects like immunogenicity and long-term biodistribution further complicates the path to approval, creating a challenging and often lengthy process [ , , , ]. Furthermore, significant barriers related to securing intellectual property (IP) for novel formulations and navigating the specific requirements for clinical trial registration for nutraceutical-based nanomedicines add further layers of complexity.
To overcome these formidable barriers, the field is witnessing a convergence of advanced engineering and biological understanding, paving the way for innovative future directions that map directly to key translational bottlenecks:Bottleneck: Poor In Vivo Stability and Off-Target Effects A primary challenge is the rapid clearance of nanocarriers by the immune system and their lack of specificity for CSC niches. Solution: Biomimetic and Cell-Mediated Delivery. To address this, biomimetic strategies that cloak nanoparticles with cell membranes (e.g., from red blood cells or cancer cells) are being employed. This camouflage allows nanoparticles to evade immune detection, prolonging their circulation time and reducing opsonization [ , ]. Furthermore, "cellular hitchhiking"—using naturally tumor-homing cells like mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) as delivery vehicles—leverages their innate tropism to carry therapeutic payloads deep into the tumor microenvironment, directly to the sites of recurrence [ – ].
Bottleneck: Manufacturing Reproducibility and Inaccurate Preclinical Models The translation of nanomedicines is hampered by difficulties in producing uniform nanoparticles at scale and the failure of standard in vitro assays to predict clinical outcomes. Solution: Microfluidics and Tumor-on-Chip Models. Microfluidic technologies solve the manufacturing problem by enabling the high-throughput, reproducible synthesis of nanoparticles with precise control over size, composition, and drug loading, which is crucial for regulatory approval [ ]. To improve preclinical testing, tumor-on-chip models are being developed. These microfluidic platforms recapitulate the 3D architecture and microenvironment of human tumors, allowing for more accurate and predictive screening of nanocarrier efficacy and toxicity before moving to costly animal trials [ , ].
Bottleneck: Design Complexity and Patient Heterogeneity Designing an optimal nanocarrier is a multifactorial problem, and patient-to-patient variability makes a one-size-fits-all approach ineffective. Solution: Artificial Intelligence (AI) and Machine Learning (ML)( – ). For instance, machine learning models (e.g., XGBoost, Graph Convolutional Networks, and Deep Neural Networks) are already being used to optimize lipid nanoparticle (LNP) formulations for mRNA vaccines, a process directly analogous to designing nanocarriers for nutraceuticals [ , ]. Crucially, AI can analyze patient-specific data to predict individual responses to a given nanomedicine, paving the way for personalized treatment regimens where formulations and dosing are tailored to each patient's unique tumor biology [ , , – ].

However, the predictive power of these models is highly dependent on the quality of training data. Overcoming challenges such as data bias and the lack of large, standardized datasets will be critical for the robust and equitable clinical implementation of AI in nanomedicine.
These emerging technologies represent a critical shift towards overcoming current barriers in nanomedicine [ , ]. By combining advanced engineering solutions with a deeper understanding of tumor biology, they promise to enhance reproducibility, improve screening, enable precision synthesis, and ultimately bridge the gap from bench to bedside, ushering in an era of highly effective and personalized cancer therapies targeting the elusive cancer stem cells [ , ] (Fig.  ). 

## Forging a clinical pathway for Anti-CSC nutraceutical nanocarriers
Translating an anti-CSC nanomedicine from the laboratory to the clinic requires a clear strategy with well-defined, translatable endpoints. The preclinical evaluation of anti-CSC activity must move beyond simple viability assays. Efficacy should first be established in vitro using functional assays like the sphere-forming assay to quantify effects on self-renewal. These findings must then be validated in in vivo models using limiting dilution transplantation assays, which measure the frequency of tumor-initiating cells (TICs)—the functional equivalent of CSCs [ , ]. The ultimate goal is to connect these preclinical readouts to clinically meaningful endpoints, such as the monitoring of CSC markers in circulating tumor cells (CTCs), achieving minimal residual disease (MRD) negativity, and, most importantly, demonstrating a significant improvement in patient relapse rates and progression-free survival [ , ].
A hypothetical first-in-human roadmap for a candidate, such as a CD44-targeted nanocarrier delivering curcumin, could serve as a template. A suitable initial indication might be triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC), a disease known for its reliance on a CD44-positive CSC population [ , ]. The nanocarrier would likely be evaluated as a combination partner with standard-of-care chemotherapy (e.g., paclitaxel) to simultaneously target the bulk tumor and the resistant CSCs. Following preclinical toxicology studies, a Phase I dose-escalation trial would establish the maximum tolerated dose [ ]. Crucially, such a trial must include robust pharmacodynamic (PD) biomarkers to prove target engagement. This would involve analyzing patient tumor biopsies or liquid biopsies to measure a reduction in the CD44high/ALDH + CSC fraction via flow cytometry and to confirm downregulation of target pathways, such as NF-κB or Wnt, using methods like RNA sequencing or immunohistochemistry [ , ]. This biomarker-driven approach is essential for validating the mechanism of action and guiding the successful clinical development of anti-CSC therapies.

## Conclusions
Cancer stem cells (CSCs) represent the root of therapeutic resistance and tumor relapse, demanding a shift towards highly specific and multi-modal therapeutic strategies. This review has synthesized the current evidence demonstrating the powerful synergy between nanomedicine and nutraceuticals as a leading strategy to target CSCs. Nanocarriers are indispensable for overcoming the inherent pharmacokinetic limitations of potent anti-CSC nutraceuticals—such as curcumin, resveratrol, and EGCG—by enhancing their stability, bioavailability, and precision delivery to CSC niches. While preclinical evidence is compelling, the path to clinical translation is fraught with challenges, including manufacturing scalability, regulatory complexities, and the immense biological barriers of the tumor microenvironment.
The future of targeted CSC therapy at the "STEM Frontier" depends on overcoming these hurdles through technological innovation. Emerging platforms like biomimetic delivery systems, microfluidics, tumor-on-chip models, and artificial intelligence are poised to solve critical bottlenecks in manufacturing, preclinical evaluation, and personalized treatment design. Ultimately, success will be driven by a paradigm of multidisciplinary collaboration—uniting nanotechnologists, cancer biologists, clinicians, and data scientists—to thoughtfully design and integrate these advanced therapies into rigorous clinical trials that can finally deliver on the promise of eradicating cancer at its root.
