Despite advancements in treatments such as surgery, radiotherapy, and chemotherapy, the overall survival rate remains unsatisfactory, with the 5-year survival rate stagnating at 15–25%. Therefore, there is an urgent need to identify biomarkers that effectively predict patient prognosis to improve clinical management and therapeutic strategies. In-depth exploration of the molecular mechanisms underlying ESCC, along with achieving early diagnosis and effective targeted therapy, is of paramount importance.
Currently, research on ESCC is gradually increasing; however, systematic studies investigating its biological mechanisms remain insufficient. The existing literature indicates that metabolic reprogramming and immune evasion in tumor cells are key drivers of ESCC progression. Coenzyme Q10 (COQ10) is a bioquinone compound known for its antioxidant properties, which can reduce the production of free radicals and reactive oxygen species in damaged cells. Additionally, it plays a crucial role in regulating cellular energy metabolism and apoptosis through redox signaling. COQ10 influences the expression of numerous metabolism-related genes, as well as cellular transport, transcriptional regulation, and cell signaling transduction, potentially contributing to tumorigenesis and development. Recently, mitochondrial chaperone proteins, including COQ10B, have emerged as crucial regulators in tumor metabolic reprogramming and cancer progression. While COQ10B is known to be closely associated with cellular energy metabolism and redox homeostasis, , its specific biological role in cancer biology remains largely underexplored. Particularly in ESCC, a prominent knowledge gap exists: while metabolic reprogramming and immune evasion are recognized as key drivers of ESCC malignancy, it remains completely unknown whether COQ10B serves as a mechanistic link orchestrating these two critical processes. Therefore, research focusing on the specific role of COQ10B and its potential signaling axis in the ESCC microenvironment remains an urgent unmet need.
This study comprehensively investigated the relationship between COQ10B expression and the clinical characteristics, metabolic status, and immune cell infiltration in patients with ESCC. It also explored potential mechanisms by integrating survival analysis, pathway enrichment analysis, single-cell sequencing, and in vivo/in vitro experiments. This multidimensional research approach facilitated a deeper understanding of the biological functions and underlying mechanisms of COQ10B in ESCC, providing novel insights and directions to improve ESCC patients’ prognosis. The findings of this study yielded potential biomarkers and targeted intervention strategies for the early diagnosis and treatment of esophageal cancer, thereby laying the foundation for the development of more effective clinical treatment protocols in the future.

## Materials and Methods

## Public Database
Clinical survival data and RNA-Seq data, including TNM staging and DNA methylation data, were obtained from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) and Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) databases. TIMER was utilized to explore, visualize, and analyze multidimensional cancer genomic data. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified in the high and low COQ10B groups, respectively, using a screening threshold of P < 0.05 and |log2(FC)| > 1. Genes that met these criteria were classified as DEGs. Survival analysis was conducted employing both univariate and multivariate analyses. The nomogram of risk scores and associated clinical information was described using the survival (version 3.2) and rms (version 6.3) packages.

## Analysis of Tumor Immune Infiltration and Immune Checkpoints
Gene Expression Profiling Interactive Analysis (GEPIA) was utilized to conduct a comprehensive analysis of the interactions between tumors and immune molecules. Spearman correlation analysis was employed to visualize the associations between COQ10B and various gene markers of tumor-infiltrating immune cells. Correlations were deemed significant based on the criteria (p<0.05, Pearson’s r>0.2). Count data were converted to Transcripts Per Million (TPM), and the TCGA-UM data were normalized using the log2(TPM + 1) transformation. Multiple methods, including QUANTISEQ and MCP-counter, were applied to assess the reliability of the results. The relative proportions of 22 immune cell types, including SIGLEC15, TIGIT, CD274, HAVCR2, PDCD1, CTLA4, LAG3, and PDCD1LG2, were calculated.

## Single-Cell Sequencing and Data Processing
We previously analyzed published datasets and followed recommended best practices to reanalyze them. Single-cell sequencing data were filtered using the R software Seurat package (version 4.1.1), excluding cells that did not meet data quality standards. Gene expression quantiles were normalized using “Harmony version 0.1.0”. Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) was employed with the number of highly variable genes set to 3000. Cells were clustered using the k-nearest neighbors (KNN) method at a resolution of 1.0 and annotated using cell markers obtained from prior literature.

## Cell Culture and COQ10B Overexpression Lentiviral Transfection
The esophageal squamous cell carcinoma (ESCC) cell line TE-1 (Shanghai Genechem Co., Ltd., China) was cultured in RPMI1640 medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), 100 U/mL penicillin, and 100 μg/mL streptomycin, and maintained in a 37°C incubator with 5% CO2. The AKR mouse esophageal squamous cell carcinoma cell line (ATCC, China) was cultured in DMEM medium at 37°C under 95% air and 5% CO2. TE-1 and AKR cells in logarithmic growth phase were seeded at a density of 2×105 cells per well in 6-well plates. When cell confluence reached approximately 60%, lentivirus overexpressing COQ10B and the corresponding negative control were added to the cells at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 20. After 12 hours of infection, the medium was replaced with RPMI1640 and DMEM containing 10% fetal bovine serum and double antibiotics, respectively. The cells were then cultured until they reached 70–80% confluence. The cells were collected for subsequent experiments, and Western blotting was performed to assess the efficiency of COQ10B overexpression.

## Western Blotting
Protein samples from each group of cells were extracted using RIPA buffer. After quantifying protein concentration with the BCA kit, 20 μg of protein from each group was separated by SDS-PAGE. The protein bands were subsequently transferred onto membranes, which were blocked with a protein-free rapid blocking buffer at room temperature for one hour. Primary antibodies against COQ10B and β-actin were added and incubated overnight at 4°C. Following washing, secondary antibodies were added and incubated at room temperature for one hour, followed by exposure and development. Quantitative analysis was performed using ImageJ software (version 1.53k, NIH, USA). Tumor tissues from each group of mice were collected for total protein extraction, and the protein expression levels of COQ10B, PD-L1, PI3K, p-PI3K, AKT, p-AKT, and HIF-1A were detected and analyzed using the aforementioned methods.

## Plate Cloning Assay
Cells (8 × 102 cells/well) were seeded into 6-well plates and cultured aseptically for approximately two weeks. When macroscopically visible clones appeared on the culture plates, the cultivation was terminated. During this period, the medium was replaced every three days. After washing, each well was fixed with 1 mL of paraformaldehyde for 15 minutes and stained with crystal violet for 20 minutes. After drying, photographs were taken, and the number of cell clones was counted under an optical microscope.

## EDU
Cells from each experimental group were seeded into 24-well plates at a density of 3 × 104 cells per well, with three replicate wells for each group. The following day, the EDU working solution was prepared and incubated with the cells at a low concentration in a cell culture incubator for 4 to 6 hours. After removing the medium, 500 μL of 4% paraformaldehyde was added to each well, and the cells were fixed at room temperature for 15 minutes. Following fixation, the cells were washed three times, and 1 mL of 0.3% Triton X-100 per well was added for permeabilization at room temperature for 15 minutes. After removing the permeabilization solution and washing three times, 200 μL of the pre-prepared Click reaction solution was added to each well and incubated at room temperature in the dark for 30 minutes. Subsequently, 500 μL of 1×Hoechst 33342 solution was added to each well and incubated at room temperature, protected from light, for an additional 30 minutes. Finally, the cells were washed three times with washing buffer and photographed under a fluorescence microscope for analysis.

## Wound Healing Experiment
The cell density of each experimental group was adjusted to 3 × 105 cells/mL. One mL of the cell suspension was seeded into a 6-well cell culture plate, ensuring complete cell adhesion to achieve 70–80% confluency. The culture medium was aspirated, and the cells were washed 1–2 times with PBS. A 200 μL pipette tip was utilized to create a perpendicular “cross-shaped” scratch on the bottom of each well, and detached cells were gently washed away with PBS. Serum-free medium was added, and culturing was continued in the incubator. The cells were observed and photographed under a microscope at 0 and 24 hours, and the migration rate for each group was subsequently calculated.

## Transwell (with Matrigel Matrix) Invasion Assay
Each Transwell chamber was coated with 50 μL of Matrigel matrix overnight. The following day, cells from each experimental group in the logarithmic growth phase were resuspended in serum-free medium at a density of 5×104 cells per well. Subsequently, 200 μL of the cell suspension was added to the upper chamber, while 500 μL of complete medium was added to the lower chamber. After 24 hours of incubation, the cells were fixed with paraformaldehyde, stained with crystal violet, and air-dried. Microscopic images were captured, and the number of migrated cells was quantified and recorded using ImageJ (version 1.53k, NIH, USA).

## Subcutaneous Tumor Formation in Mice and Group Intervention
AKR-negative control cells in the logarithmic growth phase and stable overexpression cell lines were collected, washed three times with PBS, and resuspended in serum-free medium at a concentration of 2×106 cells per 200 μL. During transportation, the cells were maintained in an ice box.Thirty-six female C57BL/6 mice (SPF grade), aged 5–6 weeks, were selected for the study and randomly assigned to three groups (n=12 per group). This sample size was determined empirically based on standard preclinical xenograft protocols and comparable published literature in ESCC studies, which ensures sufficient observational power while accounting for potential inter-individual biological variance. Different cell suspensions, each containing 2×106 cells per mouse, were prepared in 200 μL PBS mixed with a 1:1 volume ratio of Matrigel (100 μL Matrigel:100 μL PBS) and subcutaneously injected into the right anterior axilla of each mouse. In the overexpression + LY294002 group (12 mice), intraperitoneal injections of LY294002 (50 mg/kg) and saline were administered three times per week for four weeks. Body weight was measured every five days, and tumor length (L) and width (W) were measured once with a vernier caliper. Tumor volume was calculated using the formula V = (L × W2)/2. The Experimental Animal Welfare and Ethics Committee approved all animal studies.
Following the experiment, euthanize the mice via cervical dislocation. Carefully dissect the tumors using surgical scissors, then rinse them with saline and allow them to dry. Weigh the tumors using a precision balance, measure their dimensions with a ruler, and capture photographs. Each tumor should be divided into three sections: one section must be immediately immersed in a 10% formaldehyde solution for preservation, another section should be freshly preserved at 4°C for flow cytometry analysis, and the final section should be flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80°C.

## Flow Cytometry Analysis of Tumor-Associated Macrophages
The mouse tumor tissue was cut into approximately 0.5 to 1 mm3 particles within an EP tube. One mL of HBSS buffer was added, and the mixture was transferred to a 15 mL centrifuge tube. The tumor particles were resuspended with 8 mL of HBSS buffer, followed by the incorporation of 1 mL of 10× mixed digestive enzymes, and mixed thoroughly. The mixture was digested on a shaker at 80 rpm and 37°C for 1 hour. The tissue blocks were pipetted every 30 minutes until complete digestion was achieved. The mixture was filtered through a 100 μm sieve, washed with HBSS buffer, and centrifuged. The cell pellet was resuspended in HBSS to a concentration of 1×107/mL. One hundred μL of the single-cell suspension was transferred to a new tube and incubated with 5 μL each of CD3 (FITC), F4/80 (PE), CD8 (PerCP/Cyanine5.5), CD4 (APC-Cy7), and CD45 (APC) antibodies in the dark at 2–8°C for 30 minutes. After washing, the cells were fixed with IC fixation buffer for 60 minutes, permeabilized with 1X permeabilization buffer, and incubated with CD206 (PE-Cy7) antibody for 30 minutes. After a final wash, the stained cells were resuspended in flow cytometry staining buffer and analyzed via instrument. For flow cytometry data analysis, the general gating strategy was as follows: cells were first gated based on forward and side scatter FSC–Avs.SSC–A to exclude debris. Leukocytes were identified as CD45+. Within the leukocyte population, T cell subsets were gated as CD3+CD4+ or CD3+CD8+. Macrophages were identified as F4/80+ and further subtyped as M2 macrophages (CD206+).

## Statistical Methods
All in vitro experiments were performed in at least three independent biological replicates. The data were analyzed using R software (version 4.1.1), GraphPad Prism 10.0, and SPSS 19.0. Normally distributed measurement data are presented as mean ± standard deviation. One-way ANOVA was employed for comparisons among multiple groups, followed by the LSD test (for homogeneity of variance) or the Dunnett-T3 test (for heterogeneity of variance) for pairwise comparisons. Non-normally distributed measurement data are expressed as M (P25–P75), with the Kruskal–Wallis H-test used for comparisons among multiple groups, followed by the Kruskal–Wallis one-way ANOVA (k-sample) for multiple pairwise comparisons. Statistical significance is denoted by * for p < 0.05, ** for p < 0.01, and *** for p < 0.001.

## Results

## Elevated COQ10B Expression Exhibits a Potential Association with Poorer Survival Outcomes in Esophageal Squamous Cell Carcinoma Patients
To investigate the role of COQ10B in ESCC, we evaluated the relationship between COQ10B expression and various clinical characteristics of ESCC. Survival analysis indicated that elevated COQ10B expression showed a modest association with poor prognosis in ESCC (p < 0.05, ). Furthermore, COQ10B expression levels exhibited specific pairwise variations associated with promoter methylation status ( ). However, regarding histological grades and tumor stages ( and ), while statistical differences were observed during specific discrete clinical transitions (such as from Stage I to Stage II, and Stage II to Stage III), substantial overlap was evident among individual clinical samples. Due to this extensive distribution, no continuous, stepwise linear trend could be established across the entire cohort. Therefore, the association between COQ10B and clinical progression should be interpreted with caution, reflecting potential inter-patient heterogeneity rather than acting as an absolute linear grading marker. 

## The COQ10B Pathway is Enriched in the PI3K/AKT and HIF1 Signaling Pathways, and is Associated with Cancer Stem Cell Characteristics
To investigate the role of COQ10B in the development and progression of ESCC, we employed the single-sample Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (ssGSEA) algorithm to calculate pathway enrichment scores for each ESCC sample. We analyzed the correlation between COQ10B and these pathway scores. The results demonstrated a significant association between COQ10B and the PI3K/AKT pathway as well as the HIF1 pathway (p < 0.05, and ). Furthermore, co-expression analysis revealed positive correlations between COQ10B and several cancer stem cell genes, including CD44, ABCG2, ALCAM, EZH2, and KDM5B (p < 0.05) ( ). These data suggest that COQ10B may play a crucial role in the malignant progression of tumors, including proliferation, drug resistance, and recurrence, by regulating cancer stem cell properties. 

## The Relationship Between COQ10B and Infiltrating Stromal/Immune Cells
To further explore the underlying mechanisms, we investigated the relationship between COQ10B and tumor-infiltrating immune cells. The heatmap visually demonstrated the heterogeneity of the immune microenvironment between the two groups ( ). The COQ10BHigh group exhibited weak but statistically relevant associations with certain pro-tumor or immunosuppressive immune cells, such as M0 macrophages and Tregs. In contrast, the COQ10BLow group showed a relatively higher infiltration of anti-tumor immune cells, such as CD8+ T cells. Notably, as indicated by the modest effect sizes (Pearson r < 0.2) and some non-significant Mantel p-values, the overall shift in immune cell populations, including the minimal changes in CD8+ T cells, was subtle rather than representing a massive immune remodeling ( ). Furthermore, heterogeneous expression of immune checkpoint molecules was observed between the two groups ( ). The COQ10BHigh group demonstrated slightly elevated expression of multiple key immune checkpoints (eg., CTLA4, HAVCR2), suggesting a potential, albeit modest, link to immune evasion pathways. Collectively, these findings indicate that while COQ10B does not globally remodel the immune microenvironment, it may incrementally contribute to an immunosuppressive state. 

## Cellular Distribution and Localization of COQ10B in Single-Cell Sequencing Analysis of ESCC
To elucidate the molecular physiological significance of COQ10B in ESCC, we analyzed single-cell sequencing data ( ). The t-distributed stochastic neighbor embedding (t-SNE) and uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) plots demonstrated the integration of ESCC patient samples, revealing the distribution of COQ10B among fibroblasts and immune cells, including macrophages, B cells, and T cells ( ). Our findings indicate that COQ10B is widely distributed across multiple cell types within the ESCC microenvironment. As demonstrated by the quantitative distribution in the heatmap ( ), the overall expression levels are relatively low and uniform across diverse populations, including both immune cells (such as T cells and mast cells) and stromal cells (such as fibroblasts and pericytes). Without a single dominant cell population, these data suggest that COQ10B plays a broad biological role across various cell types in the tumor microenvironment. 

## Association Between COQ10B Gene and Tumor Glycolytic Metabolism
The potential link between COQ10B expression and tumor glycolytic metabolism was evaluated through bioinformatic profiling. The overall glycolysis enrichment score was significantly elevated in the COQ10BHigh cohort compared to the COQ10BLow cohort, indicating that high COQ10B expression is closely associated with an upregulated glycolytic gene signature ( ). Consistent with this finding, the expression levels and relative proportions of key rate-limiting glycolytic transcripts—including LDHA, HK2, and PKM2—were significantly greater in the COQ10BHigh group. These transcriptomic profiles demonstrate a robust positive association between COQ10B and metabolic reprogramming markers , , ( ). Furthermore, Pearson correlation analysis revealed a moderate, yet statistically significant, positive correlation (r = 0.54) between COQ10B expression and the glycolysis score ( ). Collectively, while these multi-omics data strongly support a positive transcriptomic association with glycolysis, definitive functional pathway validation is warranted to conclude whether COQ10B actively promotes metabolic reprogramming in ESCC cells. 

## Effects of COQ10B on Proliferation, Migration, and Invasion of Esophageal Squamous Cell Carcinoma
We transfected TE-1 cells with lentivirus expressing COQ10B to investigate its effect on the malignant biological behavior of esophageal squamous cell carcinoma. Western blotting results indicated that the COQ10B protein expression level in the oe-COQ10B group was significantly higher than that in both the oe-NC and control groups (p < 0.05). No significant difference in COQ10B protein expression was observed between the oe-NC and control groups ( ). This finding confirms the successful lentiviral transfection for COQ10B overexpression, demonstrating that the empty lentivirus did not significantly affect COQ10B expression. Through EdU and cell cloning assays, we observed that COQ10B-overexpressing cells exhibited enhanced proliferative capacity ( and ). Additionally, wound healing assays revealed that COQ10B overexpression significantly increased the migratory ability of TE-1 cells ( ). Transwell chamber assays (with Matrigel) further corroborated that COQ10B overexpression enhanced the invasive capability of TE-1 cells ( ). 

## In vivo Validation of the Effect of COQ10B Overexpression on Tumor Growth and the Immune Microenvironment in Esophageal Squamous Cell Carcinoma
Based on in vitro data, overexpression of COQ10B has been shown to enhance the proliferation, migration, and invasion of ESCC cells. To further validate the effects of COQ10B overexpression on tumor growth in vivo and its regulatory influence on the immune microenvironment of ESCC, this study conducted animal experiments. The results from Western blotting indicated that in mouse esophageal cancer cells (AKR), the expression level of COQ10B protein in the oe-COQ10B group was significantly higher than that in both the oe-NC group and the control group (p < 0.05). No significant differences were observed between the oe-NC group and the control group ( and ). Tumor volume and mass measurements revealed that the oe-COQ10B group exhibited significantly accelerated tumor growth compared to the control group. In contrast, the oe-COQ10B + LY294002 group demonstrated markedly slower tumor growth than the oe-COQ10B group ( ). Additionally, flow cytometry and Western blot analyses showed that COQ10B overexpression decreased CD4+ T cells and increased M2-type macrophages, a process which was closely associated with the activation of the PI3K/AKT/HIF-1A signaling pathway. These immunosuppressive and oncogenic effects were partially reversed by the PI3K inhibitor LY294002 ( ). 

## Discussion
Esophageal squamous cell carcinoma (ESCC) ranks as the sixth most lethal malignancy globally. Its treatment faces multiple challenges, including key biological features such as chemotherapy resistance, immune evasion, and metabolic reprogramming. In recent years, immune checkpoint inhibitors combined with chemotherapy have shown survival benefits in advanced ESCC, , , yet patient response rates remain limited due to tumor microenvironment heterogeneity. , Additionally, further exploration is warranted for therapeutic targets driven by metabolic abnormalities. COQ10B is a crucial gene in the coenzyme Q10 (CoQ10) biosynthesis pathway, and its dysfunction is associated with various mitochondrial dysfunction-related diseases; however, its mechanistic role in ESCC remains unclear. , This study aims to investigate the function of COQ10B and develop novel therapeutic strategies for ESCC.
Existing studies have demonstrated that COQ10B is closely associated with cellular metabolism, proliferation, and stemness. A reduction in COQ10B levels can worsen mitochondrial respiratory chain dysfunction, resulting in the release of cytochrome c and the activation of caspase-3, which subsequently triggers apoptosis. Furthermore, as a molecular chaperone for coenzyme Q10 (COQ10), COQ10B enhances the localization and functional activity of COQ10 on mitochondrial membranes. COQ10 effectively inhibits free radical chain reactions by preventing lipid peroxidation initiation, thereby safeguarding the phospholipid structure of cell membranes from oxidative damage.
Our current study reveals that high COQ10B expression is potentially associated with poor prognosis in patients with ESCC. However, it should be noted that the survival separation observed in our cohorts was modest. Furthermore, the reliability of late survival differences is limited by a sharp drop in the number-at-risk after approximately three years. Therefore, COQ10B should be cautiously interpreted as a potential, rather than a definitive, prognostic indicator. Furthermore, COQ10B expression levels show positive correlations with tumor histological grade and clinical stage, suggesting its potential role in the occurrence and progression of ESCC, as well as its value as a prognostic marker and a potential therapeutic target. To further investigate the biological functions of COQ10B, we analyzed its related signaling pathways. The results indicate that COQ10B may exert its pro-tumorigenic effects by activating the PI3K/AKT/HIF-1A signaling axis. This pathway is closely associated with resistance to radiotherapy and chemotherapy, as well as immune evasion, in ESCC. Specifically, aberrant activation of the PI3K/AKT pathway can upregulate HIF-1A expression, thereby driving metabolic reprogramming processes, including enhanced glycolysis (the Warburg effect), which provides energy and biosynthetic precursors for tumor cells. , Notably, the HIF-1A-mediated formation of an immunosuppressive microenvironment may represent a key mechanism through which COQ10B promotes immune escape. This process involves multiple factors, including promoting M2 macrophage polarization and suppressing T cell infiltration. Moreover, our study finds that COQ10B exhibits a positive correlation with the expression of multiple cancer stem cell characteristic-related genes (such as CD44 and ABCG2), providing new evidence to elucidate its role in cancer progression. These findings not only deepen our understanding of COQ10B’s function in ESCC development but also offer a theoretical foundation for developing novel therapeutic strategies targeting this signaling pathway. As a member of the coenzyme family, COQ10B may contribute to malignant tumor phenotypes by influencing energy metabolic pathways. This study reveals a critical link between COQ10B and tumor cell metabolism. Elevated expression of COQ10B enhances glycolytic metabolism in ESCC. , , This metabolic reprogramming not only confers survival advantages to tumor cells but may also be linked to drug resistance, underscoring the significant role of COQ10B in tumor metabolic adaptation. In summary, we systematically demonstrated, using public databases, that COQ10B plays a notable cancer-promoting role in ESCC, with high expression correlating with poor prognosis, and proposed its pivotal mechanism in driving tumor progression through the PI3K/AKT/HIF-1A signaling axis.
To validate the results of our bioinformatics analysis, we systematically investigated the effects of COQ10B overexpression on the malignant biological behavior and immune microenvironment of ESCC through both in vitro and in vivo experiments. By constructing a lentivirus that overexpresses COQ10B and transfecting TE-1 cells, our functional analyses revealed that, compared to the negative control group (oe-NC), ESCC cells overexpressing COQ10B (oe-COQ10B) exhibited significantly enhanced capabilities in proliferation, migration, and invasion (p < 0.01). These findings indicate that COQ10B promotes the malignant biological behavior of ESCC cells.A previous study [Yu Wei et al, 2022 (retracted)] exploring the knockdown of COQ10B in esophageal cancer has been retracted. The retraction of this literature underscores the critical necessity for rigorous and reproducible functional investigations to correct the scientific record. As a foundational step toward a comprehensive re-evaluation, our current research specifically established a robust gain-of-function (overexpression) model to uncover the oncogenic potential of COQ10B and its novel impact on the tumor immune microenvironment. Moreover, our comprehensive analysis reveals for the first time that COQ10B overexpression not only enhances tumor growth but also significantly alters the intricate mechanisms of the tumor immune microenvironment, thereby offering new insights into the role of COQ10B in esophageal tumorigenesis. In our in vivo experiments, the allograft tumor model with COQ10B overexpression demonstrated a 48% increase in tumor volume and a 52% increase in tumor weight (p < 0.05), further confirming that COQ10B facilitates ESCC progression. We further investigated the regulatory effect of COQ10B on the PI3K/AKT/HIF-1A axis using a mouse homograft tumor model. The results demonstrated that the oe-COQ10B group exhibited significantly increased phosphorylation of PI3K and AKT, along with markedly increased HIF-1A protein expression (p < 0.05). This process was inhibited by the PI3K inhibitor LY294002, confirming that COQ10B may promote ESCC tumor progression by activating the PI3K/AKT/HIF-1A axis.
Moreover, the oe-COQ10B group exhibited significantly increased PD-L1 protein expression in tumor tissues, elevated M2-type macrophages, and reduced CD4+ T cells, effects that could be inhibited by LY294002. Our study demonstrates that COQ10B overexpression may regulate macrophage polarization and T cell exhaustion through the activation of the PI3K/AKT/HIF-1A axis. This finding aligns with previous research indicating that aberrant activation of the PI3K/AKT/HIF-1A signaling axis plays a pivotal role in the tumor microenvironment and chronic inflammatory diseases, significantly impacting the efficacy of adaptive immune responses by modulating macrophage polarization and antigen-presenting functions. Studies have shown that excessive activation of the PI3K/AKT pathway can induce macrophage polarization toward the M2 phenotype. This phenotypic switch is accompanied by the suppression of pro-inflammatory factors (eg., TNF-α, IL-6) and the increased secretion of anti-inflammatory factors (eg., IL-10, TGF-β), thereby creating an immunosuppressive microenvironment. 
Additionally, activation of this pathway can induce apoptosis of CD4+ T cells by modulating IL-2 expression. HIF-1A, as a key downstream transcription factor of PI3K/AKT, can directly bind to hypoxia response elements (HREs) in the promoter region of MHC class II molecules to suppress their transcriptional activity while simultaneously upregulating the expression of immune checkpoint molecules such as PD-L1, thereby establishing a dual immune escape mechanism. In summary, our experimental results, supported by relevant literature, indicate that COQ10B overexpression induces the formation of an immunosuppressive microenvironment. This occurs through the activation of the PI3K/AKT/HIF-1A signaling axis, which subsequently leads to increased PD-L1 protein expression within the tumor microenvironment, elevated infiltration of M2-type macrophages, and a reduction in CD4+ T cell populations.
While our single-cell analysis indicated that COQ10B is widely distributed across various cells within the TME, our functional experiments specifically focused on its tumor-intrinsic role. We hypothesize that the aberrant upregulation of COQ10B within epithelial tumor cells acts as a primary metabolic initiator, which secondarily orchestrates the remodeling of the extrinsic immune microenvironment. As a mitochondrial chaperone involved in redox homeostasis, the exact mechanism by which tumor-intrinsic COQ10B activates the PI3K/AKT cascade warrants further investigation. Since no direct physical interaction has been established, we postulate that this activation is mediated indirectly. Altered mitochondrial function may lead to the accumulation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) or the cytoplasmic leakage of oncometabolites (such as lactate), which are well-documented to trigger PI3K/AKT signaling and stabilize HIF-1A. Furthermore, this HIF-1A-mediated immunosuppressive remodeling underscores the complex interactions within the TME, which can significantly impact the efficacy of immunotherapy. The TME functions as a dynamic network where various components—including specific immune cells, cytokines, and tumor-associated factors—intricately modulate immune responses. Recent studies have highlighted that understanding these interactions, particularly how cytokines and the TME influence therapeutic outcomes, is crucial for developing effective cancer treatments. , Therefore, targeting tumor-intrinsic COQ10B-mediated metabolic and immune remodeling may offer a synergistic approach to enhance current immunotherapies.
However, several limitations in the present study should be acknowledged. First, our functional assays relied exclusively on the gain-of-function (overexpression) model. Given that COQ10B is already highly expressed in ESCC, solely overexpressing it limits the translational impact of our findings. Future studies employing rigorously designed and reproducible loss-of-function models (such as CRISPR-mediated knockout or shRNA knockdown) are urgently needed to definitively validate COQ10B as a viable therapeutic target. Second, without specific downstream rescue experiments (such as HIF-1A knockdown), our in vivo data demonstrate an association with PI3K/AKT/HIF-1A pathway activation rather than establishing a definitive causal mechanistic axis. Third, regarding the statistical methodology, our study was primarily exploratory. The clinical survival analyses were largely unadjusted for potential confounding factors (eg., age, gender, and smoking history) due to the limited completeness of patient covariates in the public datasets. Additionally, formal a priori power calculations were not performed, and some high-throughput multi-omics analyses relied on nominal p-values rather than stringent multiple-testing corrections, which may increase the risk of false positives. Finally, the restricted sample size and reliance on public databases highlight the need for further validation in larger, well-annotated, independent clinical cohorts.
In summary, this study systematically investigates the role of COQ10B in esophageal squamous cell carcinoma. Our findings reveal that elevated COQ10B expression is closely associated with an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment and an upregulated glycolytic metabolism signature, potentially mediated via the activation of the PI3K/AKT/HIF-1A signaling pathway. These associations highlight its role in accompanying the malignant biological behavior of tumor cells. These findings offer novel insights into the pathogenesis of ESCC and establish a foundation for the development of future targeted therapies and immunotherapeutic strategies. Despite the aforementioned limitations, the correlative research on COQ10B highlights its potential importance in cancer progression, warranting further in-depth functional and mechanistic exploration.
