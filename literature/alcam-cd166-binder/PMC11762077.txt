## Background
The high mortality rate of metastatic colorectal cancer (CRC) is primarily attributed to resistance to chemotherapy, where cancer stem cells (CSCs) play a crucial role. Deubiquitinating enzymes are essential regulators of CSC maintenance, making them potential targets for eliminating CSCs and overcoming chemotherapy resistance. This study aims to identify key deubiquitinating enzymes regulating CSCs and drug resistance of CRC.

## Methods
RNA sequencing was performed to examine the mRNA expression of known deubiquitinating enzymes in CRC tissues from patients with alternate response to chemotherapy. Gain- and loss-of-function experiments were performed to evaluate the function of USP4 in regulation of stemness and drug sensitivity in CRC. High-throughput virtual screening and target management assays were conducted to identify small molecule inhibitor targeting USP4. Cell lines, organoids and animal models were used to evaluate the function of USP4 and its small molecule inhibitor in stemness and chemotherapy response.

## Results
The expression of USP4 was significantly elevated in CRC samples from progressive disease (PD) or stable disease (SD) patients compared to partial response (PR) specimen. USP4 promoted stemness by stabilizing the β-catenin and Twist1 proteins in CRC cells. A natural small molecule product U4-I05 diminished the stem-like features of CSCs and enhanced their sensitivity to oxaliplatin and 5-fluorouracil by targeting inhibition of its deubiquitinating enzyme activity through binding the catalytic domain of USP4 (311 cysteine site) at nanomolar concentrations, triggering proteasome-mediated degradation of β-catenin and Twist1. Treatment with U4-I05 also inhibited tumor metastasis and extended survival in a genetically engineered CRC mouse model.

## Conclusions
This study identifies U4-I05 as a USP4 inhibitor with significant therapeutic efficacy against CRC, offering a promising avenue for the development of new treatments targeting cancer stemness and chemotherapy resistance.

## Introduction
Colorectal cancer (CRC) is one of the most prevalent cancers worldwide, and metastatic CRC (mCRC) presents significant challenges in treatment and patient management [ ]. Although the five-year survival rate for patients diagnosed with localized CRC is approximately 90%, this rate drops drastically for those with metastatic disease, falling to around 14–20% [ ]. Recent advancements in treatment modalities have led to improved outcomes; however, the overall prognosis for mCRC remains poor, highlighting the urgent need for early detection and innovative therapeutic strategies [ ]. The persistent challenges in managing mCRC can be largely attributed to the development of chemotherapy resistance. Current treatment protocols typically utilize platinum-based compounds and fluorouracil regimens, yet the effectiveness of these therapies is often compromised due to development of drug resistance [ ]. Therefore, understanding the mechanisms of drug resistance and developing robust intervention strategies to enhance therapeutic sensitivity are crucial for improving survival outcomes for patients with metastatic CRC.
Cancer stem cells (CSCs) are a small but important subpopulation of tumor cells that have self-renewal capabilities, tumorigenic potential, and heterogeneity [ ]. CSCs can contribute to treatment failure through mechanisms such as enhanced drug efflux, altered metabolic pathways, and the capability to enter a quiescent state, which allows them to evade the cytotoxic effects of chemotherapy. Moreover, CSCs play a pivotal role in driving tumor heterogeneity and facilitating the invasive properties that lead to metastasis [ ]. Early identification of CSCs in CRC was based on the expression of specific markers such as CD133, followed by the characterization of other markers such as CD24, CD29, CD44, CD166, Bmi-1 and LGR5 [ – ]. Moreover, the epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition (EMT), a pivotal process in embryonic development, has been associated with enhanced stemness and therapy resistance in tumors [ ]. Key signaling pathways, notably the WNT/β-catenin pathway, along with transcription factors like SNAI1, TWIST, and ZEB1, are critical regulators of EMT and cancer stemness in CRC [ – ]. Targeting these pathways is being actively pursued, with agents such as CWP232291, which inhibits the Wnt/β-catenin pathway, currently under investigation in clinical trials [ – ].
The aberrant accumulation of nuclear β-catenin due to APC mutations is a fundamental event in CRC tumorigenesis, occurring in 80–90% of cases [ ]. Moreover, activation of the WNT/β-catenin signaling pathway enhances self-renewal, cell migration, and resistance to chemotherapy [ ]. Additionally, Twist1, a vital transcriptional regulator of EMT, enhances CSC characteristics and correlates with poor patient prognoses in CRC [ , ]. Given the essential roles of β-catenin and Twist1 in EMT and stemness in CRC, these proteins have garnered substantial attention in drug development efforts. However, despite ongoing efforts to develop β-catenin and Twist1 inhibitors, the clinical effectiveness of these drugs remains inadequate [ ]. Notably, the protein levels of β-catenin and Twist1 in tumor cells are tightly regulated by the ubiquitin-proteasome system, making the targeting of their degradation a compelling strategy for eliminating cancer stem cells in various cancers [ , ]. Emerging evidence also highlights the significant role of deubiquitinating enzymes (DUBs) in modulating the signaling pathways associated with the survival of CSCs [ ]. Given that DUBs represent promising targets for cancer therapy, exploring DUB inhibitors as part of CSC-targeted treatments is particularly relevant [ ]. Therefore, we have chosen to focus on the deubiquitinases in the current study.
Recent insights reveal that the protein levels of β-catenin and Twist1 are tightly regulated by the ubiquitin-proteasome system (UPS), suggesting that targeting their degradation represents a compelling strategy for CSC elimination [ , ]. Given the emerging roles of deubiquitinating enzymes (DUBs) in modulating CSC signaling pathways [ ], they present valuable targets for therapeutic intervention [ , ]. In this study, we identify ubiquitin-specific peptidase 4 (USP4) as a critical regulator of cancer stemness by mediating the stability of β-catenin and Twist1 in CRC cells. In addition, we present U4-I05, a novel small molecule that targets USP4, thereby promoting the degradation of β-catenin and Twist1. U4-I05 effectively inhibits CSC features and reduces resistance to oxaliplatin, offering a promising strategy to enhance chemotherapy efficacy in CRC.

## Results

## Expression of USP4 is associated with CSC features and resistance to chemotherapy in CRC
To investigate the association between deubiquitinases and the response to chemotherapy in CRC, we collected primary tumor specimens from 8 metastatic CRC patients who underwent neoadjuvant chemotherapy for RNA sequencing analyses (Fig.  A-B). Among these patients, 4 exhibited a partial response (PR), while the remaining 4 showed progressive disease (PD) or stable disease (SD) based on MR/CT evaluations (Fig.  A). Notably, the intestinal stem cell (ISC) signature [ ], which is linked to poor survival outcomes (Figure A), was significantly enriched in PD/SD tumors compared to PR tumors (Fig.  C). Additionally, genes associated with cancer stem cells, including MYC, STAT3, NANOG, TGFB2, YBX1, CD44, and MMP1, were upregulated in PD/SD samples (Figure B). In contrast, there was no significant difference in the levels of β-catenin and Twist1 between PR and PD/SD samples (Figure B), suggesting that the regulation of these two transcription factors at the protein level plays a critical role in CRC drug resistance. Among various deubiquitinases, the mRNA levels of USP4 and USP46 were significantly higher in tumors from PD/SD CRC patients compared to those from PR CRC patients (Fig.  D and S1C). Importantly, USP4 has been previously reported to be upregulated in CRC and to contribute to tumor progression through its interaction and stabilization of both β-catenin and Twist [ , , ]. We therefore focus on USP4 and confirmed its upregulation in PD/SD samples relative to PR samples by immunohistochemistry staining (Fig.  E). Furthermore, analyses utilizing the public single-cell database revealed a correlation between USP4 expression and drug resistance [ , ], as well as ISC signature in CRC tumors (Fig.  F-I). Remarkably, high USP4 expression was associated with high score of ISC signature and a poor disease-free survival prognosis in CRC patients from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) (Fig.  J-K). Protein expression analysis of USP4 in the Human Protein Atlas revealed that USP4 levels were significantly higher in CRC compared to other human cancers (Figure D). Additionally, mRNA levels of USP4 were elevated in therapy-resistant CRC patients compared to those with therapy-sensitive tumors in the GSE28814 dataset [ ] (Figure E). These findings underscore the potential of USP4 as a therapeutic target for overcoming chemoresistance in CRC.

 

## Knockdown of USP4 suppresses CRC stemness and inhibits resistance to oxaliplatin
Next, we examined the relationship between USP4 expression and cancer stemness across human colorectal cancer (CRC) cell lines. The analyzed lines (RKO, LoVo, SW480, and SW620) demonstrated elevated levels of pluripotent transcription factors MYC and SOX2, coupled with significantly enhanced tumorsphere formation capabilities in comparison to the HT-29 and HCT116 cell lines (Figure A-B). Notably, USP4 expression was markedly higher in RKO, LoVo, SW480, and SW620 cells compared to HT-29 and HCT116 cells (Fig.  A). Corroborating this observation, a positive correlation between USP4 expression and tumorsphere formation was noted (Fig.  B). Furthermore, siRNA-mediated knockdown of USP4 substantially inhibited the tumorsphere formation capacity in LoVo and SW620 cells (Fig.  C-D and C). Importantly, the reduction of USP4 expression led to a dramatic decrease in the CD44+Lgr5+EpCAM+ cancer stem cell sub-population (Fig.  E and D). The effect of USP4 on the expression of CD133 was negligible (Figure E). Similar trends were observed in RKO cells (Figure F-H). Additionally, USP4 knockdown in MC38 cells significantly impaired their capacity to initiate tumors in nude mice (Fig.  F-G). Importantly, organoid culture experiments illustrated that USP4 knockdown resulted in a decrease in both the number of spheroids and CD44 expression (Fig.  H-I), signifying that USP4 is a crucial regulator in sustaining the stemness of CRC cells.

 

Considering that USP4 functions as a deubiquitinase for β-catenin and Twist1, we further investigated the necessity of these factors in USP4-mediated cancer stemness. Notably, knocking down of either β-catenin or Twist1 significantly diminished the cancer stemness induced by USP4 (Fig.  J-L and S2I-J). Given that CSCs are implicated in drug resistance, we hypothesized that USP4 might enhance chemoresistance in CRC. Indeed, as illustrated in Fig.  M-N and K, USP4 knockdown markedly lowered the IC50 of oxaliplatin and heightened the apoptotic response upon oxaliplatin treatment. Collectively, these findings underscore the pivotal roles of USP4 in mediating cancer stemness and chemoresistance to oxaliplatin in CRC cells.

## Identification of U4-I05 as a small molecule inhibitor targeting USP4
To identify small molecules that effectively inhibit the enzymatic activity of USP4, we conducted a virtual screening employing AutoVina, utilizing the crystal structure of USP4 alongside a library of 110,920 small natural molecules sourced from the ZINC database (Fig.  A). This analysis was conducted based on the understanding that the USP domain of USP4 serves as the active site for C311, which is crucial for its deubiquitinating function [ ] (Fig.  B). Following the virtual screening, we selected the top 11 small molecules with the highest binding affinities to USP4 for further evaluation through molecular-protein interaction assays (Fig.  C). The interaction affinities of these molecules with recombinant purified USP4 truncation (296-490AA) were subsequently analyzed using a Biolayer Interferometry assay. Among these, U4-I05 (ZINC000008877313) exhibited a notably low dissociation constant, yielding a KD of 2.39E-07 M with an acceptable error of 4.03E-08 M (Fig.  C-D and A). The interaction of U4-I05 with USP4 was further validated using the CB-dock tool, which indicated that U4-I05 engages with USP4 through multiple molecular interactions within the C311 pocket (Figure B). Additionally, the cellular interaction of U4-I05 with USP4 was corroborated via a cellular thermal shift assay. Treatment with U4-I05 (10 nM) over a 72-h period resulted in a significant destabilization of USP4 protein but not USP4C311A against a gradient of increasing temperatures (Fig.  E and B).

 

Notably, U4-I05 also inhibited CD44 protein expression in organoids derived from MC38 cells (Fig.  F). Treatment with U4-I05 significantly suppressed tumorsphere formation and reduced the proportion of CD44+EpCAM+Lgr5+ cells (Fig.  G-H and C). Importantly, while U4-I05 exhibited no effects on stemness in cells with USP4 knockdown, it markedly inhibited stemness features in cells overexpressing wild-type USP4 (Fig.  I-K and D-H). In contrast, overexpression of the USP4C311A mutant did not impact cancer stemness (Fig.  J-K and D-H). Furthermore, ectopic expression of β-catenin or Twist1 was found to reverse the U4-I05-mediated inhibition of the CD44+Lgr5+EpCAM+ population (Fig.  L and I-J). Collectively, these results demonstrate that U4-I05 effectively targets USP4 to inhibit stemness characteristics in CRC cells, an effect that is contingent upon its catalytic activity.

## U4-I05 inhibits the expression of Twist1 and β-catenin by targeting USP4
Since Twist1 and β-catenin are critical substrates of USP4 involved in cancer stemness and tumor progression [ , ], we sought to evaluate the effect of U4-I05 on their expression. Treatment with U4-I05 resulted in a dose-dependent reduction of both Twist1 and β-catenin levels in CRC cells (Fig.  A). Notably, U4-I05 significantly diminished the nuclear localization of β-catenin, a hallmark indicative of Wnt signaling pathway activation (Fig.  B). Moreover, U4-I05 led to a marked reduction in the mRNA expression of Wnt/β-catenin target genes, including LGR5, MYC, and AXIN2 (Fig.  C), which mirrored the phenotypic changes observed upon USP4 knockdown (Figures A-B).

 

To elucidate the underlying mechanism by which U4-I05 affects the levels of Twist1 and β-catenin, the stability of these proteins was assessed. U4-I05 significantly shortened the half-life of both Twist1 and β-catenin proteins (Fig.  D), and this effect was reversible in the presence of the proteasome inhibitor MG132 (Fig.  E). Additionally, U4-I05 promoted the polyubiquitination of β-catenin and Twist1, an effect that could be attenuated by USP4 overexpression (Fig.  F-I). Importantly, U4-I05 did not disrupt the physical interaction between USP4 and either β-catenin or Twist1, even when in the presence of the USP4C311A mutant (Figure C). USP46 is known to downregulate p-AKT expression [ ]. If USP46 were an off-target for U4-I05, we would expect p-AKT levels to increase following treatment. To assess potential off-target effects on USP46, we evaluated p-AKT expression after U4-I05 treatment. The results showed that U4-I05 treatment not only failed to increase p-AKT levels but decreased them (Figure D), indicating that USP46 is not a target of U4-I05. Crucially, U4-I05 demonstrated potent inhibition of the deubiquitinase activity of USP4 in vitro (Fig.  J). Moreover, it effectively reduced the levels of β-catenin and Twist1 in cells overexpressing USP4 (Fig.  K-L). Taken together, these findings indicate that U4-I05 inhibits the expression of β-catenin and Twist1 primarily by impairing the deubiquitinase activity of USP4, thereby promoting protein degradation.

## Targeting USP4 enhances sensitivity to chemotherapeutic agents in CRC
Next, we evaluated the effect of U4-I05 on drug resistance in CRC. SW620 and LoVo cells with relatively high USP4 expression were treated with oxaliplatin in the presence or absence of U4-I05. Cell viability and apoptosis assays showed that U4-I05 significantly decreased the IC50 of oxaliplatin (Fig.  A), inhibited cell growth (Fig.  B and A) and increased apoptosis rates (Fig.  C-D and B-C) in oxaliplatin-treated SW620 and LoVo cells. In contrast, U4-I05 treatment did not affect the IC50 of oxaliplatin, cell growth or apoptosis in oxaliplatin-treated HCT116 or HT-29 cells with relatively low USP4 expression (Fig.  E-H and D-F). Furthermore, U4-I05 significantly increased the apoptosis rates in oxaliplatin-treated CD44+ SW620 and LoVo cells (Fig.  I and G). Notably, ectopic expression of β-catenin or Twist1 reversed the resistance to oxaliplatin in SW620 cells (Figure H). These results indicate that U4-I05 effectively overcomes chemotherapy resistance specifically in cells with elevated USP4 expression, suggesting a pivotal role for USP4 in mediating such resistance.

 

To further elucidate the mechanism by which USP4 contributes to chemotherapy resistance in CRC cells, oxaliplatin-resistant and 5-Fluorouracil (5-Fu)-resistant DLD-1 cells were established. Knockdown of USP4 or treatment with U4-I05 dramatically enhanced sensitivity to both oxaliplatin and 5-Fu in these resistant cells (Fig.  J-K and Figure I). Conversely, U4-I05 had no effect on the sensitivity to oxaliplatin or 5-Fu in resistant cells where USP4 had been knocked down, underscoring the specificity of U4-I05 for cells with high USP4 expression (Fig.  J-K).
Given that DNA damage repair mechanisms and multidrug resistance transporters are critical regulators of chemotherapy resistance, the expression levels of key regulators such as PARP1, TP53, and ABCB1 were examined. The results revealed that PARP1, TP53, and ABCB1 were significantly upregulated in the 5-Fu-resistant DLD-1 cells (Fig.  L). Notably, only PARP1 and TP53 expression levels were downregulated following USP4 knockdown (Fig.  L), suggesting that USP4 may promote drug resistance by inducing the activation of the DNA damage repair pathway.

## U4-I05 suppresses tumor growth and enhances sensitivity to oxaliplatin in nude mice
To investigate the in vivo role of U4-I05, a tumor xenograft mice model was established by subcutaneously implanting SW620 cells into nude mice (Fig.  A). The results showed that both oxaliplatin monotherapy and U4-I05 monotherapy exhibited moderate efficacy in inhibiting tumor growth (Fig.  A-E). However, the combination therapy of U4-I05 and oxaliplatin resulted in a significant reduction in tumor growth compared to either treatment alone (Fig.  A-E). Moreover, the combined treatment significantly decreased Ki67 expression, a marker of cell proliferation, while concurrently increasing Cleaved-Caspase 3 expression, an indicator of apoptosis, compared to the vehicle control group (Fig.  F-G). Notably, U4-I05 monotherapy did not induce significant changes in Cleaved-Caspase 3 levels (Fig.  F-G), suggesting that the anti-tumor effects of U4-I05 may occur independently of apoptosis and could involve the attenuation of CSC proliferation. Importantly, U4-I05 treatment significantly reduced the levels of Twist1 and β-catenin within the tumors (Fig.  H-I). Interestingly, oxaliplatin treatment also resulted in a moderate reduction of Twist1 and β-catenin levels (Fig.  H-I), corroborating findings from prior research [ , ]. Furthermore, U4-I05 treatment markedly suppressed the accumulation of CD44+LGR5+EpCAM+ cells within the tumors, while oxaliplatin monotherapy had a minimal effect on their enrichment (Fig.  J and A). Collectively, these results indicate that U4-I05 exerts a potent anti-tumor effect by downregulating Twist1 and β-catenin expression and enhancing the sensitivity of tumor cells to oxaliplatin.

 

## Safety evaluation of U4-I05 in mice
To assess the potential clinical applicability of U4-I05, a comprehensive evaluation of blood composition, as well as liver and kidney function, was conducted in xenograft mice treated with U4-I05. Body weight measurements were comparable across all treatment groups, indicating no significant weight loss associated with U4-I05 administration (Figure B). Blood component analysis revealed that U4-I05 had no discernible effects on hematological and myeloid parameters, while treatment with oxaliplatin led to a reduction in the ratio of hematopoietic cells (Figure C-D). Although alanine transaminase (ALT) levels were elevated in the combination therapy group, mono-treatment with U4-I05 did not significantly affect ALT levels (Figure E). This finding suggests that the combination therapy may potentially compromise liver function, while U4-I05 alone appears to be safe in terms of hepatic and renal parameters.

## U4-I05 suppresses cancer progression in the iKAP mouse model
Given its inhibitory effects on Twist1 and β-catenin, U4-I05 was hypothesized to reverse EMT and impede cell migration. Immunofluorescence assays demonstrated a marked increase in E-cadherin expression in SW620 cells following U4-I05 treatment; however, no significant changes were observed in HT-29 cells (Fig.  A). Furthermore, U4-I05 dose-dependently promoted mRNA expression of E-cadherin in both SW620 and LoVo cells (Figure A-B). Notably, U4-I05 significantly inhibited the invasiveness of SW620 cells, while exhibiting no effect on HT-29 cells (Fig.  B).

 

To assess the effects of U4-I05 on cancer progression, a previously established spontaneous CRC mouse model (iKAP), which is characterized by APC loss of function and putative activation of WNT/β-catenin signaling was applied [ , ]. Following induction with Dox and 4-OHT for 4 weeks, iKAP mice with similar tumor volumes underwent endoscopic evaluation and were subsequently randomized to receive either vehicle or U4-I05 treatment (Fig.  C). U4-I05 treatment significantly extended the survival time of the iKAP mice (Fig.  D). Moreover, U4-I05 substantially impeded the invasion of primary tumor cells into the outer wall of the rectum (Fig.  E) and significantly reduced the number of primary tumors in iKAP mice (Fig.  F). Importantly, U4-I05 effectively halted the malignant progression of CRC, as evidenced by the tumors in the U4-I05 group remaining at the adenoma or adenocarcinoma stage, in contrast to the majority of tumors in the vehicle group, which progressed to carcinoma (Fig.  G-H). Additionally, U4-I05 treatment significantly reduced levels of Twist1 and β-catenin while concurrently increasing E-cadherin levels (Fig.  I and C). Collectively, these data suggested that U4-I05 suppresses EMT, cancer progression, and metastasis in CRC.

## Discussion
Our study highlights the significant role of USP4 in chemotherapy resistance and cancer stemness in CRC. knocking down USP4 was found to suppress CRC stemness and inhibit resistance to oxaliplatin treatment. We also introduced a novel small molecule, U4-I05, which specifically targets USP4 in CRC. U4-I05 promotes the degradation of β-catenin and Twist1 by inhibiting their deubiquitination through USP4. By reducing the deubiquitinating enzyme activity of USP4, U4-I05 effectively diminishes the stem cell properties, overcomes oxaliplatin resistance, and impedes the malignant progression of CRC (Fig.  J). Notably, USP4 is commonly overexpressed in CRC correlates with a resistant phenotype, highlighting the potential of U4-I05 as a treatment option. However, systematic studies are needed to evaluate USP4 expression in larger clinical cohorts alongside chemoresistance data, which represents a key area for future investigation.
Targeting CSCs has emerged as an effective strategy to combat chemotherapy resistance [ ]. Numerous agents that inhibit pathways known to regulate CSCs, such as Sonic Hedgehog, Notch, Wnt/β-catenin, and NF-κB, are currently under investigation in preclinical and early clinical trials. Initial findings indicate good tolerance for these agents in patients with advanced solid tumors and leukemia [ ]. However, no CSC-targeting agents have yet been approved for treating solid tumors [ ]. Interest also lies in developing agents that inhibit core transcription factors involved in EMT. For instance, WP1130, a small molecule that inhibits DUB3, has been shown to degrade SNAIL1, subsequently reducing growth and migration in breast cancer cells [ ]. Similarly, harmine, which targets Twist1, inhibits growth in non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), though its mechanism of action remains unclear [ ].
As a key transcription factor in the Wnt/β-catenin pathway, β-catenin represents a promising therapeutic target in CRC. Strategies to inhibit transcriptional activity and expression are advancing, with several agents undergoing preclinical and clinical evaluation [ ]. Notably, the small molecule MSAB, which binds to β-catenin and induces its proteasome-mediated degradation, has exhibited significant anti-tumor effects in WNT/β-catenin-dependent CRC xenograft models [ ]. Nevertheless, further investigation is needed to assess the impact of MSAB on CSCs. Despite these advancements, a considerable gap persists in the development of effective and safe small molecule inhibitors targeting CSCs in CRC. Ongoing exploration of compounds like U4-I05 may yield valuable insights and therapeutic options for overcoming chemotherapy resistance and improving treatment outcomes in CRC.
USP4 has been recognized as an oncogene in various cancer types [ ]. It stabilizes β-catenin and Twist1 through a deubiquitinating mechanism, thereby enhancing stem cell-like properties in CRC cells [ , ]. USP4 deubiquitinates multiple substrates across different cancers, including TGFβR1, TAK1, and ARF-BP1, contributing to its oncogenic roles in these contexts [ – ]. The current study provides evidence that USP4 increases the protein levels of Twist1 and β-catenin, subsequently promoting stemness and drug resistance in CRC. Importantly, the inhibition of β-catenin and Twist1 effectively suppresses the stemness induced by USP4.
Previous studies have shown that USP8 and USP20 can deubiquitinate β-catenin, while DUB3 and USP51 target Twist1 [ – ]. However, our analysis of RNA-seq analysis comparing PD and SD samples to PR samples indicated that USP8 and USP20 did not exhibit differential expression, and DUB3 as well as USP51 were undetectable. Furthermore, USP4 expression was elevated in PD/SD samples and cell lines with high stemness, indicating that USP4 may be the primary deubiquitinase regulating the stability of β-catenin and Twist1 in CRC. This emphasizes the importance of USP4 in the development of chemotherapy resistance. Although USP46 was also upregulated in PD/SD tumors,, potential off-target effects of U4-I05 on USP46 were excluded, as U4-I05 treatment resulted in downregulation of p-AKT, which was inhibited by USP46 [ ].
Two other inhibitors, Neutral Red (NR) and Vialinin-A, have been documented to target USP4 [ – ]. NR is known as an uncompetitive inhibitor of USP4 and has demonstrated its effects in CRC cancer. Specifically, NR-treated cells exhibited decreased β-catenin stability and downregulated β-catenin target genes. Furthermore, treatment with NR diminished colony formation and cell migration, as well as suppressing tumor growth in mouse models. However, the requirement for a high dosage (40 µM) to inhibit USP4 activity and decrease β-catenin protein expression limits its utility in vivo [ ]. Vialinin-A, another ubiquitin-specific peptidase inhibitor, does not specifically target USP4. Instead, it has been reported to inhibit USP5 activity and influence the release of TNF-α [ , ]. Additionally, Vialinin-A has been shown to attenuate inflammation and fibrosis in S100-induced hepatitis models through Rheb/mTOR signaling [ ].
Our study demonstrates that U4-I05 treatment significantly inhibits USP4 and reduces the expression of β-catenin and Twist1 at low doses (10 µM for 8 h or 10 nM for 48 h). This inhibition allows U4-I05 to exert its antitumor effects primarily by specifically targeting USP4, thereby inhibiting its deubiquitinating activity without disrupting its interactions with β-catenin and Twist1. As a result, the degradation of these critical regulators of CSC properties is enhanced, leading to the selective elimination of CSCs. Consequently, U4-I05 effectively reverses chemotherapy resistance and improves overall treatment efficacy. Given that USP4 is highly expressed in various cancer types, including colorectal, pancreatic, ovarian, and lung cancers, we anticipate that U4-I05 may possess anti-tumor efficacy across these malignancies. However, further investigations are necessary to validate this potential using more reliable models, such as patient-derived tumor xenografts and organoids established from patient tumor tissues. Despite these promising results, several limitations need to be considered. First, the specificity of U4-I05 for USP4 compared to other deubiquitinating enzymes requires further investigation. Second, its efficacy may vary across different tumor microenvironments, significantly impacting drug uptake, distribution, metabolism, and overall response [ ]. Therefore, a comprehensive understanding of U4-I05’s specificity and activity in varying contexts is essential for optimizing its clinical application. Additionally, the differential expression of USP4 across various tumors emphasizes the need for improved biomarker detection methods to reduce potential off-target effects of U4-I05. Future research should focus on elucidating the precise mechanisms by which U4-I05 interacts with its target. Employing techniques such as Tandem Mass Tag (TMT) spectrometry in cellular thermal shift assays will provide valuable insights into how U4-I05 acts on USP4 and associated pathways, ultimately facilitating its safe and effective application in clinical settings.
In summary, our findings highlight the crucial role of USP4 in CRC stemness and chemotherapy resistance. U4-I05 is a promising therapeutic agent targeting USP4, with potential to enhance treatment efficacy and counteract chemotherapy resistance. Future development will prioritize structural modifications and optimization of U4-I05 to improve its drug-like properties. Comprehensive safety and pharmacodynamic evaluations should be performed using preclinical models, including patient-derived tumor xenografts (PDX) and organoids from CRC tissues. Furthermore, investigating U4-I05’s specificity and potential off-target effects will be vital for refining its clinical application and minimizing adverse effects.

## Materials and methods

## Cell culture
The CRC cell lines HT-29, RKO, HCT116, DLD-1, LoVo, and SW480 were cultured in RPIM-1640 medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum, 100 units/ml penicillin, and 100 ug/ml streptomycin. Oxaliplatin-resistant DLD-1 cells were obtained by culturing with oxaliplatin (10 µM) or 5-fluorouracil (5-Fu, 50 µM) for 3 months. SW620 cells were cultured in DMEM medium under the same supplement conditions. Cell identity was confirmed by short tandem repeats (STR) identification, and routine testing ensured absence of mycoplasma contamination. For the tumorsphere assay, cells were cultured in DMEM/F12 medium supplemented with 1 X B27 (Invitrogen), 20 ng/ml EGF, and 20 ng/ml bFGF.

## Cell growth inhibition assay
Cells were seeded at a density of 10,000 per well using 96-well plates and cultured in regular medium supplemented with or without U4-I05 (5 µM or 10 µM) and the indicated dose of oxaliplatin. Photos of living cells were captured using the Incucyte system after 72 h of treatment. For the MTS assay, the cell viability of SW620 stable cells treated with the indicated dose of oxaliplatin for 72 h was assessed using the MTT assay following previously described procedures. The inhibition curves were fitted, and IC50 values were calculated using the ‘Log[inhibitor] vs normalized response’ program in GraphPad Prism v8.0 software.

## Plasmids and lentivirus infection
The construction of plasmids was performed following previously described methods [ ]. In brief, recombinant lentiviruses expressing human USP4, USP4C311A, β-catenin, or Twist1 were generated using a pLVX-puro vector. The oligos encoding the 296-490AA domain of USP4 were subcloned into the pHIS2 prokaryotic expression plasmid fused with a His tag at the N-terminal. Short hairpin RNA (shRNA) constructs targeting USP4 were generated using the pLKO.1-puro vector, while sgRNA was cloned into the lentiCRISPR v2 plasmid [ ]. All plasmid constructs were validated by DNA sequencing. The primers used for plasmid construction in this study are listed in Table .

## Single-cell isolation and flow cytometry analyses
Single cells from xenograft tumors were isolated following previously established procedures [ ]. In brief, the tumor single cells were obtained using the Tumor Dissociation Kit (#130-095-929, Miltenyi Biotec, Bergisch Gladbach, Germany) as per the manufacturer’s protocol. The digested cells were then passed through 40 μm filters into RPMI-1640 and centrifuged at 300 × g for 5 min at 4 °C. Erythrocytes were removed from the cell mixture using Red Cell lysis buffer for 1 min at room temperature. Cells cultured in vitro were harvested and suspended in cold PBS. Subsequently, 1 × 10^6 cells from tumors or in vitro cultures were incubated with antibodies conjugated with specific fluorescence biotin for CD44, LGR5, and EpCAM for 30 min on ice. Following incubation, the cells were washed twice with PBS and analyzed using a Beckman cytometer with specified channels. For the apoptosis assay, cells treated with oxaliplatin with or without U4-I05 were collected, stained with RNase/PI buffer, and the apoptosis ratio was determined using a Beckman cytometer with the PC5.5 channel.

## Western blot analysis, co-Immunoprecipitation assay and ubiquitination assay
Western blot analysis, co-immunoprecipitation assay, and in vivo ubiquitination assay were conducted following previously described methods [ ]. The antibody specific for USP4 (sc-376000) was procured from Santa Cruz Biotechnology (Burlingame, CA, USA), while antibodies specific for Twist1 (AF4009), β-Actin (AF7018), and GAPDH (AF7021) were obtained from Affinity Biosciences (Jiangsu, China). Additionally, the antibody specific for β-catenin (66379) was purchased from Proteintech (Wuhan, China), the antibody specific for Flag (F1804) was obtained from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, USA), and the antibody specific for HA (3724) was purchased from Cell Signaling Technology (Massachusetts, USA).

## Immunohistochemistry (IHC) staining
Tumors obtained from xenograft mice were embedded in paraffin and sectioned to a thickness of 4 μm. The slides underwent an IHC assay, following previously described methods [ , ], utilizing specific antibodies for USP4 (sc-376000), Twist1 (AF4009, Affinity Biosciences), β-catenin (66379, Proteintech), cleaved-Caspase 3 (AF7022, Affinity Biosciences), Ki67 (bsm-60738R, Bioss Antibodies), and E-cadherin (20874, Proteintech). The average optical density (AOD) was calculated using the formula: AOD = IOD/Area [ ].

## Immunofluorescence (IF) staining
IF was conducted following previously described procedures [ ]. Briefly, cells were seeded on glass slides in 24-well plates, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min, permeabilized with 0.1% Triton-100 for 15 min, and then blocked with 5% BSA at room temperature for 1 h. The cells were subsequently incubated with a primary antibody specific to β-catenin (66379, Proteintech) and then with an appropriate secondary antibody. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI. Images were acquired using a confocal fluorescent microscope (ZEISS).

## Tumorsphere assay
The cells were harvested, filtered through forty-mesh filters, and the cell count was determined. Subsequently, 20,000 single cells were seeded onto a 6-ultra-low-adherent plate (Corning) and cultured for 7 days at 5% CO2 and 37 °C. Tumorspheres were then observed and photographed under a light microscope, and those with a diameter exceeding 100 μm were enumerated.

## Real time quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR)
Cellular RNA was extracted using RNAiso Plus (9109, TaKaRa, Japan) and reverse-transcribed following the manufacturer’s guidelines. Subsequently, RT-qPCR reactions were performed using the CFX-384 Real-Time PCR System (Bio-Rad, CA, USA) and Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix (cat# MQ101-01, Vazyme, Nanjing, China) as instructed by the manufacturer. Relative quantitation values were calculated using the ΔΔCt method, with the primer sequences for RT-qPCR provided in Table .

## Animal experiments
All animal care and experimental procedures were conducted in accordance with institutional ethical guidelines and approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Sun Yat-sen University (approval number # L025504202409010). In the nude mice experiment, 5 × 106 SW620 cells were subcutaneously injected into 6-week-old BALB/C nude mice (n = 6/group). Once the tumor volume reached 100 mm3, the mice were randomly assigned to different groups and housed under standard conditions. Subsequently, oxaliplatin (6 mg/kg/mouse) was administered intraperitoneally on day 1 post-grouping, with or without U4-I05 (15 mg/kg/mouse) treatment given once daily for 5 consecutive days. Tumor volume and mouse weight were monitored at specified intervals. On day 12 after the initial treatment, the mice were euthanized, tumors were excised, and photographs were taken. Tumor samples were then subjected to IHC staining, and single cells were isolated for flow cytometry analysis. Peripheral blood samples were collected, and the blood cell composition was analyzed using the Procyte Dx system (IDEXX, USA). Serum obtained after centrifugation at 3,000 g for 10 min was used for alanine aminotransferase (ALT) concentration analysis with the BS-240VET system (Mindray, China).
For extreme limited dilution assay, the indicated number of MC38 stable cells were subcutaneously injected into nude mice. Seven days post-injection, tumors were harvested from the mice, and the tumor initiation frequency was calculated using online tool (https://bioinf.wehi.edu.au/software/elda/).
For the induction of CRC in the iKAP mouse model [ ], mice were administered Doxycycline in drinking water (2 mg/mL) continuously after being injected with 4-OH-Tamoxifen (4-OHT, 10 mg/mL, diluted in 100% EtOH) rectally at 8 weeks old. Mice with similar tumor volumes, as assessed by mouse endoscopy, were randomly allocated to receive either vehicle or U4-I05 (15 mg/kg) treatment for 5 days (n = 5–6/group). Survival time was monitored until natural death occurred. Upon death, intestinal tissues were dissected, photographed, and fixed in 4% formaldehyde. At the 50th day post-vehicle or U4-I05 treatment, when the last mouse from the vehicle group died, all mice were euthanized, intestinal tissues were dissected, photographed, and fixed in 4% formaldehyde for subsequent IHC analyses.

## Biolayer interferometry (BLI)
The small molecules were procured from Vitas-M (Hong Kong, China). The recombinant 296-490AA domain of USP4, fused with a His tag at the N-terminus, was expressed and purified following previously established methods [ ]. Binding kinetics and affinities of molecular interactions were assessed using Biolayer Interferometry (BLI) with an Octet Red instrument (ForteBio, Inc., Menlo Park, USA), in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions and as detailed in prior studies [ ]. In brief, the purified recombinant 296-490AA domain of USP4, tagged with His, was biotinylated, immobilized on streptavidin-coated biosensors, and exposed to various small molecules. The binding affinity (KD) values were determined through analysis using Octet Pro software.

## In vitro deubiquitinase activity assay
HEK293T cells were transfected with Flag-USP4 expression plasmids. 48 h after transfection, cells were harvested and used for immunoprecipitation by anti-Flag antibody (F1804, Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, USA). Then the immunoprecipitated Flag-USP4 protein was used for deubiquitinase activity assay according to manufacturer’s instructions (Deubiquitinase Assay Kit, ab241002, Abcam).

## Cellular thermal shift assay
SW620 cells treated with U4-I05 (10 nM) for 72 h were collected for cellular thermal shift assay as described previously [ ]. Briefly, harvested cells were heated in indicated temperature by water-bath, then were subjected to western blot to examine the protein levels of USP4. The expression levels of USP4 were quantified with gray value using ImageJ software.

## Organoid culture
MC38 stable cells were subcutaneously injected into nude mice. 7 days after injection, tumors were dissociated and digested for organoid culture according to the manufacturer’s instructions (AIMINGMED, Hangzhou, China). 10 days after feeding, organoids were harvested and digested into single cells. A total of 20,000 cells were plated in 24-well plates for organoid culture, and after seven days of culture, the number of spheroids was quantified. For the flow cytometry assay, organoids were harvested and digested into single cells, which were then stained with a FITC-conjugated anti-CD44 antibody (CL488-65117, Proteintech, China). Organoids were treated with or without U4-I05 (10 nM) for 72 h, and subsequently, the protein levels of CD44 were examined using flow cytometry.

## RNA sequencing analysis
Total RNA was extracted using RNAiso Plus (9109, TaKaRa, Japan) following the manufacturer’s protocol. The RNA quality was evaluated using an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies, Palo Alto, CA, USA) and confirmed through RNase-free agarose gel electrophoresis. Following RNA extraction, rRNAs were depleted to enrich mRNAs and ncRNAs. The enriched mRNAs and ncRNAs were fragmented into short segments using a fragmentation buffer and then reverse transcribed into cDNA with random primers. Subsequently, second-strand cDNA synthesis was carried out using DNA polymerase I, RNase H, dNTPs (with dUTP substituted for dTTP), and buffer. The resulting cDNA fragments were purified with the QiaQuick PCR extraction kit (Qiagen, Venlo, The Netherlands), subjected to end repair, poly(A) tail addition, and ligation to Illumina sequencing adapters. Subsequently, Uracil-N-Glycosylase (UNG) was employed to degrade the second-strand cDNA. The digested products underwent size selection via agarose gel electrophoresis, PCR amplification, and sequencing on the Illumina HiSeq TM 4000 platform by Gene Denovo Biotechnology Co. (Guangzhou, China).

## Statistics analysis
The correlation coefficients were calculated using Pearson’s rank correlation test. Unpaired two-tailed Student’s t-test was employed for comparing two groups. Two-way ANOVA analyses were conducted to compare groups in cell growth inhibition and animal experiments. In vitro experiments with cells presented data from a minimum of three replicates except specific described, while studies involving animals displayed data from specified sample sizes. Statistical analyses were carried out using GraphPad Prism V8 software. Significance levels were denoted as follows: * p < 0.05, ** p < 0.01, *** p < 0.001, and “ns” indicating nonsignificant results.
