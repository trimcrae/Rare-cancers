## ABSTRACT
Systemic target engagement constrains the therapeutic index of many antibody drugs, particularly in inflammatory disease and immuno-oncology. Protease-cleavable masking can localize activity to disease sites, but existing approaches often tradeoff masking strength, reversibility, and developability. Here, we evaluated the insulin-like growth factor II (IGF-II) as a compact, structurally defined steric masking domain for protease-dependent conditional activation of therapeutic antibodies. IGF-II masking domains, including a receptor-silent variant (IGF-II-s), were engineered as N-terminal fusions to therapeutic antibodies via protease-cleavable linkers and characterized using antigen-binding and cell-based functional assays. IGF-II masking attenuated anti-TNFα binding and neutralization potency in a protease-dependent manner and activity was largely restored following cleavage. In vivo, an MMP2/9-cleavable masked anti-TNFα antibody retained efficacy comparable to adalimumab and infliximab in a collagen antibody-induced arthritis model, yet, unlike the reference antibodies, did not measurably reduce survival in a Listeria monocytogenes infection challenge model at the dose tested. IGF-II-s showed no detectable IGF receptor binding and provided stronger masking than native IGF-II. This generalized approach was demonstrated across multiple antibodies, enabling efficient protease-dependent conditional activation of trastuzumab (anti-HER2), an anti–IL-1β antibody, and bevacizumab (anti-VEGF). Together, these results established an engineered IGF-II-based masking domain as a versatile, IGF-II receptor-silent platform enabling protease-dependent conditional activation of therapeutic antibodies, with potential to improve safety and expand therapeutic windows.

## Introduction
Therapeutic biologics, particularly monoclonal antibodies, Fc-fusion proteins, bispecific antibodies, and antibody-based conjugates, have become foundational modalities for treating cancer, immune-mediated and inflammatory diseases, and other complex conditions. Their high target specificity, modular engineering flexibility, and long systemic half-life have enabled pharmacologic intervention in pathways that are difficult to drug with small molecules. Reflecting sustained innovation and broadening clinical adoption, the global portfolio of approved antibody therapeutics and the late-stage pipeline have continued to expand rapidly. 
However, the therapeutic index of many antibody drugs remains constrained by on-target off-disease mechanism-based toxicities arising from systemic target engagement in normal tissues. For example, TNFα inhibitors, such as adalimumab and infliximab, while highly effective in autoimmune diseases, are associated with serious infections and elevated malignancy risk, prompting ongoing efforts to refine patient selection and infection risk mitigation. Similarly, IL-1β blockade has been associated with an increased risk of serious infections in clinical use. In oncology, HER2 and VEGF pathways, widely targeted in oncology, have physiologic roles in cardiac and vascular homeostasis, and their inhibition can contribute to cardiac dysfunction and vascular toxicity in susceptible patients. , Likewise, immune checkpoint blockade, particularly CTLA-4 inhibition, can trigger life-threatening immune-related adverse effects due to nonspecific T-cell activation, , and novel immunotherapies such as anti-CD47 may cause on-target hematologic toxicities because of antigen expression on normal hematopoietic cells. Finally, bispecific T-cell engagers and CAR-T therapies redirect immune effectors to kill diseased cells but may cause severe cytokine release syndromes and off-tumor cytotoxicity. , Antibody-drug conjugates (ADCs) precisely deliver cytotoxic payloads to antigen-positive cells, yet unintended binding in healthy tissues expressing the same or related antigens can yield significant toxicity that limit therapeutic dosing and breadth of use. In short, these safety liabilities reduce patient eligibility and narrow the therapeutic windows for many biologic drugs.
To broaden therapeutic indices, there is increasing emphasis on conditional activation strategies that restrict biologic activity to disease sites while minimizing systemic engagement of healthy tissues. A prominent strategy is the development of masked (“prodrug”) antibodies, in which an appended masking moiety blocks the antigen-binding site until it is removed or displaced in response to disease-associated cues. Protease-dependent unmasking, via cleavage of engineered linkers by proteases enriched in tumors and inflamed microenvironments, is one of the most widely used activation mechanisms. Conditional antibody activation can also be achieved through non-proteolytic triggers, including pH- or ATP-responsive designs that exploit acidic and ATP-rich disease milieus and light- or ultrasound-triggered release systems that enable spatially controlled unmasking. , 
Current antibody masking strategies broadly fall into two principal classes. Steric hindrance-based masking relies on appending structural domains that physically obstruct the antibody paratope. Representative-masking elements include the latency-associated peptide (LAP) from TGFβ, albumin, XTEN polypeptides, the IgG1 hinge segment, leucine-zipper coiled-coil motifs, and CaM-CBP peptide clamp. Steric masking can also be achieved through architectural designs that restrict Fab accessibility, such as placing the target-binding Fab as the “inner” domain of a dual-variable-domain antibody or engineering a releasable, disulfide-stabilized Fv fused to a bulky CH3 domain with limited flexibility. , The second class, affinity-based masking, uses peptides or engineered antigens to occupy the binding site until dissociation and/or proteolysis liberates the paratope for target engagement. In the ProBody technology, target-specific epitope-mimetic peptides (mimotopes) were used to mask therapeutic antibodies against EpCAM, CD166, and PD-L1, with several derived antibody prodrugs advancing into clinical trials. Mimotopes have also been deployed in alternative formats, such as mimotope peptide-DNA conjugates. Beyond peptides, protein-based masking domains with affinity for the paratope have been developed, including anti-idiotypic Affibodies, cross-masked scFvs, and the proTriTAC platform. Together, these paradigms demonstrate the feasibility of conditional antibody activation, but each has trade-offs with respect to masking efficiency, unmasking kinetics, immunogenicity, manufacturability, and broad applicability across antibody formats. , 
Accordingly, there remains an unmet need for novel masking domains that confer robust steric occlusion, are amenable to efficient disease-specific unmasking, have low immunogenic potential, and integrate seamlessly with diverse antibody scaffolds without compromising developability or pharmacokinetic integrity. Insulin-like growth factor II (IGF-II) is a compact, structurally defined ligand in the insulin/IGF superfamily whose mature form is a ~ 7.5 kDa single-chain peptide in which the A and B domains form the core structural framework, are connected by the C domain, and are stabilized by three intramolecular disulfide bonds. These features support its use as a relatively small steric masking element when positioned near antibody variable domains. In our design, IGF-II was configured in split form, with the A-chain and B-chain placed separately on the antibody heavy and light chains, respectively, via protease-cleavable linkers, to reconstitute a compact disulfide-linked steric mask near the Fab. This configuration was intended to sterically hinder antigen engagement when the masking domain was positioned proximal to the paratope, while allowing activity to be restored following protease-mediated removal of the mask. Biologically, IGF-II plays its most prominent roles during fetal and embryonic growth and development, whereas its functions postnatally are more limited and context-dependent, supporting the rationale that its use as an engineered masking domain may have a reduced likelihood of perturbing normal adult physiology. Importantly, the binding interfaces through which IGF-II engages IGF1R, IGF2R, and the insulin receptor (IR) have been well defined by structural and biochemical studies. , These well-characterized receptor-binding determinants provided a clear basis for rational engineering, allowing receptor engagement to be selectively abolished through targeted substitutions while preserving the overall structural integrity of the IGF-II domain.
The design of the protease-cleavable linker, including the protease substrate sequence and flanking spacer elements, is an important determinant of conditional activation. MMP2/9 are attractive prototype protease triggers because they are commonly upregulated in tumors and inflamed tissues and have been widely used in protease-activatable designs. , MMP3 and uPA also represent relevant protease environments for conditional activation because both participate in extracellular matrix remodeling, tumor invasion, and inflammatory tissue remodeling. , More broadly, protease substrate selection is guided by compatibility with disease-relevant protease activity, evidence of efficient cleavage, suitability for incorporation into engineered fusion proteins, and minimal disruption of masking geometry and antibody developability. In this context, PLGLAG serves as an example of a short, well-established MMP2/9-cleavable substrate suitable for incorporation into protease-activatable biologics. Likewise, the design of flanking spacer elements is important for balancing masking strength, protease-dependent unmasking, and overall molecular stability. Whereas excessively long or highly flexible spacers may reduce masking efficiency by permitting transient target access, overly short or rigid spacers may impair protein folding, expression, or accessibility of the cleavage site. Flexible GGGGS-based spacers are therefore commonly used to provide conformational freedom for protease access while maintaining effective steric occlusion. , 
In this work, we leveraged the structural properties and engineering tractability of IGF-II to generate an optimized, receptor-silent IGF-II masking domain that provided potent, protease-dependent conditional masking across multiple therapeutic antibodies. By integrating tailored protease-cleavable linkers with IGF-II-mediated steric shielding, this platform enhanced target selectivity, widened therapeutic windows, and improved the safety profiles of therapeutic antibodies.

## Materials and methods

## Antibody expression and purification
Gene fragments encoding the antibody heavy and light chains were synthesized by IDT (Coralville, IA) and cloned into the pcDNA3.4 expression vector (Thermo Fisher Scientific, A14697). The plasmids were transiently co-transfected into Expi293F cells using the ExpiFectamine™ 293 transfection kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, A14525) at a heavy:light chain molar ratio of 1:3. After 5 days of incubation at 37°C, antibodies secreted into the culture supernatant were purified by Protein A affinity chromatography using a MabSelect SuRe column (Cytiva, 29,049,104), followed by buffer exchange using a desalting column. Protein concentrations were determined by UV absorbance at 280 nm using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific).

## Protease digestion
Recombinant human proteases MMP2 (R&D Systems, 902-MP), MMP3 (R&D Systems, 513-MP), and urokinase-type plasminogen activator (uPA) (R&D Systems, 1310-SE) were activated according to the manufacturers’ instructions and then incubated with the test antibodies at 37°C overnight. Following digestion, samples were analyzed by reducing SDS-PAGE using 4–12% Bis-Tris gels (Thermo Fisher Scientific, NP0322BOX) to resolve the heavy-chain and light-chain bands. The gels were stained with SimpleBlue SafeStain (Thermo Fisher Scientific, LC6060) and recorded using a chemiluminescent Western blot imager (Azure, 300).

## ELISA binding assays
Recombinant human TNFα (R&D Systems, 210-TA), HER2 (R&D Systems, 10,126-ER), IL-1β (R&D Systems, 201-LB), VEGF (R&D Systems, 293-VE), or IGF2R (R&D Systems, 6418-GR) was coated at 1 μg/mL in Dulbecco’s phosphate-buffered saline DPBS (pH 7.2) onto MaxiSorp 96-well plates. After blocking, serial dilutions of test antibodies were added and incubated for 2 h at room temperature with shaking. Plates were washed four times with wash buffer, followed by incubation with an HRP-conjugated anti-human IgG secondary antibody (Jackson ImmunoResearch, 109–036-098) for detection. After an additional four washes, TMB substrate (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 34029) was added for color development. The absorbance values were measured at 450 nm using a SpectraMax i3X plate reader (Molecular Devices).

## HEK-Blue TNFα and IL-1β reporter assays
HEK-Blue TNFα reporter assays were performed using HEK-Blue TNFα cells (InvivoGen, hkb-tnfdmyd), which expressed TNFα receptor complexes and an NF-κB/AP-1-inducible secreted embryonic alkaline phosphatase (SEAP) reporter. HEK-Blue IL-1β reporter assays were performed using HEK-Blue IL-1β cells (InvivoGen, hkb-il1bv2), which expressed IL-1 receptor complexes and the same NF-κB/AP-1-inducible SEAP reporter. For both assays, 5 × 104 reporter cells were seeded per well in 96-well plates and treated with serial dilutions of test antibodies in the presence of either 3 ng/mL recombinant human TNFα (R&D Systems, 210-TA) or 1 ng/mL recombinant human IL-1β (R&D Systems, 201-LB). Following overnight incubation, the SEAP activity was quantified using a Quanti-Blue detection kit (InvivoGen, rep-qbs).

## SK-BR-3 cell viability assay
SK-BR-3 cells (ATCC, HTB-30) were seeded at 3,000 cells per well in 96-well plates and treated with serial dilutions of test antibodies. After 6 days of incubation, the cell viability was quantified using the CellTiter-Glo luminescent cell viability assay (Promega, G7571). The luminescence values were read on a SpectraMax i3X plate reader (Molecular Devices).

## HUVEC cell proliferation assay
Human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs; Lonza, C2519A) were seeded at 10,000 cells per well in 96-well plates in serum-free EBM medium (Lonza, CC-3121). Cells were treated with serial dilutions of test antibodies in the presence of 25 ng/mL recombinant human VEGF (R&D Systems, 293-VE). After 3 days, HUVEC proliferation was quantified using the CellTiter-Glo luminescent cell viability assay (Promega, G7571). The luminescence values were read on a SpectraMax i3X plate reader (Molecular Devices).

## VEGF sequestration assay
SK-BR-3 cells (ATCC, HTB-30) were seeded at 10,000 cells per well in 96-well plates and treated with serial dilutions of test antibodies. After 24 h, culture supernatants were collected and free (unbound) VEGF secreted by SK-BR-3 cells was quantified using the Human VEGF DuoSet ELISA kit (R&D Systems, DY293B).

## ADCC and ADCP reporter assays
Antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity (ADCC) reporter assays were performed using Jurkat-Lucia™ NFAT-CD16 cells (InvivoGen, jktl-nfat-cd16), which express CD16A (FcγRIIIa) and an NFAT-inducible Lucia luciferase reporter. Antibody-dependent cellular phagocytosis (ADCP) reporter assays were performed using Jurkat-Lucia™ NFAT-CD32 cells (InvivoGen, jktl-nfat-cd32), which express CD32A (FcγRIIa) and the same NFAT-inducible reporter. For both assays, 5 × 104 reporter cells and 2.5 × 104 SK-BR-3 target cells were co-cultured per well in 96-well plates and treated with serial dilutions of test antibodies. After overnight incubation, luciferase activity was quantified using a Quanti-Luc detection kit (InvivoGen, rep-qlc1). The luminescence values were read on a SpectraMax i3X plate reader (Molecular Devices).

## Collagen antibody induced arthritis model
The study was performed by BioMedcode Hellas SA, a contract research organization (CRO), at 34 Al. Fleming, 16,672 Vari, Athens, Greece. All animal procedures were conducted only after protocol review and approval by the Directorate of Agricultural and Veterinary Policy (DAVP) of the Attica Region, Hellenic Republic, in accordance with Presidential Decree No. 56/2013 (Government Gazette No. A’ 106), which implements Directive 2010/63/EU in Greece (approval license protocol no. 30,388/14–01-2020). Mice were housed in a specific pathogen-free facility (12:12 light cycle, 22 ± 1°C, 60% humidity) in individual ventilated cages (5 mice/cage) on dry, dust-free granulate composed of selected local plant fibers (TierWohl) bedding. Animals had ad libitum access to standard maintenance diet and filtered/UV treated tap water. Animals were handled by trained personnel to minimize distress. The collagen antibody-induced arthritis (CAIA) study was conducted in Tg1278/TNFKO mice (Biomedcode), which lack murine TNFα but are heterozygous for multiple copies of a human TNFα transgene expressed under physiologically regulated conditions. CAIA was induced by intraperitoneal injection of 8 mg Arthritomab antibody cocktail (MD Biosciences, CIA-MAB-2C) on Day 0, followed by 10 μg lipopolysaccharide (LPS) on Day 3. On the day of LPS administration, mice were randomized into body weight- and arthritis score-matched groups (n = 5 per group) and dosed subcutaneously with test antibodies (5 mg/kg) twice weekly for 2 weeks. Arthritis severity was assessed daily in forelimbs and hindlimbs, and clinical scores were assigned on a 0–4 scale per limb (maximum total score 16) as described in Supplemental Table S1.

## Listeria infection model
This study was also performed by BioMedcode Hellas SA at 34 Al. Fleming, 16,672 Vari, Athens, Greece. All animal procedures were conducted only after protocol review and approval by the Directorate of Agricultural and Veterinary Policy (DAVP) of the Attica Region, Hellenic Republic, in accordance with Presidential Decree No. 56/2013 (Government Gazette No. A’ 106), which implements Directive 2010/63/EU in Greece (approval license protocol no. 513,761/17–07-2020). Mice were housed in a specific pathogen-free facility (12:12 light cycle, 22 ± 1°C, 60% humidity) in individual ventilated cages (5 mice/cage) on dry, dust-free granulate composed of selected local plant fibers (TierWohl) bedding. Animals had ad libitum access to standard maintenance diet and filtered/UV treated tap water. Animals were handled by trained personnel to minimize distress. Due to the use of infectious material, the study was executed in the Biosafety Level 2 facility according to the relevant safety rules and regulations. The Listeria infection study was conducted in Tg1278/TNFKO human TNFα transgenic mice (Biomedcode). TNFKO mice (Biomedcode) were included as a control to model impaired host defense due to the complete absence of functional TNFα. Infection was established by intraperitoneal inoculation with 1 × 104 CFU Listeria monocytogenes. Two hours prior to infection, mice received a single intraperitoneal dose of test antibodies (0.3 mg/kg). Survival was monitored daily for 14 days. Eight animals were assigned to each treatment group, and survival rates were calculated over the study period.

## Statistical analysis
Assay readouts (raw or normalized) were plotted versus the log-transformed concentrations of test articles. EC50/ IC50 values were determined by fitting the data using a four-parameter logistic (4PL) sigmoidal dose–response model in GraphPad Prism 10.2.3 (GraphPad Software). Where applicable, 95% confidence intervals for the fitted EC50/ IC50 values were reported.

## Results

## Masking an anti-TNFα antibody by IGF-II-based masking domain
The IGF-II-masked antibody incorporated an IGF-II-based masking domain connected to the Fab via protease-cleavable linkers, engineered as N-terminal fusions to the antibody chains ( ). Specifically, the heavy chain contained the IGF-II A-chain followed by a protease substrate sequence (e.g., the MMP2/9 motif PLGLAG), flanked by flexible GGGGS linkers and fused to the N-terminus of the antibody heavy chain. In parallel, the light chain contained the IGF-II B-chain followed by protease substrate sequence, likewise flanked by GGGGS linkers and fused to the N-terminus of the antibody light chain ( ). To produce the masked antibody, the heavy- and light-chain expression constructs were co-transfected into Expi293 cells, and the secreted product was purified by Protein A affinity chromatography. In the assembled antibody, disulfide-mediated association between the IGF-II A- and B-chains formed a steric barrier that reduced Fab accessibility to the target antigen, thereby conferring the masking effect. Upon exposure to the cognate protease, cleavage at the engineered substrate sequence released the IGF-II-based masking domain and enabled unmasking to restore antigen binding ( ).

 An anti-TNFα antibody, ADAH2×L1, was used to assess the feasibility of attenuating antigen binding using an IGF-II-based masking domain. Protease substrate sequences, PLGLAG (MMP2 and MMP9), RPKPVEVWRK (MMP3), and LSGRSDNH (uPA), were engineered into the ADAH2×L1 IgG1 antibody together with native IGF-II chain sequences, generating the masked antibodies IGF-II-MMP2/9-ADAH2×L1, IGF-II-MMP3-ADAH2×L1, and IGF-II-uPA-ADAH2×L1, respectively. Binding of the masked antibodies to TNFα was quantified by ELISA. IGF-II-MMP2/9-ADAH2×L1 bound TNFα with an EC50 of 209.8 (147.7–298.0) ng/mL, compared with 11.8 (9.6–14.6) ng/mL for native ADAH2×L1, corresponding to ~18-fold reduction in apparent binding potency due to IGF-II masking ( ). Consistently, in independent assays IGF-II-MMP3-ADAH2×L1 showed ~4-fold reduction (EC50 107.6 (58.4–198.5) ng/mL for IGF-II-MMP3-ADAH2×L1 vs. 26.5 (17.2–41.0) ng/mL for ADAH2×L1), and IGF-II-uPA-ADAH2×L1 showed ~13-fold reduction (EC50 578.5 (498.7–671.0) ng/mL for IGF-II-uPA-ADAH2×L1 vs. 43.9 (34.1–56.7) ng/mL for ADAH2×L1) ( and Supplemental Figure S1A).

 In addition to antigen binding, we assessed functional neutralization of TNFα using a TNFα-driven HEK-Blue reporter assay. The native ADAH2×L1 antibody potently inhibited TNFα-induced reporter gene expression with an IC50 of 4.8 (3.4–6.8) ng/mL, whereas the IGF-II-MMP2/9-ADAH2×L1 masked antibody exhibited an IC50 of 243.4 (173.1–342.4) ng/mL, corresponding to ~50-fold loss in neutralization potency ( ). Similarly, IGF-II-MMP3-ADAH2×L1 showed ~44-fold reduction in potency (IC50 185.0 (96.7–353.9) ng/mL vs. 4.2 (2.4–7.4) ng/mL for ADAH2×L1), and IGF-II-uPA-ADAH2×L1 showed ~52-fold reduction (IC50 210.4 (117.4–377.2) ng/mL vs. 4.1 (1.7–9.5) ng/mL for ADAH2×L1) ( and Supplemental Figure S1B).

## Unmasking of IGF-II-masked antibody via proteolytic mask removal
The masked antibodies were subjected to protease digestion to determine whether antigen binding and TNFα neutralization could be restored following removal of the IGF-II-based masking domain. IGF-II-MMP2/9-ADAH2×L1 was incubated with MMP2, and the heavy and light chains before and after cleavage were analyzed by reducing SDS-PAGE. Following MMP2 treatment, both chains migrated as slightly lower-molecular weight bands relative to the uncleaved masked antibody and were comparable in size to the corresponding chains of native ADAH2×L1 ( ), consistent with efficient removal of the IGF-II masking domain.
Consistent with this biochemical shift, MMP2-cleaved IGF-II-MMP2/9-ADAH2×L1 bound TNFα with an EC50 of 10.5 (7.7–14.3) ng/mL by ELISA, essentially matching native ADAH2×L1 (EC50 ~11.8 (9.6–14.6) ng/mL) and markedly improved relative to the uncleaved masked antibody (EC50 ~209.8 (147.7–298.0) ng/mL) ( ). Similarly, in the TNFα-driven HEK-Blue reporter assay, the cleaved antibody inhibited TNFα-induced reporter gene expression with an IC50 of 11.2 (7.9–15.9) ng/mL, approaching the potency of native ADAH2×L1 (IC50 ~4.8 (3.4–6.8) ng/mL) and representing a substantial recovery compared with the uncleaved masked antibody (IC50 ~243.4 (173.1–342.4) ng/mL) ( ).
Similarly, the IGF-II masking domain was removed from IGF-II-MMP3-ADAH2×L1 upon MMP3 digestion, as indicated by the expected decreases in the apparent molecular weights of the heavy and light chains on reducing SDS-PAGE ( ). Consistent with unmasking, MMP3 cleavage restored TNFα binding potency, improving the ELISA EC50 to ~36.1 (20.1–64.8) ng/mL compared with ~107.6 (58.4–198.5) ng/mL for the masked antibody ( ). TNFα neutralization was also substantially recovered in the HEK-Blue reporter assay, with an IC50 of ~18.8 (9.6–36.9) ng/mL after cleavage versus ~185.0 (96.7–353.9) ng/mL before cleavage ( ).
Likewise, uPA treatment removed the IGF-II masking domain from IGF-II-uPA-ADAH2×L1 (Supplemental Figure S1C), accompanied by marked recovery of both TNFα binding (EC50 67.2 (63.6–71.0) ng/mL after cleavage vs. 578.5 (498.7–671.0) ng/mL before cleavage) (Supplemental Figure S1A) and TNFα neutralization (IC50 6.5 (3.0–14.2) ng/mL after cleavage vs. 210.4 (117.4–377.2) ng/mL before cleavage) (Supplemental Figure S1B).

## Dual-protease “OR-gate” design enabled flexible modulation
Beyond incorporating an IGF-II masking domain cleavable by a single protease, our design also enabled installation of different protease substrate sequences on the heavy and light chains, allowing the IGF-II masking domain to be cleaved from the heavy chain and/or light chain by distinct proteases. As an example, we generated IGF-II-MMP2/9-MMP3-ADAH2×L1, in which the MMP2/9 substrate sequence was placed between the IGF-II A-chain and the ADAH2×L1 heavy chain, whereas the MMP3 substrate sequence was placed between the IGF-II B-chain and the ADAH2×L1 light chain (Supplemental Figure S2A).
Consistent with the added masking domains, IGF-II-MMP2/9-MMP3-ADAH2×L1 displayed higher apparent molecular weight heavy and light chain bands than native ADAH2×L1 on reducing SDS-PAGE (Supplemental Figure S2B). Following MMP2 digestion, the heavy chain shifted to a lower-molecular weight band while the light chain remained unchanged, indicating selective detachment of the IGF-II masking domain from the heavy chain. In contrast, MMP3 digestion left the heavy chain unchanged while the majority of the light chain shifted to a lower-molecular weight band, consistent with selective cleavage on the light chain. Digestion with both MMP2 and MMP3 resulted in shifts of the majority of both heavy and light chain bands to sizes comparable to those of native ADAH2×L1 (Supplemental Figure S2B).
We next assessed TNFα neutralization after unmasking by the different protease treatments. The masked IGF-II-MMP2/9-MMP3-ADAH2×L1 antibody exhibited an IC50 of 216.2 ng/mL, representing ~48-fold reduction in potency relative to native ADAH2×L1 (IC50 4.5 ng/mL) (Supplemental Figure S2C). MMP2-mediated cleavage (removing the mask from the heavy chain) substantially restored neutralization activity (IC50 18.0 ng/mL), with potency comparable to digestion with both MMP2 and MMP3 (IC50 ~12.2 ng/mL). MMP3-mediated cleavage (removing the mask from the light chain) yielded a more modest recovery (IC50 40.7 ng/mL) (Supplemental Figure S2C), likely due to incomplete MMP3 cleavage under these conditions (Supplemental Figure S2B). Together, these data supported the feasibility of OR-gate activation in this platform and underscore a unique advantage of the split IGF-II masking strategy, namely, the ability to engineer-independent protease control of the heavy- and light-chain masking elements.

## Efficacy of a masked anti-TNFα antibody in a CAIA model
The efficacy of the masked anti-TNFα antibody in suppressing inflammation was evaluated in CAIA model using Tg1278/TNFKO mice, which lacked murine TNFα but were heterozygous for multiple copies of a human TNFα transgene. The study was performed by BioMedcode in Greece, following prior protocol review and approval by the Directorate of Agricultural and Veterinary Policy (DAVP) of the Attica Region. CAIA was induced by administration of an anti-collagen monoclonal antibody cocktail followed by lipopolysaccharide (LPS). This model recapitulates key pathological features of rheumatoid arthritis (RA), including inflammation, pannus formation, and bone erosion, and has been reported to be TNFα-dependent based on amelioration of disease by anti-TNFα antibodies. 
The efficacy of the masked anti-TNFα antibody IGF-II-MMP2/9-ADAH2×L1 was evaluated alongside two reference anti-TNFα antibodies, adalimumab and infliximab, in the CAIA model. The parental (unmasked) ADAH2×L1 antibody was an adalimumab variant with comparable binding affinity, as determined by bio-layer interferometry (KD 3.1 nM for ADAH2×L1 vs. 4.5 nM for adalimumab). Consistent with similar binding, ADAH2×L1 showed comparable potency to adalimumab in neutralizing TNFα activity in the HEK-Blue TNFα reporter assay (IC50 8.6 (6.1–12.1) ng/mL for ADAH2×L1 vs. 12.1 (9.1–16.1) ng/mL for adalimumab; Supplemental Figure S3).
Following CAIA induction, PBS-treated mice developed progressively worsening arthritis over the two-week study period ( ). In contrast, treatment with IGF-II-MMP2/9-ADAH2×L1, adalimumab, or infliximab (5 mg/kg) significantly ameliorated disease with broadly similar response kinetics. By Day 14 post-dose, IGF-II-MMP2/9-ADAH2×L1, adalimumab, and infliximab significantly reduced arthritis scores by 40%, 49%, and 59%, respectively, relative to the PBS group ( ). Throughout the study, all treatment groups maintained normal food intake and stable body weights ( ). Together, these data indicated that the IGF-II-masked anti-TNFα antibody retained in vivo efficacy comparable to unmasked anti-TNFα antibodies in blocking TNFα-driven arthritis in the CAIA model.

 

## Susceptibility to opportunistic infection following treatment with a masked anti-TNFα antibody in a Listeria infection model
TNFα is a proinflammatory cytokine that plays a critical role in activating innate host defenses against bacterial, fungal, and viral infections. Accordingly, TNFα blockade is associated with serious on-target toxicities, including severe infections and an increased risk of malignancy. , Listeriosis, caused by Listeria monocytogenes, is a well-recognized opportunistic infection in patients receiving anti-TNFα therapies. In our model-establishment study, intraperitoneal challenge with 1 × 104 CFU L. monocytogenes resulted in 87.5% mortality in TNFKO mice (1/8 survival), whereas all Tg1278/TNFKO mice (human TNFα transgene on a TNFα- knockout background) survived through Day 14 (8/8 survival) ( ). These results confirmed successful establishment of a model suitable for interrogating TNFα-dependent host defense against infection. This study was likewise performed by BioMedcode in Greece, following prior protocol review and approval by the Directorate of Agricultural and Veterinary Policy (DAVP) of the Attica Region.

 Using this Tg1278/TNFKO Listeria infection model, we assessed susceptibility to opportunistic infection following treatment with the masked anti-TNFα antibody IGF-II-MMP2/9-ADAH2×L1 and two reference anti-TNFα antibodies. Mice received a single intraperitoneal dose (0.3 mg/kg) of antibody 2 hours prior to intraperitoneal infection with L. monocytogenes. Treatment with adalimumab resulted in 100% lethality by Day 7, and infliximab resulted in 50% lethality by Day 14 ( ), consistent with substantial impairment of host defense. In contrast, mice treated with IGF-II-MMP2/9-ADAH2×L1 exhibited 100% survival through the end of the monitoring period, indicating that, at the dose tested, the masked anti-TNFα antibody did not measurably compromise host defense against L. monocytogenes infection.

## Optimization of the IGF-II masking domain to abrogate binding to IGF-II receptors
IGF-II is a potent mitogenic growth factor, although its growth-promoting function is largely restricted to embryonic development. To minimize potential on-target toxicities associated with using IGF-II as an antibody masking domain, we optimized the IGF-II moiety by abrogating its binding to IGF receptors, including the type I (IGF1R) and type II (IGF2R) IGF receptors and the insulin receptor (IR), through mutations at residues critical for receptor engagement. , Specifically, we introduced a V43L substitution in the IGF-II A-chain and a Y27A substitution in the IGF-II B-chain to generate a receptor-silent IGF-II masking domain ( ), termed IGF-II-s. The resulting IGF-II-s-MMP2/9-ADAH2×L1 antibody, engineered with this silent masking domain, showed no detectable binding to the high-affinity IGF2R receptor in an ELISA-based binding assay ( ), whereas the corresponding antibody containing native IGF-II (IGF-II-MMP2/9-ADAH2×L1) exhibited concentration-dependent binding. Collectively, these results confirmed that the V43L/Y27A substitutions effectively silenced IGF-II receptor engagement.

 Binding of the masked antibodies to TNFα was quantified by ELISA. IGF-II-s-MMP2/9-ADAH2×L1 bound TNFα with an EC50 of 2965 (2343–3751) ng/mL, compared with 13.8 (10.3–18.7) ng/mL for native ADAH2×L1, corresponding to an approximately 214-fold reduction in apparent binding potency due to masking ( ). Notably, IGF-II-s masking was nearly 9-fold more effective than native IGF-II masking, as IGF-II-MMP2/9-ADAH2×L1 exhibited an EC50 of 337.6 (292.1–390.1) ng/mL.
Consistent with the ELISA results, in the TNFα-driven HEK-Blue reporter assay, IGF-II-s-MMP2/9-ADAH2×L1 showed a nearly 7000-fold reduction in TNFα neutralization potency (IC50 38,420.0 (7793–189404) ng/mL vs 5.5 (3.4–9.0) ng/mL for ADAH2×L1) ( ). In contrast, IGF-II-MMP2/9-ADAH2×L1 with the native IGF-II masking domain displayed a more modest reduction (IC50 231.4 (173.4–308.9) ng/mL, ~42-fold vs ADAH2×L1), further supporting the stronger masking conferred by the receptor-silent IGF-II-s domain.
Size-exclusion chromatography (SEC) showed that IGF-II-s-MMP2/9-ADAH2×L1 eluted as a single, monodisperse peak with an apparent molecular weight slightly larger than ADAH2×L1, consistent with addition of the IGF-II-s masking domain and protease-cleavable linker (~15.5 kDa) ( ). No appreciable secondary peaks were observed, indicating minimal aggregation or degradation.

## Broad applicability of a receptor-silent IGF-II masking domain across multiple therapeutic antibodies
The applicability of the receptor-silent IGF-II masking domain was evaluated across multiple therapeutic antibodies. To this end, IGF-II-s-MMP2/9-Tras was generated by fusing the IGF-II-s masking domain to trastuzumab (Tras) through an MMP2/9-cleavable linker. Consistent with the addition of the masking domain, IGF-II-s-MMP2/9-Tras eluted as a higher apparent molecular-weight peak by SEC ( ) and displayed higher-molecular-weight heavy- and light-chain bands on reducing SDS-PAGE compared with native Tras ( ). Following MMP2 digestion, both chains shifted to lower-molecular-weight bands that migrated only slightly above those of Tras ( ), consistent with proteolytic removal of the masking domain. IGF-II-s-MMP2/9-Tras bound HER2 with an EC50 of 4885.0 (3897.0–6123.0) ng/mL, compared with 204.9 (143.3–292.9) ng/mL for native Tras, representing an approximately 24-fold reduction in apparent binding potency due to masking ( ). Importantly, MMP2-mediated cleavage substantially restored HER2 binding (EC50 392.4 (279.5–550.9) ng/mL), demonstrating protease-dependent unmasking of the antibody.

 Functionally, in a cell viability assay, the masked IGF-II-s-MMP2/9-Tras showed little to no activity in reducing the viability of HER2-positive SK-BR-3 cells, whereas native Tras inhibited cell viability with an IC50 of 50.2 ng/mL ( ). Importantly, MMP2-mediated removal of the masking domain largely restored activity, with the unmasked antibody inhibiting SK-BR-3 viability at an IC50 of 63.7 (42.1–109.5) ng/mL.
Consistent with antigen masking, in an ADCC reporter assay, IGF-II-s-MMP2/9-Tras exhibited an approximately 14-fold reduction in potency (EC50 171.6 (137.6–218.9) ng/mL vs 12.6 (9.1–17.3) ng/mL for Tras) in promoting engagement of SK-BR-3 target cells with effector cells and activating CD16a (FcγRIIIa)-dependent reporter gene expression ( ). Similarly, in an ADCP reporter assay, IGF-II-s-MMP2/9-Tras showed an approximately 14-fold reduction in potency (EC50 502.2 (464.5–542.8) ng/mL vs 35.0 (29.7–41.7) ng/mL for Tras) relative to Tras ( ).
The applicability of the receptor-silent IGF-II masking domain was further evaluated using the anti-IL-1β antibody TAVO103A by generating IGF-II-s-MMP2/9-TAVO103A. Masking resulted in an approximately 14-fold reduction in IL-1β binding potency for IGF-II-s-MMP2/9-TAVO103A (EC50 130.2 (106.2–160.7) ng/mL) relative to unmasked TAVO103A (EC50 9.3 (6.5–13.2) ng/mL) ( ). Consistently, in an IL-1β-driven HEK-Blue reporter assay, IGF-II-s-MMP2/9-TAVO103A exhibited an approximately 34-fold reduction in IL-1β neutralization potency (IC50 78.4 (67.9–90.1) ng/mL vs 2.3 (1.9–2.7) ng/mL for TAVO103A) ( ).

 As a final example, the receptor-silent IGF-II masking domain was applied to the anti-VEGF antibody bevacizumab (Bev) to generate IGF-II-s-MMP2/9-Bev. Masking reduced VEGF binding potency by approximately 37-fold, with IGF-II-s-MMP2/9-Bev exhibiting an EC50 of 229.8 (196.9–270.2) ng/mL compared with 6.2 (2.9–10.2) ng/mL for Bev ( ). Functionally, masking nearly abolished the ability of IGF-II-s-MMP2/9-Bev to sequester VEGF secreted by SK-BR-3 cells ( ), whereas Bev showed potent VEGF sequestration activity (IC50 74.2 (47.4–119.2) ng/mL). In addition, IGF-II-s-MMP2/9-Bev demonstrated an approximately 46-fold reduction in potency for neutralizing VEGF-driven HUVEC proliferation (IC50 4.6 (2.9–9.2) μg/mL) relative to Bev (IC50 0.1 (0.04–0.30) μg/mL) ( ).

 In conclusion, the IGF-II-s masking architecture was applied to anti-HER2 (Tras), anti–IL-1β, and anti-VEGF (Bev) antibodies with potent masking of binding and functional activities that could be reversed upon MMP2 cleavage. These consistent results across multiple antibody classes demonstrated the plug-and-play nature of the IGF-II-s masking platform.

## Discussion
Systemic target engagement remains a central limitation for many antibody therapeutics, particularly when the intended target also plays important physiologic roles in healthy tissues. Conditional activation strategies aim to decouple efficacy at diseased sites from systemic liabilities by restricting active target binding to locations enriched in disease-associated cues. In this study, we fused split IGF-II A- and B-chain elements to the N-termini of antibody heavy and light chains, respectively, via protease-cleavable linkers to create an intramolecular, disulfide-stabilized steric barrier that reduces Fab accessibility until proteolytic cleavage removes the masking domain. Across multiple antibody specificities, the IGF-II platform enabled efficient masking and rapid restoration of function following cleavage, supporting its potential as a broadly transferable conditional activation modality.
A central requirement for antibody masking platforms was strong pre-activation suppression without compromising antibody integrity or post-cleavage potency. The IGF-II domain provided several attributes that could support this balance: it was compact, structurally defined, and disulfide-constrained, features that could promote predictable folding and consistent steric occlusion when positioned proximal to the variable domains. Importantly, masking generalized across distinct antibodies and assay formats, including ligand neutralization, cell viability, and Fc-effector readouts (ADCC/ADCP reporter assays). In parallel, proteolytic cleavage restored antigen binding and functional activity across multiple protease activities (MMP2/9, MMP3, and uPA), suggesting that the rigid IGF-II fold and linker geometry maintained the cleavage site in a solvent-exposed configuration that was permissive to protease access. This breadth contrasted with affinity-based masking paradigms, which often required antibody-specific paratope binders or mimotopes whose affinity and kinetics must be tuned for each antibody. In comparison, a steric masking domain that relied primarily on geometry and proximity rather than paratope complementarity was inherently more “plug-and-play” across antibodies and formats.
Relative to other steric masking elements, IGF-II was substantially smaller than bulky domains such as LAP, albumin and XTEN. In addition, IGF-II was derived from a human protein that could have fewer immunogenicity and developability concerns than certain engineered non-human motifs (e.g., coiled-coils, CaM-CBP clamps and XTEN), although the immunogenicity risk of this format remains to be formally evaluated, particularly with respect to engineered IGF-II variants and fusion junctions. Future studies should assess this risk through approaches such as in silico T-cell epitope prediction, in vitro immunogenicity assays using human donor immune cells, and relevant preclinical evaluation where appropriate. One additional limitation of this study is that the thiol formation chemistry and disulfide-bonding pattern of the IGF-II masking domain were not directly characterized. Future liquid chromatography-mass spectroscopy-based analyses would be useful to clarify cysteine capping, disulfide connectivity, and possible molecular heterogeneity in this format.
Within steric masking paradigms, linker architecture is frequently a dominant determinant of masking efficiency and protease accessibility. Highly flexible or overly long linkers may permit transient exposure of the paratope, resulting in partial masking, whereas overly short or rigid linkers could improve occlusion, but risk perturbing variable-domain folding or compromising expression and stability. The current data suggested that the present linker architecture achieved a favorable balance between masking strength and protease accessibility; however, cleavage-site length, local flexibility, and residues flanking the protease motif remain important variables for further optimizing the balance between pre-cleavage suppression and post-cleavage recovery of activity. Such optimization could be antibody- and epitope-dependent and could be especially important for applications requiring maximal pre-cleavage suppression.
Protease-triggered activation is attractive for both oncology and inflammatory indications because proteolysis is a hallmark of tumor invasion, stromal remodeling, and chronic inflammation. In tumors, matrix remodeling and invasion are frequently associated with elevated metalloproteinases, serine proteases, and other proteolytic activities derived from tumor cells, stroma, and infiltrating immune cells. , In autoimmune and inflammatory diseases, proteases can also be elevated in affected tissues (e.g., synovium in arthritis) due to chronic leukocyte infiltration and tissue remodeling. , Our demonstration of efficient masking and unmasking across multiple proteases suggested that the platform could be adapted to diverse protease triggers. However, while in vitro protease treatment robustly restored activity, the kinetics and completeness of unmasking under physiologic protease activity in vivo remained to be quantified. Indeed, although tumors and inflamed tissues often exhibit increased protease activity, the identity and abundance of specific proteases can vary with histology, disease stage, immune context, and prior therapies. Accordingly, quantitative protease profiling in relevant disease settings should guide trigger selection tailored to indication, tissue compartment, and disease stage. In addition, cleavage specificity and the potential for off-site activation (e.g., during wound healing or systemic inflammation) should be assessed using broader protease panels and appropriate in vivo contexts characterized by elevated proteolysis.
Heterogeneity in protease expression can limit the robustness of single-protease designs if the chosen trigger protease is limiting or functionally inhibited at the disease site. A notable feature of our platform was the ability to install distinct substrate motifs on the heavy and light chains (e.g., IGF-II-MMP2/9-MMP3-ADAH2×L1), enabling unmasking by different proteases. This architecture functioned as a practical “OR” logic gate, in which cleavage of either chain could relax steric occlusion and restore activity, while complete masking domain removal by dual cleavage could further enhance activation. Such dual-input logic could improve robustness in complex microenvironments where one protease was scarce but another was abundant, increasing the probability of effective unmasking across heterogeneous patient populations. More broadly, multi-input activation could be tuned by selecting protease substrates matched to a given indication (e.g., metalloproteinases in tumor stroma and inflamed joints, serine proteases in specific tumor contexts), potentially improving both selectivity and activation efficiency.
Although IGF-II’s physiologic roles as a mitogenic growth factor are most prominent during fetal development and its postnatal functions are more context-dependent, its use as a masking domain may raise safety considerations, particularly unintended engagement of IGF receptors or related pathways. To mitigate this risk, we engineered a receptor-silent IGF-II variant (IGF-II-s) by introducing targeted substitutions at residues implicated in receptor binding. The resulting IGF-II-s-containing antibodies showed no detectable binding to IGF2R in the assay used. In addition, prior structural and mutational studies indicated that residues V43 and Y27 were critical for IGF-II engagement with IGF1R and IR, and substitutions at these positions markedly reduce receptor binding. Together, these observations suggested that IGF-II-s could pose minimal safety risk related to IGF receptor biology. Notably, IGF-II-s also conferred substantially stronger masking than native IGF-II, as reflected by markedly larger reductions in TNFα binding and neutralization potency. This enhanced masking could reflect subtle changes in packing or orientation of the IGF-II domain relative to the Fab that favored more complete steric occlusion and could translate into a larger therapeutic index for conditionally activated biologics.
The therapeutic promise of conditional activation is to preserve disease-site efficacy while reducing mechanism-based systemic toxicities. We addressed this directly using two in vivo models probing complementary aspects of TNFα biology. In the CAIA model, the masked anti-TNFα construct retained efficacy comparable to reference anti-TNFα antibodies, consistent with sufficient activation within inflamed tissues to neutralize TNFα-driven pathology. In contrast, in the Listeria infection challenge model, reference anti-TNFα antibodies reduced survival under the tested conditions, whereas the masked construct did not measurably reduce survival. This divergence was consistent with the intended mechanism: systemic TNFα blockade was reduced when the antibody remained predominantly masked in circulation and non-inflamed tissues, yet local protease activity at sites of inflammation could generate an active fraction adequate for therapeutic benefit. Together, these data supported the hypothesis that IGF-II-based conditional activation could widen the therapeutic index.
One apparent disconnect between the modest reduction in binding observed in vitro and the larger in vivo separation between efficacy and off-disease functional liability may reflect the fact that neither biological activity nor in vivo outcome scales linearly with binding. In our TNFα system, masking produced a greater reduction in functional neutralization than in apparent binding in vitro, indicating that partial attenuation at the binding level can translate into a larger reduction in biological activity. In addition, the predictive value of a simple in vitro binding shift may be limited because in vivo outcome is influenced not only by intrinsic-binding potency, but also by exposure, tissue context, and the extent of local activation. Reduced target engagement prior to activation may further alter the pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic behavior of the masked antibody relative to the unmasked form, including systemic exposure, tissue distribution, and local target binding. Together, these factors may help explain how a construct showing only modest attenuation in vitro can nevertheless exhibit a larger in vivo separation between retained efficacy and reduced off-disease functional toxicity. Whether this principle extends to other targets will likely depend on target biology, local protease activity, and pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic context.
Nonetheless, additional in vivo studies, including tumor models for anti-HER2 and anti-VEGF constructs and inflammatory models for anti-IL-1β, will be needed to establish the generalizability of therapeutic index improvement. Besides, although our in vivo studies separately supported efficacy and reduced off-disease functional liability, they did not directly demonstrate both endpoints in a single integrated experimental setting. Future studies designed to assess these outcomes in tandem will therefore be important to further define the therapeutic advantage of this masking strategy. In addition, future studies incorporating uncleavable masked-antibody controls would help further clarify the extent to which residual activity reflects incomplete masking versus protease-dependent activation. From a pharmacokinetic perspective, masked antibodies could exhibit altered systemic exposure and tissue distribution relative to unmasked antibodies because target binding was reduced in healthy tissues while activation and binding could occur locally in diseased sites. Future work should therefore extend in vivo evaluations across a broader range of doses, infection burdens, and time points and incorporate pharmacokinetic/pharmacodynamic analyses that quantify circulating masked versus unmasked fractions and tissue-local activation. Such studies would strengthen mechanistic interpretation and define exposure thresholds that preserve efficacy while minimizing systemic risk.
Although not the focus of this study, the IGF-II masking strategy may be particularly impactful for modalities with narrow therapeutic windows and prominent systemic liabilities, including CD3-based T-cell engagers, immune checkpoint therapies (e.g., CTLA-4), CD47-targeting agents, and ADCs. For these modalities, reducing systemic engagement while preserving disease-site activation could mitigate cytokine release, off-tumor cytotoxicity, and on-target toxicities in healthy tissues, potentially enabling higher effective dosing or broader patient eligibility. Future work should also prioritize developability of IGF-II-s as a masking domain, including optimization of expression titers, comprehensive biophysical characterization such as thermostability, and immunogenicity risk assessment of fusion junctions and engineered IGF-II variants.
In summary, we established an engineered IGF-II-based masking domain as a compact, structurally defined steric element that enabled robust and broadly transferable conditional activation of therapeutic antibodies via protease-mediated unmasking. These findings supported continued development of IGF-II-based conditional activation as a versatile platform to improve the safety-efficacy balance of antibody biologics and potentially other high-liability immune and oncology modalities.
