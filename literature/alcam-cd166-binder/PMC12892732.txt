## Abstract

## Background
Mesenchymal stromal/stem cell (MSC) exosomes were previously shown to be effective in alleviating joint pain and degeneration in a rat model of temporomandibular joint osteoarthritis (TMJ-OA). However, the role of MSC exosomes in regulating complement activity implicated in OA inflammation remains to be elucidated. Here, we investigate the effects of MSC exosomes on the assembly of terminal complement complex, C5b-9, and its associated inflammation and matrix degradation in TMJ-OA.

## Methods
Following TMJ-OA induction, the rats received 3 weekly intra-articular injections of MSC exosomes or phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). The sham animals were given needle pricks. At 4-weeks post-treatment, tissue samples were harvested for transcriptomic profiling, micro-computed tomography (micro-CT), histology, immunohistochemistry, histomorphometry, C5b-9 enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA), and multiplex cytokine analyses. Chondrocyte cultures were used to assess the effects of MSC exosomes on complement serum-induced chondrocyte inflammation and matrix degradation. The role of exosomal CD59 on complement assembly was also evaluated through neutralization with an anti-CD59 antibody.

## Results
We observed that MSC exosomes mediated joint repair by suppressing pain and inflammation, reducing fibrosis and matrix degradation, and restoring the condylar cartilage and subchondral bone. Concurrently, there was suppressed complement activity with exosome treatment, as evidenced by the reduced levels of C5b-9 in the condylar cartilage and overlying synovium, and preferential synovial mRNA expression of complement inhibitors over effectors. These were accompanied by the decreased levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-12 (p70), IL-17 A, IFN-γ, IL-18, and MIP-3α, and increased levels of anti-inflammatory cytokines including IL-2, IL-4, and IL-10 in the synovium with exosome treatment. Using chondrocyte cultures, we attributed the suppression of OA inflammation and matrix degradation to exosomal CD59, which inhibits complement activation. Specifically, MSC exosomes reduced C5b-9 formation and complement-induced inflammatory gene expression and MMP13 secretion in chondrocytes, and these effects were abrogated upon neutralization with an anti-CD59 antibody.

## Conclusions
These findings demonstrated that MSC exosomes alleviate OA inflammation and matrix degradation by inhibiting complement activity via a CD59-dependent pathway.

## Introduction
Temporomandibular joint disorders (TMD) are among the most common orofacial disorders, with an estimated 40–75% of individuals showing at least one sign and 33% reporting at least one symptom of the TMD [ ]. Temporomandibular joint osteoarthritis (TMJ-OA) is a progressive form of TMD characterized by progressive cartilage degradation, subchondral bone erosion, synovitis and chronic pain [ , ]. Currently, there is no curative treatment for TMJ-OA, and the management focuses on conservative and minimally invasive modalities including physical therapies, non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, arthrocentesis, and intra-articular injections of hyaluronic acid [ ].
In recent years, mesenchymal stromal/stem cells (MSCs) have emerged as a promising therapy for TMJ repair [ , ]. In clinical studies, intra-articular injections of autologous bone marrow nucleated cells [ ] or fat-derived stem cells containing MSCs [ ] have been reported to improve the clinical outcomes in patients with TMJ-OA or joint derangement. Despite their therapeutic efficacy, clinical translation of such cell-based approaches remains limited due to the high cost of cell harvest, culture, and processing, as well as challenges in maintaining optimal cell viability and potency.
Recently, there has been a paradigm shift in the therapeutic mechanism of MSCs in tissue repair, moving from a focus on cellular differentiation and cell replacement to one based on trophic secretion and paracrine signaling. Among the broad spectrum of trophic factors, extracellular vesicles (EVs), particularly exosomes, have garnered interest due to their therapeutic efficacy in several preclinical animal models of injuries/diseases, such as myocardial ischemia/reperfusion (I/R) injury [ ], pulmonary hypertension [ ], corneal scarring [ ], cartilage and bone defects [ , ], as well as osteoarthritis (OA) [ ]. In these animal models, MSC exosomes reportedly alleviated the immune dysfunction and inflammation to promote tissue repair and regeneration. However, the complex interplay between MSC exosomes and the immune system, and the underlying mechanisms, remains to be fully elucidated [ ].
Despite the therapeutic potency of MSC exosomes, several challenges remain to be addressed. The therapeutic efficacy of MSC exosomes preparation is influenced by various factors, including donor attributes, cell sources, culture conditions, and isolation protocols. Immortalizing primary MSCs and establishing monoclonal MSC lines have been demonstrated as a feasible strategy to produce MSCs and their derived exosomes with enhanced scalability, consistency, and reduced heterogeneity [ ]. Previously, MYC transformation of human embryonic stem cell (hESC)-derived MSCs was reported, resulting in the generation of a clonal E1-MYC 16.3 MSC line [ ]. These immortalized E1-MYC MSCs expressed CD29, CD44, CD49a, CD49e, CD73, CD90, CD105, and CD166; lacked the expression for MHC I, HLA-DR, CD34, and CD45; and retained the ability to differentiate into chondrocytes and osteocytes. Relative to their parental cells, they grow faster and exhibit increased telomerase activity while retaining the parental karyotype, thus providing an unlimited supply of MSCs for a scalable production of MSC exosomes in a consistent and reproducible manner [ ].
The complement system is an integral component of innate immunity which serves to eliminate invading pathogens and remove damaged cells through generation of pro-inflammatory mediators such as anaphylatoxins C3 and C5a, opsonization of pathogenic surfaces, and targeted cell lysis via the formation of terminal complement complex, C5b-9, popularly known as the membrane attack complex (MAC). Clinical data have revealed significantly higher levels of complement components such as C3a and C5b-9 in the synovial fluids of OA individuals, as compared to healthy individuals [ , ]. Moreover, excessive C5b-9 deposition during OA has been reported to mediate chondrocyte lysis, while the sub-lytic C5b-9 activation induces the expression of several pro-inflammatory cytokines and matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs) [ ]. Among these, IL-1β, IL-6, IL-8, and TNF-α are the predominant pro-inflammatory cytokines driving cartilage destruction by inhibiting matrix synthesis and exacerbating matrix degradation through the secretion of MMPs [ – ]. For example, MMP13 is the key matrix-degrading enzyme that mediates the degradation of collagen and proteoglycan in articular cartilage during OA [ ]. Given the cardinal role of complement in mediating OA pathogenesis, inhibiting their assembly and activation thus represents a potential therapeutic strategy to alleviate OA inflammation and promote joint repair.
We have previously reported that MSC exosomes alleviate TMJ-OA pain and degeneration in rats [ ]. However, the immunomodulatory effects of MSC exosomes in OA and the underlying mechanisms remain to be fully elucidated. In this study, we sought to investigate if MSC exosomes exert immunomodulatory effects to promote joint repair in TMJ-OA through complement inhibition.

## Methods

## Preparation and characterization of MSC exosomes
MSC exosomes were purchased from Paracrine Therapeutics Pte Ltd, Singapore. Briefly, the exosomes were derived from an immortalized E1-MYC 16.3 hESC-derived MSCs cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s Medium (DMEM) containing 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). For exosome preparation, the conditioned medium (CM) was prepared by culturing the cells at 80% confluence in a chemically defined medium composed of DMEM supplemented with 1% non-essential amino acids, 1% glutamine, 1% insulin-transferrin-selenium-X (ITS-X), 1 mM sodium pyruvate, 0.05 mM β-mercaptoethanol, 5 ng/ml fibroblast growth factor (FGF)−2 (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and 5 ng/ml platelet-derived growth factor (PDGF)-AB (Cytolab). The CM was size fractionated by tangential flow filtration and concentrated 50 × using a membrane with a molecular weight cut-off of 100 kDa (Sartorius, Göttingen, Germany). The exosome preparation was assayed for protein concentration using the Pierce™ Coomassie Plus (Bradford) protein assay kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific), particle size distribution and concentration by ZetaView (Particle Metrix GmbH) and CD73/ecto-5´-nucleotidase activity using the PiColorLock™ Gold Phosphate Detection System (Innova Biosciences) in accordance with the Minimum Information for the Study of Extracellular Vesicles (MISEV) guidelines [ , ], and more specifically, with the identity and potency metrics proposed for MSC-sEV preparations [ , ]. The protocol for exosome preparation has been used for the preparation of more than 100 batches of exosomes with a high batch-to-batch consistency and reproducible manner. For this study, MSC exosomes (Batch AC113) were characterized to have a protein concentration of 1.053 mg/ml, particle concentration of 1.4 × 1011 particles/ml, particles of modal size of 139.29 nm (Fig. S1), CD73/NT5E activity of 22.71 ± 0.54 mU/µg protein, and CD59 concentration of 2.54 ± 0.15 ng/mg protein. The exosomes were filtered with a 0.22 μm filter (Merck Millipore, Billerica, MA, USA) and stored in a − 80 °C freezer until use.

## Animal experiments
All procedures were performed according to the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee at the National University of Singapore under protocol number: R18-1295, and reported in line with the ARRIVE guidelines 2.0. Forty-two Sprague-Dawley rats (8-week-old, female) with a mean weight of 223.2 ± 5.3 g were used in the animal study (Fig. A-B). Forty-two rats (84 TMJs, with 2 TMJs per rat) were randomly assigned to three groups: OA+PBS (14 rats), OA+Exo (14 rats) and Sham (14 rats). The rat model of TMJ-OA has been previously reported and validated [ ]. Briefly, 0.5 mg monosodium iodoacetate (MIA) was injected into the upper compartment of bilateral TMJs to induce OA. Two weeks after OA induction, OA+Exo rats received three weekly intra-articular injections of 50 µg exosomes in 50 µl PBS, while the OA+PBS rats received equivalent 50 µl PBS injections. The rats in the Sham group received only needle pricks. At 4-weeks post-treatment, animals were euthanized using carbon dioxide (CO2) and tissue samples were harvested for transcriptomic profiling, micro-computed tomography (micro-CT), histology, immunohistochemistry, histomorphometry, C5b-9 enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA), and multiplex cytokine analyses. All animals were housed in pairs under standard temperature with a 12-h light/dark cycle and allowed to move freely with access to food and water.
 

## Quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR)
Total RNA was isolated from the rat synovium tissues using the Allprep RNA isolation kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany), following the manufacturer’s instructions. For cells, total RNA was isolated using Life PureLink® RNA Mini kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The RNA was then reverse transcribed to cDNA using iScript™ reverse transcription Supermix (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Hercules, CA, USA) and then amplified using CFX Connect™ real-time PCR system (Bio-Rad) with iTaq™ Universal SYBR® Green Supermix (Bio-Rad). The list of primers for genes associated with inflammation and the complement system is shown in Table S1. The PCR cycling conditions comprised an initial denaturation at 95 °C for 30 s, followed by 40 cycles of amplification, including a 15 s denaturation at 95 °C and a 30 s extension at 60 °C. Relative mRNA expression levels were normalized against glyceraldehyde 3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH), calculated using the comparative ΔCT method [ ], and expressed as fold changes. Using the Sham group for normalization, transcriptional profiling of TMJ synovium tissues of OA + Exo and OA + PBS rats at 4-weeks post-treatment was generated using the online software - Morpheus (https://software.broadinstitute.org/morpheus/ ). Samples were hierarchically clustered using one minus the Pearson correlation coefficient as the distance metric and average linkage for both rows and columns.

## Multiplex cytokine analyses
Total protein was extracted from the rat synovium tissues using the Mammalian Cell & Tissue Extraction kit (Biovision, Milpitas, CA, USA), following the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, tissues were homogenized in the extraction buffer mix and vortexed for 5 s. Samples were centrifuged at 12,000 g for 3 min and supernatants were collected and stored at −80 °C. Following the manufacturer’s instructions, a panel of 23 cytokines including G-CSF, GM-CSF, GRO/KC, IFN-γ, IL-1α, IL-1β, IL-2, IL-4, IL-5, IL-6, IL-7, IL-10, IL-12 (p70), IL-13, IL-17 A, IL-18, M-CSF, MCP-1, MIP-1α, MIP-3α, RANTES, TNF-α, and VEGF were measured using the Bio-Plex 2200 analyzer (Bio-Rad) with the Bio-Plex® Pro Rat Cytokine 23-plex Assay Kit. Protein concentration was measured using the Pierce™ BCA Protein Assay kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific), and absorbance readings were taken at 562 nm using a microplate reader (Spark® multimode, Tecan™, Männedorf, Switzerland). The cytokine concentrations were normalized against the protein concentrations and expressed as Cytokine/Protein (pg/µg).

## ELISAs
Total protein was extracted from the rat synovium and condylar cartilage tissues using the Mammalian Cell & Tissue Extraction Kit (Biovision). For chondrocyte culture experiments, culture supernatant samples were collected. Following the manufacturer’s instructions, the concentrations of C5b-9 and MMP13 were measured using the ELISA kits (MyBiosource, San Diego, CA, USA). Absorbance readings were taken at 450 nm using a microplate reader (Spark® multimode, Tecan™). Protein concentration was measured using the Pierce™ BCA Protein Assay kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific), with absorbance readings taken at 562 nm using the microplate reader. The C5b-9 and MMP13 concentrations were then normalized against the total protein concentrations and expressed as C5b-9/Protein (pg/µg) and MMP13/Protein (pg/µg), respectively.

## Micro-CT
At 4-weeks post-treatment, animals were euthanized, and bilateral TMJs were harvested. The dissected TMJ condyles were fixed in 10% (v/v) neutral buffered formalin (pH 7.4) overnight and analyzed by a high-resolution micro-CT system (Quantum FX, Perkin-Elmer, USA). Samples were scanned coronally at 90 kV and 160 µA with a 10 μm-effective pixel size. The sagittal and top images of the condyle were reconstructed. A set of 200 coronal slides across the entire condyle was selected and CTAn version 1.13 (Bruker microCT, Kontich, Belgium) was used for measurement of the percentage of bone volume over total volume (BV/TV, %), trabecular thickness (Tb.Th, mm), trabecular separation (Tb.Sp, mm), and trabecular number (Tb.N, 1/mm).

## Histology, immunohistochemistry, and histomorphometry
Following micro-CT scanning, all fixed tissue specimens were decalcified in 30% (v/v) formic acid for 2 weeks before being processed for histology. According to standard protocol, the samples were dehydrated and embedded in paraffin for microtomy. Serial 5-µm sections were collected and stained with haematoxylin and eosin (HE) for the observation of general morphology and toluidine blue (TB) for the sulphated glycosaminoglycan (s-GAG) deposition. Using the TB-stained sections, the quality of joint repair was graded by three blinded independent observers using the Mankin scoring system, as described in Table S2, where the score of 0 indicates normal cartilage and the maximum score for degenerated cartilage is 14 [ , ]. Synovial inflammation was assessed by three independent blinded assessors using the synovial membrane inflammation scoring system (Table S3) [ ]. Histomorphometry analysis of the different regions and cartilage thickness was performed, as previously described [ ]. The condyle head was divided into three regions from the articular surface to the condyle neck: the anterior, central, and posterior regions, and condylar height and cartilage thickness were measured at each region. The percentage of cartilage thickness was calculated by dividing the cartilage thickness by the condylar height at each region. The percentage of TB-positive stained area for s-GAG in the cartilage layer was measured at 200 × magnification using the ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health (NIH), Bethesda, MD, USA). Immunohistochemistry was performed according to our standard protocol [ ] to detect C5b-9 (HM3033; 1:100; Hycult Biotech, Netherlands), α-smooth muscle actin (α-SMA) (ab7817; 1:2500; Abcam, Cambridge, MA, USA), MMP13 (ab39012; 1:1000; Abcam), IL-1β (ab9722; 1:400; Abcam), and tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-⍺) (ab6671; 1:500, Bio-Rad) using the biotin-streptavidin Lab Vision UltraVision detection system (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Semi-quantitative analysis of C5b-9, α-SMA, MMP13, IL-1β, and TNF-⍺ expression levels was performed by counting the number of positively stained cells in three random fields at 200 × magnification and calculating the percentage of positive cells. For analysis of C5b-9 expression in the synovium, the number of positively stained cells were counted at 200 × magnification and expressed as the number of positive cells per mm2 of the total synovium area.

## Isolation and culture of TMJ condylar chondrocytes
The superficial condylar cartilage layer of TMJ was dissected from 20 Sprague-Dawley rats (8-week-old, female). Tissues were washed 3 times with PBS and digested in 0.2% (w/v) collagenase I and II (Worthington, Lakewood, NJ, USA) for 4 h at 37 °C. Cells were passed through a 40 μm nylon cell strainer (Corning®, Corning, NY, USA) to obtain single cells before culturing at a density of 2 × 104 cells/cm2. The cells were maintained in DMEM/F12 supplemented with 10% FBS, 1% penicillin/streptomycin (P/S; Thermo Fisher Scientific) and 25 µg/ml ascorbic acid-2-phosphate (Sigma, St Louis, MO, USA) under a humidified atmosphere of 5% CO2 at 37 °C, with medium change every alternate day. Upon confluency, chondrocytes were dissociated and sub-cultured to passage (P) 2 for subsequent experiments.

## Complement serum treatment and CD59 neutralization
P2 chondrocytes were seeded at a density of 2 × 104 cells/cm2 and cultured for 16 h before the medium was replaced with a serum-free medium (DMEM/F12) for 4 h, and then treated with the 30% rat complement serum (CS) (Innovative Research, MI, USA) or heat-inactivated serum (HS) at 37 °C for 1 h. To inactivate the complements, HS was prepared by incubating the CS at 56 °C for 45 min [ ]. To determine the effects of varying concentrations of MSC exosomes on complement activation, chondrocytes were treated with 30% CS in the absence or presence of exosomes (1, 5 and 10 µg/ml) at 37 °C for 1 h. To investigate the role of exosomal CD59, 10 µg/ml CD59 neutralizing antibody (YTH53.1; Thermo Fisher Scientific) was added to 10 µg/ml exosomes and incubated for 15 min before the addition of 30% CS as described above. Following treatment, chondrocytes were harvested for immunofluorescence staining and gene expression analysis, and culture supernatants were collected for MMP13 ELISA.

## Immunofluorescence staining
P2 chondrocytes were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (Sigma) for 30 min, permeabilized with 0.3% Triton X-100 in PBS for 15 min, and blocked with PBS buffer containing 10% FBS and 0.1% Triton X-100 for 2 h. The cells were then incubated with primary antibodies for C5b-9 (HM3033, 1:100; Hycult Biotech) overnight at 4 °C and MMP13 (ab39012, 1:200; Abcam) for 1 h at room temperature. After incubation, cells were washed with PBS and incubated with Alexa Fluor 594 goat anti-rabbit secondary antibody (A11012, 1:1000; Thermo Fisher Scientific) and Alexa Fluor 488 goat anti-mouse secondary antibody (A11001, 1:500; Thermo Fisher Scientific) for 2 h at room temperature. After washing, nuclear staining was performed using 4´,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and cells were imaged using an inverted fluorescence microscope (DMi8, Leica Microsystems, Germany). The staining intensities of C5b-9 and MMP13 in five randomly selected fields at 200 × magnification were measured using ImageJ software (NIH) and expressed as relative mean fluorescence intensities (MFIs).

## Statistical analysis
All quantitative data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Differences between groups were determined by Student’s t-test or one-way ANOVA followed by Scheffe’s post-hoc test for normally distributed data, and Mann-Whitney U test or Kruskal-Wallis test followed by Dunn-Bonferroni post-hoc test for non-normally distributed data. The normality of data was checked using a histogram, Q-Q plot and Shapiro-Wilk test. Pearson’s correlation was used to evaluate the association between two variables, and the data were presented as r (Pearson correlation coefficient), n (sample size), and P (statistical significance level). The Corrplot R package was used to plot a correlation heatmap for visualization [ ]. To detect a difference of 1 in the synovial membrane inflammation score between the 2 groups with a power of 80% and a 2-sided alpha error of 5%, a minimum of 6 samples in each group would be needed. All statistical analyses were performed using the statistical software package IBM SPSS Statistics version 26.0 (IBM, Chicago, IL, USA) with statistical significance set as P < 0.05.

## Results

## MSC exosomes promote TMJ cartilage repair and subchondral bone restoration
At 4-weeks post-treatment, OA+Exo rats showed restoration of the TMJ condylar structure with a smoother cartilage surface, improved condylar height, lesser fibrous cartilage thickening, and minimal depletion of s-GAG, as compared to the OA+PBS rats (Fig.  C). Across all three condylar regions (anterior, central, and posterior), OA+Exo rats showed significantly greater condylar height than the OA+PBS rats, and these readings were comparable to that in the Sham rats (Fig. S2A). Relative to OA+PBS rats, OA+Exo rats also displayed reduced fibrous cartilage thickening, particularly in the posterior region (P = 0.004), consistent with the lower percentage of α-SMA+ cells (P = 0.007) (Fig. S2A). In terms of matrix deposition, OA+Exo rats displayed significantly higher percentage areal deposition of s-GAG (P < 0.001) and a reduced percentage of MMP13+ cells (P = 0.033), as compared to the OA+PBS rats (Fig. S2B). As a result, the OA+Exo rats had a Mankin score of 2.4 ± 0.7, which was significantly better than the OA+PBS rats with 4.8 ± 0.5 (P < 0.001), and was comparable to the Sham rats with 1.6 ± 0.9 (P = 0.137) (Fig. S2B).
Next, we investigated the structural changes in subchondral bone upon exosome treatment (Fig.  D). Notably, OA+Exo rats showed improved subchondral bone restoration with an increase in BV/TV of 10.1% (P = 0.03) and a decrease in Tb.Sp of 13.6% (P = 0.018) over that in OA+PBS rats (Fig.  E). On the other hand, the OA+PBS group showed significant deterioration relative to the Sham group, as evidenced by decrease in BV/TV of 9.1% (P = 0.03) and Tb.N of 8.5% (P = 0.014), and an increase in Tb.Sp of 22.2% (P = 0.004) (Fig.  E). Importantly, no statistically significant differences were detected in the bone structural parameters between OA+Exo and Sham rats, highlighting the effects of MSC exosomes on subchondral bone restoration.

## MSC exosomes attenuate complement activation and reduce inflammation in TMJ-OA
Since synovitis is the earliest indicator of OA, we examined the effects of MSC exosomes on complement activity and synovial inflammation (Fig.  A-F). Histological analysis of the synovium indicated reduced synovial inflammation upon exosome treatment, as evidenced by the significantly improved synovial membrane inflammation score in OA+Exo rats with 0.6 ± 0.5, relative to 1.8 ± 0.9 in the OA+PBS rats (P = 0.037) (Fig.  A, B). In addition, transcriptomic profiling of the synovial tissues revealed a preferential gene expression of complement inhibitors over complement effectors and pro-inflammatory cytokines with exosome treatment (Fig.  C, D). Notably, OA+Exo rats showed significantly lower expression levels of genes encoding pro-inflammatory cytokines (e.g. IL-1β, TNF-α, and IL-17 A) and complement effectors (e.g. C4, C6, and CFP), and higher expression levels of genes for complement inhibitors (e.g. CFI), as compared to the OA+PBS rats (Fig.  C, D). To further validate how exosome treatment can affect the cytokine landscape at the protein level during OA, we performed multiplex cytokine analysis of the synovial tissues. Multiplex cytokine analysis showed that OA+Exo rats exhibited significantly lower expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines (e.g. IL-12 (p70), IL-17 A, IL-18, MIP-3α, IFN-γ) and higher expression of anti-inflammatory cytokines (e.g. IL-2, IL-4, IL-10) compared to the OA+PBS rats (Fig.  A, B).
 Consistent with the reduced expression of complement effectors and pro-inflammatory cytokines with exosome treatment, reduced numbers of IL-1β+ and TNF-α+ cells in both synovium and condylar cartilage of OA+Exo rats compared to the OA+PBS rats was observed (Fig.  A-E). In addition, OA+Exo rats had lower percentages of C5b-9+ cells in the synovium (P < 0.001) and condylar cartilage (P = 0.011), as compared to OA+PBS rats, and were comparable to that of the Sham rats (Fig.  C, D). Quantitative measurements of C5b-9 further confirmed that OA+Exo rats had significantly lower levels of C5b-9 than the OA+PBS rats, in both synovium (P < 0.001) and condylar cartilage (P = 0.04), and were comparable to that of the Sham rats (Fig.  E). By Pearson’s correlation analysis, high percentages of IL-1β+ cells (r = 0.455, n = 24, P = 0.026), TNF-α+ cells (r = 0.652, n = 24, P = 0.001), and C5b-9+ cells (r = 0.413, n = 24, P = 0.045) were positively correlated with the increase in Mankin scores for OA degenerative changes (Fig. S3). High expression levels of IL-12 (p70) (r = 0.685, n = 18, P = 0.002), IL-17 A (r = 0.503, n = 18, P = 0.034), IL-18 (r = 0.528, n = 18, P = 0.024), IFN-γ (r = 0.748, n = 18, P < 0.001), MIP-1α (r = 0.478, n = 18, P = 0.045), MIP-3α (r = 0.496, n = 18, P = 0.036), and TNF-α (r = 0.626, n = 18, P = 0.005) were also positively correlated with the increase in C5b-9 measured in the TMJ synovial lysate (Fig. S4).
 

## MSC exosomes inhibit complement-induced inflammation and matrix degradation through a CD59-dependent pathway
To ascertain the effects of complement activation on chondrocytes, we isolated chondrocytes from healthy rats and initiated complement activation by treatment with the rat complement serum (Fig. S5). As revealed by immunofluorescence staining, complement serum (CS) treatment of chondrocytes induced the formation of C5b-9 (MAC) on the cell surface (Fig. S5A). We observed that C5b-9 colocalized with MMP13, suggesting that the assembly of C5b-9 induces MMP13 production by chondrocytes (Fig. S5A). Of note, significantly higher MFI levels of C5b-9 (P = 0.037) and MMP13 (P = 0.009) were observed in CS-treated chondrocytes compared to HS-treated chondrocytes (Fig. S5B). We also assayed for MMP13 release following CS treatment and confirmed significantly higher levels of secreted MMP13 (P = 0.014) and MMP13/Protein (P = 0.028) in the CS-treated chondrocytes, as compared to the HS-treated chondrocytes (Fig. S5C). This increased expression of C5b-9 and MMP13 was also concomitant with the significantly upregulated expression of pro-inflammatory genes including IL-6 (P = 0.003) and IL-8 (P = 0.006) (Fig. S5D), as determined by qRT-PCR. These results highlight the effects of C5b-9 assembly and complement activation on chondrocyte inflammation and matrix degradation.
To determine whether MSC exosomes can intervene complement-induced inflammation and matrix degradation, chondrocytes were treated with increasing concentrations of exosomes in the presence of CS (Fig.  A-D). Notably, exosome treatment reduced the assembly of C5b-9 in a dose-dependent manner, with maximum suppression observed at 10 µg/ml (P = 0.029). In parallel, the MFI of MMP13 was significantly reduced with increasing concentrations of exosomes, showing approximately a 2-fold decrease (P = 0.005) following treatment with 10 µg/ml exosomes (Fig.  B). Similarly, the levels of secreted MMP13 and MMP13/Protein were significantly reduced following treatment with MSC exosomes from 5 µg/ml (P < 0.01) (Fig.  C). Genes associated with chondrocyte inflammation, including IL-6 and IL-8, were also significantly downregulated with exosome treatment. Specifically, the expression of IL-6 mRNA was significantly downregulated following treatment with 5 µg/ml (P = 0.049) and 10 µg/ml exosomes (P = 0.009), whereas IL-8 mRNA was significantly downregulated with 10 µg/ml exosomes (P = 0.007) (Fig.  D). The findings suggest that MSC exosomes can inhibit complement assembly and activation to reduce complement-induced inflammation and matrix degradation.
 MSC exosomes have been reported to inhibit the formation of C5b-9 through their expression of CD59 [ ]. To investigate whether exosomal CD59 mediates the inhibitory effects of MSC exosomes on complement activation, a neutralizing antibody against CD59 was added prior to the addition of complement serum (Fig.  A-D). Notably, the MFIs of C5b-9 and MMP13 were significantly reduced with 10 µg/ml exosome treatment, but increased to levels comparable to those with CS treatment, upon anti-CD59 antibody treatment (Fig.  B). Similarly, the levels of secreted MMP13 (P = 0.015) and normalized MMP13/Protein (P = 0.003) were significantly reduced with exosome treatment, but increased to levels comparable to those with CS treatment in the presence of anti-CD59 antibody (Fig.  C). In line with this, the expression of pro-inflammatory cytokine IL-8 mRNA was significantly reduced following exosome treatment (P = 0.036) but increased upon anti-CD59 antibody neutralization (Fig.  D). Collectively, these results clearly demonstrate that MSC exosomes inhibit C5b-9 assembly and complement activation, thereby suppressing OA inflammation and matrix degradation, in a CD59-dependent manner.
 

## Discussion
We had previously reported that weekly intra-articular injections of human MSC exosomes effectively alleviate OA pain and degeneration in a rat model of TMJ-OA [ ]. However, the complex interplay between MSC exosomes and the immune system, and the underlying mechanisms, remains to be fully elucidated. Using a rat model of TMJ-OA, we demonstrated that MSC exosomes inhibit the assembly of terminal C5b-9 (MAC) complement complex, and associated tissue inflammation and matrix degradation, to restore the TMJ osteochondral tissues. Notably, MSC exosomes modulated the complement system by reducing the synovial mRNA expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines (e.g. IL-1B, TNF-a, and IL17A) and complement effectors (e.g. C4, C6, and CFP), while enhancing the expression of complement inhibitors (e.g. CFI). Consistent with this, the numbers of C5b-9+, IL-1β+, and TNF-α+ cells were also reduced with exosome treatment in both the synovium and cartilage. Additionally, there was a preferential upregulation of several anti-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-2, IL-4, and IL-10, and downregulation of pro-inflammatory cytokines including IL-12 (p70), IL-17A, IL-18, MIP-3α, and IFN-γ, with positive correlation between several of these pro-inflammatory cytokines and the increase in C5b-9 measured in the synovial lysate, implicating the role of complement activation in OA inflammation. By 4-weeks post-treatment, the exosome-treated animals showed effective restoration of the condylar structure, matrix deposition and subchondral bone mineralization, comparable to that of the Sham animals.
The therapeutic use of MSC exosomes against OA has been largely predicated on their potent regenerative and immunomodulatory capacities [ ]. Indeed, MSC exosomes have been reported to exhibit diverse immunomodulatory activities to mediate tissue repair in many animal models. Using a rat osteochondral defect model, MSC exosomes were found efficacious in enhancing the infiltration of M2 macrophages, while reducing the levels of M1 macrophages and pro-inflammatory synovial cytokines, including IL-1β and TNF-α [ ]. This macrophage immunomodulation by MSC exosomes, leading to reduced inflammation and enhanced tissue repair, has also been observed in other animal disease models such as skeletal muscle injury [ ], bronchopulmonary dysplasia [ ], and corneal scarring [ ]. Apart from macrophage immunomodulation, MSC exosomes have also been reported to modulate other components of the immune system, such as the complement system [ – ]. Notably, MSC exosomes were found to alleviate complement activation in animal models of spinal cord injury [ ] and psoriasis [ ].
Here, we showed for the first time that MSC exosomes suppress complement activation and associated inflammation to promote joint repair in a rat model of TMJ-OA. Using chondrocyte cultures, we demonstrated that the assembly of C5b-9 complement complex on chondrocytes can directly trigger the expression of inflammatory genes such as IL-6 and IL-8, and the release of the matrix-degrading enzyme, MMP13. Further experiments revealed that MSC exosomes inhibit the assembly of C5b-9 complement complex on chondrocytes through their expression of CD59, a known inhibitor of the final stage of complement activation cascade [ ]. This inhibition of the C5b-9 complement complex assembly and activation is particularly significant, as high levels of C5b-9 have been reported in the synovial fluids of individuals following knee trauma and with OA [ , ], where excessive C5b-9 deposition reportedly mediates cell lysis, while sub-lytic concentrations induce chondrocytes’ gene expression of several pro-inflammatory cytokines and catabolic molecules, such as CCL2, CCL5, and MMP13 [ ]. As exosomal CD59 only inhibits the assembly of C5b-9 complement complex, which represents the final product of the complement activation cascade, the initiation of complement activation and subsequent formation of several complement peptides such as C3a, an opsonin to enhance phagocytosis, and C5a, a chemoattractant that recruits other immune cells, such as macrophages, are not affected. Based on these findings, exosomal CD59 is a critical attribute in mediating the inhibitory effects of MSC exosomes on chondrocyte inflammation and matrix degradation, and therefore can be used to predict the immunomodulatory potency of MSC exosome preparations.
This study has some limitations. Comparative evaluations of TMJ repair should ideally be made against the naive TMJ of unoperated animals. The present study evaluated outcomes only up to 4 weeks post-treatment, and longer-term observations − extending to 8 weeks or beyond − may be necessary to determine the progression and longevity of the joint repair. In this study, we used the MIA-induced TMJ-OA model to maintain consistency with our previous work [ ]. Further studies would be needed to evaluate MSC exosomes in other TMJ-OA models, such as the unilateral anterior cross-bite model, to determine their therapeutic efficacy in different clinical scenarios. As chondrocyte proliferation is often associated with dedifferentiation, it leads to a loss of the chondrocyte phenotype and a reduced capacity for matrix synthesis [ ]. Conventional two-dimensional (2D) monolayer culture has inherent limitations, as chondrocytes expanded in monolayers tend to lose their chondrogenic phenotype during passaging and undergo dedifferentiation. In contrast, 3D culture systems better preserve the chondrocyte phenotype, as the 3D matrix supports the chondrogenic characteristics of encapsulated chondrocytes and even promotes the redifferentiation of previously dedifferentiated articular chondrocytes [ ]. Therefore, adopting 3D culture approaches in future in vitro studies may provide a more physiologically relevant platform for evaluating the effects of exosomes on chondrocytes while maintaining their chondrogenic phenotype. Although immortalized MSCs offer methodological advantages, including scalable and consistent exosome production, they may not fully replicate the biological properties of primary MSCs, and future studies may need to validate our observations using exosomes derived from primary MSCs. In addition, the therapeutic use of exosomes from immortalized MSCs raises regulatory and safety concerns, among which the potential risk of tumorigenesis is a key consideration. Reassuringly, a previous study demonstrated that E1-MYC 16.3 MSCs and their derived exosomes do not promote tumor initiation or progression [ ]. Importantly, the findings of the present work have provided molecular insights into the downstream targets and immunomodulatory mechanismsof MSC exosomes in joint repair, which are critical for advancing their clinical translation. 

## Conclusion
Our study establishes a key role for complement activation in TMJ-OA pathogenesis by showing that elevated C5b-9 complement assembly is associated with increased inflammation and matrix degradation. Importantly, we demonstrate that MSC exosomes promote joint repair by expressing CD59 to inhibit C5b-9 complement assembly and activation, thereby reducing inflammation and matrix degradation, and thus providing critical mechanistic insights into their immunomodulatory capacity in OA treatment.
