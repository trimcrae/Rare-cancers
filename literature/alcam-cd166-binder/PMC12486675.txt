## Abstract

## Background
Unexplained recurrent spontaneous abortion (URSA) is a distressing pregnancy disorder with no effective medical intervention. As a potential treatment for URSA, human umbilical cord mesenchymal stem cells (hucMSCs) undergo apoptosis and release apoptotic bodies (ABs) shortly after transplantation. However, the medical effects and the exact doses of ABs in the therapy for URSA remain unclear.

## Methods
Staurosporine (STS) and ultraviolet (UV) induced apoptosis in hucMSCs. Differential centrifugation, transmission electron microscopy, immunofluorescent staining, and flow cytometry were used to isolate and characterize hucMSC-derived ABs (hucMSC-ABs), respectively. Two mouse abortion models (LPS-induced and CBA/J×DBA/2 immune-mediated) mimicked URSA. Flow cytometry, real-time quantitative PCR, immunofluorescence staining, and western blotting were used to analyze macrophage polarization and inflammatory pathways. Untargeted metabolomic analysis was used to detect the metabolic composition of hucMSC-ABs. RNA sequencing analysis was used to detect the transcriptome of macrophages (Macs). Dorsomorphin was used to inhibit the AMPK signaling pathway. Small interfering RNA (siRNA) was used to knock down Gys1 in raw246.7 macrophages.

## Results
STS- and UV- induced hucMSC-ABs effectively alleviated abortion in both models, depended on Macs. However, excessive dosage of hucMSC-ABs induced pro-inflammatory Macs (pro-inflam Macs), worsening fetal loss. Appropriate doses suppressed the NF-κB pathway in Macs, while high doses activated it. Neither RNase nor trypsin fully abolished hucMSC-ABs’ regulatory effects on macrophage polarization. Metabolomics identified linoleamide and palmitic acid (PA) as the dominant hucMSC-AB metabolites (~ 1:1 ratio). RNA-seq revealed hucMSC-ABs modulated Nfkb1 and Gys1 in Macs, linked to linoleamide. Appropriate doses of the 1:1 mixture of linoleamide and PA induced anti-inflam Macs, while high dosages of the mixture induced pro-inflam Macs. Linoleamide alone activated AMPK, suppressed NF-κB via Gys1, and protected pregnancy, whereas PA induced pro-inflam Macs and promoted abortion.

## Conclusion
Appropriate doses of hucMSC-ABs induce anti-inflam Macs in the uterus and prevent fetal loss, while high doses promote the polarization of pro-inflam Macs and lead to fetal loss. The effects of hucMSC-ABs in regulating Macs and treating URSA are related to different doses of linoleamide and PA on Macs. Linoleamide activates AMPK and suppresses NF-κB via Gys1. This study revealed the effect and mechanism of hucMSC-ABs intervention in fetal loss and provided a new idea for the treatment of URSA.

## Introduction
Recurrent spontaneous abortion (RSA) is defined as two or more consecutive spontaneous losses of pregnancy before 28 weeks of gestation, affecting approximately 2.5% of women attempting to conceive [ ]. The underlying cause is complex and includes abnormalities in chromosomes, anatomical uterine defects, endocrinological disorders, and disturbances in immune tolerance [ ]. In over 50% of RSA cases cannot be well explained, which is called unexplained recurrent spontaneous abortion (URSA) [ ]. The available treatment mainly targets potential risk factors for pregnancy loss, including surgical intervention, hormone supplementation, and anti-inflammatory medication [ , ]. Nevertheless, the efficacy of current medical interventions remains a matter of contention.
In recent years, stem cell therapies have gained attention for their potential to modulate immunity and promote tissue repair [ , ]. It was previously believed that MSCs elicit their therapeutic effects by secreting growth factors, cytokines, and extracellular vesicles when they are “alive” [ ]. However, recent studies have indicated that only patients with high cytotoxic activity against MSCs responded to MSC infusion, whereas those with low activity did not [ ]. Others indicated that in wild-type mice, MSCs undergo extensive apoptosis and release apoptotic bodies (ABs) shortly after transplantation [ ]. This led to the hypothesis that “apop” MSCs and MSC-derived apoptotic bodies (MSC-ABs) may play a crucial role in the therapeutic effects of MSCs.
Macrophages (Macs), the professional phagocytes responsible for clearing apoptotic issues, are the second most significant type of leukocytes at the maternal-fetal interface [ ]. Macs can differentiate into two distinct subpopulations in response to environmental cues, namely pro-inflammatory macrophages (pro-inflam Macs) and anti-inflammatory macrophages (anti-inflam Macs). Most Macs tend to be polarized into anti-inflam Macs to maintain immune tolerance in normal pregnancy [ ], while decidual pro/anti-inflam Macs imbalances occur in URSA during early pregnancy [ ]. Previous studies have indicated that Macs are the target cells of ABs and that AB treatment polarizes them towards the anti-inflammatory phenotype [ , ]. Nevertheless, the precise mechanisms by which MSC-ABs regulate macrophage polarization remain to be elucidated.
This study identified the appropriate doses of hucMSC-ABs for the treatment of URSA. Furthermore, we identified the crucial lipids in hucMSC-ABs that have therapeutic effects or bring detrimental consequences in URSA and key genes that lead to altered macrophage polarisation. This knowledge may guide the medical intervention and improve the quality of hucMSC-AB-based therapy.

## Materials and methods

## Animal experiments
The in vivo studies were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of the Zhejiang Academy of Medical Sciences (X2303191). They followed the National Institutes of Health guidelines for animal care (NIH Publications No. 8023, revised 1978). The work has been reported in line with the ARRIVE guidelines 2.0 (Additional file 1).
The experimental unit is a single mouse. The mice used in the study were all healthy, and all female mice were the first time mated with male mice. All mice had normal immune status, no genetic modification, and had never been exposed to any previous procedures. During the rearing period, the mice were housed in a barrier animal room with adequate air, water, and food supplies. Each cage contained three females and one male with adequate space. Each group contained nine females and three males, and mice with similar times of vaginal plug detection were taken to continue subsequent experiments with no other exclusion criteria, and the final sample size of each group was five female mice. No additional trapping of mice was performed except for vaginal plug testing and intraperitoneal injection. The mice were not abused. At sacrifice, they were euthanized in accordance with institutional guidelines, American Veterinary Association (AVMA) guidelines and approved protocols. The mice were placed in a 10 L transparent container, without dead animals or pre-filled gas, into which CO₂ was gradually introduced at 6 L/min until the mice showed no discernible respiratory movements. Rapid cervical dislocation was then performed. No other animal anaesthesia or live animal sampling was performed. The person numbering and grouping and the person collecting and analyzing samples were different.
Lipopolysaccharide (LPS)-induced abortion model [ ]: Eight-week-old female and male ICR mice were purchased from Hangzhou Ziyuan Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd (Hangzhou, China). Female ICR mice were mated in a natural cycling manner with the ICR males. The day of the vaginal plug detection was considered to be day 0.5 of pregnancy. Subsequently, pregnant mice were randomly allocated to several groups: a control group, an abortion group, and several experiment groups. Confounders were not controlled. The control group received a daily intraperitoneal injection of 200 µl PBS starting on GD8.5. The abortion group received an intraperitoneal injection of 200 µl LPS (20 µg/kg) on GD9.5, 11.5, and 13.5 and an intraperitoneal injection of 200 µl PBS on GD8.5, 10.5, and 12.5. The experimental group received an intraperitoneal injection of experimental drug resuspended in 200µL PBS on GD8.5, 10.5, and 12.5 and 200µL LPS (20 µg/kg) on GD9.5, 11.5 and 13.5. The clearer scheme is shown in Supplementary Fig.  C. Experimental groups include 100 µg STS-hucMSC-ABs, 100 µg UV-hucMSC-ABs, 30 µg STS-hucMSC-ABs, 1000 µg STS-hucMSC-ABs, 500µmol linoleamide, and 500µmol palmitic acid.
The spontaneous abortion model [ ] is as follows: The mice used in this study were purchased from Beijing HFK Bioscience Co., Ltd. and included eight-week-old female CBA/J mice, male DBA/2 mice, and male BALB/C mice. Before being caged together, CBA/J females were randomly injected intraperitoneally with 200 µl PBS or the experimental drug resuspended in 200 µl PBS. This was followed by the random selection of PBS-injected CBA/J females, which were then placed in cages with Balb/c males to serve as the control group. The remaining PBS-injected CBA/J females were placed in cages with DBA/2 males to serve as the abortion group. The CBA/J females that had been injected with the experimental drug were caged to DBA/2 males, forming the experimental group. The day on which the vaginal plug was detected was considered to be day 0.5 of pregnancy. On GD8.5, 10.5, and 12.5, the experimental group was again injected intraperitoneally with the experimental drug. The control and abortion groups were injected with 200 µl PBS, respectively. The clearer scheme is shown in Fig.  A. Experimental groups include 100 µg STS-hucMSC-ABs and 100 µg UV-hucMSC-ABs.
 The protocol for depleting Macs in LPS-challenged mice using PLX-3397 is as follows: Normal caged, then pregnant ICR mice were randomly divided into three groups: an LPS-challenged group, an STS-hucMSC-ABs treated group, and a PLX-3397 treated group. In the LPS-challenged group, mice were injected intraperitoneally with 200 µl LPS at GD 10.5 and 13.5 and injected intraperitoneally with PBS at GD8.5, 9.5, 11.5, and 12.5. In the STS-hucMSC-ABs treated group, mice were injected intraperitoneally with 100 µg STS-hucMSC-ABs in 200 µl PBS at GD 9.5 and 12.5, 200 µl LPS (20 µg/kg) at GD 10.5 and 13.5, and 200 µl PBS at GD8.5 and 11.5. In the PLX-3397 treated group, mice were injected intraperitoneally with 20 µg PLX-3397 in 200 µl PBS at GD8.5 and 11.5, 100 µg STS-hucMSC-ABs in 200 µl PBS at GD 9.5 and 12.5, and 200 µl LPS (20 µg/kg) at GD 10.5 and 13.5. The clearer scheme is shown in Fig.  F.
All mice were sacrificed at GD 14.5. The embryo abortion rate of pregnant mice, crown-rump length, fetal weight, and placental weight of pups, and the frequency of anti-inflam Macs at the maternal-fetal interface were measured.

## Culture and characterization of HucMSCs
Human umbilical cord mesenchymal stem cells (hucMSCs) were bought from Zhejiang Jinshidai Biotechnology Co., Ltd. and were incubated in the complete culture medium consisting of Dulbecco’s modified Eagle medium/F-12 (DMEM/F-12, Gibco), 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco), and 1% Penicillin-Streptomycin Solution (Gibco). When cell density reached 80%-90% confluence, the adherent cells were detached with 0.25% Trypsin-EDTA (Solarbio) and passaged. To avoid the potential effects of long-term culture-associated senescence and attenuated properties, experiments were conducted using hucMSCs in passages 3–7 [ ].
HucMSCs were identified in accordance with previous studies [ ]. Following the estimation of the cell morphology, hucMSCs were harvested and suspended in PBS at a concentration of 5 × 106 cells/ml to detect surface markers. HucMSCs were incubated with FITC-conjugated anti-CD105, FITC-conjugated anti-CD44, FITC-conjugated anti-CD73, FITC-conjugated anti-CD166, FITC-conjugated anti-CD34, FITC-conjugated anti-CD45, FITC-conjugated anti-CD14 and FITC-conjugated anti-HLA-DR (OriCell, HUXMX-09011) at room temperature for 30 min. The percentages of positively stained cells were analysed by flow cytometry (Beckman Coulter, USA) and FlowJo 10.0 software (Flow Jo LLC, USA). The differentiation potential of hucMSCs was evaluated through adipogenic, osteogenic, and chondrogenic assays. Adipogenic differentiation was induced using OriCell’s adipogenic induction medium (HUXUC-90031) for 12–20 days. Confirmation of induction was achieved through Oil Red O staining. Osteogenic differentiation was induced using OriCell’s osteogenic induction medium (HUXUC-90021) for 2–4 weeks. Calcium deposition was demonstrated via Alizarin red staining. Chondrogenic differentiation was induced using OriCell’s chondrogenic induction medium (HUXUC-90041) for 2–3 weeks. Positive induction was observed using Alcian Blue staining. Photographs were taken using an inverted optical microscope (Olympus, Japan).

## Detection of HucMSCs apoptosis in vivo
The apoptosis of hucMSCs in vivo was identified through the use of TUNEL staining. In brief, undifferentiated hucMSCs were labeled with a fluorescent lipophilic tracer, CMDiL (Invitrogen), and intraperitoneally injected into pregnant ICR mice. Following a 12-, 24-, and 48-hour period, the uteri were harvested and embedded in an optimal cutting temperature compound (OCT) (Leica). Then, the uterine specimens were sectioned at a thickness of 7 μm and air-dried cryosections were permeabilised by 0.3% Triton X-100 (Sigma) for 30 min. This was followed by a 30-minute blockade in 5% bovine albumin (BSA, Sigma). Then, TUNEL staining was conducted in accordance with the instructions provided in the One Step TUNEL Apoptosis Assay Kit (Beyotime Biotechnology), with counterstaining by DAPI (Sigma). Photographs were captured using a confocal microscope (FV1000, Olympus, Japan) and analyzed using ImageJ software.

## Induction of HucMSCs apoptosis
Two methods were employed to induce apoptosis in hucMSCs [ , ]. Initially, extracellular vesicles (EVs) were removed from fetal bovine serum (FBS) by ultracentrifugation at 100,000 g for 18 h. Then, the undifferentiated hucMSCs were washed twice with PBS. To obtain staurosporine (STS)-induced apoptotic hucMSCs, the culture medium was substituted with a complete medium containing EV-free FBS and 0.5 µM STS. Following a 12-hour treatment period, the apoptosis of hucMSCs was assessed by morphological observation. To induce apoptosis by radiation, undifferentiated hucMSCs were exposed to UVC light (30 W, 254 nm, Philip, China) for 30 min at 600 mJ/cm2 in slightly covered culture dishes. Following a 24-hour incubation period, the culture medium was collected.

## Collection and identification of hucMSC-ABs
HucMSC-ABs were isolated using an optimized protocol. Firstly, the supernatant was collected and centrifuged at 800 g for 10 min at 4 °C in order to remove cells and debris. Subsequently, the supernatant was centrifuged at 16,000 g for 30 min at 4 °C, and the pellet was washed twice in PBS. The isolated ABs were quantified by measuring the protein concentration via a BCA Protein Assay Kit (TIANGEN) and then suspended at 2 µg/µl in 100 µl PBS. The samples were stored at -80 °C for subsequent analysis if required. The morphological features of hucMSC-ABs were observed using a transmission electron microscope (TEM) (Hitachi, Japan). The expression of Annexin V in hucMSC-ABs was examined by immunofluorescent staining and flow cytometric analysis. In order to ascertain whether hucMSC-ABs can home to the uterine and be phagocytosed by Macs, the ABs were labelled with CMDiL and observed using a small-animal imaging and confocal microscope.

## Untargeted metabolomics by high-performance liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry
Metabolic extracts were obtained from hucMSC-ABs following methanol-assisted protein precipitation. Subsequently, the extracts were subjected to analysis by liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC-MS), utilising the Waters 2777 C UPLC (Waters, USA) in conjunction with the Q Exactive HF high-resolution mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). The mobile phase consisted of 0.1% formic acid (A) and acetonitrile (B) in the positive mode, and in the negative mode, the mobile phase consisted of 10 mM ammonium formate (A) and acetonitrile (B). The gradient conditions were as follows: The mobile phase was as follows: 0–1 min, 2% B; 1–9 min, 2%-98% B; 9–12 min, 98% B; 12–12.1 min, 98% B to 2% B; and 12.1–15 min, 2% B. The flow rate was 0.35 mL/min, and the injection volume was 5 µL. The Q Exactive HF (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) was employed to obtain MS/MS spectra via an information-dependent acquisition during the LC/MS experiment. Subsequently, the mass spectrometry data was imported into Compound Discoverer 3.3 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) software for analysis. This involved the use of the BMDB (BGI metabolome database), McCloud database, and ChemSpider online database.

## Metabolite quantification
Given that the quantitative method for butyric acid was more established, we proceeded to test the quantification of butyric acid in hucMSC-ABs by high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC). Briefly, 0.01 ml hucMSC-ABs were vortexed with 0.09 ml 5% perchloric acid for 5 to 10 min. Subsequently, all samples were subjected to ultrasonication at 4 °C for 60 min and centrifugation at 4000 rpm for 10 min. Then, all samples were filtered through a 0.22-mm filter to remove any residual solids. The HPLC method was employed to determine the butyric acid content of the aliquot. The column used was: The ZORBAX SB-Aq (4.6 × 250 mm, 5-micron) column was used, with a mobile phase comprising 0.02 mol/L sodium dihydrogen phosphate (pH = 2.2): acetonitrile = 85:15, with a flow rate of 1.0 mL/min, an injection volume of 20 µL, and a detection wavelength of 210 nm. The column temperature was maintained at 40 °C.

## Culture of bone marrow-derived macrophages (BMDMs)
The culture of bone marrow-derived macrophages (BMDMs) was initiated by gently blowing out the bone marrow using a 1 ml syringe, following the removal of the epiphyses from both ends of the femur and tibia in mice. Subsequently, the bone marrow was thoroughly mixed with 5 ml PBS and filtered through a 70 μm filter. The cell suspension was centrifuged at 300 g for 5 min and treated with erythrocyte lysate (Solarbio). Cells were then cultured in RPMI 1640 (Gibco) supplemented with 10% FBS, 1% P/S, and 20 µg/ml recombinant mouse granulocyte-macrophage colony-stimulating factor (GM-CSF) (Novoprotein). During the culture process, the medium was replaced every two to three days. Following seven days of induction culture, the mature BMDMs were successfully collected.

## RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) analysis
BMDMs were treated with hucMSC-ABs at 20 ng/ml for 24 h and stimulated with 100 ng/ml LPS for 6 h. Total RNA was extracted from the BMDMs using Trizol (Invitrogen). RNA sequencing libraries were sequenced using the BGISEQ platform (BGI-Shenzhen) following the manufacturer’s recommendations. The DESeq2 R package software was used for count normalization and differential gene expression analysis of RNA-seq data. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) (Fold change > 1.3 and Q value < 0.05) were included for further functional analysis based on the KEGG and GSEA databases. Correlational relationships between DEGs enriched in the KEGG analysis and the top 10 abundant metabolites were assessed using the Omiscribe tools at https://omiscribe.bgi.com/api/AppsList/#metabo/. The standards used to screen out significantly correlated DEGs and metabolites were set at |r|≥0.7 and P < 0.05. The details of all the identified genes are listed in Supplementary Table .

## Small interfering RNA (siRNA) knockdown
Macs were transfected with siRNA negative control (si-NC) or siRNA-Gys1 (si-Gys1) (Guannan Bio, China) using RNAiMAX (13778150, Invitrogen) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The transfection efficiency was measured by qRT-PCR and Western blotting at 48 h post-transfection. Linoleamide was added to the siRNA-transfected Macs at 48 h post-transfection.

## Drug dissolution
Staurosporine (CST-9953 S), dorsomorphin (MCE-HY-13418 A) and linoleamide (Aladdin) were dissolved in DMSO, following the manufacturer’s instructions. Palmitic acid (Sigma-P9767) was prepared by first dissolving in 75 °C ddH2O to 200 µM, then mixing 1:1 with centrifuged (12,000 rpm, 30 min) 40% fatty acid-free BSA (Sigma) in PBS while maintaining 75 °C, followed by 0.22 μm filtration to yield 100 µM PA solution. For cell treatments: hucMSCs were exposed to 0.5 µM STS for 12 h; BMDMs were treated with 10µM dorsomorphin for 24 h. BMDMs and PBMCs were treated with either hucMSC-ABs (10, 20, 50 ng/ml) or linoleamide/PA (50, 100, 250 µM, matching hucMSC-ABs concentrations) for 24 h. To deplete linoleamide, hucMSC-ABs (2 µg/µL) were incubated with 0.2 M NH₂OH·HCl (PBS, pH 7.4) at 37 °C for 6 h with shaking (200 rpm). The reaction was stopped with 10 mM EDTA (10 min, ice), followed by centrifugation at 16,000 g for 30 min at 4 °C.

## Flow cytometry
BMDMs and PBMCs were stimulated by 100ng/ml LPS for 6 h. Then, the cells were collected with 0.25% Trypsin-EDTA (Solarbio), followed by antibody staining for 30 min at room temperature. After washing with PBS twice to exclude non-specific staining, the surface expression was detected by flow cytometry. For intracellular expression, the cells were fixed with IC Fixation Buffer (Invitrogen) at 4 °C overnight. Antibodies including FITC-conjugated Annexin V/PE-conjugated PI (V13242, Invitrogen), FITC-conjugated anti-F4/80 (11-4801-82, Invitrogen), APC-conjugated anti-CD163 (17-1631-80, invitrogen), PE-Cy7-conjugated anti-CD206 (25-2061-82, Invitrogen), PE-conjugated anti-iNOS (12-5920-82, Invitrogen), APC-conjugated anti-CD14 (17-0149-42, Invitrogen), PE-conjugated CD86 (12-0869-42, Invitrogen) and PE-Cy7-conjugated CD163 (25-1639-42, Invitrogen).
To detect uterine macrophage, the uteruses of mice were removed and minced on ice. The tissues were digested and isolated with collagenase IV (1 mg/ml, Gibco) and DNase I (0.1 mg/ml, Sigma-Aldrich) for 40 min at 200 rpm and 37 °C. Then, the cell suspensions were collected and incubated with antibody staining for 30 min at room temperature. Finally, the cells were washed with PBS twice to exclude non-specific staining, and the surface expression was detected by flow cytometry. The antibodies used were as follows: FITC-conjugated anti-F4/80 (11-4801-82, Invitrogen), APC-conjugated anti-CD163 (17-1631-80, Invitrogen), PE -conjugated anti-CD86 (12-0869-42, EBioscience) and 7-amino-actinomycin D (MB5442-1, MeilunBio).

## Immunofluorescence staining
Cells were washed with PBS 3 times, then underwent blockage with 4% Paraformaldehyde (PFA) at room temperature for 30 min, permeabilization with 0.01% Triton X-100 (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) for 30 min at room temperature, and incubation with primary antibodies at 4 °C overnight. The next day, the cells were incubated with fluorescence secondary antibody (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) at room temperature for 1 h. Last, the nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) for 10 min at room temperature. Fluorescence imaging was performed using a confocal microscope (FV1000, Olympus, Japan) and analyzed by the ImageJ software. Primary antibodies including NF-κB-p65 (CST-8242P) and F4/80 (MF48000, Invitrogen).

## RNA isolation and qRT-PCR
In accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions, total RNA was extracted from BMDMs using an RNA-Quick Purification Kit (ES-RN001, Shanghai Yishan Biotechnology). Subsequently, cDNA was synthesised using HiScript II Q RT SuperMix for qPCR (R222-01, Vazyme). Then, real-time PCR analysis was conducted on a Roche LightCycler480 automated system with a ChamQ SYBR qPCR Master Mix (without ROX) (Q321-02, Vazyme). Quantification was performed by using GAPDH as the internal control and calculating the relative expression level of each gene with the 2–ΔΔCT method. The primer sequences are presented in Table  .
 

## Western blotting
BMDMs were stimulated by 1 µg/ml LPS for 15 min [ ]. The culture medium was discarded, and the proteins from BMDMs were extracted using RIPA lysis buffer (Solarbio) with protease inhibitor (Beyotime). After being quantified by BCA assay, the proteins were diluted with Laemmli protein sample buffer (Bio-Rad). Then, equal amounts of proteins were loaded on sodium dodecyl sulfate - polyacrylamide (SDS) gels and transferred to polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes (Millipore). The membranes were blocked in the TBS-T buffer containing 5% skimmed milk for 1 h at room temperature and then incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C. Finally, primary antibodies were removed by washing the membranes three times in TBS-T, and the membranes were then incubated with secondary antibodies (1:5000) (Abcam) for 1 h. The protein bands were detected by the X-ray film. Primary antibodies used GAPDH (proteintech, 60004-1-Ig), HDAC2 (Abcam, ab32117), NF-κB-p65 (CST, 8242 S), Phospho-NF-κB p65(CST, 3033 S), IκBα (CST, 4814 S), Phospho-IKKα/β (CST, 2697 S), IKKβ (CST, 8943 S), Gys1 (CST, 3893 S), p-AMPK (Abcam, ab131357) and t-AMPK (Abcam, ab207442).

## Statistical analysis
Each sample was represented as a point. All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 10.1.2. Data normality was assessed with Shapiro-Wilk tests (α = 0.05). Normally distributed data were analyzed by one-way ANOVA, while non-normally distributed data were examined using Kruskal-Wallis tests. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05.

## Results

## Identification and characterization of hucMSC-ABs induced by STS and UV
Firstly, the hucMSCs were characterised. The cells showed a spindle-shaped morphology (Figure A) [ ] and demonstrated the capacity for osteogenic, lipogenic, and chondrogenic differentiation (Figure B) [ ]. Furthermore, the cells expressed high levels of stem cell markers, including CD44, CD73, CD105, and CD166, while lacking expression of differentiated cell markers, such as CD11b, CD34, CD45, and HLA-DR (Figure C) [ ], suggesting that hucMSCs used for the experiments was qualified.
Consistent with previous studies [ , ], TUNEL staining showed that hucMSCs underwent extensive apoptosis after injection, and the percentage of TUNEL-positive hucMSCs peaked 24 h after injection (Fig.  A). Then, we used STS and UV to induce apoptosis in hucMSCs and isolated hucMSC-ABs by the optimized gradient centrifugation (Fig.  B). After apoptosis induced by STS or UV, hucMSCs showed vacuolization of the cell membrane and decreased cell volume (Fig.  C), with high expression of Annexin V and PI (Fig.  D). Flow cytometry and immunofluorescence showed positive staining of annexin V on STS-hucMSC-ABs and UV-hucMSC-ABs (Fig.  E-F), and transmission electron microscopy (TEM) confirmed the typical vesicular structure and approximately 1000 nm diameter of STS-hucMSC-ABs and UV-hucMSC-ABs (Fig.  G), which are qualified according to previous research [ ].
 

## HucMSC-ABs effectively alleviated abortion in mice abortion models, which effect depended on Macs
Previous studies have reported that stem cell therapy was effective in mice with recurrent miscarriages [ , ]. Considering the findings that stem cells undergo apoptosis shortly after injection [ ] and those non-apoptotic stem cells do not have a beneficial therapeutic effect [ ], it is hypothesised that it is the ‘apoptotic’ stem cells that play the crucial role in the stem cell therapy process. Therefore, we separated and extracted STS-hucMSC-ABs and UV-hucMSC-ABs and administered them intraperitoneally in the two abortion models, the LPS-induced abortion model and the spontaneous abortion (SA) model (Fig.  A, Figure C). First, the signal from CMDil-labelled hucMSC-ABs was positive in the uteri at 6 h after injection by vivo imaging system (Figure A), indicating that hucMSC-ABs could reach the uteri through the intraperitoneal administration. And Macs engulfed ABs after being cocultured for 24 h, indicating that hucMSC-ABs could be phagocytosed by Macs (Figure B).
In the SA model, mice treated with STS-hucMSC-ABs or UV-hucMSC-ABs showed decreased abortion rates, as well as increased crown-rump length and fetal weight compared to abortion mice (Fig.  B, C). No significant differences were found between STS-hucMSC-AB-treated and UV-hucMSC-AB-treated mice (Fig.  B, C). Anti-inflam Macs were markedly increased in the mice treated with hucMSC-ABs (Fig.  D), though the MFI of CD86 was also increased (Fig.  E). In the LPS-challenged model, hucMSC-ABs could also prevent fetal loss (Figure D, E), with increased anti-inflam Macs compared to untreated LPS mice, though there was no significant difference in the MFI of CD86 in F4/80+ uterine cells (Fig.  SF, G).
In addition, the macrophage scavenger PLX-3397, an inhibitor of the colony-stimulating factor 1 receptor, was used to deplete Macs in the LPS-challenged mice. The rate of embryo loss was higher after macrophage scavenging and hucMSC-ABs could not prevent abortion in mice with PLX-3397, suggesting that Macs play an important role in maintaining pregnancy and that hucMSC-ABs exert their therapeutic effects mainly through Macs.
In conclusion, hucMSC-ABs effectively alleviated abortion in mice abortion models, which effect depended on Macs.

## Excessive dosage of hucMSC-ABs induced pro-inflam Macs and aggravated abortion
Although hucMSC-ABs present a protective property in two abortion-prone models, other evidence indicates that phagocytosis of apoptotic substances can prime Macs for inflammatory response and activate the JNK pathway [ ]. Moreover, in preliminary laboratory experiments, excessive doses of hucMSC-ABs did not prevent fetal loss but rather exacerbated abortion. Therefore, we further investigated the effects of different doses of hucMSC-ABs on pregnancy protection.
LPS-challenged mice were administrated 30 µg, 100 µg, and 1000 µg STS-hucMSC-ABs respectively. Mice treated with 1000 µg STS-hucMSC-ABs had increased fetal resorption rate and decreased fetal crown-rump length and weight compared with LPS-challenged mice with no treatment (Fig.  A, B). Flow cytometry showed that excessive doses of hucMSC-ABs significantly increased the MFI of CD86 in F4/80+ in uterine cells, while there was no significance in the anti-inflam Macs (Fig.  C).
 The findings indicated that while appropriate doses of hucMSC-ABs protected pregnancy, excessive dosage resulted in fetal loss, which effect depended on Macs.

## The NF-κB pathway in Macs was inhibited by appropriate doses of hucMSC-ABs but promoted by excessive dosage of hucMSC-ABs
To explore the underlying mechanism by which hucMSC-ABs regulate macrophage polarization and abortion, we cultured BMDMs in vitro and used LPS to mimic the inflammatory microenvironment. STS-hucMSC-ABs and UV-hucMSC-ABs were added to BMDMs in 10 µg/ml, 20 µg/ml, and 50 µg/ml, respectively. Flow cytometry showed that the increased percentage of pro-inflam Macs (iNOS+ F4/80+) in BMDMs under LPS stimulation was significantly rescued by 20 µg/ml STS-hucMSC-ABs and UV-hucMSC-ABs. In contrast, the decreased rate of anti-inflam Macs (CD163+ CD206+ F4/80+) under LPS stimulation was found considerably increased under treatment with 20 µg/ml STS-hucMSC-ABs and UV-hucMSC-ABs (Fig.  A, B). However, unlike 20 µg/ml, when BMDMs were incubated with 50 µg/ml hucMSC-ABs, the percentage of pro-inflam Macs (iNOS+ F4/80+) was increased compared to those only stimulated by LPS, and there was no significance in the anti-inflam Macs (CD163+ CD206+ F4/80+) (Fig.  A, B). Meanwhile, the expression levels of inflammatory-related genes, including interleukin-1β (IL-1β), interleukin-6 (IL-6), tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α) and arginase-1 (ARG-1) were investigated. After LPS stimulation, the expression of pro-inflammatory markers (IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α) was increased while the expression of ARG-1 was decreased. IL-1β, IL-6, TNF-α, and ARG-1 were significantly reversed by 20 µg/ml STS-hucMSC-ABs and UV-hucMSC-ABs. However, different from the group of 20 µg/ml hucMSC-ABs, IL-1β and IL-6 were upregulated when BMDMs were incubated with 50 µg/ml hucMSC-ABs. Interestingly, TNF-α was still downregulated, and ARG-1 was still upregulated in the 50 µg/ml hucMSC-ABs group (Fig.  C).
 Since hucMSC-ABs regulated the expression of pro-inflammatory markers, we attempted to explore the potential signaling pathways involved in this process. Previous studies have shown that nuclear factor- kappa B (NF-κB) plays a crucial role in the transcription of pro-inflammatory genes such as IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α [ ]. Thus, we detected the activation of NF-κB-p65, inhibitor of kappa B kinase (IKK) α/β, and the expression of NF-κB inhibitor α (IκBα) in BMDMs. After being treated with 20 µg/ml hucMSC-ABs, the phosphorylation levels of NF-κB-p65 and IKK α/β in BMDMs were lower, while the protein levels of IκBα were higher, compared to those only stimulated by LPS (Fig.  D). Meanwhile, immunofluorescent staining showed that the high expression of NF-κB in the nucleus of BMDMs stimulated by LPS was rescued by 20 µg/ml hucMSC-ABs (Fig.  E). However, 50 µg/ml hucMSC-ABs conversely activated the NF-κB signaling pathway in BMDMs (Fig.  F).
Meanwhile, different doses of STS-hucMSC-ABs were incubated with the Macs derived from human peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs). The results showed that only 20 µg/ml STS-hucMSC-ABs could increase anti-inflam Macs (CD163+CD14+), while there was no significance in the expression of CD86 on CD14+ cells (Figure A).
In conclusion, appropriate doses of hucMSC-ABs polarized Macs toward anti-inflam Macs and suppressed the transcription of pro-inflammatory genes by inhibiting the NF-κB pathway. In contrast, excessive doses activated the NF-κB pathway and induced pro-inflam Macs.

## Linoleamide and PA are the two most abundant metabolites enriched in hucMSC-ABs, with a ratio of approximately 1:1
Given that excessive doses and appropriate doses of hucMSC-ABs have a distinctly different impact on macrophage polarization, we sought to investigate the contents of hucMSC-ABs and the underlying mechanism by which hucMSC-ABs exert their influence. HucMSC-ABs contain a diverse range of contents, including nucleic acids, proteins, and metabolites. Flow cytometry and QPCR analysis showed that STS-hucMSC-ABs were still able to polarize Macs towards anti-inflam Macs after depleting the nucleic acids with RNase or the proteins with trypsin in the STS-hucMSC-ABs (Fig.  A, B). Consequently, we hypothesized that there are crucial metabolites contained within the hucMSC-ABs that have therapeutic effects. An untargeted metabolomic analysis was conducted on STS-hucMSC-ABs, UV-hucMSC-ABs, and parental hucMSCs. There was no apparent separation of samples in each group (Fig.  C). There were 4140 metabolites identified (Supplementary Table ), while 1770 were differential metabolites (DEMs), within which 1020 were significantly upregulated in hucMSC-ABs. We then focused on these enriched metabolites and carried out functional analysis. Most metabolites enriched in hucMSC-ABs were included in “lipids” (Fig.  D) and were associated with “Amino acid metabolism,” “Lipid metabolism,” and “Carbohydrate metabolism” (Fig.  E). Linoleamide and palmitic acid (PA) were the two most abundant metabolites enriched in STS-hucMSC-ABs and UV-hucMSC-ABs, with a ratio of approximately 1:1. (Fig.  F). Linoleamide is a fatty acid amide derived from linoleic acid and has been shown to inhibit SPCA2, a Golgi secretory pathway Ca2+-ATPase that transports Ca2+ and Mn2+ [ ]. However, the relationship between linoleamide and the immune system remains unknown. PA is a saturated fatty acid that activates various signaling pathways, such as LPS-mediated toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4) activation, and triggers inflammatory responses [ ]. Other top 10 abundant metabolites are shown in Fig.  F.
 Taken together, we found that linoleamide and PA are the two most abundant metabolites enriched in hucMSC-ABs, with a ratio of approximately 1:1, which may play a vital role in the function of hucMSC-ABs.

## HucMSC-ABs reprogram the transcriptome of Macs
To elucidate the role of hucMSC-ABs in the gene expression of BMDMs, we performed RNA-seq analysis on BMDMs treated with 20 µg/ml hucMSC-ABs and BMDMs only stimulated by LPS. There was no apparent separation among samples in each group (Fig.  A). A total of 374 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified in the STS-hucMSC-ABs-treated group (Supplementary Table ), including 247 upregulated genes and 127 downregulated genes. With regard to the functional analysis based on KEGG pathway analysis, the DEGs were annotated to multiple inflammatory-related pathways, including ‘Cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction’, ‘MAPK signaling pathway’, and ‘TNF signaling pathway’. In particular, the DEGs were found to be associated with multiple pathways related to glucose metabolism, including the ‘PI3K-Akt signaling pathway’, and ‘glycolysis/gluconeogenesis’ (Fig.  B). GSEA analysis also revealed that STS-hucMSC-ABs altered the expression of genes associated with inflammatory, and glucose metabolism, such as ‘Negative regulation of lymphocyte activation’, ‘Arginine metabolic process’, and ‘Cellular carbohydrate biosynthetic process’ (Fig.  C). Following the aforementioned findings, RNA-seq analysis revealed that the genes associated with pro-inflam polarization, including Il6, Tnf, and Nfκb1, suffered a decline in BMDMs treated with STS-hucMSC-ABs. In contrast, the expression of anti-inflam genes, such as Arg1 and Vegfa, was upregulated. Furthermore, genes associated with glycogen synthesis (Gys1, Pck2) were observed to be upregulated (Fig.  D-E). Correlation analysis of these anti-inflam DEGs and glycogen synthesis-related DEGs showed a significant negative correlation with Nfκb1, while pro-inflam DEGs showed a positive correlation (Fig.  F). After identifying 291 DEGs, similar transcriptional reprogramming was found in UV-hucMSC-AB-treated BMDMs (Figure A-C).
 To reveal the relationship between the exact metabolites in hucMSC-ABs and the gene transcription in Macs, a correlation analysis of the hucMSC-AB metabolomics and the macrophage transcriptome was performed. In particular, linoleamide exhibited a significant negative correlation with pro-inflam DEGs, including Il6, Tnf, and Nfκb1. Conversely, it demonstrated a positive correlation with anti-inflam DEGs (Vegfa) and glycogen synthesis-related DEGs (Gys1, Pck2) (Fig.  G). The above effects of linoleamide on macrophage transcript levels were subsequently validated by qPCR (Fig.  H and E). Consequently, linoleamide in hucMSC-ABs may be a vital messenger that mediates macrophage polarization.
 

## Linoleamide in hucMSC-ABs induced anti-inflam macs, whereas PA induced pro-inflam Macs
Then, we investigated the influence of linoleamide and PA, the two most abundant lipids, on macrophage polarization. Firstly, butyric acid was quantified as approximately 1 µg in 20 µg hucMSC-ABs by high-performance liquid chromatography. Then, we calculated the quantity of linoleamide and PA based on the untargeted metabolomic data in hucMSC-ABs. Afterward, BMDMs were treated with different doses of linoleamide and PA, comparable with those doses in hucMSC-ABs, and the polarization of Macs was detected. Interestingly, BMDMs treated with 100µM linoleamide and PA showed a lower percentage of pro-inflam Macs (iNOS+ F4/80+) and a higher percentage of anti-inflam Macs (CD163+ CD206+ F4/80+). The transcription levels of pro-inflam genes (IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α) were decreased in BMDMs incubated with 100µM linoleamide and PA, while ARG-1 was increased. Moreover, The NF-κB signaling pathway was inhibited considerably in BMDMs treated with 100µM linoleamide and PA (Fig.  A-C). However, when BMDMs were incubated with 250µM linoleamide and PA, the percentage of pro-inflam Macs (iNOS+ F4/80+) was increased, though there was no significance in the anti-inflam Macs (CD163+ CD206+ F4/80+) (Fig.  A). As for the transcription levels of inflammatory-related genes, different from the 100µM group, IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α were upregulated when BMDMs were incubated with 250µM linoleamide and PA, though ARG-1 was still upregulated (Fig.  B). Moreover, the NF-κB signaling pathway was activated, comparable with 50 µg/ml hucMSC-ABs (Fig.  C).
To further investigate the effects of linoleamide and PA on macrophage polarization, we added linoleamide and PA to BMDMs respectively. Our results showed that linoleamide decreased the percentage of pro-inflam Macs (iNOS+ F4/80+) in a dose-dependent manner (Fig.  D). Meanwhile, linoleamide significantly increased the anti-inflam Macs (CD163+ CD206+ F4/80+) but not ARG-1 in BMDMs (Fig.  D, E). The NF-κB signaling pathway was inhibited considerably in BMDMs treated with linoleamide (Fig.  F). However, consistent with previous findings [ ], PA transited Macs to the pro-inflam Macs in a concentration-dependent way (Supplementary Figure A-C).
In parallel, PBMCs were treated with varying concentrations of linoleamide and PA. Notably, only 100 µM linoleamide and its 100 µM mixture significantly increased the proportion of anti-inflam Macs (CD163+ CD14+), and 250µM PA significantly decreased the proportion of CD163+ CD14+ cells (Supplementary Figure D). Unlike observations in BMDMs, 250µM linoleamide failed to promote anti-inflammatory polarization in human PBMCs, suggesting a dose-dependent immunomodulatory effect that may underlie the optimal therapeutic dosage observed for hucMSC-ABs.
To investigate the role of linoleamide in hucMSC-AB-mediated immunomodulation, we pretreated hucMSC-ABs with hydroxylamine hydrochloride (an amide-cleaving agent) to remove linoleamide [ ]. The results showed that hydroxylamine-treated hucMSC-ABs failed to increase the proportion of CD163 + CD14+ anti-inflam Macs in PBMCs compared to untreated hucMSC-ABs (Fig.  G). Importantly, no significant difference was observed between linoleamide-depleted hucMSC-ABs and LPS groups, indicating that linoleamide is the principal bioactive metabolite in hucMSC-ABs responsible for inducing anti-inflammatory macrophage polarization.

## Linoleamide inhibited the NF-κB pathway through Gys1
To investigate the immunomodulatory mechanism of linoleamide, we pretreatmented BMDMs with 100 µM linoleamide, which significantly enhanced AMPK activation compared to LPS stimulated alone (Fig.  H). Notably, our transcriptome data (Fig.  ) revealed significant Gys1 upregulation—a glycogen synthase that converts UDPG to glycogen [ , ] and inhibits NF-κB pathway activity in Macs [ , ]. Since Gys1 is transcriptionally regulated by AMPK activation [ , ], suggesting a mechanistic link between AMPK activation, Gys1 and NF-κB signaling. After BMDMs were treated with 10µM dorsomorphin (an AMPK inhibitor), WB showed that the linoleamide-induced Gys1 upregulation was reversed (Fig.  H). Then, we transfected raw246.7 macrophages with si-Gys1 and its si-NC, and investigated the linoleamide effects on Gys1-knockdown Macs. After Macs were transfected by siGys1, flow cytometry showed that the decreased percentage of pro-inflam Macs (iNOS+ F4/80+) with linoleamide treatment was significantly reversed. In contrast, the increased rate of anti-inflam Macs (CD206+ F4/80+) under linoleamide treatment was found considerably decreased (Fig.  I). Meanwhile, the expression levels of inflammatory-related genes (IL-6, TNF-α) decreased by linoleamide were increased in siGys1-Macs, while the expression of Vegfa was decreased (Fig.  J). Moreover, the NF-κB signaling pathway was activated, compared to siNC-Macs with linoleamide treatment (Fig.  K).
These findings revealed that linoleamide in hucMSC-ABs inhibited the NF-κB pathway in Macs, which is mediated by Gys1.

## Linoleamide improved pregnancy outcomes, whereas PA aggravated miscarriages
To further investigate the effects of linoleamide and PA on abortion mice, we injected linoleamide and PA intraperitoneally in LPS-challanged mice, respectively. Our results demonstrated that mice treated with 500 µmol linoleamide showed a reduction in embryo resorption, while fetal crown-rump length and weight were remarkably rescued compared with untreated LPS mice (Fig.  A, B). However, mice treated with the same dose of PA exhibited an increased embryo abortion rate and a further reduction in fetal crown-rump length and weight (Fig.  A, B). Flow cytometry analysis showed that linoleamide significantly increased anti-inflam Macs (CD163+ F4/80+) but had no significant influence on CD86 MFI. In contrast, PA significantly increased the MFI of CD86 in F4/80 uterine cells and reduced anti-inflam Macs (CD163+ F4/80+) (Fig.  C, D).
 

## Discussion
The efficacy of current medical interventions for URSA remains a debate. In this study, we showed that only appropriate doses of hucMSC-ABs effectively alleviated abortion in both models, depending on Macs. However, excessive dosage of hucMSC-ABs induced pro-inflam Macs and led to fetal loss. Appropriate doses of hucMSC-ABs inhibited the NF-κB pathway in Macs, while excessive doses promoted it. Neither RNase nor trypsin fully abolished hucMSC-ABs’ regulatory effects on macrophage polarization. Linoleamide and PA were the dominant hucMSC-AB metabolites (~ 1:1 ratio). RNA-seq revealed hucMSC-ABs modulated Nfkb1 and Gys1 in Macs, linked to linoleamide. Appropriate doses of the 1:1 mixture of linoleamide and PA induced anti-inflam Macs, while high dosages of the mixture induced pro-inflam Macs. Further investigation showed that linoleamide alone induced anti-inflam Macs, and hydroxylamine hydrochloride-mediated depletion of linoleamide from hucMSC-ABs significantly attenuated their anti-inflammatory macrophage-polarizing capacity. At the molecular level, linoleamide activated AMPK, suppressed NF-κB via Gys1, and protected pregnancy, whereas PA induced pro-inflam Macs and promoted abortion. Consequently, linoleamide in hucMSC-ABs may be a vital messenger that mediates macrophage polarization, with its effects being regulated through the AMPK-Gys1-NF-κB axis.
Among the various treatments for reproductive disorders, stem cell-based therapy has shown great potential [ ]. Nevertheless, it is noteworthy that only patients with high cytotoxic activity against MSCs responded to MSC infusion, whereas those with low activity did not [ ]. Consequently, it is postulated that all patients could be treated with ex vivo apoptotic MSCs or MSC-ABs. In our previous study, hucMSC-ABs could induce macrophage immunomodulation in vitro, and the hucMSC-AB-laden hyaluronic acid hydrogel could reduce endometrial fibrosis and promote its regeneration [ ]. However, in URSA, the medical effects and doses of hucMSC-ABs are still unclear. In this study, we first proved that only appropriate doses of hucMSC-ABs can reduce the abortion rate and improve fetal-maternal outcomes in two abortion-prone models. In contrast, excessive doses are detrimental to the pregnancy. Furthermore, we first proved that the effect of hucMSC-ABs on abortion mice depended on Macs.
A successful pregnancy depends on a timely tolerance status in the maternal system [ ]. Once the immune tolerance is broken, it can lead to miscarriage [ ]. Approximately 50% of URSA were due to an immune disorder [ ], and aberrant macrophage polarization is responsible for the disturbed immune environment at the maternal-fetal interface in URSA [ ]. Zheng et al. indicated that calreticulin (CRT) is exposed on the surface of MSC-ABs and acts as an “eat-me” signal to Macs, mediating macrophage regulatory effects of MSC-ABs [ ]. In this study, we illuminated that after engulfing appropriate doses of hucMSC-ABs, Macs can be transformed into anti-inflam Macs, consistent with previous studies [ , ]. We showed that only appropriate doses of hucMSC-ABs inhibited the activation of NF-κB, a main inflammatory pathway responsible for the expression of inflammatory response genes in Macs, such as IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α [ ]. Furthermore, appropriate doses of hucMSC-ABs upregulated the expression of Gys1, an enzyme that catalyzes the synthesis of glycogen by transferring uridine diphosphate glucose (UDPG) [ , ]. Many previous studies have shown that Gys1 inhibits the NF-κB pathway [ – ]. Therefore, we further tested its effect in hucMSC-AB treatment.
Though MSC-ABs have attracted substantial attention in recent years for their ability to carry various contents and act as tissue messengers [ , ], their current knowledge still needs to be improved. Zheng et al. demonstrated that CRT was expressed on MSC-ABs, which mediated their phagocytosis by Macs. The proteomic analysis revealed that MSC-ABs transported VASP, PRKAR2A, and CRYAB, thereby influencing macrophage M2 polarization [ ]. In a separate study, Li et al. demonstrated that microRNA (miR)-21-5p was highly enriched in MSC-ABs, which potentially targets Kruppel-like factor 6 (KLF6) in Macs, thereby inducing M2 polarization [ ]. However, our study demonstrated that despite depleting nucleic acids and proteins, STS-hucMSC-ABs were still able to polarize Macs towards anti-inflam Macs. Consequently, the metabolite composition of hucMSC-ABs was analyzed. In our studies, we identified linoleamide and PA as the two most abundant metabolites. Linoleamide is a competitive inhibitor of both phospholipase A2 (PLA2) and fatty acid amide hydrolase (FAAH) [ ]. While previous studies have established that pharmacological inhibition of PLA2 (e.g., by NSAIDs) suppresses arachidonic acid metabolism and pro-inflammatory mediator production [ ], and FAAH inhibition elevates anandamide levels to modulate inflammation via CB2, PPARγ, and TRPV1 pathways [ ], there is no evidence connecting linoleamide with macrophage functions or pregnancy. In our study, we demonstrated for the first time that linoleamide promoted the polarization of anti-inflam Macs and improved pregnancy outcomes in abortion-prone mice. Mechanistically, similar to other PLA2/FAAH inhibitors [ – ], linoleamide restores AMPK activity, which directly upregulates Gys1 expression [ , ]. Furthermore, we found that Gys1 upregulation mediates linoleamide’s suppression of NF-κB signaling pathway. After linoleamide was removed from hucMSC-ABs using hydroxylamine hydrochloride (an amide-binding agent), the vesicles’ ability to promote anti-inflammatory macrophage polarization was significantly attenuated, suggesting that linoleamide is likely the primary active component mediating the therapeutic effects of hucMSC-ABs. Consistent with previous work, our study demonstrated that PA can polarize Macs towards pro-inflammatory phenotypes, particularly at excessive doses, which may contribute to the adverse effects observed in hucMSC-ABs in the treatment of URSA.
Linoleamide and PA are common components of edible oils used in daily food preparation. Therefore, our study suggests the possible influence of diet and lipid metabolism on the maintenance of pregnancy. This is further supported by clinical evidence showing that lipid emulsion therapy (containing unsaturated fatty acids such as linoleic acid) improves outcomes in patients with recurrent pregnancy loss (RPL) or recurrent implantation failure (RIF) associated with NK cell dysfunction [ – ].
There are limitations in our study. While we used hydroxylamine hydrochloride to bind linoleamide in hucMSC-ABs, this agent is not specific to linoleamide, highlighting the need to develop linoleamide-specific antagonists. As a preclinical study, clinical validation is still required to assess the therapeutic potential of stem cells, their apoptotic vesicles, or linoleamide in recurrent miscarriage patients. Currently, our findings are based on in vitro experiments using human macrophages. Further clinical trials are currently undergoing ethical review.

## Conclusion
In conclusion, we first proved that only the appropriate dose of hucMSC-ABs induces anti-inflam Macs and prevents fetal loss. Conversely, an excessive dose promotes the polarization of pro-inflam Macs and leads to fetal loss. The effects of hucMSC-ABs in regulating Macs and treating URSA may be related to the different doses of linoleamide and PA on Macs. Linoleamide in hucMSC-ABs activated AMPK and inhibited the NF-κB pathway in Macs, which is mediated by Gys1. These findings revealed the effect and mechanism of hucMSC-ABs intervention in fetal loss and provided a new idea for the treatment of URSA.
