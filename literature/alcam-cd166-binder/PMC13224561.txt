## Abstract

## Objectives
This study aims to investigate the therapeutic effects of allogeneic umbilical cord-derived mesenchymal stromal cells (UC-MSCs) on refractory lupus nephritis (LN) and to evaluate the benefits for patients with different baseline renal functions.

## Methods
A total of 120 eligible patients with refractory LN were included in this study. Primary outcomes were the rate of renal response and relapse. Secondary outcomes included laboratory parameters and prednisone dosage. Adverse events (AEs) were recorded using telephone questionnaires and the medical record system.

## Results
Cumulative renal response rates at 3, 6, and 12 months were 38.3% (46/120), 53.3% (64/120), and 56.7% (68/120), respectively. Cumulative relapse rates at 6 and 12 months were 2.9% (2/68) and 8.8% (6/68), respectively. At the 12-month mark, estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) significantly improved in chronic kidney disease stages 2 (CKD 2) and 3a (CKD 3a) patients (P = 0.032 and P = 0.046, respectively). Patients with a baseline eGFR of ≥ 45 ml/min/1.73 m² had a substantially higher renal response rate (66.3%). A baseline eGFR of ≥ 45 ml/min/1.73 m² was an independent predictor for renal response (OR = 2.104, 95% CI 1.063–4.167). One hundred twenty patients received 146 UC-MSCs infusions. Hyperacute AEs occurred in 2.1% (3/146) and infections in 10.9% (16/146). No severe AEs occurred.

## Conclusion
UC-MSCs therapy shows a favorable safety profile and promising efficacy in refractory LN, markedly reducing proteinuria and improving renal function. The therapeutic efficacy is particularly prominent for patients with a baseline eGFR ≥ 45 ml/min/1.73 m².
Trial registration The Clinical Trial Number is NCT00698191.

## Introduction
Systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) is a chronic and relapsing autoimmune disease characterized by the production of diverse autoantibodies, which induce inflammation and damage across multiple organs [ , ]. The conventional treatment for lupus nephritis involves corticosteroids in combination with immunosuppressants, such as cyclophosphamide (CYC) and mycophenolate mofetil (MMF) [ ]. Unfortunately, some patients exhibit minimal or no response to these standard regimens, a condition termed refractory LN, which carries a high risk of progression to ESKD [ ]. This has prompted investigators to explore novel treatment strategies to improve patient prognosis.
Mesenchymal stromal cells (MSCs), sourced from bone marrow, umbilical cord, and adipose tissue, have emerged as a promising candidate for refractory LN [ ]. Several studies have demonstrated the mechanisms by which MSCs treat SLE. These include restoring the regulatory T cells (Tregs) and T-helper 17 cells (Th17) [ ], promoting the proliferation and inhibiting the apoptosis of CD1c+ dendritic cells [ ], cleaving CCL2 to suppress B cell activation [ ], and facilitating tissue repair and regeneration after homing to damaged sites [ ]. Clinically, MSCs infusion has been shown to reduce disease activity in patients with SLE [ – ]. Specifically, a 2014 study from our centre reported a 12-month renal response rate of 60.5% without serious adverse events (AEs) in 81 patients with refractory LN receiving MSCs therapy [ ]. However, previous clinical studies have been limited by the use of MSCs from heterogeneous sources (e.g., umbilical cord-derived [UC-MSCs] and bone marrow-derived [BM-MSCs]), potentially confounding the interpretation of therapeutic efficacy. Furthermore, the influence of baseline renal function on treatment outcomes has not been sufficiently evaluated. To address these gaps, we utilized a larger cohort to investigate the efficacy of a uniform UC-MSCs therapy, specifically assessing how treatment response varies with baseline renal function to generate more precise, evidence-based recommendations.

## Methods

## Participants
Patients who were diagnosed with refractory LN at Nanjing Drum Tower Hospital between January 2009 and December 2022 were enrolled in the study. All participants met the 1997 American College of Rheumatology (ACR) criteria for SLE. The LN was defined by the presence of at least one of the following: (1) Clinical and laboratory criteria (proteinuria > 0.5 g/24 h, and/or the presence of cellular casts, including red blood cells, haemoglobin, granular, tubular, or mixed in urinary sediment). Among them, a spot urine protein to creatinine ratio (UPCR) of > 500 mg/g can be substituted for the 24-hour proteinuria measurement, and active urinary sediment (> 5 RBC/HPF, > 5 WBC/HPF in the absence of infection, or cellular casts limited to RBC or WBC casts) can be substituted for cellular casts. (2) Biopsy-proven International Society of Nephrology/Renal Pathology Society proliferative Class III or IV LN with or without coexisting Class V, or pure Class V LN. “Refractory LN” was defined as unresponsive to previous treatment with one or more successive or simultaneous conventional immunosuppressive drugs (CYC 500–750 mg/m2/month, MMF ≥ 1000 mg/day, or leflunomide (LEF) 20 mg/day, azathioprine (AZA) 100 mg/day, tacrolimus (TAC) ≥ 2 mg/day, alone or in combination for more than 6 months).
Patients were excluded from the study based on the following criteria: (1) Severe organ dysfunction such as heart failure (New York Heart Association functional classification III or IV), hepatic failure, or respiratory failure. (2) Women who were pregnant or lactating, or a woman or man who intended to initiate a pregnancy in the following 6 months. (3) Patients with follow-up time shorter than 12 months. The procedures followed were in accordance with the ethical standards of the Helsinki Declaration and approved by the local Ethics Committee at the Drum Tower Hospital of Nanjing University Medical School (No. 2008017).

## Allogeneic UC-MSCs preparation
Allogeneic UC-MSCs were prepared by the Stem Cell Center of Jiangsu Province under Good Manufacturing Pratice (GMP) conditions and clinical grade reagents for preparation of the cells. Fresh umbilical cords were obtained from healthy mothers in local maternity hospitals after normal deliveries. The cords washed in PBS with penicillin and streptomycin were then cut into 1 mm2 sized pieces and incubated in dulbecco’s modified eagle medium - low glucose (DMEM-LG) medium containing 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), at 37 °C in humid air with 5% CO2. Nonadherent cells were removed by washing. The medium was replaced every 3 days after the initial plating. When well-developed colonies of fibroblast-like cells appeared after 10 days, the cultures were treated with trypsin and subcultured into new flasks for further expansion. At about 80–85% confluence, the adherent cells were detached by treatment with 0.125% trypsin and 0.1% ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) Before infusing, cell viability was determined by trypan blue testing. The culture supernatant was also examined for microorganisms, bacteria, and fungi by direct culture analysis. In addition, supernatant virus indexes were determined by enzyme-linked immunosorbent assays. Release criteria for allogeneic UC-MSCs for clinical use included confirmation of spindle-shaped morphology and a cell viability greater than 92%, absence of visible clumps and of pathogen contamination, including screening for the following viral markers: hepatitis B surface antigen, hepatitis B core antibody, hepatitis C virus antibody, human immunodeficiency virus antibodies I and II, cytomegalovirus IgM, and syphilis antibody. Immunophenotype analysis indicated that the cultured allogeneic UC-MSCs had positive expression of CD29, CD105, CD166, and CD44 and negative expression of CD34, CD45, CD14, CD164, and HLA-DR [ , ]. In vitro osteogenic and adipogenic induction of UC-MSCs was also verified [ ].

## Allogeneic UC-MSCs infusion
The standardized infusion procedure included antiplatelet prophylaxis (aspirin 100 mg/day or clopidogrel hydrogen sulfate 75 mg/day) initiated one day before UC-MSCs infusion and continued for 14 days thereafter. Additionally, patients received 5 mg intravenous dexamethasone 30 min before infusion, followed by 1–2 million UC-MSCs per kilogram of body weight. After the infusion of UC-MSCs, the patient continued to receive treatment according to the original low-dose glucocorticoid and immunosuppressive therapy regimen.

## Data collection
Patients were examined before the intervention (baseline) and during follow-up visits at 3, 6, and 12 months. The following characteristics were recorded: (1) Demographic and general information (age, sex, height, and weight). (2) Disease characteristics: disease duration (month), laboratory parameters (complete blood cell count, serum complement 3, serum complement 4, IgA, IgM, IgG, anti-dsDNA antibody, and serum creatinine, urea nitrogen, albumin, 24-hour proteinuria/UPCR, estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR), urine analysis, and renal biopsy), SLEDAI-2000, system/organ damage, and prednisone dosage, prior immunosuppressant drugs history. (3) Hyperacute AEs: AEs occurring within 24 h after UC-MSCs infusion, including fever (axillary temperature ≥ 37.5℃), fatigue, dizziness, headache, nausea, vomiting, insomnia, constipation, and localized reactions at the injection site. (4) Infection and embolism events: the confirmed infection and embolism events occurring within 12 months after UC-MSCs infusion. (5) Malignant tumors, renal replacement therapy and death events: an electronic questionnaire was distributed to patients in April 2024 to assess their survival status, the presence of any malignant tumors, and renal replacement therapy (including hemodialysis, kidney transplantation, or peritoneal dialysis) since the first UC-MSCs infusion. (6) Serious AEs were defined as death, permanent disability or functional loss, congenital abnormalities, or congenital disabilities occurring within 12 months after UC-MSCs infusion.

## Outcome characteristics
The primary outcomes were the rate of renal response (complete response [CR] and partial response [PR]) and relapse. The secondary outcomes included 24-hour proteinuria, serum albumin, eGFR, serum urea nitrogen, serum creatinine, and prednisone dosage. CR was defined as UPCR < 500 mg/g (equivalent to proteinuria < 0.5 g/24 h) and serum albumin ≥ 35.0 g/L and normal or stable eGFR (within 10% of baseline eGFR, if previously abnormal). PR was defined as UPCR/24-hour proteinuria decreased to less than 50% of the baseline level and UPCR < 3000 mg/g (equivalent to proteinuria < 3.0 g/24 h), serum albumin > 30.0 g/L, and normal or stable eGFR (within 10% of baseline eGFR, if previously abnormal). Disease relapse was defined as an increase in proteinuria (in patients with CR, proteinuria ≥ 1.0 g/24 h or UPCR ≥ 1000 mg/g, in patients with PR, proteinuria/UPCR increased by ≥ 50% from baseline) or a 25% decline in eGFR from baseline. Non-renal response (NR): requiring higher doses of prednisone or immunosuppressant for disease control or failure to reach CR or PR standards for more than 12 months.

## Statistical analysis
Continuous variables were tested for normality using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test, and the median (interquartile range) was used to describe non-normal distribution data. Categorical variables were described in terms of frequencies and percentages. The missing continuous variables were handled using the median imputation method (less than 10% of the data were missing). The Kaplan-Meier survival curve analysed the CR, PR, and relapse rate at different follow-up times. The differences in data before and after the infusions of UC-MSCs were evaluated using the Friedman test. Patients were classified as the response group or the NR group according to whether renal response was achieved within 12 months. Baseline characteristics between the two groups were compared with the Mann-Whitney U and chi-square tests. Additionally, Cox regression analysis identified predictors of renal response. Results were reported as hazard ratios (HR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI). All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 25.0. Figures were displayed by GraphPad software (version 9.5) and Adobe Illustrator 2022 (version 2022).

## Results

## Participant characteristics
Between January 2009 and December 2022, 204 patients with refractory LN were screened. Ultimately, 120 patients (87.5% female) met eligibility criteria and were enrolled (Fig.  ). Exclusions (n = 84) comprised 50 with follow-up time less than 12 months, 26 who did not meet the criteria of refractory LN, and 8 with severe organ dysfunction. Baseline characteristics were presented in Table  . The median age was 36.2 (29.8, 45.7) years, with a median LN disease duration since diagnosis of 118.5 (71.5, 164.0) months. One hundred twenty patients received 146 UC-MSCs infusions within 12 months (22 patients who experienced disease relapse or were deemed at high risk of relapse based on laboratory indicators and clinical symptoms received repeat UC-MSCs infusions during the one-year follow-up period. Specifically, 19 patients received two infusions, 2 patients received three infusions, and 1 patient received four infusions). The baseline proteinuria level was 3.2 (1.4, 5.9) g/24 h, serum albumin was 29.7 (25.0, 35.4) g/L, and eGFR was 89.6 (40.0, 139.5) ml/min/1.73m2. The renal function was staged according to the Guidelines for Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO). Patients with eGFR ≥ 90 ml/min/1.73 m² (CKD stage 1 and normal renal function) accounted for 48.3% (58/120). CKD 2 patients accounted for 19.2% (23/120), CKD 3a for 7.5% (9/120), CKD 3b for 14.2% (17/120), and CKD 4 and CKD 5 together made up a total of 10.9% (13/120). The median SLEDAI-2000 score was 13 (9.0, 18.0). Before receiving MSCs, 94 (78.3%) patients received hydroxychloroquine (HCQ), 76 (63.3%) patients received intravenous CYC, 72 (60.0%) patients received MMF, 47 (39.2%) patients received TAC, 45 (37.5%) patients received LEF, and 11 (9.2%) patients received AZA.
 

## Primary outcome
The probability of renal response was 38.3% (46/120) at 3 months, 53.3% (64/120) at 6 months, and 56.7% (68/120) at 12 months (Fig.  a). We found that compared with the response group, the NR group had a longer disease duration (P = 0.015), higher baseline serum creatinine (P = 0.013), lower baseline eGFR (P = 0.022) and baseline disease activity (P = 0.027). Renal response occurred in 61.0% of patients with higher eGFR (above the median) versus 55.7% with lower eGFR, and in 70.0% with shorter disease duration compared with 46.7% in those with longer disease duration (above the median) (Additional Fig.  ). However, there were no statistically significant differences in patient age, sex, baseline proteinuria levels, and prior medication history between the two groups (Table  ). The cumulative relapse rate was 2.9% (2/68) at 6 months, 8.8% (6/68) at 12 months (Fig.  b).
 

## Secondary outcomes
Following the MSCs infusion, there was a considerable decrease in proteinuria (from 3.2 g/24 h at baseline to 2.2 g/24 h at 3 months, 1.5 g/24 h at 6 months, 1.2 g/24 h at 12 months) (Fig.  c) and a commensurate increase in serum albumin levels (from 29.7 g/L at baseline to 34.1 g/L at 3 months, 34.6 g/L at 6 months, 36.8 g/L at 12 months) (Fig.  d). Although eGFR was slightly higher (from 89.6 ml/min/1.73m2 at baseline to 97.0 ml/min/1.73m2 at 12 months) (Fig.  e) and levels of serum creatinine and urea nitrogen were slightly lower (from 0.86 mg/dl at baseline to 0.80 mg/dl at 12 months, and from 8.6 mmol/L at baseline to 7.3 mmol/L at 12 months, respectively), these differences were not statistically significant, an additional figure shows this in more detail (Additional Fig.  ). The dosage of oral prednisone was significantly decreased following UC-MSCs infusion. It decreased from 20.0 mg/day at baseline to 15.0 mg/day at 3 months, and remained stable at 10.0 mg/day at 6 and 12 months (Fig.  f).

## Subgroup analysis based on renal function
We categorized the patients into distinct subgroups based on their baseline renal function to more accurately assess the impact of varying renal function on the study outcomes. Patients with CKD 2 and CKD 3a experienced significant improvements in renal function following UC-MSCs infusion (from 72.6 ml/min/1.73m2 at baseline to 91.0 ml/min/1.73m2 at 12 months (P = 0.032), and from 53.8 ml/min/1.73m2 at baseline to 87.1 ml/min/1.73m2 at 12 months (P = 0.046), respectively). Patients with CKD 3b also exhibited an improvement trend in renal function (from 36.5 ml/min/1.73m2 at baseline to 41.4 ml/min/1.73m2 at 12 months), while the difference was not statistically significant (P = 0.884) (Fig.  a). In each subgroup, a reduction in proteinuria was observed. However, no statistically significant decrease in proteinuria was noted within the CKD 4–5 subgroup (P = 0.392) (Fig.  b). Furthermore, in comparison to patients with baseline eGFR < 45 ml/min/1.73 m² (CKD 3b-5), those with baseline eGFR ≥ 45 ml/min/1.73m2 (normal and CKD 1-3a) had a significantly greater renal response rate at 12 months (66.3% vs. 34.4%, P = 0.004) (Fig.  c).
 

## Predictors of renal response
Cox regression analysis was conducted to identify predictors of renal response (Table  ). After controlling for confounding variables such as age, gender, disease duration, baseline prednisone dosage, baseline SLEDAI-2000 score, and the number of UC-MSCs infusions, patients with a baseline eGFR ≥ 45 ml/min/1.73 m² were significantly more likely to achieve a renal response after UC-MSCs treatment (OR = 2.104, 95% CI: 1.063–4.167), which is consistent with the findings from the subgroup analysis. Additionally, we found that a history of prior TAC treatment was associated with a poorer response to UC-MSCs therapy (OR = 0.583, 95% CI: 0.344–0.987). No other demographic, laboratory or immunosuppressive variables reached statistical significance.
 

## Safety
As shown in Fig.  and 146 UC-MSCs infusions were performed on 120 patients during a 12-month follow-up. The hyperacute AEs were fever (2/146, 1.37%) and fatigue (1/146, 0.7%). 16 (10.9%) infections occurred within 12 months following the infusion of UC-MSCs. Ten cases involved respiratory tract infections and required hospitalisation. Additionally, there were 1 case of digestive tract infection, 1 case of skin infection, and 2 cases of urinary tract infection. Notably, unique infections included 1 case of cytomegalovirus and 1 case of Epstein-Barr virus (the CMV case presented with low-grade fever and perioral HSV-like lesions and the rash subsided after taking valacyclovir orally, while the EBV case represented subclinical viremia [1.18 × 103 IU/ml] without clinical infectious mononucleosis, clearing spontaneously at follow-up). Three thrombotic events were observed: 2 cases of deep venous thrombosis (4th and 11th month after UC-MSCs infusion, respectively) and 1 case of cerebral infarction (4th month after UC-MSCs infusion). Three patients had malignant tumors during follow-up: 1 with hepatocellular carcinoma (49th month after UC-MSCs infusion), 1 with rectal cancer (16th month after UC-MSCs infusion), and 1 with cervical cancer (17th month after UC-MSCs infusion). By April 2024, five patients had passed away. Two patients died from severe pneumonia leading to respiratory failure (129th and 108th month after UC-MSCs infusion), one from circulatory failure due to ruptured aortic dissection (79th month after UC-MSCs infusion), one from cardiac arrest caused by hyperkalemia (36th month after UC-MSCs infusion), and one from an undetermined cause (87th month after UC-MSCs infusion). There is no evidence to prove that these events are related to the infusion of UC-MSCs. Eighteen (15.0%) patients needed renal replacement therapy: 16 (13.3%) underwent haemodialysis (three of whom later received renal transplant) and 2 (1.7%) received peritoneal dialysis. No serious AEs were discovered.
 

## Discussion
Since 2007, our center has been investigating the therapeutic potential of MSCs in SLE. In 2014, 81 patients with refractory LN were included. After receiving MSCs (BM-MSCs and UC-MSCs) treatment, the 12-month renal response rate reached 60.5% [ ]. Due to the difficulty in obtaining bone marrow donors and our preliminary clinical findings indicating a comparable efficacy between BM-MSCs and UC-MSCs in inducing remission for refractory lupus nephritis, so our center has gradually adopted UC-MSCs for the treatment of patients with refractory LN [ ]. In this study, we have expanded the cohort to 120 refractory LN patients who were treated solely with UC-MSCs and were followed up for 12 months to evaluate renal responses and AEs comprehensively. In addition, we conducted a subgroup analysis based on the baseline eGFR levels to evaluate whether the efficacy of UC-MSCs varied with baseline renal function. The results demonstrate that UC-MSCs treatment shows a favorable safety profile and promising efficacy. Notably, patients with a baseline eGFR ≥ 45 mL/min/1.73 m² achieved greater efficacy. The in vivo half-life of MSCs is relatively short [ ],[ ]. The data from our previous clinical trials indicated that the maximal therapeutic benefit of MSCs infusion in SLE patients occurred between 6 and 9 months after infusion [ ]. Therefore, patients who did not achieve renal response beyond 12 months were classified as NR. In this study, UC-MSCs infusion significantly reduced proteinuria and increased serum albumin. The 12-month cumulative renal response rate reached 56.7%, which is similar to the 60.5% observed in our 2014 cohort. International studies have also corroborated the efficacy of MSCs in LN. An Iranian trial employing adipose-derived MSCs reported that MSCs reached the peak of effectiveness at 3 months, and the renal response rate was 44.4% [ ]. Barbado et al. treated three LN patients using MSCs and observed rapid and sustained reductions in proteinuria within the first month. However, only one patient exhibited improved renal function [ ]. These discrepancies may be attributed to the heterogeneity of MSCs (including donor and tissue sources, culture conditions, cell isolation methods, cryopreservation, and thawing protocols), ethnic diversity, and inconsistent criteria for renal response [ , ]. In light of these considerations, future studies should adopt uniform renal response criteria and systematically compare the therapeutic effectiveness of MSCs from various sources in different ethnic populations. Recently, some researchers have proposed using induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs) as an alternative cell source [ ]. In a recent clinical trial, iPSCs-derived MSCs achieved complete remission in 50% of patients with steroid-refractory graft-versus-host disease, highlighting their therapeutic potential and warranting further exploration in SLE/LN [ ]. Finally, the 12-month relapse rate in our study was 8.8%, substantially lower than the 22.4% reported in 2014. We hypothesize that this discrepancy can be attributed to modifications in the criteria for defining renal relapse.
Although renal function improved following UC-MSCs therapy, the change did not reach statistical significance. Advanced LN is characterized by irreversible pathological lesions—including interstitial fibrosis, crescent formation, and glomerulosclerosis—that markedly diminish the kidney’s responsiveness to pharmacological therapies [ , ]. Therefore, we stratified patients by baseline eGFR to evaluate the relationship between baseline renal function and UC-MSCs efficacy. We found that patients with CKD 2 and CKD 3a achieved the most pronounced improvements in renal function after UC-MSCs infusion, and a consistent reduction in proteinuria was observed across all stages of CKD. The 12-month renal response rate was 66.3% among patients with baseline eGFR ≥ 45 mL/min/1.73 m2, but only 34.4% in those with eGFR < 45 mL/min/1.73 m2. These data demonstrate a significant association between baseline renal function and treatment response to UC-MSCs. An eGFR ≥ 45 mL/min/1.73 m2 more likely indicates that renal injury remains within a reversible window, characterized by mild interstitial fibrosis and a relatively intact capillary network that provides ample homing sites and paracrine space for UC-MSCs. Indeed, the microenvironment in patients with advanced CKD may impair the therapeutic efficacy of UC-MSCs. A study has indicated that uremic toxins can disrupt the redox balance of MSCs, increasing reactive oxygen species and down-regulating hypoxia-inducible factor-1α (HIF-1α) [ ]. Moreover, Wang et al. found that the erythropoietin deficiency further attenuated the repair capacity of MSCs [ ]. Additionally, following the progression of CKD, progressive vascular stenosis and fibrosis also impede the homing of MSCs to injured renal tissue via the bloodstream [ ]. Eighteen patients ultimately required renal replacement therapy in our study. Prospective controlled studies are still required to demonstrate whether UC-MSCs can provide long-term benefits and prevent the progression of ESKD. Additional studies should also evaluate UC-MSCs efficacy across distinct LN pathological types.
In 2019, Wen et al. identified predictors of MSCs treatment reactivity in SLE patients: older age, absence of arthritis, and no prior exposure to CYC or HCQ were associated with complete remission [ ]. In addition, laboratory indicators were also important predictors of the response of active SLE patients to MSCs therapy. SLE patients with higher baseline levels of interferon-γ (IFN-γ) and lower levels of interleukin-6 (IL-6) showed better clinical benefits after receiving MSCs treatment [ ]. Our study confirmed the prognostic value of baseline eGFR, as patients with eGFR ≥ 45 mL/min/1.73 m2 experienced significantly greater therapeutic benefits. Furthermore, patients who had received TAC previously had a poor reaction to UC-MSCs treatment. We speculated that this might be related to the nephrotoxicity of TAC [ ].
Infection and malignancy constitute the two principal safety concerns in MSCs therapy. Patients with SLE are more susceptible to infections and cancers because of disease-related immune dysregulation and long-term immunosuppressive therapy. A Japanese cohort study documented 127 cases of severe infections among 604 patients with autoimmune diseases during 2 years [ ]. In addition, a prospective study recorded 118 cases of severe infection in 1048 SLE patients, and there was no significant difference between the conventional treatment group and the rituximab/belimumab group [ ]. In the study, the one-year incidence of infection was 10.9%. Infection events ranged from mild skin, Epstein-Barr virus, or cytomegalovirus infections to respiratory tract infections requiring hospitalization. Therefore, in contrast to the studies mentioned above, infusion of UC-MSCs was not associated with a significant increase in the risk of infection. On the contrary, emerging evidence has suggested that UC-MSCs may help reduce infection incidence. Proposed mechanisms included the secretion of antimicrobial peptides, disruption of bacterial cell walls, and enhancement of host antibiotic sensitivity [ ]. Future controlled trials are needed to validate this hypothesis. The relationship between UC-MSCs migration and tumorigenesis remains uncertain. As of April 2024, three malignancies have been recorded in our study. However, no evidence proves that UC-MSCs are directly responsible for these occurrences.
In addition to UC-MSCs and BM-MSCs, MSCs from various tissue sources have been explored for treating inflammatory disorders. For instance, adipose-derived stem cells (ASCs) have been successfully used for perianal fistulas in Crohn’s disease [ ], and decidual stromal cells (DSCs) have been investigated for multiple sclerosis, gastrointestinal graft-versus-host disease (GVHD), and infertility-related disorders [ – ]. However, large-scale head-to-head randomized controlled trials directly comparing the therapeutic efficacy of MSCs from different tissue sources in inflammatory diseases are currently lacking. Future research should focus on conducting rigorously designed head-to-head clinical trials and elucidating the mechanisms underlying the survival, homing, and functional interactions of MSCs from diverse origins with tissue-resident immune cells in authentic inflammatory microenvironments.
This study has limitations. Firstly, this research is not a prospective, randomized, double-blind controlled trial. Although the study enrolled refractory LN patients who had failed conventional immunosuppressants, it lacked a control group receiving conventional therapy without UC-MSCs. Consequently, we can only conclude that UC-MSCs combined with immunosuppressive drugs can induce renal response in refractory LN. Secondly, the absence of universal renal biopsy precluded a comprehensive assessment of renal pathology. Future studies should include biopsies and perform analyses stratified by histological class to more precisely evaluate the efficacy of UC-MSCs therapy in LN. Finally, the cohort comprised only Chinese patients, limiting generalizability to other ethnic groups. Multicentric, randomized, controlled trials are urgently required to validate these findings and guide clinical practice.

## Conclusion
Allogeneic UC-MSCs exhibit a excellent safety and promising therapeutic effects, as evidenced by proteinuria reductions and eGFR improvements in patients with refractory LN. The renal benefit is most pronounced in those with a baseline eGFR ≥ 45 mL/min/1.73 m2. Thus, UC-MSCs represent a valuable immunomodulatory option for refractory LN, demonstrating the potential to improve both renal function and overall prognosis.

The registration number is NCT00698191. Informed consent was obtained from all individual participants included in the study. Patients signed informed consent regarding publishing their data.
