## Abstract
Hepatic stellate cells (HSCs) and their activated derivatives, often referred to as myofibroblasts (MFs), play a key role in progression of chronic liver injuries leading to fibrosis, cirrhosis, and hepatocellular carcinoma. Until recently, MFs were considered a homogenous cell type majorly due to lack of techniques that allow complex molecular studies at a single-cell resolution. Recent technical advancements in genetic lineage-tracing models as well as the exponential growth of studies with single-cell transcriptome and proteome analyses have uncovered hidden heterogeneities among the HSC and MF populations in healthy states as well as chronic liver injuries at the various stages of tissue deformation. The identification of different phenotypes along the HSC/MF axis, which either maintain essential liver functions (“good” HSCs), emerge during fibrosis (“bad” HSCs), or even promote hepatocellular carcinoma (“ugly” HSCs), may lay the foundation for targeting a particular MF phenotype as potential treatment for chronic liver injuries.
Keywords: Hepatic stellate cells, myofibroblasts, liver fibrosis, hepatocellular carcinoma

## Summary
This review provides the emerging landscapes of heterogeneity among hepatic stellate cells in healthy liver and during the progression of liver injury from fibrosis to cancer. In addition, approaches to target hepatic stellate cells to treat liver injuries have been discussed in this review.

Hepatic stellate cells (HSCs) reside in the space of Disse, which spreads between hepatocytes and liver sinusoids, and represent up to 10% of resident non-parenchymal liver cells. Due to a “star-like” morphology, HSCs maintain direct physical contact with hepatocytes, endothelial cells, Kupffer cells, and neighboring HSCs. Their physiological functions encompass regulation of retinoid storage and metabolism, as well as secretion of cytokines and growth factors, and potentially antigen presentation. , , , Following liver injury, HSCs transdifferentiate to pro-fibrogenic myofibroblasts (MFs). The cells lose their vitamin A droplets, become contractile, highly proliferative, responsive to proinflammatory cytokines, and produce excessive extracellular matrix (ECM) to physically restrain the injury. The process of HSC activation is sequential and can be divided into an initiation and a perpetuation phase. Paracrine signals from neighboring cells such as hepatocytes, immune cells, and liver sinusoidal endothelial cells, as well as changes in ECM composition, contribute to the initiation phase. Macrophages secrete transforming growth factor-beta (TGF-β) and platelet-derived growth factor (PDGF), key cytokines that are crucial for ECM synthesis and HSC proliferation, respectively. Liver sinusoidal endothelial cells activate a latent form of TGF-β, which can bind to type I TGF-β receptor on the HSC surface and induce the expression of fibrogenic genes via SMAD3 and the MAPK signaling cascade. Dying hepatocytes expose damage associated molecular patterns and reactive oxygen species. Expression of early immediate genes rapidly increases, rendering HSCs responsive to these stimuli. The perpetuation phase refers to amplification and maintenance of the activated phenotype, which is achieved by both autocrine and paracrine signals.
Until 2013, HSCs were characterized as an important fibrogenic cell types, but their relative contribution to liver fibrogenesis in vivo remained undetermined. It was speculated that MFs derive from various cellular sources and were triggered depending of the type of chronic liver injury. Already in 2010, the potential contribution of hepatocytes and cholangiocytes to the MF pool was excluded with the help of cell fate tracing studies. However, there were still discussions regarding the role of bone marrow-derived fibrocytes in chronic liver disease. One of the earlier in vivo techniques to genetically trace HSC was based on glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP) promoter-driven Cre recombinase. Based on studies from Robert Schwabe and his group, it appears that some strains of GFAP transgenic mice not only show little or no labeling of HSC, but also mark other hepatic cells, such as cholangiocytes. Such labeling patterns in the GFAP-Cre model might have skewed some results from earlier cell fate studies. , Using a Cre reporter driven by the HSC-specific lecithin-retinol acyltransferase (Lrat) gene promoter, Robert Schwabe’s group brought unequivocal evidence that HSCs are the main source of hepatic MFs, regardless of disease trigger. 

## HSC Heterogeneity in Healthy Liver
HSCs are typically viewed as a homogenous cell population. Accumulating evidence, mainly derived from transcriptional profiling studies, however, revealed that HSCs already show heterogeneity in non-conditioned normal liver. One of the first reports on HSC heterogeneity appeared in 2011. Using two different methods of HSC isolation—(1) density gradient centrifugation followed by vitamin A autofluorescence-based fluorescence-activated cell sorting and (2) purification of green fluorescent protein (GFP)-positive cells from the livers of Col1a1-GFP transgenic mice—authors have shown that healthy, uninjured liver may contain at least 2 types of HSCs. These populations differ in retinoid storing capacity as well as expression of activation-associated genes. Even though the total lipid volume was similar in both populations, HSCs purified based on vitamin A autofluorescence (termed “AF-HSC”) had higher contents of retinyl esters, triglycerides, and larger lipid droplets in general, whereas the droplets from GFP-positive HSC (termed “GFP-HSC”) were more numerous and smaller. Transcriptional profiling of AF-HSC and GFP-HSC indicated more than 5300 differentially expressed genes. Transcripts upregulated in GFP-HSC included classic activation markers such as Col1a1 and Acta2, as well as triglyceride-, diacylglycerol-, and lipoprotein lipases, phospholipases, retinyl ester hydrolases, and retinoid-catabolizing enzymes. The latter can explain the reduced retinoid and triglyceride content and the increased number of lipid droplets. Loss of vitamin A droplets is a well-known feature of HSC activation. Therefore, authors speculated that liver contains “pre-activated” HSCs even in physiological conditions. These pre-activated HSCs probably aim to rapidly respond to environmental stimuli upon liver injury and induce activation of neighboring quiescent HSCs.
In later years, more detailed studies on HSC heterogeneity became possible with single-cell RNA sequencing (RNAseq) techniques. In 2021, Payen et al analyzed HSCs from normal human liver and recovered 246 single-cell HSC transcriptomes. HSCs fell into two clusters with unique gene expression signatures. Both clusters expressed well-established HSC markers such as COLEC11, RGS5, HGF, and NGFR, although levels of these transcripts varied. The separation into distinct populations did not primarily reflect the activation status, since expression of known fibrogenic markers was distributed between both clusters, although a tendency of higher expression of activation-associated markers and lower expression of quiescence-associated genes was observed in cluster “HSC-1.” Gene ontology (GO) analysis attributed signature genes of “glycosaminoglycan metabolism” and “elastic fiber” networks to HSC-1, whereas a “HLA class II” network was enriched in the HSC-2 cluster. Some other studies indeed discussed potential antigen-presenting properties of HSC. , , Payen et al, however, could not identify expression of two key HLA class II co-stimulatory molecules, CD80 and CD86, in the HSC-2 population, raising doubts about antigen-presenting properties at least under physiological conditions. Using in situ hybridization of 2 HSC signature genes of the clusters, GPC3 and DBH, they analyzed their spatial zonation across the liver lobule. Although HSC-1 cluster cells were restricted to periportal and pericentral areas, HSC-2 cells were distributed throughout the parenchyma. Both HSC-1 and HSC-2 clusters expressed unique types of collagens and proteoglycans, indicating that both populations participate in ECM homeostasis. The two HSC clusters also differentially expressed cytokines (for instance, HSC-1 displayed higher levels of CCL2, CCL21, and HDGF, whereas HSC-2 expressed HGF, CXCL14, and PGF).
Andrews et al performed single-cell transcriptomic profiling of human healthy liver combining scRNA-seq and snRNA-seq, the latter allowing to capture mesenchymal cells with higher frequencies. Mesenchymal cells were divided into 7 clusters. Clusters 1, 5, and 7 expressed quiescent HSC markers, such as HGF and LRAT, whereas the other clusters were enriched for transcripts characteristic for activated MFs. Cellular identity was further dissected by cross-species correlation analysis with single-cell transcriptomic data from Dobie et al. Although clusters 2 and 4 coincided with fibroblasts, clusters 3 and 6 coincided with vascular smooth muscle cells (VSMCs). Only cluster 1 strongly correlated with both healthy and fibrotic murine HSC signatures. Cluster 5 showed lower expression of quiescent markers, but more transcripts related to antigen presentation and inflammation. Noteworthy, Payen et al, also found a population of HSCs exhibiting gene expression patterns related to antigen-presenting properties. Finally, Andrews et al analyzed zonation pattern with spatial transcriptomics and confirmed disperse distribution of HSCs in parenchyma, whereas PFs and VSMCs were rather present in the periportal area. 

## HSC Heterogeneity in Fibrotic Liver
Single-cell transcriptomes from 5 healthy and 5 cirrhotic donors were analyzed by Ramachandran and colleagues. Resting HSCs were characterized by the expression of RGS5 and absence in the fibrotic septae. According to pseudo-temporal ordering, these RGS5+ cells gave rise to activated MFs, which were termed “scar-associated mesenchymes” (SAMes). Upon trans-differentiation SAMes lost RGS5, ADAMTS1, IGFBP5, and GEM expression with concomitant upregulation of TIMP1, COL3A1, COL1A1, COL1A2, and PDGFRA. SAMes were further subdivided into two clusters, SAMesA and SAMesB ( ). Because Ramachandran et al did not proceed with sub-clustering of resting HSCs, post-hoc analysis was performed by Payen et al. Single-cell HSC transcriptomes from the 5 healthy donors could be separated into 3 different populations. Two of these populations were found to be enriched for “HSC-1” and “HSC-2” signature genes from the Payen dataset, confirming that clusters identified previously could be robustly reproduced in the additional dataset from healthy human donors.
 In 2019, Dobie et al performed scRNA-seq of Pdgfrb expressing cells from healthy and injured livers of Pdgfrb-GFP knock-in reporter mice. HSCs were distinguished from VSMCs and portal fibroblasts by Rgs5 expression and split into two clusters with unique transcriptional signatures. Interestingly, Ngfr, one of the signature HSC genes, was not uniformly distributed between two clusters, and the same finding was later confirmed by Payen et al. Co-staining with known hepatocyte markers revealed that Ngfr-high HSCs residing together with portal vein-associated E-cadherin+ hepatocytes, and Adamtsl2-high HSC located around the central vein, neighboring Cyp2e1+ hepatocytes. The same zonation pattern was also confirmed in the human healthy liver by co-staining of NGFR and ADAMTSL2 with pan-HSC marker RGS5. This contrasts findings from Payen et al, which identified vessel-associated HSC-1 and diffuse HSC-2 clusters. Importantly, zonation-associated gene expression signatures persisted upon carbon tetrachloride (CCl4)-induced centrilobular injury. However, although portal vein-associated HSCs (paHSCs) only produced proliferative response, central vein-associated HSCs (caHSCs) gave rise to the majority (88%) of collagen-producing activated MFs, which populated the fibrotic niche. These cells lost expression of Colec11, Rgs5, Reln, Vipr1, Hgf, Ecm1, and Pth1r with parallel upregulation of Col1a1, Col1a2, Col3a1, and Lox. Dobie et al did not induce periportal liver injury in mice, and this leaves room to speculate that upon periportal necrosis paHSCs might become the main contributors to ECM deposition. Of note, Payen et al performed post-hoc analysis of the dataset from this work to compare their HSC-1 and HSC-2 clusters with caHSC and paHCS. Even though some common differentially expressed genes were found (GEM, SPON2, LOXL1, PDN in caHSC and HSC-1; NGFR, IGFBP3, TNFRSF11B, CXCL14 in paHSC and HSC-2), a consistent pattern was lacking, underlying the differences in HSC biology between murine and human tissue.
Another work describing HSC heterogeneity in healthy and CCl4-treated mouse liver was conducted by Krenkel and colleagues. Contrary to previous findings, quiescent HSCs from uninjured tissue were highly homogenous. In contrast, activated MFs separated into four different clusters. Both the HSC and MF identities were defined by the expression of Pdgfrb, whereas markers such as Acta2, Col3a1, and Tgfbi distinguished activated cells. MF clusters I, III, and IV expressed high levels of ECM-associated genes, which were attributed to “collagen fibril organization” and “extracellular matrix buildup” GO categories. Cells from cluster II displayed much lower activity of matrix-associated genes, but at the same time were enriched for transcripts related to immune regulation and inflammation, such as leukocyte protease inhibitor (Slpi), complement factor C3 (C3), serum amyloid A3 (Saa3), and cluster of differentiation 74 (Cd74). Thus, this MF population may possess immunomodulatory roles rather than contribute to ECM accumulation and contractility. In accordance with these findings, the classic activation marker Acta2 was almost undetectable in cluster II. Interestingly S100A6 appeared to be uniformly upregulated in all 4 clusters of activated MF. Finally, the authors also subjected in vitro activated primary mouse HSC to scRNA-seq at different timepoints of harvesting. Secretion of chemokines was detectable only at the early timepoints, and this capacity was lost with time, indicating that in vitro activation cannot fully recapitulate the data observed in in vivo studies.
Later, post-hoc analysis of the Krenkel scRNA-seq dataset was performed to get more information about transcriptional dynamics during HSC in vitro activation. Cells harvested on day 0, 1, 3, 7, and 9 of in vitro culture separated into 14 clusters, containing 7 quiescent and 7 activated HSC subpopulations ( ). Trajectory analysis revealed that HSCs from early culture (days 0 and 1; state 1) branches into 2 distinct HSC states (state 2 and 3), both representing activated myofibroblasts and expressing high levels of Acta2, Ccn2, and Mmp10. Although state 3 represented classically culture-activated HSC, cells in state 2 displayed 2 unique transcriptomic features: (1) high expression of ribosomal genes, which can reflect enhanced translational capacity, and (2) high levels of tumorigenesis-related transcripts, some of them known to be involved in liver carcinogenesis. Analysis of publicly available bulk RNA-seq datasets revealed that the state 2 HSC expression signature was closely related to the HSC-derived cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) signature. Thus, this subpopulation may potentially contribute to the establishment of a premalignant environment. Importantly, the above described branching and HSC states were reproduced in a post-hoc analysis of scRNA-seq dataset from Dobie et al. Using GeneSwitches statistical framework, authors defined the order of gene expression, which can be critical for HSC transition between different states. Although Cdkn1a, Tnfrsf12a, Hmox1, Thbs1, Acta2, Timp1, and Xist were upregulated during early HSC activation, expression of Vipr1, Steap4, Angptl6, Rgs5, Tgfbi, and Pth1r started to fall down right after the beginning of in vitro culture. The expression of Fau and Rpl30 was distinct for “classic” culture-activated HSCs (state 3), and Rps27, Eif4a1, Krtcap2, Clic1, and Ankrd1 were upregulated during state 2 HSC branching. Because state 2 HSC are transcriptionally close to HSC-derived CAF, genetic manipulation of switching genes may render a great therapeutic potential to prevent the establishment of tumor-prone environments. However, compared with MFs in fibrosis, the origin, heterogeneity, and activation process of CAF in liver cancer is poorly understood. Thus, investigating the potential contribution of other cell types to the CAF pool is warranted.
Yang et al performed trajectory analysis of HSC in healthy mouse liver as well as in CCl4- and bile duct ligation (BDL)-injured tissues. HSCs expressed known markers like Des, Lrat, Pdgfrb, Rgs5, Reln, Pth1r, Tmem56, Vipr1, Angptl6, and Fcna. Interestingly, the authors identified 2 genes, whose expression have not been previously described as HSC-specific: Guanylate Cyclase 1 Soluble Subunits Gucy1a1 and Gucy1b1. Bulk RNA-seq and analysis of Gucy1a1-EGFP reporter mice confirmed that this gene was not expressed in other liver cell types. When control, CCl4, and BDL conditions were pooled together, HSCs divided into 2 clusters—quiescent (qHSCs) and activated (aHSCs). Noteworthy, expression of Gucy1a1 and Gucy1b1 was significantly higher in qHSCs. HSC trajectory analysis revealed 4 gene modules along the pseudo-timeline. Module 1 contained well-established quiescence markers like Lrat and Rgs5, but also Vipr1 and newly identified Gucy1a1 and Gucy1b1. Their expression decreased with early activation. Genes from the 2nd module, which contained inflammatory cytokines Ccl2 and Cxcl10, peaked at the early stage of activation and then quickly decreased. Module 3 encompassed contractility and migration-related transcripts, such as Acta2 and Tagln. Finally, module 4 was represented by collagens, whose expression was rising slowly and peaked at the end stage of activation. Importantly, gene expression dynamics consisting of 4 modules is in line with findings from Krenkel, who defined 4 clusters of HSCs, showing different levels of collagen expression and 1 cluster composed of cytokine-expressing HSCs. Based on the defined modules, aHSCs were further subdivided into 3 subclusters: cytokine expressing stage 1 aHSCs, contractile stage 2 aHSCs, and ECM-producing stage 3 aHSCs ( ). Although aHSCs in CCl4-injured livers were mostly composed of stage 2 and 3 cells, the aHSC pool in the BDL model was rather represented by stage 1 aHSCs, showing that HSCs become more activated in CCl4-induced fibrosis. However, overall collagen deposition is higher in BDL compared with the CCl4 model, which contradicts significantly lower numbers of stage 2 and 3 aHSCs in BDL. Authors came to the conclusion that the main collagen-producing cell type in cholestatic injury consist of periportal fibroblasts, which highly resemble stage 3 aHSCs according to scRNA-seq and overlap with this HSC population in BDL, less in CCl4, and are completely distant in uninjured livers. Moreover, cell fate tracing studies with the newly identified HSC-specific gene Gucy1a1, showed labeling of almost all collagen-producing cells in CCl4 injury, but not in BDL. In contrast, ECM deposition was mediated by Thy1-positive PF in BDL. Finally, pharmacological activation of soluble guanylate cyclase, whose expression was lost in aHSCs, was only effective for CCl4-induced, not BDL-induced, fibrosis amelioration. This contradicts previous findings from Lrat-Cre lineage tracing studies, stating that HSCs are main drivers of fibrosis in cholestatic injury. As the authors speculate, Lrat may also be expressed in PF precursors during embryonic development, resulting in labeling of these cells. Additional experiments with Lrat-driven inducible Cre recombinase and co-staining with PF markers will shed more light on the source of ECM-producing cells in cholestatic liver injury.
Kostallari and colleagues primarily focused on the stiffness heterogeneity of liver tissue. As expected, stiffness dramatically increased upon CCl4- and BDL-induced injuries. Moreover, tissue response to deformation varied in different parts of the organ, implying that collagen deposition was uneven. To answer the question whether stiffness heterogeneity can promote HSC heterogeneity via mechanosensitive pathways, authors performed scRNA-seq and distinguished HSCs from other cell types by Des, Pdgfra, Pdgfrb, Dcn, Col1a1, and Col1a2 expression. Two subclusters were identified both in healthy and CCl4-injured liver, one of which showed high Col1a1, Adamtsl2, Acta2, and Alcam expression. This subcluster was enriched in collagen-high areas and termed collagen-high HSC (HSCcol-high). On the opposite, the second sub-cluster had low Col1a1 and Adamtsl2 expression, but high levels of quiescence-associated transcripts: acid-binding protein 1 (Fabp1) and betaine-homocysteine S-methyltransferase (Bhmt). These cells were referred to as collagen-low HSCs (HSCscol-low). Interestingly, HSCcol-high cluster was also present in healthy tissue, even though it lacked collagen-rich areas. This data confirms previous assumptions on the existence of a “prone to activation” population of HSCs. However, HSCscol-high from healthy and fibrotic liver differed in the upregulated pathways, because only HSCscol-high from fibrotic liver had focal adhesion transcriptomic signatures, implying that physical interaction with collagen may shape intracellular signaling pathways. HSCscol-high from fibrotic livers were further sub-clustered with different resolution and divided into 3 populations. One of these subclusters displayed higher expression levels of focal adhesion molecules: Fhl2, Alcam, and Hspb1. Thus, heterogeneity can be found even within activated myofibroblasts residing in stiff, collagen-rich areas. Fhl2, one of the most upregulated transcripts in the above-mentioned population, was highly specific for HSCs vs other cell types. Interestingly, its expression was upregulated in vitro when primary human HSCs were cultured on stiffer matrix yet was not affected by PDGF-BB and TGFβ treatment. Further research is warranted to assess Fhl2 potential as HSC-specific target for fibrosis gene and pharmacotherapy.

## HSC Heterogeneity in Nonalcoholic Fatty Liver Disease and Nonalcoholic Steatohepatitis
He and colleagues performed post-hoc analysis of 6 available scRNA-seq datasets from 4 healthy and 2 nonalcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD)-affected human livers with fibrosis. In both conditions, 2 HSC sub-clusters were identified—resting HSCs (rHSCs) and aHSCs, both of which changed expression profiles upon transition from NAFLD to nonalcoholic steatohepatitis (NASH) ( ). In NASH, there was a clear increase in the proportion of aHSCs, a finding that was also confirmed in mouse models of CCl4-induced fibrosis and NASH-associated fibrosis in mice. Weighted gene co-expression network analysis revealed that increase in the aHSC proportion is concomitant with immune response-related pathways upregulation. This is in line with recent findings from Yang et al, who reported that an immune response gene module is upregulated in early stages of HSC activation. Further, He and colleagues demonstrated that, upon transition to NASH, there is not only a shift in the rHSC to aHSC ratio, but also in certain transcriptional changes in both sub-populations. For instance, NASH-associated rHSCs increased the expression of ID3, NOTCH3, and CRIP1, whereas NASH-associated aHSC showed elevated IFITM1, MIF, COL1A1, COL1A2, and COL3A1 transcripts levels. Interestingly, acquisition of NASH-associated aHSC signature expression could be reproduced in vitro by adding TGF-β to primary mouse HSCs. Post-hoc analysis of 2 bulk RNA-seq datasets showed that NASH-associated aHSC signature becomes more prominent with transition from mild to advanced fibrosis in NAFLD-affected human livers, and thus can represent the severity of fibrotic changes. Bioinformatic analysis of upstream regulators identified SDC1, GRP, SDC4, and MUC1 as crucial for HSC activation, and JunB was critical for the NASH-associated aHSC signature.
The Friedman research group recently addressed the role of HSC in NASH. ScRNA-seq was performed in both human and mouse liver samples, including unaffected and NASH-affected tissues. Although no heterogeneity was observed, neither in healthy nor in NASH conditions, HSCs from patients with NASH were transcriptionally different. In accordance with previous studies, “cell-matrix adhesion” and “integrin signaling” were among upregulated pathways in NASH-associated HSCs. Moreover, bioinformatic analysis with the CellPhoneDB database predicted increased ligand-receptor interactions between HSCs, cholangiocytes, and endothelial cells in NASH. This is in line with previously reported elevated paracrine and autocrine communication between HSCs with endothelial and immune cells in fibrotic liver. , The mouse NASH model, which was a combination of CCl4 injections and Western diet, recapitulated the data obtained from human samples. HSCs were separated into “HSC1,” mostly derived from control samples and expressing Lrat and Des, and NASH-associated “HSC2,” displaying elevated levels of Acta2, Timp1, Timp2, Mmp2, Pdgfrb, Col1a1, and Col3a1 transcripts. No HSC heterogeneity was reported within healthy and NASH conditions individually, in accordance with the observations made in human liver tissue. Most importantly, researchers not only confirmed increased interactions of HSCs with cholangiocytes and endothelial cells in silico but also employed laborious techniques to assess physical contacts. Evidence from reconstructed high-resolution 3-dimensional images indicated elevated numbers and lengths of HSC projections during NASH progression. HSCs established physical contacts with each other, creating a network for more efficient autocrine and paracrine regulation. Researchers further focused on the Neurotrophic tyrosine kinase receptor type 3—Neurtrophin-3 (NTRK3-NTF3) autocrine receptor-ligand pair, whose role in HSC activation was not elucidated before. Ntrk3 expression was drastically increased in mouse NASH-associated HSC2, and its pharmacological inhibition in vivo reduced all major fibrosis characteristics. Interestingly, a recent scRNA-seq analysis of mouse NASH livers from Wang et al contradicts observations of facilitated ligand-receptor HSC interactions reported in the present study, as well as in Xiong et al’s study. Wang and colleagues, on the opposite, described a vast reduction in HSC communication with other cell types using CellPhoneDB. However, all 3 studies utilized different NASH models. While the Friedman group kept the mice on a Western diet combined with 5% CCl4 injections for 24 weeks and Xiong et al used an amylin liver NASH diet for 20 weeks, Wang et al employed a choline-deficient diet for only 3 weeks. Thus, there is a possibility that HSC interactions with other cell types transiently decrease at the early stages of NASH.

## Functional HSC Heterogeneity in Hepatocellular Carcinoma
Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) mostly occurs in cirrhotic livers with an overall incidence of 1% to 6% per year. Such a tight connection of organ fibrosis and carcinogenesis is not observed in desmoplastic tumors such as intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma or pancreatic carcinoma, which are characterized by the presence of cancer-associated fibroblasts. The co-incidence of cirrhosis and HCC development, however, does not automatically establish a causal relationship. Although the role of activated HSCs as the main source of excessive ECM deposition in chronic liver disease is well-established, far less information is available on their precise role in promoting HCC.
In a recent study from the Schwabe group, faithful lineage tracing model of Lrat-driven Cre was utilized to elucidate the role of HSCs in multiple models of fibrosis-associated HCC. The first surprising finding was that HSC-specific knockouts of Tp53 or RelA, 2 key mediators of senescence, did not promote or inhibit HCC growth as measured by liver to body weight ratio, tumor size, and numbers of tumor nodules. Previous studies showed conflicting data. , , Although Lujambio et al showed a protective role of p53-induced HSC senescence, Yoshimoto et al reported that HSC senescence rather promotes liver cancer. Likewise, a few studies indicated a positive correlation between fibrosis severity and HCC risk, , whereas others did not find conclusive evidence. These results prompted the Schwabe group to focus on a more global role of HSCs beyond senescence. They generated mice with genetically activated or inhibited HSCs by introducing HSC-specific Lhx2 or Yap knockouts, respectively, and combined these genetic models with HSC depletion by diphtheria toxin or ganciclovir. Putting these mice under multiple HCC-inducing protocols, the researchers proved that HSCs overall exerted an HCC-promoting role. Moreover, reduced tumorigenesis upon HSC depletion or inactivation was accompanied by reduced αSMA and collagen deposition, which confirms a previous hypothesis that fibrosis may play a causal role in promoting HCC development. The authors then addressed whether HSCs are able to alter the premalignant environment towards tumor formation. Transcriptomic profiling of tumor and non-tumor tissue in control mice and mice lacking expression of activating Yap in HSCs attributed most differences to the surrounding tissue, but not the tumors. Indeed, HSC inactivation or depletion reduced hepatocyte death and hepatocyte proliferation signals outside, but not within the tumors. Likewise, less activated HSCs residing in the nodules did not affect HCC development, providing an early indication that their role lies outside established tumors.
To identify mechanisms and specific mediators, through which HSCs change the milieu, the Schwabe group performed scRNA-seq and snRNA-seq from mouse and human livers, respectively. Based on the calculated signature score, composed of the activation markers (Acta2, Timp1, Lox, and Col1a1), 2 distinct HSC populations were identified—most activated and least activated. According to top deregulated pathways, these populations were termed either myofibroblastic HSCs (myHSCs), which are enriched for ECM genes, or cytokine HSCs (cyHSCs), which are enriched for cytokine/growth factor genes. Importantly, myHSCs gradually increased and cyHSCs gradually decreased during fibrosis progression. Col1a1 and Hgf, top upregulated genes in the 2 populations, respectively, showed the same dynamic expression changes in fibrosis development.
At the functional level, Col1a1ΔHSC mice showed reduced tumorigenesis only in severe but not in moderate underlying fibrosis, indicating a threshold for liver stiffness that favors HCC development. Indeed, a previous report suggested that patients with liver stiffness measurements >12 kPA are at higher risk for HCC. Of note, Col1a1ΔHSC mice did not display less HSC activation or hepatocyte death, but showed lower hepatocyte proliferation and a trend toward increased immune cell infiltration in both tumor and non-tumor compartments, indicating a role of type I collagen (Col1) in mounting a physical barrier for immune cells. Reduced levels of 3 known components of a mechanosensitive pathway, p-Akt, TAZ, and YAP, supported the prominent role of tissue stiffness for HCC progression. Consistently, hepatocyte-specific TAZ deletion reduced tumorigenesis and hepatocyte proliferation outside the tumors. This evidence supports the role of the Col1-stiffness-TAZ pathway in creating a pro-tumorigenic milieu in the fibrotic liver.
The extracellular domain of discoidin domain receptor 1 (DDR1) binds to collagen and promotes fiber alignment in triple-negative breast cancer, which in turn inhibits immune cell entry and assists malignant nodules to escape immune surveillance. Filliol and co-workers observed increased DDR1 abundance in both tumors and adjacent tissues in human and mouse livers with HCC. Moreover, at the cellular level, DDR1 expression was attributed to hepatocytes and HCC cells, thus indicating the response of parenchymal cells to ECM production by HSCs. Of note, hepatocyte-specific knockout of DDR1 reduced hepatocyte proliferation only within the tumors, but not in surrounding tissue.
Recent work of Baglieri et al demonstrated that transgenic mice producing collagenase-resistant type I collagen developed more severe hepatic fibrosis but remained less prone to HCC. How do these findings reconcile with the now presented data that fibrosis grade and liver stiffness positively correlate with HCC incidence? This discrepancy was solved by Filliol et al via elegant experiments, which showed that DDR1 is activated by degraded but not intact collagen. Moreover, analysis of The Cancer Genome Atlas dataset showed a correlation of poor survival with high expression of collagen-degrading matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs) in patients with HCC. Similarly, Su et al demonstrated that a collagenolysis-DDR1 pathway is associated with poor outcome in pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma. Thus, the collagen degradation status may directly affect carcinogenesis via DDR1 upregulation in HCC. Importantly, TAZ levels were not reduced in the liver from Ddr1ΔHep mice. The authors concluded that, unlike the Col1-stiffness-TAZ pathway, which modifies the premalignant environment, the Col1-DDR1 pathway supports development of established tumors.
The scRNA-seq data identified hepatocytes as the main cell population expressing the hepatocyte growth factor (HGF) receptor c-Met. 
HgfΔHSC mice displayed more fibrosis and tumorigenesis, with increased hepatocyte proliferation and liver injury parameters. Additionally, targeted Hgf deletion in HSC did not affect hepatocyte proliferation after partial hepatectomy (PH). Previous studies indicated reduced hepatocyte expansion after PH in mice with conditional c-Met knockout in the liver. , , In accordance to c-Met knockout models, 2 studies that utilized conditional Hgf knockout also demonstrated impaired hepatocyte proliferation after a single CCl4 dose or PH. However, in contrast to the present study, where Hgf deletion was induced specifically in HSC by Lrat-Cre, in the 2 above-mentioned studies, Hgf expression was shut down in the whole liver by Mx1-Cre, Cre-ERT, or adenoviral vector-encoded Cre. Thus, residual HGF from endothelial cells might compensate for compromised regeneration. Filliol et al showed that per cell HGF mRNA level in cyHSCs declines with chronic liver disease progression. However, net expression in the entire liver increases, probably representing a compensation through higher numbers of HSCs. This compensation, however, fails in advanced disease stages as demonstrated by reduced HGF expression in HCC nodules. Likewise, a cohort of patients with HCC with reduced HGF displayed poor survival. The same was true for increased myHSC/cyHCS ratio. Patients with a higher prevalence of myHSC over cyHSC were more prone to HCC development.
Filliol and colleagues separated HSCs into most and least activated clusters—myHSC and cyHSC, respectively. The cyHSC subpopulation restrains hepatocyte proliferation and death through secreted HGF, whereas myHSCs produce excessive ECM, in particular, type 1 collagen ( ). Collagen deposition gradually increases, making the liver stiffer up to a threshold that favors tumor development via the Col1-TAZ pathway. Concomitant upregulation of MMPs leads to collagen degradation, whose fragments are sensed by DDR1 on the hepatocyte surface. The Col1-DDR1 pathway supports the growth of already established tumors. The decrease of HGF-enriched cyHSC population is compensated by overall HSC proliferation that still produce HGF but fail to restore HGF levels at advanced disease stages. This results in further necrosis, increased hepatocyte proliferation, and formation of new tumors.
 One of the major questions relates to the exact triggers that induce cyHSC decline and myHSC expansion. According to the trajectory and pseudotime analyses in this study, myHSCs do arise from cyHSCs. Depleting HGF-expressing cells at early stages of chronic liver disease could prevent the expansion of myHSCs at later stages. At the same time, cyHSC ablation would abolish the hepatoprotective effect of cyHSCs. HSC inactivation in more progressive fibrosis, when the myHSC/cyHSC ratio is already elevated, would probably be more clinically relevant; however, the consequences in an ongoing inflammatory environment remain unknown. Currently, there are no precise tools to selectively shut off the expression of Col1a1 or Hgf in myHSCs and cyHSCs, respectively. Thus, Col1 and HGF functions can only be investigated in the whole HSC population. Moreover, since it is likely that cyHSCs and myHSCs display their functions not only via HGF and Col1, other mediators warrant further investigation. Even though type I collagen is one of the major components of scar tissue, ECM composition is diverse and encompasses other collagens, glycoproteins, and proteoglycans. Hence, the set of receptors sensing myHSC-derived ligands goes far beyond DDR1. Moreover, HSC-derived CAF can exert tumor-promoting roles via ECM-independent mechanisms. These include nutrient supply via autophagy and contribution to drug resistance through impaired drug delivery. 

## HSC Zonation
The aspect of HSC zonation was addressed in a number of studies. Andrews et al mapped HSC sub-clusters on frozen liver sections with spatial transcriptomic technology, which allows visualization of transcripts distribution within the tissue context. Ramachandran et al solely relied on fluorescent immunohistochemistry, whereas Payen, Dobie, and Filliol combined in situ hybridization with antibody staining. , , Dobie additionally employed co-staining with hepatocyte markers, thus obtaining information on HSC sub-classes distribution in relation to central and portal vein. 
HSC zonation is most comprehensively characterized in fibrotic tissue, particularly in hepatotoxic injury. According to Ramachandran, Filliol, Dobie, and Kostallari, activated, collagen-producing HSCs reside in fibrotic septae, whereas less activated HSCs stay in unaffected parenchyme ( ). This zonation pattern may represent a positive-feedback loop, where the activated HSCs contribute to increased collagen production, leading to heightened tissue stiffness. In turn, stiffness triggers mechanosensitive pathways within activated HSCs, which result in a more activated phenotype and might also affect less activated HSCs. A similar feedback loop is described for CAF in breast cancer, where stiff ECM drives YAP signaling within CAF, thereby sustaining tumor-promoting phenotype. 
 In contrast, the available data on zonation in a healthy liver are less conclusive. Andrews et al and Ramachandran et al did not report spatial heterogeneity in uninjured tissue. Payen et al, on the other hand, identified 2 distinct HSC subpopulations, one in proximity to the vessels and the other residing in the parenchyma. Dobie et al annotated HSCs associated with central and portal vein branches. Consequently, there is a need for more extensive efforts to unravel HSC zonation in the healthy liver. In this regard, employing co-staining with markers for other cell types, for which zonation is firmly established, could be beneficial. Additionally, the characterization of HSC spatial distribution in NASH and cholestatic liver injury is way less definitive. The latter is particularly significant, because the question of whether HSC or portal fibroblasts are the main contributors to the MF pool remains unresolved.

## Potential Approaches for Therapeutic Targeting of HSCs in Vivo
Targeting a particular cell type has always been a challenging task in biomedical research. Growing knowledge on HSC heterogeneity suggests that many of the preexisting targeting approaches require further precision and advancement. The principle of any targeting strategy is based on the usage of a peptide or another molecule that would specifically interact with a protein, which is exclusively (or abundantly) expressed on HSCs. Such targeting molecules can be attached to lipid nanoparticles, expressed on adenoviral or adeno-associated virus (AAV) capsids, or even used independently from a vector agent, being directly conjugated to therapeutic compound. , The 2 examples of the latter are mannose 6 phosphate (M6P) group, which interacts with M6P/insulin-like growth factor II (M6P/IGFII) receptor, and PDGFRβ-recognizing cyclic peptide (PPB), similar to receptor-binding part of the PDGF B-chain. IGFIIR is expressed predominantly on HSCs and is strongly upregulated upon activation ; hence, it may serve as alternate for targeted delivery into HSCs. However, its affinity to M6P is low. Moreover, M6P itself is negatively charged, and therefore can bind to scavenger receptor type A, which is expressed on other non-parenchymal liver cells. In contrast, PPB specifically interacts with PDGFRβ. Bansal et al established HSC-targeted delivery of either full length interferon (IFN)γ or IFNγ peptidomimetic conjugated with PPB or bicyclic PPB, respectively. , Both approaches effectively reduced fibrosis in a mouse model of CCl4-induced liver injury, whereas administration of IFNγ alone did not result in strong improvements, emphasizing the strength of targeted delivery.
Targeting molecules can also be conjugated to the capsids of adenoviruses or AAV. This strategy is used for nerve growth factor synthetic peptide, which binds to p75NGF receptor. , Even though such route of delivery is suitable for animal experiments, utilization of viral vectors in the clinics may carry a number of risks due to immunogenicity and toxicity. Adenoviral vectors are highly immunogenic; doses as low as >1013 viral particles per kg induce a lethal immune response in large animals. Compared with adenoviral vectors, AAVs are significantly less immunogenic. However, dosages as high as 2 × 1014 can also be lethal in humans. Fatal cases of acute liver failure were reported in patients with X-linked myotubular myopathy (MTM1) and spinal muscular atrophy (SMA), who received high-dose AAV during clinical trials. Gene therapy with recombinant AAV increases HCC incidence in mice with diet-induced NAFLD or after partial hepatectomy. The probability of random rAAV integration in the genome is likely higher in actively proliferating hepatocytes, which is the case in the context of liver injury. Though rAAV-related HCC was not documented in human clinical trials, the potential risk has to be considered. 
Utilization of lipid nanoparticles (LNPs) is a good alternative to viral vectors. Targeting molecules being tested in preclinical studies include collagen type VI receptor binding peptide, , vitamin A, , M6P conjugated with human or bovine serum albumin, IGFIIR-specific peptide or aptamer, and anisamide-tethered lipidoid. LNPs conjugated with the latter compound were recently introduced by Han et al. Anisamide ligand binds to sigma receptor on the HSC surface, which is upregulated upon transdifferentiation into MF. Using this targeting property of the compound, Han and colleagues delivered HSP47-specific siRNA to activated hepatic MFs of mice with CCl4-injured livers and showed a significant reduction of collagen deposition. 
However, despite numerous preclinical studies on promising molecules, to date, the only clinically tested HSC-targeting agents are vitamin A-coupled LNPs harboring HSP-47 siRNA. Besides the fact that current animal models cannot fully recapitulate all the aspects of human liver disease, with the expansion of knowledge on HSC heterogeneity, it becomes clear why many of targeting strategies show less prominent effects as expected or even fail to display any. The use of the synthetic peptide approach may be hindered due to non-uniform expression of peptide-binding receptor among different HSC populations. On the other hand, utilization of pan-HSC markers for targeting, such as PDGFRβ, may result in depletion or disturbance of physiological functions of quiescent HSCs, which can have deleterious consequences for liver regeneration and protection against cancer.

## Conclusion
With the help of recent advancements in genetic lineage tracing and scRNA-seq technology, it has become evident that HSCs are not a homogenous cell population. HSCs exhibit heterogeneity in healthy liver, fibrosis, and end-stage liver diseases such as cirrhosis and HCC. However, HSC separation into different clusters highly depends on the scientific question and can vary from one experimental criterion to another, such as gene expression signatures, zonation across the liver lobule, vein-associated differences among HSCs, etc.
HSC heterogeneity in certain liver disease models was investigated more precisely than in others. Likewise, there are numerous scRNA-seq studies in the context of toxin-induced injury, whereas cholestatic and NAFLD/NASH-associated changes are less characterized. At the same time, NAFLD is the most prevalent liver disease, affecting almost one-fourth of the population worldwide. Therapeutic agents designed for patients with NAFLD/NASH reduce lipid accumulation, insulin resistance, and metabolic abnormalities in hepatocytes; however, none of these possess direct antifibrotic action or primarily target HSCs. Characterization of HSC diversity and transcriptional dynamics in cholestatic liver injury is also crucial, as the contribution of HSCs to the MF pool in this context is still debated.
The majority of studies focus on HSC heterogeneity either in healthy tissue or during the course of the disease. Describing HSC subpopulations in disease resolution is of paramount importance, as it may reveal therapeutic targets controlling senescence, deactivation, and reversal to quiescence.
Finally, functional validation of HSC subpopulations and their characteristic markers is imperative. Transcriptional changes cannot faithfully predict protein expression. Immunostaining of liver tissue, in vitro functional tests, and in vivo pharmacological and genetic manipulation of the targets would answer whether newly identified cell populations exist and whether their markers are therapeutically relevant. The same applies to trajectory analysis. It gives valuable insights into HSCs’ plasticity and how they adjust transcriptional programs to acquire distinct phenotypes. A combination of trajectory inference with lineage tracing techniques will help to better comprehend the temporal dynamics of HSC states during the course of disease and in response to therapeutic interventions.
Further research related to HSC proteome and cellular interactions of different HSC populations is warranted to develop molecular tools to target deleterious HSC subpopulations in fibrosis, cirrhosis, and HCC, while preserving quiescent HSCs to restore the essential liver functions.
