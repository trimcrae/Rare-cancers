Throughout the body’s life, old cells are replaced with new ones, even to a small extent in the nervous system, where such processes are thought not to occur. Most differentiated cells can proliferate based on mitotic division, which is the basic physiological process. This ability is also called unipotency, i.e., cells can differentiate along only one lineage [ ]. On the other hand, adult stem cells (ASCs) residing in tissues are responsible for replenishing cells that die within a given organ, either due to physiological (wear and tear) or pathological processes. They are capable of differentiating into tissue-specific cells. This ability is called multipotency, which is a characteristic of stem cells. ASCs maintain their ability to self-renew through symmetrical mitotic divisions. When necessary, such as in diseases, mechanical injuries, or tissue defects, as a result of asymmetric division, one ASC cell is formed from one ASC cell, identical to the maternal cell, and one precursor cell, which, undergoing subsequent mitotic division, generates specialized cells [ ]. For example, hematopoietic stem cells (HSCs) give rise to blood cells [ ], mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) and adipose-derived stem cells (ADSCs) give rise to osteoblasts, chondrocytes and adipocytes [ , ], and neural stem cells (NSCs) give rise to neurons, astrocytes, and oligodendrocytes [ ]. The main characteristics associated with ASCs are the ability to self-renew and differentiate. Thanks to them, this type of cell is used in regenerative medicine or tissue engineering. However, it is also worth mentioning that they can also synthesize and secrete biologically active substances, thanks to which they can modulate the behavior of the other cell types residing in the ASCs microenvironment, and this ability disappears with the process of differentiation [ ]. This secretome can affect the proliferation of other cells, vascularization processes, migration, or immunomodulation.
In this review article, we present how NSC secretome can regulate inflammatory processes in the central nervous system (CNS) in pathophysiological conditions such as neurodegenerative diseases.

## Neural stem cells
The ontogenetic origin of the CNS has its source in the neural tube, in which NSCs are located [ ]. Due to their multipotent nature, during development, NSCs generate neuronal precursors (neurogenesis) which differentiate into neurons, and glia precursors (gliogenesis) that give rise to astrocytes and oligodendrocytes (Fig.  ).

 

In adult life, NSCs reside in strictly defined structures. In mammals, NSCs-related adult brain neurogenesis has been documented in the specific “neurogenic” brain regions like subventricular zone (SVZ) of the lateral ventricles [ ] where new neurons are generated from radial glia-like cells and then migrate through the rostral migratory stream to the olfactory bulb to become interneurons and to the subgranular zone (SGZ) of the dentate gyrus (DG) of the hippocampus where new dentate granule cells are generated [ ]. Another structure of the nervous system in which NSCs are located is the spinal cord. The spinal cord contains one neurogenic niche in the ependyma of the central canal in which NSCs are present in an undifferentiated and self-renewable state [ ]. NSCs of the SVZ and SGZ express glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP), glutamate aspartate transporter (GLAST), nestin [ ], brain lipid-binding protein (BLBP) [ ], vimentin [ ] (also for glia lineage), and sex-determining region Y-box 2 (Sox2). NSCs, however, differ from RG-like cells in their slower proliferation rate [ ]. NSCs are involved in adult neurogenesis and gliogenesis, a process that can be divided into the following stages: 1-proliferation, 2-differentiation, 3-migration, 4-axonal/dendritic targeting, and 5-synaptic integration [ , ]. During the successive stages, the expression of characteristic proteins can be observed: stage 1- the above-mentioned characteristic proteins for NSCs, stage 2- doublecortin (DCX) or polysialylated neural cell adhesion molecule (PSA-NCAM), stage 3- DCX and PSA-NCAM, and immature neurons markers such as Tuj-1b and TUC-4 or NeuroD, stage 4- calretinin and NeuN, and calbindin in the fifth and final stage [ , ]. New neurons can physiologically affect functions of the hippocampus, such as learning, motor performance, and memory [ ]. Pathological conditions in adult life, such as neurodegenerative diseases often associated with chronic inflammation, can disrupt this process [ ]. In this case, NSCs act as natural endogenous regulators of these processes, and their activity can promote the resolution of inflammation.

## The role of NSCs in inflammatory processes
In the case of NSCs, it is particularly noteworthy that they are immunomodulatory cells in the CNS [ ]. Through interactions with other cells and the secretion of bioactive molecules, NSCs influence their niche environment. In the CNS, a disrupted homeostatic state, as seen in injury or disease, triggers a complex immune response known as neuroinflammation. This inflammatory response involves the production of reactive oxygen species, chemokines, cytokines, and secondary messengers by endothelial cells, peripheral immune cells, and resident glial cells (microglia and astrocytes) [ ]. Neuroinflammation has wide-ranging consequences, impacting immunological, physiological, metabolic, and psychological functions. Human fetal (h)NSC transplantation was found to modulate the T cell-mediated immune response in the Theiler’s murine encephalomyelitis virus (TMEV) model of multiple sclerosis (MS) [ ]. This was demonstrated by an increase in CD4 + CD25 + FOXP3 + regulatory T-cells, a decrease in interferon-gamma (IFNγ) and tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNFα), and an increase in interleukin-10 (IL-10) production from T cells isolated from draining cervical lymph nodes [ ]. The supernatants of NPCs co-cultured with T cells showed elevated amounts of PGE2 and nitric oxide (NO). Compared to T cells only or NPC-only control cultures, both NO and PGE2 were induced in T cells co-cultured with NPCs, indicating that interactions with T cells may encourage or improve NPC production of NO and PGE2 Furthermore, only NPCs co-cultured with T cells exhibited inducible NOS (iNOS) and microsomal type 1 PGE synthase (mPGES-1), supporting the idea that activated T cells trigger the production of NO and PGE2 in NPCs. Particular inhibitors for NO and PGE2 production were studied to ascertain whether NO and PGE2 were involved in T cell suppression by NPCs, and it was discovered that they restored T cell proliferation [ ]. Since the NPCs were pre-treated with the competitive inhibitor of HO-1 protoporphyrin IX (SnPP) before being co-cultured with T cells, they partially lost their immune suppressive (e.g., anti-proliferative) actions, indicating that NPC-secreted heme oxygenase-1 (HO-1) has also been linked to T cell suppression [ ]. Another study demonstrates that NPC transplantation induces invading T cells to undergo apoptosis [ ] in addition to inhibiting T-cell growth [ ]. The induction of death receptor ligands such as FasL, Trail, and Apo3L and the stimulation of soluble molecules linked to mitochondrial-mediated apoptosis, such as GDNF, interferon-γ, and leukemia inhibitory factor, are two possible ways to accomplish this [ ]. Despite the widespread belief that VEGF promotes inflammation, a growing body of research has demonstrated that VEGF released by NSCs has anti-inflammatory effects. According to some research, VEGF reduces the activation of T cells and antigen-presenting dendritic cells in addition to preventing their formation [ , ]. In particular, Manoonkitiwongsa et al. [ ] discovered that low-dose VEGF treatment of ischemic brain injury decreased macrophage number while high-dose VEGF increased macrophage density. VEGF might also have a dose-dependent effect on macrophage proliferation. Notably, additional research demonstrated that VEGF played a crucial role in the immunosuppressive effects of NSCs and could change the blood-brain barrier’s integrity [ ]. VEGF loss in adult NSCs and their progenitors caused a spike in radial-glia-like neural stem cell (RGL) proliferation, which was followed by the RGL pool being exhausted [ ]. The in vivo adult mouse hippocampal RGLs and cultured DG NSCs both displayed this phenotype of disturbed quiescence and exhaustion, indicating that it was not dependent on reciprocal signaling with nearby cell types. SVZ NSCs were not observed to exhibit VEGF expression, indicating that this secretome-mediated self-maintenance mechanism is specific to RGLs in the hippocampus [ ]. Another factor secreted from the RGLs, insulin-like growth factor 2 (IGF2), was found to be strongly expressed in GFP + RGLs and their intermediate progenitor cells (IPCs) offspring, which were isolated from Sox2:GFP mice using fluorescence-activated cell sorting. IGF2 expression in the adult mouse hippocampal region was verified to be specific to DG by in situ hybridization and antibody labeling, but not to SVZ, NSCs, or IPCs. In vitro and in vivo, IGF2 knockdown with a lentivirus that expresses a short hairpin RNA against Igf2 had no effect on SVZ NSCs, but it dramatically reduced the proliferation of infected cells in the adult hippocampus and cultured hippocampal rglNSCs [ ]. These findings further demonstrate the variability across the regional NSC secretomes in the adult brain by strongly indicating that IGF2 is a RGLs-specific, self-secreted factor that promotes RGL maintenance [ ]. The immunoregulatory function of NSC transplantation in spinal cord damage has been verified by another study. This was caused by a decrease in M1-type macrophage production following injury and an increase in M2-type macrophage production. M2-type macrophages phagocytized disintegration products and released anti-inflammatory factors, which in turn suppressed the inflammatory response [ ].
Microglia, the primary immune cells in the brain, orchestrate a significant portion of the innate immune response [ ]. Activated microglia release signals that stimulate the recruitment of peripheral immune cells from both the innate and adaptive arms of the immune system. For instance, matrix metalloproteinase-9 (MMP9) disrupts the blood-brain barrier’s tight junctions, thereby facilitating infiltration by peripheral immune cells [ ]. Microglia also act as antigen-presenting cells (APCs), playing a critical role in the autoimmune response by reactivating myelin-specific T cells and directing the immune response toward the CNS [ ]. Persistent microglia activation, in the absence of proper resolution of inflammation, can inflict long-term damage to healthy tissues. Chronic, unresolved inflammation is implicated in the pathogenesis of various disorders, including autoimmune diseases such as multiple sclerosis (MS), neuropsychiatric conditions such as major depressive disorder [ , ], and neurodegenerative diseases such as Alzheimer’s disease (AD) [ , ], Parkinson’s disease (PD) [ ] and amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) [ , ].
Similar to microglia, CD4 T cells (including Th1 and Th17 cells) are recruited to the site of inflammation in the CNS. Both cell types exhibit pro-inflammatory phenotypes and produce cytokines like interferon-γ (IFN-γ), tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), and other pro-inflammatory factors [ ]. An imbalance between pro- and anti-inflammatory phenotypes can lead to chronic neuroinflammation, potentially causing CNS damage and progression of neurodegenerative diseases [ ].
Astrocytes contribute to maintaining brain homeostasis and also regulate immune cell activation and trafficking. They possess immunocompetence, and can recognize danger signals and respond by releasing cytokines and chemokines to trigger the adaptive immune response [ ]. Microglial cells and astrocytes can phagocytize and remove cellular debris, such as neuronal debris, axonal mitochondria, and pathological protein aggregates resulting from conditions such as neurodegenerative diseases or injuries [ , ]. It is worth mentioning that NSCs can regulate these processes. NSCs have also been shown to exhibit phagocytic activity by expressing high levels of P2 × 7 receptors [ ]. Phagocytosis of NSCs is also stimulated by the Vitamin K-dependent Factor Protein S [ ]. This ability to remove dead cells may be of major physiological significance as it implies that NSCs underpin brain tissue homeostasis not only by engendering neuronal and glial cells but also by contributing to apoptotic cell clearance. The two distinct forms of mobility exhibited by neural cells are tangential and radial migration. Tangential migration is a non-radial, glia-guided neuronal translocation, while radial migration is the migration of newly formed neurons in the neocortex along radial glial processes [ ]. Radial migration is mostly observed in specific regions of the cerebellum and olfactory bulb, as well as during embryonic cortical development. Additionally, whereas tangential migration occurs in all developing brains, it only continues in the RMS of adult brains, where migratory neuroblasts move from the SVZ to the olfactory bulb over extended distances via a glial tunnel made of astrocytes. When neuroblasts reach the olfactory bulb, they transition using radial migration to their target. Interestingly, the migration pattern was shown to change from tangential to radial in response to cortical injury [ ]. Neurological disorders and trauma trigger the migration of NSCs to the injury site. Both innate and adaptive immune responses are activated during this process, potentially impacting the NSC niche and ultimately altering NSC behavior (proliferation, migration, or differentiation). Factors released due to inflammation, including IFN-γ, IFN-α, TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-4, IL-6, IL-10, IL-15, leukemia inhibitory factor (LIF), nitric oxide (NO), and hydrogen peroxide (H2O2), can influence NSC function [ ]. Although it has been demonstrated that several distinct NSPC-derived proteins affect important cellular functions, a comprehensive characterization is yet to be performed.
Denninger et al. [ ] assessed secretome derived from mouse hippocampal NSPCs cultured on conditioned media and probed for over 300 growth factors, cytokines, chemokines, adipokines, angiogenic factors, proteases, soluble receptors, and soluble adhesion molecules with a protein antibody array [ ]. Among the top intensity factors were vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), insulin-like growth factor binding protein (IGFBP) family (IGFBP3, IGFBP2, and IGFBP5), soluble fragments of receptors that promote NSC maintenance and proliferation such as tyrosine-protein kinase receptor TYRO3, known regulators of apoptosis that promote NSPC survival, FAS and SPP1, and CD70, shown to interact with CD27 on CD4 + T-cells to induce FAS-mediated apoptosis of T cells [ ]. Next, unbiased identification of NSPC-produced proteins using liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC-MS) was applied to get beyond the limitations of antibody-based protein detection techniques. Among the top 50 proteins secreted to conditioned medium, IGFBP-3, IGFBP-2, progranulin, and collagen type XVIII alpha 1 chain (COL18A1) were detected. The most abundant NSC lysate proteins included epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR), secreted protein acidic and rich in cysteine (SPARC), activated leukocyte cell adhesion molecule (ALCAM), TR10B (TNF-related apoptosis-inducing ligand TRAIL2 and TNF receptor superfamily member 10b TNFRSF10B), milk fat globule-EGF factor 8 (MGfe8), β-Catenin (CTNB1), C-terminal Src kinase (CSK), and F3. Further, LC-MS-based semi-quantitative analysis of conditioned medium from NSC culture identified IGFBP3, IGFBP2, IGFBP5, IGFBP7 CCL2, and tissue inhibitor of metalloproteinases 2 and 4 (TIMP2, TIMP4), fractalkine (X3CL1), VEGFC and MFge8 [ ].
NSCs hold promise for promoting neuroregeneration and neurological function recovery through various mechanisms. Studies have shown that NSC transplantation demonstrates significant therapeutic efficacy in treating traumatic brain injury, spinal cord injury, cerebral ischemia, hemorrhagic stroke, and hypoxic-ischemic encephalopathy [ , ]. Evidence suggests that the immunomodulatory properties of NSCs involve inhibiting immune cell activation and reducing pro-inflammatory mediator secretion through direct cell contact or paracrine signaling [ – ]. Their secretome, i.e., the complex of factors they release, warrants particular attention. Beyond its local effects, the NSC secretome can exert biological influence on distant structures outside the NSC niche. NSCs offer a promising therapeutic avenue for treating neurological disorders by promoting neuroregeneration and modulating the neuroinflammatory response. Further research is necessary to fully elucidate the complex interactions between NSCs and the neuroinflammatory environment (Fig.  ).

 

## Paracrine cross-talk between neural stem cells and immune cells
Based on studies related to NSC transplantation, it was found that the associated introduction of factors biosynthesized by these cells allowed for the protection of damaged cells from further degradation, promoted regeneration, and reduced inflammation [ ]. However, the contents and potential of the NSC secretome are still emerging topics of research. As mentioned earlier, the secretome of NSCs combines many biologically active agents that affect the pleiotropic microenvironment into which they are released. This review presents factors found in the secretome of NSCs that, based on literature data, can significantly modulate the immune response (Table  ).

 

## TGF-β
Transforming growth factor-β, which is secreted by neural precursor cells [ ], is involved in a plethora of biological processes during development, wound healing, lineage commitment, proliferation, migration, and cell survival [ , ]. TGF-β is implicated in regulating functional aspects of several distinct immune cell populations, including CNS resident microglia [ ]. TGF-β is an anti-inflammatory cytokine that is also constitutively expressed in neurons, astrocytes, and NSCs [ – ]. TGF-β has been shown to reduce the expression of pro-inflammatory interleukins such as IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α in a murine microglial line BV2 [ ]. This factor also inhibits the activation of CD4 + T cells, increases the number of Treg [ ], and transforms monocytes into anti-inflammatory phenotypes [ ]. The regulatory and neuroprotective effects of TGF-β are mediated by TGF-β receptors that activate the Smad pathway, which has been considered as one of the main signal transduction pathways activated by these receptors. TGF-β-Smad signaling is involved in the induction of the quiescent phenotype of microglia within the CNS [ ]. It has been shown that in mice lacking the TGF-β receptor (Tgfbr2), microglia were characterized by distinct morphological changes. Control microglia showed typical non-inflammatory ramified morphologies. On the other hand, the microglial knockout population presented a heterogeneous morphology. Transcriptome analysis using RNAseq revealed that loss of TGF-β signaling results in upregulation of microglia activation [ ]. Additionally, approaches to silencing microglial TGF-β signaling in mouse models of CNS diseases have resulted in exacerbated disease progression due to uncontrolled microglia reactivity [ ]. Other studies have shown that TGF-β1 modulates microglial phenotype and promotes recovery via decreased Il6 gene after intracerebral hemorrhage [ ].

## CXCL12/CXCR4
The chemokine CXCL12, acting through its receptor CXCR4, plays a pivotal role in regulating various biological processes in the central nervous system (CNS), including neuroinflammation and neurogenesis. This axis is essential for the recruitment of immune cells, migration of neural progenitor cells, and modulation of microglial activation [ ]. CXCL12-mediated chemoattraction explains how neuron progenitors reach their ultimate locations, and progenitor migration is disrupted when CXCR4 signaling is absent. Defects in the area of cortical GABAergic interneurons [ , ], developmentally significant Cajal-Retzius cells [ , , ], and GnRH-secreting forebrain neurons [ ] were observed in CXCR4 knockout mice. Neuronal cells in the adult DG also express CXCL12. Both immature DG granule cells and DG neural progenitor cells located in the SGZ express CXCR4 receptors [ , ]. The close proximity of CXCL12 and CXCR4 raises the possibility that CXCR4 plays a significant role in controlling adult neurogenesis in this area of the brain. According to recent research, DG neurons, including DG GABAergic interneurons such as granule cells and Basket cells, really store CXCL12 in neurotransmitter vesicles. Therefore, CXCL12 may typically aid in balancing the continuous level of neurogenesis with the degree of neuronal activity in the DG [ ]. Signaling between CXCL12 and CXCR4 plays a crucial role in neurogenesis, that takes place in the development of brain pathology. The brain, for instance, strives to heal itself when it experiences neurodegeneration, such as that which follows a stroke. The SVZ and other locations send endogenous neural progenitors to brain healing sites, e.g., the region around an infarct, as a part of this reaction [ ]. Several studies have highlighted the involvement of the CXCL12/CXCR4 axis in neuroinflammatory processes. CXCL12 increases the adhesion of CD4 + and CD8 + T cells to activated human brain microvascular endothelial cells (HBMEC), facilitating their migration into the brain parenchyma [ ]. Additionally, Gavriel et al. proposed that Aβ plaques in Alzheimer’s disease attracted microglia via CXCL12/CXCR4 signaling, subsequently triggering an inflammatory cascade [ ]. This suggests that the CXCL12/CXCR4 axis contributes to the chronic neuroinflammation associated with neurodegenerative diseases. Conversely, the CXCL12/CXCR4 axis also plays a critical role in neurogenesis. Global knockdown of the CXCL12/CXCR7 chemokine pathway results in learning deficits and impaired neural progenitor maturation in mice, emphasizing the importance of this axis in adult hippocampal neurogenesis [ ]. Moreover, CXCL12 secreted by basal progenitors in the developing cerebral cortex attracts CXCR4-expressing microglia to the ventricular zone, suggesting a coordinated interaction between neural progenitors and microglia during brain development [ ]. Given the dual role of the CXCL12/CXCR4 axis in both neuroinflammation and neurogenesis, targeting this pathway offers promising therapeutic strategies for various neurological disorders. CXCL12 expression was increased in the hippocampus of prenatally stressed offspring, and administration of antidepressant drugs (tianeptine, venlafaxine, and fluoxetine) reduced these changes [ ]. Inhibiting CXCL12/CXCR4 signaling could be beneficial in reducing neuroinflammation in diseases like Alzheimer’s disease, while enhancing this signaling might promote neurogenesis and repair in conditions such as stroke [ ]. The dual role of CXCL12/CXCR4 signaling in the central nervous system influences both neuroinflammation and neurogenesis/repair depending on the context. In diseases such as AD, chronic neuroinflammation driven by microglial activation contributes to neurodegeneration [ ]. CXCL12/CXCR4 signaling can exacerbate neuroinflammation by recruiting inflammatory immune cells to the brain, activating microglia and astrocytes, leading to increased cytokine production and enhancing BBB dysfunction, allowing peripheral immune cells to enter the CNS. In conditions like stroke or traumatic brain injury, CXCL12/CXCR4 signaling plays a beneficial role in tissue repair by promoting neural progenitor cell migration to damaged areas, enhancing the restoration of oxygen and nutrients, and supporting neuronal survival and synaptic plasticity [ ]. However, further research is needed to fully understand the complex mechanisms underlying the role of CXCL12/CXCR4 in the CNS and to develop targeted therapies that can modulate this pathway without causing adverse effects. In conclusion, the CXCL12/CXCR4 axis is a multifaceted signaling pathway with profound implications for neuroinflammation and neurogenesis. By understanding the mechanisms underlying the actions of this axis, novel therapeutic approaches can be developed to treat neurological disorders and promote neural repair [ ].

## MFge8
MFge8 is a multifaceted protein that significantly impacts immune function. Its ability to recognize and bind to apoptotic cells, as well as its influence on immune cell activation and polarization, makes it a key player in maintaining tissue homeostasis and resolving inflammation [ ]. One of MFge8’s primary roles is to facilitate the clearance of apoptotic cells. MFge8 binds to phosphatidylserine, a phospholipid exposed on the surface of apoptotic cells, and acts as an “eat-me” signal for phagocytes. This efficient removal of cellular debris is crucial for preventing the release of harmful cellular contents and the subsequent development of inflammatory responses [ ]. Beyond its role in cell clearance, MFge8 has been implicated in the regulation of inflammatory responses. MFge8 inhibits inflammasome-induced IL-1β production, a key pro-inflammatory cytokine [ ], suggesting that MFge8 can limit excessive inflammation and promote tissue repair. Exhaustion of adult NSC pool development, which is implicated in neurodegenerative diseases like AD, where neural stem cell function declines over time, is prevented by autocrine Mfge8 signaling. Mfge8 is significantly overexpressed in quiescent RGLs in the dentate gyrus. Adult RGL-specific deletion of Mfge8 results in RGL overactivation and depletion, and Mfge8 null animals show reduced adult dentate neurogenesis. Likewise, in the early postnatal dentate gyrus, loss of Mfge8 stimulates RGL activation, which leads to fewer label-retaining RGLs in maturity [ ]. MFge8 has been shown to play a significant role in neuroinflammation and neurodegenerative diseases. For instance, Brousse et al. reported that MFge8-expressing neural progenitor cells could modulate microglial activity and protect against demyelination [ ]. Additionally, recombinant human MFGE8 can alleviate neurological impairments and reduce apoptosis and inflammation following traumatic brain injury [ , ]. These findings suggest that MFge8 may have therapeutic potential for neurodegenerative disorders. While significant progress has been made in understanding the role of MFge8 in the immune response, further research is needed to fully elucidate its mechanisms of action. Exploring the potential of MFge8 as a therapeutic target for various diseases, including autoimmune disorders, cancer, and neurodegenerative diseases, is an exciting area of investigation.

## CD47-SIRPα
The CD47-signal regulatory protein alpha (SIRPα) axis plays a crucial role in regulating immune cell function, particularly in the context of inflammation and neurodegeneration. CD47, a transmembrane protein, interacts with SIRPα, a receptor expressed on various immune cells, including macrophages and microglia [ ]. This interaction protects cells from phagocytosis. A study investigating individuals with 16p11.2 deletion syndrome, a genetic condition associated with neuropsychiatric disorders and brain overgrowth, utilized human induced pluripotent stem cells (hiPSCs). These hiPSCs were differentiated into neural progenitor cells (NPCs) and oligodendrocyte progenitor cells (OPCs), both of which are precursors to NSCs [ ]. The study found that CD47 was overexpressed in NPCs and OPCs from 16p11.2 deletion carriers, leading to reduced phagocytosis by macrophages and microglia. Treatment of 16p11.2 deletion NPCs and OPCs with an anti-CD47 antibody restored phagocytosis to the control levels, indicating that blocking CD47 can promote the clearance of unhealthy neural cells [ ]. In the context of neuroinflammation, the CD47-SIRPα axis: (i) inhibits microglial phagocytosis – neuronal SIRPα limits the interaction between microglia and CD47, thereby regulating microglial phagocytosis and synaptic pruning [ ]; (ii) modulates neurodegenerative diseases – dysregulation of the CD47-SIRPα axis has been implicated in neurodegenerative diseases such as AD because excessive microglial activation and phagocytosis of healthy neurons can contribute to neuronal loss and disease progression [ ]; (iii) influences neurogenesis – while the specific role of CD47-SIRPα in neurogenesis is less well-understood, likely this axis may indirectly influence neurogenesis by modulating the inflammatory environment and promoting neural stem cell proliferation and differentiation [ ]. In addition to its role in neuroinflammation, the CD47-SIRPα axis also impacts other immune processes. Immune cell activation and differentiation: CD47-SIRPα signaling can modulate the activation and differentiation of various immune cells, including T cells and B cells. Disruption of CD47-SIRPα signaling can lead to increased inflammation and tissue damage, as evidenced by studies on models of ischemic stroke and spinal cord injury [ – ]. Targeting the CD47-SIRPα axis represents a promising therapeutic strategy for various neurological disorders. By modulating this pathway, it may be possible to reduce neuroinflammation, promote neurogenesis, and improve functional outcomes. However, it is important to carefully consider the potential side effects of targeting this axis, as it could also impact other immune functions. Further research is needed to fully elucidate the complex role of CD47-SIRPα signaling in the CNS and to develop targeted therapies that can modulate this pathway without causing adverse effects.

## CD200-CD200R
The CD200-CD200R axis plays a crucial role in maintaining immune tolerance and regulating inflammatory responses. CD200, a transmembrane glycoprotein, interacts with its receptor, CD200R, on various immune cells, including microglia and macrophages [ ], thereby inhibiting the activation and inflammatory response of these cells. In the context of neuroinflammation, CD200-CD200R signaling has been shown to play a protective role. CD200-CD200R interaction modulates microglial activation both in vivo and in vitro. By inhibiting microglial activation, CD200 can help prevent neuroinflammation and neurodegeneration [ ]. Additionally, impaired CD200R signaling can lead to increased neuroinflammation and worsened outcomes after stroke [ ]. CD200 is necessary to prevent the host tissue’s immune system from rejecting transplanted allogeneic NPC, but it is dispensable for reducing acute autoimmune neuroinflammation. T cell activation and proliferation were effectively inhibited by CD200-deficient NPC, but their capacity to suppress macrophages was diminished [ ]. This suggests that the CD200-CD200R axis is essential for maintaining brain health and promoting recovery from injury. CD200-CD200R signaling has also been implicated in neurogenesis and neurodegenerative diseases. Conditional upregulation of CD200 expression in a mouse model of AD improved hippocampal neurogenesis and enhanced amyloid-beta phagocytosis by microglia [ ]. Moreover, Chamera et al. have shown that disruptions in CD200-CD200R signaling can contribute to neurodevelopmental disorders, such as schizophrenia [ , ]. These studies highlight the importance of CD200-CD200R signaling in maintaining brain health and preventing neurodegenerative diseases. The therapeutic potential of targeting the CD200-CD200R axis is significant. By modulating this pathway, it may be possible to reduce neuroinflammation, promote neurogenesis, and improve cognitive function. However, further research is needed to fully understand the complex mechanisms underlying the role of CD200-CD200R signaling in the brain. In conclusion, the CD200-CD200R axis plays a crucial role in maintaining immune tolerance and regulating neuroinflammation. By understanding the molecular mechanisms underlying this pathway, we may be able to develop novel therapeutic strategies for neurodegenerative diseases and other inflammatory disorders.

## FABP5
Fatty acid-binding protein 5 (FABP5) is a small, intracellular lipid-binding protein that plays a crucial role in various cellular processes, including lipid metabolism, cell signaling, and inflammation. Recent studies have highlighted the role of FABP5 in regulating inflammatory responses [ ]. NanoLiquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry peptide analysis, followed by proteome studies, revealed that NSCs differentiated in vitro from human embryonic stem cells secrete FABP5, which is involved in the PPAR pathway. Recombinant human FABP5 inhibited BV2 murine microglial inflammation by activating the PPAR-γ pathway [ ], suggesting that FABP5 may have therapeutic potential for neuroinflammatory diseases. Furthermore, FABP5 deficiency in macrophages leads to increased inflammation, as evidenced by elevated levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines. This effect is mediated by the adenosine-monophosphate-activated protein kinase/nuclear factor kappa-light-chain-enhancer of activated B cells (AMPK/NF-κB) signaling pathway [ ]. FABP5 also plays a role in T cell differentiation. Rao et al. showed that FABP5 inhibition promotes the differentiation of T cells into regulatory T cells (Tregs), which have immunosuppressive properties [ ]. This finding suggests that targeting FABP5 could be a potential strategy for treating autoimmune diseases. FABP5 is involved in the intracellular transport of fatty acids. S-glutathionylation of FABP5 can protect tissues against inflammation by modulating lipid metabolism and reducing oxidative stress [ ]. Additionally, FABP5 deficiency can lead to impaired mitochondrial function and increased inflammation [ ]. Given its role in regulating inflammation and immune responses, FABP5 has emerged as a potential therapeutic target in various inflammatory diseases. Modulating FABP5 expression or activity could offer new therapeutic strategies for conditions such as autoimmune diseases, neurodegenerative disorders, and inflammatory bowel disease. However, further research is needed to fully understand the complex mechanisms underlying FABP5’s role in inflammation and to develop safe and effective therapeutic interventions.

## PGs
Prostaglandins (PGs), which are expressed by NSCs [ , ], are lipid-derived molecules that have important functions in the body. The synthesis of PGs is a multistep process that begins with the formation of arachidonic acid from diacylglycerol and phospholipase A2. Arachidonic acid can be transformed via the cyclooxygenase (COX) pathway or the lipoxygenase pathway. PGs are considered the agents that are most commonly secreted in inflammatory states [ , ]. Prostaglandins are important mediators of inflammation and promote vasodilatation, smooth muscle contraction, and pain. Nonetheless, studies suggest that in some contexts, PGs can be anti-inflammatory [ ]. While some prostaglandins are known to promote inflammation, others, like prostaglandin D2 (PGD2) and its metabolite 15-deoxy-Δ12,14-prostaglandin J2 (15d-PGJ2), can have anti-inflammatory effects via COX inhibition during the late phase of the inflammatory response which might prevent the resolution of inflammation by inhibiting PGD synthase activity and the production of PGD2/PGJ2. Also, PGE2 can inhibit the inflammatory response by inhibiting NF-κB activation [ ]. Besides, activated Th1 cells can secrete IFN-γ to induce the secretion of PGE2 by NSCs and promote the apoptosis of Th17 cells and the generation of Tregs [ ]. Other PGs, in particular PGD2 and 15d-PGJ2, exert predominantly inflammation-inhibitory effects via targets such as PPAR-γ and NF-κB. These effects may be segregated in time and contribute to the resolution of inflammation. In addition, PGE2 secreted by NSCs has been shown to reduce the level of IL1β expression in mononuclear phagocytes in mice [ ].

## LIF
Leukemia inhibitory factor (LIF) is a cytokine with a complex role in the body. While it was initially discovered to inhibit leukemia cell growth, it is now known to have a wide range of functions, including neuroprotective and anti-inflammatory properties [ ]. Its levels are increased in a variety of animal and human inflammatory conditions. In the nervous system, LIF mRNA levels dramatically increase soon after injury [ ]. The lack of LIF can also lead to premature neuronal death [ ]. LIF secreted by NPCs in the adult brain [ ] can reduce inflammation by decreasing the production of molecules that promote inflammation, such as TNF-alpha and IL-1β, and can also stimulate the production of molecules that help resolve inflammation, such as IL-10. LIF can upregulate the activation of ERK and its downstream SOCS3 in Th17 cells, then inhibits the phosphorylation of STAT3, and finally resists the differentiation of Th17 cells via the IL-6 signaling pathway [ ]. It is important to note that LIF’s role can vary depending on the specific conditions; in some cases, it might even promote inflammation. However, in many situations, it appears to have a protective role against excessive inflammation, such as cross-talk between the neuro-immune systems, engaging regulatory T cells that release LIF and suppress IL-6 signaling, or the ability to combat oxidative stress [ , ].

## Conclusion
NSCs possess a valuable ability to combat inflammation within the CNS. NSCs exert their anti-inflammatory effects through multiple mechanisms, promoting the release of anti-inflammatory factors, reducing the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines, and inhibiting the activation of pro-inflammatory immune cells. Their ability of neuroprotection ability is also worth mentioning. NSCs can release factors that protect neurons from the inflammatory damaging effects, such as excessive activity of microglia or other immune cells, or the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines [ ]. This is crucial in conditions where inflammation can lead to neuronal death and dysfunction. Due to their ability to differentiate into different types of cells of the nervous system, they promote tissue repair. Also, NSCs can help clear cellular debris and, by their phagocytic activity [ ], other inflammatory byproducts from the site of injury or inflammation. This helps to resolve the inflammatory process and promotes healing. NSCs-based therapies have great potential for the treatment of CNS inflammation due to the immunomodulatory and anti-inflammatory effects of the NSC secretome. The mechanism of NSC-mediated immunomodulation has not been studied in depth. By reducing inflammation and promoting repair, NSCs hold promise as a potential therapy for neurological conditions.
