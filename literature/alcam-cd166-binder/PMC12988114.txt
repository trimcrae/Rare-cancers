## Background
Thyroid cancer stem cells (CSCs) have been implicated in the recurrence and metastasis of thyroid cancer. This study, therefore, explored the properties of thyroid CSC-like cells, cell–cell interactions, and genes associated with prognosis by analyzing single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) data and clinical data.

## Methods
We analyzed two independent scRNA-seq datasets (GSE191288 and GSE250521) using the Seurat R package. Tumor cells were identified based on copy number variations, and stemnessHigh (CSC-like) and stemnessLow cells were defined using CytoTRACE. Protein–protein interaction (PPI) networks were constructed, after which hub genes were identified using STRING and cytoHubba. Gene ontology analysis was performed using the clusterProfiler R package. CellChat was used for cell–cell interaction analysis. Furthermore, survival analysis was performed using the TCGA database.

## Results
Compared to stemnessLow cells, stemnessHigh cells showed upregulation of 1,498 (GSE191288) or 1,274 genes (GSE250521), with 109 genes being commonly upregulated in both datasets. Furthermore, hub genes identified from the PPI network constructed from the co-upregulated genes in both two datasets were implicated in angiogenesis. Subsequent cell–cell interaction analysis revealed strong interactions between JAG1 in stemnessHigh cells and NOTCH1/4 in endothelial cells. Analysis of clinical data showed that thyroid cancer patients with high stemness exhibiting high JAG1 expression and that patients with high JAG1 expression had significantly poorer prognosis than did those with low JAG1 expression.

## Conclusions
This study provides new insights into the gene expression profile of thyroid CSC-like cells and their interactions with cells constituting tumor tissues.

## Introduction
Thyroid cancer is the seventh most common type of cancer worldwide, with an incidence rate three times higher in women than in men [ ]. Surgery is the first-line treatment for most thyroid cancers. Radioactive iodine (RAI) therapy is added when indicated based on the stage and histological type. Systemic pharmacotherapy is generally used for advanced, unresectable, or RAI-refractory disease. Although mortality rates from thyroid cancer have been lower than those in more aggressive types of cancers such as lung, colorectal, and liver cancers, its metastasis and recurrence rates remain a significant clinical challenge [ – ]. Studies have reported that obesity, smoking, alcohol consumption, and lack of exercise are risk factors for thyroid cancer [ , ]. Thyroid cancer can be classified into four types: papillary, medullary, follicular, and anaplastic. Among these subclassifications, papillary thyroid cancer (PTC) is the most common, accounting for approximately 90% of thyroid cancers [ ]. Patients with PTC frequently present with the BRAFV600E mutation (40%–50% of PTC patients), which has been reported to be associated with aggressiveness [ , ]. Although most PTC patients remain asymptomatic, symptoms such as hoarseness or lumps may appear as the disease progresses.
Therapeutic agents, such as the kinase inhibitors sorafenib and lenvatinib, the BRAF inhibitor encorafenib and dabrafenib, and the mitogen-activated protein kinase kinase (MEK) inhibitor binimetinib and trametinib, have been used for the treatment of PTC. Although many patients respond to treatment, some develop resistance and experience recurrence and/or metastasis [ , ]. These phenomena have been linked to the presence of cancer stem cells (CSCs) [ ]. CSCs are capable of self-renewal and differentiation. Given that they remain in a quiescent state, they exhibit resistance to conventional anticancer drugs and radiation therapy [ ]. Therefore, even if existing treatments are able to shrink the cancer, surviving CSCs can still reproduce cancer cells, leading to recurrence and/or metastasis to other tissues. Despite the need to eliminate CSCs for a complete cure, no drug targeting CSCs has been approved for clinically use to date.
In recent years, a considerable amount of research utilizing next-generation sequencing has employed RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) for not only gene expression analysis but also for identifying genetic mutations. Although bulk RNA-seq using whole tumor tissues has been widely performed, the heterogeneity of tumor tissues has hindered the accurate measurement of tumor cell-specific gene expression patterns. Recently, single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq), which can measure gene expression levels at the single-cell level, has been gaining popularity. This approach can elucidate the tumor ecosystem comprising not only cancer cells but also immune cells, vascular endothelial cells, and other components. However, only a few studies have evaluated the properties of CSCs in PTC tissues using scRNA-seq. Therefore, the current study aimed to elucidate the properties of thyroid CSCs and their interactions with cells constituting tumor tissues by analyzing scRNA-seq data from two independent PTC tissue samples deposited in a public database, restricting the analysis to BRAFV600E-positive tumors to standardize the genetic background and minimize driver-specific intertumoral heterogeneity. Our findings showed a strong interaction between thyroid CSCs and endothelial cells, with the JAG1–NOTCH1/4 signaling pathway being particularly important.

## Methods

## Preprocessing of scRNA-seq data
This study utilized two independent PTC scRNA-seq datasets deposited in the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO). Data obtained from the GEO were analyzed using the Seurat R package (version 5.3.0) [ ]. For quality control, cells meeting the following criteria were excluded: in the GSE191288 dataset, cells with < 200 or > 8,000 expressed genes, a total unique molecular identifier (UMI) count of ≥ 50,000, or mitochondrial gene counts exceeding 20%; in the GSE250521 dataset, cells with less than 200 expressed genes, > 100,000 total UMIs, or mitochondrial gene counts > 25%. Next, data normalization, detection of highly variable features, and scaling were performed using NormalizeData, FindVariableFeatures, and ScaleData functions, respectively. Principal component analysis was then implemented, after which clustering was performed using data up to 30 dimensions. Doublets were removed using the DoubletFinder R package (version 2.0.6) [ ]. Parameters were kept at their default values, aside from the dimensionality (which was set from 1 to 30) and the estimated doublet formation rate (which was adjusted to 5%). Subsequently, data integration was performed using the IntegrateLayers function. The uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) was used for cluster visualization. For clustering, the “resolution” parameter for the FindClusters function was set to 0.5.

## Cell annotation
Cell annotations were performed using the SingleR R package (version 2.8.0) and the Human Primary Cell Atlas reference from celldex (version 1.16.0) [ ]. SingleR results were validated by examining the expression of representative marker genes.
Tumor cells were identified using the inferCNV R package (version 1.22.0). Thyrocytes were designated as the targets for observation, whereas B and T cells were designated as references. Thereafter, inferCNV was ran at the default settings, after which the CNV score (mean squared copy number variations [CNVs]) was calculated. Next, thyrocytes were extracted and subclustered, and the standard deviation (SD) of the CNV score for each cluster was calculated. Identification of tumor cells was based on a high CNV SD threshold, defined as an SD exceeding that observed in thyrocytes derived from normal tissue [ ]. To ensure accuracy of the tumor cell analysis, normal tissue–derived cells that were misclassified into tumor clusters were excluded, thereby preventing potential bias in the results.
The CytoTRACE R package (version 0.3.3) was used to define stemnessHigh cells within tumor cells [ ]. Accordingly, tumor cells were extracted from thyrocytes and subclustered, with clusters exhibiting higher scores (more undifferentiated) defined as stemnessHigh cells. Additionally, gene set variation analysis (GSVA) was performed to validate the CytoTRACE results using the GSVA R package (version 2.0.7) [ ]. After stem/progenitor cell-related gene sets were obtained from the molecular signatures database (MSigDB) using the msigdbr R package (version 24.1.0), GSVA was performed.

## Constructing protein–protein interaction (PPI) networks and selecting hub genes
Using Seurat’s FindMarkers function, we identified significantly upregulated genes in stemnessHigh cells and extracted the genes common to both datasets. Next, PPI networks were constructed using the STRING plugin in Cytoscape (version 3.10.3). During this process, the “Maximum additional interactors” parameter was set to 10, allowing inclusion of potential interactors that were not present in the initial query list. To identify key nodes within the resulting network, the top 20 nodes were screened using four algorithms (MCC, MNC, Degree, and EPC) implemented in the cytoHubba plugin. Genes that were consistently identified across all four algorithms were selected as hub gene candidates. To ensure consistency with our scRNA-seq results, these candidates were further filtered to retain only genes that were significantly upregulated in both scRNA-seq datasets, thereby defining the final set of hub genes. To evaluate the cellular functions of the identified genes, gene ontology (GO) analysis was performed using the enrichGO function from the clusterProfiler R package (version 4.14.6) [ ].

## Cell–cell interaction analysis
Cell–cell interactions were explored using CellChat R package (version 2.2.0) according to the manufacturer’s instructions [ ]. The netVisual_bubble function was used to examine interactions between normal thyrocytes, stemnessLow cells, stemnessHigh cells, and endothelial cells. Additionally, the netVisual_aggregate function was used to create chord diagrams to visualize interactions between JAG1 and NOTCH1/4. To classify endothelial cells into two groups based on the activation of NOTCH signaling, GSVA was performed using the “GOMF_NOTCH_BINDING” gene set, with cells scoring within the top and bottom 50% being defined as NOTCHHigh and NOTCHLow cells, respectively.

## Survival and differential expression analyses using TCGA data
Clinical and gene expression data for thyroid cancer patients (TCGA-THCA) were obtained using the TCGAbiolinks R package (version 2.34.1) [ ]. To classify patients into JAG1High and JAG1Low groups, z-scores of the gene expression data for each patient were first calculated and then used to divide the patients into two groups. The threshold for dividing the JAG1High and JAG1Low groups was determined using the surv_cutpoint function as implemented in the survminer R package. Subsequently, the log-rank test was used to determine whether differences in survival rates existed between the two groups.
In addition, to investigate differences in gene expression profiles between patients with high and low cancer stemness, GSVA scores were first calculated using the GSVA package with four stemness-related gene sets (i.e., GAL_LEUKEMIC_STEM_CELL_UP, OISHI_CHOLANGIOMA_STEM_CELL_LIKE_UP, RAMALHO_STEMNESS_UP, and ZHANG_BREAST_CANCER_PROGENITORS_UP). The resulting GSVA scores were then subjected to hierarchical clustering using the “ward.D2” method implemented with the hclust function, thereby classifying patients into two distinct groups. Thereafter, gene expression between these two groups was analyzed using the edgeR R package (version 4.4.2) [ ].

## Statistical analysis
Statistical analysis was performed using R (version 4.4.3). All statistical methods used are described in the corresponding figure legends. For all analyses, a P < 0.05 value indicated statistical significance.

## Results

## Single-cell atlas of normal and tumor thyroid tissues
We cross-validated our findings using two independent scRNA-seq datasets of PTC tissues to ensure biological reproducibility and robustness [ , ]. Cell annotation was performed based on SingleR results and the expression of representative marker genes (B cells: CD79A, CD79B, and MS4A1; T cells: CD3D, IL7R, and TRAC; NK cells: KLRD1, FCGR3A, and IL2RB; myeloid cells: LYZ, CD14, and CSF1R; mast cells: KIT, ENPP3, and MITF; endothelial cells: CDH5, CLDN5, and VWF; fibroblasts: COL1A2, TAGLN, and ACTA2; thyrocytes: TG, MGST1, and KRT18). To ensure a consistent genetic background, the tumor tissues used in this study were limited to those harboring the BRAFV600E mutation.

## Annotation of the GSE191288 dataset
First, we analyzed scRNA-seq data from five tumor tissues and one normal tissue extracted from three PTC patients following the procedure of Wang et al. [ ] and annotated them as B cells (n = 1500), T cells (n = 5424), NK cells (n = 794), myeloid cells (n = 1213), mast cells (n = 89), endothelial cells (n = 2471), fibroblasts (n = 2892) and thyrocytes (n = 10,233) (Fig. a and b). Relative to normal tissue, tumor tissues demonstrated higher levels of immune cells, particularly T cells (Fig. a). T cells were distinguished into CD4+ and CD8+ T cells based on CD4 and CD8A expression (Fig. S2a). Unlike in the original paper, the analysis here failed to identify distinct pericyte clusters [ ]. This is likely due to the exclusion of samples not harboring the BRAFV600E mutation or to differences in scRNA-seq data preprocessing.

## Annotation of the GSE250521 dataset
Next, we analyzed scRNA-seq data from two tumor tissues and three normal tissues extracted from two PTC patients following the procedure of Liao et al. [ ] and successfully annotated the following cell types: B cells (n = 859), T cells (n = 10,875), NK cells (n = 1142), myeloid cells (n = 3099), endothelial cells (n = 982), fibroblasts (n = 1155) and thyrocytes (n = 7994). Interestingly, unlike the GSE191288 dataset, the GSE250521 dataset showed a decrease in the proportion of T cells. Conversely, the proportion of myeloid cells was significantly higher in tumor tissues than in normal tissues (Fig. c and d, S1b, S2b).

 

## Identifying malignant cells
Next, to distinguish normal from malignant thyrocytes, the thyrocytes were subclustered.

## GSE191288 dataset
In the GSE191288 dataset, thyrocytes were divided into 12 clusters (Fig. a). Among them, clusters 3, 5, 6, and 9 contained cells derived from normal tissues and were therefore annotated as normal thyrocytes (n = 2,601) (Fig. b). Song et al. defined cell populations with high CNV SDs as cancer cells [ ]. CNV scores were estimated using the inferCNV package. Since all clusters except 3, 5, 6, and 9 showed an SD higher than that observed in normal cells, these cells were defined as tumor cells (n = 7,632; Fig. c and d). Furthermore, genes such as SLC26A4, TPO, IYD, and TG, known to be highly expressed in normal thyrocytes, were significantly elevated in normal cells. Conversely, GDF15, TNFRSF12A, and TMSB4X, which are upregulated in thyroid cancer cells, were significantly increased in tumor cells [ – ] (Fig. e).

## GSE250521 dataset
In the GSE250521 dataset, thyrocytes were divided into 11 clusters (Fig.  f). Given that almost all of these clusters, except clusters 4 and 5, consisted of cells derived from normal tissues, they were defined as normal thyrocytes (n = 6,910; Fig.  g). Clusters 4 and 5 had higher CNV SDs than normal thyrocytes (Fig.  h), so they were defined as tumor cells (n = 1,084; Fig.  i). As in the GSE191288 dataset, the GSE250521 dataset also showed significantly increased expression of SLC26A4, TPO, IYD, and TG in normal cells, and significantly higher expression of GDF15, TNFRSF12A, and TMSB4X in tumor cells (Fig.  j).

 

## Identifying thyroid cancer stem-like (stemnessHigh) cells
Next, to distinguish thyroid cancer cells from thyroid cancer stem-like cells, tumor cells were subclustered and evaluated for their differentiation state using CytoTRACE.

## GSE191288 dataset
Subclustering of tumor cells resulted in the division into 10 clusters (Fig.  a). Based on the CytoTRACE results, clusters 0, 2, and 6 were defined as cancer stem-like (stemnessHigh) cells, whereas the remaining cells were defined as cancer (stemnessLow) cells (n = 3,143 and 4,489, respectively; Fig.  b and c). To evaluate the CytoTRACE results, GSVA was performed using four gene sets related to cancer stem/progenitor cells. StemnessHigh cells showed significantly higher scores in all gene sets than did stemnessHigh cells, indicating that stemnessHigh cells possess high cancer stemness (Fig.  d).

## GSE250521 dataset
Subclustering of tumor cells resulted in six distinct clusters (Fig.  e). Based on the CytoTRACE results, clusters 2, 3, and 5 were defined as stemnessHigh cells (n = 453), whereas the remaining cells were classified as stemnessLow cells (n = 631; Fig.  f and g). GSVA results showed that stemnessHigh cells consistently exhibited significantly higher scores than did stemnessLow cells, indicating that stemnessHigh cells possess high cancer stem cell properties in this dataset as well (Fig.  h).

 

## Upregulated genes in stemnessHigh cells are related to angiogenesis
Next, differential gene expression analysis revealed that compared to stemnessLow cells, stemnessHigh cells showed increased expression of 1498 and 1274 genes in the GSE191288 and GSE250521 datasets, respectively (Fig.  a). Among these genes, 109 were commonly upregulated. GO analysis revealed that these commonly upregulated genes are involved in angiogenesis, ERK1/2 signaling, etc. (Fig.  b). Next, we constructed a PPI network using the STRING database from the commonly upregulated genes (Fig.  c). We focused on the most robust network nodes by intersecting the top 20 rankings obtained from four independent algorithms. From this intersection, eight genes (i.e., ATF4, CXCL8, EP300, ETS1, KLF2, KLF6, NFIL3, and TNFAIP3) were consistently identified and were further confirmed to be upregulated in both scRNA-seq datasets. These genes were therefore defined as hub genes (Fig.  d). GO analysis revealed that these hub genes are primarily involved in angiogenesis (Fig.  e).

 

## StemnessHigh cells interact with endothelial cells via JAG/NOTCH signaling
As shown in Fig. , genes whose expression was elevated in stemnessHigh cells across both datasets, as well as hub genes, were associated with angiogenesis. Therefore, we investigated the interaction between stemnessHigh and endothelial cells within tumors using the CellChat R package. Our analysis revealed that stemnessHigh cells interact with various cells within the tumor, including endothelial cells (Fig. a). Among these interactions, we investigated those between normal thyrocytes, stemnessLow cells, stemnessHigh cells, and endothelial cells, and we found that stemnessHigh cells specifically interacted with endothelial cells via JAG1–NOTCH1/4 in both datasets (Fig. S3a and b). As shown in the chord diagram, JAG1 from normal thyrocytes and stemnessLow cells did not interact with NOTCH1 or NOTCH4 in endothelial cells. In contrast, JAG1 from stemnessHigh cells interacted with NOTCH1 and NOTCH4 in endothelial cells (Fig. b and c). Upon examining JAG1 expression, we found that it was almost absent in stemnessLow cells but was significantly elevated in stemnessHigh cells (Fig. d). Conversely, NOTCH1 and NOTCH4 were specifically expressed in endothelial cells (Fig. e). To investigate the contribution of NOTCH signaling in endothelial cells, gene expression analysis was performed by dividing endothelial cells into those with high (NOTCHHigh) and low (NOTCHLow) NOTCH signaling activity using GSVA. Notably, we found that NOTCHHigh endothelial cells had a significantly higher expression of KDR genes (coding VEGFR2), which are crucial for angiogenesis and important for cancer growth and metastasis, than did NOTCHLow cells (Fig. f) [ ].

 

## JAG1 serves as a prognostic marker for thyroid cancer patients
Finally, survival analysis was performed to investigate the importance of JAG1 in cancer prognosis. Based on the patient data included in the TCGA-THCA dataset, we divided patients into the JAG1High and JAG1Low groups and conducted a survival analysis. Importantly, JAG1High patients showed significantly poorer prognosis than did JAG1Low patients (Fig.  a). Next, to investigate whether the results obtained from scRNA-seq analyses were reproducible in the RNA-seq data from the TCGA cohort, we performed GSVA and classified patients into stemnessHigh and stemnessLow groups using hierarchical clustering (Fig.  b). Gene expression analysis revealed that stemnessHigh patients not only exhibited significantly higher expression of representative CSC markers ALCAM and ALDH1A1 but also showed significantly higher JAG1 expression than did stemnessLow patients (Fig.  c). These results indicate that JAG1 may serve not only as a marker for thyroid CSCs but also as a useful marker for predicting prognosis.

 

## Discussion
Although numerous studies have indicated that CSCs contribute to the initiation and progression of thyroid cancer, the relationship between thyroid CSCs and the surrounding tumor microenvironment remains poorly understood. A key strength of our study is the use of two independent scRNA-seq datasets of thyroid cancer to minimize dataset-specific biases. As shown in Fig. , the proportions of cell populations constituting tumor tissues differ markedly between these two datasets. By integrating datasets with such distinct cellular compositions, our study provides a more comprehensive and balanced representation of the PTC microenvironment than could be achieved using either dataset alone. Among the various proposed methodologies for defining CSCs, the current study employed CytoTRACE, a sophisticated approach frequently used not only for CSCs but also for identifying undifferentiated cells. Indeed, our GSVA results showed that cells with high CytoTRACE scores were confirmed to have high stemness.
Several studies on thyroid CSCs have been conducted to date. For example, Zhu et al. reported that THRB expression is reduced in anaplastic thyroid cancer and that exogenously inducing THRB expression suppresses stem cell properties [ ]. Furthermore, another study reported that CDKN2B-AS1, a type of lncRNA contained in exosomes derived from CSCs, suppresses the proliferation and metastasis of thyroid cancer [ ]. Meanwhile, our findings demonstrated that JAG1 could be used as a marker for thyroid CSCs and may contribute to tumor malignancy by interacting with endothelial cells. Interestingly, Kim et al. reported that JICD1, an intracellular domain of JAG1 in astrocytes, induces CSC properties and contributes to tumor formation, invasion, and drug resistance [ ]. Another study reported that silencing JAG1 suppresses proliferation and colony-forming ability in the thyroid cancer cell line MDA-T68 [ ]. Although the function of JAG1 in thyroid CSCs remains unclear, the aforementioned studies suggest that JAG1 is highly likely to contribute to the regulation of stem cell properties in thyroid cancer.
Although various driver mutations have been observed in thyroid cancer, the current study limited its analysis to patients harboring the BRAFV600E mutation, the most frequently identified driver mutation in thyroid cancer. This approach was followed to standardize the genetic background. Nonetheless, evidence has also shown that the BRAFV600E mutation itself contributes to the functional control of stem cells. For example, in colon organoids harboring the BRAFV600E mutation, a dedifferentiation transcriptional program similar to that observed in BRAFV600E-driven colorectal cancer is induced [ ], while hematopoietic stem/progenitor cells harboring the BRAFV600E mutation exhibit abnormalities in proliferation and differentiation [ ]. As such, a detailed analysis is needed to clarify the relationship between the BRAFV600E mutation and CSC properties in PTC.
Recent studies have reported that CSCs constitute a highly heterogeneous population, with molecular signatures that often vary across individuals and biological contexts [ , ]. The current study found that 109 genes showed increased expression in stemnessHigh cells across both datasets analyzed. These shared genes constitute a relatively small fraction of the total differentially expressed genes. However, this limited overlap likely reflects the inherent biological heterogeneity of CSCs across different cohorts. Importantly, the conservation of these 109 genes suggests that they represent a robust and fundamental molecular core underlying the CSC phenotype in PTC. Moreover, GO analysis revealed these genes are associated with angiogenesis. Therefore, a detailed analysis of the relationship between stemnessHigh cells and endothelial cells was conducted using CellChat. Various ligand–receptor interactions were observed between thyrocytes containing cancer cells and endothelial cells; however, the current study focused on the JAG1–NOTCH1/4 signaling pathway as a specific interaction for stemnessHigh cells. Although several reports regarding the involvement of NOTCH signaling in CSCs have been published [ ], the importance of its counterpart JAG1 remains largely unknown. The results of the present study indicate that JAG1 is highly expressed in thyroid CSCs and that JAG1 from thyroid CSCs may promote angiogenesis by binding to NOTCH1/4 on endothelial cells within the tumor microenvironment. Furthermore, one study reported that excessive NOTCH1 activation in endothelial cells within the tumor tissue promotes neutrophil infiltration and cancer cell metastasis [ ]. Meanwhile, another study by Eng et al. reported that NOTCH4 was highly expressed in vascular endothelial cells within tumor tissue and that treatment with a NOTCH4-blocking antibody significantly suppressed tumor growth [ ]. Furthermore, survival analysis revealed that JAG1 was highly expressed in the tissues of stemnessHigh patients and that patients exhibiting high JAG1 expression had significantly poorer outcomes that did those exhibiting low JAG1 expression. These results indicate that JAG1 not only serves as a marker for thyroid CSCs but may also functionally contribute to cancer malignancy.
For thyroid cancers with the BRAFV600E mutation, targeted drugs such as dabrafenib and encorafenib (BRAF inhibitors), as well as trametinib and binimetinib (MEK inhibitors), have demonstrated clinical efficacy [ , ]. These agents primarily suppress tumor cell proliferation through mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) pathway inhibition. However, the JAG1–NOTCH1/4 signaling identified in the current study represents an angiogenesis-related mechanism that is largely independent of MAPK signaling. Consequently, even when BRAF/MEK inhibitors effectively suppress tumor cell proliferation, CSC–endothelial interactions mediated by JAG1–NOTCH1/4 may remain active, thereby contributing to treatment resistance and microenvironmental remodeling. From this perspective, rational combination strategies integrating BRAF/MEK inhibitors with anti-VEGFR agents, and potentially with future JAG1–NOTCH-targeted approaches, may enhance long-term treatment efficacy in BRAFV600E-positive thyroid cancer. Therefore, while our findings provide a novel framework for considering complementary treatment strategies alongside currently available targeted therapies, they remain hypothesis-generating in nature.
Despite the multi-layered computational evidence discussed here, our study has notable limitations. One limitation of the current study is the lack of experimental evaluation using in vitro and/or in vivo experiments. Studies involving the use of tumorspheres, which are used as models for CSCs in various cancers, and experiments using patient specimens are important for detailed analysis on the stem cell regulatory mechanisms of thyroid CSCs. Another limitation of this study is that survival analyses were performed exclusively using the TCGA cohort. A recent study has indicated that racial differences exist in both the clinical presentation and disease severity of thyroid cancer [ ]. Therefore, further investigations using larger and more diverse cohorts are warranted to determine whether the findings of this study represent a universal phenomenon.
