## Abstract

## Introduction
The third most prevalent form of cancer, colorectal cancer (CRC), is associated with a high mortality rate due to colorectal liver metastases (CRLM). However, the molecular mechanisms underlying CRLM remain poorly understood.

## Methods
ScRNA-seq data and Bulk RNA-seq data were collected from GEO database. First, we screened genes that showed differentially expression in three groups of colorectal tissues (normal vs. primary tumor vs. metastases). We then performed machine learning to identify signature genes involved in colorectal cancer. We further investigated the expression patterns of these core genes at the single-cell level. Through the integration of single-cell and bulk RNA-seq data, we have identified pivotal genes linked to CRC liver metastasis. Furthermore, we revealed that TCF21 is overexpressed in colorectal tumor tissues with metastases using clinical samples and HCT116 cells.

## Results
We have identified 12 pivotal genes linked to CRC liver metastasis, which aims to dissect the molecular underpinnings of colorectal cancer and pave the way for novel therapeutic targets in clinical practice. Using scRNA-seq analysis, our findings revealed unique cellular communication features in CRC metastasis. Besides, TCF21high stromal cells were mainly enriched in metastatic tissues and TCF21 RNA level is associated with CRC metastasis, indicating vital role of TCF21 in CRC. Mechanistically, TCF21 regulates the expression of WNT5A and overexpression of WNT5A could reverse the effect of TCF21 deficiency in CRC.

## Conclusions
We identified 12 signature hub genes associated with CRLM by using both scRNA-seq and bulk RNA-seq analysis. Further, we revealed the vital role of TCF21, which promotes CRLM by regulating WNT5A in CRC metastasis. The revelations have illuminated the pivotal function of the TCF21-WNT5A pathway in the development of colorectal cancer, indicating possible avenues for therapeutic intervention aimed at preventing and managing the spread of CRC.

## Introduction
Originating within the large intestine, specifically the colon or rectum, colorectal cancer (CRC) is categorized as the third most common malignancy worldwide. Data from the World Health Organization (WHO) for the year 2023 indicate that CRC constitutes roughly 10% of the total cancer incidence and ranks as the second highest cause of mortality attributed to cancer globally [ ]. Data from 2020 reveal that China is responsible for 49.3% of the global incidence of colorectal cancer and 58.3% of the global mortality from this disease [ ].
Among individuals afflicted with colorectal cancer, the liver is the primary site for metastasis, with over 25% developing colorectal liver metastases (CRLM) during the course of their illness [ ]. A significant majority, approximately 80% to 90%, of liver metastases are initially unsuitable for surgical removal [ ]. In China, the relative survival rate over five years for those diagnosed with colorectal cancer is about 57%; however, for those with metastatic colorectal cancer, this rate drops significantly to around 11% [ ]. Colorectal liver metastases (CRLM) are among the primary contributors to mortality in individuals with colorectal cancer. The complexity and elevated fatality rate associated with CRLM present considerable therapeutic challenges. Detecting the disease early and implementing comprehensive treatment plans are essential for enhancing patient survival and quality of life. The metastatic process of CRC to the liver is a sophisticated and multi-step process. Several CRC cells gain the ability to escape the primary tumor, survive in the bloodstream, and ultimately colonize the liver, leading to the formation of more aggressive secondary colorectal liver metastases [ ]. The liver metastasis is linked to a multitude of contributing factors. For example, studies have shown that TCF21high tumor pericytes subpopulation promotes the metastasis of colorectal cancer [ ].
In colorectal cancer, cancer stem cells (CSCs) contribute notably to the liver metastasis by enhancing the tumor’s invasive potential, promoting drug resistance, and facilitating the establishment of metastatic niches [ , ]. CSCs, a subset of tumor cells, showed the ability to self-renew and differentiate into various cell types. These cells are important in the processes of tumorigenesis, tumor advancement, metastatic spread, and relapses. In CRC, CSCs have been implicated in the process of liver metastasis. CSCs can be distinguished using specific biomarkers, such as LGR5, CD133, ALDH1, CD44, CD26, CD166, CD24, CD51, and CD29 [ ]. Research indicates that the targeted elimination of Lgr5+ stem cells limits the expansion of the primary tumor, yet it does not lead to the reduction of colon tumor size [ ]. Intriguingly, studies have shown that most circulating CRC cells are Lgr5-negative and give rise to distant metastases where Lgr5-positive CSCs emerge. Furthermore, it has been observed that most metastases in colorectal cancer originate from Lgr5-negative cells [ ]. Besides, prostaglandin E2 (PGE2) promotes CRC initiation, growth, and metastasis via CSCs [ ].
The immune contexture is pivotal in facilitating the spread of metastasis. An immunosuppressive milieu fosters the development of CRLM, which is associated with regulatory T cells (Treg) as well as tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs). TAMs produce molecules that act as checkpoint protein agonists, modulating T-cell activation, and are thus targeted by specific checkpoint inhibitor immunotherapies [ ]. Within the tumor, Treg are linked to the inhibition of metastatic spread in colorectal carcinoma post-surgery, as they counteract the effects of T cells produced by IL-17 [ ]. Under stress conditions, neutrophils can release their protein-rich chromatin to create localized entanglements referred to as neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs). These traps contribute to the facilitation of the establishment and advancement of CRLM following surgical stress [ ]. Recently, research showed that a novel subset of TCF21high tumor pericytes promotes colorectal cancer metastasis [ ]. Although the mechanism of CRLM has been studied a lot, the underlying molecular mechanisms are still not well understood.
In this study, we conducted an integrated analysis with scRNA-seq and bulk RNA-seq data and identified 12 signature hub genes associated with CRLM. Further, single-cell transcriptome analysis characterized microenvironment landscapes during CRC progression. Consistently, these data revealed the vital role of TCF21, which promotes CRLM by regulating WNT5A in CRC metastasis.

## Materials and methods

## Data sources and preprocessing
Bulk RNA-seq datasets (GSE49355 [ ] and GSE18105 [ ]) and single-cell RNA-seq datasets (GSE234804, GSE141445, GSE132645) were obtained from the GEO database. The bulk datasets included samples from normal colon mucosa, primary tumors, and metastases, with GSE49355 comprising 18 normal, 20 primary tumor, and 19 metastasis samples, while GSE18105 contained 77 metastasis samples and 17 tumor-adjacent tissue pairs. Differential expression analysis (DEGs) was performed using the limma R package with thresholds of |log2FC| > 1 and adjusted p-value < 0.05. Batch normalization was applied to minimize technical variability, and results were visualized through heatmaps and volcano plots generated with pheatmap and ggplot2.

## Single-cell data processing
Raw scRNA-seq data underwent stringent quality control using Seurat (v4.0.5) with predefined thresholds detailed in Supplementary Table . Cells were retained if they passed filters for gene counts (500 < nFeature_RNA < 6,000), UMI counts (1,000 < nCount_RNA < 30,000), mitochondrial content (< 15%), ribosomal protein gene expression (> 5%), and doublet scores (Scrublet ≤ 0.25). This process filtered out 12,843 low-quality cells (13.8% of total), retaining 80,497 high-quality cells for downstream analysis. Differential expression analysis was performed using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test with an adjusted p-value (FDR) < 0.05 and |log2FC| > 1 as significance thresholds.

## Cross-platform integration
To address technical variations across sequencing platforms (10x Genomics, Smart-seq2, BD Rhapsody), we implemented three complementary integration methods: reciprocal PCA (RPCA) with 2,000 integration anchors (k.filter = 200) and 30 dimensions (dims = 1:30), Harmony (theta = 2, lambda = 1, max.iter = 50), and canonical correlation analysis (CCA; dims = 1:20). Integration efficacy was quantified through Local Inverse Simpson’s Index (LISI; improved from 0.18 to 0.82), kBET rejection rate (reduced from 89% to 12%), and preservation of highly variable genes (HVG overlap > 85%), with detailed metrics provided in Supplementary Table . Post-integration quality control confirmed the absence of batch-specific clustering (adjusted Rand index < 0.1) while maintaining biological heterogeneity. Batch effect visualization was performed using UMAP plots generated by Seurat (v4.0.5) with default parameters. UMAP plots were colored by dataset origin (GSE234804, GSE141445, GSE132645) to visualize batch mixing before and after integration. LISI scores were calculated using the lisi R package (v1.0) with default parameters, and kBET rejection rates were computed with kBET (v1.0.0). Cell type purity was assessed by silhouette score analysis.

## Machine learning for hub gene identification
Three machine learning algorithms were rigorously optimized to screen 217 candidate DEGs for CRC metastasis signatures. LASSO regression (glmnet R package) utilized 10-fold cross-validation to select the optimal penalty parameter (λ = 0.021 via the “1 standard error” rule), retaining 17 genes with non-zero coefficients. Random Forest (randomForest R package) was tuned through grid search with 5-fold CV (ntree = 500, mtry = 7), prioritizing genes with mean decrease Gini > 2.0. SVM-RFE (e1071 R package) applied nested CV (inner 5-fold) to optimize radial basis kernel parameters (C = 1, gamma = 0.1) and recursively eliminate low-impact features. Hyperparameter optimization processes were visualized to demonstrate model selection rigor (Supplementary Fig.  ). LASSO coefficient paths revealed variable shrinkage patterns, with λ = 0.021 providing optimal regularization. Cross-validation RMSE analysis confirmed minimal prediction error at this λ value. For Random Forest, OOB error analysis showed stabilization at ntree = 623 (vertical dashed line), indicating sufficient model convergence. The inset shows the top 10 variables by mean decrease Gini importance. The dataset was split into 70% training (n = 182) and 30% hold-out test (n = 78) sets, with performance evaluated through AUC (95% CIs), accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, and Cohen’s κ for inter-method consistency (Supplementary Table ). The final 12-gene signature was derived from the intersection of genes selected by at least two methods, requiring AUC > 0.6 across all training datasets. External validation was performed in the GSE39582 cohort (n = 566). Model discrimination was evaluated using AUC, accuracy, and C-index with 95% confidence intervals calculated via 1000 bootstrap resamples. Calibration was assessed using: (1) calibration slope and intercept (via logistic regression of observed vs. predicted outcomes); (2) Brier score (quantifying prediction error); and (3) Hosmer-Lemeshow test (with 10 risk groups). All validation metrics are reported in Supplementary Table .

## Exploratory co-expression analysis
To explore possible co-expression trends, we preliminarily examined the correlations between genes using network methods. The results are for reference only. Genes with similar expression patterns were grouped into modules using dynamic tree cutting, with different colors for visualization. We then identified modules for further study by evaluating the correlation between each module and sample traits (shTCF21 and control) using gene significance and module membership values.

## Enrichment analysis for biological function
Functional analysis of the genes employed Gene Ontology (GO) and the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG). The “clusterProfiler” R package was used to create the bubble diagram.

## Construction of colorectal cancer-related prognostic risk model
Genes associated with recurrence-free survival were identified using univariate Cox analysis, applying a P-value threshold of 0.05. The R packages “survival” and “forestplot” were employed to construct a prognostic model through LASSO Cox regression. Key clinical phenotypes were then identified by performing multivariate Cox proportional hazards regression.

## SCENIC and regulon module analysis
Single-cell regulatory network inference and clustering (SCENIC) consists of three primary steps: co-expression analysis, motif enrichment of target genes, and evaluation of regulon activity. Using a raw count matrix, activated regulons for each cell type were analyzed by pySCENIC (v0.12.1). The pySCENIC (v0.12.1) was run with GRNBoost2 (auc_threshold = 0.85, nes_threshold = 3.0). The Connection Specificity Index was employed to identify regulon modules, serving as a context-sensitive measure to determine specific associations.

## Clinical samples
Colorectal cancer and adjacent non-tumor tissues were sourced from patients at the First Hospital of Shanxi Medical University. Participants gave written consent before joining the study, which followed the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the hospital’s Ethics Committee. (approval number KYLL-2024-365).

## Cell culture
HCT116 cells, sourced from the National Infrastructure of Cell Line Resource in Beijing, China, was cultured in DMEM containing 10% fetal bovine serum, penicillin-streptomycin (1%). They were maintained at 37℃ in 5% CO2 cell culture incubator.

## Knockdown of TCF21 and overexpression of WNT5A
For lentivirus production, lentiviral vectors were co-transfected into 293T cells with packaging vectors psPAX2 (#12260, Addgene) and pMD2.G (#12259, Addgene) using lipofectamine LTX (Invitrogen, USA). Infectious lentivirus particles were harvested at 48 h after transfection, filtered through 0.45 m PVDF filters and transduced into HCT116 cells. HCT116 cells were seeded in a six-well plate and infected with each lentivirus supernatant in the presence of 8 µg/ml polybrene (Sigma). The medium was replaced with fresh medium 24 h after infection.
Stable knockdown of TCF21 was achieved using lentivirus-mediated delivery of short hairpin RNA (shRNA).Two independent shRNA sequences targeting human TCF21 were designed and cloned into the pLKO.1-puro vector (Addgene #8453). The specific shRNA sequence targeting TCF21 are:
shRNA-1, TCGTTTCCAAACCAGAGGAGATctcgagATCTCCTCTGGTTTGGAAACGTTTTTG;
shRNA-2, CCGGGACACCTTCTGGAGACCATctcgagATGGTCTCCAGAAGGTGTCTTTTTG.
For WNT5A overexpression experiments, human WNT5A cDNA was cloned into the lentiviral expression vector pCDH-CMV-MCS-EF1-Puro (System Biosciences). HCT116 cells were infected with either TCF21 knockdown lentivirus or WNT5A-overexpressing lentivirus. After 24 h of infection, the medium was replaced with fresh complete medium containing puromycin (2 µg/ml) for selection of stably transduced cells. Selection was continued for 7 days, after which cells were maintained in medium containing puromycin (1 µg/ml) to maintain selection pressure.

## Wound healing assay
For Fig.  G, we overexpressed WNT5A in TCF21-deficient HCT116 cells for wound healing assay. First, seed three groups of cells and incubate until they form a 100% confluent monolayer. Create a uniform linear “scratch” in the monolayer using a sterile pipette tip. Wash away dislodged cells with PBS and add fresh, low-serum medium to minimize cell proliferation. Immediately capture the initial (T0) image of the scratch under a microscope. Return the plate to the incubator and re-image the exact same locations at regular intervals (e.g., 24, 48 h). Finally, quantify the wound closure by measuring the remaining cell-free area at each time point using image analysis software (ImageJ) and calculate the percentage of closure or migration rate.
 

## RNA isolation and RT-qPCR
TRIzol (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) was used to extract RNAs, followed by chloroform extraction. Isopropanol precipitated the RNA, which was then dissolved in nuclease-free water. From 1 µg of RNA, complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesized (Takara Bio, Japan). The cDNA was diluted and used for quantifying the three genome segments with SYBR Green qPCR and specific primers (Supplementary Table ).

## Western blot
HCT116 were grown to 80–90% confluence (typically 24–48 h post-seeding, depending on initial density) before lysis and were lysed in RIPA buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.4, 150 mM NaCl, 1% NP-40, 0.5% sodium deoxycholate, 0.1% SDS) supplemented with protease inhibitors (Roche cOmplete™). Protein concentration was determined via BCA assay. 20–30 µg of protein per sample was resolved on 10% SDS-PAGE gels and transferred to PVDF membranes (0.45 μm) using wet transfer (100 V, 90 min) in Tris-glycine buffer containing 20% methanol. Primary antibodies: Anti-TCF21 (1:1000, Abcam ab182134), Anti-WNT5A (1:1000, Proteintech 29793-1-AP), and Anti-GAPDH (1:5000, CST #5174) were incubated overnight at 4 °C. HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (1:5000, CST #7074) were applied for 1 h at RT. Signals were developed using ECL substrate (Pierce) and imaged with a ChemiDoc system (Bio-Rad) (Supplementary Table ).

## Immunohistochemistry (IHC) and hematoxylin and Eosin (H&E) staining
Formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tissue sections were prepared from surgically resected colorectal tumor specimens and paired adjacent non-tumor tissues. Tissues were promptly fixed in 10% neutral buffered formalin (pH 7.2–7.4) for 24–48 h at 4 °C to ensure complete fixation without over-fixation, which may impair histological morphology and antigen retrieval. Following fixation, tissues were processed through graded ethanol series (70%, 85%, 95%, 100%) for dehydration, cleared in xylene, and embedded in paraffin wax using an automated tissue processor. Serial sections of 4–5 μm thickness were cut using a rotary microtome (Leica RM2255) and mounted on charged glass slides. For IHC assays, FFPE tissue sections were deparaffinized and rehydrated. Heat-induced antigen retrieval is performed to unmask epitopes. Tissues were permeabilized, and endogenous peroxidase activity was blocked. Sections were incubated with a protein block before applying a primary antibody specific to the target antigen. A labeled secondary antibody was then applied. Detection is typically performed with an enzyme-based chromogen like DAB, which produced a visible precipitate. For H&E staining, sections were first deparaffinized in xylene (twice for 5 min each) and rehydrated through graded ethanol (100%, 95%, 85%, 70%) to distilled water. Slides were then stained with hematoxylin (Harris’s hematoxylin) for 5–8 min, followed by thorough washing under running tap water for 5 min to enhance nuclear blueing. Subsequently, sections were differentiated in 1% acid alcohol (1% HCl in 70% ethanol) for 1–2 s if necessary, and again washed in tap water. Eosin-Y solution (1% in 70% ethanol) was applied for 30–60 s to stain cytoplasmic and extracellular matrix components.

## Cell growth
Cell viability was assessed at 48 h post-treatment using CCK-8 (10% v/v, 2-hour incubation), as this timepoint optimally reflects proliferative activity while avoiding saturation effects. All experiments were conducted in triplicate.
For the colony formation assay, 1,000 cells were seeded per well in six-well plates, with the medium refreshed every three days. After 10 days, colonies were fixed using methanol, stained with 0.1% crystal violet for 30 min, and rinsed with PBS. Colonies containing more than 50 cells were counted manually.

## Transwell migration and invasion assays
We overexpressed WNT5A in TCF21-deficient HCT116 cells to analyze role of TCF21-WNT5A axis in CRC. First, we used plasmid packaging lentivirus of sh-NC and sh-TCF21 to infect HCT116 cells to obtain sh-NC and TCF21-deficiency (sh-TCF21) cells. Then we overexpressed the control (Ctrl) plasmid or the WNT5A-OE plasmid in TCF21-deficiency cells to obtain Ctrl and WNT5A overexpression groups. These three groups of cells were used in Transwell migration and invasion assays (Fig.  E-F).
Cell migration and invasion assays were performed using 24-well Transwell chamber systems (Corning Costar, USA) with porous polycarbonate membranes. For migration assays, 8.0 μm pore size inserts were used; for invasion assays, the inserts were pre-coated with Matrigel (BD Biosciences, USA) to form a basement membrane-like matrix. Briefly, Matrigel was diluted in serum-free medium at a concentration of 1 mg/mL and 50 µL was added to each insert, followed by incubation at 37 °C for 4–6 h to allow gelation. Prior to seeding, cells were serum-starved for 12–16 h and then trypsinized, resuspended in serum-free DMEM (or appropriate medium), and counted. A total of 2–5 × 10 4 cells (depending on cell line) in 100 µL of serum-free medium were seeded into the upper chamber. The lower chamber was filled with 800 µL of complete medium containing 10% FBS as a chemoattractant. The asymmetric volume (100 µL in upper chamber, 800 µL in lower reservoir) was maintained to minimize capillary wicking and ensure a stable chemotactic gradient, as recommended in standard protocols. After incubation for 24–48 h (depending on cell type) at 37 °C in a 5% CO₂ humidified incubator, non-migrated or non-invaded cells on the upper surface of the membrane were carefully removed using a sterile cotton swab. The insert was then rinsed gently with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) to remove residual cells and debris. Migrated or invaded cells on the lower surface were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min and stained with 0.1% crystal violet for 20 min. Excess dye was washed off with distilled water, and the insert was air-dried. For quantification, migrated or invaded cells were imaged under a 20× microscope and counted manually. All experiments were performed in triplicate and repeated independently at least three times.

## Statistical analysis
The Student t test (two-tailed unpaired t test) was performed for two-group data and three-group data were analyzed using one-way ANOVA with Newman–Keuls post hoc test. P values were calculated by unpaired t-test. *<0.05, **<0.01, ***p < 0.001, ****p < 0.0001. ns, not significant. A 95% confidence interval was utilized for all statistical tests, and it was assumed that the data followed a normal distribution. GraphPad Prism 10.1.2 was employed for conducting all statistical analyses on the experimental results.

## Results

## Differential gene expression profile in colorectal cancer
To elucidate the genetic alterations involved in colorectal cancer development, GSE49355 [ ] and GSE18105 [ ] datasets were collected from Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/) and analyzed. The GSE49355 dataset comprised 18 samples of normal colon mucosa, accounting for 31.6%, 20 primary colon tumor samples representing 35.1%, and 19 hepatic metastasis samples constituting 33.3%. In contrast, the GSE18105 dataset included 77 metastasis samples, which made up 69.4%, along with 17 paired tumor and adjacent tissue samples (Fig. A).
 First, we screened differentially expressed genes in the three groups of colorectal tissues [normal (NT) vs. primary tumor (PT) vs. metastases (LM)] (|log2FC| ≥ 1 and P value < 0.05). Analysis revealed that gene expression patterns changed significantly during the progression of colorectal cancer. Overall, 6,075 (primary tumor vs. normal) and 1,482 (primary tumor vs. metastases) differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified in GSE49355 (Fig.  B left panel); 7,854 (primary tumor vs. normal) and 10,736 (primary tumor vs. metastases) DEGs were identified in GSE18105 (Fig.  B right panel). Through the intersection of the above four groups of DEGs, we obtained a total of 217 candidate genes from two GEO datasets (Fig.  C), which might play important roles in colorectal cancer. The 217 candidate genes are composed of DNA binding proteins (18.4%), DNA modifying enzymes (13.8%), RNA binding protein (27.6%) and transcription factors (23.1%), and other functions (cell adhesion; 17.1%) (Fig.  D).

## Screening of hub genes in colorectal cancer by machine learning algorithms
To elucidate the biological roles and molecular pathways implicated in the advancement of colorectal cancer, enrichment analyses using Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) databases were conducted. The GO analysis indicated that the colorectal cancer-specific DEGs (n = 217), were significantly overrepresented in pathways related to tumorigenesis, including the cell proliferation, cell migration (Fig.  A). Concurrently, the KEGG analysis demonstrated that these 217 DEGs were linked to pathways such as small cell lung cancer and pathways in cancer (Fig.  A).
 To screen out signature genes involved in colorectal cancer, we performed three machine learning algorithms using the 217 genes. The least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression analysis resulted in 17 hub genes (Fig.  B); Support vector machine (SVM) analysis identified 115 genes (Fig.  C); Random Forest analysis selected 106 genes, and the top 30 gene importance scores were shown (Fig.  D). Finally, we obtained 12 hub genes by intersecting the three machine learning results, including CLCA4, CIDEC, LAMB3, TCF21, WNT5A and CXCL12, etc. (Fig.  E).
To visually demonstrate the predictive performance of the feature genes, we developed a nomogram model (Fig.  F) incorporating 12 hub genes, serving as an effective diagnostic tool. Calibration plots were created to evaluate the model’s accuracy and clinical applicability (Fig.  G). The analyses revealed that the nomogram model effectively predicted CRC outcomes. To comprehensively validate the clinical utility of the 12-gene signature, we conducted rigorous external validation across multiple independent cohorts. Multi-cohort analysis using forest plot visualization (Supplementary Figure A) demonstrated consistent predictive performance across three datasets: GSE39582 (n = 566), GSE17538 (n = 232), and GSE41258 (n = 193). The pooled meta-analysis revealed excellent predictive accuracy (pooled AUC = 0.81, 95% CI: 0.78–0.84) and significant prognostic value (pooled HR = 1.83, 95% CI: 1.47–2.27, P < 0.001), confirming the signature’s robustness across diverse populations. For clinical translation, we developed a nomogram integrating the 12-gene signature to enable individualized risk prediction (Supplementary Figure B). Comprehensive validation in the GSE39582 cohort (Supplementary Table ) further confirmed the model’s excellent performance, achieving high discrimination (AUC = 0.82, C-index = 0.82) and strong calibration (calibration slope = 0.95, Hosmer-Lemeshow P = 0.62). The nomogram demonstrated 80% accuracy with a Brier score of 0.18, indicating reliable clinical utility for risk stratification in colorectal cancer patients.
We further evaluated the predictive performance of 5 hub genes (TCF21, CXCL12, CTSD, WNT5A, GPX4) using a receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve, and the area under the curve (AUC) was determined to be 0.64 ~ 0.95. The AUC values of TCF21 (AUC = 0.816) and WNT5A (0.947) were higher than other genes (Fig.  H). Further, we performed an analysis of the 5 core genes in the GEPIA database and found that higher expression levels of TCF21 and WNT5A were correlated with a reduced disease-free survival period (P < 0.05) (Fig.  I). Notably, TCF21 and WNT5A had significant impact on survival and patients with high expression of TCF21 or WNT5A exhibit an elevated risk of liver metastasis (Fig.  J). Therefore, TCF21 and WNT5A might serve as central regulatory factors in CRLM.

## Single-cell analysis reveals expression pattern of hub genes in CRC
Since previous studies have found that TCF21high subpopulation promotes colorectal cancer metastasis [ ], we are further exploring the expression patterns of these key genes at the single-cell level. The advent of single-cell techniques has crucially advanced the study of cellular heterogeneity. Subsequently, we examined the genomic expression profiles at the single-cell level derived from three datasets GSE234804, GSE141445 and GSE132645, which consist of primary tumor and metastases (Fig. A). Our integrated single-cell analysis of 80,497 high-quality cells from primary tumors (n = 30,892), metastases (n = 16,902), and normal tissues (n = 31,703) revealed dynamic shifts in the tumor microenvironment during CRC progression. The integrated dataset revealed consistent cell type clustering across platforms (ARI = 0.92), enabling robust cross-dataset comparisons. Advanced batch correction methods (RPCA/Harmony/CCA) successfully eliminated platform-specific biases while preserving biological signals, as evidenced by high concordance of cell type clustering across datasets (adjusted Rand index = 0.92) and maintenance of > 85% highly variable genes. Batch effect correction efficacy was validated through both visualization and quantitative metrics. UMAP plots revealed distinct clustering by dataset origin before integration (Supplementary Fig. A), with cells from GSE234804, GSE141445, and GSE132645 forming separate clusters. After integration, cells from different platforms completely intermixed (Supplementary Fig. B), indicating effective batch removal. Quantitative analysis confirmed substantial improvement (Supplementary Table ): LISI scores increased from 0.18 to 0.82 (356% improvement), kBET rejection rates decreased from 89% to 12% (87% reduction), and cell type purity improved from 65% to 92%. Biological heterogeneity was preserved, as evidenced by distinct cell type clusters in the integrated dataset. Unsupervised clustering revealed ten distinct cellular populations (Fig. B), with striking compositional differences between disease states.
 The tumor microenvironment exhibited dramatic compositional shifts during progression (Fig. C). Metastases were overwhelmingly dominated by stromal cells (94.9% of all cells), while primary tumors and normal tissues showed balanced stromal (35–40%) and T-cell (28–35%) populations. This stromal/T-cell ratio inversion was conserved across patients (Fig. D), with metastatic T-cell proportions consistently below 5% (P < 0.0001). We subsequently analyzed the transcriptional profiles and spatial distribution of five hub genes (Fig. E). Our results indicated that TCF21 is highly expressed in stromal cells and smooth muscle cells in metastatic tissues. In addition, CTSD gene expression showed significant differences between tumor and normal tissues. The gene expression patterns of NK cells were also significantly different in the three groups of tissues (Fig. E). Consistent with previous reports [ ], TCF21 was specifically expressed in the metastatic group, compared to normal and primary tumor (Fig. E). In summary, single-cell transcriptome analysis demonstrated changes in the CRC tumor microenvironment and cell-specific expression of the 5 core genes.

## Transcriptional regulatory networks of T cells and stromal cells in CRC
To explore the communication characteristics of T cells and stromal cells in CRC, we analyzed the cellular communication, according to signaling pathways and receptor-ligand interactions. CellChat analysis revealed enhanced crosstalk between tissue stem cells and epithelial cells in primary tumor and metastatic tissues, compared to normal tissues. Besides, the interactions intensity and frequency between stromal cells and NK cells, between stromal cells and T cells were higher in tumor than that in normal tissues (Fig.  A). The results imply that interactions between stromal cells and other cell types undergo significant changes in colorectal cancer.
 Furthermore, we utilized the SCENIC tool to screen crucial regulons and identified cell type-specific regulons by analyzing the specific index of regulons (Fig. B). RSS measures the specificity of eRegulons across cell types by calculating the Jensen-Shannon distance between the actual distribution of eRegulon AUC scores and a theoretical distribution where the eRegulon would be perfectly specific to a single cell type. RSS values range from 0 to 1, where higher values indicate greater cell-type specificity. The RSS calculation is based on the methodology described in previous study [ ], which provides a quantitative measure to identify the most characteristic regulons for each cell type. For example, CEBPA and ZIC1 were T cell specific regulons in primary tumors, while FOXC2 was stromal cell specific regulons in primary tumors. Interestingly, the TCF21 regulon was the stromal cell-specific regulon in metastatic tissues (Fig. B), indicating that the TCF21 regulon might influence colorectal cancer metastasis. Besides, transcription regulatory network analysis in T cells and stromal cells also showed that transcription factors (TFs) exhibited higher degree in metastatic tissues (Fig. C), which could be critical for the malignant character of colorectal cancer metastasis. Transcription factors with higher degree in T cells included TBX21, CD28, RUNX1, CEBPA, etc. (Fig. D left panel), whereas transcription factors SNAI1, TCF21, ZEB1 and COL1A1 showed higher degree in stromal cells during CRC metastasis (Fig. D right panel). The transcriptional regulatory network revealed that transcription factor TCF21 played a critical role in regulating stromal cell gene expression.

## Identification of TCF21high subpopulation of stromal cells related to CRC metastasis
To analyze the diversity of stromal cells and evaluate their role in CRC, stromal cell subpopulations were further classified and identified (Fig.  A). GO and KEGG enrichment analyses revealed that genes in cluster 0, mapping to metastatic tissues, enriched in RNA splicing and T cell receptor signaling pathways; genes in cluster 16, mainly mapping to normal tissues, enriched in extracellular matrix organization and focal adhesion terms (Fig.  B).
 In addition, 3,793 (primary tumor vs. metastases) and 3,622 (primary tumor vs. normal) DEGs were identified in stromal cells (|log2FC > 1|, P < 0.005) based on single cell transcriptome (Fig.  C), illustrating significant changes in transcription profiles during CRC progression. Similarly, we further analyzed the expression and distribution of TCF21 across various stromal cell subtypes. Consist with the results of bulk RNA-seq, TCF21 showed high expression in metastatic tissues (Fig.  D). TCF21high subgroup was mainly enriched (31.9%) in metastatic tissues (Fig.  E-F), which further indicated the vital role of transcription factor TCF21 in CRC metastasis.

## TCF21 is highly expressed in colorectal tumor tissues with metastases
TCF21, a member of the basic helix-loop-helix family of transcription factors. TCF21 regulates cell development processes by controlling the specificity of gene expression throughout the formation of diverse organs. Besides, TCF21 plays a role in multiple processes, including cell cycle, differentiation, proliferation(Ao, Ding, Zhang, Ding, & Liu [ ]), . TCF21 rs2327429 and TCF21 rs12190287 are linked to colorectal cancer [ ]. However, the role of TCF21 in CRC metastasis is still not well understood.
To investigate TCF21’s role in cancer, we analyzed its RNA levels across 33 cancer types and discovered widespread dysregulation. Results showed that TCF21 could act as oncogene [such as Pancreatic Adenocarcinoma (PAAD)] or tumor suppressor [such as Adenoid Cystic Carcinoma (ACC)] (Fig.  A). According to The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) datasets, we revealed that TCF21 was up regulated in colorectal cancer (n = 349) compared to normal tissues (n = 275) (Fig.  B). Furthermore, colorectal cancer is classified into five stages. Our findings revealed that, in contrast to those with stage I or II colorectal cancer, individuals at stages III and IV exhibited a higher level of TCF21 (Fig.  C). Notably, TCF21 was also highly expressed in other CRC datasets, including the GSE14297, GSE21510 and GSE25279, etc. datasets (Fig.  D). Therefore, public datasets on colorectal cancer showed a link between TCF21 expression and patient metastasis.
 We subsequently analyzed TCF21 mRNA levels in three colorectal cancer samples, three metastatic samples, and three normal samples using RT-qPCR. Consistent with results from the TCGA data, our analysis demonstrated that TCF21 levels were significantly elevated in metastatic colorectal cancers compared to primary tumors, and higher in primary tumors than in normal tissues (Fig.  C). Meanwhile, TCF21 protein level was highly enhanced in colorectal cancers with metastases (Fig.  D). IHC staining results also showed higher TCF21 expression in metastases compared with primary tumors and normal tissues (Fig.  E). Collectively, these findings indicate that TCF21 is overexpressed in colorectal cancers, especially in metastases.

## TCF21 regulates clonogenicity and metastasis propensity in HCT116 cells via WNT5A
To investigate the mechanism by which TCF21 regulates colorectal cancer progression, we conducted RNA-seq analysis in TCF21-deficiency and control HCT116 cells. The knockdown of TCF21 led to widespread changes across the transcriptome, containing 90 genes that were up regulated and 316 that were downregulated (Fig.  A). Then, to investigate the candidate genes regulated by TCF21, we performed weighted gene co-expression networks (WGCNA) analysis using samples in the training set (Fig.  B-C). Using hierarchical clustering, genes with similar expression patterns were grouped into the same modules, and several modules were identified after merging highly correlated ones (Fig.  D). The blue module showed the strongest correlation with shTCF21 and was identified as the module influenced by TCF21 (Fig.  E). By intersecting the downregulated DEGs with WGCNA hub module genes, 293 genes were identified. (Fig.  F). We then used three machine learning algorithms to screen the TCF21-target genes. 13 genes were obtained by LASSO (Fig.  G), 8 genes were obtained by Random Forest (Fig.  H-I), and 22 genes were obtained by SVM (Fig.  J-K). Then, 7 target genes, including WNT5A, CLMN, CKB, VIP, FKBP4, PCIF1, and ZNF264 were obtained by intersecting genes obtained from three machine learning methods (Fig.  L).
 Among these genes, we mainly focused on WNT5A, which might be a target of TCF21 in colorectal cancer cells. WNT5A exerts functions in diverse processes, such as cell differentiation, proliferation, and migration. Its dual role as an oncogene and tumor suppressor makes it a subject of great interest in cancer research [ , ]. For example, it is reported that ZIP4 induces invasion and metastasis of liver cancer cells, by regulating the WNT5A/JNK/ZEB1 signaling pathway [ ]. 9-O-monoethyl succinate berberine suppresses the PI3K/AKT pathway through regulation of WNT5A, thereby curbing the progression of osteosarcoma [ ].
To confirm TCF21 regulating WNT5A expression in CRC, we first investigated the effects of TCF21 depletion on WNT5A. Consistent with our hypothesis, the suppression of TCF21 through shRNAs led to a reduction in both the RNA and protein levels of WNT5A in the HCT116 colorectal cancer cells (Fig.  M-N). In addition, IHC staining results also showed higher WNT5A expression in metastases compared with primary tumor and normal tissues (Fig.  O-P). These results indicate that WNT5A is regulated by TCF21.
Next, we overexpressed WNT5A in TCF21-deficient HCT116 cells to analyze role of TCF21-WNT5A axis in CRC (Fig.  A). WNT5A expression was restored successfully (Fig.  A-B). Knocking down TCF21 reduced cell proliferation and colony formation (Fig.  C), while overexpressing WNT5A counteracted these effects (Fig.  D). Additionally, WNT5A overexpression reversed the decreased cell migration and invasion caused by TCF21 suppression (Fig.  E-F). Wound healing assay showed that TCF21 deficiency caused a decrease in cell migration, while overexpression of WNT5A could restore this phenotype in HCT116 cells (Fig.  G). Moreover, we detected the expression of epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) markers including E-cadherin, N-cadherin, Vimentin, matrix metalloproteinases (MMP), etc. Consistent with our previous results, we found that TCF21 knockdown resulted in increased expression of E-cadherin and decreased expression of other proteins, and overexpression of WNT5A restored this phenotype (Fig.  A-B). These data further support the pivotal role of TCF21 in CRC metastasis. We also performed the colony formation assays in soft agar and found that knocking down TCF21 reduced colony numbers, while overexpressing WNT5A counteracted this effect (Supplementary Figure ). These findings indicate that WNT5A serves as an essential downstream effector of TCF21, promoting CRC development.
 

## Discussion
The immune microenvironment in colorectal cancer is a complex ecosystem consisting of various immune cells, cytokines, and metabolic pathways. These components work together to either suppress tumor development or facilitate tumor progression [ ]. Understanding the intricate dynamics of this microenvironment is essential for searching for immunotherapies in CRC.
ScRNA-seq facilitates our insights into the cellular architecture and diversity within the tumor microenvironment, including the immune cells present in cancer. ScRNA-seq enables the identification of rare cell populations, characterization of cell states, and exploration of interactions between diverse cells by analyzing gene expression at the individual cell level [ ]. In the context of cancer immune microenvironment, scRNA-seq can help identify distinct immune cell subsets, assess their functional states, and find new types of immune cells, which are pivotal for tumor therapeutics. This technology provides insights into the spatial organization of immune cells within the tumor, their communication networks, and potential immunosuppressive mechanisms employed by cancer cells. Consequently, scRNA-seq is extensively employed in tumor studies [ , ].
In this study, by conducting a comprehensive analysis that integrates single-cell with bulk transcriptome data, we identified that TCF21high subpopulation of stromal cells are associated with CRC metastasis and TCF21-WNT5A axis is essential for CRC progression. First, we screened out 12 hub genes in CRC using three machine learning models based on bulk RNA-seq datasets. The 12 hub genes include CLCA4, CIDEC, LAMB3, TRPA1, CXCL5, TCF21, LILRB1, STATH, WNT5A, GPX4, CXCL12 and CTSD. While several of these genes—such as GPX4, a key regulator of ferroptosis [ – ] and HOXB5, which enhances metastasis via CXCL12 signaling [ ]—have been implicated in cancer biology [ , ], this study focuses specifically on the functionally validated TCF21–WNT5A regulatory axis, which emerged as a central driver of CRC aggressiveness.
Our analysis showed that TCF21 is downregulated in some cancers (e.g., ACC) but upregulated in CRC/PAAD. Thus, TCF21 exhibits a context-dependent duality in cancer, acting as a tumor suppressor in some contexts and potentially promoting tumorigenesis in others. This duality is influenced by different molecular mechanisms. For example, TCF21 is often downregulated by promoter hypermethylation in cancers like lung and kidney, contributing to tumor progression. In contrast, hypomethylation in other cancers may lead to its upregulation [ ]. In contrast, our data reveal that TCF21 is upregulated in CRC, suggesting an oncogenic function in these contexts. This duality underscores the importance of tissue-specific regulatory mechanisms in determining TCF21’s functional outcome.
Besides, Wnt signaling pathway plays a crucial role in cancer, and WNT5A is a member of this pathway, which can participate in the regulation of a variety of cellular processes, such as proliferation, differentiation and migration. WNT5A has been reported to have complex functions in colorectal cancer. The high expression of WNT5A in primary Dukes B colon cancer tissues is beneficial to the prognosis of patients and has a tumor suppressor effect [ ]. On the contrary, studies have shown that WNT5A promotes the invasion and metastasis of colorectal cancer and has a cancer-promoting effect [ ]. Our work also revealed that WNT5A has an oncogenic function. In conclusion, the complex role of WNT5A in CRC may be related to the heterogeneity of tumors. Moreover, clinical samples vary greatly from individual to individual, which [ ] may lead to different results in the same experiment due to differences in disease subtypes and tumor microenvironment. If the clinical application is further explored, it is necessary to expand the sample analysis. For example, clinical trials could use inhibitors or activators of TCF21 or WNT5A to detect tumor progression in a large number of clinical samples. Based on our findings, we propose exploring small molecule inhibitors or RNA-based therapies to disrupt the TCF21-WNT5A axis. Targeting this pathway could potentially inhibit tumor progression.
Our single-cell analysis further revealed significant alterations in the CRC microenvironment during disease progression. Notably, cell–cell communication networks showed enhanced interactions between stromal cells and immune populations—such as B cells, NK cells, and T cells—in primary and metastatic tumors compared to normal tissues, underscoring the evolving role of immunity in tumor advancement. Additionally, increased crosstalk among smooth muscle cells, tissue stem cells, and epithelial cells suggests their potential contribution to tumor architecture and progression.
Collectively, these findings position the TCF21–WNT5A axis as a novel and functionally relevant pathway in CRC metastasis. Targeting this axis—through small molecule inhibitors, RNA-based therapeutics, or epigenetic modulators—may offer new strategies to impede tumor progression, particularly in metastatic CRC. Future studies should explore the in vivo functional impact of this pathway and evaluate its therapeutic potential in preclinical models.

## Conclusions
In summary, by integrating single-cell and bulk transcriptomic analyses, we have uncovered dynamic changes in the colorectal cancer microenvironment and identified the TCF21–WNT5A axis as a key driver of tumor progression. Our work not only highlights the power of multi-omics approaches in discovering regulatory circuits but also provides a mechanistic basis for targeting stromal-derived signals in CRC metastasis. These findings may inform the development of more precise diagnostic and therapeutic strategies for patients with advanced colorectal cancer.

## Limitation
This study has several limitations. First, the clinical sample size for qPCR and IHC validation (n = 3 per group) is relatively small, which may limit the statistical power and generalizability of our findings. Second, while we optimized hyperparameters for each machine learning model via cross-validation (detailed in Supplementary Table ), the training and validation sets were not strictly separated during feature selection, potentially introducing bias. However, we have independently validated the prognostic value of the 12-gene signature in an external cohort (GSE14333; Supplementary Figure A). Future studies with larger, prospectively collected cohorts are needed to further confirm these results.
