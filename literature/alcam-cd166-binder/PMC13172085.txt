## Abstract
Over the past two decades, anti-CD20 monoclonal antibodies have become a cornerstone in the management of B cell malignancies and autoimmune diseases, including multiple sclerosis (MS). Although their ability to induce profound depletion of peripheral B cells is well established, the broader spectrum of their immunomodulatory actions remains incompletely understood. This gap is clinically relevant, as treatment responses in MS do not consistently correlate with peripheral B cell counts. Moreover, tissue-resident B cells, particularly those within the central nervous system (CNS) and secondary lymphoid organs, as well as long-lived plasma cells, are largely unaffected by anti-CD20 therapy. These observations underscore the need to elucidate additional mechanisms contributing to therapeutic efficacy, including effects in antibody-mediated disorders such as neuromyelitis optica spectrum disorder (NMOSD). In this review, we focus on preclinical and clinical evidence concerning the effects of anti-CD20 therapies on soluble immunological mediators—cytokines, chemokines, and adhesion molecules—across MS. Emerging data suggest anti-CD20 treatments may restore the balance between pro-inflammatory and regulatory pathways, indirectly modulating other immune cell populations. However, available studies are constrained by small sample sizes, heterogeneous methods, and variable patient populations. Future systematic investigations are needed to clarify mechanisms of action, guide rational combination therapies, improve understanding of treatment response and side effects, and deepen insights into the underlying disease itself.
Keywords: Multiple sclerosis, B cell depletion, Neuroinflammation, Immunomodulation, Autoimmune diseases, Mechanisms of action, Soluble markers, Monoclonal antibody therapies

## Key Summary Points

 

## Introduction
Over the past two decades, anti-CD20 monoclonal antibody therapies have emerged as a cornerstone in the management of both autoimmune diseases and B cell malignancies [ , ]. The breakthrough came with rituximab (RTX), a pioneering chimeric monoclonal antibody approved by the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) in 1997 for relapsed or refractory non-Hodgkin lymphoma, which revolutionized lymphoma treatment. Subsequently, further humanized therapeutic agents such as ocrelizumab (OCR), ofatumumab (OFA), ublituximab (UBI), and obinutuzumab (OBI) were developed, to enhance efficacy through better B cell depletion, reduced immunogenicity, and more convenient administration profiles [ , , ]. All of these therapies share the same pharmacological mechanism of action targeting the CD20 surface antigen, a transmembrane protein expressed on pre-B cells, immature, naive, mature, and memory B cells, but notably absent from hematopoietic stem cells, plasmablasts, and antibody-producing plasma cells [ – ]. This specific expression pattern allows for selective depletion of B lymphocyte subpopulations involved in pathological immune responses, while preserving the ability for B cell regeneration and long-term immune protection [ , ].
Anti-CD20 therapies have shown clinical efficacy across various trials in different disease entities, including multiple sclerosis (MS) [ , – ], rheumatoid arthritis (RA) [ – ], lupus nephritis [ ], and various non-Hodgkin lymphomas [ – ]. In the context of MS, anti-CD20 therapies have demonstrated significant reductions in relapse rates and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) lesion activity, making them a mainstay in the treatment of relapsing forms of the disease [ , ]. Moreover, OCR is also the only approved therapy to treat primary progressive MS (PPMS) [ , ]. In B cell malignancies, refined anti-CD20 monoclonal antibodies such as OBI are consistently administered in conjunction with chemotherapy or newer targeted drugs to improve treatment efficacy [ ]. Coupled with a favorable safety profile [ ], anti-CD20 agents play a major role in the treatment of multiple clinical conditions [ , , , , ].
Despite their clinical success, the precise mechanisms by which anti-CD20 therapies exert their therapeutic effects are still not fully understood. The canonical modes of action (MoA) include antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity (ADCC), in which immune effector cells (e.g., natural killer (NK) cells) mediate the depletion of opsonized B cells; complement-dependent cytotoxicity (CDC), which activates the classical complement cascade leading to B cell lysis; and direct induction of apoptosis including Fab-mediated cell death [ , , ].
Additionally, evidence of non-depleting immunomodulatory effects, such as modulation of T cell responses and alterations in cytokine and chemokine secretion profiles, keeps rising [ – ]. These effects are of particular interest as clinical responses only inconsistently correlate with the degree of peripheral B cell depletion [ – ]. Moreover, clinical benefits in MS, such as mean annual relapse rate, persist even after substantial peripheral B cell recovery, suggesting that mechanisms beyond B cell suppression contribute to treatment efficacy [ ].
Additionally, tissue-resident B cells, especially those located in the central nervous system (CNS) or within lymphoid organs, are largely resistant to anti-CD20-mediated depletion and may persist despite B cell treatment [ ]. This is a critical consideration, as MS is a CNS-specific autoimmune disease, and anti-CD20 antibodies, which do not efficiently cross the blood–brain barrier (BBB), are unlikely to achieve their full therapeutic effect solely through peripheral B cell depletion. Moreover, the clinical efficacy of anti-CD20 therapies in antibody-mediated disease such as CNS-specific neuromyelitis optica spectrum disorders (NMOSD) strongly suggests that their mechanisms of action are more complex and multifaceted than previously appreciated.
Anti-CD20 therapies induce profound and sustained depletion of peripheral B cells and, to a lesser extent, modulate T cell populations [ , , – ]. Given these pronounced effects on immune cells, corresponding alterations in soluble inflammatory markers would be anticipated. If such changes occur, the identified soluble markers could provide valuable insights into the pathogenesis of autoimmune diseases. This paradigm mirrors observations on the cellular effects of disease-modifying therapies (DMTs) in MS, which primarily target memory B cells and central memory Th17/1 cells, underscoring the critical role of these immune subsets in MS autoimmunity [ , ].
In this review, we undertake a critical examination of contemporary evidence regarding the mechanistic and therapeutic effects of anti-CD20 therapies that extend beyond mere B cell depletion. While the primary focus resides within the context of MS, we broaden the analytical scope to encompass other diseases in which these therapies are employed to augment the available corpus of data to provide a broad, integrative understanding of their pleiotropic mechanisms. We discuss their effects on soluble immune mediators and suggest potential research directions to better understand the complex immunological landscape they create. Our focus lies on their effects on cytokines, chemokines, and adhesion molecules, drawing insights from both clinical and preclinical studies.

## Methods
A structured literature search of PubMed and Web of Science was performed between June 2025 and October 2025. All terms related to anti-CD20 therapies (“anti-CD20 antibodies,” “anti-CD20 therapies,” “rituximab,” “ocrelizumab,” “ofatumumab,” “ublituximab,” “obinutuzumab”) were systematically combined with immunology-related terms (“immunological molecules,” “cytokine,” “interleukin,” “chemokine,” “adhesion molecule,” “humoral immunity”) using Boolean operators; these combinations were then further combined with the disease term “multiple sclerosis.” Each anti-CD20-related search term was paired with each immunology-related term to ensure comprehensive coverage of the literature. Eligible studies included clinical trials, in vitro experiments, animal studies, and case reports. Additionally, references of relevant articles were searched by hand to identify further pertinent literature. In total, 39 studies met the inclusion criteria and were incorporated into this review.
We structured this review by grouping the evidence into four main categories: (1) pro-inflammatory cytokines, (2) anti-inflammatory cytokines, (3) chemokines, and (4) adhesion molecules. This framework facilitates a coherent presentation of data while maintaining mechanistic distinctions between immune pathways. It also highlights areas of consistency and divergence across studies, providing context for how these immunological effects may relate to the clinical profile of anti-CD20 therapies. We primarily focused on findings from studies in MS but also included selected evidence from other disorders to provide a comprehensive overview.

## Ethical Approval
This article is based on previously conducted studies and does not contain any new studies with human participants or animals performed by any of the authors.

## Pro-inflammatory Cytokines and Anti-CD20 Therapies

## Interleukin-6
An analysis of cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) from 70 patients with relapsing–remitting MS (RRMS) and 55 healthy controls revealed that interleukin-6 (IL-6) levels were significantly elevated in RRMS at baseline; however, following a switch to RTX therapy, IL-6 concentrations decreased significantly over the 2-year observation period, suggesting a partial normalization of this pro-inflammatory cytokine [ ]. Additionally, the phenotypic and functional profiles of reconstituting B cells were investigated in six patients with MS after they began anti-CD20 treatment. This analysis was performed on the reemerging B cells prior to the next infusion, between 6 and 7 months after the initial treatment. Compared to their pre-treatment state, the reconstituting B cell population as a whole showed a significant alteration in cytokine production, but most notably a marked decrease in the pro-inflammatory cytokine IL-6 [ ]. Moreover, a foundational preclinical study employed the experimental autoimmune encephalomyelitis (EAE) model in mice to investigate the immunological consequences of B cell depletion. Mice received anti-CD20 monoclonal antibody treatment at different disease stages, and cytokine quantification in spleen and CNS tissues was conducted. The study revealed a significant reduction in IL-6 expression in both peripheral and CNS compartments, which correlated with improved clinical outcomes [ ]. Further evidence for IL-6 decrease by anti-CD20 therapy comes from studies in non-MS populations. Thirty-one patients with myasthenia gravis (MG) treated with OFA exhibited decreased IL-6 levels at 1–3 months post-treatment in peripheral blood [ ]. A prospective study of patients with HIV and Castleman disease (MCD) (n = 18), performed serial serum cytokine measurements. This investigation revealed a marked and sustained decrease in circulating IL-6 levels in most patients, which paralleled clinical remission of MCD symptoms [ ]. Lastly, in 45 patients with RA, RTX therapy was associated with significantly reduced IL-6 levels, along with decreased C-reactive protein (CRP) levels and erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) and improved clinical outcomes [ ].
Conversely, a different trend was observed in 22 patients with primary progressive MS (PPMS) receiving OCR in a prospective observational study. Serum IL-6 levels were measured at baseline and after 12 months. Interestingly, a significant decline in IL-6 was observed only in non-responders, while levels remained unchanged in responders, defined as the absence of relapses and new/enlarged T2 lesions on the brain and cervical MRI [ ].
In contrast, in 45 patients with RRMS or PPMS receiving OCR, IL-6 levels increased transiently at day 14 after the first treatment. IL-6 levels declined to baseline after the initial dose and did not rise again during follow-up infusions over the course of a year [ ]. Analysis of B cell supernatants following anti-CD40/IL-4 stimulation revealed a two- to sixfold increase in IL-6 blood levels which was further upregulated by consequent RTX-dependent trogocytosis [ ]. In a pharmacodynamic study, nine future kidney transplant recipients with prolonged waiting time due to anti-HLA antibodies received RTX to reduce HLA antibodies, and serum IL-6 levels were measured immediately before and 1 h post-infusion. A transient spike in IL-6 was observed in eight of the nine patients, which was interpreted as an infusion reaction, potentially linked to B cell lysis and innate immune activation [ ].
Taken together, evidence indicates that anti-CD20 therapies reduce IL-6 levels (Table  ), while an infusion-associated increase might be due to cytokine release related to B cell lysis. The interactions involving IL-6 are especially interesting because it plays a crucial role in acute-phase responses during inflammation, maturation of B cells into plasma cells, and differentiation of naive CD4+ Th cells into Th17 cells while inhibiting the formation of regulatory T (Treg) cells [ ]. These functions highlight their role in autoimmune diseases but also as therapeutic targets [ – ].
 

## Tumor Necrosis Factor Alpha
An ex vivo study using memory B cells from untreated patients with MS showed that B cell subsets secrete tumor necrosis factor alpha (TNFα) upon activation. Following RTX treatment, the B cell-derived TNFα secretion was significantly reduced [ ]. In EAE, administration of anti-CD20 monoclonal antibodies intraperitoneally to mice at peak disease phase showed a marked reduction in TNFα production, which correlated with clinical improvement and reduced demyelination [ ]. These findings suggest that B cell depletion may attenuate local TNFα expression also within inflammatory CNS lesions. Lastly, a single case report of a 26-year-old female patient with secondary progressive MS (SPMS) treated with two intrathecal RTX injections found a notable reduction of TNFα levels in CSF and showed clinical stability after a follow-up of 13 months [ ].
Conversely, paradoxical pro-inflammatory effects in a cohort of 22 patients receiving anti-CD20 therapy for a mean of 15 months in various neurological autoimmune disease including MS, NMOSD, and autoimmune neuropathies were observed. Monocytes isolated from these patients showed an increased TNFα secretion upon lipopolysaccharide (LPS) stimulation in vitro, compared to both pre-treatment levels and healthy controls [ ]. An increase of TNFα levels was also reported in 45 patients with MS at 14 days after an initial OCR infusion; however, these levels returned to baseline in the following infusions over 1 year [ ]. On the other hand, in PPMS, 22 patients treated with OCR for 24 months showed no dynamics in TNFα levels over time [ ]. Lastly, synovial biopsy from 12 patients with RA obtained at baseline and 12 weeks following RTX treatment revealed no changes in TNFα concentrations during the follow-up period [ ], findings that align with observations of unchanged TNFα levels despite RTX-dependent trogocytosis following anti-CD40/IL-4 stimulation [ ].
Current evidence on the impact of anti-CD20 therapy on TNFα are heterogeneous (Table  ), suggesting that these effects may vary according to underlying disease, specific cell type, and treatment administration routes. An increase associated with recent anti-CD20 therapy initiation could again partially be associated with a cytokine release phenomenon.
TNFα plays a central role in various immune-mediated, inflammatory, and neurodegenerative diseases, mainly through the cytotoxic and pro-inflammatory effects induced by binding the tumor necrosis factor receptor 1 (TNFR1). It also triggers the release of several other inflammatory molecules, including various cytokines and chemokines, amplifying the inflammatory response [ – ]. Elevated TNFα concentrations have been detected in both serum and CSF, correlating with greater disease activity and symptom severity [ ].
Evidence indicates that dysregulated TNFα signaling may exacerbate demyelination and apoptosis, may have a role in sustaining chronic intrathecal inflammation, and may drive axonal damage and neurodegeneration, accelerating disease progression and the accumulation of disability [ – ]. These observations make the impact of anti-CD20 treatments on TNFα of relevance.

## Lymphotoxin Alpha/Tumor Necrosis Factor Beta
Two studies examining cytokine profiles in patients with RRMS revealed that memory B cells exhibit aberrant pro-inflammatory CD4+ and CD8+ T cell responses after activation, with secretion of significantly higher levels of lymphotoxin alpha (LTα) and TNFα. RTX therapy markedly lowered the release of activation-associated B cell factors following depletion, primarily reflecting a loss of LTα and TNFα production by memory B cells [ , ].
The limited data available thus indicates that anti-CD20 therapy disables the pro-inflammatory B cell subset response for producing LTα. Elevated levels of LTα have been documented in the CSF of individuals with MS [ ], and increased expression of LTα messenger ribonucleic acid (mRNA) has not only been detected in peripheral blood lymphocytes preceding relapses in RRMS but also in B cells of patients with SPMS [ ]. Therefore, lowering effects by anti-CD20 therapies may aid in inhibiting the disease.

## Interferon Gamma
In a study of 22 patients with PPMS treated with OCR, no changes in serum interferon gamma (IFNγ) levels were observed between responders and non-responders at 24 months [ ]. Similarly, analysis of B cell supernatants under anti-CD40/IL-4 activation showed a two- to sixfold increase in multiple cytokines, including IFNγ, but RTX-dependent trogocytosis had no effect on IFNγ levels [ ]. Among nine anti-HLA-sensitized dialysis-dependent patients treated with RTX, IFNγ was undetectable before and 1 h after infusion [ ]. In mice treated with anti-CD20 therapy for 16 weeks, serum IFNγ was again undetectable at baseline and after the follow-up period [ ]. A study of 56 patients involving patients with latent tuberculosis infection and controls without found no significant changes in IFNγ release after 1 year of RTX therapy [ ].
In contrast, following the first OCR infusion in 45 patients with MS, IFNγ levels rose significantly, yet no IFNγ elevations were observed during later infusions given every 6 months, once again indicating the possible involvement of an infusion reaction [ ]. Finally, a case report of a patient treated for hemophilia A with RTX described a fourfold increase of IFNγ mRNA expression at day 26 post-treatment [ ].
Although many studies reported no significant changes in serum IFNγ levels, some investigations demonstrate a reduction in IFNγ-producing T cells following B cell-depleting therapy. In a report of 12 treatment-naive and 8 patients with MS treated with OFA, stimulated CD4+ T cells showed decreased IFNγ production after treatment [ ]. Similarly, a multicenter prospective study of 53 patients with PPMS revealed a significant decline in IFNγ-producing CD4+ and CD8+ T cells 6 months after OCR initiation [ ]. In these studies, however, serum IFNγ concentrations were not measured. A single trial including seven patients with non-Hodgkin lymphoma reported a significant reduction of blood IFNγ immediately after RTX, with no reductions reported in follow-up appointments [ ].
The available evidence suggests that anti-CD20 therapies do not alter blood IFNγ concentrations but might reduce IFNγ-positive T lymphocytes. IFNγ is a major product of Th17/1 cells, which have crucial roles in the pathogenesis of MS [ ]. In a small placebo-controlled trial published in 2001, inhibition of IFNγ contributed to an improvement in Expanded Disability Status Scale (EDSS) in MS [ ]. Nevertheless, the precise role of IFNγ in MS remains ambiguous, with evidence suggesting a stage-specific contribution in both MS and EAE [ ]. To date, no approved MS therapy directly targets IFNγ.
In contrast to the findings related to IFNγ, a study involving 20 patients with MS found that levels of IFNα2, a type I interferon, known to promote proliferation, antigen presentation, and the maturation of immune cells such as dendritic cells and NK cells [ ], were significantly reduced after 12 months of OCR treatment [ ]. This suggests a potential differential effect of anti-CD20 treatments on type I versus type II interferons. Further studies on IFNα are needed to clarify its role in the context of B cell depletion therapy.

## Interleukin-1 Beta
A prospective observational study involving 22 patients with PPMS receiving OCR demonstrated no changes in interleukin-1 beta (IL-1β) levels 12 months post-treatment [ ]. Similarly, analysis of B cell supernatants under anti-CD40/IL-4 activation showed a two- to sixfold increase in IL-1β levels, while consequent RTX-treatment did not change this release in any way [ ]. On the other hand, in a group of 13 patients with MS, peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were isolated before and after OCR infusions to assess cytokine responses ex vivo. IL-1β release was detected following LPS stimulation before treatment, but was suppressed after OCR infusion [ ]. Moreover, reduced IL-1β gene expression has been reported in patients with RA 6 months after RTX treatment, coinciding with improvements in DAS28 and CRP [ ]. Together, these findings may indicate a favorable immunomodulatory effect, as elevated IL-1β levels in MS have been associated with relapses and lesion burden, potentially through induction of BBB disruption, demyelination, and axonal injury [ , ]. Collectively, however, the available evidence suggests that anti-CD20 therapies exert no consistent effects on IL-1β concentrations (Table  ).

## Interleukin-12
Interleukin-12 (IL-12) activates NK cells and promotes the differentiation of naive T cells into Th1 cells, thereby playing a role in Th1-driven autoimmune diseases [ , ]. In a prospective observational study, CSF cytokine profiles in 70 patients with RRMS receiving RTX were analyzed and compared to 55 healthy controls. CSF samples were collected at baseline, after 6 and 24 months. RTX treatment resulted in significant reductions in CSF IL-12 levels, approaching those observed in healthy individuals, suggesting that the mode of action of anti-CD20 therapy includes normalization of the IL-12/Th1-associated cytokine milieu in the CSF [ ]. Similarly, in a subgroup of 13 patients from a total of 45 patients with MS, PBMCs were isolated before and after OCR infusions to assess cytokine responses ex vivo. Upon LPS stimulation, IL-12 release was observed prior to treatment; however, after OCR infusion, IL-12 release was inhibited [ ]. Consistent with this observation, a small real-world cohort of 12 patients with RRMS and 12 with PPMS showed a reduction in IL-12p70 concentrations 12 months after OCR initiation [ ]. However, a study involving 22 patients with PPMS treated with OCR for 24 months showed no dynamics in IL-12 levels [ ]. Together, the effects of anti-CD20 therapies on IL-12 warrant further evaluations.

## Interleukin-15
A prospective observational study examined CSF cytokine profiles in 70 patients with RRMS undergoing RTX treatment, comparing them to 55 healthy controls. CSF samples were taken at baseline, 6 months, and 24 months. The findings showed that RTX significantly lowered CSF interleukin-15 (IL-15) levels, bringing them closer to those seen in healthy individuals [ ]. Additionally, a study of patients with RA reported significant reductions in serum IL-15 24 weeks following RTX administration, coinciding with clinical improvement [ ]. These findings suggest that anti-CD20 therapy may have the potential to attenuate IL-15-driven pro-inflammatory circuits. Given that IL-15 promotes chronic inflammation by sustaining long-lived cytotoxic lymphocytes and enhancing microglial activation [ ], and that elevated IL-15 levels in serum and CSF are implicated in MS immunopathogenesis [ ], such reductions may contribute to the therapeutic benefits of B cell depletion.

## Interleukin-17
Anti-CD20 therapies demonstrated variable modulatory effects on interleukin-17 (IL-17) pathways across multiple diseases. In PPMS, 22 patients treated with OCR for 24 months showed no significant decline in IL-17A levels over time [ ]. Complementary mechanistic insights came from studies in relapsing MS, where PBMCs from patients treated with OFA had lower IL-17 production upon stimulation compared to treatment-naive controls [ ]. Similarly, after two anti-CD20 treatment cycles, Th17/1 central memory cells, a major source of IL-17, were significantly reduced among 15 patients with MS [ ]. In RA, serial serum analyses of 33 patients receiving RTX revealed progressive reductions in IL-17, with declines correlating to improved disease activity (DAS28 score) [ ]. In the nonobese diabetic (NOD) mouse model of type 1 diabetes, anti-CD20 antibodies also reduced the serum levels of IL-17A and IL-17F [ ]. Similarly, patients with MG treated with OFA exhibited decreased IL-17 levels at 1–3 months post-treatment in peripheral blood [ ]. Tissue-specific effects were observed in Sjögren’s syndrome, where biopsies from 15 patients after RTX therapy showed reduced IL-17 concentrations in the salivary gland [ ]. These findings align with observations in renal transplant recipients, where stimulated CD4+ T cells produced less IL-17 when co-cultured with lymph node B cells from patients pretreated with RTX versus untreated controls [ ]. Lastly, RTX also reduced IL-17 production in vitro induced by Candida albicans [ ].
In summary, evidence suggests that anti-CD20 therapies reduce IL-17 production in various immune-mediated diseases, also in those that seem to have components that are Th17-driven, such as MS or RA (Table  ) [ , , ]. The impact of anti-CD20 therapy on IL-17-associated pathways represents an important avenue for future research.

## Interleukin-23
Interleukin-23 (IL-23) supports the expansion and maintenance of Th17 cells, which are potent pro-inflammatory cells and critical in chronic inflammation and autoimmune diseases [ ]. Elevated IL-23 levels have been reported in MS [ ], yet evidence of anti-CD20 treatments on IL-23 is scarce. In patients with MS who switched to RTX, a modest reduction in CSF IL-23 p40 levels was observed, although this subunit does not necessarily reflect changes in composite IL-23 levels [ ]. In a NOD mouse model of type 1 diabetes, anti-CD20 antibodies decreased serum IL-23 levels [ ]. However, the paucity of data underscores the need for further investigation.

## Other Pro-inflammatory Cytokines
In line with the partly fragmented evidence on the effects of anti-CD20 therapies on key pro-inflammatory molecules such as IL-6, IFNγ, IL-17, and TNFα, data on other pro-inflammatory cytokines remain limited. In nine patients with MS, intrathecal RTX triggered a sustained rise in serum B cell activating factor (BAFF) while decreasing BAFF in the CSF [ ]; these findings contradict those observed with a NOD mouse model of type 1 diabetes treated with anti-CD20 antibodies, where decreased serum BAFF levels were reported [ ]. In another study, OCR treatment in 19 patients with MS with sustained depletion of B cells and CD20+ T cells, increased BAFF levels in both serum and CSF were observed 12–19 months after therapy start [ ]. On the other hand, the serum levels of BAFF and IL-20 significantly decreased among another heterogeneous MS group of 24 patients treated with OCR [ ]. A prospective observational study involving 22 patients with PPMS treated with OCR showed no significant change in IL-2 levels after 12 months of follow-up [ ]. A few investigations reported reductions in GM-CSF-producing immune cells. OCR decreased the number of all CD19+ B cells including the ones producing GM-CSF as reported by a multicenter prospective study including 53 patients with PPMS [ ]. Similarly, in eight patients with MS treated with OFA, a reduction in GM-CSF-producing CD4+ T cells was observed, yet no levels of GM-CSF were measured [ ].
As a result of limited data and the absence of longitudinal studies, we could not identify consistent trends in the expression of these cytokines.

## Anti-inflammatory Cytokines and Anti-CD20 Therapies

## Interleukin-10
The effects of anti-CD20 antibodies on interleukin-10 (IL-10) levels appear highly context-dependent. In 12 patients with PPMS treated with OCR, serum IL-10 levels were significantly reduced after 12 months of treatment [ ]. In EAE models, CD11b+ cells from mice treated with anti-CD20 antibodies produced less IL-10 but more pro-inflammatory TNFα [ ]. A prospective cohort study of 45 patients with RA supports these findings. Patients received two doses of RTX 2 weeks apart which led to decreased serum IL-10 levels after 6 months [ ]. While these data point to a long-term suppressive effect of RTX on IL-10 production in such patients, there are studies that report a different trend. Among 45 patients with RRMS or PPMS treated with OCR, IL-10 levels peaked 14 days within the first treatment year [ ]. Additionally, a study primarily analyzing B cell subsets in MS found that IL-10 production was predominantly associated with naive (CD27 negative) B cells. Following B cell reconstitution induced by RTX treatment, IL-10 expression levels rose, indicating a shift toward a more regulatory B cell phenotype [ ].
Finally, in a cohort of 22 patients with PPMS treated with ocrelizumab for 24 months, serum IL-10 concentrations remained unchanged over time [ ]. Consistently, while stimulation of B cells with anti-CD40 and IL-4 enhances IL-10 secretion, RTX treatment did not impact IL-10 levels [ ]. In transgenic mice (expressing a dominant-negative transforming growth factor beta receptor type II (dnTGFβRII)), serum IL-10 remained undetectable during 16 weeks of anti-CD20 therapy [ ].
Taken together, available evidence suggests that anti-CD20 therapies can modulate IL-10 levels in either direction (Table  ), depending on the immune milieu, treatment timing, and disease stage. An increase in IL-10 levels could likely be beneficial because IL-10 helps control immune responses by inhibiting the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines and by downregulating the expression of MHC class II and co-stimulatory molecules on antigen-presenting cells [ , ]. Through these mechanisms, IL-10 preserves immune homeostasis, limits tissue damage from excessive inflammation, and exerts protective effects in autoimmune disorders, malignancy, and transplantation [ ].
 

## Other Anti-inflammatory Cytokines
There is not much data on the impact of anti-CD20 therapies on additional anti-inflammatory cytokines. However, some may play roles in immunoregulation and even in autoimmune diseases. For example, interleukin-35 (IL-35), a heterodimeric cytokine secreted mainly by Treg cells and regulatory B cells, exerts potent immunosuppressive effects by expanding IL-35-producing Treg cells, suppressing effector T cell proliferation, reducing IFNγ and IL-17 secretion, and has been shown to ameliorate disease severity and demyelination in EAE [ – ]. In a cross-sectional study of 31 patients with type 1 diabetes, RTX treatment increased serum IL-35 at 7, 14, 21, and 28 days post-treatment and restored CD4+ T cell frequencies, suggesting that B cell depletion may enhance regulatory pathways via IL-35 induction [ ]. Interleukin-4 (IL-4) plays a central role in promoting the differentiation of naive CD4+ T cells into Th2 cells and inducing immunoglobulin class switching in B cells to IgE and IgG4 [ ]. A prospective observational study involving 22 patients with PPMS treated with OCR reported no change in IL-4 levels after 12 months of follow-up [ ].
Interleukin-38 (IL-38) is an anti-inflammatory cytokine that belongs to the IL-1 family and is expressed by epithelial cells, B cells, monocytes, and in various tissues affected by inflammatory diseases [ ]. IL-38 exhibits anti-inflammatory functions in autoimmunity and beneficial effects in sepsis [ ]. Recombinant IL-38 can inhibit IL-17 and IL-22 production in human PBMCs, and reduces Th17 responses [ ]. This cytokine may serve as biomarker for disease activity in patients with RA [ ]. A single study identified within our review reported that IL-38 was increasingly released from CD19+ B cells in vitro following RTX administration [ ].

## Chemokines and Anti-CD20 Therapies
In MS, elevated CSF levels of C–X–C motif chemokine ligand 13 (CXCL13) and C–C motif chemokine ligand 19 (CCL19) are consistently associated with intrathecal immune activity. CXCL13 correlates with B cell infiltration, oligoclonal immunoglobulin synthesis, and the presence of ectopic meningeal lymphoid follicles, particularly in progressive MS, while higher CCL19 levels indicate active CNS inflammation and contribute to the development of perivascular lymphoid aggregates [ – ]. Thus, both chemokines are well-established biomarkers of compartmentalized CNS immune activation and are linked to disease activity and progression. Although evidence regarding the modulation of these chemokines by anti-CD20 therapies is limited, available data suggests a suppressive effect. A phase II RTX trial showed a significant reduction in both CXCL13 and CCL19 and no changes in CCL4 in serum and CSF 6 months post-treatment among 30 patients with RRMS, accompanied by lower relapse rates and diminished MRI activity [ ]. In a prospective cohort of 101 patients with RRMS treated with OCR, CSF levels of CXCL13 and CXCL10 decreased by 63% and 23%, respectively, by week 52 [ ]. Another prospective observational study including 70 patients with RRMS treated with RTX demonstrated a significant reduction in CSF concentrations of CXCL10 and CXCL8, approaching near-normal levels [ ]. CXCL10 and CXCL8 are pro-inflammatory chemokines that mediate T cell and neutrophil recruitment to the CNS, playing roles in neuroinflammatory diseases including MS [ , ]. Some studies have found elevated levels in the CSF during MS relapses or in NMOSD [ , ]. Their reduction by anti-CD20 therapies may reflect attenuation of CNS inflammation.
In a cohort of 20 patients with RA treated with RTX and methotrexate, those with persistent B cell depletion at 6 months had lower serum CXCL13 levels [ ]. Similarly, in another study of 33 patients with RA, CXCL10 behavior diverged between responders and non-responders: CXCL10 tended to increase in non-responders, while it remained stable or decreased in responders [ ]. Additionally blood from 16 volunteers treated with therapeutic concentrations of RTX resulted in reduced chemokine C–C motif ligand 20 (CCL20) levels in approximately one-third of subjects [ ].

## Adhesion Molecules and Anti-CD20 Therapies
Soluble intercellular adhesion molecule 1 (sICAM-1) and soluble vascular cell adhesion molecule 1 (sVCAM-1) are circulating isoforms of adhesion molecules shed from endothelial and immune cells. They regulate leukocyte adhesion and migration including the CNS and are therefore importantly involved in neurological inflammatory disorders [ , ]. In MS, elevated serum and CSF levels have been associated with disease activity, correlating with relapses and new MRI lesions [ ]. The VCAM-1/very late antigen 4 (VLA-4) interaction is particularly critical for the entry of T and B cells into the CNS and is blocked by the monoclonal antibody natalizumab, a highly potent drug used in MS [ – ]. Although these adhesion molecules are thought to play critical roles in MS pathophysiology, data on their modulation by B cell-depleting therapies are limited. In a prospective observational study, CSF cytokine profiles were analyzed in 70 patients with RRMS receiving RTX and compared with 55 healthy controls, with samples collected at baseline and at 6 and 24 months. RTX treatment resulted in significant reductions in CSF sVCAM-1 and sICAM-1 levels, approaching those observed in healthy individuals [ ]. These findings highlight a potentially underappreciated mechanism of anti-CD20 therapy and underscore the need for dedicated studies to explore how anti-CD20 therapies impact adhesion molecules. ## Anti-CD20 Therapies and Immunoglobulins
This review does not focus on the effects of B cell-targeted therapies on immunoglobulins (Ig); however, for completeness, a brief discussion is provided to better contextualize the mechanism of action of anti-CD20 therapies. Anti-CD20 therapies selectively deplete B cells at the pre-B, immature, naive, and memory stages, while sparing antibody-producing CD20− plasma cells [ , ]. Over time, however, reflecting impaired replenishment of antibody-producing B cell precursors, reductions in IgM, followed by IgG and IgA are frequently observed. Although these decreases are generally modest, prolonged therapy or repeated dosing can result in clinically significant hypogammaglobulinemia, increasing susceptibility to infections [ – ]. Monitoring immunoglobulin levels during treatment is therefore recommended to balance therapeutic efficacy with immune safety [ ]. Anti-CD20 therapies also impair vaccine responses and blunt pathogen-specific antibody production to novel antigens, whereas recall responses to previously encountered antigens appear to be largely preserved [ – ]. Interestingly, the mechanism underlying the rapid efficacy of anti-CD20 therapies in autoantibody-mediated autoimmune disorders, declines in autoantibody levels are observed after several months–years [ ], remains incompletely understood.

## Discussion
Despite the widespread clinical use of anti-CD20 therapy, data regarding their effects on soluble inflammatory mediators remain scarce. Across available studies, no soluble molecule has been consistently shown to change following B cell-targeting therapies, which is noteworthy given the central immunoregulatory role of B lymphocytes. Some studies indicate that anti-CD20 treatments reduce key pro-inflammatory cytokines, including IL-6, TNFα, LTα, IL-17, IL-15, and IL-12, which may contribute to decreased inflammation and improved clinical outcomes (Fig.  ) [ , , – , – , – , , , – ]. IL-15 levels have been linked to disease severity in RRMS [ ], and enhanced IL-17A levels were associated both with BBB breakdown in RRMS and with joint erosion in patients with RA [ , ]. Therapeutic blockade of IL-17A, for example, has shown clinical benefit in RA, supporting the relevance of these cytokines as potential mediators of anti-CD20 treatment effects [ , ]. Transient increases in IL-6 and TNFα immediately after infusion likely reflect cytokine release syndrome secondary to profound B cell depletion [ , ]. Effects on IFNγ are heterogeneous, generally showing no significant serum changes while some note reductions in IFNγ-producing T lymphocytes [ , , , – , , , ]. In fact, we only identified one study that demonstrated a decrease in soluble IFNγ levels in body fluids [ ], while most others reported either no changes [ , , – ] or even transient increases [ , ]. These discrepancies may, however, also relate to differences in sampling time points, tissue compartments, or disease context.
 Consistent with the heterogeneous effects observed for pro-inflammatory mediators, anti-inflammatory cytokines display variable responses to B cell depletion, with reports of transient increases, decreases, or no significant changes depending on disease context and timing. We did not identify data on the effects of anti-CD20 treatment on TGFβ in patients with MS; however, evidence from other disorders indicates that TGFβ concentrations can increase following anti-CD20 therapy [ , , ], reflecting its role in immune homeostasis and tissue repair. Enhanced TGFβ expression may exert neuroprotective and antioxidant effects in MS [ ], where higher CSF concentrations correlate with shorter disease duration, milder disability, and relapsing–remitting disease courses [ ]. Furthermore, TGFβ facilitates remyelination within the CNS [ ]. IL-10, a key immunoregulatory cytokine, also exhibits variable responses, with studies reporting increased [ , ], decreased [ , , ], or unchanged levels [ , , , ] in chronic autoimmune conditions. In MS, higher IL-10 levels have been linked to decreased disease activity in relapsing–remitting courses, whereas lower levels correlate with longer disease duration, increased relapse frequency, and greater motor disability [ ]. The overall inconsistency of IL-10 findings, however, argues against a uniform IL-10-mediated mechanism underlying anti-CD20 therapy.
Chemokines such as CXCL13 and CCL19 are critical for the organization of adaptive immune responses, mediating the recruitment of B cells, follicular helper T cells, and dendritic cells to lymphoid tissues. In MS, elevated CSF concentrations of CXCL13 and CCL19 correlate with intrathecal immune activation, B cell infiltration, ectopic lymphoid follicle formation, and CNS inflammation, and may therefore serve as biomarkers for disease activity and progression [ , ]. Anti-CD20 therapies have been reported to reduce CSF and serum levels of these chemokines, paralleling clinical improvements and decreased MRI activity [ , ]. Adhesion molecules, including soluble ICAM-1, VCAM-1, and ALCAM, facilitate leukocyte migration and CNS entry and are elevated during active MS [ – ]. These findings highlight their contribution to MS pathogenesis and raise the possibility that modulation of adhesion molecule expression may represent an additional mechanism of anti-CD20 therapy. However, as only a single study in our review reported decreased levels of these molecules after treatment, no definitive conclusion can be drawn.
Furthermore, changes in soluble immune mediators may help explain clinically observed long-term immune effects. In a real-world observational cohort of patients with MS treated with OCR (up to 5.6 years of follow-up), serum immunoglobulin levels (IgM and IgG) declined over time, and lower IgG levels were associated with higher infection rates and subprotective antibody responses to pneumococcal and Haemophilus influenzae antigens [ ]. Similarly, a multicenter retrospective study reported that hypogammaglobulinemia occurred in a substantial proportion of patients with MS treated with OCR or RTX and was associated with an elevated risk of severe infections over a median follow-up of more than 2 years [ ]. In addition, large retrospective cohort data indicated that higher cumulative RTX exposure was linked to lower IgG levels and an increased risk of both serious and recurrent outpatient infections, underscoring the influence of humoral immune alterations on clinically relevant outcomes [ ]. However, existing literature indicates that infection risk is not solely determined by total immunoglobulin levels but is influenced by additional immunological and individual factors. Accordingly, changes in other soluble immune mediators may play a complementary role, as interleukins and chemokines critically regulate humoral immunity and B cell function. For instance, IL-21 and IL-6 directly promote B cell differentiation into antibody-secreting plasma cells, support class switching, and sustain germinal center responses, thereby influencing immunoglobulin production and immune competence [ , ]. Chemokines such as CXCL13, which are involved in B cell recruitment, intrathecal immunoglobulin synthesis, and inflammatory activity in MS, may similarly reflect or modulate long-term immune dynamics after anti-CD20 therapy [ ]. This suggests that profiling such mediators could enhance biomarker-driven monitoring strategies.
This discrepancy between the well-established effects of anti-CD20 monoclonal antibodies on peripheral B cell counts [ , , – ] and the less defined impact on soluble immunological markers may arise from either weaker downstream effects or the possibility that key molecules remain underinvestigated. It is also crucial to emphasize that cytokine changes, such as transient increases in IL-6, TNFα, and IFNγ within the first hours after infusion [ , ], without subsequent long-term alterations, should be interpreted as infusion-related responses to anti-CD20 therapy, primarily reflecting acute pharmacodynamic effects rather than sustained immunological changes [ ]. Addressing this gap, a recent study proposed and validated a serum-based panel of 18 proteins that reliably distinguishes between active and stable disease MS [ ]. The included molecules span three mechanistic categories: immunomodulatory markers reflecting cytokine and B cell-related regulation; neuroinflammatory markers indicating glial activation and BBB dysfunction (e.g., CXCL13); and markers of neuroaxonal integrity and myelin biology (e.g., myelin basic protein, myelin-associated glycoprotein), linked to tissue injury and repair. Future studies should assess the effects of anti-CD20 therapies on these emerging serum biomarkers in well-characterized MS cohorts.
While the present review focuses on soluble immune mediators, it is important to note that anti-CD20 therapies also impact T cell populations, including reductions in pro-inflammatory CD4+ and CD8+ subsets and alterations in follicular helper T cells, as recently reported in multiple studies [ , , , ]. These findings highlight that B cell depletion can indirectly modulate cellular immunity, expanding the broader immunological context of anti-CD20 therapy, though a detailed review of T cell effects falls outside the scope of this work.
With recent advances in high-throughput proteomic technologies enabling the simultaneous analysis of over 1000 proteins, comprehensive profiling of soluble mediators will become increasingly feasible. Such approaches may enhance immune-monitoring, support biomarker-guided precision therapy, and clarify mechanisms of action beyond peripheral B cell depletion.

## Conclusions
Anti-CD20 monoclonal antibodies effectively deplete peripheral B cells, yet their broader immunomodulatory effects on cytokines, chemokines, and adhesion molecules remain incompletely understood and likely underinvestigated. Evidence suggests increased levels of the anti-inflammatory cytokine TGFβ and overall reductions in pro-inflammatory mediators, including IL-6, TNFα, LTα, IL-17, IL-15, and IL-12, following treatment. However, the magnitude and consistency of these soluble immune alterations are too inconsistent to support firm conclusions, and the available evidence is limited by small sample sizes, variable analytical methods, and heterogeneous patient populations. Future research will be critical to determine whether these soluble immunological mediators can serve as accessible and dynamic biomarkers that predict therapeutic responses, anticipate the risk of adverse events, and ultimately enable more personalized disease management.
