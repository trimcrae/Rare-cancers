## Background
Sepsis survivors frequently develop compromised humoral immunity, manifesting as prolonged immunosuppression that increases susceptibility to secondary infections with high associated mortality. While mesenchymal stromal cells (MSCs) have demonstrated efficacy in mitigating initial inflammatory responses and improving early survival rates in murine cecal ligation and puncture (CLP) models, they show limited effectiveness against subsequent secondary infections. Given that lymphopenia represents a primary driver of this infection vulnerability, we sought to investigate whether spleen-targeted MSC therapy could prevent lymphocyte depletion and enhance protection against secondary infections.

## Methods
In this study, we developed CXCR5-overexpressing MSCs (MSCCXCR5) to enhance their targeted migration toward splenic CXCL13. Using a CLP mouse model followed by secondary Staphylococcus aureus (S. aureus) infection, we systematically evaluated the therapeutic potential of MSCCXCR5 in combating post-sepsis immunosuppression.

## Results
MSCCXCR5 demonstrated enhanced splenic migration, specifically homing to CXCL13-rich B-cell zones, and conferred significant protection against both S. aureus secondary infection and sepsis-induced lymphopenia. Mechanistic investigations revealed that MSCCXCR5 treatment preserved follicular and germinal center B-cell populations, maintaining splenic architecture and potentiating antigen-specific B-cell responses to CLP challenge. These effects collectively contributed to markedly improved survival outcomes in CLP mice following secondary infection.

## Conclusion
Our findings demonstrate that MSCCXCR5 confers dual-phase protection in sepsis, improving survival during both the initial hyperinflammatory phase and subsequent immunosuppressive period. This comprehensive therapeutic effect stems from MSCCXCR5’s ability to preserve B-cell function and prevent secondary infections in septic mice.

## Introduction
Sepsis mortality follows a biphasic pattern, with distinct peaks in patient fatalities. The initial peak occurs within days of onset, resulting from uncontrolled infection and hemodynamic collapse leading to cardiopulmonary failure [ ]. The second mortality peak emerges weeks later, driven by persistent immunosuppression [ ]. While advances in critical care have reduced early-phase mortality, most sepsis deaths now occur during this prolonged immunosuppressive phase, highlighting its crucial role in sepsis outcomes [ , ]. Growing evidence confirms that immunosuppression represents the predominant contributor to sepsis mortality in modern clinical practice. The immunological basis of this late-phase mortality was first demonstrated by Meakins et al., who identified impaired delayed-type hypersensitivity responses to recall antigens in fatal sepsis cases [ ]. Subsequent work by Hotchkiss et al. revealed extensive apoptosis of both innate and adaptive immune cells as the pathological hallmark of terminal sepsis [ ]. These findings suggest that preserving immune cell populations during the immunosuppressive phase could significantly improve survival outcomes.
Preclinical studies proved that acute-phase immunological activation induces splenomegaly initially [ , ], mirroring the hyperinflammatory state in early sepsis. Subsequently, progressive lymphocyte apoptosis leads to splenic atrophy [ , ], paralleling the immunosuppressive phase. This pathological lymphocyte loss severely compromises adaptive immunity, particularly impairing the survival and functional maintenance of B-cell subsets, which play a pivotal role in host defense through antibody production, establishing critical immunological memory against recurrent infections [ ]. Their activation and maturation are orchestrated by CXCR5 + PD-1 + Bcl6 + follicular helper T (Tfh) cells, which recognize cognate antigens and secrete cytokines to initiate germinal center (GC) formation [ ]. Within GCs, repeated antigen exposure drives B cell differentiation into long-lived plasma cells and memory B cells, ultimately generating high-affinity antibodies for effective pathogen neutralization [ ]. Emerging evidence reveals that sepsis disrupts this protective mechanism, with mortality correlating strongly with impaired B cell maturation and GC formation in secondary lymphoid organs. Notably, septic patients demonstrate progressive B cell depletion during later disease stages [ – ], suggesting that therapeutic strategies preserving splenic GC responses could offer dual benefits: enhanced clearance of primary infections and protection against lethal secondary infections.
Mesenchymal stromal cells (MSCs) function as pleiotropic immune modulators capable of influencing diverse immune cell populations, including B cells, T cells, NK cells, and dendritic cells [ , ]. This broad immunomodulatory capacity has prompted clinical evaluation of MSC therapy for sepsis, particularly given their demonstrated efficacy in controlling hyperinflammation in preclinical models [ , – ]. However, their therapeutic potential during the prolonged immunosuppressive phase of sepsis remains controversial. Our investigations using the murine CLP model reveal that while MSCs improve early-phase survival during acute inflammation, they fail to protect against secondary S. aureus infection or prolong survival during the immunosuppressive phase (Fig. ). This limitation may stem from inadequate splenic homing, as current MSC therapies demonstrate poor trafficking to this critical lymphoid organ [ , ] - a potentially crucial factor determining therapeutic efficacy in sepsis-associated immunosuppression.
Previous engineering strategies have demonstrated that MSCs overexpressing CCR7 or ICAM-1 exhibit enhanced homing to splenic T-cell zones and lymph nodes following systemic administration, leading to more effective T-cell modulation [ , ]. These studies established that improved splenic localization correlates with enhanced immunomodulatory capacity and better clinical outcomes [ ]. Notably, MSC localization in the marginal zone (MZ) has been shown to promote MZ B-cell survival and strengthen antimicrobial immunity in post-stroke infection models [ ]. Building on this foundation, our prior work demonstrated that CXCR5 overexpression enhances MSC migration toward CXCL13 both in vitro and in vivo [ ]. Given that CXCL13 is constitutively expressed at high levels in the spleen, where it directs the follicular trafficking of CXCR5 + B cells and Tfh cells to maintain germinal center reactions and antibody affinity maturation, we hypothesize that CXCR5-engineered MSCs (MSCCXCR5) would precisely target B-cell-rich splenic regions. Such targeted localization could potentially rescue sepsis-induced B-cell dysfunction and reduce late-phase mortality by restoring adaptive immune competence.
Here, we demonstrate that MSCCXCR5 exhibit enhanced splenic tropism with specific localization to B-cell zones, leading to significant expansion and functional enhancement of splenic B-cell populations. Crucially, MSCCXCR5 administration dramatically improved survival in CLP mice following secondary S. aureus infection by: [ ] preserving splenic B-cell numbers and [ ] potentiating antigen-specific humoral responses. This dual-phase therapeutic efficacy protected against both the initial hyperinflammatory phase and subsequent immunosuppressive state, establishing splenic MSC-immune cell crosstalk as a critical determinant of sepsis outcomes.

## Materials and methods

## Mice
Male C57BL/6 mice were obtained from GemPharmatech Co., Ltd. All mice were housed in a specific pathogen-free facility with 12 h light/dark cycles, with ad libitum access to food and water. Mice exhibiting no visible abnormalities in appearance or behavior were randomly divided into four groups using a random number generator: the sham group (n = 35), CLP group (n = 40), CLP + MSCEGFP group (n = 40), and CLP + MSCCXCR5 group (n = 40). The sample size was reasonably estimated by referring to the experimental literatures [ , ] related to CLP. At least 5 mice were used for each group in each independent experiment, and a single mouse is an experimental unit. The mice were anesthetized with ketamine. Mice were euthanized by cervical dislocation following deep anesthesia when necessary. For each mouse, two researchers were involved: one researcher was responsible for grouping and manipulation, and the other researcher was responsible for recording experimental results. The Sun Yat-sen University Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee reviewed and approved all the animal protocols. The work has been reported in line with the ARRIVE guidelines 2.0.

## Experimental design
To evaluate the therapeutic effect of MSCs in a murine model of sepsis, mice were randomly divided into the sham group and the CLP group. Four hours after model induction, mice in the CLP group received either 1 × 10⁶ MSCs or an equal volume of normal saline via tail vein injection, while mice in the sham group were administered an equal volume of normal saline. Survival rates were monitored and recorded over the following two days.
To evaluate the therapeutic effect of MSCs on secondary infection in septic mice, animals were randomly assigned to Sham and CLP groups. Four hours after CLP surgery, mice in the CLP group received either 1 × 10⁶ MSCs or an equal volume of normal saline via tail vein injection, while mice in the Sham group were administered an equal volume of normal saline. Two weeks later, all groups were challenged with a secondary infection by intravenous injection of S. aureus. Survival was monitored and recorded following the secondary infection to assess treatment efficacy.
To investigate the effects of MSCCXCR5 and MSCEGFP on splenic immune cells in septic mice, animals were divided into four groups: sham group, CLP group, CLP + MSCEGFP group, and CLP + MSCCXCR5 group. The timing and dosage of treatment were consistent with previously described protocols. Two days after the treatment, splenocytes were isolated from each group, and immune cell subsets were analyzed using flow cytometry.

## Isolation and characterization of MSCs
Human bone marrow samples were obtained from healthy donors with their informed consent. Then MSCs were isolated and cultured as previously described [ ]. Shortly, 20 ~ 30 ml bone marrow was diluted 1:1 with human MSCs culture medium including low-glucose Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (L-DMEM; Hyclone, Logan, UT, USA) and 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Hyclone). MSCs were separated by Ficoll-Paque (1.077 g/mL; Amersham Biosciences, Uppsala, Sweden) density gradient centrifugation and seeded at a density of 1 × 105/cm2. After three days, the culture medium was changed to remove the nonadherent cells. The cells were passaged by 0.25% trypsin-EDTA at 80 ~ 90% confluence and designated as passage 1. After the fourth passage, the cells were tested for the expression of CD29, CD44, CD73, CD90, CD105, and CD166 (MSC markers), but not CD34 or CD45 (hematopoietic markers). And their multipotent differentiation potential was verified by induction into osteoblasts, chondrocytes, and adipocytes using lineage-specific media.

## Lentiviral vector construction and production
Lentiviral vectors pLV/puro-EF1a-CXCR5-IRES-EGFP and pLV/puro-EF1a-EGFP were constructed using the Gateway cloning method as previously described [ ]. The vectors contain a constitutive EF1α promoter for efficient expression of the target genes and an IRES-EGFP sequence for simultaneous tracking of transduced cells. The CXCR5 gene was inserted into the vector following the standard Gateway cloning protocol.
Lentiviral particles were produced by transient co-transfection of 293FT cells with the pLV/puro-EF1a-CXCR5-IRES-EGFP or pLV/puro-EF1a-EGFP constructs, along with the ViraPower Lentiviral Packaging Mix (Invitrogen), using Lipofectamine 2000 (Invitrogen) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. For transfection, 293FT cells were plated at 70–80% confluence in 10 cm culture plates. The transfection was carried out with a total of 10 µg plasmid DNA (5 µg lentiviral vector and 5 µg packaging plasmids) in 10 mL serum-free media, and Lipofectamine 2000 was used at a ratio of 1 µL reagent per 1 µg DNA. After 4 h, the transfection medium was replaced with complete DMEM/F-12 medium containing 10% FBS.
Three days post-transfection, the culture supernatant containing lentiviral particles was collected, filtered through polyether sulfone membranes (pore size: 0.45 μm), and concentrated by ultracentrifugation at 50,000 × g for 90 min using an Optima L-80 K preparative ultracentrifuge (Beckman Coulter). The resulting viral pellet was resuspended in cold phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and stored at − 80 °C until use.

## MSCs transduction and selection
MSCs at passage 3 were dissociated into single-cell suspensions using 0.125% TrypLE Select (Invitrogen) and resuspended in MSCs culture medium. To enhance lentiviral transduction efficiency, 5 µg/mL Polybrene (Sigma-Aldrich) was added to the cell suspension. Cells were seeded at 1 × 10⁵ cells/cm² in 6-well plates and infected with lentiviral particles (MOI = 20) for 48 h. After transduction, cells were maintained in culture for another 24 h. MSCCXCR5 and MSCEGFP were purified by FACS (Influx, Becton Dickinson). Stably transfected MSCs were cultured continuously after sorting. Transduction efficiency was monitored by fluorescence microscopy (for EGFP expression), and CXCR5 expression was evaluated by flow cytometry using an anti-CXCR5 antibody (BioLegend). mRNA expression was confirmed by quantitative real-time PCR. The chemotactic function of CXCR5-modified MSCs was assessed using a transwell migration assay toward a CXCL13 gradient.

## Cecal ligation and puncture
Mice were anesthetized using ketamine 100 mg/kg and xylazine 10 mg/kg. Abdominal access was gained via a midline incision. The cecum was isolated and ligated below the ileocecal valve for the lethal CLP and ligated at the distal 25% of the cecum for the sublethal CLP with a 4 − 0 silk ligature. Punctured the cecum by single through-and-through puncture midway between the ligation and the tip of the cecum with a 25 G needle. Stool (approximately 1 mm) was extruded from the hole, and the cecum was placed back into the abdominal cavity. The abdomen was closed with two layers of 6 − 0 silk nonabsorbable surgical sutures. After surgery, 1 ml saline was injected i.p. for resuscitation. Sham-operated mice had the cecum isolated and then returned to the peritoneal cavity without being ligated or punctured. Sham mice also received resuscitative fluid as described above. The premature death of mice due to improper surgical manipulation was not included in subsequent experiments and data statistics.

## Microbial counts
Mice were anesthetized using Pentobarbital Sodium 50 mg/kg. Spleen were collected and homogenized for 30 s with a PowerGen 125 homogenizer (Fisher Scientific) in sterile saline. Spleen homogenate, peritoneal lavage fluid, or blood collected in 20 mM EDTA to prevent clotting, was serially diluted 10-fold in sterile saline, and plated on nutrient agar. Three to four dilutions were plated per mouse in triplicate, and plates were incubated at 37 °C. Colonies were counted after 16 h, and average CFUs per milliliter of spleen homogenate, blood or lavage fluid were calculated per mouse.

## Bacterial Preparation and infection
The Staphylococcus aureus (BeNa Culture Collection) was grown to log phase in Lysogeny broth media. Bacteria were diluted to 109 CFU in 0.2 ml sterile saline and injected via the tail vein. Serial dilutions of the same culture used for injections were plated and grown overnight at 37 °C and colonies counted to independently determine the actual number of injected bacteria.

## Immunofluorescence staining
Spleens were harvested at the indicated time points, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (Sigma), washed in phosphate buffer saline (PBS), and dehydrated overnight in 30% sucrose (Sigma). The samples were embedded in Tissue-Tek OCT compound (Bio-Optica) and frozen in an ethanol dry-ice bath. Section (20-mm thick) were placed on glass slides (Bio-Optica), blocked for 30 min with PBS-Tween 0.05% plus normal goat serum, and then incubated with the appropriate primary and secondary antibodies (see Supplementary Table S2 and S3; diluted in blocking solution) overnight at 4 °C and for 45 min at room temperature, respectively. Nuclei were then stained with DAPI (Fluka). Sections were washed and mounted in fluorescent mounting medium (Dako). Images were acquired using Zeiss LSM780 confocal microscope and Zeiss AxioScan.Z1.

## Flow cytometry
At the indicated time points, spleens were dissociated using 70-µm mesh and the plunger of a syringe. Blood was collected in 20 mM EDTA in PBS. Splenocytes and peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were isolated by Mouse Lymphoid Separation Medium (Dakewe Biotech). Red blood cells for all cell preparations were lysed for 60 s using ACK lysis buffer. Cells were then counted and stained for flow cytometry. For staining, cells were incubated for 30 min with the appropriate antibody (see Supplementary Table S4) in the dark at 4 °C and then analyzed by flow cytometry. Each analysis was performed on at least three separate cell preparations. Flow cytometric analyzes were performed with CytoFLEX (Beckman Coulter) flow cytometers, and the data were analyzed with the FlowJo10.6.2 (Treestar software packages.

## Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)
The levels of IL-6, IL-10, TNF-α (eBioscience) and IgG (Neobioscience) in the blood of mice were measured by commercially available ELISA kits according to the manufacturer’s recommended procedures. For NP-specific IgG, a 96-well ELISA plate (Costar) was coated with NP-BSA (Biosearch Technologies) in PBS overnight and washed. 1:800 dilution of each serum sample was then loaded into the plate and incubated at 37 °C for 1 h. After washing, the plate was further incubated with HRP-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG (Genetex) at 1:2000 for 1 h before development with TMB Substrate Set (eBioscience). The chromogenic reaction was stopped with stop solution (eBioscience) and optical density (OD) was measured at 450 nm on a microplate reader (Tecan).

## TUNEL labelling of cell death
Spleen sections were harvested as described above. Apoptotic cells were measured using CF640 TUNEL Cell Apoptosis Detection Kit (Servicebio) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Sections were then incubated with B220 for 2 h at room temperature and DAPI for 5 min. After washing, sections were mounted in fluorescent mounting media (Dako) and images were captured using a Zeiss LSM780 confocal microscope (Zeiss).

## In vitro B cells apoptosis induction
Mouse splenocytes were acquired as described above. B cells were sorted using a MoFlo Astrios Cell Sorter (Beckman Coulter) and cultured in RPMI 1640 supplemented with 5% FCS, L-glutamine and MEM non-essential amino acid (Gibco). Then, B cells were stimulated with 100ng/ml LPS and 1µM dexamethasone and cocultured with MSCs for 48 h. Propidium Iodide PI and Annexin V FITC (BD Biosciences) were stained to analyze the apoptosis of B cells.

## Statistical analysis
All data in this study are expressed as mean ± SD obtained from at least three independent experiments. Comparisons between two groups were performed using Student’s t-test. The Tukey test in one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used for multiple comparisons. As for survival studies, we used a log-rank test. P values < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyzes were performed with Prism 8 software.

## Results

## MSCs fail in the treatment of secondary infection after sepsis
We first established the mouse model of CLP and 1 million MSCs were administered via tail vein injection at 4 h post-CLP. As control, an equivalent volume of normal saline was administered to a separate group of CLP mice and sham-operated mice (Fig.  A). We found that MSC treatment significantly improved the survival rate of the CLP mice (Fig.  B). Since early mortality in sepsis is associated with systemic hyperinflammation caused by cytokine storm, we subsequently analyzed the serum concentrations of sepsis-related inflammatory factors and found a remarkable reduction in TNF-α and IL-6 levels, while serum IL-10 levels were increased in the MSC-treated group after 6 h of injection (Fig.  C), indicating that MSC treatment ameliorated the hyperinflammatory state in these mice. Furthermore, MSC treatment exhibited notable reductions in lung injury scores, apoptosis of splenic cells, and serum amylase/ALT/AST levels, while increases in hepatic glycogen storage capacity (Fig.  D-G), suggesting a protective effect against organ damage induced by CLP. In conclusion, our study confirms the beneficial effects of MSC treatment on CLP-induced sepsis.
Since the most cases die from secondary infections caused by long-term immunosuppression, we wondered if MSCs could effectively combat it. To address this question, we followed the sepsis with secondary infection by tail vein injection of S. aureus on day 15 of the modeling while maintaining MSC treatment at 4 h after CLP induction. As control, S. aureus was administered to an additional group of CLP mice and sham-operated mice at the same time point (Figure S1). Compared to the sham-operated group, both the CLP group and MSC-treated group exhibited significantly lower survival rates following secondary infection, indicating that even with MSCs, there was no prevention against death from secondary infection for CLP mice (Fig. H). Additionally, as the immunoglobulin G (IgG) is the main antibody produced in humoral immunity to protect the body against infection [ ], we measured the serum IgG levels and found there was no significant difference between MSC-treated group and CLP group after secondary infection (Fig. I). Furthermore, we observed a significant increase in bacterial loads in the blood, abdominal cavity, and spleen of CLP mice and MSC-treated group following secondary infection compared to the sham group (Fig. J, K). In summary, administration of MSCs can improve the initial hyperinflammatory state of sepsis, but does not confer therapeutic benefits against subsequent infections post-sepsis.
 

## MSCCXCR5 can target to the splenic follicular B cell region
The compromised humoral immunity following sepsis renders the body incapable of resisting secondary infection [ , ]. Therefore, if the early-stage apoptosis of B cells can be inhibited, patients with sepsis may acquire the ability to combat secondary infection [ ]. To address this issue, we hypothesized that MSCs, specifically designed to target the B-cell region of the spleen, could have the protective effects on B cells.
The expression of CXCL13 is highly concentrated in the B-cell region of the spleen and is secreted by dendritic cells [ ]. In this study, we transfected MSCs with lentiviral vectors encoding either CXCR5 or EGFP (Figure S2A, B). Both cell lines maintained their MSC properties, including the expression of specific positive markers and the ability to differentiate into osteogenic, adipogenic, or chondrogenic lineages (Figure S2C-F). Compared to MSCEGFP, MSCCXCR5 exhibited significantly higher mRNA and cell surface expression levels of CXCR5 (Figure S2G, H). Importantly, after induction with 20ng/ml CXCL13 for 10 h, MSCCXCR5 displayed a stronger chemotactic effect than MSCEGFP (Figure S2I, J). Both types of MSCs demonstrated an inhibitory effect on B cell apoptosis in vitro (Fig. A, B).
To validate the targeted migration effect of MSCCXCR5 in CLP mice in vivo, we administered 1 million MSCCXCR5 or MSCEGFP cells at 4 h post-modeling and assessed the quantity and distribution of MSCs in spleen two days after injection (Fig.  C). Flow cytometry analysis revealed that the EGFP + cells of spleen were 0.20 ± 0.06% in the MSCEGFP group and 0.323 ± 0.056% in the MSCCXCR5 group (Fig.  D, E). We then selected few of the largest longitudinal sections of the spleen for fluorescence detection and found that the counts of EGFP+ cells in the MSCCXCR5 group were almost twice as high as those in the MSCEGFP group, and that MSCCXCR5 were located in the white pulp region, while MSCEGFP were in the red pulp region (Fig.  F, G). Specifically, immunofluorescence staining allowed us to visualize the distinct localization patterns of these two cell types within the spleen, that MSCCXCR5 predominantly resided within follicular B cell region, while MSCEGFP primarily occupied the marginal zone (Fig.  H). According to the CXCL13 expression in various organs after CLP (Figure S3A), we found that plenty of MSCEGFP or MSCCXCR5 are trapped in lungs, but there is no significant difference between two groups. Interestingly, compared with the MSCEGFP cells, more MSCCXCR5 distributed in the spleen. The possible reason might be due to CXCL13 expression in second lymphoid organs and the primary influence of MSCCXCR5 on immune cells appears to occur in the spleen (Figure S3B-C). These findings strongly suggest that MSCCXCR5 is more likely than MSCEGFP to migrate to the spleen, particularly the B cell-rich zone.
 

## MSCCXCR5, but not MSCEGFP, was effective in treating secondary infection
We first administered two types of MSCs to CLP mice to evaluate their therapeutic potential in sepsis (Figure S4A). The study showed that both MSCCXCR5 and MSCEGFP exhibited enhanced early survival rates in CLP mice after two days of treatment (Figure S4B), and significantly reduced serum levels of inflammatory factors TNF-α and IL-6, while increasing concentrations of IL-10 (Figure S4C). These findings indicate that both cell types maintain therapeutic effects against early hyperinflammation in sepsis.
Subsequently, S. aureus was injected on day 15 post-modeling, as previously described, into all four groups (Fig.  A). Noticeably, only MSCCXCR5 treatment proved a significant improvement in the survival rate of CLP mice with secondary infection (Fig.  B). Furthermore, following secondary infection, MSCCXCR5 treatment resulted in an increase in serum IgG levels among CLP mice, whereas no significant change was observed in the MSCEGFP treatment group (Fig.  C). Moreover, MSCCXCR5 treatment notably decreased bacterial loads in the blood, abdominal cavity and spleen of CLP mice, showcasing its ability to clear bacteria which unmodified MSCs lack (Fig.  D-G). Finally, we detected the apoptosis of splenic B cells in each group by immunofluorescence staining in 48 h after MSCs treatment. The results showed that a substantial presence of B220+TUNEL+ apoptotic B cells in the spleens of mice after CLP induction, while in comparison to MSCEGFP, MSCCXCR5 displayed superior efficacy in protecting B cells from apoptosis (Fig.  H, I). Overall, it could be concluded that MSCCXCR5 treatment exhibits better preventive effects against secondary infection following sepsis, which is potentially achieved through enhanced preservation of B cells from apoptosis via augmented MSC-B cell interactions.
 

## MSCCXCR5 improves the defect of FO B cells and Tfh cells in CLP mice
Previous research has indicated that CLP leads to the depletion of mononuclear cells, especially B cells, in the spleen through the process of apoptosis [ , , , ]. Subsequently, we analyzed the quantity and function of splenic B cells, and T cells, obtained at 48 h post CLP and investigated the impact of MSCs location on them. A schematic representation of the experimental procedure is shown in Figure S5A. At 48 h post CLP, it was unsurprising to observe a significant decrease in the total numbers of mononuclear cells in the spleen in CLP and CLP + MSCEGFP group when compared to the sham group, however, MSCCXCR5 treatment could maintain these cells (Figure S5B). Specifically, the total number of B cells and T cells in spleen are decreased in CLP and CLP + MSCEGFP group, but not CLP + MSCCXCR5 group compared to sham group (Fig. A, B, S5C). What’s more, we also observed that MSCCXCR5 effectively increased the number of circulating B and T lymphocytes in peripheral blood (Figure S5D, E). To further specifically explore the protective effect of MSCCXCR5 on splenic cells, we detected the subtypes of splenic B and T cells. Mature B cells in spleen can be categorized into two distinct subtypes: follicular (FO, IgMlo/CD21lo) or marginal zone (MZ, IgMhi/CD21hi) B cells. Within the FO B cell population, a subset known as germinal center (GC, CD95+/GL7+) B cells play a crucial role in the formation of germinal centers and the initiation of antibody responses. Based on our findings, it was observed that the quantities of splenic FO and GC B cells in CLP mice were comparatively lower than those of the sham control group at 48-hours post CLP, while the MSCCXCR5 treatment exhibited a significant ability to enhance the population of these mature B cells (Fig. C-F). However, the number of MZ B and plasmablast (PB, CD138+/IgD−) B cells did not change much in the three CLP groups (Figure S5F, G). Total immature splenic B cells (CD93+) and T1 (early emigrant) immature splenic B cells (T1 B, CD93+/IgMhi/CD23−), that finally develop to FO and MZ B cells, were also depleted at 48 h post CLP, while MSCCXCR5 treatment could markedly restored the numbers of these cells (Fig. G-J). Nonetheless, mature B cells in the spleen were responsible for the most portion of the decline in total B cells. More importantly, T follicular helper cells (Tfh, CD3+/CD4+/PD1+/CXCR5+) interact with FO B cells to support an efficient B cell response, the development of germinal center and antibody production [ , – ]. At 48 h following CLP, there were less splenic CD4 Tfh cells compared to the sham controls, and MSCCXCR5 therapy significantly boosted both the percentage and quantity of Tfh cells (Fig. K, L). We also examined the changes in macrophages two days after MSC treatment. Compared to the untreated and MSCEGFP groups, MSCCXCR5 treatment did not change the proportion of splenic M1 and M2 macrophage subsets significantly (Figure S6A, B).
To determine the long-term therapeutic efficacy of MSCCXCR5, we performed the immunofluorescence staining and found that MSCCXCR5 remained detectable for at least 7 days post-transplantation, while only minimal signals were observed in the 10-day group (Figure S7A, B). Furthermore, we analyzed changes in splenic B cells and their subsets across four groups of mice 10 days after CLP. The results demonstrated that the MSCCXCR5 group exhibited increased numbers of B cells, FO B, and GC B compared to the untreated and MSCEGFP groups, which was consistent with our results at 2 days post-treatment (Figure S7C-H). Taken together, MSCCXCR5 significantly prevents the CLP-induced loss of FO B and GC B cells in the spleen at least for 10 days, which may improve the antibody response to infection.

## MSCCXCR5 treatment improves CLP-induced damage of Splenic architecture
Humoral immunity plays an important role in the body’s resistance to secondary infection in the late stage of sepsis, and the intact GC structure is crucial for the development and maturation of B cells and the production of antibodies [ ]. As a result, we explored the change of the GC’s structure following CLP with or without MSC treatment. At first, we observed both MSCEGFP and MSCCXCR5 treated mice gained back the weight they initially lost in 1 week (Figure S8A). Whereas MSCCXCR5 remarkably increased spleen size and weight in mice with CLP (Fig. A, B). Then we detected that scarcely GC forming (as stained with PNA and B220) in the CLP group compared to the sham group (Fig. C). And the MSCCXCR5 treated mice was found to have increases in number of GCs (Fig. C, D) and in the area of the spleen taken up by germinal centers (Fig. E and Figure S8B-D). As a result, MSCCXCR5 not only increases the number of lymphocytes that necessary for antibody production, but also enhances the GC structures and B cell humoral immune response.
 

## MSCCXCR5 treatment increases the antigen-specific B cell response to CLP
As MSCCXCR5 treatment results in overall alterations in the B cell response to CLP (Figs.  and ), to further assess the role of MSCCXCR5 treatment in GC response, we immunized mice with 4-hydroxy-3-nitrophenylacetyl-Keyhole Limpet Hemocyanin (NP-KLH) in alum 2 days after surgery. The experimental approach is illustrated as the Fig.  A. Twenty-one days after immunization, the number of immune cells in the spleens was examined, and compared to sham-operated mice, the quantity of Tfh cells, GC B cells and PB cells in CLP mice was significantly decreased (Fig.  B-D). In addition, MSCCXCR5 treatment could markedly restore these cells in the CLP mice, while MSCEGFP could not (Fig.  B-D). Furthermore, we found that the NP-specific IgG (NP-IgG) concentration in serum was obviously decreased in CLP and MSCEGFP treated mice. However, with MSCCXCR5 treatment, the CLP mice produced even the same levels of NP-IgG as the sham-operated mice (Fig. E). Above all, we demonstrate that MSCCXCR5 therapy could increase the proliferation of FOB cells to form GC structure, which promote the differentiation of GC B cells into long-lived plasma cells and the antigen-specific B cell response to CLP.
 

## Discussion
The growing recognition of late-phase sepsis mortality underscores our incomplete understanding of sepsis pathophysiology. While mesenchymal stromal cells (MSCs) represent a promising therapeutic approach, their efficacy in adoptive cell therapy critically depends on precise tissue targeting post-infusion [ , ]. Our findings demonstrate that CXCR5-mediated homing directs MSCs to splenic B-cell zones, where they potently enhance humoral immunity and significantly improve survival against secondary infections in septic mice (Fig. ). These results establish targeted splenic delivery as a key determinant of MSC therapeutic efficacy in sepsis.
We demonstrate both MSCEGFP and MSCCXCR5 provided significant protection against mortality and organ failure during the acute hyperinflammatory phase of sepsis, underscoring their therapeutic potential through these immunoregulatory mechanisms. Beyond their well-documented capacity to modulate B cell activity and suppress T cell proliferation of MSC [ ], the therapeutic mechanism likely involves MSC-mediated reprogramming of resident and circulating monocytes/macrophages [ ], as evidenced by reduced systemic TNF-α and IL-6 levels, and elevated IL-10 production post-treatment.
Our findings further indicated that non-engineered MSCs failed to prolong the survival time during the immunosuppressive phase in septic mice, which may arise from an insufficient spleen homing function. CXCR5 drives immune cells, especially mature B cells, to secondary lymphoid organs (SLOs) for infection response [ , ]. Since MSCs expressed the low levels of CXCR5 mRNA and cell surface protein expression [ ], we transfected the CXCR5 gene to MSCs via lentivirus infection to target B-cell zones in SLOs. Following this work, we found that MSCCXCR5 accumulated more efficiently than MSCEGFP in B-cell-rich zones, significantly enhancing secondary infection survival and IgG production, and restored antigen-specific humoral immunity in CLP mice, by preserving FO B cells, Tfh cells, and splenic GC structures. The preserved GC structure suggests MSCCXCR5 may recreate the immune synapse between B/Tfh cells, which may mainly rely on the CXCL13-CXCR5 axis, enabling GC initiation, and Tfh-B cell interaction [ ]. Notably, similar GC-rescue strategies have shown translational potential in improving humoral immunity during sepsis-related immunosuppression [ , , ].Our study emphasizes the dual-phase therapeutic efficacy of MSCCXCR5 in sepsis management.
We previously reported that genetically modification of MSC surface with CXCR5 increased MSC homing to sites of inflammation following systemic administration [ ]. It was also reported that mRNA electroporation to engineer MSCs could also achieve the purpose of enhancing the therapeutic effect [ – ]. Compared to DNA-based approaches, mRNA offers a safer alternative for cellular engineering due to its ability to transiently engineer cells without the risk of insertional mutagenesis. Chemically modified mRNA enables precise, dose-controlled temporary protein production while minimizing immune responses, making it particularly valuable for therapeutic applications [ , ]. While our study establishes proof-of-concept that CXCR5-engineered MSCs can enhance splenic B-cell function and improve survival in sepsis models, we recognize that the mechanistic depth remains limited. In particular, inducible CXCR5 overexpression mouse models would provide a more rigorous approach to dissect the causal role of CXCR5 in MSC homing and immune modulation. However, within the scope of this work, we employed short-term chase and transplantation strategies to provide an initial demonstration of targeted MSC localization and functional preservation of B cells. We consider these approaches sufficient for a first-line proof-of-concept, while acknowledging that future studies using inducible genetic models and molecular interrogation will be essential to deepen mechanistic insights. We have noted these limitations explicitly and highlighted them as future directions.
 

## Conclusions
In summary, our findings demonstrate that MSCCXCR5 therapy provides dual-phase protection in sepsis, effectively mitigating both initial hyperinflammatory responses and subsequent immunosuppression-related mortality. By enabling targeted MSC localization to splenic B-cell zones, this approach significantly reduces B-cell apoptosis and enhances humoral immunity against secondary infections. These results establish a novel therapeutic strategy for improving long-term sepsis outcomes through precise immunomodulation of the splenic microenvironment.
