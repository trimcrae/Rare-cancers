## Summary
With the rapid evolution of precision medicine, diagnostic, and therapeutic strategies for lupus nephritis (LN) are progressively shifting towards greater personalisation and accuracy. On the diagnostic front, the discovery and deployment of novel molecular biomarkers, together with the integration of artificial intelligence (AI) and machine learning (ML) technologies, have opened new avenues for delineating disease subtypes and forecasting treatment responses, thereby optimising clinical decision-support systems. In the therapeutic domain, innovations in targeted biologics and the precise application of diverse targeted modalities have shown promise in enhancing efficacy while reducing adverse effects. The triadic synergy of molecular biomarker breakthroughs, iterative advances in intelligent computational tools and pioneering targeted therapies is poised to usher LN in a new era of customised diagnosis and intervention.
Keywords: Lupus nephritis, Precision medicine, Targeted therapy, CAR-T-cell therapy, Pharmacogenomics

## Search strategy and selection criteria
The data for this review were obtained through comprehensive searches of the PubMed, Web of Science, and Cochrane Library databases. The following key terms were utilised in various combinations: “Lupus Nephritis”, “LN”, “Precision Medicine”, “Personalised Medicine”, “Individualised Therapy”, “Biomarkers”, “Artificial Intelligence”, “Machine Learning”, “Deep Learning”, “Clinical Decision Support Systems”, “Targeted Therapy”, “Biologics”, “B-Cell Depletion”, “CAR-T”, “Chimeric Antigen Receptor T Cells”, “PROTAC”, “TPD”, “Proteolysis Targeting Chimaera”, “Protein Degradation Therapy”, “Pharmacogenomics”, “Genetic Polymorphisms”, and “Drug Metabolism”. The inclusion criteria were as follows: articles (original research, reviews, and meta-analyses) that focused on precision medicine approaches for LN or SLE, including biomarker identification, AI applications, and targeted therapeutics; and studies that provided sufficient methodological details and clinical relevance to the field. The exclusion criteria included non-peer-reviewed articles and articles not available in English. In selected cases, peer-reviewed conference abstracts published in indexed journals were included to capture emerging clinical data not yet available in full article form. Additionally, the reference lists of the selected articles were screened to identify further relevant studies. The final reference list was curated based on the originality, clinical relevance, and contribution to advancing LN precision medicine frameworks. The deadline for literature screening was April 15, 2025.

## Introduction
Systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) is a complex multisystem autoimmune disease characterised by the loss of immune tolerance and the production of autoantibodies, with approximately 3.4 million individuals diagnosed with SLE worldwide. Lupus nephritis (LN) is one of the most severe forms of organ damage in patients with SLE. LN affects approximately 60% of patients with SLE globally, with approximately 30% of patients with LN progressing to end-stage renal disease (ESRD), significantly increasing mortality rates and leading to increased long-term treatment costs. 
The pathogenesis of LN is complex and is driven by both systemic factors and local renal inflammation. It primarily arises from immune dysregulation inherent to SLE, whereby genetic susceptibility and environmental factors synergistically lead to impaired nucleic acid clearance. This defect results in the abnormal accumulation of endogenous chromatin and oxidised mitochondrial DNA, among other nucleic acid components, which in turn excessively activate nucleic acid sensors such as TLR7/9 and the cGAS–STING pathway. This overactivation triggers the release of type I interferons and various proinflammatory cytokines. These inflammatory signals perpetuate the continuous activation of autoreactive B and T cells, leading to the production of antinuclear antibodies and the deposition of immune complexes in the glomeruli. Subsequent activation of the complement system and local inflammatory responses cause renal tissue injury and fibrosis, ultimately resulting in progressive renal dysfunction. Throughout this process, the interplay between innate and adaptive immunity, the cytokine storm, and the formation of aberrant immune memory collectively constitute the complex and critical pathogenic mechanisms of LN. , All patients with LN have chronic kidney disease (CKD), and therefore, the treatment of LN must address both immune and nonimmune factors. Traditional treatments, such as glucocorticoids and cyclophosphamide, can protect renal function to some extent, but these treatments have significant side effects. New immunosuppressants, such as mycophenolate mofetil (MMF), have been introduced, but treatment responses vary owing to factors such as ethnicity, sex, and genetics. Even among patients who respond to treatment, a significant likelihood of relapse remains. , 
Since the inception of the field, precision medicine has been one of the core goals of medical developments. With advancements in science and technology, particularly in sequencing technologies, the importance of individual variability in disease treatment has become increasingly prominent. , , Precision medicine encompasses not only the application of genetic sequencing data and deep learning but also phenotypic analysis and the classification of disease subgroups, providing new insights into autoimmune diseases, including LN. 
With the continuous evolution of precision medicine, the diagnostic and therapeutic approaches for LN are gradually shifting from isolated testing to a multidimensional, comprehensive evaluation. Biomarkers derived from blood, urine, and multiomic data not only provide crucial clues for early screening and disease subtyping but also lay the foundation for a deeper understanding of LN pathophysiology. Moreover, through the deep mining and integration of these massive, multidimensional datasets, artificial intelligence (AI) technology can identify subtle alterations that traditional methods might overlook, thereby achieving a more precise diagnosis and risk prediction. Moreover, based on a precise diagnostic framework, targeted therapeutic strategies are anticipated to be tailored to ensure that each patient receives an optimal treatment regimen that corresponds to their molecular characteristics and clinical manifestations. These three interdependent elements interact synergistically to form a closed-loop diagnostic and therapeutic system—from early detection and refined subtyping to individualised intervention—providing patients with LN with an unprecedented integrated pathway for precision medicine ( ). In this article, the latest advancements in the diagnosis and treatment of LN are systematically reviewed from the perspective of precision medicine, with a particular focus on the application of personalised treatment plans and innovative therapies. The aim of this review is to provide new theoretical perspectives and practical strategies for the precise treatment of LN.
 

## Optimised diagnostic strategies for lupus nephritis in the context of precision medicine
With advancements in the precision medicine field, the diagnosis of LN is gradually evolving towards increased accuracy and personalisation. In this context, emerging biomarkers provide essential tools for the early diagnosis of LN and the differentiation of LN subtypes. The aim of biomarker discovery is to assist clinicians in identifying the various clinical subtypes of LN, monitoring disease progression, and predicting patient outcomes. Moreover, the introduction of AI and machine learning (ML) technologies has opened new possibilities for enhancing diagnostic accuracy and personalisation. Supported by multidimensional data and the analysis of big data, these technologies are expected to assist clinicians in making more precise diagnostic decisions and in providing more individualised treatment plans for patients with LN. The continuous improvement of precision diagnostic strategies will not only enhance the efficiency of clinical practice but also offer robust theoretical support for personalised therapy.

## Biomarkers for diagnosing or distinguishing disease subgroups
With the advancement of sequencing technologies, the roles of various indicators, including serum, plasma, urine, and genetic variant loci, have been determined in the diagnosis of LN. High genetic risk scores are significantly associated with an early onset of SLE, exacerbation of organ damage (especially in the kidneys), increased incidence of cardiovascular events, and reduced overall survival rates. Notable gene expression disturbances are present in the peripheral blood of patients with active LN, and gene expression in the urine and renal immune cells of patients with LN is highly correlated, indicating that urinalysis has the potential to serve as a substitute for renal biopsy. 
 summarises potential biomarkers identified in studies enrolling more than 50 patients and highlights the role of each marker in the diagnosis, prognostication and treatment response of patients with LN. We applied a three-tier grading system to assess the strength of the clinical evidence: Grade A (high credibility) includes multicentre investigations with ≥200 patients that have been independently validated and demonstrate consistent sensitivity and specificity across diverse populations; Grade B (moderate–high credibility) covers studies of 100–199 patients (potentially multicentre) with limited external validation, indicating that clinical utility remains to be confirmed; and Grade C (exploratory) refers to single-centre cohorts of 50–99 patients or markers with only preliminary validation, which require further research.
 The levels of anti-double-stranded DNA (dsDNA) antibodies are closely associated with LN activity, particularly during episodes of kidney damage, when their levels typically increase significantly. This association has been incorporated into the Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO) guidelines for diagnosis. Up to 70% of proteins and peptides in urine originate from the kidneys, which are the site of LN; thus, various urine biomarkers are useful for the diagnosis of LN and assessments of disease activity and treatment outcomes. Vascular cell adhesion molecule 1 (VCAM1), CD163, and activated leucocyte cell adhesion molecule (ALCAM) have emerged as promising urinary biomarkers that assist in the diagnosis of LN and have the ability to identify patients with active LN. , , , , , , In addition to traditional testing methods, skin biopsy, as an alternative to renal biopsy, offers a new possibility for LN diagnosis. Single-cell RNA sequencing of skin and renal biopsy samples from patients with LN revealed a correlation between the interferon (IFN) response scores of renal tubular cells and those of keratinocytes, suggesting that skin biopsy findings may provide important information for monitoring LN. 
Despite the promising prospects of some biomarkers in clinical applications, several challenges, and limitations remain. Clinical laboratories typically employ various techniques to measure biomarker levels. Each technique differs in sensitivity, specificity, affinity, and testing protocols, often necessitating the combination of multiple methods to achieve optimal interpretative outcomes. This diversity hampers the development of standardised protocols for biomarker detection, thereby limiting their widespread use in clinical decision-making. Moreover, although some biomarkers have shown potential in small-scale studies, large-scale clinical validation studies are still insufficient. Further prospective research is needed to confirm their efficacy and applicability across diverse populations.

## Artificial intelligence and machine learning in assisted diagnosis

## Artificial intelligence-assisted digital pathology
Renal biopsy has always been crucial for the classification of LN, and repeated renal biopsies can help determine the prognosis of LN. The findings of a second renal biopsy can more accurately predict progression to ESRD. With respect to the analysis of pathological images, traditional histopathological review relies on the experience of pathologists, resulting in considerable interobserver variability, prolonged analysis times, and a limited range of diagnostic parameters. Additionally, potential features related to the treatment response may not be fully recognised by the naked eye. With respect to the global trend towards the digital transformation of pathology, digital pathology shows promise in assisting clinicians with decision-making, improving diagnostic accuracy, and enhancing hospital information systems. In this context, integrating AI and ML into LN pathology research provides an opportunity to advance the digital transformation of LN diagnosis. For example, a study involving 37 patients with nephrotic syndrome demonstrated that applying desorption electrospray ionisation mass spectrometry to renal biopsy samples combined with ML enabled the rapid classification of samples from patients with nephrotic syndrome, thereby facilitating an early LN diagnosis and clinical decision-making. In LN image segmentation evaluation, an improved bee foraging mechanism-based artificial swarm optimisation algorithm (ASMA) enhances the performance of multilevel threshold image segmentation (MLTIS), significantly improving segmentation quality with higher precision and reliability. Additionally, using an improved modified Mucor algorithm (RWGSMA) for multithreshold image segmentation exhibited superior performance compared with existing methods, particularly demonstrating marked advantages in LN image segmentation. Recently, a study employed various deep learning networks to extract features from renal biopsy pathological images with different stains using tertiary lymphoid structures, glomerulosclerosis, interstitial fibrosis, and tubular atrophy as predictive features. An AI model for the early and efficient prediction of the efficacy of induction therapy in patients with LN was constructed and externally validated, showing favourable predictive performance and offering precise decision support for the individualised management of patients with LN. A novel binary rough hypervolume-driven spherical evolutionary algorithm combined with group intelligent sampling (bRGSE) has good performance in distinguishing between proliferative LN (PLN) and membranous LN (MLN). Advancements in these technologies will increase the quality and efficiency of the pathological diagnosis, reshape pathology workflows, and assist physicians in providing more accurate and efficient diagnoses and treatments. However, although deep learning-based approaches for interpreting pathological biopsies can identify subtle pathological changes with high accuracy, these technologies are currently at the laboratory stage or preliminary multicentre validation stage and have not yet received widespread regulatory approval.

## Machine learning for multiomics integration and noninvasive diagnosis
Although renal biopsy remains the gold standard for diagnosing LN, its invasive nature necessitates a careful risk–benefit evaluation. While diagnosing LN through biomarkers undoubtedly provides greater benefits, relying on a single biomarker poses challenges. For example, some patients may exhibit positive anti-dsDNA antibodies with low titres yet remain clinically stable. Therefore, integrating genomics, immunomics, and metabolomics—with the discovery of novel biomarkers—is critical for developing multimodal indices that enable the noninvasive diagnosis and disease classification of LN. In parallel, constructing models based on clinical parameters to predict histological activity in patients with LN can support biopsy decisions and therapeutic strategies. A novel spherical evolutionary algorithm based on a binary rough supervolume combined with grouped intelligent sampling (bRGSE) has shown excellent dimensionality reduction capabilities across 12 high-dimensional datasets and has shown promising performance in distinguishing proliferative LN (PLN) from membranous LN (MLN). A model combining bRGSE with AdaBoost (bRGSE_AdaBoost) achieved an accuracy of 96.687% and a sensitivity of 97.833%, effectively discriminating between PLN and MLN. Furthermore, a model that integrates a support vector machine (SVM) with the hybrid moth–fire algorithm (HMFO) has also yielded favourable results in diagnosing PLN and MLN. In addition, a deep learning model that utilised time series data from 1694 patients—incorporating factors such as anti-dsDNA, haemoglobin, and urine protein levels—effectively predicted LN relapse, holding promise for improving the management of patients with LN. However, despite the relatively large patient cohort, this single-centre retrospective study still requires external validation. A machine learning algorithm trained on whole-blood RNA sequencing data, which incorporates various genes into its model, has accurately distinguished patients with LN from healthy individuals or from patients with SLE without renal involvement. Moreover, a longitudinal clustering analysis of autoantibody data from patients with SLE revealed four distinct clustering patterns that can effectively predict disease activity, organ damage, treatment needs, and the mortality risk. 
Overall, the integration of multiomic data via ML provides new perspectives for the diagnosis of LN, whereas the consolidation of multidimensional biomarker data using AI has the potential to further optimise disease subtyping and improve therapeutic efficacy predictions. However, effectively processing and analysing these high-dimensional data remain challenges. Standardisation and consistency issues between different data sources may lead to decreased model performance. Furthermore, many studies have small sample sizes and lack sufficient clinical validation, which limit the broad clinical application of these models. Before these models can be implemented for risk stratification and treatment decision-making in clinical practice, rigorous standardisation and large-scale validation are necessary.

## Personalised immunotherapy
The traditional treatment options for LN primarily include glucocorticoids combined with cyclophosphamide (CYC) and mycophenolate acid analogues (MPAAs). CYC, an affordable medication, is widely used for the treatment of rapidly progressive glomerulonephritis and during the acute phases of LN. However, the long-term use of CYC may have adverse effects on patient health. Nevertheless, the short-term use of CYC remains effective and cost-efficient in critical situations. MMF, which is included in the MPAA category, is considered to have the potential to replace CYC. Early application of MMF may reduce the incidence of severe flares in patients with newly diagnosed SLE and lower the incidence of LN, although no significant differences have been observed in achieving a lupus low disease activity state (LLDAS) or reducing the severity of organ damage. In stable patients with SLE, the discontinuation of MMF has similar effects as the continuation of maintenance therapy, with no significant disadvantages in the clinical assessment of disease relapse. The use of antimalarial drugs, including hydroxychloroquine (HCQ), is associated with higher complete remission (CR) rates, lower rates of renal relapse, and a reduced risk of progression to renal failure. However, a clinical dilemma exists in HCQ administration: lower doses may lead to suboptimal efficacy, whereas higher doses are associated with an increased risk of retinal toxicity. , , 
Overall, while existing standard treatment regimens are widely applied in clinical practice, the long-term use of immunosuppressants and corticosteroids may lead to severe side effects and may not be effective for all patients. , SGLT-2 inhibitors offer definite nephroprotection by reducing glomerular hyperfiltration and albuminuria and represent a favourable therapeutic approach for patients with LN, particularly where renal and cardiac complications are present. With the rapid development of precision medicine, novel immunotherapies, molecular targeted drugs, and nanomaterial-driven treatment strategies have gradually shown significant application potential. Particularly in the realm of personalised treatment, the introduction of modern immunosuppressive therapies represents a new phase in the personalisation and precision of treatment methods. These new therapeutic strategies not only provide superior treatment outcomes but also aim to reduce adverse reactions, potentially becoming important options for the treatment of LN in the future.

## Targeted biologics
Compared with rheumatoid arthritis and ankylosing spondylitis, targeted biologics for LN and SLE have lagged. However, significant progress has been made in the last decade in targeting B cells, T cells, and inflammatory pathways, providing unprecedented treatment options for patients with LN or SLE ( ).
 

## Biologics targeting B cells and plasma cells
RTX is a CD20 antibody that was initially approved for treating relapsed or refractory low-grade or follicular non-Hodgkin lymphoma and has since been expanded for use in treating various autoimmune diseases, including SLE and LN. Although the data indicate that RTX can reduce proteinuria levels and improve renal activity indices, both the EXPLORER randomised controlled trial—evaluating RTX in patients with SLE—and the LUNAR study—assessing RTX in patients with proliferative LN—failed to meet their primary endpoints. , , The main reasons include the lack of humanisation of the antibody, incomplete clearance of peripheral blood B cells, and inability to deplete long-lived plasma cells substantially. Unlike chimeric RTX, ocrelizumab, and ofatumumab are humanised anti-CD20 monoclonal antibodies. Ocrelizumab binds to the CD20 molecule via its Fab region, while its Fc region mediates immune effector functions to eliminate B cells. Although its phase III clinical trial demonstrated improvements in the renal response rate among patients with LN, the differences were not statistically significant, and the trial was prematurely terminated owing to an increase in adverse events, including infections. Similarly, ofatumumab, which binds to the extracellular epitope of CD20, has been investigated as an alternative for B-cell depletion. Small-scale studies and case reports indicate that ofatumumab is well tolerated and associated with improvements in disease activity markers, , but further randomised controlled trials and large-scale clinical studies are needed to fully evaluate its potential.
Obinutuzumab is the world's first humanised type II anti-CD20 monoclonal antibody. Glycosylation is incorporated into the structure of obinutuzumab to remove fucose from the Fc fragment, thereby increasing its cytotoxicity against B cells and providing a longer duration of action in vivo. The phase II NOBILITY study indicated that the addition of obinutuzumab to standard treatment for LN increased the remission rate in patients with PLN; improved renal function; and did not increase the incidence of serious adverse events, severe infections, or mortality. Currently, the phase III study of obinutuzumab (REGENCY) has met its primary endpoint. The results indicate that combining obinutuzumab with standard LN treatment is significantly more effective than standard treatment alone; patients in the obinutuzumab group exhibited marked improvements in proteinuria and achieved better complete renal remission (CRR) while maintaining lower doses of glucocorticoids. This finding provides new evidence supporting the use of a B-cell depletion strategy and is important for reducing glucocorticoid usage. However, importantly, the obinutuzumab group experienced an increased risk of infections, and the phase III REGENCY study did not document a significant advantage in protecting the eGFR. Future investigations should explore the long-term renoprotective effects of obinutuzumab and conduct direct comparisons with rituximab to optimise its clinical application.
Belimumab is a humanised monoclonal antibody that functions by modulating the binding of BAFF to its receptors, inhibiting the maturation and survival of B cells, and ultimately reducing the number of plasma cells and the production of autoantibodies. When added to RTX and CYC treatment regimens, belimumab further decreases the conversion of immature B cells to naïve B cells and promotes the selective removal of autoreactive B cells. The BLISS-LN trial was a 104-week phase III clinical study that showed a significant improvement in CRR rates among patients with active LN when belimumab was added to standard therapy, as well as reductions in renal-related events and the mortality risk. The efficacy of belimumab combined with standard therapy emerged early in treatment and persisted until the end of the trial, with no new safety risks identified. This study also contributed to the FDA approval of belimumab. Subgroup analyses indicated that belimumab significantly improved renal outcomes in East Asian patients with LN. A post hoc analysis of the BLISS-LN trial further revealed that, compared with the placebo, belimumab improved renal outcomes in both patients with newly diagnosed and relapsing LN, with greater benefits observed in relapsing patients. Although the BLISS-LN trial confirmed the safety and efficacy of belimumab for treating LN, patients who had received dialysis within one year or had an eGFR <30 ml/min/1.73 m2 were excluded. An analysis of data from four phase III clinical trials revealed that low-dose intravenous belimumab combined with antimalarial agents significantly reduced the risk of renal flares, suggesting a potential synergistic effect between these two treatment modalities. Furthermore, a multicentre study based on a U.S. electronic health records database revealed that in patients with non-renal SLE, belimumab was associated with a lower risk of serious infections than oral immunosuppressants, indicating that belimumab may provide a reduced infection risk relative to oral immunosuppressive agents. 
Ianalumab is a human monoclonal antibody with a dual mechanism of action based on its ability to bind to the BAFF receptor and mediate antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity to eliminate B cells expressing the BAFF receptor. This monoclonal antibody has been shown to effectively reduce immune activity in patients with primary Sjögren's syndrome. A phase III trial is currently underway to evaluate ianalumab and MMF as induction therapies for LN types III, IV, and V, with completion expected in 2030 (ClinicalTrials.gov Identifier: NCT05126277). Daratumumab is a monoclonal antibody that targets CD38 and has been proven to effectively clear CD38-expressing plasma cells in the bone marrow. Previous studies have shown that daratumumab successfully treated two patients with refractory SLE, achieving all SLE-related treatment goals and significantly improving their quality of life. A clinical study involving six patients with refractory LN indicated that monotherapy with daratumumab resulted in a significant reduction in proteinuria in five patients, with notable improvements in renal function and no serious adverse events recorded. Daratumumab shows promise as a novel treatment option for refractory LN; however, given the small sample size and nonrandomized study design, its efficacy needs to be confirmed in well-designed clinical trials.
B-cell-targeted therapeutic strategies have been incorporated into the KDIGO guidelines for LN, becoming an important component of clinical practice. Overall, the BLISS-LN trial and other randomised controlled trials have shown that initial triple therapy results in better outcomes than dual therapy does. Clinically, the most effective treatment should be initiated before irreversible nephron loss occurs. The most potent regimen should be applied at the onset of LN to achieve rapid inflammation control, prevent disease relapse, and preserve renal function. Despite the clear efficacy of various B-cell-targeted therapies in improving renal lesions, reducing proteinuria, and slowing the decline in renal function, patient responses to B-cell-targeted treatments remain heterogeneous. Under the framework of precision medicine, biomarker analyses—such as serum BAFF levels, characteristics of circulating B-cell subsets, and autoantibody profiles—can be utilised to predict patient responses to specific B-cell-targeted drugs, thereby enabling more precise individualised treatment. Additionally, the incorporation of artificial intelligence allows for treatment decisions based on multiomic data. For example, machine learning models can integrate gene expression, proteomics, and clinical data to identify patient subgroups that are most likely to benefit from B-cell-targeted therapies.

## Biologics targeting T cells
As research into the roles of regulatory T cells (Tregs), Th17 cells, and other subpopulations in immune responses has progressed, targeting T-cell-related signalling pathways to suppress inflammatory responses has become an important strategy for treating SLE and LN. , Voclosporin is a new CNI that forms a complex with immunophilin, inhibits calcineurin activity and blocks the NFAT pathway, thereby suppressing T-cell activation and the production of inflammatory cytokines, ultimately aiming to increase the efficacy of LN treatment. In the phase III AURORA 1 study targeting adult patients with active LN, with the addition of voclosporin to an MMF and low-dose glucocorticoid regimen, the voclosporin group outperformed the placebo group in terms of an improvement in proteinuria, preservation of renal function, and attainment of clinical remission. As an extension of the AURORA 1 study, the AURORA 2 trial confirmed the long-term efficacy and safety of voclosporin in patients with LN over a three-year treatment period. In terms of efficacy, sustained reductions in the urine protein-to-creatinine ratio (UPCR), increased rates of CRR, and stable renal function all indicate the positive therapeutic effects of voclosporin on LN. Notably, patients in the voclosporin group achieved a CRR earlier during AURORA 1 and maintained stable renal function throughout the three-year period, whereas those in the control group exhibited a slight decline in the eGFR slope, reflecting the natural progression of LN. These findings suggest that early renal remission is associated with long-term renal protection. In terms of safety, voclosporin displayed good tolerability, with adverse event rates and severity within an acceptable range. Collectively, the results from AURORA 2 provide robust long-term data supporting the use of voclosporin in LN treatment. When considered alongside findings from AURORA 1, the data further reinforce the favourable benefit–risk profile of voclosporin in combination with MMF and low-dose corticosteroids as part of standard therapy for LN, providing valuable clinical guidance and revealing the potential to improve long-term outcomes. Nevertheless, limitations remain, and further large-scale and long-term studies are warranted to validate its sustained efficacy and safety.
Abatacept is a fusion protein composed of human cytotoxic T-lymphocyte-associated antigen 4 (CTLA-4) linked to the Fc region of an IgG1 antibody. Abatacept binds to CD80/CD86 ligands on antigen-presenting cells (APCs) through the extracellular domain of CTLA-4, blocking the interaction between CD80/CD86 and CD28 on T cells and thereby inhibiting T-cell activation by blocking costimulatory signals. Abatacept has been used in multiple countries to treat diseases such as rheumatoid arthritis, significantly slowing disease progression and resulting in good safety profiles. Although two phase II clinical trials demonstrated that abatacept combined with standard treatment significantly reduced serological marker and urine protein levels in patients with LN, no significant impact on renal outcomes was observed. , Further evaluation in additional clinical trials is needed to determine the clinical value of abatacept in treating patients with LN.
The interaction between CD40 and its ligand (CD40L) plays a significant role in the pathogenesis of SLE. Ruplizumab and toralizumab are both humanised anti-CD40L monoclonal antibodies; however, studies of both were terminated because of the occurrence of severe thrombotic events. Dapirolizumab pegol (DZP) is a polyethylene glycol-conjugated anti-CD40L Fab fragment monoclonal antibody. Although the primary endpoint was not met in the phase II clinical trial of DZP, the results indicated that the various DZP dosage groups showed improvements in clinical and immunological outcomes compared with the placebo group, with a lower incidence of adverse events. BI 655064 is a human anti-CD40 monoclonal antibody that selectively blocks the CD40–CD40L interaction. A dose‒response relationship for the primary endpoint was not observed in a phase II trial of BI 655064 for the treatment of active LN. However, the findings of the post hoc analysis suggested that the 180 mg dose may have potential efficacy. The incidence of serious infections in the high-dose 240 mg group was greater than that in the other groups. In both the 180 mg and 240 mg dose groups, the levels of activated B-cell subsets decreased. The incidence of anti-drug antibodies (ADAs) was low in this trial, and ADAs did not affect drug exposure levels. 
The CD6–ALCAM pathway mediates T-cell responses related to the pathogenesis of LN. Itolizumab targets the CD6 activation pathway, inhibiting pathogenic T effector cells while preserving Tregs. The results from a recently published study on the efficacy and safety of itolizumab in treating patients with LN revealed that when itolizumab was used in combination with MMF/mycophenolic acid (MPA) and corticosteroids, higher rates of CR and partial remission (PR) were achieved than when it was used with standard treatment methods, with a more rapid and significant reduction in the urine protein-to-creatinine ratio. 

## Biologics target cytokines and the complement cascade
Inflammatory cytokines are core drivers of immune-mediated inflammatory diseases, and therapies targeting these mediators have become a research hotspot. Additionally, the activation of the complement system also triggers inflammatory responses and the deposition of immune complexes, leading to renal tissue damage.
The critical role of type I IFN in the onset and progression of SLE is widely recognised. Anifrolumab, a monoclonal antibody approved in multiple regions, binds with high specificity and affinity to IFNAR1, blocking the activity of type I IFN and subsequently inhibiting downstream inflammatory and immune processes, as well as blocking plasma cell differentiation and normalising peripheral T-cell subsets. In a phase IIb clinical trial, anifrolumab significantly reduced disease activity in patients with moderate to severe SLE, particularly in those with high baseline expression of IFN gene signatures, where the efficacy of anifrolumab was even more pronounced. These findings led to two phase III trials, one of which had positive findings, whereas the other had negative findings. In their 2023 guidelines for the management of SLE, the European Alliance of Associations for Rheumatology (EULAR) recommends anifrolumab as a first-line treatment option for patients with moderate to severe SLE without kidney involvement and as a second-line option for patients with mild SLE, with the highest level of evidence (1a/A). Although anifrolumab has not yet been included as a treatment option for LN, a phase II clinical trial evaluating anifrolumab for the treatment of active LN revealed that the addition of anifrolumab to a standard treatment regimen significantly increased the remission rate in patients with LN and helped reduce the dosage of oral corticosteroids required. 
Secukinumab and ixekizumab are both IL-17 inhibitors that reduce inflammatory responses primarily by targeting IL-17. This mechanism of action has shown significant efficacy in treating immune-mediated diseases such as psoriasis. In the field of LN treatment, two phase III clinical trials (NCT04181762 and NCT05232864) of secukinumab are currently being conducted to evaluate its long-term efficacy, safety, and tolerability in the treatment of LN. Pegcetacoplan is a peptide inhibitor that targets complement C3 and C3b, which can prevent the excessive deposition of C3 and C5 breakdown products in the glomeruli, thereby reducing glomerular inflammation and kidney damage. Preliminary results from a phase II study assessing the safety and bioactivity of pegcetacoplan in patients with glomerular diseases, including LN (NCT03453619), indicate that pegcetacoplan can reduce proteinuria in patients with glomerular diseases and that pegcetacoplan has a favourable safety profile. 
Eculizumab, a complement C5 inhibitor, may have therapeutic value for some patients with severe complement activation-related LN, although most of the evidence was obtained from small case reports. Further clinical research on the specific efficacy and safety of eculizumab is needed to support its use. Ravulizumab, a humanised monoclonal antibody and a second-generation C5 inhibitor derived from eculizumab, is currently being studied in a randomised, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial for the treatment of PLN or IgA nephropathy (NCT04564339). One study revealed the therapeutic potential of ravulizumab in the treatment of IgA nephropathy. Enpatoran is a highly selective and potent dual inhibitor of TLR7/8 that acts upstream to inhibit downstream IL-6 or IFN in a dose-dependent manner, effectively modulating various immune cells and improving treatment outcomes. Phase II clinical trials are currently underway to evaluate the efficacy and safety of oral enpatoran in treating SLE and cutaneous lupus erythematosus (CLE) (NCT05162586 and NCT05540327).

## The role of chimeric antigen receptor T cells in the treatment of lupus nephritis
Chimeric antigen receptor T (CAR-T) cell therapy is a form of gene-engineered treatment in which T cells are harvested from the patient, genetically modified to express chimeric antigen receptors (CARs), expanded ex vivo, and then reinfused into the patient ( ). In patients with SLE or LN, CAR-T-cell therapy targets B cells for profound depletion, thereby resetting the immune system. Currently, CAR-T-cell therapy for the treatment of SLE and LN is mostly limited to small-scale clinical studies. In a phase I open-label clinical trial, most patients with LN showed a normalisation of complement levels three months after CAR-T-cell therapy. At 46 months of follow-up, 10 of the 13 patients exhibited significant improvements in renal function within 90 days of treatment. This study is the first to show that CD19-targeted CAR-T-cell therapy is effective at achieving major clinical responses (MFRs) and depleting pathogenic autoantibodies in patients with LN, with a favourable safety profile.
 In another report, five patients with refractory SLE showed a marked clinical improvement after CAR-T-cell therapy, with no disease relapse observed during long-term follow-up, and all achieved the MFR-free status. Complete B-cell depletion was observed after treatment; although B cells gradually repopulated during follow-up, the returning population was predominantly naive B cells, and no SLE relapse occurred. Only mild side effects were reported during the treatment course. Additionally, a 15-year-old female patient with an SLE Disease Activity Index (SLEDAI) score of 23 achieved remission and improved renal function after receiving CD19-targeted CAR-T-cell therapy. In a study involving two paediatric patients with refractory SLE who received CD19 CAR-T-cell therapy, both 12-year-old girls had baseline SLEDAI-2K scores of 12. After treatment, both patients experienced symptom remission, reduced proteinuria, and decreased SLEDAI-2K scores. Currently, one clinical trial is evaluating the safety and efficacy of CD19-targeted CAR-T cells in paediatric patients with refractory SLE (NCT06222853), whereas another trial is assessing the efficacy of CD19/BCMA CAR-T-cell therapy in both paediatric and adult patients with SLE (NCT05030779).
CD19-targeted CAR-T cells can rapidly and persistently disrupt the B-cell–mediated autoimmune response in patients with SLE. Compared with targeted monoclonal antibodies, a notable advantage of CAR-T-cell therapy is its ability to infiltrate and persist within tissues, thereby effectively eliminating target cells, whereas monoclonal antibodies achieve only incomplete B-cell depletion. Although most studies of CAR-T-cell therapy for LN have involved small sample sizes and single-arm designs, necessitating larger-scale studies and longer follow-up to confirm these findings, the therapeutic outcomes have already shown tremendous potential in treating LN. Furthermore, the quantity of disease-driving B cells in patients with SLE is considerably lower than that observed in patients with B-cell malignancies, suggesting that CAR-T-cell therapy for autoimmune diseases such as lupus may require lower doses. By evaluating the varying B-cell levels in patients, a personalised selection of the number of therapeutic cells could substantially reduce the immune-related side effects associated with CAR-T-cell therapy. However, CAR-T-cell therapy still faces challenges, such as high treatment costs and the resulting economic burden. Moreover, chimeric antigen receptor natural killer (CAR-NK) cell therapy, an allogeneic universal product that is not restricted by major histocompatibility complex compatibility, reduces the high costs associated with autologous CAR-T-cell therapy and has a relatively low side effect profile. Consequently, CAR-NK cell therapy holds promise as a novel therapeutic approach for LN. 

## Targeted protein degradation
Targeted protein degradation (TPD) technologies, represented by proteolysis-targeting chimaeras (PROTACs), have become a research hotspot. The chemical structure of TPD molecules typically comprises three key components: a warhead that binds to the target protein of interest (POI), a ligand that interacts with E3 ubiquitin ligases or cell surface receptors, and a linker connecting these two moieties. Molecules such as PROTACs and molecular glues induce TPD through the ubiquitin‒proteasome pathway, pioneering a novel therapeutic approach. The core of PROTAC technology lies in the use of the intracellular E3 ubiquitin ligase system to degrade target proteins. When the targeted portion of a PROTAC molecule binds to both the target protein and the E3 ligase, it facilitates the addition of ubiquitin tags to the target protein by the E3 ligase ( ). The ubiquitin-tagged target protein is subsequently recognised and degraded by the 26S proteasome. This technology has shown potential for treating LN.
 Research has indicated that Bruton's tyrosine kinase (BTK), which targets the PROTAC degrader L18I, can effectively alleviate symptoms in lupus mice by reducing antibody secretion. Given the multiple side effects associated with ibrutinib due to off-target effects and the emergence of new mutations leading to treatment resistance, L18I may represent a safer and more effective alternative to BTK inhibitors for treating autoimmune diseases. However, it remains in the experimental stage and requires further validation and clinical studies.
Iberdomide, a cereblon E3 ubiquitin ligase immunomodulator, is not a PROTAC molecule in the traditional sense. However, it impacts the pathogenesis of SLE by targeting the degradation of the transcription factors Ikaros and Aiolos ( ). Although not a traditional PROTAC molecule, iberdomide also employs E3 ligase to degrade its target proteins. A 24-week phase II clinical study involving 288 patients (NCT03161483) indicated that the highest dose of iberdomide (0.45 mg) significantly improved the SLE response index-4 (SRI-4) compared with that of the placebo group. An analysis revealed that iberdomide significantly reduced the numbers of B cells and plasmacytoid dendritic cells (pDCs) and autoantibody levels while increasing the number of Tregs and IL-2 levels, with the most pronounced treatment effects observed in patients with high baseline type I IFN gene expression. The 0.45 mg dose showed significant efficacy in patients with subacute and chronic CLE, whereas no significant improvement was noted in patients with acute CLE. Further large-scale studies with longer follow-up periods are still needed to confirm the efficacy and safety of iberdomide in patients with SLE.
Because various E3 ligases are expressed at different times and in different tissues, tissue-specific target protein knockdown can be achieved, thereby improving treatment specificity and safety. Overall, although TPD technologies are currently still in preclinical research, they hold promise for providing new treatment opportunities to patients with LN in the future.

## Pharmacogenomics
Pharmacogenomics (PGx) is an interdisciplinary field studying how genetic polymorphisms affect drug metabolism, drug efficacy, and adverse reactions, and PGx has gradually become an essential component of precision medicine. For patients with LN, the heterogeneity of the disease and the complexity of immune mechanisms lead to significant differences in treatment responses to the same medication among different patients. Therefore, PGx research can provide crucial insights into the genetic basis of individual drug responses and thereby promote precision therapy.
The efficacy of CYC is closely related to genetic variants in the hepatic cytochrome P450 enzyme system. The CYP2B6 enzyme is responsible for converting CYC into the intermediate metabolite 4-hydroxycyclophosphamide, which affects the bioactivation of CYC. Studies have shown that the CYP2B6 c.516G>T polymorphism (rs3745274) is significantly associated with the efficacy and toxicity of CYC in patients with LN. , The therapeutic effect of CYC can be strengthened by increasing the expression of CYP2B6. By assessing the CYP2B6 genotype, patients' responses to CYC can be predicted, thereby facilitating personalised treatment. This phenomenon has also been observed in patients with membranous nephropathy. The pharmacokinetics of MPA are also influenced by genetic polymorphisms. Studies have shown that polymorphisms in genes such as UGT1A9, UGT2B7, and SLCO1B1 significantly affect the metabolism of MPA. , For example, carriers of the UGT1A9 -275T>A polymorphism exhibit significantly lower exposure to MPA than do individuals carrying the wild-type allele. Variants of these genes can assist in personalising MPA dosing to reduce the risk of adverse reactions and increase treatment efficacy.
In summary, advancements in PGx have enabled clinicians to guide drug selection and dosage adjustments based on individual genotypes. By accurately identifying genetic variants related to drug metabolism, safer and more effective treatment options can be provided for patients with LN and SLE, thereby reducing side effects, improving therapeutic outcomes, and promoting the realisation of personalised medicine.

## Innovations in precision drug delivery systems
Nanotechnology has promising applications in various renal pathological conditions. , Given that different kidney diseases affect the renal barrier in distinct ways, adopting tailored strategies to ensure precise drug targeting based on the specific pathophysiological mechanisms is crucial. In the treatment of LN, nanotechnology has a unique scientific rationale and application potential due to its precision in targeting LN, optimised drug delivery, and programmable immunomodulation.
Anti-dsDNA antibodies are considered one of the core factors driving the development of LN pathology. Therefore, blocking the synthesis of anti-dsDNA antibodies has become an important therapeutic direction. In this field, mimetic peptides, particularly the ALW peptide, have shown considerable potential. The ALW peptide can specifically bind to both mouse and human anti-dsDNA antibodies and slow disease progression. , However, these peptides have a short half-life and limited retention time in the kidneys, which restricts their efficacy. Researchers have developed D-ALW nanoparticles (D-ALW NPs) to overcome this issue. These nanodrugs were shown to target anti-dsDNA antibodies and significantly reduce the symptoms of LN in MRL/lpr mice. By increasing renal retention, reducing immune complex deposition, and improving renal histological features, D-ALW NPs prolong the survival of mice. 
The use of immunosuppressants has expanded the therapeutic options for patients with LN; however, their application is often accompanied by significant side effects and uncertainties. The rise of nanotechnology-based targeted delivery systems offers new avenues to optimise both the efficacy and safety of these drugs. For example, traditional cyclosporine A (CsA) therapy, which is limited in patients with SLE due to poor tissue distribution and nephrotoxicity, has been improved by the use of a CD71-targeted P2Ns-GA nanoparticle that enhances the lymphatic delivery efficiency of CsA. This nanoparticle increases the lymphatic distribution of CsA by 4- to 18-fold, thereby significantly alleviating disease symptoms in SLE mouse models and reducing anti-DNA antibody titres, lymphatic inflammation, renal injury, and proteinuria. Furthermore, a nanoparticle formulation of the immunosuppressant tacrolimus has also shown favourable efficacy. Hydrophobically modified polyethylene glycol-coated chitosan (HGC-TAC) nanomicelles effectively mitigate inflammation, fibrosis, and apoptosis in murine LN models, indicating the potential to improve drug delivery efficiency and patient compliance. Concurrently, a dexamethasone prodrug nanoparticle formulation, ZSJ-0228, has effectively been used to treat lupus nephritis in mouse models without notable side effects; compared with traditional therapy, the nanoparticle formulation has substantial advantages. The accumulation of excessive amounts of cell-free DNA (cfDNA) is believed to be closely associated with the pathogenesis of SLE. , In one study, ZSJ-0228, a dexamethasone (DXM) prodrug, was shown to self-assemble into micelles, thereby improving their water solubility. Leveraging a unique renal-targeting mechanism known as ELVIS, ZSJ-0228 significantly improved treatment outcomes in murine models of LN while minimising systemic toxicity. Similarly, a DXM-loaded nanogel designed for cfDNA clearance and equipped with renal-homing capability preferentially accumulated in the inflamed kidneys of lupus-prone mice, enabling both targeted cfDNA removal and localised DXM delivery. This strategy markedly alleviated LN symptoms and highlighted promising avenues for combination therapy. 
By enhancing drug targeting, retention, and stability in the kidneys, nanotechnology provides new solutions for the treatment of SLE and LN, with significant potential to overcome the limitations of current therapeutic approaches. Further research should focus on the safety, efficacy, and long-term stability of nanogels. Currently, nanodrug delivery in LN treatment is primarily at the animal experimental stage. Although renal filtration and immune responses are highly conserved across species, interspecies physiological differences remain a major challenge in translating nanotechnology-based targeted delivery from animal models to human applications. Evaluating the long-term effects and treatment cycle impacts using more complex animal models may better elucidate its clinical potential.

## Outstanding questions
Although the prospects of precision medicine in LN are promising, its application in LN remains in the early stages compared with fields such as oncology and genetic diseases. Owing to the high heterogeneity of LN, the instability of biomarkers, individual variability in targeted therapy, and insufficient research investment, the full potential of precision medicine has not yet been realised.
First, most candidate biomarkers have been identified in small, single-centre cohort studies that lack the external validation needed to ensure consistency across diverse populations, geographic regions, and analytical platforms. Moreover, assays for these markers typically depend on specialised laboratory infrastructure, making the standardisation of results—and thus intercentre data comparability and reproducibility—difficult to achieve. As an approach to address these shortcomings, large-scale, multicentre cohort studies must be undertaken alongside the establishment of unified quality-management frameworks to evaluate biomarker stability and generalisability. In parallel, although deep learning models and other advanced computational techniques have shown excellent performance in both imaging and multiomic data analysis, their “black-box” nature can undermine clinicians' confidence. Research into model interpretability and the development of fully traceable AI diagnostic workflows will be crucial. Finally, the lack of interoperability between electronic health record systems and laboratory platforms, compounded by stringent regulations governing medical data sharing, continues to impede the training and validation of AI tools. The adoption of common data standards and the deployment of secure, privacy-preserving data-sharing technologies are therefore essential.
In the realm of personalised immunotherapy, targeted biologics are beginning to enter larger clinical trials, yet patient-to-patient variability in efficacy and safety remains insufficiently characterised. Cell-based therapies and gene delivery systems specific to LN are even more common than routine use: long-term follow-up is scarce, and potential immunogenicity or off-target effects have not been fully assessed. Robust animal models and prospective nonhuman primate studies will be necessary to optimise dosing regimens and delivery modalities. In addition to these scientific hurdles, the prohibitive cost and limited accessibility of multiomic assays, biologics and advanced cellular therapies pose significant barriers to their adoption in resource-constrained settings. Regulatory and ethical frameworks for AI-enabled diagnostics and novel cell therapies are still evolving, with few clear pathways for approval or ongoing safety monitoring. Moving forwards, coordinated efforts at both the academic and policy levels must aim to ensure that innovations in LN precision medicine remain affordable, accessible and underpinned by rigorous evidence.

## Conclusions
Precision medicine in the LN field is rapidly transforming traditional diagnostic and treatment models, bringing new hope to patients with LN. Through the in-depth exploration of LN diagnostic biomarkers, targeted biologics, innovative drug delivery systems, immunotherapy, pharmacogenomics, and protein-targeted degradation technology, precision medicine has provided us with a comprehensive and multidimensional treatment approach. The tripartite synergy of innovations in molecular biomarkers, iterative advances in intelligent computational tools and breakthroughs in targeted therapies will effectively drive refined patient stratification, multidimensional diagnosis and prognostic evaluations, ultimately realising a closed loop from molecular diagnosis to personalised intervention. In the future, conducting further large-scale studies and clinical trials, as well as developing interpretable multimodal AI decision support systems, will strongly propel the transition of precision medicine for LN from proof-of-concept to clinical practice, ultimately achieving a shift from a one-size-fits-all model to a tailored, personalised approach.
