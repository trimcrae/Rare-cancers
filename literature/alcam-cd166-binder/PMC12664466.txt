## Abstract
Neuroinflammation is characterized by activation of the immune response in the central nervous system (CNS). In Alzheimer's disease (AD), this involves stimulation of glial cells, including microglia and astrocytes, that surround senile plaques and affected neurons. The complement system is a crucial component of the innate immune system, responsible for rapidly eliminating pathogens and dead or dying cells, while also influencing the magnitude and duration of the inflammatory immune response. Moreover, the complement system plays both neuroprotective and neurodestructive roles. In AD, dysregulation of the complement system contributes to excessive microglial phagocytosis of synapses, with such synaptic loss representing the major correlate to cognitive decline in the course of the disease. However, the detailed mechanism for complement activation in AD had remained poorly understood until the discovery that complement factors were aberrantly S-nitrosylated, representing a redox-mediated posttranslational modification that controls the complement cascade. Nitrosative stress, caused by excessive generation of reactive nitrogen species (RNS), including nitric oxide (NO)-related species, had been recognized as a critical factor in the pathogenesis and progression of AD. Recent publications highlighted in this review support the notion that the NO-related species support aberrant S-nitrosylation of complement proteins, leading to pathological activation of the complement system, thus contributing to synaptic loss in AD.
Keywords: Alzheimer's disease, Synapse loss, Neuroinflammation, Complement system, S-Nitrosylation

## Introduction
Alzheimer's disease (AD) is the most common neurodegenerative disorder associated with dementia, marked by the progressive loss of cognition and memory. Currently, over 47 million people live with AD and related dementias (ADRD), which include frontotemporal degeneration (FTD), Lewy body dementia (LBD), vascular dementia (VaD), and multiple etiology dementias (MED). This number is expected to rise to approximately 131 million patients worldwide by 2050 [ , ]. The primary neuropathological characteristics of AD include extracellular deposition of amyloid-β (Aβ) in plaques, and hyperphosphorylated tau protein in intracellular dystrophic neurites and neurofibrillary tangles. Aggregated α-synuclein protein (αSyn) is also often found in human AD brains and not just in LBD brains as previously thought [ ]. Other pathological features of AD include microgliosis, neuroinflammation, and synaptic loss [ ]. Notably, synaptic loss is the best neuropathological correlate to the degree of cognitive decline in AD [ , , ]. Animal models have also shown that synapse dysfunction and loss, rather than neuronal degeneration, correlate most strongly with cognitive decline [ ].
The synapse is a specialized structure for communication between neurons. Synaptic dysfunction is caused by changes in the number, size, or morphology of synapses, as well as alterations in the expression, composition, and localization of synaptic proteins. The disruption of any of these properties, which alters normal synaptic physiology, can lead to widespread neurophysiological dysfunction and contribute to cognitive decline [ , ]. Brain disorders characterized by synaptic dysfunction as a key component of their pathogenesis are referred to as synaptopathies [ , ]. In particular, AD is generally thought to represent a synaptopathy, with this form of damage occurring prior to neuronal loss. Evidence supports the notion that synaptic failure is a critical stage in disease pathogenesis [ , ], but, on the other hand, could be rescued as a therapeutic strategy.
The complement system is a crucial component of innate immunity regulating neuroinflammation, acting as the first line of defense against pathogens and aiding in their elimination, as well as in clearing apoptotic cells and debris [ ]. In the brain, the complement system is vital during development [ ]. Within this context, distinct proteins and pathways of complement are described as essential contributors to the formation, development, migration, and refinement of neurons and particularly their synapses [ , , ]. However, increasing evidence from both animal models and human brains indicates that dysregulated complement activity may be a key factor in the neuroinflammatory response that contributes to neurodegenerative diseases, including AD [ , , , , , ].
At early stages of AD, formation of pathogenic Aβ oligomers occurs due to abnormal cleavage of amyloid precursor protein (APP) by β- and γ-secretases. Along these lines, Aβ monomers are intrinsically disordered and tend to oligomerize and aggregate into Aβ plaques, and genetic mutations in the genes encoding amyloid precursor protein (APP) or presenilin (PSEN)1/2 can promote this process [ ]. Extensive evidence indicates that Aβ aggregates activate microglia, causing them to clear Aβ via phagocytosis and proteolysis [ , , ]. However, when this clearance is ineffective, microglia become chronically activated, further promoting Aβ aggregation and inflammation. Chronically activated microglia release proinflammatory cytokines and toxic products, such as reactive oxygen/nitrogen species (ROS/RNS), which include nitric oxide (NO)-related species. This process amplifies the immune response and contributes to neurotoxicity [ , ]. Critically, complement and microglia have been shown to mediate synaptic loss, including evidence from AD transgenic mouse models with implications for human AD brain [ , ]. Additionally, recent evidence has shown that nitrosative stress, causing aberrant protein S-nitrosylation of complement component 3 (C3), contributes to synapse loss mediated by human induced pluripotent stem cell (hiPSC)-derived microglial-like cells [ ]. This topic is highlighted in the context of AD/ADRD in the current review. We will focus on the relationship between aberrant S-nitrosylation of complement proteins and synapse loss.

## Redox Stress, Protein S-Nitrosylation, and AD
In most mammalian cells, reactive oxygen and nitrogen species (ROS/RNS) are produced at low levels, serving as important signaling molecules in various cellular processes [ ]. However, exposure to environmental toxins, specific genetic mutations, or normal aging can disrupt the balance between ROS/RNS production and cellular defense systems in the brain by altering the expression level or activity of antioxidant enzymes, molecular chaperones, and glutathione. This imbalance can lead to excessive ROS/RNS, including superoxide anion (O2·-) and nitric oxide (NO·), thus causing oxidative and nitrosative stress [ , , ]. Neurons may be more susceptible to this stress due to their high energy needs and compromise of antioxidant defenses with aging than other types of cells [ ]. Increased levels of ROS/RNS can contribute to neuropathological processes such as protein misfolding, metabolic/mitochondrial dysfunction, neuroinflammation, and synaptic injury, and eventually neuronal loss [ , , , ]. In particular, the production of NO-related species can regulate various cell-signaling pathways, which control multiple pathophysiological responses.
While NO is thought of as a short-lived gas that regulates the cardiovascular, immune, and nervous systems in mammalian cells via modulation of guanylyl cyclase activity, it can also have more persistent effects via protein S-nitrosylation (forming SNO-proteins). NO is mainly synthesized by NO synthase (NOS) through the conversion of l-arginine to NO and l-citrulline [ ]. Three isoforms of NOS have been found in the central nervous system: NOS1 (or neuronal NOS [nNOS]), NOS2 (or inducible NOS [iNOS]), and NOS3 (or endothelial NOS [eNOS]). These three isoforms have different activity patterns: (i) nNOS, which localizes predominantly to neuronal synaptic spines, astrocytes and the loose connective tissue surrounding blood vessels in the brain; (ii) iNOS, which is calcium (Ca2+)-independent and not constitutively expressed in astrocytes and microglia but can be induced in response certain pathological conditions such as inflammatory stimuli; and (iii) eNOS, which is present in both cerebral vascular endothelial cells and some neurons [ , , ]. Protein S-nitrosylation represents a covalent chemical reaction wherein NO-related species react with a thiol group (-SH) or, more properly, thiolate anion (-S−) on a target protein cysteine residue, thereby regulating its function ( ). This nitrosation reaction leads to the formation of an S-nitrosothiol (-SNO) adduct on the protein [ , , , ]. The NO group can also be transferred from one protein to another via a transnitrosylation reaction. In this process, the thiolate anion of one protein performs a reversible nucleophilic attack on the nitroso nitrogen of another protein, effectively transferring NO from one protein to another. Transnitrosylation reactions can proceed either enzymatically or non-enzymatically [ , ]. Alternatively, the NO donor can be a low molecular weight (LMW) carrier, such as S-nitrosoglutathione, S-nitrosocysteine, or S-nitroso-coenzyme A (SNO-CoA) rather than a SNO-protein [ , ]. Alternatively, other forms of RNS, i.e., peroxynitrite (ONOO−), can induce disulfide bond formation between vicinal cysteine residues or nitration of tyrosine residues, producing 3-nitrotyrosine, which can contribute to pathology ( ) [ , ].
 Under normal conditions, S-nitrosylation modulates protein function, regulating diverse biological processes to impact cell signaling and function (e.g., protein conformation, activation or inhibition of protein activity, protein binding, protein-protein interactions, protein localization, transcription factor binding to DNA, or ion channel activity) [ , ]. However, under pathological conditions, aberrant S-nitrosylation on specific proteins promotes detrimental cellular processes, supporting protein misfolding/aggregation, endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, synaptic damage, inflammation, and cell death [ , , , , , , , , , ]. Aberrant protein S-nitrosylation reactions have been linked to several neurological disorders, ranging from acute hypoxia-ischemia to chronic neurodegenerative and neurodevelopmental diseases.
Initially, we and colleagues demonstrated a neuroprotective mechanism of S-nitrosylation affecting N-methyl-d-aspartate-type glutamate receptors (NMDARs) by inhibiting their overactivation, thus suppressing excitotoxic neuronal damage [ ] ( ). In neurons, NMDARs are located predominantly at the postsynaptic density, serving as a key molecule regulating synaptic plasticity by allowing calcium ion entry into the cell and interacting with intracellular signaling molecules such as PSD-95 and nNOS. Additionally, S-nitrosylation of PSD-95 inhibits synaptic clustering of receptors [ , , , ]. With glutamate and co-agonist glycine stimulation, synaptic (s)NMDAR-operated ion channels mediate Ca2+ and Na+ ​influx, with Ca2+ leading to nNOS activation and thus elevation of intracellular NO levels. These elevated NO levels serve as a negative-feedback system on NMDARs activity by S-nitrosylating the receptors [ , ]. Under normal physiological conditions, moderate nitric oxide (NO) production regulates normal synaptic signaling pathways, promoting synaptic function and neuroprotection. In contrast, with hyperstimulation of sNMDARs, which occurs in the ischemic brain due to neuronal depolarization, massive influx of Ca2+ promotes overproduction of NO by nNOS as well as other free radicals, e.g., ROS via NADPH oxidase, thus contributing to pathological signaling, cell injury, and death [ , , , , , ].
 Unlike physiological sNMDAR stimulation, extrasynaptic (e)NMDARs are hyperactivated under various pathological conditions to produce even more NO than sNMDARs via activation of nNOS, causing aberrant gene transcription, mitochondrial dysfunction, protein aggregation, and neurodegeneration, thus contributing to neurodegenerative disorders including AD [ , , , , , ]. Taken together, these discoveries suggested that the NMDAR/NO pathway could serve as a therapeutic target for a number of neurodegenerative diseases [ , , ].
Concerning AD, numerous studies have provided strong evidence that S-nitrosylation contributes to the pathogenesis of the disease [ , ]. A recent study reported nearly 1,500 SNO-proteins in postmortem human AD and control brains [ ]. The aberrantly S-nitrosylated proteins in AD brain were involved in various cell-signaling pathways known to play a critical role in neurodegenerative disorders. On the other hand, in addition to the NMDAR, S-nitrosylation of several synaptic proteins plays a dynamic role in normal signal transduction pathways in the CNS. For example, S-nitrosylation of the α-amino-3-hydroxy-5-methyl-4-isoxazolepropionic acid (AMPA) receptor subunit GluA1 (forming SNO-GluA1) increases the receptor's conductance and endocytosis. Additionally, S-nitrosylation of its regulatory proteins (e.g., Stargazin, N-ethylmaleimide-sensitive factor [NSF], or Thorase) plays a complex role in regulating surface expression of AMPA receptors [ , , , ].
In AD brain, aberrant protein S-nitrosylation of the mitochondrial fission GTPase Drp1 promotes mitochondrial fragmentation with bioenergetic compromise and consequent synaptic loss through transnitrosylation networks [ ]. Also, aberrant S-nitrosylation reactions cause metabolic/mitochondrial dysfunction, resulting in energy compromise and adding to synapse loss [ ]. Additionally, SNO-proteins cause dysregulation of the ubiquitin-proteasome system (UPS), autophagy/mitophagy, transcriptional activity, and extracellular matrix pathways; and can lead to increased ER stress and nitrooxidative stress; each of which contributes to synapse loss and neuronal damage. Finally, innate inflammatory pathways in microglia are triggered by aberrant S-nitrosylation, contributing to synapse loss [ ], as described in detail below in this review.

## Neuroinflammation and Synapse Loss
Neuroinflammation is a primary mechanism that helps maintain brain homeostasis, playing a critical role in protecting and restoring synaptic function in response to trauma or infectious disease [ ]. The outcome of the neuroinflammatory response largely depends on the various cell types involved, including neurons, microglial cells, and astrocytes, and the degree of the response. Mild activation of microglia and astrocytes usually provides neuroprotective effects and may alleviate early signs of neurodegeneration. This process begins with the release of chemokines, tumor necrosis factor (TNF), small molecule messengers, pro-inflammatory cytokines (e.g., interleukin (IL)-1β, IL-6, and interferon (IFN)-γ), and ROS/RNS produced by microglia and astrocytes. However, under pathological conditions, when normal resolution mechanisms fail, prolonged activation of the inflammatory response leads to chronic neuroinflammation, which can contribute to neuronal dysfunction and synapse loss—hallmarks of conditions such as AD, Parkinson's disease (PD), and other neurodegenerative disorders [ , ]. Along these lines, some studies have suggested that chronic use of nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) may reduce the risk of AD [ , , , ]. One target of NSAIDs is thought to be microglial inflammation, which affects cytokine production and Aβ uptake and clearance [ ].
Microglia, the resident immune cells of the CNS that are part of the glial system of non-neuronal cells, serve as the first line of defense against pathogens that invade and damage the CNS, and play a role in both innate and adaptive immune responses at a local level. In fact, microglia appear to serve a dual role in maintaining synaptic balance. Under normal physiological conditions, microglia contribute to synaptic pruning, a critical developmental process that eliminates excessive synapses to refine neural circuits [ ]. However, during pathological conditions, microglia become overactivated, releasing a range of pro-inflammatory cytokines [ ]. While these cytokines are crucial for the acute immune responses, their chronic elevation can lead to synaptic dysfunction. Additionally, activated microglia increase the production of complement proteins, such as C1q and C3, which tag synapses for phagocytic elimination, further contributing to synapse loss [ ].
Astrocytes play a crucial role in propagating the inflammatory response by releasing ROS/RNS and various inflammatory molecules, which can disturb synaptic plasticity [ ]. Under inflammatory conditions, astrocytes can undergo a reactive transformation, losing their regular homeostatic function and instead promoting damage, for example, via excitotoxicity due to release of glutamate. This shift in reactive astrocyte function contributes to synapse instability and increases neuronal vulnerability, thus exacerbating the harmful effects of microglial activation [ ], for example, by releasing toxic lipids [ ]. Recent studies suggest that astrocytes also participate in complement-mediated synapse elimination. Reactive astrocytes increase the expression of C3, leading to enhanced synaptic tagging for microglial phagocytosis [ ]. This interaction between astrocytes and microglia intensifies synaptic loss, particularly in the hippocampus and cortex, which are critical areas for learning and memory [ , ].
A key factor linking neuroinflammation to synapse loss is the production of RNS. Along these lines, we have reported that excessive NO-related species foster aberrant protein S-nitrosylation of complement factor C3, resulting in enhanced microglial engulfment and elimination of synapses [ ].
In addition to microglia and astrocytes, signals from the peripheral immune system can contribute to central neuroinflammation and synapse loss. Systemic inflammation, arising from bacterial, viral, and parasite infection or chronic metabolic disorders, can exert effects across the blood-brain barrier (BBB), for example, via infiltration of peripheral cytokines into the CNS [ ]. Such infectious and other insults can result in activation of the complement system in the CNS [ ]. Intriguingly, merging evidence suggests that vaccination against various pathogens (e.g., influenza, pneumococcus, tetanus, diphtheria, herpes zoster, tuberculosis, and typhoid) may be associated with reduced risk for AD and dementia [ , , ]. Nevertheless, vaccination may also activate the immune system to promote protection against pathogens, and these events could conceivably contribute to AD pathology [ ]. Overall, the interaction between systemic and CNS factors can exacerbate neuroinflammation and accelerate synaptic dysfunction, underscoring the need for therapeutic strategies that target both CNS and systemic inflammation. With all of these considerations, targeting neuroinflammatory pathways to preserve synapses represents an important avenue for therapeutic intervention.

## The Complement System in the Pathogenesis of AD
The complement system is an essential component of the innate immune system, comprising over 40 protein components. Complement factors play a vital role in the rapid recognition and elimination of pathogens, apoptotic cells, and debris by facilitating mobilization of adaptive immune responses involving activation of T cells and B cells [ ]. In the CNS, numerous complement proteins are produced locally, regulating nervous system development and physiological processes, thereby affecting neural plasticity. While the effects of the complement system can be either beneficial or detrimental depending on the timing and circumstances, recent studies have focused on the role of complement in AD [ , ]. Along these lines, while known to aid in immune surveillance, complement proteins can also exacerbate neuroinflammation and lead to synapse loss in the context of AD [ ]. The complement system can be activated through three arms: The classical, lectin, and alternative pathways. Each pathway triggers a cascade of events activating enzymes and cleaving proteins, enabling various immune functions to occur.
In the classical pathway ( ), the C1 complex, composed of a recognition subcomponent, C1q, and two serine protease subcomponents, C1r and C1s in a 1:2:2 ratio, binds to the Fc domain of an antibody in immune complexes, or to non-immunoglobulin activators such as apoptotic cells and other polyanionic substances (e.g., fibrillar Aβ or hyperphosphorylated tau) [ , ]. This binding activates C1r, which then cleaves and activates C1s. Activated C1s cleaves C4 into C4a and C4b, with C4b attaching to the surface of the activator to recruit C2. C1s subsequently cleaves C2 into C2a and C2b, forming the C3 convertase (C4b2b) that cleaves C3 into C3a and C3b. C3b provides opsonization signals for phagocytic ingestion and can remain bound to C4b2b, forming the C5 convertase (C4b2b3b) that cleaves C5 into C5a and C5b, facilitating the formation of the membrane attack complex (MAC).
 The lectin pathway ( ) is activated in similar fashion, using mannan-binding lectin (MBL) and related serine proteases (MASP1/2), which are triggered by specific carbohydrates. MASP1/2 cleaves C4 and C2, resulting in the formation of the C3 convertase (C4b2b) and facilitating the subsequent steps in the cascade.
The alternative pathway ( ) is initiated by spontaneous hydrolysis of C3 to C3(H2O), which causes a structural change, allowing complement factors B (CFB) and D (CFD) to not only generate C3 convertase (C3(H2O)Bb), but also to facilitate formation of additional C3 convertase (C3bBb) through the amplification loop of C3 cleavage to C3b [ , ].
Notably, the complement cascade plays a crucial role in the clearance of Aβ oligomers and plaques, probably a beneficial effect early in AD, but subsequently participates in the pathological elimination of synapses, making complement factors key participants in AD progression. Aβ fibrils have been shown to activate both the alternative and classical complement pathways [ , , ]. Complement proteins such as C1q, C3b, C4b, and properdin colocalize with fibrillar Aβ plaques in both human AD and mouse model brains, suggesting in vivo activation of these pathways [ , , , , ]. Recent reports revealed that the classical complement system contributes to synapse loss in the brain during aging and in AD mouse models [ ]. Increased levels of C1q and C3 were found in the brain early in the disease process in AD transgenic mice, tagging the synapses, and leading to decreased synapse density. This loss of synapses was observed prior to the accumulation of amyloid plaques [ , ]. In these studies, synapse elimination occurred through microglial ingestion via the complement receptor, CR3 (the receptor for C3b) [ , ].
Additionally, the presence of the MAC in postmortem human AD brain in areas containing fibrillar plaques and tangles suggests that the complete complement cascade occurs during the progression of AD pathology [ , ]. This also serves as evidence for the generation of C3a and C5a, which leads to the recruitment of microglia to the plaque. Moreover, under these conditions, microglial phagocytic activity is stimulated by C3a and C5a binding to their cognate receptors, thus transducing intracellular signaling pathways that contribute to neuroinflammation and neurotoxicity. C3aR gene expression (encoding the receptor for C3a) is increased in postmortem human AD brain, in some tauopathies, and in mouse models of AD. These data are consistent with the notion that C3a-C3aR signaling may be important for synapse loss in AD [ ]. Additionally, C5aR1 (the receptor for C5a) is mainly expressed on microglia and shows increased expression in the brains of AD mouse models [ ]. Pharmacologic inhibition or genetic ablation of the C5a-C5aR1 interaction in AD mouse models has been shown to rescue cognitive decline, suggesting a detrimental role for C5a in AD [ , ]. Concerning application to humans, despite various pharmacological and antibody therapies targeting complement factors (e.g., C5) that have either recently been approved or are in trials for systemic diseases [ ], it is not yet known whether they are effective in the CNS [ ].

## S-Nitrosylation of Complement Factor C3 Triggers Synapse Loss in AD
In the complement cascade, C3 serves as a central hub, playing a crucial role in the immune response ( ). Recent evidence suggests that C3 is not only involved in pathogen clearance but also in various homeostatic processes, including tissue regeneration, synapse pruning, debris clearance, and control of tumor progression. During normal brain development, microglia engulf synapses to sculpt their connections using the complement system as guide [ , ]. Synaptic pruning by microglia involves the classical complement pathway, employing the same canonical mechanism that is used for eliminating pathogens and dead cells. However, under pathological conditions, such as the AD brain, complement-dependent pathways elicit excessive microglial activation, leading to synaptic loss and cognitive dysfunction [ ]. For example, investigating what triggers complement activation in AD could lead to new therapeutic strategies and targets to slow the progression of the disease.
Emerging evidence suggests that C3 plays a crucial role in AD, particularly in synapse loss and neuroinflammation, both of which contribute to disease progression in human brains and transgenic mouse models of AD. Significantly elevated expression of C3 mRNA and protein is observed in human postmortem AD brains, especially in the hippocampus and cortex, which are areas severely affected by AD pathology [ , ]. C3 expression and/or deposition are particularly prevalent around amyloid plaques in AD mouse brain [ , , , ]. Importantly, C3-deficient AD mice show considerably less synapse loss and improved cognitive performance on learning and memory tasks compared to WT C3 AD mice despite displaying more cerebral Aβ plaques. Overall, the protection of synapses suggests that C3 or C3-mediated activation of the downstream complement pathway plays an important role in the progression of disease in AD transgenic mice [ ].
As alluded to above, the C3 cleavage products, C3a and C3b, bind to microglial complement receptors C3aR and CR3, respectively. Reportedly, in the presence of Aβ plaques or tau pathology, microglial CR3 recognizes C3 and its cleaved forms C3b/iC3b, leading to microglial phagocytosis of synaptic endings, thus resulting in synapse loss [ , , ]. Additionally, C3–C3aR signaling plays a role in the cognitive deficits associated with AD [ ]. While CR3 is expressed only on microglia, C3aR can be found on neurons, astrocytes or microglia, and is involved in mediating pro-inflammatory responses as well as chemotaxis of microglia and astrocytes [ ]. Binding of C3 to neuronal C3aR leads to aberrant neuronal and dendritic morphology, and defects in synaptic plasticity [ ]. Furthermore, C3 binding to microglial C3aR alters the expression of immune networks, mediates neuroinflammation, and modulates amyloid and tau pathology in transgenic mouse models of AD/ADRD [ , ]. Finally, the C3/C3aR-mediated signaling pathway has also been associated with neuron-astrocyte-microglial crosstalk affecting immune function and AD pathology [ , , ].
Nonetheless, the mechanism(s) triggering excessive C3 activation and its consequent effect on synapse loss remains poorly understood. In this context, we and our colleagues recently showed that RNS represent a key regulator of complement activation, including complement component C3, in AD and in other neurodegenerative conditions [ ]. Moreover, accumulating evidence suggests that both RNS and ROS can modulate C3 activity, contributing to neuroinflammation and synaptic dysfunction [ , , ]. In particular, NO-related species play diverse roles in microglial and astrocytic pathways associated with neuroinflammation in various neurodegenerative disorders [ ]. Under physiological conditions, microglia express low levels of iNOS to maintain homeostatic concentrations of NO suitable for cell signaling. However, when exposed to inflammatory stimuli, microglia become activated, inducing overexpression of iNOS and resulting in potentially pathological levels of NO production, e.g., during progression of AD [ ]. The inflammatory response thus contributes to the increase in S-nitrosylated proteins observed in AD brain [ , ].
Recently, our relatively unbiased analysis of the S-nitrosoproteome using the specific SNOTRAP probe coupled with mass spectrometry (MS) led to the discovery that human AD brains contained multiple complement proteins that were aberrantly S-nitrosylated [ ]. Gene Ontology (GO) analysis revealed pathways affected in both male and female human AD brains included “Immune response_Classical complement pathway” and “Complement activation, alternative pathway.” Notably, C3 was found to be one of the most abundantly S-nitrosylated proteins, particularly in female even more than male AD brains compared to controls [ ]. Previously, it was known that women represent nearly two-thirds of AD cases. To account for this fact, it has been postulated that estrogen may offer neuroprotection to young women but, after menopause, the loss of estrogen may exacerbate AD-related pathology [ ]. In line with this idea, estrogen has been shown to suppress iNOS expression and inhibit microglial activation, apparently via activation of the mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) pathway [ , ]. Accordingly, as estrogen falls in older women and iNOS is activated, aberrant S-nitrosylation of C3 (forming SNO–C3) would be expected to occur and thus trigger engulfment of synaptic terminals by microglia. To test this hypothesis, we exposed hiPSC-derived microglia (hiMG) to Aβ oligomers, αSyn protofibril aggregates, or LPS-stimulated inflammation, known to induce the generation of NO and thus foster formation of SNO–C3. We then compared the effect of these inflammatory stimuli in hiMG preincubated in 17β-estradiol to suppress iNOS activation, and found that this biologically active form of estrogen could inhibit formation of SNO–C3 [ ]. Taken together, these findings are consistent with the notion that S-nitrosylated C3 triggers microglial phagocytic activity, thereby mediating excessive synaptic pruning in postmenopausal female AD brain.

## Aberrant S-Nitrosylation of other Complement-Associated Proteins in Human AD brain
In addition to C3, including several proteins associated with the complement system exhibited significantly increased S-nitrosylation in human AD brains compared to controls ( ) [ ]. For example, complement factor H (CFH), recognized as a negative regulator of C3 in the alternative pathway, was found to be the second most-highly increased S-nitrosylated complement protein in human AD brain. Intriguingly, both SNO–C3 and SNO-CFH were also detected in our S-nitrosoproteome MS analysis of human LBD brains compared to controls [ ].
 Polymorphisms and mutations of CFH are associated with several diseases characterized by dysregulation of the complement system, including age-related macular degeneration (AMD), atypical hemolytic uremic syndrome (aHUS), and dense deposit disease (DDD) [ ]. Moreover, decreased levels of CFH have been found in human AD brain and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF), suggesting that a decrement in CFH may serve as a potential biomarker for disease [ , , ]. Another recent study revealed that CFH binds to Aβ and apolipoprotein E (ApoE) isoforms, a protein involved in lipid transport and whose ApoE4 isoform is a prominent risk factor for late-onset AD pathogenesis. This interaction with CFH is reportedly decreased in ApoE4 vs. other ApoE isoforms (ApoE2 or ApoE3), thus relatively promoting formation of toxic Aβ oligomers and their interaction with CR3, leading to increased microglial phagocytosis [ ]. How protein S-nitrosylation of CFH or ApoE2/3 [ , ] might affect this interaction is as yet unknown and remains for future study.
Furthermore, S-nitrosylation of complement C4A, complement C1r subcomponent, and complement component C6 were also significantly elevated in human AD brains, while complement C1s subcomponent and complement component 1q subcomponent binding protein (C1qbp) were found to be S-nitrosylated in human LBD brains compared to controls [ , ]. C1r and C1s are components of the C1 complex, which, along with the recognition protein C1q, initiates the classical pathway. C1 complex can bind to synapses via C1q on the postsynaptic membrane surface [ ], subsequently activating C1r protease, which cleaves and activates C1s protease. Active C1s sequentially cleaves C4 (to C4a and C4b) and C2 (to C2a and C2b), forming C3 convertase (C4b2b) [ ]. Notably, C4 exists in two isotypes, C4A and C4B, and SNO–C4A levels were found to be elevated in human AD brains [ ]. Additionally, C1qbp is a globular head receptor for C1q, and is known to bind to C1q to inhibit its activation. When C1qbp binds to C1q, it prevents assembly of C1r and C1s, thereby inhibiting formation of functional C1 complex [ ]. Although effect of S-nitrosylation on these various complement proteins remains unclear and, hence, the subject of future investigations, our MS S-nitrosoproteomic results implicate S-nitrosylation as a potential critical factor in regulating multiple components of the complement system in AD/ADRD brains.

## Conclusions
We had previously noted that aberrant S-nitrosylation of multiple proteins in neurological diseases can systematically affect and dysregulate entire pathways of mitochondrial dynamics, metabolism, autophagy/mitophagy, transcription, and the integrated stress response (ISR) [ , , , , , , , , , , , ]. Here, we review evidence that the multiple components of the complement cascade are similarly affected by aberrant protein S-nitrosylation in AD/ADRD. Collectively, this emerging evidence strongly suggests that aberrant protein S-nitrosylation may represent a previously unrecognized and concerted series of events that contribute to the pathogenesis of AD/ADRD, and, most importantly, present multiple new potential therapeutic targets to treat these devastating diseases.
In AD/ADRD, synaptic dysfunction and synapse loss closely correlate with cognitive decline and represent crucial, early events in disease progression. The effect of nitrosative stress on neuroinflammation, including dysregulation of the complement system, contributes to this synaptic loss. Increasing evidence highlights the crucial role of S-nitrosylation of complement factor C3, which has emerged as a potential contributor to pathological synaptic pruning in AD. Additionally, aberrant S-nitrosylation of other key complement proteins, such as CFH, C4A, and components of the C1 complex, further implicates this protein modification in the pathogenesis of AD. Targeting neuroinflammatory pathways to preserve synapses is a promising avenue for therapeutic intervention, and future research should investigate strategies to ameliorate complement activity in the face of aberrant S-nitrosylation. Understanding the complex mechanisms of complement factor dysregulation by aberrant S-nitrosylation should advance the field toward innovative therapeutic approaches for disease modification of AD/ADRD.
