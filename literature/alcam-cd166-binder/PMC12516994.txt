Most patients will develop metastatic recurrence. Malignant cells use a combination of changes in cellular adhesion molecules, extracellular matrix remodeling enzymes, and their functions to achieve invasion and metastasis. 
The interaction between T cells and antigen-presenting cells (APCs) is a central event in the induction of adaptive immune responses, involving a series of sequential and complex intercellular communications. Cell adhesion molecules play a critical role in T cell-APC interactions, particularly for maintaining stable contact, which is essential for T cell proliferation and activation. Activated leukocyte cell adhesion molecule (ALCAM; CD166), a member of the immunoglobulin superfamily, mediates both homotypic (ALCAM-ALCAM) and heterotypic (ALCAM-CD6) interactions, significantly affecting T cell function. The bidirectional interaction between ALCAM and CD6 receptor significantly impacts T cell function. ALCAM is widely expressed in normal epithelial cells, cancer cells, and various immune cells, functioning as a mediator in cell-cell and cell-extracellular matrix communications, regulating cancer progression and the formation of an immunosuppressive microenvironment. 
In lung cancer, ALCAM exhibits complex biology. On one hand, miR-483-5 p inhibits ALCAM expression, enhancing the invasive and metastatic abilities of lung adenocarcinoma cells. High ALCAM expression correlates with better prognosis and survival in NSCLC patients ; on the other hand, ALCAM expression is higher in metastatic lesions than primary tumors, and high expression in primary tumors correlates with poor overall survival (OS). This indicates a complex role of ALCAM in lung cancer metastasis and recurrence.
To deeply explore the molecular mechanisms of ALCAM in stage III NSCLC metastatic recurrence, we employed single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) and histo-cytometry to comprehensively analyze patients who underwent surgery for stage III NSCLC and had at least 2 years of follow-up. We found that the gene expression patterns of epithelial cells in recurrence (R) and non-recurrence (NR) patients are significantly different. ALCAM modulates T cell activity by interacting with T cell surface CD6 receptors, exacerbating the immunosuppressive microenvironment, thereby promoting tumor growth and metastasis. Additionally, the transcription factor (TF) MYB regulates ALCAM expression, affecting cell cycle-related pathways and epithelial cell behavior. This study not only elucidates the molecular mechanisms of ALCAM in stage III NSCLC recurrence but also identifies potential therapeutic targets and strategies.

## Methods
Patient characteristics: In a prospective study, we collected tumor tissues from four patients with stage III NSCLC and prepared them as single-cell suspensions. A follow-up was conducted for 2 years, during which single-cell sequencing was performed. Among these patients, metastatic recurrence occurred in two individuals, while the other two remained free of metastatic recurrence until the end of the follow-up. As of the publication of this study, all participants were still alive.
Moreover, to further validate our experimental results, we retrospectively collected the clinical information of all patients who underwent surgical treatment and were pathologically staged as stage III NSCLC at the Affiliated Tumor Hospital of Nanjing Medical University from August 2018 to July 2021. The final follow-up time was August 2023, with the shortest follow-up period being 24 months. Ultimately, we included 27 patients (135 samples) of stage III NSCLC with complete clinical information and tissue slices for research. Written informed consent was obtained from every patient.
scRNA-seq: Following the thawing and revival of the single-cell suspension, the BD Rhapsody system was used to capture single-cell transcriptomic information. Using the limited dilution method, barcoded beads were added into more than 200,000 microwells to capture individual cells. Cells were lysed in the microwells, allowing mRNA molecules to hybridize to the capture reagent on the beads’ barcodes. The beads were then collected into a tube for reverse transcription and ExoI digestion. After cDNA synthesis, each cDNA molecule was tagged at the 5′ end (that is, the 3′ end of a mRNA transcriptome) with a unique molecular identifier and cell barcode to indicate its originating cell. These libraries were subsequently quantified and sequenced during 150 bp paired-end runs using a high-throughput sequencer (Illumina, San Diego, California, USA).
Analysis of scRNA seq data: We used the Seurat R package (V.4.3.0.1) to filter out genes in each cell to remove cells with <200 genes, >25% mitochondrial genes, and genes expressed in <3 cells. Gene expression levels were normalized. The harmony R package (V.1.0.3) was applied to eliminate potential batch effects. Subsequently, principal component analysis (PCA) and uniform manifold approximation projection (UMAP) were performed for dimensionality reduction, with the first ten dimensions used for UMAP reduction. A graph-based clustering method was implemented at a resolution of 0.7. Following this, 2000 highly differentially expressed genes were obtained and the identity of each cell cluster was determined based on marker genes. Expression levels of selected marker genes in each cluster were summarized into UMAP plots. The FindMarkers function in the Seurat package was utilized to identify differentially expressed genes between subgroups, with a criterion of log2 fold change >0.5 and Q value<0.05.
In addition, we also analyzed a total of 10 samples of tumor tissue and normal tissue from the public dataset GSE131907 using the same methods.
Gene regulatory network analysis: We conducted computational analysis using Single-Cell Regulatory Network Inference and Clustering workflow (SCENIC, V.1.3.1, pySCENIC V.0.12.1).
Cell-cell interaction network: To investigate the intercellular interactions, we utilized the CellChat (V.1.6.1) tool for predictive analysis.
Gene score calculation: We employed the CellCycleScoring function in Seurat (V.4.3.0.1) to calculate and visualize cell cycle scores. The genes used for cell cycle scoring are listed in the . Inhibitory-receptor score was summarized at single-cell resolution with the AUCell algorithm from the AUCell package (V.1.22.0).
Gene set variation analysis: FindAllMarkers in Seurat (V.4.3.0.1) was used to identify highly variable genes across cell subsets. Subsequently, the gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) was performed using the clusterProfiler packages. The GSEA analysis was conducted on a functional gene set matrix downloaded from the MSigDB database (http://software.broadinstitute.org/gsea/index.jsp).
Pseudotime trajectory analysis: We used Palantir (V.1.3.2) to identify key TFs that drive lineage fate, simulate the differentiation trajectories of epithelial cell subpopulations, and ultimately determine the subpopulation of cells that initiate metastasis.
Analysis of Bulk RNA seq data: Our bulk RNA analysis used sequencing data from a total of 1068 NSCLC patients, including 81 stage III NSCLC patients, obtained from public databases The Cancer Genome Atlas (https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/) and Gene Expression Omnibus (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo, GSE14814 (based on the GPL96 platform), GSE29013 (based on the GPL570 platform), GSE31210 (based on the GPL570 platform), GSE50081 (based on the GPL570 platform). We performed data preprocessing steps, including normalization, filtering, and batch-effect correction. Survival-related information corresponding to the samples was extracted. Subsequent analyses, including gene correlation and survival analysis, were then conducted. For the correlation analysis, we employed the R package aplot (V.0.2.6) to generate plots. The survival analysis was conducted using the R packages survminer (V.0.4.9).
Online database analysis: To investigate the association between ALCAM gene expression and survival rate in NSCLC, we used the online database developed by Gyorffy et al accessible at (https://kmplot.com/analysis/). Additionally, to explore the potential effects of knocking out the TF MYB on cells, we used the GPSAdb online gene perturbation database developed by Guo et al available at (https://www.gpsadb.com/).
Cell culture: NSCLC cell lines (H460, H1975, A549, and SK-MES-1) were obtained from American Type Culture Collection (Manassas, Virginia, USA). NSCLC cell lines (H460, H1975, A549) were cultured in RPMI 1640 medium (Corning) supplemented with fetal bovine serum (FBS) (10%), L-glutamine (2 mM), penicillin/streptomycin (50 U/mL), sodium pyruvate (1 mM), and sodium bicarbonate (0.075%) at 37°C and 5% CO₂. The SK-MES-1 cell line was cultured in MEM medium (Corning) supplemented with FBS (10%), L-glutamine (2 mM), penicillin/streptomycin (50 U/mL), sodium pyruvate (1 mM), and sodium bicarbonate (0.075%) at 37°C and 5% CO₂.
siRNA transfection: The si-ALCAM, si-MYB, and their negative control were purchased from RiboBio (Guangzhou, China). H1975 and H460 cells were seeded at a density of 5×104 cells in each well of a 6-well plate. siRNA (20 nM) and 4 μL JetPRIME reagent (PolyplusTransfection, Illkirch, France) were mixed with 200 μL buffer and incubated for 10 min at 25°C before being added to each well. 48 hours after the transfection, the cells were collected and performed other experiments. The siRNA sequences are as follows: (1) si-ALCAM_001: GTACGACGATGTACCAGAA; (2) si-ALCAM_002: CTTGGATGGTATACTGTAA; si-MYB_001: GGAAAGTTATTGCCAATTA; si-MYB_002: GACACCCTCTCATCTAGTA.
Co-culture assay: Collect 5 mL whole blood in EDTA-containing tubes, and human mononuclear cells were selected using gradient density centrifugation using Ficoll-Paque Plus (Cytiva). For tumor-reactive T cell preparation, the peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were cultured with plate-bound functional grade CD3/CD28 antibody (#TC0011, Nanjing Faun Biological and Technology), and supplemented with IL2 (#TC0035, Nanjing Faun Biological and Technology), and tumor antigen for 2 days. Then 5×105 tumor-reactive T cells were co-cultured with 1×105 H460 cells into each well of a 12-well plate after transfection as described above. After 2 days, the suspended T cells were collected and subsequently stained with FACS antibodies ( ) for surface staining at 4°C 20 min, then cells were quantified using a flow cytometer (BD Biosciences). Data were analyzed by FlowJo (V.10.4; FlowJo). Each assay was performed in triplicate.
Cell cycle assay: The cells were subjected to Trypsin (Gibco) treatment and subsequently harvested. The cells were washed with ice-cold PBS and then fixed overnight at 4°C in 75% ice-cold ethanol before being stained with propidium iodide (PI) for 30 min at 37°C. The DNA content of the cells was then measured using a BD Biosciences flow cytometer.
Transwell assay: 1×104 cells in serum‐free growth medium were seeded in the upper wells of 24‐well invasion or migration chambers (Corning). The lower side of the chamber contained the growth medium with 10% FBS. After 72 hours of seeding, the lower side cells of the chamber were fixed, stained, and photographed.
Wound healing assay: The cells were inoculated (1×105 cells per well) in 12‐well plates. When the density of cells increased to 90%, a 200 µL pipette tip scratched the cells to make the wound. Images were collected under an inverted microscope at 0 hour and 48 hours.
Colony formation assay: The cells were plated (1000 cells per well) in 12-well plates, and the medium was refreshed every 2 days for 10 days of culture. Colonies were washed gently with PBS, fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 20 min at room temperature, and stained with 0.5% crystal violet and photographed.
RNA isolation and real-time (RT)-quantitative (q)PCR analysis: Total RNA isolation was performed using EZ-10 DNAaway RNA Mini-Preps Kit (Sangon, B618133-0250). A total of 1 µg of RNA was used to synthesize the cDNA according to the HiScript III 1st Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (+gDNA wiper) (Vazyme, Cat#R312). Real-time qPCR was performed with gene-specific primers for GAPDH, MYB, and ALCAM (primers named qPCR F/R in ) using the ChamQ SYBR Color qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme, Q441-03) and analyzed by the Applied Biosystems ViiA 7 Real-time PCR system (Life Technologies, Grand Island, New York, USA).
Protein extraction and immunoblotting: The cells were washed three times with ice-cold PBS, then harvested by centrifugation at 5000 rpm for 5 min, and lysed using the Total Protein Extraction Kit (KeyGEN, Nanjing, China) for 20 min at 4°C. Following lysing, the supernatant was collected by centrifugation at 12,000 g for 10 min at 4°C.
For the immunoblotting assay, the denatured lysates were separated into 10% or 15% SDS-PAGE gels and then transferred to NC membranes (Millipore). Subsequently, the membranes were blocked using 5% non-fat dry milk for 1 hour at room temperature and then incubated with the primary antibodies (in ) overnight at 4°C. The membranes were subjected to standard immunoblotting procedures using HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies, the bands were visualized by the ECL Ultra Western HRP substrate (KeyGEN, Nanjing, China) and images were acquired by Chemiluminescence Gel Imaging System (Bio-rad). The immunoblots were quantified with Image J software and target proteins were standardized with GAPDH as a protein loading control.
Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) and ChIP-qPCR assays: ChIP was performed by the Pierce Agarose ChIP kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The antibody against MYB was incubated with ChIP grade protein A/G Plus Agarose on ice for 1 hour. The digestion products were then incubated with antibody-treated agarose at 4°C overnight. Antibody/chromatin complexes were eluted. Eluted DNA fragments were purified with spin columns.
After reversing cross-linking and DNA purification, immunoprecipitated DNA and DNA from the input (diluted 1:10) were analyzed by qPCR using SYBR green PCR master mix with a ViiA7 real-time thermocycler for 40 cycles. The results were analyzed as relative fold enrichment with respect to the input and calculated based on the CT as 100/2∆CT where ΔCT=CT (IP)–(CT input–log2 dilution factor). Primer sequences used for qPCR analyses are given in .
Animal experiments: For LLC mice metastasis model, the C57BL/6 (6 weeks old) male mice were obtained from GemPharmatech. All animal protocols were approved by the Laboratory Animal Research Center of Jiangsu University. All animals were provided with adequate food and water, subjected to a 12-hour light-dark cycle, and maintained at 22°C with humidity levels between 55% and 70%.
For the metastatic mouse model, the mice were randomly divided into two groups, and the luciferase-labeled LLC-NC or LLC-shALCAM cells were injected into the mice via the tail vein, respectively. Metastasis lesions were evaluated by bioluminescence imaging and monitored every 7 days. After 4–6 weeks, the mice were sacrificed, and their lungs, kidneys, and livers were extracted, weighed, and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for H&E and IHC analyses staining.
Staining for immunohistochemistry and H&E: All experimental animal samples were prepared into 5 µm formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) slices. Following deparaffinization with xylene and rehydration through a series of decreasing concentrations of ethanol, endogenous peroxidase activity was blocked by incubation in 3% H2O2 methanol solution. Antigen retrieval was performed in citrate buffer (10 mM sodium citrate and 0.05% Tween20, pH=6/9) in a dark room. After washing the slices in deionized water and rinsing in PBS, they were blocked with 5% BSA in PBS at room temperature for 30 min and incubated overnight with primary antibodies in a humidified chamber at 4℃, as detailed in . After washing, the slices were incubated with anti-rabbit/mouse IgG monoclonal antibody at room temperature for 1 hour. Staining was conducted using diaminobenzidine, followed by counterstaining with hematoxylin. The immunohistochemical image processing was performed using ImageJ (V.1.54g). For H&E staining, mouse lung tissue slices were stained with H&E for evaluation by a pathologist to determine the presence and perform quantitative analysis of microscopic pulmonary metastatic foci.
Immunofluorescence (IF) staining: For IF imaging, a total of 27 FFPE samples from stage III NSCLC patients were retrospectively enrolled, the paraffin slices (5 µm) of these samples and conducted IF staining were heated in a 60°C oven for 1 hour and deparaffinized with xylene three times, each for 10 min. After hydration, antigen retrieval was performed in sodium citrate buffer solution, then blocked and incubated with antibodies (4°C, overnight), as detailed in ). Subsequent steps involved dehydration, fixation, and mounting.
Images were taken by LEICA STELLARIS8 confocal microscope system. All images were acquired under the same exposure time, magnification, and fluorescence intensity. For quantification, we imaged five random fields under the same slices at a 20× objective and calculated the fluorescence intensity of each cell indicated by Imaris V.9.9.0 software (Oxford Instruments). Colocalization analysis was subsequently performed in ImageJ (V.1.54g); Pearson’s correlation coefficients (Rr) were calculated.
Histo-cytometry analysis: We initially used the surface module of Imaris V.9.9.0 software to extract the fluorescence intensity of cells in the slices. Subsequently, for cell phenotype analysis and quantification similar to flow cytometry, we employed FlowJo (V.10.8.1) software to analyze the cellular fluorescence intensity. To identify appropriate cell populations, five random fields were captured for each slice, and the field with the lowest content of positive cells was used as the threshold for cell population delineation. Finally, the average cell proportion from the five fields was used to represent the cell proportion for each patient for statistical analysis.
Quantification and statistical analysis: Statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism (V.8.4.3), SPSS (V.26.0), and R (V.4.3.1). For comparisons between two groups, we initially assessed the normality of the data using the Shapiro-Wilk test. If the data were normally distributed, we employed the t-test to compare the means of the two groups, followed by Levene’s test to assess variance homogeneity. In cases where variance homogeneity was confirmed, the t-test was continued; otherwise, the Mann-Whitney U non-parametric test was used.
In correlation analysis, the Pearson correlation coefficient was used to evaluate the linear relationship between two continuous variables, with coefficient values ranging from −1 to +1. A value of −1 indicated a perfect negative correlation, +1 a perfect positive correlation, and 0 no linear correlation. When data did not follow a normal distribution or contained outliers, the Spearman rank correlation coefficient was applied for non-parametric analysis.
Survival analysis was performed using the Kaplan-Meier method, with differences assessed by the log-rank test. Statistical significance was defined as *p<0.05, **p<0.01, ***p<0.001, and ****p<0.0001.

## Results

## Cellular composition landscape in the metastatic relapse microenvironment of stage III NSCLC patients
To better understand the tumor microenvironment of stage III NSCLC, we applied scRNA-seq technology to analyze the cellular composition in surgical resection samples from four patients. All patients were followed up for 2 years after surgery, and by the end of the follow-up period, two patients experienced recurrent disease ( ). After quality control and batch effect correction (see Methods section, ( ), a total of 44,950 cells were obtained from the sequencing data, with 28,145 cells from the relapse group (R) and 16,805 cells from the non-relapse group (NR). We performed PCA and uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) algorithms implemented in Seurat software to cluster the cells into seven major cell subtypes, including T cells (CD3E, CD4, CD8A, LEF1, and NKG7), B cells (CD79A and MS4A1), plasma cells (MZB1 and JCHAIN), epithelial cells (EPCAM and KRT19), myeloid cells (CD14, FCGR3A, LYZ, and FCER1A), endothelial cells (PTPRB and FLT1), and fibroblasts (DCN and COL1A1). Among them, T cells constituted the largest proportion in both groups, while epithelial cells showed a significantly higher proportion in the R group compared with the NR group. The markers used are well known ( , ). Specifically, to eliminate the impact of batch effect correction on intergroup differences, we removed the harmony correction from the post-annotation data ( ). Notably, when we removed the Harmony batch correction, the differences in T cell and epithelial cell between the R and NR groups became more evident. This observation suggests a prominent association of these cell populations with recurrence, warranting further investigation into their functional roles.
 

## ALCAM-CD6 axis plays a key role in metastatic relapse of stage III NSCLC
Gene regulatory networks (GRNs) are complex regulatory circuits between chromatin, TFs, and genes, which can elucidate the recombination events that drive differences among cell types, cellular states, disease states, treatment methods, and organisms. To identify key regulatory mechanisms affecting metastatic recurrence in stage III NSCLC patients, we performed comparative analysis of GRNs using SCENIC software on two groups of patients. This process consisted of three parts: (1) identification of potential TF targets based on coexpression modules, (2) TF-motif enrichment analysis and identification of regulators using RcisTarget, and (3) activity scoring using AUCell. After conducting correlation analysis on the regulon activity scores (RAS) of each cell, we found a significantly reduced correlation in RAS among epithelial cells in both groups, indicating significant differences in GRNs of epithelial cells between the R group and NR group ( ).
To further investigate the communication mechanisms between epithelial cells and other cells, we used the CellChat software tool for predictive analysis. We observed a decreased communication between epithelial cells and T cells in the R group compared with the NR group (NR vs R=11 vs 9, ). Moreover, there was a notable absence of cell communication between ALCAM expressed by epithelial cells and CD6 receptors expressed by ligand T cells in the R group ( , , ). Additionally, we also found the presence of the ALCAM-CD6 axis in both normal and tumor tissues ( ), which highlights its significance in the context of its absence in the R group.
ALCAM is expressed in B cells, monocytes/macrophages, plasma cells, and epithelial cells, but its expression level on epithelial cells is significantly reduced in the R group ( ). In contrast, CD6 is primarily expressed in T cells and shows a significant downward trend in the R group ( ). These changes in expression pattern may reflect the reshaping of cellular communication networks and alterations in the functional status of immune cells in the R group. We also observed a positive correlation between ALCAM and CD6 gene expression in bulk-seq data of early (r=0.36, p<0.05) and late stage NSCLC patients (r=0.37, p<0.05, ). Furthermore, we observed an apparent co-localization effect of ALCAM and CD6 in the tissue slices of the NSCLC patients, which confirmed the cell communication relationship between ALCAM and CD6 ( ). PANCK co-localizes with ALCAM, indicating the presence of ALCAM-positive epithelial cells. CD3 co-localizes with CD6, indicating the presence of CD6-positive T cells. Although the correlation between ALCAM and CD6 in the co-localization analysis is weak, we believe this is not due to coexpression within the same cell, but rather results from cell-cell contact between different cell types.

## The ALCAM-CD6 axis reshapes the tumor immune microenvironment in patients with metastatic recurrence
To further investigate the impact of the ALCAM-CD6 axis on the cancer immune microenvironment, we established a co-culture model with PBMCs from 12 healthy volunteers and NSCLC cells H460 ( ). The H460 cells were transfected with negative control or si-ALCAM ( ). We performed flow cytometry-based phenotypic analysis of the immune cells after co-culture. A significant decrease in CD6+ T cells was observed under the condition of co-culturing with si-ALCAM H460 cells (p<0.05, ), indicating that the reduction of ALCAM led to downregulation of the CD6 receptor on T cells. Furthermore, we conducted in-depth annotation of T cell subpopulations and identified nine subtypes, including memory T cells, cytotoxic T cells (CTLs), proliferative T cells, NK cells, T helper 1 cells (Th1), T helper 2 cells (Th2), T helper 17 cells (Th17), follicular helper T cells, and regulatory T cells (Tregs). These cell subtypes belong to CD4+ T cells, CD8+ T cells, and NK cells, respectively. Analysis of the proportions of these T cell subpopulations in the two groups revealed a decreased proportion of CD4+ T cells and NK cells and an increased proportion of CD8+ T cells in the R group ( , ).
 In addition to changes in the number and proportion of T cells and NK cells, we also focused on the functional status of these immune cells. Since the anti-tumor activity of T cells and NK cells is regulated by a series of inhibitory receptors, we calculated the inhibitory receptor scores for CD4+ T cells, CD8+ T cells, and NK cells. We observed a significant increase in the inhibitory receptor scores of CD4+ T cells and CD8+ T cells in the R group (p<0.05, , ), indicating that the immune microenvironment of patients with metastatic relapse is in an immune-suppressive state.
Furthermore, in the co-culture model, we observed that the downregulation of ALCAM on tumor cells decreased the proportion of CD4+ T cells, while increasing the proportion of CD8+ T cells. However, activated CD4+ T cells and CD8+ T cells were both reduced ( and ). We also found varying degrees of positive correlations between the ALCAM and the CD4, CD8B, and HLA-DRA ( ), particularly a significant positive correlation between the expression of ALCAM and the HLA-DRA in stage III NSCLC patients (I–II: r=0.53, III: r=0.7, p<0.05).
Collectively, our findings show that disrupting the ALCAM-CD6 axis via ALCAM knockdown in tumor cells leads to: (1) reduced CD6 expression on T cells, (2) altered proportions of CD4+ T and CD8+ T cell subpopulations, (3) increased inhibitory receptor scores on T cells (suggesting an immunosuppressive state), and (4) a significant reduction in activated T cells. These results, coupled with the strong positive correlation between ALCAM and HLA-DRA expression in stage III NSCLC patients, highlight the critical role of ALCAM-CD6 signaling in modulating T cell differentiation and function, potentially contributing to an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment that may facilitate disease recurrence.

## Downregulation of ALCAM contributes to the malignant phenotype of lung cancer cells
The role of the ALCAM in NSCLC has been controversial, and to elucidate its specific function in metastatic relapse of stage III NSCLC, we first conducted GSVA enrichment analysis on differentially expressed genes based on ALCAM expression levels in epithelial cells (based on scRNA-seq data). The results showed that when ALCAM expression levels were decreased, signaling pathways such as E2F, MYC V1, and G2M were activated, indicating that reduced ALCAM expression may promote epithelial cell proliferation ( ).
 Subsequently, we used the SCENIC method to identify 262 significant regulators ( ). It is worth noting that these 262 regulators were organized into 10 major modules ( ). By analyzing the average activity scores of these modules and mapping them onto UMAP space, we found that in the group experiencing metastatic relapse, epithelial cells and T cells exhibited the most significant differences in module M7 ( and ). Further functional enrichment analysis of the target genes of these regulators revealed that module M7 mainly activated E2F, MYC V1, and G2M signaling pathways ( , ).
Based on the above findings, we performed cell cycle scoring for all cell types annotated by scRNA data. The results showed that both T cells and epithelial cells were predominantly in the G2M phase, with a subset of epithelial cells appearing to be completely in the G2M phase, consistent with our previous enrichment analysis of signaling pathways ( , ).
To validate our analysis, we knock down ALCAM in H460, H1975, A549, and SK-MES-1 cells. The colony formation assay showed that knockdown of ALCAM significantly enhanced tumor cell proliferation ( ). Downregulation of ALCAM also arrested cell cycle at G2/M phase ( , ) and decreased the expression of cyclin-A2 ( ). Collectively, our integrated analysis (GSVA, SCENIC, cell cycle scoring) and experimental validation show that ALCAM downregulation drives cell proliferation and induces G2/M phase arrest.

## Downregulation of ALCAM in tumor cells initiates tumor proliferation and metastasis as initiating cells of dissemination
Further subclustering of the scRNA-seq data annotated epithelial cells revealed nine epithelial cell subgroups ( ). To study the differentiation structure of these cells, we first used force-directed graph (FDG) visualization to clarify the structure. The FDG plot suggested that cType2 and cType3 were in a more primitive stage ( ). Therefore, we conducted pseudotime analysis and found that cType3 represented the initiating cells ( ), with trajectory relationships to other cell clusters shown in . Furthermore, we observed a significant decrease in ALCAM expression in the cType3 cluster of the R group ( ), with top genes including H19, TOP2A, TPX2, and ASPM, all of which are associated with the malignant phenotype of tumor cell metastasis ( ). Enrichment analysis of the expressed genes in cType3 revealed their association with cell cycle and mitosis ( ). This suggests that the cType3 cell subset may serve as the initiating cells of metastasis in NSCLC, with the ability to re-establish tumor growth in distant locations compared with the bulk population of cancer cells. The transwell assay and wound healing assay further validated that knockdown of ALCAM significantly enhanced the migration and invasion ability of tumor cells and regulated migration-related protein expression ( ). These results suggest that in stage III NSCLC, cells with downregulation of ALCAM expression seem to be metastasis-initiating cells, which will lead to metastatic recurrence of tumors.
 

## TF MYB regulates ALCAM expression mediating metastatic relapse in stage III NSCLC
To identify key TFs that regulate ALCAM in epithelial cells of stage III NSCLC, we first selected all TF binding sites (TFBS) within 500 bp upstream and 200 bp downstream of the ALCAM gene, resulting in a total of 520 sites. We then filtered these sites to select TFs with at least 5 motif binding sites, resulting in 141 TFBS. Through R SCENIC analysis on scRNA-seq data, we obtained 262 TFs and intersected them with the TFs regulating ALCAM to obtain 41 TFs ( ). Among these TFs, only two (NFATC3, MYB) were found in M7. To ensure the reliability of the results, we further analyzed the scRNA-seq data using pySCENIC software and obtained a total of 395 inferred regulators, which were organized into 10 major modules ( ). The average activity scores of the modules were mapped onto UMAP space, revealing that epithelial cells and T cells exhibited the most significant differences in module M4 in the group experiencing metastatic relapse. Module M4 contained a total of 24 TFs ( ). Combining the results of R SCENIC analysis, we found that the TF MYB may play a critical role in regulating ALCAM expression in our samples ( , ). This regulatory relationship has not been reported in previous studies. Through analysis of Bulk RNA-seq data, we observed a significant correlation between MYB and ALCAM (I-II: r=0.70; III: r=0.37, p<0.05, ). Functionally, using an online perturbation database, we discovered that silencing MYB activated signaling pathways associated with tumor cell proliferation ( ). Furthermore, we identified the TFBS MA0100.3, which is regulated by MYB for ALCAM ( ). Additionally, through ChIP-qPCR experiments, we found that MYB transcriptionally regulates ALCAM through the binding site MA0100.3 ( ).
 To elucidate the role of MYB in tumor cells, we knock down MYB in H460, H1975, A549, and SK-MES-1 cell lines. The results showed that silencing MYB not only decreased ALCAM expression but also regulated cell cycle-related and migration-related proteins expression, enhanced the proliferative capacity of lung cancer cells, arrested cell cycle at G2/M phase, as well as increased invasion and migration abilities of lung cancer cells ( , ).

## ALCAM silencing promoted intrapulmonary metastasis and induced an inhibitory tumor microenvironment
We first successfully established a stable ALCAM-silenced LLC cell line expressing luciferase fluorescent protein for in vivo imaging and tracking ( ). The mice were divided into two groups, each consisting of 15 mice, and the cell lines were transplanted into C57BL/6 mice via tail vein injection and monitored the tumor growth. The in vivo imaging results showed that ALCAM-silenced LLC cell line formed metastatic foci in the C57BL/6 mice, with significantly higher bioluminescent intensity compared with the control group ( ). After 28 days, six mice accidentally died during anesthesia, and the other mice were sacrificed. Detailed macroscopic and microscopic examinations were conducted on their lungs. Gross observation revealed a significant increase in the number of visible metastatic tumors in the lung of the ALCAM-silenced group compared with the negative-control group ( ). Additionally, subcutaneous and kidney metastatic tumors were also observed in some mice, but there was no significant difference in terms of their number and size between the two groups ( ). Subsequently, the lung and other relevant organs were subjected to H&E staining and immunohistochemical analysis. H&E staining results demonstrated that the number and size of metastatic foci in the lung slices of the ALCAM-silenced group were significantly larger than those in the control group, and the degree of tumor cell infiltration into the surrounding normal tissues was deeper. Immunohistochemical analysis showed effective silencing of ALCAM protein expression in tumor cells ( , ).
 Furthermore, immunohistochemical analysis revealed changes in the tumor microenvironment after ALCAM gene silencing. In comparison to the control group, the ALCAM-silenced group exhibited a significant decrease in the number of CD3+, CD4+, and CD6+ T cells ( ), while there was a significant increase in the number of CD8+ T cells and CDK2+ cells ( ). This indicates that the tumor immune microenvironment is suppressed after ALCAM gene silencing.

## Altered levels of ALCAM are related to the prognosis of patients with stage III NSCLC
In this study, we also retrospectively analyzed 27 cases of surgically treated and pathologically diagnosed as stage III NSCLC patients and followed up on their progress. The median follow-up time was 36 (range 33–40) months using the reverse Kaplan-Meier method. Patient baseline characteristics are shown in . During follow-up, disease recurrence was documented in 16 patients, whereas 11 patients remained recurrence-free at the last visit. 10 patients died of NSCLC-related causes.
To further investigate the prognostic significance of ALCAM expression in patients with stage III NSCLC, we performed eight-color IF staining on patient tissue slices to quantify the expression of CD3, CD4, CD6, CD8, HLA-DR, MYB, and ALCAM ( ). Additionally, using histo-cytometry technology, we extracted fluorescence intensity data from individual cells ( ). The results showed that patients who developed recurrence early in the follow-up period tended to have “immune desert” features, where the number of CD3+, CD4+, CD6+, and CD4+HLA-DR+ T cells is relatively low. In contrast, patients who did not develop recurrence until later in the follow-up period or who did not develop it at all often exhibited “hot tumor” characteristics, rich in various immune cells. Notably, although the number of CD8+ T cells increased, the number of CD8 HLA-DR+ T cells significantly declined ( ), which indicates that these additional CD8+ T cells may lack effective anti-tumor killing functions. These changes in cell quantity are closely related to the survival benefits of patients ( ). Further analysis on the public datasets with 2166 NSCLC patients revealed that high expression levels of ALCAM are positively associated with good prognosis in NSCLC ( ). In addition, tumors with both elevated ALCAM and CD6 had the best prognosis ( ).
 

## Discussion
As early as 2014, some researchers questioned the effectiveness of ALCAM as a biomarker for NSCLC tumorigenesis. The study by Tachezy et al indicated that high ALCAM expression is correlated with a lower lymph node metastasis rate and a smaller tumor volume, which is inconsistent with the definition of tumorigenesis. Additionally, the absence of ALCAM expression significantly impairs the adhesion capability of circulating tumor cells, potentially promoting tumor spread. This is particularly evident in advanced-stage lung cancer. Our research findings are consistent with previous studies, showing that the downregulation of ALCAM enhances the motility and invasiveness of cancer cells. However, ALCAM is more frequently considered a marker associated with cancer stem cells such as CD44 and CD133, representing tumor cells with stem-like properties that are strongly related to enhanced tumor invasion and poor prognosis. In this study, we found that high ALCAM expression is related to a lower risk of recurrence and better outcomes in patients with Stage III NSCLC. As a cell adhesion molecule, the dynamic regulation of cell attachment is crucial for distinguishing normal cells from malignant ones. The disparate results of these studies suggest that the role of ALCAM in regulating the cell attachment process is not yet clear, and the diversity of adhesion mechanisms offers a new perspective on understanding the progression and metastasis of cancer. 
This study not only focuses on the changes in ALCAM itself but also explores its role in forming a suppressive immune microenvironment, which contributes to the recurrence of NSCLC. We discovered that a reduction in ALCAM ligands leads to a decrease in CD6 receptors, thereby limiting the activation of T cells. Moreover, the study by Morosi et al also demonstrated that, in NSCLC, blocking ALCAM expression on cDC1 cells abrogates T-cell activation. Further investigation revealed that disruption of ALCAM-CD6 communication impairs cDC1-CD8+ T cell synapse formation. The ALCAM-CD6 axis plays a role in the early recurrence of NSCLC, which is also influenced by environmental factors. The ALCAM-CD6 axis has long been a focus of attention in the field of autoimmune diseases. In cancer, the function of this axis is like a “see-saw,” where an overactive immune system may exacerbate autoimmune diseases, while a weakened immune system could promote the invasion and spread of cancer. Previous research indicates that after ALCAM binds to its ligand, CD6, it forms a complex composed of connector and effector enzymes that triggers an activating signal cascade or regulates the strength of the signal in the opposite direction. The CD6 receptor is made up of intracellular and extracellular domains, where the extracellular binding domain produces inhibitory signals for T cell activation, while the intracellular domain promotes it. This bifunctionality is mediated by alternative splicing variants that are separate from the T cell receptor mechanism, so they cannot inhibit signal transduction. Through a series of complex mechanisms and interactions that can be regulated during the activation process, CD6 is able to differentially regulate signals in various T cell subsets, and the specificity of these alternatively spliced isoforms depends on the environmental conditions surrounding the cell. However, the precise mechanism by which ALCAM ligands specifically regulate T cell activation remains unclear. Additionally, the combination of ALCAM and CD6 affects the adhesion and migration of T cells. By interacting with ALCAM, CD6 can enhance the close contact between T cells and APCs, improving T cell recognition and activation; this interaction can also promote the migration of T cells, making it easier for them to reach sites of inflammation or infection. The reduction of this interaction could lead to the growth and spread of cancer. 
Based on the GRN analysis in this study, we revealed the regulatory role of the TF MYB on ALCAM expression and identified the corresponding transcriptional regulatory elements. We found that MYB regulates the expression of ALCAM, thereby affecting the proliferation, invasion, and metastasis of NSCLC tumor cells, which eventually manifests as the appearance of distant recurrence foci in clinical practice.
In summary, this study not only enhances our understanding of the role of the ALCAM-CD6 axis in the late-stage transformation and recurrence of NSCLC but also emphasizes their core position in forming the tumor immune microenvironment. Despite the limited sample size, and there is a lack of large-scale, multicenter, prospective clinical trials to fully assess the potential value of ALCAM as a predictor of NSCLC transferable recurrence and prognosis, we used multiple public databases to validate its prognostic value. These discoveries provide a new clinical perspective and therapeutic basis for early identification and intervention in patients with NSCLC who are at risk of developing distant recurrences. We look forward to future research that will use larger samples and more advanced technology to validate the predictive significance of the ALCAM-CD6 axis and develop new diagnostic and treatment strategies based on this axis.
