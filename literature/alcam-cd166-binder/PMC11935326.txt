## Abstract

## Background
Left ventricular assist device (LVAD) implantation improves survival in advanced heart failure. Despite this, angiodysplastic bleeding complications remain a significant driver of costs as well as morbidity. Mechanisms implicated in post-LVAD implant bleeding include the dysregulation of angiogenic factors seen in this population. The present pilot study evaluates the safety of umbilical cord lining stem cells (ULSCs) in LVAD recipients while exploring any early evidence of efficacy to improve bleeding.

## Methods
In a 3 + 3 design, 9 patients received an intravenous (IV) infusion of ULSCs at escalating doses. The primary endpoint was safety and tolerability, secondary exploratory outcomes included antibodies against hemoglobin to quantify the amount of blood in stool without the need for dietary restriction.

## Results
The primary safety and tolerability outcomes were met as no infusion-related adverse events or toxic responses were observed. There was no sensitization after administration of ULSCs as assessed by panel reactive antibody. An increase in angiopoietin-1 levels and a decrease in angiopoietin-2 levels from baseline to 30 days were observed in 4 patients. Quantitative Faecal Immunochemical Test suggested a decrease in the mean blood content of stool from baseline to 30 days.

## Conclusions
In this first-ever IV administration of ULSCs in LVAD patients, infusion was noted to be safe and tolerable and did not cause immune sensitization. Half of the patients were noted to have angiogenic stabilization, and there was a trend toward decreasing amounts of blood noted in the stool, suggesting an early signal of efficacy.

Keywords: left ventricular assist device, LVAD, bleeding, stem cells, angiodysplasia

## Background
Left ventricular assist device (LVAD) implantation has continued to provide advanced heart failure patients with tremendous gains in survival and quality of life. The most recent Interagency Registry for Mechanical Assisted Circulatory Support's annual report highlights these improvements in survival over successive eras of implantation, with “all-comer” 5-year survival after LVAD implantation now greater than 50%. Although adverse event profiles have improved, particularly with the use of newer generation devices, there remains a high burden of complications from long-term durable mechanical circulatory support. Gastrointestinal bleeding (GIB) is one such challenging complication. While 1-year freedom from GIB has improved, nearly 1 in 5 patients supported with an LVAD will experience a GIB within 1 year of implantation. This often leads to hospitalizations and invasive procedures, placing significant tolls on the health care system, and increasing the cost of care for LVAD patients. , , Therefore, in addition to continued efforts to define more biocompatible LVADs, medical therapy and interventions directed toward reducing the incidence and consequence of LVAD-associated GIB remain a key priority.
While the pathophysiology of GIB in LVAD patients is not completely understood, it is hypothesized to include both platelet dysfunction and the acquired von Willebrand syndrome noted in this population, coupled with angiodysplasia manifesting itself as arteriovenous malformations which develop throughout the body. This has been postulated to be related to systemic inflammation and abnormal angiogenesis created by dysregulation of angiogenic factors, specifically angiopoietin-1 and angiopoietin-2 (Ang-1 and Ang-2) ( ). Ang-1 is a protein synthesized by perivascular cells and binds as an agonist to the receptor tyrosine kinase Tie-2 on the surface of endothelial cells, resulting in intracellular signaling that promotes vascular maturity and stability, and supports normal endothelial survival in conjunction with vascular endothelial factor. Ang-2 is synthesized by endothelial cells and following release, it is available as an autocrine factor to bind Tie-2 cell surface receptors in a competitive fashion with Ang-1, antagonizing Ang-1 signaling and promoting endothelial barrier permeability. Such dysregulation in the physiological balance of Ang-1/Ang-2 levels has been shown to lead to vascular destabilization, inflammation, vascular malformations, and mucosal angiodysplasia in multiple in models. , , More recently, alterations in the Ang-1/Ang-2 pathway have been correlated with nonsurgical bleeding in the LVAD population. 
 Human umbilical cord–derived mesenchymal stem cells, derived from the matrix or lining of the umbilical cord, known as umbilical cord–derived mesenchymal stem cells or umbilical cord lining stem cells (UCLSCs), potently promote vascular stability and reduce disruption of endothelial barrier function in multiple preclinical models, frequently resulting in amelioration of hemorrhagic tendency. Animal model data have documented that administration of UCLSCs leads to decreased circulating Ang-2 levels while increasing both the level of circulating Ang-1 and the expression of Tie2 cell-surface receptor, which combine to provide vascular stability as well as improved functional outcomes. , While these effects can be seen across mesenchymal stem cells, in-vitro data suggest the umbilical cord–derived cells are superior to bone marrow–derived cells as it relates to cardiac differentiation potential, migration, and paracrine profile effects. Additionally, the potency of bone marrow–derived cells can be diminished by donor age and presence of comorbidities, introducing significant variability. This potential superiority, coupled with the benefit that umbilical cord–derived cells do not require an invasive harvesting procedure and represent an available “off-the-shelf” product, lead to the selection of UCLSCs for this investigation. Human studies suggest mechanisms for vascular improvement include a transfer of Ang-1 mRNA to the damaged endothelium, resulting in an ongoing increase in Ang-1 secretion. Ang-1 has also been demonstrated to prevent bleeding complications associated with anticoagulation in multiple preclinical models by augmenting vascular stability and preventing hemorrhage despite a persistent anticoagulant state. These mechanistic findings are supported by prior clinical data that stem cell therapy at the time of LVAD implantation significantly decreased both the rate of major GIB events, as well as its overall cumulative incidence. , 
Given the mechanistic and clinical suggestions of the role of stem cell therapy in angiogenic regulation, we sought to investigate the use of intravenous administration as an outpatient treatment. Specifically, the uSTOP LVAD BLEED (Utilization of umbilical cord lining Stem cells TO Prevent LVAD-associated angiodysplastic BLEEDing) pilot study was designed to evaluate the safety and tolerability of escalating doses of umbilical cord lining stem cells (ULSCs) in LVAD recipients to ameliorate the dysregulation of angiogenic factors seen in this population.

## Methods
The rationale and design of the uSTOP LVAD BLEED pilot study have been detailed previously. Briefly, this was a Phase Ia, single ascending dose, first in human, open-label trial. Outpatients ≥18 years of age supported with a HeartMate 3 LVAD (Abbott, Inc), who had been on a stable medical regimen for at least 2 weeks without adjustments and inclusive of angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors, angiotensin receptor blockers, or angiotensin receptor/neprilysin inhibitors, were eligible for enrollment. Key exclusion criteria included durable biventricular support, presence of any inflammatory bowel disease, prior heart transplant recipients or current use of immunosuppression, any known hypo- or hyper-coagulable state, and inability to take coumadin. All patients were on aspirin 81 mg and coumadin to maintain an INR of 2 to 3 per clinical protocols. Complete inclusion and exclusion criteria are summarized in .
 ULSCs were a kind donation (RESTEM, Corona, CA). Briefly, umbilical cords were donated under an informed consent and, after careful screening of the donor, were processed under current good manufacturing practice regulations. Umbilical cords were culture expanded as previously described up to passage 4 and cryopreserved prior to infusion. ULSCs were characterized based on the presence of cell surface markers CD90, CD73, CD44, CD105, CD166, and STRO-1 while being negative or having negligible amounts of CD45, CD34, CD-19, and HLA-DR.
Patients were enrolled in a 3 + 3 dose escalation design. The first 3 patients were administered 50 × 106 ULSCs and monitored for adverse events or toxicities, immediately following dosing, and again at 7 and 30 days. After review of safety data, the data safety monitoring board provided permission for dose escalation to 100 × 106 and subsequently 200 × 106 ULSCs ( ). In addition to safety and tolerability, immune sensitization as a result of ULSC infusion was assessed via panel reactive antibody testing at baseline, 30 and 90 days. Study product was reconstituted in HTS-FRS (Biolife Solutions) in a volume of 250 ml of 0.9% sodium chloride solution and infused over 45 minutes. Patients were monitored for 4 hours in an ambulatory clinic setting and monitored for any evidence of infusion reaction or dose-limiting toxicity. Patients were reassessed via telephone at 3 days and returned for in-person assessments at 7 and 30 days.
 Exploratory secondary outcomes included angiopoietin-1 and angiopoietin-2 levels measured at baseline, 30, and 90 days. A panel of 65 other cytokines, inflammatory markers, and mediators of angiogenesis were also assessed at 30 days ( ). Additionally, blood content of stool was assessed via Quantitative Faecal Immunochemical Test (qFIT) analysis at baseline, 30, and 90 days.
The study protocol was approved by the University of Florida institutional review board, all patients provided written informed consent, and an investigational new drug application was obtained from the Food and Drug Administration. Potential risks of study participation disclosed to patients included infusion and allergic reactions, as well as the risk of immune sensitization and the potential for tumorigenicity. The study was registered on ClinicalTrials.gov Identifier: NCT04811261.

## Results
A total of 9 patients received ULSCs. As no infusion-related adverse events, toxic responses, or other safety concerns were identified, the first cohort of 3 patients received 50 × 106 ULSCs, with the subsequent cohorts of 3 patients receiving 100 × 106 ULSCs and 200 × 106 ULSCs, respectively. The median age was 66 years (mean 65.2, SD 4.6); 44.4% of patients were Black and 22.2% were women. All patients were supported with an HM3 LVAD with a median duration of support at time of cell therapy of 673 days (mean 858.7, SD 628.9). Complete baseline demographics are summarized in .
 The primary safety and tolerability outcomes were met. Specifically, no fever, pain at infusion site, evidence of allergic reaction, dyspnea or hypoxia during infusion were noted and no patient required a cessation of the infusion. All safety data were adjudicated by an independent data safety monitoring board, which noted no safety concerns at any time point during follow-up. No patients had evidence of thromboembolic events during the study period, and there was no change in lactate dehydrogenase levels from baseline to 30 days (246.3 iU/liter vs 242.6 iU/liter). No patients required a blood transfusion during the study period. Additionally, there was no sensitization after administration of stem cells as assessed by panel reactive antibody ( ).
 Data for exploratory endpoints are only available in 8 patients as patient 9 withdrew consent during the follow-up period. Four patients were noted to have an increase in angiopoietin-1 levels from baseline to 30 days ( ). Similarly, 4 patients were noted to have a decrease in angiopoietin-2 levels from baseline to 30 days ( ). qFIT suggested a decrease in the mean and median amount of blood in stool from baseline to 30 days, with this change persisting to 90 days post ULSC infusion ( ). Changes from baseline to 30 days in the levels of other cytokines, inflammatory markers, and mediators of angiogenesis included increases in hepatocyte growth factor, epidermal growth factor, IL-9, and IL-10 ( ).
 

## Discussion
To the best of our knowledge, this is the first-ever IV administration of ULSCs in LVAD patients, and infusion was noted to be safe and tolerable in an ambulatory setting with short-term observation. Furthermore, the cell-based therapy did not cause immune sensitization. Half of all patients were noted to have angiogenic stabilization, and there was a trend toward decreasing amounts of blood detected in the stool, suggesting an early signal of efficacy.
The safety demonstrated in the present investigation builds upon earlier data in the growing heart failure population. Two previous Phase IIa trials demonstrated the safety of IV infusions of stem cells with a similar dose range. , We have extended this safety profile by documenting the tolerability of “off-the-shelf” cell therapy in the LVAD population, in an ambulatory setting and with an abbreviated observation period. Such advances are crucial, should cell-based therapies be scaled to meet the growing needs of advanced heart failure patients. Furthermore, our study confirms previous findings that stem cell therapy is not associated with allosensitization, an important consideration for future heart transplant candidates. It should be noted that our data demonstrate short-term safety, and while there have been no findings associated with an increased likelihood of malignancy to date, we cannot comment on longer-term tumorigenicity. Previous studies evaluating umbilical cord-derived stem cells in patients with various cardiovascular diseases, however, have not shown an association with increased tumorigenicity after stem cell therapy with reassuring safety data to 1 year. , , 
An additional advancement of the present study is the IV delivery method in LVAD recipients. Previous reports of stem cell therapy in the LVAD population relied on intramyocardial injections at the time of implantation rendering repeat dosing challenging and at higher risk of complication. That this prior data were obtained in a prior generation axial-flow device, with a negative primary endpoint of LV recovery, and via an invasive delivery platform, significantly diminished the unexpected findings of a 76% reduction in the rate of major GIB events, and a decrease in its cumulative incidence of 48%. Nevertheless, that was confirmation of earlier pilot data suggesting a longer time to first hospitalization due to major GIB. These data led the FDA to grant orphan drug designation to the cell therapy product utilized in the cite trial. Our data was built upon these findings in a contemporary LVAD population, with a reproducible and feasible delivery method.
Our findings of improvements in Ang-1 and Ang-2 patients in a subset of patients are intriguing and merits further investigation. Furthermore, that quantitative measurements of the blood content in stool appeared to be reduced after stem cell therapy is very compelling. This potentially furthers our mechanistic understanding of vascular stability and bleeding in LVAD patients, a pathophysiology whose unlocking is required in order to provide more thoughtful approaches to preventing GIB in this population. The most recent consensus statement on best practices for durable mechanical support was largely unable to provide meaningful interventions or strategies related to GIB due to this deficiency, and therefore continued investigation should be prioritized. 
In any such future exploration, the timing of cell therapy in relation to both implantation as well as degree of underlying pre-existing inflammation and angiogenic dysregulation should be evaluated. Specifically, as Interagency Registry for Mechanical Assisted Circulatory Support demonstrates a significantly higher event rate in GIB within the first 3 months of LVAD implantation vs all subsequent time points (0.558 vs 0.18 events per patient year) earlier investigation and therapy may yield better informative and more clinically relevant results. Additionally, in the Multi-Ethnic Study of Atherosclerosis, a prospective cohort study of individuals free of recognized cardiovascular disease, Ang-2 was associated with incident heart failure or death among adults without baseline cardiovascular disease, and associated with disease severity in individuals with existing heart failure. Similar relationships have been observed in heart failure with reduced ejection fraction specific populations and have further been associated with angiodysplasia and GIB in heart failure with reduced ejection fraction. This may suggest that the degree of inflammation and resultant angiogenic dysregulation pre-LVAD implantation may be variable from patient to patient and potentially influence postimplantation bleeding complications. In this context, we propose a Phase II clinical trial in which Ang-1, Ang-2, qFIT, inflammatory cytokines, and other markers of angiogenesis would be obtained preimplantation and frequently within the first 3 months post-LVAD, with a cohort of patients receiving cell therapy prior to LVAD implantation and a second cohort receiving cell therapy postimplantation. While our study has key strengths (e.g., first in humans, off-the-shelf administration, etc), several limitations deserve mention. Chief among them, the small sample size. While both the size and 3 + 3 design of this investigation were appropriate for a Phase Ia study with a primary safety outcome, it renders the interpretation of exploratory findings challenging. Additionally, while the protocol stipulated the continued use of angiotensin-II antagonists, doses of these medications that impact Ang-1 and Ang-2 were not standardized. Additionally, clinically indicated dosage adjustments for the management of blood pressure, an important contributor to LVAD complications, were not accounted for. Similarly, use of other medications that can affect the Ang-1 and Ang-2 pathway or markers of systemic inflammation, such as digoxin and fish oil, may have confounded the results. Furthermore, aortic valve opening time, degree of native LV function, and pump parameters are potential confounders that cannot be accounted for.

## Conclusion
The findings of the uSTOP LVAD BLEED pilot study confirm the safety and tolerability of IV ULSCs in LVAD patients. The exploratory endpoints are suggestive of a signal of early efficacy which should be explored in a larger, late-phase study with appropriate power to detect a change in clinical outcomes.
