## Abstract
The skin is one of the earliest organs in the human body to exhibit signs of aging, with photoaging mainly caused by chronic ultraviolet (UV) exposure and recognized as a major form of extrinsic aging. Small extracellular vesicles derived from human umbilical cord mesenchymal stem cells (hucMSC-sEVs) have been shown to delay skin aging by upregulating pregnancy zone protein (PZP), which modulates inflammatory responses, oxidative stress, and extracellular matrix remodeling. However, the core regulatory network underlying these effects remains unclear. Focusing on PZP, this study integrated bioinformatics to identify GATA2 as a potential upstream transcriptional regulator and GRP75 as a possible downstream target, indicating a GATA2/PZP/GRP75 signaling axis that may regulate photoaging. ChIP assays and dual-luciferase reporter analyses confirmed that GATA2 binds to the promoter region of PZP and upregulates its transcription. Knockdown and overexpression experiments further demonstrated that GATA2 suppresses or promotes PZP expression, thereby significantly influencing the senescence phenotype of dermal fibroblasts under UV irradiation. In addition, protein docking, co-immunoprecipitation (CoIP), and immunofluorescence colocalization assays validated the interaction between PZP and GRP75. PZP alleviates mitochondrial calcium overload and dysfunction by inhibiting the abnormal elevation of GRP75, thus delaying photoaging. These findings offer novel insights and targets for skin-aging intervention.

## Introduction
Aging is an irreversible biological process in humans, with the skin being one of the most visibly affected organs. It is also among the first organs to exhibit signs of aging [ ]. Among various intrinsic and extrinsic factors, ultraviolet (UV) radiation-induced photoaging is recognized as the primary cause of extrinsic skin aging. Clinically, it is characterized by deepened wrinkles, roughness, dryness with scaling, hyperpigmentation, and an increased risk of malignancies such as actinic keratosis and invasive squamous cell carcinoma [ , ]. The senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP) generated by aged skin can also affect other tissues and organs in the body, thereby contributing to systemic aging [ ]. Current clinical approaches for the prevention and treatment of photoaging primarily include the use of sunscreens, laser therapy, and chemical peels. However, these interventions are limited by poor skin penetration, short-lived efficacy, invasiveness, and notable side effects to varying degrees [ , ]. Therefore, identifying novel targets and elucidating the underlying mechanisms of photoaging are critical for developing more effective therapeutic strategies. Mitochondrial dysfunction is one of the key driving forces of aging [ ]. UV irradiation induces excessive production of reactive oxygen species (ROS), and the accumulation of ROS disrupts the mitochondrial membrane potential (ΔΨm), leading to abnormal opening of the mitochondrial permeability transition pore (mPTP) [ ]. During this process, intracellular Ca²⁺ influx into the mitochondria causes Ca²⁺ overload, which exacerbates mitochondrial damage, disrupts cellular homeostasis, and consequently accelerates aging [ , ]. Investigating mitochondrial dysfunction may therefore reveal novel therapeutic targets for skin photoaging.
In recent years, mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) have been effectively applied in tissue repair, anti-aging, and regenerative medicine due to their unique capabilities for self-renewal, multilineage differentiation, and immunomodulation [ – ]. Subsequent studies have shown that the beneficial effects of MSCs largely depend on their paracrine secretion of small extracellular vesicles (sEVs), which carry various bioactive molecules that mediate intercellular communication and play a critical role in maintaining cellular homeostasis and repairing damaged tissues [ , ]. For example, Dorronsoro et al. [ ] demonstrated that bone Marrow-derived MSC-EVs from young mice could extend Lifespan, reduce the number of senescent fibroblasts, and restore their function, highlighting the therapeutic potential of MSC-EVs in anti-aging interventions. However, the mechanisms by which MSC-sEVs mitigate skin photoaging remain insufficiently understood. Some studies have focused on the regulatory roles of miRNAs carried within sEVs, particularly through interactions with the 3’-untranslated regions (3’-UTRs) of target genes, offering a certain degree of specificity and research value [ , ]. Nevertheless, relying solely on miRNA-mediated effects may not fully elucidate this complex process, especially considering the limited stability of small RNA fragments, which constrains their potential as reliable biomarkers for clinical diagnosis and prognosis. Therefore, we aim to investigate how MSC-sEVs delay aging through broader regulatory axes.
Pregnancy zone protein (PZP), a member of the α2-macroglobulin family, was previously thought to be primarily associated with immune tolerance during pregnancy. However, recent studies have revealed its involvement in neurodegenerative diseases, antioxidant stress responses, and protease inhibition [ ]. Notably, PZP levels have been reported to decrease more than tenfold in senescent cells, suggesting that it may function as a positive regulator of cellular senescence and serve as a novel biomarker for aging [ ]. Based on RNA-seq results, our previous study was the first to demonstrate that hucMSC-sEVs can delay skin aging by upregulating the expression of PZP, thereby reducing ROS production and DNA damage [ ]. However, the mechanism by which hucMSC-sEVs regulate PZP expression remains unclear.
In this study, we further elucidated the regulatory network through which hucMSC-sEVs modulate PZP to protect against UV-induced skin photoaging. We identified GATA2 as an upstream transcriptional regulator of PZP and GRP75 as its downstream target. GRP75, a mitochondrial chaperone protein, serves as a molecular bridge between the endoplasmic reticulum and mitochondria, playing a critical role in maintaining intracellular calcium homeostasis and mitochondrial function [ ]. Collectively, these findings preliminarily reveal a GATA2/PZP/GRP75 signaling axis potentially involved in the regulation of photoaging. Furthermore, our experimental results demonstrated that hucMSC-sEVs can deliver the transcription factor GATA2, which binds to the promoter region of PZP and upregulates its expression, thereby suppressing the abnormal elevation of GRP75 and alleviating UV-induced mitochondrial dysfunction. These findings enrich the molecular regulatory network underlying skin aging, provide a novel therapeutic target for combating skin aging, and offer a theoretical basis for the identification of relevant diagnostic biomarkers.

## Materials and methods

## Isolation and characterization of MSCs and MSC-sEVs
Human umbilical cord mesenchymal stem cells (hucMSCs) were isolated using the tissue explant method. Fresh umbilical cords were washed with PBS, blood vessels removed, and tissues cut into 1–2 mm³ pieces. Explants were cultured in α-MEM (Gibco, C12571500BT) with 15% FBS (Vazyme, F101-03) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin at 37 °C in 5% CO₂. Medium was changed every three days. Spindle-shaped cells were passaged at confluence to obtain P1 cells. Multipotency was confirmed by osteogenic and adipogenic induction, followed by Alizarin Red S and Oil Red O staining. Flow cytometry was used to analyze surface markers of P3 cells.
MSC-sEVs were collected from passage 3–7 HucMSCs cultured in exosome-depleted FBS medium for 48h. Supernatants were harvested and sequentially centrifuged at 300 g, 2,000 g, and 10,000 g to remove cells and debris. The resulting supernatant was concentrated using a 100 kDa ultrafiltration tube (Millipore, UFC910096a), followed by two rounds of ultracentrifugation at 100,000 g. Pellets were washed with PBS (Meilunbio, MA0015), sterilized using a 0.22 μm filter (Millipore, SLGP033RS), and stored at − 80 °C. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM, FEI Tecnai, Philips) was used to observe vesicle morphology. Nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA, Particle Metrix) measured size distribution, concentration, and ζ-potential. sEVs markers were detected by western blotting.

## Establishment of in vivo and in vitro photoaging models and MSC-sEVs treatment
Thirty 6-week-old ICR mice were purchased from the Experimental Animal Center of Jiangsu University and acclimated for 5 days. Dorsal hair was removed to expose the skin, and mice were randomly divided into five groups: CTR, UV, UV + MN, UV + MN + PBS, and UV + MN + sEVs. The CTR group received no treatment. All other groups underwent four cycles of UV exposure (each cycle lasting 7 days: 1 day pretreatment, 5 consecutive days of UV irradiation, and 1 rest day). On the first day of each UV irradiation cycle, mice in the UV + MN, UV + MN + PBS, and UV + MN + sEVs groups received their respective treatments. For sEVs administration, a sterile microneedle roller (UV-sterilized overnight) was applied to the dorsal skin in a cross-hatch pattern under consistent pressure to enhance permeability. Subsequently, 1 × 10¹⁰ sEV particles dissolved in PBS were evenly applied to the microneedle-treated area and gently spread with a sterile pipette tip to promote absorption. All sEVs preparations were sterile-filtered using a 0.22 μm disposable filter before application. The combined UVA and UVB irradiation dose was determined based on the minimal erythema dose (MED), where 1 MED corresponds to 717 mJ/cm² of UVA and 53 mJ/cm² of UVB. In the first cycle, mice received 1 MED, and the dose was increased by 0.5 MED in each subsequent cycle. The work has been reported in Line with the ARRIVE Guidelines 2.0. At the end of the experiment, mice were euthanized by cervical dislocation following anesthesia induced by intraperitoneal injection of Delivector™ Avertin (Dowobio, Shanghai, China), and dorsal skin tissues subjected to UV exposure and treatment were collected for further analysis.
SD neonatal rats (1–2 days old) were euthanized, and dorsal skin was collected under sterile conditions. After washing three times with PBS containing 1% penicillin-streptomycin, fat and blood vessels were removed. Skin was cut into strips and digested overnight at 4 °C in 0.25% Dispase. The next day, epidermis and dermis were separated, and dermis was cut into 1–2 mm³ pieces and plated in 3.5 cm dishes. Once adherent, cells were cultured in α-MEM with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin-streptomycin, with medium changes every 3 days, to obtain dermal fibroblasts (DFs). DFs were seeded into 6-well plates, pretreated with sEVs (1 × 10¹⁰ particles/mL) for 24 h, then irradiated with 365 nm UVA or 311 nm UVB (Philips, China) after PBS rinse. UV dose was monitored in real time using a radiometer (LINSHANG).

## Western blot
Proteins were separated by SDS-PAGE using 1× SDS running buffer. Pre-stained protein marker (Vazyme, MP102-01) and samples were loaded, and electrophoresis was performed at 60 V for 30 min, followed by 90 V until clear band separation. Proteins were then transferred to methanol-activated PVDF membranes (Millipore, IPVH00010) at a constant current of 300 Ma for 90 min. Membranes were blocked with 5% skim milk at room temperature for 2 h, then incubated with primary antibodies (1:500) overnight at 4 °C. After TBST washes, membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (1:4000) for 2 h at room temperature. Signals were visualized using ECL reagent (Vazyme, E422-02) and detected with a chemiluminescence imaging system.

## Immunohistochemical staining
Immunohistochemical staining was performed according to the manufacturer’s protocol (Boster, Cat# SA1020, Wuhan, China).

## Cell Immunofluorescence staining
Cell coverslips were removed from 12-well plates, washed with PBS, and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 10 min. After permeabilization with 0.1% Triton X-100 for 10 min, cells were blocked with 5% BSA for 1 h. Primary antibodies (1:100) were added and incubated overnight at 4 °C. After PBS washes, appropriate fluorescent secondary antibodies were applied and incubated for 2 h at room temperature. Nuclei were stained with Hoechst 33,342 (1:200, Sigma Aldrich) for 10 min. Following PBS washes, coverslips were mounted with antifade reagent and imaged using a fluorescence microscope.

## Chromatin Immunoprecipitation (ChIP)
ChIP was performed using the Pierce™ Magnetic ChIP Kit (Thermo, 26157) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Cells were cross-linked with 1% formaldehyde for 10 min at room temperature, quenched with glycine, washed with PBS, and collected by scraping. After lysis on ice, chromatin was digested with MNase to obtain appropriately sized DNA fragments. Digestion was stopped, and nuclear components were collected by centrifugation and further sheared by sonication. 10% of the chromatin was saved as input; the remainder was incubated overnight at 4 °C with IgG, RNA Polymerase II antibody (positive control), or GATA2 antibody. The next day, Protein A/G Magnetic beads were added and incubated for 2h. After washing to remove nonspecific binding, elution was performed at 65 °C for 40 min. Samples were treated with NaCl and proteinase K at 65 °C for 1.5 h to reverse cross-links. DNA was purified and analyzed by qPCR to detect enrichment of specific target sequences.

## Dual-Luciferase reporter assay
DFs were seeded into 24-well plates to reach ~ 50% confluency the following day. Four sterile 1.5 mL microcentrifuge tubes were prepared, each containing 50 µL jetPRIME buffer. Plasmid combinations were added at a ratio of 0.25 µg: 0.25 µg: 0.025 µg as follows: (1) pcDNA3.1(+) + empty pGL4-Basic + pRL-TK, (2) pcDNA3.1(+) + rat PZP promoter-pGL3-Basic + pRL-TK, (3) homo-GATA2-pcDNA3.1(+) + empty pGL4-Basic + pRL-TK, and (4) homo-GATA2-pcDNA3.1(+) + rat PZP promoter-pGL3-Basic + pRL-TK. Each tube received 1 µL jetPRIME transfection reagent and was incubated at room temperature for 15 min. The transfection mixtures were then added to the corresponding wells and incubated for 48 h. After transfection, cells were washed with PBS, lysed with 1× Cell Lysis Buffer for 5 min, and centrifuged at 12,000×g for 2 min, and luciferase activities were measured using a luminometer. Promoter activity was evaluated by calculating the ratio of Firefly to Renilla luciferase signals.

## RT-qPCR
qPCR was performed using SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme, Q312-02) following the manufacturer’s protocol. Each 20 µL reaction contained 10 µL Master Mix, 0.8 µL primers, 7.2 µL ddH₂O, and 2 µL cDNA. Reactions were run under standard cycling conditions. Relative gene expression was calculated using the 2^−ΔΔCT method based on the obtained CT values. Primer sequences, siRNA sequences, and plasmid information are provided in the supplementary materials. All primers, siRNAs, and plasmids were purchased from Sangon Biotech (Shanghai) Co., Ltd.

## Bioinformatics analysis
Potential transcription factors of the target gene were predicted using JASPAR, PROMO, and TFDB databases, and intersecting results were retained. Protein–protein interaction (PPI) networks were analyzed using the STRING database. Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analyses were performed via the DAVID database, with an initial cutoff of P < 0.05. Statistically significant GO terms and KEGG pathways were further selected using a threshold of FDR < 0.05 for downstream analysis.

## SA-β-Gal staining analysis
Senescence-associated β-galactosidase staining was performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions using the SA-β-gal Staining Kit (Yeasen Biotechnology, Shanghai).

## Statistical analysis
Statistical analysis was performed in GraphPad Prism 9.0. Data are shown as mean ± SD. Two-group comparisons used unpaired t-tests, and one-way ANOVA was applied for multiple groups. P < 0.05 was considered significant.

## Results

## Characterization of MSCs and MSC-sEVs
The isolated and cultured hucMSCs exhibited a typical spindle-shaped, fibroblast-like morphology under the microscope and grew in a whirlpool-like pattern (Fig. A). Upon induction under osteogenic and adipogenic conditions, the hucMSCs successfully differentiated into mineralized nodules and lipid droplets, respectively (Fig. B-C). Flow cytometry analysis further confirmed high expression levels of surface markers CD105 (96.7%), CD73 (99.1%), and CD166 (98.9%), while the negative markers CD34, CD45, and CD11b were expressed at levels below 1%, effectively excluding the presence of non-MSC populations (Fig. D). These results collectively demonstrate the successful acquisition of hucMSCs with typical multipotent differentiation capacity.
HucMSC-sEVs were obtained from the collected conditioned medium through ultrafiltration and ultracentrifugation. TEM revealed that the MSC-sEVs exhibited a characteristic cup-shaped, spherical morphology (Fig. A). NTA showed that the vesicles had an average diameter of 152 nm, an average concentration of 1.2 × 10¹¹ particles/mL, and an average zeta potential of −35.94 ± 0.43 mV (Fig. B-C). Compared with parental MSCs, the MSC-sEVs expressed typical markers, including CD9, CD81, Alix, and TSG101, while the negative marker Calnexin was absent (Fig. D). These results confirm the successful isolation of hucMSC-sEVs.
 

## HucMSC-sEVs modulate PZP to defend against UV-Induced Photoaging in ICR mice
Our previous study identified PZP as a novel target for regulating skin photoaging, and its role was validated in clinical samples of actinic keratosis (AK) and squamous cell carcinoma (SCC), as well as in a photoaging model using nude mice [ ]. However, due to the severely compromised immune function, thinner stratum corneum, and weakened skin barrier and wound healing capacity in nude mice, UV irradiation often results in atypical skin damage that does not accurately reflect human photoaging. To overcome these limitations, we established a novel photoaging model in ICR mice induced by combined UVA and UVB exposure (Fig. E). In vivo imaging results demonstrated that microneedle-assisted delivery significantly enhanced the local retention and skin penetration efficiency of sEVs compared to direct topical application (Fig. E). Upon completion of modeling, gross observation of ICR mouse skin revealed that, compared to the relatively smooth skin in the control group with no evident wrinkles or erythema, UV-irradiated mice exhibited pronounced photoaging features, including increased roughness, erythema, and deepened wrinkles. Notably, neither microneedle rolling alone nor the negative control group showed therapeutic effects, whereas the sEVs-treated group displayed a marked reduction in wrinkle formation (Fig. F). Furthermore, hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining and Masson’s trichrome staining demonstrated abnormal thickening of the stratum corneum, inflammatory cell infiltration, and reduced collagen density in the model group, all of which were significantly alleviated following sEVs treatment, with skin morphology approaching normal levels (Fig. G-H and J). Skin tissues from ICR mice were collected for immunohistochemical staining and RT-qPCR analysis of PZP expression. Consistent with our previous findings, PZP expression was downregulated following UV irradiation and significantly upregulated after sEVs treatment (Fig. I). Accordingly, we assessed the mRNA expression levels of PZP along with other genes known to play key roles in the aging process, including COL1A1, IL-6, and P53 [ ], sEVs treatment effectively reversed the aberrant expression of these genes (Fig. K).
For biosafety assessment, after the 28-day experimental period (four UV irradiation cycles), liver (ALT, AST) and kidney (BUN, Cr) function markers showed no significant changes, and histology of major organs revealed no pathological abnormalities, indicating favorable biosafety of hucMSC-sEVs under experimental conditions (Fig. A-B).

## The transcription factor GATA2 carried by hucMSC-sEVs positively regulates PZP expression
To explore how sEVs regulate PZP expression, we proposed two hypotheses: (1) sEVs directly deliver endogenous PZP to increase its expression. (2) sEVs carry specific transcription factors that activate PZP transcription. Western blot analysis revealed that PZP was either absent or expressed at extremely low levels in sEVs (Fig. A). We then employed three databases (JASPAR, PROMO, and TFDB) to predict transcription factors potentially binding to the PZP promoter region. Cross-referencing the results identified 10 transcription factors with high confidence (Fig. A). Subsequent protein–protein interaction (PPI) network analysis using the STRING database revealed that members of the GATA family formed a tightly connected cluster, with GATA2 showing the highest interaction score, suggesting it may play a key role in regulating PZP expression (Fig. B). Notably, a study by Li et al. [ ] demonstrated that GATA2 is closely associated with the proliferation, differentiation, and functional maintenance of MSCs. GO analysis revealed that the identified transcription factors were enriched in biological processes such as transcriptional regulation and DNA-binding transcription factor activity, further supporting our hypothesis (Fig. C). Analysis of skin tissue samples from the GEPIA database showed a positive correlation between GATA2 and PZP expression levels (Fig. D). We then performed a bioinformatic analysis of GATA2 binding sites within the PZP promoter region. The distribution of enriched motifs was consistent with the known binding preference of zinc finger transcription factors for A/T-rich sequences (Fig. E). Furthermore, molecular docking predictions indicated the potential for hydrogen bonding and π–π stacking interactions between GATA2 and the PZP promoter (Fig. F). These results collectively suggest a high likelihood of direct binding between GATA2 and the PZP promoter region.
 Western blot analysis of three independently prepared batches of hucMSC-sEVs confirmed the stable presence of GATA2 within the vesicles (Fig. G). We isolated and characterized dermal fibroblasts (DFs) and verified that sEVs were efficiently internalized by DFs (Fig. B-D). Subsequently, we assessed GATA2 expression in both in vivo and in vitro photoaging models following sEVs treatment. Western blot results showed that UV exposure reduced the expression of GATA2 and PZP, whereas sEVs intervention restored their levels (Fig. H). Immunofluorescence further confirmed nuclear localization of GATA2, with stronger fluorescence signals observed in the sEVs-treated group compared to the UV group (Fig. I). In addition, immunohistochemical staining of mouse skin tissue further confirmed the expression of GATA2 (Fig. J). To determine whether GATA2 directly binds to the PZP promoter and regulates its transcriptional activity, we performed chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) using a GATA2-specific antibody. The results showed significantly increased enrichment of GATA2 at the PZP promoter region compared to the IgG control, and the PCR-amplified products were validated by agarose gel electrophoresis (Fig. K). We then constructed a luciferase reporter vector containing the PZP promoter sequence (PGL3-PZP) along with an empty vector control (PGL3-basic). These constructs were co-transfected with a GATA2 overexpression plasmid (pcDNA3.1-GATA2) and a luciferase control plasmid (pcDNA3.1). Compared to the empty vector, GATA2 overexpression significantly enhanced luciferase activity of the PGL3-PZP construct (Fig. L). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that GATA2 specifically binds to the PZP promoter and enhances its transcriptional activity, further supporting our hypothesis.

## Effects of GATA2 overexpression or knockdown on hucMSC-sEVs-Mediated PZP regulation and cellular senescence
To further verify the role of GATA2 expression changes in the anti-photoaging effects of sEVs, we overexpressed GATA2 in MSCs. Western blot and RT-qPCR analyses confirmed that transfection with GATA2 overexpression plasmids and siRNAs effectively increased and decreased GATA2 expression levels in MSCs, respectively (Fig. A-D). sEVs were then isolated from GATA2-overexpressing and GATA2-knockdown MSCs. Western blot results showed that the GATA2 content in the derived sEVs varied accordingly (Fig. E-F). Compared with the sEVs group, si-GATA2-sEVs treatment failed to upregulate PZP expression in DFs, indicating that GATA2 suppression impaired the positive regulation of PZP (Fig. A). Following UV irradiation, senescent cells were detected using SA-β-gal staining. Statistical analysis revealed that the ability of si-GATA2-sEVs to reduce senescent cell positivity was weakened compared to the control sEVs group (Fig. B). The production and accumulation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) is a key factor in the aging process [ ]. ROS levels were elevated in the si-GATA2-sEVs group compared to the control sEVs group (Fig. C). We then assessed the expression of aging markers P53 and γ-H2AX, a marker of DNA damage, via immunofluorescence. sEVs treatment reduced the UV-induced increase in fluorescence intensity of P53 and γ-H2AX, whereas si-GATA2-sEVs treatment failed to significantly suppress their expression (Fig. D). Conversely, treatment with GATA2-overexpressing sEVs significantly upregulated PZP expression (Fig. E), reduced the percentage of senescent cells (Fig. F), suppressed ROS accumulation (Fig. G), and decreased the fluorescence intensity of P53 and γ-H2AX (Fig. H), compared to the control sEVs group. These results indicate that GATA2 plays a critical role in hucMSC-sEVs–mediated regulation of PZP expression and in mitigating the progression of photoaging.
 

## HucMSC-sEVs upregulates PZP expression and targets GRP75
To further investigate the downstream targets of PZP and elucidate its regulatory role in photoaging, we performed Co-IP to enrich PZP and its interacting proteins, followed by LC-MS/MS analysis of the SDS-PAGE–separated products. Subcellular localization analysis revealed that the identified interacting proteins were primarily distributed in the cytoplasm (34.91%), nucleus (21.89%), and mitochondria (10.65%), suggesting that PZP may exert regulatory functions through multiple cellular compartments (Fig. A). KEGG pathway enrichment analysis indicated that these proteins may be involved in biological processes such as energy metabolism, aging, and protein folding (Fig. B). GO functional analysis further revealed that the PZP-interacting proteins were enriched in processes including GTPase-mediated signal transduction, ATP synthesis, and redox reactions, suggesting that PZP may play a role in regulating mitochondrial homeostasis (Fig. C). Based on IPR analysis and protein abundance data, we selected GRP75, a mitochondrial chaperone involved in protein folding, transmembrane transport, and maintenance of mitochondrial homeostasis, for further validation (Fig. D-E). Protein docking analysis predicted a potential interaction interface between PZP and GRP75 (Fig. F). Co-IP experiments subsequently confirmed the presence of GRP75 in the IP group but not in the IgG control group, suggesting that GRP75 may be a direct interacting partner of PZP (Fig. G). Moreover, fluorescence co-localization analysis demonstrated high co-localization of PZP and GRP75 in DFs, indicating that the two proteins may coexist in the same subcellular compartments (Fig. H).
 In the in vitro model, we confirmed through WB, IF, and RT-qPCR that UV irradiation significantly increased GRP75 expression, whereas sEVs treatment suppressed this upregulation (Fig. I-K). A similar trend was observed in the in vivo model (Fig. L). To investigate the relationship between PZP and GRP75, we conducted PZP overexpression and knockdown experiments in DFs. The efficiency of PZP modulation was validated by Western blot and RT-qPCR (Fig. A-D). Knockdown of PZP led to an increase in GRP75 expression (Fig. M), whereas PZP overexpression significantly downregulated GRP75 levels (Fig. N). These results suggest that PZP may participate in the hucMSC-sEVs–mediated protective mechanism against photoaging by suppressing GRP75 expression.

## Inhibition of GRP75 alleviates mitochondrial dysfunction in UV-induced Photoaging
GRP75 is a molecular chaperone located at mitochondria–endoplasmic reticulum contact sites (MERCs), where it plays a key role in Ca²⁺ transport and mitochondrial homeostasis [ ]. We hypothesized that aberrant upregulation of GRP75 may lead to mitochondrial Ca²⁺ overload, resulting in mitochondrial dysfunction and ultimately accelerating the photoaging process [ ]. To determine whether suppression of GRP75 by PZP could alleviate aging phenotypes and mitochondrial dysfunction, we employed the selective GRP75 inhibitor MKT-077. A CCK-8 assay was performed to assess the effects of different concentrations of MKT-077 on DFs viability at various time points. Based on the results, a concentration of 1 µM was selected for subsequent experiments (Fig. A). Immunofluorescence analysis showed that both MKT-077 and sEVs treatment effectively reduced the UV-induced increase in GRP75 fluorescence intensity, with a further decrease observed in the combined treatment group (Fig. B). Western blot results confirmed this trend (Fig. C).
 To assess whether GRP75 inhibition could alleviate UV-induced mitochondrial Ca²⁺ imbalance, we used the Rhod-2 AM probe to measure mitochondrial Ca²⁺ levels. Both MKT-077 and sEVs treatments significantly reduced Ca²⁺ fluorescence intensity, indicating relief of Ca²⁺ overload (Fig. D). Calcein AM staining was then used to evaluate changes in mitochondrial membrane permeability. The results showed that increased membrane permeability induced by UV exposure was alleviated following GRP75 inhibition (Fig. E). Furthermore, JC-1 staining revealed that the UV-induced loss of mitochondrial membrane potential was partially restored under conditions of GRP75 inhibition mediated by either MKT-077 or sEVs (Fig. F). In addition, MKT-077, sEVs, and their combination significantly reduced mitochondrial ROS production (Fig. G). To further assess mitochondrial morphology, we increased the UVA irradiation dose and performed Mito-Tracker staining. Quantitative analysis revealed that UV exposure led to shortened and disorganized mitochondrial structures, whereas treatment with MKT-077 or sEVs partially restored mitochondrial length and morphology (Fig. H). These findings suggest that GRP75 inhibition effectively mitigates UV-induced mitochondrial structural and functional abnormalities, thereby alleviating the extent of skin photoaging following UV irradiation (Fig. ).
 

## Discussion
This study further validates the findings of Zhang et al. [ ] and our previous work, confirming that hucMSC-sEVs can alleviate UV-induced skin photoaging [ ]. We systematically characterized the isolated hucMSCs and their derived sEVs, confirming the expression of typical surface markers and vesicular morphology [ ]. To better mimic the natural composition of UV light, we employed an ICR mouse model with intact hair and normal immune function, using combined UVA and UVB irradiation [ ]. As before, we used microneedle rollers to assist sEVs delivery, addressing the limitations of subcutaneous injection in terms of penetration and distribution efficiency [ ]. Based on prior RNA-seq screening and validation with clinical samples, we identified PZP for the first time as a key anti-photoaging protein. To further clarify its mechanism, we explored and confirmed the critical role of the GATA2/PZP/GRP75 regulatory axis in sEVs-mediated photoaging protection. Our findings show that PZP acts not only as a downstream response protein to UV-induced damage but also has upstream transcriptional regulation and downstream roles in maintaining mitochondrial homeostasis. Specifically, hucMSC-sEVs deliver the transcription factor GATA2, which binds to and activates the PZP promoter, leading to increased PZP expression. This, in turn, suppresses GRP75 activity, improves mitochondrial dysfunction, and ultimately delays skin photoaging. In summary, this study reveals a novel regulatory pathway by which MSC-sEVs mitigate skin aging and provides strong evidence supporting this mechanism.
Photoaging, an exogenous form of aging, serves as a window into systemic aging. Its core mechanism involves sustained UV-induced activation of stress pathways, leading to mitochondrial dysfunction, disrupted DNA repair, and epigenetic reprogramming, which together drive cells into an irreversible senescent state [ , ]. Stem cells and their derived sEVs have been widely reported in anti-aging research. For example, Zou et al. [ ] used multi-omics analysis to establish a photoaging model and found that conditioned medium from hucMSCs effectively alleviated UVB-induced photodamage. Another study showed that extracellular vesicles derived from human adipose-derived stem cells improved matrix synthesis in DFs and protected against UVB-induced damage through their bioactive components [ ]. In addition, Cui, et al. (2025) proposed the involvement of the miR-125b-5p/TGF-β1/Smad axis in the anti-photoaging effects of hair follicle MSC-derived sEVs.
Our study does not aim to simply replicate the anti-aging effects of sEVs, but rather to address three key scientific questions: (1) Do endogenous proteins within sEVs have functional specificity? (2) Can a vertical regulatory logic of “transcriptional regulation–key target–mitochondrial homeostasis” be established? (3) Does this regulatory axis hold potential for therapeutic intervention? Unlike miRNA-mediated non-coding regulatory mechanisms, we observed that GATA2, a functional protein, is enriched in hucMSC-sEVs. Previous studies have shown that GATA2 plays a critical role in maintaining stem cell homeostasis [ ]. In our experiments, both knockdown and overexpression of GATA2 significantly affected the proliferation rate of MSCs, highlighting its critical role in stem cell function. Based on bioinformatic analysis, we hypothesized that GATA2 in sEVs may enter recipient cells in its protein form and regulate PZP expression, rather than increasing PZP levels through direct delivery of the protein itself. This hypothesis was further supported by ChIP assays and dual-luciferase reporter analysis, which confirmed the transcriptional regulatory role of GATA2 on PZP.
In our previous studies, we demonstrated that PZP exerts protective effects against photoaging by inhibiting MMP1 expression and reducing collagen degradation. Notably, we also observed that increased PZP expression was accompanied by decreased MitoSox signaling, suggesting a potential role in modulating mitochondrial activity. Given that mitochondrial dysfunction is a key driver of cellular senescence [ ], we further explored the multifunctional nature of PZP. Through proteomic screening and bioinformatic analysis, we identified GRP75 as a potential interacting candidate, which was subsequently validated by Co-IP and colocalization assays. The interaction of PZP with both MMP1 and GRP75 suggests that it may act as an upstream multitarget homeostatic regulator [ ]. Such multitarget interaction patterns are not uncommon among anti-aging proteins; for instance, SIRT1 and FOXO3a also exhibit cross-pathway regulatory functions [ – ]. PZP may contribute to skin aging defense through a dual mechanism—maintaining ECM integrity extracellularly, and intracellularly stabilizing mitochondrial function, possibly by interfering with GRP75-mediated calcium signaling. This multilayered protective role also highlights the potential of designing biologic delivery systems for more advanced systemic interventions.
The functional role of GRP75 also warrants further investigation. As a member of the HSP70 family, GRP75 has traditionally been associated with protective mechanisms such as stress response, anti-apoptosis, and protein folding. For example, one study reported that GRP75 enhances the connection between the endoplasmic reticulum and mitochondria following axonal injury, promoting mitochondrial ATP production to support axonal regeneration [ ]. Notably, in our photoaging model, GRP75 expression was significantly upregulated, yet this was accompanied by loss of mitochondrial membrane potential, ROS accumulation, and increased cellular senescence. In contrast, GRP75 inhibition alleviated these phenotypes—a finding supported by studies from Yuan et al. [ ] and Li et al. [ ]. This stands in stark contrast to GRP75’s conventional role as a cytoprotective protein, suggesting a context-dependent duality in its function. Such biphasic behavior highlights GRP75 as a highly plastic regulatory node within complex physiological settings.
Although this study is the first to reveal the pivotal role of the GATA2/PZP/GRP75 signaling axis in hucMSC-sEVs-mediated protection against skin photoaging, several limitations remain to be addressed in future research. First, the mechanism by which GATA2 is selectively enriched in hucMSC-sEVs and how it specifically recognizes the PZP promoter remains unclear; current evidence supports its transcriptional activity only at the functional level. Second, our investigation primarily focused on fibroblasts, whereas photoaging involves multiple skin cell types, including keratinocytes and melanocytes [ ]. Whether the GATA2/PZP/GRP75 axis exerts similar effects across these cell populations remains to be determined. Future studies could employ organoid models and humanized skin grafts for more comprehensive evaluation. We aim to further advance the mechanistic understanding and translational potential of sEVs-based precision interventions in subsequent work.

## Conclusion
In summary, this study systematically demonstrates that hucMSC-sEVs delay UV-induced skin photoaging by delivering the transcription factor GATA2, which upregulates PZP expression and subsequently inhibits GRP75 mediated mitochondrial calcium overload and dysfunction. We are the first to identify the critical role of the GATA2/PZP/GRP75 signaling axis in the anti-aging effects mediated by MSC-sEVs. This work refines the functional network centered on PZP and lays a foundation for its development as a potential clinical diagnostic target.
