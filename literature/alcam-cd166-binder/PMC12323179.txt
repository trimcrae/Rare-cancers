## Abstract
Obesity alters the adipose tissue (AT) immune microenvironment (TIME) and induces inflammation. Nevertheless, the specific time when TIME dysfunction emerges in AT remains elusive. Obesity mouse models were constructed and categorized into Lean (Ctrl), moderately obese (Mid_Ob, with body weight exceeding Ctrl by > 10%), and Obese (Ob, with body weight exceeding Ctrl by > 20%) groups. Single-nucleus RNA sequencing (snRNA-seq) and immunofluorescence localization were employed to analyze changes in cell populations, cluster marker genes, and signaling pathways in mouse epididymal white adipose tissue (eWAT). A total of 23,068 cells obtained from three mouse eWAT samples (Ctrl, Mid_Ob, and Ob) underwent snRNA-seq. Eighteen distinct clusters were identified, and seven cell types were annotated with representative markers. In-depth analysis unveiled the functions of the adipose AT-associated Krt23+fibroblast (ATAKF) cell subtype and refined the crosstalk hypothesis concerning ATAKFs, macrophages, and neutrophils. We further demonstrated that ATAKFs were present in the Mid_Ob group. Additionally, from the Mid_Ob to Ob stage, AT inflammatory factors, macrophages, and neutrophils significantly increased while neurons markedly decreased; however, these factors did not change significantly from the Ctrl to Mid_Ob stage. Cell–cell communication analysis revealed the central role of ATAKFs and their regulation of macrophages and neutrophils. Only when obesity reaches the Mid_Ob stage AT fibroblasts specifically express Krt23+, recruit neutrophils, and secrete CCL2, CCL6, and CCL9 to attract macrophages, thereby altering the eWAT TIME. ATAKFs contribute to immune microenvironment disorders during the development of mouse AT obesity. This study offers valuable resources and a foundation for understanding the timing of TIME dysfunction.

## Introduction
Obesity in people has emerged as a significant public health issue, posing a global threat to health. Diseases such as hyperlipidemia, atherosclerosis, hypertension, diabetes, and coronary heart disease are often caused in obese individuals, presenting a serious menace to human well-being [ , ]. Obesity results from the prolonged intake of excessive calories, with a high-fat diet being a crucial contributing factor [ ].
Adipose tissue serves as a vital immune and endocrine organ [ ]. Obesity induces adipose tissue dysfunction, and dysfunctional adipose cells are the main source of proinflammatory cytokines that aggravate systemic low-grade inflammation and further promote the progression of obesity-related disorders [ ]. However, it remains unknown whether adipose tissue releases proinflammatory cytokines in the early stages of obesity development. The adipose tissue microenvironment comprises various cell types, including adipocytes, immune cells, fibroblasts, and endothelial cells. Stromal cells, mainly fibroblasts and endothelial cells, are important components of the adipose tissue microenvironment. An imbalance in the immune microenvironment (TIME) and its metabolism will lead to the secretion or regulation of related hormones (ultimately resulting in leptin and insulin resistance), causing inflammation in adipose tissue [ ]. The TIME is crucial for maintaining adipose tissue homeostasis and is an important factor underlying obesity development [ , ]. Nevertheless, the specific time when TIME dysfunction occurs in adipose tissue during obesity progression remains unclear.
Single-nucleus RNA sequencing (snRNA-seq) analysis of immune cells in adipose tissue (AT) of obese and healthy mice revealed that obesity modifies the immune cell composition within the tissue [ ]. Aitin et al. identified the immune cell map in AT of obese individuals and discovered that among 14 immune cellular subsets, a group of CD8+ T cells with heightened expression of metallothionein genes is associated with obesity [ ]. Hill et al. also uncovered a CD9+ type macrophage related to inflammation via snRNA-seq, suggesting that CD9+ macrophages constitute a unique type linked to obesity [ ]. Anitta Kinga Sarvari et al. performed snRNA-seq on mouse epididymal white adipose tissue (eWAT) and demonstrated that the eWAT profile in diet-induced obesity causes significant transcriptional changes in locus gene expression of all cell types in preadipocyte tissues of epididymal fat[ ]. Although the authors of these studies conducted preliminary analyses of the TIME characteristics in AT of obese individuals, the characteristics of cell types and dynamic changes in the TIME during the development of nutritional obesity remain unclear.
We hypothesize that during different stages of nutritional obesity, the AT immune microenvironment (TIME) continuously changes and affects fibroblasts and related genes in AT. The aim of this study is to analyze the internal mechanisms (genes and signaling pathways) governing these immune microenvironment changes in the eWATs of nutritionally induced obese mice using snRNA-seq and determine the time when TIME dysfunction occurs during obesity development in the eWAT of obese animals.

## Methods

## Establishment and grouping of animal models
Animal work was performed in accordance with the National Institute of Health (NIH) guidelines for the use of experimental animals and the study protocol of Animal Experiment Ethics Committee of Shandong Sport University (No. 2022002). Five-week-old C57BL/6J male mice were procured from Jiangsu Huachinnuo Medical Technology Co., Ltd. (China). The mice were housed in a temperature-controlled (25 °C) environment with a 12-h light/dark cycle. Mice in the control group were fed an ordinary diet, while those in the high-fat diet (HFD) group were given a HFD (60% fat) (D12492, Beijing small millet You Tai). The mice were weighed at a fixed time weekly. Normal control mice (Ctrl group), those in the process of becoming obese (Mid_Ob group, with body weight 10% higher than that of the Ctrl group), and those that were obese (Ob group, with body weight over 20% higher than that of the Ctrl group) were selected, with eight mice in each group. The Ctrl group was euthanized at week 9, the Mid_Ob group at week 7 (after 2 weeks on HFD), and the Ob group at week 9 (after 4 weeks on HFD). For the Mid_Ob group, modeling was considered successful when the average body weight exceeded 10% of that of the Ctrl group. Mice were defined as obese when their body weight was > 20% higher than that of the Ctrl group [ ].

## Biochemical index evaluation in blood
Mouse blood was collected from the eyes every two weeks. On Sunday, the mice were fasted for 12 h, and then testing was conducted at 8:00 am on Monday. Subsequently, serum was separated from the blood for further testing. Total serum cholesterol (TC), triglyceride (TG), low-density lipoprotein (LDL) levels, and high-density lipoprotein (HDL) were determined using a double-antibody sandwich method as per the ELISA kit instructions (Jiangsu Jingmei Biological Technology Co., Ltd., Jiangsu, China). Additionally, blood inflammatory factor levels of IL-6 and TNFα were determined for each mouse according to the ELISA kit instructions (JM-02446M1, JM-02415M1, Jiangsu Jingmei Biological Technology Co., Ltd., Jiangsu, China).

## Extraction and separation of AT
Upon successful modeling, eight mice were randomly chosen from each group. A total of 24 C57BL/6J male mice were fasted from food and water for 12 h prior to blood and tissue sampling. For anesthesia, a 3% (w/v) pentobarbital sodium solution (equivalent to 30 mg/kg) was injected intraperitoneally at 1 ml/kg, following standard rodent anesthesia protocols [ ]. The mouse abdominal cavity was opened, the liver was cut, and epididymal adipose was removed with forceps. After removing the adipose tissue (AT), it was rinsed with 0.9% saline. Eight ATs from each group were placed in fixative bottles containing 4% paraformaldehyde for histological fixation. The remaining ATs were cut, placed in pre-labeled 2-mL cryopreservation tubes, and immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen. After sampling, the tubes were stored at − 80 °C for single-nucleus RNA sequencing (snRNA-seq) by Shanghai Ouyi Biomedical Technology Co., Ltd. Under sterile conditions, the tissues were washed twice with precooled RPMI 1640 medium containing 0.04% bovine serum albumin (BSA), then cut into small pieces of approximately 0.5 mm3 in size with surgical scissors. The pieces were transferred into prepared Liberase-TM (2.5 μg/ml, Roche) and DNase I (20 U/ml; Roche) for digestion at 37 °C for 1 h. The digested cell suspension was filtered through a BD 40 μm cell screen 1–2 times and centrifuged at 4 °C and 300 × g for 5 min. After resuspending the precipitate in the appropriate medium, an equal volume of red blood cell lysate (MACS, No. 130-094-183) was added, mixed, and incubated for 10 min at 4 °C. The cell suspension was centrifuged at 300 × g for 5 min, and the supernatant was discarded. The precipitate was washed once in medium and centrifuged at 300 × g for 5 min before discarding the supernatant. The cell precipitate was resuspended in a 100-μl medium, and the cell concentration and viability were determined using a Luna cell counter. Samples of epididymal white adipose tissue (eWAT) from 8 mice were mixed in a tube for detection.

## Liver oil red O staining and neuron Nissl staining
For tissue sectioning, sections with a thickness of 8 μm were cut. The tissue sections were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 10 min, washed three times with PBST for 5 min each, and then dried. Subsequently, the slices were immersed in oil red O dye solution in the dark for 10 min. Three seconds after removal, the sections were immersed in hematoxylin for re-staining for 5 min and then successively placed in three containers of distilled water for 5, 10, and 30 s, respectively. Differentiation was carried out in differentiation acid with 60% alcohol as the solvent for 5 s. After that, the sections were immersed in two containers of distilled water for 10 s each. The sections were placed in the reverting blue liquid for 1 s, and again, the slices were immersed in two containers of distilled water for 5 and 10 s, respectively. Finally, a glycerin gelatin tablet was used to seal the slides, and images were collected and analyzed using a microscope (Olympus BX53, Shanghai, China).
The Nissl staining process was as follows: The sections underwent conventional dewaxing in water (xylene I and xylene II for 15 min each), followed by a gradient alcohol dehydration series of 100% I, 100% II, 95%, 90%, 80%, 70%, and 50% for 5 min each. The samples were rinsed with distilled water three times for 5 min each and placed in a 60 °C temperature box with 1% toluidine blue stain for 40 min (or with tar purple stain for 30 s). After washing with distilled water, the dyed sections were dehydrated in 70%, 80%, 95%, and 100% ethanol and xylene. Finally, neutral gum was applied to seal the slides.

## Immunofluorescence localization
Slides were dewaxed and baked at 60–65 °C for 1 h. Subsequently, they were placed in xylene for 5–10 min to melt paraffin. Samples were transferred to absolute ethanol for tissue hydration (10 min in 100%, 5 min each in 95%, 90%, 80%, and 70%) and then cleaned with water for 3–5 min. Sections were repaired with citric acid buffer in a microwave for 10 min and cooled to room temperature. Thereafter, sections were covered with goat serum and incubated at 37 °C for 50–60 min. Goat serum was removed with fine filter paper, and samples were incubated with rabbit anti-rat primary antibody to CD43 (1:1000, Invitrogen, PA5-80067) at 4 °C overnight. For negative control, primary antibody was replaced with 0.01 phosphate buffered saline (PBS) or rabbit serum. After removal from wet box and placement at room temperature for 15 min, slides were rinsed three times with PBS for 5 min each. Sheep anti-rabbit IgG secondary antibody (1:1000, SolelyBio, ZB-0311) was Alexa Fluor 488 was added as working mixture, incubated at 37 °C in wet box for 50–60 min, and then the slides were returned to room temperature for 15 min and washed three times with PBS for 5 min each. GFAP was stained with primary antibody anti-GFAP (1:250, Invitrogen, PA5-16291) and secondary antibody following the same process. Finally, DAPI staining solution was applied to slides, incubated in the dark for 10 min, and sealing tablets were added. Similarly, colocalization analysis of COL1A1 and Krt23 was performed using anti-COL1A1 (1:250, Abcam, ab309248) and anti-Krt23 (1:250, Thermo fisher scientific, MA5-55746) according to the above methods. For cell staining in flow cytometry, a viability dye, propidium iodide (PI), was used to exclude dead cells from the analysis.

## 10 × Genomics snRNA-seq
The 10 × Genomics platform employs microfluidic technology to encapsulate cell barcode beads and cells within droplets. This enables the collection of cell-coated droplets and subsequent cell rupture within the droplets, facilitating the connection of mRNA in the cells to the cell barcodes. Single-cell gel beads in emulsion (GEMs) are formed, and a cDNA library is constructed via a reverse-transcription reaction within the droplets. The sample index in the library sequence is utilized to distinguish the sample source of the target sequence.
Sample quality control was carried out using Cell Ranger, the official 10 × Genomics software incorporating STAR software [ ]. Reads were compared with the reference genome (https://cf.10xgenomics.com/supp/cell-exp/refdata-gex-mm10-2020-A.tar.gz) to obtain quality control results such as the number of high-quality cells, number of genes, and genome comparison rate in the original data for evaluating each sample's quality. Based on preliminary quality control with Cell Ranger, the experimental data underwent further quality control, and data for multiple cells, two cells, or uncombined cells were excluded from downstream analysis. In this project, the quality control standards were cells with more than 200 retained genes, more than 1000 unique molecular identifiers (UMIs), greater than 0.7 log10GenesPerUMI, less than 5% mitochondrial UMIs (transcripts), and fewer than 5% red blood cell genes, which were ultimately regarded as high-quality cells. Subsequently, DoubletFinder software was employed for double-cell removal and downstream analyses [ ].
Dimension reduction and cluster analysis. For dimension reduction and cluster analysis in single-nucleus RNA sequencing (snRNA-seq), one sample typically represents one batch. In this experiment, there were three batches. As all mice were purchased simultaneously, the mutual nearest neighbor (MNN) dimensionality reduction algorithm was employed to eliminate batch effects. Subsequently, based on the MNN algorithm, single-cell clustering was visualized using the UMAP algorithm. Shared nearest neighbor was utilized in the clustering algorithm to obtain the optimal cell group. Additionally, Harmony was used for dimensionality reduction.
Similarity analysis among cell populations. Regarding similarity analysis among cell populations, the degree of similarity between two cell populations was determined by calculating the Pearson's correlation coefficient for the average gene expression between them.
Cell-type identification. Cell-type annotation was performed using a single package based on the ImmGen reference dataset [ , ]. The correlation between a single-cell reference expression profile dataset and the cell expression profile to be identified was calculated, and the cells were annotated as the cell type with the highest correlation to the reference dataset.
Marker gene identification. For marker gene identification, PRESTO was utilized to assess the difference between a specified cell population and all other cell populations. The screening conditions were logic threshold > 0 and min.pct (gene expression ratio in all cells) > 0.25 to obtain all marker genes in each cell population. In this study, with logfc > 0 as the threshold for marker gene identification, the aim was to obtain more differentially expressed genes and systematically analyze the gene expression of each cell group in adipose tissue in the Ctrl, Mid_Ob, and Ob groups.
Differential gene enrichment analysis. Regarding differential gene enrichment analysis, the Gene Ontology (GO) database was employed to classify and annotate genes and gene products in three aspects: biological process (BP) encompassing numerous biological processes, molecular function (MF) involving realized molecular functions, and cellular component (CC) comprising various subcellular components. Through GO enrichment analysis of differentially expressed genes (DEGs), it is possible to determine which biological functions or cellular pathways are associated with the DEGs under different conditions. The Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) database was used for pathway analysis of differentially expressed protein-coding genes, and the hypergeometric distribution test was employed to calculate the significance of differential gene enrichment in each pathway entry.
Differential gene protein network-interaction analysis. The Search Tool for the Retrieval of Interacting Genes/Proteins (STRING; https://string-db.org/) database harbors predicted functional correlations among proteins [ ]. Functional links between proteins can frequently be inferred from genomic correlations of the genes encoding them. For instance, groups of genes essential for the same function often exhibit similar species coverage and are typically located in proximity in the genome (in prokaryotes); gene fusion events are common with such genes. Hence, the STRING database serves as a global resource for predicting and analyzing these correlations. In this analysis, we obtained the interactive relationships of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) by integrating them in the STRING database and constructing an interaction network map.

## Flow cytometry
Following the method of Qian Wang et al. [ ], single-cell suspension was obtained by passing through a 100-μm cell strainer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Cell surface markers were analyzed using fluorescent-coupled antibodies from eBioscience. Flow cytometry was carried out using BD LSRFortessa acoustic focused cytometry. BD LSRFortessa software v3.1.2 was used for data analysis, and FlowJo v9.9.6 (Tree Star, Inc.) was employed for quantitative analyses. Cell surface and intracellular staining was performed using the following fluorescent antibodies: Neutrophils were stained with anti-Ly6G-PE (Cat No. 12-9668-82, eBioscience) and anti-CD11b-FITC (Cat No. 11-4031-82, eBioscience), macrophages with anti-F4/80-APC (Cat No. 12-0112-82, eBioscience) and anti-CD11b-PerCP-Cy5.5 (Cat No. 45-4031-80, eBioscience). After cell straining, red blood cells were lysed using ACK buffer (150 mM NH4Cl, 10 mM KHCO3, 0.1 mM EDTA) for 5 min at room temperature. Dead cells were excluded by propidium iodide (PI) staining (1 μg/mL, 5 min on ice). Antibodies were diluted in FACS buffer (PBS + 2% FBS) and incubated with cells for 30 min at 4 °C in the dark.

## Statistical analysis
All data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation or standard error of the mean (SEM). GraphPad Prism 9.0 (RRID: SCR_002798) was employed for mapping and all statistical analyses. Differences were evaluated using t-tests or one-way or two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey's multiple-comparison test. A P-value < 0.05 was considered significant.

## Results

## Mouse body weight, biochemical indices in blood, oil red O staining of liver tissue sections, and neuron Nissl staining results
Obese individuals can induce changes in the adipose tissue (AT) microenvironment, leading to inflammation and a reduction in neurons [ ]. In this study, lean mice were used as controls to understand the alterations in the AT microenvironment at different obesity stages. In our dynamic obesity model, a 10% increase in body weight was regarded as the obesity transition state, and a 20% increase was considered obese [ ]. An obese mouse model was successfully established by feeding a high-fat diet (60% adipose), and changes in indicators such as TC, TG, HDL, LDL, IL-6, and TNFα were measured through blood sampling every two weeks (Supplementary Fig.  & Supplementary Fig.  ). Compared to the Ctrl group, the body weight (P < 0.01), Lee’s index (P < 0.01), body adipose ratio (P < 0.01), serum TC (P < 0.01), TG (P < 0.01), LDL-C (P < 0.01), IL-6, and TNF-α (P < 0.01) were significantly elevated in the Mid_Ob group, whereas HDL-C (P < 0.01) was significantly reduced. The Oil red O staining experimental results showed that the cells in liver tissue sections exhibited obvious orange adipose drops. Compared to the Mid_Ob group, the body weight (P < 0.01), Lee's index (P < 0.05), body adipose ratio (P < 0.01), serum TC (P < 0.01), TG (P < 0.05), LDL-C (P < 0.01), IL-6, and TNF-α (P < 0.01) were significantly increased in the Ob group, whereas HDL-C (P < 0.01) was significantly reduced. The number of orange lipid droplets in tissue sections of the Ob group was also elevated (Supplementary Fig.  ), and the cell number gradually increased from the Ctrl group to the Ob group. The number of neurons decreased continuously with the formation and development of obesity according to the neuronal Nissl-type staining results (Supplementary Fig.  ), consistent with the findings of previous studies [ , ].
 

## SnRNA-seq revealed that the expression of inflammatory factors in AT is upregulated with the development of obesity
Although AT secretes various hormones and cytokines, the timing of proinflammatory cytokine secretion and the sequence between the development of obesity and the onset of inflammation remains unclear. Among the differentially expressed genes we mainly screened the interleukin family (IL-1β, IL-6) and tumor necrosis factor family (TNF-α) as representative inflammatory factors.
SnRNA-seq of AT revealed that the inflammatory factors did not change significantly in the Ctrl compared to Mid_Ob mice (Supplementary Table 1); however, the inflammatory factors increased significantly in the AT from Mid_Ob to Ob mice rose significantly (Supplementary Table 2). Although our results suggest that the secretion of inflammatory cytokines increases as the adipose cells grow larger, whether inflammation occurs later in the development of obesity remains unknown.

## SnRNA-seq Atlas of AT cells
To further understand the factors that regulate inflammation during obesity, we performed snRNA-seq on mouse eWAT in the Ctrl, Mid_Ob, and Ob groups (Fig.  a). Single-cell nucleus transcriptomic sequencing showed that the number of high-quality cells for quantitative quality control of each sample from Cell Ranger ranged from 5021 to 14,621. After quality control (i.e., the elimination of double, multiple, and apoptotic cells), the number of cells ultimately obtained cells ranged from 4442 to 11,672 (Supplementary Table 3). The percentage of gene numbers in unit UMIs (log10GenesPerUMI) and the percent Mito are shown in Supplementary Fig.  a, b.
 The cells were divided into 18 groups after dimensional reduction clustering. The different colors represent different cell subgroups, and the number of cells in each is listed on the right (Fig.  b). As shown in the correlation heatmap of cell clusters, the deeper the red, the greater the correlation between the two cell subgroups where a high correlation indicates that cells are likely of the same type (Fig.  c). We observed high correlations between cell clusters 1 and 6, 7 and 17, 2 and 10, and 3 and 5. Therefore, we hypothesized that these highly correlated cellular subsets were of the same cell type.
18 clusters of cells were identified by cell type according to the number of different types of cells contained in each cell cluster, of which clusters 1 and 6 were identified as macrophages; clusters 3, 5, 9, and 18 as fibroblasts; clusters 7, 8, 16, and 17 as epithelial cells; and cluster 11 as T cells. Cluster 12 was identified as endothelial cells, cluster 14 as stem cells, cluster 4 as neutrophil cells, clusters 13, 14, and 15 as germ cells, cluster 2 as adipocytes, and cluster 10 as preadipocytes. Each line represents the cell-type annotation from the reference dataset, and each list shows the identified cells. The greater the color intensity, the greater the correlation, indicating that the identified cell type was most similar to the reference data cell type (Fig.  d).
Three time points were selected for the potential onset of obesity in mice and found that the fibroblasts in the fifth cluster accounted for the largest proportion in the Ctrl group, followed by those in clusters 1 and 2. In the Mid_Ob group, the ninth cluster accounted for the largest proportion, followed by fibroblasts, followed by clusters 2 and 8. In the Ob group, the first cluster accounted for the largest proportion, followed by macrophages and clusters 2 and 4 (Fig.  e). Therefore, we speculated that macrophages and fibroblasts are critical to mouse eWAT and are closely related to the onset of inflammation (Fig.  f, g).

## DEG analysis revealed that the inflammatory response in eWAT is significantly enhanced when obesity reaches a certain degree level
We next investigated whether inflammation occurred at the beginning of obesity development. Our results demonstrated that, contrasting with the Ctrl group, the number of DEGs in the Mid_Ob group was 707, including 317 were upregulated and 390 were downregulated DEGs. Contrasting with the Mid_Ob group, the number of DEGs in the Ob group was 449, including 373 were upregulated and 82 were downregulated DEGs. The number of DEGs in the Ob group, as compared with the Ctrl group, was 1146, including 467 were upregulated and 679 were downregulated DEGs (FC > 1.5, P < 0.05) (Supplementary Table 4). We did not uncover any genes related to the inflammatory and immune responses in the top 20 up/downregulated DEGs from the Ctrl to Mid_Ob groups (Fig.  a). Analysis of the top 20 up/downregulated DEGs in the Ob group, as compared with the Mid_Ob group, revealed that Cd36, Fyb, Alcam, and Cnksr2 were related to inflammation and immune responses (Fig.  b). The relevant signaling pathways included the ECM receptor interaction and PPAR, AMPK, Rap1, T-cell receptor, and chemokine signaling pathways.

## Differential gene enrichment analysis revealed that the immune system is activated and neuronal activity is reduced in AT when obesity reaches a certain degree
We assumed that inflammation occurred in the middle stages of obesity, and that when obesity reached a certain degree (Mid_Ob), the body began to develop an inflammatory response. In the GO and KEGG enrichment analyses, the Mid_Ob group showed enhanced lipid metabolism compared to the Ctrl group, and lipid metabolism was also closely related to the signaling pathways (Fig.  a). Immune system processes, inflammatory responses, and lipid binding were upregulated in the Ob group compared to those in the Mid_Ob group; however, signaling pathways such as glycation metabolism and fatty acid biosynthesis were downregulated. Lipid metabolism was chiefly enhanced during the progression from the Ctrl group (i.e., non-obese) weight to overweight in the Mid_Ob group; however, we observed no enhancement of the immune process or inflammatory response. Instead, the immune process and inflammatory response were significantly enhanced during the progression from Mid_Ob to Ob (Fig.  b). Inflammation may lead to changes in the proportion of cells, such as fibroblasts and macrophages. From Ctrl to Mid_Ob, from Mid_Ob to Ob, the number of neurons decreased, and the number of inflammatory cells increased (Fig.  c).

## Fibroblasts in AT change with the development of obesity
We next sought to determine the cells and cytokines that were responsible for inflammation during Mid_Ob. 1758 fibroblasts were identified in the Ctrl group, accounting for 32.1% of the total; 1143 in the Mid_Ob group, accounting for 24.7%; and 2499 in the Ob group, accounting for 19.3%. While fibroblasts accounted for the largest number of cellular subsets, the number of fibroblasts tended to decrease, and the proportion of total cells increased during obesity formation and development (Fig.  a).

## Marker gene identification and enrichment analysis
We determined that the ninth cell cluster changed significantly at three time points during the development of obesity and thus hypothesized that this cluster is crucial for obesity development. Two cluster 9 cells were present in the Ctrl group, accounting for approximately 0.04%, while the Mid_Ob group demonstrated 689 cluster 9 cells, accounting for approximately 15.51%. We did not discern any cluster 9 cells in the Ob group (Fig.  b). Moreover, human protein mapping showed that Krt23 was distributed in fibroblasts of adipose tissue (Figs.  c, ).
 

## Macrophages, neutrophils, and epithelial cells in AT change with the development of obesity
969 macrophages were identified in the Ctrl group, accounting for 18.4%; 687 in the Mid_Ob group, accounting for 15.5%; and 4325 in the Ob group, accounting for 37.1%. The numbers and proportions of macrophages increased significantly in the Ob group. Moreover, the first and sixth clusters were macrophages, and the numbers in these clusters increased significantly in the Ob group (Fig.  a, b).
 94 neutrophils were identified in the Ctrl group, accounting for 1.8%; 67 in the Mid_Ob group, accounting for 1.5%; and 2,221 in the Ob group, accounting for 19.0%. The numbers and proportions of neutrophils increased significantly in the Ob group (Fig.  c, d).
396 epithelial cells were identified in the Ctrl group, accounting for 7.5%; 1193 in the Mid_Ob group, accounting for 26.9%; and 498 in the Ob group, accounting for 4.3%. Thus, epithelial cells increased first and then decreased in AT, accounting for the largest proportion in the Mid_Ob group (Supplementary Fig. 5). However, we found no significant differences in the expression of KC, MIP-2, or G-CSF in this study. Although epithelial cells comprise a large proportion of AT, epithelial cells may not be the key to AT formation in obese animals. Next we analyzed the crosstalk among fibroblasts, macrophages, and neutrophils.

## Intimate crosstalk among fibroblasts, neutrophils, and macrophages in the TIME
To explore the crosstalk between neutrophils and other cells in the microenvironment of mouse epididymal white adipose tissue (eWAT), we employed CellPhoneDB, an interactive web application that infers cellular interactions based on ligand–receptor signaling [ ]. Our data suggested that ATAKFs attracted neutrophils via the BMP8B-PLAUR, CSF1-SIRPA, CXCL12-CXCR4, FAM3C-FFAR2, FAM3C-HLA-C, GRN-TNFRSF1A, and GRN-TNFRSF1B axes, whereas macrophages were recruited by neutrophils through the CCL3L3-CCR1, L1B-L1RI, L1RN-L1RI, LTB-LTBR, NAMPT-P2RY6, TNFSF14-LTBR, TNFSF14- TNFRSF14, VEGFA-EPHB2, VEGFA-FLT1, VEGFA-NRP1, and VEGFA-NRP2 axes. Plaur, Sirpa, Tnfrsf1b, Vsir, and Cd44 are marker genes of central granulocytes [ ] and were highly expressed in the Ob group compared to the Mid-Ob group. Neutrophils recruited by ATAKFs into the AT of obese mice also began to recruit macrophages (Table  ). 
 

## Flow cytometry analysis of neutrophils and macrophages
According to the flow cytometry analysis results, the proportions of neutrophils in the Ctrl, Mid_Ob, and Ob groups were 2.62%, 3.29%, and 22.6%, while those of macrophages were 11.2%, 17.5%, and 43.5%, respectively. In the Ctrl, Mid_Ob, and Ob groups, the macrophage/neutrophil proportion in AT was 4.27, 5.32, and 1.92, respectively (Fig.  ).
 

## Discussion
Obesity in individuals is a chronic metabolic disorder with a rapidly rising incidence in recent years. Additionally, obesity causes various diseases as it can alter the quantity and function of diverse types of immune cells in adipose tissue (AT), leading to inflammation [ ]. In fact, the white adipose tissue (WAT) of obese mice is infiltrated by various immune cells, resulting in low-grade inflammation that induces WAT browning [ , ]. Immune cells determine the tissue microenvironment of AT, maintain immune cell homeostasis, and contribute to metabolic health [ , ]. Numerous immune cells such as macrophages/monocytes, eosinophils, neutrophils, group 2 innate lymphoid cells (ILC2s), T and B cells, dendritic cells, and NK cells play multiple roles in the regulation of immune homeostasis and inflammation [ ]. Our research team previously demonstrated ferroptosis in some anti-inflammatory immune cells in AT, leading to an increase in proinflammatory immune cells that generated systemic inflammation and insulin resistance and ultimately contributed to the development of obesity [ ]. In this study, we found that obesity significantly modified the immune microenvironment (TIME) of epididymal white adipose tissue (eWAT) in mice but had no such effect in the early stages of obesity.
Although we did not uncover any significant alterations in the expression of inflammatory factors in AT from Ctrl to Mid_Ob, the expression levels of inflammatory factors increased significantly with the evolution of obesity when the ATAKFs appeared at the Mid_Ob stage. Previous studies have indicated that cytokines such as leptin and adiponectin are produced in AT and exert a certain anti-inflammatory effects [ , ]. AT can also produce numerous inflammatory cytokines, such as TNF-α, MCP-1, IL-1, and IL-6, which can promote insulin resistance, recruit macrophages to AT, and produce local and systemic inflammation [ , ]. When the body possesses more hypertrophic adipose cells, it usually contains supernumerary proinflammatory cytokines [ ].
We found that the number of AT cells (primarily in the form of fibroblasts) changed as a result of obesity. Fibroblasts support homeostasis and are important in maintaining the structural integrity of most tissues [ ]. Fibroblasts play various roles in inflammatory diseases, including rheumatoid arthritis (RA) [ ], and are major sources of IL-6. Many cytokines and inflammatory mediators have been revealed to significantly induce IL-6 expression in fibroblasts (including TNF; IL-17 and IL-1β; LPS; and IFNs-α, -β, and -γ), and fibroblasts constitute the main source of inflammatory cytokines and chemokines in inflammatory diseases, such as RA. Fibroblast activation and phenotypic control also greatly depend on the inflammatory environment [ ]. Therefore, we hypothesize that ATAKFs are critical to the pathogenesis of inflammation engendered by obesity.
We discovered that the proportion of fibroblasts in the Ctrl and Mid_Ob groups comprised nearly half of the total number of cells and that the proportion of fibroblasts in the Ob group was slightly reduced. The proportion of neutrophils in the Ctrl and Mid_Ob groups was smaller, whereas the proportion of neutrophils in the Ob group was significantly increased. Keratin 23 (Krt23+), whose functions include structural molecular activity and the formation of intermediate filaments, is involved in the estrogen signaling pathway and is mainly enriched in neutrophils in AT. X-linked Kx blood group-related 7 (Xkr7), as determined by GO analysis, is involved in the formation of membranes, phagocytosis of apoptotic cells, exposure of phosphatidylserine to the apoptotic cell surface, and development of the apoptotic process. Neurotensin receptor 1 (Ntsr1) is involved in membrane formation and the regulation of ester synthesis and participates in neural processes. The signaling pathways involved include the calcium signaling and neural active ligand‒receptor interaction pathways. Ntsr1 mainly recruits macrophages and neutrophils in visceral adipose. Major urinary protein 4 (Mup4) participates in the synthesis of small molecules, regulation of glucose homeostasis, and regulation of lipid metabolism. However, the number and proportion of fibroblasts carrying the marker gene Krt23+ increased statistically, with this gene significantly expressed only in the ninth cell cluster. Therefore, we recognize that Krt23+ is the specific keratin found in fibroblasts in AT, and studies have depicted HFDs as more likely to lead to higher Krt23+ levels [ ]. We therefore preliminarily identified Krt23+ as the prominent fibroblast marker in the AT of obese mice and demonstrated that this gene was only expressed in fibroblasts. Conventional scientific wisdom states that Krt23+ is essential for neutrophil recruitment. Investigators have principally divided the krt degradation process into the following four steps: a complete stage with no macrophage infiltration with krt, but a few neutrophils persisting; a neutrophil recruitment stage with no macrophage infiltration; a neutrophil-dominant stage, encompassing a small and dense accumulation of macrophages, neutrophil infiltration, and strong K17 positivity; and a macrophage-dominant stage, with CD68-, mononuclear CD163-, and multinuclear CD204-positive macrophages that show phagocytosis and isolation [ ].
Neutrophils are the first cell type to enter AT during the development of obesity and play an obvious role in AT. Neutrophils have been shown to be rapidly recruited to WAT within 3 days after HFD feeding, and this elevation remained unaltered for 90 days after HFD feeding [ – ]. While Elgazar-Carmon et al. demonstrated that obesity facilitated the infiltration of neutrophils in AT and that the number of neutrophils in the tissue increased significantly during the first week of high-fat treatment, the number of neutrophils in the AT plateaued after 1 week, as revealed by flow cytometry. This finding suggests that neutrophils in AT assume different roles at different stages of obesity. For example, neutrophils are important in regulating AT inflammation during the early stages of obesity [ ], and knockout of neutrophil elastase reduces the proportion of neutrophils and macrophages in AT, attenuating the tissue inflammation characteristic of mouse obesity following HFD feeding [ ]. However, neutrophils did not enter the AT before ATAKFs appeared at the Mid_Ob stage, instead, only appearing only after obesity had reached a certain level (Mid_Ob).
When we compared the Ob and Mid_Ob group, we observed that the CCL2, CCL6, and CCL9 genes in the former were significantly upregulated in fibroblasts. It has been suggested that macrophages replace neutrophils when the chemokines CCL2, CCL3, and CCL5 stimulate the release of macrophages into wounds [ ]. Additionally, the chemokine C–C motif chemokine ligand (CCL)−2 and CXC motif chemokine ligand (CXCL)−10 have been reported to be associated with Th1 immune responses [ ]. Based on the above analysis, investigators have confirmed that ATAKFs not only recruit neutrophils at the initial stage of obesity but also secrete CCL2-, CCL6-, and CCL9-related cytokines, displaying critical functions in recruiting macrophages [ , ]. We also noted that the proportion of macrophages in AT increased significantly from the Ctrl group to the Ob group, reaching 56.1%. In conclusion, ATAKFs are crucial for importance to macrophage recruitment.
We also discerned that the proportions of epithelial cells in AT changed significantly, rising from 7.5% to 26.9%, before falling to 4.3%. However, we did not further investigate the functions of epithelial cells in AT from obese mice. The proportions of T, epithelial, stem, and germ cells in the three stages of obesity were small, and alterations in these cell types were not obvious. Thus, we did not further explore these cell types. This study focused on changes in the immune microenvironment and did not define and study changes in the number of adipose cells.
In our study, the ratio of macrophages/neutrophils in AT first increased and then decreased, consistent with our analysis of snRNA sequencing data, possibly because the proportion of macrophages and neutrophils increased significantly with the development of obesity.
We also identified that Gm5916, Gm48697, 9230102K24Rik, Xkr7, Ntsr1, Gm48597, Krt23+, Gm3434, Mup4, and Gm4861 as marker genes in fibroblasts. However, as these types of fibroblasts were not found in previous studies; they were not subjected to further analysis. We reevaluated the function of Krt23+ fibroblasts and found that they only appeared in the second stage with no significant change in the immune microenvironment of eWAT before their appearance. After the appearance of these cells, an inflammatory reaction was observed in AT. Suggesting that Krt23 and other genes play an important role in regulating inflammation. Next we functionally analyzed the top 10 marker genes in fibrogenesis and found that only Krt23 played a role in tissue immune microenvironment regulation. This finding together with the keratin functional study results indicate that this protein functions in neutrophil recruitment. Systematic analysis of single-cell sequencing data further revealed that Krt23+ fibroblasts play an important role in AT formation. Possibly due to differences in sequencing depth, the number of cells obtained, and the dimensionality reduction resolution, previous studies have yielded relatively few cell clusters. Sarvari, A. K. et al. discovered that fibro-adipogenic progenitors decreased with obesity formation using COL1A1 and DCN, which is consistent with the changes in the number of fibroblasts in this study [ ]. However, in the study by Margo P. Emont et al., fibroblasts were not defined [ ]. Our data from this study have been made publicly available in the NCBI GEO database, and they are accessible for further acquisition and analysis by relevant researchers, and this study established a reference model and general framework for studying the mechanisms, biomarkers, and therapeutic targets [ , ]. In further experiments, we will further verify the hypothesis proposed in this paper by injecting an AAV virus that interferes interfering with Krt23 into the AT of the epididymis.
The present study design has the following scientific limitations:①Single observation time-point risk: Using a 10% body weight threshold above the control group as the sole observation node may insufficiently capture the spatiotemporal dynamics of Krt23⁺ fibroblast changes. This approach fails to cover the continuous pathological stages of obesity progression, potentially missing molecular events in critical windows.②Age bias confounding effect: This study did not systematically evaluate the potential impact of intergroup age differences (e.g., a 2-week age gap between the Mid-Ob and other groups) caused by experimental timing on cellular phenotypes. This time-age confounding factor may interfere with the accurate interpretation of mechanisms underlying Krt23⁺population changes. ③Inadequate resolution of phenotypic heterogeneity: Only the characteristics of Krt23⁺ subpopulations in the Mid-Ob group were described, without in-depth analysis of the molecular mechanisms behind their disappearance in the Ob group [ – ]. Such heterogeneity may be closely associated with adipose tissue microenvironment remodeling (e.g., differences in fibrosis degree and immune cell infiltration). ④Lack of functional localization: This study did not verify the spatial association between Krt23⁺cells and crown-like structures through colocalization analysis, precluding confirmation of the direct role of this subpopulation in lipid clearance or inflammatory regulation and limiting the in-depth interpretation of its pathophysiological significance.

## Conclusion
Mouse with obesity leads to significant modifications in the AT microenvironment, principally in immune and neurologic functions. When ATAKFs appear at the Mid_Ob stage, the inflammatory response is significantly upregulated, and neurologic function is significantly reduced. We herein classified mouse eWAT into 18 cellular subsets and seven types of cells, including fibroblasts, macrophages, and T cells, endothelial cells, germ cells, and epithelial cells. Fibroblasts accounted for the largest number of cellular subsets, followed by macrophages. In the analysis of the subsets of fibroblasts and macrophages, ATAKFs only appeared only when obesity reached a certain level (Mid_Ob). Furthermore, cell–cell crosstalk analysis revealed the central role of ATAKFs and their ability to regulate macrophages and neutrophils. We consider that the internal mechanism(s) underlying the TIME changes in the eWAT of nutrient-obese mice induced by obesity involve ATAKFs, which specifically recruit neutrophils, secrete cytokines such as CCL2, CCL6, and CCL9, and recruit macrophages (Fig.  ).
