## Abstract

## Background
The prognostic value of D-glucuronyl C5-epimerase (GLCE) and mast cell infiltration in Ewing sarcoma (ES) has not been well specified and highlighted, which may facilitate survival prediction and treatment.

## Methods
Several qualified datasets were downloaded from the GEO website. Common differentially expressed genes between normal subjects and ES patients in GSE17679, GSE45544, and GSE68776 were identified and screened by multiple algorithms to find hub genes with prognostic value. The prognostic value of 64 infiltrating cells was also explored. A prognostic model was established and then validated with GSE63155 and GSE63156. Finally, functional analysis was performed.

## Results
GLCE and mast cell infiltration were screened as two indicators for a prognostic model. The Kaplan‒Meier analysis showed that patients in the low GLCE expression, mast cell infiltration and risk score groups had poorer outcomes than patients in the high GLCE expression, mast cell infiltration and risk score groups, both in the training and validation sets. Scatter plots and heatmaps also indicated the same results. The concordance indices and calibration analyses indicated a high prediction accuracy of the model in the training and validation sets. The time-dependent receiver operating characteristic analyses suggested high sensitivity and specificity of the model, with area under the curve values between 0.76 and 0.98. The decision curve analyses suggested a significantly higher net benefit by the model than the treat-all and treat-none strategies. Functional analyses suggested that glycosaminoglycan biosynthesis-heparan sulfate/heparin, the cell cycle and microRNAs in cancer were upregulated in ES patients.

## Conclusions
GLCE and mast cell infiltration are potential prognostic indicators in ES. GLCE may affect the proliferation, angiogenesis and metastasis of ES by affecting the biosynthesis of heparan sulfate and heparin.

## Introduction
Ewing sarcoma (ES), arising in the bone and soft tissue, is the second most common primary bone malignancy in children, adolescents and young adults [ , ]. It is also a highly aggressive malignancy with a poor prognosis in patients with recurrent and metastatic tumors. These patients have a dismal 5-year survival rate of less than 30% [ , , ]. Moreover, approximately 20–25% of newly diagnosed patients already have metastases [ ]. Currently, ES is known as a genetic translocation-driven malignancy characterized by homogeneous small round cells with chromosomal translocations, and in 85% of cases, a translocation t (11; 22) (q24; q12) of EWSR1 to FLI1 is detected [ , , , ]. Pathological diagnosis of ES mainly depends on molecular pathology by FISH analysis, RT‒PCR, or even next-generation sequencing [ , ]. In addition, immunohistochemical staining for CD99 is also widely used. The management of ES varies slightly in different countries, and the general treatment in North America and Europe is induction chemotherapy followed by surgery and/or radiation therapy and then by additional adjuvant chemotherapy [ ]. In addition, additional local treatment is needed if any metastatic site is present. It was over 30 years since intensive chemotherapy was introduced and improved the outcome for ES patients. Frustratingly, although the survival of patients with localized tumors has improved significantly from 10% to approximately 70%, the survival of patients with disseminated and recurrent tumors after standard treatment has not improved for decades [ , , ]. Even myeloablative chemotherapy with stem cell transplantation and ES-targeted CAR-T cells have not shown clinical benefits [ ]. Therefore, novel therapies that take into account prognostic factors are reasonable strategies for patients with metastatic and recurrent disease [ , , ].
With the development of bioinformatics, the genetic heterogeneity of ES tumors can be better analyzed and interpreted. Many novel biomarkers and therapeutic targets have been identified in ES by bioinformatics methods, such as NSUN7 [ ], STAG2 [ ], ATG2B [ ], and WSB1 [ ]. However, more biomarkers are still needed since improving the prognosis of patients with disseminated and recurrent ES is a complex problem that has not yet been well specified. A better understanding of the genes with prognostic value and their corresponding mechanisms can provide more possibilities for the discovery of novel therapeutic targets. Our study can also contribute to the genetic understanding of ES and may help to improve the survival of patients with ES.
In addition, accumulating evidence has shown that infiltrating immune cells in the tumor microenvironment play an important role in tumorigenesis, progression, invasion and metastasis [ ]. In addition, immunotherapy has made great progress in cancer treatment in recent decades, and durable clinical responses have been obtained in many cancers [ ]. However, effective immunotherapies for ES remain to be developed [ ]. Therefore, it is also important to explore immune cell infiltration in the ES microenvironment, which has not yet been well clarified and may also contribute to the treatment of ES.

## Materials and methods
2.1.

## Data collection
Datasets with a sample size of more than 30 and containing whole exon/genome RNA expression data of both Ewing sarcoma patients and normal subjects were searched in the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/gds). Only GSE17679, GSE68776, and GSE45544 were determined to be qualified. The GSE17679 dataset included expression (Affymetrix Human Genome U133 Plus 2.0 Array), clinical and survival data of 64 ES samples and expression data of 18 normal samples submitted by the Laboratory of Cytomolecular Genetics, University of Helsinki, Finland on Aug 17, 2009. The GSE68776 dataset included expression data (Affymetrix Human Exon 1.0 ST Array [transcript (gene) version]) of 32 ES and 33 normal tissue samples submitted by the University of Michigan, USA, on May 11, 2015. The GSE45544 dataset included expression data (Affymetrix Human Exon 1.0 ST Array [transcript (gene) version]) of 14 ES and 22 normal tissue samples submitted by the University of Michigan, USA, on May 11, 2015. Because GSM1108349 in GSE45544 was derived from peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) rather than tissue bulks, this sample was excluded from our analysis. These three datasets were used to screen common differentially expressed genes (DEGs) and common differentially infiltrating immune cells between normal subjects and ES patients.
In addition, three datasets (GSE17679, GSE63155 and GSE63156) with the largest sample size containing whole exon/genome RNA expression data of ES tissues in the GEO database were also downloaded. The common set GSE17679 was used as the training set, and the other two were used as validation sets. The GSE63155 dataset included expression (Affymetrix Human Exon 1.0 ST Array [transcript (gene) version]), clinical and survival data of 46 ES tissue samples from the COG Biorepository in Columbus, Ohio, submitted by University of Michigan, USA on Nov 10, 2014. The GSE63156 dataset included expression (Affymetrix Human Exon 1.0 ST Array [transcript (gene) version]), clinical and survival data of 39 ES tissue samples from the EuroEwing tumor biorepository submitted by University of Michigan, USA on Nov 10, 2014.
The expression data of these sets were all normalized data. While different batches were found in some sets, the “removebatcheffect” function of the “Limma” package was used to remove batch effects [ ]. After removing the batch effect, samples with abnormal gene expression profiles in both boxplots and density plots were identified as outliers and excluded [ ]. 2.2.

## Identification of common DEGs and prognostic genes
The “Limma” package is a popular package for gene discovery through differential expression analyses of microarray and RNA-seq data [ ]. DEGs between the normal subjects and ES patients were identified by this package in the GSE17679, GSE68776, and GSE45544 sets (|log2FC| > 1 and adjusted p value < 0.05). Then, common DEGs in the three sets were selected for subsequent screening. Univariate Cox regression analysis with a p value < 0.05 was used to identify genes directly associated with the survival of ES. Kaplan‒Meier analysis was then adopted to screen potential prognostic genes with a p value < 0.05 between the high and low expression groups (stratified by median). Thereafter, least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression analysis (λ = 1 se) [ , ] and random forest (RF) feature selection [ , ] were used to screen prognostic genes. LASSO is a regression method that involves penalizing the absolute size of the regression coefficients [ , ], and RF is a commonly used machine learning algorithm that consists of multiple decision trees [ , ]. Prognostic genes confirmed by both LASSO regression analysis and RF feature selection were selected as the final genes for a prognostic model.

2.3.

## Calculation of immune cell infiltration scores and identification of their prognostic values in ES patients
“xCell” is a gene signature-based method that can perform cell type enrichment analysis for 64 immune and stromal cell types from gene expression data [ ]. Therefore, the “xCell” package was adopted to calculate the infiltration scores of 64 immune and stromal cells, which could reflect their relative abundance in samples. These infiltrating cells were screened by univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses (p value < 0.05) in GSE17679. Then, Boruta feature selection, a feature-selecting wrapper built around the random forest classification algorithm, was used to explore the prognostic value of the infiltrating cells by importance score. Thereafter, the relative abundance of the prognostic infiltrating immune cells was explored in the GSE17679, GSE68776 and GSE45544 sets. Common differentially infiltrating cells between normal and ES tissues with a p value < 0.05 by the Kruskal test in the three sets were selected as the final cells for a prognostic model.

2.4.

## Establishment of a prognostic model and its evaluation in the training set
A prognostic model was established by the final genes and infiltrating immune cells, and the risk score was calculated by summing the product of the values of each indicator and the corresponding coefficients. Kaplan‒Meier (KM) analysis was used to preliminarily evaluate the prognostic value of the final genes, immune infiltrating cells and risk scores in GSE17679. Characteristics of samples in the high- and low-risk score groups were explored by scatter plots and heatmaps. The Cox proportional hazards model was visualized by a nomogram, which could be used to predict the survival of patients. The concordance index (C-index) and calibration analysis were used to evaluate the prediction accuracy of the model. Time-dependent receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis was used to evaluate the prediction sensitivity and specificity of the model. Finally, decision curve analysis (DCA) was used to evaluate the net benefit patients could obtain from the model and other strategies.

2.5.

## Validation of the model in two independent external validation sets
The risk score was also calculated by the final genes and infiltrating immune cells. Then, KM analysis, scatter plots and heatmaps were used to evaluate the prognostic value of these indicators in the GSE63155 and GSE63156 sets. The Cox proportional hazards models were visualized by nomograms. Finally, the prediction accuracy and discriminatory capacity of the model in validation sets were evaluated by the C-index, calibration analysis, time-dependent ROC analysis and DCA.

2.6.

## Functional analysis of the DEGs between the normal and tumor groups
Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analyses were performed to explore the functional enrichment of the common DEGs in the GSE17679, GSE68776 and GSE45544 sets (ranked based on the p value). Then, gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) in each set was performed to find common down- or upregulated pathways and common pathways containing genes of interest in ES patients (ranked based on the adjusted p value).

2.7.

## Functional analysis of the hub gene-correlated DEGs
Hub gene-correlated genes were identified by the “cor.test” function in R with a p value < 0.05 in GSE17679, and the top correlated genes were visualized by a lollipop plot. Then, functional enrichment of the hub gene-correlated genes was explored by GO and KEGG enrichment analyses and GSEA to identify coregulated pathways in ES patients. A protein‒protein interaction (PPI) network analysis of the proteins encoded by the top correlated genes was performed on the STRING website (https://cn.string-db.org/) with an interaction score ≥0.4 [ ] and visualized by Cytoscape software.

2.8.

## Histological and immunohistochemical staining
Toluidine blue staining was used to stain mast cells, and immunohistochemical staining of D-glucuronyl C5-epimerase (GLCE) was used to identify the expression of GLCE in the ES and the corresponding adjacent tissues. ES and the corresponding adjacent paraffin-embedded tissues were collected from Jiangxi Provincial Hospital, which was approved by the Ethics Committee of Jiangxi Provincial People's Hospital (no. 2022–059). The paraffin-embedded tissues were sliced (HistoCore BIOCUT, Leica, Germany), and sections were deparaffinized with xylene and hydrated with gradient alcohol. For mast cell staining, sections were stained with toluidine blue. For immunohistochemical staining of GLCE, sections were then boiled in antigen retrieval buffer (1 mM EDTA, pH 8.0) for 20 min, treated with 3% hydrogen peroxide to eliminate endogenous peroxidase for 10 min, blocked with 5% bovine serum albumin (Servicebio, Wuhan, China; https://www.servicebio.cn/goodsdetail?id=7702, catalog number: GC305010) for 30 min, incubated with primary antibody (1:200, CUSABIO, Houston, TX, USA, https://www.cusabio.com/Polyclonal-Antibody/GLCE-Antibody-1028905.html, catalog number: CSB-PA009492LA01HU) at 4 °C overnight and with secondary antibody (1:100, Servicebio, Wuhan, China, https://www.servicebio.cn/goodsdetail?id=266, catalog number: GB23303) at 37 °C for 30 min, stained with 3,3′-diaminobenzidine and restained with hematoxylin. After dehydration and sealing, the sections were observed under a microscope (ZEISS Axio Lab. A1, Carl Zeiss, Germany).

2.9.

## Statistical analysis
In this study, R software v4.2.2 was used to process data and generate charts. The PPI network analyses were explored on the STRING website (https://cn.string-db.org/) (interaction score ≥0.4) and visualized by the Cytoscape software v3.9.1 [ ]. Flexible statistical methods were used for the statistical analysis.

## Basic clinical features of the training and validation sets

3.3.

## Identification of common DEGs and prognostic genes
A total of 3950, 2629 and 1610 DEGs were identified between the normal and ES tissues with an absolute log2FoldChange >1 and adjusted p value < 0.05 by the “Limma” package in GSE17679, GSE68776, and GSE45544, respectively ( A, ), and 295 common DEGs were identified. Then, 121 common DEGs were identified with prognostic value by univariate Cox regression analysis in GSE17679 ( B). Subsequently, 83 genes with a p value < 0.05 in the KM analysis were selected for further screening. Finally, GLCE, ADGRG2, CBX5, CLEC11A, ZC3H13, VRK1, LOXHD1, RCAN2, ASPM, LMLN, ZFC3H1, CPEB2, AASDH, KDSR, EGR2, PAQR5, POU4F2, LRRC8D, STEAP3, and SYNPR were selected by LASSO regression analysis (lambda = 1se) ( C–D), and GLCE, DDB2, CENPF, CDK13, CCDC88A, and SCRN1 were selected by RF feature selection ( E–F). The common gene GLCE, identified by the two algorithms, was selected as the final gene for a prognostic model.
 
3.4.

## Calculation of the immune cell infiltration scores and identification of their prognostic values
The infiltration scores of 64 cells in normal and ES tissues in the GSE17679 set are shown in A. The relative abundance of most infiltrating immune cells was significantly different between the normal and tumor groups. CD4+ naive T cells, CD4+ T cells, CD4+ effector T (Tem) cells, CD8+ central memory T (Tcm) cells, eosinophils, erythrocytes, hematopoietic stem cells (HSCs), M2 macrophages, mast cells, natural killer T cells (NKTs), plasma cells, skeletal muscle, and T helper 2 (Th2) cells were selected by univariate Cox regression analysis with a p value < 0.05 ( B, ). Thereafter, M2 macrophages, mast cells, Th2 cells, NKTs, CD4+ Tem cells, and CD8+ Tcm cells were selected by multivariate Cox regression analysis with a p value < 0.05 ( C, ) and were all confirmed to have prognostic value by Boruta feature selection ( D–E). The importance scores of the 6 cells from high to low were mast cells, NKTs, CD8+ Tcm cells, Th2 cells, M2 macrophages, and CD4+ Tem cells, which suggested that infiltrating mast cells were most relevant to the prognosis of ES patients. In GSE17679, the relative abundances of the 6 cells between normal and ES tissues were all significantly different, with a p value < 0.01 ( A). In GSE68776, significant differences were also found in the relative abundances of mast cells, CD8+ Tcm cells, Th2 cells, and M2 macrophages between normal and ES tissues ( B). In GSE45544, only the infiltration of mast cells and CD4+ Tem cells was significantly different between the normal and ES tissues ( C). Thus, infiltrating mast cells were the only common differentially infiltrating cells between the normal and ES tissues with prognostic value in the three sets and were selected for a prognostic model along with GLCE.
 
3.5.

## Establishment of a prognostic model and its evaluation in the training set
A prognostic model was established by GLCE and infiltrating mast cells, and then the risk score was calculated (risk score = −0.6852597*GLCE−62.5097906*mast cell infiltration). The KM analysis of GLCE ( A), infiltrating mast cells ( B) and risk score ( C) in GSE17679 showed that the outcomes of patients in the groups with high and low GLCE expression, mast cell infiltration and risk scores were significantly different, with p values of 4.53e-06, 0.0022 and 4.53e-06, respectively. Patients in the high GLCE expression, mast cell infiltration and risk score groups had better outcomes than those in the low GLCE expression, mast cell infiltration and risk score groups, which suggested that these factors might be protective factors in ES patients. The characteristics of the samples in the high- and low-risk score groups of GSE17679 are shown in D. The scatter plots in D show that patients were stratified into high- and low-risk score groups by the median, and patients in the high-risk score group tended to have fewer deaths and longer overall survival times. The heatmap in D shows that the expression trend of GLCE in patients with increasing risk scores was consistent with the increasing trend of the risk score. The overall trend of the relative abundance of infiltrating mast cells in patients with increasing risk scores was consistent with the increasing trend of the risk score as well, although 9 discordant cases were observed in each group. The nomogram of the Cox proportional hazards model in GSE17679 could easily predict the survival rate of ES patients ( E). The lower the expression of GLCE, the higher the risk score and the lower the survival rate of the patients. In addition, the lower the infiltration of mast cells, the higher the risk score and the lower the survival rate of the patients. In addition, the longer length of the scale line of GLCE in the nomogram indicated its higher weight in the model. The C-index in GSE17679 was 0.764 (95% confidence interval (CI): 0.725–0.803), suggesting a high overall prediction accuracy of the model [ ]. In addition, the calibration analysis of the model showed that the colored broken lines were close to the gray dotted ideal line, suggesting a high prediction accuracy of the model at different time points ( F). The time-dependent ROC analysis showed that the area under the curve (AUC) values were 0.83, 0.83, and 0.86 in 1-, 3-, and 5-year prediction, respectively, suggesting a high prediction sensitivity and specificity of the model ( G). The DCA for 1-year prediction showed that patients could obtain a higher net benefit by the model and by GLCE- and mast infiltrating cell-only strategies than by treat-none and treat-all strategies. In addition, we found that at thresholds above approximately 7.5%, patients with the GLCE strategy had an even greater net benefit than those with the model strategy ( H).

## Functional analysis of the DEGs between the normal and tumor groups
A total of 295 common DEGs were clustered by GO and KEGG enrichment analyses. The top 3 biological processes (BP), cellular components (CC) and molecular functions (MF) clustered by GO enrichment analysis (based on p value) were negative regulation of cell cycle, mitotic sister chromatid segregation, and regulation of cell size, chromosomal region, nuclear speck, and chromosome centromeric region, and tubulin binding, microtubule binding, and DNA secondary structure binding, respectively ( A), which were mainly associated with cell cycle and cell division. The top clustered pathways by KEGG enrichment analysis were proteoglycans in cancer, transcriptional misregulation in cancer, cGMP-PKG signaling pathway, and tight junctions ( B). In addition, 2 common DEGs were clustered in the cell cycle pathway. By GSEA, the cell cycle and microRNAs in cancer were upregulated, and oxidative phosphorylation and the calcium signaling pathway were downregulated in ES patients, which were the top enriched gene sets in GSE17679 ( C, ). Glycosaminoglycan biosynthesis - heparan sulfate/heparin, the hub gene-involved pathway, was also found to be upregulated in ES patients ( C–D). D shows the top enriched pathways of the top 1000 DEGs (ranked by absolute logFC), and GLCE was enriched in the glycosaminoglycan biosynthesis - heparan sulfate/heparin pathway. In GSE68776, the top upregulated pathways were also cell cycle and microRNAs in cancer, and the top downregulated pathways were the apelin signaling pathway and relaxin signaling pathway ( A, ). Glycosaminoglycan biosynthesis - heparan sulfate/heparin was also upregulated in ES patients ( A). In this set, GLCE was also found to be a top DEG involved in glycosaminoglycan biosynthesis - heparan sulfate/heparin ( B). In GSE45544, the cell cycle and microRNAs in cancer were also the top upregulated pathways in ES patients, while the top downregulated pathways were the autophagy-animal and the apelin signaling pathway ( C, ). Glycosaminoglycan biosynthesis - heparan sulfate/heparin was also upregulated in this set, and GLCE was one of the top DEGs involved ( D). In summary, we found that in the three different sets, cell cycle, microRNAs in cancer and glycosaminoglycan biosynthesis - heparan sulfate/heparin were all upregulated in the ES group.

## Functional analysis of the hub gene-correlated DEGs
A total of 1224 GLCE-correlated DEGs were identified by the cor.test function with a p value < 0.05 in GSE17679. The top GLCE-correlated DEGs with absolute correlation values ≥ 0.7 are shown in A. The top 3 BPs, CCs and MFs clustered by the GO enrichment analysis of the 1224 correlated DEGs were energy derivation by oxidation of organic compounds, regulation of mRNA metabolic process, and glycogen biosynthetic process, nuclear speck, cell-substrate junction, and mitochondrial matrix, and actin binding, phosphatase activity, and actin filament binding, respectively ( B). In addition, the citrate cycle (TCA cycle), cell cycle, microRNAs in cancer, and Wnt signaling pathway were the top clustered pathways of interest by KEGG enrichment analysis, and the corresponding correlated DEGs involved in these pathways are also shown in C. GSEA of these genes showed that they were involved in the upregulation of microRNAs in cancer, the Wnt signaling pathway, and signaling pathways regulating the pluripotency of stem cells and the downregulation of the citrate cycle (TCA cycle) and oxidative phosphorylation ( D). The PPI network of proteins encoded by GLCE-correlated DEGs with a correlation value > 0.6 (disconnected nodes were hidden) is displayed outside the green circle in E, while the proteins potentially interacting with GCLE, also predicted by the STRING website, are located inside the green circle. In addition, JAK1, FDXR, LIAS, ALDH7A1, AMPD2, and PRKCB were hub proteins in this network, which were highly connected central proteins or key proteins connecting different subnetworks in the scale-free PPI networks. In addition, PRKCB was also found in C, which is involved in microRNAs in cancer and the Wnt signaling pathway.

## Discussion
Patients with ES are usually stratified into standard-risk, high-risk localized and very high-risk metastatic groups by metastases, histological response to chemotherapy, tumor volume and age at diagnosis, which can preliminarily predict the prognosis of a patient [ , ]. However, the genetic and molecular type may play a more crucial role in their prognosis [ ]. Most cases of ES are characterized by a chromosomal translocation that fuses a member of the FET family of proteins (encoded by FUS, EWSR1 and TAF15) with a member of the ETS transcription factor family, usually EWSR1-FLI1, and this alteration usually results in cascade reactions in cell proliferation, cell differentiation, the cell cycle, angiogenesis and apoptosis [ ]. Genes or pathways downstream of the oncogenic chimera, such as VEGF, FGF, and EphA2 receptor, are associated with angiogenesis in ES [ , ]; miR-22, miR-30a-5p, miR-145, and miR-7a are associated with cell stemness [ ]; the PKA/PPP1R1A/PP1 axis, NPY/Y5R/RhoA axis, and HDGF/ALCAM/GTPases axis are associated with the tumorigenesis and metastasis of patients [ , , ]; and Beclin-1, ATG2B and ATG4B are associated with the autophagy of ES cells [ , , ]. Therefore, genes involved in these pathways may affect the prognosis of ES patients, and it is of great significance to explore the prognostic value of the DEGs between normal subjects and ES patients, which may also be potential therapeutic targets for ES treatment.
In this study, we used multiple popular bioinformatics methods and several different datasets to screen prognostic indicators. Indicators from two different perspectives, genetic and immune cell infiltration, can also improve the accuracy and stability of prediction. In the gene screening process, the common DEGs related to prognosis were preliminarily screened by univariate Cox regression analysis and KM analysis. Further screening was performed by two machine learning algorithms (LASSO regression analysis and RF feature selection), and only genes selected by both algorithms were adopted as prognostic genes for a prognostic model, which made our findings more robust and reliable. Accordingly, infiltrating immune cells with independent prognostic value were also identified by univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses and then confirmed by Boruta feature selection in the training set. To make our results more reliable, only cells that were differentially infiltrating between normal and ES tissues in all three different cohorts were selected for a prognostic model, and only infiltrating mast cells were qualified. The differential expression of GLCE and differential infiltration of mast cells were confirmed by histological and immunohistochemical staining.
The prognostic model was first evaluated in the training set to determine its predictive value for ES. Multiple indices and analyses were adopted to evaluate the model. The KM analyses suggest that GLCE, infiltrating mast cells and risk score were of good prognostic value in ES. The survival characteristics of patients suggested that patients in the high risk score group tended to have a better prognosis than those in the low risk score group, which was consistent with the results of the KM analysis. We also found that the increasing trend of the risk score was in line with the increasing expression of GLCE, indicating its greater predictive power in the model, and this was also the reason for the same appearance of the KM plots for the risk score and GLCE. The nomogram of the model also suggested that compared with infiltrating mast cells, GLCE received a higher weight in the model. The results of the C-index and calibration analysis suggested high prediction accuracy of the model. The time-dependent ROC analysis suggested high prediction sensitivity and specificity of the model with AUC ≥0.83 in 1-, 3- and 5-year prediction. DCA suggested that the nomogram was a good strategy for clinical decision-making and that the GLCE strategy was second to it.
The predictive value of the model was successfully validated in two independent sets. The KM analyses of GLCE, infiltrating mast cells and the risk score in GSE63155 and GSE63156 also suggested the prognostic value of these indicators. Only the p value of infiltrating mast cells in the KM analysis of GSE63155 was 0.051, which might be due to the simple grouping method, differences in baseline data and the small sample size. However, we cannot dismiss its prognostic value just because of this. In addition, it is normal that the performance of the three indicators in the KM analysis of the validation sets was not as good as that in the training set but still had high prognostic value. The survival characteristics of patients in the validation set also suggested that patients in the high-risk score group tended to have a better prognosis than patients in the low-risk score group. In GSE63155, the weight of GLCE in the model was higher, whereas in GSE63156, the weight of infiltrating mast cells in the model was higher by the nomogram and KM analysis. As to why the p value of the risk score in GSE63156 did not outperform the p value of GLCE in the KM analysis, this might be due to the low fitting efficiency of the model in this dataset. In addition, both GLCE and infiltrating mast cells were found to be protective factors in the three sets. The C-index was 0.736 in GSE63155 and 0.776 in GSE63156, which suggested good overall accuracy of the model in both sets. The calibration analysis also suggested good accuracy in the prediction of different time points in GSE63155 and GSE63156. In GSE63155, the C-index and calibration analysis of the model were not as good as those in the training set but still had satisfactory prediction accuracy. The AUC values in 1- and 3-year prediction in GSE63155 and GSE63156 were just as good as those in the training set, and some were even better than those in the training set. However, in the 5-year prediction of the two validation sets, the AUC values were lower than those in the training set, with a minimum value of 0.76. The DCA in the two validation sets suggested that compared with the GLCE strategy and the infiltrating mast cell strategy, the model obtained a more stable net benefit in different sets. In summary, the model was successfully validated in two independent external sets.
GO, KEGG, GSEA and PPI network analysis of the DEGs and hub gene-correlated DEGs between normal and ES tissues was performed to investigate the potential reason for the results above. By GO and KEGG analyses, we found that most common DEGs participated in cell cycle and cell division processes, which could affect the proliferation of ES cells and result in a poor prognosis for ES patients. By GSEA, we found that the cell cycle, microRNAs in cancer and glycosaminoglycan biosynthesis - heparan sulfate/heparin were all upregulated in ES patients in all three sets, suggesting that they might play an important role in the pathogenesis and progression of ES. Our hub gene GLCE was involved in the glycosaminoglycan biosynthesis-heparan sulfate/heparin pathway, suggesting that it probably affects the prognosis of ES patients through this pathway. Functional analysis of the GLCE-correlated DEGs in GSE17679 showed that they were involved in the cell cycle, microRNAs in cancers, citrate cycle (TCA cycle), and Wnt signaling pathway, suggesting that the prognosis of ES patients might also be affected by these pathways.
It has been reported that GLCE encodes a protein that can convert d-glucuronic acid residues adjacent to N-sulfate sugar residues to l-iduronic acid residues in both maturing heparan sulfate (HS) and heparin chains in the Golgi apparatus and plays an important role in the heparan sulfate proteoglycan (HSPG) biosynthetic process [ , , , ]. HSPG is an important component of the extracellular matrix and cell surface and plays a pivotal role in cell‒cell and cell-matrix interactions by interactions between the HS-side chains and a variety of proteins, including cytokines, growth factors, and their receptors [ , ]. In addition, the heparosan-N-sulfate-glucuronate 5-epimerase activity of GLCE can determine the specificity of interactions between these glycosaminoglycans and proteins [ , , ], and as an important part of the tumor microenvironment, the composition of the extracellular matrix, especially the type and amount of extracellular matrix proteins, can affect the metastasis and angiogenesis of ES [ ]. Accumulating evidence indicates that HS and HSPGs play an important role in the proliferation, angiogenesis, metastasis, and drug resistance of multiple tumors [ , , ], and GLCE is one of the crucial enzymes that can affect the structure of HS and heparin chains [ , , ]. Several studies have indicated that GLCE, regulated by the TCF4/β-catenin complex, is significantly decreased and functions as a tumor suppressor in breast tumors [ , , ]. Tatiana Y Prudnikova et al. also found that GLCE could inhibit the proliferation of MCF7 breast cancer cells by suppressing the cell cycle, angiogenesis and metastasis pathways [ ]. The posttranscriptional regulation of GLCE was shown to be affected by miRNA-218 in human breast tissue [ ]. In addition, GLCE could downregulate several pro-angiogenic growth factors and their receptors to inhibit cell proliferation and tumor growth in small-cell lung cancer [ ]. It has also been reported that GLCE can inhibit the tumor epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition process in endometrial cancer stem cells via the MONC/miR-636/GLCE axis [ ]. In addition, GLCE can also suppress the normal vascularization of the retina, and miR-218 can release this function [ ]. However, in prostate cancer, GLCE was found to be associated with advanced disease and could promote angiogenesis [ ]. Jie Li et al. reported that GLCE could promote cell growth and inhibit the neuritogenesis of PC12 cells by SMAD/ID3 signaling [ ]. BMP/SMAD signaling was also found to be upregulated in GLCE knockout mouse embryonic fibroblast cells [ ]. In our study, we found that GLCE played a protective role in the prognosis of ES, which might be related to the antitumor effect reported in the literature. However, we also found that GLCE and the glycosaminoglycan biosynthesis-heparan sulfate/heparin pathway were upregulated in ES patients; therefore, we speculated that they might be secondary to other alterations in ES patients, such as the cell cycle and microRNAs in cancer pathways, and act as an antitumor mechanism in the body. In addition, miR-636 and miR-238 were reported to affect the expression of GLCE, but the factors upstream of GLCE in ES still need further research.
Mast cells are myeloid immune cells that mainly exist in connective tissue and have a variety of physiological functions, such as vasodilation, angiogenesis, and elimination of bacteria and parasites. In addition, they are also implicated in the pathophysiology of many disorders, including tumors [ ]. However, the role of mast cells in tumors is not well specified. The role of mast cells in different tumors is often contradictory [ , , ]. More literature supports the view that mast cells are tumor promoters that can facilitate angiogenesis, lymphangiogenesis, fibrosis, and metastasis in tumors [ , , , , , ]. Other studies have highlighted that mast cells can release mediators capable of recruiting other immune cells, such as cytotoxic T cells and NK cells, and thus act as tumor suppressors [ , , , , , ]. Recent studies have tended to divide polarized mast cells into two possible phenotypes: M1 (antitumoral) and M2 (protumoral). They also showed that the localization of infiltrating mast cells tended to correlate with patient outcomes: peritumoral mast cells often suggest a poor prognosis, whereas intratumoral mast cells might indicate a good prognosis [ ]. In ES, mast cells were almost exclusively present at the tumor-bone interface and presumed to be associated with tumor osteolysis [ ], while our study demonstrated that they were associated with a good prognosis [ ]. It has been reported that ES is a typical “cold” tumor with low immune and inflammatory cell infiltration [ ], and while CD8+ T cell infiltration was found to be associated with good prognosis, CD68+ tumor-associated macrophage infiltration was associated with poor outcome in ES [ , ]. In this study, we found that infiltrating mast cells were associated with a good prognosis in ES patients and speculated that they were more likely to be polarized mast cells of the M1 phenotype, which could recruit cytotoxic T cells and NK cells and exert antitumor effects. However, the detailed mechanism of this phenomenon still needs further research.
In summary, we found that GLCE and the glycosaminoglycan biosynthesis-heparan sulfate/heparin pathway were upregulated in ES patients and that high GLCE expression was associated with a good prognosis, presumably due to its possible influence on ES proliferation, angiogenesis, and metastasis. Infiltrating mast cells were also screened as a positive prognostic indicator in ES, which may be associated with the M1 phenotype polarization of mast cells and their ability to recruit cytotoxic T cells and NK cells.
Some limitations of this study should be noted. Due to the limited information in the primary data, the basic information on the patients is limited. In addition, our conclusions are mainly based on bioinformatics analysis and still need experimental and clinical validation, such as the use of RNA interference techniques to understand the role of GLCE in ES.

5.

## Conclusion
GLCE and mast cell infiltration are potential prognostic indicators in ES. GLCE may affect the proliferation, angiogenesis and metastasis of ES by affecting the biosynthesis of heparan sulfate and heparin.
