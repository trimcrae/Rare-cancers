## Abstract

## Purpose
Corneal endothelial cells (CECs) play a vital role in maintaining corneal transparency and function, with their hexagonal morphology being a hallmark of proper cellular packing and barrier integrity. This study investigates the functional role of miR-196a in regulating key cellular processes, including viability, cell cycle progression, stemness, mitochondrial function, and senescence.

## Methods
Human CECs were cultured and transfected with miR-196a mimics or inhibitors. Cell viability, proliferation, and cell cycle progression were assessed using the CCK-8 assay and flow cytometry. Gene expression was analyzed through real-time polymerase chain reaction and Western blotting. Mitochondrial function was evaluated using JC-1 staining and by measuring mitophagy markers. Senescence was induced using transforming growth factor β (TGF-β) and detected by senescence-associated β-galactosidase staining. Cytoskeletal changes were visualized through immunofluorescence staining. Signaling pathway activation was assessed using Western blotting and immunofluorescence.

## Results
miR-196a overexpression enhanced cell viability, promoted cell cycle progression, and upregulated stemness markers, while its inhibition had opposite effects. It increased mitochondrial membrane potential and suppressed mitophagy markers. miR-196a modulated autophagy, cytoskeletal dynamics, and inflammation-related pathways by regulating ROCK1, ROCK2, and PAD4. It activated prosurvival pathways (YAP, MAPK) and reduced p53 levels. Importantly, miR-196a mitigated TGF-β–induced senescence by improving mitochondrial function, suppressing autophagy, and modulating TGF-β signaling components.

## Conclusions
These results showed the protective role of miR-196a in maintaining cellular function and suggest its therapeutic potential for addressing aging-related diseases, fibrotic disorders, and mitochondrial dysfunction. This study underscores miR-196a as a promising target for therapies aimed at counteracting cellular senescence and promoting regenerative capacities.

## Translational Relevance
miR-196a may serve as a novel therapeutic target for preserving corneal endothelial function and preventing age-related degeneration.

## Introduction
The corneal endothelium plays a crucial role in maintaining corneal transparency and hydration through a “pump-leak” mechanism. , This single layer of flattened cells on the inner surface of the cornea performs several vital functions, including hydration control, barrier function, and transparency maintenance of the cornea. , Corneal endothelial dysfunction leads to visual problems requiring corneal transplantation. , Thus, the study of novel treatments for corneal endothelial regeneration is necessary because of the global shortage of donor corneas and limitations of current treatments. 
MicroRNAs (miRNAs) are small, noncoding RNA molecules approximately 21 to 25 nucleotides in length that play crucial roles in regulating gene expression. miRNAs play important roles in regulating corneal endothelial cell (CEC) function, proliferation, and aging. Several miRNAs are differentially expressed in CECs during aging, including upregulation of miR-34c and miR-124 and downregulation of miR-31, miR-695, miR-183, miR-182, and miR-194 in older mice. miR-195-5p restricts the proliferation of human CECs and Fuchs endothelial corneal dystrophy cells. Modulating specific miRNAs may offer new approaches to promote CEC proliferation and treat corneal endothelial disorders. miR-196a has been investigated for its potential role in various cellular functions and pathologies by regulating gene expression through binding to the messenger RNA of target genes, leading to their degradation or inhibition of translation in cancer. Misexpression of miR-196a induces eye anomaly in a dose-dependent manner in Xenopus laevis. MiR-196a is known to target genes involved in cell cycle regulation and apoptosis, which could enhance the limited proliferative capacity of human CECs and suppress fibrosis and inflammation in other tissues by targeting pathways like transforming growth factor β (TGF-β) signaling, which may be beneficial for maintaining corneal clarity and preventing fibrosis-related vision loss. , Therefore, although this miR-196a has the potential to promote the regeneration of corneal endothelial cells, its effects have not been studied. In this study, we investigated the effects of miR-196a on the regeneration of CECs.

## Methods
This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Hallym University Kangnam Sacred Heart Hospital. Cadaveric donor corneas were purchased from Eversight (Ann Arbor, MI, USA).

## Cell Culture and Size
Human CECs (hCECs) were isolated from three independent donor corneas (ages 18, 35, and 38 years). Each donor-derived culture was treated as one biological replicate (n = 3). Human CECs were cultured as described previously. , CEC–Descemet's membrane complex was harvested using forceps. After a complex of the CEC–Descemet's membrane was treated overnight with 2 mg/mL Collagenase I (SCR103; MilliporeSigma, Darmstadt, Germany), centrifugation was performed at 1500 rpm for 3 minutes. The cells were seeded on surfaces coated with a fibronectin–collagen mixture (Athena Environmental Sciences, Baltimore, MD, USA). The basal medium was composed of Opti-MEM I (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), 8% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Lonza, Walkersville, MD, USA), 5 ng/mL human recombinant EGF (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA), 20 ng/mL human recombinant nerve growth factor (Invitrogen), 100 µg/mL bovine pituitary extract (Invitrogen), 0.5 mM L-ascorbic acid 2-phosphate (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA), 200 mg/L calcium chloride (Sigma-Aldrich), 0.08% chondroitin sulfate (Sigma-Aldrich), and 50 µg/mL gentamicin (Invitrogen). When the cells reached 90% confluence, they were passaged at a 1:3 dilution with 0.25% trypsin/0.02% EDTA (L0931; Biowest, Nuaillé, France). All in vitro experiments were performed at passages 5 to 7 to ensure stable morphology and consistent proliferative capacity. Cultured cells were imaged under phase-contrast microscopy at a fixed magnification. Cell size was expressed in pixels and quantified using dedicated image analysis software (Axiovert 4.7.1; Zeiss, Oberkochen, Germany).

## Transfection of hCECs With miRNA
We seeded hCECs onto a 6-well culture plate at 5 × 104 cells per well and cultured them for 24 hours in growth medium at 37°C under 5% CO2. The cells were then washed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and transfected using miRNAs (50 nM) with or without recombinant human TGF-β1 (10 ng/mL, ab50036; Abcam, Cambridge, UK) for 48 hours using Lipofectamine RNAiMAX reagent (Invitrogen) according to the manufacturer's instructions. miR-196a mimic (5-UAGGUAGUUUCAUGUGUUGGG-3), negative control (NC) mimic (SMC-3002), miR-196a-3p inhibitor (5-CAACAACAUUAAACCACCCGA-3), miR-196a-5p inhibitor (5-UAGGUAGUUUCAUGUUGUUGGG-3), and NC inhibitor (SMC-3102) were purchased from Bioneer (Seoul, Korea).

## Quantitative Real-Time Polymerase Chain Reaction Analysis
The miRNA was extracted using the mirPremier microRNA Isolation Kit (SNC50; Sigma-Aldrich). First-strand complementary DNA was synthesized using a Mir-X miRNA First-Strand Synthesis Kit (638313; Takara Bio, San Jose, CA, USA). The real-time quantification of transcripts was performed on a LightCycler 96 (Roche Life Science, Mannheim, Germany) using the AccuPower 2X GreenStar qPCR Master Mix (K-6253; Bioneer, Daejeon, Korea). Quantitative reverse transcription PCR (RT-qPCR) results were presented as fold changes relative to the control. The relative quantification was calculated using the ΔΔCT method. U6 was used as the endogenous control to normalize miRNA expression levels. The primers are described in .

## Cell Viability Assay
Cell viability assays were performed using a CCK-8 kit (CK-04; Dojindo, Kumamoto, Japan). Cells (1 × 104) were cultured in a 96-well plate and transfected with miRNAs for 48 hours. The plates were incubated with CCK-8 solution for 3 hours. Cell viability was determined by measuring absorbance at 450 nm using a microplate spectrophotometer (Synergy HTX; BioTek Instruments, Winooski, VT, USA) and expressed as the mean ± standard deviation as a percentage of the control (100%). The experiments were repeated three times, and a representative experiment is shown.

## Cell Cycle Analysis
Cell cycle analysis was performed using Cytoflex flow cytometry (Beckman Coulter, Brea, CA, USA) with propidium iodide (PI) (P4170; Sigma-Aldrich) staining according to the manufacturer's protocol. The cells were harvested using trypsinization, washed with cold PBS, and fixed with cold 70% ethanol. The samples were stored at −20°C. Cells were treated with 50 µg/mL PI and 100 µg/mL RNase A. Flow cytometry experiments were performed independently in each donor-derived culture (n = 3 biological replicates), with one acquisition per condition per donor.

## Mitochondrial Membrane Potential
JC-1 (11230-05; BioVision, Milpitas, CA, USA) was used to obtain images of the mitochondrial membrane potential with a fluorescence microscope (Leica, Wetzlar, Germany) and to detect changes in it. The data were analyzed using a Cytoflex flow cytometer.

## Senescence-Associated β-Galactosidase Assay
Senescence-associated β-galactosidase assay (SA-β-gal) was performed using the SA-β-gal staining kit (BioVision), following the manufacturer's instructions. For each donor-derived culture, five random microscopic fields were quantified, and the average value per donor was used as one biological replicate (n = 3). Five random fields per condition were imaged by bright‑field microscopy using identical exposure settings. SA‑β‑gal–positive cells were defined as showing distinct blue cytoplasmic staining above a threshold determined from untreated controls, with both dark and light staining counted as positive. The percentage of SA‑β‑gal–positive cells was calculated as (positive cells/total cells) × 100%.

## Western Blotting
At 48 hours after treatment, protein samples were harvested. Samples were run on sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis and then transferred to a polyvinylidene fluoride membrane. Membranes were blocked with 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA) at room temperature for 2 hours and then incubated at 4°C overnight with the primary antibodies against nestin (sc-21248; Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Dallas, TX, USA), parkin (sc-32282; Santa Cruz Biotechnology), PINK1 (sc-518052; Santa Cruz Biotechnology), p-p53 (sc-377567; Santa Cruz Biotechnology), p53 (sc-126; Santa Cruz Biotechnology), LC3 (M186-3; Medical & Biological Laboratories, Tokyo, Japan), ROCK1 (sc-17794; Santa Cruz Biotechnology), ROCK2 (sc-398519; Santa Cruz Biotechnology), PAD4 (sc-365369; Santa Cruz Biotechnology), pYAP (PA5-17481; Thermo Fisher Scientific), YAP (sc-376830; Santa Cruz Biotechnology), pERK1/2 (sc-136521; Santa Cruz Biotechnology), ERK1/2 (sc-514302; Santa Cruz Biotechnology), and GAPDH (LF-PA0018; Thermo Fisher Scientific), followed by incubation with horseradish peroxidase–conjugated secondary antibodies (goat anti-mouse IgG antibody, 31430 [Pierce, Rockford, IL, USA] or goat anti-rabbit IgG antibody, 31460 [Invitrogen]). Immunoband was detected using a WEST-Queen Western Blot Detection Kit (iNtRON Biotechnology, Seongnam, Korea) or Claro SupremoPlus ECL solution (BioD, Gwangmyeong-si, Korea).

## Immunofluorescence Staining
Immunofluorescence staining was performed as follows. Cells were fixed by 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 minutes. Samples were permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 for 10 minutes and blocked with 5% BSA, incubated with antibodies for CD166 (ab109215; Abcam), ZO1 (sc-10804; Santa Cruz Biotechnology), N-cadherin (13116S; Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA, USA), E-cadherin (sc-8426; Santa Cruz Biotechnology), and YAP (sc-376830; Santa Cruz Biotechnology). The Alexa Fluor 488–conjugated anti-rabbit IgG antibody (1:100, A-11008; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA) or rabbit anti-goat antibody (1:100, A-11001; Thermo Fisher) was treated at room temperature for 2 hours, followed by counterstaining with Hoechst 33342 (1:2000; Molecular Probes, Eugene, OR, USA). The staining results were observed under a fluorescence microscope (Leica). Nuclear translocation of YAP was evaluated using the nuclear-to-cytoplasmic ratio. The nuclear-to-cytoplasmic ratio of YAP is determined by measuring the fluorescence intensity in a nuclear region and an adjacent cytoplasmic region of equal size. The corresponding DAPI or Hoechst staining image is used to distinguish nuclear from cytoplasmic regions. The ratio is calculated by dividing the mean fluorescence intensity of the nuclear region by that of the cytoplasmic region. 

## miRNA Target Prediction
Potential miR-196a target genes were identified using the miRanda algorithm (http://www.microrna.org) and TargetScan version 8.0 (http://www.targetscan.org). Both algorithms scan the entire 3′UTR sequences for potential binding sites based on seed region complementarity (nucleotides 2–8), thermodynamic stability, evolutionary conservation, and local sequence context. Binding site positions are annotated numerically from the 3′UTR transcription start site.

## Statistical Analysis
All experiments were performed using three independent donor-derived cultures (n = 3 biological replicates). Unless otherwise specified, n represents biological replicates. Technical replicates were performed within each donor culture as described in the corresponding assay sections, and the mean value per donor was used for statistical analysis. Statistical analysis of data was performed using GraphPad Prism 10 Software (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). Data were represented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 10. Normality of the data was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. For comparisons between two groups, unpaired two-tailed Student's t-tests were used. For comparisons involving more than two groups, one-way analysis of variance was performed, followed by Tukey's multiple comparisons test for all pairwise comparisons. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Data are presented as mean ± SD from three independent biological replicates (n = 3).

## Results

## Identification of Cultured Human Corneal Endothelial Cells
To confirm the CEC identity and structural integrity of the cultured cells, we first examined cellular morphology and junctional marker expression. Phase-contrast microscopy demonstrated that confluent cultures exhibited a characteristic contact-inhibited monolayer morphology resembling in vivo CECs ( A). Immunofluorescence analysis further confirmed endothelial identity. ZO-1 staining revealed continuous circumferential localization at cell–cell borders, indicating intact tight junction formation and barrier integrity ( B). CD166 (ALCAM), a recognized surface marker of functional CECs, was robustly expressed across the monolayer ( C). N-cadherin was clearly expressed at cell–cell borders, supporting the presence of intact adherens junctions in these cultures ( D). E-cadherin was barely expressed in human corneal endothelial cells, as indicated by only minimal green fluorescence at cell–cell borders ( E).
 

## miR-196a Regulated the Proliferation of hCECs
To determine whether miR-196a regulates proliferative capacity in hCECs, we first confirmed successful transfection efficiency. RT-qPCR demonstrated a robust increase in miR-196a expression in mimic-transfected cells compared with miR-control (8.6 × 106-fold increase, P = 0.0033;  A), validating experimental manipulation. miR-196a increased hCEC viability in a dose-dependent manner across 10, 25, and 50 nM, with the largest increase at 50 nM ( ); 50 nM was therefore used in subsequent experiments. To evaluate cell viability, the CCK-8 assay was performed using a highly water-soluble tetrazolium salt, WST-8 [2-(2-methoxy-4-nitrophenyl)-3-(4-nitrophenyl)-5-(2,4-disulfophenyl)-2H-tetrazolium, monosodium salt]. Cell viability showed a 16.3% increase in miR-196a–transfected cells compared to control and a 7.3% and 6.1% decrease in miR-196a inhibitor-transfected cells (P = 0.0152, 0.0009, and 0.0016;  B,  C). To further characterize the proliferative effect, we analyzed cell cycle distribution using flow cytometry. The percentage of mir-196a–transfected cells was lower in the G0/G1 phase and higher in the S phase, suggesting a shift of cells from the G0/G1 phase to the S phase (P = 0.0022 in the G0/G1 phase and P = 0.0098 in the S phase;  D–F). Nestin levels, which are expressed in stem and progenitor cells, were increased in mir-196a–transfected cells (37.9%, P = 0.0021;  G,  H).
 miR-196a inhibitors were used to investigate the role of miR-196a by targeting miR-196a-3p or miR-196a-5p. miR-196a expression was inhibited by the miR-196a-3p inhibitor and miR-196a-5p inhibitor (P < 0.0001 for both; I). The cell count was reduced in cells transfected with the miR-196a-3p inhibitor (P = 0.0478 and 0.0184; J). Phase-contrast microscopy revealed that miR-196a inhibition induced noticeable cellular enlargement ( K), which was confirmed by quantitative analysis of cell size (121.6%, P = 0.0005;  L). Increased cell size is commonly associated with cell cycle arrest or early senescence-like changes, supporting the cell cycle data observed with miR-196a suppression. Cell cycle analysis revealed an increased percentage of cells in the G0/G1 phase and a decreased percentage of cells in the S phase in miR-196a-3p inhibitor–transfected cells (P = 0.0003 and 0.0498 in the G0/G1 phase and P < 0.001 and 0.0023 in the S phase;  M–O). Nestin expression levels were decreased in cells transfected with the miR-196a-3p inhibitor (16.9%, P = 0.0005; M,  N).

## miR-196a Inhibited the Mitochondrial Autophagy via ROCK/YAP Pathway
To investigate whether the pro-proliferative effect of miR-196a is associated with mitochondrial regulation, we next examined mitochondrial membrane potential and mitophagy-related pathways. JC-1 was used to evaluate the mitochondrial membrane potential ( A–C). JC-1 staining demonstrated a significant increase in the proportion of cells with high mitochondrial membrane potential following miR-196a overexpression (P = 0.0400;  A–C). To determine whether mitochondrial quality control pathways were altered, we analyzed the expression of PINK1 and parkin. Both proteins showed a decrease in miR-196a–transfected cells (28.0%, P = 0.0225 in parkin and 34.7%, P = 0.0003 in PINK;  D–H). Since PINK1/parkin activation is a hallmark of mitophagy initiation, these findings suggest that miR-196a suppresses mitophagy under basal conditions. p53 levels, which regulate numerous cell cycle–associated genes, were decreased 34.7% in the miR-196a–transfected cells compared to the control (P = 0.0230;  G,  H). LC3II levels, which are a key protein in the autophagy process, were reduced in the miR-196a–transfected cells compared to the control (54.4%, P = 0.0019;  I,  J), indicating attenuation of autophagosome formation. Together, these data support the notion that miR-196a promotes mitochondrial stability while limiting mitophagic turnover. To explore upstream regulatory mechanisms, we assessed ROCK1 and ROCK2 expression. ROCK1 and ROCK2 levels, which are two highly homologous serine/threonine kinases, decreased in the miR-196a–transfected cells compared to the control (25.5%, P = 0.0125 and 42.0%, P = 0.0057; K–M). Since ROCK signaling is implicated in cytoskeletal tension, mitochondrial dynamics, and senescence-related pathways, its suppression provides a mechanistic link between miR-196a and mitochondrial stabilization. In contrast, PAD4 levels did not show any difference in the miR-196a–transfected cells compared to the control ( N,  O). We next examined downstream signaling pathways associated with proliferation and survival. pYAP levels were elevated in the miR-196a–transfected cells compared to the control (41.3%, P = 0.0348;  P,  Q). As phosphorylation of YAP promotes cytoplasmic retention, immunofluorescence analysis confirmed a reduction in nuclear YAP localization in the miR-196a–transfected cells compared to the control (P = 0.0273;  R,  S), consistent with enhanced Hippo-mediated sequestration. In parallel, pERK1/2 levels were elevated in the miR-196a–transfected cells compared to the control (6.5%, P = 0.0030;  T,  U), indicating activation of the MAPK pathway, which is known to promote cell proliferation and survival. 
 

## miR-196a Inhibitors Activated the Mitochondrial Autophagy via Activating ROCK and Suppressing YAP Pathway
To determine whether endogenous miR-196a is required for maintaining mitochondrial stability and suppressing mitophagy, we performed loss-of-function experiments using miR-196a inhibitors. JC-1 analysis revealed that inhibition of miR-196a significantly reduced the proportion of cells with high mitochondrial membrane potential (miR-196a-3p inhibitor: P = 0.0331; miR-196a-5p inhibitor: P = 0.0416;  A–D). The shift toward increased green fluorescence indicates mitochondrial depolarization, suggesting impaired mitochondrial functional integrity. Consistent with mitochondrial depolarization, inhibition of miR-196a significantly increased expression of parkin and PINK1 (41.7%, P = 0.0043 and 54.7%, P = 0.0010 in parkin and 61.1%, P = 0.0433 in PINK;  E–G), indicating activation of the PINK1/parkin-dependent mitophagy pathway. p53 levels were higher in the cells transfected with miR-196a-3p inhibitor (P = 0.0230 and 0.0411;  H,  I). LC3II levels were also elevated in both miR-196a-3p and miR-196a-5p inhibitor–treated cells (33.2%, P = 0.0011 and 20.4%, P = 0.0127;  J,  K). PAD4 levels were increased in the cells transfected with miR-196a-3p inhibitor (P = 0.0265;  L,  M). In contrast to the suppressive effect observed with miR-196a overexpression, inhibition significantly increased ROCK1 and ROCK2 levels (44.8%, P = 0.0088 and 18.3%, P = 0.0175;  N–P). Consistent with ROCK activation, phosphorylation of YAP was significantly reduced following miR-196a inhibition (P < 0.0001;  Q–S). Reduced YAP phosphorylation was accompanied by increased nuclear localization of YAP (38.8%, P = 0.0301 and 15.2%, P = 0.0043;  T–U), indicating enhanced nuclear translocation and potential transcriptional activation. In parallel, pERK1/2 levels were decreased (23.4%, P = 0.0113), suggesting attenuation of MAPK-mediated prosurvival signaling.
 

## miR-196a Suppressed TGF-β–Induced Senescence
To determine whether the regulatory effects of miR-196a extend to stress-induced senescence, we next evaluated its role in a TGF-β–mediated senescence model. Using computational prediction algorithms (miRanda and TargetScan), we identified that miR-196a sequence targets TGFBR1, TGFBR2, ROCK1, and ROCK2 ( A). The predicted binding site in ROCK1 3′UTR is located at position 3292, representing a distal site consistent with the observation that functional miRNA binding sites can be distributed throughout long 3′UTRs. , These findings suggest that miR-196a may directly modulate TGF-β signaling at multiple regulatory nodes. Thus, we used TGF-β to evaluate the effect of miR-196a on TGF-β–induced senescence. Exposure to TGF-β induced a pronounced increase in cell size, characterized by cellular enlargement and flattened morphology consistent with senescence-like changes ( B). Quantitative analysis confirmed a significant increase in cell size following TGF-β treatment (P = 0.0006). Importantly, cotransfection with miR-196a significantly attenuated this enlargement (P = 0.0055), restoring morphology toward control levels ( C). These findings indicate that miR-196a counteracts TGF-β–induced structural features of senescence. SA-β-gal assay revealed a marked increase in SA-β-gal–positive cells following TGF-β treatment (P = 0.0048;  D–E), confirming successful induction of senescence. miR-196a overexpression significantly reduced the proportion of SA-β-gal–positive cells compared with TGF-β alone (P = 0.0014), indicating attenuation of senescence-associated enzymatic activity. Flow cytometric analysis demonstrated that TGF-β treatment induced G0/G1 arrest (P < 0.001) and significantly reduced the S phase fraction (P < 0.0001;  F–H), consistent with cell cycle exit during senescence. Cotreatment with miR-196a significantly reduced G0/G1 accumulation (P < 0.001) and restored the S phase population (P = 0.0001), indicating partial reversal of TGF-β–induced cell cycle arrest.
 

## miR-196a Suppressed Mitochondrial Autophagy Inhibiting the TGF-β Signaling Pathway
To determine whether the antisenescent effect of miR-196a involves mitochondrial regulation under stress conditions, we examined mitochondrial integrity and associated signaling pathways in TGF-β–treated cells. JC-1 analysis demonstrated that TGF-β treatment significantly reduced the proportion of cells with high mitochondrial membrane potential (P = 0.0079;  A–C), indicating mitochondrial depolarization and functional impairment. Importantly, cotransfection with miR-196a significantly restored mitochondrial membrane potential (P = 0.0006), suggesting that miR-196a protects mitochondrial integrity. Consistent with mitochondrial depolarization, TGF-β significantly increased parkin and PINK1 levels, which were attenuated by miR-196a (49.8%, P = 0.0088 and 105.7%, P = 0.0002 in parkin, 37.9%, P = 0.0035 and 42.8%, P = 0.0019 in PINK; D–F). TGF-β treatment significantly elevated p53 levels (56.3%, P = 0.0246;  G,  H), consistent with induction of growth arrest and senescence. miR-196a significantly reduced TGF-β–induced p53 expression (89.3%, P = 0.0028), suggesting inhibition of stress-responsive cell cycle arrest pathways. LC3II levels were higher in the TGF-β–treated cells, which were attenuated by miR-196a (29.5%, P = 0.0291 and 82.7%, P < 0.0001;  I,  J). ROCK2 levels were higher in the TGF-β–treated cells, which were attenuated by miR-196a (P = 0.0060 and 0.0085;  K,  M), consistent with predicted targeting and upstream inhibition of TGF-β signaling. pYAP levels were reduced in TGF-β–treated cells (34.7%, P = 0.0310;  N–O), accompanied by increased nuclear localization of YAP (P < 0.0001;  P–Q), indicating enhanced nuclear translocation and altered Hippo pathway dynamics.29 miR-196a reversed these effects by restoring pYAP levels (35.0%, P = 0.0297) and reducing nuclear YAP localization, suggesting reactivation of Hippo-mediated cytoplasmic sequestration under stress conditions. PAD4 levels were higher in the TGF-β–treated cells, which were attenuated by miR-196a (24.8%, P = 0.0244 and 30.5%, P = 0.0097;  R,  S). In parallel, miR-196a increased pERK1/2 levels (51.8%, P = 0.0110 and 91.7%, P = 0.0317;  T–U), suggesting activation of MAPK-mediated prosurvival signaling even under TGF-β–induced stress.
 

## Discussion
In this study, we characterized human CEC cultures expressing the established markers CD166 and ZO-1, confirming their endothelial phenotype and junction integrity. The use of CD166 and ZO-1 has been reported as markers for characterizing human CECs. CD166 (ALCAM) is a recognized surface marker for CECs. ZO-1 (zonula occludens 1) is commonly used to demonstrate the CECs and is crucial for tight junction assembly. 
Our findings reveal that miR-196a facilitates cell cycle progression and promotes CEC proliferation, with important implications for CEC regeneration strategies. , , The findings showed the dual impact of miR-196a overexpression and inhibition on cellular behavior, offering potential implications for its therapeutic targeting. RT-qPCR analysis confirmed the successful overexpression of miR-196a in transfected cells. Functionally, this overexpression enhanced cell viability, which is consistent with the notion that miR-196a may promote cell survival and proliferation. , Cell cycle analysis further revealed that overexpression of miR-196a promoted a shift of cells from the G0/G1 phase to the S phase, indicating its role in facilitating cell cycle progression. , These findings suggest that miR-196a-3p may regulate key pathways associated with cell cycle control, potentially by modulating the expression of genes critical for the G1-to-S phase transition. The observed changes in nestin expression emphasize the role of miR-196a in cellular plasticity. While nestin is commonly associated with neural stem/progenitor cells, its expression in CECs does not indicate true stemness or dedifferentiation. Human CECs are terminally differentiated cells derived from neural crest that lack self-renewal capacity and do not form a proliferative stem cell niche in vivo. , Rather, nestin upregulation in this context reflects proliferative reactivation and enhanced regenerative potential within the differentiated CEC phenotype. This finding is compatible with previous reports of microRNAs influencing stemness and differentiation pathways. 
The increase in mitochondrial membrane potential with miR-196a overexpression suggests a protective role in maintaining mitochondrial homeostasis, which is critical for CEC survival in vivo, where oxidative stress is a constant challenge. Parkin and PINK1, the key components of mitochondrial quality control through mitophagy, , were downregulated by miR-196a, which suggests that miR-196a overexpression may suppress mitophagy, potentially favoring mitochondrial biogenesis or other compensatory pathways, – whereas miR-196a-3p inhibition enhances mitophagy, reflecting its role in mitochondrial turnover. , miR-196a modulates p53-dependent pathways, which are central to cell cycle arrest, apoptosis, and stress responses. – This modulation may be particularly important in the context of oxidative and TGF-β–induced stress in CECs. Although p53 plays a complex role in regulating mitophagy, primarily through its interaction with parkin, mitochondrially localized p53 can enhance mitophagy by modulating mitochondrial membrane potential, which is a key trigger for PINK1 stabilization and subsequent parkin recruitment. miR-196a regulated LC3, which is essential for the formation and maturation of autophagosomes, as well as for selective autophagy, a cellular process responsible for the degradation and recycling of intracellular components. LC3II proteins play a crucial role in mitophagy, the selective degradation of damaged mitochondria through interacting with cardiolipin. In parkin-mediated mitophagy, LC3-II is recruited to ubiquitinated outer mitochondrial membrane proteins. The suppression of ROCK1 and ROCK2 by miR-196a has important mechanistic implications. ROCK1 and ROCK2 are isoforms of Rho-associated coiled-coil containing protein kinases, which are serine/threonine kinases regulated by the small GTPase RhoA. They play critical roles in cellular processes such as actin cytoskeleton organization, cell adhesion, motility, proliferation, and apoptosis. PAD4, which regulates chromatin structure and inflammation, , was similarly modulated, suggesting that miR-196a may influence inflammatory responses and chromatin remodeling. Significant changes in YAP and ERK1/2 signaling pathways were also observed. Increased pYAP and pERK1/2 levels in miR-196a–transfected cells indicate activation of the Hippo and MAPK pathways, potentially promoting proliferation and survival. , Phosphorylation of YAP, particularly at Ser127, promotes its cytoplasmic retention and inhibits its transcriptional activity. In this context, the observed increase in pYAP, together with reduced nuclear localization, suggests that miR-196a enhances Hippo-mediated cytoplasmic sequestration of YAP rather than promoting nuclear transcriptional activation. The observed increase in nuclear YAP localization with miR-196a-3p inhibition highlights a potential mechanism for transcriptional regulation by YAP. Conversely, reduced YAP phosphorylation following miR-196a inhibition was accompanied by increased nuclear localization, consistent with enhanced transcriptional activity.
TGF-β is known to induce cellular senescence by promoting cell cycle arrest and enhancing senescence markers such as SA-β-gal activity. , Our results showed that miR-196a can counteract TGF-β–induced senescence. This effect is likely mediated by miR-196a's ability to target key components of the TGF-β signaling pathway, including TGFBR1, TGFBR2, ROCK1, and ROCK2, as demonstrated in this study. Mitochondrial dysfunction is a hallmark of cellular senescence, characterized by reduced mitochondrial membrane potential and dysregulation of mitophagy. , TGF-β–treated cells exhibited reduced mitochondrial membrane potential and increased levels of parkin and PINK1, which are markers of mitophagy activation. , Interestingly, miR-196a overexpression mitigated these effects, restoring mitochondrial membrane potential and reducing parkin and PINK1 levels. These findings suggest that miR-196a may modulate mitochondrial quality control, thereby alleviating TGF-β–induced mitochondrial dysfunction. The study also showed the role of miR-196a in regulating autophagy and cell signaling. Elevated LC3II levels in TGF-β–treated cells, indicative of enhanced autophagic activity, , were attenuated by miR-196a, suggesting a suppressive effect on autophagy. The modulation of ROCK2, PAD4, and YAP signaling supports the role of miR-196a in regulating pathways linked to cytoskeletal dynamics, inflammation, and transcriptional control. Notably, miR-196a restored pYAP levels and inhibited YAP nuclear translocation, indicating a potential mechanism for repressing transcriptional programs associated with senescence. The elevation of p53 levels in TGF-β–treated cells, a key regulator of cell cycle arrest and senescence, , was significantly reduced by miR-196a. This reduction may contribute to the observed attenuation of G0/G1 arrest and senescence markers. Furthermore, miR-196a increased pERK1/2 levels, suggesting activation of the MAPK signaling pathway, which could promote cellular proliferation and survival, , counteracting the effects of TGF-β–induced senescence. Overall, these findings reveal the multifaceted role of miR-196a in modulating TGF-β–induced senescence. These results underscore the potential of miR-196a as a therapeutic target for conditions associated with senescence, such as aging-related diseases and fibrotic disorders.
This study has several limitations. First, human CECs were derived from three independent donors (ages 18, 35, and 38 years), and although the effects of miR-196a were consistent across donor-derived cultures, the limited number of biological replicates may restrict statistical power. While normality was assessed prior to parametric testing, the small sample size reduces the sensitivity of such tests. Second, although multiple comparisons were corrected using appropriate post hoc analyses, larger cohorts will be required to confirm robustness and to evaluate potential age-related variability. Future studies, including a broader donor age range and in vivo validation, will further strengthen the translational relevance of these findings.
An important consideration for any CEC regeneration therapy is the potential risk of inducing endothelial-to-mesenchymal transition (EMT) or uncontrolled proliferation, as seen in posterior polymorphous corneal dystrophy. Our data suggest that miR-196a promotes controlled proliferation without initiating pathologic EMT. First, CECs maintained CEC-like cell morphology after miR-196a transfection. Second, the increase in S phase cells was modest and physiological rather than transformative. Third, miR-196a suppressed TGF-β signaling and ROCK expression, both of which are key drivers of EMT in CECs. , Indeed, ROCK inhibitors are currently used clinically to maintain the CEC phenotype and prevent EMT-mediated fibrosis. 

## Conclusions
This study demonstrates that miR-196a promotes cell survival and proliferation by enhancing mitochondrial health, suppressing autophagy, and activating key signaling pathways such as YAP and MAPK. Its ability to counteract TGF-β–induced senescence by modulating mitochondrial quality control, autophagic activity, and p53-dependent pathways underscores its potential as a therapeutic target. By targeting critical components of the TGF-β signaling pathway and associated regulatory networks, miR-196a offers protective effects against cellular senescence and dysfunction. These insights provide a compelling basis for further exploration of miR-196a as a therapeutic strategy for aging-related CEC diseases and conditions involving mitochondrial and cellular stress.
