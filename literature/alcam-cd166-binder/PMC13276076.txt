## Abstract
Laryngeal squamous cell carcinoma (LSCC) frequently presents with lymph node metastasis and poor prognosis. Cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) are key stromal components that modulate tumor progression. We performed single-cell RNA sequencing on nine matched samples, including primary tumors (PT), adjacent non-tumor tissues (NT), and metastatic lymph nodes (LN), from three LSCC patients to profile epithelial and fibroblast heterogeneity. Three malignant epithelial subtypes (EP0-EP2) were identified, with distinct trajectories and functional phenotypes. EP0 was enriched in EMT-related signatures and dominant in metastatic lesions, while EP2 displayed high stemness and proliferative potential. Five fibroblast subtypes (F0-F4) were characterized, showing a pseudotemporal transition from immune-regulatory (F1, F3) and matrix-remodeling (F2) states toward a contractile, pro-invasive F0 subtype, which was enriched in LN and highly expressed ACTA2 and CAV1. Cell-cell communication analysis revealed increased fibroblast-epithelial interactions in LN, primarily via FN1 and COLLAGEN ligands targeting CD44. Functional assays confirmed that CD44 knockdown in vitro reduced proliferation, migration, invasion, and in vivo tumorigenesis, supporting its role in metastasis. These findings reveal the dynamic remodeling of epithelial and fibroblast compartments in LSCC and identify CD44 as a key driver of metastasis through fibroblast-epithelial interactions.

## Introduction
Laryngeal squamous cell carcinoma (LSCC) is one of the most prevalent malignancies in the head and neck region . Despite advances in comprehensive treatment strategies, the overall survival rate of LSCC remains poor due to frequent local recurrence and cervical lymph node metastasis . Lymph node involvement is a well-established prognostic indicator in LSCC and is associated with advanced disease stage and poor clinical outcomes . However, the molecular mechanisms that drive lymph node metastasis in LSCC remain largely unresolved.
Recent studies underscore the critical role of the tumor microenvironment (TME) in influencing cancer progression and metastasis , . The TME comprises a complex network of tumor cells, immune infiltrates, mesenchymal components, cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs), and extracellular matrix (ECM) elements . Among these, CAFs are particularly important for modulating ECM architecture, facilitating tumor-stroma crosstalk, and establishing metastatic niches – . The functional and phenotypic heterogeneity of CAFs has been increasingly recognized as a key determinant of tumor behavior .
While bulk RNA sequencing has advanced our understanding of transcriptomic alterations in the TME, it fails to resolve cell type-specific transcriptional profiles due to signal averaging across heterogeneous populations , . In contrast, single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) enables high-resolution profiling at the individual cell level, uncovering rare subpopulations, dynamic transcriptional states, and cell-cell communication networks that are otherwise masked in bulk data , . This approach provides a powerful tool to dissect epithelial and fibroblast cell types, both of which play pivotal roles in tumor invasion and metastasis.
In this study, we applied scRNA-seq to matched primary tumors (PT), adjacent non-tumor tissues (NT), and metastatic lymph nodes (LN) from LSCC patients to resolve the heterogeneity of epithelial and fibroblast populations. We reconstructed developmental trajectories of malignant epithelial cells and CAFs identified metastasis-associated subclusters and transcriptional programs. To address the unresolved question of how CAF produced extracellular matrix signals influence epithelial malignancy, we further examined CD44 as the key epithelial receptor that may mediate the signals coming from CAFs. These findings provide new insights into the cellular and molecular landscape of LSCC metastasis and reposition CD44 as a central epithelial receptor that responds to CAF produced extracellular matrix signals and promotes metastatic progression.

## Results

## Single-cell transcriptional landscape of LSCC primary tumors and lymph node metastases
An overview of the study design and analytical workflow is provided in Fig. . We conducted scRNA-seq on nine samples derived from three patients with LSCC exhibiting lymph node metastasis, including primary tumors (PT; n = 3: LC_C2, LC_C4, LC_C5), adjacent non-tumor tissues (NT; n = 3: LC_P2, LC_P4, LC_P5), and lymph node metastases (LN; n = 3: LC_N2, LC_N4, LC_N5) (Table ). After quality control, dimensionality reduction, and unsupervised clustering, we annotated cell types and identified six major populations: T cells, epithelial cells, fibroblasts, macrophages, endothelial cells, and B cells (Fig. ). UMAP visualization demonstrated that T cells and epithelial cells occupied the largest regions, indicating their high abundance and underscoring their critical roles in the tumor microenvironment. Notably, significant differences in cellular composition were observed across samples and patients, particularly in T cell and epithelial cell proportions. In LN, T cells and epithelial cells were markedly enriched, suggesting potential immune-tumor cell interactions during metastatic progression (Fig. ). The proportional composition of cell types across all samples, visualized by a proportional composition plot, revealed T cells (32.99%, 22,869 cells) and epithelial cells (24.75%, 17,156 cells) as the dominant populations, followed by fibroblasts (21.2%, 14,699 cells) (Fig. and ). LN samples showed higher T cell and epithelial cell proportions but fewer fibroblasts, implying microenvironmental remodeling during metastasis (Fig. ). The relative abundance of distinct cell lineages exhibited substantial variation across tissue types and samples (Fig. ), indicating a heterogeneous cellular landscape characterized by inter-sample diversity. To validate cell type identities, we analyzed marker gene expression (Fig. ): epithelial cells strongly expressed EPCAM, KRT19, KRT7, KRT17, PROM1, ALDH1A1, and CD24; T cells exhibited elevated CD3D, CCL5, IL32, CD3E, CD8A, CD2, and PTPRC; fibroblasts were enriched in COL1A1, DCN, GSN, LUM, FGF7, MME, FAP, ACTA2, and COL3A1. Furthermore, deconvolution analysis of bulk RNA-seq data from TCGA (n = 127) revealed stage-dependent cellular dynamics in lymph node metastases: T cell proportions increased from 30.8% in N0 to 38.6% in N3, while epithelial cells declined from 29.4% to 19.3%; fibroblast levels exhibited non-linear fluctuations (Fig. ). Macrophages showed a mild increase at advanced nodal stages (stage N3), whereas endothelial cells slightly decreased, collectively suggesting progressive immune-stromal remodeling during metastasis (Fig. ). Consistently, Kaplan-Meier survival analysis of LSCC patients from the TCGA cohort demonstrated a significant decrease in overall survival with advancing N stage, underscoring the prognostic importance of lymph node involvement in disease progression (Fig. ). All major analytical results, including UMAP structure, pseudotime trajectory and functional states, remained consistent after doublet removal (Figs. – ).
 

## Defining the malignant epithelial compartment based on molecular scoring
To quantify the malignant potential of epithelial cells in LSCC, we derived both malignant and non-malignant scores by performing differential gene expression analysis between tumor (n = 116) and normal samples (n = 12) from the TCGA-LSCC cohort (|log₂FC| > 1, p < 0.05) (Fig. ). Malignant scores showed a strong positive correlation with several oncogenic hallmark pathways, most notably MYC_TARGETS_V1 and OXIDATIVE_PHOSPHORYLATION, emphasizing their critical roles in LSCC progression (Fig. ). Based on these scores, epithelial cells in the scRNA-seq dataset were classified as either malignant or non-malignant (Fig. ). To justify the threshold, we visualized the distribution of net malignancy scores across all epithelial cells, which showed a heterogeneous pattern and a local separation zone around -0.02 (Fig. ). Malignant epithelial cells predominantly originated from PT (90.66%) and LN (98.64%), whereas NT contained a considerable proportion (33.92%) of malignant-classified epithelial cells (Fig. , and ). This proportion is consistent with the sensitivity-oriented design of the scoring strategy and the conservative threshold (-0.02), which prioritizes minimizing false-negative assignment of malignant-like cells and may include part of NT epithelial cells in the malignant-score group. Importantly, epithelial cells with high malignant scores in NT should not be interpreted as truly malignant cells, but rather as epithelial cells showing partial malignant-like transcriptional features captured by the scoring framework. Functional enrichment analysis revealed substantial divergence in biological programs: PT- and LN-derived malignant epithelial cells were enriched in tumor-associated processes such as Wnt signaling, apoptosis regulation, and extracellular matrix organization, whereas their NT-derived counterparts were predominantly involved in antibacterial humoral immune responses (Fig. ). Integration with TCGA bulk RNA-seq data further corroborated these findings. Genes upregulated in NT-derived malignant cells (e.g., SCGB3A2, KRT13, THSD4) were similarly elevated in normal tissues, while downregulated genes (e.g., APOE, COL1A1, FN1, MEST) also exhibited normal-like expression profiles (Fig. and ). Together, these observations suggest that NT-classified malignant epithelial cells represent a transcriptionally distinct population lacking hallmark oncogenic features. Their divergence from bona fide malignant profiles supports their exclusion from downstream analyses, thereby allowing for a more refined investigation of metastatic mechanisms in PT and LN epithelial subpopulations with preserved malignant signatures.
 

## Transcriptional programs and functional diversity of malignant epithelial subpopulations
Epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT), a key biological process underlying tumor invasion and metastasis, was systematically assessed in malignant epithelial cells using scores derived from the Hallmark “EPITHELIAL MESENCHYMAL TRANSITION” gene set. Only Patient 2 (LC_C4 vs LC_N4) showed significantly higher EMT scores in LN, whereas other paired samples did not display notable differences (Fig. ), suggesting inter-patient heterogeneity in EMT activation. These findings indicate that EMT activation may contribute to lymph-node metastatic progression in a subset of LSCC patients, although this association does not appear uniform across individuals (Table ). Unsupervised clustering stratified malignant epithelia into three transcriptionally distinct subpopulations (EP0, EP1, EP2), as visualized by UMAP (Fig. ). EMT scores were highest in EP0, moderate in EP1, and lowest in EP2 (Fig. ). Prognostically relevant genes were identified in both EP0 (CEACAM5, ADH7, IGF2BP3, KRT13, RORA) and EP2 (NPAS2, ARHGEF11, COL4A2, SLC39A10, CBFA2T2), with elevated expression correlating significantly with reduced overall survival in the TCGA-LSCC cohort (log-rank p < 0.05) (Fig. ). Moreover, we calculated proliferation and stemness scores for each malignant epithelial cell based on distinct gene sets. The proliferation score, derived from the expression of CCND1, MKI67, MCM7, PHGDH, PCNA and JAG1, was highest in EP2, followed by EP1 and EP0, suggesting enhanced proliferative potential in EP2 (Fig. ). Stemness scores, calculated based on a curated gene set including ALCAM, NID1, TWIST1, CD44, SOX4, and ALDH1A1, were highest in EP2, followed by EP0, and lowest in EP1 (Fig. ), suggesting that EP2 harbors the strongest stem-like transcriptional signature among epithelial subpopulations. Expression patterns of genes contributing to both scores were further validated across subpopulations, confirming their respective enrichment profiles (Fig. ). GO enrichment analysis of differentially expressed genes (|log2FC| > 1, p < 0.01) revealed distinct biological programs: EP0 was enriched in oxidative stress response and NF-κB signaling, EP1 in neutrophil-mediated immunity, and EP2 in mesenchymal development and stemness pathways (Fig. ). Complementary KEGG pathway enrichment analysis showed that EP0 was associated with HIF-1 and TNF signaling pathways, EP1 with apoptosis and focal adhesion, and EP2 with Wnt signaling and chromatin remodeling (Fig. ).
 

## Developmental trajectories and chromosomal instability drive epithelial heterogeneity
To further delineate the dynamic plasticity and developmental hierarchy of malignant epithelial subpopulations in LSCC, we applied pseudotime trajectory analysis. Monocle 2-based reconstruction revealed a branched developmental landscape in which all three subpopulations (EP0, EP1, and EP2), were distributed along the early pseudotime axis, suggesting a common cellular origin (Fig. ). As the trajectory progressed, EP0 and EP1 extended toward one late-stage branch, while EP2 became progressively confined to the other later-stage branch, reflecting its unique transcriptional trajectory. Annotation of pseudotime states supported this developmental model: state 1 comprised early-phase cells from all three subpopulations, forming the root of the trajectory; state 2 corresponded to the EP2-enriched later branch, and state 3 represented the later branch dominated by EP0 and EP1, which showed EMT-like features. Density distribution analysis corroborated these observations, showing an early enrichment peak in EP1, a mid-stage predominance in EP2, and a pronounced late-stage accumulation in EP0 (Fig. ). Comparison analysis between PT and LN revealed that PT-derived cells were distributed throughout the entire pseudotime trajectory, whereas LN-derived cells were significantly enriched in the late-stage regions predominantly occupied by EP0 and EP1 (Fig. ). EP2 was markedly sparse in LN and localized near the trajectory terminus, overlapping with EP0 as shown in Fig. . EMT scores progressively increased along the pseudotime trajectory, with state 3 cells displaying significantly elevated EMT activity compared to states 1 and 2 (Fig. ), implicating EMT in advanced-stage cellular progression.
Consistently, CytoTRACE analysis confirmed the highest developmental potential in EP2 cells (Fig. ). Clustering of highly variable genes along the trajectory identified three major modules associated with chromatin remodeling, oxidative stress response, and epithelial differentiation (Fig. ). Furthermore, branch-specific expression analysis revealed distinct fate-associated transcriptional programs: Cluster 1, enriched in cell fate 2, was associated with immune-related processes including leukocyte migration and chemotaxis; Cluster 2, elevated in cell fate 1, involved thrombin signaling and cytoskeletal regulation; Cluster 3, upregulated in cell fate 2, was linked to epithelial differentiation and barrier function; Cluster 4, expressed in pre-branch and cell fate 1 regions, was enriched in translational and mRNA surveillance pathways (Fig. and ). Collectively, these findings delineate a temporally ordered and fate-divergent developmental hierarchy among LSCC malignant epithelial cells, wherein EP2 represents a transcriptionally primitive and stem-like state, EP1 a transitional phase, and EP0 a more differentiated subpopulation characterized by EMT activation and enhanced migratory potential. We next examined the expression dynamics of genes associated with stemness score, proliferation score, and EMT along the pseudotime trajectory (Fig. ). Stemness-associated genes including NID1, TWIST1, and SOX4 exhibited elevated expression during the mid-to-late stages of the trajectory, supporting the enrichment of stem-like characteristics in intermediate and late-phase malignant epithelial cells. ALCAM and CD44 increased at the late stage and ALDH1A1 showed high expression at the early stage. Proliferation-related genes showed divergent trends: CCND1 peaked in the mid-to-late stages, JAG1 was upregulated toward the terminal end, MKI67 remained relatively stable, while PHGDH, MCM7, and PCNA gradually declined along pseudotime. EMT markers revealed stage-specific expression patterns. FN1 and VIM were downregulated in the later stages, whereas TJP1 and CDH1 initially decreased and subsequently re-expressed at the end of the trajectory . Together, these results delineate functional transitions within the malignant epithelial compartment across the differentiation continuum.
 To explore the role of chromosomal instability in epithelial heterogeneity in LSCC, we performed CopyKAT analysis to infer CNV status across epithelial cells. The results revealed that aneuploid cells were primarily localized within malignant epithelial regions in UMAP space and broadly overlapping with malignancy classification defined earlier (Fig. , Fig. ). To quantitatively relate transcriptional scoring to CNV status, score-based malignant and non-malignant labels were compared with CNV status in cells with high-confidence assignments, showing that 96.72% (2953/3053) of aneuploid cells fell into the malignant group, whereas only 3.28% (100/3053) were labeled as non-malignant, while malignant cells also included a substantial fraction of diploid cells (Fig. ). Quantitatively, the malignant group contained not only the majority of aneuploid cells (2953/3053; 96.72%) but also substantial fractions of diploid and undefined epithelial cells, reflecting the sensitivity-oriented nature of the transcriptional malignancy scoring (Fig. ). Tissue-specific analysis further indicated that the relative proportion of aneuploid epithelial cells was higher in LN (29.54%) than in PT (20.87%), whereas diploid and undefined states were also present in both tissues (Fig. ). Subsequent Seurat clustering of aneuploid cells identified four transcriptionally distinct subclusters with varying tissue-specific origins (Fig. ). Cluster 1 was predominantly found in PT, whereas Clusters 2 and 3 were enriched in LN. Quantification confirmed that Cluster 1 represented the majority of aneuploid cells in PT, whereas Cluster 2 accounted for the largest fraction in metastatic lesions (Fig. ). GO enrichment analysis revealed functional divergence among subclusters: Cluster 1 was enriched in histone modification and autophagy-related processes; Cluster 2 involved cell cycle progression and mitochondrial translation; Cluster 3 was associated with epidermal development; and Cluster 4 was linked to cell migration, angiogenesis, and extracellular matrix organization (Fig. ).

## Functional diversity and developmental plasticity of fibroblast subpopulations in LSCC: enrichment in lymph node metastases
To evaluate the prognostic significance of fibroblasts in LSCC, we first performed a deconvolution analysis on TCGA bulk RNA-seq data. Kaplan-Meier survival analysis based on estimated fibroblast proportions revealed that patients with higher fibroblast abundance had significantly poorer overall survival (Fig. ), indicating the clinical relevance of fibroblasts in LSCC. Subsequently, we identified five transcriptionally distinct fibroblast subpopulations (F0-F4) using unsupervised clustering and visualized their transcriptomic heterogeneity with UMAP projection (Fig. ). Each subpopulation exhibited distinct aggregation characteristics, reflecting notable intra-fibroblast diversity. Correlation analysis revealed significant negative correlations between F1 and F3, as well as F1 and F4 (p < 0.001), and a positive correlation between F2 and F4 (p < 0.05) (Fig. ). Mantel correlation analysis indicated that among the five fibroblast subpopulations, only F4 exhibited a statistically significant correlation with epithelial cells (Mantel’s r: 0.25-0.5, p < 0.01). Distribution analysis of fibroblast subpopulations across tissue types revealed that the proportion of F0 was highest in LN (86.72%, 1,097 cells), compared to 32.44% in PT and 26.87% in NT (Fig. and ). To refine the functional annotation of fibroblast subtypes, we combined canonical CAF marker gene expression with pathway enrichment data (Fig. and ). ANGPT2, a pro-angiogenic factor, RGS5, a pericyte marker, and ACTA2, a hallmark of myofibroblasts, were highly expressed in F0 , . Together with the enrichment of F0 in cytoskeletal organization, Rho/Ras signaling, and angiogenesis-related pathways, these features suggest that F0 may represent a vascular or myofibroblast-like CAF population. HLA-DRA and CD74, established markers of antigen-presenting CAFs (apCAFs), were highly expressed in the F1 subpopulation . In line with this, functional enrichment analysis revealed significant activation of immune-related pathways in F1, including pattern recognition receptor signaling, antigen presentation via MHC class II, and neutrophil-mediated immunity. These features are consistent with an antigen-presenting phenotype, suggesting that F1 may correspond to an apCAF-like subset with immunoregulatory potential in the LSCC microenvironment. F2 exhibited high expression of COL1A1 and TNC, canonical markers of matrix-remodeling CAFs (mCAFs), and also expressed moderate levels of CXCL12 and IL1R1, which have been implicated in immune-regulatory CAF phenotypes , . Combined with its enrichment in extracellular matrix organization and collagen metabolism pathways, these features are consistent with a matrix-remodeling CAF phenotype, with partial overlap with immune-regulatory programs. F3 was characterized by high expression of CXCL12 and IL1R1, aligning with its enrichment in complement activation, immunoglobulin-related, and inflammation-regulatory pathways, pointing toward an immunoregulatory CAF profile. F4 displayed a complex molecular signature. This cluster showed high expression of ACTA2, TNC, HLA-DRA, and CD74, which are markers associated with myofibroblastic, matrix-remodeling, and antigen-presenting CAF phenotypes, respectively. In parallel, GO enrichment analysis revealed pathways related to neurodevelopmental processes such as axonogenesis, tube morphogenesis, and gliogenesis. These combined features suggest that F4 displays a mixed transcriptional program with contractile, immunomodulatory, and neurotrophic functions.
 Slingshot pseudotime analysis revealed a developmental trajectory among fibroblast subpopulations, initiating from F2 and F1, progressing through F3 and F4, and ultimately extending into F0 (Fig. ). Notably, F0 displayed a broad temporal distribution and was present both in intermediate and terminal stages. Pseudotime density analysis confirmed this progression pattern: F2 and F1 peaked at early pseudotime, followed by F3 and F4 in later stages. F0 exhibited a bimodal distribution, with density peaks in both mid and late pseudotime regions, suggesting a dynamic and temporally heterogeneous role in fibroblast evolution (Fig. ). Tissue-specific distribution analysis showed that fibroblasts from PT were enriched in early pseudotime stages, while those from LN were predominantly located in mid-to-late pseudotime stages. This indicates that the metastatic microenvironment may foster more differentiated or specialized fibroblast states (Fig. ). Expression profiling of CAF markers along pseudotime revealed distinct, stage-specific expression dynamics (Fig. ). Activation-related markers (FAP and PDPN) , as well as immunity/inflammatory-related markers (CXCL12 and IL6) , , were highly expressed at the early pseudotime stage and progressively declined toward the terminal stage. ACTA2 and CAV1 exhibited marked upregulation in late pseudotime, consistent with their respective roles in myofibroblastic differentiation and stromal biomechanical remodeling. CAV1 was highly expressed in F0, which has been reported to define a tumor-promoting CAF subset undergoing metabolic reprogramming , and its overexpression in breast cancer stroma can enhance matrix tension and directional tumor cell invasion . COL1A1 displayed a biphasic expression pattern, initially downregulated during mid-pseudotime and subsequently reactivated at the terminal stage.

## Remodeling of fibroblast-epithelial communication in LN highlights CD44-associated COLLAGEN and FN1 pathways
To investigate cell-cell communication alterations in LSCC, we comprehensively analyzed the number and strength of intercellular interactions, as well as communication patterns across PT, NT and LN. Fibroblasts exhibited consistently high levels of outgoing interactions in all tissue types, suggesting their central role in signaling within the tumor microenvironment. Epithelial cells demonstrated substantial incoming interactions, primarily from fibroblasts and endothelial cells (Fig. ). Compared to PT, LN displayed increased interaction frequency and strength, particularly between fibroblasts and epithelial cells, and between epithelial cells and both endothelial and B cells. Notably, the most pronounced enhancement in signaling strength was observed from fibroblasts to epithelial cells (Fig. , ).
 We further analyzed signaling pathways associated with incoming and outgoing interactions across cell types in PT and LN. In PT, epithelial cells received fewer and weaker signals compared to other cell types, whereas in LN, B cells exhibited the lowest incoming signaling, while T cells received the most (Fig. ). In contrast, fibroblasts exhibited strong outgoing signaling in both PT and LN (Fig. ), with distinct pathway preferences at each site. We further identified distinct outgoing signaling patterns of fibroblasts in PT and LN based on pattern classification. In PT, fibroblasts were predominantly associated with Pattern 3, with top contributing pathways including COMPLEMENT, PTN, FGF, CSF, and IL6, which represent key immunoregulatory and growth factor pathways (Fig. ). By contrast, LN fibroblasts shifted toward Pattern 2, with top contributing pathways including FN1, ANGPTL, GAS, PERIOSTIN, and THY1, which are primarily associated with extracellular matrix remodeling and stromal activation (Fig. ).
To further characterize fibroblast-to-epithelial communication remodeling in LN, we examined the ligand-receptor pairs with significantly increased signaling strength. In metastatic lymph nodes, fibroblasts can influence both the epithelial compartment and the vascular niche. For completeness, we therefore included endothelial cells as potential recipients when visualizing fibroblast-derived signaling, although fibroblast-to-epithelial interactions were more abundant and formed the main focus of our analysis. Bubble plot analysis showed that most of the upregulated interactions from fibroblasts to epithelial cells in LN were concentrated in the COLLAGEN and FN1 signaling pathways, mainly involving COL6A1-CD44, COL6A2-CD44, COL6A3-CD44, COL4A1-CD44, COL4A2-CD44, COL1A1-CD44, and COL1A2-CD44 pairs (Fig. ). We next assessed whether the observed increase in signaling strength was accompanied by transcriptional upregulation of corresponding ligand-receptor pairs. Among the upregulated ligand-receptor pairs in LN, COL4A2-CD44, COL4A1-CD44, and FN1-CD44 exhibited significantly elevated expression in LN compared to PT (p < 0.01), all utilizing CD44 as the receptor (Fig. ). This pattern was further supported by violin plots (Figs. and ), which demonstrated higher transcriptional levels of both ligands and CD44 in LN, suggesting transcriptional upregulation likely underlies the enhanced fibroblast-to-epithelial signaling observed during metastasis. Importantly, after adjusting for the number of sending and receiving cells, collagen-CD44 and FN1-CD44 interactions remained among the highest-contributing fibroblast-epithelial signals in lymph nodes (Fig. , Table ). These findings suggest that CD44 may serve as an important epithelial receptor mediating the increased fibroblast-derived signals related to the COLLAGEN and FN1 pathways in LN. To further evaluate this possibility, we next performed functional assays to examine the biological consequences of CD44 perturbation.
To provide experimental support for the predicted fibroblast to epithelial signaling, we established a Transwell coculture system using commercially obtained CAFs and LSCC epithelial cells. Efficient FN1 knockdown in CAFs was confirmed by western blot (Fig. ). After 48 h of coculture, both AMC-HN-8 and TU-686 cells displayed an EMT associated expression shift in the presence of control CAFs, with reduced E cadherin and increased N cadherin compared with epithelial cells cultured alone. Notably, coculture with FN1 knockdown CAFs partially reversed these changes (Fig. ). In wound healing assays, control CAF coculture produced the fastest wound closure in AMC HN 8 cells, whereas FN1 knockdown CAF coculture weakened this pro migratory effect (Fig. ). A consistent trend was observed in TU-686 cells (Fig. ). In Matrigel invasion assays, control CAF coculture yielded the highest number of invaded epithelial cells, while FN1 knockdown in CAFs reduced CAF associated invasion (Fig. ).
To further corroborate these findings in patient tissues, we performed immunofluorescence staining on paired primary tumors and matched lymph node metastases from LSCC patients. Representative images suggested stronger FN1 and CD44 signals in lymph node lesions than in the corresponding primary tumors (Fig. ), and paired quantification across five cases supported the same overall trend (Fig. ). Staining for E-cadherin and N-cadherin indicated a site associated phenotypic shift, with E-cadherin more prominent in primary tumor nests and N-cadherin more evident in the metastatic microenvironment of lymph node lesions (Fig. ). Paired analysis across five cases was consistent with these observations (Fig. ), providing supportive in vivo evidence that FN1-CD44 associated signaling is enhanced in lymph node metastasis and accompanies EMT related changes. Collectively, these results indicate that CAF derived FN1 promotes EMT related remodeling and enhances migration and invasion of LSCC epithelial cells, and that reducing FN1 in CAFs is sufficient to weaken these pro aggressive effects.
To further investigate the cellular basis underlying the increased signaling strength of FN1 and COLLAGEN pathways observed in LN, we examined the specific signaling roles (sender, receiver, mediator, influencer) of six major cell types in both PT and LN. In the COLLAGEN network, fibroblasts remained the dominant signal senders across tissues; however, their main targets shifted from macrophages in PT to epithelial cells in LN. Similarly, in the FN1 network, fibroblasts acted as primary senders in both PT and LN, increasingly targeting epithelial cells in LN, while also retaining central roles as signal influencers (Fig. ). To resolve these interactions at higher resolution, we analyzed fibroblast-epithelial communication at the subpopulation level. In the COLLAGEN pathway, F0-F2 fibroblasts were the predominant signal sources in PT, targeting EP0 epithelial cells, whereas in LN, signaling from F0 and F2 showed increased interactions toward EP2 and EP1 (Fig. ). A similar pattern was detected in the FN1 pathway: F2 was dominant in PT targeting EP0, while F0 and F2 in LN displayed more outgoing signals toward EP2 and EP1 (Fig. ).

## CD44 is overexpressed in metastatic LSCC and functionally promotes tumor progression
To evaluate the clinical relevance and functional role of CD44 in LSCC, we first analyzed its expression using TCGA-LSCC RNA-seq data. CD44 expression was significantly upregulated in tumor samples compared to normal samples (p < 0.001, Fig. ). Kaplan-Meier survival analysis showed that patients with high CD44 expression had worse overall survival than those with low expression (p = 0.017, Fig. ), and multivariate Cox regression analysis identified high CD44 expression as an independent prognostic factor (HR = 3.46, 95% CI: 1.45-8.27, p = 0.005), along with N3 stage (HR = 9.56, 95% CI: 2.08-43.84, p = 0.004) (Fig. ). Stratified analysis by nodal stage revealed that CD44 expression was highest in patients classified as N3 stage (Fig. ).
 To investigate the biological function of CD44, we knocked down CD44 in two LSCC cell lines (AMC-HN-8 and TU686), and Western blot confirmed effective suppression of CD44 protein (Fig. ). Functional assays demonstrated that CD44 knockdown significantly inhibited cell proliferation (EdU assay, Fig. ; Fig. ), invasion (Transwell assay, Fig. ; Fig. ), and migration (wound healing assay, Fig. ; Fig. ) in both cell lines. To verify that the phenotypes were CD44 dependent, we re-expressed CD44 in CD44 knockdown cells. Western blot confirmed reduced CD44 after knockdown and restoration upon re-expression in both AMC-HN-8 and TU-686 cells (Fig. ). EdU assays showed that CD44 re-expression partially rescued the proliferation defect (Fig. ). Similarly, wound healing and Transwell assays indicated partial restoration of migration and invasion after CD44 re-expression in both cell lines (Fig. ). In vivo, xenograft tumor models using AMC-HN-8 cells showed that CD44 knockdown led to a marked reduction in tumor volume and weight (Fig. ). Together, these findings indicate that CD44 functionally promotes tumor cell proliferation, invasion, migration, and tumorigenicity.

## Discussion
LSCC remains a clinical challenge in head and neck oncology, partly due to its tendency for cervical lymph node metastasis, which contributes to disease progression and poor prognosis – . Tumor metastasis is not solely driven by the intrinsic properties of malignant epithelial cells, but is also profoundly influenced by complex interactions within the TME, where CAFs play critical regulatory roles , . In this study, we complement previous efforts by integrating high-resolution scRNA-seq from matched PT, NT, and LN samples with bulk RNA-seq data from the TCGA-LSCC cohort, to investigate epithelial and fibroblast heterogeneity and their associated transcriptional programs across stages of metastasis.
Transcriptional plasticity within epithelial cells has been increasingly recognized as a key driver of tumor progression and metastasis in epithelial malignancies – . While prior single-cell studies in HNSCC have highlighted intertumoral heterogeneity, few have mapped developmental hierarchies of epithelial subpopulations or their link to lymphatic spread in LSCC , . In this study, we identified three malignant epithelial subgroups (EP0-EP2), exhibiting functional split into an EMT-enriched, migratory phenotype (EP0) and a stem-like, highly proliferative phenotype (EP2), with EP1 as an intermediate state. Pseudotime and CytoTRACE analyses revealed divergent trajectories, with EP2 positioned at early stages and EP0/EP1 at later stages, consistent with respective stem-like and invasive phenotypes. Additionally, EP0 and EP1 were more abundant in LN-derived lesions, suggesting their potential involvement in metastatic adaptation. At the same time, the inferred early positioning of the stem-like EP2 subpopulation in pseudotime, together with its low abundance in LN, indicates that metastatic seeding is unlikely to be driven by a single epithelial state and may involve dynamic transitions among multiple subpopulations. These findings are consistent with the well-documented role of EMT in facilitating tumor cell invasion and dissemination . CNV analysis further revealed that aneuploid epithelial cells were predominantly confined to malignant clusters and more abundant in LN samples, showing transcriptomic enrichment for ECM remodeling, angiogenesis, and motility, consistent with the association between genomic instability and invasive characteristics . CNV-based clustering provided additional support for this tissue-level functional evolution. Cluster 1 was primarily localized in primary tumors and correlated with autophagy and chromatin regulation processes, whereas Cluster 2, which showed enrichment in cell cycle and metabolic pathways, was predominantly present in metastatic lymph nodes. This transition implies that epithelial clones experience functional reprogramming during metastatic dissemination. Prognostic analysis demonstrated that elevated expression of core genes from both EP0 and EP2 correlated significantly with poorer survival in the TCGA-LSCC cohort. These results indicate that both phenotypic states likely promote aggressive tumor behavior via distinct molecular mechanisms, and that their transcriptomic profiles may serve as prognostic biomarkers. Moreover, deconvolution analysis revealed that epithelial cell fractions progressively decreased with advancing nodal stage (from N1 to N3), which was associated with worse overall survival.
CAFs are known to support tumor growth, angiogenesis, invasion, and extracellular matrix (ECM) remodeling through diverse mechanisms . While previous work has established the tumor-promoting roles of CAFs in various solid tumors , – , the specific heterogeneity and pseudotemporal evolution of fibroblast states in LSCC, particularly in the context of lymph node metastases, remain uncharacterized. Our study identified five fibroblast subtypes (F0-F4) with distinct functions and pseudotemporal states. Among them, F0 with high expression of CAV1 were markedly enriched in lymph node metastases and occupied intermediate-to-terminal pseudotime stages, suggesting a metastasis-associated terminally differentiated subtype. Functionally, F0 exhibited elevated expression of proangiogenic markers (ANGPT2, RGS5) and myofibroblast markers (ACTA2) , , with enrichment in cytoskeletal organization, Rho/Ras signaling, and angiogenesis pathways. Interestingly, a single-cell study in HNSCC found that fibroblasts within lymph node metastases were primarily composed of myofibroblasts and a distinct CAF subpopulation characterized by high CAV1 expression, suggesting a potential role for CAV1⁺ fibroblasts in shaping the metastatic niche within this tumor context . Pseudotime trajectory analysis further revealed a developmental continuum originating from F2 (matrix-remodeling) and F1 (antigen-presenting), transitioning through F3 (immune-regulatory) and F4, and culminating in F0. This pattern reflects a functional evolution from immune-regulatory and matrix-remodeling roles toward pro-invasive, structurally supportive phenotypes. F4 exhibited a hybrid profile with co-expression of ACTA2, HLA-DRA, CD74, and neurodevelopmental pathway enrichment, which occupied a mid-late phase along pseudotime, possibly representing a transitional state. Notably, F4 was the only subtype significantly correlated with epithelial cells based on deconvolution analysis, suggesting a potential role in stromal-epithelial communication. The expression patterns of canonical CAF markers along pseudotime revealed a phenotypic transition from immune/inflammatory to contractile signatures. Early pseudotime was characterized by predominant expression of immune/inflammatory markers (IL6, CXCL12) and CAF activation markers (FAP, PDPN), which gradually decreased – . In contrast, late pseudotime showed increased expression of ACTA2 and CAV1, aligning with their established roles in myofibroblastic differentiation and biomechanical remodeling, respectively . COL1A1, a matrix-remodeling CAF marker , displayed an initial decrease followed by late-stage recovery in expression, indicating phase-specific ECM reorganization. These patterns support a model of dynamic CAF transition from immune-regulatory to structural and pro-invasive states during tumor progression. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that transcriptional plasticity and tissue-specific cues drive CAF subtype transitions, ultimately generating specialized states such as F0 that likely contribute to metastatic progression in LSCC. Given its distinct transcriptional features and enrichment in metastatic lesions, the F0 subtype may also have potential clinical value as a prognostic indicator, although this possibility will require confirmation in larger patient cohorts.
Fibroblasts consistently exhibited strong outgoing signals across tissue types, especially in LN. The increased frequency and strength of interactions observed in LN, particularly from fibroblasts to epithelial cells, suggest that metastatic progression is accompanied by enhanced stromal-epithelial crosstalk. A key observation was the functional shift in fibroblast-derived signaling pathways between PT and LN. Consistent with this prediction, CAFs with FN1 knockdown showed a reduced ability to induce EMT related marker changes and to promote epithelial migration and invasion in transwell coculture, and paired patient immunofluorescence supported higher FN1 and CD44 signals in LN lesions (Figs. – ). While fibroblasts in PT predominantly engaged in immunoregulatory and growth factor signals (Pattern 3), those in LN showed a switch toward matrix-remodeling and stromal activation signals (Pattern 2). In both FN1 and COLLAGEN pathways, fibroblasts remained the primary senders, while the dominant receivers shifted from macrophages in PT to epithelial cells in LN, indicating a reprogramming of fibroblast signaling toward malignant compartments during metastasis. Subpopulation analysis showed that F0 and F2 fibroblasts as the primary mediators of enhanced COL4A1/COL4A2-CD44 and FN1-CD44 signaling in LN, specifically targeting the EP2 and EP1 epithelial subpopulation. This contrasts with PT, where F0 and F2 mainly targeted EP0, which displays high EMT activity. These observations raise the possibility that fibroblasts play stage-specific roles in tumor progression. In the primary tumor, CAFs predominantly interact with EMT-high epithelial cells (EP0), potentially promoting their acquisition of invasive and migratory capabilities through paracrine signaling . At the same time, EP2 was positioned early in pseudotime but was relatively less abundant in LN. One possible explanation is that, upon dissemination, stem-like EP2 cells may undergo rapid phenotypic conversion toward more EMT-like EP0/EP1 states within the LN microenvironment, rather than persisting as a stable EP2 population. This pattern is consistent with the notion that EMT-driven plasticity promotes invasion and dissemination, whereas partial MET may facilitate metastatic outgrowth at secondary sites – . However, this interpretation remains speculative and requires validation in larger cohorts and functional studies.
Building on our intercellular communication analysis, which identified CD44 as a central epithelial receptor in fibroblast-derived signaling within the LN microenvironment, we further explored its clinical and functional significance in LSCC. CD44 expression was markedly elevated in tumor tissues and reached the highest levels in patients with advanced nodal metastases (N3 stage), suggesting a possible role in promoting lymphatic dissemination. The prognostic relevance of CD44 was underscored by its strong association with overall survival and its identification as an independent risk factor in multivariate analysis. Functionally, CD44 knockdown depressed multiple malignant properties in vitro, including proliferation, migration, and invasion, and significantly reduced tumorigenicity in xenograft models. Re-expression of CD44 in the knockdown background partially rescued these phenotypes (Fig. ), further supporting the specificity of CD44-dependent effects. These results indicate that CD44 actively contributes to the malignant behavior of LSCC cells, rather than being a passive biomarker. This is consistent with prior reports showing that CD44 regulates cancer stemness, motility, and resistance to therapy through its interaction with hyaluronan and extracellular matrix components , . Moreover, recent studies in pancreatic cancer have shown that CAF-secreted SPP1 promotes cancer cell stemness and invasiveness via a CD44-dependent mechanism , reinforcing the functional relevance of CD44 as a CAF-induced driver of metastatic progression. Taken together, these findings support a model in which CD44 serves as both a downstream effector of CAF-derived signaling and an active driver of metastatic progression, making it a promising therapeutic target in advanced LSCC.
In conclusion, our study provides a comprehensive single-cell and transcriptomic framework of LSCC progression, highlighting the transcriptional plasticity and functional heterogeneity of epithelial and fibroblast populations in relation to lymph node metastasis. We identify distinct epithelial states and fibroblast subtypes that may cooperate to shape the metastatic niche, with CD44 emerging as a key mediator of stromal-epithelial signaling. While these findings provide important insights, several limitations must be acknowledged. First, the patient cohort size was limited, and the low abundance of certain subpopulations in specific sites (for example, EP2 in LN) may restrict statistical robustness. Second, although our single-cell analyses nominate candidate cell states and communication programs, transcriptome-based inferences remain indirect and benefit from spatial and functional validation. Consistent with this need, we provide supportive spatial evidence using immunofluorescence staining of paired primary tumors and matched lymph node metastases, while higher resolution approaches such as spatial transcriptomics will be important in future studies. Third, the malignancy scoring strategy used in this study was derived from bulk RNA-seq-based gene signatures and should be interpreted as an approximation of malignant transcriptional programs rather than a definitive single-cell genomic classifier. Finally, in vitro assays may not fully capture the complexity of human metastatic microenvironments. In particular, our coculture experiments used commercially obtained CAFs derived from nasopharyngeal carcinoma rather than patient matched primary LSCC CAFs, which may not fully capture LSCC specific stromal features and should be validated using LSCC derived CAFs in future studies. Despite these limitations, our findings underscore the importance of epithelial-stromal interactions in LSCC metastasis and propose potential molecular targets, such as CD44 and CAF subtypes, for therapeutic exploration. Future studies combining larger patient cohorts with spatial transcriptomics, lineage tracing, and targeted perturbation will be crucial for validating these mechanisms and translating them into clinically actionable strategies.

## Methods

## Patient sample collection
Three patients with newly diagnosed, untreated LSCC presenting with cervical lymph node metastasis were enrolled from the Second Affiliated Hospital of Harbin Medical University. Matched samples were obtained during surgical resection, comprising PT, NT (collected at least 2 cm from the tumor margin), and LN, resulting in a total of nine specimens for scRNA-seq. Detailed clinical and pathological characteristics are provided in Table . This study was conducted under the approval of the Institutional Review Board (IRB#YJSKY2022-047), and all participants gave informed consent consistent with the Declaration of Helsinki.

## Tissue processing, single-cell suspension preparation, and library construction
Following surgical resection, fresh tissue specimens were promptly immersed in pre-chilled RPMI-1640 medium (Gibco), supplemented with 0.04% bovine serum albumin (BSA; MACS Miltenyi Biotec), to preserve cellular integrity. The tissues were finely minced into small pieces with the size of approximately 0.5 mm³. Enzymatic dissociation was then performed at 37 °C for 40 min in a digestion mixture containing collagenase I (1.5 mg/mL; Gibco 17018029), collagenase II (1.0 mg/mL; Gibco 17101015), and trypsin (0.25%; Gibco 15090046), with gentle agitation every 5–10 min to optimize digestion efficiency. Cell suspensions were filtered sequentially through 40-μm nylon meshes (Falcon 352340) to remove undigested debris, followed by centrifugation at 300 × g for 5 min at 4 °C. Red blood cell lysis was carried out by incubating the cell pellet with an equal volume of ACK lysis buffer (MACS Miltenyi Biotec 130094183) for 10 min at 4 °C. After two washes with RPMI-1640 containing 0.04% BSA, viable single-cell suspensions (>85% viability) were assessed using the Luna Automated Cell Counter (Logos Biosystems) via trypan blue staining. The sequencing libraries were constructed utilizing the Chromium Next GEM Single Cell 3ʹ Reagent Kits (10x Genomics) in accordance with the manufacturer’s protocols. Libraries were sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform, generating 150-bp paired-end reads.

## scRNA-seq data processing, quality control, and cell type annotation
Raw 10X Genomics data were initially processed using Cell Ranger and subsequently analyzed with Seurat (version 5.1.0) . Seurat objects were generated with a threshold of a minimum of 300 detected genes per cell and detection of each gene in at least 5 cells, to exclude low-quality cells and rare transcripts.
During quality control, the proportions of mitochondrial, ribosomal, and hemoglobin gene expression were calculated for each cell. Cells with mitochondrial gene expression greater than 25%, ribosomal gene expression less than 3%, or hemoglobin gene expression greater than 1% were excluded from further analysis. A total of 69,322 cells passing quality control were retained for downstream analysis. Data were normalized using the LogNormalize method with a scale factor of 10,000. Principal component analysis (PCA) was conducted, and the top 15 principal components were selected. Batch effects were corrected using the Harmony algorithm with sample identity (orig.ident) as the integration variable. Batch effect correction was first performed using the Harmony algorithm to generate corrected embeddings . Cell clustering was subsequently performed on the Harmony embeddings using the FindNeighbors and FindClusters functions in Seurat (resolution = 0.5). The resulting clusters were annotated based on the expression of canonical marker genes.
To further ensure data quality, we evaluated potential doublets after batch correction. For each sample, doublet scores were calculated using the DoubletFinder (version 2.0.6) . Predicted doublets were annotated in the Seurat object following Harmony integration and excluded from subsequent analyses. All downstream visualizations including UMAP embedding, pseudotime ordering, functional scoring, and cell-cell communication, were re-plotted using only singlet cells. Results before and after doublet removal were highly consistent (Fig. – ).

## Bulk RNA-seq data acquisition and processing
Bulk RNA-seq data for head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC) were initially obtained from the UCSC Xena platform (http://xena.ucsc.edu/), including the STAR-counts matrix (TCGA-HNSC.star_counts.tsv), survival data (TCGA-HNSC.survival.tsv), and clinical annotation files (TCGA-HNSC.clinical.tsv). Samples corresponding to LSCC were subsequently extracted based on clinical information. To perform deconvolution analysis requiring raw count data, RNA-seq HTSeq-count matrices were separately downloaded from the Genomic Data Commons (GDC) portal (https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/). Additionally, the TCGA-LSCC bulk RNA-seq data were used to evaluate the expression pattern of specific genes (e.g., CD44), assess their correlations with clinical features such as nodal stage, and perform survival analyses.

## Bulk deconvolution analysis
To estimate the cellular composition within the bulk RNA-seq data, deconvolution analysis was performed using the BisqueRNA package (version 1.0.5) . Raw count data were normalized using DESeq2 (version 1.42.1) , and ExpressionSet objects were constructed. Single-cell reference profiles were generated from manually annotated Seurat objects and converted using the SeuratToExpressionSet function. Reference-based decomposition was performed using the ReferenceBasedDecomposition function. The resulting cellular fractions were incorporated into clinical metadata. Additionally, the estimated proportions of epithelial cells and fibroblast subclusters were correlated with Hallmark pathway activities , and survival analyses were performed to assess prognostic relevance.

## Identification of malignant and non-malignant epithelial cells
Malignant status of epithelial cells was inferred using a scoring system based on differentially expressed genes (DEGs) derived from the previously acquired TCGA bulk RNA-seq data. Differential expression analysis between tumor and normal LSCC tissues was conducted using the edgeR package (version 4.0.2) . The top 50 tumor-enriched genes and the top 50 normal-enriched genes (adjusted p < 0.01) were selected to compute malignancy and non-malignancy scores, respectively, using the AddModuleScore_UCell function from the UCell R package (version 2.6.2) . For each epithelial cell, a net malignancy score was calculated as the malignancy score minus the non-malignancy score. Cells were ranked by their net scores, and and a threshold of −0.02 was chosen at a local inflection of the score distribution in accordance with a previously published strategy ; cells with net scores >−0.02 were classified as malignant epithelial cells, and those with net scores <−0.02 as non-malignant epithelial cells. To further investigate malignant-associated biological features, Hallmark pathway activities were quantified using the AUCell algorithm , . Correlations between malignancy scores and pathway activities were assessed and visualized using heatmaps generated by the pheatmap package (version 1.0.12).

## Subclustering analysis of malignant epithelial cells and fibroblasts
Malignant epithelial cells and fibroblasts were separately subjected to subclustering analysis using Seurat. Following data normalization and identification of highly variable genes, PCA was performed. The top principal components were selected based on the elbow plot and variance contribution. Subsequent nearest-neighbor graph construction and clustering were conducted using the FindNeighbors and FindClusters functions. The resolution parameters were adjusted appropriately for each cell type (malignant epithelial cells: 0.02; fibroblasts: 0.05) to optimize cluster granularity. Dimensionality reduction and visualization were performed using uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP). DEGs among subclusters were identified using the FindAllMarkers function for subsequent functional enrichment analysis.

## EMT, proliferation and stemness scoring analysis
To assess epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) activity, the AddModuleScore function in Seurat was applied based on the HALLMARK_EPITHELIAL_MESENCHYMAL_TRANSITION gene set curated from the MSigDB database . EMT module scores were computed at the single-cell level, and differences among epithelial subpopulations were evaluated by Wilcoxon rank-sum tests. Proliferation scores were separately calculated using the AddModuleScore_UCell function from the UCell R package (version 2.6.2) . The proliferation signature included six genes (CCND1, MKI67, MCM7, PHGDH, PCNA, JAG1) , – , while the stemness signature consisted of six genes (ALCAM, NID1, TWIST1, CD44, SOX4, ALDH1A1) – . EMT scores were visualized using boxplots, while proliferation and stemness scores, as well as the expression levels of selected representative genes, were displayed as violin plots.

## Pseudotime trajectory and developmental potential analysis of malignant epithelial cells
Freshly annotated malignant epithelial cells were subjected to pseudotime trajectory analysis using Monocle2 (version 2.30.1) . Highly variable genes identified by Seurat were used as input for ordering cells. Dimensionality reduction was performed with the DDRTree algorithm, and pseudotime trajectories were constructed accordingly. Cells were grouped into distinct states based on pseudotime progression. Differentially expressed genes among the pseudotime-defined states were identified using the differentialGeneTest function (q < 0.01). In parallel, CytoTRACE2 (version 1.1.0), an updated version of the CytoTRACE algorithm , was applied to assess the developmental potential of each malignant epithelial cell. Cells with higher CytoTRACE scores were considered to have higher stemness properties. Subsequent Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis was conducted on differentially expressed genes from pseudotime-defined clusters to investigate the biological characteristics associated with each developmental branch.

## Inference of copy number variation in malignant epithelial cells
Copy number variation (CNV) inference was performed using the CopyKAT R package (version 1.1.0) on epithelial cells with default parameters . The raw gene expression matrix was input into CopyKAT, and diploid reference populations were automatically estimated based on expression profiles. Cells were subsequently classified into aneuploid, diploid, or unclassified groups according to their CNV patterns. The inferred CNV results were integrated into the Seurat object as a new assay for downstream analysis. Aneuploid epithelial cells were further subjected to PCA based on their CNV profiles, followed by neighbor graph construction and clustering using the FindNeighbors and FindClusters functions in Seurat. UMAP was applied for visualization of subcluster distributions.

## Trajectory inference and pseudotime-associated gene expression analysis of fibroblasts
To investigate developmental trajectories among fibroblast subpopulations, Slingshot (version 2.10.0) was applied on UMAP embeddings derived from Seurat clustering . Trajectories were inferred using unsupervised lineage detection. Subsequently, tradeSeq (version 1.16.0) was employed to model gene expression dynamics along the pseudotime continuum . For computational efficiency, 2000 fibroblast cells were randomly sampled. Generalized additive models (GAMs) were fitted using the fitGAM function with five knots. Significant genes with dynamic expression patterns across pseudotime were identified by association testing.

## Cell-cell communication analysis
To explore intercellular communication alterations in LSCC, we applied the CellChat R package (version 1.6.1) separately to single-cell datasets from PT, NT, and LN . For each tissue type, cell-cell interaction networks were inferred based on a curated ligand-receptor database embedded in the CellChat package, and communication strength and number were quantified. Subsequently, the CellChat objects were merged to perform comparative analyses across tissues. Total interaction strength, differential pathway activities, and incoming and outgoing signaling patterns were assessed. Communication patterns were further analyzed at the subpopulation level, particularly focusing on fibroblast-epithelial interactions. Differential expression analysis of key ligand-receptor pairs was conducted to identify transcriptional changes associated with metastasis. All analyses were performed according to standard CellChat pipelines.

## Functional enrichment analysis
Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analyses were performed using the clusterProfiler R package (version 4.10.0) to functionally annotate genes of interest . The enrichGO and enrichKEGG functions were used with the default settings, and pathway visualization was performed using dot plots and bar plots implemented in ggplot2 and enrichplot packages.

## Survival analysis
Kaplan-Meier survival analysis was performed using the survival (version 3.5.7) and survminer (version 0.4.9) R packages. The optimal cutoff for gene or signature expression was determined by the surv_cutpoint function. Samples were divided into high- and low-expression groups based on this cutoff. Survival curves were generated with the survfit function and compared by the log-rank test. Hazard ratios (HR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI) were calculated using Cox proportional hazards regression.

## Cell lines and cell culture
The human laryngeal squamous carcinoma cell lines AMC-HN-8 (RRID: CVCL_5966) and TU686 (RRID: CVCL_4916) were obtained from iCell Bioscience Inc. (Shenzhen, China) and BeNa Culture Collection (Beijing, China), respectively. AMC-HN-8 cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM; Meilunbio, MA0212) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; WILBER, FBS-SaHu-500) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (P/S; Meilunbio, MA0110). TU686 cells were maintained in RPMI-1640 medium (Meilunbio, MA0212) supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% P/S. Commercial cancer-associated fibroblasts derived from nasopharyngeal carcinoma (HNaCAF) were purchased from Otwo Biotech (ShenZhen, China) and maintained in the vendor-recommended HNaCAF culture medium (HTX4259M). These CAFs were used in transwell-based coculture assays with LSCC epithelial cells. All cell lines were incubated at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO₂. Cells were passaged every 2–3 days to ensure optimal growth conditions.

## Lentiviral transduction for gene knockdown and CD44 re-expression
Short hairpin RNAs (shRNAs) targeting human CD44 or FN1 and the corresponding negative controls were purchased from Servicebio (Wuhan, China). Lentiviral particles were generated and transduced into AMC-HN-8 and TU686 cells (for CD44 knockdown) or CAFs (for FN1 knockdown) according to the manufacturer’s protocols. Briefly, cells were seeded into 6-well plates and infected with viral supernatants supplemented with polybrene. After 24 h of infection, the medium was replaced with fresh complete medium, followed by puromycin selection to establish stable cell lines. For rescue experiments, CD44 was re-expressed in CD44 knockdown cells by lentiviral transduction of a CD44 expression construct. CD44 and FN1 levels were confirmed by Western blot analysis.

## Western blot analysis
Total protein lysates were extracted from AMC-HN-8 and TU686 cells using RIPA lysis buffer (Beyotime, P0013B) supplemented with protease inhibitor cocktail (Beyotime, ST506). Protein concentrations were determined using a BCA Protein Assay Kit (Beyotime, P0010). Equal amounts of proteins were separated on 10% SDS-PAGE gels and transferred onto PVDF membranes (Millipore, 0.45 µm pore size). Membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat milk in TBST for 1 h at room temperature and incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against CD44 (Servicebio, GB113500), FN1 (Servicebio, GB152093), E-cadherin (Proteintech, 20874-1-AP), N-cadherin (Proteintech, 22018-1-AP) and β-actin (Servicebio, GB15003-100). After washing, membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibody (Servicebio, GB23303) for 1 h at room temperature. Bands were visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) detection reagent (Servicebio, G2161) and captured using a chemiluminescence imaging system. Target proteins and the loading control (β-actin) were detected on the same membrane through sequential antibody incubations, with separate ECL detections for each target. When targets had similar molecular weights, membranes were stripped using a fast stripping buffer (EpiZyme, PS107), re-blocked, and re-incubated with the indicated antibodies prior to subsequent ECL detection. For coculture experiments, epithelial cells were collected from the lower chamber for protein extraction and western blot analysis.

## EdU cell proliferation assay
Cell proliferation was assessed using a 5-ethynyl-2′-deoxyuridine (EdU) incorporation assay kit (Elabscience, E-CKA377) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, AMC-HN-8 and TU686 cells were seeded into 96-well plates at an appropriate density and incubated with EdU working solution for 2 h at 37 °C under 5% CO₂. Cells were subsequently fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, permeabilized with 0.5% Triton X-100, and stained with fluorescent azide reagents provided in the kit. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI. EdU-positive cells were visualized under a fluorescence microscope. The proportion of proliferating cells was calculated from five random fields per well. All experiments were performed in triplicate.

## Scratch wound healing assay
Cells were seeded in 6-well plates and grown to approximately 90% confluence. Linear scratches were made across the monolayer using sterile 200-μL pipette tips. After washing twice with PBS to remove detached cells, serum-free medium was added to suppress proliferation. Images were acquired at 0 and 24 h with an inverted microscope equipped with live-cell imaging capabilities. Wound closure was quantified using ImageJ by measuring the wound area at each timepoint. Each assay was performed in triplicate, and representative results were presented.

## Transwell invasion assay
Cells were serum-starved for 24 h prior to the assay. Invasion ability was assessed using Matrigel-coated Transwell chambers (8 μm pore size; LABSELECT, 14341). The upper surface of the membranes was pre-coated with Matrigel (200 μg/mL; BD Biosciences, 356234) at 37 °C for 1 h. Subsequently, 1 × 10⁵ cells suspended in 200 μL of serum-free medium were seeded into the upper chambers, while 600 μL of medium supplemented with 20% FBS was added to the lower chambers as a chemoattractant. After incubation for 48 h at 37 °C with 5% CO₂, non-invading cells on the upper surface were gently removed. Invaded cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and stained with 1% crystal violet solution. The number of invaded cells was quantified by counting five random fields at 200× magnification. All experiments were performed independently three times with technical triplicates.

## Immunofluorescence staining and quantification of patient tissues
Paired primary tumors (PT) and matched lymph node metastases (LN) were surgically collected from five LSCC patients during operation. Formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) sections (4 μm) were processed for immunofluorescence staining using the TSAPLus multiplex fluorescent staining kit (Servicebio, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Primary antibodies against FN1, CD44, E-cadherin, and N-cadherin were used, followed by DAPI nuclear counterstaining. Images were acquired at 20× magnification under identical settings within each marker pair. For quantification, three regions of interest (ROIs) of comparable area were selected from tumor-associated regions in each section, and ROI-level measurements were averaged to obtain one value per sample. Signals were quantified as mean fluorescence intensity (MFI). Patient-level PT and LN values were used for paired statistical analyses.

## Xenograft tumor formation assay
BALB/c nude mice (4–6 weeks old) were purchased from Beijing Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd (Beijing, China). All animal experiments were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of the Second Affiliated Hospital of Harbin Medical University (Approval No.: YJSDW2024-296). Stably transfected AMC-HN-8 control cells and CD44 knockdown cells were suspended in sterile PBS at a standard concentration. A total of 2 × 106 cells in 100 μL were subcutaneously injected into the right flanks of nude mice (n = 6, per group). Tumor dimensions (length and width) were measured every week using calipers. Tumor volumes were calculated based on these measurements. At the endpoint, mice were euthanized by cervical dislocation (performed by trained personnel), and tumors were excised, photographed, and weighed for further analysis.

## Statistical analysis
All statistical analyses were performed using R (version 4.3.1) and GraphPad Prism (version 10.4.0). Comparisons between two independent groups were conducted using unpaired two-tailed Student’s t test, as appropriate. Paired comparisons of matched patient tissues were analyzed using paired two-tailed t test. For comparisons among multiple groups, one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post hoc test was applied. Correlations were assessed using Spearman’s correlation coefficients. P values <0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Access will be granted upon reasonable request and approval, in accordance with the data sharing policies of the OMIX repository. All processed summary-level datasets are available in the Data .
The analysis code is accessible on GitHub (https://github.com/roxannejm/LSCC-scRNA-CD44). Software tools and version information used in this study are reported in the Methods section. No restrictions apply to accessing the code.
