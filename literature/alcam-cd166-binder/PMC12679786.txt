## Abstract

## Background
Dental follicle stem cell (DFSC) therapy is an excellent option for reducing alveolar bone loss. Preconditioning DFSCs increases their resistance to the disease microenvironment and increases their efficacy. Photobiomodulation therapy (PBMT) is an effective and convenient way to precondition DFSCs. In this study, we evaluated the effects of PBMT-pretreated DFSCs on alveolar bone regeneration and investigated the underlying mechanism.

## Methods
After DFSCs were isolated and characterized, various biological properties of DFSCs following treatment with PBMT were evaluated in viability tests, wound healing assays, alizarin red staining, alkaline phosphatase staining, real-time quantitative PCR, mitochondrial membrane potential assays, and ATP and reactive oxygen species (ROS) measurements. Twenty-four 7-week-old SD rats were used to establish a periodontal defect model and were then divided into 3 groups: the control group, the DFSC group, and the PBMT + DFSC group. Microcomputed tomography and histological examination were conducted at two and four weeks.

## Results
In the in vitro study, the PBMT group showed better osteogenic capability than the control group. The gene expression levels of COL-1α, OCN, and RUNX2 in the PBMT group increased. Moreover, ROS and ATP levels and the mitochondrial membrane potential were greater in the PBMT group than in the other groups. In the in vivo study, the bone volume per total volume (BV/TV) in the PBMT + DFSC group was greater than those in the control and PBMT groups at four weeks. There was more new bone tissue, more trabeculae and higher expression of OCN and COL-I in the slices of the PBMT + DFSC group than in those of the other groups.

## Conclusions
PBMT facilitated bone regeneration in periodontal defect models by enhancing the osteogenic and mitochondrial functions of DFSCs.

## Introduction
Alveolar bone loss significantly compromises periodontal health and is prevalent across all age groups. It can lead to detrimental consequences such as occlusal weakness, tooth mobility, tooth loss, and challenges in restorative and implant procedures [ ]. Alveolar bone loss can occur due to factors such as infection [ ], injury [ ], occlusal trauma [ ], poor lifestyle habits [ , ], and systemic disease [ ]. To address this clinical issue, autologous bone grafts are commonly utilized for alveolar bone defect reconstruction in clinical practice. However, there are limitations such as limited sources, potential immune rejection, a painful process, and unknown donor status [ ]. Nevertheless, allogeneic and xenogenic alveolar bone grafts may result in immune rejection and pathogen transmission [ – ]. Combining regenerative tissue engineering techniques with stem cell therapy represents a promising alternative for reconstructing alveolar bone tissue. Odontogenic mesenchymal stem cells are considered the most suitable cells for alveolar bone regeneration [ , , ]. They demonstrate properties similar to those of bone marrow-derived stem cells but possess notable anti-inflammatory and immunomodulatory capabilities [ , , ].
In particular, dental follicle stem cells (DFSCs), which are odontogenic mesenchymal stem cells, demonstrate outstanding regenerative properties in oral tissue repair [ ]. Compared with BMSCs and other odontogenic stem cells, DFSCs are isolated primarily from the developing third molar dental germ, thus offering advantages such as enhanced plasticity, improved preservation, and reduced ethical concerns. DFSCs are the direct precursor cells of periodontal tissue and are capable of differentiating into bone, lipid, cartilage and nerve in a specific inducing environment [ – ]. Furthermore, recent studies have indicated that DFSCs may also have a beneficial effect in the treatment of inflammatory and autoimmune diseases [ ]. DFSCs are currently considered promising seed cells with significant potential for stem cell-based regenerative medicine treatments [ ].
The harsh microenvironment in vivo reduces the functionality of stem cells, leading to a decrease in the effectiveness of stem cell therapy [ – ]. Given the microenvironment within the body, which is difficult to change, one focus for enhancing the efficacy of stem cell therapy is the preconditioning of stem cells [ ]. Preconditioning DFSCs can increase resistance to the disease microenvironment and enhance the osteogenesis of DFSCs [ ]. The metabolic activity of stem cells is a vital factor that influences their function and destiny [ ]. Hence, the outcome achieved with stem cells can be controlled by regulating their metabolic activity. Photobiomodulation therapy (PBMT) is a noninvasive and convenient way to induce biological regulatory effects in cells via treatment with a laser or light from a light-emitting diode. PBMT has the potential to modulate cellular energy metabolism by rescuing mitochondrial function [ ] and modulating redox metabolism [ ], thereby increasing the efficacy of stem cell therapy [ ]. Mitochondria determine stem cells’ capacity for osteogenic differentiation by regulating the mode and efficiency of energy metabolism [ – ]. Efficient oxidative phosphorylation of mitochondria not only supplies energy but also releases key signalling molecules that promote differentiation, creating a positive feedback loop that accelerates osteogenesis [ , ]. Conversely, mitochondrial dysfunction leads to energy shortage and disrupted signalling, thereby inhibiting osteogenic differentiation [ , ]. In summary, PBMT-based regenerative medicine has the potential to contribute to tissue engineering applications and stem cell therapy [ ].
The goal of this study was to analyse the effects of photobiomodulation-pretreated DFSCs in vitro and in vivo. These findings lay the foundation for further research into PBMT mechanisms and their potential for combination with stem cell therapy in oral applications.

## Materials and methods

## Materials and methods used in vitro studies

## Lighting parameter design
The wavelength of the LED red light source used in this experiment was 635 nm, and the customized scaffold where the light source placed was 1.7 cm away from the cells. The light source was 1.95 cm away from the cells. When the cells in a well were irradiated, the other wells were removed to avoid the mutual influence different treatments. When the irradiation time was 60 s, 120 s, 180 s and 240 s, the energy density was 3 J/cm2, 6 J/cm2, 9 J/cm2 and 12 J/cm2, respectively. The irradiation frequency was once a day.

## Isolation, culture and identification of DFSCs
In this study, a total of five subjects were recruited, and three batches of DFSCs were extracted. The tissues of the dental follicles were collected from teenagers whose third molars were extracted for clinical reasons. The tissues were obtained with approval from Jinan University’s Ethical Committee (ethical approval number: JNUKY-2023-0110). All work was performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki (1964). The dental follicle tissues were then cut into pieces and washed with PBS (Gibco, Waltham, USA), after which they were cultured in DMEM (Gibco, Waltham, USA) supplemented with additives such as 10% FBS (Oricell, Guangzhou, China) and 1% penicillin‒streptomycin (Oricell, Guangzhou, China) in a humidified atmosphere. When they reached 80% confluence on the bottom of the flask, the DFSCs were digested with 0.25% trypsin-EDTA (1x) (Gibco, Waltham, USA) and subcultured to obtain enough cells. When the DFSCs were cultured in osteogenic medium (Oricell, Guangzhou, China) for 21 days, alizarin red staining (Oricell, Guangzhou, China) was performed. When the DFSCs were cultured in adipogenic medium (Oricell, Guangzhou, China) for 21 days, Oil Red O staining (Oricell, Guangzhou, China) was performed. A total of at least 2 × 106 DFSCs were incubated with CD34, CD45, CD105, and CD73 antibodies (BD Biosciences, San Diego, USA). After incubation, a flow cytometry analysis of DFSCs was carried out. When DFSCs from passages 3–5 had good outcomes in terms of identification, they were used for the study.

## Viability tests and wound healing assays
DFSCs were treated with PBMT as depicted in Table , and their viability was evaluated with Cell Counting Kit-8 (Oricell, Guangzhou, China.) at 3 h, 24 h, 48 h and 72 h. The OD value of each well was measured at 450 nm using a plate reader. Cell viability was calculated according to the following formula: Cell viability=[(As-Ab)/(Ac-Ab)], where As is the OD value of the experimental group, Ab is the OD value of the blank group, and Ac is the OD value of the control group.
 In the above experiments, the optimal radiation intensity and duration were determined. When the DFSCs reached 90%−100% confluence in 6-well plates, a vertical line was drawn on each well. The plates were washed with PBS, and DMEM was added. After PBMT (with the optimal radiation intensity and duration), the scratch in each well was observed under a microscope at 0 h, 6 h, 12 h, and 24 h, and the images were analysed, processed and used for calculations, with the following formula: migration area (%) = (wound area at 0 h–wound area at the targeted period)/wound area at 0 h ×100%.

## Alkaline phosphatase (ALP) staining
After the DFSCs were cultured in osteogenic medium for 7 days, alkaline phosphatase (ALP) staining (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) was performed. The cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (Biosharp, Shanghai, China) for 20 min, stained with the prepared alkaline phosphatase working solution for 10 min, and rinsed with PBS three times. The cells in each well were observed and photographed under a light microscope. Quantitative experiments were performed via protein extraction with a BCA protein assay kit (Coolaber, Beijing, China) and an alkaline phosphatase assay kit (Beyotime, Shanghai, China).

## Alizarin red staining
Alizarin red staining (Oricell, Guangzhou, China) was performed after the cells were cultured in osteogenic medium for 14 days, and quantitative experiments were conducted. The OD value was measured at 562 nm using a plate reader. In accordance with the instructions of the calcium quantification kit (Beyotime, Shanghai, China), the calcium content was measured and calculated.

## Real-time quantitative PCR
DFSCs were subsequently cultured in osteogenic medium for 7 days. Total RNA was extracted and reverse transcribed into cDNA using the FastPure® Cell/Tissue Total RNA Isolation Kit V2 (Vazyme, Nanjing, China) and HiScript® III All-in-one RT SuperMix (Vazyme, Nanjing, China). Quantitative real-time PCR (RT‒PCR) was performed on the system (Biosystems, South San Francisco Co., USA) using the ChamQ Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme, Nanjing, China). The primer sequences are shown in Table . Sangon (Sangon, Shanghai, China.) designed the RT‒PCR primers. The data were analysed by the 2−ΔΔCt method.
 

## JC-1 detection of the mitochondrial membrane potential
The groups were as follows: the CCCP group was the negative control group, which presented no mitochondrial membrane potential; the DFSCs in the control group received no treatment; the DFSCs in the PBMT group were treated with PBMT; the DFSCs in the OI group underwent osteogenic induction treatment; and the DFSCs in the OI + PBMT group received both osteogenic induction and PBMT treatment. After the above pretreatment for one week, the cells were digested and made into a cell suspension. The cell suspension was processed and detected by flow cytometry within 2 h according to the JC-1 dyeing solution kit instructions (Biosharp, Shanghai, China).

## Evaluation of cellular ATP content
The groups were as follows: DFSCs in the control group received no treatment; DFSCs in the PBMT group received PBMT treatment for one week; DFSCs were treated with osteogenic induction for one week in the OI group; and DFSCs were treated with both osteogenic induction and PBMT for one week in the OI + PBMT group. In accordance with the instructions of the BCA protein assay kit (Coolaber, Beijing, China) and the ATP content detection kit (Jiancheng, Nanjing, China), the ATP content of the cells was determined and analysed according to the OD value at 636 nm.

## ROS detection in cells
In the control group, the POS group was the ROS-positive group; in the PBMT group, the DFSCs were subjected to osteogenic induction for one week; and in the control group, the DFSCs were subjected to both osteogenic induction and PBMT for one week. According to the instructions of the DCFH-DA detection kit (Biosharp, Shanghai, China), the fluorescence intensity of groups was evaluated by flow cytometry within 2 hours.

## Materials and methods used in vivo studies

## Animal source
In this study, twenty-four male SD rats (7 weeks old) were selected. All the rats used in this study were from Vital River (Beijing, China).

## Generation of a periodontal defect rat model
The periodontal defect model used was described by Tan et al. [ ], as shown in Supplementary Fig. (A). After the rats were anaesthetized via an intraperitoneal injection of 1% pentobarbital sodium solution (40 mg/kg) (Merck, Darmstadt, Germany), a longitudinal incision approximately 1.5 cm long was made from the buccal side to the mesial periodontal separation of the first maxillary molar. After the flap was turned over to expose the alveolar bone, an implant round cylinder bur (diameter of 1.5 mm) was utilized to create a notch (diameter of 1.5 mm, depth of 1.5 mm) under the mesial neck of the first maxillary molar. Twenty-four male SD rats (7 weeks old) were randomly divided into three groups (ethics approval number: IACUC-20230927-04): the control group, the DFSC group, and the PBMT + DFSC group, as shown in Fig. (A).
 

## Cell transplantation
DFSCs in the PBMT + DFSC group were subjected to a PBMT of 6 J/cm2 once a day for three days. The cell injection dose in each group was adjusted to 2 × 106 cells/mL. The cell injection dose for each rat was 4 × 104 cells. The cell suspension and Matrigel matrix glue (Corning, New York, USA) were combined at a volume ratio of 1:1. The cell suspension was equally replaced with pure matrix glue in the control group. Matrigel matrix glue was applied uniformly across all groups to ensure the retention of DFSCs at the periodontal defect site. The cell matrix glue complex was injected into the rat defect site within 2 hours.

## Micro-CT examination
The SD rats in each group were killed via the intraperitoneal injection of excessive 1% pentobarbital sodium solution (Merck, Darmstadt, Germany). After modelling for two and four weeks, the maxillary bones of the rats were removed and subjected to micro-CT.

## Histological examination
Specimens were rinsed under running water overnight. Tissue dehydration was performed through a graded ethanol series. Following dehydration, tissue blocks were immersed in molten paraffin for a minimum of 2 h for infiltration. After infiltration, blocks were individually embedded, sliced, and subjected to HE staining (Beyotime, Shanghai, China), Masson staining (Beyotime, Shanghai, China), and immunohistochemical staining (Proteintech, Shanghai, China).
Sections were stained in hematoxylin solution for approximately 30 min. After three water rinses, sections were briefly placed in a solution of 1% HCl in 75% ethanol for differentiation, followed by water rinsing. Sections were immersed in a bluing solution (neutralizing alkali) for approximately 2 min to achieve nuclear bluing, followed by three water washes. Sections were then counterstained in eosin solution for 2 min. Excess stain was removed by sequentially immersing sections in 100% ethanol I, 100% ethanol II, and 100% ethanol III for 2 min each. Successfully stained sections were air-dried in a fume hood, cleared in xylene for approximately 10 min, and finally mounted with neutral balsam.
Sections were stained with Weigert’s Iron Hematoxylin working solution for 8 min, differentiated with acid-alcohol differentiation solution for 15 s, and rinsed with water. Sections were blued in Masson bluing solution for 5min and rinsed with water for 1 min. Staining was continued in Biebrich Scarlet-Acid Fuchsin (Ponceau S-Fuchsin) solution for 5 min. Sections were rinsed briefly in weak acid working solution (e.g., 1% acetic acid) for 1 min, treated with phosphomolybdic acid solution for 1 min, and rinsed again in weak acid working solution for 1 min. Sections were then stained in Aniline Blue solution for 2 min, followed by a final rinse in weak acid working solution for 1 min. Dehydration, clearing, and mounting steps were then performed as described for HE staining.
After sections were rehydration, sections were immersed in distilled water. The antigen retrieval buffer was brought to a boil, after which the slides were immersed and incubated for 15 min. Slides were then allowed to cool naturally in the buffer before removal. Endogenous horseradish peroxidase (HRP) activity was blocked by incubating sections with 3% hydrogen peroxide for 10 min. Sections were washed three times. Non-specific binding sites were blocked by applying 5% normal goat serum (NGS) in PBS onto the tissues and incubating them at room temperature for 1 h. The primary antibody, diluted 1:1000 in appropriate diluent, was applied to the sections, which were then incubated overnight at 4 °C. After warming to room temperature, sections were washed three times in PBS for 8 min each. HRP-labelled Goat Anti-Rabbit IgG secondary antibody was applied, and sections were incubated at room temperature for 2.5 h. Following three washes in PBS (8 min each), freshly prepared DAB (3,3’-diaminobenzidine) substrate solution was applied for colour development. The reaction was stopped by immersing slides in water after approximately 5 min. Cell nuclei were lightly counterstained with hematoxylin for approximately 10 s. Dehydration, clearing, and mounting steps were performed as described for H&E staining. After mounting, sections were imaged under a microscope, and the results were analysed using ImageJ software.

## Statistical analysis（SECTION 2）
All experimental data were recorded and analysed in Excel 2016 (Microsoft, Redmond, USA). Data analysis and graphing were performed using SPSS Statistics (IBM, Armonk, USA) and GraphPad Prism 10 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, USA). Flow cytometry data were documented by NovoCyte2060R (ACEA, USA) and were analysed using FlowJo software (FlowJo, LLC, Ashland, USA). All samples were compensated for fluorescence spillover and gated to exclude cellular debris and doublets, ensuring that only single cells were included in the subsequent analysis. As shown in Fig. B, cells were identified as DFSCs based on the percentage of cells negative for CD34 and CD45 and positive for CD105 and CD73. As illustrated in Fig. B, JC-1 staining was employed to assess mitochondrial membrane potential in differently treated DFSCs by measuring fluorescence in the FITC and PE channels using flow cytometry. As shown in Fig. C, intracellular ROS production was evaluated across treatment groups using the DCFH-DA assay kit via detection of DCF fluorescence intensity by flow cytometry. All fluorescence images presented in this experiment were acquired using a DMi8 fluorescence microscope (Leica, Germany), as illustrated in Fig. A. In this study, the centre line represents the mean, the error line represents the standard deviation, and all experimental results are biological replicates. The experimental data were tested for a normal distribution and homogeneity of variance. The Wilcoxon test, Kruskal‒Wallis test, and Mann‒Whitney test were used for nonparametric analysis, and multiple comparisons were made using the Dunn’s test. One-sample t tests, unpaired t tests, ordinary one-way ANOVAs, and Welch tests were used for parametric analysis, and multiple comparisons were made using the Dunnett/Dunnett T3 test.
 

## Results

## Culture, identification, and characterization of DFSCs
As depicted in Fig. (A), DFSCs were isolated from the developing third molar dental germ and observed to have migrated out of the tissue. Subsequent passages resulted in DFSCs in good condition with a typical morphology and accelerated growth. Flow cytometry analysis revealed high expression of the mesenchymal-associated markers CD105 and CD73, whereas the haematopoietic-related marker CD34 and the leukocyte common antigen CD45 were negative, as shown in Fig. (B). To confirm the multidifferentiation potential of DFSCs, they were cultured in osteogenic or adipogenic media for 21 days. Mineralized nodules and lipid droplets are formed by DFSCs, as depicted in Fig. (C). These findings demonstrated that the DFSCs used in this study exhibited high purity and proper functionality, ensuring the validity of the study.

## PBMT had a positive effect on DFSC proliferation and migration
As shown in Fig. (A), The results indicated a trend where cell viability initially increased but then decreased with increasing irradiation energy density at all time points. When DFSCs were exposed to irradiation at 6 J/cm2, the most significant effect on the proliferation of DFSCs was observed at 24 h, 48 h, and 72 hours.
 As depicted in Fig. (B), Compared with that in the control group, the migration rate in the PBMT group, with an energy density of 6 J/cm2, did not differ significantly after 6 h. However, there was a notable difference after 12 h and 24 h (n = 3, *P < 0.05). Following this time frame, the migration rate of the PBMT group was significantly greater than that of the control group (n = 3, *P < 0.05). PBMT promoted the proliferation and migration of DFSCs, with an energy density of 6 J/cm2 identified as optimal for subsequent experiments.

## PBMT effectively improved DFSCs’ osteogenic ability (SECTION 2)
Alkaline phosphatase (ALP) is a fine signal that indicates early osteogenic induction activity [ ]. After seven days of osteogenic induction, there were more bluish violet complexes in the PBMT group than in the control group. Furthermore, compared with that in the control group, the ALP content in the PBMT group was significantly greater (n = 3, *P < 0.05), as shown in Fig. (C). Alizarin red staining is a typical approach used to visualize intracellular deposits that form mineralized nodules [ ]. A macrograph and quantitative analysis of the calcium nodules are shown in Fig. (D). There were more scarlet complexes in the PBMT group than in the control group. Quantitative analysis of scarlet complexes revealed that the content of calcium nodules increased significantly in the PBMT group (n = 3, ***P < 0.001). There was more calcium staining in the PBMT group than in the control group (n = 3, *P < 0.05).
RUNX2, OCN, and COL-1α expression was highly correlated with osteoblastic differentiation, bone formation, and skeletal morphogenesis. The statistical results revealed that the expression levels of COL-1α, OCN, and RUNX2 in the PBMT group changed. The expression levels of COL-1α, OCN, and RUNX2 were significantly greater than those in the control group (n = 3, *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01), as shown in Fig. (E).

## PBMT-pretreated DFSCs presented increased mitochondrial function
Stem cell fate can be modulated through targeted enhancement of mitochondrial function. Mitochondrial functional enhancement is synergistically characterized by high membrane potential (ΔΨm), elevated ATP production, and controlled levels of reactive oxygen species (ROS) [ – ].
The JC-1 probe is a fluorescent indicator that is used to identify changes in the membrane potential of mitochondria. In Fig. (A), DFSCs treated with PBMT presented increased red fluorescence and decreased green fluorescence. This observation indicated that PBMT had an effect on DFSCs, which increased the membrane potential of mitochondria associated with cell viability and energy metabolism. These findings further substantiated the regulatory role of PBMT in the cellular biological functions of DFSCs. As shown in Fig. (B), Flow cytometry analysis revealed that, compared with those in the control group, after osteogenic induction, the cells presented increased green fluorescence and decreased red fluorescence, indicating an increase in the mitochondrial membrane potential after PBMT, which was consistent with the results obtained by fluorescence microscopy.
In Fig. (D), ATP content measurements revealed that, in comparison with the control group, cells subjected to osteogenic induction presented increased ATP production, while cells treated with PBMT presented a significantly greater ATP content than did untreated cells (n = 3, *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001).
The microscopic observations shown in Fig. (E) revealed more pronounced green fluorescence in the PBMT group than in the control group, indicating an increase in ROS content. Additionally, as depicted in Fig. (C), flow cytometry analysis revealed that the average intensity of the green fluorescence was greater in the PBMT group than in the control group (n = 3, ***P < 0.001), with the peak value approaching that of the positive control group. In summary, PBMT-pretreated DFSCs presented increased mitochondrial function.

## PBMT-pretreated DFSCs repaired periodontal defects (SECTION 2)
In Supplementary Fig. (B), DFSCs cultured in Matrigel were in good condition and suitable for cell transplantation. Two and four weeks after model establishment and injection, the periodontal defects of the rats were detected by CT scanning and 3D reconstruction to observe the formation of new bone. Bone volume per total volume (BV/TV) was an excellent indicator for evaluating the degree of defect repair in each group. As shown in Fig.  (D), BV/TV in the experimental groups was greater than that in the control group at four weeks (n = 4,*P < 0.05,***P < 0.001). The PBMT + DFSCs group was significantly different from the DFSC group (n = 4, *P < 0.05).
The HE and Masson staining results are shown in Fig. (C). There was more new bone tissue and more trabeculae in the DFSC group and PBMT + DFSC group than in the control group in the second week after surgery, and bone trabecular formation in the PBMT + DFSC group was greater than that in the DFSC group. Fibre tissue was visible in the periodontal defect area of the rats in every group, and the control group had less new bone tissue, fewer bone trabeculae, and more fibrous tissue than the other two groups did. At four weeks after surgery, a significant reduction in fibrous tissue in the defect area was observed in every group. Many new bone tissue and mature bone trabeculae with dense arrangements were observed in the bone defect area of the PBMT + DFSC group and DFSC group. In Fig. (A) and (B), the DFSC and PBMT + DFSC groups showed an excellent ability to repair periodontal defects in rats. Compared with the DFSC group, the PBMT + DFSC group exhibited better expression of COL-1α osteogenesis-related proteins at 2 weeks (n = 4, ***P < 0.001, ****P < 0.0001). The PBMT + DFSC group presented increased expression of the osteogenesis-related protein COL-1α at 4 weeks (n = 4, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001, ****P < 0.0001). Compared with the DFSC group, the PBMT + DFSC group presented better results in terms of the expression of OCN osteogenesis-related proteins at 4 weeks (n = 4, *P < 0.05). In conclusion, these results suggested that photobiomodulation-pretreated DFSCs had an enhanced ability to repair periodontal defects in rats.
 

## Discussion
Good periodontal conditions are the foundation of oral health. The loss of periodontal bone caused by various factors can have serious consequences. With the development of tissue engineering, methods for repairing alveolar loss using stem cells as the main focus have received increasing attention. DFSCs are neural crest cells that can self-renew and differentiate in multiple directions. In addition, DFSCs are the direct precursor cells of periodontal tissue and can form the periodontal ligament, dentin, and alveolar bone. Above all, DFSCs are appropriate seed cells for improving alveolar loss.
There is currently no standardized identification method for DFSCs. Various studies have reported the expression of CD29, CD44, CD73, CD90, CD105, CD146 and CD166 in DFSCs [ , – ]. Conversely, these cells do not express the CD24, CD31, CD34 and CD45 markers [ , ]. Furthermore, DFSCs can differentiate into osteoblasts, chondrocytes, or adipocytes when cultured in appropriate induction media [ , ]. In this study, DFSCs presented positive expression of CD73 and CD105, negative expression of CD34 and CD45, and osteoblastic and adaptogenic differentiation ability and could be used for subsequent experiments.
PBMT is a promising strategy for enhancing the efficacy of stem cell therapy through the modulation of cellular activity. The effects of photobiomodulation depend on various factors, such as the light source [ , ], energy density [ ], duration, and pulse structure [ , ]. Red or near-infrared light in the 600–1100 nm range is widely utilized for PBMT, which aligns with the specific wavelength of the custom light source employed in this study [ , ]. Numerous studies have indicated that PBMT promotes proliferation at lower doses (above 2 J/cm2), whereas PBMT has inhibitory effects at higher doses (16 J/cm2 or above), indicating a dose-dependent response [ , – ]. PBMT is also capable of facilitating the differentiation of susceptible stem cells and progenitor cells, thereby increasing the rate of tissue healing [ , ]. As shown in our study, after exposure to a 6 J/cm² irradiation dose, PBMT facilitated DFSC proliferation, migration, and osteogenesis.
DFSCs after PBMT showed increased ATP contents, high membrane potential and elevated ROS production in in vitro cellular assays. Although it has been nearly 50 years since the concept of PBMT was proposed, its mechanisms of action still need to be determined. In recent years, significant progress has been made in this area of research. One of the most accepted mechanisms is that PBMT induces retrograde mitochondrial signalling by activating the chromophore Cox [ – ]. The chromophore Cox binds to the mitochondrial electron transport chain, which mediates electron transport from cytochrome c to molecular oxygen [ ]. There is evidence that Cox acts as a photoreceptor and transducer of light signals in the red and near-infrared regions of the spectrum, and PBMT appears to increase the electron availability of molecular oxygen reduction in Cox’s catalytic centre, increasing the mitochondrial membrane potential and the levels of ATP, cAMP, and ROS [ , ]. ROS have unique effects on cells: low concentrations are beneficial, high concentrations are harmful, short-term exposure is beneficial, and long-term exposure is harmful [ – ]. Normal mitochondrial metabolism results in low levels of ROS; however, the level of ROS increases when the mitochondrial membrane potential either increases or decreases [ ]. In different studies, the effects of PBMT on the intracellular ROS content differ [ ], which may be related to different processing and measurement time points. In normal cells, Cox’s absorption of light leads to an increase in the mitochondrial membrane potential and a brief burst of ROS. However, when the mitochondrial membrane potential is low due to preexisting oxidative stress, excitotoxicity, or inhibition of electron transport, light absorption causes the mitochondrial membrane potential to increase towards normal levels, and ROS production decreases [ ]. In this study, an increase in the cellular ROS level was detected for a short time after PBMT, which may have resulted from a brief increase in the amount of ROS resulting from increased mitochondrial membrane potential. Enhanced mitochondrial function is defined by high membrane potential, increased ATP production, and regulated ROS levels [ – ]. These changes indicated that PBMT improved the mitochondrial functions of DFSCs.
The mitochondrial function of stem cells is a core regulatory node for periodontal bone regeneration. Moreover, it is a key point for the successful directed differentiation of stem cells and enhances periodontal bone tissue regeneration to modulate mitochondrial function and dynamics properly [ – ]. Mitochondrial function can influence the regeneration process by providing energy [ , ], regulating inflammatory responses [ , ], and affecting the fate determination of stem cells [ , , ]. During the regeneration of periodontal tissues (including alveolar bone, cementum, and periodontal ligament), the proliferation, differentiation, and mineralization activities of stem cells and osteoblasts are highly dependent on the normal function of mitochondria. Mitochondrial dysfunction can impair the energy supply required for cellular biochemical reactions, leading to a decline in regenerative capacity [ , , ]. Mitochondria serve as crucial organelles that regulate cellular energy metabolism; thus, modulating mitochondrial metabolism may increase the degree of mesenchymal stem cell differentiation and enhance the efficacy of stem cell therapy [ – ]. In this study, PBMT-pretreated DFSCs clearly presented enhanced mitochondrial function and osteogenic ability. PBMT may increase therapeutic efficacy by improving the mitochondrial function and osteogenic ability of DFSCs in the context of alveolar bone loss.
In accordance with the study by Tan et al. [ ], this model was developed to simulate alveolar bone loss to verify the treatment effect of PBMT-pretreated DFSCs. The Matrigel matrix solidifies into a gelatinous three-dimensional matrix, making it a suitable scaffold material in cellular and tissue engineering applications [ , ]. Based on our experimental findings, DFSCs are excellent seed cells that promote alveolar bone regeneration. In addition, PBMT enhanced the therapeutic potential of DFSCs in the context of alveolar bone loss.
In this study, innovative PBMT-pretreated DFSCs were used for periodontal defect repair. PBMT-pretreated DFSCs without gene editing or exogenous drug intervention have the advantages of convenient operation, low cost and few side effects. They are suitable for stem cell pretreatment protocols in clinical scenarios, especially for patients who are sensitive to light exposure treatment. PBMT can effect various metabolic changes in DFSCs, and understanding the changes in metabolic factors can help reveal the mechanism by which PBMT affects DFSCs. However, no western blot experiments were performed in this study to verify the osteogenic effect of protein levels. This study examined only the mitochondrial membrane potential, ATP, and ROS of DFSCs after PBMT. There are no specific experiments to prove the potential mechanism involved. Future research may elucidate the intricate mechanisms by which PBMT modulates mitochondrial function and oxidative stress within cells and the relationship between energy metabolism and osteogenesis. The mechanism by which PBMT-pretreated DFSCs treat periodontal defects will be explored.

## Conclusion
Our results revealed that photobiomodulation-pretreated DFSCs had better proliferation, migration and osteogenic differentiation abilities than DFSCs did. In addition, PBMT may improve the mitochondrial function of DFSCs. DFSCs are excellent seed cells that promote alveolar bone regeneration, and PBMT significantly enhances the therapeutic effect of DFSCs in a periodontal defect model.
