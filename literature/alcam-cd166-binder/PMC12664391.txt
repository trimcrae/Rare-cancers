## Background
Observational studies have suggested a potential association between autoimmune diseases and anemia, but the direction of causality remains unclear. To investigate the potential causal relationship between autoimmune diseases and anemia using Mendelian randomization (MR).

## Methods
We conducted a bidirectional two-sample MR study using summary-level genetic data from the EBI and FinnGen databases. We examined 21 autoimmune diseases as exposures and anemia as the outcome, and vice versa. Multivariable MR, meta-analysis, and two-step mediation analysis involving 43 candidate mediators were also performed. Proteomic MR and colocalization analyses were used to validate causal relationships involving proteins. Functional annotations including KEGG pathway, GO enrichment, protein–protein interaction networks, and drug enrichment analyses were performed to identify potential therapeutic targets.

## Results
Genetically determined type 1 diabetes mellitus (T1DM), rheumatoid arthritis (RA), and primary biliary cholangitis (PBC) were associated with an increased risk of anemia (P < 0.05 in meta-analysis). Reverse MR analyses suggested potential bidirectional causality between anemia and mixed connective tissue disease, hypothyroidism, and asthma. Mediation analysis indicated that hypothyroidism may contribute to anemia by elevating IgG levels. Proteomic and colocalization analyses identified several proteins associated with anemia (e.g., MCCD1, TMEM106B), T1DM (e.g., CTRB1, CTRB2, IL7RA), RA (e.g., TGFBI, IL1RN), and PBC (e.g., ALDH2, CXCL16).

## Conclusion
This MR study supports a causal effect of certain autoimmune diseases on anemia risk and highlights potential protein targets for intervention, offering new insights for anemia prevention and treatment.

## Introduction
Autoimmune diseases (AIDs) arise from the breakdown of immunological self-tolerance, wherein the adaptive immune system erroneously attacks healthy cells, tissues, and organs [ ], leading to chronic damage and affecting approximately 7.6–9.4 % of the global population [ ]. The pathogenesis of AIDs is complex, involving genetic, environmental, and immune regulatory factors [ ]. Although various immunosuppressive and immunomodulatory therapies are currently used in clinical practice, their efficacy remains suboptimal, and long-term use may result in serious side effects [ ]. These diseases are characterized by the production of autoantibodies and abnormalities in immune cell function. Treatment typically involves immunomodulatory or immunosuppressive drugs, which can impair immune function and increase the risk of infection. While research primarily focuses on the relationship between AIDs patients and respiratory infections, it is crucial to emphasize that major health risks for this group include severe infections and anemia [ ].
Anemia is a pervasive global health issue linked to poor health outcomes, increased morbidity and mortality, and substantial health and economic costs. Anemia can result from numerous conditions that cause blood loss, shorten red blood cell lifespan, or reduce hemoglobin or red blood cell production. Progress in reducing the burden of anemia has been slow and uneven globally. In 2021, the global prevalence of anemia across all age groups was 24.3 %, corresponding to 1.92 billion cases. Significant variations in anemia burden were observed by age, gender, and geographic location, with children under five, women, and individuals in sub-Saharan Africa and South Asia particularly affected. In 2021, anemia resulted in 52 million years lived with disability (YLD), with the YLD rate decreasing as the socio-demographic index increased [ ]. It remains unclear whether anemia affects specific autoimmune diseases or if there is a closer association with particular types.
Mendelian randomization (MR) analysis is a powerful tool in genetic epidemiology, widely used to explore causal relationships between diseases. Using naturally occurring genetic variations as instrumental variables, MR analysis provides strong statistical evidence for potential causal relationships between diseases [ ]. MR analysis overcomes biases caused by unrecognized confounding factors, reverse causation, and measurement errors [ ]. Thus, MR analysis can serve as an alternative to randomized controlled trials. To conduct MR analysis, three assumptions must be satisfied: (1) the genetic variation must be strongly associated with the exposure; (2) the genetic variation should not be influenced by potential confounding variables; (3) the genetic instrument variable influences the outcome only through the exposure [ ]. The second and third assumptions are collectively referred to as the independence of horizontal pleiotropy and can be statistically tested [ ]. Multivariable Mendelian Randomization (MVMR) analysis broadens the scope of MR analysis, allowing the exploration of relationships between multiple exposure factors and diseases within a unified analytical framework.
Through conducting MR studies, we assess the causal relationship between autoimmune diseases and anemia across diverse data sources, further determining if genetic variations intrinsic to different autoimmune diseases are bidirectionally linked to anemia's occurrence and severity. We also conducted a multivariate MR analysis to account for inter-disease associations, thereby enhancing the reliability of the findings. Furthermore, we identified 43 potential mediators, including blood cell counts, immunoglobulin levels, and cytokine concentrations potentially altered by autoimmune diseases and involved in hematopoiesis, to examine intermediary mechanisms between autoimmune diseases and anemia via a two-step mediation analysis. By systematically reviewing the literature and analyzing publicly available genomic data, we anticipate providing novel insights into the cross-pathological mechanisms between autoimmune diseases and anemia, offering a genetic foundation for future clinical interventions and therapeutic strategies.
With the advancement of human proteomics, more than 18,000 protein quantitative trait loci for over 4000 proteins have been identified through large-scale proteomics studies, providing valuable resources for exploring the causal relationships between circulating proteins and human diseases using Mendelian randomization designs. In this study, we conducted a genome-wide MR analysis to explore the causal effects of circulating proteins on the risk of autoimmune diseases and anemia, and further identified proteins with pleiotropic effects.

## Research design and data sources
2.1

## Ethical proof
Although this study does not involve humans or animals, we are committed to preserving the rights and confidentiality of historical documents. Since all analyses in this paper are based on publicly available summary data, this study does not require additional ethical approval from our institutional review board.

2.2

## Study design and genetic instrument selection
 depicts the MR study design and the assumptions underlying our research. Genetic variants must meet three key criteria to be considered valid instrumental variables (IVs): (1) They must be robustly associated with the exposure; (2) They must not correlate with potential confounders of the exposure-outcome relationship; (3) They must be influenced solely by the exposure [ ]. To examine the direct effects of various autoimmune diseases on anemia, we utilized a multivariable MR approach, extending the traditional univariable MR. This method adjusts for the inherent interactions among the SNPs analyzed in MR, which frequently have shared associations with various autoimmune conditions. In our study, the SNPs for multivariable MR were pooled as instrumental variables for each exposure, thereby clarifying the intricate network of associations. Furthermore, we examined the associations between circulating proteins and autoimmune diseases (RA, T1DM, PBC), as well as anemia. Coloc analysis was employed to verify the causal relationship between circulating proteins and susceptibility to outcomes. Subsequently, we conducted protein-protein interaction analysis and druggability assessment to prioritize potential therapeutic targets. 

2.3

## Data sources for exposures, mediators, and outcomes
Recognizing the potential for biased estimates when utilizing diverse populations, we confined our MR study population to individuals of European ancestry. The exposure encompasses 21 distinct autoimmune diseases, and compared our analysis with data from the FinnGen consortium R11 and the EBI database. The following autoimmune diseases were considered for inclusion in our analysis: Rheumatoid arthritis, Systemic lupus erythematosus, Sjogren's syndrome, Graves' disease, Hashimoto's thyroiditis, Coeliac disease, Type 1 diabetes, Primary biliary cholangitis, Mixed connective tissue disease, Polymyositis, Ankylosing spondylitis, Psoriasis, Crohn's disease, Ulcerative colitis, Inflammatory bowel disease, Hypothyroidism, Juvenile idiopathic arthritis, Sarcoidosis, Myasthenia gravis, Idiopathic thrombocytopenic purpura, Asthma. We systematically reviewed and summarized the characteristics of each disease, presenting the data in . Notably, 21 diseases in the FinnGen consortium are classified using ICD-9 and ICD-10 codes. The FinnGen consortium excluded individuals with uncertain gender, genotype missingness exceeding 5 %, heterozygosity beyond ±4 SDs, and non-Finnish ancestry. Logistic regression was used to calculate all genetic association effect sizes, adjusted for age, sex, and genetic principal components. All 21 diseases in the EBI database have been published online. However, the analytical methods and controlled confounders vary due to the diverse origins of the research teams. 
Following a literature review of observational and MR studies, we identified 43 potential mediators ( ), including blood cell counts, immunoglobulin levels, and cytokines that may be altered by autoimmune diseases or are involved in hematopoiesis. First, these mediators likely exist along the pathway from autoimmunity to anemia, as informed by the collective scientific knowledge. Second, these mediators represent potential accessible clinical interventions. Third, GWAS data for these mediators should be available in individuals of predominantly European ancestry from large consortia or cohorts, with minimal overlap with the GWAS datasets for exposure and outcome. Blood cell counts include: basophils, eosinophils, lymphocytes, monocytes, neutrophils, and white blood cells; serum immunoglobulin levels include IgA, IgM, IgG, IgE; cytokine concentrations altered by autoimmune diseases and involved in hematopoiesis include IL-1α、IL-1b、IL-2、IL-3、IL-4、IL-6、IL-7、IL-8、IL-10、IL-11、IL-12、IL-13、IL-15 Ra、IL-17、IL-23、IL-27、ILT-2、ILT-4、TGF-β1、TGF-β2、TGF-β3、TNF-α、IFN-γ、SCF sR、G-CSF、GM-CSF、Erythropoietin、Thrombopoietin、Thrombopoietin levels、IGF 1、PDGF-AA、PDGF-BB、PDGF-CC. We screened for mediators of the causal relationship between autoimmune diseases and anemia based on the following criteria: (1) the mediator should be causally related to anemia; (2) the mediator should exert a direct causal effect on anemia, independent of autoimmune diseases; (3) autoimmune diseases should causally precede the mediator, not vice versa; (4) the correlation between autoimmune diseases and the mediator should be inversely related to that between the mediator and anemia [ ]. Each mediator's genetic instrument should be genome-wide significant and independent (P < 1.00 × 10−5; LD r2 < 0.01 within 500 kb).
The anemia outcome was derived from FinnGen consortium R11, encompassing 138,342 European individuals (33,700 cases and 104,642 controls). The anemia dataset from the EBI database comprises 484,598 Europeans (5259 cases and 479,339 controls), and 9,587,836 SNPs. All cases were confirmed to have anemia, all participants were of European ancestry, and additionally, informed consent was obtained from all participants.

2.4

## Genetic instrument variable selection
To validate the two-sample MR hypothesis that exposure to autoimmune diseases is associated with anemia, we utilized independent single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) significantly correlated with the exposure at a genome-wide level (P < 5 × 10^-6). The selection of these SNPs ensured minimal linkage disequilibrium (r2 < 0.001) within a cluster window exceeding 10,000 kb, confirming their independence. Adhering to the same inclusion criteria would result in too few SNPs for the analysis between exposure and mediator, and mediator and outcome; thus, we adopted the following criteria. The inclusion criteria for instrumental variable SNPs in MR analysis are: P < 1 × 10^-5, r2 < 0.01, within a cluster window larger than 500 kb. To refine the hypothesis, we quantified the phenotypic variation explained by the full set of SNPs and assessed the strength of our instrumental variables using the F-statistic (β2/se2). An F-statistic above 10 indicates a robust instrument [ ]. To mitigate bias from weak instruments, we calculated the phenotypic variation explained by each instrumental variable using R2: R2 = β2/[β2 + se2∗(n-2)], where N represents the sample size [ ].

2.5

## MR analysis
We used the Wald ratio to estimate the impact of exposure on each instrumental variable (IV) outcome and subsequently applied the random-effects inverse variance weighting (IVW) method to aggregate the effect sizes of each IV. Additionally, the MR-Egger and weighted median methods complemented IVW. The weighted median method requires that at least 50 % of the analytical weight derives from variables with valid instruments, while the weighted mode method necessitates identifying the largest subset of instruments exhibiting consistent causal effects. To adjust for multiple testing in our analysis, the False Discovery Rate (FDR) correction method, which aims to control the false discovery rate—the proportion of hypotheses incorrectly declared significant—was used. We defined statistical significance of MR effect estimates as an FDR of less than 5 % to adjust for multiple testing.
To control for potential confounding factors, an MVMR analysis was conducted with exposures causally related to the outcome [ ]. To account for additional confounding factors, including smoking, alcohol consumption, and obesity, we also performed an additional MVMR2 analysis, considering anemia, smoking, alcohol consumption, and obesity as exposures and autoimmune diseases as outcomes [ , ]. In the MVMR analysis, the IVW method was the primary approach, complemented by the MR-Egger method. A fixed-effects model was used in conjunction with MR results from FinnGen and the EBI database.

2.6

## Diversity and sensitivity analysis
If the Cochran Q statistic indicates that the difference in individual effect sizes is due to actual differences between SNPs rather than sampling error, a heterogeneity test is conducted. A P-value <0.05 indicates the presence of heterogeneity. If the intercept deviates from zero, the MR-Egger regression method is used to identify potential horizontal pleiotropy [ ]. The intercept in MR-Egger regression represents the average pleiotropy of the instruments. Therefore, if the intercept from the MR-Egger test is significantly different from zero, it indicates the presence of pleiotropy. Funnel plot asymmetry can also be considered an indicator of horizontal pleiotropy [ ]. The MR-PRESSO test is designed to identify and correct outliers in IVW linear regression, including the MR-PRESSO global test, outlier detection, and distortion detection. To detect the robustness and consistency of the results, an analysis removing each SNP one by one was performed. All F-statistics were above the conventional threshold of 10. All statistical analyses and data visualization were completed in R version 4.3.3.

2.7

## Directional verification
We utilized the Steiger test to ascertain whether the observed causal relationships were influenced by reverse causality. A P-value of less than 0.05 suggests that the Steiger test does not endorse the presence of reverse causal effects between the plasma proteome and the outcomes.

2.8

## Pathway enrichment and protein-protein interaction analysis
We conducted a comprehensive analysis to elucidate enriched pathways, prioritize therapeutic targets for outcomes, and assess the druggability of causal proteins. This included Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analysis and Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis, categorized into Molecular Function (MF), Biological Process (BP), and Cellular Component (CC), to identify significantly enriched pathways among the causal proteins. Additionally, we utilized STRING to map the protein-protein interaction network for the identified proteins (https://cn.string-db.org).

2.9

## Colocalization and drug enrichment analyses
Colocalization analysis was performed to ascertain whether shared causal genetic variants exist between exposure and outcome, thereby reinforcing the validity of MR findings. For proteins yielding positive MR outcomes, we scrutinized SNPs within a ±1 MB interval surrounding the genes associated with these proteins (cis-pQTLs) and their colocalization with autoimmune diseases, anemia. This analysis is predicated on five hypotheses: H0 posits that selected SNPs are unrelated to both protein A and disease B; H1 suggests a relation to protein A but not disease B; H2 suggests a relation to disease B but not protein A; H3 indicates a relation to either protein A or disease B, with the SNPs being independent; H4 asserts a relation to both protein A and disease B, indicating a shared SNP. Given the constraints on colocalization analysis' power, we prioritized genes with a combined posterior probability (PPH3+PPH4) of 0.75 or higher. Subsequently, proteins identified as colocalization positives underwent drug enrichment analysis to discover associated medications.

## Results
3.1

## Bidirectional two-sample MR
In two-sample MR studies, 21 autoimmune diseases from the EBI database and the FinnGen consortium were analyzed using IVW method ( ).
Analysis of anemia data from the EBI database revealed that genetically predicted Type 1 diabetes (OR = 1.001, 95 %CI = 1.000–1.001, P = 0.002), Rheumatoid arthritis (OR = 1.001, 95 %CI = 1.001–1.002, P < 0.0001), and Primary biliary cholangitis (OR = 1.000, 95 %CI = 1.000–1.001, P = 0.001) have a causal relationship with anemia. This association applies to exposure data from both the FinnGen consortium and the EBI database. However, the genetic associations between Coeliac disease, Sjogren's syndrome, Graves' disease, Hashimoto's thyroiditis, Sarcoidosis, Inflammatory bowel disease, Asthma, Myasthenia gravis, and anemia differed between the FinnGen consortium and the EBI database. Nevertheless, after meta-analysis, the combined effect size of Coeliac disease using the probability instrumental variable weighted (PIVW) method was <0.05, indicating a potential genetic association between Coeliac disease (OR = 1.000, 95 %CI = 1.000–1.001, P = 0.0002) and anemia. The genetically predicted Hypothyroidism and Mixed connective tissue disease, although showing a causal relationship between the FinnGen consortium and the EBI database, did not reach significance after meta-analysis with the PIVW combined effect size >0.05, possibly due to the inherently weak genetic association between Hypothyroidism, Mixed connective tissue disease, and anemia, thus failing to achieve significance in the composite analysis. Surprisingly, despite an increasing trend in the probability of anemia for most autoimmune diseases, we found no significant genetic associations between several other autoimmune diseases (Juvenile idiopathic arthritis, Ankylosing spondylitis, Idiopathic thrombocytopenic purpura, Polymyositis, Systemic lupus erythematosus, Crohn's disease, Psoriasis, Ulcerative colitis) and anemia ( and A). 
Additionally, we investigated the causal relationship between autoimmune diseases and anemia data from the FinnGen consortium. Genetically predicted Rheumatoid arthritis (OR = 1.113, 95 %CI = 1.056–1.173, P < 0.0001), Systemic lupus erythematosus (OR = 1.064, 95 %CI = 1.044–1.084, P < 0.0001), Graves' disease (OR = 1.075, 95 %CI = 1.042–1.111, P < 0.0001), Hashimoto's thyroiditis (OR = 1.135, 95 %CI = 1.100–1.172, P < 0.0001), Type 1 diabetes (OR = 1.038, 95 %CI = 1.001–1.076, P = 0.045), and Primary biliary cholangitis (OR = 1.040, 95 %CI = 1.018–1.063, P = 3.000E−04) have a causal relationship with anemia. This association applies to exposure data from both the FinnGen consortium and the EBI database. However, the genetic associations between Coeliac disease, Crohn's disease, Ulcerative colitis, Inflammatory bowel disease, juvenile idiopathic arthritis, Sarcoidosis, Myasthenia gravis, Asthma, and anemia differ between the FinnGen consortium and the EBI database. Nevertheless, after meta-analysis, Coeliac disease and Sarcoidosis with a PIVW combined effect size <0.05 indicate a potential genetic association between Coeliac disease (OR = 1.050, 95 %CI = 1.019–1.082, P = 0.001), Sarcoidosis (OR = 1.033, 95 %CI = 1.003–1.065, P = 0.033), Asthma (OR = 1.198, 95 %CI = 1.124–1.278, P < 0.0001), and anemia. In contrast, genetically predicted Sjogren's syndrome, Mixed connective tissue disease, Hypothyroidism have a causal relationship with anemia between the FinnGen consortium and the EBI database, but after meta-analysis, the PIVW combined effect size >0.05, which may be due to the inherently weak genetic association between these autoimmune diseases and anemia, thus failing to achieve significance in the composite analysis. Despite an increasing trend in the probability of anemia for most autoimmune diseases, we found no significant genetic associations between several other autoimmune diseases (Polymyositis, Psoriasis, Ulcerative colitis, Idiopathic thrombocytopenic purpura) and anemia ( and B). 
Anemia is a common blood system disorder characterized by a lower than normal number of red blood cells or hemoglobin concentration, leading to a decreased ability of the body to transport oxygen. Previous studies have shown a correlation between anemia and various autoimmune diseases, with immune dysfunction playing a significant role in the pathogenesis of these diseases. Therefore, we explored in a reverse direction, at the genetic level, whether there is a reverse causal relationship between anemia and autoimmune diseases. Through reverse MR analysis, we found that when autoimmune disease data was derived from the EBI database and anemia data from the FinnGen consortium, there was a causal relationship between anemia and Rheumatoid arthritis (OR = 1.103, 95 %CI = 1.018–1.196, P = 0.017), with a PIVW combined effect size of less than 0.05. When anemia data was derived from the EBI database, there were causal relationships between anemia and Hashimoto's thyroiditis (OR = 1.234, 95 %CI = 1.099–1.386, P = 0.0004), Type 1 diabetes (OR = 1.976e+6, 95 %CI = 36.142–1.080e+11, P = 0.009), and Inflammatory bowel disease (OR = 1.321e+20, 95 %CI = 3.141–5.552e+39, P = 0.044). This also proves that there is a certain bidirectional causal relationship between Rheumatoid arthritis, Hashimoto's thyroiditis, Type 1 diabetes, and anemia. Furthermore, we found that anemia databases from both database sources have a causal relationship with Sarcoidosis. However, after meta-analysis, the PIVW combined effect size was greater than 0.05, which may be due to the inherently weak genetic association between anemia and Sarcoidosis, thus failing to achieve significance in the composite analysis. Despite an increasing trend in the probability of anemia for most autoimmune diseases, we found no significant genetic associations at the anemia level between anemia and several other autoimmune diseases (Systemic lupus erythematosus, Sjogren's syndrome, Graves' disease, Coeliac disease, Primary biliary cholangitis, Mixed connective tissue disease, Polymyositis, Ankylosing spondylitis, Psoriasis, Crohn's disease, Ulcerative colitis, Hypothyroidism, Juvenile idiopathic arthritis, Myasthenia gravis, Idiopathic thrombocytopenic purpura, Asthma) ( A and ). 
When data is sourced entirely from the FinnGen consortium, anemia is causally associated with Rheumatoid arthritis (OR = 1.750, 95 %CI = 1.381–2.228, P = 4.050E−06), Sjogren's syndrome (OR = 1.750, 95 %CI = 1.381–2.228, P = 4.050E−06), Coeliac disease (OR = 5.253, 95 %CI = 2.360–11.690, P = 4.830E−05), Mixed connective tissue disease (OR = 1.746, 95 %CI = 1.413–2.157, P < 0.0001), Polymyositis (OR = 1.892, 95 %CI = 1.120–3.196, P = 0.017), Crohn's disease (OR = 1.268, 95 %CI = 1.088–1.479, P = 0.002), Juvenile idiopathic arthritis (OR = 1.370, 95 %CI = 1.075–1.744, P = 0.011), Sarcoidosis (OR = 1.452, 95 %CI = 1.129–1.868, P = 0.004), and Asthma (OR = 1.310, 95 %CI = 1.222–1.408, P = 5.710E−14), with a PIVW combined effect size of less than 0.05 observed for anemia in association with Coeliac disease, Mixed connective tissue disease, Crohn's disease, and Asthma. ## Multivariable MR
Using PhenoScanner, we identified the IVs associated with SLE and other autoimmune diseases. Consequently, we conducted a multivariate analysis of other exposure factors and SLE to explore more stable exposure factors causally related to anemia. After multivariate adjustment, we found that Type 1 diabetes (OR = 1.037, 95 %CI = 1.012–1.061, P = 0.003) and Asthma (OR = 1.069, 95 %CI = 1.017–1.123, P = 0.008) exhibited a causal relationship with anemia by adjusting for SLE. We performed further MVMR2 analysis, considering anemia, smoking, alcohol consumption, and obesity as exposures and autoimmune diseases as outcomes. No significant causal relationship between anemia and autoimmune diseases was found by adjusting for smoking, alcohol consumption, and obesity ( ).

3.4

## Genetic causal relationship and correlation between plasma proteins and anemia, autoimmune diseases
Among the 4775 proteins under investigation, IVW analysis revealed causal relationships between 44 plasma proteins and the risk of anemia with a PFDR < 0.05. ## Discussion
In this study, we employed two-sample Mendelian randomization, multivariate Mendelian methods, and mediation analysis to investigate the potential causal associations between autoimmune diseases of various origins and anemia. Our analysis of anemia data from the EBI database using two-sample Mendelian randomization, followed by meta-analysis, revealed a combined PIVW effect size of less than 0.05, suggesting a significant positive causal link between T1DM, RA, and PBC with anemia. The FinnGen consortium's anemia data corroborated these findings, demonstrating a significant positive causal relationship between Rheumatoid Arthritis, Systemic Lupus Erythematosus, Graves' disease, Hashimoto's thyroiditis, T1DM, and PBC with anemia, with a combined PIVW effect size of less than 0.05 post-meta-analysis. Reverse Mendelian randomization further confirmed the bidirectional causality among these conditions. To evaluate the capacity of autoimmune diseases to induce anemia through mediation, we identified 43 potential mediators, encompassing blood cell counts, immunoglobulin levels, and cytokines influenced by autoimmune diseases, as well as factors involved in hematopoiesis. Our evaluation indicated that autoimmune diseases impact blood cell counts and immunoglobulin levels to varying extents, with hypothyroidism notably influencing anemia via IgG levels. This comprehensive MR study presents, for the first time, the causal associations between 21 autoimmune diseases from diverse sources and the risk of anemia, the role of 43 potential mediators, and proteomic analyses of anemia, T1DM, RA, and PBC.
Chronic immune activation induced by autoimmune diseases leads to the sequestration of iron from autoreactive lymphocytes and erythroid precursors, subsequently causing chronic inflammatory anemia [ ]. Numerous studies have revealed the high prevalence of anemia in different autoimmune diseases: a 20-year retrospective study showed a 29.4 % incidence of anemia in 34 cases of neonatal lupus erythematosus [ ]; in 1057 RA patients, the incidence of anemia was as high as 51.24 % [ ]. The etiology of anemia in SLE patients is complex, including chronic inflammation, renal insufficiency, and drug side effects, and its incidence is closely related to disease activity and treatment methods [ ]. Anemia in RA patients is mainly caused by anemia of chronic disease and iron deficiency, with prevalence related to the severity of the disease and the use of anti-rheumatic drugs [ ]. Patients with inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) often suffer from anemia due to chronic inflammation and malabsorption [ ]. Anemia in multiple sclerosis (MS) patients is closely linked to disease progression and fatigue [ ]. The risk of anemia in psoriasis patients is mainly related to the severity of the disease [ ]. For patients with T1DM, the risk of anemia is closely connected to blood sugar control, nutritional status, and comorbidities. Overall, anemia in autoimmune diseases is driven by complex mechanisms including inflammation, nutritional deficiencies, and treatment side effects. These findings emphasize the importance of comprehensive management for patients with autoimmune diseases to reduce the burden of anemia and improve patient outcomes. Autoimmune diseases may be influenced by various factors, including immunosuppressive/immunomodulatory drugs, disease activity, coexisting organ dysfunction, and advanced age. Our comprehensive analysis of anemia data from multiple sources found a positive causal association between type 1 diabetes, rheumatoid arthritis, and primary biliary cholangitis with anemia, with a combined PIVW effect size <0.05 after meta-analysis. Therefore, we conclude that these conditions are associated with a higher incidence of anemia.
Research indicates that patients with T1DM often suffer from chronic hyperglycemia, which not only may lead to microvascular complications such as diabetic nephropathy but also directly affects the kidney's ability to produce EPO. With the reduction of EPO, patients may develop anemia [ ]. Furthermore, chronic hyperglycemia can also cause systemic chronic inflammation, increasing the levels of inflammatory factors such as TNF and IL-6, which not only suppress the production of EPO but also directly inhibit the bone marrow's ability to produce red blood cells. Inflammation also leads to elevated levels of hepcidin, a hormone secreted by the liver that regulates iron absorption and distribution; increased hepcidin levels can sequester iron, making it unavailable for red blood cell production, thereby exacerbating anemia [ ]. Additionally, T1DM patients may face issues of malnutrition and gastrointestinal dysfunction, such as gastroparesis and malabsorption, leading to deficiencies in essential hematopoietic nutrients like iron, vitamin B12, and folic acid, which in turn can cause anemia. Lastly, insulin and other adjunctive therapeutic drugs used to manage T1DM may cause gastrointestinal discomfort and malabsorption, further affecting the patient's nutritional status and red blood cell production.
In individuals with RA, inflammatory cytokines, including TNF-α and IL-6, are elevated during active disease phases, disrupting iron metabolism and hemoglobin synthesis, which can result in anemia [ ]. Immune dysregulation associated with RA may also impair hematopoietic function in the bone marrow, thereby reducing erythrocyte production or lifespan and contributing to anemia. RA treatments, such as nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) and corticosteroids, can induce gastrointestinal adverse effects, potentially leading to chronic bleeding and anemia. Methotrexate and similar drugs may interfere with folate metabolism, precipitating folate-deficiency anemia. Additionally, RA can impact kidney function, which is essential for erythropoietin (EPO) secretion; compromised kidney function may decrease EPO levels, leading to anemia [ ].
Anemia in PBC is multifactorial. Chronic inflammation can impair iron metabolism and hinder hemoglobin synthesis. Additionally, decreased bile acid secretion may impede the absorption of fat-soluble vitamins, including vitamin B12 and folic acid, which are crucial for erythropoiesis. Patients with PBC are also at risk of developing portal hypertension and splenomegaly, conditions that can lead to enhanced splenic sequestration and premature red blood cell destruction. Furthermore, as an autoimmune disease, PBC may be comorbid with other autoimmune disorders, such as autoimmune hemolytic anemia, collectively increasing the susceptibility to anemia [ ].
Based on a comprehensive mediation analysis, we found that hypothyroidism can influence the development of anemia through IgG levels. Studies indicate that hypothyroidism may impair the bone marrow's ability to produce red blood cells. Thyroid hormones regulate hematopoietic function and affect the proliferation of erythroid precursors [ ]. Additionally, thyroid hormones are involved in the transport and utilization of iron. Anemia associated with hypothyroidism may be due to alterations in iron metabolism; thyroid hormones stimulate hemoglobin chain synthesis in vitro, promoting erythropoiesis. Furthermore, hypothyroidism is correlated with autoimmune diseases, which often correspond to increased levels of IgG. These autoantibodies may disrupt the production or survival of red blood cells, leading to anemia. Moreover, thyroid hormones stimulate erythropoiesis by enhancing the production of erythropoietin in the kidneys. Therefore, hypothyroidism may impair the production of erythropoietin, indirectly leading to anemia. The immunomodulatory function of IgG may contribute to the pathogenesis of anemia in hypothyroidism. IgG may regulate erythropoiesis by modulating the complement pathway, inhibiting dendritic cells, and activating regulatory T cells [ ].
Furthermore, we conducted a genome-wide MR and coloc analysis to explore the causal effects of over 4000 circulating proteins and their subtypes in anemia, T1DM, RA, and PBC, providing preclinical clues for drug development. The MR analysis and coloc analysis identified 2 proteins (MCCD1, TMEM106B) associated with anemia, 6 proteins (CTRB1, CTRB2, IL7RA, PCSK1, SIRPG, WARS1) associated with T1DM, 5 proteins (TGFBI, CBL, PMEL, IL1RN, RNASET2) associated with RA, and 4 proteins (ALDH2, CXCL16, FCN3, HSPB1) associated with PBC.
MCCD1, a highly polymorphic gene situated in the human MHC III region, encodes a short polypeptide featuring a coiled-coil domain and a mitochondrial targeting signal [ ]. TMEM106B is linked to several neurodegenerative conditions, such as Alzheimer's disease and frontotemporal lobar degeneration [ ]. Research indicates that alterations in TMEM106B gene expression correlate with APOE gene expression in the cerebral cortex. Additionally, the TMEM106B protein is capable of forming pathological fibrillar aggregates, which are closely associated with disease progression and aging [ ]. Furthermore, TMEM106B has been recognized as a key facilitator of lung cancer metastasis, with its ectopic expression markedly enhancing the production of vesicular lysosomes [ ].
CTRB1 and CTRB2, subtypes of chymotrypsinogen, have been implicated in type 1 diabetes risk, with elevated CTRB1 levels correlating with reduced risk and elevated CTRB2 levels with increased risk [ ]. The IL7RA gene influences susceptibility to type 1 diabetes; children with the disease exhibit lower serum sIL-7R levels if they carry protective or risk haplotypes compared to the reference haplotype, with the lowest levels observed in those with protective haplotypes [ ]. The human monoclonal antibody PF-06342674, an inhibitor of TSLP and IL7RA, is in phase I clinical trials for type 1 diabetes treatment. PCSK1, which converts proinsulin to insulin, shows reduced expression in type 1 diabetes patients, potentially contributing to proinsulin persistence and indicating a subset of β-cells in T1D patients that may be resistant to autoimmune destruction due to inefficient insulin/C-peptide production [ ]. Elevated plasma SIRPG protein levels are associated with a nearly 60 % higher risk of type 1 diabetes, and SIRPG, the most probable pathogenic gene in the 20p13 region, highlights the importance of alternative splicing in lymphocytes for autoimmune genetic risk [ ].
WARS1's role extends beyond protein synthesis, as it also functions as an innate immune activator in the extracellular compartment. While a direct association between WARS1 and type 1 diabetes remains to be confirmed, its roles in immune modulation and metabolism could be implicated in the pathophysiology of type 1 diabetes [ ]. Research indicates that TGF-β1 expression correlates with the diagnostic staging of RA, suggesting its potential as a novel biomarker for early RA diagnosis [ ]. In RA, dysregulated TGF-β1 expression is intimately tied to the disrupted balance between Th1/Th17 and Th17/Treg cell differentiation, which substantially influences the disease's progression [ ].
The CBL family proteins, a class of E3 ubiquitin ligases, have drawn research attention for their potential role in rheumatoid arthritis fibroblast-like synoviocytes (RA-FLS). The circular RNA circCBLB is hypothesized to modulate RA-FLS proliferation, apoptosis, and inflammatory cytokine production through specific regulatory pathways, thus contributing to the pathogenesis of RA. While no direct link between PMEL and rheumatoid arthritis has been established, the functional melanoma model of amyloid formation in PMEL may offer insights into RA pathologies [ ]. Variations in the IL1RN gene have been correlated with the risk and severity of RA. Notably, the IL1RN TTG haplotype is associated with increased severity of knee osteoarthritis, higher incident osteoarthritis risk, and greater RA inflammation [ ]. Anakinra, an IL-1Ra drug, demonstrates efficacy as a first-line treatment across diverse genetic backgrounds, independent of HLA-DRB1 or IL1RN variations [ ]. In RA patients, RNASET2 expression in neutrophils is elevated, correlating with neutrophil activation and the formation of neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs). Serum RNASET2 levels are significantly higher in RA patients than in healthy individuals, implying its role in RA development. In murine RA models, RNASET2 expression correlates with disease onset and severity, particularly in neutrophils and macrophages [ ].
No direct research currently links ALDH2, CXCL16, FCN3, and HSPB1 to PBC. These findings not only elucidate the potential roles of these targets in type 1 diabetes, RA, PBC, and anemia but also suggest their utility as targets for developing novel therapeutics or diagnostic tools. This could facilitate early target identification and enhance personalized precision medicine approaches.
However, this study has several limitations. First, potential horizontal pleiotropy poses a challenge in MR studies. The MR-Egger intercept test revealed no significant pleiotropic effects in exposure analyses, a finding further corroborated by MR-PRESSO analysis. Second, selecting a subset of autoimmune diseases from the FinnGen consortium may have introduced sample overlap; however, given that our instrumental variables were derived from large-scale GWAS, such overlap is unlikely to substantially influence our results. Third, due to the limited number of SNPs meeting inclusion criteria for certain autoimmune diseases, we slightly relaxed selection thresholds, potentially increasing the risk of false positives. Fourth, as all analyses were conducted in individuals of European ancestry, we expanded sample sizes through meta-analyses across multiple datasets, thereby enhancing power to detect small effects. Fifth, this study could not differentiate anemia subtypes using summary-level data. Autoimmune hemolytic anemia (AIHA)—a recognized complication of SLE and other autoimmune diseases—may thus represent a direct causal pathway rather than a parallel systemic mechanism. We posit that our primary findings likely reflect systemic mechanisms beyond direct hemolysis for two reasons: (1) robustness was systematically evaluated using two complementary pleiotropy-robust methods; and (2) MR effects were observed across multiple autoimmune diseases (TIDM, RA, and PBC) where AIHA prevalence is substantially lower than in SLE, indicating a broader, non-AIHA-specific association. Nonetheless, our effect estimates may represent an upper-bound if AIHA cases are enriched in both exposure and outcome cohorts. Future studies using individual-level biobank data are warranted to stratify analyses by anemia etiology and quantify the extent of AIHA mediation. In subsequent research, we plan to conduct multicenter studies to collect more clinical data and explore associations between autoimmune hematological disorders and various anemia types, enabling more precise evaluation of their impact on anemia incidence.

## Conclusion
In this study, we utilized various MR methods alongside meta-analyses to comprehensively assess the causal relationship between autoimmune diseases and anemia. The findings indicate that genetically predicted T1DM, RA, and PBC are associated with an increased risk of anemia. Furthermore, bidirectional MR analysis suggests a potential reciprocal causality between genetically predicted anemia and conditions such as mixed connective tissue disease, hypothyroidism, and asthma. We further investigated whether autoimmune diseases might trigger anemia by affecting potential mediators, including blood cell counts, immunoglobulin levels, and cytokine concentrations, all of which are crucial to hematopoiesis. Notably, our findings suggest that hypothyroidism may advance the progression of anemia by increasing IgG levels. Additionally, through proteomic and co-localization analyses, we identified multiple targets with potential mechanisms of action in T1DM, RA, PBC, and anemia, aiding in the early prediction of these targets and personalized precision medicine treatments. This study underscores the intricate interplay between autoimmune diseases and anemia, offering novel biological insights that may inform the development of future preventative and therapeutic strategies.
