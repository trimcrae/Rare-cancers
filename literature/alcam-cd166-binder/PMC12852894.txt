## Abstract
While many Antibody Drug Conjugates (ADCs) are investigated in solid tumors, their target expression and heterogeneity is poorly documented in metastatic breast cancer. Here, we perform RNA sequencing to investigate expression of 72 ADC targets in 909 samples from 30 female patients from our post-mortem tissue donation program UPTIDER (NCT04531696). We report that the highest median expression in metastases is seen for FN1, MUC1, LAMP1, HER3 and SLC39A6. TROP2 and HER2 have the 9th and 27th highest expression. We observe reduced expression in metastases as compared to primary untreated tumors for 15 targets but observe only limited differences in expression between metastases of different subtypes. We show that TROP2, VTCN1, NECTIN4, HER2 and HER3 are interesting targets as they have higher mRNA expression in tumor as compared to normal tissues. This is confirmed at the protein level by explorative immunohistochemistry. Correlations between targets can help guide design of bispecific ADCs. Taken together, the results of this study can help inform the design and prioritization of drugs for the treatment of patients with metastatic breast cancer.
Subject terms: Breast cancer, Tumour heterogeneity, Targeted therapies, Metastasis

Antibody drug conjugate (ADC) target expression beyond the traditional targets is poorly studied in metastatic cancer. Here, the authors analyse the expression of known and potential new ADC targets in normal and multi-organ metastatic samples of a post-mortem tissue donation program from patients with metastatic breast cancer.

## Introduction
Antibody-drug conjugates (ADCs) are a rapidly expanding class of drugs that fall under the category of targeted cancer therapy. The drug design typically consists of recombinant monoclonal antibodies chemically bound to cytotoxic agents, commonly referred to as payloads, via a cleavable or non-cleavable linker molecule . Upon successful binding of the antibody to its cognate antigen at the tumor cell surface, the ADC is internalized, and tumor cell death is initiated. Depending on the chemistry and type of the linker with the payload (i.e., cleavable vs non-cleavable linker), the cytotoxic agents also facilitate (tumor) cell death in the surrounding tumor microenvironment (TME) with the so-called bystander effect , . An ideal ADC target should meet the following requirements: (i) be predominantly expressed in the tumor on the cancer cell surface with a low expression in normal tissue, (ii) exhibit minimal expression heterogeneity between different tumor sites within a patient, (iii) be stable in plasma with sufficient half-life, and (iv) have an optimal binding affinity for internalization . However, the minimal target expression needed for effective response is unknown and might depend on the ADC and its target – . Furthermore, mechanisms independent from internalization have been described for certain ADCs, such as trastuzumab deruxtecan (T-DXd) .
Currently, 14 ADCs are approved by the United States Food and Drug Administration (FDA), of whom 8 are available for the treatment of solid cancers . Four of the eight approved ADCs are also approved for the treatment of metastatic breast cancer, namely sacituzumab govitecan (SG) and dapotamab deruxtecan (Dato-DXd) targeting the trophoblast cell surface antigen 2 (TROP2) and T-DXd and trastuzumab emtansine (T-DM1) targeting the human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2). Multiple other ADCs have received fast-track designation from the FDA, such as emiltatug ledadotin (XMT-1660) targeting VTCN1 with corresponding protein B7-H4 and bulumtatug fuvedotin (9MW2821) targeting NECTIN4, both for the treatment of triple negative breast cancer (TNBC). This fast-track designation was given based on results of preclinical data and phase 1/2 trials , .
For SG, a clinically meaningful benefit was not only seen in the treatment of breast cancer, but also in the treatment of other solid tumors . The same is true for T-DXd , , which has received FDA approval for any HER2-positive solid cancer , . This means that ADCs already developed for other solid cancers have the potential to be successfully used in breast cancer if the target is expressed in breast cancer.
SG is composed of an anti-TROP2 antibody linked to the topoisomerase I inhibitor govitecan (SN38). Currently, no prior immunohistochemistry testing for TROP2 expression is needed to treat patients with SG. However, Coates and colleagues described a patient with TNBC treated with SG who had primary resistance to SG . Analysis of all the tumor lesions collected during the rapid autopsy showed no TROP2 expression at the mRNA or protein level, suggesting that expression of TROP2 is needed for SG to work. Bardia and colleagues recently performed an exploratory analysis of the metastatic TNBC ASCENT trial (NCT02574455) and showed that although this ADC improved progression-free and overall survival compared with single-agent chemotherapy across all four quartiles of TROP2 protein expression levels as assessed on immunohistochemistry, the magnitude of benefit on overall survival seemed to increase with increasing TROP2 expression . They also suggest that efficacy in tumors with low TROP2 levels might be due to the bystander effect. Of note, the ASCENT trial considered either archival samples from the primary tumor or a metastasis for TROP2 analysis.
T-DXd consists of an anti-HER2 antibody linked to the topoisomerase I inhibitor deruxtecan. As opposed to SG, a minimal expression of HER2 is needed for a patient with metastatic breast cancer to be eligible to receive T-DXd . Recently, the DESTINY-Breast06 (NCT04494425) trial has shown that patients with HER2-ultralow tumors (defined as ≤10% of invasive cells showing faint/weak and incomplete membrane staining) could benefit from this ADC, which has led to FDA approval for the treatment of hormone receptor (HR)+/HER2-ultralow tumors with T-DXd . Since this HER2-ultralow status had to be determined on a tissue sample taken from one metastatic lesion, it indirectly assumes that one sample is representative of the whole metastatic disease. While response to therapy does not seem to be correlated with immunohistochemical HER2 protein expression, more quantitative HER2 protein or mRNA expression levels have been reported to be correlated, although available data are limited and controversial – .
Restricted sample collection and the availability of adequate samples from non-accessible metastatic organ sites represent a logistical problem for investigating the expression of these ADC targets and especially their intra-patient heterogeneity , , – . In this context, research autopsy programs enable a comprehensive investigation of a very large number of metastases present in a patient . Using metastatic samples from our post-mortem tissue donation program UPTIDER (NCT04531696) , we recently described the intra-patient inter-metastatic heterogeneity of HER2 protein expression, suggesting that, at least for HER2, one metastatic biopsy might not be representative of the disease .
Here, we investigate the mRNA expression and the inter- and intra-patient heterogeneity of ADC targets being clinically evaluated in solid cancer trials, leveraging the samples and data collected in UPTIDER. In addition to TROP2 and HER2, we identify multiple other interesting targets with high expression in but low heterogeneity across our metastatic samples, such as HER3 and VTCN1. We provide evidence for the differential expression of these targets across different organs. Furthermore, in view of the ongoing discussions about the concomitant or sequential administration of ADCs and the emergent development of ADCs with bi- or even multispecific antibodies, which can bind two or more different targets or epitopes of the same target , , we are able to demonstrate the co-expression of certain treatment targets. Next, although most ADCs are relatively well tolerated, all do have side-effects . While most adverse events are caused by off-target/off-tumor toxicities, some can be explained by off-tumor/on-target toxicity. These are caused by normal tissues expressing the target of the ADC , . Our results highlight the expression patterns of ADC targets on normal tissue samples from a large variety of organs. Lastly, we perform a first preliminary validation of our findings in metastatic and normal samples at the protein level for targets of interest. With this study, we hope to address some knowledge gaps in the rapidly evolving field of ADCs.

## Results

## Clinical and pathological characteristics of the patient cohort
Thirty female patients with metastatic breast cancer underwent tissue donation via rapid autopsy between January 2021 and November 2023. In total, 23 patients (77%) had hormone receptor (HR)+/HER2− breast cancer, 6 (20%) TNBC, and 1 (3%) HR+/HER2+ primary breast cancer (Fig.  ). Eighteen patients (60%) had primary invasive breast carcinoma of no special type (IBC-NST), 7 (23%) had invasive lobular carcinoma (ILC), 3 (10%) had mixed IBC-NST/ILC, 1 (3%) had mixed IBC-NST/metaplastic and 1 (3%) was initially diagnosed with IBC-NST and 14 years later developed a contralateral ILC tumor. The median age of primary invasive diagnosis was 49 years (range: 35–83), while the median age of first metastatic diagnosis was 56 years (range: 37–83). Seven patients (23%) had de novo metastatic disease. Four patients (13%) received an ADC as part of their treatment. Pt2001 was treated with SAR566658 targeting CA6, a sialoglycotope of MUC1. Pt2003 received T-DM1 targeting HER2, while Pt2024 and Pt2032 received SG targeting TROP2. Patient characteristics are reported in Supplementary Table  .
 The median age at death was 60 years (range: 36–91). The autopsies had a median post-mortem interval of 2.9 h per patient (range: 1.2–24.5). A total of 749 metastases with a median of 25 metastases per patient (range: 7–50) and 64 primary untreated breast cancer samples with a median of 1 untreated primary tumor sample per patient (range: 0–10) were collected. Metastatic lesions were acquired from a median of 6 organ sites per patient (range: 3–16) (Fig.  ). Metastatic samples were collected from 32 distinct organ sites (Supplementary Fig.  ). However, organ site representation was most consistent in patients with ER+ disease and in patients with a primary histology of IBC-NST (Supplementary Fig.  ). Normal samples (n = 96) were acquired from 25 distinct organ sites with a median of 4 samples per organ site (range: 1–10) (Fig.  ).

## List of ADC targets being clinically investigated in oncology
Our search strategy identified 623 clinical trials testing 166 different ADCs in solid tumors (Supplementary Table  ). 221 trials (35%) were pan-cancer trials, and 177 trials (28%) were specific to breast cancer patients, which makes breast cancer the most common tumor type in ADC trials. Of all trials, 204 (33%) were phase 1, 323 (52%) were phase 2, 75 (12%) were phase 3, and 6 (1%) were phase 4 (Fig.  ). A combination of phase 1 and 2 was seen in 10 trials, and phase 2 and 3 in 3 trials. Two trials monitored compassionate use programs. In total, we assembled a list of 72 ADC targets currently under investigation (Supplementary Data  and ). Ten targets have been used in breast cancer-specific trials (ALCAM, CD44, HER2, HER3, FOLR1, FUT2, GPNMB, PTK7, SLC39A6, TROP2). HER2 (n = 32) and TROP2 (n  =  10) were the most investigated targets.
 

## Expression of ADC targets in breast cancer metastases
The threshold level of target expression needed for ADC activity has not yet been determined for any of the existing ADCs. However, there is evidence suggesting that higher target mRNA and/or protein expression can enhance the efficacy of some ADCs, while the absence of expression could indicate resistance , , , , . This highlights the potential relevance of quantifying expression levels of ADC targets across metastases. Here, we investigated the mRNA expression of the 72 ADC targets across metastatic samples using normalized gene expression data. FN1, MUC1, LAMP1, HER3 and SLC39A6 had the highest global median expression level among all targets (median range: 8.46–10.34) (Fig.  ), while the targets from the currently clinically approved ADCs or ADCs with fast-track designation by the FDA in metastatic breast cancer TROP2, NECTIN4, HER2 and VTCN1 had the 9th, 18th, 27th and 33rd highest global median expression (median range: 4.83–7.73), respectively. Additionally, other targets used in breast cancer-specific trials also had high global median expression, for example, CD44, ALCAM, and GPNMB, which were ranked with the 7th, 8thand 14th highest global median expression among all targets. Targets with minimal expression were CFC1B, CLDN6, DLL3, DPEP3, and MSLN (median of 1.67).
In the 14 patients where repeated sampling was performed, gathering 141 samples , to investigate effects of PMI on sample quality, target expression did not vary substantially with time, with 82% of targets remaining constant in cooled tumor samples. Genes not remaining constant were among the lowest expressed genes, or changes were too subtle to be further considered (Supplementary Fig.  , Supplementary Data  ).
As most ADC targets are, per definition, expected to be preferentially expressed by tumor cells, we investigated the correlation between their mRNA expression in the tumor tissue and tumor cellularity. Here, a weak positive or negative correlation (Spearman Rho ranging between −0.25 and 0.32) was observed for 39 and 33 targets, respectively (Supplementary Fig.  ). HER2, HER3, and TROP2 displayed a weak positive correlation with tumor cellularity (range: 0.20–0.28).
To investigate the intra-patient inter-metastasis heterogeneity in target expression, we evaluated the interquartile range (IQR) per target, with increasing ranges indicating an increasing degree of heterogeneity. The targets with the highest global median IQR were LRCC15, CEACAM6, CEACAM5, FN1, and MUC1 (median range: 1.40–1.61), while a high IQR was also seen for VTCN1 (1.36). Median IQR was relatively low for HER2, HER3, TROP2, and NECTIN4 (median range: 0.75–1.07, Fig.  ), with most patients (range: 57–66%) having intra-patient median IQR below the global median IQR per target (Supplementary Fig.  ).
We further explored the expression of ADC targets within subgroups of patients with primary HR+/HER2− breast cancer (n = 556) and TNBC (n = 178). In patients with HR+/HER2− breast cancer, the highest global median expression level was seen for HER3, LAMP1, MUC1, SLC39A6, and FN1 (median range: 8.68–10.40). TROP2, NECTIN4, HER2, and VTCN1 had the 9th, 19th, 27th, and 32nd highest global median expression (median range: 4.70–7.73). Similarly, for patients with TNBC, the highest global median expression was seen for HER3, CD44, MUC1, LAMP1, and FN1 (median range 8.15–9.49). TROP2, NECTIN4, HER2, and VTCN1 had the 7th, 13th, 22nd, and 33rd highest global median expression (median range: 4.71–7.75) (Fig.  , Supplementary Fig.  ).
 

## Differences in target expression between the primary tumor and metastases
As tumors evolve from primary disease to metastatic disease, so do their molecular and TME profiles, leading to a potential increase or decrease in ADC target expression , . To investigate this, we compared target expression levels between primary untreated tumors and their corresponding metastases. Due to the relatively limited amount of primary untreated tumors in the cohort, we initially validated target expression in our primary untreated tumor samples (n = 64) by comparing normalized target gene expression with primary untreated tumors from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) cohort (n = 1111) . We observed a very high correlation (R = 0.92, p < 0.001) between the two datasets, indicating that the target expression in our primary breast cancer samples can be considered representative of untreated primary breast cancer (Supplementary Fig.  ).
We then compared target expression between metastases (n = 749) and primary untreated tumors (n = 64). The regression model revealed significantly reduced expression in metastases as compared to primary untreated tumors for 14 targets, including LRRC15, PD-L1, and CD276, with a trend for HER2 and GPNMB (Fig.  , “Methods”). Significantly higher expression in metastases was seen for 4 targets (ADAM9, CDH6, NCAM1, and SEZ6). Of these 4, CDH6 had the highest increase in expression in metastases compared to primary untreated tumors (Estimate: 1.15, Fig.  ). Since the expression of CFC1B was minimal across all metastases (Fig.  ), evidence of statistical relevance cannot be considered reliable (Fig.  , Supplementary Data  ). When comparing HR+/HER2− metastases (n = 556) to their corresponding untreated primary tumors (n = 56), statistical evidence of reduced expression in metastases was observed for 13 targets, including CD276 and VTCN1, with the greatest change being in LRRC15 (Estimate: −4.27, Supplementary Fig.  ), while an increased expression was observed for 6 targets, including CDH6. Furthermore, in patients with TNBC disease (primary n = 7, metastases n = 178), the difference in target expression between metastases and untreated primary tumors was rather limited, with reduced expression for 6 targets, including PD-L1, HER2, and SLC39A6, while increased expression was observed in 3 targets, including ADAM9 (Supplementary Fig.  , Supplementary Data  ). Additionally, a trend for higher FGFR2 expression was observed in untreated primary tumors with TNBC disease.

## Differences in target expression according to histological subtypes, molecular subtypes, and ER protein expression
We first looked at tumor histology and compared target expression between ILC (n = 265) and IBC-NST (n = 432) metastases across all patients. We observed no significant changes or trends in target expression (Fig.  , Supplementary Data  ). Second, we compared target expression between metastases from patients with primary HR+/HER2− breast cancer (n = 556) with metastases from patients with primary TNBC (n = 178). Here, our models identified statistical evidence for higher expression of SLC39A6 in HR+/HER2− metastases with no evidence or trends in TNBC metastases (Fig.  , Supplementary Data  ). It should, however, be noted that we observed higher expression of NECTIN4 and VTCN1 in TNBC, the two targets of the ADCs that got fast-track designation by the FDA for metastatic TNBC. However, this was not statistically significant after correcting for multiple testing. Third, we investigated the potential association between estrogen receptor (ER) protein loss and ADC target expression in patients with primary ER+ disease. Here, between ER+ (n = 427) and ER− (n = 63, 13% loss) metastases, evidence for significantly higher expression was observed for SLC39A6, IGF1R, and TPBG with trends for HER3 and SLITRK6 in ER+ metastases as compared to ER− metastases (Fig.  , Supplementary Data  ).

## Target expression at the organ level
Since the composition of the TME across various metastatic sites is largely organ-specific, it could be expected that ADC target expression is dependent on the local microenvironment. We therefore performed an exploratory analysis to investigate organ-specific expression of ADC targets. To this end, we considered metastases from 13 organ sites which contained at least 10 samples across the cohort (Supplementary Fig.  ). When considering the most prevalent metastatic sites in breast cancer irrespective of tumor histology: bones (n = 44), brain (n = 13), liver (n = 219) and lung (n = 44) showed distinct patterns of site-specific target expression compared to global expression across all 32 metastatic sites (Fig.  , Supplementary Data  ). Bone metastases had the largest number of targets (n = 9) with increased organ-specific expression, including LRRC15, FGFR2, and FN1 (Estimate range: 0.62–1.62). Bone metastases also had lower expression of TROP2 and NECTIN4 (estimate range: −0.77 to −1.47). Brain metastases had the largest number of targets with reduced expression, including HER2, VTCN1, and FN1 (estimate range: −0.78 to 1.86). In contrast, brain and lung metastases presented with 6 (including NCAM1) and 3 targets (including CLDN18) that displayed the highest median increase in organ-specific expression across all metastases (estimate >1.5). Additionally, lung metastases showed a mild increase in PD-L1 expression (estimate: 0.68) with reduced expression for FN1 (Estimate: −0.53). Furthermore, while liver metastases had site-specific expression of 6 targets, the change in median expression was subtle (estimate range: −0.28 to 0.62). Across the remaining 9 organs, most metastases presented with no evidence or just trends for changes in expression (Supplementary Fig.  ). These findings highlight the heterogeneity of ADC target expression among multiple metastatic sites, which could be influenced by the organ-specific TME in patients with metastatic breast cancer.
 

## Co-expression and mutual exclusivity of ADC targets in breast cancer metastases
Multiple strategies are being investigated regarding the potential benefit of sequential or concomitant administration of ADCs and bispecific, or even multispecific ADCs , , , , , . We therefore investigated the level of co-expression among targets within metastatic samples using weighted Spearman correlation. Across metastases from all patients, we identified 29 different target combinations with a strong or very strong negative or positive correlation (R > 0.6 or R < −0.6 and p < 0.05) (Supplementary Data  ) of which 23 pairs were linked to positive co-expression, while 6 pairs were linked to mutually exclusive expression. Considering our targets of clinical interest (HER2, HER3, TROP2, CD276, FGFR2, FN1, MUC1, NECTIN4, VTCN1), we found 1 combination with positive co-expression HER2-HER3 and 1 pair with negative co-expression (FN1-MUC1). Additionally, PD-L1 as immunotherapy has been used in combination with ADCs; however, none of our targets of interest had a strong positive or negative correlation, with only HER2 having a moderate negative correlation with PD-L1 (R = −0.43, p = 0.04) (Fig.  , Supplementary Data  ). Within breast cancer subtypes, in patients with HR+/HER2− metastases, strong positive correlations were observed for 39 pairs, while strong negative correlations were apparent for 16 pairs (Supplementary Fig.  , Supplementary Data  ). Considering our targets of interest, positive co-expression was observed for HER2-HER3, and negative co-expression was observed for HER3-PD-L1 and FN1-MUC1. In patients with TNBC, strong positive correlations were observed in 50 pairs, while strong negative correlations were observed in 39 pairs. Regarding our targets of interest, negative co-expression was observed for HER2-FN1, FGFR2-VTCN1, and positive co-expression was observed for TROP2-NECTIN4; however, no evidence for co-expression with PD-L1 was observed (Supplementary Fig.  , Supplementary Data  ). Overall, these findings highlight the presence of both positively and negatively co-expressed ADC target pairs in metastases across the entire cohort, but also the heterogeneity of co-expression patterns across breast cancer subtypes.
 

## Expression of ADC targets in normal samples
Although most toxicities in ADCs are off-target and driven by effects caused by the cytotoxic payload, some can be explained by on-target activity caused by the expression of targets on normal tissue . Identifying ADC targets that have a considerably higher expression in metastases compared to normal tissue can improve drug response and avoid preventable drug toxicities. Here, we studied ADC target expression in 96 normal tissue samples obtained from 25 different organ sites from patients with metastatic breast cancer (Fig.  ). FGFR2, SLC39A6, FN1, ALCAM, and MUC1 showed the highest expression in normal tissues: their median expression values ranging from 9.46 to 10.50 (Fig.  ). TROP2, NECTIN4, HER2 and VTCN1 were ranked with 21st, 25th, 37th and 42nd highest global median expression (median range: 3.34–5.60). For 32 targets, the median gene expression (Methods) was higher in metastatic samples than in normal samples including HER2, HER3, TROP2, VTCN1, and NECTIN4 and for 31 the expression was higher in normal samples including PD-L1 (Fig.  , Supplementary Fig.  ). The highest difference in median gene expression in favor of metastases was seen for TROP2, followed by VTCN1. Figure  also highlights the degree of overlap in gene expression between normal tissues and metastases (“Methods,” Supplementary Data  ). Of the 32 targets exhibiting a higher median expression in metastases, the median overlap in gene expression with normal tissues was 88.89% (range: 2.25–100%). Interestingly, we identified TROP2 as the only target in metastases with an overlap under 25% (2.25%), while HER2, HER3, NECTIN4, and VTCN1 had an overlap of 68.87%, 47.76%, 36%, and 33.33%, respectively (Fig.  ). PD-L1 had higher median expression in normal samples with a 56.79% degree of overlap in expression (Supplementary Fig.  ).
 When studying the expression levels within all organ sites, we observed the highest relative target expression (Z-score > 3) for CA9 in bladder, CFC1B in bone marrow, DLL3 in the brain, LAMP1 in the liver, CLDN18 in lung, DPEP3 in thoracic lymph nodes, CLDN6 in pancreas, CD70 in the spinal cord and LY75 in the thyroid (Fig.  ). Moderate levels of HER2 expression (Z-score: 1–1.5) were found across several normal tissues, including bladder, kidney and heart, but not in lung and liver. For TROP2, higher expression (Z-score: 1–2.64) was seen in bladder, kidney, lung, and pancreas, but not in bone marrow (Fig.  ). No association was observed between age at death, PMI, and target expression in normal samples (Supplementary Data  ). Altogether, these findings highlight target mRNA expression in normal tissues and provide insights into differences in expression across organ sites.

## Exploratory characterization of protein expression of selected targets
Eight targets were selected for immunohistochemical staining of metastatic and normal samples: (i) targets of ADCs being approved for metastatic breast cancer (HER2, TROP2) or having received fast-track designation (NECTIN4, VTCN1), (ii) target with clinical potential based on high mRNA expression in metastatic samples and low mRNA expression in normal samples (HER3) and (iii) targets with high levels of mRNA expression in metastases but also in normal tissues, likely indicative of high risk for toxicities (FN1, MUC1, FGFR2) (Figs.  and , Supplementary Fig.  , Supplementary Data  ). The analysis of the immunohistochemistry data was mainly explorative, given the relatively limited number of samples. Our main goal was to compare the expression between metastases and normal tissue samples, to see whether the conclusions we made based on the transcriptomic data were still valid on the preliminary immunohistochemical data.
 For the 4 targets from clinically approved ADCs or ADCs having received fast-track designation by the FDA (HER2, TROP2, NECTIN4 and VTCN1), we could confirm a significantly higher expression in metastases as compared to the normal samples (Fig.  ). It should be noted that for these 4 targets, the percentages of stained tumor cells ranged from 0 to 100%, highlighting the heterogeneity in expression (Fig.  , Supplementary Fig.  ). While no membranous staining and no staining at all was detected for, respectively, HER2 and VTCN1 in the normal tissues, variable percentages of cells expressing TROP2 or NECTIN4 were detected in the normal tissues (Supplementary Fig.  ). Regarding HER3, higher levels of stained cells were observed in normal tissues as compared to the metastases, which showed very low levels of expression (Figs.  and , Supplementary Fig.  ). Regarding the last 3 targets, we could not detect any difference in expression between normal and metastatic samples for FN1 and FGFR2. However, MUC1 expression was significantly higher in metastases as compared to normal tissues, but with variable percentages of cells expressing MUC1 in normal tissue samples (Figs.  and , Supplementary Fig.  ).
At the mRNA level, we observed a lower expression of HER2 in brain and pleural metastases. At the protein level, we stained 6 brain metastases for HER2; half had detectable HER2 expression (Supplementary Data  ). This suggests that while we do observe a lower mRNA expression in brain metastases, protein expression might still be present. Of the 6 pleural metastases we examined at the protein level, only 2 had detectable HER2 expression.
While these results should be interpreted with caution since only a proportion of samples were stained, they do validate what we observed in our transcriptomic data for 6 out of the 8 markers regarding differences in expression in metastatic versus normal samples. The results also clearly demonstrate the wide range of protein expression observed for all markers in metastatic breast cancer.

## Discussion
ADCs have become widely integrated as therapy for metastatic cancer, but until now, only four ADCs (i.e., SG, Dato-DXd, T-DXd, T-DM1) have been approved by the FDA for the treatment of patients with metastatic breast cancer, and two ADCs have recently received fast-track designation by the FDA (emiltatug ledadotin and 9MW2821). The knowledge about the expression of their respective targets TROP2 and HER2, on one hand, and B7-H4 (coded by the VTCN1 gene) and NECTIN4, remains so far limited in metastatic disease, and the knowledge about non-traditional targets currently under clinical investigation is even more limited. Using the large number of samples collected through our institutional post-mortem tissue donation program, we investigated the bulk mRNA expression levels of 72 targets across multiple primary untreated tumor, metastatic, and normal tissue samples acquired from various organ sites and explored protein expression for 8 targets of interest.
The highest mRNA expression was observed for targets that have been/are currently being evaluated in a limited number of clinical trials. For example, the highest global median expression associated with high intra-patient heterogeneity in metastases was observed for FN1, with comparable expression across all tumor subtypes, and overlapping expression in normal samples. The mRNA expression in normal samples, which was confirmed by our protein data, makes, in our opinion, FN1 a poor target. Currently, one FN1-ADC (PYX-201) is being clinically tested. This ADC targets the extra domain B splice variant of fibronectin and is currently being tested in a phase 1 pan-cancer clinical trial including patients with all subtypes of breast cancer (NCT05720117) , with results expected in 2026.
MUC1, which has the second-highest global median mRNA expression associated with a high degree of intra-patient heterogeneity across metastases, also has comparable expression across tumor subtypes, with a complete overlap of expression in normal samples. Our observations at the protein level were not completely consistent with the transcriptomic data, as the protein expression levels were, for most normal tissue samples, relatively low and significantly lower than in the metastases. An anti-MUC1 ADC is currently being evaluated in one ongoing pan-cancer phase 1/2 trial, including patients with breast cancer (NCT05875168) .
Our study also further increased the knowledge regarding the traditional breast cancer ADC targets HER2 and TROP2.
HER2 was ranked 27th out of the 72 evaluated targets regarding its mRNA expression levels in metastases and was associated with a relatively low intra-patient heterogeneity. Our previous study, however, documented a high degree of intra-patient inter-metastasis heterogeneity in protein expression based on the clinically acknowledged immunohistochemistry categories for HER2 . In our cohort, we did observe a trend for higher mRNA expression in primary untreated tumor samples as compared to metastatic samples. Finally, we observed a reduced mRNA expression in brain and pleural metastases as compared to all metastases, and no evidence for positive co-expression with other ADC targets. With regards to brain metastases, the cohort study of Moss et al. showed detectable protein expression of HER2 in brain metastases of 15/28 patients with HER2-negative primary breast cancer . Moreover, in the DESTINY-Breast12 trial (NCT04739761), patients with HER2+ metastatic breast cancer and stable and active brain metastases showed a response to T-DXd with a 61.6% progression-free survival at 12 months . Similar results are seen in the TUXEDO-1 trial (NCT04752059) with an overall intracranial response rate of 73.3% to T-DXd . The same was seen in the DEBBRAH trial (NCT04420598), which additionally included patients with HER2-low breast cancer. In this subgroup, an intracranial objective response rate of 50.0% in the cohort with asymptomatic untreated brain metastasis and 33.3% in the cohort with progressive brain metastasis after local therapy was observed . Immunohistochemical analysis of 6 of our brain samples shows HER2−(ultra)low in half of them. This suggests that while we do observe a lower mRNA expression in brain metastases, the level of HER2 expression might still be sufficiently high at the protein level to achieve therapeutic response with anti-HER2 ADCs. Additional investigations are warranted for pleural metastases.
While T-DXd is highly effective, it is associated with important side-effects such as hematological and gastrointestinal toxicity, but also cardiotoxicity and interstitial lung disease (ILD) . The 69% overlap in HER2 expression levels between normal and metastatic tissues observed in our study, and the moderate HER2 expression seen in the heart, could partially explain the cardiotoxicity. ILD is a spectrum of lung disorders with lung injury possibly leading to irreversible lung fibrosis . HER2 expression in normal lung is one possible mechanism of action explaining the manifestation of ILD upon treatment with T-DXd. This seems to be a less contributing source based on the relatively low mRNA HER2 expression in our normal lung tissue samples, although immunohistochemistry studies showed that normal respiratory epithelium does express HER2 . Preclinical and clinical data point to an association with the cytotoxic payload in alveolar macrophages, as similar cases of ILD were indeed described for other ADCs using a topoisomerase-1 inhibitor . In a study with cynomolgus monkeys, treatment with DXd did not cause lung toxicity, while treatment with T-DXd did cause lung toxicity, implying that the mechanism of ILD cannot be explained solely by the payload . Immunohistochemistry showed consistent protein and mRNA expression in metastases, but no membranous staining was seen in normal samples, even when there was mRNA expression.
TROP2 had high expression levels in metastases (ranked as 9th) and was the ADC target with the lowest overlap in expression between metastases and normal samples. It had a moderate intra-patient heterogeneity and no evidence for co-expression with other targets. A reduced expression was further observed in bone metastases. Of interest, there was no evidence for differences in metastatic expression according to the molecular and histopathological subtypes. The toxicity profile of SG is well known, comprising diarrhea, nausea and vomiting, ILD, and hematological toxicities. The mRNA expression levels we observed in the normal samples could, however, not explain these toxicities. Our protein data confirmed the statistically higher expression of TROP2 in metastases as compared to normal tissues. We did, however, observe some protein expression in several normal tissue samples.
For the two targets for which ADCs recently received the fast-track designation, namely NECTIN4 and VTCN1 (with protein B7-H4), we observed at the mRNA level higher expression in metastases with only limited overlap with expression in normal samples. Similar results were seen on the protein level. Enfortumab vedotin, an ADC targeting NECTIN4, was approved for the treatment of urothelial cancer after showing significantly better outcomes in phase 3 trials , . In the pan-cancer trial EV-202 (NCT04225117), 87 patients with breast cancer, regardless of NECTIN4 status, were treated with enfortumab vedotin. Response was seen in 15.6% of patients with HR+/HER2− breast cancer and in 19% of patients with TNBC . Prespecified trial goals were not met. Patient selection based on biomarker stratification could potentially improve the treatment efficacy, especially since there is preclinical evidence suggesting a high potential of targeting NECTIN4 .
Four pan-cancer trials, all including breast cancer patients and each administering a different VTCN1 targeting ADC, are currently ongoing. All four have published initial results showing a manageable safety profile and preliminary efficacy – . Interestingly, one of the dose-limiting toxicities was hyperglycemia . It is unclear whether this could be related to VTCN1 expression in the pancreas, which was moderate at the mRNA level in our normal pancreas samples.
We also want to comment on HER3 as several clinical trials evaluating anti-HER3 ADCs have been/are being conducted in patients with breast cancer , . HER3 had the fourth-highest global median expression level in metastatic samples among all targets, with a relatively low intra-patient heterogeneity but higher compared to HER2. We also observed a certain degree of expression overlap between tumor and normal tissue, as well a reduced expression in pleural metastases. In recent clinical trials, patritumab deruxtecan (HER3-DXd) has shown promising efficacy across multiple breast cancer subtypes in phase 1/2 breast cancer-specific trials , . In ICARUS-Breast01 (NCT04965766), a median progression-free survival of 9.4 months was seen with a manageable safety profile in patients with HR+/HER2− metastatic breast cancer who were treated with HER3-DXd after progression on a CDK4/6 inhibitor and one line of chemotherapy . The most common side-effects partly overlap with T-DXd and include gastrointestinal toxicity, with nausea and diarrhea, and hematologic toxicities. Grades 1–2 ILD was seen in 7% of patients . In our normal samples, no clear elevated expression of HER3 was observed in any organ site, including the stomach and intestines. HER3 also showed no marked expression in normal samples of the heart, but unlike T-DXd, HER3-DXd is not associated with frequent cardiotoxicity . The results at the protein level were somewhat surprising as they showed slightly higher levels in normal, all cytoplasmic staining, versus metastatic tissue samples, with most metastatic samples (89%) without detectable membranous expression. Another, more sensitive technique might be needed to detect low protein expression levels in the tumor cells.
Of interest, for most targets, we observed almost no difference in target expression in metastases according to major histological subtype and molecular subtype. When developing an ADC for the treatment of breast cancer, it is important to know whether a trial can include at least the major breast cancer subtypes. Looking at the target expression patterns in our cohort, most targets can be assumed to be equally active in all subtypes, although these results should be confirmed in a larger number of patients with TNBC and ILC. The positive and negative correlations between ADC target pairs documented in our cohort could potentially support the development of bispecific ADCs or guide the sequence of ADC treatments. A phase 1 trial in urothelial cancer with a TROP2-ADC SG and the NECTIN-4 ADC enfortumab vedotin has shown that these could potentially be combined, as an objective response rate of 70% was observed . In our breast cancer metastatic samples, we observed a weak to moderate positive correlation between the expression of TROP2 and NECTIN-4 (R = 0.52). This combination could therefore hold potential interest since both targets also showed high expression in metastatic samples in our cohort.
This study has several limitations. First, we did not have the corresponding primary untreated tumor samples’ sequencing data for seven patients. From our analyses with TCGA untreated breast cancer tumors, we could, however, demonstrate an excellent correlation between the expression of the targets in our samples and in those from TCGA, suggesting that our primary breast cancer samples cohort is representative of the larger population. Second, samples were obtained from heavily pretreated patients at the end-stage of their disease and at the time of death, potentially influencing target expression. We have, however, shown that the mRNA expression of the targets did remain stable independently of the sample-specific post-mortem interval (ssPMI). Also, in our patients, pretreatment with ADCs was very limited, precluding the in-depth investigation of the impact of ADC therapy on its respective target expression. Third, at the time of analysis, the study could only include one patient with HER2 overexpression. Because of this, specific subtype analyses linked to HER2 overexpression could not be done. Fourth, for certain organ sites that are more rarely metastasized, only a restricted number of metastatic samples could be acquired. Also, for the normal tissues, certain organ sites were represented by fewer samples, and others could not be sampled, such as the eyes. Finally, our study has evaluated the expression of all the targets at the mRNA level and only for 8 targets of interest at the protein level in a limited number of samples. While these immunohistochemical data are valuable, they should still be seen as exploratory. Evaluating all targets on all samples by immunohistochemistry in our cohort would have resulted in more than 72,000 stainings, which is practically too challenging and would result in a potential exhaustion of some samples. We might, in the future, complement the results with additional immunohistochemistry or other, more sensitive, protein-level analyses for some targets that deserve further investigation.
To conclude, our results expand on the earlier efforts to evaluate ADC target expression across multiple cancer types and bring additional insights specific to patients with metastatic breast cancer , . The study involved numerous metastases sampled across a wide range of organs from multiple patients with diverse primary molecular and histological breast cancer subtypes. This gave us the unique opportunity to study the intra- and inter-patient heterogeneity of these targets. The scale of overlap in expression between metastatic and normal samples can be taken into consideration when designing drugs to potentially reduce off-tumor on-target toxicity. Based on the extensive mRNA and exploratory protein expression analyses, our results support TROP2 and HER2 as good ADC targets, as well as VTCN1, NECTIN4, as they all had higher expression levels in metastases, which were also higher than in normal tissue samples. Other targets could also be of potential interest based on this criterion (such as CD276 coding for the B7-H3 protein), but the mRNA observations will need to be complemented by protein-level analyses. As the number of patients being treated with an ADC in the metastatic setting is increasing and as UPTIDER is still actively recruiting patients, this will allow us to compare the expression of the relevant ADC target in patients treated and not treated with the specific ADC. Altogether, we believe that our results provide a resource that could help prioritize ADCs to be evaluated in metastatic breast cancer and guide the development of bi- (or multi-)specific ADCs.

## Methods

## Identification of ADC targets currently under clinical investigation in solid tumors
Bosi et al. identified a list of 54 ADC target genes based on a search on clinicaltrials.gov with the 26th of September 2023 as the latest publication date. This search was based on different keywords, including “antibody-drug conjugate.” Only trials testing ADCs in solid tumors were included . We extended this search on clinicaltrials.gov for studies posted between September 26th, 2023, and March 4th, 2024, with the search term “antibody drug conjugate.” Five additional target proteins were identified (CD274, CDH3, DLK1, EPHA5, CFC1B). We performed an additional search on clinicaltrials.gov with the search term “ADC” until March 4th, 2024. Another 2 targets were identified (NT5E, ST8SIA1). The study of Fang et al. included two targets not identifiable with the earlier mentioned search strategy (CD44 and CDH3), and these were also included . Lastly, adcreview.com was searched for additional targets in solid tumors. CA9, EDNRB, EPHA2, LAMP1, LY6E, MUC16, NOTCH3, SEZ6, and STEAP1 were additionally identified. While some of these targets had a gene name different from their protein name, we renamed genes of commonly known protein markers to their corresponding protein names, such as ERBB2, ERBB3, NT5E, TACSTD2, and CD274 to HER2, HER3, CD73, TROP2, and PD-L1 for easier comprehension across analyses. Detailed information can be found in Supplementary Table  and Supplementary Data  and .

## Patient enrollment and sample collection
UPTIDER (UZ/KU Leuven Program for Post-mortem Tissue Donation to Enhance Research) is a post-mortem tissue donation program that enrolls male and female patients with metastatic breast cancer (NCT04531696) . Until now, only female patients have been included. After inclusion, archived tissue samples from primary tumors and/or metastatic biopsies were retrieved from the pathology department. Additional information regarding the study design and protocols is available in our original publication . At autopsy, samples from macroscopically identified metastases as well as from normal tissues were collected. Location of sampling was registered using ICD-O-3 organ codes . As indicated by the organ codes in ICD-O-3, the pleura and lung are classified as distinct anatomical sites. Post-mortem interval was defined as the time duration between patient death and the start of the autopsy. Samples of selected tumor tissues were repeatedly (at 1.5-h time intervals) sampled during the tissue donation procedure to assess the impact of ssPMI on sample quality. Two series of samples were obtained: (i) samples kept at room temperature; (ii) samples kept between 4 °C and 10 °C. All samples are conserved for further analysis. These samples may be shared with other researchers upon request, subject to appropriate agreements, including a Material Transfer Agreement, and ethical approvals.
Samples and data from the first 30 patients who underwent tissue donation between January 2021 and November 2023 are considered here. For these 30 patients, all available primary and metastatic samples (i.e., both historical tumor samples collected during life and samples obtained from the tissue donation program after death) were analyzed. Only untreated primary tumor samples were included in the analysis. A primary tumor was classified as untreated if the patient had not received any systemic anti-cancer therapy in the 5 years before the breast cancer diagnosis. Normal samples were selected based on the absence of oncological and non-oncological organ disease, which was confirmed on histology, across multiple patients. For example, if a patient had pneumonia at the time of death, collected lung samples were not considered as normal samples.

## Histopathological characterization
Histopathological characterization was performed on all pre-mortem and post-mortem formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) samples to define the tumor cellularity and histological tumor subtype according to the WHO classification . Tumor cellularity was defined as the relative percentage of viable tumor cells in the tissue. Samples with ≧10% tumor cellularity were selected for immunohistochemical staining with the ER antibody (clone EP1, DAKO, RTU, CE-IVD). ER and progesterone receptor (PR) were scored following ASCO/CAP 2020 guidelines with a cut-off for positivity at 1% . HER2 was assessed according to the ASCO/CAP guidelines , . Patients were considered as HR positive/ HER2 negative (HR+/HER2−) if they had either ER+ or PR+ and HER2− disease at primary diagnosis.
Eight targets were selected for immunohistochemical staining of metastatic and normal samples: FGFR2 (ab289968, EPR24075-418, 1:100, Abcam, RUO), FN1 (A0245, RTU, Dako), HER2 (A0485, Agilent, RTU, CE-IVD), HER3 (D22C5, 1:250, Cell Signaling Technology, RUO), NECTIN4 (EPR15613-68, 1:100, Abcam, RUO), MUC1 (M0613, clone E29, 1:1000, Dako), TROP2 (ab227689, clone SP293, 1:150, Abcam, RUO), VTCN1 (B7-H4 (D1M8I), 1:400, Cell Signaling Technology, RUO). Sample selection was performed to consider a range of mRNA target expression across the 6 most common organ sites. A larger number of samples were stained for HER2, TROP2, and HER3, as these were already considered for a separate project on metastatic TNBC. Regarding the normal tissue, 10 samples were taken across the liver, lung, and axillary lymph nodes. In metastatic samples, only staining in tumor cells was considered, while in normal samples, staining in every type of cell was taken into account. Immunohistochemical assessment of HER2 was done according to the ASCO/CAP guidelines, as mentioned above , . HER3 was scored according to HER2 ASCO/CAP guidelines in the metastatic samples . TROP2 was scored using the H-score, but for our analysis, only the total percentage of cells displaying membranous or cytoplasmic staining was taken into account. Due to a lack of guidelines, protein expression of the other 5 targets in metastatic samples was scored as a continuous variable, % of tumor cells, and in normal samples, the other 5 targets and HER3 were assessed as the % of all normal cells that showed any level of staining, regardless of intensity and cytological localization.

## RNA extraction and sequencing
RNA extraction was performed on fresh frozen (FF) and FFPE samples using the established protocol from our original study . To summarize, RNA from the tissue specimen was isolated using the Invitrogen™ TRIzol™ reagent, after which chloroform was added, followed by a centrifugation step. The aqueous phase containing the RNA was transferred, after which Genelute—LPA (Sigma Aldrich—56575) and isopropanol were added, and a new centrifugation step was performed. The RNA pellet was washed with ethanol, air dried, and solubilized in nuclease-free water. Extracted RNA was quantified using the NanoDrop™ One spectrophotometer. The extracted RNA was subsequently sequenced using the Lexogen protocol (3’ mRNA FWD Quantseq) for Illumina NovaSeq6000 . The FASTQ files obtained were converted to reads after mapping to the GRCh38 reference genome (primary assembly, dated 08.06.2022) using the STAR aligner (v2.7.10a) , and the transcripts were annotated using gencode (v40 for GRCh38.p13). Gene counts were calculated using the subread package (v2.0.3) and normalized using the variance stabilizing transformation (VST) method from the DESeq2 package (v1.34.0) . No bias in the number of reads between FF and FFPE samples was observed. This conclusion was drawn using a mixed quantile regression model, with assigned reads as the outcome and patient ID as a random effect (estimate for FF vs. FFPE = −77616.99, 95% CI: −542500.19 to 387266.2, p value = 0.74). Consequently, downstream analysis was not adjusted for sample conditioning.

## TCGA data
Bulk mRNA sequencing data from 1111 primary breast cancer samples of TCGA were downloaded from the GDC portal of the National Cancer Institute (https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov, January 29, 2024). Data was queried from the TCGA-BRCA project that contained 1231 samples for which bulk transcriptomic profiling was performed, and for whom gene counts (in TPKM) from the STAR workflow were available to download as a TSV file. The gene expression of ADC targets was compared with our cohort after normalizing the dataset with log2(x + 1) and rescaling the gene expression range to our cohort.

## Statistics and reproducibility
As the study was intended to generate future hypotheses, no statistical analyses were performed to estimate effect/sample sizes. No data were excluded from the analyses. The experiments were not randomized. Patients with de novo metastatic disease were included for the estimation of the median age of first metastatic diagnosis in the patient cohort. Raw gene counts were normalized using VST and were reported as a continuous parameter. All analyses were performed in R (v4.3). Boxplots were used to report the intra-patient heterogeneity of target expression using the ggplot2 package (v3.4.3). Global median target expression in metastases across patients was reported using boxplots by calculating the median expression of each target across all metastases within each patient. Global IQR was also calculated in a similar manner. Weighted Spearman correlation on median gene expression was used to identify the presence of co-expressed/mutually exclusive targets after accounting for sample size effect from each patient using the wCorr package (v.1.9.8). The Spearman correlation coefficient (Rho) threshold for strong correlation between ADC target pairs was set at 0.60 . Since wCorr does not calculate evidence of statistical significance for Rho, permutation tests with 10,000 iterations were applied to assess this evidence. Regular Spearman rank correlation was performed to report the degree of correlation (i) between target gene expression and tumor cellularity and (ii) between normalized gene expression in primary untreated tumors in TCGA and UPTIDER cohorts. This was done using the base R stats package (v4.3.0). To accurately report ER% for each unique metastasis linked to multiple metastatic lesions collected from the site of metastasis, we applied a recently published methodology that used a weighted average to attribute the ER% evaluated on multiple tumor samples to each unique metastasis. We then assigned these scores to all linked metastatic lesion samples that underwent bulk mRNA sequencing. Associations between gene expression and independent covariate (sample status: metastases vs primary untreated tumors, metastases of interest vs all metastases, ER+ vs ER− metastases, ILC vs invasive breast carcinoma of no special type (IBC-NST) metastases) were assessed by linear mixed quantile regression with random effect on patient ID using the lqmm package (v.1.5.8) . p values were derived from Wald’s test, and a correction for multiple testing was applied using the Benjamini & Hochberg procedure to control for false discovery rate (FDR). To expand the scope of hypothesis-based target detection in the cohort, evidence of statistical significance was reported for adjusted p values (FDR) < 0.10, and a trend was reported between 0.10 and 0.15. Global median target expression in normal samples across organ sites was reported using boxplots by calculating the median expression of each target at each organ site. All metastases and normal tissues, irrespective of organ site, were evaluated to compute median expression change to identify targets that had higher median levels in either metastases or normal tissues. Following this, the percentage of gene expression overlap between metastases and normal tissues was calculated by x: [Min (Q3 met, Q3 normal)−Max (Q1 met, Q1 normal)]/[(Q3 normal−Q1 normal)] × 100; with Q3 = 3rd quartile of gene expression, Q1 = 1st quartile of gene expression. No formal association test was performed between metastatic and normal samples. Z-scores were calculated using mean absolute deviation on median gene expression values per normal tissue to identify tissues having preferential target expression/targets displaying preferential expression across tissues. Statistical analysis of the repeated sampling experiment was performed as described in our earlier paper . In brief, the ssPMI was defined as the time between the death of the patient and the start of sample fixation in hours. Associations between gene expression and ssPMI were assessed by linear regressions for longitudinal data, with gene expression scores as the dependent variable, time as the independent variable, and accounting for the clustering of the data by patient and organ using the generalized estimating equation method using the glmtoolbox R package (v0.1.4). Three nested models—with constant, linear, and non-linear relationships—were compared using an ANOVA testing strategy, and the best model was retained. Non-linearity was rendered by a restricted cubic spline with three knots. Mann–Whitney U-test was used to report evidence of statistical difference in % of positively stained cells across 8 targets of interest, between metastases and normal tissue. As only female patients were included and data on gender were not collected, no analysis based on sex/gender was performed. All reported values have been rounded to the nearest 2 digits after the decimal.
