Tumor-initiating probe yellow (TiY) has been discovered for identifying tumorigenic cells by unbiased phenotypic screening with thousands of diversity-oriented fluorescence library (DOFL) compounds in a patient-derived lung cancer cell model. This study demonstrated the clinical feasibility of TiY for tumor-specific fluorescence imaging in the tissues of patients with colorectal cancer (CRC). To evaluate the efficacy of TiY in tumor imaging, surgical specimens were obtained, consisting of 36 tissues from 18 patients with CRC, for ex vivo molecular fluorescence imaging, histology, and immunohistochemistry. Orthotopic and chemically induced CRC mice models were administered TiY topically, and distinct tumor lesions were observed in 10 min by real-time fluorescence colonoscopy and ex vivo imaging. In a hepatic metastasis mouse model using splenic injection, TiY accumulation was detected in metastatic liver lesions through fluorescence imaging. Correlation analysis between TiY intensity and protein expression, assessed via immunohistochemistry and Western blotting, revealed a positive correlation between TiY and vimentin and Zeb1, which are known as epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT) markers of cancers. A comparative analysis of TiY with other FDA-approved fluorescence probes such as ICG revealed greater quantitative differences in TiY fluorescence intensity between tumor and normal tissues than those observed with ICG. Altogether, these results demonstrated that TiY has a strong potential for visualizing CRC by fluorescence imaging in various preclinical models, which can be further translated for clinical use such as fluorescence-guided surgery. Furthermore, our data indicate that TiY is preferentially uptaken by cells with EMT induction and progression, and overexpressing vimentin and Zeb1 in patients with CRC.

## Introduction
Colorectal cancer (CRC) is a primary contributor to cancer-related deaths worldwide , . Because only a small number of patients exhibit symptoms in the early stages, implementing strategies for the prevention and early detection of CRC holds clinical significance in improving survival rates associated with CRC . Conventionally, white-light endoscopy is globally used for early CRC screening; however, the adenoma detection rate during endoscopy is faced with challenging quality improvement to reduce miss rates – . Surgery is the primary method for reducing CRC mortality, and complete surgical resection of malignant tumors improves prognostic outcomes and decreases recurrence rates , . For achieving complete resection with negative margins, intraoperative direct examination plays a pivotal role in quantifying the overall cancer burden and influencing surgical strategies, particularly in regions that are less accessible to noninvasive evaluation. Nevertheless, depending solely on operator judgment or traditional biopsy for evaluating organizational characteristics is inadequate due to time constraints and the potential for sampling errors . To overcome these limitations, advanced approaches for direct visualization of tumor lesions have emerged .
Fluorescence-based tumor imaging can be a useful technique to improve cancer–normal delineation because of its advantages such as high specificity and sensitivity, real-time imaging, and absence of radiation exposure , . Nontargeted near-infrared (NIR) agents for detecting CRC, such as 5-aminolevulinic acid (5-ALA) and indocyanine green (ICG), have been widely investigated and are FDA-approved – . It is known for the preferential accumulation of nonspecific dyes in tumors due to an enhanced permeability and retention effect, which is considered less specific than tumor-specific ligand-receptor-mediated mechanisms , . Conjugation of fluorescent dyes with specific tumor-specific markers, such as antibodies and peptides, improves the specificity of this technique for tumor imaging. Various tumor-specific probes have been reported, including vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) , , epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) , colon cancer-specific protein-2 (CCSP-2) , epithelial cell adhesion molecule (EpCAM) , , endothelial A receptor (ETAR) , c-Met , and CEACAM5 . Nonetheless, these approaches have revealed the limit associated with the discordance of location between microscopic and macroscopic detection results from tumor heterogeneity .
A small-molecule tumor-initiating probe, known as TiY (tumor-initiating probe yellow), which can detect tumor-initiating cells (TICs), was developed by Lee et al. using a diversity-oriented fluorescence library approach (DOFLA) , . They convincingly demonstrated the remarkable selectivity of TiY toward TICs in lung cancer by binding to soluble vimentin, a universally acknowledged biomarker of epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT) . TiY has also been reported as an in vivo targeting probe in a lung cancer animal model .
In this study, we demonstrated the feasibility of TiY in improving the visualization of CRC lesions using molecular colon endoscopy and ex vivo detection and also conducted a comparative analysis with other fluorescent dyes in the tissues of patients with CRC.

## Results

## Tumor imaging of TiY in the CRC tissues of patients
To confirm the advantage of imaging with TiY in CRC, we analyzed the efficacy of TiY using 36 tissue samples obtained from 18 patients with CRC (Table  ) by ex vivo molecular imaging. The fluorescence intensity of tumor/normal tissues, including surgical tissue samples, was evaluated after incubation with TiY or Flamma-552 dye (Fig.  A, Supplementary Fig. ). TiY-incubated CRC tissues obtained from patients exhibited a significantly higher fluorescence intensity than adjacent normal colon tissues (4.07-fold, P = 0.0194) (Fig.  C). However, the Flamma-552 control dye, which shares a similar wavelength range with TiY, exhibited no differences in fluorescence intensity between CRC and normal tissues (Fig.  C). To precisely analyze the molecular mechanism of TiY fluorescence intensity and tumor positivity, we calculated the correlation coefficient between fluorescence intensity values and expression of tumor-specific molecules, including vimentin, which is a potential marker for TiY . All tissues used for ex vivo molecular imaging were subjected to correlation analysis. Immunohistochemistry was conducted to evaluate proliferative cancer-specific markers, particularly those associated with EMT or colon TIC surface, such as vimentin, SERBP1, E-cadherin, Zeb1, and CD133. H-scoring was performed through visual inspection by a pathologist and using reference comparisons as described in Materials and Methods (Fig.  B, Supplementary Fig. ). As depicted in Fig.  D, the TiY fluorescence intensity positively correlated with vimentin and Zeb1 expression, whereas the correlation of SERBP1, E-cadherin, and CD133 with TiY fluorescence intensity was not significant (Supplementary Fig. S2). In tissue immunofluorescence staining, vimentin-positive regions colocalized more with TiY-positive regions in the stromal tissue (> 50%), while Zeb1-positive regions correlated more with TiY-positive regions in the epithelial tissue (Fig.  E). 

## Expression of vimentin and Zeb1 in human CRC cell lines and TiY uptake
Using five colon cancer cell lines (NCT116, SW620, SW480, DLD-1, and HT29) and a normal human colon mucosal epithelial cell line (NCM460), we measured fluorescence intensity after TiY staining and the expression of vimentin, SERBP1, E-cadherin, Zeb1, and CD133 by the Western blotting. As illustrated in Fig.  A, the staining intensity of TiY was higher in CRC cell lines than in the normal cell line. The TiY fluorescence intensity of SW620 and SW480 cell lines was the highest, and that of DLD-1 cell line was the lowest among the cancer cell lines (Fig.  A, B). SW620 and SW480 cell lines overexpressed vimentin and Zeb1, whereas the other cell lines did not express vimentin and Zeb1 (Fig.  C). A quantitative analysis revealed that the band intensity value ratio with internal reference β-actin expression of vimentin and Zeb1 was higher in SW620 and SW480 cell lines than in HCT116, DLD-1, and HT29 cell lines (Fig.  D). Correlation analysis of fluorescence signal intensity and band intensity of markers revealed a significant positive correlation between TiY fluorescence intensity and vimentin (R = 0.98) and Zeb1(R = 0.96) expression (Supplementary Fig. S3). 

## CRC detection ability of TiY by fluorescence colon endoscopy of CRC lesions in AOM/DSS and orthotopic mouse model
To determine the efficacy of TiY in targeting tumor lesions in vivo, mouse models for colitis-associated CRCs were examined by fluorescence colon endoscopy. Colitis-associated CRC was induced in 11 mice according to our established research protocol involving Azoxymethane (AOM)/ Dextran sodium sulfate (DSS) administration (Supplementary Fig. S4). After a second DSS injection, mice were monitored weekly by endoscopy. During weeks 5–6 of monitoring, colon endoscopy was performed to identify tumors through histological examinations. Mice with confirmed tumor growth were subjected to intrarectal treatment with TiY (50 µM/100 µL) and Flamma-552 (50 µM/100 µL). At 10 min after TiY treatment, the fluorescence signal of the tumor exhibited an increase while maintaining a low background. However, the colon tumor in Flamma 552-treated mice exhibited weak signals on both real-time fluorescence colon endoscopy and ex vivo molecular imaging. (Fig.  A). Mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) of tumor regions was significantly higher than that of the adjacent normal region, and TiY intensity increased within 10–30 min after intrarectal treatment (Fig.  C, D). To further investigate the TiY molecular imaging of human CRC lesions, we established an orthotopic murine model with tumor growth in the colon mucosa . After the injection of HCT116 and SW620 cell lines, tumor growth was observed by white-light colon endoscopy for 4 weeks, which was confirmed to be a carcinoma by histological analysis. Mice with confirmed tumor growth were treated with TiY via intrarectal introduction. After 10 min, we detected the accumulation of fluorescence signals in tumor lesions with high intensity and minimal background (Fig.  B). 

## Metastatic tumor detection using TiY in intrasplenic injected metastatic mouse model
For visualizing liver metastases from CRC cells, we used a standardized liver metastasis assay in which GFP-expressing colorectal tumor cells were injected into the splenic parenchyma of mice (Supplementary Fig. S5). After follow-up MRI and GFP monitoring to evaluate the growth of metastatic tumors, we performed fluorescence imaging after an intravenous injection of TiY. 30 min after tail vein injection with TiY, all mice were sacrificed and the liver, lung, heart, colon, stomach, kidney, and spleen were removed. Ex vivo fluorescence imaging was taken after immediately removal of individual organs. As shown in Fig.  A, all mice injected with SW620 cells into the spleen developed tumor growth in the liver, and fluorescence imaging followed by TiY injection showed a significantly greater fluorescent signal in the livers of tumor-bearing mice than in normal mice. From ex vivo fluorescence imaging data, we quantified the fluorescence intensity values ​​of the region of interest (ROI). The mean fluorescence intensity values ​​of the livers of SW620-injected and normal mice were 32.8 × 109 [p/s/cm2/sr]/[µW/cm2] and 1.07 × 109 [p/s/cm2/sr]/[µW/cm2], respectively (P < 0.0001, Fig.  B). A TiY-positive lesion in the liver of a metastatic mouse model was evaluated as malignant tissue by histological staining (Fig.  C).

 

## Comparison with other probes for tumor imaging of CRC tissues from patients
To compare the efficacy of TiY tumor imaging with that of other FDA-approved fluorescence probes, we analyzed the fluorescence intensity using 36 tissue samples obtained from 18 patients with CRC (Supplementary Fig. S6, Table  ). The tissues were incubated with TiY/ICG/fluorescein, and the fluorescence intensity values were detected by ex vivo molecular imaging. The average fluorescence intensity values of the ROI of tumors from TiY, ICG, and fluorescein were 4.9 × 109 [p/s/cm2/sr]/[µW/cm2], 18.9 × 109 [p/s/cm2/sr]/[µW/cm2], and 1.7 × 109 [p/s/cm2/sr]/[µW/cm2], respectively (Fig.  A, B). The CRC tissues obtained from patients and incubated with TiY and ICG exhibited a significantly higher fluorescence intensity than adjacent normal tissues (Fig.  C, 4.95-fold, P = 0.0028 and 3.35-fold, P = 0.0007, respectively). When fluorescein was applied to tissues, no differences were observed between tumor and normal tissues (Fig.  B, C). Remarkably, TiY- and ICG-treated CRC tissues exhibited higher intensity than normal tissues (Supplementary Fig. S6); however, TiY exhibited a significantly greater difference between CRC and normal tissues than ICG, especially with a shorter incubation time (Fig.  A, C). Furthermore, single-dose toxicity analysis of TiY in mice, revealed no significant clinical signs or death (Supplementary Tables S1, S2). 

## Discussion
In recent years, there has been significant progress in the development of molecularly targeted probes for intraoperative fluorescence imaging in CRC surgery, with the aim of improving prognosis through precise determination of resection margins , , , . Nevertheless, the utilization of molecularly targeted probes has limitations, particularly in their appreciation through in vivo surgical imaging, attributable to their microscopically heterogeneous localization , . In this study, we demonstrated the potential of CRC visualization of the small-molecule TiY in a preclinical model of human CRC for the first time. Ex vivo molecular imaging using 36 tissues obtained from 18 patients with CRC revealed a significantly higher fluorescence intensity in tumor tissues incubated with TiY than in adjacent normal tissues. In vivo evaluations using AOM/DSS-induced mouse models and an orthotopic murine model demonstrated the efficacy of TiY in targeting CRCs, with high fluorescence signals being detected in tumor lesions. Furthermore, TiY effectively detected liver metastases in an intrasplenic injected metastatic mouse model.
TiY, the tumor-initiating probe yellow, has been discovered for identifying cancer cells with tumor-initiating capacity across various cancer cell types . Using unbiased phenotypic screening and DOFLA with a patient-derived lung cancer cell model and thousands of DOFL compounds , this small chemical dye exhibited better selectivity for tumorigenic cells than a CD166 antibody. Moreover, the novel approach described in this study involves real-time intraoperative imaging of cancer lesions using TiY, marking a significant advancement not only in the field of fluorescence imaging of tumors but also in the performance of TiY itself.
In the correlation analysis, a positive relationship was observed between TiY intensity and vimentin and Zeb1 expression in the CRC tissues. Subsequent experiments with human CRC cell lines revealed higher TiY staining intensity in cancer cells expressing vimentin and Zeb1. The positive correlation between TiY and vimentin is consistent with a previous study . Vimentin is recognized as a marker of EMT in cancer progression, metastasis, and poor prognosis , . EMT plays a vital role in embryonic development, and in CRC, activated EMT fosters cell migration, invasion, progression, and metastasis – . As one of the major regulators of EMT , Zeb1 is also associated with aggressive behavior of cancer, metastasis, drug resistance, and poor prognosis – . Moreover, studies have reported vimentin and Zeb1 coexpression in the invasive cancer tissues – , which supports the positive correlation between Zeb1 expression and TiY fluorescence intensity observed in our study. Meanwhile, in the immunofluorescence staining of CRC tissue sections derived from patients, we observed that the local vimentin expression patterns were partially inconsonant with the TiY signal. The previous study demonstrated that TiY could bind to soluble vimentin, but not the insoluble form . At the human tissue level, since vimentin constitutes a part of the cytoskeleton in mesenchymal cells, and is actively exported to the extracellular environment, immunostaining of vimentin in the tissue might reveal the insoluble filaments present in fibroblasts or interstitial tissues, apart from EMT-associated cells. Furthermore, numerous studies have indicated that cancer-associated fibroblasts express various mesenchymal markers, including vimentin and alpha-smooth muscle actin (α-SMA), constituting a heterogeneous cell type – . Therefore, our results suggest that TiY intensity is associated with soluble vimentin and Zeb1 expression, potentially targeting a subset of cells implicated in tumor EMT induction and progression within the tumor microenvironment.
Fluorescence-guided surgery has emerged as a rapidly advancing technique with diverse clinical applications in various cancers. ICG, a nontargeted FDA/EMA-approved fluorescent agent, is generally used in various clinical applications to label tumors, including in patients with CRC – . Nevertheless, it is estimated that the greater potential for imaging lies in urethral evaluation rather than tumor evaluation in CRC due to its relatively low specificity compared with that to normal tissues , . In the comparison analysis of TiY with other FDA-approved fluorescence probes in the present study, we observed greater quantitative differences in TiY fluorescence intensity between tumor and normal tissues than those observed with ICG (TiY: 4.95-fold versus ICG: 3.35-fold, P = 0.0257). Furthermore, TiY has potential advantages for laparoscopic or robotic surgery because of its relatively short incubation time and optimal introduction method .
Although we demonstrated the potential of TiY for effective and sensitive tumor imaging in CRC, there exist some limitations in its clinical use. A small number of patient tissue samples and the lack of in vivo imaging comparison with ICG could limit the universality of the results. Further investigation is required to develop the optimal wavelength range for clinical application such as near infrared (NIR) , as TiY has limit clinical utility due to short visible emission light.
In conclusion, we demonstrated the feasibility of TiY for molecular imaging and its potential in real-time fluorescence imaging-guided CRC surgery and diagnosis of metastatic lesions. Our data also suggest that TiY is preferentially uptaken by cells with EMT induction and progression, and overexpressing vimentin and Zeb1 in patients with CRC.

## Materials and methods

## Patient approval for human participant research
From October 2020 to July 2021, all surgical tissue samples and information used in this study were provided by the Institutional Review Board of Asan Medical Center (IRB; protocol No. 2017 − 0837). Approval for basic research was obtained from all patients (range of ages: 20 ~ 75), and written informed consent was obtained from all patients before participation. This study was conducted according to the Ethical Guidance of the Declaration of Helsinki.

## Clinical tissue sample collection
Fresh surgical resection specimens of colorectal tumor tissues and corresponding adjacent noncancerous normal tissues were collected from 18 patients with colon cancer at Asan Medical Center (Seoul, Republic of Korea) from 2019 to 2021. The detailed clinical information of the enrolled patients is shown in Table I. Fresh surgical resection specimens were placed in DEPC-PBS containing 1% antibiotic–antimycotic (Gibco; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Inc.) on ice for immediate transport.

## Animal study ethics
All procedures in this study involving mice were approved according to the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of Asan Institute for Life Sciences, Asan Medical Center (IACUC; Approval No. 2019-12-205). All animal care and use of experimental procedures were provided by the IACUC at Asan Medical Center and conducted as per the ARRIVE guidelines.

## Cell lines and culture
The human CRC cell lines HCT116, SW620, DLD-1, and HT29 [purchased from the American Tissue Culture Collection (ATCC; Manassas, VA, USA)], SW480 [purchased from the Korean Cell Line Bank (KCLB; Korea)], and a normal human colon mucosal epithelial cell line NCM460 (purchased from the ATCC) were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle medium/high glucose (DMEM/high glucose; Gibco; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Inc., Waltham, MA, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Hyclone, Logan, UT, USA) and 1% antibiotic–antimycotic (Gibco) and maintained at 37 °C in a humidified incubator containing 5% CO2.

## AOM/DSS-induced colitis-associated colon cancer model
Five male Balb/c mice (aged 6–7 weeks and weighing 20–23 g) were purchased from Central Lab Animal Inc (Central Lab. Animal Inc., Seoul, Korea) and placed in polycarbonate cages (3 mice per cage) under controlled conditions of temperature (23 °C ± 2 °C), humidity (55% ± 10%), and lighting (12-h light/dark cycle) with access to sufficient pelleted basal diet and pure water. Starting from 7 to 8 weeks of age, the mice were administered a single intraperitoneal injection of azoxymethane (AOM) (10 mg/kg body weight; Wako Pure Chemical Co., Osaka, Japan). At 1 week after AOM injection, colitis was induced by two intermittent 1-week cycles of administration of 2% dextran sodium sulfate (DSS; MP Biochemicals, Santa Ana, CA, USA) in drinking water. In mice that exhibited critical weight loss during the first week of DSS administration, the DSS treatment was discontinued immediately. At 1 week after the discontinuation of DSS administration, tumor progression was confirmed weekly through optical/fluorescence colonoscopy (Vetcom; Karl Storz, Tuttlingen, Germany). At 10–12 weeks after AOM administration, all mice were sacrificed by inhalation anesthesia with 2% of isofurane, excised, and opened longitudinally to remove their colons. Colon tissue samples were cleaned with DPBS, and the number of polyps was determined from the start of the cecum to the anus. Two sections (~ 2 cm) of rectum samples were recovered from each mouse, fixed in 4% formalin, embedded in paraffin or flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at − 80 °C until histological analysis. Ethical approval for the use of animals was obtained prior to the start of this study from the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Asan Medical Center (Seoul, Korea), and all the animals used in the experiments were treated humanely.

## Real-time fluorescence endoscopy imaging of mouse colon tumor
BioTracker™ TiY Live Cell Dye was purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (SCT059; Sigma-Aldrich Co., MO, USA), and Flamma® Fluors 552 N-hydroxysuccinimide ester, a labeling fluorescent dye for negative control, was obtained from BioActs (Incheon, Korea, cat. # PWS1122). All colonoscopies were performed using a small animal endoscopic system (Vetcom; Karl Storz). The IMAGE1 H3-ZF1 THREE-CHIP FULL HD Camera system with Snap-on Filter RFP/mCherry (wavelength 587–610 nm) that enables TiY fluorescence detection in the basic endoscope and the modified D-light PVET source (66100M3) were added. All endoscopy images were recorded as mp4 video files using the AIDA HD control system (Karl Storz), and sequences of endoscopy images were converted into TIF images through snapshots. The endoscopy image fluorescence intensity was analyzed ImageJ version 1.53e (NIH, Bethesda, MD, USA, https://imagej.net/ij) . Regions of interest (ROIs) were defined around the detected polyp based on the corresponding optical images. Thereafter, the ROIs of polyps were compared with adjacent normal mucosa and documented as a tumor-to-normal ratio for each site. After tumor implantation, orthotopic mice were administered TiY via tail vein injection. After 10 min, endoscopy was performed, and images were obtained using the animal endoscopic system (Vetcom). To confirm the intensity of fluorescence detection ex vivo, molecular imaging was performed using the Xenogen in vivo imaging system (IVIS) (Caliper Life Sciences, Inc., Hopkinton, MA, USA).

## Orthotopic injection of human colon cancer cell lines into mouse colon
Before performing orthotopic submucosal injection, needles were fabricated and prepared for use in the working channel of a colonoscope (Vetcom). The 30G needle was bound to the 23G needle using a flexible plastic pipe. Next, 1 × 107 cells of HCT116 and SW620 cell lines in 50–100 µL of 10% Matrigel (Corning, NY, USA)/phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) were injected into balb/c mice aged 8–10 weeks (Male, Orient Bio Inc.). The colon submucosal injection was gently inserted into the working channel of the endoscope using the modified flexible needle. Tumor formation was confirmed by colonoscopy imaging every week after injection. Colonoscopy was performed using a fluorescence endoscope (Vetcom).

## Establishment of colon cancer metastasis mouse model
GFP-labeled HCT116 and SW620 (2 × 106 cells/mouse) cell lines were injected per animal in 25 µL of PBS. Nude mice (males aged 6–8 weeks) were anesthetized with isoflurane (3% induction and 1% maintenance; with 100% O2 as carrier). A 0.5-cm incision was made on the left abdominal flank, and the spleen was exteriorized for intrasplenic injection. The prepared cells were injected into the spleen using a 0.1-mL syringe (Hamilton Co. Inc., Whittier, CA), after which the spleen was returned to the abdominal cavity, and the incision was sutured with 6 − 0 black silk (Ailee Co. Ltd., Busan, Korea). Liver metastasis was monitored using a magnetic resonance imaging scanner (Agilent 9.4-T animal MRI system) and an IVIS system every week for 11 weeks after splenic injection. After the detection of tumor growth in the liver, the mice were administered TiY via tail vein injection (50 µM/100 µL). Molecular imaging was performed to confirm the distribution of primary and metastatic cancers using the IVIS system at 10, 30, and 60 min. After sacrificing the mice, the organs were excised and imaged using the IVIS system. Then, half of the tissue was fixed in 4% buffered PFA, and the remaining tissue was fixed in a Tissue-Tek® O.C.T compound (Sakura Finetek USA, Torrance, CA, USA) immediately after cutting and frozen in a deep freezer.

## Ex vivo molecular imaging of the tissues of patients with CRC
Patient colon tissue samples (10–20 mm) were surgically excised from 18 patients. Fresh CRC tissue specimens with corresponding normal colon tissue specimens were evaluated immediately after surgery. Autofluorescence images of tumor tissues and matched normal colon tissues were obtained before fluorescence probe incubation. Before incubation, to avoid a possible false-positive effect from probe infiltration of the resection, the tissue was covered with low-melting agarose gel . CRC tissues and paired normal colon tissues were incubated with fluorescence probes, including TiY (50 µM), indocyanine green (ICG; 5 mg/mL; USP, Bethesda, MD, USA), fluorescein (FLUORESCITE; 76 µM; Alcon, Geneva, Swiss), and Flamma-552 (50 µM; BioActs), for the indicated duration (min) mentioned in the figure legends. After incubation, the tissues were washed three times with PBS, and fluorescence molecular imaging was performed using the Xenogen IVIS spectrum system (Optix MX3 system; ART Advanced Research Technologies Inc., Montreal, Canada). Detailed information regarding image acquisition is provided in Supplementary Table S3.

## Histopathology and immunohistochemistry (IHC)
For histopathological analysis, H&E staining was performed using paired carcinoma and noncancerous normal tissues of the patient. The expression levels of vimentin, ZEB1, SERBP1, CD133, and E-cadherin proteins in patient colon tissues were determined by IHC as described in the previous method. Tissue samples were immediately fixed with acetone, embedded in paraffin, and subsequently cut into 4-µm-thick sections. These tissue sections were transferred to salinized, charged slides and incubated at room temperature and 65 °C. After antigen retrieval for 64 min, the sections were incubated on a fully automated immunostainer with anti-vimentin (Abcam, Cambridge, UK; ab92547, 1:500), anti-E-cadherin (Abcam, ab76055, 1:2000), anti-SERBP1 (Abcam, ab55993, 1:200), anti-CD133 (Abcam, ab19898, 1:200), and anti-ZEB1 (Novus Biologicals, Littleton, Colorado, USA; NBP1-05987, 1:200) antibodies for 32 min at 4 °C overnight. Next, the sections were incubated with a biotin-conjugated goat anti-rabbit secondary antibody. A 3, 3′-diaminobenzidine (DAB) kit was used for staining at a 1:20 dilution. Exclusion of the primary antibody was used as a negative control. All image-staining patterns of the slides were visualized using an OptiView DAB IHC Detection Kit (Ventana Medical Systems, Inc., AR, USA). For correlation analysis between IHC results of markers and TiY intensity, we yielded the H-scores (histoscores) of interpretations of IHC based on the histochemical scoring method from the pathologists. Briefly, the staining intensity of each protein in tumor, adjacent normal, and stromal region cells were counted by 0 (no evidence of staining), 1 (weak staining), 2 (moderate staining), and 3 (strong staining), and used for calculating H-score .

## Immunofluorescence
Tissue cryosection slides fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde were incubated with vimentin and Zeb1 antibody, followed by incubation with anti-rabbit IgG Alexa Fluor™ 488 (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI, followed by examination and imaging under a fluorescence microscope. Microscopic images were obtained using a ZEISS Axio Observer (Zeiss, Oberkochen, Germany). The images were analyzed using the ImageJ version 1.53e (NIH, Bethesda, MD, USA, https://imagej.net/ij) .

## Western blotting
Cell lysates were prepared using radioimmunoprecipitation assay (RIPA) lysis buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Inc.) and protease inhibitor cocktail (GenDEPOT, Barker, TX, USA). The cell lysates were separated using 10% sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) and transferred to polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes. Protein-transferred membranes were blocked with 5% bovine serum albumin in Tris-buffered saline Tween 20 (TBS-T) for 1 h at room temperature and incubated with blocking buffer-diluted primary antibodies, including vimentin (1:1000), E-cadherin (1:1000), SERBP1 (1:1000), CD133 (1:1000), and ZEB1 (1:2000) antibodies, at 4 °C overnight. After washing the membranes, they were incubated with secondary antibodies, and immunoreactive protein expression signals were detected using the enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) substrate (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and visualized using Luminograph III (ATTO Corporation, Tokyo, Japan). The expression of anti-β-actin (Sigma-Aldrich Co.) was used as the loading control.

## Statistical analyses
All data are expressed as mean ± standard error of the mean. Significant differences were evaluated using Student’s t-test with GraphPad Prism version 10.3.0 for Windows (GraphPad Software, CA, USA, www.graphpad.com). Correlation analysis and coefficient calculation were conducted using GraphPad Prism version 10.3.0 for Windows. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.
