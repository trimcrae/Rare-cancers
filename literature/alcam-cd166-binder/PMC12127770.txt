## ABSTRACT
Vitronectin (VTN) is a potential non‐invasive biomarker for renal fibrosis, originally described in urinary extracellular vesicles (uEV) from kidney transplant patients (KTx). However, VTN's specific role in renal fibrosis is unclear, as it is involved in various physiological processes. This study aims to identify other uEV‐associated proteins linked to renal fibrosis to clarify which pathways involve VTN. uEV were isolated from 33 KTx patients and five healthy controls. uEV proteins were analysed using proximity extension assay (PEA), and data were normalized and compared using Welch's two‐sided t‐test to identify differentially expressed proteins between fibrotic (n = 31) and non‐fibrotic patients (n = 7). Urinary VTN levels and monocyte chemoattractant protein‐1 (MCP‐1) were measured by ELISA. PEA analysis identified 33 proteins overexpressed in the fibrotic group. These proteins clustered in STRING analysis, primarily associating with coagulation, fibrinolysis and TNF‐inflammation involving macrophages. ELISA detection of MCP‐1 further validated the results. High levels of VTN in the fibrotic group were accompanied by the upregulation of fibrinolytic pathway components (PAI‐1, tPA and uPAR), which are well‐known to interact with VTN. This study highlights TNF‐induced inflammation involving macrophages and fibrinolysis as key mechanisms underlying renal fibrosis with direct implications of VTN, which support VTN's potential as a biomarker for this pathological process.
Keywords: fibrinolysis, proximity extension assay, renal fibrosis, TNF, urinary extracellular vesicles, vitronectin

## Introduction
Kidney fibrosis is a major complication in kidney transplant patients (KTx), significantly affecting graft function and patient outcome. It is the common and final pathological manifestation of chronic kidney disease (CKD), characterized by the gradual accumulation of extracellular matrix (ECM), resulting in structural damage, renal dysfunction and finally to organ failure (Huang et al. ).
The development of fibrosis is driven by mechanisms that activate inflammatory cells, such as macrophages (Huang et al. ). In this context, the infiltration of macrophages has been strongly linked to the severity of kidney fibrosis (Cantero‐Navarro et al. ), and recent experiments in mice suggested that vitronectin (VTN) secreted by locally infiltrating macrophages is crucial to assembling a profibrotic microenvironment, leading to fibroblast activation and expansion. In line, elevated levels of VTN in tissue have been observed in multiple fibrotic organs, including the liver, lungs and skin, as well as in degenerative conditions affecting the nervous system (Hayashida et al. ; Shen et al. ; Shin et al. ). Notably, in fibrotic kidneys, VTN has been identified as one of the most upregulated proteins (Li et al. ), and urinary VTN has been suggested as a potential biomarker for renal fibrosis (Carreras‐Planella et al. ).
VTN is a multifunctional 75 kDa glycoprotein and a key component of the ECM. While holding promise as a biomarker for renal fibrosis, the specific role of VTN in the fibrotic mechanisms remains unclear due to its involvement in a wide array of biological processes, including cell adhesion (Burgos‐Panadero et al. ), migration (Schneider et al. ), proliferation (Tragoonlugkana et al. ), tissue remodelling (Wilkins‐Port et al. ), coagulation and fibrinolysis, complement activation (Tomasini and Mosher ) and apoptosis (Stepanek et al. ). Understanding the precise contribution of VTN to kidney fibrosis requires exploring the interactions and pathways in which VTN participates within the context of this pathological process.
Urinary extracellular vesicles (uEV) have been widely used in proteomic approaches to study the pathophysiological state of cells in the urinary system (Carreras‐Planella et al. ). Thus, we hypothesize that the increased levels of VTN found in fibrotic patients would be accompanied by the differential expression of other uEV‐associated proteins involved in any of the pathophysiological mechanisms leading to the development of renal fibrosis.
In this study, using targeted proteomics of uEV, we aimed to deepen the characterization of the specific pathways and interactions involving VTN that may contribute to renal fibrosis. Several components (together with VTN) of the fibrinolytic and the tumour necrosis factor (TNF) pathways were identified, thus reinforcing their role in the underlying mechanisms of renal fibrosis in KTx patients and stressing the usefulness of VTN as a kidney fibrosis biomarker.

2.

## Materials and Methods
2.1.

## Patients and Study Design
This was a single‐centre retrospective study, where patient recruitment and protocols were performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, and according to the local ethics committee ‘Comitè d’Ètica de la investigació clínica de HUGTiP’. Approval was granted (reference number: PI‐20‐083).
Fifty‐five kidney transplant (KTx) patients from the Germans Trias i Pujol University Hospital (Badalona, Spain) were included in the study. Both de novo KTx patients and a selected group of KTx patients with 3–5 years since transplantation, older than 18 years of age and younger than 75 years old, body mass index (BMI) >20 and <35, with stable renal function as per estimated glomerular filtration rate (GFR) >30 mL/min and urinary protein to creatinine ratio <1.5 g were included. All patients were under the conventional immunosuppressive regime, including in all cases tacrolimus as a calcineurin inhibitor.
Urine samples from the KTx patients were collected up to 2 h before renal biopsy. Both per‐cause and protocol biopsies were considered. The histopathological findings of the kidney biopsies were used to classify the patients into two groups based on their interstitial fibrosis percentage measured by optical microscopy techniques as described in the ‘assessment of renal fibrosis’ section, namely non‐fibrotic (0%) and fibrotic group (≥1%).
Since the majority of kidney grafts from cadaveric donors exhibit at least a minor degree of fibrosis on biopsy, five presumed non‐fibrotic healthy individuals, referred to hereafter as healthy controls (HC), who provided informed consent, were included. These individuals were selected from the Research Institute's biobank based on the assumption of 0% renal fibrosis, as indicated by a normal estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR >90 mL/min/1.73m2), no signs of altered albuminuria (<30 mg/g), absence of microhematuria or other abnormal urinary findings, and age <60 years. Given the ethical concerns surrounding invasive procedures like renal biopsies on healthy subjects, the absence of CKD was used as a surrogate marker for this presumed non‐fibrotic group. To further strengthen the group, three additional HC, selected using the same criteria, were included in the validation phase, increasing the sample size of the considered non‐fibrotic group (HC+ biopsy‐proven non‐fibrotic KTx patients).

2.2.

## Assessment of Renal Fibrosis
Kidney biopsies (n = 55) were prospectively evaluated according to the Banff 2018 criteria (Roufosse et al. ) by the nephropathologists P.R. and L.H. Formalin‐fixed paraffin‐embedded blocks were cut into 2 µm thickness histological sections and stained for hematoxylin‐eosin, PAS, Jones methenamine silver, and Masson's trichrome following the manufacturer's protocols (Benchmark Special Stains System; Roche). The glomeruli analysed was over 10 in 90% of the samples. Quantitative evaluation of the interstitial fibrosis was assessed under optical microscopy and reported as a percentage (%) of fibrosis.

2.3.

## Isolation of Urinary Extracellular Vesicles (uEV) by Size‐Exclusion Chromatography
uEV were isolated by size exclusion chromatography (SEC) as previously published by our group (Carreras‐Planella et al. ; Monguió‐Tortajada et al. ) and following International Society for Extracellular Vesicles’ (ISEV) criteria (Welsh et al. ). Briefly, the collected spot urine (25 mL) was centrifuged at 600 × g for 15 min to eliminate cells and debris and aliquoted, stored at −80°C (Erdbrügger et al. ) until use. For uEV enrichment, the urine was thawed overnight at 4°C and processed with Tris‐EDTA Buffer (pH 9; 20 mM) to disrupt the Tamm Horsfall polymers (Puhka et al. ). Next, the cleared supernatant was concentrated using a 100 kDa Centricon filter unit (Merck Millipore Ltd., Tullagreen, Ireland) at 2800 × g for 30 min, and the 150 µL concentrate was loaded into a 1 mL Sepharose CL‐2B (Sigma–Aldrich, St. Louis, USA) SEC column. Phosphate‐buffered saline (PBS) was used as elution buffer and twenty 100 µL fractions were collected.

2.4.

## Bead‐Based Assay Flow Cytometry
SEC fractions containing uEV were screened for the EV markers CD63 and CD9 by bead‐based flow cytometry as previously published (Monguió‐Tortajada et al. ; Clos‐Sansalvador et al. ). Ten microliters of each SEC fraction were incubated with 2 µL of 4 µm aldehyde/sulfate‐latex beads (Invitrogen‐Thermo Fisher Scientific) for 15 min at room temperature (RT) and blocked in 1 mL of BCB buffer (PBS, 0.1% bovine serum albumin (BSA), and 0.01% sodium azide (NaN3); Sigma–Aldrich) for 2 h on rotation. Then, EV‐coated beads were centrifuged at 2000 × g for 10 min and resuspended in 50 µL BCB buffer. Next, 25 µL were incubated either with the primary antibody anti‐CD63 (1:10, Clone TEA3/18) or the primary antibody anti‐CD9 (1:10, Clone VJ1/20.3.1), followed by incubation with the secondary antibody FITC‐conjugated goat F(ab’)2 anti‐mouse IgG (1:100; REF: 1032‐02, Bionova, Halifax, NS, Canada). Each antibody incubation was of 30 min and followed by a washing step with 150 µL of BCB, at 2000 × g for 10 min. Data were acquired in a FACSLyric flow cytometer (BD) and analysed using FlowJo v10.2 software (FlowJo, LLC, Ashland, OR, USA). The fractions with the highest median intensity fluorescence (MFI) for both markers were pooled and frozen at −80°C. The protein concentration of all SEC fractions was also determined at 280 nm absorbance in a Nanodrop spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific).

2.5.

## Super‐Resolution Microscopy (ONI)
To analyse the surface tetraspanin expression of uEV, super‐resolution microscopy images of uEV were obtained using a temperature‐controlled Nanoimager S Mark II microscope from ONI (Oxford Nanoimaging, Oxford, UK). Samples were prepared following established protocols (Verta et al. ; Skovronova et al. ). EV were labelled with antibodies present in the EV profiler kit (ONI, UK). Briefly, 7 µL of uEV were mixed with 2 µL of blocking solution (PBS‐5% BSA), 1 µL of conjugated antibodies mix (anti‐CD9‐Atto488, anti‐CD63‐Alexa555, anti‐CD81‐Alexa647), and plated into a previously coated well to attach overnight at 4°C. The next day, samples were washed twice with PBS, and 10 µL of the mixed ONI B‐Cubed Imaging Buffer (ONI, UK) was added. Images were taken in dSTORM mode and acquired sequentially in total reflection fluorescence (TIRF) mode. Single‐molecule data was filtered using NimOS software (v.1.18.3, ONI), processed with the Collaborative Discovery (CODI) online analysis platform www.alto.codi.bio from ONI, and finally corrected with the 0.2.3 drift version (Skovronova et al. ).

2.6.

## Cryo‐TEM
Urinary EV morphology was analysed by cryo‐electron microscopy (Cryo‐EM) in a Joel JEM 2011 (Tokyo, Japan) transmission electron microscope as previously reported (Monguió‐Tortajada et al. ).

2.7.

## Dotblot
Negative and positive EV markers were analysed by dotblot as previously published (Clos‐Sansalvador et al. ). Specifically, the negative marker calnexin and the positive EV markers ezrin and CD63 were evaluated. The antibodies and dilutions used were the following: primary antibody of calnexin (1:250, SC‐23954; Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Inc., Dallas, TX, USA); CD63 (1:300, Clone TEA3/18); and ezrin (1:5000, ab40839; Abcam, Cambridge, UK); and secondary antibodies IRDye800‐conjugated goat F(ab’)2 anti‐mouse IgG (1:15,000; LI‐COR Biosciences, Lincoln, NE, USA) and IRDye680‐conjugated goat F(ab’)2 anti‐rabbit IgG (1:15,000; LI‐COR Biosciences, Lincoln, NE, USA).

2.8.

## Olink Targeted Proximity Extension Assay (PEA)
Before performing the proximity extension assay (PEA) assay, all uEV samples were adjusted to the same protein concentration. Specifically, uEV pools obtained by SEC were frozen at −80°C and lyophilized using an Alpha 1‐4‐LDC‐1 M freeze dryer (Martin Christ, Osterode am Harz, Germany). Once lyophilized, uEV were resuspended in 45 µL of 1× diluted Radioimmunoprecipitation assay (RIPA) buffer (Millipore) + protease inhibitor AEBSF [4‐(2‐Aminoethyl) benzenesulfonyl fluoride hydrochloride] (0.138 mg/mL; Roche, Basel, Switzerland) and protein concentration was determined by Pierce microBCA (Thermo Scientific). Finally, all uEV samples were adjusted to 0.056 µg/µL using the lysis buffer as dilution buffer.
For the PEA assay, the Proseek Multiplex Cardiovascular III panel (Olink Proteomics, Uppsala, Sweden) was chosen, which included a total of 92 proteins described in Table . The Olink assay was performed at the ‘Immune Response and Biomarkers Core Facility’ at ISGlobal, following the manufacturer's instructions and including all the internal (incubation, extension and detection) and external (positive, negative and inter‐plate) controls recommended. Only the samples that passed the rigorous technical controls were considered. Briefly, samples with low protein concentrations were excluded from the assay due to interference in PEA internal quality controls, likely caused by unknown components present in undiluted or minimally diluted samples, as reported by the PEA technology service. In addition, analytes that did not meet the detection threshold were excluded. The final selection included a total of 33 out of the 55 patients, 5/5 HC and 73/92 analytes. All samples were normalized using the built‐in inter‐plate controls, and results were expressed as Normalized Protein eXpression (NPX) (Olink's arbitrary unit) on a log2 scale.

2.9.

## Enzyme‐Linked Immunosorbent Assay (ELISA)
To validate the proteomic results and explore the clinical applicability of the biomarker selected for the validation, an ELISA assay was performed using whole urine samples. Specifically, all samples from the PEA analysis were tested, along with three additional HC, to increase the size of the non‐fibrotic cohort. Urinary MCP‐1 levels were analysed using the commercial ELISA from R&D Systems, Inc. (REF: DCP00; Minneapolis, USA) following the manufacturer's protocol. Also, urinary VTN levels were analysed using a commercial ELISA kit (Cloud‐Clone Corporation, USA) as previously described (Carreras‐Planella et al. ) and following the manufacturer's protocol.

2.10.

## Statistical Analysis
To analyse Olink data, statistical analyses were performed using their publicly available statistical analysis app. Specifically, the non‐fibrotic and fibrotic groups were compared using Welch's two‐sided t‐test analysis. Proteins were considered significantly differentially expressed when p < 0.05. Gene ontology (GO), cluster heatmap and principal component analysis (PCA) were generated using SRplot (Tang et al. ), Heatmapper (Babicki et al. ) and Graphpad Prism v.10.1.0 software, respectively. String software (Szklarczyk et al. ) was used to analyse protein interaction networks and discover functional clusters of biological processes.
To analyse the data obtained from the ELISA, normality was first checked using a Shapiro–Wilk test, and then the appropriate statistical test was applied. To compare two groups of samples, a Mann–Whitney test (non‐parametric) was used. In the case of multiple group comparison, a Kruskal–Wallis with Dunn's post‐hoc analysis was chosen (non‐parametric). The correlation between different variables was checked by a Spearman's correlation test, and finally, to establish the discrimination capacity of the parameters between the non‐fibrotic and fibrotic groups, a receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was performed, and the sensitivity and specificity were determined. All these analyses were done using the GraphPad Prism v.10.1.0 software.

## Results
3.1.

## Patients and Healthy Controls Characteristics
From a total of 55 potential KTx patients, only 33 could finally be included in the study due to technical limitations (see M&M). Only in 2 of 33 KTx patients, the grade of detected fibrosis by biopsy was 0%. The clinical parameters of the KTx patients are summarized in Table  . As expected, increased proteinuria and albuminuria levels, along with higher Banff grades in all biopsy parameters were observed in the fibrotic group (Table  ).
 To increase the number of samples in the 0% fibrosis group, five HC with confirmed normal kidney function (GFR > 90 mL/min/m2; mALB <30 mg/g) were also included (Table  ).
 
3.2.

## Characterization of Urinary Extracellular Vesicles (uEV)
uEV were isolated from KTx patients’ and HC samples. The SEC elution profile showed a classical pattern, with uEV positive for CD9 and CD63 eluting in fractions 4–6 and separated from the bulk protein of the sample, as previously published (Clos‐Sansalvador et al. ) (Figure  ). The analysis of the uEV pools by cryo‐TEM confirmed the presence of round‐shaped double‐membraned nano‐sized vesicles (Figure  ). Also, the dotblot assay confirmed the presence of specific EV proteins, such as CD63 and ezrin, and the absence of non‐EV proteins, such as calnexin (Figure  ). Finally, super‐resolution microscopy revealed that uEV were mostly enriched in tetraspanin CD9, expressed low levels of CD63 and barely expressed CD81. Some double‐positive uEV (about 10%) co‐expressed CD9 and CD63 (Figure  ).
 
3.3.

## Fibrosis in the Kidney Allografts Is Reflected in uEV Proteomic Profiles
Targeted proteomics was set up to identify proteins involved in the fibrogenic process and present on uEV. Specifically, we analysed the Olink cardiovascular III panel, which contains proteins related to most of the pathways involved in inflammation and fibrosis (coagulation, fibrinolysis, apoptosis, complement system and cell migration…) (Table ). A Welch's two‐sided t‐test analysis of the proteomic data obtained from both groups (fibrotic vs. non‐fibrotic) revealed significant overexpression of 33 proteins in the KTx patients with fibrosis, confirming that the fibrotic lesions are reflected in uEV proteins. The complete list of differentially expressed proteins by the fibrotic patients is shown in Table  , and the NPX levels of these proteins in non‐fibrotic versus fibrotic samples are represented in Figure  .
 To further study the capacity of the uEV proteomic profile to cluster patients depending on their renal fibrotic state, we performed a cluster heatmap and a PCA. Thirty‐six out of thirty‐eight samples were segregated correctly into their expected groups. Only two samples (codes X185 and C184) were outplaced from the expected state (Baseline in Figure  ). Yet, in the cluster heatmap, some fibrotic samples grouped with the non‐fibrotic group (from X202 to X225), revealing an ‘intermediate’ proteomic profile in these fibrotic patients (Figure  ). Of note, the fibrotic patients displaying an ‘intermediate’ proteomic profile and clustering with HC in the PCA (light red dots in Figure  ), had a lower degree of biopsy‐proven fibrosis (mean = 9.85% vs. 19.59%) than the patients with clearly positive fibrotic profile (dark red dots in Figure  ) that cluster away from HC (Figure ).
 
3.4.

## Validation of the Proteomic Results by Enzyme‐Linked Immunosorbent Assay (ELISA)
As an additional validation of the proteomic results, urinary MCP‐1, one of the most differentially expressed proteins, was further analysed by ELISA. All patients and HC included in the proteomic assay were tested and, additionally, three extra HC were included to increase the non‐fibrotic cohort size. In line with the proteomic results, the comparison of MCP‐1 expression levels by the non‐fibrotic versus the whole fibrotic cohort revealed significantly higher levels in the KTx patients with fibrosis (Figure  ). Additionally, to observe if this protein was already altered in patients with low‐grades of fibrosis, we split the fibrotic patients into 1%–10% or >10% fibrosis (clinical parameters of these subgroups are summarized in Table ). The results of this analysis showed a significant overexpression of MCP‐1 already in KTx patients with low fibrosis percentages (Figure  ). Furthermore, MCP‐1 significantly discriminated the two groups (0% vs. ≥1%) with an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.8286 (p value = 0.0023), and a sensitivity and specificity of 78.57% and 70%, respectively (Figure  ). Lastly, to explore the translational potential of measuring the proteins directly in urine and by ELISA, we correlated the ELISA MCP‐1 results with the uEV proteomics. The Spearman's test revealed a strong correlation between both techniques with an r = 0.66 and a p value < 0.0001 (Figure  ).
 
3.5.

## Analysis of the Urinary Vitronectin Levels by ELISA
To confirm that VTN was also present in the urinary samples analysed, VTN levels were tested in this new cohort. Similar to MCP‐1, and confirming our previously reported results (Carreras‐Planella et al. ), the fibrotic KTx patients significantly expressed more VTN than the non‐fibrotic group (Figure  ). Interestingly, urinary VTN levels were already increased in patients with low fibrosis grades (1%–10%) and tend to rise with the fibrotic grade (Figure  ). Also, VTN showed better discrimination potential compared to MCP1 in this cohort, with an AUC of 0.9955 (p value < 0.0001) and a sensitivity and specificity of 96.88% and 100%, respectively (Figure  ).
 Finally, MCP‐1 and VTN levels determined by ELISA were normalized to urinary creatinine levels to account for variations in protein concentration, yielding similar results (Figure ).

3.6.

## Network and Gene Ontology Analysis of the Differentially Expressed Proteins, Together With VTN Reveals the Implication in the Fibrinolysis and TNF Pathways
Once the proteomic profiles of fibrotic and non‐fibrotic patients were identified, we focused on the interactions of the differentially expressed proteins and their biological processes’ implications. A Search Tool for the Retrieval of Interacting Genes/Proteins (STRING) network, a Kyoto Encyclopaedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway and a gene ontology–biological process (GO‐BP) analysis were performed. In addition, VTN (which was not included in the proteomic panel assessed) was also added to these analyses. The network analysis showed that all the proteins except SCGB3A2 clustered together. Furthermore, the two most important pathways of these proteins were the complement and coagulation cascades and the TNF signalling pathway (Figure  ). In line with these results, the GO‐BP analysis revealed the regulation of fibrinolysis and coagulation as the principal biological processes, together with the regulation of apoptosis and homeostasis (Figure  ).
 

4.

## Discussion
Analysing the proteins of uEV from kidney transplant (KTx) patients, we identified proteins upregulated in patients with biopsy‐proven kidney fibrosis. Specifically, the proteomic analyses revealed several proteins involved in the fibrinolytic and TNF pathways, in which macrophages are key players, and in which VTN is involved, and could explain the increased presence of VTN in the urine of fibrotic patients.
Targeting kidney fibrosis is essential for managing CKD. Despite extensive research, non‐invasive biomarkers and effective antifibrotic therapies remain elusive due to the complexity of kidney fibrosis, involving multiple mechanisms, pathways and biological processes (Huang et al. ; Ruiz‐Ortega et al. ). Key signalling pathways such as TGF‐β, Wnt, NF‐κB/TNFα, Angiotensin II/RAAS, Notch, RAS/MAPK, PI3K/AKT and JAK/STAT have all been implicated in kidney fibrosis (Sinha and Nicholas ; Yuan et al. ), along with the coagulation pathway (Oh et al. ) and the complement system (Tserga et al. ). Understanding the specific molecular mechanisms of kidney fibrosis is critical for developing new therapeutic strategies to halt and/or reverse fibrosis progression and to discover early biomarkers for clinical intervention before significant renal function is lost.
In this context, our group previously identified VTN as a potential non‐invasive biomarker for renal fibrosis (Carreras‐Planella et al. ). Later, more recent research unequivocally related VTN and renal fibrosis through gene knockout experiments (Peng et al. ), thus fully validating our observation. However, the specific pathways involving VTN that trigger renal fibrosis are not fully elucidated. Therefore, a targeted proteomic study, including proteins from TNFα signalling, the complement system, the coagulation pathway, and apoptosis, among others, was conducted on uEV from KTx patients and HC. uEV have the advantage of enabling low‐abundant protein detection, by reducing the presence of contaminants in the proteomic analysis and concentrating the sample in small volumes (Gámez‐Valero et al. ). EV also provide a molecular snapshot of their originating cell's state, effectively mirroring the pathological condition (Yáñez‐Mó et al. ). Characterization of uEV samples confirmed the high purity and integrity of the uEV preparations as previously published (Clos‐Sansalvador et al. ; Monguió‐Tortajada et al. ).
Comparing the proteomic profiles of the non‐fibrotic and fibrotic patients revealed the overexpression of several proteins associated with fibrosis. In addition, the PCA and the cluster heatmap of the proteomic profile could successfully differentiate the patients with and without fibrosis. Moreover, an intermediate fibrotic profile could be envisaged from our results, although the limited number of analysed samples precluded a significant outcome.
Most of the proteins identified have been described to be closely related to fibrogenesis. For instance, the knockout of CXCL16 has been shown to protect against renal dysfunction and fibrosis produced by angiotensin II in a mouse model of deoxycorticosterone acetate‐salt hypertension (Liang et al. ). It has also been shown to regulate the recruitment of IL‐4‐producing natural killer T cells, which regulate macrophage M2 polarization (Huang et al. ). A recent report described activated M2 macrophages as producers of VTN in fibrotic kidneys, playing a key role in fibroblast activation and proliferation, and exacerbating fibrosis (Peng et al. ). In this sense, our uEV analyses also point out a key implication of M2 macrophages due to the presence of the specific M2 marker CD163 and the overexpression of MCP‐1 for monocyte/macrophage recruitment to injury sites (Qian et al. ), confirmed by both proteomics and ELISA techniques.
The binding of MCP‐1 to its receptor CCR2 does not directly mediate the transition of monocytes to pro‐fibrotic macrophages. Instead, ligand‐receptor binding activates downstream signalling cascades such as TGF‐beta, Wnt, NFKB/TNFa, Angiotensin II/RAAS, Notch, RAS/MAPK, PI3K/AKT and JAK/STAT, to promote the expression of pro‐fibrotic transcription factors and genes, which are responsible for the macrophage polarization (He et al. ). Furthermore, this signalling process enhances the expression of breast regression protein or beta‐catenin, which potentiates the activation of more pro‐fibrotic macrophages (Xu et al. ). In the same line, we also found GFD‐15, CHIT‐1 and PAI‐1 as differentially expressed proteins, which also activate and polarize M2 macrophages (Jayashree et al. ; Takenouchi et al. ; Li et al. ; Kubala et al. ). PAI‐1, together with the presence of uPAR, tPA, TF, MMP‐3, MMP‐9, COL1A1, and the confirmed overexpression of VTN in the fibrotic cohort by ELISA evidenced the key role of the coagulation cascade and, more specifically, the inhibition of fibrinolysis as a crucial mechanism of fibrosis. Briefly, PAI‐1 inhibits the conversion of plasminogen to plasmin, which in turn halts the activation of metalloproteases and the degradation of ECM promoting more fibrosis (Luther and Fogo ). The binding of VTN to PAI‐1 stabilizes and increases its half‐life, extending the inhibition of fibrinolysis (Zhou et al. ). The analysis of VTN by ELISA of this new cohort of KTx patients further validated previous results, confirming its potential as a non‐invasive biomarker of renal fibrosis (Carreras‐Planella et al. ).
The presence of MCP‐1 also revealed an active inflammation confirmed by the overexpression of TNF receptors (TNFR1/2) observed in our fibrotic cohort. Early circulating levels of both TNFR1 and TNFR2 are significantly increased in patients with CKD (Lousa et al. ) and play a crucial role in the transition from an acute kidney injury (AKI) to CKD (Arthanarisami et al. ). Moreover, circulating TNFR2 levels have been correlated to the severity of mild and advanced tubular lesions (Lousa et al. ) and kidney fibrosis (Arthanarisami et al. ). These data show the co‐identification of fibrosis and the TNF signalling pathway. Additional indications of an active inflammation were the presence of proteins that regulate cell adhesion, such as JAM‐A, ALCAM and SELP. These proteins contribute to the recruitment of leukocytes into the inflamed endothelium (Nourshargh et al. ; Bauer et al. ; Zhang et al. ), increasing the number of ECM‐producing cells and fibrosis. Lastly, other components found that have also been linked to ECM remodelling, fibroblast activation, and fibrosis were CHI3L1, CPA1, CPB1, TRAP, IGFB‐1, IGFB‐7, CTSZ, CTSD, EPHB4, SCB3A2, PLC, EGFR and LTBR (Lee et al. ; Tokunaga et al. ; Kovács et al. ; Németh et al. ; Fox et al. ; Campden et al. ; Zhang et al. ; Wu et al. ; Kurotani et al. ; Lee et al. ; Cao et al. ; Nakayama and Bromberg ).
The clustering analysis of all these proteins suggested at least two pathways where VTN is implicated—the coagulation cascade and TNF signalling—in which M2 macrophages play a central role. Briefly, the binding of TNF to its receptors activates the endothelium, which releases MCP‐1 (Weber et al. ). MCP‐1 mediates macrophage recruitment (Ajuebor et al. ), establishing an inflammatory environment that promotes VTN expression (Peng et al. ). As VTN functions include the stabilization of the ECM and binding to PAI‐1, we may hypothesize that VTN is contributing to perpetuating the fibrotic process (Declerck et al. ; Zhou et al. ) (Figure  ).
 Existing approaches targeting the MCP‐1/CCR2 axis in fibrotic diseases in animal models (He et al. ; Xu et al. ; Ninichuk et al. ; Horuk ; Kitagawa et al. ; Chang et al. ) and clinical trials (Menne et al. ; de Zeeuw et al. ) have shown beneficial effects and seem to support its crucial implication in renal fibrogenesis. Similarly, targeting of integrin receptors such as αvβ3 or αvβ5 (where VTN may bind) has also shown promising results in improving renal fibrosis in in vivo models (Chang et al. ), confirming the potential of targeting VTN pathways and its critical role in renal fibrosis.
Although we could identify some proteins of pathways related to fibrosis and in which VTN is involved, this study still has some limitations. First, a limited number of patients that could be fully analysed due to the technical controls of the PEA. Furthermore, from 33 patients included, only 2 had 0% fibrosis, a limitation that was partially overcome by adding five HC into the analysis to increase the potency of the results. Also, VTN was not originally included in the PEA panel analysed. However, as we already identified VTN in uEV of KTx patients (Carreras‐Planella et al. ) and confirmed here the expression in the fibrotic group by ELISA we included VTN in the STRING cluster analysis. Finally, the HC provided by the biobank were all female, which could potentially influence the final outcomes of the study. However, no sex‐related differences were observed in VTN and MCP1 levels, nor in kidney function within our fibrotic cohort (data not shown), suggesting that this impact may be negligible.
In summary, our findings offer new insights into the complex mechanisms of kidney fibrosis in KTx and, most possibly, CKD patients. This may help the development of targeted therapeutic strategies and find early detection of biomarkers, such as VTN, addressing the current limitations in treating and diagnosing this condition, and ultimately improving patient outcomes.
