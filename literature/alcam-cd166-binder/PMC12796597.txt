## Summary
Differential long non-coding RNA (lncRNA) expression has been correlated to clinical characteristics of colorectal cancers (CRCs), which are the second leading cause of cancer-related deaths. LncRNA UCA1 plays a role in epigenetic gene regulation in diverse cancers. We studied CRC cell properties in CRISPR-Cas9 HT29-derived models and, interestingly, UCA1-depleted HT29 cells presented an increased stem-cell phenotype. We show that loss of UCA1 modulated SWI/SNF chromatin remodeling complexes, previously shown to be involved in maintaining stem-cell properties. Not only was UCA1 permissive for induced SWI/SNF subunit SMARCA2 (BRM) expression upon chemo-drug treatment, but it also affected subunit compositions of SWI/SNF complexes by direct interaction of UCA1 with both ATP helicase BRM and BRG1. UCA1 is known to stimulate proliferation and decrease apoptosis, and we here show that it can restrain cells from a stem cell phenotype. The dual action of UCA1 revealed in this study highlights the complex actions of lncRNAs in cancer.

## Introduction
Colorectal cancers (CRCs) have become the second leading cause of cancer-related deaths in Europe. Acquired chemoresistance and the development of metastatic lesions, associated with the presence of cancer stem cells, are a cause of poor survival rates. On average the 5-year survival rate is 14% for patients with metastatic CRC. Besides expression of protein-coding genes, differential expression of diverse long non-coding RNA (lncRNA) transcripts has also been correlated with different clinical CRC characteristics and with molecular phenotypes. 
UCA1 (GeneID: 652995) is a lncRNA that is up to 2.7 kb in length and that is implicated in diverse cancers including CRC. , , , , UCA1 resides both in the cytoplasm and nucleus of cancer cells. Recently, it was shown that N6-adenosine methylation of UCA1 increases its stability via interaction with IGF2BP2 (GeneID: 10644) in the cytoplasm. Several studies suggested that cytosolic UCA1 regulates miRNA-mediated decay of genes by acting as a competing endogenous RNA. For example, the short isoform of UCA1 (1.4 kb) can inhibit apoptosis by binding miR-27a-5p and regulating UBE2N (GeneID: 7334) expression in ovarian cancer cells. That study also reported that a long isoform of UCA1 is mainly present in the nucleus. Nuclear UCA1 stimulates, and is crucial for, cell cycle progression. , Indeed, UCA1 is known to negatively regulate transcription of cell cycle inhibitors p21cip (CDKN1A, GeneID: 1026) and p27Kip1 (CDKN1B, GeneID: 1027), , and to stimulate transcription of cyclin D1 (CCND1, GeneID: 595) at the promoter level. Epigenetic regulation of transcription by UCA1 is also highlighted by its interaction with enhancer of zeste homolog 2 (EZH2, GeneID: 2146) regulating histone 3 tri-methylation (H3K27me3) , and by the interaction with chromatin insulator CCCTC-binding factor (CTCF, GeneID: 10664). Interestingly, the presence of UCA1 resulted in less binding of Brahma-related gene 1 (BRG1, UniProt: P51532/SMARCA4, GeneID: 6597) at p21cip (CDKN1A) promoter locus in bladder cancer cells. In fact, RNA-pull down in these cells showed interaction of UCA1 with BRG1-containing SWI/SNF complexes. In our study, we investigated gene regulation by UCA1 in CRC cells.
Several studies have shown a role for SWI/SNF chromatin remodeling complexes in cancer. , , Different SWI/SNF remodeling complexes each harbor exclusively either adenosine triphosphate (ATP)-dependent helicase BRG1 (SMARCA4) or Brahma (BRM, UniProt: P51531/SMARCA2, GeneID: 6595). The assembly with other sub-units is dynamic during cellular differentiation and distinguishes different complex classes, such as PBAF (poly-bromo-associated factors; exclusively with BRG1), canonical and non-canonical BAF complexes (BAF; BRG1- or BRM-associated factors). , The PBAF complex containing BRG1, core subunits, and the specific subunits polybromo 1 (PBRM1, GeneID: 55193), AT-rich interaction domain (ARID) (GeneID: 196528) 2, and Bromodomain containing (BRD) (GeneID: 29117) 7, is implicated in the development and maintenance of cellular identity. , , Down regulation of human PBRM1 from the PBAF complex in prostate cancer cells resulted in more sphere formation, which signifies the presence of more stem cells. Interestingly, cells that are drivers of carcinogenesis are associated with multipotent stem-cell phenotypes. In general, the SWI/SNF complex that is frequently associated to stemness (esBAF), is a BRG1-containing complex assembled with core subunits in combination with supplementary subunits SMARCE1 (GeneID: 6605), SMARCC2 (GeneID: 6601), and ARID1A (GeneID: 8289). , Other BAF-complexes are tissue and cell type specific, for example in progenitor cells they specifically harbor SMARCD3 (GeneID: 6604) and SMARCD1 (GeneID: 6602) subunits (cardiac and neuronal progenitors, respectively). , The presence of either BRG1 or BRM does not classify the different SWI/SNF complexes, although BRG1 and BRM may associate with “actively marked” and “repressively marked” chromatin regions, respectively. Mutations of SWI/SNF subunits genes are frequently associated with cancer , , and may evoke druggable vulnerabilities , underlining the relevance of our study on this chromatin remodeling family.
A relation of lncRNA UCA1 with SWI/SNF-complexes is previously shown in breast cancer cells where UCA1 expression is inhibited by BAF-subunit ARID1A, and also in bladder cancer cells where an interaction of UCA1 with BRG1-containing SWI/SNF-complexes is shown. In this study, we showed that UCA1 depletion was associated with a stem cell phenotype. Transcript expression of several SWI/SNF subunits, particularly SMARCA2, was affected by UCA1, which was increased by cancer therapy drugs 5-fluorouracil (5-FU) and oxaliplatin treatment in our colorectal HT29 cell model. Moreover, we showed a direct interaction of UCA1 with both BRG1 and BRM, suggesting a role of UCA1 in the balance of the different SWI/SNF complexes. Indeed, we revealed a role of UCA1 in the shift of BRG1-containing SWI/SNF complex from PBAF to canonical BAF.

## Results

## LncRNA UCA1 expression is increased in CRC tumor tissue
High UCA1 expression is correlated with bad disease prognosis in diverse CRC studies. , , , , We measured UCA1 expression in normal tumor-adjacent (NAT) compared to tumor tissues from 13 patients of the Lille University Hospital (OrgaRES study) and showed that UCA1 expression was indeed significantly higher in tumor samples ( A; p < 0.01). 
In addition, we studied how UCA1 expression was distributed in heterogeneous tumor tissues by spatial transcriptome analysis of 12 CRC tumor samples. Our pathologist annotated the tumorous tissue regions on the Hematoxylin-Eosin (HE)-colored slide. Combined principal component analysis of the samples in Partek resulted in assignment of 13 graph-based expression clusters ( B). UCA1 expression was enriched and identified as top biomarker of tumor cluster 6 ( C; 29.6-fold increased expression, false discovery rate [FDR] ≤ 0.001). This cluster was also enriched for enterocyte marker KRT20 mRNA expression ( D; 11.0-fold increased expression, FDR ≤ 0.001).
Compared to other clusters that were annotated as tumor colonocyte tissue by our pathologist (clusters 1, 8, 9, and 13), cluster 6 was enriched for Hypoxia-Inducible Factor-1 (HIF-1) signaling and circadian rhythm pathways ( E; for both gene set enrichment analysis [GSEA] normalized enrichment score [NES] = 2.1, p value ≤ 0.001). Within cluster 6, UCA1 significantly correlated with expression of 502 genes. Consistent with earlier findings that link UCA1 with cell cycle progression, the correlated genes showed a pathway enrichment for cell cycle and DNA replication (respectively, NES = 12.3 and 9.5, p value ≤ 0.001) and included, for example, mRNA of CCND2 and CDK1 (absolute R ≥ 0.20, FDR ≤ 0.001).
To analyze UCA1 expression at a single-cell level, we studied publicly available single-cell expression data ( F). Similar to our spatial transcriptome results, we observed that UCA1 was higher expressed in tumor epithelial cells with 1.2-fold higher expressed in the high KRT20-expressing CRC epithelial cells (23,063 cells with KRT20 log2 mRNA expression > 2 compared to 90,530 cells log2 ≤ 2, p < 0.001). These results indicate UCA1 expression was increased in the colonocyte subclass of tumor cells.

## Loss of UCA1 expression induces a stem cell-like phenotype
To study effects of stable UCA1 depletion on CRC cell properties, we derived HT29 cell clones with a genomic deletion at exon 1 or exon 2 of the UCA1 gene locus using CRISPR/Cas9. We selected HT29iCAS9-delta UCA1 clones showing an alteration at the UCA1 genomic region and a loss of UCA1 expression in qPCR analysis ( A). Similar to a lot of lncRNA expressions, the HT29-derived control (HT29-derived inducible CRISPR associated protein 9 (ICas9) clone with PuroResistance, ICP) cells expressed very low UCA1 levels ( B); however, treatment of the cells with the chemo-drug 5-FU increased UCA1 levels. We studied alternative UCA1 transcript expression by RNA sequencing ( B). In the control ICP cells, UCA1 variants 213 (size 2.7 kb), 216 (2.6 kb), 214 (1.6 kb), 243 (1.8 kb), and 206 (1 kb) significantly increased upon 5-FU treatment ( B; FDR < 0.01). In both the dUCA1E1 and dUCA1E2 cells, UCA1 was negligible and only variant 207 lacking exon 2 (1.2 kb) was significantly induced by 5-FU in the dUCA1E2 cells (FDR < 0.001). When we assessed previously reported UCA1 target genes, we observed that SIRT1, SF1, GLS2, PFKFB2, HK2, BCL2, PBX3, and ZEB1 had a suggestive difference of 5-FU-induced expression changes in dUCA1 cells ( C).
Next, we performed tumorosphere formation assays to analyze stem cell properties in our model. With extreme limited dilution assay (ELDA) assays, we showed that dUCA1 cells had a significantly increased proportion of stem cells compared to the control ICP cells ( A; frequency of 0.18 for ICP, 0.29 for dUCA1E1 [vs. ICP; ∗∗p < 0.01] and 0.26 for dUCA1E2 [vs. ICP ∗p < 0.05]). When multiple cells were seeded the formed spheres tended to merge ( B). Analysis of individual cell culture assays not only confirmed that the stemness capacity was increased but also showed that spheres from both dUCA1E1 and dUCA1E2 cells were increased in size compared to ICP cells ( C; 120% and 122% of ICP cells [surface area 3958 ± 684 μm2, ∗p < 0.05], and D; representative phase gradient contrast [PGC]-images of measured spheres). These results suggest a role for UCA1 in cellular identity. 

## The ICP and dUCA1 cells have similar stem cell markers expression
The ability to form spheres is correlated with the expression of stem cell markers CD44, CD133, and CD166. Therefore, we studied the distribution of cells expressing these stem cell markers by flow cytometry. Since control ICP cells had a low population of stem cell marker-negative cells, we also included the original HT29 cells in our analysis. The overall presence of stem cell markers in the total cell population varied in HT29 compared to the control ICP cells (51% vs. 75%∗∗∗ CD44; 59% vs. 5,5% ∗∗∗∗ CD133, 7.2 vs. 75%∗ CD166; ∗∗∗∗p < 0.0001, ∗∗∗p < 0.001, and ∗p < 0.05), but it did not vary between the ICP and dUCA1 cells ( ). The analysis of the combined subsets defining stemness properties showed that the control ICP cells have a lower stem cell markers-negative (−) or simple positive population (+) and a higher proportion of cells with two or three stem cell markers (++/+++) compared to original HT29 cells ( E and ; [−] 2.5% vs. 0.4%, [+] 31% vs. 9%∗∗, [++] 40% vs. 64%∗∗, and [+++] 2.3% vs. 4.2% for the different populations of HT29 vs. ICP cells, respectively; ∗∗p < 0.01). However, no difference in cell population distribution was observed between ICP and dUCA1 cells that could have explained the differences in sphere formation capacity.
We analyzed the capacity to form spheres of cell subset with no markers (−), one of three (+), two of three (++), or all three markers (+++) expression after fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS). Indeed, ICP cells expressing one or more stem cell markers presented a higher ELDA estimated stem cell frequency based on tumor-sphere formation ( F). Stem cell frequency varied in the dUCA1 cells, but both dUCA1E1 and dUCA1E2 cells with one or more markers at the surface had a significantly increased stem cell frequency compared to the same population of ICP cells. From these results, we concluded that UCA1 depletion evoked intrinsic phenotype changes independent of CSC marker expression.

## UCA1 depletion affects the SWI/SNF subunit SMARCA2 expression
We further analyzed our dUCA1 model for differentially expressed genes that could be related to the differences in stem cell properties. We observed 158 genes that showed a significantly different change in expression upon 5-FU treatment in dUCA1 cells compared to ICP cells ( A; FDR < 0.05). No obvious stem cell candidates were revealed, but we observed a suggestive lack of increased expression upon 5-FU (ICP vs. dUCA1) for several subunits of the SWI/SNF chromatin remodeling complex ( B). 
The presence of different SMARCA2 (BRM)- and SMARCA4 (BRG1)-containing complexes has been associated with cancer stem-cell phenotypes; therefore, we assessed UCA1, SMARCA2, and SMARCA4 expression in stem cell markers sorted cells ( C). For UCA1, no different expression was observed in the different ICP cell populations. For SMARCA2, we observed in ICP cells an increased transcript expression in populations having one or more stem cell markers compared to stem cell markers negative cells ( C; p ≤ 0.01). SMARCA4 expression did not vary between the different ICP cell populations. In contrast to RNAseq that measured all alternative transcript expressions, e.g., including partial proteins ( D), no difference of the helicase-domain containing SMARCA2 or SMARCA4 gene expression by qPCR was observed between dUCA1E1 and dUCA1E2 cells ( D). Interestingly, dUCA1E1 and E2 cells differed from ICP cells as no significant changes of SMARCA2 transcript levels in the stem cell markers cell populations were observed. These results indicate that UCA1 interfered with SMARCA2 transcript levels.

## The signature of SWI/SNF subunit expressions defines a CRC risk group
To assess the importance of SWI/SNF subunits on survival of CRC patients, we assessed their expression in the Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) COAD-READ study. Based on subunits expression levels, multivariate analysis using SurvExpress defined two distinct risk groups for overall survival ( A; hazard ratio [HR] = 2.43, confidence interval: 1.58–3.74, p < 0.0001). The generated box-plot showed lower expression of BAF-related subunits (first 16 listed) and higher expression of PBAF-related subunits (number 17–21) in the low-risk group compared to the high-risk group (significantly different expression between groups ranging from p < 0.02 for SMARCA2 to p < 2.0e-10 for BRD9). In addition, multivariate analysis of TCGA data on other digestive tract cancers showed that the expression signature of SWI/SNF subunits also defined significantly different risk groups in pancreatic adenocarcinoma (PAAD, HR = 2.6, p < 0.0001), esophageal carcinoma (ESCA, HR = 3.5, p < 0.0001), stomach adenocarcinoma (STAD, HR = 1.87, p < 0.001), and in the stomach and esophageal carcinoma studies (STES, HR = 1.82, p < 0.001). 
To further analyze the association of UCA1 expression with SWI/SNF complexes, we evaluated the correlation with SMARCA2 and SMARCA4 in the COAD-READ dataset. UCA1 expression was significantly negatively correlated with SMARCA2 expression ( A; R = −0.26 and p < 0.0001), but not correlated with SMARCA4. Interestingly, similar correlations of UCA1 expression with SMARCA2 expression were observed in PAAD, ESCA, STAD, and STES studies ( B–S3E, respectively).
We also assessed expression of the two ATP-dependent helicases of SWI/SNF complexes, SMARCA2 and SMARCA4, and the correlation with UCA1 expression in our OrgaRES tissue samples. Here, transcript expression of SMARCA2 was not significantly different in the tumor compared to NAT samples, and there was no evidence for a correlation with UCA1 expression ( B). In our spatial transcriptome analysis, SMARCA2 showed a general distribution between the different graph-based expression clusters ( C). SMARCA4 expression was significantly increased in tumor compared to NAT tissue ( D; p < 0.05), but no correlation with UCA1 expression was observed in these samples, nor in our spatial transcriptome analysis ( D and 4E). The predominant expression of UCA1 in cells with high expression of the enterocyte marker KRT20, while SMARCA2 expression was broader, suggest that the presence of different cancerous tissue type influenced the correlation observed between UCA1 and SMARCA2 in bulk analysis.

## Loss of UCA1 in HT29-derived cells modulates SMARCA2 expression
To further study the relation of UCA1 with SMARCA2 and SMARCA4, we performed qPCR and western blot analysis in our dUCA1 cell line model. In the control ICP cells, UCA1 had a low expression level under standard culture conditions, which was significantly increased upon treatment with 2 μM 5-FU for 72h ( and F). In both the dUCA1E1 and dUCA1E2 cells, UCA1 can no longer be detected by qPCR. Upon treatment with 5-FU, the expression of SMARCA2 transcript was significantly increased in the UCA1 expressing control ICP cells, but not in the dUCA1 cells ( G). The expression of SMARCA4 transcript was not significantly affected by the 5-FU treatment, or by the loss of UCA1 expression. While the SMARCA2 transcript expression increased upon 5-FU treatment, the SMARCA2 protein level (BRM) remained similar in control 5-FU-treated cells compared to control cells ( H and 4I). This may result from an initial degradation of BRM after 5-FU treatment that was not entirely compensated by increased transcript translation. Indeed, in the absence of UCA1, there was no change of SMARCA2 transcript level and a decreased BRM protein level was observed in dUCA1E1/E2 cells, suggesting protein degradation ( I). Analyzing SMARCA4 (BRG1) protein level expression under these conditions showed no changes under the different conditions ( H and 4I). Similar trends were observed upon treatment of the cells with 2 μM of the chemo-drug oxaliplatin ( A–S4C). In a supplementary model derived from SW480 cells, SMARCA2 induction upon 5-FU incubation also appeared to be absent in dUCA1E1 cells ( D–S4F). These results confirm our initial findings with sorted stem cells, which indicated that UCA1 interferes with SMARCA2 at a transcription level.

## UCA1 physically interacts with the ATP-dependent helicases BRM and BRG1
Loss of UCA1 evoked less SMARCA2 (expression and protein) and more stem-like cells, and in contrast, cells bearing stem cell markers showed higher SMARCA2 expression. SWI/SNF complexes that are associated with a stem cell phenotype harbor mainly the other helicase BRG1 (SMARCA4). We hypothesized that UCA1 may also physically affect the subunit balance of SWI/SNF complexes. Therefore, we first analyzed direct physical interaction of UCA1 with BRM and BRG1 proteins in the colorectal HT29 cells using a Duolink proximity ligation assay adapted for ncRNA-protein interactions. In these cells both BRM and BRG1 interacted with UCA1 ( A–5D). Moreover, increasing UCA1 expression by treating the cells with 2 μM 5-FU for 24 h resulted in an increased number of interactions, with, in particularly, more nuclei having multiple spots ( A–5D). Some interactions were also observed in the cytoplasm. In addition, our RNA-IP analysis showed interaction of UCA1 with BRM and BRG1 complexes ( E), also suggesting that UCA1 interfered with these complexes on a protein level. 

## Loss of UCA1 results in different SWI/SNF-complexes
The interaction of the mutual exclusive ATP helicases BRM and BRG1 with either Bromodomain containing 7 (BRD7) and 9 (BRD9) proteins may reflect the balance of different SWI/SNF complexes; therefore, we studied their close interactions in our cell model treated with 5-FU using sensitive PLA assays ( A). BRD7 is reported to be present in BRG1-exclusive PBAF complexes, , , and, indeed, our PLA analysis showed no interaction of BRD7 with BRM in ICP cells ( A1). However, PLA spots were observed in dUCA1 cells ( A1). BRD9 is present in embryonic stem cell, non-canonical and progenitor BAF complexes (BRM/BRG1), , , , and our results showed an interaction of BRD9 with BRM in the ICP cells, which significantly increased in the dUCA1 cells ( A2). The interaction of BRD7 with BRG1, related to canonical PBAF complexes, was observed in the ICP cells, but decreased in the dUCA1 cells ( A3). On the contrary, the interaction of BRD9 with BRG1 (BAF complexes) was low in ICP cells, but was significantly increased in the dUCA1 cells ( A4). 
To consolidate these observations, we realized coimmunoprecipitations with BRM and BRG1 in cells with or without 5-FU treatment ( B and 6C). Based on input expressions and the constitutive subunit SMARCB1 protein expression, more BRG1 compared to BRM-containing complexes were present without 5-FU treatment ( B). Based on the ratio with SMARCB1, relatively more ARID1A subunits were associated with BRM-then BRG1-complexes in cells without 5-FU treatment ( B), but no differences were observed between control ICP and dUCA1 cells. The presence of BRD7 was only observed in BRG1-complexes, without differences between untreated ICP and dUCA1 cells. Treatment with 5-FU decreased input and immunoprecipitated BRM, making it difficult to observe differences between ICP and dUCA1 cells ( C). In contrast to the PLA, no association with BRD7 was observed for BRM-complexes of treated dUCA1 cells. After 5-FU treatment, overall BRG1 expression varied (input), but the presence of SMARCB1 in immunoprecipitated BRG1-complexes remained similar. More ARID1A was coimmunoprecipitated with BRG1 in dUCA1 cells and, similar to the PLA, less BRD7 was associated with BRG1 in treated dUCA1 cells ( C).
Taken together, different interactions and subunit associations in 5-FU treated ICP (high UCA1 levels) and dUCA1 cells, suggests that loss of UCA1 decreased BRD7-BRG1 association and shifted the balance toward BRD9-BRG1 complexes, which were previously implicated in embryonic stem cells.

## Discussion
We confirmed that lncRNA UCA1 expression is increased in CRC tumor tissues and showed that it is confined to a particular subclass of tumor cells. In the CRC cell line HT29, UCA1 expression increased with 5-FU and oxaliplatin treatment. In addition, we observed an increase in expression after the DNA damage induced by CRISPR/Cas9 transfection in these cells. CRISPR/Cas9 has recently been described to induce p53-activation in several cancer cell lines. Interestingly, UCA1 also binds several miRNAs of the p53 pathway and may play a role in this activation. However, transfection does not affect our analysis, since we used HT29 cells with inducible CRISPR/Cas9 in our studies. Hence, we observed a decreased UCA1 expression after multiple passaging of HT29iCas9 cells without Cas9 induction, showing that the increased UCA1 level after transfection was transient.
Our study showed that in tumor cells expressing UCA1, this lncRNA can interfere with the expression and assembly of specific SWI/SNF chromatin remodeling complexes. First, UCA1 depletion in HT29 cells led to an increase in the number of cells with stem cell properties and to lower levels of SMARCA2 in cell expressing the stem cell markers CD44, CD166, and CD133.
Although RNAseq data initially suggested differences between dUCA1E1 and dUCA1E2 cells ( B and C), differences of SMARCA2 expression could be pinpointed to alternative transcripts coding for partial proteins ( D). Moreover, the subsequent results were observed in both dUCA1E1 and E2 cells. These models were generated with different CRISPR/Cas9 sgRNA pairs, suggesting the observations were not CRISPR/Cas9 off-target effects. We further showed that, after treatment with 5-FU or oxaliplatin, the increased UCA1 expression is permissive for transcriptional upregulation of SMARCA2 expression, compensating BRM (SMARCA2) protein loss. This transcriptional regulation was also observed in CRC SW480 cells.
It is known that BRM may restitute BAF complex functionality in the absence of BRG1, but that it cannot universally compensate for BRG1 function. , BRG1 is present in (non-)/canonical BAF complexes, and it is the exclusive ATP helicase of PBAF complexes. Previously, inhibition of BRM-BAF complexes by lncBRM was reported to stimulate the formation of BRG1-BAF complexes and stimulated stemness. Thus, similarly, UCA1-stimulated SMARCA2 transcription may function as a molecular switch between SWI/SNF activating and repressing chromatin binding complexes.
Second, our results indicate that UCA1 physically interacts with the two ATP-helicases BRM (SMARCA2) and BRG1 (SMARCA4) within the nuclei of the colorectal HT29 cells. This suggests that UCA1 may also physically interfere with the SWI/SNF complex. Similar interactions have been described for lncRNAs affecting complex recruitment, , and also for lncRNAs affecting the balance of subunit assembly, e.g., the action of lncRNA NEAT on the balance of BRD4’s activator and repressor chromatin complexes. Indeed, our results showed that also UCA1 interferes with SWI/SNF subunit assembly. In the absence of UCA1, the interaction of BRD7 and BRG1 is decreased and that of BRD9-BRG1 is stimulated. BRD7 is present in BRG1-exclusive PBAF complexes, whereas BRD9 is present in non-canonical and progenitor BAF complexes (BRM/BRG1). , , , , 
The exact subunit composition of the UCA1-associated BAF complex requires further investigation. SWI/SNF complexes are an assembly of core subunits in combination with different more specific subunits. Because they have a dynamic composition, it is not always evident in the literature which subunits are present in the different complexes. The interaction of BRD7 with BRM, that we observed using PLA in dUCA1 cells upon 5-FU treatment, has not been reported before. Moreover, we did not confirm a physical interaction by coimmunoprecipitation. Probably BRD7 is only in close vicinity of the BRM protein, as suggested by a recent report identifying BRD7 as a significant proximity partner of BirA-tagged SMARCA2. In addition, it is unclear if BRD9 can be part of the canonical BAF complex. We showed interaction of BRD9 with BRG1 suggesting it is part of such complexes.
The impact of different SWI/SNF complex-mediated remodeling is hard to decipher. For example, several studies analyzed chromatin association of the ATP-Helicases (e.g., Giles et al. ), but these analyses do not discriminate between BRG1-PBAF and BAF complexes. The rise in stem cell characteristics observed in dUCA1 cells may be attributed to the loss of PBAF, which is often linked to differentiated cells, alongside a corresponding increase of canonical BRG1-BAF complexes, like esBAF. , 
The BRM-associated complexes elevated by UCA1 may clarify other findings. When HT29iCas9-dUCA1 cells were seeded at high densities, the resulting spheres were often merged. It is reported that BRM may regulate the formation of tight junctions and UCA1 can stabilize E-cadherin in prostate cancer. Further analysis of tight-junction formations could uncover UCA1’s role, through BRM, in the regulation of cell contact.
Several recent studies focused on vulnerabilities of cancers presenting mutations of SWI/SNF-encoding genes and the use of proteolysis-targeting chimeras to target SWI/SNF complexes. , Besides targeting the protein complexes, it could be worthwhile to target lncRNAs that coordinate assembly/stabilization of certain SWI/SNF complexes implicated in cancer aggressiveness.
In conclusion, our study demonstrates that UCA1 interacts with the ATP-helicases from the SWI/SNF chromatin remodeler complexes. This interaction influences the presence of different SWI/SNF protein subunits, and limits the ability of cells to acquire stem cell properties, underscoring the significance of lncRNAs in the assembly of protein complexes.

## Limitations of the study
Our study reveals an additional mechanism of nuclear UCA1 through its effects on SWI/SNF remodeling complex subunits. Classic ChIPseq analysis to study the impact of UCA1 on chromatin binding of the different complexes is complicated as most subunits are present in multiple SWI/SNF complexes. Therefore, the exact balance of physical interactions between subunits evoked by UCA1 and precise chromatin changes at gene locations remain to be elucidated.

## Experimental model and study participant details
The OrgaRES consortium study includes cryopreserved CRC tumor tissues and normal tumor-adjacent colon tissues, from 9 male and 4 female patients at the university hospital of Lille (CHU Lille, France). Their average age at surgery was 67 ± 9 years. Samples were in Tissue-Tek R OCT-compound. Samples were made available by the Lille ALLIANCE-CANCER biobank.
The colorectal adenocarcinoma cell lines HT29 (HTB-38, RRID:CVCL_0320) and SW480 (RRID:CVCL _0546) were authenticated through the Short Tandem Repeat (STR) DNA profile analysis according to the procedures recommended by the ATCC Institute and the derived cell models tested negative pour mycoplasma.

## Ethical approval
Tissues samples from patients were obtained with the approval of the institutional review board from the university hospital of Lille (CHU Lille, France) and the Lille ALLIANCE-CANCER biobank. All patients gave their informed consent and signed a non-opposition statement to research use of a biological sample.

## Method details

## Online dataset analysis
Survival data and normalized transcript expressions of the TCGA datasets were assessed via linkedOmics. The TCGA COAD-READ study included 391 patients histologically diagnosed with colon adenocarcinoma, 152 with rectal adenocarcinoma and 95 diagnosed with mucinous adenocarcinomas. Included data are the “HiSeq RNA” expression (Firehose_RSEM_log2) from Broad Institute of MIT and Harvard (version January 2016). Association of SWI/SNF genes with survival in the TCGA study was assessed via SurvExpress. Single cell expression data of the Human Colon Cancer Atlas (c295, 62 CRC patients; GSE178341), was assessed using the portal from the Broad Institute of MIT and Harvard (https://singlecell.broadinstitute.org). 

## RNA expression analysis
RNAs were isolated from 80%-confluent cultured cells and from cryopreserved resected patient samples using the NucleoSpin miRNA kit. The isolated RNA was reverse transcribed using M-MLV reverse transcriptase and random hexamer primers. Quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR) was performed with SsoFast EvaGreen Supermix on a Bio-Rad CFX96-C1000 Touch Thermal Cycler. To calculate relative expression (2−ΔΔCt method), RPLP0 was used as reference gene and samples were normalized vs. control. Primer sequences are listed in .
Bulk RNAseq and alternative transcript analysis was performed by RNA sequencing of two replicated experiments using the NovaSeq 6000 System (Plateforme de Genotypage et Séquençage, Institut du Cerveau, Paris, France). Data was analyzed using the European GALAXY platform : Raw paired-end sequencing reads were cleaned using Trimmomatic and ribosomal RNA reads were filtered out by SortMeRNA. Cleaned reads were aligned to the human Gencode reference (GRCh38.p13, v36) using HISAT2. Transcriptome assembly and quantification was applied by StringTie. Normalization and differential expression analysis were performed using edgeR. , 

## Spatial transcriptome expression
Tumor tissues from CRC patients (OrgaRES consortium study, France) were cryopreserved by snap freeze in Tissue-TekR OCT-compound and subjected to the 10x Visium gene expression analysis according to the manufacturer guidelines. Prior to the analysis a “spatial tissue optimization” was conducted, which determined the optimal tissue permeabilization time of 30 min. A Hematoxylin-Eosin staining was done after the tissue section was deposited onto the Visium slide capture area, images were obtained by Axioscan (Zeiss) and tumor tissue annotated by a pathologist. After reverse transcription on slide, a library was construction at the Go@L-GFS platform (PBLS - UAR2014 - US41, Lille France). The library was sequenced on an SP flowcell of the NovaSeq 6000 system (Illumina; ). Using data of the SpaceRanger pipeline, gene and differential expression analysis were performed with Partek Flow (version 11.0.23.1023). In short, regularized negative binomial regression was performed for normalization and variance stabilization (SCTransform) and sample batch variation corrected by Seurat 3 integration. Thereafter, principal component and graph-based clusters were analyzed.

## Derivation of UCA1-depleted cells using CRISPR/Cas9
The HT29 cell-line was cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10% (v/v) FBS, and penicillin -streptomycin (100 U/mL-100 μg/mL) at 37°C in humidified air containing 5% CO2. To obtain cells with inducible Cas9 expression (HT29-iCas9), HT29 cells were lipo-transfected with single-guide (sg)RNA Adeno-Associated Virus Integration Site 1 (AAVS1) ( ), Cas9-GFP and iCas9 donor DNA plasmids from González et al. The genomic DNA from the antibiotic resistant HT29-iCAS9 clones was analyzed by Sanger sequencing and the ones without AAVS1 wild type alleles were selected. These cells were cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS (v/v), 0.5 μg/mL puromycin and 250 μg/mL neomycin. Next, these HT29-iCAS9 cells were incubated with 2 μg/mL doxycycline for 2h before lipofectamine3000 transfection with 2 μg paired sgRNA constructs ( ). After 48h, cellular clones were isolated based on their GFP-expression by flow cytometry at the Lille cytometry platform (BD FACS Aria cell sorter, BICeL; “Plateformes Lilloises en Biologie et Santé“ (PLBS) - UAR 2014 – US 41), obtaining HT29-iCas9-transfected (ICP), dUCA1E1 and dUCAE2 cells.
A supplementary model derived from SW480 cells was generated using transient expressed Cas9-GFP and lentiviral-induced sgRNA expression with a puromycin resistance cassette ( ). A pool of dUCA1E1 cells were selectively cultured in DMEM with 4 μg/mL puromycin and supplemented with 10% (v/v) FBS, and penicillin-streptomycin (100 U/mL-100 μg/mL) at 37°C in humidified air containing 5% CO2.

## Sphere formation analysis
Sphere formation was assessed by cell cultures on non-adherent 96-well plates using either DMEM/F12 without serum and in the presence of growth factors or with 3D-Tumorsphere Medium XF. To determine the presence of cells with stem-cell properties, sphere formation was analyzed using both extreme limited dilution assays (ELDA) as previously described and an individual cell culturing assays. For the latter, cells were seeded at 3 cells/well onto a 96-well plate by flow cytometry. With the microscopic system cell discoverer 7 (Zeiss, Marly le Roi, France; BICeL plateform) images of spheres were acquired after 11 days of culture, and size analysis performed with ImageJ software.

## FACS-based analysis of cells expressing stem cell markers
Suspended HT29 cells were incubated with 1.25 μg fluorophore-coupled antibodies per 106 cells in DMEM culture medium supplemented with 10% (v/v) FBS and 0.5 mM EDTA for 30 min. Used antibodies were V450-coupled mouse anti-human CD44, APC-coupled mouse anti-human CD133/1, PE-coupled mouse anti-human CD166 and their isotype-controls. Thereafter, cells were sorted into a pool of cells expressing no markers (−), one of three (+), two of three (++), or all markers (+++) by flow cytometry using the BD FACSAria cell sorter at the Lille cytometry platform (BICeL). The sorted cells were either recovered in Tumorsphere Medium XF medium or in RNA-lysis buffer for transcript expression analysis.

## Protein expression
Proteins were recovered during the NucleoSpin miRNA procedure according to the manufactory’s guidelines or from cell lysates prepared with RIPA buffer. The samples were quantified and submitted to SDS-PAGE using either NuPAGE Novex 4–12% Bis-Tris or 3–8% Tris-Acetate protein gels in combination with molecular weight markers (prestained PageRuler Plus/Novex harp standards, Invitrogen). Western blot immuno-labelling was performed with a rabbit monoclonal antibody specific for BRG1 (ab110641, 1:10000), BRM (Cell signaling#1966, 1:10000) or β-Actin mouse antibody (A5441, 1:5000) accordingly to the manufactory’s protocol. Secondary HRP-labeled antibodies (Invitrogen, diluted 1:5000) and SuperSignal West Pico PLUS chemiluminescent substrates were used to visualize the proteins with the ImageQuant LAS 4000 camera (Fujifilm Corporation). Quantifications were analyzed with the ImageJ program (https://imagej.net/Fiji).

## Proximity ligation assay
Cells were cultured in Ibidi μ-Slides VI 0.4 or 8-chamber slides (Cliniscience, Nanterre, France), fixed with 4% (v/v) formaldehyde and permeabilized with 0.1% (w/v) saponin.
To study lncRNA-protein interactions, we used the Duolink Proximity Ligation assays (PLA) as previously described. First, we blocked background signals with 20 μg/mL sheared Cot-DNA in Duolink blocking solution. Secondly, the slides were incubated with 100 nM denatured hUCA1_R_Plus probe in Duolink blocking solution overnight at 4°C. After three PBS washes, an additional blocking was done with PBS containing 0.1% (v/v) Tween 20, 20 μg/mL sheared Cot-DNA and 1% (w/v) BSA at room temperature for 1h. Slides were washed respectively with PBS, SCC-2x containing 0.1% (v/v) Tween 20, and with PBS. Thereafter, the manufacturer instructions were followed using the primary rabbit antibody BRG1 (1:250), BRM (1:250) or normal Rabbit IgG control (1:250) and the secondary antibody Duolink In Situ PLA Probe Anti-Rabbit Minus.
To study protein-protein interactions, we followed the PLA protocol provided by the manufacturer using primary mouse antibodies BRG1 (1:350) and BRM (1:250), rabbit antibodies BRD7 (1:250) and BRD9 (1:250) in combination with the secondary antibodies Duolink In Situ PLA Probe Anti-Rabbit Plus and Duolink In Situ PLA Probe Anti-Mouse Minus. Control incubations with one primary antibody were performed in parallel.
The PLA signal was revealed by the Duolink in situ detection Red kit. After the washes with Duolink wash buffer B, the slides were incubated with 1:1000 diluted Alexa Fluor 488-Phalloidin in wash buffer B for 20 min, washed with buffer B and mounted with Duolink PLA Mounting Media containing DAPI. Images were acquired using a confocal microscope (LSM 710, Zeiss) and the PLA-interaction signals were counted as discrete fluorescent spots (ƛ 594 nm) in maximal intensity projections of the whole nucleus using the modified ImageJ macro “PLA v2.40” (PLA v2.40-BNmodif2024.ijm). At least 100 nuclei per experimental condition were analyzed and statistics was performed using Prism 8 (n = 4).

## RNA-immuno-precipitation assay
RNA-Immuno-Precipitation (RNA-IP) was performed as previously described. Briefly, 80% confluent HT29 cells were crosslinked with a 1% (v/v) formaldehyde solution in PBS for 15 min, and this was quenched with 125 mM glycine. Cells were washed with PBS, collected by centrifugation and there after the nuclear fraction was isolated and the chromatin was sheared by sonification to 200-500bp fragments using Bioruptor (Diagenode). Overnight immuno-precipitation was performed with either 1:100 diluted rabbit monoclonal antibody BRG1, rabbit monoclonal antibody BRM or normal Rabbit IgG control. To precipitate the immune complexes a 30 μL mixture of Protein A/G Dynabeads (1:3) was used. After several washes the bound fraction was eluted and incubated 2h at 80°C for decrosslinking. Proteins were degraded by incubation with 500 μg/mL proteinase K for 1h at 55°C, where after RNA was isolated using NucleoSpin RNA kit in the presence of carrier RNA.

## Coimmunoprecipitation
Cells of two confluent 150 mm plates were lysed with a 50 mM Tris buffer (pH 8.0) supplemented with 150 mM NaCl, 5 mM EGTA, 10% glycerol, 1.5 mM MgCl2, 1% Triton X-100 and freshly added 1X cOmplet protease inhibitors (Merck). Cell lysates were incubated for 30 min on ice, whereafter they were cleared by 5 min centrifugation at 12000g. One-third was used for overnight immunoprecipitation at 4°C with either normal rabbit IgG (1: 150), rabbit anti-BRG1 (D1Q7F)-SMARCA4 (1: 150) or rabbit anti-BRM (D9E8B)-SMARCA2 (1: 150). 1/50 of the volume was kept as input sample. To precipitate the immune complexes a 30 μL mixture of Protein A/G Dynabeads (1:3) was used. After three washes (50 mM Tris HCl (pH 8.0), 150 mM NaCl, 5 mM EGTA and 1% NP-40) the bound fraction was eluted by a 10 min incubation at 95°C in NuPAGE LDS sample buffer. Next, the samples were submitted to SDS-PAGE analysis as described above.

## Quantification and statistical analysis
For RNA transcript and protein expression analysis GraphPad Prism 8 was used to apply paired t-tests on ratios to compare UCA1 expression in NAT vs. Tumor tissues, and to apply One-way ANOVA followed by multiple comparison tests for cell line experiments. The influences of sex and gender on gene expression in tumor tissues were not assessed due to the heterogeneous nature of tumor tissue and a small sample size. Figures represent mean (with SD or SEM), number of biological replicates, and statistically significant results (p-values) as detailed in the figure legends. For bulk RNA-seq analysis, a pairwise comparison test was performed using EdgeR at the European GALAXY platform. , , Partek Flow was used to assess gene correlation and gene set enrichment in the spatial transcriptome data. In short, regularize negative binominal regression was performed for normalization and variance stabilization (SCTransform) and sample batch variation corrected by Seurat 3 integration. Thereafter, principal component analysis and graph-based clusters were analyzed. The ELDA software provided the pairwise tests for differences in stem cell frequencies (χ2) with 95% confidence intervals. With GraphPad Prism 8 survival curves and statistical analysis of averaged expression data from TCGA were performed (Mantel-Cox log rank hazard ratio (HR) and P-value (p)).
