## Abstract

## Background
A stable and standardized source of mesenchymal stem cells is a prerequisite for bone repair tissue engineering research and application. We aimed to establish a stable cell line of bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells from New Zealand rabbits and explore their osteogenic differentiation capacity.

## Methods
Primary rabbit bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells (RBMSCs) were isolated and immortalized via retroviral expression of SV40 Large T antigen (LTA). To assess the osteogenic differentiation capacity of the cells in vitro, we studied the alkaline phosphatase (ALP) expression level and calcium deposition in bone morphogenetic protein 9 (BMP9)‐induced immortalized cells using ALP staining and quantification, as well as alizarin red staining. Ectopic bone formation by the cells was assessed using micro‐computed tomography (μCT) and histological examination.

## Results
The immortalized cell line we established using SV40 LTA, which we termed iRBMSCs, was non‐tumorigenic and maintained long‐term proliferative activity. We further discovered that BMP9 (MOI = 30) effectively induced the osteogenic differentiation capacity of iRBMSCs in vitro, and there was a synergy with GelMA hydrogel in inducing osteogenic differentiation of the iRBMSCs in vivo.

## Conclusion
We confirmed that iRBMSCs are promising as a stable cell line source for bone defect repair engineering.

Keywords: bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells, bone morphogenetic protein 9, GelMA hydrogel, immortalization

## INTRODUCTION
Bone defects are usually caused by trauma, tumors, surgeries, and diseases, which are not only difficult to treat clinically but are also a scientific research problem that urgently needs a solution. , Currently, autologous or allogeneic bone grafting is the “gold standard” for repairing bone defects, but there are problems such as limited bone availability, serious complications, susceptibility of the recipient to infection, and immune reactions. In recent years, bone tissue engineering (BTE) based on mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) has provided new research directions and therapeutic promise for bone defects. 

MSCs are pluripotent stem cells with self‐renewal potential and multispectral differentiation ability. The cells can differentiate into adipocytes, osteoblasts, and chondrocytes, and they can be obtained from various sources, such as bone marrow, adipose tissue, umbilical cord, and dental pulp. Among these, bone marrow–derived MSCs comprise one of the most suitable stem cell populations for bone regeneration. , , The number of MSCs transplanted into the defect site can determine the success of bone regeneration. However, primary MSCs undergo a certain number of divisions that result in stemness changes, making it difficult to obtain sufficient numbers of primary MSCs with good stemness for long‐term use. 

To address the shortcomings of primary bone marrow MSCs, such as high heterogeneity and the limited number of passaged cells, the establishment of immortalized cell lines has shown significant potential for application. , The SV40 virus, first discovered in 1960, consists of structural proteins and antigens (large T and small T) and can achieve cell immortalization by regulating the cell cycle and stabilizing telomerase activity. , The immortalized cell lines established by this method can provide excellent seed cells for basic research and clinical application in tissue engineering.
According to incomplete statistics, there are at least 14 bone morphogenetic proteins (BMPs) in the human body, and the osteogenic activity of the BMP family has been confirmed in basic research as well as in clinical applications. Among the BMP family, BMP9 is considered to be the most effective member in inducing osteogenic differentiation of MSCs. In the treatment of bone defects, bioscaffolds can provide a piggyback platform for seed cells and osteogenic inducing factors, thereby achieving rapid repair of bone tissue at the defect site. GelMA hydrogel is a crosslinked network formed by hydrophilic polymers that forms a three‐dimensional structure similar to extracellular matrix (ECM) after free radical photoinitiated polymerization, which has become one of the most promising candidates for tissue regeneration engineering. 

The aims of this study were to establish immortalized rabbit bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells (iRBMSCs) in combination with the osteogenic inducing factor BMP9 and the biomaterial GelMA hydrogel to construct a bone tissue repair material, explore its bone differentiation function both in vivo and in vitro, and assess its application in clinical bone repair.

## MATERIALS AND METHODS
2.1.

## Animals
This study was approved by the Experimental Animal Management and Use Committee of Chongqing Medical University. General‐grade New Zealand rabbits weighing 2.0 ± 0.2 kg were purchased from Chongqing Medical University Laboratory Animal Center. Then, 8‐week‐old male athymic nude mice (SPF) weighing 20.0 ± 2 g were purchased from Beijing Viton Lever Laboratory Animal Co. Ltd., housed in the barrier facility of Chongqing Medical University Laboratory Animal Center, and fed ad libitum with regular chow. The conditions included 12 h alternating light and dark (fluorescent lighting), a temperature of 22–25°C, and relative humidity of 30%–70%.

2.2.

## Reagents and instruments
Dulbecco modified Eagle medium (DMEM) and α‐MEM (minimum essential medium) were purchased from Gibco (Massachusetts, USA); trypsin–EDTA, percoll cell isolation solution, alkaline phosphatase staining and quantitative kit, alizarin red S staining kit, Alcian blue staining kit, and MTT kit were all purchased from Solarbio (Beijing, China); osteogenic induction medium, lipogenic induction medium, and chondrogenic induction medium were all purchased from Procell (Wuhan, China). FITC‐labeled CD90, APC‐labeled CD105, and PE‐labeled CD34 and CD45 were purchased from Abcam (Cambridge, UK). GelMA hydrogel was purchased from Avatarget (Jiangsu, China); Ad‐GFP, Ad‐BMP9, Ad‐Fluc, SSR#41, and pCL‐Ampho were all derived from the Cancer Laboratory of the University of Chicago. The main instruments used were carbon dioxide (CO2) incubator (Heal Force, China), inverted fluorescence microscope (Leica, Germany), flow cytometry (Beckman, USA), micro‐CT scanner (Scanco medical, USA).

2.3.

## Cell culture
The HEK‐293 cell line was obtained from University of Chicago Oncology Laboratory. Unless otherwise stated, cells in this study were cultured in complete DMEM containing 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Vivacell, China) with 1% penicillin/streptomycin and maintained at 37°C in 5% CO2.

2.4.

## Isolation, culture, and characterization of RBMSCs

Primary RBMSCs were isolated from the femoral bone marrow of New Zealand rabbits (2 ± 0.2 kg, both males and females). The cells were cultured in complete MEM and identified using flow cytometry. See Figures  and for details.

2.5.

## Establishment of immortalized RBMSCs

We established iRBMSCs with the retroviral vector SSR#41. Briefly, SSR#41 and pCL‐Ampho plasmids were co‐transfected into HEK‐293 cells to produce a retrovirus with SV40 LTA expression. Viral fluids were collected and used to infect primary RBMSCs. Then, the cells were screened with hygromycin B (at a final concentration of 5 μg/mL), yielding the immortalized RBMSC cell pool designated as iRBMSCs.

2.6.

## Determination of tumorigenic risk of iRBMSCs

The tumorigenic potential of iRBMSCs was evaluated using bioluminescence imaging with human osteosarcoma cells 143b as a control. See the Figures  and for details.

2.7.

## Growth curve determination of iRBMSCs

Growth curves of iRBMSCs were determined using crystal violet staining and MTT. See the Figures  and for details.

2.8.

## 
RNA isolation, reverse transcription, and quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction
Trizol (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, California) was used to isolate total RNA, which was then reverse transcribed using the cDNA reverse transcription kit (EnzyArtisan, Shanghai, China). See the Figures  and for details (Table  ).
 
2.9.

## In vitro osteogenic differentiation of iRBMSCs

The RBMSCs and iRBMSCs were seeded at a density of 1.5 × 105 cells/well in 24‐well cell culture plates and infected with Ad‐GFP and Ad‐BMP9. See the Figures  and for details.

2.10.

## Subcutaneous iRBMSC implantation, ectopic bone formation, and micro‐computed tomography
RBMSCs or iRBMSCs infected with Ad‐GFP or Ad‐BMP9 for 24 h were wrapped in 50 μL of 20% GelMA hydrogel , UV‐cured for 1 min, and implanted into athymic nude mice (nu/nu) (106 cells per injection, four sites per mouse, five mice per group). After 8 weeks of implantation, the animals were killed. The implantation sites were removed for micro‐CT imaging and analysis.

2.11.

## Histologic evaluation
The retrieved ectopic bone samples were fixed, decalcified, dehydrated, and paraffin embedded before staining with H&E and Masson stains.

2.12.

## Statistical analysis
All quantitative assays were performed in triplicate. Data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism 8.0.2 software. One‐way analysis of variance (ANOVA) and t‐tests were used to analyze independent samples as well as between‐group differences. All quantitative data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

3.

## RESULTS
3.1.

## Isolation, culture, and characterization of primary RBMSCs

We isolated primary bone marrow MSCs from the femoral bone marrow cavity of New Zealand rabbits (RBMSCs). RBMSCs had a long shuttle shape with a swirling arrangement of growth and grew well for up to 8–10 passages; however, the cells became slow with a chaotic arrangement after 18 passages (Figure  ). RBMSCs from passage 5 were used for subsequent studies. Flow cytometry results showed that RBMSCs positively expressed the MSCs markers CD90 and CD105, whereas CD34 and CD45 were negative (Figure  ). RBMSCs of passage 5 were taken for trilineage differentiation induction, comprising osteoblastic, lipogenic, and chondrogenic cells (Figure  ). In summary, these results demonstrated that RBMSCs had MSC‐like characteristics and possessed the ability of multidirectional induced differentiation in vitro.
 
3.2.

## Establishment of iRBMSCs with long‐term proliferative activity
Immortalized cell lines were constructed using the tool plasmid SSR#41, which carries both the SV40 T gene and hygromycin B resistance (Figure  ). The plasmid‐infected cell lines were screened for hygromycin resistance for 3 days, and the surviving cells were the successfully constructed immortalized cells, which were named iRBMSCs and continued to be cultured. Upon microscopic observation, iRBMSC morphologies were found to be similar to RBMSCs, but with the increase in the number of culture generations, iRBMSCs grew more rapidly, whereas RBMSCs showed signs of senescence (Figure  ). To compare the proliferation of the RBMSCs and iRBMSCs, we used an MTT assay (Figure  ) and crystal violet staining, and the results demonstrated that when seeded at a similar cell density, iRBMSCs grew more rapidly than RBMSCs (Figure  ). The quantitative results of crystal violet were consistent with the staining results (Figure  ). As verified using RT‐qPCR, the established iRBMSCs overexpressed SV40 T and the positive expression of MSC surface markers CD73, CD90, CD146, and CD166 was significantly upregulated (Figure  ). Previous studies have shown that exogenous overexpression of SV40 LTA may increase the risk of tumorigenesis, , and thus we investigated the risk of iRBMSCs causing tumorigenesis in vivo. Stable tagging of iRBMSCs and a positive control cell line, human osteosarcoma cells 143b with luciferase Fluc yielded iRBMSCs‐Fluc and 143b‐Fluc. Two cell lines were implanted subcutaneously in the back of athymic nude mice. A live bioluminescence imaging analyzer detected bioluminescent signals in both groups on Day 2; on Day 14, the signal of the iRBMSCs‐Fluc group had nearly disappeared, whereas the 143b‐Fluc group maintained a stronger signal (Figure  ). In conclusion, these results indicate that iRBMSCs are able to maintain long‐term stable proliferative activity in vitro while not being tumorigenic in vivo.
 
3.3.

## 
BMP9 induces osteogenic differentiation of iRBMSCs in vitro
To test whether iRBMSCs had in vitro osteogenic potential under BMP9 induction, we used ALP staining to assay the expression of the early osteogenic marker ALP. The results showed that BMP9 was capable of inducing early osteogenic differentiation in both RBMSCs and iRBMSCs, with a certain dose‐dependence of BMP9, and a more significant effect at a multiplicity of adenovirus infection (MOI) of 30 (Figure  ). Alizarin red staining further indicated that BMP9‐stimulated RBMSCs and iRBMSCs were in an advanced stage of mineralization (Figure  ). Next, we examined the expression levels of the osteoblast transcription factors Runx2, Ocn, and Opn in Ad‐BMP9‐infected iRBMSCs after different days of infection using RT‐qPCR. iRBMSCs were infected for 3 days with a significant increase in the expression of Opn, and a slight increase in the expression of Runx2 and Ocn. After 7 days, the expression of Opn and Runx2 decreased slightly, whereas the expression of Ocn increased significantly (Figure  ). The results indicate that BMP9 can induce osteogenic differentiation in iRBMSCs in vitro.
 
3.4.

## 
BMP9 and GelMA hydrogels synergistically induce ectopic bone formation from iRBMSCs in vivo
To find the optimal concentration of GelMA hydrogel scaffolds that could be used for subsequent bone defect repair in vivo, iRBMSCs were encapsulated into different concentrations of GelMA hydrogels. It was observed that the 5% (w/v) and 10% (w/v) GelMA hydrogel structures showed different degrees of degradation and deformation at about 14 days. However, the morphological structure of 20% (w/v) GelMA hydrogel under the same culture conditions remained intact (Figure  ). Therefore, 50 μL of 20% GelMA hydrogel was used to encapsulate iRBMSCs (5 × 106) and irradiated by UV light (365 nm) to form a hemispherical 3D shape for subsequent studies. To detect the proliferation and viability of iRBMSCs in GelMA hydrogels, we used MTT (Figure  ) and calcein/PI cell activity and cytotoxicity assays. Shown in Figure  iRBMSCs maintained high proliferation and high viability throughout the 7‐day culture period. Next, the complex formed by the BMP9‐induced iRBMSCS was wrapped with 20% GelMA hydrogel to observe the osteogenic differentiation ability in vitro. ALP and alizarin red staining showed that iRBMSCs encapsulated in the GelMA hydrogel maintained the ability of osteogenic differentiation (Figure  ). These results suggested that 20% (w/v) GelMA hydrogel can synergize with BMP9 to effectively induce osteogenic differentiation of iRBMSCs and can be applied as a scaffold in subsequent studies in vivo.
 RBMSCs and iRBMSCs both infected with Bmp9 (MOI = 30) (cell density of 5 × 106) were resuspended in 50 μL of 20% (w/v) liquid GelMA hydrogel, respectively, and cured under UV light at a wavelength of 365 nm for 1 min, and then made into cylinders with a diameter of 3 mm and a height of 5 mm (Figure  ). We tested the in vivo effects of BMP9 synergized with GelMA hydrogel on ectopic bone formation in iRBMSCs by implanting a complex made of cells, osteogenic inducer BMP9, and GelMA hydrogel subcutaneously into the subcutaneous tissue back of athymic nude mice for 8 weeks (Figure  ). μCT images were acquired after 8 weeks for evaluation of ectopic bone formation in vivo. The results indicated that bone clumps were present in all groups and were especially pronounced in the iRBMSCs group under BMP9 induction, and much smaller clumps were collected in the GFP‐induced groups (Figure  ).
 The μCT analysis showed that the new born bone volume fraction (BV/TV), the number of trabeculae (Tb.N), and the thickness of trabeculae (Tb.Th) were higher in the BMP9‐induced groups than in the GFP‐induced groups, whereas the trabecular separation (Tb.Sp) was lower than that of the other groups (p < 0.05). Interestingly, compared to the bony tissue formed by the RBMSC groups, bony tissue formed by iRBMSC groups exhibited significantly increased BV/TV, Tb.N, and Tb.Th, and decreased Tb.Sp. The results confirmed that the bone reconstruction process was superior to the bone resorption process in the BMP9‐induced iRBMSC group, which promoted the formation of ectopic bone (Figure  ). H&E staining showed that BMP9‐induced iRBMSCs formed more pronounced heterotopic bone, compared to the other groups (Figure  ). Masson trichrome staining further confirmed that BMP9‐induced iRBMSCs formed a significantly mature and mineralized bone matrix (Figure  ). Overall, these results strongly indicated that the synergistic effect of BMP9 and GelMA hydrogel enhances osteogenic differentiation of iRBMSCs.

4.

## DISCUSSION
Bone defects due to disease, accidents, and aging make it difficult to restore the morphological and functional integrity of the bone by self‐healing alone, and they often require clinical intervention to achieve bone regeneration. The use of bone tissue engineering employing scaffolds, seed cells, and bioactive molecules has provided new directions for the treatment of bone defects with unique advantages over current bone grafting techniques. Bone marrow–derived MSCs have superior osteogenic capacity compared to numerous sources of MSCs and are often used as seed cells for tissue engineering in bone regeneration studies. , , Among the animal models of bone defects, the rabbit defect model is widely used in studies related to bone regeneration, and its seed cells are commonly derived from bone marrow MSCs of human or murine origin, with few literature reports on the application of rabbit MSCs of the same origin in it. Meanwhile, previous research has shown that rabbit bone marrow MSCs can have higher levels of osteogenic and chondrogenic gene expression than human MSCs. Here, we developed immortalized iRBMSCs and analyzed their potential in osteogenic differentiation in vivo and in vitro.
Due to the relatively small proportion of BMSCs in the bone marrow, if we wish to harvest sufficient stem cells, we need to culture and proliferate them in vitro, and the primary cells are not able to proliferate stably and maintain good stemness during the cultivation process. To obtain a number of cells for functional research in vivo and ex vivo, the establishment of an immortalized cell line is the key link to breakthroughs concerning cellular senescence. , , Here, primary RBMSCs were isolated from the femoral bone marrow of New Zealand rabbits and identified according to the viewpoints put forward by the International Society for Cellular Therapy. The iRBMSCs were obtained by immortalizing primary cells using the immortalization plasmid SSR#41. The ability of iRBMSCs to maintain proliferative activity in vitro for a long and stable period of time without being non‐carcinogenic is in line with previous literature reports. , , The iRBMSCs can be used for subsequent functional studies and provide a stable source of cell lines for tissue engineering of bone defects.
In bone tissue engineering, healing of bone defects depends on the interaction of seed cells with other components, and the in vivo biological efficacy of “bone substitutes” is unclear, thus limiting the successful application of cell therapy–based tissue engineering techniques in regenerative medicine. Numerous studies have shown that BMP9 (a.k.a. GDF2) can effectively induce bone and cartilage, and it is one of the least characterized but most osteogenic BMPs, being an effective biokine in bone regeneration engineering. , , To better understand the osteogenic differentiation potential of iRBMSCs, we introduced the osteogenic inducer BMP9 in this study to investigate the osteogenic differentiation potential of iRBMSCs induced by BMP9 in vitro and in vivo. We found that BMP9 could effectively enhance the osteogenic capacity of iRBMSCs, as evidenced by the enhancement of ALP activity and nodule deposition in vitro. In addition, the expression of the osteogenic differentiation genes Runx2, Ocn, and Opn in the BMP9‐induced iRBMSCs significantly increased. The results suggest that Ad‐BMP9 stimulation of osteogenic differentiation of iRBMSCs is an effective strategy for osteogenic regeneration.
Scaffolds provide an extracellular matrix (ECM)‐like microenvironment for cells and osteogenic factors to improve the viability of transplanted cells while providing some mechanical support, thereby allowing the seed cells to grow and migrate to the bone defect site and perform their functions. GelMA hydrogel is widely used in bone regeneration tissue engineering due to its biological and physical properties, and it served as a scaffold for our subsequent in vivo studies.
An ectopic osteogenesis model was made by subcutaneously implanting GelMA hydrogel‐coated BMP9‐induced iRBMSCs in the back of athymic nude mice. μCT scan analysis showed that BMP9 effectively promotes ectopic bone generation in iRBMSCs, as reflected by an increase in BV/TV, Tb.N, and Tb.Th, as well as a decrease in Tb.Sp in ectopic bone specimens. H&E staining was used to evaluate bone maturation, with a higher proportion of red staining indicating higher maturation of the bone scab during bone formation, whereas mature bone tissue was stained blue in Masson staining. The results showed that the piggyback iRBMSC group formed more mature bone trabeculae and new bone tissue. In conclusion, iRBMSCs, together with Bmp9 and GelMA hydrogel, can be used as a “bone graft” to repair bone defects, and the established immortalized cell line is also expected to expand the existing treatment methods for bone defects.
Rabbits are widely used for antibody preparation because of the difficulty in identifying rabbit‐derived cells and the high affinity and specificity of rabbit‐derived antibodies. At the same time, few antibodies have been prepared against rabbit antigens, and fewer assays can be applied, hindering the identification of surface‐specific antigens in rabbit‐derived cells. Compared to humans and mice, the lag in genomic research in rabbits has resulted in few reports of cell‐specific genes of rabbit origin, and our study is expected to fill this gap. Meanwhile, to avoid the evaluation of endogenous osteoblasts on the differentiation ability of target cells, a nude mouse heterotopic osteoblastic model was selected. Therefore, the ability of iRBMSCs to repair in situ bone defects needs to be further explored, as well as whether their osteogenesis depends on implanted seed cells or recruited endogenous osteoblasts.

5.

## CONCLUSION
The aim of this study was to provide MSCs with stable and standardized performance for bone repair tissue engineering research and application. In this study, we demonstrated that primary RBMSCs could be immortalized by SV40 T antigen, named iRBMSCs, that exhibited high proliferative activity and maintained long‐term cell proliferation under ex vivo culture conditions. We further showed that the iRBMSC osteogenic ability was enhanced by BMP9 stimulation and GelMA hydrogel both in vitro and in vivo. These results revealed that our established stable cell line, iRBMSCs, may serve as an important seed cell resource for studies of bone defect engineering.
