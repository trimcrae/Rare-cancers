## Background
Alcoholic hepatitis (AH) is an inflammatory disease associated with high mortality. Mesenchymal stromal cells (MSCs) are promising immune regulators in modulating acute or chronic inflammation. However, the specific mechanism mediating the persistent inflammatory response during AH and therapeutic potential of MSCs remains unclear.

## Methods
We performed MSCs infusion on an animal model of acute-on-chronic alcohol-induced liver injury. Inducible depletion of regulatory T cells (Treg) was achieved using Foxp3-DTR/EGFP transgenic mice. MSCs and anti-CXCL1 treatments were performed on Treg-depleted mice to further elucidate the mechanisms of Treg-mediated neutrophil infiltration.

## Results
MSCs therapy ameliorated liver damage, and elevated the proportion of Treg by secreting cellular retinoic acid binding protein 2 (CRABP2). Treg deficiency exacerbated hepatic neutrophil infiltration upon alcohol stimulation, with elevated serum TNFα and the subsequent hepatocellular CXCL1 expression. Hepatic neutrophil infiltration in Treg-depleted mice cannot be reduced by MSCs or anti-CXCL1 treatment. Consistently, AH patients had decreased peripheral Treg proportion, with the activation of hepatic and peripheral CXCL1-neutrophil inflammatory axis.

## Conclusions
Treg are critical protective regulators during acute alcohol-induced liver injury. MSCs-derived CRABP2 increases Treg proportion to ameliorate alcohol-induced liver injury through regulating CXCL1/neutrophil axis, appearing as a promising strategy for limiting the persistent inflammatory response during severe AH.

## Background
According to the report of the World Health Organization released in 2018, harmful use of alcohol is responsible for approximately 3 million deaths, accounting for 5.3% of all deaths globally [ ]. In fact, more than a quarter of global deaths from cirrhosis and other chronic liver diseases is due to alcohol use (334.9/1256.9 thousands) [ ]. Alcoholic liver disease (ALD) refers to a spectrum of liver damage including AH, steatohepatitis, steatosis, fibrosis, and cirrhosis with its complications. Among those, AH can occur as a distinct acute clinical entity or acute-on-chronic disease within alcoholic cirrhosis, with a high short-term mortality rate ranging from 20 to 50% [ ]. Despite the high mortality rate, current pharmacotherapy options for AH are still limited. For severe AH patients, corticosteroids are only recommended to treat steroid responders without contraindications [ ]. Although corticosteroids improve 28-day short term survival, its therapeutic effects disappear over 6 months [ ]. In addition, current therapeutics for those steroid non-responder or patients with contraindications are quite limited. Hence, there is an urgent need to develop more effective therapeutic strategies to prevent development and improve long term outcome of severe AH.
Innate immunity plays crucial roles in the pathological process of ALD [ ]. Neutrophil infiltration is a prominent feature of inflammatory response and associated with the severity of ALD [ , ], and has been recognized as a hallmark of the activated innate immunity. Mechanistically, activation of neutrophil recruitment promotes oxidative stress and inflammation to exacerbate hepatocyte damage in ALD [ ]. Although impacts of neutrophil infiltration on alcohol-induced liver damage are fairly identified, the specific cell types that trigger the neutrophil chemotaxis and their roles on ALD progression still remain largely unknown. Since the unresolved neutrophil recruitment in severe AH can exist consistently even with alcohol cessation and prophylactic antibiotics [ ], it is plausible that specific immune components continuously modulate intrahepatic inflammatory response during its progression. Thus, it will be important to determine roles of these immune components to develop new therapies for severe human AH.
MSCs-based therapy has emerged as a novel therapeutic approach to treat liver diseases [ ]. First discovered and isolated from the bone marrow in 1966 [ ], MSCs have demonstrated abilities in immune regulation for decades. Considering MSCs interact with both innate and adaptive immunity components with low immunogenicity [ ], it provides the possibility of applying MSCs therapy on limiting acute or chronic inflammatory response beyond autoimmune diseases. With preliminarily confirmed safety and effectiveness, 1309 MSCs-based clinical trails ranging from phase I to phase IV has been registered in ClinicalTrials.gov until June 5th, 2025. A systematic review and meta-analysis included a total of 336 patients suggested that MSCs infusion could be an acceptable treatment for patients with steroid-refractory acute graft-versus-host disease [ ], indicating the potential effects of MSCs on controlling the inflammatory response in steroid non-responders. Besides, several clinical trails had demonstrated the therapeutic effects of MSCs on alcoholic cirrhosis [ , ], and we have previously described the regulatory effects of MSCs on immune cells in a mouse model of experimental immune-mediated liver injury [ ]. Thus, we hypothesize that MSCs may modulate the behaviors of certain immune cells to limit the persistent inflammatory response during alcohol-induced liver injury.
In this study, we investigated the impact of MSCs on hepatic and peripheral immune cells, as well as whether they played functional roles during the alcohol-induced inflammation. We found that MSCs promoted the expansion of Treg through CRABP2, which subsequently controlled the CXCL1-neutrophil inflammatory axis during the intrahepatic inflammatory response. MSCs-mediated regulation of this inflammatory axis contributed to maintain hepatic homeostasis during alcohol-induced liver injury, indicating that MSCs therapy may act as a promising approach for the treatment of severe AH.

## Methods

## Patients
This study conforms to the ethical guidelines of the Declaration of Helsinki. The use of human samples has been approved by the Ethics Committee from the third affiliated hospital of Sun Yat-sen university with written informed consent [2020] 02–032-04.

## Human liver samples
Unstained paraffin-embedded liver tissues from the explanted livers of patients diagnosed with hepatic hemangioma (NC, n = 5) or AH (n = 5) were used for pathological evaluation. Clinical characteristics are shown in Supplementary Table 1.

## Human serum and PBMC samples
Serum and PBMC were obtained from NC (patients diagnosed with polyp of colon or esophageal achalasia with normal liver function, n = 7) or patients diagnosed with AH (n = 8). Clinical characteristics are shown in Supplementary Table 2.

## Animals
All animals received human care in accordance with institutional guidelines. No exclusion criteria were set beyond the institutional guidelines. Animal experiments were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee from the third affiliated hospital of Sun Yat-sen university.

## Mice
6–8-week-old female C57BL/6 wild-type (WT) or C57BL/6-Transgenic Foxp3-DTR/EGFP (Tg-Foxp3-DTR/EGFP) 23.2Spar/Mmjax mice were used and randomly assigned for animal experiments in this study. Mouse strains were housed and maintained in cages of the third affiliated hospital of Sun Yat-sen university following institutional guidelines.

## The modified NIAAA model (the Gao-binge model)
Mice were randomly assigned and modeled using 4% Lieber-DeCarli ethanol (vol/vol) or control liquid diet as previously described [ ]. After a 5-day acclimatization period, mice were maintained on an ethanol-containing liquid diet for 10 days, followed by an additional single dose of ethanol (5 g/kg, 31.5% vol/vol) or isocaloric maltose solution (9 g/kg, 45% wt/vol) 9 h before experimental endpoint. Liquid intake is comparable among different groups.

## Cell therapy
Each mouse received 1 × 106 MSCs resuspended in 150 μl PBS or an equal volume of PBS via tail vein injection on day 10 and 15 of the modified NIAAA modeling.

## DT treatment
C57BL/6 WT or Tg (Foxp3-DTR/EGFP) mice received 800 ng intraperitoneal doses of DT injection every four days to enable inducible Treg depletion.

## Isotype/anti-CXCL1 therapy
Foxp3-DTR/EGFP transgenic mice were treated with Isotype or anti-CXCL1 antibody intraperitoneally (20 μg/mouse/every other day) after Treg depletion. A single dose (50 μg/mouse) of antibodies was given 1 h before oral gavage.

## TGF-βR-blockage/anti-IL-10 therapy
Mice were treated with PBS or TGF-βR inhibitor (1 mg/mouse/3 times per week), and isotype or anti-IL-10 antibody (20 μg/mouse/every other day) intraperitoneally from day 4 of the acclimatization period to day 16 of NIAAA modeling.

## Mass spectrometry-based proteomic analysis
The culture supernatants of MSCs stimulated with or without 50 mM alcohol were collected after 72 h of culturing. Protein was condensed with ultrafiltration membranes of 3 kDa, and then analyzed by data-independent acquisition proteomics after abundant protein depletion (n = 3/group). Differentially expressed proteins were identified using the criteria of fold change > 2 and P value < 0.05.

## RNA sequencing (RNA-Seq)

## RNA seq I
Liver tissues of WT mice underwent control diet, ethanol-fed (EtOH) diet and EtOH diet with MSCs treatment were prepared for RNA-seq (n = 3/group).

## RNA seq II
Liver tissues of Treg-sufficient (WT + DT) and Treg-depleted (Foxp3-DTR/EGFP transgenic + DT) mice underwent EtOH diet were prepared for RNA-seq (n = 3/group).
Total RNA was extracted by TRIzol and fragmented to enable reverse transcription and amplification. cDNA library was sequenced using Illumina Novaseq6000. Clean reads were mapped to the reference genome and FPKM (Fragments Per Kilobase Million) values were calculated to quantify its expression abundance and variation. DEGs were identified using the criteria of fold change > 2 and false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.05 by DESeq2. Further analysis including gene ontology enrichment analysis, pathway enrichment analysis is performed based on these identified DEGs.

## Histology
Liver tissues of human or mouse were fixed with formalin and embedded with paraffin for section preparation. Further histopathological staining and analysis were performed after deparaffinization and rehydration.

## Evaluation of liver damage
Hematoxylin–eosin (HE) -stained slides were scored for liver damage by experienced pathologists, according to a previously described scoring system [ ]. An overall pathological score was determined by summing the scores for liver steatosis, inflammation and cell injury [ ].

## Immunohistochemistry
Slides were stained with rabbit CXCL1 antibody (1:200), rabbit myeloperoxidase antibody (1:100) or recombinant rabbit Ly6G antibody (1:2000) after antigen retrieval and blocking. Horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjunct secondary antibody was applied, followed by 3,3′-Diaminobenzidine (DAB) and hematoxylin staining.

## Quantification of immunohistochemistry
Raw histology images were collected by ECLIPSE Ni-U and analyzed by Image-Pro Plus 6.0 after background calibration. Positive signals were determined by fixed parameters. Mean density of integral optical density (MOD) of each field was calculated as integral optical density (IOD) sum of positive regions, divided by area sum of total regions. Five high-power fields that were randomly selected from the peri-vessel region of each sample were used for data analysis.

## Immunofluorescence
Slides were stained with CXCL1 polyclonal antibody (1:100), myeloperoxidase mouse monoclonal antibody (1:500), arginase-1(D4F3M) antibody (1:100) or FoxP3 (D6O8R) rabbit monoclonal antibody (1:400) after antigen retrieval, permeabilization and blocking. Rabbit-/mouse-origin secondary antibody was applied followed by DAPI staining.

## Preparation and characterization of human MSCs
Human umbilical cords were obtained from healthy donors under the ethical approval of the Third Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat-sen University. MSCs were isolated and cultured according to the a previously reported procedure and with modifications following quality control [ ]. In brief, we use serum-free medium to optimize the culture environment. Membranal CD14, CD29, CD34, CD44, CD45, CD73, CD90, CD105, CD166 and HLA-DR were used to identify the MSCs phenotype through flow cytometry. Differential potentials of lipogenesis and osteogenesis were verified using oil red O and alizarin red S staining. MSCs at passage 3 to 5 were used for the cell therapy.

## MSCs tracking
MSCs were pre-labeled with chloromethylbenzamido dye in prior to infusion therapy according to manufacturer instructions. Liver tissues obtained from mice underwent unlabeled or labeled MSCs treatments were harvested, frozen, embedded and sliced for fluorescence detection. LSM 880 confocal laser scanning microscope was used for data collection.

## Real-time quantitative PCR
RNA extraction was conducted on primary hepatocytes or hepatic non-parenchymal cells from Treg-sufficient (WT + DT) and Treg-depleted (Foxp3-DTR/EGFP transgenic + DT) mice underwent EtOH diet after the two-step collagenase perfusion. After the process of reverse transcription, real-time quantitative PCR was performed using SYBR green assays on LightCycler 480.

## Functional assays

## Cytometric bead array
Mouse serum samples were separated from peripheral blood after centrifugation. Prepared mouse serum samples were incubated with capture beads and detection reagent according to manufacturer instructions. Serum levels of TNF-α, IFN-γ, IL-6, IL10 and IL-17 were detected by flow cytometry. FCAP Array Software 3.0 was used for data analysis.

## AST and ALT assay
Prepared mouse serum samples were analyzed by an automatic biochemistry analyzer according to manufacturer instructions.

## Human serum CXCL1 ELISA
Serum samples obtained from normal controls and patients with AH were separated from peripheral blood. Two technical replicates for each sample were detected using Epoch Synergy H1 according to manufacturer instructions.

## Isolation of murine primary hepatocytes and hepatic non-parenchymal cells
Murine primary hepatocytes were isolated by a two-step collagenase perfusion technique as previously reported with modifications [ , ]. Hepatic non-parenchymal cells that remained in the supernatant were pelleted at 600 g × 10 min after hepatocyte removal (with additional centrifugation at 80 g × 3 min, 3 times), and washed with PBS for subsequent qRT-PCR analysis. For FACS-based immune cell phenotype analysis, hepatic non-parenchymal cells were further purified by 40%/70% Percoll gradient centrifugation and cells in the interface were collected for flow cytometry [ ].

## TNF-α treatment
Primary murine hepatocytes were embedded in 6-well rat tail collagen-coated plate at 1 × 106 per 2 mL culture medium. The culture medium was replaced after cell adhesion and the hepatocytes were treated with 200 ng/mL TNF-α for 9 h.

## Flow cytometry
Human PBMC, mouse splenic or hepatic non-parenchymal cells were prepared as single-cell suspensions. After blocking with CD16/32, cells were incubated with viability dye in PBS. For surface staining, cells were washed and stained with fluorophore-conjugated antibodies in PBS containing 1% fetal bovine serum. For intracellular staining, cells were fixed and permeabilized before incubation with fluorophore-conjugated antibodies. LSR II was used for data collection. Flowjo v10.8.1 was used for data analysis.

## Isolation of human PBMC
Human PBMC were isolated by density gradient centrifugation as previously described with modifications [ ]. Whole blood was anticoagulated with ethylenediamine tetraacetic acid (EDTA) and added to tubes loaded with Ficoll-Paque. PBMC were isolated by Ficoll gradient centrifugation and the PBMC-enriched fraction was collected and washed with PBS containing 1% fetal bovine serum. Red blood cells were removed and the enriched PBMC were counted and plated for FACS analysis or in vitro experiments.

## MSCs and PBMC co-culture

## In vitro Treg induction
CD4 + T cells were sorted magnetically from isolated human PBMC according to manufacturer instructions. MSCs cultured to passage 3–5 were detached and cell suspension was prepared for co-cultured with sorted CD4 + T cells. CD4 + T cells and MSCs were seeded in a 5:1 ratio, and were co-cultured for 72 h in RPMI-1640 containing 10% heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum and 5 ng/mL human FGF-basic, with or without the stimulation of 0.5 μg/ml anti-CD3/CD28 antibody or 50 mM EtOH. For Treg induction procedure, CD4 + T cells were cultured with or without the stimulation of 10 μg/mL human recombinant CRABP2. For intracellular CRABP2 detection, cell activation cocktail was added 6 h prior to sample collection according to manufacturer instructions followed by permeabilization and anti-CRABP2 antibody incubation (1:100). Cell suspension was harvested and filtered for the following FACS procedure.

## In vitro effector T cells assay
MSCs cultured to passage 3–5 were detached and cell suspension was prepared for co-cultured with human PBMC. PBMC and MSCs were seeded in a 5:1 ratio, and were co-cultured for 48 h in RPMI-1640 containing 10% heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum and 5 ng/mL human FGF-basic, with or without the stimulation of 500 ng/mL or 50 mM EtOH. Cell activation cocktail was added 6 h prior to sample collection according to manufacturer instructions. Cell suspension was harvested and filtered for the following FACS procedure.

## In vitro macrophage assay
Human macrophages were derived from PBMC. Briefly, PBMC were plated at 1 × 10⁷ cells in RPMI-1640 medium with 1% FBS, and incubated for 3–4 h to allow monocyte adhesion. The adherent cells were subsequently cultured in RPMI-1640 containing 10% heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum and 20 ng/mL recombinant M-CSF for 5 days to drive macrophage differentiation. Prior to co-culture, MSCs were seeded into 4 μm pore transwell inserts and cultured overnight. These MSC-loaded inserts were then transferred to wells containing the differentiated macrophages, and the entire system was activated with 1 μg/mL LPS and 20 ng/mL IFN-γ for 48 h. Control macrophages were treated in the absence of MSCs. Cells were treated with a Cell Activation Cocktail (with Brefeldin A) for 6 h prior to harvest. Intracellular cytokine staining for TNF-α and IFN-γ was performed and analyzed by flow cytometry.

## Hepatocyte and Treg co-culture
The THLE-2 hepatocyte cell line was plated in 24-well plates at 2 × 105 cells/well. To isolate Treg, human CD4 + T cells were sorted based on the phenotype CD4 + CD25 + CD127 (low/-), following an established protocol [ ]. Treg were then co-cultured with THLE-2 at a 1:5 ratio. The culture medium was supplemented with 50 μM ethanol, which was also added to THLE-2 monoculture system, serving as the control. After a 48-h incubation, floating cells were discarded, and the adherent THLE-2 cells were washed twice with PBS. Total RNA was then isolated from the THLE-2 cells, and expression level of TNF-α were assessed by qRT-PCR.

## Gene overexpression by lentiviral transduction
CD4⁺ T cells overexpressing human CRABP2 (NM_001878.4) was established via lentiviral transduction. HEK293 cells were used for lentiviral vector production and titration. CD4 + T cells (1 × 106 cells per well) were pre-sorted magnetically and then transduced with CRABP2-encoding lentivirus at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 4 in the presence of 5 μg/mL polybrene. At 24 h post-transduction, CD4 + T cells were treated with 1 μg/mL puromycin and cultured for an additional 72 h. CRABP2 expression level and the proportion of Treg among CD4 + T cells were analyzed by flow cytometry.

## Statistics
Data analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 9. Comparisons among three data sets were analyzed using one-way ANOVA with Tukey's post-hoc test (normality and Brown-Forsythe test, P > 0.05), Brown-Forsythe ANOVA with Dunnett's T3 post-hoc test (normality test, P > 0.05; Brown-Forsythe test, P < 0.05) or Kruskal–Wallis test with Dunn's post-hoc test (normality test, P < 0.05). Comparisons between two data sets were analyzed using unpaired t test or paired t test (normality test, P > 0.05; F test, P > 0.05), unpaired t test with Welch's correction (normality test, P > 0.05; F test, P < 0.05) or Mann Whitney test (normality test, P < 0.05). Data are represented as mean ± SEM.

## Results

## Adoptive transfer of MSCs ameliorates alcohol-induced liver injury
Unresolving inflammatory response is a pathological feature of ALD, especially in severe AH, which may lead to the initiation of inflammatory cytokine cascades and poor prognosis [ ]. MSCs are a group of cells act as immune regulator, showing promising immunomodulatory effects during the pre-clinical research and clinical practice. As for the unresolved inflammatory process aggravated ALD, we hypothesized that MSCs ameliorate alcohol-induced liver injury by regulating specific immune components. To test this, we performed MSCs characterization (Fig. S1A-D) and the following intravenous MSCs infusion on the chronic and binge alcohol feeding model (the NIAAA model, Fig.  A). Confocal microscopy analyses showed MSCs retention in liver and spleen (Fig.  B), indicating the survival and migration of MSCs after transplantation. Additionally, MSCs were enriched in the perivenular regions surrounding the central veins (Fig. S1E), suggesting that the engrafted MCS preferentially localized to regions where liver damage or inflammation was present. To quantify engraftment, we pre-labeled 1 × 106MSCs with the cell membrane fluorescent probe DIL and analyzed hepatic non-parenchymal cells (NPC) 9 h after gavage (Fig. S1F). Our results suggest that 1.04 ± 0.05% of NPC are DIL + (Fig. S1G), showing the engraftment rate of MSCs. Compared to mice without treatment, mice underwent MSCs infusion showed histological improvement (Fig.  C, Fig. S1H). Since the presence of neutrophil infiltration is a crucial pathological feature of ALD, we evaluated the expression levels of myeloperoxidase (MPO), which was considered as a marker of neutrophil activation, and found that its expression was significantly upregulated in mice upon alcohol stimulation and subsequently downregulated by MSCs therapy (Fig.  D-E). Consistently, we also observed decreases in serum aspartate transferase (AST) and alanine aminotransferase (ALT) levels after cell therapy (Fig.  F). 
We performed protein expression profiling on the culture supernatant of MSCs upon alcohol stimulation. A total of 11 differentially expressed proteins were identified (Fig. S1I), indicating the potential contributors responsible for MSCs-mediated modulation in response to alcohol. Concurrently, to identify the therapeutic mechanisms of MSCs, we performed a genome-wide RNA-seq analysis of liver tissues from pair-fed, ethanol (EtOH) -fed, and MSCs treatment group (MSC). Principal component analysis showed distinct transcriptomes among the three populations (Fig.  G). In transcriptomics, pairwise analyses revealed the Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) of pair-fed vs. EtOH and EtOH vs. MSC, as shown in the volcano plot (Fig.  H). Among those, 279 DEGs were common to the intersections from the pairwise comparisons (Fig.  I), indicating the potential mechanisms responsible for therapeutic effects of MSCs. Thus, we matched these overlapping DEGs with the pathological processes associated with alcohol-induced liver injury, including inflammatory response, lipid metabolic and reaction to ROS and oxidation according to their gene ontology (GO) terms (Fig.  J). DEGs involved in these processes were displayed and their alterations after MSCs therapy were observed. To determine the therapeutic targets of MSCs upon alcohol stimulation, reactome pathway enrichment analysis and Kyoto encyclopedia of genes and genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analysis were performed (Fig.  K, Fig. S2A-B), demonstrating that these overlapped DEGs were mostly enriched in immune system. Taken together, these results suggest the alterations of hepatic immune environment and the regulatory effects of MSCs on immune system during alcohol-induced liver injury.

## Adoptive transfer of MSCs promotes hepatic and peripheral treg differentiation
Considering the transcriptional signature of liver tissues, we hypothesized that MSCs modulate specific immune components to make an impact on the progression of alcohol-induced liver injury. To test this, we screened liver infiltrating and peripheral immune cells that might be functionally relevant to alcohol-induced liver injury, including cell components of innate and adaptive immunity following the adoptive transfer of MSCs (Fig.  A). Pre-gated on alive hepatic CD45 + immune cells, total CD4 + T cell proportion of EtOH group was increased compared with pair-fed group, and decreased after MSCs infusion (Fig.  B). In addition, as the prominent features of liver inflammation, hepatic infiltrating Ly6G + neutrophil proportion was also induced in response to EtOH modeling and reduced after the adoptive transfer of MSCs. These results indicate the synergistic activation and MSCs-mediated regulations of adaptive and innate immunity during alcohol-induced inflammatory response. 
To further investigate the specific targets of MSCs, we analyzed the intrahepatic Treg and Ly6Clo macrophage proportions due to their natural roles in maintaining hepatic immune homeostasis. We found no significant difference in intrahepatic Treg frequency after alcohol exposure. Interestingly, a preferentially increase of intrahepatic Treg proportion was observed after the MSCs treatment, while the proportion of CD4 + T cell was decreased. Given that inducible Treg are mainly differentiated from naïve T cells [ ], which present as CD4 + CD25-cells [ , ], we analyzed the hepatic naïve T cell population to determine the precursors of Treg. Indeed, the proportion of naïve T cells decreased after MSCs therapy (Fig.  B), suggesting that MSCs promote the differentiation of naïve T cells into Treg. We next analyzed the changes in anti-inflammatory macrophages. Two functionally distinct subsets of CD11b + F4/80 + macrophages were differentiated based on the expression level of Ly6C. While Ly6Chi macrophage showed pro-inflammatory effect, Ly6Clo macrophage were generally considered as anti-inflammatory [ ]. However, proportions of Ly6Clo and Ly6Chi macrophages were not significantly different after MSCs infusion, suggesting that anti-inflammatory macrophage induction or macrophage phenotype transition might not be the major therapeutic mechanisms of MSCs-mediated amelioration of alcohol-induced liver injury. The proportion of CD3 + T cells, CD8 + T cells and total macrophage did not change significantly after MSCs treatment (Fig.  C).
Spleen is the largest peripheral lymphoid organ. Splenic immune cell proportions reveal changes of the immune environment of peripheral pool. Therefore, we performed peripheral phenotype analyses of splenic immune cells (Fig.  D). Consistent with the immune cell proportions observed in the liver, peripheral CD4 + T cells were increased in the EtOH group, suggesting the activation of the systemic adaptive immune system (Fig.  D, Fig. S3A). This activation was inhibited by MSCs as the proportion of CD4 + T cells decreased after MSCs transplantation. However, although trend towards an increased peripheral neutrophils proportion was observed, peripheral neutrophils did not change significantly after EtOH modeling (Fig.  D), indicating that the current alcohol-induced liver inflammation was mainly confined to the liver and had not yet caused a robust systemic inflammatory response. Interestingly, peripheral Treg proportion was higher compared with intrahepatic Treg in all groups (Fig. S3B-D). A decreased peripheral Treg percentage was observed in response to alcohol, and this alteration can be reversed after MSCs treatment (Fig.  D, Fig. S3B). Correspondingly, the peripheral Naïve T cells proportion in MSCs treatment group was lower than that in EtOH group (Fig.  D, Fig. S3C), suggesting the effect of MSCs-mediated Treg differentiation also exists in the peripheral Treg pool. We further analyzed Helios expression, a functional indicator of Treg function, in hepatic Treg proportion from PBS- and MSC-treated mice (Fig. S3E). Our data indicated that MSC treatment modulated both Helios + and Helios- Treg subsets. Specifically, MSC infusion promotes the local expansion of Treg within the injured liver, involving increases in both Helios + and Helios- populations. To identify the spatial relationship between MSCs and Treg, we pre-labeled MSCs with the DIL and analyzed the localization of MSCs (Dil +) and Treg (Foxp3 +) (Fig. S3F). Foxp3 + cells located around Dil-MSCs were observed with the exceedingly low abundance. Another population of immunomodulatory cells, Ly6Clo macrophages, was not significantly different among the three groups. Other peripheral immune cell populations, including Ly6Chi macrophages, CD3 + T lymphocytes, CD8 + T lymphocytes, and total macrophages, did not show any significant difference after MSCs treatment. Collectively, these data demonstrate that alcohol exposure leads to the intrahepatic and systemic activation of adaptive immunity, and a significantly decrease of peripheral Treg frequency. MSCs promote the differentiation of hepatic and peripheral Naïve T cells into Treg, and help maintain immune homeostasis during the alcohol-induced liver injury.

## Treg deficiency exacerbates alcohol-induced liver injury
Since alcohol consumption made a distinct impact on peripheral Treg and triggered hepatic neutrophil infiltration, we next tested the hypothesis that Treg play a significant role in modulating hepatic neutrophil behaviors during alcohol-induced liver injury. Utilizing transgenic mice with DT receptor following a Foxp3 promoter, we were able to establish the chronic and binge alcohol feeding model with controllable Treg depletion (Fig.  A, upper). After verifying the efficiency of Treg depletion (Fig.  A, lower), we first determined whether Treg depletion cause liver damage in pair-fed liver. Foxp3-DTR/EGFP transgenic mice and its WT littermates were all injected with DT during 16-day control liquid diet. There was no significant difference in histological damage between Treg-sufficient and Treg-depleted mice in pair-fed group (Fig.  B-C), indicating that the absence of Treg in the uninjured liver did not cause significant liver structural damage. We next established a EtOH feeding model in Treg-depleted and Treg-sufficient mice. In this model, Treg-depleted mice showed a more severe liver structural damage and immune cell infiltration compared with Treg-sufficient mice (Fig.  B-C). Serum AST and ALT levels also indicated that Treg depletion led to aggravated liver damage after EtOH consumption, while no significant difference was observed between Treg-sufficient and Treg-depleted mice in pair-fed group (Fig.  D). 
Considering the roles of Treg in maintaining immune tolerance and homeostasis, the association between neutrophil infiltration and severity of ALD, and the preliminary histological findings of aggravated inflammatory infiltration in mice with Treg deficiency after EtOH intervention, we hypothesized that Treg depletion might exacerbate alcohol-induced liver damage by affecting neutrophil infiltration. To test this, neutrophil infiltration of peri-vessel region was assessed based on MPO and Ly6G immunostaining in pair-fed and EtOH group, respectively (Fig.  E). Consistent with our hypothesis, Treg-depleted mice fed with EtOH diet showed more severe neutrophil infiltration in EtOH group relative to Treg-sufficient mice (Fig.  F), and these differences were not observed in mice with control diet (Fig.  E-F). In accordance with these results, flow cytometric quantification of hepatic infiltrating neutrophil also revealed that Treg-depleted mice fed with EtOH exhibited notably increased neutrophils accumulation, while no significant difference was observed between the Treg-sufficient and Treg-depleted mice fed with control diet (Fig.  G). Taken together, Treg depletion results in severe intrahepatic neutrophil infiltration in mice fed with EtOH diet instead of control diet, suggesting that the neutrophil recruitment mediated by Treg depletion occurs in the presence of alcohol-induced inflammatory response.

## MSCs ameliorate alcohol-induced liver injury through Treg-CXCL1 axis
To determine whether MSCs-mediated anti-inflammatory immunity is dependent upon the presence of Treg, therapeutic effects of MSCs on Treg-depleted mice were tested after EtOH modeling (Fig.  A). Treg depletion was performed prior to MSCs or PBS treatment, and no significant difference in liver structural damage were observed (Fig.  B, Fig. S4A). Consistently, MSCs treatment in the absence of Treg failed to improve the liver function, as measured by AST and ALT levels (Fig.  C). The intrahepatic neutrophil infiltration in Treg-depleted mice received MSCs transplantation did not alleviate relative to mice without MSCs transplantation (Fig.  D-G). These results demonstrate that Treg are required to support MSCs-based regulate of hepatic inflammation during alcohol-induced liver injury. 
Given that the severity of neutrophil infiltration predicts the prognosis of clinical ALD [ , ], and our previous findings have suggested that Treg play significant roles in alleviating intrahepatic neutrophil infiltration, we next set to elucidate the mechanisms responsible for Treg-mediated regulation on neutrophil recruitment in alcohol-induced liver injury. RNA sequencing of liver tissues from Treg-sufficient and Treg-depleted mice after EtOH modeling was performed, and a total of 109 DEGs were identified. Top 50 DEGs with the highest absolute value of fold changes and a landscape of gene expression alterations were displayed (Fig.  H-I). Considering the therapeutic effect of MSCs is dependent upon the presence of Treg (Fig.  A-F), and the inhibitory effect of Treg on neutrophil recruitment (Fig.  ), it is plausible that MSCs ameliorate neutrophil recruitment through Treg induction. Thus, to screen out the specific mechanisms by which Treg regulate neutrophil recruitment during alcohol-induced liver injury, we merged the 109 DEGs from Treg-sufficient vs. Treg-depleted group with the previous 279 DEGs that had been overlapped in pair-fed vs. EtOH and EtOH vs. MSC group. 13 DEGs were identified at the intersection of the 3 gene sets (Fig.  J). Surprisingly, CXCL1 ranked as the most striking DEG among those gene sets, suggesting that CXCL1 might act as a key component in the control of Treg-neutrophil inflammatory axis. Additionally, to identify the relationship between MSC and CXCL1, we pre-labeled MSCs with the DIL and analyzed the localization of MSCs (Dil +) and CXCL1 + area (Fig. S4B), we found that Dil-MSCs mostly located nearby CXCL1 + area.
CXCL1 plays critical roles in angiogenesis, cancer development, neutrophils migration and infiltration [ ]. To further verify the functional pathways enriched in the absence of Treg during alcohol-induced liver injury, and the role of CXCL1 in these pathways, we performed a gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) of DEGs between Treg-sufficient vs. Treg-depleted group according to GO terms. CXCL1 was significantly enriched in 11 pathways in the GO database (Fig.  K, Fig. S4C). Notably, more than 50% of those pathways (6 out of 11) were directly related to neutrophil behavior, and all suggested neutrophil activation in the absence of Treg. Together, these results suggest that Treg modulate CXCL1 expression to control neutrophil recruitment during alcohol-induced liver injury.

## Treg modulate hepatocellular CXCL1 expression during alcohol-induced liver injury
We next investigated how Treg regulate hepatic CXCL1 expression and its functional target. To do so, hepatic expression levels of CXCL1 among mice underwent different interventions were analyzed (Fig.  A). Consistent with the pathological features, hepatic CXCL1 was upregulated over tenfold after alcohol consumption and downregulated after MSCs treatment in the presence of Treg (Fig.  A, upper, middle), verifying the pivotal role of CXCL1 in inflammatory pathway during alcohol-induced liver injury. In addition, hepatic CXCL1 level in the absence of Treg exhibited the highest positive fold change of expression compared with Treg-sufficient mice (Fig.  A, lower). In accordance with the transcriptional profile, protein level of CXCL1 were also significantly elevated in the absence of Treg (Fig.  B-C). These results confirm the pro-inflammatory role of CXCL1 and the impact of Treg on inhibiting hepatic CXCL1 expression in response to alcohol. 
To determine the major cellular sources of the elevated hepatic CXCL1, we isolated primary hepatocytes and hepatic non-parenchymal cells to quantify their CXCL1 expression from Treg-sufficient and Treg-depleted mice after alcohol exposure. Notably, primary hepatocytes were the predominant source of elevated CXCL1 (Fig.  D), while no significant difference but a slightly increased tendency of CXCL1 was observed in hepatic non-parenchymal cells from the comparison between Treg-sufficient and Treg-depleted mice (Fig.  E). Since CXCL1 expresses on both primary hepatocytes and hepatic non-parenchymal cells [ – ], we compared their CXCL1 levels in the absence of Treg to identify the predominant origin of total hepatic CXCL1 expression. Our data demonstrated that higher expression level of CXCL1 was observed in primary hepatocytes relative to hepatic non-parenchymal cells (Fig.  F). Collectively, these results suggest that Treg deficiency preferentially facilitates the hepatocyte-origin expression of CXCL1, which plays a dominant role in the CXCL1-mediated intrahepatic neutrophil recruitment after alcohol exposure.
We next elucidated the specific mechanisms responsible for Treg suppression on hepatocyte CXCL1. It has been previously described that Treg deficiency in ALD mice developed a more severe phenotype characterized by hyper-inflammatory responses (Fig.  ), indicating an intense activation of the intrahepatic immunity. Pro-inflammatory cytokines secreted from hepatic non-parenchymal cells during the hyper-inflammatory state could result in life-threatening inflammatory reactions. Therefore, we hypothesized that Treg suppress hepatocyte CXCL1 expression by regulating the secretion of pro-inflammatory or anti-inflammatory cytokines. To test this, we screened cytokines from a series of potential regulators of CXCL1 [ , – ]. The expression of these cytokines in hepatic non-parenchymal cells in the absence of Treg were quantified with real-time quantitative PCR (qRT-PCR). Indeed, there were pronounced increases in TNF-α and IFN-γ expression, while no significant alterations were observed in IL-6, IL-10 and IL-17 (Fig.  G). We next investigated the serum protein levels of these cytokines. Consistently, a markedly elevation of serum TNF-α and an increasing trend of serum IFN-γ were observed in Treg-depleted mice (Fig.  H). To directly test whether Treg suppress TNF-α production by hepatocytes, we sorted CD4 + CD25 + CD127- Treg and co-cultured them for 48 h with ethanol-treated THLE-2 cells. We observed a significantly reduced level of TNF-α expression in THLE-2 cells co-cultured with Treg compared with Treg-free controls (Fig. S4D), supporting that Treg inhibit hepatocyte TNF-α expression. To determine the effect of TNF-α on CXCL1, uninjured primary hepatocytes were purified and treated with TNF-α. After 9 h, CXCL1 expressed in primary hepatocyte was significantly induced upon TNF-α stimulation, as measured by qRT-PCR (Fig.  I). These outcomes support the hypothesis that during alcohol-induced liver injury, Treg inhibit the secretion of TNF-α from hepatic non-parenchymal cells to suppress hepatocyte-origin expression of CXCL1 and the subsequent neutrophilic inflammation.

## Neutralization of the CXCL1-neutrophil axis ameliorates alcohol-induced liver injury in the absence of Treg
To test whether CXCL1 is the dominating mediator of neutrophil recruitment in the absence of Treg, Treg deficient mice were treated with either isotype control or anti-CXCL1 during the EtOH intervention (Fig.  A). In contrast to isotype treatment, where Treg deficiency aggravated liver damage, we found that mice exhibited phenotypic improvement (Fig.  B-D) and a distinct reduction in hepatic inflammatory burden upon CXCL1 blockade (Fig.  E-H). Therefore, we next identified that if CXCL1-neutrophil axis could act as a potential therapeutic target during severe hepatic inflammation in response to alcohol. Since it has been demonstrated that hepatocellular CXCL1 increased in response to TNF-α stimulation (Fig.  I), serum levels of cytokine TNF-α, IFN-γ, IL-6, IL-10 and IL-17 from Treg deficient mice were accessed by CBA after isotype or anti-CXCL1 treatment. However, no statistically differences were observed in any of these cytokines (Fig.  I). These results suggest that neutrophil recruitment in the absence of Treg is predominantly mediated by CXCL1. However, therapeutic approach targeting CXCL1-neutrophil axis does not effectively rescue the hepatocellular damage stimulated by TNF-α, although the CXCL1 blockade significantly reduce the hepatic neutrophil infiltration. 

## MSCs-derived CRABP2 promotes Treg differentiation
We next verified whether MSCs-mediated differentiation towards Treg can be induced upon alcohol stimulation on human PBMC. CD4 + T cells were pre-sorted and then co-cultured with MSCs in the presence of EtOH (Fig.  A) after validating the feasibility of Treg culture system (Fig. S5A). After 72 h, we found that MSCs promoted human Treg differentiation in vitro (Fig.  B-C). Intriguingly, similar effect was observed in CD4 + T cells co-cultured with MSCs upon alcohol stimulation (Fig.  B-C), suggesting the therapeutic potential of MSCs in promoting human Treg differentiation after alcohol exposure. To further investigate the mechanisms responsible for MSCs-mediated Treg induction, we performed pairwise analyses of differentially expressed proteins in the culture supernatant of MSCs upon alcohol stimulation (Fig. S1I, Fig.  D), and discovered a promising candidate Cellular Retinoic Acid-Binding Protein 2 (CRABP2), a molecule that transports retinoic acid into the nucleus. Since it had been reported that retinoic acid played a crucial role in Treg induction [ ], CRABP2 appeared to be the potential contributor of MSCs-mediated Treg induction. To test this, pre-sorted CD4 + T cells were transduced with lentiviral particles to express the human CRABP2, following by an increased expression level of CRABP2 compared with control (Fig. S5B). Consistent with our hypothesis, CD4 + T cells transduced with CRABP2 exhibited a higher proportion of Treg after 72 h of culture (Fig.  E). In addition, we stimulated the pre-sorted CD4+T cells with or without human recombinant CRABP2. After 72h, the proportion of Treg stimulated with CRABP2 was higher than Treg cultured without CRABP2 (Fig. S5C). Simultaneously, treatment with recombinant CRABP2 protein led to a significant increase in the proportion of CRABP2 and Foxp3 double-positive cells among CD4+ T cells (Fig. S5D). These results indicated that CRABP2 played a critical role in MSC-mediated Treg induction. We next examined the uptake of exogenous recombinant CRABP2 using an in vitro assay. We added recombinant human CRABP2 to the Treg culture system, harvested the suspension cells and discarded the supernatant to detect the cellular CRABP2 protein levels under both permeabilized (Fig. S5E) and non-permeabilized (Fig. S5F) conditions. Our results show the CRABP2 levels under both conditions (Fig. S5E-F) are significantly higher in groups with recombinant antibody treatment, indicating the potential for CRABP2 uptake by CD4 + T cells. Since TGF-β and IL-10 are recognized as effective regulatory molecules that promote the upregulation of Treg proportions, we quantified the levels of TGF-β and IL-10 by quantitative ELISA in MSC culture supernatants under basal and ethanol-stimulated conditions. However, the concentration of these cytokines was below the limit of detection under the current assay conditions. To elucidate whether TGF-β and IL-10 play a predominant role in MSC-induced Treg upregulation during alcoholic liver injury, we performed in-vivo TGF-βR blockage and IL-10 neutralization experiment (Fig. S5G). Consistently, our results indicated that blocking TGF-β and IL-10 neutralization did not significantly reduce the proportion of CD25 + Foxp3 + Tregs in MSC-treated group (Fig. S5H), suggesting that MSC-induced Treg upregulation involves the participation of molecules beyond TGF-β and IL-10.

 

It has been reported that cytokines mediated the inflammatory response in human AH [ ]. Consistently, we have previously observed that dense neutrophil infiltration in Treg deficient mice occurred with the elevated serum TNF-α, and an increasing trend of serum IFN-γ (Fig.  ), which were recognized as pro-inflammatory mediators in human AH [ , ]. In animal model, MSCs interacted with hepatic and peripheral adaptive immunity, modulating CD4 + T cells and Treg during alcohol-induced liver injury (Fig.  ). Thus, we next investigated the roles of MSCs on human adaptive immunity upon alcohol stimulation. Given that CD4 + and CD8 + T cells are dominating sources of TNF-α and IFN-γ during adaptive immune response [ , ], we hypothesized that MSCs suppress effector T cells, including TNF-α-/IFN-γ-producing effector CD4 + T cells, and TNF-α-/IFN-γ-producing CD8 + cytotoxic T cells, to control TNF-α and IFN-γ production in response to alcohol. After alcohol exposure, the frequencies of TNF-α-/IFN-γ-producing effector CD4 + T cells and TNF-α-/IFN-γ-producing CD8 + cytotoxic T cells were all significantly decreased in PBMC co-cultured with MSCs relative to PBMC that cultured alone (Fig.  F, Fig. S5I-K). As chronic alcohol abuse leads to lipopolysaccharide (LPS) exposure, we treated human PBMC with LPS in our in vitro co-culture model. Similarly, TNF-α-/IFN-γ-producing T cells decreased significantly, or showed a decreasing trend (TNF-α-producing effector CD4 + T cells, P = 0.24) in PBMC co-cultured with MSCs upon LPS stimulation (Fig.  F, Fig. S5I-K). Similarly, in the co-culture system of MSCs and macrophages, MSCs significantly reduced the population of TNF-α-/IFN-γ producing macrophages (Fig. S5L). These findings suggest that MSCs can target and modulate multiple types of inflammatory cells.

## AH patients exhibited a reduced peripheral Treg proportion and activation of the CXCL1–Neutrophil axis
Patients with AH present with acute hepatic inflammation, which is histologically characterized by hepatocellular injury and neutrophil infiltration (Fig.  G, upper). Previous studies have demonstrated that the mRNA expression level of CXCL1 was higher in patients with AH compared to healthy subjects [ , ]. Thus, we analyzed the protein levels of hepatic MPO and CXCL1 to identify whether CXCL1-neutrophil axis is activated during human AH. MPO and CXCL1 expression were quantified by immunohistochemistry (Fig.  G-H). Dual immunofluorescence staining on human liver sections showed CXCL1 + signal was predominantly detected in hepatocytes near the necrotic foci, where MPO + neutrophils formed dense clusters within the same areas (Fig.  I), suggesting the activation of CXCL1-neutrophil axis during AH. In addition, a systemic activation of CXCL1-neutrophil axis was also observed, as measured by serum CXCL1 level and peripheral neutrophil population in AH patients (Fig.  J-K). Next, we sought to determine if patients with AH exhibit impaired Treg proportion. Intriguingly, consistent with the reduced peripheral Treg that has been observed in mice with acute-on-chronic alcoholic liver injury, AH patients also presented with decreased peripheral Treg proportion (Fig.  L), revealing the potential impact of Treg on maintaining immune homeostasis during the alcohol-induced acute inflammation. These results suggest that the reduction of Treg proportion and activation of CXCL1-neutrophil axis occur in human AH, and MSCs promoted Treg differentiation, as well as inhibited inflammatory cytokine production of effector T cells upon alcohol stimulation (Fig.  ). 

## Discussion
Numerous preclinical studies and clinical trails of immune disorders employing MSCs-based therapy have revealed its multifaceted functions in immune modulation [ ]. Focused on the alterations in transcriptional profiles and immune cell phenotypes, our data demonstrated that adaptive immunity was also activated during ALD in addition to innate immunity, where MSCs acted as immune regulators by control of both. Our findings revealed the multifaceted regulatory activities of MSCs, and highlighted the functional relationship between MSCs and Treg, as well as Treg and CXCL1-neutrophil axis during alcohol-induced liver injury.
The altered immune cell populations and cytokine phenotypes have been observed in animal model and human ALD [ ]. The activation of inflammatory cells and their corresponding pathways in ALD are well characterized [ ]. However, changes of Treg proportion in ALD still remain controversial [ – ]. Seemingly inconsistent, although several studies have reported the increased peripheral Treg proportion during ALD [ – ], we observed a decreased peripheral Treg proportion after alcohol intervention, which was also observed in patients with AH. However, the increased percentage of Treg in ALD were observed in mice underwent chronic ethanol feeding [ , – ]. In contrast, Treg frequency is declined in mice with acute-on-chronic alcohol-induced liver injury and patients with AH [ , ]. Thus, these results are actually compatible, since it is plausible that immune system acts differently while in different status from the spectrum of ALD. Taken together, it reveals the possibility that Treg as a critical regulator to maintain homeostasis during the acute phase of alcohol-induced inflammatory response. Interestingly, our data adds to these findings and indicates the unchanged intrahepatic Treg population after alcohol intervention. Considering the Treg frequency in normal liver is at a low baseline relative to peripheral pool, with an average of 0.81% versus 8.64% (Fig. S3D, left), it is rational that the declined frequency of Treg caused by alcohol consumption was less pronounced in the liver compared to the periphery. The specific alterations and functions of immune components in liver and periphery require further investigations due to their different manifestations within the spectrum of ALD and their potential as therapeutic targets.
While prior studies have established that MSCs can induce Tregs via secreted factors like TGF-β and IL-10 [ ], the concentration of these cytokines were below the limit of detection under the current assay conditions. Our proteomic analysis identified a total of 11 differentially expressed proteins. Of these, we discovered a promising candidate CRABP2. Recent study has identified that retinoic acid and its receptor play crucial roles in the induction of Treg [ ]. Since CRABP2 facilitates the transport of retinoic acid into the nucleus, it may serve as a significant contributor to MSCs-mediated Treg induction. Our study corroborated the role of CRABP2 in Treg induction and refined the immunoregulatory mechanisms of MSCs.
It is notable that Treg play unique roles in organ-specific manner and modulate different target cells through multiple mechanisms in addition to their direct immunosuppressive properties. In a murine model of epidermal barrier regeneration, Treg regulated CXCL5 expression from interfollicular epidermal cells to modulate epidermal regeneration [ ]. Besides, in mice with opioid withdrawal syndrome, fragile-like Treg mediated synaptic instability in nucleus accumbens neurons [ ]. Our results revealed that Treg are required to control the expression of hepatocellular CXCL1 and subsequent hepatic neutrophil accumulation, adding to a growing body of evidence to demonstrate the functional heterogeneity of Treg in different tissues.
Being recognized as a neutrophil chemoattractant, CXCL1 mediates neutrophil recruitment during tissue inflammation [ ]. Several published RNA-seq data sets have demonstrated the elevated expression of hepatic CXCL1 during human AH [ , ], with one study highlighting the specific upregulation of hepatic CXCL1 rather than others, including patients with non-alcoholic fatty liver disease and hepatitis C virus infection [ ]. Consistently, our transcriptome-based datasets identify increased hepatic CXCL1 expression in the NIAAA model, with elevated protein level observed in mice and patients with AH, and we highlight that Treg control of hepatocellular CXCL1 expression modulates the inflammatory response during ALD. For spatial interrelationship between MSC and Treg, although we observed Foxp3 + cells located around Dil-MSC, the precise mapping of their spatial interrelationship posed a major challenge, primarily because of the exceedingly low abundance of hepatic infiltrating Treg. There is a pressing need for more advanced technologies to further elucidate the comprehensive and precise spatial relationship between them. It has been reported that blockade of CXCL1 ameliorated liver injury and hepatic neutrophil infiltration in mice with ALD [ ], and our results support the protective role of anti-CXCL1 treatment in inhibiting neutrophil chemotaxis. However, CXCL1 blockade fails to reduce the production of TNF-α and other proinflammatory cytokines in the absence of Treg during ALD, indicating that the potential stimulators of hepatocellular CXCL1 exist in the hepatic microenvironment after CXCL1 blockade. As TNF-α participates in the pathogenesis of human AH and rodent models of ALD [ ], it may be more appropriate to target Treg or TNF-α as therapeutic candidates rather than CXCL1.
Considering the critical role for Treg in maintaining immune homeostasis during the acute inflammation in response to alcohol, therapeutic approaches to increase Treg are promising strategies to limit the inflammatory response during severe AH. As a direct way to enlarge Treg pool, adoptive transfer of Treg has been applied to treat several immune disorders. However, as the effects of immune components are less well defined in the pathogenic mechanisms of AH, it is difficult to determine how to set up the appropriate immuno-receptors responsible for AH, or how to perform an autologous Treg expansion within the therapeutic window during the acute inflammation. Alternatively, pharmacologic therapeutic approaches targeting TNF-α have been tested in clinical trials for the treatment of AH. However, as higher mortality rate [ ] or increased risk of infection [ ] were observed, neutralizing TNF-α with etanercept or infliximab did not show promising results. These outcomes may be due to the impact of TNF-α gene polymorphism, efficacy of neutralizing antibodies, and additional pathogenic effects mediated by other inflammatory cytokines during AH. To date, MSCs have emerged as a potential therapeutic candidate for limiting the persistent inflammatory response during AH due to their Off-the-Shelf usage and potent immunoregulatory properties. Previous studies and our results have shown that MSCs regulate a variety of immune cells and their cytokine production to limit the inflammatory process [ ]. Notably, several clinical trials have demonstrated the increased frequencies of Treg and reduced serum levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines during MSCs-mediated immune modulation in acute immune disorders [ , ]. Taken together, these phenotypes may suggest MSCs therapy as a promising cell-based therapeutic approach for treatment of severe AH. Our study identifies and highlights that MSCs ameliorate the inflammatory response upon alcohol stimulation in a Treg dependent manner, and Treg function as a crucial modulator to regulate the TNF-α-mediated activation on CXCL1-neutrophil axis. Although MSCs therapy targeting Treg may be an effective approach in maintaining immune homeostasis during severe AH, efforts towards a more comprehensive concept for AH immunity should be made before it comes into clinical practice.

## Conclusions
MSCs-derived CRABP2 regulates Treg-mediated control of CXCL1-neutrophil axis, appearing as a promising strategy for limiting the persistent inflammatory response during severe AH.
