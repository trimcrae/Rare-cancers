## Purpose
The purpose of this study was to investigate the relationship between primary cilia and mitochondrial function in cultured human corneal endothelial cells (HCECs).

## Methods
HCECs obtained from donor corneas were cultured and categorized into either differentiated cells or cell-state transition (CST) cells based on the expression of CD44, a non-kinase transmembrane glycoprotein. The frequency of primary cilia was then compared between the differentiated and CST cells using immunofluorescence. Expression of ADP-ribosylation factor-like protein 13B (ARL13B) was inhibited with small interfering RNA (siRNA) to assess the effects on key proteins and mitochondrial function following siRNA treatment via reverse-transcription quantitative polymerase chain reaction, immunofluorescence, and Seahorse XFe24 Extracellular Flux Analyzer (Agilent Technologies) findings.

## Results
Compared with the differentiated cells, the CST cells exhibited a higher frequency of primary cilia, a lower cell density, and larger nuclei. ARL13B knockdown significantly reduced alpha-tubulin messenger RNA (mRNA) expression, yet did not affect the frequency of primary cilia. On the other hand, ARL13B knockdown altered the localization of aquaporin-1 (AQP1) to the cell membrane and significantly increased mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) without affecting glycolysis.

## Conclusions
The findings in this study highlight the importance of ARL13B in cultured HCECs and its impact on mitochondrial function and AQP1 localization. Although ARL13B knockdown did not affect the frequency of primary cilia, it significantly enhanced mitochondrial OXPHOS and led to a shift in AQP1 localization, thus suggesting a functional modulation of metabolism in cultured HCECs.

They are dynamic structures that form during the G0 phase and disassemble as cells enter mitosis, with the basal body transitioning into the spindle apparatus. After mitosis, the basal body reforms cilia as cells that return to quiescence. , Ciliary dysfunction, such as defects in ciliary proteins intraflagellar transport 88 (IFT88) or kinase family member 3A (KIF3A), disrupts cell polarity, spindle formation, and cell-cycle progression. Thus, primary cilia and cell division are mutually exclusive and strongly related. Moreover, primary cilia reportedly play crucial roles in regulating signaling pathways, including cell proliferation, motility, and polarity. – 
Corneal endothelial cells (CECs) join to form a single-layer tissue (endothelium) located at the most anterior side of the cornea’s three-layered structure, and play a crucial role in maintaining corneal transparency by draining water from the corneal stroma to the anterior chamber. Primary cilia have been identified in CECs, as well as in the cells of other ocular tissue. In research using confocal microscopy 3D imaging of rabbit corneal endothelium, our findings revealed that primary cilia protrude toward the anterior chamber. In a previous study involving murine CECs, primary cilia were found to be present in newborn mice, yet disappear as those mice matured, and very interestingly, they re-emerged during wound healing. Those findings suggest that primary cilia are involved in the regenerative response and have a potential role in tissue repair. In regard to human CECs (HCECs), primary cilia are rarely seen in the cells located at the central region of the cornea, yet are present in approximately 50% of the cells located in the peripheral region. However, the exact reason why the frequency of primary cilia differs between the central and peripheral regions has yet to be elucidated.
We previously reported that primary cilia are present in the endothelium of the central region of the cornea in patients with bullous keratopathy (BK). As primary cilia are rarely seen in the CECs of the central region of the healthy cornea, there may be some relationship between the appearance of primary cilia and corneal endothelial dysfunction, as observed in ocular disorders such as BK. We also discovered that subpopulations exist in cultured HCECs, and that they can be classified by cell-surface cluster of differentiation 44 (CD44) antigen into two types: differentiated cells with good cell morphology, and cell-state transition (CST) cells with low differentiation and heterogeneity. , In differentiated cells, mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) is upregulated, and, in CST cells, glycolysis is upregulated. Moreover, our findings revealed that the metabolic pattern of differentiated cells corresponds to healthy corneal endothelium, whereas that of CST cells corresponds to endothelial dysfunction such as Fuchs endothelial corneal dystrophy. , Although several previous studies investigated the relationship between primary cilia and mitochondrial function, – there have been few reports on this relationship in CECs. We hypothesized that primary cilia expression is induced by corneal endothelial dysfunction and linked to mitochondrial function in CECs.
Hence, we herein investigated the relationship between primary cilia and mitochondrial function using messenger RNA (mRNA) expression and immunofluorescence in cultured HCECs, and used RNA interference of proteins related to primary cilia to elucidate the effect of primary cilia on mitochondrial homeostasis in cultured HCECs.

## Methods

## Donor HCECs
The HCEC tissues used in this study originated from human donor corneas provided by CorneaGen (Seattle, WA, USA) Eye Bank, and were managed following the tenets set forth in the Declaration of Helsinki. From those donor corneas, CECs were subsequently obtained and then cultured for experimental purposes. All procedures adhered to the previously reported protocols for tissue procurement, , , which included obtaining informed written consent for research-related eye donation. The list of donors used in this study is shown in the  . All donor corneas were preserved in Optisol-GS (Chiron Vision, Inc., Irvine, CA, USA) storage medium and transported internationally by air for research purposes. According to the accompanying donor profiles, all corneas were deemed healthy with no evidence of corneal disease, and none of the donors had a known history of chromosomal abnormalities.
 

## Reagents
Rho-associated protein kinase (ROCK)-inhibitor Y-27632 and epidermal growth factor were purchased from Wako Pure Chemical Industries, Ltd. (Osaka, Japan), and p38 MAP kinase inhibitor SB203580 was obtained from Cayman Chemical (Ann Arbor, MI, USA). SB431543 (SB4; initially utilized to counteract the adverse effects of transforming growth factor-β in cultures), Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM), OptiMEM I, and fetal bovine serum were obtained from Thermo Fisher Scientific (Waltham, MA, USA). Culture plates were obtained from Corning, Inc. (Corning, NY, USA). Unless otherwise indicated, all other chemicals were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich, Inc. (St. Louis, MO, USA).

## Culture of HCECs
The culture of HCECs was performed according to the previously published protocols, , and differentiated and CST HCECs were established according to the procedures described previously. Briefly, human Descemet's membranes with attached CECs were carefully peeled from the donor corneas and subjected to enzymatic digestion using 1 mg/mL collagenase A (Roche Applied Science, Penzberg, Germany) at 37°C for 2 hours. The isolated HCECs were then seeded into type I collagen-coated six-well plates (Corning), one well per sample. Cell culture was carried out using a medium formulated based on previously described methods. , The HCECs were maintained at 37°C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2, and the medium was replaced twice weekly. Upon reaching confluence, the cultured HCECs were passaged at a density of 800 cells/mm² following 12 minutes of incubation with TrypLE Select Enzyme (10×; Thermo Fisher Scientific) at 37°C. The differentiated CECs elicited a proportion of CD44–/+ of more than 95%, whereas the CST CECs were designated and found to elicit a proportion of less than 70%.

## Reverse Transcription-Quantitative Polymerase Chain Reaction
Total RNA was isolated from the HCECs using the miRNeasy Mini Kit (QIAGEN, Hilden, Germany). The cDNA was synthesized via a high-capacity cDNA reverse transcription kit, including RNase inhibitor (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) was performed using a reaction mixture containing cDNA, gene-specific primers, and THUNDERBIRD SYBR qPCR Mix (TOYOBO Co., Ltd., Osaka, Japan) on an Applied Biosystems QuantStudio 3 real-time PCR system (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The primers used in the experiments are listed in . The expression levels of ADP-ribosylation factor-like protein 13B (ARL13B), tubulin aplha-1A (TUBA1A), Na+/K+ ATPase (ATP1A1), zonula occludens-1 (ZO-1), aquapolin-1 (AQP1), and SLC4A11 were normalized to glyceraldehyde 3-phosphate dehydrogenase levels, and the data were expressed as 2−ΔCt (relative units of expression).

## Immunoblotting
The protein concentration was measured using a Qubit 2.0 Fluorometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Equal amounts of proteins were applied on 4% to 12% sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) and transferred to nitrocellulose membranes using the iBlot 2 Dry Blotting System (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The blocking and antibody reactions were performed using the iBind Western System (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The primary antibodies were ARL13B and TOM20, and the secondary antibodies were goat anti-mouse IgG and goat anti-rabbit IgG (H+L; Southern Biotech, Birmingham, AL, USA). MagicMark XP Western Protein Standard (Thermo Fisher Scientific) was used as molecular weight markers. The protein bands were made visible by Western BLoT HRP substrate series (Takara Bio Inc., Kusatsu, Japan). Luminescence was observed by use of an ImageQuant LAS-3000 (FUJIFILM, Tokyo, Japan) dedicated charge-coupled device camera system. Quantification of ARL13B and TOM20 expression normalized to β-actin was performed using lane profile analysis in the ImageJ image-processing software program.

## Immunofluorescence
HCECs were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde or methanol at room temperature for 10 minutes, permeabilized for 15 minutes with 0.2% Triton X-100 (Sigma-Aldrich), treated with ethanol (50% and 100%), and blocked with 1% bovine serum albumin in phosphate buffered saline (PBS) for 1 hour. Specimens were then incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies against ARL13B (17711-1-AP; Proteintech Group Inc., Rosemont, IL, USA), acetylated alpha-tubulin (T5168; Sigma-Aldrich), Na+/K+ ATPase (ATP1A1, No. 05-369; Merck & Co., Rahway, NJ, USA), ZO-1 (No. 33-9100; Thermo Fisher Scientific), AQP1 (ab168387; Abcam Ltd., Cambridge, UK), and SLC4A11 (HPA018120; Merck). After washing 3 times in PBS, fluorescent-labeled Alexa-Fluor (Thermo Fisher Scientific) secondary antibodies (1:2000 dilution) were applied overnight at 4°C in the dark. The samples were then stained with 4',6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI, VECTASHIELD; Vector Laboratories, Inc., Newark, CA, USA) antifade mounting medium, and, for actin staining, Rhodamine-Phalloidin (1:400; Cytoskeleton, Inc., Denver, CO, USA) was added. The slides were then examined under a confocal laser-scanning microscope (FV1200; Olympus Corporation, Tokyo, Japan). The analysis of cell density and nuclear size was performed using an automated cell counting tool in the ImageJ software based on DAPI staining. Primary cilia were defined as cilia-like structures co-stained with both ARL13B and acetylated alpha-tubulin, and were manually identified.

## Quantification of Membrane and Cytosolic AQP1 Fluorescence Intensity
To quantify the subcellular localization of AQP1 in the CECs, fluorescence images were analyzed using ImageJ software. Images of AQP1-immunostained cells were acquired under identical imaging conditions across all samples. For each image, 10 cells were randomly selected for analysis. For each selected cell, two square regions of interest (ROI) of identical size were manually defined; i.e., one at the cell periphery to represent the plasma membrane, and the other within the cytoplasm, avoiding the nuclear region. The membrane ROI was placed over the ring-like AQP1 signal at the cell border, while the cytoplasmic ROI was placed within the cell body where the signal was diffused. The mean gray value (average fluorescence intensity per pixel) was measured for each ROI, and the membrane-to-cytosol ratio of the mean gray values was then calculated for each cell.

## Flow Cytometry Analysis of HCECs
HCEC samples were harvested from culture dishes using TrypLE Select Enzyme, and prepared for fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS) as previously detailed. , , , Antibodies used for FACS analysis included FITC-conjugated anti-human CD90, PE-conjugated anti-human CD166, PerCP-Cy5.5-conjugated anti-human CD24, PE-Cy7-conjugated anti-human CD44, and APC-conjugated anti-human CD105 (all obtained from BD Biosciences, San Jose, CA, USA, and eBioscience, Inc., San Diego, CA, USA). Following staining and washing with FACS buffer, the cells were analyzed using a FACSCanto II System (BD Biosciences) flow cytometer.

## RNA Interference
Differentiated CECs were transfected with 5 nM Silencer Select siRNA targeting ARL13B (s47283: siARL13B) or a negative control #1 siRNA (siControl; all from Thermo Fisher Scientific) using DharmaFECT Transfection Reagents (Horizon, Cambridge, UK) per the manufacturer’s instructions. Cells were then incubated for 48 hours and fixed for immunofluorescence according to the previously published method. 

## Mitochondrial Respiration Assay
Mitochondrial function in live corneal endothelial cells was evaluated in regard to extracellular flux using a Seahorse XFe24 Analyzer (Agilent Technologies, Inc., Santa Clara, CA, USA). Differentiated CECs were transfected by siRNA above for 24 hours, then seeded in XF24 Analyzer plates and incubated 24 hours, and the Mitochondrial Respiration XF Cell Mito Stress Test (Agilent Technologies) was conducted according to the manufacturer's guidelines. Prior to analysis, the culture medium was replaced with XF DMEM supplemented with 2 mmol/L glutamine, 10 mmol/L glucose, and 1 mmol/L sodium pyruvate (pH 7.4). Oxygen consumption rate (OCR) and extracellular acidification rate (ECAR) were measured under basal conditions and following sequential additions of 1 µM oligomycin, 1 µM carbonyl cyanide-p-trifluoromethoxyphenylhydrazone (FCCP), and 0.5 µM rotenone/antimycin A. Results were normalized to viable cell counts determined using the Cellinsight NXT (Thermo Fisher Scientific) high content screening platform.

## Statistical Analysis
Data were analyzed using Prism software version 10.2.3 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA) statistics software. An unpaired two-tailed Student's t-test or Mann–Whitney U test was applied where appropriate. A P value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

## Results

## Increase of ARL13B-Positive Primary Cilia in CD44-High CST Cells
In our previous study, we reported that primary cilia were present in the corneal endothelium of BK cases, yet absent in regions with normal cellular alignment in keratoconus cases, thus suggesting that primary cilia expression may vary depending on cellular conditions. Hence, in this present study, we first compared the protein levels of ARL13B, the CEC density, the nuclear size, and primary cilia expression between CD44-low-expressing differentiated cells and CD44-high-expressing CST cells. The findings revealed that in the CST cells, the protein level of ARL13B was lower than that in the differentiated cells ( A, B). Moreover, the CEC density was significantly lower (P < 0.0001) and the nuclei were significantly larger (P < 0.0001). Interestingly, the proportion of cells expressing primary cilia was significantly higher in the CST cells (P < 0.0001;  A,  B). This finding aligns with the previous findings in BK cases, where primary cilia were observed more frequently in those cases than in the controls, thus suggesting that primary cilia may be expressed in response to changes in corneal endothelial function.
 

## Confirmation of ARL13B Knockdown
Next, to investigate whether or not the regulation of primary cilia affects cultured HCECs, we used siRNA targeting ARL13B. The findings in previous studies have shown that ARL13B siRNA can reduce the expression of primary cilia, and we previously confirmed ARL13B expression in HCECs. The siARL13B significantly reduced ARL13B mRNA levels, compared to siControl (0.48-fold, P = 0.000156). Furthermore, the expression of TUB1A, a precursor of acetylated alpha-tubulin that constitutes primary cilia, was also significantly decreased (0.54-fold, P = 0.00261;  A). In addition, the findings in a previous study demonstrated that knockout of ARL13B leads to shortening of primary cilia, as visualized by acetylated alpha-tubulin staining, suggesting that ARL13B may play a role in the regulation of TUBA1A gene expression. Next, to confirm ARL13B knockdown at the protein level, cells were harvested 48 hours after siRNA transfection, and Western blot analysis was performed. A marked reduction in ARL13B protein expression was observed, consistent with a successful knockdown ( A, B). However, siRNA treatment did not alter cell density, nuclear size, or the proportion of cells expressing primary cilia (P = 0.889, 0.132, and 0.918; B,  C). Hence, we found that siARL13B altered the mRNA expression of proteins associated with primary cilia, yet did not affect the proportion of primary cilia.
 

## AQP1 Becomes More Membrane Localized Following ARL13B Knockdown
Next, we investigated whether or not ARL13B knockdown affects the core proteins of cultured HCECs. The mRNA expression revealed no differences in the expression of ZO-1, ATP1A1, AQP1, and SLC4A11, compared to siControl (0.90-fold, 0.91-fold, 1.03-fold, and 1.03-fold, P = 0.0889, 0.657, 0.771, and 0.734;  A). However, whereas immunofluorescence of ZO-1, ATP1A1, and SLC4A11 remained unchanged between the control and siARL13B-treated cells, AQP1 exhibited a notable shift in localization toward the cell membrane in siARL13B-treated cells compared with the controls ( B). Quantitative analysis of AQP1 localization using immunofluorescence imaging revealed a significantly higher membrane-to-cytosol fluorescence intensity ratio in siARL13B-transfected cells compared to the siControl (lot no. 220517; percentage of differentiated cells: 69.9%; membrane/cytosol ratio: 2.12 vs. 1.35, P < 0.0001). This increase was also observed in cells derived from different donors, yet the magnitude of the difference tended to diminish as the proportion of differentiated cells increased (lot no. kCG08; percentage of differentiated cells = 88.7%; membrane/cytosol ratio = 2.15 vs. 1.68, P = 0.0056; lot no. ABJ24-01; percentage of differentiated cells = 97.0%; and membrane/cytosol ratio = 2.31 vs. 1.99; P = 0.180; C, C, C). AQP1 plays a key role in water transport from the corneal stroma to the anterior chamber, , thus suggesting that siRNA treatment could potentially enhance corneal endothelial water transport function.
 

## ARL13B Knockdown Does Not Affect Differentiation
Next, we focused our attention on confirming whether or not siARL13B alters the proportion of differentiated cells using flow cytometry. The proportion of differentiated cells defined as CD24-CD44−/+ cells(gate 1) did not differ among the negative control, siControl, and siARL13B treatments (69.9%, 69.8%, and 69.8%, respectively; ). Consistent with the results described above, no differences were observed among the three groups in the additional two donor lots (88.7%, 93.4%, and 90.0% in kCG08, and 97.0%, 95.4%, and 95.4% in ABJ24-01, respectively; ).
 

## Enhanced Mitochondrial OXPHOS in siARL13B CECs
Finally, we investigated whether, or not, siRNA influenced CD44 expression and mitochondrial metabolism in CECs, and assessed mitochondrial respiration in these cells using a flux analyzer. In lot no. 220517, siARL13B significantly increased mitochondrial OXPHOS, as indicated by a rise in the OCR (P = 0.00439, 0.00119, and 0.000383), whereas the ECAR remained unchanged (P = 0.0973, 0.109, and 0.119), compared to siControl ( ). In lot no. kCG08, which had a differentiated cell proportion of 88.7%, siARL13B similarly led to a significant increase in OCR (P = 0.0149, 0.00258, and 0.000721) and a trend toward elevated ECAR (P = 0.0402, 0.0646, and 0.471; ). In contrast, lot no. ABJ24-01, in which 97.0% of cells were differentiated, showed a more modest increase in OCR (P = 0.638, 0.0128, and 0.0856) and a consistent trend toward increased ECAR (P = 0.0716, 0.0202, and 0.0488; ). These findings suggest that the magnitude of ARL13B knockdown-induced changes in mitochondrial OXPHOS was greater in cell populations with a lower proportion of differentiated cells.
 To further evaluate whether ARL13B knockdown affected mitochondrial abundance, we quantified TOM20 expression as a surrogate marker for mitochondrial content. Across all tested cell lots, TOM20 protein levels were consistently reduced in siARL13B-treated cells compared to the siControl (see A, A, A). Those findings suggest that siARL13B enhances corneal endothelial function by altering AQP1 localization and upregulating mitochondrial respiration.

## Discussion
In this study, we investigated the role of primary cilia and ciliary protein ARL13B in HCECs, and found that CST cells exhibited a higher proportion of primary cilia compared with the differentiated cells. ARL13B knockdown via siRNA reduced alpha- tubulin expression and altered the localization of AQP1 to the cell membrane, potentially enhancing water transport. Although primary cilia proportions and other key proteins remained unchanged by ARL13B knockdown, mitochondrial OXPHOS significantly increased after ARL13B knockdown, suggesting improved metabolic function. These findings highlight the potential of targeting ARL13B to enhance CEC function by modulating AQP1 localization and mitochondrial activity ( ).
 Interestingly, the proportion of primary cilia was significantly higher in the CST cells than in the differentiated cells. The metabolic pattern of differentiated cells was previously found to align with that of the normal corneal endothelium, whereas CST cells exhibited metabolic characteristics corresponding to endothelial dysfunction, such as those observed in Fuchs endothelial corneal dystrophy cases. We also previously reported the presence of primary cilia in the CECs of patients with BK. Combining the findings in those reports with the present findings appear to indicate that the primary cilia observed in the CECs of patients with BK are associated with cellular metabolism and are not artifacts.
Primary cilia coordinate various signaling pathways, including Hedgehog, Wnt, and G protein-coupled receptor signaling, to regulate cell proliferation, differentiation, and migration. , Primary cilia are essential for maintaining homeostasis in multiple tissues, including the skin, hair follicles, and adipose tissue. , However, the number of reports on primary cilia in CECs is limited, and their functions remain a subject of debate. In CST cells or CECs affected by BK, primary cilia may emerge as a survival mechanism in response to intracellular metabolic changes, possibly to detect alterations in the extracellular environment. They may also play a role in initiating cell division in response to functional decline or a reduction in cell number. Investigating receptors or ion channels expressed on the primary cilia of CECs and identifying the pathways associated with primary cilia may shed light on their role in BK.
ARL13B is a small GTPase associated with Joubert syndrome, a type of ciliopathy, and plays a critical role in ciliary function and protein transport. ARL13B is also expressed in CECs, yet its role in CECs remains unclear. Although several studies have reported that ARL13B knockdown or knockout leads to the loss of primary cilia, , it did not significantly reduce the proportion of primary cilia in this current study. One possible explanation for that findings is that the 48-hour period following knockdown may have been too short to observe changes in the proportion of primary cilia.
Conversely, ARL13B knockdown did alter the localization of AQP1 and affected mitochondrial respiration. To the best of our knowledge, there have been no previous studies on the relationship among ARL13B, AQP1, and mitochondrial OXPHOS. AQP1 is a water channel protein widely distributed in the kidneys and other tissues, and it is also expressed on the cell membrane of CECs, playing a critical role in water flux. In addition, mitochondrial respiratory capacity is an essential factor for water flux. Thus, the knockdown of ARL13B contributed to the enhancement of water flux in the CECs. Because primary cilia are associated with the cell cycle, , ARL13B knockdown might influence the cell cycle through primary cilia, leading to changes in intracellular metabolism and resulting in AQP1 localization and mitochondrial respiration.
At present, the relationship between ARL13B and mitochondrial OXPHOS in CECs remains unclear. Although ARL13B expression was higher in the differentiated cells, which also exhibited enhanced OXPHOS activity, ARL13B knockdown increased OXPHOS in less differentiated CST cells. This paradox may indicate that ARL13B may not directly suppress mitochondrial OXPHOS, but rather modulate it in a context-dependent manner – possibly through primary cilia-mediated signaling pathways that exert greater influence under metabolically immature or stressed conditions. , Further studies are needed to clarify whether ARL13B modulates mitochondrial OXPHOS via cilia-dependent signaling mechanisms such as Hedgehog or mTORC1 pathways.
Interestingly, an association was observed between the proportion of differentiated cells and the extent of mitochondrial OXPHOS enhancement following ARL13B knockdown. In donor lot no. ABJ24-01, which had a high proportion of differentiated cells (i.e. 97.7%), the increase in mitochondrial OXPHOS was minimal. In contrast, lot no. kCG08 (88.7% differentiated cells) showed a moderate enhancement, and lot no. 220517 (69.9% differentiated cells) exhibited a pronounced increase. These findings suggest that ARL13B knockdown may be more effective in cells with a lower differentiation status, such as CST-like cells. As this observation is based on only three donor-derived HCEC lots, further studies involving a larger number of donors are needed to verify whether this trend holds true.
To assess mitochondrial content under each condition, quantitative analysis of TOM20 protein levels was performed. Compared with differentiated cells, CST cells exhibited reduced TOM20 expression, thus indicating lower mitochondrial abundance. Moreover, ARL13B knockdown consistently led to a decrease in TOM20 protein levels relative to the siControl across all donor-derived cell lots. These results suggest that mitochondrial protein abundance, as represented by TOM20 levels, does not necessarily correlate with respiratory capacity. Despite reduced TOM20 expression following ARL13B knockdown, mitochondrial respiration was paradoxically enhanced, implying a dissociation between mitochondrial mass and function. This may reflect improved mitochondrial quality and efficiency per organelle, potentially mediated by alterations in cilia-dependent signaling pathways.
Metabolism is known to play a significant role in the cell fate decisions of stem cells. , It has also been suggested that in embryonic stem cells (ESCs) and epithelial cells, changes in the balance between mitochondrial OXPHOS and glycolysis, mediated by intracellular pH, are involved in cell fate decisions such as differentiation and tumorigenesis. We also previously reported that intracellular pH affects mitochondrial OXPHOS in CECs, and understanding the relationship among primary cilia, intracellular pH, intracellular metabolism, and the cell cycle in CECs enables functional evaluation of CECs via observing primary cilia by specular microscopy, thus possibly leading to therapeutic approaches targeting primary cilia.
It should be noted that even though the findings in this study highlight the impact of primary cilia on CECs, the study had several limitations. First, the study did not assess the long-term consequences of ARL13B knockdown, which could offer deeper insights into the dynamics of primary cilia and mitochondrial function. Second, we did not investigate the knockdown of other proteins expressed in primary cilia. The number of CEC donors used in this study was limited (n = 3), and further validation using a larger donor cohort would be optimal. Finally, siRNA may introduce off-target effects that could influence the results.
In conclusion, the findings of this study highlight the role of ARL13B in CECs and its impact on mitochondrial function and AQP1 localization. Although ARL13B knockdown did not alter primary cilia frequency, it significantly enhanced mitochondrial OXPHOS and caused a shift in AQP1 localization, suggesting functional modulation of CECs. These findings indicate that ARL13B influences cellular metabolism and water transport, potentially contributing to corneal endothelial function. Further investigation into the mechanisms linking primary cilia, ARL13B, and mitochondrial activity may provide new therapeutic strategies for corneal endothelial dysfunction and enhance our understanding of primary cilia’s role in tissue homeostasis.
