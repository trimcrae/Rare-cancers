## Abstract
Bone formation, metabolism, and the stability of the bone marrow microenvironment are all impacted by the imbalance in the differentiation potential of bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs). Despite this, it is unknown how BMSCs affect congenital scoliosis (CS). As a result, our research now focuses on explaining its associated impact and mechanism. In eukaryotic cells, N6-methyladenosine (m6A) is the most prevalent post-transcriptional alteration. The role of fat mass and obesity-related genes (FTO), an m6A demethylase, in regulating the differentiation of BMSCs is still unknown. We assessed alterations in the mRNA and protein levels of genes linked to the differentiation of BMSCs using samples taken from CS. According to our findings, According to our findings, FTO inhibits osteogenic differentiation and promotes the adipogenic differentiation of BMSCs. MMP1 knockdown has an inhibitory effect on BMSC osteogenic differentiation, whereas MMP1 overexpression promotes it. A specific ERG inhibitor called PD98059 prevents MMP1-mediated promotion. Additionally, our research revealed that FTO affects how BMSCs can differentiate in CS patients by regulating MMP1 levels. FTO-mediated MMP1 m6A modification underlies MMP1’s promotion of osteogenic differentiation via the ERK pathway, implying that it could be a viable treatment target for CS.

## Introduction
Congenital scoliosis (CS) is a three-dimensional spinal deformity in which vertebral bodies are poorly segmented or obstructed during embryonic development, with an incidence of approximately 0.5–1/1000 CS is usually accompanied by congenital heart disease, urinary system deformities, rib emigration, and other deformities, which seriously affect the growth and development of adolescents . Literature reports that many factors cause congenital scoliosis, including genetic factors, environmental factors, vitamin deficiencies, etc – . Although a lot of research has been done on the pathogenesis of CS, the disease mechanism of the majority of CS is still unclear. N6-methyladenosine (m6A) modification is the most common genetic modification in mammalian cells and occurs in the adenosine base at position 6 of the mRNA nitrogen , . Unlike other genetic modifications, m6A modification is a dynamic reversible process that regulates the maturation, translation, and degradation of precursor mRNA – . Studies have shown that m6A methylation is involved in the development of bone-related diseases, such as osteoporosis, osteoarthritis, and bone tumors . m6A RNA methylation plays a crucial role in regulating bone formation and resorption by affecting cytokines, hormones, and signaling pathways , . In recent years, more and more attention has been paid to the new role of m6A modification in stem cell fate determination and differentiation, especially the impact of osteogenic differentiation and its impact on related bone diseases.
Mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs) are a type of readily accessible and abundant progenitor cells that are widely distributed in various tissues, including the umbilical cord, placenta, adipose tissue, and dental pulp. These cells possess remarkable self-renewal capabilities and exhibit multidirectional differentiation potential . Under specific in vitro or in vivo conditions, BMSCs can be induced to differentiate into a variety of specialized cell types, such as osteoblasts, adipocytes, neurons, and chondrocytes. Accumulating evidence from numerous studies has highlighted the significant role of N6-methyladenosine (m6A) modification in regulating the multidirectional differentiation of BMSCs. For example, METTL3 promoted the phosphoinositide 3-kinase (PI3K)-protein kinase B (AKT) signaling pathway, which positively regulated the osteogenic differentiation of BMSCs and dramatically increased the expression of bone formation-related genes (such as RUNX2 and OSX) and mineralized nodule formation . You et al.’s research indicates that WTAP-mediated m6A modification can regulate the differentiation potential of bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells and lead to the occurrence of osteoporosis . Additionally, YTHDF1 regulates BMSCs expansion and aging by suppressing inflammatory pathway activation through its recognition and degradation of m6A-modified transcripts .
Although the osteogenic differentiation ability of BMSCs in bone disease has been widely evidenced , the relevant effect and mechanism of BMSCs on CS remain enigmatic. It is reported that WISP2 knockdown suppressed the osteogenic differentiation of NC-BMSCs, while WISP2 overexpression facilitated the osteogenesis of CS-BMSCs via acting on the Wnt/β-catenin pathway .
In this study, to clarify whether M6A methylation is involved in the occurrence and development of CS patients through BMSCs, we collected BMSC samples from CS patients and compared the differences in m6A methylation with those of normal controls. We searched for genes closely related to vertebral dysplasia through RNA-seq analysis and explored the possible mechanism of their role in the pathogenesis of CS combined with epigenetic abnormalities.

## Materials and methods

## Human sample collection
All patients were treated in our hospital between June 2020 and June 2023. Fifteen CS and 20 control specimens were collected for verification. The inclusion criteria for the CS group and the control group were: children who signed informed consent, completed clinical and imaging data, were first diagnosed with CS in our hospital based on medical history, physical examination, and auxiliary examinations, and had not received any previous treatment. Patients aged 10–18 years with lumbar disc herniation or thoracolumbar fracture were selected as controls. The exclusion criteria for both groups were unknown informed consent, a clear history of maternal medication or toxic substances during pregnancy, incomplete patient data, a history of spinal brace treatment, and the presence of any syndrome.

## Ethics statement
All volunteers signed informed consent forms, and the study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Xiangya Hospital of Central South University (Ethical review number: 2022020575). This study statement confirms that all methods were carried out in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations in the declaration.

## BMSCs sorting and culturing
During general anesthesia, before pedicle screw placement, blood was collected from the intra-screw tract of the diseased vertebra in patients with CS, and blood was collected from the intra-screw tract of the normal vertebra in the control sample to isolate primary BMSCs. The cells were seeded in a 100 mm culture dish and cultured in MEM/F12 medium. When the confluence of BMSCs reached 70–80%, the cells were digested with 0.25% trypsin and subcultured into a new cell culture flask at a ratio of 1:3, and the third generation (P3) BMSCs were used for subsequent experiments.

## Osteogenic differentiation induction and detection
BMSCs at passage 3 (5 × 103 cells/cm2) were seeded into 6-well plates and cultured to 70–80% confluence, and then the complete medium was replaced with an osteogenic differentiation medium (Cyagen Biosciences, China). Alkaline phosphatase activity testing was performed using the Alkaline Phosphatase Activity Test Kit (Beyotime, China). At the same time, osteogenic differentiation ability was detected by alkaline phosphatase (ALP), and calcium deposits were detected by Alizarin RedS (ARS) using the osteogenic assay kit (Cyagen Biosciences, China).

## Adipogenic differentiation induction and detection
BMSCs at passage three were seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 5 × 104 cells/well, cultured to 100% confluence, and cultured in an adipogenic differentiation medium (Cyagen Biosciences, China) for 3 weeks. BMSCs were stained with OilRedO staining kit (Beyotime, China), and the staining conditions were observed.

## Chondrogenic differentiation induction and detection
BMSCs (2 × 104 cells/well) were seeded into a 12-well cell culture plate. 1 ml of BMSCs complete medium was added to each well, and the cells were cultured until the confluence reached 100%. Then, the medium was replaced with BMSCs chondrogenic differentiation medium (Cyagen Biosciences, China), and the medium was changed every three days for four weeks. Finally, it was stained with the AlcianBlue staining kit (Beyotime, China) and observed under a light microscope.

## Flow cytometry
BMSCs were identified using flow cytometry. BMSCs at passage three was resuspended in a flow cytometry buffer, and the cell concentration was adjusted to an adjusted cell concentration of 3 × 106 cells/mL. Subsequently, the surface markers of cultured cells, including CD105, CD73, CD166, CD44, CD45, CD11b, CD34, and CD14, were analyzed using a human BMSCs surface marker detection kit.

## Lentivirus/siRNA transfection
Lentivirus transfection [negative control (Vector), FTO overexpressing lentivirus (OE-FTO), and MMP1 overexpressing lentivirus (OE-MMP1) were performed at a cell density of 30–50%. The medium was changed 24 h after transfection and every 3 days until the cells reached 80–90% confluence. Seventy-two hours after transfection, stably transfected BMSCs were screened with 2 μg/mL puromycin. siRNA (RIBOBIO, China) was used for gene knockdown. Before transfection, the BMSCs medium was removed, and fresh serum-free medium (Gibco) was added, following the manufacturer’s instructions.

## RNA extraction and qRT-PCR analysis
RNA was extracted from BMSCs using TRIzol (CWBIO, China). According to standard procedure, the extracted mRNA was synthesized into cDNA using a Prime Script RT kit (Thermo Fisher, China). Real-time quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR) was performed using SYBR Premix Ex Taq. Qualitative analysis was performed using the QuantStudio 7 Flex system (Thermo Fisher Scientific, China).

## Western blot
Cells were washed three times on ice with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), and BMSCs total protein was obtained with RIPA lysis buffer (Beyotime, China). The protein concentration of each sample was measured using the BCA Protein Assay Kit (Beyotime, China). The protein was mixed with 5 × SDS-PAGE loading buffer (Beyotime, China) and boiled for 10 min. Proteins were separated using 4–20% FuturePAGE protein pre-glue (ACE, USA) and transferred to polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes (Millipore Sigma, USA). After blocking, primary and secondary antibody treatment, the bands were visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescent kit (NCMBiotech, China), and the gray value of each protein band was measured using Bio-RadImageLab software (Hercules, USA).

## RNA m6A quantification
Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent (CWBIO, China). The relative m6A content was measured using the EpiQuikm6ARNA Methylation Quantitation Kit (EpiGentek, USA) following the instructions. Measure the absorbance at 450 nm using a microplate reader.

## Immunofluorescence staining and microscopy
BMSCs were cultured in 24-well plates. The cells were washed three times with cold PBS, fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde at room temperature for 20 min, 0.5% Triton X-100 penetrated the cells at room temperature for 15 min and blocked with 5% BSA for 1 h at room temperature. Cells were then incubated with anti-FTO antibody (1:200; AbCam, USA) overnight at 4 °C. Incubate for 1 h with fluorescent secondary antibody. After washing five times in PBS and staining with DAPI (Beyotime, China) for 5 min, images were collected under a fluorescent microscope.

## Statistical analysis
All experiments were repeated at least three times and results were expressed as mean ± standard error (SEM). The comparison between the two sets of data was performed using Student’s t test. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used to compare the groups. Data with p values less than 0.05 were considered statistically different.

## Results

## Phenotypic identification of bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells
After BMSCs were cultured and passaged, the third-passage cells were selected and relevant surface markers were detected by flow cytometry. As shown in Fig.  A, the cells were positive for MSC-related surface markers (CD29, CD105, CD73, and CD166) and negative for hematopoietic markers (CD45, CD11b, CD34, and CD14), following the International Society for Stem Cell Research (ISSCR) and the International Society for Cell Therapy (ISCT) identification criteria of BMSC.
 In order to confirm the multi-directional differentiation potential of the extracted cells, BMSC were induced to differentiate into osteoblasts, chondrocytes, and adipocytes with the corresponding medium. ALP staining (Fig.  B), ARS staining (Fig.  C), oil red O staining (Fig.  D), and alcian blue staining (Fig.  E) were used to identify the multi-directional differentiation potential. As shown in Fig.  B–E, these BMSC were detected to be ALP positive (Fig.  B), and mineralized nodules were observed in osteogenic BMSC (Fig.  C). Lipid droplets were present in adipogenic BMSC (Fig.  D), and the number of glycosaminoglycans in cartilage-forming BMSC increased (Fig.  E). All these results indicate that BMSC have been successfully sorted and cultured.

## Increased expression of FTO in bone tissue of patients with congenital scoliosis
To explore the potential role of m6A methylation in osteogenic differentiation, we first compared the levels of m6A methylation in bone tissue from patients with congenital scoliosis. We found that, compared to normal samples, the levels of m6A methylation were significantly increased (Fig.  A). Since m6A is written/erased by a series of methylases, demethylases, and binding proteins such as METTL3, METTL14, WTAP, FTO, ALKBH5, YTHDF1, and IGF2BP1, to determine which gene is primarily responsible for the increase in m6A methylation, We performed real-time PCR (RT-PCR). The results showed that FTO expression was upregulated in patients with congenital scoliosis, while the expression differences of METTL3, METTL14, WTAP, ALKBH5, YTHDF1, and IGF2BP1 were not significant (Fig.  B). Western blotting (Fig.  C) and immunofluorescence (Fig.  D) were used to detect the protein levels of FTO in congenital scoliosis, and it was found that FTO expression was increased in these patients. Additionally, Western blotting (Fig.  E) was used to detect the protein levels of METTL3, METTL14, WTAP, ALKBH5, YTHDF1, and IGF2BP1 in patients with congenital scoliosis, and no significant differences were observed compared to normal samples.
 

## FTO impaired osteogenic differentiation and promoted adipogenic differentiation of BMSCs
We investigated the role of FTO in the differentiation of BMSCs. Three independent silencing sequences (siFTO-1, siFTO-2, and siFTO-3) were used to silence FTO in BMSCs derived from CS patients. The knockdown efficiency of siFTO was verified using qRT-PCR (Fig.  A) and Western blot (Fig.  B). The corresponding results showed that after transfection of CS-BMSCs with siFTO, the mRNA and protein expression of FTO were downregulated compared to those without siFTO transfection. Due to the highest knockdown efficiency of siFTO-2, it was used for subsequent experiments. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 1a, siFTO promoted the transcription of osteoblast-related genes during osteogenic differentiation, including type I collagen (Col1), runt-related transcription factor 2 (Runx2), osteopontin (Opn), and osteocalcin (Ocn). The protein levels of COL1, RUNX2, OPN, and OCN exhibited the same trend (Fig.  C). Additionally, the positive areas of ALP staining and alizarin red staining (ARS) were significantly increased (Fig.  D). On the other hand, knockdown of FTO reduced the adipogenic differentiation ability of BMSCs. During adipogenic differentiation, the mRNA levels of adipogenic genes (peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor-γ (Ppar), CCAAT/enhancer-binding protein α (C/ebpα), fatty acid-binding protein 4 (Fabp4)) (Supplementary Fig. 1b) and protein levels (PPAR, C/EBPα, and FABP4) (Fig.  E) were decreased in the siFTO group. Overexpression of FTO mediated by lentivirus (Supplementary Fig. 1c and Fig.  F) decreased the mRNA and protein expression of osteoblast-related genes (Supplementary Fig. 1d and Fig.  G). During osteogenic differentiation, OE-FTO inhibited the osteogenic differentiation capacity of BMSCs (Fig.  H), and during adipogenic differentiation, OE-FTO increased the mRNA and protein expression of adipogenic genes (Supplementary Fig. 1e and Fig. I). Our preliminary results indicate that FTO can simultaneously decrease the osteogenic potential of BMSCs and increase their adipogenic potential.
 

## FTO impaired osteogenic differentiation of BMSCs and promoted adipogenic differentiation of BMSCs by suppressing MMP1 expression
To explore the mechanism by which FTO regulates BMSCs differentiation, we performed high-throughput sequencing of RNA of BMSCs during osteogenic differentiation in the Vector and OE-FTO groups (Fig.  A). We used qRT-PCR to verify the sequencing results of BMSCs in the Vector and OE-FTO groups and found that after FTO overexpression, MMP1 and FBN2 were under expressed, while LOX and CSF3 were overexpressed during osteogenic differentiation (Fig.  B). We used bone samples from normal controls and CS patients for verification and found that the expression level of MMP1 in bone tissue of CS patients was decreased (Fig.  C). Further verification with larger samples showed that the expression of MMP1 was decreased in bone tissue of CS patients (Fig.  D). At the cellular level, we first induced osteogenesis of BMSCs and found that the expression of MMP1 increased (Fig.  E). In contrast, the expression of MMP1 was significantly increased after FTO knockdown (Fig.  F).
 Next, we explored the effect of MMP1 on the differentiation potential of BMSCs. BMSCs were treated with lentiviral overexpression and siRNA knockdown of MMP1, and the gene interference efficiency was determined by qRT-PCR (Supplementary Fig. 2a). Western blot was used to verify the overexpression efficiency of OE-MMP1 and the knockdown efficiency of siMMP1 (Fig.  G). OE-MMP1 and siMMP1 respectively increased and inhibited the transcription (Fig.  H) and protein expression levels (Fig.  I) of osteogenesis-related genes in BMSCs during osteogenesis induction. ALP staining, alizarin red staining, and ALP enzyme activity assay results showed that MMP1 overexpression increased extracellular matrix mineralization and ALP activity during the osteogenic differentiation of BMSCs, while MMP1 knockdown inhibited extracellular matrix mineralization and ALP activity (Fig.  J, K). During adipogenic differentiation of BMSCs, MMP1 overexpression decreased the expression levels of adipogenic-related genes of BMSCs, whereas MMP1 knockdown increased the expression levels of adipogenic-related genes of BMSCs (Fig.  L and Supplementary Fig. 2b).

## Overexpression of MMP1 rescued the effect of FTO intervention on osteogenic differentiation of CS BMSCs
When we transfected FTO-overexpressing cells with OE-MMP1 lentivirus, we observed a partial improvement of the decreased expression of osteogenic-related genes (Fig.  A and Supplementary Fig. 3a) as well as an increase in ALP activity and extracellular matrix mineralization (Fig.  B, C). The increased expression of adipogenic-related was also partially inhibited (Fig.  D and Supplementary Fig. 3b). When siMMP1 was transfected into FTO-knockdown cells, we found that siMMP1 reduced the expression of osteogenic-related markers (Fig.  E and Supplementary Fig. 3c), ALP activity, and extracellular matrix mineralization (Fig.  F, H). However, siMMP1 was transfected into FTO-knockdown cells, promoting adipogenic-related markers (Fig.  G and Supplementary Fig. 3d). In conclusion, FTO affects the differentiation capacity of BMSCs by altering the expression of MMP1.
 

## MMP1 increased phosphorylation of ERK in BMSCs
To investigate the mechanism of MMP-1 in promoting osteogenic differentiation of BMSCs, we performed Western blotting to detect the expression levels of proteins such as P38, ERK, AKT, and JNK. The results indicated that after MMP1 knockdown treatment, the phosphorylated expression of ERK decreased, while the phosphorylated expressions of P38, JNK, and AKT, as well as the total protein levels of JNK, ERK, P38, and AKT, remained unaffected (Fig.  A, B). Following MMP1 overexpression treatment, Western blot results showed an upregulation in ERK phosphorylation, with no changes observed in the phosphorylated expressions of P38, JNK, and AKT or in the total protein levels of JNK, ERK, P38, and AKT (Fig.  C, D). Furthermore, compared to the untreated OE-MMP1 group, the OE-MMP1 group treated with PD98059 (a selective ERK inhibitor) exhibited significantly lower ALP/ARS staining signals and COL1/Runx2/OPN/OCN expression (Fig.  E, F), indicating that the ERK inhibitor blocked the MMP1-mediated promotion of osteogenic differentiation in CS BMSCs.
 

## Discussion
Congenital scoliosis (CS) is caused by abnormal vertebral development, including malsegmentation of the vertebral body, formation disorders and both . It has occurred during the fetal period, and its etiology and mechanism are still unclear. Therefore, in addition to observation, braces and surgery, the treatment of CS patients has not been able to make substantial progress.
M6A is one of the most common dynamic reversible post-transcriptional modifications in eukaryotes. The enzymes involved in the regulation of m6A mainly include methyltransferase, demethylase, and m6A-binding proteins – . Together with m6A, these enzymes extensively affect mammalian development, cell differentiation, immunity, metabolism and other life processes, and play an important role in skeletal abnormal diseases , .
M6A methylation can affect the activity and differentiation process of bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells, osteoblasts, and osteoclasts in multiple ways, thereby regulating bone metabolism , . For example, studies have shown that zoledronic acid, a drug used to treat osteoporosis, can regulate the bone resorption capacity of osteoclasts by affecting the m6A methylation level of NFATc1 . METTL3-mediated m6A methylation modification promotes the osteogenic differentiation of BMSCs through different pathways and plays a role in inhibiting the progression of osteoporotic disease . Shen et al. believed that FTO was low expressed in osteoblast differentiation, and the GDF11-FTO-PPARγ signaling axis supported the differentiation of BMSCs into adipocytes rather than osteoblasts, ultimately promoting the progress of osteoporosis . YTHFD1 can promote the osteogenesis of BMSCs through translational regulation of ZNF839 , . So, does m6A modification also regulate the differentiation of BMSCs in CS patients?
Our team has found in previous studies that patients with congenital scoliosis are characterized by low bone density , . Data based on iliac peak bone biopsies show that the iliac peak cancellous bone of CS patients contains less mineralized bone and thinner cancellous bone compared to healthy controls , . These studies reported a significant decrease in lumbar bone mineral density in patients with CS. In addition, there have also been reports that patients with CS have confirmed a lack of serum vitamin D3, which suggests that patients with CS may have serious bone metabolism disorders . Therefore, in our study, human BMSCs were classified and cultured as needed. More importantly, the osteogenic differentiation ability of CS BMSCs is weaker than that of NC-BMSCs, indicating that the decrease in osteogenic differentiation of CS BMSCs may be related to the decrease in bone mineral density and the occurrence of CS.
The mechanism by which FTO regulates the differentiation of BMSCs in CS is unclear. FTO is the first methylase discovered, which causes oxidative removal of m6A and affects alternative splicing and processing of precursor mRNA. We found that FTO is down-regulated during osteogenic differentiation of BMSCs in CS patients and up-regulated during adipogenic differentiation of BMSCs , . In order to further explore the mechanism by which FTO regulates osteogenic differentiation of BMSCs in CS, we performed RNA sequencing. We found that when FTO was overexpressed, MMP1 expression decreased. Alkaline phosphatase has always been considered to be an important indicator of osteogenic differentiation. Transcription factors COL1A1, RUNX2, OPN, and OCN are important markers that regulate different stages of bone formation. In this study, when MMP1 was overexpressed, alkaline phosphatase activity and staining, mineralization ability, and expression of osteogenic differentiation markers of BMSCs increased. Further experiments confirmed that knocking down MMP1 can reduce alkaline phosphatase activity, mineralization ability, and the expression of COL1A1, RUNX2, OPN, and OCN in BMSCs. Taken together, these findings suggest that MMP1 can enhance the osteogenic differentiation potential of BMSCs.
Previous studies have shown that proliferation, cell death, and differentiation of many cell types are mediated by regulating of MMP1 expression through ERK, AKT, and p38 mitogen-activated protein-dependent (MEK) signaling pathways , . Therefore, we speculated whether MEK was involved in MMP1-mediated osteogenic differentiation of BMSCs. Our experimental results show that overexpression of MMP1 increases the expression of p-ERK in BMSCs. Knockdown of MMP1 inhibited p-ERK in BMSCs. In addition, the specific inhibitor PD98059 can reduce the activation of the ERK pathway induced by MMP1, while inhibiting the activity, staining, and mineralization of alkaline phosphatase, indicating that MMP1 may play its function in BMSCs through the ERK signaling pathway.
Although our data demonstrate consistent regulation of the FTO/MMP1/ERK pathway in human BMSCs, the absence of in vivo evidence (e.g., bone regeneration assays in CS animal models) means these findings should currently be interpreted as mechanistic hypotheses. In the future, we will verify this mechanism process through in vivo experiments.
Overall, our study initially suggests that FTO regulates the expression of MMP1 through m6A-related pathways, and MMP1 promotes the osteogenic differentiation potential of BMSCs through the ERK signaling pathway. Our study reveals the key role of the FTO-MMP1-ERK pathway in regulating the differentiation function of BMSCs in CS (Fig.  ). This means that the m6A-related pathway between FTO and MMP1 may be a potential therapeutic target for osteoporosis in patients with CS.
