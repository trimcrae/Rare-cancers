## ABSTRACT
mRNA‐based therapeutics offer significant potential for cancer treatment owing to their ability to induce transient, non‐integrating expression of therapeutic proteins. However, effective and tumour‐specific delivery remains a major barrier. In this study, we report a cell membrane vesicle (CMV)‐based delivery platform for targeted mRNA therapy, employing engineered CMVs enriched with the surface molecule CD6 for the selective delivery of Il24 mRNA to CD166‐overexpressing tumour cells. CMVs were derived from NIH‐3T3 cells via cytochalasin B induction and genetically modified to express CD6, enabling biomimetic targeting through the CD6–CD166 axis. Il24 mRNA was loaded into CD6‐CMVs through a digitonin‐assisted permeabilisation strategy, achieving high encapsulation efficiency and sustained intracellular release. In vitro, CD6‐CMVs facilitated enhanced uptake in CT26 cells, leading to elevated IL‐24 expression, activation of apoptotic and autophagic pathways, and suppression of migration and invasion. In mouse colorectal cancer and squamous carcinoma models, CD6‐CMVs/Il24 mRNA demonstrated effective tumour targeting, increased intratumoral IL‐24 translation, and potent antitumor efficacy, with minimal off‐target accumulation or systemic toxicity. Notably, this treatment promoted immune cell infiltration and reshaped the tumour microenvironment towards a pro‐inflammatory state. This study presents a scalable, low‐immunogenicity CMV platform capable of targeted mRNA delivery and cytokine expression, offering a promising strategy for precision immunotherapy for solid tumours.

## Materials and Methods
2.1

## Cell Culture and Animals
NIH‐3T3, CT26, and SCC‐7 cells were purchased from iCell Bioscience Inc. NIH‐3T3 cells were cultured in α‐MEM (Gibco, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco, USA) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Gibco, USA). CT26 cells and SCC‐7 cells were cultured in RPMI‐1640 (Gibco, USA) supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin/streptomycin. Cells were maintained at 37°C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2 and were passaged every 2 days.
Five‐to‐six‐week‐old BALB/c mice were purchased from the Department of Laboratory Animal Science of Peking University. Five‐to‐six‐week‐old C3H mice were purchased from Shanghai Model Organisms Center, Inc., China. All animal procedures were approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of Peking University (Approval number: LA2024115). All animals were housed in animal facilities with specific pathogen‐free conditions at the Laboratory Animal Science of Peking University Health.

2.2

## Preparation of CMVs
CMVs were generated from NIH‐3T3 cells when the cells reached 90%–100% confluence in 10 cm culture dishes. The cells were washed three times with PBS and incubated in 3 mL of serum‐free α‐MEM containing 10 µg/mL cytochalasin B (Solarbio, China) at 37°C for 30 min. Morphological changes were monitored in real time using a fluorescence microscope (Olympus, Japan). To detach the cells and release CMVs, the culture was treated with 0.25% (w/v) trypsin and 0.01% (w/v) EDTA (Gibco, USA) at 37°C for 1 min, followed by trypsin inactivation with an equal volume of complete culture medium. The suspension was then transferred to a 15 mL centrifuge tube and vortexed for 30 s to promote CMV release. The mixture was then centrifuged at 200 × g for 5 min to remove intact cells and aggregated CMVs. The pellet was discarded, and the supernatant was collected. A second centrifugation was performed at 3000 × g for 30 min to pellet the CMVs, which were subsequently resuspended in a medium and either used immediately or aliquoted and stored at −80°C for subsequent use.

2.3

## Characterization of CMVs
The fluorescent‐labelled CMVs were obtained from fluorescent‐labelled NIH‐3T3 cells. The cells were stained with 10 µg/mL DiI (Beyotime, China) at 37°C for 10 min, washed with PBS three times, and subsequently stained with 10 µg/mL cFDA‐SE (Beyotime, China) in serum‐free medium at 37°C for 1 h. Then, the CMVs were collected after separation from the parental cells and observed via laser scanning confocal microscopy (CLSM). The morphology and structure of the CMVs were also observed under TEM (FEI, USA). Samples were fixed, dehydrated, embedded, ultrathin‐sectioned (70–90 nm), stained on copper grids, and imaged under TEM. CMV size measurements (via DLS at room temperature) and 𝜁‐potential measurements were performed using a Malvern Zetasizer nano ZS.

2.4

## Preparation of Engineered CMVs With High CD6 Expression
The lentivirus overexpressing Cd6 and eGFP was obtained from the GeneChem company (China). NIH‐3T3 cells were seeded into 12‐well plates at a density of 2 × 105 cells per well and treated with 1.5 mL of fresh culture medium. After incubation at 37°C with 5% CO2 for 4 h, the lentivirus was added at an MOI of 100. Following 24 h of infection, the medium was replaced with fresh complete medium containing 5 µg/mL puromycin. The cells were then transferred to 10 cm culture dishes for purification, which was carried out for 48 h. The resulting stable gene‐modified cell line (CD6‐3T3) was expanded and stored for further experiments. CD6‐CMVs were prepared using the methods previously used for CD6‐3T3 cells.

2.5

## Western Blotting
Total proteins from the cells and CMVs were extracted using RIPA lysis buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) containing a mixture of protease and phosphatase inhibitors and quantified using a BCA protein assay kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Approximately 15 µg of total protein per sample was loaded and separated by 10% SDS‐PAGE, followed by transfer onto a PVDF membrane (Millipore, USA). After blocking with 5 % bovine serum albumin (Sigma, USA) in Tris‐buffered saline with 0.1 % Tween‐20 (TBS‐T) at room temperature for 1 h, the membrane was then incubated overnight at 4°C with the following primary antibodies: anti‐CD6 (37217, CST), anti‐CD166 (T57159, Abmart), anti‐IL‐24 (26772‐1‐AP, Proteintech), anti‐Beclin‐1 (T55092, Abmart), anti‐Bax (T40051, Abmart), anti‐Caspase‐9 (T40046, Abmart), and anti‐β‐actin (AA128, Beyotime). After the membrane was washed three times, species‐specific horseradish peroxidase‐conjugated goat anti‐rabbit IgG (A0208, Beyotime) was added for 1 h at 25°C. The expression of each protein was examined using an enhanced chemiluminescence detection system. Data analysis was performed using FIJI software, and the results were presented as levels normalised to controls for each experiment.

2.6

## qRT‐PCR
Total RNA was extracted from cells using a Total RNA kit (Takara, Japan), and reverse transcription to cDNA was performed using a cDNA synthesis kit (Accurate Biology, China). The qRT‐PCR for each gene was performed in triplicate using the SYBR green qPCR kit (Yeasen, China) on a QuantStudio 3 Real‐Time PCR System (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). The specific primer sequences are listed in Table . Relative mRNA expression was calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method.

2.7

## Analysis of mRNA Loading in and Release From CMVs

Il24 mRNA was generated by in vitro transcription (IVT) from a linearised plasmid (GS‐CMV‐100A‐BspQI, GenScript, China) containing a T7 promoter, 5′ UTR, Kozak sequence, IL‐24 coding sequence, stop codon, and 3′ UTR. The synthesised mRNA included a Cap1 structure and N1‐methylpseudouridine modification to enhance stability and translation efficiency. The CMV quantity was defined based on the number of source cells used for vesicle production, taking advantage of the relatively stable vesicle yield per cell (Pick et al. ). CMVs were approximated as spherical particles, and their mass was estimated according to their diameter, assuming a density of 1 g/cm3. The calculated vesicle mass was then used to represent the quantity of CMVs for subsequent experiments. Serum‐free α‐MEM was used as the loading buffer. mRNA loading was performed using a fixed ratio; each 20 µL loading system contained 500 µg of CMVs, 10 µg of mRNA (GenScript, China), and 2 µg/mL digitonin (Solarbio, China). The mixture was incubated in a metal bath at 37°C with shaking at 300 rpm for 20 min. CaCl2 was then added, and the incubation continued under the same conditions for an additional 10 min. The loaded CMVs were collected via centrifugation at 3000 × g for 30 min. The pellet was then washed twice with PBS by resuspension and centrifugation under the same conditions and resuspended in PBS for further experiments.
To determine whether Il24 mRNA was encapsulated within CMVs, an RNase protection assay was performed. CMVs loaded with Il24 mRNA were incubated with RNase A in the presence or absence of 0.1% Triton X‐100 at 37°C for 30 min to degrade unprotected RNA. After incubation, vesicles were collected by centrifugation, and total RNA was extracted, and the remaining Il24 mRNA was quantified by qRT–qPCR to evaluate the extent of RNase protection.
To evaluate the mRNA encapsulation efficiency, CMVs were lysed using 1% Triton X‐100 to release the encapsulated mRNA, and the mRNA content was quantified using a RiboGreen assay kit (Invitrogen, USA). To assess the mRNA stability from CMVs, the loaded CMVs were incubated at 4°C and sampled at 0, 3, 6, 12, 24, and 48 h. At each time point, CMVs were washed, centrifuged, and lysed, and the mRNA content was measured using the RiboGreen assay.
For uptake analysis, CMVs were prepared from DiI‐stained 3T3 cells and loaded with Cy5‐labelled mRNA and observed using CLSM. Additionally, the CMVs encapsulating Cy5‐labelled mRNA were resuspended in PBS and analysed via flow cytometry. FlowJo software was used for data analysis and visualisation.

2.8

## 
In Vitro Chemotaxis Assay
To evaluate the chemotactic effects of CMVs and CD6‐CMVs in vitro, flow cytometry was used to assess their cellular uptake. DiI‐labelled CMVs and CD6‐CMVs were co‐incubated with CT26 and SCC‐7 cells for 0, 3, 6, 12, and 24 h. After incubation, the cells were digested, centrifuged, and fixed, followed by a fluorescence intensity analysis using flow cytometry. To further compare the differences in chemotactic effects between CMVs and CD6‐CMVs, DiO‐labelled CMVs and CD6‐CMVs were co‐cultured with CT26 cells for 6 h, fixed, and analysed using CLSM.

2.9

## 
In Vitro Assessment of Antitumor Effect
To evaluate the antitumor effects of CD6‐CMVs/Il24 mRNA in vitro, CT26 cells cultured in 6‐well plates were treated with PBS, CD6‐CMVs, and CD6‐CMVs/Il24 mRNA for 48 h. Apoptosis was analysed via Annexin V/PI staining (Servicebio, China) followed by flow cytometry and TUNEL staining (Invitrogen, USA). qRT‐PCR and western blot analyses were performed to assess the expression of apoptosis‐ and autophagy‐related proteins, including Beclin‐1 (T55092, Abmart), Bax (T40051, Abmart), and Caspase‐9 (T40046, Abmart). For the live/dead cell staining analysis, the cells were stained with Calcein‐AM and PI (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) and then imaged using an Olympus microscope.

2.10

## Transwell Migration and Invasion Assay
The effect of CD6‐CMVs/Il24 mRNA on the migration and invasion abilities of tumour cells was assessed by performing Transwell migration and invasion assays (Corning, USA). CT26 cells were seeded in the upper chamber, followed by the addition of PBS, CD6‐CMVs, or CD6‐CMVs/Il24 mRNA. The lower chamber was filled with complete medium. After 48 h of incubation, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, and non‐migrating cells in the upper chamber were gently removed with a cotton swab. The migrating cells were stained with 0.3% crystal violet, imaged under a light microscope, and quantified using ImageJ. For the invasion assay, Transwell chambers with 8 µm pores were coated with Corning Matrigel (Corning, USA). Matrigel was diluted in serum‐free medium at a 1:3 ratio, and 100 µL was added to each chamber, followed by incubation at 37°C for 1 h. Excess liquid was then removed, and the remaining procedures were performed as described for the migration assay.

2.11

## Biodistribution of CD6‐CMVs in Vivo

CMVs and CD6‐CMVs were prepared from DiI‐stained 3T3 and CD6‐3T3 cells. CT26 cells (1 × 106 cells) were subcutaneously inoculated into the right flanks of BALB/c mice. SCC‐7 cells (1 × 106 cells) were subcutaneously inoculated into the right flanks of C3H mice. After 9 days, 100 µg of DiI‐labelled CMVs or CD6‐CMVs suspended in 100 µL PBS were intravenously injected into mice. The biodistribution of the DiI‐labelled vesicles was then examined using an IVIS imaging system 2, 24 and 48 h after injection.

2.12

## RNA Sequencing
Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol (Invitrogen, USA). RNA purification, reverse transcription, library preparation, and sequencing were performed by Novogene Technology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China) using the Illumina NovaSeq platform. Raw sequencing data were subjected to quality control using fastp (https://github.com/OpenGene/fastp). Transcript assembly and quantification were conducted based on the FPKM method. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified using a threshold of |fold change (FC)| > 1.2 and p < 0.05 and visualised using volcano plots and heatmaps. Functional enrichment analysis of DEGs was subsequently performed using Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) databases. For KEGG analysis, the most significantly enriched pathway under each primary KEGG category was selected for visualisation. Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) was conducted based on the entire ranked gene set rather than only DEGs. Gene sets with a false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.25, p < 0.05, and |normalised enrichment score (NES)| > 1.5 were considered significantly enriched, and representative pathways with potential biological relevance were selected for presentation. Bioinformatic analyses were performed using the OmicStudio tools (https://www.omicstudio.cn/tool).

2.13

## 
In Vivo Antitumor Effects
CT26 cells (1 × 106 cells) were subcutaneously inoculated into the right flanks of BALB/c mice. SCC‐7 cells (1 × 106 cells) were subcutaneously inoculated into the right flanks of C3H mice. The mice were then randomly divided into three groups (n = 5) when the tumour volume reached ∼80 mm3. Next, these tumour‐bearing mice were each treated intravenously with PBS, CMVs/Il24 mRNA, and CD6‐CMVs/Il24 mRNA three times per week. The longest tumour dimension (L) and shortest tumour dimension (W) were recorded three times per week. The tumour volume was calculated as follows: V = 0.5 × L × W2. On day 14 post‐treatment, the mice were euthanised, and the tumour, heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney were isolated for analysis.

2.14

## Histological Analysis
Tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde at 4°C for 24 h, followed by dehydration through a graded ethanol series. Dehydrated tissues were embedded in paraffin and sectioned to a thickness of 5 µm. For frozen sections, freshly harvested tissues were gently rinsed and rapidly embedded in pre‐chilled OCT compound and then snap‐frozen in liquid nitrogen. Frozen tissues were sectioned using a cryostat.
H&E staining was performed to assess morphological changes. For immunofluorescence staining, tissue sections were incubated with an anti‐CD31 primary antibody (ab222783, Abcam) at 4°C overnight, followed by incubation with a corresponding secondary antibody at room temperature for 1 h. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI. For immunohistochemistry, sections were incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies against Ki‐67 (GB111499, Servicebio), CD45 (GB153886, Servicebio), CD3 (GB13084, Servicebio), CD8 (GB15068, Servicebio), CD14 (GB15254, Servicebio), and CD86 (GB150054, Servicebio), followed by HRP‐conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. DAB was used for colour development, and the sections were counterstained with hematoxylin. TUNEL staining was performed to detect apoptotic cells. Sections were permeabilised with 0.5% Triton X‐100, incubated with the TUNEL reaction mixture at 37°C in the dark for 1 h, and counterstained with DAPI. All images were acquired using a Leica confocal microscope and quantified using ImageJ software.

2.15

## ELISA and Caspase‐3 Activity Assay
Mouse tumor tissues were harvested, rinsed, and rapidly snap‐frozen in liquid nitrogen. Tissues were ground into a homogeneous lysate and centrifuged to remove debris. The supernatant was then collected as the sample. For ELISA, samples and standards were diluted to appropriate concentrations and added to IL‐24 (SEC064Mu, Cloud Clone Corp), IFN‐γ (GEM0006, Servicebio), TNF‐α (88–7324, Invitrogen), and IL‐10 (88–7105, Invitrogen) antibody‐coated 96‐well plates, followed by incubation at 37°C for 1 h. After thorough washing, the secondary antibody solution was added and incubated for another h. The substrate solution was then added, and plates were incubated in the dark for 15 min. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader. Sample concentrations were determined based on a standard curve generated from the OD values of known standards. Caspase‐3 activity in the samples was measured using a commercial Caspase‐3 activity assay kit (Multi Sciences, China).

2.16

## Statistical Analysis
Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 8.0.1. Data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). Differences between two groups were analysed by performing unpaired Student's t‐tests, whereas comparisons among three or more groups were conducted via one‐way ANOVA. A p‐value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

## Results and Discussion
3.1

## Preparation and Characterization of CD6‐CMVs
In this study, NIH‐3T3 cells were treated with Cytochalasin B (CB), and morphological changes were observed under a light microscope. The cells showed contraction, tubular extensions, and bead‐like protrusions. After CB removal and culture in fresh medium, their normal morphology was gradually restored (Figure ). Cell membranes were labelled with DiI, and the cytoskeleton was labelled with phalloidin. Confocal imaging showed that the cytoskeleton was normally organised into a reticular, spindle‐like structure but was largely disrupted after 30 min of CB treatment, with cells contracting into a rounded shape. Tubular membrane extensions remained at some sites (Figures and ). Upon phosphate‐buffered saline (PBS) removal and further culture, the cytoskeleton returned to normal (Figure ). These results confirm that CB induces reversible cytoskeletal disruption and membrane deformation, consistent with results from previous studies (Bousquet et al. ). After vortexing and gradient centrifugation, transmission electron microscopy showed that CMVs were generally spherical or ellipsoidal with intact membrane structures (Figure  ). CMVs prepared from DiI/cFDA‐SE‐co‐labelled 3T3 cells displayed red‐labelled membranes and green‐labelled cytoplasmic contents under confocal microscopy (Figure  ), confirming that they retained both the membrane and cytoplasmic components from parent cells, consistent with previous reports (Pick et al. ; Sanger and Holtzer ; Gomzikova et al. ).
 To enhance the tumor‐targeting capability of CMVs, we generated CD6‐enriched CMVs via the lentiviral transduction of 3T3 cells. As previously described, in CMVs, the parent cell membrane structure is preserved; thus, overexpression of the membrane protein CD6 in 3T3 cells enabled the production of CD6‐enriched CMVs. Following transduction with a lentiviral vector encoding CD6 and eGFP, stronger eGFP fluorescence was observed in transduced cells compared to that in wild‐type 3T3 cells, indicating successful transduction (Figure ). qRT‐PCR analysis confirmed a significant increase in CD6 mRNA expression in the transduced group compared to that in the vehicle control (Figure  ). Meanwhile, western blot analysis further demonstrated elevated CD6 protein levels in both transduced 3T3 cells and their derived CMVs, relative to those in their wild‐type counterparts (Figure  ). To evaluate the impact of trypsin treatment on CD6 integrity, CD6‐overexpressing 3T3 cells were subjected to brief trypsin digestion (∼1 min) prior to analysis. Western blot showed no obvious difference in CD6 expression levels or molecular weight compared with untreated cells (Figure ), indicating minimal proteolytic effect under these conditions. Consistently, isolated CD6‐CMVs exhibited a strong CD6 signal at the expected molecular weight (Figure  ), suggesting that CD6 was largely preserved during CMV preparation.
Transmission Electron Microscope (TEM) imaging revealed that CD6‐enriched CMVs (CD6‐CMVs) retained intact membrane structures (Figure ). Further, a dynamic light scattering (DLS) analysis revealed that the average sizes of CMVs and CD6‐CMVs were 1305 and 1028 nm, respectively (Figure  ). Repeated experiments showed that the size of CMVs varied between batches but consistently ranged from 500 to 1500 nm, in line with previous results (Wang et al. ; Gomzikova et al. ; Peng et al. ; Chulpanova et al. ; Gomzikova et al. ; Oshchepkova et al. ). This variation is mainly due to differences in vortexing, as CMVs form when CB‐induced membrane protrusions are sheared off during this process. Factors such as the vortex mixer type and manual operation introduce variability. However, these size differences have a minimal effect on biological performance, as CMVs consistently maintain their spherical structure and membrane–cytoplasm composition, ensuring stable bioactivity and safety. The zeta potential of CMVs ranged primarily from −20 to 5 mV, with an average value of −9.86 mV, whereas CD6‐CMVs showed a slightly higher average zeta potential of −8.20 mV (Figure  ). Although CMVs are mainly composed of a lipid bilayer, they exhibited near‐neutral zeta potentials with only minor differences, likely due to the non‐uniform and locally influenced surface charge distribution.

3.2

## Construction of CMVs/Il24 mRNA
In this study, digitonin was used to permeabilise CMVs for Il24 mRNA loading. Digitonin is a type of saponin and a naturally amphiphilic molecule, featuring a lipophilic aglycone core and hydrophilic sugar side chains, allowing it to interact with cell membranes by binding specifically to cholesterol and forming pores (Sudji et al. ; Malabed et al. ; Frenkel et al. ; Wang et al. ; Fan and Heerklotz ). This interaction is non‐covalent and reversible; upon removal of digitonin, the digitonin–cholesterol complexes can dissociate, allowing the lipid bilayer to reorganise and reseal (Miyamoto et al. ). Additionally, it can spontaneously assemble with drugs into micelles or vesicles in solution, facilitating the delivery of peptides, nucleic acids, and other molecules across membranes (Liao et al. ). The incorporation of saponins into cationic liposomes has been shown to enhance transfection efficiency (Lohchania et al. ; Hasanzadeh et al. ), and studies by Sama et al. confirmed that saponins can improve DNA and RNA delivery both in vitro and in vivo (Sama et al. ; Sama et al. ).
We investigated the optimal concentration of digitonin for mRNA loading. The mRNA loading efficiency initially increased with the digitonin concentration, reaching a peak at 2 µg/mL, and then gradually declined with further increases in the concentration (Figure ). Under optimised conditions, digitonin was used to permeabilise CMVs for Cy5‐labelled Il24 mRNA loading. Flow cytometry showed a marked increase in the fluorescence intensity following mRNA loading (Figure  and Figure ), with 53.8% of CMVs exhibiting a Cy5 signal, indicating a loading efficiency of 53.8%. Confocal microscopy images further confirmed the co‐localisation of DiO‐labelled CMVs and Cy5‐labelled Il24 mRNA (Figure  ). We further investigated the stability of Il24 mRNA loaded in CD6‐CMVs. The mRNA content remained stable within the first 6 h, with no significant changes observed. However, a decline was noted at 12 h, with mRNA levels decreasing to 65.9% of the initial amount, followed by near‐complete release between 24 and 48 h (Figure  ). To verify whether Il24 mRNA was encapsulated within CMVs rather than merely adsorbed on the vesicle surface, an RNase protection assay was performed. RNase treatment alone reduced the detectable Il24 mRNA level to 61.4% (Figure ), indicating that a portion of the mRNA was accessible to RNase digestion and likely associated with the vesicle surface. In contrast, additional treatment with Triton X‐100, which disrupts the vesicle membrane, resulted in a further decrease in Il24 mRNA levels. These results suggest that while some Il24 mRNA is surface‐associated, a substantial fraction is protected within the lumen of CMVs, confirming successful encapsulation.

3.3

## Evaluation of Targeting Ability of CD6‐CMVs in Vitro

CD166 is a cell surface glycoprotein of the immunoglobulin superfamily, broadly expressed and involved in cell adhesion. It is highly expressed in various tumours and cancer stem cells and is associated with increased invasiveness and metastasis (Ferragut et al. ; Jezierska et al. ; Darvishi et al. ). CD6, expressed on immune cells, such as T cells, B cells and monocytes, is a natural ligand of CD166. The interaction between these molecules plays a key role in stabilising immune synapses and promoting T cell activation (Zimmerman et al. ; Bowen et al. ). This interaction is known to occur between CD166+ antigen‐presenting cells and T cells, where both molecules are recruited to the contact interface during immune synapse formation (Zimmerman et al. ). A similar mechanism may underlie the targeted binding between CD6‐CMVs and CD166+ tumor cells, facilitating active vesicle–cell interactions and thereby enhancing cellular uptake of the vesicles. Although the mechanisms underlying vesicle uptake by cells remain unclear, recent studies have demonstrated that CD166 is involved in the uptake of exosomes within the tumor microenvironment (CardeÃ±es et al. ), suggesting a potential role in vesicle internalisation.
We next examined expression of the target molecule CD166 in the tumor cells used in both in vitro and in vivo experiments. qRT‐PCR analysis showed that CD166 expression was significantly higher in CT26 and SCC‐7 cells than in 3T3 cells, with CT26 exhibiting the highest levels (Figure ). Western blot analysis confirmed the same trend observed via qRT‐PCR (Figure ). We further evaluated the cellular uptake of DiI‐labelled CMVs and CD6‐CMVs at 0, 3, 6, 12, and 24 h using flow cytometry. CD6‐CMVs showed faster uptake, with 69.5% positivity at 3 h and 87.3% at 6 h, compared to 38.4% and 59.7%, respectively, for CMVs. By 12 h, both reached similar uptake levels (∼98%), and they remained stable at 24 h (Figure   and Figure  ). The fluorescence intensity in CT26 cells increased over 24 h after treatment with CMVs or CD6‐CMVs. CD6‐CMVs exhibited a faster increase within the first 6 h, with both groups reaching similar levels by 12 h and levels continuing to increase through 24 h (Figure  ). To further assess cellular uptake, DiO‐labelled CMVs and CD6‐CMVs were co‐incubated with CT26 cells for 6 h, followed by fixation and nuclear staining with diamidino‐2‐phenylindole (DAPI). Confocal microscopy images showed that CD6‐CMVs exhibited markedly higher intracellular accumulation than CMVs at the 6‐h time point (Figure  and Figure  ). These results demonstrate that CD6 on the CMV surfaces remains biologically active, promoting effective tumor targeting. In vitro, both CMVs and CD6‐CMVs achieved nearly 100% delivery efficiency, but CD6‐CMVs acted faster, reaching maximal delivery in 6 h, versus 12 h for CMVs. This may be attributed to the simplicity of the in vitro environment, where extended incubation allows even non‐targeted CMVs to achieve a high delivery efficiency. The targeting capability of CD6‐CMVs was further evaluated through in vivo experiments (described in subsequent sections).
 
3.4

## CD6‐CMVs/Il24 mRNA Effectively Deliver mRNA to Tumor Cells and Upregulate IL‐24 Expression
We then evaluated the intracellular delivery and expression of mRNA mediated by CD6‐CMVs/Il24 mRNA in vitro. CD6‐CMVs loaded with DiO and Cy5‐labelled Il24 mRNA were incubated with CT26 cells for 6 h, followed by fixation and nuclear staining with DAPI. Confocal microscopy images revealed the accumulation of green fluorescent CD6‐CMVs around the nuclei, with visible red fluorescence from the encapsulated mRNA, indicating effective delivery of Il24 mRNA into tumor cells (Figure  ).
CT26 cells were treated with PBS, CMVs, CD6‐CMVS, CMVs/Il24 mRNA, or CD6‐CMVs/Il24 mRNA for 24 h, followed by qRT‐PCR and western blot analysis to evaluate intracellular Il24 mRNA and IL‐24 protein expression. qRT‐PCR results showed significantly higher Il24 mRNA levels in both treatment groups than in the control, CMV alone, or CD6‐CMV alone groups, with CD6‐CMVs/Il24 mRNA showing higher expression than CMVs/Il24 mRNA (Figure  ). Western blot analysis confirmed the same trend at the protein level (Figure  ). To further confirm that the observed effects were specifically driven by Il24 mRNA delivery, a noncoding control mRNA was included. CT26 cells treated with CD6‐CMVs alone or CD6‐CMVs loaded with control mRNA showed IL‐24 protein levels comparable to the blank control, whereas a marked increase in IL‐24 expression was detected in cells treated with CD6‐CMVs loaded with Il24 mRNA (Figure ). These results indicate that CMVs successfully deliver Il24 mRNA into CT26 cells, where it is effectively expressed, and that CD6‐CMVs exhibit superior delivery efficiency compared with non‐targeted CMVs.

3.5

## CD6‐CMVs/Il24 mRNA Significantly Promote Tumor Cell Apoptosis and Exert Antitumor Effects in Vitro

Studies have shown that IL‐24 exerts multifaceted antitumor effects. For example, it regulates the mitochondrial apoptosis pathway and oxidative stress, with overexpression leading to upregulation of the expression of pro‐apoptotic genes such as Bax and Bad and downregulation of the expression of anti‐apoptotic genes including Bcl‐xL and Bcl‐2 (Lebedeva et al. ; Lebedeva et al. ). IL‐24 also induces cytotoxic autophagy through endoplasmic reticulum stress and ceramide generation. Its interaction with IL‐20/IL‐22 receptors triggers reactive oxygen species‐mediated downstream signalling cascades and upregulates Beclin‐1 expression (Park et al. ; Yacoub et al. ; Hamed et al. ; Yacoub et al. ). In addition to its cytotoxic effects, IL‐24 inhibits tumor cell migration and invasion (Ramesh et al. ).
In this study, we evaluated the in vitro antitumor effects of CD6‐CMVs/Il24 mRNA. A live/dead assay was performed to assess the cytotoxic effects of CD6‐CMVs/Il24 mRNA on CT26 cells. Treatment with CD6‐CMVs/Il24 mRNA significantly increased the number of dead cells compared with that in the other groups (Figure  and Figure  ). A CCK‐8 assay was performed to quantitatively evaluate cell viability following treatment (Figure ). After 48 h, CT26 cells treated with CD6‐CMVs/Il24 mRNA showed a moderate reduction in viability (∼88% of control). We also evaluated the pro‐apoptotic effect of CD6‐CMVs/Il24 mRNA on CT26 cells. Western blot and qRT‐PCR results showed that CD6‐CMVs/Il24 mRNA treatment significantly upregulated the expression of the autophagy marker Beclin‐1 in CT26 cells (Figure  and Figure ). Additionally, both mRNA and protein levels of the pro‐apoptotic protein Bax and the apoptosis‐related marker Caspase‐9 were significantly elevated (Figure  and Figures and ). Flow cytometric analysis following Annexin V‐FITC/propidium iodide (PI) staining revealed increased proportions of both early and late apoptotic cells in the CD6‐CMVs/Il24 mRNA group compared with those in the PBS and CD6‐CMVs groups (Figure  and Figure  ). Consistently, terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase dUTP nick end labelling (TUNEL) staining showed a significant increase in apoptotic cells in the CD6‐CMVs/Il24 mRNA group, whereas minimal apoptosis was observed in the PBS and CD6‐CMVs groups (Figure  and Figure  ). Next, we investigated the effects of CD6‐CMVs/Il24 mRNA on CT26 cell migration and invasion. Transwell assays revealed that, compared with those in the PBS and CD6‐CMVs groups, CD6‐CMVs/Il24 mRNA significantly inhibited the migratory and invasive abilities of CT26 cells (Figure  , Figure  , and Figure  ).
 CT26 cells were then cultured with PBS or CD6‐CMVs/Il24 mRNA for 2 days, prior to transcriptome sequencing. Transcriptome analysis revealed 361 DEGs, with 186 upregulated and 175 downregulated in the CD6‐CMVs/Il24 mRNA group compared with those in the PBS group. Volcano and heat maps were generated to illustrate the DEGs (Figure  and Figure ). KEGG enrichment analysis indicated activation of necroptosis, cytokine–cytokine receptor interaction, and other related pathways (Figure ). Meanwhile, GO enrichment analysis revealed significant enrichment of pathways related to cytokines, chemokines, and immune responses, including positive regulation of T cell proliferation, cytokine and chemokine activity, and the inflammatory response (Figure  ). The heatmap of key immune‐related DEGs is shown in Figure  . We further performed GSEA, which revealed significant enrichment of cytokine‐related pathways, including cytokine activity, receptor binding, and chemokine signalling. TNF signalling was also upregulated (Figure  ). Moreover, immune response pathways were enhanced, including leukocyte chemotaxis, monocyte/macrophage recruitment, dendritic cell differentiation, and MHC‐I expression (Figure  ). Meanwhile, DNA replication and repair pathways were suppressed, suggesting cell cycle arrest and reduced DNA damage repair, potentially promoting apoptosis and increasing sensitivity to agents such as poly(ADP‐ribose) polymerase inhibitors and cisplatin (Figure  ).

3.6

## CD6‐CMVs/Il24 mRNA Exhibit Effective Tumor‐Targeting Ability in Vivo

To evaluate the in vivo targeting efficiency of CD6‐CMVs/Il24 mRNA, we established mouse models of CRC and SCC, representing prototypical adenocarcinoma‐ and epithelial‐derived solid tumours, respectively. In vivo imaging conducted 2 h post‐injection, a time point selected to capture the early biodistribution of vesicles in mice with rapid systemic circulation, revealed strong fluorescence signals in both the tumor and liver regions of mice treated with CMVs/Il24 mRNA, whereas CD6‐CMVs/Il24 mRNA primarily accumulated at the tumor sites. Additional observations at 24 and 48 h were also performed to further characterize the temporal distribution of CMVs (Figure ). A similar trend was observed in the SCC model (Figure ). Frozen sections of CRC tumor tissues, collected 24 h after injection to evaluate cellular uptake of vesicles, were analysed by confocal microscopy. Tumours from the CD6‐CMVs group exhibited extensive, diffuse red fluorescence, indicating widespread uptake. In contrast, tumor tissues from the CMVs/Il24 mRNA group exhibited more limited and scattered fluorescence signals (Figure  ). This differential fluorescence pattern—diffuse versus punctate—likely reflects variations in cellular uptake and membrane fusion over time. Since DiI labels lipid membranes, a diffuse signal suggests the fusion of DiI‐labelled CMV membranes with tumor cell membranes, whereas punctate signals may correspond to internalised or aggregated vesicles. These results indicate that CD6 modification enhances tumor targeting and accelerates vesicle accumulation at the tumor site.
 At 48 h following intravenous injection of PBS, CMVs/Il24 mRNA, and CD6‐CMVs/Il24 mRNA, tumours and major organs were collected, and IL‐24 protein levels were measured via enzyme‐linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA). IL‐24 levels in tumor tissues were higher in the CD6‐CMVs/Il24 mRNA group than those in the CMVs/Il24 mRNA group, and levels in both groups were higher than those in the PBS group (Figure  ). This indicates that the drug delivery system can effectively target tumor tissues and facilitate the expression of the IL‐24 molecule in vivo. In the CMVs/Il24 mRNA group, IL‐24 levels in the liver and lung tissues were significantly higher, with notable differences among the three groups. This suggests that CMVs primarily accumulate in the liver and lungs during circulation, which aligns with the findings from previous studies and may be related to phagocytosis by the reticuloendothelial system (Wang et al. ; Peng et al. ). Notably, relatively high IL‐24 levels were detected in the kidney across all groups, including the PBS control, without significant differences among treatments. The kidney participates in the clearance and regulation of circulating cytokines, which may lead to relatively higher background signals of cytokine proteins in renal tissue homogenates.
Compared to the in vitro setting, the advantages of CD6‐CMVs are more evident in vivo. Within the complex circulatory system, faster transfection reduces off‐target capture by non‐tumor tissues. Furthermore, our in vitro studies suggest that CMVs retain high levels of mRNA during the first 6 h, followed by a gradual release by 12 h and complete release by 24 h. Therefore, CD6‐CMVs that reach target cells earlier may carry relatively higher mRNA loads, enhancing the drug delivery efficiency.
Previous studies have explored multiple strategies to enhance the targeting capability of extracellular vesicles or membrane‐derived vesicles, including ligand modification, membrane protein engineering, and antibody conjugation. For example, Peng et al. chemically conjugated the aptamer AS1411, which recognises nucleolin overexpressed on cancer cell membranes, onto cell membrane‐derived vesicles to improve tumor targeting (Peng et al. ). Similarly, Smyth et al. employed copper‐catalysed azide–alkyne cycloaddition (click chemistry) to functionalise exosome surfaces with targeting ligands (Smyth et al. ). These chemical modification approaches have demonstrated effective targeting performance; however, they may introduce additional complexity in vesicle fabrication and downstream purification, potentially affecting stability, immunogenicity, or scalability. Alternatively, genetically engineered strategies have been developed to introduce targeting molecules in a more biologically integrated manner. For instance, Alvarez‐Erviti et al. achieved targeting by engineering dendritic cells to express Lamp2b fused with the neuron‐specific RVG peptide, enabling the production of exosomes with inherent targeting capability (Alvarez‐Erviti et al. ). This approach, similar in concept to our strategy, relies on endogenous expression of targeting components and avoids additional chemical modification steps.
In the present study, we leveraged the natural interaction between CD6 and its ligand CD166, which is frequently overexpressed in various tumor types. By incorporating a physiologically relevant adhesion molecule while preserving the intrinsic membrane structure of CMVs, this biologically inspired strategy may facilitate selective tumor localisation and maintain membrane integrity and functional compatibility. In addition, receptor–ligand interactions derived from endogenous proteins may provide more stable and adaptable targeting under complex in vivo conditions.
Nevertheless, despite these advantages, CD166 is not exclusively tumor‐specific and is also expressed in certain normal tissues including antigen‐presenting cells, which may result in potential off‐target interactions. However, CD166 expression is frequently upregulated in various tumor types such as CRC and SCC (Darvishi et al. ; Badic et al. ). In addition, Tumours are characterised by enhanced vascular permeability and dense vasculature, which facilitate the retention and accumulation of nanoscale vesicles. Therefore, despite the possibility of off‐target interactions, these features may contribute to the preferential accumulation of CD6‐CMVs in tumor tissues. Moreover, both CMV vesicles and IL‐24 exhibit favourable biosafety profiles. Previous clinical studies have reported minimal systemic toxicity associated with IL‐24 (Cunningham et al. ; Tong et al. ). Consistently, our in vivo experiments showed no obvious pathological abnormalities in major organs, as described in the following sections. Taken together, although potential off‐target effects cannot be completely excluded, the overall safety profile of the system suggests a low likelihood of severe off‐target toxicity.

## 
In Vivo Antitumor Efficacy of CD6‐CMVs/Il24 mRNA
To evaluate the in vivo antitumor effects of CD6‐CMVs/Il24 mRNA, we established mouse models of CRC and SCC. Mice were randomly divided into three groups and intravenously injected with PBS, CMVs/Il24 mRNA, or CD6‐CMVs/Il24 mRNA three times per week. Subsequently, tumor volume and body weight changes were monitored, and after 2 weeks of treatment, the mice were euthanised to collect tissues for further analysis (Figure  ). Both CMVs/Il24 mRNA and CD6‐CMVs/Il24 mRNA significantly inhibited tumor growth in the mouse CRC model, with CD6‐CMVs/Il24 mRNA demonstrating a stronger inhibitory effect (Figure  , Figure  , and Figure ). No significant differences in body weight were observed among the groups during the treatment period (Figure ). We observed a similar trend in the mouse SCC model (Figure ). Tumours did not show the typical J‐shaped growth pattern, likely due to the strong immune response in the mice, which slowed tumor growth. To assess the immunomodulatory effects of IL‐24, we chose to maintain the use of immunocompetent mice instead of switching to immunodeficient models. By day 21, although overall tumor sizes remained relatively small, significant differences in tumor volume between the control and treatment groups had already emerged. Therefore, the experiment was concluded as planned.
Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining showed that, in the control group, tumor cells were tightly packed with obvious nuclear atypia, increased mitotic activity, minimal stroma, and no visible necrosis or immune cell infiltration. In contrast, tumours from the CMVs/Il24 mRNA and CD6‐CMVs/Il24 mRNA groups showed reduced cellular density and areas of darker nuclear staining, suggesting chromatin condensation and early signs of apoptosis or necrosis. Vacuolar degeneration, a sign of cellular damage, was also observed. The CD6‐CMVs/Il24 mRNA group exhibited significantly more necrosis, with nuclear shrinkage, fragmentation, and dissolution, along with increased inflammatory cell infiltration, indicating an enhanced immune response (Figure  and Figure ). Ki‐67 IHC results showed a reduced proportion of positive cells in all treatment groups in both the CRC and SCC models, indicating that both CMVs/Il24 mRNA and CD6‐CMVs/Il24 mRNA suppressed tumor proliferation (Figure  , Figure  , and Figure ). TUNEL staining showed that CD6‐CMVs/Il24 mRNA significantly promoted apoptosis in tumor cells (Figure  and Figure  ). Further analysis of activity of the core apoptotic enzyme Caspase‐3 revealed the significant upregulation of active Caspase‐3 levels in the CD6‐CMVs/Il24 mRNA group (Figure  ).
IL‐24 has been reported to inhibit tumor angiogenesis by interacting with the IL‐20/IL‐22 receptor complex on endothelial cells (Ramesh et al. ). Furthermore, IL‐24 suppresses the production of vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) in tumor cells (Inoue et al. ), thereby directly or indirectly inhibiting angiogenesis. Immunofluorescence staining for the endothelial cell marker CD31 in tumor sections from both the CRC and SCC models showed a significant reduction in angiogenesis in the CD6‐CMVs/Il24 mRNA group (Figure  and Figure ), indicating that CD6‐CMVs/Il24 mRNA inhibits angiogenesis.
Notably, IL‐24 overexpression in non‐tumor cells does not significantly affect cell viability (Jiang et al. ; Sauane and al. ), a property, although not fully understood mechanistically, is valuable for addressing the severe side effects often associated with cytokine therapies. We assessed the in vivo toxicity of CMVs/Il24 mRNA and CD6‐CMVs/Il24 mRNA using the CRC and SCC models. Histological analysis of major organs showed no significant differences in the heart, liver, spleen, lungs, or kidneys across treatment groups compared with the PBS group, despite some off‐target effects (Figures and ). These results suggest that CMVs/Il24 mRNA and CD6‐CMVs/Il24 mRNA exhibit no significant in vivo toxicity.

## 
In Vivo Immunoinductive Effect of CD6‐CMVs/Il24 mRNA
Another important physiological function of IL‐24 is immune activation. Using an immunocompetent mouse model of CRC, Ma et al. demonstrated that IL‐24 promotes the secretion of IFN‐γ by CD4+ and CD8+ T cells and enhances the cytotoxicity of CD8+ T cells in vivo, thereby suppressing tumor progression (Ma et al. ). Similar findings were reported by Menezes et al., where the intratumoral injection of an IL‐24‐expressing adenovirus led to increased CD8+ T cell infiltration and significantly elevated IFN‐γ expression in the tumor microenvironment. In our study, the in vitro RNA‐sequencing analysis suggested that CD6‐CMVs/Il24 mRNA promotes immune cell proliferation, differentiation, and chemotaxis.
These findings were validated in vivo. IHC analysis of the immune cell marker CD45 revealed a progressive increase in immune cell infiltration across the PBS, CMVs/Il24 mRNA, and CD6‐CMVs/Il24 mRNA groups in the CRC and SCC models, confirming an immune‐activating effect (Figure  , Figure  , and Figure ). A further analysis of immune cell subtypes showed increased infiltration of CD3+ cells (primarily T cells), CD8+ cells (primarily Cytotoxic T cells), CD86+ cells (mainly dendritic cells and M1‐type macrophages), and CD14+ cells (monocyte–macrophage lineage), as shown in Figure  and Figure  . ELISA results demonstrated elevated levels of the pro‐inflammatory and antitumor cytokines IFN‐γ and TNF‐α, along with a decrease in the immunosuppressive cytokine IL‐10 (Figure  , Figure  , and Figure  ). We observed that the cytokine levels between the CMVs/Il24 mRNA and CD6‐CMVs/Il24 mRNA groups were comparable. This may reflect the tight regulation of inflammatory cytokine production in vivo. For instance, cytokine signalling can activate negative feedback pathways mediated by SOCS proteins (Bidgood et al. ), which limit excessive immune activation and may lead to a plateau in cytokine levels once a certain activation threshold is reached. In addition, ELISA of tumor homogenates measures total cytokine levels from diverse cell populations within the tumor microenvironment, potentially masking localised differences between targeted and non‐targeted delivery systems.
 A transcriptomic analysis was performed to further investigate the underlying mechanisms. The volcano plot revealed DEGs related to immune responses (Figure  ). GO enrichment analysis showed the significant enrichment of immune response‐associated pathways, including chemotaxis of various immune cell types and the upregulation of IL‐1, IL‐6, and TNF signalling (Figure  ). Additionally, pathways related to apoptosis were activated, whereas the DNA biosynthetic process was downregulated (Figure  ).

## Conclusion
In this study, we successfully developed engineered CMVs as a drug delivery platform for tumor‐targeted therapy. CMVs derived from NIH‐3T3 cells retained their natural membrane structures and exhibited excellent biocompatibility. By transfecting NIH‐3T3 cells with a CD6‐encoding lentiviral vector, we generated CD6‐enriched CMVs capable of targeting tumor tissues via the CD6/CD166 chemotactic axis. These CD6‐CMVs effectively encapsulated and delivered Il24 mRNA, maintaining its stability and bioactivity. The system exhibited potent antitumor effects in vitro and demonstrated selective targeting and therapeutic efficacy in both CRC and SCC animal models. Collectively, our findings highlight CD6‐enriched CMVs loaded with Il24 mRNA as a promising and versatile platform for targeted cytokine therapy, offering a potential strategy to enhance therapeutic precision while minimising systemic toxicity during cancer treatment.
