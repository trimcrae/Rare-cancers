## Abstract
Despite intensive, multimodal therapy, only half of children diagnosed with high-risk neuroblastoma will survive 5 years, and survivors harbor significant short- and long-term treatment-related comorbidities. Although mAb therapy targeting GD2 has improved outcomes, GD2-directed immunotherapy remains one of the only FDA-approved immunotherapies for pediatric cancer, and therapy is toxic due to GD2 expression on pain fibers. Thus, there is a critical need to uncover new immunotherapy targets in neuroblastoma. Activated leukocyte cell adhesion molecule (ALCAM) is a cell adhesion molecule that promotes tumor growth in a variety of cancers and is highly expressed in neuroblastoma. We generated three inducible CRISPR inhibition cell lines to deplete ALCAM and elucidate its role in neuroblastoma. Depletion of ALCAM reduced cell growth, reduced Ki-67 staining, and increased cleaved PARP. To determine the mechanism of ALCAM overexpression, we used chromatin immunoprecipitation sequencing to show MYCN oncoprotein binding at the ALCAM promoter. We generated luciferase reporters from the ALCAM promoter and a putative upstream (10 kb) enhancer, which we defined using Promoter-based Capture-C. Treatment with the MYC(N)/MAX dimerization inhibitor MYCi975 reduced ALCAM expression by immunoblotting and luciferase signal from the ALCAM promoter. We validated the activity of the upstream enhancer and uncovered an AP-1–binding motif that is critical for enhancer activity. Finally, as ALCAM is expressed in several normal tissues, we investigated an ALCAM-targeted conditionally activated antibody–drug conjugate, CX-2009 (praluzatamab ravtansine), which delayed tumor growth in two of three patient-derived xenograft models. Together, these findings credential ALCAM as an immunotherapeutic target in neuroblastoma.

## Introduction
Neuroblastoma is an extracranial solid malignancy arising from the developing sympathetic nervous system. Half of neuroblastomas are considered “high-risk,” and only half of children diagnosed with high-risk disease will survive with modern intensive multimodal therapy ( , ). mAbs targeting the disialoganglioside GD2 prolongs survival in neuroblastoma, establishing immunotherapy for the treatment of this disease ( ). Additionally, recent and ongoing clinical trials of chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) T cells targeting GD2 ( , ), as well as the oncoprotein GPC2 ( ), underscore the potential for T cell–based immunotherapies to provide new avenues for improving survival. However, despite the success of GD2-targeted therapies, expression of GD2 on pain fibers and other neurons as well as frequent disease relapse on or after anti-GD2 therapy remains a concern, highlighting the critical need for development of new immunotherapeutic strategies for neuroblastoma.
Activated Leukocyte Cell Adhesion Molecule (ALCAM) is a member of the immunoglobulin superfamily ( , ). ALCAM mediates low-affinity, homotypic adhesion to neighboring cells, and this interaction is primarily avidity controlled ( ). ALCAM-mediated adhesions are regulated by proteolytic cleavage through the metalloprotease ADAM17, which liberates ALCAM as a soluble negative regulator of adhesion ( , ). Alternative splicing of ALCAM also enables additional regulation by the protease MMP-14 ( ). ALCAM expression in retinal ganglion cell growth cones enhances axon pathfinding and elongation, underscoring the crucial role it plays in the developing nervous system ( – ). Additionally, ALCAM binds to its receptor CD6, which is expressed on T and NK cells ( , ). CD6 plays a complex role in T-cell function, inhibiting T-cell activation independent of ALCAM binding but promoting T-cell adhesion to antigen-presenting cells ( ). Efforts to target the ALCAM–CD6 interaction with blocking antibodies and soluble CD6 have resulted in improved survival in preclinical cancer models, highlighting the potential of this pathway for immunotherapy development ( , ).
High ALCAM expression is correlated with poor prognosis in many adult malignancies, including melanoma, pancreatic, and prostate cancers ( , ). ALCAM is a cancer stem cell marker in colorectal and lung cancers, and in prostate cancer, proteolytic cleavage of ALCAM promotes bone metastasis ( , , ). Additionally, ALCAM has been shown to inhibit apoptosis in several cancer types. In seven breast cancer cell lines, ALCAM knockdown led to an increase in cleaved PARP ( ), whereas in multiple myeloma, ALCAM expression protected against chemotherapy-induced apoptosis ( ). These data are consistent with studies indicating that ALCAM promotes signaling through the PI3K–AKT pathway, thereby protecting cells from apoptosis ( ). In neuroblastoma, ALCAM may localize to the tumor plasma membrane and to the neuropil in the extracellular matrix ( ). In patients with nonmetastatic tumors, expression of ALCAM on the tumor plasma membrane is associated with local and metastatic relapse, whereas the inverse is true for the neuropil. Furthermore, differentiation of neuroblastoma cells with retinoids in vitro relocalizes ALCAM to the newly formed neurites, consistent with the role of ALCAM in guiding axonal migration during neuronal development.
Although ALCAM has been investigated as potential therapeutic target, its normal tissue expression makes traditional immunotherapeutic platforms infeasible. However, several methods now exist to target otherwise attractive cell-surface proteins while minimizing on-target, off-tumor toxicity. Recent work identified bona fide AND-gated CAR T cells that split CAR activation into two downstream signals ( ). Additionally, an investigational new drug, PROBODY CX-2009 (praluzatamab ravtansine), a novel ALCAM-targeted conditionally activated antibody–drug conjugate (ADC) conjugated to a DM4 payload, was designed with masking peptides linked to the anti-ALCAM antibody via protease-cleavable linkers to reduce antigen binding in normal tissue. The masking peptides are cleaved by tumor-associated proteases in the tumor microenvironment, allowing for antigen binding and internalization ( – ). In addition to ALCAM, this approach has been leveraged to target EGFR and CD71, in which cleavable linkers were designed with sensitivity toward urokinase plasminogen activator, membrane-type serine protease 1 (matriptase), and legumain due to the expression of these proteases in tumors but not healthy tissues ( , ). Although CX-2009 showed evidence of clinical activity in adult malignancies ( ), it has not been investigated in pediatric cancer ( ). In this study, we characterize ALCAM as an immunotherapeutic target in neuroblastoma.

## Materials and Methods

## Cell culture and chemicals
Human neuroblastoma cell lines were obtained from the Children’s Hospital of Philadelphia cell line bank, the Children’s Oncology Group, and the ATCC. Cell lines used included the following: SK-N-BE(2)-C (RRID: CVCL_0529), SH-SY5Y (RRID: CVCL_0019), NB-EbC1 (RRID: CVCL_E218), NB-1643 (RRID: CVCL_5627), NBL-S (RRID: CVCL_2136), SK-N-BE(2; RRID: CVCL_0528), NLF (RRID: CVCL_E217), COG-N-415 (RRID: CVCL_AQ23), NB69 (RRID: CVCL_1448), Kelly (RRID: CVCL_2092), SK-N-FI (RRID: CVCL_1702), SK-N-AS (RRID: CVCL_1700), NB1 (RRID: CVCL_1440), COG-N-534 (RRID: CVCL_LF63), COG-N-496 (RRID: CVCL_LF61), SK-N-DZ (RRID: CVCL_1701), COG-N-453 (RRID: CVCL_LF59), IMR-5 (RRID: CVCL_1306), SK-N-SH (RRID: CVCL_0531), NB-1691 (RRID: CVCL_5628), SMS-SAN (RRID: CVCL_7136), NB-SD (RRID: CVCL_LF68), IMR-32 (RRID: CVCL_0346), and NMB (RRID: CVCL_2143). Cell line genomic identity was confirmed using short tandem repeat sequencing at the University of Pennsylvania Genomics Core on frozen stocks prior to freezing. Additionally, cell lines were tested for Mycoplasma contamination after each thaw using the University of Pennsylvania Cell Center Services Core. Cells were cultured in RPMI-1640 medium unless otherwise noted, containing 10% FBS, 2 mmol/L L-Glutamine, and 1% penicillin/streptomycin at 37°C under 5% CO2, and maintained at sub-20 passage count. COG cell lines were maintained in IMDM medium containing 20% FBS, 2 mmol/L L-Glutamine, 1× insulin–transferrin–selenium–ethanolamine (Thermo Fisher Scientific, #51500056), and 1% penicillin/streptomycin. After transduction with doxycycline-inducible plasmids, cells were cultured in RPMI-1640 medium containing 10% Tet System–Approved FBS (Takara Bio, #631101; henceforth, Tet-free RPMI) and the aforementioned reagents.
To induce ALCAM knockdown, cells were seeded at ∼20% confluency in Tet-free RPMI and treated with doxycycline (Sigma-Aldrich, #D3072-1ML) at a concentration of 2 μg/mL, refreshed every 48 to 72 hours after induction. Control cells were treated with the same volume of DMSO. Treatment was performed for 7 days before cells were harvested for downstream assays. Depletion of MYCN was achieved using MYCi975 (Selleckchem, #S8906) in DMSO or an equivalently diluted volume of DMSO as control.

## CRISPR inhibition
Guide RNAs were designed using the Broad Institute CRISPick algorithm for CRISPR inhibition (CRISPRi) targeting the ALCAM gene using the SpyoCas9 (NGG) setting and the Hsu (2013) tracrRNA.
Forward and reverse oligos were obtained from Integrated DNA Technologies with the following sequences:
Forward (sg1): 5′- TCC​CCG​GGT​CTC​CTC​AGG​TGG​CAG-3′
Reverse (sg1): 5′- AAA​CCT​GCC​ACC​TGA​GGA​GAC​CCG-3′
Forward (sg2): 5′- TCC​CGG​GCG​GCG​GGT​CTC​CTC​AGG-3′
Reverse (sg2): 5′- AAA​CCC​TGA​GGA​GAC​CCG​CCG​CCC-3′
Doxycycline-inducible CRISPRi cell lines were produced using a two-vector system. First, oligos were cloned into the FgH1tUTG vector (RRID: Addgene_70183) using Golden Gate Assembly. Briefly, oligos were annealed in T4 DNA ligase buffer with 10 mmol/L ATP (NEB, #B0202S) and T4 PNK (NEB, #M0201S). After annealing, oligos were diluted 1:10 in nuclease-free water and one-step ligation was performed using Esp3I enzyme (NEB, #R0734S) and 100 ng FgH1tUTG vector. Ligation was performed for 20 cycles at 37°C for 5 minutes, followed by 20°C for 5 minutes. A final heat inactivation step was performed at 80°C for 20 minutes. Ligation product was then transformed into One Shot Stbl3 Chemically Competent Cells (Thermo Fisher Scientific, #C737303). Successful cloning was verified by Sanger sequencing (primer sequence: 5′- CAGACATACAAACTAAAGAAT-3′).
To generate the cell lines, lentivirus was produced using the pMD2.G VSV-G envelope plasmid (RRID: Addgene_12259) and psPAX2 packaging plasmid (RRID: Addgene_12260) with either the FgH1tUTG cloned vectors or lenti-dCas9-KRAB-MeCP2 (RRID: Addgene_122205) at equimolar ratios (1.7 pmol per plasmid). Plasmids were transfected into 15e6 HEK-293T cells (RRID: CVCL_0063, ATCC, CRL-3216) using Lipofectamine 3000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, #L3000008). The viral supernatant was harvested after 48 hour, filtered with a 0.45-μm filter, and added to cells at a 1:3 virus to media ratio. First, lenti-dCas9-KRAB-MeCP2 was transduced, and after 24 hours, cells were selected using 20 μg/mL blasticidin for 2 weeks. After antibiotic selection, FgH1tUTG-containing virus was added, and cells were sorted using FACS for GFP expression 48 hours after transduction.

## Luciferase reporter assays
Luciferase reporter backbones encoding PEST-destabilized NanoLuciferase [pNL2.2 (NlucP/Hygro), #N1071; pNL3.2 (NlucP/minP), #N1041] or constitutive promoter–driven (TK) Firefly Luciferase control [pGL4.54 (luc2/TK), #E5061] were obtained from Promega.
Primers flanking the ALCAM promoter were designed and obtained from Integrated DNA Technologies as follows:
Forward: 5′-ATG​CGA​GCT​CGC​TGA​AGA​TAG​GGC​TTA​ATA​GTT​TAG-3′
Reverse: 5′-ATG​GCA​GTG​GCA​ATG​TGC​ATG​AAA​C-3′
Primers flanking the ALCAM enhancer were designed and obtained from Integrated DNA Technologies as follows:
Forward: 5′-AAA​GGT​ACC​ACC​TTC​TCT​CCT​CCC​ACT​GCC-3′
Reverse: 5′-AAA​CTC​GAG​CTC​TTA​AGA​GGC​CTC​CTT​GTT​CTG​AAT​G-3′
Genomic DNA was extracted from 1 × 106 SK-N-BE2C cells using the Monarch Genomic DNA Purification Kit (NEB, #T3010S). PCR amplification of genomic DNA was performed using the above primers. For the ALCAM promoter, we used Q5 High-Fidelity DNA Polymerase (NEB, #M0491S) according to the manufacturer’s instructions, with a two-step PCR protocol, 35 cycles of extension at 72°C, and a five-minute extension time per cycle. For the ALCAM enhancer, we used Platinum SuperFi II DNA Polymerase (Thermo Fisher Scientific, #12361010) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. PCR products were purified using QIAquick PCR & Gel Cleanup Kit (QIAGEN, #28704) and then restriction digested with SacI-HF (NEB, #R31156S) and XhoI (NEB, #R0146S) or KpnI-HF (NEB, #R3142S) and XhoI. After heat inactivation, samples were separated on a 1% agarose gel run at 100V for approximately 2 hours and then purified using QIAquick PCR and Gel Cleanup Kit as above. Additionally, corresponding vector backbones were digested with the same enzymes to produce complementary overhangs. Ligation was performed using T4 DNA Ligase (NEB, #M0202S) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Bacterial transformation was performed using One Shot OmniMAX 2 T1 competent cells (Thermo Fisher Scientific, #C854003), and plasmid DNA was extracted using a Plasmid Mini Kit (QIAGEN, #12125). Sequence verification was performed using Sanger sequencing. Finally, an additional ALCAM enhancer construct was created with a 6-bp substitution for the putative AP-1 site (AGTGAC to AAAAAA) and obtained as a gene fragment from TWIST Bioscience, with the same restriction overhangs as the initial enhancer construct.
For all experiments, cells were plated at ∼70% confluence in tissue culture–treated 96-well white/clear bottom plates (Thermo Fisher Scientific, #12-566-71). After 24 hours, cells were transfected with reporter constructs (250 ng/well) and firefly controls (50 ng/well) using Lipofectamine 3000. The next day, treatments were added as indicated. Otherwise, plates were incubated for an additional 48 hours before data were collected using the Nano-Glo Dual-Luciferase Reporter Assay System (Promega, #N1630). Plates were read using a Promega GloMax Discover Microplate reader. Luminescence values were normalized within well to firefly control for promoter experiments, as well as empty vector control for enhancer experiments.

## Western blotting
Protein was extracted from cell pellets using the RIPA lysis buffer system (Santa Cruz, #sc-24948) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Protein concentration was determined using BCA Protein Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, #23225). Approximately 20 μg protein was prepared in 4× Laemmli Sample Buffer (Bio-Rad, #1610747) supplemented with 2-mercaptoethanol (Bio-Rad, #1610710) at a 9:1 ratio. Samples were run on a 4% to 15% Tris-glycine gel (Bio-Rad, #5671085) at 200V for approximately 1 hour using Tris-Glycine Buffer (Bio-Rad, #1610771) containing 0.1% SDS. Samples were transferred in ice-cold Tris-Glycine Buffer containing 20% methanol using the Bio-Rad transfer system at 50 V for approximately 1 hour to a 0.45-μm Immobilon-P membrane (Millipore Sigma, #IPVH00010). Blocking was performed for 1 hour at room temperature in 5% (w/v) blotting-grade milk (Bio-Rad, #170-6404) in TBS with Tween 20 (TBST, Cell Signaling Technology, #9997). Antibodies were prepared in 5% w/v milk in TBST, and samples were incubated overnight at 4°C. Antibodies used for Western blotting include (from Cell Signaling Technology at a dilution of 1:1,000 unless otherwise noted): ALCAM (RID: AB_2936926, Abcam, #ab109215, 1:5,000), GAPDH (RRID: AB_2107445, Millipore Sigma, #MAB374, 1:10,000), β-actin (RRID: AB_330288, #4967S, 1:5,000), MYC (RRID: AB_3632258, #9402S), MYCN (RRID: AB_1564849, #84406S), and PARP (RRID: AB_2160739, #9542S). Membranes were washed three times in TBST before addition of secondary antibody (Invitrogen, #A16110; #31432; 1:5,000) prepared in 5% (w/v) blotting-grade milk in TBST for 1 hour at room temperature. Three more washes with TBST were then performed. Western blots were developed using SuperSignal West Femto Maximum Sensitivity Substrate (Thermo Fisher Scientific, #34095) and the Azure Sapphire Biomolecular Imager using automatic exposure detection.

## Flow cytometry
Approximately 1 × 106 cells were collected for each flow cytometry experiment. Cells were detached using versene (0.02% EDTA in HBSS), washed with PBS +1% FBS (henceforth FACS buffer), and stained for 30 minutes on ice in the dark with ALCAM/CD166 PE-conjugated antibody (RRID: AB_2223887, R&D Systems, FAB6561P) at a 1:100 dilution. After 30 minutes, cells were washed twice with 3 mL FACS buffer, once with PBS, and fixed for 30 minutes in 1% formalin. For Ki-67 staining, after washing, cells were fixed and permeabilized by adding 70% ice-cold ethanol dropwise under constant vortexing and then stored at −20°C until downstream analysis. Then, cells were washed three times with 3 mL FACS buffer and stained with anti–Ki-67 BV421 (RRID: AB_2686897, Fisher scientific, #562899) for 30 minutes. Cells were then flowed on a Beckman Coulter CytoFlex LX (6 laser) flow cytometer at the Children’s Hospital of Philadelphia Flow Cytometry Core. Data analysis was performed using the FlowJo v10 software (RRID: SCR_008520).

## Cell growth assays
Cell growth assays were conducted on the Incucyte SX5 Live-Cell Monitoring System (RRID: SCR_026298) at 37°C in 5% CO2. ALCAM CRISPRi cell lines were cultured for 1 week in RPMI medium (as above) containing doxycycline (2 μg/mL) or DMSO (0.02%). Cells were detached using versene and seeded in quadruplicate in 24-well tissue culture–treated plates (Corning, #3524) at a density of 5,000 cells/well in either doxycycline or DMSO-containing RPMI. Cells were cultured on the Incucyte until controls reached ∼90 to 100% confluence (200–400 hours). Images were captured every 1 to 3 hours using the whole-well imaging module and 4× objective. Analysis definitions were created using approximately 5 to 10 representative images from each cell line covering each condition, using default settings. Data were then exported and plotted using GraphPad Prism 9 (RRID: SCR_000306).

## Scratch-wound assays
Scratch-wound migration assays were conducted on the Incucyte SX5 Live-Cell Monitoring System at 37°C in 5% CO2. ALCAM CRISPRi cells were cultured and seeded according to the same protocol used for cell growth assays with the following differences. For scratch-wound assays, cells were seeded at ∼95% to 100% confluence in a 96-well Incucyte Imagelock 96-well plate (Sartorius, #BA-04856). Prior to seeding, plates were coated for 1 hour at 37°C with 150 μg/mL fibronectin (Sigma-Aldrich, #F1141-2MG) diluted in PBS. Fibronectin was aspirated, and cells were seeded in full-serum RPMI and allowed to adhere overnight. The next morning, cells were washed with 200 μL/well of serum-free RPMI containing 2 mmol/L L-glutamine, and the Incucyte Woundmaker tool was prepared according to the manufacturer’s instructions by washing for 5 minutes in 70% ethanol, followed by 5 minutes in sterile water. After wounding, plates were washed again with 200 μL/well of serum-free RPMI to remove debris, and then 200 μL/well of serum-free RPMI containing 0.02% DMSO or 2 μg/mL doxycycline were added to each well. Plates were imaged on the Incucyte using the scratch-wound assay module (RRID: SCR_017316) using the manufacturer’s recommended settings, and an analysis definition was created as for the cell growth assays.

## Cell viability assays
ADC (CX-090), isotype control [ChKTi (IgG)-DM4], and DM4 were obtained from CytomX Therapeutics, Inc., corresponding to the sequence of praluzatamab ravtansine ( ). Cells were plated at approximately 60% confluence in tissue culture–treated 96-well white/clear bottom plates (Thermo Fisher Scientific, #12-566-71) in RPMI-1640 medium containing 10% FBS and 2 mmol/L L-glutamine and allowed to adhere overnight. The next day, fivefold serial dilution series were created for each drug and added to the plate in duplicate for each dose. After 5 days, CellTiter-Glo reagent (Promega, #G7571) was added at a 1:1 media to reagent ratio to each well. Plates were agitated for 2 minutes at 350 rpm, incubated for 10 minutes at room temperature, and luminescence was read using the Promega GloMax Discover Microplate reader. Luminescence values for each treatment was normalized to the untreated control. GrapPhad Prism 9 was used to plot normalized viability curves and determine IC50 values.

## Cell line–derived xenograft generation and tumor growth curves
To generate subcutaneous CDX models of CRISPRi cell lines, 10-15e6 cells from each transduced cell line were injected in the right flank of 6- to 8-week-old female NOD/SCID gamma mice (RRID: IMSR_JAX:005557, The Jackson Laboratory, strain# 005557) mixed with Matrigel (Corning, cat. #354234). Mice were monitored for tumor establishment. Once the tumors reached a size of 0.5 to 0.7 cm3, equal-sized tumor chunks were engrafted into the flanks of each study mouse (n = 6/arm). To induce ALCAM depletion in vivo, experimental groups were fed Purina Mouse Chow with 625 ppm doxycycline (Research Diets, #C13510Gi). Tumor growth was followed by measuring tumor sizes twice a week. Satellite tumors were harvested at approximately 0.5 to 0.7 cm3 for Western blot and processed as previously articulated. Mice were maintained under barrier conditions, and experiments were conducted using protocols approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee at the Children’s Hospital of Philadelphia.

## Conditionally activated ADC efficacy studies
Probody–drug conjugate (CX-2009; praluzatamab ravtansine), ADC (CX-090), isotype control [ChKTi (IgG)-DM4], and DM4 were obtained from CytomX Therapeutics, Inc., corresponding to the sequence of praluzatamab ravtansine ( ). CB17 SCID female mice ages 6 to 8 weeks from Charles River Laboratories (RRID: IMSR_CRL:236, CB17/Icr-Prkdcscid/IcrIcoCrl, stock number 236, common name FOX CHASE SCID or CB17SCID) were used to propagate subcutaneous xenograft models. Mice were maintained under barrier conditions, and experiments were conducted using protocols approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee at the Children’s Hospital of Philadelphia. A total of 8 mice were randomized into one of four arms (vehicle, ChKTI-IgG-DM4, CX-090, or CX-2009) for a total of 2 mice per arm. This protocol was established after a retrospective analysis of 67 in vivo drug screening studies from the Pediatric Preclinical Testing Program determined that any single mouse accurately predicted the objective response rate in 95.28% of studies ( ). Tumors were grafted in 8- to 10-week-old SCID mice, treatment was initiated at enrollment when tumors reached a volume of 0.150 to 0.250 cm3, and drug was dosed subcutaneously at 5 mg/kg once per week for 4 weeks. Tumor volume and weight were measured twice weekly until tumors reached a size of 2.0 cm3.

## Results

## ALCAM is highly expressed in neuroblastoma
We first investigated ALCAM expression in snRNA-seq data obtained from a previous study conducted by our laboratory, in which we used snRNA-seq to compare transcriptomes of neuroblastoma patient tumors before and after chemotherapy ( ; ref. ). We found that ALCAM expression was high in all cell subtypes within the tumor, especially the immune cells, Schwann cells, and the neuroblastoma cells ( ). Because prior snRNA-seq and spatial omics studies have identified conflicting roles for ALCAM in the tumor microenvironment ( , ), we investigated ALCAM expression in tumor-associated macrophages (TAM; ) and found that ALCAM is expressed in all subtypes of macrophages, suggesting it is a pan-TAM marker ( ). Next, because ALCAM plays a critical role in the development of the central and peripheral nervous systems, we used the Developmental Programs in Childhood Neuroblastoma Data Visualizer to investigate ALCAM expression in single-cell RNA-seq data from fetal adrenal glands ( ). ALCAM expression was high across the developing adrenal gland but particularly high in neuroblasts, cycling neuroblasts, and late neuroblasts (Supplementary Fig. S1A and S1B), which have been suggested to be the cell type of origin of neuroblastoma ( ). We next investigated ALCAM expression in bulk RNA-seq data of neuroblastoma patient tumors by comparing its expression with tumors from The Cancer Genome Atlas, the UCSC Treehouse Cancer Initiative (Treehouse), and the Therapeutically Applicable Research to Generate Effective Treatments RNA-seq datasets (Supplementary Fig. S1C). ALCAM was more highly expressed in neuroblastoma than essentially all other pediatric and adult cancers. We also compared ALCAM expression in neuroblastoma with normal tissue expression from the GTEx database (Supplementary Fig. S1D), showing ubiquitous normal tissue mRNA expression, with higher ALCAM expression in thyroid, lung, and blood vessels than in neuroblastoma. Next, we performed immunoblotting in a panel of 24 neuroblastoma cell lines and showed that ALCAM was highly expressed in a majority, with some heterogeneity across models ( ). To further understand this heterogeneity, we quantified this Western blot and compared ALCAM protein expression in MYCN-amplified and nonamplified cell lines and found no difference in expression between groups (Supplementary Fig. S1E). Finally, we selected three cell lines with moderate–high ALCAM expression based on our immunoblotting data (SK-N-BE2C, SH-SY5Y, and NB-EBc1; henceforth referred to as BE2C, SY5Y, and EBC1, respectively) that represented a variety of genomic backgrounds and generated doxycycline-inducible CRISPRi cell lines ( , ) using the Fgh1tUTG sgRNA expression plasmid (AddGene, #70183) and lenti_dCas9-KRAB-MeCP2 plasmid (Addgene, #122205). Treatment with doxycycline induced robust ALCAM depletion that required 7 days ( ) with some expression heterogeneity ( ), with the long time course required for ALCAM depletion suggesting a long protein half-life.
 

## ALCAM modulates neuroblastoma cell proliferation and migration
To determine the effects of ALCAM depletion on neuroblastoma proliferation, we performed live-cell monitoring assays using the CRISPRi cell lines after ALCAM knockdown. Doxycycline treatment inhibited cell growth in in all three models to varying degrees with elimination of EBC1 cells ( ). Consistent with these findings, we found that ALCAM depletion increased cleaved PARP ( ), suggesting an increase in apoptosis, and reduced Ki-67 staining in all three cell lines ( ), correlating with our live-cell monitoring assays. Additionally, using live-cell monitoring, we profiled cell migration after ALCAM depletion in scratch-wound assays and found significantly decreased migration in all three cell lines ( and ). Next, we investigated the impact of ALCAM depletion in vivo using our CRISPRi cell lines. We found that doxycycline (625 ppm) chow led to robust ALCAM depletion in all three lines ( ), and ALCAM depletion delayed tumor growth in BE2C and EBC1, without affecting SY5Y tumors ( ). Together, these data suggest that ALCAM is an important regulator of cell growth, migration, and survival and is a mediator of apoptosis evasion in neuroblastoma.
 

## ALCAM is expressed in adrenergic and mesenchymal neuroblastomas
We next sought to determine the mechanism of ALCAM overexpression in neuroblastoma. We leveraged ChIP-seq data in BE2C targeting the members of the neuroblastoma core regulatory circuit (CRC), a suite of transcription factors forming an autoregulatory loop driving a proliferative adrenergic phenotype ( , – ). In contrast, mesenchymal neuroblastomas are thought to be slower to proliferate, more metastatic, and more chemotherapy-resistant. We found substantial CRC binding approximately 10 kb upstream of the ALCAM transcription start site, suggesting the presence of an enhancer element ( ). This prompted us to investigate ALCAM expression in adrenergic and mesenchymal neuroblastomas. Using an RNA-seq–based pipeline developed by our laboratory ( ), we found that ALCAM mRNA expression is significantly higher in adrenergic tumors and cell lines, although expression is maintained in mesenchymal tumors ( ). This is in contrast to bona fide adrenergic genes such as ALK and GPC2, whose mRNA expression is lost in mesenchymal neuroblastoma. Furthermore, in RNA-seq data obtained from inducible overexpression of the mesenchymal master regulator PRRX1 ( ), we found that ALCAM expression is fully maintained in the cell lines SK-N-BE(2) and KPNYN after PRRX1 overexpression ( ). An exploration of the epigenetic regulation of the ALCAM locus showed substantial H3K4me3 and H3K27ac in the ALCAM promoter and H3K27ac in the putative upstream enhancer in NB-1643, BE2C, and NB-LS, indicative of active gene expression and open chromatin. These cell lines also have among the highest ALCAM protein expression, consistent with these epigenetic markers ( ).
 

## MYCi975 impairs ALCAM transcription
Because we found binding of MYCN to the ALCAM promoter in BE2C in our examination of the CRC, we decided to more closely investigate ChIP-seq data targeting MYC and MYCN in a panel of neuroblastoma cell lines that are either MYCN-amplified (BE2C, NGP, COG-N-415, LAN-5, NB-1643, and Kelly) or MYC-overexpressing (NB-69, SK-N-SH, and SK-N-AS). Although we saw no difference in ALCAM protein expression between MYCN-amplified and nonamplified cell lines in our large panel Western blot ( ; Supplementary Fig. S1E), the ALCAM promoter showed substantial MYC and MYCN binding, comparable with MYCN binding to known MYCN targets, GPC2 and ODC1 (Supplementary Fig. S2A; ref. ), suggesting MYC(N) may be modulating ALCAM overexpression ( ). To characterize the effects of MYCN on ALCAM expression, we treated BE2C (MYCN-amplified) and EBC1 (MYCN-overexpressing) cell lines with the MYC(N)/MAX dimerization inhibitor MYCi975 for 1 to 9 days and performed immunoblotting targeting ALCAM and MYCN ( ). MYCi975 is a highly selective inhibitor of MYC(N) binding to MAX, impairing binding to MYC(N) pathway genes and their expression ( , ). Consistent with the ChIP-seq binding of MYCN to the ALCAM promoter, we found that MYCi975 significantly reduced ALCAM expression at 6 and 9 days ( ), which is a similar time course required for ALCAM depletion by CRISPRi. We next sought to determine whether treatment with MYCi975 directly impaired transcription at the ALCAM promoter. Thus, we generated a luciferase reporter construct containing a 1,604-bp fragment of the ALCAM promoter upstream of a PEST-domain destabilized nanoluciferase, which has a half-life of approximately 20 minutes. Treatment with MYCi975 after transfection with this construct in BE2C significantly reduced luciferase signal by 1 hour of treatment, suggesting MYCi975 directly disrupts ALCAM transcription ( and ).
 

## An upstream enhancer drives ALCAM expression
We used Promoter Capture-C and ATAC-seq to investigate the CRC-bound region ∼10 kb upstream of the ALCAM transcription start site. These data showed a region of open chromatin that was predicted to be an enhancer by the LILY algorithm ( ). Promoter Capture-C also showed that this region makes physical contact with the ALCAM promoter ( ). Next, we generated a 1,032-bp fragment of the region spanning the ATAC-seq region of open chromatin ( ; Supplementary Fig. S2B). Searching for this 1,032-bp sequence in the TRANSFAC transcription factor binding motif database revealed a predicted AP-1 motif within the ATAC-seq window (Supplementary Table S1), so we also generated an additional 1,032-bp fragment that lacked this AP-1 motif (AGTGAC to AAAAAA). These fragments were cloned into a PEST-domain destabilized nanoluciferase reporter construct containing a minimal promoter and transfected into NB-1643 and BE2C ( ). As a control for enhancer activity, we used a validated LMO1 enhancer sequence containing a GATA motif that we previous discovered in neuroblastoma ( ). We saw no activity of the ALCAM enhancer construct in BE2C, consistent with the minimal ATAC signal in that region ( and ). However, we saw robust signal in NB-1643, which was abolished after deletion of the AP-1 motif, suggesting there is a critical dependence on this AP-1 motif in this cell line for enhancer activity ( ). Finally, we generated doxycycline-inducible NB-1643 and BE2C CRISPRi cell lines targeting the enhancer region, including one guide RNA directly targeting the AP-1 motif ( ). Treatment with doxycycline depleted ALCAM protein expression in all three guide RNAs in NB-1643 ( ) but had no effect in BE2C, suggesting this enhancer region is one potential mechanism by which ALCAM expression is regulated.
 

## An ALCAM-targeting conditionally activated ADC is cytotoxic to neuroblastoma cells in vitro and in vivo
We next sought to investigate the therapeutic potential of an ALCAM-targeted conditionally activated ADC in neuroblastoma. PROBODY CX-2009, developed by CytomX Therapeutics, Inc., is designed with masking peptides linked via protease-cleavable linkers to the anti-ALCAM antibody. The masking peptides block the antigen binding domain. Upon liberation of the masking peptide by tumor-associated proteases, CX-2009 can bind to its target and mediate cytotoxicity ( , ). For in vitro assays, we used the unmasked version of CX-2009, CX-090, because of potentially limited expression of these proteases in cell culture and the presence of protease inhibitors in cell culture serum. We profiled cell-surface ALCAM expression using flow cytometry with CX-090, which confirmed specificity of CX-090 binding ( ). Additionally, we investigated ALCAM expression in our PDX models using previously published RNA-seq data and selected three models with high ALCAM expression for in vivo studies ( ). CX-090 demonstrated nanomolar to subnanomolar IC50 values in five cell lines, including two cell lines with TP53 mutations (BE2C and NB-SD; ; Supplementary Table S2). For in vivo experiments, treatment was performed weekly for 4 weeks with vehicle, isotype control (chKTi (IgG)-DM4), CX-090, or CX-2009. These studies were performing using the PPTC n = 2 mouse study design ( ). We saw no effect of either CX-090 or CX-2009 in COG-N-424x, whereas both Felix-PDX and EBC1 showed significant growth delay in response to CX-090, and EBC1 also showed a significant growth delay in response to CX-2009 ( ).
 

## Discussion
Relapsed high-risk neuroblastoma remains largely incurable despite advances in the field of immunotherapy ( – ). In this work, we sought to determine whether ALCAM is a suitable immunotherapy target in neuroblastoma. snRNA-seq data showed that ALCAM is expressed in all compartments of neuroblastoma patient tumors and is a pan-TAM marker. Consequently, we found that ALCAM is highly expressed in neuroblastoma cell lines, PDXs, and patient tumor samples and is more highly expressed in neuroblastoma than the majority of human cancers. Additionally, we found that ALCAM is highly expressed in the developing adrenal gland, including in the cell types thought to give rise to neuroblastoma ( ). This likely reflects the derivation of neuroblastoma from the developing sympathetic nervous system, in which ALCAM is critical for axon extension and retinal ganglion cell maturation ( – ). Using doxycycline-inducible CRISPRi, we showed that ALCAM depletion leads to diminished cell growth and migration and promotes apoptosis, consistent with findings from a variety of adult tumors such as prostate cancer and melanoma ( , , ). Because ALCAM is a cell adhesion molecule, depletion of ALCAM likely reduces cell–cell adhesion, which we showed impairs neuroblastoma cell growth and induces apoptosis. This is consistent with data showing that ALCAM inhibits apoptosis in a variety of cancer types, possibly by signaling through the PI3K/AKT pathway ( – ). Although loss of cell–cell adhesions often promotes metastasis (such as the loss of E-cadherin in the epithelial to mesenchymal transition), ALCAM has been previously established as promoting metastasis in melanoma and prostate cancer ( , ). Our scratch-wound migration data are consistent with this finding, which underscores the dynamic and subtle role that cell adhesion molecules play in metastasis. The fact that shed ALCAM retains the majority of its extracellular domain, including the domains that mediate homotypic interactions with ALCAM, may be one mechanism by which ALCAM promotes metastasis. Future studies of metastasis could leverage this fact using CRISPRi-mediated knockdown in vivo to investigate the impact of ALCAM depletion on metastasis. Additionally, we found that BE2C and EBC1, but not SY5Y, had impaired cell growth in vivo, in contrast to our in vitro data showing that all three cell lines had impaired growth. In the case of SY5Y, because we saw adequate depletion of ALCAM expression in these xenograft models, this heterogeneity in behavior likely reflects the importance of the cell adhesion role of ALCAM or the heterogeneity between models. This is especially true of EBC1, which grows more aggressively in vivo than in vitro, a fact that may explain why we saw only modest growth delay in our CDX system.
Because neuroblastomas harbor few somatic mutations, epigenetic dysregulation is a critical mechanism of tumor growth and survival ( ). This is reflected in the literature delineating the adrenergic and mesenchymal subtypes of neuroblastoma, each of which is driven by its own suites of transcription factors termed the CRC. Whereas adrenergic cells are thought to reflect the neuronal original of neuroblastoma (due to expression of genes such as TH and DBH), mesenchymal cells are slower growing and express gene programs characteristic of neuronal stem cells ( , – ). We found that ALCAM is no exception to this complexity; its expression is consistent with epigenetic marks that are present for actively transcribed genes, featuring substantial H3K4me3 and H3K27ac. Additionally, although we found no difference in ALCAM protein expression between MYCN-amplified and nonamplified cell lines, we also found that there is significant MYCN binding to the ALCAM promoter, similar to known MYCN targets GPC2 and ODC1, and we used the MYC(N)/MAX dimerization disruptor MYCi975 to show reduced ALCAM expression and luciferase signal in our luciferase reporter constructs containing the ALCAM promoter. We performed these experiments in BE2C (MYCN-amplified) and EBC1 (MYCN-overexpressing), although we also saw binding of MYC in MYC-overexpressing cell lines NB69, SKNSH, and SKNAS. MYC regulation may explain why our quantified panel Western blot showed no difference in ALCAM protein expression, and future studies might elucidate whether this mechanism of overexpression is MYCN specific, or shared between MYC and MYCN.
Additionally, a region 10 kb upstream of the transcription start site showed robust binding by the CRC, as well as a physical interaction with the ALCAM promoter. Previous work has identified significant enrichment of CRC transcription factors TBX2, PHOX2B, HAND2, and GATA3 binding in enhancer regions ( ). However, despite this binding, ALCAM expression is reduced but not absent in mesenchymal neuroblastoma, in contrast to complete loss of ALK and GPC2 mRNA expression ( ), suggesting the mechanism may be more complicated than simple subtype delineation. Indeed, our enhancer luciferase reporter constructs validated this complexity. Loss of the predicted AP-1 motif eradicated luciferase signal in NB-1643, whereas BE2C showed no enhancer signal at all, regardless of AP-1 status. This surprising finding may be explained by lack the lack of ATAC signal in this region in BE2C, or lack of expression of AP-1 family transcription factors in BE2C compared with NB-1643, and reflects the dynamic nature of the adrenergic and mesenchymal subtypes. Conversely, NB-1643 harbors an activating mutation in the oncogene ALK (R1275Q), whereas BE2C does not. Studies in anaplastic large-cell lymphoma, which frequently shows expression of a constitutively active nucleoplasmin–ALK fusion protein, have demonstrated a direct link between constitutive ALK signaling and expression of cJun, an AP-1 family transcription factor. This may explain why we saw activity of the ALCAM enhancer in only NB-1643 ( ). Furthermore, there may be deeper complexity in the regulation of this putative enhancer, wherein AP-1 family transcription factors facilitate the binding of CRC transcription factors. Together, these data illustrate the complexity of the mechanism of ALCAM expression, suggesting it is multi-faceted and heterogenous across cell lines. Further studies may elucidate the potential cross-talk between these transcriptional mechanisms.
Finally, our in vitro and in vivo data show the potential preclinical efficacy of a novel conditionally active ADC targeting ALCAM. CX-2009 has a masking peptide that blocks antigen binding until cleavage by tumor-associated proteases, allowing for the targeting of proteins that are also expressed in normal tissues ( ). Because the unmasked ADC, CX-090, does not bind mouse ALCAM, we were unable to assess on-target, off-tumor toxicity in our models. However, toxicity was investigated in a phase I/II clinical trial of CX-2009, which showed that 12% of patients required discontinuation of therapy, of which five were because of ocular-related toxicity without evidence of on-target, off-tumor activity ( ). Despite the relatively high DM4 IC50 values in vitro in several of our cell lines, we saw significant impaired tumor growth in EBC1 and Felix-PDX in vivo. Importantly, the payload of CX-2009, the maytansinoid DM4, is one of the least efficacious payloads in neuroblastoma ( ). Conversely, DNA-damaging payloads show far more potency against neuroblastoma in vitro and in vivo ( , ). Thus, the failure to eradicate COG-N-424x, or to achieve more potent IC50 values in vitro, may not reflect a failure of target selection. This fact is further highlighted by the subpicomolar potency of DM4 against EBC1 in vitro and the success of CX-090 and CX-2009 against EBC1 in vivo. Differential sensitivity of Felix to CX-090 and CX-2009 may further reflect a lack of relevant protease expression in vivo, although direct assessment of unmasking potential would be required, using techniques such as zymography. Notably, previous work has shown that the DNA-damaging agent PBD is highly efficacious against neuroblastoma because of its ability to target tumor-associate vasculature ( , ), and our snRNA-seq data showed that ALCAM is expressed in all tumor microenvironment subtypes, including endothelial cells and TAMs.
In the context of anti-GD2 therapy, ALCAM-targeting by therapies such as CX-2009 may represent attractive alternative strategies due to its ubiquitous expression across epigenetic states, as well as its promotion of neuroblastoma proliferation. However, the fact that ALCAM is highly expressed in normal tissues is a significant limitation for therapeutic development. Probody–drug conjugates targeting ALCAM, as well as EGFR and CD71, have shown promising preclinical efficacy that validate this modality for further development in neuroblastoma ( , ). Future therapies, such as recently developed AND-gated CAR T cells ( ), may also facilitate targeting of ALCAM. Previous work has identified both scFv-based and CD6 extracellular domain–based CAR T cells that show efficacy in osteosarcoma and colorectal cancer models ( , ). These CAR platforms may be adaptable to AND-gated technologies, alongside rational selection of target pairs. Potential partners for AND-gated CAR development would likely require limited normal tissue expression, such as ALK or GPC2, although deeper investigation using normal tissue expression data, such as GTEx, may elucidate additional novel target pairs for ALCAM that do not have neuroblastoma-exclusive expression. Future studies could leverage these normal tissue data to identify genes that are not coexpressed in normal tissue but are coexpressed in neuroblastoma. Together, these data credential ALCAM as an attractive candidate for future dual-targeting immunotherapy development in neuroblastoma.
