## Abstract
The many surface
proteins on extracellular vesicles (EVs) allow
them to target recipient cells and modulate cellular responses. Despite
their importance, relating surface protein and EV function is challenging
due to surface protein heterogeneity. Here, we create a bottom-up,
cell-free protein-synthesis platform to engineer artificial nanovesicles
(ANVs) that display different EV surface protein domains. The platform
is termed VESSEL (Vesicle Engineering Systems using Synthetic Expression and Loading). The surface proteins are selected based on proteomics data
of native EVs from placental mesenchymal stem cells (PMSCs). To create
VESSEL, we establish a protein anchor based on the bacteria membrane
protein Aquaporin-Z. This anchor allows the flexible and cell-free
protein synthesis of 39 different EV surface protein domains, each
anchoring into more than 108 ANVs per μL. Furthermore,
we measure the ANVs using high-fidelity assays, including single-ANV
flow cytometry, super-resolution imaging, and vesicle-based ELISA.
Next, we show the impact of each EV surface protein on cellular uptake.
Specifically, we find that certain EV surface protein domains govern
ANV uptake into HEK293FT cells, explaining the variable observations
in the field. We discovered new proteins, such as CADM1 and NPTN,
that mediate high-efficiency cellular uptake. Additionally, five proteins
were selected for our neuroprotection assay, where three proteins
were significant in increasing SH-SY5Y neurite growth. Our work demonstrates
a high-throughput cell-free synthesis platform for studying surface
proteins of EVs. It enables the systematic interrogation of EV’s
function as “signalosomes” and facilitates the designing
of well-defined EV mimetics to mediate cellular function.
Keywords: Cell-Free Protein Synthesis, Nanovesicle, Protein
Anchor, Neuroprotection, Cellular Uptake

## Introduction
Although extracellular vesicle (EV) surface proteins have been
recognized as key mediators of function, their heterogeneous expression
and lack of scalable engineering approaches have hindered precise
functional studies.
−

 EV surface proteins perform functions that were once thought to
be solely due to the cargo, such as neuroprotection, immunomodulation,
and tissue repair.
−

 Previously, we showed that when surface galectin-1 (GAL-1) is blocked
using a neutralizing antibody, neuroprotective function of native
EVs derived from placental mesenchymal stem cells (PMSCs) is decreased,
indicating the neuroprotective function is mediated by the protein
present on the EV membrane. In terms
of therapeutic applications, previous work has modified EVs by introducing
new proteins to the vesicle surface including CD9, CD63, LAMP-2B,
and tLyp-1.
−

 However, native EVs are known to be heterogeneous
in the proteins present on a single EV.
,
 As a result,
it is unclear which surface proteins are therapeutically relevant
or essential for which functions. For instance, CD9 was thought to
enhance EV uptake into cells. However,
Tognoli et al. recently showed that CD9 and CD63, another common EV
surface protein, are not needed for cell uptake.
,
 The difficulty in dissecting the specific functions of individual
EV proteins has significantly hindered EV research and applications.
,

One method of studying EV-protein function is by knocking
it down
in the parent cell or inhibiting it through peptides or antibodies.
,,
 This effectively blocks the proteins’
ability to interact with the targets, generating a loss of function.
An alternate method is the addition of the protein to native EVs or
the synthesis of defined EVs. Native EV protein addition is typically
done through modification of the parent cell. Such work, however, still utilizes native EVs with high protein
heterogeneity. To overcome this challenge, defined or synthetic EVs
could be created from the bottom up with well-defined protein composition. For instance, Staufer et al. generated water-in-oil
liposomes and attached recombinant proteins to the liposome surface
through HIS tag binding. However, synthetic
EVs primarily utilize recombinant proteins, which can be expensive
or difficult to scale up to study the many surface proteins of native
EVs.
,−

 To accelerate the study
of EV surface proteins, a scalable and rapid method is critically
needed to generate synthetic EVs with defined surface proteins.
To this end, cell-free protein synthesis is a potential approach
that uses cellular machinery to generate proteins without cells. Surface
proteins can be synthesized in the presence of liposomes, allowing
cotranslational insertion into the lipid bilayers. This approach can simplify the study of EV surface proteins,
because the proteins can be generated and inserted into the synthetic
vesicles in one step. This has direct benefits over other protein
conjugation methods, as in theory multiple different protein variants
can be made in parallel, greatly increasing the output. Tremendous
work has used cell-free systems to synthesize membrane proteins on
supported lipid bilayers and giant unilamellar liposomes,
−

 but few have focused on the size range of natural EVs (diameter
∼ 100 nm). Nomura et al. used
commercial wheat germ cell-free systems to display Apo Cytochrome
B5 on large proteo-liposomes, but produced vesicles in the micrometer
range. The cell-free approach benefits
from the ease and speed at which different proteins can be generated
and tested on a liposome membrane; however, testing these proteins
at a different size range does not provide translatable function association
between the artificial nanovesicles (ANVs) and native EVs. Additionally,
cell-free protein display is hindered by the reaction condition optimization
required for every protein tested.
,
 This can decrease
the scalability of cell-free protein expression and reduce its advantage
for parallel, multiprotein studies. To harness the potential of cell-free
protein expression to create ANVs, a plug-and-play solution that functionalizes
vesicles in the correct size range must be generated.
Here,
we create a modular platform that integrates E. coli cell-free protein synthesis and liposomes to study EV surface proteins.
Our study utilizes the cell-free protein synthesis machinery outside
of the liposome, thereby utilizing an outside-in generation approach.
This approach allows for unimpeded access to the membrane. Furthermore,
as the soluble protein domain is designed at the C-terminal, the synthesized
protein is biased to be displayed in our desired form – anchor
domain in membrane, with surface proteins at the outside of the liposomes.
We use a new protein anchor to display the extracellular domain of
select proteins on well-defined liposomes. To focus our study on the
protein interactions, our platform utilizes a simple membrane composition
to disregard charge, native lipid composition, and other biophysical
properties. The simplistic membrane resulted in defined ANVs that
mimic the size of native EVs, reduce protein heterogeneity down to
a single protein, and solely focus on the protein function. The anchor
protein allows a single synthesis condition to generate different
ANVs. Our work enables the largest-scale study of EV surface proteins
to date: > 39 proteins found on native PMSC EVs. All 39 protein-ANV
functions are tested through HEK293FT cell uptake as a proof of concept.
Additionally, some ANVs are tested for uptake and neuroprotective
capabilities in a well-established neuroprotection model using SH-SY5Y
neuroblastoma cells. Our platform can rapidly prototype ANVs with
defined surface proteins for downstream therapeutic work.

## Results
2.1.

## Established Artificial Nanovesicles and Cell-Free
Protein Synthesis Systems
We created the VESSEL (Vesicle Engineering Systems using Synthetic Expression and Loading) system
as a platform to simplify the study of EV protein function by reducing
heterogeneity to just a single species of displayed protein. VESSEL
allows researchers to take native EV proteomics data, determine key
EV membrane or soluble proteins of interest, generate them rapidly
through cell-free protein synthesis, and perform functional tests
on these EV mimetics, termed artificial nanovesicles (ANVs) ( A). To generate ANVs,
a vesicle chassis that mimics the sizing of native EVs was needed.
We generated small liposomes through sonication, as their speed of
generation and fully customizable composition were important for this
study. We utilized two different liposome compositions, one containing
99.85% DOPC and 0.15% CY5 PE, and the other containing 98.85% POPC,
0.15% CY5 PE, and 1% biotinylated PE. DOPC and POPC were selected
for their biocompatibility and previous use in studies to mimic eukaryotic
membranes (see Section ).
−

 CY5 PE lipids
were used for detection for filtering, normalization, and uptake studies,
while the biotinylated PE lipids were utilized for strep-tag attachment.
POPC lipids were used with biotinylated PE to provide better tail
length matching. While there is a recent interest in the role of lipids
in EV function, we kept our compositions simple to focus on the role
of the displayed protein.

 Thin film hydration and sonication were used to create synthetic
vesicles. Our nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA) showed the size
of the DOPC vesicles, in which the majority fall within the 50–150
nm diameter that is seen with native EVs (see ) ( B). We used
NTA and dynamic light scattering (DLS) to measure the size, zeta potential,
and polydispersity index (PDI) of these DOPC vesicles in their native
form, after cell-free mixture addition and protein generation and
then after SEC and Amicon filtering (Figure S1). Using NTA, we observed that the size of our liposomes increased
upon addition of the cell-free system but did not further increase
upon addition of a protein to the ANV surface. The liposome size also
increased after filtering, but there was no significant difference
in the liposome size after the addition of a membrane protein. Across
all tested conditions, the liposome size was consistently within the
50–150 nm range observed for native EVs. This size range is
slightly increased when analyzed using DLS. However, there was no
significant difference between the native liposomes and their cell-free
modified and filtered counterparts. Using DLS, we measured the PDI
and found no significant difference among the samples. The PDI range
was 0.3 to 0.4. Our ANV falls within a PDI range generally considered
moderately polydisperse and aligns with the reported PDI of other
native EVs.
−

 Finally, the zeta potential was investigated. We
observed a decrease in the zeta potential across all of our samples
after cell-free modification. The zeta potential observed after cell-free
modification agrees with other reported zeta potential values for
native EVs.
,
 This decrease in zeta potential
is likely due to both the displayed protein on the surface as well
as the formation of a potential protein corona from our cell-free
system. The effect of this protein corona is accounted for throughout
this work by adding cell-free and liposome conditions as the standard
control. Additionally, we did not observe any hindrance to protein
display from this potential corona. We also did not observe any effect
on cell viability (Figure S2). Circularity
was validated through super-resolution microscopy, where our DOPC
vesicles were imaged with a circular phenotype (see Section ) ( C). Cryo-EM was used to validate the circularity and lamellarity
of our formed vesicles (see Section ) ( D). While circularity
was achieved, some vesicles were multilamellar in nature (Figure S3). The multilamellar particles did not
affect our subsequent studies due to our outside-in protein generation,
meaning that cell-free synthesized proteins were localized to the
outer membranes. Altogether, our ANVs retained the native EV size
range, despite their multilamellar nature.

With the size and circularity confirmed, we next tackled the
filtering
and purification procedures of the VESSEL platform. These steps maintained
each protein bound to the surface of the vesicles by removing any
nonbound proteins. Size exclusion chromatography (SEC) was used to
capture the 50–150 nm vesicles while removing any free proteins
(see Section ). Amicon filtration was utilized
to reconcentrate the vesicles for in vitro tests.
To confirm vesicle elution, an elution profile was performed using
plate reader analysis of the DOPC–CY5 positive liposomes ( E). Plate reader
analysis showed that the vesicles elute between 1000 and 1500 μL
of PBS. This 500 μL amount was then filtered through a 100 kDa
Amicon filter to reconcentrate the ANVs. This method generated double-filtered
vesicles, with SEC removing free proteins and Amicon removing proteins
smaller than 100 kDa.
To enable rapid protein synthesis, we
used our custom cell-free
protein synthesis system. Our cell-free
system can synthesize proteins as large as 159 kDa, which cannot be
achieved using chemical peptide synthesis.
,
 In this system, one bacteria strain (BL21-DE3 star) was grown and
sonicated to harvest the intracellular machinery needed for protein
synthesis (see Section ). The machinery
was mixed with a plasmid coding for the protein of interest. In a
< 6-h reaction, this protein was synthesized without cells ( F). Before adding
the protein to vesicles, we first benchmarked our cell-free system.
We generated GFP with two different whole cell extract (WCE) batches
and utilized plate reader analysis to quantify the protein concentration,
which was around 0.4 mg mL–1 ( G).

2.2.

## Aquaporin-Z Provides Robust
Cotranslational
Insertion into ANV
With both a platform to generate liposomes
that mimic native EVs physical characteristics and a platform to synthesize
proteins without the need for cells, we integrated these to display
proteins on ANVs. Synthesizing full-length membrane proteins in a
cell-free system is challenging, as each protein can require a unique
salt condition that is needed for synthesis and folding. We circumvented
this problem through two different modifications of the protein design.
The first was the elimination of the transmembrane domain and the
replacement of it with an experimentally determined universal membrane
anchor. In replacing the transmembrane domain, this design removes
the trial and error of having proteins self-inserting into the membrane
by utilizing a standardized domain (see Section ). The second change is to use only the extracellular domain
of the EV protein. There have been several studies that show membrane
proteins can retain their function after isolating the extracellular
domain.
−

 To simplify the attachment of our proteins to our
ANVs, we used only the extracellular domain because these domains
dictate EV-protein interaction in cell uptake and targeting. To ensure the correct functional domain of the
protein is used, only single-pass membrane proteins were utilized
for this study, except three tetraspanins (CD9, CD63 and CD81) and
two soluble proteins (HGF and GAL-1). These two modifications both
standardized the plasmid assembly and eliminated the complex chemical
conditions for cotranslation insertion.
For the universal protein
anchor, we tested three different anchors to find the most compatible
solution. The first was the transmembrane domain of Transferrin, a
human blood protein (Figure S4). The second was Aquaporin-Z (AQP-Z), an E. coli water channel protein which has previously been
used within cell-free protein synthesis liposome display (Figure S5).
−

 Both proteins allowed
cotranslational insertion. As the protein was being synthesized, it
was inserted and folded into the liposome membrane. Utilizing self-inserting
membrane domains provided a robust anchor as no other proteins or
material was needed for anchoring. The third method was strep-tag,
which worked by generating strep-tagged proteins binding to streptavidin,
which then bound to biotinylated lipids on the liposome surface (Figure S6). The
strep-tag anchor system worked by attaching the protein after they
were generated and did not rely on transmembrane protein folding and
cotranslational insertion ( A).
 To validate the insertion and
robustness of each anchor, each anchor
was generated with the second extracellular domain of CD9, filtered
through SEC and Amicon filtration, and then a Western blot was performed
(see Section ) ( B). The 6X HIS tag at the C terminus of CD9
was the target for all tests. Both Transferrin and Aquaporin-Z showed
strong bands on the blot, while the strep-tag signal was lost (Figure S7). The strep tag signal loss was likely
due to the need for multiple binding partners, leading to a significantly
lower robust protein display. Quantification of these Western blots
showed that Aquaporin-Z and Transferrin had significant display of
11 and 15 μg mL–1 of protein respectively
( C). To further
validate the ANVs, we performed ELISA quantification, where Transferrin
and Aquaporin-Z once again showed significant protein display when
compared to the normalized control; Strep-tag once again had no signal
(see Section ) ( D) (Figure S8).
Next, single nanoparticle flow cytometry was performed for a larger
population analysis using a FITC anti6x HIS tag antibody (see Section ) (Figures S9–S10). Transferrin and Aquaporin-Z resulted in around 45–50% of
the total liposome population featuring detectable amounts of displayed
protein ( E).
Finally, super-resolution microscopy was utilized to quantify the
total number of proteins displayed per vesicle (see Section ). All ANV variants showed detectable amounts
of displayed protein ( F) (Figure S11). Next, we determined
whether there were any differences between our membrane anchors in
terms of detected protein display. We used the ONI super resolution
Nanoimager to image single-molecule localizations of the proteins. We used the images to compare localizations
of our membrane anchors (by Alexa488-HIS antibody) and lipids (CY5-DOPC/CY5-POPC-Biotinyl).
To examine sample loading, we analyzed only CY5 signals and did not
observe any differences among our samples ( G). Next, we colocalized Alexa488 and CY5.
Once again, Transferrin and Aquaporin-Z showed statistically significant
differences when compared to the negative controls. Both membrane
anchors had an average of 40–50 higher localizations than the
controls and Strep-Tag. Of the three membrane anchors, we proceeded
with Aquaporin-Z due to its amount of vesicle display and the robustness
of protein display after filtering.

2.3.

## VESSEL
Allows for Scaling Out of Protein-ANV
Variants
To demonstrate the modularity of our platform, we
utilized PMSC EV proteomics data and selected the top 140 proteins
based on their abundance. From this list,
single-pass transmembrane proteins were selected, and each extracellular
domain of the proteins was isolated. Golden gate synthesis was utilized
to construct all the plasmids (see Section ). Golden gate, coupled with the anchor-protein approach, allowed
for the rapid and flexible creation of ANV that displays any protein
domains of interest without the size limitation chemical-based protein
synthesis methods have ( A).
 We generated 39 different protein-ANVs
and examined them for display
efficacy. (see Section ). All proteins,
except for TM154, were classified into 4 generalized categories based
on annotated protein function (Supplementary Table 1). TM154 was left uncategorized due to its unknown function
in the literature. To validate the robust display of the proteins
on ANV, we performed a custom ELISA on filtered ANV (see Section ) ( B). 38 of 39 ANVs were statistically significant for protein
display when compared to the cell-free only control. One exception
was CLMP-ANV, which showed a significantly lower display percentage.
With a more sensitive method like single nanoparticle flow cytometry,
all 39 ANV variants showed statistically significant display percentages
(see Section ) ( C). All ANV variants featured detectable
protein on 45–65% of the total liposome population, except
for CLMP-ANV, which had only around 20% display on ANV. Using Aquaporin-Z
as a membrane anchor and tagging extracellular domains of proteins
to Aquaporin-Z allowed for the quick expansion of tested proteins.
These proteins, except for CLMP-ANV, are expressed similarly on the
surface of liposomes. Therefore, this platform can be applied to a
library of EV proteins while retaining a similar percentage of vesicles
with surface expression of the proteins.

2.4.

## VESSEL
Enables Systematic Study of Extracellular
Vesicle Protein Function in HEK293FT Uptake Assays
With the
construction of all 39 protein-ANVs using our VESSEL platform, we
proceeded to test the protein function. With a wide range of vesicles,
all spanning a minimum of 4 different functional groupings, there
was no one assay that could encompass all protein functions. Ultimately,
we decided to investigate uptake into HEK293FT cells (see Sections , ). Extracellular
vesicle uptake into cells is relatively low, and modifying the uptake
effectiveness of these vesicles is a well-studied field. HEK293FT was selected for its availability,
ease of cultivation, and its widespread use within extracellular vesicle
studies. VESSEL provides a clear benefit
within this niche as the protein–protein interactions that
facilitate EV uptake can be characterized systematically, and new
protein targets for native EV uptake can be discovered.
Our
model of EV uptake involved taking the 39 ANV variants, dosing them
into cultures of HEK293FT cells, and after a 4 h incubation, imaging
the cells using confocal microscopy and detaching cells for cell-based
flow cytometry (see Section ) ( A). In our confocal
microscopy, we visualized CY5-positive ANV variant uptake into HEK293FT
cells, especially in LAMP1-ANV ( B). This imaging was performed for all doses and proteins
that were tested (Figures S12–16). The doses tested were 20,000, 4000, and 2000 ANV/cell, denoted
as high, medium, and low, respectively. For whole population analysis,
the HEK293FT cells were trypsinized, and cell-based flow cytometry
was performed ( C). All uptake flow cytometry data shown was placed through multiple
gates, removing the debris, doublet cells, and dead cells, resulting
in uptake data for single, alive cells (Figures S17–18). Of all 39 proteins, 32 of them were at least
statistically significant for uptake percentage, with a majority being
statistically significant for both uptake and geometric mean at the
medium (4000 ANV/Cell) dose. The 7 proteins not statistically significant
were CD9-ANV, JAM1-ANV, CD81-ANV, GAL-1-ANV, SHPS1-ANV, BASI-ANV and
AMGO2-ANV (Supplementary Table 2). CD9-ANV,
JAM1-ANV, CD81-ANV and GAL-1-ANV have the lowest uptake of all proteins
tested. SHPS1-ANV, BASI-ANV and AMGO2-ANV, while having higher uptake
than several proteins, have high variability in their function. One
notable protein that was not statistically significant was CD9-ANV,
with a just 10% uptake. This provides supporting evidence that CD9
does not play a major role in the HEK293FT vesicle uptake. CD81-ANV,
another tetraspanin, had a low uptake of just 15%. Due to these two
proteins in similar classes having such low uptake, CD9 can be ruled
out as a protein that mediates HEK293FT uptake. This data corroborates
with the claim made by Tognoli et al., which utilized a HEK293FT model
to study EV uptake, and found that CD9 is not essential for EV Uptake. At the medium dose, all proteins covered the
entire range of uptake effectiveness, from LAMP1-ANV which was taken
up into 82.73% of HEK293FT cells, to CD9-ANV with only 10.05% of cellular
uptake. The results highlight the power of VESSEL, in eliminating
combinational effects or other surface features, and the pure EV protein
effect on uptake can be quantified.
 VESSEL additionally benefits from the fact that it is a bottom-up
synthetic platform, allowing for customization. We tested this customization
by reducing the plasmid concentration in the cell-free reactions to
half (0.5X) and a quarter (0.25X) of its initial concentration (see
Section ). We validated this with 2 proteins,
ITM2C and CADM1, and first performed single nanoparticle flow cytometry
(see Section ) ( D). Both proteins showed a decrease in displayed
protein percentage as the plasmid concentration decreased to half
(0.5X) and a quarter (0.25X) of the original concentration. Both the
half (0.5X) and quarter (0.25X) concentrations have a significantly
lower percentage of protein display than the standard (1X) condition.
CADM1-ANV seems to follow a linear decrease in the percentage of protein
display, while ITM2C-ANV display percentage decreases initially but
remains at the same level with 0.25X plasmid concentration. This result
hints at some plasmids/proteins being more robust to lowering plasmid
amounts than others. However, further experiments will be needed to
explore the nonuniform effects. These ANV variants with lower plasmid
concentrations were further tested on HEK293FT uptake ( D). All vesicles were dosed
at the same vesicle concentration (12,000 ANV/Cell). Therefore, the
only difference in uptake is due to the change in the amount of protein
display. We observed that both proteins followed a relatively linear
decrease in uptake percentage, decreasing from ∼80% to just
∼40–60%. This result demonstrates the power of VESSEL
in modulating protein display on ANV to regulate its uptake into cells.
To further examine the differences in HEK293FT uptake across proteins,
we examined all samples across the three doses that were tested (Figures S19–20). This approach addresses
a key concern in the EV field regarding proper dose control of EVs. All samples were normalized to their 20,000
ANV/cell condition, and the decreasing fold change across the 4000
and 2000 ANV/cell conditions was examined ( E–G). The dose–response curves
allowed for all 39 proteins to be classified into three groups based
on their effectiveness for HEK293FT uptake. “Robust-uptake”
proteins are those that maintain high uptake across the dosing curve,
maintaining a convex curve overall. “Standard-uptake”
are proteins that show decreased uptake linearly with the dosing amount.
Finally, “sensitive-uptake” are proteins whose uptake
effectiveness drops sharply with decreasing dosing amount. AMGO2-ANV
is classified as a “sensitive-uptake” protein due to
its high variability. While 32 of the total 39 proteins were statistically
significant, we consider only “robust-uptake” and “standard-uptake”
proteins to be beneficial for uptake due to the stable uptake trends
despite the dosing strategy. While 14 proteins from the “sensitive-uptake”
category were statistically significant, the sharp decrease of uptake
across the dosing amount means these “sensitive-uptake”
proteins are unlikely viable for future EV uptake studies.
Here,
we discovered 5 “robust-uptake” proteins and
13 “standard-uptake” proteins. LAMP1-ANV, CADM1-ANV,
NPTN-ANV, GGT1-ANV and ITM2C-ANV are the 5 “robust-uptake”
proteins, with consistently high uptake across all the doses. These
proteins are prime candidates for enhancing synthetic or native vesicle
uptake into HEK293FT cells and can be studied further for potent uptake
into other cell lines. The 13 “standard-uptake” proteins,
such as CD166-ANV, MMP14-ANV, and IGSF8-ANV, still maintained a relatively
high uptake profile across the doses. In contrast to literature data,
VESSEL enables a systematic dosing study of 39 proteins, of which
18 of the proteins are viable for increasing the level of HEK293FT
uptake. Our work also addresses a main controversy in the field regarding
the proper controls for dosing nanovesicles and their impact on functional
outcomes. To take this work a step further,
we performed HEK293FT uptake using native EVs while blocking three
“robust-uptake” proteins (Figure S21). The three proteins, LAMP1, CADM1, and NPTN, were blocked
using antibody blocking to the EV surface, and two different EV lines,
PMSC 795 and PMSC 808, were utilized (Section , ). We found that all three antibodies
had significant display and blocking of these proteins using single
nanoparticle flow cytometry. Furthermore, we found that when NPTN
was blocked on PMSC 795 EVs, there was a significant decrease in vesicle
uptake into HEK293FT cells. Additionally, when PMSC 808 EVs were blocked,
both with single proteins and combinatorially, we found that CADM1
and NPTN + CADM1 resulted in significantly lower HEK293FT uptake.
Two of the three “robust-uptake” proteins had translatable
function when tested on native PMSC EVs, apart from LAMP1. While the
lack of translated function of LAMP1 could be due to potential protein
density differences between our ANV platform and the native material,
or LAMP1 could work combinatorially with another protein on native
EV membranes, we did not find the protein effective for mediating
uptake in our native EV model. VESSEL can help nominate EV proteins
for functional testing. We show that 2/3 uptake-associated proteins
had an effect on native EVs. This approach provides a complementary
pathway for protein studies, and in the case of NPTN and CADM1, has
provided translatable function for two proteins that were relatively
unstudied for their cell-uptake function.
Next, we examined
the correlation between the size, net charge
of proteins, and cell uptake percentage ( H). We observed a charge-independent uptake
pattern, as there are both positively and negatively charged proteins
on both ends of the uptake spectrum (Supplementary Table 2). LAMP1-ANV, the protein with the highest uptake efficiency,
has a net positive charge; CADM1-ANV, another robust-uptake protein,
has a net negative charge. Additionally, we see EFNB2-ANV, a protein
with a similar net charge to LAMP1-ANV (3.25 vs 4.37), showing poor
uptake into HEK293FT cells (Figure S22).
The size of the protein domain does not show any correlation with
cell uptake percentage. Some proteins, like SHPS1-ANV and BASI-ANV
(66.99 and 61.78 kDa, respectively), have a similar size to LAMP1-ANV
(67.83 kDa), but show a statistically significant difference in HEK293FT
uptake percentage. Finally, we compared the uptake efficiencies to
the ANV display percentages shown in to determine if protein display efficiency/density
affects HEK293FT uptake (Figure S23). We
did not observe a correlation between the display and uptake effectiveness.
For example, PDPN had high display but was in the “sensitive-uptake”
group, while CLMP had low display but was at the top of the “standard-uptake”
group. This indicates that the observed function from our ANV is based
mainly on the protein domain function without strong correlation with
charge, size, or display density.
Next, we determined whether
the results uncovered new insights
into the proteins ( H) (see Section ). CADM1 is a protein
associated with cell adhesion, and is commonly used as a biomarker
for cancer.
,
 Its role on the EV surface, however,
is currently unknown, with only one publication hinting at its potential
role in the adhesion of EVs to cellular membranes. Here, we show, at least for HEK293FT cells, that CADM1
is a potent facilitator of cellular uptake, showing one of the highest
uptake percentages across all doses. Next, we investigated NPTN, a
protein that facilitates the formation of excitatory synapses.
,
 This protein, like CADM1, has not been associated with cellular
uptake but was the second-best performing protein from our tested
proteins. Based on the discoveries, the VESSEL platform has successfully
isolated proteins from proteomics data and validated them for uptake
into HEK293FT cells. Cellular uptake of EVs is a wide studied field,
Williams et al. conducted a study investigating 28 different cell
lines. HEK293T, a similar cell line to
HEK293FT, had an average uptake profile.
,
 This indicates that HEK293FT is an ideal testing cell line; however,
we wish to investigate other cell lines for function. VESSEL can be
applied to any EV and cell studies, allowing validation or hypothesis
testing of EV proteins.
Finally, we performed a cholesterol
study to observe the effect
of cholesterol on HEK293FT uptake as well as the protein display on
the liposomes (Figure S24). Three proteins
were tested, ITM2C, ATI1B, and GAL-1, chosen from the three uptake
groups “robust-uptake”, “standard-uptake”
and “sensitive-uptake”, respectively. Starting with
the protein display on the liposome surface, ITM2C and GAL-1 had no
significant difference in display, while ATI1B had significantly higher
protein display with the 40% cholesterol formulation. For ANV uptake
into HEK293FT cells, we observed a protein-dependent response. ITM2C-ANV
exhibited a significant decrease in uptake at the 40% cholesterol
formulation across all three doses. However, ITM2C-ANV with 20% cholesterol
did not affect uptake significantly. ATI1B-ANV, however, at the 20%
and 40% cholesterol formulations, exhibited decreased uptake at all
doses, apart from the low dose, where only the 20% condition was affected.
Finally, GAL-1-ANV had no significant decrease in HEK293FT uptake
with the addition of cholesterol at any dose. Ultimately, this cholesterol
screen suggested that cholesterol had a minimal impact on protein
display on the ANV. However, it can affect protein-ANV uptake. This
result is expected, as the role of cholesterol in nanoparticle uptake
has been stated. Our platform may be
used to study such compounding factors in the future.

2.5.

## VESSEL Allows Systematic Evaluation of EV
Protein Function in Neuronal Healing and Uptake Assays
VESSEL
is versatile in enabling a systematic investigation into the functions
of the displayed proteins. We conducted an SH-SY5Y neuroprotection
healing assay, in which staurosporine was added to an SH-SY5Y culture,
causing cytotoxicity (see Sections , ). VESSEL ANVs were then added to examine any
neuroprotective effects ( A). We examined 5 EV proteins: CD166-ANV, PDPN-ANV,
AMGO2-ANV, GAL-1-ANV, and HGF-ANV. CD166 has been found to promote
neurite extension, and additionally axon growth. PDPN has been found to be upregulated in several different
tumors, including brain cancer. Finally,
AMGO2 is required for the survival of cerebellar granule neurons,
indicating potential benefit to neuroprotection. The other 2 proteins, GAL-1 and HGF, were selected for
their demonstrated neuroprotective ability.
,
 VESSEL allows us to single out proteins that have previously demonstrated
or suggested benefits for neuroprotection, which, in turn, can help
to inform therapeutic applications.
 Our metric for neuroprotection is the growth of
SH-SY5Y projections
following standard methods used in the field (see Sections , ). Calcein-AM staining was performed for all samples. There
were significant differences between the no-protein control, and CD166-ANV,
PDPN-ANV, and AMGO2-ANV ( B). Representative images of all samples show that
PBS only, cell-free-ANV, and HGF-ANV decreased the amount of SH-SY5Y
growth, while the other proteins induced SH-SY5Y growth (Figure S25). This observation is further exemplified
when we applied an imaging mask that converted the images to skeletonized
projections (Figure S26). This conversion
allowed us to quantify the total length of the SH-SY5Y branches, and
to quantitatively compare the samples to one another. We found that
when compared to negative controls CD166-ANV, PDPN-ANV, and AMGO2-ANV
all exhibited significant neuroprotection of our SH-SY5Y cultures
( C). For CD166,
while most of its function in literature is attributed to cell-adhesion
and cancer cell invasion, it has been shown to play a role in neurite
formation.
,
 Our study agrees with this known
CD166 function. PDPN, another protein associated with cancer, has
more prior data showing its relationship to brain development. Our results corroborate that knowledge by showing
PDPN-ANV has neuroprotection functions. Finally, AMGO2, also denoted
as AMIGO2 in the literature, was confirmed to play a role in neuroprotection
in the mouse hippocampus region. Here, our data supports that evidence. GAL-1 and HGF, both proteins that have been
previously described as neuroprotective, did not show consistent results
in our study.
,
 The discrepancy suggests a few
possibilities: the protein’s role in neuroprotection may require
other proteins, or the tethering of these soluble proteins to ANV
surface does not recapitulate their natural forms and functions. Due
to both proteins are naturally soluble, further studies may be needed.
We additionally performed the same uptake study following the same
methods as the HEK293FT cells. Six proteins were tested, two from
each of the uptake groups (“robust-uptake”, “standard-uptake”,
and “sensitive-uptake”). When comparing the flow cytometry
analysis of the 6 proteins on ANV, only MMP14-ANV and PDPN-ANV were
statistically significant for uptake (see Section ) ( D). All the other proteins, including LAMP1-ANV and CADM1-ANV, which
showed “robust-uptake” with HEK293FT cells, had significantly
lower uptake at the medium dose with SH-SY5Y cells. When comparing
all 6 protein-ANVs across the three doses, every protein-ANV follows
the “sensitive-uptake” trend ( E). The discrepancy of uptake level between
cell types has previously been reported by Williams et al. They studied the uptake of EVs in relation to
the surface glycans. Of the 28 cell-lines
tested, SH-SY5Y was ranked in the lowest 3 cell-lines for extracellular
vesicle uptake, and was much lower ranked than HEK293FT. Therefore, our observed uptake trend of the
ANVs behaving differently for different cell types was consistent
with the literature. Still, finding differences in the uptake percentage
and the potential uptake advantage of MMP14-ANV and PDPN-ANV could
point to new ways to engineer EVs. These experiments demonstrate the
power of VESSEL for enabling the systematic study of EV protein functions,
especially without interference from other proteins or EV cargo.

## Conclusions
Our platform, VESSEL, enables
the generation and testing of protein-ANV
variants, allowing for new discoveries for EV-protein function. We
showed that Aquaporin-Z, along with the human blood protein domain
Transferrin, provides robust insertion and anchoring of cell-free
generated protein extracellular domains into liposomes, generating
ANVs. This platform was scaled up and successfully produced 39 different
protein-ANV variants, all of which had a significant display of protein.
These variants were tested through HEK293FT uptake and SH-SY5Y neuroprotection,
in which new discoveries were made. All 39 variants were tested for
HEK293FT uptake. With each protein, we observed different results
of uptake. For instance, CADM1 has not been referenced as a protein
that facilitated vesicle uptake; therefore, we are excited to demonstrate
its effectiveness. We also showed that the uptake effectiveness of
our ANVs was not correlated to the charge or size of each protein,
indicating that specific protein interactions likely facilitated the
vesicle uptake. Additionally, to demonstrate the use of VESSEL, we
applied this model to SH-SY5Y neuroprotection, in which 3 of the 5
protein-ANVs were significant for neurite outgrowth and repair.
In addition, we observed a lack of effective ANV uptake into SH-SY5Y
cells. While prior studies have shown that SH-SY5Y has relatively
low vesicle uptake, this was still surprising as CADM1-ANV and other
previously effective proteins lacked uptake effectiveness. We, however,
see this as validation for why a platform such as VESSEL could offer
insights into the extracellular vesicle field. VESSEL can produce
specific protein-ANV for functional analysis or binding characterization
and can inform EV studies on useful surface proteins. Once an effective
protein for a target function has been validated, it can then be upregulated
or synthetically added to the surface of native EVs. In the future,
the field could expand the number of proteins tested for cell uptake
as well as investigate other therapeutically relevant cell lines.
Additionally, the kinetic timeline for cellular uptake could be expanded
to multiple days instead of hours. These studies could investigate
how EV proteins affect vesicle elimination/clearance.
A limitation
of the VESSEL platform in its current form is the
use of single-pass transmembrane proteins. Our method, with anchoring
an extracellular domain using Aquaporin-Z, allowed for rapid scaling
up due to insertion and display being consistent across proteins.
This method, however, limits the number of proteins that can be studied
as only single-pass transmembrane proteins can be used with functional
certainty. However, we note that this work also tested a small number
of multipass membrane proteins, CD9, CD63, and CD81. It has been previously
reported that only the second extracellular domain is needed for these
proteins to be functional, which we used in this study. Therefore, VESSEL can also be used to isolate
extracellular domains of proteins, attach them to vesicles, and test
these domains for function. Our previous work, MEMPLEX, which generated
full-length membrane proteins through high-throughput droplet printing,
could be combined with VESSEL to further study EV proteins. This combination will enable systematic comparison
of surface protein display vs native membrane proteins on a bottom-up
platform. Additionally, this platform can be used to further test
the two large soluble proteins of GAL-1 and HGF to verify their roles
in neuroprotection. However, the studies will require tremendous effort
to properly compare protein structure and functions as well as an
expansive list of proteins to reach definite mechanistic and generalizable
conclusions. Finally, there has been a large focus in the synthetic
biology field on utilizing cell-free systems that provide post-translational
modifications, such as Chinese Hamster Ovary cells.
−

 Our E. coli-derived cell-free system lacks these post-translational
modifications. Our system still produced >39 ANVs and provided
interesting
insight into previously low-researched proteins, something that can
only be achieved with knockout models with great expense and a large
amount of time. We, however, understand that these modifications could
impact EV uptake into cells. Future work could test different cell-free
systems, including those from mammalian cells, to examine how EV proteins
are affected in both their function and surface display. Additionally,
future work could test HGF and GAL-1 with a mammalian cell-free system
to investigate if the soluble forms are better generated and if the
reported function is recapitulated.
In conclusion, this work
provides a platform in which any protein
can be cloned, generated, displayed on ANV, and then tested for function
in a rapid manner. This bottom-up approach allows researchers to isolate
the protein of interest while maintaining the physical characteristics
of the native extracellular vesicle for testing. We aim to take this
platform further and study combinatorial effects of these proteins
in the future to enhance the VESSEL platform’s application.
We expect this platform to enable a deeper understanding of EV protein
interactions and help in resolving existing debates in the field of
EV, such as the effect of EV surface proteins versus cargo and stoichiometry
requirement of various biomolecules on EV for specific functions.
Furthermore, we anticipate further use of this platform to enhance
both synthetic and native EVs as therapeutics.

## Methods
4.1.

## Cell Culture
Human
Embryonic Kidney
Cells 293 FT (HEK293FT) were cultured using Millipore-Sigma Dulbecco’s
Modified Eagle’s Medium (DMEM) (11965-092, Life Technologies)
with 10% Thomas Scientific LLC fetal bovine serum (FBS) (35-010-CV,
Corning) and 100U mL–1 penicillin/streptomycin (15140122,
Thermofisher). SH-SY5Y cells were cultured using Dulbecco’s
Modified Eagle Medium, 5% fetal bovine serum, and 100U mL–1 penicillin/streptomycin. PMSC cells were cultured using Dulbecco’s
Modified Eagle Medium, 5% fetal bovine serum, 100U mL–1 penicillin/streptomycin, 20 ng mL–1 recombinant
human fibroblast growth factor (FGF) (3718FB01M, R&D systems)
and 20 ng mL–1 recombinant human epidermal growth
factor (EGF) (AF-100-15-500UG, PeproTech). All cells were grown to
confluency using a spectrum of 25 cm2 and 75 cm2 tissue culture treated flasks (972-95727-ZI, 972-95730-P5, Spectrum).
All cells were cultured at 37 °C, 5% CO2 in a humidity
pan incubator. HEK293FT and SH-SY5Y cells were passaged to collagen
coated glass bottom 24 well plates for uptake and neuroprotection
assays (P24-1.5P, Cellvis). Collagen well coating was performed by
incubating 0.02 mM collagen at room temperature for 1 h, the collagen
was removed and the well washed 3 times with DI water then a 30 min
incubation in the cell culture hood ultraviolet light with the lid
off (50302, Ibidi).

4.2.

## Liposome and PMSC Extracellular
Vesicle Preparation
For the liposomes used with Transferrin
and Aquaporin-Z, 18:1 (Δ9-Cis)
PC (DOPC) (850375P-1g, Avanti Polar) and 18:1 Cy5 PE (850-483, Avanti
Polar) were used in a mole fraction of 99.85% and 0.15% respectively.
For Liposomes used with strep-tag, 16:0–18:1 PC (POPC) (850457C-25
mg, Avanti Polar), 18:1 Cy5 PE and 16:0 Biotinyl PE (870277P-25 mg,
Avanti Polar) were used in a mole fraction of 98.85%, 0.15%, and 1%
respectively.
To generate liposomes, all lipids were mixed in
a glass vial and dried to form a thin lipid film using a nitrogen
gas stream. The thin lipid film was transferred to a vacuum chamber
and desiccated for a minimum of 3 h. These films were then resuspended
using 500 μL of liposome buffer (0.5 M HEPES-KOH, 0.012 M magnesium
glutamate, 0.18 M potassium glutamate, 2% PEG 8000, 0.0592 M potassium
phosphate monobasic (BDH9268, VWR) (see Supplementary Table 3) and sonicated using a probe sonicator for 1250 J (50%
Amplitude, 10 s on, 10 s off) (Qsonica Q125). The liposomes were centrifuged
at 10,000g for 3 min, and the bottom 450 μL
was taken and stored at 4 °C to be used for downstream experiments.
To isolate EVs, exosome free FBS was prepared by spinning FBS at
112,700g using a Beckman Coulter L7 Ultracentrifuge
with a SW28 rotor. The FBS was spun for 16 h at 4 °C and was
stored at −20 °C. PMSC 795 and 808 were seeded in 5-layer
flasks (353144, Corning Life Sciences) at passage 4 at a cell density
of 20,000 cells cm–2. These cells were passaged
using the media outlined in M.1., however, the exosome free FBS replaced
the normal FBS. After 48 h, the conditioned media was collected, centrifuged
at 300g for 10 min at 4 °C, then the supernatant
was transferred, and centrifuged at 2000g for 20
min at 4 °C. The supernatant, except for the bottom 1 mL to prevent
debris contamination, was transferred to a 0.2 μm filter and
vacuum filtered. Here, 25 mL of the filtered media was transferred
to a thick-wall polypropylene tube (355642, Beckman Coulter) and centrifuged
at 8812g using the L7 ultracentrifuge for 30 min
at 4 °C. The supernatant was filtered through a 0.22 μm
filter and centrifuged for 90 min at 112,700g. The
supernatant was discarded, and the pellet was washed with PBS and
centrifuged again at 112,700g for 90 min at 4 °C.
The pellet was resuspended in triple filtered PBS and aliquoted. These
underwent NTA and were stored at −80 °C (see Section ).

4.3.

## Whole Cell Extract Preparation
BL21-DE3
Star bacteria were grown in 300 mL of 2X YT (31g L–1) (Y2377-250G, Sigma-Aldrich) at 37 °C at 250 rpm on a large
shaking incubator (Thermofisher MaxQ 4000, 19 mm orbit). These bacteria
were grown until an optical density 600 (OD600) of 0.05–0.15
was achieved, and then 120 μL of 1 M Isopropyl β-D-1-thiogalactopyranoside
(IPTG) (11411446001, Sigma-Aldrich) was added to the flask. The E. coli was incubated until an OD600 of 0.9–1.0 was
achieved, in which they were removed from the flask and transferred
to a chilled 300 mL bottle and centrifuged at 4000g, 4 °C for 10 min. The supernatant was removed, and 20 mL of
sonication buffer was added (0.01 M tris acetate (C993H97, Thomas
Scientific), 0.014 M magnesium acetate (0131–500G, Amersco),
0.06 M potassium gluconate (AC229322500, Acros Organics)). The pellet
was resuspended, and the sample was centrifuged again. The supernatant
was removed, 20 mL of sonication buffer was added, and the cells
were resuspended and transferred to a weighed 50 mL conical (EP0030122178,
Sigma-Aldrich). The E. coli was centrifuged for 10
min, the supernatant was removed, and the sample was centrifuged once
more for 5 min to remove all remaining supernatant. The tube was weighed
to determine cell pellet mass, and sonication buffer with 2 mM DTT
(97061-340, VWR) is added to the cell pellet to achieve a concentration
of 1g mL–1. The E. coli was resuspended,
500 μL was added to 1.5 mL Eppendorf tubes and sonicated using
a probe sonicator at 50% amplitude, 10 s on and 10 s off (Qsonica
Q125) until 1000 J was achieved. The E. coli was
centrifuged at 12,000g for 20 min at 4 °C. The
supernatant is collected and stored at −80 °C.

## Synthesis of Artificial

4.7.

## Artificial Nanovesicle
Filtering Methods
35 nm size exclusion chromatography (ICS-35,
IZON) columns were
rinsed using 6 mL of 0.22 μm filtered PBS. ANVs were resuspended
to a total volume of 150 μL in PBS, then added to the column.
850 μL of PBS was added to the column and discarded, and then
500 μL of PBS was added and collected. The 500 μL filtered
vesicles were added to a 0.5 mL, 100 kDa Amicon filter (UFC5100, Sigma-Aldrich)
and centrifuged at 14,000g for 15 min. The filter
was flipped and placed in a fresh collection tube and centrifuged
at 1000g for 4 min. ANVs are then either used for
direct analysis or normalized for cell-based assays (see Section ).

4.8.

## Normalization Strategy
for Artificial Nanovesicles
Vesicles are normalized by loading
2 μL of filtered ANVs
into 18 μL of 0.22 μm filtered PBS onto a 384 well black
microreader plate (757-11401-CS, Thermofisher). The plate is analyzed
using a microplate reader (Biotek Synergy H1) for fluorescence detecting
Cy5 (Excitation: 639 nm, Emission: 669 nm). Fluorescence values are
then normalized to an experimentally determined fluorescence (224.58
A.U. for this work). All normalization dilutions are done using PBS.
To determine the specific ANV/cell number values, ANV samples after
normalization underwent NTA to determine the ANV concentration at
the fluorescence normalization value.

4.9.

## Western
Blotting
ANV samples were
mixed with an equal volume of Western blot buffer (95% V/V 2X laemmli
buffer and 5% V/V β-mercaptoethanol) (1610737, 1610710, Bio-Rad).
The samples were incubated at 37 °C for 10 min using a heat block,
then centrifuged at 6800g for 30 s to account for
evaporation. 4–20% Mini-PROTEAN TGX precast gels (4561095,
Biorad) were loaded into the SDS-Page gel electrophoresis rig (1658004,
Biorad) and the tank was filled with 1X Tris/Glycine/SDS buffer (1610732,
Biorad). Ten μL of the sample was loaded to each well, and the
SDS was run at a constant 200 V until the dye ran off the gel. The
gels were removed from the cassette and loaded into the Trans-Blot
Turbo Transfer System (1704150EDU, Biorad) with a PVDF (1704272, Biorad)
membrane according to manufacturer instructions. The membrane was
removed from the transfer system, rinsed with DI water, and then incubated
with ponceau (89167-800, VWR) for 5 min on a rotating shaker to confirm
protein transfer. The membrane was rinsed with DI water until all
ponceau was removed, then incubated in TBST buffer (1X Tris Buffered
Saline, 0.1% Tween 20) (J640-4L, VWR) (M147 −1L, AMRESCO) for
5 min to remove band staining. The membrane was blocked for 1 h at
room temperature using blocking buffer (TBST and 5% blotting grade
blocker) (1706404XTU, Biorad) on a rotating shaker. A 1:1000 dilution
of 6X-HIS Tag Monoclonal antibody (HIS.H8) (MA1-21315, Thermofisher)
in blocking buffer was created, and 10 mL was added to the blot. The
membrane was incubated overnight at 4 °C on a rotating shaker.
The antibody was removed, and the blot was rinsed using TBST for
5 min on a rotating shaker 3 times at room temperature. The membrane
was blocked using blocking buffer for 1 h at room temperature using
a rotating shaker. A secondary antibody solution was generated using
blocking buffer and a 1:25,000 dilution of goat antimouse IgG antibody
horseradish peroxidase conjugate (31430, Pierce Thermo Scientific).
The blot was incubated with 10 mL of the secondary antibody solution
and incubated at room temperature on a rotating shaker for 1 h. For
analysis, the blot was rinsed once with TBST and incubated with Clarity
Western ECL Substrate (1705061, Biorad) while wrapped in plastic wrap
for 5 min in the dark. The Western blot was analyzed by using a gel
imager (Syngene PXi) for chemiluminescence. Quantification was performed
by using ImageJ.

## HEK293FT
and SH-SY5Y Uptake
ANV
samples were generated, filtered, and normalized by using the protocols
above. HEK293FT and SH-SY5Y cells were cultured and passaged onto
collagen coated 24 well glass bottom plates (P24–1.5P, Cellvis)
at a density of 50,000 cells/well. Twenty-four h after passaging,
the ANV samples are added to the wells at three densities (20,000

using the Cytation C10 confocal microscope (Brightfield 6 ms, CY5
500 ms). The cells were then incubated for 4 h at 37 °C, 5% CO2 and then imaged again. The media per well was collected,
and each well received an ice-cold trypsin treatment for 5 min at
37 °C. The cells were then scraped off the well, and the trypsin/cell
mixture was added to the media and centrifuged at 300g for 5 min. The supernatant was removed, and the cell pellet was
resuspended with 250 μL of PBS. Samples were stored on ice while
flow cytometry was being performed. To assess cell viability, each
sample received 0.5 μL of 150 μg mL–1 DAPI added right before the sample was run. Due to DAPI’s
low cell permeability, cells that are DAPI positive with this short
incubation time have a disrupted membrane, indicating cell death.
These dead cells are then removed through flow cytometry gating from
the living non-DAPI positive population (Figure S17).
For PMSC extracellular vesicle uptake, PMSC EV
were incubated overnight with 2 μM cell-trace far red (C34572,
Thermofisher) at 30 °C at 300 rpm on a rotating shaker. These
PMSC EVs then were filtered using size exclusion chromatography and
Amicon filtration to remove the free cell-trace far red (see Section ). NTA was performed to determine the concentration
of the EVs and were normalized to 4.667E11 EVs per mL. These EVs were
aliquoted to 1.5 μL and the respective antibodies were added
to each aliquot; LAMP1 (53-1079-42, Thermofisher), NPTN (NBP2-97104AF488,
Novus Biologicals), and CADM1 (75-120-FL490, Thermofisher). All antibodies
were added for a final concentration of 120 μg per mL. NPTN
and CADM1 antibodies were incubated at room temperature for 2.5 h.
LAMP1 was incubated at 37 °C for 2.5 h. Any combinations that
included LAMP1 were incubated at 37 °C for 2.5 h, while any combinations
that did not include LAMP1 were incubated at room temperature for
2.5 h. After incubation, all aliquots received PBS to match the volume
to the all-antibody condition. After PBS addition, 3 uL of each aliquot,
totaling 2.33E8 EVs, was added to a well of HEK293FT cells that were
cultured following the same protocol as above. All subsequent incubation,
cell capture, and cell viability tests were performed the same as
above.

were generated, filtered, and normalized using the protocols above.
SH-SY5Y cells were cultured and passaged onto Costar 48 well TC treated
plates (3548, Corning) at a density of 100,000 cells/well. Cells were
only cultured in the center 24 wells. 120 h after passaging, each
well received 0.5 μM staurosporine (9953S, Cell Signaling Technologies)
and was incubated at 37 °C, 5% CO2 for 4 h. After the incubation,
the staurosporine medium was removed, and fresh medium was added to
each well. ANVs were added to each well at 3 concentrations (20,000
ANV/cell, 4000 ANV/cell, 2000 ANV/cell). After 4 h, uptake imaging
was performed using Cytation C10. The cells incubated for 5 days
at 37 °C, 5% CO2, after which the media was removed
and a 200 μL solution of PBS and 2 μM of Calcein-AM (564061,
BD Biosciences) was added. The stained cells were imaged using the
Carl Zeiss Axio Observer D1 inverted microscope and were analyzed
using the in-house image processing and skeletonization (see Section ).

## Image Processing and Skeletonization
for
Quantification of Neuronal Process Length
This workflow used
Python 3.9.20, OpenCV 4.10.0, NumPy 1.26.4, and scikit-image. It began
by loading each grayscale image with OpenCV and applying a binary
threshold at a pixel intensity value of 50. Pixels above 50 nm became
white (255). Pixels below 50 became black (0). This produced a binary
mask that isolated the branch structures. The skeletonize function
from skimage.morphology reduced each branch structure to a single-pixel-wide
line. Using measure.label with connectivity = 2 identified individual
skeletonized branches. The measure.regionprops function computed each
region’s perimeter. These perimeter values served as proxies
for the branch lengths. Summing all of the perimeters yielded the
total branch length for each image. This process ran automatically
on all images in a specified directory. The script recorded file names
and total branch lengths. The data were organized into a Pandas DataFrame
and exported to an Excel file. Consider experimenting with different
threshold values to see whether it affects the accuracy of process
length measurements.
