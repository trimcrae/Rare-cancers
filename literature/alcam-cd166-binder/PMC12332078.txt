Pro-angiogenic galectin-3 is elevated in diabetic serum and promotes systemic insulin resistance that may impair wound healing. However, the exact role of galectin-3 in the regulation of diabetic wound healing remains unclear. Here, we demonstrate that galectin-3 promotes skin wound healing and angiogenesis via binding to its receptor integrin α5β1, and enhances downstream focal adhesion kinase phosphorylation by forming a liquid-liquid phase separation with integrin α5β1. Under diabetic conditions, aberrant accumulated advanced glycation end-products bind to galectin-3, blocking its interaction with integrin α5β1 and impairing angiogenesis. Topical treatment of recombinant galectin-3 in hydrogels promotes diabetic wound healing in rodents without causing systemic insulin resistance and synergizes with insulin. This study clarifies the binding of galectin-3 to integrin α5β1, instead of advanced glycation end-products, forming phase separation to promote angiogenesis and diabetic wound healing, laying the foundation for local galectin-3 therapy to treat diabetic foot ulcers.

This study shows that Galectin-3 promotes wound healing by binding to and undergoing phase separation with integrin α5β1, a process disrupted by AGEs in pre-clinical models of diabetes. Topical Galectin-3 restored healing in synergy with insulin.

## Introduction
For patients with diabetes mellitus, diabetic foot ulcers (DFUs) present a formidable complication that is characterized by its high incidence, elevated risk of amputation and disability, increased mortality, and poor cure rate. Up to 19–30% of diabetic patients develop DFUs, with the recurrence rate within 3–5 years reaching ~65% . In addition, the mortality of diabetics with DFUs is 2.5-fold higher than those without them . Impaired wound healing is considered a main contributor to the development of DFUs in diabetic patients . Facilitating wound healing, therefore, remains challenging yet full of potential to prevent and treat DFUs in people with diabetes.
Angiogenesis is significantly decreased in DFUs, which negatively impacts wound healing. Thus, promoting angiogenesis stands as a potential therapeutic approach to treat DFUs . Existing therapies, such as bioengineered dressings, oxygen therapy, and negative pressure wound therapy, are either expensive or only effective for mild to moderate wounds, and these interventions are also insufficient for the prevention of amputation . For example, RegranexTM (a therapeutic containing recombinant human platelet-derived growth factor, PDGF-BB) is the only FDA-approved wound dressing containing angiogenic growth factor to treat DFUs. Unfortunately, that dressing shows modest efficacy and increases the risk of cancer as well as the mortality rate for diabetic patients . Dermagraft®, a bioengineered skin substitutes that secrets angiogenic molecules like vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), keratinocyte growth factor (KGF), and insulin-like growth factor 1 (IGF-1), etc, is a promising dermal graft for difficult-to-heal wounds . However, its usages are limited by several reasons, including the safety issues due to the presence of allogeneic dermal fibroblasts, the necessity for multiple treatments, potential risks for infections and cellulitis, and the high cost of Dermagraft® . Thus, there is an urgent need to identify new therapeutic pro-angiogenic agents to treat DFUs.
Galectins are a family of proteins that generally bind β-galactoside-based sugars, and are widely expressed in various human organs and tissues . The role of galectins in angiogenesis has been explored in previous studies, particularly in oncology field , . For instance, our in vitro study has reported that galectin-3 (Gal-3), one chimera member of galectins, increases vascular endothelial cell migration by binding to CD146 . Another study has shown that Gal-3 promotes VEGF or basic fibroblast growth factor (bFGF)-mediated angiogenesis in mice corneal injury model . The regulatory mechanisms of Gal-3-mediated angiogenesis may vary in different organs and tissues. Nevertheless, the role of Gal-3 in diabetic wound healing has yet to be elucidated.
Notably, plasma levels of Gal-3 are elevated in obese and diabetic patients , and elevated plasma Gal-3 in obese or db/db mice is known to promote insulin resistance of hepatocytes, adipocytes, and myocytes . Mechanistically, Gal-3 binds to the insulin receptor and inhibits insulin-induced tyrosine phosphorylation of this receptor, thereby impairing downstream signaling, including insulin receptor substrate 1 (IRS1) and AKT phosphorylation . Insulin treatment could promote diabetic wound healing, suggesting that insulin resistance may harm the healing process . Thus, elevated levels of plasma Gal-3 may be detrimental to wound healing in diabetes by inducing insulin resistance. In this regard, addition of Gal-3 may exert unfavorable influence on wound healing and DFUs. Therefore, it is important to clarify the controversial role of Gal-3 in regulation of DFUs.
In the present study, we used topical application of Gal-3 to examine the functional impact of Gal-3 in angiogenesis, and wound healing in diabetes. We found that Gal-3 levels are significantly decreased in diabetic skin vasculature, whereas local treatment of the skin with Gal-3 enhances wound healing and promotes angiogenesis without causing systemic insulin resistance in diabetic rodent model. Mechanistically, Gal-3 binds to and potentiates activation of its receptor integrin α5β1 via liquid-liquid phase separation (LLPS), and amplifies downstream signaling by promoting phosphorylation of FAK. In diabetes, excessive advanced glycation end-products (AGEs) bind to Gal-3 to inhibit the binding of Gal-3 to integrin α5β1, thus resulting in inactivation of the integrin α5β1-FAK axis and inhibition of angiogenesis. Importantly, topical administration of Gal-3 embedded in hydrogels can effectively promote diabetic wound healing. Taken together, our data clarify the beneficial role and mechanism of Gal-3 in diabetic wound healing, and provide a potential approach to treat DFUs by local administration of Gal-3.

## Results

## Gal-3 promotes diabetic wound healing and angiogenesis in vivo
To investigate the role of galectins in diabetic wound healing, we analyzed one previous proteomic atlas in the ProteomeXchange database (dataset identifier: PXD019909), which provides a large and comprehensive characterization of the quantitative cell-type and location-specific proteome of human skin . Among the 12 human-derived galectins, only galectin-1, −3, −7, −8, −9 were detected in mass spectrometry-based proteomic analysis of skin . Importantly, levels of galectin-1, Gal-3, and galectin-7 were the most highly expressed (Supplementary Fig.  ). We therefore analyzed the expression levels of these three galectins in diabetes state. In diabetic patient skin, expression of Gal-3 detected by immunohistochemistry (IHC), was largely decreased compared to non-diabetic individuals, whereas no difference was observed with galectin-1 and galectin-7 (Fig.  ). In particular, Gal-3 levels in skin vasculature were reduced by nearly 50% in diabetic patients (Fig.  and Supplementary Fig.  , upper panel), being the most pronounced compared to the decrease of Gal-3 in epidermis and hair follicles (Supplementary Fig.  ). This observation is consistent with that in streptozotocin (STZ)-induced diabetic rats (Fig.  and Supplementary Fig.  , lower panel), suggesting that deficiency of Gal-3 in skin vasculature may be associated with impaired healing of diabetic wounds. 
In the skin, various cell types can express Gal-3, such as keratinocytes, inflammatory cells, fibroblasts, and endothelial cells . To investigate the role of Gal-3 in diabetic wound healing, we made a circular full-thickness wound (20 mm in diameter) on the dorsum of rats with STZ-induced diabetes. Then, adenovirus encoding Gal-3 under the control of the CMV constitutive promoter was injected into the edge of these wounds. Two days after infection, the levels of Gal-3 in the skin were significantly increased (Supplementary Fig.  ), whereas plasma Gal-3 levels were not altered (Supplementary Fig.  ). The skin wound closure rate was increased by ~21% on day 9 in the Gal-3 overexpression group (Fig.  ). In support of this, the thickness of the epidermis and granulation tissue was significantly enhanced in the healing skin (Supplementary Fig.  ), representing a better healing quality in the rat wound model . Type I collagen, the predominant type of collagen in skin, is one of the indicators of wound healing, because it replaces type III collagen during the healing process and increases the tensile strength of the skin . Picrosirius red staining showed that the proportion of type I collagen in the sum of type I and III collagen, was increased by ~49% in wound granulation tissue; and the sum of collagens tended to increase (Fig.  ). Western blotting also revealed a significant elevation in type I collagen (Supplementary Fig.  ). Vascular density, reflected by the CD31 marker, was assessed by IHC to determine the pro-angiogenic effect of Gal-3 in healed skin. The vascular density was significantly increased in the Gal-3 overexpression group (Fig.  ), which was also confirmed by western blotting (Fig.  ). The epithelial-mesenchymal transition plays an important role in the wound-healing process . mRNA expression of the re-epithelialization marker Cdh1 (encoding E-cadherin) was significantly upregulated, whereas mesenchymalization marker Cdh2 (encoding N-cadherin) showed a trend toward reduction (Supplementary Fig.  ). Consistently, immunofluorescence analysis revealed similar patterns (Supplementary Fig.  ), suggesting a better repair of the epidermal layer. Thus, local Gal-3 overexpression in skin can promote wound healing in diabetic rats. In addition, blood glucose, blood lipids, and body weight were not altered between the two groups (Supplementary Fig.  ). Hepatic and renal toxicity, as quantified by the plasma level of alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), and blood urea nitrogen (BUN), was not observed when Gal-3 was locally overexpressed in skin (Supplementary Fig.  ).
Furthermore, the effects of Gal-3 on wound healing in mice with a high-fat diet (HFD) and STZ-induced type 2 diabetes mellitus were investigated. An 8-mm diameter circular full-thickness skin wound was created on the dorsum of diabetic mice. The proportion of type I collagen and total collagen deposition in wound granulation tissues was significantly increased in the skin receiving local Gal-3 overexpression (Fig.  ). Vascular density was also significantly increased (Fig.  ), demonstrating the pro-wound healing effects of Gal-3 in HFD/STZ mice. Notably, overexpression of Gal-3 with adenovirus was able to promote wound healing in normal healthy mice without diabetes, because the wound closure rate and type I collagen expression were both significantly increased (Supplementary Fig.  ).
Next, we verified the functional significance of endogenous Gal-3 in wound healing by inhibiting Gal-3 with GB1107, a galactoside that binds to the Gal-3 carbohydrate-recognition domain (CRD) to block its binding with substrates . GB1107 has been described as a high-affinity galectin-3 inhibitor, which has 100-fold selectivity compared with Gal-1, more than 500-fold selectivity with Gal-7, and a range of 3.5- to >500-fold selectivity with other galectins as determined by fluorescence polarization assays . When locally applied in the wounds of normal female mice, the epidermal thickness was decreased by ~30% (Supplementary Fig.  ), and the percentage of type I collagen was decreased by ~38% (Fig.  ), whereas the vascular density was reduced by ~25% in GB1107-treated female mice (Fig.  ). Similarly, in GB1107-treated male mice, the epidermal thickness and vascular density were also significantly decreased (Supplementary Fig.  ). Together, these data indicate that blockage of Gal-3 inhibits wound healing both in female and male mice in vivo.
A previous study reported that Gal-3 promoted insulin resistance in the liver, muscle and adipose tissues of HFD-induced obese mice . We wondered whether Gal-3 could enhance insulin resistance in diabetic skin. While Gal-3 impaired insulin-induced AKT phosphorylation in mouse primary hepatocytes as expected (Supplementary Fig.  , left panel), we discovered that Gal-3 did not significantly alter insulin-induced AKT phosphorylation in cultured vascular endothelial cells (Supplementary Fig.  , right panel). In vivo, local application of Gal-3 to wounds, both under basal conditions and insulin injection, did not affect AKT phosphorylation in diabetic liver or skin tissue (Supplementary Fig.  ). Likewise, local injection of recombinant Gal-3 in wounds did not aggravate FoxO1 expression in diabetic skin (Supplementary Fig.  ). These results suggest that Gal-3 does not affect the insulin signaling in skin endothelial cells. Additionally, local application of Gal-3 on skin does not induce insulin resistance in the liver, likely due to the insufficient circulating levels of Gal-3 after topical administration (Supplementary Fig.  ). Collectively, these results indicate that local application of Gal-3 promotes wound healing and angiogenesis without aggravating insulin resistance in diabetic skin tissue.

## Gal-3 promotes angiogenesis by binding to integrin α5β1 and potentiating its activation
Based on in vivo observations, Gal-3 overexpression could promote wound healing and increase vascular density, which could be blocked by its functional inhibitor GB1107. We speculated that improvement of diabetic wound healing by Gal-3 may be related to angiogenic regulation. Therefore, microvascular endothelial cells (HMEC-1) were used to explore the effect of Gal-3 on cell migration and tube formation in the diabetic state. An insulin-resistant model of HMEC-1 cells was established (Supplementary Fig.  ), and cell migration and tube formation were significantly upregulated upon addition of recombinant Gal-3 under insulin-resistant state (Fig.  ). The mRNA levels of pro-angiogenic factors VEGFA and FGF2, but not HGF, were significantly increased by Gal-3 in a dose-dependent manner (Fig.  ). Addition of Gal-3 did not affect cell proliferation or apoptosis (Supplementary Fig.  ). These results indicate that under insulin resistance, Gal-3 supplementation promotes endothelial cell migration and tube formation without eliciting cellular toxicity. Consistently, overexpression of Gal-3 significantly elevated cell migration and tube formation (Supplementary Fig.  ). 
We next sought to explore the molecular mechanisms by which Gal-3 promotes angiogenesis. Gal-3 was reported to increase keratinocyte migration by retaining plasma membrane epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) , and enhance in vitro angiogenesis of HUVECs by retaining plasma membrane vascular endothelial growth factor receptor 2 (VEGFR2) . In addition, Gal-3 could elevate VEGF-mediated migration of HUVECs via interacting with N-glycans of integrin αvβ3 . Interestingly, by using pull-down assay, we observed that Gal-3 interacted with VEGFR2 and also weakly interacted with EGFR or integrin αv in cell lysates of HMEC-1 cells, while similar patterns of Gal-3 binding to these three proteins were also observed in cell membrane lysates (Supplementary Fig.  ). However, phosphorylation of EGFR or VEGFR2 was not upregulated in the presence of Gal-3 (Supplementary Fig.  ). Moreover, the compounds ZD1839 (EGFR inhibitor) and PTK787 (VEGFR2 inhibitor) failed to inhibit Gal-3-induced HMEC-1 cell migration (Supplementary Fig.  ), despite that they efficiently decreased the phosphorylation of EGFR and VEGFR2 (Supplementary Fig.  ). Similarly, knockdown of integrin αv failed to decrease Gal-3-induced HMEC-1 cell migration (Supplementary Fig.  ). Therefore, EGFR, VEGFR2, and integrin αvβ3 are not functional mediators of Gal-3-induced angiogenesis in HMEC-1 cells.
Secreted Gal-3 is thought to bind to cell surface glyco-conjugates, thereby mediating functions, such as angiogenesis, apoptosis, and T cell activation . To identify the functional receptor of Gal-3 in angiogenesis, we incubated His-Gal-3 with plasma membrane proteins of HMEC-1 cells, and then performed immunoprecipitation and mass spectrometry analysis. Gal-3 bound proteins in the gel were shown by silver staining (Supplementary Fig.  , left panel). For mass spectrometry analysis, we set the following criteria to identify potential Gal-3 binding candidates: 1) more than two unique peptides of each protein (unique peptide sequences acquired from the trypsin-digested full-length proteins) were detected, and 2) classified as cellular membrane proteins. A total of 15 membrane proteins were identified according to the criteria (Supplementary Fig.  , right panel). After this, we examined the expression level of the top 5 proteins identified (i.e., CD166, CD146, ECE1, integrin β1 and catenin α-1) in human skin endothelial cells using the proteomic atlas in the ProteomeXchange database (dataset identifier: PXD019909) . Of the 5 proteins, catenin α-1 and integrin β1 were the most highly expressed (Fig.  ). Functional validation showed that integrin β1 deficiency by siRNA significantly inhibited Gal-3-induced HMEC-1 cells migration by ~70% and tube formation by ~63%, whereas catenin α-1 deficiency inhibited Gal-3-induced migration by ~27% and failed to affect tube formation (Fig.  and Supplementary Fig.  ). Therefore, these data suggest integrin β1 as a prominent and previously unrecognized binding partner of Gal-3 to induce angiogenesis. A pull-down assay was performed to validate the interaction between Gal-3 and integrin β1 (Fig.  ). Importantly, Gal-3-mediated cell migration was significantly inhibited by 60-65% when integrin β1 was knocked down by shRNAs (Fig.  and Supplementary Fig.  ). A similar degree of inhibition was also observed using the integrin β1-blocking antibody TDM29 (Fig.  ).
Given that the integrin complex contains both α and β subunits, we next investigated which α subunit was partnered with the integrin β1. Basically, the β1 subunit can form heterodimeric complexes with 12 different α subunits (Fig.  ). In this regard, integrin α3, α5, and α6 were detected by our mass spectrometry (Fig.  and Supplementary Fig.  ). Mechanistically, Gal-3 binds to the N-glycans of integrin , meaning that the binding of Gal-3 to integrin α3 or α5 might be more prevalent than that on integrin α6, because there are more N-glycosylation sites for integrin α3 or α5 as predicted by the UniProt database (https://www.uniprot.org/) (Supplementary Fig.  ). Virtual docking in silico showed that integrin α5 has the highest binding free energy (−12.9 kcal/mol) to Gal-3 compared with integrin α6 (−8.7 kcal/mol) or integrin α3 (4.4 kcal/mol) (Supplementary Fig.  ). In line with that, integrin α5 showed the strongest interaction with GST-tagged Gal-3 in cell lysates and membrane lysates of HMEC-1 cells (Fig.  ). Functionally, knockdown of integrin α5 in HMEC-1 cells significantly inhibited Gal-3-induced cell migration, whereas knockdown of integrin α6 or α3 exerted no effects (Supplementary Fig.  ). Gal-3 mediated cell migration was also inhibited by ~64% with integrin α5 functional blocking antibody (P1D6) (Fig.  ). Together, these data strongly suggest that the α5 subunit is a major partner with the β1 subunit in mediating Gal-3-induced angiogenesis. In agreement, Gal-3 mediated cell migration was inhibited by ~56% with the integrin α5β1 inhibitor (ATN-161) (Fig.  ). The interaction between Gal-3 and integrin α5β1 was also confirmed by co-localization of Gal-3 and integrin α5 through immunofluorescence assay (Supplementary Fig.  ).
The integrin complex regulates downstream signals through the activation of the integrin β1 subunit via its phosphorylation . In HMEC-1 cells, Gal-3 treatment induced the phosphorylation of the β1 subunit in a time-dependent manner (Fig.  ). Moreover, phosphorylation of integrin β1 was increased in the skin of HFD/STZ mice after local overexpression of Gal-3 (Fig.  ). In conclusion, these results suggest that Gal-3 promotes migration and tube formation in endothelial cells by binding integrin α5β1 and potentiating its activation.

## Blockage of the integrin α5β1-FAK pathway inhibits the beneficial effect of Gal-3 on diabetic wound healing
To further investigate the functional role of integrin α5β1 in wound healing in vivo, we administered the integrin β1 function-blocking antibody to the wound overexpressing Gal-3 via adenovirus in STZ-induced diabetic rats (Supplementary Fig.  ). Compared with Gal-3 overexpression alone, inhibition of integrin β1 reduced Gal-3-mediated healing significantly on day 9 post wounding (Fig.  ). The thickness of epidermis and granulation tissue in healed skin was significantly decreased (Fig.  ); the percentage of type I collagen in healed granulation tissues was inhibited by ~31% (Fig.  ); western blotting also showed decreased type I collagen (Supplementary Fig.  ). Vascular density was significantly diminished by ~34% (Fig.  ), and protein levels of CD31 were reduced in healed granulation tissues (Fig.  ). These data indicate that blocking integrin β1 effectively inhibits Gal-3-induced wound healing in diabetic rats. 
Next, we examined whether integrin α5β1 of skin endothelial cells is essential for the Gal-3 mediated pro-healing effect in diabetes. Adenovirus encoding Gal-3 was injected into the wound edge of diabetic rats, whereas conditional knockdown of integrin α5 in skin endothelial cells was performed via local injection of AAV2/VEC-TIE-shRNA virus (Supplementary Fig.  ). Strikingly, the reduced proportion of type I collagen and vascular density in diabetic rats were completely reversed by Gal-3 overexpression; however, the Gal-3 pro-healing effects were abolished upon endothelial knockdown of integrin α5 (Fig.  ), indicating the indispensable role of integrin α5 in mediating Gal-3 function. Taken together, we conclude that integrin α5β1 is the functional receptor of Gal-3 in regulation of angiogenesis and diabetic wound healing.
We next explored how integrin β1 regulated Gal-3-mediated wound healing by promoting neovascularization. For this, we assessed the role of FAK, Src and IKK as downstream signaling molecules of integrin β1 , . Results showed that Gal-3-induced cell migration was blocked by FAK or Src-inhibitor (PF573228 or 1-Naphthyl PP1, respectively), but not by IKK inhibitor (IKK 16) (Fig.  ), indicating that both FAK and Src may function as downstream signals for integrin β1. Presently, it is known that phosphorylation of FAK upon integrin β1 activation serves as the primary downstream signal, which subsequently leads to activation of Src . In line with this model, FAK phosphorylation was increased in a time-dependent fashion after Gal-3-treatment in HMEC-1 cells (Fig.  ). FAK inhibitor (FAKi) abolished Gal-3-mediated FAK phosphorylation (Supplementary Fig.  ), followed by inhibition of tube formation (Fig.  ), thus confirming that FAK kinase activation is important for Gal-3-induced wound healing. Conversely, knockdown of integrin β1 significantly decreased Gal-3-induced FAK phosphorylation (Fig.  ). These findings strongly suggest that FAK serves as a crucial downstream molecule in the Gal-3-integrin α5β1 pathway that regulates angiogenesis in vitro. To verify the role of FAK in Gal-3-mediated diabetic wound healing in vivo, FAKi was injected into the wound edge of HFD/STZ mice that locally overexpressed Gal-3. In mice treated with FAKi, the percentage of type I collagen in wound granulation tissues was decreased by ~41%, and total collagen was also attenuated though no significance (Fig.  ), and vascular density was reduced by ~35% (Fig.  ). Therefore, the beneficial effect of Gal-3 in diabetic wound healing occurs via the integrin α5β1-FAK signaling axis.

## Gal-3 binds to integrin α5β1 to induce liquid-liquid phase separation that promotes in vitro angiogenesis
To clarify how Gal-3 regulates integrin α5β1, we first asked whether Gal-3 intervention enhances integrin β1 expression. Our data show that Gal-3 treatment did not alter mRNA level of integrin β1 (Supplementary Fig.  ), as well as protein level on the cell membrane as determined by the biotin-labeled pull-down assay (Supplementary Fig.  ). Interestingly, the immunofluorescence assay showed many clusters of integrin α5β1 on the cell membrane upon administration of Gal-3 (Fig.  ). These clusters were diminished when β-lactose (competitive binding partner of Gal-3) was added to the medium (Fig.  ), implicating that exogenous Gal-3 might form droplet-like condensates with integrin α5β1 on the cell membrane, a characteristic of phase separation. To validate this, we monitored phase separation by microscopy imaging with mixtures of GFP-Gal-3 and integrin β1, whereas CD146 as a positive control because it has been shown to undergo Gal-3 induced LLPS (Supplementary Fig.  ). Strikingly, GFP-Gal-3 formed micrometer-sized droplets with integrin β1, which was diminished upon addition of lactose (Fig.  and Supplementary Fig.  ). Consistent with this observation, Gal-3 induced phase separation across a range of concentrations (2, 4, 10, and 20 μM) (Supplementary Fig.  ). Additionally, the turbidity of mixtures containing Gal-3 and integrin β1 was increased compared to Gal-3 alone, an effect that was also mitigated by lactose (Fig.  ). The droplet-like condensates of Gal-3 and integrin α5β1 were also observed, while diminished upon lactose treatment (Fig.  ), showing increased solution turbidity in a Gal-3 concentration dependent manner (Fig.  ). Next, fluorescence recovery after photobleaching (FRAP) experiments were applied to examine the mobility of molecules within the condensates. After bleaching, the fluorescence of Gal-3/integrin β1, Gal-3/integrin α5β1, and Gal-3/CD146 condensates recovered in ~90 s, ~60 s and ~80 s, respectively (Fig.  ). The recovery kinetics suggest dynamic molecular exchange within these condensates, consistent with a highly mobile state. LLPS of Gal-3/integrin β1, and its attenuation by lactose, suggesting that this effect is mediated by N-glycans on integrin β1. 
Integrin α5β1 is a membrane protein with 14 proposed glycosylation sites for the α5 subunit and 12 on the β1 subunit in extracellular domain according to the UniProt database (https://www.uniprot.org/). Binding to these glycans is the primary way for Gal-3 to mediate regulation of signal transduction . To determine whether N-glycosylation is required for integrin α5β1 to undergo LLPS with Gal-3, we treated integrin α5β1 with peptide-N-glycosidase F (PNGase F), an N-glycan hydrolyzing endoglycosidase. On SDS-PAGE, de-gylcosylated α5β1 showed a faster migration band compared to corresponding untreated proteins (Supplementary Fig.  ). Consistently, the number and size of condensates formed with GFP-Gal-3 and de-glycosylated α5β1 was significantly decreased (Fig.  ), suggesting that Gal-3 binds to N-glycans on integrin α5β1 to induce phase separation.
We next asked whether Gal-3 could phase separate with integrin α5β1 in live cells to exert biological regulation. Stable overexpression of mCherry-tagged integrin α5 was successfully achieved in CHO-K1 cells following lentiviral transfection (Supplementary Fig.  ). The addition of GFP-Gal-3 to the cells resulted in the formation of droplet-like condensates, showing co-localization of GFP-Gal-3 and mCherry-integrin α5 puncta (Fig.  ). Treatment with 1,6-hexanediol (1,6-HD), an agent that disrupts droplet-like condensates , dramatically reduced the number and size of droplet-like condensates (Fig.  ). As a control, GFP-Gal-3 did not induce condensate formation in mCherry expressing cells (Supplementary Fig.  ). The Gal-3/integrin α5 condensates formed in a time-dependent manner, with noticeable aggregation at 5 minutes, whereas larger and more numerous condensates were observed after 15 minutes (Supplementary Fig.  ). Additionally, at low-concentration gradients, we found a small number of condensates at the cell membrane (Supplementary Fig.  ). FRAP experiments illustrated a rapid recovery of the fluorescence of Gal-3/integrin α5 condensates in cells after photobleaching, suggesting dynamic molecular mobility, often associated with viscoelastic properties (Fig.  ). Importantly, the phosphorylation of FAK induced by Gal-3 was entirely blocked by lactose or 1,6-HD (Fig.  ), highlighting the critical role of phase separation in Gal-3-mediated integrin α5β1 downstream activation. These data indicate that Gal-3 undergoes phase separation with integrin α5 in cells, enhancing FAK phosphorylation and cellular function via forming LLPS.
Gal-3 can phase separate with α5β1 or CD146, respectively. We therefore explored whether they can co-phase separate into the same condensates. CHO-K1 cells were transfected with lentivirus encoding mCherry-tagged integrin α5 and mTagBFP2-tagged CD146, and dual-tagged expressing cells were sorted by flow cytometry. Very interestingly, upon adding GFP-Gal-3 to these cells, triple-positive fluorescent condensates of Gal-3, integrin α5 and CD146 formed on the cell membrane. These triple-positive fluorescent of Gal-3/integrin α5/CD146 condensates were disrupted by lactose treatment but not sucrose; notably, 1,6-HD could disintegrate them, thus reducing their number and size (Fig.  ). To resolve the effects of triple-protein condensates on Gal-3-induced angiogenesis, we knocked down integrin β1 and CD146 individually or concurrently in HMEC-1 cells (Supplementary Fig.  ). Knockdown of either integrin β1 or CD146 alone decreased Gal-3-induced tube formation by ~52% and ~59%, respectively. In contrast, simultaneous knockdown of integrin β1 and CD146 synergistically inhibited this by ~92% (Fig.  and Supplementary Fig.  ), suggesting that both integrin β1 and CD146 are critical for tube formation and act synergistically in combo and exert a more pronounced effect. In general, Gal-3 mediates the activation of downstream signaling pathways and angiogenesis mainly by forming LLPS on the cell membrane.

## AGEs suppress Gal-3-mediated angiogenesis by inhibiting Gal-3/integrin α5β1 interaction
We next explored the physiological relevance of Gal-3/integrin α5β1 phase separation in diabetes. The serum of diabetic patients was added to the Gal-3/integrin α5β1 phase separation system to simulate the diabetic microenvironment, and the particle size was significantly decreased compared with non-diabetic serum (Supplementary Fig.  ), suggesting the inhibited phase separation, which is consistent with the impaired angiogenesis and wound healing in diabetes. Indeed, incubation of human diabetic serum (7.5%, v/v) in endothelial cells largely obstructed Gal-3-mediated tube formation (Fig.  and Supplementary Fig.  ). Interestingly, endothelial cells under an insulin-resistant state (induced by hyper-insulin addition) failed to exhibit impairment of Gal-3-mediated tube formation (Fig.  and Supplementary Fig.  ), suggesting the inhibition of diabetic serum on Gal-3-regulated angiogenesis may be independent of insulin resistance. Given that Gal-3 levels are actually elevated in the serum of diabetic patients , whereas reduced in the vasculature of diabetic skin tissues (Fig.  ) and failed to promote angiogenesis in diabetes as shown above, we therefore postulated that certain components in circulation may block the Gal-3 function in diabetic skin. AGEs (glycated proteins or lipids) aberrantly elevate in serum and skin wound of diabetic patients , . The primary types of AGEs include the non-fluorescent adducts, Nε-carboxymethyllysine (CML), and Nε-carboxyethyllysine (CEL), as well as fluorescent cross-linked structure, pentosidine and argpyrimidine. CML was identified as the most prevalent type in diabetes . AGEs were previously reported as a direct binding target with Gal-3 . Therefore, we hypothesized that AGEs may impede the pro-angiogenic effect of Gal-3 by disrupting its interaction with integrin α5β1. In this regard, AGEs significantly reduced Gal-3-mediated tube formation of endothelial cells in a dose-dependent manner (Fig.  and Supplementary Fig.  ). The AGEs used in this study were identified as CML-BSA . Concurrently, phosphorylation of integrin β1 and binding of Gal-3 to integrin β1 were both blocked by AGEs (Fig.  ). Furthermore, the previously reported internalization of Gal-3 in vascular endothelial cells , was also hindered by AGEs (Supplementary Fig.  ). Overall, these data demonstrate that accumulated AGEs in diabetic serum inhibit binding of Gal-3 to integrin α5β1, as well as Gal-3-induced integrin α5β1 activation and downstream effects. 
On the molecular level, Heteronuclear Single Quantum Coherence (HSQC) NMR showed that 15N-labeled Gal-3 bound to both integrin β1 and AGEs, as illustrated with 1H-15N-weighted Gal-3 chemical shift changes (Δδ) for the binding of integrin β1 and AGEs (Fig.  ). Chemical shift maps that plot Δδ vs. Gal-3 sequence indicate that the majority of significant shift changes occur within the Gal-3 CRD S-face, consistent with glycan binding to Gal-3 (Fig.  ). Maximal values were reached at ~0.5 μM for integrin β1 (Fig.  ) and at 2 μM for AGEs (Supplementary Fig.  ), indicating that Gal-3 binds about 4-fold more strongly to integrin β1 than to AGEs. This implies that integrin β1 is more heavily glycosylated than AGEs, has a greater binding affinity than AGEs, and/or simply has an overall greater avidity for integrin β1 than for AGEs. Moreover, these interactions were inhibited by lactose (Supplementary Fig.  ), indicating glycan-mediated binding to the canonical, sugar-binding S-face of Gal-3. Furthermore, Gal-3 bound to AGEs ~100-fold more strongly than to bovine serum albumin (BSA) (Supplementary Fig.  ), consistent with AGEs being more glycosylated than BSA. More importantly, AGEs competed with Gal-3 for binding to integrin β1, as evidenced by biolayer interferometry (BLI) sensorgrams that yield a Kd of 6.43 μM for the interaction between Gal-3 and integrin β1, showing a significant reduction in the sensorgram signal when AGEs are added to Gal-3 and integrin β1 solution (Fig.  and Supplementary Fig.  ). These data further confirm that AGEs can suppress the binding of Gal-3 to integrin β1. Furthermore, administration of AGEs inhibited condensation of integrin β1 and GFP-Gal-3, showing reduced number and size of particles (Fig.  ) compared with BSA-treated controls. Consistently, AGEs additively reduced the number and size of the Gal-3/integrin α5 condensates in cells, suggesting the inhibition of phase separation (Fig.  ). Therefore, we conclude that AGEs inhibit the formation of LLPS between Gal-3 and integrin α5β1 and blunt the downstream signaling pathways in the diabetic state.
To further investigate the role of AGEs in mediating Gal-3 regulation of diabetic wound healing in vivo, we manipulated AGE levels by using a specific inhibitor. Hydrogels are three-dimensional networks with a cross-linked structure, thus being more efficient at local and long-term drug release . Therefore, we employed hydrogels containing the AGEs inhibitor (AGEi) pyridoxamine in the diabetic rat dorsal wound model. Notably, AGEs levels in wounded skin tissue were dramatically elevated in diabetic rats, and significantly decreased by ~71% upon treatment with pyridoxamine (Supplementary Fig.  ). Consistently, absolute quantification of CML using liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC–MS/MS) showed a significant reduction of ~63% upon AGEs inhibitor treatment (Supplementary Fig.  ). Correspondingly, wound closure rates were reduced in diabetic rats but improved significantly on days 3 and 9 with AGEi treatment (Fig.  ). The same trend was observed with the vascular density in wounds (Fig.  ). Additionally, the reduced phosphorylated-integrin β1 was also increased by ~60% upon treatment with AGEi (Supplementary Fig.  ), suggesting the restoration of impaired Gal-3-integrin α5β1 signaling under diabetes. Collectively, reducing the amounts of AGEs in vivo improves diabetic wound healing by enhancing the effects from the Gal-3-integrin α5β1 axis.

## Hydrogels loaded with Gal-3 promote diabetic skin wound healing in vivo
Insulin, the therapeutic agent used for the management of diabetes, and attempts at DFUs treatment , , reduced the amounts of AGEs in circulation , . Further, insulin treatment could promote skin wound healing in HFD/STZ rats . To explore whether Gal-3 synergizes with insulin in promoting diabetic wound healing, recombinant Gal-3 and insulin were administered to the wound edge of diabetic rats. The amount of AGEs present in healed wounds decreased by ~23% after Gal-3 treatment and ~16% after insulin treatment. Combo treatment included both Gal-3 and insulin, however, decreased the amount of AGEs in healed wound by ~54% (Supplementary Fig.  ). As expected, the epidermal and granulation thickness was increased upon treatment of Gal-3 or insulin alone. The increase was more robust following the combo treatment with the Gal-3 and insulin (Fig.  ). The percentage of type I collagen was increased after Gal-3, insulin, and combo treatment, being statistically significant for the Gal-3 and combo treatment, and with total collagen deposition being enhanced after the insulin and combo treatment (Fig.  ). Moreover, vessel density increased both in Gal-3 and insulin groups, and much more in the combo group (Fig.  ). Thus, we conclude that administration of Gal-3 and insulin synergistically promotes diabetic wound healing and may serve as a potential therapy for DFUs. 
A previous report showed that Gal-3 was susceptible to degradation in the circulation . Thus, a thermosensitive hydrogel, designed for sustained delivery of Gal-3, has been developed to treat diabetic skin wounds, whereas the potent pro-angiogenic factor VEGF packed in a similar type of hydrogel was used as a positive control . Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) has shown that neither Gal-3 nor VEGF changed the structure or pore size of hydrogels (Supplementary Fig.  ). The kinetics of released Gal-3 or VEGF from the hydrogel scaffolds were shown in the release curves (Supplementary Fig.  ). The release of Gal-3 from the hydrogel scaffold reached ~700 ng over 48 hours (Supplementary Fig.  ), which is close to the dose of 1000 ng recombinant Gal-3 administered to the topical wound once every two days, with effective promotion of wound healing in the above experiments. On the other hand, the release of VEGF from the hydrogel scaffold reached ~7 ng over 48 hours (Supplementary Fig.  ), a similar value that has been reported to have therapeutic benefits on angiogenesis in diabetic wounds . The hydrogels themselves exhibited no significant cytotoxicity as indicated by cell-counting assays (Supplementary Fig.  ). These results support the observation of sustained release, as well as the safety of hydrogels infused with Gal-3 or VEGF.
Hydrogel embedded with Gal-3 or VEGF was topically applied to wounds in STZ-diabetic rats, compared with commercial Comfeel® dressing (containing carboxymethylcellulose and one layer of elastic adhesive tape) that is used for exuding wounds, including foot ulcers . As expected, the wound closure rate of diabetic rats was significantly delayed compared with non-diabetics; Gal-3 significantly promoted wound healing 3 days and 9 days after application, which was comparable to that of non-diabetic rats and Comfeel® dressing; VEGF showed a strong trend of accelerated skin wound healing, albeit without statistical significance (Supplementary Fig.  ). Notably, topical administration of Gal-3-loaded hydrogel did not alter plasma Gal-3 levels (Supplementary Fig.  ). The treatment of Gal-3 hydrogel increased the percentage of type I collagen, total collagen deposition, and vessel density, similar to that of the VEGF group and Comfeel® dressing groups (Supplementary Fig.  ). Collagen fibers, which occupy ~85% to 90% dry weight of skin, are essential for tensile strength and mature gradually during wound healing . In diabetic rats, collagen fibril diameters in the dermal ultrastructure underwent a transition toward thinner dimensions observed by TEM. Gal-3 treatment partially reversed this change by thickening the fibril diameters (Supplementary Fig.  ). Specifically, Gal-3 treatment showed a decrease in the proportion of fibrils with diameters ranging from 60–100 nm, accompanied by an increase in those larger than ~120 nm (Supplementary Fig.  ). Similarly, VEGF treatment and Comfeel® dressing also thickened the fibril diameters with the increased proportion of fibrils in the range of 120–180 nm (Supplementary Fig.  ). Collectively, these data indicate that hydrogels embedded with Gal-3 provide a benefit for diabetic wound healing as effectively as VEGF and commercial Comfeel® dressing. Further, the pro-wound healing effect of Gal-3 can be enhanced by topical co-administration with insulin.
To further evaluate the therapeutic potential of Gal-3 under more severe wound conditions, we expanded our experiments to include the STZ-induced db/db mouse model. In this model, a relatively low dose of STZ (40 mg/kg) was administered to mimic type 2 diabetes. Additionally, Comfeel® dressing was used as a positive control. Our findings indicate that treatment with the Gal-3/PM hydrogel significantly enhanced skin wound closure rates by 20.4%, 16.9%, 29.3%, and 23.6% on days 5, 7, 9, and 11, respectively, compared to the db/db group, achieving outcomes comparable to Comfeel® dressing (Fig.  ). Furthermore, Picrosirius red staining revealed that the proportion of type I collagen relative to the total type I and III collagen content increased by approximately 53% in the healed skin wound granulation tissue, accompanied by a significant elevation in total collagen deposition (Fig.  ). Vascular density was also significantly elevated following Gal-3 treatment (Fig.  ). To assess the functional efficacy of Gal-3-induced neovascularization, Laser Doppler perfusion measurements were conducted throughout the wound healing process (days 0, 3, 7, 9, and 11). The perfusion levels in the Gal-3/PM hydrogel group were markedly higher than those in the db/db group, reaching levels comparable to the Comfeel® dressing (Supplementary Fig.  ). Moreover, three-dimensional reconstruction of the vascular network in healing wounds demonstrated substantial increases in total vascular volume (422.1%), surface area (617.3%), and branch number (478.2%) in the Gal-3/PM hydrogel-treated group compared to db/db group (Supplementary Fig.  ). Taken together, these results provide strong evidence of Gal-3’s therapeutic potential in promoting diabetic wound healing.

## Discussion
To date, the role of Gal-3 in diabetic wound healing remains poorly understood. In this study, we discovered that Gal-3 significantly enhances diabetic wound healing, with integrin α5β1 as a functional receptor of Gal-3. In the presence of integrin α5β1, Gal-3 induces LLPS formation that further promotes angiogenesis in skin. In the diabetic state, moreover, AGEs accumulate and inhibit the interaction between Gal-3 and integrin α5β1, thus perturbing downstream FAK signaling-dependent angiogenesis. Because of these findings, we found that topical application of Gal-3-infused hydrogels presents a promising way to enhance diabetic wound healing, whereas Gal-3 and insulin treatment exert more profound effects. Our working model of Gal-3 regulation in diabetic wound healing is summarized in Fig.  . 
Based on the previous report, Gal-3 may delay diabetic wound healing by inducing insulin resistance in liver, muscle, and adipose tissues in diabetic mice . Therefore, our study was required to elucidate the precise role of Gal-3 in diabetic wound healing. Here, we demonstrated that skin overexpression of Gal-3 increases the wound closure rate in STZ-induced and HFD/STZ-induced diabetic mice. Histologically, the vascular density and collagen deposition in the granulation tissue of healed skin wounds were significantly increased upon treatment with Gal-3. Notably, previous studies reported that Gal-3 whole body knockout mice showed no effect on skin wound healing . Our results in mice demonstrated that local application of a Gal-3 inhibitor indeed suppressed skin wound healing. This apparent conundrum suggests that certain factors, such as compensation of Gal-3 by other proteins, may alter wound healing in Gal-3 knockout mice, a hypothesis that required further investigation.
Our study also found that administration of Gal-3 on skin did not worsen insulin resistance in its major targeted organs, such as liver, which may be due to unelevated levels of Gal-3 in the circulation. Moreover, insulin resistance in skin tissue was not aggravated by topical application of Gal-3, as it was in skin dermal vascular endothelial cells. Because of this, we speculated that in diabetes, variation of N-glycans on the insulin receptor in different tissues may contribute to the discrepancy in insulin sensitivity. By binding to N-glycans, Gal-3 inhibits activation of the insulin receptor, an effect that is likely due to conformational changes , and finally leading to insulin resistance. N-glycans on the insulin receptor can be altered with changes in physical or pathological conditions . This suggests that these N-glycans may be tissue-specific, resulting in Gal-3-induced insulin resistance that is tissue-type dependent. Furthermore, Gal-3-loaded hydrogels accelerated wound healing without aggregated insulin resistance, representing a safe approach to treat diabetic wounds. This therapeutic treatment is as effective as using pro-angiogenic VEGF-loaded hydrogels and Comfeel® dressing, indicating the potency of Gal-3 in the application of treating DFUs. Given the previously reported allergic contact dermatitis due to the usage of Comfeel® dressing , hydrogels embedded with multiple pro-angiogenic factors (Gal-3, VEGF, etc) may serve as a new promising treatment for diabetic wound healing.
Because Gal-3, like other lectins, binds to many glycoproteins on the cell surface, it is essential to identify which binders are functionally crucial to diabetic wound healing. Ours and other previous studies found that Gal-3 could bind to CD146 which promotes endothelial cell migration and tube formation , . However, knocking down CD146 only decreases Gal-3-induced cell migration by ~35%, suggesting that other unidentified receptors are also involved , . In the present study, we identified integrin α5β1 as an important functional receptor. Integrin α5β1 is a member of the integrin family that regulates growth, tissue repair, angiogenesis, inflammation and hemostasis . In turn, Gal-3 promotes wound healing in vivo by binding to and potentiating phosphorylation of integrin subunit β1, an activity that can be blocked by an integrin α5β1 antibody. Interestingly, both integrin β1 and CD146 are indispensable but sufficient for Gal-3-mediated angiogenesis. Knockdown of integrin β1 or CD146 inhibits nearly 50% of Gal-3-mediated tube formation, whereas simultaneous knockdown of integrin β1 and CD146 almost completely blocks Gal-3-mediated tube formation. Defining Gal-3 receptors in vascular endothelial cells advances our understanding of the mechanism of action of Gal-3 in wound healing and provides direction for development of therapeutic targets to treat diabetic wound healing.
LLPS is a process in which a homogeneous liquid solution of macromolecular components separates into two distinct phases. LLPS has recently emerged as a widespread mechanism for intracellular organization of various molecules . For instance, LLPS facilitates MAPK signaling and actin polymerization in T cells and regulates chromatin-compartmentalization by histone modifications , . In the present study, we found that Gal-3 induces LLPS in the presence of integrin α5β1 and promotes phosphorylation of the integrin α5β1-FAK axis, an effect that can be inhibited by Gal-3-binding lactose and 1,6-HD. These observations demonstrate that LLPS is indispensable for activation of integrin α5β1 and FAK induced by Gal-3. Interestingly, we found that Gal-3, integrin α5β1 and CD146 can co-phase separate into the same condensates, thus acting synergistically in combo to promote angiogenesis. Based on our current results that both integrin α5β1 and CD146 are indispensable for Gal-3-mediated angiogenesis, we speculate the LLPS forming by ligand and its two receptors may serve as “microenvironment” to facilitate downstream signal transduction, and promotes angiogenesis and wound healing. Collectively, our results provide new mechanistic insight into the role of Gal-3 in regulating chronic diabetic wound healing.
Serum levels of Gal-3 are increased in patients that are obese and/or have diabetes . Interestingly, we found that Gal-3 was decreased in vasculature of diabetic skin. Furthermore, we found that Gal-3-mediated angiogenesis was inhibited by human diabetic serum. Speculatively, certain requisite components in blood may block Gal-3 binding to membrane receptors, a pivotal process for Gal-3 functional regulation . AGEs are heterogeneous substances produced by non-enzymatic reactions between reducing sugars, proteins, lipids or nucleic acids . Compared to normal individuals, obese or diabetic patients produce more AGEs in their plasma and accumulate in skin wounds , thus providing more glycans for Gal-3 binding . Our data showed that integrin α5β1-FAK axis activation is greatly suppressed by AGEs in a glycation-dependent manner. Thus, in the diabetic state, accumulated AGEs likely diminish pro-angiogenic effects from Gal-3. Notably, we hypothesize that the reduction of local Gal-3 in the intracellular of endothelial cells of diabetic skin is primarily driven by AGEs accumulation, which inhibits Gal-3 internalization. However, we cannot rule out the possibility that transcriptional alterations across various cell types under diabetic conditions may also play a role. Meanwhile, Gal-3 recombinant protein promoted diabetic skin wound healing and demonstrated effects comparable to, or even more potent than, those of insulin. Importantly, we discovered that combination of insulin with Gal-3 is more beneficial in healing diabetic wounds. Given that almost all therapeutic drugs for diabetes may decrease AGEs content because of reduction of blood glucose, it is reasonable to propose that Gal-3 may act synergistically with diabetes drugs (GLP-1R agonist, SGLT-2 inhibitor, etc) in treating DFUs, highlighting the great potential of Gal-3 in clinical application, which requires further investigation.
In total, our study has found that Gal-3 promotes angiogenesis and diabetic wound healing by binding to its functional receptor integrin α5β1 via LLPS formation. Therapeutically, we discovered that combination of insulin with Gal-3 further enhances effects from Gal-3 alone in treating diabetic wounds. Lastly, we observed that topically applied hydrogels infused with Gal-3 provide a promising tool in the treatment of DFUs.

## Limitations of study
First, further research needs to be carried out to identify the exact site that Gal-3 interacts with integrin α5β1. This, in turn, may lead to the discovery or development of small molecules that could be substituted for Gal-3. Second, despite angiogenesis represents critical stage for wound healing, other processes including epithelial-mesenchymal transition of keratinocytes, inflammation, etc are also involved. Thus, the comprehensive role and mechanism of Gal-3 in regulating diabetic wound healing at multiple stages need to be investigated.

## Methods
This research was approved by the Ethics Committee of Sun Yat-sen Memorial Hospital, Sun Yat-sen University, and complies with all relevant ethical regulations.

## Human skin and blood serum samples
Perilesional skin tissue was obtained from six male patients with DFUs who had ulcers below the ankle that persisted for more than one month without healing. The wound infection was effectively controlled, as evidenced by the absence of fever, significant purulent exudate, redness, or swelling on the wound surface; there was no notable elevation in skin temperature compared to the healthy side, and inflammatory markers such as hemogram parameters, C-reactive protein (CRP), and procalcitonin (PCT) had returned to normal levels. After cleaning the wounds, we collected granulation tissue measuring 2 mm × 2 mm between the ulcers and non-ulcers tissues. Non-diabetic control skin tissue was collected from the lower extremities of six patients (three males and three females) without diabetes who underwent reconstruction due to foot injury. To investigate the effect of diabetic serum on Gal-3-mediated angiogenesis in vitro, serum from six diabetic patients (three males and three females) and seven non-diabetic patients (five males and two females) was collected. All participants provided written informed consent and the sex of the participants was randomly selected and not specifically designated. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Sun Yat-sen Memorial Hospital and is congruent with the Declaration of Helsinki (NO. SYSKY-2024-697-01).

## Animals
Male Sprague-Dawley (SD) rats (6–8 weeks old) were obtained from Sun Yat-sen University Animal Center (Guangzhou, China), and male or female C57BL/6 J mice (8 weeks old) were purchased from Zhuhai BesTest (Zhuhai, China). Male C57BL/KsJ-db/db mice and their littermate controls C57BL/KsJ-+/+ (8 weeks old) were purchased from GemPharmatech (Guangdong, China). Animals were housed under a 12 h light/dark cycle at room temperature (22 ± 2 oC) with food and water provided ad libitum in a specific pathogen free (SPF) environment. The experiments were blind to treatment grouping information during the experiment and quantification. All animal experiments were conducted in accordance with the ARRIVE guidelines. All procedures conducted on animals were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Sun Yat-sen University and performed following institutional guidelines (NO. SYSU-IACUC-2021-B1795).

## Diabetic wound models
Rats were intraperitoneally injected with streptozotocin (STZ, 65 mg/kg, 18883-66-4, Sigma, USA) dissolved in 0.1 M citrate buffer (pH 4.2) to establish diabetic model. All rats were fed a chow diet (fbsh, P1101, China). Rats were evaluated 2 days later and a 6-hour fasting glucose levels ≥ 16.7 mmol/L, along with weight loss, polyuria and polydipsia, were considered developing diabetes model . To establish a type 2 diabetic model, mice were randomly divided into two groups according to body weight. The diabetic group was fed a 60% high-fat diet (HFD, TP23300, TROPHIC, China), while the control group received a 10% low-fat diet (LFD, TP23302, TROPHIC) for 6 weeks. Then, diabetic mice were intraperitoneally injected with STZ (100 mg/kg) and maintained on the HFD until sacrifice. Age-matched, LFD-fed mice injected intraperitoneally with citrate buffer were used as controls. One month later, HFD/STZ mice with a 6-hour fasting glucose levels ≥ 11.1 mmol/L were considered to have developed diabetes model . To establish a more severe diabetes model, db/db mice were intraperitoneally injected with a low dose of STZ (40 mg/kg) to induce pancreatic islet damage. In non-treated db/db mice, the random blood glucose level was 23.5 ± 0.77 mmol/L. One month after STZ injection, the blood glucose level increased significantly to 30.8 ± 0.89 mmol/L in db/db + STZ mice, confirming that STZ administration exacerbated hyperglycemia in db/db mice . All db/db mice were fed a chow diet (fbsh, P1101, China). After the establishment of these diabetic rodent models, rats or mice were anesthetized with isoflurane followed by skin shaving, depilating, and rinsing with alcohol. A circular excisional, full-thickness skin wound with a diameter of 20 mm or 8 mm was created on the back of each rat or mouse. Male rats/mice were used for diabetes induction because they are more sensitive to STZ, resulting in higher modeling success rates and greater stability. This approach ensures the reliability and reproducibility of the experimental data.

## Treatments of animals
For overexpression of Gal-3, the lagls3 gene encoding Gal-3 in adenovirus (GenBank accession no. NM_031832.1; HY20200911YXY-AD01, Hanbio, China) was intracutaneously (i.c.) injected into the peri-wound skin at a dose of 5.0 × 109 PFU/rat or 2.0 × 109 PFU/mouse after wounding . For Gal-3 inhibition, GB1107 (100 μM for male and 133 μM for female, HY-114409, MCE, USA) was i.c. injected in the peri-wound skin of wildtype C57BL/6 J mice at 100 μL/mouse every two days until sacrifice .
For knockdown of integrin α5, recombinant AAV2/VEC-TIE-mCherry vector encoding shRNA targeting integrin α5 (AAV-sh-ITGA5) or the empty vector (AAV-shscramble) (HH20240403FTM-AAV01) (Hanbio, China) was i.c. injected into the dorsal skin of rat at a dose of 1 × 1010 vector genomes (vg) per square centimeter two weeks before wounding.
For functional blockage of integrin β1, anti-integrin β1 antibody (100 μg/rat, 555003, BD Biosciences, USA) was i.c. injected into the peri-wound skin of rats after wounding .
For functional blockage of focal adhesion kinase (FAK), FAK tyrosine kinase inhibitor PF573228 (15 μM, S2013, Selleck, USA) was dissolved in 0.1% DMSO and administered via i.c. injection around the wound site with 100 μL/mouse every two days until sacrifice .
Endotoxin-free recombinant Gal-3 (1 μg/rat) was prepared as described previously and was i.c. injected into the peri-wound skin of rats every day until sacrifice. Insulin (0.1 U/rat, L5500, Sigma, USA) was drop added to the wounds once every day .
For hydrogel experiments, a thermosensitive hydrogel based on Pluronic F-127 (PF) and methylcellulose (MC) was prepared (named PM hydrogel hereafter) as previously described for drug delivery . Briefly, 2% methylcellulose (M7027, Sigma, USA) was dissolved in PBS at 60 °C for 5 min, followed by addition of 15% Pluronic F-127 (PF-127, P2443, Sigma, USA). This mixture was stored at 4 °C until completely dissolved. Gal-3, VEGF (11066-HNAH, Sino biological, China) or the inhibitor of AGEs (AGEi) pyridoxamine dihydrochloride (P9380, Sigma, USA) were mixed with PM hydrogel at a final concentration of 100 μg/mL (Gal-3), 150 ng/mL (VEGF) and 100 mg/mL (AGEi), respectively. Dressing Comfeel®(3533, Coloplast, Denmark) without hydrogels was directly applied to the wound. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM, HIACHI Regulus 8100, Japan) was used to observe the morphology of the hydrogels.
To further validate release of Gal-3/VEGF/PM hydrogels in vitro, human Gal-3 and VEGF ELISA kits (EK0764; EK0539, Boster, China) were used to measure the concentration of Gal-3 and VEGF released from Gal-3/PM hydrogel and VEGF/PM hydrogel according to manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, 100 μL Gal-3/PM hydrogels or VEGF/PM hydrogels were added to a 24-well plate and incubated at 37 °C for 30 min, followed by addition of 1 mL phosphate buffered saline (PBS). Supernatants obtained at the indicated time were used to quantify the release of Gal-3 or VEGF.
To evaluate the cytotoxicity from Gal-3/VEGF/PM hydrogels, a cell-counting kit-8 (CCK-8) assay was used to assess HMEC-1 cell viability in response to Gal-3/VEGF/PM hydrogel intervention. Cells (1 × 104/well) were placed in a 96-well plate, then 100 μL of Gal-3/VEGF/PM hydrogel was immersed into 900 μL of medium for 48 h at 37 °C and centrifuged. 200 μL supernatant was added to the plate. After culturing for 48 h, the culture medium was replaced with 200 μL of fresh culture medium containing 10% CCK-8 regent (C0037, Beyotime, China). The absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a microplate absorbance reader (Sunrise, Tecan, Switzerland).
To apply PM hydrogels in vivo, the wound model in STZ-induced diabetic rats was established and housed as described previously (see above). 100 μL PM hydrogels or Comfeel® dressings were applied to the wounds, and then all wounds were covered with sterile gauze and bandage every two days until sacrifice.
All animals were monitored daily for signs of distress and were euthanized using carbon dioxide (CO₂) inhalation at the end of the experiment.

## Measurement of skin wound healing
High resolution images of wounds were photographed during the experiments. The wound area was measured using Fiji image processing software (ImageJ, National Institute of Health, USA) and standardized using a measurement ruler. The percentage of wound closure was calculated as previously reported : (initial wound area - actual wound area)/initial wound area × 100%.

## Blood serum and tissue collection
All rats and mice were sacrificed under anesthesia upon complete wound healing. Wound skin samples were collected and divided into multiple parts. Parts of healed skin samples were fixed and paraffin-embedded for histological analysis, parts were stored at −80 °C for western blotting, and parts were fixed with 2.5% glutaraldehyde stationary liquid for electron microscopic analysis. Blood samples were collected and centrifuged at 1500 × g for 10 min, then serum was stored at −80 °C for further measurements. Liver samples were collected and stored at −80 °C for further analysis.

## Measurements of biochemical indexes
Blood glucose levels were measured from the tail vein by glucometer (ACCU-CHEK Performa, Roche, Switzerland) two days after STZ induction. For fasting glucose, rats or mice were fasted for 6 h preliminarily, and blood glucose levels were assessed. Plasma biochemical parameters including alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), total cholesterol (TC), triglycerides (TG) and blood urea nitrogen (BUN), were measured using commercial assay kits (C009-2-1, C010-2-1, A111-1-1, A110-1-1, C013-2-1, NanJingJianCheng, China). Furthermore, serum Gal-3 was also assessed using Rat Gal-3 ELISA kits (CSB-EL012887RA, CUSABIO, China).

## Histopathological analysis
Skin tissues were fixed, embedded and sectioned (4 μm). Hematoxylin and Eosin (H&E) staining followed standard staining procedures. Picrosirius red staining was performed as previously described . Images were obtained using a polarizing light microscope (DM6B, Leica, Germany). Under polarized light, type I collagen appeared yellow, orange, or red, while type III collagen exhibited a green color in the healed wound. For immunohistochemistry (IHC), standard staining procedures were performed, images were captured with microscopy (BX63, Olympus, Japan) and CD31 staining was analyzed at ×200 magnification. All fields containing granulation tissue were captured and analyzed using Fiji ImageJ processing software (ImageJ, National Institute of Health, USA) or Image-Pro Plus software 6.0 (Media Cybernetics, USA) while more than three sections were captured for each animal. The following primary antibodies were used: Gal-3 (1:100, ab76245, Abcam, USA), Gal-1 (1:200, 11858-1-AP, Proteintech, China), Gal-7 (1:200, 16730-1-AP, Proteintech, China), CD31 (1:500, ab182981, Abcam, USA), AGEs (1:100, BS-1158R, Bioss, China), The following secondary antibodies were used: goat anti-mouse HRP-conjugated (1:500, CW0102, CWBIO, China), goat anti-rabbit HRP-conjugated (1:500, CW0103, CWBIO, China). For immunofluorescence, harvested skin were embedded in Tissue-Tek OCT and flash-frozen in isopentane cooled in liquid nitrogen. Staining is described as previously . Primary antibody used was anti-CD31 (1:500, ab182981, Abcam, USA), anti-N-cadherin (1:200, 66219-1-1 g, Proteintech, China), anti-E-cadherin (1:200, bs-10009R, Bioss, China). Secondary antibody used was goat anti-rabbit antibody (1:500, Alexa Fluor® 488, Abcam, UK), goat anti-mouse antibody (1:500, Alexa Fluor® 488, Abcam, UK), donkey anti-rabbit antibody (1:500, Alexa FluorTM 594, Invitrogen, USA). Representative images were recorded with microscopy (LSM780, Zeiss, Germany). For quantitative analysis, we randomly selected 3–5 fields (under a ×20 objective lens) in each tissue section based on the size of the granulation tissue. Image Pro Plus software (Media Cybernetics, USA) was used to calculate the areas of collagen I and collagen III based on their distinct staining patterns, and further calculated the total collagen (COL1 + COL3) and the ratio of collagen I to total collagen (COL1/(COL1 + COL3)).

## Cell culture
HMEC-1 cells (CRL-3243, ATCC, USA) were grown in MCDB 131 medium (10372019, Gibco, USA) containing 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco, USA), 10 mM L-glutamine (25030081, Gibco, USA), 10 ng/mL recombinant human epidermal growth factor (EGF, 105-04, Shanghai Prime Gene Biotech Co, Ltd, China), 1 μg/mL hydrocortisone (HY-N0583, MCE, USA), and 100 IU/mL penicillin and 100 μg/mL streptomycin. HUVECs (CRL-1730, ATCC, USA) were cultured in F-12K medium (SH30526.01, Hyclone), supplemented with 10% FBS, 0.1 mg/mL heparin, 30 μg/mL endothelial cell growth supplement, 100 IU/mL penicillin and 100 μg/mL streptomycin. HEK-293T cells (GNHu17, Cell Bank of the Chinese Academy of Sciences, China) were cultured in DMEM medium (11965092, Gibco, USA) with 10% FBS. Chinese hamster ovary (CHO-K1) cells (CCL-61, ATCC, USA) were cultured in F-12K medium with 10% FBS. Primary mouse hepatocytes were isolated from mouse liver and cultured in DMEM/F12 medium (11320033, Gibco, USA) with 10% FBS . All cell lines were incubated in a humidified incubator at 37 °C with 5% CO2.

## Gene manipulation
For overexpression of the human ITGA5 or MCAM genes, lentiviruses were produced by co-transfecting HEK-293T cells with two packaging plasmids (psPAX2 and pMD2.G) and integrin α5 or CD146 overexpression plasmids. The lentivirus was harvested from the supernatant of HEK-293T cells at 48 h post-transfection and concentrated. CHO-K1 cells were infected with virus and persistently maintained in medium with puromycin (30 µg/mL, Invivogen, USA).
For overexpression of the human Gal-3 protein, the HUVECs were infected with adenovirus encoding Gal-3 (GenBank accession no. NM_031832.1; HY20200911YXY-AD01, Hanbio, China) and trypsinized and harvested for cell migration and in vitro angiogenesis assays.
For gene knockdowns of ITGB1, ITGA3, ITGA5, ITGA6 and ITGAV in HMEC-1 cells, oligonucleotides of plasmid-based short hairpin RNA (shRNA), listed in Table  , were used to produce lentiviruses as described above.
For gene knockdowns of ITGB1, CTNNA1 (encoding Cateninα-1), MACM (encoding CD146), HMEC-1 cells were seeded into a six-well plate for 24 h. The indicated siRNA (50 nM), listed in Table  , was transfected alone or together into cells using RNAiMAX (Thermo Scientific, USA). After 48 h, cells were trypsinized and harvested for cell migration and in vitro angiogenesis assays.
All sequences oligonucleotides (e.g., primers, siRNA) used in the study are provided in Supplementary Table S – .

## Cell migration assay
Transwell plates (Corning) were used to conduct the migration assay. Approximately 1 × 105 cells in 200 μL of serum-free MCDB 131 medium were placed in the upper chamber, and 600 μL of the same medium containing Gal-3 (0.33 μM) was added to the lower chamber. For knockdown experiments, 6 × 104 cells in 200 μL culture medium were plated instead. Cells were incubated at 37 °C with 5% CO2 for 12 h and then fixed in 4% PFA for 15 min at room temperature. Cells in the upper chamber of filters were removed using cotton swabs. Cells on the lower side of the filters were stained with Giemsa and counted using an Olympus microscope at ×100 magnification.
For experiments with inhibitors of HMEC-1 migration, EGFR tyrosine kinase inhibitor ZD1839 (25 μM, S1025, Selleck, USA), VEGFR tyrosine kinase inhibitor PTK787 (25 μM, S1101, Selleck, USA), and FAK tyrosine kinase inhibitor PF573228 (25 μM, S2013, Selleck, USA) were added to both the upper and lower chambers and remained throughout the assay.
For functional blocking studies of HMEC-1 migration, 6 × 104 cells in 200 μL culture medium were first incubated with 10 μg/mL functional blocking antibodies separately: integrin α5 (MAB1956Z, Chemicon, USA), integrin β1 (CBL481, Chemicon, USA) for 30 min at 37 oC. Then the cells, together with the medium and antibodies, were transferred to the upper chamber. Serum-free MCDB 131 medium (600 μL) containing the functional blocking antibody and Gal-3 (0.33 μM) were added to the lower chamber as the chemoattractant. For integrin α5β1 inhibition, 6 × 104 cells were pre-treated with 200 μL culture medium containing ATN-161 (100 nM) for 48 h and then transferred to the upper chamber, with ATN-161 (100 nM) added to the upper chamber.
For migration experiments of HUVECs overexpressing Gal-3, serum-free medium containing 3 × 104 cells were transferred to the upper chamber, then 600 μL medium containing 1% FBS (V/V) were added to the lower chamber.

## In vitro angiogenesis assay
The matrigel assay was used to assess the in vitro angiogenic activity. For Gal-3-induced angiogenesis of HMEC-1 cells or HUVECs in vitro following the aforementioned experimental protocols (see above), the harvested cells (5 × 104 for HMEC-1 cells or 3 × 104 for HUVECs) in 200 μL serum-free medium were added to the matrigel-coated 96-well plate in the presence or absence of Gal-3 (1 µg/mL). Four hours later, images were taken using an Olympus microscope at ×100 magnification and analyzed using the plugin Angiogenesis Analyzer from the Fiji software.
For HUVECs overexpressing Gal-3, serum-free medium containing 3 × 104 cells were added into matrigel-coated 96-well plate for matrigel assay as described above.
To examine the impact of diabetic patient serum on Gal-3-induced angiogenesis in vitro, HUVECs cells were pre-treated with 7.5% (V/V) diabetic patient serum and 2.5% (V/V) FBS for 48 h and then harvested for matrigel assay in the presence or absence of Gal-3 (1 µg/mL) as above.

## Flow cytometry
Cell cycle analysis was performed using the cell cycle analysis kit (C1062S, Beyotime, China). HMEC-1 cells were treated with serum-free medium containing Gal-3 (0 µg/mL, 1 µg/mL, 5 µg/mL and 10 µg/mL) for 12 h. The cells were harvested and stained with Annexin V-FITC and Propidium Iodide according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Then, cells were subjected into flow cytometer (CytoFLEX, Beckman Coulter, California, USA) and the raw data were analyzed using Flow Jo software (version 10, Treestar). Gating strategies for all flow cytometry experiments were included in the Supplementary method.

## Gal-3 endocytosis assay
GFP-Gal-3 (0.33 µM) was incubated with AGEs or BSA at a 1:6 molar ratio at 4 °C for 3 h. Then, the mixture was added to the HEK-293T cells and incubated for 30 min. Cells were washed with PBS, fixed and imaged by microscopy (IX71, Olympus, Japan).

## Immunofluorescence assay
CHO-K1 cells were starved overnight and stimulated with GFP-Gal-3 (0.33 μM) at the indicated time. Cells were fixed, blocked and then incubated with primary antibody against integrin α5 (1:100, ab150361, Abcam, UK) at 4 °C overnight. Cells were washed with PBS, and incubated with goat anti-rabbit antibody (1:500, Alexa Fluor® 594 or Alexa Fluor® 488, Abcam, UK) at room temperature for 1 h. Cells were imaged by confocal fluorescence microscopy (LSM780, Zeiss, Germany). Fluorescence intensity was measured using Fiji image processing software (ImageJ, National Institute of Health, USA).

## Protein purification assay
Gal-3, GFP or His-tag fusion Gal-3, 15N-labeled Gal-3, and CD146 extracellular full-length recombinant proteins were purified as previously described , .

## Immunoprecipitation assay
To identify the binding proteins of Gal-3 in HMEC-1 cells, immunoprecipitation and mass spectrometry analysis were performed. HMEC-1 cells were lysed in lysis buffer. The homogenates were incubated with or without His-Gal-3 (100 μg) for 3 h, then His-tag antibody (2 μg, KM8001, Sungene Biotech, China) was added and incubated at 4°C overnight. After this, protein G beads (30 μL) were added and incubated at 4°C for 3 h. The affinity or control beads were boiled in 2× loading buffer and analyzed by using 9% SDS-PAGE followed by silver staining. Bound proteins were analyzed by mass spectrometry using a Nano LC-ESI-MS/MS system by Suzhou ProtTech Biotechnology Co. Ltd (Suzhou, China).

## Pull-down assay
HMEC-1 cell lysates (100 μg) were incubated with GST-Gal-3 (0.33 μM) at 4 °C for 1 h, then glutathione SepharoseTM 4B (GS, 20 μL, G4510, Millipore, USA) beads were added. The beads were recovered, boiled in 2× loading buffer, and then analyzed by 9% SDS-PAGE and western blotting. For pull-down proteins on the cell membrane, cells were first incubated with GST-Gal-3 (0.33 μM) at 4 °C for 1 h. After washing off the unbound proteins, DTSSP (3,3’-dithiobis (sulfosuccinimidyl propionate), 21578, Thermo Scientific, UK), (a homo-bifunctional, thiol-cleavable, and membrane-impermeable cross-linker) was added to the plate and incubated for 1 h on ice. Cells were washed with PBS to remove unreacted DTSSP, followed by the addition of 20 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.2) to terminate the reaction. To detect competitive inhibition of Gal-3-integrin β1 binding by AGEs (ab51995, Abcam, USA), His-Gal-3 was first incubated with AGEs or BSA at a 1:6 molar ratio of HMEC-1 cell lysates (100 μg).
For the biotin pull-down assay, HMEC-1 cells were pre-treated with or without Gal-3 (0.33 μM) for the indicated time. Cells were then treated with EZ-Link Sulfo-NHS-SS-Biotin (21331, Thermo Scientific, USA) and incubated on ice for 1 h. Cells were washed three times with ice-cold PBS and then terminated with Tris-HCl (pH 7.2). The cells were lysed with RIPA buffer and incubated with NeutrAvidin agarose beads (20 μL, 29200, Thermo Scientific, USA) at 4 °C for 3 h. After washing five times, the beads were boiled in SDS loading buffer, and proteins were separated by SDS-PAGE.

## Imaging of phase separation in vitro
Purified GFP-Gal-3 (80 µM) was mixed with integrin β1 (400 nM) (10587-H08H1, Sino Biological, China), integrin α5β1 (400 nM), integrin β1 (400 nM) + lactose (20 mM), integrin α5β1 (400 nM) + lactose (20 mM), or CD146 (400 nM) in PBS and incubated at 37 °C for 5 min. PNGase F-treated integrin α5β1 was prepared by incubating 4 μg of integrin α5β1 with 1000 U of PNGase F at 37 °C for 2 h using a PNGase F Kit (P0704S, NEW ENGLAND BioLabs, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. GFP-Gal-3 (40 µM) was incubated with AGEs or BSA at a molar ratio of 1:6 and mixed with integrin β1 (400 nM) for droplet analysis. Then, representative images of droplets from each experiment were randomly captured under a Differential Interference Contrast Microscope (BX63, Olympus, Japan) or Immunofluorescence and Phase contrast microscope (ECLIPSE Ti2-E, NIKON, Japan) at ×100 oil objective lens. The diameter and number of droplets in fluorescence field (4-7 representative images each group) were measured using Fiji image processing software (ImageJ, National Institute of Health, USA).

## Imaging of phase separation in cells
CHO-K1 cells were transfected with lentivirus encoding mCherry-integrin α5 or mTagBFP2-CD146. Dual-tagged cells co-expressing mCherry-integrin α5 and BFP2-CD146 were sorted using flow cytometry (MoFlo Astrios EQs, Beckman Coulter, USA). CHO-K1 cells expressing mCherry-integrin α5, mTagBFP2-CD146 or co-expressing mCherry-integrin α5, mTagBFP2-CD146 were plated on confocal dish (TCD10035, BIOFIL, China) with a 50% cell density. Before imaging, the medium was discarded, and the cells were washed with PBS twice. Then, purified Gal-3 (3.3 µM, containing 0.8 µM GFP-Gal-3 and 2.5 µM Gal-3) with or without lactose (10 mM), sucrose (10 mM), BSA (19.8 µM) and AGEs (19.8 µM) were applied to the cells and incubated in a humidified incubator at 37 oC with 5% CO2 for 15 min. Then, 1.5% 1, 6-HD (H103708, Aladdin, China) was added into the medium and incubated for 2 min. Cells were imaged by confocal fluorescence microscopy (LSM780, Zeiss, Germany). Fluorescence intensity was measured using Fiji image processing software (ImageJ, National Institute of Health, USA).

## Turbidity assay and dynamic light scattering assay (DLS)
For turbidity assay, indicated concentration of Gal-3, CD146, integrin β1, integrin α5β1, or lactose were mixed in PBS and incubated at 37 °C for 5 min. The absorbance at 400 nm was measured using a Nanodrop 2000 microvolume spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific, USA). DLS experiments were performed using a Nano-ZS ZEN3600 particle size analyzer (Nano-ZS ZEN3600 instrument, Malvern Instruments) with each measurement comprising 3 runs, conducted at 37 °C and the data were collected at 37°C.

## Fluorescence recovery after photobleaching (FRAP)
FRAP was performed using a confocal microscope (LSM780, Zeiss, Germany). Briefly, 100 μM of GFP-Gal-3 was mixed with integrin β1 (400 nM), integrin α5β1 (400 nM), or CD146 (400 nM) in PBS and incubated at 37°C for 5 min. Then, 10 μL of the mixture was added onto a glass slide and sealed with coverslips. The selected regions of interest (ROI) were photobleached using a 488 nm laser until the fluorescence intensity decreased by approximately 40% to 60%.
In live cells, purified Gal-3 (3.3 µM, containing 0.8 µM GFP-Gal-3 and 2.5 µM Gal-3) was added into the cells expressing mCherry-integrin α5 and incubated in a humidified incubator at 37°C with 5% CO2 for 15 min. Then, the cell subjected into chamber at 37°C. FRAP was performed using Super Resolution Microscope (A1R N-SIM N-STORM, Nikon, Japan). The condensates with co-localization between Gal-3 and integrin α5 were randomly selected for bleaching. A 488 nm laser was used to photobleach selected ROI until the fluorescence intensity diminished by about 40% to 60%. The fluorescent intensity of bleached area over time was calculated by NIS-Elements AR. Signals were normalized with pre-bleached levels set as 100% and 0 second after bleaching set as 0. The equation used to calculate values is: Normalized Intensity = (Intensity–Intensitypost-bleach)/(Intensitypre-bleach – Intensitypost-bleach) × 100%.

## Western blotting
Tissues or cells were lysed in RIPA buffer (PC102, EpiZyme, USA), containing 1× protease inhibitor cocktail (CW2200, CWBIO, China) and phosphatase inhibitor (CW2383, CWBIO, China) at 4°C for 30 min and then centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 15 min. Supernatants (15–20 μg) were analyzed by 9% or 10% SDS-PAGE and transferred to PVDF membranes (GE Healthcare or Millipore, USA). After blocking with 5% non-fat dry milk in PBST (PBS containing 0.05% Tween-20), blots were incubated overnight at 4°C with the following primary antibodies: CD31 (1:1000, ab281583, Abcam, USA), β-Actin (1:1000, 612657, BD Biosciences, USA), Gal-3 (1:1000, ab76245, Abcam, USA), collagen I (1:1000, GB114197, Servicebio, China), collagen III (1:1000, EPR17673, Abcam, USA), AKT (1:1000, 9272 s, cell signaling technology, USA), p-AKT (Ser473) (1:1000, 9271 s, cell signaling technology, USA), FoxO1 (1:1000, 18592-1-AP, Proteintech, China), integrin β1 (1:1000, 610467, BD Biosciences, USA), integrin α3 (1:1000, 66070, Proteintech, China), integrin α5 (1:1000, sc-10729, Santa Cruz, USA), integrin α6 (1:1000, ab181551, Abcam, USA), p-integrin β1 (Y783) (1:1000, ab62337, Abcam, USA), VEGFR2 (1:1000, 2479S, Cell signaling, USA), p-VEGFR2 (Tyr1175) (1:1000, 2478S, Cell signaling technology, USA), EGFR (1:1000, 4267S, Cell signaling, USA), p-EGFR (Tyr1068) (1:1000, 3777S, Cell signaling technology, USA), integrin αv(1:1000, 27096-1-AP, Proteintech, China), FAK (1:1000, 12636-1-AP, Proteintech China), p-FAK (Y397) (1:1000, 611806, BD Biosciences, USA), CD146 (1:1000, 611208, BD Biosciences, USA), GST (1:1000, KM8005, Sungene Biotech, China), His-tag (1:1000, RM1001, Beijing Ray Antibody Biotech, China), α-Tubulin (1:1000, F0063, Selleck, USA). The following secondary antibodies were used: anti-rabbit HRP (1:5000, CW0103, CWBIO, China) and anti-mouse HRP (1:5000, CW0102, CWBIO, China). After incubation with the ECL reagent, blots were scanned with a Chemiluminescent Imaging and Analysis System (MiniChemi, Beijing Sage Creation Science Co., Ltd, China). Signal intensity relative to β-Actin was quantified by using Fiji image processing software. Uncropped and unprocessed scans of the blots were provided in the Source Data file.

## Real-time qPCR
Extraction of RNA from HMEC-1 cells or tissues was performed using RNAiso plus (9108Q, Takara, China). The cDNA was synthesized using Evo M-MLVRT kit (AG11728, Accurate Biotechnology, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. qPCR was performed with SYBR Green Premix Pro Taq HS qPCR kit (AG11701, Accurate Biotechnology, China) and LightCycle480 II thermal cycler (Roche, Switzerland). Changes in mRNA levels were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCT method. Relative expression levels of each gene were normalized to β-Actin. The primers are listed in Table  . All primers are purchased from Generay Biotechnology.

## Transmission electron microscopy (TEM)
Hydrogel samples were sprayed with a thin gold layer, and the morphologies of freeze-dried hydrogels were examined by using a field emission scanning electron microscope (FE-SEM, HIACHI Regulus 8100, Japan). All pores of each hydrogel within a predetermined ROI on the digitized image were measured using Fiji image processing software.
To observe the ultrastructure of regenerated skin tissue, tissues were fixed in the pre-cold 2.5% glutaraldehyde in 0.1 M PBS for 2 h and post-fixed with 1% osmium tetroxide for another 2 h. Tissues were washed with PBS for 20 min, followed by dehydration and embedding with 100% resin in a beam capsule. Tissues were sliced into 40–50 nm thick sections and stained with uranyl acetate under a transmission electron microscope (HIACHI Regulus 8100, Japan). Skin fibril diameter was analyzed from transverse section images. Fiji image processing software was employed to measure hydrogel section parameters and the minor axis of skin fibrils.

## Nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopy
Uniformly 15N-labeled Gal-3 (20 μM) was dissolved in potassium phosphate buffer (20 mM, pH 6.9), prepared using a 95% H2O/5% D2O mixture. 1H-15N Heteronuclear Single Quantum Coherence (HSQC) NMR experiments were used to investigate the binding of 15N-labeled Gal-3 to BSA, AGEs, integrin β1, lactose, and the combination of AGEs and integrin β1. The 1H and 15N resonance assignments for recombinant human Gal-3 were previously reported .
NMR experiments were carried out at 30°C on a Bruker 850 MHz spectrometer equipped with H/C/N triple-resonance probes and x/y/z triple-axis pulse field gradient units. A gradient sensitivity-enhanced version of the 1H-15N HSQC experiment (32 to 64 scans per transient) was applied with 256 (t1) × 2048 (t2) complex data points in 15N and 1H dimensions, respectively, and a sweep width of 16 ppm in the 1H dimension and 30 ppm in the 15N dimension. 1H-15N-HSQC spectra were acquired with 15N-labeled Gal-3 in the absence or presence of unlabeled BSA, AGEs, lactose and/or integrin β1. Raw data were converted and processed using NMRPipe and were analyzed using NMRview.

## Bilayer interferometry (BLI) assay
Binding kinetics were measured at 25°C using a Fortebio Octet RED 96 instrument as described previously . Briefly, Ni-NTA sensors (Octet NTA Biosensors) were loaded with His-integrin β1 (212.2 nM) and subsequently exposed the sensors to Gal-3 (200, 400, 600, 800, 1000, 1200 and 1400 nM) or Gal-3 (1.4 µM) plus different concentrations of AGEs (0, 11.2, 22.4 and 44.8 µM) for association and then to PBST (PBS buffer containing 0.1% Tween-20) without Gal-3 for dissociation. These proteins were diluted in PBST buffer. The procedures consist of the following steps: loading integrin β1 for 180 s, setting in PBST for 60 s, Gal-3 association for 120 s or 180 s, and Gal-3 dissociation for 120 s or 180 s. To determine the binding parameters, such as dissociation constants KD, data were analyzed using ForteBio Data Analysis Software 7.0.

## Skin wound blood flow measurement
The mice were anesthetized, and skin wound perfusion was assessed using a laser Doppler blood flow monitor (Blood FlowMeter, INC191, ADInstruments). Blood flow measurements were taken at the wound site, with five replicates per measurement point. For each measurement, the probe film was gently placed on the designated skin area on the mouse’s back and left in position for approximately five minutes until the waveform stabilized, after which the value was recorded.

## Systemic vascular perfusion and microCT imaging
Mice were anesthetized and perfused with Microfil (MV-130, Flow Tech Inc., USA) on day 11 after wounding, following a previously established protocol . After perfusion, wound tissue was excised and scanned using a Bruker SkyScan 1276 microCT scanner at a 10 µm resolution. The vascular architecture of the entire wound area was then reconstructed using OsiriX software and analyzed with Fiji for relevant vascular parameters.

## LC–MS/MS analysis
The chromatographic run is carried out by AB SCIEX 5500 series triple quadrupole mass spectrometer. Skin tissue was homogenized in ice-cold water at a ratio of 1:9 (w:v, g:mL), followed by ultrasonication (5 min) and vortexing (3 min). A 150 μL aliquot of the homogenate was mixed with 500 μL of 5 mmol/L pentafluoropropionic acid and incubated at room temperature for 2 h. After adding 2 mL of 200 g/L trichloroacetic acid, the sample was centrifuged at 2000 × g for 10 min. The pellet was washed once with 100 g/L trichloroacetic acid, then hydrolyzed in 500 μL of 6 mol/L HCl at 110 °C for 20 h. The hydrolysate was evaporated under nitrogen at 80 °C and reconstituted in 270 μL of 5 mmol/L pentafluoropropionic acid containing 30 μL of 50 ng/mL cotinine-d₃ internal standard. After filtration through a 0.22 μm membrane, 150 μL of the solution was subjected to LC–MS/MS analysis. Peak areas for the selected ions are determined using Analyst v1.6.3, and main LC–MS/MS parameters are reported in Supplementary Table  .

## Statistical analysis and reproducibility
Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism 9.0 (GraphPad Software). All values were expressed as mean ± s.e.m. with individual data points. The normal distribution of data was evaluated using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Unpaired Student’s t-test or Mann–Whitney U test was employed for comparisons between two groups, depending on the distribution of the data. One-way ANOVA with Fisher’s LSD post hoc test or Kruskal-Wallis test with Dunn’s post hoc test was used for multiple group comparisons, depending on the distribution of the data. For all tests, P < 0.05 was considered significant. Each experiment was repeated independently at least two to four times with similar results. At least 3 independent samples were included in each experiment. Source data are provided as a Source Data file.
