## Abstract
Emerging evidence positions tumour cell-intrinsic complement proteins as regulators of cancer cell behaviour and immune crosstalk, yet their role in triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) remains undefined. Here, transcriptomic profiling across breast cancer molecular subtypes reveals that C1R, C1S and C3 are preferentially expressed in TNBC cells, both in patient tumours and in vitro, and that their expression is upregulated by pro-inflammatory cytokines. In situ mRNA hybridization and hyperplex SeqIF immunofluorescence demonstrate that C1R and C1S share more closely matched expression patterns with one another than with C3 within TNBC cell populations. These patterns associate with distinct transcriptional programs: metabolic and cell cycle gene sets are enriched in C1R-, C1S- and C3-high cells, whereas inflammatory and apoptotic signatures are specifically linked to C1R and C1S. Functionally, silencing of C1r or C1s modestly impairs TNBC cell proliferation, viability and tumoursphere formation — effects not rescued by extracellular protein supplementation, supporting an intracellular mechanism. C3 silencing exerts minimal effects. C1R- and C1S-high tumour cells are enriched in chemoattractant chemokines in patient tumours, and their secretion decreases upon complement gene silencing in vitro. C1R and C1S expression further correlates with cytotoxic T cell infiltration and improved patient survival. Together, these findings indicate that whilst cell-intrinsic C1r and C1s enhance modestly tumour cell fitness, their more consequential contribution lies in shaping the tumour immune microenvironment through regulation of chemokine secretion and cytotoxic T cell infiltration, highlighting their role as enhancers of anti-tumour immunity in TNBC.

## Introduction
Breast cancer is the most frequently diagnosed cancer among women worldwide, accounting for over 2.3 million new cases and more than 670,000 deaths in 2022 [ ]. Based on immunohistochemical evaluation of estrogen receptor (ER), progesterone receptor (PR) and human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2), it is categorized into four principal molecular subtypes: luminal A, luminal B, HER2-positive and triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC). These molecular subtypes largely determine disease severity and guide treatment strategies. TNBC, defined by the absence of all three receptors, is associated with the poorest prognosis and has limited targeted treatment options [ ]. In addition to molecular subtypes, interactions between breast cancer cells and the immune system have been shown to shape disease development and progression [ ]. In particular, the innate immune system, including the complement system, plays a complex and not yet fully understood role in breast cancer progression [ , ].
Complement proteins, predominantly liver-derived and circulating in plasma, exert both tumour-promoting and tumour-suppressive functions in breast cancer. Experimental models using complement-deficient mice have demonstrated their contribution to breast cancer growth and progression [ – ], while C1q and C3 exhibit anti-tumoural effects by inhibiting angiogenesis in Her2/neu transgenic models [ , ]. Beyond systemic production, complement components can also be synthesised locally by cancer cells.
Recent evidence has fundamentally shifted complement biology, demonstrating that complement components can act through cell-intrinsic, including intracellular, mechanisms, including in cancer cells [ , , ]. Tumour cell-intrinsic expression of C1R, C1S, C3, CFB, CFH and CFI has been documented across renal, lung, pancreatic, skin and other cancers, with knockdown directly affecting proliferation, survival and other tumour cell properties [ – ]. In breast cancer, evidence remains limited to a single study reporting reduced TNBC aggressiveness upon CFH knockdown [ ]. Tumour-derived C1s and FH modulate the TME by suppressing T cell activation and skewing macrophage polarisation, respectively in clear cell renal cell carcinoma (ccRCC) and breast cancer [ , ]. Complement inhibitors currently entering clinical trials for solid tumours to improve the efficacy of checkpoint inhibitors [ , ] primarily target extracellular complement cascade. Whether tumour cell-intrinsic complement proteins act extracellularly — and are thus potentially targetable by existing inhibitors — or intracellularly, where current drugs cannot reach, remains to be established. Equally critical is determining whether their net effect is pro- or anti-tumoural, as this will dictate whether therapeutic strategies should aim to inhibit or, conversely, enhance their activity.
Here we report that C1R, C1S and C3 are preferentially expressed in TNBC cells within the breast cancer TME, and that their expression is regulated by pro-inflammatory cytokines. Functionally, tumour cell-intrinsic C1r and C1s support TNBC cell fitness through an intracellular mechanism, while the effect of C3 is minimal. Cell-intrinsic C1r and C1s regulate secretion of chemoattractant chemokines by TNBC cells, and these cells spatially associate with activated cytotoxic T cells in patient tumours. This could contribute to explain the correlation of C1R and C1S expression with improved patient survival. Collectively these results point to a predominant role of tumour cell-intrinsic C1r and C1s in shaping anti-tumour immunity over driving cancer cell growth.

## Methods

## Bioinformatic analysis of publicly available datasets: Bulk RNA-seq
R software version 4.4.1 was used to analyse gene expression (RNASeqV2) and clinical data from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) PanCancer-BRCA cohort, obtained through the “cBioPortalData” package [ ]. Breast cancer molecular subtypes were assigned to TCGA-BRCA samples based on hormone receptor and HER2 status derived from ‘nationwidechildrens.org_clinical_patient_brca.txt’ (Genomic Data Commons [GDC], https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/, accessed on 27 March 2024). Information on HER2 status (by immunohistochemistry [IHC] and fluorescence in situ hybridization [FISH]), as well as ER and PR statuses by IHC, was extracted. HER2 status was resolved by classifying FISH-positive cases as HER2-positive regardless of IHC results, with FISH status was used to resolve cases with equivocal or indeterminate IHC when available. Patients were classified into four subtypes based on ER, PR and resolved HER2 status: luminal A (ER- and PR-positive, HER2-negative), luminal B (ER-positive with either PR-negative or HER2-positive), HER2-positive (ER- and PR-negative with HER2-positive tumours) and TNBC (ER- and PR-negative, HER2-negative). Patients with missing ER or PR information or unresolved HER2 status were categorized as “Unknown” and excluded from downstream analyses. Male patients and histological types other than breast invasive ductal carcinoma were also excluded.
To study the expression of complement genes (C1R, C1S, C3, CFB, CFH and CFI) across the four molecular subtypes, gene expression data were transformed into z-scores to visualise relative expression patterns. Further comparisons of C1R, C1S and C3 expression among subtypes were performed using log₂(TPM + 1) values and analysed with the Kruskal-Wallis test, followed by Dunn’s multiple comparisons post-hoc test with Benjamini-Hochberg correction. Associations between the expression levels of these genes and clinical outcomes, such as Overall Survival (OS) and Progression-Free Survival (PFS), were also assessed by generating Kaplan-Meier curves using the “survminer” package with optimal cut-off values.
Subsequent analyses focused particularly on TNBC samples. A correlation matrix of C1R, C1S and C3 expression was generated using the “corrplot” package. To explore immune-related associations, leading-edge genes enriched in C1R-high and C1S-high cancer cells (see Bioinformatic analysis of publicly available datasets: scRNA-seq) were intersected with cytokine-related genes from HGNC-curated gene groups. The resulting intersected genes were then correlated with C1R, C1S and C3 expression in TCGA-BRCA TNBC samples. Spearman correlations were visualised as dot plots, with statistical significance represented as the negative log10 of the false discovery rate (FDR)-adjusted p-values (Benjamini-Hochberg method). Finally, abundance scores for immune and stromal cell populations were computed using the “MCPcounter” package [ ] and correlated with the expression levels of C1R, C1S and C3. Hierarchically clustered Spearman correlations were visualized as a heatmap using the “ComplexHeatmap” package.
RNA expression data for 27 TNBC cell lines were retrieved from ‘OmicsExpressionProteinCodingGenesTPMLogp1.csv’ (DepMap Public 23Q4, The Broad Institute Cancer Dependency Map Portal, https://depmap.org/portal/, accessed on 5 November 2024). Spearman correlation analyses were performed between C1R, C1S and C3 expression and cytokine-related gene expression, mirroring analyses conducted in TCGA-BRCA samples. Additionally, the expression of the genes encoding the receptors for interleukin-1 beta (IL-1β; IL1R1, IL1RAP), interleukin-6 (IL-6; IL6R, IL6ST), tumor necrosis factor alpha (TNFα; TNFRSF1A, TNFRSF1B), transforming growth factor beta (TGF-β; TGFBR1, TGFBR2), interferon alpha and beta (IFNα/β; IFNAR1, IFNAR2), and interferon gamma (IFNγ; IFNGR1, IFNGR2) was specifically assessed in the TNBC cell lines SUM-159PT and MDA-MB-468.

## Bioinformatic analysis of publicly available datasets: scRNA-seq
scRNA-seq data from 22 primary human breast tissue samples were obtained from Pal, B. et al. 2021 [ ], including 8 luminal (1 PR-positive, others ER-positive), 6 HER2-positive and 8 TNBC tumours. Data was processed using the “Seurat” R package. Samples were grouped by molecular subtype, merged and analysed separately. Quality control excluded cells with < 500 or > 8,000 genes, > 50,000 UMIs, > 20% mitochondrial content, or > 1% red blood cell gene expression (HBA1, HBA2, HBB). Doublets were identified and removed using the “DoubletFinder” package. SCTransform normalization was applied, regressing out cell cycle effects and mitochondrial content, followed by PCA and Harmony integration to correct batch effects across patients. Dimensionality reduction was conducted with UMAP, and clustering was performed using the “FindClusters()” function at a resolution of 0.1. Cell type annotation was carried out using the “SingleR” package with the Human Primary Cell Atlas reference on log-normalized data. To distinguish tumour epithelial cells from normal epithelial cells, copy number variation (CNV) analysis was performed using the “infercnv” package, with T cells, macrophages and endothelial cells as reference populations. Epithelial clusters with significantly elevated CNV scores (Mann-Whitney U test) were classified as tumour cells. Clusters were manually annotated by examining the cell-type composition. To visualise gene expression patterns, log-normalized values for C1R, C1S and C3 were plotted on UMAPs. Clusters were grouped into major cell types, and dot plots were generated to display mean scaled (z-score) expression levels and the percentage of expressing cells within each labeled Seurat cluster. In TNBC samples, additional dot plots were created based on SingleR-annotated cell types.
Cancer cells were subset by molecular subtype. Cells with log-normalized expression > 0 for C1R, C1S or C3 were considered positive, and gene overlaps were visualised using the “ggVennDiagram” R package. For gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA), cancer cells were stratified into high and low groups based on the median log-normalized expression of each gene (C1R, C1S and C3). To ensure gene-specific comparisons, cells co-expressing either of the other two complement genes (UMI count > 0) were excluded from each analysis. Differential expression between high and low groups was computed using the “presto” package. Ranked log₂ fold changes served as input for preranked GSEA (“fgsea” method) using the “clusterProfiler” package [ ] and the 50 hallmark gene sets from the Molecular Signature Database [ ]. GSEA results were visualised using the “gseaplot2()” function from the “enrichplot” package, and leading-edge genes were extracted from each enriched pathway. Focusing on C1R-high and C1S-high cancer cells, leading-edge genes from enriched immune-related Hallmark pathways, including interferon responses, inflammatory response, TNFα signalling via NF-κB, complement and IL-2/STAT5 signalling, were compared using the “ggvenn” package. Finally, these immune-related leading-edge genes were intersected with HGNC-curated gene groups, including cytokine ligands (interleukins, chemokines, interferons, TNF superfamily and TGF-β family) and cytokine receptors (chemokine receptors, interferon receptors, interleukin receptors, and members of the TNF receptor superfamily), corresponding to HGNC Groups 601, 483, 598, 781, 1664, 189, 599, 602, and 782, respectively.

## Immunohistochemistry (IHC)
Formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tissue blocks from primary luminal A, luminal B, HER2-positive and TNBC human tumours were obtained from the France Tissue Bank. Sections were cut at a thickness of 4 μm and stained for C1r, C1s, C3c and C3d. Details of the primary antibodies used, along with antigen retrieval and staining conditions, are provided in Table . Tissue slides were deparaffinized in three baths of Clearene (Leica Biosystems, Cat. No. 3803600E) and rehydrated through a graded series of ethanol solutions (100%, 90%, 70%, and 50%) for 2 min each. Antigen retrieval was performed using the PT Link system (Dako, Cat. No. PT200) with EnVision FLEX Retrieval Solutions (Dako; low pH, Cat. No. K8005; high pH, Cat. No. K8004) at 97 °C for 30 min. The choice of low- or high-pH buffer was determined by the specific requirements of each primary antibody. Endogenous peroxidase activity was blocked using a 3% hydrogen peroxide solution (Gilbert, Cat. No. 6553036) for 15 min, followed by a wash in 0.04% v/v TBS-Tween. Nonspecific binding was subsequently blocked with Protein Block (Dako, Cat. No. X0909) for 30 min, followed by incubation with primary antibodies for 30 min. After two washes, sections were incubated for 30 min with a ready-to-use HRP-labelled polymer anti-rabbit secondary antibody (Dako, Cat. No. K4002) and staining was then developed using DAB chromogen (Dako, Cat. No. K3468). Slides were counterstained with hematoxylin (Dako, Cat. No. CS700), mounted using Glycergel (Dako, Cat. No. C0563), and scanned with a NanoZoomer scanner (Hamamatsu).

## Automated multiplexed sequential immunofluorescence (seqIF)
Automated multiplexed seqIF staining and imaging were performed on 4-µm sections from FFPE tissue blocks of primary TNBC (obtained from the FTB) using the COMET platform (Lunaphore Technologies) [ ]. The panel comprised 13 antibodies targeting αSMA, CK, C1r, C1s, C3d and immune cell markers (CD45, CD68, CD163, HLA-DR, CD8, CD4, Granzyme B, PD-1) (Table ). This panel was extracted from a larger hyperplex, based on the in situ complementomics approach [ ]. Secondary Alexa fluorophores 555 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat. No. A32727) and 647 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat. No. A32733) were used at 1:200 and 1:400 dilutions, respectively. Image processing and figure preparation were performed using HALO AI software (version 4.1; Indica Labs). Nuclear segmentation was performed with the default AI algorithm, and marker positivity was analysed with the Highplex FL module, which was also used to perform spatial proximity analyses.

## RNAscope in situ hybridization
4-µm FFPE tissue sections from primary TNBC human tumours (obtained from the FTB) were analysed for C1R, C1S and C3 mRNA expression using the RNAscope in situ hybridization assay with the RNAscope 2.5 HD Brown detection kit (Advanced Cell Diagnostics [ACD], Cat. No. 322310). Briefly, sections were deparaffinized in xylene followed by rehydration in ethanol, and endogenous peroxidases were blocked using RNAscope Hydrogen Peroxide (ACD, RNAscope H2O2 & Protease Plus Reagents Cat. No. 322330). Slides were then subjected to target retrieval using RNAscope Target Retrieval Reagent (ACD, Cat. No. 322000) followed by protease digestion with RNAscope Protease Plus (ACD, RNAscope H2O2 & Protease Plus Reagents Cat. No. 322330). Gene-specific probes targeting C1R (RNAscope Probe – Hs-C1R, ACD, Cat. No. 508951), C1S (RNAscope Probe – Hs-C1S, ACD, Cat. No. 508961), and C3 (RNAscope Probe – Hs-C3, ACD, Cat. No. 430701) were hybridized to the sections for 2 h at 40 °C in the HybEZ II oven (ACD). Signal amplification and chromogenic detection were performed using the RNAscope 2.5 HD Brown assay (ACD, Cat. No. 322310). Slides were counterstained with Gill’s hematoxylin, mounted with EcoMount mounting medium (Biocare Medical, Cat. No. EM897L) and scanned using a NanoZoomer scanner (Hamamatsu).

## Cell lines
The luminal breast cancer cell lines MCF-7 and T-47D, as well as the TNBC cell line SUM-159PT, were kindly provided by Dr. Sébastien Jauliac’s team (Institut de recherche Saint-Louis, Paris, France). The HER2-positive cell line SK-BR-3 (ATCC Cat. No. HTB-30), along with the TNBC cell lines MDA-MB-231 (ATCC Cat. No. HTB-26) and MDA-MB-468 (ATCC Cat. No. HTB-132), were employed. MCF-7 and T-47D cells were cultured in RPMI 1640 medium with GlutaMAX™ (Gibco, Cat. No. 61870-010). SK-BR-3 cells were maintained in McCoy’s 5 A medium (Gibco, Cat. No. 16600-082). TNBC cell lines (MDA-MB-231, MDA-MB-468, and SUM-159PT) were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) with GlutaMAX™ (Gibco, Cat. No. 61965-026). All media were supplemented with 10% heat-inactivated fetal calf serum (FCS; Eurobio Scientific, Cat. No. CVFSVF00-01) and 1× penicillin/streptomycin (Gibco, Cat. No. 15140-122). All cell lines were cultured in a humidified atmosphere of 5% CO₂ at 37 °C, routinely tested for Mycoplasma contamination using the MycoAlert® Mycoplasma Detection Kit (Lonza, Cat. No. LT07-318), and used only if found negative. Cells were used up to passage 20.

## Cytokine stimulation
SUM-159PT and MDA-MB-468 cells were plated in 6-well plates, and the following day, wells were washed once with PBS before 2 mL of Opti-MEM medium (Gibco, Cat. No. 31985-062) was added. Cytokine stimulation was performed in duplicate using either 10 ng/mL of IL-1β (Peprotech, Cat. No. 200-01B), IL-6 (Peprotech, Cat. No. 200-06), TNFα (R&D Systems, Cat. No. 210-TA), TGF-β1/2 (R&D Systems, Cat. No. 304-B3), IFN-β (Peprotech, Cat. No. 300-02B-20UG), IFN-γ (Peprotech, Cat. No. 300-02-100UG), or 5,000 U/mL IFN-α2 (PBL Assay Science, Cat. No. 11100-1), or medium alone as a control. After 24 h of stimulation, cell supernatants and cell lysates were collected for subsequent measurement of C1r, C1s and C3 levels by QuantiGene RNA assay and ELISA.

## Gene silencing
SUM-159PT and MDA-MB-468 cells were plated in 6-well plates, 10-cm² Petri dishes or T75 flasks 24 h prior to transfection to achieve approximately 50% confluence at the time of treatment. A 1:1 mixture of lipofectamine RNAiMAX (Thermo Fisher, Cat. No. 13778150) and siRNA was prepared in Opti-MEM and pre-incubated at room temperature for 30 min. Cells were then transfected with either a control siRNA (Qiagen AllStars Negative Control siRNA Cat. No. SI03650318), a siRNA targeting C1R (Qiagen Hs_C1R_7 FlexiTube siRNA Cat. No. SI03071586), a pool of four siRNAs targeting C1S (Qiagen Hs_C1S_1 FlexiTube siRNA Cat. No. SI00027356, Hs_C1S_2 FlexiTube siRNA Cat. No. SI00027363, Hs_C1S_3 FlexiTube siRNA Cat. No. SI00027370, Hs_C1S_5 FlexiTube siRNA Cat. No SI03022873), or a pool of four siRNAs targeting C3 (Qiagen Hs_C3_6 FlexiTube siRNA Cat. No. SI02622872, Hs_C3_8 FlexiTube siRNA Cat. No. SI03041871, Hs_C3_9 FlexiTube siRNA Cat. No. SI03076689, Hs_C3_11 FlexiTube siRNA Cat. No. SI05095902). Pooled siRNAs were mixed in equal volumes to match the total siRNA input of single-probe conditions and obtain a final siRNA concentration of 50 nM. For the combined silencing of C1R and C1S, cells were co-transfected with siRNAs targeting each gene at equal concentrations while maintaining the same total siRNA concentration. Equal amounts of lipofectamine were used across all conditions. After 24 h, the transfection medium was replaced with standard cell culture medium (see Cell lines). At 96 h post-transfection, immunofluorescence imaging and functional assays were performed on silenced cells, while parallel cultures were harvested for downstream molecular analyses, including Luminex cytokine profiling and QuantiGene RNA assay, ELISA and Western blot to assess silencing efficacy. Cells treated with siRNAs targeting C1R, C1S, C3, or both C1R and C1S were referred to as siC1r, siC1s, siC3 and siC1r + siC1s, respectively, while cells transfected with control siRNA targeting an irrelevant sequence were designated siC.

## QuantiGene RNA assay
RNA transcripts of C1R, C1S and C3 were quantified in silenced and cytokine-stimulated cells using a custom-designed QuantiGene Plex Gene Expression Assay, following the manufacturer’s instructions. The multiplex panel included complement (C1R, C1S, C2, C3, C4A, C4B, C5, C7, CFB, CFH, SERPING1, C3AR1, C5AR1, C5AR2) and reference genes (HPRT1, GUSB, RPL19, POLR2A). Signal detection was performed with the Luminex FLEXMAP 3D Instrument System (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Mean fluorescence values were normalized to housekeeping gene expression and expressed as fold change relative to non-stimulated controls.

## ELISA
C1s and C3 concentrations in supernatants and cell lysates from silenced and cytokine-stimulated cells were measured using a homemade sandwich ELISA, as previously described [ , ]. C1r levels were assessed using a commercial ELISA kit (Invitrogen, Cat. No. EH56RB), following the manufacturer’s instructions. Quantification for all proteins was performed using four-parameter logistic (4PL) standard curves generated from serial dilutions of purified C1s (QuidelOrtho, Cat. No. A425) and purified C3 (QuidelOrtho, Cat. No. A401), while C1r values were derived from the kit-provided standards. Results are expressed in ng/mL. For cytokine-stimulated supernatants, results are shown as fold change relative to non-stimulated controls. To assess chemokine secretion following gene silencing, cells were reseeded 48 h post-transfection in 6-well plates and cultured for 24 h. The medium was then replaced with 300 µL of standard culture medium per well, and supernatants were collected after a 24-hour secretion period. CCL20 levels were measured using a commercial ELISA kit (Invitrogen, Cat. No. EHCCL20), following the manufacturer’s instructions.
For all ELISA measurements, supernatant concentrations were normalized to cell number and cell lysate concentrations to total protein determined by the Bradford assay. Values below the lower limit of quantification (LLOQ) were assigned a value of LLOQ/2 for statistical analysis.

## Multiplex chemokine quantification (Luminex)
Using the same supernatants collected from silenced cells after the 24-hour secretion period, additional chemokines were quantified using a custom Human XL Cytokine Luminex® Performance Assay kit (R&D Systems, Cat. No. FCSTM18B) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The panel included CCL2 (MCP-1), CXCL1 (GROα), CXCL2 (GROβ), CXCL10 (IP-10) and CXCL8 (IL-8). Fluorescence signals were acquired using a Luminex FLEXMAP 3D instrument, and chemokine concentrations were calculated from standard curves using 5PL curve fitting with relative weighting to improve interpolation of low-end values within the standard range. Supernatant concentrations were normalized to cell number and expressed in pg/mL.

## Western blot
Lysates and supernatants from silenced cells were used for Western blot experiments. For supernatants, corresponding cell counts were used to adjust sample dilutions. For total cell lysates, cells were lysed in RIPA buffer (Abcam, Cat. No. ab156034) supplemented with protease inhibitors (Roche, Cat. No. 11836145001), and loading volumes were adjusted based on protein concentrations measured by the Bradford assay. NuPAGE LDS sample buffer (4X, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat. No, NP0007) with DTT was added to the samples, which were then denatured at 90 °C for 5 min. Samples were loaded onto 10% Bis-Tris gels (Invitrogen, Cat. No. NW00100BOX) and transferred onto nitrocellulose membranes (Invitrogen, Cat. No. IB23001). After blocking for 1 h with 5% BSA w/v tris-buffered saline (TBS), membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with the primary antibodies (Table S3) diluted in blocking buffer under agitation. For silencing validation western blots, purified proteins were used as positive controls: C1r proenzyme (CompTech, Cat. No. A101), C1r enzyme (CompTech, Cat. No. A102), C1s proenzyme (CompTech, Cat. No. A103), C1s enzyme (CompTech, Cat. No. A104), C3 (CompTech, Cat. No. A113), C3b (CompTech, Cat. No. A114) and iC3b (CompTech, Cat. No. A115). Primary antibody binding was detected by incubating the membranes with the corresponding HRP-conjugated secondary antibody (Table S3) for 1 h under agitation. The blot was developed by chemiluminescence using an HRP substrate (SuperSignalTM West Dura Extended Duration Substrate, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat. No. 34076) and visualised using the iBright Western Blot Imaging System (iBright™ CL1500 Imaging System, Thermo Fisher Scientific). Western blot analyses of signalling pathways were performed using ImageJ software (version 2.16).

## Flow cytometry
48 h post-transfection, cells were incubated with 1:500 CFSE reagent (Invitrogen, Cat. No. C34554) for 30 min at 37 °C, then cultured for an additional 48 h in 6-well plates in the presence or absence of purified complement proteins. Cells were treated with 10 µg/mL total purified C1r (equal amounts of proenzyme and enzyme; CompTech, Cat. No. A101 and A102, respectively) or C1s (equal amounts of proenzyme and enzyme; CompTech, Cat. No. A103 and A104, respectively), or with purified C3 (CompTech, Cat. No. A113). In separate experiments, cells were treated with C1 esterase inhibitor (C1-INH) (QuidelOrtho, Cat. No. A426) at concentrations of 0.1, 1, or 10 µg/mL, with control wells receiving medium alone. On the day of flow cytometry acquisition, supernatants containing non-adherent dead cells and adherent cells were recovered, washed and stained with 1:400 LIVE/DEAD reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat. No. L10119A) for 15 min on ice, then fixed with 0.5% paraformaldehyde (PFA). Data were acquired using a BD LSRFortessa flow cytometer (BD Biosciences) and analysed with FlowJo v11 software. Acquisition was set to stop at 10,000 events per sample, and samples not reaching this threshold were excluded from the analysis. Dead cells were excluded from the proliferation analysis.

## Cell viability and cell death assays
Cell viability and cell death were assessed using the CyQUANT™ XTT Cell Viability Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat. No. X12223) and the CyQUANT™ LDH Cytotoxicity Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat. No. C20301), respectively, according to the manufacturer’s instructions. At 48 h post-transfection, cells were seeded into flat-bottom 96-well plates and cultured for an additional 48 h in 100 µL of standard medium per well in the presence or absence of purified complement proteins at the same concentrations used for flow cytometry (see “Flow cytometry”). In separate experiments, cells were treated with C1 esterase inhibitor (C1-INH) at concentrations of 0.1, 0.5, 1, 2, or 10 µg/mL, while control wells were left untreated. For the LDH assay, 50 µL of supernatant from each well was transferred to a new plate to measure extracellular LDH activity, and lysis buffer was then added to the remaining medium to release intracellular LDH. Optical density for XTT and LDH assays was measured using an Infinite M200 Pro plate reader (Tecan). Cell death was calculated as the percentage of LDH activity in the supernatant relative to the total LDH activity after cell lysis, and cell viability was expressed as the percentage of absorbance relative to control cells.

## Immunofluorescence
48 h post-transfected cells were seeded on round cover glasses (15.000 cells per cover glass) and cultured for two days. Next, cells were fixed with 4% PFA for 15 min, permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 v/v in PBS for 10 min and blocked with 1% BSA w/v PBS for 1 h at room temperature. Cells were stained with 1:50 α-Ki67 mAb (MIB-1, Dako, Cat. No. 7240) overnight at 4 °C, and then cells were stained for F-actin using Phalloidin-IFluor 488 reagent (Abcam, Cat. No. ab176753) for 90 min in the dark. Finally, cells were incubated with 1:200 α-mouse-AF55 (Alexa Fluor™ Plus 555 Goat anti-Mouse IgG, Lunaphore Technologies, Cat. No. DR555MS) and 1:500 DAPI (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat. No. 62248) for 1 h. Stained slides were mounted with ProLong™ Diamond Antifade Mountant reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat. No. P36961) and scanned at 20X magnification with AxioScan (Zeiss). The image processing and figure preparation were performed using the HALO AI software. Nuclear segmentation was performed with the default AI algorithm and the intensity of nuclear Ki67 staining was analysed using the Highplex FL module.

## Spheroid imaging and staining
10,000 cells collected 48 h post-transfection were seeded into 96-well cell-repellent plates (Greiner Bio-One, Cat. No. 650970) in 50 µL of culture medium and incubated overnight. The next day, 150 µL of fresh medium was added per well. For live imaging, spheroids were monitored over 120 h after seeding using the EVOS XL Core Digital Imaging System (Invitrogen). Spheroid area was quantified at multiple time points in ImageJ software, and measurements were normalized to the initial surface area (2 h after the assay initiation). For immunostaining experiments, a separate set of spheroids was cultured for 48 h after the addition of fresh medium, then fixed with 4% PFA for 30 min, permeabilized with 0.5% Triton X-100 v/v in PBS and blocked with 1% BSA w/v PBS for 1 h at room temperature. Spheroids were then incubated overnight at 4 °C with 1:200 Phalloidin Alexa Fluor 488 (Invitrogen, Cat. No. A12379) and 1:100 Ki67 Alexa Fluor 647 (Abcam, Cat. No. ab196907). Following washes, spheroids were counterstained with 1:5000 DAPI (Invitrogen, Cat. No. D21490). Brightfield and fluorescence images were acquired using the EVOS™ M5000 Imaging System (Invitrogen).

## Statistical analysis
R and Prism software were used to generate statistical analyses. The Kruskal-Wallis test was used to compare continuous variables across independent groups, followed by Dunn’s multiple comparisons post-hoc test. Spearman’s rank correlation test was used to assess the relationship between two continuous variables. To assess phenotype rescue following treatment with purified complement proteins, a two-way ANOVA was performed with condition (siC vs. gene silencing) and treatment as factors, followed by Šídák’s multiple-comparisons post hoc test. When applicable, p-values from multiple testing were adjusted using the Benjamini–Hochberg method. Data are presented as means ± standard deviation. Statistical significance: * p-value ≤ 0.05, ** ≤ 0.01, *** ≤ 0.001, **** < 0.0001.

## Results

## Complement genes C1R, C1S and C3 are enriched in TNBC and expressed by cancer cells
We explored the expression of complement genes with known tumour cell-intrinsic functions (C1R, C1S, C3, CFB, CFH and CFI) in breast invasive ductal carcinoma samples from the TCGA-BRCA cohort, stratified by molecular subtypes. Among these, C1R, C1S and C3 showed average higher expression in TNBC compared to luminal A, luminal B and HER2-positive subtypes (Fig.  A). This pattern was confirmed at the individual patient level (Fig.  B), where expression of these genes was significantly elevated in TNBC compared to the other subtypes (Fig.  C). To identify the cell types expressing C1R, C1S and C3 within the breast cancer TME, we analysed a scRNA-seq dataset comprising 22 primary breast tumours, including 8 luminal, 6 HER2-positive and 8 TNBC. Across these subtypes, the three genes were most highly expressed in cancer-associated fibroblasts, both in terms of expression levels and the proportion of cells expressing them. Expression in immune cells was largely absent, except for C3, which was also expressed in monocytes, macrophages and dendritic cells (DCs). Although expression in malignant epithelial cells was comparatively lower overall than in fibroblasts, subsets of cancer cells also expressed C1R, C1S and C3, particularly in TNBC patients (Fig.  D, Fig. ).
 We therefore asked whether the TNBC-associated enrichment observed at the bulk tumour level was also retained within the malignant-cell compartment. To address this, we next focused our analysis on tumour cell-intrinsic complement expression. Since normal epithelial and malignant epithelial cells clustered together, we next subsetted cancer cells based on their CNV profiles. Analysis of these cancer cell populations revealed that TNBC tumours had significantly higher proportions of cancer cells expressing C1R, C1S and C3 compared to luminal and HER2-positive subtypes (Fig.  E). Tumour cell-intrinsic expression of these complement components was further supported by protein staining, which revealed positive, often cytoplasmic localisation (Fig. ).
Consistent with findings in primary tumours, TNBC cell lines exhibited higher expression of the three complement components at both the mRNA and protein levels compared with luminal and HER2-positive breast cancer cell lines (Fig. ). RNA-seq data from the DepMap portal and in-house QuantiGene assays revealed elevated mRNA levels of C1R, C1S and C3 in TNBC cell lines, particularly in SUM-159PT and, to a lesser extent, in MDA-MB-468. Notably, MDA-MB-468 cells expressed C1R and C3, but not C1S (Figs. A, B). Because complement proteins can be either retained intracellularly or secreted, we measured their levels in culture supernatants by ELISA and confirmed protein secretion in SUM-159PT (Fig. ). In contrast, the remaining cell lines showed low or undetectable levels.

## Tumour C1R and C1S expression associated with improved patient outcome
Given the overexpression of complement gene expression in TNBC tumours and cancer cells, we interrogated the association between their expression and patient outcome. Higher levels of C1R and C1S were associated with improved overall survival (Fig.  F), and C1S with improved progression-free survival (Fig. ), whereas C3 expression showed no significant association. While this signal likely reflects multiple cellular sources, such as fibroblasts, complement produced by tumour cells may also contribute to this association, prompting us to investigate tumour cell-intrinsic complement in TNBC.

## Within TNBC cells, C1R and C1S share more closely matched expression patterns with one another than with C3
To further characterize C1R, C1S and C3 expression in cancer cells from TNBC patients, we examined their unique and overlapping patterns. Among these cells, 3% co-expressed all three genes, while 18% co-expressed C1R and C1S (Fig.  A). This co-expression was supported by a strong positive correlation between C1R and C1S expression in bulk RNA-seq data from TCGA-BRCA TNBC tumours (ρ = 0.87), although part of this association may arise from contributions by cancer-associated fibroblasts and other cell types (Fig.  B). C3 expression was also positively correlated with both C1R (ρ = 0.58) and C1S (ρ = 0.62), albeit weaker than the C1R–C1S correlation. Nevertheless, a substantial proportion of cancer cells expressed only one of the three complement genes, most commonly C1S (44%), followed by C1R (16%) and C3 (13%) (Fig.  A).
 RNAscope in situ hybridization was used to study the spatial distribution of C1R, C1S and C3 transcripts in TNBC tumours. Transcripts for all three complement genes were detected, within stromal regions consistent with fibroblast localisation (Fig.  C), where C1R, C1S and C3 showed comparable expression patterns. Within the tumour compartment, however, C1R and C1S displayed more closely matched expression profiles than C3. At the protein level, IHC performed on consecutive sections showed staining in both stromal and tumour compartments, with more prominent labelling in cancer cells (Fig.  D). Concordant results were observed in two additional tumours in both RNAscope and IHC analyses. The spatial distribution within cancer cells was further investigated using SeqIF staining in a representative TNBC tumour (Fig.  E, F). While some CK-positive cancer cells showed intracellular staining for all three proteins (Fig.  E), other regions contained CK-positive cancer cells co-expressing C1r and C1s but lacked C3, consistent with the patterns observed in scRNA-seq and RNAscope analyses (Fig.  A, C). Notably, although scRNA-seq data indicated higher mRNA levels of C1R, C1S and C3 in fibroblasts, protein detection by SeqIF and IHC revealed minimal or undetectable protein levels of these complement factors in αSMA-positive cancer-associated fibroblasts (Fig.  E), as well as weaker staining within the stromal compartment (Fig.  D).

## Metabolic and cell cycle pathways are enriched in C1R-, C1S- and C3-high TNBC cells, while inflammatory and apoptotic signatures link specifically with C1R and C1S
To investigate potential biological functions related to C1R, C1S and C3 expression in cancer cells, we stratified cells into high and low expression groups for each gene, and performed GSEA. Of the three genes, C1R-high cells showed the greatest number of enriched Hallmark gene sets, whereas C1S- and C3-high cells were linked to fewer pathways (Fig.  ). GSEA revealed that metabolism- and cell cycle-related gene sets were commonly enriched in C1R-, C1S- and C3-high cancer cells. Metabolic pathways such as mTOR signalling, glycolysis and oxidative phosphorylation were consistently enriched across all three groups. This enhanced metabolic activity was accompanied by transcriptional programs linked to cell proliferation and active cell cycle progression, as evidenced by enrichment of E2F targets, G2/M checkpoint and MYC target gene sets (Fig.  A-C). By contrast, apoptosis- and inflammation-related gene sets were specifically enriched in C1R- and C1S-high cancer cells, but not in C3-high cells. The leading-edge genes contributing to the enrichment of apoptosis-related gene sets included both pro-apoptotic factors and genes associated with apoptosis resistance (Tables S4-6). These cells also showed enrichment in immune signalling and pro-inflammatory cytokine pathways, including TNFα, IFNα, IFNγ and IL-2–STAT5 signalling (Fig.  A, B).
 

## Tumour cell-intrinsic C1r and C1s modulate TNBC cell behaviour in vitro independently of extracellular complement activity

## Establishment of an in vitro model for C1R, C1S and C3 knockdown
Given that gene sets related to regulation of apoptosis and cell cycle progression were enriched in complement-high cancer cells, we investigated whether C1r, C1s and C3 directly influence TNBC cell behaviour. To address this, we established gene-silencing models in SUM-159PT and MDA-MB-468 cell lines. Knockdown efficiency was validated in both cell lysates and supernatants using QuantiGene (Fig. A for SUM-159PT and Fig. A for MDA-MB-468), ELISA (Figs. B, C for SUM-159PT) and Western blot (Figs. G-I for SUM-159PT and Fig. B for MDA-MB-468). Western blot analysis further showed that C1r was secreted as the pro-enzyme form, C1s as both pro-enzyme and activated enzyme forms, and C3 as intact protein and cleaved fragments. Schematics of these proteins and their cleavage products are provided to aid interpretation of Western blot results (Figs. D-F). Of note, C1s could not be explored in MDA-MB-468 cells, as it was not expressed at levels detectable by our ELISA or Western blot assays.

## Knockdown of C1r and C1s decreases viability and proliferation and impairs 3D growth of TNBC cells
Knockdown of C1r and C1s in SUM-159PT cells increased cell death, as evidenced by a higher percentage of dead cells measured by live/dead staining (Fig.  A) and elevated LDH release (Fig.  B). However, only C1r knockdown significantly reduced cell viability in the XTT assay, while the reduction observed for C1s was not significant (Fig.  C). The effect of C1r knockdown was confirmed in MDA-MB-468 cells using both LDH and XTT assays (Fig. C, D). C3 knockdown in MDA-MB-468 cells led to a smaller, yet significant, increase in LDH release, although no changes in viability were detected.
 Knockdown of C1r reduced the proliferation of SUM-159PT cells, as measured by CFSE dilution (Fig.  D). This phenotype was further supported by a decrease in the proportion of Ki67-positive cells following C1r knockdown, as assessed by immunofluorescence (Fig.  E, F). In contrast, C3 knockdown led to an increase in proliferation in the CFSE assay, although this effect was not supported by Ki67 staining. No differences were observed for C1s. Similar trends were seen in MDA-MB-468 cells (Fig. E, F); however, these experiments lacked sufficient statistical power to reach significance (Fig. G).
To further explore the behaviour of knockdown cells in a 3D culture context, we performed spheroid formation assays. Although C1r- and C1s-knockdown SUM-159PT cells initially formed spheroids comparable to controls, these became less cohesive over time and showed progressive cell detachment compared to siC and siC3 (Fig.  G). After washing and fixation, spheroids from the C1r and C1s knockdown conditions were smaller (Fig.  H, I). In MDA-MB-468 cells, siC1r, and to a lesser extent siC3, also produced smaller spheroids (Fig. H, I). Area under the curve analysis confirmed significantly reduced spheroid growth over 5 days following C1r knockdown (Fig. J). Notably, spheroids derived from C1r-knockdown MDA-MB-468 cells were fragile and disintegrated during washing and fixation, precluding further analysis. As spheroid morphology reflects not only proliferation but also viability and cell-cell interactions, we stained spheroids for Ki67 to assess proliferation in 3D culture. Ki67 staining of SUM-159PT-derived spheroids in the siC1r condition showed visibly fewer positive cells (Fig.  J), in agreement with results observed in 2D culture.

## Combined knockdown of C1r and C1s does not produce additive effects but partially attenuates single knockdown phenotypes
Simultaneous silencing of C1r and C1s was performed to assess potential synergistic effects, in light of the partially overlapping phenotypes observed in single knockdown experiments. Combined knockdown was performed in SUM-159PT cells and validated in both cell lysates and supernatants by ELISA (Fig. ). Overall, simultaneous silencing did not exacerbate the phenotypes observed in single knockdowns and instead tended to attenuate them. In the live/dead assay, the proportion of dead cells in the combined knockdown condition was comparable to control levels and lower than that observed after C1s knockdown alone (Fig.  J). Similarly, LDH release remained elevated relative to control cells but was reduced compared with C1r knockdown (Fig.  J). Consistent with these observations, combined knockdown did not further decrease cell viability and instead partially restored viability relative to the C1r knockdown condition (Fig.  K). Finally, CFSE assay showed that the proliferation defect observed after C1r knockdown was not enhanced by additional C1s silencing (Fig.  L).
To further explore potential mechanisms underlying the observed phenotypes, we investigated whether PI3K/AKT and ERK1/2/MAPK signalling pathways were altered following knockdown by examining phosphorylated and total AKT and ERK1/2 levels by Western blot. Despite some variability between replicates, no consistent differences were detected between knockdown and control conditions, either in single or combined C1r/C1s knockdown (Fig. ).

## Extracellular modulation of C1r and C1s does not rescue the single or combined knockdown phenotypes
As complement proteins act through their enzymatic activity in the extracellular space, the contribution of extracellular complement to knockdown phenotypes was investigated. To address this, cells were supplemented with purified complement proteins (C1r and C1s in both pro-enzyme and active forms, or C3) or treated with C1-INH. Proteins were added at 10 µg/mL, a concentration exceeding endogenous C1r, C1s and C3 levels detected in SUM-159PT cell supernatants (Fig. C). In LDH assays, both C1r and C1s knockdown increased cell death, whereas in the live/dead assay only siC1s showed a significant increase (Fig. A, B). Cell viability measured by XTT was reduced after C1r and C1s knockdown (Fig. C). Proliferation decreased following C1r knockdown but increased after C3 knockdown (Fig. D). None of these phenotypes were rescued by addition of the corresponding purified complement proteins. Likewise, addition of C1r and C1s failed to rescue the phenotype in combined knockdown cells (Fig. E, F), and spheroid formation remained unaffected (Fig. G). In parallel, inhibition of extracellular complement activity was also tested. Because SUM-159PT cells do not express C1-INH (Fig. H) and based on the concentrations of C1r and C1s detected in the supernatant (Fig. C), C1-INH was tested across concentrations spanning near-stoichiometric to large molar excess. Treatment with C1-INH did not significantly affect cell viability, LDH release, cell death or proliferation at concentrations up to 2 µg/mL (Fig. I-L). A reduction in viability accompanied by increased LDH release was only observed at the highest concentration (10 µg/mL), likely reflecting nonspecific cytotoxicity of the compound.

## C1r, C1s and C3 mediate cytokine-chemokine interactions between TNBC cells and the immune microenvironment

## Expression of C1R, C1S and C3 correlates with immune infiltration and is spatially associated with T cells in TNBC tumours
GSEA of complement-rich TNBC cells, particularly those expressing C1R and C1S, identified enrichment of immune signalling and pro-inflammatory cytokine pathways. This observation prompted us to further study the relationship between complement gene expression and immune and inflammatory features in TNBC (Fig. ). We first analysed TCGA-BRCA TNBC samples to evaluate the association between C1R, C1S and C3 expression and MCP-counter scores [ ], which estimate the relative abundance of eight immune and two stromal cell populations. Expression of these complement genes was strongly positively correlated with immune cell infiltration, especially T cells, cytotoxic lymphocytes, monocytes/macrophages/DCs (labelled as monocytic lineage in MCP-counter), as well as natural killer (NK) cells (ρ = 0.41–0.58). Importantly, C1R and C1S displayed higher Spearman correlation coefficients with these immune populations than C3. By contrast, correlations with stromal populations, including cancer-associated fibroblasts and endothelial cells were more modest (ρ = 0.21–0.39) (Fig. A). To determine whether these correlations could be explained by direct expression of complement genes within immune cells, we revisited the scRNA-seq datasets and examined expression patterns across MCP-counter-defined cell types. Most immune cell types exhibited little or no detectable expression of C1R, C1S, or C3, except for C3 in subsets of monocytes and macrophages. Conversely, approximately half of fibroblasts expressed high levels of these genes (Fig. B). Together, these findings suggest that the strong correlations between complement gene expression and immune infiltration in bulk RNA-seq are unlikely to result from direct expression by immune cells and may instead reflect paracrine influences from other components of the TME, such as cancer cells. To test this hypothesis in situ, we stained TNBC tissues for multiple immune markers using hyperplex SeqIF and analysed the spatial proximity of immune populations to cancer cells positive for C1r, C1s and C3. About 50% of activated cytotoxic T cells were located within 10 μm of C1r- and C1s-positive cancer cells, with a lower proportion located near C3-positive cells (Fig. C). In contrast, exhausted cytotoxic T cells were rarely found within this distance. Macrophages, including M1-like and M2-like subsets, were generally more distant, with ~ 20% located within 10 μm, and both subtypes exhibited similar spatial distributions. Representative images illustrating these spatial relationships are shown in Fig. D.
 

## TNBC tumours with high C1R, C1S and C3 expression exhibit a pro-inflammatory cytokine signature
We further interrogated the leading-edge genes driving the enrichment in immune-related Hallmark pathways. As many of the these were associated with pro-inflammatory cytokine signalling, we focused on those encoding cytokines and cytokine receptors. To determine whether they were linked to C1R, C1S and C3 expression in tumours, we assessed their correlation in TNBC samples from the TCGA-BRCA cohort. While C3-high cells did not show this enrichment, C3 was included in the analysis for comparison, as it is also expressed by TNBC cells. Significant positive correlations were observed for all three genes with several cytokine- and receptor-related genes, including chemoattractant chemokines such as CCL2, CCL20, CXCL1, CXCL2 but also CXCL10, the pro-inflammatory cytokine TNF and cytokine receptors including IFNAR2, IFNGR1 and IL6ST (Fig.  E). Correlations were generally stronger with cytokines than with cytokine receptors. Similar patterns were found in vitro across multiple TNBC cell lines (Fig. ). Additionally, other leading-edge genes from the enriched immune-related Hallmark pathways encoded cytokine signalling intermediates such as STATs, NF-κB subunits, MAP kinases and TRAF adaptor proteins (Tables , 5).
Taken together, these findings suggest potential crosstalk between C1R-, C1S- and C3- high cancer cells and immune cells within the TME. This may involve (1) infiltrating immune cells that secrete cytokines driving C1R, C1S and C3 upregulation in cancer cells; (2) secretion of chemotactic factors by complement rich cancer cells that attract immune cell populations; or (3) a combination of both processes.

## Pro-inflammatory cytokines induce C1R, C1S and C3 expression in TNBC cells
On the one hand, to determine if cytokines can modulate complement gene expression in cancer cells, we stimulated the TNBC cell lines SUM-159PT and MDA-MB-468 with TME-associated cytokines, including IL-1β, IL-6, TNFα, TGF-β, IFNα, IFNβ and IFNγ. Both cell lines were confirmed to express receptors for these cytokines (Fig. A). QuantiGene analysis showed that IL-1β, TNFα, IFNα and IFNγ were the main inducers of C1R, C1S and C3 expression in both SUM-159PT (Fig.  F) and MDA-MB-468 cells (Fig. B). Of note, C1S expression in MDA-MB-468 cells, initially very low or undetectable under basal conditions, reached log₂ fold changes of up to ~ 5.8 following cytokine stimulation. ELISA of SUM-159PT supernatants confirmed increased production of C1r, C1s and C3 following cytokine treatment (Fig.  G). Notably, IL-1β and TNFα significantly increased C3 levels, while IFNα primarily induced C1r and IFNγ induced C1s. Individual values for SUM159-PT experiments are also shown (Fig. C, D; QuantiGene and ELISA, respectively). Due to assay sensitivity limitations, ELISA was not feasible for MDA-MB-468 supernatants.

## Knockdown of C1r, C1s and C3 reduces chemokine secretion by TNBC cells
On the other hand, to assess whether complement gene expression could influence chemokine secretion by TNBC cells, we performed C1r, C1s and C3 knockdown and measured chemokines previously identified as leading-edge genes (Fig.  E), such as CCL2, CCL20, CXCL1, CXCL2, CXCL8 and CXCL10, in SUM-159PT supernatants. Knockdown of C1r and C3 significantly reduced the secretion of all measured chemokines, whereas C1s knockdown had a more limited effect, with a significant decrease observed only for CCL2. Combined C1r/C1s silencing partially restored CXCL2 and CXCL10 secretion compared with C1r knockdown alone, while other chemokines remained unchanged, indicating that regulation of chemokine secretion by these complement components is not additive (Fig.  H). CXCL8 (IL-8) was also measured; however, most sample OD values exceeded the highest standard, preventing reliable quantification. To support these in vitro findings, scRNA-seq analysis revealed that C1R-high, C1S-high, and C1R/C1S-high TNBC cancer cells displayed increased expression of the studied chemokines, while C3-high cells did not show this pattern consistently (Fig. S12).

## Discussion
Our study identifies tumour cell-intrinsic complement components as regulators of TNBC biology (Fig.  ). Although their expression in TNBC cells can be enhanced by pro-inflammatory cytokines, we found that intracellular C1r and C1s are not merely passive bystanders but actively support cancer cell fitness. C1r-, C1s-, and C3-expressing TNBC cells actively shape the TME by secreting chemoattractants that recruit immune cells, particularly activated cytotoxic T cells. The resulting cytokine signals may further enhance complement expression, suggesting the existence of a positive feedback loop. This, together with the positive correlation between C1R and C1S expression and patient survival position tumour cell-intrinsic C1r and C1s as important, tumour cell-intrinsic modulators of the immune microenvironment.
 Using bulk and single-cell transcriptomics and protein tissue staining, we identified a tumour cell-intrinsic complement expression program: elevated C1R, C1S and C3, enriched in TNBC compared to luminal and HER2-positive subtypes. Cancer-associated fibroblasts are major contributors to complement production in the TME [ , ], and our transcriptomic data confirmed higher fibroblast expression, followed by tumour cells. Multiplex SeqIF, RNAscope and IHC suggest that tumour cells accumulate the complement proteins they express in the cytoplasm, while they are mostly likely secreted by the fibroblasts, as intracellular staining was rarer. This is consistent with emerging evidence that complement proteins can function through intracellular mechanisms in cancer cells [ , – , , , – ]: tumour cell-intrinsic C1r, C1s and C3 promote proliferation, survival, resistance to cell death and immune evasion across multiple cancer types [ , , , , , – , ], although functions are context-dependent, as C1r acts as a tumour suppressor in hepatocellular carcinoma [ ].
scRNA-seq revealed that C1R, C1S and C3 define overlapping yet distinct cancer cell subsets, with C1R/C1S co-expression being more frequent than co-expression involving C3 - a pattern confirmed in situ by RNAscope and SeqIF. The three genes were associated with transcriptional programs including E2F targets, G2/M checkpoint, MYC targets, oxidative phosphorylation, glycolysis and mTOR signalling - all features of highly proliferative and metabolically active cells [ ]. Knockdown experiments reinforced these associations while revealing divergent roles: C1r silencing reduced proliferation, whereas C3 silencing increased it alongside a trend towards higher pAKT/AKT ratios, consistent with MYC-related gene set enrichment in C3-high cells, and similar to reports in prostate cancer [ ] and without affecting apoptosis or viability. C1R and C1S, by contrast, were associated with apoptosis, p53 signalling and UV response gene sets, with leading-edge genes enriched for apoptosis resistance and survival functions. Accordingly, C1r and C1s silencing increased cell death, LDH release and reduced metabolic activity, confirming their role in TNBC cell viability.
Our findings show that tumour cell-intrinsic C1r and C1s promote TNBC cell survival and growth, in line with findings in other cancers. Loss of C1r or C1s induces apoptosis and reduces proliferation in cutaneous squamous cell carcinoma (cSCC) [ , ], and in oral squamous cell carcinoma (OSCC) [ ]. C1r and C1s also support tumour progression in esophageal squamous cell carcinoma (ESCC) [ , ] and clear cell renal cell carcinoma (ccRCC) [ ]. Although C1s knockdown produces similar phenotypes in cSCC and ccRCC [ , ], transcriptomic responses differ, suggesting cell-type–specific mechanisms that may explain why C1s silencing in TNBC affected cell death but not proliferation.
Because C1r and C1s knockdown showed overlapping phenotypes, we tested whether their functions are intracellular or autocrine, redundant or synergistic. Extracellular modulation of C1r or C1s concentration did not rescue the knockdown phenotype, and C1Inh did not phenocopy it arguing for intracellular mode of action, as in in ccRCC [ ]. This contrasts with cSCC, where enzymatic inhibition extracellularly reproduces the knockdown phenotype [ , ]. Combined silencing attenuated rather than enhanced the phenotype. C1r and C1s may interact intracellularly, as they do in the C1 complex, constraining each other’s activity. We hypothesise that loss of one leaves the other unpaired and able to engage other partners or substrates, whether through direct protein interactions or enzymatic activity, while simultaneous depletion of both eliminates this imbalance and thereby attenuates the phenotype. Both proenzyme and active forms were detected in TNBC cell supernatants, although the proenzyme predominated. Which of the two forms acts intracellularly remains to be studied. Mechanistically, in cSCC and OSCC C1r or C1s activate ERK1/2 and PI3K/AKT signalling [ , ], although these pathways were unchanged in TNBC cells. C1r and C1s can also enhance invasiveness by inducing matrix metalloproteinases and remodelling the extracellular matrix in cSCC [ ], consistent with ECM-related transcriptional changes in ESCC and ccRCC [ , ]. In melanoma, ALCAM-mediated clustering increases C1r and C1s expression and invasiveness [ ]. These mechanisms may explain the altered morphology and reduced cohesion of C1r- and C1s-deficient TNBC spheroids. Further investigation should show the mechanism by which C1r and C1s pro-enzymes or enzymes exert these functions and through which binding partners.
Complement-rich TNBC cell subsets also exhibited enrichment in immune signalling and pro-inflammatory pathways. Consistent with this observation, analysis of bulk RNA-seq data from TNBC tumours revealed that C1R and C1S expression, and to a lesser extent C3, positively correlated with immune cell scores, particularly T cells signatures. Notably, these genes were expressed at low levels in immune cells themselves, implying that non-immune populations, such as cancer or stromal cells, may influence immune infiltration through paracrine mechanisms. Spatial proximity analyses in patient tumors reinforced this possibility, as nearly 50% of activated cytotoxic T cells found in a TNBC tissue were located within 10 μm of C1r- and C1s-positive cancer cells, and about 40% near C3d-positive cells. Together, these observations point to potential crosstalk between complement-expressing TNBC cells and infiltrating immune cells.
Leading-edge genes of the enriched immune pathways in complement-rich TNBC cells from patients predominantly encoded cytokine receptors positively correlating with C1R, C1S and C3, consistent with enrichment of the cytokine–cytokine receptor interaction pathway in C1R-high ESCC cells [ ]. Cytokine stimulation confirmed that complement expression is inflammatory-responsive: IFNα induced C1r, IFNγ induced C1s, and TNFα induced C3, in line with reports in lung cancer and ccRCC [ , ]. TNFα and IL-1β activate NF-κB, whereas IFNα and IFNγ signal through JAK–STAT1 [ – ]. Consistently, C1R- and C1S-high cells were enriched in cytokine-responsive genes (STATs, NF-κB subunits, MAP kinases and TRAF adaptors), indicating active responses to inflammatory cues. C1R- and C1S-high cells in patients were further enriched in immunosupressive myeloid cells chemoattractant chemokines CCL2, CCL20, CXCL1, CXCL2 but also the pro-immunogenic cytotoxic T cells chemoattractant CXCL10 [ , ], whose expression correlated with C1R, C1S and C3 in TNBC tumours and was markedly reduced upon C1r or C3 knockdown in vitro. C1s knockdown selectively decreasing CCL2, consistent with data from ccRCC cells [ ]. Together, these data support a model in which cytokines secreted by infiltrating immune cells such as TNFα, IFNγ and IL-1β [ , ], induce complement proteins expression in TNBC cells, which reciprocally sustain immune recruitment through chemokine secretion. The immune-inflamed TNBC microenvironment [ , ] may thus itself drive the high C1R, C1S and C3 expression observed in these tumours.
To determine whether tumour cell-intrinsic C1r, C1s and C3 promote immune surveillance or immune evasion, we examined the correlation between their expression and immune cell infiltration and their prognostic impact in TNBC patients. The correlation between C1R and C1S expression and cytotoxic T cell scores, together with the spatial proximity of activated T cells to complement-positive cancer cells, favours a surveillance role, likely mediated by CXCL10 for C1r. This may partly explain the favourable prognosis associated with C1R and C1S expression, though larger cohorts are needed to confirm this. C3 shows no such association, possibly reflecting its weaker correlation with immune responses, and may contribute to poor prognosis through distinct mechanisms [ ]. Our results suggest that cell-intrinsic C1r and C1s are involved in two opposing forces – on one hand the modest enhancement of tumour cell fitness, on the other – the strong impact on the chemokines secretion and immune infiltrate. The secreted chemokines were chemoattractants of immunosuoressive macrophages and MDSC but also of cytotoxic T cells. Therefore, based on these results and the association with patient survival, TNBC could be classified more towards Complement-protective rather than Complement-aggressive category [ ]. Interestingly, tumour cell-intrinsic/intracellular C1s modulate immune infiltrate in other cancers as well, being linked to CD8⁺ T cell exhaustion in ccRCC [ ] and promoted M2-like macrophage migration in glioma [ ]. Both these cancers fall into the Complement-aggressive category. How the complement cascade is activated in TNBC vs. ccRCC for example also requires further investigation. These observations highlight the need for appropriate mouse models to dissect the tumour-intrinsic functions of C1r and C1s and determine how they differentially shape immune responses in Complement-protective versus Complement-aggressive cancers. Injecting control versus C1r-, C1s- or C3-deficient TNBC cells into immunocompetent and immunodeficient mice represents a priority experiment to formally establish the contribution of tumour cell-intrinsic complement to anti-tumour immunity.
In summary, our data identify tumour cell–intrinsic C1r and C1s as regulators of TNBC biology. They are involved in cancer cell fitness on one hand but also into a more consequential cytotoxic immune microenvironment modulation, positioning TNBC within the complement-protective tumour spectrum and reveal tumour-intrinsic complement signalling as a previously underappreciated axis shaping anti-tumour immunity.
