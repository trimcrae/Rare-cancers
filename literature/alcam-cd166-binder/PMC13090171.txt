## ABSTRACT
Glioblastoma (GBM) recurs rapidly despite surgery and radiochemotherapy and is associated with profound natural killer (NK) cell dysfunction. Adoptive NK cell transfer has emerged as a plausible immunotherapy for GBM and is being tested clinically; however, its efficacy is limited by the suppressive tumor microenvironment, low NK infiltration, and metabolic exhaustion. 4‐Acetylantroquinonol B (4‐AAQB), a ubiquinone derivative purified from Antrodia cinnamomea, has demonstrated antitumor and anti‐inflammatory properties. These properties suggest that 4‐AAQB may rescue or enhance NK cell activity against GBM cells. Therefore, we evaluated the effects of 4‐AAQB on the cytotoxicity of NK‐92 cells against human GBM cells using NK cell cytotoxicity assays. Treatment with 4‐AAQB significantly enhanced the cytotoxicity of NK‐92 cells against GBM cells, without affecting normal MRC5 fibroblasts. Additionally, in a GBM xenograft mouse model, 4‐AAQB and NK‐92 cells individually suppressed tumor growth, whereas their combination produced a synergistic antitumor effect without inducing liver or kidney toxicity. Bulk RNA sequencing of excised tumors revealed distinct differentially expressed genes in the combination group that were not shared with either monotherapy. Notably, the combined therapy upregulated immune effector genes involved in apoptotic cell clearance and NK cell maturation, while simultaneously downregulating key survival and resistance pathways. Furthermore, pathway analysis revealed suppression of autophagy and mitophagy, suggesting that 4‐AAQB sensitizes GBM cells to immune‐mediated apoptosis by disrupting intrinsic survival circuits. These findings support 4‐AAQB as an orally administered adjuvant that enhances NK cell immunotherapy for GBM and warrant further translational development.

Combined 4‐AAQB and NK therapy suppresses glioblastoma growth in vivo by inducing a distinct tumor stress state characterized by proteostasis disruption and autophagy‐associated programs, thereby sensitizing tumors to immune‐mediated control.

## Cell Culture
U87‐MG human brain glioma cells (U87 cells), MRC‐5 human fibroblasts (MRC‐5 cells), and NK‐92 human NK cells (NK‐92) were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (Manassas, VA, USA). U87 and MRC‐5 cells were grown in DMEM supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and 1× antibiotic–antimycotic solution. The NK‐92 was maintained in α‐MEM (without ribonucleosides and deoxyribonucleosides; with 2 mM l‐glutamine and 1.5 g/L sodium bicarbonate) supplemented with 0.2 mM myoinositol, 0.1 mM beta‐mercaptoethanol, 0.02 mM folic acid, 200 U/mL IL‐2 (PeproTech, USA), 12.5% FBS, and 12.5% horse serum. All cells were cultured at 37°C in an incubator containing 5% CO2. There was no mycoplasma contamination prior to all experiments.

2.5

## 
NK Cell Cytotoxicity Assay
The CytoTox 96 Non‐Radioactive Cytotoxicity Assay (Promega) was used to evaluate NK‐92 cell cytotoxicity according to the manufacturer's protocol. Briefly, NK‐92 cells co‐cultured with target cancer cells at various effector: target (E:T) ratios, including 10:1, 5:1, and 1:1, were plated into a round‐bottom 96‐well culture plate at a final volume of 100 μL/well, mixed gently, centrifuged at 250 × g for 5 min, and then incubated at 37°C and 5% CO2 for 24 h. Subsequently, 50 μL of the supernatant from each well was transferred to the corresponding well of a flat‐bottom 96‐well enzymatic assay plate, and 50 μL of the CytoTox 96 Reagent was added to each well of the plate. The plate was incubated at room temperature in the dark for 30 min followed by the addition of 50 μL stop solution to each well of the plate. Absorbance was measured at 490 nm. The percent cytotoxicity for each E:T ratio was calculated as follows:
percent cytotoxicity=experimental culture medium background−effector cell spontaneous−release culture medium background−target spontaneous−release culture medium background/target maximum release volume correction control−target spontaneous−release culture medium background×100.

## Flow Cytometry
The cells were washed twice, blocked, and incubated with saturating concentrations of the appropriate mAbs for 30 min at 4°C. Thereafter, the cells were washed twice and analyzed using a FACSCalibur system (Becton Biosciences). To evaluate the surface expression of NK‐related markers, NK cells (with or without treatment) were labeled with anti‐human/anti‐mouse antibodies. The fluorescence intensities of at least 1 × 105 cells were recorded and analyzed using the CellQuest software (Becton Dickinson).

2.7

## Enzyme‐Linked Immunosorbent Assay (ELISA)
The secretion of interferon gamma (IFN‐γ) was measured via an NK VUE Kit (NKMAX, Seongnam‐si, Korea) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Briefly, cell culture supernatants were centrifuged at 11,500 × g for 1 min at room temperature. Clarified supernatants were added to ELISA wells preloaded with the diluent and incubated for 1 h at room temperature. After washing to remove unbound substances, the wells were incubated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)‐conjugated anti‐IFN‐γ antibodies for 1.5 h. Tetramethylbenzidine substrate was added, and the mixture was incubated for 30 min, followed by a final wash to remove excess antibody‐HRP complexes. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm to quantify the IFN‐γ levels.

2.8

## 
RNA Preparation and Isolation
RNA was extracted with TRIzol and chloroform, and then precipitated with isopropanol according to the manufacturer's recommendations. Further purification was performed using an RNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen).

2.9

## 
RNA Sequencing and Differential Expression Analysis
RNA sequencing libraries were prepared using an Illumina TruSeq RNA Library Prep Kit (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Barcoded libraries were sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform. Raw sequencing reads were aligned to the human reference genome (GRCh38, Ensembl release 84) using the STAR RNA‐seq aligner. Gene‐level read counts were quantified using RSEM. Batch effects were assessed and corrected using the ComBat function in the sva R package, where appropriate. Differential gene expression analysis was performed on normalized read counts using the edgeR R package. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were defined as genes with an absolute log2 fold change (log2FC) greater than 0.5 and an adjusted p‐value < 0.05. For data visualization, including principal component analysis (PCA) and heat map generation, gene expression levels were quantified as transcripts per million (TPM) and log2‐transformed [log2(TPM + 1)]. Pathway enrichment analysis of the DEGs was conducted using the ConsensusPathDB (CPDB) [ ].

The PrimeScript cDNA Synthesis Kit (Takara, Japan) was used for the reverse transcription of RNA into cDNA. qRT‐PCR was performed using an Applied Biosystems StepOne Plus Cycler with SYBR Green PCR Master Mix (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The primers used are listed in (Table  ).

## Immunoblotting Assay
Cell lysates from U87 glioma cells (control vs. 4‐AAQB incubation) were centrifuged and quantified using a Bio‐Rad (CA, USA) protein assay. The lysates were mixed with sample buffer, denatured at 95°C for 5 min, and separated via electrophoresis on an SDS polyacrylamide gel. Following protein transfer to a PVDF membrane, primary antibodies were incubated overnight at 4°C, followed by incubation with HRP‐conjugated secondary antibody for 1 h. An imaging system (UVP ChemStudio Plus) captured the chemiluminescent substrate using a CCD camera. The primary antibodies used are listed in Table  .

## In Vivo Xenograft Animal Study
Female nonobese diabetic/severe combined immunodeficient mice (BioLasco, Taiwan), which were 6–8 weeks old and weighed 25.99 ± 1.62 g, were housed in plastic cages with free access to water and food. All animal experiments in this study were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Chang Gung Memorial Hospital (Approval No. CGU2023061303) and performed in accordance with the Guide for the Use of Laboratory Animals [ ]. To establish a human GBM xenograft mouse model, the mice were subcutaneously injected with 2 × 106 U87 GBM cells on the right side of the dorsal flank. When the tumors reached a volume of 150–200 mm3, the mice were randomly divided into four groups according to the animal ear tag used (control, 4‐AAQB, NK‐92MI, and NK‐92MI + 4‐AAQB; N = 3). An oral gavage of 5 mg/kg 4‐AAQB was administered thrice weekly, and 5 × 105 NK‐92 cells were injected intravenously once per week for two cycles. Tumor sizes were measured weekly in an unblinded manner, and the mice were sacrificed when the tumors reached the maximum allowed volume. Additionally, the serum levels of blood urea nitrogen (BUN), glutamic‐oxaloacetic transaminase (GOT), and glutamic‐pyruvic transaminase (GPT) were determined via an automated clinical chemistry analyzer (Dri‐Chem NX500i, Fujifilm, Japan).

2.13

## Statistical Analysis
All the experiments were conducted in triplicate or more. Data are presented as the means ± standard errors of the means (SEMs) and were evaluated using Student's t‐test. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism software (version 9.0.2; GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

## Results
3.1

## 4‐AAQB Increases NK Cytotoxicity Against GBM Cell Lines In Vitro
The cytotoxic effects of 4‐AAQB on NK cells were relatively low. The viability of NK‐92MI cells was not affected by treatment with 1 or 5 μM 4‐AAQB (Figure  ). Next, we examined LDH release from a co‐culture of NK cells with U87 (GBM cells) or MRC5 (normal fibroblasts) cells, with or without 4‐AAQB treatment. U87 cells were pretreated with different doses of 4‐AAQB at an E:T ratio of 5:1. As shown in Figure  , treatment with 4‐AAQB (at doses of either 1 or 5 μM) significantly enhanced NK cytotoxicity against target U87 GBM cells. However, 4‐AAQB had no effect on NK cell cytotoxicity in MRC5 cells or in normal fibroblasts (Figure  ). The effect of 4‐AAQB on NK cell cytotoxicity was further elucidated at E:T ratios of 10:1, 5:1, and 1:1. Consistent with previous findings, 1 μM 4‐AAQB treatment significantly augmented NK cell cytotoxicity against U87 GBM cells at a 10:1 E:T ratio without inducing cytotoxicity toward MRC5 cells (Figure  ).

3.2

## 4‐AAQB Increases the Function of NK Cells Against Glioma Cells
The increased expression and release of perforin and granzyme B are associated with NK cell efficiency [ ]. Treatment of co‐cultured glioma U87 and NK‐92 cells with 4‐AAQB (0, 1, or 5 μM) significantly increased the number of perforin‐expressing cells (Figure  ). Additionally, perforin expression was validated using qRT‐PCR. As shown in Figure  , perforin mRNA levels in NK‐92 cells significantly increased after treatment with 1 μM 4‐AAQB. Granzyme B, a key serine protease, was also elevated in NK‐92 cells after treatment with 1 μM 4‐AAQB, as demonstrated by both flow cytometry and qRT‐PCR (Figure  ). IFN‐γ is another cytotoxic cytokine produced by NK cells. Consistent with perforin and granzyme B, IFN‐γ expression in NK‐92 cells was significantly elevated after treatment with 1 μM 4‐AAQB, as determined via ELISA (Figure  ).
 
3.3

## In Vivo Therapeutic Efficacy of the Combination of 4‐AAQB and NK Cells
We further examined the antitumor effects of 4‐AAQB with and without NK cells in a human GBM U87 xenograft model in vivo. Once the U87 tumors reached a measurable size (approximately 100 mm3), each group was treated with vehicle control or 4‐AAQB (5 mg/kg) three times per week for 14 days and NK‐92 cells (5 × 105 cells) once per week for 2–4 weeks. Compared with vehicle control, treatment with only NK‐92 cells, only 4‐AAQB, or the NK‐92 cell and 4‐AAQB combination efficiently suppressed tumor growth, both in terms of tumor volume and weight (Figure  ). Treatment with 4‐AAQB did not affect body weight (Figure  ), and no evident renal or liver toxicity was observed at the given dose, as indicated by BUN levels, serum creatinine levels, and GOT/GPT ratios (Figure  ). Additionally, spleen weight was measured to estimate immunoreactivity. Spleens from the vehicle group had significantly higher weights than those from both the 4‐AAQB alone group and the combination of 4‐AAQB and NK‐92 treatment group, indicating that 4‐AAQB may improve immune system activity (data not shown).
 Further analysis of the xenograft tumors via qRT‐PCR revealed that the gene expression levels of tumor necrosis factor (TNF‐α; TNF), IFN‐γ R2 (IFNGR2), and perforin (PRF1) were highly increased following 4‐AAQB administration (Figure  ). These pro‐NK cell signals were increased by 4‐AAQB but were less pronounced in the combined group. However, the levels of hematopoietic cell signal transducer (HCST; also known as DAP‐10) and natural cytotoxicity‐triggering receptor 3 (NCR3) were significantly increased in the combined group (Figure  ). Programmed death ligand‐1 (PD‐L1), an immune checkpoint molecule, was downregulated in 4‐AAQB‐treated tumor samples (Figure  ).
 
3.4

## 
RNA‐Seq Analysis Reveals a Distinct Gene Expression Pattern Induced by Combined 4‐AAQB and NK Treatment
To investigate the global transcriptomic effects of the combined 4‐AAQB and NK cell treatment, we performed RNA sequencing, followed by PCA, differential expression analysis, and unsupervised clustering. PCA, based on normalized gene expression values, demonstrated a clear separation of samples according to the treatment conditions (Figure  ). The first two principal components explained 73.2% (PC1) and 10.25% (PC2) of the total variance. Notably, samples receiving the combined 4‐AAQB and NK cell treatment formed a distinct cluster clearly separated from the control, 4‐AAQB monotherapy, and NK cell monotherapy groups. This separation indicates that the combined treatment induces a global gene expression pattern that substantially differs from either a single treatment alone or the untreated control, supporting the notion that the combination treatment elicits a unique transcriptomic response rather than a simple additive effect. DEGs were defined as genes with an absolute log2FC greater than 0.5 and an adjusted p‐value < 0.05. Using these criteria, 264 DEGs were identified across all the pairwise comparisons (Table  ).
 Volcano plot analyses were performed to compare gene expression changes between the combined treatment and control groups, as well as between the combined treatment and NK cell monotherapy groups (Figure  ). A comparison between the combined treatment and the control revealed a substantial number of significantly dysregulated genes (Table  ), whereas fewer DEGs were observed when comparing the combined treatment with NK cells alone (Table  ). Conversely, a comparison between the combined treatment and 4‐AAQB monotherapy identified only a limited number of DEGs (Figure  , Table  ), suggesting that a considerable portion of the transcriptional changes observed in the combined group may be primarily driven by 4‐AAQB. Consistent with this observation, both monotherapies exhibited only a small number of DEGs compared with the control (Figures  and ), indicating that 4‐AAQB or NK cells alone did not strongly perturb the transcriptome under the conditions tested. Together, these results suggest that, while NK cells contribute to the overall biological context of the combined treatment, most transcriptional alterations observed in the combined treatment group were associated with the presence of 4‐AAQB, with the combination treatment producing a transcriptomic profile distinct from that of either monotherapy (Tables  and ).
To further visualize the expression patterns of the genes affected by the combined treatment, we generated a heatmap of the top 50 DEGs using unsupervised hierarchical clustering (Figure  , Table  ). Gene expression values were scaled by row‐wise Z‐scores to emphasize relative expression differences across samples. The heatmap revealed that genes dysregulated in the combined treatment group exhibited expression patterns that were clearly distinct from those observed in the control, 4‐AAQB alone, or NK alone groups. Samples clustered primarily according to the treatment group, with the combined treatment forming a coherent and separable cluster, further supporting the conclusion that the combined treatment induces a unique transcriptional state.
To explore the biological processes underlying the transcriptional changes induced by combined 4‐AAQB and NK cell treatment, DEGs identified from the combined versus control comparison (Table  ) were subjected to pathway enrichment analysis using CPDB. The analysis revealed prominent enrichment of pathways associated with translational regulation and protein homeostasis, including SRP‐dependent cotranslational protein targeting to the membrane, translation initiation and termination, and nonsense‐mediated mRNA decay (Table  ). Collectively, these pathways converge on a proteostasis/translation stress‐related transcriptional signature, suggesting that long‐term combination treatment induces a broad remodeling of protein synthesis, targeting, and quality control at the transcriptome level.
In addition to this dominant proteostasis‐related signal, CPDB analysis highlighted a secondary, yet biologically coherent, enrichment of stress‐adaptive pathways, including selective autophagy, chaperone‐mediated autophagy, and PINK1–PRKN–mediated mitophagy. Although these autophagy‐related pathways did not reach a stringent false discovery rate threshold, their recurrent appearance among enriched terms was notable, particularly given that autophagy and mitophagy are predominantly regulated at the post‐transcriptional and protein turnover levels and may not be fully captured by steady‐state mRNA measurements alone.
Taken together, these transcriptomic findings suggest that while the combined treatment primarily affects global proteostasis and translational control programs, it may also engage in autophagy‐ and mitochondrial quality control‐related mechanisms as downstream or adaptive responses.
To further investigate this possibility and assess whether 4‐AAQB can rapidly modulate autophagy‐associated signaling within a short and experimentally tractable timeframe, we performed short‐term in vitro validation (4‐AAQB, 24 h) using targeted molecular assays. Based on RNA‐seq and CPDB analyses, representative markers spanning the key stages of autophagy and mitophagy, including BECN1, LC3, SQSTM1/p62, and BNIP3L, were selected for subsequent evaluation using western blotting and qRT‐PCR.

3.5

## 
qRT‐PCR and Western Blot Validation of Autophagy‐Related Responses Induced by 4‐AAQB

qRT‐PCR analysis demonstrated that treatment with 4‐AAQB led to consistent upregulation of multiple autophagy‐related genes, including Beclin‐1, BNIP3L, LC3, and p62, compared to untreated controls (Figure  ). Induction was observed at both 1 and 5 μM concentrations, with a generally stronger response at the higher dose. These findings indicate that 4‐AAQB is sufficient to transcriptionally activate a coordinated set of genes involved in autophagosome initiation, elongation, and mitochondrial quality control. Western blotting further supported the activation of this pathway at the protein level (Figure  ). Treatment with 4‐AAQB increased BNIP3L and p62 expression. The accumulation of BNIP3L protein is consistent with engagement of mitophagy‐related signaling, while increased p62 levels suggest the activation of autophagy‐associated cargo recognition processes. Taken together, these qRT‐PCR and Western blot results demonstrate that short‐term exposure to 4‐AAQB is sufficient to induce both transcriptional and protein‐level changes in key autophagy‐ and mitophagy‐related markers in glioblastoma cells. These findings provide experimental support for the autophagy‐associated pathways identified in the RNA sequencing analysis of long‐term combined treatment and establish a mechanistic link between the transcriptomic stress‐adaptive signature and early cellular responses to 4‐AAQB.

## Discussion

A. cinnamomea
 is an exclusive fungus native to Taiwan. Extracts of 
A. cinnamomea
 have been reported to possess multiple functions, such as immunomodulatory, anti‐inflammatory, and anticancer effects [ , , ]. Regarding immune modulation, 
A. cinnamomea
 elicits an immune response through Toll‐like receptors [ ]. 4‐AAQB is a bioactive compound isolated from 
A. cinnamomea
 and has been reported to exert anti‐inflammatory effects by modulating the p53 and MAPK/NFκB signaling pathways [ , ]. Additionally, Li et al. reported that 4‐AAQB enhances dendritic cell function in hepatoma stem cells [ ].
In the present study, we demonstrated that 4‐AAQB effectively enhanced NK cell killing in U87 glioma cells, but not in U87 cells or normal cells, using an E:T NK killing assay. In addition to the E:T assay results, 4‐AAQB increased the secretion of perforin, granzyme B, and IFN‐γ, which are important in vitro NK cytolysis factors [ , ]. Antrodia camphorate (a strain different from 
A. cinnamomea
) has been reported to modulate immune responses by increasing perforin‐secreting CD8+ lymphocytes [ ].
We utilized an in vivo assay to assess the efficacy of 4‐AAQB and NK cells. A rodent glioma xenograft model was designed to compare the effects of 4‐AAQB, NK cells, and their combination therapy. The dosages of 4‐AAQB and NK cell injections have been described previously [ , , ]. This xenograft model provides rapid, direct observation without frequent animal MRI imaging. The combination of 4‐AAQB and NK cells effectively inhibited the progression of U87 glioma cells. Compared to 4‐AAQB or NK cell injection alone, the combined therapy further inhibited tumor growth. In this mouse model, the experiments were partly restricted to tail vein injections. Given that multiple injections may increase tumor control, mice received NK cell injections once per week until tumor reaches approved maximal size.
To further elucidate the in vivo effects of 4AAQB and NK cells, we performed qRT‐PCR on xenograft tumors. Compared with the control group, TNF, IFNGR2, and PRF1 were upregulated in the 4‐AAQB treatment group, and the injection of NK cells alone did not significantly alter the expression of IFNGR2 or PRF1. Only IFNGR2 was significantly upregulated in the combination therapy group. The expression levels of HCST (or DAP‐10) and NCR‐3 were increased in the 4‐AAQB+NK‐92 cell group, suggesting a combinatorial effect. High expression of TNF in xenograft tumors may indicate glioma suppression, whereas high IFNGR2 expression is also a prognostic factor in low‐grade glioma [ , , , ]. The NKG2D‐DAP10 complex is crucial for NK cell‐mediated signaling. The combination of 4‐AAQB and NK‐92 cells significantly increased HCST (DAP‐10) expression, indicating enhanced NK cell cytotoxicity [ ]. NCR3 (encoding NKp30), a functional membrane receptor crucial for NK cell activation, was also increased in the combination treatment group [ ]. Finally, PD‐L1, an immune checkpoint protein, was inhibited by 4‐AAQB treatment, indicating a potential role for immune checkpoint inhibition [ ].
Beyond the enhancement of NK cell effector functions and immune receptor signaling observed in vitro and in vivo, our transcriptomic analyses provided important mechanistic insights into how 4‐AAQB reshapes the tumor cellular state in the context of NK‐based immunotherapy. RNA sequencing revealed that the combined 4‐AAQB and NK cell treatment induced a global gene expression program that was clearly distinct from either monotherapy, as demonstrated by PCA and unsupervised clustering. This separation indicates that the combination treatment does not merely reflect an additive effect of NK cell cytotoxicity and pharmacological modulation; rather, it establishes a unique transcriptional state within the tumor microenvironment.
Notably, pathway enrichment analysis of the DEGs revealed a dominant signature associated with translational regulation, including SRP‐dependent cotranslational targeting and class I MHC‐mediated antigen processing and presentation (Table  ). Such pathways are increasingly recognized as hallmarks of cellular stress adaptation in rapidly proliferating tumors, particularly under immune or metabolic pressure [ ]. Disruption of proteostasis places tumor cells in a vulnerable state, reducing their ability to buffer additional cytotoxic insults, including immune‐mediated killing [ ]. In addition to translational stress signatures, RNA‐seq pathway analysis identified autophagy‐related processes, including selective autophagy, chaperone‐mediated autophagy, and PINK1–PRKN‐mediated mitophagy. Although autophagy is traditionally viewed as a programmed cell death pathway in glioblastomas, it plays a well‐documented protective role, helping tumor cells survive metabolic and therapeutic stresses [ ]. Autophagy and mitophagy are predominantly regulated at the post‐transcriptional and protein turnover levels, and may therefore be underestimated by steady‐state mRNA profiling alone [ ].
From a functional perspective, the activation of autophagy in GBM has been described as a double‐edged sword. Although basal autophagy supports tumor survival under stress, excessive or dysregulated autophagy can promote growth arrest, immunogenic cell death, or enhanced susceptibility to immune attack [ , ]. The RNA‐seq results suggest that long‐term (in vivo) combined treatment pushes GBM cells toward a stress‐adaptive yet metabolically constrained state, potentially lowering the threshold for NK cell‐mediated cytotoxicity rather than unidirectional oncogenic or tumor‐suppressive signaling.
Transcriptomic analysis revealed that compensatory metabolic responses were activated under prolonged (28 days) and heightened cellular stress, especially upregulated DEGs. Inhibition of autophagy can promote tumor suppression by inducing cellular senescence [ ]. In our system, although autophagy‐related buffering appeared to be attenuated, we observed an upregulation of miR‐1244, a microRNA reported to suppress senescence in cancer cells [ ]. This apparently paradoxical finding may reflect a compensatory response activated by tumor cells to counteract the senescence pressure induced by heightened proteotoxic and mitochondrial stress. However, this anti‐senescence signaling was insufficient to offset the immune‐mediated cytotoxicity, as evidenced by the enhanced NK‐cell‐dependent tumor cell killing observed in our study. Similarly, the induction of TDO2 and LYPLA1 [ , ], which have been implicated in immune suppression and GBM malignancy, may represent stress‐driven metabolic and immune evasion programs under immune challenge.
Notably, several downregulated DEGs, including EIF5B, HMGN5, and BEST1, have been linked to translational activity, chromatin accessibility, and cellular homeostasis, indicating a reduction in biosynthetic and adaptive buffering capacity [ , , ]. Collectively, these transcriptional changes are consistent with the remodeling of proteostasis and autophagy‐related programs, wherein the partial suppression of autophagic buffering may limit the ability of tumor cells to resolve immune‐induced proteotoxic and mitochondrial stress. This imbalance likely lowers the threshold for NK‐cell‐mediated cytotoxicity, providing a mechanistic context in which autophagy modulation contributes to enhanced immune susceptibility rather than direct tumor suppression. A summarized pathway interaction network was generated via CPDB analysis of 166 DEGs identified from the combined 4‐AAQB and NK cell treatment compared with the control (Figure  ). The network identified interconnected pathways linking proteostasis and stress adaptation, specifically those involving diverse autophagy mechanisms and endoplasmic reticulum protein processing. The convergence of these pathways suggests coordinated remodeling of cellular stress‐buffering capacity. Ultimately, these changes reflect impaired proteostasis and increased susceptibility to immune‐mediated cytotoxicity following the combined treatment.
 Subsequent short‐term (24 h) in vitro validation using western blotting was consistent with the RNA‐seq predictions, demonstrating that 4‐AAQB induced the coordinated upregulation of key autophagy and mitophagy markers, including BECN1, LC3, SQSTM1/p62, and BNIP3L (Figure  ). BNIP3L induction is particularly notable as it links mitochondrial quality control to cellular stress responses and has been implicated in regulating tumor sensitivity to immune‐mediated killing [ ]. Increased p62/SQSTM1 levels may reflect active autophagic flux or enhanced cargo recognition under stress conditions [ ]. Importantly, these molecular changes were observed following short‐term exposure to 4‐AAQB, suggesting that autophagy‐related signaling represents an early and direct response to the compound, rather than a secondary consequence of long‐term tumor regression.
Previous studies have consistently implicated 4‐AAQB as an autophagy‐modulating agent in various cancer models (pancreatic and ovarian cancers), where the suppression of autophagic flux is linked to enhanced cytotoxicity and improved chemosensitivity [ ]. In aggressive epithelial and chemoresistant settings, 4‐AAQB has been reported to inhibit autophagy through PI3K/Akt‐centered signaling, including the PI3K/Akt/mTOR/p70S6K and PI3K/Akt/MDR1 pathways, thereby augmenting cell death and drug response [ ].
Extending these observations to a distinct tumor context, our study provides multilayer evidence in GBM that the autophagy/mitophagy demand is transcriptionally induced, while autophagic resolution is functionally constrained, as reflected by p62 accumulation. This uncoupling of induction from effective flux offers a mechanistic basis by which 4‐AAQB may reduce the stress‐buffering capacity and lower the threshold for immune‐mediated cytotoxicity, highlighting autophagy flux suppression as a conserved action of 4‐AAQB with context‐dependent therapeutic implications.
Although autophagy is traditionally viewed as a programmed cell death pathway in GBM, it plays a well‐documented protective role by enabling tumor cells to survive metabolic and therapeutic stress [ ]. However, recent research has emphasized that the role of autophagy in glioma is context‐dependent and dichotomous, with both cytoprotective and cytotoxic effects reported depending on the stage of tumor progression and the specific therapeutic context [ ]. Notably, autophagy has been implicated in glioma therapeutic resistance, including the maintenance of glioma stem cells and modulation of the tumor microenvironment, and other studies have shown that stimulating autophagy may also induce glioma cell death [ , ].
Taken together, our findings support a dual‐mechanism model, in which 4‐AAQB acts on both immune effector cells and tumor‐intrinsic stress pathways. On the immune side, 4‐AAQB enhances NK cell cytotoxicity and effector molecule secretion and activates receptor signaling while suppressing PD‐L1 expression. On the tumor side, 4‐AAQB induces proteostatic stress and activates autophagy‐ and mitophagy‐associated programs, potentially reducing the capacity of the tumor to withstand immune attacks. This coordinated immune‐tumor reprogramming provides a mechanistic explanation for the superior antitumor efficacy observed with combined 4‐AAQB and NK cell therapy in vivo.
These data suggest that the pharmacological modulation of tumor stress‐adaptive pathways represents a viable strategy for sensitizing glioblastoma to NK‐based immunotherapy. Future studies that integrate autophagy flux assays, metabolic profiling, and orthotopic glioblastoma models will be essential to clarify the therapeutic exploitation of stress‐induced vulnerability. Our study suggests that 4‐AAQB is a promising immunometabolic adjuvant capable of enhancing the efficacy of NK cells through both immune activation and tumor‐intrinsic destabilization.
This study has certain limitations. First, RNA‐seq analyses may be influenced by batch‐related technical variability; however, pathway‐level conclusions were derived using stringent false discovery rate‐adjusted criteria and were corroborated by independent transcript and protein validations. As a naturally derived compound, the translation of 4‐AAQB requires additional work on dose standardization, safety and toxicology, and pharmacokinetic characterization. Notably, a recent review emphasized that the dose–effect relationship and potential side effects remain insufficiently defined, highlighting synthesis and metabolism as key priorities for future research [ ]. In the context of GBM, further studies are required to evaluate central nervous system exposure and blood–brain barrier penetrance, as well as formulation optimization and in vivo pharmacodynamics. Given its oral bioavailability, 4‐AAQB may play a potential role in immune enhancement in combination with CAR‐NK or patient‐derived NK cells.

## Conclusion
Our study demonstrated that 4‐AAQB effectively enhanced the NK cell‐mediated antitumor activity against GBM. Our findings showed that 4‐AAQB augmented NK cell cytotoxicity and effector molecule production in vitro and significantly suppressed tumor growth in a xenograft model when combined with NK cell therapy without evident systemic toxicity. Additionally, RNA‐seq revealed that the combined treatment induced a distinct gene expression program characterized by alterations in translational regulation and cellular stress‐adaptive pathways. Consistent with these findings, short‐term molecular analyses confirmed that 4‐AAQB directly upregulated key autophagy‐related markers. Collectively, these results indicate that 4‐AAQB enhances NK cell immunotherapy through both immune activation and modulation of tumor‐intrinsic stress responses, supporting its potential as a safe and effective adjuvant for NK cell‐based therapeutic strategies for GBM.
