## Abstract

## Background
Prenatal nicotine exposure (PNE) has been shown to significantly impact fetal development, leading to various pathologies in adulthood.

## Objectives
In this study, we analyzed the epigenetic effects of PNE on the proximal colon.

## Results
We show here that PNE decreases the length of the colon crypts and induces a higher Ki-67 index. The genes related to metabolic pathways, tight junctions, and neurodegenerative processes were downregulated in PNE colons, and genes related to inflammation and oxygen transporter activity were upregulated. We observed a decrease in acetylated H4Ac and H3K9Ac marks. Genome-wide DNA methylation analysis demonstrated reduced DNA methylation of genes related to cell adhesion and metabolic pathways and increased methylation levels at thermogenesis, GABAergic synapsing, and non-alcoholic fatty liver disease-related genes. The Notch signaling pathway was deregulated in PNE rats.

## Conclusion
PNE leads to perturbation of proximal colon functioning, plausibly through nicotine-induced epigenetic alterations.

## Background
Maternal smoking contributes to reduced fetal growth, preterm birth, brain anomalies, respiratory and cardiovascular problems, and increased risk of obesity and diabetes in adults [ – ]. Smoking at an early age is likely contributing to the increased risk of colon cancer [ – ]. In our previous study, we showed that gestational exposure to nicotine impacts reproductive function via epigenetic mechanisms. Specifically, we determined that genes related to nervous system signaling were differentially methylated in rat testis upon PNE [ ]. Using RNA-seq analysis of the pituitary gland from nicotine-exposed animals, we also determined that genes essential for cell migration, cell adhesion, and GABAergic signaling showed altered expression [ ]. This study confirmed the observation of other authors (e.g., reviewed in [ , ]) that both central and peripheral nervous systems are the targets of nicotine actions. The gut has its own nervous system, the enteric nervous system or ENS, an extensive neuronal network that innervates the gastrointestinal tract. ENS is highly autonomous and can function with or without input signals from the central nervous system (reviewed in [ ]). The ENS is derived from neural crest cells [ ] with functions ranging from nutrient digestion to blood flow regulation and immunological defense [ ]. The ENS neurons morphologically and electrophysiologically present a highly heterogeneous group intimately associated with enteric glia, which, both at the morphological and molecular levels resemble astrocytes and produce different neurotransmitters [ ]. Many factors, including changes in gut microbiota, could affect the function of the ENS. Indeed, the microbes metabolize dietary fibers that give rise to the major end-products, such as short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs), particularly propionate, acetate, and butyrate, among others. SCFAs influence the function of ENS via alterations in neurotransmitter production, mitochondrial function, immune activation, lipid metabolism, and gene expression [ ]. Therefore, changes in the gut microbiota could impact a variety of cellular functions. Alterations in microbiota metabolites have been found associated with several neurodegenerative diseases, such as Alzheimer’s (AD), Parkinson’s (PD), amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS), multiple sclerosis (MS), depression, autism, schizophrenia, vascular dementia, and anxiety, among others [ ] suggesting an impact of metabolic alterations of the gut on the central nervous system. For example, Alzheimer’s disease was found to be associated with a decreased number of Firmicutes and Actinobacteria, and an increased number of Bacteroidete species [ ].
This study is part of a comprehensive project aimed at assessing the link between nicotine exposure, mother’s gut microbiota alterations, and the consequences of nicotine exposure on the gut morphology, epigenetics, and gene expression in adult male progeny. In a previous study, it was determined that maternal exposure to nicotine leads to an alteration of microbiota composition, and it alters fetal exposure to circulating short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) [ ]. The connection between altered microbiota, SCFAs, and histone modifications has been recently reviewed [ , ]. Specifically, the microbiota-derived SCFAs, acetate, propionate, and butyrate act in a wide range of intestinal cells, including epithelial stem cells, columnar epithelial cells, and resident immune cells. Butyrate and propionate could exert their effects through their well-known activity as histone deacetylase (HDAC) inhibitors (e.g. [ ]); additionally, butyrate can directly provide an acetyl group for histone acetylation [ ]. The composition of the gut microbiome and SCFAs have emerged as significant contributors to colorectal cancer pathogenesis [ ]. Butyrate is the key energy source to colonocytes, where it is metabolized to acetyl-CoA, which augments histone acetyltransferase (HAT) activity [ ]. Increased histone acetylation promotes open chromatin configuration and impacts gene expression.
The work described here prompted the authors to explore this connection upon PNE exposure.
Here, we investigate the epigenetic effects of nicotine exposure on the gut and show that PNE reduces the size of crypts, increases cell proliferation, affects the expression of genes, and impacts major regulatory histone acetylation marks and DNA methylation in the colon. We report that genes known to be deregulated in neurogenerative diseases are also perturbed in nicotine-exposed rat colons.

## Results

## Gestational nicotine exposure alters the lifespan of intestinal epithelial cells
To explore the effects of maternal nicotine exposure on the colon morphology in the progeny, we compared hematoxylin and eosin-stained frozen colon sections in 11-week-old male animals born either to nicotine-exposed or control mothers. We examined the sections of the proximal colon for the size of the crypts. The proximal colon includes the cecum, a pouch that resides at the connection point between the small intestine and the colon, the ascending colon, the right colon flexure, and the transverse colon, the part of the colon that runs across the body between the ascending and the descending parts of the colon. In the colon, several cell types are present: enterocytes (the absorptive cells), the mucus-secreting Goblet cells, and the peptide hormone-secreting enteroendocrine cells.
Frozen colon sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin, and the images were taken with nanoZoomer. The length of the crypts from the bottom to the top layer of cells (Fig.  A) was measured using the Fiji tool [ ]. We compared the average values from at least 15 crypts from 4 different control and 4 nicotine-exposed males. We found a 0.8-fold decrease in the length of crypts in exposed animals compared to controls (Fig.  B).
 Since we observed a decrease in the length of the crypts, we decided to verify proliferation efficiency, as reduced cell numbers per crypt could result from decreased proliferation in the exposed animals. Ki-67 staining is routinely used to evaluate cell proliferation; thus, Ki-67 was selected as a biomarker for this analysis (Fig.  C). The number of Ki-67 positive cells in colonic crypts (15 crypts/animal) was counted and reported as a Ki-67 positive index (number of positive cells/total number of cells evaluated/per crypt). We determined that the Ki-67 index was higher in the nicotine-exposed group by 0.5-fold (Fig.  D). We also analyzed the length of telomeres (TL), a marker of cell divisions. To this end, DNA was extracted from the proximal colon. The analysis was performed in a mixed pool of cells. We observed the tendency for TL to increase, suggesting the possibility of a higher potential for division in nicotine-exposed cells.
To determine if TL could be increased due to increased activity of telomerase reverse transcriptase (TERT), we decided to explore TERT expression in nicotine-exposed guts. It has been determined that TERT protein is expressed in normal mucosa, though weaker than in carcinoma [ ]. We performed immunostaining of frozen sections using antibodies against TERT and Ki-67. We found that TERT-positive cells are colocalized with Ki-67 cells in some areas (Fig.  F). We counted the number of positive cells per area and compared the data from the control and nicotine groups. Our analysis showed that TERT-positive cells are 1.8 times more abundant in nicotine-exposed guts (Fig.  G).
Our observation showing an increase in TERT and a tendency to elevation in TL coupled with the decrease in the size of crypts, suggests that there are fewer cells in the crypts of the nicotine-exposed animals and that following the division, nicotine-exposed cells have a shorter lifespan compared to the control. In summary, prenatal exposure to nicotine led to a reduced number of cells in crypts, most likely due to reduced lifespan time.

## RNA-seq analysis reveals a decrease in the expression of metabolic and neural system-related genes and an increase in the expression of inflammatory genes
To explore the molecular consequences of nicotine exposure in the digestive system, we performed transcriptomic analysis of the proximal colon using paired-end stranded RNA sequencing using three biological replicates from the nicotine-treated and control male rats. We identified 369 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between nicotine-exposed and control groups (FDR < 0.1, FC > 1.5) (Fig.  A, Table S1). Of DEGs, 239 were down-regulated and 130 were up-regulated (Fig.  B). Notably, among highly differentially expressed genes were genes such as Pomc, a key satiety regulator [ ]. The reduced expression of Pomc was linked to weight gain in the prenatally exposed to nicotine humans [ ]. Nr4a1 is associated with decreased adipogenesis [ ], the increase in Nr4a1 in our study could be related to a compensatory mechanism to reduce fat storage.
 We performed a functional annotation of the DEGs with the gene ontology program DAVID separately for downregulated and upregulated genes (Fig.  C). Among downregulated genes, we identified genes that are expressed in brush border (Vil1, Myo1d, Scin, Cdgr2, Myi7b, Cdhr5, Slc5a1, Ezr, Ralgds), genes encoding proteins involved in cell adhesion (Jup, Itgb4, Cdhr2, Cdhr5, Dsg2, Pkp3, Ezr, Ptprf), and genes encoding proteins with oxidoreductase activities (Aldh1l1, Aldh16a1, Acad10, Aldh1a7, Gapdh). The alternative functional analysis by Enrichr revealed that many downregulated genes are related to metabolic pathways (Fig.  D), and genes deregulated in Alzheimer’s disease (AD) (Fig.  E). The annotation by DAVID of upregulated genes revealed that there is enrichment in the genes related to haptoglobin-hemoglobin complex (Hba-a3, Hbb-b1, Hbb, Hba-A2, Hba-a1, Hbb-bs), and genes encoding proteins which play role in the bacterial response (Tnnt2, Ccl2, Ifi44, Hba-a2, Hba-a1, Cflar, F3) (Fig.  F-G), among others. Notably, high haptoglobin levels are associated with an inflammatory disease; markedly increased haptoglobin levels are found in patients with inflammatory diseases [ ].
In summary, RNA-seq analysis of the colon gene expression showed that genes associated with metabolism, signaling, cell migration, and cell adhesion were downregulated in the nicotine-exposed group; in contrast, genes related to inflammatory processes were upregulated in the nicotine-exposed group. In addition, many genes previously found to be deregulated in neurodegenerative diseases such as AD were among the downregulated genes.

## The acetylation regulatory histone marks were decreased in the nicotine-exposed colon
Since nicotine exposure leads to changes in microbiota and decreases in the availability of short fatty acid chain groups [ ], we decided to analyze the major regulatory histone marks that could be affected. To examine the level of modified histones in the colon, we immunostained colon tissue with antibodies against histone marks as described in the Methods section and performed the quantitative analysis of images. We examined hyperacetylated H4 histone marks (acetylated at K5, K8, K12, and K16)/H4penta; these H4 acetylated marks are known to be important for chromatin remodeling (e.g. [ ]). We co-stained sections for H4penta together with a reader of the histone bromodomain factor BRD4. BRD4 normally accumulates at hyper-acetylated and transcriptionally poised chromatin regions and serves as a nucleation center for the assembly of large protein complexes that promote RNA-PolII activity, stimulating transcription initiation [ ]. Normally, both markers show a bright nuclear staining pattern (Fig.  A). We measured the fluorescence level of both markers and determined a 0.8-fold decrease in H4Ac (Fig.  B) and 0.77-fold tendency to decrease for BRD4 (Fig.  C).
 H3K9Ac mark is associated with active promoters (reviewed in [ ]). Moreover, it is widely recognized that H3K9Ac is a hallmark of active transcription. In this respect, H3K9ac showed a strong nuclear staining pattern (Fig.  D) and demonstrated a significant 0.4-fold decrease in the global level of H3K9Ac in response to nicotine exposure (Fig.  E).
Importantly, H3K4me3 is a pivotal mark of actively transcribed genes. To explore the question of whether H3K4me3 levels are altered in the nicotine-exposed colon, we performed immunofluorescence analysis (Fig.  F). We determined that there is a tendency for H3K4me3 level to decrease upon PNE (Fig.  G).
In summary, we observed lower levels of H3K9Ac, and H4Ac, and a tendency for a decrease in BRD4 and H3K4me3 in nicotine-exposed colons, suggesting the impact of nicotine exposure on global histone acetylation levels. Our observation is consistent with an overall reduction in gene expression, suggesting that maternal exposure to nicotine led to reduced levels of histone acetylation in the fetus, which could be responsible in turn for the lower accessibility of chromatin to the transcription factors and RNA polymerases.

## Nicotine affects DNA methylation in cell adhesion, cell migration, metabolism-related, and GABAergic genes
To further explore the impact of nicotine on epigenetic regulation, we sought to study DNA methylation in the colon. To this end, we extracted DNA from the colon and carried out a DNA methylation enrichment experiment using the EpiMark enrichment kit. 52,043 methylated regions were detected. We identified 291 differentially methylated regions (DMRs) that showed an altered pattern of DNA methylation (FDR < 0.15) in response to nicotine exposure (Fig.  A). Among differential peaks, 205 had decreased DNA methylation, and 86 regions showed increased DNA methylation (Fig.  A, Table S2). For example, we found decreased DNA methylation near Txnrd1 (Fig.  B), an increase in Gnb2 (Fig.  C). We performed analysis using the ChIPseeker package and determined that most of the differentially methylated regions (DMRs) ~ 36% are genomic elements located distally from the gene promoters (Fig.  C). However, nearly ~ 24% of DMRs were found in genic regions such as introns, 9% of DMRs were in exons, and ~ 17% in promoters (Fig.  C).
 To determine the genes controlled by the DMRs, we assigned regions to the genes using ChIPseeker. The program revealed that 270 genes are located in the vicinity of differential peaks. Functional annotations of genes corresponding to the low methylated regions in the nicotine group by DAVID showed that they are enriched in genes related to cell adhesion (Cdh4, Alcam, Rt1-ha, Ncam2, Cdh15), cell migration (Enpep, Foxe1, Pik3cA, Prag1, Cdh15), and metabolic pathways (Mtmr1, Chst9, Tigar, Mvk, Mmab, Prdx6, Acaca, Ggta1, Mtmr7, Pigc, Fig.  , Pik3cA, Rdh10, Dsel, Gne) (Fig.  D). Notably, the expression of cell adhesion and migration-related genes was also enriched among downregulated genes in the nicotine group (Fig.  C). Among genes corresponding to highly methylated regions, there are genes associated with thermogenesis (Ndufb6, Zfp516, Cox7b2, Prkab1) and GABAergic synapse (Grm8, Disc1, Cit) -related functions (Fig.  E).
To reveal the potential impact of transcription factors on DNA methylation alterations, we examined enriched motifs at DMRs. To this end, we extracted sequence information of DMRs in fasta format and performed a motif search using MEME-Chip, which performs comprehensive motif analyses (including motif discovery) on sequences. We used the eukaryotic motif database MOUSE/HOCOMOCOv11 for the search. Three common motifs were found in many sequences. Parts of the first common motif are similar to the binding site for the AHR and ZBTB14 transcription factors (Fig.  F). In the second motif, we did not find known binding sites for the transcription factors, and part of the third enriched motif had a similarity to the binding site of EGR1. Due to the importance of EGR1 for colon function [ ], we took a closer look at the genes that have EGR1 binding. We determined that these are large groups of genes known as transport-related genes. For example, this group includes genes encoding the proteins for the potassium channels, namely, Kcnu1, Kcnk6, Kcnip2, Kcnc3, and Hcn3. Notably, we determined that genes that reduced expression in EGR knock mice were found to have decreased DNA methylation in PNE-exposed mice, including Ice1, Tusc3, Rps29, Gnb2, Sgk1, Rps27a, and Prdx, confirming our finding that the effects of nicotine could be mediated via EGR1.
We thought to verify whether changes in DNA methylation correlate with gene expression changes. Interestingly, only a limited number of genes were found. 23 DMRs were localized near genes with significantly altered expression at p < 0.05 (highlighted in bold in Table S2). These are mainly regions with decreased methylation, largely located in promoters and inside the genes (Fig.  G). For example, the down-regulated Mvk gene has a DMR in the gene body (Fig.  H).
We also examined the impact of all DMRs on gene expression without taking into account the p-value. We analyzed DMR regions with FC > 1.2. We determined that 117 of DMRs (66%) have impacted gene expression. Specifically, 30% and 22% of low methylated DMRs were located near down- and up-regulated genes, respectively, and 48% of low DMRs did not impact the gene expression. 23,5% and 23,5% of increased methylation DMRs were localized near downregulated and up-regulated genes, respectively, and 53% of increased DMR regions did not impact the gene expression. 56% of the DMR regions that impact RNA expression were localized near the gene bodies and promoters, and 44% of DMR regions were located distally. 34% of DMRs that did not impact RNA expression were located mainly in gene bodies and promoters.
In summary, nicotine exposure induced alterations in the DNA methylation in the genes relevant to cell adhesion, migration, metabolism-related, thermogenesis, and in genes with GABAergic synapsing functions. We propose that abnormal activities of the AHR and EGR1 transcription factors could contribute to the alterations in gene expression, as they are known to be a master regulator of many processes, including the regulation of inflammation and homeostasis [ , ].

## Integrative analysis of testis and colon DNA methylation revealed 30 common differentially methylated genes
In our previous work, we showed that exposure to nicotine alters the reproductive function in males [ ]. We also demonstrated that PNE leads to alterations in the DNA methylation in the genes associated with diverse biological activities such as cell migration, cell adhesion, and GABAergic signaling. During development, global reprogramming of the embryonic chromatin occurs, so the entire genome undergoes rounds of demethylation and re-methylation to establish new cellular layers (reviewed in [ ]). Precursors of germ cells undergo a second wave of demethylation and re-methylation to establish a new population of germ cells that are inherently different from somatic cells. To explore the commonality in these processes, we compared the differentially methylated gene list in the testis and the colon and identified 30 common genes that changed in both datasets (Fig.  I), the list includes genes that encode for transcription factors (Foxe1, Foxj3, Zfp516) and DNA repair factors (Aptx), (Table S3 and Fig.  K). Of the common differentially methylated genes, 23 genes had the differentially methylated region located at the same position in the genome; however, 7 DEGs had differentially methylated regions located at different nonoverlapping regions in the testis and in the colon (Table S3). Most of these differentially methylated genes have a similar direction in the fold changes. Thus, our comparative analysis revealed common genes that are differentially methylated in the testis and colon, suggesting that these genes undergo similar alterations during reprogramming windows.
In summary, PNE exposure mostly causes a tissue-specific impact on DNA methylation, and most of the differentially methylated genes have tissue-specific and distinct DNA methylation patterns. Notably, genes encoding migration-related proteins and GABAergic signaling factors were affected in both tissues.

## The Notch signaling is altered in nicotine samples
The Notch signaling pathway appears to be critical for neural stem cell regulation and maintenance, and it plays a role in late neuroglial development [ ]. The Notch pathway is also essential for the development of the enteric nervous system (e.g., reviewed in [ , ]). NOTCH1-positive cells were detected in the colonic crypt in the absorptive progenitor cell population [ , ]. It has been found that in mouse intestines where either Notch1 or Notch2 were deleted, an impaired crypt regeneration after radiation was observed, suggesting that Notch1 and Notch2 regulate epithelial cell proliferation, cell fate determination, and post-injury regeneration in the gut [ ].
To explore the role of Notch signaling in nicotine-induced pathologies, we analyzed the expression of Notch signaling genes in the colon. To this end, we assembled a list of 127 genes known to be implicated in Notch signaling using the Amigo database https://amigo.geneontology.org/amigo (Table S4). We extracted the normalized counts for these genes from our RNA-seq data and identified 18 genes that have altered expression (p < 0.05), Fig.  A, that include Maml3, Ncstn, and Cepbpa genes. It is suggested that Notch1is regulated via super-enhancers (SEs, e.g [ ].). SEs are large clusters of transcriptional enhancer arrays that regulate the expression of genes required for cell identity establishment. In mice, the SE element mSE_04418 is located in the vicinity of the Notch1 gene [ ]. Moreover, DNA methylation marks seem to be preserved in mouse sperm DNA [ ] and overlap with mSE_04418 (Fig.  B). We postulated that a similar SE element is likely to be functional in rats, as the region of Notch1 is conserved between species, and the DNA methylation pattern is preserved in the colon (Fig.  C). It has been found that SEs are generally marked by histone acetylation H3K27Ac in the tissue where the gene is active. To confirm that and to validate the NOTCH1 antibody, we prepared frozen sections from embryonic E15.5 and 3-month-old mouse brains and immunostained them against NOTCH1 and H3K27Ac. NOTCH proteins are ligand-activated transmembrane receptors [ ]. Our analysis revealed that in the embryonic brain, H3K27Ac and NOTCH1 localized to the same cell, but NOTCH1 preferentially shows the cytoplasm-membrane distribution. Notably, NOTCH1 staining is clustered in a few regions (Fig.  D, top). In adults, NOTCH1 is expressed in the astrocyte cells of the brain (Fig.  D, bottom) [ ]. In the colon, abundant expression of Notch1 and its ligand Jag1 was observed in the proliferative zone located within the middle third of the crypt [ ]. In our study, we determined that NOTCH1 appears to be expressed in the colonic crypts as a cluster, with larger ones in nicotine groups (Fig.  E-F). To reveal the role of H3K27Ac in the regulation of Notch signaling genes, we performed ChIP-qPCR analysis of H3K27Ac occupancy at several genes using proximal colon tissue. We compared the ChIP-to-Input ratio and determined that genes related to Notch signaling, such as Nsctn, that encode for component gamma-secretase complex required for the proteolysis of NOTCH1 [ ] and Maml3 (which is part of transcriptionally activating complexes of NOTCH1 [ ]) are highly dependent on H3K27Ac (Fig.  G), suggesting that acetylation may be responsible for the activation of Notch-dependent genes. We also observed the decreased H3K27Ac at Nsctn and Maml3, which is consistent with the decreased expression of these genes (Fig.  G).
 We also examined whether the decrease in the expression of Notch signaling genes could be due to reduced H3K4me3 levels; to explore other pathways, we added to our analysis metabolic and apoptotic genes. To this end, we performed ChIP using H3K4me3 -qPCR analysis of eluted DNA. Our analysis revealed that H3K4me3 had a reduced level in Notch signaling (Notch1), metabolic (Ezr), and apoptotic (Fadd) genes (Fig.  H), suggesting a possible link between altered histone acetylation and H3K4me3 levels.
Thus, our data support the notion that there is an alteration in the Notch signaling pathway as well as a NOTCH1 protein in response to nicotine exposure. We suggest that the decreased acetylation level does not activate normally Notch- downstream target genes. Overall, the perturbed Notch signaling may contribute to the reduced maintenance of the cells in the colon crypts.

## Discussion

## The effects of PNE on gut structure
Here, we report several observations with respect to the colon in the progeny of the nicotine-exposed rats. Firstly, we observed a decrease in crypt size together with the elevated Ki-67 index. Importantly, similar results were obtained in a mouse study where the animals received cigarette smoke condensate for 2 weeks [ ]. In that study, mice developed induced intestinal inflammation and a reduction in villus-crypt length [ ]. In another study, mice were chronically exposed for 6 months by nose-only inhalation to electronic cigarettes (e-cigarettes), and it was documented that e-cigarette exposure decreased colon cell proliferation [ ]. Since we observed an increase in the Ki-67 index and a decrease in villus length, we suggest that PNE decreases the survival of colonic cells.
Secondly, we established a decrease in the expression of genes related to the regulation of microvillus length, such as Vil1, Cdhr2, Cdhr5, and Ezr. The small intestine is functionally dependent on the state of the brush border, an array of microvilli that forms the apical surface of enterocytes. In the core of each microvillus, actin filaments are bundled by two major proteins, villin and fimbrin. Villin has an important function in wound repair [ ]. Constant wound healing is required to maintain the integrity of the intestinal epithelial barrier because of exposure to many environmental factors. Actin-binding protein villin drives both apical microvilli disassembly and promotes lamellipodial extension. Thus, villin plays a role in enterocyte remodeling upon injury [ ], and reduced expression Vil1 could be linked to the higher sensitivity of cells to injury. An increase in the expression of genes related to bacterial responses (Tnnt2, Ccl2, Ifi44, Hba-a2, Hba-a1, Cflar, F3) supports that notion. It is conceivable that reduced expression of Vil1 also contributes to the observed decrease in enterocytes’ lifespan.
On the other hand, we observed reduced expression of genes important for focal adhesion (Gak, Lasp1, Prag1, Itgb4). Focal adhesions are integrin-containing, multi-protein structures that form mechanical links between intracellular actin and the extracellular substrate. The dynamic assembly and disassembly of focal adhesions play a central role in cell migration. During this process, both the composition and the morphology of the focal adhesion change. It is suggested that the size of focal adhesions predicts cell speed migration [ ]. Thus, diminishing the expression of focal adhesion genes could impact not only the extracellular matrix but also cell migration, which is essential for constantly moving cells to reach the periphery.
Thirdly, we determined the global impact on the expression of the genes related to the degeneration of nervous system function (Chrm1, Capn1, Ndufb10, Fzd5, Tubb4b, Nefl, Sdha, Tubb3, Uba1). Several studies revealed the alterations in gene expression relevant to the nervous system upon nicotine exposure. For example, nicotine administered to pregnant rats led to increased serotonin transporter (SERT) density in the forebrain of offspring on postnatal day 22 [ ]. Prenatal exposure to nicotine led to a deficient catecholamine synthesis in the adrenals [ ]. Another study showed that gestational exposure to nicotine induces long-lasting changes in nicotinic cholinergic receptor nAChR expression that may underlie the vulnerability of adolescents to nicotine dependence [ ]. Combined ethanol and nicotine exposure led to increased expression of GABA(B1) and GABA(B2)R in the rat brain [ ]. We and others showed that prenatal nicotine exposure leads to alterations in the glutamatergic system [ , ].
Fourthly, we observed the decreased expression of many metabolic genes related to glycolysis and gluconeogenesis (Gpi, Pfkl, Hkdc, Eno1, Gapdh). It was shown that maternal nicotine administration during pregnancy and lactation suppressed glycogenolysis and glycolysis [ ]. It was also suggested that inhibition of glycolysis by nicotine leads to an increase in oxygen uptake [ ]. The decreased expression of glycolytic genes observed in our study, together with the increased expression of oxygen transporter activity genes, is in line with these observations. On the other hand, reduced glycolysis could be linked to hyperglycemia and increased gluconeogenesis in the liver. We also observed alterations in genes related to non-alcoholic fatty liver disease in our RNA-seq (Ndufb10, Sdha) and MeDIP-seq (Ndufb6, Cox7b2, Prkab1) experiments. In this respect, it has been shown that maternal nicotine exposure during lactation leads to fatty liver (steatosis) in adult male offspring [ ]. Thus, our results also suggest that exposure to nicotine could be related to obesity. The increased body mass index in association with prenatal exposure to cigarettes was observed both in human [ ] and rat [ ] studies.
In conclusion, nicotine exposure impacted gut morphology and resulted in alterations in the expression of genes associated with a diverse roster of biological activities, such as glycolysis, microvillus formation, focal adhesion, and nervous system pathways.

## Epigenetic effects of nicotine
Nicotine exposure in our model resulted in a global decrease in histone acetylation. This is consistent with a prior report in a rat cartilage defect model treated with nicotine for 12 weeks. Exposure to nicotine led to reduced acetylation of H3K9 and H3K14 in the promoter of Sox9 [ ]. It is conceivable that a low level of SCFA can lead to decreased availability of acetate moiety and subsequently decreased histone acetylation in the colon. The pregnant rats exposed to nicotine had lower levels of acetate in amniotic liquid [ ].
We identified many EGR1 binding sites in DMRs of differentially expressed genes. EGR1 is a member of the immediate early family of transcription factors playing a role in memory formation. In experiments using EGR1-based chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) followed by high-throughput DNA sequencing (ChIP-seq) a large number of genes related to synaptic vesicle transport and transmission of signals elicited by calcium influx were identified [ ]. In another study, developmental nicotine exposure was associated with increased cell excitability, accompanied by compensatory changes in the activity of potassium channels in motoneurons in nicotine-exposed neonatal rats. Both studies suggest that the increased potassium and calcium currents in nicotine-exposed neurons compensate for elevated excitatory synaptic input [ ]. In our study, we also observed that several potassium channel genes (Kcnu1, Kcnk6, Kcnip2, Kcnc3, and Hcn3) demonstrated altered DNA methylation, suggesting a similar role of potassium channels in compensation for nicotine excitation. Moreover, knockdown of Egr-1 increased colon cancer cell proliferation, migration, and invasion [ ], suggesting that EGR1 has a suppressive role in colon cell proliferation.
We noted, a low correlation between DNA methylation and gene expression. To get insight into the nature of not-impacted genes, we compared the base means, which are the average values of the normalized counts of impacted and not-impacted genes, and determined that the average value of the base mean of not-impacted genes is three times lower, suggesting that some DMR regions are located near low-expressed genes. Thus, it is conceivable that these genes may be expressed more highly in other tissues. Since nicotine exposure occurred during the reprogramming window, including genome-wide DNA demethylation and remethylation in early embryos, DNA methylation alterations at some regions reflected the epigenetic scars of events that occurred in the past and may impact other organs or cells than the colon.
In summary, our study suggests that the previously observed impact of nicotine on the mother’s microbiota and lower SCFA levels could, in turn, impact acetylation and DNA methylation in male progeny and thereby affect gene expression. The global reduced histone acetylation could contribute to the decreased expression of the vast majority of differentially expressed genes. The decrease in gene expression essential for injury healing and cell migration could make enterocytes more vulnerable and prone to damage. We propose that reduced gut functionality could, in turn, impact the operation of many other organs and lead to susceptibility to pathogens.

## Epigenetic perturbations of Notch signaling genes upon nicotine exposure
We observed an increase in the clustering of NOTCH1-positive cells in PNE samples. Notch signaling plays an important role in the cellular response to colon injury and the regeneration of crypts. The blocking of Notch signaling using γ-secretase inhibitor dibenzazepine (DBZ) disrupts the intestinal barrier and induces colitis [ ]. We suggest that NOTCH1 mediates its effects here via H3K27Ac. Indeed, it has been shown that Notch1 haploinsufficiency impacted H3K27ac distribution at Notch1-bound enhancers, dysregulating transcription of more than 1000 genes involved in osteogenesis, inflammation, and oxidative stress [ ]. In this study, we observe that the downstream target of NOTCH1, Maml3, which encodes for a component of an activation complex, and Nsctn, which encodes for a component gamma-secretase complex required for the proteolysis of NOTCH1, showed reduced expression in nicotine-exposed colons. The reduced expression was also associated with lower levels of H3K27Ac in treatment compared to control. High enrichment of H3K27Ac at Nsctn and Maml3 promoters confirms that H3K27Ac could be essential for the recruitment of RNA polymerase complex. The reduced histone acetylation and histone methylation at promoters of the Notch signaling genes could be induced by PNE.

## Comparative analysis of our study and data from smokers and non-smokers in Crohn’s disease
To relate our experimental findings in a rat model to human health outcomes, such as gastrointestinal disorders in smokers, we compared our DEGs with human data ([ ] “Differential Impact of Smoking on Methylome and Transcriptome in Crohn’s Disease and Ulcerative Colitis”). The authors there compared the state of colonic tissues from current smokers and non-smokers in Crohn’s Disease, which is a type of inflammatory bowel disease that may affect any segment of the gastrointestinal tract. Notably, 27 genes identified in our study were also detected in smokers with Crohn’s disease, including genes encoding transcription factors (Gata2, Erg4, Tbx19, Taf1d, Mrtfb, Tcf4), and several metabolic genes (D2hgdh, Apold1, Lpcat4, Mgam). Thus, this analysis confirms that maternal exposure to nicotine could be responsible for gastrointestinal disorders of their progeny.

## Limitations of our study
In our study, we focused on males, which makes the study sex biased. We focus on male progeny because maternal smoking had a greater impact on males than females [ ], with more differentially expressed genes in liver tissue and higher liver pathway activation [ ]. Our data are in line with previous observations that male embryos present a greater in-utero vulnerability to environmental toxicants [ – ], e.g., a study suggesting that there is less immune compatibility in maternal–fetal interactions for males, which may trigger a higher immune response in male fetuses, thereby resulting in a pro-inflammatory state [ ].

## Methods

## Animal treatment
Specific pathogen-free male and female Sprague Dawley rats were purchased from Envigo (USA). To reduce the number of animals, the same animals were used for experiments described in this work and for microbiome and blood pressure studies that will be described in a separate study (manuscript in preparation). Specific pathogen-free (SPF) animals are routinely used in such studies to maintain controlled baseline microbiota, reduce confounding variables, and enhance reproducibility as well as improve animal health. Females were purchased at 8 weeks of age and were allowed 1 week to acclimate. At 9 weeks of age, females were randomly assigned to one of 4 treatment groups. Two groups of females were mated, and two groups of females remained virgins. Mating included placing a single female overnight with a male and examination on the following morning for the presence of sperm via vaginal swabs. If sperm was present, the dam was weighed and placed in a sPrenatal nicotine exposure induces epigenetic alterations in the Notch signaling genes in the proximal colon in ratseparate cage, and this period was marked as gestational day (GD) 0. If no sperm was detected, the pair remained together, and the female was examined daily until pregnancy and GD0 were confirmed. Using a sterile technique, a 28-day osmotic mini-pump (2ML4-ALZET, Durect Corp., Cupertino, CA, USA) filled with either sterile saline (control) or a nicotine tartrate (6 mg/kg), dissolved in sterile saline was placed subcutaneously between the scapulae, as described previously [ , ]. Three dams in each group had successfully inserted 2ML4-ALZET pumps. The male offspring from separate dams were used for each assay. On GD19, or 13 days following the implant of the pump, dams gave birth. Male progeny from both control and nicotine-exposed dams was sacrificed at 11 weeks of age. We selected 11-week-old male rats for this study because this age represents early adulthood when the animals have reached physiological maturity but have not yet begun age-related decline. Rats between 8 and 12 weeks old are considered “adults” (e.g. [ ]) and are commonly used in studies of metabolic regulation and neurophysiological behavior, allowing for comparison with existing literature and ensuring the stability of biological systems under investigation. Animals were anesthetized at a surgical plane with isoflurane (2.5% in 100% oxygen) for blood collection. Following this, all animals were given an overdose of sodium pentobarbital (> 200 mg/kg). Several organs, including the part of the colon containing the ascending, and a proximal part of the transverse colon of the males used in this study, were collected and preserved in liquid nitrogen.

## RNA extraction, sequencing, and data processing
Colons stored at −80ºC from the control and treatment offspring groups were used for RNA extraction. Colon samples were lysed and homogenized using TissueLyser (Qiagen). Approximately 30 mg of tissue was used (6 biological replicates) for RNA extraction using RNeasy Plus Mini Kit 250 (Qiagen, 74,134). Purified RNA was eluted in 50 µL of RNase-free water. RNA was treated with DNase I (QIAGEN, 79,254) to remove residual DNA. We used three biological replicates from the control (n = 3) and nicotine (n = 3) groups of rats. One microgram of extracted total RNA was used for a strand-specific library preparation protocol using NEBNext Ultra II Directional RNA Library according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Quality control and genome-wide sequencing were performed at the GenomEast platform at the Institute of Genetic, Molecular, and Cellular Biology (IGBMC), Strasbourg, France. The sequencing was performed as massively parallel sequencing using paired-end mode, with the size of the sequencing tag 100 bp. Reads in FASTQ format were processed for quality control using the FastQC tool (http://www.bioinformatics.babraham.ac.uk/projects/fastqc/). An average of 57 million sequencing reads per sample were processed; the number of reads for each sample is provided in Table S7. The reads were mapped to the reference genome [Rattus Norvegicus rn7 sequence] using the HISAT2 [ ] alignment program with default parameters, and the alignment files were generated as BAM files. These files were used as the input for FeatureCounts to calculate the gene abundance using the gene annotation file ncbiRefSeq.gtf.gz from UCSC. Differential gene expression was assessed using the package DESeq2 (2.11.40.7) with the option of filtering genes with low counts. Genes were considered differentially expressed if FDR < 0.1, FC > 1.5. Functional annotation of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) was performed with the DAVID [ ] using “Biological process”, “Cellular component”, or “Molecular function” ontologies, and the Enrichr [ ] program. All analyses were performed using the Galaxy platform [ ].

## DNA extraction and telomere length analysis
DNA was extracted using the DNA Easy Blood and Tissue Kit (Qiagen, 69,506) according to instructions provided by the manufacturer. DNA concentrations were measured using a fluorescent reader (Promega). We extracted and analyzed the telomere length (TL) in the entire colon tissue. To study the relative telomere length, quantitative PCR (qPCR) was used according to the protocol [ ]. In each sample, the ratio of the copy number of telomeric repeats to the copy number of a single gene (T/S) of the Rplp0 was determined. This ratio is proportional to the average telomere length.

## MeDIP, library preparation, and MeDIP-seq analysis
The technology is based on the enrichment of methylated DNA fragments that are bound to the methyl-CpG binding domain of the human MBD2 protein. MBD2 protein is fused to the Fc tail of human IgG1 and coupled to protein A beads. 10 µg of DNA was used for MeDIP with methylated DNA Enrichment Kit (NEB, #E2600S). DNA was Diluted and sonicated in a Qsonica 700 sonicator (Q700-110, Newtown, Connecticut, USA) supplied with cup horn 431C2 using the following conditions: 20 s pulse on, 20 s pulse off, total sonication time 8 min; these parameters generated ~ 300 bp DNA fragments. Methylated DNA was then enriched with incubation—MBD2—protein A- magnetic beads. Beads were washed, methylated DNA was eluted, and the concentration of methylated DNA was determined by fluorescence. Equal amounts of methylated DNA and input (7 ng) were used for library preparation. Sequencing libraries were prepared with the NEBNext Ultra DNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina (E7645S; NEB) using dual-indexed primers. Sequencing was performed on an Illumina NextSeq 2000 sequencer using a paired-end 50-base read in multiplexed mode. An average of 43 million sequencing reads per sample were processed; the number of reads for each sample is provided in Table S8. The reads were mapped to the reference genome rn7 v2.5.0 using BWA. From the aligned reads, DNA methylated peaks were identified using 4 biological replicates and the corresponding input by the MACS2 (2.2.7.1) [ ]; the following parameters were applied: a shift-size window of 73 bp, nomodel, and a q-value threshold < 0.05. To compare the nicotine-exposed and control samples, differential peaks were identified by counting reads at each peak using bedtools MultiCovBed (Version 2.30.0) [ ]. Statistical significance was calculated using DeSeq2 (v2.11.40.8) [ ], filtering peaks with low counts. For finding DMRs, we used 2 replicates of control and 2 from the nicotine group. We performed functional annotation of the differential peaks using the web-based tool ChIPseeker v1.28.3 [ ] using default parameters. The numbers of mapped reads were normalized by a scale factor to adjust the total number of reads using the Samtools program. For the visualization of ChIP-seq tracks, each.bam file was converted to BedGraph tracks by using Genome Coverage 2.30.0. Integrative Genomics Viewer (IGV) [ ] was used to visualize the tracks. Functional annotation of genes localized in differentially methylated regions was performed with the DAVID program using “Biological process”, “Molecular function”, and “Cellular Component” terms [ ]. We used SRplot [ ] for the building heatmaps, bubble plots, volcano plots, and Venn diagrams.

## Immunostaining
Fresh colon samples were placed in the OCT compound before cryostat sectioning and kept at −80°C until use. Samples were cut with a microtome set at 7µm and mounted on Matsunami TOMO® hydrophilic adhesion slides. The slides were left to dry at room temperature for about 30 min. Once dried, slides were fixed in 4% buffered paraformaldehyde for 8 min. Slides were quenched with 0.1M Glycine in PBS and washed twice with PBS to remove residual glycine. A blocking solution (4% BSA in PBS) was added for 30 min at RT. Slides were then rinsed with PBS three times and stained with rabbit 1:500 anti-H3K9Ac (07–352, Millipore), or 1:500 rabbit anti-H4penta (06–956, Milipore0 and anti-mouse 1:500 anti BRD4 (sc-518021, Santa Cruz), or 1:200 anti-rabbit Ki-67 (MA5-14,520, Invitrogen), or 1:200 anti-mouse NOTCH1 (sc-376403, Santa Cruz) and 1:500 anti-rabbit H3K27Ac (ab4729, Abcam) or 1:200 mouse anti-TERT (sc-393013, Santa Cruz) and 1:200 rabbit anti-Ki-67 (MA5-14,520, Invitrogen), or 1:500 rabbit anti-H3K4me3 (07–473, Millipore). The primary antibodies were Diluted in DAKO. Slides were incubated with antibodies overnight at 4ºC. After rinsing, secondary antibodies Diluted in DAKO at a Dilution of 1:500 (Invitrogen AlexaFluor®) were added and incubated at RT for 1 h. After three washes, Vectorshield solution containing DAPI was added to each slide. The cell count was performed using ImageJ. The ratio of Ki67-positive cells divided by the total cell number in crypts was determined. For TERT analysis, we counted TERT- positive cells that co-localize with Ki-67-positive cells and data were divided by area and presented as averaged values compared to control. For H3K9Ac and H4Ac, we used lasso tools for analysis and measured fluorescence in this area. The signal was also measured in the area without a signal to estimate the background. We follow the tutorial (https://theolb.readthedocs.io/en/latest/imaging/measuring-cell-fluorescence-using-imagej.html). In this analysis, we measure the Corrected Total Cell Fluorescence (CTCF) which is an area of selected cells multiplied by the mean fluorescence of background readings [ ]. For NOTCH1 analysis, the size of the clusters was determined by Fiji and compared between control and nicotine samples.

## Hematoxylin–eosin staining
The samples for this procedure were prepared and analyzed in the same way as described for immunostaining. Briefly, for morphology analysis, colon sections from control and nicotine-treated groups were fixed in 4% (w/v) PFA solution for 8 min and washed. Sections were stained with Hematoxylin and Eosin (H&E) according to standard protocols. Images were taken with NanoZoomer, and quantitative analysis was performed using ImageJ. In images encompassing the entire crypt, we measured the thickness from the bottom of the crypts to the top of the cell layers. We measured the length of the crypts in a minimum of 15 crypts. Statistical significance was assessed with a nonparametric Mann–Whitney test.

## Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) and ChIP-qPCR
ChIP from cells was performed as previously described [ ] with small modifications. We performed ChIP-qPCR experiments using 6 replicates for control or nicotine-exposed samples. Approximately 30 mg of tissue was cut in 1 ml of PBS, fixed in 1% paraformaldehyde for 10 min at room temperature, quenched with 0.125 M Glycine, and pelleted. Then, the samples were filtered in a cell strainer and the resulting solution was pelleted and resuspended in the following buffer: 0.25% (vol/vol) Triton X-100, 10 mM EDTA, 0.5 mM EGTA, 10 mM Tris pH8. Samples were centrifuged at 1100 rpm for 5 min, 4 °C and the pellet, containing cells, were resuspended in 300 μl of SDS lysis buffer (1% (wt/vol) SDS, 10 mM EDTA, and 50 mM TrisCl pH8) adding a protease inhibitor. Chromatin was sonicated in SDS lysis buffer at 60% amplitude for 8 min (20 s on, 20 s off, Qsonica 700 sonicator supplied with cup horn 431C2); these parameters allow to obtain ~ 300 bp fragments. After sonication, samples were centrifuged at 12,800 rpm for 10 min, at 4 °C, and the supernatant containing sonicated chromatin was transferred and Diluted using 1,7 ml of the following buffer: (0.01% (1.1% (vol/vol) Triton X-100, 1.2 mM EDTA, 16.7 mM TrisHCl, 167 mM NaCl). A solution containing 10 μl of Dynabeads (10002D, Invitrogen) and one microliter H3K27Ac antibody (ab4729, Abcam) or 0.5 ul of H3K4me3 (07–473, Mlipore) was added to the sample tubes and incubated overnight at 4°C. Before adding the antibody and Dynabeads, 10 μl of each sample was collected as “Input samples” (starting material). After overnight incubation with Dynabeads and the antibody of interest, the beads were washed 5 min each, with the following four buffers: (1) Low salt buffer: 0.1% (wt/vol) SDS, 1% (vol/vol) Triton X-100, 2 mM EDTA, 20 mM TrisHCl, 150 mM NaCl; (2) High salt buffer: 0.1% (wt/vol) SDS, 1% (vol/vol) Triton X-100, 2 mM EDTA, 20 mM TrisCl pH8, 500 mM NaCl; (3) LiCl buffer: 0.25 M LiCl, 1% (vol/vol) Igepal, 1 mM EDTA, 10 mM TrisCl, pH8, 1% (wt/vol) deoxycholic acid; (4) TE buffer (two washes). After the washing steps, the beads were treated two times with a 50 μl solution containing 1% (wt/vol) SDS, 0.1 M NaHCO3 pH9, and incubated at 65 °C for 15 min to elute the precipitated chromatin from the beads. Subsequently, the eluted chromatin was reverse cross-linked by adding 9 μl of 5 N NaCl and incubating at 65 °C for 4 h. Then, proteins were removed by adding proteinase K and incubating the samples for 1 h at 45°C. The precipitated DNA was purified with MinElute Reaction Clean-Up kit (28,004, Qiagen), and the DNA concentration was measured using the QuantiFluor dsDNA system (E2670, Promega). A minimum ~ 2 ng of DNA was obtained. Input and DNA were diluted to a similar concentration and were used for qPCR with primers indicated in Table S9.

## Statistical analyses
We used the minimum number of animals required to obtain a statistically significant set of data according to the requirements of the University of Florida IACUC. The number of animals used is specified for each experimental procedure. A nonparametric Wilcoxon-Mann–Whitney test was used for qPCR experiments, immunofluorescence, and crypt length quantifications.
