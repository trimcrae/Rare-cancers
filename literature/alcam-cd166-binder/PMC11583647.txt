## Abstract

## Background
Gastric cancer stem cells (GCSCs) are key contributors to tumorigenesis, recurrence and metastasis, complicating gastric cancer (GC) treatment. Rhaponticin (RA), a potential novel anticancer drug, has unexplored effects on GCSCs.

## Methods
GCSCs were isolated using CD133 and CD166 markers with magnetic bead separation method and then evaluated their response to the IC50 concentrations of RA (16.90 µg/mL for BGC-823 and 22.18 µg/mL for SGC-7901), and effects on cell proliferation, migration, invasion, and stemness were measured. We analyzed the GCSC-related microarray dataset GSE111556 and explored RA’s role in restoring programmed cell death ligand 1 (PD-L1) function in CD133+/CD166 + cells post-PD-L1 knockdown. RA’s impact on tumour growth and immune microenvironment was assessed in a xenograft mouse model.

## Results
The CD133+/CD166 + subpopulation exhibited stem-like characteristics, with the highest proportion in BGC-823 (38.85%) and SGC-7901 (43.81%) cells. These cells formed tumour spheres and had increased expression of stemness markers Sox2 and Oct-4 (compared to the parental cell line, P < 0.001). RA treatment showed no toxicity to normal GES-1 cells but reduced the viability of CD133+/CD166 + cells in a dose-dependent manner, with IC50 values of 16.90 µg/ml for BGC-823 and 22.18 µg/ml for SGC-7901. RA also decreased the proportion of CD133+/CD166 + cells and their stem-like properties (P < 0.001). Analysis of the GEO database identified PD-L1 as a key target gene of RA, with high expression in GC tissues. Knocking down PD-L1 in CD133+/CD166 + cells and introducing RA did not significantly change PD-L1 expression (P>0.05), suggesting RA’s effect may be PD-L1 dependent. In a xenograft mouse model, the tumour size in the RA treatment group was approximately one-sixth that of the CD133+/CD166 + group (P < 0.001). Post-RA treatment, there was an elevation in the expression levels of CD4 and CD8, alongside a reduction in PD-L1 expression (P < 0.001).

## Conclusions
RA suppresses GCSC stem - like phenotype by inhibiting PD - L1 and enhancing T cell tumour infiltration in the studied models. These findings suggest that RA may have potential for further exploration as a candidate for GC treatment, but extensive preclinical and clinical studies are required to determine its true therapeutic value.

## Introduction
Gastric cancer (GC) is the fifth most common cancer and third leading cause of cancer-related death worldwide. It is an important global health problem, causing nearly 800 000 deaths annually [ ] and is approximately twice as common in men as in women, and most cases occur after 60 years of age [ ]. The incidence of GC varies widely among geographic regions, and East Asia and some countries in Eastern Europe and South America have the highest rates, whereas North America and Africa have the lowest [ ]. Globally, more than 70% of GC cases occur in developing countries, and a large proportion of them are in China [ ]. The clinical stage of GC at the initial diagnosis directly determines the prognosis of a patient. Unfortunately, owing to the occurrence of the obscure early symptoms of GC, more than half of patients with GC are accompanied by metastasis at the time of definitive diagnosis. Patients with early GC usually have a high five-year overall survival rate (> 60%), whereas the five-year survival rate of patients who develop local and distant metastases drops dramatically to 30% and 5%, respectively [ ]. For patients with early GC, surgical resection is the best treatment option, whereas chemotherapy is the most important treatment for those who cannot undergo surgical resection or who have advanced metastasis [ ]. However, the mode combining neoadjuvant therapy, surgery and postoperative chemotherapy often loses its effect in the late stage of the disease, and patients often fail completely because of metastasis, recurrence and drug resistance. Therefore, finding key molecular targets that affect the progression of GC, fully studying their mechanism of action and developing effective molecular targeted drugs are the most important means to improve the current treatment dilemma of GC.
Cancer stem cells (CSCs) rarely appear in tumour tissues and are capable of self-renewal, proliferation, differentiation, plastic adaptation and immune regulation, mediating tumorigenesis, metastasis and treatment resistance [ ]. CSCs are mostly found in solid tumours, including GC. Similar to normal stem cells, gastric cancer stem cells (GCSCs) can self-renew and differentiate into heterogeneous tumour cells, causing rapid tumour growth [ ]. In recent years, GCSCs have attracted widespread attention in the field of GC therapy. Experimental studies targeting GCSC markers have been carried out internationally, and a portion of candidate tumour stem cell surface markers can serve as novel targets for GC therapy. However, the biological properties of highly specific markers for GCSCs are still unclear; this gap is an important topic in research on GC therapy using tumour stem cells [ ]. Although CSCs only occupy a small proportion in malignant tumour tissues, they have become the main driving factor for tumour proliferation, development and transformation because of their self-renewal ability, unlimited proliferation, multidirectional differentiation, ability to promote metastasis and resistance to radiotherapy and chemotherapy and are the root cause of tumour recurrence and transformation. Therefore, the in-depth investigation of the regulatory mechanism of stem cells may help to improve the prognoses of patients with GC.
Rhaponticin (RA) is a stilbene derivative widely found in Polygonum spp. RA is pharmacologically active, binds to human serum albumin and is transported to a target site to exert its effects. RA is a promising lead compound for novel drug development. To date, it has anti-tumour activity against a variety of cancer cells, such as hepatocellular carcinoma, breast cancer, lung adenocarcinoma, human oral epidermoid carcinoma and human prostate cancer, and its main mechanism of action includes the inhibition of tumour cell proliferation, induction of apoptosis, inhibition of metastasis and angiogenesis and inhibition of fatty acid synthetase activity and expression in tumour cells and other pathways. Kim et al. [ ] found that RA regulates malignant tumour cells by inhibiting the spread of primary tumours into distant organs. Mickymaray et al. [ ] proposed that RA is highly toxic to osteosarcoma cells, can considerably inhibit the growth of osteosarcoma cells and cause changes in cell morphology. Wang et al. [ ] demonstrated that RA treatment improved chemotaxis and histopathological changes in lung tissues, including a decrease in tumour markers (AHH, CEA and LDH) and pro-inflammatory cytokines. However, pharmacological effects of RA on GC cells or GC stem cells have not been elucidated.
In this study, we first obtained GC stem–like cells using two tumour markers, CD133 and CD166 and then analysed their phenotypic characteristics after RA treatment. Then, the differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified by mining the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database, and the effect and mechanism of RA on the characteristic phenotype of GC stem–like cells were elucidated around the target genes. This study aims to explore the effect and mechanism of traditional Chinese medicine RA on the characteristic phenotype of GC stem–like cells, in order to fill the gap in the theory and treatment research of GC stem–like cells, lay the experimental foundation for exploring the new direction and strategy of RA anti-tumour and provide the basis for the development of clinical effective anti-tumour traditional Chinese medicine preparations.

## Materials and methods

## Cell culture
The human GC cell lines SGC-7901 and BGC-823 were purchased from the American Type Culture Collection (Manassas, VA, USA), and were identified by Short Tandem Repeat (STR). The cells were cultured in an RPMI-1640 medium containing 10% FBS (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Gibco, Waltham, MA, USA) in a humidified 5% CO2 environment, and the temperature was set at 37 °C.

## Magnetic-activated cell sorting (MACS)
After the digestion and resuspension of cells in the logarithmic growth phase, the cell concentration was adjusted to 2 × 107 cells/ml. Then, 60 µl of cell suspension was mixed with 20 µl of CD133 MicroBeads and 20 µl of FcR blocking reagent, incubated for 20 min at room temperature and protected from light. The cells were washed with 2 ml of buffer and then centrifuged at 300 ×g for 8 min, and magnetic bead sorting was conducted.
Detailed description of the microbead separation process: The filter column was first moistened and washed according to the instructions. The cell suspension obtained after incubating the CD133 magnetic beads was added to the filter column. This step is crucial for ensuring that the column is properly prepared to separate the cells based on their affinity to the magnetic beads. The moistening helps to prevent the beads from sticking to the column walls, while the washing ensures that any residual contaminants are removed, providing a clean surface for cell binding.
Then, 4 ml of buffer was added to the column, which helps to flush through the column and carry the unbound cells (negative population) away, while the CD133 + cells, which have bound to the magnetic beads, are retained within the column. This separation is based on the principle that the magnetic beads will be attracted to the magnetic field of the column, and any cells bound to these beads will be held in place.
The effluent cell suspension, which is the negative (CD133−) cell population, is collected, and the positive (CD133+) cell population is adsorbed within the filter column. To release the adsorbed cells, the filter column is removed from the magnet, and 1 ml of new buffer is added. This buffer is used to elute the CD133 + cells from the column by disrupting the magnetic field’s hold on the beads.
The CD133 + cell population is harvested by meticulously transferring the remaining cell suspension from the filter column to a fresh collection tube, ensuring that no cells are lost in the process. It is crucial to properly label the collection tube to prevent any confusion or misidentification of the cell sample.
Next, 107 orders of magnitude of CD133 + or CD133 − cells were collected, and the above operation was repeated to ensure a sufficient number of cells for further analysis. The cells were resuspended with 100 µl of buffer, and 10 µl of CD166-PE antibody and 20 µl of FcR blocking reagent were added and mixed and incubated for 10 min at 4 °C away from light. This incubation allows the antibody to bind specifically to the CD166 antigen on the cell surface, which can then be detected using a fluorescence-activated cell sorter (FACS).
Paint cells were washed by adding 1–2 ml of buffer and centrifuged at 300 ×g for 7 min. The cell precipitate was then resuspended in 80 µl of buffer, and 20 µl of anti-PE magnetic beads was added. The mixture was incubated at 4 °C for 15 min. This step allows the magnetic beads to bind to the PE-tagged antibody, which is attached to the cell surface, enabling further magnetic separation based on the presence of the CD166 antigen.
After further rinsing and sorting, CD133+/CD166+, CD133+/CD166−, CD133-/CD166+, and CD133−/CD166 − cells were obtained. These subpopulations were isolated based on their expression of CD133 and CD166, which can provide valuable insights into the heterogeneity of the cell population.
Finally, the samples of these four cell subpopulations were collected and tested for magnetic bead separation efficiency by flow cytometry (Becton Dickinson and Company). Flow cytometry is a powerful tool that allows for the rapid analysis of the separated cells, confirming the efficiency of the magnetic bead separation and providing a quantitative measure of the purity of each cell subpopulation.

## Cell transfection
The sh-PD-L1 and sh-NC for silencing PD-L1 were synthesised and provided by Genepharma (Shanghai, China). For stable transfection, the cells were transfected with Lipofectamine 3000 reagent (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

## Stem cell spheroid experiment
The sorted CD133+/CD166+, CD133+/CD166−, CD133-/CD166 + and CD133−/CD166 − cells and parental cells were collected by centrifugation and resuspended for counting, and the cells were resuspended in a serum-free suspension medium prepared, adjusted to a cell concentration of 2 × 104/ml in ultra-low-adhesion six-well plates and spread at 2 ml per well. The cells were collected every two days, and the liquid was separated through centrifugation and discarded. The growth of tumour ball formation was observed and photographed with a light microscope. After seven days of tumour ball formation, the diameter of the tumour ball exceeded 50 μm, the number of tumour balls in each group was counted and the difference was statistically calculated. Subsequently, the tumours spheres were collected and dissociated into single-cell suspensions with a StemPro Accutase cell dissociation solution for the next experiments.

## CCK-8 assay
Changes in the proliferative activity of cells in each group were confirmed by CCK-8 assay. The cells with different treatments were seeded into 96-well plates at a density of 3 × 103 per well, and 10 µl of CCK-8 reagent was added after the medium was replenished to 100 µl at 0, 24, 48 and 72 h. The absorbance (A) value at 450 nm was detected by an enzyme marker after 4 h, and a low OD450 nm indicated low cell viability.

## Clone formation assay
Different treated cells were inoculated in six-well plates at 1000 cells per well for 24 h. The culture was continued for 10 days with fluid changes every three days. The cells were stained with 0.5% crystal violet for 20 min, and the cell colonies were photographed and counted.

## Transwell assay
Cell suspension (100 µl) was collected from each group and added to a Transwell chamber. Transwell chambers lined with substrate glue and were used to detect cell invasion and the Transwell chambers without substrate glue were used to detect cell migration). Meanwhile, 600 µl of medium containing 10% FBS was added to the Transwell bottom chamber. After the cells were cultured for 24 h, the cells on the top surface layer of the filter membrane of each Transwell chamber were wiped off, and then the cells that penetrated the bottom surface layer of the filter membrane were stained with 0.2% crystal violet. Finally, the cells were observed using a microscope and photographed.

## Bioinformatics analysis
Microarray data are available with accession number GSE111556 from the GEO database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/). The GSE111556 dataset was based on the GPL16791 platform (Illumina HiSeq 2500 [Homo sapiens]), which included three sample sets of GC-derived stem cells and three sample sets of normal stem cells. After the downloaded data were normalised, the gene expression of different subgroups in each dataset was analysed separately with limma package (|log fold change| ≥0.5 and P < 0.05 indicated a DEG).

## Western blotting
Total cellular proteins were extracted with RIPA lysis buffer, and protein concentration was quantified by the BCA method. The aliquots of protein samples were separated on 8–12% SDS-PAGE and then transferred to nitrocellulose membranes. After being closed in 5% skimmed milk, the membrane was incubated with primary antibodies (Sox2, Oct-4, PD-L1) overnight at 4 °C. On the following day, the membrane was washed with TBST and then incubated with HRP–goat anti-rabbit IgG at room temperature for 1 h. After washing again, the bands were visualised with ECL chemiluminescent reagent and photographed with a gel imaging system. Finally, the relative protein expression was analysed by ImageJ software. GAPDH was used as the total protein and cytoplasmic protein internal reference. Protein relative expression = grey value of the target band/grey value of the corresponding internal reference band × 100%.

## In vivo experiments
Animal experiments were approved and supervised by the animal ethics committee of Shaanxi Provincial People’s Hospital. Five-week-old male C57BL/6 mice were divided into BGC823 (CD133+/CD166+) and RA groups with six mice each by using the random number table method. BGC823 (CD133+/CD166+) cells were digested, centrifuged and prepared as single-cell suspensions in 50 µl of DMEM culture medium, and then 1 × 105/cells of GC cells were inoculated into the subcutis of each mouse. The GC C57BL/6 mice subcutaneous transplantation tumour model were successfully constructed when the subcutaneous nodules grew to the size of rice grains (which took about 1 week). After 1 week, 200 mg/Kg of RA was administered by intraperitoneal injection. Specific euthanasia method: To reduce stress and suffering in mice, all experimental personnel are trained and assessed. During the operation, the C57BL/6 mouse is placed on a wire mesh, and the experimenter firmly holds the mouse’s tail with one hand, while the other hand uses tweezers to press on the mouse’s neck. With a forceful pull, the cervical vertebrae are dislocated, separating the spinal cord from the brain stem. This method quickly leads to the death of the mouse and loss of consciousness. This study adheres to relevant ethical and operational standards to ensure that animals are properly cared for and protected during the experimental process. Tumour formation was observed every 6 day for 6 weeks after the mice were killed by neck breaking. Tumour tissues were fixed in 4% formalin, embedded in paraffin and examined by immunohistochemistry.

## Immunohistochemistry
Tumour tissues were isolated and dissected into small pieces, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and embedded in paraffin sections. After deparaffinisation and antigen repair, tissue Sect. (4 μm thick) were closed in a containment solution (1% goat serum containing 0.3% Triton100, Sigma, USA) for 10 min and then incubated with rabbit anti-CD4, CD8 and PD-L1 (Abcam, Cambridge, UK) in a refrigerator at 4 °C overnight. After three washes with PBS (5 min each), sheep anti-rabbit IgG antibody-HRP multimer was added dropwise. and the sections were incubated in a wet box at 37 °C for 30 min. HRP-IgG secondary antibody was incubated at 37 °C for 30 min and then washed with PBS before the the DAB colouring reagent was added. A negative control was prepared by replacing the primary antibody with PBS. A positive cell showed tan or clay colour in the cytoplasm, nucleus or cell membrane. Five fields of view were randomly selected at high magnification (×400), 200 cells were counted in each field of view and the mean percentage was calculated.

## Statistical analysis
ImageJ, Excel and FlowJo software were used for statistical analysis, and Graphpadprism8 was used for graphing, which was repeated more than three times. Differences between two groups were assessed using independent samples t-test. Comparisons among multiple groups were performed using one-way ANOVA, and two-by-two comparisons were performed using the LSD test. A P of < 0.05 indicated that the difference in the results was statistically significant.

## Results

## CD133+/CD166 + human GC cell subpopulation has the phenotypic characteristics of tumour stem–like cells
The study initially utilized flow cytometry to assess the proportions of CD133−/CD166−, CD133−/CD166+, CD133+/CD166−, and CD133+/CD166 + cells in BGC-823 and SGC-7901 cells. Findings revealed that the CD133+/CD166 + double-positive cells were the most prevalent, constituting 38.85% and 43.81% in BGC-823 and SGC-7901 respectively. Other cell populations included CD133+/CD166- cells at 5.83% and 4.37%, CD133-/CD166 + cells at 8.71% and 1.87%, and CD133-/CD166- cells at 46.61% and 49.95% (Fig.  A). To isolate GC stem–like cells from GC cells, we sort out CD133+/CD166 + GC cell subsets from GC cells with immunomagnetic beads. The sorting effect was verified by flow cytometry. The results showed that CD133−/CD166−, CD133−/CD166+, CD133+/CD166 − and CD133+/CD166 + GC cell subsets were effectively obtained by immunomagnetic cell sorting (Fig.  B). Subsequently, the GC cell subsets of each group were cultured in a serum-free medium, and whether CD133+/CD166 + cells formed tumour spheres were investigated, that is, whether they possessed the sphere-forming property of a stem cell phenotype. The results showed that the CD133+/CD166 + cells formed tumour spheres, indicating that the CD133+/CD166 + cells had the characteristics of sphere formation of GC stem–like cells (Fig.  C). We further evaluated the proliferation, migration and invasion abilities of each cell subset. CCK-8 and colony formation assays showed that the CD133+/CD166 + cells had the strongest proliferation ability among the parental cells. The proliferation ability of the CD133+/CD166 − cells was higher than that of the CD133−/CD166 + cells and parental cells (Fig.  D and E), suggesting that CD133 plays a predominant role in the stemness of GC cells. Similarly, CD133+/CD166 + cell subsets manifested stronger invasion ability than the other groups, especially the parental cells and CD133-/CD166- cell subsets (Fig.  F). Additionally, stem cell marker proteins, including Sox2 and Oct-4, were further examined in the cell subsets of each group. The expression levels of Sox2 and Oct-4 protein in the CD133+/CD166 + cells were conspicuously higher than those in the other three groups. Specifically, Sox2 protein expression was increased by 5.19-fold compared to the BGC-823 parental strain and by 4.19-fold compared to the SGC-7901 parental strain. Oct-4 protein expression was increased by 4.63-fold compared to the BGC-823 parental strain and by 3.66-fold compared to the SGC-7901 parental strain (Fig.  G). These findings underscore that CD133+/CD166 + cells had the characteristics of GC stem–like cells.
 

## RA can suppress the stem-like cell phenotype of CD133+/CD166 + human GC cell subpopulation
The human gastric epithelial cell line GES-1 was treated with RA (0, 5, 10, 50 and 100 µM) for 48 h and found that RA had no significant effect on the activity of GES-1 cells, indicating that RA had no obvious toxic side effects on normal cells (Fig.  A). We then treated the CD133+/CD166 + subsets of BGC-823 and SGC-7901 cells with RA at a series of concentrations (0.25, 0,5, 1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32, 64 and 128 µM) and found that RA reduced the viability of CD133+/CD166 + cells in a dose-dependent manner (Fig.  B). The IC50 of RA against CD133+/CD166+ (BGC-823) was 16.90 µg/ml and that against CD133+/CD166+ (SGC-7901) was 22.18 µg/ml. Subsequently, treatment of the parental BGC-823 and SGC-7901 cells with RA at their respective IC50 concentrations led to a reduction in the proportion of CD133+/CD166 + cells from 39.30 to 23.97% and from 43.51 to 18.24%, as shown by flow cytometry (Fig.  C). Furthermore, the effects of RA on the proliferation, migration, invasion, and stem-like properties of CD133+/CD166 + cells were assessed. RA treatment reduced the proliferative capacity of these cells, as evidenced by a decrease in clone numbers from 465.33 to 176.33 in BGC-823 cells and from 582.33 to 205.66 in SGC-7901 cells (Fig.  F and G). It also decreased the number of spheres formed (Fig.  E), the number of invasive cells (with BGC-823 cells dropping from 365.66 to 127.33 and SGC-7901 cells from 420.33 to 164.66, Fig.  H-I), and the expression levels of stemness markers Sox2 and Oct-4 (with BGC-823 cells showing a decrease to 0.16 and 0.25, respectively, and SGC-7901 cells to 0.31 and 0.23, respectively, Fig.  J). Collectively, these results suggest that RA can suppress the malignant biological characteristics of GC stem-like cells.
 

## GEO database analysis shows that PD-L1 is the key target gene of RA
We obtained the GC stem cell–related microarray dataset GSE111556 from the GEO database. The dataset contains three groups of GC-derived stem cells and three groups of normal stem cells. The two groups were separated (Fig.  A). A total of 1324 upregulated DEGs and 1257 downregulated DEGs were identified in GC-derived stem cells (Fig.  B). The top 20 DEGs with the most significant differences were used for further verification, including MEST (log2FoldChange= -9.0967883, p = 3.73E-250), CAMK2N1 (log2FoldChange = 2.9777235, p = 2.61E-160), CPA4 (log2FoldChange= -7.0922825, p = 3.83E-156), ABCC3 (log2FoldChange = 6.5378527, p = 4.33E-141), LAMC2 (log2FoldChange= -6.3767659, p = 7.15E-130), FENDRR (log2FoldChange = 4.6414672, p = 2.66E-124), TRPA1 (log2FoldChange = 7.0890911, p = 8.26E-122), PD-L1 (log2FoldChange = 5.2983093, p = 1.39E-121), TNFRSF11B (log2FoldChange = 3.9276152, p = 4.47E-118), CFLAR (log2FoldChange = 2.5556836, p = 1.77E-116), SOCS2 (log2FoldChange = 4.1044625, p = 3.06E-115), MGAT5 (log2FoldChange = 2.1085002, p = 4.48E-114), LAMA2 (log2FoldChange = 2.8884689, p = 2.52E-113), FOXF1 (log2FoldChange = 4.3671554, p = 1.16E-111), PTGFRN (log2FoldChange= -5.5812276, p = 1.86E-107), HSPA2 (log2FoldChange = 5.0760926, p = 2.78E-106), TENM3 (log2FoldChange= -3.0185678, p = 2.97E-101), MAP3K5 (log2FoldChange = 2.4095307, p = 7.21E-97), FGF7 (log2FoldChange = 5.1116897, p = 4.34E-94) and CD9 (log2FoldChange = 3.4118427, p = 6.85E-93). RT-qPCR results showed that compared with normal human MSCs, ABCC3, FENDRR, TRPA1, PD-L1, SOCS2, MGAT5, LAMA2, FOXF1, MAP3K5, FGF7 and CD9 were upregulated in the CD133+/CD166+ (BGC-823) cells. However, CPA4, LAMC2, PTGFRN and TENM3 were downregulated in the CD133+/CD166+ (BGC-823) cells. After the introduction of RA, the expression levels of ABCC3, PDL1, MGAT5, LAMA2 and FGF7 were reversed to decrease, and the expression level of LAMC2 was reversed to enhance. The most significant difference in PD-L1 expression was observed (Fig.  C). Subsequently, we examined changes in the expression of PD-L1 in the CD133+/CD166+ (BGC-823) cells regulated by RA through Western blot analysis. The results showed that the protein expression of PDL1 was upregulated fivefold in the CD133+/CD166+ (BGC-823) cells compared with human MSCs, and the protein level of PD-L1 was considerably reduced after RA treatment (Fig.  D). The PD-L1 protein levels were upregulated in the BGC-823 and SGC-7901 parental cell lines compared with GSE-1, whereas the expression was further increased in the CD133+/CD166+ (BGC-823) and CD133+/CD166+ (SGC-7901) cells (Fig.  E), indicating that PD-L1 was preferentially expressed in the GC-derived stem cells. Interestingly, our results obtained in the GEPIA database showed that PD-L1 was highly expressed in the GC tissues (Fig.  F).
 

## RA prevents the stemness and aggressive phenotype of human CD133+/CD166 + GC cell subsets by inhibiting PD-L1
We knocked down PD-L1 in the CD133+/CD166+ (BGC-823) cells and introduced RA. Western blot analysis revealed that PD-L1 expression was downregulated to 0.15 upon transfection with sh-PD-L1, and further treatment with RA resulted in PD-L1 expression levels of 0.17, indicating no significant change (Fig.  A). Next, we utilized flow cytometry to assess the impact of PD-L1 knockdown on the proportions of CD133−/CD166−, CD133−/CD166+, CD133+/CD166−, and CD133+/CD166 + cells in the BGC-823 and SGC-7901 parental cell lines. The results showed that the percentage of CD133+/CD166 + cells in both BGC-823 and SGC-7901 cells was decreased from 39.88% and 45.69–16.22% and 26.64%, respectively, following PD-L1 gene knockout (Fig.  B). Functional assays post-PD-L1 knockout demonstrated a significant reduction in the proliferation rate (Fig.  C), number of spheres formed (Fig.  D), colony formation (Fig.  E, from 488.33 to 169.66), and cells traversing the polycarbonate membrane (Fig.  F, from 342.33 to 163.66) in CD133+/CD166 + BGC-823 cells. Additionally, the expression levels of stemness markers Sox2 and Oct-4 were markedly decreased (to 0.37 and 0.44, respectively, Fig.  G). Interestingly, the introduction of RA after PD-L1 knockout did not result in any significant changes in these cellular functions, with clone numbers at 152.33, invasion numbers at 149.33, and Sox2 and Oct-4 expression levels at 0.30 and 0.46, respectively (Fig.  C-G). This suggests that RA may not have a target in the absence of PD-L1, rendering it ineffective following PD-L1 depletion.
 

## RA regulates the tumour microenvironment in CD133+/CD166 + xenograft mice by suppressing PD-L1 expression
PD-L1 is abnormally highly expressed in many tumour cells and can synergise with other immunosuppressive signals, contributing to the formation of an inhibitory immune microenvironment. Accordingly, we constructed a CD133+/CD166+-transplanted tumour mouse model to further evaluate the effects of RA treatment on tumour growth and immune microenvironment formation in mice. Tumour tissues from each group were removed after 42 days of modelling and a considerable reduction in graft tumour size was observed in the RA treatment group compared with the CD133+/CD166 + group. The tumour volume growth curve of the RA treatment group was gradually pulled away from that of the CD133+/CD166 + group after 24 days, andthe tumour size of the RA treatment group was approximately one-sixth of that of the CD133+/CD166 + group by 42 days, and the difference between the two groups was statistically significant (P < 0.001) (Fig.  A–C).
 Next, we applied immunohistochemistry to measure the expression levels of CD4, CD8 and PD-L1 in each group of the transplanted tumours. The expression levels of CD4 and CD8 increased, and the expression of PD-L1 decreased after RA treatment (Fig.  D). The CD4 + T cell expression level in the RA treatment group was significantly different from that in the CD133+/CD166 + group (P < 0.001). The difference in CD8 + T cell expression between the two groups was also statistically significant (P < 0.001). The difference in PD - L1 expression level between the two groups was extremely significant (P < 0.001). This suggests that RA facilitated the infiltration of the tumour cells by the CD4 + T and CD8 + T cells. This effect may have been achieved through the inhibition of PD-L1. While these results in the mouse model are promising, it should be noted that there are significant physiological, pathological, and immunological differences between mice and humans. Therefore, the translation of these findings to human gastric cancer treatment requires further investigation and validation.

## Discussion
CSCs are considered causes of cancer, gradually forming tumours through self-renewal and differentiation and then hiding in the tumour cell population. CSCs prevent the destruction of tumours by radiotherapy and chemotherapy and promote tumour recurrence after these treatments [ ]. Therefore, for the current treatment of GC, the targeted inhibition of GC stem–like cells is a strategy for GC treatment. CD133 and CD166 are the markers of CSCs and greatly contribute to the self-renewal, proliferation and differentiation of stem cells [ , ]. Some GC stem–like cells in mouse and human GC cell lines and tissues have been successfully labelled and isolated. However, most of these GC stem–like cells are only labelled by a single marker, which has certain limitations. Thus, the combination of two cell surface markers can facilitate the accurate extraction of GC stem–like cells. The results showed that CD133+/CD166 + cells sorted by magnetic-activated cell sorting (MACS) exhibited stronger proliferation, colony formation, invasion, sphere formation and stem cell marker expression (Sox2 and Oct-4) than other cell subsets and parental cells. In this study, a combination of two cell surface markers, CD133 and CD166, CD133+/CD166+, CD133+/CD166−, CD133−/CD166 + and CD133−/CD166 − subsets of the human GC cell lines (BGC-823 and SGC-7901) were successfully obtained by magnetic bead sorting. Then, the sorting efficiency was analysed by flow cytometry. The results confirmed that MACS had strong specificity, which laid a foundation for the characterisation of the GCSCs.
Traditional Chinese medicine has unique benefits for tumour therapy, including alleviating the side effects of chemotherapy and improving the quality of survival of patients with tumours [ ]. In recent years, RA has attracted considerable interest for its remarkable anti-tumour activity. Czop et al. [ ] showed that RA affected viability and induced apoptosis in all leukaemia cell lines. Li et al. [ ] found that RA inhibited fatty acid synthase activity and downregulated its expression in human breast cancer MCF-7 cells, in which fatty acid synthase plays a key role in the fatty acid biosynthesis pathway in cancer cells. These findings suggest that RA can be applied for the prevention and treatment of cancer. Roupe et al. [ ] suggested that RA showed concentration-dependent anticancer activity in hepatocellular carcinoma cells. However, the role of RA on tumour stem cells is unclear, and the underlying mechanisms are not clear. The results of the present study confirmed that RA inhibited the stem-like phenotypic properties of GCSCs, including proliferation, spheroidogenesis, invasion, migration and expression of stem cell markers. Sox2 and Oct-4 are key transcription factors regulating stem cell pluripotency [ ]. RA exhibited a considerable inhibitory effect on the tumour stem–like cell phenotypic properties of CD133+/CD166 + human GC cell subsets.
In this study, we obtained microarrays about GCSCs and normal stem cell specimens through the public gene chip data GEO, analysed the DEGs present in both and provided a reference for further research on the tumour markers of GC. We analysed the action targets of RA to elucidate the possible mechanism of action of RA in the treatment of GC. The analysis showed that PD-L1 protein was upregulated in BGC-823 and SGC-7901 parental cell lines and further upregulated in CD133+/CD166+ (BGC-823) and CD133+/CD166+ (SGC-7901), suggesting that PD-L1 was preferentially expressed in the GC-derived stem cells and the protein level of PD-L1 was conspicuously reduced after RA treatment. Programmed death receptor-1 (PD-1, CD279) was initially identified because of upregulated expression during cell death in a T-cell hybridoma [ ]. PD-L1, one of the ligands of PD-1, is a type I transmembrane protein composed of 290 amino acids and consists of a transmembrane region and two extracellular regions: IgC and IgV [ ]. Many tumour cells express PD-L1, which can bind to PD-1 on the surfaces of cytotoxic T cells, leading to impaired T cell function. PD-L1 can synergise with other immunosuppressive signals to contribute to the formation of a suppressive immune microenvironment [ ]. PD-L1 is expressed in many solid tumours, such as prostate cancer, ovarian cancer, melanoma, bladder cancer, hepatocellular carcinoma, GC, glioma, thyroid cancer, thymic epithelial tumour and head and neck cancer [ – ]. The expression of PD-L1 in tumour cells in vitro is generally lower than that in vivo, and the expression of PD-L1 is upregulated when tumour cells are transplanted into mice. Studies on the correlation between PD-L1 expression in tumours and prognosis have shown that the expression of PD-L1 in lung, endometrial, breast, renal and colon cancer; adenocarcinoma; and GC is closely related to poor prognosis, and that blockade of PD-L1 can improve the efficacy of immunotherapy. Therefore, drugs that target PD-L1 tend to exhibit enhanced anti-tumour efficacy. Wei et al. [ ] discovered that Astragalus polysaccharides can reverse gefitinib resistance in lung cancer cells by inhibiting the PD-L1/SREBP-1/EMT signalling pathway. Cha et al. [ ] demonstrated that tumour tissue from patients with breast cancer treated with metformin exhibited reduced PD-L1 levels, indicating that metformin enhanced cytotoxic T lymphocyte anti-tumour activity by blocking PD-L1 inhibitory signalling. The results of the present study showed that the proliferative capacity of CD133+/CD166+ (BGC-823) cells, number of cell spheres formed, number of clones formed, number of cells crossing the polycarbonate membrane and expression levels of stemness marker proteins Sox2 and Oct-4 were greatly reduced after the knockdown of PD-L1. Interestingly, none of the above cellular functions were markedly changed after the introduction of RA when PD-L1 was knocked down. Accordingly, the present study confirmed that RA lacked targets and therefore failed to function probably because of PD-L1 knockdown. Regarding the potential mechanisms by which RA regulates the expression of PD-L1, we speculate that RA could interfere with signalling pathways associated with PD-L1 expression, such as the MAPK or PI3K/Akt pathways. By inhibiting the activity of these pathways, RA can indirectly reduce the expression levels of PD-L1. Suresh Mickymaray and colleagues have confirmed that RA robustly suppresses the PI3K-Akt-mTOR signaling pathway within osteosarcoma cells, thereby effectively curbing their proliferative capacity [ ]. Furthermore, RA potently suppresses the inflammatory response induced by lipopolysaccharide in endothelial cells through the downregulation of key inflammatory mediators, including iNOS, COX2, NFκB, and the MAPK signaling pathways [ ]. Additionally, RA might affect the stability of the PD-L1 protein, making it more susceptible to degradation. This could be achieved by regulating proteins that interact with PD-L1, thereby accelerating its metabolism. Research has substantiated the promising potential of developing cutting-edge cancer immunotherapy strategies that focus on the modulation of PD-1/PD-L1 ubiquitination [ ]. Our results indicate that RA affects the proliferation, migration, invasion, and stemness of GCSCs in a manner related to PD - L1. However, given the complexity of cellular biology, it is likely that other mechanisms or factors may also be involved, and further research is needed to fully elucidate the comprehensive action mechanism of RA on GCSCs. In summary, the mechanism by which RA reduces PD-L1 expression is a complex process that may involve regulation at multiple levels. Future research in this study aims to employ a comprehensive array of technical approaches to fully elucidate the specific mechanisms involved.
In conclusion, RA suppressed the CD133+/CD166 + stem–like phenotype of GC stem–like cells by suppressing PD - L1 expression and enhanced the immune infiltration of CD4 + T and CD8 + T cells to tumour cells in vivo. This effect can be achieved by the targeted inhibition of PD - L1. However, this study has several limitations. Firstly, GC stem–like cells were sourced from cell lines (BGC − 823 and SGC − 7903), not from patients’ circulating CSCs or primary GC cells. Cell lines may change genetically and phenotypically during long - term cultivation, differing from in vivo tumor cells, potentially affecting the assessment of GC stem–like cells and drug efficacy, and limiting clinical application. Secondly, experimental techniques had limitations. In cell sorting by magnetic beads, despite optimization, inaccuracies in sorting certain cell subsets may have occurred due to marker heterogeneity or other reasons, affecting cell subset studies and drug effect evaluation. In protein expression detection (e.g., Western blotting), antibody specificity, sensitivity, and sample processing could introduce biases, affecting result accuracy and the determination of drug mechanism - related protein changes. Thirdly, some experiments had a relatively small sample size. In animal experiments, only 6 mice per group were used. A small sample size may not fully reflect cell population diversity and drug effects in vivo, missing potential differences and affecting conclusion generality. It may also reduce statistical power, preventing actual differences from reaching significance and affecting result interpretation. Finally, there are differences between the mouse model and humans in physiology, pathology, and the immune system. Physiologically, the gastrointestinal tracts of mice and humans differ in structure and function, affecting drug absorption, distribution, and metabolism and thus drug effects on tumor cells. Pathologically, gastric cancer in mice and humans may have different pathogenesis and characteristics, as human gastric cancer is affected by multiple factors not fully simulated in mice. Immunologically, the immune systems of mice and humans differ in composition, function, and regulation, and the drug’s effect on the tumor immune microenvironment may differ, limiting immunotherapy application.
