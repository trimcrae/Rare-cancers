## Background
Pancreatic cancer, recognized as a refractory tumor, has an overall survival rate of less than 10%, and its mortality rate continues to rise. Due to the low immune activity induced by its unique tumor microenvironment, pancreatic cancer is classified as a “cold” tumor and is insensitive to current immunotherapies. However, little is still known about the identification and functional mechanisms of key regulatory molecules in the formation of “cold” tumors.

## Methods
In this study, we identified the pancreatic ductal cell niche through single-cell sequencing. By calculating CNV scores using inferCNV to distinguish malignant from non-malignant cells and analyzing differences in transcriptional levels, we constructed a 15-gene model. The inhibitory effect of UBE2H on the immune microenvironment was studied through single-cell sequencing analysis, including the inhibition of T cell function, impairment of antigen presentation in macrophages and neutrophils, and activation of neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs).

## Results
Our results revealed the presence of a UBE2H+ cell population within the pancreatic ductal cell niche, primarily observed in pancreatic cancer samples. Notably, single-cell sequencing analysis of UBE2H overexpression revealed an enhanced T cell PD-L1 interaction axis and a downregulation of antigen presentation pathways in macrophages and neutrophils (antigen processing and presentation, macrophage activation, and neutrophil activation). Further in vivo experiments confirmed that high UBE2H expression promotes tumor progression, leading to increased T cell exhaustion (PD1) and decreased activation (CD69, GZMB, IFNR) in the immune microenvironment, impaired antigen presentation of macrophages and neutrophils (H2Kb, I-Ab, I-A/E), and increased neutrophil extracellular trap formation (MPO, NE, CITH3).

## Conclusions
This study revealed the presence of a UBE2H+ cell population within the pancreatic ductal cell niche and analyzed the inhibition of UBE2H on the immune microenvironment of pancreatic cancer through single-cell sequencing and in vivo experiments, providing important clues for the formation of “cold” tumors in pancreatic cancer and opening new directions for exploring new treatment strategies.

## Introduction
Pancreatic cancer (PC) is one of the most aggressive diseases, with a poor prognosis, while surgical resection is currently the only treatment method with curative potential. However, approximately 80%−85% of patients are diagnosed at an advanced stage, inoperable or with metastasis [ ]. Even if the surgery is successful, more than 50% of patients will eventually develop local recurrence or metastasis [ ]. Although chemotherapy is the most fundamental treatment option for advanced pancreatic cancer, intrinsic and acquired chemoresistance remains a significant issue in the treatment of PC [ ]. Although the research on new therapies, including immunotherapy and targeted therapy, is ongoing, most drugs have not shown satisfactory results [ ]. Therefore, in addition to surgery, comprehensive treatment after multi-disciplinary diagnosis should be emphasized. It is particularly important to conduct in-depth research on the schemes and molecular mechanisms that affect the effectiveness of pancreatic cancer treatment [ ].
Pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) is the most common type of PC, characterized by high intratumor heterogeneity and poor prognosis. A comprehensive description of intratumor heterogeneity in PDAC and the underlying mechanisms driving PDAC progression is essential for understanding and developing targeted therapies. Previous studies have profiled primary PDAC tumors and control pancreatic tissues, identifying multiple malignant and stromal cell types, including two ductal subtypes (ductal cell type I, ductal cell type II, and ductal cell type MKI67+) with distinct and aberrant gene expression profiles [ , ]. However, current research on the classification of different ductal cell subpopulations remains insufficient, and the regulation of conversion/reprogramming between different ductal cell types remains unknown. In a comprehensive study involving 83 PDAC samples analyzed through bulk sequencing, proteomics/phospho-proteomics, single-cell sequencing, spatial transcriptomics, and high-resolution cellular imaging, researchers identified transitional populations, including acinar-to-ductal metaplasia (ADM) and pancreatic intraepithelial neoplasia (PanIN), as well as non-transformed acinar cells, ductal cells, and PDAC cell populations. The ADM population co-expresses oncogenes and tumor suppressor genes and exhibits significant upregulation of epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) and stemness-related genes compared to tumor cells [ ]. The unique expression profile of ADM as an intermediate state suggests dynamic transitions between tumor and acinar fates, with progression toward PDAC driven by the acquisition of oncogenic KRAS events [ ]. The study highlights significant heterogeneity in PDAC, including spatially segregated driver clones, subtype heterogeneity within the same patient, and multiple transitional cell populations, such as ductal-like, ADM and PanIN [ ]. Moreover, recent bulk analyses of increasing numbers of PDAC samples have characterized differences in tumor microenvironment composition and revealed cell types and interactions associated with PDAC phenotypes [ , ].
It is well-known that antigen processing and presentation are the cornerstone of adaptive immunity. In human species, two major classes of glycoproteins responsible for antigen presentation include MHC class I and class II molecules, which present antigenic peptides to CD8+ T cells and CD4+ T cells, respectively [ ]. It provides a way to survey host proteins and their modifications for the adaptive immune system, with any changes that may indicate the presence of invaders such as viruses and bacteria or mutations leading to malignancies being detectable. The antigen presentation process involves multiple steps, including antigen acquisition, marking, proteolysis, delivery, peptide loading, and presentation. The presentation of MHC molecules on the cell surface is influenced by various factors, such as cytokines and cell types [ ]. Understanding the structure and function of MHC molecules, as well as the key steps in antigen presentation, provides a solid foundation for drug development in the fields of oncology and vaccines [ ]. The MHC antigens in mice, also known as H-2 antigens, are located on chromosome 17. The MHC molecules in mice consist of 11 subclasses, which can be divided into classical MHC class I molecules (also called MHC-Ia), including H-2D, H-2K, and H-2L; non-classical MHC class I molecules (MHC-Ib), including H-2Q, H-2M, and H-2T; classical MHC class II molecules (MHC-IIa), including H-2A (I-A), H-2E (I-E), and H-2P; and non-classical MHC class II molecules (MHC-IIb), including H-2M and H-2O [ ].
Neutrophils are the most abundant and efficient defenders against pathogens, but they play opposite roles in different types of cancer [ ]. However, due to their short half-life, it is still challenging to explore how neutrophils function in cancer [ ]. Research reports that neutrophils exhibit extraordinary complexity, with 10 different states, including inflammation, angiogenesis, and antigen presentation [ ]. It is worth noting that the antigen presentation process is associated with good survival rates in most cancers. These neutrophils can further induce antigen-specific and non-antigen-dependent T cell responses [ ]. Neutrophils produce neutrophil extracellular trap (NETs) by decondensing chromatin, decorating it with cytoplasmic or granular effector proteins, and then extruding it from the cell [ ]. Initially reported as an antibacterial strategy for trapping and killing bacteria, an increasing body of evidence suggests that NETs have other functions in autoimmunity, coagulation, and cancer [ ].
In our research, through the analysis of integrated single-cell sequencing data, we identified the pancreatic ductal cell niche (ductal cell type I, ductal cell type II, ductal cell type MKI67 and ductal cell type UBE2H). Next, by establishing an in vivo PC model, we demonstrated that overexpression of UBE2H in PC cells promotes tumor progression. Additionally, through flow cytometry and immunofluorescence assays, we showed that the overexpression of UBE2H is accompanied by T cell functional inhibition, impaired antigen presentation in macrophages and neutrophils, as well as the activation of NETs. With these efforts, we aim to better understand the regulation of immune microenvironment homeostasis by UBE2H and explore the potential mechanisms underlying cancer malignancy progression induced by UBE2H. These findings provide new strategies for overcoming the immune escape mechanisms mediated by UBE2H in PC.

## Materials and methods

## Databases and software
Data from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA, https://tcga-data.nci.nih.gov/tcga/) are downloaded for PC patients’ level 3 data. These datasets include relevant clinical data and survival information files. The scRNA-seq datasets were downloaded from GEO database under accession GSE212966. The Limma R package is used for differential expression analysis, with thresholds set at |LogFC| = 1, P < 0.05. The ClusterProfiler tool is used for Gene Set Enrichment Analysis, Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes, and Gene Ontology richment analysis, all with a significance threshold of P < 0.05. The CIBERSORT, SSGSEA, and XCELL tools are used for immunological infiltration analysis based on transcriptomic data.

## Cell culture
The Panc02 cell line, ASPC cell line and Mia PaCa-2 (Mia) cell line used in this experiment is from Procell (Wuhan, China), and tested and validated to ensure that they were free of mycoplasma contamination. Intratumoral neutrophils were isolated using Percoll density gradient centrifugation according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The general procedures for maintaining these cells in culture have been described elsewhere. In brief, the cells were cultured in DMEM medium (Gibco, New York, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Invitrogen) and maintained in a humidified incubator at 37℃ in 5% CO2. For transfection, the cells were plated at 50% confluence 24 h before the procedure and then transfected with Lipo3000 according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

## Coculture of PC cells and lymphocytes
Lymphocytes were transferred to 24-well cell culture plates precoated with 2.5 µg/mL anti-mouse CD3 followed by the addition of 0.5 µg/mL anti-mouse CD28 for 24 h. For the experiments in Panc02 cells were cocultured with lymphocytes, the PC cells were cultured for 24 h, and then lymphocytes were added. Finally, the activation and exhaustion levels of T cells were detected by flow cytometry.

## Mouse subcutaneous tumor model
Four-week-old male C57BL/6 mice were selected, and 3*10^6 Panc02 cells were injected subcutaneously. After 21 days, tumors were collected, and single-cell suspensions were prepared for subsequent experiments. The cell suspensions were stained and labeled, and immune cell infiltration, T cell activation and exhaustion, macrophage antigen presentation, as well as neutrophil antigen presentation and NETs, were measured using flow cytometry. For the animal study, all the experiments were performed in accordance with the guidelines of the ethics committee of Guangdong Provincial People’s Hospital. The mice were housed at Guangzhou LingFu TopBiotech Co, Ltd. at a temperature of 22–26℃ and humidity of 40–60% under a 12-hour light/12-hour dark cycle

## Antibodies and chemicals.

 

## Western blotting
Cells were harvested with NP-40 lysis buffer containing 250 mM NaCl, 50 mM Tris (pH 7.4), 1 mM Na3VO4, 5 mM EDTA, 1% Nonidet™ P40 (NP40), 50 mM NaF, 0.02% NaN3, 2 µg/ml protease cocktail inhibitor and 1mM PMSF by dropwise addition to the plates and kept on ice for few minutes. After sample preparation, the samples were heated in a water bath to 100℃ for 10 min to denature the proteins. Then, using a rapid PAGE gel kit from Yes Biotech, they are separated by SDS-PAGE electrophoresis, and then transferred to a PVDF membrane at 4℃. After the transfer is complete, the PVDF membrane is blocked with 5% BSA at room temperature for one hour, and then gently shaken overnight at 4 ℃ with the addition of the primary antibody. The next day, on the platform oscillator, the membrane is washed 3 times with 1×TBST (Tris-buffered saline, 0.1% Tween-20), and then incubated with a secondary antibody conjugated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP) at room temperature for one hour. Afterward, the membrane is washed 3 more times with 1×TBST. A high-sensitivity ECL chemiluminescence detection kit was subsequently used to visualize the protein bands using a gel imaging system.

## Lentiviral vectors and transduction
The mouse UBE2H gene was amplified and inserted into the pSin-EF2-Puro lentiviral vector via the restriction enzymes BamHI and EcoRI.
The sequences used for FLAG-UBE2H amplification.

 

 293 T cells were co-transfected with the UBE2H overexpression plasmid and packaging vectors using Lipofectamine 3000 transfection reagent (Invitrogen, L3000015). The lentivirus was collected 48 h after transfection, followed by cell infection.

## Immunofluorescence
For tissue immunofluorescence, the sections were deparaffinized, and antigen retrieval was performed using citrate buffer (pH 6.0). The sections were then blocked with 10% goat serum at room temperature for 1 h. Subsequently, staining with primary and secondary antibodies was carried out, followed by counterstaining of the cell nuclei with DAPI. Finally, the sections were mounted with anti-fade mounting medium.

## Preparation of a cDNA library
A cell suspension was loaded onto a Chromium Single-Cell Controller (10×Genomics), and a single-cell 3’ library kit, a Gel Beads-In EMulsion Kit v3.1 (10×Genomics, Cat. No. 1000121) and a Chromium NextGEM Chip G single-cell kit (10×Genomics, Cat. No. 1000120) were used to prepare the cDNA library according to the manufacturers’ instructions. An Agilent 4200 instrument was used to generate, amplify, and assess the quality of the cDNA library. An Illumina NovaSeq 6000 sequencing system was used to sequence the cDNA library with a minimum depth of 100,000 reads per cell following a dual-end 150 bp (PE150) read strategy.

## Single-cell sequencing
Use the software bcl2fastq (version 2.19.0.316) provided by Illumina to convert the original data files from the Illumina NovaSeq6000 platform into fastq files. Align the cDNA reads with the mouse reference genome (mm10). Use the software cellranger (version 6.1.2) to screen for low-quality cells and genes, counting barcodes and UMIs, and obtaining a filtered gene-cell matrix. Then use the “Seurat” R package (version 3.2.3) to further filter out low-quality cells and genes, log-normalize gene expression levels, and identify highly variable genes for PCA dimensionality reduction. Additionally, use the “Harmony” R package to integrate gene-cell matrices from different samples. Match cluster-specific genes with known cell group characteristics from previous studies and the CellMarker database to annotate each cell cluster.

## Statistical analysis
R language and GraphPad Prism 9.0 were used to collect, summarize, statistically analyze, and graph the data. Experiments were performed at least in triplicate and error bars were presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). Student’s test was used to evaluate statistical significance, defined as *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001. A P value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

## Results

## Single-cell sequencing identified the UBE2H+ pancreatic ductal cell niche.
To characterize the heterogeneity of the tumor microenvironment in PDAC samples, we analyzed single-cell sequencing data from normal pancreatic tissues and PDAC samples (Fig. A). By integrating existing cell annotation strategies, we revealed differences in cellular composition between normal and PDAC tissues (Fig. B-C, Fig. S1 A-B). Using inferCNV to calculate CNV scores, we distinguished malignant from non-malignant ductal cells (Supplementary Table 1). Functional analysis demonstrated that malignant ductal cells exhibited activation of cell cycle pathways and various metabolic processes (Fig. D). By analyzing differential transcriptional profiles and integrating survival data, we identified multiple key genes (Fig. E). In addition, through gene annotation of cellular subpopulations and analysis of the pancreatic ductal cell niche revealed a UBE2H-high subpopulation with partial similarity to ductal cell type II, which we defined as ductal cell type UBE2H+ (Fig. F-I, Fig. S1 C-E) [ ]. Cell-cell communication analysis showed significantly enhanced interactions involving UBE2H+ ductal cells in PDAC samples, particularly in oncogenic pathways such as WNT pathway and VEGF pathway (Fig. J-K). In vitro and in vivo experiments further demonstrated that high UBE2H expression in Panc02 pancreatic cancer cells promoted proliferation and accelerated subcutaneous tumor progression in C57BL/6 mice (Fig. L-P, Fig. S1 F).

 

## A 15-gene index was constructed based on key genes associated with CNV scores.
Then, we further analyzed the CNV-related differentially expressed genes using LASSO regression analysis and obtained a list of 15 genes associated with the malignancy of pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (Fig. S2 A-B). The receiver operating characteristic curves (ROC) curve for the model and the 3-year survival rate ROC curve indicate that the model has high accuracy (Fig. S2 C-D). The survival analysis shows that patients with higher scores have a worse prognosis, and as the score increases, the number of cases with poor outcomes also increases (Fig. S2 E-G). Additionally, the functional analysis revealed that patients with higher 15-gene model scores showed suppressed immune-related signal pathways such as lymphocyte activation, leukocyte activation, and T cell differentiation, and activated cancer progression-related signals such as MET signaling, PI3K-AKT signaling, and VEGF signaling (Fig. S2 H-M). They also exhibited disrupted metabolic, ionic, chemical, and tissue homeostasis, as well as enhanced cellular migratory and invasive abilities (Fig. S2 H-M). We used other datasets from the “Kaplan Meier plotter” database to assist in the survival analysis of these genes, and UBE2H had the largest lasso coefficient of 0.63 (Fig. S2 N-O). Finally, through the comprehensive analysis of immune components using XCELL, it was revealed that the high-scoring group exhibits lower levels of CD4+ T cells, CD4+ memory T cells, CD8+ T cells, and natural killer T cells (Fig. S2 P-Q). To assess the potential application value of the 15-gene model associated with the malignant chromosomal abnormalities of PDAC cells in the immunotherapy and chemotherapy of pancreatic cancer, we further analyzed the tracking of indels by decomposition (TIDE) database and found that, as expected, patients with a high 15-gene model score have characteristics such as a significant reduction in TIDE score, impaired T cell function, an increase in myeloid-derived suppressor cells, and an increase in T cell exclusion, which are often associated with poor prognosis (Fig. S2 R-Y). In summary, these results indicate that patients with a high 15-gene model score are more likely to benefit from immunotherapy. By combining data from the cancer genome atlas on PC transcriptomes with computational drug sensitivity predictions using the “pRRophetic” software. Among these drugs, we have identified several drugs with potential application value such as Dasatinib_1079, Erlotinib_1168, and Sapitinib_1549 (Fig. S2 Z).

## Single cell sequencing analysis reveals the global regulation of the immune microenvironment by UBE2H.
To characterize the impact of high UBE2H expression on the immune microenvironment, we performed subcutaneous tumorigenesis experiments using C57BL/6J mice inoculated with Panc02 or Panc02-UBE2H cells. Through flow cytometric selection of Cd45+ cells, followed by rigorous live-dead staining and single-cell library construction protocols, we built up datasets for analysis. To analyze the composition of Cd45+ cells, we used principal component analysis and uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) clustering, identifying 31 main clusters (Fig. A, Fig. S3R-S). After cell marker database searching for various immune cell markers, we eventually identified macrophage markers such as Fcgr2b, Cd86, Mcr1, Adgre; NK/T cell markers like Cd3g, Cd8a, Klrk1; Circulating tumor cell (CTCs) markers including Mki67, Stmn1; neutrophil markers such as S100a8, S100a9; Mast cell markers like Cma1; DC cell markers like Napsa (Fig. A-E; Fig. S3A-Q) [ , ]. Moreover, cell proportion analysis indicates that the UBE2H group has a higher percentage of CTCs/neutrophil and a lower percentage of macrophages (Fig. F). Additionally, specific cell population marker gene UMAP plots demonstrate the validity of cell annotation (Fig. G). To analyze the potential role of cell-to-cell interactions in the immunosuppression induced by UBE2H, we performed cell communication analysis, which revealed the existence of a complex cell communication network where signals from one cell not only affect itself but also influence other cell populations (Fig. H-J). We found that the Cd274-Pdcd1 axis plays a significant role in regulating NK/T cells, where Cd274 is highly expressed in cell populations other than NK/T cells, and Pdcd1 is highly expressed in NK/T cells, with neutral granulocytes and circulating tumor cells being the main sources of signals recognized by NK/T cells (Fig. K-M). Additionally, the UBE2H group exhibited high expression of Pd-l1 and Pd-1 in various types of cells (Fig. M).

 

The above analytical results suggest that the overexpression of UBE2H by cancer cells could affects the cellular communication within the immune microenvironment and might induces functional disorder of specific cell populations, thereby promoting the progression of tumors.

## UBE2H regulates T cell dysfunction by inducing terminal exhaustion.
To analysis the ecological impact of the high expression of UBE2H in the immune microenvironment on T cells heterogeneity [ , ]. We performed principal component analysis and uniform manifold approximation and projection clustering, using well-known T-cell type markers to identify cell clusters: Tregs (Ctla4, Foxp3), Trms (Cd69), and Tnks (Klrd1) (Fig. A-B; Fig. S4X-Y) [ , ]. Through cell ratio analysis, we found that the high expression of UBE2H is associated with a lower proportion of Trms (Fig. C). Through UMAP marker expression analysis, we observed higher expression of Ctla4, Pdcd1, and lower expression of Cd69, Hspa1a, Hspa1b, and Hsp90aa1 in the high expression of UBE2H (Fig. E; Fig. S4A-O). By pseudotime analysis, the results indicate that there are more cells from the high-expression group of UBE2H at the beginning of differentiation, while the end of differentiation is primarily composed of Trms and Tregs (Fig. F). To analysis the impact of cell-to-cell interactions on the function of T-cell subsets, cellchat analysis revealed the importance of the Cd274-Pdcd1, Alcam-Cd6, Lamc1-Cd44 communication axes (Fig. G-K). In addition, there are many other communication axes involved. These analytical results make us realize that regulating the communication between T-cell subsets may be an important strategy for controlling the immunological microenvironment in the future. Multiple immunofluorescence was used to detect C57 subcutaneous tumor tissue. The experiment showed that the high expression group of UBE2H had lower infiltration of activated CD8 T cells (Fig. L).

 

## In vivo experiments have shown that UBE2H regulates T cell dysfunction.
To analysis the regulation of UBE2H expression on T cell function. First, we isolated lymphocytes from the spleens of C57BL/6J mice and co-cultured them with pancreatic cancer cells in vitro. The experimental results showed that the exhaustion of T cells in the high expression group of UBE2H was significantly increased (Fig.  A-B). At the same time, we also conducted a T cell activation assay. The experimental results showed that the CD3+ CD4+ cells, CD3+ CD+ cells, CD4+ CD69+ cells, and CD8+ CD69+ cells in the high UBE2H expression group were significantly reduced (Fig.  C-D). Next, in order to study the regulation of T cell immunity by UBE2H in vivo, we harvested the subcutaneous tumors of C57 mice. Through flow cytometry, we found that the percentage of CD3+ CD4+ T and CD3+ CD8+ T cells in the UBE2H overexpression group was reduced, and the expression of PD1 was elevated (Fig.  E). Flow cytometry analysis of CD69 revealed that the UBE2H overexpression group had fewer CD4+ CD69+ and CD8+ CD69+ cells (Fig.  F-G). Flow cytometry analysis of Granzyme B (GZMB) and IFNR revealed that the UBE2H overexpression group had fewer CD4+ GZMB+ and CD8+ GZMB+ cells as well as fewer CD+ IFNR+ and CD8+ IFNR+ cells (Fig.  H-K). These results suggest that high expression of UBE2H in tumor cells regulates the exhaustion of T cells and inhibits their activation. In addition, we obtained a gene list related to terminal exhaustion of T cells from the GSEA database. The results showed that the high UBE2H expression group had a higher terminal exhaustion score, and the expression of related genes also led to the same conclusion (Fig. S4P-V). Through single-cell gene set variation analysis (GSVA) analysis, we found that high UBE2H expression exhibited inhibition of NK-T and T cell proliferation, differentiation, and activation (Fig. S4W).

 

## UBE2H overexpression induces reduction of antigen presentation molecules and impaired macrophage function
To analysis the regulation of macrophage heterogeneity in the immunological microenvironment by the overexpression of UBE2H in tumor cells [ ]. Previous researchers have proposed the existence of several macrophage subsets, including IFN-producing TAMs, regulatory TAMs, Inflammatory Cytokine-rich TAMs, Lipid-associated TAMs, Angiogenesis-promoting TAMs, Resident tissue TAMs, and Proliferation-inducing TAMs [ ]. After performing principal component analysis and Uniform Manifold Approximation and Project clustering, and identifying specific cell markers, we annotate the cell populations as follows: IFN-TAMs are characterized by the markers Cd86, Reg-TAMs express the markers Arg1 and Mrc1, LA-TAMs are characterized by the markers Apoe, and Angio-TAMs are identified by the markers Spp1 (Fig. A-E; Fig. S5A-I) [ ]. By analyzing the gene expression of specific cell markers through UMAP, we find that the macrophages in the high-expression group of UBE2H have higher levels of Arg1, Mrc1, Spp1, and Fn1 expression, while genes related to chemotaxis, such as Cx3cr1 and Ccl3, are expressed at lower levels (Fig. F-K). Through cell communication analysis, we discovered the interaction networks, APP, CCL, THBS and MHC-1, and these interaction networks are usually involved in the antigen presentation and inflammatory regulatory functions of macrophages (Fig. L-N; Fig. S5J).

 

To analysis the regulation of macrophage antigen presentation function by high expression of UBE2H in pancreatic cancer, we used the C57BL/6J mice tumors and analyzed the macrophage antigen presentation molecules H2Kb, I-Ab, and I-A/E by flow cytometry. The experimental results showed that not only were Cd45 + immune cells reduced in the high UBE2H expression group, but the proportion of macrophages also showed a decrease. These results are consistent with the single-cell results (Fig.  O-Q). In addition, the flow cytometry results of antigen presentation molecules showed that the high expression of UBE2H in pancreatic cancer reduced the expression of macrophage antigen presentation molecules H2Kb, I-Ab, and I-A/E (Fig.  O-Q). Single-cell GSVA analysis also indicated that the expression of UBE2H not only affected the activation, chemotaxis, and cytokine production of macrophages but also influenced their antigen presentation function (Fig.  R; Fig. S5K).

## UBE2H impaired antigen presentation function and induced the NETs formation of neutrophil
Through UMAP clustering of neutrophil data in single-cell analysis, we obtained four clusters. By annotating with known neutrophil marker genes, we finally identified Ppia+ Neu, G0s2+ Neu, Cd274+ Neu, and Cadm1+ Neu (Fig.  A-C). The cell proportion analysis showed that the high UBE2H expression group had a higher proportion of G0s2+ Neu and Cd274+ Neu as well as a lower proportion of Ppia+ Neu and Cadm1+ Neu (Fig.  D). Subsequently, through single-cell GSVA analysis, we found that the expression of UBE2H in pancreatic cancer not only affected processes including neutrophil activation, cell cell recognition, and neutrophil mediated immunity but also reduced multiple antigen presentation-related signaling pathways, such as MHC protein binding (Fig.  E). Next, we analyzed the expression of key genes in the biological processes related to neutrophils, and the results showed that these genes exhibited significant differences in the experimental group. For example, Cd274 was highly expressed in neutrophils of the high UBE2H expression group, while Itgam was less expressed in neutrophils of the high UBE2H expression group (Fig.  F).

 

Previous studies have reported that neutrophils exhibit remarkably complex characteristics, including inflammation, angiogenesis, and antigen presentation across ten different states. Notably, the antigen presentation process is associated with favorable survival in most cancers. To investigate the regulation of antigen presentation by neutrophils in the microenvironment due to high UBE2H expression, we examined neutrophils and their antigen presentation-related molecules in tumor tissues using flow cytometry. The experiment showed that the expression of antigen presentation molecules (H2Kb, I-Ab, I-A/E) in neutrophils was reduced in the high UBE2H expression group (Fig.  G-L). Furthermore, the antigen presentation role of dendritic cells has long been considered an important link in the immune cascade. To further demonstrate that UBE2H regulates global suppression of the immune microenvironment, we also examined antigen presentation by dendritic cells. Consistent with our research results, the expression of antigen presentation molecules (H2Kb, I-Ab, I-A/E) in dendritic cells was reduced in the high UBE2H expression group (Fig. S6A-F).
To further analyze the regulation of UBE2H on neutrophils, we examined NETs markers and found that the expression of NETs molecules (MPO, NE, CITH3) was elevated in the high UBE2H expression group (Fig.  A-F). By extracting intratumoral neutrophils for protein level detection, we discovered that the high UBE2H expression group had higher protein expression of NETs molecules (MPO, NE, CITH3) (Fig.  G). Immunofluorescence analysis showed that there was more neutrophil infiltration and MPO expression in the high UBE2H expression group, while CD8 + T cell infiltration was less (Fig.  H-I).

 

Finally, we propose that the malignant progression of pancreatic cancer caused by high UBE2H expression is multifaceted. Among them, due to the E2 ubiquitin ligase function of UBE2H, combined with our findings that high UBE2H expression inhibits antigen presentation molecules in macrophages, neutrophils, and dendritic cells, as well as promotes neutrophil NETs activation, induces immune microenvironment suppression.

## Discussion
This study combined single-cell sequencing data with in vivo and in vitro experiments to systematically reveal the key regulatory role of ductal cell type UBE2H + in the PC immune microenvironment. Our results indicate that high expression of UBE2H significantly alters the composition and function of immune cells in the immune microenvironment, including inhibition of T cell function and impaired antigen presentation in macrophages and neutrophils, thereby promoting the malignant progression of the tumor (Fig.  ). Our research expands the understanding of the functional role of UBE2H and explores its potential reference value for the future immune microenvironment, especially regarding antigen presentation, based on its E2 ubiquitin ligase function.

 

Previous studies on UBE2H mainly focused on erythroid maturation and brain development [ , ]. However, the role of UBE2H in the progression of pancreatic cancer and its impact on the immune microenvironment has not been thoroughly investigated. In this study, through single-cell sequencing and in vivo and in vitro experiments, we revealed that high expression of UBE2H in pancreatic cancer regulates a global suppression of the immune microenvironment, including T cell dysfunction and impaired antigen presentation by immune cells. This finding deepens our understanding of the interaction between UBE2H and tumor immunity.
In previous studies, the antigen presentation process was often considered a key link in the immune response [ , ]. The initiation and recognition of the immune response depend on antigen presentation, where MHC molecules (class I and II) play an important role. MHC molecules activate cellular and humoral immunity by distinguishing between endogenous/exogenous antigens, serving as the core bridge for adaptive immune responses [ ]. Their precise division of labor and collaboration ensure efficient defense against diverse pathogens. Therefore, the development of functional antibodies against related MHC molecules is significant for deeply understanding the antigen presentation process and studying the regulation of biomarkers on antigen presentation [ ]. In this study, using flow cytometry antibodies, we analyzed the protein regulation of H2Kb, I-Ab, and I-A/E in macrophages, dendritic cells, and neutrophils by UBE2H expression. Through the application of flow cytometry, we found that tumor-infiltrating immune cells with high UBE2H expression had lower levels of H2Kb, I-Ab, and I-A/E proteins. Thus, this study indicates that high UBE2H expression in PC may impair antigen presentation function in immune cells and suppression of the immune microenvironment.
NETs are a type of DNA fiber network structure released by activated neutrophils, which play complex roles in cancer development and progression [ ]. On the one hand, NETs can protect the organism by capturing and killing pathogens; on the other hand, overactive NETs can promote tumor angiogenesis, metastasis, and chemoresistance [ , ]. This study found that the high expression of UBE2H enhanced the expression of NETs-related genes (MPO, NE, CITH3), and more NETs were observed in flow cytometry and immunofluorescence experiments. This suggests that UBE2H may support tumor growth by inducing NETs formation. Notably, high levels of NETs are also accompanied by lower CD8+ T cell infiltration, which further confirms the immune suppression effect mediated by UBE2H.
Currently, the research on the strategy development targeting E2 ubiquitin ligases is insufficient. In previously reported relevant studies, in order to identify small molecule inhibitors of UbcH5c, an active compound named 2α,6α-diacetoxy-4β-hydroxy-11(13)-pseudoguaien-12,8α-olide (DHPO) was discovered through molecular docking and virtual screening tests [ ]. Similarly, in a study on UBE2T, PGG was identified as an UBE2T inhibitor with high binding affinity through surface plasmon resonance experiments [ ]. In addition, in a study on UBE2N, researchers pointed out that although covalent UBE2N inhibitors are currently the fastest - progressing small molecules, non - covalent inhibitors have significant advantages and can overcome the limitations of covalent inhibitors, especially in terms of selectivity [ ]. Overall, these studies indicate that although the strategy development of E2 ubiquitin ligases faces many difficulties, the development of targeted therapies is still feasible, and more research findings can deepen our understanding of E2 ubiquitin ligases and the development of inhibitors. Currently, there has been no research on drug development targeting UBE2H. Therefore, we hope that our research on UBE2H can provide a reference for future studies.
Despite the use of multiple methods to confirm the role of UBE2H in the PC immune microenvironment, this study has some limitations. Firstly, the study initially relied on public repositories (GEO and TCGA), which means that the sample size and patient population are relatively limited, lacking large-scale and multi-cohort studies to deepen its clinical translation value. Additionally, although we used pancreatic cancer cells to construct a subcutaneous tumor model and performed single-cell sequencing, it still does not completely match the human pancreatic anatomical structure and immune characteristics. The use of genetically engineered mice can provide more specific and accurate insights into the mechanisms regulated by UBE2H and its effects on tumor growth and immune modulation. We must acknowledge that these are some of the limitations and deficiencies in our study. Finally, although this study revealed the impact of UBE2H on the antigen presentation function of immune cells, its exact mechanism of action requires further research, especially regarding the ubiquitination and degradation of target proteins.
UBE2H is not only involved in various biological processes within PC cells but also profoundly affects the interactions among different types of immune cells in the immune microenvironment. Given that UBE2H promotes tumor progression and inhibits anti-tumor immunity at multiple levels, it is expected to become a new therapeutic target. More research in the future needs to focus on the E2 ubiquitin ligase function of UBE2H and further study its potential mechanisms of action. In addition, considering the association between UBE2H expression levels and patient prognosis, monitoring the changes in this gene may help achieve personalized medicine and provide a basis for developing more precise and effective treatment strategies.
In conclusion, this study analyzed the pancreatic ductal cell niche revealed a UBE2H-high subpopulation with partial similarity to ductal cell type II, which defined as ductal cell type UBE2H+. The multiple functions of UBE2H in PC through single-cell sequencing and in vitro/vivo experiments, providing important clues for understanding the underlying molecular mechanisms and opening up new directions for exploring new treatment strategies. This study provides a new strategy for future immunotherapy of pancreatic cancer, namely, by targeting UBE2H to regulate the antigen presentation process, the initiation and activation of immune responses can be effectively carried out. More experimental validation and clinical trials are still needed to confirm the practical application value of these preliminary conclusions.
