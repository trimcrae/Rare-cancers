## Summary
Traditional dressings face clinical challenges in skin wound healing, including inadequate infection control, poor regulation of inflammation, and insufficient angiogenesis. This study prepared a composite sponge (CuMOFs/CTMG/Exos) using copper-based metal-organic frameworks (CuMOFs), trimethylolpropane-modified chitosan derivatives (CTMGs), and human umbilical cord mesenchymal stem cell exosomes (hUMSC-Exos). The sponge has a three-dimensional porous structure and synergistically releases Cu2+ and hUMSC-Exos continuously in vitro. It exhibits broad-spectrum antibacterial activity, promotes Cu2+-mediated angiogenesis, and possesses a strong antioxidant capacity. In a rat full-thickness skin-wound model, the sponge suppressed inflammatory cell infiltration, reduced the expression of pro-inflammatory factors, and promoted angiogenesis and collagen deposition, offering an effective therapeutic approach for skin wound healing.

## Introduction
The skin serves as the body’s primary barrier against trauma, acting as a key protective structure. When an open wound compromises the skin’s integrity, multiple serious complications can ensue. Such wounds compromise the skin’s protective function, permitting the invasion of bacteria, viruses, and other pathogens and thereby markedly increasing the risk of infection. Studies demonstrate that approximately 10%–20% of open wounds develop infections of varying severity, with the most severe cases potentially progressing to systemic diseases (e.g., sepsis) and posing a serious threat to patient safety. Furthermore, delayed wound healing not only prolongs patient suffering but also increases medical costs and compromises daily activities and occupational capacity. , Currently, the clinical management of open wounds presents notable challenges. Although traditional therapies, such as simple debridement and antibiotic use, can partially prevent infections, they are often ineffective in addressing the complex issues associated with wound healing. Prolonged antibiotic administration in skin-wound management can exacerbate antibiotic resistance and impair infection control. Moreover, advanced technologies, such as skin grafting, are limited by donor shortages and the risk of immune rejection, restricting their widespread application. Thus, there is an urgent need for comprehensive and effective wound treatment strategies.
Human umbilical cord mesenchymal stem cell-derived exosomes (hUMSC-Exos) have emerged as a key focus in skin wound-healing research. Numerous studies have demonstrated that hUMSC-Exos carry multiple bioactive molecules that exert significant effects in promoting skin-wound repair. For instance, adipose-derived MSC exosomes activate the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway, promote cell proliferation and migration, and thereby facilitate wound closure and tissue regeneration. In a mouse skin wound model, administration of adipose-derived MSC exosomes significantly upregulated the expression of proliferating cell nuclear antigen. It reduced the wound closure time by approximately 30% compared with the control group. hUMSC-Exos modulate the TGF-β/Smad pathway, suppress inflammatory responses, inhibit myofibroblast differentiation, and promote angiogenesis. , However, hUMSC-Exos are confronted with challenges, including poor in vivo stability, short half-life, and rapid clearance by the reticuloendothelial system, factors that limit their therapeutic efficacy. , , Studies have demonstrated that the half-life of hUMSC-Exos ranges from minutes to hours upon intravenous administration, impeding their capacity to sustain pro-healing effects. Metal-organic frameworks (MOFs), particularly CuMOFs, have emerged as highly promising biomaterials in the biomedical field to address these challenges.
CuMOFs offer novel opportunities for wound therapy due to their excellent biocompatibility, antimicrobial properties, and ability to promote cell proliferation. The release of Cu2+ disrupts bacterial metabolism, while the porous structure of CuMOFs can regulate the release of bioactive molecules and establish a conducive microenvironment for wound healing. , , , , Chitosan and its derivatives have been extensively utilized as wound dressings, with trimethylolpropane-modified chitosan derivative (CTMG) composite sponges representing a prominent example. CTMG exhibits excellent antimicrobial, hemostatic, and adhesive properties. In animal studies, CTMG composite sponges demonstrated superior performance in a hepatic hemorrhage model, with an adhesion strength of 113 kPa on porcine skin, markedly surpassing that of fibrin glue. , , Although these materials exhibit efficacy when utilized individually, they possess inherent limitations. The synergistic integration of hUMSC-Exos, CuMOFs, and CTMG composite sponges is anticipated to mitigate the limitations of individual materials through synergistic effects. hUMSC-Exos convey miRNAs and growth factors that facilitate cellular repair and regeneration. , For instance, exosomal miR-21 can accelerate wound healing by targeting specific genes to enhance fibroblast proliferation and migration. CuMOFs exert sustained antimicrobial effects through the release of copper ions and synergize with CTMG to improve the mechanical properties of the dressing. , The robust adhesive and hemostatic properties of CTMG composite sponges further foster a conducive microenvironment for wound repair. In this study, we propose integrating hUMSC-Exos, CuMOFs, and CTMG composite sponges for the treatment of open wounds to address this research gap.
Based on the facts mentioned earlier, we studied the physicochemical properties of the CuMOFs/CTMG composite system and the influence of wound microenvironmental factors (pH value, H2O2 concentration) on the release behavior of Cu2+, as well as whether hUMSC-Exos could enhance its antimicrobial, antioxidant, and angiogenesis-promoting functions. Furthermore, the molecular mechanism underlying the synergistic wound repair effect of hUMSC-Exos and the CuMOFs/CTMG composite (including the collaborative action modes of Cu2+-mediated bacterial metabolism disruption, hUMSC-Exos-regulated inflammatory signaling pathways, and CTMG-facilitated tissue adhesion) was investigated. We also applied the biocompatible CuMOFs/CTMG/Exos composite sponge to test the effect of CuMOFs/CTMG, hUMSC-Exos, and their combination on skin wound healing when used alone and in combination through a rat full-thickness skin wound model. This study investigates the synergistic and multifunctional effects of hUMSC-Exos-modified CuMOFs/CTMG composite systems in protecting the wound microenvironment and promoting tissue regeneration in vitro, while simultaneously verifying their therapeutic efficacy in accelerating skin wound healing in vivo.

## Results

## Preparation and characterization of CuMOFs
CuMOFs were prepared via the strong synergistic interaction between copper ions and benzene-1,3,5-tricarboxylic acid (BTC) ligand, which is a key factor in forming their unique crystal structure ( A). Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) and scanning electron microscopy observations showed that the synthesized CuMOFs nanoparticles exhibit an irregular nanosheet morphology ( B and 1C). Particle size distribution analysis indicated the particle sizes are concentrated within the 200–450 nm range ( ). Brunauer-Emmett-Teller (BET) analysis and pore size distribution data ( , ) revealed that the CuMOFs have a BET-specific surface area of 678.324 m2/g, a pore volume of 0.336 cm3/g, and an average pore size of 2.919 nm. Energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy analysis ( D, 1E, and ) revealed that the primary elemental composition of the nanoparticles includes Cu (48.14%), C (38.66%), O (13.12%), and N (0.08%). X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) analysis ( F) demonstrated that the copper photoelectron peak predominantly appears at approximately 930.73 eV (2p 3/2), corresponding to copper in the Cu(II) oxidation state. X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis ( ) revealed characteristic peak positions of each crystal plane (dhkl) in the CuMOFs crystal structure at 2θ angles: 5.8° (111), 6.7° (200), 9.5° (220), 11.6° (222), 14.7° (331), 15° (420), 16.5° (422), 17.5° (333), 19.0° (440), 20.2° (442), 21.3° (620), 23.4° (444), 24.1° (551), 26.0° (553), 27.7° (733), 28.7° (660), and 35.2° (951), with the positions and intensities of all characteristic peaks highly matching the CuMOFs characteristic peaks in the ICDD standard database and literature reference, and no significant interference from extraneous peaks. 
In vitro cytotoxicity studies ( G and 1H) showed that CuMOFs at 250 μg/mL caused a large number of human umbilical vein endothelial cell (HUVEC) deaths after 1 and 3 days of co-culture, while no significant cell deaths were observed in the 125 and 75 μg/mL groups, indicating copper ion concentrations below 125 μg/mL do not affect cell proliferation. Gelation tests ( ) indicated CuMOFs/CTMG-1, CuMOFs/CTMG-3, and CuMOFs/CTMG-5 failed to achieve gelation. In contrast, CuMOFs/CTMG-7 and CuMOFs/CTMG-9 succeeded, with gelation times of 0.92 ± 0.14 s and 0.91 ± 0.12 s, respectively, and CTMG-7 showed a gelation time of 1.32 ± 0.13 s; CuMOFs leverage synergistic effects between their unique structures and metal nodes to enhance reaction efficiency among components, thereby enabling effective regulation of gelation behavior and ultimately conferring robust ligand adaptability to the system. Copper ion release tests ( I and 1J) were conducted under pH 5.0 (simulating the acute phase of infection or inflammation, where bacterial metabolism and inflammatory cells cause local acidification) and pH 7.4 (simulating the physiological pH of normal tissue fluid, corresponding to the wound proliferation/remodeling phase), showing accelerated release under acidic conditions (pH 5.0) and slowed release under physiological conditions (pH 7.4); the release rate accelerated as H2O2 concentration increased from 125 to 500 μM. 2,2-diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl (DPPH) radical scavenging test results ( K) indicated CuMOFs at 125 and 250 μg/mL have similar antioxidant properties with no significant difference, confirming 125 μg/mL as the optimal concentration. For enzymatic catalytic mechanism investigation, NaN3 (sodium azide), BQ (p-benzoquinone), and IPA (isopropanol) were selected to capture 1O2, O2−, and ·OH radicals in the CuMOFs + H2O2 reaction system; a slight decrease in absorbance at 405 nm was observed when IPA was added to the colorimetric system ( ), while the addition of BQ and NaN3 did not cause significant color changes, indicating CuMOFs can promote the production of a large amount of ·OH radicals from H2O2, and the presence of ·OH was further verified through DPPH analysis.

## Preparation and characterization of CuMOFs/CTMG
The synthesis process of CuMOFs/CTMG composite sponges is relatively straightforward ( A). CTMG is prepared via an amidation reaction, wherein the carboxyl group of TMG reacts with the amino group of CS through an EDC/NHS chemical reaction; the abundant hydroxyl groups in the TMG monomer promote the formation of numerous hydrogen bonds on the surface and within the hydrogel, facilitating CTMG crosslinking to form the hydrogel. CuMOFs are subsequently added to CTMG and mixed. B displays the microstructure and pore-size distribution of CuMOFs/CTMG composite sponges at different concentrations compared to CS sponges. A–S7F indicates that the pore size distribution of CTMG sponges (median: 45–170 μm) is significantly narrower than that of CS sponges (median: ∼225 μm); as the CuMOFs/CTMG concentration increases, enhanced crosslinking strength reduces the porous structure, resulting in tighter internal connections and smaller pore sizes within the gel, and this porous architecture endows the CuMOFs/CTMG composite sponges with superior adsorption capacity. FTIR spectroscopy results showed that the characteristic peaks of CuMOFs/CTMG covered the prominent peaks of CuMOFs and CTMG ( C). For CuMOFs, the N-H broadened vibrational absorption peak was observed at approximately 3,487 cm−1, the C=O broadened vibrational peak at 1,647 cm−1 was associated with amide or carboxylic acid groups, the characteristic peak at 1,415 cm−1 corresponded to the C=C and C-N-phosphorus peaks of the aromatic rings, and the absorption peak in the 769 cm−1 region is associated with metal-organic coordination. For CTMG, the broad peak at 3,360 cm−1 is an overlap of amino (N-H) and hydroxyl (O-H) stretching vibrations in CS, its absorption peak at 2,914 cm−1 correlates with the C-H stretching vibration of CS, the absorption peak at 1,647 cm−1 corresponds to the stretching vibration of the amide I band in the acetyl group, the absorption peak at 1,594 cm−1 is related to the bending vibration of the amino group (N-H), and the peaks near 1,647, 1,594, and 1,415 cm−1 indicate the presence of amide functional groups in CS. An in-depth analysis was conducted using the full spectrum and high-resolution N 1s spectrum of the XPS. The full XPS spectrum clearly displays characteristic peaks for C 1s, N 1s, and O 1s, and CS and CTMG retain the characteristic peaks for C, N, and O elements ( A). The N 1s spectrum of CS exhibits a single peak at 397.9 eV corresponding to the free amino group, while the N 1s spectrum of CTMG splits into two peaks: 399.8 eV (corresponding to the amide bond) and 397.9 eV (corresponding to the free amino group). Comparing the spectra of CS and CTMG, the peak intensity of CTMG in this band significantly increases, indicating an amidation reaction between CS and TMG ( B). shows the XRD patterns of CS and CTMG. The XRD pattern of CS presents a typical broad peak, with the characteristic peak center located at 2θ = 20.1°, and the XRD pattern of CTMG shows a significant decrease in the intensity of its characteristic peak around 2θ = 20°. 

## Rheological properties of CuMOFs/CTMG
Rheological studies of CuMOFs/CTMG composite sponges focused on the energy storage modulus (G′) and loss modulus (G′′). As depicted in D, the dynamic strain scanning results show that the maximum strain value within the linear viscoelastic region increases with an increase in the CuMOFs/CTMG concentration. E presents the dynamic frequency scan results over a frequency range of 0.01–10 Hz, where the G′ values for CuMOFs/CTMG-7 and CuMOFs/CTMG-9 consistently exceed those of G′′. Additionally, G′ increased with the CuMOFs/CTMG concentration. Notably, CuMOFs/CTMG-7 exhibited the highest energy storage modulus compared to CuMOFs/CTMG-9.

## Solubility of CuMOFs/CTMG
As shown in F, the CuMOFs/CTMG composite sponges exhibited remarkable swelling characteristics, with CuMOFs/CTMG-9 and CuMOFs/CTMG-7 reaching equilibrium within 30 min, with maximum swellings of 1,350% and 1,380%, respectively. Notably, the increase in swelling properties slightly diminished from CuMOFs/CTMG-7 to CuMOFs/CTMG-9. As shown in G, the water retention capacities of CuMOFs/CTMG-7 and CuMOFs/CTMG-9 exceeded those of the lower-concentration samples, losing approximately 70% of the water within 3.5 h, which was significantly better than those of CuMOFs/CTMG-1, CuMOFs/CTMG-3, and CuMOFs/CTMG-5 (reaching equilibrium within 2.5 h). The dynamic expansion process is shown in . The degree of amino substitution was measured via the trinitrobenzenesulfonic acid method, and the results in show the following values: CuMOFs/CTMG-1 (26.7%), CuMOFs/CTMG-3 (44.3%), CuMOFs/CTMG-5 (53.0%), CuMOFs/CTMG-7 (64.9%), and CuMOFs/CTMG-9 (63.8%). As the concentration of CuMOFs/CTMG increases, the degree of substitution increases, with CuMOFs/CTMG-7 having the highest degree of crosslinking.

## Compressibility testing of CuMOFs/CTMG
The mechanical strength of CuMOFs/CTMG sponges was evaluated via compression and cyclic compression tests. As shown in H, the stress levels of five different concentrations of CuMOFs/CTMG increased progressively at a strain of 70%, indicating that CuMOFs/CTMG-7 can withstand greater deformation stress at the same strain level ( ). The compressive strength values of the composite sponges in ascending order were as follows: CuMOFs/CTMG-1 (0.18 ± 0.01 MPa), CuMOFs/CTMG-3 (0.20 ± 0.01 MPa), CuMOFs/CTMG-5 (0.33 ± 0.01 MPa), CuMOFs/CTMG-7 (0.91 ± 0.05 MPa), and CuMOFs/CTMG-9 (0.81 ± 0.01 MPa). The CuMOFs/CTMG-7 composite sponges exhibited excellent mechanical properties, effectively protecting wounds and meeting the requirements of hemostatic materials.

## Bioadhesion and bursting pressure of CuMOFs/CTMG
As shown in A, the CuMOFs/CTMG composite sponges showed significant adhesion to various biological tissues, including the heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney. Laparose tests were performed to assess the adhesion properties of the composite sponges on tinplate, glass, and pigskin. B shows that CuMOFs/CTMG-3 exhibited the highest adhesion strength of 476.68 ± 31.97 kPa on the tinplate surface. The adhesion strengths of all composite sponges on glass surfaces significantly exceeded 247.36 ± 24.74 kPa ( C), with CuMOFs/CTMG-3 showing the strongest adhesion strength. D illustrates the adhesion properties on pigskin; all five concentrations of CuMOFs/CTMG composite sponges adhered well, with CuMOFs/CTMG-3 having an average adhesion strength of 161.28 ± 12.90 kPa, followed by CuMOFs/CTMG-5 (115.78 ± 11.56 kPa), CuMOFs/CTMG-7 (100.94 ± 6.09 kPa), CuMOFs/CTMG-1 (93.69 ± 7.49 kPa), and CuMOFs/CTMG-9 (70.81 ± 3.08 kPa). A schematic diagram of the burst pressure experiments is shown in E. F presents a comparative analysis of the burst pressures of commercially available medical hemostatic sponges and different concentrations of CuMOFs/CTMG: 0.022 ± 0.003 MPa for the commercially available hemostatic sponge, 0.037 ± 0.006 MPa for CuMOFs/CTMG-1, 0.043 ± 0.006 MPa for CuMOFs/CTMG-3, 0.053 ± 0.006 MPa for CuMOFs/CTMG-5, 0.084 ± 0.006 MPa for CuMOFs/CTMG-7, and 0.083 ± 0.003 MPa for CuMOFs/CTMG-9. G and show that when the pig stomach was injured, the gel sponge firmly adhered to the wound surface and effectively withstood the pressure, preventing fluid exudation. 

## Enzymatic degradability of CuMOFs/CTMG
To evaluate the biodegradability of CuMOFs/CTMG composite sponges, we measured the concentration of reducing sugars in lysozyme-added and lysozyme-free solutions. shows that the degradation rates of CS and CuMOFs/CTMG increased significantly after lysozyme treatment compared to the rates without lysozyme. Among the samples treated with lysozyme, CuMOFs/CTMG-7 exhibited higher degradability than CuMOFs/CTMG-9. This suggests that at lower concentrations, CuMOFs/CTMG may lack enough acetylation units to limit lysozyme’s effectiveness. In comparison, at higher concentrations, as in CuMOFs/CTMG-9, excessive acetylated units could hinder degradation by preventing lysozyme binding or creating a shielding effect. Additionally, high concentrations may also increase solution viscosity, further decreasing lysozyme activity.

## Antimicrobial properties of CuMOFs/CTMG
To evaluate the antibacterial performance of CuMOFs/CTMG composite sponges, E. coli and Staphylococcus aureus were used as model strains, with comparisons made against a 1.0 McFarland standard ( ). The initial bacterial concentration was determined to be 1.5 × 106 CFU/mL via OD600 measurement. Agar plate tests showed that CuMOFs/CTMG composite sponges produced negligible bacterial colonies ( A). All five tested concentrations exhibited a 99.99% inhibition rate against both E. coli and S. aureus ( B and 4C). Biofilm removal experiments revealed that CuMOF nanoparticles eliminated pre-formed biofilms of S. aureus and E. coli at effective concentrations of 125 or 250 μg/mL, which were lower than the bacterial growth inhibition concentration (700 μg/mL) ( ). Even at the lowest tested concentration (75 μg/mL), biofilm biomass on plastic surfaces decreased by approximately 62.4% compared to the control group. 

## DPPH free radical scavenging by CuMOFs/CTMG
Free radical scavenging activity is observed in CuMOFs/CTMG composite sponges. The free radical scavenging activity of CuMOFs/CTMG composite sponges was assessed using the DPPH method ( D). DPPH radicals are initially purple and turn yellow after reduction by antioxidants. The free radical scavenging rates of CuMOFs/CTMG-7 and CuMOFs/CTMG-9 were 43.27 ± 1.20% and 37.59 ± 1.94%, respectively, which were higher than those of the other concentrations but lower than the standard value of vitamin C.

## Biocompatibility of CuMOFs/CTMG
Hemocompatibility assessment revealed that the hemolysis rates of CuMOFs at concentrations of 75 and 125 μg/mL were below 5% ( ). CuMOFs/CTMG composite sponges also exhibited hemolysis rates of <5% across all tested concentrations ( E). Cytotoxicity evaluations using CCK-8 assays revealed that high concentrations of CuMOFs (250 μg/mL) induced significant cell death in HUVEC cells after three days of co-culture. In contrast, lower concentrations (75 and 125 μg/mL) did not cause notable cytotoxicity ( ). MTT tests conducted according to ISO 10993-5:2009 demonstrated that HUVEC survival rates exceeded 70% when co-cultured with CuMOFs/CTMG at concentrations ranging from 10 to 200 μL, confirming their non-toxic profile in vitro ( F).

## In vitro whole blood coagulation of CuMOFs/CTMG
The coagulation index (blood clotting index [BCI]) evaluation showed that all CuMOFs/CTMG composite sponges exhibited lower BCI values compared to medical hemostatic sponges ( G). Specifically, the BCI values were 92.50 ± 4.28% for medical hemostatic sponge, 70.48 ± 1.97% for CuMOFs/CTMG-1, 61.20 ± 2.15% for CuMOFs/CTMG-3, 66.43 ± 0.96% for CuMOFs/CTMG-5, 40.14 ± 1.52% for CuMOFs/CTMG-7, and 35.93 ± 2.10% for CuMOFs/CTMG-9. Among these, CuMOFs/CTMG-7 and CuMOFs/CTMG-9 demonstrated the lowest BCI values, indicating superior hemostatic potential. The enhanced coagulation ability of CuMOFs/CTMG-7 was attributed to its compact, porous structure.

## In vivo hemostatic effect of CuMOFs/CTMG in rat liver
To evaluate the in vivo hemostatic performance of five different concentrations of CuMOFs/CTMG gel materials, we established a liver hemorrhage model in Sprague Dawley (SD) rats. We plotted the experimental results, as shown in A. C is a schematic diagram of the model. In vivo hemostatic performance evaluation using a rat liver hemorrhage model demonstrated that all tested CuMOFs/CTMG gel materials significantly reduced blood loss compared to the blank control and commercial hemostatic sponges ( B). Specifically, the mean blood loss was 0.802 ± 0.061 g in the untreated group, 0.196 ± 0.004 g with commercial sponges, and 0.169 ± 0.007 g with CS sponges. Among CuMOFs/CTMG dressings, blood loss values were 0.150 ± 0.0108 g for CuMOFs/CTMG-1, 0.123 ± 0.001 g for CuMOFs/CTMG-3, 0.036 ± 0.012 g for CuMOFs/CTMG-5, 0.024 ± 0.003 g for CuMOFs/CTMG-7, and 0.023 ± 0.004 g for CuMOFs/CTMG-9. CuMOFs/CTMG-7 and CuMOFs/CTMG-9 exhibited the lowest blood loss, outperforming both commercial sponges and CS sponges. 

## Isolation, identification, and characterization of hUMSCs and hUMSC-Exos
Phase-contrast microscopy revealed that the isolated cells exhibited a characteristic spindle shape on the culture plate and formed colonies at generations 0 and 3 ( A). Following induction of osteogenic, adipogenic, and chondrogenic differentiation, these cells successfully differentiated into osteoblasts, adipocytes, and chondrocytes, as confirmed by positive Alizarin red, Oil Red O, and Alphanizarin blue staining ( B). Flow cytometry analysis showed positive expression of the cell surface markers CD29, CD73, CD90, CD105, and CD166, and negative expression of the markers CD11b, CD14, CD19, CD34, CD45, and HLA-DR ( A–S17E). Further analysis of purified hUMSC-Exos using Nanosight tracking analysis and TEM showed that most particles had a diameter of approximately 138.1 nm ( D), with TEM imaging revealing the characteristic cup-shaped morphology of hUMSC-Exos ( C). Western blot analysis confirmed the detection of exosome surface markers, including Calnexin, TSG101, and CD63 ( E). Co-culture experiments showed that HUVECs’ uptake of exosomes was observed via immunofluorescence after 12 h of co-culture with hUMSC-Exos ( F). 

## Antimicrobial activity of CuMOFs/CTMG/exos
The antibacterial effect of CuMOFs/CTMG/Exos and CuMOFs/CTMG composite sponges against E. coli and S. aureus was evaluated via the direct contact method. Inhibition zone measurements revealed that the CTMG, CuMOFs/CTMG, and CuMOFs/CTMG/Exos groups exhibited significant antimicrobial properties compared to the CS group ( A). The CuMOFs/CTMG/Exos group exhibited more potent antimicrobial activity than the CuMOFs/CTMG group ( B). 

## In vitro cytotoxicity of CuMOFs/CTMG/exos
The cytotoxicity of CuMOFs/CTMG/Exos and CuMOFs/CTMG composite sponges was examined via calcein-AM/PI fluorescence staining ( C). Extracts of these materials did not significantly hinder cell proliferation after 1 and 3 days of co-culture with HUVECs. Both CuMOFs/CTMG/Exos and CuMOFs/CTMG composite sponges significantly promoted the proliferation of HUVECs compared to the control group ( E), indicating that both materials exhibit good biocompatibility under these experimental conditions.

## In vitro ROS elimination of CuMOFs/CTMG/exos
The ROS-scavenging ability of CuMOFs/CTMG/Exos and CuMOFs/CTMG composite sponges was further evaluated ( D). Under H2O2 stimulation, the fluorescence intensity of the CuMOFs/CTMG/Exos group was significantly lower than that of the positive control group and the CuMOFs/CTMG composite sponges group. The free radical inhibition rate of the CuMOFs/CTMG/Exos group (97.93 ± 0.44%) was significantly higher than that of the CuMOFs/CTMG composite sponges group (60.50 ± 2.79%) ( F).

## Exosomal release of CuMOFs/CTMG/exos
The exosome release profile from CuMOFs/CTMG/Exos composite sponges exhibited a distinct biphasic pattern ( G). The initial phase (days 1–2) featured rapid exosome release, likely due to surface-adsorbed exosomes, followed by a slower, sustained release phase (days 3–7) attributed to gradual diffusion of internally entrapped exosomes. Over 7 days, the composite sponges maintained consistent exosome release, confirming their slow-release capacity. BCA protein assay analysis ( A) showed pH-dependent release kinetics. Under pH 7.4, cumulative exosome release reached 52.63% ± 2.00% by day 4 and plateaued at 61.00% ± 1.61% by day 7. In acidic conditions (pH 6.0), release was slower, with 34.47% ± 1.25% released by day 4 and 48.50% ± 1.68% by day 7. This pH responsiveness likely stems from changes in the charge polarity of chitosan, enabling tunable exosome release. Exposure to H2O2 significantly influenced release dynamics ( B). After 7 days, cumulative exosome release was highest in 100 μM H2O2 (74.03% ± 1.40%), followed by 50 μM H2O2 (62.30% ± 1.42%), and lowest in 0 μM H2O2 (35.40% ± 2.38%). While high H2O2 accelerated hydrogel degradation, the composite sponge retained its ability to modulate release rates in response to ROS levels, confirming ROS as a key stimulus for this process. Korsmeyer-Peppas model fitting ( ) revealed a strong goodness of fit (R2 > 0.9) across all groups. The release mechanism varied with microenvironmental conditions: the 0 μM H2O2 (n = 0.48) and pH 6.0 (n = 0.59) groups primarily followed Fickian diffusion, whereas most other groups (n = 0.53–0.79) exhibited non-Fickian (anomalous) diffusion, indicating a combination of diffusion and swelling-driven release. pH (neutral > acidic) and ROS (high >low) influenced release through modulation of the rate constant k and release exponent n. Collectively, these findings demonstrate the composite sponge’s capacity for stimulus-responsive, sustained exosome delivery while scavenging reactive ROS radicals.

## In vitro cell scratching, cell migration, and in vitro angiogenesis of CuMOFs/CTMG/exos
Scratch assays, Transwell migration assays, and tube-forming assays were used to evaluate the impact of composite sponges on HUVEC migration and angiogenic capacity. Scratch assay ( A and 8D) and trans-well migration assay ( B and 8E) results showed that CuMOFs/CTMG/Exos treatment significantly enhanced HUVEC migration ability, with higher migration efficiency compared to other groups. Tube-forming assay ( C) revealed that HUVECs treated with CuMOFs/CTMG/Exos formed more capillary-like structures. Quantitative analysis showed a significant increase in the total number of branch points ( F). 

## CuMOFs/CTMG/exos for accelerated wound healing
To assess the efficacy of CuMOFs/CTMG/Exos on wound healing, a rat model of full-thickness skin defects was established. As depicted in A, the healing process was significantly faster in the CuMOFs/CTMG/Exos-treated group compared to the control and CuMOFs/CTMG groups, especially during the epithelialization phase (days 7–14). The control group exhibited signs of inflammation on days 0 and 7, whereas the CuMOFs/CTMG and CuMOFs/CTMG/Exos groups showed no significant signs of infection. Mean body weights of rats in each group ( ) exhibited a consistent growth pattern, indicating no adverse effects on body weight. After 14 days of treatment, wounds treated with CuMOFs/CTMG/Exos composite sponges showed good healing quality ( B and 9C). The wound recovery heatmap ( D) visually presented wound area changes via color gradients: the blank control group had dark wound areas with slow reduction, the CuMOFs/CTMG group had a moderate gradient, and the CuMOFs/CTMG/Exos group had light colors with rapid area reduction. 
H&E staining was performed to examine the development of wound skin tissue ( A). The CuMOFs/CTMG group and CuMOFs/CTMG/Exos group showed significantly more granulation tissue formation than the control group. By day 14, the epithelial regeneration rate of the CuMOFs/CTMG/Exos group was 90.18 ± 1.46%, significantly higher than that of the CuMOFs/CTMG group (73.39 ± 5.90%) and the blank group (53.09 ± 4.78%) ( C). The granulation tissue thickness of the CuMOFs/CTMG/Exos group peaked at 300.99 ± 1.93 μm on day 14, which was much higher than that of the CuMOFs/CTMG group (248.12 ± 1.89 μm) and the blank group (190.79 ± 1.74 μm) ( D). Masson’s trichrome staining ( B) showed that collagen deposition in the CuMOFs/CTMG/Exos group was 83.90 ± 4.81%, substantially higher than that in the CuMOFs/CTMG group (66.60 ± 4.43%) and the blank group (47.34 ± 6.18%) ( E). A large amount of collagen deposition was observed in the wound center of the CuMOFs/CTMG/Exos group, while the other two groups had relatively limited collagen deposition. 

## Immunohistochemical analysis of CuMOFs/CTMG/exos
Immunohistochemical staining for IL-6 and TNF-α was performed on postoperative days 7 and 14 to assess infection and inflammation ( A and 11B). The control group exhibited significant inflammation, whereas both CuMOFs/CTMG and CuMOFs/CTMG/Exos groups showed reduced IL-6 ( D) and TNF-α ( E) expression compared to the control. Notably, the CuMOFs/CTMG/Exos group demonstrated a more pronounced reduction in inflammatory cytokine levels than the CuMOFs/CTMG group. CD31 immunohistochemical staining ( C) revealed a significantly increased number of CD31-positive vessels in both CuMOFs/CTMG and CuMOFs/CTMG/Exos groups compared to the control group. The CuMOFs/CTMG/Exos group exhibited more pronounced vascularization, with a higher number of CD31-positive cells ( F), indicating enhanced angiogenic responses in wound tissues. 

## Discussion
Skin-wound healing remains a significant clinical challenge due to inadequate infection control, difficulties in inflammatory regulation, and insufficient angiogenesis. Traditional dressings, like simple debridement or antibiotic use, often lead to drug resistance, while donor shortages and immune rejection constrain skin grafting. Consequently, there is an urgent clinical need to develop novel dressings with multifunctional therapeutic capabilities. This study integrates CuMOFs, CTMG, and hUMSC-Exos to construct a CuMOFs/CTMG/Exos composite sponge dressing. This approach aims to overcome the limitations of single materials through synergistic interactions, offering an effective strategy for skin wound healing. The composite sponge exhibits a three-dimensional porous structure regarding material design and properties. This structure not only endows the material with outstanding swelling capacity (maximum swelling rates of 1,380% and 1,350% for CuMOFs/CTMG-7 and CuMOFs/CTMG-9, respectively) and water retention (only approximately 70% water loss within 3.5 h), enabling effective absorption of wound exudate and maintenance of a moist healing environment, but also regulates the release behavior of active components. , 
In vitro release experiments confirm that the composite sponge enables synergistic sustained release of Cu2+ and hUMSC-Exos: Cu2+ release is controlled by microenvironmental pH and H2O2 concentration, accelerating under simulated infection conditions (pH 5.0) or high ROS levels (500 μM H2O2). Initial rapid release achieves efficient bactericidal effects, while subsequent slow release maintains physiological functions. hUMSC-Exos exhibited biphasic release characteristics: rapid surface-adsorbed exosomes during the first two days, followed by slow release of encapsulated exosomes from days 3–7, ensuring sustained bioactivity supply throughout the healing process. Additionally, the material exhibits excellent mechanical properties (CuMOFs/CTMG-7 compressive strength: 0.91 ± 0.05 MPa), adhesion (maximum adhesion strength to porcine skin: 161.28 ± 12.90 kPa), and biocompatibility (hemolysis rate <5%, HUVEC cell viability >70%), meeting the fundamental requirements for clinical application as a wound dressing. , , 
In vitro and in vivo experimental results further validated the therapeutic efficacy of the composite sponge. In vitro tests demonstrated that the composite sponge achieved a 99.99% inhibition rate against E. coli and S. aureus, while eliminating bacterial biofilms. , Its DPPH radical and ROS scavenging rates reached 43.27 ± 1.20% and 97.93 ± 0.44%, respectively, significantly outperforming the single-material group. Concurrently, it promoted HUVEC migration and tube formation, laying the foundation for angiogenesis. In a rat full-thickness skin wound model, the composite sponge-treated group achieved an epithelial regeneration rate of 90.18 ± 1.46% at 14 days post-surgery, with granulation tissue thickness reaching 300.99 ± 1.93 μm. The collagen deposition rate was 83.90 ± 4.81%, all significantly higher than the CuMOFs/CTMG and blank control groups. It also effectively suppressed inflammatory cell infiltration, reduced expression of pro-inflammatory factors such as IL-6 and TNF-α, and promoted angiogenesis by increasing the number of CD31-positive vessels, ultimately accelerating wound healing and improving healing quality. From a mechanism perspective, the wound healing efficacy of the CuMOFs/CTMG/Exos composite sponge stems from the synergistic effects of its components. First, the antibacterial mechanism works as follows: Cu2+ released from CuMOFs disrupts bacterial metabolic processes; CTMG’s amino groups carry positive charges that interact with negatively charged proteins on bacterial surfaces; hUMSC-Exos deliver antimicrobial peptides like lysozyme and LL-37 to destroy bacterial cell walls directly. This tripartite synergy achieves broad-spectrum antibacterial effects while avoiding antibiotic resistance. , Second, the antioxidant mechanism functions as follows: CuMOFs catalyze H2O2 to generate ·OH radicals, scavenging excess ROS. CTMG’s hydroxyl groups further enhance free radical scavenging capacity, while hUMSC-Exos modulate intracellular oxidative stress pathways to synergistically boost antioxidant efficacy and improve the wound’s oxidative microenvironment. Third, the pro-angiogenic mechanism works as follows: Cu2+ activates vascular endothelial cell proliferation signals, while bioactive molecules like VEGF carried by hUMSC-Exos regulate pathways such as TGF-β/Smad to promote endothelial cell migration and tube formation. The material’s porous structure provides a spatial scaffold for angiogenesis, collectively driving the construction of a vascular network in the wound area. Fourth, anti-inflammatory and tissue repair mechanisms function as follows: hUMSC-Exos suppress inflammatory cell activation and downregulate pro-inflammatory factors like IL-6 and TNF-α. CuMOFs and CTMG mitigate inflammatory responses by modulating the wound microenvironment while promoting fibroblast proliferation and collagen deposition, accelerating granulation tissue formation and epithelial regeneration. 
In summary, the CuMOFs/CTMG/Exos composite sponge developed in this study effectively addresses critical challenges in skin wound healing by integrating antimicrobial, antioxidant, pro-angiogenic, and anti-inflammatory functions. Its material design balances controlled release of active components, excellent biocompatibility, and mechanical properties. Both in vitro and in vivo experiments confirm its superior wound healing efficacy, offering a highly effective dressing option for clinical skin wound treatment with broad translational application prospects. , 

## Limitations of the study
Although this study focused on the role of CuMOFs/CTMG/Exos composite sponges in skin wound healing and examined the synergistic effects of CuMOFs, CTMG, and hUMSC-Exos, hUMSC-Exos contain multiple active components (such as specific miRNAs and growth factors other than pro-angiogenic factors). The study did not delve into the independent biological effects of these individual components, and their exact mechanisms of action require further investigation. Furthermore, this study employed only SD rats for in vivo skin wound healing experiments, excluding female rats or adopting a mixed-sex experimental design. Given the current lack of gender diversity in animal models, future research should incorporate female rats or utilize mixed-sex models to address this gap, thereby providing more comprehensive evidence for the clinical application of composite sponges across diverse patient populations.

## Isolation and characterization of hUMSCs
After removing the amniotic membrane, umbilical vein, and umbilical artery, the remaining tissue containing Wharton’s jelly was processed as previously described. , The extracted material was cut into small pieces until a milky-white suspension was formed. ## Isolation and characterization of hUMSC-Exos
When the hUMSCs reached 70–80% fusion, they were rinsed with PBS and incubated in exosome-complete medium containing 10% FBS for 48 h. The average particle size of the obtained hUMSC-Exos was approximately 138.1 nm. The isolated hUMSC-Exos (purity ≥95.0%) were characterized using transmission electron microscopy. ## Synthesis and preparation of CuMOFs
CuMOFs were prepared using a mild solvothermal method. In a typical synthesis, 0.399 g of 1.8 mM Cu(NO3)2·3H2O and 0.831 g of 3.4 mM benzene-1,3,5-tricarboxylic acid (BTC) were dissolved in 18 mL of deionized water (DW) and ethanol (EtOH). The mixed solution was stirred under magnetic stirring for 30 min and then transferred to a polytetrafluoroethylene reactor at 120°C for 12 h. The reaction was performed in a polytetrafluoroethylene reactor. After the reaction, the product was obtained by centrifugal washing three times at 4000 rpm with EtOH/DW (1:1, v/v). Finally, CuMOFs crystal powder was obtained by vacuum drying. BTC served as the organic ligand, and the synthesis was carried out using a mixed solvent system of DW and EtOH. Based on particle size distribution analysis, the synthesized CuMOFs nanoparticles exhibited a size range of 200–450 nm.

## Synthesis and preparation of CuMOFs/CTMG
2 mL of 1% glacial acetic acid was added to 200 mL of deionized water, and the mixture was stirred. Chitosan (12.4 mmol) was added, and the mixture was stirred until completely dissolved. Then, 14.1 mmol of 1-ethyl-(3-dimethyl aminopropyl) carbodiimide hydrochloride (EDC) and 12.7 mmol of N-hydroxysuccinimide (NHS) were added and stirred in the chitosan solution for 30 min to induce crosslinking. Then, 33.5 mmol of Tris (hydroxymethyl) methyl glycine (TMG) was added to the mixture, and an amount of CuMOFs was added and stirred uniformly to maintain the concentration of CuMOFs at 125 μg/mL. The mixture was stirred for 48 h at room temperature. The mixture was then placed in a 3500 MWCO cellulose dialysis bag and dialyzed in deionized water for 4 days to remove unreacted oligomers. Post-freeze-drying was performed after dialysis to obtain CuMOFs/CTMG composite sponges.

## Enzymatic degradability of CuMOFs/CTMG
The degradation activity of the CuMOFs/CTMG composite sponges was evaluated using a colorimetric assay established by Miller (1959), which was quantitatively analyzed by measuring the color intensity of reducing sugars in the DNS reagent reaction system. The procedure was as follows: A gel sponge (50 mg) was weighed, mixed with 2 mL of phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, pH 7.0), and 0.5 mL of lysozyme working solution (100 U/mL) was added. The mixture was then incubated for 24 h at 37°C. The gel sponge was then incubated with 0.5 mL of lysozyme working solution (100 U/mL), and then incubated with 0.5 mL of lysozyme working solution (100 U/mL) for 24 h. At the end of the incubation period, 1.5 mL of DNS colorant was added immediately to terminate the enzymatic reaction, and the reaction system was heated in a boiling water bath for 15 min. After cooling the samples to room temperature, the absorbance of the solution was measured at 575 nm using a spectrophotometer. An equal volume of PBS without lysozyme was used as a blank control, and the control samples were prepared using the same procedure. Three parallel experiments were performed for each group.

## In vivo hemostatic properties of CuMOFs/CTMG
The hemostatic properties of CuMOFs/CTMG were evaluated in the SD rat model of traumatic hepatic hemorrhage. The rats were anesthetized by inhalation of isoflurane and fixed on a surgical fixation plate inclined at 30°. A 10-mm-long, 5-mm-deep wound was incised in the parenchyma of the left lobe of the rat liver under aseptic conditions. Immediately, 50 mg of CS and various concentrations of CuMOFs/CTMG composite sponges dressings were precisely applied to the wound, and slight pressure was applied until hemostasis was achieved. When no active bleeding was observed, the dressing was removed, the weighed filter paper with adsorbed blood was collected, and the weight gain of the filter paper was measured using an electronic balance to quantify blood loss. A blank control group (no dressing) and a positive control group (a commercially available medical hemostatic sponge) were established, and all operations were performed according to the same surgical procedure. Bleeding was calculated using the following formula, where B denotes blood loss (g), Mpost is the mass of the filter paper after hemostasis (g), and Mpre is the mass of the filter paper before hemostasis (g). (Equation 7)B=Mpost−Mpre

## Antibacterial activity of CuMOFs/CTMG/exos
The antibacterial activities of CuMOFs/CTMG and CuMOFs/CTMG/Exos composite sponges against E. coli and S. aureus were evaluated using the direct contact method. 200 μL of the bacterial suspension at a concentration of 106 CFU/mL was evenly spread on the surface of LB agar medium. The sterilized cylindrical sponge samples (6 mm in diameter and 1 mm in thickness) were aseptically manipulated to cover the center of the inoculated area. The samples were incubated in a constant-temperature incubator for 24 h at 37°C. At the end of the incubation period, the diameter of the transparent inhibition ring around the sample was observed and recorded.

## Cytocompatibility of CuMOFs/CTMG/exos
The cytocompatibility of the materials was evaluated using fluorescence staining of live/dead cells, as well as a cell proliferation assay. HUVECs were inoculated in culture plates at a density of 1.5 × 104 cells/cm2 and co-cultured with sterilized sponge scaffolds for 3 days. At the end of the culture period, the scaffolds were gently rinsed with PBS to remove any unadhered cells. The Calcein-AM/PI Fluorescence Staining Kit was used to specifically label live (calcein-labeled, green fluorescence) and dead (propidium iodide-labeled, red fluorescence) cells, and the cell survival status was observed using inverted fluorescence microscopy. To assess cell proliferation, a Calcein-AM/PI solution was added to the wells at 1 and 3 days of culture, according to the instructions for the Calcein-AM/PI kit. After incubation, the formazan product was dissolved, and absorbance was measured at 450 nm using an enzyme marker. 

## In vitro antioxidant capacity of CuMOFs/CTMG/exos
The antioxidant capacities of CuMOFs/CTMG and CuMOFs/CTMG/Exos extracts (final concentration of CuMOFs 125 μg/mL) were evaluated using the DPPH radical scavenging assay. The reaction was carried out in a 96-well plate with 200 μL of DPPH solution mixed proportionally with the sample extracts. The mixture was then incubated for 30 min at room temperature, protected from light. At the end of the reaction, the absorbance was measured at 517 nm using a UV-Vis spectrophotometer, and the free radical scavenging (IP) was determined using the following equation:(Equation 8)IP=(1−ODsample/ODcontrol)×100%

## ROS scavenging ability of CuMOFs/CTMG/exos
The ROS-scavenging ability of the composite dressings was assessed using the DCFH-DA fluorescent probe method. HUVECs were inoculated in 24-well plates at a density of 1 × 105 cells/mL. After the cells were attached to the wall, complete medium containing sample extracts (final volume, 1 mL) was added, and the cells were incubated for 24 h at 37°C in a 5% CO2 incubator. Subsequently, the medium was discarded, and the cells were washed twice with serum-free DMEM. Oxidative stress was then stimulated by the addition of medium containing 0.3 mM H2O2, followed by incubation for 30 min at 37°C. After removing the stimulating solution, the cells were incubated with medium containing the sample extracts (final volume 1 mL) in the wells. After removing the stimulating solution, 10 μM DCFH-DA staining solution (dissolved in serum-free DMEM) was added to the wells and incubated for 20 min at 37°C under light protection. Immediately after terminating the staining, the cell fluorescence signal (excitation wavelength 488 nm, emission wavelength 525 nm) was observed, and images were captured using an inverted fluorescence microscope. Blank control cells were cultured in complete medium only, without sample extract treatment or H2O2 stimulation.

## Exosomal release of CuMOFs/CTMG/exos
BCA protein quantification was used to assess the slow release of exosomes. 200 μL of CuMOFs/CTMG hydrogel precursor solution was mixed with 25 μg of exosome solution, and the mixture was inoculated into 24-well plates (with three parallel wells in each group). The plates were then incubated at 37°C in a constant temperature incubator. After hydrogel formation, 200 μL of PBS buffer (pH 7.4) was added to each well and incubated at 37°C. Supernatants were collected on days 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, and 7, and the protein concentrations were determined using a BCA kit. The culture system was maintained by replenishing it with an equal volume of fresh PBS. The analysis was performed using Origin.

## Scratch experiments with CuMOFs/CTMG/exos
An in vitro scratch healing assay was used to evaluate the effects of CuMOFs/CTMG and CuMOFs/CTMG/Exos composite sponges on the migration of HUVECs. HUVECs were inoculated into 6-well plates at a density of 1×105 cells/cm2. When the cell monolayer fusion reached 90%, the bottom surface of the plate was uniformly scraped with a sterilized 1 mL pipette tip to form a cell-free gap of uniform width. The cells were then gently rinsed with PBS three times to remove the detached cells, replaced with low sugar DMEM medium containing 1% FBS (100 μg/mL of sample extract was added to the experimental group, and only the same amount of solvent was added to the control group), and placed in an incubator at 37°C with 5% CO2 for further incubation. At 0, 24, 48, and 72 h after scratch formation, the same field of view was photographed under an inverted phase-contrast microscope, and the width of the scratched area was measured using ImageJ software.

## Transwell experiments with CuMOFs/CTMG/exos
The ability of CuMOFs/CTMG and CuMOFs/CTMG/Exos composite sponges to induce vertical migration of HUVECs was assessed. Transwell chambers were placed in 6-well plates, and 1 mL of complete medium (DMEM, 10% FBS) containing 10 mg/mL sponge scaffolds was added to the lower chambers. The upper chamber was inoculated with 1×104 HUVECs cell suspension (200 μL serum-free DMEM). The Transwell chambers were carefully removed, and the surface of the filter membrane was gently rinsed with PBS. Non-migrating cells in the upper chamber were then wiped away with a cotton swab. Migrating cells on the lower surface of the membrane were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min, stained with 0.5% crystal violet solution for 20 min, rinsed with water, and counted using an inverted microscope. The mean values were used for comparisons between the groups.

## In vitro Matrigel tube formation assay using CuMOFs/CTMG/exos
The ability of CuMOFs/CTMG and CuMOFs/CTMG/Exos composite sponges to induce angiogenesis in HUVECs was evaluated. First, 200 μL of matrix gel (Matrigel, pre-cooled to 4°C) was evenly spread on the bottom of the 24-well plate wells and incubated at 37°C for 30 min to allow complete gelation. HUVECs were resuspended in 500 μL of complete DMEM containing 10% FBS at a density of 6×104 cells/well and inoculated on the gelatinized Matrigel surface. In the experimental group, 100 μg/mL of sponge scaffold extract was added to the upper medium. In the negative control group, an equal amount of complete medium without the scaffold extract was added. Three parallel wells were established in each group. The plates were incubated at 37°C in a 5% CO2 incubator for 12 h. Images of each well were captured using an inverted phase-contrast microscope and analyzed using ImageJ, a software for image processing.

## Whole skin defect modeling and treatment
To establish a full-thickness skin defect model, 6-8 week-old male SD rats (weighing 180–220 g) were anesthetized by inhalation. Hair at the surgical site on the back was shaved using a razor, and residual short hairs were removed with depilatory cream. After disinfecting the surgical area with a 5% povidone-iodine solution, a full-thickness skin defect was created using a 1 cm diameter circular punch. Rats were randomly assigned to three groups: (i) blank control group (no intervention), (ii) CuMOFs/CTMG sponge scaffold group, and (iii) CuMOFs/CTMG/Exos sponge scaffold group. Sponge materials were secured flush with the wound edges using gauze bandages. Wound area measurements were performed at days 0, 7, and 14 post-surgery using ImageJ image processing software.(Equation 9)Woundclosurerate=[(A0−At)/A0]×100%

## Assessment of histology and inflammation of CuMOFs/CTMG/exos
On postoperative days 7 and 14, the rats were euthanized, and skin tissue samples were collected. The specimens were fixed in a 4% paraformaldehyde solution for 24 h, then subjected to serial concentrations of ethanol dehydration, paraffin embedding, and sectioning at a thickness of 5 μm. On postoperative days 14 and 21, the tissue sections were stained with H&E to observe the process of regenerative epithelialization, and collagen deposition in the wound area was detected using Masson’s trichrome staining. For the evaluation of inflammatory response, immunohistochemical staining with antibodies specific to IL-6 and TNF-α was applied to the tissue sections on days 7 and 14 postoperatively, and after DAB color development and hematoxylin re-staining, the average optical density value (IOD/area) of positively stained areas was analyzed using ImageJ software to detect the expression level of inflammatory factors. 

## Angiogenesis assessment of CuMOFs/CTMG/exos
On postoperative day 14, the rats were euthanized, and their skin samples were collected and fixed using 4% paraformaldehyde for 24 h, followed by graded ethanol dehydration, paraffin embedding, and sectioning (5 μm thickness). The angiogenic status of the wound area was assessed using CD31 immunohistochemical (IHC) staining, where primary antibodies were incubated overnight at 4°C, followed by staining with the corresponding secondary antibodies. The average density (IOD/area) of CD31-stained positive areas was analyzed using ImageJ software to assess the effect of CuMOFs/CTMG/Exos on angiogenesis. 

## Quantification and statistical analysis
All statistical analyses were performed using Origin 2021 (OriginLab Corporation, Northampton, MA, USA), ImageJ (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA), and Avantage 6.9 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) software. Independent samples t-tests were used for comparisons between two groups, while one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was employed for comparisons among multiple groups. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05 (denoted as ∗p < 0.05, ∗∗p < 0.01, ∗∗∗p < 0.001; ∗∗∗∗p < 0.0001). The meaning of n values is defined according to the type of experiment: in animal experiments, n represents the number of SD rats per group; in cell and in vitro experiments, n represents the number of biological replicates or independent experimental replicates. Specific n values are provided in the corresponding figure legends and the “ ” section. All data are expressed as “mean ± standard deviation (SD)”. All statistical details (including test types, definitions of n, and measures of dispersion) can be found in the relevant figure legends and “ ” descriptions.
