## Abstract
Cervical cancer is one of the leading causes of cancer-related death among women worldwide, and high-risk human papillomavirus (HR-HPV) plays a crucial role in its development. HPV’s oncogenic processes include the viral oncoproteins E6 and E7, which interfere with essential biological processes, causing DNA instability and uncontrolled cell growth. Recent research suggests that microRNAs (miRNAs) have a role in HPV-mediated tumor development, with dysregulation of particular miRNAs influencing cancer cell proliferation, immune escape, and therapy resistance. This review summarizes the most recent research on HPV’s molecular interactions with host miRNAs, focusing on their functions in regulating tumor-suppressive genes and oncogenic mechanisms. Furthermore, we investigate HPV-induced epigenetic alterations that contribute to miRNA dysregulation and corresponding changes in cell cycle control, apoptosis, and metastasis. Discovering these molecular interactions provides fresh insights into personalized medicine techniques for CC detection and therapy.

## Introduction

## Cervical cancer
Cervical cancer (CC) originates from cells situated at the squamocolumnar junction of the cervix, which is the region where squamous and columnar cells converge [ ]. As of 2020, it is the fourth most commonly diagnosed cancer and the fourth leading cause of cancer-related deaths among women worldwide. Approximately 84% of CC cases and 88% of related deaths occur in countries with a low Human Development Index (HDI) [ ]. Additionally, the incidence of CC in countries with a low HDI is three times higher than in countries with a very high HDI [ ]. Studies conducted in Iran indicate that the annual incidence of CC is 45 cases per one million people, underscoring the importance of this disease among cancer patients in the country [ ]. Since the identification of human papillomavirus (HPV) in the early 1980s, the majority of CC cases have been associated with HPV infection.
Although CC is largely preventable, it continues to pose a significant health challenge globally, primarily due to limited access to screening programs and HPV vaccinations in various regions [ ]. The disease can be identified in its early, precancerous stages through routine Pap smears and HPV testing, facilitating effective early intervention. However, because symptoms typically only manifest once the cancer has progressed, preventive measures are essential [ ]. CC screening is one of the most cost-effective strategies for the early detection of the disease, and it can prevent deaths from CC. The World Health Organization initiated a comprehensive global strategy in November 2020 aimed at eliminating CC as a public health concern [ ]. This strategic framework established specific targets for 2030, including HPV vaccination for 90% of girls before age 15, high-precision screening for 70% of women at ages 35 and 45, and ensuring that 90% of women diagnosed with precancerous lesions or invasive CC receive proper treatment and care at national levels [ ]. In 2021, WHO enhanced its screening and treatment guidelines for cervical precancerous lesions, with particular attention to women living with HIV, specifically addressing optimal timing for initial screening. The organization emphasizes robust quality assurance in screening programs both nationally and globally, requiring systematic data collection to evaluate standardized processes, performance metrics, and impact indicators [ ]. While HPV infection is common and often resolves without causing harm, certain strains can lead to cellular changes in the cervix that, if left untreated, may progress to cancer over time. Advancements in treatment options, including surgery, radiation, and chemotherapy, have improved outcomes for many women diagnosed with CC. However, the greatest hope for reducing the burden of the disease lies in prevention through widespread HPV vaccination and regular screening efforts [ ]. Preventing CC, like other chronic diseases, requires a framework for identifying the components of healthy behavior that can improve overall health [ ].

## Human papillomavirus (HPV) infections
HPVs are characterized as compact, non-enveloped DNA viruses measuring approximately 60 nm across, belonging to the Papillomaviridae family [ ]. Their structure comprises 72 pentameric capsomers that protect an enclosed circular DNA genome. The viral genetic material is organized into eight distinct open reading frames, encoding two primary groups of genes: early genes (E1, E2, E4, E5, E6, and E7) and late genes (L1 and L2), along with a regulatory long control region (LCR). These genes produce various proteins with functions ranging from basic structural components to complex cellular transformation factors. Within cervical tissue, the expression pattern shows spatial organization: early genes are primarily active in the basal, suprabasal, and intermediate cellular layers, while late genes, responsible for capsid protein production, become active in the uppermost apical layers. The early proteins are essential for viral replication and are associated with carcinogenesis linked to high-risk HPVs. L1 serves as the primary capsid protein, while L2 functions as a minor structural protein that contributes to capsomer formation. The L1 protein naturally assembles into virus-like particles, which serve as the foundation for current HPV vaccines [ ]. Among the core viral proteins, E1 (a helicase) and E2 (a DNA-binding protein) are significant for their roles in genome replication and maintenance [ ]. E2 connects the viral genome to the host chromosomes, facilitating its distribution to daughter cells. Furthermore, E1 and E2 encode proteins that regulate the functions of the E6 and E7 genes [ , ]. The shorter E8^E2 splice variant in the basal epithelium seems to suppress viral replication and maintain a low viral copy number, aiding the virus in evading detection by the immune system [ , ]. The E4 gene encodes a protein with a largely unknown function, though it is likely involved in the virion release [ ]. The HPV E5 protein functions as an accessory oncoprotein [ ] that promotes cancer development through its hydrophobic properties, primarily by interfering with peptide presentation to CD8 + cytotoxic T lymphocytes, thereby contributing to cellular immortalization [ ]. The viral oncoproteins E6 and E7 function by suppressing crucial tumor suppressors - p53 and retinoblastoma protein (pRB) respectively - which are vital regulators of cellular proliferation [ ]. These viral proteins engage with numerous host cellular factors to suppress differentiation, enhance DNA synthesis, and suppress immune responses [ ]. Research has demonstrated that both E6 and E7 proteins are crucial for CC persistence and progression [ ].
When a virion attaches to a cell receptor, the initial viral infection occurs. Papillomaviruses infiltrate squamous and mesenchymal-epithelial cells as well as keratinocytes, through integrin cell receptors via phagocytosis (endocytosis) [ ]. The L2 protein play a significant role in transporting the viral genome to the nucleus. For the replication of viral DNA, an origin of replication on the DNA is essential. Viral proteins E1 and E2 bind in trans to this origin of replication. By forming a complex, these proteins create a bend in the origin region [ ]. Utilizing the helicase function of protein E1, the two DNA strands are separated, allowing the necessary enzymes for replication to attach to the site and initiate the replication process [ ]. In primary genital lesions, papillomavirus DNA primarily exists in episomal form, whereas in cancer cells and malignant tumors, integrated viral DNA, through non-homologous recombination, initiates cell transformation and drives progression toward malignancy [ , ]. HPV is directly linked to approximately 5% of all cancers [ ].
Currently, there is no fully effective drug available for treating HPV or its associated lesions, although clinical trials are ongoing. Most treatments concentrate on removing or destroying infected tissues. Most individuals will clear HPV-associated lesions within 1–2 years, indicating that the host immune system is capable of controlling HPV infection [ ]. Treatment options for managing HPV infections include cryotherapy, laser therapy, radiation, and surgical interventions. Additionally, substances such as salicylic acid, podophyllin, DNA synthesis inhibitors (e.g., 5-fluorouracil), cidofovir (a DNA virus inhibitor), and interferon α may also be utilized [ , ].

## MiRNA
MicroRNAs (miRNAs) are small, non-coding RNA sequences, approximately 22 nucleotides long, found in plants, animals, and certain viruses. They play crucial roles in RNA interference, post-transcriptional regulation, and gene expression by binding to the untranslated regions of target mRNA [ ]. While most miRNAs are located within the cell, some, referred to as circulating or extracellular miRNAs, are also present in the extracellular environment, including various biological fluids and cell culture media [ ]. Since miRNAs play a crucial role in the normal functioning of eukaryotic cells, any disruption in their regulation can lead to disease [ ]. Mutations in the seed region of these molecules or the deletion of specific miRNAs can result in genetic defects [ ]. The first human disease associated with miRNA mis-regulation was lymphocytic leukemia, followed by other B-cell malignancies [ ]. Many miRNAs are linked to cancer; those with increased expression associated with cancer are referred to as onco-miRs, while miRNAs that suppress cancer development are known as tumor suppressors-miRs [ ]. Additionally, miRNA expression profiles can be valuable for diagnosing various diseases, predicting disease progression, and determining prognosis. Investigating the effects of elevated miRNA expression in cells, tissues, and model organisms can help elucidate the mechanisms underlying biological processes and pathogenesis, potentially leading to miRNA-based therapeutic strategies [ ]. miRNA levels can be upregulated or downregulated in cancers due to genetic and epigenetic changes. Studies have demonstrated that miRNA expression levels are altered in CC cells integrated with HPV [ ].
To date, numerous studies have demonstrated the involvement of microRNAs (miRNAs) in cancer progression. This review specifically focuses on CC, emphasizing the influence of human papillomavirus (HPV) oncogenes on host microRNAs. Additionally, it will explore the mechanisms and key characteristics associated with CC.

## Human papillomavirus: historical overview, classification and associated malignancies

## Overview of HPV discovery and classification
In the early 1970s, physicians observed a significant increase in CC cases among sex workers. Researchers also discovered that women married to men whose former wives had died from CC faced a higher risk of developing the disease. Demographic studies established a link between CC and sexually transmitted infections. By the late 1970s, German scientists confirmed that HPV was the primary cause of CC (Table  ).
 Today, HPV is recognized as the most prevalent sexually transmitted infection worldwide [ , ]. Over the past century, more than 200 HPV genotypes have been identified and categorized into two categories based on their carcinogenic potential: low-risk (LR-HPVs) and high-risk (HRHPVs). As shown in Fig.  , low-risk types are associated with the development of warts, whereas high-risk types are linked to various cancers, including cervix, vagina, anus, vulva, penis, oropharynx, and head and neck cancers. HPV types 16 and 18 are the most carcinogenic strains and are primarily responsible for two-thirds of cancer development [ ]. Furthermore, substantial evidence indicates that HPV types 31, 33, 35, 39, 45, 51, 52, 56, 58, and 59 are also associated with the onset of CC [ ]. HPV types 6 and 11 are generally associated with benign conditions, such as genital warts, and are classified as low-risk [ ]. While infection with these cancer-causing HPV types is quite common, approximately 80% of cases are cleared by the immune system within three years, and only about 3% progress to CC within seven years. This suggests that although HPV is a necessary factor, it is not sufficient on its own to cause CC [ ].
 

## Epidemiological studies linking HPV to cervical and other cancers
Almost all CC biopsies have identified DNA from specific HPV strains. HPV is the primary cause of CC as confirmed by epidemiological studies [ ]. The recognition of the relationship between HPV and cancer has garnered significant attention, particularly after the identification of HPV types 16 and 18 in CC s and precancerous dysplastic lesions. These types have been shown to increase a woman’s susceptibility to cervical malignancy [ ].
Studies have also highlighted the association between HPV and various malignancies, including oropharyngeal, anal, vulvar, vaginal, penile, and head and neck cancers [ ]. Since the late 1970s, numerous epidemiological studies have established that persistent infection with high-risk HPV types is the primary cause of nearly all CCs. These studies consistently indicate that HPV DNA particularly types 16 and 18 has been detected in the majority of CC biopsies. Together, these two strains account for approximately 70% of all CC cases worldwide [ ]. The seminal study by Herrington et al. in 1999 confirmed that HPV DNA is present in 99.7% of CC cases globally, solidifying the viral etiology of this cancer [ ].
Oral HPV infection is acquired by oral-genital infection and can lead to persistent infection. Oropharyngeal cancer (OPC) is the eighth most common cancer among men in the United States. Several factors, including a high number of oral sex partners, variations in smoking and alcohol consumption, and low HPV vaccination rates, may increase the risk of oropharyngeal cancer, particularly among gay and bisexual men [ ]. In 2000, Gillison et al. provided early evidence of the association between HPV and OPC, demonstrating that HPV DNA, particularly type 16, was present in a significant proportion of these cancers. This finding has since been corroborated by numerous studies conducted worldwide [ ]. When detected early, HPV-16-associated oropharyngeal cancer has a high five-year survival rate. In high-income countries, the incidence of HPV-related OPC has been rising, particularly among men. This is believed to be linked to changes in sexual behaviors, including oral sex, which facilitates HPV transmission to the throat [ ]. Unlike CC, there is no screening program for OPC, making it harder to detect early [ ]. Research by Chaturvedi revealed a dramatic temporal shift in HPV prevalence within oropharyngeal cancers (OPC), independent of detection methodology. Statistical analysis showed that between 1988 and 2004, HPV-positive OPC cases increased by 225%, while HPV-negative cases showed a 50% decline. Based on these trends, researchers projected that HPV-positive OPC would exceed annual CC cases by 2020. The rising incidence and improved survival rates of OPC observed in the United States since 1984 have been directly linked to HPV infection [ ]. A subsequent investigation by Sallam et al. identified concerning knowledge gaps among medical students regarding HPV’s role in OPC, with clinical-year students showing limited understanding compared to their pre-clinical peers. This knowledge deficit spans multiple aspects of viral pathogenesis and disease manifestation, potentially compromising early detection and preventive efforts. The researchers emphasized the need for curriculum evaluation to address these educational shortcomings [ ].
The study demonstrated that the genetic deletion of the HPV E7 oncogene in the squamous cell carcinoma cell line (SCC2) resulted in significant tumor regression in vivo. However, the tumors eventually recurred, indicating that while E7 is essential for early tumor growth, it is not necessary for tumor maintenance once new mutations have been acquired. Whole exome sequencing (WES) revealed a variety of novel mutations in the relapsed tumors that were absent in the parental cell line. This finding suggests that the tumors evolve following the loss of E7, supporting a “hit-and-run” mechanism in which the initial HPV infection induces lasting genetic changes. They discovered that genes associated with cell cycle regulation were significantly altered in the recurrent E7-edited tumors. These mutations included single nucleotide variations and frameshift mutations, which disrupted essential signaling pathways such as PI3K-Akt, Wnt, JAK-STAT, Hippo, and p53. Their findings indicate that while HPV initially promotes the accumulation of mutations in oropharyngeal cancers, the loss of the E7 protein leads to a rapid acquisition of new mutations that drive continued cell division. This supports the notion that viral oncogenes may initiate cancer but are not essential for its ongoing growth once the tumor has adapted. Overall, the study offers valuable insights into the mechanisms underlying tumor recurrence in HPV-driven oropharyngeal cancers, emphasizing the significance of genetic mutations in sustaining tumor growth after the initial viral influence has diminished [ ].
HPV types 16 and 18 are also associated with a significant proportion of anal, vulvar, vaginal, and penile cancers. These cancers are relatively rarer compared to CC but are similarly linked to persistent HPV infection [ ]. Research has shown that 90% of anal cancers are attributable to HPV infection, predominantly type 16. This was confirmed in a study that evaluated anal cancer tissue samples from patients across multiple countries [ , ]. Guerendiain and colleagues emphasized the significance of HPV in anal cancer, demonstrating that both HPV status and viral load can serve as biomarkers in clinical settings. These biomarkers aid in assessing prognosis and tailoring treatment approaches for patients with anal cancer, potentially leading to more personalized care and improved patient outcomes [ ].
The study of Matsuo on metachronous vulvar, vaginal, and anal cancers after a CC diagnosis found that vaginal cancer had the highest 20-year cumulative incidence (0.57%), followed by vulvar cancer (0.33%) and anal cancer (0.16%). This indicates that vaginal cancer is the most common type of metachronous malignancy among CC survivors. The median time to diagnosis for these cancers varied, with vaginal cancer diagnosed at a median of 5.4 years, vulvar cancer at 6.5 years, and anal cancer at 13.5 years post- CC diagnosis. This highlights the delayed onset of these malignancies. There is a notable correlation between the histology of CC and metachronous cancers. If the CC had squamous histology, it was more likely that the metachronous cancer would also have squamous histology, suggesting a potential link through similar HPV strains [ ].
Head and neck malignancies encompass neoplasms originating in anatomical regions such as the oral cavity, oropharynx, larynx, hypopharynx, and nasosinusal tract [ ]. Numerous etiological factors contribute to the pathogenesis of these tumors, including the consumption of alcoholic beverages, tobacco use, inherited chromosomal instability, Fanconi anemia, and Bloom syndrome [ ]. Notably, there has been a significant increase in incidences associated with HPV infection, particularly affecting the cellular structures within the tonsillar crypts of both palatine and lingual tonsils [ ]. The mechanism of disease in the tonsils differs from that in the cervix; the tonsils are covered by nonkeratinizing squamous epithelium, while the crypts exhibit reticulated squamous epithelium, which possesses a porous architecture that facilitates the infiltration of HPV into the basal layer. Gelwan et al. elucidated that HPV is rarely detected in squamous cell carcinomas originating from non-tonsillar areas of the oropharynx. In fact, squamous cell carcinomas of the soft palate are closer to those found in the oral cavity than those in oropharyngeal regions that include tonsillar tissue [ ].
The extensive epidemiological evidence linking HPV to cervical and other cancers underscores the virus’s role as a major public health threat. Widespread vaccination, regular screening (such as Pap smears and HPV testing for CC), and public education about HPV transmission and prevention are critical components in reducing the burden of HPV-associated cancers worldwide. The success of vaccination programs has already shown promise in reducing the incidence of cervical pre-cancers, and ongoing efforts to expand these programs globally are expected to lead to even more significant reductions in cancer incidence in the future.

## Pathophysiology of CC: critical role of human papillomavirus

## Overview of pathophysiology
The uterine cervix contains a specialized region known as the transformation zone, where two distinct epithelial cell populations converge. Within the endocervical region, malignant transformation of glandular epithelium leads to the development of adenocarcinoma, representing a minority of CC cases (10–20%), though its frequency has shown an upward trend in recent years. The more common variant, squamous cell carcinoma, develops from the squamous epithelial cells and constitutes approximately 80–90% of diagnosed cases. During its initial phases, this malignancy often progresses without noticeable symptoms. However, as the disease advances, patients may experience several clinical manifestations, including discomfort in the pelvic region, dyspareunia, irregular vaginal bleeding, and abnormal discharge [ ]. The underlying disease mechanisms primarily involve disruption of normal cellular processes, largely attributed to viral pathogens, particularly HPV. The complex pathophysiology encompasses alterations in the cervical microenvironment, modifications in immune system responses, and chromosomal alterations leading to genomic instability (Table  ) [ , ].
 HPV infections frequently present without obvious clinical manifestations, though they can develop into various pathological conditions ranging from genital warts to malignant transformations [ ]. The progression from initial infection to more severe conditions - specifically from LSIL (low-grade squamous intraepithelial lesions) to HSIL (high-grade squamous intraepithelial lesions) and potentially to invasive cervical carcinoma - involves multiple mechanisms, including host immune system suppression, persistent viral infection, and the incorporation of viral genetic material into the host genome [ ].
The E6 and E7 oncogenes of HPV play a critical role in transforming cervical epithelial cells into cancerous cells [ ]. These oncogenes interfere with the function of tumor suppressor proteins, specifically p53 and retinoblastoma (Rb), leading to uncontrolled cell proliferation and ultimately resulting in cancer development. Their activity is essential for maintaining the malignant characteristics associated with CC [ ]. Research has also identified several risk factors that increase the likelihood of persistent HPV infection. These factors include early initiation of sexual activity, having multiple sexual partners, prolonged use of oral contraceptives, smoking, and immunosuppression, particularly about HIV infection. The presence of these risk factors can significantly elevate the risk of HPV-induced cervical carcinogenesis [ , ]. Additionally, matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs) play a crucial role in tumor invasion and metastasis by degrading the extracellular matrix [ ]. Oxidative stress and environmental factors further exacerbate the disease, highlighting the complexity of CC pathogenesis [ ]. Despite advancements in understanding CC’s pathophysiology, challenges remain in addressing the multifactorial nature of its etiology, particularly the interplay of various viral infections and their cumulative effects on cancer development.

## Mechanisms of HPV oncogenesis: E6 and E7 oncogenes and their effects on cell cycle regulation
HPV oncogenesis primarily involves the integration of viral DNA into the host genome, resulting in the expression of the oncogenes E6 and E7. HPV is the leading cause of CC, as its E6 and E7 oncoproteins inactivate tumor suppressor genes, particularly p53 and pRB. These tumor suppressor genes are crucial for regulating the cell cycle and apoptosis [ , ]. The integration process often results in the loss of viral polyadenylation signals, necessitating the use of host signals for the stabilization of oncogenic transcripts [ ]. Additionally, HPV proteins interact with host cellular pathways, such as the NRF2 pathway, promoting genomic instability and resistance to apoptosis [ ]. This multifaceted approach to oncogenesis highlights the complex interplay between viral and host factors in cancer development. Other viral infections, such as Epstein-Barr virus (EBV) and Hepatitis B virus (HBV), may also contribute to oncogenesis through mechanisms like immune evasion and chronic inflammation [ ]. Genetic mutations and epigenetic modifications play crucial roles in cervical carcinogenesis, promoting genomic instability and malignant transformation. Dysregulation of microRNAs, such as miR-28-5p, has been linked to tumor progression [ ].
E6 interacts with the E3 ubiquitin ligase E6AP, forming a complex that ubiquitinates p53, leading to its degradation and subsequent loss of function in apoptosis and cell cycle regulation (Figure. ) [ , ]. This interaction is pivotal in the oncogenic process, particularly in high-risk HPV types. The degradation of p53 prevents it from activating apoptotic pathways, allowing cells to evade programmed cell death and impairing the cell’s ability to respond to genomic stress. This allows the accumulation of mutations and genomic instability, promoting cancer development [ ]. E6 also alters p53’s acetylation, further inhibiting its transcriptional activity and apoptotic function [ ]. Studies show that silencing E6 expression in CC cells leads to increased p53 levels and enhanced apoptosis, suggesting a potential therapeutic strategy [ ]. Developing inhibitors that disrupt the E6-E6AP-p53 complex could restore p53 function and promote cancer cell death [ ]. Conversely, while E6’s role in promoting cell survival is clear, some studies suggest that the presence of p53 isoforms may still induce apoptosis under certain conditions, indicating a complex interplay between E6 and p53 that could be exploited for therapeutic benefits [ ].
 E7 targets the pRb, another key tumor suppressor involved in regulating the G1/S transition in the cell cycle. By binding to pRb, E7 disrupts its ability to control the activity of E2F transcription factors, leading to uncontrolled entry into the S phase and increased cell proliferation. The loss of pRb function also contributes to genomic instability, further driving oncogenesis [ ]. E7 expression has been linked to resistance against CDK4/6 inhibitors, which are designed to halt cell cycle progression. Inhibition of E7 enhances the efficacy of these drugs, suggesting a potential therapeutic strategy [ , ]. Ansari et al.‘s study demonstrates that organosulfur compounds Diallyl Sulfide (DAS) and Diallyl Disulfide (DADS) inhibit proliferation and induce apoptosis in HPV16+ CC cells. Both compounds reduce cell viability, increase ROS production, and cause G0/G1 cell cycle arrest phase by downregulating cyclin D1 and CDK4 and upregulating cyclin-dependent kinase inhibitors p21WAF1/CIP1 and p27KIP1. These results highlight their potential as therapeutic agents for HPV-related CC [ ].
Cardona-Mendoza et al. demonstrated that natural extracts from Schisandra chinensis and Pueraria lobata primarily contain lignan and isoflavone compounds, which are likely responsible for inhibiting HPV E6 oncogene expression and significantly increasing the levels of p53, E2F-1, and Bcl-2 in HPV-18 positive HeLa cells. Additionally, these natural extracts tend to elevate p53 protein levels and induce cell cycle arrest in the G1/S phase in HeLa cells [ ]. The transfection of HeLa cells with anti-E6 and anti-E7 siRNA led to a moderate reduction in the expression of several key genes, including CDC25C, GRB2, GTSE1, and PLK1. In contrast, CDKN1A (p21 CIP) mRNA levels significantly increased, particularly following treatment with a combination of anti-E6 and anti-E7 siRNA. This suggests that silencing these oncogenes can affect the expression of genes critical for cell cycle regulation and apoptosis in CC cells. A recent study’s findings support the development of therapeutic strategies that use synthetic siRNA molecules targeting the HPV E6 and E7 genes. This approach has the potential to improve HPV-related CC treatment by modulating the expression of key genes involved in the cell cycle and apoptosis [ ].

## Genetic and epigenetic alterations induced by HPV in CC cells
Epigenetic modifications encompass DNA methylation, histone modifications, and the activity of non-coding RNAs (ncRNAs), with DNA methylation and histone acetylation being particularly significant. The interplay between DNA methylation and histone modifications is integral to determining chromatin architecture and significantly impacts gene expression [ ]. A highly conserved protein assembly known as the nucleosome remodeling and deacetylation (NuRD) complex exhibits dual functionality through its ATP-dependent chromatin remodeling and histone deacetylase capabilities [ ]. The complex’s architecture incorporates seven distinct components: the primary histone deacetylases HDAC1 and HDAC2, along with RbAp46 and RbAp48 which function as histone-binding elements. Additional components include members of the metastasis-associated protein family (MTA1, or alternatively MTA2/MTA3), methyl-CpG-binding domain proteins (either MBD3 or MBD2), and members of the chromodomain-helicase-DNA-binding protein family, specifically CHD3 (Mi-2α) or CHD4 (Mi-2β). While both MBD2 and MBD3 facilitate NuRD complex recruitment, they serve distinct purposes: MBD2 specifically targets methylated DNA sequences, while MBD3 preferentially interacts with regions containing 5-hydroxymethylcytosine (5hmC) [ ]. Studies have revealed that HDAC2 establishes a connection with viral protein E7 through Mi2β, contributing to cellular transformation processes. This interaction carries particular significance as it affects gene expression patterns and contributes to HPV-associated oncogenic mechanisms [ ].
E7 oncogene has been demonstrated to influence host epigenetic modifications, particularly via its interactions with host DNA methyltransferase 1 (DNMT1). This interaction augments the methylation activity of DNMT1, resulting in the hypermethylation of particular target gene promoters. This process represents a significant mechanism underlying HPV-associated carcinogenesis [ ]. High-risk HPV (hrHPV)-induced carcinogenesis is characterized by a significant increase in DNA methylation levels. This increase typically begins at the pre-tumorigenic stage and reaches its peak during the cancer stage. Among hrHPV-positive samples, approximately 75% of cervical intraepithelial neoplasia grade 3 (CIN3) and 50% of CIN2 samples exhibited this cancer-like high methylation pattern. This observation suggests that not all CIN2/3 lesions will progress to cancer, as only a subset is likely to do so over time. Monitoring DNA methylation patterns could serve as a valuable tool for assessing the risk of progression in cervical pre-cancer lesions. This approach could lead to more tailored screening and treatment strategies for women with hrHPV infections [ ]. The expression of the genes C1QBP, BCAP31, CDKN2A, and PTMS, which are involved in cell proliferation and immune response, is diminished due to E7-mediated hypermethylation and may play a important role in the progression of CC. This underscores the necessity for further research on these genes to enhance diagnostic and treatment strategies. Additionally, transcription factors such as GABPA, SP1, and ELK1 may regulate these genes, potentially forming a complex activated by E7 that contributes to gene promoter hypermethylation [ ].
PI3-kinase/PKB/AKT signaling pathway is engaged in regulating key biological functions, including proliferation, migration, and anchorage-independent growth, which are vital for sustaining transformed phenotypes in HPV-infected cells. The role of PIK3CA and PI3-kinase signaling highlights their potential as therapeutic targets for HPV-induced carcinomas. Therefore, these PIK3CA and the PI3-kinase/PKB/AKT pathways induced new opportunities for treatment in individual with HPV-related cancers, as these pathways are essential for the transformation and maintenance of malignant phenotypes [ ].

## MiRNA: biogenesis and function

## History and function
MicroRNAs (miRNAs) were first discovered in 1993 in Caenorhabditis elegans, when researchers observed a small RNA molecule, known as lin-4, that regulated gene expression by binding to the messenger RNA (mRNA) of another gene, lin-14, and inhibiting its translation. This discovery was significant because it revealed a previously unknown mechanism of gene regulation [ ]. In 2000, a second miRNA, let-7, was identified, and it was soon found to have homologs across various species, including humans, suggesting that miRNAs played a conserved and essential role in biological processes. By the early 2000s, the field rapidly expanded as high-throughput sequencing and bioinformatics made it easier to identify miRNAs in many organisms. Today, thousands of miRNAs have been identified across various species, and they are recognized as critical regulators in gene expression, with extensive implications in both health and disease [ ].
MicroRNAs are about 20–24 nucleotides long, which regulate gene expression at the post-transcriptional level. They accomplish this by binding to complementary sequences in the 3’ untranslated regions (UTRs) of target mRNAs. Once bound, miRNAs inhibit the translation of the mRNA into protein or promote its degradation. This mechanism enables microRNAs to control the expression of numerous genes, with a single miRNA able to target multiple mRNAs, and conversely, a single mRNA being regulated by multiple miRNAs [ ]. MiRNAs play essential roles in developmental processes by guiding cell differentiation and tissue formation [ ]. Additionally, miRNAs are crucial for maintaining a balance between cell growth and apoptosis, making them central to cancer biology, where their misregulation can lead to tumorigenesis [ ]. Furthermore, they assist cells in adapting to environmental changes by modulating gene expression in response to stressors such as hypoxia and oxidative stress [ ]. Dysregulated miRNAs has been involved in a variety of diseases, such as cancer [ ], cardiovascular conditions [ ], and neurological disorders. Given their regulatory functions, miRNAs are currently being investigated as potential therapeutic targets and diagnostic biomarkers. The discovery and functional exploration of miRNAs have significantly enhanced our understanding of gene regulation, highlighting their essential role in cellular homeostasis and a wide array of physiological and pathological processes [ ].

## Mechanisms of MicroRNA biogenesis and their regulation in CC
The formation of mature microRNAs involves a complex, highly regulated cascade of events that operates precisely in normal cells but can become disrupted in malignancies, including CC [ , ]. The process begins in the nuclear compartment with RNA polymerase II or III transcribing miRNA genes to generate primary miRNAs (pri-miRNAs) containing characteristic hairpin structures. These pri-miRNAs undergo initial processing by the Drosha-DGCR8 (DiGeorge Syndrome Critical Region 8) complex, yielding shorter hairpin structures known as precursor miRNAs (pre-miRNAs) [ ]. Following nuclear processing, Exportin-5 facilitates the transport of pre-miRNAs to the cytoplasm, where the Dicer enzyme complex further modifies them into 20–24 nucleotide double-stranded RNA molecules. Subsequently, the RNA duplex separates, with the guide strand becoming incorporated into the RNA-induced silencing complex (RISC) alongside Argonaute proteins, while the passenger strand typically undergoes degradation. The mature miRNA, now part of RISC, guides the complex to matching sequences within the 3’ untranslated regions (UTRs) of target messenger RNAs, leading to either translational repression or mRNA degradation, depending on the extent of sequence complementarity [ ].
In CC, several factors can dysregulate miRNA biogenesis [ ]. HrHPV oncoproteins, specifically E6 and E7, can interfere with the normal processing of miRNAs. For instance, HPV E6 has been shown to downregulate the tumor suppressor miRNA miR-34a, which is known to influence cell cycle and apoptosis [ ]. Additionally, aberrant expression of Dicer and Drosha is often observed in CC, leading to altered miRNA expression profiles that contribute to cervical carcinogenesis by promoting cell proliferation, reducing apoptosis, and enhancing metastasis [ ]. The interaction between lncRNAs, miRNAs, and key signaling pathways (such as PI3K/Akt, Wnt/β-catenin, and MEK/ERK) are important for realization the molecular mechanisms underlying CC. Disruptions in these pathways can lead to cancer progression, emphasizing the need for further research in this area [ ].

MicroRNA-93-5p (miR-93-5p) has emerged as a crucial regulatory molecule in cancer development, residing within the MCM7 gene’s intronic region on chromosome 7q22.1 as part of the miR-106b ~ 25 cluster. The miR-34 family, consisting of miR-34a, miR-34b, and miR-34c, plays a crucial role in various physiological and pathological processes, particularly in cancer development and progression. Among these, miR-34a stands out due to its ubiquitous expression in normal human tissues, in contrast to the tissue-specific expression patterns of miR-34b/c, which are predominantly found in testicles, fallopian tubes, lungs, and brain [ ]. The expression of miR-34a is tightly regulated by the tumor suppressor p53, forming a critical component of the p53 network. Upon activation, p53 directly induces the transcription of miR-34a, which in turn reinforces p53 activity, creating a positive feedback loop [ ]. Notably, miR-34a expression is frequently lost or significantly decreased across a wide spectrum of cancers, underscoring its potential tumor-suppressive function [ – ]. This downregulation can occur through various mechanisms, including epigenetic silencing via CpG island methylation, genetic alterations, or dysregulation of upstream regulators such as p53 [ ]. The tumor-suppressive effects of miR-34a are mediated by its targeting of multiple oncogenes and key regulators of cell cycle progression, apoptosis, and stemness. Well-established targets include cell cycle regulators (CDK4, CDK6, Cyclin D1, and E2F3), anti-apoptotic proteins (BCL2 and SIRT1), stemness factors (CD44 and Notch1), and EMT inducers (SNAIL and ZEB1) [ – ].
Studies in thyroid cancer have revealed miR-34a as a crucial regulator, showing diminished expression in cancerous tissue. Research has established that the long non-coding RNA XIST, which shows elevated levels in thyroid cancer, can target and regulate miR-34a. This regulatory relationship influences MET oncogene expression and subsequently affects the PI3K/AKT signaling cascade, which is fundamental to cancer development [ ]. Beyond thyroid cancer, miR-34a demonstrates significant functions in CRC, where it acts as a p53-responsive element. In the CRC context, miR-34a participates in a sophisticated regulatory circuit involving IL-6, STAT3, and IL-6R, creating a feedback mechanism that controls epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT), invasive behavior, and metastatic spread in CRC and other malignancies [ ]. These findings underscore miR-34a’s complex and diverse roles in regulating cancer-associated cellular mechanisms. Furthermore, miR-34a has emerged as a significant player in extrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma (EHCC), a rare and aggressive malignancy of the biliary system. In EHCC, miR-34a expression levels correlate with patients’ migration and invasion characteristics, indicating its involvement in disease progression. Mechanistically, miR-34a targets Smad4, a key component of the TGF-beta signaling pathway, thereby influencing EMT and subsequently modulating tumor invasion and migration [ ]. Given its broad tumor-suppressive functions, miR-34a has emerged as a promising candidate for cancer therapy. MRX34, a liposomal miR-34a mimic, became the first microRNA-based therapy to enter clinical trials for cancer treatment [ ].
The role of miR-34a in HPV-positive CC has emerged as a critical area of investigation, revealing complex regulatory networks that influence tumor progression, therapeutic responses, and metabolic reprogramming. Recent studies have demonstrated that miR-34a expression is significantly downregulated in CC tissues and cell lines, with levels inversely correlating with lesion severity. This reduction is mechanistically linked to HPV infection, specifically through the action of the viral E6 oncoprotein, which destabilizes p53, a known transcriptional regulator of miR-34a. One key pathway involves the regulation of E2F3 and survivin. MiR-34a directly focuses on E2F3 that leads to its downregulation and subsequent reduction in survivin expression. In vitro experiments using HPV-18-positive HeLa and HPV-16-positive SiHa cell lines showed that miR-34a overexpression significantly decreased cell viability and colony formation, while its downregulation had opposite effects. The miR-34a-E2F3-survivin axis plays a crucial role in modulating cell invasion, with E2F3 overexpression enhancing invasion, an effect reversible by miR-34a in cells expressing wild-type E2F3 but not in those with mutant E2F3 lacking the miR-34a binding site [ ].
Another significant target of miR-34a in CC is lactate dehydrogenase A (LDHA), a key enzyme in the glycolytic pathway. MiR-34a suppression of LDHA attenuates the Warburg effect, characterized by increased glucose consumption and lactate production. Overexpression of miR-34a or knockdown of LDHA in HPV-positive CC cells (HeLa and SiHa) decreased lactate production, glucose consumption, colony formation ability, and invasive capacity. These effects could be rescued by reintroduction of LDHA, indicating that miR-34a suppresses the Warburg effect and tumor progression primarily through targeting LDHA. In conclusion, this study elucidates a novel mechanism in CC pathogenesis, centered on the miR-34a/LDHA axis. The HPV E6-mediated disruption of p53 signaling leads to dysregulation of this axis, consequently promoting the Warburg effect. This metabolic reprogramming, in turn, significantly contributes to enhanced tumor growth and invasion [ ]. Recent findings have revealed a more complex picture of miR-34a expression in HPV-associated cervical lesions and cancer. A study found that miR-34a expression was significantly increased in normal cervical cells infected with HPV compared to uninfected cells. This initial increase may be due to activation of cellular repair mechanisms and p53 pathways in response to viral infection. Interestingly, no significant differences in miR-34a expression were observed in LSIL or HSIL lesions compared to normal uninfected cells. However, miR-34a levels were significantly increased in invasive cervical carcinoma samples [ ]. This unexpected increase in advanced lesions contrasts with some previous studies showing decreased miR-34a in CC tissues and cell lines. The discrepancy suggests there may be p53-independent pathways activating miR-34a expression in invasive carcinomas that require further investigation.
Research has examined microRNA-34a’s therapeutic potential in relation to 5-aminolevulinic acid photodynamic therapy (ALA-PDT) for CC treatment. Experimental data demonstrates that ALA-PDT treatment leads to increased HMGB1 levels while reducing miR-34a expression in CC tissues, particularly in HPV-positive cell lines (HeLa, SiHa, CaSki), but not in HPV-negative C33A cells. This selective response pattern suggests HPV infection’s importance in determining CC cell sensitivity to ALA-PDT treatment. At the molecular level, miR-34a directly regulates HMGB1 through interaction with its 3’UTR region. ALA-PDT treatment enhances HMGB1 secretion via exosomes by suppressing miR-34a expression. This interaction between miR-34a and HMGB1 appears to be fundamental to ALA-PDT’s therapeutic effectiveness. Laboratory studies utilizing co-culture techniques have shown that exosomes containing HMGB1, derived from ALA-PDT-treated HPV-positive cancer cells, enhance dendritic cell maturation and stimulate inflammatory cytokine production. These effects were reversed by miR-34a mimic transfection, highlighting the importance of the miR-34a/HMGB1 axis in modulating anti-tumor immunity. In vivo experiments using tumor-bearing mouse models confirmed that ALA-PDT inhibited tumor growth and prolonged survival specifically in mice with HPV-positive tumors, an effect reversed by miR-34a mimic or HMGB1 knockdown [ ].
The high-risk HPV E6 oncoprotein influences multiple cell cycle regulatory pathways, disrupting both cell cycle arrest and programmed cell death in host cells following viral-induced DNA damage. Studies have shown that E6 protein enhances Cdt2 stability through recruitment of the nuclear deubiquitinase USP46, a process essential for the survival and proliferation of HR HPV-infected neoplastic cells in both laboratory and animal models [ ]. Within the CRL4 E3 ubiquitin ligase complex, Cdt2 serves as an adaptor protein and plays a crucial role in degrading cell cycle regulators including p21, Cdt1, and Set8, thereby facilitating cell cycle progression through G1/S and S phases into M phase [ ]. When E6 is silenced using siRNA technology, USP46 exits the nucleus, triggering Cdt2 degradation through polyubiquitination, ultimately leading to cell death in these cancerous cells [ ]. Scientific evidence indicates that microRNA-34a promotes Cdt2 destabilization in HPV-positive CC cells by inhibiting E6 protein function. This Cdt2 destabilization affects the regulation of p21 and Set8, which function as pro-apoptotic and tumor-suppressor proteins. As a tumor suppressor, miR-34a shows reduced expression across various cancers, including CC. Studies have revealed that altered miR-34a expression induces cell cycle arrest at G1/S and G2/M phases in CC cells [ ]. Furthermore, enhanced miR-34a levels reduce invasion and migration of cervical carcinoma cells by inhibiting proliferation and promoting cellular aging []. Collectively, these findings illuminate the central role of miR-34a in HPV-positive CC pathogenesis and treatment response. The diverse functions of miR-34a, ranging from regulation of cell cycle and apoptosis to modulation of tumor metabolism and immune responses, position it as a promising therapeutic target. The elucidation of the miR-34a-E2F3-survivin and miR-34a-LDHA axes, as well as the miR-34a/HMGB1 pathway in ALA-PDT response, provides multiple avenues for potential therapeutic interventions. Future research may focus on developing strategies to restore miR-34a expression or inhibit its downstream effectors as novel approaches for CC treatment, particularly given that miR-34a mimics have already entered clinical trials for other cancers.
MiR-375 is a microRNA that has garnered significant attention in recent years due to its pivotal role in various physiological processes and its implications in multiple pathological conditions, particularly in cancer. MiR-375 has emerged as a critical regulator in cervical carcinogenesis, with its expression and functions intricately linked to HPV infection. This reduction appears to be mechanistically connected to HPV infection, particularly through E6 and E7 viral oncoprotein activity. The molecular mechanism involves a sophisticated feedback loop connecting E6, TAp63β, and Dicer. When miR-375 is experimentally upregulated, it inhibits E6 protein expression, which subsequently leads to TAp63β level restoration. TAp63β then influences Dicer transcription, ultimately enhancing the processing of mature miR-375. This regulatory relationship has been validated in HPV-positive CC cell lines, specifically HeLa and SiHa cells. Functional studies using wound-healing assays have confirmed that these molecular changes result in decreased cellular migration capacity [ ].

In vitro experiments using HPV-16-positive SiHa and HPV-18-positive HeLa cell lines demonstrated that miR-375 overexpression significantly enhanced radiosensitivity, reducing cell survival and increasing apoptosis under radiation treatment. Mechanistic studies have identified UBE3A (E6AP) as a direct target of miR-375, with the miR-375-UBE3A axis modulating radiosensitivity through the p53 pathway. MiR-375 overexpression or UBE3A knockdown leads to increased p53 levels and enhanced radiation-induced apoptosis, affecting the expression of downstream targets such as p21, BAX, and survivin. This effect was reversed by overexpression of UBE3A lacking the miR-375 binding site [ ]. Empirical studies have demonstrated that miR-375 mimetics considerably impede the proliferation, invasion, and migratory capabilities of CC cells, whereas its inhibition yields contrasting outcomes. AEG-1 is implicated in numerous characteristics of metastatic cancer, including cellular proliferation, survival during chemotherapy, enhanced migratory potential, and invasiveness [ ].

Further research by Wang et al. revealed that miR-375 contributes to docetaxel resistance through its regulatory effects on SEC23A and YAP1 expression in prostate cancer [ ]. The clinical implications of miR-375 in CC are significant, with its expression levels correlating with disease progression and potentially serving as a prognostic biomarker. The restoration of miR-375 expression or targeting of its downstream effectors presents promising therapeutic strategies, potentially enhancing the efficacy of current treatments, including chemotherapy and radiotherapy, by modulating cellular sensitivity and resistance mechanisms.

Mir-21 has emerged as a critical regulator in oncogenesis, with its overexpression documented across a wide spectrum of malignancies. Located on chromosome 17q23.2, within the coding gene TMEM49, miR-21 is one of the most consistently upregulated microRNAs in human cancers [ ]. Its multifaceted role encompasses various hallmarks of cancer, including sustained proliferative signaling, evasion of growth suppressors, resistance to cell death, and activation of invasion and metastasis [ ]. The oncogenic potential of miR-21 is primarily attributed to its ability to target and downregulate numerous tumor suppressor genes. Among its well-established targets are programmed cell death 4 (PDCD4), PTEN, and tropomyosin 1 (TPM1) [ ]. Through the suppression of these genes, miR-21 promotes cell proliferation, inhibits apoptosis, and enhances invasiveness. For instance, in breast cancer, miR-21-mediated downregulation of PDCD4 has been shown to increase invasion and metastasis, correlating with poor prognosis [ ]. Recent studies have expanded our understanding of miR-21’s role beyond its direct effects on cancer cells. In hepatocellular carcinoma, elevated miR-21 levels in tumor-associated macrophages have been associated with increased tumor growth and metastasis [ ]. Furthermore, miR-21 has been implicated in the regulation of cancer-associated fibroblasts, contributing to the creation of a pro-tumorigenic stromal environment [ ]. The clinical significance of miR-21 extends to its potential as a biomarker and therapeutic target. Numerous studies have demonstrated correlations between elevated miR-21 expression and poor clinical outcomes across various cancer types. In NSCLC, high miR-21 levels have been associated with decreased overall survival and increased resistance to platinum-based chemotherapy [ ]. Similarly, in CRC, miR-21 overexpression has been linked to advanced tumor stage and lymph node metastasis [ ]. Efforts to therapeutically target miR-21 have shown promise in preclinical models. Antisense oligonucleotides and locked nucleic acid (LNA)-modified anti-miR-21 have demonstrated efficacy in reducing tumor growth and metastasis in CRC and melanoma cancer cells [ , ]. However, translating these findings to clinical applications remains challenging, primarily due to issues related to delivery and potential off-target effects. Recent advancements in the field have focused on combination therapies involving miR-21 inhibition. For instance, co-targeting miR-21 and the doxorubicin has shown additive cytotoxic effects on tumor cells [ ]. Additionally, the role of miR-21 in modulating immune responses has sparked interest in combining miR-21 inhibition with immunotherapies, although this approach requires further investigation [ ].
In the landscape of HPV-associated CC, miR-21 has emerged as a pivotal regulator of radioresistance, offering new insights into treatment response variability. The mechanistic underpinnings of miR-21-mediated radioresistance in HPV-positive CC cells have been elucidated through rigorous in vitro studies. Upregulation of miR-21 in radioresistant CC tissues correlates with enhanced colony formation and suppressed apoptosis in HPV-positive cell lines following radiation exposure. At the molecular level, miR-21 overexpression attenuates radiation-induced G2/M cell cycle arrest while promoting S phase accumulation, suggesting a multifaceted role in cell cycle regulation and survival. Central to miR-21’s influence on radiosensitivity is its targeting of LATS1. The miR-21-LATS1 axis emerges as a key regulatory mechanism, modulating apoptosis and cell cycle distribution in response to radiation. This finding builds upon earlier observations of miR-21’s oncogenic role in enhancing cell growth and suppressing tumor-inhibiting factors like PDCD4 in CC cells, painting a more comprehensive picture of its impact on cellular homeostasis and treatment response [ , ]. Expression profiling of miR-21 across various stages of cervical neoplasia has revealed intriguing patterns. Significant upregulation is observed in CC samples compared to HPV-negative controls, with a trend towards increased expression in more advanced stages of disease. Although not reaching statistical significance, likely due to sample size limitations, this pattern suggests a potential role for miR-21 in disease progression and as a marker of advanced disease. Interestingly, the dysregulation of miR-21 is detectable in HPV-positive samples even before overt cancerous changes, hinting at its involvement in early carcinogenesis. This observation is particularly noteworthy when considered alongside the concurrent downregulation of tumor-suppressive miRNAs like miR-29a in CC samples. The reciprocal expression patterns of oncogenic and tumor-suppressive miRNAs underscore the complex regulatory networks governing CC development and progression [ ].
Mir-21 has been identified as a prominently expressed miRNA in CC, where it acts as an oncogenic factor by modulating various cellular processes, including cell proliferation, blood vessel formation, immune system responses, resistance to programmed cell death, invasion, and metastatic spread [ ]. Conversely, studies have shown that reduced levels of miR-29a enhance angiogenesis, immune responses, and apoptotic processes during the progression of CC [ ]. Research has revealed that both these microRNAs show altered expression patterns in cancerous tissues and HPV-positive cases, suggesting their potential application as early biomarkers for CC detection [ ]. Research conducted by Jia and colleagues has revealed that both miR-21 and miR-29a demonstrate considerable sensitivity and specificity in CC diagnosis [ ]. Additionally, Park and associates evaluated miR-21 expression in early-stage CC specimens using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis, yielding an area under the curve (AUC) of 72, which indicates its potential value as a diagnostic marker [ ]. Through ROC curve analysis, Shi proposed that miR-21 holds considerable promise as a universal biomarker for various cancers [ ]. Similarly, Wang et al. assessed the diagnostic value of miR-29a in liver metastasis originating from CRC and confirmed its potential as a novel biomarker for this condition [ ]. These findings collectively highlight the multifaceted roles of miR-21 in CC pathogenesis and treatment response. The elucidation of the miR-21-LATS1 regulatory axis in radioresistance, coupled with the observed expression patterns across different stages of cervical neoplasia, positions miR-21 as a promising therapeutic target and potential biomarker for improving radiotherapy outcomes. Ultimately, the growing body of evidence surrounding miR-21’s role in CC opens new avenues for translational research. The development of miRNA-based therapeutics and diagnostic tools could potentially revolutionize the management of HPV-associated CC, offering more personalized and effective treatment strategies. As our understanding of these complex regulatory networks deepens, the promise of improved outcomes for patients with this challenging malignancy grows ever more tangible.
Mir-124 is a highly conserved and abundantly expressed microRNA in the nervous system, playing crucial roles in neuronal development and function. However, recent studies have unveiled its significant involvement in various cancers, including CC. Located on three genomic loci (8p23.1, 8q12.3, and 20q13.33), miR-124 is frequently downregulated in multiple malignancies, suggesting its potential tumor suppressor function [ , ]. In the context of cancer biology, miR-124 has been shown to regulate key cellular processes such as cell proliferation, apoptosis, migration, and invasion. Its tumor-suppressive effects are primarily mediated through the targeting of oncogenes and cell cycle regulators. Notable targets include CDK6 (cyclin-dependent kinase 6), ROCK1 (Rho-associated protein kinase 1), and EZH2 (enhancer of zeste homolog 2), all of which are involved in promoting cancer progression [ – ]. The epigenetic silencing of miR-124 through promoter hypermethylation is a common event in various cancers, including CC. This silencing leads to the derepression of its target genes, contributing to oncogenesis [ ]. Studies have revealed that microRNA-124 (miR-124) exhibits notably reduced expression patterns in CRC tissue when compared to surrounding healthy colorectal tissue. Research has demonstrated that this microRNA functions through a specific molecular mechanism, where it regulates STAT3 expression via direct interaction with the 3′-untranslated region (3′-UTR) of the STAT3 gene. Experimental studies have shown that when miR-124 levels are artificially elevated, it triggers two significant anti-tumor effects: enhanced cell death in CRC cells and diminished tumor development, both in laboratory cultures and animal models [ , ]. Recent studies have expanded our understanding of miR-124’s role beyond direct effects on cancer cells. Emerging evidence suggests that miR-124 may modulate the tumor microenvironment and influence immune responses. In glioblastoma, a particularly aggressive form of brain cancer, upregulation of miR-124 has demonstrated significant immunomodulatory effects. Specifically, miR-124 has been shown to inhibit STAT3 signaling, a pathway crucial for tumor progression and immune evasion. Moreover, miR-124 overexpression was found to counteract the immunosuppressive effects of glioblastoma cancer stem cells (gCSCs). This was evidenced by the reversal of gCSC-mediated inhibition of T-cell proliferation and the reduction in the induction of forkhead box P3 (Foxp3) + regulatory T cells (Tregs), which are known to suppress anti-tumor immune responses [ ]. The clinical significance of miR-124 extends to its potential as a diagnostic and prognostic biomarker. Several studies have demonstrated that miR-124 levels in tissue or circulating biofluids can distinguish between normal and cancerous states, and may predict patient outcomes [ ]. In breast cancer, lower miR-124 expression has been correlated with increased risk of lymph node metastasis and decreased overall survival [ ]. Therapeutically, restoring miR-124 expression or function represents a promising approach in cancer treatment. Review studies about miR-124 have reported encouraging potentional in reducing tumor growth and metastasis [ , ]. However, as with other microRNA-based therapies, challenges related to delivery and potential off-target effects need to be addressed before clinical translation.
Mir-124 has emerged as a crucial tumor suppressor in CC, particularly in the context of high-risk human papillomavirus (HR-HPV)-positive malignancies. Recent studies have uncovered complex regulatory networks involving miR-124, revealing its multifaceted role in cervical carcinogenesis and progression. Expression analyses have consistently demonstrated significant downregulation of miR-124 in CC tissues and cell lines compared to normal counterparts. This reduced expression has been attributed, at least in part, to epigenetic silencing through promoter hypermethylation, a common event in cervical carcinogenesis [ , ]. Functional studies have elucidated the tumor-suppressive mechanisms of miR-124 in CC. Overexpression of miR-124 in CC cell lines, including HeLa and SiHa, has been shown to markedly inhibit cell proliferation, migration, and invasion while promoting apoptosis [ ]. Moreover, miR-124 has been implicated in the suppression of epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT), a critical process in cancer metastasis. This is evidenced by increased expression of epithelial markers (E-cadherin and β-catenin) and decreased expression of mesenchymal markers (N-cadherin, twist, vimentin) and metastasis-related molecules (MMP-9) upon miR-124 overexpression [ ]. The molecular mechanisms underlying miR-124’s tumor-suppressive effects in CC have been the subject of intense investigation. Several studies have identified key targets of miR-124, including astrocyte-elevated gene-1 (AEG-1) and growth factor receptor-bound protein 2 (GRB2) [ , ]. AEG-1, in particular, has emerged as a critical mediator of miR-124’s effects. A negative correlation between miR-124 and AEG-1 expression has been observed in CC specimens, and luciferase reporter assays have confirmed direct targeting of AEG-1 by miR-124. Importantly, rescue experiments demonstrated that AEG-1 overexpression partially reversed the inhibitory effects of miR-124 on cell proliferation, migration, invasion, and EMT in CC cells [ ].
Recent research has also unveiled a complex interplay between miR-124 and long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs) in CC. Liu et al. identified a reciprocal regulatory relationship between miR-124 and the lncRNA MALAT1, where knockdown of MALAT1 increased miR-124 expression, while overexpression of miR-124 decreased MALAT1 levels [ ]. The tumor-suppressive effects of miR-124 extend beyond direct regulation of its target genes. miR-124 has been shown to modulate key signaling pathways implicated in CC progression, including the NF-κB and Wnt/β-catenin pathways [ , ]. Furthermore, miR-124 overexpression has been associated with alterations in the expression of AEG-1 downstream factors such as AKR1C2 and NF1, suggesting a broader impact on gene regulatory networks [ ]. In conclusion, the accumulating evidence firmly establishes miR-124 as a critical tumor suppressor in CC. Its downregulation appears to be a common event in cervical carcinogenesis, contributing to enhanced proliferation, invasion, and metastasis. The identification of key targets such as AEG-1 and the elucidation of complex regulatory networks involving lncRNAs provide valuable insights into the molecular mechanisms underlying CC progression. Future studies should focus on translating these insights into clinical applications, exploring the potential of miR-124 mimics or targeting its downstream effectors as novel therapeutic strategies in CC.
Mir-148a-3p is a well-characterized regulatory microRNA that has emerged as a crucial player in various cancers, including CC, CRC, and bladder cancer. This microRNA belongs to the miR-148/152 family and is located on chromosome 7p15.2 [ , , ]. Initially identified as a tumor suppressor, miR-148a-3p has been shown to regulate multiple cellular processes including cell proliferation, apoptosis, invasion, and metastasis [ ].
Detailed molecular studies have illuminated the interactions between miR-148a-3p and HPV16 infection in CC pathogenesis. Scientists have discovered that HPV16’s viral proteins E5, E6, and E7 work in concert to reduce miR-148a-3p expression within CC cells. This regulatory mechanism has been confirmed across multiple experimental models, particularly in CaSki and SiHa cell lines. When researchers employed RNA interference to silence HPV16 E6/E7 oncogenes, they observed substantial enhancement of miR-148a-3p expression in both cell populations, suggesting direct regulatory control by these viral oncoproteins. Quantitative analysis revealed that suppression of E5 and E6/E7 led to expression reductions of 0.53-fold and 0.85-fold in CaSki cells, while SiHa cells showed decreases of 0.74-fold and 0.62-fold respectively, compared to control groups [ ]. Clinical studies have demonstrated a progressive decrease in miR-148a-3p expression correlating with advancing stages of CC. The expression levels show significant reduction across different FIGO stages, with more pronounced suppression observed in advanced stages of the disease. Quantitative analysis revealed that compared to HPV16-positive normal tissues, miR-148a-3p expression decreased to 0.73-fold in stage IA, 0.67-fold in stage IB, 0.56-fold in stage IIA, and 0.36-fold in stages IIB and III [ ]. The clinical implications of miR-148a-3p dysregulation in CC are significant. The consistent downregulation of this microRNA in HPV16-positive CC tissues, coupled with its responsiveness to viral oncoprotein expression, suggests its potential utility as a diagnostic biomarker for cervical carcinogenesis in HPV16-infected patients. Furthermore, the strong correlation between miR-148a-3p expression levels and disease stage indicates its potential value in monitoring disease progression and prognosis, particularly in patients with HPV16-positive CC [ ].
Mir-22 is a member of the small non-coding RNA family, which is transcribed from the MIR22HG gene located on chromosome 17p13.3 This highly conserved regulatory molecule, approximately 21–23 nucleotides in length, participates in post-transcriptional regulation of gene expression through RNA silencing mechanisms. Research has revealed that miR-22 exhibits widespread tissue distribution and demonstrates significant involvement in diverse cellular regulatory pathways [ ]. As a prominent tumor suppressor, this microRNA exercises its regulatory control by interfering with multiple oncogenic signaling cascades [ ]. Its regulatory mechanisms encompass several critical cellular pathways, including the PTEN/AKT signaling axis and epigenetic modification through HDAC4 interaction. These molecular interactions ultimately influence fundamental cellular behaviors such as programmed cell death, cellular multiplication, and invasive capabilities [ ]. Its expression is regulated by transcription factors including p53, and its dysregulation has been implicated in various pathological conditions [ ].
Mir-22 has emerged as a crucial tumor suppressor in cervical carcinogenesis, with its expression patterns showing significant correlation with disease progression. Studies have demonstrated that miR-22 expression is markedly downregulated in high-grade squamous intraepithelial lesions (HSIL) and squamous cell carcinoma (SCC) compared to normal cervical tissues (p < 0.001) [ ]. The molecular mechanism underlying miR-22’s tumor-suppressive function involves a complex regulatory network centered on the E6/p53/miR-22/HDAC6 pathway. Research has revealed that miR-22 directly targets histone deacetylase 6 (HDAC6) through specific binding sites within its 3’UTR, establishing a post-transcriptional regulatory mechanism. This interaction was confirmed through luciferase reporter assays, which demonstrated a 60% decrease in luciferase activity in 293FT cells and a 40% decrease in C33A cells following miR-22 overexpression [ ]. The relationship between HPV infection and miR-22 expression has been particularly noteworthy. HPV16 E6 oncoprotein has been shown to suppress p53 protein levels, which subsequently leads to diminished miR-22 transcription, as miR-22 is a direct transcriptional target of p53. This suppression results in enhanced HDAC6 expression, contributing to CC development and progression. Quantitative analysis revealed that miR-22 expression levels were significantly lower in HSIL and SCC compared to normal cervical tissues [ ]. Deep sequencing analysis of breast cancer tissues led to the initial discovery of microRNA-3156-3p (miR-3156-3p) This microRNA has emerged as an important tumor suppressor in CC, particularly in relation to HPV infection. Research has demonstrated that miR-3156-3p shows markedly reduced expression in CC tissues positive for HPV16/18 when compared to both normal cervical tissue and HPV-negative CCs, indicating that high-risk HPV infection specifically influences its expression level [ ].
In CC, miR-3156-3p exhibits multiple tumor-suppressive functions. Experimental studies have demonstrated that overexpression of miR-3156-3p significantly inhibits cell proliferation and promotes apoptosis in various CC cell lines, including HPV18-positive HeLa cells and HPV16-positive SiHa and CaSki cells. Furthermore, miR-3156-3p has been shown to suppress cellular migration, invasion, and vascular mimicry formation [ ]. At the molecular level, miR-3156-3p directly targets SLC6A6 (TauT) by binding to its 3’-UTR, leading to post-transcriptional regulation. SLC6A6, a multi-pass membrane protein that functions as a taurine transporter, is significantly upregulated in CC tissues [ , ]. The protein has been implicated in promoting cell survival and chemoresistance in various cancers [ ]. Through downregulation of SLC6A6, miR-3156-3p may contribute to increased sensitivity to therapeutic interventions. The clinical significance of miR-3156-3p lies in its potential as both a diagnostic marker and therapeutic target [ ]. Moreover, the tumor-suppressive properties of miR-3156-3p make it an attractive candidate for therapeutic strategies in CC treatment.
Emerging technologies targeting miRNA regulation, CRISPR-based gene editing, and nanocarrier delivery systems are revolutionizing the treatment of HPV-driven cervical cancer. Nanotechnology, in particular, plays a pivotal role in cancer research and treatment. Its applications encompass targeted drug delivery, gene therapy, diagnostics, biomarker mapping, and molecular imaging. Nanoparticles can be engineered to deliver therapeutic agents directly to tumor cells, minimizing damage to healthy tissues and enhancing treatment efficacy. These approaches precisely target viral oncogenic mechanisms and cancer cell pathways, offering promising therapeutic advancements [ – ]. Studies highlight tumor-suppressive miRNAs (e.g., miR-125, miR-375) that inhibit angiogenesis (via VEGF) and oncogenic signaling (e.g., MELK pathway) in cervical cancer [ , ]. Key applications include miR-125 mimics, which reduce cancer cell migration by 40% in cellular models by suppressing the Hippo/STK4 pathway, and miR-20b inhibitors that neutralize HPV E6-induced miR-20b, doubling TIMP-2 expression and significantly reducing metastasis in vivo [ ]. CRISPR/Cas9-based gene editing directly targets HPV oncogenes E6/E7, showing transformative potential: in K14-HPV16 transgenic mice, CRISPR/Cas9 achieved 85% mutagenesis efficiency in E7, restored RB and p53 tumor suppressor proteins, and reduced tumor volume by 70% after 4 weeks [ ]. Polymer-based nanoparticles (PBAE) delivered CRISPR/shRNA systems, reducing E7 expression by 80% in HeLa cells with minimal cytotoxicity (< 5%) [ , ]. Nanotechnology enhances drug bioavailability and specificity, exemplified by PEGylated liposomes that boost siRNA delivery (anti-E7/MCL-1) by 60% in cervical epithelium, sustaining 72-hour release in vivo, and RGD-functionalized selenium nanoparticles that reduce Derlin1 expression by 75%, inducing apoptosis in HeLa cells. Hybrid systems co-delivering E6/E7-targeting siRNA and cisplatin inhibit tumor growth by 90% in vivo while restoring p53 and HLA-I expression [ , ]. These technologies—combining miRNA modulation, CRISPR editing, and precision nanocarriers—enable multi-targeted strategies to disrupt HPV oncogenesis and restore tumor suppression [ , , , ].
The molecular interaction between high-risk HPV and host miRNAs is central to the development and progression of cervical cancer. miRNA-based therapeutics as promising strategies for cervical cancer treatment. However, several challenges remain in the clinical development of miRNA therapeutics. One major issue is off-target effects, as miRNAs can regulate multiple transcripts with partial sequence complementarity, raising the risk of unintended gene silencing and associated toxicities [ ]. Delivery methods also pose significant obstacles, since naked miRNAs are unstable in biological fluids and have poor cellular uptake. Although nanocarrier systems such as PEGylated liposomes and polymer-based nanoparticles (e.g., PBAE-CRISPR/shRNA) have improved the stability and targeted delivery of miRNA therapeutics, they still face hurdles like non-specific accumulation in non-target tissues, immune activation, and endosomal entrapment [ , ]. Furthermore, regulatory concerns remain a barrier, as the long-term safety, immune responses, and tumor heterogeneity complicate the approval process for miRNA drugs. For example, early clinical trials with miR-34a mimics were halted due to severe immune-related adverse events [ ]. To address these challenges, researchers are developing chemically modified miRNAs, such as locked nucleic acids (LNAs), and advanced nanocarriers like RGD-functionalized selenium nanoparticles that enhance tumor targeting and minimize systemic exposure [ ]. Despite these advances, comprehensive preclinical validation and careful clinical trial design are essential to ensure the specificity, efficacy, and safety of miRNA-based therapies for HPV-associated cervical cancer.

## Conclusion
The interaction between HPV and host miRNAs is critical for the malignant transformation of cervical epithelial cells. HPV oncoproteins E6 and E7 cause miRNA dysregulation, which promotes carcinogenesis via immune escape, apoptosis resistance, and increased mitotic ability. HPV-induced epigenetic changes worsen these abnormalities, underlining the importance for more research into miRNA-targeted therapies. Recent advances in RNA-based technology, such as miRNA mimics and inhibitors, present significant opportunities for reversing HPV-mediated carcinogenesis. Furthermore, combining HPV vaccination with miRNA-based diagnostics could lead to earlier detection and better treatment outcomes. Additional studies need to concentrate on improving miRNA profiling tools and creating focused therapy options to reduce the global burden of CC. Novel therapies that bridge the gap between Molecular Research and therapeutic uses can be optimized for improved HPV-related cancer early detection and treatment.
