## Abstract

## Background
The NONO protein plays a crucial role in RNA metabolism and DNA repair. It undergoes various post-translational modifications, including phosphorylation, ubiquitination, acetylation and methylation, all of which regulate its diverse cellular functions. However, the role of O-GlcNAcylation in regulating NONO's function in DNA damage repair is not well understood.

## Results
This study demonstrates that O-GlcNAcylation of NONO at Serine 147 (Ser147) is essential for its recruitment to DNA damage sites. Specifically, O-GlcNAcylation at Ser147 reduces NONO ubiquitination and stabilizes its interaction with SFPQ, regulating the alternative splicing of the histone methyltransferase SETMAR. A deficiency in O-GlcNAcylation at Ser 147 impairs NONO's binding to SETMAR pre-mRNA, leading to an increased production of the truncated isoform of SETMAR (SETMAR-S). The resulting SETMAR-S suppresses the generation of H3K36me2 and inhibits the recruitment of Ku70 at DNA damage sites, ultimately impairing non-homologous end joining (NHEJ) repair. Furthermore, the disruption of O-GlcNAcylation at Ser147 sensitizes liver cancer cells to ionizing radiation treatment, both in vitro and in vivo.

## Conclusions
O-GlcNAcylation at Ser 147 of NONO mediates the alternative splicing of SETMAR and facilitates NHEJ repair. Collectively, our findings suggest that targeting NONO O-GlcNAcylation may provide a novel therapeutic strategy for cancer treatment.

## Background
Cells are constantly exposed to various threats, including metabolites, DNA replication errors, and ultraviolet (UV) and ionizing radiation (IR), all of which can cause different types of DNA damage [ ]. Among these, double-strand breaks (DSBs) represent the most deleterious form of DNA lesions [ – ]. Two primary repair pathways, non-homologous end joining (NHEJ) and homologous recombination (HR) are responsible for the repair of the majority of DSBs [ – ]. In human cells, NHEJ is the predominant repair pathway, resolving approximately 80% of all DSBs [ ]. During NHEJ, the Ku70/80 complex binds to the DNA ends of DSBs, forming a complex with DNA-dependent protein kinase (DNA-PKcs) [ , ], which facilitates the direct ligation of DNA ends [ , ]. Disruption of this process can lead to chromosomal instability and tumorigenesis [ ]. While DNA damage response (DDR) pathways are crucial for normal cell survival, cancer cells often exploit these mechanisms to resist damage induced by radiotherapy or chemotherapy, thereby contributing to radioresistance and chemoresistance. Enhanced DSB repair capacity is a critical factor in the radioresistance of cancer cell [ – ]. Therefore, identifying molecular targets within these pathways is essential for improving radiosensitivity in cancer patients.
NONO, a non-POU domain-containing octamer-binding protein, is a member of the human splicing protein DBHS (Drosophila behavior/human splicing) family [ ]. It plays a vital role in numerous cellular processes, including DNA damage repair, RNA splicing, transcriptional regulation, DNA unwinding, RNA nuclear retention, cell proliferation, and paraspeckle formation [ ]. NONO forms a complex with the splicing factor rich in proline/glutamine (SFPQ) and paraspeckle component 1 (PSPC1), contributing to RNA processing and gene expression regulation [ ]. Accumulating evidence underscores the significant role of NONO in tumorigenesis and disease progression. It functions as a PARP-1-dependent regulator of DSB repair, facilitating NHEJ and promoting cell survival following irradiation [ ]. Recent studies have also revealed that NONO acts as an attenuator of pre-mRNA synthesis and serves as a nucleolar detainer of nascent transcripts, thereby promoting NHEJ-mediated DSB repair [ ]. Furthermore, the NONO/SFPQ complex modulates the alternative splicing of SETMAR (histone methyltransferase) precursor mRNA, leading to the production of full-length SETMAR (SETMAR-L). Notably, SETMAR-L, but not the truncated SETMAR (SETMAR-S), facilitates the formation of H3K36me2 at DSBs sites and enhances the recruitment of early DNA repair factors, such as Ku70 and NBS1, thus facilitating NHEJ [ – ].
Post-translational modifications(PTMs) are crucial for the repair of DSBs [ , ]. Upon DSBs formation, a series of PTMs, including phosphorylation [ ], ubiquitination [ ], ADP-ribosylation [ ] and O-GlcNAcylation, are activated to regulate the functions of various DNA damage repair factors [ ]. Among these, O-GlcNAcylation is a dynamic and reversible modification mediated by a single pair of enzymes, O-GlcNAc transferase (OGT) and O-GlcNAcase (OGA). OGT adds O-GlcNAc to serine or threonine residues on target proteins, while OGA removes it [ – ]. By modulating protein stability, structure, and function, O-GlcNAcylation serves as a critical regulator of various cellular processes [ – ]. Our previous studies have shown that both OGT and OGA are rapidly recruited to DNA damage sites, where they mediate the reversible O-GlcNAcylation of repair-associated proteins [ , , ]. Notably, inhibition of OGT sensitizes HeLa cells to radiation [ , ], suggesting that O-GlcNAcylation plays a significant role in radioresistance by modulating DSB repair. Intriguingly, O-GlcNAcylation occurs on serine or threonine residues that can also be phosphorylated [ , ], indicating potential crosstalk between these modifications during DNA damage signaling [ , , ]. Furthermore, O-GlcNAcylation interacts with other PTMs, including acetylation [ , ], ubiquitination [ , ] and poly (ADP-ribosyl)ation (PARylation) [ , ], suggesting that it serves as an additional layer of regulation in DSB repair.
NONO undergoes several PTMs, including phosphorylation [ ], ubiquitination [ ], acetylation [ ] and methylation [ ], all of which regulate its diverse cellular functions. For instance, acetylation at Lys371 attenuates NONO’s recruitment to the IL-8 gene promoter, thereby modulating IL-8 expression [ ], while methylation within the coiled-coil domain impairs NONO’s binding to mRNAs, leading to nuclear retention of target transcripts [ ]. Additionally, NONO is ubiquitinated and degraded upon UV-induced DNA damage [ ]. While the role of NONO in DNA repair and its PTMs have been explored, the impact of O-GlcNAcylation on NONO and its function in DSB repair remain unknown. In this study, we demonstrate that NONO is O-GlcNAcylated at Ser147 in response to DNA damage. This modification promotes NONO’s recruitment to DSB sites and enhances its stability. Moreover, O-GlcNAcylation stabilizes the NONO-SFPQ complex, facilitating the alternative splicing of SETMAR to generate the SETMAR-L. SETMAR-L facilitates the formation of H3K36me2 at damage sites and enhances Ku70 binding, thereby promoting NHEJ repair. Importantly, blocking Ser147 O-GlcNAcylation sensitizes liver cancer cells to IR both in vitro and in vivo. Our findings unveil a mechanistic link between NONO O-GlcNAcylation and NHEJ efficiency, suggesting a potential therapeutic strategy to overcome radioresistance by targeting this modification.

## Results

## DNA damage enhances the association between NONO and OGT
Previous studies have demonstrated that DNA damage actively induces O-GlcNAcylation, highlighting the critical role of OGT-dependent O-GlcNAcylation in the cellular response to DNA damage [ , ]. We first investigated whether NONO undergoes O-GlcNAcylation in response to various DNA damaging agents. We found that NONO O-GlcNAcylation is highly responsive to multiple forms of DNA damage, including IR, UV light, methyl methanesulfonate (MMS), cisplatin, mitomycin C (MMC) and etoposide (Additional file 1: Fig. S1A). To assess the dose-dependent response of NONO O-GlcNAcylation to DNA damage, we conducted an IR dose escalation study. In cells exposed to increasing doses of IR, we observed a corresponding increase in NONO O-GlcNAcylation signals (Fig.  A). Time-course analyses indicated that NONO O-GlcNAcylation started immediately following 10 Gy of IR treatment, peaked at approximately 1 h, and subsequently declined, suggesting a dynamic process of O-GlcNAcylation in response to DNA damage (Additional file 1: Fig. S1B).
 Since O-GlcNAc transferase (OGT) is the sole enzyme responsible for O-GlcNAcylation, we next explored the interaction between OGT and NONO. Co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) assays using exogenously tagged proteins confirmed a physical interaction between NONO and OGT (Fig.  B, C). Furthermore, we investigated the endogenous interaction and observed that IR treatment significantly enhanced the association between NONO and OGT (Fig.  D). These results suggest that IR-induced DNA damage enhances the association of NONO and OGT.

## NONO O-GlcNAcylation promotes its protein stability
As NONO is recruited to DNA damage sites in response to DNA damage [ ], we investigated whether O-GlcNAcylation affects its recruitment using a laser microirradiation assay in U2OS cells, with and without treatment with the OGT inhibitor OSMI-1. To visualize NONO localization in living cells, we constructed a GFP-NONO fusion protein. The results demonstrated that NONO was rapidly recruited to DNA damage sites following localized DNA damage. Strikingly, inhibition of OGT nearly completely abolished NONO’s recruitment to DNA damage sites (Fig.  A). Next, we assessed the effect of O-GlcNAcylation on NONO’s chromatin-binding ability, with or without OSMI-1 treatment. SFB-tagged NONO was transfected into cells, followed by chromatin fractionation. Upon OGT inhibition, NONO’s chromatin binding was significantly impaired (Fig.  B). Previous studies have indicated that O-GlcNAcylation stabilizes protein substrates [ ]. Therefore, we assessed whether O-GlcNAcylation affects the expression levels of NONO protein. Western blot analysis revealed a time-dependent decrease in NONO protein levels upon OGT inhibition (Fig.  C). Furthermore, in the presence of cycloheximide (CHX) to block new protein synthesis, NONO degradation was markedly accelerated when O-GlcNAcylation was inhibited by OSMI-1 (Fig.  D). Collectively, these findings suggest that O-GlcNAcylation plays a critical role in NONO’s recruitment to DNA damage sites, its chromatin-binding capacity, and overall protein stability.
 

## Ser147 is a major DNA damage-induced O-GlcNAcylation site on NONO
To identify potential O-GlcNAcylation sites on NONO in response to DNA damage, we expressed SFB-tagged NONO in HEK293T cells and subjected them to IR. Subsequently, SFB-tagged proteins were immunoprecipitated using streptavidin beads, followed by SDS/PAGE separation and staining with Coomassie Brilliant Blue. NONO bands were excised and subsequently subjected to in-gel digestion and mass spectrometry analysis. This analysis identified two potential O-GlcNAcylation sites on NONO: Ser147 (S147) and Thr440 (T440) (Additional file 1: Fig. S2A, B). A recent study demonstrated that NONO O-GlcNAcylation at Thr440, located within the C-terminal disordered region, serves as a key regulator of paraspeckle formation and has been proposed as a candidate therapeutic target in colon cancer [ ]. To validate the major glycosylation site(s), we generated single-point mutants S147A and T440A, as well as a double mutant S147A/T440A (2A). Notably, both the S147A mutant and the double mutant exhibited a significant reduction in the O-GlcNAcylation signal compared to wild-type NONO, while the T440A mutation did not show a similar effect (Fig.  A). This indicates that Ser147 is a key site for IR-induced O-GlcNAcylation. Structurally, NONO contains two tandem RNA recognition motifs (RRM1 and RRM2), a conserved NONA/paraspeckle (NOPS) domain, and a C-terminal coiled-coil motif (Fig.  B). Given that O-GlcNAcylation at Thr440 has been implicated in modulating paraspeckle formation and cancer cell proliferation [ ], and considering that Ser147 resides within the RRMs—regions essential for NONO’s function, and is highly conserved across mammals, we focused our subsequent investigation on the role of Ser147 O-GlcNAcylation in DNA damage repair.
 

## O-GlcNAcylation at Ser147 promotes NHEJ-mediated DNA damage repair
We next assessed the recruitment of NONO-S147A mutant to DNA damage sites using laser micro-irradiation. Wild-type NONO was rapidly recruited to the laser-induced DNA damage sites, maintaining a strong signal for up to 30 min post-damage, while the recruitment of NONO-S147A mutant was significantly impaired (Fig.  C). The finding is consistent with previous observations following OGT inhibition, suggesting that the observed phenotype results from a deficiency in NONO O-GlcNAcylation. Given that O-GlcNAcylation might influence NONO protein stability, we further investigated the recruitment of both NONO-WT and the S147A mutant to DNA damage sites in the presence of the proteasome inhibitor MG132. Our results indicated that, even with MG132 treatment, the recruitment of the NONO-S147A mutant to DNA damage sites remained significantly impaired (Additional file 1: Fig. S2C). To exclude the possibility that these effects were due to the S147A mutation itself, we analyzed the secondary and three-dimensional structures of both wild-type NONO and the S147A mutant, finding no structural differences between them (Additional file 1: Fig. S3A-E). Previous studies have demonstrated that the recruitment of NONO to DNA damage sites is dependent on poly(ADP-ribose) (PAR) [ ]. We then investigated whether the abrogation of NONO Ser147 O-GlcNAcylation affects its binding to PAR. HEK293T cells were transfected with SFB-tagged NONO-WT or S147A, followed by treatment with IR. The cells were then collected and subjected to a dot blotting assay. Our results indicated that the S147A mutant exhibited reduced binding to PAR compared to wild-type NONO. Similar results were observed following OGT inhibition (Additional file 1: Fig. S4A), suggesting that the impaired recruitment of the S147A mutant to DNA damage sites is due to its diminished PAR-binding capacity rather than low protein stability. Interestingly, we found that the S147A mutant demonstrated a marked decrease in chromatin binding in the absence of MG132 treatment (Fig.  D). However, under MG132 treatment, the chromatin binding of the S147A mutant was restored to a level comparable to that of NONO-WT (Additional file 1: Fig. S4B). This suggests that the reduced chromatin-binding capacity of NONO-S147A in the absence of MG132 is more likely attributed to decreased protein stability rather than a direct consequence of O-GlcNAcylation. This finding is consistent with previous studies indicating that the recruitment of NONO to DNA damage sites depends on PAR signals but not its DNA binding ability. Collectively, these findings indicate that the O-GlcNAcylation of NONO at Ser147 is essential for facilitating its recruitment to DNA damage sites.
NONO plays a crucial role in promoting NHEJ-mediated DSB repair [ ]. To investigate the impact of NONO O-GlcNAcylation on NHEJ-mediated DSB repair, we generated HEK293T cell lines with stable NONO knockdown (Additional file 1: Fig. S4C) and reconstituted them with either shRNA-resistant SFB-tagged NONO wild-type (NONO-WT) or the S147A mutant. Following exposure to 10 Gy of IR, we assessed the kinetics of DSB repair using comet assays under neutral conditions. The knockdown of NONO significantly impaired DSB repair, which was fully restored by the reconstitution of NONO-WT but not by the S147A mutant (Fig.  E and Additional file 1: Fig. S4D). Additionally, using the NHEJ reporter assay, we observed a significant decrease in NHEJ repair efficiency upon NONO knockdown. Reintroduction of NONO-WT, but not the S147A mutant, effectively rescued NHEJ repair (Fig.  F and Additional file 1: Fig. S4E). We also exmianed whether NONO O-GlcNAcylation affects HR repair and found that the depletion of NONO or the disruption of O-GlcNAcylation does not impact HR repair (Additional file 1: Fig. S4F-G). These results underscore the importance of Ser147 O-GlcNAcylation in the regulation of NHEJ-mediated DSB repair.

## Ser147 O-GlcNAcylation of NONO antagonizes its ubiquitination by decreasing interaction with RNF8
Having observed the effects of OGT inhibition on NONO stability, we next investigated whether Ser147 O-GlcNAcylation promotes NONO stability. Consistently, our results indicated that the S147A mutant exhibited significantly lower protein levels compared to wild-type NONO. Notably, the proteasome inhibitor MG132 rescued the degradation of the S147A mutant (Fig.  A), suggesting that NONO Ser147 O-GlcNAcylation represses its degradation by modulating the ubiquitin–proteasome pathway. As expected, Western blot analysis showed that the degradation rate of the S147A mutant was much faster than that of wild-type NONO (Fig.  B). Next, we investigated the mechanism by which Ser147 O-GlcNAcylation of NONO promotes its stability. Previous studies have shown that RNF8, a key E3 ubiquitin ligase, targets NONO for degradation in response to UV damage [ ]. Therefore, we examined whether Ser147 O-GlcNAcylation of NONO has crosstalk with ubiquitination. To do so, wee transiently co-expressed SFB-tagged NONO or the S147A mutant with Myc-RNF8 and HA-Ub in cells, followed by an in vivo ubiquitination assay. After immunoprecipitating either wild-type NONO or the mutant, we analyzed HA-Ub conjugation via Western blotting using an anti-HA antibody. The S147A mutant exhibited increased ubiquitination compared to wild-type NONO (Fig.  C). To determine whether the observed changes in ubiquitination were due to altered interaction between NONO and RNF8, we tested the interaction between Myc-RNF8 and SFB-tagged NONO-WT or the S147A mutant through Co-IP assays. The S147A mutant showed a marked increase in binding to Myc-RNF8 compared to wild-type NONO (Fig.  D). These results suggest that O-GlcNAcylation of NONO at Ser147 antagonizes its ubiquitination, likely by decreasing its affinity for RNF8.
 

## O-GlcNAcylation of NONO modulates the alternative splicing of SETMAR pre-mRNA
NONO is known to directly interact with splicing factor proline/glutamine rich (SFPQ) to regulate the alternative splicing of SETMAR, a histone-lysine N-methyltransferase [ ]. NONO modulates exon skipping of SETMAR pre-mRNA primarily through its RRM2 domain, resulting in the production of two isoforms: full-length SETMAR (SETMAR-L) and a truncated form (SETMAR-S) [ ]. SETMAR-L exhibits higher NHEJ efficiency compared to SETMAR-S, as it is capable of recruiting NEHJ factors and enhancing repair process (Fig.  A). We first investigated the effect of NONO O-GlcNAcylation on its interaction with SFPQ. The S147A mutant displayed a weaker interaction with SFPQ compared to wild-type NONO (Fig.  B), suggesting that O-GlcNAcylation at Ser147 of NONO enhances its binding to SFPQ, potentially facilitating the alternative splicing of SETMAR. Next, to explore whether O-GlcNAcylation of NONO regulates the alternative splicing of SETMAR pre-mRNA, we performed RNA immunoprecipitation (RIP) assays. Our findings demonstrated that compared to the S147A mutant, wild-type NONO significantly favored the inclusion of SETMAR exon 2, leading to the production of the long isoform (Fig.  C). Moreover, RNA pull-down assays revealed that wild-type NONO bound more efficiently to biotin-tagged oligonucleotides (CAGGCAGG), a binding motif derived from the SETMAR intron sequence, than the S147A mutant (Fig.  D). Quantitative PCR (qPCR) analysis indicated that the S147A mutant significantly reduced the inclusion of exon 2 in SETMAR (SETMAR-L), while promoting its exclusion (SETMAR-S) (Fig.  E). Subsequently, we conducted RNA fluorescence in situ hybridization (RNA-FISH) to detect the expressions of SETMAR-L and SETMAR-S. The results revealed that reconstituting NONO-WT, but not the S147A mutant, in NONO-knockdown cells markedly increased the ratio of SETMAR-L to SETMAR-S (Fig.  F). Similarly, overxpression of NONO-WT, but not the S147A mutant, yielded comparable results, which is consistent with the qPCR findings (Additional file 1: Fig. S5A). We also investigated the effect of NONO O-GlcNAcylation on the protein expressions of SETMAR isoforms. A predicted band corresponding to SETMAR-L(78 kD) was detected, however, a specific band for SETMAR-S(45 kD) was not identified (Additional file 1: Fig. S5B). The results showed that NONO-WT, but not the NONO-S147A mutant, increased the protein level of SETMAR-L, which exhibits higher NHEJ efficiency than SETMAR-S [ ]. Collectively, these findings indicate that O-GlcNAcylation of NONO promotes the formation of SETMAR-L by modulating the alternative splicing of SETMAR pre-mRNA.
 

## Molecular dynamics simulations reveal O-GlcNAcylation enhances NONO stability and RNA binding
Molecular dynamics simulations were performed on both unglycosylated and glycosylated forms of RNA-bound human NONO at the Ser147 site to elucidate the regulatory effects of O-GlcNAcylation on NONO-mediated alternative splicing. Root mean square deviation (RMSD) analysis revealed that both glycosylated and unglycosylated systems reached a stable state within 200 ns. Notably, the RMSD values for the glycosylated NONO exhibited smoother fluctuations, indicating that O-GlcNAcylated NONO is more stable compared to its unglycosylated counterpart (Additional file 1: Fig. S6A). To assess the impact of O-GlcNAcylation at Ser147 on protein structural flexibility, we analyzed the root-mean-square fluctuation value (RMSF) of the trajectories. O-GlcNAcylation reduced the fluctuations of amino acid residues adjacent to Ser147 (amino acid 146–236) (Additional file 1: Fig. S6B), suggesting that O-GlcNAcylation at Ser147 enhances NONO’s interaction with RNA. Additionally, we employed the gmx_MMPBSA method to calculate the energy contribution of amino acid residues involved in NONO-RNA binding. Comparison between the glycosylated and unglycosylated systems revealed that Ser147 O-GlcNAcylation of NONO enhanced the stability of the protein-RNA complex through electrostatic interactions with residues such as Arg75 and Phe104 (Additional file 1: Fig. S6C). RMSF analysis of RNA fluctuations demonstrated that O-GlcNAcylation significantly reduced RNA mobility, contributing to a more stable NONO-RNA interaction (Additional file 1: Fig. S6D). Furthermore, motion trajectory analysis revealed a more concentrated RNA binding region in NONO upon O-GlcNAcylation (Additional file 1: Fig. S6E), thereby enhancing the binding stability of NONO to SETMAR pre-mRNA.

## NONO O-GlcNAcylation facilitates Ku70 recruitment through SETMAR-dependent dimethylation of H3K36
SETMAR is known to enhance the recruitment of the early NHEJ factor Ku70 and facilitate NHEJ repair by promoting the formation of H3K36me2 at DSBs [ ]. Based on our findings that NONO O-GlcNAcylation promotes the production of SETMAR-L, we next investigated the role of NONO O-GlcNAcylation in modulating the recruitment of Ku70 to DNA damage sites. To this end, we transiently expressed EGFP-Ku70 in U2OS cells transfected with an empty vector, SFB-tagged NONO-WT, or the S147A mutant. Following laser microirradiation, we observed that NONO-WT, but not the S147A mutant, significantly enhanced the recruitment of Ku70 to DNA damage sites compared to the control group (Fig.  A). Even with MG132 treatment, the recruitment of the NONO-S147A mutant to DNA damage sites remained significantly impaired (Additional file 1: Fig. S7). Furthermore, we examined whether NONO O-GlcNAcylation facilitates Ku70 binding to chromatin. U2OS cells transfected with an empty vector, SFB-tagged NONO-WT or the S147A mutant were exposed to IR and allowed to recover for specified time periods. Proteins were extracted from both total and chromatin-bound fractions and analyzed by Western blotting using the indicated antibodies. The results demonstrated that the S147A mutant significantly impaired Ku70 binding to chromatin, underscoring the critical role of NONO O-GlcNAcylation in facilitating Ku70’s chromatin association (Fig.  B). To assess whether the S147A mutant decreases the recruitment of Ku70 to DNA damage sites and chromatin binding by reducing SETMAR-dependent H3K36me2 levels, we reconstituted stable NONO knockdown cell lines with either NONO-WT or NONO-S147A. We then examined the impact of Ser147 O-GlcNAcylation on H3K36me2 levels via Western blot analysis. The results revealed that NONO knockdown significantly reduced H3K36me2 levels. Reintroduction of NONO-WT, but not the S147A mutant, restored H3K36me2 levels (Fig.  C), indicating that the S147A mutation compromises NHEJ repair. This finding is consistent with the requirement for H3K36me2 formation in optimal NHEJ repair of DSBs [ ]. Collectively, these results suggest that O-GlcNAcylation of NONO promotes the recruitment of Ku70 to DSBs and enhances Ku70’s chromatin binding by increasing H3K36me2 levels, thereby improving the efficiency of NHEJ repair.
 

## O-GlcNAcylation of NONO at Ser147 enhances radioresistance of liver cancer
Previous studies have demonstrated that NONO facilitates DNA damage repair and enhances the radioresistance of tumor cells [ , ]. Given that O-GlcNAcylation of NONO promotes NHEJ-mediated DSB repair, a key mechanism driving tumor radioresistance, we investigated the role of NONO O-GlcNAcylation in tumor radioresistance. Our findings reveal significantly higher levels of NONO O-GlcNAcylation in three human liver cancer cell lines (HepG2, HCCLM9 and Huh7) compared to the normal liver cell line THLE-2 (Fig.  A). Additionally, data from the GEPIA database indicated a positive correlation between the expression levels of NONO and O-GlcNAc transferase (OGT) mRNA in liver cancer tissues (Additional file 1: Fig. S8A), suggesting that elevated O-GlcNAcylation of NONO may be driven by increased OGT expression in liver cancer.
 To assess whether the absence of NONO O-GlcNAcylation at Ser147 enhances radiosensitivity of liver cancer, we established stable NONO-knockdown HCCLM9 and Huh7 cells using two distinct short hairpin RNAs (shNONO#1 and shNONO#2). The efficacy of NONO knockdown was confirmed by Western blotting (Fig.  B, Additional file 1: Fig. S8B). Knockdown of NONO significantly sensitized liver cancer cells to IR, a phenomenon also observed in NONO-knockout colon cancer cells (HCT116) [ ], bone osteosarcoma epithelial cells (U2OS) [ ], cervical cancer cells (HeLa) cells, and breast cancer cells (MDA-MB-231) [ ]. Notably, reconstitution with wild-type NONO, but not the S147A mutant, fully restored the colony formation capacity in NONO knockdown cells (Fig.  C and Additional file 1: Fig. S8C). These findings indicate that Ser147 O-GlcNAcylation of NONO contributes to the radioresistance of liver cancer cells in vitro.
To further investigate the role of NONO O-GlcNAcylation in radioresistance in vivo, we established a mouse subcutaneous xenograft tumor model. NTG mice were subcutaneously injected with HCCLM9 cells, NONO-knockdown HCCLM9 cells, and NONO-knockdown HCCLM9 cells reconstituted with either wild-type NONO or the S147A mutant. When the xenograft tumors reached a volume of approximately 100 mm3, the mice were subjected to either no treatment or local radiation with two doses of 8 Gy of IR (Fig.  D). In the absence of radiation, tumors derived from NONO-knockdown cells were significantly smaller compared to those from the control group, indicating that NONO plays a significant role in tumorigenesis and progression in liver cancer. Post-irradiation, depletion of NONO inhibited tumor growth, and this inhibitory effect was rescued by the reconstitution of wild-type NONO, but not by the S147A mutant (Fig.  E, F and Additional file 1: Fig. S8D). These results demonstrate that Ser147 O-GlcNAcylation of NONO promotes liver tumor growth and confers significant radioresistance. Further immunohistochemical analysis of tumor tissues showed that IR-treated tumors derived from NONO-knockdown cells reconstituted with wild-type NONO exhibited higher levels of H3K36me2 compared to those reconstituted with the S147A mutant (Fig.  G, H). Taken together, these results underscore the crucial role of NONO O-GlcNAcylation at Ser147 in promoting tumor growth and conferring radioresistance in liver cancer cells, both in vitro and in vivo (Fig.  ).
 

## Discussion
NONO is a multifunctional protein that plays a pivotal role in various physiological processes, including DNA damage repair, cell proliferation, gene expression regulation and tumorigenesis [ , ]. Emerging evidence indicates that NONO undergoes multiple PTMs [ ], such as O-GlcNAcylation [ ], ubiquitination [ ], phosphorylation [ ], methylation [ ], and acetylation [ ], which modulate its interactions with various effectors, including proteins and RNAs. In response to DNA damage, NONO is subjected to ubiquitination [ ] and O-GlcNAcylation [ ]. Ubiquitination facilitates the degradation of NONO, a crucial event for deactivating ATR-CHK1 checkpoint signaling in response to UV damage. Our previous studies demonstrated that IR increases the O-GlcNAcylation levels of NONO [ ]. A recent study identified Thr440 as a major O-GlcNAcylation site on NONO and demonstrated that this modification regulates paraspeckle component assembly, contributing to colon cancer cell proliferation [ ]. However, our study identified Ser147 as a significant O-GlcNAcylation site in response to DNA damage and showed that O-GlcNAcylation at this site mediates the alternative splicing of SETMAR, facilitating NHEJ repair. Elevated O-GlcNAcylation of NONO in response to IR creates a feedback loop that enhances DSB repair capacity and augments radioresistance in liver cancer through the NHEJ pathway. Mechanistically, we found that Ser147 O-GlcNAcylation enhances NONO stability and its binding to DNA damage sites. Interestingly, while previous studies showed that O-GlcNAcylation of NONO at Thr440 affects interactions with SFPQ and PSPC1 [ ], our findings indicate that O-GlcNAcylation at Ser147 also facilitates the assembly of the NONO-SFPQ complex. The complex plays a pivotal role in regulating the alternative splicing of SETMAR pre-mRNA, leading to increased levels of H3K36me2 at DSB sites. Elevated H3K36me2 promotes the recruitment of the early NHEJ factor, Ku70 [ ], thereby enhancing DSB repair efficiency. Ultimately, this improved repair capacity contributes to increased radioresistance. While we demonstrate the effect of NONO O-GlcNAcylation on the expression of the SETMAR-L protein, we were unable to identify a specific band for the SETMAR-S protein due to the absence of a reliable SETMAR antibody, which is necessary for further investigation.
Post-translational modifications (PTMs) play crucial roles in regulating the structure and function of various DNA damage repair factors. Among these modifications, O-GlcNAcylation has emerged as a pivotal regulator of the DNA damage response [ ]. Key regulatory factors involved in DNA damage repair, such as MDC1, DNA-PK, BRCA1 and PARG, are modulated by O-GlcNAcylation [ ]. Our previous research demonstrated that O-GlcNAcylation of YTHDC1 at Ser396 promotes homologous recombination repair by facilitating its interaction with m6A RNA and the recruitment of RAD51 to DNA damage sites [ ]. Additionally, extensive crosstalk exists between O-GlcNAcylation and other PTMs. In this study, we investigated the interplay between O-GlcNAcylation and ubiquitination of NONO in the context of DNA damage repair. Our findings revealed that O-GlcNAcylation at Ser147 reduces the ubiquitin–proteasome pathway-mediated degradation of NONO, thereby stabilizing the protein. Furthermore, NONO’s recruitment to DNA damage sites has been shown to depend on PAR produced by PARP1 [ ]. Our results indicate that O-GlcNAcylated NONO exhibits increased binding affinity for PAR without altering its chromatin-binding ability, thereby enhancing its recruitment to DNA damage sites. Previous studies have shown that NONO contains a highly charged helix-turn-helix (HTH) motif at its C-terminus, adjacent to the coiled-coil region, which is responsible for binding double-strand DNA [ , ]. Therefore, it is reasonable conclude that Ser147 O-GlcNAcylation, located within RRM2, does not impact its chromatin binding ability. Notably, our prior study suggested that O-GlcNAcylated NONO might also regulate RNA metabolism in a distinct nuclear compartment, separate from its role in DNA damage repair [ ]. This study confirms that O-GlcNAcylation of NONO regulates the alternative splicing of SETMAR pre-mRNA by promoting the formation of a stable NONO-SFPQ complex, thereby facilitating NHEJ repair and contributing to the development of radioresistance. Collectively, our findings underscore the crucial regulatory role of O-GlcNAcylation in NONO-mediated NHEJ repair.
Tumor radioresistance remains a major challenge that limits the efficacy of radiotherapy. Tumor cells with highly efficient DNA repair mechanisms are often prone to radioresistance, whereas defective repair pathways render these cells more radiosensitive. Various inhibitors targeting key DNA damage repair factors have been developed to sensitize tumors to radiotherapy. Notable examples include the ATM inhibitor AZD1390, the ATR inhibitor VE-821, the DNA-dependent protein kinase inhibitor peposertib, and the PARP inhibitor Olaparib, all of which have shown efficacy in increasing radiosensitivity across various cancer types [ – ]. Our research suggests that targeting the O-GlcNAcylation of NONO at Ser147 may present a novel strategy to enhance the sensitivity of liver cancer cells to radiation. Recently, small molecules targeting Cys145 of NONO have been identified. These compounds selectively interact with NONO, leading to alterations in the transcriptomes and proteomes of cancer cells and suppression of pro-tumorigenic transcriptional networks [ ]. Given the proximity of Cys145 to Ser147, it is plausible that these compounds might also affect the O-GlcNAcylation of NONO at Ser147. However, further research is required to confirm this hypothesis.

## Conclusions
The study provides valuable insight into the regulatory role of O-GlcNAcylation of NONO in DNA damage repair and radioresistance. Targeting O-GlcNAcylation of NONO at Ser147 could potentially reduce radioresistance, offering a promising avenue for enhancing the effectiveness of radiation therapy in cancer treatment. Future investigations should focus on the development of specific inhibitors targeting NONO O-GlcNAcylation and their potential synergistic effects when combined with existing radiotherapy protocols.

## Methods

## Cell culture, antibodies and reagents
The HEK293T, U2OS, THLE2, Huh7, HepG2 and HCCLM9 cell lines were purchased from American Type Cell Culture (ATCC), and cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle medium (Gibco) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) (Gibco), 100 U/mL penicillin, and 100 μg/mL streptomycin in the presence of 5% CO2 (v/v) at 37 °C. All cell lines used in our study were authenticated by STR profiling and free from mycoplasma contamination. For transient transfection experiments cells were transfected with indicated constructs, using lipofectamine 2000 (Invitrogen) following the manufacturer’s protocols. Antibodies used in this study include the following: Anti-DDDDK-tag mAb (MBL, M185-3L), Anti-Myc-tag mAb (MBL, M047-3), Anti-GAPDH (Proteintech, 60,004–1-Ig), OGT polyclonal antibody (Proteintech, 11,576–2-AP), NONO polyclonal antibody (Proteintech, 11,058–1-AP), RL2 (Santa cruz biotechnology, SC-59624, which is an antibody against O-GlcNAc), Histone-H3 polyclonal antibody (Proteintech, 17,168–1-AP), anti-HA (Sigma, 66,006–2-Ig), Anti-PAR monoclonal antibody (R&D, 4335-MC-100-AC), Ku80 polyclonal antibody (Proteintech, 16,389–1-AP), SFPQ polyclonal antibody (Proteintech, 15,585–1-AP), anti-Histone H3(di-methyl K36) polyclonal antibody (abcam, AB9049), Ku70 polyclonal antibody (Proteintech, 10,723–1-AP), anti-SETMAR polyclonal antibody (abcam, ab129455), anti-RAD51 monoclonal antibody(abcam, ab133534). Reagents used in this study include the following: Thiamet G (Selleck, S7213), Mitomycin (MCE, HY-13316), Cisplatin (MCE, HY-17394), Etoposide (MCE, HY-13629), MMS (Sigma, 129,925), OSMI-1 (Selleck, S4475), MG132 (Selleck, S2619).

## Generation of reconstituted stable cell lines
Reconstituted cell lines were generated by using lentiviruses to mediate shRNA-mediated knockdown of endogenous NONO or Ku80. The shRNA sequences and a scrambled control sequence were individually cloned into pLKO.1-Puro vectors using the Age I and EcoR I sites. The shRNA sequences used were listed in Additional file 2: Table S1. Lentiviruses were generated in HEK293T cells using lipofectamine 2000-mediated co-transfection of lentiviral-based shRNA plasmids and the psPAX2 (packaging) and pMD2.G (envelope) plasmids. After 48 h post-transfection, the viral vector was harvested, purified, and concentrated. HEK293T/HCCLM9/Huh7 cells were transduced with the virus in media supplemented with polybrene (10 μg/mL), incubated for 24 h, and then cultured in fresh media for an additional 24 h. Stable cells were selected using 2 μg/mL puromycin for 2 weeks.

## Coimmunoprecipitation
Cells were harvested and lysed with NETN300 buffer (50 mM Tris–HCl pH 8.0, 300 mM NaCl, 0.5 mM EDTA, and 0.5% NP-40) for 20 min on ice. The supernatants were diluted with an equal volume of NETN buffer (50 mM Tris–HCl pH 8.0, 0.5 mM EDTA, and 0.5% NP-40) and incubated with streptavidin-conjugated beads or indicated antibodies, followed by incubation with protein A/G Sepharose beads for 12 h at 4 °C. NONO O-GlcNAcylation was confirmed by denatured immunoprecipitation, where cells were lysed with NETN300 buffer containing 1% SDS and boiled at 95 °C for 15 min, and subsequently subjected to ultrasonic cracking, and centrifugation. The beads were rinsed three times with NETN100 buffer (50 mM Tris–HCl pH 8.0, 100 mM NaCl, 0.5 mM EDTA, and 0.5% NP-40), and bound proteins were eluted using SDS-loading buffer for SDS–PAGE.

## Western blotting
Following cell lysis, proteins were separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred to a PVDF membrane (Millipore) using a Trans-Blot SD Semi-Dry Transfer Cell (Bio-Rad). Membranes were blocked in 5% BSA (Bovine Serum Albumin, Solarbio) for 1 h at room temperature, then incubated with primary antibody (1:1,000 dilution) in 1% BSA at 4 °C overnight. After incubation, membranes were washed in TBST (three times, 10 min each) and incubated with (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibody (1:5,000 dilution) in TBST for 1 h at room temperature. Blots were then washed again in TBST (three times, 10 min each) and developed using ECL reagents (Thermo) on a ChemiDoc XRS + molecular imager (Bio-Rad, Bio-Rad Image Lab 4.1).

## Laser microirradiation and imaging
Cells were seeded in 35 mm glass-bottomed cell culture dishes (NEST) and transfected with indicated plasmids. After 24 h, GFP-expressing cells were selected for laser microirradiation. The microirradiation was performed using an OLYMPUS FV3000 inverted fluorescence microscope equipped with a Micropoint Laser Illumination system. Images were captured using cellSens standard (Version 1.3) software, and GFP strips were recorded at indicated time points. Image adjustments for contrast and brightness were standardized across all experiments using Image J software. The laser microirradiation experiments were repeated three times, with 10 cells per experiment.

## Colony formation assay
Cells were seeded per well in 6-well plates and cultured in complete medium for 7–10 days post-exposure to the indicated doses of IR radiation. The cells were then washed twice with PBS, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) for 30 min, and stained with crystal violet for 30 min. Colony number were counted, and the surviving cell fractions were calculated by comparing the number of colonies formed in irradiated cultures to those in untreated controls. Three independent experiments were performed.

## Separation of cytosolic and nuclear proteins
Cells were harvested at the specified time points following treatment and washed twice with PBS. The cells were then suspended in the NETN420 buffer (50 mM Tris–HCl pH 8.0, 420 mM NaCl, 0.5 mM EDTA, and 0.5% NP-40) and centrifuged to obtain the soluble fraction, which constituted the total extract. For cytosolic extracts, the cells were lysed with NETN100 buffer, incubated on ice for 10 min, and then centrifuged. The resulting supernatants were collected as cytosolic extracts. The remaining pellets were washed three times with NETN100 buffer, resuspended in NETN420 buffer, and incubates for 5 min at room temperature, followed by an additional incubation on ice for 10 min. After centrifugation, the supernatants were collected as nuclear extracts.

## NHEJ/HR reporter assay
HEK293T cells, transfected with NONO shRNA, were overexpressed with NONO-SFB WT or S147A mutants, followed by transfection with DR-GFP, I-SceI, and m-Cherry plasmids or the NHEJ-GFP and m-Cherry plasmids. Stable HEK293T cells with Ku80 or RAD51 knockdown served as a positive control. After 48 h, cells were harvested, and the ratio of GFP + -positive cells to m-Cherry-positive cells was analyzed by flow cytometry.

## RNA FISH (Fluorescence In Situ Hybridization)
Cells cultured on coverslips were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min at room temperature, permeabilized with 0.5% Triton X-100 at room temperature for 15 min, and blocked with 1 × blocking buffer in PBS, prepared with RNase-free, DEPC-treated water. Subsequently, cells were incubated with 20 × SSC at 37 °C for 30 min. Hybridization was carried out with a probe at 37 °C overnight. Following incubation, the cell nucleus was stained with DAPI. Fluorescence analysis was conducted using microscope (Olympus). FAM or CY3 modified FISH probes were used to detect SETMAR-L and SETMAR-S, following the manufacturer’s protocol (GenePharma). FISH probes were listed in Additional file 2: Table. All operations were performed under RNase-free conditions. The measurements were followed by previous study [ ].The measurement parameters included sum of total area (Area), total cell number (CN), background average fluorescence intensity (BAFI) and integral fluorescence intensity (IFI). The image was converted to gray scale image and the values were quantified. The data was expressed as average IFI per cell ((IFI-Area*BAFI)/CN).

## Xenograft model in NTG mice
All animal experiments were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of Hebei University (Approval No. 2020XG013). Subcutaneous injections of approximately 7 × 106 normal (shNC), NONO-depleted (shNONO), or shNONO-reconstituted expression of WT and S147A NONO HCCLM9 cells were administered to 6-week-old male NTG mice. The volume of the tumors and weight were measured every three days. Once tumors in the shNC xenograft group reached 100 mm3, the mice were subjected to local irradiation with 8 Gy of IR twice. Tumor volume was calculated using the following formula: length × width2 × 0.5. Mice were euthanized after indicated days, and tumors were collected and weighed.

## Quantitative PCR (qPCR)
Total RNA was extracted using Trizol (Invitrogen) and utilized for first-strand cDNA synthesis with Superscript II reverse transcriptase (Invitrogen). Quantitative PCR was conducted using Fast SYBR™ green master mix (Thermo Fisher) in CFX96TM Real-time systems (BIO-RAD). The qPCR reactions were conducted in triplicate, and expression levels were normalized to GAPDH to account for variations in RNA input. The relative expression of SETMAR-L and SETMAR-S was calculated using the 2-ΔΔCt method (Ct, cycle threshold), enabling us to determine the ratio of SETMAR-L to SETMAR-S. The primers for qPCR reactions are listed in Additional file 2: Table.

## Immunohistochemical analysis (IHC)
Tumors were fixed overnight in 4% paraformaldehyde at room temperature, transferred to 70% ethanol and embedded in paraffin. Four micrometer sections were dewaxed in xylene, and rehydrated in ethanol (100, 95, 80, 70, and 50%) and PBS buffer. For immunohistochemistry of NONO and H3K36me2, sections were blocked with 5% goat serum in PBS buffer, then incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against NONO and H3K36me2. After washing three times with PBS, the sections were incubated with secondary antibodies, washed again, and stained with DAB for detection. DNA was stained with hematoxylin.

## Neutral comet assays
24 h after transfection with the indicated plasmids, HEK293T cells treated with NONO shRNA were irradiated with or without IR (10 Gy), and allowed to recover in medium for the indicated duration at 37 °C. Cells were collected and rinsed twice with ice-cold PBS, and 5000 cells were combined with 1% low-melting-point agarose (LMP) and immediately pipetted onto slides at room temperature for 30 min. The slides were immersed in the neutral lysis solution (2% sarkosyl, 0.5 M Na2EDTA, 0.5 mg/mL proteinase K at pH 8.0) overnight at 37 °C in the dark followed by washing in the rinse buffer (90 mM Tris–HCl buffer, 90 mM boric acid, 2 mM Na2EDTA at pH 8.5) for 30 min with two repeats. Next, the slides were subjected to electrophoresis at 25 V 300 mA for 60 min and stained in 2.5 μg/mL propidium iodide for 30 min. All images were captured using a fluorescence microscope, and the data were analyzed by CASP software.

## Site mapping of NONO O-GlcNAcylation by Mass Spectrometry (MS)
HEK293T cells expressing SFB-NONO were irradiated and allowed to recover for 1 h. Purified SFB-NONO was resolved using 10% SDS–PAGE and stained with Coomassie blue. The NONO protein bands were excised, rinsed with Milli-Q water, and destained twice for 30 min, followed by dehydration with acetonitrile. In-gel digestion was performed at 37 °C for 16 h using trypsin at a concentration of 12.5 ng/μL. Peptides were extracted using 50% acetonitrile and dried in a vacuum centrifuge. The sample was reconstituted in water containing 0.1% formic acid and subjected to liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC–MS/MS) analysis using an EASY-nLC 1000 nano-LC system (Thermo Scientific) coupled with an Orbitrap Lumos mass spectrometer (Thermo Scientific). For data analysis, the raw MS files were processed and searched using PEAKS Studio 11.0. Precursor and fragment ion mass tolerances were set to 15 ppm and 0.5 Da, and trypsin was set as the digestion enzyme. O-GlcNAc (ST), acetylation (protein N-terminal) and oxidation (methionine) modifications were set as variable modifications, and carbamidomethyl was set as a fixed modification. A false discovery rate (FDR) of 1% was applied at both the peptide and protein levels. Ascore ≥ 20 was required for O-GlcNAc site localization. The RAW MS data has been submitted to the ProteomeXchange Consortium via the iProX partner repository with the dataset identifier PXD.

## RNA immunoprecipitation (RIP) assays and biotin-labeled RNA pull-down
Cells transfected with the indicated plasmids were harvested and subjected to immunoprecipitation. RNA was extracted from the immunoprecipitated complexes using Trizol reagent (Sigma, T9424) and analyzed by qPCR. The probe (CAGGCAGG) was labeled with biotin and incubated with streptavidin beads, with a poly A probe serving as a negative control. Cells were lysed by NETN300 buffer, and the beads-biotin-probe complex was added to the lysates, followed by incubation at 4 °C overnight. After washing with NETN100 buffer, proteins associated with the probe were pulled down in coprecipitation assays and examined by western blot. All operations were conducted under RNase-free conditions. The primers for RIP-qPCR were listed in Additional file 2: Table.

## Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations
The complex structure of NONO-SFPQ (PDB ID: 7LRQ) was set as a receptor for binding RNA motifs. The 3D structure of the RNA motif CAGGCAGG was constructed through the 3dRNA Web Server. The binding mode of NONO and the RNA motif was predicted by NPDock. The O-GlcNAcylation at S147 of NONO was established using the Glycan Reader & Modeler module. The role of O-glycosylation in NONO recognition of RNA was investigated via molecular dynamics simulations. The systems of unglycosylated- or glycosylated-NONO combined with RNA motif complexes were established on the CHARMM-GUI web server. The solvation system was modeled with TIP3P water molecules, then 150 mM KCl was added to the system, and the final simulation box contained 96,901 atoms. The complex systems were performed based on the GROMACS program and CHARMM36m force field. Firstly, the system underwent energy minimization. Next, the Nose–Hoover temperature coupling method was used to increase the system temperature to 310.15 K in the NVT ensemble, and the Parrinello-Rahman pressure coupling method was used to stabilize the system pressure to 1 bar in the NPT ensemble. Molecular dynamics of 200 ns simulated the generated phase of the system three times. Then, the RMSD (root-mean-square deviation) and RMSF (root-mean-square fluctuation) programs built in GROMACS were used for data analysis. The contribution of binding energy and residue energy was calculated using the gmx_MMPBSA tool. PyMOL was used to visualize the complex system.

## Statistics
The in vitro experiments were repeated at least three times unless stated otherwise. Data are plotted with error bars representing the standard error of the mean (SEM) or standard deviation of the mean (SD), as indicated in Figure legends. Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism (version 10.2.0). Comparisons between two groups were done by two-tailed unpaired Student’s t-test. Comparisons among three or more groups were done with one-way or two-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s multiple comparison post-test. Statistical significance levels are denoted as follows: *P < 0.05; **P < 0.01; ***P < 0.001; ns, not significant.
