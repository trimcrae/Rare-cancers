## Abstract
Radiotherapy (RT) remains a cornerstone of cancer management but is fundamentally constrained by normal tissue toxicity, intrinsic and acquired radioresistance, and hypoxia- and microenvironment-driven dose–response plateaus. Engineered nanomaterials offer a versatile toolbox to reshape this therapeutic landscape by coupling enhanced energy deposition with microenvironmental and biological reprogramming. This review summarizes recent advances in nanomaterial-enabled radiosensitization from four interlocking dimensions: (i) high-atomic-number platforms that amplify local dose via photoelectric and related interactions and thereby increase microscopic energy deposition; (ii) chemical radiosensitization through modulation of reactive oxygen species (ROS), including Fenton/Fenton-like catalysis and depletion of glutathione (GSH)/thioredoxin antioxidant networks; (iii) tumor microenvironment (TME) remodeling strategies that alleviate hypoxia, buffer acidity, rewire redox and metabolic states, relieve immune suppression, and normalize vasculature and extracellular matrix (ECM) to broaden the effective therapeutic window; and (iv) biological radiosensitization targeting DNA damage response (DDR), cell-cycle redistribution, and multiple programmed cell-death pathways such as apoptosis and ferroptosis. We further discuss nano-delivery architectures—passive EPR-based systems, ligand-directed and biomimetic carriers, and stimuli-responsive (pH, hypoxia, redox, or irradiation triggered) formulations—that co-load radiosensitizers, chemotherapeutics, and molecularly targeted agents, as well as theranostic platforms integrating computed tomography (CT)/magnetic resonance imaging (MRI)/optical contrast for image-guided, dose-adapted treatment. Emerging multimodal regimens, including radiotherapy combined with photothermal, sonodynamic, chemotherapy, and immunotherapy on a single nano-platform, are highlighted for their capacity to achieve genuine “1 + 1>2” synergy. Finally, we outline key translational challenges—industrial-scale, standardized manufacturing; long-term safety and clearance; inter-patient and spatiotemporal heterogeneity; quantitative linkage between imaging signals, dose, and biological effect; and the integration of biomarkers and artificial intelligence into personalized nano-radiotherapy—and propose future directions to accelerate clinical implementation.
Keywords: Nanomaterials, Radiosensitization, Nanodelivery systems/platforms, Theranostics, Multimodal synergy

## Introduction
Radiotherapy is one of the most effective and widely used modalities for the treatment of malignant tumors [ ]. However, current RT techniques face several clinical challenges. First, high-dose irradiation inevitably damages surrounding normal tissues while killing tumor cells [ , ]. Second, a subset of tumor cells exhibits pronounced radioresistance [ , ]. Third, conventional radiosensitizers are often limited by substantial adverse effects and suboptimal efficacy [ , ]. These issues are particularly prominent in malignancies such as nasopharyngeal carcinoma (NPC), where RT is the mainstay of treatment and hypoxia within the TME markedly reduces radiosensitivity [ ]. In addition, the relatively low tissue specificity of X-ray irradiation means that damage to normal tissues adjacent to the tumor remains a major challenge for RT [ ].
With decades of advances in technology and nanoscience, a variety of functional nanobiomaterials have been introduced into cancer therapy. Nanomaterials exhibit unique biological advantages in RT enhancement. High atomic number (high-Z) nanomaterials, such as gold nanoparticles (AuNPs), have become ideal radiosensitizers owing to their distinctive photoelectric attenuation properties [ , ]. These materials can increase X-ray energy deposition within tumor tissues, thereby achieving physical radiosensitization [ , ], and can further catalyze radiosensitizing effects by exploiting radiation-induced secondary electrons and energy transfer to significantly enhance RT efficacy [ , ].Nanomaterials can also modulate the TME to indirectly enhance radiosensitivity. For example, they can generate ROS and other intermediates to overcome tumor cell radioresistance [ ]. In particular, nanozymes with catalase-like activity can convert elevated hydrogen peroxide(H2O2) in the TME into oxygen, effectively alleviating hypoxia-induced radioresistance [ ].
In terms of precise targeting and toxicity reduction, functionalized nanomaterials can increase intratumoral accumulation through active molecular targeting (e.g., antibody modification) and passive enhanced permeability and retention (EPR) effects, while magnetic targeting can further improve tumor selectivity [ , , ]. These strategies help reduce radiation exposure to normal tissues and expand the therapeutic window. Meanwhile, nanoparticle-based radioprotectors can selectively protect healthy tissues, and, when combined with dose-optimization strategies (such as low-dose RT plus nano-radiosensitization), can maintain or even enhance antitumor efficacy while reducing systemic toxicity, thereby improving the overall therapeutic index of RT [ , ]. Importantly, some nanomaterials possess intrinsic imaging contrast (e.g., visible on CT or MRI), enabling radiosensitization and imaging to form a mutually reinforcing evidence chain. This supports image-guided dose modulation and real-time response evaluation, thus facilitating an integrated theranostic loop.
Furthermore, nanomaterials provide a unified platform for multimodal synergy with RT. On the one hand, they can co-deliver chemotherapeutic agents and enable radiation-triggered or schedule-optimized drug release, thereby achieving precise RT–chemotherapy combinations (e.g., controlled drug release from bismuth-based nanosheets under low-dose X-ray exposure) [ ]. On the other hand, they can amplify RT-induced immunogenic cell death (ICD) and remodel the tumor immune microenvironment, thereby improving responses to immune checkpoint-based therapies [ , ]. In addition, energy-converting materials (such as zirconium carbide [ZrC]) can integrate radiosensitization with photothermal therapy (PTT) and/or sonodynamic therapy (SDT), enabling cooperative RT–PTT/SDT regimens that enhance both local tumor control and systemic antitumor immunity [ , ].
The integration of nanotechnology into RT represents a major breakthrough in the field of cancer treatment [ , ].Compared with conventional RT, nanomaterial-based platforms offer multiple advantages: they can increase tumor selectivity via molecular targeting [ , ], realize precise therapy through stimuli-responsive drug-release systems [ , ], and enable multimodal therapeutic strategies that combine RT, PTT, and immunotherapy, among others [ , ].In particular, gold-based nanomaterials have achieved remarkable progress as radiosensitizers and provide important design paradigms for next-generation radiosensitizing agents [ , ].These advances make it possible to achieve effective tumor control at relatively low radiation doses [ ], while optimizing local dose distribution to maximize therapeutic efficacy and protect surrounding normal tissues [ , ]. The clinical translation of nano-enabled RT is expected to address key limitations of conventional RT, including dose constraints and treatment-related toxicity.
In this review, we summarize the underlying mechanisms and therapeutic advantages of nanomaterials in enhancing RT, and highlight the major challenges and future directions for the clinical translation of nanomaterial-assisted radiotherapy.

2.

## Nanomaterial-mediated mechanisms and applications of radiosensitization
2.1.

## Physical radiosensitization mechanisms and applications of high-Z materials
High-Z nanomaterials act as radiosensitizers primarily by enhancing local energy deposition within tumors through the photoelectric effect and Compton scattering [ ].The physical basis of high-Z nanomaterials as RT sensitizers lies in their markedly different energy-absorption properties compared with soft tissue. The effective atomic number of soft tissue is approximately 7.3, whereas high-Z elements such as gold, silver, bismuth and gadolinium possess much higher atomic numbers; the photoelectric cross-section of such elements scales roughly with Z^3–Z^4, greatly amplifying the photoelectric effect [ , ].
In the photoelectric effect, an inner-shell electron absorbs an incident photon and is ejected, followed by relaxation of outer-shell electrons with emission of fluorescent photons and a large number of low-energy Auger electrons. These secondary electrons have very short ranges and highly localized energy deposition, generating dense ionization events on the micrometer scale and thereby markedly increasing local dose [ , ].For incident photons in the keV to low-MeV range, energy deposition is dominated by the photoelectric effect and Compton scattering [ ]. In Compton scattering, photons transfer only part of their energy to weakly bound electrons, resulting in relatively sparse ionization events and a weaker dependence on Z. Under low-energy kV X-ray irradiation, photoelectric absorption predominates, and high-Z nanoparticles (NPs) can substantially increase energy deposition within the tumor region [ , ]. Under clinically used MV X-ray conditions, Compton scattering is the main interaction, and purely theoretical physical dose enhancement is expected to be modest [ , ]. However, multiple in vitro and in vivo studies have demonstrated that high-Z NPs still produce significant radiosensitization even under MV irradiation, indicating that, beyond physical dose enhancement, additional chemico-biological mechanisms such as increased radical yield and amplified DNA damage are likely to contribute [ , ].
Early radiosensitization efforts using high-Z materials were exemplified by iodine. Direct intratumoral injection of iodine before RT significantly increased local absorption dose of low-energy X-rays (by ∼30%) and prolonged the median survival of animals bearing brain tumors from 25.5 days with RT alone to 38.5 days [ ]. Compared with bulk or larger-sized high-Z metal particles, whose diffusion in densely packed tumor tissue and uptake by cancer cells are limited, small-molecule iodide and iodine-based nanomaterials offer better translational potential [ ].
Among various nanoparticles, AuNPs are regarded as one of the most promising radiosensitizers owing to their excellent biocompatibility and high X-ray absorption cross-section. Intravenous administration of AuNPs prior to 250 kVp photon irradiation yielded an approximately 86% long-term (>1 year) cure rate in EMT-6 murine subcutaneous breast tumors, compared with only 20% for RT alone [ ]. Albumin-modified gold nanoparticles (Alb-AuNPs) showed favorable tumor accumulation and biocompatibility in a non–small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) model, achieving a sensitizer enhancement ratio of 1.432 when combined with RT; this significantly improved antitumor efficacy without notable toxicity, highlighting their potential as high-Z radiosensitizers [ ].
Beyond AuNPs, silver nanoparticles (AgNPs) also exhibit radiosensitizing effects. Compared with gold nanomaterials, AgNPs are more cost-effective, and silver possesses intrinsic antimicrobial activity, enabling simultaneous antitumor and antibacterial effects. For example, Morais et al. investigated the combined effects of AgNPs and RT in the 786-O clear cell renal cell carcinoma (ccRCC) line. AgNPs pretreatment rendered these otherwise radioresistant 786-O cells markedly sensitive to irradiation. Mechanistically, this response was associated with elevated ROS levels, mitochondrial membrane depolarization, and S-phase arrest, ultimately inhibiting proliferation and inducing apoptosis, suggesting that AgNPs may serve as radiosensitizers for ccRCC [ ].In glioma models, some studies have even reported that, at equivalent mass and molar concentrations, AgNPs exhibit stronger radiosensitizing capability than AuNPs and induce a higher proportion of apoptotic cell death [ ].
Other high-Z elements such as platinum, hafnium and bismuth also play important roles in RT enhancement. To achieve efficient treatment of NPC, researchers developed RGD-targeted platinum-based nanoparticles (RGD-PtNPs, abbreviated as RPNs). These NPs consist of an RGD-conjugated outer shell and a core cross-linked by cisplatin (CDDP). Owing to RGD-mediated targeting, RPNs accumulate efficiently within tumors, penetrate tumor tissue and are internalized by cancer cells, thereby improving CDDP delivery. Once adequately enriched in the tumor, CDDP-loaded RPNs function simultaneously as radiosensitizers and chemotherapeutic agents [ ]. Zhang et al. designed a multifunctional nano-sensitizer (RPB7H) comprising HfO2 NPs and a PROTAC prodrug, BPA771. In this system, HfO2 amplifies X-ray energy deposition, exacerbates DNA damage and oxidative stress, while BPA771 is activated by RT-induced ·OH to trigger BRD4 degradation and RAD51AP1 inactivation, thereby blocking DNA damage repair. This combined mechanism resulted in pronounced radiosensitization and tumor growth inhibition in a murine head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC) model [ ]. Huang et al. developed transferrin-bismuth nanoparticles (TBNPs) that cross the blood–brain barrier (BBB) via transferrin-mediated transport and exploit the high-Z characteristics of bismuth to enhance X-ray absorption, amplify DNA damage and induce ICD, along with other immunomodulatory effects. In an orthotopic glioma model, TBNPs enabled synergistic RT–immunotherapy tumor suppression while reducing off-target toxicity and improving biosafety [ ].
Overall, the radiosensitization conferred by high-Z nanomaterials originates primarily from enhanced photoelectric absorption in the kV energy range, leading to micrometer-scale, high-density energy deposition. Under MV conditions, although the pure physical dose gain is limited, high-Z NPs can still achieve substantial chemico-biological radiosensitization via mechanisms such as ROS upregulation, amplified DNA damage with impaired repair, and induction of ICD, collectively demonstrating strong potential for clinical translation.

2.2.

## ROS–mediated chemical radiosensitization
The predominant indirect cytotoxic effect of radiotherapy arises from water radiolysis, which generates ROS such as superoxide anion (O2•-), hydroxyl radicals (•OH), and H2O2 that can directly damage cellular DNA and induce cell death. When ROS production exceeds the cellular reducing capacity, oxidative stress occurs, leading to mitochondrial damage and ultimately apoptosis. Nanomaterial-based chemical radiosensitization acts mainly by modulating ROS metabolism. Around the dual strategy of “enhanced generation and reduced consumption,” ROS-oriented chemical sensitization by nanomaterials can be broadly classified as follows: (i) increasing ROS production and (ii) decreasing ROS scavenging.
(i)Enhancing ROS production. As noted above, high-Z nanomaterials under irradiation can emit abundant secondary electrons, which interact with surrounding water molecules to generate •OH and other ROS. For example, ultrathin gold nanowires (Au, Z = 79) used as radiosensitizers produced more free radicals under X-ray irradiation, induced stronger oxidative stress in both cellular and murine models, and significantly improved tumor suppression [ ]. As a typical high-Z material, gadolinium oxide NPs generated markedly increased ROS under X-ray exposure in a dose-dependent manner compared with irradiation alone, with enhancement factors of approximately 1.6–1.94, indicating efficient promotion of ROS production [ ]. Leveraging this mechanism, Deng et al. employed folate-inserted, red blood cell (RBC) membrane–coated bismuth nanoparticles (F-RBC Bi NPs) as radiosensitizers in a murine breast cancer model. Under X-ray irradiation, the high-Z effect of bismuth amplified ROS generation; in parallel, the RBC membrane prolonged circulation time and folate mediated tumor targeting, while post-treatment dissolution facilitated clearance. This design resulted in pronounced tumor growth inhibition and improved survival in mice [ ]. A second route is Fenton- or Fenton-like amplification. Transition metal–based nanoplatforms containing Fe, Cu, Mn, or Co can catalyze in situ generation of •OH from H2O2 under mildly acidic, H2O2-rich TME conditions, and these radicals synergize with radiation-induced species. For instance, one of the radiosensitizing mechanisms of the 2D multifunctional nanoplatform CuNS@Pt developed by Chen et al. is a Cu-mediated Fenton-like reaction that converts H2O2 into •OH within the TME, thereby further enhancing RT efficacy [ ].A third strategy is X-ray–induced photodynamic therapy (X-PDT) synergy. Nanoscintillators can convert X-rays into visible light to activate nearby photosensitizers, which in turn excite surrounding oxygen into singlet oxygen (1O2). For example, Wang et al. constructed a purely organic phosphorescent nanosystem that integrates both scintillator and photosensitizer functions; in deep-seated tissues, this platform efficiently generated 1O2 at X-ray doses as low as 0.4 Gy, achieving radiosensitization with negligible adverse effects [ ]. Additional X-PDT and other synergistic modalities are discussed in detail in Section on multimodal combinations.
(ii)Reducing ROS scavenging. Tumor cells rely on GSH, the thioredoxin/thioredoxin reductase (Trx/TrxR) system, and the NRF2 stress-response network to eliminate ROS and maintain redox homeostasis. Inhibition or depletion of these antioxidant systems (e.g., Trx/TrxR, GSH) can sensitize tumors to RT; for example, the TrxR inhibitor auranofin increases mitochondrial oxidative stress and aggravates DNA damage [ ]. Liang et al. synthesized a series of 1,2,5-selenadiazole compounds as potent TrxR inhibitors. Under irradiation, these agents significantly sensitized radioresistant A375 melanoma cells by promoting excessive ROS accumulation, subsequently triggering ROS-mediated apoptotic pathways, mitochondrial dysfunction, and caspase activation, thereby enhancing RT efficacy [ ]. Chen et al. developed a MnO2/Pt@BSA nanoplatform that markedly promotes •OH generation and concomitantly consumes GSH, thus suppressing •OH scavenging and strengthening its therapeutic action [ ]. In addition, certain nanozymes can deplete intracellular GSH by mimicking glutathione oxidase (GSHOx) or glutathione peroxidase (GPx) activities, or by interfering with the reduction of oxidized glutathione (GSSG), thereby achieving radiosensitization [ ]. An example is the Cu2WS4-PEG (CWP) nanozyme [ ].
In summary, ROS-oriented chemical radiosensitization follows a coherent paradigm of increasing ROS production, suppressing ROS clearance, and integrating combination therapies: high-Z and Fenton/Fenton-like systems amplify the generation of •OH and other radicals during irradiation, while inhibition or depletion of the GSH/Trx/NRF2 antioxidant network reduces ROS dissipation. Together, these strategies establish an integrated “source generation–pathway inhibition–downstream amplification” framework for effective radiosensitization.

2.3.

## TME–remodeling mechanisms of radiosensitization
The hypoxia, acidity, high reducing capacity, immunosuppression, and dense stroma of the TME jointly drive radioresistance. Nanomaterials can remodel oxygen supply, pH/metabolism, redox balance, immunity, and matrix mechanics, thereby eliciting indirect yet durable biological effects that collectively enhance radiosensitivity. Molecular oxygen can “fix” radiation-induced radical damage, whereas hypoxia markedly reduces the oxygen enhancement ratio (OER) and leads to radioresistance [ ]. Accordingly, oxygen supply/generation is a major strategy for radiosensitization. Yang et al. designed a hollow manganese dioxide nano-platform (H-MnO2) that exhibits catalase-like activity in H2O2-rich TMEs, catalyzing the in situ decomposition of H2O2 into O2 and thereby alleviating hypoxia and significantly improving therapeutic efficacy [ ]. Building on this principle, Murphy et al. developed O2-releasing MnO2 NPs (with 50:50 PLGA–MnO2 as the optimal formulation). By exploiting the hydrophobicity of the polymer to fine-tune the catalytic O2-release kinetics from endogenous H2O2 in bone-metastatic TMEs, these particles continuously corrected hypoxia, downregulated HIF-1α and immunosuppressive genes, reduced regulatory T cells (Tregs), and enhanced natural killer (NK) cell cytotoxicity, thus achieving long-term control of hypoxic lesions and prolonging survival in tumor-bearing mice [ ]. Calcium peroxide nanoparticles (CaO2 NPs) represent another innovative strategy for cancer therapy; in acidic TMEs, they decompose to release O2 and H2O2, alleviating hypoxia and thereby enhancing tumor suppression in murine triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) models [ ].Perfluorocarbons (PFCs) are inert, gas-dissolving liquids. PFC-based nanoemulsions have been shown to deliver oxygen to tissues and significantly extend the lifetime of 1O2. PFC nanodroplets can extravasate and accumulate within tumors; upon ultrasound activation, they undergo acoustic droplet vaporization (ADV) to generate O2 microbubbles, acting as “oxygen shuttles” that provide on-demand local oxygenation and a practical route to RT enhancement [ ].The acidic TME drives DNA repair and promotes invasive behavior. Nano–CaCO3 (CaCO3 nanoparticles) can buffer extracellular pH toward physiological levels and selectively inhibit the growth and migration of MDA-MB-231 cells without affecting fibroblasts [ ]. In other words, buffering/neutralization strategies such as nano–CaCO3 can increase peritumoral pH, suppress acid-dependent invasion, repair, and metastasis-related phenotypes, and create a more favorable milieu for combination RT. From a metabolic standpoint, reducing lactate burden and transport can relieve functional suppression of myeloid cells and T cells, improve antitumor immunity, and enhance RT response [ ]. Redox reprogramming of the TME has been discussed in Section 1.2 and is not reiterated here.
The TME is typically immunosuppressive. RT can induce ICD in tumor cells, promoting dendritic cell (DC) maturation and cross-priming, thereby enhancing CD8+ T cell–mediated tumor clearance and triggering systemic antitumor responses. Nanomaterials can amplify this process to achieve radiosensitization. By re-oxygenating tumors and elevating ROS levels, nanoplatforms further strengthen ICD signals (e.g., calreticulin exposure, ATP release, HMGB1 release). In addition, Mn2+-containing nanomaterials can activate the cGAS–STING pathway, reinforcing type I interferon responses and further augmenting distant antitumor immunity [ , ]. At the same time, the hypoxia–HIF-1α–PD-L1 axis–driven immunosuppression blunts RT–immune synergy. Therefore, nanomaterial-based alleviation of hypoxia combined with PD-L1 downregulation can improve T cell infiltration and synergize with immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs), achieving more effective immune modulation and thereby enhancing radiosensitivity and systemic tumor control [ , ].
The dense, rigid ECM characteristic of many tumors increases interstitial pressure, restricts drug and oxygen diffusion, and is associated with RT resistance [ ]. Targeting ECM remodeling and vascular normalization can improve perfusion and oxygen delivery, thereby widening the therapeutic window for RT and combination therapies. In pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDA), abundant stromal hyaluronan (HA) forms a major barrier to the perfusion, diffusion, and convection of small-molecule therapeutics. Provenzano et al. used PEGPH20 (a recombinant hyaluronidase) to deplete stromal HA in spontaneous PDA models, durably remodeling the TME, markedly enhancing drug and blood flow into tumors, and providing the physical basis for oxygen-mediated RT enhancement [ ]. Regarding vascular normalization, Cantelmo et al. showed that tumor endothelial cells (ECs) are highly glycolytic. Partial loss or inhibition of the glycolytic activator PFKFB3 reduced VE-cadherin endocytosis in ECs, thereby tightening the vascular barrier while normalizing tumor vasculature (improving vessel maturity and perfusion). This in turn decreased cancer cell invasion, intravasation, and metastasis, and facilitated tumor oxygenation [ ].
Overall, TME-remodeling nanoradiosensitization centers on multidimensional synergy—oxygen supply/generation, pH and metabolic buffering, redox reprogramming, relief of immunosuppression, and ECM/vascular normalization. These strategies reinforce oxygen-dependent radiosensitization and radical chemistry, potentiate RT-induced ICD and systemic (abscopal) immunity, and optimize intratumoral perfusion and molecular diffusion. Such approaches offer on-demand spatiotemporal activation with durable effects, complementing RT as well as ICI- and DDR–targeted strategies. They are particularly suited to hypoxic, desmoplastic, and poorly perfused tumor phenotypes, with the potential to lower the effective dose threshold and expand the therapeutic window.

2.4.

## Nanomaterial-mediated biological mechanisms of radiosensitization
In nanomaterial-based radiosensitization systems, beyond the aforementioned physical dose enhancement, ROS amplification, and TME remodeling, an increasing body of evidence indicates that “biological radiosensitization” via targeting key intracellular signaling pathways is also a critical determinant of therapeutic efficacy.
On the one hand, nanoplatforms can interfere with the DDR and repair pathways, making it difficult for ionizing radiation–induced DNA double-strand breaks (DSBs) to be repaired in a timely manner and thereby amplifying cell killing at a given physical dose. Inhibition of DDR targets such as ataxia-telangiectasia mutated (ATM), ataxia-telangiectasia and Rad3-related (ATR), DNA-dependent protein kinase catalytic subunit (DNA-PKcs), and poly(ADP-ribose) polymerase (PARP) has been validated as an important strategy to overcome radioresistance [ ]. Nanocarriers can increase the intratumoral accumulation and exposure time of such small-molecule inhibitors, markedly enhancing both local tumor control and systemic benefit when combined with RT and PARP inhibitors. For example, Ven et al. formulated the PARP inhibitor olaparib into a nanosystem (NanoOlaparib), which significantly increased γ-H2AX foci formation and improved RT-mediated tumor growth inhibition in multiple radioresistant prostate cancer cell lines [ ]. Tian et al. encapsulated the ATM inhibitor KU55933 into tumor-targeted polymeric nanoparticles (NP-KU55933), achieving more sustained DDR inhibition and stronger suppression of clonogenic survival than free drug, and consequently demonstrating superior radiosensitizing effects in a NSCLC xenograft model [ ].
On the other hand, cell cycle redistribution represents a key phenotypic basis of nanomaterial-induced radiosensitization. Radiosensitivity is tightly linked to the cell cycle phase, with G2/M-phase cells being the most sensitive to irradiation. Thus, inducing G2/M arrest in tumor cells via nanomaterials can increase the overall biological effect without escalating the radiation dose [ ]. Nie et al. systematically compared the effects of different valence selenium agents combined with X-rays and found that various selenium-based nanomaterials markedly enhanced RT-induced G2/M arrest and activated caspase-3–mediated apoptosis, thereby inhibiting lung cancer cell proliferation and migration [ ]. Similarly, in a prostate cancer model, shiitake polysaccharide–modified selenium nanoparticles (LET-SeNPs) developed by Zou et al. significantly increased radiosensitivity and tumor control by reinforcing G2/M arrest, suppressing DNA repair, and downregulating anti-apoptotic proteins [ ].Building on this principle, other metal- or polymer-based nanoplatforms have been designed to exploit G2/M enrichment. For instance, Guo et al. developed a prostate-specific membrane antigen (PSMA)–targeted high-Z nanoplatform that not only increased local dose deposition but also substantially prolonged G2/M-phase residency of prostate cancer cells and weakened their DNA repair capacity, thereby achieving dual radiosensitization via dose enhancement and cell-cycle regulation [ ]. Roberts et al. reported that AuNPs in veterinary tumor RT models induced pronounced G2/M arrest and reduced repair efficiency, resulting in increased radiation-induced cell killing [ ].
In addition, nanoplatforms can further amplify the biological damage after RT by modulating multiple forms of programmed cell death. One class of nanomaterials preferentially induces mitochondrial apoptosis. For example, AgNPs combined with γ-irradiation upregulated Bax and caspase-3, downregulated Bcl-2, and were accompanied by mitochondrial membrane potential loss and cytochrome c (Cyt c) release, thereby enhancing radiosensitivity in hepatocellular carcinoma cells [ ]. Another class acts through ferroptosis, autophagy regulation, or ICD. Li et al. constructed an Fe–Bi–based metal–organic framework (MOF) nano–ferroptosis inducer that, upon activation by RT, triggered lipid peroxidation and glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4) inhibition. This design not only improved local RT killing efficiency but also amplified ICD signals and antitumor immune responses via the release of ferroptosis-associated damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) [ ].Furthermore, clinical-stage high-Z nanoradiosensitizers such as NBTXR3 have been shown, upon RT activation, to induce lysosomal membrane permeabilization and lipid peroxidation. Their ability to enhance both local and distant antitumor immune responses can be partially explained by the combined contribution of ferroptosis and ICD [ ].
Taken together, nanomaterial-mediated biological radiosensitization operates through a multi-node synergy of “impairing repair, reprogramming the cell cycle, and reshaping cell death modes,” thereby markedly amplifying biological effects at an equivalent physical dose. Mechanistically, these processes complement the above-described physical dose enhancement, ROS-centered chemical amplification, and TME remodeling, transforming nanoplatforms from passive energy carriers into finely programmable intracellular signal modulators. This lays the foundation for constructing an integrated “physical–chemical–microenvironmental–biological” radiosensitization framework. In other words, these four mechanisms should not be regarded as isolated and parallel processes.
Rather, they constitute a coordinated radiosensitization cascade with extensive cross-talk. Physical dose enhancement frequently serves as the initiating event by increasing local energy deposition and secondary-electron production [ ], which in turn amplifies ROS generation and other chemical effects. These oxidative events are further modulated by tumor microenvironmental factors such as hypoxia, acidosis, redox buffering capacity, vascular status, and immune suppression [ , , , , ]. Meanwhile, nanomaterial-mediated microenvironmental remodeling can sustain and broaden the physicochemical damage by improving oxygen availability, depleting antioxidants, and relieving immunosuppressive barriers [ , , , ]. Ultimately, these upstream changes converge on downstream biological responses, including DNA repair inhibition, cell-cycle redistribution, regulated cell death, and antitumor immune activation [ , , , ]. In turn, biological events such as ICD may further reshape the TME, forming a positive-feedback loop that reinforces radiosensitization [ , , ].
Based on this integrated perspective, summarizes the interactive framework of nanomaterial-enabled radiosensitization, highlighting how physical triggering, chemical amplification, microenvironmental reprogramming, and biological execution cooperate across multiple spatial and temporal scales to improve radiotherapeutic efficacy. This mechanistic framework underpins the subsequent development of an integrated “physical–chemical–microenvironmental–biological” nanoradiotherapy system at the levels of delivery system design, multimodal synergy, and clinical translation.

## Nanomaterials as delivery systems to assist radiotherapy and enable theranostics
3.1.

## Tumor passive-targeting delivery systems
Conventional radiosensitizers, such as small-molecule agents used in RT, are often limited by high systemic toxicity, non-specific biodistribution, short plasma half-life, and photosensitivity, making it difficult to balance efficacy, safety, and dose controllability. To address these issues, nanoplatforms can improve in vivo delivery via “passive targeting.” Owing to abnormal architecture, increased permeability of tumor neovasculature, and impaired lymphatic drainage/slow venous return, solid tumors often exhibit the EPR effect (as illustrated in ), which facilitates nanoparticle extravasation and selective retention in the tumor interstitium. This, in turn, increases local drug exposure and delivery efficiency while reducing off-target damage to normal tissues [ , , ].
 5-Iodo-2′-deoxyuridine (IUdR), a classic radiosensitizer, is limited in clinical use by systemic toxicity and photosensitivity, and is therefore often combined with nano- or local-delivery strategies to reduce adverse effects and enhance intratumoral exposure. Kargar et al. developed a nanoplatform based on poly (lactic-co-glycolic acid) (PLGA)–functionalized nano–graphene oxide (NGO) (PLGA–NGO) for IUdR delivery. Under near-infrared (NIR) and X-ray irradiation, IUdR-loaded PLGA–NGO significantly reduced clonogenic survival and exhibited the highest cytotoxicity in U87MG glioma cells, indicating efficient radio–photothermal targeted combination therapy [ ].Similarly, Khoei et al. constructed a poly(ε-caprolactone)–poly(ethylene glycol) (PCL–PEG)-coated superparamagnetic iron oxide nanoparticle (SPION) carrier for IUdR loading (IUdR/MNPs). Human glioma U87MG cells effectively internalized this nanosystem, and the combination of IUdR/MNPs + X-rays + hyperthermia markedly decreased clonogenic formation and increased the proportions of apoptotic and necrotic cells [ ].These findings indicate that SPIONs/PCL–PEG can serve as a carrier for IUdR to enhance the cytotoxic effects of RT and hyperthermia, functioning as a combined radio–thermal sensitizer.
Tirapazamine (TPZ) is a prototypical hypoxia-activated prodrug (HAP). In hypoxic TMEs, TPZ undergoes bioreductive activation by cellular reductases to generate cytotoxic radicals/intermediates that induce DNA strand breaks and selectively kill hypoxia-associated, radioresistant tumor cells, thereby complementing RT. However, its clinical use is limited by systemic toxicity, therapeutic inconsistency arising from heterogeneous tumor hypoxia, and suboptimal in vivo delivery. To improve efficacy and safety, Pan et al. used mesoporous silica–gold nanoparticles (MSN–AuNPs) as carriers to load TPZ and modified the surface with hyaluronic acid (HA), constructing a RT-sensitizing nanoplatform (HA@TPZ–MSN–AuNPs, HTMA). In an in vivo pancreatic cancer model, HTMA exhibited superior antitumor activity and biocompatibility compared with TPZ alone [ ].
Collectively, these studies demonstrate that nanomaterials offer an engineering route for the “second development” and combinatorial use of traditional radiosensitizers by prolonging intratumoral exposure, improving selectivity, and optimizing pharmacokinetics/toxicokinetics. It should be emphasized, however, that inter-patient variability in the EPR effect, tumor heterogeneity, reticuloendothelial system (RES) clearance, and batch-to-batch consistency at scale remain major bottlenecks. The integration of active targeting and image guidance with rigorous dosimetry and good manufacturing practice (GMP) compliance will be crucial to advancing clinical translation.

3.2.

## Tumor-specific/active-targeting delivery systems
Despite its advantages, the EPR effect exhibits marked interindividual variability and spatiotemporal heterogeneity, and reliance on “passive leakage” alone often fails to achieve robust tumor accumulation. Recent evidence suggests that active endothelial transcytosis, rather than simple paracellular leakage, is the dominant route by which many drugs enter solid tumors. This has shifted delivery-system design from “making vessels leakier” toward “being actively transported by the endothelium.”
Nanomaterials can be engineered with tumor-specific targeting ligands on their surface to achieve precise delivery and markedly increase selective accumulation in tumor tissues. Through the combination of passive targeting and active targeting, nanomedicines can improve the bioavailability of conventional drugs and reduce systemic toxicity [ ]. Therefore, integrating active and passive targeting is an ideal strategy for designing nanoplatforms to support tumor radiotherapy.
3.2.1.

## Ligand-based targeting
Ligand-modified nanoplatforms—using antibodies or antibody fragments, aptamers, peptides, or small molecules—are key tools to “unlock” active transport pathways by engaging receptor-mediated endocytosis and transcytosis. CDDP -based NPs hold promise for enhancing NPC treatment, but their radiosensitizing efficacy and tumor specificity remain suboptimal. Meanwhile, epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) is highly overexpressed in multiple epithelial tumors, including head and neck squamous cell carcinoma, NSCLC, colorectal cancer, and glioblastoma. To exploit this, Şeniz et al. constructed a gold nanoparticle (GNP)–based nanocomposite co-conjugated with cetuximab (CTX, an anti-EGFR monoclonal antibody) and CDDP. This nanodrug significantly increased the radiosensitivity and cytotoxicity of UPCI-SCC-131 cells via ligand–receptor–mediated active targeting [ ]. Joohee et al. developed an EGFR antibody–conjugated liposomal nanoplatform (EGFR: LP) loaded with CDDP (EGFR: LP–CDDP). In an A549-derived xenograft mouse model, EGFR: LP–CDDP combined with RT significantly delayed tumor growth, while treated mice showed no significant differences from untreated controls in terms of body-weight loss, survival, or nephrotoxicity [ ].
Proteins overexpressed in malignant tissues are important targets for developing targeted therapies, and aptamers have emerged as attractive affinity ligands for therapy and drug delivery. Given nucleolin overexpression in breast cancer and the high affinity of AS1411 for nucleolin, Ghahremani et al. prepared BSA-shelled gold nanoclusters (Apt–GNCs) conjugated with the AS1411 aptamer. In 4T1 cells, Apt–GNCs increased tumor-cell uptake by approximately fourfold and achieved significant radiosensitization under MV RT, enhancing tumor cell killing with a dose enhancement factor (DEF) of ∼2.7 in clonogenic assays [ ].Among widely used targeting ligands, peptides containing the arginine-glycine-aspartic acid (RGD) sequence are notable. RGD exhibits high affinity for the integrin αvβ3 receptor, which is overexpressed in many solid tumors. RGD thus effectively targets multiple cancer types and has been extensively adopted in nanoplatform-based oncology applications. Ding et al. designed the SPHN platform, in which an RGD-conjugated amphiphilic block copolymer encapsulated SPION-based radiosensitizers and CDDP to form a core–shell structure. When combined with RT, SPHN specifically accumulated in NPC tumor regions in vivo and significantly enhanced tumor-growth inhibition [ ].
Similarly, Tang et al. developed a DSPE–RGD–PEG3500-grafted liposomal nanoplatform that encapsulated gemcitabine (GEM), generating RGD–GEM–LPs. This system exhibited stronger antiproliferative effects and sustained-release behavior in SKOV3 cells in vitro. In vivo imaging confirmed favorable tumor-targeting capacity and robust antitumor efficacy. Given that GEM is a classic radiosensitizer, this platform shows considerable potential for use in RT-based combination regimens [ ]. Certain tumor types overexpress highly specific surface targets, such as PSMA, which can be harnessed for active targeting. Afsharzadeh et al. prepared self-assembled PEG–PLA NPs co-loaded with guggulsterone (GBA) and docetaxel and decorated them with ACUPA❲(S)-2-(3-((S)-5-amino-1-carboxypentyl) ureido)pentane dioic acid❳, a small-molecule PSMA ligand. ACUPA modification markedly increased nanoparticle uptake in PSMA+ prostate cancer cells and enhanced cytotoxicity toward LNCaP cells [ ].
Compared with healthy tissues, folate receptors—especially folate receptor-α (FRα)—are highly expressed in many epithelial tumors, most notably ovarian cancer and lung adenocarcinoma, but show low expression in most normal tissues, making folate an attractive active-targeting ligand. Based on this, Menon et al. designed an integrated core–shell–targeting multifunctional nanoplatform (MDNP). The PLGA core was loaded with NU7441 (a DNA-PK inhibitor and potent radiosensitizer), while the shell consisted of a semi–interpenetrating network (SIPN) of poly(N-isopropylacrylamide) (PNIPAm) and carboxymethyl chitosan (CMCS) carrying GEM, and folate was conjugated on the surface for active targeting. MDNP exhibited biphasic NU7441 release (initial burst plus sustained release), pH-dependent GEM release (accelerated under acidic TME/endosomal–lysosomal conditions), and good stability, hemocompatibility, and in vitro cytocompatibility. In addition, it was efficiently internalized by lung cancer cells in a dose-dependent manner via caveolae-mediated endocytosis. These results indicate that MDNP is a promising nanocarrier to simultaneously deliver chemotherapy and achieve radiosensitization for lung cancer treatment [ ].
In summary, ligand-mediated active targeting using antibodies, aptamers, peptides, or small molecules can markedly enhance receptor-mediated endocytosis and transcytosis of nanoplatforms at tumor sites, enabling efficient accumulation and coordinated release of radiosensitizers and chemotherapeutics (as illustrated in ). This improves therapeutic efficacy while reducing systemic toxicity and widening the systemic therapeutic window. Nonetheless, performance is constrained by target-expression heterogeneity, ligand density/affinity and multivalency, and endosomal escape after internalization. Future engineering strategies—such as dual-/multi-target and switchable ligands, TME-responsive release, and subcellular targeting—may help overcome the “binding-site barrier,” further improving radiosensitization and translational feasibility in combination RT.
 
3.2.2.

## Biomimetic targeting
Compared with conventional ligand-based strategies, biomimetic coatings such as cell membranes or exosomes endow nanoplatforms with “natural” targeting advantages, including immune evasion, homotypic adhesion, and transendothelial transport. When combined with high-Z cores (e.g., Au/Bi/Hf), TME-modulating components (e.g., MnO2), or chemical/genetic radiosensitizers, such systems frequently show enhanced DNA damage markers (e.g., γ-H2AX upregulation) and tumor growth inhibition, providing strong support for RT enhancement.For example, Wang et al. used hollow mesoporous manganese dioxide (HMnO2) as a carrier to efficiently load the photosensitizer indocyanine green (ICG), followed by coating with HeLa cell membranes to obtain M-HMnO@ICG nanoparticles (M-HMnO@ICG NPs). The membrane camouflage further increased tumor accumulation and conferred partial immune evasion, thereby enhancing drug delivery and therapeutic efficacy [ ]. HMnO2 can also contribute to radiosensitization through multiple mechanisms, indicating that this system has strong potential for combination with RT. Chen et al. developed a nanoplatelet system (PCA), in which gold nanocages (AuNs) loaded with CDDP were encapsulated within platelet membranes. This design improved the immune stealth and targeting capacity of AuNs. After administration, PCA actively adhered to tumor cells and avoided rapid immune clearance. CDDP released from PCA damaged tumor-cell DNA and directly killed tumor cells, while AuNs concentrated radiation energy within the tumor, jointly reducing RT resistance. In vitro and in vivo, PCA effectively inhibited tumor proliferation under low-dose RT (2 Gy), enhanced the efficacy of conventional RT drugs, and did not induce inflammatory side effects [ ].
Beyond single-membrane coatings, hybrid membranes can further balance targeting and immune evasion. Sun et al. constructed gold nanocages loaded with doxorubicin (DOX) and coated them with a hybrid membrane derived from breast cancer cells and erythrocytes (CM–EM–GNCs@DOX). This hybrid coating combined the strong homotypic targeting of cancer-cell membranes with the immune camouflage of RBC membranes, leading to high tumor accumulation and reduced clearance. Efficient uptake of CM–EM–GNCs@DOX by cancer cells, together with the radiosensitizing effect of the nanocages, significantly enhanced RT efficacy and allowed dose reduction [ ].
Hybrid membranes can also improve multi-cell–type targeting within the TME. Cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) are key contributors to radioresistance by virtue of their intrinsic radiation tolerance and radiation-induced phenotypic adaptation. Chang et al. developed a biomimetic MOF nanoplatform (IZB) for dual-targeted co-delivery of the focal adhesion kinase (FAK) inhibitor IN10018 and bismuth (Bi) to enhance cervical cancer radiosensitivity. IZB NPs were further coated with a hybrid membrane derived from CAFs and cancer cells, enabling precise targeting of both cell types [ ]. This dual-targeting strategy illustrates how integrating nanotechnology with targeted therapy can improve RT efficacy and patient outcomes.
In summary, biomimetic membrane cloaking provides nanoplatforms with a dual advantage of “arriving at the site” (targeting/adhesion) and “staying at the site” (immune evasion/prolonged circulation). When combined with conventional radiosensitization strategies, such systems can markedly improve tumor accumulation and DNA damage markers (e.g., γ-H2AX), achieving stronger tumor suppression at lower RT doses and supporting multimodal synergy, as illustrated in . Current bottlenecks include membrane-source consistency and traceability, interference from protein corona formation, potential immunogenicity, and scalable manufacturing. Engineering strategies such as hybrid/multitarget membrane design, quantitative surface-protein quality control, and microfluidics-based GMP processes are expected to improve reproducibility and translational feasibility.
 
3.2.3.

## Other targeting strategies
In addition to ligand-based and biomimetic targeting, several other concepts can be exploited for “active targeting” in RT-enhancing nanoplatforms: Physical field–guided targeting (e.g., external magnetic fields) can drive SPIONs or magnetic hybrid carriers to accumulate within the irradiated volume, and SPIONs themselves tend to act as radiosensitizers for multiple tumor cell types [ ].Temporal/RT-priming targeting uses a low-dose or fractionated pre-irradiation to transiently increase vascular permeability, inflammatory adhesion, and myeloid-cell infiltration, thereby enhancing subsequent nanoparticle extravasation and residence time in tumors [ ].Bioorthogonal pretargeting involves metabolic glycoengineering to introduce azido groups on tumor cell surfaces, followed by intravenous administration of nanodrugs bearing complementary groups such as dibenzocyclooctyne (DBCO). Copper-free click reactions in vivo “lock” the nanoplatforms onto tumor cells at high density, enabling strong anchoring [ ].Albumin hitchhiking across the endothelium leverages pathways such as gp60/scavenger receptor class B type 1 (SR-B1) and SPARC (secreted protein acidic and rich in cysteine) to promote transcytosis and stromal anchoring, providing a broadly applicable route for transendothelial active transport in many tumor types [ ].These strategies can be combined with high-Z cores or pharmacologic radiosensitizers (e.g., CDDP prodrugs, PARP or DNA-PK inhibitors). Typical readouts include reduced clonogenic survival, increased γ-H2AX/53BP1 foci, and enhanced in vivo tumor growth inhibition. By optimizing the timing of administration within pre- and post-irradiation windows, such approaches can further expand the therapeutic window of RT.

3.2.4.

## Interpatient variability and limitations of tumor targeting in vivo
Despite the growing diversity of targeting strategies, tumor targeting in patients should not be regarded as a uniform or guaranteed outcome, but rather as a highly variable and context-dependent process. The in vivo accumulation and intratumoral distribution of nanoplatforms may differ markedly not only among patients, but also among distinct lesions within the same patient, owing to variations in vascular permeability, stromal density, interstitial fluid pressure, immune-cell infiltration, prior treatment history, and organ-specific microenvironmental features [ , , ]. Importantly, these factors affect not only passive EPR-based retention, but also the actual performance of active targeting strategies by limiting endothelial transport, receptor accessibility, ligand–receptor engagement, and deep tumor penetration [ , , ]. Consequently, even well-designed ligand-based, biomimetic, or externally guided systems may fail to achieve consistent tumor selectivity or reproducible therapeutic benefit in clinical settings. This patient-level and lesion-level heterogeneity therefore represents a major limitation of nano-enabled radiotherapy platforms [ , ].
Given the inherent variability and limitations of tumor targeting in vivo, further improvement in delivery precision often requires not only preferential accumulation, but also selective activation after tumor entry. Distinct biochemical features of the tumor microenvironment, together with externally applied physical triggers, therefore provide an additional level of control for nanoplatform-mediated radiotherapy.

3.3.

## Stimuli-responsive delivery systems
3.3.1.

## Endogenous stimuli
Distinct features of the TME compared with normal tissues—such as hypoxia, acidity, and a highly reducing milieu with elevated GSH—can be exploited to achieve stimulus-responsive drug release from nanoplatforms. This enables controlled, TME-triggered cargo release, minimizes off-target exposure, and enhances RT efficacy. The TME is typically mildly acidic, with an extracellular pH of ∼6.5–6.9 versus ∼7.4 in normal tissues. This difference can be harnessed to design pH-responsive nanotherapeutics. Zhao et al. prepared a pH-responsive nanodrug (AuNRs–Mn3O4–Ag2S Ve) by emulsification/self-assembly, co-encapsulating an NIR-II (950–1700 nm) fluorescent probe (Ag2S quantum dots), a multifunctional nanozyme (Mn3O4 nanoparticles), and gold nanorods (AuNRs, as radiosensitizers) within a pH-sensitive polymer (P4VP). After intravenous administration, the nanodrug disassembled in the acidic tumor region, releasing AuNRs and Mn3O4 NPs that effectively enhanced radiosensitization in tumors [ ].Du et al. further combined homotypic/platelet biomimetic targeting with pH-responsive release to design [Dbait–ADM@ZIF-8]OPM nanoparticles( ). In this system, a ZIF-8 MOF nanocarrier loaded the radiosensitizer Dbait and the classical chemotherapeutic DOX, and was coated with a hybrid membrane derived from osteosarcoma cells and platelets (OPM). In the TME, the pH-sensitive ZIF-8 framework disassembled to release its payload. Owing to the excellent targeting of the hybrid membrane and the high loading capacity of the carrier, [Dbait–ADM@ZIF-8] OPM significantly improved RT efficacy in tumor-bearing mice with minimal observable toxicity [ ].
 Hypoxia is another hallmark of the TME. Exploiting this feature in combination with surface-antigen targeting, Hua et al. constructed an ALP–(MIs)n nano–delivery system. This platform used a hydrophobic core of a multi–prodrug polymer P-(MIs)n (based on metronidazole, MIs), co-assembled with Angiopep-2–DSPE–PEG2000 and lecithin via one-step nanoprecipitation. The resulting nanoplatform could be further loaded with DOX to form ALP–(MIs)n/DOX. Under hypoxic conditions, DOX was preferentially released, while MIs were reduced to radical intermediates that mimic oxygen fixation of DNA damage and act as radiosensitizers. Benefiting from Angiopep-2–mediated BBB penetration and glioma targeting, this system not only achieved efficient drug delivery but also triggered hypoxia-specific radiosensitization, resulting in synergistic chemo–radiotherapy against glioma [ ].
The high-GSH environment of the TME provides specific triggers for “chemical locks” such as disulfide bonds. Iyer et al. developed dual-stimuli nanoparticles (E-DSNPs) comprising: (1) a core in which a disulfide linkage enabled GSH-triggered release of encapsulated CDDP; and (2) a shell designed to release the DNA-PK inhibitor NU7441 in response to irradiation. The nanoparticle surface was functionalized with EphA2 antibodies to target Ephrin type-A receptor 2 (EphA2), which is highly expressed on lung cancer cells. Lung cancer cells, but not normal lung epithelial cells, showed high uptake of E-DSNPs, with successful dual-triggered release of both drugs, resulting in markedly reduced in vitro cancer cell surviving fractions (∼0.019 and ∼0.19 in different treatment arms) [ ].
In summary, TME-oriented stimuli—acidic pH, hypoxia, reducing/high-GSH conditions, and radiation itself—provide a spatiotemporally controlled “arrive first, release later, then synergize with RT” strategy for nanoplatforms. Under conditions of pH ≈ 6.5–6.9, low oxygen, high GSH, or irradiation, cargoes such as high-Z cores, chemotherapeutics, and DDR inhibitors are selectively released, amplifying oxygen fixation and DNA damage, increasing intratumoral exposure, and substantially lowering systemic toxicity and the effective dose. Key challenges include TME heterogeneity and fluctuating trigger thresholds, the trade-off between circulation stability and release kinetics, as well as scalability and safety evaluation. Coupling multiple “locks,” integrating hybrid biomimetic coatings, and using quantitative imaging–pharmacokinetic readouts can further improve reproducibility and translational feasibility.

3.3.2.

## Exogenous stimuli
In addition to endogenous cues, exogenous stimuli such as X/γ irradiation, ultrasound, light, magnetic fields, and heat can be used to “press the button” and unlock drug release or activation specifically within the irradiated volume/energy-deposition zone, spatiotemporally aligning payload activation with RT.
For X-ray–responsive systems, anticancer drug–loaded nanocarriers that release cargo upon irradiation are often referred to as radiation-controlled drug delivery systems (DDSs). A key challenge is to minimize the radiation dose required to trigger drug release. Zhang et al. constructed an efficient DDS based on a diselenide-containing block copolymer that was dual-responsive to X-rays and the high-ROS TME. At relatively low radiation doses (2 Gy), the system was activated to release its payload. In a mouse xenograft model, the loaded drug accumulated effectively in tumors and, in combination with X-ray irradiation, produced pronounced antitumor effects (lowest tumor vol/wt) without evident systemic toxicity [ ]. A similar shell design was employed in the E-DSNPs system described in Section 2.3.1.
Heat-triggered release (hyperthermia) can serve as a programmable “switch” to locally activate nanodrugs within tumors. Thermosensitive liposomes (TSLs) are a prototypical example: they are stable at physiological temperature but rapidly release their cargo under mild local hyperthermia (∼42–43 °C), thereby confining radiosensitizer exposure to the heated tumor volume. Besse et al. demonstrated this concept using ThermoDox (a DOX-loaded TSL) in combination with hyperthermia and RT. ThermoDox induced cytotoxicity and enhanced radiosensitization only when combined with hyperthermia, effectively restricting sensitizer toxicity in non-heated normal tissues while boosting therapeutic efficacy in heated tumors [ ]. The same principle has been validated preclinically and clinically. In the phase I TARDOX trial, focused ultrasound was used to generate mild hyperthermia within liver tumors, triggering localized ThermoDox release and increasing intratumoral DOX concentrations by approximately 3.7-fold compared with pre-heating levels, thereby confirming a spatially co-localized, dose-controlled “heat–drug–RT” synergy framework [ ].
NIR light can also function as an external switch to control intratumoral drug release. You et al. designed a folate (FA)-modified biodegradable liposomal system co-loading ICG and CDDP. Under 808 nm NIR irradiation, ICG mediated photothermal conversion, inducing lipid-membrane phase transitions and gradual degradation, thus promoting on-demand drug release and enhancing RT [ ].Beyond these modalities, ultrasound and magnetic fields can serve as additional exogenous triggers, synchronizing their effects with RT in the same volume and time frame to achieve cooperative radiosensitization.
Overall, exogenous stimuli-responsive nanodelivery systems utilize physical triggers, including X/γ irradiation, heat, light, ultrasound, and magnetic fields, to achieve precise spatiotemporal control over drug release, such that the irradiated volume matches the release volume and the irradiation time aligns with the activation time. This design enables enhanced local radiosensitization while minimizing systemic exposure, with a schematic illustration of ultrasound-triggered release shown in . Their advantages include spatiotemporal co-localization, dose controllability, and programmable activation. Major bottlenecks include trigger thresholds at clinically acceptable radiation doses, energy attenuation in deep tissues (e.g., for NIR), spatial uniformity of thermal/ultrasound fields, and the calibration of dose–release relationships with real-time imaging monitoring. By integrating multi-lock designs and imaging–dose–thermal/acoustic feedback loops, and combining these systems with active targeting and EPR, it may be possible to further improve reproducibility and translational feasibility.
 

3.4.

## Theranostic integration
Beyond directly enhancing radiation-induced cytotoxicity, nanoplatforms can leverage targeted delivery to achieve “theranostics” (integrated therapy and diagnosis). Pi et al. constructed a core–shell CeO2–MnO2 nanocomposite as a novel radiosensitizer [ ]. The MnO2 shell catalyzes intratumoral H2O2 into O2, thereby alleviating hypoxia in the TME, while the CeO2 core, owing to its relatively high atomic number, enhances radiation energy deposition and promotes ROS generation, jointly improving radiosensitivity. In addition, degradation of the nanocomposite releases Mn2+, enabling T1-weighted MRI for real-time monitoring and imaging guidance. Taken together, CeO2–MnO2 integrates microenvironment modulation, radiosensitization, and imaging, providing an effective theranostic strategy for tumor radiotherapy [ ].
Fluorescent carbon dots doped with functional metal ions also enable integrated imaging and therapy. For example, Shi et al. synthesized Gd/Ru co-doped carbon dots (Gd/Ru-CDs) via a one-step microwave approach [ ]. Gd and Ru doping endowed the carbon dots with excellent MRI contrast properties and significantly improved their fluorescence (particularly in the red-emission region), while maintaining high ROS-generating capacity, low cytotoxicity, and favorable biocompatibility. In vivo, Gd/Ru-CDs achieved light-induced tumor suppression and allowed non-invasive monitoring of the treatment process via dual FL/MRI imaging [ ]. Such “imaging + therapy” carbon dots provide a transferable model for RT scenarios: they can serve as tools for image guidance and response assessment, and can be integrated with low-dose X-ray–based strategies (e.g., RT–radiodynamic therapy [RT–RDT] or X-PDT) to further enhance theranostic management of deep-seated tumors.
Similarly, Lipengolts et al. designed a Fe3O4@Au core–shell theranostic nanosystem in which the gold shell provides a high-Z component for physical radiosensitization, whereas the Fe3O4 core serves as an MRI contrast agent, yielding combined CT/MRI visibility [ ].PEG- and glucose-coated Fe3O4@Au NPs were intravenously injected into C57Bl/6 mice bearing Ca755 mammary adenocarcinomas, followed by CT/MRI scans at multiple time points between 5 and 102 min post-injection and on day 17. The NPs markedly enhanced contrast in tumors and tumor vasculature, and no signs of toxicity were observed throughout the observation period [ ].Overall, Fe3O4@Au combines CT/MRI dual-modal imaging with potential radiosensitization, enabling real-time assessment of nanoparticle biodistribution and treatment monitoring, and offering an image-guided theranostic platform for RT. Tumor hypoxic regions are closely associated with malignancy and represent critical targets for high-precision diagnosis and therapy [ ]. Yuki et al. developed a hypoxia-targeted theranostic agent, SPION-PG-NI. This system uses polyglycerol-functionalized SPIONs (SPION-PG, core diameter 8.8 ± 1.9 nm) as the MRI contrast core, and grafts 2-nitroimidazole (NI) onto the surface to confer hypoxia recognition. In a murine glioblastoma model, SPION-PG-NI produced concentration-dependent contrast enhancement and showed significantly higher accumulation at 24 h post-injection compared with SPION-PG controls. Immunohistochemistry demonstrated strong colocalization between SPION-PG-NI–rich regions and hypoxia markers, with no evidence of nanoparticle penetration into normal brain parenchyma or induction of neurotoxicity. In terms of RT synergy, while both formulations modulated ROS and exerted some cytotoxicity, SPION-PG-NI achieved significantly stronger radiosensitizing effects in hypoxic tumor cells (p < 0.01). At day 38 after 12 Gy irradiation, the mean normalized tumor volume in the SPION-PG-NI group was 288%, significantly lower than 882% in controls (p < 0.05) [ ]. These findings indicate that SPION-PG-NI enables hypoxia imaging and RT co-potentiation, providing a safe and effective theranostic solution for precise identification and management of hypoxic tumor regions.
More broadly, several “generic” platforms can serve as translational references. AGuIX, an ultrasmall Gd-based nanoparticle, combines T1-MRI contrast and high-Z–mediated energy deposition to enable image-guided RT [ , ].Tantalum oxide (TaOx) NPs function as excellent contrast agents for X-ray/CT imaging and, by virtue of their high atomic number, also exert radiosensitizing effects, making them suitable for integrated “imaging assessment + RT synergy” applications [ ].
Collectively, theranostic nanoplatforms spatiotemporally couple “imaging–sensitization–response assessment”: they visualize intratumoral accumulation and microregional features such as hypoxia or TME heterogeneity, while simultaneously amplifying local energy deposition and ROS effects. This supports imaging-based individualized dosimetry loops and treatment monitoring. Their advantages include traceable delivery, tunable dosing, and controllable toxicity, and they can be combined with RT, chemotherapy, and immunotherapy in multimodal regimens. Future work must address quantitative correlation between imaging signals and actual dose/therapeutic effect, long-term safety and clearance of heavy metals, scalable GMP-compliant production, and cross-center standardization, in order to accelerate clinical translation.

3.5.

## Comparative analysis of delivery strategies
Passive targeting is structurally simple and broadly applicable, and remains attractive for nanosystems requiring scalable fabrication and prolonged tumor exposure; however, its performance is highly dependent on the heterogeneity of the EPR effect across tumor types and patients [ , , ]. Active or bioinspired targeting can improve cellular uptake, transendothelial transport, and regional selectivity through ligand engineering, membrane camouflage, or external-field guidance, but this strategy often increases formulation complexity and may still be limited by biological barriers in vivo [ , , ]. Stimulus-responsive delivery offers superior spatiotemporal control by coupling drug release or radiosensitizer activation to endogenous tumor cues or exogenous triggers, making it particularly suitable for reducing off-target toxicity and synchronizing activation with irradiation; nevertheless, its translational potential depends on trigger penetration, response thresholds, and reproducibility under clinically relevant conditions [ , , ]. Theranostic systems add an imaging dimension that enables visualization of biodistribution, target delineation, and treatment monitoring, and are therefore especially valuable for personalized RT and adaptive treatment paradigms, although they face additional challenges in quantitative imaging–therapy correlation, long-term biosafety, and manufacturing standardization [ , , ]. Overall, passive targeting is advantageous for simplified delivery, active targeting for enhanced selectivity, stimulus-responsive systems for controllable release, and theranostic platforms for image-guided precision management. In practice, these strategies are often complementary rather than mutually exclusive, and rational integration of two or more modules may provide the most effective design route for nanoradiosensitizer delivery. Therefore, the selection of delivery strategy should be guided by the therapeutic objective, tumor accessibility, dominant biological barriers, and translational requirements such as simplicity, reproducibility, and imaging compatibility.
In summary, this chapter has systematically reviewed, from four perspectives—passive accumulation, active/bioinspired targeting, stimulus-responsive delivery, and theranostics—the key strategies and characteristics by which nanoplatforms enable precise delivery, controllable activation, and imaging guidance in radiotherapy. Nanodrugs can passively accumulate in tumor lesions by exploiting abnormal tumor vasculature and the EPR effect, and can also achieve active transendothelial transport and precise targeting of tumor cells via ligand modification, cell-membrane camouflaging, or externally applied magnetic fields ( A and B). On this basis, endogenous TME characteristics such as acidity, elevated GSH, and hypoxia, as well as exogenous triggers including NIR light, ultrasound, magnetic fields, and X/γ-rays, can be harnessed to realize on-demand release and cooperative activation of drugs and high-Z cores, thereby markedly increasing effective intratumoral drug exposure and radiosensitization within the irradiated volume ( C). Furthermore, some platforms integrate CT/MRI/fluorescence imaging modules with radiosensitizers to construct theranostic nanosystems that enable visualization of intratumoral distribution and real-time assessment of therapeutic response ( D). The coupling of these delivery and imaging functions provides the engineering and technological foundation for subsequent multimodal combination regimens and for clinically translating an integrated “drug administration–planning–irradiation–evaluation” paradigm in nanotechnology-enabled RT.
 

4.

## Multimodal synergistic therapeutic strategies
As outlined in Section 2.3.2, many exogenous stimuli can be used not only to trigger drug release from nanocarriers but also as independent treatment modalities. When such stimuli (e.g., light, heat, ultrasound) are combined with RT, and two or more modalities are co-localized and temporally coordinated on a single nanoplatform, a prototype of multimodal therapy emerges. In the RT context, “multimodal synergy” arises when RT is integrated with chemotherapy, immunotherapy, or other physical therapies on the same nanosystem, enabling spatiotemporal co-localization and sequential regulation. The core advantage is not simply additive (“1 + 1 > 2”) but lies in nanometer-scale energy coupling, chemical amplification, and immune reprogramming. These mechanisms collectively remodel the TME, oxygen metabolism, and DNA damage repair pathways, thereby enhancing radiosensitivity, widening the therapeutic window, and reducing normal-tissue injury.
4.1.

## RT–PTT systems
Nanomaterials exhibit unique advantages in RT–PTT combination systems. High-Z nanomaterials can not only enhance radiosensitivity but also generate localized hyperthermia upon NIR light irradiation [ ]. This synergy operates via dual mechanisms: on the one hand, PTT can increase tumor vascular permeability and ameliorate hypoxia in the TME, thereby improving RT response [ ]; on the other hand, ionizing radiation can induce additional ROS production from the nanomaterials, leading to a cooperative cytotoxic effect between RT and hyperthermia [ ]. Such dual-modality systems have demonstrated superior therapeutic outcomes compared with monotherapy in multiple tumor models [ ].
For example, gold nanorods can achieve NIR photothermal conversion efficiencies exceeding 90%, while their high atomic number significantly increases radiation energy deposition [ ].In a “greener” approach, Ghaffarlou et al. synthesized Au@Alg-DA nanoparticles (Au@Alg-DA NPs). In 4T1 breast cancer cells, combination treatments with Au@Alg-DA plus either X-rays (4 Gy, 6 MV) or NIR irradiation (808 nm, 1 W/cm2, 5 min) reduced cell viability more than controls, whereas the triple combination (Au@Alg-DA + X-rays + NIR) yielded the lowest clonogenic survival (∼0.37) and the highest levels of cell death. In murine models, the triple-therapy group exhibited the most pronounced tumor regression with no obvious toxicity in major organs [ ].MXenes, a class of 2D nanomaterials with strong photothermal properties and programmable surfaces, are particularly suitable as platforms for RT–PTT multimodal therapy because they can be conveniently assembled with high-Z metals and chemotherapeutics. Zhu et al. designed a platform termed APMP, consisting of MXene nanosheets as the core, coated with a shell of gold nanorods and CDDP, and further modified with poly(vinyl alcohol) (PVA). Compared with RT alone, APMP increased ROS generation to approximately 2.6-fold, significantly enhancing radiosensitivity. The combination of APMP with both PTT and RT enabled complete tumor ablation and overcame radioresistance. In a glioblastoma mouse model, the APMP + RT + PTT group showed the strongest tumor shrinkage, with higher apoptosis and lower proliferation markers than other groups [ ]. A schematic representation of this MXene-based APMP nanoplatform and its RT–PTT synergistic mechanism is shown in .
 Overall, nanomaterials facilitate RT–PTT multimodal synergy through spatiotemporally co-localized “energy-deposition amplification plus on-demand thermal control.” This approach alleviates hypoxia, reverses radioresistance, and increases ROS load while coordinating with drug and immune effects. Consequently, effective doses can be reduced, normal-tissue toxicity minimized, and tumor control rates substantially improved, offering clear translational potential for image-guided and individualized RT.

4.2.

## RT–chemotherapy co-loaded platforms
Classical agents such as paclitaxel and CDDP are widely used in concurrent chemoradiotherapy, but conventional administration is limited by heterogeneous biodistribution, systemic toxicity, and the difficulty of precisely matching peak drug exposure to fractionated RT time windows. Nanocarriers provide an ideal platform for spatiotemporal coordination of RT and chemotherapy: by facilitating tumor accumulation and programmable release, they allow pharmacologic and radiation effects to be superimposed in the same volume with optimized timing. Co-loading chemotherapeutics (e.g., CDDP, docetaxel) and nanoradiosensitizers (e.g., hafnium oxide, gadolinium oxide) into a single nanoplatform can achieve controlled drug release and cooperative radiosensitization [ ].These platforms offer three main advantages: (1) enhanced tumor accumulation via the EPR effect, which reduces systemic toxicity [ ]; (2) RT-induced damage to tumor vasculature that improves nanoparticle extravasation and drug release [ ]; and (3) chemotherapeutics that suppress post-RT tumor-cell repair, yielding a “radiosensitization–chemotherapy synergy” [ ].Endogenous stimuli-responsive nanocarriers (e.g., pH- or GSH-sensitive systems) can further improve precision by triggering drug release specifically within the TME [ ].Preclinical studies have shown that such co-loaded platforms can increase tumor growth inhibition by 2–3-fold while markedly reducing chemotherapy-induced damage to normal tissues [ ].
For instance, Chen et al. prepared mesoporous Ta2O5 NPs modified with PEG (mTa2O5–PEG). These NPs served as a drug-delivery platform for DOX (mTa2O5–PEG/DOX) and exhibited pH-responsive release [ ]. The high-Z tantalum component provided intrinsic radiosensitization during RT by enhancing X-ray–induced DNA damage. Simultaneously, the mildly acidic TME promoted DOX release, leading to significant synergy between RT and chemotherapy, with reduced adverse effects. Heavy-metal-based nanoscale metal–organic frameworks (nMOFs) can also serve as potent radiosensitizers by enhancing energy deposition and ROS production. Xu et al. exploited this property and covalently incorporated an X-ray–activatable prodrug (an SN38 prodrug) into a Hf-based nMOF to obtain Hf-TP-SN [ ]. Upon X-ray irradiation, amplified •OH generation both potentiated RT and triggered local SN38 release, with a fivefold increase in SN38 liberation compared with a molecular control. In colon and breast cancer mouse models, Hf-TP-SN effectively suppressed tumor growth. Similarly, as shown in , the SPHN nanoplatform enables combined chemoradiotherapy and achieves significant inhibition of tumor growth in vivo, holding promise as a versatile nanotechnology platform for future chemoradiotherapy of NPC.
 In summary, nanocarriers provide a spatiotemporally co-localized framework for “radiation energy deposition–chemotherapy-induced damage.” Through tumor enrichment and stimuli-responsive release (acidic, reductive, or irradiation-triggered), chemotherapeutic inhibition of repair and high-Z–mediated dose/ROS amplification are superimposed within the same irradiated volume at optimal time points. This markedly improves tumor control while limiting systemic toxicity. Future work should focus on quantitative coupling among dose, drug release, and fractionation schedules, as well as imaging-based monitoring, alongside comprehensive pharmacokinetic/toxicokinetic evaluation and GMP-consistent manufacturing to enable clinical translation.

4.3.

## RT–immunotherapy synergistic mechanisms
Nanomaterials play a pivotal bridging role in RT–immunotherapy combinations. RT-induced ICD releases tumor antigens, while nanoplatforms can concomitantly deliver ICIs, such as anti–PD-1/PD-L1 antibodies, and radiosensitizers to generate an “in situ vaccine” effect [ ]. The synergistic mechanisms include: (1) high-Z nanomaterials augmenting RT-induced DNA damage and thereby enhancing tumor antigen release and cross-presentation [ ]; (2) nano-delivered immunomodulators reversing the immunosuppressive TME, promoting DCs activation and T cell infiltration [ ]; and (3) cooperative activation of type I interferon signaling by RT and nano-immunotherapy [ ].
Recent studies have demonstrated that MOFs and related nanomaterials can serve as both radiosensitizers and carriers for immunoadjuvants, triggering robust systemic antitumor immunity and effectively suppressing distant metastases after local RT [ ]. For example, Wang et al. designed a bifunctional Hf-based MOF (MOF/MSA-2) for RT–immunotherapy. The framework consists of Hf6 secondary building units (SBUs) and a hexakis (4′-carboxy[1,1′-biphenyl]-4-yl) benzene linker, with the STING agonist MSA-2 covalently conjugated. The Hf6 SBU enhances X-ray RT and elicits strong immune stimulation in the TME, whereas MSA-2 provides sustained STING activation and promotes immune-cell infiltration and activation within tumors, further amplifying RT. MOF/MSA-2 induced potent STING activation and marked tumor regression under low-dose X-ray irradiation [ ]. Deng et al. reported a related strategy using biomineralized MnO2 NPs loaded with anti–PD-L1 antibody (αPDL1@MnO2) to enhance RT-induced systemic antitumor immunity [ ].This therapeutic nanoplatform significantly increased tumor-cell killing by overcoming hypoxia-driven radioresistance and remodeling the immunosuppressive TME, effectively inducing ICD. In acidic TME conditions, released Mn2+ activated the cGAS–STING pathway and promoted DC maturation, while αPD-L1 released from αPDL1@MnO2 enhanced cytotoxic T lymphocyte (CTL) infiltration and triggered systemic antitumor responses with pronounced abscopal effects, thereby inhibiting metastasis y [ ].
Tumor associated macrophage (TAM) reprogramming is another key avenue in cancer immunotherapy. When combined with RT, TAM modulation can enhance antigen presentation, promote M1 polarization, and alleviate immunosuppression, generating substantial synergy. For instance, Zhang et al. developed a multifunctional nanoparticle (PIC) composed of polylysine, iron oxide, and CpG oligonucleotides [ ].Under RT, PIC improved antigen presentation, increased the intratumoral M1:M2 TAM ratio, and strengthened type I interferon responses. In syngeneic, immunologically “cold” mouse tumor models, the RT + PIC + immune checkpoint blockade (ICB) combination significantly improved tumor responses and overall survival, achieving cures in multiple mice and establishing durable tumor-specific immune memory [ ]. A schematic illustration of RT- and PIC-mediated TAM reprogramming and the conversion of immunologically “cold” tumors into “hot” tumors is shown in .
 Taken together, nanoplatform-enabled RT–immunotherapy synergy offers a powerful approach to overcoming tumor immune escape [ ].It closes the loop from “local RT-induced antigen release” to “systemic immune mobilization”: on one side, it amplifies energy deposition and ICD, activates cGAS–STING, and remodels the TME (relieving hypoxia, reducing immunosuppression, and promoting M1 polarization); on the other side, it delivers ICIs and immunoadjuvants with spatial and temporal precision, enhances abscopal effects, and suppresses metastases, with the potential to reduce RT/ICI doses and systemic toxicity at equivalent efficacy. In combination with DDR inhibitors, tumor vaccines/personalized neoantigen strategies, and radiomics-guided RT, such “RT + nano-immunotherapy” composite regimens may convert immunologically cold tumors into hot ones, expand the population that benefits from immunotherapy, and achieve more durable systemic disease control.

4.4.

## Comparative analysis of nanoplatform-enabled RT-based combination strategies
In RT–photothermal combinations, nanoplatforms can simultaneously serve as photothermal converters, radiosensitizers, and tumor-localizing carriers, thereby enabling localized heat generation, improving tumor oxygenation/perfusion, and amplifying oxidative and DNA damage within the irradiated region. This design is particularly suitable for achieving rapid local tumor control [ , , ]. However, at the nanoplatform level, its performance may still be limited by heterogeneous intratumoral distribution of photothermal agents, uneven heat generation, and the mismatch between laser penetration and nanoparticle accumulation depth; more broadly, light attenuation also restricts its applicability in deeply seated tumors [ , ]. In RT–chemotherapy combinations, nanoplatforms are particularly valuable for synchronized co-delivery, schedule-controlled release, and reduction of off-target toxicity, allowing chemotherapeutic agents to interfere more effectively with DNA replication, mitosis, or damage repair during irradiation [ , , , ]. Nevertheless, this strategy still faces challenges related to drug resistance, loading/release optimization, and the coordination of drug pharmacokinetics with RT fractionation, while broader chemotherapy-associated toxicity also remains relevant [ , ]. In RT–immunotherapy combinations, nanoplatforms provide an effective means for the co-delivery of immunomodulators and radiosensitizers, thereby enhancing ICD, improving antigen presentation, and reprogramming the immune microenvironment so that local radiation-induced damage can be more efficiently translated into systemic antitumor immunity [ , , , , , ]. However, its translational performance may still be limited by inefficient or heterogeneous intratumoral delivery of immunomodulators, difficulty in balancing immune activation with safety, and substantial interpatient immune heterogeneity; in addition, broader immunotherapy-related adverse events remain a clinical concern [ , , ]. Overall, nanoplatform-enabled RT–PTT is more suitable for localized and accessible tumors requiring strong local ablation; nanoplatform-enabled RT–chemotherapy is more advantageous when precise co-delivery and cytotoxic reinforcement are prioritized; whereas nanoplatform-enabled RT–immunotherapy is particularly valuable when durable systemic control and immune memory are desired. In practice, the selection of platform-assisted synergistic modalities should be guided by tumor location, biological phenotype, therapeutic objectives, and translational feasibility, and these strategies may also be rationally integrated into more complex multimodal nanosystems.
In summary, this chapter has systematically discussed three representative multimodal paradigms—RT–photothermal, RT–chemotherapy, and RT–immunotherapy—highlighting how nanoplatforms construct a synergistic framework through spatially co-localized energy deposition, dose/drug-release coordination, and immune reprogramming. At the nanoscale, different therapeutic modalities ultimately converge on several common critical processes—imbalance between DNA damage and repair, amplified ROS burden, and remodeling of the TME—thereby achieving an integrated antitumor effect that combines local radiosensitization with systemic disease control. As illustrated in , nanoplatforms function as convergence hubs for irradiation, thermal/photonic energy, chemotherapeutics, and immunomodulators within a unified spatiotemporal window. While these multimodal paradigms share common mechanistic foundations, they differ substantially in their strengths, limitations, and preferred application scenarios. To provide a clearer comparative overview of the major nanomaterial categories used in RT, we have added , which summarizes their representative radiosensitization mechanisms, major toxicity concerns, biodegradability, imaging roles, and current level of clinical maturity.
 

5.

## Clinical translation: key issues
5.1.

## Dose-distribution optimization and RT planning
The primary challenge for clinical application of nanomaterials as radiosensitizers is to achieve precise and controllable spatial dose distribution. High-Z nanomaterials (e.g., gold-based agents) can enhance the photoelectric effect and increase secondary/Auger electron yield, thereby amplifying local energy deposition within tumors and improving RT response. To translate this physical enhancement into deliverable clinical benefit, however, “nanoinformation” must be accurately integrated into the treatment planning system (TPS) [ , ].
Previous studies have shown that, without increasing the total prescribed dose, metal-based nanomaterials can substantially elevate the DEF and relative biological effectiveness in the tumor region via secondary electron emission [ ]. Nevertheless, the magnitude of radiosensitization is influenced by tumor type, beam energy spectrum, tissue microenvironment, and heterogeneous nanoparticle distribution, leading to pronounced variability across lesions and anatomical sites. This necessitates individualized dose-calculation and optimization models based on nanoparticle distribution, such as voxel-level DEF mapping and target-specific weighting schemes [ ]. Recent advances indicate that AI-assisted plan optimization can integrate nanoparticle spatial distribution inferred from multimodal imaging, thereby achieving high concordance between dose “hot spots” and tumor targets while improving prescription coverage under organ-at-risk (OAR) constraints [ ].
5.1.1.

## Nanodosimetry and physical modeling
High-Z NPs can markedly increase the photoelectric effect in the keV energy range and generate high-dose “hot spots” at the cellular and subcellular scales. This phenomenon has been validated in animal and cell experiments [ ], and can be quantitatively analyzed using Monte Carlo frameworks such as Geant4/Geant4-DNA to compute microscopic dose and secondary electron spectra. These simulations yield particle size/material/energy-dependent DEF curves and parameter spaces that reproduce experimental observations [ , , ].
On the clinical side, to avoid underestimating hot spots with a simplified “homogeneous medium” assumption, DEF should be incorporated into the dose engine as voxel-wise weighting, constructing a “nanomodulated dose voxel (NDV)” map that can be used for parameterized optimization within the TPS (see below).

5.1.2.

## Quantification of distribution and TPS parameterization
Theranostic nanoplatforms with imaging visibility, such as Gd-based AGuIX that provides T1-weighted enhancement, offer the basis for “what-you-see-is-what-you-plan” parameterization. Early clinical data in patients with brain metastases have demonstrated the safety and feasibility of AGuIX and revealed a clinically useful intratumoral retention window, allowing quantitative mapping of nanoparticle distribution prior to planning and during follow-up verification [ , ].
On the TPS side, the spatial distribution of NPs can be parameterized as a voxel-wise weighting field and used to modify the prescribed dose or biologically effective dose (BED):
Deff(x)=D(x)∗DEF(x),BEDeff=nd(1+d∗DEF(x)α/β)where is the planned physical dose, is the local dose enhancement factor, is the dose per fraction, and is the number of fractions. Nano-corrected dose and volume histogram (DVH) constraints and robustness margins can then be imposed for both target volumes and organs at risk (OAR). In parallel, knowledge-based planning and deep-learning–based dose prediction can be incorporated as a second channel for plan quality assurance, improving consistency and efficiency while preserving machine deliverability [ , ].

5.1.3.

## Motion and uncertainty management
The spatiotemporal distribution of nanomaterials can drift when superimposed on tumor motion (respiration, organ deformation). It is therefore advisable to use 4D-CT/4D-MRI combined with deformable image registration to generate time-resolved NDV maps and perform phase-weighted robust optimization; respiratory management and adaptive workflows can follow the recommendations of AAPM TG-290/TG-324 consensus reports [ ]. Online, MR-Linac systems enable daily imaging and on-line adaptive planning, which have been shown across multiple tumor sites to improve DVH target coverage while respecting OAR constraints, with favorable dosimetric gains and safety profiles [ ]. For proton therapy, in addition to conventional range and setup uncertainties, “nanoparticle distribution uncertainty” should be incorporated into joint robust optimization. Recent methodological advances in robust planning based on linear energy transfer (LET) and dose–LET volume histogram (DLVH; 3D LET–volume histogram) analysis provide a useful conceptual framework and terminology for implementing such approaches.

5.2.

## In vivo pharmacokinetic characteristics
The in vivo absorption, distribution, metabolism, and excretion (ADME)/pharmacokinetic (PK) behavior of nanomaterials directly determines the temporal window of radiosensitization and the optimal schedule for RT combination. Intratumoral accumulation efficiency depends not only on blood perfusion, interstitial pressure, and tissue architecture, but is also shaped by clearance via the mononuclear phagocyte system (MPS), dynamic formation of the protein corona, and material biotransformation [ , ]. Systematic evidence indicates that, under clinically translatable conditions, the median fraction of injected dose reaching solid tumors is low and highly variable between individuals, suggesting that a simple “homogeneous EPR assumption” is insufficient to guide RT timing and prescription. Instead, actual distribution patterns and PK parameters should be used to back-calculate optimal “drug–irradiation” schedules [ , ].
5.2.1.

## Determinants of intratumoral accumulation and the “time window”
Intratumoral accumulation results from the combined effects of vascular perfusion, permeability/interstitial pressure, exosome and stromal barriers, and material–biological interfacial properties. Tumor vessel “normalization” strategies can, within a defined dose window, improve perfusion and tissue penetration in a size-dependent manner, thereby extending the effective post-injection time window for irradiation—an effect particularly evident for small-to-medium nanoplatforms [ ]. In theranostic systems, ultrasmall, imageable NPs (e.g., Gd-based AGuIX with a hydrated diameter of only a few nanometers) enable “distribution imaging–window selection” workflows. Early clinical studies in patients with brain metastases have demonstrated the safety and feasibility of AGuIX and shown that it can remain within lesions for approximately one week, providing a rational basis for fractionation and re-evaluation schedules [ ].

5.2.2.

## Biotransformation and clearance
Different inorganic materials follow distinct biotransformation and clearance routes in the RES, lung, liver, and spleen. For example, ferrite NPs (Fe3O4/γ-Fe2O3) can gradually degrade within lysosomes and enter endogenous iron metabolic pathways, whereas poorly soluble, highly persistent materials such as TiO2 tend to remain in the lung and hepatosplenic tissues for prolonged periods. When combined with RT, these materials require careful assessment and long-term monitoring of organ burden [ , ]. Particle size is a key determinant of clearance route: ultrasmall particles (or their degradation fragments) with hydrated diameters <∼5–6 nm are more likely to undergo renal excretion with short half-lives, whereas larger and/or more hydrophobic systems are predominantly cleared via the hepatobiliary pathway with transient sequestration in the liver and spleen [ ].

5.2.3.

## Protein corona, MPS recognition, and in vivo fate
Upon contact with biological fluids, nanomaterials rapidly acquire a biomolecular corona that reshapes their “biological identity,” thereby influencing circulation time, cellular uptake, and organ distribution. Interfacial engineering strategies (e.g., PEGylation) can extend circulation and reduce nonspecific uptake to some extent, but a balance must be struck between “stealth” and “penetration” to avoid compromising tissue penetration and intratumoral homogeneity [ ].

5.2.4.

## Route of administration and integration with RT planning
Intravenously administered, imageable platforms (e.g., AGuIX) are well suited to “injection → quantitative imaging → windowed irradiation” workflows. In contrast, intratumoral radiosensitizing injectables (e.g., HfO2 NPs such as NBTXR3) are characterized by high local concentration and sustained retention, making them more compatible with pre-defined RT schedules and concurrent external-beam treatments. NBTXR3 has already shown pathological response benefits and acceptable safety in randomized trials of soft-tissue sarcoma [ ].

5.2.5.

## PK–planning integrated modeling and decision-making
Population PK and physiologically based pharmacokinetic (PBPK) analyses indicate that the median tumor delivery efficiency of many nanomedicines is in the low single-digit percentage range and decays over time; therefore, parameters such as , , and should be integrated with quantitative functional imaging (MRI/positron emission tomography (PET) or intrinsic contrast) to define the optimal drug–irradiation timing and fractionation strategy, and key PK metrics should be brought forward into the planning optimization and longitudinal follow-up stages [ ].

5.3.

## Framework for long-term biosafety assessment
Clinical translation of nanotechnology-enabled RT requires a life-cycle–spanning framework for long-term safety assessment and risk management. Although metal-based nanomaterials (e.g., gold and silver NPs) can substantially enhance RT response, their persistent retention and chronic accumulation may induce organ-specific toxicities and immune reactions. The magnitude of these risks is jointly determined by core composition, particle size/shape, surface chemistry, and protein corona dynamics [ , , , ]. Accordingly, safety evaluation must extend beyond “short-term tolerability” to include biopersistence, biotransformation, degradation/ion release, immuno- and hemocompatibility, reproductive and neurotoxicity risks, and environmental exposure, and must be closely linked to RT planning, dosing schedule, and follow-up protocols [ , ].
In the RT setting, particular attention should be given to: (i) chronic exposure to metal ions or degradation fragments and associated organ burden; (ii) localized inflammation and fibrosis in sensitized “hot spot” tissues; (iii) the impact of different dose metrics (mass, surface area, particle number) on toxicological interpretation; and (iv) the boundary between synergistic and antagonistic interactions with ionizing radiation [ ]. To ensure comparability and reproducibility, material characterization and biological testing should follow standardized guidelines, accompanied by minimum information reporting standards to support cross-study comparison and meta-analysis [ , ]. International frameworks such as the ISO 10993 series, OECD guidance on nanomaterial safety testing and dosimetry, and Minimum Information Reporting in Bio–Nano Experimental Literature (MIRIBEL) reporting standards provide practical tools for establishing auditable and traceable risk-assessment and regulatory pathways [ , ].
5.3.1.

## Evaluation dimensions and methodological considerations

(1)Biopersistence and systemic distribution.Long-term fate should be characterized by multi–time point tissue/blood quantification, imaging-based tracking, and PBPK modeling. AuNPs generally exhibit good biocompatibility but may cause long-term tissue deposition and surface chemistry–dependent inflammatory risks. AgNPs, due to soluble Ag+ release and ROS stress, are more frequently associated with hepatotoxicity, nephrotoxicity, and vascular endothelial injury, warranting particular caution in scenarios of chronic exposure or repeated dosing [ , , , ].
(2)Immuno- and hemocompatibility. Comprehensive evaluation of complement activation, hemolysis, coagulation cascades, and cytokine profiles is required, with special attention to complement activation-related pseudoallergy (CARPA)-like reactions and delayed inflammatory amplification. Because protein corona formation reshapes biological identity and markedly alters MPS uptake, circulation time, and organ distribution, corona characterization should be incorporated into material release specifications [ , , ].
(3)Reproductive/developmental and neurotoxicity risks. Some nanomaterials can cross the placental barrier or BBB, with probabilities strongly influenced by particle size, charge, and surface modification. In reproductive-age or pregnant populations and in patients with central nervous system disease, stricter inclusion/exclusion criteria and extended follow-up are warranted [ , , , , ].
(4)Degradation/ion release and long-term metabolic impact. For degradable or ion-releasing systems (e.g., Ag, Gd complexes, certain metal oxides), accelerated and long-term release studies should be performed under physiologic and RT-relevant conditions. Ion exposure should then be quantitatively linked to organ functional endpoints (hepatic/renal function, hematopoiesis, cardiopulmonary indices) [ , ].
(5)Interactions with RT. Ionizing radiation may alter surface chemistry and local microenvironments, thereby modifying toxicokinetics. Toxicological validation should therefore be conducted under clinically relevant fractionation, total dose, and energy spectrum conditions to ensure that “sensitization gains” are not achieved at the cost of unacceptable long-term risks.

5.3.2.

## A tiered, stepwise safety evaluation system
Tier 0: Material–process–dosimetry prequalification. Following ISO/TR 10993-22 and ISO 10993-1, comprehensive material characterization should be performed (size/distribution, morphology, surface chemistry, solubility/dispersion, zeta potential, electron microscopy/spectroscopic analyses, endotoxin levels), together with dose metrics (mass, surface area, particle number). Sample preparation/dispersion protocols and in vivo/in vitro dosimetry must conform to OECD guidelines. Preparation of a MIRIBEL-compliant minimum information report is essential for traceability [ , ].
Tier 1: In vitro multi-endpoint screening. This tier includes assays of cytocompatibility, oxidative stress and mitochondrial function, genotoxicity, inflammatory and immune-related readouts, and hemocompatibility, supplemented by evaluation of surface chemistry and protein corona changes under RT-equivalent conditions (post-irradiation).
Tier 2: Multi–time point in vivo studies in small animals. Acute and subchronic toxicity, whole-body distribution and long-term retention, hepatic/renal/hematologic/cardiopulmonary function, and histopathology should be assessed. When necessary, reproductive and developmental toxicity and neurobehavioral tests should be performed, with PBPK-based extrapolation to clinical starting doses and drug–RT timing strategies.
Tier 3: Early-phase clinical trials and long-term follow-up. Phase I/II trials should focus on feasibility and initial safety—including imaging-based monitoring of residuals/clearance—compatibility with RT, and preliminary signals of radiosensitization. Subsequently, real-world data and registry studies should be used to build long-term safety databases. Existing evidence indicates that Gd-based ultrasmall NPs such as AGuIX and HfO2-based NBTXR3 exhibit acceptable safety profiles and synergistic RT effects in multiple early-phase and randomized studies, providing initial human evidence for the risk–benefit balance of nanoradiotherapy [ , ].
Ultimately, the key to long-term safety evaluation is not “how many tests are performed,” but whether the most decision-relevant evidence for clinical translation is generated in a standardized, traceable manner. With robust material characterization and dosimetry as prerequisites, integrating biopersistence/distribution, immunohematologic effects, reproductive/neurotoxicity, ion release, and RT interactions into a tiered, reusable evidence system—aligned with planning optimization and longitudinal monitoring—is essential to safely translate the radiosensitization benefits of nanotechnology into clinical practice [ , ].

5.4.

## Why promising nanoplatforms stall in translation
Taken together, the issues discussed in Sections .3 indicate that the clinical translation of nano-enabled RT platforms are limited not simply by whether they can enhance radiation response in principle, but by whether that enhancement can be integrated into radiotherapy planning, delivered reproducibly in vivo, and sustained within an acceptable long-term safety and manufacturing framework. In this sense, the translational bottleneck reflects both the specific demands of nanoradiotherapy and the broader challenges that have long slowed the clinical development of nanomedicine more generally.
First, as highlighted in Section , even impressive preclinical radiosensitization does not automatically translate into clinically useful benefit unless nanoparticle-mediated enhancement can be quantitatively incorporated into treatment planning. The spatial magnitude of dose amplification depends not only on material composition and beam energy, but also on heterogeneous intratumoral distribution, tissue context, and temporal retention. Without imaging-based mapping of nanoparticle accumulation, nanodosimetric modeling, and treatment-planning-system integration, the apparent gain observed in preclinical studies may remain disconnected from real radiotherapy workflows. This problem is particularly important for nanoplatforms because their efficacy is inseparable from where, when, and how uniformly they accumulate relative to the irradiated volume.
Second, as discussed in Section , many promising nanoplatforms fail because their in vivo delivery behavior is far less controllable in patients than in preclinical models. Tumor accumulation in murine xenografts is often overinterpreted as evidence of effective “targeting,” whereas meta-analyses have shown that the median fraction of injected NPs reaching solid tumors is very low, and that delivery varies markedly with tumor type, vascular phenotype, stromal density, and host physiology [ , ]. In addition, nanoparticle entry into tumors is not governed solely by passive EPR leakage; active transendothelial transport, immune-cell trafficking, and tumor-associated phagocyte sequestration can all reshape intratumoral distribution [ ]. These observations reflect a broader nanomedicine problem: the gap between “designed properties” and actual biological identity in vivo. Once exposed to blood and tissue fluids, nanomaterials rapidly acquire a biomolecular corona that can alter colloidal stability, circulation half-life, immune recognition, organ tropism, and even the accessibility of targeting ligands [ ]. Consequently, nanoplatforms that perform well in small, rapidly growing, highly permeable animal tumors may fail in patients with heterogeneous lesions, prior treatments, fibrosis, hypoxia, and more complex blood-flow patterns [ , , ].
Third, as emphasized in Section , successful translation requires much more than favorable short-term efficacy. Multifunctional radiosensitizers often combine material composition, payload release, imaging visibility, radiation responsiveness, and clearance behavior within a single system, which substantially increases the difficulty of long-term safety assessment, batch reproducibility, and regulatory evaluation under clinically relevant fractionation conditions [ , , ]. Small changes in size distribution, surface chemistry, or batch purity may shift pharmacokinetics, safety, and radiosensitization performance, while complex multi-component formulations are difficult to scale and standardize under GMP conditions [ , ]. Moreover, many preclinical studies still rely on simplified endpoints such as short-term tumor growth delay, single-dose irradiation, or subcutaneous models, which do not fully capture the practical requirements of clinical radiotherapy, including treatment-planning integration, image-guided timing of administration, repeated fractionation, and long-term toxicity follow-up [ , , ].
Therefore, the translational barrier is not merely a lack of efficacy, but a lack of robustness, comparability, and clinical fit. Future progress will likely depend on simplifying nanoplatform architecture, prioritizing formulations with clear structure–activity relationships and scalable chemistry, integrating biodistribution imaging and nanodosimetric information earlier into treatment design, and using orthotopic or immunocompetent models with clinically relevant irradiation schemes and long-term follow-up. Standardized reporting frameworks such as MIRIBEL should also be incorporated earlier in development [ , , , ]. In this sense, the most clinically promising nanoplatforms may not be the most complex ones, but those that achieve the best balance among radiosensitization potency, planning compatibility, manufacturability, safety, and compatibility with real-world radiotherapy practice.

6.

## Future perspectives and translational opportunities
Building on the translational barriers outlined above, future progress in nanoradiotherapy will likely depend on advances in four interconnected directions: standardized and scalable manufacturing, biomarker-guided personalized treatment, artificial intelligence–assisted system optimization, and multicenter translational validation.
6.1.

## Toward standardized and scalable manufacturing of nanoradiotherapy platforms
A critical step toward the clinical adoption of nanoradiotherapy platforms is the establishment of standardized and scalable manufacturing strategies. Although many nanomaterials have demonstrated strong potential in radiotherapy enhancement, their translation into clinically useable products requires far greater control over synthesis reproducibility, physicochemical uniformity, and long-term stability than is typically achieved in proof-of-concept studies. High-Z nanomaterials such as gold-based NPs remain promising, but their scale-up is often limited by batch-to-batch variability and insufficiently controlled surface modification [ ]. Likewise, two-dimensional materials such as MXenes provide attractive functional properties for radiotherapy applications, yet their large-scale synthesis and storage stability remain insufficiently mature for industrial deployment [ ]. In addition, standardized industrial specifications for key parameters—including particle size distribution, surface chemistry, impurity profile, colloidal stability, sterilization compatibility, and shelf-life—have yet to be clearly established for many metal-based or multifunctional nanoplatforms [ ]. As these parameters directly influence biodistribution, safety, and therapeutic consistency, improving manufacturing robustness is not merely a technical requirement but a translational opportunity [ ]. Future efforts should therefore prioritize scalable synthesis routes, simplified material architectures, GMP-compatible processing, and unified quality-control criteria to accelerate the production of nanoradiotherapy platforms that are both high-performing and clinically reliable.

6.2.

## Pathways toward personalized treatment strategies
Epigenetic biomarker–based prediction systems offer new avenues for designing personalized RT regimens. Epigenetic markers identified as predictors of RT response in medulloblastoma provide a molecular basis for tailoring individual treatment strategies [ ]. Advances in radiomics now enable the use of advanced MRI and PET–derived biomarkers to guide personalized RT in glioblastoma [ ].For patients at cardiovascular risk, individualized risk assessment indicates that precise radiation dose allocation must balance tumor control probability against normal organ risk [ ].Moreover, real-time monitoring of TME characteristics—such as hypoxia level and GSH status—can inform the personalized use of microenvironment-responsive nanomaterials (e.g., Mn-based platforms) [ , ],allowing patient-specific adjustment of dosing, timing, and combination schemes to maximize radiosensitization while minimizing toxicity. Biomarker-guided stratification and imaging-based risk assessment have already accumulated a degree of translational evidence in selected tumor types and clinical scenarios [ , ], whereas their integration into nano-enabled radiotherapy platforms remains at a relatively early and predominantly preclinical stage [ ].
In summary, the future of nanoradiotherapy will increasingly depend on biomarker-guided and patient-specific treatment design. By integrating molecular profiling, functional imaging, and dynamic assessment of the tumor microenvironment, nano-enabled radiotherapy platforms may be tailored to individual biological conditions, thereby improving therapeutic precision while reducing unnecessary toxicity.

6.3.

## Artificial intelligence–assisted nanoradiotherapy systems
Artificial intelligence (AI) holds great potential for optimizing nanoradiotherapy systems. By applying machine learning algorithms to tumor heterogeneity data, predictive models can be built to link nanomaterial properties, radiation dose parameters, and therapeutic outcomes [ ]. Deep learning–assisted RT planning systems can integrate nanoparticle-enhanced dose-distribution information to enable automated tumor-target delineation and dose calculation [ ].In the context of graphene-based nanomaterials, AI-supported real-time monitoring systems have been proposed to dynamically regulate treatment courses for neurodegenerative diseases [ ], and similar technological routes are applicable to efficacy assessment in tumor RT. Furthermore, computational simulation platforms can accelerate virtual screening and performance optimization of novel stimuli-responsive nanomaterials [ ], thereby reducing experimental workload and shortening the development cycle from design to preclinical validation. While AI-assisted planning has begun to influence conventional radiotherapy workflows [ , ], the integration of AI into nano-enabled radiotherapy platforms remains at a relatively early stage, with current progress relying largely on computational modeling, design optimization, and preclinical validation rather than established clinical evidence [ ].
Overall, AI is expected to become an important enabling tool throughout the development and implementation of nanoradiotherapy, from material screening and treatment planning to response prediction and adaptive regulation. By improving data integration, automation, and predictive accuracy, AI may help transform nanoradiotherapy from a conceptually promising strategy into a more efficient, precise, and clinically actionable therapeutic system.

6.4.

## Design of multicenter translational clinical studies
Advancing nanoradiotherapy toward clinical implementation will require robust multicenter translational research frameworks. Current evidence suggests that successful translation depends not only on therapeutic efficacy, but also on standardized cross-center evaluation of pharmacokinetics, long-term biosafety, toxicity, and treatment response. Studies of metal-coordination nanomaterials combined with RT highlight the necessity of systematic in vivo pharmacokinetic and biosafety assessment [ , ], whereas work on micro-/nanomaterial-based radiosensitizers for hepatocellular carcinoma underscores the importance of harmonized efficacy criteria, particularly for combined external and internal irradiation regimens [ ]. In parallel, multicenter studies should prioritize validation of nanomaterial-enhanced RT-immunotherapy synergy [ ] and the development of toxicity-management strategies for combination therapies [ ]. Accordingly, a standardized framework integrating RT plan optimization, nanomaterial dose quantification, systematic toxicity monitoring [ , ], pharmacokinetic profiling, long-term biosafety evaluation, and harmonized efficacy assessment [ , , ]will be critical for improving cross-center comparability, supporting regulatory review, and promoting evidence-based clinical implementation. Overall, the future advancement of nanoradiotherapy will depend not on a single technological breakthrough, but on coordinated progress in manufacturing standardization, personalized treatment design, intelligent system optimization, and multicenter clinical validation ( ). Together, these interconnected pathways may accelerate the clinical translation of nano-enabled RT platforms and facilitate their integration into precision oncology.
