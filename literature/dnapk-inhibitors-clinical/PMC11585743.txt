## Main text
Allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell (HSC) transplantation from healthy donors has long been the standard therapy for treating monogenic inherited immunodeficiencies and blood cell disorders, such as Wiskott-Aldrich syndrome, Fanconi anemia, severe combined immunodeficiency, chronic granulomatous disease, and hyper-immunoglobulin M (hyper-IgM) syndromes. However, this approach is limited by the availability of donors and the risk of clinical complications, including infections, graft-versus-host disease, and engraftment failure.
Over the past two decades, HSC gene therapies for monogenic hematological disorders have emerged as a viable alternative to allogeneic transplantation. Techniques such as lentiviral gene addition and CRISPR-Cas9-mediated gene editing of genes or lineage-specific control regions via non-homologous end joining (NHEJ) are particularly effective when the goal is to overexpress or knock out the expression of specific genes. These strategies have been successfully employed in the treatment of monogenic hematological disorders such as adenosine-deaminase-deficient severe combined immunodeficiency, sickle cell disease, and beta-thalassemia major. , However, these gene therapy approaches cannot be applied in hyper-IgM syndromes.
X-linked hyper-IgM syndrome (XHIM) is a primary immunodeficiency disorder characterized by deficient T cell function and Ig class-switching failure, leading to reduced levels of IgG and IgA. This disease is caused by germline loss-of-function mutations in the CD40 ligand (CD40L) gene. In preclinical murine models, retroviruses expressing CD40L cDNA were used to transduce murine bone marrow or thymic cells from CD40L-deficient mice, which were then transplanted. Unfortunately, this resulted in the development of a T lymphoproliferative disorder, suggesting that constitutive non-regulated expression of CD40L can result in neoplastic T cell transformation, necessitating endogenous control of CD40L gene expression levels and regulation. Further complicating this issue, germline mutations in CD40L have been identified throughout the gene without a specific mutational hotspot. Therefore, mutational correction via base editing, prime editing, or other precision medicine approaches is unsuitable for this disease. 
In the September 2024 issue of Molecular Therapy Methods and Clinical Development, Pugliano and co-workers focused on the clinically relevant development of CRISPR-mediated homology-directed repair (HDR)-based gene editing in XHIM. This approach allows for the genomic insertion of wild-type CD40L cDNA under the control of the endogenous promoter. CRISPR-mediated HDR is especially relevant when a single gene contains multiple variants, requiring the design of multiple target-specific guide RNAs for precise correction using base-editing or prime-editing techniques. The authors utilized mobilized peripheral blood stem cells as targets; they electroporated the cells to introduce CRISPR-Cas ribonucleoproteins (RNPs) and delivered the corrective DNA cassette with AAV6.
HDR poses several challenges, particularly low efficiency and toxicity in fragile long-term repopulating HSCs, the target cell population in XHIM. HDR is also limited by cell cycle restrictions, as it is primarily active during the S/G2 phases. Additionally, double-stranded DNA breaks and exogenous repair cassettes introduced during HDR can lead to HSC toxicity by activating the p53 pathway as well as innate intracellular antiviral pathways that promote apoptosis. The vector chosen to deliver editing components into HSCs, such as AAV, can also activate p53-mediated apoptosis. While high levels of HDR editing in CD34+ hematopoietic stem and progenitor cells (HSPCs) can be achieved in vitro, the levels of HDR editing in primitive HSPCs engrafted in xenograft models and particularly in non-human primates are much lower, generally below the predicted therapeutic threshold for most human diseases. , , 
One strategy to increase HDR efficiency is to transiently inhibit the NHEJ pathway using small molecules, such as DNA-dependent protein kinase (DNA-PK) inhibitors. Pugliano et al. applied the DNA-PK inhibitor AZD7648 to increase in vitro HDR efficiency up to 60% in mobilized peripheral blood CD34+ cells with both a marker gene and CD40L cDNA; AAV6 was used to introduce the corrective DNA cassette. They also reported an increased proportion of engrafted HDR-edited HSPCs in vivo with the addition of the DNA-PK inhibitor in primary and secondary recipients (xenotransplant model mice). Importantly, human HSPCs recovered from the engrafted mice demonstrated normal T cell differentiation, CD40L regulation, and activation in an in vitro system. Despite these successes, overall human engraftment in recipients unfortunately decreased by approximately one-third in most experiments, and the sustained levels of HDR were only ∼5%–15%. However, this level of correction may be sufficient to correct the phenotype based on murine models and the milder form of XHIM that presents in female patients with profoundly skewed random X chromosome inactivation.
Overall, caution and further optimization of these approaches are still warranted, especially considering HSC toxicity and the engraftment failure reported in a pioneering HDR-editing clinical trial for sickle cell disease that ultimately led to discontinuation. In conclusion, this study highlights the optimization of HDR-mediated gene editing as a potential clinical tool for treating XHIM. Moreover, the enhancement of HDR efficiency in HSPCs achieved with DNA-PK inhibitors may have therapeutic applications beyond this disease.
