## Abstract
Peposertib is an orally administered inhibitor of DNA‐dependent protein kinase. We evaluated the effect of food on its pharmacokinetics, and examined the pharmacokinetics of an oral suspension (OS) of disintegrated tablets, in a phase I, open‐label, crossover three‐period study (NCT04702698). Twelve healthy volunteers were randomized to one of six treatment sequences. They received a single dose of peposertib 100 mg as film‐coated tablets under fasted or fed conditions (“tablet fasted” or “tablet fed”) or as an OS under fasted conditions (“OS fasted”), with washout between treatments. Using healthy volunteers was possible because, despite its mechanism of action being suppression of DNA repair, peposertib has shown no genotoxic effect in animals. A mild food effect was observed with peposertib tablets. Fed‐to‐fasted ratios were: area under the curve from time 0 to time t (AUC0–t), 123.81% (90% confidence interval [CI]: 108.04, 141.87%); AUC from zero to infinity (AUC0–∞), 110.28% (90% CI 100.71, 120.77%); and maximum concentration (C
max) 104.47% (90% CI: 79.15, 137.90%). C
max was delayed under fed conditions (median time to maximum concentration [T
max] was 3.5 h [tablet fed] vs. 1 h [tablet fasted]). OS‐to‐tablet (fasted) ratios were: AUC0–t, 124.83% (90% CI: 111.50%, 139.76%); AUC0–∞, 119.05% (90% CI: 104.47, 135.67%); and C
max 173.29% (90% CI: 135.78, 221.16%). Median T
max was 0.5 h (OS fasted) versus 1 h (tablet). All treatments were well‐tolerated in healthy volunteers. Peposertib tablets can be taken with or without food; if combined with chemotherapy or radiotherapy, the delay in C
max must be considered to optimize the chemo‐ or radiosensitizing effect. The peposertib OS form represents an alternative route of administration in patients with specific cancers causing dysphagia. However, the OS form should be part of future dose optimization strategies in relevant settings.

## Safety
Three single doses of 100 mg peposertib, administered as two 50 mg tablets under fasted or fed conditions or as an OS of two disintegrated 50 mg tablets under fasted conditions, were well‐tolerated. No deaths, serious adverse events, other significant TEAEs, or discontinuations due to TEAEs occurred. Two of the 12 participants experienced three TEAEs: oropharyngeal pain and oral herpes in one participant, and musculoskeletal stiffness in another participant. All TEAEs were mild (grade 1). No TEAEs were considered related to peposertib. No clinically relevant changes over time were observed for standard laboratory parameters, vital signs, or ECG parameters.

## DISCUSSION
In this food effect and pharmacokinetic study, consumption of a high‐fat, high‐calorie meal had only a mild effect on peposertib exposure following a single dose of 100 mg, but with a delayed C
max. The AUCs for the OS form of peposertib 100 mg and film‐coated tablets were comparable, but a higher C
max was seen with the OS form. The concentration‐time profile of the metabolite, M467, mirrored that of peposertib, with the metabolic ratios of exposure in the range of 33%–42% of the parent drug. The observed pharmacokinetic profile of M467 was independent of peposertib treatment, and no relevant additional accumulation is expected at steady‐state for once‐daily or twice‐daily dosing. Overall, single doses of peposertib 100 mg were well‐tolerated by healthy participants when administered either as tablets or in OS form.
Peposertib shows a significant margin of selectivity for DNA‐PK over its closest family members, phosphoinositide 3‐kinase α, ‐β, ‐δ, and ‐γ, and is inactive against a broad panel of PKs. As functional DNA‐PK confers a degree of resistance to RT and CT by repairing therapy‐induced double‐strand breaks, peposertib could increase the sensitivity of tumor cells to these treatments. Indeed, peposertib has been shown to sensitize multiple cancer cell lines to ionizing radiation. Furthermore, administration of oral peposertib plus ionizing radiation to two xenograft models of human cancer strongly potentiated the antitumor activity of the radiation and led to complete tumor regression at non‐toxic doses. 

Traditionally, anticancer drug development focused on chemotherapeutics, and these could not be administered to healthy volunteers due to ethical concerns over their toxic effects. However, the current immunomodulatory and molecularly targeted drugs are rarely genotoxic and have better safety profiles than chemotherapeutics, and can be administered to healthy volunteers. Furthermore, the use of healthy volunteers instead of patients in clinical pharmacology studies (e.g., those evaluating food effects, drug–drug interactions, and thorough QT trials), is advantageous because recruitment can be faster and dropout rates are lower, rich sampling is easily possible, sampling can be more precisely timed, difficult pre‐analytical procedures are possible, and costs can be lower. , There is also lower risk of confounding by comorbidities or concomitant medications. Notably, using healthy participants removes the ethical concern of administering subtherapeutic doses to patients with disease when studies of curative or palliative intent may be more appropriate. , In the absence of RT or CT, the constitutive level of DNA‐PK is low in healthy volunteers and DNA‐PK mediated repair of double‐strand DNA damage is considered negligible. Additionally, as the half‐life of peposertib is short at ~5.5 h, any risk to healthy volunteers, due to unexpected DNA damage upon peposertib exposure, was considered to be within acceptable limits of clinical pharmacology trials. These advantages have to be weighed against the fact that the pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic properties of the drug may not be identical in healthy volunteers and patients, in some cases, and that engagement of the drug target may have different safety consequences in patients. Nonetheless, the case for using healthy volunteers is strengthened if the preclinical efficacious dose is equal to or less than the no observed adverse effect level (NOAEL) and the drug is non‐genotoxic. Overall, healthy volunteers are being increasingly enrolled in pharmacological trials of drugs in oncologic indications for the development of small molecule therapeutics. 

Nonclinical safety data showed that peposertib was not mutagenic or genotoxic in a standard battery of genotoxicity tests (see Data  : Nonclinical Safety for more details). Specifically, genotoxicity testing included a bacterial reverse gene mutation assay, a chromosomal aberration test, a hypoxanthine‐guanine phosphoribosyl transferase gene mutation test, and a rodent micronucleus study. Observations from general toxicity studies indicated reversibility and monitorability of target organ effects, and, as expected, these were later demonstrated to be manageable in the first‐in‐human study. The main target organs of peposertib toxicity, as identified by 4‐week and 13‐week repeat‐dose toxicity studies in rats and dogs, are the lymphatic system, the male and female reproductive systems, and the epithelia, including skin and mucosae of the digestive tract. An embryofetal developmental study in rats showed that peposertib induced fetal malformations, increased number of fetal skeletal variations, and resulted in maternal toxicity. Hence, women of child‐bearing potential were excluded from this clinical study and contraceptive measures were in place for male participants.
Peposertib as monotherapy demonstrated modest efficacy in a phase I dose‐escalation trial among unselected patients (n = 31) with advanced solid tumors. Dose selection for the current study was based on safety data from the above‐mentioned trial and on preliminary safety data from three other clinical trials that are currently investigating peposertib in oncological indications (NCT02516813, NCT03770689, and NCT03724890). A comparison of the observed human exposure at 100 mg/patient to the exposure at NOAEL in dogs (the more sensitive species) yielded an animal/human exposure ratio (safety margin) of 3.7 and 4.8 for AUC and C
max, respectively. Additionally, as no dose‐limiting toxicities were observed when patients were repeatedly exposed to 400 mg twice daily dose level of peposertib as a capsule for a duration of 21 days, this was selected as the recommended phase II dose. The safety margin between this dose and the dose planned to be administered in healthy participants was estimated to be eight‐fold (AUC) and four‐fold (C
max) at the time of study initiation. In the current study, healthy volunteers were exposed to only a single 100 mg dose of peposertib per treatment period, which further minimized any safety risk associated with multiple dosing. The use of this dose level is further substantiated by the fact that, after the current study was initiated, findings from a population pharmacokinetic analysis indicated higher exposure levels for the peposertib tablet compared to the capsule formulation, with an average 75% estimated increase in AUC and C
max.
As the current study found no marked effect of food on peposertib exposure, tablets can be taken with or without food. However, when combined with CT and RT, peposertib administration without food should be considered as C
max occurs earlier and has a smaller range under these conditions. Additionally, when timing is key (i.e., synchronized exposure of CT/RT and peposertib), administration in the fasted state would help ensure that C
max is achieved in a more predictable timeframe.
As the AUCs of the OS form and the tablet were comparable, the OS form represents a useful alternative in patients with dysphagia, which occurs frequently in certain cancers. However, because C
max was higher after administration of the OS form, dosing and schedule should be part of the future dose optimization strategies in the relevant population and combination settings.
A potential limitation of this study is the fact that only male participants were included, although the protocol allowed for the inclusion of female participants; any potential variability resulting from sex differences would therefore not have been detected.

## CONCLUSION
This food effect study showed that a single dose of 100 mg peposertib can be taken with or without food. Administration without food should be considered if combined with CT and/or RT to ensure a more predictable time to reach C
max. Peposertib can also be administered in OS form to alleviate discomfort in patients with dysphagia. However, dose optimization strategies in relevant settings are needed for the OS form.
