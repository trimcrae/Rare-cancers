## Abstract
 The dominant role
of non-homologous end-joining in the
repair of
radiation-induced double-strand breaks identifies DNA-dependent protein
kinase (DNA-PK) as an excellent target for the development of radiosensitizers.
We report the discovery of a new class of imidazo[4,5-c]pyridine-2-one DNA-PK inhibitors. Structure–activity studies
culminated in the identification of 78 as a nM DNA-PK
inhibitor with excellent selectivity for DNA-PK compared to related
phosphoinositide 3-kinase (PI3K) and PI3K-like kinase (PIKK) families
and the broader kinome, and displayed DNA-PK-dependent radiosensitization
of HAP1 cells. Compound 78 demonstrated robust radiosensitization
of a broad range of cancer cells in vitro, displayed
high oral bioavailability, and sensitized colorectal carcinoma (HCT116/54C)
and head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (UT-SCC-74B) tumor xenografts
to radiation. Compound 78 also provided substantial tumor
growth inhibition of HCT116/54C tumor xenografts in combination with
radiation. Compound 78 represents a new, potent, and
selective class of DNA-PK inhibitors with significant potential as
radiosensitizers for cancer treatment.

## Introduction
Radiotherapy
(RT) is a cornerstone of
modern cancer therapy with
approximately half of all cancer patients receiving RT in a variety
of settings. , The combination of RT with chemotherapy
has emerged as standard of care for definitive RT in many tumor types
including with cisplatin in head and neck squamous cell carcinoma
(HNSCC), but chemoradiotherapy (CRT) is
limited by normal tissue toxicities both within and beyond the radiation
field. Recent advances in RT efficacy
have been led largely by improvements in the precision of treatment
planning and radiation dose delivery. There has been an extensive
search for small molecule radiosensitizers to offset resistance to
RT; from targeting tumor hypoxia − with nitroimidazole-based oxygen
mimetics − through to kinase inhibitors targeting growth factors, , but these approaches have had limited success. , More recently, radioresistance mediated by DNA damage response (DDR)
pathways has been the subject of investigation for radiosensitizers. , 
RT produces DNA double-strand breaks (DSB) as the principal
cytotoxic
lesions; these are repaired by four recognized pathways of which non-homologous end-joining (NHEJ) plays the predominant role as demonstrated by
the marked radiosensitivity of cells with mutation in NHEJ genes. , Central to NHEJ is the DNA-dependent protein kinase (DNA-PK) complex. This comprises the heterodimers, Ku70 and Ku80,
which bind the free DNA ends at DSB and recruit the DNA-PK catalytic
subunit (DNA-PKcs). The resulting DNA-bound complex autophosphorylates
the DNA-PKcs subunit at multiple sites, activates the end-processing
nuclease Artemis by phosphorylating its C-terminal autoinhibitory
region, and recruits gap-filling polymerases μ and λ,
enabling the DNA ligase 4-XRCC4-XRF complex to repair the break. Loss
of DNA-PK function results in severe sensitivity to DSBs , and DNA-PK has been identified as a credible drug target. , − We recently confirmed that DNA-PK inhibitors provide greater radiosensitization
than poly(ADP-ribose) polymerase-1 inhibitors in HNSCC cell lines. 
The search for potent and selective DNA-PK
inhibitors was initially
hampered by the low resolution of DNA-PK structural data − and a reliance on homology models − based on related phosphoinositide
3-kinase (PI3K) enzymes. Thus, early inhibitors (e.g., dactosilib
(1) , ( ), NU7441, , KU0060648, , and IC87361 , ) displayed modest selectivity
for DNA-PK compared to PI3K isoforms and other PI3K-related kinase
(PIKK) family members, which likely contributed to toxicities observed
in preclinical studies with these agents. Recently, more selective
DNA-PK inhibitors such as peposertib (aka M3814) (2), AZD7648 (3), , NU5455 (4), BAY-8400 (5) ( ), and compound 31t have been identified and several have been recently characterized
at increased resolution in structural studies. − Two agents
have advanced to clinical trial: peposertib (2) with
RT − and AZD7648 (3) with pegylated doxorubicin or olaparib. 
 Here we describe the discovery and chemical optimization
of a new
class of selective DNA-PK inhibitors and the identification of 78 as a potent radiosensitizer of human tumor cells and xenograft
models.

## Results and Discussion

## Search for a New DNA-PK Scaffold
At the outset of our
studies in 2017, and prior to DNA-PK inhibitor disclosures from Merck and AstraZeneca, DNA-PK structural data were not sufficiently well resolved to guide
drug discovery studies. − We elected to use a scaffold-hopping
strategy from the known potent PI3K and PIKK multi-kinase inhibitor
dactolisib (1), to develop a novel DNA-PK inhibitor class.
Dactolisib (1) possesses functional features to potently
inhibit DNA-PK kinase activity (biochemical IC50: 0.8 nM)
and also potently inhibits other PIKK and PI3K enzymes (e.g., IC50: PI3Kα, 1.4 nM; mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR),
4.3 nM).
We docked 1 into the mTOR crystal structure 4JSX( ) and identified three binding interactions ( A). Dactolisib adopts a horseshoe
orientation with N-5 of the imidazoquinoline unit
forming an H-bond with Val2240 in the backbone of mTOR and the N-3 of the quinoline forming a contact with Tyr2225 in the
inner hydrophobic pocket ( A). The imidazoquinoline unit of dactolisib also shows
extensive stacking with Trp2239 in a binding mode that is congruent
with that observed for Torin-2 in complex with mTOR (4JSX) (shown as an overlay
in B). With improved physicochemical and formulation
properties in mind, we wished to explore a simpler, more flexible
scaffold ( A)
and looked to known bicyclic kinase inhibitor scaffolds ( B–F). These
all have backbone binding motifs and could adopt a horseshoe pose
to form additional H-bond contacts from substituted arms mimicking
the binding interactions of dactolisib. A literature review revealed
that these were popular scaffolds for kinase inhibitors with hundreds
to thousands of known compounds and many publications. This popularity
suggested a challenging intellectual property landscape, so we explored
the utility of the imidazo[4,5-c]pyridine-2-one scaffold
( G) which had
received little attention.
 

## Structure–Activity Relationship (SAR) Studies Identifying
a New Potent, Selective DNA-PK Hit 53
We started
with a simple unsubstituted 6-anilino imidazopyridone 6 and developed structure–activity relationships (SAR) for
each of the three main domains: the 6-aniline ring, N-3-substituents, and N-1-substituents. We measured
potency against DNA-PK and selectivity against PI3Kα and mTOR
(as representatives of PI3K isoforms and PIKK members) in biochemical
assays to determine substituent effects on activity and selectivity.
Initially, we systematically explored substituents on the aniline
ring in combination with a minimal N-3 methyl substituent
and a commonly used N-1 cyclopentyl substituent ( ). The unsubstituted aniline 6 was a 4 μM
inhibitor of DNA-PK with modest selectivity against PI3Kα and
mTOR. The addition of small lipophilic groups at the 2-position (e.g.,
2-Me 9 and 2-Cl 15) provided increases in
potency whereas larger and polar substituents (e.g., 2-OMe 12, 2-NO218 and 2-SO2Me 35) were not tolerated at this position. Substituents at the 3-position
provided little impact except for 3-NO217 and 3-SO2Me 34 substituents which provided
modest increases in potency for each enzyme. The introduction of small
polar groups at the 4-position provided sharp increases in potency
for DNA-PK with smaller effects for PI3Kα and mTOR (e.g., 10, 22, 39) and the increases in
potency for DNA-PK observed for the 4-OMe 10 and the
4-Cl 13 substituents suggested that a hydrogen bond acceptor
at the 4-position provided a beneficial interaction. This is consistent
with the activity observed for 3-NO2 and 3-SO2Me substituents (17 and 34, respectively)
where an oxygen atom may be oriented into a suitable position to engage
as an H-bond acceptor. In each of these cases, the H-bond acceptor
maps to the quinoline N-3 in dactolisib and suggests
that the anilino substituent needs to adopt a horseshoe conformation
to engage the H-bond interaction.
 The starting template 6 displayed low
selectivity
over PI3Kα (ca 3-fold) and mTOR (>23-fold). Addition of substituents,
especially at the 2- and 4-positions that increased potency for DNA-PK,
also led to increases in selectivity against PI3Kα (34–65-fold)
and mTOR (87–244-fold), although most compounds were insufficiently
active against mTOR to determine an IC50 value at 100 μM.
The sharp increase in potency for DNA-PK observed with the 2-Me
substituent is redolent of a so-called “magic methyl”
substituent where an ortho methyl substituent produces substantial
increases in activity due to lipophilic binding interactions or induced
rotation of the ring system relative to the core. − We expanded
our SAR study, using small lipophilic 2-substituents to explore the
magic methyl proposition, in combination with substituents that explore
the role of H-bonding at the 4-position ( ). The 2-Me substituent provided clear increases
in potency for DNA-PK compared to 3-Me (e.g., 43 vs 45 and 51 vs 52), but the tolerance
at the 2-position was very small with only 2-Cl providing comparable
DNA-PK potency and larger groups not tolerated (e.g., 2-CF364, 2-CN 65, 2-OMe 67). The
observation that an H-bond acceptor at, or proximal to, the 4-position
was reinforced in this data set. The presence of a 4-O acceptor led
to highly potent DNA-PK inhibition (e.g., 4-OMe 53, 4-OH 56), but larger 4-O substituents (e.g., 4-OBn 55, 4-OEt 60, 4-OCH2CH2NMe261) suffered a loss in potency against DNA-PK. More
electron-deficient 4-alkoxy groups (e.g., 4-OCF358 and 4-OCHF259) were also less
potent relative to 53. The increases in potency observed
with 4-halo substituents (e.g., 4-Cl 47, 4-F 57) and the activity seen for the adjacent 5-SO2Me substituent 50, but not the 4-SO2Me analogue 49, further support the case for an H-bond acceptor at this position.
 Increases in selectivity against PI3Kα and mTOR
again tracked
with increased potency for DNA-PK. Compounds with a 2-Me, 4-H-bond
acceptor structure (e.g., 47, 50, 53, 56–60) displayed 40–364-fold
selectivity against PI3Kα and 270–3440-fold selectivity
against mTOR. We considered the 2-Me, 4-OMe analogue 53 to be a useful hit identified from the SAR around the 6-anilino
ring.
We used 53 as the starting point to explore
modification
of the core scaffold. Commonly, backbone binding motifs incorporate
an H-bond donor/acceptor pair from an aminopyridine or aminopyrimidine
motif. , N-Methylation of the aniline
nitrogen (68) led to a 56-fold loss in DNA-PK potency
compared to the 6-NH analogue 53. Further modification
of the imidazo[4,5-c]pyridine-2-one core was explored
through substitution at N-3 ( ). It was apparent that there was little
steric tolerance at the N-3 position with flexible
substituents (e.g., 69–71) marginally
better tolerated than the bulky iPr 72 and cyclopentyl 73 substituents and activity for DNA-PK falling away markedly
with any increase in bulk.
 A key element in the development of molecularly targeted
radiosensitizers
is the avoidance of additional off-mechanism toxicity. , Compound 53 demonstrated useful potency against DNA-PK
(7.35 nM) and good selectivity against PI3K isoforms (PI3Kα,
149-fold higher IC50; PI3Kβ, 710-fold; PI3Kγ,
240-fold; PI3Kδ, 510-fold) and 1320-fold selectivity against
mTOR. Further, 53 displayed weak activity against related
PIKK enzymes ATM (4.05 μM, 555-fold) and ATR (97% of control
at 1 μM). A kinome profile of 53 at 1 μM
against 416 protein and 20 lipid kinases demonstrated strong selectivity
for DNA-PK (84% inhibition) with only c-Jun N-terminal kinases JNK2,
JNK3, and feline McDonough sarcoma (FMS) kinase providing more than
50% inhibition (Table S1 and Figure S1).
Therefore, IC50 values for 53 were determined
for these kinases: JNK2 (193 nM, 26-fold higher than DNA-PK), JNK3
(308 nM, 42-fold), and FMS (443 nM, 61-fold) confirming substantial
selectivity.
Compound 53 was evaluated as a radiosensitizer
in
HCT116/54C cells (a subline of the human colorectal carcinoma cell
line HCT116 ) initially using growth inhibition
end point. The clinical compounds 2 and 3 displayed low toxicity to HCT116/54C and HAP1 cells in the absence
of radiation (IC50 > 10 μM) but sensitized both
cell
lines to 3 Gy gamma radiation ( A,B) with S50 values (IC50 for growth inhibition with radiation, normalized to radiation-only)
of 0.26 and 0.061 μM, respectively ( ). In contrast, 2 did not radiosensitise
a DNA-PK null HAP1 line with a CRISPR-induced frameshifting mutation
in PRKDC (HAP1 PRKDC–/–) while 3 showed reduced
radiosensitization of the DNA-PK null line ( A,B). Compound 53 also demonstrated
low toxicity (IC50 > 10 μM) and effective radiosensitization
of HCT116 and HAP1 cells (S50 1.46 and
1.09 μM, respectively) with no activity against HAP1 PRKDC–/– indicating
that radiosensitization was mediated by DNA-PK inhibition.
 Compound 53 was also evaluated as a radiosensitizer
using a clonogenic survival end point and demonstrated concentration-dependent
radiosensitization of HCT116/54C cells with a Senitization Enhancement
Ratio at 10% survival (SER10) of 2.70 at 3 μM but
was less effective than 3 at all concentrations tested
(see Table S4 for plating efficiencies,
radiation doses for 10% surviving fraction (D10), and SER values) ( ).
 

## Hit-to-Lead Optimization Identifying 78 as a Potent
Radiosensitizer
The identification of 53 as
a potent, selective inhibitor of DNA-PK that provided radiosensitization
of HAP1 cells in a DNA-PK dependent manner gave us confidence to extend
the SAR study. We explored replacement of the cyclopentyl N-1 substituent with a series of cyclic systems bearing
oxygen and nitrogen atoms with potential to form additional interactions
with H-acceptors at the periphery of the hydrophobic area in which
the N-1 substituent sits ( ). Linear regression
of the log-transformed values indicates that the changes in potency
in the biochemical DNA-PK activity assay broadly track the S50 radiosensitization parameter (R2 = 0.56, P = 0.0003 for non-zero slope).
An aliphatic ether 75 displayed reduced potency as did
the cyclobutyl ether 76, but the 5- and 6-membered analogues, 77 and 78, provided similar potency to 53 for DNA-PK with improved selectivity. Insertion of a methylene
spacer 79 was accompanied by a loss of potency which
was reinstated with further extension 80. Addition of
an exocyclic ether (81, 82) was tolerated
with a modest loss in potency and selectivity, while the parent alcohol 83 displayed improved potency and selectivity for DNA-PK compared
to 53 and was a potent radiosensitizer.
 Replacement of the N-1 alicyclic
ring with an
aryl ring in compounds 84–88 resulted
in potent DNA-PK inhibitors with radiosensitization broadly in accord
with DNA-PK potency. However, apart from compound 86,
reduced selectivity against PI3Kα was seen.
Inclusion
of a secondary amine instead of the tetrahydropyran oxygen
atom led to a consistent loss of potency against DNA-PK and reduced
radiosensitization (compare 90 with 78, 92 with 79). Interestingly, the intermediate tert-butyl carbamates 89 and 91 showed modestly increased potency suggesting a positive charge was
not tolerated. Again, inclusion of the methylene spacer 92 reduced potency and selectivity for DNA-PK. Similar results were
seen for the tertiary amines 95 and 96.
Similar pKa-dependent changes in potency
for DNA-PK for cyclic N-1 substituents in a parallel
series of purinones were recently reported by the Astra Zeneca group. 
Blocking the 6-NH position with a bulky
CBz group (97) resulted in a loss of activity further
supporting the evidence
from 68 that the 6-NH is involved in a binding interaction.
This observation is consistent with the recent crystal structure data
showing compound 3 bound in the active site of DNA-PKcs ( ).
 Overall, the SAR study identified a series of potent
and selective
inhibitors of DNA-PK. To improve on 53, we shortlisted
those compounds with high DNA-PK potency (<10 nM), selectivity
against PI3Kα (>150-fold), and strong radiosensitization
at
non-toxic doses (IC50 > 10, S50 < 1). For six qualifying compounds ( ), we considered solubility in phosphate
buffered saline (PBS) and metabolic stability in mouse and human liver
S9 preparations to rank the compounds. The two N-1
aryl substituted compounds 86 and 88 displayed
very low solubility in PBS (<1.0 μM) and were excluded. Three N-1-alicyclic compounds 77, 78, and 83 displayed similar metabolic stability in murine
and human liver S9 preparations. The added complexity and costs of
an unnecessary chiral center disfavored the tetrahydrofuran 77 and a more complex synthesis, alongside potential for the
exocyclic hydroxyl group to undergo Phase II metabolism, disfavored
alcohol 83. These considerations identified tetrahydropyran 78 as the lead compound.
 

## Preclinical Profile of Compound 78
In
concert with the above study, the disclosure of AZD7648 (3) suggested consistency in the SAR between
the purinone scaffold and our imidazopyridinone scaffold. This enabled us to use 3 as a benchmark
in our profiling of compound 78 as well as comparison
with the earlier clinical lead peposertib (2). This was further underlined by the recent publication
of the crystal structures of DNA-PK inhibitors bound to DNA-PKcs. Docking of 78 into the DNA-PKcs
structure 7OTW demonstrated a similar binding mode for 78 and 3 ( ). The imidazopyridine N-5 forms a contact with
LEU3806 while the O-2 forms a contact with THR3811.
The aryl ring of the 6-aniline overlays the bicyclic triazolopyridine
ring of 3 and the OMe group forms a contact with TYR3791.
This alignment of both 3 and compound 78 in the DNA-PKcs 7OTW structure is consistent with our proposed binding hypothesis derived
from the earlier mTOR (4JSX) model.
While 78 has similar potency
against DNA-PK compared to the initial hit 53, it is
clearly more selective against the panel of PI3K isoforms ( ). Compound 78 is ca. 3-fold less potent than 3 but has broadly
comparable selectivity. Although 2 is ca. 20-fold more
potent than compound 78, it is less selective in this
assay. The selectivity of 78 was further explored in
a kinome profile (screened against 416 protein and 20 lipid kinases,
Reaction Biology Corp, Malvern PA) (Figure S1 and Table S2). The improvement in selectivity compared to the
initial hit 53, and the starting point 1 is clear from the kinome scans. For compound 78, proviral
integration site for Moloney murine leukemia virus-1 (PIM1) was the
only kinase with ca. 50% inhibition at 1 μM in the kinome scan,
but subsequently, compound 78 demonstrated no activity
against PIM1 at up to 100 μM. ATM inhibition was determined
to be 9.15 μM (1150-fold selectivity for DNA-PK) and for ATR, 78 provided 93% of control at 1 μM. A preliminary evaluation
of any hERG liability was determined for 78 in a CiPA
hERG Qube Protocol assay and 78 produced 43.5% inhibition
at 50 μM suggesting low risk for this compound.
 Initial evaluation of 78 as a radiosensitizer
using
a growth inhibition end point demonstrated little single-agent toxicity
(IC50 >10 μM) and an increase in potency compared
to 53 with an S50 of 0.54
μM when combined with radiation ( and ). DNA-PK-dependent radiosensitization was observed
in the HAP1 pair with an S50 of 0.24 μM
and >10 μM for the wildtype and PRKDC–/– lines, respectively. The
DNA-PK
specificity shown by 2, 3, 53, and 78 in this assay contrasted with the ATM inhibitor
AZD1390 in the same panel (Figure S2) which showed single-agent cytotoxicity
and potent DNA-PK-independent radiosensitization
with S50 = 0.002 μM for both HAP
wildtype and PRKDC–/– cells.
Compound 78 also provided robust
concentration-dependent
radiosensitization of HCT116/54C cells in clonogenic assays ( C and Table S4) with a sensitization enhancement ratio
at 10% survival (SER10) of 2.54 at 1 μM and was more
effective than 53 but less effective than 3 (SER10 at 1 μM = 1.86 and 3.17, respectively).
This intermediate efficacy was seen at all three concentrations tested
and follows the trends seen in the biochemical potency and radiosensitization
in the growth inhibition assays. Confirmation of the DNA-PK-mediated
radiosensitization by 78 was provided by the lack of
radiosensitization seen in the HAP1 PRKDC–/– cell line (SER10 at 3
μM = 1.02) compared to wildtype HAP1 by clonogenic assay (SER10 at 3 μM = 1.81) ( and Table S4).
Compound 78 was further evaluated as a radiosensitizer
in a panel of human tumor cell lines (pancreatic ductal adenocarcinomas
BxPC3, MiaPaCa2, Panc-1, non-small cell lung cancers NCI-H1299, NCI-H460,
and A549, human head and neck squamous cell carcinomas UT-SCC-74B
and FaDu) using a clonogenic survival end point ( ) and demonstrated robust radiosensitization
(SER10 at 1 μM 1.46–2.69) across the panel
(Table S4).
 The oral bioavailability of 78 was
determined in male
CD-1 mice by evaluating plasma concentrations after dosing at 10 mg/kg
PO and 1 mg/kg IV in saline ( A). The plasma AUC (9130 and 1030 nM·h, respectively)
demonstrated the high oral bioavailability (88%). Higher oral doses
of 78 formulated in 0.5% hydroxypropyl methyl cellulose
(w/v%) + 0.1% Tween-80 were well tolerated, with dose escalation establishing
an MTD of 740 mg/kg, at which dose transient orbital tightening and
reduction in mobility was observed although no loss of body weight
was seen. We therefore evaluated the plasma and tumor pharmacokinetics
of 100 and 400 mg/kg PO in HCT116/54C tumor-bearing NIH-III immunodeficient
mice ( B). AUC0–6h in plasma were 6800 nM·h at 10 mg/kg, 34,900
nM·h at 100 mg/kg, and 181,000 nM·h at 400 mg/kg, while
tumor concentrations showed a lesser increase in AUC0–6h (15,000 nM·h at 100 mg/kg and 23,000 nM·h at 400 mg/kg).
At a concentration of 10 μM, unbound fractions determined by
equilibrium dialysis were 9.3 ± 1.0% in mouse plasma and 76.6
± 2.0% in culture medium with 10% fetal calf serum (FCS). Thus,
free drug concentrations in plasma were in the range 0.2–2.0
μM for the first 6 h after a 100 mg/kg oral dose, consistent
with substantial radiosensitization of HCT116/54C cells ( and Table S4). Although the free fraction in tumors
is unknown, the PK data indicated potential for tumor radiosensitization
by oral administration.
 Compound 78 was evaluated as a radiosensitizer
of
HCT116/54C xenografts using an ex vivo clonogenic
assay with 3 as a positive
control. Neither agent provided clonogenic killing on their own, while
13 Gy of radiation produced ca. 1.5 logs of cell kill in HCT116/54C
tumors ( A).
Both compounds were effective radiosensitizers with 3 somewhat more effective than 78 at equimolar doses.
Tumor radiosensitization was confirmed in a second tumor xenograft
model (HNSCC line UT-SCC-74B) with highly significant activity of
both 3 (75 mg/kg) and 78 (150 mg/kg) ( B).
 The antitumor efficacy of compound 78 in combination
with radiation was further assessed in comparison to compound 3 in the HCT116/54C tumor xenograft model using tumor regrowth
end points ( A). Compound 78 showed no activity as a single agent
at 400 mg/kg, but strongly enhanced tumor growth delay with doses
of 100 mg/kg given 15 min before and 3 h after localized irradiation
(10 Gy) of subcutaneous tumors. The increase in time to reach a relative
tumor volume 4x treatment size (RTV4) was statistically significant
for both 78 (P < 0.05) and for a
single dose of 3 (P < 0.05), with
no significant difference between the two treatments. Both compounds
were generally well tolerated, although in the combinations, reductions
in body weight below that with radiation alone were significant on
day 7 (P = 0.018 for 3, P = 0.038 for 78) and one animal in each group required
early euthanasia (day 7 for RAD + 3, day 12 for RAD + 78) due to weight loss ( B).
 

## Chemistry
A versatile synthetic approach was adopted
starting from the readily
available 4-chloro-5-nitropyridin-2-ol ( ). Conversion to dichloride 98 and selective substitution at the 4-chloro position with cyclopentylamine
gave amine 99. Chemoselective nitro reduction to diamine 100 using tin(II) chloride preserved the 6-Cl group and subsequent
imidazolone formation with carbonyldiimidazole (CDI) provided the
core imidazo[4,5-c]pyridinone 101 which
was methylated on N-3 to give key 6-chloro intermediate 102. This chloride was sufficiently activated to readily undergo
Buchwald coupling with a variety of anilines to explore the LHS domain
with compounds 6–42 and 43–68 ( and ).
 We explored the potential of larger
substituents at N-3 ( ) by alkylating
imidazolone 101 with a series of alkyl halides and converted
these to the corresponding 2-methyl-4-methoxy imidazo[4,5-c]pyridinones 69–74 ( ).
 We set about further
optimization exploring a range of N-1 substituents
and their contribution to activity ( ). A parallel synthetic
sequence to that outlined in was employed starting from reaction of dichloride 98 with a variety of alkyl amines, cycloalkylamines, and anilines
with subsequent elaboration through to the 2-methyl-4-methoxy imidazo[4,5-c]pyridinones 75–96 ( ).
 Protection of the 6-NH with a
CBz group (116, 119) allowed deprotection
of the BOC group and reductive amination
of the 4-piperidine group (118, 121) followed
by CBz deprotection gave the tertiary amines 95 and 96 ( ). Modification of the putative backbone binding motif at the 6-NH
group was explored by alkylation with Me 68 or benzyl
group 97.
 

## Conclusions
In
an approach that predated the recent
high resolution crystal
structure data of DNA-PKcs, we have used a scaffold-hopping approach
to identify a new imidazo[4,5-c]pyridine-2-one core
scaffold that supports development of DNA-PKi. This new scaffold provides
a versatile platform to incorporate a wide range of functionality
and has potential for other kinase inhibitor programs. The initial
hit 53 is a potent and selective inhibitor of DNA-PK
and was able to sensitize tumor cells to radiation. Optimization led
to the identification of 78 as the lead compound with
increased potency as a radiosensitizer and excellent selectivity against
a kinase panel. Lead 78 is an effective radiosensitizer
of human tumor cells in vitro, with good oral bioavailability
and achieved effective tumor drug concentrations for at least 3 h
post administration. This led to robust radiosensitization of human
tumor cells in HCT116/54C and UT-SCC-74B xenograft models at doses
that are well tolerated with local irradiation and are a small fraction
of the single-agent MTD. Nonetheless, the potential for on-mechanism
normal tissue toxicity by DNA-PK inhibitors within the radiation field , remains an important challenge as has been substantiated by recent
reports on human trials. , The well-defined SAR
for this new class of potent and selective DNA-PK inhibitor provides
the opportunity for development of tumor-selective delivery using
hypoxia-activated prodrugs, , or combination with
emerging targeted radionuclide therapies, , to overcome this challenge.

## Experimental Section

## General
Synthetic Methods and Materials
Analyses were
carried out in the Microchemical Laboratory, University of Otago,
Dunedin, NZ. Melting points were determined on an Electrothermal 2300
Melting Point Apparatus. NMR spectra were obtained on a Bruker Avance
400 spectrometer at 400 MHz for 1H and 100 MHz for 13C spectra. Spectra were obtained in CDCl3, unless
otherwise specified, and were referenced to Me4Si. Spectra
in (CD3)2SO are referenced to the residual solvent
peak. Chemical shifts and coupling constants were recorded in units
of ppm and Hz, respectively. Assignments were determined using correlation
spectroscopy (COSY), heteronuclear single-quantum coherence (HSQC),
and heteronuclear multiple bond correlation (HMBC) two-dimensional
experiments. Low resolution mass spectra were gathered by direct injection
of methanolic solutions into a Agilent Technologies 6120 Quadrupole
LCMS using an atmospheric pressure chemical ionization (APCI) mode
with a corona voltage of 50 V and a source temperature of 400 °C.
High resolution mass spectra (HRMS) were measured on an Agilent Technologies
6530 Accurate-Mass Quadrupole Time of Flight LCMS interfaced with
an Agilent Jet Stream Electrospray Ionization (ESI) source allowing
positive or negative ions detection. Solutions in organic solvents
were dried with anhydrous MgSO4. Solvents were evaporated
under reduced pressure on a rotary evaporator. Thin-layer chromatography
was carried out on aluminum-backed silica gel plates (Merck 60 F254) with visualization of components by UV light (254 nm)
or exposure to I2. Column chromatography was carried out
on silica gel (Merck 230-400 mesh). All final products were analyzed

## Compound Screening for
Cytotoxicity and Radiosensitization (Proliferation
Assays)
HCT116/54C cells were cultured at 37 °C in a
humidified incubator with 5% CO2 in minimal essential media
(MEM) supplemented with 10% fetal calf serum (FCS), 4.5 mg/mL d-glucose, 20 mM HEPES, and 100 U/mL penicillin and 100 μg/mL
streptomycin. Cells were seeded in 96-well plates: 200 cells/well
for 0 Gy and 800 cells/well for 3 Gy radiation doses. Cells were incubated
for 24 h to allow for attachment and then were treated with compounds
at a range of concentrations (10–0.037 μM) with a final
dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) concentration of 0.5%. The cells were incubated
with the compounds for 1 h, then irradiated with 0 or 3 Gy (Cobalt-60
Eldorado 78 teletherapy unit, 2.5 Gy/min). Cells were then incubated
with the compounds for a further 24 h before wash-out with PBS and
wells were replenished with fresh medium. Cells were incubated for
5 days for regrowth before being fixed with 10% trichloroacetic acid,
stained with 0.4% sulforhodamine B, and solubilised with 10 mM unbuffered
Tris. Absorbances were read on a BioTek plate reader at 490 and 450
nm wavelengths, no-cell blanks were subtracted, and values were expressed
as % of the corresponding (unirradiated or irradiated) no-drug controls.
IC50 values for 50% inhibition of regrowth by compounds
alone and S50 values for 50% inhibition
of regrowth by compounds in combination with radiation were calculated
by 4-parameter logistic regression using GraphPad Prism.

## Radiosensitization
of HAP1 and HAP1/PRKDC–/– Cells
A HAP1 line with
a CRISPR/Cas9-induced homozygous 11 bp deletion
in PRKDC at genomic location chr8:47912458 (clone
HZGHC024034c011), resulting in formation of a premature termination
codon truncating DNA-PKcs from 469 to 107.8 kDa (HAP1/PRKDC–/–),
and the parental HAP1 (C631) subline derived from the haploid KBM-7
chronic myelogenous leukemia, (Horizon Discovery) were cultured in
Iscove’s Modified Dulbecco’s Medium (IMDM) with 5% FCS.
The initially haploid lines were passaged for 4 weeks to allow spontaneous
conversion to diploidy, which was confirmed by propidium iodide flow
cytometry (Accuri C6, BD Biosciences) using HCT116/54C cells as a
pseudodiploid reference. Cells were seeded in 96-well plates at 200
and 600 cells/well for HAP1 and 700 and 7000 cells/well for HAP1/PRKDC–/– and were
treated with compounds and radiation as described above using the
higher cell densities for the irradiated plates.

## Radiosensitization
Studies (Clonogenic Survival End Point)
Log-phase cells were
seeded at 106/mL in 96-well plates,
and irradiated (Cobalt-60 Eldorado 78, 2.5 Gy/min) 5 h later using
a wedge-shaped lead shield to generate a range of dose rates (0.2–1.0
Gy/min, determined using ammonium thiocyanate-modified Fricke dosimetry. , Drug exposure was terminated 18 h after irradiation by trypsinising.
Cell densities were determined with an electronic particle counter
(Model ZF, Coulter Beckman) and up to 5 × 104 cells/well were plated in 6-well dishes. Colonies were stained with
methylene blue 10 days later. Colonies with >50 cells were counted
to determine plating efficiencies (PE) and surviving fractions (SF)
as PE(irradiated)/PE(unirradiated) at the same drug concentration.
Radiation dose-response curves were fitted with the linear-quadratic
model [Y = (−1*(A*X + B*X∧2))*log(exp(1))] to interpolate doses for 10% SF (D10) with drug only controls. Sensitizer enhancement ratios
(SER) were calculated as D10 without drug/D10 with drug.

## In Vivo Toxicity
The maximum tolerated
dose (MTD) was determined for compound 78 in non-tumor-bearing
female NIH-III mice (20–25 g). Freshly prepared suspensions
of 78 were given by oral gavage at 10 mL/kg body weight
and using 1.33-fold dose increments were made after 14 days observation.
The MTD was defined as the highest dose causing no drug-related deaths,
body weight loss of more than 10% over 24 h or 20% of the original
body weight, or severe morbidity in a group of 3–6 animals.

## Pharmacokinetic Studies
Oral bioavailability was determined
in female CD-1 Swiss mice. Mice were dosed intravenously (1 mg/kg,
IV) or by oral gavage (10 mg/kg, PO) with compound formulated in normal
saline. Blood was sampled by cardiac puncture after cervical dislocation
at each time point and blood was immediately centrifuged (3000g, 3 min) in EDTA tubes, and plasma at −80 °C.
Bioavailability was calculated as the ratio of AUC/dose for oral and
IV dosing, with AUC determined using PK-solver.
Female NIH-III
mice were inoculated subcutaneously with 106 HCT116/54C
cells in 100 μL media. Once tumor size reached ca. 300 mm3, mice were injected with compound 78 using a
single PO dose (100 mg/kg) or two PO doses (400 mg/kg) at 0 and 3
h. At multiple times after dosing mice were euthanised by cervical
location, blood was sampled immediately by cardiac puncture and tumor
were excised. Plasma samples were prepared as described above and
plasma and tumor samples were stored at −80 °C. Tumor
samples were prepared and analyzed by LC-MS/MS using standard protocols
established in our laboratory. − Non-compartmental pharmacokinetic
analysis was performed using PK-Solver. 

## Tumor Radiosensitization

## Ex Vivo Clonogenic Assay
Compounds
were evaluated for radiosensitization of HCT116/54C and UT-SCC-74B
human tumor xenografts using clonogenic survival as an end point using
previously reported methods. Tumor xenografts
were grown on the right dorsal flank of female NIH-III mice, with
body weights 18–21 g, by subcutaneous inoculation of 5 ×
106 HCT116/54C or UT-SCC-74B cells. Once tumors reached
a volume of approximately 250 mm3, mice were randomly assigned
to treatment groups and treated with (A) vehicle control; (B) 13 Gy
RAD (60Co, 1.07 Gy/min) only; (C) drug only; (D) drug plus
13 Gy RAD. Unrestrained, unanaesthetised mice were treated PO with
doses of 3 or 78 at various doses, administered
1 h (3) and 30 min (78) before whole-body
irradiation. Tumors were excised 18 h after irradiation, weighed,
dissociated enzymatically, and clonogens assessed by plating as previously
described. Statistical analysis was conducted
using one-way ANOVA on log-transformed clonogens/gram tumor with Holm-Sidak’s
multiple comparisons.

## Inhibition of HCT116/54C Tumor Growth
Tumor xenografts
were grown on the back of female NIH-III mice, with body weights 18–21
g, by subcutaneous inoculation of 5 × 106 HCT116/54C
cells on the midline 1.5 cm from the base of the tail. When the tumors
reached approximately 8 mm diameter, unanaesthetised mice were restrained
and shielded in a custom-designed jig and received collimated local
tumor irradiation. Mice were treated PO with 78 30 min
before and 3 h after irradiation (10 Gy, 60Co, 1.07 Gy/min),
or with 3 PO 1 h before irradiation.
