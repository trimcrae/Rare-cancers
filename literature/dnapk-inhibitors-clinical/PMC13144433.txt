## ABSTRACT
Esophageal squamous cell carcinoma (ESCC) is characterized by intrinsic radioresistance, primarily mediated by hyperactivation of DNA damage response (DDR) pathways. This repair capacity significantly diminishes radiotherapy efficacy, contributing to poor patient outcomes. Targeting DNA‐dependent protein kinase catalytic subunit (DNA‐PKcs), a master regulator of non‐homologous end joining (NHEJ), represents a promising strategy to overcome radioresistance. Immunohistochemistry (IHC) was performed on paired biopsy specimens from esophageal squamous cell carcinoma (ESCC) patients (n = 20) collected before neoadjuvant radiotherapy and after surgical resection. In vitro, ESCC cell lines (TE13 and Eca9706) were co‐treated with DNA‐dependent protein kinase catalytic subunit (DNA‐PKcs) inhibitors (AZD7648 or NU7741) or PRKDC‐targeting siRNA plus irradiation. Subsequent functional assays were conducted to assess cell viability, apoptosis, cell cycle distribution, and underlying molecular mechanisms. In vivo, the combined efficacy of AZD7648 and irradiation was evaluated in xenograft tumor models. Phosphorylated DNA‐PKcs (Ser2056) in ESCC tissues was significantly elevated following irradiation. Inhibition of DNA‐PKcs markedly enhanced radiosensitivity in both ESCC cell lines and xenograft models, correlating with prolonged G2/M arrest and increased apoptosis. Mechanistically, the combined therapy activated the ATM (Ser1981)‐Chk2 (Thr68) axis and concurrently enhanced phosphorylation of Chk1 at Ser345, reinforcing G2/M arrest through Wee1 kinase‐mediated inhibitory phosphorylation of CDC2 at Tyr15. Consequently, irreparable DNA double‐strand breaks (DSBs) accumulated, triggering Caspase‐3‐dependent cleavage of PARP and execution of the intrinsic apoptosis pathway. Our study reveals a novel mechanism by which DNA‐PKcs inhibition augments radiosensitivity in ESCC. Targeting DNA‐PKcs could represent a promising strategy to improve the efficacy of radiotherapy in ESCC, offering a potential therapeutic approach to overcome radioresistance.
Keywords: DNA damage response (DDR), DNA‐PKcs inhibition, esophageal squamous cell carcinoma (ESCC), radiosensitivity

Radiotherapy induces double‐strand DNA breaks in esophageal squamous cell carcinoma, triggering DNA‐PKcs phosphorylation and activating the non‐homologous end joining (NHEJ) repair pathway, which contributes to radioresistance. Inhibiting DNA‐PKcs disrupts this repair mechanism, enhancing radiosensitivity by blocking NHEJ, amplifying DNA damage, and promoting apoptosis.

## Introduction
Esophageal carcinoma (ESCA) is a highly aggressive malignancy characterized by poor prognosis and suboptimal response to standard treatments, particularly radiotherapy. According to the GLOBOCAN 2020 report, ESCA ranks as the seventh most common cancer worldwide and the sixth leading cause of cancer‐related mortality [ ]. Esophageal squamous cell carcinoma (ESCC) is the predominant histological subtype, especially prevalent in East Asia. In China, ESCC represents a major public health challenge, accounting for over half of global cases and ranking as the fourth leading cause of cancer‐related deaths in the country [ , , ].
Current treatment strategies for ESCC include surgery, radiotherapy, and chemotherapy [ ]. Radiotherapy is particularly critical for patients unsuitable for surgical intervention [ , , ]. While neoadjuvant radiotherapy followed by esophagectomy and radical radiotherapy are widely adopted in the management of locally advanced ESCC, the therapeutic efficacy of radiotherapy is often limited by two major challenges: radioresistance in tumor cells and toxicity to adjacent healthy tissues [ , , , ]. Radiation primarily damages cancer cells by generating DNA double‐strand breaks (DSBs) and inducing oxidative stress via reactive oxygen species (ROS) [ ]. Despite this, ESCC cells frequently exhibit radioresistance through enhanced DNA repair mechanisms, while nearby organs, such as the lungs and heart, are vulnerable to radiation‐induced damage [ , , , ]. This limits the radiation dose that can be safely administered, thus diminishing treatment efficacy.
DNA damage response (DDR) pathways are crucial in maintaining genomic integrity and play a key role in conferring ESCC radioresistance [ , , ]. Among these, DNA‐PK is a critical enzyme in the non‐homologous end joining (NHEJ) pathway, which is the primary mechanism for DSB repair following radiotherapy [ , ]. Evidence strongly suggests that dysregulated DNA‐PKcs (the catalytic subunit of the DNA‐PK complex) activity is strongly linked to the progression of various cancers [ , , , ]. And overexpression of DNA‐PKcs has been observed in various cancers and is closely linked to radioresistance [ ]. Inhibitors targeting DNA‐PKcs have shown the potential to disrupt DDR, thereby enhancing the therapeutic efficacy of radiation, making them attractive candidates for clinical use [ , , , ].
In this study, we explore the potential of DNA‐PKcs inhibition as a strategy to enhance radiosensitivity in ESCC. Specifically, we investigate how blocking DNA‐PKcs can modulate DDR pathways, intensify radiation‐induced DNA damage, and promote apoptosis, ultimately improving the therapeutic efficacy of radiotherapy in ESCC.

## Materials and Methods
2.1.

## Cell Culture
TE‐13 and Human Esophageal Epithelial Cells (HEEC) were acquired from Zolgene Biotechnology Co. Ltd. (Fuzhou, China), while Eca109 and Eca9706 cell lines have been maintained in our laboratory for several years. TE‐13 cells were cultured in RPMI 1640 medium (Gibco), HEEC in Dulbecco's modified Eagle medium (DMEM; Gibco), Eca109 in DMEM, and Eca9706 in high glucose‐DMEM (H‐DMEM; Gibco). All cell lines were cultured at 37°C with 5% CO2, supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Pricella Biotechnology) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (HyClone).

2.2.

## Irradiation
Radiation treatment was conducted using a 6‐MV linear accelerator (LINAC; 2300EX; Varian Co., Palo Alto, CA, USA). The culture flasks containing the cells were centrally located within a designated treatment area measuring 20 × 20 cm. A single irradiation dose was administered at a 5.0 Gy/min dose rate. The distance from the irradiation source to the target was consistently maintained at 100 cm.

2.3.

## Clinical Tissue Specimens
We collected ESCC tissues from patients diagnosed with ESCC through pre‐neoadjuvant irradiation biopsy and post‐irradiation surgical resection (n = 20). Following neoadjuvant radiotherapy, these patients underwent surgical treatment in our department between January 2020 and December 2023. Cases with postoperative pathologic complete response (PCR) or inadequate histological quality were excluded, and two experienced pathologists independently confirmed all clinicopathological diagnoses.

2.4.

## Cell Transfection
ESCC cells were seeded in plates to reach 60% confluence, ready for transfection. For the transfection, Opti‐MEM (Gibco) mixed with siRNA (GenePharma) and Lipofectamine 2000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific) in separate tubes were combined after 5 min and allowed to bind for 20 min. During this, the well medium was refreshed with an antibiotic‐free medium. The transfection mixture was then added to each well and, after 4–6 h, replaced with a complete culture medium for ongoing experiments.

2.5.

## Cell Proliferation and Colony Formation Assay
Cell viability was determined using the Cell Counting Kit‐8 (CCK‐8, GlpBio). Cells were seeded at 5 × 103 cells per well in 96‐well plates. After a 96‐h incubation, 10 μL of CCK‐8 solution was added to each well, containing 100 μL of medium. The plates were then incubated at 37°C with 5% CO2 for 2 h, and absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a SpectraMax iD3 Multi‐Mode Microplate Reader (Molecular Devices). For the colony formation assay, 2 × 103 cells were plated per well in 6‐well plates and incubated at 37°C with 5% CO2. After 2 weeks, cells were fixed with methanol, stained with 0.1% crystal violet (Beyotime), and colonies were counted.

2.6.

## Cell Cycle Analysis
ESCC cells were cultured in six‐well plates without serum for 18 h and subsequently divided into four groups: control, AZD7648, irradiation, and combined treatment. Twenty‐four hours post‐exposure, the cells were harvested, washed with cold PBS, and fixed in 75% ethanol at 4°C overnight. Before flow cytometry analysis, all samples were washed with PBS and then treated with PI/RNase Staining Buffer (BD Biosciences). The samples were incubated at room temperature for 15 min and protected from light. DNA content and cell cycle distribution were analyzed using flow cytometry (FACSCanto (TM) II, BD Biosciences). All experimental procedures were repeated in triplicate.

2.7.

## Apoptosis Analysis
As said before, ESCC cells were seeded in six‐well plates and cultured for 12 h before being divided into four groups. Following a 96‐h exposure, all cells were harvested and washed with cold PBS. Apoptosis was assessed using FITC Annexin V/PI staining solution (BD Biosciences). The samples were analyzed by flow cytometry (FACSCanto (TM) II, BD Biosciences). All experiments were conducted in triplicate.

2.8.

## Western Blot Assay
ESCC cell proteins were extracted using RIPA extraction reagent (NCM Biotech) supplemented with Protease and Phosphatase Inhibitor Cocktail (NCM Biotech), and their concentrations were determined by the BCA method. Subsequently, proteins were separated by SDS‐PAGE and transferred to PVDF membranes (Millipore). After blocking with QuickBlock Blocking Buffer for Western Blot (Beyotime), PVDF membranes were incubated overnight at 4°C with appropriate primary antibodies (Table  ). Following incubation with secondary antibodies, including HRP‐conjugated Affinipure Goat Anti‐Rabbit IgG (SA00001‐2, Proteitntech) and HRP‐conjugated Affinipure Goat Anti‐Mouse IgG (SA00001‐1, Proteitntech), protein bands were visualized using WesternBright ECL HRP substrate (Advansta).

2.9.

## Immunohistochemistry (IHC) Staining
The paraffin‐embedded ESCC slides (4 μm) were deparaffinized, rehydrated, and boiled in Citrate Antigen Retrieval Solution (Beyotime). The slides were treated with 3% H2O2 to block endogenous peroxidase activity, followed by incubation with 3% BSA to prevent nonspecific binding. The slides were then incubated with appropriate primary antibodies (Table  ) at 4°C overnight. After washing the slides three times with TBST buffer, they were treated with a secondary antibody for 30 min at room temperature. Another round of washing was followed by applying a DAB solution to highlight the target areas.

2.10.

## Immunofluorescence (IF) and EdU Cell Proliferation Assay
Cells were treated for 12 h, then fixed and blocked using a DNA Damage Assay Kit (Beyotime). Subsequently, cells were incubated with a primary antibody specific for p‐Histone H2A.X (γ‐H2AX) followed by secondary antibodies (both supplied by Beyotime). After staining with DAPI (Beyotime), images were captured using a High Content Analysis System (Operetta CLS, PerkinElmer). EdU cell proliferation assay was conducted using BeyoClick EdU Cell Proliferation Kit with Alexa Fluor 488 (Beyotime). ESCC cells were treated for 24 h and then incubated with EdU for 2 h before fixation. Fluorescence images were captured using a High Content Analysis System (Operetta CLS, PerkinElmer). All experiments were performed in triplicate to ensure reproducibility.

2.11.

## Comet Assay
Cells were seeded into 6‐well plates at a density of 1 × 105 cells per well. After treatment at specified concentrations and durations, cells were harvested and then utilized for comet assays following the manufacturer's protocol. Briefly, single‐cell suspensions were embedded in low‐melting‐point agarose on glass slides. Slides were immersed in lysis buffer for 1 h at 4°C. Subsequently, slides were incubated in electrophoresis buffer for 30 min to facilitate DNA unwinding. Electrophoresis was performed at 25 V for 30 min in the same buffer. After electrophoresis, DNA was stained with propidium iodide (PI) and covered with a coverslip. Comet visualization and quantification were conducted using fluorescence microscopy. Tail length and tail moment were measured to evaluate the degree of DNA damage.

2.12.

## In Vivo Mouse Study
Mouse studies were conducted following Institutional Animal Care and Ethical Committee‐approved animal guidelines and protocols. All mouse experiments were performed in age‐ and gender‐matched NSG (NOD‐scid IL2Rγnull). Tumor cells suspended in PBS were mixed at a 1:1 ratio with BD Matrigel Basement Membrane Matrix (catalog no. 356231; Corning) and then subcutaneously injected into the left and right flanks of the mice (TE‐13 cells: 1.8 × 106/injection; Eca9706 cells: 3.0 × 106/injection). Fourteen days post‐injection, once tumors were palpable, the mice were randomly assigned to one of four treatment groups: (i) control; (ii) AZD7648 (75 mg/kg/bid, continuous infusion for 3 weeks); (iii) a single 2 Gy dose of daily radiation over the first 5 days; (iv) combined AZD7648 and irradiation. Tumor size was measured by a caliper every 3 days. Tumor volume was calculated using the formula: (a × b2)/2, with “a” and “b” representing the length and width of a tumor, respectively.

2.13.

## Public Databases
GEPIA2 database (http://gepia2.cancer‐pku.cn/#index) was utilized to investigate and validate the expression of PRKDC in ESCA and adjacent normal tissues. UALCAN database (https://ualcan.path.uab.edu/index.html) was utilized to investigate the relationship between PRKDC and various clinical pathological parameters, including sample types, individual cancer stages, nodal metastasis status, tumor grade, patient age, and TP53 mutation status. Timer database (https://cistrome.shinyapps.io/timer/) was utilized to analyze the correlation between PRKDC expression and several other DDR key genes, including XRCC4, Ku70, Ku80, NHEJ1, ATM, ATR, PARP1, PARP2, and CHK1 in ESCA.
Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) was conducted using GSEA software on the transcriptomic dataset GSE222658, sourced from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database. Enrichment analysis for Gene Ontology (GO) and hallmark gene sets was performed using the clusterProfiler package in R software (version 4.4.1).

2.14.

## Statistical Analysis
All experiments were performed with at least three replications. Data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation. GraphPad Prism 9.0 was applied for statistical analysis and plotting, the Shapiro–Wilk test for normal distribution was utilized, and statistical differences between multiple groups were analyzed using the one‐way ANOVA test. Statistical test results of p < 0.05 were used as the criterion for statistically significant differences.

3.

## Results
3.1.

## Irradiation Induces DSBs and Activates the DDR Signaling Pathway in ESCC

Radiotherapy induces DNA damage, primarily in the form of DSBs, which subsequently trigger DDR to maintain genomic stability. To investigate DDR activation in ESCC, we initially performed Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis in irradiated and non‐irradiated subgroups of oral squamous cell carcinoma (OSCC) as a model. We observed significant enrichment of DNA repair and DDR activity in the irradiated subgroup (Appendix Figure  ). Furthermore, analysis of lung cancer data revealed that high expression of PRKDC, which encodes DNA‐PKcs, was associated with poorer survival, linking DNA‐PKcs expression to adverse clinical outcomes.
DNA‐PKcs is a core component of the non‐homologous end joining (NHEJ) pathway and plays a critical role in the rapid repair of DSBs, thereby promoting radioresistance in multiple cancers. To comprehensively assess the role of PRKDC in tumorigenesis and its clinical relevance, we analyzed its expression patterns and prognostic significance across cancers. Pan‐cancer comparison of tumor versus paired normal tissues from 33 cancer types showed that PRKDC was significantly upregulated in most malignancies, including Esophageal Carcinoma (ESCA), Breast Invasive Carcinoma (BRCA), Cholangiocarcinoma (CHOL), Colon Adenocarcinoma (COAD), Liver Hepatocellular Carcinoma (LIHC), and Lung Adenocarcinoma (LUAD) (Appendix Figure  ). In contrast, PRKDC was downregulated in a few cancer types such as Kidney Chromophobe (KICH), suggesting context‐dependent roles in different tumor microenvironments. Furthermore, hazard ratio (HR) forest plots revealed that high PRKDCexpression was significantly associated with worse overall survival (OS) in several cancers (Appendix Figure  ). Collectively, these results indicate that PRKDCis frequently dysregulated in cancer, with overexpression in most tumors correlating with poor prognosis, highlighting its potential as both a prognostic biomarker and a therapeutic target.
In ESCC patients, immunohistochemistry (IHC) of paired pre‐ and post‐radiotherapy tissues showed marked increases in phosphorylated DNA‐PKcs (Ser2056) and γ‐H2AX after treatment (Figure  ). Previous studies have consistently shown the overexpression of DNA‐PKcs in multiple cancers, which contributes to tumor progression [ , , , ]. Similarly, GEPIA2 database analysis confirmed elevated expression of PRKDC (the gene encoding DNA‐PKcs) in ESCA compared to normal tissues (Figure  ). Additional analysis using the UALCAN database demonstrated that PRKDC expression was significantly higher in primary tumors compared to normal tissues, across various cancer stages, nodal metastasis statuses, tumor grades, and TP53 mutation statuses (Appendix Figure  ). Western blot analysis of ESCC cell lines and HEEC showed increased DNA‐PKcs expression, underscoring its oncogenic role (Figure  ). Correlations between PRKDC and other DDR genes such as XRCC4, Ku70, Ku80, NHEJ1, ATM, ATR, PARP1, PARP2, and CHK1 were explored using the TIMER database, all of which showed significant associations (Appendix Figure  ). We also examined DDR‐related proteins in TE‐13 and Eca9706 cell lines before and after irradiation. Western blot results showed increased phosphorylation of DNA‐PKcs (Ser2056), ATM (Ser1981), ATR (Thr1989), Chk1 (Ser345), and Chk2 (Thr68), without changes in their total expression (Figure  ). These findings suggest that irradiation induces robust DDR activation in ESCC, which may contribute to the observed radioresistance.
 
3.2.

## 
DNA‐PKcs Inhibition Increases the Radiosensitivity in ESCC Cells
To assess whether DNA‐PKcs inhibition could enhance the radiosensitivity of ESCC, we treated TE‐13 and Eca9706 cells with DNA‐PKcs inhibitors (AZD7648 or NU7441) in combination with radiation. Cell viability, measured by the CCK8 assay, significantly decreased in cells treated with both inhibitors and radiation (Figure  ). Clonogenic survival assays further demonstrated that DNA‐PKcs inhibition significantly reduced colony formation in TE‐13 cells following radiation exposure, with a similar effect observed in Eca9706 cells (Figure  ). Similarly, the combination of AZD7648 with irradiation also reduced Edu‐labeled proliferative cells in both TE‐13 and Eca9706 cell lines (Figure  ; Appendix Figure  ). Moreover, comet assays revealed substantially increased tail moments 24 h after treatment in cells receiving the combined therapy compared to irradiation alone, indicating elevated DNA damage (Figure  ). Collectively, these findings highlight the synergistic effect of DNA‐PKcs inhibitors with irradiation.
 To further investigate the molecular mechanisms underlying this radiosensitization, we knocked down PRKDC by using siRNA. Western blot analysis confirmed reduced DNA‐PKcs expression in siRNA‐transfected cells (Appendix Figure  ). PRKDC knockdown resulted in notable reductions in cell viability and colony formation in both TE‐13 and Eca9706 cells (Appendix Figure  ). These findings underscore the critical role of DNA‐PKcs in promoting radioresistance in ESCC and suggest that its inhibition could serve as a promising therapeutic strategy to improve radiotherapy outcomes.

3.3.

## 
DNA‐PKcs Inhibition Promotes Irradiation‐Induced Cell Cycle Distribution and Enhances Apoptosis of ESCC Cell Lines
The radiosensitivity of cancer cells to irradiation varies across different cell cycle phases, with the G2/M phases being the most vulnerable to irradiation. GSEA using Hallmark gene sets revealed that the irradiated OSCC subgroup exhibited downregulation of the G2/M checkpoint and upregulation of apoptosis, as detailed in the enriched gene set (Appendix Figure  ). To investigate the combined impact of DNA‐PKcs inhibition and irradiation on ESCC cell cycle distribution, we performed flow cytometry analysis, revealing that irradiation induced significant G2/M cell cycle arrest in both TE‐13 and Eca9706 cells, which was further intensified by AZD7648 or PRKDC‐siRNA transfection (Figure  ; Appendix Figure  ). Next, we assessed the effect of DNA‐PKcs inhibition on apoptosis following irradiation using Annexin V/PI staining. While irradiation alone increased apoptosis, pre‐treatment with AZD7648 or PRKDC‐siRNA further enhanced apoptotic cell death in TE‐13 and Eca9706 cells (Figure  ; Appendix Figure  ). These results suggest that DNA‐PKcs inhibition not only promotes G2/M cell cycle arrest but also increases irradiation‐induced apoptosis, contributing to enhanced radiosensitivity.
 We then examined the expression of key proteins involved in apoptosis and cell cycle regulation through western blot in ESCC cell lines. In the combined therapy group (AZD7648 + irradiation), we observed a significant increase in Wee1(Ser642) and cdc2 (Tyr15) phosphorylation, alongside a reduction in Cyclin B1 and phosphorylated histone H3 (Ser10), compared to irradiation alone (Figure  ). This indicates enhanced G2/M checkpoint activation: Wee1 phosphorylates cdc2‐Tyr15 to inactivate the Cyclin B1/cdc2 complex (MPF), preventing mitotic entry. Concomitantly, H3S10ph reduction confirms mitotic arrest, as this marker peaks during chromosome condensation. Moreover, AZD7648 significantly increased the cleavage of Caspase‐3 and PARP in TE‐13 and Eca9706 cells post‐irradiation, indicating DNA damage‐dependent apoptotic activation (Figure  ). These findings suggest that AZD7648 promotes apoptosis while simultaneously reinforcing G2/M cell cycle arrest in ESCC cells following irradiation, thereby enhancing therapeutic efficacy. The prolonged G2/M arrest extends the time window for unrepaired DNA damage accumulation, particularly critical in ESCC where DNA‐PKcs overexpression drives radioresistance by facilitating NHEJ‐mediated DSB repair. Prolonged G2/M arrest heightens tumor cell susceptibility to radiotherapy, facilitating greater DNA damage and apoptosis. Together, these findings highlight the potential of combining DNA‐PKcs inhibition with radiotherapy to improve ESCC treatment outcomes by dual targeting of cell cycle checkpoints and apoptosis pathways, while potentially overcoming NHEJ‐mediated resistance mechanisms.

3.4.

## 
DNA‐PKcs Inhibition Enhances Irradiation‐Induced DSBs and Modulates Associated DDR

γ‐H2AX serves as a highly sensitive biomarker for DSBs, with each focus representing a single DSB site and enabling quantitative biodosimetry of DNA damage. Immunofluorescence (IF) analysis revealed a marked increase in γ‐H2AX foci in cells treated with both AZD7648 and irradiation compared to those receiving irradiation alone (Figure  ; Appendix Figure  ). Similarly, western blot analysis indicated that the co‐administration of AZD7648 and irradiation significantly elevated γ‐H2AX expression in both TE‐13 and Eca9706 cells (Figure  ). These results confirm that combining a DNA‐PKcs inhibitor with irradiation substantially enhances DSB induction in ESCC cells.
 DNA‐PKcs, ATM, and ATR are critical protein kinases integral to DNA repair and cell cycle regulation in the DDR pathway [ ]. We next examined the expression of key DDR‐related proteins. In the group treated with both AZD7648 and irradiation, we observed significant increases in the phosphorylation of ATM (Ser1981), Chk2 (Thr68), and Chk1 (Ser345) (Figure  ). This hyperactivation reflects compensatory signaling: DNA‐PKcs inhibition forces reliance on ATM‐mediated backup repair pathways, amplifying checkpoint activation. However, the expression levels of Ku70, Ku80, p‐ATR (Thr1989), and p‐Chk1 (Ser296) remained largely consistent across the irradiation and combined therapy groups (Figure  ), indicating that AZD7648 selectively disrupts DNA‐PKcs‐dependent NHEJ without broadly suppressing upstream DSB sensors. Further analysis of DDR‐related protein expression across various treatment groups—negative control, PRKDC‐siRNA alone, irradiation alone, and combined therapy—revealed consistent upregulation of ATM (Ser1981), Chk2 (Thr68) and Chk1 (Ser345) (Figure  ).
AZD7648 provides more potent and immediate inhibition of DNA‐PKcs phosphorylation compared to PRKDC‐siRNA due to its direct enzymatic inhibition, leading to more effective disruption of NHEJ‐mediated DSB repair post‐irradiation. Disruption of NHEJ leads to increased apoptosis and a compensatory activation of HR‐mediated DSB repair. Additionally, the Chk1/Wee1/Cdc2 axis is hyperactivated through ATM/Chk2‐mediated phosphorylation of Chk1 (Ser345), inducing sustained G2/M arrest that blocks mitotic entry of DNA‐damaged cells and promotes synthetic lethality with irradiation (Figure  ). This intricate interaction underscores the potential of DNA‐PKcs inhibition as a complement to irradiation, offering a promising strategy to improve ESCC treatment outcomes.

3.5.

## Combined DNA‐PKcs Inhibition With Irradiation Suppresses ESCC Tumor Growth In Vivo
To evaluate the therapeutic potential of combining DNA‐PKcs inhibition with irradiation in vivo, we utilized a xenograft model derived from ESCC cells. In a xenograft model from TE‐13 cells, irradiation alone significantly restricted tumor growth compared to the control group (p < 0.05) (Figure  ). Notably, the combined therapy of AZD7648 and irradiation resulted in significantly greater tumor suppression than irradiation alone (P < 0.01) (Figure  ). Tumors remaining after the 3‐week treatment regimen in the combined therapy group were significantly smaller and lighter than those in the other treatment groups (Figure  ). Similar effects were observed in the Eca9706 xenograft model, where combined therapy notably reduced tumor growth compared to irradiation alone (p < 0.05) (Figure  ), mirroring the findings from the TE‐13 model. The remaining tumors in the combined therapy group of the Eca9706 model were also significantly lighter than those observed in the other groups (Figure  ). IHC analysis of the Eca9706 residual tumors demonstrated that the combination of AZD7648 with irradiation significantly increased DNA DSBs (γ‐H2AX), reduced tumor cell proliferation (Ki‐67), and enhanced apoptosis (Caspase‐3 cleavage) (Figure  ). These findings demonstrate that inhibition of DNA‐PKcs synergizes effectively with radiotherapy in ESCC preclinical models, corroborating our in vitro results.

## Discussion
Radiotherapy is a cornerstone treatment for ESCC patients, particularly in patients ineligible for surgery. However, its effectiveness is limited by the occurrence of radioresistance and potential damage to surrounding healthy tissues, which restricts the maximum radiation dose that can be safely administered. Therefore, Identifying mechanisms to enhance tumor radiosensitivity is critical for improving ESCC outcomes.
DNA‐PKcs inhibitors have emerged as potent radiosensitizers, showing promising results in both in vitro and in vivo studies across various cancers, including non‐small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), breast cancer, hypopharyngeal carcinoma, malignant melanoma, colon cancer, and glioblastoma [ , , , , , ]. In our study, western blot analysis confirmed significantly elevated DNA‐PKcs levels in ESCC cell lines compared to HEEC. Additionally, post‐irradiation analysis via western blot and IHC demonstrated a pronounced increase in DNA‐PKcs expression, emphasizing its critical role in ESCC radioresistance. These findings highlight DNA‐PKcs as a key factor contributing to treatment resistance and a potential target for enhancing therapeutic outcomes.
In this study, we employed a range of experimental approaches to assess the impact of DNA‐PKcs inhibition on improving radiotherapy efficacy in ESCC. CCK8 assays revealed that DNA‐PKcs inhibitors significantly amplified irradiation‐induced reductions in cell viability in TE‐13 and Eca9706 cells. Additional evaluation with colony formation and EdU assays demonstrated that this combined treatment robustly suppressed ESCC cell proliferation. Moreover, comet assays indicated elevated DNA damage in combined treatment group. To further explore the effects of DNA‐PKcs depletion, we developed a PRKDC knockdown model using siRNA transfection. CCK8 and colony formation assays in these knockdown cells showed a marked improvement in irradiation efficacy. Complementary in vivo experiments confirmed that DNA‐PKcs inhibition significantly amplified tumor growth suppression following irradiation. Together, these results suggest that DNA‐PKcs inhibition synergizes with irradiation to enhance treatment effectiveness in ESCC, highlighting DNA‐PKcs as a promising therapeutic target for enhancing radiotherapy outcomes in ESCC.
Cells in the G2/M phase are known to be especially vulnerable to radiation, as DNA damage incurred during this phase can lead to improper chromosome distribution and cell death [ , , , ]. The kinase cdc2 regulates the G2/M transition in partnership with Cyclin B1. Wee1 kinase acts as a negative regulator by phosphorylating and inhibiting cdc2, thereby preventing premature entry into mitosis. Through flow cytometry analysis of cell cycle distribution and western blot for protein expression, we found that combining AZD7648 with irradiation induced a pronounced G2/M arrest in TE‐13 and Eca9706 cells. This effect likely results from activation in the Chk1/Wee1/cdc2 axis and decreased Cyclin B1 expression. The prolonged G2/M phase not only halts cell proliferation but also amplifies radiation‐induced DNA damage, leading to increased Caspase‐3/PARP mediated apoptosis. These results underscore the critical roles of G2/M phase arrest and apoptosis in enhancing radiotherapy efficacy when combined with DNA‐PKcs inhibition, highlighting the therapeutic potential of this approach in ESCC.
The core novel finding of our study is the identification of a distinct, ESCC‐relevant apoptotic signaling pathway triggered by DNA‐PKcs inhibition. While DNA‐PKcs's role in NHEJ is well‐known, the subsequent fate of cells that fail to repair DSBs upon its inhibition—specifically the precise signaling routes to cell death—remains context‐dependent and less understood. Our study reveals that in ESCC, combining DNA‐PKcs inhibition with irradiation does more than cause DSB accumulation; it activates ATM‐Chk2‐Chk1 axes. This activation reinforces a prolonged G2/M arrest via the Wee1/CDC2 (Tyr15) pathway and ultimately drives robust Caspase‐3‐dependent apoptosis. Mapping this detailed signaling cascade from DNA‐PKcs inhibition to apoptotic execution represents a significant advance in understanding the radiosensitization mechanism unique to ESCC.
While our preclinical data establish a compelling mechanistic rationale for targeting DNA‐PKcs to radiosensitize ESCC, the translational path must rigorously address clinical feasibility, particularly regarding toxicity. Consistent with lessons from unsuccessful DDR inhibitor trials outlined in our prior review, dose‐limiting toxicities remain a major translational hurdle [ ]. While DNA‐PKcs inhibitors like peposertib(M3814) show manageable monotherapy safety profiles, combination regimens escalate risk, as seen with AZD7648 plus chemotherapy [ , ]. A critical translational challenge is that radiosensitization is not tumor‐specific; preclinical evidence confirms that it equally potentiates damage in rapidly proliferating normal tissues like the intestinal mucosa and bone marrow [ ]. The toxicity risk may be mitigated by combining DNA‐PKcs inhibition with localized/targeted DNA‐damaging therapies (like radiotherapy), rather than systemic chemotherapy [ ]. Furthermore, the efficacy and safety of such a combination likely depend on leveraging highly conformal radiotherapy. Ongoing advances in targeting precision of radiotherapy inherently spare normal tissue and could thus help mitigate combined toxicity.
Radiotherapy eradicates cancer cells by inducing DSBs, leading to genomic instability, cell cycle disruption, apoptosis, and ultimately cell death. In response, cancer cells often develop adaptive DDR mechanisms to counter these effects, leading to treatment resistance. Deciphering the specific DDR mechanisms evoked post‐radiation is therefore essential for developing effective strategies to overcome treatment resistance. Our research demonstrates that AZD7648 significantly diminishes DNA‐PKcs phosphorylation in TE‐13 and Eca9706 cells. Pre‐treatment with AZD7648 or PRKDC‐siRNA before irradiation disrupts the NHEJ repair pathway and triggers G2/M arrest, thus causing more DNA damage and apotosis. These findings further demonstrate the therapeutic potential of a DNA‐PKcs inhibitor in boosting radiotherapy efficacy in ESCC and suggest promising clinical strategies for radiosensitization by targeting key DNA repair pathways.
This study faces two primary translational gaps. First, because PRKDC expression is not routinely profiled in ESCC patients at our institution, we lack the comprehensive clinical data needed to directly correlate DNA‐PKcs levels with patient survival or treatment response. Prospective studies with larger ESCC cohorts will be essential to validate DNA‐PKcs as a predictive biomarker. Second, detailed toxicity profiles of DNA‐PKcs inhibitors—particularly their effects on normal tissues—remain undefined. To address these limitations, a critical next step involves systematic evaluation in ESCC patient‐derived xenograft (PDX) models. These studies should concurrently assess antitumor efficacy and the impact on normal tissues, especially rapidly proliferating compartments, following treatment with DNA‐PKcs inhibitors both alone and in combination with radiotherapy.

5.

## Conclusions
Our study demonstrates that inhibiting DNA‐PKcs significantly enhances ESCC radiosensitivity in vitro and in vivo. Combining DNA‐PKcs inhibitors with radiotherapy leads to greater tumor suppression, reduced cell viability, pronounced cell cycle arrest, and increased apoptosis. These results provide compelling evidence for the potential of DNA‐PKcs inhibitor as a promising adjunct to conventional radiotherapy in ESCC treatment. We recommend the integration of DNA‐PKcs inhibitor with radiotherapy in future clinical trials to improve therapeutic outcomes for ESCC patients.
