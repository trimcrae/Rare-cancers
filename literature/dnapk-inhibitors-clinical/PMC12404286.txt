## INTRODUCTION
Head and neck cancer (HNC) comprises a group of malignant tumors originating in the head and neck region, including the oral cavity, pharynx, larynx, paranasal sinuses, nasal cavity, and salivary glands [ ]. According to the World Health Organization classification, HNC may be categorized as carcinoma, papilloma, teratocarcinosarcoma, hamartoma, sarcoma, hematolymphoid tumors, neuroectodermal tumors, neuroblastoma, melanoma, or paraganglioma, based on cellular pathological origins [ ]. HNC ranks as the seventh most prevalent cancer globally, according to Global Cancer Statistics 2020 [ ]. It is projected to cause more than 700,000 annual deaths and incur economic losses approaching $535 billion by 2030 [ ]. Even among survivors, decreased quality of life remains a significant concern, given the close anatomical relationship of these tumors to critical organs at risk (OARs), including the eyes, eardrums, cochlea, salivary glands, thyroid glands, and cranial nerves. Furthermore, tumors can obstruct airways and swallowing pathways, significantly impairing daily function [ ].
Radiation-based therapies are effective for regional tumor control and size reduction, especially in cases of HNC where complete surgical resection may be improbable or even impossible due to tumor size, stage, or anatomical position. Surgical procedures also place patients at risk of severe or lethal complications [ , ]. Radiotherapy employs high-energy electromagnetic radiation to deliver concentrated energy directly to tumors, eliminating cancer cells either by directly inducing DNA strand breaks or indirectly by generating intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS) through water radiolysis, ultimately leading to cell death ( ).
A critical challenge in achieving complete cancer eradication is the intrinsic capacity of cancer cells to adapt, resist, and evade therapeutic interventions. Tumors are designated as “radiation-resistant” if recurrence is detected within 6 months following the initial course of radiation treatment. Approximately 25% of patients experience local recurrence after radiotherapy [ ]. Upon the emergence of radioresistance, surgical salvage is often the primary option for tumor eradication, resulting in a 4-year survival rate of approximately 43% [ ]. However, neither re-irradiation therapy nor surgical salvage is always feasible, particularly in advanced-stage HNC cases where tumors may reside in anatomically unresectable locations. Additionally, severe toxicity often accompanies reirradiation [ - ].
For HNC, surrounding OARs frequently constrain the available area for therapeutic contours. Conventional radiotherapy seeks to exploit the radiobiological differences between tumors and normal tissues, described as the “5 R’s” of radiobiology: radiosensitivity, repair, reoxygenation, redistribution, and repopulation, to achieve a therapeutic window [ ]. Currently, most radiotherapy treatments employ fractionated schedules administered daily to weekly. This fractionated approach effectively minimizes acute and late toxicity in normal tissues [ ], while maintaining reasonable tumor control. Nevertheless, prolonged treatment intervals allow cancer cells to repopulate between radiation fractions, potentially leading to the emergence of radioresistant subpopulations. This phenomenon represents one of the most frequent causes of therapeutic failure [ ].
This review discusses mechanisms involved in HNC radioresistance from the perspectives of both cancer cells and the tumor microenvironment (TME), and explores available radiation-sensitizing strategies designed to overcome radioresistance in HNC.

## MECHANISMS OF RADIORESISTANCE

## Cellular mechanisms in radioresistance development

## Enhancement of DNA damage repair
Maintaining genomic DNA stability is essential for cell survival, as DNA integrity is fundamental for cell replication and transcription. Cells experience approximately 70,000 instances of DNA damage daily [ ], including single-strand breaks (SSBs), double-strand breaks (DSBs), base damage, sugar damage, DNA cross-linking, and clustered damaged sites. DNA damage, if not repaired promptly and correctly, can cause mutations, broader genomic aberrations, and ultimately lead to cell death. Ionizing radiotherapy primarily induces lethality by generating DSBs, the most detrimental form of DNA damage, as these leave no intact complementary strand for repair [ ]. To prevent cell death induced by DNA damage, mammalian cells have evolved multiple repair pathways collectively known as the DNA damage response (DDR) [ ].
Cells with DNA damage typically cease replication due to cell cycle arrest, resulting from checkpoint activations largely regulated by the tumor suppressor protein p53 [ ]. Often referred to as the “guardian of the genome,” activated p53 accumulates and causes cell cycle arrest either in the G1 phase through increased expression of p21, or in the M phase through cyclin B degradation [ ]. This arrest provides cells additional time to identify and repair genomic lesions. TP53 is the most commonly mutated gene in human cancers, occurring in more than 50% of primary tumors. Approximately 80% of TP53 mutations are missense mutations, resulting in gene inactivation [ ]. Different mechanisms repair specific DNA lesions: replication errors are detected and corrected by DNA polymerases and mismatch excision repair (MMR) enzymes [ ], whereas DNA SSBs are resolved by base excision repair (BER) for single-nucleotide base damage or nucleotide excision repair (NER) for lesions involving two or more bases [ ]. Poly(ADP-ribose) polymerases (PARPs) are key proteins involved in BER [ , ]. DSB repair principally occurs via two pathways: homologous recombination (HR) and nonhomologous end-joining (NHEJ) ( ) [ ].
HR involves repair proteins that replicate the identical sequence from an intact sister chromatid to correct the damaged chromatid, predominantly during the S and G2 phases [ ]. Utilizing a recently duplicated homologous template, HR resolves interchromosomal joint molecules through synthesis-dependent strand annealing pathways [ ], thereby minimizing potential loss of heterozygosity caused by somatic crossover [ ]. Key proteins in the HR pathway include RAD51, the MRN complex, ataxia-telangiectasia mutated (ATM), ataxia telangiectasia and rad3-related (ATR), breast cancer susceptibility proteins (BRCA1 and BRCA2), and phosphatase and tensin homolog. Conversely, NHEJ functions throughout the cell cycle without a homologous template and is considered more error-prone and mutagenic [ ]. This pathway directly ligates broken DNA ends, typically involving minimal end-processing [ ], which is a lossy process that is more error-prone and inherently mutagenic. The molecular machinery for this repair pathway includes DNA ligase IV, the Ku70/Ku80 complex, XRCC4, and DNA-dependent protein kinase (DNA-PK), an XRCC7 product [ ]. Additional proteins, such as retinoblastoma protein, significantly influence both HR and NHEJ by forming complexes with BRG1, XRCC5, and XRCC6 [ ].
Without an effective DDR system, HNC cells would be highly susceptible to DNA-damaging treatments such as radiotherapy. At diagnosis, patients with HNC typically exhibit downregulated expression of NER and BER genes but overexpression of DSB and MMR genes, including RAD51, XRCC2, MLH1, MSH2, and MSH3 [ ]. Several HR proteins, such as BRCA1, BRCA2, Rad51, and RPA1, show increased activation in radiation-resistant HNC cell lines and xenografts [ ]. Furthermore, inhibition of ATM expression enhances radiosensitivity in HNC and squamous cell carcinoma (SCC) mouse models [ ]. The NHEJ pathway is also implicated in radiation resistance, as DNA-PK-related DDR mechanisms have been frequently associated with radioresistance in HNC [ , ]. Thyroid hormone receptor interactor 3, another protein associated with the NHEJ pathway, reportedly promotes chemoresistance in HNC by supplying energy necessary for the DNA-PK complex assembly [ ]. Many DDR inhibitors are currently being developed and investigated as potential radiation-sensitizing agents, as discussed later in this review.

## Cell death and cell death evasion
Although cell death is the primary goal of cancer treatments, malignant cells often evade it or even exploit it for their benefit, presenting significant therapeutic challenges. Necrosis and apoptosis are two of the most extensively studied cell death pathways. Necrosis is an acute, rapid, and uncontrolled form of cell death, typically occurring as an “accidental” outcome of severe stress. It can result from conditions such as hypoxia, drug toxicity, inflammation, physical compression, or other trauma [ ]. Despite its uncontrolled nature, necrosis can benefit cancer cells. Most tumors contain necrotic cores, which arise from cell death due to hypoxia caused by inadequate blood flow as the tumor exceeds its blood supply [ ]. These necrotic regions are particularly resistant to radiation treatment because of their hypoxic state [ ]. Moreover, tumor necrotic cores can harbor metastatic cell populations [ ].
Apoptosis, in contrast, is a physiologically induced and regulated form of cell death that occurs without eliciting a significant host inflammatory response. Unlike necrosis, apoptosis depends on specific genetic and biochemical pathways, and is tightly controlled, leading to its characterization as “regulated” cell death. Apoptosis can be triggered intrinsically by factors such as DNA damage, nutrient deprivation, accumulation of unfolded proteins, or elevated ROS, or extrinsically through ligand binding, including tumor necrosis factors (TNFs), TNF-related apoptosis-inducing ligands, or Fas ligands interacting with their corresponding death receptors [ ]. Activation of these death receptors triggers pro-apoptotic BCL-2 family proteins such as BAX, BAK, and BOK, which promote cytochrome c release from mitochondria, initiating the caspase cascade and ultimately causing cellular protein and nuclear degradation [ ]. Anti-apoptotic proteins, including BCL-2, BCL-xL, and MCL-1, typically bind and inactivate their pro-apoptotic counterparts, thus preserving mitochondrial integrity and inhibiting apoptosis [ ].
Cancer cells evade apoptosis by neutralizing triggering factors. For instance, the G1-to-S phase transition during mitosis, a period when cells exhibit heightened sensitivity to DNA damage due to increased chromosome mobility, is often delayed in cancer cells to establish treatment resistance. This G1 phase delay places cancer cells in a dormant state, allowing them to survive treatment [ - ]. Heat shock proteins (HSPs), which clear misfolded proteins, also contribute to various forms of treatment resistance in HNC owing to their anti-apoptotic properties [ , ]. Overexpression of anti-apoptotic BCL-2 family proteins is correlated with resistance to radiotherapy in HNC [ , ]. Additionally, the tumor suppressor protein p53 plays a crucial role in cell death regulation ( ). Besides promoting DNA repair, activated p53 can induce apoptosis through transcription-dependent and transcription-independent mechanisms [ , ]. Loss-of-function TP53 mutations represent the most frequent genomic alteration observed in HNC, and the p53 protein can be bound and functionally inactivated by the human papillomavirus (HPV) E6 protein in HPV-positive HNC patients [ ]. Alterations in p53 function are known to modulate radiosensitivity in HNC [ ]. Notably, HPV-positive HNC tumors are generally more radiosensitive than HPV-negative tumors. This increased radiosensitivity is hypothesized to result from HPV oncoproteins E6 and E7 binding to p53 and Rb proteins—two key DDR regulators—and facilitating their proteasomal degradation [ ]. Furthermore, HPV-positive HNC tumors typically exhibit lower TP53 mutation rates, possibly contributing to their greater radiosensitivity [ ].

## Oxidative stress response and altered metabolism
In addition to directly damaging DNA, ionizing radiation also produces secondary toxic effects mediated by ROS, which are generated through water radiolysis and play a critical role in the efficacy of radiotherapy against cancer. ROS naturally exist at moderate levels in normal cells as metabolic byproducts, primarily derived from the mitochondrial electron transport chain during oxidative phosphorylation (OxPhos) [ ]. However, excessive ROS can directly damage cellular proteins, lipids, and nucleic acids, causing DNA damage, cell membrane disruption, cell cycle arrest, and ultimately apoptosis-mediated cell death [ ]. To avoid such damage, normal and cancer cells use multiple pathways to counteract oxidative stress, among which the NRF2-Keap1 pathway is most prominently studied [ ]. Under physiological conditions, NRF2 expression is tightly regulated by its interaction with Keap1, a protein that mediates NRF2 ubiquitination and subsequent proteasomal degradation [ ]. As shown in , in the presence of ROS, critical Keap1 cysteine residues are oxidized, preventing it from binding to NRF2. This allows NRF2 to translocate to the nucleus and bind the antioxidant response element promoter region of several antioxidant target genes, including NAD(P)H quinone oxidoreductase 1 (NQO1), glutathione S-transferase, cysteine-glutamate transporter, and heme oxygenase 1 [ ]. The products of these genes scavenge and neutralize ROS, relieving cells from oxidative stress. NRF2 mutations enhance radioresistance in HNC [ ], and an increase in alterations within the NRF2 pathway has been identified in radiation-resistant laryngeal SCC tumors, with NQO1 mRNA expression proposed as a potential biomarker for radiotherapy response [ ].
Cancer energy metabolism may also significantly affect tumor sensitivity to ROS-induced toxicity following radiation. By altering their metabolic profiles, cancer cells can reduce ROS accumulation, generate substrates necessary for genome repair, and survive with minimal energy requirements. Warburg [ ] first described the differences in energy metabolism between normal and cancer cells. Analyzing the ratio between OxPhos and glycolysis, he observed particularly high levels of aerobic glycolysis in aggressive tumors compared to benign tumors and normal tissues—later termed the “Warburg effect” [ ]. Although glycolysis produces less adenosine triphosphate (ATP) per molecule of glucose than OxPhos, it provides cancer cells with several survival advantages, such as rapid ATP generation, metabolic flexibility, and biosynthetic support [ ]. By limiting OxPhos, cancer cells can reduce the production of ROS byproducts of mitochondrial activity [ ], thereby decreasing their sensitivity to radiation-induced ROS toxicity. The Warburg effect has been suggested to play a role in HNC radiosensitivity [ ], as upregulated glycolysis is correlated with tumor progression and immune evasion in HNC [ ]. The PI3K/AKT/mechanistic target of rapamycin (mTOR)/hypoxia-inducible factor 1α (HIF-1α) signaling pathway primarily regulates the cellular metabolic switch from OxPhos to glycolysis [ - ]. High phosphorylation levels of AKT and mTOR have been observed in HNC patients previously treated with adjuvant radiotherapy, irrespective of recurrence status [ ]. Additionally, glycolytic enzymes such as pyruvate kinase and pyruvate dehydrogenase kinase isoforms (PDK1 and PDK3) have been associated with radioresistance in nasopharyngeal carcinoma [ ].
Autophagy represents another mechanism by which cancer cells mitigate treatment toxicity, involving the disassembly and recycling of unnecessary or dysfunctional cytoplasmic components. Protective autophagy enables cancer cells to clear debris resulting from radiation-induced damage, such as unfolded proteins, impaired organelles, and ROS byproducts [ ]. The mTOR pathway, in addition to promoting treatment resistance through glycolysis activation, serves as a key inhibitory regulator of autophagy [ ]. mTOR signaling repression has been observed in radiation-resistant HNC [ , ]. The Wnt signaling pathway is also strongly linked to autophagy-mediated resistance in cancer treatment. Originally recognized for its diverse roles in embryonic development, dysregulated Wnt/β-catenin signaling in cancer cells can induce uncontrolled proliferation, epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT), and metastasis [ ]. However, under stressful conditions such as exposure to ionizing radiation, cancer cells may deactivate Wnt/β-catenin signaling and trigger autophagy via activation of P62 [ ]. Conversely, Wnt signaling can positively regulate autophagy through ATG5 activation [ ], which is linked to autophagy-mediated radioresistance in nasopharyngeal carcinoma [ ].

## Morphological transformation
Among all malignant tumors diagnosed in adults, 85% originate from epithelial cells [ ]. The EMT is a process in which cells shift from a stationary epithelial morphology to a migratory mesenchymal morphology [ ]. This process plays a significant role in HNC development, as it allows cancer cells to breach the basal extracellular matrix (ECM) membrane and initiate metastatic cascades [ ]. HNC cells stimulated by epithelial-mesenchymal crosstalk-conditioned media exhibit increased radioresistance due to enhanced cell survival and improved DNA repair capacity [ ]. EMT-associated resistance has also been linked to cancer stem cell (CSC) characteristics [ ]. Similar to normal pluripotent stem cells, CSCs possess self-renewal capabilities, resistance to apoptosis, angiogenic induction, and differentiation potential [ ]. The presence of a CSC subpopulation correlates with poor prognosis and resistance to photon and carbon ion radiation therapy in HNC [ ]. Cells undergoing incomplete EMT can display enhanced stem cell properties and transition between differentiation states, adopting a hybrid epithelial/mesenchymal morphology. Moreover, critical EMT-inducing transcription factors such as SNAI, TWIST, and ZEB1 can confer stem cell characteristics on cancer cells [ ]. Targeted inhibition of these transcription factors and EMT-related signaling pathways—including NOTCH, Wnt, and transforming growth factor (TGF)—has been shown to reverse acquired treatment resistance and cancer stemness [ , ]. Partial EMT significantly contributes to HNC progression [ ], and stem cell-like properties have been reported in radiation-resistant HNC cell lines [ - ].

## Tumor heterogeneity
Modern analytical techniques, such as next-generation sequencing and single-cell sequencing, have provided insights into tumor heterogeneity and its implications for treatment efficacy [ ]. The capacity to evade cell death and impaired DNA damage repair processes make cancer cells prone to genomic mutations and alterations, which represent critical intrinsic driving forces behind tumor genetic and morphological heterogeneity [ ]. Spatially, solid tumors can consist of multiple geographically distinct and molecularly diverse cell populations [ ], or they may harbor key driver mutations ubiquitously, accompanied by an uneven distribution of additional secondary mutations [ ]. When treatment fails to achieve complete eradication, the residual tumor can retain resistant cellular subpopulations, potentially leading to later therapeutic failure or disease recurrence [ ]. Temporally, fractionated radiation therapy may exert selective pressure, promoting tumor heterogeneity by enabling cancer cell repopulation between radiation fractions [ , ]. Enhanced CSC signaling was identified during HNC tumor repopulation across various radiation doses [ ]. Once resistant cancer clones emerge, tumors become significantly more refractory to conventional therapies. Various tumor microenvironmental components—including ECM, cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs), myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs), and tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs)—also contribute to tumor heterogeneity, as their secreted growth factors, cytokines, and chemokines can shape cancer cells into different clonal morphologies [ ] ( ).

## TME in radioresistance development

## Tumor hypoxia region
Cancer frequently coexists with hypoxia. While small tumors may receive adequate perfusion from surrounding blood vessels, larger tumors require neo-angiogenesis to sustain nutrient supply, oxygenation, and waste removal [ ]. Oxygen can only effectively diffuse approximately 100–140 microns from blood vessels; thus, rapidly growing tumors quickly surpass their blood supply, developing hypoxic and necrotic cores observed in approximately 90% of solid tumors [ ]. Rapidly proliferating cancer cells significantly consume oxygen, causing levels to drop from the typical 2%–9% to less than 2% [ ]. Oxygen availability strongly correlates with radiotherapy sensitivity, particularly concerning radiation-induced DNA damage and ROS generation. At oxygen partial pressures (pO2) of approximately 1 mmHg, cancer cells exhibit threefold greater resistance to radiation compared to normally oxygenated cells [ ]. Additionally, the quantity of lethal DNA DSBs induced by ionizing radiation decreases two to threefold under hypoxic conditions [ ]. This phenomenon, explained by the oxygen fixation hypothesis, asserts that oxygen stabilizes (“fixes”) radiation-induced lesions. In its absence, DNA radicals can be alternatively repaired by sulfhydryl-containing compounds, restoring the DNA to its original state [ ].
HIF-1 has provided significant insights into cellular responses to hypoxia. HIF-1α is an oxygen-sensitive protein stabilized under hypoxic conditions [ ]. Among the genes with HIF-1 binding sites, EGFR, PDK1, VEGF, ALKBH5, LDHA, BCL2L2, MAPK6, and MAFK are the most notable [ ]. With over 100 downstream target genes, HIF-1 activation regulates multiple cellular functions, including glucose and lipid metabolism, autophagy, survival and proliferation signaling, differentiation, ECM remodeling, and immune evasion [ , ]. HIF-1α expression correlates with reduced complete remission rates of primary tumors, increased lymph node metastasis, and poorer local control, disease-free, and overall survival in radiotherapy-treated oropharyngeal cancer patients [ ]. It also predicts worse local tumor control following radiotherapy in early-stage glottis carcinoma [ ].

## TME and CAF manipulation
Alongside hypoxic regions, the TME serves as a critical protective barrier shielding cancer cells from harmful external factors. The ECM, a network of extracellular macromolecules and minerals including collagen, glycoproteins, glycans, elastins, and fibronectin, structurally supports tissues, facilitates intercellular communication, and stores growth factors [ ]. Early in tumor development, ECM may act defensively, preventing malignant cells from invading adjacent tissues [ ]. As cancer progresses, tumor cells actively remodel the ECM either directly by secreting extracellular vesicles containing nucleic acids, lipids, and proteins or indirectly by recruiting host stromal cells such as fibroblasts and endothelial cells [ ].
Physically, dense, remodeled ECM layers confer resistance by reducing radiotherapy penetration, as demonstrated in three-dimensional nasopharyngeal carcinoma models [ ]. Tumor-TME interactions can provide alternative survival signals, activating proliferation-promoting pathways like focal adhesion kinase and receptor tyrosine kinase amplifiers, including EGFR, ErbB2, and Met [ ], which helps them avoid radiation-induced cell death [ ]. While certain tumor cells produce collagen and ECM-associated proteins themselves [ , ], CAFs predominantly contribute to ECM remodeling, actively secreting ECM components and shaping the tumor stroma [ ].
In normal wound healing, fibroblasts produce ECM components for tissue regeneration [ ] and then revert to a resting state or undergo apoptosis after repair [ ]. CAFs, however, are persistently activated by cancer-derived signals such as pathogen- and damage-associated molecular patterns [ ], TGF-β, interleukin (IL)-1, IL-6, IL-11, IL-13, and IL-21 [ , ]. Therefore, they remain in the secretory phenotype without returning to quiescence. These fibroblast cells, in turn, could promote cancer cell chemoresistance by secreting ECM and CSC-inducing chemokines, including IL-6, IL-1, and TGF-β [ - ]. Additionally, CXCL1 and long non-coding RNA DNM3OS secreted by CAFs enhance radioresistance by improving DNA repair [ , ]. CAF-secreted chemokines also modulate immune responses within the TME [ ]. Furthermore, CAFs promote the survival of irradiated nasopharyngeal carcinoma cells through the nuclear factor kappa-light-chain-enhancer of activated B cells (NF-κB) pathway [ ].

## Immune system modification
Ionizing radiation exerts not only local tumor control but also systemic antitumor effects by enhancing immune responses against non-irradiated metastatic sites, a phenomenon termed the abscopal effect [ ]. Observed in several cancers, including HNC, radiation-induced cell death and DNA damage facilitate neoantigen cross-presentation in dendritic cells, stimulating CD8+ T cells through toll-like receptor 4 and type 1 interferon signaling pathways [ , ]. However, cancer cells frequently counteract immune responses by upregulating inhibitory receptors, such as PD-L1 or CTLA-1, to neutralize cytotoxic T cells [ ]. Immune checkpoint inhibitors, which counteract this mechanism, have demonstrated substantial clinical efficacy and represent an important treatment strategy in oncology [ ]. Thus, combined radiotherapy and immunotherapy are increasingly studied, a topic discussed later in this review.
Tumors also manipulate inflammation for their advantage by inducing pathological activation of MDSCs, which inhibit cancer-killing immune responses by blocking T-cell homing [ - ], inhibiting T-cell function [ ], and exhausting T cells by overexpressing immunosuppressive signals, such as PD-L1, VISTA, Tim-3/galectin-9, and TIGIT [ - ]. Cancer cells that produce CCL2 and CSF1 can also transform monocytes into TAMs [ , ]. TAMs suppress natural killer cell activation via HLA-C, HLA-E, and HLA-G expression [ ], downregulate immune responses through PD-1 and CTLA-4 receptors [ ], and recruit regulatory T cells through chemokines such as CCL20, CCL22, and CCL5 [ - ]; thereby establishing an immunosuppressive microenvironment favorable for tumor growth. MDSCs and TAMs secrete cytokines (IL-10, IL-6, TGF-β) promoting EMT and CSC phenotypes [ - ] and growth factors that protect cancer cells from apoptosis [ ], contributing to radioresistance.

## RADIOTHERAPY SENSITIZATION OPTIONS
As our understanding of cancer biology and responses to radiotherapy improves, we gain additional strategies to address radioresistance. Numerous molecular targets have been identified and utilized to develop promising radiation-sensitizing agents employing diverse mechanisms; many of these agents are currently under clinical investigation, as summarized in .
Inhibitors of O6-methylguanine-DNA methyltransferase and PARP-2—crucial components of DDR pathways—have been developed, although none have yet received approval for radiotherapy use due to increased toxicity [ ]. PARP inhibitors (PARPi) have demonstrated specific efficacy in treating BRCA-mutant tumors [ ]. Olaparib, a PARPi, is currently in clinical trials combined with radiotherapy for treating stage II–III laryngeal and HPV-negative oropharyngeal SCC (NCT02229656). DNA-PK, a critical kinase in the NHEJ pathway, has also been explored. The radiosensitizing effects of the PK inhibitor (PKi) peposertib have been validated in vivo [ , ]. Peposertib showed favorable tolerability at daily doses of up to 200 mg in a phase I clinical trial assessing radiosensitization in HNC [ ]. Currently, peposertib in combination with radiotherapy for advanced HNC patients intolerant to cisplatin is under investigation (NCT04533750). Additionally, combined PARPi and PKi significantly reduced HNC cell survival compared with radiation alone [ ]. Inhibiting checkpoint kinase 1 (CHK1), another key NHEJ protein, eliminated radiation-induced G2 arrest in p53-deficient HNC cell lines (HN4 and HN5), forcing cells into mitosis with unrepaired DNA damage [ ]. Furthermore, a clinical trial evaluating CHK1 inhibition in locally advanced HNC is ongoing (NCT02555644).
The pan-BCL-2 inhibitor AT-101 enhances radiation-induced apoptosis in HNC in vitro, with radiosensitization achievable at clinically relevant plasma concentrations [ ]. Another apoptotic strategy involves promoting caspase-9 activation using second mitochondria-derived caspase mimetic activators like birinapant. Birinapant exhibited effectiveness in vivo [ ] and is currently undergoing a phase I clinical trial combined with intensity-modulated radiation therapy (IMRT) (NCT03803774). HSPs, which prevent cancer cell apoptosis, have been targeted using the HSP90 inhibitor onalespib, demonstrating promising radiosensitizing effects in combination with radiation [ ].
Given the altered metabolic pathways in HNC, mTOR inhibitors (mTORi) are investigated as radiosensitizers. A meta-analysis of 11 studies involving 232 patients reported enhanced partial responses with mTORi combined with chemotherapy and/or radiation, with grade 4/5 adverse events being rare [ ]. Combining mTORi, PI3K inhibitors, and radiation significantly enhanced inhibitory effects in radioresistant oral SCC cell lines [ ]. Clinical trials investigating this combination are ongoing (NCT02537223 and NCT02282371).
Anti-PD-1/PD-L1 agents, when combined with radiotherapy, have shown promising therapeutic outcomes [ ], including significant pathological responses and clinical-to-pathological downstaging in locally advanced HNC patients receiving stereotactic body radiation [ ]. Clinical trials assessing PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors combined with radiotherapy in HNC are ongoing (NCT03721757, NCT03635164, and NCT03618134). Furthermore, combined PD-1 inhibition and CTLA4 targeting with radiation therapy in stage IVA–B HNC is under evaluation (NCT03162731).
Advances in radiotherapy technology, informed by new insights into tumor heterogeneity and radiobiology, continue to evolve. Techniques such as IMRT and volumetric-modulated arc therapy deliver more conformal and uniform dose distributions, achieving higher target tumor doses compared to older methods [ ]. Selecting appropriate dose-fractions for radiation and immunotherapy trials requires careful consideration of tumor immune characteristics; radiation doses vary from low-dose (0.1–1 Gy), standard clinical dose (1.8–2.2 Gy), to high-dose radiotherapy, including ablative (5–10 Gy) and sublative doses (>12 Gy), depending on immunotherapy type [ ]. Emerging radiation approaches, such as ultra-high dose rate radiotherapy (FLASH) and spatially fractionated radiotherapy (GRID), are being explored to target intra-tumor-resistant populations [ ]. GRID delivers highly heterogeneous doses with ablative-level intensities to intratumoral regions while sparing surrounding sensitive OARs [ ], effectively debulking large HNCs and improving local control [ ]. FLASH therapy administers ultra-high-dose radiation pulses (>40 Gy/sec) over milliseconds [ ], exhibiting enhanced therapeutic efficacy in HNC and attenuating radiation-induced salivary gland damage in mice [ ]. Metal nanoparticles (NPs), such as gadolinium chelates and ultrasmall gold NPs, also increase intratumoral radiation toxicity by enhancing photoelectric absorption and generating additional secondary photons and short-range electrons. This amplifies radiation dose deposition, oxidative stress, free radical generation, cell cycle disruption, and DNA repair inhibition in NP-loaded regions [ ]. These techniques represent promising strategies for completely eliminating residual radioresistant cancer subpopulations, especially in conjunction with immunotherapy [ , ]. The oxygen level could be used as a guide to deliver intratumor-boosted radiation doses [ ], and absolute oxygen-guided radiotherapy has been reported to improve tumor control in three preclinical murine tumor models [ ]. Hypoxia-targeted therapies, including hypoxia-activated agents such as CP-506 (a nitrogen mustard prodrug), have shown significant tumor control in FaDu cells when combined with single-dose radiation [ ]. Hyperbaric oxygenation is another hypoxia-targeting strategy that, despite potential benefits in enhancing local tumor control in HNC, carries risks of severe radiation injury and oxygen toxicity-related seizures [ ].

## CONCLUSIONS
Decades of extensive research have substantially improved our comprehension of radiotherapy mechanisms and their interactions with cancer, specifically highlighting the diverse pathways through which HNC develops resistance to treatment. These mechanisms include increased activation of DSB repair pathways, enhanced evasion of cell death, alterations in energy metabolism, morphological transformations, and manipulations of the TME. Collectively, these factors significantly contribute to treatment failure and poor patient prognosis. Recognizing and understanding these defensive mechanisms is crucial, as it facilitates the development of targeted radiosensitizing strategies in both experimental and clinical settings, providing hope for improved management of radioresistant HNC. Integrative approaches involving sensitizing agents, combinations of radiotherapy and immunotherapy, optimized radiation fractionation and dosing schedules, and recent innovations like ultra-high dose-rate radiation have demonstrated promising potential in treating radiotherapy-resistant HNC. Although many of these therapeutic strategies remain under active investigation, advancements in resensitizing tumors to radiation treatment offer encouraging prospects for significantly enhancing outcomes in both curative and palliative settings.

## HIGHLIGHTS
▪ Radioresistance contributes to treatment failure and a poor prognosis in head and neck cancer (HNC).
▪ Radioresistance mechanisms of HNC include the activation of DNA damage repair pathways, increased evasion of cell death, altered energy metabolism, morphological transformations, and manipulation of the tumor microenvironment.
▪ The use of radio-sensitizing agents has shown potential in treating radiotherapy-resistant HNC.
