## Background
Eukaryotic initiation factor 6 (eIF6) is an essential regulator of ribosome biogenesis that prevents the premature association of the 60S and 40S ribosomal subunits. eIF6 eviction from nascent 60S particles, mediated by the ribosome biogenesis factors SBDS and EFL1, is required for the formation of translationally competent 80S ribosomes. SBDS and EFL1 deficiencies cause Shwachman-Diamond syndrome (SDS), a ribosomopathy mainly characterized by exocrine pancreatic insufficiency, bone marrow failure and a predisposition to hematological malignancies. Somatic EIF6 mutations that reduce the amount of eIF6 (e.g., nonsense mutation, deletion) or its ability to bind to the 60S subunit (e.g., N106S missense mutation) are frequently found in hematopoietic cells of SDS individuals. These somatic EIF6 mutations represent indirect somatic genetic rescue (SGR) in the context of SDS, as they increase cellular fitness to cause clonal expansion and are associated with clinical improvement in some cases. However, the functional consequences of these eIF6 mutations in human SDS cells have never been reported to date.

## Methods
As fibroblasts from individuals with SDS carrying biallelic pathogenic variants in either SBDS or EFL1 exhibit impaired ribosome production and reduced translation, we employed these cells, in addition to fibroblasts from a healthy donor, as a cellular model to analyze the impact of the EIF6 N106S mutation, which was introduced by CRISPR/Cas9-mediated genome editing. The assessment of eIF6 localization, ribosome biogenesis, ribosomal RNA (rRNA) processing, global protein synthesis, cellular fitness, and p53 pathway activation was conducted in these cells.

## Results
Endogenous expression of the eIF6 N106S mutant altered the subcellular distribution of eIF6 and induced pronounced defects, including impaired ribosomal RNA 3′-ETS processing, reduced availability of mature 60S subunits, and accumulation of halfmer polysomes. These alterations were associated with reduced cellular fitness in both healthy donor and SDS fibroblasts. In the context of SBDS deficiency, the N106S mutation partially alleviated aberrant retention of eIF6 on the 60S subunit but failed to restore global translation or confer a proliferative advantage.

## Conclusions
Our study reveals that the eIF6 N106S mutation uncouples partial correction of eIF6–60S dissociation from proper rRNA biogenesis and cellular fitness. These findings reveal intrinsic limits to altering eIF6–60S interactions without disrupting ribosome biogenesis. Furthermore, these results emphasize that therapeutic strategies targeting the eIF6–60S interaction must carefully balance rescue of ribosomal stress against disruption of ribosome biogenesis, particularly in disease contexts such as Shwachman-Diamond syndrome.

## Background
Ribosome biogenesis is a fundamental and highly conserved cellular process that initiates in the nucleolus and is completed in the cytoplasm. This multistep pathway can consume up to 80% of the cellular energy, and is therefore tightly regulated (James et al. ). One of the final steps of 60S ribosomal subunit maturation involves the removal of eukaryotic initiation factor 6 (eIF6) from the pre-60S particle through the coordinated action of the ribosome biogenesis factors SBDS (Shwachman-Bodian-Diamond Syndrome protein) and EFL1 (Elongation Factor-Like GTPase 1). The eviction of eIF6 is required to enable the joining of the 40S and 60S subunits, leading to the formation of a functional 80S ribosome (Warren ).
In yeast Saccharomyces cerevisiae, complete loss of eIF6 (Tif6 in yeasts) is lethal and results in reduced 60S subunit levels and defective pre-rRNA processing (Basu et al. ). In contrast, a mouse model of eIF6 haploinsufficiency is viable but displays global growth retardation, increased accumulation of 80S ribosomes, and impaired insulin-stimulated protein translation (Gandin et al. ). While eIF6 overexpression has been associated with poor prognosis in several cancers, dysregulation of eIF6 activity is also a hallmark of the inherited ribosomopathy Shwachman-Diamond syndrome (SDS) (Boocock et al. , Miluzio et al. , Gao et al. ).
SDS (OMIM #260400) is a rare autosomal recessive disorder characterized by bone marrow failure, pancreatic insufficiency, growth delay, skeletal anomalies, and an increased risk of blood cancers (Warren ). The majority of SDS cases are caused by biallelic germline pathogenic variants (GPV) in the SBDS gene (Boocock et al. ), although biallelic GPV in EFL1 have also been found recently associated with SDS in some cases (Stepensky et al. , Tan et al. , Cario et al. , Lee et al. ). Because deficiency in SBDS or EFL1 impairs ribosomal subunit assembly and protein synthesis, SDS is classified as a ribosomopathy that presumably affect translation in all tissues. It has been furthermore demonstrated that the ribosomal stress resulting from human SBDS deficiency caused p53 activation and cell growth arrest (Elghetany and Alter , Hao et al. ). The activation of the p53 pathway in a SBDS-deficient context was further confirmed in murine and zebrafish models (Tourlakis et al. , Zambetti et al. , Oyarbide et al. ).
We and others have recently reported that ~ 60% of SDS patients, but not healthy donors, harbor mosaic somatic EIF6 mutations in hematopoietic cells (Kennedy et al. , Machado et al. , Tan et al. ). These mutations represent indirect somatic genetic rescue (SGR) in SDS, as they promote clonal expansion and are associated with clinical improvement in some cases (Kennedy et al. , Tan et al. , Khan et al. , Revy et al. ). While most EIF6 somatic mutations found in blood cells from individuals with SDS are nonsense or destabilize the eIF6 protein, some recurrent heterozygous missense mutations, including the N106S eIF6 mutation, do not affect the expression of eIF6 but reduce its binding affinity for the 60S subunit (Kennedy et al. , Tan et al. ). We previously demonstrated that overexpression of the eIF6 N106S variant in yeast, Dictyostelium discoideum, and Drosophila melanogaster SDS models confers a fitness advantage by alleviating the ribosomal assembly defects associated with SBDS deficiency (Machado et al. , Tan et al. , Menne et al. ).
Although reduced eIF6 dosage or the presence of the N106S mutation at the heterozygous state appears beneficial in an SDS context, two studies have demonstrated deleterious effects of the N106S mutation in human colon cancer cell line HCT116. Indeed, the introduction of the eIF6 N106S mutant in a homozygous state into the otherwise wild-type HCT116 resulted in a reduction in translation rate, impaired in vitro cell proliferation and lower cancer invasiveness in vivo (Elliff et al. , Roshan et al. ). However, the functional consequences of endogenous, homozygous expression of the eIF6 N106S variant have never been investigated in the context of SDS. Consequently, determining the impact of the eIF6 N106S mutant is of fundamental interest to understand the mechanisms undercovering the in vivo effect of the disruption of the eIF6–60S interaction in cells from both healthy and SDS individuals.
Here, we used CRISPR/Cas9-mediated genome editing to introduce the N106S mutation at a homozygous state into the EIF6 locus of human fibroblasts. Cells were derived from healthy individuals as well as from SDS patients carrying biallelic pathogenic variants in either SBDS or EFL1. We then analyzed the molecular and functional consequences of physiological expression of the eIF6 N106S mutation in both wild-type and SDS contexts. Our results provide unprecedented observations demonstrating that although homozygous expression of the N106S mutation alleviates aberrant eIF6 retention on the 60S subunit in SDS cells, it results in reduced cell proliferation and defective ribosomal RNA biogenesis (rRNA). These findings indicate that this dose-escalation model of eIF6 N106S does not recapitulate the physiological clonal rescue observed in hematopoietic cells of SDS patients. Instead, these results highlight the potential toxic effects of therapeutic strategies aimed at disrupting the eIF6–60S interaction in the context of SDS.

## Methods

## Patients
Informed and written consent were obtained from donors and patients. The study and protocols comply with the 1975 Declaration of Helsinki, as well as with the local legislation and ethical guidelines from the Comité de Protection des Personnes de l’Île de France II and the French advisory committee on data processing in medical research. The INSERM Institutional Review Board also approved this study. Fibroblasts were obtained from skin biopsy. These cells were immortalized by stable expression of TERT after transduction of the lentiviral vector pLV-hTERT-IRES-hygro (a gift from Tobias Meyer; Addgene plasmid #85140; http://n2t.net/addgene:85140; RRID: Addgene_85140) and transformed by transfection of the SV40 large T antigen expressing vector pLAS-wt (Daya-Grosjean et al. ).

## Cytosolic/nuclear protein extraction
Cytosolic proteins were isolated by incubating cells in hypotonic buffer (HEPES 10 mM, MgCl2 1.5 mM, KCl 10mM, DTT 0.5 mM, EDTA 1 mM, phosphatase and protease inhibitors (Sigma P5726; Sigma P0044; Roche 04693159001) for 15 min on ice followed by addition of NP-40 (0.2% final), quick vortex and centrifuge 20 s at 12,500 RPM at 4 °C. Supernatant was collected and represent the Cytosolic fraction. Pellets were washed once with hypotonic buffer, centrifuge 2 min at 12,500 RPM at 4 °C. Pellets were resuspended and incubated in 150 µL of isotonic buffer (HEPES 5 mM, MgCl2 1.5mM, NaCl 300 mM, DTT 0.5mM, EDTA 0.2mM, Glycerol 20%, phosphatase and protease inhibitors (Sigma P5726; Sigma P0044; Roche 04693159001) for 30 min on ice with 19G syringe mixing every 5 min. Samples were then centrifuge at 10,000 RPM for 10 min at 4 °C. Supernatant was collected and represent the nuclear fraction.

## Immunofluorescence
Cells were fixed 10 min in PBS, 4% paraformaldehyde. Slides were then incubated 30 min in a permeabilization/blocking buffer (PBS 1X, 1% BSA, 0.5% Triton X-100, 1 mM EDTA). Slides were stained 1 h at room temperature with eIF6 primary antibody (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, #sc-390432; 1:200 dilution). After washing in PBS, slides were incubated 30 min at room temperature with secondary antibody, Alexa Fluor™ 488 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, #A-11001; 1:1000 dilution). Nucleus (DAPI 1:1000) and Nucleolus (Nucleolus Bright Red (1:500 Dojindo) were stained for 10 min at room temperature. Slides were then sealed with FluorSave™ Reagent (Millipore). Images were taken using spinning disk (Zeiss Axio-observer Z1) and analyzed using Fiji (ImageJ2 v2.14.0/1.54f) and Qupath (v0.4.3).

## Western blot
Except for the cyto/nuclear fractions described above, the protein extracts were obtained by cellular lysis in TNE 0.5% buffer (Tris HCl pH 7.5 50mM; NaCl 150mM; EDTA 3mM; NP40 0,5% + phosphatase and protease inhibitors (Sigma P5726; Sigma P0044; Roche 04693159001) for 20 min at 4 °C. Protein concentration was determined by Bradford quantification (Biorad #50000006). Proteins were loaded on acrylamide/bis-acrylamide 37.5:1 Tris-HCl SDS PAGE 10–15% gel. After migration, proteins were transferred on PVDF membrane, then blocked one hour in Intercept® (TBS) Blocking Buffer (Licorbio™ 927-60001). Primary antibodies were incubated overnight at 4 °C (eIF6 (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, #sc-390432, 1:1000); SBDS (Thermofischer, #PA5-28950, 1:1000); EFL1 (Proteintech, #24729-1, 1:1000); P53 (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, #sc-126, 1:1000); Tubulin (Proteintech, #11224-1-AP, 1:1000); Vinculin (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, #sc-73614, 1:1000); H3 (Abcam, #ab1791, 1:1000); eL8 Proteintech, #15340-1-AP, (1:500)). Secondary antibodies were incubated one hour at room temperature (IRDye 800cW; IRDye 680RD (Licorbio™), 1:10000). Odyssey® CLx Imaging system was used to detect fluorescent signals on the membrane.

## Translation rate assessment
Global protein synthesis was measured by OP-Puromycin integration using ClickiT™ HPG Alexa Fluor™ 594 Protein Synthesis Assay Kit (Thermofisher). OP-Puromycin was incorporated for 30 min in the SV40-hTERT fibroblasts and 90 min in the hTERT-primary fibroblasts models. As the primary fibroblasts exhibited a lower OP-Puro incorporation rate compared to the SV40-transformed fibroblasts, we extended the labelling time for primary cells to obtain a sufficient, intense signal for analysis. Sample treated with 50 µg/mL cycloheximide was used as a negative control of translation. Sample were analysed by FACS analysis (Agilent NovoCyte® Flow Cytometer) and results analysed using FlowJo v10.10 software (FlowJo, Ashland, OR).

## Competition assay
The HD, SBDS and EFL1 cell lines wild type for EIF6 were transduced with pLVX-EF1α-GFP-IRES-mCherry lentivirus. Cell sorting was conducted to obtain 100% GFP+ HD eIF6WT, SBDS eIF6WT and EFL1 eIF6WT cell lines. An equal number of GFP+ eIF6WT cells and GFP− cells WT for eIF6 (eIF6WT) or carrying the eIF6 N106S mutation in a homozygous state (eIF6N106S) were mixed to obtain ~ 50% GFP+ cells and ~ 50% GFP− cells. The percentage of GFP− cells was followed by FACS over time.

## Generation of EIF6 mutated clones by CRISPR/Cas9
Gene editing was performed on SV40-hTERT transformed and hTERT primary fibroblasts from HD and SDS patients’ cells. In vitro assembled Cas9-sgRNA complex was transfected by nucleofection (Amaxa™ 4D-Nucleofector™ program FF-104). Two guides targeting intron 2 and 3’UTR of EIF6 were used to generate the eIF6 KO cellular model. To introduce the EIF6 N106S mutation, we mixed a RNA guide neighbouring the N106S site with the Cas9 protein. The RNA-Cas9 complex was co-transfected with a DNA matrix carrying the EIF6 N106S. Transfected cells were incubated during 72 h with 10µM DNA-PKcs inhibitor Nedisertib to improve mutagenesis. After 72 h, we performed limiting dilution cloning by targeting ~ 1 cell per well in 96 well plates. After ~ 3 weeks of culture, clones were harvested and genotyped by PCR and Sanger sequencing.

## Polysome profile
Cells were cultivated in 15-cm tissue culture dishes (one for SV40-hTERT fibroblasts and two for hTERT primary fibroblasts) up to 80% confluence. Cells were then washed twice in cold hypotonic buffer (Tris HCl pH 7.5 5mM; KCl 1.5mM; MgCl2 2.5mM; cycloheximide 50 µg/mL) and scrapped in isotonic buffer (hypotonic buffer complemented with Triton X-100 0.5%; Na-Deoxycholate 0.5%; DTT 3mM; RNAse Inhibitor 120U/mL; Protease Inhibitor 1X). Lysate were centrifuged 10 min at 3000 g and the lysate collected. Protein quantification was measured by Bradford method. 1 mg of each sample was loaded on 10–50% sucrose gradient (20mM Tris pH 7.5; 80mM NaCl; 5mM MgCl2; 1mM DTT) and ultracentrifuged at 4°C during 140 min at 36,000 rpm using SW41Ti rotor. Polysome profiles were analyzed by measuring RNA absorbance at 254 nm using Gilson N2 data recorder. Fractions were collected for Western Blot analysis.

## Isolation of total RNAs and northern blot analyses
For total RNA isolation, cell pellets were mixed in TRI reagent and extracted following the manufacturer’s instructions. The upper aqueous phase was further purified by phenol/chloroform/isoamyl alcohol (25:24:1) extractions (Sigma) prior to isopropanol precipitation. Quantifications were performed at 260 nm with an Implen NP80 spectrophotometer (Mettler Toledo). Pre-rRNA intermediates were separated on a 1.1% agarose gel containing 1.2% formaldehyde and 1× Tri/Tri buffer (30 mM triethanolamine, 30 mM tricine, pH 7.9) (3 µg total RNAs/lane) or on a 6% polyacrylamide gel (19:1) in 1× TBE buffer containing 7 M urea (1.5 µg total RNAs/lane). 5S and 5.8S rRNAs were separated on a 6% polyacrylamide gel (19:1) in TBE buffer containing 7 M urea (2 µg total RNAs/lane). RNAs were transferred to Hybond N+ nylon membrane (GE Healthcare) and cross-linked under UV light. Membrane pre-hybridization for 2 hours in 6× SSC, 5× Denhardt’s solution, 0.5% SDS, 0.9 µg/mL tRNA and then incubated overnight with the corresponding 5’-radiolabeled oligonucleotide probe. The probes were: 5’ETS (5’-AGACGAGAACGCCTGACACGCACGGCAC-3’), 5’ITS1 (5’-CCTCGCCCTCCGGGCTCCGTTAATGATC-3’), ITS1-5.8 S (5’-CTAAGAGTCGTACGAGGTCG-3’), ITS2 (5’-CTGCGAGGGAACCCCCAGCCGCGCA-3’ and 5’-GCGCGACGGCGGACGACACCGCGGCGTC-3’), 28 S-3’ETS (5’-CACGCGCGCGCGGACAAACCCTTG-3’), 18 S (5’-TTTACTTCCTCTAGATAGTCAAGTTCGACC-3’), 28 S (5’-CCCGTTCCCTTGGCTGTGGTTTCGCTAGATA-3’), 5.8 S (5’-GTTCTTCATCGACGCACGAGC-3’), 5 S (5’-TTCCGAGATCAGACGAGATCGGG-3’), 7SK (5’-CATGGAGCGGTGAGGGAGGA-3’ and 5’-GTGTCTGGAGTCTTGGAAGC-3’). After washing twice for 10 min in 2× SSC, 0.1% SDS and once in 1× SSC, 0.1% SDS, the membrane was exposed to a PhosphorImager screen. Radioactive signals were revealed using a Typhoon Trio PhosphorImager (Amersham, Cytivia) and quantified using the ImageGauge software. RAMP analyses were performed as previously described (Wang et al. ).

## Results

## The subcellular localization of eIF6 is affected by the N106S mutation
Firstly, we sought to determine the functional consequences of the eIF6 N106S mutation in cells from a healthy donor (HD) by introducing this mutation via CRISPR/Cas9. Prior to the implementation of CRISPR/Cas9-mediated genome editing, the HD fibroblasts were stably transfected with the large T antigen from SV40 (LT-SV40)-encoding gene to induce transformation, and transduced with a lentiviral vector expressing the human catalytic subunit of the telomerase complex (hTERT) to achieve cellular immortalization (Bodnar et al. , Hahn et al. ). The eIF6 N106S mutation was introduced by means of an RNA guide targeting the EIF6 coding sequence in the vicinity of the N106 codon position, in association with a 142-nucleotide single-stranded DNA molecule carrying the c.327 A > G; N106S mutation in EIF6. Clones carrying homozygous eIF6 N106S mutation (thereafter annotated eIF6N106S) were detected by Sanger sequencing (Fig.  A). Immunoblots resulted in a similar detection of eIF6 protein in HD eIF6WT and HD eIF6N106S clones, demonstrating that the endogenous N106S mutation did not impact eIF6 expression and/or stability in these cells (Fig.  B). We thus tested whether the N106S mutation could affect the subcellular localization of eIF6. Immunoblots of the cytoplasmic and nuclear fractions of HD cells showed that the presence of eIF6 in the nuclear fraction was significantly reduced in HD eIF6N106S compared to HD eIF6WT cells (Fig.  C). Quantitative immunofluorescence analysis confirmed the significant decrease of the nuclear localization of the eIF6 N106S mutant (Figs.  D-E). Immunofluorescence experiments in the presence of a nucleolus staining dye allowed us to quantify the presence of eIF6 specifically in nucleoli (Figs.  D). We observed a decrease in the amount of eIF6 in the nucleoli of HD eIF6N106S compared to HD eIF6WT clones, of the same order of magnitude as that observed in the whole nucleus (Fig.  E). Taken together, these results suggest that the N106S mutation perturbs the subcellular localization of eIF6 and reduces its localization in the nucleus without abolishing its ability to be part of the nucleolus. Future studies will be necessary to determine whether the reduced nuclear localization of the eIF6 N106S mutant is due to altered shuttling, nuclear import, export, retention, or nucleolar residence time. 

## The eIF6 N106S mutation reduces translation rate and causes cellular growth defect
Previous studies have demonstrated that the introduction of the EIF6 N106S mutation in a homozygous state reduces both the rate of translation and proliferation in HCT116 cancer cells (Elliff et al. , Roshan et al. ). We therefore investigated whether a similar effect would be observed in our HD eIF6N106S cellular model. The measurement of OP-puro incorporation highlighted an approximate 30% reduction in the translation rate in HD eIF6N106S cells in comparison to HD eIF6WT cells (Fig.  A). We next conducted a competitive assay to determine the impact of the eIF6 N106S mutant on cellular proliferation. HD eIF6WT cells were transduced with a GFP-expressing lentiviral vector and cell sorted to obtain a 100% GFP+ population (Suppl. Figure 1). These cells were then cocultured with non-fluorescent (GFP−) HD eIF6N106S or HD eIF6WT clones. Cells were then harvested over time and analyzed by FACS to determine the ratio of GFP+ and GFP− cells. Any change in the GFP−/GFP+ ratio observed over time by flow cytometry would provide insight into the proliferation/survival capacity of the cell population tested (Fig.  B). We consistently measured a rapid loss of the GFP− HD eIF6N106S cells cocultured with GFP+ HD eIF6WT cells, indicating a growth advantage of the wild-type cells (Fig.  C). We also observed an unexpected decrease over time in the proportion of HD eIF6WT GFP− mixed with HD eIF6WT GFP+ cells, but with a much slower kinetics. Taken together, these results indicated that the cells expressing the eIF6 N106S mutant exhibited a reduction in translation rate accompanied by a strong selective disadvantage over HD eIF6WT cells.

 

## eIF6 N106S mutation perturbs ribosome biogenesis
Given the established role of eIF6 in the production of functional ribosomes, we next sought to determine whether the expression of an endogenous eIF6 N106S mutant could affect polysome formation in HD cells. Following sucrose sedimentation, we consistently found a strong reduction in the ratio of 60S:40S ribosomal subunits in extracts from HD eIF6N106S as compared to HD eIF6WT, suggesting a defect in 60S subunit synthesis or stability (Figs.  A). In addition, the polysome profiles derived from HD eIF6N106S cells demonstrated the presence of halfmers, which are indicative of the presence of stalled pre-initiation 48S complexes (Moore JBt, Farrar et al. ) (Fig.  A, arrows). This finding indicates that the exclusive expression of the eIF6 N106S mutant in cells from a HD results in a significant defect in the formation of functional 80S ribosomes. This phenomenon may underlie the observed reduction in translation (Fig.  A).

 

As was demonstrated in previous studies, eIF6 shuttling from the cytoplasm to the nucleus is associated with rRNA biogenesis (Basu et al. , Sanvito et al. , Gantenbein et al. , Senger et al. , Ye et al. , Brina et al. ). Furthermore, proteomic and cryoEM studies have shown that eIF6 is part of the early precursors of the 60S subunits (Vanden Broeck and Klinge , Vanden Broeck and Klinge ). Having observed a reduced amount of nuclear eIF6 N106S mutant and an aberrant polysome profile in cells expressing this mutant, we asked whether the eIF6 N106S mutant could affect ribosome biogenesis. To test this hypothesis, we analyzed the rRNA precursors (pre-rRNAs) by northern blot. As compared to HD eIF6WT cells, HD eIF6N106S clones exhibited precursors abnormally extended with the 3’ETS (ITS2, 3’ETS and 28S-3’ETS probes) (Fig.  B). This defect in 3’ETS removal was associated with impaired cleavage of the ITS1 at site 2, as revealed by the accumulation of the 36S and 36S-C pre-rRNAs (probe ITS1-5.8S) (Fig.  C). In addition, the ITS2 probe showed bands migrating below the 32S pre-rRNA, which likely correspond to pre-rRNA degradation products. Furthermore, analysis of the 5.8S rRNA showed a change in the ratio between the short and the long forms, suggesting that eIF6 N106S mutation affects the maturation of the 5’ end of this rRNA (Fig.  D, E). These results show that the eIF6 N106S mutant significantly affects pre-rRNA processing at an early step, consistent with the presence of eIF6 in early ribosomal precursors. This pre-rRNA processing defect is expected to affect 60S subunit production and likely explains the deficit in free 60S subunits observed on sucrose gradient in HD eIF6N106S (Fig.  A).

## Impact of the eIF6 N106S mutation in cells from SDS individuals
The somatic eIF6 N106S mutation is frequently identified in blood cells from individuals with SDS. This mutation corresponds to somatic genetic rescue as it confers a selective advantage to the cells and induce their clonal expansion in vivo (Kennedy et al. , Tan et al. , Revy et al. ). We thus wanted to determine the functional impact of the eIF6 N106S mutant in SDS cells. We used SV40-transformed and hTERT-immortalized fibroblasts from SDS individuals carrying biallelic germline pathogenic variants in either SBDS or EFL1 (henceforth referred to as SBDS and EFL1, respectively). These SDS cellular models are pertinent as they have been previously demonstrated to exhibit impaired production of functional 80S ribosomes and reduced translation rate (Tan et al. , Finch et al. , Tan et al. ). In accordance with previous studies (Tan et al. , Finch et al. , Tan et al. ), SBDS and EFL1 SV40-hTERT fibroblasts demonstrated diminished levels of SBDS and EFL1 proteins, respectively, in comparison to HD cells (Fig.  A). These cells were then subjected to CRISPR/Cas9-induced mutagenesis to introduce the EIF6 N106S mutation in a homozygous state. We obtained SBDS and EFL1 clones carrying the N106S mutation in a homozygous state (denoted SBDS eIF6N106S and EFL1 eIF6N106S, respectively) and compared them with SBDS and EFL1 clones not mutated in EIF6 (referred to as SBDS eIF6WT and EFL1 eIF6WT, respectively). Similarly to what observed in HD cells (Fig.  B), the endogenous expression of the N106S mutation at a homozygous state did not affect the expression and/or stability of eIF6 in the SBDS and EFL1 cells (Figs.  B, C).

 

The subcellular localization of eIF6 was analyzed using both immunoblots on cytoplasmic/nuclear extracts and immunofluorescence assays in SBDS eIF6N106S and EFL1 eIF6N106S cells. As observed in HD cells, a decrease in eIF6 nuclear and nucleolar localization was observed in the SBDS eIF6N106S and EFL1 eIF6N106S in comparison with the SBDS eIF6WT and EFL1 eIF6WT, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 2). We next analyzed the translation rate in SDS cells expressing or not the eIF6 N106S mutation. In line with previous studies, SBDS eIF6WT and EFL1 eIF6WT exhibited a reduced incorporation of OP-Puro in neosynthesized proteins as compared to HD eIF6WT cells, confirming a reduced translation rate in SDS cells (Fig.  D) (Tan et al. , Finch et al. ). Interestingly, in contrast to what observed in HD cells (Fig.  A), the expression of the N106S mutant in SDS cells did not result in a significant variation in the translation rate as evidenced by the comparable OP-Puro incorporation levels observed in the SBDS eIF6WT, SBDS eIF6N106S, EFL1 eIF6WT and EFL1 eIF6N106S clones (Fig.  D). These results suggest that the eIF6 N106S mutation does not ameliorate the observed translation defect in SDS cells when present in the homozygous state, nor does it worsen the defect as seen in HD cells.
We next conducted competition assay to analyze the effect of the eIF6 N106S mutant on cell proliferation and cellular fitness in SDS cells. Surprisingly, the SBDS eIF6N106S and EFL1 eIF6N106S were rapidly lost when cocultured with SBDS eIF6WT and EFL1 eIF6WT, respectively (Fig.  E). Taken together, this indicated that the eIF6 N106S mutation endogenously expressed in a homozygous state is deleterious in SDS cells.

## The eIF6 N106S mutation impairs ribosome biogenesis in SDS cells
The eviction of eIF6 from the 60S subunit is impaired in SBDS mutated cells (Finch et al. , Wong et al. ), and previous results demonstrated that the overexpression of the eIF6 N106S mutant in SBDS cells could reduce the level of eIF6 bound to the 60S subunit (Kennedy et al. , Tan et al. ). We therefore wondered whether this effect would hold true in a context where the eIF6 N106S mutant is endogenously expressed. Extracts from SBDS eIF6WT and SBDS eIF6N106S cells were fractionated by sucrose gradient sedimentation and immunoblotted to visualize eIF6 and eL8, a component of the 60S subunit used as control. In line with previous studies, we found a strong co-elution of eIF6 with the 60S fraction in SBDS cells, indicative of a defective dissociation of eIF6 from the nascent 60S ribosomal subunit in a context of impaired SBDS function (Fig.  A) (Kennedy et al. , Tan et al. ). The amount of eIF6 co-eluting with the 60S subunit was found to be significantly reduced in the SBDS eIF6N106S cells, indicating that endogenous expression of the eIF6 N106S mutant suffices to alleviate the aberrant binding of eIF6 to the 60S caused by the SBDS deficiency (Fig.  A). Furthermore, in line with previous studies, the polysome profile obtained after sucrose sedimentation in the SBDS eIF6WT and EFL1 eIF6WT cells showed a defective 80S ribosome production characterized by the accumulation of the 60S subunit resulting in a high 60S:40S ratio as compared to HD eIF6WT cells (Tan et al. , Finch et al. , Wong et al. ) (Fig.  B, C). Strikingly, the ratio measured in SBDS eIF6WT and EFL1 eIF6WT decreased in SBDS eIF6N106S and EFL1 eIF6N106S cells, reaching a level that was either similar or lower to that of HD eIF6WT cells. This finding suggests that the expression of the eIF6 N106S mutant might allow partial relief of aberrant eIF6 retention in 60S subunit and therefore reduce the accumulation of free 60S in SDS cells. However, analysis of the SBDS eIF6N106S and EFL1 eIF6N106S cells revealed no restoration of functional 80S formation. In contrast, these cells accumulated halfmer polysomes in a manner analogous to HD eIF6N106S cells. This finding suggests a deficiency in functional 60S subunits also in SDS eIF6N106S cells (Figs.  B, C).

 

We next analyzed pre-rRNAs by northern blot in the SDS cells expressing either the WT or the N106S mutated form of eIF6. We observed only minor variations in the pre-rRNA profile in the SDS cells (SBDS eIF6WT and EFL1 eIF6WT) compared to the HD eIF6WT (Figs.  D-G). This indicates that the accumulation of 60S subunits observed in SBDS and EFL1 cells is not due to impaired rRNA biogenesis. In contrast, SBDS eIF6N106S and EFL1 eIF6N106S cells exhibited a pronounced pre-rRNA processing defects similar to the one found in HD eIF6N106S cells. This observation suggests that the eIF6 N106S mutant impairs rRNA biogenesis in SDS as in HD cells, which generates a similar deficit in 60S subunit formation (Fig.  D-G).

## Impact of eIF6 mutations in WT and SBDS-deficient primary fibroblasts
The finding that the homozygous eIF6 N106S mutation did not improve cellular fitness in SDS SV40-hTERT fibroblasts contrasted with the positive effect of this mutation, when expressed at a heterozygous state, in hematopoietic cells from SBDS-deficient individuals (Kennedy et al. , Machado et al. , Tan et al. ). We reasoned that the absence of detection of a beneficial effect of the N106S mutation in SDS clones might be due, at least in part, to the transformed status of the cellular model used in our study. Indeed, expression of the large T antigen of the SV40 virus, which interferes with the p53 and Rb pathways (Hahn et al. ), could alter the behavior of the cells and mask a putative positive effect of the eIF6 N106S mutation in SDS cells. To test this hypothesis, we introduced the EIF6 N106S mutation via CRISPR/Cas9 in primary fibroblasts from a healthy donor and a SBDS-mutated individual. Prior to mutagenesis, we transduced the primary fibroblasts with an hTERT-expressing lentiviral vector in order to maintain their telomere length (a prerequisite for avoiding cellular senescence, which would prevent the production of cell clones) without affecting the p53 and Rb pathways, therefore making them immortalized but not transformed (Bodnar et al. , Hahn et al. ). Subsequently, these cells were submitted to CRISPR/cas9-mediated genome editing to generate EIF6 deletion or introduce the EIF6 N106S mutation. EIF6 heterozygous KO clones (hereafter denoted eIF6WT/KO) were retrieved in both HD and SBDS-mutated primary fibroblasts. It is noteworthy that 72 h following CRISPR/Cas9-mediated mutagenesis, the introduced N106S mutation was detected at similar frequencies in both the HD (9–32%) and SBDS (13–29%) bulk primary fibroblast populations, suggesting equivalent efficiency in editing in both (Supplementary Fig. 3). However, N106S mutated clones were obtained exclusively in SBDS cells and only in a heterozygous state (hereafter denoted eIF6WT/N106S). This observation suggests that N106S-mutated HD cells were likely lost during the cloning process, possibly due to a deleterious effect of the mutation in primary HD fibroblasts.
As was the case in SV40-transformed fibroblasts, the protein synthesis rate was reduced in SBDS primary fibroblasts in comparison to HD primary cells, thus confirming an impaired translation efficiency in these SDS cells (Fig.  A). The deletion of one copy of EIF6 gene caused a significant decrease in the rate of protein synthesis in HD cells. In contrast, in SBDS-mutated cells, the translation rate was not modified by EIF6 hemizygosity (Fig.  A). Consistent with previous studies demonstrating that SBDS-deficiency causes ribosomal stress that resulted in p53 stabilization (Hao et al. ), we observed a readable increased p53 protein amount in SBDS primary fibroblasts as compared to their HD counterpart (Fig.  B). Strikingly, the p53 stabilization was alleviated in eIF6WT/N106S and eIF6WT/KO SBDS primary cells (Figs.  B). These results suggested that reduced eIF6 dosage or heterozygous expression of the eIF6 N106S mutant is sufficient to prevent ribosomal stress-induced p53 stabilization in SBDS primary cells. However, competition assay indicated that it was not accompanied by a selective advantage to the unmodified SBDS cells but rather has a deleterious effect (Fig.  C). We conclude from these results that, in this non-transformed SDS-derived cellular model, EIF6 hemizygosity leads to reduced p53 pathway activity, but that this is not sufficient to ameliorate cellular fitness and/or proliferation as compared to the unmodified SBDS cells.

 

## Discussion
Biallelic mutations in the SBDS or EFL1 genes cause a ribosomopathy known as Shwachman-Diamond syndrome. It is now well established that the proteins SBDS and EFL1 cooperate to evict the anti-association factor eIF6 from the pre60S ribosomal subunit, thus allowing its interaction with the 40S subunit to form active 80S ribosomes that carry out protein synthesis (Warren ). In hematopoietic cells from SBDS-mutated individuals, somatic mutations that affect EIF6, known as SGR, are frequent and associated with an improvement of the cellular fitness (Kennedy et al. , Machado et al. , Tan et al. , Revy et al. ). In the present study, we investigated the functional consequences of the eIF6 N106S mutation in human fibroblasts from a healthy donor and SDS individuals carrying biallelic pathogenic variants in either SBDS or EFL1. In the first instance, we used SV40-transformed hTERT-immortalized fibroblasts in which we introduced the mutations by CRISPR/Cas9 in a homozygous state to facilitate the examination of the effect of this mutation in the absence of any potential confounding effects that may be induced by the wild-type eIF6 protein. A number of studies have demonstrated the essentiality of eIF6 in different cellular and animal models (Basu et al. , Gandin et al. , Oyarbide et al. , Sanvito et al. ). The fact that we successfully generated clones that carried the eIF6 N106S mutation in a homozygous state suggests that the N106S mutation retains some function compatible with cellular viability and is therefore likely to be hypomorphic. These eIF6N106S cellular models provided a unique opportunity to study the effect of this mutation in human cells from HD and SDS individuals. Although normally expressed, the eIF6 N106S mutant showed a disturbed subcellular localization, with a reduced amount of protein found in the nucleus. Further studies are required to ascertain the molecular cause of this abnormal eIF6 localization. However, the eIF6 N106S mutant demonstrated the capacity to accumulate in the nucleolus, consistent with a recently reported homozygous eIF6 N106S mutated HCT116 cellular model (Roshan et al. ), but contrasting with the outcome observed in an overexpression system that indicated the absence of the eIF6 N106S mutant in the nucleolus (Kennedy et al. ). The reason for this discrepancy is currently unclear.
We found that the eIF6 N106S mutant expressed in a homozygous state in SV40-tranformed HD fibroblasts perturbed polysome profiles, induced halfmers, and resulted in defective cellular proliferation. Consistently, the eIF6 N106S mutant induced an early rRNA biogenesis impairment highlighted by a defect in the elimination of the 3’ETS. The accumulation of 3’ETS extended precursors was previously observed upon depletion of the U8 snoRNA (Langhendries et al. ), the absence of 60S subunit maturation factors like BOP1, DDX51 (Strezoska et al. , Srivastava et al. ) or URB1 (Shan et al. ), or upon depletion of the 3’-5’ exonuclease ISG10-L2, which is involved in 3’-ETS removal (Aubert et al. ). In line with the impact of the N106S mutation on ribosome biogenesis, eIF6 is part of the earliest 60S subunit precursors characterized by cryo-EM and remains associated to the particles during the subsequent maturation steps (Vanden Broeck and Klinge , Sanghai et al. ). Interestingly, it has recently been shown that the N106S mutation abolishes the interaction between eIF6 and BCCIP (Roshan et al. ), a factor previously implicated in pre-rRNA processing and 60S subunit production (Ye et al. ). Further investigation is required to determine whether the defect in rRNA biogenesis observed in eIF6N106S cells is due to impaired eIF6/BCCIP interaction, reduced nuclear import, or both. Notably, with the exception of the reduction in translation rate only found in HD cells, all the effects induced by the eIF6 N106S mutant in HD SV40-fibroblasts were also observed in SDS cells (Fig.  ). Interestingly, despite the absence of deleterious effect of the homozygous eIF6 N106S mutation on translation rate in the SDS models, these clones are still counter selected in competition assay suggesting that the fitness defect is not fully driven by a translation rate reduction. Thus, the impaired ribosome biogenesis observed in those clones might lead to more complex defects not only impacting the polysome formation and translation initiation, including ribosomal stress responses. Further investigations are required to determine if others stress responses are involved and could lead to reduce the cell viability.

 

Interestingly, the deleterious effect of the homozygous eIF6 N106S mutation we found in HD SV40-transformed fibroblasts is reminiscent to what recently reported by the Origanti lab in the HCT116 colorectal cancer cells (Elliff et al. , Roshan et al. ). In the HCT116 cells, the deleterious effect of the eIF6 N106S mutant was accompanied by an increase in p53 and p21 levels (Roshan et al. ). Importantly, in contrast to SV40-transformed cells in which the p53 protein is destabilized by the large T antigen of SV40, in HCT116 cells the p53 protein amount is not directly affected by the transformed status of these cells. However, the HCT116 cell line carries a heterozygous gain-of-function mutation in PPM1D, the WIP1-encoding gene, which results in altered p53-dependent responses to several stimuli, including genotoxic stresses and regulation of the cell cycle (Kleiblova et al. , Burocziova et al. ). It is therefore conceivable that in both our SV40-transformed cellular model and in HCT116 cells, the perturbed p53 pathway could allow clones homozygous for the eIF6 N106S mutation to be tolerated by bypassing potential checkpoint and cellular stress, whereas in primary fibroblasts we were unable to obtain eIF6 N106S homozygous mutants. Furthermore, in primary fibroblasts, we obtained heterozygous N106S mutated clones in SBDS but not in HD cells. This suggests that in non-transformed HD cells the eIF6 N106S mutation, even in a heterozygous state, is not tolerated, unlike in primary SBDS cells. However, to our surprise, we also found that although both the heterozygous N106S mutation and EIF6 hemizygosity reduced the amount of p53 in SBDS-deficient primary fibroblasts, EIF6 hemizygosity did not confer a proliferative advantage over unmodified cells. This unexpected observation contrasted with the well-established improved cellular fitness conferred by heterozygous deletion or nonsense mutation of the EIF6 gene in SBDS-deficient haematopoietic cells (Kennedy et al. , Machado et al. , Tan et al. ). One can imagine that the rather low proliferation rate of primary fibroblasts and the optimal culture conditions used in our study are not conducive to detect a possible benefit of EIF6 hemizygosity in the context of SBDS deficiency. Future analyses under stressful conditions (e.g., starvation, genotoxics, oxydative stress) will be necessary to test this hypothesis. On the other hand, it cannot be excluded that the selective advantage conferred by eIF6 haploinsufficiency (and the N106S mutant) in the SBDS mutant condition is particularly pronounced in hematopoietic cells. This assumption is supported by the observation that the hematopoietic system is particularly affected in SDS (Warren ). Furthermore, it has recently been reported that impaired ribosome production strongly sensitizes the hematopoietic system to ferroptosis (Zhao et al. , Papadimitriou-Tsantarliotou et al. ). Interestingly, the generation of induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs) from peripheral blood cells of SBDS patients has recently been reported (Hamabata et al. ). In vitro differentiation of SBDS-mutant iPSCs revealed hematological abnormalities seen in SDS, including impaired granulopoiesis, reduced colony-forming potential and defect in neutrophil chemotaxis (Hamabata et al. ). We therefore propose that the generation of iPSCs from our cellular model of SBDS-mutant primary fibroblasts, either hemizygote for EIF6 or carrying the N106S mutation in a heterozygous state, could be an ideal tool to further investigate whether these EIF6 mutations are beneficial during hematopoietic differentiation in the context of SDS.
Finally, compounds that compromise eIF6 interaction with the 60S subunit have been proposed to represent a promising therapeutic strategy in cancers and SDS (Roshan et al. , Pesce et al. ). However, the present findings indicate that the eIF6 N106S mutant affects proper rRNA biogenesis and polysome profile in both HD and SDS cells. This suggests that the dosage of such a drug should be used cautiously to reduce nucleolar stress-induced p53 pathway and improve cellular fitness without perturbing rRNA biogenesis.

## Limitations of the study
The primary objective of this study was to assess the impact of the eIF6 N106S mutant, endogenously expressed, in cells from both HD and SDS individuals. We therefore focused our analysis on cells expressing the N106S mutant in a homozygous state in order to avoid any potential confounding effects that may be exerted by the wild-type eIF6. As we were unable to obtain the homozygous EIF6 N106S mutation in the hTERT-immortalized primary cells, we had to use SV40-transformed cells as a substitute. Although the use of these cells provided unprecedented observations on the effect of the eIF6 N106S mutant, the large T antigen from SV40 perturbs the p53/Rb pathway. This may have limited the analysis of the impact of the eIF6 N106S mutation. In addition, the eIF6 N106S is found at a heterozygous state in the blood cells of individuals with SDS. We successfully obtained N106S heterozygous clones in SBDS hTERT-immortalized primary fibroblasts. However, we did not observe any positive effect of the mutation in these low-proliferating cells. Future analyses under stressful conditions (e.g., starvation, genotoxics) will be necessary to detect a possible positive effect of the eIF6 N106S mutation at a heterozygous state in SDS primary fibroblasts, as observed in SBDS blood cells. Despite these limitations, our study demonstrated the deleterious effect of the eIF6 N106S mutant on rRNA biogenesis, polysome production (with the presence of halfmers), and cell growth in both HD and SDS SV40-transformed fibroblasts. These results are of significance since therapeutic strategies targeting the eIF6-60S interaction have been proposed for individuals with SDS. The findings of this study suggest that these strategies should be approached with caution, as there is a possibility that they may result in significant adverse effects.
