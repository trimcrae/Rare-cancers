Through the secretion of key ECM proteins and cell adhesion molecules, MSCs regulate HSPC proliferation, differentiation, homing, retention, and maintain their quiescence necessary for effective hematopoiesis. Understanding the intricate dynamics between MSCs and HSPCs in the BM is essential for advancing HSPC-based therapies. Targeting niche components, such as ECM proteins and CAMs, can enable HSPC expansion and engraftment, benefiting hematopoietic stem cell transplantation and gene therapy outcomes. In this review, we provide an overview of the niche components involved in MSCs-HSPCs interactions and its co-culture in improving the therapeutic outcomes, discuss existing challenges and propose possible solutions.

## Introduction
Hematopoietic stem and progenitor cells (HSPCs), located in bone marrow are a heterogeneous hierarchy composed of distinct subpopulations with unique self-renewal and differentiation capacities [ ]. Long-term Hematopoietic stem cells (LT-HSCs) possess durable self-renewal and lifelong multilineage reconstitution potential, whereas short-term HSCs (ST-HSCs) exhibit limited self-renewal but robust short-term engraftment capacity [ ]. ST-HSCs give rise to multipotent progenitors (MPPs), which lack self-renewal but retain multilineage differentiation potential. Downstream, lineage-restricted progenitors emerge, including common myeloid progenitors (CMPs), which further generate granulocyte–macrophage progenitors (GMPs) and megakaryocyte–erythroid progenitors (MEPs), and common lymphoid progenitors (CLPs), which produce B, T, and NK cells [ , ]. Bone marrow microenvironment or niche provides a specialized environment that influences the behaviour of HSPCs, including their self-renewal, differentiation, and maintenance in a quiescent state. This microenvironment is crucial for ensuring the proper functioning of the hematopoietic system and preventing the exhaustion of stem cells [ , ]. HSPCs are the cornerstone of hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT), a widely practiced and highly successful cell therapy as a curative treatment for genetic blood disorders and haematological malignancies [ ]. Although HSCs are predominantly found in the BM and a small population circulate in peripheral blood (PB) in a pre-activated, lineage-primed state, which may not be ideal in terms of quantity and quality for HSCT. Hence, HSPCs are mobilized from the BM to PB using repeated granulocyte-colony stimulating factor (G-CSF) cytokine administrations, to activate G-CSF receptors in BM monocytes and macrophages. This activation induces the release of the proteolytic enzymes and reduces the retention of HSCs in the BM and by decreasing the retention molecules like CXCL12 (also known as stromal cell-derived factor 1 - SDF1a) and integrins. Alternatively, AMD3100 (Plerixafor), a CXCR4 receptor antagonist, disrupts SDF-1 interaction, rapidly mobilizing HSPCs into circulation for collection via apheresis [ ]. This method allows for the collection of large HSPC doses for both allogenic and autologous transplants.
The functional integrity of HSPCs is a decisive factor for successful HSCT outcomes. In monogenic haematological disorders, ex vivo expansion of HSPCs plays a pivotal role, especially after gene correction or modulation of functional protein expression. Maintaining HSPC stemness during ex vivo expansion has prompted the development of refined culture systems which includes the use of small molecules (e.g., UM171, resveratrol, StemRegenin), co-cultures with mesenchymal stromal cells (MSCs), and activation of the Notch signaling pathway to preserve their undifferentiated state [ – ]. Cord blood (CB) is a well-established alternative source of HSCs for transplantation offering benefits such as immediate availability, minimal donor risk, and greater tolerance for HLA mismatches [ , ]. However, its clinical application, particularly in adult recipients, is constrained by challenges like delayed engraftment, heightened infection risk, and limited cell doses. Ex vivo expansion technologies are instrumental in addressing these limitations by enabling a single CB unit to meet the higher cell dose demands required for successful adult transplantation [ ].
On another front, breakthroughs in genome engineering have revolutionized the field of autologous transplantation by enabling precise genetic modification of a patient’s own HSPCs, addressing limitations such as donor availability and graft-versus-host disease (GVHD). However, autologous HSPCs require ex vivo culture for manipulation and stress induced by gene manipulation protocols can impair HSPC functionality affecting the engraftment and repopulation potential in vivo [ ]. Prolonged ex vivo culture can downregulate cell adhesion molecules, reducing the engraftment potential, and enrich pro-inflammatory cytokines like IL-1β and IL-6 in the culture medium, decreasing functional activity [ , ]. Additionally, HSPC responses to gene manipulation processes, such as antiviral restriction factors and DNA damage responses, can reduce HSPC proliferation and increase apoptosis [ , ].
Towards improving the quality of the graft and to enhance the HSPC gene therapy (HSPC-GT) outcomes, multiple attempts have been made in understanding the BM niche components, cell types including MSC [ , ] and physiological state of the niche [ ] to faithfully recapitulate the complex BM microenvironment in vitro, thereby supporting more robust and functional HSPC expansion and modification. This review delves into the intricate interplay between niche components and HSPCs, highlighting the latest pre-clinical research efforts aimed at replicating niche conditions in vitro.

## The bone marrow niche: exploring HSPCs interactions with a focus on MSCs
Within the BM, the HSC niche consists of various cellular components, including stromal cells (such as MSCs, osteoblasts, and endothelial cells), immune cells (such as macrophages and lymphocytes), and other hematopoietic cells (Figure. ). These cells secrete a plethora of signalling molecules, growth factors, cytokines, and chemokines that create a complex microenvironment capable of regulating the equilibrium between the quiescence and activation followed by the HSC fate decisions. Alterations in normal functioning of the cells in the BM niche dysregulates the cross talk with HSPCs resulting in malignant transformation [ , ].
In BM niche, two distinct endothelial cells (ECs) (such as arterial and sinusoidal) play differential roles in regulating HSPC function. Arterial ECs (AECs) maintain HSPC quiescence and ROS levels, creating a protective microenvironment ideal for LT-HSCs. In contrast, sinusoidal ECs (SECs) are more permeable and support HSPC activation and trafficking, but their exposure to circulating plasma elevates ROS, compromising HSPC survival and repopulation capacity. Endothelial barrier integrity, controlled by FGF and CXCL12/CXCR4 signalling, is essential for HSPC retention and metabolic stability. Disruption of EC function leads to increased HSPC mobilization, apoptosis, and reduced regenerative potential, highlighting the dynamic role of ECs in both stem cell maintenance and clinical mobilization strategies [ ].

 

Within the BM niche, two distinct MSCs populations which includes Nestin-positive (Nestin⁺) and leptin receptor-positive (LepR⁺) MSCs play complementary roles in regulating HSPCs. Nestin⁺ MSCs, predominantly located in perivascular regions, are closely associated with sympathetic nerve fibers and are crucial for maintaining HSC quiescence and retention through the secretion of key factors such as CXCL12 and SCF. Conversely, LepR⁺ MSCs, which constitute a significant portion of the adult BM stromal population, are instrumental in supporting haematopoiesis contributing significantly to HSC homing, localization, and maintenance. They also contribute to bone formation and adipogenesis, particularly during stress responses and following chemotherapy-induced damage [ , ]. A comprehensive description of MSCs and their clinical relevance is provided in the subsequent sections of this review.
Osteoblast (OB), the bone-forming cells located at endosteum of the bone are essential components of the BM niche and play a critical role in the regulation of HSCs. They anchor HSPCs near the endosteal region and enhance hematopoiesis through secretion of regulatory cytokines and adhesion molecules, facilitating HSC homing and retention. OB number correlates with HSC population size—ablation leads to HSC reduction, while increased OBs boost HSC numbers. However, their exact role is complex, as not all OB-derived signals are uniquely essential, and their influence may depend on stress state, age, or HSC subtype [ – ].
Various literatures have shown that Megakaryocytes (MKs) help maintain HSCs quiescence in the BM by releasing signalling molecules like platelet factor 4 (PF4) and TGF-β1 [ , ]. However, in the absence of the proteoglycan serglycin (SRGN) higher levels of PF4 are released into the BM disrupting the BM microenvironment, leading to fewer functional HSCs and impaired hematopoiesis [ ].
The sympathetic nervous system plays a pivotal role in regulating HSPC dynamics within the BM niche, particularly by modulating osteoblast activity and the expression of CXCL12, a key chemokine involved in HSPC retention and mobilization. Additionally, sympathetic nerve fibers interact directly with MSCs, influencing the production of essential niche factors such as CXCL12, Stem Cell Factor (SCF), and vascular cell adhesion molecule-1 (VCAM-1), all of which are critical for HSPC maintenance [ ]. A recent study has uncovered a novel regulatory mechanism whereby dopamine, secreted by sympathetic nerves, promotes HSPC proliferation. This occurs through the upregulation of tyrosine-protein kinase Lck, which subsequently activates the mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) pathway, enhancing HSPC expansion [ ]. These findings underscore the complex and multifaceted role of sympathetic innervation in orchestrating HSPC fate and function within the BM microenvironment.

## Mesenchymal stromal cells
MSCs are multipotent stromal cells that can differentiate into various cell types and are an exceptionally rare cell population, comprising only 0.01% to 0.001% of the total cells in BM [ ]. Friedenstein and colleagues first identified MSCs in the early 1970s from BM [ ]. Other tissues where they have been found include adipose tissue (AT) [ ], umbilical cord (UC) [ ], Wharton’s Jelly (WJ) [ ], blood [ ] muscle [ ], cardiac [ ], spleen [ ], chorionic villi [ ], lung [ ], thymus [ ], pancreas [ ], and dental pulp [ ]. Based on tissue origin MSC were classified into adult tissue and fetal tissue sources. This broad tissue distribution allows for different sources of MSCs with potential applications in regenerative medicine. According to the International Society for Cellular Therapy (ISCT), MSCs derived from any of the sources should follow the following criteria to be defined as human MSCs: (1) adherent on plastic surface on in vitro culture; (2) positive for immunophenotypic markers including CD105, CD73, and CD90 and negative for CD45, CD34, CD14 or CD11b, CD79a or CD19, and HLA-DR; and (3) capable of differentiating into cells of the osteogenic (osteoblasts), adipogenic (adipocytes), and chondrogenic (chondroblasts) lineages under appropriate in vitro culture conditions [ , ].

## Extracellular matrix (ECM)
In the BM niche, ECM plays a crucial role in regulating niche architecture and cellular function and serves as a multifunctional scaffold, providing both biophysical support and signalling cues that initiate various aspects of stem cell activity [ ]. It is known that MSCs specifically express and interact with several different ECM proteins that contribute to the maintenance of the microenvironment in the BM niche pivotal for HSPC maintenance. Some of the key ECM proteins expressed or influenced by MSCs in the BM niche include
Fibronectin: MSCs are the major source of fibronectin in the BM, and it is a key component of the native BM ECM, playing a central role in regulating HSPC behaviour and niche dynamics. Fibronectin is a prominent extracellular element in decellularized BM bio-scaffolds utilized as inductive microenvironments for HSC expansion [ ]. When human BM MSCs were utilized to populate a porous hydroxyapatite scaffold, resulting in the formation of a biomimetic HSC niche, a significant accumulation of fibronectin was evident [ ]. Another study displayed the impact of direct contact between Wharton’s jelly mesenchymal stromal cells (WJ-MSCs) and umbilical cord blood-derived CD34+ cells (UCB-CD34+ cells) on expanding the CD34+ cell population. The short-term co-culture setting showed a rapid expansion of the various subtypes of HSPCs, indicating a broad influence on hematopoietic progenitor cells. The study highlights the importance of WJ-MSCs and UCB-CD34⁺ cells interaction in enhancing the proliferation and the functionality of the HSPCs cultured in vitro. Additionally, the use of a feeder layer of MSCs and fibronectin in the culture system provides insights into the microenvironment that supports the observed effects [ ].
Developmentally-regulated endothelial cell locus-1 (Del-1): In adult BM, MSCs significantly express Del-1 within the HSC niches [ ]. Del-1 has been identified as a regulator of LT-HSCs proliferation and influences the differentiation of hematopoietic progenitors toward myeloid lineages [ , ].
Collagens: MSCs produce various types of collagens that are involved in interactions with HSCs. In BM, not only osteoblasts express collagen type I but it is also expressed by BM stromal cells, including MSCs [ ]. Freshly isolated CD34+ HSPCs from UCB demonstrate that 20% of these cells express integrin α2β1, serving as a collagen type I receptor. When HSPCs are cultured on surfaces coated with collagen type I fibrils, proliferation is reduced resulting in enhanced retention of stemness. Similarly, the differentiation of HSPCs to MKs and erythroid lineages were altered. These findings suggest that collagen type I may play a role in inducing quiescence of HSCs within the endosteal niche [ ]. The utilization of the growth factor G-CSF for progenitor cell mobilization is well-established. It has been noted that G-CSF reduces the expression of mRNA for collagen type VI, leading to a decrease in collagen VI secretion and accumulation, particularly mediated by MSCs. Additionally, these observations suggest that the interaction between HSPCs and their niche is partly shaped by the presence of collagen type VI in this microenvironment, with G-CSF also affecting the expression of specific ECM component [ ]. Therefore, the HSPCs interaction with their niche may be partially influenced by the presence of collagen type VI in the microenvironment, as well as its regulation by G-CSF.
Components of the ECM within the HSC niche, including syndecan-3, syndecan-4, and glypican-1, are expressed on the surface of marrow stromal cells. It has been suggested that these Heparan Sulfate Proteoglycans (HSPGs), along with perlecan, play crucial roles in the formation and maintenance of the HSC niche [ ].

Hyaluronic acid (HA) is a non-sulfated linear Glycosaminoglycan (GAG) found in the ECM of various tissues. Unlike other GAGs such as chondroitin sulfate or heparan sulfate, HA is not bound to a core protein, making it unique in its structure and function. The basic repeating unit of HA consists of two sugar molecules: D-glucuronic acid and N-acetylglucosamine. These disaccharide units are linked together in a linear fashion, forming long chains of HA. Its interacts with cell surface receptors and other ECM molecules, influencing cellular behaviour and function [ ]. In the HSC niche, HA interacts with various receptors, among which CD44 and RHAMM (Receptor for Hyaluronan-Mediated Motility) are particularly notable. CD44 plays crucial roles in HSC anchoring, migration, and homing, particularly through its interactions with HA and SDF-1α [ ]. RHAMM is an important player in the process of HSPC mobilization and trafficking [ ]. HA Synthase (HAS) is expressed in both mouse and human BM-MSC. MSCs are known for their ability to support HSCs by providing a supportive microenvironment within the BM niche [ ]. When HAS2 expression is disrupted, it can affect the production and organization of hyaluronan within the MSC niche, thereby compromising the ability of MSCs to support hematopoiesis. This impairment can manifest as decreased proliferation or differentiation of HSCs and ultimately impact the overall functionality of the hematopoietic system [ ]. Altogether, these results indicate that MSC-secreted HA serves as an essential mediator of hematopoietic homeostasis within the BM niche.

## Laminin
The supernatant from MSCs contains elevated levels of laminin [ ]. In the BM, α5‑chain–containing laminin isoforms (LM‑511 and LM‑521) produced by MSCs, vascular, and endothelial niche cells promote adhesion of CD34⁺ HSPCs via integrin α7β1 and BCAM/Lu, suppress proliferation, and regulate migration and lineage differentiation [ ]. Loss of laminin α4 in mice leads to reduced HSPC cycling, impaired homing, and diminished competitive repopulation and colony‑forming potential. These defects are partially compensated by upregulated expression of α5‑containing laminin isoforms (laminin‑521/522), which helps mitigate the hematopoietic deficiencies [ ]. Both laminin‑8 and laminin‑10/11 enhanced SDF‑1α‑stimulated transmigration of CD34⁺ cells through an integrin α6‑dependent mechanism, underscoring isoform‑specific adhesive interactions that regulate hematopoietic migration [ ]. Siler et al. demonstrated that laminin γ2 is expressed exclusively in vivo by BM stromal cells but not by ECs or MKs. They further showed that the laminin‑5 isoform (α3β3γ2) acts as an adhesive substrate for a specific subset of bone marrow mononuclear cells and peripheral blood platelets, indicating that γ2‑containing laminins can mediate adhesion of human hematopoietic cells [ ]. These findings establish MSC-derived laminins as critical regulators of hematopoietic homeostasis, orchestrating adhesion, migration, quiescence, and differentiation of HSPCs within the BM niche.

## Tenascin-C
Tenascin-C(TNC) is a glycoprotein highly expressed in the healthy BM microenvironment, in compared to other tenascin family members such as tenascin-R, -W, and -X [ ]. It has a direct functional role in regulating the proliferation and differentiation of hematopoietic cells within the BM niche [ ]. In the BM niche, expression of the TNC gene is mainly found in stromal and ECs [ ]. While TNC⁻/⁻ mice show normal blood cell production under steady-state conditions, they fail to recover from BM ablation and exhibit high mortality. These mice also have a reduced ability to support hematopoietic recovery from transplanted wild-type cells. In vitro, TNC promotes HSPC proliferation through integrin α9–mediated signalling, which increases cyclin D1 and E1 expression and decreases cell cycle inhibitors like p57^Kip2, p21^Cip1, and p16^Ink4a, facilitating cell cycle progression [ ]. TNC plays a crucial role in stress-induced hematopoiesis. TNC initiates hematopoietic differentiation of human pluripotent stem cells (hPSCs) and directs their commitment toward specific lineages, with a particularly pronounced effect on the development of T‑lymphoid progenitors [ ]. In TNC mutant mice does not affect the size of the bone marrow HSC pool [ ] but is essential for anchoring thymic T cell progenitors—its absence leads to redistribution into other lymphoid tissues and altered homing of lymphoid‑primed progenitors via the integrin α9β1 receptor. Additionally, lack of TNC impairs hematopoietic reconstitution after transplant, increases progenitor mobilization, and elevates circulating mature T cells [ ].

## Periostin
In the BM microenvironment, periostin (POSTN) is secreted by BM-MSCs [ , ]. POSTN regulates HSC behaviour by binding to integrin αv (Itgav) on HSCs, initiating outside-in signalling. This POSTN–Itgav interaction suppresses the FAK/PI3K/AKT pathway and upregulates p27^Kip1 expression in HSCs, promoting quiescence and stemness. Through this signaling axis, periostin maintains genomic stability and supports the long-term functional integrity of HSCs. Disruption of POSTN expression or integrin αv signalling leads to increased HSC proliferation, accumulation of DNA damage, and a decline in HSC fitness and regenerative potential [ ]. POSTN promotes progenitor cell proliferation, LTC‑IC maintenance, and stromal niche function via integrin αvβ3 and downstream FAK/ERK signalling, operating in an autocrine/paracrine manner. It enhances support for both normal hematopoietic progenitor cells and leukemic progenitors [ ].

## Osteopontin
BMSCs produce osteopontin (OPN), which plays a critical role in the retention and maintenance of HSCs. OPN also acts as a physiological negative regulator of HSC proliferation [ ]. Thrombin-cleaved osteopontin (trOPN), a major form of OPN, functions as an important inhibitory regulator of fetal BM HSPCs through interactions with α4β1 and α9β1 integrins [ , ]. OPN produced by MSCs within the BM niche serves as an important regulatory signal for HSPCs [ ]. OPN plays a crucial role in the maintenance of HSPCs during the aging process. In aged mice, OPN deficiency results in anemia, thrombocytopenia, altered lineage output, and impaired differentiation into multipotent progenitors. This loss of regenerative potential is primarily linked to reduced quiescence and insufficient maturation of HSCs [ ]. The study found that aging reduces OPN expression in BM-MSCs and other stromal cells, leading to impaired engraftment, altered lineage balance, and loss of polarity of HSPCs. Exposure of young HSPCs to an OPN-deficient niche produced similar effects, whereas treatment of aged murine HSPCs with thrombin-cleaved OPN reversed several age-related defects [ ].

## Fibrillin
Fibrillin-1, an ECM protein deposited by MSCs [ ]. In Fbn1-deficient mice, loss of fibrillin-1 resulted in a reduced HSC frequency compared with wild-type controls and enhanced erythropoiesis, evidenced by increased circulating red blood cells. Mechanistically, fibrillin-1 modulates local TGFβ signaling in a niche-specific manner: regulating HSC expansion in the stem cell niche while restraining erythroid progenitor maturation in the erythroblastic niche [ ]. These results indicate that fibrillin-1 functions as a regulator of the BM niche, influencing hematopoiesis.

## Osteonectin
MSCs in the BM secrete osteonectin, a matricellular glycoprotein also known as SPARC (secreted protein acidic and rich in cysteine). It’s mainly produced by OBs and ECs but forms part of the MSC-secreted support network in the niche [ ]. SPARC-deficient mice showed reduced erythroid progenitors (BFU-E, CFU-S8) and lower hemoglobin, indicating impaired erythropoiesis driven by microenvironmental defects. Exogenous SPARC restored BFU-E formation in SPARC⁻/⁻ BM cells and promoted erythroid colony formation from human cord blood CD34⁺ cells [ ]. Beyond erythropoiesis, SPARC deficiency alters the BM microenvironment, leading to reduced primitive HSPCs (LSK cells) and impaired B-lymphopoiesis, showing its role in niche support [ ]. Under stress conditions, such as chemotherapy (5-FU), SPARC-deficient niches accelerated HSC return to quiescence, protecting them from exhaustion [ ]. Together, these findings establish MSC-derived ECM components as pivotal regulators of hematopoietic homeostasis, orchestrating progenitor expansion, lineage commitment, and stress responses within the BM niche.

## Cell adhesion molecules (CAM)
MSCs express CAMs that interact with HSPCs within the BM niche (Fig. ). These interactions are crucial for regulating HSPCs behaviour, including homing, proliferation, differentiation, and retention within the BM microenvironment [ ]. CAMs expressed by MSCs that interact with HSPCs include N-Cadherin, Immunoglobin like adhesion molecules such as Intercellular Adhesion Molecule-1(ICAM-1) and Vascular Cell Adhesion Molecule-1(VCAM), and Junctional Adhesion Molecules (JAMs).
 Integrins related to HSPCs were summarized based on their surface expression. Human mobilised peripheral blood (mPB) CD34+ cells show a mixture of adherent and supernatant cell populations when cultured on decullarized ECM in the same way as HSPCs co-cultures with MSC. A polarization of CXCR4 towards ECM proteins was observed in adherent cells. They found that adherent cells express ITGαIIb, ITGαV, and ITGβ3 on their surfaces, and this suggests an important role for ITG αvβ3 in adhesion and migration between HSPC and ECM [ ]. 
ICAM belongs to the immunoglobulin superfamily of adhesion proteins. These proteins share a common structural feature characterized by repeating domains that resemble immunoglobulin-like structures (ICAM1). When ICAM-1 binds to its ligand, the β2 integrin known as lymphocyte function-associated antigen-1 (LFA-1), it facilitates cellular crosstalk. This interaction plays a significant role in mediating communication among various immune cells [ ]. ICAM-1 is expressed across various cell types within the BM environment, including endothelial cells (ECs), MSCs, osteoblasts, and CXCL12-abundant reticular cells (CARs). Moreover, the study highlights that ICAM-1 deficiency within the BM niche disrupts the quiescence and repopulation capacity of HSCs. Specifically, HSCs in ICAM-1 deficient mice exhibit heightened proliferation and struggle to maintain a dormant state. Furthermore, the absence of ICAM-1 leads to a failure in retaining HSCs within the BM, accompanied by alterations in the expression profile of stromal cell-derived factors. These findings collectively underscore the regulatory role of ICAM-1 within the BM microenvironment [ ]. Overall, MSCs also express ICAM-1, which can interact with its receptor LFA-1 on HSPCs. Through ICAM-1/LFA-1 interactions, MSCs facilitate the adhesion and migration of HSPCs within the BM niche. These interactions play a pivotal role in regulating HSPCs localization and function, contributing to the maintenance of hematopoiesis and tissue homeostasis within the BM microenvironment.
VCAM: Previous literature findings have indicated that the use of antibodies targeting VCAM-1 highlights the pivotal involvement of the α4β1/VCAM-1 axis in the retention of HSPCs within the BM [ ]. VCAM-1 is prominently expressed on ECs as well as MSCs that are positive for the leptin receptor (LepR+) [ ]. It has been reported that G-CSF lowers the expression of VCAM1 in BM stromal cells. G-CSF modulates the BM microenvironment by reducing VCAM1 expression on MSCs and their retention capacity, thereby resulting in HSPC mobilization. This suggests that enzymatic cleavage of VCAM1 on these stromal cells may remove a critical retention ligand for HSPCs, which express Very Late Antigen-4 (VLA4 also term as α4β1) [ – ].The study underscores the crucial interplay between membrane-bound stem cell factor (mSCF) which play essential role for maintains of long-term HSC and VCAM-1 guiding the homing and retention of HSCs in the BM niche. This interaction leads to a polarized morphology in HSCs, enhancing adhesion and facilitating mSCF/cKit recruitment, primarily mediated by PI3K signalling and cytoskeletal reorganization. While VCAM-1 contributes to increasing adhesion area and baseline adhesive strength, its main role lies in supporting mSCF-induced polarization and up-regulating adhesion strength in conjunction with mSCF [ ]. VCAM-1 is expressed by MSCs within the HSPC niche. VCAM-1 on MSCs interacts with its receptor VLA-4, which is present on the surface of HSPCs. This interaction facilitates the adhesion of HSPCs to the MSCs and is essential for processes such as HSPCs homing to the BM niche and regulation of HSPCs function within the microenvironment. Previous studies have shown that blocking the interaction between VCAM-1 and its receptor VLA-4 with antibodies is enough to enhance mobilization [ – ].

## Melanoma cell adhesion molecule (MCAM)
MSCs express MCAM (CD146). MSCs expressing CD146+ cells support transplantable human hematopoietic stem cell potential in culture and secreting hematopoietic regulatory genes [ , ] whereas not CD146− cells [ ]. In perivascular regions of BM various MSC subtypes are interact with HSPCs. CD146−/low MSCs are found near bone-lining cells in low oxygen tension area where they are closely linked to LT-HSPCs. In contrast, CD146⁺ MSCs are predominantly located around sinusoids, where they are associated with proliferating HSPCs [ ]. Thus, these findings confirm the MCAM expressing MSCs can forms a BM niche for supporting hematopoietic activity.
N-Cadherin: MSCs express N-Cadherin, a cell adhesion molecule that interacts with Cadherin molecules present on the surface of HSPCs. Zhao et al.. illustrate that N-cadherin-positive (N-cad+) cells, originating from the endosteal region, serve as the primary source of BM stromal progenitor cells (BMSPCs, also known as MSCs) exhibiting colony forming unit fibroblast (CFU-F) activity. Their study reveals that N-cad+ BMSPCs are pivotal in osteoblast generation and crucial for maintaining reserve HSCs, as evidenced by experiments involving N-cad + cell depletion followed by BM transplantation. Furthermore, the genetic deletion of Cxcl12 or SCF from N-cad+ cells disrupt HSC homeostasis, indicating that N-cad+ stromal cells support HSCs by providing SCF, particularly during chemotherapeutic stress [ ]. Cell adhesion mediated by N-cadherin is crucial for establishing hematopoiesis after BM transplantation (BMT) and plays a role in HSPCs engraftment. Overexpressing wild-type N-cadherin in HSCs promoted quiescence and preserved HSC activity, indicating that cadherin-mediated adhesion is linked to quiescence and long-term self-renewal. Inhibiting N-cadherin by transducing short hairpin RNA (shRNA) targeting N-cad transcripts reduced the allocation of donor HSCs to the endosteal surface, significantly decreasing long-term engraftment. N-cadherin expression in HSCs is specifically necessary in the BM, as it is maintained in the spleen for up to six months following BM [ ]. Cadherins are glycoproteins that mediate cell-cell adhesion through a homophilic binding mechanism. Cadherin-N is expressed in human MSCs and HPCs, and it is found at the junction between these cells. N-cadherin plays a crucial role in the adhesion of HPCs to MSCs and supports the maintenance of long-term culture-initiating cells (LTC-IC) [ ]. Co-culture with MSCs preserved a primitive immunophenotype (CD34⁺, CD133⁺, CD38⁻) in hematopoietic progenitor cells (HPCs) for more population doublings and delayed the up-regulation of differentiation markers (CD13, CD45, CD56). Early passage MSCs maintained CD34 expression in HPCs over more divisions and enhanced their proliferation rate compared to late passage MSCs. Knocking down N-cadherin and VCAM1 in MSCs increased the fraction of slow-dividing HPCs, while knockdown of integrin beta 1(ITGB1) and CD44 impaired HPC differentiation. N-cadherin facilitates the adhesion of HPCs to MSCs and supports the maintenance of LTC-ICs [ ]. Through N-Cadherin-mediated adhesion, MSCs establish physical connections with HSPCs, contributing to the maintenance of HSPC niche integrity within the BM microenvironment. This interaction plays a crucial role in regulating the localization and distribution of HSPCs within the BM, thereby influencing hematopoietic stem cell functions such as self-renewal, differentiation, and mobilization. N-Cadherin-mediated adhesion between MSCs and HSPCs is essential for the proper functioning of the BM niche and the regulation of haematopoiesis.
JAMs: JAMs are proteins with a structure-resembling immunoglobulins (Ig), characterized by two extracellular Ig domains. They also feature a brief cytoplasmic tail and a motif that binds to PDZ domains [ ]. Specifically, JAM-B and JAM-C, engage in heterophilic interactions at cell-cell contacts. JAM-B plays a crucial role in organizing and maintaining the structure of interendothelial junctions, with JAM-C being dependent on JAM-B for its localization within these complexes [ ]. HSC express the JAM-C and the level of its expression is closely associated with the stemness of HSCs as well as their ability to mobilize [ ]. The expression of Jam-b is observed on BM-MSCs, and HSCs adhere to JAM-B through JAM-C dependent manner. Mice lacking Jam-B show a deficiency in regulating the hematopoietic stem cell population [ ]. In the human hematopoietic system, a functional adhesive interaction is observed between JAM-B and JAM-C on human HSPCs and MSCs. However, this interaction does not occur between HSPCs and human ECs or OBs. JAM-C Contributes to human HSPCs homing to the BM and is also implicated in the adhesion of HSPCs to MSCs. Inhibition of Jam-b/Jam-c interaction induces mobilization of HSPCs and reduces homing of human and mouse progenitors into BM [ ].
CAM components expressed by MSCs such as N-cadherin, ICAM-1, VCAM-1, MCAM, and JAMs play indispensable roles in anchoring HSPCs within the BM niche. Through these adhesive interactions, MSCs regulate HSPC homing, retention, quiescence, and lineage commitment. Disruption of CAM-mediated signalling impairs HSPC localization, mobilization, and long-term engraftment, underscoring their central role in niche integrity. Collectively, MSC-derived CAMs represent key regulators of hematopoietic homeostasis, linking structural adhesion to functional support of stem cell fate.

## Advancements in HSCT and gene therapy for haematological disorders
HSCT extends beyond hematopoietic malignancies and plays a vital role in treating various non-hematopoietic disorders, particularly Inborn errors of metabolism (IEM), autoimmune diseases, neurodegenerative conditions and BM failure (BMF) [ – ]. IEM are a distinct group of diseases, primarily caused by single-gene mutations that lead to defects in metabolic pathways [ ]. Treatment approaches for IEM include enzyme replacement therapy (ERT) and HSCT. However, HSCT offers a long-lasting therapeutic effect in IEM through a mechanism known as cross-correction, where donor derived myeloid cells secrete the deficient enzymes, thereby compensating for the metabolic defect. HSPCs can cross the blood-brain barrier (BBB) and reach the central nervous system (CNS), where they differentiate into resident macrophages(microglia). These cells play a role like native microglia, secreting enzymes and contributing to neuronal recovery [ ]. However, in certain conditions, this process alone may not be sufficient to fully correct the disease. HSC-GT also represents a promising curative approach for these diseases, providing a solution to overcome the limitations of traditional HSC [ , ].
The success of HSCT and HSC-GT is intricately linked to the integrity of the BM microenvironment. In hemoglobinopathies such as sickle cell disease (SCD), the BM niche is often compromised. Studies have shown that while BM-MSCs from SCD patients exhibit comparable growth kinetics and immunophenotypes to those from healthy donors [ ], murine models reveal altered gene expression profiles, diminished stem cell properties, and heightened oxidative stress in MSCs, partly due to chronic haemolysis. These dysfunctional MSCs exhibit reduced capacity to support HSC maintenance, leading to impaired engraftment and increased HSC mobilization. Moreover, activation of Toll-like receptor-4 signaling pathways exacerbates MSC dysfunction. Interestingly, interventions such as transfusions have demonstrated potential in ameliorating oxidative stress within MSCs, thereby enhancing their supportive role for HSCs [ ]. Addressing the BM niche abnormalities is thus crucial for optimizing HSCT outcomes in SCD and similar disorders.
Efficient mobilization of HSPCs is a critical prerequisite for successful HSCT and GT. Conventional mobilizing agents such as granulocyte-colony stimulating factor (G-CSF) and plerixafor are commonly used. However, their limited efficacy and adverse effects particularly the risk of vaso-occlusive crises in SCD patients pose significant clinical challenges. To address these limitations, emerging strategies aim to enhance mobilization efficiency while minimizing toxicity [ ]. A promising approach involves the synergistic use of CXCR2 agonists and VLA-4 inhibitors, which rapidly promote HSPC egress from the BM into the peripheral blood. Notably, the combination of WU-106, a VLA-4 inhibitor, with plerixafor has shown robust and rapid mobilization of HSPCs in preclinical SCD models, enabling efficient gene correction and phenotypic rescue [ ]. Importantly, these advances underscore the need for a deeper understanding of HSPC–BM niche interactions, as unravelling the molecular mechanisms governing stem cell retention and release will be key to developing safer, more effective mobilization regimens tailored for diverse patient populations, including those in resource-constrained settings.

## Therapeutic applications of MSCs
Given their remarkable multipotency and immunomodulatory capabilities, BM-MSCs have been extensively investigated in clinical trials for the treatment of a wide range of diseases. Their low immunogenic potential characterized by minimal expression of major histocompatibility complex (MHC) class I and an absence of MHC class II makes BM-MSCs a versatile graft for both allogeneic and autologous transplantation. BM-MSCs exert their therapeutic effects primarily through paracrine signalling, mediating anti-inflammatory and wound-healing responses by secreting a variety of cytokines. Additionally, they release extracellular vesicles that further contribute to tissue repair and immunomodulation. Phase III trial (NCT04738981) evaluated the efficacy and safety of combining umbilical cord MSCs with basiliximab as a second-line therapy for steroid-refractory acute graft-versus-host disease (SR-aGVHD) in adult recipients of allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplants, including those from haploidentical donors (HID HSCT). Eligible participants (aged 18–70) had persistent or progressive aGVHD despite high-dose steroids. The intervention arm received MSCs alongside standard basiliximab, while controls received basiliximab alone. The study demonstrated significantly higher 4-week complete response (CR) rates in the MSC plus basiliximab group, with no increase in serious toxicity, infection, or relapse, and an associated survival benefit. This trial provides compelling evidence for the efficacy and safety of MSC therapy in SR-aGVHD, particularly in the context of haploidentical transplantation, and underscores its potential role in clinical practice [ ]. Table highlights a selection of completed clinical trials registered on ClinicalTrials.gov under the search term “MSCs.” Notably, Ryoncil (remestemcel-L) has emerged as a milestone in the field, being the first MSC-based therapy to receive FDA approval. It also represents the first off-the-shelf allogeneic cell therapy developed specifically for the treatment of steroid-refractory GVHD [ ].

 

Given that BM-MSCs and HSPCs are in close proximity within the BM niche, and that isolation and characterization protocols for BM-MSCs are highly refined compared to other cellular components, numerous studies have leveraged BM-MSCs to enhance the viability and functionality of HSPCs in vitro. This approach has proven valuable in both autologous and allogeneic transplantation contexts, where optimizing HSPC fitness is crucial for successful outcomes.

## Hypoxia-mediated MSC–HSPC interactions
Beyond the cellular constituents, BM microenvironment is characterized by a heterogeneous distribution of oxygen levels, creating hypoxic niches that are crucial for the maintenance and function of HSPCs. The oxygen levels in the BM microenvironment range from 1% to 6% [ ]. This localized hypoxia is crucial for the hematopoietic microenvironment that regulates the development of hematopoietic cells [ ]. Within the BM niche, the most dormant and primitive HSCs reside in unique areas with very low blood perfusion due to negligible blood flow [ ]. HSCs survival, quiescence, self-renewal and multi lineage differentiation are predominantly influenced by the reactive oxygen species (ROS) and BM hypoxic environment prevent the quiescent HSCs to exhaust by limiting the supply of molecular oxygen to the BM niche cells. HSCs rely on a delicate balance to maintain their quiescence, self-renewal, survival, and differentiation capabilities. ROS produced via mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation can disrupt these properties, leading to apoptosis and loss of HSC function. The hypoxic BM microenvironment mitigates oxidative stress by limiting oxygen availability and stabilizing HIF-1α, which shifts HSC metabolism toward glycolysis, reducing mitochondrial ROS production. Key enzymes like pyruvate dehydrogenase kinases (Pdks) play a vital role in this metabolic shift, with their expression regulated by HIF-1α. Experimental models show that HIF-dependent signals are essential for HSC survival and maintenance, highlighting the critical role of hypoxia in preserving HSC properties through metabolic reprogramming [ ].
Quiescent and primitive HSCs predominantly reside in the endosteal niche, a microenvironment characterized by low oxygen tension. To better mimic these physiological conditions, several research groups have attempted to culture HSPCs in vitro under hypoxic conditions (ranging from 1% to 5% O₂), rather than the standard 21% O₂ typically maintained in conventional incubators. An interesting study have shown that HSCs possess the functionality even with the complete absence of O2. Culturing highly primitive HSCs under 3% O₂ for five days preserved the expression of primitive markers at levels comparable to uncultured HSPCs. Interestingly, when cells were cultured under anoxic conditions (0% O₂), they observed an increased frequency of SCID-repopulating cells (SRCs), and the HSCs survived by relying on anaerobic mitochondrial respiration. These findings indicate that primitive HSCs utilize fumarate respiration, a metabolic strategy reminiscent of cancer stem cells, allowing them to thrive and maintain stemness in hypoxic and anoxic microenvironments [ ]. To mimic the natural BM niche ex vivo, niche microenvironments are recreated by simulating tissue-specific oxygen levels and using feeder cells such as MSCs. Under co-culture conditions, adipose-derived stromal cells (ASCs), a sub-type of MSC with similar developmental origin compared to BM-MSCs demonstrated a robust capacity to expand cord blood cells (CBCs) at both normoxic (20% O₂) and hypoxic (5% O₂) oxygen levels. Notably, the number of colony-forming cells (CFCs) was 1.6-fold higher under 5% O₂ compared to 20% O₂. This enhancement was primarily attributed to an increased proliferation of multipotent progenitor cells, highlighting the supportive role of ASCs in promoting hematopoietic expansion under physiologically relevant oxygen conditions [ ]. A recent study demonstrated that culturing cord blood-derived hematopoietic stem and progenitor cells (CB-HSPCs) with WJ-MSCs under hypoxic conditions (1% O₂) resulted in a significantly higher proportion of CD34⁺Lin⁻ cells. This low oxygen environment markedly enhanced the secretion of vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), while concurrently suppressing the release of key hematopoietic cytokines—IL-6, interleukin-7 (IL-7), SCF, and thrombopoietin (TPO)—from WJ-MSCs, all of which are typically involved in promoting HSPC proliferation. Interestingly, this cytokine modulation, along with the hypoxia-induced activation of critical developmental signaling pathways such as Notch, Wnt/β-catenin, and Hedgehog in CB-HSPCs, collectively contributed to the preservation of stemness and the maintenance of HSPC quiescence [ ]. Similarly, it was observed that in growth-arrested ASCs, hypoxic conditions induced transcription of genes related to cell-cell adhesion molecules (ICAM1, HCAM/CD44), cell-matrix adhesion molecules (ITGs), ECM production (COLs) and remodelling (MMPs, HAS1). The alteration contributed to the formation of the highest level of cobble-stone area forming cells (CAFC - considered to be long-term-initiating cells) during physiological hypoxia [ ]. A similar study showed that co-culturing cord blood CD34+ cells with MSCs at low oxygen levels maintains HSCs compared to traditional culture methods. The activity of SCID repopulating cells was higher on co-culture at 1.5% oxygen compared to freshly isolated CD34+ cells and this is due to the MSCs which produce interleukin-6 at low oxygen levels contributing to this effect [ ]. All these studies underscore the critical role of physiologically relevant microenvironments in optimizing ex vivo HSPC expansion and maintenance for therapeutic applications.

## MSC derived EVs for HSPC maintenance
In addition to supporting HSPCs through direct contact, MSCs also influence HSPC fate through the release of extracellular vesicles (EVs). These MSC-derived EVs play a critical role in determining the fate of HSPCs. Numerous studies have documented the interactions between MSC-derived EVs and HSCs, highlighting the importance of these vesicles in modulating HSC behaviour, including their proliferation, differentiation, and overall maintenance. The available information on the biological effects of MSC-EVs to improve hematopoietic function and expansion in preclinical models, with a focus on the possible mechanisms of these effects, was reviewed in the already published article [ – ]. EVs derived from BMSCs, from both fetal and adult sources, have shown a strong ability to support the ex vivo expansion of UCB-HSPCs. Detailed analyses have revealed that these EVs are enriched with important regulatory molecules, including the microRNA cluster miR-99b/let-7e/miR-125a and components of the TGF-β signaling pathway. These factors play key roles in maintaining stemness, enhancing self-renewal, and preventing differentiation of HSPCs [ ]. Exosomes isolated from placental MSCs increased the absolute number of CB derived CD34+CD38−Lin− HSCs and generated higher number of total CFUs [ ]. Another interesting study showed that the MSC-EVs derived from young donors with a median age of 22 years promoted HSPCs expansion, along with upregulating genes involved in cell growth and division compared to those derived from aged donors with a median age of 70 years [ ]. Recent research has unveiled a pivotal role for MSC-derived extracellular vesicles (MSC-EVs) in orchestrating hematopoietic dynamics within the BM. These vesicles, through paracrine signalling, disrupt the quiescent state of HSPCs, steering them toward a myeloid lineage commitment. Central to this process is the activation of the Toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4) pathway. Upon engagement by MSC-EVs, TLR4 recruits the adaptor protein MyD88, initiating a cascade that activates the NF-κB signalling pathway. This activation leads to the upregulation of genes such as Hif-1α and CCL2, which are instrumental in promoting inflammation and cellular proliferation. Consequently, there’s a notable expansion of myeloid progenitors. However, inhibition of TLR4 significantly diminishes this expansion, underscoring the critical nature of this signalling axis [ ]. Deniz et al. uncovered that MSCs generate distinctive extracellular structures known as migrasomes. These recently identified vesicles were formed at the tips of retraction fibers left behind by the migrating MSCs and exhibits a unique molecular signature, expressing classical MSC markers (CD44, CD73, CD90, CD105, and CD166), the late endosomal GTPase Rab7, the exosomal marker CD63, and the chemokine SDF-1. Functionally, co-culture experiments with HSPCs revealed that MSC-derived migrasomes have a chemotactic effect and this recruitment was significantly impaired by either a CXCR4 antagonist or the presence of recombinant SDF-1, underscoring the central role of the SDF-1/CXCR4 axis in this interaction. Furthermore, HSPCs were capable of internalizing migrasomes, suggesting a mechanism for direct molecular exchange. This study sheds light on a novel mechanism through which MSCs may influence the hematopoietic niche, expanding our understanding of extracellular vesicle-mediated communication in stem cell biology [ ]. Altogether, these studies underscore that MSC-EVs and newly described migrasomes serve as potent regulators of HSPC fate within the BM niche. By transferring microRNAs, cytokines, and signalling mediators, MSC-EVs can preserve stemness, enhance self-renewal, and direct lineage specification. These findings highlight MSC-EVs as emerging players in the design of next-generation strategies for ex vivo HSPC expansion and therapeutic modulation of hematopoiesis.

## MSCs rescuing haematopoiesis in acute radiation syndrome (ARS)
Acute radiation syndrome (ARS) is a life-threatening condition resulting from high-dose ionizing radiation exposure over a short duration, causing profound injury to rapidly proliferating cells. Among the affected systems, the hematopoietic compartment is particularly vulnerable, and several studies have demonstrated that intravenous infusion of MSCs post-exposure can significantly mitigate radiation-induced damage and promote recovery (Fig. ). S. Shim et al. demonstrated that human UCB-derived MSCs(hUCB-MSCs) significantly enhance recovery following total body irradiation, outperforming granulocyte colony-stimulating factor (G-CSF). Treatment with hUCB-MSCs led to a marked increase in leukocyte counts, robust regeneration of white blood cells, and modulation of key hematopoietic cytokines, including Flt-3 ligand (Flt-3L) and transforming growth factor-beta 1 (TGF-β1) [ ]. MSCs have been shown to confer radioprotective effects on HSPCs by increasing the population of CD34⁺CD38⁻ cells, reducing DNA damage, and inhibiting apoptosis. Following intravenous administration, MSCs rapidly home to the BM, where they mitigate cellularity loss and alleviate pancytopenia in irradiated mice. This protective effect is largely attributed to Jagged1-mediated activation of the Notch signalling pathway, which enhances HSPC self-renewal and upregulates anti-apoptotic gene expression, underscoring the pivotal role of Notch pathway activation in facilitating hematopoietic recovery after radiation injury [ ]. In contrary, a recent study showed no signs of amelioration of radiation-induced hematopoietic syndrome despite infusing MSCs 3–30 h post irradiation, resulting in pancytopenia [ ]. Studies have demonstrated the efficacy of EVs derived from marrow MSCs counteracting radiation-induced BM injury by stimulating hematopoietic cells proliferation and alleviate DNA damage and apoptosis. EVs act as paracrine mediators in repairing injured target cells, bypassing the need for engraftment or repopulation [ ]. Monocytes treated with exosomes derived from MSCs primed with LPS demonstrated therapeutic effects within 24 h of irradiation resulting in 80% of survival mice for over 40 days. This is mediated by the production of interleukin-6 which are involved in decreasing the inflammatory responses [ ].
 Pre-conditioning process of HSCT with permanent Total body irradiation (TBI) impairs BM stroma, hindering HSC reconstitution. This impairment can be reversed by intra-bone transplantation of freshly isolated multipotent BM stromal stem cells. Surprisingly, this rescue effect is not observed with cultured stromal cells. Co-transplantation of HSCs with primary BMSCs enhances donor HSCs expansion and mitigates transplant related side effects [ ]. All these studies shows that MSCs and their EVs offer a promising therapeutic strategy for radiation-induced hematopoietic injury.

## Genetically modified MSCs (GM-MSCs) as hematopoietic niche for HSPCs
The self-renewal and differentiation of HSPCs are tightly regulated by BM niches in vivo. However, once isolated and cultured ex vivo, they are removed from this supportive microenvironment, leading to a loss of essential regulatory signals required for their maintenance. Recent studies have demonstrated that GM-MSCs are gaining recognition as a promising tool for supporting the expansion and maintenance of HSPCs (Fig. ). MSCs can modified to overexpress specific growth factors, cytokines, or ECM proteins. Consequently, this can be utilised to create an optimized microenvironment that mimics the natural BM niche in vitro condition. In this context, an interesting study identified five niche factors (Klf7, Ostf1, Xbp1, Irf3, and Irf7 collectively referred to as KOXII) that are critical for HSPC maintenance. When these genes were transduced into MSCs, resulting in revitalized MSCs (rMSCs) capable of enhancing the BM niche activity for ex vivo expansion. When rMSCs were used in co-culture conditions, they achieved a 1.7-fold increase in HSPC expansion ex vivo without affecting the differentiation potential of the MSCs. rMSCs restored HSC niche function during co-culture, demonstrated higher repopulation capacity, and protected lethally irradiated recipient mice in vivo. Additionally, rMSCs prevented DNA damage accumulation in the in vitro expansion of HSCs, a common issue associated with aging and replication stress. Competitive transplantation assays showed approximately a six-fold increase in functional HSC expansion in the rMSC group and the reconstitution capacity was also retained in secondary transplantation. MSCs act through a common downstream effector of myocyte enhancer factor 2c (Mef2c) which are enriched in rMSCs, exhibits high expression levels in both rMSCs and freshly isolated niche cells compared to control MSCs. Knockdown studies showed that reducing Mef2c expression in rMSCs resulted in lower expression of niche factors like Scf, Cxcl12, and Vcam1, and reduced the ability of rMSCs to expand HSCs in co-culture. On the other hand, overexpression of Mef2c also hindered HSC expansion, suggesting that a balanced regulation of Mef2c is essential for MSC revitalization and their supportive role for HSPCs [ ].

 

Preciado S et al. demonstrated that CD34+ cells co-cultured with gene modified MSCs expressing HIF exhibited a significant increase in the expression of CD34, CXCR4, and ITGA4 compared to those co-cultured with wild type (WT) MSC. These molecules are known to play important roles in HSPCs engraftment and homing to the BM niche. In vivo experiments demonstrated a significant increase in the engraftment ability of CD34+ cells when co-transplanted with HIF-MSC compared to co-transplantation with MSC WT or when transplanted alone suggesting that co-transplantation with HIF-MSC enhances the engraftment and functional properties of CD34+ cells. This approach provides a valuable therapeutic strategy for addressing issues of poor graft function or graft failure in the context of allogeneic stem cell transplantation. The mechanisms behind these improvements could be related to the observed changes in key molecular expression and clonogenic potential [ ].
Towards developing a better ex vivo culture condition system, we developed a BM mimetic niche comprising genetically modified, BM-MSCs(GM-BM MSCs) for intrinsically secreting the growth factors in hypoxic condition for mitigating cellular stress to augment HSPC expansion without compromising stemness. These GM-BM MSCs were genetically modified using lentiviral vector system to express key growth factors in HSPCs culture including Flt-3L, IL-3, SCF, and TPO. Compared to standard condition, HSPCs showed two-fold higher CD34+CD90+ cell population and 50% more CD34+CD90+CD45RA−CD38−CD49f+ cell population when co-cultured with GM-MSCs under hypoxic condition. HSPCs expanded in the external cytokine free culture system showed successful multilineage differentiation and engraftment in vivo [ ]. GM-MSCs demonstrate promising potential in supporting HSPC maintenance and expansion, offering a novel strategy for decreasing the dependency of cytokine supplements paving a way for affordable GT.
Although GM-MSCs have been utilized to support hematopoietic systems, several significant challenges remain in achieving stable gene expression in these cells. One major hurdle is the inherently low transduction efficiency of MSCs, which varies greatly depending on the tissue source, cell passage number, and the type of transfection reagent used. Another critical issue is the heterogeneity of MSC populations, which arises from multiple factors such as differences in tissue origin, donor variability, and inconsistencies in isolation protocols. Recent reviews suggest that this heterogeneity can be mitigated through pooling strategies, which may offer a more uniform and reproducible MSC population for genetic modification [ ]. Another critical challenge is the potential alteration of MSC functionality following genetic modification. Such changes can impact the MSC phenotype, compromise stemness, and ultimately impair the functional properties of GM-MSCs. Overcoming these issues necessitates the development of optimized gene delivery strategies, a deeper understanding of MSC biology, and strict adherence to safety and regulatory guidelines to ensure therapeutic efficacy and clinical applicability [ ].

## Explore BM-MSCs for culturing gene edited (GE) HSPCs
MSCs are increasingly being explored as a supportive feeder layer for the ex vivo culture of gene-manipulated HSPCs, with the goal of reducing the cellular stress and toxicity associated with genetic manipulation. Since HSPCs are mobilized, genetically modified, and maintained outside their native microenvironment, reintroducing them to a supportive niche that mimics physiological conditions as provided by MSCs may help mitigate the adverse effects and enhance cell viability and functionality. In a recent study, Crippa et al. investigated the supportive role of MSCs on clustered regularly interspaced short palindromic repeats (CRISPR)-Cas9 gene-edited (GE) HSPCs using MSCs as a feeder layer. On ex vivo co-culturing GE-HSPCs with BM-MSCs feeder layer demonstrated a suitable strategy for expanding GE-HSPCs while preserving their primitive phenotype and specific subsets, including HSCs (CD90+, CD45RA-), Multilymphoid Progenitors (MLPs, CD90-, CD45RA+), and Multimyeloid Progenitors (MMPs, CD90-, CD45RA-). Additionally, MSCs exert their paracrine activity through direct contact with HSPCs, enhancing protection from the detrimental effects of GE-HSPCs. BM-MSCs produce several hematopoietic supportive factors and exhibit anti-inflammatory activity, which helps alleviate proliferation arrest and mitigate apoptotic and inflammatory responses in GE-HSPCs. This ultimately improves their expansion and clonogenic potential in vitro. In the presence of MSCs, superior human engraftment and increased clonal output of GE-HSPCs were observed, contributing to the early phase of haematological reconstitution in the peripheral blood in transplanted mice [ ]. In the context of homology-directed repair (HDR) in HSPCs, one of the major challenges are the low HDR frequency and the significant toxicity associated with donor DNA templates, which often leads to reduced cell viability following gene editing. While the use of DNA-PK inhibitors such as AZD7648 has been shown to enhance HDR efficiency, this improvement is frequently accompanied by substantial loss in cell viability post-manipulation, thereby limiting the therapeutic potential of the approach. To overcome the limitations associated with HDR-mediated gene editing in HSPCs, our recent study demonstrated that co-culturing gene-edited HSPCs with BM-MSCs led to a fourfold increase in absolute HDR-positive cell frequency in vitro. This approach not only enhanced HDR efficiency by reducing indel formation but also significantly improved overall cell viability. Functionally, co-cultured HSPCs exhibited elevated fetal hemoglobin (HbF) levels, comparable to those achieved via Cas9 RNP-mediated indel formation at the BCL11A enhancer and HBG promoter regions. These findings highlight BM-MSC co-culture as a promising strategy to improve both the efficiency and safety of single-stranded oligodeoxynucleotide (ssODN)-based gene editing in HSPCs. Notably, BM-MSCs outperformed WJ-MSCs, likely due to their greater physiological similarity to the native BM niche, thereby offering superior support for stress recovery and cellular maintenance [ ]. Collectively, these findings show that BM-MSCs create a protective, physiologically relevant microenvironment for ex vivo culture of gene-edited HSPCs.

## HSPCs maintenance under inflammation condition
MSCs have the ability to detect inflammation and secrete significant amounts of CCL2. Under inflammation conditions, MSC-derived CCL2 facilitates macrophage differentiation from granulocyte-macrophage progenitors (GMP) by triggering myeloid differentiation in vivo. RNA-seq analyses have shown that human BM cells regulate their fate with CCL2. It has been shown in previous literature that patients with myeloproliferative neoplasms exhibit cellular responses to disease, aging, infection, and MSC-secreted factors [ ]. Peng X. and colleagues investigated how an inflammatory microenvironment influences BM-MSCs under aging conditions. They cultured BM-MSCs up to passage 3 (P3) to represent young cells and up to passage 10 (P10) to represent aging cells. Young BM-MSCs were then cultured in conditioned media (CM) from either P3 or P10 cells. The study found that CM from aged MSCs reduced the stemness and differentiation potential of young BM-MSCs and induced senescence. An inflammation assay with serum from young and old mice revealed that aging increases the pro-inflammatory cytokine IL-6 and decreases the anti-inflammatory cytokine IL-10 [ ].

## Cocultured HSPCs altering the fate of the MSCs
During the expansion of cbHSPCs, MSCs experienced significant functional changes, leading to the emergence of early stromal progenitors with both osteogenic and adipogenic potential. This transformation was evidenced by the increased expression of MSC genes related to osteo and adipogenic differentiation, increased alkaline phosphatase activity, and modified osteoprotein profiles. When MSCs underwent a brief 72-hour osteoinduction prior to coculture, there was an even more notable shift in their bipotential gene expression and osteoprotein profiles. This osteoinduction led to a higher proportion of late primitive CD133+/CD34+ cbHSPCs and unipotent CFUs, indicating that cbHSPCs expanded on osteo induced MSCs were more lineage committed compared to those cocultured with undifferentiated MSCs [ ].

## HSCs/HSPCs contribution to MSCs
BMT is frequently preceded by irradiation, which causes extensive damage to the BM microenvironment, especially to MSCs. Irradiation inflicts severe mitochondrial damage on BM MSCs, impairing their functionality. Research by Golan K et al.. has shown that healthy donor HSPCs can transfer functional mitochondria to the damaged MSCs, a process critical for the restoration of MSC mitochondrial function and overall BM regeneration. This transfer relies on direct cell contact and is mediated by connexin-43 (Cx43) present in HSPCs. Cx43 enhances mitochondrial transfer to MSCs, while adenosine monophosphate–activated protein kinase (AMPK) inhibits it. Mice lacking Cx43 exhibit reduced mitochondrial transfer, leading to delayed mesenchymal and osteogenic regeneration. Restoration of Cx43 expression or culturing with isolated mitochondria from Cx43-deficient HSPCs can mitigate this deficiency. Reduced AMPK activity in HSPCs promotes mitochondrial transfer to MSCs. This study underscores the significance of mitochondrial transfer from donor HSPCs to BM stroma in aiding the recovery of the BM microenvironment [ ]. Another study demonstrated that direct co-culture with HSPCs significantly promoted the proliferation of MSCs and enhanced their ability to differentiate into chondrocytes. In contrast, indirect co-culture using a transwell assay, where MSCs were seeded on the bottom of the transwell without direct contact with HSCs had minimal impact. MSCs co-cultured with HSCs showed higher expression of differentiation markers, indicating improved differentiation capacity. Conversely, MSCs co-cultured with c-Mpl knockout HSCs exhibited reduced proliferation and lower expression of MSC markers (PDGFRa and Sca-1) compared to those co-cultured with wild-type HSCs, suggesting the crucial role of the c-Mpl signal from HSCs in maintaining MSC properties. Additionally, Microarray analysis revealed an upregulation of transcription factors such as SRCIN1, BNC1, and PCK1 in MSCs after co-culture with HSCs, which are associated with cell expansion, migration, and regulation of adipogenesis. Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) identified significant differences in gene expression related to type I collagen synthesis, adipogenesis, Wnt signaling, and histone modification (H3K27me3), further indicating that HSC signals influence MSC properties. The study underscores the importance of hematopoietic signals, particularly the c-Mpl signal, in sustaining MSC characteristics [ ].

## Advancements in understanding MSCs-HSPCs interactions
Based on the review, it is evident that MSCs play a crucial role in regulating HSPCs homeostasis, both in experimental setups and in living organisms. Since, MSCs are a rare cell population, their profiling has been conducted after these cells undergo a period of ex vivo expansion. This process involves cultivating the MSCs outside their natural environment in controlled laboratory conditions. Further, ex vivo expanded BM-MSC transcriptomic analysis revealed that they are enriched mainly with adipo and osteo-MSC subpopulation [ ]. It’s important to note that this ex vivo expansion can significantly alter the transcriptional profile of MSCs compared to their in vivo homeostatic state, which refers to their natural condition within the body where they maintain their normal functions and characteristics.
In patients’ femoral head tissue section analyses identified at least six mesenchymal subsets, two endothelial subsets, and one smooth muscle population in the BM microenvironment. All MSC subsets met the minimal criteria for MSCs defined by the ISCT, with Fibro-MSCs being the closest to this definition. Osteo and Fibro-MSCs were located nearer to the trabecular bone region, whereas Adipo and THY1-MSCs were predominately found in the BM regions, suggesting that MSC heterogeneity may depend on distinct spatial phenotypes. Additionally, they demonstrated that Adipo- and THY1-MSCs are the most transcriptionally specialized MSC to support hematopoiesis, indicated by their high expression of key niche factors such as CXCL12, KITLG, IL7, and CSF1 [ ].
Multiple studies have demonstrated that long-term in vitro expansion of MSCs affects their proliferation, alters their fundamental characteristics, and impacts their immunosuppressive properties [ – ]. Ex vivo cultured cells significantly modified the molecular signatures of primary MSCs, impacting processes such as cell cycling, and differentiation, even after just a single passage. Another key characteristic of MSCs is their immunomodulatory capacity, which is also compromised during in vitro expansion by downregulation of immune response and complement activation processes. These findings underscore the critical importance of the MSC source, emphasizing the need to optimize culture conditions to minimize impacts on MSC biology and to transition towards in vivo methodologies for studying MSC function [ ]. MSCs adjust to their ECM environment, potentially undergoing pathological changes that could reduce therapeutic effectiveness. The production and composition of ECM proteins by MSCs can change after ex vivo expansion [ ]. These ECM proteins are crucial for the physical and biochemical support of HSPCs, and any alterations in ECM composition can affect the adhesion, migration, and differentiation of HSPCs within the coculture system.

## Reconstructing the BM niche in vitro
MSCs are a central component of the BM niche, providing structural support, secreting cytokines, and remodeling the ECM to regulate HSPC adhesion, quiescence, and proliferation. Incorporating MSCs into 3D culture systems with biomimetic components more effectively reproduces the functional outcomes of the native BM.
Puramatrix hydrogel-based co-cultures with MSCs(3D-MSCs) significantly improved HSPC expansion by preserving a large pool of primitive, quiescent stem cells with enhanced phenotypic and functional properties. This was driven by the CXCR4–SDF1α axis, integrin β1-mediated adhesion, MSC-secreted supportive factors, and physiologic hypoxia gradients. Importantly, 3D-MSC niches sustained more LT-HSCs, prevented premature cycling, and achieved ~ 1.8-fold higher engraftment in secondary recipients compared to 2D cultures [ ]. Gelatin Methacryloyl (GelMA) hydrogels were used to study MSC–HSPC crosstalk and matrix remodeling in regulating HSC expansion and quiescence. A low-diffusivity hydrogel with a 1:1 HSPC: MSC co-culture effectively maintained hematopoietic progenitors over 7 days. Inclusion of MSCs markedly enhanced quiescence, yielding a 129-fold increase in LT-HSCs and a 7.7-fold increase in total HSCs compared to monocultures. Hydrogel-based co-culture sustained quiescence more efficiently than conventional liquid cultures, and MSC density–dependent upregulation of MMP-9 with altered mechanical properties suggests that MSC-driven matrix remodelling contributes to a dynamic and supportive culture environment [ ]. A biomimetic niche model using RGD-modified macroporous PEG diacrylate scaffolds was developed to mimic the trabecular structure of BM for HSPC culture. Co-culture with BM–derived MSCs enhanced HSPC proliferation and preserved CD34⁺ stemness more effectively than umbilical cord MSCs, with 3D scaffolds outperforming 2D systems [ ].
Among various biomaterials tested (PCL, PLGA, fibrin, collagen), fibrin scaffolds with UC-MSC support achieved the highest CD34⁺ expansion, preserved primitive phenotypes, and enhanced migration/adhesion UC-HSPCs. These cultures yielded superior multi-organ engraftment and multilineage differentiation in NSG mice, highlighting fibrin-based 3D niches as optimal for clinical translation [ ]. A collagen-based 3D culture with MSCs was designed to mimic BM niche conditions for HSPCs. Two microenvironments emerged: proliferative HSPCs in suspension and primitive CD34⁺/CD38⁻ cells migrating within collagen fibers. BM-MSCs enhanced clonal growth and preserved stem-like HSPCs, while UC-MSCs drove proliferation with lineage commitment [ ].
A 3D bioprinted scaffold of alginate and gelatin, cross-linked by calcium chloride, was engineered for CD34⁺ cell co-culture with UC-MSCs. Compared to conventional 2D systems, the 3D scaffold improved cell vitality and reduced early apoptosis of CD34⁺ cells. After 10 days, expansion of CD34⁺/CD38⁻ cells reached 33.6-fold and CD34⁺/CD184⁺ cells 16.7-fold, with higher VLA-4 and VLA-5 expression linked to scaffold-mediated clustering [ ]. Another study used AggreWell microwells, surface-modified with a multilayer coating of hyaluronic acid and chitosan, to develop a scaffold-free 3D co-culture platform (“micromarrows”) where murine HSPCs (LSK cells) and MSCs formed uniform spheroids. Compared to conventional 2D co-cultures, micromarrows supported approximately twice the expansion of LSK cells, indicating enhanced self-renewal. Moreover, MSCs in 3D aggregates expressed significantly higher levels of key niche factors (Ang-1, SCF, Jagged-1, and SDF-1) than MSCs in 2D culture [ ].
Huang X. et al. established a 3D culture system using bio-derived bone scaffolds seeded with BM-MSCs and OBs differentiated from MSCs. MSCs alone performed better than OBs in preserving primitive HSPCs, but their combination provided the most balanced, niche-like support. This biomimetic microenvironment secreted key cytokines and ECM proteins, closely resembling the BM niche. Compared to 2D systems, it significantly enhanced CD34⁺ and CD34⁺/CD38⁻ HSPC expansion while maintaining quiescence, with functional assays confirming superior colony formation, LTC-IC output, and long-term engraftment in NOD/SCID mice, underscoring its translational potential [ ]. Another study developed a 3D culture system using human bio-derived bone scaffolds seeded with BM-MSCs and HUVECs to mimic the vascular niche. 3D-Mix with MSCs and HUVEC outperformed by significantly increasing CD34⁺ and CD34⁺/CD38⁻ expansion, maintaining quiescence, boosting LTC-IC frequency, and achieving higher engraftment in NOD/SCID mice [ ]. Integrating MSCs with endothelial and osteoblastic cells in 3D biomimetic cultures closely recreates the BM niche, enhancing HSPC maintenance, quiescence, and function.
A 3D human BM–mimetic system was developed using hydroxyapatite scaffolds coated with MSC-derived osteoblastic stroma and ECM under perfusion flow. This engineered niche supported strong expansion and maintenance of CD34⁺ HSPCs—including HSCs, MPPs, and MLPs—relative to scaffold-only controls. Primitive HSPCs remained embedded within the stromal ECM, whereas committed cells were released into the liquid phase, reflecting in vivo–like compartmentalization. Functional assays demonstrated sustained clonogenic activity and long-term engraftment in NSG mice, establishing this model as a tunable proxy for the human BM niche [ ]. A 3D in vitro bone-like matrix was generated by microencapsulating mouse MSCs in type I collagen constructs and inducing osteogenic differentiation, which remodelled the collagen into a matrix enriched with osteocalcin, osteopontin, calcium deposits, and BMP2. Following decellularization, the matrix was repopulated with human MSCs and seeded with cord blood–derived CD34⁺ HSPCs. Compared to pure collagen scaffolds, this engineered matrix supported significantly greater HSPC adhesion and more MSC–HSPC interactions, with calcium and osteogenic proteins recreating key osteoblastic niche cues [ ]. Collectively, these studies suggest that MSCs are a pivotal component of the BM niche. Incorporating MSCs into 3D in vitro models provides a powerful platform to dissect cell–cell and cell–matrix interactions that govern HSC fate and niche function.

## Present challenges
MSCs sourced from adult tissues exhibit significant variability in gene expression, differentiation capabilities, proliferation rates, and colony-forming capacities across different donors [ – ]. This heterogeneity poses a substantial challenge in MSC research. Moreover, factors influencing MSC manufacturing, such as the origin of the starting material and culture conditions (like seeding density, passage number, media supplements, and expansion devices), vary significantly among different research centres and laboratories worldwide [ , , ]. When considering the coculture HSPCs with MSCs, it’s crucial to recognize that the MSCs undergo significant changes during ex vivo expansion. During ex vivo expansion, MSCs are exposed to different environmental conditions, such as changes in oxygen levels, nutrient availability, and mechanical forces, which can lead to a shift in their gene expression patterns. This alteration may influence their functional properties, including their ability to support HSPCs. Therefore, identifying novel strategies to rejuvenate senescent/aged MSCs to broaden the range of therapeutic applications of MSCs.
While MSCs are often considered immune-privileged, there is evidence suggesting that they can elicit immune responses under certain conditions, particularly upon differentiation. Long-term safety studies are essential to address these concerns [ ]. Given the supportive role of GM-MSCs in promoting HSPC maintenance in vitro, their production along gene-modified HSPCs must comply with stringent Good Manufacturing Practice (GMP) standards to ensure consistency, safety, and therapeutic efficacy. The development of robust potency assays and comprehensive quality control frameworks is imperative to meet regulatory requirements and facilitate successful clinical translation.

## Future perspectives
Induced pluripotent stem cell (iPSC)-derived MSCs offer a promising alternative to traditional MSCs by potentially circumventing the limitations posed by donor variability and age-related decline. These cells can be generated from young donors and from patient-specific iPSCs. As they can be generated in large quantities from a single iPSC clone, providing a uniform cell source eliminating concerns related to donor variability and age-related functional decline, superior proliferative capacity and delayed senescence, offering greater consistency and reliability in therapeutic outcomes (Fig. ). Future research should focus on optimizing the inflammatory response of iPSC-derived MSCs, ensuring their functionality in various therapeutic contexts. By harnessing the regenerative potential of iPSC-derived MSCs, we can advance the development of effective treatments for age-related diseases and improve outcomes in regenerative medicine. MSCs derived from iPSCs with a knockdown of the p53 gene were developed to enhance HSPC expansion. Results demonstrated a significant increase in the CD34⁺CD45RA-CD90⁺ (phenotypic HSC) population. Additionally, a synergistic effect was observed when combined with small molecules, leading to an even higher expansion rate and significantly greater chimerism levels compared to the control [ ].

 

Another research investigating MSCs derived from hiPSCs (pMSCs) indicates that co-culturing with pMSCs notably enhances the expansion and functional preservation of UCB derived CD34⁺ HSC(UCB-CD34⁺ HSCs), both in laboratory settings and in living organisms. pMSCs demonstrate heightened expression of ECM genes (e.g., collagen, fibronectin, laminin, proteoglycans) and HSC niche-associated genes like NES and MCAM. This suggests that direct interaction between HSCs and pMSCs may improve the functionality of HSCs. Additionally, pMSCs express elevated levels of Wnt signalling inhibitors SFRP1 and SFRP2, potentially aiding in cell cycle modulation and supporting the maintenance of UCB-CD34⁺ HSPCs by counteracting Wnt activation [ ]. Overall, iPSC-derived MSCs represent a next-generation niche platform that can overcome donor-related variability, aging-associated decline, and limited scalability of primary MSCs. By providing a uniform, rejuvenated, and customizable cell source, they hold significant promise for improving HSPC expansion, maintenance, and engraftment.

## Conclusion
MSCs are an exceptionally rare cell population in BM cells, they profoundly shape the BM microenvironment and significantly affect HSC function. MSCs secrete a variety of cytokines, growth factors, and ECM proteins that are essential for the maintenance and regulation of HSCs. As key components of the BM stroma, MSCs create a supportive niche for HSCs, regulating their self-renewal, proliferation, and differentiation. They provide structural support and produce signals that influence HSC behaviour ensuring a balanced production of blood cells. In normal conditions, MSCs help maintain HSC quiescence and prevent their exhaustion by creating a protective microenvironment that shields HSCs from excessive proliferation and differentiation stimuli. In adverse conditions, such as during hematopoietic stress or injury, MSCs adapt to support HSCs. They can modulate their secretory profile to enhance HSC survival, promote repair, and facilitate recovery from BM damage condition. For instance, in response to inflammation or chemotherapy, MSCs increase the production of specific cytokines and growth factors that aid in the regeneration of the hematopoietic system. This adaptive capability highlights the resilience and pivotal role of MSCs in maintaining hematopoietic homeostasis under varying conditions like radiation syndrome in vivo and editing or manipulation in vitro environment. Their ability to modulate the BM microenvironment and support HSC function underscores their importance in haematopoiesis and potential therapeutic applications for haematological disorders and BM transplantation.
