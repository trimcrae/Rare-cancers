Department of Radiation Oncology, Mayo Clinic, Rochester, Minnesota, USA Radiation triggers a range of DNA lesions, and cells depend on a coordinated DNA damage response (DDR) to ensure recovery from such genotoxic damage. Dysregulation of DDR components during tumorigenesis facilitates the accumulation of tumor-promoting genomic alterations but also renders tumor cells more susceptible to pharmacologic DDR inhibition. This vulnerability has stimulated the development of various DDR inhibitors that suppress the repair of radiation-induced DNA damage and sensitize tumors to radiation. With approximately 85% of GBM ultimately progressing within the radiation field, there is a compelling rationale to explore the integration of these inhibitors with therapeutic radiation in GBM in hopes of improving tumor control and survival.
The ataxia-telangiectasia mutated (ATM) kinase is a key orchestrator of DDR. Following radiation, ATM is recruited to damage sites and phosphorylates numerous downstream targets to facilitate a coordinated response to DNA damage, including cell cycle arrest, focal chromatin relaxation, activation of DNA repair, and modulation of cellular senescence and apoptosis. Consistent with the central role of ATM in recovery from therapeutic radiation, homozygous ATM inactivation or complete ATM knockdown results in profound radiation sensitivity. Based on these data, multiple efforts are underway developing small molecule ATM inhibitors as possible radiosensitizers.
The blood-brain barrier (BBB) limits drug distribution into the brain and is a critical consideration for therapeutic strategies for any central nervous system pathology. , The unperturbed BBB is characterized by continuous tight junctions between capillary endothelial cells that limit paracellular diffusion into the brain and expression of drug efflux pumps on the luminal surface of endothelial cells that transport drugs back into the bloodstream. Because GBM all have significant tumor burden “protected” by the BBB, development of drugs specifically optimized for brain distribution is critically important. With a goal of developing drugs for brain tumors, Wayshine Biopharm developed the brain penetrant ATM inhibitor WSD0628. The data reported here provide the rationale for the ongoing first-in-human evaluation of WSD0628 in combination with radiation in recurrent GBM patients (NCT05917145).

## Materials and Methods

## Cell Culture and Reagents
Origins of the cell lines were: U251 from long-term lab stocks, MCF-7, Caco-2 from ATCC, HT-29 from ECACC, hERG (Kv11.1)-HEK293 from BPS Bioscience, and MDCKII-MDR1 from Netherlands Cancer Institute. Generation of PDXs at Mayo Clinic was approved by the Mayo Clinic Institutional Review Board and the Mayo Clinic Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC). Cell line authenticity was assessed using short tandem repeat profiling (Genecopoeia, Rockville, MD), and mycoplasma testing was performed routinely. Cells were cultured and transduced as described and in . WSD0628 from Wayshine Biopharm was prepared according to the methods for compound 14 in United States Patent #11919899B2. All other inhibitors were from Selleck Chemicals. Cell irradiation was performed in an X-Rad 320 Biological Irradiator Cabinet (Precision X-Ray).

## In Vitro Drug Metabolism and Pharmacokinetics (DMPK) Studies
Time-resolved Fluorescence Energy Transfer (TR-FRET), kinase assay, kinase screen, human Ether-a-go-go-related gene (hERG) assessment, efflux transport, hepatocyte and Aldehyde Oxidase (AO) Stability, and in-cell western assays were performed by Pharmaron Life Science in Beijing, China as described in .

## Western Blotting
Detailed methods and antibodies are described in . In brief, following protein extraction, equivalent amounts of protein were resolved by polyacrylamide electrophoresis and transferred to PVDF membranes. Membranes were blocked in 5% nonfat milk before overnight incubation with the primary antibody followed by incubation with a secondary antibody for 1 hour and chemiluminescent development. Images were captured on a c600 imager (Azure Biosystems) and processed using Adobe Photoshop.

## Clonogenic Assays
U251 and M12 cells were incubated for 4 hours before treatment with WSD0628 30 minutes prior to RT. Two to twenty-four hours after drug dosing, cells were rinsed with PBS, media was replaced, and cells were incubated for 2 weeks before colonies were stained with Coomassie Blue and counted to determine plating efficiency and cell survival.

## Extreme Limiting Dilution Assay
GBM120 cells were used in an extreme limiting dilution assay as described and treated with WSD0628 and RT at the doses indicated.

## WSD0628 Pharmacokinetic Measurements
WSD0628 in plasma and cerebellum was measured using LC-MS/MS as described and in .

## Immunostaining
Immunofluorescence (IF) in U251 and GBM43 cells was performed as described. Details of the IF assay performed on GBM43eGFP-fLuc2 intracranial tumors are in . Briefly, following antigen retrieval, 5-micron sections were incubated with the primary γH2AX antibody overnight at 4°C and then for 1 hour with a fluorophore-conjugated secondary antibody. Cells were imaged on a fluorescence microscope, and foci were manually counted. Immunohistochemical (IHC) staining of M12 was performed in the Mayo Clinic Pathology Research Core and details can be found in . A histological review of the samples was performed by a neuropathologist. Neurons (NeuN-positive) counted in 5 high-power (40X) image fields per slide were scored for statistical evaluation.

## In Vivo Efficacy and Pharmacodynamic/Pharmacokinetic (PD/PK) Studies
Animal studies were approved by the Mayo Clinic IACUC. A detailed protocol for intracranial xenografting can be found in . Briefly, 100 000 (GBM12, GBM43, GBM43eGFP-fLuc2, M12eGFP-fLuc2) or 300 000 (GBM120) cells were injected intracranially into female athymic nude mice (Charles River, Wilmington, MA). For efficacy studies, mice with established tumors were randomized and treated with WSD0628 15–30 minutes before RT, and 24 hours after RT (as indicated). RT was delivered using a 10 or 15-mm circular collimator as described and in . Mice were monitored daily and euthanized upon reaching a moribund state. Additional treatment arms and the control arms included in the studies presented in and , and were published previously. , Brains from efficacy studies were formalin-fixed and paraffin-embedded (FFPE; n = 4–10). For the PD/PK GBM43eGFP-fLuc2 study, a single oral dose of 7.5 mg/kg WSD0628 was given 30 minutes before 5 Gy, and mice were sacrificed 1.5 hours later (n = 4–5). Whole blood was collected by cardiac puncture and plasma was isolated. The cerebellum was removed and flash-frozen, while the supratentorium was prepared for FFPE.

## Statistical Analysis
Comparisons across groups for in vitro toxicity and damage-inducible foci used a two-sample t-test. In clonogenic assays, the interaction between drug treatment groups and RT dose levels was first tested, and then a two-sample t-test was used to compare individual differences in survival at individual RT levels. Survival fractions were fitted to a linear-quadratic model to estimate the sensitizer enhancement ratio at 10% survival (SER10) as described. Median survival of intracranial efficacy studies was estimated using the Kaplan–Meier method and compared across groups by log rank. The statistical analysis of these survival studies is provided as . Body weight changes at specific time points were compared across groups using analysis of covariance, adjusting for baseline body weight. In all cases, P-values <.05 were considered statistically significant.

## Results

## Biochemical and Biological Properties of WSD0628
A medicinal chemistry campaign was initiated with the goal of developing a brain-penetrant, small-molecule kinase inhibitor of ATM. Through this strategy, we identified R-1-(3,3-difluoro-1-methylpiperidin-4-yl)-8-(6-methoxypyridin-3-yl)-3-methyl-1,3-dihydro-2H-imidazo[4,5-c]quinolin-2-one (WSD0628) as our lead compound ( ; US Patent 11919899B2, Mar. 5, 2024). Our primary screen was based on a time-resolved fluorescence resonance energy transfer (TR-FRET) assay for ATM kinase activity. Evaluating kinase inhibition across a range of ATP and WSD0628 concentrations, the data were analyzed in a Lineweaver–Burk plot. This demonstrated the Km increases and the Vmax decreases as the concentration of WSD0628 increases ( ), and the apparent IC50 for WSD0628 was 0.097 ± 0.026 nM against ATM kinase activity ( ). Thus, WSD0628 is a potent inhibitor of ATM kinase activity in a cell-free assay. Extending our analysis across a panel of 216 kinases, 1 mM of WSD0628 demonstrated significant inhibitory activity against only 8 kinases (PASK—61.6% inhibition; Ret—52.5%; DYRK3 – 64.4%; HGK—84.3%; CLK4 – 85.4%; CLK1 – 93.2%; IRAK4 – 95.6%; , ). Further inhibitory screening of IRAK4 and CLK1 using a mobility shift kinase inhibition assay demonstrated relatively weak inhibitory effects (IC50 value 194.8 ± 2.0 nM and 440.4 ± 7.5 nM, respectively; ). Thus, WSD0628 is a relatively selective inhibitor of ATM kinase activity in a cell-free kinase screen.
 The cellular activity and specificity of WSD0628 for ATM, relative to the related DNA-PKcs and ATR kinases, were evaluated in cell culture studies. ATM-dependent phosphorylation of serine-824 of KAP1 is activated by etoposide-induced DNA double-strand breaks (DSBs) and can be used to evaluate cellular ATM activity. Pre-incubation of MCF7 cells with WSD0628 prior to etoposide treatment resulted in robust inhibition of phosphor-S824 KAP1 with IC50 of 1.32 ± 0.06 nM. Using a similar assay with HT-29 cells exposed to 10 Gy radiation and autophosphorylation of DNA-PKcs as a readout, WSD0628 had a less robust inhibition of radiation-induced phospho-S2056 DNA-PKcs with an IC50 of 169.1 ± 13.4 nM. Similarly, gemcitabine-induced replication stress activates cellular ATR activity; gemcitabine-induced Chk1-S345 phosphorylation was marginally inhibited by WSD0628 with an IC50 of 763 ± 64nM ( ). Thus, at a cellular level, WSD0628 is a relatively selective inhibitor of ATM kinase activity as compared to DNA-PKcs or ATR.
The distribution of kinase inhibitors across the BBB is critically mediated by efflux transporters, and part of our screening strategy involved evaluating efflux liability in a transwell assay. Using Madin-Darby Canine Kidney II (MDCKII) cells stably expressing multi-drug resistant 1 (MDR1), the bi-directional permeability ratio of A to B and B to A (20.6 × 10−6 cm/s vs. 17.7 × 10−6 cm/s, respectively) was low at 0.86 and indicates that WSD0628 is not an MDR1 substrate ( ). Caco-2 cells express the high levels of another important efflux transporter, Breast Cancer Resistance Protein (BCRP). These cells were used in a similar transwell assay that included co-treatment with and without the BCRP inhibitor novobiocin. The bi-directional permeability ratio of A to B and B to A was 21.7 × 10−6 vs. 14.5 × 10−6 cm/s in the absence and 24.4 × 10−6 vs. 16.1 × 10−6 cm/s in the presence of novobiocin. With a WSD0628 efflux ratio of 0.67 and 0.66 in the absence and presence of novobiocin, respectively, these data indicate that WSD0628 is not a substrate of BCRP ( ). The limited efflux liability suggests that WSD0628 is more likely to readily cross the BBB and distribute into brain tumors.
 Key pharmacologic liabilities of WSD0628 were assessed in a series of in vitro assays ( ). The potential interaction of WSD0628 with human Ether-a-go-go related gene (hERG) was evaluated in HEK293 expressing exogenous hERG. Using a manual patch-clamp system, WSD0628 exhibited a concentration-dependent increase in the current inhibition with 0%, 1.1%, 25.1%, and 36.1% inhibition at 1, 3, 10, and 30 μM, respectively ( ). The concentration-response curve and an accurate IC50 were not obtained because WSD0628 did not produce ≥50% mean inhibition at the highest concentration (30 μM) tested. Metabolic stability and intrinsic clearance of WSD0628 were evaluated in vitro using rat, dog, and human hepatocytes and measured as 7.9 ± 0.7, 10.1 ± 1.2, and 2.8 ± 0.2 μL/min/106 cells, respectively ( ). Finally, the intrinsic clearance (CLint) of WSD0628 was evaluated in human liver cytosol in the presence or absence of the AO inhibitor raloxifene. WSD0628 was relatively stable in this assay over 120 minutes with a CLint of 0.58 mL/min/mg protein ( ). To predict safety liabilities, WSD0628 was tested against a panel of ion channels, receptors, transporters, and enzymes and was within a typical range for drugs administered to humans ( ). In conjunction with our previous description of murine PK and additional small and large animal safety studies not reported here, these data suggest that WSD0628 has drug-like properties and is expected to have a reasonable safety margin suitable for first-in-human studies.

## Effects of WSD0628 on DNA Damage Signaling in Brain Tumor Models
WSD0628 was specifically developed as a highly brain-penetrant molecule for brain tumor therapy. Therefore, the impact of WSD0628 on radiation-induced ATM signaling was evaluated in U251 glioma cells. As expected, radiation alone induced robust phosphorylation of multiple proteins in canonical DDR pathways including ATM (S1981), DNA-dependent protein kinase (DNA-PK; S2056), Krüppel-associated box associated protein 1 (KAP1; S824), Checkpoint kinase 2 (Chk2; T68), and Checkpoint kinase 1 (Chk1; S345; and ). Consistent with disruption of ATM-dependent signaling, pretreatment with 10 to 100 nM WSD0628 suppressed ATM autophosphorylation (S1981) and downstream phosphorylation of KAP1 (S824) but had no effect on DNA-PK autophosphorylation (S2056; and ). Interestingly, 10 nM WSD0628 appeared to enhance the phosphorylation of Chk2. To further evaluate this finding, U251 cells were pretreated with 30 nM WSD0628, radiated, and lysates collected at times ranging from 30 minutes to 16 hours after irradiation. Again, there was robust inhibition of ATM-dependent, radiation-induced signaling except for paradoxical induction of Chk2 phosphorylation at times ranging from 2 to 8 hours post-irradiation ( ). Interestingly, WSD0628 also suppressed Chk1 phosphorylation (S345), which is typically ascribed to ATR kinase activity. An analysis of radiation-induced DNA damage signaling was extended to PDXs from GBM (GBM43, GBM120) and melanoma brain metastasis (M12). In all 3 PDXs, WSD0628 robustly inhibited ATM autophosphorylation and downstream KAP1 phosphorylation in the low nanomolar ranges ( and and ). Interestingly, WSD0628 suppressed radiation-induced Chk1 phosphorylation only in the GBM PDXs, and a paradoxical increase in Chk2 phosphorylation only was seen in GBM43 ( and ). These data highlight robust inhibition of ATM activity across tumor models with some tumor-specific differences in radiation-induced DNA damage signaling of downstream pathways.
 The effects of WSD0628 on DNA damaging signaling were compared to other known DDR inhibitors. Specifically, WSD0628 (100 nM), the ATM inhibitor AZD1390 (30 nM), the ATR inhibitor VX970 (300 nM), or the DNA-PKcs inhibitor M3814 (300 nM) were evaluated in combination with 5 Gy radiation in U251 and GBM43 cells. As expected, both ATM inhibitors effectively suppressed ATM and KAP1 phosphorylation, which were unaffected by DNA-PK inhibition. While both ATM inhibitors suppressed Chk2 phosphorylation 30 minutes after radiation, WSD0628, but not AZD1390, enhanced Chk2 phosphorylation 4 hours after radiation. This effect of WSD0628 on elevated p-Chk2 is similar to that of VX970 at the same 4-hour timepoint ( ). While these data are consistent with robust inhibition of ATM by WSD0628, there was a question of whether this drug might also affect ATR signaling in U251 and GBM43 cells.
ATR is an important mediator of replication stress, and hydroxyurea (HU) or other replication inhibitors are commonly used to study ATR signaling. Because stalled replication forks can degenerate into DNA DSBs, HU treatment also can activate ATM. Therefore, we compared the effects of HU in combination with WSD0628, AZD1390 or VX970 in U251 cells with CRISPR deletion of ATM (U251 ATM-KO#4, #22) vs. U251 cells stably expressing Cas9 only (U251Cas9). In both cell lines, HU-promoted KAP1 phosphorylation was elevated by co-incubation with the ATR inhibitor (VX970) but neither ATM inhibitor ( ). Similarly, VX970 but neither ATM inhibitor promoted HU-induced Chk2 phosphorylation in both ATM-intact and ATM-deleted cells. In contrast, both WSD0628 and VX970 promoted Chk2 phosphorylation 4 hours after irradiation in U251Cas9 cells, while the signal for either was lost in the ATM-KO cells ( ). Collectively, the data presented demonstrate that WSD0628 effectively suppresses ATM signaling but may also have indirect effects on ATR signaling not seen with AZD1390.

## Radiosensitization of Brain Tumor Cells by WSD0628-Mediated ATM Inhibition
The radiosensitizing effects of WSD0628 were evaluated in clonogenic assays. There was a significant interaction between WSD0628 and radiation therapy (RT; , P < .001). Pretreatment of U251 with 30 nM WSD0628 resulted in robust enhancement in radiation cell killing (P < .01 at 2.5 and 5 Gy) with a sensitizer enhancement ratio at 10% survival (SER10) of 2.9 ± 0.4. Intermediate levels of radiosensitization were seen with 10 nM WSD0628 (SER10 = 1.6 ± 0.1), while 3 nM WSD0628 had no effect on radiation survival ( ). To identify an optimal duration of ATM inhibition for maximal radiosensitization, U251 cells were treated with 30 nM WSD0628 prior to irradiation (5 Gy), and then the drug was removed with the replacement of cell culture media 2, 4, 8, 12, 16, and 24 hours after irradiation. Significantly increased cell killing was observed with as little as 8 hours of incubation with WSD0628, and the extent of radiosensitization continued to increase with up to a 24-hour drug incubation ( ). The M12 patient-derived xenograft (PDX) grows well in adherent culture and is suitable for clonogenic assays. In this relatively radioresistant model, there was also a significant interaction between WSD0628 and RT ( , P = .001). After a 24-hour incubation with 30 and 100 nM, WSD0628 resulted in robust radiosensitization with SER10 of 2.0 ± 0.2 and 2.6 ± 0.2, respectively ( ). Finally, the evaluation of WSD0628 in an extreme limiting dilution assay (ELDA) using GBM120 cells demonstrated significantly greater cell kill following 2 Gy when co-treated with 30 nM WSD0628 ( ). Thus, WSD0628 provided robust in vitro radiosensitizing effects in both GBM and melanoma brain metastatic models.
 RT-induced DNA DSBs trigger an accumulation of DNA damage-induced foci. Specifically, ATM-dependent phosphorylation of H2AX on S139 (γH2AX) is commonly used to visualize unrepaired DNA damage. Radiation of U251 cells resulted in robust γH2AX foci induction (83.4% ± 3.9%) that was reduced to near baseline with WSD0628 co-treatment (18.5% ± 5.0%; P < .001; ). A similar effect was observed in GBM43 cells (87.8% ± 12.2% vs. 18.4% ± 12.0%, respectively; P < .002; ). To evaluate whether WSD0628 was affecting other damage-inducible foci, U251 cells were analyzed for RT-induced 53BP1 foci. Consistent with 53BP1 being an ATM-independent sensor for DNA damage, WSD0628 had minimal impact on RT-induced 53BP1 foci (75.0% ± 6.3% with WSD0628/RT vs. 79.5% ± 4.1% with RT alone, P = .36; ). These data are consistent with robust inhibition of radiation-induced ATM signaling.

## WSD0628 Provides Robust In Vivo Radiosensitizing Effects
WSD0628 efficacy was evaluated in mice with orthotopic GBM PDXs. For each experiment, intracranial tumors were established via stereotactic injection. Mice were subsequently randomized to treatment with vehicle/sham radiation, radiation only, and radiation + WSD0628. With short treatment duration and limited expectations for single-agent activity, a drug-only arm was omitted from these studies. In GBM43, conventionally fractionated radiation (2 Gy × 5 fractions) alone had a marginal impact on survival compared to sham treatment (median survival 34 vs. 29 days, respectively; P = .003), while pretreatment with either 5 or 10 mg/kg WSD0628 before each fraction of radiation significantly extended survival relative to RT alone (54 and 73 days, respectively; P = .002 in each case; ). In this pilot study, mice co-treated with either 5 or 10 mg/kg WSD0628 had significant body weight loss, compared to radiation alone, 12 days after completion of treatment (average 9%, 20%, and 0%, respectively; P < .001 for either treatment relative to RT only; ). This effect is consistent with radiosensitization of the mucosa within the radiation field (15 mm circle), which included the roof of the mouth. Therefore, in subsequent radiation studies, a smaller radiation field (10 mm circle) was used with scrupulous attention paid to radiation targeting to spare the entirety of the oral cavity and oropharynx.
 GBM120 was derived from a patient with recurrent GBM, so this model was treated with a clinically relevant hypofractionated radiation regimen (3.5 Gy × 5 fractions). Radiation significantly extended median survival compared to sham treatment (89 vs. 51 days, respectively; P = .003), and co-treatment with 7.5 mg/kg WSD0628 further extended survival (231 days, P = .002 relative to RT only; ). Importantly, the modified radiation regimen used for subsequent studies was better tolerated without significant weight loss associated with WSD0628 combined with radiation ( - ). Thus, WSD0628 co-treatment resulted in profound enhancement in survival when combined with clinically relevant fractionated radiation schedules.

## Pharmacokinetic and Pharmacodynamic Effects of WSD0628 and RT on GBM PDX Model
The distribution of WSD0628 into the brain and effects on radiation-inducible γH2AX foci were evaluated in GBM43. Mice with orthotopic GBM43 tumors were treated with vehicle, 7.5 mg/kg WSD0628, 5Gy RT, or 7.5 mg/kg WSD0628 30 minutes before 5Gy RT (n = 4–5 pertreatment group), and plasma and brain were harvested 1.5 hours after RT. WSD0628 levels were assessed in plasma and cerebellum by LC/MS-MS and the unbound drug concentrations were determined using the previously reported unbound fraction (fu) values for mouse plasma and brain. Two hours after drug treatment, unbound plasma levels averaged 181 ± 20 nM, while levels in the cerebellum averaged 46.1 ± 4.1 nM ( ). The pharmacodynamic, DDR was assessed on GBM tumor cells in the FFPE supratentorial brain by performing immunofluorescence for γH2AX foci. After RT alone, 78.3% ± 25.7% of cells had greater than 25 γH2AX foci/nuclei, which decreased to 20.3% ± 16.4% with WSD0628 + RT (P = .01; ; γH2AX foci/nuclei for vehicle 3.2% ± 4.4% and WSD0628 alone 1.5% ± 1.1%). Taken together, the PK and PD results indicate that WSD0628 levels achieved in GBM43 orthotopic tumors are sufficient to robustly suppress the RT-induced DNA damage signaling.

## Interaction of Radiation Schedule in Combination With WSD0628
The in vivo studies were extended with several different radiation fractionation regimens. Using the linear-quadratic model to define equivalent radiation fractionation schemes and assuming an a/b ratio of 10 for tumor cells, a single 12.5 Gy radiation dose is predicted to be equally effective as 2.3 Gy × 10 fractions. Therefore, both radiation schedules were evaluated in combination with 7.5 mg/kg WSD0628 co-treatment in GBM43 and GBM12. Similar to the initial study with GBM43, the combination of daily WSD0628 with 10 fractions of radiation resulted in significant survival extension as compared to radiation only (143 vs. 44 days, respectively; P < .0001), WSD0628 alone, or vehicle ( and ). In GBM12, an even more profound survival benefit was observed with WSD0628 combined with 2.3 Gy × 10 fractions as compared to radiation only (408 vs. 55 days, respectively; P < .0001; ). In GBM43, WSD0628 combined with the single 12.5 Gy fraction provided a significant extension in survival as compared to 12.5 Gy alone (234 vs. 48 days, respectively; P = .03). Interestingly in GBM12, 12.5 Gy alone was markedly more effective than 2.3 Gy × 10 fractions (284 vs. 55 days, respectively; P = .001), and the addition of WSD0628 to 12.5 Gy did not further extend survival (323 days; P = .33 in comparison to 12.5 Gy alone; and ). The collective data demonstrate robust radiosensitizing effects of WSD0628 in vivo across 4 different PDX models.
 Brain metastases often are treated with a single, high dose of radiation using radiosurgery. Therefore, we evaluated the integration of WSD0628 with a “radiosurgical” dose of radiation (12.5 Gy) in a melanoma brain metastatic PDX—M12. Radiation alone significantly extended median survival compared to sham treatment (39 and 17 days, respectively; P = .004; ). Because the duration of DNA repair may extend beyond 24 hours following high doses of radiation, WSD0628 was dosed just prior to radiation and then 24 hours later. This combination resulted in profound prolongation in survival with 4 of 5 mice euthanized at 190 days without clinical signs of tumor burden (P = .002 relative to RT only), and H&E analysis of brains in this study demonstrated tumor control in all mice treated with combination therapy ( ; Supplementary Table 4). In these combination therapy mice, a small number of residual melanoma cells were admixed with numerous melanin-laden macrophages (melanophages), highlighted by CD68 immunostain. Within this “scar” region, modest GFAP staining intensity was consistent with reactive gliosis ( ). To assess the potential impact of radiation combined with WSD0628 on neuronal toxicity, the density of NeuN-positive neurons was quantitated in the contralateral basal ganglia and compared to untreated athymic nude mice. This demonstrated no significant difference in NeuN-positive cells across the 3 treatment groups (P = .36 between RT alone vs WSD0628 + RT; /F). Collectively, these data highlight the potential for profound enhancement of radiation sensitivity without notable enhancement in neuronal toxicity.

## Discussion
RT is a critical component of treatment for GBM and provides a meaningful survival benefit for many patients. However, intrinsic radioresistance limits ultimate RT efficacy with the majority of GBM recurring within the RT field. Previous radiation dose-escalation studies have not consistently demonstrated enhanced local control. To respect the radiation tolerances for normal brain tissue, these dose-escalation studies used highly constrained radiation target volumes to spare normal tissues, but this likely resulted in a portion of bulk tumors not being adequately treated with the highest-dose volume. As an alternative strategy to maintain a therapeutic window, numerous pharmaceutical companies are developing DDR inhibitors that can be combined with radiation to disrupt the efficient repair of radiation damage and enhance cytotoxicity to tumor cells. Based on the premise that dysregulation of DNA repair underpins the genomic instability of tumors, further pharmacologic disruption of DNA repair pathways might differentially sensitize tumors vs. normal tissues to RT. With this goal in mind, multiple potent ATM and DNA-PK kinase inhibitors have been developed. In this manuscript, we describe the novel brain-penetrant ATM inhibitor WSD0628. This drug is a relatively selective inhibitor of ATM signaling and provides robust radiosensitizing effects both in vitro and in vivo without evidence for increased late radiation toxicity in the brain.
The ATM kinase is a key orchestrator of the cellular response to DNA DSBs. Within minutes of radiation exposure, ATM is recruited and activated at sites of DNA damage and subsequently phosphorylates numerous downstream targets. These alterations ultimately control multiple signaling pathways modulating DNA repair, DNA replication, apoptosis, and cell cycle progression. Homozygous germline inactivation of ATM results in the Ataxia-Telangiectasia syndrome, which is characterized by degeneration of cerebellar Purkinje cells, subcutaneous capillary dilations termed telangiectasias, and profound sensitivity of epithelial tissues to radiation. Ataxia-Telangiectasia patients are predisposed to malignancies, but RT is rarely used due to extreme radiosensitivity of rapidly proliferating epithelial tissues in the aerodigestive tract and skin. , Importantly, not all normal tissues are sensitized by loss of ATM function; neurons in juvenile ATM knockout mice are actually more radio-resistant than corresponding wild-type mice. , In tumors, genetic inactivation of ATM results in profoundly enhanced radiation sensitivity, and this observation led to the development of small molecule ATM kinase inhibitors. Wayshine Biopharma, EMD Serono, and AstraZeneca all have active ATM inhibitor programs with clinical lead compounds (WSD0628, M4076, and AZD1390, respectively). All 3 agents are sub-nanomolar, selective inhibitors of ATM kinase activity and are potent radiosensitizers both in vitro and in vivo. Similar to studies with AZD1390, WSD0628 robustly radiosensitized the 3 TP53-mutant GBM PDXs tested here, and we hypothesize that TP53 mutation may be a useful predictive biomarker for radiosensitizing effects of ATM inhibitors. However, the differential effects on Chk2 signaling of RT/WSD0628 and RT/AZD1390 in wild-type versus ATM knockout cells highlight the potential for variable biological effects across tumor lines and has stimulated our ongoing efforts to evaluate the radiosensitizing effects of WSD0628 in over 40 orthotopic PDXs to better refine potential predictive biomarkers.
WSD0628 was intentionally optimized for enhanced brain penetration. Many lipophilic small molecule kinase inhibitors are substrates for the BCRP and MDR1 efflux transporters that are active at the BBB. Therefore, screening for BCRP and MDR1 liability was integral to the medicinal chemistry approach used to identify WSD0628. This yielded a free fraction of WSD0628 in brain vs. plasma (Kp,uu) of 0.44 (rat) and 0.3 (mouse), which compares favorably to AZD1390 (0.17 and 0.04, respectively). , The cross-species brain penetration for WSD0628 provides confidence that this will translate to favorable brain distribution in humans. Notably, the M4076 ATM inhibitor from EMD Serono was not optimized for brain distribution and is not suitable for therapy in GBM.
Other medicinal chemistry parameters that were considered in WSD0628 development included minimizing the risk of prolonged cardiac depolarization by evaluating possible effects on the human Ether-a-go-go-related Gene (hERG) and unfavorable metabolism by AO. Using standard in vitro bioassays, WSD0628 has a more favorable profile with essentially no hERG liability (IC50 > 30 mM vs. 6.6 mM for AZD1390) and minimal metabolism by AO with a CLint in human liver cytosol of 0.58 mL/min/mg protein (t1/2 >1000 min) as compared to a CLint of 3.5 mL/min/mg protein (t1/2 =199 min) for AZD1390. In fact, the AO liability for the first-generation AstraZeneca ATM inhibitor, AZD0156, is associated with a 10-fold lower exposure in humans as compared to predictions, and preliminary PK data with AZD1390 suggest similar issues with exposure. Similarly, clinical development of M3541 from EMD was discontinued due to a lack of dose-proportionality to exposure in PK studies. In contrast, WSD0628 has excellent medicinal chemistry features and is a promising and highly potent ATM inhibitor.
The data presented in this manuscript provide the basis for the first-in-human evaluation of WSD0628 combined with radiation in recurrent high-grade glioma (NCT05917145). Based on the viability and relatively normal early development of ATM knockout mice and Ataxia-Telangiectasia patients, we anticipate that short-term ATM inhibition during RT will have minimal ATM-mediated systemic toxicities, and the major, on-target, dose-limiting toxicities will be related to enhanced acute radiation effects. Consistent with this, preclinical testing in our laboratory confirmed profound enhancement in radiation-induced oral mucositis in mice co-treated with AZD1390 or WSD0628. , In contrast to most other peripheral tumors with intimate adjacency to various epithelial tissues, anatomic separation between an intracranial tumor and essentially all adjacent epithelial tissues (skin, nasal and nasopharyngeal mucosa, cornea, and inner, middle, and external ear) enables minimizing radiation exposure using standard intensity-modulated RT to prevent enhanced radiosensitivity to these normal tissues. ATM inhibitors exert their radiosensitizing effects primarily through inhibition of homologous recombination-mediated repair, which is only active in cells transiting S or G2/M phases of the cell cycle. Thus, non-proliferative neural tissues are likely relatively resistant to the radiosensitizing effects of ATM inhibitors. Unfortunately, preclinical models for late radiation-induced toxicities may have limited direct translational relevance. Therefore, in lieu of extensive preclinical modeling, more conservative radiation planning constraints are used in our clinical trial to limit the cumulative radiation doses being delivered both to critical CNS structures (optic nerve, optic chiasm, hippocampus, and brain stem) and to adjacent epithelial tissue (skin, oral cavity, inner ear, nasopharynx). This strategy of refining allowable radiation exposures for critical normal tissues is an important concept for the development of all novel agents with potent radiosensitizing effects in normal tissues.

## Conclusions
WSD0628 is a brain-penetrant ATM inhibitor with potent radiosensitizing effects in GBM cell lines and PDX models. Based on the results presented, this drug is being evaluated in combination with RT in patients with recurrent high-grade gliomas.
