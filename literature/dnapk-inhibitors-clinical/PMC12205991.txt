## Abstract
Poly(ADP-ribose) polymerase inhibitors (PARPi) have greatly improved survival of cancer patients harboring BRCA1 mutations. However, therapy resistance develops via either restoration of homologous recombination or replication fork stabilization. Therapeutic targets to overcome PARPi resistance are critically needed. We identified the deubiquitinase USP37 as a key determinant of PARPi toxicity in BRCA1-deficient cells via whole-genome CRISPR screens. USP37 ablation enhanced PARPi sensitivity in BRCA1-deficient cells and also overcame PARPi resistance due to 53BP1 loss. USP37 interacts with and deubiquitinates replication protein A (RPA) at stalled replication forks to limit excessive RPA accumulation, progressive RPA exhaustion, and the conversion of RPA-coated single-stranded DNAs to DNA double-strand breaks. Moreover, USP37 limits helicase-like transcription factor (HLTF) accumulation at replication forks and thus prevents MRE11-dependent fork degradation upon replication stress. Depletion of HLTF reversed the replication-associated damage observed in USP37 knockout cells. Our data suggest that USP37 protects replication fork stability by counteracting HLTF function and promotes survival of BRCA1-deficient cells, making it a promising drug target to overcome PARPi resistance in BRCA1-deficient tumors.

## Introduction
Germline mutations in BRCA1 or BRCA2 predispose patients to hereditary breast and ovarian cancers as well as other cancers such as pancreatic and prostate cancer [ , ]. BRCA1 and BRCA2 play critical roles in promoting homologous recombination (HR), which helps precisely repair DNA double-strand breaks (DSBs) that arise either from normal DNA replication or in response to various DNA damage agents [ ]. BRCA proteins also protect replication forks by preventing stalled replication forks from nuclease attack upon replication stress [ ].
BRCA1- and BRCA2-deficient cells are especially sensitive to poly(ADP-ribose) polymerase (PARP) inhibitors (PARPi) because PARPi can trap PARP1 on DNA, which interferes with DNA replication and promotes fork collapse into DSBs that cannot be repaired due to HR deficiency in these cells [ ]. The milestone discovery of synthetic lethality between PARPi and BRCA deficiency led to the approval of several PARPi by the US Food and Drug Administration for the treatment of metastatic breast cancer and advanced ovarian cancer carrying deleterious BRCA mutations [ ].
Although clinical benefit of PARP inhibitors was observed in a broad population of patients harboring BRCA1/2 mutations, most of these patients eventually develop resistance, which poses a significant obstacle to patient care [ ]. There are several mechanisms of PARPi resistance. The most common one is the restoration of HR capacity through genetic reversion mutations, which lead to the expression of partially functional BRCA1 or BRCA2 proteins and thereby render tumors resistant to PARPi [ , ]. Alternatively, reacquisition of DNA end resection capability due to the loss of 53BP1 or its downstream end resection-associated factors including RIF1, REV7, shieldin complex, and CTC1–STN1–TEN1 complex, is another mechanism of PARPi resistance specifically in BRCA1-deficient tumors [ ]. Moreover, stabilization of replication forks from nucleolytic degradation also confers PARPi resistance, and this process is thought to be independent of HR [ , ]. Diminished trapping of PARP1 through PARP1 mutations or loss of PARG in PARPi-treated cells also confers PARPi resistance [ , ]. Additional PARPi resistance mechanisms may be due to increased expression of P-glycoprotein efflux pumps [ , ]. Thus, there is an urgent need to identify novel drug targets that can be used to overcome PARPi resistance.
Using CRISPR-based whole-genome screening in an engineered BRCA1-deficient cell line, we identified the deubiquitinase USP37 as a key determinant of PARPi toxicity upon BRCA1 loss. USP37 ablation reduces viability and enhances PARPi sensitivity, probably by destabilizing replication fork in BRCA1-deficient cells. Additionally, USP37 depletion also partially overcome 53BP1-dependent PARPi resistance. Mechanistically, USP37 interacts with and deubiquitinates RPA at unstable replication fork to prevent RPA exhaustion caused replication catastrophe. Loss of USP37 leads to high vulnerabilities upon cells challenged with HU or ATR/CHK1 inhibitors induced replication stress, exhibiting excessive accumulation of RPA at stalled forks that coupled with increased DSBs, demonstrating the role of USP37 in suppressing replication-associated damage. Moreover, by another CRISPR screening in USP37 KO cells upon ATR inhibition, we found that further depletion of helicase-like transcription factor (HLTF) reverses the reduced replication speed, the sensitivity to replication stress, excessive accumulation of RPA at chromatin, and coupled DSBs in USP37 KO cells. USP37 limits HLTF accumulation at replication fork, which may restrict its fork reversal activity and prevent fork degradation by MRE11 upon replication stress. Accordingly, depletion of HLTF could rescue the USP37 loss-induced cell lethality, enhanced sensitivity to PARPi, and unstable replication forks in BRCA1-deficient cells. Taken together, we showed that USP37 suppresses replication-associated damage by counteracting HLTF function at stalled replication forks. We speculate that USP37 is a promising drug target that can be used to overcome PARPi resistance in BRCA1-deficient tumors.

## Materials and methods

## Cell culture
HeLa and HEK293A cells were purchased from the American Type Culture Collection (Manassas, VA, USA) and Thermo Fisher Scientific separately and were cultured in Dulbecco Modified Eagle Medium containing 10% fetal calf serum at 37°C with 5% CO2. SUM149PT cells were cultured in Ham F-12 Nutrient Mixture (Sigma–Aldrich) supplemented with 5% fetal bovine serum (Sigma–Aldrich), 10 mM HEPES, 1 μg/ ml hydrocortisone, and 5 μg/ml insulin. hTERT-RPE1 P53−/− and hTERT-RPE1 P53−/−BRCA1−/− cells were obtained from Dr Daniel Durocher’s laboratory and were cultured in Dulbecco Modified Eagle Medium containing 10% fetal calf serum at 37°C with 5% CO2.
CRISPR-Cas9 technology was used to generate KO cells. In brief, single guide RNAs (sgRNAs) targeting indicated genes were ligated into pLenti-V2 plasmid and transfected to indicated cell lines. After puromycin selection, cells were seeded into 96-well plate to pick up single clones. Correct single clones were subjected to western blot detection and confirmed by genome sequencing. sgRNA sequence used were listed:
USP37 sgRNA: AATGTGGTGCTTCGACCCAG
HTLF sgRNA: GTTGGACTACGCTATTACAC
53BP1 sgRNA: TTGTTCAGGACAGTCTTTCC

## AID-tagging and dTAG-tagging of endogenous BRCA1
The CRISPR/Cas9 technology was used to knock in the BSD-mAID-EGFP tag in HeLa cells and BSD-FKBP1236V in HEK293A cells at the N-terminus of endogenous BRCA1 to obtain, respectively, the HeLa BRCA1 mAID and HEK293A BRCA1 degradation tag (dTAG) cells. For HeLa BRCA1 mAID cells, HeLa cells were first established with Tet-inducible expression of OsTIR1. Next, HeLa OsTIR1 cells were co-transfected with BRCA1 NKI gRNA containing PX330 plasmid and a BRCA1 knock-in donor vector (PUC19 backbone) containing blasticidin resistance selection gene, P2A self-cleavage site, mini-AID (mAID), and EGFP flanked by ∼1 kb of homology arms. For HEK293A BRCA1 dTAG cells, HEK293A cells were co-transfected with BRCA1 NKI gRNA-containing PX330 plasmid and a BRCA1 knock-in donor vector (PUC19 backbone) containing blasticidin resistance selection gene, P2A self-cleavage site, and FKBP1236V flanked by ∼1 kb of homology arms. After blasticidin (10 μg/ml) selection, the remained cells were seeded in 96-well plates, and positive clones were first screened by genomic PCR and further validated via western blot analysis. The BRCA1 NKI gRNA sequence was as follows: 5′-ATATATCTAAGCATTTGCAA-3′.

## CellTiter-Glo assay
A total of 1000–2000 cells in a volume of 150 μl were plated into each well of 96-well plates on day 0. At Day 1, serially diluted drugs in 50 μl of medium were added to the cells in technical triplicates. After 4 days of incubation, cell viability was measured using a CellTiter-Glo luminescent cell viability assay (G7572; Promega, Madison, WI) with a BioTek Synergy™ 2 multi-mode microplate reader.

## Clonogenic survival assay
For HEK293A cells, 5000 cells were seeded onto six-well plates in triplicate 1 day before the addition of NEG-V1 or dTAG-V1. For HeLa cells, 200–600 indicated cells used in the study were seeded onto six-well plates in triplicate and treated with various doses of indicated drugs. After 10–14 days incubation, colonies were fixed and stained with 0.5% crystal violet. Relative cell viability was measured using ImageJ or counted manually.

## Immunofluorescence staining
Immunofluorescence was carried out as described previously [ ]. Briefly, cells seeded on glass coverslips were first fixed in 3% formaldehyde for 10 min on ice, followed by permeabilization with 0.5% Triton X-100 solution for 15 min at room temperature. For RPA2 immunostaining, cells were first pre-extracted using pre-extraction buffer (10 mM PIPES at pH 6.8, 100 mM sodium chloride, 300 mM sucrose, 3 mM magnesium chloride, 1 mM ethylene glycol tetraacetic acid (EGTA), 0.2% Triton-X100) for 5 min on ice before fixation and permeabilization. Cells were then blocked with 3% bovine serum albumin for 1 h and then incubated with the indicated primary antibodies for 2 h at 37°C or overnight at 4°C, followed by secondary antibody incubation, including Alexa 488 or Alexa 555, for 1 h at room temperature. After being washed with phosphate buffered saline with 0.05% Tween 20 (PBST) three times, coverslips were mounted using DAPI-containing anti-fade solution (Thermo Fisher Scientific).

## Whole-genome CRISPR gRNA screening
CRISPR screens, using the Toronto Knock Out Library v3 (TKOv3) gRNA library, were conducted as described previously [ ]. Briefly, HeLa BRCA1 mAID cells or HeLa WT and USP37 KO cells were infected with the TKOv3 library lentiviruses at a low multiplicity of infection (<0.3). At 24 h after infection, cells were replaced with fresh medium containing 2 μg/ml puromycin. After 2 days of selection, 30 million cells were collected for T0, and then the remaining cells were split into different groups with two replicates containing at least 30 million cells in each group. For HeLa BRCA1 mAID cells, cells were divided into four groups that incubated with DMSO, olaparib (25 nM), 5-Ph-IAA (0.5 mM) + doxycycline (1 μg/ml), or 5-Ph-IAA (0.5 mM) + doxycycline (1 μg/ml) + olaparib (25 nM); for HeLa WT and USP37 KO cells, each group of cells was incubated with dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) and ATRi (0.2 μM). All cells with the indicated treatment for the screens were passaged every 3 days, and at least 30 million cells were maintained for further culture. At day 21 (T21), 30 million cells of each group and each replicate were collected, and genomic DNA was extracted from T0 and T21 using a QIAamp Blood Maxi Kit (Qiagen). The inserted gRNAs were amplified via PCR using primers harboring Illumina TruSeq adapters with i5 and i7 barcodes. The resulting libraries were sequenced on Illumina NextSeq 500 High Output platform to determine gRNA representations. The sequencing results were analyzed by a model-based analysis of genome-wide CRISPR/Cas9 KO (MAGeCK) and Drug Z algorithm [ ].

## Native BrdU assay
Cells cultured on coverslips were first pulse-labeled with 10 μM BrdU (Sigma–Aldrich) for 15 min before exposure to 4 mM HU for 3 h. After two times phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) washes, cells were permeabilized with 0.5% Triton X-100 for 5 min at 4 °C and then fixed with 3% paraformaldehyde for 10 min at room temperature. After three PBS washes, the fixed cells were incubated sequentially with anti-BrdU antibody (BD Biosciences) for 1 h and Alexa Fluor 555-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG (Thermo Fisher Scientific) for 1 h. After washing with 1× PBST three times, coverslips were mounted using ProLong Gold Antifade Mountant (Thermo Fisher Scientific).

## DNA fiber assay
DNA fiber analyses were carried out as reported previously, with minor modification [ ]. Briefly, cells were first pulse-labeled with 25 μM IdU for 30 min, then pulse-labeled with 200 μM CldU for 30 min with different treatments. Cells were harvested and resuspended in PBS for a final concentration of 1500 cells/μl and then lysed in lysis buffer (200 mM Tris–HCl, pH 7.5, 50 mM ethylene diamine tetraacetie acid (EDTA), 0.5% sodium dodecyl sulfate). DNA fibers were stretched onto positively charged glass slides and fixed in 3:1 methanol:acetic acid. For immunostaining of the DNA fibers, DNA was first denatured with 2.5 M HCl for 1 h, then washed with PBS three times and blocked with 5% bovine serum albumin at 37°C for 1 h. The newly replicated ldU and CIdU tracks were incubated with mouse-anti-BrdU (1:200, 347580, BD Biosciences) and rat-anti-BrdU (1:500, Ab6326, Abcam) primary antibodies at 37°C for 2 h, followed by incubating with Alexa Fluor 546 goat-anti-mouse IgG1 (1:500, A-21123, Thermo Fisher Scientific) and Alexa Fluor 488 goat-anti-rat (1:500, A-11006, Thermo Fisher Scientific) secondary antibodies at 37°C for 1 h. After washing with PBST three times, coverslips were mounted using ProLong Gold Antifade Mountant (Thermo Fisher Scientific).

## Metaphase spreads
Cells were treated with 0.5 μg/ml of nocodazole for 3 h and then harvested. Cells were swelled in 75 mM KCl for 30 min at 37°C and fixed in ice-cold 3:1 methanol:acetic acid (v/v) at −20°C. Samples were dropped on glass slides and air-dried. For visualizing chromosomal aberrations, slides were first washed with PBS three times and then dehydrated in a series of 70%, 90%, and 100% ethanol. After being air-dried, slides were mounted in DAPI-containing ProLong Gold mounting medium (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Metaphases were imaged on using a 40X objective. More than 20 spreads were analyzed per sample per repeat.

## Immunoprecipitations
Cells were transfected with the indicated plasmids and harvested 48 h after transfection. The collected cells were lysed in NETN buffer (20 mM Tris–HCl, pH 8.0, 1 mM EDTA, 150 mM NaCl, 0.5% NP-40, with phosphatase and proteinase inhibitors) for 30 min and centrifuged at 14 000 rpm for 15 min at 4°C. The supernatant was incubated with streptavidin-conjugated beads (Amersham) for 2 h at 4°C. The beads were then washed with NETN buffer for four times, boiled in Laemmli buffer, and analyzed by immunoblotting.

## DR-GFP reporter assay
U2OS cells stably expressing HR reporter DR‐GFP were gifts from Dr Guang Peng lab in MD Anderson Cancer Center. Briefly, 1 × 106 U2OS DR‐GFP cells were transfected with 50 nmol indicated small interfering RNAs (siRNAs) (control siRNA, BRCA1 siRNA, USP37 siRNA all from OriGene Technologies) with RNAiMAX. Twenty-four hours later, 1 μg of I‐SceI expression plasmid (pCBASce) was transfected into the cells with X-tremeGENE™ HP DNA Transfection Reagent. Cells were cultured for another 48 h and subjected to flow cytometry analysis to determine the percentage of GFP‐positive cells.

## Chromatin extraction
Cells treated with CHK1i or HU were collected and then lysed with 10 volumes of NTEN buffer with low salt (20 mM Tris–HCl, pH 8.0, 10 mM NaCl, 1.5 mM MgCl2, 1 mM EDTA, 0.5% Nonidet P-40, 20 mM NaF, 1 mM Na3VO4, 1 mg/ml aprotinin, and 1 mg/ml pepstatin) at 4°C for 1 h, then centrifuged at 15 000 rpm for 15 min. Supernatant was removed, and the remaining was the chromatin pellet. The chromatin-enriched pellet was washed with PBS three times and resuspended in 0.2 M HCl for 30 min on ice to extract the protein from chromatin. The resultant soluble extract was 1:1 neutralized with 1M Tris–HCl (pH 8.5) and collected for further western blot analysis.

## Flow cytometry
Cells for cell cycle analysis were treated or untreated with ATRi for 24 h and pulse-labeled with 10 μM EdU for 30 min immediately before fixation with 3% paraformaldehyde. After fixation, cells were permeabilized with 0.5% Triton X-100 in PBS for 15 min and blocked with 3% bovine serum albumin in PBS for 30 min. Cells were then incubated with Click-iT cell reaction buffer (Invitrogen) for 30 min. After washed with PBS, cells were stained with FxCycle™ PI/RNase Staining Solution (Thermo Fisher Scientific) for 30 min, followed by flow cytometry analysis.

## Statistical analyses
All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism version 8.2.1. The number of replicates statistical tests applied for each analysis are included in the figure legends. P values were derived from a one-way ANOVA or two-way ANOVA with Tukey’s multiple comparisons test. Significance was set at P ≤ .05 for all experiments. *P < .05, **P < .01, ***P < .001, ****P < .0001.

## Results

## USP37 depletion displays synthetic lethality with BRCA1 loss
To explore the genetic determinants of responses to PARPi in BRCA1-deficient cells, we first generated a HeLa derivative inducible BRCA1-depletion cell line using AID technology similar to what we reported previously [ ]. Briefly, the F-box protein OsTIR1 was inducibly expressed with doxycycline in HeLa cells, which would be integrated into an endogenous Skp1-Cullin-F-box ubiquitin E3 ligase complex that recognizes the AID degron in an auxin-dependent manner. We inserted a mAID tag to the N-terminus of endogenous BRCA1 with the help of an HR-dependent CRISPR knock-in strategy ( and B). With the addition of auxin derivative 5-Ph-IAA (indole-3-acetic acid) and doxycycline, we were able to achieve depletion of endogenous BRCA1 ( ). As expected, we found that BRCA1 depletion led to reduction of cell survival and increased sensitivity to PARP inhibitor olaparib ( and E).
Next, we carried out unbiased whole-genome CRISPR screens using the TKOv3 lentiviral library, as described previously [ ], in this engineered cell line upon conditional BRCA1 depletion with or without treatment with PARPi (Fig. ). The drugZ algorithm [ ] was used to identify both positive and negative regulators of cell lethality and sensitivity to olaparib induced by BRCA1 loss, in which negative scores indicate that loss of a given gene expression leads to more sensitive (negative regulators) to cell lethality, and positive scores indicate resistance (positive regulators) to cell lethality induced by these treatments. As shown in Fig. and , RNASEH2A, RNASEH2B, PARP1, and XRCC1 were the top-ranked genes showing synthetic lethality with BRCA1 loss in response to olaparib, while 53BP1 and ATMIN were the top-ranked genes showing synthetic survival under these conditions, which is consistent with previous studies [ , , ]. When we examined the genes that showed synthetic lethality with BRCA1 loss alone (Fig. and ), most genes, such as RNASEH2A, RNASEH2B, XRCC1, 53BP1, and ATMIN, exhibited the same or similar phenotypes as those in the olaparib-treated and BRCA1-depleted group, implying that BRCA1 loss and BRCA1 loss plus treatment with PARPi may share the same mechanism in regulating cell survival, i.e. treatment with PARPi enhances cell lethality caused by BRCA1 depletion.
 To uncover potential targets for the treatment of PARPi-resistant BRCA1-deficient tumors, we paid attention to the genes that, when loss, could eliminate BRCA1-deficient cells or enhance PARPi sensitivity in BRCA1-depleted cells. We identified the deubiquitinase USP37 as a potential target, since USP37 loss conferred both reduced cell survival and hypersensitivity to olaparib in BRCA1-depleted cells (Fig. and  and and ). These data suggest synthetic lethality relationship between USP37 loss and BRCA deficiency.
We next employed several paired isogenic cell lines to determine whether indeed USP37 loss selectively impacts cell survival or PARPi sensitivity in BRCA1-deficient cells. We generated USP37 knockout (KO) in HeLa BRCA1 mAID cells (Fig. and  ) and confirmed that USP37 KO led to increased cell lethality and hypersensitivity to olaparib when BRCA1 was depleted (Fig. and F). Additionally, we created another inducible BRCA1 depletion cell line using HEK293A cells by introducing the dTAG, which utilizes a selective VHL-recruiting dTAG molecule, i.e. dTAGV-1, for the rapid degradation of FKBP12F36V-tagged proteins [ ]. Again, we used the HR-mediated CRISPR knock-in strategy to insert the FKBP12F36V tag onto the N-terminus of endogenous BRCA1 at its genomic loci ( ) and validated the clone by PCR and western blot analysis ( and G). We further confirmed the clone by cell survival assay and found that degradation of endogenous BRCA1 in HEK293A cells led to a significant reduction in cell survival and sensitivity to olaparib ( and I), which is similar to those observed in HeLa BRCA1 mAID cells. We then knocked out USP37 in HEK293A BRCA1 dTAG cells (Fig. and  ). As shown in Fig. and I, loss of USP37 significantly impaired proliferation in HEK293A BRCA1 inducibly depleted cells, which is consistent with results observed in HeLa BRCA1 mAID cells.
Moreover, we examined the impact of USP37 in BRCA1-mutant tumor models such as SUM149PT and UWB1.289 cells. We first infected these cells with inducible-Cas9 expression vector and then introduced USP37 sgRNA, which allows inducible depletion of USP37 in these cells. As anticipated, USP37 depletion led to significantly reduced cell proliferation in both SUM149PT cells ( ) and UWB1.289 cells ( ). Additionally, we examined the impact of siRNA-mediated USP37 depletion in hTERT-immortalized retinal pigmented epithelial (RPE1) cells, and USP37 depletion showed synthetic lethality with BRCA1 loss ( ). To test whether USP37 loss could also show synthetic lethality with BRCA2 deficiency, we knocked down USP37 with two different siRNAs in BRCA2-mutated cell line-CAPAN-1 cells ( ). We found that USP37 depletion could also significantly reduce cell proliferation in CAPAN-1 cells ( and L), suggesting that USP37 depletion induced cell lethality in either BRCA1- or BRCA2-deficient cells. There may be a shared mechanism underlying these observations.
We sought to further determine whether USP37 depletion or inhibition could potentially overcome PARPi resistance in BRCA1-deficient tumors. Because loss of 53BP1 leads to PARPi resistance in BRCA1-deficient cells by partially restoring HR [ , ], we knocked out 53BP1 in HeLa BRCA1 mAID cells and confirmed that 53BP1 loss led to PARPi resistance when BRCA1 was depleted ( ). When we further knocked out USP37 in 53BP1 KO cells and induced BRCA1 degradation, we observed partial restoration of PARPi sensitivity in 53BP1&USP37 dual-knockout (DKO) cells when compared with 53BP1 KO in HeLa BRCA1 mAID cells ( ), suggesting that USP37 inhibition could at least partially restore PARPi efficacy against the resistance mechanism due to 53BP1 loss. Nevertheless, USP37 KO led to dramatic PARPi sensitivity in BRCA1-depleted cells ( ), indicating that mechanistically, USP37 and 53BP1 may operate in distinct pathways.
Collectively, our data revealed that loss of USP37 is synthetic lethal with BRCA1 deficiency. USP37 is a potential therapeutic target that can be used for the treatment of both PARPi-sensitive and PARPi-resistant BRCA1-deficient cancers.

## USP37 protects replication forks against uncontrolled degradation of nascent DNA
To determine how USP37 affects cell proliferation in BRCA1-deficient cells, we characterized DNA lesions arisen from USP37 loss in BRCA1-mutant cells. We examined DNA damage signaling in USP37&BRCA1 DKO cells with or without treatment with PARPi. As shown in Fig. and B, in BRCA1 wild type (WT; referred to as BRCA1+/+) cells, under non-treated or olaparib-treated conditions, cells with USP37 depletion displayed mildly increased γH2AX staining compared with WT cells. However, when BRCA1 was degraded (referred to as BRCA1−/−), cells with USP37 depletion displayed significantly increased γH2AX signaling compared with control cells (Fig. and B). γH2AX signaling was further elevated when cells were treated with olaparib for 48 h, indicating increased DNA damage (Fig. and B). We also quantified the percentage of cells with micronuclei, and we found that depletion of USP37 in BRCA1−/− cells induced many more micronuclei than that observed in WT cells, especially when cells were treated with olaparib (Fig. ). Moreover, we found that depletion of USP37 in BRCA1−/− cells induced many more chromosome abnormalities such as chromosome breaks or radical chromosome ( and B). These data suggest that USP37 deficiency would induce genomic instability, especially when BRCA1 is absent.
 We sought to understand the mechanisms underlying genomic instability observed in USP37 KO cells. One possible explanation is that USP37 KO cells may be defective in HR repair, as previously reported [ ]. We carried out HR reporter assay in U2OS DR-GFP cell lines and found that knocking down USP37 could significantly decrease HR efficiency using this reporter assay ( ), which is consistent with the previous report. However, when we examined RAD51 foci formation in BRCA1-proficient and -deficient conditions, we found that upon treatment with olaparib, cells with loss of USP37 showed no difference in RAD51 foci formation compared with WT cells (Fig. and E). We also examined RAD51 foci formation in HeLa WT and USP37 KO cells together with geminin staining upon ionizing radiation (IR) treatment. We found that USP37 KO cells showed only mild decreased RAD51 foci formation ( and E). Moreover, we examined whether depletion of USP37 could further decrease HR efficiency when BRCA1 is absent by using the U2OS DR-GFP reporter cell line ( and G). We found that both BRCA1 and USP37 knockdown could decrease HR efficiency, and knocking down BRCA1 has a more pronounced HR defect than knocking down USP37, which is similar to the data shown in . However, double knockdown of BRCA1 and USP37 did not show much difference when compared with knocking down BRCA1 only ( and G), indicating that depletion of both BRCA1 and USP37 together did not show a synergistic defect in HR repair. Based on our findings, we speculate that although USP37 may play a role in HR process, it may not be essential for homology-directed repair, especially under BRCA1-deficient conditions with or without treatment with olaparib or IR. Another mechanism may exist to explain the synthetic lethality relationship between USP37 and BRCA1.
BRCA1 was reported to help protect replication forks, preventing stalled replication forks from nuclease attack upon replication stress, and this function of BRCA1 is speculated to be independent of its role in HR [ ]. Thus, we thought USP37 probably could help stabilize replication forks in BRCA1-deficient cells. We then assessed the impact of USP37 depletion on pulse-labeled nascent DNA fibers in BRCA1-deficient cells upon replication stress. Nascent DNA was sequentially labeled with 5-iodo-20-deoxyuridine (IdU, red) and 5-chloro-2-deoxyuridine (CldU, green), and then cells were treated with replication inhibitor HU for 4 h. As shown in Fig. and G, BRCA1 depletion led to a significantly reduced CIdU/IdU ratio and further depletion of USP37 led to exacerbated reduced CIdU/IdU ratio, indicating that indeed USP37 may help stabilize replication forks, especially in BRCA-deficient cells.
Taken together, our data suggest that USP37 depletion induces genomic instability in BRCA1-deficient cells, which is likely due to uncontrolled degradation of nascent replication forks in response to replication stress.

## Cells with loss of USP37 show high vulnerabilities upon challenge with ATRi/CHK1i or HU-induced replication stress
Our and another group’s previous CRISPR screening results revealed that USP37 loss confers enhanced sensitivity to ataxia telangiectasia and Rad3-related (ATR) inhibitors (ATRi) and CHK1 inhibitors (CHK1i) [ , , ], which is consistent with the potential role of USP37 in replication fork stabilization mentioned above. To validate the results from these unbiased CRISPR screens, we generated HeLa derived control and USP37 KO cells ( , ) and conducted clonogenic survival assays using inhibitors against four kinases that play crucial roles in DNA damage response. As shown in Fig. , USP37 KO cells exhibited significant sensitivity to ATRi or CHK1i, but not ATM or DNAPK inhibition, which is consistent with previous CRISPR screening data [ ]. The elevated sensitivity to ATRi or CHK1i in USP37 KO cells may be due to increased DNA damage. We thus treated cells with 1 μM ATRi or CHK1i and evaluated DNA damage signaling in USP37 KO cells compared with that in WT cells at various time points during treatment. As shown in Fig. and , upon ATR or CHK1 inhibition, USP37 depletion was associated with enhanced DSB-induced DNA damage signaling, which was represented by gradually increased γH2AX level as well as increased phosphorylated KAP1 at S824, phosphorylated DNAPKs at S2056, and phosphorylated ATM at S1981. Moreover, USP37 ablation was also associated with high levels of replication protein A (RPA)2 phosphorylation at S4/S8 and S33 sites (Fig. and ), which are markers for single-strand DNA (ssDNA) and/or resected DNA repair intermediates. Furthermore, immunostaining of chromatin-binding RPA2 showed that USP37 ablation led to the excessive accumulation of RPA (Fig. and D). It was suggested ATR inhibition triggers hyperloading of RPA on S phase chromatin followed by fork breakage [ ]. Thus, our data indicate that increased levels of chromatin-loaded RPA in USP37 KO cells correlate with increased breakage of stalled forks upon ATR inhibition.
 We also determined the response of USP37 KO cells to various DNA-damaging agents. We found that USP37 KO cells showed significant sensitivity to treatment with HU but did not display any increased sensitivity to other treatments such as mitomycin C, ultraviolet, camptothecin, and olaparib, while showed only mild effects to IR (Fig.  ), suggesting that USP37 plays a specific role in response to replication stress. USP37 ablation was also associated with high levels of RPA2 phosphorylation and enhanced DSBs, as well as decreased phosphorylated CHK1 levels in response to treatment with HU but not to camptothecin (Fig. and ), again indicating that USP37 plays a specific role in replication checkpoint control. We further examined whether USP37 KO could affect cell proliferation and cell cycle distribution with or without treatment with ATRi by using EdU incorporation assay. We found that USP37 KO cells showed similar cell cycle distribution to that of WT cells but displayed significant decreased EdU intensity, which was more significant when ATR was inhibited ( ), further suggesting that loss of USP37 may affect DNA replication.
The role of USP37 in replication checkpoint control appears to be unique because it acts to maintain cell proliferation, replication checkpoint activation (i.e. CHK1 phosphorylation), and prevent DSB formation. An early study suggests that ATR may avert replication catastrophe by preventing global exhaustion of RPA, whereby ATR-CHK1 signaling ensures the maintenance of RPA surplus over ssDNA after replication stress [ ]. Our data indicate that USP37 depletion may lead to replication catastrophe upon replication stress due to excessive ssDNA and RPA accumulation at stalled forks, progressive RPA exhaustion, and finally the conversion of RPA-coated ssDNAs to DSBs.

## USP37 may be involved in RPA deubiquitination and its deubiquitination activity is required for cell survival in USP37&BRCA1 DKO cells or in response to ATR inhibition
USP37 is a deubiquitinating enzyme that plays roles in diverse cellular events, such as antagonizing the E3 ubiquitin ligase anaphase-promoting complex to remove ubiquitin chains from cyclin A and promotes S phase entry [ ], regulating chromosomal cohesion and mitotic progression by deubiquitinating WAPL [ ], promoting lung cancer cell proliferation by stabilizing C-Myc in lung cancer [ ], and regulating DNA damage response through stabilizing and deubiquitinating BLM [ ].
Based on the aforementioned observations that depletion of USP37 leads to significant accumulation of phosphorylated RPA on chromatin upon replication stress, we would like to determine whether USP37 could interact with and deubiquitinate RPA. We first conducted a co-immunoprecipitation experiment with Myc-tagged USP37 and SFB-tagged RPA1, RPA2, and RPA3, and we found that all three components of RPA complex could pull down USP37 (Fig. ), confirming an interaction between USP37 and RPA. We then performed in vivo deubiquitination assays. We co-expressed SFB-RPA2 and HA-ubiquitin (Ub) in 293T cells with Myc-tagged WT or Myc-tagged catalytic dead mutant C350S [ ] of USP37. We purified RPA2 with streptavidin beads and analyzed the associated HA-ub. As shown in Fig. , USP37 activity may be involved in RPA2 deubiquitination, because the catalytically dead mutant of USP37 C350S was unable to reduce RPA2–Ub conjugates. Moreover, when we performed the deubiquitination assays in USP37 KO cells, we found RPA2-ub conjugates increased slightly when compared with that in WT cells (Fig. ), further supporting that USP37 maybe involved in RPA2 deubiquitination.
 To determine whether the deubiquitinase activity of USP37 is required for cell survival upon ATR inhibition or BRCA1 deficiency, we reconstituted USP37 WT or C350S mutant in HeLa USP37 KO cells and measured their sensitivity to ATRi. As shown in Fig. , reconstitution with WT but not the C350S mutant of USP37 fully rescued sensitivity to ATRi. We further detected DNA damage signaling and cell death in these reconstituted cells upon treatment with ATRi. We found that again reconstitution with WT but not the catalytic inactive mutant of USP37 fully reversed increased DSB signals, as well as cell death, represented by cleaved caspase 3 and cleaved PARP1 (Fig. ). Moreover, the increased chromatin-loaded RPA signal in USP37 KO cells was significantly reduced when reconstituted with USP37 WT but not the catalytic inactive mutant, suggesting decreased breakage of stalled forks (Fig. and G).
Similarly, we also reconstituted USP37 WT or C350S mutant in HeLa BRCA1 mAID USP37 KO cells (Fig. ). When BRCA1 was degraded, reconstitution with WT but not C350S USP37 could fully rescue cell lethality in these HeLa BRCA1&USP37 DKO cells (Fig. and J).
Taken together, our data indicate that USP37 may be involved in RPA deubiquitination. Additionally, the deubiquitinase activity of USP37 is required for cell survival in USP37&BRCA1 DKO cells or upon ATR inhibition.

## HLTF counteracts replication catastrophe induced by USP37 loss
To further understand mechanistically how USP37 KO cells are sensitized to ATRi-induced replication stress, we conducted another CRISPR screen (Fig. ). The drugZ algorithm was again used to analyze the negative and positive regulators of USP37 KO cells in response to ATR inhibition. Consistent with previous CRISPR screen data [ ], we found that loss of USP37, as well as POLE3 and POLE4, in HeLa WT cells led to significant sensitivity to ATRi ( ), indicating that our screening data are reliable. We then analyzed the screening data in USP37 KO cells upon treatment with ATRi and found that HLTF ranked as the top hit that could rescue cell lethality caused by USP37 loss upon ATR inhibition (Fig. and ). HLTF also promoted cell proliferation in USP37 KO cells without treatment with ATRi (Fig. and ), but this effect was not as prominent as that observed in cells treated with ATRi.
 To validate our screening results, we knocked out HLTF in HeLa USP37 KO cells and determined whether USP37 KO-induced replication catastrophe could be reversed by HLTF depletion (Fig. and  ). The CellTiter-Glo assay showed that depletion of HLTF in USP37 KO cells could fully rescue sensitivity to ATRi (Fig. ), as well as HU (Fig. ). Western blot analysis revealed that enhanced RPA phosphorylation and coupled DSB-associated phosphorylation events in USP37-depleted cells upon treatment with ATRi or CHK1i were completely rescued by HLTF ablation (Fig. ). Apotosis represented by cleaved Caspase 3 and cleaved PARP1 was also rescued by HLTF ablation in USP37 KO cells ( ). Moreover, depletion of HLTF significantly reduced chromatin-loaded RPA in USP37 KO cells, suggesting that accumulated breakage of stalled forks in USP37 KO cells could be fully resolved by HLTF ablation (Fig. and I). We also checked whether the decreased cell proliferation in USP37 KO cells could be rescued by HLTF ablation. As shown in , the EdU signal was significantly increased in USP37&HLTF DKO cells when compared with USP37 KO cells, indicating a successful rescue. The decreased cell proliferation might affect replication fork replication speed; we then measured fork replication speed under non-treated condition. As shown in , USP37 deficiency significantly decreased fork replication speed, which is consistent with a previous report [ ]. However, HLTF depletion was able to restore replication fork speed in USP37 KO cells ( ). We also measured fork progression upon ATR inhibition and found that the reduced replication fork stability in USP37 KO cells could also be rescued by HLTF depletion (Fig. and K). To further determine the relationship between USP37 and HLTF, we performed chromatin fraction assay and found that HLTF was significantly enriched on chromatin in USP37 KO cells, suggesting that USP37 limits HLTF chromatin association especially upon replication stress (Fig. ).
Together, these data suggest that USP37 genetically interacts with HLTF, HLTF facilitates replication catastrophe induced by USP37 loss. USP37 loss leads to aberrant accumulation of HLTF, which may promote fork reversal and excess RPA accumulation on replication forks, ultimately leading to RPA exhaustion that converts RPA-coated ssDNAs to DSBs and triggering replication catastrophe.

## HLTF depletion rescues cell lethality upon USP37 loss in BRCA1-deficient cells
HLTF has been predominantly known for its functions in stimulating replication fork reversal in response to replication stress [ ]. Because USP37 loss leads to aberrant accumulation of HLTF on chromatin, we then examined whether USP37 KO could regulate HLTF-dependent functions at stalled replication forks. Since annealing of the extruded nascent DNA strands during replication fork reversal could produce ssDNA end on the regressed arm, which could be detected by the native 5-bromo-2′-deoxyuridine (BrdU) immunofluorescence assay [ , ], we performed native BrdU immunofluorescence assay to measure HU-induced BrdU foci formation (Fig. ). We found USP37 depletion significantly increased, and HLTF depletion significantly decreased, HU-induced BrdU foci formation (Fig. and C), while HU-induced BrdU foci formation in USP37 and HLTF double knockout cells was similar to that in WT cells (Fig. and C). These data indicate that USP37 may suppress HLTF-dependent regulation of fork stability, possibly the role of HLTF in fork reversal.
 USP37 loss led to reduced cell viability when BRCA1 was depleted (Fig. ), and this effect was exacerbated when cells were treated with PARPi. One of the potential PARPi resistance mechamisms is the restoration of fork protection in BRCA1/2 deficient cells. HLTF belongs to SNF2-family DNA translocase that remodels stalled replication forks, promotes the degradation of nascent DNA catalyzed by the MRE11 nuclease in BRCA1/2- deficient cells when treated with replication stress-inducing agents [ ]. Depletion of HLTF could restore replication fork stability and reduce genomic instability in BRCA1/2-deficient cells [ ]. Therefore, we sought to determine whether further depletion HLTF in USP37 KO cells could rescue cell lethality and sensitivity to PARPi when BRCA1 was also absent. We generated HLTF KO cells in USP37 KO cells (Fig. ) and performed colony survival assay in HeLa BRCA1 mAID cells. As shown in Fig. - , loss of USP37 led to significant cell death and PARPi sensitivity, while depletion HLTF could partially rescue the USP37 loss induced cell lethality with or without treatmentwith PARPi. Furthermore, we monitored nascent DNA degradation on single DNA fibers in BRCA1 mutated cells (SUM149PT cells) subjected to siRNA-mediated depletion of USP37 and HLTF (Fig. ). Fork degradation was then assessed by measuring the ratio of IdU to CldU tract lengths in response to 2 mM HU for 4 hours (Fig. ). As expected, depletion of HLTF significantly increased the CIdU/IdU tract length compared with si-control cells in BRCA1-deficient cells (Fig. ). Depletion of USP37 significantly reduced the CIdU/IdU tract length compared with sicontrol cells, which is consistent with the above data in HeLa BRCA1 mAID cells. Remarkably, this reduction was abolished upon co-depletion of HLTF. Further, we monitored nascent DNA degradation upon MRE11 inhibition with Mirin in siRNA-mediated USP37-depleted cells (Fig. ). We found that inhibition of MRE11 significantly increased the CIdU/IdU tract length in USP37 knock-down SUM149PT cells (Fig.  ), suggesting that USP37 loss-induced nascent fork degradation in BRCA1-deficient cells may depend on MRE11. Taken together, these results indicate that HLTF promotes MRE11-dependent nascent DNA degradation upon USP37 ablation in BRCA1-deficient cells in response to replication stress.

## Discussion
With PARPi resistance emerging in the clinic, enhancing PARPi therapy and overcoming PARPi resistance is a key need. Our findings in the current study offer a potential therapeutic target for tumors with BRCA1 deficiency, which are currently treated with PARPi. Through genome-wide CRISPR screens, we identified USP37 as a novel target in which loss of function showed synthetic lethality with BRCA1 deficiency, with or without treatment with PARPi. Mechanistically, we showed that this effect relies on USP37 deubiquitination activity, which may target RPA at stalled replication forks to prevent RPA exhaustion. In addition, USP37 also may limit fork reversal caused by factors such as HLTF and thus prevent MRE11-dependent fork degradation upon replication stress (Fig. ).
The DNA replication process is frequently challenged by endogenous and exogenous genotoxic stress, including DNA lesions, nucleotide depletion, and the presence of sequences that are difficult to replicate [ , ]. Faithful DNA replication during each cell cycle is essential for the maintenance of genome stability [ ]. Proliferating cells rely on DNA replication checkpoints to ensure orderly completion of genome duplication, and dysfunction in these checkpoints may lead to catastrophic genome disruption, including unscheduled firing of replication origins, stalling and collapse of replication forks, massive DNA breakage, and ultimately cell death [ ]. ATR kinase is essential for ensuring faithful duplication of a challenging genome [ , ]. In response to replication stress, ATR is recruited to stressed or stalled forks by RPA-coated ssDNA and activated by TOPBP1 and/or ETAA1 [ ]. Once activated, ATR and its downstream effectors, such as CHK1, play crucial roles in limiting origin firing, stabilizing stressed forks, and promoting fork restart. ATR-CHK1 signaling is required for protecting stressed replication forks via multiple mechanisms [ , , ]. One of these mechanisms is to suppress ssDNA accumulation during DNA replication. When cells are exposed to HU together with ATRi or CHK1i, very high levels of ssDNA are generated, leading to RPA exhaustion and replication catastrophe [ ], suggesting that ATR-CHK1 signaling ensures the maintenance of RPA surplus over ssDNA after replication stress [ ]. It remains unknown mechanistically how ATR-CHK1 pathway prevents RPA exhaustion following replication stress.
In the current study, we showed that USP37 depletion leads to replication catastrophe and progressive massive DSB accumulation upon replication stress, a phenotype similar to that described in cells with ATRi or CHK1i induced replication stress [ ], indicating a link between USP37 and the ATR/CHK1 pathway. Indeed, USP37-deficient cells were also sensitive to ATR or CHK1 inhibiton, which further establishes that USP37 may be involved in ATR-CHK1 pathway. The precise connection between USP37 and ATR-CHK1 pathway remains to be determined. USP37 may regulate ATR-CHK1 signaling. USP37 is a deubiquitinating enzyme that plays roles in diverse cellular events. For example, USP37 antagonizes the E3 ubiquitin ligase anaphase-promoting complex to remove ubiquitin chains from cyclin A and therefore promote S phase entry [ ]. USP37 has also been shown to regulate chromosomal cohesion and mitotic progression by deubiquitinating WAPL [ ]. In addition, USP37 could promote cell proliferation by stabilizing C-Myc in lung cancer [ ]. Moreover, USP37 may regulate DNA damage response through stabilizing and deubiquitinating BLM [ ] and/or CHK1 to promote cellular response to replication stress [ ]. Although our findings indicated that USP37 is not responsible for CHK1 stabilization as previously reported [ ], we did find that USP37 is required for sustained CHK1 activation, because USP37 depletion significantly decreased phosphorylated CHK1 levels upon treatment with HU (Fig. ). Thus, USP37 may indirectly promote ATR-CHK1 signaling upon replication stress, possibly via the ability of USP37 to limit chromatin association of HLTF and therefore prevent fork reversal and the conversion of ssDNAs into DSBs. We reason that these aberrant DSBs due to fork reversal are likely the targets of nucleases such as MRE11 and others, as previously reported [ ], which result in fork degradation and genome instability.
DNA damage tolerance pathways are crucial responses to replication stress [ ]. Replication fork reversal is one kind of DNA damage tolerance pathway to protect fork integrity during replication stress [ ]. Replication stress induces the reversal of stalled replication forks from a three-way junction into a four-way junction by coordinating the annealing of two nascent DNA strands behind the fork to form a regressed fork, or “chicken foot structure” [ ]. Different types of genotoxic stress may lead to uncoupling of DNA polymerases from DNA helicase and ssDNA accumulation, and fork reversal is thought to restrain replication fork progression and prevent ssDNA accumulation at the fork [ , , ]. Indeed, SNF2-family DNA annealing helicase, which rewinds two DNA strands (such as HLTF, SMARCAL1, and ZRANB3) in an ATP-dependent manner, participates in fork remodeling via replication fork reversal [ ]. However, fork reversal also leads to the degradation of nascent DNA by MRE11 and probably other nucleases upon replication stress. Thus, fork reversal needs to be tightly regulated at the replication fork. We speculate that one of these mechanisms is USP37-dependent removal process, which limits HLTF at stalled replication forks. In the current study, we found a strong genetic interaction between USP37 and HLTF. USP37 KO cells showed unique sensitivity to ATR inhibition, and this sensitivity is fully rescued by HLTF depletion (Fig. ), suggesting that one major function of USP37 is to counteract HLTF, probably by preventing its chromatin association. Precisely how USP37 regulates HLTF chromatin association remains to be further elucidated.
With CRISPR screens in USP37 KO cells with or without ATRi, we found that HLTF genetically interacts with USP37. Depletion of HLTF can fully rescue USP37 loss-induced replication defects such as cell proliferation, replication speed, replication fork progression, RPA exhaustion, DSB accumulation, and cell death upon treatment with ATRi. Moreover, depletion of HLTF can also rescue USP37 loss-induced cell death with or without treatment with PARPi in BRCA1-deficient cells, as represented by stabilized replication forks. We speculated that USP37 may inhibit the fork reversal activity of HLTF upon replication stress, because loss of USP37 results in significant accumulation of HLTF at chromatin. HLTF has been predominantly known for its functions in post-replication repair by acting as an E3 ubiquitin ligase for PCNA polyubiquitination and by stimulating replication fork reversal in response to replication stress [ ]. HLTF contains an ATPase domain, an E3 ubiquitin ligase domain, and a HIRAN domain. HIRAN domain binds specifically to 3′-OH ssDNA ends. HLTF’s ATPase and 3′-ssDNA binding activity, but not E3 ubiquitin ligase domain, are required for fork reversal in vitro [ ]. Our data showed that when USP37 was depleted, HLTF and RPA both were extensively accumulated at chromatin. Because USP37 may be involved in the deubiquitination of RPA, further studies may need to investigate whether HLTF could ubiquitinate RPA and whether this ubiquitination activity is required for fork reversal. Study of these processes may help elucidate the antagonized genetic interaction between USP37 and HLTF.
DNA repair factors are believed to protect stalled replication forks upon replication stress, to ensure the completion of DNA replication and maintenance of genome integrity. Indeed, besides their important roles in HR repair, the tumor suppressors BRCA1 and BRCA2 have been shown to prevent nascent DNA degradation by MRE11 nuclease upon replication stress [ , , ]. Fork reversal is also required for nascent DNA degradation, as we discussed earlier, and this was confirmed by the observation that depleting SMARCAL1, ZRANB3, and/or HLTF could inhibit nascent DNA degradation, restore fork integrity, and reduce genome instability upon replication stress in BRCA1/2-deficient cells [ ]. However, it remains unclear whether BRCA1 and BRCA2 directly participate in fork reversal. One possible explanation is that BRCA1 and BRCA2 are required for HR-mediated replication fork restart upon replication stress. In the absence of BRCA1 or BRCA2, stalled replication forks may not be restarted appropriately and therefore cells may use the fork reversal mechanism to avoid having extended ssDNA regions at stalled replication forks, which are prone to nucleolytic digestion. However, fork reversal can also result in DSBs, which are prone to degradation by MRE11 and other nucleases. In this scenario, BRCA1 and HR pathway act independent of fork reversal pathway that relies on HLTF, which is actively suppressed by a USP37-dependent process. Nevertheless, defects in either BRCA/HR-mediated replication fork restart pathway or USP37-mediated suppression of fork reversal would result in nascent fork degradation, genome instability, and cytotoxicity. Therefore, we observed the synthetic lethality between BRCA1 loss and USP37 depletion in our study (Fig. ).
Given that BRCA1 and USP37 operate in distinct pathways involved in fork protection, it is reasonable to speculate that USP37 could be a potential therapeutic target, because its loss or inhbition destabilizes replication forks in BRCA1-deficient tumors and overcomes PARPi resistance due to partial restoration of HR function via loss of 53BP1 or 53BP1-dependent pathway, as we demonstrated in the current study. Unfortunately, there are currently no available inhibitors for USP37. Future endeavors are needed to develop specific inhibitors targeting USP37, which may be used in combination with PARPi to enhance anti-tumor efficacy and overcome therapy resistance.
