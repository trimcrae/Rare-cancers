## Abstract
DNA damage response (DDR) pathways are essential for maintaining genomic integrity. Accumulating pan-cancer evidence, however, reveals that alterations within these networks act as widespread drivers of tumor initiation, progression, and therapeutic vulnerability. This review synthesizes the biological mechanisms and clinical implications of DDR defects across solid tumors. We first detail how the disruption of major DDR pathways leads to profound genomic instability and characteristic mutational heterogeneity. Building on this mechanistic foundation, we evaluate the interaction between DDR deficiencies and the tumor immune microenvironment to highlight emerging opportunities for immunotherapy. We also critically examine recent advances in targeted treatment paradigms based on synthetic lethality, focusing particularly on the evolving clinical landscape of PARP inhibitors. Finally, we link these biological insights directly to clinical practice by discussing genetic risk assessment, biomarker development, and precision therapeutic decision-making. Ultimately, a deeper understanding of these alterations is essential to develop more effective targeted and combinatorial therapies for patients with DDR-deficient malignancies.
Keywords: DNA damage response, Genomic instability, Gene mutations, Synthetic lethality, PARP inhibitors

## Introduction
Maintenance of genomic integrity is essential for normal cellular function and organismal survival [ ]. The DNA damage response (DDR) network comprises a complex system of signaling pathways responsible for detecting DNA lesions, coordinating cell cycle checkpoints, and facilitating DNA repair ( ). These pathways involve multiple specialized repair mechanisms, including homologous recombination (HR), non-homologous end joining (NHEJ), and mismatch repair (MMR). Notably, base excision repair (BER) plays a central role in processing non-bulky lesions induced by endogenous metabolic stressors, such as reactive oxygen species (ROS). Collectively, these coordinated mechanisms protect the genome from a diverse array of endogenous and exogenous damage [ ]. Defects in DDR genes can lead to the accumulation of genomic instability, a hallmark of cancer development, and contribute to tumor initiation, progression, and therapeutic resistance [ ]. Recent advances in high-throughput sequencing technologies have revealed that alterations in DDR genes are prevalent across a wide spectrum of human malignancies [ , , , , , , ]. Mutations in key DDR components such as BRCA1, BRCA2, ATM, ATR, PALB2, and mismatch repair genes not only influence cancer susceptibility but also affect tumor behavior and response to treatment. Importantly, the discovery of synthetic lethality has opened new avenues for targeted therapy, exemplified by the clinical success of poly (ADP-ribose) polymerase (PARP) inhibitors in tumors with homologous recombination deficiency (HRD) [ ]. In addition to guiding targeted therapies, DDR alterations are increasingly recognized as potential biomarkers for patient stratification and predictors of response to immunotherapy and DNA-damaging agents. As a result, understanding the biological and clinical implications of DDR gene mutations has become a critical area of cancer research. In this review, we summarize the molecular mechanisms underlying DDR gene alterations, their roles in genomic instability and tumor evolution, and their emerging clinical applications in precision oncology, including targeted therapies, biomarker development, and future therapeutic strategies.
 

## Epidemiological characteristics and molecular profiles of DDR mutations across different tumors

## Pan-cancer prevalence and distribution of DDR alterations
Genomic alterations affecting DDR pathways are widespread across human malignancies but display marked tumor lineage specificity [ ]. Large-scale pan-cancer sequencing efforts have revealed that alterations affecting DDR pathways are widespread across human malignancies but display marked tumor lineage specificity [ ] ( ). Notably, the prevalence of HR-related DDR alterations varies substantially among tumor types. Endometrial, biliary tract, bladder, and ovarian cancers exhibit some of the highest frequencies of HRR gene mutations, whereas mesenchymal tumors such as sarcomas and gastrointestinal stromal tumors display comparatively lower rates [ ]. Prostate cancer represents a particularly well-characterized example, in which DDR alterations are enriched in advanced disease stages. Germline mutations in DDR genes occur in approximately 8–12% of metastatic prostate cancers, while the combined prevalence of germline and somatic alterations may exceed 25%, highlighting the clinical relevance of DDR testing in this disease [ ]. Beyond homologous recombination genes such as BRCA1, BRCA2, ATM, and ATR, alterations in chromatin regulators and DNA replication stress response pathways also contribute to the DDR landscape. In melanoma, for instance, more than one-fifth of tumors harbor functional HRR gene mutations, frequently involving genes such as ARID1A, ATM, and FANCA [ ]. Similarly, driver-negative non-small cell lung cancer exhibits a high burden of DDR alterations, which has been associated with enhanced sensitivity to platinum-based chemotherapy [ ]. Importantly, emerging evidence suggests that DDR alterations are not only markers of genomic instability but also determinants of therapeutic vulnerability [ ]. Tumors harboring DDR defects often exhibit increased tumor mutational burden, altered mutational signatures, and enhanced responsiveness to DNA-damaging agents and targeted therapies.
 

## Germline and somatic DDR alterations across populations
Accumulating evidence from population-scale genomic studies indicates that both germline and somatic alterations in DDR genes exhibit substantial ethnic and geographic variability [ , ]. These differences are shaped by population genetic background, founder mutations, and environmental exposures, collectively contributing to heterogeneous cancer susceptibility and therapeutic responses across populations. Population-specific germline variants in DDR genes have been widely reported. For instance, analyses of hereditary breast and ovarian cancer (HBOC) cohorts in Brazil reported BRCA1/2 germline mutation rates of approximately 21.5%, with several recurrent variants accounting for a significant proportion of identified mutations [ ]. Likewise, studies in Middle Eastern breast cancer patients have identified multiple recurrent BRCA1/2 variants that may represent potential founder mutations within this population [ ]. These findings highlight the importance of population-specific mutation spectra in guiding genetic testing strategies. Beyond germline variants, ethnic differences are also evident in the somatic mutation landscape of multiple tumor types. Comparative genomic analyses have shown that African American patients with triple-negative breast cancer exhibit higher frequencies of TP53 mutations but lower rates of PIK3CA alterations compared with European American patients [ ]. Similar disparities have been observed in gastric cancer, where several driver genes—including APC, ARID1A, PIK3CA, and PTEN—display significantly lower mutation frequencies in Asian patients than in Caucasian cohorts [ ]. These differences may reflect distinct etiological factors, including environmental exposures, dietary patterns, and inherited genetic variation. Notably, some studies have also identified population-specific mutational processes. For instance, analyses of laryngeal cancer cohorts revealed differences in mutation spectra between African American and European American patients, including variations in the frequency of C > A and C > G substitutions [ ]. Such findings suggest that both endogenous mutational processes and exogenous exposures may shape the genomic architecture of tumors in a population-dependent manner. Collectively, these observations underscore the importance of incorporating ethnic diversity into cancer genomics studies. Understanding population-specific DDR alteration patterns is essential for improving genetic risk assessment, optimizing biomarker-driven therapies, and advancing equitable implementation of precision oncology.

## Environmental influences on DDR-associated mutational landscapes
Environmental exposures play a critical role in shaping the mutational landscapes of cancers through their interaction with DDR pathways [ ]. A variety of endogenous and exogenous factors—including chemical carcinogens, ionizing radiation, and oxidative stress—can induce DNA lesions that require efficient repair by DDR mechanisms. When these repair pathways are compromised, unrepaired DNA damage accumulates and generates characteristic mutational signatures that contribute to tumor initiation and progression. Inherited defects in DDR genes can further modify the impact of environmental exposures on cancer susceptibility. For instance, germline mutations in homologous recombination genes such as BRCA1 and BRCA2 markedly increase the risk of breast cancer, while environmental and hormonal factors may further modulate disease development [ ]. Similarly, pathogenic variants in other DDR genes have been associated with increased pan-cancer susceptibility, suggesting that impaired repair capacity may sensitize cells to environmental mutagens [ ]. Together, these findings highlight the complex interplay between environmental exposures and DDR deficiency in shaping cancer evolution. A better understanding of these interactions may improve cancer risk prediction and inform targeted surveillance and prevention strategies.

## Biological mechanisms of DDR defects driving tumor progression

## Genomic instability and the emergence of mutational signatures
Defects in DDR pathways are a major driver of genomic instability and play a key role in shaping the mutational landscapes of cancer genomes ( ). When DNA repair mechanisms are compromised, cells increasingly rely on alternative and often error-prone repair pathways, leading to the accumulation of characteristic mutational signatures and structural genome alterations. One major consequence of DDR dysfunction is the emergence of distinct single-base substitution patterns that reflect underlying DNA repair defects. For example, loss of the tumor suppressor gene FHIT has been associated with increased single-nucleotide substitutions, particularly C > T and T > C transitions, resembling age-related mutational signatures observed in human cancers [ ]. More broadly, deficiencies in genome maintenance mechanisms can generate reproducible mutational signatures that serve as molecular footprints of defective DNA repair processes. DDR status also influences the selection of DNA repair pathways during replication stress. Key regulators such as TP53 maintain genome stability by suppressing error-prone repair mechanisms. Loss or mutation of TP53 disrupts replication restart fidelity and promotes genomic instability [ ]. In addition, persistent expression of the cell-cycle regulator p21WAF1/Cip1 can inhibit high-fidelity repair pathways while favoring alternative repair mechanisms, thereby increasing mutational burden [ ]. Beyond point mutations, DDR defects frequently result in large-scale structural variations and chromosomal rearrangements. Replication-associated repair processes such as microhomology-mediated end joining can generate structural variants characterized by microhomology at breakpoint junctions. In extreme cases, catastrophic genome remodeling events such as chromothripsis may occur, in which chromosomes undergo extensive fragmentation and reassembly during a single cellular crisis. Chromothripsis has been detected in a substantial proportion of human cancers and is often associated with telomere dysfunction and DDR defects [ , ]. The genomic scars generated by DDR deficiency have important therapeutic implications. Tumors exhibiting HRD, often referred to as “BRCAness,” show increased sensitivity to platinum-based chemotherapy and PARP inhibitors [ ]. In addition to classical repair deficiencies, endogenous mutagenic processes also contribute to DDR-associated genome instability. Members of the APOBEC3 cytidine deaminase family can induce clusters of localized hypermutation known as kataegis, generating widespread cytosine substitutions across cancer genomes and contributing to tumor evolution [ , ]. Mechanistically, APOBEC3 enzymes specifically target single-stranded DNA (ssDNA). In DDR-deficient tumors, unresolved DNA damage leads to stalled replication forks and defective homologous recombination, thereby exposing extensive regions of ssDNA. This provides an optimal substrate for APOBEC3, explaining the enrichment of kataegis in these malignancies. Together, these findings illustrate that DDR defects promote tumor development through multiple mechanisms, including mutational signature formation, structural genome alterations, and catastrophic chromosomal events.
 

## DDR status reshapes the tumor immune microenvironment
The tumor immune microenvironment has emerged as a central regulator of tumor progression and therapeutic response, attracting increasing attention in cancer research [ , , , , , ]. DDR alterations play an important role in remodeling the tumor immune microenvironment ( ). Defects in DDR pathways can increase genomic instability and tumor mutational burden (TMB), thereby enhancing tumor immunogenicity and promoting immune cell infiltration. Several studies have demonstrated that tumors harboring DDR alterations often display elevated TMB and microsatellite instability, both of which are associated with improved responses to immune checkpoint blockade. For example, advanced non-small cell lung cancers with DDR mutations exhibit higher TMB, increased CD8⁺ T-cell infiltration, and reduced macrophage infiltration compared with DDR-proficient tumors [ ]. Beyond mutational burden, DNA damage can directly activate innate immune signaling pathways. Cytosolic DNA fragments generated during DNA damage activate the cGAS-STING pathway, leading to the production of type I interferons and pro-inflammatory cytokines [ ]. In parallel, ATM and ATR signaling pathways can induce inflammatory gene expression and influence immune cell recruitment, thereby linking genomic instability with immune activation [ ]. Specific DDR-related genes also contribute to immune regulation. For instance, alterations in PTEN not only affect PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling but also modulate DNA repair processes and tumor immune responses [ ]. Similarly, mutations in DDR genes in esophageal squamous cell carcinoma have been associated with increased immune cell infiltration, including T cells, dendritic cells, and monocytes [ ]. These findings suggest that DDR status may serve as an important determinant of tumor–immune interactions. Collectively, DDR alterations can reshape the tumor immune microenvironment through increased neoantigen formation, activation of innate immune pathways, and modulation of immune cell infiltration. These mechanisms provide a biological rationale for combining DDR-targeted therapies with immunotherapy.
 

## Crosstalk between DDR pathways, oncogenic signaling, and metabolic networks
DNA damage response pathways are tightly interconnected with oncogenic signaling and cellular metabolic networks. This crosstalk plays an essential role in regulating genome stability, tumor progression, and therapeutic responses. Key cell-cycle checkpoint regulators serve as important nodes linking DDR signaling with oncogenic pathways. For example, the checkpoint kinase CHK2 is regulated by the E3 ubiquitin ligase SIAH2, whose expression is induced under hypoxic conditions. Hypoxia-mediated SIAH2 activation promotes CHK2 degradation, thereby weakening DNA damage checkpoints and contributing to genomic instability in tumors [ ]. Homologous recombination repair is also regulated by multiple oncogenic signaling pathways. TOPBP1 has been shown to promote PLK1-dependent phosphorylation of RAD51, facilitating homologous recombination and influencing cellular sensitivity to PARP inhibitors [ ]. In addition, the CHK2–BRCA1 signaling axis helps maintain mitotic integrity by suppressing Aurora-A kinase activity and ensuring proper spindle assembly [ ]. Epigenetic and transcriptional regulators further integrate DDR signaling with broader cellular programs. For instance, the histone methyltransferase MLL1 participates in the regulation of senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP) gene expression, linking DNA damage signaling with inflammatory responses in the tumor microenvironment [ ]. Similarly, microRNA-mediated regulation can modulate DNA repair pathways; overexpression of miR-155 suppresses FOXO3a and reduces DNA polymerase δ expression, thereby promoting error-prone DNA repair and genomic instability [ ]. Finally, several signaling pathways implicated in tumor progression interact with DDR components to influence therapeutic responses. Inhibitors of the phosphatase WIP1, such as GSK2830371, enhance the sensitivity of breast cancer cells to chemotherapy and MDM2 antagonists by reinforcing DNA damage signaling [ ]. In addition, transcriptional networks such as the NF-κB–HOTAIR axis have been reported to modulate DNA damage responses and contribute to chemotherapy resistance [ ]. Taken together, these findings highlight that DDR pathways do not function in isolation but are embedded within complex signaling and metabolic networks. Understanding this multilayered crosstalk may reveal new opportunities for combination therapies targeting both oncogenic signaling and DNA repair vulnerabilities.

## DDR gene defects and the molecular basis of synthetic lethality

## Genomic instability as a unifying feature across tumor types
Genomic instability represents a hallmark of cancer and serves as a common biological feature linking diverse tumor types. It arises from defects in DDR pathways and contributes to tumor initiation, progression, and therapeutic resistance [ ]. Chromosomal instability (CIN) is a major manifestation of genomic instability and can be broadly categorized into numerical chromosomal instability (W-CIN) and structural chromosomal instability (S-CIN) [ ]. W-CIN involves abnormal chromosome segregation and is often associated with whole-genome doubling, whereas S-CIN is driven by chromosomal rearrangements that occur when homologous recombination is defective, forcing the cell to rely on the error-prone 'mis-repair' of double-strand breaks via alternative pathways such as NHEJ and Microhomology-Mediated End Joining (MMEJ).
Both forms of CIN have been associated with tumor progression and poor clinical outcomes [ ]. Genomic instability can be driven by both intrinsic and extrinsic stressors. Endogenous mutagenic processes, such as the activity of the APOBEC3 cytidine deaminase family, contribute to widespread mutagenesis and increased tumor mutation burden across multiple cancer types [ ]. In addition, tumor microenvironmental factors, particularly hypoxia, can further promote genomic instability by increasing DNA damage, suppressing DNA repair pathways, and inducing epigenetic alterations [ ]. The consequences of genomic instability are not limited to tumor evolution but also have important clinical implications. For example, genomic instability signatures have been developed to predict clinical outcomes across multiple cancers, including non–small cell lung cancer, colorectal cancer, and ovarian cancer [ ]. Moreover, genomic instability detected in circulating tumor cells has been proposed as a potential biomarker reflecting tumor heterogeneity and metastatic potential [ ]. Together, these observations highlight genomic instability as a central mechanism connecting DDR defects with tumor heterogeneity, disease progression, and treatment response.

## Molecular principles and therapeutic advantages of synthetic lethality
The concept of synthetic lethality describes a genetic interaction in which the simultaneous impairment of two genes results in cell death, a principle that has been successfully exploited in DDR-deficient tumors. This concept has become a powerful strategy for targeting tumors harboring DDR defects. The most well-established example is the use of PARP inhibitors in cancers with mutations in BRCA1 or BRCA2 [ ]. These tumors exhibit HRD and rely heavily on alternative DNA repair mechanisms. Inhibition of PARP enzymes blocks single-strand break repair and leads to the accumulation of DNA damage that cannot be efficiently repaired in HR-deficient cells, ultimately resulting in cell death [ ]. Beyond BRCA-associated cancers, synthetic lethality strategies are being expanded to target additional DDR components and oncogenic contexts. For instance, inhibitors targeting ATR, ATM, WEE1, and other DNA damage checkpoint regulators have shown promise in tumors characterized by replication stress or defective DNA repair pathways [ ]. Similarly, synthetic lethal interactions have been explored in cancers driven by oncogenic mutations such as KRAS, where tumor cells become dependent on specific stress response and DNA repair pathways [ ]. Beyond the classical BRCA-PARP axis, the modern synthetic lethality landscape includes several emerging paradigms. For instance, tumors with ATM loss exhibit profound sensitivity to ATR inhibitors, exploiting the reliance on ATR for replication fork stability. Similarly, ARID1A deficiency—a common chromatin remodeling defect—creates actionable vulnerabilities to various DDR-targeted therapies, including PARP and ATR inhibitors. However, therapeutic resistance remains a major challenge. Mechanisms such as restoration of homologous recombination, secondary mutations in DDR genes, and activation of alternative repair pathways can reduce sensitivity to synthetic lethal therapies [ ]. Consequently, ongoing research focuses on identifying new synthetic lethal gene pairs and developing rational combination therapies to overcome resistance. Overall, synthetic lethality provides a powerful framework for selectively targeting tumors with DDR defects and represents a cornerstone of precision oncology.

## Pan-cancer detection and standardization of homologous recombination deficiency
Accurate detection of HRD is critical for identifying patients who may benefit from DDR-targeted therapies. Multiple genomic approaches have been developed to measure HRD across different cancer types. One widely used strategy involves genomic scar analysis based on copy number alterations. One widely used strategy involves genomic scar analysis based on copy number alterations, specifically measuring Loss of Heterozygosity (LOH), Telomeric Allelic Imbalance (TAI), and Large-scale State Transitions (LST) to generate a composite HRD score. For example, the HRD signature implemented in clinical assays such as FoundationOne®CDx integrates copy number features to generate an HRD score, which has demonstrated high analytical reliability [ ]. Large pan-cancer studies have further shown that HRD scores vary significantly across tumor types and are strongly associated with biallelic alterations in homologous recombination repair genes, particularly BRCA1 and BRCA2 [ ]. Computational approaches have also been developed to predict HRD using mutational signatures and structural variation patterns. Methods such as HRDsum and CHORD integrate multiple genomic features to identify tumors exhibiting ‘BRCAness’ [ ]. Population-scale studies indicate that pathogenic variants in homologous recombination repair genes occur across many cancer types, including breast, ovarian, prostate, and pancreatic cancers [ ]. Importantly, HRD status has been consistently associated with increased sensitivity to PARP inhibitors and platinum-based therapies, highlighting its clinical relevance [ , ]. Despite these advances, challenges remain in the standardization of HRD testing, including variability in detection methods and threshold definitions. Continued efforts to harmonize HRD assays and validate predictive biomarkers will be essential for expanding the application of DDR-targeted therapies in pan-cancer settings.

## Therapeutic strategies targeting DDR gene alterations

## Development of drugs targeting DNA damage repair deficiencies
Substantial progress has been made in the development of therapies targeting DDR defects in cancer, leading to the emergence of multiple DDR-targeted therapeutic strategies ( ). As summarized in , a wide range of DDR-targeted agents is currently under investigation. It is important to distinguish between clinical-grade inhibitors and those in the discovery pipeline. Currently, PARP inhibitors such as olaparib, rucaparib, niraparib, and talazoparib have received FDA approval for specific indications in breast, ovarian, and prostate cancers. In contrast, inhibitors targeting other key nodes—such as ATR (ceralasertib), WEE1 (adavosertib), and DNA-PK (peposertib)—are currently being evaluated in early-to-late phase clinical trials, either as monotherapies or in combination with standard-of-care treatments. The most successful example is the use of PARP inhibitors, which exploit the principle of synthetic lethality in tumors harboring homologous recombination repair deficiencies, particularly those with BRCA1 or BRCA2 mutations. By blocking the repair of single-strand DNA breaks, PARP inhibition leads to the accumulation of DNA damage that cannot be effectively repaired in HR-deficient cells, ultimately resulting in tumor cell death. PARP inhibitors have demonstrated significant clinical efficacy in several cancer types, including ovarian, breast, prostate, and pancreatic cancers. In high-grade serous ovarian cancer, these agents have become a cornerstone of targeted therapy, particularly for patients with BRCA mutations or HRD [ ]. Their clinical indications continue to expand as biomarkers such as HRD signatures are increasingly used to guide patient selection. Beyond PARP inhibition, multiple other DDR components are emerging as therapeutic targets. Inhibitors targeting key checkpoint kinases and DNA damage signaling regulators—such as ATR, ATM, and WEE1—have shown promising activity in tumors characterized by replication stress or defective DNA repair pathways [ , ]. These agents aim to further exploit vulnerabilities in cancer cells that rely heavily on residual DNA repair mechanisms for survival. Advances in genomic technologies, including next-generation sequencing (NGS) and circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) analysis, have facilitated the identification of DDR gene alterations in clinical settings [ ]. These molecular profiling approaches enable the detection of actionable mutations and support the implementation of precision oncology strategies for patients with DDR-deficient tumors. Acquired resistance remains a formidable barrier. Notably, BRCA1/2 reversion mutations—secondary mutations that restore the protein’s open reading frame—have emerged as a key clinical mechanism for cross-resistance to platinum salts and PARP inhibitors. Tumor cells can develop resistance to DDR-targeted therapies through mechanisms such as restoration of homologous recombination, activation of alternative DNA repair pathways, or drug efflux. Understanding these resistance mechanisms is essential for the development of next-generation DDR inhibitors and combination treatment strategies.
 

## Synergistic interactions between immunotherapy and DDR alterations
Increasing evidence suggests that DDR alterations can enhance the efficacy of cancer immunotherapy. Tumors with defects in DNA repair pathways often exhibit increased TMB and neoantigen formation, which can improve immune recognition and response to immune checkpoint blockade [ ]. Clinical and genomic studies have demonstrated that mutations in DNA repair genes are associated with improved responses to immune checkpoint inhibitors in several cancers, including bladder cancer, pancreatic cancer, and prostate cancer [ , , ]. In these tumors, DDR defects contribute to genomic instability and increased immunogenicity, thereby promoting antitumor immune responses. Combination strategies involving DDR-targeted therapies and immunotherapy are therefore being actively explored. For example, PARP inhibitors have been shown to enhance antitumor immunity by increasing DNA damage, activating the cGAS–STING pathway, and promoting type I interferon signaling [ , ]. These immune-stimulatory effects provide a biological rationale for combining PARP inhibition with immune checkpoint blockade. The combination of PARP inhibitors and immune checkpoint inhibitors presents unique safety challenges, notably overlapping toxicities such as myelosuppression and rare but serious pneumonitis. To optimize the therapeutic index, clinical trials are increasingly exploring intermittent dosing schedules of PARPi to allow for hematologic recovery without compromising the priming of the immune microenvironment. In addition, DDR alterations may serve as predictive biomarkers for selecting patients who are more likely to benefit from immunotherapy-based treatment strategies. Overall, integrating DDR-targeted therapies with immunotherapy represents a promising direction for improving clinical outcomes in patients with DDR-deficient tumors.

## Clinical translation of DDR gene alterations

## Genetic risk assessment and early cancer detection
Identification of germline mutations in DDR genes has become an essential component of cancer risk assessment and early detection strategies, facilitating the clinical translation of DDR alterations into precision oncology ( ). Pathogenic variants in genes such as BRCA1, BRCA2, PALB2, ATM, and mismatch repair genes are strongly associated with hereditary cancer predisposition syndromes and significantly increase the lifetime risk of multiple malignancies. Large population-based registries and surveillance programs have demonstrated the clinical value of genetic screening in high-risk families. For example, systematic monitoring of individuals with Lynch syndrome has significantly reduced colorectal cancer mortality through early detection and preventive interventions [ ]. Similarly, germline testing for BRCA1/2 mutations has become standard practice in patients with breast, ovarian, prostate, and pancreatic cancers [ ]. Advances in genomic technologies have enabled the use of multigene panel testing to assess inherited cancer risk. These panels allow simultaneous evaluation of multiple DDR-related genes and have substantially improved the identification of individuals at elevated cancer risk. A major challenge in multigene panel testing is the identification of Variants of Uncertain Significance (VUS). The high frequency of VUS in DDR genes complicates genetic counseling and clinical decision-making, necessitating the integration of functional genomic assays and longitudinal data to reassess variant pathogenicity. In addition, risk prediction models integrating genetic variants, family history, and clinical factors are increasingly used to guide personalized screening strategies [ ]. Reflecting the clinical utility of these biomarkers, both NCCN and ESMO guidelines now provide a Category 1 recommendation for germline and somatic DDR testing in patients with metastatic castration-resistant prostate cancer. Enhanced surveillance approaches have been implemented for individuals carrying high-risk DDR mutations. For example, magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and mammography are recommended for women with hereditary breast cancer susceptibility, while targeted surveillance programs are being explored for high-risk populations predisposed to pancreatic or prostate cancer [ , , ]. These strategies aim to detect malignancies at earlier stages and improve long-term clinical outcomes.
 

## Precision therapeutic decision-making in DDR-deficient tumors
The identification of DDR gene alterations has transformed therapeutic decision-making in oncology and enabled the development of precision treatment strategies. Tumors harboring defects in homologous recombination repair pathways are particularly sensitive to DNA-damaging agents, including platinum-based chemotherapy and PARP inhibitors. Platinum-based chemotherapy has long been recognized as an effective treatment for cancers with impaired DNA repair capacity. In tumors with BRCA1/2 mutations or homologous recombination deficiency, platinum compounds induce DNA crosslinks that cannot be efficiently repaired, resulting in enhanced cytotoxicity. These observations provided the biological rationale for exploiting DDR defects as predictive biomarkers for treatment response. The introduction of PARP inhibitors represents a major breakthrough in the targeted treatment of DDR-deficient cancers. These agents selectively kill tumor cells with defective homologous recombination by inducing synthetic lethality. PARP inhibitors have demonstrated substantial clinical benefits in ovarian, breast, prostate, and pancreatic cancers with BRCA mutations or HRD features, and they are now integrated into multiple treatment guidelines [ , ]. In clinical practice, comprehensive genomic profiling—including next-generation sequencing and liquid biopsy approaches—is increasingly used to identify actionable DDR alterations. These technologies facilitate patient stratification and support the implementation of personalized treatment strategies based on molecular characteristics of the tumor.

## Conclusion and perspectives
DDR gene alterations have emerged as a central driver of genomic instability and tumor evolution across multiple cancer types. In recent years, advances in genomic sequencing technologies have greatly expanded our understanding of the mutational landscape of DDR pathways, revealing their critical roles in tumor initiation, progression, and therapeutic response. These discoveries have fundamentally reshaped the paradigm of precision oncology, enabling the identification of actionable vulnerabilities in cancers with defective DNA repair mechanisms. One of the most significant clinical breakthroughs arising from DDR research is the development of synthetic lethality–based therapies, exemplified by the success of PARP inhibitors in tumors harboring homologous recombination deficiency. In addition, increasing evidence indicates that alterations in DDR pathways not only influence sensitivity to DNA-damaging agents but also modulate tumor immunogenicity, thereby providing a biological rationale for combining DDR-targeted therapies with immunotherapy. The integration of molecular profiling technologies, including next-generation sequencing and ctDNA analysis, has further facilitated the clinical implementation of DDR biomarkers in cancer diagnosis, risk assessment, and treatment stratification. ctDNA analysis offers a non-invasive 'real-time' window into tumor evolution. Beyond initial diagnosis, ctDNA can detect emergent resistant clones—such as reversion mutations—weeks or months before radiographic progression, allowing for timely therapeutic switching. Despite these advances, several challenges remain in translating DDR biology into broader clinical benefit. These include the heterogeneity of DDR alterations across tumor types, the lack of standardized approaches for HRD and DDR testing, and the emergence of resistance to DDR-targeted therapies. Addressing these challenges will require deeper mechanistic understanding of DNA repair networks, improved biomarker development, and more refined patient selection strategies. Looking forward, future research should focus on expanding the synthetic lethality landscape, developing next-generation DDR inhibitors, and exploring rational combination therapies involving DDR targeting, immunotherapy, and radiotherapy. Advances in systems biology, computational modeling, and artificial intelligence–assisted drug discovery are expected to accelerate the identification of novel therapeutic targets within DDR networks. In summary, continued integration of molecular biology, clinical genomics, and translational research will be essential for fully exploiting DDR vulnerabilities in cancer. These efforts are likely to drive the next generation of precision oncology strategies and ultimately improve outcomes for patients with DDR-deficient tumors.
