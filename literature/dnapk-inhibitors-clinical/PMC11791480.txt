## Abstract
Major risk factors of head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC) are tobacco use and human papillomavirus (HPV). HPV E6 oncoprotein leads to p53 degradation, whereas HPV-negative cancers are frequently associated with TP53 mutations. Peposertib is a potent and selective, orally administered small-molecule inhibitor of the catalytic subunit of the DNA-dependent kinase (DNA-PKcs), a key regulator of nonhomologous end joining (NHEJ). NHEJ inhibition along with irradiation (IR)-induced DNA double-strand breaks has the potential to increase antitumor treatment efficacy. In this study, we investigated the responses of a panel of HNSCC models with distinct HPV and p53 status to treatments with IR, DNA-PKcs inhibition, and their combination in vitro and in vivo. IR-induced DNA damage combined with peposertib administration shortly before IR results in decreased cell viability and proliferation and causes DNA repair delay in all studied HNSCC cell lines. However, our data confirm that the actual cell fate upon this treatment is determined by cellular p53 and/or HPV status. Cells lacking functional p53 due to its degradation by HPV or due to a loss-of-function mutation are arrested in the G2/M phase of the cell cycle and eliminated by apoptosis, whereas p53-proficient HNSCC cell lines preferentially undergo senescence. This is also recapitulated in vivo, where HPV+ UD-SCC-2 xenografts display stronger and more durable responses to the combined treatment as compared with p53 wild-type UM-SCC-74A tumors. In conclusion, DNA-PKcs inhibitor peposertib should be further studied as a potential radiosensitizer for HNSCCs, taking into consideration the genetic background and the HPV status of a particular tumor.

## Introduction
Head and neck squamous cell carcinomas (HNSCC) are among the most frequently diagnosed tumors worldwide, with mortality exceeding 500,000 deaths yearly ( ). Major risk factors leading to the development of this malignancy include various environmental substances and pollutants (e.g., tobacco use, excessive alcohol consumption, etc.) as well as chronic infection with cancerogenic strains of human papillomavirus (HPV) and Epstein–Barr virus ( ). Whereas HPV-positive (HPV+) HNSCCs have favorable prognoses and good treatment response rates, continuous exposure to tobacco smoke worsens the burden of mutations and leads to poorer outcomes. Mutations in the p53 coding gene TP53 and other cell-cycle and DNA repair key regulators are among the most frequent genetic alterations found in HNSCC ( ). Approximately 86% of HPV-negative (HPV−) and 3% of HPV+ HNSCCs carry a mutation in TP53 ( ).
Radiotherapy (RT), surgical resection, and systemic chemotherapy are prime modalities used in the management of patients with local or locoregionally confined HNSCC. The application of ionizing radiation (IR) is not limited only to early-stage disease but is also relevant for the treatment of advanced HNSCC stages as well as oligometastases ( ). EGFR inhibitors, immunotherapeutic agents, and targeted agents are currently under broad clinical investigation in combination with IR ( , ). The main concerns are the high toxicity of such combined treatment regimens and the low efficacy gain compared with treatment-induced side effects. Radiosensitizing agents can potentially limit the toxicity of IR by decreasing its doses and volumes but maintaining, or potentially, also increasing its efficacy ( ).
IR generates multiple DNA double-strand breaks (DSB), which can be physiologically repaired either by the error-free homologous recombination (HR) repair depending on the availability of sister chromatid or by the error-prone nonhomologous end joining (NHEJ) pathway ( ). Peposertib (formerly M3814) is a potent and selective small-molecule inhibitor of the catalytic subunit of DNA-PK (DNA-PKcs), a key effector of NHEJ ( ). Up to date, multiple studies have shown that NHEJ inhibition increases the antitumor treatment efficacy of various DNA-damaging agents, including IR and topoisomerase II inhibitors in distinct cancer types ( , ). For instance, peposertib demonstrated a strong radiosensitizing effect on human cancer cells of various origins in vitro and in vivo ( , ) and an improved chemoradiation effect in some rectal cancer models ( ).
Considering the disadvantages of conventional therapies and the persisting limitations of advances in HNSCC management, we addressed in this study the need for a novel treatment approach for these cancers by inhibition of DNA-PKcs prior to IR. We hypothesized that this combination may improve HNSCC treatment outcome due to a defective repair of RT-induced DSBs, particularly in head and neck cancer cells with p53 and/or HPV alterations, which are compromised in the maintenance of the G1/S cell-cycle checkpoint.
We show that peposertib radiosensitizes HNSCC tumors regardless of their p53 and HPV status by significantly increasing IR-induced γH2AX and RAD51 foci formation and causing a delay in DNA repair. However, inhibition of NHEJ combined with IR results in different cell fates depending on p53 functionality. Whereas HPV+ and p53-mutated cells are predominantly eliminated by apoptosis due to a common alteration in p53-related pathways, p53 wild-type (WT) cells preferentially undergo senescence. Combining DNA-PKcs inhibition and IR leads to distinct treatment responses in HNSCC models with dysfunctional p53 as compared with p53-proficient models both in vitro and in vivo. These clinically relevant observations suggest that p53 and/or HPV status of a tumor should be considered when using DNA-PKcs targeting as a precision medicine–based treatment of HNSCC.

## Materials and Methods

## Cell lines
UM-SCC-10B (RRID: CVCL_7714), UM-SCC-14A (RRID: CVCL_7779), UM-SCC-17B (RRID: CVCL_7725), UM-SCC-74A (RRID: CVCL_7779), and UM-SCC-104 (RRID: CVCL_7712) squamous cell carcinoma cell lines kindly provided by Prof. Thomas Carey (University of Michigan) were cultured in DMEM (Sigma-Aldrich, D6046) supplemented with 10% heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum (FBS hi; Sigma-Aldrich, Cat. no. F7524), 1% antibiotic–antimycotic (AA: penicillin 100 U/mL, streptomycin sulfate 100 U/mL, and 412 amphotericin B 0.25 mg/mL; Gibco, Cat. no. 15240-062), and 1% nonessential amino acids (Sigma-Aldrich, M7145). The UD-SCC-2 cell line (RRID: CVCL_E325) was obtained from Prof. Dr. Henning Bier (University of München) and cultured in RPMI (Sigma-Aldrich, Cat. no. R8758) supplemented with 10% FBS hi, 1% AA, and 2 mmol/L L-glutamine (Sigma-Aldrich, Cat. no. 59202C). The UPCI-SCC-154 cell line was purchased from the German Collection of Microorganisms and Cell Cultures GmbH (DSMZ Cat. # ACC-669, RRID: CVCL_2230) and cultured in minimum essential medium (Sigma-Aldrich, Cat. no. 51412C) supplemented with 10% FBS hi, 1% AA, 1% nonessential amino acids, and 2 mmol/L L-glutamine. All cells were maintained at 37°C with a 5% CO2 supply and were kept in culture for up to 2 months to perform the experiments. The UM-SCC-10B, -14A, -17B, and -74A cell lines have been authenticated by whole-exome sequencing and transcriptomic profiling ( ). All cells used in the study were tested negative by PCR (IDEXX BioAnalytics) for Corynebacterium bovis, Corynebacterium sp. (HAC2), and Mycoplasma sp. prior to performing the experiments. No other cell line authentication was performed by the authors for this study.

## 
In vitro treatments
The DNA-PKcs inhibitor (DNA-PKcsi) peposertib (previously M3814 or MSC2490484A) was developed and synthesized in the healthcare business of Merck KGaA ( , ). The compound was dissolved in DMSO (Sigma-Aldrich, Cat. no. D8418), aliquoted, and used at a final concentration (f.c.) of 300 nmol/L. Cells were treated 24 hours after seeding in all experiments. Cells were irradiated using a 137Cs source (Gammacell 40, MDS Nordion) at a dose rate of 0.80 Gy/minute. In all experiments and unless specified differently, cells were irradiated with a single dose of 4 Gy 30 minutes after incubation with DMSO or peposertib. Overall, four treatment groups were used: control (DMSO), peposertib (300 nmol/L, 30 minutes of incubation prior to IR), 4 Gy irradiation (IR), and peposertib (300 nmol/L, 30 minutes of incubation prior to IR) in combination with 4 Gy IR (peposertib + IR).

## Protein extraction and Western blotting
Cells were lysed 1 hour after treatment in urea buffer [20 mmol/L 2-[4-(2-hydroxyethyl)piperazin-1-yl] ethanesulfonic acid (HEPES), pH 8.0; 9.0 mol/L urea; 1 mmol/L sodium orthovanadate; 2.5 mmol/L sodium pyrophosphate, and 1 mmol/L β-glycerol-phosphate] and sonicated (Bandelin Sonopuls, HD2070). Total protein concentration was determined with the Bio-Rad protein quantification reagent (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Inc.). Equal amounts of protein (50 μg) were separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred to polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membranes. The membranes were blocked in 5% BSA (Roche, 10 735 086 001) in TBS with Tween (TBS-T) before overnight incubation at 4°C with the following primary antibodies: pDNA-PKcs (Abcam Cat. # ab124918, RRID: AB_11001004), p53 (Cell Signaling Technology Cat. # 9282, RRID: AB_331476), p-p53 (Cell Signaling Technology Cat. # 9284, RRID: AB_331464), p21 (Millipore Cat. # 05-655, RRID: AB_309880), cleaved lamin A (Cell Signaling Technology Cat. # 2035, RRID: AB_2234647), p-S10 H3 (Millipore Cat. # 06-570, RRID: AB_310177), and β-actin (Millipore Cat. # MAB1501, RRID: AB_2223041). Immunodetection was performed on an Odyssey imager (Li-Cor Biosciences) following incubation with secondary antibodies (IRDye 680RD goat anti-mouse, LI-COR Biosciences Cat. # 925-68070, RRID: AB_2651128, and IRDye 800CW goat anti-rabbit, LI-COR Biosciences Cat. # 925-32211, RRID: AB_2651127). The densitometric quantification of the blots was performed using ImageJ software. The values obtained for target proteins were divided by the values of the loading control (β-actin) and further normalized to the untreated control of each cell line.

## Cell-cycle assay
Cells were fixed 7, 24, 36, and 48 hours after the corresponding treatments in 70% EtOH, washed in PBS, and resuspended in 200 μL propidium iodide (PI) solution (50 μg PI + 40 μg RNAse A/mL). PI incorporation was measured by flow cytometry (BD LSR II, BD Biosciences) and analyzed using FlowJo software (RRID: SCR_008520).

## Carboxyfluorescein succinimidyl ester generation tracking
Generation tracking has been performed using CellTrace CFSE Kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Invitrogen). Before plating, cells were stained with carboxyfluorescein succinimidyl ester, a fluorescent dye, which covalently binds proteins and is equally distributed to the daughter cells upon division. Five days after treatment, the assay was terminated, and the intensity of the signal was measured by flow cytometry. On the day of treatment, an untreated sample was collected to serve as a basal intensity signal. Signal intensity was measured by flow cytometry using LSR II (BD Biosciences) and plotted with FlowJo (RRID: SCR_008520).

## Cell proliferation assay
Cells were seeded in 24-well plates, treated with either DMSO or peposertib, exposed to different doses of IR (0, 2, and 4 Gy), and then allowed to proliferate for 9 days. At the end of the experiment, the cells were fixed and stained with 2% crystal violet (Sigma-Aldrich, C0775) dissolved in acetic acid:methanol (2:1). Cell density was calculated by determination of the covered area using ImageJ (RRID: SCR_003070) and normalized to the corresponding nonirradiated control. Cell proliferation data were further fitted using the Emax model ( ). The Emax model is a generalization of the more common Hill model in which the response (1 - cell proliferation) can be nonzero for zero doses. To reduce the number of fitted parameters, we assumed a full response (1.0) for a very high dose. To assess the uncertainty of the estimated parameters, we used a parametric bootstrap based on the empirical mean response values and the SEM. Median estimated parameters were used to numerically determine the IR dose needed to reach response 0.5 (LD50). The dose-modifying factor (DMF) for each cell line is the LD50 ratio between untreated and treated cells, respectively.

## Clonogenic assay
Single cells were seeded in triplicates in 6-well plates, treated with either DMSO or peposertib, and exposed to different doses of IR (0, 2, and 4 Gy). Fourteen days after plating, cells were fixed and stained with 2% crystal violet (Sigma-Aldrich, C0775) dissolved in acetic acid:methanol (2:1). Clonogenic growth was determined using Colcount, Charm Enhanced Algorithm (Oxford Optronics). The clonogenic fraction of irradiated cells was normalized to the corresponding nonirradiated control.

## 2,3-Bis[2-methoxy-4-nitro-S-sulfophenynl]H-tetrazolium-5 carboxanilide inner salt assay
Cells were plated into a 96-well plate in 50 μL of the corresponding medium (six wells per treatment condition). After 24 hours, DMSO or peposertib solutions were added to reach the volume of 150 μL, and the plate was irradiated (4 Gy). Ninety-six hours later, cellular proliferation was measured using a commercially available kit and the recommended protocol (Roche, Cat. no. 11465015001). Cells were incubated with the 2,3-bis[2-methoxy-4-nitro-S-sulfophenynl]H-tetrazolium-5 carboxanilide inner salt (XTT) reagent at 37°C, 5% CO2 for 2 hours. During the incubation, the yellow tetrazolium salt XTT is cleaved to formazan, the amount of which is correlated to the number of metabolically active cells. Quantification was performed by measuring the absorbance at 490 nm (reference wavelength: 655 nm) using a Tecan plate reader, and relative metabolic activity was normalized to the corresponding nonirradiated control.

## Cell viability and toxicity assay
Cell death and viability were assessed using Live/Dead Assay Kit (Molecular Probes, Cat. no. L23105) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, cells were allowed to grow for 96 hours after treatment and then stained with calcein-AM (green fluorescence; f.c. 1.6 μmol/L) and ethidium homodimer-1 (red fluorescence; f.c. 4 μmol/L) to detect viable and dead cells, respectively. Cells were imaged using a fluorescence microscope (Leica 4000) at 4× magnification and quantified with ImageJ (RRID: SCR_003070).

## Apoptosis assessments

## Caspase-3 activity assay
Cells were lysed in assay buffer [100 mmol/L HEPES, pH 7.5, 1% sucrose, and 0.1% 3-[(3-cholamidopropyl)dimethylammonio]-2-hydroxy-1-propanesulfonic acid (CHAPS)] 72 hours after treatment, and cell protein content was determined with the Bio-Rad protein quantification reagent [Bio-Rad Laboratories (RRID: SCR_008426)]. Caspase-3 activity was assessed in duplicates via a fluorogenic assay using the Ac-DEVD-AMC–specific caspase-3 substrate (Calbiochem). After substrate addition, fluorescence was measured using a TECAN Infinite 200 pro plate reader, and the signal of caspase-3 activity was normalized to the samples’ protein content and the corresponding nonirradiated control.

## Annexin V assay
Apoptosis was assessed using FITC Annexin V kit (BD Pharmingen, Cat. no. 556570) according to manufacturer’s instructions. Floating cells were collected, and adherent cells were trypsinized, counted, and stained with Annexin V-FITC and PI 48 hours after treatment. Events were acquired using a FACS Fortessa flow cytometer, and the analysis was performed with FlowJo 10.10.0 (RRID: SCR_008520).

## Senescence-associated β-galactosidase assay in vitro
Cells seeded and treated in 6-well plates or 8-chamber slides (Falcon) were stained 9 to 11 days after treatment. Reagents and staining for senescence-associated β-galactosidase (β-Gal) were implemented as described previously ( ). In short, the cells were fixed for 7 minutes in 2% formaldehyde/0.2% glutaraldehyde solution at room temperature, washed with PBS, and further incubated with X-Gal (Sigma-Aldrich, Cat. no. B4252) solution overnight at 37°C. Images were obtained using an inverted fluorescent microscope (Leica 4000 and Nikon).

## RNA silencing
UM-SCC-74A cells were transfected with Lipofectamine 2000 (Invitrogen, Cat. no. 11668027) and 25 nmol/L siRNA p53 (Qiagen, Hs_TP53_9, Cat. no. SI02655170) or AllStars negative control siRNA (Qiagen, Cat. no. 1027280). Cells were trypsinized, counted, and seeded for further assays 48 hours after transfection.

## RT-PCR
RNA was extracted using Roche High Pure RNA Kit (Cat. No. 11828665001) following the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA (1 μg) was reversely transcribed with Omniscript Kit (Qiagen, Cat. no 205113) and random primers (Roche, Cat. no 11034731001). cDNA (25 ng) was used for RT-PCR using p53 (Applied Biosystems, Cat. no. Hs01034249_m1) and GAPDH primer probe (Applied Biosystems, Cat. no. Hs02786624_g1) with FastStart Universal Probe Master Mix (Roche, Cat. no 04913957001).

## γH2AX and RAD51 foci immunostaining
Cells were seeded in 8-chamber microscopy slides and fixed in 4% formaldehyde (Sigma-Aldrich, 158127) at 3, 24, and 48 hours after the corresponding treatment. After fixation, the cells were permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 (Fluka, 93426) and blocked with the blocking solution containing 3% goat serum (Dako, Cat. no. X0907), 0.1% sodium azide (the healthcare business of Merck KGaA, Cat. no. 6688), 0.5% casein (Sigma-Aldrich, Cat. no. C-8654), and 0.025% Tween (Sigma-Aldrich, Cat. no. P2287) dissolved in TBS pH 7.5. Washes were performed with TBS and TBS-T. The cells were incubated overnight with primary antibodies targeting γH2AX (Cell Signaling Technology Cat. # 2577, RRID: AB_2118010) and RAD51 (GeneTex Cat. # GTX70230, RRID: AB_372856) foci. Slides were mounted with Vectashield Antifade Mounting Medium (Vector Laboratories, Cat. no. H-1000) and prepared for imaging using an inverted fluorescent microscope (Leica 4000) with 63× immersion objective magnification after 1 hour of incubation with secondary antibodies [goat anti-mouse IgG 546 (Molecular Probes Cat. # A-11030, RRID: AB_144695) and goat anti-rabbit IgG 488 (Molecular Probes Cat. # A-11008, RRID: AB_143165)] and counterstain with 4',6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) (Sigma-Aldrich, Cat. no. D-9542). The brightness of the presented images was enhanced. Quantification of foci per nuclei was performed on original images with a modified pipeline used for foci count on CellProfiler image analysis software (RRID: SCR_007358).

## Mouse xenograft model
Animal experiments were performed in the Central Animal Facility of the University of Bern following the general guidelines and regulations. All experiments were approved by the local experimental animal committee of the Canton of Bern according to Swiss laws for animal protection (animal license BE128-20). Mouse xenograft models using UM-SCC-74A (p53 WT) and UD-SCC-2 (HPV+) cell lines were established. Two million cells diluted in 50 µL solution of Matrigel Basement Membrane Matrix (Corning, Cat. no. 356237) and Dulbecco’s PBS (1:1) were injected subcutaneously to the right posterior flank region of 5-week-old male and female NMRI-nu mice (RRID: IMSR_RJ:NMRI-NUDE, Janvier Labs). Tumor formation was monitored twice a week by palpation and caliper measurement. Tumor volumes were calculated using the following formula: tumor volume = (A2 × B)/2, in which A is the diameter of the largest measurement and B is the diameter of the smallest measurement. Once the tumors reached a volume between 100 and 300 mm3, mice were randomized to four different treatment groups (vehicle, peposertib, IR, and peposertib + IR, n = 7 in each group for the longitudinal and n = 3–4 for the histologic cohorts). Experimenters were blinded when injecting the cells and performing tumor measurements but not to the assigned treatment modality due to technical complexity of the treatment (SmART irradiator, gavage feeding, etc.). Since the first day of the treatment, regular follow-up, including animal weight and tumor size measurement, was maintained for 45 days or until the euthanasia criteria (e.g., tumor size above 1,000 mm3) were met for the longitudinal cohort and for 8 days (or 72 hours after the last treatment dose) for the histologic cohort. At the end of the follow-up, the mice were euthanized with pentobarbital (150 mg/kg, Esconarkon, Streuli Pharma AG), and tumor tissues were collected. Each tumor was half-dissected and fixed in 10% buffered formalin (Simport, Cat. no. M960-40FMA) or freshly cryopreserved in isopentane (Sigma-Aldrich. Cat. no. M32631) to proceed with immunostaining.

## 
In vivo interventions
Peposertib powder was suspended in a vehicle solution containing Methocel 0.5% (Sigma-Aldrich, Cat. no. 94378), Tween-20 0.25% (Sigma-Aldrich, Cat. no. P2287), and Na-citrate buffer 300 mmol/L (pH = 2.5). The vehicle solution alone was administered orally via a gavage needle with a volume of 200 mL for five consecutive days. Peposertib dissolved in vehicle solution was delivered in the same way with a single dose of 100 mg/kg (the cumulative dose was 500 mg/kg). Two Gy of IR per fraction were delivered precisely to the tumor site for five consecutive days (at a cumulative dose of 10 Gy) by the small-animal RT system (SmART; Precision X-Ray, Inc.). When combining the two perturbations, the drug was given 15 minutes before RT. Mice were anesthetized in the induction chamber with a mix of 5% isoflurane and 100% oxygen (1 L/minute) and then moved to the animal stage of the SmART machine in which the anesthetic flow was maintained via face mask throughout the whole procedure. For each mouse, the single IR dose (2 Gy) was calculated by Monte Carlo dose calculation (SmART-ATP) on the first day of treatment based on the acquired high-resolution CT scan [PilotXRAD software; University of Colorado Anschutz Medical Campus Cancer Center Small-Animal Image-Guided Radiation Therapy Core Facility (RRID: SCR_021996)] of the s.c. tumor. The delivery was performed using a SmART tube (collimator field size −10 mm × 10 mm, filter: 5 mm Cu) from two beaming angles (180°). On treatment days 2 to 5, the IR dose was delivered with the same plan using CT-guided tumor targeting.

## Tissue staining
Formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded sections of 5 μm thickness and fresh cryosections of 10 μm were prepared from the xenograft tumors. Half of the formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded sections were deparaffinized, immersed in a citrate buffer for 30 minutes (Ki67) or 10 minutes (yH2AX) at 95°C to 100°C, incubated with peroxidase blocking solution 3% H2O2 (Millipore Sigma, 107209), and blocked with 1% normal goat serum (DAKO, Cat. no. X0907). Washes were performed with TBS and TBS-T. The slices were incubated overnight at +4°C with a primary antibody targeting Ki67 (Cell Signaling Technology Cat. # 9027, RRID: AB_2636984) for both longitudinal and histologic cohorts and yH2AX (Cell Signaling Technology Cat. # 2577, RRID: AB_2118010) for the histologic cohort only. After 1 hour of incubation with goat biotinylated anti-rabbit IgG (Vector, Cat. no. PK-6101) at room temperature, the sections were incubated with ABC reagent solution (Vector, Cat. no. PK-6101) for 30 minutes and with 3,3'-diaminobenzidine (DAB) solution (Vector, Cat. no. SK-4100) for 4 minutes. Afterward, the slices were counterstained with hematoxylin, dehydrated, and coverslipped with Eukitt mounting medium (Sigma-Aldrich, Cat. no. 03989). The second half of the slices were stained for apoptosis assessment according to the DeadEnd Fluorometric terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase–mediated dUTP nick end labeling (TUNEL) System (Promega, Cat. no. G3250) protocol. In short, the sections were deparaffinized, fixed in 4% formaldehyde, permeabilized with 20 μg/mL proteinase K solution, equilibrated, and incubated with terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase (TdT) for 60 minutes at 37°C in a humidified chamber. The reaction was stopped by immersing the slides in 2× saline sodium citrate (SSC), counterstained with DAPI, and mounted with Vectashield Antifade Mounting Medium. Washings were performed with 0.85% NaCl and PBS. The fresh cryopreserved sections were spared for β-Gal senescence assay. Tumor slices were fixed with 2% formaldehyde/0.2% glutaraldehyde solution (Sigma-Aldrich, Cat. no. G6257) for 15 minutes at room temperature. The sections were incubated with freshly prepared β-Gal solution (Sigma-Aldrich, Cat. no. B4252) overnight at 37°C. The slides were counterstained with hematoxylin and coverslipped with Eukitt mounting medium. The washes were done with PBS. All slides were light-protected and stored at +4°C before imaging using a slide scanner [3DHISTECH Pannoramic 250 Flash II slide scanner (RRID: SCR_022184)], and the quantification was performed with the Fiji package [ImageJ (RRID: SCR_003070)]. The DAB-positive area (Ki67 and yH2AX) was quantified through the percent area measurement presented as a ratio of positively stained DAB cells/nuclei and hematoxylin-positive cells. In the histologic cohort, TUNEL and β-Gal mean values were measured through equally applied thresholding.

## Statistical analysis
Statistical analysis and the graphical presentation of the data were performed using GraphPad PRISM [GraphPad Prism (RRID: SCR_002798), version 9.5.0]. Some of the elements of the figures were created with BioRender (RRID: SCR_018361). One-way or two-way ANOVA with post hoc tests have been performed. The log-rank (Mantel–Cox) test has been performed for survival analysis. Sample size for the in vivo longitudinal cohort was calculated using power analysis (one-way ANOVA). All experiments were replicated independently three times unless indicated differently. The data in the text are presented as relative averages or fold-induction ± SD, as indicated. The bars in the graphs are represented as the mean ± SEM. Differences between the groups were considered significant with P values < 0.05 (ns, nonsignificant; *, P < 0.05; **, P < 0.01; ***, P < 0.001; ****, P < 0.0001).

## Results

## DNA-PKcs phosphorylation in HNSCC following IR occurs independently of cellular HPV, and p53 status and can be inhibited by peposertib
RT with or without concomitant chemotherapy is a standard therapeutic option for most head and neck cancers. As these treatments cause early and late toxicities and their outcomes vary substantially depending on factors such as tumor subtype, localization, or molecular composition, novel therapeutic options, including rational combination approaches, are direly needed. In this study, we aimed to study the efficacy of IR in combination with the DNA-PKcsi peposertib in HNSCC cell lines that are genetically heterogenous and differ also in their HPV and p53 status. Whereas TP53 mutations are widely prevalent in HPV− head and neck cancers and encompass more than 80% of these cases ( ), in HPV+ tumors, the E6 oncoprotein of HPV leads to the degradation of p53 via an E3 ubiquitin ligase and hence prevents the detection and elimination of abnormal and potentially cancerous cells by p53 ( ). We have assessed basal and RT-induced DNA-PKcs Ser2056 and p53 Ser15 phosphorylation as well as total protein expression of p53 and p21 in a panel of seven HNSCC cell lines, including HPV− models with either functional or mutated p53 as well as HPV+ cells ( ). We detected basal p53 and p21 protein levels in the two p53 WT cell lines (UM-SCC-17B and UM-SCC-74A) and an increase in both total and Ser15-phosphorylated levels of p53 after IR. Cell lines bearing either a missense (UM-SCC-10B; ref. ) or nonsense (and UM-SCC-14A; ref. ) p53 mutation also express the p21 protein and comparably high basal levels of total p53, but no basal or RT-induced p53 Ser15 phosphorylation can be detected ( ). Lastly, HPV+ cell lines (UD-SCC-2, UM-SCC-104, and UPCI-SCC-154) express neither total nor phosphorylated p53 and negligible p21 levels ( ).
 Importantly, DNA-PKcs Ser2056 phosphorylation was induced after IR in all the studied cell lines, thus indicating the activation of repair of DNA DSBs via the NHEJ pathway ( ). At the same time, when the selective DNA-PKcsi peposertib was administered 30 minutes before RT, the DNA-PKcs Ser2056 phosphorylation levels were considerably lower across the entire cell line panel as compared with IR treatment alone. We also observed a DNA-PKcs inhibition–dependent and p53-independent increase in basal and irradiated p21 levels in the p53-mutated cell line UM-SCC-10B and both p53 WT cell lines ( ).

## Peposertib-mediated HNSCC radiosensitization occurs in vitro in p53 WT, p53-mutated, as well as HPV+ cell lines
To investigate how DNA-PKcs inhibition before IR impacts the viability and survival of HNSCC cancer cell lines with distinct HPV and p53 status, we performed crystal violet staining ( ; Supplementary Fig. S1A) and colony-forming assays (Supplementary Fig. S2D), respectively. We did not observe a significant decrease in viability upon peposertib treatment alone compared with the control condition in any of the cell lines except for the HPV+ UPCI-SCC-154 cells in which the viability was reduced by ∼24% ( ). On the contrary, IR alone decreased the viability of all the included cell models, albeit to a different extent. A single dose of 2 or 4 Gy reduced the viability of HPV+ and p53-mutated cells on average by ∼30 ± 7 (for 2 Gy) and ∼58% ± 5% (for 4 Gy) whereas of the p53 WT cells only by ∼3 ± 2 (for 2 Gy) and ∼16% ± 7% (by 4 Gy), respectively. Importantly, pretreatment with the DNA-PKcsi peposertib 30 minutes before 2 Gy of IR compared with IR alone decreased the viability significantly in all cell lines, whereas the combination treatment using 4 Gy had a similar effect solely in p53 WT and HPV+ HNSCC cell lines, most probably due to the already high radiation sensitivity of p53-mutated cell lines at this dose. To test whether there is a difference in the degree of radiosensitization by peposertib between the different cell lines or the three cell line groups, DMFs by peposertib for each cell line were computed based on changes in LD50 values determined by the Emax model (Supplementary Fig. S1B; ref. ). DMFs for all tested cell lines were above 2.5 and even substantially higher for the two p53-mutated models UM-SCC-10B and UM-SCC-14A, hence confirming that peposertib is a potent radiosensitizer of HNSCC cell lines regardless of their genetic background.
In line with these findings, the administration of peposertib alone does not impair the ability to form colonies in any of the cell lines, whereas already 2 Gy of IR provokes a decreased survival rate. When the cells are treated with both peposertib and 2 Gy of IR, clonogenic survival of most of them is further impaired (Supplementary Fig. S2D).
Besides, we screened this panel of cell lines for metabolic activity using an XTT assay ( ). Similarly to the data resulting from the crystal violet and colony-forming assays, peposertib alone does not significantly impact the metabolic activity of any of the studied cell lines. However, its combination with IR significantly radiosensitizes all the cells except the p53 WT-expressing UM-SCC-17B cell line, in which we detected a substantial but not significant reduction in metabolic activity. In addition, HPV+ cell lines seem to be more sensitive to both IR and combined peposertib + IR treatments compared with p53-mutated HNSCC cells and cells with functional p53. Noteworthy, we could observe a higher fraction of dead cells after IR and a combination of IR and peposertib compared with the control in all cell lines (Supplementary Fig. S3A).

## Combination of DNA-PKcs inhibition and IR arrests preferentially p53-dysfunctional HNSCC cells in the G2/M phase of the cell cycle
As HPV inactivates p53 to reinitiate cell division after terminal differentiation, we aimed to investigate whether HNSCC cell lines with distinct p53 and HPV status display different cell-cycle distribution patterns upon DNA-PKcs inhibition before IR. The results of a PI-based cell-cycle assay reveal that HPV+ [UD-SCC-2 – 73% ± 11% (vs. 13% ± 7%; P < 0.0001), UM-SCC-104 – 46% ± 11% (vs. 20% ± 4%; P = 0.0003), and UPCI-SCC-154 – 50% ± 12% (vs. 19% ± 3%; P = 0.0002)] and p53-mutated cell lines [UM-SCC-10B – 37% ± 7% (vs. 16% ± 4%; P = 0.0020) and UM-SCC-14A – 66% ± 3% (vs. 13% ± 1%; P < 0.0001)] treated with peposertib before IR strongly accumulate in the G2/M phase of the cell cycle over a time course of 24 to 48 hours compared with their respective untreated controls ( ; Supplementary Fig. S2A–S2C) Notably, G2/M accumulation of HPV+ and p53-mutated cells exposed to IR alone peaks around 24 to 36 hours before being released and steadily decreases to basal levels (Supplementary Fig. S2A–S2C). On the contrary, IR in combination with the DNA-PKcsi peposertib does not seem to induce any substantial G2/M arrest in the p53 WT cell lines at 48 hours after treatment [UM-SCC-17B – 27% ± 15% (vs. 16% ± 4%; P = 0.0044) and UM-SCC-74A – 14% ± 6% (vs. 16% ± 1%; P = 0.6746)] or at earlier time points ( ; Supplementary Fig. S2A–S2C).
 To further distinguish between the G2 and M phases of the cell cycle, Ser10 phosphorylation levels of histone H3 (pSer10-H3) as a mitotic marker were assessed by Western blotting in a representative cell line from each of the groups ( ). Upon combined treatment with peposertib and IR, H3 Ser10 phosphorylation is strongly reduced in both p53 WT (UM-SCC-74A) and HPV+ (UD-SCC-2) cells but not in the p53-mutated cell line UM-SCC-14A as compared with the untreated and single-modality–treated cells ( ). This, together with the PI-based cell-cycle data, indicates that the majority of the UM-SCC-74A cells found in the G2/M phase (∼13%) are in fact in a G2 arrest. Furthermore, although most of the p53-depleted cells are found in the G2/M phase 48 hours after combination treatment, H3 Ser10 phosphorylation decreases only in the UD-SCC-2 but not UM-SCC-14A cells. No reduction in the M-phase cells of the p53-mutated cell line UM-SCC-14A indicates that more than 40% of these cells are arrested in the G2 phase following IR + peposertib treatment. On contrary, the decrease in pSer10-H3 levels upon combined treatment in the HPV+ UD-SCC-2 cells infers that 70% of the designated G2/M cells are residing at this time point in the G2 phase of the cell cycle.
We next performed a generation tracking experiment to clarify whether the cell-cycle arrest of HPV+ and p53-mutated cells is terminal and whether p53 WT cell lines are arrested in the cell phase they reside in ( ). In this assay, a stronger curve shift from the basal intensity signal indicates higher proliferation due to dye loss in dividing daughter cells. As seen in , all HNSCC cell lines treated with peposertib alone proliferate at the same rate as untreated controls and they partially or completely recover from IR-inflicted damage, restore their proliferative capacity, and reenter the cell cycle. On the contrary, almost a complete arrest of proliferation in all cell lines after combining the drug with IR can be observed. This suggests that although a particular genetic background promotes diverse cell phase distribution patterns, cell division after a combination of IR and DNA-PKcs inhibition is impaired independently of the p53 status.

## Peposertib- and IR-induced abrogation of proliferation promotes cell fates determined by their p53 and/or HPV background
Sun and colleagues ( ) reported previously that p53 functionality impacts the mode of cell death upon DNA-PKcs inhibition in combination with IR in cancer cells of various origins. Hence, we next aimed to determine whether the distinct patterns of cell-cycle distribution in the p53 WT, p53-mutated, and HPV+ HNSCC models following IR + peposertib lead to different cellular fates. As p53 triggers apoptosis and controls the balance between activation of prosenescent and proapoptotic pathways ( ), we assessed the induction of apoptosis ( and ; Supplementary Fig. S3B) and senescence ( ) following the various treatments in the cell line panel. Our data indicate that only cell lines lacking functional p53 (e.g., HPV+ and p53-mutated cells) undergo apoptosis after IR and/or after the combination of IR and DNA-PKcs inhibition, as seen from increases in cleaved lamin A levels ( ), caspase-3 activity ( ), and annexin V positivity (Supplementary Fig. S3B). On the contrary, no lamin A cleavage, caspase-3 activation, or annexin+ cells can be observed following these treatments in neither of the two p53 WT cell lines UM-SCC-17B and UM-SCC-74A, indicating that these cells completely circumvent activation of the caspase cascade. Instead, a significant increase in the number of senescent cells can be visualized by the cytochemical β-Gal assay solely in these p53 WT cells upon IR, and this increase is yet significantly substantiated when peposertib treatment is used prior to IR ( ). To validate the effect of the p53 function on the cell fate outcome, we silenced the TP53 gene in the UM-SCC-74A cell line ( ) and assessed the induction of apoptosis and senescence following the different treatments with annexin V ( and ) and β-Gal assays ( ), respectively. Upon p53 silencing, a significant increase in the percentage of apoptotic cells ( and ) and a reduced number of senescent cells ( ) after the combination of IR and DNA-PKcs inhibition can be detected, hence resembling the treatment outcome of the other p53-depleted cell lines used in this study.
 

## Peposertib administration before IR delays DNA repair in HNSCC cell lines regardless of their genetic background
To assess the level of DNA damage in HNSCC after various perturbations and to estimate the capacity of the cells to repair the damaged DNA via HR when NHEJ is blocked by peposertib treatment, we performed immunostaining targeting γH2AX and RAD51, respectively, 3, 24, and 48 hours after treatment ( ; Supplementary Fig. S4). We did not observe any significant differences in γH2AX or RAD51 foci formation between the cell line groups at 3 hours after treatment: there was neither an increase in foci count upon peposertib distribution alone as compared with unperturbed cells nor any impact of the drug on the elevated γH2AX and RAD51 foci formation upon IR. However, a dramatic and highly significant increase in the number of γH2AX and RAD51 foci after combining DNA-PKcs inhibition and IR compared with IR alone can be observed almost uniformly across all the studied cell models at later time points, especially 48 hours after treatment. This might indicate a delay in DNA repair and the presence of unresolved DSBs upon peposertib administration prior to IR due to the blockage of the NHEJ pathway. Altogether these data suggest that DNA-PKcs inhibition does not affect basal DNA damage levels in HNSCC in vitro models but at the same time significantly increases IR-induced γH2AX foci formation as well as RAD51 activity, and this occurs independently of the genetic background of the HNSCC cells, including their HPV or p53 status.
 

## HPV+ UD-SCC-2 xenografts respond significantly better to the combination of DNA-PKcs inhibition and IR than p53 WT UM-SCC-74A tumors
To study the response of HNSCC cell lines with distinct HPV backgrounds in vivo, two s.c. xenograft mouse models were established using the HPV+ UD-SCC-2 and p53 WT UM-SCC-74A cell lines ( ). Once the tumors reached a size of 150 to 300 mm3, mice were randomized to four different treatment groups (Supplementary Fig. S5A), which included the fractionated IR regimen with a cumulative dose of 10 Gy alone or in combination with peposertib administered orally 15 minutes before IR for five consecutive days. Regular assessments of weight and overall health status, as well as visual skin inspection, did not indicate any toxicity arising from either peposertib, IR, or their combined treatment in any of the animals (Supplementary Fig. S5C). Although we did not find any significant differences in tumor growth between vehicle and peposertib-treated groups in both UD-SCC-2 and UM-SCC-74A xenografts ( and Supplementary Figs. S5B, S6A and S6C), IR treatment alone and DNA-PKcs inhibition in combination with IR significantly impacted tumor sizes in both animal models throughout the whole follow-up. Interestingly however, whereas DNA-PKcs inhibition in combination with IR compared with IR alone caused a significant and sustained tumor growth suppression in the HPV+ UD-SCC-2 xenografts until the end of the experiment [V (IR + peposertib) = 232 ± 324 mm3 and V (IR) = 1,098 ± 400 mm3, respectively], the p53 WT UM-SCC-74A tumors, whose growth was initially strongly delayed by the combination treatment, eventually relapsed throughout the follow-up [V (IR + peposertib) = 641 ± 576 mm3 and V (IR) = 957 ± 413 mm3, respectively]. Consequently, we did not observe any significant differences in UM-SCC-74A tumor sizes following IR treatment in the presence or absence of DNA-PKcsi on the day of experiment termination. These findings suggest a possibly higher benefit of DNA-PKcs inhibition in combination with IR in the HPV+ UD-SCC-2 HNSCC models as compared with the p53 WT UM-SCC-74A xenografts.
 

## HPV+ xenografts are cleared by apoptosis whereas p53 WT tumors undergo senescence upon combined treatment with IR and DNA-PKcs inhibition
To determine whether the distinct cell fates observed following the combined DNA-PKcs inhibition and IR treatment in vitro are also recapitulated in the more complex in vivo environment, we assessed the presence of apoptotic and senescent cells in tumor tissues collected either 72 hours after the last treatment dose (“histologic cohort”) and at the end of the follow-up experiment (“longitudinal cohort”) by performing TUNEL staining for nuclear DNA fragmentation and β-Gal assay ( and ; Supplementary Fig. S6B and S6D), respectively. We also assessed the proliferation status (Ki67 staining; and ; Supplementary Fig. S6B and S6D) and the presence of DNA DSBs (yH2AX staining; and ) in these tissues. Seventy-two hours after IR treatment alone or in combination with peposertib, a significant reduction in the tumor growth ( and ) in both xenograft models was observed, albeit plausibly due to distinct underlying processes.
 In the p53 WT UM-SCC-74A xenograft, Ki67 staining was significantly reduced in both the IR and combination treatment groups, indicating a significant reduction in tumor cell proliferation ( ). At the same time, no presence of DNA damage could be detected by yH2AX staining 3 days after treatment, indicating the possibility that the IR-induced damage has already been resolved by this time ( ). In accordance with the in vitro data, no apoptosis but an increased number of senescent cells after IR as well as the combination treatment could be observed in the UM-SCC-74A tumors ( ).
In contrast, in the HPV+ UD-SCC-2 xenograft model, no significant decrease in the Ki67-positive area could be detected after any of the treatments ( ), even though a reduced tumor size 3 days after treatment ( ) and a better long-term treatment response ( and ) were observed after IR and the combination treatment in the histologic and the longitudinal cohorts, respectively. However, high DNA damage levels after IR and particularly after the combination treatment are present in these tumor tissues, indicating a delay in the repair of DNA DSBs after blocking of the NHEJ pathway ( ). In line with the in vitro data, large apoptotic areas and no senescent cells could be seen in the HPV+ UD-SCC-2 xenografts 72 hours after treatment ( ).
In tumor tissues collected at the end of the experiment of the longitudinal cohort, no major differences in Ki67 expression or in the presence of fragmented DNA (TUNEL staining) between the treatment groups within the same tumor type could be found (Supplementary Fig. S6B and S6D). Nevertheless, and in accordance with the in vitro data, we observed significantly larger apoptotic regions in the HPV+ UD-SCC-2 tumors (Supplementary Fig. S6D) compared with the p53 WT UM-SCC-74A xenografts (Supplementary Fig. S6B) that almost did not feature apoptosis (33.7% and 3%, respectively). Additionally, the p53 WT UM-SCC-74A tissues stained positive for β-Gal after peposertib treatment alone and weakly positive after exposure to IR combined with peposertib administration (Supplementary Fig. S6B).

## Discussion
Although cure rates of patients with HNSCC diagnosed at an early stage are as high as 80% ( ), management of patients with cancer with locally advanced tumors is very challenging, and novel treatment approaches that would reduce the risk of recurrence without increasing toxicities are needed ( ). Identification of molecular markers that shape tumor response to therapeutic interventions and could be used for patient stratification may significantly improve treatment outcome. In this study, we assessed the effectiveness of NHEJ blocking by peposertib in combination with IR in distinct HNSCC models. As TP53 mutations and HPV positivity are two molecular characteristics commonly found in HNSCC tumors (60%–80% and 84%, respectively; ref. ), we also investigated how HPV and p53 status affect the outcome of this intervention.
Some recent studies also investigated the effects of DNA-PKcs inhibition on various endpoints in preclinical HNSCC models ( – ). For example, Lee and colleagues ( ) assessed the effects of two preclinical DNA-PK inhibitors, KU-57788 and IC87361, on clonogenic growth of a panel of HPV− HNSCC cell lines, whereas Dobler and colleagues ( ) studied the effect of DNA damage response inhibitors, including the dual DNA-PK/mTOR inhibitor CC-115, on the induction of senescence as one of the pathways of cell inactivation in HNSCC. These reports did not include any comparisons between HPV− and HPV+in vitro and in vivo HNSCC models and, furthermore, all the studied DNA-PK compounds were preclinical drugs, whereas the selective DNA-PKcsi peposertib used in this study is in active clinical development ( ).
When using peposertib as a radiosensitizer, none of previous studies reported its effects on a panel of distinct HNSCC models, and solely the HPV− HNSCC cell line FaDu was included in some reports among cancers of different origins ( , , ). In this study, we implemented two distinct HNSCC xenograft models with clinically relevant treatment scenarios.
Our data confirm that DNA-PKcs is a valid target in irradiated HNSCC models as we observed an IR-induced increase in DNA-PKcs Ser2056 autophosphorylation, an established pharmacodynamic biomarker for DNA-PKcs activity, in all tested cell lines. As previously reported ( ), this IR-induced Ser2056 autophosphorylation was substantially impaired when the cells were exposed to 300 nmol/L of the DNA-PKcsi peposertib. As to the p53 status of the studied models, total and phosphorylated p53 protein is absent in HPV+ cells due to the physiologic ability of the viral E6 protein to degrade p53 ( ). Similarly, the presence of the total but absence of the phosphorylated p53 protein in p53-mutated cell lines suggests that the loss of p53 function phenotypically resembles these cells to HPV+ HNSCC lines.
Compared with the exposure to IR alone, the administration of the selective DNA-PKcsi peposertib prior to IR-induced DNA damage resulted in decreased cell viability and proliferation in all studied HNSCC cell lines. Our findings show that HPV+ cells and their derived tumors are more sensitive to IR, especially compared with the p53 WT models. These observations are in line with previously published preclinical and clinical data ( , ). We did not observe any IR dose-dependent responses in p53-mutated UM-SCC-10B and UM-SCC-14A cells combined with peposertib (their proliferation after 2 and 4 Gy IR was affected to the same extent), likely due to the high IR sensitivity of models featuring p53 mutations ( , ). We also did not observe any reduction in the pSer10-H3 mitotic marker levels 48 hours after peposertib therapy in combination with the 4 Gy dose as compared with the combination with 2 Gy in the p53-mutated cell line UM-SCC-14A, indicating the maintenance of the mitotic capacity and thus less prominent treatment response with higher IR dose. This finding indicates that the same effect can be achieved by the addition of peposertib to a lower IR dose compared with IR alone.
Based on most of the readouts, all cell lines reduce their proliferation after combined treatment, but only p53-depleted cell lines predominantly arrest in the G2/M phase of the cell cycle. As p53 is mainly involved in the transition from G1- to S-phase, p53-proficient cells cannot progress further in the cell cycle, whereas cells lacking p53 can pass the G1/S checkpoint but subsequently arrest in the G2 phase through p53-independent regulators ( , ). Partially in accordance with our findings, Sun and colleagues ( ) demonstrated that a single dose of 5 Gy IR causes G1/M and G2/M phase arrests in p53 WT A375 melanoma and A549 lung cancer cell lines. In their study, the combination of 5 Gy IR with 1 μmol/L peposertib arrested the cells predominantly in the G2/M phase with no cells found in the S phase for three consecutive days. On the contrary, only partial and unstable arrest after combination treatment was observed in HPV+ HeLa cells.
Following DNA-PKcs inhibition, IR-induced DNA damage cannot be repaired via NHEJ, and the cells presumably rely on alternative pathways including HR-based repair ( , ). Impairment of NHEJ leads to the accumulation of unrepaired DNA DSBs, which activates ATM/ATR signaling and DDR machinery and thus phosphorylates p53, leading to cell-cycle arrest, DNA repair, or distinct cell fates ( ). As our data show, the level of DNA damage induced by the combined treatment is significantly high even 48 hours after treatment regardless of the cell line, thus suggesting persisting damage and delay in its resolution, which activates cell death and/or senescence pathways. These observations are also in accordance with other studies demonstrating the delay in DNA repair when DNA-PKcs inhibitors are combined with DNA-damaging agents ( , ). Although p53 impacts cell-cycle progression and survival in DNA repair–dependent and -independent ways ( ), we did not observe any significant differences in DNA damage levels (yH2AX foci) and DNA repair via HR (RAD51 foci) between the cell lines featuring functional and nonfunctional p53. This is to a certain extent a surprising finding as p53 has been shown to directly regulate NHEJ as well as to associate with multiple NHEJ proteins such as DNA ligase IV, XRCC4, or Artemis ( – ). Our results further support the notion that the cell fate after the DNA damage induced by DNA-PKcs inhibition in combination with IR is affected by its p53 status. It has been previously reported that cells lacking functional p53 due to its degradation by HPV proteins or due to the presence of a loss-of-function mutation arrest in the G2/M cell-cycle phase upon the combined treatment of peposertib and various DNA-damaging agents, and are subsequently eliminated by apoptosis, whereas cells with functional p53 undergo senescence ( , , – ). High-level DNA damage induced by IR combined with NHEJ inhibition in cells with proficient p53 activates the p53/ATM pathway (e.g., p21, CHK1, CHK2, and cell-cycle proteins) and therefore induces premature senescence to protect genome integrity ( , ). In the absence of p53, the cells suffer from the increased level of DNA DSBs and die by mitotic catastrophe due to severe chromosomal aberrations ( ).
Although we could not observe the induction of apoptosis in p53 WT HNSCC cells, cell death was detected after combination treatment in all tested cell lines, indicating that nonapoptotic cell death mechanisms such as necrosis might be involved. Importantly, we show that upon the combined peposertib and IR treatment, p53 WT tumor cells mainly undergo senescence, a nonproliferating cell state with a dual anti- and pro-tumorigenic phenotype. It has been demonstrated that other genotoxic chemotherapies induce senescence, and these senescent cells can create local and systemic inflammatory responses ( ). It is important to note that the panel of cell lines used in this study is, similarly to the HNSCC landscape, genetically very distinct, and the differences between these cells are by far not restricted only to their p53 or HPV status. Nevertheless, even in this heterogenous panel of HNSCC models, p53 functionality seems to determine quite well the cell fate following a combined application of IR as a source of DNA damage and peposertib as a DNA repair inhibitory agent. To confirm that normal/abnormal p53 function is the most critical and possibly unique factor that underlines cell fate decisions in these settings, p53 isogenic models should be used in follow-up mechanistic experiments. We partially accounted for this limitation of our study by silencing p53 in the p53 WT cell line UM-SCC-74A. Indeed, p53-deficient UM-SCC-74A cells reverted their phenotype after IR + peposertib treatment, thus mimicking cell outcome effects of other p53-depleted cells.
In our work, RT was delivered by the SmART precision RT platform, which allows to image the lesion and to plan the radiation procedure similarly as done in the clinic. Precision radiation targets the region of interest by sparing healthy tissues from unnecessary IR. To the best of our knowledge, peposertib efficacy in combination with IR was previously assessed solely by using cabinet irradiators. Hence, this is the first study to use precise IR treatment for this purpose. The significant suppression of tumor growth upon combination treatment in both p53 WT UM-SCC-74A as well as HPV+ UD-SCC-2 models is in line with recent data reported by Zenke and colleagues ( ). Regarding tumor regrowth after treatment, we did not detect any recurrences of HPV+ tumors within the 45-day follow-up period, whereas p53 WT xenografts relapsed 30 days after treatment. Notably, Zenke and colleagues ( ) reported tumor regrowth in the p53-deficient HNSCC cell line FaDu 30 days after treatment initiation. However, a remarkable treatment response was achieved by increasing either the peposertib dose (up to 150 mg/kg) or by applying a longer, 6-week RT regimen with peposertib doses of up to 50 mg/kg. We hypothesize that high levels of apoptotic cells and the absence of senescence can be one of the reasons for the sustained treatment response of UD-SCC-2 tumors ( and ). Although tumor growth suppression and reduced proliferation capacity of the p53 WT UM-SCC-74A models after IR and combination treatment could be documented as well, treatment-induced senescent cells possibly contribute to the promotion of tumor recurrence observed in the longitudinal study. If further preclinical or clinical studies would indicate that p53 WT HNSCC cancers relapse soon after the IR + peposertib combination treatment, the therapy-induced senescent cells could be subsequently eliminated by senolytic agents ( ).
In conclusion, diverse patterns of cell-cycle distribution and tumor control upon the combined IR + peposertib treatment in the heterogenous panel of HNSCC models strongly suggest that the outcome of this treatment combination is determined by the p53 and HPV status. Although peposertib radiosensitizes HNSCC tumors independently of their genetic background, a better treatment response was observed both in vitro and in vivo in models with dysfunctional p53. HPV and p53 thus emerge as critical factors that may shape treatment outcomes for patients with HNSCC eligible to receive a combined IR and DNA-PKcs–targeting treatment. Precise patient stratification based on these two parameters might be relevant also in the case of other malignant conditions. Pending further studies, whereas the treatment-induced senescence detected in this study solely in p53 WT models can become a target for the follow-up senolytic approach, the strong induction of apoptosis in p53 dysfunctional tumors could be synergistic with proapoptotic therapies that are currently on the horizon in the clinic.
