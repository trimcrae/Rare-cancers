## ABSTRACT

## Introduction
The pathogenesis of diabetes mellitus (DM) and its complications has been previously linked to elevated levels of interleukin‐6 (IL‐6); IL‐6 triggers intracellular pathways through interaction with the IL‐6 receptor (IL‐6R). This study aimed to determine the association of single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) IL‐6 −597G/A (rs1800797), −572G/C (rs1800796), −174G/C (rs1800795), its receptor (IL‐6R) Asp358Ala (+48892A/C, rs2228145) and DM or its complications in the Czech population.

## Materials and Methods
In total, 669 subjects participated in this case‐control study, including 167 controls, 119 patients with type 1 DM (T1DM), and 383 patients with type 2 DM (T2DM). The subjects were monitored for glycemia, glycated hemoglobin, lipid profile, glomerular filtration rate (GFR), and common complications, such as diabetic nephropathy (DN), retinopathy (DR), (poly)neuropathy (DPN), and periodontitis (P).

## Results
Frequencies of the IL‐6 and IL‐6R alleles or genotypes, and IL‐6 haplotypes were similar between the controls and the patients with T1DM or T2DM (P > 0.05). However, lower values of GFR were observed in diabetic patients with IL‐6 −174CC homozygotes compared to other (CG and GG) genotypes (P ≤ 0.05). In addition, the IL‐6R Asp358Ala variant was associated with DN (P = 0.031, OR = 1.74, 95% CI: 1.05–2.89). After subclassification according to the type of DM, the significant association of the IL‐6R polymorphism with DN was only found in T1DM (P = 0.013, OR = 2.90, 95% CI: 1.25–6.71).

## Conclusions
This study implies a significant relationship between the IL‐6R Asp358Ala polymorphism and DN in Czech T1DM patients.

Keywords: Diabetic nephropathy, IL‐6, Polymorphisms

## INTRODUCTION
Diabetes mellitus (DM) is a group of metabolic diseases characterized by hyperglycemia resulting from dysregulated insulin secretion, defects in insulin action in target tissues, or both . Type 1 diabetes mellitus (T1DM) occurs due to autoimmune destruction of pancreatic β cells leading to a loss of insulin secretion. In contrast, type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) develops due to increased insulin resistance, ranging from hyperinsulinemia to a decreased insulin secretion. The high blood glucose level in diabetic patients can eventually lead to the development of diabetic macrovascular or microvascular complications, including nephropathy (DN), retinopathy (DR), polyneuropathy (DPN), and periodontitis (P) .
Inflammation contributes to the pathogenesis of both types of DM. In the case of T1DM, inflammation may play a role in the inhibition of β‐cell function, causing peripheral insulin resistance . Low‐grade chronic inflammation and activation of the immune system are also associated with the development of obesity‐related insulin resistance and T2DM . Patients with either T1DM or T2DM exhibit elevated levels of inflammatory markers including interleukin‐6 (IL‐6) , , , .
The pleiotropic cytokine IL‐6 is involved in immune regulation, control of inflammatory processes, hematopoiesis, and oncogenesis. IL‐6 is produced by various types of cells ; it promotes acute phase response and stimulates the transition from acute to chronic inflammation . Increased IL‐6 released by the skeletal muscle following exercise may improve insulin sensitivity . IL‐6 also generates insulin resistance in the liver, and in conjunction with other pro‐inflammatory cytokines, it may cause damage to β cells . IL‐6 is associated with glomerular basement membrane thickening . It was also reported to improve nerve function in an experimental model of DN in rats and to facilitate differentiation of osteoclasts and bone resorption, a process that occurs in the progression of periodontal disease .
IL‐6 triggers intracellular pathways through interaction with its receptor (IL‐6R) complex consisting of signal transducing chain glycoprotein 130 (gp130) and the binding chain IL‐6R, which exists in a membrane or soluble form . Whereas gp130 is expressed by all cells, expression of membrane‐bound IL‐6R is limited to a few types of cells, such as hepatocytes, neutrophils, monocytes, or pancreatic islet cells , . Anti‐inflammatory classical IL‐6 signaling is mediated through membrane‐bound IL‐6R, whereas soluble IL‐6R (sIL‐6R) activates pro‐inflammatory trans‐signaling. sIL‐6R can be generated either by mRNA alternative splicing or ectodomain shedding of the membrane‐bound IL‐6R via members of a disintegrin and metalloproteinase (ADAM) family, ADAM 10 and ADAM 17 , . The polymorphism IL‐6R Asp358Ala (+48892A/C, rs2228145) located near the ADAM cleavage site enhances the proteolytic cleavage of membrane‐bound IL‐6R, resulting in elevated levels of sIL‐6R in the blood of individuals homozygous for the Ala (allele C) variant , , and altered susceptibility to immune‐based diseases including T1DM and T2DM , , , .
Since the expression of the IL‐6 gene can be affected by the promoter single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) −174G/C (rs1800795), −572G/C (rs1800796) and −597G/A (rs1800797) , , their association with the development of DM and its complications has been extensively studied , , , . However, existing case‐control studies conducted in various populations and focusing mainly on the allele and genotype frequencies of IL‐6 provided inconsistent findings. In contrast, the IL‐6R Asp358Ala variant has been very recently associated with T2DM DN in the Iranian population .
Our study aimed to analyze the allele, genotype, and haplotype frequencies of polymorphisms in the IL‐6 and IL‐6R genes in Czech healthy subjects and patients with T1DM and T2DM. We also investigated the relationship between IL‐6 and IL‐6R gene variants with diabetic clinical parameters and complications of DM.

## MATERIAL AND METHODS
The study was performed with the approval of the Ethics Committees of the Faculty of Medicine, Masaryk University Brno (No. 15/2009, approved on December 21, 2009; No. 13/2013, approved on May 20, 2013) and St. Anne's University Hospital (without number/2005, approved on June 30, 2005). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants in line with the Declaration of Helsinki before inclusion in the study.

## Study population, inclusion and exclusion criteria, and clinical examinations
In this case‐control study, 669 unrelated adult subjects of Caucasian descent from the Czech Republic were enrolled. One hundred and nineteen patients with T1DM and 383 patients with T2DM were selected from the patients' pool of the outpatient Departments of Internal Medicine (St. Anne's and Brno‐Bohunice University Hospitals, Czech Republic). The participants had different stages of diabetic kidney disease at baseline. They were originally enrolled to be followed prospectively for the study of the natural history of diabetic complications between 2005 and 2013. The study design, inclusion/exclusion criteria, and baseline characteristics of the study population have been published previously . DM diagnosis was established from clinical symptoms (such as polyuria, polydipsia, and weight loss) and biochemical parameters (glycemia, glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c), ketoacidosis, and autoantibody status). All patients were treated with oral antidiabetic drugs and/or insulin. The duration of diabetes was classified as the period from diabetes onset until the enrollment in this study. Levels of glycemia, glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c), total cholesterol, triglycerides, high‐density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL‐C), low‐density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL‐C), body mass index (BMI), and further metabolic or clinical parameters were documented at the time of enrollment. The stage of DN was based on urinary albumin excretion (UAE) and the stage of chronic kidney disease by glomerular filtration rate (GFR) was assessed by creatinine clearance (based on 24‐h urine collection) in a subset of DM patients (N = 380) and repeatedly measured at least once in 6 months. The study group consisted of 62 normoalbuminuric subjects (urinary albumin excretion (UAE) <30 mg/24 h), 109 subjects with persistent microalbuminuria (UAE 30–300 mg/24 h), 153 subjects with persistent proteinuria (UAE >300 mg/24 h), and 56 subjects with end‐stage renal disease (ESRD) with renal replacement therapy (GFR <15 mL/min/1.73 m2 or permanent renal replacement therapy) followed up in the specialized Departments of Nephrology of Brno University Hospitals. The presence of ophthalmologically verified diabetic retinopathy (DR) and periodontitis (P) was documented. Exclusion criteria for T1DM and T2DM were current pregnancy or lactation (not the use of contraceptives), acute inflammation at the time of diagnosis (e.g., respiratory and urinary tract infections) and recent surgery. The controls were selected from subjects referred to the Clinic of Stomatology for reasons other than periodontal disease (such as dental caries, orthodontic consultations, and preventive dental checkups) during the same period as patients and matched for age and gender. Exclusion criteria were a history of systemic diseases, such as diabetes mellitus, cardiovascular disorders (e.g., coronary artery diseases), malignancy, primary immunodeficiency, or HIV infection, and current pregnancy or lactation (not the use of contraceptives). The periodontal status was assessed in a subgroup of 294 subjects (127 diabetic patients and 167 healthy controls). The diagnosis of periodontitis/non‐periodontitis was determined based on a thorough clinical examination, medical and dental history, tooth mobility, and radiographs, as in our previous studies , .

## Genetic analysis
Genomic DNA was extracted from peripheral blood leukocytes by standard proteinase K digestion and the phenol–chloroform extraction method .
SNPs −597G/A (rs1800797), −572G/C (rs1800796), −174G/C (rs1800795) in the IL‐6 gene and Asp358Ala (+48892A/C, rs2228145) in the IL‐6R gene were determined by PCR‐restriction fragment length polymorphism (RFLP). Details of genotyping for the IL‐6 −597G/A (rs1800797), −572G/C (rs1800796), −174G/C (rs1800795) polymorphisms were previously published , .
Amplification of DNA fragment containing IL‐6R Asp358Ala (+48892A/C, rs2228145) polymorphism was carried out in a reaction volume of 25 μL containing 75 ng (1.5 μL) of genomic DNA, 0.24 μM (0.6 μL) of forward GTTAAGCTTGTCAAATGGCCTGTT and reverse primers CAGAGGAGCGTTCCGAAGG according to Galicia et al. , 1 U (0.2 μL) of DNA polymerase (Thermo Fischer Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), 3.5 mM (3.5 μL) of MgCl2, 2.5 μL 10× MgCl2–free reaction buffer with (NH4)2SO4 and 0.2 mM (0.2 μL) deoxyribonucleoside triphosphate mix (Thermo Fischer Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). Denaturation for 5 min 95°C was followed by 25 cycles of 95°C for 1 min, 57°C for 1 min, and 72°C for 1 min. The last synthesis step was extended to 10 min 72°C. The PCR products were then digested with HinfI restriction enzyme. The restriction was performed in a volume of 30 μL consisting of 25 μL of the PCR product, 3 μL 10× CutSmart Buffer (New England Biolabs, Ipswich, MA, USA), and 5 U (0.1 μL) of HinfI enzyme (New England Biolabs, Ipswich, MA, USA) and incubated overnight at 37°C. The digested products were electrophoresed in 2% agarose gel and visualized by ethidium bromide staining. Sizing of products was performed using a 50 bp ladder (Thermo Fischer Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). The genotypes were identified as CC (115 + 190 bp), CA (115 + 190 + 305 bp) and AA (305 bp – wild‐type, common allele).

## Statistical analysis
The power analysis was performed using Fisher's exact test assuming standard settings of probability measures, that is, alpha level <0.05 and power = 0.80. Data were presented and suitable analysis was done according to the type of data obtained for each parameter; they are presented as mean and standard deviation (SD) for continuous variables and absolute/relative frequencies for categorical variables. Deviations from Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium expectations and comparison of the differences in the genotype frequencies were determined using the Chi‐square test. The allele frequencies were counted from the observed numbers of genotypes using Fisher's exact test. Contingency table analysis, odds ratios (ORs), 95% confidence intervals (CI), and significance values were estimated and the value of P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Haplotype frequencies were calculated using the SNP Analyzer 2 program (http://snp.istech.info/istech/board/login_form.jsp). Pairwise LD for all possible two‐way comparisons among three variants in the IL‐6 gene cluster was determined according to the Lewontin standardized disequilibrium coefficient D′. One‐way analysis of variance (anova) or Kruskal–Wallis anova was performed to compare continuous variables among the groups. The association of potential predictive and risk factors with assessed endpoints was evaluated via odds ratio (OR) estimation. Both univariate and multivariate adjusted logistic regression models were used and final OR estimates were supplied with 95% confidence intervals and P‐values. Multivariate models were adjusted for age, sex, and BMI as the most important confounders.
All statistical calculations were done using Statistica version 14 (StatSoft Inc., Tulsa, Okla., USA).

No significant differences were found in the allele and genotype frequencies of all four studied polymorphisms between controls and DM patients (P > 0.05). The frequencies of AC + CC genotypes of the IL‐6R Asp358Ala (+48892A/C, rs2228145) variant were non‐significantly higher in T1DM patients than in healthy subjects (P = 0.06, OR = 1.50, 95% CI = 0.94– 2.42).
 Haplotype analysis was performed for SNPs (−597G/A, −572G/C, and −174G/C) of the IL‐6 gene. Distributions of IL‐6 haplotypes were similar between the control and DM groups (P > 0.05, Table  ). Strong linkage disequilibrium (LD), measured by squared correlation coefficient (r
2), was observed between the variants −597G/A and −174G/C in the IL‐6 gene (D′ = 1.00 in controls, data not shown).
 The Kruskal–Wallis anova test showed significant differences in glycemia levels and GFR among genotypes IL‐6 −174G/C variant in patients with DM (P ≤ 0.05). The values of GFR tend to decrease from DM patients with IL‐6 −174GG genotypes (median and interquartile range = 1.15; 0.69, 1.75 mL/s) to CG heterozygotes (0.97; 0.71, 1.58 mL/s) followed by CC carriers (0.78; 0.57, 1.33 mL/s, see Table  ). According to the data, the highest glycemia levels were found in carriers of IL‐6 −174CC genotype (8.30; 6.85, 11.40 mmoL/L). Lower levels were detected in individuals with CG genotypes (8.00; 6.80, 10.30 mmoL/L) and the lowest in GG homozygotes (7.60; 6.20, 9.90 mmoL/L, Table  ). Similar findings were found for the IL‐6 −597G/A variant which is in a strong linkage disequilibrium (D′ = 0.996 in diabetic patients) with the IL‐6 −174G/C polymorphism (Table  ). The analysis of the association between the other clinical parameters or diabetic complications and studied SNPs was carried out in DM patients without regard to the type of DM. No statistically significant differences in relationship to the IL‐6 and IL‐6R gene variability and lipid levels, HbA1c, and periodontal index parameters, that is, clinical attachment loss (CAL) or periodontal depth (PD), were observed in DM patients (P > 0.05, data not shown).
 The occurrence of diabetes complications including DR, DPN, or P was mostly not affected by the IL‐6 and IL‐6R polymorphisms (data not shown). However, a comparison of the allele and genotype frequencies of the IL‐6R Asp358Ala polymorphism in diabetic patients with DN (microalbuminuria, proteinuria, or ESRD) and those without DN (normoalbuminuria) showed statistically significant differences. The frequencies of the AC and CC genotypes of this variant were slightly, although non‐significantly, higher in diabetic patients with DN (59.1% vs 41.9%, P = 0.079, OR = 1.79, 95% CI: 0.93–3.43), similarly, as in patients (N = 56) with only ESRD (66.1% vs 41.9%, P = 0.18, OR = 1.94, 95% CI: 0.73–5.13) than in diabetics without nephropathy. Additionally, the frequency of the C allele was higher in diabetic patients with DN (37.7% vs 25.0%, P = 0.031, OR = 1.74, 95% CI: 1.05–2.89) similarly, as in diabetics with ESRD (40.2% vs 25.0%, P = 0.228, OR = 1.55, 95% CI: 0.76–3.14) compared to diabetics without nephropathy (Table  ).
 After classifying patients according to the type of DM, the significant association of IL‐6R AC and CC genotypes and C allele with DN was present only in T1DM (66.7% vs 33.3%, P = 0.019, OR = 3.88, 95% CI: 1.25–11.98, and 42.7% vs 20.8%, P = 0.013, OR = 2.90, 95% CI: 1.25–6.71, respectively). However, no differences in genotype or allele frequencies (57.6% vs 53.9%, P > 0.05, OR = 1.02, 95% CI: 0.43–2.42, and 36.8% vs 30.8%, P > 0.05, OR = 1.21, 95% CI: 0.63–2.31, respectively, Table  ) were found between T2DM without and with DN.
 

## DISCUSSION
Diabetes mellitus is a complex, multifactorial disease where genetic and environmental factors and their interactions play a significant role , . Although IL‐6 is generally considered a pro‐inflammatory cytokine, recent studies suggest its protective, regenerative, and anti‐inflammatory role , . The type of receptor to which IL‐6 binds determines whether it activates the pro‐inflammatory or anti‐inflammatory pathway. IL‐6 pro‐inflammatory trans‐signaling is mediated through sIL‐6R, while anti‐inflammatory classical signaling occurs through membrane‐bound IL‐6R.
Elevated levels of IL‐6 were observed in patients with T1DM or T2DM , , , . Since the expression of the IL‐6 gene can be affected by its promoter polymorphisms IL‐6 −597G/A (rs1800797), −572G/C (rs1800796), −174G/C (rs1800795) , , we investigated frequencies of these SNPs in the Czech patients with T1DM and T2DM and healthy controls. Our study found no association between the SNPs IL‐6 −174G/C (rs1800795) and T1DM in the Czech population, which agrees with the meta‐analysis performed by Xu et al. However, a recent meta‐analysis suggests that the IL‐6 ‐174G/C polymorphism may be associated with susceptibility to decreased T2DM and increased T1DM risk . In contrast, our study did not confirm the relationship of SNPs IL‐6 −174G/C (rs1800795) , , and −572G/C (rs1800796) to T2DM development, which is consistent with the findings of Chen and co‐workers . In summary, the results describing the association of IL‐6 promoter polymorphisms with DM in the literature are inconsistent , , , , , .
In addition, we conducted haplotype analysis since SNPs in the IL‐6 promoter may not affect transcription through an additive mechanism, but rather through complex interactions defined by the haplotype . This study did not show any impact of IL‐6 haplotypes on the development of T1DM or T2DM. Our results are inconsistent with those of a Danish study of T2DM patients and individuals with normal glucose tolerance, where IL‐6 composite genotypes were associated with T2DM and metabolic syndrome , but they are consistent with a study by Lu and co‐workers. Their findings do not support substantial associations between the IL‐6 polymorphisms and the risk of T2DM .
The IL‐6 −174G/C (rs1800795) and −597G/A (rs1800797) polymorphisms were found to be in strong LD with each other and were associated with glycemia and GFR values in this study. The IL‐6 −174G allele exhibits higher transcriptional activity than the IL‐6 −174C variant, and the IL‐6 −174G allele carriers have elevated levels of IL‐6 in comparison to homozygotes CC , . In this study, DM patients carrying the IL‐6 −174GG genotypes had decreased glycemia and increased GFR compared with those with other genotypes. These results are consistent with prior research, which has suggested that intensive glycemic control can reduce the risk of developing diabetic complications, including DN . It can also improve kidney function and slow down the rate of GFR decline . Regarding IL‐6 −174G/C (rs1800795) and glycemia, our results are in line with a study of a family sample from the USA , in which fasting plasma glucose was significantly lower in the IL‐6 −174GG homozygotes.
Functionally active polymorphism IL‐6R Asp358Ala (+48892A/C, rs2228145) affects the proteolytic cleavage of membrane‐bound IL‐6R , , . The IL‐6R 358Asp variant was shown to be a risk factor for T2DM in the Danish population . However, this study, similarly to other studies , , did not confirm the association of the IL‐6R variant with T2DM. In contrast, the IL‐6R 358Asp variant and Asp358Asp genotype were statistically more prevalent in Chilean patients with T1DM than in healthy controls . The same variant and genotype were also more frequent in the Czech patients with T1DM.
Described results support the protective role of the Ala variant against inflammatory diseases based on the hypothesis that soluble gp130 (sgp130) and sIL‐6R constitute a buffer in the blood for IL‐6 , , . The disturbance of the IL‐6‐neutralizing sIL‐6R‐sgp130 buffer was observed in T2DM patients . The binding of sgp130 to the IL‐6/sIL‐6R complex neutralizes IL‐6 activity, resulting in an anti‐inflammatory effect that may be more efficient in the blood of individuals with increased levels of sIL‐6R (i.e., IL‐6 358Ala homozygotes). The development of diabetes complications is a result of a combination of chronic hyperglycemia in T1DM and T2DM patients, as well as their lifestyle and genetic predispositions . Although the IL‐6 variants were suggested as susceptibility loci for diabetes complications , , , this study did not confirm such associations for DR, DPN, or periodontitis. However, our investigation of the IL‐6R Asp358Ala (+48892 A/C, rs2228145), which had previously been conducted only in relation to DN in a smaller cohort without significant results , detected the association with DN and ERSD, particularly in T1DM. This partially agrees with a very recent study from the Iranian population, which found an association of this IL‐6R polymorphism with DN in T2DM .
IL‐6 appears to be involved in the pathological changes occurring in the kidneys during DN . In addition to the expansion of the mesangial cell, it also contributes to glomerular basement membrane thickening and hypertrophy of podocytes , , . The increased production of IL‐6 has been observed in patients with nephropathy . IL‐6 is produced by renal cells and IL‐6 mRNA local expression in the kidneys is enhanced in patients, which correlates with the severity of the mesangial expansion . Moreover, the levels of IL‐6 in the plasma or urine are positively associated with the progression of DN , . As inflammation is known to contribute to DN progression and IL‐6 pro‐inflammatory activity is mediated through IL‐6R trans‐signaling, the functional relationship of the IL‐6R variant with DN in the Czech population is not surprising. IL‐6R trans‐signaling triggers the transition from acute to chronic inflammation . As a result, neutrophils accumulate at sites of inflammation, resulting in the shedding of IL‐6R from their membrane; this, in turn, results in increased IL‐6 trans‐signaling. It then leads to the expression of IL‐6 and the mononuclear cell‐attracting cytokine monocyte chemoattractant protein‐1 (MCP‐1), which recruits monocytes. Interestingly, mesangial cells in the kidneys exhibit a similar process . However, since these cells do not express membrane‐bound IL‐6R, they are responsive only to the IL‐6/sIL‐6R complex. IL‐6 trans‐signaling induces MCP‐1 synthesis and secretion in mesangial cells. This mechanism could be responsible for the protection of IL‐6R Asp358Asp (AA) homozygotes, which produce lower amounts of sIL‐6R , , against the development of DN and progression to ERSD in Czech diabetic patients. Due to the lower levels of sIL‐6R in IL‐6R Asp358Asp subjects, their renal cells, including mesangial cells, might be less activated by IL‐6 trans‐signaling.
There are limitations to this study that need to be considered. First, the case‐control study design is generally vulnerable to population stratification. However, the studied population was relatively homogeneous and of Czech Caucasian origin, restricted to a limited geographical area. Second, DM as a multifactorial disease arises from interactions between several gene variants and environmental factors, and each genetic polymorphism generally has only a small effect on the observed phenotype. However, we focused on one variant in the IL‐6R gene and three polymorphisms in the IL‐6 gene, as well as their haplotypes, as they may have a greater impact on the progression of DM than the individual variants alone. Another complicating factor of this study was the relatively low number of subjects enrolled, especially in the T1DM group. Despite this limitation, the sizes of the studied groups were comparable to those in other case‐control studies. Another drawback of this study is that lipid profiles and HbA1c parameters were not examined in the control group. Nevertheless, the presence of diabetes was excluded based on anamnestic data and glucose level/concentration testing with a glucose meter before collection for genetic analysis. Finally, we did not measure IL‐6 and IL‐6R mRNA or protein expression, as the functional characteristics of their gene polymorphisms have been previously analyzed and are widely accepted.
Study strengths include the investigation of the relationship between the IL‐6 and IL‐6R gene variants and their association with DM, diabetic complications, and clinical and biochemical parameters. This evaluation provided us with better insight into the role of these polymorphisms in the pathogenesis of DM and its complications. To the best of our knowledge, this study is the first to identify IL‐6R as a candidate gene in the development of DN in T1DM.
In conclusion, our study suggests that the IL‐6R polymorphism contributes to the development of DN and ERSD in T1DM patients. Furthermore, IL‐6 −174G/C (rs1800795) and −597G/A (rs1800797) variants may influence GFR and glycemia levels. However, further research involving a larger independent cohort is necessary to validate our findings.
