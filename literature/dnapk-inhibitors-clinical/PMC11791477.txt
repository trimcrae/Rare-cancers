## Abstract
Ataxia Telangiectasia–mutated (ATM) inhibitors are being developed as radiosensitizers to improve the antitumor effects of radiotherapy, but ATM inhibition can also radiosensitize normal tissues. Therefore, understanding the elevated risk of normal tissue toxicities is critical for radiosensitizer development. This study focused on modeling the relationship between acute mucosal toxicity, radiation dose, fractionation schedule, and radiosensitizer exposure. The ATM inhibitor WSD0628 was combined with single or fractionated doses of radiation delivered to the oral cavity or esophagus of Friend Leukemia virus B (FVB) mice. The potentiation by WSD0628 was quantified by a sensitizer enhancement ratio (SER), which describes the changes in radiation tolerance for radiation combined with WSD0628 relative to radiation-only regimens. WSD0628 profoundly enhanced radiation-induced acute oral and esophageal toxicities. For oral mucosal toxicity, the enhancement by WSD0628 with 3 fractions of radiation resulted in an SER ranging from 1.3 (0.25 mg/kg) to 3.1 (7.5 mg/kg). For the 7.5 mg/kg combination, the SER increased with increasing number of fractions from 2.2 (1 fraction) to 4.3 (7 fractions) for oral toxicity and from 2.2 (1 fraction) to 3.6 (3 fractions) for esophageal toxicity, which reflects a loss of the normal tissue sparing benefit of fractionated radiation. These findings were used to develop a modified biologically effective dose model to determine alternative radiation schedules with or without WSD0628 that result in similar levels of toxicity. Successful radiosensitizer dose escalation to a maximally effective therapeutic dose will require careful deliberation of tumor site and reduction of radiation dose volume limits for organs at risk.

## Introduction
The Ataxia Telangiectasia–mutated (ATM) kinase plays a key role in the DNA damage response after exposure to ionizing radiation. ATM is activated by radiation-induced DNA double-strand breaks and phosphorylates a host of downstream targets to orchestrate DNA repair, cell-cycle arrest, apoptosis, and cellular senescence pathways ( ). ATM function is deficient in the autosomal recessive disorder Ataxia Telangiectasia (A-T), which is characterized by defects in DNA repair and cell-cycle arrest and results in profound radiation hypersensitivity. Therefore, pharmacologic inhibition of ATM presents an attractive strategy to radiosensitize otherwise radioresistant tumors. Several ATM inhibitors are under development and have improved the efficacy of radiotherapy (RT) in preclinical models ( – ). Because treatment of patients with A-T with conventional doses of RT is associated with substantial radiation-induced normal tissue toxicities, an increased risk of radiation toxicities is expected from radiosensitizers that mimic the A-T phenotype ( , ). Thus, the effective clinical translation of ATM inhibitors will require understanding how normal tissue radiosensitization impacts the therapeutic window.
Acute normal tissue toxicities of the aerodigestive tract and other rapidly proliferating tissues limit the radiation dose that can be safely delivered to most solid tumors. In single-fraction stereotactic RT, the use of high doses requires very conformal radiation fields with rapid dose fall off into normal tissues to minimize toxicities. Alternatively, conventionally fractionated RT uses lower doses and less steep dose gradients, and instead, achieves normal tissue sparing through the superior DNA repair capacity of normal tissues compared with tumors ( ). By dividing treatment into multiple daily fractions, normal tissues can more effectively repair and recover between fractions, which improves radiation tolerance and reduces toxicities ( ). However, understanding of the impact of ATM inhibitors on the normal tissue tolerance to single or fractionated RT is lacking. In the present study, we evaluated the potentiation of normal tissue effects by WSD0628, a novel, highly potent ATM inhibitor being evaluated in a phase 1 clinical trial in combination with RT (NCT05917145). To inform RT planning, we developed a mucosal toxicity model integrating radiation dose, fractionation schedule, and WSD0628 dose and exposure. Our results demonstrate that WSD0628 markedly radiosensitizes the mouse mucosa and eliminates the normal tissue–sparing benefit of fractionated RT. These findings provide important information for the development and clinical translation of WSD0628 and other novel radiosensitizers.

## Materials and Methods

## Animal experiments
All animal procedures were performed according to guidelines established by the NIH (Bethesda, MD) and approved by the Mayo Clinic Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (Rochester, MN). Non–tumor-bearing female FVB mice (6–10 weeks old; Charles River Laboratories International, Inc.; RRID: MGI:3609372) were housed on a 12-hour light/dark cycle with unlimited access to food and water and were allowed to acclimate to the facility for at least 48 hours before an experiment. Animals were stratified by body weight and randomized across groups. Daily observations and weights were used to assess toxicity after treatment. Mice with ≥20% body weight loss after treatment were euthanized.

## Drug and radiation delivery
The ATM inhibitor WSD0628 provided by Wayshine Biopharm International was prepared according to the methods of compound 14 described in U.S. Patent #11919899B2 ( ). The drug was formulated as a suspension in 0.5% w/v hydroxypropyl methyl cellulose, 0.1% w/v Tween 80, and distilled water. Both vehicle and drug were administered via oral gavage 15 to 30 minutes before each radiation treatment.
Radiation was delivered under general anesthesia with a mixture of 2.5% vaporized isoflurane and pure oxygen and immobilized using a stereotactic bite block. All irradiations were performed at 225 kVp, 20 mA, and 0.3 mm of Cu filtration using the X-RAD SmART irradiator (Precision X-ray Inc.; RRID: SCR_025774) with integrated cone beam CT (CBCT) and fluoroscopic imaging. For oral cavity irradiations, CBCT was used to accurately position the first animal in each group, and the same CBCT-based coordinates were used to treat the remaining animals in each group. The intermouse positioning reproducibility of this method is on the order of 1 mm ( ). The oral cavity was irradiated with a single anterior–posterior 10-mm circular beam, as described previously ( ). The SmART-ATP (Scientific Solutions B.V.) software package was used to determine the dose rate of this setup.
For esophageal irradiation, fluoroscopic imaging was used to accurately position the first animal in each group, and the same fluoroscopy-based coordinates were used to treat the remaining animals in each group. A pilot study revealed intergroup positioning reproducibility on the order of 1 mm (Supplementary Data S1). The thoracic esophagus was irradiated using opposed anterior–posterior–posterior–anterior beams with a primary 15-mm square collimator (Precision X-ray Inc.) and a secondary 5 × 15-mm copper collimator. The delivered dose rate with this setup was determined with EBT4 radiochromic films using a methodology previously described ( ).

## Histology and immunofluorescence
Formalin fixed/paraffin embedded tissue were sectioned and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E). For immunofluorescence (IF) staining, tissues were submerged in 4% paraformaldehyde for 3 to 5 days followed by 2 days in 30% sucrose in PBS. The fixed tissues were embedded in Tissue-Tek optimal cutting temperature compound (Sakura), frozen on dry ice, and stored at −20°C. A Leica CM1860 cryostat (RRID: SCR_025772) was used for sectioning tissue into 6 μm thickness. IF staining was performed using a standard protocol for frozen tissue described elsewhere ( ). IF images were acquired using the LSM 780 confocal microscope with a 63× objective (Zeiss Group; RRID: SCR_020922).

## Fractionation scheme and follow-up
The oral mucosal toxicity endpoint was defined as a body weight loss ≥20% (compared with pretreatment) between days 7 and 14 after the start of treatment. The rate of oral mucosal toxicity (proportion of animals meeting toxicity endpoint) was measured for 1-, 3-, 5-, and 7-fraction radiation regimens with or without 7.5 mg/kg WSD0628 as well as 3-fraction radiation regimens combined with 0.25, 1, 2.5, or 5 mg/kg WSD0628. Each treatment schedule consisted of equally sized fractions. For increasing number of fractions and/or WSD0628 doses, the radiation dose per fraction was reduced to produce similar rates of toxicities across schedules and build dose–response curves (see Supplementary Table S1 for information about doses).
In a preliminary esophageal toxicity study, mice losing ≥10% of their pretreatment weight failed to recover to baseline 2 weeks after treatment. Therefore, the esophageal toxicity endpoint was defined as a body weight loss ≥10% (compared with pretreatment) between days 7 and 14 after the start of treatment. The rate of esophageal toxicity was measured for 1- and 3-fraction regimens with and without 7.5 mg/kg WSD0628. Each treatment group consisted of 5 to 6 mice. Data collection was conducted over several studies, in which each study consisted of 5 to 8 groups (see Supplementary Table S1 for information about treatment groups and sample sizes).

## Drug exposure analysis
The exposure of the WSD0628 doses has been measured previously for single doses of 0.1, 1, 5, and 10 mg/kg ( ). Using these data to fit a pharmacokinetic model, total concentration–time profiles in plasma were simulated for a single dose of 0.25, 1, 2.5, 5, and 7.5 mg/kg WSD0628. The area under the curve (AUC) between 0 and 24 hours (AUC0–24) was computed as a measure of total drug exposure using the linear trapezoidal rule.

## Biologically effective dose modeling
The α–β ratio (α/β) for the oral mucosa datasets (RT-only or RT+7.5 mg/kg WSD06278) was estimated by using a logistic model that incorporated a quadratic term:fi(ni,di;b0,b1,b2)=11+exp[‐(b0+b1nidi+b2nidi2)](B)In which ni is the number of fractions, di is the radiation dose per fraction of a given treatment i, and b0, b1, and b2 are estimated parameters representing the intercept, linear, and quadratic effects, respectively. The α/β ratio was estimated as the ratio of b1/b2 ( ).
The biologically effective dose (BED) is a quantity used for the comparison and conversion between different radiation fractionation schedules, but the classical BED model does not account for radiosensitizing drugs. Therefore, the BED model was modified by rearranging to incorporate the effects of 7.5 mg/kg WSD0628 (Supplementary Data S2). The modified BED model was used to calculate radiation doses of RT-only and RT + WSD0628 that have equivalent BED values and would result in similar levels of oral mucosal toxicity. As an external validation, the same model and the same parameters were tested on the esophageal toxicity dataset. To evaluate the performance of the model, residuals were computed as the difference between the total radiation dose calculated using the modified BED model and the D50 values estimated from dose–response modeling.

## Results

## ATM kinase inhibitor WSD0628 blocks radiation-induced γH2AX foci formation
IF analysis of irradiated tissue was performed to evaluate ATM activation after treatment. ATM phosphorylates H2AX (γH2AX) and rapidly forms foci at DNA double-strand breaks almost immediately after radiation. To evaluate the inhibition of ATM by WSD0628 in the oral epithelium of FVB mice, 5 Gy was delivered to the oral cavity ( ). Tongues (N = 3 per treatment) were collected 15 minutes after RT and processed for γH2AX staining ( ; Supplementary Fig. S1A). Irradiated tissues showed robust γH2AX foci formation relative to control, whereas the combination with 7.5 mg/kg WSD0628 markedly suppressed γH2AX foci formation. These results confirm that WSD0628 effectively inhibited radiation-induced ATM kinase activity in the mouse oral epithelium.
 

## WSD0628 radiosensitizes mouse oral mucosa
Radiation-induced oral mucositis is associated with pain and decreased oral intake. In mice, this toxicity manifests as acute weight loss 1 to 2 weeks after the start of treatment. To characterize acute mucositis in mice, radiation was delivered to the oral cavity of mice, and body weights were collected daily ( ). In a pilot study, 3 fractions of 5 Gy each (3 × 5 Gy; N = 7) resulted in minimal weight loss following radiation. In contrast, 3 × 10 Gy (N = 7) resulted in significant weight loss, with six of seven animals meeting the toxicity endpoint (≥20% weight loss) between days 9 to 11 after the start of treatment. The regimen 3 × 5 Gy combined with 7.5 mg/kg WSD0628 (N = 7) resulted in a similar level of toxicity as 3 × 10 Gy alone. These results demonstrate that the ATM inhibitor WSD0628 exacerbates RT-induced acute weight loss.
The impact of RT with or without WSD0628 on oral mucosal integrity was evaluated via H&E staining of tongues (N = 2 per treatment) harvested on day 10 after the start of treatment ( ). Tongues of untreated mice (Supplementary Fig. S1B) and mice treated with 3 × 5 Gy exhibited thick stratified keratinized epithelium with abundant papillae and a well-organized palisading basal layer. In contrast, tongues of mice treated with 3 × 10 Gy and 3 × 5 Gy + WSD0628 showed striking histologic changes in the irradiated region characteristic of mucositis, including near-complete epithelial loss and inflammation extending into the submucosal tissue. Collectively, these data are consistent with clinical manifestations of oral mucositis in patients and support the concept that acute changes in body weight can be used as a surrogate for the extent of oral mucositis associated with radiation and radiosensitizers.

## Radiosensitization of the oral mucosa is related to drug exposure
The radiosensitizing effect in the oral mucosa was evaluated across a range of WSD0628 doses. Radiation delivered to the oral cavity in 3 fractions was combined with 0, 0.25, 1, 2.5, 5, or 7.5 mg/kg WSD0628. At higher WSD0628 doses, the total radiation dose was reduced (see Supplementary Table S1 for information about doses) to generate dose–response curves spanning similar rates of toxicity (proportion of animals reaching ≥20% weight loss) across treatment combinations ( ). The radiation dose–response for 0 mg/kg demonstrates a negligible rate of toxicity below 22.1 Gy, which progressively increases with radiation doses and reaches a 100% rate of toxicity at 30 Gy. The dose–response curves shift to lower radiation doses with increasing WSD0628 doses. For a dose of 7.5 mg/kg, the rate of toxicity is 0% below 6 Gy and reaches 100% at 10.8 Gy. The total radiation dose required to reach the toxicity endpoint in 50% of mice (D50) was used as a measure of radiation tolerance and was estimated for each dose–response curve using logistic regression. The relative decrease in D50 compared with RT-only was quantified by a SER and ranged from 1.3-fold (0.25 mg/kg) to 3.1-fold (7.5 mg/kg; ).
 Understanding the relationship between drug exposure and toxicity is essential in drug development. Simulated plasma concentration–time profiles ( ) were used to compute the AUC0–24 as a measure of drug exposure. The AUC0–24 from simulated pharmacokinetic profiles agreed with the AUC0–24 from measured total plasma concentration data for a subset of doses (Supplementary Fig. S2), so simulated profiles were used in this analysis. The AUC0–24 values represent a greater than dose proportional increase in exposure. Additionally, the radiation tolerance of the oral mucosa decreased nonlinearly as drug exposure increased (shown in linear-log scale, ). This complex relationship between WSD0628 dose, exposure, and radiosensitization highlights the importance of exposure–toxicity studies in guiding dose-escalation trials evaluating radiosensitizer safety and tolerability.

## WSD0628 eliminates tissue sparing of fractionated RT in the oral mucosa
The impact of WSD0628 on the normal tissue–sparing benefit of fractionated RT was assessed in the oral mucosa. Radiation was delivered in 1, 3, 5, or 7 fractions ( ) with or without 7.5 mg/kg WSD0628. For increasing numbers of fractions, the radiation dose per fraction was reduced to produce similar rates of toxicities across schedules (see Supplementary Table S1 for information about doses). Dose–response curves were generated for each regimen by measuring the rate of toxicity (proportion of animals reaching ≥20% weight loss) for a range of radiation doses ( ). For each fractionation regimen, RT + WSD0628 combinations resulted in dose–response curves at lower radiation doses compared with RT-only. The RT-only dose–response curves shifted to higher total radiation doses with increasing numbers of fractions, whereas the RT + WSD0628 dose–response curves were relatively unchanged across the different fractionation schedules. The D50 value was estimated for each dose–response curve ( ). WSD0628 was associated with a 2.2-, 3.1-, 4.0-, and 4.3-fold decrease in D50 for 1, 3, 5, and 7 fractions, respectively, as quantified by the SER. Isoeffect curves consist of D50 values as a function of number of fractions and represent treatment regimens that result in equivalent levels of toxicity ( ). As expected, the D50 value associated with RT-only increased with increasing numbers of fractions. Interestingly, RT + WSD0628 regimens resulted in D50 values that were relatively constant with increasing numbers of fractions. In other words, fractionated RT-only provided normal tissue sparing and improved radiation tolerance, but fractionation of RT in WSD0628 combinations did not increase radiation tolerance. These data demonstrate that 7.5 mg/kg WSD0628 eliminates the normal tissue–sparing benefit of fractionated RT in the oral mucosa.
 

## WSD0628 radiosensitizes the mouse esophagus
Mucosal surfaces throughout the aerodigestive tract are rapidly proliferating tissues, and acute mucositis in all these tissues are dose-limiting complications of RT. To examine whether our findings were applicable in a distinct mucosal tissue, the impact of WSD0628 on radiation tolerance was examined in the thoracic esophagus. In preliminary studies, irradiation of a 5 mm length of thoracic esophagus with high doses of RT, with or without WSD0628, was relatively well tolerated (Supplementary Fig. S3A and S3B). In contrast, irradiation of a 15 mm length of thoracic esophagus resulted in approximately 10% to 20% weight loss, so studies evaluating esophageal toxicity were performed with the larger radiation field size ( ). In a pilot study, 1 × 12.5 Gy (N = 8) resulted in minimal weight loss following radiation ( ). In contrast, 1 × 17.5 Gy (N = 7) and 1 × 12.5 Gy + 7.5 mg/kg WSD0628 (N = 8) resulted in significant weight loss with all animals meeting the toxicity endpoint (≥10% weight loss) between 8 and 10 days after treatment. Thus, the ATM inhibitor WSD0628 exacerbates weight loss induced by irradiation of the esophagus.
 Esophageal tissues (N = 2–3 per treatment) were harvested on day 8 after the start of treatment to evaluate the esophageal mucosal integrity via H&E staining ( ; Supplementary Fig. S3C). In untreated mice, the esophagus consisted of well-preserved stratified squamous epithelium, lamina propria, and muscularis without significant inflammation (Supplementary Fig. S3D). Mice treated with 12.5 Gy exhibited damage to the squamous epithelium, with focal areas of ulceration. In contrast, ulceration and epithelial damage extended circumferentially in mice treated with 17.5 Gy alone and 12.5 Gy + WSD0628. These mice exhibited extensive acute inflammation extending into the lamina propria and muscularis with necrotic debris distending the esophageal lumen. Collectively, these data support the concept that acute changes in body weight can be used to evaluate the extent of esophageal toxicity associated with radiation and radiosensitizers.

## WSD0628 eliminates tissue sparing of fractionated RT in the esophagus
The impact of WSD0628 on fractionated RT to the esophagus was assessed to confirm the elimination of tissue sparing in another acute responding organ. Radiation delivered in 1 or 3 fractions was combined with or without 7.5 mg/kg WSD0628 (see Supplementary Table S1 for information about doses). Dose–response curves were generated for each regimen by measuring the rate of toxicity (proportion of animals reaching ≥10% weight loss) for a range of radiation doses ( and ). RT + WSD0628 combinations resulted in dose–response curves at lower radiation doses compared with RT-only. For RT-only, the 3-fraction dose–response curve shifted to higher total radiation doses compared with 1-fraction curve, whereas the 1- and 3-fraction RT + WSD0628 dose–response curves were relatively similar. The D50 value was estimated for each dose–response curve ( ). WSD0628 was associated with a 2.2- and 3.6-fold decrease in the D50 value for 1 and 3 fractions, respectively, as quantified by the SER. In agreement with the oral mucosal toxicity results, the D50 value for RT-only increased with increasing numbers of fractions, but the D50 value for RT + WSD0628 was unchanged for increasing numbers of fractions. Taken together, these data demonstrate that WSD0628 robustly sensitizes the esophageal mucosal to radiation and eliminates the normal tissue–sparing benefit of fractionated RT.

## A modified BED model to compare RT-only and RT + WSD0628 treatments
The BED model can be used to manipulate total radiation dose and dose per fraction to determine an alternative treatment schedule that results in similar levels of toxicity. The classic BED model uses the α/β ratio, which describes the sensitivity of tissue to different radiation fraction sizes. The α/β ratio was estimated using a logistic model with linear and quadratic effects ( ), in which b1 represents the linear term, b2 represents the quadratic term, and the b1/b2 ratio is an estimate of α/β. The RT-only and RT + 7.5 mg/kg WSD0628 oral mucosal toxicity datasets were fit separately to evaluate changes in α/β by WSD0628. An α/β value of 8.0 Gy was estimated for RT-only regimens. In contrast, RT + WSD0628 resulted in an α/β value approaching ± infinity, which corresponds to an increase in b1 and a b2 value not different from zero ( ). These changes in α/β indicate a loss in sensitivity to changes in fraction size and are consistent with our results that WSD0628 eliminates the normal tissue–sparing benefit of fractionated RT.

## Discussion
Integration of novel radiosensitizers with standard RT regimens has potential for improving tumor control but also elevates the risk for enhanced normal tissue toxicities. One major class of radiosensitizers is inhibitors of ATM, a central mediator of DNA damage response to radiation. Multiple ATM inhibitors have demonstrated robust radiosensitizing effects in preclinical tumors ( – ) and also in rapidly proliferating normal tissues ( – ). The potential of elevated radiation-induced normal tissue toxicities is a key consideration in radiosensitizer development, because equal enhancement of tumor and normal tissue effects would not improve the therapeutic window ( ). In the current study, RT combined with the ATM inhibitor WSD0628 was evaluated for the risk of acute toxicities in mucosal epithelia. We demonstrated that WSD0628 significantly reduces radiation tolerances and eliminates the normal tissue–sparing benefit of fractionation. Thus, integrating radiosensitizers with RT requires modifications to treatment planning that adequately spare organs at risk in order to maximize the therapeutic window for these combinations.
Preclinical studies of radiosensitizing effects can help guide the translation of treatment modifications to maximize the therapeutic window. Some preclinical studies evaluating toxicity have used histologic changes as the primary toxicity endpoints, whereas clinical studies evaluate toxicity based on patient-reported outcomes or physician reporting using grading scales ( ). In this context, the functional endpoint used in the present study may be more directly translatable to clinical effects. Similar to the evolution of radiation mucositis in patients with cancer, mucositis and associated decrease in oral intake, as measured by body weight loss, occurred approximately a week after high-dose radiation ( ). Evaluating histopathologic changes in preclinical studies can be highly informative, but using a histologic endpoint in the large-scale dose-exploration assays performed in this study would be incredibly labor-intensive. Instead, functional changes associated with mucositis can be readily characterized by acute body weight loss and at a much larger scale. Similar strategies for functional readouts could be used across other acutely responding tissues in the context of developing these potent radiosensitizers for additional indications.
Radiation dose-intensification can markedly enhance tumor control, but the extent of intensification is limited by elevated risks for radiation-induced toxicity. The benefits of radiation dose-intensification are best exemplified by the profound improvements in local tumor control for most solid malignancies achieved with stereotactic body RT (SBRT; ref. ). In comparison with conventional RT that delivers curative doses of radiation in small (2–3 Gy), daily radiation fractions over several weeks, SBRT strategies deliver a similar total dose in five or fewer radiation treatments. These SBRT strategies are only possible by significantly reducing the volume of normal tissues exposed to the highest doses of radiation ( ). In the present study, combination of the ATM inhibitor WSD0628 with radiation profoundly enhanced the sensitivity of oral and esophageal mucosa to radiation with a relatively steep drug exposure–response relationship. This result suggests that the volume of normal tissues exposed to the combination of drug and radiation needs to be significantly limited, similar to SBRT, in order to allow effective dose-escalation of a potent radiosensitizer. Highlighting this concept, a recent phase 1 clinical trial of peposertib, a DNA-PK inhibitor, combined with radiation resulted in a high rate of toxicity in patients treated with palliative doses of radiation, and meaningful dose-escalation of peposertib was not possible for patients with head and neck cancer treated with definitive radiation ( ). Homozygous inactivation of either ATM or DNA-PK are associated with marked sensitivity to whole-body irradiation in mouse models ( , ), which likely reflects gut mucosal toxicity, and patients with inactivation of ATM are similarly highly sensitive to the acute effects of radiation ( ). Collectively, these findings emphasize the importance in accounting for potential radiosensitization of essentially all acutely responding tissues when integrating these potent radiosensitizers with RT.
Conventional RT capitalizes on the potential for normal tissues to repair radiation-induced DNA damage more efficiently than tumor tissues to achieve a therapeutic window ( ). This differential DNA repair capacity presumably is linked to deficiencies in DNA repair that evolve during tumorigenesis and enable genomic instability characteristic of most tumors. In this study, ATM inhibition by WSD0628 resulted in more profound radiosensitization with increasing number of fractions and eliminated the normal tissue–sparing benefit of fractionated RT. ATM is a central mediator of DNA damage response, and ATM inhibition restricts the repair of DNA damage ( – ). Therefore, loss of the sparing effects of fractionation likely results from a reduced normal tissue DNA repair capacity and accumulation of damage between fractions, which would result in enhanced tissue damage. Similarly, a recent study demonstrated more profound radiosensitization with the ATM inhibitor AZD0156 when combined with two fractions of radiation as compared with a single fraction of radiation ( ). These results highlight the importance of further studies to define radiation fractionation schedules that optimize tumor cell kill enhancement while maintaining adequate normal tissue sparing.
The BED model is commonly used to compare different radiation fractionation schedules with regard to predicted efficacy and toxicity ( ). In this model, the magnitude of the sparing effects of fractionation is characterized by the α/β ratio, in which a larger α/β ratio corresponds less sparing effects ( ). We estimated an α/β value of 8 Gy for the mouse oral epithelium, which is consistent with prior studies ( ), whereas integration of a full dose of WSD0628 resulted in an α/β value approaching infinity. These changes in α/β reflect the loss of the sparing effects of fractionation and suggest that the level of toxicity associated with RT + WSD0628 depends only on the total radiation dose and drug exposure. To extend the utility of the BED model for comparing radiation toxicities combined with WSD0628, we developed a modified BED model. In theory, such modified model could be used to revise existing normal tissue radiation exposure constraints. For example, if the mean dose to the oral cavity is normally limited to <15 Gy for patients receiving hypofractionated radiation (30 Gy in 5 fractions), then the modified BED model estimates that this constraint should be reduced to <4.3 Gy for an equivalent level of toxicity when radiation is combined with a maximally effective exposure to WSD0628. The transportability of our model across two different mucosal sites (the oral cavity and esophagus) highlights the potential to generalize our results across gastrointestinal mucosal surfaces and possibly other mucosal sites ( ). Further work will be required to understand how ATM inhibitors or other potent radiosensitizers impact the tolerability for small volumes of normal tissue. Nonetheless, the data provided suggest a preclinical strategy that could be used to model increased risks for normal tissue toxicities and to critically inform radiation planning dose constraints when developing these novel inhibitors as clinical radiosensitizers.
Successful clinical development of novel radiosensitizers will critically depend on limiting the risk for enhanced radiation-associated normal tissue toxicities. Several potent radiosensitizers targeting ATM, DNA-PK, or other core repair pathways are in clinical development for various disease sites. In this context, extension of preclinical modeling across other acutely responding tissues could be highly informative. Although the pathophysiology of acute radiation effects is relatively well understood, there is a limited understanding of the mechanisms driving late radiation effects. Late toxicities can be especially challenging to address in early-phase clinical trials due to their delayed manifestation and the relatively rare incidence of severe toxicities. Preclinical modeling presents a valuable strategy to bridge this gap in knowledge and inform how novel radiosensitizers impact the risk of late radiation–induced toxicities. For instance, a previous study in ATM knockout mice demonstrated protection against radiation-induced neuronal apoptosis ( ), and a recent study by our group demonstrated no brain pathology after cotreatment with the ATM inhibitor AZD1390 and radiation in mice ( ). These preliminary data suggest that ATM inhibitors will have a reasonable toxicity profile in combination with radiation for brain tumor indications in the two ongoing clinical trials with AZD1390 (National Clinical Trial 03423628) and WSD0628 (National Clinical Trial 05917145). As clinical experience with these and other radiosensitizer progresses, coupled with additional preclinical modeling, the radiobiologic model described in this study could be improved to estimate risks for both acute and late effects and provide an important tool for clinical development of novel radiosensitizers.

## Conclusion
Preclinical models integrating radiation dose, fractionation, radiosensitizer drug, and exposure are critical to help guide dose-finding studies of potent radiosensitizers in early-phase clinical trials. Modifications to RT treatment planning are necessary to maximize the therapeutic window in the clinical translation of ATM inhibitors as radiosensitizers.
