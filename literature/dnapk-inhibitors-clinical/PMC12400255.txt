## ABSTRACT

## Background
Microscopic colitis (MC) is still an underdiagnosed disease due to its primarily histological appearance. We aimed to address the scarcity and inconsistency of data on MC risk factors.

## Methods
Our protocol was prospectively registered in PROSPERO (CRD42022286624). We systematically searched PubMed, Embase, and Cochrane from inception to January 6, 2025. Cohort, case–control, and cross‐sectional studies were included. Controls were distinguished with or without a histopathological examination. We used the random‐effect model to calculate pooled odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs).

## Results
The systematic search yielded 6493 articles, of which 45 were meta‐analyzed. We found increased odds for MC in the case of nonsteroidal anti‐inflammatory drug (NSAID) and statin use compared to histological (OR = 2.57, CI: 1.45–4.53; OR = 2.15, CI: 1.14–4.05) and random (OR = 2.56, CI: 1.13–5.79; OR = 1.84, CI: 0.58–5.80) controls. Our results did not show an association between proton pump inhibitors (PPIs) and MC, compared to histological controls (OR = 1.81, CI: 0.75–4.35), except in the case of random controls (OR = 4.31, CI: 1.66–11.20). Neither current (OR = 1.35, CI: 0.88–2.06) nor previous smoking (OR = 1.26, CI: 0.67–2.39) increased the odds of MC compared to histological controls. In contrast, the odds doubled for current smoking and slightly changed for past smoking when MC patients were compared to random controls (OR = 2.40, CI: 1.64–3.50; OR = 1.11, CI: 0.93–1.31).

## Conclusions
NSAID and statin treatment were associated with an increased risk for MC compared to histological controls. PPIs and smoking showed only an association with MC when random controls were used.

Keywords: histological controls, MC, microscopic colitis, NSAIDs, PPIs, smoking

## Introduction
Microscopic colitis (MC) has an incidence of approximately 11.4 cases per 100.000 person‐years [ ]. MC is a chronic inflammation of the large bowel, with chronic watery diarrhea as a leading symptom that leads to impaired quality of life. Based on histopathological features, MC's three subtypes can be differentiated: collagenous colitis (CC), lymphocytic colitis (LC), and incomplete microscopic colitis (iMC) [ ]. Therefore, its diagnosis requires well‐trained general practitioners [ ], gastroenterologists with endoscopic knowledge, and up‐to‐date pathologists. Thus, the number of cases is likely to be underestimated.
Various risk factors for MC have been reported; however, the results are contradictory. Firstly, while most new MC cases are reported in the elderly population [ ] and account for one of the most common organic causes of watery diarrhea among them [ ], around one‐fourth of MC patients are under 45 years old when diagnosed [ ]. Secondly, while MC diagnosis in females outnumbers that in males by an overall ratio of 3:1 [ ], from the age of 80, male incidence surpasses that of women in the LC subtype [ ]. Thirdly, while obesity provokes chronic inflammation, dysbiosis, and immune dysregulation [ ], which are all factors in MC's etiopathology [ , ], weight gain itself has been reported to have an inverse relationship with the risk of MC [ ], and a BMI below 30 is potentially predictive of MC [ ]. Fourthly, while alcohol consumption has been reported to increase the risk of MC development [ , , ], other findings showed no association [ , ]. Lastly, a strong relationship was found between smoking and MC [ ], graded moderate evidence level in MC's 2021 European guideline [ ]; by contrast, recent studies described no association when comparing MC patients to colonoscopy‐referred diarrhea controls [ , ].
Similarly, in the context of MC development, oral contraceptives (OCs) and hormonal replacement therapy (HRT) present conflicting results [ , , ], yet no comprehensive analysis has been conducted. While links between MC and some other medications have been investigated in several meta‐analyses [ , , , ], including MC's 2021 European guideline with a low evidence level [ ], these meta‐analyses all carry certain limitations. Some were conducted without any previously registered protocol, which might affect investigation transparency; some used search keywords of medications, potentially resulting in missed studies, nonsignificant results, or overlapping populations, which might have led to overestimated results [ , ]. Furthermore, outdated tools [ , , ]—or no tools at all [ ]—were used for risk of bias assessment. Finally, despite MC's histopathological diagnosis [ ], some articles lacked separate analyses based on more precisely defined control types [ , ].
To summarize all the points mentioned above, we consider it of high importance to investigate MC's risk factors to diagnose it as early as possible, distinguishing between histopathologically examined controls and population‐based random controls due to MC's primary histological diagnosis. Thus, our primary aim was to assess the risk factors for MC, differentiating MC subtypes where possible in comparison with distinct controls.

2.

## Materials and Methods
Our study protocol was registered beforehand with PROSPERO (CRD42022286624). We followed the PRISMA 2020 statement (Table  ) and the Cochrane Handbook.
2.1.

## Eligibility Criteria
Our PEO question framework was formulated by investigating the adult population (P) (older than 15 years) with unchangeable and changeable risk factors (E) and the histopathological diagnosis of MC [ ] (O). All cohorts, case–control studies, and cross‐sectional studies were eligible if they reported on patients with MC or MC subtypes and the risk factors for MC or MC subtypes, compared to histologically verified and non‐verified random controls.

2.2.

## Information Sources
A systematic search was conducted primarily on the 18th of December 2021 in three databases: MEDLINE (via PubMed), Embase, and Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials (CENTRAL); the first search was supplemented on January 6, 2025, conducted in the same databases with the same search terms. Besides, the systematic search was expanded by the backward and forward reference search of the eligible articles on January 28, 2023, and then on January 9, 2025.

2.3.

## Search Strategy
In the search key, only MC and its three subtypes were included (Supporting Information ).

2.4.

## Selection Process
The search results were integrated into a reference manager (EndNote X9, Clarivate Analytics, Philadelphia, PA, USA). All selection steps were performed by two independent review authors (AR and MO). After automatic and manual duplicate removal, the records were selected first by title and abstract and then by full text. If potentially eligible abstracts lacked full text, complete texts were requested from the authors. Cohen's kappa coefficient (κ) was calculated at every selection step to check the inter‐rater reliability. Any potential disagreement was resolved by a third independent investigator (BT).

2.5.

## Data Collection Process
Data collection from the eligible articles was conducted by two authors (A.R. and G.W.) independently using a predesigned Excel spreadsheet (Office 365, Microsoft, Redmond, WA, USA). Any disagreements were resolved with the help of a third author (B.T.). The data were populated into 2 × 2 contingency tables containing the number of MC cases with risk factors, the number of total MC participants, the number of control patients with risk factors, and the total number of control cases. Further information can be seen in Supporting Information .

2.6.

## Data Items
We extracted the following data: first author, year of publication, digital object identifier (DOI), study design, study period, country of origin, study centers, MC (including its subtypes—LC, CC, and iMC) cases—sample size and the percentage of participating females; control patient characteristics—sample size, control type (with or without a histopathological examination), origin, and percentage of women.

2.7.

## Study Risk of Bias Assessment and Sensitivity Analysis
To evaluate the risk of bias (RoB), we used the Quality in Prognosis Studies (QUIPS) tool. RoB assessment for quantitative syntheses was performed per outcome; however, in the case of qualitative syntheses, RoB was assessed per study. The RoB evaluation was carried out independently by two reviewers (A.R. and M.O.). If differences arose, a third review author (B.T.) would decide. A detailed description of the RoB assessment can be seen in the Supporting Information .

2.8.

## Synthesis Methods
2.8.1.

## Extraction for Odds Ratio (OR)
Odds ratios (ORs) were used as an effect size measure with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). To calculate study ORs and pooled ORs, the number of all patients and those with the event of interest in each group separately (referred to as “raw data”) was extracted or calculated from the studies where it was available. If only OR was provided without “raw data”, the OR and its 95% CI were used (assuming a Wald‐type interval on a logarithmic scale if not specified).

2.8.2.

## Extraction for Mean Difference (MD)
The differences between mean values (MD) were used as an effect size measure with 95% CIs in the case of continuous outcomes. To calculate study MDs and pooled MDs, the sample size, the mean, and the corresponding standard deviation (SD) were extracted from each study (in each group separately) if available.

2.8.3.

## Reporting the Results
For OR, the results present the odds of an event of interest in the MC group compared to the odds of an event of interest in the absence of MC as the control group. For MD, the mean values of the control group were subtracted from the mean values of the experimental group.
On forest plots: For MD at an individual level, the CI was reported based on the t‐distribution. In the case of 0 cell counts, the individual study OR with a 95% CI was calculated by adding 0.5 as a continuity correction (it was used only for visualization on the forest plot).

2.8.4.

## Random‐Effects Pooling Methods
Pooled OR based on raw data was calculated using the Mantel–Haenszel method. The exact Mantel–Haenszel method (without continuity correction) was used to handle zero cell counts. The inverse variance weighting method was used to calculate the pooled OR (based on directly given data OR without raw data) and MD.
To estimate the heterogeneity variance measure (τ
2), the Paule–Mandel method (recommended by Veroniki et al.) was used with the Q profile method for CIs for “raw” OR. If only OR was provided, and for MD, the restricted maximum‐likelihood estimator was used with the Q profile method for CIs.

2.8.5.

## Analysis of Subgroups
In the case of subgroup analysis, a fixed‐effects “plural” model (aka mixed‐effects model) was used. Different τ
2 values were assumed in the subgroups. A “Cochrane Q” (an omnibus test) was used between subgroups to assess the difference between the subgroups [ ]. The null hypothesis was rejected with a 5% significance level.
All statistical analyses were made with R (v4.1.2) using base R functions, the meta (v6.2‐1) package for basic meta‐analysis calculations and plots, and the dmetar (v0.0.9000) package for additional influential analysis calculations and plots.

2.9.

## Protocol Deviation
We included cross‐sectional studies, two short reports, and one research correspondence to understand the investigated risk factors comprehensively. Two studies investigated with a lower age limit of 15 years—these were considered eligible for the analyses. No separate data were available on the iMC.

## Results
3.1.

## Search and Selection
In total, 6493 records were identified by the systematic search, supplemented by 2922 hits, which resulted from the eligible studies' backward and forward citation chases. Altogether, 73 articles were selected in our review, and 45 [ , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , ] were considered suitable for meta‐analysis (Figure  ).
 
3.2.

## Basic Characteristics of Included Studies
Included were 23 cohorts, 35 case–control studies, 11 cross‐sectional studies, two short reports, one correspondence, and one article of unspecified study design. Four records investigated participants mainly from Africa, 29 from America, three from Asia, and 36 from Europe, while the location of one study was not specified. Data from nearly 63.000 MC patients were analyzed, with more than two‐thirds female representation (see Table  ). Study periods, centers, and control origins are summarized in Table  .

## Quantitative Analysis

## Unchangeable Risk Factors
3.3.1.1.

## Age and Sex
Thirteen [ , , , , , , , , , , , , ] and 15 studies [ , , , , , , , , , , , , , , ] reported data about MC patients' age and female sex distribution compared to histological controls. MC patients were older than their histological controls, by a mean of 5.93 years (CI: 2.08–9.77; I

## Changeable Lifestyle Risk Factors
3.3.2.1.

## Alcohol Consumption
Alcohol use was reported in five studies [ , , , , ]. Our findings show that alcohol consumption results in 1.64 increased odds (CI: 1.26–2.14; I
2 = 10%, CI: 0–81) for having MC compared to random controls (Figures  and ).
 
3.3.2.2.

## Smoking
Although single studies [ , ] showed contradictory results in the comparison of never smoker MC patients with histological controls, when compared to random controls, results showed 0.52 decreased odds (CI: 0.39–0.70; I
2 = 26%, CI: 0–69) for having MC in the case of smoking abstinence. Three studies [ , , ] compared never smoking CC patients to histological controls, resulting in a 0.89 ORs (CI: 0.63–1.24; I
2 = 0%, CI: 0–90) for having CC. Separate data for never smoker CC and LC patients compared to random controls were available in six [ , , , , , ] and four studies [ , , , ]; both results showed that never smoking is inversely associated with CC and LC (OR = 0.55, CI: 0.41–0.74; I
2 = 70%, CI: 29–87; OR = 0.48, CI: 0.29–0.81; I
2 = 40%, CI: 0–80) (Figures  , , and – ).
 Four [ , , , ] and 10 articles [ , , , , , , , , , ] provided data about former smoker MC patients compared to histological and random controls. Analyses showed a 1.26 (CI: 0.67–2.39; I
2 = 58%, CI: 0–86) and a 1.11 (CI: 0.93–1.31; I
2 = 28%, CI: 0–65) OR increase in the odds of having MC. Five studies [ , , , , ] reported separate numbers for past smoking CC and LC cases, compared to random controls. Results showed 1.57 (CI: 0.77–3.21; I
2 = 76%, CI: 41–90) and 1.37 (CI: 0.80–2.35; I
2 = 61%, CI: 0–85) higher odds for having CC and LC in the case of former smoking (Figures  , , and – ).
 Current smokers among MC patients were reported in 10 articles [ , , , , , , , , , ] and 14 articles [ , , , , , , , , , , , , , ] compared to histological and random controls. In the case of histological controls, we did not find an association between smoking and the risk of having MC, the odds being 1.35 (CI: 0.88–2.06; I
2 = 46%, CI: 0–74). In contrast, when random controls were used, results showed more than doubled odds (OR = 2.40, CI: 1.64–3.50; I
2 = 96%, CI: 94–97) for having MC. Eight articles [ , , , , , , , ] and seven articles [ , , , , , , ] investigated current smoking among CC and LC patients compared to random controls. Results showed tripled odds (OR = 2.96, CI: 1.70–5.18; I

NSAIDs were investigated in 14 [ , , , , , , , , , , , , , ] and 13 studies [ , , , , , , , , , , , , ] among MC patients, compared to histological and random controls. We found significantly two‐and‐a‐half‐fold higher odds in both cases (OR = 2.57, CI: 1.45–4.53; I

## Selective Serotonin Reuptake Inhibitors (SSRIs)
SSRIs were examined as a risk factor for MC in eight [ , , , , , , , ] and eight articles [ , , , , , , , ] in comparison to histological and random controls. Both cases showed increased odds (OR = 1.56, CI: 0.62–3.89; I

3.3.3.5.

## Statins
Statins were used in six [ , , , , , ] and eight articles [ , , , , , , , ] by MC patients compared to histological and random controls. We found doubled odds for having MC compared to histological controls (OR = 2.15, CI: 1.14–4.05; I

## Aspirin
Six studies [ , , , , , ] assessed aspirin use in MC patients compared to histological controls, and we calculated 2.29 ORs (CI: 0.54–9.65; I
2 = 82%, CI: 62–92) for having MC without statistical significance. Three records [ , , ] investigated the risk for MC in the case of aspirin use compared to random controls, in which the odds of having MC were shown to be quadrupled (OR = 4.24, CI: 2.64–6.80; I

## Risk of Bias Assessment
Supporting Information ,d parts present the risk of bias assessment tables.
The results of the risk of bias assessment of the meta‐analysis are summarized in Tables  and . Study participation was deemed a high risk of bias in 21/45 cases in the quantitative synthesis, primarily due to the low number of study participants. Study attrition received a moderate risk of bias in 17 studies where participant dropout was described, but a flow diagram was not provided. Prognostic factor measurement sections were downgraded due to poor definitions of risk factors. The studies' outcome measurements were mainly assessed as low risk of bias, except where MC's histological reconfirmation data were absent. Studies' confounding factors were evaluated, with one‐third of studies having low risk, one‐third having moderate or no adjustment of the confounding factors, and one‐third having a high risk of bias for lacking the factors. Statistical analysis and reporting domains were deemed to have a low risk of bias in all cases. Systematic review studies' risk of bias assessment can be seen in Table  .

## Discussion
MC incidence rates show an augmenting tendency in Western Europe [ ]. Its risk factors are controversial and lacking consensus, and it may take years to diagnose due to its primarily histological appearance. Therefore, we investigated these factors, distinguishing between histologically verified and non‐verified random controls due to the assumption that the former cohort represents more reliable results. Our results showed that increased age, female sex, NSAID, and statin use are associated with the development of MC when compared to histological controls, while current smoking, alcohol use, previous appendectomy, PPI, SSRI, aspirin, and benzodiazepine use were found to increase the odds of MC only when compared to random controls.
MC's multifactorial etiopathogenesis is three‐pillared: In genetically predisposed individuals, luminal factors enter the bowel wall through the impaired mucosal barrier, initiating immune dysregulation [ ]. Our data confirm that MC patients are older by a mean of 5.93 years compared to their histological controls. Moreover, we showed that females have higher odds of having MC than histological controls. Furthermore, we found that postmenopausal status tends to be a risk factor for MC. While postmenopausal HRT was suspected as a factor for increasing MC's incidence [ ], our results did not confirm the association of MC's diagnosis with HRT.
Alcohol consumption was found to increase the odds of having MC, although all available data come from the comparison of MC patients with random controls. MC's increased incidence might be due to heavy drinking, highlighting alcohol's effect on the gut microbiome [ ], considering that altered microbial populations were associated with MC development [ ]. Moreover, smoking can also contribute to this phenomenon. Both current and former smoking slightly increased the odds for MC compared to histological controls without statistical significance. In contrast, the odds doubled compared to random controls. We did not find any data on alcohol consumption reduction or smoking cessation per se; therefore, it is hard to tell if these modifiable factors have a reversible effect. Current literature is also scarce on whether these factors worsen flares of the disease.
Our findings revealed that among medical risk factors, NSAIDs stand out, with two‐and‐a‐half‐fold odds for MC compared to histological controls. Although considerable heterogeneity was present, only one publication [ ] showed an OR lower than one. One systematic review summarized the effect of NSAIDs on the gut microbiome [ ], which might synergize with MC development mentioned earlier. NSAIDs interfere with prostaglandin synthesis, leading to damaged gut barrier and increased toxin influx, thus playing a role in gut inflammation and MC development. Ad hoc NSAID self‐administration should be more restricted, especially in elderly females who might be more susceptible to this disease.
Statins were the second substantial medical risk factor, with doubled odds for MC in our meta‐analysis; however, individual studies' multivariate analysis did not confirm their relationship [ , ]. Therefore, other confounding factors or medications may play a role in the statin‐attributable risk. Previous meta‐analyses found no significant association either when colonoscopy or diarrhea controls were used [ , ]. Based on the above, we would not suggest statin treatment withdrawal from patients who need them.
In contrast with previous findings [ , , , ], in our results, PPIs tend to increase the odds of MC; however, statistical significance was only reached when compared to random controls. A study from 2022, which was not included in earlier meta‐analyses, showed no effect of PPIs on MC development [ ]. A previous meta‐analysis claimed PPIs' role in the gut microbiota change [ ]. Zylberberg et al. 2021 [ ] highlighted that controls with the gut‐histological examination are potentially PPI users with gastrointestinal symptoms. In contrast, random, population‐based (healthy) controls may be less likely to be PPI users, thus more likely to be asymptomatic. This might explain the difference in results from comparing MC patients to the distinct types of controls. Our findings of different PPIs showed no association with MC compared to histological controls.
In our results, SSRIs showed a slight trend toward increased odds for MC compared to histological controls; however, mathematical significance was lacking. Although a previous study found a strong two‐and‐a‐half times increased odds for MC [ ], newer articles [ , , ] have similar findings to ours. MC patients report psychological welfare impairment [ ], anxiety, and depression [ ]; so, in MC patients, both gastrointestinal symptoms and social limitations might explain the underlying use of SSRIs.

5.

## Strengths and Limitations
The main strength of our study lies in the preregistered protocol and a broad search key. Accordingly, we included many studies with thousands of patients' data, using a rigorous methodology that followed PRISMA guidelines and the Cochrane Handbook. Unchangeable and changeable lifestyle and medication risk factors were investigated to assess the cumulative effects on patients' lives. MC patients were compared to distinct control groups, highlighting the importance of histologically verified controls, due to MC's histopathological diagnosis.
We acknowledge the limitations of this meta‐analysis. Although many case–control studies were included, most used age‐ and sex‐matched controls, and few articles reported only on female participants [ , , , ]. Risk factor definitions varied across the studies and in some cases were completely lacking. We could not pool multivariate analyses together. The moderate and high risk of bias in prognostic and confounding factors presents an additional limitation.

6.

## Implications for Practice
The incidence of MC grows linearly. Therefore, we would like to raise awareness of MC's risk factors among gastroenterologists, general practitioners, endoscopists, and pathologists. There is a need for interdisciplinary collaboration and high‐quality colonoscopy and histology in diagnosing the disease, with the potential to improve diagnosis efficiency by reducing unnecessary appointments, endoscopy interventions, and their associated costs. All teaching materials should extend the MC and its risk factors curriculum [ , ].
It is important to recognize the potential of MC in the unknown origin of chronic watery diarrhea patients who are taking NSAIDs and/or statins, especially if that occurs in elderly women. However, based on our findings, we do not encourage the statin takeaways, but NSAID therapy should be re‐evaluated in such patients.

7.

## Implications for Research
Further prospective data collection is needed to gain more insight regarding this topic. We highlight the need for international and national registries, assessing the negative lifestyle factors, pack‐years of smoking, and medication doses, time, and frequency with long follow‐ups. MC's risk factors and microbiome interactions should be deeply mapped.

8.

## Conclusion
Our data confirm that older age, female sex, NSAID, and statin use are clinically relevant and statistically significant risk factors for MC, using histological controls; current smoking, alcohol use, previous appendectomy, PPI, SSRI, aspirin, and benzodiazepine showed an association with MC only when random controls were used.
