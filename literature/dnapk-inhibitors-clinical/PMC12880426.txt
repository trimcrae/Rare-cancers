## Background
Obesity in the older adults is a health concern that increases the risk of several life-threatening diseases. Previous research has been revealed that alterations in the gut microbiota composition is related to obesity. So, understanding the gut microbiota changes in older adults' obesity may help to provide promising strategies for their health management.

## Objectives
Here we conducted a systematic review that investigate the alteration of gut microbiota composition in association with obesity and its indices in the older adults.

## Design
Systematic review.

## Setting
A comprehensive systematic search was performed through PubMed, Web of Science, Scopus and Embase databases for all relative studies up to 2023 with the main search concepts as Microbiota, Obesity and Elderly. The data about gut microbiota in association with obesity indices had been extracted.

## Participants
Older adults (≥60 years).

## Intervention
None.

## Measurements
None.

## Results
Within 10741 recordes, 11 studies met the inclusion criteria and were included in this systematic review. Most of them indicated the gut microbiota alterations in obese compared with non-obese older adults. However, the gut microbiome composition in obese older adults is affected by other underlying diseases like diabetes and metabolic syndrome. The most important taxa that had abundance alteration in association with obesity in older adults were Christensenellaceae, Porphyromonadaceae and Rikenellaceae, Akkermansia, Blautia, Prevotella, Ruminococcus, Bacteroides and Faecalibacterium.

## Conclusion
The gut microbiota composition is associated with obesity in older adults. Considering the other factors affecting the composition of gut microbiota, such as age, underlying diseases and lifestyle, a more accurate conclusion about this matter requires more future studies.

## Introduction
The World Health Organization (WHO) defines obesity and overweight as a health-threatening, abnormal or excessive accumulation of fat ( ). The prevalence of obesity in epidemic dimensions has increased by about 100% over the last five decades, and currently an estimated one-third of the world's population is classified as overweight or obese ( , ). Previous studies evaluating obesity trends have shown an increase in its prevalence across all ages, regardless of geographic location, ethnic background, or socioeconomic status ( ). Obesity as a major public health concern, negatively affects almost all physiological functions of the body. It increases the risk of several diseases, such as diabetes ( ), cardiovascular disease ( , ), various types of cancer ( ), a range of musculoskeletal disorders ( ) and poor mental health ( ). These factors have adverse effects on quality of life, work productivity, and health care costs, and can cause premature disability and death ( , ).
Due to the accelerating global aging rate, prioritizing the health of older adults is of utmost importance. One of the serious problems of the older adults is obesity and its consequences. According to studies, in the United States, the prevalence of obesity and overweight among individuals aged over 60 is 36.6% and 76.5% in men, 42.3% and 73.5% in women respectively ( ).
Obesity in the older adults is associated with an increased risk of metabolic disorders, cardiovascular diseases, sarcopenia, physical disability, morbidity, and mortality ( , , ).
Current understanding suggests that obesity is linked to changes in gut microbiota composition, which differs between obese and non-obese individuals ( , , , ). An accurate comprehension of this association can be crucial in the prevention and management of obesity. Studies have shown that gut microbiota can influence various aspects, including energy intake from food, intestinal permeability, lipid metabolism, expression of genes regulating lipogenesis, interaction with bile acids, breakdown of polysaccharides, insulin resistance, immune response, endotoxemia, endocrine functions and hormonal changes ( , ).
Although the gut microbiota is relatively stable throughout adult life, during aging, physiological changes in the gastrointestinal tract, changes in dietary patterns, and declines in cognitive function and immunity are associated with alterations in the gut microbiota. Studies that have examined the composition of the microbiota of older adults show that their gut microbiota is characterized by reduction of bacterial diversity, dominant species changes, decrease in beneficial microorganisms, an increase in facultative anaerobic bacteria, and the lower availability of total short-chain fatty acids ( ).
In line with honoring older adults, the importance of obesity in these individuals, and the role of microbiota in health maintenance, we conducted a systematic review that assessed the changes in gut microbiota composition in association with obesity and its indices in older adults.

## Methods

## Study design and searches
This systematic review adhered to the PRISMA guidelines for conducting and reporting the study findings ( ), and was registered with PROSPERO International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (Registration ID: CRD42022380771). Concisely, a comprehensive systematic search was performed through PubMed, Web of Science, Scopus and Embase databases for publications retrieval, up to May 2023. The main search concepts used for publications retrieval were as follows; Microbiota, Obesity and Elderly. Details of our search strategy and terms are presented in Supplementary Table 1. The results of all databases' searches were imported and combined in EndNote Reference Manager. The search results were inspected and duplicate ones were removed by the software function and manually operated by two independent investigators. Moreover, reference lists of the included studies were screened to obtain further eligible publications.

## Eligibility criteria and study selection
The retrieved publications were screened based on the titles and abstracts and two separate investigators performed an initial exclusion of irrelevant studies. The inclusion criteria consisted of: A) Observational studies (case-control, cross-sectional, and cohort studies) that examined the gut microbiota composition of obese older adults. B) Studies with full-text articles available in English. The exclusion criteria consisted of: A) Animal and intervention studies, letters, comments, review articles, ecological studies, and studies with insufficient data. The process of study selection is presented in the PRISMA flow diagram.

## Data extraction
The full-text article of potentially relevant studies that met the inclusion criteria have been reviewed and the data have been extracted and summarized in a pre-designed data extraction table by two independent investigators. The following information has been extracted from the articles:•Author
•Date
•Design
•Country
•Sample size
•Participants'characteristics
•Participants' underlying disease
•Microbiota assessment method
•Outcome.

Disagreements were settled by consulting with the principal investigator.

## Quality assessment
The Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS) was used for quality assessment of included studies' selection, comparability, and outcome, and each study received a score ranging from 1 to 9. Blinded quality assessment of each study was performed by two investigators. Any disagreements in the scoring process were resolved through discussion or consensus between the two investigators.

## Results

## Searches and study selection
Overall, we identified a total of 10,741 articles through the searches. After removing duplicates and ineligible articles, the titles and abstracts of 5,624 articles were screened. Of those, 5,541 articles were excluded due to being review article, animal study and irrelevant to the main topic. Out of the remaining 83 full-text articles, studies with no relevant or insufficient outcomes, as well as those that did not represent the target population, were excluded and, finally, 11 studies were considered for this systematic review ( , , , , , , , , , , ). ## Data extraction
The included studies were published between 2010 and 2021. The sample sizes of the studies ranged from 20 to 530 participants. The data were extracted from cross-sectional data of included studies. Seven studies were conducted in Europe, two in North America, one in Asia and one in Australia. The ages of the included subjects varied between 55 and 102 years old. Different techniques were used to assess the gut microbiota in these studies, including Genomic DNA sequencing in one study, 16S rRNA amplicon sequencing in eight studies, Species-specific qualitative polymerase chain reaction (PCR) in one study and, Culture and count colony forming unit in one study. The obesity indices considered in these articles included weight, body mass index (BMI), waist circumference (WC) and waist-to-hip ratio (WHR). Add the result of gut microbiota assessment in these 11 studies, Christensenellaceae, Porphyromonadaceae, Rikenellaceae, Ruminococcaceae, Lachnospiraceae, Oxalobacteraceae, Aerococcaceae, Erysipelotrichaceae, Bacteroidaceae, Veillonellaceae families and, order Clostridiales Incertae Sedis XIII and, Prevotella, Akkermansia, Bacteroides, Blautia, Anaerostipes, Roseburia, Dialister, Sutterella, Coprococcus-2, Acidaminococcus, Clostridium, Faecalibacterium, Lachnobacterium, Staphylococcus, Oscillibacter genera had alterations in association with obesity indices ( , , , , , , , ).
In two studies, Christensenellaceae, Porphyromonadaceae and Rikenellaceae families abundance had a negative association with obesity ( , ). The Prevotella genus abundance was associated with lower weight in two studies ( , ).
The other taxa that were positively associated with obesity indices are Blautia, Clostridiales Incertae Sedis XIII ( ) Roseburia, Dialister and Sutterella, Coprococcus-2, Acidaminococcus ( ) Aerococcaceae, ( ) Erysipelotrichaceae ( ) and staphylococcus ( ). Other negatively associated taxa with obesity indices were Lachnospiraceae, Akkermansia ( ), Anaerostipes, Clostridium, unnamed Clostridiales ( ), Coprococcus-1 and Ruminococcus-1 ( ). There was controversy about the abundance of Prevotella and Bacteroides, Faecalibacterium and Ruminococcus in studies ( , , , ). Lactobacillus and Clostridium species were not associated with obesity and its related indices in two studies ( , ).

## Studies included Participants with age-related metabolic disorders
Two studies included participants with metabolic syndrome ( , ) and both studies reported a negative association between Prevotella abundance and BMI. One study had participants with impaired glucose metabolism and metabolic syndrome. In this study, Erysipelotrichaceae genera were detected in obese subjects with metabolic syndrome and/or with impaired glucose metabolism, Clostridium and unnamed Clostridiales, Faecalibacterium and Lachnobacterium were seen in non-obese subjects with normal glucose tolerance and/ or were without metabolic syndrome. Also, Bacteroidaceae were less abundant in obese female study participants along with Veillonellaceae in obese mostly male study participants ( ). Also, in one study that its participants had type 2 diabetes, impaired glucose tolerance and normal glucose tolerance, no association with gut microbiota and obesity has been reported ( ).

## Discussion
This systematic review aimed to investigate gut microbiota alteration in relation to obesity and its related indices in older adults. Although not too many observational studies have been done in this regard and there is not sufficient data for a comprehensive conclusion, in the included studies, there is a great diversity in gut microbiota and their changes in obese older adults.
Particularly, microbiota studies shows that there is a substantial association between obesity and gut microbiota changes, regardless of age ( , , , ). On the other hand, variations in the gut microbiota with age are well evident ( ). So, the diversity, composition, and functional features of the gut microbiota in obese older adults can be specific to these individuals. Given that, the gut microbiota is affected by several hosts and environmental factors such as, Genetic, metabolome, diet, physical activity and diseases ( ), and considering the complexity of the health status and lifestyle of the older adults, microbiota studies on these individuals are challenging and valuable.
In the results of our investigation, the Christensenellaceae, Porphyromonadaceae and Rikenellaceae families had a negative association with obesity. In Castaner et al. and Ejtahed et al. systematic review, the Christensenellaceae family and its metabolites have been positively associated with lean BMI ( , ). This association may be due to secondary bile acid and some amino acids (Branched-chain amino acids metabolism) ( ). Also, the Christensenellaceae family (mostly known with Christensenella minuta species) are Short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) producing bacteria, with promising potential effects on weight loss ( , ). Notably, Healthy aging has been associated with Christensenellaceae ( , ). Alistipes (Rikenellaceae family) is reported to be more abundant in lean individuals in the XU et al. systematic review ( ). As well as Christensenellaceae, the underlying mechanism of the effects of both Porphyromonadaceae and Rikenellaceae may be related to SCFAs production and their anti-inflammatory properties ( , ). Generally, evidences showes that, higher amounts of Christensenellaceae, Porphyromonadaceae and Rikenellaceae may protect against cardiovascular and metabolic diseases ( ). Also, in a study, Alistipes was negatively correlated with age in Chinese adults ( ). Therefore, these taxa may be potential markers of healthy aging and improve life expectancy ( , ).
Recently Christensenella minuta, Akkermansia muciniphila (A. muciniphila) and Blautia, are known as obesity-related heritable microbiota ( ). Zhong et al. in their study, indicated that Blautia is positively associated with BMI ( ). But in some studies, its abundance is negatively associated with visceral fat accumulation ( ) and body and adipose tissue weights ( ). Blautia is a bacteria genus belongs to the Lachnospiraceae family, and its abundance is affected by diet, physical activity, age, and geography of the host ( , ). So, the isolation of new species and more future studies can be helpful for drawing a correct conclusion about these bacteria properties.
A. muciniphila is recognized for its negative association with obesity and other metabolic disorders ( , , ). Regulating metabolism and energy balance, improving insulin sensitivity and glucose homeostasis, maintenance of a healthy gut barrier and enhancing low-grade inflammation are the modulatory effects of A. muciniphila in obesity ( , ). Interestingly, a study showed that Akkermansia abundance has increased in semi-supercentenarians ( ) and overall, Akkermansia abundance increases with aging, but decreases in some disorders associated with unhealthy aging ( , ). So, aligned with our study, lower abundance of Akkermansia in non-obese healthy older adults is rational. The A. muciniphila Peptidoglycan MucT function and SCFAs production are of the mechanisms that cause its promising features ( ). In some previous studies, Prevotella and Ruminococcus abundances are mostly positively associated with obesity and its related metabolites ( , ); but according to the XU et al. systematic review on gut microbiota in obesity and metabolic disorders, in studies from the West, Prevotella and Ruminococcus are associated with obesity, whereas in the East, they are associated with leanness ( ). Also, there are differences in the association of Bacteroides and Faecalibacterium with obesity in previous studies ( ). On the other side in Badal et al. systematic review on gut microbiota and aging, Prevotella, Bacteroides and Faecalibacterium were relatively lower in older adults and results about Ruminococcus are different in this study ( ). So, the controversy in our result can be due to these genera contrasting associations with obesity and aging, and probably is at the species level. Also, the controversy about Prevotella could be due to the participants suffering from metabolic syndrome in the two previously mentioned studies. In previous studies, Coprococcus, Dialister, Bacteroides, Clostridium and Roseburia shows controversy in association with obesity and metabolic disorders ( ). Also, Coprococcus and Sutterella are positively associated with aging in Shen et al. study ( ). So, we cannot draw a conclusion about them until now, but it may be concluded that the gut microbiota composition in obese older adults is different from other obese adults.
In the study conducted by Tavellaa et al., Christensenellaceae, Porphyromonadaceae, and Rikenellaceae were found to be associated with reduced visceral adipose tissue and a healthier metabolic profile in obese older adults ( ). Additionally, previous research has indicated a link between gut microbiota alterations and adipose tissue distribution in obesity ( ). Given that cardiovascular diseases (CVDs) are related to adipose tissue accumulation and older adults are more susceptible to such conditions, understanding the role of microbiota alterations and adipose tissue distribution becomes crucial, warranting further investigation in future studies.
Considering the important role of dietary compounds in obesity, one of the major factors affecting gut microbiota role in regulating weight and obesity, is their potential to metabolize various nutrients. For instance, the fermentation of indigestible substrates like dietary fibers by some specific beneficial gut microbiota that produce SCFAs, can lead to obesity reduction. On the other side, gut microbiota dysbiosis may promotes diet induced obesity by dysregulation of immune system, energy absorption and gut hormone, and proinflammatory mechanisms ( ). So, the association between gut microbiota, nutrition, and obesity should be considered. But the included studies didn't provide enough information about individuals' dietary habits.
Physical activity is another factor that is strongly associated with obesity and gut microbiota alterations ( , ). As aging is often accompanied by reduced physical activities and metabolic changes, this issue likely plays a crucial role in the gut microbiota composition of obese older adults ( ). In a study by Langsetmo et al., the older adults with more physical activity had higher relative abundance of Cetobacterium and lower relative abundance of taxa from the genera Coprobacillus, Adlercreutzia, Erysipelotrichaceae CC-115 after multivariable adjustment including age, BMI ( ). However, this association has not been thoroughly investigated in other studies.
Of our study strengths is that we have a comprehensive search through multiple databases and hand searches. Here, we tried to include all the older adult groups in the study and review various indicators related to obesity. But we also had some limitations. The small sample size and low quality of some studies make their results somewhat unreliable. Also, due to heterogeneity in the studies, the causality between gut microbiota alteration and obesity is not clear. In the case of gut microbiota studies in older adults, we have multiple influential factors that make these studies challenging. As a result of physiological, metabolic and lifestyle changes, aging causes changes in the microbiota composition. In addition, Obesity is a comorbid condition with some common aging-associated diseases like diabetes, metabolic syndrome, sarcopenia and osteopenia ( ) and this issue makes it more difficult to investigate gut microbiota alterations in relation to obesity in older adults. Moreover, cognitive impairment and immunosenescence can also affect the gut microbiota composition of older adults ( , ). Studying the effect of these factors on the elderly's gut microbiota composition can be suggested as a future research objective.

## Conclusion
In this study, we investigated the alteration in gut microbiota composition of older adults in association with obesity and its indices. The most important taxa that had an alteration in association with obesity in older adults were Christensenellaceae, Porphyromonadaceae and Rikenellaceae, Akkermansia, Blautia, Prevotella, Ruminococcus, Bacteroides and Faecalibacterium. Although these changes are well evident, since microbiota composition is related to both obesity and aging separately, more studies are needed to understand the microbial signature of obesity in older adults. This microbial signature has to be determined in different populations of multiple geographical regions and at species levels, and the metabolic profile should also be assessed. Expectantly, such studies can lead to the use of microbiota knowledge in the treatment of obesity in older adults and improve their quality of life.
