## Significance
The REV1 C-terminal domain (CTD) serves as a central scaffold for coordinating mutagenic translesion synthesis (TLS) by recruiting inserter and extender polymerases. Here, we show that isolated expression of the human REV1-CTD in cancer cells acts as a potent dominant-negative inhibitor of TLS by sequestering key pathway components into nonproductive complexes. This interference impairs lesion bypass, increases genomic instability, reduces mutagenesis, and sensitizes cells to multiple DNA-damaging agents. The inhibitory effect requires both known interaction interfaces of the CTD, providing direct genetic evidence that these interactions are essential for TLS function. These establish REV1-CTD expression as a valuable tool for dissecting the roles of REV1 and POLζ in DNA damage tolerance and have clinical potential.
Keywords: REV1, translesion synthesis, chemotherapy, cisplatin, cancer

## Abstract
The mutagenic translesion synthesis (TLS) pathway, which is critically dependent on REV1’s ability to recruit inserter TLS polymerases and the POLζ extender polymerase, enables cancer cells to bypass DNA lesions while introducing mutations that likely contribute to the development of chemotherapy resistance and secondary malignancies. Targeting this pathway represents a promising therapeutic strategy. Here, we demonstrate that the expression of the C-terminal domain (CTD) of human REV1, a ca. 100 amino acid scaffold essential for TLS polymerase interactions, disrupts REV1/POLζ-dependent TLS in mammalian cells. Inducible expression of REV1-CTD in multiple human and murine cancer cell lines sensitizes cells to DNA-damaging agents such as cisplatin, benzo[a]pyrene diol epoxide, and methyl methanesulfonate, without intrinsic cytotoxicity. REV1-CTD expression increases genomic instability, decreases mutagenesis, and enhances G2 arrest following genotoxic stress. Mutational disruption of the CTD’s interaction interfaces abrogates these effects, confirming a dominant-negative mechanism via sequestration of TLS components. In a xenograft mouse model, REV1-CTD expression markedly enhances cisplatin efficacy, significantly reducing tumor burden. These findings establish the REV1-CTD as an effective dominant-negative inhibitor of TLS and support its development as a therapeutic agent delivered to cancer cells to enhance the efficacy of genotoxic chemotherapy.

Translesion synthesis (TLS) was one of the last recognized fundamental molecular mechanisms by which cells cope with DNA damage ( , ) and has subsequently been found to play multiple highly significant roles in biology, including cancer development and therapeutic responses. TLS utilizes specialized DNA polymerases to carry out DNA synthesis over lesions that block the progression of normal replicative DNA polymerases. All TLS DNA polymerases are of lower fidelity than highly accurate replicative polymerases, because they lack proofreading exonucleases and have a decreased capacity to distinguish between incoming nucleotides ( – ).
Although certain TLS polymerases can nevertheless be relatively accurate when inserting nucleotides across from their cognate lesions, both eukaryotes and prokaryotes possess an extremely important mutagenic TLS pathway that not only promotes survival by allowing cells to complete a cycle of DNA replication after damage by copying over a wide variety of lesions but also introduces mutations during the process ( , , , ). In eukaryotes, including humans, mutagenic TLS depends on REV1 and POLζ (REV3/REV7/POLD2/POLD3). The nonmutable phenotype of mutants lacking REV1, REV3, and REV7 indicates that most of the mutations that result from DNA damage are actively introduced into DNA during the process of mutagenic TLS ( ). Although it is a member of the Y family of TLS DNA polymerases, REV1 has only a very limited ability to insert a nucleotide opposite a lesion but it plays a master role in mutagenic TLS by acting as scaffolding protein that recruits other TLS polymerases via its ca. 100 amino acid C-terminal domain (CTD) ( , – ). The REV1 CTD is a four-helix bundle that has two critical interfaces ( – ). One interface recruits “inserter” DNA polymerases (POLη, POLκ, POLι), which introduce a nucleotide opposite the lesion, while the other interface recruits the “extender” DNA polymerase POLζ, which extends DNA synthesis from the noncanonical primer terminus ( ). REV3 is the catalytic subunit of POLζ, while REV7 is a critical noncatalytic protein. In addition to REV3 and REV7, POLζ contains two accessory proteins also found in the replicative DNA polymerase POLδ, POLD2, and POLD3 ( , ).
 REV1/POLζ-dependent mutagenic TLS is of particular importance in cancer because it affects both tumorigenesis and chemotherapeutic response ( , , – ). This DNA damage tolerance pathway is a special vulnerability of many cancer cells because they are much more dependent on REV1/POLζ-dependent mutagenic TLS than normal cells. Indeed, TLS activity is essential in a number of cancer cell lines ( , ). This increased dependence on mutagenic TLS in numerous cancers arises in part from the activation of oncogenes and loss of tumor suppressors during tumor development ( ). Activated oncogenes are known to cause replicative stress through a variety of mechanisms, which then triggers a checkpoint replicative stress response that blocks DNA replication ( ). Increased utilization of mutagenic TLS can allow cancer cells to continue replicating by overcoming this block. In addition, since some tumor suppressor genes such as BRCA1 and BRCA2 encode components of major accurate DNA repair systems, their loss results leads to an increased dependence upon DNA damage tolerance alternatives such as mutagenic TLS that play a lesser role in normal cells ( , ).
The increased reliance of cancer cells on REV1/POLζ-dependent mutagenic TLS has important consequences for cancer cell treatment. Specifically, the increased mutation frequency caused by mutagenic TLS action favors the development of chemotherapy-induced genetic alterations in tumor cells ( ). Thus, tumor cells that survive chemotherapy are heavily mutagenized, leading to the development of acquired resistance to subsequent treatment—including mutations that underlie resistance to second round therapies to which the tumors have no previous exposure. This issue, in part, underlies the unfortunate reality that 50% of late-stage cancer patients relapse following genotoxic chemotherapy with fatal treatment-refractory disease ( ). The critical need to improve the efficacy of genotoxic chemotherapy has led to numerous suggestions that mutagenic TLS is a very attractive target for clinical intervention ( , , – ). As pointed out by Nayak et al. ( ), targeting mutagenic TLS has the potential to uniquely eradicate tumors without harming noncancer tissues because cancer cells rely on it more than normal cells.
To date, most efforts to develop inhibitors of REV1/POLζ-dependent mutagenic TLS potentially suitable for clinical applications, including our own, have focused on the development of a small molecule inhibitors ( , – ). Since all DNA polymerases share similar active sites, which makes the development of highly selective inhibitors of catalysis challenging for TLS DNA polymerases ( ), we instead focused our efforts on developing drugs that disrupt key protein–protein interactions mediated by the REV1 CTD. We have demonstrated that small molecules that bind either to the REV1 CTD interface for recruiting inserter polymerases or the interface for recruiting the extender DNA polymerase POLζ can effectively block mutagenic TLS ( , , ). Collectively, our in vivo studies have demonstrated that a small molecule or small RNA that inhibits REV1/POLζ-dependent mutagenic TLS can be used as an adjuvant to strikingly increase the efficacy of genotoxic chemotherapy in multiple types of cancers ( , – ). Additionally, recent studies have shown that in BRCA-deficient cancers, which are deficient in both homology-directed repair and fork protection, inhibition of REV1/POLζ-dependent mutagenic TLS by a small molecule inhibitor can be partially effective as a monotherapy and more effective in combination with either a PARP inhibitor (PARPi) or a DNA-damaging chemotherapeutic ( , , ).
Despite the encouraging progress that has being made, the use of small molecule inhibitors has a variety of potential liabilities including the possibility of resistance arising by activation of export pumps, unexpected off-target effects that might not be recognized until a clinical trial, and in particular the difficulty of targeting them selectively to cancer cells to improve their efficacy. A conceptually independent strategy for making a class of clinically useful drug to block the action of the REV1/POLζ-dependent mutagenic TLS pathway that is different from using a small molecule inhibitor or small RNA would be to introduce a fragment of protein capable of interacting with other pathway proteins and trapping them in nonproductive complexes ( ). Our accumulated knowledge about the structure and physiological roles of mammalian REV1-CTD with its two recruitment interfaces encouraged us to consider using it to test this idea, as did our much earlier observation ( ), that elevated levels of a portion of the yeast REV1 C-terminal region conferred a strong dominant-negative effect on Saccharomyces cerevisiae viability and mutagenesis induced by DNA damage.
We here describe proof-of-principle that overexpression of the REV1-CTD is sufficient to disrupt REV1/POLζ-dependent mutagenic TLS in mammalian cells. By competing with native REV1 to bind key TLS partner proteins, REV1-CTD forms nonproductive complexes that impair lesion bypass and sensitize cancer cells to DNA-damaging agents. These findings underscore the potential of REV1-CTD as a dominant-negative inhibitor of TLS and support further exploration of TLS disruption as a therapeutic strategy to enhance chemotherapy efficacy. Use of this strategy for blocking REV1, REV3, and REV7 action could also offer insights into some of the less well-understood roles of these proteins in other processes.

## Results

## Establishment and Characterization of HT1080 Cell Lines with Inducible REV1-CTD Expression.
To test the hypothesis that expression of an isolated ca. 100 amino acid REV1-CTD domain could inhibit mutagenic TLS in mammalian cells, we generated HT1080 cell lines with inducible expression of REV1-CTD by using a lentivirus to insert the DNA encoding the CTD of REV1 under the control of a tetracycline-inducible promoter into the genome. This system allowed for the controlled induction of REV1-CTD expression upon doxycycline administration (SI Appendix, Fig. S1A). The induction of REV1-CTD expression was quantitatively validated by qPCR. Our results confirmed that the mRNA levels of REV1-CTD were tightly correlated with the doxycycline dose, thereby establishing the suitability of Tet-on system for this purpose (SI Appendix, Fig. S1B). Consistent with qPCR results, Western blot analysis demonstrated detectable REV1-CTD protein expression upon doxycycline treatment (SI Appendix, Fig. S1C). Immunofluorescence staining was performed to determine the subcellular localization of the induced REV1-CTD. The fluorescence signal was distributed throughout the cell, confirming the expression and widespread localization of the protein ( ). Since the interacting partners of REV1 are localized to the nucleus, we also generated cell lines expressing the REV1-CTD with the SV40 nuclear localization signal (NLS) fused to the N-terminus (SI Appendix, Fig. S1A). As expected, cells expressing this NLS-tagged version of REV1-CTD showed a predominant nuclear localization. ( ). qPCR and Western blot analyses confirmed the expression of NLS-REV1-CTD upon doxycycline induction, with expression levels comparable to those of the untagged REV1-CTD (SI Appendix, Fig. S1 B and C). For both constructs, mRNA levels plateaued at 2.5 μg/mL doxycycline, and thus this concentration was used in all subsequent experiments. A cell viability assay was conducted 24 h after doxycycline induction to assess the immediate cytotoxic effects of REV1-CTD expression. While no significant impact on viability was observed at this time point (SI Appendix, Fig. S1D), we monitored the cells over several days and did not detect changes in population doubling time or morphology. This suggests that the induction of REV1-CTD expression, even at the highest levels, does not adversely affect cell viability.

## Enhanced Sensitivity to DNA-Damaging Agents in Cells Expressing the REV1-CTD.
Our colony formation assay revealed a marked increase in sensitivity to cisplatin killing in HT1080 cells expressing the REV1-CTD upon doxycycline induction ( ) similar to what we have previously observed when we have added small molecule inhibitors of mutagenic TLS ( , ). Quantification of colonies post–cisplatin treatment revealed a substantial reduction in colony numbers in the expressing group that demonstrated the enhanced susceptibility of these cells to the DNA damage agent ( ). The increased sensitivity to cisplatin correlated with the dose of doxycycline used to induce REV1-CTD expression and at the highest concentration of doxycycline there was a greater than 50% decrease in survival fraction compared to control cells. The sensitization effect plateaued at a doxycycline concentration of 2.5 µg/mL ( ). As also expected, a similar synergistic effect on cell killing of cisplatin and the REV1-CTD was also observed if we instead introduced an mRNA encoding an NLS-tagged REV1-CTD (SI Appendix, Fig. S1E). However, we continued with the lentiviral system for practical experiment reasons.
Using a luminescence-based viability assay, we generated a dose–response curve by treating these cells with a range of cisplatin concentrations (0.5, 1, and 2 µM; ). The results indicated that cells with induced expression of REV1-CTD exhibited lower viability compared to the control cells across all tested concentrations. The consistent reduction in cell viability across the tested cisplatin concentrations in NLS-REV1-CTD-expressing cells provides an empirical basis for selecting a dosage range that may enhance cisplatin efficacy while minimizing off-target effects. Additionally, while the TLS inhibitor JH-RE-06 has been found to induce autophagy ( ), REV1-CTD expression does not (SI Appendix, Fig. S1F), suggesting either a slight difference in mechanism or that JH-RE-06 may have an unknown off-target effect.

## Generalization of Cisplatin Sensitization Phenotype in Multiple Cell Lines with REV1-CTD Expression.
To ensure that our observation that expressing the REV1-CTD increased cisplatin sensitivity was not confined to the HT1080 cell line, the study was expanded to include the human melanoma cell line A375 and K-rasG12D/p53−/− mouse lung carcinoma (KP) cells. Both cell lines were genetically modified to express human REV1-CTD using the same lentiviral Tet-on system used for HT1080 cells.
Upon induction of REV1-CTD expression with doxycycline, both A375 and KP cells exhibited an increased sensitivity to cisplatin, analogous to the phenotype observed in HT1080 cells ( ). This trend was observed at both cisplatin concentrations tested (0.5 and 1 µM), indicating a robust and reproducible phenotype. The heightened sensitivity to cisplatin observed in both A375 and KP cells upon REV1-CTD expression suggests a universal response across different cell types and species. Furthermore, since the KP cells are p53 deficient, these observations indicate that the increased sensitivity to cisplatin killing is not dependent on the p53 status of the cells. As expected, based on the known nuclear localization of its binding partners, we observed that the NLS-tagged REV1-CTD was generally more potent at sensitizing the cells to DNA damage than the derivative lacking the NLS, so we used this construct in subsequent experiments.
 

## Expression of REV1-CTD Enhances Genomic Instability and Micronuclei Formation.
To assess the impact of REV1-CTD expression on genomic stability, we evaluated micronuclei formation under different treatment conditions. Immunofluorescence microscopy ( ) revealed a marked increase in micronuclei in cells expressing NLS-REV1-CTD only when cotreated with cisplatin, compared to control groups. In contrast, REV1-CTD expression alone, in the absence of DNA damage, did not significantly elevate micronuclei frequency above baseline. Quantitative analysis ( ) confirmed that the proportion of micronucleated cells was significantly higher in the doxycycline-induced REV1-CTD-expressing group following cisplatin exposure, consistent with a synergistic enhancement of genomic instability. These findings indicate that REV1-CTD disrupts lesion bypass in a manner that exacerbates DNA damage consequences specifically under genotoxic stress conditions.

## REV1-CTD Expression Reduces Cisplatin-Induced Mutagenesis.
Based on the HPRT mutagenesis assay, cells expressing REV1-CTD exhibited a lower mutational frequency compared to the no doxycycline treatment control even in undamaged cells, consistent with reduced mutagenic TLS activity ( ). Upon treatment with cisplatin, there was a notable increase in the mutation rate in both groups ( ). However, the expression of REV1-CTD significantly mitigated this increase, resulting in a reduced mutational frequency ( ). In contrast, control cells, which did not express REV1-CTD, showed no such reduction in mutation rate ( ). Our demonstration that expression of the REV1-CTD reduces cisplatin-induced mutagenesis, is strong evidence that it is acting by interfering with mutagenic REV1/POLζ-dependent TLS. By disrupting the interactions between endogenous REV1 and the other pathway components, REV1-CTD hinders the ability of cells to bypass DNA lesions efficiently. This interference leads to replication arrest and inhibits the introduction of mutations that would otherwise occur during lesion bypass.

## Cell Cycle Arrest and Sensitivity to Cisplatin in NLS-REV1-CTD Expressing Cells.
Expression of the NLS-tagged REV1-CTD reduced the number of colonies following cisplatin treatment, suggesting impaired cell proliferation and survival. To investigate the underlying mechanism, we performed cell cycle analysis. In the absence of cisplatin, REV1-CTD expression had minimal impact on cell cycle distribution, indicating that it does not induce arrest under basal conditions ( and SI Appendix, Fig. S2A). Upon cisplatin treatment, both control and REV1-CTD-expressing cells exhibited G2 phase accumulation; however, this effect was markedly enhanced in cells expressing REV1-CTD ( and SI Appendix, Fig. S2A), consistent with elevated replication stress and impaired DNA damage resolution. These findings indicate that REV1-CTD disrupts REV1/POLζ-dependent mutagenic TLS, leading to persistent DNA lesions, replication arrest, and G2 phase accumulation. This cell cycle delay likely contributes to the observed decrease in clonogenic survival and underscores a key mechanism by which REV1-CTD potentiates cisplatin cytotoxicity.
 

## Broad Sensitization by REV1-CTD Expression to DNA Damage and Replication Stress Pathway Inhibitors.
In addition to cisplatin, HT1080 cells expressing REV1-CTD exhibited increased sensitivity to BPDE and MMS, DNA-damaging agents with distinct mechanisms of action. Colony formation assays revealed a significant reduction in survival compared to control cells ( ), suggesting that REV1-CTD broadly sensitizes cells to genotoxic stress. We further assessed whether REV1-CTD expression synergizes with inhibitors of DNA damage response pathways. Inhibitors targeting WEE1, ATR, and ATM strongly enhanced cisplatin cytotoxicity in REV1-CTD expressing cells ( ), consistent with the idea that checkpoint loss elevates TLS dependence ( , ). CHK1 inhibition also synergized, likely due to its role in fork stabilization. Similarly, G-quadruplex stabilizers enhanced sensitivity, potentially by reducing endogenous REV1 levels and compounding the TLS blockade ( ). In contrast, inhibition of USP1, MRN, DNA-PK, or PARP did not result in a synergistic decrease in cell viability when combined with REV1-CTD expression ( ). The lack of synergy with USP1 inhibition suggests that REV1-CTD acts downstream of PCNA monoubiquitination and is not dependent on disrupting this regulatory step. Similarly, the absence of an enhanced effect with MRN or DNA-PK inhibitors implies that REV1-CTD does not interfere with the major pathways of double-strand break resection or nonhomologous end joining. The lack of synergy with PARP inhibition is likely due to the limited responsiveness of HT1080 cells, which are BRCA-proficient and thus relatively resistant to PARP inhibitors, rather than a lack of efficacy from REV1-CTD.

## Genetic Evidence that the Role of the REV1-CTD in the Cell Is Mediated by Its Two Recruitment Interfaces.
To confirm that synergistic effects between cisplatin and the REV1-CTD were indeed due to its ability to recruit pathway partners, we generated stable cell lines with various point mutations in the two different interaction domains ( ) that were based on previous structural papers ( ). Compared to cells expressing normal NLS-REV1-CTD, mutations at the insertion (RIR) interface (W1175A and D1186A) resulted in a significant decrease in sensitivity to cisplatin, indicating that these residues are crucial for the sensitizing effect of REV1-CTD ( and SI Appendix, Fig. S2B). Similarly, mutations at the REV7 interface (K1211E, R1215D, and Y1242A) also significantly affected cell survival after cisplatin treatment compared to the reference sequence, indicating that these interactions are also critical for effects of overexpressed REV1-CTD ( and SI Appendix, Fig. S2B). Using an affinity-tagged REV1-CTD and cell lysates, we showed that normal REV1 was able to bind most of the REV7, but all three point-mutants in the REV7 interaction domain showed a much lower affinity for REV7, showing that the Y1242A, K1211E, and R1215D mutations are significantly disrupting the interaction with REV7 ( and SI Appendix, Fig. S2C).
 

## The REV1-CTD Improves Tumor Cell Response to Cisplatin in a Xenograft Mouse Model.
While this strategy of using the REV1-CTD to inhibit mutagenic TLS and increase cell killing by cisplatin, worked well in vitro, there was no guarantee it would also work in a preclinical animal model of cancer, To test this, we used the A375 human melanoma cells we engineered to express the CTD of the REV1 protein (A375 NLS-REV1-CTD) to establish a xenograft model in NCRNU-F (immunodeficient nude) mice. The modified cells were implanted subcutaneously into the mice, resulting in the development of xenograft tumors with an average volume of 100 mm3. Postimplantation, the mice were randomly assigned to one of four treatment groups: 1) a control group receiving standard water and saline injections; 2) a group receiving doxycycline-supplemented water and saline injections to induce REV1-CTD expression; 3) a group receiving standard water and cisplatin injections; and 4) a group receiving both doxycycline-supplemented water and cisplatin injections. All groups underwent biweekly treatment protocols for a duration of 5 wk.
The results ( ) show that the combination of doxycycline and cisplatin treatment led to a striking suppression of tumor progression, nearly to the point of stasis, compared to the other treatment groups. Tumor volumes were markedly reduced in the group receiving combined doxycycline and cisplatin treatment, demonstrating enhanced sensitivity to cisplatin ( ). These findings indicate that expression of the REV1-CTD significantly potentiates the sensitivity of cancer cells to cisplatin, highlighting its potential as a therapeutic target to enhance the efficacy of chemotherapeutic agents in vivo.

## Discussion
Many cancer cells are more reliant on REV1/POLζ-dependent mutagenic TLS than normal cells—a phenotype that has been attributed to enhanced replicative stress caused by the acquisition of oncogenes and compensation for the loss of major DNA repair systems. Unfortunately, increased TLS activity also makes cancer cells unresponsive to genotoxic chemotherapy. Moreover, TLS activity can promote the development of new mutations that favor the development of chemoresistance and secondary malignancies. These considerations have prompted numerous investigators, including ourselves, to suggest that REV1/POLζ-dependent mutagenic TLS is a very attractive target for clinical intervention.
In this paper, we report strong evidence that an attractive alternative inhibitor for mutagenic TLS is the ca. 100 amino acid CTD of REV1 ( ). Although we expressed the human REV1-CTD from a lentivirus for experimental convenience in most of these studies, we also showed that as expected, introducing an mRNA (messenger RNA) encoding the NLS-tagged REV1-CTD protein is likewise effective. A clinical drug that used nanoparticle technology to introduce the mRNA encoding the NLS-REV1-CTD would have multiple attractive features. First, the use of tumor-targeting nanoparticles ( ) would largely confine the delivery of the inhibitor of mutagenic TLS to cancer cells. Second, the delivery of a REV1-CTD inhibitor of mutagenic TLS should allow equivalent cancer cell cytotoxicity to be achieved with a lower dose of genotoxic agent while also reducing mutations. Third, the use of a lower dose of the genotoxic chemotherapeutic agent would reduce the amount of DNA damage to noncancer cells, which would make the chemotherapy much more tolerable.
In addition, we have already shown that nanoparticles designed to deliver a cisplatin prodrug as well as an inhibitory RNA can be very effective in vivo ( ). Using tumor-targeting nanoparticles to deliver both the DNA-damaging agent (e.g., a cisplatin prodrug) and our mRNA-encoded REV1-CTD inhibitor of mutagenic TLS could in principle lead to a major improvement in chemotherapy. It would not only improve the efficacy of the cytotoxic chemotherapy while reducing the risk of acquired chemoresistance and secondary malignancies, but it could also drastically reduce the DNA damage to noncancer cells that is responsible for most debilitating effects of present-day genotoxic chemotherapies.
Another attractive therapeutic aspect of use an mRNA encoding the REV1-CTD, versus a small molecule targeting TLS activity, is that the level of the protein can be tuned by controlling its expression, rate of translation, and its half-life. Other features of the REV1-CTD are also potentially tunable. For example, although multiple protein partners of REV1 (POLη, POLκ, POLι, POLD3, and XRCC1) ( , , , ) all bind to the inserter interface the CTD, they do not all make identical contacts. Thus, it should be possible to develop derivatives of the REV1-CTD that bind certain partners but not others. The use of such derivatives, as well the original REV1-CTD, could also potentially prove useful as research tools to help elucidate the still poorly understood roles of REV1 in other biological processes such as somatic hypermutation and class switch recombination, maintenance of repressive chromatin marks, gene silencing, replication of G-quadruplexes, and possibly heterochromatin replication ( – ).
Finally, our observations suggest some interesting possibilities for future research. For example, assessing replication stress markers (e.g., pRPA, pKAP1, pCHK1), exploring complementary mutagenesis assays like SUPF, and evaluating the mutation spectrum would strengthen the mechanistic understanding of REV1-CTD function. Similarly, exploring dominant-negative strategies targeting other error-prone polymerases such as POLQ could open new therapeutic directions.

## Materials and Methods
Mammalian cell culturing, cell viability assay, RNA transfection, base editing, HPRT mutagenesis assay, and cell cycle analysis are described in SI Appendix, SI Materials and Methods.

## Cell Line Construction.
REV1-CTD and NLS-REV1-CTD were cloned into the vector pCW57-RFP-P2A-MCS (Addgene). These plasmids and the empty vector were transfected into HEK293T cells along helper plasmids to generate lentivirus using standard protocols ( ). These viruses were then used to infect HT1080, A375, and KP cells. After selection with puromycin (Gibco), induction of the Tet-On promoter was stimulated by doxycycline, and positive, RFP expressing cells were sorted using FACS. Single-cell clones were then expanded and screened for REV1 CTD expression with at least three isolates of each being selected for further experiments.

## Clonogenic Survival Assay.
800 cells were plated in triplicate in 6-well plates for 24 h. Cisplatin (cis-diammineplatinum (II) dichloride, Sigma-Aldrich) was added to relevant wells for 24 h. All plates were incubated at 37 °C for 24 h, and cells were allowed to recover for 7 d. To stain the resulting colonies, media were aspirated and then fixed with the fixative (50% methanol and 40% acetic acid 10% water) for 10 min, followed by the addition of 0.25% crystal violet stain (Sigma) in 20% methanol for an hour. Colonies that stained dark blue were counted. Colony formation assay was calculated by dividing colony counts from treated samples by the untreated controls.

## Micronuclei Staining.
10k cells were seeded in each well of 12-well plates containing glass coverslips and then grown and treated as before. Cells were fixed in 4% formaldehyde for 10 min at room temperature. Cells were then mounted on glass slides using ProLong Gold Antifade with DAPI (Invitrogen). Images were taken using a Nikon Eclipse microscope. Eight fields of view were scored for each condition with any cell containing extranuclear foci counted as containing a micronucleus.

## Protein Pulldowns and Western Blots.
Point mutants of REV1-CTD were generated using site-directed mutagenesis and then REV1-CTD proteins were purified as previously described ( ). Cell lysates of HT1080 cells were made using RIPA buffer (Thermofisher). 0.1 µg of purified REV1-CTD was added to 50 μL of cell lysate with 450 μL of buffer (50 mM NaH2PO4, 100 mM NaCl) and incubated at 4 °C for 1 h. 50 μL of washed Ni-NTA magnetic beads (NEB) were added and incubated for a further hour. The supernatant was then collected, and the pellet was washed five times with buffer, then eluted in buffer with 250 μM imidazole. Samples were run and transferred using standard SDS-PAGE methods. The blots were then probed with either a custom α-REV1-CTD antibody or α-REV7 (Abcam AB180579).

## Murine Xenograft Tumor Model.
NCRNU-F (nude) female mice, aged 6 to 8 wk, were randomly divided into four treatment groups (n = 6 per group): 1) no doxycycline, no cisplatin; 2) no doxycycline, with cisplatin; 3) with doxycycline, no cisplatin; and 4) with doxycycline, with cisplatin. Doxycycline was administered via supplementation in the drinking water. To establish xenografts, three million A375 cells mixed with Matrigel (Corning) were injected subcutaneously into each flank of the mice, generating approximately 10 to 12 tumors per treatment group. Once tumors reached a minimum total volume of 100 mm3, treatments were initiated. For cisplatin administration, 1 mg/kg was injected directly into the tumors, twice weekly for 5 wk. In groups designated for cisplatin treatment (with or without doxycycline), each injection had a total volume of 100 μL and was formulated in 10% ethanol, 40% PEG400, and 50% saline. On each dosing day, mice were sedated with isoflurane, tumor dimensions were measured using calipers, body weights were recorded, and the drugs were administered intratumorally. Tumor volumes were calculated using the formula (W2 × L)/2, where W is tumor width and L is tumor length, as previously described ( ).
