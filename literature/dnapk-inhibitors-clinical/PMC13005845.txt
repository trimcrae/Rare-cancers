## Abstract
Currently, there is no permanent treatment for the group of severe monogenic fragile skin conditions epidermolysis bullosa (EB). The recent US Food and Drug Administration (FDA)-approved in vivo gene replacement therapy beremagene geperpavec (Vyjuvek®) provides a promising solution, but it requires ongoing application and is not applicable to all forms of EB. Targeted gene editing approaches directly addressing pathogenic mutations hold great promise for the development of durable personalized therapies. Here, we comprehensively describe the clustered regularly interspaced short palindromic repeats (CRISPR)/CRISPR-associated protein (Cas) gene editing landscape for EB, critically review the advantages and limitations of emerging therapeutic strategies, and present some future perspectives. We find that the widespread application of Cas9 nuclease is currently hindered by off-target genotoxicity, which can be mitigated using Cas9 nickases. Further, new tools including prime editing have recently emerged and hold significant potential for EB gene therapy. Ongoing developments in gene editing technologies focused on improving safety and editing precision offer significant promise for the future clinical translation of potentially lifelong treatments for people with EB.

## Key Points

 

## Introduction

## Epidermolysis Bullosa (EB)
Epidermolysis bullosa (EB) encompasses a group of rare, inherited, and currently incurable fragile skin disorders associated with significant morbidity and mortality [ ]. The exact prevalence is unknown, but it is estimated to affect approximately 500,000 people worldwide. The fragile skin of individuals with EB is often highly susceptible to persistent blistering following minor mechanical trauma. Blisters can affect the skin and mucosal membranes (e.g., oral, gastrointestinal, esophageal) and are frequently accompanied with itch and pain, the severity of which can vary widely based on the disease subtype [ ]. Pathogenic mutations in genes encoding critical structural proteins around the junction of the dermal and epidermal skin layers constitute the primary determinant of disease severity. However, symptoms and clinical outcomes can also be modulated by environmental factors, resulting in a heterogeneous range of disease subtypes [ , ]. In mild forms of EB, blistering is typically superficial and confined primarily to regions of the body frequently exposed to friction, including the extremities. These patients generally do not exhibit serious long-term health complications. For severe forms of EB, patients often present with severely fragile skin from birth, resulting in extensive blistering covering large areas of the body. In such cases, blistering can be intensely painful, severely reducing patients’ quality of life and increasing the risk of systemic complications such as infection, sepsis, nutritional deficiencies, and developmental delay [ ]. Severe disease subtypes are also frequently associated with high rates of aggressive skin cancers and infant mortality [ ]. In the absence of a permanent curative therapy for any EB subtype, treatment is palliative and focuses on supporting patients, managing wounds, and preventing extracutaneous disease manifestations [ ]. Given the wide-ranging nature of extracutaneous symptoms (e.g., cardiovascular, respiratory, gastrointestinal, and ophthalmic), disease management is typically guided by a multidisciplinary team of relevant professionals [ ]. Overall, the economic burden of disease can be significant, with an annual treatment cost of > $30,000 (United States dollars) per patient [ , ]. Thus, the development of novel therapeutics is essential.
Gene therapy aims to address the genetic factors underlying EB and therefore represents a promising therapeutic avenue for providing a permanent cure [ ]. The ease of accessibility to skin tissue simplifies the development process, as cells can be harvested from skin biopsies and expanded in the laboratory for ex vivo therapy development [ ]. Alternatively, treatments can be topically applied in vivo, with the goal of skin reconstruction, as was exemplified by the transient gene replacement therapy—beremagene geperpavec (brand name Vyjuvek®)—recently approved by the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) for treating recessive dystrophic EB (RDEB) [ ]. In this review, we focus our attention on the gene editing strategies under development for EB and discuss their potential capacity to provide a permanent, one-time application treatment for patients. We focus on the promising recent advancements using the versatile suite of gene editing tools based on the clustered regularly interspaced short palindromic repeats (CRISPR) and CRISPR-associated protein (Cas) gene editing tool CRISPR/Cas. Additionally, we address the major roadblocks currently hindering the translation of these therapies into the clinic.

## EB Classification
EB is a hereditary group of monogenic disorders caused by pathogenic mutations in more than 20 different genes encoding key structural proteins within the basement membrane zone, which encompasses the dermal-epidermal junction of the skin and mucosal membranes [ ]. Progressive levels of information are used to classify EB into different subtypes. In recent decades, subtyping has been revised by an international consensus group to reflect refinements in clinical phenotypes and advancements in the understanding of underlying disease mechanisms [ ]. According to the most recent consensus classification, EB can be broadly categorized into four major types based on the implicated protein and the corresponding level of skin fragility and blistering [ ]. In general, disease subtypes affected by proteins present in lower planes of the basement membrane zone correlate with more severe clinical outcomes [ ] (Fig. ).
 EB simplex (EBS) represents the most prevalent disease type and is generally associated with the least severe clinical outcomes. It is most commonly inherited in an autosomal dominant fashion, with mutations in keratin 5 (KRT5) or 14 (KRT14) constituting >70% of all reported cases [ ]. Typically, dominant mutations in KRT5 or KRT14 perturb their proteins assembly into intermediate filaments—critical structures for providing intercellular adhesion between keratinocytes—leading to intraepidermal blistering (Fig. ) [ , ]. Additionally, rare syndromic EBS subtypes can involve mutations in several other genes, including PLEC, EXPH5, CD151, KLHL24, and DST [ ].
Junctional EB (JEB) is a rare disease type inherited in an autosomal recessive manner. It is characterized by skin separation below the epidermis at the dermal-epidermal junction, primarily within the lamina lucida (Fig. ) [ ]. The generalized severe JEB subtype (previously referred to as Herlitz JEB) is typically lethal within the first 2 years from birth and is commonly caused by mutations in the LAMB3 gene (encoding the β3 subunit of the laminin-332 heterotrimer), which underlies 70% of all cases [ ]. Milder JEB subtypes (previously referred to as non-Herlitz JEB) are commonly caused by mutations in COL17A1 encoding type XVII collagen [ ].
Dystrophic EB (DEB) represents another rare disease type and is characterized by blistering below the dermal-epidermal junction within the papillary dermis and can be inherited in a dominant (DDEB) or recessive (RDEB) manner (Fig. ). It is caused by mutations in the COL7A1 gene encoding type VII collagen (C7), synthesized by both keratinocytes and dermal fibroblasts, and assembled into macromolecular anchoring fibril structures, which provide adhesion between the basement membrane and the underlying dermis [ ]. Severe RDEB represents the most clinically severe subtype and is associated with a high risk of aggressive squamous cell carcinomas and mortality during childhood and adolescence [ , ].
Finally, Kindler EB represents an extremely rare disease type caused by mutations in KIND1, through which blistering can occur within any of the aforementioned skin layers [ , ].

## Gene Therapy for EB
The severity of EB symptoms has instigated investigation into therapies aimed at correcting the underlying molecular disease mechanisms. This includes several cell-, protein-, gene-, and small molecule-based therapies that are currently under development [ ]. Promising results have been reported for some of these approaches, with a cell-based therapy gaining approval for the treatment of a subset of JEB patients in Japan [ ]. However, gene therapy confers a potential advantage as it addresses the mutations underlying the disease and, therefore, may enable a permanent curative therapy. Gene therapy is broadly categorized into gene replacement and gene editing approaches. Of these, gene replacement is the most clinically advanced and has been successfully used on a compassionate basis in an ex vivo setting to treat LAMB3-deficient JEB patients [ , , ]. For RDEB, a similar gene replacement strategy was assessed in a phase 1/2a clinical trial (NCT01263379) assessing the long-term safety and efficacy of grafting epidermal sheets composed of autologous keratinocytes that were transduced ex vivo with retroviral vectors harboring the COL7A1 gene. After a mean follow-up time of 5.9 years, 70% of all treated sites across seven RDEB patients demonstrated wound healing of ≥ 50% without any serious adverse events [ ]. A subsequent phase 3 clinical trial (NCT04227106) focusing on the treatment of large chronic wounds resulted in 81.4% of treated wounds showing ≥ 50% healing after 24 weeks, again without any adverse serious events. The success in these trials led to the development of Zevaskyn™ by Abeona Therapeutics Inc., the first FDA-approved combinatorial cell and gene therapy for RDEB.
Despite the success of the ex vivo gene replacement strategy, retroviral vectors integrate semi-randomly into the genome and thus potentially pose a risk of insertional oncogenesis. Variable transgene expression may also be problematic depending on the site of transgene integration. Recently, a non-integrating, topically applied in vivo gene replacement approach—Vyjuvek®—was developed by Krystal Biotech and gained FDA approval for topically delivering the COL7A1 gene packaged within a herpes simplex virus type 1 vector (HSV-1) directly to RDEB wounds [ ]. This was evaluated in 31 RDEB patients in a randomized, placebo-controlled phase 3 clinical trial (NCT04491604). Weekly treatment significantly improved wound healing, including in large chronic wounds, achieving 71% wound closure after 3 months and 67% after 6 months, with no serious adverse events [ ]. Longer term follow-up of up to 112 weeks in 47 patients demonstrated wound closure rates ranging from 61.1 to 89.5%, confirming the long-term efficacy of this treatment [ ]. Since a non-integrating HSV-1 vector is employed for gene delivery, the risk of vector integration is minimal, but consequently necessitates consistent treatment reapplication to maintain wound closure. While C7 is a stable protein with a long half-life at the basement membrane (> 1 month) [ ], this system may prove incompatible with other causative EB proteins. Further, Vyjuvek® may not be effective for treating dominantly inherited subtypes, for example, DDEB and most patients with EBS.
Gene editing offers a highly targeted and potentially permanent strategy theoretically capable of correcting any pathogenic mutation (dominant and recessive) across all genes implicated in EB. It also allows for the restoration of protein expression under the control of the endogenous regulatory machinery. Among the available gene editing technologies, the CRISPR/Cas9 system currently represents the most effective and advanced toolkit. CRISPR-based medicine gained its first clinical approval in 2023 when Casgevy entered the market for treating sickle cell disease and transfusion-dependent beta thalassemia [ ]. Multiple clinical trials investigating CRISPR therapeutics are also currently ongoing for various indications [ ]. In contrast, gene editing for EB remains at the preclinical stage. Despite significant improvements in recent years, key challenges related to safety, suboptimal editing efficiency, and delivery of genetically corrected cells need to be addressed before clinical application. For strategies targeting EB keratinocytes, efficient rates of targeted gene editing are imperative to facilitate long-lived therapies by ensuring the correction of a sufficient population of epidermal stem cells.
While gene editing for EB has predominantly focused on the ex vivo correction of skin cells (i.e., fibroblasts and keratinocytes), genetic correction of inaccessible mucosal tissues such as the gastrointestinal tract and esophagus remains a significant challenge and has not been demonstrated to date. Ex vivo CRISPR-based gene editing targeting COL7A1 for RDEB in mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) and hematopoietic stem cells (HSCs) has been explored as an alternative strategy for addressing this issue [ , ]. Following intravenous delivery, these gene-corrected cells may have the capacity to migrate to sites of tissue damage and inflammation and facilitate a therapeutic effect. This includes the expression of C7, immunomodulation, tissue regeneration, and the potential differentiation into epithelial or fibroblastic cells [ , ].

## The CRISPR/Cas9 Genome Editing System

## Cas9 Nucleases and Nickases
The CRISPR/Cas system has become a widely adopted tool for fundamental and applied science. Initially discovered in prokaryotes as an adaptive immunity system, it has since been repurposed as a highly efficient genome editing tool [ ]. A wide variety of CRISPR/Cas systems have been discovered across many different bacterial species. Among these, the class 2, type II system from Streptococcus pyogenes based on the single effector molecule—Cas9 endonuclease—has been the most widely adopted due to its versatility and ease of use. For use in gene editing, only two components are required: a single guide RNA (sgRNA) complexed with a Cas9 endonuclease [ ]. The sgRNA is composed of two smaller RNAs: a CRISPR RNA (crRNA) of 36–43 nucleotides fused to a trans-activating CRISPR RNA (tracrRNA) of 67–89 nucleotides. While the tracrRNA serves as the scaffold by binding the sgRNA to Cas9, the crRNA contains a programmable ‘spacer’ with a user-defined sequence complementary to a target site in the genome. This enables targeting of the sgRNA/Cas9 ribonucleoprotein (RNP) complex to the target site where the Cas9 can generate a double-stranded DNA break (DSB) [ , ]. A prerequisite for the generation of a DSB by Cas9 from S. pyogenes (spCas9) is the presence of a trinucleotide (NGG) protospacer adjacent motif (PAM) located directly downstream on the 3ʹ end of the target site of the non-hybridized genomic strand. However, Cas9 orthologs from other bacterial species recognize distinct PAM sites. Efforts to engineer Cas9 variants with less stringent PAM requirements have further broadened the targeting range for CRISPR genome editing, including the development of a near PAM-less Cas9 variant (spRYCas9) [ ]. Additionally, Cas12a, another class 2 CRISPR system, recognizes T-rich PAM sites (5ʹTTTV-3ʹ), making it a favorable option for gene editing in AT-rich regions [ ].
Following hybridization of the spacer portion of the sgRNA and target genomic sequence, two nuclease domains (RuvC and HNC) cleave each DNA strand [ ]. Following the generation of the subsequent DSB, two primary endogenous DNA repair mechanisms are employed for repair. End joining (EJ), including non-homologous end joining (NHEJ) pathways and microhomology-mediated end joining (MMEJ), represents the dominant repair choice as it is active throughout all phases of the cell cycle [ , ]. These processes involve direct ligation of the two broken DNA ends and are highly efficient but also error prone, commonly resulting in small (< 10 bp) insertions and deletions (INDELs), which can be efficiently employed for either gene inactivation or gene reframing approaches (Fig. ). Alternatively, the homology-directed repair (HDR) pathway represents a high-fidelity repair mechanism that relies on the inclusion of a donor DNA repair template containing the desired edit flanked by homologous sequences to promote integration into the target site via homologous recombination (Fig. ). In contrast to NHEJ, HDR is only active during the G2 and S phases of the cell cycle and therefore represents a lower efficiency repair mechanism [ , ].
 A primary goal of gene editing for EB is the precise correction of any pathogenic mutation, restoring a mutant DNA and protein sequence to its wild-type version. In recent years, significant improvements in the rates of therapeutic HDR have been achieved. However, these approaches have only been demonstrated for a limited number of mutations and still lack consistent and clinically useful efficiencies between different loci (Table ) [ ]. Consequently, strategies exploiting the more efficient EJ-repair pathways have been investigated (Table ) [ – ]. Despite the high efficiencies, such strategies can produce a high level of undesirable INDELs and protein variants with unknown functionality [ ]. Moreover, off-target genotoxicity mediated by partial homology between the spacer sequence and unintended off-target sequences in the genome represents a further limitation. To mitigate this risk, a newer Cas9 variant—Cas9 nickase (Cas9n)—was developed by inactivating one of the nuclease domains. The D10A variant with a mutant HNH nuclease domain has proven to be the most effective version and generates a single-stranded nick on the non-target strand. However, the H840A variant (mutant RuvC nuclease domain) is also commonly utilized [ ]. While single-stranded DNA (ssDNA) nicks are typically repaired with high fidelity via the base-excision repair pathway, the use of dual Cas9-nickases (dual Cas9n) in close proximity can enable the nicking of each DNA strand to generate a staggered DSB substrate for HDR (Fig. ) [ ]. The major advantage of this system is the required hybridization of two sgRNAs, which significantly increases specificity for the on-target site while decreasing non-specific activity at off-target sites [ , ].

 

## Base Editors and Prime Editors
More recently, prime editing (PE) and base editing (BE) technologies were added to the gene editing toolbox [ , ] and investigated for EB with the aim of enhancing safety and precision (Fig. ; Table ). These editing modalities do not rely on DSBs or donor repair templates and therefore can potentially reduce the frequency of unintended on-target INDELs and off-target activity. BE aims to precisely install single nucleotide variants using an ssDNA-nucleobase modifying enzyme fused to either a Cas9n or a dead Cas9 [ ]. Either an adenine deaminase (adenine base editor [ABE]) or cytosine deaminase (cytosine base editor [CBE]) is used to facilitate A·T to G·C or C·G to T·A transitions, respectively. The deamination process initially generates mutagenic intermediates (uracil for CBE and inosine for ABE), which are endogenously repaired via the high-fidelity base excision repair pathway. A Cas9n fused to the deaminase generates a nick on the opposite strand to bias the repair process to the strand containing the deaminated base. This strand then serves as a template to install the edit on the opposite strand [ ] (Fig. ). A potential concern regarding BE is the unintended conversion of other eligible nucleotides within the deamination window (defined by its distance from the PAM site), which can potentially generate bystander mutations. A lack of suitable PAM sites can also limit the mutations that can be targeted, and the mutations themselves are limited to point mutations. However, new BE variants with improved characteristics continue to be developed (see reference [ ] for a review).
For PE, a Cas9n (H840A) is fused to a modified reverse transcriptase. To facilitate gene editing, a specialized PE guide RNA (gRNA) (pegRNA) is employed. In addition to encoding a programmable spacer sequence for target recognition, the 3ʹ end of the pegRNA also contains a reverse transcription template (RTT) encoding a desired edit and an adjacent primer binding site (PBS) to initiate reverse transcription. To initiate gene editing, the Cas9n first generates a nick three nucleotides upstream of the PAM site on the non-target strand. This liberates a 3ʹ and 5ʹ DNA flap, resulting in hybridization of the 3ʹ flap with the PBS portion of the pegRNA and installation of the desired edit following reverse transcription [ ]. PE is a highly versatile tool theoretically capable of correcting at least 89% of all disease-causing variants, with very few INDEL byproducts [ ].

## Methods for Assessing Gene Editing Efficiency
We note that the studies listed in Table used a variety of assays to assess HDR and NHEJ-mediated editing rates. These include next-generation sequencing (NGS), Sanger sequencing of cloned cells, Sanger sequencing of cloned polymerase chain reaction (PCR) amplicons and long-range sequencing technologies, including Oxford Nanopore Technologies. Each assay comes with limitations that make it difficult to compare editing rates between these studies, some of which are discussed later in this review in Sect. . Of particular concern is that unexpected large deletions may lead to primer site loss so PCR-based approaches may not reflect the true editing landscape (see [ ] for a comprehensive review of structural variants generated by CRISPR-based gene editing). Therefore, some studies may have overreported editing rates. For example, when analyzing a 10-kb amplicon spanning a specific edit site, we previously reported unexpected large on-target deletions [ ]. Of these, ~10% of long-range reads had a deletion that ablated at least one primer site required for short-range (1-kb) amplification [ ]. This phenomenon has also recently been demonstrated experimentally by Cullot et al. (2024), where apparent HDR gain, as assessed by short-read NGS, is shown to be skewed upwards by the presence of large deletions detected using long-range sequencing [ ].

## CRISPR/Cas9-Based Gene Editing Strategies Under Development for EB

## Gene Repair Facilitated by Homology-Directed Repair

## Cas9 Nuclease Strategies
Early Cas9 nuclease-based gene editing studies for EB relied on selection mechanisms to enrich for corrected cells. In 2016, the first demonstration was reported [ ]. Targeting RDEB, plasmids encoding the editing reagents and HDR templates were delivered into primary RDEB fibroblasts targeting COL7A1. A puromycin selection cassette flanked by LoxP sequences was included adjacent to the HDR template to select for cells that had undergone HDR (HDR+). Eight out of 17 clones demonstrated correction at the genomic level after selection. The puromycin gene located within an adjacent intron was subsequently removed using Cre recombinase, leaving behind a small LoxP footprint. Induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs) were generated from the selected cells and differentiated into multiple cell types including MSCs, hematopoietic cells, and keratinocytes, representing a strategy that could be applied to treat the broad systemic nature of RDEB. Unfortunately, analysis of unintended off-target Cas9 cleavage using the surveyor assay showed evidence of editing within the ACAP3 gene [ ]. Using a similar selection strategy, Hainzl and colleagues targeted a prevalent exon 80 COL7A1 mutation (c. 6527insC) in primary RDEB keratinocytes. Approximately 17% gene correction was reported post-selection based on the analysis of PCR products from 94 clones. Similar to the previous study, a 116 nucleotide LoxP footprint was left within intron 80 [ ]. In a separate study, Jacków and colleagues used fluorescence-based cell sorting to enrich for HDR+ iPSCs derived from primary RDEB fibroblasts following the correction of two COL7A1 mutations present in two RDEB patients (patient 1: c.2470insG/c.2470insG; patient 2: c.2470insG/c.3948insT) [ ]. Cas9 nuclease and sgRNAs were electroporated into the iPSCs as RNP complexes, along with single-stranded oligodeoxynucleotide (ssODN) repair templates and a plasmid encoding  green fluorescent protein (GFP) to select for transfected cells. When targeting the homozygous mutation in patient 1, up to 58% of the analyzed clones showed biallelic correction following GFP enrichment. Keratinocytes and fibroblasts generated from the gene-corrected iPSCs were subsequently engineered into skin equivalents (SEs) and transplanted onto immunodeficient mice, with normal C7 expression and anchoring fibril formation observed for up to 2 months at the dermal-epidermal junction [ ]. Also using iPSCs, another group aimed to repair a COL7A1 mutation using the piggyBac transposon system to excise a puromycin selection cassette integrated along with an HDR template at a target site [ ]. Before excision of the cassette, correct HDR integration was only reported in 8% of the analyzed clones. Additionally, this strategy was relatively complex, as it required a second transfection with a transposase to facilitate excision of the cassette, after which the iPSCs could be differentiated into fibroblasts and keratinocytes, with C7 expression confirmed. In a more recent study also using iPSCs, Neumayer and colleagues aimed to simplify the iPSC derivation workflow [ ]. This involved a single manufacturing step achieved by transfecting RDEB fibroblasts with iPSC reprogramming factors together with high-fidelity Cas9 nuclease (hfCas9)-RNPs and ssODN repair templates. After 2 weeks, iPSC colonies from three donors harboring the COL7A1 Colorado mutation (c.7485+5G>A) were screened by Sanger sequencing of PCR clones, revealing monoallelic correction in up to 4% of cells. Gene-corrected iPSCs were differentiated into organotypic skin grafts and grafted onto immunodeficient mice, with C7 observed at the dermal-epidermal junction for up to 1 month [ ]. Overall, this strategy allowed the derivation of gene-corrected RDEB-derived iPSCs in approximately 4 weeks, overcoming some of the previous limitations, including extensive cell culture time and the requirement for an additional cell cloning step [ ].
Targeting a LAMB3 mutation for JEB, Benati and colleagues co-transduced Cas9 editing components within adenovectors and the entire LAMB3 complementary DNA (cDNA) within integration-deficient lentiviral vectors [ ]. Low levels of HDR (< 0.5%) were reported at the genomic level. However, the authors exploited the in vitro adhesion advantage of laminin-332+ keratinocytes to select for corrected cells, resulting in 17% correction following two rounds of selection. Unfortunately, low levels of off-target activity (0.9%) were reported at one site, as well as unintended integration-deficient lentiviral vector genomic integration events [ ].
Selection of gene-corrected cells can be cumbersome and cytotoxic, which is likely incompatible with translation into the clinic. Antibiotic selection can also leave LoxP footprints within the genome when using the methods described above, precluding the ultimate goal of traceless gene repair. Subsequent improvements in HDR efficiencies have enabled the evaluation of selection-free strategies. In one study, Izmiryan and colleagues delivered Cas9 and HDR template editing reagents into primary RDEB keratinocytes and fibroblasts using integration-deficient lentiviral vectors, achieving 11% and 15.7% correction of a COL7A1 mutation at the transcript level, respectively. Restoration of C7 protein and anchoring fibril formation was observed in engineered SEs for up to 2 months following grafting onto immunodeficient mice, with no dermal-epidermal blistering [ ]. This is despite previous reports estimating that correction efficiencies of at least 20–35% are required for complete in vivo phenotypic reversion for RDEB [ , ]. A likely explanation for the reported functional repair was the selection of corrected keratinocyte stem cells in vivo [ ]. Nevertheless, higher HDR rates are likely necessary to consistently facilitate sufficient stem cell targeting.
The strategies described thus far are associated with off-target Cas9 nuclease activity [ , ] and unintended retroviral integration events [ ]. To mitigate this risk, subsequent selection-free strategies adopted delivery of the Cas9 and sgRNA as RNP complexes delivered via electroporation [ , , ]. In contrast to viral vector or plasmid-based delivery, which can result in prolonged Cas9 expression and which can randomly integrate into the genome, RNPs have a short intracellular half-life, allowing for reduced off-target activity while maintaining robust on-target activity [ , ]. In one study, Bonafont and colleagues corrected two COL7A1 mutations in keratinocytes derived from two RDEB donors with a prevalent homozygous mutation in exon 80 (c.6527insC) and a third donor with a homozygous mutation in intron 79. RNPs were co-delivered with adeno-associated vectors (AAVs) harboring HDR repair templates. Notably, the repair templates spanned up to 11 COL7A1 exons, depending on the template design tested, covering approximately 35% of the reported COL7A1 mutations in the DEB registry [ ]. The templates were designed without intron 79, enabling a simple genotyping method of cloned cells based on size discrimination of agarose gel bands corresponding to corrected and uncorrected PCR products. While HDR efficiencies of up to 50% were reported targeting both mutations, only a limited number of clones were analyzed. Further, since the efficiencies were estimated based on densitometric analysis of the different size agarose gel bands, PCR bias in favor of the shorter, corrected allele lacking intron 79 cannot be ruled out. Nevertheless, normal skin architecture without blistering was reported in SEs generated from gene-corrected RDEB keratinocytes for up to 12 weeks following grafting onto immunodeficient mice. The authors also employed the same editing strategy in MSCs and cord blood-derived CD34+ cells derived from healthy donors with comparable efficacy, an approach that could potentially be used to treat the systemic complications of RDEB [ ]. In a more recent study, Berhault and colleagues delivered RNPs and ssODN repair templates into primary skin cells to correct a COL7A1 mutation in exon 80 [ ]. Notably, they used an sgRNA targeting intron 79 to minimize mutagenic INDEL formation within the COL7A1 coding sequence. Characterization of HDR via the cloning of PCR products from gene-edited RDEB fibroblasts revealed that 20 of 37 clones showed evidence of mutation correction. However, the majority of these events were accompanied by INDELs within intron 79, with only four clones (10.8%) showing evidence of precise HDR. Although the INDELs were intronic, such events still carry a risk of disrupting splicing motifs. Moreover, this strategy will not be applicable when targeting mutations that rely on sgRNA cleavage within exons.
Assessment of HDR based on the quantification of PCR products from a limited pool of clonal cells is potentially prone to sampling and PCR bias. NGS represents an alternative approach capable of analyzing bulk-edited cell populations (representative of thousands of individual cells) to provide a more accurate and comprehensive assessment of HDR. Such an analysis was undertaken by one group who targeted a COL17A1 mutation for the potential treatment of JEB by electroporating RNPs and ssODN repair templates into primary JEB keratinocytes [ ]. NGS-based Illumina sequencing analysis of bulk-edited cells revealed impressive gene correction rates of up to 38% using an hfCas9 nuclease. Analysis of engineered SEs cultured for 3 weeks demonstrated restoration of type XVII collagen protein accurately localized to the dermal-epidermal junction and normal skin architecture [ ]. More recently, our laboratory employed a similar strategy to repair three separate COL7A1 mutations. Here, HDR rates of up to 32% and 75% were reported in RDEB fibroblasts and keratinocytes, respectively. These precise gene correction rates represent the highest in the field to date and enabled C7 protein re-expression at the dermal-epidermal junction in in vitro engineered SEs [ ]. To achieve this, the small molecule M3814 was used to inhibit NHEJ repair by binding to the canonical NHEJ repair factor DNA-dependent protein kinase. It is worth noting that NHEJ inhibition has been shown to promote genomic instability and increase cell cycle arrest and apoptosis [ , ]. Therefore, while this study represents a promising advancement in the efficiency and consistency of precision gene editing for EB, thorough safety analysis will be necessary to evaluate any potential genotoxic effects before the use of these compounds in the clinic.

## Dual-Cas9n Strategies
To enhance the safety of HDR-mediated repair, some groups have pursued a dual-Cas9n approach to precisely repair pathogenic variants. These have typically fallen short of the correction efficiencies achieved with Cas9 nuclease [ , , , ], with earlier studies requiring selection mechanisms to enrich for HDR+ cells [ , ]. For example, Kocher and colleagues aimed to precisely repair a dominant mutation within the KRT14 gene. A minicircle vector carrying an HDR template spanning intron 3 to the 3ʹ untranslated region (UTR) of KRT14, and expression plasmids harboring sgRNA and Cas9 D10A nickase were co-transfected into an EBS patient-derived keratinocyte cell line. Two rounds of blasticidin selection were used to initially enrich for transfected cells before an assessment of HDR in 96 clones revealed an HDR efficiency of up to 32% [ ]. Later, the same group employed a similar selection strategy, this time targeting a COL7A1 mutation in RDEB keratinocytes, achieving up to 89% correction post-selection [ ].
More recently, RNP-based and selection-free dual-Cas9n strategies have been investigated to potentially facilitate a faster route into the clinic [ , ]. Targeting a prevalent COL7A1 mutation, Kocher and colleagues co-electroporated RNPs and ssODNs into primary RDEB skin cells, achieving precise HDR correction rates of up to 21% and 10% based on NGS in RDEB keratinocytes and fibroblasts, respectively. Assessment of engineered SEs revealed patchy C7 restoration, and only a slight improvement in dermal-epidermal separation, highlighting the potential need for further improvements in HDR efficiencies [ ]. Recently, our group established a dual-Cas9n strategy in primary JEB keratinocytes targeting a hotspot LAMB3 mutation (c.1903C>T) [ ]. Here, subtle ssODN template modifications enabled up to 54% precise correction of the heterozygous mutation, assessed using Oxford Nanopore Technology sequencing analysis. This resulted in the restoration of laminin-332 secretion and rescue of JEB keratinocyte adhesion in vitro. Accurate laminin-332 deposition at the basement membrane and restoration of the transcription factor Np63α—critical for epidermal stem cell maintenance—was also observed in in vitro engineered SEs [ ]. This strategy is potentially widely applicable to a high proportion (approximately a third) of JEB patients who harbor this mutation [ ]. While a significant advancement in precision HDR, the consistency of this strategy across multiple loci needs to be demonstrated before widespread adoption into the clinic or personalized therapies.

## Gene Editing Strategies Based on End-Joining Repair

## Inactivation of Dominant-Negative Alleles
Exploitation of efficient EJ repair facilitated by Cas9 nuclease to knock down a dominant mutant allele has been evaluated to overcome dominant-negative protein effects [ , ]. However, these approaches are limited to mutations with a nearby PAM site in the correct orientation so that the sgRNA target sequence overlaps with the mutant sequence to avoid Cas9 cleavage of the wild-type allele. Shinkuma and colleagues provided the first demonstration of this approach [ ]. Expression vectors containing the editing reagents were co-electroporated into iPSCs generated from DDEB fibroblasts. Four clones with different frameshift mutations were differentiated into fibroblasts and keratinocytes for further analysis. While truncated C7 generated by editing in these clones was detected at low levels within differentiated cells (as opposed to a complete knock-out), the authors demonstrated that these truncated variants were unable to form trimers with wild-type C7, thus reversing the dominant negative C7 interaction [ ]. However, this study highlights the importance of screening individual clones for evaluating expression levels and functionality, a process that can require extensive time and effort.
The gene inactivation strategy has also been employed for specifically disrupting a mutant allele for EBS targeting either KRT5 [ ] or KRT14 [ ]. For example, Cattaneo and colleagues efficiently inactivated a mutant KRT14 allele in up to 94% of bulk primary EBS keratinocytes, resulting in the restoration of normal intermediate filament assembly. Epidermal sheets composed of the gene-edited EBS keratinocytes demonstrated enhanced structural integrity comparable to wild-type cells, with a reversion of intraepidermal blistering also evident in engineered SEs. Notably, the authors showed that edited epidermal stem cells were retained following the editing process [ ]. Similarly, Bchetnia and colleagues also demonstrated highly efficient and specific knock-out targeting a dominant-mutant EBS allele, further highlighting the efficiency of this strategy [ ]. However, in both studies, no in vivo experiments were performed to confirm phenotypic reversion, which is important given the generation of mutant INDEL products with unknown function.

## Reading Frame Restoration
Gene editing strategies based on EJ repair have proven highly efficient for achieving reading frame restoration [ – , , ]. Following induction of a DSB, these approaches rely on the error-prone nature of EJ repair to introduce semi-unpredictable INDEL events which may restore the open reading frame of a gene and result in protein re-expression [ , , ]. For example, Kocher and colleagues aimed to introduce the most prevalent Cas9-mediated INDEL product—a single adenine sense-strand insertion—predicted to reframe COL7A1 [ ]. The predicted insertion was reported in > 30% of editing events in immortalized RDEB keratinocytes, leading to the restoration of C7 localization at the dermal-epidermal junction in engineered SEs. After accounting for all INDEL events, > 70% COL7A1 reframing was achieved. However, no in vivo experiments were performed to validate the function of any reframed C7 protein variants. It is also important to note that the predictable adenine insertion exploited in this study would most likely not apply to all sgRNA designs [ ]. Another group employed a similar strategy using an allele-specific sgRNA overlapping with a COL7A1 mutation to specifically reframe the mutant allele [ ]. The authors reported that 17 out of 50 clones (34%) had been successfully reframed and chose two reframed mutants for further analysis. Both analyzed clones demonstrated restoration of C7 triplex helix formation and accurate basement membrane deposition in vivo for up to 8 weeks following intradermal injection of the clonal cells into immunodeficient mice [ ].
Although gene reframing represents an efficient strategy, it is likely to suffer from inconsistencies between loci due to differences in the INDEL profiles generated at each site. Additionally, these strategies are complicated by the need to screen single-cell clones to evaluate the functionality of reframed protein variants. To overcome these limitations, reframing strategies employing two sgRNAs can be implemented to generate deletions encompassing entire in-frame COL7A1 exons harboring frameshift mutations for RDEB. These strategies have proven remarkably efficient at generating precise deletions spanning the two Cas9 nuclease cleavage sites, with minimal unintended on-target INDEL events within coding sequences [ , ]. This was first demonstrated by Bonafont and colleagues, who efficiently removed exon 80 of COL7A1 containing a prevalent frameshift mutation (c.6527insC), achieving up to 80% targeted exon removal [ ]. Grafting of engineered SEs onto immunodeficient mice showed restoration of C7 expression at the dermal-epidermal junction for up to 12 weeks, with complete phenotypic reversion [ ]. Later, our group implemented a similar strategy targeting three novel COL7A1 exons (31, 68, 109), achieving between 89 and 95% exon deletion. Restoration of C7 expression and accurate localization in engineered SEs was demonstrated following the deletion of exon 31 [ ]. Future in vivo experiments will be required to confirm whether the truncated C7 lacking these exons retains function. Recently, the same strategy was further extended to two larger COL7A1 exons (73 and 105). The authors reported average exon deletion efficiencies of 82% and 74% for exon 73 and 105, respectively. However, this was assessed using densitometry of PCR bands, which lacks accurate quantification and is prone to PCR bias [ ]. Nevertheless, thermal stability analysis of the C7 variants lacking exons 73 or 105 revealed comparable triple helix stability to a healthy donor, indicating that the removal of either exon did not perturb normal triple helix formation. Further, grafting of exon-deleted engineered SEs confirmed C7 deposition and absence of blistering after up to 11 weeks [ ]. While exon deletion approaches have enabled the highest rates of targeted gene editing for EB, the requirement for two sgRNAs potentially increases the risk of off-target Cas9 cleavage [ ]. Moreover, exon skipping is only applicable to mutations within a subset of dispensable exons typically residing within the helical domains of COL7A1 and COL17A1 [ ]. We note that natural exon skipping within other EB genes (e.g., LAMB3, PLEC) has been reported to ameliorate symptoms [ ].
A dual-Cas9n approach has also been employed for achieving reading frame restoration [ ]. Here, one group employed a dual-Cas9n reframing strategy targeting a COL17A1 mutation in primary JEB keratinocytes [ ]. One sgRNA was designed to overlap with the mutation site to ensure INDEL formation only on the mutant allele. Overall, 28.3% of total editing events were found to reframe the mutant COL17A1 allele (+2/−1 frame), leading to type XVII collagen restoration in vitro and accurate deposition in engineered SEs [ ]. However, in vivo experiments were not performed to evaluate the functionality of the reframed protein variants [ ].

## Gene Repair with Base Editors and Prime Editors
Recently, a few groups have investigated the use of the newer BE and PE technologies for the potential treatment of EB [ – ]. Osborn and colleagues reported the first use of BE in the field [ ]. Here, an sgRNA and an enhanced ABE known as ABEmax delivered as mRNA were used to target distinct homozygous or heterozygous mutations in two RDEB fibroblast cell lines, achieving correction rates of up to 23.8% and 8.2%, respectively. However, when targeting the heterozygous mutation, two bystander mutations within the deamination window were found, with one showing higher editing than the targeted nucleotide. Off-target activity (~5%) was also reported within the UBA7 gene [ ]. Another study also employed the ABEmax system to target COL7A1 mutations in RDEB fibroblasts [ ]. Targeted correction efficiencies of up to 30.6% were reported, with minimal INDEL byproducts (0.1–3.3%). However, bystander mutations of up to 44.5% as well as off-target activity of > 20% were reported at one site [ ]. In a separate study, Sheriff and colleagues utilized the newer BE variant ABE8e containing mutations within the deaminase enzyme [ ], which significantly enhances on-target deamination [ ]. Electroporation of the editing reagents as mRNA into primary RDEB fibroblasts enabled remarkably efficient correction rates of 94.6%, with C7 restoration reported in engineered SEs [ ]. Later, the same group used the same BE variant, optimizing the delivery of mRNA encoding the editing reagents into RDEB fibroblasts using Lipofectamine MessengerMax™. Again, remarkably high editing efficiencies were reported, emphasizing the high efficacy of this system [ ]. Recently, the ABE8e system was used again, this time targeting four distinct COL7A1 mutations, with correction efficiencies consistently exceeding > 90% in primary RDEB keratinocytes [ ]. Bystander mutations were observed at two of the loci, reaching frequencies of up to 20% at one locus, which could be eliminated when using the ABEmax BE variant. Further, off-target activity (~ 11%) at one off-target site was detected in one donor [ ]. However, engineered SEs composed of base-edited RDEB keratinocytes and fibroblasts demonstrated sustained C7 localization at the basement membrane after 10 weeks following engraftment onto immunodeficient mice, underscoring the high efficacy of the ABE8e BE technology [ ].
Cytosine BE has also been investigated for EB [ ]. In one study, electroporation of mRNA encoding the CBE3 variant and sgRNA into RDEB-derived iPSCs and RDEB fibroblasts enabled editing efficiencies of up to 23.5% and 46%, respectively. However, bystander mutations were evident in up to 4.8% and 19.4% of NGS reads in base-edited fibroblasts and iPSCs, respectively. Nevertheless, C7 and de novo anchoring fibril formation was observed following engraftment of SEs composed of base-edited RDEB fibroblasts onto immunodeficient mice [ ]. Taken together, BE represents a promising gene editing strategy capable of achieving highly efficient targeted gene editing rates. However, off-target activity still represents a potential limitation of this technology and can occur in a gRNA-dependent and independent manner [ ]. In addition, BE can generate a high frequency of bystander mutations and is restricted to addressing point mutations, limiting its applicability [ ].
PE is a relatively new innovation [ ], and as such, its use for EB has only been reported in three studies to date [ – ]. In one study, up to 10.5% correction of a COL7A1 mutation was achieved in RDEB fibroblasts using the PE3 system (see reference [ ] for a recent review about PE systems). Notably, accurate C7 localization was reported following grafting of SEs generated with the PE-treated RDEB fibroblasts and uncorrected RDEB keratinocytes onto immunodeficient mice [ ]. In a recent study, Steinbeck and colleagues employed a twin PE reframing approach using PEmax and the new PE6 variant (rationally engineered to optimize the RT and Cas9 domains) to excise exon 5 of COL7A1 containing a frameshift mutation [ ]. Electroporation of plasmids encoding the PE reagents into RDEB skin cells and iPSCs promoted exon deletion with minimal (< 5%) INDEL formation, and restoration of C7 expression. Notably, attB recombinase attachment sequences were incorporated into the twin pegRNAs and concomitantly installed at the target site during exon excision. This subsequently enabled the reintegration of a wild-type copy of exon 5 facilitated by the Bxb1 recombinase [ ]. This proof-of-concept study highlights the potential of PE to facilitate precise in-frame deletions and integrations, whilst bypassing the need for DSBs.
Recently, Hierl and colleagues demonstrated the first use of PE targeting JEB [ ]. Electroporation of PE3 mRNA into primary JEB keratinocytes enabled efficient COL17A1 correction of up to 60% and 30% when targeting two distinct compound heterozygous variants, without detectable genotoxicity as assessed by chromosomal aberrations analysis by single targeted linker-mediated PCR sequencing (CAST-Seq). Notably, COL17A1-corrected cells displayed a selective advantage following grafting onto immunodeficient mice, constituting > 90% of the basal keratinocyte layer within the skin grafts after 6 weeks [ ]. Overall, PE is a highly promising technology, and new variants with improved efficacy continue to be developed, holding great potential for personalized EB gene therapies. Additionally, the commercial availability of a Cas9n-RT fusion protein may encourage more widespread adoption of PE in the future.

## The Off-Target Gene Editing Landscape for EB

## Off-Target Detection Methods
Unintended off-target editing remains a hurdle to the clinical progression of CRISPR-based therapeutics. We recently published a review highlighting the high prevalence of on- and off-target structural variants facilitated by Cas9 nuclease gene editing in a range of human cell types, in some cases reaching strikingly high frequencies of up to 40% [ ]. For EB, off-target Cas9 editing has been frequently reported, ranging from < 1% up to 96% [ , , , , , , , , , ]. Sequencing of short PCR amplicons (< 1 kb) spanning a small number of in silico predicted off-target sites represents the most common technique to evaluate off-target activity. Unfortunately, this method is inadequate for detecting larger structural variants, which are proving to be common byproducts of gene editing facilitated by Cas9 nuclease [ , , ]. Such analyses also suffer from low sensitivity when Sanger sequencing is utilized. Additionally, in silico prediction tools are incapable of identifying all bona fide off-target sites. This was recently demonstrated by Klermund and colleagues in primary EB keratinocytes following Cas9-based editing [ ]. Here, they utilized a modified version of their CAST-Seq pipeline, an unbiased tool for identifying genome-wide off-target editing events (see reference [ ] for an overview of CAST-Seq). For one of the analyzed sgRNAs targeting COL7A1, this protocol enabled the discovery of 17 sites with evident off-target editing, with one showing > 90% INDELs, where earlier in silico analysis had predicted only one off-target site [ ]. Thus, the routine adoption of robust and unbiased techniques is mandatory for providing a more accurate picture of the gene editing landscape for EB and guiding future efforts in the field. The fact that off-target effects have been reported in all gene editing studies for EB when unbiased techniques have been implemented (e.g., CAST-Seq, genome-wide unbiased identification of DSBs enabled by sequencing [GUIDE-Seq], Abnoba-Seq, linear amplification-mediated PCR [LAM-PCR]), emphasizes this point (Table ). It is important to highlight that off-target editing occurring at low frequencies or within non-coding sequences may not have any functional consequences. However, this will need to be evaluated on a case-by-case basis.

## Patient-Specific Guide Design
The incidence of off-target events varies greatly between different sgRNAs [ ]. Therefore, careful guide design can help to minimize the risk of off-target events. Several algorithms are used to this end (e.g., CHOPCHOP, CRISPOR, Cas-OFFinder, CRISOT), but they generally fail to consider polymorphisms and genetic variation between individuals [ , ]. We [ ], and others [ ], have shown that this is an important consideration and that polymorphisms common in some ancestral groups can generate new off-target sites. For example, an in silico analysis of a published EB-specific sgRNA [ ] identified one variant, present in an East Asian population with a Popmax frequency (allele frequency of a variant in the ethnicity where it is most prevalent) of 0.35, that reduced the mismatch to an off-target site from three to two base pairs [ ]. Although the impact of this was not validated experimentally, it demonstrates that guide design should incorporate a variant aware component to help to minimize unwanted effects.

## Harnessing Alternative Cas9 Variants with Enhanced Safety
Precise gene editing facilitated by dual Cas9n has become more widely adopted for correcting pathogenic EB mutations due to its enhanced safety profile [ ]. This has been demonstrated by several groups who have compared the off-target effects between the dual-Cas9n strategy and Cas9 nuclease [ , , , ]. To date, despite the frequent reports of off-target Cas9 cleavage in the field, off-target editing using nickases has not been reported. Thus, Cas9n currently represent a more favorable gene editing strategy but require further improvements in consistent gene repair efficiencies before their full potential can be realized. Protein engineering may augment the Cas9 nuclease strategy by enhancing on-target specificity. For example, the commercially available hfCas9 nuclease (available from Integrated DNA Technologies [IDT]) encompassing a single point mutation (p.R691A) was demonstrated to significantly reduce off-target editing to ~1% across multiple off-target loci, which showed up to 27% editing with wild-type Cas9 [ ]. This variant has subsequently been adopted in the field [ , – ]. Targeting the COL17A1 gene in primary JEB keratinocytes, Petković and colleagues demonstrated that off-target cleavage facilitated by wild-type Cas9 (of up to 32% at one site) could be ablated when using the p.R691A hfCas9 variant [ ]. However, off-target editing, albeit at low levels, has still been reported using this variant [ ], and thus, it remains to be seen whether this approach can confer the same level of safety as the dual-Cas9n approach. Newer gene editing technologies such as BE and PE also offer distinct safety advantages over wild-type Cas9 nuclease. For example, while base editors can generate DSBs, they typically occur at very low levels [ ]. However, it is worth mentioning that off-target deamination is still reported using BE, in some cases at high frequencies [ , , , ]. The PE strategy confers a high level of on-target specificity and safety, with therapeutic rates of targeted gene correction demonstrated for EB [ , , ]. With new iterations consistently being generated, PE holds great promise for highly efficacious gene editing in the future.

## Alternative CRISPR Delivery Strategies Under Investigation for EB
To date, gene editing therapies for EB have primarily been evaluated in an ex vivo setting, typically using electroporation to deliver the editing reagents into EB-derived skin cells [ ]. The ease of accessibility of human skin makes this a feasible approach since skin cells can be easily obtained through patient skin biopsies and expanded in the laboratory. Following gene editing, the cells can be screened for potential genotoxicity, as well as stem cell targeting, before being grafted back onto patient wounds using established skin engineering methods, as demonstrated by the successful gene replacement trials for JEB [ , , ]. However, the ex vivo strategy can pose a significant interventional burden for patients. For example, the need to harvest punch biopsies from EB patients can be challenging if large areas of the body are unavailable due to widespread ulceration. In addition, in the case of severely affected patients, extensive hospitalization time and surgery may be required to graft large areas of the body. This was demonstrated in the groundbreaking gene replacement trial for LAMB3-deficient JEB whereby gene-modified engineered SEs retrovirally transduced with wild-type LAMB3 were grafted onto approximately 80% of a young boy’s total body surface area [ ]. Although the treatment was lifesaving, the surgical procedure was highly complex and high risk given the boy’s critical condition, and he was continuously sedated for 12 days post-operatively to ensure immobilization of the grafts [ ].
To bypass the limitations of ex vivo approaches, several groups have investigated alternative delivery strategies to electroporation that have the potential to be repurposed for future in vivo applications. Using a vector-based approach, García and colleagues evaluated the in vivo delivery capacity of helper-dependent adenoviral (HDAd) vectors carrying Cas9 nuclease and previously published sgRNAs [ ] targeting excision of COL7A1 exon 80 [ ]. Fibrin-embedded HDAd vectors were delivered into excisional wounds of RDEB skin grafts within a humanized mouse model. The treated wounds showed continuous C7 deposition at the basement membrane for up to 8 weeks following treatment, with assessment of skin fragility using a suction stress test [ ] of the treated skin demonstrating similar resistance to control grafts composed of healthy skin cells. After a second treatment application, antibodies against the HDAd vector were detected. Although the reported antibody levels generated during the first treatment were insufficient to reduce vector infectivity during a second treatment, preexisting immunity against HDAd will need to be considered for future applications. Additionally, the capacity of HDAd vectors to facilitate high-level and long-term transgene expression represents a potential concern that could increase the risk of off-target toxicity. Validation of this delivery strategy in RDEB keratinocytes using CAST-Seq revealed a rare on-target translocation with an intronic sequence of the MAMLD1 gene. However, it is important to highlight that this was only evident in four of 150,000 alleles in the cells derived from one donor [ ].
An alternative delivery strategy evaluated for EB is cationic branched polymers, which carry low immunogenicity and high biodegradability [ ]. They have been shown to condense and deliver plasmid DNA containing full-length COL7A1 into the skin of COL7A1−/− mice [ , ]. Later, this strategy was repurposed to deliver plasmids encoding the CRISPR machinery into primary RDEB skin cells in vitro to excise exon 80 of COL7A1, achieving > 40% targeted efficiencies [ , ].
In vivo delivery of the CRISPR reagents as mRNA or RNPs represents a potentially more favorable format as they are associated with more transient Cas9 expression, which can reduce off-target effects [ ]. In one study, Wu and colleagues intradermally injected Cas9 nuclease-RNPs into the tail skin of COL7A1−/− RDEB mice, followed by electroporation of the treated areas using a square wave pulse generator to generate controlled electrical pulses to the skin and increase membrane permeability. However, modest rates (~2%) of targeted editing were achieved [ ]. Moreover, this system is relatively complex and potentially cytotoxic, and would be limited to treating localized skin areas.
Nanoinjection represents a further potential method for the delivery of CRISPR payloads directly to the skin. In a proof-of-principle study, one group used an array of biodegradable nanoneedles to deliver fluorescent dyes into the skin of mice, achieving highly efficient cytoplasmic delivery [ ]. In a recent study for EB, this technology was used to facilitate cytoplasmic delivery of an ABE in vitro [ ]. Here, RDEB fibroblasts carrying a heterozygous COL7A1 mutation (c.5047C>T) were cultured on porous silicon nanoneedles coated with mRNA encoding the ABE8e system and an sgRNA, facilitating cytoplasmic delivery and highly efficient (> 90%) single nucleotide correction. Notably, RDEB fibroblasts cultured on the nanoneedles maintained normal morphology and cell viability. While a bystander mutation was generated at a high frequency (71.5% of transcripts), the nucleotide change was silent and did not result in an amino acid substitution [ ].

## Future Perspectives
A shared limitation of all aforementioned CRISPR tools is that they are restricted to generating edits ranging from a single nucleotide up to ~50 bp. With these tools, EB patients would require the development of potentially time-consuming and costly personalized therapies tailored to individual mutations. To bypass this requirement, new tools capable of integrating large DNA sequences including entire genes have recently been developed [ – ]. Two groups combined the programmability of PE with serine integrases to enable targeted gene integration [ , ]. For example, Yarnall and colleagues developed programmable addition via site-specific targeting elements (PASTE), which utilizes a prime editor to insert an attB attachment site, allowing integration of entire genes containing the related attP attachment site via a fused serine recombinase [ ]. A similar strategy—prime-assisted site-specific integrase gene editing (PASSIGE)—was independently reported by a separate group, but unlike PASTE, the prime editor and serine integrase are not used as a fusion protein [ , ]. While these tools enabled integration of cargo of up to 36 kb at efficiencies of 50–60% in human cell lines, modest integration rates of 4–5% were initially reported in primary human cells [ ]. Improvements in PASSIGE were later reported by Pandey and colleagues, who used phage-assisted continuous evolution (PACE) to evolve engineered Bxb1 recombinase variants (eeBxb1 and evoBxb1), resulting in the development of ePASSIGE [ ]. Notably, ePASSIGE outperformed PASTE and enabled a 3.2-fold improvement compared to wild-type Bxb1 integrase, allowing integration efficiencies of up to 30% in primary human fibroblasts [ ].
A limitation of PASTE/PASSIGE is the requirement for multiple enzymatic steps to occur in concert and the need to resolve complex DNA intermediates, which together can result in incomplete insertions and low level INDEL formation [ , , ]. Recently, CRISPR-derived transposases (CASTs) were reported as an alternative DSB-free method for integrating large DNA sequences [ , ]. These natural bacterial systems utilize a guide crRNA to direct site-specific insertions of large (> 1-kb) transposons facilitated by Tn7-like transposases [ , ]. Attempts to integrate large DNA sequences using CASTs initially suffered from low efficiencies (< 1%) [ ]. However, the same group later used PACE to evolve improved transposase variants (‘evoCAST’), enabling integration efficiencies of large cargo (> 1 kb) of up to 28% in HEK293T cells; although with concomitant low level off-target integration [ ]. Notably, in comparison to PASTE/PASSIGE, CASTs produce below background levels of INDELs and bypass the need for complex pegRNA optimization. However, the efficacy of this system in primary human cells is yet to be determined. Collectively, DSB-free methods such as PASSIGE/PASTE and CASTs overcome the ‘cut-and-fix’ strategy of nuclease-based gene editors, reducing the risk of contaminating on- and off-target editing outcomes. Additionally, their ability to insert entire genes would enable a unified approach for all patients with certain EB subtypes (for example, all RDEB patients), simplifying the development process and potentially enabling a faster route into the clinic. Unlike HDR-based approaches, which are limited to dividing cells, these strategies do not rely on endogenous cellular repair pathways and therefore can be used in non-dividing cells. However, integration rates in primary mammalian cells still require further optimization before clinical translation.

## Conclusions
The precise and safe correction of any pathogenic mutation facilitated by gene editing remains a highly desirable goal of EB therapeutics. To date, ex vivo gene editing based on the transplantation of genetically modified skin cells onto patient wounds has been pursued as a potential therapeutic strategy. The highly successful case of the young boy who had 80% of his total body surface area grafted with genetically modified epidermal sheets provided proof-of-principle for such approaches [ ]. Significant improvements in the rates of ex vivo CRISPR/Cas9-mediated targeted gene editing have recently been achieved for EB; however, inconsistent efficacy between loci and unintended on- and off-target editing represent safety concerns. The use of alternative CRISPR/Cas9 tools have been used to partially address these concerns. For example, dual-Cas9n strategies can nearly completely ablate off-target INDEL formation [ ], while the newer PE and BE technologies, which do not rely on DSBs, can also enhance safety as well as improve intended editing purity [ ].
Despite the significant progress achieved in CRISPR-based gene editing for EB, ex vivo approaches are invasive and carry a high interventional burden as they require extensive surgeries and time frames to harvest skin cells and for skin grafts to successfully integrate. Thus, the in vivo delivery of gene editors is the ultimate goal for developing minimally invasive and simple to administer therapies. The choice of a suitable vehicle capable of facilitating delivery in the skin, as well as systemically, to address internally affected tissues, is a crucial next step in this process. Overall, remarkable engineering improvements in the CRISPR/Cas9 system have brought this technology to the forefront of EB gene therapy, and hold tremendous promise for future gene editing efforts.
