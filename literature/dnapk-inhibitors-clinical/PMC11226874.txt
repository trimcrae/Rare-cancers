School of Biological Sciences, Nanyang Technological University Singapore, Singapore, Singapore Such a therapeutic strategy adopts the “one size fits all” approach without considering the biological variations among individuals and only benefits a subset of patients. Despite this first-line multimodal treatment, recurrence is almost inevitable, after which treatment options are limited. The prognosis is poor, with a median overall survival of 15 months upon diagnosis and 2–9 months for recurrent GBM. These clinical challenges highlight the need for more precise treatment of GBM.
Large-scale genomic studies on histologically identical patient specimens have identified signatures corresponding to 3 molecular subtypes, namely, proneural (PN), classical (CL), and mesenchymal (ME), that correlate with decreasing median survival. , PN and ME subtypes differ in prevalence, prognosis, and response to treatments. A more favorable prognosis is associated with the PN subtype, while the ME subtype constitutes 30%–49% of all isocitrate dehydrogenase wild type (IDH-wild type) GBM. The latter is also the dominant subtype in recurrent GBM. , The acquisition of ME signatures confers a marked increase in tumor aggressiveness and resistance to TMZ, which are not satisfactorily explained by their O-6-methylguanine-DNA methyltransferase (MGMT) status. Evidently, there is an unmet clinical need to develop more effective treatments against the ME subtype. One possible approach is to resensitize the ME subtype to TMZ by attenuating the intrinsic resistance mechanism.
The cellular kinome profile dictates many distinct features among GBM subtypes and their responses to the tumor microenvironment. Many kinase inhibitors have been approved by the US FDA, with more than 200 inhibitors in the clinical pipeline. Considering their well-characterized pharmacokinetics, pharmacodynamics, and safety profiles, these inhibitors constitute a huge drug depository that can be readily repurposed for GBM treatment, dramatically reducing the time, cost, and manpower needed for drug discovery. Recent findings have demonstrated the feasibility of targeted intervention by exploiting the dependency of the ME subtype on specific kinases. , Nonetheless, it is increasingly clear that GBM, especially the ME subtype, is not a single-pathway-driven malignancy that would easily succumb to targeted monotherapies. Upon exposure to stressors or insults, the subtype undergoes extensive rewiring of cellular signaling to adapt and resist the challenge, quickly developing escape routes to circumvent simplistic monotherapy and confer a survival advantage. Hence, understanding the adaptive response of the ME subtype, in addition to its chemoresistance mechanism, is of paramount importance to formulate a multitargeted strategy that adequately disrupts key networks to improve the clinical outcome.
Herein, we employed integrated analysis to reveal the differential kinase activities between patient-derived PN and ME glioma-propagating cells (GPCs). We further exploited small-molecule kinase inhibitors to abolish the adaptive mechanism in ME GPCs and regain chemosensitivity to TMZ, underscoring the clinical feasibility of subtype-specific therapy in primary and recurrent GBM.

## Materials and Methods

## Cell Culture and Transcriptional Subtype Classification
The phenotypes, transcriptomes, and karyotypes of PN (GLIO-0084, GLIO-0081, GLIO-0091, GLIO-0122) and ME (GLIO-0062, GLIO-0082, GLIO-0087, GLIO-0094, and GLIO-0111) GPCs were similar to their primary tumors, following previously published characterization criteria. All GPCs were cultured as neurosphere suspensions in a serum-free growth medium supplemented with growth factors. The molecular profiles of GPCs were interrogated with single-sample Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (ssGSEA) using glioma-intrinsic signatures and “ssgsea.GBM.classification” R package (Supplementary ). 
The p38MAPKresistant GLIO-0082 line, GLIO-0082-R, was developed by subjecting the parental cells to repeated cycles of increasing concentration of TMZ and ralimetinib. Initially, GLIO-0082 parental cells were treated with a combination of 5 μM TMZ and 0.5 μM ralimetinib for a period of 5 days. This was followed by a 2-day recovery phase in a drug-free medium. With each subsequent treatment cycle, the concentrations of both drugs were doubled. Eventually, the resulting resistant cell line was consistently cultured in a medium containing 200 μM TMZ and 5 μM ralimetinib, ensuring stability across for at least 3 generations.

## Kinome Screens and Drug Synergism
GPC cell lysates were used for protein tyrosine kinase (PTK) and Ser/Thr Kinase (STK) PamChip® kinome arrays. Differential kinomic activities between PN and ME GPCs were analyzed with an in-house computational pipeline. 
The kinase inhibitors, their molecular targets, and their concentrations are described in . Cell viability was assessed by alamarBlue staining (Invitrogen, CA, USA). Comparing the cytotoxicity of ME to PN GPCs, inhibitors that resulted in a relative cytotoxicity of ≥1 when used as a monotherapy and in combination with TMZ were shortlisted for further analysis.
Dose-response curves of the shortlisted inhibitors, TMZ, and combined therapy were assessed 5 days posttreatment. Drug–drug interactions were examined using the Chou–Talalay method, where a combination index (CI) < 1, CI = 1, or CI > 1 represents synergism, additive effect, and antagonism, respectively. CI and fraction affected (Fa) were computed using CompuSyn software (ComboSyn Inc., NJ, USA).

## qPCR and Immunoblotting
RNA extraction, qPCR, and immunoblotting were performed as previously described. The primer sequences for qPCR are shown in . Antibodies used are shown in .

## Cell Surface Expression of ATP-Binding Cassette Transporters and Drug Efflux Capacity Assay
Surface expression of ATP-binding cassette (ABC) transporters was quantified using flow cytometry and the antibodies are shown in . Treated cells were labeled with the antibodies and analyzed with AccuriTM C6 Cytometer. The percentage of ABC transporter-expressing cells was determined using non-labeled cells as the control. Drug efflux capacity assay was performed on GPCs using an EFLUXX-ID® Green Multidrug Resistance Assay kit (Enzo Life Sciences Inc. NY, USA). Drug retention score was calculated based on the mean fluorescence intensity of live cells.

## P38MAPK Signature Genes and Patient Stratification
The p38MAPK signature has 147 experimentally proven genes from Ingenuity Pathway Analysis (Qiagen, Germany). A pairwise Pearson correlation using the TCGA-GBM RNAseq dataset (n = 169) on the normalized counts of the 147 genes, resulted in positively and negatively correlated gene clusters, termed p38MAPK-Hi and p38MAPK-Lo gene signatures, respectively ( ). The predictive value of the p38MAPK-Hi and p38MAPK-Lo gene signatures was validated on 3 glioma/GBM patient cohorts, TCGA-GBM RNAseq (n = 169), TCGA-GBM Affymetrix HT_HG-U133A microarray (n = 558) and Gravendeel (n = 276; GSE16011) datasets, using CMScaller. Prognostic value of subtyping based on p38MAPK status was also evaluated using the Kaplan–Meier method, and the comparison was made with the log-rank test.

## Intracellular TMZ Accumulation
Intracellular TMZ levels were quantified using high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) according to protocols from previous studies with modifications. GPCs were treated with p38MAPK inhibitors for 24 h, followed by 500 µM TMZ. The cells were collected and lysed at 0, 5, 15, 30, 60, 120, and 180 min after TMZ exposure using modified RIPA lysis buffer (pH 3) containing 100 µM dexamethasone as the internal standard. Cell lysate was collected, and 10 µL was aliquoted for the Bradford protein assay. Four volumes of ethyl acetate were added to the remaining cell lysate followed by vortexing for 5 min on ice and centrifuged at 10,000 × g for 5 min. The upper organic layer was collected and evaporated using a stream of nitrogen gas. The residue was reconstituted in 100 µL of ammonium acetate (20 mM, pH 3):acetonitrile (1:1) and analyzed with HPLC. The HPLC details are available in .

## Alkaline Single-Cell Gel Electrophoresis (SCGE)
SCGE was performed according to a published protocol. Briefly, clean microscope slides were coated with 1% (w/v) normal melting point agarose and allowed to solidify. A total of 20 µL of GPCs (1 × 105 cells) was resuspended in 0.8% (w/v) low melting point agarose and applied onto the coated slides. The cell suspension was spread with a coverslip to form a thin layer. When the gel hardened, the slides were immersed in ice-cold alkaline lysis buffer (2.5 M NaCl, 1 mM EDTA, 10 mM Tris, 1% Triton, pH 10) and maintained on ice for 1 h for cell lysis. After lysis, the slides were soaked in denaturing buffer (300 mM NaOH, 1 mM EDTA) to unwind DNA, followed by electrophoresis in the same buffer at 25 V (1 V/cm) for 20 min. The slides were then stained with DAPI and visualized with a Carl Zeiss Axio Observer 7 microscope (Carl Zeiss AG, Germany). Image analysis of the damaged DNA was performed using OpenComet. 

## Apoptosis Assay
GPC cultures were dissociated with AccutaseTM Cell Detachment Solution (BD Biosciences, NJ, USA), while tumor xenografts were dissociated into single-cell suspensions using Type 3 collagenase (1 mg/mL) and DNAseI (10 U/mL) for 30 min at 37 ° C. The cell suspensions were stained with propidium iodide (Sigma–Aldrich) and Annexin V-FITC (Miltenyi Biotec, Germany) and detected by flow cytometry.

## Intracellular pH Assay and Calcium Imaging
A fluorescent-based pH indicator, BCECF-AM, was used to examine intracellular pH of GPCs. Intracellular calcium was determined using a Fluo-4 DirectTM Calcium Assay kit (Invitrogen, CA, USA). Microscopic images were captured with a JuLITM Stage fluorescence microscope (NanoEnTek Inc., South Korea).

## Intracranial Patient-derived Xenograft (PDX) Mouse Model
Animal experimentation was performed according to protocols approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of the Nanyang Technological University, Singapore. Intracranial PDXs were created as previously described, , , implanting 500,000 patient-derived GPCs into the right cerebral cortex of NOD-SCID gamma mice (NSG, Jackson Laboratories). The coordinates used were anteroposterior = +1 mm; mediolateral = +2 mm; dorsoventral = −2.5 mm. In the initial model, vehicle (n = 6), TMZ (n = 6) or ralimetinib (n = 6), or the combination (T+R; n = 6) were administered orally by gavage 3 weeks post-implantation of GLIO-0082 parental line. Ralimetinib was administered twice a day at 100 mg/kg for 10 days while TMZ was administered once daily at 20 mg/kg for 5 days. For the recurrent model, vehicle (n = 8), T+R (n = 10), or T+R with binimetinib (T+R+B; n = 7) were administered by oral gavage 3 weeks post-implantation of p38MAPKresistant GLIO-0082 line, GLIO-0082-R. Binimetinib was administered once daily at 50 mg/kg for 5 days. Mice were euthanized by transcardiac perfusion with 4% paraformaldehyde upon presentation of neurological deficits with ataxia, cachexia, lethargy, or seizure. Kaplan–Meier survival curves were plotted. Animal survival difference was evaluated using the log-rank test in Graph Prism 5 software (GraphPad Software, Inc.).

## Immunohistochemistry
Tissue sections of 5 μm thickness were stained with the antibodies stated in Supplementary . For quantitative analysis, the percentage of stained tumor cells and intensity of staining were evaluated under high-power fields on tissue sections using optical microscopy. H-scores were derived from both the staining intensity (scale of 0–3) and the percentage of positive cells (0%–100%), generated a score ranging from 0 to 3. Briefly, the percentage of weakly stained cells was multiplied by one plus moderately stained cells multiplied by 2 plus strongly stained cells multiplied by 3. At least 5 random fields were counted, and scoring was performed blinded to clinical data.

## Statistical Analysis
Unless mentioned otherwise, data are represented as the mean ± SD from at least 3 independent replicates per cell line. One-way ANOVA was used to compare means across different treatments, followed by post hoc Tukey’s test. Dependent variables with repeated measures were analyzed using mixed-model ANOVA. A P value <.05 was considered statistically significant.

## Results

## Integrative Transcriptomics and Functional Analysis Identified p38MAPK as an ME-Specific Target
Among the 3 IDH-wild type GBM molecular subtypes, PN and ME are the most stable subtypes across different studies, with the former being associated with a more favorable outcome and the latter in patients with the worst prognosis. , , Using patient-derived GPCs, we demonstrated that TMZ at up to 500 μM marginally impaired the viability of ME GPCs by approximately 20%, in contrast to a 40% reduction in PN GPCs ( ). GPCs with unmethylated MGMT promoter status are indicated ( ). Hence, strategies that restore susceptibility to TMZ in the ME subtype may overcome its poor prognosis. Comparative analysis of the phosphorylation dynamics between the PN and ME subtypes revealed substantial activation of all 3 families of MAPK in ME GPCs ( ). Kinase inhibitor screens also showed that inhibitors targeting MAPK signaling exhibited superior cytotoxicity in ME compared to PN GPCs in combination with TMZ. Inhibiting several molecular targets under the umbrella of MAPK signaling, such as p38MAPK and MEK/ERK, as well as their downstream signal transducer ribosomal S6 kinase, restored the chemosensitivity of ME GPCs to TMZ ( , ). To substantiate the subtype-specific kinases, we leveraged 3 public datasets, namely, the Gravendeel clinical database, and 2 independent TCGA-GBM cohorts, to identify enriched protein kinases based on the differentially upregulated genes of the ME to PN subtypes. Integrating experimentally derived differential kinases and in silico analysis of patient cohorts revealed 10 common kinases associated with the ME subtype and most of which are functionally linked to p38MAPK signaling ( ).
 To understand if the activity of p38MAPK offers any prognostic value to GBM patients, we constructed a correlation matrix using the expression profile of p38MAPK target genes from the TCGA-GBM RNAseq cohort to establish a set of positively co-expressing p38MAPK genes, which we termed the p38-Hi gene signature, and a set of inversely correlated genes as the p38-Lo gene signature ( ). Stratification of the 3 glioma/GBM cohorts based on their p38MAPK status using the gene signatures showed an over-representation of p38-Hi in ME but not CL and PN subtypes ( ). Furthermore, p38-Hi was also associated with a worse prognosis in the Gravendeel and TCGA-GBM (microarray) cohorts ( ). Increased phosphorylation of p38MAPK was also observed in ME compared to PN GPCs, and the specific inhibitors SB202190 and SD169 markedly diminished p38MAPK activation ( , , ). Importantly, the inhibitors of p38MAPK, but not other MAPK families, synergized favorably with TMZ to enhance cytotoxicity in ME GPCs ( , ). Taken together, our data highlight that p38MAPK signaling is linked to poor outcome and aggressiveness of the ME subtype. Targeting the pathway presents an exciting approach to curb TMZ resistance in a subtype-specific manner.

## p38MAPK Diminishes TMZ Retention in GPCs via Transcriptional Modulation of ATP-binding Cassette Transporters
Overexpression of ABC transporters, which often accounts for the chemoresistance phenotype in cancers, is linked to p38MAPK overactivity. , However, p38MAPK-dependent activation of ABC transporters is not well characterized in GBM. Hence, we investigated the mRNA and surface expression of 10 major ABC transporters (ABCB1, ABCB11, ABCC1-ABCC6, ABCG1 & ABCG2) to explore their roles in TMZ challenge. ME GPCs had higher basal expression of ABCC1, ABCC2, and ABCC3 than PN GPCs, but TMZ markedly upregulated ABC transporters in PN GPCs but not in ME GPCs ( , ). In contrast, p38MAPK inhibitors SB202190 and SD169 downregulated ABC transporters in ME but not PN GPCs ( , Supplementary Figure 2B). Our data suggest that ME and PN GPCs exhibit different drug response mechanisms. The former strongly depends on p38MAPK signaling to maintain an enhanced level of efflux activity from ABCC1-3 to respond rapidly to pharmacological assaults, while the latter lagged and increased ABC transporters only upon exposure to a certain drug.
 Cotreatment with p38MAPK inhibitors and TMZ effectively suppressed the expression of ABCC1-3 and other ABC transporters in a dose-dependent manner, except for ABCC4 and ABCG2, which showed an upregulation trend ( , ). The data revealed a dependency of both subtypes on p38MAPK-mediated efflux activity in response to TMZ and a compensatory efflux mechanism mediated by ABCC4 and ABCG2 when other transporters were shut down. Functionally, p38MAPK inhibitors disrupted drug efflux activity and selectively enhanced dye retention in ME GPCs ( ). Compared to verapamil and novobiocin, which are ABCB1 and ABCG2 inhibitors, specific inhibitors of ABCC transporters, that is, MK-571 and probenecid, retained more fluorescent dye in ME GPCs. When ABCC1-3 were individually knocked down in ME GPCs via RNA interference, the cells showed increased sensitivity to TMZ-sensitive, and enhanced cell death after TMZ exposure ( ). In PN GPCs, however, the knockdown of ABCC1, ABCC2, and ABCC3 did not enhance TMZ sensitivity due to their inherently low basal expression. The siRNAs were designed to target the 3ʹ UTR of endogenous ABCC1, ABCC2, and ABCC3 mRNA transcripts. Their subsequent rescue of expression was achieved by co-transfecting cells with expression plasmids containing only the protein-coding sequences of the respective ABCC efflux pumps. As expected, rescue of ABCC1, ABCC2, and ABCC3 protein expression mitigated the impaired viability caused by the knockdown and TMZ exposure in ME GPCs, allowing the cells to regain resistance phenotype. Likewise, overexpression or rescue of ABCC1, ABCC2, and ABCC3 protein expression also conferred a resistance phenotype in PN GPCs, as exemplified by the enhanced viability post-TMZ treatment ( ). Thus, higher basal ABCC1, ABCC2, and ABCC3 expression mediated by p38MAPK signaling are crucial players in the chemoresistance properties of ME GPCs.
Targeting p38MAPK decreased the expression of multiple ABC transporters. We also demonstrated that multitarget inhibition with p38MAPK inhibitors followed by TMZ could enhance TMZ retention in GPCs. In ME GPCs, pretreatment with SB202190 and SD169 increased TMZ retention by 42.1% and 34.9%, respectively, bringing intracellular TMZ more comparable to that in PN GPCs ( , ). In summary, p38MAPK-dependent defense mechanisms against TMZ involve the transcriptional regulation of multiple ABC transporters. An increased intracellular TMZ concentration exposed ME GPCs to higher chemotoxicity, which may contribute to drug synergism and chemoresistance.

## p38MAPK Activates the DNA Repair Mechanism in ME GPCs
Our comparative transcriptomics between SB202190- and vehicle-treated ME GPCs supported the suppressive effect of SB202190 on transmembrane transport ( ; ), thus confirming the regulatory role of p38MAPK signaling on drug efflux pumps. Furthermore, SB202190 also attenuated the DNA repair mechanism in ME GPCs by downregulating key DNA repair genes for nonhomologous DNA end joining ( , ). Based on comet assays, there were more DNA breakages in ME GPCs treated with TMZ+p38MAPK inhibitor cotreatment than TMZ alone, whereas in PN GPCs, the cotreatment did not induce added DNA damage ( , ). Accumulation of DNA breaks enhanced replication errors in ME GPCs, resulting in increased apoptotic cell death ( ).
 To understand the relationship between p38MAPK signaling and DNA repair, we overexpressed a constitutively active form of MKK3 (caMKK3), an upstream regulator of p38MAPK kinase, in ME GPCs to enhance p38MAPK phosphorylation ( ). caMKK3 ablated the suppressive effect of p38MAPK inhibitor on DNA repair ( , ). Targeting DNA repair signaling with AZD7648, a DNA-PK inhibitor, exacerbated DNA damage in control ME GPCs but was less effective in those with caMKK3 ( , ). Hence, DNA repair capacity is affected by the p38MAPK cascade. Together, ME GPCs retain higher basal activity of p38MAPK to enable heightened drug efflux and DNA repair activities, both of which contribute to the intrinsic chemoresistance of ME subtypes. Repurposing p38MAPK inhibitors as an adjuvant treatment can resensitize ME GPCs to TMZ.

## ME GPCs Trigger pH- and Calcium-Mediated MEK/ERK Adaptive Mechanisms to Circumvent p38MAPK Inhibition
Almost all GBM will recur, and a positive response to subsequent treatment is very minimal. Likewise, we observed that ME GPCs with stable knockdown of p38MAPK lost their sensitivity to TMZ after only 2 passages ( , ). This observation resembles the chemoresistance of recurrent GBM and underlines a hitherto unknown adaptive mechanism that circumvents the suppression of p38MAPK signaling. To identify the underlying adaptive mechanism, we revisited the RNA-seq data and found that acid secretory activity and calcium signaling were markedly perturbed by p38MAPK inhibition ( ). Interestingly, the 2 molecular activities have been reported to be coregulated in GBM. , Furthermore, many solute carrier transport proteins were found to be differentially expressed, 7 of which were directly linked to transmembrane transportation of hydrogen ions ( ). Hence, we asked if p38MAPK inhibition affected intracellular pH regulation, which subsequently interfered with the pH-dependent conversion of TMZ into 3-methyl-(triazen-1-yl)imidazole-4-carboximide (MTIC). Unlike PN GPCs, the intracellular pH of ME GPCs was more acidic. Inhibition of p38MAPK further lowered the intracellular pH from pH 7.55 to pH 7.33 (SB202190) and pH 7.31 (SD169) ( ). Since the cellular processes operate within a narrow physiological pH range, the observed pH drop caused by p38MAPK inhibitors could adversely affect the hydrolysis of TMZ to MTIC.
 Intracellular pH reduction caused by p38MAPK inhibition or deliberate calibration with a protonophore, nigericin, enhanced cytosolic calcium retention in ME GPCs by 5%–10% ( , ). In contrast, the proapoptotic effect of the p38MAPK inhibitor and TMZ cotreatment was exacerbated in ME GPCs using BAPTA-AM, which is a calcium chelator that clears cytosolic calcium ions ( , Supplementary Figure ). Notably, selected calmodulin-dependent protein kinases, such as CaMK2A, were highly active in ME GPCs ( ). The importance of calcium signaling in the oncogenic adaptive mechanism has been linked to the MEK/ERK pathway, which is also an ME-specific kinase target based on our kinomic screens ( ). Hence, we examined MEK/ERK phosphorylation in resistant ME GPCs to establish the regulatory relationship between intracellular calcium and MEK/ERK signaling. Indeed, MEK/ERK phosphorylation was significantly increased in response to p38MAPK inhibitors + TMZ cotreatment but was reversed with BAPTA-AM ( ). Dual inhibition of p38MAPK and MEK/ERK performed better than the p38MAPK inhibitor alone in the potentiation of TMZ cytotoxicity in ME GPCs but not PN GPCs ( , ). Likewise, binimetinib, an orally FDA-approved MEK1/2 inhibitor, also restored TMZ sensitivity in ME GPCs with stable knockdown of p38MAPK ( ). Collectively, in resistant ME GPCs, pH homeostasis is influenced by p38MAPK. Acidosis induced by p38MAPK inhibition increases cytosolic calcium, which subsequently activates MEK/ERK signaling as an adaptive response to prolong survival. Concomitant attenuation of the p38MAPK and MEK/ERK signaling pathways effectively combats TMZ chemoresistance in ME GPCs.

## Dual p38MAPK and MEK Inhibition Sustains TMZ Efficacy in Intracranial ME Tumor Xenografts
The intracranial PDX model is essential for assessing p38MAPK and MEK inhibitors in GBM, especially considering the uncertain blood-brain-barrier penetrance of SB202190 and SD169. We identified ralimetinib, a brain-penetrating p38MAPK inhibitor tested in a phase 1 trial as an adjuvant regime to radiotherapy with concurrent TMZ in newly diagnosed GBM patients. , SynergySeq analysis predicts that p38MAPK inhibitors like ralimetinib and talmapimod show low transcriptomic concordance with TMZ+ radiotherapy and high discordance with the mesenchymal signature, indicating potential synergy with the standard care and reversal of the ME disease profile ( ). The potent subtype-specific synergism of ralimetinib with TMZ was confirmed using viability assays, enhancing cytotoxicity in ME but not PN GPCs ( ). Both ralimetinib and other p38MAPK inhibitors, when combined with TMZ, activated calcium-mediated MEK/ERK signaling, which was reversible by BAPTA-AM ( ). Oral ralimetinib dosage in mice was titrated to achieve substantial inhibition on the phosphorylation of Hsp27, a downstream protein target of p38MAPK in brain tissues, evident at 100 mg/kg of ralimetinib, clearly showing a remarkable blood-brain-barrier penetrance ( ). A higher dosage of 200 mg/kg of ralimetinib did not yield a stronger inhibitory effect ( ).
Orthotopic PDX mouse models bearing GLIO-0082 (ME GPC) brain tumors were treated with TMZ (once daily; 20 mg/kg), ralimetinib (twice daily; 100 mg/kg), or their combination (T+R) via oral gavage ( ). The combined therapy (T+R) significantly prolonged the median survival (72.5 days), compared to vehicle (65 days), or monotherapy of TMZ (63 days), or ralimetinib (62.5 days) ( ). Both monotherapies have no impact on the overall survival compared to vehicles. PDX tumors treated with T+R combined therapy showed sustained p38MAPK suppression and reduced proliferation as shown by the immunohistochemical assessments of Hsp27 phosphorylation and Ki-67 ( ). This highlights a link between p38MAPK signaling with aggressive tumor phenotype. These benefits were also observed in subcutaneous tumor xenograft models using SB202190, a structurally different p38MAPK inhibitor from ralimetinib ( ). In mice with GLIO-0087 (ME GPC) tumors, cotreatment with TMZ and SB202190 stunted tumor growth and increased apoptosis ( ). In contrast, GLIO-0091 (PN GPC) xenografts already sensitive to TMZ showed no additional benefits from the adjuvant therapies ( ). Together, inhibition of p38MAPK selectively weakens primary ME tumor xenografts to TMZ which translates to better prognosis.
 For adaptive chemoresistance in p38MAPKresistant and recurrent ME tumors, 2 different approaches were used. Firstly, we established a p38MAPKresistant GLIO-0082 cell line, GLIO-0082-R, by exposing the parental cells to multiple cycles of increasing concentrations of TMZ and ralimetinib ( ). The GLIO-0082-R GPCs, maintained in a medium containing 200 μM TMZ and 5 μM ralimetinib, were used in intracranial PDX tumor models. These models were then subjected to T+R combined therapy, with and without binimetinib ( , ). Unlike ralimetinib alone, which showed efficacy comparable to vehicle, the dual inhibition strategy targeting p38MAPK and MEK in combination with TMZ, improved the survival of mice with GLIO-0082-R brain tumors and reduced tumor proliferation ( ).
In our second approach, we developed a recurrent GBM model by reimplanting viable cells from subcutaneous GLIO-0087 xenografts into naïve mice. These tumors were previously treated with TMZ and p38MAPK inhibitor (TMZ+SB202190) or p38MAPK-MEK dual inhibitors (TMZ+SB202190+binimetinib) ( ). The tumor cells that had previously been exposed to the combined treatment of TMZ+SB202190 + binimetinib exhibited delayed recurrence and a slower growth rate compared to those treated with TMZ+SB202190 alone ( ). Notably, recurrent ME tumors previously treated with TMZ+SB202190 became refractory to the same regimen ( ), whereas TMZ+SB202190 + binimetinib continued to effectively inhibit the growth of recurrent tumors and induce apoptosis ( ). Prolonged treatment with the TMZ+SB202190+binimetinib regimen, administered 8 doses over 8 weeks, led to full remission in all ME xenografts, in contrast to the TMZ+SB202190 group, where only 50% achieved remission ( ).
Clearly, p38MAPK inhibition is critical to restore TMZ chemosensitivity in ME GPCs in vivo. However, this effect is quickly countered by the activation of MEK/ERK signaling, leading to adaptive chemoresistance. Our data derived from different GBM PDX tumor models underscores the effectiveness of dual p38MAPK-MEK inhibition in achieving long-lasting TMZ resensitization by simultaneously targeting intrinsic and adaptive resistance mechanisms.

## Discussion
Our results demonstrate marked chemoresistance in ME GBM compared to the PN subtype. By examining the kinomic landscapes of the 2 subtypes, we discovered that p38MAPK signaling is constitutively activated in the ME subtype to confer higher resting drug efflux activity and DNA repair capacity, leading to TMZ resistance. Importantly, we also identified pH-/calcium-dependent MEK/ERK activation as an adaptive chemoresistance mechanism to nullify the resensitization of TMZ mediated by p38MAPK inhibition ( ). Hence, the dual inhibition of p38MAPK-MEK is a promising strategy to revitalize TMZ sensitivity in the mesenchymal subtype in its primary and recurrent forms.
We dedicated our efforts to combat TMZ resistance in the ME subtype with adjuvant pharmacotherapy through repurposing existing small-molecule kinase inhibitors for a few reasons. First, GBM is clearly not a single-pathway-driven malignancy, as exemplified by the failures of most monotherapy-based GBM clinical trials even when patients have corresponding genomic mutations or abnormal protein activities. Second, despite the ongoing debates on the molecular subtyping of GBM, the ME subtype remains one of the most stable subtypes found across studies using different approaches (i.e. bulk RNAseq and single-cell transcriptomes). , , ME tumors with low transcriptional heterogeneity are associated with poorer prognosis exemplified by faster tumor recurrence, higher rate of treatment failure, and shorter overall survival when compared to other subtypes. , , , Third, tackling chemoresistance is a viable therapeutic modality considering the limited antineoplastic agents with decent blood–brain-barrier crossing capacity. The resensitization of TMZ in the ME subtype also addresses the adaptive resistance that is often seen in recurrent GBM. 
Integrative analysis of transcriptomic, functional kinomic, and clinical datasets collaboratively supports the repositioning of p38MAPK inhibitors to synergize with the cytotoxicity of TMZ in ME GPCs. The feasibility of an p38MAPK inhibitor (ralimetinib) alongside TMZ and radiotherapy showed an overall survival of 24.5 months and tolerable adverse effects. Overactive p38MAPK is associated with poorer prognosis in GBM patients, , which partly explained the enrichment of the ME subtype among patients with high p38MAPK activity. Our findings shed new light on the subtype-specific implications of p38MAPK in chemoresistance to TMZ. The inhibition of p38MAPK weakens the chemoresistance of the ME subtype in 2 steps: increases intracellular TMZ accumulation by reducing basal drug efflux capacity and exacerbates TMZ-induced genomic damage via impairment of the DNA repair mechanism.
A major knowledge gap is whether chemoresistance arises due to the selection and expansion of preexisting cancer cells called adaptive resistance or through new mutations resulting from chemotherapy, known as acquired resistance. Interestingly, the initial TMZ sensitization by p38MAPK inhibitors in ME GPCs was nullified by active pH-/calcium-sensitive MEK/ERK signaling, which was detected in the kinase inhibitor viability screen, suggesting a rapid trigger of an adaptive mechanism potentially through signaling network rewiring. Acidosis, calcium signaling, and the MEK/ERK pathway have been independently shown to strengthen GBM survival, whereas the regulation of their activities is closely intertwined. , Being the adaptive mechanisms that are typically quiescent without p38MAPK suppression, targeting the pH, calcium, or MEK/ERK signaling may be inadequate to combat the ME subtype. By inhibiting p38MAPK and ERK/MEK simultaneously, we achieved sustained TMZ sensitivity and suppressive effects on the growth of primary and recurrent ME tumor xenografts. Hence, therapeutic modalities targeting the 2 MAPK families could potentially be developed into a subtype-specific treatment for GBM of the ME subtype to target adaptive chemoresistance in recurrent tumors.
Given the complexity of chemoresistance mechanisms and plasticity of the ME subtype, efforts that aim to discover targeted monotherapy for GBM are less likely to achieve a long-lasting TMZ resensitization effect and in treating most patients. In this study, silencing of the p38MAPK cascade was counteracted by MEK activation in a growth factor-independent manner. We advocate the use of multitargeted interventions to prolong drug potency. One drawback of a multitargeted regimen is the increased side effects, which can be partly overcome by using drug combinations with excellent synergism to achieve a therapeutic concentration at a lower dosage. The effectiveness of dual p38MAPK and MEK inhibition in tumors with unmethylated MGMT promoters also warrants comprehensive assessment given their unique chemoresistance mechanism. Nonetheless, we envisage that our successful proof-of-concept study promises a more targeted and efficacious treatment strategy, and to spurs the development of more blood–brain permissive drugs or delivery strategies, offering hope for significantly improved clinical outcomes in patients with ME GBM. 

## Conclusions
Our study demonstrated that adjuvant therapy combining dual inhibition of p38MAPK and MEK inhibition not only prolongs TMZ sensitivity but also effectively counters adaptive chemoresistance in both primary and recurrent GBM tumor xenografts, particularly the ME subtype. This approach holds great promise as a precision therapeutic strategy, specifically targeting and overcoming the challenges posed by the ME subtype tumors.
