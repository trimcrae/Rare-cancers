## Main text
So-called inborn errors of metabolism (IOMs), such as phenylketonuria (PKU), have long been treated through dietary therapy consisting of dietary restrictions accompanied by supplementation with specific amino acids, carbohydrates, fatty acids, and/or vitamin cofactors. While dietary therapy has demonstrated benefits, including for PKU, its limitations have led to the development of alternative approaches, including gene therapy. However, marketing approval for gene therapy has been slow, at least relative to the expectations of patients and care providers, and the uptake of approved gene therapies has been disappointing in many cases. This shortfall represents a critical unmet need, especially since many IOMs, including PKU, are detected early in life by population-based newborn screening and require early treatment to prevent morbidity and mortality. Furthermore, with regard to IOM, the limitations of commonly proposed strategies, such as liver-targeted adeno-associated virus (AAV) vector-mediated gene therapy have presented hurdles to achieving the long-term treatment necessary for early-onset genetic disorders.
Examples of the limitations of AAV vectors for the treatment of IOM are well documented. We and others have developed AAV vectors for effective, in vivo, liver-targeted gene therapy in IOMs, including PKU, glycogen storage disease, propionic acidemia, and methylmalonic acidemia (MMA). , , However, the duration of efficacy has been limited, as episomal AAV vector genomes in the liver are lost due to hepatocyte division. , , This loss of efficacy presents a hurdle for gene therapy administered early in life. Tellingly, clinical trials of gene therapy for IOM have typically enrolled juveniles, adolescents, or adults, despite the need for earlier treatment during infancy.
Due to the lack of durable benefits from AAV vector-mediated gene therapy, an alternative approach of genome editing for IOM has been developed by multiple laboratories, consisting of a clustered regularly interspaced short palindromic repeats (CRISPR)-Cas9 nuclease that creates a double-stranded DNA break, accompanied by integration of a therapeutic transgene through homology-directed recombination (HDR). , , Alternative approaches that avoid the use of a nuclease have achieved lower-frequency HDR, which decreases efficacy. This strategy was previously developed for PKU by Richard et al. and further advanced by Martinez et al., as reported in the current issue of Molecular Therapy Nucleic Acids. As noted by the authors, alternative and potentially safer approaches that avoid the use of a nuclease, including base editing and prime editing, have limitations related to the large number of pathogenic variants in IOM that would need to be corrected by a variant-specific approach, as well as the challenges of in vivo implementation. 
Many IOMs could be treated by hepatic correction with genome editing, with an efficiency of 3%–10%. , , However, to date, the demonstrated efficiency of hepatic HDR-mediated genome editing has generally been <1% of alleles in the absence of an enhancing drug treatment or a selective advantage for the correction of hepatocytes. , The new study by Martinez et al. in this issue explores multiple methods for enhancing HDR to achieve efficacy by lowering plasma phenylalanine concentrations in the mouse model for PKU. 
In the current study, Martinez et al. adopt a two-vector AAV approach to deliver both an SpCas9 nuclease and a PAH expression cassette flanked by homologous sequences, along with an sgRNA to target integration to the murine Pah locus ( ). As in the previous study, which used a mutation-specific repair template, this approach yielded detectable, albeit sub-therapeutic, responses in vivo. Notably, inclusion of an non-homologous end joining (NHEJ) inhibitor, vanillin, and an MMEJ inhibitor, novobiocin, boosted HDR-mediated genomic integrations from 0.25% in dual-vector-treated animals to 7.44% in animals receiving vanillin, and 9.71% in those receiving both vanillin and novobiocin. By further administering sapropterin dihydrochloride, the pharmaceutical analog of the natural PAH cofactor, serum phenylalanine levels were reduced from >2000 μM in untreated animals to just 396 μM on average, with some animals achieving complete normalization of serum phenylalanine to below the therapeutic threshold.
 In addition to pharmacologic enhancement of HDR, Martinez et al. also explored a novel selective approach for corrected cells by incorporating a cytochrome P450-targeting short hairpin RNA (shRNA) into the repair template, such that corrected cells could be selected by resistance to acetaminophen-induced toxicity. However, inclusion of the shRNA cassette drastically impaired AAV genome replication, reducing the proportion of AAV containing full-length genomes from 47.2% in non-shRNA-containing vectors to only 7.8%. Unexpectedly, incorporation of the shRNA cassette also induced severe motor dysfunction in over half of the treated animals, but the exact cause of this toxicity remains unknown. Despite the low frequency of intact AAV vector genomes, mice that received the shRNA-containing repair template with Cas9 vector, vanillin, and acetaminophen selection achieved 9.55% and 3.45% genome insertion rates in male and female mice, respectively. Assuming the issues of vector purity and limiting toxicity could be addressed by future engineering of the shRNA cassette, the acetaminophen selection protocol could yield the highest liver transgene insertion rate of any technique so far.
Though Martinez et al. demonstrate significant normalization of phenylalanine levels in Pah null Dexon1 mice, they also clearly acknowledge that the treatment is not ready for human clinical trials due to the frequency of dominant-negative PAH variants that would necessitate a greater transgene insertion frequency to disrupt mutant PAH expression. Moreover, the early administration at 3–5 days of age provides an HDR advantage compared to administration at 12 days of age, which better approximates the stage of development for human infants (hair present, eyes open). One consideration not addressed in the study is the overall safety of nuclease-based editing combined with NHEJ and MMEJ inhibition by vanillin and novobiocin ( ). A recent study of a similar DNA-PK inhibitor, AZD7648, raised serious concerns regarding the use of NHEJ inhibitors in HDR-based Cas9 strategies, given the significant increase in megabase-scale deletions, chromosome arm loss, and chromosomal translocations. Currently, it remains unknown whether this is a limitation of all DNA-PK-targeting NHEJ inhibitors, such as vanillin and AZD7648, or whether a safe dose exists for genome-editing purposes.
In summary, the strategy of whole PAH transgene insertion at the PAH locus via HDR provides a framework for the treatment of all PKU patients, regardless of the underlying variant, a “universal” PKU gene therapy. By augmenting the effective rate of HDR with pharmacologic inhibitors of competing DNA repair pathways and cell-selective regimens, the authors achieve a best-in-class response for the treatment of PKU. However, several translational challenges, including a more comprehensive analysis of on- and off-target effects and further evaluation in a dominant-negative PKU model, are needed to demonstrate clinical viability. With this in mind, augmented HDR may become the ideal therapeutic, not just for PKU, but for IOMs as a whole.
