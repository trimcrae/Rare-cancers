## Abstract
Glioblastoma (GBM) remains one of the most therapy-resistant malignancies with frequent local failures despite aggressive surgery, chemotherapy, and ionizing radiation (IR). Small molecule inhibitors of DNA-dependent protein kinase (DNA-PKi) are potent radiosensitizers currently in clinical trials. Determining which patients may benefit from radiosensitization with DNA-PKi is critical to avoid unnecessary increased risk of normal tissue toxicity. In this study, we used GBM patient-derived xenografts (PDX) in orthotopic murine models to study the relationship between molecular features, pharmacokinetics, and the radiosensitizing potential of the DNA-PKi peposertib. We show that peposertib radiosensitizes established and PDX GBM lines in vitro at 300 nmol/L and above, with a significant increase in radiosensitization by maintaining post-IR exposure for >12 hours. Radiosensitization by peposertib is mediated by catalytic inhibition of DNA-PK, and knockdown of DNA-PK by short hairpin RNA (shRNA) largely abolished the radiosensitizing effect. Peposertib decreased auto-phosphorylation of DNA-PKcs after IR in a dose-dependent manner with a delay in resolution of γH2AX foci at 24 hours. The addition of peposertib to IR significantly increased survival in GBM120 orthotopic xenografts, but not in GBM10. There was no difference in plasma or average tumor concentrations of peposertib in the two cohorts. Although the mechanism underpinning this discordant effect in vitro versus in vivo is not clear, there was an association for greater sensitization in TP53 mutant lines. Transfection of a dominant-negative TP53 mutant in baseline TP53 wild-type GBM lines significantly delayed growth and decreased nonhomologous end joining efficiency (but not homologous recombination), after peposertib exposure.

## Introduction
Glioblastoma (GBM) remains an aggressive and therapy-resistant disease. Patients diagnosed with GBM have a poor prognosis, with a median survival of 14 to 18 months, despite an aggressive multimodal treatment approach of surgery, ionizing radiation (IR), and chemotherapy. Tumor progression frequently occurs within the radiation field, despite dose-escalated IR regimens with concurrent chemotherapy, highlighting the rationale to develop drugs to effectively sensitize GBM to radiation.
The efficacy of radiotherapy is related to the failure to completely repair radiation-induced DNA double-strand breaks (DSB). There are two main pathways for repairing DSB in cells: homologous recombination (HR) and nonhomologous end joining (NHEJ). HR is a high-fidelity repair pathway and is only active in the S and G2 phases of the cell cycle when a sister chromatid is available as a repair template. NHEJ is active throughout the cell cycle and in some circumstances remains the chief mechanism for DSB repair even when HR is active. DNA-PKcs is the key transducer kinase of the NHEJ pathway, and inhibition or loss of expression of DNA-PKcs can result in profound radiosensitivity.
Peposertib (M3814) is a highly selective and orally bioavailable small molecule inhibitor of DNA-PKcs (DNA-PKi) and is being studied in multiple clinical trials as a radiosensitizer in multiple solid malignancies (NCT—04172532, 04071236, 04750954, 03770689, 04068194, 04533750, and 02516813) and specifically in GBM (NCT04555577; refs. – ). Understanding the features that predict effective radiosensitization by peposertib will be important to develop patient selection criteria for further clinical development. In this study, we use clinically relevant GBM models to investigate some key molecular features associated with response.

## Materials and Methods

## Animal studies
Animal studies were approved by the Mayo Clinic Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC). Athymic nude mice (female, strain code 553, ages between 6 and 7 weeks) were used for orthotopic studies as detailed previously ( ). Time from inoculation to treatment start was 7, 11, and 26 days postinoculation for GBM10, 46, and 120 respectively, based on tumor kinetics from prior survival studies of untreated mice. The mice were treated as specified, monitored daily, and humanely euthanized at predetermined timepoints. IACUC endpoint guidelines for euthanasia included weight loss ≥20%, inability to reach food/water, immobility, hunched posture, lethargy, spasticity, seizures, circling, and paralysis.

## Cell culture
GBM patient-derived xenograft (PDX) cultures were generated, maintained, and authenticated as previously described ( , ). Briefly, short-term explants cultures (at passage <2–5) were grown in Stem Pro cell media [StemPro NSC SFM kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat. # A1050901); KnockOut DMEM/F-12 media, 500 mL; StemPro NSC SFM supplement, 10 mL; FGF supplement, 10 μg; EGF supplement, 10 μg; L-glutamine (Corning, Cat. # 25005CI), 10 mL (2%); and Pen/strep (Thermo Fisher, Cat. # MT-30-001-Cl), 5 mL (1%)]. The PDX models were routinely authenticated by short-tandem repeat (STR) assessments via the Mayo Genome Facility Genotyping Core. Clonogenic survival and other assays were performed as previously described using GBM PDX lines at <5 to 15 passages ( ). U251-MG cells (RRID: CVCL_0021) authenticated by STR (GeneCopoeia) were obtained, maintained, and modified to overexpress MGMT using a lentivirus vector, pGIPZ-MGMTPuro (U251MGMT), or an empty control vector (U251V), as previously described ( ). U251V and U251MGMT cell lines were maintained in DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS—Premium Select (Atlanta Biologicals) and 1% Pen/Strep. Cell cultures were assessed for mycoplasma contamination (LT07-318, MycoAlert, Lonza).

## Drugs and radiation
Peposertib was generously provided by EMD Serono under a CRADA with the Cancer Therapy Evaluation Program at the National Cancer Institute. A stock solution of peposertib was dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) and stored at −20°C. For in vivo experiments, peposertib was formulated in a suspension containing 0.25% hydroxypropyl methylcellulose, and 0.25% Tween20, in sodium citrate buffer (500 mmol/L, pH 2.5) administered via oral gavage. Radiation was administered to anesthetized mice using a 10-mm circular collimator with opposing lateral beams (225 kVp, 20 mA, 0.3-mm Cu filter) delivered with a PXI X-RAD SmART + irradiator. Analysis of peposertib concentration via liquid chromatography–mass spectrometry (LC-MS) was conducted following established methods as previously published ( ).

## Western blotting and immunofluorescence
Western blotting was performed as previously reported ( ). Antibodies used included p-Chk2 (Thr68), Cell Signaling Technology, 2661, 1:1,000, RRID: AB_331479; Chk2, Millipore, 05-649, 1:1,000, RRID: AB_2244941; pKAP1 (S824), Abcam: ab70369, 1:1,000, RRID: AB 1209417; Anti Rb-HRP, Cell Signaling Technology, 7074, 1:5,000, RRID: AB 2099233; DNA-PK, Abcam: ab70250, 1:1,000, RRID: AB_1209452; p-DNA-PK (S2056), Thermo Fisher, PA5-78130,1:1,000, RRID: AB_2736365; KAP1,100kDa, Cell Signaling Technology: 4123, 1:1,000, RRID: AB_2256670; p-p53 (S15), Cell Signaling Technology, 9284, 1:1,000, RRID: AB_331464; p-ATM (S1981), Abcam, ab79891, 1:1,000, RRID: AB_10670395; ATM, Abcam, ab17995, 1:1,000, RRID: AB_444169;Vinculin, and Cell Signaling Technology: 13,901, 1:1,000, RRID: AB_2728768, and 53BP1 antibody (BD Transduction, Cat.# 612523). γH2AX foci were performed as described previously ( ).

## Drug level analyses

## Sample preparation and MALDI-MSI analysis
Frozen tissue specimens were cryo-sectioned to a thickness of 10 μm and subsequently mounted onto glass slides coated with indium tin oxide and serial sections were affixed to microscopy slides for hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining imaged using a 10X objective on a Zeiss Observer Z.1. For peposertib tissue quantification, healthy mouse control brain tissues were homogenized and spiked with peposertib at concentrations spanning from 0.1 to 50 μmol/L. The resulting nine spiked homogenates were punched into a nine-well gelatin (40%) tissue microarray array mold featuring channels with a 1.5-mm core diameter ( ). The tissue mimetics were frozen, cryo-sectioned at the same thickness as the mouse brain tissues, and prepared together with the mouse brain sections.
For matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization mass spectrometry imaging (MALDI-MSI), tissue sections were treated with a matrix solution containing 2,5-dihydroxybenzoic acid (160 mg/mL) in a 70% methanol:30% water with 0.1% trifluoroacetic acid (TFA) mixture and 1% DMSO. The application was carried out uniformly using a robotic sprayer (HTX Technologies, Chapel Hill, NC) in a two-pass cycle. Specific parameters included a flow rate of 0.18 mL/minute, spray nozzle velocity of 1,200 mm/minute, nitrogen gas pressure of 10 psi, spray nozzle temperature of 75°C, and track spacing of 2 mm.
Data for mass spectrometry imaging was obtained utilizing a timsTOF fleX mass spectrometer (Bruker Daltonics, Billerica, MA) operating in positive ion mode. A multiple reaction monitoring method was employed, scanning the m/z range of 100 to 650. The mass spectrometry method was established and optimized by directly infusing a peposertib solution into the electrospray ionization source. The transition from the precursor ion at m/z 482.139 to 446.163, with a collision energy of 30 eV and a 3 m/z isolation window was monitored. Subsequently, the acquired data underwent analysis and visualization using SCiLS Lab software (version 2024a premium, Bruker Daltonics). Peposertib concentrations in plasma samples were determined by LC-MS/MS using liquid–liquid extraction as previously described ( ).

## Somatic mutations and copy number variants in PDX lines
Whole exome sequencing (WES) was performed for PDX lines as previously described ( ). The genomic characterization of these PDX lines is available online (https://www.cbioportal.org/study/clinicalData?id=gbm_mayo_pdx_sarkaria_2019). Mutations and copy number variants (CNV) from WES are shown for DNA damage response genes identified in a previous analysis of The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA; ref. ), only core pathway genes are shown in the heatmap.

## Gene set enrichment analysis
To analyze pathway activity in the PDX lines, z-scores from mRNA-sequencing data were used to perform gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA; v4.3.2). Default parameters were used with 1,000 permutations to check for the 10 top upregulated hallmark pathways in PDX lines 6, 10, 22, 46, 76, and 120. Additionally, enrichment scores for DNA repair, p53 signaling, and cell cycle pathways were assessed by running GSEA using the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes legacy and reactome pathways (min size: 10). Normalized enrichment scores (NES) and the FDR-adjusted q-values were used to generate plots in R (v4.3.2) using ggplot2 (v3.5.0; refs. , ).

## Dominant-negative TP53 (p53DD) transfection
Dominant-negative TP53 models were prepared as previously described ( ). Briefly, a modified lentivirus vector, pGIPZ-p53DD-puro was developed by replacing the turbo green fluorescent protein tag of pGIPZ with a cDNA that encodes truncated p53 protein (aa 300–393, p53DD) with dominant negative function, excised from T7-p53DD-pcDNA3 ( ) obtained from Addgene (Plasmid, # 25989). Lentiviral particles were packaged in human embryonic kidney 293T cells that were cotransfected with lentiviral vector and helper plasmids (psPAX2 and pMD2.G encoding Gag/Pol and vesicular stomatitis virus glycoprotein, respectively). Transduction was performed in the presence of 5-μg/mL polybrene (MilliporeSigma) as previously described ( ). Similarly, GBM14 cells transduced with lentiviral particles packaged from pGIPZ or pGIPZ-p53DD were expanded after puromycin selection.

## FM-HCR assays
GBM10-GFP and GBM10-p53DD cells at low passage (below P4) were released by trypsinization, collected by centrifugation, and seeded into 12-well plates at a density of 50,000 cells per well in a 1 mL volume of complete media (DMEM, 10% FBS, 1% pen/strep). The following day, two wells per cell line were treated with either 1 μL of M3814 solution (300 μmol/L in DMSO) or 1 μL of DMSO vehicle such that the final concentration in each well was 0.1% DMSO in control wells and 300 nmol/L M3814% and 0.1% DMSO in wells treated with inhibitor. Two hours later, cells were transfected with BFP_NHEJ and mCherry_HR reporter plasmids as previously described ( ). Briefly, Lipofectamine 3000 was used according to the manufacturer’s protocol to transfect one well of cells (per treatment condition) with 100-ng BFP_NHEJ, 100-ng mCherry_HR, 100 ng pMax_AmCyan transfection control plasmid, and 1,000 ng of pMax_mCherry HR donor plasmid and a second well of cells with the corresponding wild-type control plasmids (100-ng pMax_BFP, 100-ng pMax_mCherry, 100-ng pMax_AmCyan, and 1,000-ng pMax_mCherry donor plasmid). After the addition of the DNA-Lipofectamine complex to the cells, the plate was gently swirled and incubated in a humidified mammalian cell culture incubator (5% CO2, 37°C) for 4 hours. Media was then removed by aspiration and changed to fresh media containing either 0.1% DMSO or 300 nmol/L M3814/0.1% DMSO as appropriate. The plate was returned to the cell culture incubator and incubated overnight. The following day (24 hours after transfection), cells were trypsinized, collected by centrifugation, and analyzed by flow cytometry using an Attune NxT flow cytometer with the following parameters: (channel—excitation wavelength, filter): BFP (VL1—405, 450/40); AmCyan (VL2—405, 525/50); mCherry (561, 620/15). Compensation and gating were established using single-color controls as described in detail previously ( ). Each experiment was repeated four times. Repair efficiency was calculated by multiplying the percentage of fluorescent-positive cells by the mean fluorescence intensity and normalizing to the transfection control as previously described ( ).

## Statistical analyses
The in vitro data presented represent the mean ± SEM derived from a minimum of three independent experiments unless specified otherwise. Group comparisons were conducted using the two-sample t test. Overall survival distributions were assessed using the Kaplan–Meier method, and group comparisons were performed using the log-rank test. In all cases, P values of <0.05 were considered statistically significant.

## Results

## Peposertib radiosensitization of U251 isogenic sublines
As patients without methylation of MGMT promoter derive less benefit from IR + TMZ, the dependence of radiosensitization with a DNA-PK inhibitor (DNA-PKi) based on MGMT expression was investigated. Clonogenic survival assays were performed on a previously established cell line transduced to overexpress MGMT (U251MGMT) and a matched control vector line (U251V, MGMT promoter methylated) after exposure to peposertib (30–1,000 nmol/L). In both sublines, treatment with peposertib monotherapy (≥300 nmol/L; P < 0.0001) decreased survival by ∼30%, with no difference based on MGMT expression ( ). More importantly, the radiosensitization was dose-dependent with prominent potentiation of radiation effects at 300 to 1,000 nmol/L of peposertib (P < 0.0001), with nominally greater radiosensitization in the MGMT overexpressing subline at 300 and 1,000 nmol/L (P = 0.03 and 0.02 respectively; ).
 An essential step in translating target concentrations for peposertib from in vitro to in vivo drug exposures involves establishing the temporal exposure required for effective radiosensitization. Therefore, we evaluated clonogenic survival after IR (2.5 Gy) with progressively longer exposures to 300 nmol/L peposertib ( ). U251V cell exposure to peposertib for 4 and 8 hours exhibited nominal enhancement in cell kill, whereas more pronounced, statistically significant, effects were observed with 12-, 16-, and 24-hour exposures (P ≤ 0.01 for each). Consistent with inhibition of DNA repair, peposertib delayed γH2AX foci resolution at 24 hours (P < 0.0001; ). Following radiation, 53BP1 also nucleates at sites of DNA damage and can mediate repair pathway choice between HR and NHEJ. With the addition of peposertib, there was a reduction in 53BP1 foci immediately after radiation exposure, with no apparent difference observed at the 24-hour time point (Supplementary Fig. S1A), possibly indicating delayed preferential activation of HR over NHEJ with BRCA1 antagonism of 53BP1.
Consistent with the dose–response observed in clonogenic survival, DNA damage repair signaling analysis by immunoblot indicated effective inhibition of DNA-PKcs auto-phosphorylation (Ser2056) in both U251MGM and U251V ( ) sublines, at concentrations ≥100 nmol/L. Commensurate with reduced phospho-DNA-PK, increasing concentrations of peposertib were associated with elevated levels of phospho-ATM, -Kap1, and -p53 at 6- and/or 24-hour timepoints, which is consistent with delayed resolution of radiation-induced DNA damage ( , ).
To confirm that the radiosensitizing effect of peposertib results from the inhibition of DNA-PK catalytic activity, increasing doses of IR ± 300 nmol/L peposertib were evaluated via clonogenic survival in a knockdown model of U251 cells after nontargeted shRNA (shNT; ) or shRNA knockdown of DNA-PK (shPRKDC; ). As expected, knockdown of DNA-PK increased the radiosensitivity of U251 shPRKDC cells and reduced the radiosensitizing effect of peposertib compared with U251 shNT cells. This finding agrees with previous studies demonstrating the GBM lines lacking DNA-PK activity (MO59J) are not radiosensitized by peposertib ( ).

## Peposertib radiosensitizes GBM PDX lines
The radiosensitizing effects of peposertib were evaluated in the more clinically relevant PDXs, GBM10 and GBM120 (https://www.cbioportal.org/study/clinicalData?id=gbm_mayo_pdx_sarkaria_2019; ref. ). The addition of peposertib to IR demonstrated significant radiosensitization compared with IR alone in both lines (P < 0.0001, both) as measured by extreme limiting dilution assay (ELDA; ). From the ELDA analysis, the frequency of surviving clonogenic cells following each treatment could be estimated. As seen in , the addition of peposertib to IR significantly reduced the fraction of viable clonogenic cells. To further investigate the effects of peposertib on DNA damage and repair kinetics in the PDXs, γH2AX foci formation and resolution were evaluated following IR (5 Gy) ± peposertib (300 nmol/L). Consistent with the decreased survival noted in ELDA, the addition of peposertib to IR demonstrated significantly increased γH2AX foci compared with IR alone at both 1 and 24 hours in both lines (P < 0.0001, both; ). The correlation of NES of HR, microhomology-mediated end joining (MMEJ), NHEJ, and mRNA PRKDC rpkm to viability in four PDX GBM lines (GBM 6, 10, 22, and 76) was assessed (Supplementary Fig. S2). This demonstrated that the degree radiosensitization of IR + peposertib normalized to IR alone was correlated with the NES of NHEJ and mRNA PRKDC rpkm, but not the NESs of HR or MMEJ.

## Radiosensitization of orthotopic PDX GBM models by peposertib in vivo
Using a dosing regimen for radiation and peposertib previously defined for melanoma brain metastasis PDXs ( ), the interaction of radiation and peposertib was evaluated in GBM10, GBM46, and GBM120 orthotopic PDXs. After orthotopic tumor inoculation, treatment was started 7-, 11-, and 26-day postinoculation for GBM10, 46, and 120 respectively, based on tumor kinetics from prior survival studies of untreated mice. Mice were treated with a 5-day treatment regimen of peposertib (125 mg/kg twice daily via oral gavage), and a once-daily dose of IR (3.5 Gy). The first daily dose of peposertib was administered 15 to 30 minutes prior to IR, whereas the second was administered 7 hours later. The treatment regimen is illustrated in , and a representative image of the radiation setup and dose distribution map are shown in . In GBM120, the addition of peposertib to IR significantly improved survival compared with IR alone (P < 0.0001, median survival of 230 vs. 59 days, respectively). In contrast, the addition of peposertib to fractionated radiation did not significantly affect survival in either GBM10 (median survival of 49 vs. 60 days, respectively) or GBM46 (median survival of 286 vs. 256 days, respectively; , respectively). Treatment was well tolerated with nominal body weight loss during or after radiation treatment ( ).
 

## Pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics of peposertib in PDX tumors
The adequacy of drug distribution into orthotopic brain tumors was assessed in the PDX models. Mice with established orthotopic GBM10 and GBM120 tumors were dosed with peposertib (125 mg/kg), and then, plasma and brain were collected 7 hours later to assess the “trough” levels just prior to the typical second peposertib dose. Analysis of the plasma samples demonstrated similar drug levels between GBM10 and GBM120 cohorts (mean ± SD of 8,592 ± 4,743 nmol/L and 8,081 ± 4,744 nmol/L, respectively; P = 0.975; ). These plasma levels are consistent with plasma levels associated with robust radiosensitization in orthotopic metastatic melanoma PDXs (∼1,000–3,000 nmol/L; ref. ).
 The brain specimens were evaluated by MALDI-MSI in order to assess the adequacy of drug distribution into orthotopic brain tumor tissue. For these assays, a single coronal brain section through each tumor was analyzed for each mouse, and the drug concentrations across the section were determined ( and ), relative to a tissue-mimetic standard curve (Supplementary Fig. S3). Using the H&E as a guide to delineate tumor, the average drug concentrations were calculated in the tumor and adjacent normal brain. This analysis demonstrated comparable average drug levels in GBM10 (2266 nmol/L) and GBM120 (2460 nmol/L) orthotopic tumors ( and ; P = 0.823), with no difference in the percent of tumor area with concentrations greater than 1-, 2-, 5-, or 10-μmol/L peposertib ( ). In our prior studies of melanoma PDXs, these drug levels were sufficient to achieve radiosensitization in vivo in that disease context.

## Molecular features associated with response in PDX lines
Additional studies were undertaken to evaluate molecular features associated with effective radiosensitization. In order to confirm whether knockdown of DNA-PKcs increased radiosensitivity, orthotopic murine GBM models demonstrated increased survival in knockdown U251 shPRKDC compared with control U251 shNT after IR alone (4 Gy × 5 fractions; P < 0.017, median survival 141 vs. 90 days, respectively; ). Additionally, adding peposertib to IR in the TP53 mutant cell line U251 significantly decreased viability as measured by Cell Titer Glo assay at 14 days post-IR when treated with nontargeted shRNA (shNT), but no significant difference was seen after shRNA knockdown of DNA-PK (shPRKDC) or in the TP53 wild-type (TP53wt) line SVG ( ). As expected, knockdown of DNA-PK increased the radiosensitivity of U251 shPRKDC cells and reduced the radiosensitizing effect of peposertib in comparison with U251 shNT cells.
 As prior studies have found an association with TP53 mutation status and radiosensitization with DNA-PKis ( , – ), the viability of multiple GBM PDX lines and normal tissue cell line (SVG) after IR + peposertib were investigated in vitro via Cell Titer Glo (CTG) in TP53 mutant lines compared with wild-type lines ( ) and found that the TP53 mutants had significantly decreased viability compared with IR alone, whereas the TP53wt lines did not ( ). We then examined the PRKDC methylation status (Supplementary Fig. S1B) and PRKDC mRNA z-scores of the GBM PDX lines and found a significant correlation with the degree of radiosensitization with peposertib and lower mRNA PRKDC z-scores ( and ). To examine the molecular profiles of these lines in greater detail, we then reviewed CNVs Log2 Fold Change (Supplementary Fig. S1C), somatic mutations ( ), and performed a GSEA ( ) of key DNA damage response (DDR) pathways. We found that the degree of radiosensitization with peposertib in these GBM PDX lines was significantly correlated with lower NHEJ NESs (P < 0.0029) but not MMEJ or HR scores (Supplementary Fig. S2)—and that TP53mut lines showed a greater degree of radiosensitization regardless of the degree of HR/NHEJ/MMEJ pathway enrichment.
Next, in order to investigate the relationship of TP53 functional status on radiosensitization with peposertib we used four GBM lines (A172, U87, GBM10, and GBM14), which are TP53 wild-type (TP53wt) and used lentiviral transfection to express a truncated p53 protein (aa 300–393, p53DD) with dominant negative function (TP53dd). Adding peposertib to IR significantly decreased confluence normalized to controls in the TP53dd lines, but not in the TP53wt lines, in A172, GBM10, and GBM14 (P < 0.05) with a trend in U87, which did not reach significance ( ). This along with the differential radiosensitization in TP53wt versus TP53mut lines in vitro ( ) and in vivo ( ) suggest that functional TP53 status has a significant impact on the degree of radiosensitization with peposertib in GBM models. We then examined the activity of HR and NHEJ after peposertib exposure in GBM10 TP53wt compared with TP53dd lines using fluorescence multiplex host cell reactivation (FM-HCR) assay. This demonstrated that peposertib significantly decreased NHEJ activity in TP53dd lines compared with TP53wt ( ) but did not significantly impact HR based on TP53 status ( ). This suggests that inhibition of DNA-PK may be more effective in radiosensitizing TP53 mutants compared with wild-type tumors via enhanced suppression of the NHEJ repair pathway.

## Discussion
Inherent radiation resistance limits the efficacy of radiation in GBM. Approximately 85% of patients with GBM experience local progression within the radiation therapy field, which provides a compelling rationale for developing novel drug therapies that can enhance the efficacy of radiation. With a central role in the repair of radiation-induced DNA DSB, several groups are developing small molecule kinase inhibitors of DNA-PKcs. In this study, we evaluated the DNA-PKcs inhibitor peposertib in preclinical GBM models. Despite demonstrable radiosensitizing effects in vitro, the impact of peposertib on radiation response in vivo was variable, and additional studies are required to fully understand the mechanisms underlying this heterogeneity in response.
The most interesting finding in this study is the discordance between the in vitro and in vivo data of radiosensitization of peposertib in GBM10 and GBM120. Peposertib is a known substrate for the P-gp and Bcrp1 efflux transporters active within the blood–brain barrier ( ), and our initial hypothesis was that poor distribution into orthotopic GBM10 tumors was responsible for the limited radiosensitization. However, analysis of drug levels via MALDI-MSI demonstrated comparable peposertib drug levels in GBM120 as compared with GBM10 orthotopic tumors ( ). Similarly, significantly higher peposertib concentrations were observed in melanoma brain metastases, relative to normal brain, and drug levels achieved in this latter study were similar to those observed here ( ). These data suggest that GBM120 and GBM10 may have a relatively disrupted blood–brain barrier within the tumor that allows adequate drug accumulation. However, in humans, every GBM has significant regions of tumor with an intact blood–brain barrier ( ), and adequate delivery of peposertib throughout the radiation target region will be important for a significant impact on the efficacy of radiation ( ). Thus, the results from an ongoing surgical window of opportunity clinical trial assessing peposertib accumulation within contrast-enhancing (BBB disrupted) and noncontrast enhancing (BBB intact) regions of GBM tumors are eagerly awaited.
Achievement of adequate intracellular drug levels is another key factor that could influence the efficacy of peposertib. Beyond activity within the BBB, both P-gp and Bcrp1 are expressed by tumor cells and can limit intracellular drug uptake. This is especially prominent in several solid malignancies and may reflect the large variations in the in vivo radiosensitizing effects of peposertib observed in multiple other malignancies ( , , , – ). Others have demonstrated that peposertib acts as a competitive inhibitor of Bcrp1 efflux activity for known substrates at 300 to 1,000 nmol/L and reversed Bcrp1-mediated drug resistance ( ). Peposertib increased Bcrp1 ATPase activity, which is consistent with peposertib being a Bcrp1 substrate. This is especially relevant in GBM as temozolomide is a weak substrate for both P-gp and Bcrp1 ( , ). Previous reports have identified Bcrp1 and P-gp expression in GBM ( – ), and further studies would be important to evaluate whether the expression of these efflux transporters on glioma cells also contributes to the limited efficacy in some tumors.
Apart from drug delivery, the difference between GBM10 and GBM120 survival might be explained by differences in molecular features that are synthetically lethal with DNA-PK inhibitors. Other investigations have suggested that TP53 mutations render cancer cells more resistant to DNA-PK monotherapy (in the absence of radiation), but that targeting the MMEJ pathway and Pol Theta specifically may overcome this resistance and offer a novel synthetic lethal strategy ( ). A study more focused on the use of DNA-PK inhibitors for radiosensitization demonstrated that TP53 mutant tumors were more sensitive to treatment with DNA-PK inhibitor + IR, as they were more likely to undergo mitotic catastrophe ( ). By contrast, the wild-type TP53 cells demonstrated more activation of ATM, TP53, and CHK2 leading to cell cycle arrest and senescence ( ). Previous analysis of the TCGA Firehose legacy GBM dataset has demonstrated that TP53 mutants have upregulation/enrichment of PRKDC, many components of the HR pathway, and cell cycle components relative to TP53 wild-type GBM ( ). Consistent with these data, GBM120 and U251, which were robustly radiosensitized by peposertib, are both TP53 mutant tumors, and in our CTG assay, the TP53 mutant PDX lines were more robustly sensitized to radiation ( ). These data are similar to our recent study, which demonstrated preferential radiosensitization of TP53-mutant tumors by the ATM inhibitor AZD1390 ( ). Further studies will be required to more clearly define the impact of TP53 mutation and other DDR pathway alterations on the efficacy of these potent radiosensitizers.
Additional molecular features were considered with regard to the radiosensitizing effects of peposertib. In this study, we evaluated general molecular features important in GBM including DDR CNVs (Supplementary Fig. S1C), somatic mutations ( ), and performed a GSEA ( ), of core DSB repair pathway proteins of HR and NHEJ between the PDX lines and found that both GBM10 and GBM46 are TP53 wild-type and had deep deletions of CDKN2A and CDKN2B, whereas GBM120 carries two TP53 point mutations (R249S and R158H, both inactivating loss of function missense mutations in the DNA binding domain) with no alterations in CDKN2A/B. We found that transfection of TP53wt GBM lines with a truncated TP53 mutant protein with dominant-negative function increased response to IR + peposertib compared with TP53wt lines ( ) and that peposertib showed superior suppression of NHEJ activity in TP53dd lines with no change in HR ( and ). This corroborates prior studies indicating that TP53 status and cell cycle dysregulation may correlate with the degree of radiosensitization with DNA-PKis ( , , ).
Although genomic or epigenomic differences potentially can explain the differential sensitizing effects of peposertib in GBM10 versus GBM120 in vivo, both lines demonstrated at least some sensitization in vitro. In examining the ELDA data, GBM120 has a much lower estimated clonogenic cell frequency ( ) at baseline compared with GBM10. If the fraction of cells with clonogenic potential as estimated from our ELDA assays translates to a similar fraction in orthotopic tumors, we might speculate that the much lower fraction in GBM120 translated into a more profound impact on survival compared with GBM10. This also may reflect differences in the single fractions used in the in vitro studies compared with the multifractionated regimens used in the murine models. Overall, these results highlight the need for further studies to identify relevant patient selection markers that might indicate which patients are most likely to benefit from DNA-PK inhibition during radiotherapy.

## Conclusion
Peposertib radiosensitizes GBM models regardless of MGMT expression at concentrations of ≥300 nmol/L, and the degree of radiosensitization improves with longer drug exposure, up to 24 hours, following IR. As expected, the radiosensitizing effects of peposertib are lost in cells after shRNA knockdown of DNA-PK compared with nontargeted shRNA controls. Despite achieving adequate plasma and tumor concentrations, the radiosensitizing effects of peposertib in vivo were limited to TP53-mutant GBM tumors. Transfection of GBM TP53 wild-type tumors with dominant-negative TP53 mutant increased radiosensitization with peposertib, and peposertib significantly decreased NHEJ activity in these lines compared with TP53 wild-type controls.
