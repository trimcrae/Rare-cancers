Among these, BIN1 and RIN3 have emerged as susceptibility genes, yet their functional relationship in AD remains largely unknown. Here, we investigated how BIN1 and RIN3 interaction regulates RAB5 activity and endosomal pathology. RIN3 has been shown to bind BIN1, and we previously reported that this interaction modulates amyloid-β (Aβ) precursor protein (APP) trafficking and Aβ generation in vitro. Together, these findings establish BIN1 as a critical regulator of RIN3-driven RAB5 activation and neuronal endosomal homeostasis.

## INTRODUCTION
The three major pathological hallmarks of Alzheimer’s disease (AD) include extracellular amyloid-β (Aβ) plaques, intracellular neurofibrillary tangles consisting of hyperphosphorylated Tau, and neuroinflammation, all of which begin decades before the onset of clinical symptoms of AD ( – ). Genome-wide association studies (GWASs) have revealed association of AD risk with single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) in more than 70 AD risk genes ( ), among them, genes encoding proteins involved in endocytic trafficking, including the RAS and RAB interactor 3 (RIN3) and bridging integrator 1 (BIN1). BIN1 is a major AD susceptibility gene with an effect size on risk second only to APOE ( – ). BIN1, the product of BIN1, is a multifunctional effector protein that regulates membrane architecture via its intrinsically curved membrane-binding Bin/amphiphysin/Rvs (BAR) domain ( , ). BIN1 mediates protein-protein interactions via its Src homology 3 (SH3) domain to regulate small G proteins (heterotrimeric guanine nucleotide–binding proteins), including RHO and dynamin guanosine triphosphatase (GTPases) involved in cytoskeleton assembly/disassembly and/or endosomal trafficking ( – ).
BIN1 undergoes extensive splicing, leading to at least 12 isoforms (BIN1V1 to BIN1V12). The BIN1V1 isoform, characterized by its high molecular weight (BIN1hi), contains clathrin and AP-2 (adaptor protein complex 2)–binding sites (CLAP domain) at residues 334 to 376. This isoform is predominantly found in neurons and astrocytes and has been implicated in both amyloid and Tau pathology in AD ( ). The CLAP domain is absent in other isoforms of BIN1 (BIN1V2, BIN1V3, and BIN1V7 to BIN1V12), characterized by their low molecular weight (BIN1lo) ( ). BIN1lo isoforms, such as BIN1V9, are mainly found in oligodendrocytes and microglia and have been reported to regulate postnatal myelination in mouse brain ( , ), at least partially explaining the perinatal lethality of homozygous Bin1 knockout (KO) mice ( – ).
We and others have previously shown that the neuronal and nonneuronal isoforms of BIN1 play different roles in AD pathophysiology. A recent report demonstrated that endosomal abnormality observed in neurons derived from human-derived induced pluripotent stem cells (hiPSCs) or brain organoids lacking BIN1 could be rescued by overexpressing BIN1V1, but not BIN1V9 ( ). We previously reported that the BIN1-binding partner RIN3 binds to both BIN1V1 and BIN1V9 with equal affinity. However, only translocation of BIN1V1 to RAB5-positive (RAB5+) endosomes results in attenuated trafficking of Aβ precursor protein (APP) and reduced Aβ generation ( ). Overexpression of constitutively active RAB5 (RAB5QL) could not recruit BIN1V1 to endosomes in the absence of RIN3 expression, suggesting that the endosomal translocation of BIN1V1 is RIN3-specific ( ). RIN3 binds to BIN1 together with their mutual binding partner RAB5, forming a tripartite complex ( , ). RIN3 up-regulation has been associated with endosomal pathology in AD mice ( ). In addition to BIN1, the product of another AD-associated gene, CD2-associated protein (CD2AP), also binds with RIN3 at the same unstructured N-terminal (1 to 568) proline-rich domain (RIN3-PRD) via conserved SH3 domains ( , ).
Here, we set out to investigate the potential roles of BIN1, RIN3, and their binding of these proteins in RAB5 activation and AD-related endosomal pathogenesis. First, we showed a 50% loss of RIN3 expression in Rin3 constitutive KO mice (Rin3-CKO), in which exon 3 (transcript NM_177620.4) is flanked by LoxP sites. In the Rin3-CKO mouse brain, endosomal enlargement is attenuated compared to the wild type (WT). Second, we showed that CRISPR-Cas9–mediated KO of BIN1 in hiPSC-derived neurons enhances RIN3-mediated activation of RAB5, leading to endosomal enlargement. Third, analysis of whole-genome sequencing (WGS) data from the National Institute of Mental Health (NIMH) multiethnic AD family dataset led to the identification of two relatively rare missense mutations (rs74074811 and rs74074812) in exon 6 of RIN3, which are in tight linkage disequilibrium (LD; D′ = 1) with the AD-associated SNPs at the RIN3 locus previously identified by GWAS ( – ). The two RIN3 missense mutations reside in the BIN1-binding domain of RIN3, and we show that CRISPR-Cas9–mediated introduction of these mutations, individually or together, in hiPSC-derived neurons resulted in a loss of BIN1 binding to RIN3 and increased endosomal enlargement, an early hallmark of AD pathology. Both SNPs previously have been linked to Paget’s disease of bone ( ). We also performed RNA sequencing of CRISPR-Cas9–edited BIN1-deficient hiPSCs and identified several molecular pathways relevant to neurodegeneration.
In summary, we demonstrate a direct role for RIN3 in RAB5 activation and endosomal enlargement, which is negatively regulated by BIN1 in neurons. Disruption of this interaction by BIN1 KO or introduction of familial AD loss-of-function RIN3 missense mutations results in hyperactivation of RAB5 and AD-related endosomal pathology.

## RESULTS

## Rin3-CKO mice show reduced RAB5+ enlarged endosomes in the brain
RIN3 is a guanine nucleotide exchange factor (GEF) that activates the RAB5 pathway by exchanging guanosine diphosphate (GDP) for guanosine 5′-triphosphate (GTP) and converting the inactive RAB5-GDP to active RAB5-GTP ( , – ). RAB5 regulates endosomal homeostasis and endocytosis to maintain cellular homeostasis. Recent reports have shown that hyperactivation of RAB5 promotes endosomal enlargement, an early neuropathological hallmark of AD ( , – ). Increased RAB5 activity and endosomal enlargement have been reported in the brains of PSEN1 E280A mice ( ). RAB5+ enlarged early endosomes (RAB5+ eEEs) have been reported to be markedly increased in transgenic mice overexpressing pathological RAB5 (PA-RAB5) ( ).
To better understand the role of RIN3 in endosomal pathology in vivo, we generated Rin3-CKO mice. These mice are viable, and the open field test (OFT) revealed no significant behavioral differences between the 5-month-old Rin3-CKO and WT mice, suggesting no abnormal locomotor activity or anxiety-like behavior in the KO mice ( ). We validated the Rin3-CKO mice by genotyping using the primer pairs CTAAACAACTTGGCCACACTCC (forward) and CCAACTGCTGTTTTATCATGTCC (reverse). We detected the expected polymerase chain reaction (PCR) fragment of 247 base pairs (bp) for the WT, while Rin3-CKO generated 247-bp (WT) and 357-bp (KO) PCR fragments (fig. S1), as expected. We also performed Western blot analysis by probing the brain extracts of WT or Rin3-CKO mice with an anti-RIN3 rabbit polyclonal antibody (Proteintech, 12709-1-AP) that recognizes both mouse and human RIN3. As expected, levels of RIN3 in the Rin3-CKO mice were reduced by ~50% compared to WT mice [ ; immunoblot (IB): anti-RIN3, n = 2]. However, RAB5 levels, which were used as a background control, remained unchanged between the two groups ( ; IB: anti-RAB5),
 After completing behavioral tasks, the WT and Rin3-CKO mice were perfused with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) for immunocytochemistry. To gain ultrastructural insights into endosomal pathology, we performed immunostaining using an antibody against total RAB5 (anti-RAB5) that detected RAB5+ (RAB5+ve) puncta ( and fig. S2; green puncta). We also used an antibody specific for the GTP-bound active RAB5 (anti-RAB5*) to detect RAB5*-positive (RAB5*+ve) puncta ( , red). We validated the specificity of the anti-RAB5* antibody by using a RAB5 Activation Assay (Abcam), demonstrating that the anti-RAB5* antibody only recognized RAB5 from cells treated with 100 μM guanosine 5′-O-(3′-thiotriphosphate) (GTP-γ-S) (active) but not with 100 μM GDP (inactive) (fig. Fiji/ImageJ particle analysis was performed to measure the number and frequency of RAB5+ve puncta of sizes representing early endosomes (EEs) or enlarged early endosomes (eEEs). Three to six images per animal were examined, each containing 30 to 70 Hoechst-positive cells (n = 3 per genotype, male and female) in the cortex and hippocampus. Quantification revealed a nearly 60% reduction in the number of RAB5+ve eEEs (0.5 to 2.0 μm2) in Rin3-CKO compared to the WT mouse brains ( ; WT: 16 ± 1.4% versus Rin3-CKO: 3.5 ± 1.1%). In contrast, we found a modest (not significant) increase in the number of smaller RAB5+ve EEs (0.1 to 0.5 μm2) in Rin3-CKO compared to the WT mice ( ; WT: 84 ± 1.4% versus Rin3-CKO: 96 ± 1.1%). While the number of eEEs decreased significantly in Rin3-CKO mice, the average size of these eEEs was not significantly different between WT and Rin3-CKO brains (WT: 0.756 ± 0.11 μm2 versus Rin3-CKO: 0.68 ± 0.02 μm2), suggesting that the decrease in the eEE levels was compensated by the modest increase in the EE levels. Quantification of the percentage of RAB5*+ puncta ( ) between the WT and Rin3-CKO mice revealed no significant differences ( ), suggesting that the partial loss of RIN3 lowered endosomal enlargement but not RAB5 activation. To further confirm, we compared the levels of RAB5* in the WT and Rin3-CKO mouse brains by immunoprecipitating the endogenous active RAB5 by an anti-RAB5* antibody and immunoblotting with total or active RAB5 antibodies anti-RAB5 or anti-RAB*, respectively [ ; immunoprecipitation (IP): anti-RAB5*; IB: anti-RAB5 or RAB5*]. Anti-RAB5* pulled down a significantly higher level of RAB5* from the brain homogenates pretreated with GTP-γ-S compared to GDP ( ), as expected ( – ). Immunoprecipitated RAB5* from GDP-treated extracts indicated the pull-down of endogenous RAB5*. Quantitative analysis revealed that the anti-RAB5* antibody pulled down approximately twofold RAB5* from GTP-γ-S–treated extracts compared to GDP, validating RAB5 activation. Analysis of the fold changes (FCs) or average intensities of RAB5* bands in the WT and Rin3-CKO immunoprecipitates remained largely unchanged, confirming no statistically significant impact of the partial loss of RIN3 on RAB5 activation in vivo ( ; n = 3 per genotype). A reverse pull-down assay also demonstrated little or no alteration in the RAB5* levels between the WT and Rin3-CKO (data not shown).
Collectively, these results show that a partial knockdown of Rin3 significantly reduces the number of RAB5+ enlarged endosomes in the brain without affecting the levels of total or active RAB5. Notably, RIN3 is a member of vacuolar protein sorting–associated protein 9 (VPS9) domain–containing protein family, and at least seven well-characterized RAB5 GEFs have been identified within this family ( ). Thus, the partial loss of RIN3 may perturb endosomal homeostasis, while the impact on RAB5 activity may be compensated by RAB5 GEFs other than RIN3.

## BIN1-KO leads to hyperactivation of RAB5 in hiPSC-derived neurons
We previously reported that RIN3 specifically translocates the neuronal isoform of BIN1 (BIN1V1) to RAB5+ EEs and attenuates the endocytosis of APP, resulting in reduced Aβ generation. We and others have hypothesized that BIN1V1 binds to and sequesters RIN3, thereby inhibiting RIN3-mediated RAB5 activation, leading to altered APP and β-secretase 1 (BACE1) trafficking and amyloidogenic processing of APP within endosomes ( , ). Despite being one of the major AD risk genes, further elucidating the functional role of BIN1 in AD pathogenesis in vivo has been challenging because Bin1-KO mice are postnatally lethal ( ).
To assess the role of BIN1 in AD, we used human induced pluripotent stem cells (hiPSCs) differentiated into neural progenitor cells and, subsequently, into matured cortical neurons. We generated BIN1-KO hiPSC lines from hiPSCs obtained from both male (M) and female (F) donors using the CRISPR-Cas9 technology. We targeted BIN1 with the single guide RNA (sgRNA) sequence CTGCCTCCTACCTTTGACGG and introduced one nucleotide frameshift. Probing the parental hiPSCs obtained from a male donor [WT (M)] and hiPSCs of three BIN1-KO clones with an anti-BIN1 antibody (Abcam), we confirmed a complete loss of BIN1 expression in the KO clones [ ; WT (M) versus BIN1-KO clones: 11.2 (M), 19.2 (M), 8.4 (F), and 8.8 (F)]. In addition to Western blot analysis, we also validated the KO hiPSCs by sequencing the DNAs from the WT and BIN1-KO (M19.2) hiPSCs (fig. S4). In subsequent experiments, the WT (M) and 19.2 (M) clones have served as the WT control and BIN1-KO hiPSCs, respectively. Next, we differentiated the hiPSCs into human-induced neural progenitor cells (hiNPCs) and subsequently into matured cortical neurons. We confirmed their maturation by probing with the neuronal marker Tuj1 [ , anti-Tuj1 (red)]. The matured cortical neurons derived from hiPSCs are subsequently denoted as human-induced neurons (hiNs). Approximately 10% of cells were GFAP (glial fibrillary acidic protein)–positive astrocytes (data not shown), consistent with another report ( ).
 As expected, anti-BIN1 antibody labeling of the Tuj1-positive hiNs detected BIN1 in the WT but not in the BIN1-KO hiNs ( ), further confirming the loss of BIN1. Notably, the anti-BIN1 antibody is not isoform-specific. The low-molecular-weight BIN1 isoform, BIN1lo/BIN1V9, is ubiquitous or nonneuronal, whereas the CLAP domain–containing high-molecular-weight BIN1 isoform, BIN1hi/BIN1V1, is neuronal ( ). Thus, to investigate whether matured neurons differentially express the neuronal and nonneuronal BIN1 isoforms, we probed the total protein extracts from hiNPCs derived from the WT or BIN1-KO hiPSCs and 6- or 12-day differentiated hiNs with an anti-BIN1 antibody. hiNPCs from the WT hiPSCs predominantly expressed the BIN1lo/BIN1V9 isoform before differentiation [ ; parental (WT), hiNPCs]. In contrast, the high-molecular-weight neuronal isoform BIN1hi/BIN1V1 was predominantly expressed in 6- or 12-day matured hiNs derived from the WT hiNPCs [ ; parental (WT), hiNs, 6 or 12 days]. This is consistent with studies demonstrating that the BIN1hi/BIN1 isoform is detectable only in matured neurons (hiNs) but not in hiNPCs ( ). As expected, we did not detect BIN1 in hiNPCs and hiNs from BIN1-KO cells ( , BIN1-KO). Thus, 6- or 12-day mature hiNs serve as a reliable neuronal model to study the molecular pathways associated exclusively with the neuronal BIN1 isoform.
We next asked whether the loss of BIN1 leads to hyperactivation of RAB5 in 12-day matured hiNs derived from the WT or BIN1-KO hiPSCs in three dimensions. We pulled down endogenous RAB5 with an anti-RAB5 antibody (IP: anti-RAB5) and probed with anti-RAB5* and detected a robust increase in active RAB5* levels in BIN1-KO hiNs compared to the WT ( ; IP: anti-RAB5; IB: anti-RAB5*). Quantitative analysis revealed that the loss of BIN1 increased levels of RAB5* by nearly twofold [ ; 2.0 ± 0.2 (arbitrary unit) in the WT versus 4.8 ± 0.3 in BIN1-KO hiNs (n = 3 separate experiments)]. Collectively, these findings show that the KO of BIN1 leads to hyperactivation of RAB5 (twofold increased levels of RAB5*) in neurons.

## BIN1-KO leads to endosomal enlargement in hiPSC-derived neurons
To test whether hyperactivation of RAB5 affects endosomal homeostasis, we assessed endosomal enlargement by measuring the mean size of RAB5+ EEs and eEEs in the WT and BIN1-KO hiNs. Indirect immunofluorescence (iIF) of the 12-day matured hiNs with an anti-RAB5 antibody detected RAB5+ve endocytic vesicles ( , RAB5, green puncta). Anti-Tuj1 (neuronal marker) labeling of the WT and BIN1-KO hiNs detected no differences in their morphology ( , Tuj1, red), suggesting no effect of BIN1-KO on the differentiation of hiPSCs into hiNs. We also observed no significant difference in RAB5 distribution between the WT and BIN1-KO ( ).
 Next, we performed a quantitative analysis by counting the RAB5+ve puncta of sizes ranging from 0.1 to 0.5 μm2, representing RAB5+ve EEs ( , a and c), or 0.5 to 2.0 μm2, representing the eEEs ( , b and d). We counted the RAB5+ve puncta (EEs and eEEs) of four equal square areas (0.04 mm2) of each image [three images from each genotype (WT or BIN1-KO)]. We performed Fiji/ImageJ particle analysis to count the number of EEs (0.1 to 0.5 μm2) and eEEs (0.5 to 2.0 μm2) after thresholding, as described before. Quantitative analysis of the iIF confocal images where 25 to 30 cells from the WT or BIN1-KO hiNs were counted from triplicate (n = 3) experiments revealed that the loss of BIN1 significantly (P = 0.0025) increased the number of RAB5+ eEEs ( ). We also counted the number of EEs and eEEs per hiNs (100 μm long) derived from the WT or BIN1-KO hiPSCs ( ). Quantitation revealed no significant change in the numbers of RAB5+ve EEs ( ). In contrast, we detected a significant (n = 6 neurons per phenotype; P = 0.042) increase in the RAB5+ eEE levels ( ). Next, we tested the impact of BIN1’s loss on amyloid pathology by measuring the BACE1-mediated cleavage of APP, which is the rate-limiting step for Aβ generation. We detected a significant (P = 0.03, n > 4) elevation of the soluble APP β (sAPPβ) levels relative to total sAPP (sAPPtot) in BIN1-KO compared to the WT hiNs ( ), linking endosomal enlargement with amyloid pathology. The α-secretase–cleaved neuroprotective sAPPα levels remained unaltered.
We also found a reverse effect of neuronal BIN1V1 overexpression on RIN3-mediated RAB5 activation in vitro. N2AAPP cells coexpressing RIN3flag + BIN1V1GFP markedly reduced active RAB5* levels compared to cells expressing only RIN3 (fig. S5). We previously reported that the coexpression of RIN3flag + BIN1V1GFP perturbs the endocytic trafficking of APP and reduces Aβ generation compared to cells expressing only RIN3. Together with our previous studies, these findings suggest that BIN1 plays a key role in both Aβ generation and the accumulation of RAB5+ eEEs in neurons ( , – ).

## Familial AD RIN3 variants cause RAB5 hyperactivation and endosomal enlargement
Several common variants at the RIN3 (rs12590654, rs12881735, and rs12881735) and BIN1 loci (e.g., rs4663105 and rs6733839) have been previously identified in GWAS as major genetic risk factors for AD and have been associated with Tau pathology, intracellular Aβ accumulation, and EE enlargement ( – ). Analyses of WGS data of the NIMH AD family cohort ( ) led to the identification of two relatively rare [minor allele frequencies (MAFs) of 0.01 to 0.02] SNPs in RIN3: rs74074811 and rs74074812, both located in exon 6. The two SNPs are in tight LD with each other (r2 ranging from 0.6 to 1 depending on the population) and also in tight LD (D′ = 1) with the common SNPs near RIN3 previously found to be strongly associated with AD risk in GWAS. Both RIN3 SNPs result in missense mutations in the BIN1-binding domain of RIN3: rs74074811 (G > A, Arg427>Gln [R427Q]) and rs74074812 (C > T, Pro477>Ser [P477S]).
 The two RIN3 missense mutations were present in the same 13 subjects from 11 late-onset AD families; in addition, rs74074811 was present in three subjects from two other late-onset AD families. One family had three siblings consisting of an affected carrier of both SNPs [age 75, apolipoprotein E ε3/ε4 (APOE ε3/ε4)], an unaffected noncarrier (age 79, APOE ε3/ε4), and an affected noncarrier (age 78, APOE ε3/ε3). A second family had five siblings consisting of two affected carriers (age 71, APOE ε3/ε4, and age 64, APOE ε4/ε4), one unaffected noncarrier (age 79, APOE ε4/ε4), one unaffected subject with a missing genotype (age 82, APOE ε3/ε4), and one unaffected carrier (age 80, APOE ε3/ε4). Another family consisted of two paternal half-siblings that were both affected carriers of these mutations. Four families had two siblings: one affected carrier and one affected noncarrier each. Three families had only one child; two of those three children were affected carriers (age 75, APOE ε3/ε4, and age 82, APOE ε3/ε4), and one was a relatively young unaffected carrier (age 46, APOE ε3/ε4). Last, two families carried rs74074811 (but not rs74074812), both with two siblings. One of these families had two affected carriers (ages 74 and 83, both APOE ε3/ε4), while the other had one affected carrier (age 84, APOE ε3/ε3) and one affected noncarrier (age 83, APOE ε3/ε3).
These data indicate the partial penetrance of these RIN3 missense mutations with regard to AD risk. This, in combination with the low population frequency (0.01 to 0.02) of these mutations, likely explains the nonsignificant P values for association with AD risk. However, the strong degree of LD between these two mutations and common intronic SNPs at RIN3 (rs12590654, rs12881735, and rs12881735) previously reported to exhibit genome-wide significant association with AD risk [r2 ranging from 0.6 (admixed American) to 1 (European) according to the National Institutes of Health (NIH) LDLink (https://ldlink.nih.gov/)] suggests that they represent a small fraction of the association of AD risk with the RIN3 locus. Notably, in an analysis of a multiethnic dataset with unrelated AD cases and controls from the Alzheimer’s disease sequencing project (ADSP; ), rs74074811 showed nominally significant association of these same two RIN3 mutations with AD risk in the Hispanic population (P = 0.035).
On the basis of these findings, we next asked whether these two RIN3 missense mutations affect the RIN3 function. Given that both missense mutations are located in the BIN1-binding PRD of RIN3 ( ), we specifically tested whether they affect the binding of RIN3 to BIN1. An in silico protein structure modeling study using SwissProt revealed that the missense mutations (R427Q or P477S) are not predicted to alter the overall structure of RIN3. Superimposition of the RIN3-binding partner RAB5 with RIN3 also predicted no changes in the interaction between RIN3 and RAB5 because of the presence of these mutations (fig. S6), which is consistent with in silico protein modeling of RAB-GTPases and their interacting protein partners [e.g., RAB GEFs, GTPase-activating proteins (GAPs), or GDP dissociation inhibitors (GDIs)] [reviewed by Pylypenko et al. ( )]. However, the presence of either of the two RIN3 mutations revealed an alteration in the superimposed structures of RIN3 and BIN1 when compared to WT ( ).
 To experimentally test whether the RIN3-R427Q and/or P477S mutations impair the binding of RIN3 to BIN1 in vitro, we performed a co-IP assay in neuro 2A (N2A) cells ( ) coexpressing flag epitope–tagged WT RIN3 (RIN3wtflag) or its single or double mutant RIN3(R427Q)flag, RIN3(P477S)flag, or RIN3(R427Q, P477S)flag [RIN3wt, RIN3 (RQ), RIN3 (PS), or RIN3 (RQPS), respectively] with a green fluorescent protein (GFP) epitope–tagged BIN1V1 (BIN1V1GFP). Co-IP assays revealed that RIN3wt pulled down BIN1, as expected. In contrast, we observed significantly reduced RIN3-BIN1 binding in the presence of one or both RIN3 missense mutations as compared to RIN3wt ( ). Despite diminished binding affinity, RIN3 (RQ) was still able to recruit BIN1V1GFP to endocytic vesicles ( ). The result is expected given that we previously reported that BIN1V1 and BIN1V9 bind to RIN3 with equal affinity, while only BIN1V1, and not BIN1V9, translocates to RAB5+ EEs ( ). Thus, RIN3 binding is secondary to RIN3-dependent translocation of BIN1V1 to the RAB5+ EEs. RIN3 (PS) and RIN3 (RQPS) also allowed for translocation of BIN1V1 to endocytic vesicles (data not shown).
We next observed that both RIN3 mutations led to the translocation of BIN1V1GFP to endocytic vesicles that were larger than the average endosomes (0.1 to 0.5 μm2) ( , arrowheads) ( ). We characterized these large vesicles by imaging the cells expressing RIN3wt or the RIN3 single and double mutants [RIN3 (RQ), RIN3 (PS), or RIN3 (RQPS)] with GFP-tagged RAB5 (RAB5GFP) ( , upper panel, green puncta). The WT and mutant RIN3 were labeled with an anti-flag antibody ( , upper panel, red puncta). The merged yellow puncta represent RAB5GFP-positive endosomes in the cells expressing WT and mutant RIN3 ( , lower panel). Quantitation revealed that the frequencies of RAB5+ puncta >0.1 μm2 significantly (P < 0.05) increased in cells coexpressing RAB5GFP and RIN3 mutants (RQ, PS, or RQPS) compared to RAB5GFP and RIN3WT [ ; RIN3WTflag (WT, gray), RIN3 (RQ)flag (RQ, violet), RIN3 (PS)flag (PS, blue), or RIN3 (RQPS)flag (RQPS, green)].
Although we denoted 0.1- to 0.5-μm2 and 0.5- to 2.0-μm2 RAB5GFP+ve puncta as EEs and eEEs, respectively, we also detected RAB5GFP+ve puncta larger than 2 μm2 (reaching ~15 μm2) in cells overexpressing the RIN3 mutants (RQ, PS, or RQPS). These findings suggest a possible transition of RAB5+ endosomes into multivesicular bodies, which can develop into late endosomes, lysosomes, or autophagosomes. Several studies have identified RAB5 as crucial in regulating the progression to RAB7+ late endosomes and the fusion of multivesicular bodies with lysosomes. This process is vital for maintaining endolysosomal network integrity, and its disruption has been linked to abnormalities observed in the early stages of AD pathogenesis ( , ).
Collectively, these findings show that the two RIN3 missense mutations residing in the BIN1-binding domain are present in combined 14 familial AD families in the NIMH dataset, are significantly associated with AD risk in the Hispanic population of the ADSP dataset, and are in tight LD with the common RIN3 SNPs previously reported in GWAS to exhibit genome-wide significant association with AD risk. Functionally, these missense mutations impair the binding of RIN3 to BIN1, leading to pathological endosomal enlargement.

## BIN1 binding–deficient RIN3 leads to RAB5 hyperactivation in hiPSC neurons
The enlargement of RAB5+ endosomes by RIN3 mutations that impair binding to BIN1 raised the question of whether BIN1-free RIN3 leads to endosomal-lysosomal dysfunction downstream of RAB5 hyperactivation. Notably, both mRNA and protein levels of RIN3 have been reported to be significantly higher in the hippocampus and cortex of APP/PS1 transgenic mice compared to WT mice ( ). To test this and verify our overexpression studies, we performed CRISPR-Cas9 editing to introduce either or both RIN3 mutations into the BIN1-binding domain [RIN3 (RQ), RIN3 (PS), or RIN3 (RQPS)] in neurons derived from hiPSCs. For this purpose, we used the sgRNAs ACGGCCCTGAGGACACGCCC to target chr14: 92,652,308 to 92,652,327 or CGTTCTCTAAGGGAGCTGGG to target chr14: 92,652,471 to 92,652,490 (GRCh38/hg38) and single-stranded oligodeoxynucleotide (ssODN; 120-mer) donor templates containing the desired nucleotide change (G > A or C > T) and a silent mutation creating a restriction enzyme motif for rapid initial PCR-based detection of hiPSC clones harboring the mutations ( ). We next performed a proximity ligation assay (PLA) using Duolink PLA Probes (Millipore) after labeling endogenous BIN1 with anti-BIN1 (mouse) and RIN3 with anti-RIN3 (rabbit) together with the Duolink in situ Detection Reagent Green (following the manufacturer’s protocol). We confirmed that in hiNPCs derived from unedited parental (WT) hiPSCs, endogenous RIN3 efficiently bound to endogenous BIN1 ( , parental, puncta). However, we detected little to no interaction between RIN3 and BIN1 in the hiNPCs derived from the hiPSCs in which the RIN3 mutations were introduced [ , RIN3 (RQ) and RIN3 (RQPS); little or no puncta].
 Next, we evaluated the effect of RIN3 mutations on RAB5 activation in the context of impaired RIN3-BIN1 binding. To this end, we measured levels of active RAB5* in hiNs derived from WT and RIN3-mutant hiPSCs ( ). We observed an approximately threefold increase in active RAB5* levels in hiNs harboring the BIN1 binding–deficient RIN3 (RQ) mutation compared to WT (n = 3, P = 0.023; ). In contrast, the increase in RAB5* levels in cells containing only the RIN3 (PS) mutation was modest and did not reach statistical significance (n = 3, P = 0.076). This result is consistent with our GWAS findings, which identified a nonsignificant association between the rs74074812 (C > T, P477S) SNP and AD risk despite its strong LD with common intronic RIN3 variants previously associated with AD. Notably, hiNs containing both RIN3 mutations (RQPS) showed a highly significant elevation in active RAB5* levels (n = 3, P = 0.003) compared to the R427Q mutation, possibly synergistically, in the presence of the P477S mutation. As expected, RAB5* levels were also significantly increased in BIN1-KO hiNs compared to WT (n = 2, P = 0.0026; ).
To investigate whether the hyperactivation of RAB5 in the presence of the two RIN3 mutations leads to endosomal enlargement, we labeled WT and RIN3 (RQ) hiPSC–derived hiNs with an anti-RAB5 antibody. Confocal microscopy of the iIF images confirmed the presence of RAB5+ puncta ( ). Subsequent Western blot analysis revealed no significant differences in the total RAB5 levels between WT and RIN3 (RQ) hiNs ( ). We next examined the morphological changes in RAB5+ puncta by quantifying the number of EEs (0.1 to 0.5 μm2) and eEEs (0.5 to 2.0 μm2) in randomly selected 5-μm2 areas from each genotype: WT (n = 18 neurons) and RIN3 (RQ) (n = 12 neurons). After thresholding, we applied a masking method to enhance the detection of EEs and eEEs ( , masked). Using ImageJ particle size analysis, we plotted the number of EEs and eEEs from the masked images in violin plots to compare the two genotypes ( ).
We observed a significant (P = 0.002) increase in the number of RAB5+ eEEs in RIN3 (RQ) hiNs as compared to WT hiNs [ ; WT eEEs (gray with stripes) versus RIN3 (RQ) eEEs (purple with stripes)]. Conversely, there was a modest reduction in the number of RAB5+ EEs in RIN3 (RQ) hiNs compared to WT hiNs, but it was not significant (P = 0.966) [ ; WT EEs (gray) versus RIN3 (RQ) EEs (purple)]. Quantitation of these data revealed that 73.9% of puncta in WT and 49.83% in RIN3 (RQ) hiNs (0.22 ± 0.1 and 0.23 ± 0.1 μm2, respectively) represented RAB5+ EEs. In contrast, the average sizes of 26.1% of puncta in WT and 50.16% of puncta in RIN3 (RQ) hiNs were 1.01 ± 0.4 and 1.15 ± 0.44 μm2, respectively, representing RAB5+ eEEs [n ≅ 800 for WT and n ≅ 600 for RIN3 (RQ) from five images]. At the single neuron level (100 μm long), we observed a nearly fourfold increase in eEEs in RIN3 (RQ) hiNs compared to the WT (31.0 ± 5.7 versus 7.9 ± 3.9%, respectively; P = 0.041, n = 3 each genotype, ~10 single neurons).
Thus, inefficient binding of BIN1 to RIN3 owing to the presence of loss-of-function missense mutations in the BIN1-binding domain of RIN3 results in hyperactivation of the RIN3-RAB5 pathway. RIN3-mediated RAB5 hyperactivation leads to a two- to fourfold increase in the number of enlarged RAB5+ endosomes but with no significant changes in total RAB5 levels or RAB5+ EEs. Thus, BIN1 binding to RIN3 normally constrains RIN3-mediated RAB5 hyperactivation, alleviating endosomal-related AD pathology in neurons.
Collectively, the combined genetic and functional findings presented here show that these two RIN3 missense mutations that are in tight LD with GWAS RIN3 SNPs exhibiting genome-wide significant association with AD and that are present in combined 14 late-onset AD families impair RIN3-BIN1 binding and lead to RAB5 hyperactivation and endosomal enlargement, an abnormality observed in early-stage AD pathogenesis.

## BIN1 deficiency alters molecular pathways related to AD pathogenesis
We carried out RNA sequencing of WT and CRISPR-Cas9–edited BIN1-KO hiNs. Differential gene expression analysis (RNA sequencing of two biological replicates in duplicates; 20 million reads per sample) ( , ) identified 11,781 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) and ranked them on the basis of their calculated differential expression levels (table S4). In the “Rank List,” we detected a modest up-regulation of RIN2 with a rank score of 1.98. RIN2 is the closest relative of RIN3 in the Rin family (RIN1, RIN2, and RIN3) ( ) and has the highest expression level in the brain followed by RIN3 and RIN1 ( ). We also detected small up-regulation of RIN3 but with a rank score value <1. The DEG analysis revealed that most of the DEGs are expressed in the brain (fig. S7A).
Applying a false discovery rate (FDR) as 5% (P = 0.05), we identified 330 DEGs, of which 285 were up-regulated and 45 were down-regulated (fig. S7B, volcano plot, and tables S3 and S4). The heatmap of the top 20 up-regulated and down-regulated genes ( ) identified up-regulation of genes such as NR2F1 that plays a role in brain development and is associated with several neurodevelopmental disorders. The top up-regulated genes also include FOXB2 (forkhead box B2), which activates the Wnt (wingless-related) signaling pathway implicated in tissue and organ development.
 BIN1 is a multifunctional effector protein that also interacts with the regulators of RHO GTPases (RHOA and RAC1) via the intrinsic membrane curvature–binding BAR domain to regulate cytoskeletal organization and synaptic plasticity ( ). The ARHGAP (RHO/RAC GAP) family of proteins plays a crucial role in regulating RHO GTPases. Emerging evidence suggests that dysregulation of certain ARHGAP proteins, e.g., ArhGAP11A, mediated Aβ generation and induced neuropathology in an AD-like mouse model ( ). We identified up-regulation of ARHGAP15 and ARHGAP29 ( ). ARHGAP15 has been more directly linked to cognitive deficits and epilepsy in models of neurodegeneration through its regulation of RAC1. Meanwhile, ARHGAP29’s direct role in neurodegenerative disease is less explored. Further research is needed to fully understand the intricate relationship between these RHO GAPs and the pathogenesis of various neurodegenerative diseases, which influences the architecture and function of hippocampal neurons and cognition ( ).
As membrane restructuring depends on lipid or cholesterol remodeling in the lipid bilayer, we also hypothesized that several gene/protein functions regulating lipid metabolism might be altered in the absence of BIN1. FunRich analysis identified a series of RHO and RAS GTPases and their regulators that were highly differentially expressed in BIN1-KO cells ( ). In addition to RHO- and RAS-regulating genes, we detected up-regulation of several DEGs involved in cholesterol homeostasis (e.g., LRP4, LRP1B, SOAT1, APOE, and LRP2) ( ). Low-density lipoprotein receptor–related protein 2 (LRP2) mediates the endocytosis of high-density lipoproteins, while sterol O-acyltransferase 1 (SOAT1) stores cholesterol in the brain by converting free cholesterol to cholesteryl ester (CE), found to be elevated in the AD brain ( – ). We also identified a series of DEGs involved in the WNT/β-catenin pathway, e.g., DKK1, WNT1, and FZD1 ( ).
To validate the DEGs at the transcriptional level, we performed quantitative reverse transcription PCR (RT-qPCR) analysis of the purified mRNA or conducted Western blot analysis of protein extracts from the WT or BIN1-KO hiN. Using GAPDH as an internal control, our RT-qPCR analysis revealed markedly lower cycle threshold (Ct) values of the DEGs LRP2 and FZD1 in BIN1-KO compared to the WT, indicating higher transcript abundance. The analysis revealed ~10- and ~8-fold changes of LRP2 and FZD1, respectively [ ; FC and log2(FC)].
Probing the WT and BIN1-KO hiN extracts with antibodies against several products of the DEGs, including APHGAP15 and SOAT1, we found a significant up-regulation of ARHGAP15 [ ; IB: anti-APH15, P = 0.0164, n = 3, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA)]. In contrast, SOAT1 levels between the WT and BIN1-KO hiNs remained unchanged ( ; IB: anti-SOAT1, P = 0.987, n = 3, one-way ANOVA). We used β-actin as the housekeeping control. We used an additional cholesterol-modifying enzyme ACAT1 (acetyl-coenzyme A acetyltransferase 1), which is closely related to SOAT1 but was not detected among the DEGs in our RNA sequencing analyses. ACAT1 and its homolog ACAT2 were found up-regulated in a cerebral organoid model of tauopathy ( ). As expected, we detected no significant differential expression of either ACAT1 (data not shown) or the housekeeping control β-actin ( ).

## BIN1 deficiency links EMT and WNT-signaling pathways to AD pathogenesis
Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) of the DEGs (FDR = 0.05 or P < 0.05) identified 50 gene sets exhibiting high normalized enrichment scores. Thirty-four gene sets among the 50 were significantly up-regulated, while 16 were down-regulated (tables S6 and S7). Gene Ontology (GO) annotation of the DEGs identified genes encoding proteins significantly enriched in the collagen-containing extracellular matrix (GO: 0062023; P = 1.4 × 10−14) and the lumen of intracellular compartments including the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) (GO: 0005788; P = 6.4 × 10−5) and Golgi (GO: 00016342; P = 1.9 × 10−3). We also detected significant enrichment in the catenin complex (GO: 0016342; FDR = 1.01 × 10−3) ( ).
 The MSigDB (Molecular Signatures Database) identified the “Epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition (EMT) genes” gene set with 173 up-regulated genes, the top ranked pathway (P = 1.2 × 10−11) enriched by DEGs. The EMT pathway has recently been linked to AD pathogenesis ( ). The “Wnt/β-catenin signaling” gene set containing 38 up-regulated genes was also highly enriched (P = 1.9 × 10−8) ( ). PANTHER (Protein Analysis Through Evolutionary Relationships) detected the enrichment of “Cadherin signaling pathway (P00012),” “Wnt-signaling pathway (P00057),” “Alzheimer’s disease-presenilin pathway (P00005),” and “TGF-β (transforming growth factor–β) signaling pathway (P00052)” gene sets exhibiting strong significance (P = 2.8 × 10−9, P = 1.5 × 10−7, P = 3.2 × 10−5, and P = 3.0 × 10−3, respectively) and very high odds ratio (7.60, 4.69, 6.24, and 4.52, respectively) ( ).
Using GSEA of the DEGs (FDR = 0.05 or P < 0.05) examining the genetic connections between drug targets and known disease genes through a statistical analysis of drugs and diseases (MAGMA) ( , ) revealed a strong association of cholesterol-lowering drugs such as atorvastatin and other 3-hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl-coenzyme A reductase inhibitors with genes such as MAPK10, TGM2, and SOAT1 ( ). Analyzing the dbGaP (database of Genotypes and Phenotypes), we found significant association of the DEGs with heart attack (myocardial infarction; P = 2.3 × 10−5), Tau proteins (P = 7.7 × 10−4), and cholesterol (P = 4.3 × 10−3) ( ). Genetic network analysis showed that membrane remodeling and lipid metabolism genes are interconnected, leading to APOE ( ).

## DISCUSSION
We previously reported that the neuronal form of BIN1, BIN1V1, is recruited to RAB5+ EEs and reduces Aβ levels by attenuating APP endocytosis in a RIN3-dependent manner ( ). RAB5+ eEEs are an early pathological hallmark of AD, which we show here to be significantly reduced in the 6-month-old Rin3-CKO mouse brain as compared to WT. However, a 50% loss of RIN3 in the mouse brain did not affect the total or active RAB5 levels or the numbers of RAB5+ EEs, suggesting that the partial loss of RIN3 may perturb endosomal homeostasis, while the impact on RAB5 activity may be compensated by RAB5 GEFs other than RIN3. Notably, RIN3 is a member of VPS9 domain–containing protein family, and at least seven well-characterized RAB5 GEFs have been identified within this family ( ). We also showed in human iPSC–derived neurons (hiNs) that BIN1 KO leads to overactivation of RAB5 owing to the loss of negative regulation of RIN3-mediated activation of RAB5 by BIN1. Furthermore, we described two rare RIN3 missense mutations found in 14 late-onset AD families. Both mutations are in tight LD with common RIN3 SNPs exhibiting genome-wide significant association with AD in GWAS. The two mutations reside in the Bin1-binding domain of RIN3, impairing the binding to BIN1, thus leading to RAB5 hyperactivation and RAB5+ endosomal enlargement. These results suggest that RIN3 is tightly regulated by BIN1 and is a key activator of RAB5 driving the early-stage endosomal pathology in neurons.
Collectively, our findings reveal a direct role for RIN3 in regulating RAB5-mediated endosomal enlargement without altering the total or active RAB5 levels. This distinction is critical, as RAB5-mediated endosomal trafficking is a fundamental process that maintains cellular homeostasis by regulating the internalization and sorting of membrane-bound receptors, nutrients, and signaling molecules. Our data suggest RIN3 as a promising therapeutic target for reducing RAB5-dependent endosomal enlargement in AD without impairing basal RAB5 activity required for normal endosomal maturation and homeostasis.
RAB5 hyperactivation is a major driver of AD pathology ( ). Several signaling pathways involved in membrane trafficking and remodeling via the members of the RAB-GTPase superfamily, such as RAB5 and RAB7, are regulated by the RIN3-binding partners BIN1, CD2AP, CRK (C10 regulator of kinase), and CRKL (CRK-like kinase). BIN1 and CD2AP have garnered increasing interest in AD research because of their strong genetic association with risk and their roles in APP trafficking and Aβ and Tau pathology. RIN3 and CD2AP are localized in the endomembrane, whereas BIN1 is a cytosolic protein recruited to the RAB5+ EEs in a RIN3-dependent manner. This unique characteristic of neuronal BIN1 makes the RIN3-BIN1 interaction a critical regulatory axis in the endosomal function. Overexpression studies in neuronal and nonneuronal cells have previously demonstrated that CD2AP regulates APP degradation in RAB7+ late endosomes/lysosomes ( ). Sequential cooperation between RAB5+ EEs and RAB7+ late endosomes/lysosomes has been shown to determine the maturation of the RAB5+ EEs and, subsequently, RAB7+ late endosomes ( , ). Targeting early endocytic dysfunction regulated by RAB5 may be more impactful than targeting RAB7-mediated late-endosomal/lysosomal abnormalities for preventing early pathological hallmarks of AD ( , – ). Therefore, delineating the pathways and molecular mechanisms regulating both the BIN1-RIN3 and CD2AP-RIN3 axes may help uncover previously unidentified therapeutic targets for the treatment of early-stage AD pathology.
Prior studies have also reported that neuronal BIN1 modulates Tau pathology ( , , , – ). An earlier report using two in vitro models demonstrated that the neuronal BIN1V1, but not the nonneuronal BIN1V9, inhibits the propagation of Tau pathology. BIN1 levels and propagation of Tau pathology have been inversely correlated, i.e., loss of BIN1 promotes Tau pathology, most likely by hyperactivating RAB5 and increasing endocytic flux ( ). Another recent study showed that the BIN1 SNP rs744373, an AD-associated risk variant ( ), is associated with faster Tau accumulation in the presence of Aβ pathology, exacerbating cognitive decline ( ).
APP processing by BACE1 in RAB5+ EEs is the rate-limiting step for Aβ generation. The accumulation of Aβ in the hippocampus and entorhinal cortex is an early event in AD pathogenesis ( ). We previously reported that the neuronal and nonneuronal BIN1 bound to RIN3 with equal affinity, but only the neuronal BIN1V1 slows the endocytosis of APP and reduces Aβ generation and does so in a RIN3-dependent manner. RIN3 mutations that impair binding to BIN1 hyperactivate RAB5 and markedly increase endosomal enlargement without altering the endosomal recruitment of BIN1V1 to RAB5+ endosomes. Thus, both translocation to the RAB5+ EEs and binding to RIN3 are required for the negative regulatory activity of BIN1 in neurons. We propose that RIN3-mediated activation of RAB5 recruits cytoplasmic BIN1V1 to RAB5+ EEs and initiates the formation of the tripartite complex BIN1-RAB5*-RIN3 ( , ) to negatively regulate RIN3-mediated RAB5 activation and prevent the development of enlarged endosomal pathology in early-stage AD. Thus, BIN1 may play a dual role as an effector and a GDI by first being recruited to endosomes by active RAB5* as an effector and subsequently by inhibiting RIN3-mediated RAB5 activation in a feedback-based process. Previous studies have shown that the RAB5 GEF RABEX-5 hyperactivates the RAB5 pathway via a poorly understood feedback loop involving a complex with activated RAB5 ( , ). Dissecting the role of BIN1 as an effector, a GDI, or both in regulating RIN3-mediated activation of RAB5 should serve to facilitate the development of therapeutic strategies targeting BIN1 or RIN3 to prevent AD.
AD pathology begins decades before clinical symptoms, including plaques, tangles, and neuroinflammation. Aβ deposition is considered the initial driver of AD neuropathogenesis, supported by recent clinical success in Aβ immunotherapies, such as Leqembi and Kisunla, which induce microglial clearance of amyloid and slowed cognitive decline in patients with very early-stage AD ( , ). However, the associated safety concerns and the high cost of monitoring the patients to manage potential side effects make anti-Aβ immunotherapy less accessible. The current consensus is that clinical management of Aβ levels would be the most effective if administered at the early stages of the disease, long before symptoms appear. Thus, there is an urgent need to thoroughly understand the precise molecular pathways that regulate AD pathology during the “preclinical” stage of AD.
Emerging evidence suggests that endocytic dysfunction and endolysosomal abnormalities, such as the accumulation of RAB5+ enlarged endosomes in neurons, are early-stage pathological hallmarks of AD ( , – ). RAB5 hyperactivation, observed in a PA-RAB5 mouse model, led to endosomal enlargement and AD-like memory deficits ( ). Regulating RAB5 hyperactivation could be useful for AD therapy. However, therapeutically reducing levels of RAB5 is not feasible because of its essential role in endosome biogenesis and protein trafficking ( – ). In addition to RAB5 GEFs, GAPs, and GDIs ( ), RAB5 activity is tightly regulated through the recruitment of RAB5 effectors to the endosomes to activate downstream endocytic pathways ( ).
Here, we show functionally interactive roles for two known AD genes, BIN1 and RIN3, by showing that BIN1 is a negative regulator of RIN3-mediated RAB5 activation and specifically modulates early-stage endosomal AD pathology in neurons by binding to RIN3 in the endosomes. RIN3-mediated hyperactivation of RAB5 leading to endosomal enlargement in neurons is tightly regulated by the interaction of BIN1 and RIN3 in endosomes. RAB5 induces EE formation and endocytosis, which is tightly regulated by RAB5 GEFs and GDIs ( ). RIN3 is an endomembrane-bound RAB5 GEF that activates RAB5 by exchanging GDP for GTP. We propose that RIN3 hyperactivates RAB5 in the absence of GDI activity, which leads to endosomal enlargement ( ). Moreover, we propose that BIN1, specifically the high-molecular-weight neuronal form BIN1hi, plays a dual role in RAB5-mediated endosomal homeostasis in neurons. First, the cytoplasmic BIN1hi binds to RIN3 and RAB5-GTP, forming a tripartite complex and translocating to RAB5+ EEs ( ). Thus, BIN1 functions as an effector in the RIN3-RAB5 pathway in neurons. Second, binding BIN1 to RIN3 in endosomes leads to inhibition of RIN3-mediated RAB5 activation ( ), suggesting that BIN1 acts as a GDI in this pathway, inhibiting excessive RAB5 activation, thereby preventing endosomal enlargement in neurons.
 In support of this regulatory role, GSEA of WT versus BIN1-KO hiNs showed a significant up-regulation of GTPase-activating genes, such as RABGAP, ARHGAP15, and ARHGAP29. This suggests a compensatory activation mechanism in response to the absence of BIN1 or its interaction with RIN3, potentially to maintain endosomal homeostasis. This compensatory pathway highlights the intricacies of cellular mechanisms that respond to disturbances in key regulatory proteins like BIN1, suggesting that even partial loss of BIN1 function could initiate alternative regulatory pathways with relevance to neurodegeneration.
BIN1 loss up-regulated several overlapping genes in the EMT, WNT/β-catenin, WNT, and cadherin signaling pathways. These include genes in the Frizzled (FZD), Dickkopf (DKK), and Cadherin (CDH) families. FZD8 integrates WNT-11 and TGF-β signals to promote EMT ( ). CDH6 plays a critical role in cell differentiation and morphogenesis that are associated with diseases including AD ( ). Dickkopf WNT-signaling pathway inhibitors (DKKs; the products of DKK gene) are also implicated in bone formation and bone disease, cancer, and AD ( ). DKK1 plays an important role in amyotrophic lateral sclerosis where it locally inhibits WNT-regulated signaling pathways ( ).
The canonical WNT-signaling pathway, also known as the WNT/β-catenin pathway, stabilizes β-catenin protein, leading to its translocation to the nucleus to promote gene transcription. The β-catenin–dependent WNT-signaling pathway plays crucial roles in brain development by regulating proliferation, differentiation, migration, and synaptic formation and has been implicated in AD pathogenesis ( , – ). Our finding indicates that the β-catenin–dependent WNT-signaling pathway is affected by BIN1 deficiency in neurons. WNT signaling is also implicated in the noncanonical pathway regulating cell polarity and migration via small GTPases, such as RHOA and RAC1 ( ). RHO regulatory proteins, specifically the RHO GEFs, play a critical role in endosomal membrane homeostasis and metabolism, leading to molecular changes in neuronal cells [reviewed by Eckenstaler et al. ( )]. RHO GAPs, such as ARHGAP6, ARHGAP29, and ARHGAP24, suppress RHO signaling and play an essential role in blood vessel tubulogenesis ( ). RASGEFs, such as RASGEF18 and RASGEF1B, are mediators of neuronal cell cascades and RAB5 coactivators ( – ).
A particularly noteworthy finding is the significant up-regulation of the SOAT1 gene, which plays a role in cholesterol homeostasis in the brain. Previous studies have demonstrated that the pharmacological inhibitors of SOAT1, also referred to as ACAT1 (CP 113,818 and CI-1011), reduce CE levels and markedly reduce the generation of Aβ in vitro and in vivo ( , ). More recent studies showed that the ACAT1 inhibitors K-604 and F12511 attenuate AD pathology by enhancing Aβ clearance in vitro and in vivo ( ). While the mechanisms of action of these inhibitors are largely unclear, we previously reported that CP 113,818 and CI-1011 lower neuronal CE levels, resulting in a reduction of Aβ production by inhibiting the BACE1-mediated amyloidogenic cleavage of palmitoylated APP, which is a preferential substrate for BACE1 in cholesterol-rich lipid rafts ( ). K-604 and F12511 also increase Aβ clearance by up-regulating microglial intracellular lipid raft-like microdomains found at the ER-mitochondria contact sites, also known as mitochondria-associated (ER) membranes ( , ). K-604 has also been shown to restore Aβ clearance by reducing microglial CE levels in models deficient in AD risk genes such as TREM2 or APOE ( ). Given that cholesterol imbalance is one of the earliest observed disruptions in AD, understanding the influence of BIN1 in these pathways may provide vital insights into the cellular and molecular events that trigger the onset of AD.
In summary, here, we have shown the following: (i) Rin3-CKO mice exhibit decreased levels of RAB5+ enlarged endosomes in the brain. (ii) BIN1-KO leads to hyperactivation of RAB5 in hiNs. BIN1-KO leads to endosomal enlargement in hiNs. (iii) Two RIN3 missense mutations found to be overrepresented in AD cases in late-onset AD families are in tight LD with common RIN3 SNPs that exhibit genome-wide significance for association with AD risk by GWAS. (iv) These two RIN3 mutations reside in the BIN1-binding domain of RIN3 and impair binding to BIN1, leading to hyperactivation of RAB5 and pathological endosomal enlargement. The data reveal familial AD loss-of-function RIN3 missense mutations that impair the binding of RIN3 to BIN1, leading to RAB5 hyperactivation and endosomal enlargement, an abnormality observed in AD pathogenesis. Our findings suggest RIN3-BIN1 binding and the effects on RAB5 activation as a potentially promising target pathway for addressing early-stage endosomal enlargement and associated pathology in AD.

## MATERIALS AND METHODS

## Experimental models

## 
hiPSC-based neural culture model

The pluripotency of hiPSCs allows for in vitro models that closely mimic human physiological conditions. We obtained two hiPSC lines from male (M) and female (F) healthy donors. We generated hiNPCs from hiPSCs and differentiated these into mature cortical neurons, following the method described by Tchieu et al. ( ), with modifications. Briefly, hiPSCs were plated at a density of 250,000 to 300,000 cells per cm2 in mTeSR Plus medium (StemCell Technologies) supplemented with 10 μM Y-27632 (StemCell Technologies). The cells were incubated for 24 hours at 37°C to facilitate adherence. At day 0 of the hiNPC generation, hiPSC E6 media (Gibco) containing 500 nM LDN193189 (Apexbio Technology LLC), 10 μM SB431542 (Selleck Chemical LLC), and 5 μM XAV939 (Sigma-Aldrich) were added to the cells. On day 3, the media were changed to E6 media containing 500 nM LDN193189 and 10 μM SB431542 . Media were changed every other day. Starting on day 10, the cultures were maintained for an additional 10 days in N2B27 medium consisting of Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM)/F-12 (Gibco) supplemented with d-(+)-glucose (1.6 g/liter; Sigma-Aldrich) and 1× B-27 supplement (Gibco). On day 21, the cells were dissociated with Accutase (Gibco) to generate a single-cell suspension and passaged at a 1:6 ratio for further expansion or differentiation into cortical neurons. hiNPCs were maintained at a high density in N2B27 medium supplemented with epidermal growth factor (EGF) (20 ng/ml; Sigma-Aldrich) and basic fibroblast growth factor (bFGF) (Gibco) for expansion. Growth factors were withdrawn from the culture to initiate differentiation into cortical neurons. While most of the differentiated cells are Tuj1-positive neurons, ~10% of the cells were GFAP-positive astrocytes, consistent with another report ( ). We denote the hiPSC-derived neuronal culture as hiNs. Notably, hiPSCs, hiNPCs, and hiNs were all cultured in tissue culture plates or glass/plastic coverslips coated with 1:100 (v/v) Matrigel (354234, BD Biosciences) in DMEM/F12 (Gibco) ( ).

## 
3D human neural cellular models of AD

We used a clonally selected human neural progenitor (ReN)–derived culture system expressing GFP and FAD (familial AD) mutations in the APP gene (ReN-GA). These cells recapitulate AD pathology, namely, Aβ oligomer–driven neurofibrillary tangles when differentiated in a three-dimensional (3D) matrix ( – ). We also used naïve ReN cells (Millipore). ReN cells (naïve and ReN-GA) were maintained in DMEM/F12 supplemented with l-glutamine (Gibco), serum-free B-27 supplement (Gibco), bFGF, and EGF. For differentiation, the cells were suspended in Matrigel (1:10) to form a 3D matrix and differentiated for 10 days in culture media supplemented with media without bFGF or EGF, as described previously ( ).

## 
N2A cell culture

We used N2A cells grown in DMEM (Gibco) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum, penicillin (100 U/ml), and streptomycin (100 μg/ml), as previously described ( ).

## 
Mouse models

We generated Rin3 constitutive KO mice (Rin3-CKO) in collaboration with Taconic Biosciences in which exon 3 (transcript NM_177620.4) is flanked by LoxP sites (the size of the LoxP-flanked region is ~2.0 kb). Briefly, the mouse genomic homology arms and the LoxP-flanked region containing exon 3 of the Rin3 gene were derived from bacterial artificial chromosomes of the C57BL/6J RPCI-23 bacterial artificial chromosome library. The targeting construct was designed to include a puromycin resistance cassette flanked by Flp recognition target sites, inserted into intron 2 to enable positive selection in mouse embryonic stem (ES) cells. For negative selection, a thymidine kinase cassette was placed downstream of the 3′ homology arm. C57BL/6N Taconic ES cells (Art B6/3.6), validated for genomic stability, were electroporated with the linearized targeting vector and subjected to dual selection with puromycin and ganciclovir according to standard protocols. A total of 312 puromycin-resistant ES cell clones were initially screened by PCR to identify homologous recombination events. Candidate clones were further validated by Southern blot analysis of genomic DNA digested with appropriate restriction enzymes using 5′ and 3′ external probes, as well as an internal probe recognizing the puromycin resistance cassette. The presence of the LoxP sites was confirmed by PCR and sequencing. Three correctly targeted clones were injected into BALB/c blastocysts (3.5 days post coitum), which were subsequently transferred into pseudopregnant NMRI females. Chimerism was determined on the basis of coat color contribution from the ES cells. Highly chimeric males were bred with Tg(CAG-flpe) deleter mice to excise the puromycin cassette and generate heterozygous mice carrying the conditional Rin3 floxed allele. For generation of a constitutive Rin3 KO allele, mice were crossed with Gt(ROSA)26Sor(cre) deleter animals. Proper allele configurations were verified by PCR genotyping.
We obtained nontransgenic WT and transgenic AD mice (5XFAD) from the Jackson Laboratory. Validation of KO mice was confirmed by genotyping. All animal experiments were performed following the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) animal protocol (no. 2023N000052) and NIH guidelines, including the use of ARRIVE (Animal Research: Reporting of In Vivo Experiments) guidelines for reporting animal studies (species, strain, sex, age, source, genetic modifications, housing, and diet).

## 
Behavioral OFTs

Three days before the experiments, handling tubes were placed in the cage for at least 5 min, and the mice were transferred via these handling tunnels to a new cage and transferred back to their home cage. The training was conducted once a day for three consecutive days. The handling tubes were removed from the cages after each training. On the day of the experiment, all mice were acclimated to the room for at least 30 min before the OFT started. For the test, mice were transported via handling tubes one at a time, placed in the corner of an empty plastic arena (50 cm by 50 cm), and allowed to move freely for 10 to 30 min. The locomotor activity and moving paths of the mice were recorded for the analysis of the video. After the test, each mouse was transferred back to its home cage. The brains were removed. Isolated brains were bisected longitudinally, and hemispheres were separated. The left hemisphere was stored and frozen on dry ice for biochemical studies. The right hemisphere was stored in a 30% sucrose solution for immunostaining.

## 
CRISPR-Cas9–mediated KO of BIN1 in hiPSCs

The sgRNA sequences (table S1) were designed using an online tool (www.benchling.com/) and selected on the basis of their on-target score ( ) and minimizing the predicted off-target effects. To generate BIN1-KO lines, the WT hiPSC male cell line (subject ID NDS00159) and female cell line (subject ID NDS00242) obtained from the National Institute of Neurological Disorders and Stroke (NINDS) were electroporated with a ribonucleoprotein complex, consisting of TrueCut Cas9 protein v2 (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and the corresponding sgRNA, in a 1:5 molar ratio using the Nucleofector 2b device (Lonza). Following electroporation, cells were cultured in mTeSR Plus (StemCell Technologies) supplemented with 1× CloneR (StemCell Technologies) and allowed to recover for 48 to 72 hours. Single cell–derived colonies were isolated, and identification of clones with a frameshift insertion/deletion was carried out by Sanger sequencing performed at the Massachusetts General Hospital (MGH) DNA sequencing core facility. Last, protein depletion was confirmed via Western blot analysis using a recombinant monoclonal BIN1 antibody (Abcam, ab182562).

## 
Knockin of RIN3 mutations in human iPSCs

Homozygous RIN3 knockin (RIN3-KI) hiPSC lines were generated via CRISPR-Cas9–mediated editing of the WT hiPSC male cell line (NINDS, subject ID NDS00159). The 120-mer ssODN donor repair templates (table S1) were designed with homologous genomic flanking sequences centered on the predicted CRISPR-Cas9 cut site. The ssODNs included the mutation of interest and a silent mutation that created a unique restriction enzyme motif for positive clone selection. The ribonucleoprotein complex, comprising the Cas9 protein and sgRNA, was used in a molar ratio of 1:5:2 with the ssODN. Single cell–derived colonies were analyzed to confirm the successful introduction of the genetic variants via homology-directed repair. PCR products from the selected clones were amplified using the respective primers (table S1) and subjected to restriction enzyme digestion (table S2). The resulting digestion patterns were compared to untreated controls to confirm the desired edits. Subsequently, Sanger sequencing was performed to further validate editing accuracy.

## 
RAB5 activation assay

We performed the RAB5 activation assay following the method described by Pensalfini et al. ( ), with modification. Twelve-day mature hiNs in 3D were lysed using 50 mM tris-HCl, pH 7.5, 250 mM NaCl, 5 mM Mg acetate, 0.5% Triton X-100, and protease inhibitors. We immunoprecipitated total RAB5 using an anti-RAB5 rabbit polyclonal antibody (Abcam, ab18211) and probed it with a mouse monoclonal antibody against active RAB5-GTP (anti-RAB5*, NewEast Biosciences, 26911). We validated the specificity of the anti-RAB5* antibody using the RAB5 Activation Assay Kit (ab173259) following the manufacturer’s protocol, with modification. N2A cells overexpressing mCherry epitope–tagged RAB5 (RAB5mCherry) were used. The cells were lysed in lysis buffer not containing SDS (150 mM NaCl, 1% NP-40 or Triton X-100, 50 mM tris, pH 8.0, supplemented with protease inhibitors). A chemical reaction was carried out with equal amounts of each lysate by adding 10 μM GDP or GTP-γ-S at 30°C for 30 min before IP of RAB5 with an anti-RAB5 antibody at 4°C overnight (ON). GDP stabilizes inactive RAB5 (negative control).
Whereas GTP-γ-S is a nonhydrolyzable G protein–activating analog of GTP, it stabilizes active RAB5-GTP (positive control). After ON incubation, the antigen (RAB5)-antibody (anti-RAB5) complex was pulled down with protein A/G agarose beads (IP10, Millipore-Sigma). The beads were washed three times for 10 min with the lysis buffer and resuspended in 1× Laemmli sample buffer containing 1% SDS, 5% β-mercaptoethanol, 20% glycerol, and 0.004% bromophenol blue in 0.125 M tris-HCl, pH 6.8. SDS denatures RAB5, which makes anti-RAB5* unsuitable for detecting RAB5 or RAB5*. However, immunoblotting the immunoprecipitated samples (1% SDS) using anti-RAB5*, we detected a robust amount of RAB5mCherry from cells treated with GTP-γ-S but not GDP, confirming the specificity of anti-RAB5* for active RAB5-GTP. Notably, immunoblotting of the lysates, as opposed to IP, with an anti-RAB5* antibody did not detect RAB5-GTP in the cell lysates, as expected.

## 
Immunocytochemistry

After completing behavioral tasks, the WT and Rin3-CKO mice were perfused with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), and then right hemispheres were placed in a solution containing 4% PFA and 2% glutaraldehyde in sodium cacodylate buffer. The brains were dissected, and the left hemispheres were frozen for biochemical analysis. The right hemispheres were cut into 40-nm-thick sections using a microtome. The sections were placed in a cryoprotectant containing monobasic sodium phosphate, dibasic sodium phosphate, glycerol, and ethylene glycol; washed three times for 10 min with PBS; and blocked using buffer containing 5% donkey serum and 0.3% Triton X in PBS for 1 hour. Sections were incubated with primary antibodies in antibody dilution buffer ON at 4°C, followed by incubation with the appropriate Donkey Alexa Fluor–conjugated secondary antiserum mixtures (1:600; Thermo Fisher Scientific) for 1 hour at room temperature. Immunolabeling of EEs was performed using rabbit anti-RAB5 (1:100) and mouse monoclonal anti-RAB5* (1:100). Hoechst was used to label the nuclei.

## 
Indirect immunofluorescence (iIF)

hiPSC-derived hiNPCs or hiNs were immunostained with primary antibodies against RAB5, RAB5-GTP, BIN1, or RIN3 [anti-RAB5 (Abcam), anti-RAB5* (NewEast Biosciences), anti-BIN1 (Invitrogen, 3B6F10), or anti-RIN3 (Proteintech, 12709-1-AP), respectively] followed by appropriate Alexa-conjugated secondary antibodies as described in our previously published protocol ( ).

## Counting of RAB5+ puncta
Coronal sections containing both the cortex and hippocampus were used to quantify RAB5- and RAB5-GTP–positive puncta in the mouse brain. For the coronal sections, 30 to 50 neurons per animal (n = 3 per genotype) were analyzed from 40× magnification images following the method described by Pensalfini et al. ( ). Image analysis was performed using Fiji/ImageJ software after thresholding (Command: Threshold) and masking (Command: Convert to Mask). RAB5 and RAB5* punctum intensity and size were determined using the “Analyze Particle” command, setting the size limits of 0.1 to 0.5 μm2 or 0.5 to 2.0 μm2, representing RAB5+ EEs or eEEs, respectively.
The hiN, anti-RAB5, and anti-RAB5* antibody–stained confocal images were divided into four equal sections each (total >5 images from each phenotype). Images were opened using Fiji/ImageJ, and Watershed was applied. Next, we used the “Analyze Particle” command, setting the size limits to 0.1 to 0.5 μm2 or 0.5 to 2.0 μm2, as before. RAB5+ EEs and eEEs were also counted from individual neurons after “straightening” the neurons using the ImageJ “Straighten” command. More than 20 neurons were counted for each phenotype (n = 3 mice). All data are expressed as the means ± standard error of the mean (SEM) relative to WT and represented as bar graphs showing individual data points for each mouse.

## 
Identification of RIN3 mutations in late-onset AD families

WGS of the NIMH AD family cohort was performed using the HiSeq 2000 System at Illumina Inc. and has been previously described ( , ). We performed sample quality control and variant quality control on the basis of identity by descent, genotyping rate, inbreeding coefficient, Mendel errors, and Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium. Variant calls in vcf format for the National Institute of Aging (NIA) ADSP cohort were obtained from the National Institute on Aging Genetics of Alzheimer’s Disease Data Storage Site (NIAGADS) under accession number NG00067.v9. GEMINI and SNPEff tools were used to perform functional annotation of the variants. In family-based datasets, we used the family-based association test ( ) to perform association analysis with an offset of 0.15. The case-control portion of NIA ADSP was divided into four subpopulations on the basis of self-assignment: non-Hispanic white (NHW), African American (AA), Hispanic ethnicity (HISP), and Asian (ASIA). We verified the population assignment on the basis of principal components analysis using the Jaccard matrix and removed outliers that were more than 5 standard deviations (SD) away from the mean on the basis of each of the first 10 principal components (PCs). PCs were calculated on the basis of an LD-pruned subset of rare variants using the Jaccard index ( ). For case-control datasets, we used PLINK to perform logistic regression in each subpopulation with covariates (age, sex, sequencing center, sample set, and five PCs to adjust for the population structure). Age was not included as a covariate in the NHW subpopulation because of the study design in this group: Controls were much older than cases, which introduced confounding between age and affection status.

## 
In silico protein modeling

Interaction modeling was done in the SwissProt server (https://swissmodel.expasy.org/). The RIN3 (Q8TB24.1, 985 amino acids) amino acid sequence was submitted/used as input, and it resulted in numerous templates from the database for model building. The crystal structure for full-length RIN3 was available, and subsequently, independent models for mutant RIN3 were built by replacing arginine (R) with glutamine (Q) at 427 residue or proline (P) with serine (S) at 477 residues. The BIN1 (O00499_BIN1_HUMAN) amino acid sequence was used to build the interaction model. BIN1’s full sequence structure was taken from the database and used as a template. Next, RAB5 bound to GDP (1tu4.1.A, 2 to 169 amino acids) was selected to build the RAB5 model. RIN3wt/R427Q/P477S was superimposed with RAB5-GDP, BIN1, or both.

## 
Co-immunoprecipitation (co-IP)

Co-IP assay in cells expressing GFP epitope–tagged BIN1V1GFP without or with the WT or mutant flag epitope–tagged RIN3 [RIN3wtflag, RIN3(RQ), (PS), or (RQPS)flag] was performed following the previously described method ( ). Briefly, RIN3 and its mutants were immunoprecipitated with a human RIN3-specific anti-RIN3 rabbit polyclonal antibody (Sigma-Aldrich, HPA039836) and immunoblotted with an anti-BIN1 mouse monoclonal antibody (Invitrogen, 3B6F10).

## 
PLA interaction in CRISPR-edited hiPSCs

PLA conducted with Duolink PLA reagents can detect and visualize protein interactions in fixed cells at the single-molecule level. We performed PLA assay on hiNPCs derived from the WT hiPSCs or CRISPR-Cas9–edited hiPSCs containing mutations in RIN3 [RIN3(RQ), RIN3(PS), or RIN3(RQPS)] using Duolink PLA assay reagents (Sigma-Aldrich), with modification. Briefly, hiNPCs plated on coverslips were fixed in 3.6% PFA and washed three times for 10 min with cell permeabilization antibody diluting buffer (1% bovine serum albumin, 0.1% gelatin, 0.1% Triton X-100, and 0.05% Tween 20 in PBS containing calcium and magnesium) for 45 min. The permeabilized cells were incubated at 4°C ON with the primary antibodies, mouse monoclonal anti-BIN1, and rabbit polyclonal anti-RIN3 antibodies prepared in antibody diluting buffer (1:1000). After removing the primary antibodies, the cells were washed three times for 10 min with antibody wash buffer (0.1% Triton X-100 and 0.05% Tween 20 in PBS containing calcium and magnesium). Next, cells were incubated with Duolink In Situ PLA Probe Anti-Rabbit PLUS (DUO92002, Millipore-Sigma) and anti-Mouse MINUS (DUO92004, Millipore-Sigma) at 1:100 dilution for 45 min. Cells were then subjected to in situ polymerization assay using Duolink In Situ Detection Reagents Green (DUO92014), following the manufacturer’s protocol. After polymerization, cells were washed three times for 10 min with the wash buffer from the reagent kit and labeled with Hoechst. The coverslips were mounted on antifade mounting media (ProLong Gold antifade reagent, P36930, Invitrogen) and imaged by confocal microscopy. The detection probes contain a fluorophore (λex = 495 nm and λem = 527 nm), which can be visualized using the filter for fluorescein isothiocyanate.

## 
RNA sequencing analysis

The WT and BIN1-KO hiPSCs were differentiated for 12 days. Total RNA was isolated from the differentiated hiNs. RNA sequencing libraries were prepared and sequenced in duplicates by pooling samples from two independently generated WT and BIN1-KO hiNs. Each fastq file was assessed for quality using FASTQC. The adaptors were clipped by TRIMMOMATIC ( ). The processed forward and reverse sequences were aligned to the human genome (GRCh38) using STAR ( ). The generated bam files were used for feature counts. The differential gene expression analysis was performed using DESeq2 in R ( ). Subsequently, a principal components analysis plot, volcano plot, and differential gene expression list were generated in R using DESeq2.

## 
Quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR)

Total RNA was isolated from the WT or BIN1-KO hiNs using an RNA extraction kit (Qiagen). cDNA was synthesized from 1 μg of RNA using a reverse transcription kit, and quantitative PCR was performed using TaqMan chemistry. The gene-specific primers targeting LRP2, FZD1, and the reference gene GAPDH were used for RT-qPCR analysis. Each reaction was run in technical triplicate (n = 3). The Ct values were obtained using the instrument’s default threshold settings. Relative expression levels were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method, with GAPDH as the endogenous control.

## 
Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA)

The sorted DEG gene set was used for GSEA (Broad Institute). Briefly, the expression file and a condition file (WT and BIN1-KO) was loaded in GSEA against the search in Hallmark.all.v2024.1.Hs.symbols.gmt databases.
