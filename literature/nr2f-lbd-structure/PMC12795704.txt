## Abstract
One of the major metabolic functions of glucocorticoids (GC) is to maintain circulating glucose levels during stress, as glucose is the preferred energy source for the brain. Because of their potent anti-inflammatory and immune modulatory activities, GC are frequently used to treat inflammatory and autoimmune diseases. Chronic GC exposure, which can be a result of long-term GC pharmacotherapy and prolonged stress, however, causes undesired adverse effects that include hyperglycemia and insulin resistance. These adverse effects limit the application of GC therapy. GC act through an intracellular GC receptor (GR), a transcriptional regulator, to modulate the transcriptional rate of specific genes to exert physiological responses. The liver is a major target tissue of GC to modulate glucose homeostasis. In this review, we discuss the mechanisms of GR-activated transcription of genes involved in glucose metabolism and how hepatic GR primary target genes participate in the regulation of insulin sensitivity and glucose homeostasis. Transcriptional coregulators involved in GR-regulated transcription of glucose metabolism genes and signaling pathways specifically activated upon chronic GC exposure to induce glucose disorders are introduced. Metabolic profiles of liver-specific GR knockout mice are also reviewed. Finally, individual-specific GC responses and mechanisms underlying these phenomena are discussed. Overall, more extensive studies of the mechanisms of GR-regulated hepatic glucose homeostasis not only will expand our knowledge of the regulation of metabolic homeostasis but are also critical for developing improved GC pharmacotherapy and novel approaches to tackle metabolic disorders by targeting GR.
Keywords: glucocorticoids, glucocorticoid receptor, gluconeogenesis, glucose metabolism, insulin resistance, liver

## Essential Points
Glucocorticoids are steroid hormones that have anti-inflammatory properties and are used as a therapeutic; they are secreted in times of stress, such as fasting, to maintain circulating glucose levels
Glucocorticoids exert their actions through the glucocorticoid receptor, which is a transcriptional regulator that modulates the transcription of genes involved in glucose homeostasis
The glucocorticoid receptor works with coregulators and other DNA binding transcription factors (also known as accessory factors) at glucocorticoid response units on the promoter or the enhancer of target genes
There are 2 categories of glucocorticoid primary target genes that regulate glucose homeostasis: genes that directly encode a gluconeogenic enzyme and genes that encode proteins that regulate hepatic gluconeogenesis in an independent manner
Chronic glucocorticoid exposure, such as when taken as a therapeutic, leads to adverse metabolic effects including increased hepatic glucose production, hyperglycemia, and insulin resistance
Specific signaling pathways that enhance adverse metabolic effects are induced specifically by chronic glucocorticoid exposure
Further understanding of human genetic variants that affect glucocorticoid receptor functions and contribute toward individual-specific glucocorticoid responsiveness could provide novel insights for developing improved glucocorticoid therapy

## Glucocorticoids (GC) and Hepatic Glucose Metabolism
Glucocorticoids (GC) are steroid hormones that play a key role in metabolic adaptation upon stress, such as fasting and starvation. The name “glucocorticoid” (portmanteau of glucose, cortex, and steroid) originates from the early observation of the profound effects of these hormones on the induction of circulating glucose levels. Indeed, one of the major metabolic functions of GC during stress is to preserve blood glucose levels. To achieve this, GC promote gluconeogenesis, mainly in the liver. The ability of GC to increase gluconeogenesis is important for the ability of mammals to survive during stress ( ).
GC regulate hepatic gluconeogenesis through several mechanisms. First, GC affect the availability and the function of other hormones that modulate the rate of gluconeogenesis. GC have been shown to control the circulating glucagon levels. Adrenalectomized rats have decreased glucagon levels and glucagon-driven production of glucose ( ). Treatment with dexamethasone, a synthetic glucocorticoid, raised basal plasma glucagon levels in humans ( ). Second, GC increase the availability of substrates for hepatic gluconeogenesis by inducing lipolysis in white adipose tissue and proteolysis in skeletal muscle ( , ). These processes lead to an increase of glycerol and amino acids, respectively, to be shunted into the gluconeogenic pathway ( , ). Third, in hepatocytes, GC enhance the rate of the gluconeogenic pathway by increasing the transcription of genes encoding gluconeogenic enzymes. GC also inhibit the action of insulin, which suppresses gluconeogenesis, in part by reducing the transcription of gluconeogenic gene transcription.
Besides gluconeogenesis, glycogen breakdown (glycogenolysis) is another mechanism to maintain plasma glucose levels. In fact, during fasting, glycogenolysis occurs first. When glycogen levels are reduced, gluconeogenesis becomes a major pathway to supply glucose in circulation. Glycogen phosphorylase mediates glycogenolysis while glycogen synthase catalyzes glycogenesis ( ). In the liver, however, GC increase glycogen storage instead of promoting glycogen breakdown. Adrenalectomy has no effect on active hepatic glycogen phosphorylase levels in rats but rather decreased the levels of inactive phosphorylase ( ). Another study in fasted adrenalectomized rats found a loss in activation of glycogen synthase compared to fasted wild-type (WT) mice ( ). Administration of prednisolone succinate, a synthetic glucocorticoid, increased liver glycogen levels in both fasted and ad libitum mice ( ). These studies suggest that GC promote glycogen storage in the liver, though the mechanism underlying this glucocorticoid effect is unclear. Whereas the function of glucagon is an immediate response to fasting by increasing blood glucose levels, the goal of GC is to overall have more glucose available during durations of stress. Increasing glycogen synthesis is a way to store more glucose and thus have glucose readily available in the body. Thus, GC regulation of hepatic glycogen metabolism differs from that of glucagon. Also, GC play a different role in the regulation of glycogen metabolism in other tissues, such as skeletal muscle. This has been discussed in other articles ( ).
Interestingly, GC have also been shown to play a role in postprandial glucose metabolism. Using liver-specific glucocorticoid receptor (GR) knockout (KO) mice, a subset of GR-dependent hepatic feeding-induced genes is identified, including glucokinase (Gck), a rate-limiting enzyme in hepatic glycolysis ( ). Liver-specific GR KO mice have impaired glucose uptake, phosphorylation, and glycogen storage during the fed state ( ). Notably, long-term GC treatment also promotes glycolysis in rodent studies ( ). However, whether increased glycolysis contributes to chronic GC-induced glucose disorders is unclear.

## Pharmacological Use of GC and Hepatic Glucose Homeostasis
GC have been used clinically for their potent anti-inflammatory and immunomodulatory properties ( ). In 2013, about 1% of the population in the United States took oral GC as a therapeutic regimen ( ). This percentage has continued to increase and is up to 2% to 4% in other areas such as the United Kingdom, Denmark, and Iceland ( ). The prevalence of GC use as a therapeutic is increased among the elderly ( ). Notably, chronic GC exposure, such as when taking GC as a therapeutic or under prolonged stress, leads to metabolic side effects including an increase in hepatic gluconeogenesis, which leads to hyperglycemia and insulin resistance. Compared to short-term cyclical GC treatment, patients who take GC continuously over the course of 6 weeks have a 50% higher incidence of developing hyperglycemia ( ). An analysis of patients admitted to Oxford University Hospitals between 2013 and 2023 found that 1.8% of patients treated with GC developed new-onset hyperglycemia ( ). GC are also used as an anti-inflammatory after organ transplant. A study identified that 87% of posttransplant patients without prior diabetes experienced hyperglycemia, and 66% required insulin treatment from GC therapy after kidney transplantation ( ). The impacts of therapeutic GC use have been reviewed in detail ( , , ).

## GR Is a Transcriptional Regulator
GC exert their physiological effects mainly through an intracellular GR. Without ligands, GR is sequestered in the cytoplasm, bound to a heat shock protein 90 containing chaperone complex ( , ). Upon binding to GC, GR releases from the chaperone complex and translocates into the nucleus, where it directly associates with genomic glucocorticoid response elements (GREs) and recruits transcriptional coregulators to modulate the transcription of nearby genes. Notably, the regulation of gene transcription by GR can be modulated by other transcription factors that bind nearby ( ). These DNA binding transcription factors are called accessory factors, as they are required for GR to confer the maximal GC response ( ). Thus, GREs and these accessory factor binding sites form a glucocorticoid response unit (GRU) ( ) or a composite GRE ( ). The transcription factors that act as accessory factors for GR are discussed later. GR can also modulate gene transcription through a tethering mechanism ( ). In this case, GR does not directly bind to DNA but is recruited to genomic loci by interacting with DNA binding transcription factors to enhance or repress transcription ( ).
 Several genetic variants of the human Nr3c1 (also known as GR) gene have been identified and are summarized in . Metabolic phenotypes are described for the single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) rs6195, rs41423247, and rs6189/rs6190. Rs6195 (also known as N363S; human GR amino acid 363 changes from N to S) and rs41423247 (also known as BclI; in intron 2 of human Nr3c1 gene, a guanine can be substituted for a cytosine)] have been associated with increased GC sensitivity ( , ) and, unsurprisingly, cause worsened metabolic outcomes. Individuals with N363S have increased body mass index and increased insulin response after GC treatment. In the meantime, individuals with the BclI have increased obesity in the abdominal region and potentially affected skeletal bone sensitivity, although more studies are needed to confirm its role on bone mineral density ( ). Notably, the increased GC sensitivity, however, can be beneficial in terms of therapeutics. Pediatric patients with the BclI that were diagnosed with inflammatory bowel disease had better results with GC therapy compared to patients without the polymorphism ( ). A study in an elderly population (53-82 years old) of individuals with rs6189/rs6190 (also known as ER22/23EK; in exon 2 of the Nr3c1 gene, which are linked mutations where rs6189 is a silent mutation and rs6190 is R23K, a substitute of arginine to lysine at codon 23) SNPs had decreased GC sensitivity and thus had lower fasting insulin and higher endogenous cortisol levels ( ). Mice genocopying rs6190 have been generated ( ). These mice have increased muscle insulin sensitivity and reduced obesity-induced adverse effects and exercise intolerance ( ). It is intriguing that the R24K substitution of mouse GR (R23K in human GR) results in an increased dexamethasone-induced nuclear localization of GR ( ). This R24K mutation causes a gain of transactivation and the activation of unique target genes, such as Foxc1 and Arid5a ( ). Thus, this mutation alters the behavior of GR. The induction of these 2 genes in skeletal muscle have been shown to confer positive metabolic effects for these rs6190-genocopying mice ( ). It is unclear whether this SNP affects hepatic glucose metabolism. However, the studies in the liver of rs6190-genocopying mice show that cholesterol metabolism is modulated through the enhanced GR activation of Pcsk9 and Bhlhe40 ( ). This contributes to the elevated levels of cholesterol and severity of atherosclerotic lesions on the proatherogenic hAPOE*2/*2 background ( ). Indeed, rs6190 is associated with increased cholesterol in women according to the UK Biobank and All Of Us datasets ( ).
 The metabolic phenotypes of many other GR variants have not been reported. A small cohort of pediatric patients with Crohn's disease indicated the rs6196, rs6190, rs7701443, and rs860457 was associated with resistance to GC therapy ( ). The exact mechanisms of these SNPs have yet to be identified. Another SNP, rs6198, leads to increased expression of GRβ, the dominant negative GR splice variant that lacks helix 12 and is thus incapable of binding GC ( ). Rs6198 causes a reduced GC sensitivity and is associated with increased risk of rheumatoid arthritis, altered blood pressure, and depression ( ). The SNP rs10482605 (located in the promoter and a nucleotide change of thymine to cytosine) negatively regulated the expression of GRα, the transcriptionally active splice variant of the Nr3c1 gene ( , ). The promoter carrying this minor C allele had weaker promoter activity based on the reporter gene assays in human brain cells ( ). The rs33388/33389 SNPs (located in intron 2) increase the expression of GRγ ( ). GRγ is an alternative isoform that has an extra arginine encoded in the DNA binding domain ( ). A report identified increased expression of GRγ in pediatric leukemia patients, who were GC resistant, while another study demonstrated no differences in GC sensitivity in cells ( , ). GRγ showed limited activity in the activation of reporter genes that harbor known GREs in cell culture experiments ( ). Microarray and chromatin immunoprecipitation sequencing (ChIP-seq) experiments showed that GRα and GRγ have overlapping but not identical target genes ( ). Thus, the role of GRγ on GC sensitivity is still unclear. It is important to note that how human Nr3c1 SNPs contribute to hepatic glucose homeostasis is largely unclear as their effects on GR action in hepatocytes has not been studied.
In this article, we review (1) mechanisms of GR-regulated transcription of genes encoding glucose metabolism enzymes, (2) how hepatic GR primary target genes that do not encode enzymes in glucose metabolism participate in the modulation of gluconeogenesis, (3) the role of transcriptional coregulators that are involved in GR-activated gluconeogenic gene transcription, (4) the signaling pathways specifically induced by chronic GC exposure that regulate hepatic gluconeogenesis, (5) the phenotypes of liver-specific GR KO mice, and (6) individual-specific responses to glucocorticoid treatment.

## Mechanisms of GR-regulated Transcription of Genes Encoding Glucose Metabolism Enzymes
Glucose is synthesized using several substrates, including pyruvate, glycerol, lactate, and gluconeogenic amino acids, especially alanine and glutamine, through an enzymatic regulated process. Lactate, a product of glycolysis, and certain amino acids can be converted to pyruvate to enter the gluconeogenic pathway ( ). Pyruvate carboxylase (Pcx) converts pyruvate to oxaloacetate, which is then converted to phosphoenolpyruvate by phosphoenolpyruvate carboxykinase (Pck1) ( ). Both are key steps in gluconeogenesis. Two other key steps in gluconeogenesis include the conversion of fructose-1,6 bisphosphate to fructose-6-phosphate (F6P) by fructose-1,6-bisophosphatase (Fbp1) and the removal of the phosphate group from glucose-6-phosphate (G6P) by glucose-6-phosphatase (G6pc1) ( ). Later we discuss the mechanisms of GR transcriptional activation of genes encoding these 4 enzymes and 6-phosphofructo-2-kinase/fructose-2,6-bisphosphatase (Pfkfb), solute carrier family 37 member 4 (Slc37a4), pyruvate dehydrogenase kinase 4 (Pdk4), and tyrosine aminotransferase (Tat), which also participate in gluconeogenesis. lists GR primary target genes that have been shown to be involved in gluconeogenesis, including these gluconeogenic genes.
 

## Pck1
The mechanism of transcriptional activation of the rat Pck1 gene by GR has been extensively analyzed. The rat Pck1 gene transcription is activated by a GRU, which consists of 2 GREs and accessory factor-binding sites ( ) for other transcription factors that include FOXA1/2 (also known as HNF3α/β), FOXO1/3 (also known as FKHR and FKHRL1), HNF4 (also known as NR2A4), COUP-TF (NR2F1/NR2F2), and CCAAT-enhancer binding protein α and β (C/EBPα and C/EBPβ), cAMP response element binding protein (CREB) ( ). The mutation of each AF binding site in a synthetic gene that harbors rat Pck1 gene promoter results in a reduction of approximately 50% of GC response ( , , ). The key molecular observation that underlies the function of the Pck1 GRU is the nonconsensus nature of the Pck1 GREs. This results in very weak GR binding and an inability of GR to mediate a GC response without accessory factors ( ). The accessory factors assist the GR binding to the GRE ( ), and the transactivation domain of accessory factors are required for the potentiation of GC response ( ). Consequently, other hormones, such as insulin, can indirectly modulate GC signaling by targeting accessory factor activity rather than GR itself. Interestingly, chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) assays show that FOXA2, CREB, C/EBPα and C/EBPβ, and HNF4 already occupy their respective binding sites without dexamethasone treatment ( ). In contrast, FOXO1, FOXO3, and COUP-TF and transcriptional coactivators, CREB binding protein (CBP), p300, and SRC1 levels in the GRU are enhanced upon dexamethasone treatment ( ). Upon insulin treatment, which suppresses dexamethasone induction of the Pck1 gene, GR, FOXO1, FOXO3, FOXA2, coactivators, and RNA polymerase II are dissociated from the Pck1 promoter ( ). Thus, dexamethasone treatment induces the formation of a transcriptional regulatory complex on the GRU to enhance Pck1 gene transcription. The human and rat Pck1 gene have conserved exon-intron structures, and exons 2-10 are corresponding sequences. The transcription start site is also conserved between the 2 species. While 2 GREs are well defined in the rat Pck1 promoter, the second GRE site on the human Pck1 promoter may instead be further downstream from −337 to −323. Other binding sites such as the CRE, CAAT, TRE, and TATA box are mostly conserved ( ). Functionally, the human Pck1 promoter in a CAT fusion gene showed increased CAT activity in response to dexamethasone treatment in human Huh7 cells but not rat H4IIE cells. Similarly, the rat Pck1 promoter CAT fusion gene showed increased dexamethasone-responsive CAT activity in the H4IIE cells but not Huh7 cells ( ). The mechanism underlying these species-specific responses to dexamethasone is unclear.
Recently, a nuclear receptor, RAR-related orphan receptor gamma (RORγ), has been shown to bind to a RAR-related orphan receptor response element (RORE) between −250 and −238 on the mouse Pck1 promoter under chronic GC treatment ( ). An antagonist of RORγ and genetic knockdown of RORγ suppressed the induction of gluconeogenesis by chronic GC treatment and Pck1 expression ( ). Interestingly, GR occupancy on the GREs was also reduced in mice treated with RORγ antagonists ( ). These results highlight the role of RORγ as an accessory factor for GR activation of Pck1 gene during chronic GC exposure.
It has been shown that basic helix-loop-helix transcription factor E47 binds to an E-box sequence, CANNTG, near the GREs of mouse Pck1 genes ( ). E47, also known as transcription factor 3, is a transcription factor encoded by the gene E2A. E47 is a splice variant of E2A. GC activation of hepatic Pck1 gene expression was significantly reduced in the E47 KO mice ( ). When these mice were treated with dexamethasone, they were protected from GC-induced hyperglycemia and had reduced gluconeogenic capacity ( ). Coimmunoprecipitation experiments confirmed an interaction between GR and E47 through its LXXLL motifs. ChIP assays in the liver of E47 KO mice showed loss of GR and FOXO1 occupancy on the Pck1 promoter under dexamethasone treatment and fasting ( ). Thus, E47 is another accessory factor for GC activation of Pck1 gene transcription.
Liver X receptor β (LXRβ), a nuclear receptor, has been shown to be involved in GC regulation of the mouse Pck1 gene. LXRβ KO mice (LXRβ−/−) mice are protected from dexamethasone-induced hyperglycemia ( ). Interestingly, another isoform of LXR, LXRα, has not been found to be involved in GC activation of Pck1 gene transcription ( ). Dexamethasone-treated LXRβ−/− mice have improved hyperinsulinemia and are more insulin sensitive than dexamethasone-treated WT mice. Notably, there is no LXRβ response element found in the mouse Pck1 promoter. However, LXRβ was shown to be essential in GR recruitment to the Pck1 GREs ( ). However, it is unknown whether LXRβ is recruited to the GRU of the Pck1 gene or is acting indirectly via another factor. Treating WT mice with an LXRβ-specific antagonist leads to attenuation of dexamethasone upregulation of the Pck1 gene by disrupting MED1, C/EBPβ, and GR recruitment to GRU and is sufficient to lower de novo hepatic glucose production compared to WT mice treated with dexamethasone only ( ). Thus, LXRβ antagonist could be a potential approach to improve chronic GC-induced hyperglycemia and insulin resistance ( ).

## G6pc1
The mouse G6pc1 GRU contains 3 GREs along with binding sites for HNF1, HNF4, FOXA1/2 (also binds to FOXO1/3), and CREB ( ). GC activation of the luciferase reporter harboring the G6pc1 GRU is reduced when the individual binding site of these DNA binding transcription factors is mutated ( ). Thus, these transcription factors function as accessory factors for GR activation of the G6pc1 gene. H4IIE cells transfected with the mouse G6pc1 promoter or human G6pc1 promoter luciferase fusion gene had an increase in activity from dexamethasone treatment, respectively ( ). The HNF4 binding site is also conserved between the mouse and human promoters, although neighboring nucleotides vary between the mouse and human sequence. The variation in these base pairs is key for peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor gamma coactivator-1α (PGC-1α) interaction with HNF4 in mice but not humans ( ). Thus, PGC-1α might play a more important role in the activation of mouse G6pc1 gene transcription than human G6pc1. As with the mouse Pck1 gene, E47 binds to an E-box near the GREs of the mouse G6pc1 promoter ( ). However, a significant reduction of G6pc1 expression was not observed in dexamethasone-treated E47 KO mice ( ). Notably, ChIP-seq assays identified E-box sequences to be enriched near GREs and overlapping cistromes between E47 and GR. In addition to Pck1 and G6pc1, their common target genes include Gpam, Gck, and Igfbp1 ( ). E47 KO mutant mice are protected from GC-induced metabolic disorders due to reduced recruitment of GR and other associated DNA binding transcription factors to target metabolic genes ( ). RORγ has been shown to bind to an RORE from −393 to −380 of the mouse G6pc1 promoter under chronic GC treatment ( ). Both genetic knockdown of RORγ and a RORγ antagonist reduced chronic GC-induced G6pc1 gene expression, and the RORγ antagonist reduced GR occupancy at the G6pc1 GREs ( ). This regulatory mechanism is similar to that of the Pck1 gene.

## Pcx
Pyruvate carboxylase (Pcx) encodes the gluconeogenic enzyme that converts pyruvate to oxaloacetate, which provides the necessary substrate for PCK1 ( ). Dexamethasone treatment in rats increased mitochondrial PCX activity ( ). However, it is unclear whether Pcx transcription is directly activated by GR. GR ChIP-seq assays in mouse liver identified a GR binding site upstream of the mouse Pcx gene transcription start site and multiple GR binding sites in the intron of the Pcx gene ( ). It is unclear whether any of these GR binding sites mediate the GC transcriptional response on the Pcx gene. While upstream stimulatory factor 1/2, FOXA2, and CCAAT binding sites are conserved in the promoters of the rat and human Pcx gene, the presence of GR binding sites in the human promoter needs to be further analyzed ( ).

## Fbp1

Fbp1 is the gene that encodes for the liver subtype of fructose-1,6-bisphosphatase. FBP1 catalyzes the conversion of fructose-1,6-bisphophate to fructose-6-phosphate. FBP1 activity is increased in response to dexamethasone treatment in mouse fetal livers ( ). However, it is unclear whether Fbp1 gene transcription is directly activated by GR. GR ChIP-seq assays identified GR binding sites upstream of the mouse Fbp1 transcription start sites ( ). The function of these GR binding sites has not been analyzed. Overall, the role of GR in the regulation of Pcx and Fbp1 requires more studies.

## Pfkfb
In mammals, 6-phosphofructo-2-kinase/fructose-2,6-bisphosphatase (PFKFB; also known as the bifunctional enzyme) plays an important role in glycolysis and gluconeogenesis with 4 different isoforms encoded by different genes (Pfkfb1-4) ( ). Based on its regulation by allosteric factors, PFKFB either converts F6P to fructose-2,6,-bisphosphate (F2,6BP) or degrades F2,6BP to F6P. F2,6BP activates 6-phosphofructo-1-kinase, which increases glucose uptake and glycolytic flux and inhibits FBP1, a rate-controlling enzyme in gluconeogenesis ( ). Adrenalectomized rats lose 70% of Pfkfb1 mRNA levels compared to WT rats, and treatment with triamcinolone (a synthetic glucocorticoid) restores its expression ( ). A GRU has been identified in the first intron of the rat Pfkfb1 gene. It contains 2 imperfect GRE palindromes that are 3.5 kb downstream of the transcription start site as well as binding sites for FOXA1/2, HNF6 (also known as ONECUT1), NF-1, and C/EBP ( , ).
Short-term dexamethasone treatment increases mRNA levels of Pfkfb3 in WT mice but not in liver-specific GR KO mice ( ). In another study, liver-specific GR KO mice demonstrate dysregulation in circadian expression of Pfkfb3 under chow and high-fat diets ( ). GR binding sites are detected in the mouse Pfkfb3 gene by ChIP-seq assays ( ), but their role in GC-activated Pfkfb3 gene expression has not been studied. Two GREs have been identified in the human Pfkfb4 gene ( ), and GR ChIP-seq assays in mouse liver also identified several GR binding sites in the mouse Pfkfb4 gene ( ). Thus, both Pfkfb3 and Pfkfb4 are likely GR direct target genes.

## Pdk4
Pyruvate dehydrogenase kinase 4 (PDK4) is an isoenzyme of pyruvate dehydrogenase kinase and a component of the pyruvate dehydrogenase complex. This complex catalyzes the conversion of pyruvate to acetyl-CoA ( ). Overexpression of hepatic PDK4 increases expression of gluconeogenic genes while hepatic PDK4 knockdown decreases gluconeogenic gene expression in mouse primary hepatocytes ( ). PDK4 protein levels are affected by cAMP treatment while inhibiting PDK4 decreased cAMP levels, suggesting a feedforward loop between PDK4 levels and cAMP signaling pathways ( ). Thus, the reduction of gluconeogenic genes by PDK4 knockdown is likely the result of decreased cAMP signaling ( ). Pdk4 is highly expressed in the liver, and the regulation of its expression has been well-characterized. Treatment of rat hepatoma 7800 C1 and human hepatoma HepG2 cells with dexamethasone increased Pdk4 mRNA levels ( , ). GR was identified to bind to a GRE in the −824 to −809 region in human Pdk4 promoter ( ). FOXO1a and FOXO3a both bind to insulin response element sites at −738/−731, −533/−526, and −361/−354 on the human Pdk4 promoter and are required for dexamethasone stimulation of Pdk4 expression via interaction with p300/CBP ( ). Akt phosphorylates the FOXO transcription factors, which causes nuclear exclusion and attenuates the induction of Pdk4 by dexamethasone ( ). Two functional GR binding sites at −6460 and −6386 were identified in the rat Pdk4 gene through mutagenesis and reporter gene assays ( ). Cotreatment of insulin with dexamethasone abolished the dexamethasone-induced rat Pdk4 promoter luciferase activity. GR binding to these regions was also confirmed in rat hepatoma cells via ChIP assays. Interestingly, GR was also found to bind to the proximal promoter. However, the proximal promoter does not contain consensus GRE sequences. GR interaction with the proximal promoter is suspected to be indirect and due to the looping of DNA and the presence of FoxO transcription factors ( ). Dexamethasone-induced GR recruitment to the GRE is abolished by insulin treatment ( ).

## Slc37a4
Solute carrier family 37 member 4 (Slc37a4; also known as G6pt) encodes a glucose 6 phosphate translocase, which is involved in the regulation of glycogenolysis and gluconeogenesis ( ). SLC37A4 moves G6P from the cytoplasm into the endoplasmic reticulum whereas G6PC1 hydrolyzes G6P to glucose. G6pt expression is induced by GC, and a GRE was identified in the mouse Slc37a4 promoter ( , ).

## Tat
Tyrosine aminotransferase (TAT) converts tyrosine to 4-hydroxyphenylpyruvate, which can be converted to fumarate after several enzymatic reactions ( ). In the tricarboxylic acid cycle, fumarate can be converted to oxaloacetate, which will be converted to glucose in the gluconeogenic pathway ( , ). The transcription of Tat is highly activated by GC and is mediated by cooperative interaction between 2 GRUs that are located at −2.5 kb and −5.5 kb of the rat Tat gene ( , ). These GRUs contain multiple GREs and the binding sites for C/EBP, FoxA1/2, and the ETS families of transcription factors. In the −2.5 kb GRU, 1 of the GREs, GRE2, is a high-affinity GR binding site and is essential for the GC response in reporter gene assays. Mutations of individual accessory factor binding sites result in a modest reduction of the GC response. However, when multiple binding sites are mutated, the GC response is significantly compromised ( , ). Notably, a transgene containing the artificial −2.5 kb GRU confers a strong GC response in various tissues of transgenic mice ( ). However, the regulation of this artificial −2.5 kb GRU containing transgene differs from the endogenous Tat gene in cell-type specificity, developmental regulation, and cross-talk with other hormonal pathways ( ). These data suggest that DNA binding transcription factors outside of this GRU provide additional regulation of the GC response.
Without dexamethasone treatment, only ETS family proteins bind to the −2.5 kb GRU in hepatoma cells ( ). However, upon dexamethasone treatment, chromatin remodeling occurs in the GRU, which allows FOXA1/2 to be recruited ( ). Interestingly, GR does not need to remain associated with GREs for FOXA1/2 occupancy in the GRU. After 1 to 2 days of dexamethasone treatment, a more stable recruitment of C/EBP and GR to −2.5 kb GRU is observed. In fact, the stable GR occupancy in the GRU is only found when all accessory factors are present ( ). Interestingly, after 2 to 3 days of dexamethasone treatment, CpGs within −2.5 kb GRU are demethylated, which allow other transcription factors to be recruited to the GRU ( ). The demethylated status is stable and is not affected by GC withdrawal. When the gene is stimulated a second time, recruitment of accessory factors and transcription activation occurs more rapidly. Thus, DNA demethylation has provided gene memory to the GC response.

## Gck

Gck encodes for glucokinase, which is a rate-controlling enzyme of glycolysis that converts glucose to G6P. Thus, GCK plays a critical role in hepatic glucose uptake and glycogen synthesis ( , ). In liver-specific GR KO mice, the expression of hepatic Gck was reduced in preprandial and early postprandial states, which likely results in a decreased early postprandial hepatic glucose uptake, phosphorylation, and glycogen storage ( ). GR binding sites of the mouse Gck gene are identified in an enhancer region that is located around −4.6 kb from the transcription start site ( ). When this enhancer is inserted into a luciferase reporter, it can be activated by dexamethasone. More importantly, using CRISPR to disrupt this GR binding site in mouse hepatocytes resulted in a reduced GC induction of Gck expression ( ). Thus, this GR binding site contains functional GRE(s). Notably, another feeding-induced gene that is involved in glycogen metabolism, protein phosphatase 1 regulatory subunit 3b (Ppp1r3b) ( , ), is also a potential GR primary target gene ( ). However, the role of the GR binding site in the GC-induced Ppp1r3b gene and the PPP1R3B action in GC-regulated glycogen metabolism have not been evaluated.

## GR Primary Target Genes Involved in the Regulation of Hepatic Gluconeogenesis
In addition to enzymes involved in glucose homeostasis, GR also regulates genes encoding transcriptional regulators and signaling molecules that participate in the regulation of glucose metabolism. For some of these GR primary target genes, their role in GC-regulated glucose homeostasis have been well studied. However, for some other GR primary target genes, although their roles in the regulation of glucose metabolism have been studied under chow and high-fat diet feeding, their functions in GC-regulated hepatic glucose homeostasis have not been evaluated. Both categories of GR primary target genes are discussed next.

## Klf9
GC have been shown to induce the expression of Krüppel-like factor 9 (Klf9), which encodes a DNA binding transcription factor. GR binding sites have been identified in the human Klf9 gene that are located at least −50 kb from the transcription start site ( , ). To regulate hepatic gluconeogenesis, Klf9 directly binds to the Pgc1α promoter and activates its transcription ( ). Pgc1α is a coactivator for GR, explaining, in part, the mechanism by which PGC-1α promotes Pck1 and G6pc1 transcription ( , ). PGC-1α also interacts and coactivates with FOXO1 and HNF4, which serve as accessory factors for GR to promote Pck1 and G6pc1 transcription ( ). Knockdown of KLF9 in mouse AML12 hepatocytes under high glucose and high palmitic acid conditions reduced gluconeogenic activity and expression of gluconeogenic genes ( ). Hepatic deletion of Klf9 reduced dexamethasone-induced expression of Pck1 and G6pc1 and protected against dexamethasone-induced hyperglycemia and glucose intolerance ( , ).

## 
FoxO1 and FoxO3
Both FoxO1 and FoxO3, members of the FOXO transcription factor family, are GR primary target genes ( , ). When the first 2 kb of the mouse FoxO1 promoter is ligated to a luciferase reporter gene, the resulting fusion gene is responsive to GC ( ). There is 1 highly conserved GRE and 8 GRE half sites found within this region of mouse FoxO1 promoter ( ). Deletion of the promoter regions containing these potential GREs diminishes the GC response. The exact GREs that mediate the GC response, however, have not been identified. For the mouse FoxO3 gene, GREs have been identified in the −17 kb, +45 kb, and +71 kb regions in mouse C2C12 myotubes. Both the +45 kb and +71 kb GREs are located in introns ( ). While these specific GREs have yet to be confirmed in liver cell lines, FOXO3 as a GR target gene in the liver has been confirmed. Mice treated with dexamethasone for 3 hours have significantly increased hepatic mRNA and protein levels of FOXO3 compared to nondexamethasone-treated mice ( ). The role of FOXO1 and FOXO3 in the potentiation of hepatic gluconeogenesis is well established, as it activates the transcription of gluconeogenic genes, such as Pck1 and G6pc1 ( , ). FOXO1 and FOXO3 also bind to accessory elements required for the maximal GC induction of the Pck1 and G6pc1 genes ( , , ). It has been shown that FOXO1 and FOXO3 have an additive effect on glucose homeostasis, though only hepatic deletion of FOXO1 but not FOXO3 resulted in a modest reduction of blood glucose levels ( ). Similarly, another report shows that a double deletion of hepatic FoxO1 and FoxO3 in mice had similar effects on blood glucose and glucose tolerance as compared to knockout of the FoxO1 gene alone ( ). Finally, the liver-specific knockout of FOXO1/3/4 prevents the induction of G6pc1 during fasting and reduces hepatic gluconeogenesis ( , ), though FOXO4 has not been shown to be regulated by GC. Notably, the extent of the roles of FOXO1 and FOXO3 as accessory factors to potentiate maximal GC response can be limited or further amplified depending on the individual genetics. One genotype of the rs17446614 SNP of FOXO1 was identified to have an increased risk of diabetes ( ). Thus, individuals carrying this particular SNP variant who require GC treatment are even more at risk of experiencing the disruptions in glucose homeostasis caused by chronic GC treatment.
GC can also regulate glucose homeostasis indirectly via FOXO1. For example, it has been shown that GC increase MAP kinase phosphatase 3 (Mkp3; also known as Dusp6) expression through their induction of FoxO1 expression in murine Hepa1-6 hepatoma cells ( ). The concept of indirect effects of GC being mediated via FOXO1 is supported by the observation that the Mkp3 gene does not have GR binding sites in or nearby its genomic region, and the dominant negative form of FOXO1 prevents dexamethasone induction of Mkp3 ( ). Mkp3 dephosphorylates MAP kinases such as the extracellular signal-regulated kinases (ERK 1/2) and attenuates their activity ( ). MKP3 also dephosphorylates and activates FOXO1 by promoting its nuclear translocation. This MKP3-FOXO1 axis leads to an increase of Pgc1α transcription and subsequent activation of gluconeogenic gene expression ( ). Another study found that both MKP3 and dexamethasone are able to synergistically activate Pck1 in rat H4IIE hepatoma cells ( ). Notably, Mkp3 KO (Mkp3−/−) mice were protected from dexamethasone-induced insulin resistance ( ). GC-induced FOXO1 expression has also been shown to be required for the potentiation of glucokinase (Gck) transcription by insulin ( ).
Notably, during a fasted state when GC levels are high whereas insulin levels are low, more than half of FOXO1 binding sites are co-occupied with GR in the GRUs of gluconeogenic genes, such as Pck1 and Tat ( ). Conversely, during a fed state when insulin levels are high and GC levels are low, FOXO1 and GR occupancies on GRUs are reduced ( ). In fact, more than 80% of feeding-repressed genes in the liver are associated with a nearby enhancer bound by GR and/or FOXO1 ( ). Finally, FOXO1 can bind to the promoter of the human GR gene (also known as NR3C1) in human hepatoma Huh7 cells, which suggests that FOXO1 regulates GR gene transcription ( ).

## 
Pparα
The transcription factor peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor α (PPARα) is upregulated by fasting. Treatment of primary rat hepatocytes with various synthetic GC increased PPARα mRNA levels. This induction was abolished by antagonizing GR with RU486 treatment ( ). There are several GREs in the mouse Pparα gene; however, ChIP assays showed that GR preferentially binds the −1007 to −993 site ( ). Independently, PPARα regulates the expression of key gluconeogenic genes such as Pck1 and G6pc1. PPARα KO mice are hypoglycemic upon fasting ( ). Interestingly, ChIP-seq analysis of PPARα and GR identified subsets of overlapping peaks. About 56% of PPARα peaks overlapped with 13% of GR peaks ( ). This analysis suggests that GR and PPARα work together to regulate genes encoding key enzymes in gluconeogenesis and lipid metabolism. Both positive and negative crosstalk between GR and PPARα are reported. For example, in mice deficient in low-density lipoprotein receptor (Ldlr−/−) but with WT PPARα (Ppara+/+Ldlr−/−), dexamethasone treatment induced hyperglycemia and insulin resistance. These dexamethasone effects are compromised in Ppara−/−Ldlr−/− mice ( ). In human hepatocytes, cotreatment of dexamethasone and WY-14643, a PPARα agonist, synergistically induces Pck1 gene expression ( ). However, another report shows that treatment with fenofibrates, PPARα agonists, prevents GC-induced glucose intolerance, hyperinsulinemia, and hepatic G6pc1 expression by interfering with recruitment of GR and RNA Pol II to GREs ( ). Moreover, PPARα agonists also suppress 11βHSD1 expression, which reduces active GC levels ( ). Based on these studies, it appears that there is a negative feedback in the GR-PPARα axis, in which PPARα activation can restrict GC signaling. The exact reasons behind the different observations on GR and PPARα interactions in these studies are unclear. In 1 study, Ppara+/+Ldlr−/− and Ppara−/−Ldlr−/− male and female mice were treated with dexamethasone every other day through intraperitoneal injection for 5 months ( ), whereas in another study female C57BL6/J mice under high-fat diet feeding for 7 weeks were treated with dexamethasone for 1 week ( ). The difference in genetic backgrounds of mice used in the studies and the dexamethasone treatment regimens might be the cause of the distinct observations. These studies suggest that the GR-PPARα crosstalk could be affected by genetic, nutritional, and physiological factors.

## Angptl4
ANGPTL4 is a secreted protein that inhibits lipoprotein lipase and increases adipocyte lipolysis through stimulation of cAMP signaling, which induces phosphorylation of key lipolytic proteins like HSL and PLIN1 ( ). Angptl4 is a primary GR target gene whose expression is highly induced upon fasting. A GRE was identified and verified using ChIP assays in the +6267 to +6281 region of the rat Angptl4 gene in H4IIE hepatoma cells ( ). Overexpression of ANGPTL4 is protective against glucose intolerance, hyperglycemia, and hyperinsulinemia in db/db mice ( ). In contrast, hepatocyte-specific Angptl4 deletion improves glucose tolerance and enhances insulin sensitivity in high fat diet-fed mice ( , ). Adipose-specific deletion of Angptl4 is reported to also protect against high fat-induced glucose intolerance ( ). Chronic GC exposure-induced glucose intolerance and insulin resistance are compromised in Angptl4 null mice ( ). However, it is unclear whether ANGPTL4 produced from both liver and adipose tissue contributes to the induction of insulin resistance and glucose intolerance by chronic GC exposure. Mechanistically, ANGPTL4 is required for chronic GC-induced hepatic accumulation of ceramides and sphingosine-1-phosphate, which initiate the signaling pathways that reduce hepatic insulin signaling and gluconeogenesis ( , ). While the exact mechanism is unclear, it is proposed that ANGPTL4 is required for GC-induced lipolysis in adipocytes, which promotes the mobilization of fatty acids, such as palmitate, from adipose tissues to the liver. In the liver, palmitate is converted to palmitoyl-CoA, which is the precursor for the synthesis of ceramides and S1P.

## Klf15
Krüppel-like factor 15 (KLF15), which encodes a DNA binding transcription factor, is a GR primary target gene whose expression is induced upon fasting ( ). Two GREs are identified in the first 2 introns of the human and mouse Klf15 genes ( ). In mice, the GREs are located at +980 to +998 and +1085 to +1103. In humans, the GREs are located at +1293 to +1311 and +1399 to +1417 ( ). Klf15 KO mice display severe hypoglycemia after an overnight fast. The defective amino acid catabolism in Klf15 null mice is the reason for the development of hypoglycemia ( ). Intraperitoneal injection of pyruvate, but not alanine, rescues fasting hypoglycemia in Klf15 null mice ( ). This is because KLF15 plays a role in converting alanine to pyruvate by inducing transcription of target genes such as alanine aminotransferase 1 (Alt1). Another study identified the role of Klf15 in the activation of the expression of 4-hydroxyphenylpyruvate dioxygenase (Hppd or Hpd), an enzyme involved in amino acid metabolism, indicating its role in providing gluconeogenic substrates ( ). Overexpression of Klf15 increased Pck1 expression and activity ( ). The role of KLF15 has also been proposed to switch from regulating the expression of genes involved in lipogenesis in the fed state to genes involved in gluconeogenesis in the fasted state ( ). Overall, the physiological actions of KLF15 are consistent with those of GC in the liver. However, whether altered hepatic KLF15 expression modulates the actions of GC on hepatic glucose homeostasis has not been reported.

## Zbtb16
Zinc finger and BTB domain containing 16 [Zbtb16; also known as promyelocytic leukemia zinc finger protein (Plzf)] is a GR primary target gene that encodes a DNA binding transcription factor. The mouse Zbtb16 gene contains a half site GRE between −1121 to −630 in the promoter region, and its expression is induced by dexamethasone treatment in mouse AML12 cells ( ). It has been shown that PGC1α acts as a GR coactivator for Zbtb16 gene induction ( ). ZBTB16 is typically associated with its role as a transcriptional repressor but has recently been found to act as a transcriptional activator when deacetylated by SIRT1 ( ). ZBTB16 overexpressing mice are hyperglycemic, and the expression of genes involved in hepatic gluconeogenesis, such as Pck1 and G6pc1, is increased ( ). Conversely, hepatic ZBTB16 knockdown mice have reduced hepatic gluconeogenesis ( ). Overexpression of ZBTB16 in mouse AML12 hepatocytes increases the expression of Pck1 and G6pc1. However, there is no report that shows ZBTB16 binding to Pck1 and G6pc1 promoters ( ). Phosphorylation of FOXO1, insulin receptor substrate, and Akt, markers of the insulin signaling pathway, are decreased by ZBTB16 overexpression ( ). Thus, it is likely that the suppression of insulin signaling by ZBTB16 indirectly results in the elevation of Pck1 and G6pc1 expression.
Interestingly, another study showed that in cortisol-treated Zbtb16 depleted zebrafish larvae, Pck1 expression was enhanced as compared to those of WT larvae ( ). The cortisol response on other GR target genes, such as Fkbp5 and Gilz (also known as Tsc22d3), was also increased ( ). These results were similar to those observed in human fibroblast-like synoviocytes and choroidal endothelial cells, which showed that GC response on target gene expression is elevated in ZBTB16 depleted cells ( ). Based on these results, ZBTB16 appears to play a negative role in GR action, though the mechanism underlying this model has not been explored. It is unclear why the Zbtb16 knockdown effect on Pck1 expression is different between zebrafish larvae and mouse primary hepatocytes. However, it is important to note that the role of ZBTB16 in GC-induced Pck1 gene expression in hepatocytes and gluconeogenesis has not been directly examined.

## Sgk1
The rat serum and glucocorticoid-regulated kinase 1 (Sgk1) gene encodes a serine/threonine kinase that was first identified in the Con8.hd6 rat mammary tumor cell line ( , ). Subsequent studies have found that the Sgk1 gene is activated by GC in various cell types, including hepatocytes. An imperfect but functional GRE has been identified at −1159 of the human Sgk1 gene ( ). It has been shown that chow diet-fed liver-specific Sgk1 KO mice develop glucose intolerance and insulin resistance ( ). This is because SGK1 inhibits ERK1/2 in the liver to improve insulin sensitivity ( ). Adenoviral-mediated overexpression of SGK1 in the liver of db/db mice improves glucose tolerance and insulin sensitivity ( ). However, a different study found liver-specific Sgk1 KO mice had decreased hepatic gluconeogenic expression in the fasted state and had improved glucose tolerance and insulin sensitivity under high-fat diet feeding, effects proposed to be mediated by inhibition of AMPK ( ). In this study, although chow diet-fed liver-specific Sgk1 KO mice have no difference in glucose tolerance as compared to control mice, they maintain euglycemia following a glucose challenge with lower plasma insulin levels, which suggests an increased insulin sensitivity ( ). Reasons for these conflicting phenotypes of the same liver-specific Sgk1 KO mice are yet to be fully established. Notably, the role of SGK1 in modulating the action of GC on hyperglycemia and insulin resistance has not been evaluated.

## Dusp1
Dual-specificity phosphatase 1 (DUSP1; also known as Mkp1) is a MAP kinase phosphatase that inhibits various MAP kinase signaling pathways ( ). ChIP assay scanning and mutagenesis studies identified a functional GRE between −1337 and −1323 in the human Dusp1 promoter, a region that is conserved in the mouse Dusp1 promoter ( , ). Other GREs were identified at −4.6 kb, −24 kb, and −28 kb in the human promoter. These GREs, however, are not conserved in the mouse Dusp1 gene ( ). In contrast, the mouse Dusp1 gene contains GREs in a −29 kb region that is not conserved in the human Dusp1 gene ( ). Liver-specific Dusp1 knockout increases gluconeogenesis and causes hepatic insulin resistance under normal chow diet feeding ( ). But under high-fat diet feeding, hepatic Dusp1 KO mice are protected from hepatic steatosis ( ). Hepatic Dusp1 KO mice have lower circulating interleukin-6 and fibroblast growth factor 21 levels, which result in impaired skeletal muscle mitochondrial oxidation and susceptibility to diet-induced obesity ( ). While the role of DUSP1 in the anti-inflammatory response is established ( ), whether it modulates the hepatic GC response has not been evaluated. Notably, mouse Fgf21 gene has been shown to be directly regulated by GR ( ). However, the role of FGF21 in GC-regulated glucose homeostasis has not been tested.

## 
Gadd45β
Growth arrest and DNA damage-inducible 45β (Gadd45β) encodes a transcriptional coactivator that regulates cell growth and survival ( , ). Gadd45b has been identified as a GR primary target gene and is also induced by fasting to contribute toward hepatic gluconeogenesis ( , ). Two half GRE sites, located between −1474 and −1469 and between −496 and −491, were identified in the mouse Gadd45b gene ( ). In fusion genes containing the mouse Gadd45b promoter, mutation of either site resulted in a 50% reduction of the dexamethasone response. Mutation of both sites abolished the dexamethasone response. ChIP assays further confirmed the recruitment of GR to these GREs ( ). Hepatic overexpression of GADD45β caused hyperglycemia, enhanced gluconeogenic capacity and glucose intolerance, and increased mRNA levels of Pck1 and G6pc1 in mice fed with a chow diet. Mechanistically, GADD45β modulates DNA methylation at the Ppargca1 (also known as Pgc1α) promoter to increase its expression, which in turn enhances gluconeogenic gene transcription ( ). Under both fasting and high-fat diet feeding, whole body Gadd45β KO and hepatic Gadd45β knockdown mice had reduced expression of gluconeogenic genes and blood glucose levels, an effect mediated by an action on FoxO1 protein stability ( ). Notably, the role of GADD45β in modulating the effects of chronic GC treatment-promoted hyperglycemia and insulin resistance have not been examined. Interestingly, another study showed that GADD45β is required for the suppression of gluconeogenic gene expression by constitutive androstane receptor (CAR; also known as Nr1i3) ( ). CAR suppresses G6pc1 and Pck1 expression, in part, by facilitating the degradation of PGC1α leading to improvement of insulin sensitivity ( ). It is unclear how GADD45β acts with CAR to control the protein stability of PGC1α. However, these studies suggest that GADD45β can play either a positive or a negative role in the regulation of glucose homeostasis.

## C/EBP
C/EBP are transcription factors with multiple isoforms. As discussed previously, C/EBPα and C/EBPβ are accessory factors of GR target gluconeogenic genes, including Pck1, Pfkfb, and Tat. A ChIP-seq study identified 62% overlapping binding sites between GR and C/EBPβ. This includes genes involved in glucose homeostasis such as Tat and Fkbp5 ( ). C/EBPβ also promotes GR recruitment by increasing chromatin accessibility. DNase-sequencing using a dominant negative C/EBP suggests that it is necessary to maintain chromatin accessibility at specific genomic regions that are essential for GR binding ( ). A study performed in human fibroblasts and blood leukocytes indicated the formation of a complex between GR and C/EBPα ( ). Both C/EBPα and C/EBPβ can serve as accessory factors for GR-activated rat α1-acid glycoprotein (also known as orosomucoid 1 or AGP) gene transcription. Interestingly, protein-protein interaction between GR and C/EBPα or GR and C/EBPβ is critical for the GC-induced AGP gene ( , ). Notably, the expression of C/EBPα and C/EBPβ has been shown to be induced by GC in the rodent liver ( , ). The GR binding sites within mouse C/EBPα and C/EBPβ genes are identified though ChIP-seq in mice liver ( ). Whether these binding sites mediate GC responses, however, has not been studied. Independent of GR, C/EBP plays a role in the regulation of glucose homeostasis. Whole body C/EBPα KO mice are nonviable and die shortly after birth due to hypoglycemia ( ). Liver-specific C/EBPα KO mice had worsened glucose tolerance compared to WT controls ( ). Expression of Pck1 was unchanged, but Gck was reduced in the liver-specific KO mice. C/EBPβ KO mice have reduced gluconeogenesis and the expression of Pck1 and G6pc1 ( ).

## Transcription Factors Involved in Regulation of GR Levels and Hepatic Glucose Metabolism
As discussed, the activation of genes involved in glucose homeostasis by GR ( ) requires its interaction with DNA binding transcription factors such as C/EBPs, FOXA1/2, and FOXO1/3 in hepatocytes ( ). There are other transcriptional regulators involved in the regulation of hepatic glucose metabolism by GR by directly modulating the expression of GR itself. The nuclear receptor, farnesoid X receptor (FXR) regulates gluconeogenic gene expression in the liver by regulating expression of GR. FXR KO (FXR−/−) mice have lower Pck1 and G6pc1 expression and reduced hepatic glucose production in pyruvate tolerance tests. Interestingly, the GR promoter contains an FXR response element. Thus, FXR−/− mice have blunted GR expression and a reduced gluconeogenic response ( ).
 Yin yang 1 (YY1) is a transcription factor that is induced by fasting. Overexpressing YY1 in the liver of mice showed an increase of hepatic gluconeogenesis in pyruvate tolerance tests and increased expression of Pck1 and G6pc1 ( ). GR expression is regulated by YY1. Hepatic knockdown of YY1 decreased GR and gluconeogenic gene expression, whereas overexpression caused the opposite effects. A binding site for YY1 was identified in the promoter region of the mouse GR gene ( ).

## Transcriptional Coregulators Involved in GC-regulated Hepatic Glucose Homeostasis
Transcriptional coregulators play a key role in GR-regulated gene transcription. In this section, we discuss the transcriptional coregulators involved in GR-regulated hepatic glucose metabolism. A list of these coregulators and their involvement in GR-regulated glucose metabolism is shown in .
 

## CBP/p300
CBP and p300 are transcriptional coactivators and histone acetyltransferases ( , ). CBP/p300 has been shown to regulate transcription of gluconeogenic genes. Adenoviral knockdown of CBP and p300 in fasted mice led to significantly decreased fasting blood glucose levels and expression of genes such as Pck1 and G6pc1 ( ). However, another study found that hepatic knockout of CBP alone does not affect expression of G6pc1 and Pck1 ( ). Treatment with A-485, a selective CBP/p300 inhibitor compound, reduced fasting glucose levels and hepatic glucose production in mice under chow and high-fat diet conditions ( ). CBP/p300 has been shown to be recruited to the GRU of Pck1 and G6pc1 upon dexamethasone treatment in H4IIE cells ( ). This recruitment was blocked upon insulin treatment, which suppresses dexamethasone activation of the Pck1 gene ( ). Thus, they are components of the dexamethasone-induced GR containing transcriptional regulatory complex that activates Pck1 gene transcription.

## SRC1
Expression of steroid receptor coactivator 1 (SRC1; also known as Ncoa1), a member of the p160 coactivator family, is increased by fasting ( ) and is essential for hepatic gluconeogenesis. SRC1 KO mice were found to be hypoglycemic and have decreased expression of Pck1 and Fbp1 but not G6pc1. Hepatic overexpression of SRC1 in SRC1 KO mice was able to restore glucose homeostasis ( ). SRC1 coactivates transcription in conjunction with HNF4 and FOXA2 ( ). In a rat Pck1 promoter-reporter fusion gene, replacement of accessory factor binding elements with yeast transcription factor GAL4 binding sites reduced dexamethasone induction of reporter activity. Overexpressing a fusion protein that contained the GAL4 DNA binding domain and SRC1, however, restored the dexamethasone response ( ). Thus, the functions of accessory factors include recruiting transcriptional coactivators to stabilize the transcriptional regulatory complex at the Pck1 GRU. ChIP assay experiments in H4IIE cells also show that SRC1 is recruited to the promoter of the Pck1 gene upon dexamethasone treatment ( ).

## MED1
MED1 (also known as TRAP220) is a coactivator that can directly interact with the GR ligand binding domain via its LXXLL motifs ( ). GR induction of a luciferase fusion gene construct that contains the mouse mammary tumor virus promoter GREs is blunted in MED1 KO mouse embryonic fibroblasts ( ). The induction of Pck1, G6pc1, and Slc37a4 by dexamethasone is attenuated in the liver of liver-specific MED1 KO mice ( ). Interestingly, GC-induced hepatic steatosis is relieved in liver-specific MED1 KO mice ( ). In WT mice, dexamethasone treatment for 3 days suppresses the expression of fatty acid oxidation enzymes. This effect is reduced in liver-specific MED1 KO mice ( ). Liver-specific MED1 knockout also showed reduced fasting-induced expression of GR primary target genes, including Pck1, G6pc1, Lpin1, Tat, Igfbp1, and Klf9 ( ).

## PGC1α
Peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor gamma coactivator-1α (PGC1α; also known as Ppargc1a) is a transcriptional coactivator induced by fasting ( ) and synergistically activated by cAMP and GC ( , ). It is unclear whether the Ppargc1a gene is directly activated by GR, but there are several GR binding sites identified by ChIP-seq assays at the mouse Ppargc1a gene locus ( ). PGC1α can interact with GR ( ), HNF4 ( ), and FOXO1 ( ). The latter 2 factors serve as accessory factors for GR activation of gluconeogenic gene transcription ( , , ). In a study using H4IIE hepatoma cells, the data suggested that without PGC1α, dexamethasone still can activate Pck1 gene transcription. However, the presence of PGC1α enhanced GR activation of the Pck1 gene. Thus, PGC1α plays a role as amplifier of GR response on the Pck1 gene ( ).

## CRTC2
The CREB regulated transcription coactivator 2 (CRTC2) physically interacts with the ligand binding domain of GR and functionally potentiates the GR response on a reporter gene containing the mouse mammary tumor virus long terminal repeat ( ). In H4IIE hepatoma cells, dexamethasone treatment induces the recruitment of CRTC2 to the GRE of the G6pc1 gene ( ). Suppression of CRTC2 expression by small hairpin RNA in mice liver abolishes fasting, and GC-induced Pck1 and G6pc1 gene expression and reduces serum glucose levels ( ). Liver-specific Crtc2 KO mice had reduced fasted glucose levels and gluconeogenic gene expression under high-fat diet feeding ( ).

## EHMT2
Euchromatic histone-lysine N-methyltransferase 2 (EHMT2; also known as G9a) is a methyltransferase that deposits mono- or di-methyl groups on histone H3 lysine 9 (H3K9me1 and H3K9me2), an epigenetic mark associated with gene repression ( , ). EHMT2 has also been shown to act as a coactivator for GR ( ). The coactivation function of EHMT2 requires an auto-methylation at lysine 185 of human EHMT2 (lysine 182 of the mouse counterpart) ( ). This methylation creates a docking site for CBX3 (also known as HP1γ) ( ) that is critical for the transcriptional coactivation by EHMT2 ( ). Thus, mutating lysine 182 of mouse EHMT2 to arginine (K182R) abolishes the coactivation function of EHMT2 without affecting the corepression function since the methyltransferase activity of EHMT2 remains intact ( ). Hepatic knockdown of Ehmt2 worsened chronic GC-induced insulin resistance ( ). Interestingly, mice with K182R mutations of EHMT2 (Ehmt2K182R/K182R) had a similar phenotype ( ). Thus, the worsened insulin resistance in GC-treated mice is due to the loss of the coactivation function of EHMT2. RNA sequencing analyses found that EHMT2 coactivation is required for a subset of GC-activated genes in the liver (8.4%) ( ). One of these EHMT2 coactivation-dependent GR-induced genes is Irs2. The Irs2 GR binding site is located at 42 kb 5′ of the mouse Irs2 gene promoter ( ), though the exact GRE sequences have not been identified. Overexpression of Irs2 in dexamethasone-treated Ehmt2K182R/K182R mice was able to alleviate worsened glucose intolerance and insulin resistance ( ). Conversely, compared to WT mice, hepatic Irs2 knockdown mice have worsened GC-induced insulin resistance, which mimics the phenotypes of Ehmt2K182R/K182R mice. Thus, not only does GR stimulate the transcription of genes that promote gluconeogenesis (such as Pck1 and G6pc1) and inhibit insulin response, it also activates insulin-sensitizing genes such as Irs2 ( ). Therefore, the extent of insulin resistance caused by GC is determined by the balance between the expression of GR-induced insulin resistance and insulin-sensitizing genes. As a coactivator, EHMT2 restricts GC-induced insulin resistance by coactivating GR to stimulate Irs2 gene transcription.

## TAZ and YAP
The transcriptional co-activator with a PDZ-binding motif (TAZ) has been identified to interact directly with GR at its ligand binding domain and restricts GR's activation of gluconeogenic genes by preventing its binding to the GREs of the Pck1 and G6pc1 genes ( ). Liver-specific knockdown of TAZ in mice on a chow diet resulted in increased expression of Pck1 and G6pc1 and increased gluconeogenic capacity as measured by pyruvate tolerance tests ( ). In addition, liver-specific TAZ KO mice had increased fasting glucose levels. Hepatic overexpression of TAZ reversed these effects. Knockdown of TAZ in mouse primary hepatocytes enhanced dexamethasone-induced gluconeogenic expression ( ). TAZ expression is reduced upon fasting, which allows GR to potentiate gluconeogenic gene expression ( ). Overall, TAZ restricts the GC response on gluconeogenesis by directly perturbing the GR and GRE interaction.
Yes-associated protein 1 (YAP1; also known as YAP) is a transcriptional coregulator related to TAZ. TAZ and YAP are paralogs ( ). Constitutively active YAP was able to suppress dexamethasone-induced Pck1 and G6pc1 expression in mouse primary hepatocytes. Hepatocyte-specific knockdown of YAP in mice enhanced both glucagon and dexamethasone-induced gluconeogenic expression ( ). Unlike TAZ, the role of YAP does not directly interfere with the action of GR on gluconeogenic gene transcription. Instead, it acts through suppression of PGC1α ( ). YAP inhibits the ability of PGC1α to be recruited to the GREs of gluconeogenic genes and activate their transcription ( ).

## NCOR1/2
Nuclear receptor corepressor 1/2 (NCOR1/2) functions in transcriptional corepressor complexes that contain histone deacetylase 3 (HDAC3), G Protein pathway suppressor 2, and transducing-beta-like protein 1 ( , ). However, double knockout of NCOR1 and NCOR2 in mouse livers causes a significant reduction in glycogen storage and gluconeogenic gene expression and fasting hypoglycemia, suggesting a role in transcriptional activation rather than repression in this context ( ). This is not observed in hepatic knockout of NCOR1, NCOR2, or HDAC3 alone. This HDAC3-independent activation function of NCOR1 and NCOR2 is due to a loss of chromatin accessibility that reduces GR binding to the GREs of gluconeogenic genes, which further enhances their transcription ( ). Interestingly, the sites bound by GR that were lost in the double NCOR1/NCOR2 KO livers showed an enrichment for HNF4 motifs. Indeed, NCOR1 occupies over 90% of HNF4-dependent GR binding sites ( ). It has been shown in hepatocytes that HNF4 assists GR occupancy on weak GRE motifs ( ). Overall, HNF4-NCOR1/NCOR2 provides the specificity for NCOR1/NCOR2 knockout to specifically regulate GR occupancy in selective GR binding sites, which include those of gluconeogenic GR primary target genes.

## Chronic GC Induced Signaling Pathways that Regulate Hepatic Glucose Homeostasis
Chronic GC exposure leads to hepatic accumulation of ceramides and sphingosine-1-phosphate (S1P) ( ). This GC effect is not seen in short-term GC exposure and requires Angptl4, a primary GR target gene. GC-induced gluconeogenic gene expression and insulin resistance are reduced in dexamethasone-treated Angptl4 KO (Angptl4−/−) mice. Dexamethasone treatment induces the expression of several enzymes along the ceramide synthesis pathway, which are compromised in Angptl4−/− mice ( ). The ANGPTL4-dependent and dexamethasone-induced genes include ceramide synthase 3-6 (Cers3-6). Among them, CERS6, which mainly generates C16:0 ceramides, has been linked to the development of insulin resistance ( , ). CERS5 also plays a role in maintaining C16:0 ceramides levels, and CERS5 null mice have reduced weight gain and improved systemic glucose homeostasis ( ). Interestingly, GR binding sites are found in the genomic region of CERS6 ( ). Nonetheless, the role of CERS6 in chronic GC-induced insulin resistance has not been confirmed. Two downstream effectors of ceramides, protein phosphatase 2A and protein kinase Cζ, are activated by long-term dexamethasone treatment, and suppressing either pathway improves dexamethasone-induced insulin resistance ( ) ( ).
 Ceramides can be converted to sphingosines, which are further phosphorylated to form S1P by sphingosine kinases 1 and 2 ( ). S1P can be exported to extracellular space from the inside of hepatocytes and bind to its cognate receptors S1P receptor isoform 1-5 (S1PR1-5) ( , ). Hepatic S1PR3 knockdown in dexamethasone-treated mice elevates fasting glucose levels and worsens glucose tolerance, while hepatic S1PR2 knockdown improves glucose tolerance and reduces hyperinsulinemia ( ). In contrast, S1PR1 knockdown in the liver does not affect glucose tolerance but elevates fasting insulin levels in dexamethasone-treated mice ( ). Thus, S1PR1 2 and 3 play distinct roles in mediating GC-regulated insulin sensitivity and glucose homeostasis. Further studies of the mechanism of the role of hepatic S1PR2 signaling in chronic GC found that it antagonizes GC-suppression of RORγ expression. This results in an increased expression of RORγ. RORγ is recruited to mouse Pck1 and G6pc1 gene GRUs to act as an accessory factor for GR to promote the transcription of Pck1 and G6pc1 genes ( ) ( ).
Notably, the role of ANGPTL4 in chronic GC-induced hepatic ceramide and S1P accumulation has not been exactly identified. The increase of hepatic ceramide and S1P production by long-term GC treatment likely requires the mobilization of lipids from adipose tissues to the liver. ANGPTL4 plays a role in this process because it is involved in GC-promoted lipolysis in adipocytes ( ). This model, however, needs further confirmation.
Notably, during short-term GC exposure, such as fasting, GC suppress lipogenesis in the liver ( ) and promote lipolysis in white adipose tissues ( , ). Chronic GC exposure still induces lipolysis in white adipose tissue but enhances lipogenesis in both liver and white adipose tissue ( ). These lead to central obesity and hepatic steatosis. The ectopic accumulation of triglycerides on the liver have been shown to contribute toward insulin resistance and thus disrupts glucose homeostasis ( ). Several mechanisms have been proposed for lipid-induced insulin resistance. These include the signaling pathways activated by the diacylglycerol (DAG)-protein kinase C, free fatty acid-toll-like receptor, and ceramides ( ). We discussed the role of ceramides and S1P in chronic GC-induced glucose disorders earlier. The role of other pathways in chronic GC-induced hepatic insulin resistance has not been tested, though previous studies showed that long-term GC treatment can increase hepatic DAG levels ( ). GC also increase the activity and the expression of hepatic phosphatidate phosphatase, which converts phosphatidate to DAG and is encoded by the lipin family of genes (Lpin1, 2, and 3) ( , ). More studies are needed to determine whether chronic GC-induced hepatic lipogenesis contributes to the development of insulin resistance and enhanced gluconeogenesis.

## Metabolic Phenotypes of Liver-specific GR KO Mice
Liver-specific GR KO mice (LGRKO) have been created to study hepatic GR action ( , , , ). In Opherk et al, LGRKO mice were generated by crossing homozygous GRloxP/loxP mice with mice expressing Cre recombinase under the control of α-fetoprotein promoter ( ). These floxed mutant mice possess LoxP sites flanking exon 2 of the mouse Nr3c1 gene. Dexamethasone induction of the expression of gluconeogenic genes such as Pck1, G6pc1, and Tat was significantly diminished in the liver of LGRKO mice ( ). LGRKO mice also develop hypoglycemia after 28 hours of fasting ( ). Streptozotocin treatment causes hyperglycemia in mice as pancreatic β cells are destroyed. This hyperglycemic phenotype is ameliorated by liver-specific GR deletion ( ). Notably, streptozotocin treatment in rodents can cause hypercorticosteronemia ( , ).
In Bose et al, LGRKO mice were created by crossing GRLoxP/LoxP mice with mice expressing Cre recombinase under the control of albumin promoter ( ). These GRLoxP/LoxP mice are different from the previous study as they possess LoxP sites flanking exon 3 of the Nr3c1 gene. In these LGRKO mice, dexamethasone induction of gluconeogenic and glycogen metabolism genes was reduced in the liver ( ). Dexamethasone treatment caused insulin resistance and increased hepatic glycogen contents. LGRKO mice, however, were more insulin sensitive and had lower hepatic glycogen levels ( ). Interestingly, the expression of these genes was elevated in the kidney, likely a compensatory mechanism to the lack of GR in the liver ( ). The glycogen contents in the kidney were reduced by dexamethasone treatment in WT mice. However, in LGRKO mice, dexamethasone treatment resulted in elevated glycogen storage in the kidney.
In Præstholm et al, LGRKO mice were created by infecting GRLoxP/LoxP mice with adeno-associated virus serotype 8 expressing Cre recombinase. The control mice GRLoxP/LoxP mice were infected with adeno-associated virus serotype 8 expressing GFP. These GRLoxP/LoxP mice also have LoxP sites flanking exon 3 of the mouse Nr3c1 gene and with FVB/N strain background ( ). However, they were backcrossed with C57B6/J mice for multiple generations before the experiments. Interestingly, in this study, the expression of genes induced upon the fed state, such as Gck, was reduced in the liver of LGRKO mice, which likely results in a reduced hepatic glucose uptake and glycogen storage ( ). These results highlight the role of GR in the regulation of glucose metabolism in a postprandial state. Notably, chronic GC exposure has been shown to increase hepatic glycolysis by activating the transcription factor carbohydrate response element binding protein ( ). However, whether this process is involved in chronic GC-induced glucose disorders has not been evaluated.
GR knockout in adipose tissue and skeletal muscle could potentially affect GC-regulated glucose metabolism in the liver indirectly. As discussed earlier, GC effects on these 2 tissues generate substrates that are needed for gluconeogenesis. Also, the insulin sensitivity modulated by GR knockout in these tissues could affect hepatic GR actions. The physiological effects of tissue-specific GR knockout in mice have been reviewed ( , , , ).

## Individual-specific Response to GC Treatment
Patient response to GC therapy can vary from individual to individual. Dexamethasone-treated iPSC-derived hepatocyte-like cells from 11 individuals exhibited individual-specific dexamethasone responses. While genes such as Angptl4 were induced by dexamethasone in all hepatocyte-like cells, some other genes such as Apoc2 and Cpt1c had individual-specific responses ( ). There are multiple factors that contribute to individual-specific responses to GC treatment. First, the variation in GR occupancy on target promoters can influence individual GC sensitivity. ChIP-seq analysis of dexamethasone-treated iPSC-derived hepatocyte-like cells found partial overlapping GR binding sites across 11 individuals. Among them, 14 904 GR binding sites were individual-specific ( ). Notably, GR expression was similar across all individuals. These individual-specific GR binding sites correlated with the individual-specific dexamethasone-responsive genes. Thus, the variation in GR genomic occupancy plays a role in individual-specific GC sensitivity and response. Second, the individual-specific binding occupancy can be attributed to SNPs within the target gene promoters themselves or SNPs that alter chromatin accessibility. The rs13038139 SNP is a GR binding site located 18 kb downstream of the human Pck1 gene. The C allele of this SNP has been linked to allele-selective induction of Pck1 expression by dexamethasone ( ). Some of the individuals' iPSC-derived hepatocyte-like cells were heterozygous for this SNP and had increased GR binding ( ). Another SNP rs6026774 affects dexamethasone response of lipopolysaccharide binding protein (Lbp) ( ). This SNP also affects GR binding to the GR binding site nearby Lbp gene. Adult patients with the GG allele of rs6026774 who were treated with GC were protected from hyperglycemia ( ). Hepatic organoids differentiated from iPSC cells carrying rs6026774 showed reduced dexamethasone-induced LBP expression and glucose production ( ). These suggest that Lbp is a GR primary target gene in human hepatocytes that participates in GC-regulated glucose metabolism. Another SNP rs2060982 is located approximately 1 kb downstream of the carnitine palmitoyltransferase 1A (Cpt1a) gene, which encodes a rate-limiting enzyme in fatty acid oxidation. The GG allele of this SNP showed genotype-specific dosage effect on GR binding and dexamethasone response ( ). Inhibition of CPT1A has been shown to reduce gluconeogenesis ( ). Indeed, the AA allele is associated with decreased risk of hyperglycemia-induced GC ( ). Similar results of unique individual-specific dexamethasone responses and GR binding were found in human stem cell-derived adipocytes ( ). Overall, the natural genetic variations in the human genome can modulate GR binding to affect metabolic effects of GC. This knowledge provides valuable insights that could be applied to design new GC therapies. For example, GC-induced gene expression profiles of iPSC-derived hepatocyte-like cells isolated from individual patients might be able to determine the severity of hyperglycemia and insulin resistance caused by GC therapy for particular patients. This precision medicine approach could greatly improve the application of GC treatment.

## Conclusion and Future Directions
GC play a key role during stress in maintaining circulating glucose levels. The liver is an important target organ of GC regulation of glucose homeostasis. This review highlights the current understanding of the mechanisms of hepatic GC action on glucose metabolism ( ). Excessive GC signaling, either through prolonged stress or long-term treatment with high GC levels, causes hyperglycemia and insulin resistance. The findings discussed here offer novel insights that could be applied to help alleviate these symptoms and develop improved GC therapies. One major strategy to improve GC therapy by the pharmaceutical industry is to isolate new ligands that can activate GR's anti-inflammatory activity but with fewer adverse effects. These ligands are known as selective glucocorticoid receptor modulators (SGRMs) ( , ) ( ). Vamorolone (also known as VB-15) is a dissociated steroid that received marketing approval for treating Duchenne muscular dystrophy ( , ). In vitro experiments showed that Vamorolone efficiently represses the reporter gene containing the NFkB binding site but has reduced activity to activate the reporter containing the Sgk GRE. In phase 1 clinical trials, the effect of fasting glucose is similar between placebo and those treated with Vamorolone up to 20 mg/kg body weight per day ( ). In the phase 2b trial, it appears that Vamorolone still causes adrenal suppression as with prednisone ( ). One SGRM currently undergoing clinical trials is Relacorilant (CORT125134) with the goal of reducing cortisol activity in individuals with Cushing's syndrome and reducing hyperglycemia and hypertension ( ). AZD9567 is a nonsteroidal SGRM ( ). In primary human hepatocytes, AZD9567 did not affect Tat gene expression and dose-dependently suppressed the induction of Tat gene expression by prednisolone ( ). Phase 1 studies showed that, compared to prednisolone, the AZD9567-treated group had better postprandial glycemic control ( ). In the phase 2a clinical trials, type 2 diabetic patients were treated with AZD9567 or prednisolone. Those treated with AZD9567 had significantly reduced treatment-induced hyperglycemia ( ). GRM-01 is another SGRM that is currently under phase 1 clinical trials and has been reported to dissociate the hyperglycemic effects of chronic GC treatment from its anti-inflammatory effects ( ). In HepG2 cells, treatment of GRM-01 had a lower induction of TAT enzymatic activity compared to prednisolone ( ). Dosing of GRM-01 in rats reduced swelling without affecting glycemic levels ( ). There are more SGRMS under the preclinical studies, which have been recently reviewed ( , , ).
 Despite the development of these SGRMs, more approaches are needed to improve GC therapies as it is unlikely that there is a SGRM that will not induce adverse effects and can be applied to all diseases that require GC treatment. The GR primary target genes, GR coregulators, and GC-induced signaling pathways, as discussed in this review, could be valuable markers for identifying these treatments. Additionally, targeting GR primary target genes and proteins that act with GR to promote gluconeogenesis and suppress hepatic insulin action may help reduce hyperglycemia and insulin resistance. Another method of mitigating the hyperglycemic effects of chronic GC treatment is to cotreat patients with established drugs that reduce blood glucose levels. These include sulfonylureas, metformin, thiazolidinediones, and incretin-based therapies. The use of these drugs with glucocorticoids has been recently reviewed ( ). Thus, a combination of GC and other agents that reduce adverse effects could offer a promising approach to improving GC therapy.
Many aspects in the GC regulation of hepatic glucose remain to be explored. First, GR regulates hundreds of primary target genes in hepatocytes. However, most of their metabolic functions are yet to be analyzed. Elucidating the functions of these target genes will reveal novel mechanisms underlying hepatic GR actions. Targeting primary target genes that cause insulin resistance and hyperglycemia could be a straightforward approach to mitigate chronic GC-induced glucose disorders ( ). Second, certain transcriptional coregulators are involved in the regulation of different subsets of GR target genes. The transcriptional coregulators that are specifically involved in hepatic glucose metabolism or anti-inflammation remain unidentified. Such coregulators could be intriguing targets to dissociate beneficial and undesired adverse effects of GC. Moreover, it could be possible to modulate GR's ability to cause insulin resistance and hyperglycemia or improve insulin sensitivity by targeting coregulators specifically in these processes ( ). Third, more signaling pathways specifically induced by long-term GC exposure that are involved in the development of hyperglycemia and insulin resistance also remain to be investigated ( ). These pathways could be the targets for dampening chronic GC-induced glucose disorders without affecting normal GC functions. Fourth, recent studies in rodent models suggest that dosing frequency and circadian administration of GC improve skeletal and cardiac muscle glucose and protein metabolism ( ). It is crucial to investigate the effect of intermittent and circadian GC treatment on GC actions in the liver. Finally, most mechanistic studies of GR actions are conducted in rodent models. In contrast, GR-regulated gene transcription in human hepatocytes is poorly understood. The studies of human GR actions could include several aspects. In-depth analyses of the human liver transcriptome in response to acute and chronic GC treatment are imperative for understanding GC action to improve GC therapy. While there are studies of the peripheral blood mononuclear cells transcriptome, adipose tissue transcriptome, and serum metabolome in response to GC treatment, the liver has yet to be studied in this aspect ( ). The studies of the mechanisms underlying human SNPs that affect GR activity and GC's regulation of individual-specific target genes could provide valuable knowledge from both basic science and clinical perspectives ( ). Notably, although certain GRE sequences are conserved in the mouse, rat, and human genome, the studies of human Pck1 and G6pc1 genes suggest that the mechanisms underlying GR activation of these human genes could be different from those of rodent genes.
Overall, although significant progress has been made in unraveling hepatic GR actions over the past several decades, many challenges still remain. With new technologies and concepts, we expect that more exciting discoveries are ahead, which could uncover new perspectives in our understanding of the regulation of mammalian glucose homeostasis. These not only will allow us to design improved GC therapies but also to develop novel approaches to improve metabolic health by targeting GR signaling.
