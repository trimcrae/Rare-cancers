## Abstract
Dendritic cells (DCs) are central players in the immune system, serving as a bridge between innate and adaptive immunity. Their unique abilities to capture, process, migrate, and present tumor-associated antigens to T cells make them essential actors in initiating and regulating anti-tumor immune responses. This review provides an overview of the phenotypic heterogeneity and functional diversity of DC in cancer immunity, with an emphasis on their interactions with other immune cells within the tumor microenvironment, highlighting their collaborative efforts in driving effective antitumor immunity. Additionally, we examined the impact of tumor-derived signals and immunosuppressive mechanisms on DC functions and their implications for DC-based cancer immunotherapy.
Keywords: Dendritic cell, cancer, cancer vaccination, immunotherapy, DC-based vaccines, combination therapies, tumor microenvironment, DC interactions

## Introduction
Our recent understanding of the role of the immune system in antitumor responses and immune escape has led to the development of cancer immunotherapies. Immune checkpoint blockade (ICB) therapy, which is designed for unleashing or enhancing the antitumor activity of T cells, has been approved for treating various cancers. However, the efficacy of ICB is limited to some cancer patients and can trigger severe immune-related adverse events. This has led to the continuous investigation and/or development of new drugs, including monoclonal antibodies, small molecules, and cellular therapies targeting immune cells, with most efforts directed at T cells, while clinically available therapies directly targeting dendritic cells are still limited. Although DCs represent a very small proportion of leukocytes in the circulation and tissues, they play crucial roles in the cancer response by initiating adaptive immunity through antigen-specific T lymphocyte priming. DCs are the most effective antigen-presenting cells (APCs), capturing and processing antigens, migrating to secondary lymphoid organs (SLOs) to interact with T cells, expressing costimulatory molecules, and secreting cytokines leading to T-cell activation. Besides initiating immune responses, DCs also maintain central and peripheral tolerance. In cancer, this tolerogenic ability can become protumoral, aiding immune escape and progression.
To perform their functions, DCs continuously patrol and engulf antigens in their environment. They express a wide range of pattern recognition receptors (PRRs), such as Toll-like receptors (TLRs) and C-type lectin receptors (CLRs), which can detect pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs) or damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs). Engulfment of antigens and/or engagement of PRRs dictate DCs fate toward tolerogenic or immunogenic functions. , Whereas immature DCs excel in antigen uptake, upon maturation, they specialize in antigen presentation and T-cell activation through the upregulation of major histocompatibility complex I and II molecules (MHC-I/II), co-stimulatory molecules (CD40, CD80, and CD86) and chemokine receptors such as CCR7, allowing migration to SLOs. 
DCs can be divided into subpopulations based on ontogeny, phenotype and function ( ). , 
 A widely recognized distinction exists between plasmacytoid DC (pDC), monocyte-derived DC (moDC), conventional DC (cDC, type 1, and 2), and the recently defined DC3, which originate from different lineages and have distinct surface markers and functions.
In addition to transgenic mouse models which are critical for DC comprehension, continuous new insights on DCs granularity and complexity are described in both mouse and human, thanks to high dimensional multiparametric and spatial state-of-the art technologies.
This review details the current knowledge on DC diversity, interactions and functions in cancer context as well as their potential as therapeutic targets.

## Dendritic cell subsets

## cDC1
Type 1 conventional DC (cDC1) stand as a key player for initiating antitumor immunity both at the tumor site and in the lymph node. cDC1 differentiation from hematopoietic stem cells relies on the transcription factors IRF8 and BATF3 , and growth factors such as Flt3-L and GM-CSF. cDC1 strongly express BDCA3 (CD141) and specific markers such as CLEC9A (DNGR1) and CADM1 (NECL2). Besides, XCR1 is expressed by approximately 80% of cDC1 in the blood, lungs, and spleen and has been identified as a marker of terminally differentiated cDC1. 
cDC1 lack SIRPα, making them insensitive to the CD47 “don't eat me” signal used by tumor cells to evade immunity. , cDC1 are able to recognize double-stranded RNA via TLR3, leading to the production of type I and III interferons (IFN), inflammatory cytokines and chemokines, notably IL-12, CXCL9, and CXCL10, to attract and activate T cells at inflammation sites. , We recently reported that their ability to produce IFN-III in breast cancer that was associated with a good prognosis. cDC1 are specialized in the cross-presentation of tumor antigens to T lymphocytes, particularly from necrotic cells, supporting their role in antitumor immunity. The cDC1 transcriptional signature and cDC1 frequencies are associated with an increased abundance of CD8+ T cells and natural killer (NK) cells, a better survival, and favorable responses to ICB in multiple human cancers. , Moreover, cDC1 have been shown to be the only antigen-presenting cells able to transport intact antigens to the lymph nodes and prime tumor-specific CD8+ T cells. , cDC1 were also described to contribute to CD4+ T cell priming through MHC-II and CD40 molecules for optimal antitumor immunity. Expansion and activation of CD103+ cDC1 in melanoma tumors through systemic FLT3L and intratumoral polyI:C injections potentiated responses to PD-L1 and BRAF blockade. CD103+ cDC1 also trigger the recruitment of effector T cells into the tumor via the production of CXCL10. Recent studies have identified multiple tumor-intrinsic mechanisms that impair cDC1 function and development. Tumor-derived prostaglandin E2 may impair cDC1 function through EP2 and EP4 receptors, leading to IRF8 loss. Similarly, tumor-associated IL-6 inhibits cDC1 differentiation through the induction of C/EBPβ in common dendritic cell progenitor. Furthermore, TIM4-dependent capture and engulfment of cell-associated antigens by tissue-resident lung cDC1 are inhibited during the progression of mouse lung tumors. Tumor-secreted gelsolin inhibited CLEC9A-dependent cross-presentation of dead cell-associated antigens by cDC1. Finally, in irradiated tumors, adenosine produced through CD73 reduces cDC1 recruitment, an effect that can be reversed by CD73 blockade. 

## cDC2
Type 2 conventional DC (cDC2) are the major population of human DC found in blood, SLOs, and nonlymphoid tissues. However, their tissue infiltration can be impacted by malignant transformation. In non-small-cell lung cancer (NSCLC), their proportion decreased compared to healthy tissue in contrast to other DC populations. 
Human cDC​​​​2 express notably BDCA1 (CD1c) and CLEC10A. Recent single-cell analyses and mass cytometry revealed four cDC2 subclusters in healthy donor PBMC indicating their diverse functional specificities and response capabilities. Moreover, six clusters of cDC2 have been described across 15 types of cancers. Upon activation, cDC2 promote Th1, Th2, and Th17 responses. While the use of multiple markers may help capture cDC2 heterogeneity, defining clear boundaries between “true cDC2” and other DC subsets that share overlapping features, such as DC3 or monocyte-derived DC (moDC), also called inflammatory DC (infDC), remains a subject of investigation.
cDC2 have emerged as key regulators in the tumor microenvironment, exhibiting both pro- and antitumor functions depending on the context. Notably, cDC2 can migrate from the tumor site to tumor-draining lymph nodes (tdLNs), where they present tumor-derived antigens to CD4+ T cells, thereby initiating antitumor immune responses. Moreover, the intratumoral density of cDC2 has been positively correlated with the presence of CD4+ T cells and improved responses to anti-PD-1 therapy in melanoma patients, particularly those with low levels of Tregs. In addition to directly presenting tumor antigens, cDC2 may also transfer antigens to lymph node–resident dendritic cells, including other cDC2, amplifying the immune response. , Similar to cDC1, CD11b+ cDC2 can cross-present tumor antigens to CD8+ T cells in an IFN-I–dependent manner, thereby contributing to cytotoxic T cell–mediated antitumor immunity. In contrast, it was also demonstrated in a mouse model that cDC2 may contribute to tumor progression by limiting antitumor CD4+  effector T cell responses. 

## DC3
DC3 are the newly identified subset of DC positioned between cDC2 and monocytes. However, neither cDC2 nor monocytes can differentiate into DC3 in vitro, suggesting DC3 is a standalone subset from an independent differentiation lineage that appears to begin early in the myeloid lineage. Nevertheless, a recent study observed that cDC2 acquired a DC3-like phenotype (CD14+ CD1c+ CD163+) mediated by IL-6 and prostaglandin E2 (PGE2) in a patient-derived colorectal cancer organoid model. 
DC3 are found in the blood of healthy donors, SLOs of systemic lupus erythematosus patients, healthy peripheral tissue and tumors. , , , They appear to be enriched in cancer tissue and metastatic lymph nodes compared to healthy counterparts, with an abundance comparable to cDC2. , They are able to infiltrate luminal breast cancer primary tumors and correlate with infiltration of CD8+ CD103+ D69+ tissue-resident memory T cells. 

## Mature DC
Mature DC enriched in activation and immunoregulatory molecules are referred to as mregDC, LAMP3+ DC, or CCR7+ DC according to different studies. Transcriptomic data revealed their conservation across species and tissues and showed that they can be derived from both cDC1 and cDC2 populations. , 
Mature DC are found in lymph nodes, peripheral healthy and inflamed tissues, mainly near blood vessels, but not in blood. , They also express higher levels of costimulatory molecules (CD40, CD80, CD86, and CD83), DC-LAMP, MHC class II molecules and the chemokine receptor CCR7, allowing their migration to lymph nodes. , , This migration of mature DC toward sentinel tdLN is notably necessary for immunoradiotherapy efficacy. Functionally, mature DC express high levels of inflammatory cytokines such as IL-12, IL-10, and TNF-α. 
In the context of cancer, following tumor antigen capture, many mature DC migrate to tdLN, but some remain at the tumor site, where they can also present tumor antigens to tumor-infiltrating T cells. This local antigen presentation enhances immune activation by increasing the cytolytic activity of PD-1+ CD8+ T cells through OX40L. , Interestingly, transcriptional differences exist between mature DC in the tumor microenvironment and those in the tdLN, suggesting distinct roles or functions in regulating immune responses at different locations. 
Upon antigen uptake, tumor-infiltrating mature DC also upregulate a distinct set of immunoregulatory genes (Cd274 (PD-L1), Pdcd1lg2 (PD-L2), and Cd200) and maturation genes (Cd40, Ccr7, and Il12b). It was also demonstrated that PD‑L1 upregulation is induced by the receptor tyrosine kinase AXL, while the induction of IL‑12β depends on IFN‑γ and is modulated by IL‑4. , In contrast, mature DC in peripheral healthy tissues express lower levels of PD-L1, PD-L2, and IL-12, indicating a tumor-specific maturation program promoting immune suppression.
Finally, several studies have demonstrated that the presence of mature DC within tertiary lymphoid structures (TLS), defining so-called mature TLS, is associated with improved prognostic outcomes in several cancer types compared to immature TLS without mature DC. , 

## Plasmacytoid DC (pDC)
Plasmacytoid DCs (pDCs) differ from other DCs because of their unique plasma cell-like morphology and their singular ontogeny and function. Both common dendritic cell progenitor (CDP) and common lymphoid progenitor (CLP) can give rise to pDCs. , Whether CLP-derived pDC must be considered as a dendritic cell or an innate immune cell is a current matter of debate. Historically, contaminating AXL+ “pDC-like” cells, which are part of transitional DCs (tDCs), may have skewed the characterization of proper pDCs. However, even after the removal of pDC-like cells, activated pDCs can acquire antigen-presentation capacity. Phenotypically, human pDCs express CD123 (IL-3Ra) and MHC class II molecules less intensively than cDCs. Notably, TLR-activated pDCs can also cross-present antigens to CD8+ T cells in vitro but this remains to be proven in vivo. 
Under steady-state conditions, pDCs are found in the blood and patrol into SLO through high endothelial venules (HEV), while they can also migrate to peripheral tissues during infections, autoimmune disorders, or tumors. , 
pDCs recognize viral nucleic acids through TLR7 and TLR9, leading to IFN-α production through the IRF7 pathway. However, in the tumor microenvironment (TME), IFN-α production by pDCs is impaired by factors such as TGF-β, TNF-α, and PGE2. , Consequently, altered pDCs can promote immunosuppression and regulatory T cell expansion in various cancers. 

## Transitional DC/AXL+ DC
As mentioned above, recent single-cell transcriptomic analyses with deep phenotyping have identified a new population of AXL+ SIGLEC6+ DC (AS-DC) within Lin-HLA-DR+ cells, also called transitional DCs (tDCs), that encompass the so-called “pDC-like cells,” which can give rise to cDC2. , , , AXL+ SIGLEC6+ tDCs were proposed to originate from pDC progenitors rather than the CDP and can give rise to CD5+ CX3CR1− cDC2 (ESAM+ CX3CR1− cDC2 in mice). Moreover, in response to viral infection, tDCs can also mature and produce IL-1β. Alternatively, it was shown that tDCs can differentiate into CX3CR1+ cDC2 promoting Th17 response, demonstrating the dual potential of this population. 
However, the role of such cells in the context of cancer remains to be explored.

## Langerhans cells
The origin of Langerhans cells (LC) is equivoque, as they share transcription factors with DC and macrophages lineages, respectively. LC can be phenotypically distinguished from cDC2 because of their increased expression of Langerin, CD1a, and EpCAM. , LC are found in stratified squamous epithelia, such as the epidermis and mucosal epithelia, integrated into the epithelial layer via E-cadherin, EpCAM, and tight junction proteins.
LC can induce regulatory T cells and IL-22 in response to CD1a-restricted antigens. In contrast, LCs can mature into tumor cross-presenting cells, produce IL-15, and present mycobacterial glycolipid antigens to CD8+ T cells. Studies in humans have shown ex vivo that after migration in lymph nodes, LCs are able to activate CD8+ and CD4+ T cells toward Th2-type polarization. , Despite differences from cDC1, LCs share strong antigen priming and cross-presentation functions, as assessed by their capacity to induce MART-1-specific CD8+ T cell proliferation following autologous presentation of the MART-121−35 long peptide. Consistently, LCs appear to contribute to the antitumor response in melanoma and oral carcinoma contexts. , , 

## Monocyte-derived DCs (moDCs)
In inflammatory settings or tumor tissues, CD14+ monocytes exhibit the capacity to differentiate into macrophages, monocytic myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSC), or monocyte-derived DCs (moDCs), also called inflammatory DCs (infDCs). , Conversely, moDCs are not observed in healthy tissues. The transcription factors and growth factor receptors predominantly expressed in this DC subsets are shared with both macrophages (EGFR1, EGFR2, and CSFR1) and blood DC (IRF4, FLT3), suggesting that lnfDC/MoDC occupies an intermediate position between these two populations. Notably, in mice, moDCs can be found in the classical migratory cDC2 gate (MHC-IIhi CD11chi CD11b+ CD24+ ), express the activation marker CD169, and cross-present tumor antigens. 
Moreover, chemotherapy such as anthracyclines induces moDC recruitment into the tumor bed in a CCL2/CCR2-dependent manner, allowing local T cell proliferation and efficient tumor rejection. Similarly, in a mouse melanoma model, the administration of local immunostimulatory agents induced tumor and lymph-node moDC, which are necessary and sufficient for T-cell-mediated antitumor response. 

## DC cross-talk with other immune cell populations

## cDCs–T cells
The orchestration of an efficient cytotoxic immune response requires intricate interplay between DC CD4+ helper T cells, and CD8+ cytotoxic T cells (CTL). Even though both cDC1 and cDC2 are able to prime naïve T cells, in vitro and in vivo data have shown that cDC1 are more proficient in CD8 T cell activation compared to cDC2. , Conversely, cDC2 activate CD4+ T cells more efficiently than cDC1. , , Nevertheless, cDC1 are required for the priming of OVA-specific CD4+ T cells to respond to OVA-expressing tumors, as shown in Irf8-deficient mouse models. This finding suggests that cDC1 may be superior in MHC-II presentation to CD4+ T cells compared to cDC2 in some tumor contexts. This may be due to the efficient capture of tumor antigens through specific receptors, such as CLEC9A. Furthermore, following intranasal vaccination of Batf3−/− mice with SIINFEKL-Q11 peptide nanofibers, cDC2 were able to mount a strong CD8+ T cell response in the lung and dLN even in the absence of cDC1. Following this concept, using CD40L-LIPSTIC technology in a melanoma mouse model, cDC1 and cDC2 with similar hyperactivated transcriptional phenotypes (Cd80, Cd86, Cd40, Cd82, Cd200, Cd274, Nfkbiz, and Tnfaip3) were found to interact with CD4+ and/or CD8+ T cells in both tumors and tumor dLNs. Anti-CTLA-4 blockade largely enhanced these interactions, especially with cDC2.
Those cDC1-T cells interactions are rendered possible by the expression of CXCL9/10 and XCR1 by cDC1 that can respectively interact with CXCR3 and XCL1 expressed by CD8+ T cells and CD4+ T cells , ( ).
 In human lung cancer, organized immune hubs were shown to be associated with response to PD-1-blockade immunotherapy. Those so-called “organized stem immunity hubs” were composed of stem-like TCF7+ PD-1+ CD8+ T cells, mature DC CXCL10+ /CXCL11+ macrophages, and CCL19+ fibroblasts but deprived of B-cell follicle characteristics of mature tertiary lymphoid structures (TLS). Inside those hubs, mature DCs were frequently in closer proximity to TCF7+ CD4+ T cells and Treg than to other cells. In parallel, DC-LAMP+ mature DCs were already shown to interact with CD4+ PD1+ CXCL13+ T cells, called T helper tumor (Tht) cells, in human nonsmall cell lung carcinoma. These Tht cells are known to correlate with better survival or response to ICB therapy. Additionally, in D4M.3A-pOVA melanoma mouse models, CCR7+ CXCL16+ IL-15+ mature DCs also interact with CXCR6+ TCF-1neg CD8+ effector-like CTL in perivascular niches and trans-present IL-15 to support their survival in the TME. Additionally, following neoadjuvant PD-1 blockade in hepatocellular carcinoma, responding patients exhibited a higher frequency of triads consisting of mregDCs, stem-like PD-1+ TCF-1+ CD8+ T cells, and CXCL13+ CD4+ T cells compared to nonresponders. Some reports have highlighted the central role of cDC1 beside mature DCs in DC-T cross-talk at the tumor bed. For instance, MHC-IIhiCCR7negCXCL9+ cDC1 form niches with TCF1+ CD8+ T cells, leading to their proliferation and differentiation into IFN-γ+ GzmB+ TIM3+ T cells. These niches are correlated with cancer patient survival. In addition, we recently demonstrated that the spatial organization of cDC1 with CD8+ T cells is critical for the response to ICB in melanoma patients. We found that the median distance between CD8+ T cells and cDC1 was markedly reduced in responding patients while it was not the case for pDCs and mature DCs, underlying the pivotal role of cDC1-T cell cross-talk in the context of cancer. A proficient crosstalk was described in tumors between mature CCR7+ cDC1, which produce high amounts of IL-12 in response to IFN-γ secreted by neighboring T or NK cells. In return, IL-12 drives CD8  antitumor activity and successful anti-PD-1 immunotherapy. 
Moreover, cDC interactions with CD4+ and CD8+ T cells can be concomitant, as recently shown in a melanoma and fibrosarcoma mouse model. Specific CD8+ and CD4+ T cells forming a “triad” with a common CD11c+ DC triggered the most efficient antitumor response compared to separated “dyads” of CD8+-DC and CD4+-DC. Importantly, those intratumoral triads were found to be associated with responses to ICB in human patients afflicted by malignant pleural mesothelioma. These DC–T cell interactions can influence both DC and T cells in a positive feedback loop. For example, in a fibrosarcoma mouse model, cDC1 were essential for the early priming of both CD4+ and CD8+ T cells, while naïve CD4+ T cells licensed cDC1 through CD40 for efficient CD8+ T cell activation, in line with the importance of the CD40‒CD40L interaction. , 

## DCs–regulatory T cells (Treg)
DC subsets can also interact with Treg in the TME, resulting in the upregulation of immune suppressive molecules such as IDO and a lower expression of maturation markers on the DCs surface. Besides, CTLA-4+ Treg infiltration impairs the capacity of tumor-associated cDC2 (TA-cDC2) to induce protective ICOS+ PD-1low CD4+ T cells within the TME in head and neck cancer, thus limiting patient survival. Recently, it was shown in a tumor mouse model that a high dose of neoantigen peptide vaccination induced cDC2-dependent cytotoxic type 1 regulatory CD4+ T cells (Tr1), expressing IL-10, granzyme B, and perforin, able to eliminate cDC1 cells and thus preventing tumor rejection compared to low dose vaccination. Moreover, mregDCs were shown to recruit Treg in the perilymphatic region of the tumor stroma. Treg activation was sustained in Treg-mregDC-lymphatic niches, hampering the migration of tumor antigen-bearing DC to dLNs and antitumor immunity, which predicts the worse outcome in human cancers. 
In breast cancer, especially triple-negative breast cancer, we showed that TA-pDCs with impaired IFN-α production favor Treg expansion, which may contribute to cancer progression. Moreover, in breast and ovarian cancer, tumor-associated ICOSL+ pDCs interact with ICOS+ Treg participating in TA-Treg expansion and the secretion of IL-10+ by TA-CD4+ T cells. Finally, in human ovarian carcinoma, pDCs activate FoxP3+ Treg and induce suppressive IL10+ CCR7+ CD45RO+ CD8+ regulatory T cells. 

## cDCs–NK cells
The bidirectional crosstalk between DC and NK cells has been investigated in several studies. While DC can activate NK cells, the latter can in turn affect the recruitment and maturation of DC. , FLT3L is essential for the in situ development and proliferation of cDCs and pDCs and is produced mainly by NK cells in the TME. , Furthermore, CCL5 and XCL1 secreted by NK cells induce CCR5+ XCR1+ cDC1 migration, promoting tumor immune control. Our team also demonstrated that TNF-α and IFN-γ produced by NK cells significantly increase the ability of moDCs and cDC1 to cross-present tumor antigens, inducing a specific CD8+ T cell response. On the other hand, cDCs release cytokines and chemokines such as IL-12, IL-15, IL-18, and CXCL9/10 to promote NK cell activation and recruitment to tumor sites. This bidirectional cross-talk between DC and NK cells forms a positive loop against cancer growth. However, it can be impaired by the production of PGE2 in cancer cells. Indeed, the latter reduces the accumulation of cDCs by impairing NK viability and chemokine production, causing immune evasion. Finally, in human NSCLC, it was shown that DC–NK crosstalk can also be detrimental, as it impairs DC maturation, particularly through CTLA-4 on NK cells. 

## DCs–γδ T cells
Unlike conventional αβ T cells, which require antigen processing and presentation by dendritic cells (DC) via MHC molecules for priming, γδ T cells typically recognize antigens in an MHC-independent manner. They are able to recognize various surface ligands, such as phosphorylated antigens. Because of their broad antigen specificity and NK-like cytotoxicity, the importance of γδ T cells in tumor immunology has been highlighted. Their cytokine profile and cytotoxicity are seemingly determined by cross-talk with microenvironment components, but tumor-infiltrating γδ T cells have been reported to bear the most favorable prognostic immune population among many cancer types. , However, tumor can subvert γδ T cells to exhibit tumor-promoting functions, altering the surface expression of natural cytotoxicity receptors (NCR), killer Ig-like receptors (KIR), and immune checkpoints. Several studies have suggested a bidirectional interaction between moDCs or pDCs and γδ T cells. Indeed, IFN-I produced by moDCs or pDCs was shown to trigger IFN-γ secretion by γδ T cells (Vγ9Vδ2 subset), which in turn promoted the maturation of moDCs and their secretion of IL12p70. , In the context of melanoma, patients with tumor-infiltrating pDCs are able to trigger a high proportion of CD69+ γδ T cells in vitro showed a better clinical outcomes. Nevertheless, the positive bidirectional crosstalk between pDCs and γδ T cells was impaired in the blood of tumor patients compared to blood of healthy donors. The interplay between the two populations includes several immune checkpoints, such as OX40/OX40L, ICOS/ICOSL, PD1/PD-L1, 41BB/41BB-L, GITR/GITR-L, TIM3, and LAG3. , 

## pDCs–cDC1–cDC2
The division of labor between DC subsets raises the question about their cooperation to efficiently activate the cytotoxic response against cancer antigens. However, where and how the interplay between cDC1, cDC2, pDCs and other DC subsets occurs in the tumor setting are poorly understood.
The analysis of DC subsets within dLNs during vaccinia virus infection showed that infected cDCs were able to recruit CCR5+ pDCs into CD8+ T cell priming site through CCL3/CCL4. Conversely, mobilized IFN-I-producing pDCs were able to increase the expression of maturation markers (CD80, CD86, and CD40) and the antigen presentation capability of XCR1+ cDC1 recruited by activated CD8+ T cells via the XCL1/XCR1 axis. 
Similarly, in the context of cancer, a study conducted on a mouse model inoculated with E.G7 tumor cells and immunized by antigen-pulsed pDCs and/or cDCs showed that, when pulsed in vitro together, pDCs can increase the capacity of cDCs to activate antigen-specific CD8+ T cells and limit tumor growth. This crosstalk was mainly due to cell-to-cell contact, but no specific ligand–receptor was identified.
Recently, we demonstrated that IFN-III produced by cDC1 directly promotes pDC survival, activation, and TLR7 expression in both human blood and tumors, enhancing their responsiveness to TLR7 agonists. Notably, it counteracts TME-induced inhibition, including suppression mediated by TGF-β or PGE2, and restores pDC production of IFN-α. This may be one of the reasons explaining that IFN-III produced by cDC1 is associated with good clinical outcomes in breast cancer. 
Moreover, the TLR7 agonist imiquimod, which is used to treat both local and distant metastases, has been shown to activate pDCs, which in turn produce systemic IFN-I, leading to DC and macrophage sensitization to local imiquimod through the upregulation of TLR7/8. This sequential activation is essential for CD8+ T cell activation and antitumor efficacy. 

## DCs–neutrophils
Neutrophils are key players of innate immunity against extracellular pathogens. They are abundant in the blood stream and invade tissue upon inflammation. Once infiltrated into the tissue, they can produce various alarmins (HMBG-1, LL-37, lactoferrin, etc.) and neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs), which positively impact DC recruitment and maturation. LL-37 can bind formyl peptide receptor 2 (FRP2) on DC or form complex with DNA. The latter drives IFN-α production by pDCs. , In particular, LL-37 was shown to drive the generation of cross-presenting CD103+/CD141+ DC with enhanced costimulatory and migratory capacity, thereby boosting CD8+ T-cell priming and promoting tumor regression in preclinical cancer models. , Conversely, neutrophils can also release factors such as myeloperoxidase (MPO), which can inhibit antigen uptake/processing by DC and reduce their CCR7 expression. 
It is known that human activated neutrophils can induce moDC maturation in vitro through interaction between DC-SIGN on DC and Mac-1 on neutrophils. It was also shown that CCL3 produced by neutrophils during Leishmania infection in mice was required to recruit moDCs to the site of infection and subsequent T cell activation in dLNs. , Additionally, Mycobacterium tuberculosis (Mtb)-infected human neutrophils can produce NETs leading to IFN-I production by surrounding pDCs. 
In the context of cancer, neutrophils can also produce various factors with either protumor (IL-10, iNOS) or antitumor (TNF-α) activity, highlighting their potential role in modulating DC functionality. Further research on DC and neutrophil interplay in cancer remains to be performed.

## DCs–other myeloid populations
The crosstalk between DC and other myeloid populations, such as macrophages and mast cells, also represents a pivotal axis of immune regulation in the TME. In mammary carcinoma models, tumor-associated macrophage (TAM)-derived IL-10 suppresses IL-12 production by DC during chemotherapy, which is consistent with clinical data linking IL12A expression to better responses in breast cancer. 
Importantly, therapeutic strategies targeting these suppressive myeloid compartments can enhance DC-based immunotherapy. For instance, TAM depletion using CSF-1R inhibitor improved both the survival and antitumor efficacy of bone marrow–derived DC vaccines in mesothelioma cancer models. Similarly, vaccination with genetically modified DC producing IL-12 and IL-18 not only amplifies T cell responses but also reduces TAM density in a murine colon cancer model (MC38). 
Furthermore, in melanoma models, IFN-γ-driven activation of the enzyme aconitate decarboxylase (ACOD1) in macrophages increases itaconate production, which inhibits DC antigen presentation and cross-priming, decreases CD8+ T cell responses and promotes tumor immune escape. 
Finally, mast cells have also been shown to contribute to tumor-mediated immunosuppression by enhancing MDSC activity through CD40L–CD40 signaling in a preclinical prostate cancer model. , 

## Tumor cell death impact on DC functions
The mode of tumor cell death, such as accidental necrosis or regulated cell death (RCD), including apoptosis, immunogenic cell death (ICD), pyroptosis, necroptosis, or ferroptosis, affects how DC receive “licensing” signals and capture antigens. , In particular, current therapies, such as chemotherapy, radiotherapy, antibody‒drug conjugates (ADCc), and other targeted agents, can trigger ICD, thereby converting tumor cells into sources of immunostimulatory antigens that enhance both innate and adaptive antitumor immunity. ICD is characterized by exposure of calreticulin (CALR) and the release of ATP, HMGB1, and type I interferons. Surface CALR promotes engulfment via CD91 and supports DC maturation and antigen presentation. HMGB1–TLR4 signaling enhances TLR-dependent DC activation and migration to tdLN, while extracellular ATP engages P2RX7/P2Y receptors on DC to activate the NLRP3 inflammasome and induce IL-1β secretion, which is required for effective CD8⁺ T-cell priming. , In parallel, tumor DNA and cGAMP activate the cGAS‒STING axis in DC to drive IFN-I production, which is essential for efficient cross-priming of cDC1. Overall, these DAMPs act as “find‑me/eat‑me” signals that attract DCs and promote antigen uptake, maturation, and cross‑presentation. While molecular pathways may vary between ICD and other RCD such as pyroptosis (caspase‑gasdermin-driven lytic death) or necroptosis (RIPK3-MLKL‑dependent necrotic death), they can lead to immunogenic outcomes that strongly support DC activation and antitumor response. Notably, ferroptosis (iron‑dependent oxidative death) can exert a dual influence on DC as early ferroptotic tumor cells can release DAMPs that activate DC maturation and cross-priming, whereas late or uncontrolled ferroptosis may instead impair DC function and blunt antitumor immunity. , Moreover, by providing abundant tumor antigens in an inflammatory milieu, ICD, and ICD-like death may foster epitope spreading, thereby broadening the repertoire of tumor-reactive T cells. , 

## DC for cancer therapies
Over the past decade, the identification of immune checkpoint receptors and their targeting have revolutionized cancer treatment. However, due to strong tumor-induced immunosuppression already established in cancer patients, ICB can fail to restore efficient antitumor immunity, especially under adjuvant or metastatic conditions. Moreover, while ICB is more likely to be effective in “hot” tumors that are infiltrated by previously primed tumor-reactive T cells, alternative strategies must be considered for “cold” tumors, which are characterized by a lack of T cell infiltration.

## Tumor antigen delivery as a cancer vaccine
For direct antigen delivery, the type of antigen is crucial, and several approaches are being developed using shared or personalized antigens. The emergence of whole-exome sequencing and RNA sequencing has allowed huge advances in the identification of a more tailored neoantigen repertoire. Tumor antigens are broadly classified into two categories: tumor-associated antigens (TAAs) and tumor-specific antigens (TSAs). TAAs are self-antigens that are aberrantly expressed or overexpressed in tumor cells but are also found, to a lesser extent, in normal tissues. Due to their presence in nonmalignant cells, TAAs typically exhibit low tumor specificity and limited immunogenicity. In contrast, TSAs, or neoantigens, are generated from tumor-specific somatic mutations and are absent from normal tissues. This endows TSAs with high tumor specificity and strong immunogenic potential; however, their novelty to the host immune system also results in minimal central tolerance, which may impact immune recognition.
For instance, TEDOPI is a peptide-based vaccine composed of 9 synthetic peptides from 5 TAA (HER-2/neu, CEA, MAGE 2, MAGE 3, and p53). Ninety percent of lung cancer patients express at least one of these TAA and derived peptides are presented by HLA-A2, which occurs in up to 45% of the population. Interestingly, the phase III study ATALANTE-1 showed for the first time an overall survival gain for the vaccine strategy compared to the standard of care (mainly docetaxel or pemetrexed) in ICB-resistant NSCLC patients. Further clinical trials are currently ongoing to explore the clinical benefit of adding the TEDOPI to immunotherapy or chemotherapy in ICB-resistant patients. However, peptide-based cancer vaccines alone are poorly immunogenic and require strong adjuvants to produce efficacious response. The importance of testing and adding a proficient costimulation was shown in MAGE-A3 trials. Melanoma-associated antigen 3 (MAGE-A3) is a cancer-testis antigen that is preferentially expressed in melanoma, myeloma, and NSCLC. A randomized phase 2 trial showed that a MAGE-A3 vaccine including TLR9 agonist (AS15) induced stronger humoral and T cell response and showed greater clinical benefit compared to TLR4-agonist-adjuvant (AS02B) MAGE-A3 vaccine. , However, a phase 3 clinical trial (DERMA) using AS15-MAGE-A3 vaccine in patients with stage IIIB or IIIC melanoma in the adjuvant setting did not show significant differences compared to placebo. Thus, improvement of adjuvants, identification of patient-specific neoantigens, and better selection of patients susceptible to mount a strong immune response need to be established to drive successful therapies.
More recently, driven by technological advances, the use of mRNA to deliver tumor antigens has emerged. It offers several advantages, including rapid and scalable manufacturing, low toxicity, and the capacity to encode a wide range of tumor antigens. The delivery of exogenous self-amplifying mRNAs (SAMs) encoding tumor-specific antigens into host somatic cells enables endogenous antigen production and subsequent activation of antigen-specific immune responses. Even though this strategy was initially developed for cancer treatment, its development has dramatically accelerated during the SARS-CoV-2 pandemic, resulting in the large-scale production of mRNA-based vaccines against SARS-CoV-2 over the past 5 y. , This targeted mechanism may lead to more durable responses and an improved quality of life for patients while also contributing to the prevention of tumor recurrence and metastasis. For instance, the phase 2b KEYNOTE-942 trial (NCT03897881) investigated the efficacy of mRNA-4157 (V940)—a personalized mRNA-based cancer vaccine encoding patient-specific neoantigens—in combination with the immune checkpoint inhibitor pembrolizumab, as adjuvant therapy for patients with high-risk resected melanoma. The vaccine was custom designed for each patient based on the unique mutational landscape of their tumor. The results demonstrated a significant improvement in recurrence-free survival (RFS) for combination therapy compared to pembrolizumab monotherapy. The combination tended to reduce the risk of recurrence or death by 44%, with 18-month RFS rates of 78.6% in the combination arm versus 62.2% in the monotherapy arm. The treatment was well tolerated, with a favorable safety profile and no unexpected adverse events beyond those associated with pembrolizumab. Given these promising clinical outcomes, mRNA-4157 (V940) is the first personalized mRNA-based tumor vaccine to progress into phase 3 clinical trials, representing a major milestone in the development of individualized cancer immunotherapies. , 
Finally, given the differences in CTL activation ability of DC subsets, targeting a specific DC subpopulation for antigen delivery might increase vaccine immunogenicity. In particular, targeting and activating cDC1 in vivo seems to be an attractive strategy. For example, peptide vaccination with a synthetic OVA peptide fused with XCL1 showed an increased CTL response when compared to OVA with free XCL1. Moreover, an adenovirus vaccine may be modified with an anti-CLEC9A antibody to target vaccine delivery to the cDC1 compartment. Finally, the association of a proper adjuvant can improve the antitumor response induced by in vivo DC targeting. In a phase II trial of high-risk melanoma patients treated with a DC-targeted anti-DEC205-NY-ESO-1 vaccine, the use of an FLT3L adjuvant increased the activation of circulating DC and CD8+/CD4+ T cells compared to vaccine alone. However, treatment-related toxicities such as local skin reactions and lymphoproliferative disorders, have limited the development of this strategy in larger studies.

## Ex vivo pulsed DC as a cancer vaccine
Instead of providing tumor antigens to DC in vivo, another strategy is to pulse DC ex vivo and deliver them to patients. In this context, the sipuleucel-T vaccine (patient-derived moDC pulsed ex vivo with PAP, a prostate-specific antigen, and GM–CSF) was approved in prostate cancer, because of its significant but small survival benefit over the placebo. Other clinical trials using pulsed autologous DC are currently ongoing or terminated with preliminary mixed results. For instance, in the phase 2 clinical trial NCT01446731, patients with metastatic castration-resistant prostate cancer (mCRPC) received a DC-based vaccine in combination with docetaxel chemotherapy. To do so, autologous DC were electroporated with mRNAs encoding prostate-specific antigen (PSA), prostatic acid phosphatase (PAP), survivin, and human telomerase reverse transcriptase (hTERT). The combination therapy demonstrated an acceptable safety profile, with minimal toxicity observed. However, no significant improvements in survival outcomes were reported compared to chemotherapy alone. Conversely, the phase 1/2 trial NCT01197625 investigated the use of autologous DC transfected with total RNA extracted from high-risk prostate cancer following radical prostatectomy or with synthetic mRNAs encoding hTERT and survivin. The treatment was well tolerated, with no significant adverse events reported. Notably, 11 out of 20 patients remained free of biochemical recurrence (BCR) for a median of 96 months. Among patients with extraprostatic extension, those who exhibited immunologic responses to the vaccine experienced significantly longer BCR compared to nonresponders.
Moreover, a phase 2 clinical trial assessed the efficacy of TriMix mRNA—comprising mRNA encoding CD40L, CD70, and caTLR4 (a constitutively active variant of Toll-like receptor 4), and mRNAs encoding one melanoma-associated antigen—for electroporating autologous DC and delivering them in combination with the anti-CTLA4 ipilimumab in patients with advanced melanoma. This combination therapy elicited significant tumor-specific CD4⁺ and CD8⁺ T cell responses, particularly among patients with stage III disease. Importantly, patients demonstrating strong IFN-γ–producing CD8⁺ T-cell responses exhibited a longer overall survival, underscoring the correlation between functional T-cell responses and clinical outcomes. 
Notably, given the complex process involved in isolating and loading autologous DC ex vivo in a clinical setting, allogeneic DC and “semi-allogeneic” DC (with one haplotype mismatch) have also been investigated in clinical trials. Overall, allogeneic DCs seem to induce less antitumor response than autologous or “semiallogeneic” DCs do. However, in a phase II trial, vaccination of relapsed high-grade glioblastoma patients using heterokaryons (allogeneic DC fused with autologous tumor cells) significantly improved overall survival compared with that of matched nonvaccinated patients, showing the interest of boosting antigen presentation with allogeneic effects. 
MoDCs are used for the majority of DC-based therapies because of their relatively easy generation protocol via culturing patient bone marrow or blood-derived myeloid cells with GM–CSF and IL-4. MoDC vaccines are relatively safe and can induce antitumor immunity, but their clinical efficacy is limited. However, the majority of clinical trials employing this strategy were conducted prior to the emergence of modern technologies for cancer antigen identification and the adoption of differentiation protocols resulting in immature moDCs, which may have limited their immunogenic ability. As we described above, another reason for this limited efficacy may be the less efficient antigen cross-presentation and CTL activation of moDC compared to cDC, which limits their ability to induce an in vivo antitumor response. Finally, cDC1 and moDC vaccines seem to differ from a mechanistic point of view. Indeed, it was shown in mouse models that moDC-based vaccine efficacy requires antigen transfer to host cDC1 to mount an efficient immune response, while cDC1-based vaccines are independent of host cDCs. 
In this regard, more recent trials have evaluated autologous pDC and cDC2 vaccines in metastatic melanoma patients, but even though antigen-specific T cell responses have been shown in phase I/II trials, no relapse-free survival benefit was reached in a phase III trial comparing the combination of autologous pDC and cDC2 adjuvant therapy versus placebo in resected stage III melanoma. 
As described above, antigen uptake by cDC1 could induce strong activation of TAA-specific CD8 T lymphocytes by cross-presentation. Ex vivo-generated cDC1 are similar to their natural counterparts in terms of their gene expression profile, response to poly(I:C) and cytokine production. A phase I trial showed feasibility and early signs of antitumor activity induced by an intratumoral co-injection of autologous unpulsed cDC1 and cDC2 in ICB-refractory melanoma patients. Further studies are needed to evaluate the efficacy of vaccination using pulsed cDC1 alone in humans.

## Promoting endogenous DC activity in vivo
Directly activating DC within the tumor microenvironment may be an alternative strategy to enhance local immune priming and promote a robust systemic antitumor response against initial and new tumor epitopes. This concept is reinforced by emerging evidence highlighting the importance of epitope spreading as a key mechanism contributing to effective and sustained antitumor immunity. This process involves the expansion of the T-cell response to recognize tumor-associated antigens beyond the initially targeted epitope. Typically, epitope spreading is triggered following therapeutic interventions that mediate tumor cell lysis, leading to the release of additional tumor antigens into the microenvironment. These antigens are subsequently captured, processed, and presented by DC thereby facilitating the activation of novel T-cell clones or the re-engagement of pre-existing tumor-reactive T cells. 
For instance, CD40 agonists can be exploited to activate DCs, particularly cDC1. The anti-CD40 agonist antibody sotigalimumab associated to nivolumab (anti-PD-1), was shown to provide a long-lasting tumor response in a subgroup of melanoma patients who progress to immune checkpoint inhibitors. Conversely, the association of sotigalimumab and gemcitabine/nab-paclitaxel and/or nivolumab did not show relevant clinical benefits in advanced pancreatic cancers. Interestingly, despite the absence of benefit to the intention-to-treat population, higher pretreatment frequencies of cross-presenting DC and Tbet+ CD4 Th1 cells were associated with longer survival. Several treatment-related toxicities, including cytokine release syndrome (CRS), hepatotoxicity, and thrombocytopenia were observed in a majority of patients.
Thus, the development of new strategies with limited side effects and even more specific delivery to DCs has been explored. For instance, bispecific antibodies targeting CD40 activation preferentially on DC by coupling CD40 agonist arm with CD11c-, DEC-205-, or CLEC9A-targeting arms showed a better safety profile compared to CD40 antibodies while triggering potent antitumor activity in mouse models. 
Moreover, as largely used for T cells, targeting immune checkpoint receptors on DC may be interesting both at precancer and invasive cancer stages. In particular, TIM-3 blockade at the precancer stage was shown to decrease the tumor burden through enhanced antigen presentation in lung adenocarcinoma mouse models. Furthermore, targeting TIM-3 in triple-negative and luminal B mouse models improved the response to paclitaxel chemotherapy in a Batf3-, Irf8-, and CXCR3-dependent manner. Alternatively, the efficacy of PD-L1 blockade seems to depend more on PD-L1 expressed on DCs rather than on tumor cells. Further investigations of other immune checkpoint blockades that more specifically target DCs in preclinical and clinical trials remain to be performed.
Similarly, AcTakines (activity-on-target-cytokines) consist of mutated cytokines coupled to a specific targeting domain to reduce the side effects of cytokine-based strategies to boost DCs in vivo. A bispecific CLEC9A/PD-L1-targeted actaferon has been recently engineered to specifically release the IFN I payload to CLEC9A+ cDC1 and PD-L1-positive immune and cancer cells. Bispecific CLEC9A/PD-L1-targeted actaferon drove tumor control in a mouse model, accompanied by changes in the levels of various cell types, including myeloid cells, DC antigen-specific CTLs, NK, and NKT cells, as well as a decrease in tumor-resident Treg.
Conversely, engineering DC to directly overexpress cytokines, which can then be released in a paracrine manner or displayed on the cell surface, is a promising strategy to optimize cytokine delivery to interacting DC and surrounding T and B cells. , , Notably, human DC progenitors engineered to express IL-12 and FLT3L were able to differentiate into cDC1 and suppressed tumor growth in melanoma and liver models without requiring preloading of antigens. 
Additionally, DC metabolic modulation represents another promising approach, since DC activity is tightly linked to their bioenergetic state. For example, the increase in aerobic glycolysis (glycolytic burst) and fatty acid synthesis (FAS) induced by PRR engagement allows DC activation. In contrast, excess lipid droplet biogenesis after PPARα signaling or fatty acid oxidation induced by AMPK activation skews DCs toward a tolerogenic state. , Importantly, the activity of metabolic pathways may vary depending on DC subsets, features of the microenvironment (e.g., pH, hypoxia) and the timepoint following antigen engulfment. Interestingly, the anti-diabetic agent metformin has been tested in the clinic to treat cancer patients because of its anti-proliferative effects on tumor cells, but it has also been described as an inducer of tolerogenic DC, which may explain mitigated clinical outcomes. Further preclinical and clinical investigations of pharmaceutical molecules to boost DC metabolism and override TME immunosuppression are needed.
Beyond the activation of tissue-infiltrating DC, recalling antigen presenting cells at the tumor site might be an interesting strategy to overcome resistance to cell-based therapy in cold tumors. For instance, systemic administration of the growth factor FLT3L can recall and activate CD103+ DC in a mouse model, enhancing tumor responses to anticancer treatments. , In this context, the use of chemokines as immunological adjuvants in cancer vaccines is currently under investigation. In preclinical studies, CCL21 has demonstrated the ability to enhance the recruitment and activation of DC and T lymphocytes, thereby improving antitumor immune responses across multiple cancer types. , 
Ex vivo-derived MoDCs engineered with adenoviral vectors to express CCL21, enhanced intratumoral infiltration of CD4+ and CD8+ T cells as well as DEC205+  cDC1 in murine models. This approach was subsequently evaluated in phase I clinical trials in NSCLC, where a subset of patients exhibited systemic antigen-specific immune responses, increased tumor infiltration by CD8⁺ T cells, and upregulation of PD-L1 within the tumor microenvironment, highlighting its potential for combination with PD-1/PD-L1 blockade. , 
Moreover, considering the central role of T cell‒DC cross-talk within the TME to elicit an effective antitumor response, a new family of bispecific dendritic‒T cell engager (BiCE) was developed with the aim of promoting physical interaction between DC and T cells in the TME. CLEC9A-PD1-targeted BiCE is the first-in-class molecule able to promote the interaction between cDC1 and PD1+ T cells in dLN. In a preclinical B16-OVA melanoma mouse model, it promoted cDC1-CD8+ cross-talk and antitumor immunity at both the tumor bed and dLN.
Finally, a more recently innovative approach was proposed to reprogram tumor cells to exhibit dendritic cell-like functions, with the capacity to present antigens and mount tumor-specific T cell responses. In a murine immune-competent melanoma model, intratumoral delivery by adenoviral vectors of key transcription factors, PU.1, IRF8, and BATF3 (PIB), led to in vivo generation of cDC1-like cells within tumors. Moreover, it resulted in TME remodeling, the formation of TLS and the expansion of polyclonal cytotoxic and memory T cells. Interestingly, this strategy demonstrated a synergistic therapeutic effect with anti-PD-1 or anti-CTLA-4 immunotherapies. However, further investigations are needed to understand the clinical relevance of this approach, especially in the metastatic setting.

## Future of DCs in cancer
The advent of high-dimensional technologies such as mass cytometry (CyTOF), flow/spectral cytometry, and single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) permitted extensive characterization of previously identified DC populations as well as the discovery of new DC subsets. , , However, the tissue environment and the activation state of DC can significantly influence their phenotype, transcriptomic profile, and functional properties. Thus, distinguishing between new populations of cells from simply different activation states of the same cell population represents a technical challenge. While genetic fluorescent trackers can help to address this challenge in mouse models, human studies need to rely on multiomics approaches (including epigenetics, proteomics, transcriptomics, and ribosome profiling) to better delineate the evolution of DC populations within the TME, healthy tissues, and SLO.
Moreover, the above technologies require tissue dissociation and cannot provide the spatial context to demonstrate cell-to-cell interactions or niche localization, which could hint at the functional properties of subsets. In this context, technologies that preserve spatial information such as multiplex immunofluorescence, spatial transcriptomics, and proteomics will be essential for accurately defining the localization of DC within their ecosystem and better understanding of their neighborhood and functions.
Moreover, DC must be seen as an evolutive compartment throughout tumor development in terms of phenotype, interaction, and function. The comparison of DC in the TME and SLO at different cancer stages will bring key insights to predict cancer progression and establish new immune interception therapies and cancer vaccines. , , Despite the poor clinical efficacy of historical DC-based vaccine strategies for solid cancers, especially in advanced stages, new technologies promoting DC activation, DC–T cell cross-talk, and limiting the treatment-related side effects at the early stage may pave the way for a new era of DC-based cancer therapies.
From this therapeutic perspective, understanding of DC subset heterogeneity and the development of protocols to generate custom-made DC will allow to improve DC vaccines. In particular, the use of antigen-pulsed moDCs may be replaced with more tailored DC such as cDC1, coupled with adjuvants, loaded with tumor-specific antigens, and equipped with receptors/ligands, using mRNA technology, to efficiently activate T cells once they are administered to patients. The choice of antigen is a critical step that depends on the patient's tumor characteristics (tissue of origin of tumor cells, DNA mutations/amplifications, RNA expression/translation, and immunopeptidome). For this purpose, it is important to consider not only neoepitopes derived from nonsynonymous DNA mutations but also peptides (in particular, MHC-associated peptides assessed by mass spectrometry) derived from unmutated aberrantly expressed genomic regions or noncanonical splicing, which may represent the majority of peptides presented by tumor cells. 
Regarding strategies to harness DC potential in vivo, we believe that future treatments will likely need to integrate several synergistic mechanisms highlighted in this review. These include tumor antigen specificity, DC-targeted delivery, the induction of ICD, and direct DC stimulation, as key components for an effective DC-driven antitumor response. In this context, ADC and bi-/tri-specific antibodies represent promising avenues to achieve such combinatorial effects. Ideally, the next generation of drugs would be designed as multifunctional antibodies or molecular platforms capable of simultaneously engaging a tumor antigen, a DC receptor, and a DC-activating molecule while also delivering a cytotoxic payload to induce ICD or ICD-like cell death.
Finally, trained immunity, defined by epigenetic imprinting and metabolic rewiring of myeloid progenitors and innate immune cells, represents a powerful potential strategy to strengthen DC functions to prevent cancer emergence or recurrence. , This mechanism has been extensively described using BCG vaccination or other inflammatory stimuli, such as heat-killed M. tuberculosis or β-glucans. , Translating these insights into clinical practice will require further development and careful evaluation of potential risks such as autoimmunity.
