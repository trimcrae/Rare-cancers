## Abstract
The incidence of cancer globally is increasing, predominantly because of an aging population and bringing with it substantial challenges. Cancer vaccines are based on the premise that there are unique features of a cancer or agents causing a cancer which are recognizable by the immune system. Cancer vaccines can be classified as preventive and therapeutic. We searched Trialtrove and identified 2100 clinical trials of cancer vaccine published January 1, 2000 to November 1, 2024, numbers of trials have been explosive growth since 2008: 37% were phase-1, 21%, phase-1/phase-2, 32%, phase-2, 2%, phase-2/phase-3, 5%, phase-3 and 1%, phase-4. Most studies are in the USA (45%). 1% were in infants, 5% in children, and 94% on adults. The most common cancer studied is non-small-cell lung cancer (N=298; 12%) followed by melanoma (N=277; 11%). In conclusion, the research of cancer vaccines is rapidly advancing, particularly in therapeutic cancer vaccines. An increasing number of multicenter clinical trials of cancer vaccines are being initiated globally, and these efforts hold significant promise for bringing hope to patients with cancer.

 Anticancer vaccines are based on the premise that there are unique features of cancer, or of agents causing cancer, which are recognizable by the immune system. Cancer vaccines can be classified as preventive and therapeutic. Preventive vaccines target infections associated with cancer development, typically viruses such as some strains of human papillomavirus (HPV) and Epstein-Barr virus (EBV) and hepatitis B virus (HBV). In theory, gallbladder cancer could be prevented by immunization against Schistosoma hematobium antigens.
Therapeutic cancer vaccines are based on the premise some cancer cells produce specific cancer-associated or cancer-specific proteins. The latter are termed neo-antigens. In theory, recognition of neo-antigens by the immune system can inhibit tumor progression or eliminate cancer cells. 
Historically, vaccines have been used to prevent infectious diseases such as smallpox, measles, mumps, rubella, and tuberculosis. As such, vaccines to HPV, EBV, and HBV fall into this category. Therapeutic cancer vaccines are different. Their goal is not prevention but control or cure of an existing cancer. Preventive cancer vaccines are generic and can be given to anyone at risk to develop the related cancer. Many, but not all therapeutic cancer vaccines are person-specific. When a cancer-associated antigen is common to a type of cancer, the therapeutic vaccine for cancer can be generic. An example is Sipuleucel-T for prostatic cancer. At other times, a therapeutic cancer vaccine is directed at a neo-antigen(s) specific to an individual. An example is NEO-PV-01 (NCT02897765). Such vaccines are termed personalized. There are four types of therapeutic cancer vaccines: (1) cells; (2) peptides; (3) virus; and (4) nucleic acid.
Two vaccines are approved by the US FDA to prevent cancer and one to treat cancer, specifying that all three approved vaccines are generic ( ). Cancer vaccine approvals in other countries include CIMAvax in Cuba for non-small cell lung cancer. In a cancer context, BCG is used as an immune adjuvant in bladder cancer.
 We interrogated Trialtrove (https://clinicalintelligence.citeline.com/) for trials of anticancer vaccines. We found data from a total of 2466 trials and 2100 trials from January 1, 2000 to November 1, 2024. A number of cancer vaccine trials have been increasing with an annualized growth rate of −64.29% to 265%. Numbers of clinical trials have been >100 per year since 2009 save 2022 ( ). Clinical trials have been done for >59 cancer types. The most frequent cancers are non-small-cell lung cancer (298 trials; 12%). More than 200 clinical trials were done for 8 cancer types including non-small cell lung cancer, melanoma (277 trials; 11%), breast cancer (265 trials; 11%), colorectal cancer (230 trials; 9%), pancreas cancer (214 trials; 9%), prostate cancer (212 trials; 9%), bladder cancer (210 trials; 9%) and glioblastoma (203 trials; 8%). 100–200 clinical trials were done for 7 cancers including ovary cancer, unspecified solid cancers, head and neck cancer, liver cancer, gastric cancer, kidney cancer, and esophagus cancer. 48 clinical trials were in diverse metastatic cancers ( ).
 346 trials (14%) were open-label trials, 1187 (49%) have been completed, 235 (10%) are in the planning stage, and 554 (23%) were stopped. Most trials 907 (37%) were phase-1, 527 (21%), phase-1/ phase-2, 798 (32%), phase-2, 40 (2%), phase-2/ phase-3, 125 (5%), phase-3 and 26 (1%), phase-4. Among the completed phase-3 and phase-4 trials, the most common cancer types were bladder cancer (10), non-small-cell lung cancer (8), and prostate cancer (8; ). 27 trials (1%) were in infants, 98 (5%) in children, and 1781 (94%) in adults ( ).
1078 trials (48%) were sponsored by academia, 741 (33%) by pharma, 301 (13%) by government, 120 (5%) by cancer cooperative groups, 14 by not-for-profit funding entities, and 9 by industry (generic; ).
1062 of trials (43%) were done in the Americas, 508 (20%) in Europe, 816 (33%) in Asia, 81 (3%) in Oceania, and 16 (1%) in Africa ( ). 986 trials (45%) were or are being done in the USA. The top 10 other countries were in Europe and Asia. Germany (146 trials; 7%), the UK (109 trials; 5%) and Spain (107 trials; 5%) were the top European countries. Japan (397 trials; 18%) and China (294 trials; 13%) were the top Asian countries. Australia (78 trials; 4%) was top country in Oceania. Egypt (nine trials) was the top African country ( ).
Next, clinical trials involving preventive cancer vaccines and BCG were excluded, and 2312 therapeutic cancer vaccine clinical trials were retained and analyzed. We found therapeutic vaccine clinical trials grew at a rate paralleling that of overall cancer vaccines. The number of trials surged in 2009 consistently exceeding 100 per year thereafter, save 2020–2022, probably because of the SARS-CoV-2 pandemic ( ). 49% of these trials have been completed, 38% were phase-1 and 32%, phase-2 ( ). Non-small cell lung cancer, melanoma, breast, colon, and pancreatic cancers were the top five in therapeutic vaccine clinical trials. Trials in bladder and cervix cancers declined ( ). 44% of trials were in the Americas, 32% in Asia, and 21% in Europe ( ). The USA, Japan, China, Germany, and the UK were the top five sites ( ).
The clinical application prospects for cancer vaccines are highly promising. Machine learning, artificial intelligence, and whole-exome sequencing facilitate the screening of highly immunogenic neoantigens. Innovations in adjuvant design, such as the stimulator of interferon genes agonist-based ER-targeting molecules, which effectively deliver antigens and amplify T-cell activation compared with conventional adjuvants. Lipid nanoparticle technology enables efficient lymph node-targeted mRNA delivery, augmenting immune cell infiltration into tumor and suppressing tumor growth. Strategic combination therapies further demonstrate clinical potential: mRNA-4157 (V940), an individualized neoantigen therapy, combined with pembrolizumab significantly improves recurrence-free survival and distant metastasis-free survival in resected high-risk melanoma versus pembrolizumab. Collectively, these technological advances and integrated therapeutic approaches are accelerating the clinical translation of cancer vaccines.
Despite the promise of innovative cancer vaccines, there remain substantial challenges, including better methods to detect neo-antigens, manufacturing issues, and detection of clinically relevant immune responses. Most therapeutic cancer vaccine trials use a survival endpoint. Cancer vaccines act slowly, so short-term responses may be inappropriate. Lack of predictive response biomarkers such as mutation topography, HLA type, and immune responses is limiting. Optimal timing, dose, and schedule of most therapeutic cancer vaccines are unknown as is whether they should be used alone or combined with other immune therapies such as checkpoint inhibitors.
In conclusion, technological progress and the implementation of standardized clinical trials will expedite the clinical translation of cancer vaccines, providing renewed promise for patients with cancer.
