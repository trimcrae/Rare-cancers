## Abstract
Neoantigens exhibit high immunogenic potential and confer a uniqueness to tumor cells, making them ideal targets for personalized cancer immunotherapy. Neoantigens originate from tumor-specific genetic alterations, abnormal viral infections, or other biological mechanisms, including atypical RNA splicing events and post-translational modifications (PTMs). These neoantigens are recognized as foreign by the immune system, eliciting an immune response that largely bypasses conventional mechanisms of central and peripheral tolerance. Advances in next-generation sequencing (NGS), mass spectrometry (MS), and artificial intelligence (AI) have greatly expedited the rapid detection and forecasting of neoantigens, markedly propelling the development of diverse immunotherapeutic strategies, including cancer vaccines, adoptive cell therapy, and antibody treatment. In this review, we comprehensively explore the discovery and characterization of neoantigens and their clinical use within promising immunotherapeutic frameworks. Additionally, we address the current landscape of neoantigen research, the intrinsic challenges of the field, and potential pathways for clinical application in cancer treatment.
Keywords: Neoantigen, Immunotherapy, Cancer vaccine, Adoptive cell therapy, Antibody-based therapy, T-cell receptor mimic antibody, Immune checkpoint inhibitor

## Introduction
The malignant transformation of cells is contingent upon the progressive accrual of DNA damage, which subsequently results in the synthesis of tumor-associated antigens (TAAs).[ ] Nonetheless, cancer cells have established strategies to withstand anti-tumor immune responses. To counteract these immune evasion strategies, a variety of cancer immunotherapies have been designed, including adoptive cell therapies (ACTs), cancer vaccines, and antibody-based therapies.[ ] These therapeutic approaches primarily target TAAs, which results from proteins that are abnormally overexpressed or inappropriately expressed in malignant tissues compared to normal tissues. Although TAAs exhibit highly restricted expression in normal tissues, they represent non-mutated self-antigens with the potential to lead to central T-cell tolerance, thereby diminishing the efficacy of T-cell responses.[ ] In contrast, tumor-specific antigens (TSAs) arise uniquely within tumor cells as a result of genetic mutations, offering a more promising target for tumor-directed immunotherapy.[ ]
The concept of neoantigens, a subset of TSAs, was initially explored by Coley in the late 19th century,[ , ] but only gained traction with Gross’s demonstration of antitumor immunity in mice in the mid-20th century.[ , ] Decades later, De Plaen’s group identified the first T cell-recognized neoantigen from a murine tumor model.[ , ] Subsequent discoveries revealed neoantigens in human cancers [Figure ]. The early 2000s saw the first successful clinical application, with neoantigen-reactive T cells inducing tumor regression in melanoma patients.[ , ] Advances in next-generation sequencing (NGS) and mass spectrometry (MS) have significantly accelerated the clinical translation of neoantigen-based cancer therapies.[ – ]
 Neoantigens—distinguished as aberrant proteins absent in normal tissues—result from abnormalities at the genomic, transcriptomic, and proteomic levels within malignancies[ ] [Table ]. Somatic genomic mutations, including gene fusions, single-nucleotide variants (SNVs), and insertions and deletions (INDELs), are principal drivers of neoantigen generation.[ ] Post-transcriptional events, including the formation of aberrant transcript variants, and post-translational modifications (PTMs) expand the landscape of potential neoantigens. Alternative messenger RNA (mRNA) processing, which includes splicing events and the expression of purportedly non-coding regions, enhances the variety of neoantigens.[ , ] Furthermore, dysregulated translation and atypical PTMs during carcinogenesis can produce proteomic variants, contributing novel sources of tumor-specific neoantigens.[ , ] Viral proteins integrated into the host genome, particularly in virus-induced tumors, represent another class of neoantigens that are markedly different from endogenous proteins and capable of triggering T-cell responses.[ ]
 Neoantigens can be captured by antigen-presenting cells (APCs) in the tumor microenvironment (TME), which include dendritic cells (DCs), macrophages, and B cells. APCs subsequently transfer the captured neoantigens to lymph nodes and present processed peptides enclosed within major histocompatibility complex (MHC) molecules to T cells. Neoantigens processed through endocytosis are mostly presented on MHC-II to activate CD4+ T cells, whereas endogenous antigens are displayed on MHC-I to stimulate CD8+ T cells.[ ] Once activated, T cells migrate to the TME and identify the neoantigen displayed by tumor cells, resulting in their destruction. The heterogeneous nature of neoantigens shields the neoantigen-specific T-cell repertoire from central tolerance, and their tumor-restricted expression ensures targeted therapeutic action without collateral damage to normal tissues.[ ]
The identification of immunogenic neoantigens with diverse, patient-specific origins is crucial for developing effective immunotherapies. Advancements in NGS, MS, and artificial intelligence (AI) have expedited the discovery of patient-specific neoantigens and the development of neoantigen-based immunotherapeutic approaches.[ , ] Continuous advancements and interdisciplinary integration of biotechnology, materials science, chemistry, and synthetic biology promise to enhance the efficacy and safety of these strategies.[ , ] This review offers an in-depth evaluation of the techniques used for neoantigen prediction and the therapeutic applications of neoantigen-driven immunotherapies. Additionally, it addresses the opportunities and challenges in applying these therapies, suggesting potential solutions for overcoming clinical hurdles.

## Identification and Selection of Neoantigens
Given the substantial variability among tumor types, lesions, and individual patients, the identification of diverse immunogenic neoantigens is a critical step in advancing effective immunotherapies. Leveraging NGS techniques allows for comprehensive analyses of genomic mutations together with human leukocyte antigen (HLA) alleles, facilitating the in silico prediction of neoantigen peptidomes.[ ] This immunogenomic strategy is particularly advantageous for anticipating the emergence of neoantigens from aberrant genomic, transcriptomic, and epigenomic alterations.[ ] Furthermore, an immunopeptidomic approach employing MS enables the examination of peptides presented by the MHC, thus revealing neoantigens that originate from proteomic variants[ ] [Figure ]. Recently, several T-cell receptor (TCR)-guided neoantigen discovery methods have been designed for the systematic identification and mapping of immunogenic neoantigens.[ ]
 

## Application of NGS technology in neoantigen prediction
Immunogenomic strategies leverage NGS technologies, such as whole-exome sequencing (WES) and RNA sequencing (RNA-seq), to compare genetic differences between tumor and normal tissues. WES facilitates the mapping of tumor-specific genetic abnormalities by examining both tumor and normal DNA.[ ] Whole-genome mate-pair sequencing further enhances this analysis by detecting chromosomal rearrangements, enabling the identification of neoantigens from structural variations.[ , ] RNA-seq provides insights into the expression of mutant alleles and alternative splicing variants, as well as other biological details, including copy number variations, and the presence of transposable elements and microbial contamination. Compared with other NGS methods, full-length transcriptome sequencing strategies, exemplified by the MinION device, a palm-sized portable device for sequencing, offer rapid and precise gene sequencing with enhanced affordability and convenience. This method covers entire transcripts at sufficient depth and approximately 90% accuracy, allowing for the detection of neoantigens arising from frameshift mutations, irregular isoforms, and allele-specific transcription and splicing.[ , ]
Beyond sequencing of DNA and RNA, MS-based proteomics enables the detection of neoantigens at the protein level by directly analyzing immunoprecipitated, MHC-bound peptides. MS has significantly advanced the validation of in silico-predicted neoantigens derived from NGS data by directly examining the antigen repertoire, collectively known as the immunopeptidome.[ ] Notably, a substantial proportion of mutation-derived neoantigens predicted by immunogenomic techniques do not appear in the proteomic profiles of peptides linked to HLA molecules. Therefore, it is necessary to validate the predicted neoantigens by comparing the MS data with predicted peptide sequences. Mapping the tumor HLA ligandome yields potent targets for therapeutic neoantigen-specific cancer immunotherapy, particularly for uncommon HLA alleles and HLA class II ligands.[ ] Furthermore, MS is used with NGS to improve the detection of neoantigens generated by non-coding RNAs, dysregulated post-translational modifications (PTMs), and alternative proteasome splicing during oncogenic transformation. Thus, MS-based proteomics is the most reliable method for validating neoantigens derived from aberrant DNA sequences and RNA expression, and for detecting neoantigens that are not identifiable through DNA and RNA analyses alone.

## Advances in bioinformatics algorithms used to predict neoantigens
The collection of parallel genomic, transcriptomic, and proteomic data of patient tumor tissues and normal tissues allows for the prediction of potential neoantigen peptides. A typical in silico neoantigen prediction workflow includes key steps, such as mutation calling, HLA typing, and filtering and prioritizing based on HLA-binding affinity.[ ] Numerous integrated technologies have been developed to identify and classify neoantigens from different sources. The two primary categories of these tactics are integrated scoring system strategies and filtering strategies based on stepwise analysis. Efficient one-stop prediction tools can accept data (e.g., WGS and RNA-seq data) as input, apply multiple filtering steps, and use selected cutoff criteria (e.g., sequence coverage, gene expression, binding affinity of peptides and MHC, and variant allele frequency) to compile a list of potential neoantigens while minimizing false positives. Integrated scoring systems quantitatively assess neoantigen immunogenicity by considering essential neoantigen characteristics, such as the affinity difference between mutated and normal peptides, variant allele frequency, and gene expression levels. Recently, machine learning models for evaluating neoantigen immunogenicity have been proposed, enhancing prediction accuracy and reducing false positives.[ ]
In addition to mutation detection and neoantigen prioritization, the highly polymorphic character of each patient’s HLA locus and its potential for mediated antigen presentation also influence neoantigen scoring and ranking. Humans possess over 24,000 unique alleles that encode HLA class I (-A, -B, and -C) and class II (-DQ, -DP, and -DR) molecules.[ , ] Furthermore, tumor-induced loss of heterozygosity in HLA can impair neoantigen presentation, aiding immune evasion. Determining a patient’s HLA genotype helps define a repertoire of tumor-specific neoantigens recognizable by T cells. Computational methods, such as ATHLATES, LOHHLA, HISAT genotype, HLA-HD, and Optitype, use WES- or WGS-derived data to determine HLA genotypes.[ , ] Other tools, such as arcasHLA, seq2HLA, and HLAProfiler, utilize RNA-seq data to provide unbiased data coverage of both parental alleles.[ ] The integrated analysis of neoantigen anchors, which facilitate peptide binding to an individual patient’s HLA I alleles, improves the neoantigen identification process. The accuracy of HLA antigen prediction can be improved by the use of MS proteomic data, particularly HLA ligandome data. NetMHCpan is a “pan-specific” machine learning system that targets MHC-I alleles to increase tumor neoantigen prediction efficiency using antigen-HLA binding affinity and MS data. Tools such as EDGE and MARIA further enhance HLA antigen prediction accuracy through deep learning architectures by integrating proteomic and transcriptomic data.[ , ]
The recognition of peptide-bound MHC (pMHC) by TCRs is crucial for triggering an immune response, and the prediction of neoantigens involves assessing their potential for specific recognition by high-affinity T-cell clonotypes. Neoantigen-specific T-cell recognition is predicted using in silico methods, such as NetCTL/NetCTLpan and Neo-intline, which compute a composite score based on proteasomal hydrolysis, HLA binding, transporter associated with antigen processing (TAP) transport, and TCR recognition.[ , ] Databases such as McPAS-TCR and VDJdb provide annotations of the TCR repertoire from various perspectives to predict TCR-specific recognition of antigens. TCR sequences linked to different diseases are listed in McPAS-TCR, while TCR:pMHC interactions are described in depth from an epitope-centric perspective in VDJdb.[ , ] TCRs that recognize the same epitopes are grouped using clustering techniques such as pMTnet and GLIPH. Utilizing these databases, machine learning and deep learning can be applied to enhance the efficiency of TCR-peptide/pMHC binding predictions.[ , ]

## Methods for validating the immunogenicity of neoantigens
Before developing clinical treatment plans targeting candidate neoantigens, it is necessary to experimentally validate their immunogenicity. The immunogenicity of neoantigens can be verified or screened using a variety of T cell-based techniques, such as T-cell immunogenicity tests, multicolor MHC tetramer labeling, enzyme-linked immunospot (ELISpot) analysis, and T-cell library analysis.
The most straightforward technique for evaluating the immunogenic potential of putative neoantigens is T-cell immunogenicity analysis. This involves stimulating T lymphocytes taken from healthy donors or patients with a comprehensive array of mutated peptides identified through cancer exome or RNA-seq analysis. Flow cytometry is used to measure activation markers, including 4-1BB and OX-40, and ELISpot analysis is used to measure interferon (IFN)-γ production, which helps evaluate the responsiveness of neoantigen-specific T lymphocytes grown in vitro.[ ] Multicolor MHC tetramer technology employs techniques such as DNA barcoding, lanthanide coding, or fluorochrome coding to provide a highly sensitive method for evaluating T-cell reactivity to numerous potential epitopes.[ ] While these methods have excellent sensitivity, their limited throughput and strong reliance on epitope prediction effectively cover just a tiny portion of the human MHC-I allele repertoire.
Advanced methods based on unbiased systematic TCR ligand discovery have significantly enhanced the study of neoantigen immunogenicity. For instance, yeast-displayed pMHC libraries can be utilized to identify neoantigen-specific TCRs.[ ] However, the production of soluble TCR reagents is time-consuming, and random peptide recognition may not reflect physiological TCR-pMHC interactions. Innovative strategies using co-culture systems have emerged to overcome these limitations. Antigenic epitopes can be screened using signaling and antigen-presenting bifunctional receptors (SABRs). SABRs present pMHC complexes extracellularly, triggering intracellular signaling upon binding to cognate TCRs. Screening large SABR libraries allows for the identification of epitopes that elicit TCR-like signals.[ ] Another approach utilizes endocytosis to precisely label target cells undergoing TCR-pMHC interactions, enabling the identification of neoantigen-specific TCRs through the isolation and sequencing of these cells.
TCR sequencing enables the systematic, direct analysis of the TCR repertoire activated by a neoantigen. Combined with neoantigen prediction guided by WES and TCR sequencing of short-term peptide-pulsed T-cell cultures, the mutation-associated neoantigen functional expansion of specific T cells (MANAFEST) assay can effectively characterize neoantigen-specific TCR Vβ clonotypes, regardless of HLA haplotypes. MANAFEST can utilize formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded or frozen tissue samples to detect neoantigen-specific T cells, or liquid biopsies to track neoantigen-specific T-cell responses for real-time monitoring of the efficacy of immunotherapy. To further enhance detection sensitivity, TCR sequencing can be integrated with single-cell RNA-seq (scRNA-seq) to discover tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs) with paired TCR sequences and high expression of inteferon-γ (IFN-γ) and interleukin (IL)-2.[ ]
The biomechanically assisted T-cell triggering for large-scale exogenous pMHC screening (BATTLES) method uses spectrally coded beads to present pMHC, allowing the examination of neoantigen-specific T-cell responses under biophysical forces. T-Scan is a TCR epitope scanning method that operates independently of prediction algorithms by evaluating the physiological cytotoxic activity of T cells, thereby enabling the exploration of a broader antigen space compared with previous technologies.
Overall, these advanced methods for TCR ligand discovery significantly enhance the study of neoantigen immunogenicity and provide new targets for the development of immunotherapies.

## Optimization of neoantigen selection criteria
Progress in personalized immunotherapy has been consistently hindered by the lack of identifiable tumor neoantigens. The integration of immunogenomic and immunopeptidomic strategies, particularly the combination of NGS-based genomic and transcriptomic sequencing data with MS-based HLA-associated peptidomic data, can be utilized to systematically and precisely evaluate personalized neoantigens. The use of advanced deep learning algorithms further enhances the sensitivity and specificity of neoantigen detection.[ ] The ongoing enhancement of computational techniques is limited by the accessibility of datasets containing verified immunogenic neoantigens.[ , ] Nevertheless, for clinical applications, a comprehensive, efficient computational workflow or a standardized benchmark for existing computational neoantigen detection methods is still necessary. Such a streamlined computational approach can also facilitate the assessment of neoantigens as prognostic biomarkers and predict responses to tumor immunotherapy based on large datasets.
Beyond computational predictions, T-cell-based antigen discovery methods can be applied for impartial mapping of immunogenic neoantigens. Various pMHC libraries, such as yeast display, bead display, and SABR libraries, offer flexible and scalable neoantigen epitope screening.[ , , ] Rather than solely relying on TCR-pMHC binding affinity, the T-Scan approach utilizes cytotoxic T lymphocyte (CTL) activity, facilitating the investigation of a wider antigen spectrum without dependence on prediction algorithms.[ , ] The straightforwardness and scalability of these T-cell ligand identification methods are essential for examining the immunogenicity of prospective neoantigens. Furthermore, single-cell sequencing and TCR sequencing-based approaches enable rigorous immune monitoring in clinical therapies, validating the accuracy of predicted neoantigens and the efficacy of immunotherapy, which is crucial for improving neoantigen-based therapeutic strategies.[ , ]
Identification of specific immunogenic neoantigens and matching TCRs is a crucial yet time-consuming step in the development of personalized cancer therapies. The significant variability in mutations and their manifestation in tumors across different cancer types pose a substantial challenge. Further investigation into neoantigen prediction, and the establishment of standardized methodologies for the identification and selection of pertinent neoantigens, are essential to enhance the efficacy and broader application of this therapeutic approach.

## Neoantigen-Based Therapeutic Vaccines
Neoantigen-based treatments can precisely target tumors with specific immunogenic epitopes, thereby addressing the limitations of TAAs and offering a viable avenue for the advancement of tailored tumor immunotherapy. Current developments in cancer immunotherapies targeting neoantigens encompass cancer vaccines, ACTs, and antibody treatment.[ ] Vaccines activate the host’s immune system to generate specific humoral and/or cellular immunological responses, thereby conferring immunity against harmful microbes and preventing infectious illnesses.[ ] Utilizing these immunity-enhancing properties, therapeutic vaccines for tumors have been created to direct and amplify the immune response against malignant neoplasms.[ ] Several forms of neoantigen-based vaccines are under clinical investigation for their efficacy in diverse cancer types, including cell-free vaccines (e.g., peptide/proteins and nucleic acids), vector-based vaccines (e.g., viral vector), and cell-based vaccines (e.g., DCs and genetically engineered cells)[ , ] [Figure ]. Furthermore, delivery systems utilizing nanomaterials and extracellular vesicles (EVs) can simultaneously transport antigens and adjuvants, thereby guaranteeing stable and effective dissemination of neoantigens into target cells and augmenting the strength, quality, and longevity of the immune response triggered by vaccination.
 

## Cell-free molecular-based vaccines
Neoantigen epitopes designed to initiate antigen presentation and subsequent immune responses can be formulated as peptides or nucleic acids for introduction into the body.[ ] Peptide-based neoantigen vaccines have emerged as a compelling strategy within the rapidly evolving field of personalized immunotherapy. Their high specificity, cost-effectiveness, and established safety profile have garnered considerable attention in the pursuit of targeted cancer therapies.[ ] Neoantigen peptides derived from tumor-specific mutations can be produced using a variety of methods. Genetically encoded long peptides allow for flexibility in sequence design and scalable production, fused polypeptides enhance the breadth and efficacy of the immune response, and chemically synthesized short peptides offer high purity and precise control over the final product. Addressing the challenges of tumor heterogeneity and HLA haplotype diversity, multi-epitope peptides containing several neoantigens are often favored. These longer peptides elicit robust T-cell responses by engaging a wide array of TCRs. Peptides derived from fusion gene sequences (e.g., SYT–SSX and BCR–ABL) have successfully induced targeted CTL responses without causing severe adverse effects.[ , ] Vaccination with a DNAJB1–PRKACA fusion neoantigen peptide elicited enduring T-cell responses and, in contrast to prior therapies, achieved almost 21 months of relapse-free survival (RFS).[ ] Similarly, customized neoantigen peptide vaccinations of melanoma patients have been shown to elicit persistent T-cell responses and augment tumor-specific cytotoxicity. A study of eight patients with advanced melanoma showed that, nearly 4 years after vaccination with the NeoVax personalized neoantigen peptide, all patients were alive, with 75% exhibiting no active disease. These patients demonstrated long-term persistence of neoantigen-specific T-cell responses, diversification of T-cell clones, and T-cell tumor infiltration, underscoring the vaccine’s efficacy in eliciting durable antitumor immune responses (NCT01970358).[ ] Another clinical trial indicated that the neoantigen peptide vaccine EVX-01, when combined with programmed death-1 (PD-1) therapy, was also safe, induced broad T-cell responses, and achieved an objective response rate (ORR) of 67% in patients with metastatic melanoma, suggesting its potential for further exploration (NCT03715985).[ ]
A multifaceted delivery system has enhanced the efficient transport of peptide vaccines to lymph nodes and augmented their immunogenicity.[ , ] The cGAMP-STINGΔTM complex co-functions as a delivery vehicle and potent adjuvant for peptide vaccination, activating the stimulator of IFN genes (STING) signaling independently of the endogenous cellular STING protein.[ ] Lipid/hydrophobic adjuvant conjugation has also demonstrated potential, enhancing immunogenicity and safety in the absence of conventional adjuvants, and exhibiting efficacy through several administration routes.[ ]
Nucleic acid vaccines in the form of RNA or DNA share similar advantages with peptide vaccines, including cost-effectiveness and independence from HLA. These vaccines allow the co-administration of several neoantigens in a solitary dosage, thereby activating both cellular and humoral immune responses to tumors. Unlike DNA, which must enter the nucleus to be translated, mRNA only requires cytoplasmic localization, thus mitigating risks associated with genome integration. Compared with peptide vaccines, mRNA vaccines possess notable advantages, particularly the facilitation of PTMs and the accommodation of a variety of epitopes without HLA constraints, allowing multiple neoepitopes to be encoded within a single mRNA molecule. Having overcome early concerns about mRNA instability, innovations in delivery systems—primarily cell-based methods—now effectively protect mRNAs from rapid degradation by RNases.[ , ] The in vitro transcription (IVT) technique remains the predominant method for synthesizing neoantigen-coding mRNAs. Capping structures are added to enhance stability and reduce the immunogenicity of the synthesized neoantigen mRNAs, which are then purified, encapsulated using liposomes or polymers, delivered into cells, and translated to activate immune responses.[ , ] The efficacy of the COVID-19 mRNA vaccines has expedited the advancement of mRNA-based cancer vaccines. A lipopolyplex-delivered mRNA cancer vaccine elicits strong neoantigen-specific T-cell responses and exhibits antitumor efficacy in mice.[ ] Furthermore, synthetic self-amplifying mRNAs (samRNAs) that encode a replicase with RNA-dependent RNA polymerase (RdRp) activity enable sustained high expression of antigens, offering a novel avenue for the development of mRNA-based neoantigen cancer vaccines. In the phase I clinical trial of pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma, 16 patients who underwent surgical resection received sequential treatment with atezolizumab, autogene cevumeran (an individualized vaccine), and mFOLFIRINOX (modified folinic acid, fluorouracil, irinotecan, oxaliplatin). The eight patients who responded developed high levels of vaccine-induced, neoantigen-specific CD8+ T cells, with a median T-cell lifespan of 5.5 years. A 3-year median follow-up indicated that responders had significantly longer RFS than non-responders (not reached vs. 13.4 months; hazard ratio = 0.14; 95% confidence interval: 0.03–0.60; P = 0.007). Notably, 85% of the CD8+ T-cell clones persisted into the memory phase, producing IFN-γ and tumor necrosis factor (TNF)-α, and degranulating upon neoantigen rechallenge. These results suggest that mRNA vaccines can effectively induce long-lasting functional T cells, fulfilling a critical requirement for cancer vaccines (NCT04161755).[ ] In another phase I/II study, researchers evaluated the safety and tolerability of a therapeutic vaccine encoding 20 neoantigens derived from common driver mutations, in combination with immune checkpoint blockade (ICB), in patients with advanced solid tumors. This study employed a ChAd68-based vaccine to deliver samRNA targeting Kirsten rat sarcoma virus (KRAS)-related neoantigens. Mid-term phase I results showed good vaccine tolerability; however, the overall response rate was 0%, with a median progression-free survival (PFS) of 1.9 months and a median overall survival (OS) of 7.9 months. Further analysis revealed that T-cell responses favored the vaccine-encoded TP53 neoantigen rather than the KRAS neoantigen expressed in the patients’ tumors, indicating a neoantigen immune hierarchy. This prompted the development of an optimized vaccine specifically targeting KRAS-related neoantigens, which is now undergoing evaluation in a phase II clinical trial.[ ] In addition, a novel circular RNA vaccine platform has demonstrated superior efficacy in treatment and prevention of hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) in various murine tumor models, eliciting a robust T-cell immune response.[ ]
Unlike RNA and peptide formulations, DNA vaccines can accommodate any sequence while maintaining stability and solubility, and feature cost-efficient production and convenient storage without extensive cold-chain requirements. DNA sequences encoding neoantigens are inserted into prokaryotic expression vectors, replicated in bacteria (e.g., Escherichia coli), and administered by subcutaneous or intramuscular injection combined with electroporation to induce neoantigen expression and immune activation.[ ] DNA vaccines are particularly proficient at boosting CD8+ and CD4+ T cells to augment both humoral and cellular immune responses while activating innate immunity through their double-stranded DNA structure.[ ] DNA vaccines targeting multiple neoantigens have shown similar therapeutic efficacy to peptide-based vaccines against mammary tumors in mouse models (E0771 or 4T1).[ ] Furthermore, the combination of therapeutic DNA vaccination with anti-PD-1 therapy has shown effectiveness in controlling tumor growth. A recent phase 1/2 clinical trial assessed the therapeutic efficacy of a neoantigen-encoding DNA plasmid administered alongside an IL-12 therapeutic cancer vaccine and pembrolizumab in patients with advanced HCC.[ ] The combination therapy exhibited a favorable safety profile, with injection site reactions as the most common adverse event (41.6%). Among the 36 study patients, the ORR was 30.6%, including three patients who achieved complete responses. Neoantigen-specific T-cell responses were observed in 86.4% of evaluable patients. These findings indicate the effectiveness of the neoantigen DNA vaccine in inducing antitumor T-cell responses and the promising clinical activity of the combination therapy in advanced HCC (NCT04251117).[ ] In a clinical context, polyepitope neoantigen DNA vaccines, when combined with ICB therapy, elicit strong immune responses against neoantigens in patients with pancreatic neuroendocrine tumors.[ ] However, the clinical efficacy of DNA vaccines is constrained by their suboptimal transfection efficiency and immunogenicity. Carrier materials, including biopolymers and polymeric nanoparticles (NPs), are being developed to improve the stability of DNA and mitigate its rapid degradation and clearance from the body.[ , ] DNA vaccines targeting neoantigens are under clinical investigation in various solid tumor types, including pediatric recurrent brain tumors, glioblastoma, pancreatic cancer, advanced small cell lung cancer (SCLC), and triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) [Table ].
 

## Cell-based vaccines
As the most potent APCs, DCs are crucial for the successful clinical deployment of cancer vaccines. To enhance the potency, quality, and duration of the immune response, it is essential to ensure the stability and efficient delivery of neoantigens to these target cells.
DCs can be harvested from patients, infused with neoantigens formulated as peptides, RNA, or DNA, and reintroduced into the body to stimulate an immune response.[ ] In the mid-1990s, mRNA-transfected DCs were pioneered as a form of cancer vaccine. Mouse-derived DCs transfected with tumor-derived whole RNA or synthetic mRNA encoding specific antigens (e.g., ovalbumin) effectively stimulated antigen-specific CTLs in vitro and conferred protection against tumor challenges in vivo. Notably, the regression of lung metastases was successfully induced by transfecting DCs with whole or poly(A)+ RNA from an F10.9 melanoma, which is characterized by minimal immunogenicity.[ ] This approach was quickly transitioned to human studies and initiated in clinical trials using RNA as the antigen source.[ ] While early methodologies relied on “passive pulsing” to introduce mRNA into DCs, electroporation soon became the preferred technique, markedly enhancing the efficiency of mRNA loading without necessitating liposomes.[ ] Transfection of DCs with whole-tumor mRNA has been shown to stimulate T-cell responses and enhance survival in cancer patients, exhibiting strong immune responses. A phase 1/2 clinical trial demonstrated that a melanoma vaccine comprising DCs loaded with autologous tumor mRNA had a favorable safety profile and effectively induced tumor-specific immune responses in 31 patients with metastatic melanoma, achieving an intradermal vaccination response rate of 80% (16/31).[ ] Furthermore, immune responders exhibited significantly prolonged median OS compared with controls (14 months vs. 6 months, P = 0.030), with 1-year and 2-year survival rates of 43% and 23%, respectively. These findings support further investigation of this vaccine, particularly in combination with ICB (NCT01278940).[ ] These mRNA-derived vaccines have been shown to safely induce T-cell responses to neoantigens in patients with different types of malignancies, including renal, prostate, uterine, ovarian, melanoma, colorectal, and pancreatic cancers, as well as acute myeloid leukemia (AML) and multiple myeloma. By introducing functional proteins into DCs, mRNA delivers neoantigens while providing additional signals that promote cell activation and maturation, ultimately bolstering the immune response.
Neoantigens may also be administered to DCs through other modalities, including synthetic peptides, autologous whole-tumor lysates (WTLs), and tumor cell fusion. Transfection of DCs with mRNA enables neoantigen generation without the necessity for prior epitope identification, whereas peptide-loaded DCs require precise epitope prediction and the synthesis of full-length proteins or peptides for optimal presentation via the HLA repertoire in patients. Personalized neoantigen peptide-pulsed DCs have been evaluated in numerous clinical trials for different malignances. The variety of neoantigen-specific T cells in melanoma is increased when DCs are activated with synthetic long peptides along with the adjuvant poly (I:C). For example, DCs exposed to a peptide generated from the paired box-forkhead homologue in rhabdomyosarcoma (PAX-FKHR) fusion protein elicited destruction of rhabdomyosarcoma cells, and DCs pulsed with neoantigen peptides from fusion proteins such as Ewing sarcoma breakpoint region 1-Friend leukemia integration 1 (EWS/FLI-1) and PAX3/FKHR led to immune responses against the fusion breakpoint peptide.[ ] The combination of neoantigen peptide-pulsed autologous DC vaccines with chemotherapy or ICB is being actively investigated for advanced pancreatic and lung cancers (NCT05195619, NCT04627246, and NCT02956551). In clinical trial NCT02956551, a personalized neoantigen peptide-based DC vaccine exhibited a favorable safety profile, with all adverse events classified as grade 1 or 2, and demonstrated modest efficacy in patients with metastatic lung cancer. The trial recorded a disease control rate of 75%, an ORR of 25%, a median PFS of 5.5 months, and a median OS of 7.9 months.[ ] These clinical studies provide new insights and directions for cancer immunotherapy.
Utilization of autologous WTLs to generate neoantigen-specific immune responses offers a more cost-effective and convenient alternative to patient-specific neoantigen sequencing and computational analysis. WTLs, which contain both mutated neoantigens and non-mutated TAAs, may mitigate immune evasion and resistance. Nevertheless, the prevalence of nonimmunogenic self-antigens and immunosuppressive factors in WTLs can impede DC maturation and T-cell activation.[ ] WTLs pulse DCs with different antigens, amplifying T-cell responses, including the generation of clones with enhanced avidity against known neoepitopes. Tumor lysate processing, using methods such as irradiation and hypochlorous acid oxidation, has been shown to increase immunogenicity.[ , ] Neoantigen loading can also be achieved through selective electrofusion of DCs with tumor cells, preserving viability while boosting antigen presentation and co-stimulation capacity. DC–tumor cell fusion vaccines have been investigated for kidney, breast, melanoma, and multiple myeloma malignancies, showing promising immune responses leading to disease regression in subsets of treated patients.[ , ]
Overall, the utilization of neoantigen-based DC vaccines significantly broadens the antigen repertoire and enhances the antitumor immune responses, offering a safe immunotherapeutic strategy for various solid tumors.[ ]

## Vector-based vaccine platforms
Vector-based platforms, including viral vectors, NPs, and EVs, enhance in vivo expression of vaccine neoantigens, thereby boosting immune targeting efficacy.

## Viral vector-based vaccine platforms
Recombinant viral vectors, including SV40 virus, adenovirus, poxvirus, herpes virus, vesicular stomatitis virus, and lentivirus, have been widely used to deliver antigens. Compared with traditional subunit vaccines, viral vector vaccines can induce high immunogenicity and durable immune responses without the use of adjuvants. Adenovirus-based technology has received widespread attention in neoantigen vaccines. Combined with anti-PD-1 antibodies, adenovirus-based tumor neoantigen vaccines can eradicate large tumors in mouse models.[ ] Compared with anti-PD-1 antibodies alone, activated TILs induced by the combined use of gorilla adenovirus (GAd) vectors and antibodies exhibit a more diverse TCR repertoire.[ ] The utilization of non-human viral species, including chimpanzee adenovirus (ChAd) and GAd, in vectors has circumvented preexisting human immunity to adenoviruses, thereby enabling the clinical investigation of adenovirus-based neoantigen vaccines.[ , ] In a phase 1/2 study (NCT03639714), a personalized heterologous ChAd68-mediated samRNA neoantigen vaccine, when combined with nivolumab and ipilimumab, exhibited a favorable safety and tolerability profile, with no reported dose-limiting toxicities, and effectively induced durable CD8+ T-cell responses.[ ] In addition, some patients with microsatellite-stable colorectal cancer experienced improved OS, indicating that this therapeutic approach merits further investigation in larger, randomized trials.[ ] A neoantigen vaccine based on GAd20 has also been combined with pembrolizumab to treat metastatic melanoma. Combined with pembrolizumab, the NOUS-PEV vaccine, which expresses 60 neoantigens, demonstrated good safety in treatment-naïve patients with metastatic melanoma, with no serious adverse events observed (phase Ib trial NCT04990479). The trial results showed that the vaccine expanded the T-cell population and enhanced the antitumor response.[ ]

## NP-based vaccine platforms
Recent advancements in nanomaterial-based delivery systems, designed to co-deliver antigens and adjuvants, have gained significant attention for their augmentation of cancer vaccination strategies. NPs offer an alternative to viral vectors for the effective transportation of neoantigen epitopes and adjuvants to lymphoid organs, thus enhancing neoantigen presentation. Various NPs, including lipid NPs, exosomes, protein nanocages, bacterial membrane material-based carriers, and polyplexes, improve vaccine efficacy by facilitating delivery and tissue penetration.
Lipid-based NPs, including those using polyethyleneimine (PEI), support cancer vaccine production. In studies, PEI NPs have activated neoantigen-specific CD8+ T cells.[ ] Mesoporous silica micro-rods have been used for neoantigen delivery, eliminating lung metastases in mice. Clinical trials have explored lipid NP-formulated mRNA vaccines (mRNA-4157, mRNA-4650) combined with PD-1 inhibitors for solid tumors. Moderna’s novel neoantigen mRNA cancer vaccine, mRNA-4157/V940, is formulated with adjuvant lipid NPs and encodes up to 34 neoantigens tailored to the unique tumoral mutations of each patient (NCT03313778). Results from a phase 2b clinical trial (NCT03897881) demonstrate that the combination of mRNA-4157 with pembrolizumab reduces the risk of recurrence and death by nearly half and decreases the risk of metastasis by 62% in melanoma patients. Based on these findings, the US Food and Drug Administration designated this combination as a breakthrough therapy and initiated a phase 3 clinical trial (NCT05933577) to further evaluate its efficacy in high-risk melanoma patients.[ , ] Another clinical study targeting metastatic gastrointestinal cancer demonstrated that the mRNA-4650 vaccine is safe and capable of eliciting mutation-specific T-cell responses. Although no objective clinical responses were observed in the four patients, the results suggest that combining this vaccine with ICB or adoptive T-cell therapy may provide potential clinical benefits for patients with common epithelial cancers (NCT03480152).[ ] Advanced RNA-lipoplex vaccines have demonstrated significant potential in eliciting robust CD4+ and CD8+ T-cell immune responses against neoantigens (NCT02410733 and NCT02316457).[ ]
Self-assembling DNA-RNA nanocapsules deliver neoantigen peptides and adjuvants (e.g., DNA CpG), enhancing CD8+ T-cell memory formation and impeding cancer progression. High-density lipoprotein-mimicking nanodiscs have shown superiority over soluble vaccines in clinical trials, exhibiting potent CTL activation. Platforms such as SNP-7/8a feature neoantigen peptide conjugates linked with Toll-like receptor (TLR) 7/8a adjuvants to enhance T-cell immunity.[ ] NPs used for viral vaccines have also shown efficacy against Epstein-Barr virus (EBV) and hepatitis B virus in models, highlighting the promise of NP-based platforms for novel immunotherapies.

## EV-based vaccines
EVs derived from tumors or immune cells have emerged as promising vaccine platforms for improving the maturation of DCs and the presentation of neoantigens. Tumor-derived EVs effectively transport diverse tumor antigens to DCs, facilitating neoantigen cross-presentation. These vesicles are rich in immunostimulatory molecules that prompt DCs to emit innate immune signals. For example, the integration of tumor-derived exosomes, nanoclay, and gelatin methacryloyl into hydrogels enables the staggered release of proinflammatory molecules, such as CCL21a and granulocyte-macrophage colony-stimulating factor (GM-CSF), which subsequently recruit and activate DCs.[ ] In addition, cancer cell apoptotic bodies containing both neoantigens and TAAs offer robust immune activation and maintain high clinical adaptability. By integrating apoptotic bodies into immunomodulatory strategies, this vaccine platform can alleviate immunosuppression via cascade amplification, thereby enhancing immunotherapy.[ ]
Beyond tumor-derived EVs, those sourced from DCs serve as potent neoantigen presenters to immune cells. DC-derived EVs transfected with chimeric RNA have been used to create a cell-free anticancer vaccine known as DEXA-P, offering scalability, genetic modifiability, and extended shelf life compared with traditional cell-based vaccines.[ ] These exosome vaccines, adorned with targeting ligands, antigens, and peptide immunoadjuvants, tailor responses to a patient’s unique tumor genetic makeup, engaging both innate and adaptive immunity to provoke strong antitumor effects. This method potentially allows for ongoing antigenic “boosting” without the need for exhaustive neoantigen characterization.[ ]
To mitigate host DNA contamination, ovalbumin-loaded red blood cell-derived EVs are being employed to encourage MHC-I antigen presentation and T-cell priming.[ ] EVs can also be engineered with anti-DEC-205 (CD205) receptor antibodies or early secreted antigenic target 6 KDa (ESAT-6) to enhance DC antigen presentation and T-cell activation.[ , ] Human serum exosomes likewise facilitate peptide neoantigen delivery, improving DC uptake and antigenic peptide immunogenicity. Owing to their accessibility, serum exosomes utilized as delivery systems expedite the clinical application of peptide antigen-based personalized immunotherapy.[ ] As nanovaccines (NVs), they have demonstrated effective cargo delivery to lymph nodes, eliciting broad-spectrum T- and B-cell responses while maintaining biosafety and biocompatibility. EV-based vaccines have the capacity to modify immunological microenvironments and systemic responses, which may lead to the conversion of “cold” tumors into “hot” ones.

## ACT
Traditional cancer vaccines activate and expand neoantigen-specific T cells by delivering neoantigens to APCs. ACTs circumvent processes such as neoantigen delivery and presentation by using the patient’s TILs, or T cells with genetically engineered TCRs or chimeric antigen receptors (CARs), to directly obtain large numbers of neoantigen-targeting immune cells[ ] [Figure ]. Recent advancements in neoantigen-based ACTs have demonstrated significant efficacy in treating various cancer types. These innovative strategies underscore the potential of ACTs to augment and sustain effective antitumor immunity by directly leveraging the body’s cellular defenses.
 

## TILs
ACTs that employ neoantigen-specific TILs hold significant promise as a treatment modality, particularly for epithelial malignancies with a modest mutational burden. For instance, patients with metastatic cholangiocarcinoma, even those with low tumor mutational burden (TMB), have exhibited substantial tumor regression lasting up to 35 months following treatment with neoantigen-reactive T cells.[ ] Beyond their direct cytotoxic effects, neoantigen-specific TILs can significantly influence tumor progression. For example, CD4+ T-helper 1 cells specifically reactive to neoantigens derived from an ERBB2 interacting protein (ERBB2IP) mutation have been implicated in controlling the spread of epithelial carcinoma.[ ] Notably, TILs that target the prevalent driver mutation KRASG12D effectively stimulate antitumor immunity, preventing metastasis in colorectal and gastrointestinal cancers[ ]. Furthermore, TILs that recognize both tumor neoantigens and viral antigens have demonstrated complete immune responses against human papillomavirus (HPV)16+ metastatic cervical squamous cell carcinoma.[ ]
TILs have shown potential in treating metastatic malignancies resistant to conventional therapies, including radiotherapy, chemotherapy, and anti-PD-1 therapy. In one case report, a woman with chemotherapy-resistant and hormone receptor-positive metastatic breast cancer showed a full and persistent response (lasting over 5.5 years) attained by combining IL-2, pembrolizumab, and TILs that specifically targeted the mutant genes CADPS2, SLC3A2, KIAA0368, and CTSB. Autologous TIL transfer combined with IL-2 resulted in a 50–70% ORR in patients with metastatic melanoma unresponsive to other therapies. A combination of TILs, IL-2, and anti-PD-1 therapy led to encouraging outcomes in patients with metastatic non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) resistant to anti-PD-1 monotherapy. Of 13 evaluable patients, 11 exhibited tumor regressions and two achieved complete remission lasting over 1.5 years[ ] (NCT03215810; NCT04032847).
While these studies present compelling evidence for the clinical benefits of neoantigen-reactive T cells, several challenges remain in optimizing the efficacy and consistency of TIL therapy. The abundance and effectiveness of tumor-reactive TILs vary significantly across different cancer types, highlighting the complex relationship between TILs and antitumor immune responses. For instance, CD8+ T lymphocytes, a key component of tumor-reactive TILs, represent only about 10% of cells in ovarian and colorectal cancers.[ ] Furthermore, in certain instances, TCRs with reactivity against tumor antigens have not been identified. In addition to their specificity for tumor antigens, human CD8+ TILs recognize epitopes derived from viral antigens, thereby eliciting bystander T-cell responses. These bystander T cells, acting as effector cells, infiltrate the tumor tissue.[ , ] However, research in metastatic NSCLC, gastrointestinal cancer, and breast cancer has revealed that infusion TILs from nonresponsive patients contain neoantigen-reactive TILs. This observation underscores the importance of considering the frequency and breadth of TIL repertoires within the tumor and their capacity to recognize both tumor-specific and bystander antigens. Such factors are critical for accurately predicting the effectiveness of immunotherapy in treating human cancer.
Strategies to enhance the antitumor efficacy of TILs focus on enriching and expanding populations of neoantigen-specific T cells, separated from bystander and regulatory TILs. For example, the selection of tumor-reactive T cells across different cancer types is made possible by the biomarker CD39, which is linked to both T-cell antitumor activity and T-cell exhaustion. Although bystander TILs resemble tumor-specific CD8+ T cells, they do not express CD39 or show any indication of sustained antigen activation at the tumor site.[ ] Advances in single-cell technologies, including cellular indexing of transcriptomes and epitopes by sequencing (CITE-seq) and TCR sequencing, enable the detection of neoantigen-reactive TCRs within TILs that express CD39 and chemokine (C-X-C motif) ligand 13 (CXCL13). Neoantigen-specific TCR identification in NSCLC is successful in 45% of CD8+ T cells and up to 66% of CD4+ T cells.[ ] In addition, immunomagnetic cell sorting has been employed to isolate CD39-expressing tumor-specific TILs, which exhibit stemness and self-renewal capabilities, resulting in a 60% increase in median survival rates in mouse models.[ ] These strategies overcome the limitations of conventional TIL-enrichment methods by employing biomarker-targeted enrichment of specific T-cell populations with enhanced antitumor activity.
Intrinsic characteristics of TILs, such as their phenotype, avidity, and persistence, significantly impact the efficacy of neoantigen-directed ACT. Patients who respond to ACT possess a reservoir of neoantigen-specific TILs with stemness properties. This TIL reservoir allows for rapid proliferation and differentiation into diverse subpopulations, contributing to the sustained presence of neoantigen-specific T cells and long-term tumor control. Conversely, patients unresponsive to ACT lack this stem-like TIL reservoir.[ , ] Continuous exposure to antigens in the TME causes TILs to phenotypically change into an exhausted state (PD-1+CD39+) typified by increased expression of inhibitory receptors, such as PD-1, and a progressive reduction in CD8+ T-cell function.[ ] Recent evidence indicates the presence of a less differentiated, stem-like population of PD-1+CD8+ T cells within the TME. These memory-like or progenitor-fatigued cells have self-renewal capabilities and enhanced antitumor efficacy in vivo, potentially refilling the terminally tired T cells that mediate tumor cell destruction. Despite their exhausted state, primary exhausted cells exhibit weaker central memory characteristics than genuine central memory T cells.[ ]
Quality enhancement of the neoantigen-specific T-cell repertoire within tumors is essential for optimizing the efficacy of ACT. Further investigation into the properties of TILs, such as phenotype, activation, and persistence, is essential to mitigate T-cell depletion, enhance stem-like TILs, and refine neoantigen targeting, thereby advancing more effective and tailored TIL-based therapies.

## TCR-engineered T cells
The TCR-T strategy entails the direct genetic modification and production of large numbers of neoantigen-specific T cells that express specific TCRs in vitro, in contrast to the traditional method of selecting neoantigen-specific T cells from TILs. Briefly, neoantigen-specific TCRs are inserted into T cells via CRISPR/Cas9 or transposon-based gene editing techniques. The modified cells expressing neoantigen-specific TCRs are subjected to extensive testing for tumor reactivity before being infused into patients.[ ]
The expression of highly active engineered TCRs endows CD8+ T cells with potent cytotoxic activity against tumors that specifically express the neoantigens. For instance, TCRs targeting the recurrent CBFB-MYH11 fusion gene have shown anti-leukemic activity in vitro and in patient-derived xenograft models.[ ] Similarly, peripheral blood lymphocytes expressing TCRs with high reactivity against KRASG12V and KRASG12D mutations effectively recognize various HLA-A*11:01+ pancreatic cell lines harboring the corresponding mutations in xenograft models.[ , ] Currently, several clinical trials (NCT04146298, NCT05438667, NCT03745326, and NCT03190941) are investigating the safety and efficacy of autologous TCR-T cells specifically targeting KRASG12V or KRASG12D/HLA-A*11:01 complexes.[ , ] Furthermore, clinical trials of autologous T cells modified with customized neoantigen-specific TCRs for the therapy of solid tumors, including lung cancer, gynecological malignancies, cholangiocarcinoma, colorectal cancer, and ovarian cancer, are underway (NCT05292859, NCT05194735, and NCT04520711). In the phase 1 clinical trial (NCT05194735), TCR-T therapy targeting driver gene mutations (KRAS, TP53, and EGFR) in solid tumors exhibited a manageable safety profile. Among the three patients treated, varying degrees of clinical benefit was observed, including one case of partial response and another of stable disease. In addition, persistent TCR-T cell engraftment was documented, providing supportive evidence for further dose escalation and expansion studies.[ ]
Replacement of endogenous TCRs with neoantigen-specific TCRs (neoTCRs) allows T cells to precisely redirect their targeting towards tumor cells presenting specific neoantigens in the context of HLA. Recent advancements in non-viral technologies for precise genome editing enable the simultaneous knockout of endogenous TCR or chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) genes and the introduction of neoTCR or specific CARs, significantly accelerating the production of clinical-grade T cells.[ ] In a pilot study, 16 patients with refractory solid cancers each received three TCR-T cell products, each with a distinct individualized neoTCR. Although the best overall response in 11 of the patients was disease progression, the other five showed stable disease.[ ] The reconstitution of neoTCRs in donor T cells via non-viral CRISPR-Cas9 gene editing led to specific recognition and cytotoxicity towards patient-matched melanoma cell lines in individuals unresponsive to anti-PD-1 therapy.[ ] Despite being preliminary, these findings suggest that the development of widely applicable T-cell therapies using non-viral precise TCR replacement approaches is feasible and safe.
TCR-T therapy is rapidly evolving, presenting significant clinical potential in treating various cancers. Continued research to optimize TCR engineering technologies, develop personalized neoantigen-specific TCRs, and explore combination approaches with other immunotherapies holds enormous potential for enhancing the efficacy and safety of TCR-T therapy, ultimately leading to more durable and effective cancer treatments.

## CAR-T cells
Compared with TCR-T, CAR-T therapy is less reliant on tumor HLA expression and its mediated neoantigen presentation, offering a significant advantage in overcoming immune evasion by cancer cells. CAR molecules allow CAR-T cells to recognize any cell surface protein for which antibodies exist, triggering T-cell activation independent of HLA expression.[ ] The promising outcomes of early clinical trials utilizing CAR-T cells that target CD19 for B-cell malignancies underscore the therapeutic promise of this strategy.[ ] The emergence of tumor neoantigens and the corresponding TCRs has breathed new life into CAR-T therapy, particularly driving its translation toward solid tumors.
By incorporating single-chain variable fragments (scFvs) that recognize tumor surface pMHC complexes, CAR-T therapy can target cells presenting intracellular neoantigens. With little on-target or off-target toxicity, the integration of scFvs specific for the nucleophosmin (NPM1c) neoepitope/HLA-A2 complex into CAR-T cells led to potent cytotoxicity towards NPM1c/HLA-A2+ leukemia cells and primary AML blasts.[ ] This CAR-T therapy approach, targeting pMHC complexes, also shows promise in treating solid tumors. A notable example is the targeting of the EGFR deletion variant III (EGFRvIII), present in approximately 30% of glioblastoma patients. EGFRvIII-targeted CAR-T cells effectively suppressed tumor growth in preclinical models of glioblastoma. A clinical trial (NCT02844062) is currently evaluating the safety and efficacy of autologous anti-EGFRvIII CAR-T therapy in patients with recurrent glioblastoma.[ ] However, the high heterogeneity of glioblastoma limits the efficacy of solely targeting EGFRvIII, with the elimination of this small subset of neoantigen-expressing tumor cells leading to the outgrowth of EGFRvIII-negative, EGFR-positive glioblastoma cells.[ ] A bicistronic design that combines an EGFR-targeting bispecific T-cell engager (BiTE) with a specific CAR against EGFRvIII has been developed to overcome this challenge. Cells transfected with the bicistronic construct secrete EGFR-specific BiTE, which can reroute CAR-T cells and enlist unmodified bystander T cells to target wild-type EGFR tumor cells.[ ]
Another approach to circumvent the limitations of targeting a single antigen involves employing Boolean logic gates within CAR-T cells to refine the recognition of highly heterogeneous tumor cells.[ ] This strategy entails activating CAR-T cells with tumor-specific neoantigens and then targeting universally expressed tumor antigens, such as ephrin type-A receptor 2 (EphA2), interleukin-13 receptor subunit alpha 2 (IL13R2), and myelin oligodendrocyte glycoprotein (MOG).[ ] This “prime-and-kill” method enhances tumor killing by targeting universally expressed antigens while retaining specificity for the initial neoantigen, confining CAR-T cell cytotoxicity to the proximal area around activated immune cells. This mitigates off-target effects by preserving cells that express the target antigen while excluding those that express the activation antigen in healthy tissues. The distinct glioblastoma-specific neoantigen EGFRvIII was employed to locally stimulate the production of a tandem CAR (TanCAR) directed toward the more uniform glioblastoma multiforme cell antigens EphA2 and IL13Rα2. Furthermore, a considerable percentage of T cells may be kept in a naive/stem cell memory state through CAR activation driven by synthetic Notch (synNotch), which strengthens antitumor immunity. SynNotch-CAR cells demonstrated superior antitumor efficacy and T-cell longevity compared with traditional constitutively expressed CAR-T cells while avoiding off-tumor toxicity.[ – ]
Neoantigen-based CAR-T therapy is rapidly evolving, driven by the desire to address the limitations of traditional CAR-T approaches and leverage the unique tumor specificity of neoantigens. Continued research on innovative CAR designs, novel targeting strategies, and advanced CAR activation mechanisms offers immense hope for improving the efficacy and safety of CAR-T therapies, particularly for treating solid tumors.

## CAR-NK and CAR-M
Based on the CAR strategy, alternative cell sources, such as natural killer (NK) cells and macrophages, are utilized in immunotherapy for their considerable therapeutic efficacy against solid tumors and because they meet the aim of cultivating universally allogeneic cells.[ , ]
NK cells are regarded as a potentially superior therapeutic platform because of their cytotoxic capabilities, which parallel those of CD8+ CTLs without the reliance on MHC-I-mediated antigen presentation. The higher cell lysis activity of NK cells enables them to effectively target and eliminate tumor cells that evade T-cell recognition. This unique characteristic makes CAR-NK cells a particularly viable therapy option for cancers with low mutational burden and insufficient neoantigen presentation. For example, cytokine-induced memory-like NK cells, genetically engineered using unique CARs targeting neoantigens derived from NPM1 mutations, have demonstrated enhanced immune responses against NPM1-mutated AML without eliciting off-target effects.[ ] CAR-NK strategy potentially addresses challenges associated with patient-specific T-cell variations attributable to distinct HLA types. The NK-92 cell line, recognized for its effective in vitro proliferation and genetic modification potential, presents a promising standardized platform for “off-the-shelf” CAR treatments.[ ] Furthermore, in addition to their direct lethal effects, NK cells enhance overall antitumor immune responses by regulating the activity of other immune cells. They enhance DC maturation and neoantigen presentation by secreting GM-CSF. Moreover, C-C motif chemokine ligand 5 (CCL5) released by NK cells recruits neoantigen-specific C-C motif chemokine receptor 5 (CCR5)+CD8+ T cells, augmenting the overall immune response and potentially broadening the spectrum of malignancies amenable to immunotherapy.
Macrophages, which are a fundamental component of the innate immune system, represent the predominant immune-infiltrating cell type within the TME, comprising up to 50% of the total immune cells in certain solid tumors.[ ] Macrophages exhibit significant phagocytic ability, antigen-presenting function, cytokine and chemokine release, and the capacity to infiltrate dense tissues and localize within malignancies.[ ] Human macrophages engineered with CARs can augment tumor phagocytic activity and antigen presentation, creating new opportunities for solid tumor therapy.[ ] As early as 2006, research demonstrated the feasibility and safety of carcinoembryonic antigen-targeted and CAR-modified human monocytes for therapeutic applications.[ ] Since then, there has been a coordinated initiative to develop and enhance CAR-engineered macrophages, propelling clinical trials for CAR-M therapy. Autologous anti-HER2 CAR macrophages, designated CT-0508, specifically identify and adhere to HER2-expressing tumor cells, resulting in their phagocytosis.[ ] In preclinical models, a single administration of human CAR-macrophages (CAR-Ms) reduced tumor burden and extended survival. CAR-Ms promote a pro-inflammatory TME by releasing pro-inflammatory chemokines and cytokines, converting M2 macrophages to M1, enhancing antigen presentation, and recruiting T cells. Compared with primary macrophages, human pluripotent stem cells offer advantages for CAR-M generation, including self-renewal capacity, differentiation potential, and ease of genetic modification.[ ]
The integration of neoantigen recognition, advanced genetic engineering technologies, and the unique properties of NK cells and macrophages offers exciting opportunities for the development of more effective and broadly applicable cancer immunotherapies.[ ] CAR-NKs and CAR-Ms demonstrate synergistic cytotoxicity with CAR-T cells against tumor cells. Inflammatory substances released by CAR-T cells increase the expression of costimulatory ligands (CD86 and CD80) on CAR-Ms, augmenting the cytotoxicity of CAR-Ms through the induction of macrophage M1 polarization. The elevated CAR-M costimulatory ligands may subsequently facilitate CAR-T cell adaption and activation, resulting in markedly increased cytotoxicity.[ ] Further research to optimize the development of CAR-NK and CAR-M cell therapies and explore their combination with other immunotherapies holds significant promise for achieving durable responses and long-term cancer control.

## Antibody-Based Therapies
Antibody therapies have proven highly effective in treating various cancers, particularly in the context of ICB using anti-PD1/programmed cell death ligand 1 (PD-L1)/cytotoxic T-lymphocyte associated protein 4 (CTLA4) antibodies. However, traditional antibodies are limited in their ability to target intracellular proteins, hindering their potential for targeting neoantigens arising from mutated proteins. To address this limitation, TCR-like antibodies (TCR-mimic [TCRm] antibodies) and neoantigen-specific antibodies have emerged as powerful tools for recognizing intracellular neoantigens presented on pMHC complexes.[ ] The higher affinity of TCRm antibodies for target pMHC complexes relative to that of natural TCRs results in reduced off-target effects and enhanced therapeutic efficacy.

## Identification and formulations of TCRm antibodies
Considering the markedly greater affinity of TCRm antibodies for peptide-HLA complexes relative to that of natural TCRs, thorough screening is important to avoid cross-reactivity or binding to HLA components not associated with the target peptide. A range of technologies, including genetic platforms and yeast and phage displays, have been employed to identify human TCRm antibodies with remarkable specificity for HLA-presented neoantigens. Phage or yeast display libraries containing a diverse range of scFv sequences are used to screen for clones with specific binding to mutant peptides presented by predetermined HLA types.[ ] High-throughput genetic platforms, such as PresentER, which utilize minigenes encoding MHC-I peptide libraries, enable the identification of on-target and off-target interactions between TCR-like therapeutic agents and MHC-I ligands, aiding in the evaluation of TCRm antibody specificity.[ ] Furthermore, structural analysis of TCRm antibodies in complex with their corresponding pMHC further enhances the evaluation of TCRm antibody specificity.[ ] For instance, certain human TCRm antibodies that bind to the Wilms tumor-derived peptide/HLA-A02:01, such as ESK1 and WT1-28z, exhibit a binding mode distinct from that of natural TCRs, as revealed by the crystal structure.[ , ] This structural information facilitates the prediction of high-affinity contacts between TCRm antibodies and diverse HLA-A02 subtypes, therefore broadening the prospective patient population suitable for ESK1 therapy. In addition, it provides insights into possible off-target binding interactions, further optimizing the immunotherapeutic specificity of TCRm antibodies.[ , , ]
TCRm antibodies exhibit a significantly higher affinity for their target pMHC complex compared with native TCRs—a key characteristic that minimizes off-target effects and maximizes therapeutic efficacy. Therapeutic TCRm antibodies can be engineered in various formats, including full-length antibodies, antibody-drug conjugates, bispecific antibodies (BsAbs), and CAR-T cells[ , ] [Figure ]. Two TCRm-based antibody-drug conjugates targeting the KRASG12V/HLA-A*0201 complex exhibited specific antitumor efficacy in preclinical in vitro and in vivo models, with minimal toxicity observed.[ ] Soluble TCRm constructs can be coupled with anti-CD3 antibody components, generating a class of bispecific molecules known as BiTEs. These anticancer immune-mobilizing monoclonal TCRs can target any cell based on its proteomic profile, enabling T-cell-mediated destruction of cancer cells with minimal surface epitope concentrations.[ ] Furthermore, TCRm antibodies can be incorporated into CAR-T cells to enhance their specificity towards neoantigens, significantly improving the antitumor therapeutic efficacy of CAR-T therapy.[ ]
 

## TCRm antibody-based off-the-shelf antitumor products
Based on TCRm antibodies, off-the-shelf antitumor products expressing public neoantigens can be developed for administration to tumor patients. Public neoantigens, resulting from recurring mutations in driver genes (e.g., oncogenes and tumor suppressor genes [TSGs]), represent common targets with the potential to assist significant numbers of patients.[ ] Even when the mutated peptide of a neoantigen varies from the wild-type sequence by a single amino acid, it is feasible to generate a specific scFv that targets the neoantigen–peptide-bound (p)HLA complex. For example, phage display strategies have identified specific scFvs for mutant peptides derived from KRAS and EGFR. These scFvs can recognize neoantigen–pHLA complexes, such as EGFR/HLA-A3 and KRAS peptide/HLA-A2.[ ] TCRm antibodies with remarkable specificity for mutant latent membrane protein 2A (LMP2A)- or mutant RAS–HLA complexes were formulated into dimeric T-cell-engaging bispecific antibodies (BsAbs), facilitating T-cell-mediated cytotoxicity against cancer cells expressing minimal levels of the mutant neoantigens and corresponding HLA alleles.[ ] In addition, specific scFvs targeting public neoantigens produced by mutations in oncogenes (e.g., PIK3CA, KRAS, EGFR, and CTNNB1) hold potential for the production of off-the-shelf antitumor therapeutic products.
TCRm antibodies can target neoantigens arising from recurrent mutations in TSGs. TSGs often exhibit low expression levels due to non-recurrent mutations that cause inactivation or nonsense-mediated RNA decay and typically do not elicit an immune response. One prominent example of an “undruggable” TSG is TP53, which often exhibits reduced expression of its protein p53 or the generation of inactive variants within neoplastic cells. Peptides derived from TP53 missense mutations, such as R175H, can be presented by HLA-A02:01, resulting in the formation of mutant p53/MHC complexes on the cell surface. Phage display identified a high-affinity antibody fragment (H2) targeting the p53R175H/HLA-A02:01 complex.[ ] This fragment was combined with a CD3-binding fragment to create a BsAb that simultaneously binds tumor cells via p53R175H/MHC and T cells via CD3, strongly activating the T cells. This approach improves T-cell recognition and killing of cancer cells by overcoming the low density of mutant p53/HLA complexes on the tumor cell surface.[ ] Notably, within tumors expressing mutant p53, overexpressed wild-type p53 peptides can also be presented by MHC molecules. TCRm antibodies targeting these wild-type p53/MHC complexes have been characterized, enabling the distinction between tumors harboring mutant p53 and healthy cells expressing wild-type p53.[ ] T1-116C operates as a TCRm that targets wild-type p53/HLA-A*0201, stimulating immune effector mechanisms to eliminate cancer cells that express the p53,[ – ] peptide both in vitro and in vivo.[ ] The TCRm P1C1TM specifically targets the wild-type p53[ – ] peptide bound to HLA-A24:02, allowing its use as an antibody-drug conjugate that delivers a cytotoxic payload only to tumors expressing both mutant p53 and HLA-A24.[ ] For instance, the DNA alkylating agent PNU-159682, directly conjugated to P1C1TM, has a lethal effect specifically on colorectal cancer cells expressing mutant p53.[ ] Such immune-engaging strategies can be employed to alter the undruggable reputation of p53.[ ]
TCRm-based strategies offer a powerful approach for targeting endogenous neoantigen proteins, overcoming the limitations of conventional therapeutic modalities, and facilitating the creation of more precise anticancer treatments. The continuous advancement in our understanding of neoantigens, coupled with the development of innovative TCRm antibodies and related technologies, promises to reshape the landscape of cancer treatment by enabling more precise, effective, and personalized therapies.

## Overcoming Challenges in Neoantigen-Directed Cancer Immunotherapy: A Path Towards Clinical Success
Although initial achievements in the treatment of hematologic and solid malignancies with neoantigen-based immunotherapies have showcased the promise of this strategy, definitive efficacy has only been noted in a limited number of well-documented patient cases. To realize the potential of neoantigen-based immunotherapy as a broadly applicable and successful treatment, substantial progress is required to tackle critical obstacles, including neoantigen prediction, immune evasion, and production efficiency.

## Navigating tumor heterogeneity and evolution: challenges to neoantigen targeting
Neoantigen-targeted therapies currently undergoing clinical trials face significant limitations imposed by the inherent genetic heterogeneity of cancers. This heterogeneity encompasses the wide range of somatic mutations observed across various cancer types, individuals, and even within subclones of the same tumor.[ ] Genomic instability, a hallmark of many cancers, fuels this diversity, leading to substantial variations in mutational burden and neoantigen profiles across different tumor subpopulations.[ , ] For example, substantial genetic heterogeneity between primary and metastatic HCC is associated with considerable intratumoral variability of neoantigens in metastases, which correlates with diminished T-cell reactivity.[ ] Patients with TNBC often exhibit a higher abundance of neoantigens derived from frameshifts and mutations, correlating with a higher rate of positive response to immunotherapy compared with other types of invasive breast cancer. BRCA1-mutated TNBC has a significantly elevated mutational burden, further emphasizing the importance of individualized approaches for neoantigen identification and prediction tailored to each patient and cancer type.[ , ]
The presence of diverse mutations within tumors adds a layer of complexity to neoantigen prediction. Although contemporary technologies facilitate comprehensive genomic and transcriptomic profiling of individual tumor samples at specific time points, this approach remains limited in its ability to encompass the complete spectrum of mutations present across diverse tumor sites within a single patient.[ ] This limitation can lead to the existence of tumor cells that escape recognition by neoantigen-specific T cells. If these cells gain a competitive advantage and outgrow other clones, the clinical benefits of neoantigen-targeted therapy may be limited [Figure A]. Furthermore, due to limited T-cell infiltration into the sampled area, an individually excised tumor lesion may not represent the full heterogeneity of neoantigen-specific T cells within a patient.[ , ] This constraint can impede the identification of a comprehensive TCR repertoire suitable for therapeutic intervention.[ ] Optimization of a collection of multiple tumor samples for neoantigen identification is paramount for achieving a complete and representative understanding of each patient’s tumor mutational landscape.
 As cancers advance, their genomes undergo substantial modifications, encompassing the creation, amplification, and deletion of mutations.[ ] Loss of neoantigens to genomic alterations, such as copy number loss, epigenetic silencing, or PTMs, can cause tumor cells to resist immunotherapy. This neoantigen depletion, which is observed in a significant portion of early-stage NSCLC cases, reduces the effectiveness of personalized neoantigen-targeted therapies.[ ] Furthermore, altered genes that encode unexpressed neoantigens typically display elevated methylation levels in their promoter regions relative to wild-type parental genes. Tumor cells can also exploit chaperone proteins, such as HSP90, to sequester unfolded mutant proteins, preventing their entry into the antigen presentation pathway and degradation by proteasomes.[ , ] In addition, tumor subclones that express neoantigens may be preferentially targeted and eliminated by the immune system (e.g., CD8+ T cells), leading to the outgrowth of subclones that do not express those particular neoantigens and ultimately contributing to tumor recurrence.[ , ] To address the challenge of neoantigen depletion and enhance the durability and efficacy of neoantigen-specific immunotherapy, it is necessary to adopt a multi-neoantigen targeting approach tailored to individual patients [Figure A].
Tumor heterogeneity extends beyond the mutational landscape, encompassing genetic variations as well as differences in HLA heterozygosity and stability, which can arise in response to immune pressure.[ – ] Mutations in genes that are crucial for antigen presentation can hinder the processing and presentation of neoantigens, preventing T cells from recognizing and eliminating tumors. For instance, a specific excised lesion of lung metastasis in a patient with colorectal cancer exhibited a deletion in the chromosome 6 haplotype, altering the HLA-C*08:02 molecule. Tran et al[ ] reported that this alteration enabled tumor subclones to evade TILs targeting KRASG12D. Tumors can also downregulate HLA molecules as a result of aberrant transcription, translation, or protein stability processes[ ] [Figure B]. Changes in gene expression and splicing patterns contribute to the loss of MHC class I molecules in lung adenocarcinoma, squamous cell carcinoma, and estrogen receptor-positive cancers.[ ] Conversely, aberrant splicing can also lead to MHC class II loss, while inhibition of splicing can restore MHC class II function in some cancers.[ ] Pancreatic ductal adenocarcinomas resistant to ICB therapy have been shown to degrade MHC-I molecules through the process of autophagy.[ ] Therefore, a comprehensive evaluation of neoantigen processing and presentation is crucial for achieving accurate predictive outcomes and developing effective therapeutic strategies. Furthermore, the heterogeneity of the TME is mostly due to the variety of non-malignant cells, particularly immune cells. Single-cell transcriptomic studies have uncovered variations in myeloid and lymphoid cell populations, their behaviors, and the clonal diversity of T lymphocytes inside the TME.[ , ] For example, four distinct subtypes of macrophages were identified within the TME. Similarly, analyses of TCR repertoire heterogeneity using bulk or single-cell bioinformatic approaches have demonstrated spatial variability in T-cell density and clonality, often correlating with both ubiquitous and regional neoantigen loading. The examination of ubiquitous TCRs using blood samples can improve tailored immunotherapies.[ , ]

## Overcoming immune escape: strategies for enhanced antitumor immunity
Immune evasion constitutes a significant barrier to effective cancer eradication, especially for anticancer immunotherapies. Tumors circumvent neoantigen-targeted immunotherapy in many ways, including deletion of neoantigens, suppression of immune checkpoints, establishment of an immunosuppressive TME, and alterations in antigen peptide presentation. Neoantigen expression levels significantly influence the ability of the immune system to target tumors and the efficacy of cancer treatments. Tumors with low neoantigen expression induce tolerance within the immune system, facilitating their evasion of destruction. Colorectal cancers characterized by low mutation rates (microsatellite stability) exhibit reduced expression of neoantigens compared with those with high mutation rates (microsatellite instability). This reduced neoantigen expression results in diminished, less diverse, and less effective T-cell responses, which can be reversed by enhancing neoantigen expression.[ ] Tumor cells frequently show enhanced expression of immunosuppressive checkpoint ligands (e.g., PD-L1 and CTLA4) in response to immunotherapy, thus biologically constraining the proliferation and functionality of T lymphocytes[ ] [Figure C]. Furthermore, extrinsic factors, such as immunosuppressive cells, soluble inhibitory molecules, and inhibitory receptors on immune cells, adversely affect TILs. The observed effects, characterized by a heightened ratio of regulatory to effector T cells alongside diminished effector T-cell functionality, facilitate tumor proliferation and dissemination[ ] [Figure D]. In supportive TMEs, such as those observed in melanomas and lung cancers, CD8+ T cells exhibit signs of exhaustion from their interactions with the tumor. By contrast, dysfunctional interactions between CD4+ and CD8+ T cells are more common in less supportive environments, such as glioblastomas.[ ]
Various approaches to mitigate these evasion methods are under investigation, including neoantigen vaccination, inducible neoantigen production, combinatorial CARs, and epitope spreading. Neoantigen vaccination may allow modulation of the immune environment of immunologically cold tumors characterized by a limited number of mutations.[ , ] For example, despite the low mutation rate and immunosuppressive microenvironment, neoantigen vaccines have effectively stimulated T-cell responses in glioblastomas. Research indicates that mRNA vaccines enhance the presence of TILs with improved effector function, leading to a transition from an immunosuppressive to an immunostimulatory TME.[ – ] A spleen-targeted neoantigen DNA vaccine has been shown to improve immunotherapy outcomes in cold HCCs. This was accomplished by utilizing red blood cells, which naturally accumulate in the spleen, to deliver the vaccine and enhance neoantigen presentation by APCs.[ ]
Combinatorial CARs that target multiple antigens, including both neoantigens and TAAs, can enhance the specificity and efficacy of CAR-T cell therapy. This bivalent TanCAR approach can improve the recognition of heterogeneous tumor populations and minimize the risk of escape mutations.[ ] The use of two receptors enhances tumor selectivity by delivering complementary signals: a CAR that induces minimal T-cell activation and a chimeric costimulatory receptor (CCR) that provides a co-stimulatory signal. The “IF-BETTER” combination therapy employs an adhesion G protein-coupled receptor E2 (ADGRE2)-targeted CAR-T cell in conjunction with a CCR aimed at C-type lectin domain family 12 member A (CLEC12A). This receptor is present on AML stem cells and blasts, but is not found on healthy hematopoietic stem/progenitor cells.[ , ] Similarly, in the synNotch/CAR approach, T-cell activation necessitates the binding of the synNotch receptor to neoantigen B, which triggers proteolysis of the intracellular transcriptional effector and induces transcriptional expression of the neoantigen A-specific CAR.[ ] These methods demonstrate synergistic cytotoxicity by specifically targeting a pair of neoantigens that are co-expressed on tumors but not on normal cells.[ ]
Inducible neoantigen expression technique allows tumors to temporarily express neoantigens in response to specific stimuli, reflecting the natural progression and immune response of tumors. The use of inducible neoantigen expression via inversion-induced joining mitigates neoantigen loss and minimizes off-target effects by ensuring that antigen expression is activated only when necessary.[ ] Epitope spreading, characterized by the expansion of the immune response to include additional TAAs, serves as a mechanism to counteract immune evasion. A long-peptide vaccine (NeoVax) that targets up to 20 personalized neoantigens for each patient has shown epitope spreading, enhancing the breadth of tumor-specific killing in melanoma patients[ ] [NCT01970358].

## Production and optimization of neoantigen-reactive T cells: towards clinically relevant therapies
The successful implementation of neoantigen-based therapies hinges on the ability to efficiently generate and optimize neoantigen-reactive T cells. Optimization of the production process involves expanding the reservoir of neoantigen-reactive T cells, diversifying the TCR repertoire, enhancing neoantigen presentation, facilitating T-cell expansion, and preventing T-cell exhaustion.

## Expanding the reservoir of neoantigen-reactive T cells
TILs harbor a significant population of neoantigen-specific T cells, rendering them a promising resource for ACTs. However, TIL therapy faces limitations, including the scarcity of fresh tumor specimens, the limited presence of TILs in cold tumors, and the necessity for surgical removal of resectable lesions.[ ] Peripheral blood is an easily obtainable source for the production of substantial amounts of neoantigen-reactive T cells, offering a vital alternative to ACTs. Several clinical trials are underway to evaluate the effectiveness of circulating neoantigen-reactive T cells derived from peripheral blood in the treatment of patients with epithelial malignancies.[ – ] However, these T cells are relatively rare and require efficient isolation using identified neoantigen-specific TCRs. The presence of elevated neoTCR-expressing CD8+ T cells in the bloodstream following immunotherapy may enhance outcomes for individuals with NSCLC.[ ] The use of neoantigen peptides for in vitro stimulation of peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) generated by cancer patients can enhance the proportion of activated T lymphocytes.[ ] Peripheral blood lymphocytes can also be engineered to express neoTCRs for effective targeting and elimination of cancer cells.[ ] In addition, the range of tumor-reactive TCRs within PBMCs can be broadened by incorporating TCRs specific to the individual patient’s tumor neoantigens.[ ] The simultaneous introduction of multiple neoantigen-reactive TCRs into a patient’s PBMCs can further amplify the immune response. Notably, minimally cultured T cells expressing personalized TCRs demonstrate superior cytolytic capacity compared with exhausted and senescent TILs.
The efficacy of ACTs is constrained by the limited lifespan and terminal differentiation of numerous tumor-reactive T cells. Induced pluripotent stem cell (iPSC) technology provides a promising method for generating long-lived, naïve, and early memory T cells with stem cell-like characteristics.[ ] iPSC-derived CTLs specifically targeting the neoantigen generated from the EWS/FLI1 fusion gene effectively provoke an immune response against cancer cells expressing EWS/FLI1, demonstrating efficacy in both cultured cells and a mouse model of Ewing sarcoma xenograft.[ ] Furthermore, a three-dimensional thymic organ culture system accurately replicates thymic development in vitro, facilitating the differentiation of iPSC-derived immature T-cell lineages into neoantigen-specific T cells. This system emulates the thymic environment, supporting T-cell maturation and the expression of heterodimeric αβ co-receptors.[ ] Furthermore, iPSCs can be generated from a single neoantigen-specific CTL clone, allowing for the large-scale expansion of these CTLs.[ ]
Optimization of the in vitro neoantigen presentation pathway can bridge the gap between T-cell recognition of tumor cells in vitro and in vivo, enhancing the efficacy of antitumor immunotherapy. DCs pulsed with neoantigen peptides or transfected with tumor mRNA, as well as EBV-transformed B-cell lines, have been shown to effectively present neoantigens to T cells in vitro.[ ] However, this approach might not accurately reflect the endogenous presentation of neoantigens by tumor cells in vivo. Patient-derived xenograft models preserve the intrinsic expression, natural processing, and presentation of neoantigens, providing a more in vivo-like environment for investigating tumor growth and neoantigen expression.[ , ] Tumor organoid cultures derived from patient tumor cells better reflect the heterogeneity and microenvironment of the tumor, demonstrating immense potential in evaluating cell-based immunotherapy in vitro. Co-cultures of autologous PBMCs with tumor organoid tissues have been shown to generate personalized tumor-reactive CD8+ T lymphocytes, effectively reducing tumor growth.[ ] While further refinements are needed to fully recapitulate the tumor stroma, including bone marrow and stromal elements, these organoid models provide a promising in vitro platform for neoantigen presentation and T-cell recognition.

## Assessment and surveillance of neoantigen-specific immune responses
Dependable immune monitoring is crucial for evaluating the anticipated immunological effects of neoantigen-based immunotherapy and for identifying larger, more suitable patient groups who will benefit from these therapies. The T-cell responses that specifically target the tumor are paramount to the efficacy of various therapies. Assessing and monitoring the abundance and effectiveness of tumor-specific T cells can provide valuable insights into the efficacy of cancer immunotherapy.
A thorough assessment of T-cell reactivity typically requires a multiparametric approach that includes both phenotypic and functional attributes. Various potent indicators, such as IFN-γ, TNFα, PD-1, CD39, 4-1BB (CD137), OX40 (CD134), and T-cell immunoglobulin and mucin-domain containing-3 (TIM-3), have utility in quantifying neoantigen-specific T cells and their antitumor activity in ACT.[ , ] Chemokine CXCL13 and chemokine receptor CCR5 function as inherent markers of T-cell responsiveness to ICB.[ ] Single-cell analysis of blood neoepitope-specific T cells has been used to identify substantial clonal expansion of T cells exhibiting unique effector transcription profiles, which is commonly seen in related malignancies. Measurements of circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) levels reflect tumor burden and can indicate the presence of neoantigen-generating mutations, while sequencing of ctDNA can be used to monitor alterations in neoantigens over time during ICB treatment, offering direction for personalized immunotherapy.[ , ] A comprehensive examination of T-cell states offers a dependable assessment of the efficacy of neoantigen-directed ACT. A stem-like population of neoantigen-reactive TILs, particularly CD8+ cells exhibiting activation markers, swiftly proliferates and differentiates, thereby augmenting T-cell longevity, improving tumor management, and fortifying the antitumor response.[ ]
High-throughput transcriptome and TCR sequencing have enabled the identification of neoantigen-specific TCR clonotypes with dysfunctional properties, allowing for the detection of antitumor TCRs using limited TILs. Distinct TCR clonotypes that recognize neoantigens are a distinguishing feature of TILs in various types of tumors, facilitating the prediction of TCRs based solely on the transcriptome profiles of TILs.[ ] These signatures possess the capacity to forecast clinical responses to different immunotherapies by detecting cancer antigen-specific TCRs in both blood and tumor samples, eliminating the necessity for functional screening of putative neoantigens and thereby serving as an essential instrument for customized immunotherapy.

## Harnessing nanotechnology to improve neoantigen-based theragnostic approaches
Nanotechnology has emerged as a promising tool to enhance the effectiveness of neoantigen-based therapies via two principal mechanisms: direct enhancement of antitumor immunity, and indirect strengthening of the tumor immune defense capacity via mitigation of immunosuppressive processes within the TME. Nanomaterials can enhance the immunogenicity of neoantigens by facilitating the following: efficient co-delivery of neoantigens with adjuvants, release of neoantigens, enhanced capture of neoantigens, and prolongation of neoantigen retention time in the TME.[ ] Bi-adjuvant neoantigen NVs (banNVs) deliver a neoantigen peptide together with TLR7/8 and TLR9 agonists. These NVs enhance the anti-PD-1 therapy, resulting in the total regression of a substantial percentage of neoantigen-specific malignancies.[ ] Recent advancements in delivery systems have transcended traditional methods to include engineered microbial platforms. The engineered bacteria have the potential to deliver multiple tumor neoantigens simultaneously, thereby complicating the immune-evasion strategies of cancer cells. By genetically engineering probiotics, the production efficiency of neoantigen peptide arrays can be enhanced, improving the intracellular delivery of tumor antigens. Such strategies support the creation of targeted neoantigen delivery systems that elicit durable tumor-specific immune responses while demonstrating greater sensitivity to bloodstream clearance mechanisms and phagocytic cells, thus enhancing safety and immunogenicity. Once administered, the modified bacteria migrate to tumor tissues, persist within the TME, and continuously express the therapeutic payload, activating the immune system to eliminate metastatic cancer cells.[ ] This also represents a new approach for neoantigen-based cancer immunotherapy.
Nanomaterials utilized in photodynamic therapy, chemodynamic therapy, and sonodynamic therapy have markedly improved the immunogenic cell death of tumor cells, facilitating the release of endogenous neoantigens.[ ] Nanomaterials effectively trap tumor neoantigens through hydrophobic interactions or by engaging with diverse functional groups on the neoantigens, which include maleimide, amino, and catechol groups. Nanomaterials, owing to the enhanced permeability and retention effect, extend neoantigen residence time within the TME, thereby augmenting interactions with immune cells. This safeguards neoantigens from degradation, optimizes transport to APCs, augments neoantigen presentation, and fortifies antitumor immunity.[ ]

## Targeting public neoantigens for off-the-shelf therapies
Commercially available precision immunotherapies that target shared neoantigens present an attractive solution to the temporal and financial limitations of individualized treatments based on unique neoantigens. Shared neoantigens, which are frequently clonally preserved across metastases and consistently recur among patients, present considerable potential for widespread use. A library of TCRs targeting common neoantigens in an HLA-dependent manner has been created for patients with advanced malignancies. As additional public neoantigens and their associated TCRs are discovered, this library will benefit more patients with common cancer mutations. Widespread cancer genome sequencing and neoantigen prediction will match patients to therapies targeting public neoantigens. This approach, using readily available public neoantigens, is expected to accelerate neoantigen discovery and T-cell production, making neoantigen-based therapies more widely accessible.
Public neoantigens arise from various events, including gene fusion, abnormal splicing, and altered PTMs. Shared neoantigens are also generated via splicing, a phenomenon observed in up to 90% of melanoma cases.[ ] Carboxyethylation of cysteine 96 in integrin subunit alpha 2b (ITGA2B) results in the generation of neoantigens that efficiently stimulate CD4+ T-cell responses in the presence of HLA-DRB1*04.[ , ] In addition, certain hotspot mutation peptides in driver genes (e.g., KRAS) or TSGs (e.g., TP53) can also generate public neoantigens.[ , ] Although neoantigens often result from random mutations, certain substances can be utilized to generate public neoantigens via the alteration of commonly mutated residues. For example, ARS1620 alters the cysteine of the KRASG12C mutant, resulting in a haptenated peptide shown by MHC-I. This peptide functions as a tumor-specific neoantigen, provoking a CTL response. This method broadens the range of targetable neoantigens by employing haptens to alter mutant oncoproteins, instead of merely blocking them pharmacologically.[ , ]
Several therapeutic approaches can be employed to generate antitumor immune products targeting shared neoantigens, including tumor vaccines, BsAbs, and adoptive transfer of TCR-T/CAR-T cells.[ ] Neoantigen peptide vaccines elicit T-cell responses targeting EGFR mutations (such as L858R and T790M) in NSCLC patients resistant to conventional therapies. This finding indicates that neoantigen vaccines aimed at shared EGFR mutations may be advantageous for a specific group of NSCLC patients who are unresponsive to ICB.[ ] BsAbs employing TCRm have been used to target public neoantigens resulting from dysregulated PTMs. A BsAb targeting a phosphorylated insulin receptor substrate 2 (pIRS2) phosphopeptide/HLA-A02:01 combination exhibited excellent cytotoxicity against pIRS2-positive, HLA-A02:01-positive tumor cells.[ ] T cells that express TCRs specific to public neoantigens can be identified and grown ex vivo for subsequent adoptive transfer to patients. The adoptive transfer of TCR-T cells selectively aimed at neoantigens derived from mutant PIK3CA led to tumor regression in mice. The targeted therapeutic impact, noted exclusively in tumors carrying the PIK3CA mutation, underscores the immunogenicity and promise of this strategy for addressing malignancies with particular genetic modifications.[ ]
The development of neoantigen-based therapies is a rapidly evolving field, fueled by advancements in genetic engineering and our comprehension of tumor biology and immunology. Efforts to address the issues outlined in this section will be essential for unlocking the full potential of neoantigen-based therapeutics across various malignancies. Continued research and development in neoantigen prediction, immune evasion mechanisms, and production optimization, as well as the further exploration of public neoantigens, will pave the way for safer, more effective, and broadly applicable cancer immunotherapies.

## Current Status and Future Directions
As distinct TSAs resulting from somatic mutations, neoantigens have emerged as powerful targets for precision cancer immunotherapy, which aims to effectively and specifically eradicate tumors without harming normal tissues. This review article has provided a comprehensive overview of the current state of neoantigen-driven personalized tumor therapy, synthesizing compelling evidence that supports the role of neoantigens in various clinical cancer immunotherapies. Despite significant research advancements in neoantigen-based therapies, their translation into widely accessible clinical cancer treatments remains a challenge, particularly regarding feasibility and cost-effectiveness. Continued improvements in sequencing technologies and neoantigen prediction algorithms are crucial for improving the accuracy, speed, and cost-effectiveness of neoantigen identification. Off-the-shelf therapies that target shared public neoantigens across a broad patient population represent a promising trend for the development of readily available and cost-effective treatments. Furthermore, the existing body of research largely remains in the pre-clinical stages, and more data from robust clinical trials are needed to translate the considerable potential of this field into widespread clinical success.
Neoantigens are effective targets for cancer immune therapies, holding the potential to effectively eliminate residual tumor foci and pinpoint distant metastatic cells. The presence of neoantigens may also be a good predictor of a patient’s response to immunotherapy. Despite the hypothesized correlation between elevated TMB and the presence of neoantigens identifiable by TILs, only around 10% of tumor mutations yield altered peptides with MHC binding affinity; of these, only 1% are acknowledged by the patient’s T cells. In malignancies characterized by TMB, such as AML and pediatric brain tumors, the density of neoantigens is correspondingly diminished. Therefore, the use of the tumor neoantigen burden (TNB), measured by quantifying the number of immunogenic neoantigens, rather than the TMB of total mutations may lead to better prediction of patient response to immunotherapies such as ICB.[ ]
Tailored therapeutic approaches based on patient-specific neoantigens, including novel cancer vaccines, ACTs, and BsAbs, offer significant advantages, such as a high degree of personalization and the activation of the immune system. ACT modalities, including TILs, TCR-T, and CAR-T/M/NK, are known for their ability to elicit robust immune responses and effectively overcome immune suppression. However, these therapies are often characterized by lengthy production timelines, high costs, and limited feasibility for off-the-shelf applications. Conversely, therapeutic cancer vaccines, particularly mRNA vaccines, are relatively easy to manufacture and have the potential to induce broad immune responses, making them viable candidates for ready-made solutions with significant promise in early-stage tumor treatment.[ ] Similarly, neoantigen-based BsAb therapies also show potential for standardized application and possess a high capacity for counteracting immune suppression; however, their implementation in the early treatment of tumors remains limited, and the production costs are considerable.[ ] Despite these challenges, there is optimism that ongoing technological advancements—particularly in NGS, AI algorithms, and the rapidly evolving field of synthetic biology—will continually foster innovative therapeutic models that deepen our understanding of cancer, enhance the development of personalized neoantigen-based cancer treatment strategies, and achieve sustained breakthroughs. Notably, combination therapies based on neoantigens are beginning to reveal notable therapeutic advantages. For example, the integration of neoantigen vaccines with ICB may enhance immune responses against tumor cells. Furthermore, various strategies aimed at remodeling the TME, including immunotherapeutic interventions, should be synergistically integrated with personalized cancer vaccine treatments to effectively address immune tolerance.
The integration of neoantigen-based therapies with numerous therapeutic techniques that target various stages of the cancer immune cycle seeks to provoke strong, lasting, and tumor-specific immune responses in cancer patients. Currently, the key antitumor therapeutic strategies involve enhancing neoantigen release, increasing neoantigen presentation, boosting tumor-specific T cells, and promoting immune cell infiltration. Cell death induced by various treatments, including radiotherapy, chemotherapy, targeted therapies, photodynamic therapy, and oncolytic virotherapy can convert immunologically cold tumors into hot tumors, enhancing antitumor immune cycles.[ ] Administration of GM-CSF, IFN-α, TLR/STING agonists, and anti-CD40 can promote neoantigen presentation by APCs.[ , ] ACT and ICB enhance the number and activity of tumor-specific T cells. Improving blood vessel function in tumors through vascular normalization, using therapies such as anti-vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) drugs and VEGF receptor tyrosine kinase inhibitors, enhances the delivery of CD8+ T cells and boosts the effectiveness of immunotherapy[ ] [Figure E, F]. TME modification methods and intratumoral cytokines promote immune cell infiltration into tumors. Furthermore, delivery systems such as NPs and EVs provide a comprehensive platform for the simultaneous delivery of multiple drugs or therapeutics,[ ] effectively targeting different stages of the cancer immune cycle at the same time and enabling a reboot or expansion of the self-sustaining cycle of cancer immunity.
The field of neoantigen-based immunotherapy is rapidly evolving, offering promising strategies for treating a range of cancers. Continued research into neoantigen identification, characterization, and delivery, optimization of immune responses, and the development of effective combination therapies are all crucial if we are to fully realize the potential of this transformative approach.
