Developing from the mucosal epithelial cells of the oral cavity, pharynx, larynx and sinonasal tract, HNSCC is influenced by a wide array of risk factors that vary by anatomical site [ ]. High-risk factors include human papillomavirus (HPV) infection, as well as the consumption of alcohol and tobacco [ ]. Currently, no screening method has proven effective, and early detection primarily relies on meticulous physical examination. Although certain oral premalignant conditions, including leukoplakia (white patches) and erythroplakia (red patches), may advance to invasive carcinoma, the majority of individuals receive diagnosis at advanced stages without earlier premalignant indicators [ ]. Most HNSCC instances manifest as locally advanced conditions, necessitating combined therapeutic approaches. The therapeutic strategy generally encompasses surgical intervention followed by supplementary radiation or chemotherapy plus radiation (termed chemoradiation) based on disease progression [ ]. Despite this therapeutic aim towards a cure, a significant number of individuals will face locoregional reoccurrence and/or distant metastasis, leading to unfavourable outcomes [ ]. Given the limited therapeutic options, apart from palliative chemotherapy, the need for novel treatments is critical. The rapid advancement of immunotherapy offers promising new opportunities for HNSCC management.
With an increasingly comprehensive understanding of HNSCC and the rapid advancement of immunotherapy, HNSCC is now recognized as an immunosuppressive disease, and immunotherapy holds substantial promise in reshaping its treatment landscape [ ]. Currently, several immune checkpoint blockers that target the programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1)/programmed cell death 1 ligand 1 (PD-L1) pathway have gained regulatory approval across different disease conditions, while additional agents, used alone or in combined approaches, remain under clinical evaluation [ ]. In 2016, the PD-1 blocking agents nivolumab and pembrolizumab received authorization for treating recurrent or metastatic HNSCC (R/M HNSCC) in individuals who showed disease progression during or following platinum-based chemotherapy [ ]. Subsequently, in 2019, pembrolizumab obtained approval as initial therapy, administered either independently in PD-L1-positive malignancies or together with chemotherapy [ ]. Current clinical studies are examining the incorporation of immune checkpoint blockade (ICB) in (neo)adjuvant protocols and investigating innovative therapeutic combinations in the recurrent/metastatic context to boost treatment outcomes, extend patient survival and address resistance patterns [ ]. Immunotherapy offers new possibilities, surpassing traditional treatments by improving survival rates and reducing toxicity to non-cancerous tissues [ ]. However, challenges remain, including drug resistance and the need for more effective methods to assess patient responses. HNSCC tumours evade immune detection by establishing an immunosuppressive microenvironment, which involves the activation and/or proliferation of immune checkpoints like PD-1 and cytotoxic T lymphocyte antigen 4 (CTLA-4), the upregulation of regulatory T cells (Tregs) and alterations in human leukocyte antigen (HLA) class I presentation pathways, which impair tumour antigen processing machinery [ ]. These factors foster an environment conducive to tumour evasion and drug resistance. Additionally, high levels of Tregs, tumour-associated macrophages and myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) contribute to inhibitory immune responses [ ]. Developing reliable methods to predict patient response to immunotherapy is critical for effective patient selection and cost-efficiency analysis. Therefore, employing advanced technologies, such as artificial intelligence (AI), to predict patient responses, identify biomarkers and explore targets to overcome drug resistance and improve prognosis is essential for the successful implementation of immunotherapy in HNSCC.
The rapid advancement of statistical methodologies and big data science has markedly elevated AI’s relevance across various domains of human life [ ]. AI aims to simulate human intelligence, thought processes and reasoning models to address problems, facilitate decision-making and automate tasks [ ]. Machine learning (ML), a subset of AI, analyzes vast datasets for specific tasks, autonomously constructing predictive models and clusters, which are subsequently analyzed by computational systems [ ]. AI is centred on creating ‘thinking machines’ capable of independent decision-making, whereas ML focuses on ‘learning machines’ that execute tasks through data processing and algorithmic frameworks without explicit programming instructions [ ]. Deep learning (DL), a subset of ML, utilizes AI-powered algorithms that significantly influence biomedical investigations, individualized medicine and precision healthcare [ ]. By examining genomics, pathomics, imaging and additional biological information alongside mathematical models, ML offers new insights into patient profiles [ ]. The intersection of medicine and AI has become a rapidly developing field, driving innovation in healthcare applications ( ).
 While immunotherapy represents a significant breakthrough in cancer treatment, accurately predicting a patient’s response to therapy remains challenging [ ]. AI technology improves cancer immunotherapy success rates by generating predictive metrics, such as the immunoscore and immunophenoscore, which are designed to evaluate responses to ICB treatment [ ]. Despite their potential, challenges persist, encompassing uncertainty regarding individual biomarker predictive effectiveness, complexities in synthesizing various biomarkers into a cohesive framework and insufficient comprehensive models for ICB response prediction [ ]. A recent investigation demonstrated that combining AI-based diagnostic algorithms with clinical interpretations can markedly improve diagnostic accuracy, particularly for ambiguous cancer subtypes [ ]. AI technologies have reached about 91.66% precision in detecting major histocompatibility complex (MHC) signatures linked to immunotherapy response [ ]. Moreover, AI can standardize assessments across institutions, mitigating the subjective variability inherent in clinician interpretations [ ]. As such, AI’s integration into cancer immunotherapy holds substantial promise for improving patient outcomes.
Given the progress in immunotherapy for HNSCC and the growing significance of AI, this review examines the evolution of AI applications within HNSCC immunotherapy. Additionally, it considers the advantages, limitations and strategies for optimizing AI’s role in advancing cancer treatment.

## Immunotherapy for HNSCC
With the rapid advancements in cancer molecular biology and immunology, immunotherapy has surfaced as an encouraging therapeutic strategy for tumours. Scientists have uncovered various ‘immune checkpoints’ that influence the effectiveness of cancer immunotherapy, both positively and negatively [ ]. The detection of programmed cell death receptors and their ligands has revealed their pivotal roles in tumourigenesis, progression and immune escape [ ]. Therapies such as ICB, tumour vaccines, oncolytic viruses (OVs) and adoptive cell therapies (ACTs) are increasingly being used as adjuncts to surgery, radiotherapy and chemotherapy in treating HNSCC [ ] ( and ).
 

## ICB
ICB therapies, particularly PD-1 and PD-L1 inhibitors, remain the most widely employed in HNSCC immunotherapy. PD-1 checkpoint molecules on T cells bind with the ligands PD-L1 and PD-L2 present in cancer cells and immune-infiltrating cells, contributing to immune suppression. Interrupting this binding process can reactivate the immune system and induce durable antitumour effects [ ]. Currently, PD-1 blockers encompassing nivolumab, pembrolizumab and durvalumab have received approval for HNSCC management [ ]. Investigations indicate nivolumab enhances overall survival (OS) in individuals with platinum-refractory, relapsing HNSCC and is approved for platinum-resistant cases [ ]. Pembrolizumab is effectively used in R/M HNSCC, demonstrating good safety and clinical efficacy [ ]. Ongoing studies suggest that durvalumab extends OS and progression-free survival in individuals with R/M HNSCC exhibiting PD-L1 tumour expression ≥25%[ ]. Additionally, other anti-PD-L1 agents, including avelumab and atezolizumab, have been applied in HNSCC therapy. Avelumab has been shown to improve OS in individuals with R/M HNSCC, with only mild discomfort [ ]. Atezolizumab, administered to patients with advanced HNSCC, has yielded a positive quality of life with no major adverse events [ ]. Overall, ICB therapies restore anti-tumour immune responses, primarily mediated by CD8+ T cells, in individuals with R/M HNSCC. Notably, a study by Chen et al. demonstrated a marked link between p16 protein expression (indicative of HPV positivity) and PD-L1 expression in HNSCC, which may explain the superior response rates to PD-1/PD-L1 blockade in individuals with HPV-positive HNSCC versus their HPV-negative counterparts [ ].
Besides PD-1 and PD-L1, several other immune checkpoints are being explored for HNSCC treatment, encompassing CTLA-4, NK group 2 member A (NKG2A), T-cell immunoglobulin and mucin-domain-containing protein-3 (TIM-3), lymphocyte activation gene 3 (LAG-3) and inducible T cell co-stimulator (ICOS) [ ]. CTLA-4 demonstrates substantial expression on Tregs, where it exerts a suppressive effect on NK cells, reducing the efficacy of anti-PD-1/PD-L1 therapies. Therefore, combining anti-CTLA-4 agents, such as tremelimumab, with anti-PD-L1 drugs like durvalumab may enhance treatment outcomes in R/M HNSCC [ ]. Additional compounds targeting Tregs, namely eftilagimod alpha, INCAGNO2390 (anti-LAG-3) and INCAGN02385 (anti-TIM-3), are being tested in conjunction with pembrolizumab or retifanlimab [ ]. In a separate trial, monalizumab, an NKG2A inhibitor, has shown synergy with cetuximab, a monoclonal antibody targeting epidermal growth factor receptor, improving the objective response rate in individuals with R/M HNSCC [ ]. Furthermore, ICOS and its receptor agonist antibody, feladilimab, are being evaluated alongside 5-FU-platinum chemotherapy and pembrolizumab for R/M HNSCC therapy [ ]. In conclusion, numerous immune checkpoints and therapeutic agents are under investigation, with the integration of anti-PD-1/PD-L1 drugs and these additional agents representing a promising strategy for treating R/M HNSCC.

## Tumour vaccines
High-risk HPV infection serves a pivotal function in the development of HNSCC, with over 220 HPV types identified to date, of which 19 have been linked to HNSCC [ ]. Among these, HPV-16 is the most prevalent oncogenic subtype, detected in more than 80% of HNSCC cases [ ]. Consequently, the development of vaccines targeting HPV presents a promising therapeutic strategy for patients with HPV-positive HNSCC. Ongoing studies suggest that combining the HPV-16 vaccine with nivolumab markedly boosts specific T cell generation and improves both the overall response rate and median OS compared to nivolumab monotherapy [ ]. Therefore, reliable methods and biomarkers to assess P16 status in HPV infections are critical for effective HNSCC therapy. Currently, testing HPV DNA or RNA directly and employing immunohistochemical methods have proven successful in identifying clinically and biologically distinct HPV-positive HNSCC cases [ ].
In HNSCC, mutations in genes like TP53, PTEN, PIK3CA, CDKN2A, NOTCH1 and HRA are frequently observed [ ]. Studies have also highlighted high-frequency mutations in KEAP1, CUL3 and NFE2L2, particularly in HPV-negative HNSCC [ ]. Moreover, mutations in genes such as HLA-A, HLA-B, B2M and TGFBR2 can compromise immune cell surveillance, thereby influencing immune evasion [ ]. Consequently, cancer vaccines are being explored as potential treatments for HNSCC with these mutations. During a Phase I clinical investigation, the mRNA-4157 cancer vaccine exhibited marked enhancement in the overall response rate and a reduction in disease progression in individuals with HNSCC [ ]. Similarly, the neoantigen vaccine TG4050 has shown promising results, displaying both good tolerability and the ­ability to stimulate T-cell responses in HNSCC therapy [ ]. While studies are limited, the early findings suggest that cancer vaccines hold considerable potential.
However, some cancer vaccine trials have failed due to issues with specificity, immunogenicity, or insufficient expression of cancer cells. These challenges indicate that cancer vaccines alone may not be sufficient to overcome the barriers to effective anti-tumour immunity. To address these limitations, researchers have explored combining cancer vaccines with ICBs. For instance, a Phase II study is currently investigating the synergy between UCPVax, a CD4 TH1-inducer cancer vaccine and atezolizumab [ ]. This combination approach represents a promising strategy to overcome the shortcomings of individual therapies in HNSCC.

## Oncolytic viruses (OVs)
OVs, which are viruses that have been genetically modified or exist naturally and specifically multiply inside cancer cells, leading to their destruction while leaving healthy cells unharmed, present an encouraging treatment strategy by strengthening the immune system’s response against tumours [ ]. OVs possess the capability to attract tumour-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs), enhance the display of neoantigens, and transform ‘cold’ tumors to ‘hot’ tumours, thereby boosting the efficacy of immunotherapy [ ]. Several OVs are currently being explored for HNSCC treatment, including adenoviruses, reoviruses, vaccinia virus and herpes simplex virus [ ]. Adenoviruses, marked by their non-enveloped structure and double-stranded DNA composition, possess substantial genomic material (around 35 kb), enabling the incorporation of lengthy genetic sequences (reaching 10 kb) during genetic engineering [ ]. ONYX-015, one of the most studied oncolytic adenoviruses, has shown promising results in HNSCC treatment. Clinical trials have demonstrated that ONYX-015 markedly reduces tumour size and improves patient conditions without causing noticeable side effects [ ]. Reoviruses are wild-type, double-stranded RNA viruses from the Reoviridae family, known for their ability to replicate in tumour cells while sparing normal, untransformed cells [ ]. This selective replication confers significant oncolytic potential. During a Phase I/II clinical investigation, Reovirus type 3 Dearing (RT3D) combined with taxanes (paclitaxel and carboplatin) improved complete or partial response rates in patients with advanced HNSCC [ ]. Additionally, reoviruses combined with radiotherapy have emerged as a promising treatment modality for HNSCC [ ]. Vaccinia virus, a poxvirus historically used in the smallpox vaccine, is also under investigation for its oncolytic potential [ ]. JX-594, a genetically engineered vaccinia virus, carries mutations in the TK gene along with the GM-CSF gene and lac-Z transgenes [ ]. While clinical trials with JX-594 have not yet included HNSCC, its safety profile and ability to induce repeatable tumour necrosis in solid tumours make it a compelling candidate for future studies [ ]. Herpes simplex virus, which pioneered genetically engineered OVs, contains double-stranded DNA with an extensive genomic sequence (152 kb), enabling substantial capacity for incorporating sizeable genetic elements while avoiding insertional alterations [ ]. T-Vec, a genetically modified HSV, has shown significant efficacy when combined with cisplatin and radiation, achieving a 76.5% relapse-free local control rate in clinical trials [ ]. In addition to chemotherapy and radiotherapy, OVs have been explored in combination with ICBs, such as pembrolizumab, to further enhance their therapeutic effects [ ].

## ACTs
ACTs, including chimeric antigen receptor (CAR)-T-cell therapy, T-cell receptor (TCR)-T-cell therapy and natural killer (NK)-cell therapy, involve the isolation of immune-active cells from patients, followed by in vitro expansion, functional identification and reinfusion into the patient to directly target and kill tumour cells or trigger an immune reaction against the tumour [ ]. These therapies are characterized by their rapid onset of action and minimal interference from internal factors. CAR-T therapy, the most widely utilized form of ACT, has demonstrated remarkable effectiveness in addressing blood-related cancers and is progressively being applied to solid tumours such as HNSCC [ ]. In MUC1-expressing HNSCC, CAR-MUC1-IL22T cells exhibit strong cytotoxic effects [ ]. Additionally, CD70, highly expressed in HNSCC, can be targeted by CD70-specific CAR-T cells, which effectively recognize and eliminate CD70-positive tumour cells [ ]. The integration of synNotch technology further enhances CAR-T-cell precision by enabling the targeting and destruction of both specific and adjacent tumour cells [ ]. TCR-T-cell therapy, which encompasses the genetic engineering of T lymphocytes to express cancer-specific TCRs, strengthens the lymphocytes’ capability to detect and target tumour antigens, enabling rapid identification and elimination of cancer cells [ ]. In one study, blood-derived T cells and HLA-matched antigen-presenting cells were used to replace TILs and autologous dendritic cells, resulting in the isolation of reactive TCRs that markedly enhanced the anti-tumour activity of TCR-T cells in HNSCC [ ]. For NK-cell therapy, NK cells, sourced from embryonic stem cells, NK cell lines, umbilical cord blood or peripheral blood, exhibit potent anti-tumour activity [ ]. Whether used alone or in combination with drugs like cetuximab, NK-cell therapy shows promising efficacy against both HPV-positive and HPV-negative HNSCC cells [ ].

## Application of AI in HNSCC immunotherapy
In oncology, numerous AI algorithms, including ML and DL, integrates data from multi-omics databases to screen, detect, diagnose, classify and characterize cancer genomics, encompassing details such as the tumour microenvironment (TME) and prognostic biomarkers [ , ] ( ). These capabilities are predominantly utilized in cancer radiology and pathology. The application of AI in HNSCC is not a recent development [ , ]. With the advent of radiomics, numerous studies have employed diagnostic imaging data, specifically whole-slide histology images (WSI) and radiological imaging, to train AI models for HNSCC diagnosis, particularly in evaluating lesions [ ]. A systematic review by one research group focused on the detection and grading of malignant versus benign lesions in the head and neck region using WSI, with the majority of studies employing supervised ML models [ ]. Santer et al. executed a systematic review of AI applications, specifically highlighting studies using convolutional neural networks (CNNs) and histopathological slides for classifying cervical lymph nodes [ ]. Huang et al. developed an AI model to identify radiomic features from computed tomography (CT) scans, correlating these with molecular subtypes and mutations in patients with HNSCC [ ]. Using techniques such as minimal-redundancy-maximal-relevance and least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO), they identified key features from CT scans, including molecular phenotypes like HPV status, gene expression subtypes, DNA methylation patterns and somatic mutations. These non-invasive biomarkers facilitated patient stratification and personalized treatment planning (Supplemental table 1, 2 and 3).
 Beyond diagnosis, classification and prognosis, AI also serves a pivotal function in HNSCC treatment. Given the complexity of immunotherapy, AI can help manage and interpret these sophisticated treatments, offering tailored therapies to individual patients in the pursuit of personalized medicine [ ]. ICB therapy has evolved as an encouraging approach for treating R/M HNSCC. These inhibitors interact with signalling proteins present in immune and neoplastic cells to combat immune escape mechanisms and boost tumour elimination [ ]. Although these agents have demonstrated enhanced survival outcomes in HNSCC patients compared to conventional therapies, the majority of individuals show minimal therapeutic response and may experience treatment-induced side effects, along with possible economic burden [ ]. Notwithstanding their effectiveness, determining which patients will respond favourably to checkpoint blockade therapy continues to pose a major obstacle. Existing predictive indicators, encompassing tumour mutational burden (TMB) and PD-L1 immunostaining, demonstrate restricted prognostic value [ ] ( ).
 

## Predicting responder to immunotherapy based on AI
AI can be leveraged to construct various models that assess immunotherapy responses in patients based on distinct features and differentially expressed genes ( ). Lee et al. utilized a random forest ML model to forecast ICB response through completing patient risk stratification and identifying possible responders and non-responders in an HNSCC cohort, employing 11 or 16 clinical, laboratory and genomic features [ ]. Bigelow et al. developed a random forest ML classifier based on multiple genomic features derived from whole exome sequencing of patients with HNSCC [ ]. This algorithm evaluated genomic determinants of anti-PD1 responses, identifying potential responders to anti-PD1 immunotherapy. Hecht et al. applied ML to assess immune phenotyping parameters from the peripheral blood of patients with HNSCC treated in the CheckRad-CD8 trial, aiming to predict pathological complete response after induction immunotherapy with tremelimumab [ ]. Chen et al. used ML algorithms to classify different TME subtypes, finding that the ‘Immune-Stimulation’ subtype, commonly observed in HPV-infected HNSCC, is a promising candidate for immunotherapy [ ]. González‐García et al. combined nonlinear mixed-effects modelling of longitudinal tumour size data from patients with R/M HNSCC receiving durvalumab with an ML classification algorithm to evaluate early predictions of best overall response and OS at 6 months [ ]. Nonlinear mixed-effects model fitting to early tumour size data, coupled with ML to classify patients’ responses into complete response, partial response stable disease, progressive disease or not evaluable, minimizing total classification error. This approach successfully predicted clinical outcomes by combining traditional pharmacometrics with ML techniques. Xu et al. constructed a cancer-associated fibroblast (CAF)-cancer cell crosstalk-related gene prognostic index using two models, the fibroblast-cancer cell indirect coculture model and the fibroblast-cancer cell organoid model [ ]. The cancer cell crosstalk-related gene prognostic index included four gene pairs-IGF1-IGF1R, LGALS9-CD44, SEMA5A-PLXNA1 and TNXB-SDC1-and was used to evaluate response and prognosis to ICB therapy in patients with HNSCC. Their results indicated that a high cancer cell crosstalk-related gene prognostic index score correlated with poor benefits from ICB therapy and worse OS outcomes. Ma et al. employed LASSO regression and recursive feature elimination algorithms to identify key genes associated with nivolumab resistance in patients with HNSCC, using single-cell and bulk RNA-sequencing data [ ]. They found that high expression of PPP1R14A was linked to lower immune scores, higher expression of immunosuppressive factors, lower differentiation of infiltrating immune cells (dendritic cells and macrophages), and increased ligand-receptor interaction between HLA family and CD4, all of which were markedly associated with nivolumab resistance. Huang et al. developed the HNSCC cell differentiation score, based on differentiation-related genes, to categorize patients into three distinct molecular subtypes [ ]. The low-HNSCC cell differentiation score cohort exhibited enhanced immune activation and a stronger response to anti-PD-1/PD-L1 immunotherapy. Liu et al. utilizing multi-omics analysis of HNSCC mitophagy subtypes, applied 10 ML algorithms, including RSF, Enet, Lasso, Stepwise Cox, Ridge, CoxBoost, plsRcox, SuperPC, GBM and survival SVM, in combination to create the mitophagy subgroup risk score for forecasting ICB therapy response [ ]. Their results indicated that patients with lower mitophagy subgroup risk scores demonstrated greater responsiveness to immunotherapy. Jin et al. applied 10 ML algorithms and 101 algorithms combinations to test programmed cell death (PCD) scores in patients with HNSCC [ ]. Their findings revealed that various PCD scores were closely associated with CD4+ and CD8+ T cells, TMB, PD-L1 expression and PCD-related genes, suggesting their potential as predictive indicators for effective immunotherapy. Li et al. developed the oesophageal squamous cell cancer-pathomics signature utilizing haematoxylin and eosin (H&E)-stained WSI and outcome-supervised ViT-RNN to forecast survival in patients with ESCC receiving PD-1 inhibitors [ ]. This DL-based model outperformed manual feature extraction techniques, exhibiting superior capability to determine ESCC cases likely to respond to PD-1 blockade treatment.
 AI has also been utilized to segment individuals with HNSCC into subgroups to predict their response to immunotherapy. Han et al. developed a predictive model and nomogram grounded in 12 ion channel genes using ML, dividing individuals with HNSCC into high- and low-risk cohorts grounded in risk scores [ ]. Their findings indicated enhanced immunotherapy responsiveness in the elevated-risk category. For R/M HNSCC individuals receiving anti-PD-1, anti-PD-L1 or anti-CTLA-4 ICB treatments, random forest algorithms (11 and 16) exhibited sufficient accuracy in forecasting tumour outcomes, stratifying responders and non-responders based on overall and progression-free survival with reliable risk percentages [ ]. Additionally, a clinical biomarker assay, OncoPrism-HNSCC, was developed to identify patients who would achieve disease control in response to anti-PD-1 inhibitors [ ]. Diao et al. employed an ML approach to stratify patients with OSCC into responders and non-responders to immunotherapy utilizing integrated immune features including CD8+, CD45RO+, CD11b+, CD20+, FOXP3 and PD-1 [ ].
The advent of AI and radiomics has enabled the development of AI-based computer-aided prediction systems, which show significant promise in early prediction of immunotherapy efficacy and clinical outcomes. Nguyen et al. utilized IBEX software to extract radiomic features from CT scans based on the delineated tumour regions of patients with HNSCC [ ]. This non-invasive radiomic model demonstrated the best performance in predicting the hot/cold phenotype and effectively identified patients with HNSCC who could benefit from immunotherapy. Similarly, Zandberg et al. employed radiomic feature extraction from pre-treatment CT scans using the XGBoost algorithm, which accurately predicted the efficacy of anti-PD-1 immunotherapy and provided insights into the TME in R/M HNSCC [ ]. Furthermore, a support vector machine classifier using quantitative US radiomics data obtained after radiation therapy achieved excellent results in predicting immunotherapy outcomes for HNSCC [ ]. In the context of platinum-refractory HNSCC, Lu et al. developed a radiomic risk-stratification model to categorize patients, revealing that short-run high grey-level emphasis was more effective than PD-L1 expression for selecting candidates for ICB therapy [ ]. Given the increasing interest in γδ T-cells due to their promising potential for anti-tumour immunotherapy, Fan et al. created a radiomics model using gradient boosting to predict γδ T-cell abundance, offering a valuable tool for clinical decision-making in HNSCC immunotherapy [ ]. Hellwig et al. developed a random forest ML algorithm utilizing dynamic contrast-enhanced magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) parameters, which successfully predicted response to single-cycle induction chemo-immunotherapy in locally advanced HNSCC with high sensitivity and specificity [ ].
DL methods, a subset of ML techniques, excel in identifying complex patterns within large datasets. Tostado et al. developed a DL CNN approach to unravel the factors driving heterogeneity in the antitumour immune response using single-cell phenotypic data and single-cell transcriptomic analysis [ ]. This model offers potential as a tool for accelerating biomarker discovery aimed at predicting immunotherapy responses in patients with HNSCC. Ghahremani et al. created a DL-ready computational pathology dataset comprising restained and co-registered digitized images from patients with HNSCC [ ]. This dataset enabled objective immune and tumour cell annotations via multiplex immunofluorescence and multiplex immunohistochemistry (IHC), providing a more reproducible and accurate characterization of the TME, which is essential for precise immunotherapy stratification in HNSCC. Gessain developed a DL model for automating the quantification of the multinucleated giant cell-to-tumour cell ratio on routinely stained pathological WSIs (H&E/haematoxylin, eosin, saffron (HES)) [ ]. This model revealed that TREM2-expressing multinucleated giant cells are significant contributors to favourable immunotherapy responses in HNSCC. Koyuncu et al. developed a DL model using HE histochemistry staining from patients with oral squamous cell carcinoma (OSCC) to ascertain a multinucleation index value [ ]. Their research demonstrated that elevated multinucleation index values correlated with reduced effector immune populations across different HNSCC locations, offering an understanding of the biological mechanisms of multinucleation and its influence on immunotherapy results. The combined positive score (CPS), which assesses PD-L1 levels in tumour specimens to identify candidates most suitable for anti-PD-1/PD-L1 treatment, has received FDA authorization for HNSCC. Vahadane et al. created a DL model utilizing three-color multiplex immunofluorescence images (DAPI, PD-L1 and Pan-cytokeratin) as input to predict the CPS, presenting a cost-effective and high-quality method [ ]. Finally, Luo et al. constructed a diagnostic model based on six differentially expressed genes from the Gene Expression Omnibus, neuralHNSCC, using random forest and artificial neural network (ANN) techniques [ ]. The neuralHNSCC model can identify distinct immune cell infiltration characteristics in HNSCC tissues, highlighting a higher infiltration of naïve B cells, M0 macrophages, activated dendritic cells and activated mast cells, however, a lower infiltration of resting mast cells, offering a promising approach for immunotherapy response prediction.
AI also holds significant potential for advancing the clinical implications of immunotherapy in patients with HNSCC. Kim et al. utilized AI-powered WSI analysis to confirm that the combination of durvalumab and tremelimumab notably reshapes the TME towards immune-inflamed phenotypes, markedly improving distant recurrence-free surviva in individuals with locally advanced yet resectable HNSCC [ ]. In clinical decision-making, an AI-based model, such as ChatGPT, has been shown to assist by suggesting a broader range of therapeutic options, including immunotherapy, compared to traditional multidisciplinary tumour board discussions [ ]. Canini et al. executed a multi-centre study that demonstrated the potential of digital pathology and computational tools, specifically QuPath software, in addressing inconsistencies in human-based PD-L1 CPS scoring [ ]. This study highlighted the integration of AI techniques into clinical practice, which could enhance the reliability of PD-L1 scoring and, consequently, improve clinical decision-making and patient outcomes in HNSCC.

## Predicting sensitivity to immunotherapy based on AI
In addition to its application in predicting immunotherapy responses, AI can also predict patients’ sensitivity to immunotherapy ( ). Wang et al. developed a macrophage-related risk signature model, incorporating nine genes (IGF2BP2, PPP1R14C, SLC7A5, KRT9, RAC2, NTN4, CTLA4, APOC1 and CYP27A1), formulated through the synthesis of 101 ML algorithms combinations [ ]. The model revealed that the high-risk cohort had lower OS and enhanced levels of immune checkpoint and HLA genes, indicating a stronger immune evasion capacity. Notably, tumour immune dysfunction and exclusion scores were positively linked to the risk score, suggesting that high-risk tumour cells exhibit stronger immune escape abilities, which may contribute to greater resistance to immunotherapy. In contrast, patients with HNSCC in the low-risk cohort exhibited enhanced responsiveness to ICB therapy, demonstrating a higher positive immune response rate. Hu et al. developed a consensus immune-derived gene signature, which includes 236 consensus prognostic genes identified through 10 ML algorithms and 85 algorithms combinations [ ]. Individuals showing reduced consensus immune-derived gene signature demonstrated decreased genomic changes, enhanced immune cell presence and greater responsiveness to immune-based treatments, suggesting better clinical outcomes. Similarly, Shao et al. constructed a risk model based on 17 necroptosis-related long noncoding RNAs, which classified individuals into two clusters grounded in their expression levels to assess immunotherapy sensitivity [ ]. The model showed that higher levels of necroptosis-related long noncoding RNAs improved the sensitivity of patients with HNSCC to immunotherapy and positively influenced patient prognosis. Likewise, Wang et al. developed a predictive model using 13 interactive PANoptosis-related lncRNAs and applied five ML algorithms [ ]. The reduced PANoptosis-related lncRNAs cohort demonstrated improved survival outcomes, higher immune cell infiltration, upregulated immune response, decreased tumour mutational load and heightened responsiveness to immune-based treatments. Wu et al. created a TGF-β risk score grounded in TGF-β-related genes using the LASSO Cox algorithm [ ]. The TGF-β risk score forecasted the TME status, showing a negative correlation with NK cells and a positive correlation with macrophages and fibroblasts. Elevated TGF-β risk scores were linked to a ‘cold’ TME, and patients with higher scores exhibited greater sensitivity to immunotherapy. Finally, Lv et al. employed LASSO Cox regression analysis to develop a prognostic gene signature grounded in seven hypoxia-immune-related genes for clustering patients with OSCC according to their risk scores [ ]. The low-risk cohort suggested potentially better immunotherapy efficacy, further emphasizing the utility of AI models in assessing immunotherapy sensitivity. Wang et al. established a robust predictive model for assessing the effectiveness of immunotherapy in patients with HNSCC using seven immune-related genes [ ]. The model utilized three ML algorithms, including LASSO, random forest and Rbsurv. This model successfully identified key predictive factors associated with immunotherapy responses in patients with HNSCC. Zhang et al. developed a prognostic model grounded in 28 Golgi apparatus-related genes to classify patients with HNSCC into high- and low-risk cohorts [ ]. The results indicated that the low-risk cohort was more suitable for receiving immunotherapy, highlighting the importance of Golgi apparatus-related genes as potential biomarkers for treatment stratification. Diao et al. integrated diverse genetic, biological, clinical features and therapeutic vulnerabilities to cluster patients with HNSCC into three integrative multi-omics clusters [ ]. Using ML, they developed two signatures based on gene features extracted from these clusters, enhancing the prediction of patients’ sensitivity to immunotherapy. Tsakiroglou et al. applied a deep CNN classifier to develop the hypothesised interaction distribution method for quantifying the spatial proximity between cells [ ]. Their findings revealed that high frequencies of proximal CD8+ and PD-L1+ cells, as well as PD1+ and PD-L1+ cells, were prognostic for poor OS in individuals with HPV-negative oropharyngeal squamous cell carcinoma, suggesting its potential for assessing immunotherapy effectiveness. Xirou et al. developed a network classifier called NN3-17 using ML to evaluate TILs on H&E-stained sections with the QuPath software [ ]. The classifier demonstrated that TILs variables were linked to positive outcomes in both HPV-positive and HPV-negative HNSCC cases, positioning TILs as an independent prognostic marker for immunotherapy outcomes. Wang et al. also constructed a scoring system based on CAFs using ML algorithms to classify individuals with HNSCC into high- and low-risk cohorts [ ]. Their model revealed that individuals with high CAF scores exhibited worse OS, with tumours showing higher levels of inflammatory TAM infiltration. This infiltration contributed to an immunosuppressive TME, potentially indicating insensitivity to immunotherapy.

## Searching for biomarkers based on AI
AI has also proven valuable in identifying novel biomarkers for HNSCC immunotherapy ( ). Yang et al. demonstrated that YKT6 could serve as a prognostic and immunotherapy response predictor for HNSCC through differential expression, prognostic analysis and ML models [ ]. Further investigation revealed that lower YKT6 expression correlated with higher CD8+ T cell infiltration, influencing the TME and potentially predicting immunotherapy response in HNSCC. Huang et al. employed non-negative matrix factorization to classify patients with HNSCC based on G protein-coupled receptor [ ]. By comparing clinical and biological differences between subtypes, S1PR4 was identified as a critical G protein-coupled receptor that defined the subtypes. In CAR-T immunotherapy, the cytotoxicity of CAR-T cells was positively correlated with S1PR4 expression, which in turn facilitated the release of cytotoxic markers like GZMB and IFN. Yang et al. identified PATL1 as a key gene from RNA sequencing data of Nasal-type NK/T-cell lymphoma using support vector machine-recursive feature elimination [ ]. Due to the similarities between HNSCC and Nasal-type NK/T-cell lymphoma, further analysis using The Cancer Immunome Atlas and Tumour Immune Dysfunction and Exclusion algorithms showed that PATL1 expression was markedly associated with immune cell composition and immune checkpoint levels, positioning PATL1 as a potential predictor of immunotherapy response in individuals with HNSCC. Wang et al. leveraged lasso penalized regression and random forest algorithms to develop a classifier based on three immune-related long noncoding RNAs, BARX1-DT, KLHL7-DT and LINC02154, to predict prognosis, immune infiltration and immunotherapy response in laryngeal squamous cell carcinoma [ ]. This classifier stratified individuals with laryngeal squamous cell carcinoma into high-risk and low-risk cohorts grounded in OS and recurrence-free survival, facilitating the assessment of clinical response to immunotherapy. Zhang et al. applied similarity network fusion and MoCluster algorithms to create the getMPsub classifier, which categorized patients with HNSCC based on mononuclear phagocyte system-related multi-omics features [ ]. Elevated CS2 expression was linked to improved prognosis and enhanced immunotherapy response. microRNA (miRNA) is critically involved in modulating the anticancer immune response. Xia et al. developed a miRNA-based feature classifier incorporating 25 differentially expressed miRNAs using the LASSO method [ ]. These miRNA expression patterns were associated with TMB and have potential as biomarkers for immunotherapy response in HNSCC.
 

## Discussion
The development of cellular network models underlying cancer has provided a quantitative framework for exploring the relationship between network properties and HNSCC immunotherapy via AI-driven biological analysis. This has enabled the prediction of patient responses and sensitivities to HNSCC treatments, as well as the discovery of novel biomarkers and therapeutic targets. However, no comprehensive review exists that synthesizes the application of AI in predicting immunotherapy responses, identifying biomarkers and exploring targets in HNSCC. This review aims to fill that gap by thoroughly examining current immunotherapy approaches for HNSCC and the role of AI in predicting immunotherapy responses and sensitivities, as well as in the identification of new biomarkers and targets.
Immunotherapy has markedly improved outcomes for patients with cancers. However, to further personalize treatments, more refined prediction methods and novel biomarkers are needed to minimize unnecessary treatment and enhance efficacy. AI has emerged as a promising tool to help clinicians better understand individual patient response patterns and predict sensitivity to immunotherapy. In addition to conventional approaches, several AI-based strategies are showing potential, including AI-driven radiomics, pathology, genomics, transcriptomics, proteomics, microbiomics, multi-omics and multi-model integration [ ]. These methodologies, either individually or in combination, can predict treatment responses, sensitivities and prognosis in patients with HNSCC while also aiding in the discovery of novel biomarkers and therapeutic targets.
Despite the considerable advantages AI brings to HNSCC immunotherapy, several challenges remain. Key obstacles include the transparency of AI’s decision-making processes, the acquisition and sharing of robust medical data, privacy concerns and ensuring equitable performance across diverse patient populations [ ]. Given the clinical application of these models, there are additional challenges in integrating AI tools into existing healthcare workflows and assessing their overall impact on patient outcomes and healthcare systems. This review highlights these core challenges and outlines several approaches currently being developed by the research community to overcome them ( ).
 The interpretability of AI decisions and its integration into routine clinical workflows are pivotal challenges. The intrinsic intricacy of AI systems may result in reduced transparency, and the absence of interpretable results often breeds mistrust and resistance among clinicians. This ‘black box’ issue impedes the broader application of AI in medical environments, as healthcare providers require complete comprehension of AI-derived results to make well-informed choices [ ]. Additionally, the seamless incorporation of AI into medical workflows demands a thorough evaluation of human-AI collaboration. Medical professionals must become proficient with emerging technologies, necessitating extensive training and the integration of AI tools into their daily routines [ ]. Ultimately, clinician trust and acceptance of AI recommendations are critical to the successful adoption of these methods [ ]. Moreover, the anatomical complexity of HNSCC tissues introduces further challenges for AI utilization. High-definition imaging proves crucial in detecting the particular characteristics required for precise prediction of treatment response and patient outcomes [ ]. Differences in tissue manifestation, influenced by underlying medical conditions, patient demographics and prior interventions, make AI model development more challenging. Moreover, the closeness of essential structures in HNSCC cases requires exceptional accuracy in diagnostic processes and therapy design to prevent unintended injury [ ]. Although AI demonstrates potential in supporting human assessment, especially in intricate diagnostic reasoning, it serves most effectively as a supportive instrument rather than a replacement for expert clinical expertise [ ].
The continuous evolution of AI methods necessitates the retraining of models with new data and regular system updates to ensure ongoing efficacy. The AI model development process encompasses crucial phases: data collection, preprocessing, feature extraction and model training [ ]. Substantial data collections prove fundamental for establishing dependable AI systems capable of precisely identifying responders to immunotherapy. High-quality, annotated datasets prove fundamental for the successful implementation of AI models, as they enhance the generalizability of these systems [ ]. It is well recognized that inadequate specimen handling and artefacts can markedly influence AI performance. Common artefacts like staining inconsistencies, air bubbles and tissue folding can confuse AI models, highlighting the importance of preprocessing datasets to mitigate these issues [ ]. Preprocessing techniques like data augmentation and normalization improve dataset variability and quality, facilitating more effective AI learning [ ]. It is vital to extract relevant features from diverse datasets. Training AI models to detect and interpret artefacts requires integrating them into the training process and creating models that can distinguish between genuine pathological features and artefacts [ ]. Standardizing these imperfect datasets is essential for developing effective AI models. Standardization strengthens reproducibility, reduces variability and minimizes discrepancies arising from differing database standards and laboratory practices. Consistent histological processing and biopsy procedures help mitigate variability and enhance AI accuracy. Currently, methods such as the uniform use of fixatives and techniques like immunofluorescence, H&E and IHC are employed to ensure tissue sample uniformity and improve AI performance [ ]. For instance, IHC serves a pivotal function in strengthening AI training by offering detailed molecular understanding through specific antibodies targeting cancer cell antigens [ ]. These antibodies prove vital for detecting indicators, including Melan-A, S-100, HMB-45, Cytokeratin 5/6 and Ber-EP4 [ ]. By integrating IHC data into AI training datasets, models can learn specific features related to HNSCC, thus improving predictive accuracy. Utilizing IHC’s molecular insights strengthens the AI’s capacity to predict responses to HNSCC immunotherapy, facilitating early identification of responders and ultimately improving patient outcomes [ ].
The translation of AI models from research environments into prospective, multicentre clinical use requires rigorous and systematic validation, along with adherence to regulatory standards. First, the clinical application and objectives must be clearly defined. A precise description of the model’s intended use, whether for detection, diagnosis, prognostic prediction or treatment recommendation, will guide the selection of appropriate validation endpoints [ ]. A comprehensive validation study protocol should then be developed, detailing the study design (observational or interventional), inclusion/exclusion criteria, golden standards and primary and secondary endpoints [ ]. Data management and standardization are also crucial. In multicentre settings, data acquisition protocols should aim to harmonize equipment and technical parameters to minimize variability. Data anonymization must be performed in compliance with privacy regulations such as General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) and Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA) [ ]. Consistent data preprocessing, ensuring that input data from all sites undergo the same steps (e.g. normalization or resampling) as during model development, is essential to maintain reproducibility. Furthermore, integrating the model into the clinical workflows of each participating centre allows for practical evaluation and helps identify technical challenges in real-world settings [ ]. Statistical analysis should include calculation of predefined endpoint metrics with confidence intervals, along with subgroup analyses to assess performance consistency across different populations (e.g. age, gender or ethnicity), disease stages and equipment types [ ]. Finally, analyzing model failure cases is critical to understanding its limitations and refining its clinical applicability.
Before a clinical AI model can be deployed, a systematic sequence of steps must be completed. First, the intended clinical use and risk classification of the model must be clearly defined [ ]. A prospective, multicentre validation study should then be designed and conducted to collect relevant performance data. In parallel, documentation for regulatory submission, such as technical files and clinical evaluation reports, must be prepared. Subsequently, the appropriate regulatory pathway, such as Food and Drug Administration (FDA), Conformite Europeenne (CE) mark or National Medical Products Administration (NMPA), should be selected, followed by formal submission and approval [ ]. Once authorized, the model can be integrated into clinical workflows, with user training and deployment activities conducted to demonstrate its clinical utility. After implementation, ongoing post-market surveillance is required to monitor real-world performance, supported by continuous model iteration and updates to ensure long-term safety and efficacy, thereby completing the end-to-end development and deployment lifecycle [ ].
In addition, the development of clinical AI models necessitates addressing inherent data heterogeneity across institutions, with key challenges including batch effects, imaging heterogeneity and annotation bias [ ]. Technical biases introduced by batch effects may cause models to misinterpret technical variations as biologically meaningful signals. To mitigate this, it is essential to randomize samples across batches during data collection and apply batch effect correction methods such as ComBat [ ]. In model training, the ‘batch’ variable should be explicitly included as a feature during the training phase but excluded during prediction. Imaging heterogeneity poses another significant challenge, as models may become overly sensitive to variations in texture, contrast and noise rather than to underlying pathological structures [ ]. Promoting standardized image acquisition protocols in multicentre studies is critical to reduce such variability. Additionally, techniques such as intensity normalization, image resampling and data augmentation can substantially improve model robustness and generalizability [ ]. Annotation bias further constrains model performance and complicates evaluation. Adopting standardized annotation protocols, conducting comprehensive annotator training and performing multi-reader studies represent established strategies to enhance label consistency [ ]. More recently, emerging approaches, including noise-aware learning, crowdsourced annotation frameworks and uncertainty-aware modelling, have shown promise in further elevating model reliability and clinical applicability.
The application of AI in HNSCC holds significant promise for improving disease management, however, the ethical concerns that involved in this process should be addressed. To fully harness the potential of AI in HNSCC, five essential ethical issues require rigorous attention: algorithmic fairness and biases, transparency and interpretability, responsibility distinction, informed consent and data privacy, and technology abuse and moral boundaries [ ]. The algorithmic biases may lead to the emergence of inequity and social prejudice, which ultimately undermining public trust in AI-driven healthcare solutions [ ]. The inherent ‘black box’ nature of many AI systems further exacerbates this issue, as users cannot comprehend how their personal data is processed, leading to a erosion of trust. Additionally, the question of accountability remains unresolved-should responsibility for AI-related errors lie with developers, corporations or end-users? Another critical concern is the formalized processing of ‘informed consent’ that ignored the true right to know and privacy choice of user. Moreover, inadequate regulatory oversight and ethical safeguards may facilitate AI misuse, such as unauthorized surveillance, exploitative data mining and privacy violations [ ].
In oncology research, particular ethical attention must be given to the use of AI, especially when applied to sensitive omics data such as genomic and proteomic information. The processing of such data under the GDPR intersects directly with core AI ethics concerns. GDPR mandates fair processing, which is inherently violated if a biased AI model produces discriminatory outcomes against particular demographic groups [ ]. The regulation also establishes a right to be informed and a right to explanation, meaning data subjects are entitled to a meaningful understanding of how their data is processed, a requirement that unexplainable AI systems struggle to satisfy legally. Data protection serves as a cornerstone of GDPR, requiring technical and organizational measures such as anonymization or pseudonymization, data encryption and privacy-by-design defaults [ ]. These protections must be embedded throughout the AI lifecycle, including model training, data storage and inference phases. Furthermore, GDPR stipulates that consent must be specific, informed and unambiguous [ ]. In practice, this means research participants should be clearly informed in plain language about how their omics data will be used in AI development, potential risks and benefits, and their associated rights [ ]. When AI models are deployed in clinical trials, a clear framework of rights and responsibilities should be established beforehand. This includes defining the roles of all involved parties including sponsors, investigators, AI developers and ethics committees. Prior to trial initiation, mandatory bias assessments should be conducted, with continuous monitoring of participant diversity throughout the study [ ]. Depending on the level of risk, appropriate explainability standards should be applied; high-risk scenarios may require explainable AI techniques or even avoidance of ‘black box’ models entirely. Finally, ethics committees should have their oversight responsibilities strengthened to include review of AI tools themselves, supported by the development and enforcement of tailored ethical guidelines governing AI use in clinical research [ ].
Thus, various measures should be used to solve these problems in order to improve the application of AI in HNSCC. First, robust policies and regulations must be established to regulate the collection and processing of personal data, such as Personal Information Protection Law of the People’s Republic of China and General Data Protection Regulation [ ]. Privacy-by-design principles should be integrated throughout AI development, ensuring adherence to key concepts such as data minimization, encryption, de-identification and anonymization. In order to ensure the security and privacy of users’ information are properly protected, several technologies have been developed, including data encryption, access control and data anonymization [ ]. In addition, informed consent mechanisms should be strengthened, moving beyond perfunctory ‘agree’ buttons towards transparent disclosures that help users understand how their data will be used and the associated risks [ ].

## Conclusion
The application of AI in HNSCC immunotherapy is rapidly advancing, encompassing both algorithmic innovations and novel clinical use cases. In the coming years, a continuous increase in AI regulatory approvals and clinical trial outcomes will emerge, with applications extending beyond the current typical use of AI as a supportive tool for classification and prediction. Additionally, the emergence of large language models and multimodal data-driven applications is anticipated to further expand AI’s role in this field.
However, the future of AI in HNSCC immunotherapy will depend on more than just technological progress. Several critical questions remain that must be addressed to ensure the effective integration of AI into HNSCC immunotherapy. These issues must be collaboratively assessed by ethicists, clinicians, development teams and information technology specialists, as they serve a pivotal function in the development and application of AI models in this domain. Objective analysis of the predictive ability regarding patients’ responses and sensitivity to immunotherapy is crucial, alongside evaluating the impact of AI on clinically relevant metrics and outcomes throughout the course of immunotherapy. Additional investigation is needed to confirm the broad applicability of AI models using larger patient cohorts from multi-institutional studies. Evaluating AI’s applicability across diverse patient populations and clinical settings is also essential. Additionally, the cost-effectiveness of deploying AI solutions must be rigorously examined, particularly given the high costs associated with new, cutting-edge technologies. While AI is expected to improve clinical outcomes and reduce unnecessary immunotherapy use, its most significant impact may be in optimizing resource allocation and enhancing cost-effectiveness. Achieving these benefits will require adjustments in clinical workflows, improved access to technology and potential structural changes. Furthermore, there is a pressing need for clear, standardized processes for the clinical integration of AI, covering everything from technical standards to staff training.
As AI continues to mature in its application to HNSCC immunotherapy, it will transition from research and development to clinical implementation. This transition necessitates the establishment of objective monitoring processes to assess the ongoing influence of AI and ensure patient safety. The primary advantage of the data-driven AI approach lies in its ability to continually improve over time. To fully realize these benefits, robust regulatory frameworks and dedicated efforts to monitor AI systems over the long term will be required. Ultimately, addressing these considerations will shape the future of AI in HNSCC immunotherapy, ensuring its effective, equitable and sustainable use to improve patient outcomes.
