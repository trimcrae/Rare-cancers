Cancer care has witnessed significant advancements over the years, leading to increased cure rates and improved overall survival. This has been mainly due to the development of innovative and more effective treatment strategies, such as immunotherapy and targeted therapies. Nonetheless, patients are increasingly exposed to potential late treatment toxicities as they live longer. Simultaneously, increasing cancer drug prices present a global challenge to treatment accessibility. As health care systems worldwide struggle with the rising number of cancer cases, there is a growing need for more sustainable and patient-centred approaches to cancer treatment.

## Ultra-short therapy
Ultra-short therapy refers to an ideal treatment paradigm in which patients, highly selected on the basis of precise molecular and clinical risk, are treated with the minimum effective treatment strategy for the shortest effective time, with the intent of cure. Importantly, ultra-short therapy strategies seek to substantially reduce overall treatment burden (through reduced overall dose, duration and/or number of anticancer therapies required) whilst maximising therapeutic efficacy, through the identification of biomarkers that predict exquisite sensitivity to treatment to guide patient selection for ultra-short interventions. The ultra-short approach is dependent on the identification and validation of accurate predictive biomarkers; therefore, an emphasis on designing ultra-short strategies will drive necessary advancement of precision oncology. The ultimate goal of ultra-short therapies is to improve patient well-being through increased quality, as well as quantity, of survival, thereby reducing health care expenditure on unnecessary or ineffective treatments and their toxicities.

## Rationale for ultra-short therapy
In the current standard oncology drug development model, drug evaluation begins in the metastatic, often heavily pre-treated, setting. After demonstration of efficacy in the advanced setting, the drug will be evaluated in (neo)adjuvant trials for early-stage disease. In this phase, innovative or targeted treatments are often added to a backbone of the existing standard treatment regimen and directly compared to a standard of care in large, randomised registration trials.
This approach has a number of limitations. Firstly, biological factors that predict response to treatment, particularly to immunotherapeutics, in the metastatic setting can differ from the early-stage setting due to differences in tumour–host immune interactions. Lack of biological data in early-stage disease results in a ‘blunt’ application of novel therapies in the (neo)adjuvant setting, limiting rational patient selection. For example, programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) expression is used to select patients for immunotherapy in metastatic triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC), whereas no reliable, predictive biomarker has been established for identifying patients who will benefit from the addition of immune checkpoint inhibitors to standard neoadjuvant chemotherapy. , 
Secondly, the additive approach to combining therapies can result in the approval of novel agents as part of long, often intensive, standard chemotherapy regimens, without compelling biological rationale. Consider the same example of neoadjuvant treatment for TNBC, in which a year-long, five-drug regimen has been embraced as standard of care for almost all patients with early-stage TNBC (tumours >2 cm or node positive), despite a lack of comparative data demonstrating the optimum chemotherapy backbone for immunotherapy combinations in this setting, and several randomised and single-arm phase II trials demonstrating numerically similar results with less intensive chemotherapy and/or no adjuvant immunotherapy. , , , , , , 
Finally, an inability to define optimum duration of immune checkpoint inhibitors in the metastatic setting in many of the initial indications has led to the implementation of largely arbitrarily long durations of adjuvant immune checkpoint inhibitors. In the landmark trials that established immune checkpoint inhibitors as standard of care in metastatic melanoma, treatment according to protocol was until progression, death or unacceptable toxicity. However, at 6.5 years of follow-up, 77% of patients in the combination arm and 69% of patients in the nivolumab arm who were alive at follow-up remained off treatment and had never received any subsequent treatment. , We have thus learned that short durations of immune checkpoint inhibitors can induce durable antitumour immunity in certain patients. Despite this proof of concept, large randomised trials assessing the role of adjuvant checkpoint inhibitors in early-stage disease often implement an arbitrary duration of therapy, without comparing the relative benefit of longer or shorter durations, or omitting adjuvant treatment when response to neoadjuvant immune checkpoint inhibition is observed. The practice-changing PACIFIC trial assessed 12 months of consolidation durvalumab after chemoradiotherapy for non-small-cell lung cancer, although real-world data from PACIFIC-R have shown that over 50% of patients did not complete 12 months of therapy. Six months of checkpoint inhibition was tested in BTCRC LUN 16-081 in a similar (but much smaller) population, with numerically similar progression-free survival rates in the nivolumab arm as seen in the durvalumab arm of PACIFIC. , , 
These examples are not intended to dispute that current standard of care is based on best available evidence, but to highlight the absence of a concentrated approach to answering fundamental questions of optimum target, combination and durations of new agents in curative settings. Such shortcomings reveal that a traditional model of drug development and clinical trial design may not be fit for purpose in the era of immunotherapy and precision oncology in the curative setting. Long durations of treatment prescribed broadly to the high volume of patients with early-stage cancers in the absence of biomarkers to identify the small proportion of patients who will ultimately benefit is placing substantial financial distress on society worldwide. Costs are becoming a major societal issue, even for wealthier countries, and ongoing administration of lengthy adjuvant treatment regimens without compelling clinical and biological rationale will not be sustainable, particularly as the indications for immune checkpoint inhibitors continue to expand. Extended duration of treatment may result in increased side-effects, symptom burden and deterioration in health-related quality of life. This can be particularly important in the setting of immune-related toxicities, which are idiosyncratic and can be permanent and life-altering in nature.

## Opportunities for ultra-short therapy
Our growing understanding of the biological underpinnings of cancer as well as of intermediate endpoints [e.g. pathological complete response (pCR) in TNBC or human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER-2)-amplified breast cancer] or predictive/prognostic biomarkers like tumour-infiltrating lymphocytes and microsatellite instability (MSI) has opened the door to a new era of de-escalation opportunities, driven by an individual patient-tailored approach to treatment. A risk-guided modulation of systemic therapy can be broadly approached in two ways. Firstly, by using less intensive chemotherapy, immunotherapy and targeted therapy combinations in select populations (e.g. colorectal cancer patients with MSI), and secondly, by modifying the duration (and/or type) of adjuvant treatment according to demonstrated response (e.g. presence of pCR in HER-2-amplified breast cancer), to optimise the chance of long-term cure with a strategy proportionate to the response. They should progressively integrate validated tools for measuring quality of life, ensuring definitive evidence of patient improvement across these various domains. These assessments can be enhanced by leveraging digital technologies that are becoming increasingly available. Moreover, it is crucial to enhance clinical trial findings by integrating real-world evidence from patient registries or electronic health records. This approach is particularly valuable, at this moment, for gathering comprehensive data on quality of life and cost-effectiveness outcomes, providing a more holistic view of the treatment’s impact.

## Examples of successful ultra-short therapies
Recent examples of the successful implementation of the ultra-short concept can be found in the treatment of patients with MSI colorectal cancer and melanoma. The NICHE trial concept was built upon solid biologic and preclinical rationale and preliminary early-phase human trials that supported the feasibility of ultra-short neoadjuvant immunotherapy. In the NICHE-2 study, the therapeutic efficacy provided by this treatment model was further tested. The trial enrolled 107 patients with high-risk deficient mismatch repair (dMMR) colon cancer, treated with two neoadjuvant administrations of immunotherapy (nivolumab and ipilimumab). With only 4 weeks of treatment, unprecedented major pathological responses (MPRs) in 95% of patients with dMMR colon cancer, including 67% pCRs, were achieved with no disease recurrences reported. These results place an ultra-short neoadjuvant immunotherapy regimen, consisting of only two administrations, in a position to become the standard of care for patients with dMMR colon cancer.
In the PRADO trial, patients with high-risk stage III melanoma received two cycles of ipilimumab (1 mg/kg) and nivolumab (3 mg/kg) before surgery. Total lymph node dissection (TLND) and adjuvant therapy were omitted in patients with MPR, whilst those with partial or no pathological response underwent TLND and received adjuvant immunotherapy with or without adjuvant radiotherapy. This resulted in an impressive 24-month relapse-free survival and distant metastasis-free survival of 93% and 98%, respectively, as well as reduced morbidity and improved health care-related quality of life from the omission of surgery for patients with MPR. It is also worth highlighting that the reduced-intensity dose of ipilimumab/nivolumab (compared to the standard metastatic dose) was determined by the preceding OpACIN and OpACIN-neo trials, strategically designed to select the neoadjuvant regimen with highest pathological response rate without unacceptable toxicity. , This is an elegant example of patient-centred trial design that combines rational selection of treatment dose and duration, personalised response-directed treatment modulation and incorporation of quality-of-life outcomes. This approach could serve as a model for the design of trials evaluating ultra-short regimens.

## Challenges
In the era of precision oncology, it will not be possible to conduct a large, randomised phase III trial for every intervention for every molecular biomarker in every tumour type and stage. Additionally, there are problems with designing and conducting phase III randomised trials to demonstrate equivalence or non-inferiority between treatments. Options to address these challenges include ensuring real-time integration of biomarker evaluation into comparative trials, embracing alternative designs to demonstrate efficacy and the execution of affordable pragmatic trials with minimal data collection.
Non-inferiority trials that were conducted to shorten the duration of adjuvant trastuzumab for HER-2-amplified breast cancer highlight the statistical complexities of randomised comparative trials to demonstrate equivalent or non-inferior efficacy. Despite very large sample sizes and numerically similar long-term outcomes between treatment arms in both studies, the PHARE and ShortHER trials both failed to statistically demonstrate non-inferiority of 6 months (PHARE) or 9 weeks (ShortHER) of trastuzumab compared to the standard 12 months, limiting the application of shorter durations of trastuzumab to resource-limited settings and select low-risk patients. , , , On the contrary, excellent long-term survival outcomes in the single-arm phase II APT trial, evaluating 12 weeks of single-agent chemotherapy with the standard year of trastuzumab, have led to an adoption of this reduced-intensity strategy for select patients. , This demonstrates that non-randomised trials can change practice in the appropriate context: where there is established safety and activity of the investigational agents, a clear and biologically proven strategy to guide patient selection and an accepted benchmark for success, as well as long-term survival follow-up.
Safe de-intensification relies on accurate identification of prognostic and predictive biomarkers. In this setting, use of intermediate endpoints such as pCR is particularly useful as it allows real-time identification of responders and non-responders. Several ongoing trials are testing different ultra-short immuno-oncology strategies with a focus on translational endpoints to accurately identify biomarkers. In the context of breast cancer, POP-DURVA (NCT05215106) and the BELLINI trial, both in early-stage TNBC, are testing a short duration of immunotherapy in the preoperative setting. The key to these trials is combining a primary clinical objective with diverse translational studies involving extensive biological specimen collection, with the aim of uncovering biomarkers of exceptional sensitivity to immunotherapy in which an ultra-short course of therapy can be applied. In order to achieve this, both trials are enriched in biological specimen collection, with a diverse and extensive biologic and imaging analysis, carried out in a longitudinal manner throughout the treatment phase.
Integrating biomarker evaluation and comparative clinical trial design will improve efficiency in selecting or rejecting potential biomarker-directed approaches for further evaluation. The I-SPY2 trial is an example of a multi-arm platform trial utilising adaptive Bayesian randomisation to test multiple experimental arms against a standard control arm with pCR as the primary endpoint, such that experimental arms that increase the pCR rate in a particular breast cancer subtype will be preferentially allocated patients with that subtype of breast cancer, in order to more rapidly demonstrate efficacy. When an experimental arm reaches a pre-defined predicted probability of success in a 300-patient comparative trial, the therapy ‘graduates’ from the trial, forming the rationale for a larger confirmatory trial. Over 10 years, I-SPY2 has evaluated 25 new agents or combinations in 2500 patients and collected messenger RNA, protein and response data from 990 patients to further refine biomarker selection. As the experimental arms in I-SPY2 are novel treatments or combinations added to standard chemotherapy, this study does not specifically address ultra-short therapy, but serves as an example of trial design that allows rapid testing of multiple biomarker-driven strategies simultaneously and sequentially, allowing investigators to rapidly select, reject or refine strategies for further evaluation.

## Patient perspective
Patient advocacy groups across Europe have expressed a need for a more tailored, individualised approach to therapy, in order to reduce treatment burden and improve accessibility, in keeping with the philosophy of ultra-short therapy (European Cancer Patient Coalition, Cancer Drug Development Forum and European Federation of Pharmaceutical Industries and Associations Joint Position Paper on Personalised Oncology). Measurement of endpoints that capture patient preferences will be important to demonstrate the extent to which ultra-short therapies meet this aim. The I-SPY2 trial is lauded as a successful example of including patient advocates in trial development and conduct, early and often. Patient advocates have been instrumental in several of the success of I-SPY2, including a focus on clinical development specific to the early-stage setting (rather than first demonstrating efficacy in the metastatic setting), accelerated approvals on the basis of pCR in order to improve timeliness of access to effective therapies and advocacy for a response-adapted design to maximise the chance of being allocated to an effective intervention within the framework of a randomised trial. Each of these features is an example of methods that could be implemented in the development of ultra-short therapies and therefore illustrates the crucial role of patient advocacy in this arena.

## Future directions

## Circulating tumour DNA as a biomarker
In instances where a single biomarker remains elusive, emerging technologies may assist. For example, circulating tumour DNA (ctDNA) testing could help to identify patients who need more (ctDNA detected) or less (ctDNA not detected) intensive therapy. The DYNAMIC-II trial showed that a ctDNA-guided approach to adjuvant chemotherapy in stage II colorectal cancer resulted in significantly less chemotherapy with non-inferior 3-year recurrence-free survival outcomes, compared to a non-ctDNA guide to treatment. The role of ctDNA in selecting or refining (neo)adjuvant therapies needs to be validated in other cancer types, and availability and expertise in ctDNA testing will need to expand before this approach can be implemented in routine clinical care.

## Personalised cancer vaccines
Returning to immuno-oncology strategies, personalised cancer vaccines (PCVs) could enhance induction of long-term antitumour immunity at the same time as reducing treatment duration. Indeed, translating the concept of a vaccine from infectious disease to curing cancer embodies the goal of ultra-short treatment—to confer maximum long-term immunity with minimum doses. PCVs have recently shown impressive recurrence-free and distant metastasis-free survival results in the adjuvant treatment of resected stage IIIB-IV melanoma in the KEYNOTE-942 trial. Here, nine doses of a PCV was added to the standard 1-year of adjuvant pembrolizumab, compared to standard treatment alone. This trial did not evaluate whether the immunity induced by the PCV could shorten the overall adjuvant treatment duration (patients in both arms received 12 months of adjuvant pembrolizumab), which is now a rational question that must be addressed if PCVs are to realise their promise of long-term immunity that obviates the need for year-long adjuvant anti-PD-L1.

## Artificial intelligence
Artificial intelligence is a rapidly evolving area of medical research and development. Deep machine learning models have already shown the ability to accurately predict breast cancer relapse based on simple inputs such as digitalised pathology slides. Deep learning models that integrate complex multi-omic data of tumour cells and the microenvironment to predict treatment response are in development. Use and integration of such technologies into platforms to investigate ultra-short therapies could accelerate patient selection of such strategies.

## Health system implications
We can anticipate a positive impact of ultra-short strategies on health-related costs through a variety of levers. Faster social reintegration can be achieved by quicker completion of treatment. Mitigation of treatment-related side-effects that may hinder social participation and engagement, and less psychological stress and anxiety associated with a shorter treatment process may improve patients’ motivation and confidence in engaging with their social networks. One of the identified barriers to return to work is related to the length and nature of treatment regimens. Return to work has been shown to be detrimentally impacted by longer treatment durations and chemotherapy-containing regimens. Ultra-short treatment strategies hold the potential to expedite the return to work and care-giving, reducing health-related costs on a broader scale. A major benefit of ultra-short therapy strategies will be a reduction in the direct and indirect costs of more effective cancer therapies, rendering these treatments more accessible and affordable, ensuring wider availability, especially for those who stand to derive the greatest benefit.
Prioritisation of translational endpoints in clinical trials to streamline patient selection and drive more effective, rational and responsible treatment combinations will ultimately lead to public health solutions. We urge policy-makers and funding bodies to increase translational research spending and, in turn, we urge investigators and trial co-operatives to systematically implement patient-centred trial design.

## Global applications
The global application of ultra-short therapy has the potential to make cancer treatment more accessible and efficient across the world. However, realising this potential requires a concerted effort in several key areas.
Firstly, understanding the unique health care challenges, resource limitations and patient demographics of each region is crucial. This includes being sensitive to cultural differences and adapting treatments to align with local beliefs and practices, thereby enhancing acceptance and adherence.
To bridge the gap between these innovative treatment strategies and patient needs, leveraging technology is essential. Digital and mobile health tools can significantly improve patient monitoring, education and communication between health care providers and patients. Telehealth services are particularly valuable in providing access to specialists and consultations in remote or resource-limited areas.
Within local communities, training and education are critical components. Empowering community members and spreading health information, along with training local health care providers in new treatments and technologies, foster self-sufficiency and ensure the sustainability of these health care initiatives.
Global partnerships and participatory research are also vital. Collaborating across borders ensures that strategies are not only appropriate but also effective for the target population.
Cost considerations are equally important. Using generic drugs and innovating cost-effective technologies and treatments can make care more affordable, particularly in resource-scarce settings.
Infrastructure development and policy supportive actions are the backbones of implementing these strategies. Investing in essential health care infrastructure, such as reliable power and internet access, is fundamental.
With these strategic efforts, ultra-short therapy can be adapted and optimised for various settings, paving the way for a future where quality cancer care is a reality for all.

## Conclusion
In conclusion, the opportunity to develop ultra-short treatments for the benefit of individual patients and the broader population is becoming increasingly apparent, with successful results already reported. Ultra-short treatment regimens constitute a paradigm shift away from the current maximalist ‘kitchen sink’ practice which does not always result in a favourable number needed to treat/number needed to harm ratio. Instead, a more intelligent, efficient and targeted implementation of novel therapies is required to realise their full potential.
The path forward presents multiple challenges. Ultra-short therapies are unlikely to be a panacea for all patients and an adaptive, individualised approach is essential. A continuous cycle of biomarker prioritisation and validation, and open access pathways to guide investigation and use of biomarkers will be required. Dedicated effort to innovate clinical trial design and serious investment in translational research are needed to uncover opportunities to implement ultra-short therapies.
