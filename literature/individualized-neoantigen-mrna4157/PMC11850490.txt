However, the variability in patient responses underscores the need for reliable biomarkers to guide treatment decisions. This article reviews key biomarkers in melanoma immunotherapy, such as PD-L1 expression, tumor mutational burden (TMB), and gene expression profiles (GEPs). It also explores emerging biomarkers, including LAG-3 expression, immune cell phenotyping in tissue and blood, gut microbiota, and circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA). ## Key Points

 

## Introduction
Melanoma is one of the most lethal forms of skin cancer owing to its high metastatic potential. A small proportion of metastatic melanoma cases do not present an identifiable primary tumor and are therefore classified as melanoma of unknown primary (MUP) [ ]. Interestingly, MUPs are associated with better overall survival, even when treated with immunotherapy, compared with melanomas with an identified primary site [ , ]. This enhanced survival is likely attributed to a higher immunogenicity of MUPs. Thus, the current standard of care should also be applied to MUPs. Recent breakthroughs in immunotherapy, especially with immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) targeting CTLA-4, PD-1/PD-L1, and LAG-3 pathways, have revolutionized treatment [ ]. The 10-year melanoma-specific survival rate for patients treated with anti-PD-1 plus anti-CTLA-4 is 52%, indicating that half of the patients did not die of melanoma a decade after treatment for metastatic melanoma [ ]. For those treated with anti-PD-1 alone, the 10-year melanoma-specific survival rate is 44% [ ]. Anti-LAG-3 therapy offers additional benefits when combined with anti-PD-1 therapy. At 12 months, progression-free survival was 47.7% with relatlimab-nivolumab, compared with 36.0% with nivolumab alone [ ]. These drugs work by blocking inhibitory signals that prevent T cells from attacking tumor cells, thereby enhancing the antitumor immune response [ ]. While these therapies have led to long-term survival benefits in some patients, not all respond, underscoring the need for predictive biomarkers to personalize and optimize treatment. In addition to ICIs, adoptive T cell therapy (ACT), cancer vaccines, and T cell engaging bispecific antibodies have been explored for melanoma treatment [ ]. ACT involves the expansion of tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs) or engineered T cells specific to melanoma antigens, while vaccines aim to prime the immune system against tumor-associated antigens. T cell engaging bispecific molecules, such as tebentafusp or brenetafusp, are also called immune mobilizing monoclonal TCRs against cancer (ImmTAC) because they enforce binding of T cells to tumor cells resulting in tumor cell death [ , ]. Biomarkers emerging from these new melanoma immunotherapies are included. Taken together, this review aims to summarize the latest research on melanoma immunotherapy biomarkers, discussing validated and emerging markers (Table ). 

## Tissue-Based Biomarkers in Melanoma Immunotherapy
A wide range of biomarkers are being evaluated directly within the tumor tissue, providing valuable insights into the tumor microenvironment (TME). This method requires an adequate tissue sample, which is obtained from the primary tumor, lymph node metastases, or distant metastases.

## Expression of Immune Checkpoint Molecules
Programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) has been extensively studied as a biomarker in melanoma immunotherapy, particularly in relation to the efficacy of immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) targeting the PD-1/PD-L1 pathway. PD-L1 is expressed on tumor cells and immune cells within the tumor microenvironment, and its interaction with PD-1 on T cells leads to immune suppression and allows tumor cells to evade immune detection [ ]. Higher levels of PD-L1 expression are generally associated with improved responses to PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors, such as pembrolizumab and nivolumab, as these therapies work by blocking the inhibitory signaling and reactivating T cell-mediated tumor destruction [ ]. Combination ICI (nivolumab + ipilimumab or relatlimab) resulted in improved PFS only in patients with PD-L1 < 1% [ , ].PD-L1 expression in lymph node metastases correlated better with outcomes in patients with melanoma treated with ICIs than PD-L1 expression in primary tumors or distant metastases [ ]. This underscores the significance of tumor tissue type in the clinical utility of PD-L1 as a biomarker. PD-L1 is not a perfect biomarker. The variability in PD-L1 measurement may be attributed to tumor heterogeneity, differences in testing methodologies, and dynamic changes in PD-L1 expression during treatment [ ]. RNA expression levels of CTLA-4 combined with PD-L1 have been identified as significant biomarkers for predicting PFS and OS in patients with melanoma undergoing immunotherapy [ ]. The synergistic high expression of these markers suggests their potential utility in stratifying patients for more effective immunotherapy regimens. Lymphocyte activation gene-3 (LAG-3) is an emerging immune checkpoint molecule that has gained considerable attention as a therapeutic target in cancer immunotherapy [ ]. Similar to PD-1 and CTLA-4, LAG-3 modulates T cell function, limiting immune responses and facilitating immune evasion by tumors. LAG-3 expression correlated with occurrence of tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs) in the tumor microenvironment and with longer PFS compared with patients with < 1% LAG-3 expression [ ]. Co-expression of LAG-3 with PD-1 was observed in exhausted CD8+T cells with less IFN-γ production, suggesting a possible therapeutic synergy between PD-1 and LAG-3 inhibition [ ]. By combining LAG-3-targeted therapies with other immune checkpoint inhibitors, clinicians aim to improve clinical outcomes, and ongoing studies are striving to identify and validate robust biomarkers to better personalize these immunotherapies and avoid toxicity. MicroRNAs (miRNAs) have been shown to play a role in the progression of melanoma. Elevated levels of miRNA-221 have been observed in early-stage melanomas compared with healthy individuals, with higher levels correlating with more advanced stages of the disease [ ]. Additionally, circulating miRNA-615-3p levels have proven to be consistently effective in identifying patients with melanoma who experience disease progression while undergoing treatment with immune checkpoint inhibitors [ ].

## Tumor Mutational Burden (TMB)
TMB refers to the total number of somatic mutations present within a tumor’s genome and has emerged as a significant biomarker for predicting the response to immunotherapy, particularly in melanoma [ ]. It is determined by the number of nonsynonymous exonic mutations per megabase (Mb) [ ]. High TMB is thought to enhance the likelihood of generating neoantigens, which could potentially be recognized by ICI-reinvigorated cytotoxic T cells [ ]. In melanoma, which often exhibits a high mutational load owing to ultraviolet (UV) radiation exposure, a higher TMB has been associated with better responses to ICI, such as PD-1/PD-L1 and CTLA-4 inhibitors [ ]. In the SECOMBIT trial, TMB-high patients (≥ 10 mut/Mb) also had better outcomes when treated with BRAF/MEKi, either before or after ICI therapy [ ]. This correlation suggests that tumors with higher mutation rates are more likely to be recognized and targeted by the reactivated immune system during immunotherapy. TMB is useful for estimating the number of neoantigens [ ]. Similar to melanoma, nonmelanoma skin cancers (NMSCs), such as basal cell carcinomas (BCCs) or cutaneous squamous cell carcinomas (cSCCs). are typically associated with UV damage, which leads to high TMB [ ], and a potentially good response to ICI in these skin tumors. However, considering additional genetic factors could enhance predictions of ICI response. For instance, unlike TMB, insertions/deletions or neoantigens specific to mutations have been linked to T cell recruitment and better ICI response in melanoma [ ]. To improve prediction accuracy, TMB can be combined with other biomarkers, such as gene expression profiles or an IFN-γ signature [ ].

## Gene Expression Profiles (GEPs) and Immunohistochemistry (IHC) Signatures
GEPs and IHC signatures have emerged as critical tools in predicting responses to immunotherapy [ – ]. By examining the activity of multiple genes or proteins simultaneously, these scores offer a more comprehensive view of the tumor microenvironment and immune activity, providing valuable insights into treatment responses and resistance mechanisms. In addition, a GEP signature with 28 genes was able to predict the risk of metastasis in a cohort of primary cutaneous melanoma tumors [ ]. According to Cox regression analysis, this GEP, the American Joint Committee on Cancer stage, Breslow thickness, ulceration, and age were identified as independent predictors of metastatic risk [ ]. In melanoma, these tests are increasingly being utilized to stratify patients for immunotherapy and to develop personalized treatment strategies. One of the most well-studied signatures is the interferon-gamma-related gene signature, which includes genes such as IFNG, CXCL9, CXCL10, GZMA, PRF1, and STAT1 [ ]. These genes are involved in promoting T cell-mediated immune responses and have been linked to better outcomes with ICIs. The signature can identify immune-inflamed or “hot” tumors, which have a high presence of tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs) and are more likely to respond to immunotherapy [ ]. CXCL10 in particular has also been shown to be a strong prognostic factor for response to immunotherapy in metastatic melanoma [ ]. Simultaneously, CXCR3 ligands (CXCL9/CXCL10) seem to be involved in the immunopathology of irAEs [ ]. GEPs are being incorporated into clinical trials and treatment decision-making in melanoma [ ]. For example, patients with a high expression of an interferon-gamma-related-gene signature may be prioritized for neoadjuvant therapy with monotherapy anti-PD-1 [ ]. However, patients with low or absent immune activity, as indicated by their IFN-γ-score, may be candidates for combination therapies. Moreover, signatures are being explored as a tool for monitoring treatment responses and detecting resistance early [ ], allowing for a more personalized and adaptive treatment strategy. In addition to the immune phenotype of the TME, one can also stratify tumor-intrinsic proteins with IHC signatures, such as “Immunoprint,” which comprises a seven-marker signature (Bax, Bcl-X, PTEN, COX-2, ß-Catenin, MTAP, and CD20) [ ]. This IHC signature can help identify patients at risk in melanoma stage I–III as well as patients with very late metastases [ , ]. PTEN is a tumor suppressor gene that negatively regulates the PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling pathway, which is crucial for cell growth and survival [ ]. Loss of PTEN function is implicated in some melanomas, making PTEN an interesting target for therapeutic development for a subset of patients [ ]. Another IHC-based approached would be staining for TILs [ , ]. In addition, combination approaches are being explored. A signature combining proteomics and transcriptomics was associated with poorer responses to ICI immunotherapy in patients with metastatic melanoma [ ]. In this study, the optimal protein signature linked to response to anti-PD-1 treatment consisted of 14 proteins: ZNF384, UAP1L1, TRIM32, TLK1, SYTL2, SIRPA, RANBP10, NAGA, MYOIE, LAMTOR4, GM2A, FAM120A, DAPK1, and CLN6 [ ].

## Immune Cells, Primarily Tumor-Infiltrating Lymphocytes (TILs)
TILs are immune cells, primarily T cells, that have migrated from the bloodstream into the tumor microenvironment, and their presence has become an important biomarker in melanoma immunotherapy [ ]. High levels of TILs in melanoma tumors are often associated with a more favorable prognosis and enhanced responses to immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs), such as PD-1 [ ]. Automated quantification of CD8+ TILs on whole slide images can help to make this approach more feasible for the clinic [ ]. Lymphocytes indicate an active antitumor immune response, suggesting that the immune system is recognizing and attempting to fight the tumor. Additionally, TILs form the foundation of adoptive T cell therapy, a personalized immunotherapy approach where TILs are harvested from a patient’s tumor, expanded in the lab, and reinfused to boost the antitumor response [ ]. As a biomarker, TILs offer insight into the immune landscape of melanoma and play a critical role in both prognostication and the development of personalized immunotherapy strategies. Apart from circulatory effector CD8+ T cells, tissue resident memory T cells have also been described as being prognostically relevant in the TME of melanoma [ ]. They can function as biomarkers for immunotherapy and occurrence of immune-related adverse events (irAE), specifically of the skin and gastrointestinal tract [ ]. In contrast to cytotoxic T cells that execute effector functions following ICI, the tumor microenvironment also harbors immunosuppressive cells, such as myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs), T regulatory (Treg) cells, and tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) [ , ]. These cells release reactive oxygen species (ROS) and other factors that effectively dampen T cell activation [ ]. Additionally, epithelial cells can produce interleukin-6, which promotes an M2 macrophage phenotype [ , ]. Metalloproteinases secreted by fibroblasts, along with melanoma-derived exosomes, play a significant role in impairing natural killer (NK) cell responses [ ].

## Antigen Presentation via Human Leukocyte Antigen (HLA)-Molecules
Antigen presentation involves HLA complexes. Resistance to ICI often results from reduced or lost HLA class-I molecules. Melanoma cells lacking HLA class-I expression were resistant to anti-CTLA-4 but not to anti-PD-1 [ ]. HLA-DR expression has emerged as a potential biomarker of response to ICIs. Studies have shown that assessing HLA-DR, as part of the major histocompatibility complex (MHC) II, could unravel resistance mechanisms, helping to identify patients who may not respond or relapse post-ICI therapy [ ]. Higher melanoma-specific HLA class-II expression was linked to better responses and longer survival in patients treated with anti-PD-1 [ ]. However, the interaction between HLA class-II molecules on melanoma cells and the LAG-3 ligand expressed on infiltrating T cells may activate an immune escape mechanism, arguing for this additional checkpoint-blockade [ ]. The importance of HLA class-I heterozygosity and its interaction with tumor mutational burden (TMB) for predicting survival has also been highlighted [ ]. The HLA-A*03 allele was associated with poorer outcomes, though the mechanism is unclear [ ]. While HLA remains a key predictive factor, further research is needed to better understand its role and refine evaluation methods.

## Blood-Based Biomarkers in Melanoma Immunotherapy
Blood-based biomarkers are emerging as a vital tool in the management of melanoma, offering a noninvasive alternative to tissue biopsies. These biomarkers, found in blood samples, can provide real-time insights into tumor dynamics, helping to monitor disease progression, predict treatment responses, and detect early signs of recurrence. Increases of routine serum parameters, such as lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) or C-reactive protein (CRP), have been found to indicate melanoma progression [ , ]. LDH proved to be the most effective biomarker for classifying patients with melanoma on the basis of OS into three distinct groups [ ]. In addition to low baseline LDH, better survival was significantly associated with factors, such as absolute monocyte counts (AMC), frequencies of Lin−CD14+HLA-DR−/low-MDSCs, high absolute eosinophil counts (AEC), relative lymphocyte counts (RLC), and frequencies of CD4+CD25+FoxP3+ Tregs [ ]. A transcriptomic analysis indicated that patients with melanoma with a high baseline neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR) displayed a gene signature featuring CCNA1, LDHA, and IL18R1, and experienced poorer outcomes [ ]. As research in this area grows, blood-based biomarkers are becoming increasingly important and promising novel markers emerge.

## Circulating Tumor DNA (ctDNA)
CtDNA has emerged as a promising biomarker in uveal melanoma, offering a noninvasive means of monitoring tumor dynamics and response to treatment [ ]. In the phase 2 KEYNOTE-942 trial, the combination of mRNA-4157/V940 and pembrolizumab improved recurrence-free survival (RFS) in resected high-risk stage III/IV cutaneous melanoma, with significantly longer RFS in ctDNA-negative patients compared with ctDNA-positive patients across all study arms [ ]. In uveal melanoma, where traditional biomarkers and imaging techniques often fall short in early detection and monitoring, ctDNA holds particular promise [ ]. Owing to the rarity of uveal melanoma and the difficulty in obtaining tumor tissue samples, ctDNA provides a less invasive option for monitoring disease status. Early reduction of ctDNA can also predict overall survival and stable disease in patients with uveal melanoma treated with tebentafusp [ ]. In cutaneous melanoma, ctDNA can be detected in the bloodstream, allowing for real-time tracking of tumor burden, minimal residual disease, and treatment efficacy, particularly in patients undergoing immunotherapy [ , ]. Studies have shown that fluctuations in ctDNA levels often correlate with treatment response, with declining levels indicating effective therapy and rising levels signaling potential progression or resistance [ ]. The use of ctDNA as a biomarker in both types of melanoma is rapidly advancing, with ongoing research exploring its potential for improving early detection, tracking minimal residual disease, and personalizing treatment strategies in both cutaneous and uveal melanoma. In clinical trials of T cell engaging molecules targeting gp100 or PRAME in cutaneous melanoma, it is used as monitoring biomarker [ ]. For example, ctDNA levels can be measured to assess the reduction in tumor burden. A significant decrease in ctDNA during therapy could indicate a positive response, while stable or rising ctDNA levels may suggest resistance or disease progression, allowing clinicians to adjust the therapeutic strategy accordingly. Moreover, ctDNA analysis can reveal emerging mutations in melanoma cells that may contribute to immune escape mechanisms [ ]. Additionally, ctDNA can be used to assess minimal residual disease (MRD) [ ]. Even when imaging techniques show no visible tumors, residual melanoma cells may persist and lead to relapse.

## Immune Cells, Checkpoint Molecules
Analyzing peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) in blood samples allows for the assessment of immune cells, paving the way for less invasive biomarker research. Baseline levels of CD45RO8/CD8+ T cells and CD8+ effector memory T cells are predictive of response to immunotherapy in metastatic melanoma, whereas a higher abundance of PD-1+CD56+ T cells is associated with shorter PFS and OS [ ]. Additionally, within the myeloid compartment, the frequency of classical CD14+CD16−CD33+HLA−DRhi monocytes may act as a potential predictor for the effectiveness of PD-1 blockade immunotherapy [ ]. PD-1 expression on CD8+ T cells just before treatment was higher in responders compared with nonresponders [ ]. Responders also demonstrated greater T cell responsiveness following ex vivo T cell receptor stimulation, as indicated by increased PD-1 expression on CD3+ T cells [ ]. Additionally, the percentage of CD8+ effector memory (CD8+CD45RA−CD45RO+CCR7−) T cells was higher in responders than in nonresponders [ ]. Additionally, a higher ratio of PD-1+Ki67+CD8+ (reinvigorated) T cells to tumor burden was linked to a better clinical response, suggesting that T cell reinvigoration could serve as a marker of improved treatment responsiveness. The authors identified CD38 and HLA-DR, which showed increased expression on CD4+ T cells during treatment, as potential cell surface surrogate markers for Ki67+ cells [ ]. Patients treated with anti-PD-1 blockade and increased infiltration with LAG-3+ T cells in the TME demonstrated a shorter PFS, reinforcing the potential benefit of adding anti-LAG-3 therapy to existing ICI regimens [ ]. Interestingly, progressing melanoma during anti-PD-1 treatment was associated with high pretreatment serum levels of soluble LAG-3 (sLAG-3) but not sPD-1, sPDL-1, or sTIM-3 [ ]. Thus, sLAG-3 detected in peripheral blood may serve as a predictive biomarker for treatment response.

## Cytokines and Chemokines
Higher baseline concentrations of IL-6 were associated with shorter survival in patients with melanoma and cSCC receiving ICIs, suggesting a potential synergistic role for IL6 receptor blockade in combination with ICIs [ , ]. Prophylactic tocilizumab has been shown to be a well-tolerated and an effective treatment for managing ICI-induced toxicities and preventing flare-ups of preexisting autoimmune disorders [ ]. Prospective trials are needed to determine if a combination approach with ICI can achieve better response rates. For effective tumor killing, cytotoxic T cells need to enter the TME, arguing for a key role of chemokines in recruiting these cells [ ]. For instance, CXCL9/10/11 were upregulated in peripheral blood following anti-PD-1 treatment [ ]. These CXCR3 ligands bind to their cognate receptor on T cells and thereby attract them from the circulation to the TME [ ]. In summary, blood-based assays can provide valuable insights into the disease status of patients with melanoma, but may need to be supplemented with additional biomarkers for a more comprehensive assessment.

## Other Biomarkers in Melanoma Immunotherapy
Beyond tissue and blood biomarkers, other types of biomarkers are increasingly being explored in melanoma immunotherapy. Sex, age, body mass index, and concomitant medication have been investigated and related to melanoma immunotherapy outcome [ – ]. Novel alternative biomarker approaches, such as microbiota and radiomics, might hold the potential to further enhance personalized treatment strategies and patient monitoring.

## Gut Microbiota
The gut microbiome has been shown to impact responses to immunotherapy [ ]. Research into specific microbial profiles associated with immunotherapy responses has yielded varying results. In metastatic melanoma, various bacteria have been identified that correlated with response to immunotherapy. In one study Ruminococcaceae, Clostridiales, and Faecalibacterium were enriched in responders, while nonresponders had a predominance of Bacteroidales [ ]. Another group described that Bifidobacterium spp. improved anti-PD-L1 therapy [ ]. Whereas, antitumor effects of CTLA-4 blockade were dependent on distinct Bacteroides species [ ]. A more recent study found that response rates were higher in Ruminococcaceae-dominated microbiomes compared with those dominated by Bacteroidaceae [ ]. Despite these insights, there is no clear consensus on the specific bacteria associated with ICI responses. The role of the gut microbiome is likely more complex, and its effects may not depend on a single species. Various microbiota compositions and microbial products might produce similar immunomodulatory effects. For example, microbiota species involved in peptidoglycan remodeling and muropeptide-based therapeutics have been shown to enhance immunotherapy efficacy [ ]. Additionally, factors, such as sex, age, geography, medications, diet, and nutritional status, can all influence the microbiota. This field remains underexplored, but understanding it is crucial for advancing noninvasive biomarker assessments and developing therapeutic strategies, such as fecal transplantation or dietary modifications. Recent studies have demonstrated that fecal microbiota transplantation from responders can modify the gut microbiome and reprogram the tumor microenvironment, potentially overcoming resistance to anti-PD-1 therapy [ , ]. In addition, a positive association between a Mediterranean diet and response to treatment with ICB has been described [ ]. Research on the cutaneous microbiome of patients and ICI response are still lacking.

## Radiomic Biomarkers
Radiomic biomarkers have gained attention as promising tools for predicting and monitoring responses to immunotherapy in melanoma. By extracting quantitative data from standard imaging modalities, such as computed tomography (CT), magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), and positron emission tomography (PET), radiomics captures tumor heterogeneity, microenvironment characteristics, and immune-related changes. A study showed that changes in radiomic features over time correlated with best overall response (BOR), progression-free survival (PFS), and overall survival (OS) in patients with melanoma [ ]. Baseline parameters from 18F-fluorodeoxyglucose positron emission tomography-computed tomography (18F-FDG-PET/CT) scans, such as tumor burden and glucose uptake, have been evaluated for their predictive value in melanoma immunotherapy [ ]. Although these parameters did not significantly enhance PFS and BOR predictions, mean FDG uptake by the spleen showed potential in predicting OS [ ]. This indicates a nuanced potential for specific PET/CT-derived biomarkers in certain clinical contexts. These findings advocate for the integration of radiomic biomarkers into clinical protocols to refine treatment strategies and improve outcomes. Although still in the research phase, radiomic biomarkers hold the potential to refine patient selection, guide therapy adjustments, and complement traditional biomarkers in melanoma immunotherapy. Further validation through large-scale studies is needed to integrate these biomarkers into clinical practice, optimizing personalized treatment

## Challenges and Future Directions
Despite promising results, the clinical utility of these biomarkers is still evolving. One of the key challenges in biomarker development is the variability in testing methods and interpretation. For example, different assays are used to measure PD-L1 expression, leading to inconsistent results. Harmonizing these methodologies will be essential for ensuring that biomarkers can be reliably used in clinical practice. Given the complexity of the tumor microenvironment, single biomarkers often fail to capture the full spectrum of immune interactions. Biomarkers from the TME could be augmented with blood-based biomarkers or other environmental factors, such as microbiome composition. A multibiomarker approach, incorporating various molecular, genetic, and immune markers, is likely to provide more accurate predictions of treatment response (Fig. ). 

## Conclusions
Melanoma, an aggressive skin cancer, has long posed challenges for treatment owing to its resistance to traditional therapies. However, the introduction of immunotherapy, particularly ICI, has revolutionized melanoma treatment, leading to remarkable clinical responses. Despite these advancements, not all patients benefit from ICIs, highlighting the urgent need for predictive biomarkers to tailor treatments for better outcomes. As the range of immunotherapies, such as ICIs, TIL, and ImmTACs, expands, alongside innovative treatment approaches, such as neoadjuvant therapy, biomarkers will become even more essential for personalizing care. Recent research has explored various biomarkers, including radiomic, genetic, and immunological markers, with significant progress made in identifying their potential in melanoma immunotherapy. Established markers, such as PD-L1, TMB, and GEPs, remain crucial, while emerging biomarkers, such as ctDNA, and the gut microbiome provide promising new avenues for personalized treatment strategies. In the context of patients with stage III melanoma undergoing neoadjuvant therapy, the current standard marker used to evaluate treatment efficacy is pathological response. Future research should prioritize refining existing biomarkers, standardizing methodologies, and developing multibiomarker approaches to further optimize immunotherapy outcomes. As neoadjuvant ICIs and other immunotherapies gain prominence, biomarkers that help select and monitor patients in these settings will be critical. The growing role of biomarkers in melanoma immunotherapy reflects their increasing importance in predicting patient outcomes, guiding clinical decisions, and improving therapeutic efficacy. The development and integration of these biomarkers will be essential as we move toward more personalized and effective treatment strategies for patients with melanoma.
