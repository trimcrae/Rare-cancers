## INTRODUCTION
Immunotherapies have reshaped the landscape of cancer treatment, demonstrating that immune activation can induce profound and durable tumor regression even in advanced malignancies ( ). Platforms such as immune checkpoint blockade (ICB), adoptive cell transfer (ACT), engineered T-cell therapies including chimeric Ag receptor (CAR)-T and TCR-T cells, and neoantigen/mRNA vaccination have validated the curative potential of the immune system ( ). Yet, despite these transformative successes, durable responses remain confined to a minority of patients. Most individuals experience primary refractoriness or adaptive resistance after transient benefit, underscoring a critical barrier to the broader success of these fundamentally T cell-centered therapies ( ).
Historically, resistance has been explained through an immune-centric framework centered on deficits in T-cell recognition, infiltration, and effector function within the tumor microenvironment (TME) ( ). Defective Ag presentation through loss of MHC molecules ( ), physical exclusion arising from dense extracellular matrix or abnormal vasculature ( ), and functional exhaustion driven by metabolic stress and chronic Ag exposure ( ) have all been recognized as major obstacles. Furthermore, the accumulation of immunosuppressive subsets, including myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs), Tregs, and M2-like macrophages, reinforces a suppressive milieu that restricts cytotoxic T-cell activity and broadly impairs anti-tumor immune responses by suppressing Ag presentation, NK cell function, and dendritic cell (DC) maturation ( ). These insights have led to an expanding array of immunomodulatory interventions designed to restore T-cell priming, enhance trafficking, or reverse exhaustion. However, even when these immune defects are partially alleviated, clinical benefit remains limited, suggesting that immune-centric models are insufficient on their own to explain the depth and durability of therapeutic resistance.
These limitations show that immune dysfunction in cancer cannot always be explained solely by impaired immune activation, but may instead reflect constraints imposed by tumor cell-intrinsic programs. Conventionally, the poor response of epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR)-mutant non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) or Kirsten rat sarcoma viral oncogene homologue (KRAS)-driven adenocarcinomas to ICB was interpreted through an immune-centric framework as a simple lack of immunogenicity or T-cell infiltration ( ). However, this interpretation has proven insufficient. Increasing evidence indicates that resistance in these tumors is preconfigured by intrinsic oncogenic programs rather than imposed solely by immune failure. In EGFR-mutant NSCLC, constitutive signaling does not merely fail to attract T cells but actively suppresses the Ag-presentation machinery, enforcing a T-cell-excluded TME ( ). Similarly, KRAS mutations act as master regulators that orchestrate the recruitment of MDSCs and the remodeling of a dense stroma to neutralize potential immune attacks ( ). Together, these examples underscore that immune refractoriness is not an accidental deficiency of the immune system, but a deliberate output of the tumor’s own regulatory architecture, shifting the focus toward upstream drivers within malignant cells.
Emerging evidence now reveals that a deeper, more persistent layer of refractoriness originates within tumor cells themselves ( ). Pre-existing oncogenic mutations and chromosomal alterations, such as those affecting KRAS, V-raf murine sarcoma viral oncogene homolog B (BRAF), EGFR, myelocytomatosis oncogene (MYC), tumor protein p53 (TP53), and phosphatase and tensin homolog (PTEN), establish intrinsic programs that promote survival, dedifferentiation, metabolic rewiring (e.g., enhanced glycolysis and lactate accumulation), and immune evasion from the outset ( ). Under chronic immune pressure, inflammatory cytokines, and therapeutic stress, these malignant programs are reinforced through epigenetic remodeling (e.g., chromatin-mediated repression of Ag presentation programs), transcriptional reprogramming, and the activation of stress-responsive oncogenic signaling ( ). Through this multilayered evolution, tumor cells acquire stem-like persistence, death resistance, metabolic adaptability, and diminished antigenicity, forming the intracellular foundation of immune refractoriness ( ).
Crucially, these intrinsic programs do not remain confined within tumor cells. Instead, they project outward to remodel the surrounding microenvironment. By secreting cytokines, altering chemokine networks, activating fibroblasts, restructuring vascular architecture, and recruiting suppressive immune subsets, tumor cells actively reshape the TME into a cooperative niche that mirrors and stabilizes their own refractory state ( ), indicating that resistance may be preconfigured through tumor cell–intrinsic adaptations shaped by chronic immune selection. In this sense, tumor cells behave not as passive targets of immune elimination but as active architects of a multilayered immune-suppressive landscape. Tumor and immune cells ultimately enter an uneasy equilibrium, an immunological coexistence in which immune cells persist within the tumor yet remain functionally incapacitated.
This review proposes an integrated conceptual framework that redefines the architecture of immune resistance. We argue that durable control is unlikely to be achieved by intensifying immune activation alone, because the principal and previously underappreciated drivers of refractoriness lie within tumor cell-encoded regulatory circuits that couple oncogenic progression with immune suppression. By tracing how tumor cells coordinate multi-malignant evolution with TME remodeling through genetic, epigenetic, metabolic, and transcriptional programs, this review reframes immune resistance as a systems-level property of tumor–immune coevolution. Importantly, we advance a tumor cell-centric model that addresses dimensions of immune resistance not fully resolved by immune-centric frameworks, while recognizing that immune cell-intrinsic and tumor cell–intrinsic programs dynamically interact within the immune-refractory tumor microenvironment. Such a paradigm shift provides the basis for rational therapeutic innovation, one that pairs T cell-directed immunotherapies with strategies that dismantle the tumor cell-encoded regulatory network, enabling restoration of durable, system-level antitumor immunity.

## CLINICAL LANDSCAPE AND UNMET NEEDS OF T CELL-BASED THERAPY

## Clinical outcomes and limitations across solid tumors
T cell-based immunotherapies span a broad therapeutic spectrum, including ICB, ACT, tumor-infiltrating lymphocyte (TIL) therapy, genetically engineered CAR-T and TCR-T cells, and neoantigen/mRNA vaccination ( ). TIL therapy has demonstrated meaningful response rates in metastatic melanoma ( ) and other refractory solid tumors, and CAR-T/TCR-T cells have achieved curative efficacy in hematologic malignancies ( ). However, in most epithelial cancers, engineered T-cell therapies encounter multiple barriers, including insufficient trafficking, Ag heterogeneity, early exhaustion, and potent TME-mediated dysfunction, leading to limited and transient clinical benefits ( ). Similarly, neoantigen and mRNA vaccines expand tumor-reactive T-cell pools but offer modest monotherapy efficacy in most solid tumors ( ). Across existing indications, anti-PD-1 therapy produces durable responses in ~20%–30% of patients with metastatic melanoma or NSCLC ( ), and ~40% in microsatellite instability-high colorectal cancer ( ). Yet hepatocellular carcinoma, pancreatic cancer, and microsatellite-stable colorectal cancer remain largely unresponsive, with many patients experiencing transient responses followed by rapid adaptive resistance ( ). Collectively, these outcomes underscore a central unmet need: although multiple platforms can activate tumor-reactive T cells, durable responses remain confined to a minority, revealing that the major barriers to efficacy lie beyond T-cell activation itself.
 

## Combination strategies: rationale, achievements, and constraints
To expand efficacy, current strategies have increasingly shifted toward combination regimens that enhance T-cell activation or improve access to tumor sites. Dual checkpoint blockades, such as nivolumab plus ipilimumab, improve survival in melanoma and renal-cell carcinoma but are limited by severe immune-related toxicities ( ). Attempts to strengthen engineered T-cell efficacy, such as mesothelin-CAR-T plus anti-PD-1 in malignant pleural mesothelioma, or New York esophageal squamous cell carcinoma 1 (NY-ESO-1)-TCR-T plus checkpoint blockade in synovial sarcoma, improve persistence but rarely yield durable remission ( ). Approaches targeting the stromal and vascular barriers have yielded partial but incomplete success. The combination of anti-PD-L1 (atezolizumab) and anti-VEGF (bevacizumab) in hepatocellular carcinoma modestly enhances immune infiltration through vascular normalization ( ) but still falls short of long-term benefit. Likewise, combining EGFR inhibition with PD-1 blockade results in incremental improvements in EGFR-mutant NSCLC or colorectal cancer ( ), but resistance persists due to tumor cell-intrinsic adaptation. Early-phase studies pairing human epidermal growth factor receptor 2-CAR-T therapy with lapatinib show enhanced cytokine production and tumor shrinkage ( ), yet Ag downregulation and suppressive myeloid infiltration rapidly restore resistance. Parallel attempts, IL-2 variants, oncolytic viruses such as talimogene laherparepvec (T-VEC), or chemokine agonists, enhance infiltration or persistence but do not overcome entrenched refractoriness ( ). Even novel checkpoints, including T cell immunoreceptor with Ig and ITIM domains (TIGIT; tiragolumab) ( ), CD73/adenosine pathway ( ), lymphocyte-activation gene 3 (LAG-3; relatlimab) ( ), anti-V-domain Ig suppressor of T cell activation (VISTA) agents ( ), and ociperlimab/etigilimab combinations ( ), show only incremental benefit in early trials ( ). Altogether, these results reveal a common constraint: strategies that intensify immune activation or modulate isolated suppressive cues improve response rates only incrementally, indicating that the deeper architecture of resistance is embedded within tumor cells rather than in the immune system alone.

## Why intensification fails: a tumor cell-centric interpretation
Despite diverse combination strategies, often fail to fully dismantle the tumor cell–encoded programs that fundamentally maintain immune resistance. Clinical observations from ICB plus EGFR inhibition ( ), anti-VEGF combinations ( ), ACT-enhancing regimens ( ), and vaccine platforms ( ) converge on one insight: these approaches enhance T-cell activity but do not alter the tumor cell-encoded oncogenic and epigenetic programs, including key metabolic dependencies that enforce immune refractoriness.
Through chronic selective pressure, tumor cells establish multi-layered resistance systems involving Ag-presentation defects, IFN desensitization, persistent checkpoint induction, cytokine secretion, and stromal-directed remodeling. These circuits both underlie multi-malignant progression and directly impose immune dysfunction, making immune-centric intensification frequently insufficient. Durable control therefore requires dismantling the regulatory hierarchy inside tumor cells that integrates oncogenic signaling, immune evasion, and TME remodeling, the foundational architecture of therapeutic failure across solid tumors.

## EVOLUTIONARY DYNAMICS OF TUMOR CELL-MEDIATED IMMUNE RESISTANCE
Immune resistance arises through a multistep evolutionary process in which intrinsic oncogenic programs and external immune pressure jointly remodel tumor cell states. Foundational oncogenic mutations, such as KRAS, BRAF, EGFR, MYC, TP53, and PTEN, establish a malignant baseline that inherently favors survival under cytotoxic stress ( ). Yet this baseline marks only the beginning of the resistance trajectory. Persistent immune surveillance, chronic inflammation, and therapeutic pressure progressively impose epigenetic, transcriptional, and metabolic reprogramming, generating tumor cell populations capable not only of initiating malignancy but also of adapting to and enduring sustained immune attack. Immune evasion therefore emerges not as a late or secondary event but as an integrated consequence of tumor evolution.

## Oncogenic priming derived by mutations
While immune editing has long been recognized as a central force shaping immune-resistance evolution, accumulating evidence indicates that pre-existing oncogenic alterations in tumor cells create the foundational intracellular logic that predisposes tumors to immune evasion. Mutations in KRAS, BRAF, EGFR, and MYC not only drive unchecked proliferation but also establish intrinsic programs that enhance survival and stress tolerance ( ). Through sustained MAPK and PI3K-AKT-mTOR activation ( ), these oncogenic lesions suppress apoptotic signaling by T cells, rewire metabolism, and promote dedifferentiation. This oncogenic priming creates the molecular foundation of a multi-malignant phenotype inherently less susceptible to cytotoxic T-cell attack, setting the stage upon which subsequent immune selection acts.
 

## Immune-driven editing and adaptive selection of refractory tumor cells
Superimposed on oncogenic priming, classical immunoediting provides the selective force that not only eliminates highly immunogenic clones but also reshapes tumor cell states through sustained immune pressure. Across the canonical elimination–equilibrium–escape sequence, CTLs eradicate highly immunogenic subclones, whereas weakly immunogenic variants persist and adapt ( ). During this process, surviving tumor cells undergo profound non-genetic remodeling including epigenetic and transcriptional remodeling ( ), while genetic alterations such as JAK1/2 or B2M mutations have also been reported to contribute to acquired resistance in specific contexts ( ). For example, these epigenetic and transcriptional changes establish stable, low-immunogenic states, stress-tolerant phenotypes with malignancy that persist even without new mutations ( ). Immune-selection models illustrate this dynamic. Tumors derived from immune-sensitive P0 cells retain T-cell-responsive phenotypes, whereas those derived from CTL-selected P3 cells acquire apoptosis-resistant by T cells, and therapy-refractory states despite identical genetic backgrounds ( ). These observations show that immune pressure alone can induce heritable, non-genetic reprogramming, transforming transiently edited tumor states into stable immune-refractory lineages ( ). Together, oncogenic alterations and immune editing represent dual but convergent evolutionary axes. Oncogenic mutations encode malignant potential, while immune pressure fixes and amplifies these traits.

## Adaptive reprogramming under chronic immune pressure
Once oncogenically primed and immune-edited populations are established, continuous exposure to immune and therapeutic stress drives a second evolutionary phase: adaptive reprogramming. Some changes arise from fixed genetic lesions, β2-microglobulin loss, JAK1/2 inactivation, BAX/CASP8 mutations that permanently abolish Ag presentation and cytotoxic signaling ( ). But fixed mutations alone do not explain the dynamic, heterogeneous resistance observed clinically. Under chronic IFN-γ, TNF-α, TGF-β, nutrient deprivation, and oxidative stress, tumor cells undergo extensive epigenetic remodeling such as enhancer of zeste homolog (EZH) 2-mediated chromatin repression, metabolic rewiring including MYC-driven metabolic dominance and indoleamine 2,3-dioxygenase (IDO)-mediated tryptophan metabolism, and epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition (EMT)-associated dedifferentiation ( ). These changes generate relatively stable but flexible transcriptional states sustained by altered chromatin landscapes and metabolically optimized circuits ( ). Importantly, these adaptive changes converge with oncogenic signaling pathways such as PI3K-AKT-mTOR, NF-κB, and hypoxia-inducible factor (HIF)-1α, which integrate environmental stress inputs with intrinsic survival cues ( ). Within this context, pre-existing NANOG+ stem-like subclones emerge as archetypal survivors ( ). Initially rare, they are preferentially expanded under sustained CTL pressure, where immune stress and cytokine signaling activate pluripotency networks and epigenetic factors such as histone deacetylase 1 (HDAC1) ( ). This results in global chromatin remodeling that stabilizes NANOG reinforces anti-apoptotic programs and promotes metabolic adaptations. Once established, the NANOG-driven axis maintains dedifferentiation, suppresses responsiveness, and preserves immune-refractory phenotypes even without new mutations. Through this adaptive reprogramming, tumor cells evolve into multi-malignant, immune-resistant populations sustained by epigenetic memory and selective expansion. Resistance therefore emerges not as a static endpoint but as a continuously evolving phenotype sculpted by the interplay of oncogenic drive, immune selection, and adaptive remodeling. This dynamic evolving equilibrium sets the foundation for the next phase, in which tumor cells consolidate multi-malignancy and defense modules.

## Multi-malignant phenotypes of immune-refractory tumor cells
As tumor cells progress through oncogenic priming, immune-driven editing, and chronic stress–induced adaptive remodeling, these processes ultimately converge into a consolidated malignant state characterized by multi-malignant and immune-refractory phenotypes ( ). The earliest layers of oncogenic signaling, activated through KRAS, BRAF, EGFR, MYC, TP53, or PTEN mutations ( ), establish a survival-oriented baseline by enforcing MAPK, PI3K-AKT-mTOR, NF-κB, and HIF-1α activity ( ). These pathways suppress apoptosis, enhance metabolic flexibility, and loosen lineage constraints, generating a lineage-relaxed and stress-tolerant cellular framework even before immune pressure intensifies.
 Once immune selection becomes dominant, these oncogenic circuits intersect with epigenetic and transcriptional programs shaped by chronic CTL attack, inflammatory cytokines, and metabolic deprivation ( ). Within this environment, pre-existing or stress-induced NANOG+ stem-like subclones gain an evolutionary advantage. Immune pressure activates pluripotency-associated transcription factors and chromatin regulators such as HDAC1, stabilizing NANOG-centered transcriptional hierarchies that reinforce dedifferentiation and self-renewal ( ). These programs also strengthen resistance to apoptosis and ferroptosis through upregulation of myeloid cell leukemia 1 (MCL1), 3-hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl-CoA reductase (HMGCR) ( ), and other survival effectors, enabling these cells to remain dormant under lethal immune pressure and re-enter proliferative cycles once selection abates. As these stem-like, immune-selected states become epigenetically fixed, tumor cells sequentially acquire additional malignant capabilities. EMT transcription factors, Snail family transcriptional repressor (SNAIL), twist family BHLH transcription factor (TWIST), B-cell-specific Moloney murine leukemia virus integration site 1 (BMI1), zinc finger E-box binding homeobox 1 (ZEB1), activated through TGF-β and WNT-β-catenin signaling, reconfigure cytoskeletal and adhesion networks, promoting motility, invasion, and colonization of immune-excluded niches ( ). Parallel metabolic constraints, driven by MYC-, PI3K-AKT-, and HIF-1α-dependent transcriptional programs, amplifies glycolysis, lipid biosynthesis, mitochondrial remodeling, and redox buffering. These metabolic adaptations sustain survival in hypoxic or nutrient-depleted environments while simultaneously generating immunosuppressive metabolites, including lactate, adenosine, and kynurenine, that disable nearby effector T cells ( ).
Importantly, the signaling circuits that enforce immune refractoriness also create broad therapeutic cross-resistance. Tumor cells that have adapted to withstand T-cell cytotoxicity frequently evade targeted therapies, anti-angiogenic agents, and conventional chemotherapy ( ). This cross-resistance arises because the same oncogenic, epigenetic, and metabolic programs that stabilize immune evasion also maintain survival under diverse therapeutic insults. Thus, stemness, death resistance, invasiveness, metabolic plasticity, and immune suppression do not represent isolated traits but form an interdependent, self-reinforcing malignant architecture sustained by epigenetic memory. Together, these convergent programs define the mature immune-refractory tumor cell state: a stable yet evolvable phenotype capable of persisting under persistent immune and therapeutic pressure. As these malignant traits consolidate, tumor cells begin to externalize this resistant logic, actively remodeling stromal, vascular, and immune components of the microenvironment, thereby extending cell-intrinsic resistance into a fully integrated, TME-level refractoriness.

## TUMOR CELL-MEDIATED REFRACTORINESS OF THE TME
The immune landscape within tumors is not a passive consequence of immune pressure but a dynamically reprogrammed tumor microenvironment actively shaped by tumor cells to adapt to rapidly changing circumstances. As tumor cells evolve under chronic immune and therapeutic stress, they extend their intrinsic survival logic beyond the cellular boundary. Through sustained oncogenic signaling, cytokine release, and metabolic adaptation, they orchestrate immune suppression, stromal activation, and vascular remodeling ( ). Importantly, tumor cell-mediated refractoriness of the tumor microenvironment is not a static state but a dynamically acquired and reinforced process. Initial oncogenic signaling and metabolic stress, together with sustained immune selection, prime tumor cells to release inflammatory and immunomodulatory cues, which progressively reshape stromal, vascular, and immune compartments under chronic immune and therapeutic pressure. Over time, these adaptive interactions stabilize a refractory microenvironment that mirrors and reinforces tumor cell–intrinsic programs, ultimately locking the tumor-immune system into a durable state of immune resistance.

## Defective tumor Ag presentation by Ag-presenting cells (APCs)
A defining consequence of tumor cell-driven refractoriness is silencing of Ag presentation within the priming niche. Rather than acting solely through cytokine release, tumor cells deploy their oncogenic and epigenetic programs outward. Hyperactive KRAS, EGFR, and MYC signaling drives chromatin remodeling at cytokine and growth factor loci, elevating VEGF, IL-10, and TGF-β ( ). These cues repress IRF8 and BATF3 through HDAC-mediated deacetylation and promoter methylation, disrupting DC differentiation and cross-priming ( ). Parallel metabolic rewiring driven by PI3K-AKT-mTOR, MYC, and HIF-1α shifts glucose flux toward aerobic glycolysis; accumulated lactate and adenosine suppress STAT1-dependent IFN signaling and impair mitochondrial respiration in APCs ( ). Tumor cell-derived exosomes carrying PD-L1 and NF-κB/STAT3-regulated miRNAs further silence MHC-I and costimulatory genes ( ). Through these combined mechanisms, tumor cell-derived signals disable APC lineage commitment and Ag presentation, converting the priming niche into a tolerogenic field.

## T cell exclusion and immune-desert architecture
Tumor cells with remodeled enhancer landscapes via MYC, YAP/TAZ, EZH2, and HDAC1, actively sculpt a T-cell–excluded microenvironment ( ). Enhancer activation of TGF-β, PDGF, and CXCL12 driven by KRAS, β-catenin, or SMAD4 loss induces fibroblasts to differentiate into cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs), which deposit collagen and fibronectin through epigenetically derepressed extracellular matrix programs ( ). This generates a stiff, cross-linked matrix that physically obstructs immune infiltration. Simultaneously, epigenetic repression of CXCL9/10 eliminates chemokine gradients required for effector T-cell recruitment ( ). WNT-β-catenin signaling reduces CCL4 and CXCL10, preventing BATF3+ DC recruitment and sustaining immune exclusion ( ). Together, these alterations imprint tumor cell-intrinsic signaling onto stromal cells, yielding a mechanically reinforced, chemokine-depleted immune-desert architecture.

## T cell dysfunction and exhaustion
Even infiltrating T cells undergo metabolic and epigenetic exhaustion imposed by tumor cell-driven programs. KRAS/EGFR and MYC hyperactivation increases glycolytic flux, while hypoxia-induced HIF-1α elevates LDHA, glucose transporter 1 (GLUT1), and PDK1, creating a microenvironment dominated by glucose deprivation, lactate accumulation, and acidosis ( ). Lactate influx via monocarboxylate transporter (MCT) 4 suppresses mitochondrial respiration and mTOR activity ( ), triggering exhaustion-associated transcriptional programs mediated by TOX and NR4A family members ( ). Chromatin-level induction of PD-L1 through HIF-1α-p300 acetylation and NF-κB recruitment amplifies inhibitory signaling ( ). Concurrently, STAT3- and aryl hydrocarbon receptor-driven IDO1/TDO2 expression generates kynurenine, which activates DNA methyltransferase (DNMT) 1 and represses IL2RA and IFNG expression. In addition, this dysfunctional state is stabilized by T cell-intrinsic transcriptional and epigenetic programs governed by TOX and NR4A family members, accompanied by reduced TCF1 activity and durable lineage fixation. These metabolic and epigenetic constraints impose a bioenergetic and transcriptional shutdown, driving deep exhaustion in tumor-infiltrating T cells ( ).

## Expansion of suppressive immune subsets
Suppressive subsets arise not spontaneously, but as a programmed extension of tumor cell–encoded regulatory architecture. Epigenetic activation of inflammatory cytokine super-enhancers drives sustained secretion of GM-CSF, IL-6, IL-8, and CCL2 ( ). These factors instruct myeloid progenitors and circulating monocytes to differentiate into MDSCs or immunosuppressive macrophages. Meanwhile, metabolic byproducts such as lactic acid, prostaglandin E2, and sterol regulatory element-binding protein-driven lipids create an environment that selectively stabilizes FOXP3+ Tregs by enhancing oxidative phosphorylation and suppressing IL-2 signaling ( ). HIF-1α further skews macrophages toward an M2-like phenotype by epigenetically activating ARG1 and MRC1 ( ). MDSCs and tumor-associated macrophages suppress antitumor immunity through myeloid-intrinsic programs, including epigenetically reinforced STAT3 signaling, ARG1-mediated arginine depletion, and IDO-dependent tryptophan metabolism. Tregs further reinforce immune suppression through stabilization of FOXP3-dependent transcriptional programs that sustain suppressive identity under chronic inflammatory conditions. In this way, tumor cells use their epigenetic and metabolic programs as “long-range immunoregulatory tools,” actively constructing a suppressive immune compartment optimized for tumor persistence.

## Abnormal vasculature in TME
Aberrant vasculature is another extension of tumor cell-intrinsic programs. KRAS, PI3K-AKT, and HIF-1α activation promote VEGF-A, ANG2, and PDGF-B transcription while repressing vessel-stabilizing factors via epigenetic downregulation of NOTCH pathway components and pericyte-attracting chemokines ( ). This results in chaotic, leaky vessels with sluggish flow, limiting immune cell trafficking and amplifying hypoxia. Under these conditions, endothelial cells undergo epigenetic reprogramming via p300, BRD4, or KDM5 demethylases, and acquire immunosuppressive properties, including PD-L1 and FasL expression, selectively eliminating infiltrating CD8+ T cells ( ). Thus, tumor cell-intrinsic hypoxic and oncogenic programs restructure the vasculature as an immunological barrier rather than a conduit. In addition to immune and stromal modulation, tumor cells reshape the vascular architecture into an immunologically inert network. Upregulation of angiogenic mediators such as HIF-1α, VEGF, and ANG2 generates structurally abnormal, leaky, and poorly perfused vessels that exacerbate hypoxia while impeding immune cell adhesion and extravasation. Collectively, these processes transform the tumor vasculature from a conduit for immune surveillance into an active immunological barrier that reinforces hypoxia and selectively excludes or eliminates effector T cells.

## Immature or dysfunctional tertiary lymphoid structures (TLSs)
Functional TLSs require the coordinated spatial organization of CXCL13, CCL19/21, and intact LTβR signaling ( ). However, tumor cell-intrinsic oncogenic and epigenetic programs actively disrupt this architecture. Hyperactivation of β-catenin signaling reshapes the tumor’s chromatin landscape in ways that directly oppose TLS maturation ( ). Epigenetic repressors upregulated downstream of these pathways, enforce chromatin closure at the CXCL13 locus, thereby limiting the chemokine gradients necessary for B–T cell compartmentalization. At the same time, tumor cells suppress LTβR ligands that are essential for follicular DC development, weakening the stromal scaffold that supports productive Ag presentation within TLSs ( ). Elevated TGF-β production, another downstream output of oncogenic and stress-adaptive signaling, further interferes with lymphoid organization by inhibiting germinal center formation and preventing maturation of the TLS network. Through this combination of transcriptional suppression, epigenetic silence, and cytokine-driven disruption, tumor cells generate TLSs that are immature, spatially disordered, and functionally non-productive. In effect, the same molecular circuits that drive tumor cell-intrinsic immune resistance extends outward to destabilize the lymphoid infrastructure required for effective intratumoral immunity.
Collectively, these interconnected mechanisms demonstrate that evolved tumor cells act not as passive targets of immune selection but as active architects of an immunosuppressive ecosystem. Through coordinated signaling, metabolic, and epigenetic control, tumor cells export their intrinsic survival logic to surrounding stromal and immune compartments, constructing a microenvironment that mirrors and perpetuates immune refractoriness. This reciprocal equilibrium embodies a “sleeping with the enemy” state, an uneasy coexistence in which immune cells persist within the tumor but remain functionally disarmed. By converting chronic immune surveillance from a lethal threat into a stabilizing force, tumor cells actively reshape the TME into a cooperative, tolerant niche that sustains malignant persistence.

## FUTURE PERSPECTIVES AND CLINICAL TRANSLATION

## Current strategies to overcome resistance: immune-centric frameworks
Current immuno-oncology strategies have largely focused on strengthening T-cell activation or alleviating extrinsic immunosuppression, yet these approaches rarely dismantle the tumor cell-encoded circuits that represent the deeper engine of immune refractoriness. DC-based vaccines such as Sipuleucel-T ( ), next-generation personalized neoantigen vaccines (e.g., mRNA-4157) ( ), and oncolytic viruses like T-VEC ( ) can transiently enhance local immunogenicity, but their therapeutic effects are modest and short-lived because they do not disrupt the oncogenic-epigenetic programs that stabilize immune escape. Similarly, combination regimens aimed at depleting suppressive immune subsets, such as CXCR2 inhibition to block MDSC recruitment or CCR8-targeted therapies to eliminate intratumoral Tregs ( ), demonstrate partial improvements without achieving durable control. Clinical attempts to remodel the stroma and vasculature, best exemplified by atezolizumab plus bevacizumab in hepatocellular carcinoma, yield vascular normalization and enhanced T-cell infiltration yet still fall short of long-term remission ( ). These collective observations reveal a common constraint: most current interventions target isolated resistance nodes without disrupting the integrated regulatory hierarchy through which tumor cells couple oncogenic signaling, metabolic rewiring, and immune suppression ( ). Therefore, durable remission will require shifting from immune-centric intensification toward dismantling the tumor cell-centred control programs that foster both aggressiveness and immune refractoriness.

## Emerging directions and clinical translation: tumor-centric frameworks
The next frontier lies in decoding and disrupting the multi-malignant regulatory architecture of tumor cells and reversing immune-suppressive environment. Recent advances in single-cell and spatial multi-omics have revealed that transcriptional, epigenetic, and metabolic programs driven by master regulators function as central nodes coupling oncogenic growth with immune suppression. Preclinical studies highlight the therapeutic potential of targeting these hubs: for example, EZH2 inhibition restores IFN signaling and CD8+ T-cell infiltration in melanoma and prostate cancer models ( ), while YAP/TAZ blockade reverses hypoxia-induced PD-L1 upregulation and reduces myeloid recruitment ( ). Likewise, MYC inhibitors, long considered undruggable, are now supported by evidence that MYC-directed inhibitors synergize with PD-1/PD-L1 blockades by reversing metabolic dominance and enhancing effector T-cell infiltration ( ). Recent clinical efforts targeting tumor cell-intrinsic epigenetic and oncogenic programs highlight both the translational potential and current limitations of tumor-centric strategies ( ).
 Importantly, disease-specific oncogenic drivers themselves, such as EGFR and KRAS mutation, are emerging as actionable modifiers of immunotherapy resistance. In EGFR-mutant NSCLC, EGFR-tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs) such as osimertinib increase tumor antigenicity and T-cell infiltration as well as multi-malignant phenotype of tumor cells, providing the rationale for ongoing trials combining EGFR inhibition with PD-1/PD-L1 blockade, although toxicities remain a challenge ( ). Similarly, KRAS-G12C inhibitors (sotorasib and adagrasib) partially reprogram tumor immunogenicity by reducing IL-8, CXCL1/2, and GM-CSF output, thereby decreasing MDSC recruitment; early-phase studies combining KRAS-G12C inhibitors with anti-PD-1 therapy have shown enhanced T-cell activation and preliminary clinical activity in NSCLC and colorectal cancer ( ). These examples underscore a unifying theme: targeting oncogenic nodes not only restrain tumor cell-intrinsic growth but also dismantle the molecular infrastructure that enforces immune exclusion and dysfunction. These mechanistic insights are now being translated into rational therapeutic development. Agents targeting EZH2 (tazemetostat), YAP/TEAD signaling (IK-930), and MYC transcriptional output are progressing into the clinical pipeline, and combination strategies integrating these inhibitors with PD-1/PD-L1 or TGF-β/VEGF blockade are already demonstrating synergistic restoration of immune sensitivity in preclinical and early-phase clinical studies. We also note that several hub regulators discussed here can exert context-dependent functions in immune and stromal compartments. Notably, inhibition of regulators such as EZH2 and DNMT1 in immune cells has been shown to relieve epigenetically fixed exhaustion states and restore effector and memory-associated T cell programs, while blockade of IDO-dependent tryptophan metabolism can directly enhance T cell function within the immune compartment. However, these immune dysfunctions are frequently induced and stabilized by tumor cell–intrinsic oncogenic and epigenetic programs under chronic immune pressure, positioning tumor cells as the initiating and sustaining nodes of refractory circuitry. Accordingly, tumor cell-centric targeting remains a rational primary strategy by disrupting upstream regulatory programs at their source while offering a more favorable therapeutic window than broadly perturbing non-malignant compartments.
Ultimately, meaningful progress will depend on establishing a feedback-integrated translational niche linking discovery, validation, and clinical iteration. By coupling tumor cell-focused mechanistic insights with immune restoration strategies, oncology can move beyond incremental responses toward durable, system-level remission. In this evolving framework, immune resistance should no longer be viewed as the endpoint of therapeutic failure but rather as a targetable vulnerability embedded within the regulatory hierarchy of tumor evolution. Decoding and dismantling these integrated programs will transform immune refractoriness from an intractable barrier into a foundation for next-generation therapeutic innovation.

## CONCLUSION
Immune resistance arises from the convergence of oncogenic drive, immune selection, and adaptive remodeling within tumor cells. Foundational mutations in tumor cells establish a malignant baseline preconfigured for immune escape. Chronic immune pressure then induces adaptive remodeling (epigenetic, transcriptional, and metabolic) that consolidates stemness, death resistance, metabolic adaptability, and persistent checkpoint induction, forming a multi-malignant architecture inherently resilient to immune attack. These processes unfold through continuous immune selection, reinforcing reciprocal tumor-immune coevolution rather than unidirectional immune suppression. As these intrinsic programs mature, tumor cells externalize this resistant logic into the surrounding microenvironment, suppressing Ag presentation, disrupting chemokine gradients, activating fibroblasts, reconfiguring vasculature, and expanding suppressive myeloid and regulatory populations. This reciprocal remodeling creates an equilibrium in which tumor and immune cells coexist, but the immune system is functionally paralyzed. These insights explain why immune-centric intensification, checkpoint combinations, stromal targeting, vascular normalization, has offered only incremental benefit: such strategies modulate downstream resistance nodes but leave intact the upstream oncogenic-epigenetic hierarchy that coordinates malignancy with immune suppression. Durable therapeutic control will therefore require dismantling of these higher-order tumor cell-intrinsic programs. Future frameworks must integrate T cell-directed immunotherapies with agents targeting chromatin regulators, metabolic controllers, and oncogenic signaling networks that synchronize intrinsic survival with extrinsic immunosuppression. Advances in single-cell and spatial multi-omics now make it possible to decode this architecture and guide biomarker-driven combinations capable of reprogramming both tumor cells and the TME. Ultimately, deconstructing the regulatory circuitry that underlies immune refractoriness is essential to transform transient immune activation into durable, system-level remission.
