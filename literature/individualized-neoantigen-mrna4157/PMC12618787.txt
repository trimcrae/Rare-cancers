## Abstract
Metastatic cancer remains the leading cause of cancer-related mortality, largely owing to the limitations of conventional treatments such as chemotherapy, radiotherapy, and targeted agents that are challenged by tumor heterogeneity, acquired resistance, and systemic toxicity. In contrast, immunotherapy has emerged as a transformative modality, harnessing the power of the immune system to selectively target and eradicate tumor cells. This review systematically examines key immunotherapeutic strategies ranging from immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) and monoclonal antibodies to cancer vaccines, oncolytic viruses, and adoptive cell therapies (such as CAR-T and TCR-engineered T cells). Of particular interest are personalized approaches, including neoantigen-based vaccines and tumor-infiltrating lymphocyte (TIL) therapies, which hold promise for tailored treatment but also present significant logistical and translational challenges. Central to therapeutic outcomes are factors like immune resistance, the suppressive tumor microenvironment, and the identification of predictive biomarkers including PD-L1 expression and tumor mutational burden (TMB) that can guide patient selection and efficacy. As the field progresses, future breakthroughs will hinge on deepening our understanding of metastasis immunobiology, refining biomarker-driven precision approaches, and developing novel immunological targets and delivery platforms to improve outcomes and accessibility.
Keywords: Metastatic cancer, Immunotherapy, Immune checkpoint inhibitors, Tumor microenvironment, CAR-T cells, Cancer vaccines

## Introduction
Most cancer death worldwide is due to metastatic cancer, a late-stage diseased state in which cancer cells have migrated to distant sites. This remains a major unsolved challenge in oncology [ ]. Metastasis-colonized cancer spread of cancer, the spread of cancer cells from the primary tumor to distant organs, causes over 90% of mortalities worldwide, and thus the effective treatment of this illness remains very difficult [ ]. Traditional therapeutic strategies, including surgery, chemo and radiotherapy, are unsuccessful in the prevention of metastasis, as they are limited by systemic toxicity tumor heterogeneity, and the development of drug resistance [ , ]. Bypass the immune system by reprogramming it to say no to cancer leverage immunotherapy has been a game-changer for trying to stop cancer from growing and also spreading. Immune checkpoint inhibitors have introduced a novel therapeutic avenue for cancers that were once considered untreatable. Several metastatic cancers such as melanoma, non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), renal cell carcinoma, and more recently, triple-negative breast cancer have shown remarkable clinical success with various immunotherapeutic strategies, including ICIs (immune checkpoint inhibitors), adoptive cell therapies (ACTs), cancer vaccines, and cytokine-based treatments [ , ]. A subset of patients has shown improved survival outcomes and T-cell responses when using ICIs that target the CTLA-4, PD-1, and PD-L1 pathways [ ]. Despite these developments, immune-related adverse events (irAEs) provide further difficulties, and not all patients react to immunotherapy.
Furthermore, in metastatic situations, the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment (TME), which is made up of regulatory T cells, myeloid-derived suppressor cells, and inhibitory cytokines, frequently prevents efficient immune activation [ ]. In order to improve efficacy and get past resistance mechanisms, combined methods including immunotherapy and traditional modalities or other immune-targeting drugs are being investigated [ ]. Some immunotherapeutic strategies, such as CAR-T cell therapy, Oncolytic viruses, therapeutic cancer vaccines and bispecific T-cell engagers, are also being studied in metastatic settings, even though many of these strategies are still in the investigational stage or are only applicable to hematologic malignancies [ , ]. The full potential of immunotherapy in treating metastatic tumors is hampered by a number of issues like immune-related adverse events (irAEs), primary and acquired resistance and a deficiency of predictive biomarkers for patient selection. Other obstacles to broad clinical acceptance include the high expense and intricate logistics of many immunotherapies (Fig.  ). The full promise of immunotherapy in treating metastatic cancers is yet to be fully achieved, as several challenges persist. These include immune-related adverse events (irAEs), both primary and acquired resistance, absence of reliable predictive biomarkers for selecting suitable patients, as well as the high costs and complex logistical requirements associated with some immunotherapeutic approaches (Fig.  ).
 With new opportunities for long-lasting effects and extended survival, the development of cancer immunotherapy has completely changed treatment strategies, especially in cases of advanced and metastatic disease, some of the examples are given below.

## ICIs (Immune checkpoint Inhibitors)
The most effective type of cancer immunotherapeutics to date is immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs). By inhibiting T cell inhibitory pathways (checkpoints), including CTLA-4, PD-1, and PD-L1, these medicines improve the immune response against tumor cells. Immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs), which activate endogenous T-cell-mediated immunity, have completely changed the treatment way of metastatic malignancies. ICIs boost antitumor efficacy by restoring T-cell activity by targeting regulatory pathways, specifically CTLA-4 and PD-1/PDL1 that tumors use to inhibit immune responses [ ].

## CTLA-4 inhibition
The first checkpoint inhibitor authorized for metastatic melanoma was ipilimumab, a completely human anti-CTLA-4 antibody, showed enhanced overall survival (OS) in metastatic melanoma, marking the first significant success. Ipilimumab achieved a statistically significant survival advantage in a pivotal Phase III trial, increasing median OS by about 10 months when compared to control [ ].

## Blockade of PD-1/PD-L1
Anti-PD-1 drugs nivolumab and pembrolizumab as well as anti-PD-L1 drugs atezolizumab have shown long-lasting effects in metastatic melanoma, non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), renal cell carcinoma, and urothelial carcinoma [ ]. Pembrolizumab and nivolumab, antibodies that target the PD-1/PDL1 axis, showed excellent safety profiles and were widely approved for use in metastatic malignancies. When compared to Everolimus, Nivolumab considerably extended overall survival in individuals with metastatic renal cell carcinoma. Nivolumab and Everolimus had median overall survivals of 25.0 and 19.6 months, respectively, in the phase III CheckMate-025 study (hazard ratio 0.73; P = 0.002) [ ]. According to a study [ ], resistance mechanisms—both primary and acquired—remain a problem, and only a small percentage of patients benefit from ICIs. For metastatic melanoma and other malignancies, combination checkpoint blockade (such as nivolumab plus ipilimumab) has resulted in even more significant and long-lasting responses than single-agent therapy. Compared to monotherapy, the combination produced greater objective response rates and long-term survival advantages in pooled analyses, while having more immune-related toxicities but resistance is still a significant problem. Between 40 and 60% of patients have primary resistance, while others acquire resistance through mechanisms like immunosuppressive cells present in the tumor microenvironment, altered interferon signaling and poor antigen presentation [ ]. There is continuous research on biomarker-based ICI therapeutic modifications. Therapists can select patients who are most likely to benefit by using response predictors such as tumor mutational load, tumor-infiltrating lymphocyte (TIL) density, PD-L1 expression, and microsatellite instability [ , ].

## ACT (Adoptive cell Therapy)
Adoptive cell therapy (ACT) is a novel immunotherapeutic approach that boosts a patient’s immune system in fight against metastatic cancer. The main concept is to take lymphocytes from peripheral blood or tumor tissue, cultivate or genetically modify them outside of the body, and then return them to have potent anti-tumor effects [ ]. Tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs) and genetically modified T cells (e.g., CAR-T cells) are examples of autologous immune cells that are isolated, altered, and then reinfused as part of ACT. CAR-T therapy has demonstrated impressive results in hematological malignancies such as B-cell lymphomas; however, because of the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment and poor T cell trafficking, its effectiveness in solid metastatic tumors is still being studied clinically [ , ]. ACT has demonstrated promising outcomes in the treatment of solid malignancies, such as metastatic melanoma. In patients who have received extensive pretreatment, pooled trials of TIL therapy have shown long-term remissions, and early-phase CAR T and TCR-T studies show objective responses in resistant metastatic illnesses [ ].

## TILs (Tumor-Infiltrating Lymphocytes)
TIL treatment is the process of extracting endogenous T lymphocytes that have moved into the tumor microenvironment, enlarging them with interleukin-2, and then reintroducing them after lymphodepletion. In treating metastatic melanoma, TIL ACT has demonstrated exceptional effectiveness, with objective response rates ranging from 49 to 72% and lasting full responses in a subgroup of patients [ ].

## TCR-engineered T cells
T-CR-engineered T cells are autologous T cells that have gone through genetic modification to express T-cell receptors (TCRs) unique to tumor-associated antigens. This enables precise identification of intracellular targets that are shown using MHC. The FDA recently approved Afamitresgene autoleucel for metastatic synovial sarcoma, and it has demonstrated encouraging clinical outcomes in both synovial sarcoma and metastatic melanoma [ ].
As TCR-T cell therapies continue to emerge as a promising strategy for targeting a diverse array of tumor antigens in solid tumors, several significant challenges persist that must be addressed. These include T cell-mediated toxicities, mechanisms of resistance, and issues surrounding accessibility. To mitigate the risk of T cell activation against normal cells, it is theoretically feasible to implement logic gate strategies analogous to those proposed for CAR-T cells, wherein T cell activation or inhibition is dependent on the integration of two signals rather than one. The “A AND B” logic gate approach relies on combinatorial antigen recognition, activating T cells only when both antigens A and B are concurrently expressed by tumor cells. Conversely, “A NOT B” logic gates protect normal cells, which express antigen B but not the targeted antigen A, from T cell activation. While applying this approach to TCRs presents greater challenges than to CARs, recent advancements have been made in developing inhibitory signaling platforms for “A NOT B” gates. Additionally, implementing suicide gene systems to create a safety mechanism for engineered T cells serves as an effective strategy to halt unforeseen adverse events [ ].
Current TCR-T cell therapies predominantly involve the engineering of autologous T cells. However, the potential benefits of allogeneic approaches are notable, offering advantages such as immediate availability, the possibility of product standardization, flexibility for multiple cell modifications, ease of redosing, and reduced costs. By excising both endogenous TCRα and TCRβ chains and inserting the transgenic TCR at the TRAC locus, the risk of graft-versus-host disease can be minimized. This technological advancement should be complemented by strategies aimed at reducing host immune rejection of allogeneic T cells, including partial HLA matching or gene editing techniques (such as HLA class I deletion combined with natural killer cell inhibition) to facilitate the generation of universal T cells [ ].
In conclusion, TCR-T cell therapy has demonstrated promising outcomes in the treatment of solid tumors, particularly in cancers that exhibit poor responses to existing immunotherapies, such as sarcomas. Despite the complexities associated with this therapeutic strategy and the myriad challenges it entails, improved selection of tumor-specific antigens (TSAs) and optimization of T cell engineering are anticipated to diminish the risk of toxicity while enhancing antitumor efficacy. Furthermore, the integration of therapeutics designed to enhance T cell homing (such as oncolytic viruses and radiation therapy) or to augment the activity and persistence of infused T cells (including checkpoint inhibitors, cytokines, and cancer vaccines) may significantly elevate the therapeutic potential of TCR-T cells. It is reasonable to expect that this innovative approach, akin to next-generation CAR-T cells, will alter the natural progression of cold tumors and fulfill a critical therapeutic need.

## Chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) T cells
CAR T-Cells allow for the identification and destruction of cancer cells without the use of MHC by combining an external tumor antigen-binding domain with intracellular signaling patterns. Although these therapies are unparalleled in their ability to treat hematological cancers, they are difficult to convert into solid tumors because of problems with antigen heterogeneity, tumor infiltration, and immunosuppression in the tumor microenvironment [ ]. Solid tumor microenvironment presents unique challenges, including metabolic harshness, dense extracellular matrix, physical barriers restricting T cell trafficking, and immunosuppressive cells such as MDSCs and Tregs that block effector function [ ]. To address these issues, novel engineering strategies are being investigated, including CAR-NK cells, “armored” CARs that generate cytokines (such IL-12), and novel delivery systems like microneedle patches for improved localization [ ].
Chimeric antigen receptors (CARs) represent a pivotal advancement in antibody engineering, serving as recombinant receptors that enhance the specificity and functionality of T lymphocytes and other immune cells through a singular molecular construct. The application of CARs in cancer immunotherapy hinges on their ability to target tumor-associated antigens with rapid and uniform replication. The direct infusion of these engineered T cells circumvents the challenges associated with traditional active immunization, thereby facilitating a more efficient therapeutic approach. Typically, chimeric antigen receptors consist of an extracellular domain capable of binding to specific antigens present on tumor cells, a transmembrane domain, and intracellular signaling domains that activate T-cell responses against oncogenic targets. Tisagenlecleucel, a notable example, is a CD19-directed genetically modified autologous T-cell therapy. This involves the reprogramming of patient-derived T cells with a transgene that encodes a CAR specifically engineered to recognize and eliminate both malignant and normal CD19-expressing cells. The CAR integrates a murine single-chain antibody fragment that identifies CD19 and is fused with intracellular signaling domains derived from 4-1BB and CD3 zeta. The inclusion of the CD3 zeta domain is crucial for initiating T-cell activation and mediating anti-tumor activity, whereas 4-1BB fosters the proliferation and longevity of tisagenlecleucel. Upon interacting with CD19-expressing target cells, the CAR relays a signal that encourages T-cell expansion, activation, and the subsequent elimination of targeted cells while ensuring the persistence of tisagenlecleucel [ ].
Similarly, Axicabtagene ciloleucel represents another CD19-directed genetically modified autologous T-cell immunotherapy adept at binding to CD19-expressing malignancies and normal B cells. Upon engagement of anti-CD19 CAR T cells with CD19-positive targets, the signaling from the CD28 and CD3-zeta co-stimulatory domains initiates downstream cascades that induce T-cell activation, proliferation, acquisition of effector functionalities, and the release of inflammatory cytokines and chemokines. This orchestrated response culminates in the effective destruction of CD19-expressing cellular targets.

## Vaccines for cancer
The purpose of therapeutic cancer vaccines is to specifically target tumor antigens with an immune response. Promising results have been observed in prostate cancer (sipuleucel-T) and HPV-related tumors (therapeutic HPV vaccinations), notwithstanding the poor success in metastatic situations [ , ].
In order to control metastatic disease, therapeutic cancer vaccines aim to generate potent and focused anti-tumor immune responses. This is an important advancement in immunotherapy. According to Datta et al. 2014 and Palucka et al. 2013, these therapeutics decrease tumor burden and prevent recurrence by activating cytotoxic T lymphocytes (CTLs) with tumor-associated antigens (TAAs) or patient-specific neoantigen [ , ]. The first FDA-approved dendritic cell vaccination for metastatic castration-resistant prostate cancer is still Sipuleucel-T. According to Hammerstrom, A. E. et al., it is well tolerated and increases median overall survival (OS) by almost 4 months (HR 0.78; p = 0.03) [ ]. According to Andtbacka, R. H. I et al., [ ], T-VEC (Talimogene laherparepvec), an oncolytic herpes virus that is injected directly into melanoma metastases, demonstrated systemic anti-tumor efficacy and safety in phase II, opening the door for additional research.
The use of mRNA-based tailored vaccines, such as Moderna’s mRNA4157/V940, after surgery for melanoma showed a 49% decrease in the Chance of recurrence or mortality, and when combined with pembrolizumab, the 2.5-year recurrence-free survival increased from 56 to 75%. According to Rojas, L. A., et al., neoantigen vaccines, like autogene cevumeran, produced neoantigen-specific T-cell responses in patients with resected pancreatic cancer. Responders had a promising 18-month relapse-free survival [ ]. The EGF-targeting Cuban vaccination CIMAvax-EGF showed encouraging survival trends in advanced non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) (phase IV) when used in conjunction with chemotherapy. ICI combinations in metastatic settings are being studied in ongoing trials [ ]. The effectiveness of cancer vaccines depends on inducing T-cell responses and efficiently delivering antigens, primarily by dendritic cells. However, responses are often compromised by “cold” tumors, which have low lymphocyte infiltration and immunosuppressive tumor microenvironments (TMEs) rich in regulatory T cells and myeloid-derived suppressor cells [ , ].
Co-administration of vaccines with adjuvants (e.g., GM-CSF) or ICIs, or in situ vaccination (e.g., oncolytic viruses), are being investigated as ways to transform “cold” TMEs into more immunogenic “hot” states [ , ]. According to a 2024 meta-analysis (median OS increase ~ 1.9 months), when cancer vaccines and ICIs are combined, anti-tumor responses are greatly improved compared to monotherapy,

## Therapies using cytokines
Cytokines like interleukin-2 (IL-2) and interferon-alpha (IFN-α) were included in the first immunotherapies for melanoma and metastatic renal cell carcinoma. Despite their high toxicity and limited effectiveness, novel designed cytokines with better pharmacokinetics and tolerability are being developed, such as IL-15 superagonists [ , ].
Cytokines serve as secreted signaling proteins that are integral to the initiation, maintenance, and resolution of immune responses. Their remarkable capability to modulate immune function has spurred considerable clinical interest, especially concerning cancer, autoimmunity, and infectious diseases. However, the application of cytokine-based therapeutics has faced significant limitations. This stems largely from the inherent ability of cytokines to interact with a multitude of cell types, leading to varied biological effects that can result in dose-limiting toxicity or insufficient therapeutic efficacy.
Recent advancements in structural biology, protein engineering, and receptor pharmacology have provided profound insights into the mechanisms governing cytokine receptor activation. These findings reveal that several facets of cytokine functionality are remarkably adaptable. In this context, we will explore the pharmacological principles that underpin current efforts aimed at mitigating cytokine pleiotropy and enhancing the therapeutic applicability of this vital class of signaling molecules. Cytokines, including interleukins and interferons, are pivotal in regulating numerous physiological processes such as immune responses, developmental growth, and tissue repair. Given their central role in both health and disease, numerous attempts have been made to leverage the therapeutic potential of cytokines [ ]. While cytokine antagonists have achieved clinical success in managing autoimmune conditions, successful deployment of cytokine receptor agonists in clinical settings remains limited, especially considering the extensive variety of cytokines encoded within the human genome. This is primarily attributable to the complex biology associated with natural cytokines, which often induce opposing effects across various cell types. Although the pleiotropic nature of cytokines is critical for maintaining homeostasis and facilitating essential biological functions, it poses significant challenges for clinical development, often restricting efficacy and contributing to dose-limiting toxicity [ ].

## Interleukin-2
IL-2 is a potent T-cell growth factor that stimulates the proliferation and cytotoxic function of T lymphocytes and natural killer (NK) cells. From 1986 onwards, IL-2 was tested in metastatic melanoma with notable long-term outcomes. A meta-analysis of 3,312 patients revealed complete response (CR) rates of 4% and overall response (OR) circa 20%, with enhanced efficacy after 1994 (CR: 6.1%) [ ]. With sustained CRs lasting up to 148 months (median 70 months), single-agent high-dose IL-2 therapy achieved objective response rates (ORR) of 10–33% for metastatic melanoma, particularly in patients with a low metastatic burden and excellent performance status [ ]. IL-2 has demonstrated noteworthy therapeutic potential in metastatic RCC as well. The median response duration was about 20 months, and the ORR from traditional high-dose regimens was 14% (including CRs in approximately 4% of patients) [ ]. Additionally, low-dose outpatient IL-2 and IFN-α combos produced ORRs of about 12% and a median overall survival of almost 14.5 months [ ]. Within up to 15% of mRCC patients, IL-2 or IFN-α provide objective responses, and in some situations, they continue to be the mainstay of therapy, according to a thorough study [ ].
IL-2 therapy is effective, although it is hindered by high-grade toxicities that are linked to systemic inflammation and capillary leak syndrome. PEGylated IL-2 analogs, such as bempegaldesleukin, are examples of IL-2 variations with enhanced receptor targeting that are being developed through recent bioengineering efforts to increase anticancer activity and decrease T-regulatory cell proliferation.

## Interferon-α
IFN-α, have the ability to both activate the immune system and inhibit cell proliferation. It suppresses tumor angiogenesis, improves antigen presentation, and strengthens NK and CTL function. IFN-α was employed as adjuvant therapy in melanoma clinical settings, increasing overall and relapse-free survival in high-risk resected patients (ECOG E1684 trial: HR for OS = 0.67, p = 0.01) [ ]. IFN-α monotherapy produced ORRs of 10–16% for melanoma and mRCC, whereas combination methods occasionally produced long-lasting remissions [ ]. IFN-α in conjunction with targeted medicines such as bevacizumab effectively increased tumor response rates from 12.5 to 32.4% and progression-free survival (5.5–10.2 months) in phase III trials. But because of toxicity and the introduction of more recent targeted treatments, broad use has been constrained [ ].

## Therapeutic combinations
To increase effectiveness and overcome resistance, immunotherapy is increasingly being used in combination with targeted therapy, radiation, chemotherapy, and other immune modulators. For instance, compared to monotherapy, nivolumab and ipilimumab together have demonstrated increased survival in metastatic melanoma [ ] In the case of renal cell carcinoma, ICIs and VEGF inhibitors together have demonstrated promise [ ].
Numerous cancer types exhibit responsiveness to immunotherapy; however, a significant proportion of patients either fail to respond or do not achieve sustained clinical benefit. Consequently, there is a heightened interest in identifying predictors of response to immunotherapy, as well as in developing rational combination therapies aimed at enhancing efficacy by addressing both primary and acquired resistance. Immunotherapy, and particularly immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs), has drastically transformed cancer treatment paradigms by leveraging the host immune system to target malignancies. Despite this advancement, resistance mechanisms such as tumor immune evasion, immunosuppressive tumor microenvironments, and a deficiency in tumor immunogenicity frequently impede therapeutic efficacy.To counter these challenges, extensive research is being conducted on combination therapies that merge ICIs with targeted therapies, chemotherapy, radiation, and various immune modulators. These synergistic strategies seek to bolster antitumor immune responses, reprogram the tumor microenvironment, and ultimately improve clinical outcomes.At present, numerous studies are focused on elucidating the determinants of immune non-responsiveness at the tumor site. Notably, poor lymphocytic infiltration of antigen-specific T-cells at the tumor site has been identified as a contributing factor to the suboptimal therapy responses observed with anti-CTLA-4 or anti-PD-1 antibodies [ ]. Patients exhibiting tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes that respond to neo-antigen epitopes have demonstrated superior therapeutic responses to anti-CTLA-4 treatment. Furthermore, the combination of bevacizumab (anti-VEGF) with ipilimumab has yielded promising results, reflected in improved median overall survival (OS). These findings underscore the significance of combination therapy strategies that enhance lymphocyte infilteration as a pivotal factor in improving OS outcome [ ].

## Specific immunotherapy and biomarkers
To choose patients who would most likely benefit from immunotherapy, predictive biomarkers including PD-L1 expression, tumor mutational burden (TMB), and microsatellite instability (MSI) must be found [ ].
Immunotherapeutics provide promise, yet problems still exist such as Colitis, pneumonitis, hepatitis, and endocrinopathies are examples of immune-related adverse events (irAEs) [ ]. Another is tumor heterogeneity and immune evasion, tumors can adapt by recruiting suppressive immune cells or by downregulating antigen presentation [ ] and in low- and middle-income nations, access is restricted by high expenses. Since immunotherapy produces long-lasting effects in malignancies that were once thought to be incurable, it has become a vital component of the treatment of metastatic cancer. In order to increase the range and effectiveness of these treatments, research is still being done on improving therapeutic combinations, improving accuracy using biomarkers, and overcoming resistance mechanisms.

## What immune cells do
The migration of cancer cells from the original tumor site to distant organs is known as metastatic cancer, and it accounts for over 90% of all cancer-related deaths globally [ ]. In metastatic sickness, traditional treatments such as chemotherapy, radiation, and targeted medicines often fail to achieve long-term remission due to tumor heterogeneity, resistance, and toxicity. By using the body’s immune system to identify and eliminate tumor cells, cancer immunotherapy has revolutionized the treatment of cancer during the past ten years and offered patients new hope. Adoptive cell therapy (ACT), therapeutic vaccines, cytokine-based treatments, immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs), and other immunotherapeutic approaches have demonstrated remarkable clinical success in treating a number of metastatic cancers, including melanoma, non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), renal cell carcinoma, and triple-negative breast cancer [ ].
T cells, B cells, natural killer (NK) cells, dendritic cells (DCs), myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs), and other immune cells interact dynamically with malignancies; this knowledge is necessary to optimize immunotherapeutic techniques.

## T-cell’s function in cancer immunotherapy
The fundamental building blocks of adaptive immunity are T cells, also known as T lymphocytes. In relation to cancer, Tumor-associated antigens (TAAs) presented by MHC-I molecules are recognized by CD8⁺ cytotoxic T lymphocytes, which then destroy tumor cells directly. By producing cytokines like TNF-α and IFN-γ that activate other immune cells, CD4⁺ helper T cells encourage cytotoxic reactions (Table  ). However, regulatory T cells (Tregs) are frequently increased in the tumor microenvironment (TME) and have the ability to inhibit efficient anti-tumor immunity but not all patients respond, and because of T cell depletion and immunological evasion, combination strategies are needed [ ].
 

## B cell’s function in cancer immunotherapy
B cells and their associated tertiary lymphoid structures are significantly correlated with responses to checkpoint blockade immunotherapy and serve as prognostic indicators for the overall survival of cancer patients. The diverse functional subsets of B cells encompass professional antigen-presenting cells, regulatory cells, memory populations, and antibody-secreting plasma cells. Notably, the antibodies produced by these cells can independently activate innate immune responses and facilitate the cancer immunity cycle. Consequently, B cells and the antibody responses they mediate represent a crucial, yet often underappreciated, component of the adaptive immune system, warranting further investigation in the field of oncology. B cells are now acknowledged as important participants in anti-tumor immunity, despite previously being viewed as secondary to T cells. B cells help opsonize and eliminate tumor cells by producing antibodies against tumor antigens. Further, B cells improve T cell priming by serving as antigen-presenting cells (APCs) to T cells. Furthermore, TLSs rich in B lymphocytes are linked to improved responsiveness to ICIs and a better prognosis in metastatic malignancies [ ].
Remarkably, treatments that increase TLS production or B cell activity are being investigated in order to increase immunotherapy’s effectiveness (Table  ). B cells have two functions, even though regulatory B cells (Bregs) can generate immunosuppressive cytokines like IL-10 that speed up tumor growth [ ].

## NK (Natural Killer) cell’s function in cancer immunotherapy
NK cells are a type of innate lymphoid cell capable of destroying tumor cells without prior sensitization. In the context of metastatic cancer, NK cells play a crucial role through their ability to exert direct cytotoxic effects, particularly against tumor cells lacking major histocompatibility complex class I (MHC-I) molecules, an evasion strategy commonly employed by metastatic malignancies. To enhance NK cell function, several therapeutic strategies have been developed. These include cytokine stimulation using interleukins such as IL-2 and IL-15, which boost NK cell proliferation and cytotoxic activity [ ].
Additionally, chimeric antigen receptor-NK (CAR-NK) cells have been engineered to target specific tumor antigens, offering a promising avenue for targeted immunotherapy with potentially fewer side effects than CAR-T cell therapies. NK cell engagers, which link NK cells to tumor antigens, further enhance their ability to recognize and kill cancer cells. Together, these NK-based therapeutic approaches represent an emerging and potent strategy in the immunotherapy of metastatic cancers. Despite their potential, NK cell function is frequently compromised by the immunosuppressive Tumor microenvironment (TME), requiring multifunctional techniques [ ].

## The function of other myeloid cells and dendritic cells (DCs)
Anti-tumor T cell responses are triggered by professional APCs known as dendritic cells. According to [ ], DC vaccines, such as Sipuleucel-T, which has been authorized for prostate cancer, are designed to increase DC activation against tumor antigens. More research is being done to improve immunotherapy for metastatic cancer by finding ways to promote DC maturation and antigen presentation. However, tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) and myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) encourage angiogenesis and release inhibitory cytokines (such IL-10 and TGF-β), which result in immunosuppression and metastasis [ ]. A possible alternative strategy is to target MDSCs and TAMs in order to reprogram the myeloid compartment. Even though immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) including anti-PD-1, anti-PD-L1, and anti-CTLA-4 have demonstrated impressive efficacy, only a small percentage of patients with metastatic cancer see long-lasting effects. Immunosuppressive tumor microenvironments (TME), acquired or primary resistance and absence of T cell infiltration restrict their effectiveness.
New combination therapies aim to overcome these challenges by simultaneously targeting multiple immune system components or modifying the TME to support an effective anti-tumor response.

## New combination techniques
Combination immunotherapies are being actively developed due to the complexity of the immunological landscape in metastatic cancer [ ]. ICIs (immune checkpoints Inhibitor) in conjunction with anti-angiogenic drugs (to improve T cell infiltration and normalize tumor vasculature). However, checkpoint inhibitors along with cytokine treatment (PD-1 blockage with IL-2). Additionally, immunizations combined with checkpoint blockage to improve T cell responses specific to antigens. Early-stage clinical trials indicate that appropriate combinations can significantly improve response and survival rates. The purpose of combination treatments is to increase the number of responses, extended survival, minimize escape by the immune system, increase the advantages for non-respondents [ , ].
To increase immunogenicity and therapeutic impact, strategic combination techniques that integrate ICIs, chemotherapy, or radiation are essential [ , ].

## Dual ICI therapy: checkpoint inhibitor combinations
For instance, anti-CTLA-4 plus anti-PD-1 therapy, such as nivolumab plus ipilimumab, has demonstrated synergy by focusing on distinct phases of T cell activation. In lymph nodes, CTLA-4 inhibition improves T cell priming and by inhibiting PD 1, exhausted T cells in the TME are rejuvenated. While comparing dual blockade to monotherapy, metastatic melanoma patients showed better statistical response rates (~ 58%) [ ].

## Chemotherapy plus checkpoint inhibitors
Immunogenic cell death (ICD) is a side effect of chemotherapy that triggers the immune system by releasing tumor antigens and warning signals. Additionally, it can reduce immunosuppressive cells (such MDSCs and Tregs). In comparison to chemotherapy alone, atezolizumab (anti-PD-L1) plus nab-paclitaxel demonstrated a better progression-free survival for metastatic triple-negative breast cancer [ ]. Transforms “cold” tumors into “hot” tumors. (i.e., immunologically active)

## Radiotherapy plus checkpoint inhibitors
Radiotherapy has the ability to Increase the secretion of tumor antigen, increase the number of MHC molecules and increase T cell infiltration. The “Abscopal effect” states that when one tumor location is exposed to radiation, the systemic immune system becomes activated, which causes distant, non-irradiated cancers to shrink. Combining radiation with ICIs is being explored for melanoma and metastatic lung cancer [ ].

## Targeted therapy plus checkpoint inhibitors
Immunosuppression is encouraged by specific oncogenic pathways. If lower the levels of immunosuppressive cytokines and make tumors more immunogenic. For metastatic melanoma with a BRAF mutation- BRAF inhibitors plus MEK inhibitors plus PD-1 inhibition are used. PD-1 inhibition maintains T cell function while BRAF inhibitors initially increase T cell infiltration then cause resistance [ ].

## Anti-angiogenic agents plus checkpoint inhibitors

## Immune checkpoints plus cytokine therapies
According to Charych, D. H., et al., T and NK cells should proliferate and become active due to cytokines such as IFN-α, IL-2, and IL-15 and also boost immunity against tumors [ ]. The formulations of next-generation cytokines, such as pegylated IL-2 and IL-15 superagonists are generally more pleasant to use. Combination such as ICIs and cytokines can be used together to boost immune responses without being very harmful.

## Monoclonal antibodies
Among the most important developments in immunotherapy for metastatic cancer are monoclonal antibodies (mAbs) (Table  ). By selectively binding to antigens produced on the surface of cancer cells, these lab-engineered molecules can disrupt vital signaling pathways or cause immune-mediated tumor death. Monoclonal antibodies have transformed cancer therapy and been incorporated into normal treatment plans for a variety of cancers since the 1997 approval of rituximab. There are multiple ways in which monoclonal antibodies can produce their anticancer effects [ ].

## Directly targeting tumor cells
mAbs that bind to particular tumor-associated antigens, including cetuximab (anti-EGFR) and trastuzumab (anti-HER2), can cause apoptosis, cell cycle arrest, or receptor internalization and destruction [ , ].

## Induction of immune system
Certain monoclonal antibodies (mAbs) cause antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity (ADCC) or complement-dependent cytotoxicity (CDC), in which the destruction of antibody-coated tumor cells is mediated by complement proteins or immune effector cells [ ].

## Inhibitors of immune checkpoint
Monoclonal antibodies that block inhibitory pathways and release T-cell responses, include nivolumab (anti-PD-1), pembrolizumab (anti-PD-1), and ipilimumab (anti-CTLA-4). This results in long-lasting anticancer efficacy in malignancies such as metastatic melanoma, NSCLC, RCC, and others [ ].

## Delivery of the payload
By delivering cytotoxic drugs directly to tumor cells, antibody-drug conjugates (ADCs) such as trastuzumab emtansine (T-DM1) improve selectivity and lower systemic toxicity [ ].

## Antibodies that are bispecific
Targeted cytotoxicity is made possible by these mAbs’ ability to bind two distinct antigens at once. For example, blinatumomab connects CD3 on T cells to CD19 on B-cell cancers [ ]. In HER2-positive metastatic breast cancer, trastuzumab and pertuzumab have considerably increased survival, especially when used in conjunction with taxanes [ ]. Atezolizumab and nivolumab are examples of immune checkpoint inhibitors that are currently the gold standard for metastatic non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), especially in tumors that express PD-L1 [ ]. EGFR is the target of cetuximab and panitumumab in metastatic colorectal cancer of the KRAS wild type. An anti-VEGF monoclonal antibody called bevacizumab is also used to prevent tumor angiogenesis [ , ]. Nivolumab plus ipilimumab combination therapy has shown synergistic effects, enhancing overall and progression-free survival in RCC and metastatic melanoma [ ].
The treatment of chronic lymphocytic leukemia (CLL) and metastatic or relapsed non-Hodgkin lymphoma depends heavily on rituximab (anti-CD20) [ ].

## Problems with combination therapy

Enhanced toxicity because negative immune-related effects are often cumulative or synergistic when therapies are taken together.
Biomarkers are needed for patient selection in order to predict who will benefit.
Studies are being conducted to figure out the optimal timing and sequence for different therapies termed as treatment sequencing.

## Pre-clinical studies
Recent developments in immuno-oncology have brought antigen-specific cancer immunotherapeutics (ASCI) and immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICI) to the forefront of clinical research, particularly regarding solid tumors. Table  presents a summary of key Phase 1/2 clinical trials that elucidate the immunogenicity, safety profiles, and antitumor efficacy of these agents across various cancer types.
 The trials referenced as NCT00896480 and NCT00849875 investigated the recombinant MAGE-A3 protein-based vaccine, GSK2132231A, both as a monotherapy and in conjunction with dacarbazine for patients with melanoma. These Phase 2 studies employed comprehensive endpoints, including objective tumor response (ORR), anti-MAGE-A3 antibody titers, T-cell responses, and survival outcomes [ , ]. Notably, NCT00849875 introduced mixed response (MR) criteria and time-to-treatment failure (TTF) metrics to better characterize the often non-linear and delayed responses observed with cancer immunotherapy. Despite modest patient enrollment (24 and 48 participants, respectively), these trials emphasize the critical role of integrating immune biomarkers in understanding the effects of the vaccine beyond solely radiographic evaluations. Furthermore, the investigational ASCI detailed in NCT01149343 was evaluated for early safety, seroconversion, and humoral immune responses in a cohort of 107 subjects. This Phase 1 study demonstrated that ELISA-based antibody quantification serves as a viable surrogate for immunogenicity assessment [ ]. Although it did not include efficacy endpoints, the findings reinforce the significance of antibody titers and immune activation markers in the context of first-in-human studies.
A recent trial focused on safety, identified as NCT00706238, investigated GSK1203486A in a limited cohort of five subjects to assess dose-limiting toxicities following three immunizations. While the study had a narrow scope, such micro-dose trials are essential for establishing biological dose ranges and toxicity windows in the development of vaccine-based immunotherapies [ ]. In another study, referenced as NCT00674791, the OCPM immunotherapeutic vaccine was examined, specifically targeting PRAME (preferentially expressed antigen of melanoma). This Phase 1 study involved 13 patients and concentrated on evaluating tolerability and PRAME-specific antibody responses, as well as monitoring the best overall response (BOR) in accordance with modified RECIST criteria [ ]. The findings indicated early signs of tolerability and potential antigen-specific efficacy, supporting the viability of PRAME as a cancer-testis antigen. Further, NCT04641871 reported on a Phase 1 trial assessing the combination of checkpoint inhibitors Sym021 (PD-1), Sym022 (LAG-3), and Sym023 (TIM-3), with and without irinotecan, enrolling 78 patients. This trial was designed to evaluate objective responses, high-grade adverse events (AEs), and treatment modifications, thereby illustrating the intricate balance of immune efficacy and toxicity inherent in combinatorial checkpoint strategies [ ].
In a similar vein, NCT02815995 investigated the use of durvalumab (anti-PD-L1) and tremelimumab (anti-CTLA-4) in cases of biliary tract cancer (BTC) and esophageal squamous cell carcinoma (ESCC). The primary outcomes included overall response rates (ORR) and adverse event profiles, suggesting notable clinical activity in tumors that typically exhibit resistance to monotherapy immunotherapy [ ]. These findings further emphasize the potential of dual immune checkpoint inhibitor regimens in tumors with low immunogenicity, despite the necessity for effective toxicity management.NCT00952692 and NCT01213472 explored an innovative HER2-targeted therapeutic approach combining dHER2 with AS15 immunotherapeutic and lapatinib, which merges antigen stimulation with HER2 inhibition. The primary endpoint of NCT00952692 was progression-free survival (PFS), while NCT01213472 focused on T-cell responses and toxicity assessments. Together, these trials represent a significant step in bridging targeted therapy with immune-based platforms [ , ] The dual-phase classification (Phase 1/2) underscores the ongoing exploration of the immunologic durability and safety of this novel hybrid strategy.

## Future prospects
The future trajectory of cancer therapy is poised for significant advancement through the adoption of precision-driven, combination-based strategies that synthesize insights from genomics, proteomics, immunology, and systems biology. Biomarker-driven combinations, informed by genomic, proteomic, and immunological profiling, are facilitating the targeted application of therapies, thereby enhancing treatment efficacy while minimizing superfluous toxicity. Notably, biomarkers such as PD-L1 expression, tumor mutational burden (TMB), and immune gene expression signatures are increasingly informing the selection of checkpoint inhibitors and a range of immunotherapeutic agents.
Concurrently, the implementation of customized treatment combinations that respond to the unique tumor and immune microenvironment of individual patients is becoming foundational in clinical practice. These methodologies account for factors such as the presence of suppressive immune cells, tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes, and varying cytokine profiles to dynamically tailor therapy plans. The integration of multi-omics data—employing techniques such as single-cell analysis, transcriptomics, and genome-wide profiling—is enriching our comprehension of tumor-immune interactions, thereby facilitating the rational design of combination therapies that preemptively tackle resistance mechanisms.
Innovative therapeutic platforms, including bispecific T-cell engagers (BiTEs), oncolytic viruses, and microbiome modulation, are on the horizon as next-generation tools in immuno-oncology. These inventive agents are designed to bolster immune recognition and counteract tumor evasion. In the context of challenging metastatic cancers, including melanoma, non-small cell lung cancer, and head and neck malignancies, promising future strategies encompass prime-boost regimens initiated with in situ vaccines such as T-VEC, followed by tailored neoantigen vaccines administered through mRNA or dendritic cell platforms. These approaches hold the potential to elicit robust and sustained anti-tumor immune responses.
As the field continues to evolve, forthcoming research will increasingly hinge on the principles of rational, biomarker-guided combination designs that aim to optimize therapeutic benefit while minimizing adverse effects. The utilization of real-time patient data, artificial intelligence, and adaptive clinical trial methodologies will play a crucial role in actualizing this vision. Ultimately, the confluence of multi-modal technologies and personalized immunotherapy is set to revolutionize cancer treatment, yielding more durable responses and improved patient outcomes.
The management of metastatic cancer has changed as a result of immunotherapeutics, which provide fresh hope when traditional treatments frequently fail. In a subset of patients with a variety of metastatic cancers, treatments like immune checkpoint inhibitors, adoptive cell therapies, and cancer vaccines have produced long-lasting responses by utilizing the body’s immune system, especially the activity of T cells, B cells, dendritic cells, and other immune components. However, obstacles like as immunosuppression in the tumor microenvironment, immunological-related side events, and immune resistance still prevent universal success. New approaches, including as combination treatments and tailored immunotherapy techniques, have enormous potential to improve effectiveness and go past resistance. More study is required to optimize these treatments, improve their use to more cancer types, and ultimately improve survival and long-term recovery for patients with metastatic disease. The treatment of metastatic cancer is evolving due to new immunotherapy combination techniques. Long-lasting therapeutic effects, improved tumor rejection, and resistance can all be achieved by utilizing the complementary mechanisms of many treatments. There is no doubt that ICIs represent a shift in the therapeutic paradigm for the management of metastatic illness. They offer unprecedented long-term remission for a tiny fraction of patients. However, to realize their full potential, ongoing research needs focus on overcoming resistance, lowering toxicities, enhancing biomarkers, and exploring rational combination approaches with other medications. ACT is now a promising treatment option for metastatic solid tumors, having previously been a prototype for hematologic malignancies. Even though there are still many obstacles to overcome, progress in cellular engineering, microenvironment modification, and precision targeting could make ACT a vital component in treating metastatic cancer. The benefits of vaccine monotherapy for metastatic cancer are frequently low. Immunosuppressive TMEs and complex tumor heterogeneity restrict effectiveness. Therapeutic cancer vaccines are a rapidly emerging subfield of immunotherapy for metastatic cancer. When taken appropriately, they can result in long-lasting, systemic tumor control in conjunction with checkpoint blockade and other immunomodulatory medications. Future advancements in antigen screening, combination regimens, and vaccine delivery will likely influence the direction of metastasis-targeted therapy. Cytokine-based treatments have played a role in the age of cancer immunotherapy by proving that long-lasting remissions are possible, particularly in cases of renal cell carcinoma and metastatic melanoma. Despite its toxicity, IL-2 still produces long-lasting, comprehensive responses, and IFN-α still plays a part in certain adjuvant and metastatic situations. Developments in cytokine engineering, including receptor-biased IL-2 agonists, present encouraging opportunities to increase effectiveness while lowering side effects. In conclusion, cytokine treatments continue to be an essential part of immunotherapeutic approaches, particularly when incorporated into combination treatment plans for malignancies that have spread.

## Challenges in immunotherapeutics for metastatic cancer management
Despite significant advancements in immuno-oncology, the clinical implementation of immunotherapeutics for metastatic cancer encounters a range of persistent challenges [ ]. A primary concern is the insufficient availability of reliable predictive biomarkers that can effectively identify patients who are most likely to benefit from specific immunotherapy options. While certain biomarkers, such as PD-L1 expression and tumor mutational burden, provide some insights, they frequently exhibit limitations in sensitivity and specificity across various tumor types. This situation complicates clinical decision-making and may subject patients to unnecessary adverse effects.
Furthermore, tumor heterogeneity—encompassing both inter- and intra-tumoral diversity—represents a critical obstacle to achieving effective treatment outcomes. The coexistence of multiple genetically distinct subclonal populations within a singular tumor can enable immune evasion and enhance resistance to therapeutic interventions, particularly as immunotherapy introduces selective pressures that promote clonal evolution.
The role of cancer stem cells (CSCs) in contributing to immune escape and tumor recurrence cannot be overlooked, owing to their capabilities for self-renewal and adaptability in response to the surrounding microenvironment. Additionally, the development of resistance presents a formidable challenge, often arising from the intricate signaling networks within cancer and the branched evolutionary dynamics that permit tumor cells to evade immune-mediated destruction.
These biological complexities are further exacerbated by deficiencies in clinical trial design, which frequently overlook the importance of dynamic immune-tumor interactions and fail to stratify patients adequately based on their molecular characteristics. Moreover, the high costs associated with immunotherapies limit their accessibility, imposing significant economic burdens on healthcare systems, particularly in low- and middle-income countries.
Although immunotherapeutic agents have demonstrated remarkable efficacy in a subset of patients, many malignancies remain inadequately responsive, underscoring the urgent need for universally effective and durable treatment strategies. Future endeavors should prioritize the development of personalized combination therapies, the integration of multi-omics data for improved biomarker discovery, and the design of adaptive clinical trials that accurately reflect the complexities of tumor-immune interactions. Such strategies hold promise for addressing current limitations and extending the benefits of immunotherapy to a broader patient population.
