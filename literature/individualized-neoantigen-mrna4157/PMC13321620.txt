They are broadly categorized into self-amplifying mRNA (SAM) vaccines and non-replicating mRNA vaccines. SAM vaccines contain viral replicase elements enabling intracellular RNA amplification and strong antigen expression, whereas non-replicating mRNA vaccines encode only the target antigen and rely on direct translation for immunogenicity. Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) is a highly aggressive cancer that often exhibits resistance to standard-of-care therapy and appears to benefit from being targeted with mRNA vaccines to produce an anti-tumor immune response. Herein, we review the stepwise approach of vaccine construction of mRNA vaccines, including building a DNA template, in vitro transcription, capping, polyadenylation, and purification of the mRNA, and their delivery as lipid nanoparticles (LNPs), dendritic cells, or naked RNA. In addition, we present a synthesis of progress made through the identification of HCC-specific neoantigens and high-affinity neoantigens (HANs) that activate cytotoxic T cells, correlating with better patient outcomes. Recent preclinical and clinical studies demonstrate the safety and efficacy of mRNA vaccines that encode either tumor-associated or patient-specific neoantigens, often in combination with local therapeutics or immune checkpoint inhibitors. We offer further considerations regarding combinations of mRNA vaccines with strategies including but not limited to adoptive cell therapy, tumor suppressor gene restoration, anti-angiogenesis drugs, and metabolic reprogramming. In many ways, a key consideration is the strategies being developed to overcome the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment in the liver, and mRNA-induced co-stimulation (e.g., OX40L), TP53 gene editing, and targeting of myeloid-derived suppressor cells. Overall, our review has offered perspectives on the many types of opportunistic strategies being investigated, combining mRNA vaccines to not only prime for anti-tumor immunity or alternate human sources of anti-tumor immunity but also remodel the immunologic landscape that permeates HCC. With numerous ongoing studies and rapid technology advances, mRNA-based immunotherapies are sure to transform how we manage liver cancer.

## Introduction
Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) is now the third most common cause of cancer mortality globally and accounts for over four-fifths of primary liver cancer diagnoses, with ~ 906,000 new cases and ~ 830,000 deaths recorded in 2020 [ , ]. The worldwide burden of HCC is rising due to recurrence and a poor prognosis [ ]. Although the tumor usually arises on traditional causes like chronic hepatitis B or C infection, and cirrhosis, increasingly metabolic-related liver disease and non-alcoholic steatohepatitis (NASH) are driving incidence [ , ]. While immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) and targeted agents have created pathways for therapy, they remain limited due to a severely immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment (TME) that is unique to the liver [ ]. This suggests that mRNA vaccines could be a new treatment that has the potential to not only induce tumor-specific immune responses, but could also influence how to convert the TME.
The development of mRNA vaccine platforms has progressed rapidly over the past decade; their use has gained momentum following unprecedented success against infectious diseases, such as the COVID-19 pandemic [ ]. There are several advantages to mRNA vaccines: speed of design, capacity for large-scale production, non-integrative method of expression, and inherent strong immunogenicity make them ideal platforms for cancer immunotherapy [ , ]. In HCC, where the expression of antigens is often heterogeneous with no clear immune recognition, mRNA vaccines have the capability of designing an mRNA vaccine that can deliver tumor-associated antigens, neoantigens, or co-stimulatory molecules, or those that deliver tumor suppressor genes [ , ]. These features of mRNA vaccines also allow for immunization strategies that have the potential to be highly specific and minimally toxic off-target effects [ ].
mRNA vaccines are broadly classified into self-amplifying mRNA (SAM) vaccines and non-replicating mRNA vaccines, based on their structural and functional design [ ] SAM vaccines contain genetic elements derived from viral replicons that enable intracellular amplification of the RNA after delivery, resulting in prolonged antigen expression and robust immune activation even at low doses [ – ]. In contrast, non-replicating mRNA vaccines consist solely of a single open reading frame encoding the target antigen, along with regulatory untranslated regions, a 5′ cap, and a poly(A) tail [ ]. These vaccines do not self-replicate and rely entirely on host translational machinery, often requiring higher doses but offering simpler design and improved safety profiles. Both are complex to synthesize and formulate (including important steps like in vitro transcription, capping, polyadenylation, and purification) and challenging to deliver, typically solved by using lipid nanoparticles (LNPs), dendritic cell platforms, or direct injection of naked mRNA [ ].
Substantial advances in genomic and proteomic profiling have allowed others to identify specific neoantigens for HCC, including high-affinity neoantigens (HANs) able to bind more tightly to MHC-I that also generate robust cytotoxic T-cell responses [ ]. In all, preclinical evidence suggests that mRNA vaccines delivering one or multiple neoantigens can significantly reduce tumor growth and predispose lymphoid immune infiltration [ , ]. In addition, studies utilizing multiple synergistic approaches to pair mRNA vaccines with immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs), adoptive T-cell therapy, anti-angiogenic agents, and local ablative procedures have shown enhanced clinical effects by countering processes of immune resistance [ ].
In this review, we have described the landscape of mRNA-based vaccine research in HCC, from the design, synthesis, and delivery systems to combination treatment strategies. We outlined emerging data about how these therapeutics can reprogram the immunosuppressive peritumoral microenvironment of the liver and provide a path forward to precision immunotherapy. As mRNA-based vaccines are evaluated in early-phase clinical trials, they are a new class of therapeutics and a platform for personalized and multi-modal approaches to the treatment of liver cancer.

## Hepatocellular carcinoma and current therapeutic challenges
Despite significant progress in early detection and surgical treatments, the prognosis of HCC patients remains poor, mainly due to the disease’s complexity and the limited available effective therapies. The different classification systems for HCC consider imaging results, histopathology, and molecular studies in addition to the tumor’s clinical stage, all of which can have an impact on the disease’s prognosis and course of treatment [ ].
The stage of the cancer, the patient’s overall health, and liver function all influence the treatment plan for HCC. Curative-intent options (resection, transplantation, local ablation) benefit only the minority detected early [ ]. For the numerically larger advanced-stage population, treatment pivots to systemic therapy. Sorafenib, followed a decade later by Lenvatinib, set the standard for tyrosine-kinase inhibitors (TKIs), but each confers a modest survival benefit and eventually meets drug resistance [ ]. Immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs), such as nivolumab, pembrolizumab, and camrelizumab, have entered the clinic, either alone or combined with anti-VEGF agents; yet, overall response rates hover in the 15–30% range. The atezolizumab-bevacizumab doublet has lifted outcomes further, but durable control is still confined to a subset of patients [ , ].
Several hurdles blunt these therapies. First is the intrinsic heterogeneity of HCC and different molecular and genetic traits, which may result in treatment resistance and make it challenging to pinpoint a single therapeutic target. Second, the most challenging aspect of molecular-targeted therapy for HCC is identifying highly mutated and druggable targets; new modalities may be necessary to combat interfering mutated genes. Third, the absence of reliable molecular biomarkers to forecast medication effectiveness and choose suitable treatment plans [ ]. Fourth, an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment, which is rich in regulatory T cells, myeloid-derived suppressor cells, and inhibitory cytokines, stifles effector immunity, limiting ICI efficacy. Finally, progressive on-treatment resistance to TKIs is frequent, reflecting adaptive signaling and stromal rewiring [ – ].
Taken together, current modalities leave a sizeable therapeutic gap, underscoring the urgency for interventions that can bypass immune resistance and be tailored to tumor biology. Therapeutic vaccines, especially mRNA platforms, offer that possibility: they can encode personalized neo or tumor-associated antigens, rapidly re-engineered, and synergize with ICIs to convert “cold” tumors into “hot” ones [ , ]. Exploring mRNA vaccines, therefore, represents a logical next step in closing the efficacy gap in HCC.

## Types, synthesis, and delivery of mRNA vaccine
In HCC, the tumor immune microenvironment (TIME) is generally immunosuppressive, enriched in regulatory T cells (Tregs), myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs), and M2-polarized macrophages that create a barrier to effective anti-tumor immunity (Fig. ) [ ]. The potential for mRNA vaccines to remodel this immune environment towards immune activation is very appealing. A mechanism for this remodeling involves macrophage repolarization, wherein mRNA vaccines stimulate the secretion of cytokines and inflammatory mediators that polarize tumor-promoting M2 macrophages towards pro-inflammatory M1 macrophages. The transition from M2 to M1 polarization can increase the secretion of IL-12 and TNF-α that lead to immune activation and reduce barriers for T-cell infiltrates [ ].

 

mRNA vaccines have been shown to also potentiate T helper cell function by initiating the release of cytokines such as IFN-γ and IL-2, which have a variety of effects to enhance cytotoxic T lymphocyte (CTL) function, improve antigen presentation, and promote and maintain effector T-cell viability [ ]. This has particular relevance for HCC, where T-cell exhaustion limits the establishment of durable immunity (Fig. ) [ ]. Another possible mechanism is due to the maturation of dendritic cells (DCs). The inherent adjuvant properties of mRNA recognized by Toll-like receptors (TLR3, TLR7/8) initiate NF-κB signaling, resulting in the upregulation of co-stimulatory molecules (CD80, CD86) and inflammatory cytokines [ ]. This promotes efficient priming of naïve T cells and CTL differentiation. Furthermore, NF-kB activation strengthens this network of cytokines affecting T-cell recruitment and function, increasing Th1 polarization in an IL-12–dependent manner, while TNF-α reduces the function of immunosuppressive stromal elements [ ]. Interestingly, vaccine-induced CTLs are capable of overcoming tumor-induced immune evasion mechanisms, including downregulation of MHC class I, primarily because of the ability of DCs to present antigen in the presence of a strong CTL “cytotoxic pressure” (Fig. ) [ ]. Overall, these mechanisms outline how mRNA vaccines are reprogramming the immunosuppressive TME of HCC to an immunosupportive TME and enhancing T-cell functions in support of tumor eradications.

## Classification of mRNA vaccines
mRNA vaccines can be broadly classified into two major categories: self-amplifying mRNA vaccines (SAM) and non-replicating mRNA vaccines [ ]. These classifications differ based on their structural characteristics and how they interact within the body.

## Self-amplifying mRNA vaccines (SAM)
Self-amplifying mRNA vaccines represent a sophisticated design wherein the mRNA used in the vaccine includes sequences that allow the mRNA to replicate itself once inside the host cells [ ]. This self-replication capability is derived from the genome of a virus. However, the design ensures that the virus does not replicate itself or produce viral proteins, ensuring the safety of the vaccine. The major advantage of self-amplifying mRNA vaccines is that they require lower doses to achieve the same immune response, as the replication process increases the amount of mRNA in the body, thereby enhancing antigen expression [ ]. This type of vaccine can stimulate a stronger immune response with fewer doses and is considered more efficient compared to non-replicating vaccines.

## Non-replicating mRNA vaccines
In contrast, non-replicating mRNA vaccines contain full-length mRNA encoding an antigen of interest, which is directly introduced into the body [ ]. This type of mRNA vaccine does not replicate in the body but relies on the host’s cells to express the antigen encoded by the mRNA. These vaccines generally contain the full-length mRNA along with a 5’ cap structure and a 3’ poly(A) tail, which are critical for stability and translation efficiency [ ]. While simpler in design, non-replicating mRNA vaccines can have some drawbacks, including a short half-life and lower in vivo antigen expression compared to SAM vaccines [ ]. These limitations may necessitate higher doses to elicit a sufficient immune response.

## Synthesis and manufacturing processes of mRNA vaccines
The synthesis of mRNA vaccines is a complex multi-step process that involves the creation of DNA templates, in vitro transcription (IVT), capping, polyadenylation, and purification [ ]. The goal is to produce stable and effective mRNA that can be used as a vaccine.

## Designing DNA templates
The first step in the synthesis of an mRNA vaccine is the creation of a DNA template that encodes the desired antigen. This DNA template must include an open reading frame (ORF), which encodes the protein that will be expressed as the antigen [ ]. Additionally, the DNA template should include the necessary 5’ untranslated region (UTR) and 3’ UTR sequences, which play critical roles in regulating the stability and translation of the mRNA. The 5’ UTR helps in stabilizing the mRNA and promoting its efficient translation by the ribosome, while the 3’ UTR can enhance stability by controlling degradation processes [ ].
For self-amplifying mRNA vaccines, the DNA template also includes a long ORF, which is necessary for the replication of the mRNA once inside the host cells. The DNA template is constructed in a plasmid vector that can be used in the in vitro transcription process to generate mRNA [ ].

## In vitro transcription (IVT)
In vitro transcription is the key method used to synthesize mRNA from the DNA template. During IVT, RNA polymerase synthesizes mRNA by copying the DNA template strand. This process involves several key components: nucleotides (the building blocks of RNA), enzymes (such as RNA polymerase), and buffers to maintain the appropriate environment for transcription [ ].
IVT is typically performed under controlled laboratory conditions, where the DNA template is used to generate a complementary RNA strand [ ]. One important feature of the IVT process is ensuring that the mRNA is capped at the 5’ end and polyadenylated at the 3’ end. These modifications are essential for the mRNA’s stability and function in the body.

## Capping and polyadenylation
Capping the mRNA at the 5’ end is crucial for protecting the mRNA from exonuclease degradation and for enabling its efficient translation [ ]. The cap structure, typically a m7Gppp structure (where Gppp represents a guanosine triphosphate molecule), serves as a shield against degradation and assists in the initiation of translation by the ribosome. Capping is typically performed enzymatically during the IVT process, where RNA 5’-triphosphatase (RTPase) hydrolyzes the 5 5’-triphosphate, and then guanylyltransferase (GTase) adds the guanosine monophosphate (GMP) to form the cap structure [ ].
The poly(A) tail is added at the 3’ end of the mRNA to stabilize the molecule and facilitate translation. The poly(A) tail consists of adenine residues (typically 120–150 nucleotides) that are added enzymatically or through a DNA template with a fixed-length poly(A) sequence [ ]. The poly(A) tail plays an important role in regulating the degradation of the mRNA, its export from the nucleus, and its translation efficiency.

## Purification
After the mRNA has been synthesized and modified, the next step is purification. The purification of mRNA is critical to ensure that the final vaccine product is free from impurities and contaminants that could affect its safety and efficacy [ , ]. Purification techniques typically include chromatographic methods such as high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC), ion exchange chromatography, size exclusion chromatography, and Fast Protein Liquid Chromatography (FPLC) [ ]. These methods separate the mRNA from other unwanted molecules, such as unincorporated nucleotides, enzymes, and other by-products of the transcription process.
Purification is necessary to remove immunogenic components that could trigger unwanted immune responses in the body. The purified mRNA is then ready for formulation and delivery in the vaccine [ ].

## Delivery mechanisms of mRNA vaccines
Effective delivery of mRNA vaccines is one of the most critical challenges in the development of this technology. The mRNA must be protected from degradation, efficiently delivered into cells, and then translated into antigenic proteins to trigger the desired immune response [ ]. Several delivery systems are available for mRNA vaccines, with carrier-based systems, naked mRNA delivery, and dendritic cell-mRNA delivery systems (DCs-mRNA) being the most commonly employed methods.

## Carrier-based delivery system
Carrier-based systems use different materials to encapsulate the mRNA and facilitate its delivery to cells. The two primary categories of carrier molecules used for mRNA delivery are viral carriers and non-viral carriers. However, viral carriers have limitations, including potential immunogenicity and tumorigenicity, which have made non-viral carriers more attractive for mRNA vaccine delivery [ ].
Among non-viral carriers, lipid nanoparticles (LNPs) are the most widely used system. LNPs are composed of a mixture of ionizable lipids, cholesterol, and polyethylene glycol (PEG)-lipids [ ]. These nanoparticles encapsulate the mRNA, protecting it from degradation and aiding its entry into cells. The size and composition of LNPs allow them to closely mimic viral particles, making them effective in cellular uptake [ ]. The major advantage of LNPs is their high delivery efficiency, biocompatibility, and low immunogenicity. However, LNPs can be prone to degradation and instability during storage, posing challenges for long-term storage and distribution of mRNA vaccines [ ].

## Naked mRNA delivery
Naked mRNA delivery refers to the direct administration of mRNA without encapsulating it in a carrier molecule [ ]. This method has the advantage of being simple and potentially faster to develop, as it avoids the need for complex formulations. Naked mRNA delivery is typically performed through methods such as intradermal or intranodal injections, where the mRNA is directly delivered to antigen-presenting cells (APCs) in the skin or lymph nodes. One of the key benefits of naked mRNA delivery is that the mRNA does not integrate into the genome, reducing the risk of genetic mutations [ ].
However, naked mRNA delivery has limitations, including its instability and vulnerability to degradation before it reaches the target cells. The lack of a carrier system can also lead to inefficient protein translation, limiting the effectiveness of the vaccine [ ]. To overcome these issues, chemical modifications to the mRNA, such as the addition of stabilizing agents or optimized administration methods, are being researched.

## Dendritic cell-mRNA delivery system (DCs-mRNA)
Dendritic cells (DCs) are specialized immune cells that play a key role in initiating the adaptive immune response. Given their ability to process and present antigens, DCs are an ideal target for mRNA vaccine delivery [ ]. The goal of DC-mRNA delivery systems is to deliver mRNA encoding the target antigen directly into dendritic cells to stimulate a robust immune response [ ].
This delivery can be achieved through techniques like electroporation, lipid-based transfection, and in vitro acoustic evaporation, with electroporation being the preferred method due to its high transfection efficacy [ ]. Once the mRNA is delivered into DCs, the cells process the encoded antigen and present it to T cells, triggering both CD8 + cytotoxic T cell responses and CD4 + helper T cell responses [ , ]. DCs are also involved in producing cytokines and chemokines that further enhance the immune response.

## HCC-specific neoantigen and immune activation
Within the past few years, dramatic advances in genomic and molecular studies have provided considerable new information regarding the genomic landscape of HCC, one of the most lethal consequences of cancer worldwide [ ]. Several key mutations, pathways, and prognostic markers have been discovered in association with the development and progression of HCC. Research areas on neoantigens (tumor-specific antigens arising from mutations in tumor DNA) are important to study.
Importantly, genomic studies of HCC tumor samples suggest patients develop many somatic mutations, meaning they are generating potentially hundreds of neoantigens. For example, one study employed RNA-seq and prediction servers to find that HCC patients developed 9–15 [ , ] theoretically possible neoantigens. However, what is clear is that little to no evidence has been shown to demonstrate their actual immunogenicity. Most studies rely solely on computational data, lacking experimental evidence to support their claims. While proteomic studies utilizing mass spectrometry to detect neoantigen peptides bound to HLA molecules have been attempted, the authors have had mixed success. The source of potential confusion is the extreme predominance of self-antigens in tumor tissue [ ].
Despite these challenges, some experimental studies have demonstrated success in showing that tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs) can recognize neoantigens in the respective tumors of the same patients [ ]. Specifically, neoantigen-specific T cells have been isolated from tumor tissues and peripheral blood, suggesting the feasibility of neoantigen-centered immunotherapy. These experimental studies generate an impetus for more experimental clinical validation and offer a pathway for the development of personalized neoantigen-based immunotherapy for HCC [ ].
Genomic sequencing in HCC patients also elucidated a range of mutations and mutated pathways that contribute to the process of tumorigenesis. In particular, mutations in the TERT promoter (which activate telomerase expression) and other alterations in pathways such as WNT/β-Catenin, PI3K/AKT/mTOR, and TP53 were found to be especially relevant for the progression of HCC. They were associated with modulating not only the proliferation of cancer cells [ , ] but also contributing to the development of a profoundly immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment, which can impact potential neoantigen-based therapies.

## Prognostic controversies of tumor mutational burden (TMB) and neoantigen load in HCC
Although the genetic modifications and mutations within HCC have been well defined, the prognostic value of TMB and neoantigen load to predict outcomes for patients is less concrete [ – ]. Some studies report that a high TMB correlates with a poor prognosis, as it may reflect the progression of the tumor and immune escape mechanisms. For instance, a retrospective study of 128 patients with HCC suggested that high TMB was associated with poorer overall survival (OS) after radical hepatectomy [ ]. However, other studies show inconsistencies. Liu et al. reported that higher levels of TMB were related to improved OS in patients with HCC, reflecting a better immune response in patients with high mutational loads [ ]. This variation could be due to differences in patient population, tumor staging, particular treatments, or the methods used to evaluate TMB. In addition, studies have not shown a consistent relationship between high neoantigen burden and progression-free survival (PFS) among HCC patients who did not receive immunotherapy, which refutes the use of neoantigen load as a prognostic biomarker among HCC patients without immunotherapy [ ]. Differences in patient population, including the underlying etiology of HCC (e.g., HBV infection vs. metabolic derangement), may add variability to the assessments.
Although the correlation of TMB and neoantigen burden with prognosis has been challenging, more recent studies suggest that specific neoantigen subsets, particularly neoantigens derived from TP53 mutations, may have potential prognostic and therapeutic utility in HCC [ , ]. These neoantigens indicated improved OS and greater anti-tumor immunity by having higher immune scores and greater T cell infiltration [ ].

## High-affinity neoantigens and immune evasion in HCC
Among the different neoantigens detected in HCC, high-affinity neoantigens (HANs) have gained interest for their affiliation with better OS and more effective anti-tumor responses [ ]. AUG (high-affinity neoantigens - HANs) bind with high affinity to MHC-I molecules, which are crucial for presenting tumor antigens to CD8 + T cells. Robust binding to MHC-I is required to maximize the identification of tumor antigens and trigger an immune response [ ]. HANs differ from other neoantigens that bind with lower affinity and fail to elicit similar immune responses.
The prediction of HANs via WES-based process using tools such as NetMHCpan allows investigators and clinicians alike the opportunity to find the specific immunogeneic targets associated at a rapid pace [ ]. HANs activate and increase the proliferation of CD8 + T cells, leading to improved cytotoxic lymphocyte infiltration and corresponding better anti-tumor responses in HCC [ ]. Furthermore, the presence of HANs in patients with HCC has also demonstrated improved responses to ICI therapies, indicating that HANs could be potential biomarkers for personalized immunotherapy applications in HCC [ ]. By combining neoantigen vaccines that target HANs with ICIs (including anti-PD-1 therapies), we could greatly promote immune responses in HCC by overcoming immune evasion. Previous preclinical and early phase clinical studies have shown that personalized neoantigen vaccines can work in conjunction with ICIs to enhance T cell activation and infiltration into tumors and ultimately lead to improved therapeutic outcomes.

## Preclinical and clinical progress of mRNA vaccines in HCC
Numerous preclinical and clinical studies have demonstrated the feasibility of mRNA vaccines for cancer treatment. Some categories of mRNA cancer vaccines are in several phases of development, each employing different approaches to target tumor cells. For instance, created mRNA encoding the T-cell co-stimulator OX40L using lipid nanoparticles (LNP). Considering that OX40L is present on the surface of cells and is involved in the co-stimulation of T cells, when administered intramuscularly to syngeneic HCC-bearing mice, this vaccine markedly reduced tumour growth and increased CD4 + and CD8 + T cells in the tumour and spleen, proposing an innovative treatment method for HCC immunotherapy by harnessing mRNA vaccines [ ]. Targeted LNPs have also been used to co-deliver multiple mRNAs. For example, Fu et al. discovered the proteins CRHBP and CFHR3, which are linked to HCC, and encapsulated their mRNAs in SP94-peptide-decorated LNPs. With outstanding biocompatibility, this dual-mRNA LNP vaccine significantly inhibited the growth of orthotopic HCC tumours [ ]. A recent study has reported on the efficacy of a TriMix mRNA that encodes CD40 ligand, CD70, and a continuously active variant of TLR4, in stimulating dendritic cells (DCs). This activation, subsequently, produces an environment that attracts and stimulates T-cells. Moreover, the combined administration of tumor antigen mRNA and TriMix in mice with HCC results in the recruitment of CD4 + and CD8 + T cells that specifically recognize the antigen. In comparison to administering antigen mRNA alone, the simultaneous delivery of TriMix along with antigen mRNA greatly enhances the generation of antigen-specific T cells [ ].
Concurrently, novel tumour antigens (such as AURKA, CDC25C, KPNA2, MCM3, NEK2, and TUBG1) have been identified as promising targets for mRNA vaccines through in silico analyses of patient HCC cohorts [ ]. These preclinical advances leverage key technologies: optimized in vitro transcribed (IVT) mRNAs with nucleoside modifications (e.g., N^1-methyl pseudouridine) that enhance stability and translation and diminish innate sensing, together with advanced LNP delivery systems [ ]. HCC-specific targeting (e.g., HCC-homing peptides on LNPs) further concentrates vaccine payloads in tumors [ ]. These innovations have elicited robust, antigen-specific T-cell responses and tumor suppression in multiple HCC models.
Cancer vaccines are typically given in a therapeutic context, in contrast to preventative immunization methods for infectious illnesses. Clinical trials of mRNA vaccines in HCC are now underway, each using distinct strategies to target cancer cells. A personalized neoantigen mRNA vaccine was administered after local ablation in one phase II study. Impressively, all 12 vaccinated patients remained alive at four years, with 12- and 24-month recurrence rates of ~ 18% and 36% versus 33% and 51% in unvaccinated controls [ ]. In the Phase I/II trial (NCT03480152) testing a multi-epitope LNP-mRNA vaccine, only mild (grade 1–2) adverse events were reported (no grade ≥ 3 toxicity), and the vaccine induced neoantigen-specific CD8+ and CD4+ T-cell responses in the majority of patients. Interestingly, of the neoantigens examined, around 15.7% triggered targeted T cell responses. In this group, 59% of the neoantigens were identified as CD4 epitopes, whereas the other 41% were recognized as CD8 epitopes. These results concerning the vaccine’s immunogenicity indicate the possibility of treating HCC patients using a combination of the vaccine with other immune modulators, like checkpoint inhibitors [ ]. These clinical studies encode either shared HCC antigens or patient-specific neoepitopes: examples include oncofetal or tumor-associated antigens (e.g., glypican-3, AFP, hTERT, cancer-testis antigens) [ , ]. Notably, combination regimens are being explored. For instance, NCT05192460 (PGV002) is evaluating a neoantigen LNP-mRNA vaccine alone and with anti-PD-1/PD-L1 therapy. This research includes individuals with advanced liver cancer, esophageal cancer, and gastric cancer. Specifically, it is an investigator-led, single-site, open-label, single-arm exploratory trial that includes both a dose escalation phase and a dose expansion phase. During the dose escalation stage, patients are given the neoantigen tumor vaccine by itself. Drawing from safety and efficacy data from this phase, the dose expansion phase is carried out at the clinical dose designated by the investigator. In this stage, the therapy includes a mix of the neoantigen tumor vaccine and PD-1/L1 blockers. The goal is to continue assessing the effectiveness and safety characteristics of the neoantigen tumor vaccine at a designated dose in conjunction with PD-1/L1 inhibitors [ ]. In the open, one-arm trial NCT05761717, a custom mRNA tumor vaccine containing neonatal antigens is being used in conjunction with Sintilimab injection to assess the safety and effectiveness of preventing the recurrence of HCC following surgery in the additional treatment phase. This research follows a 3 + 3 dose escalation methodology. Trial participants will receive six cycles of a personalized cancer vaccine at 21-day intervals. In another Phase I study (NCT05738447), an mRNA vaccine was created for patients with advanced HCC who have the hepatitis B virus, identifying anti-HBV as a potential target for HCC. Crucially, no dose-limiting toxicities or severe immune-related events have been reported thus far [ ]. New research has taken mRNA vaccine technology for HCC from conception to preliminary clinical testing. The ability of LNP-delivered mRNAs encoding tumour antigens or immunomodulators to disrupt liver-tumor immune tolerance and regulate tumour growth is confirmed by preclinical models. On-target immune activation and favourable safety are demonstrated in early human trials. These investigations set the stage for more extensive studies that will ascertain whether mRNA immunisation, either by itself or in conjunction with checkpoint blockade, improves outcomes for patients with HCC. ## Combining mRNA vaccines with other therapies in hepatocellular carcinoma
Over the past few years, mRNA vaccines have attracted significant attention in cancer therapy, particularly for their ability to trigger targeted immune responses. However, in HCC, using these vaccines on their own has not been enough to overcome the disease’s complex immune barriers. As a result, researchers are now exploring how these vaccines might perform better when used alongside other therapeutic strategies [ , , ].
Checkpoint inhibitors have emerged as a key component of cancer immunotherapy, and mRNA vaccines, which can elicit both humoral and cellular immunity, are a perfect match for ICIs. mRNA vaccines can aid the immune system in identifying and targeting tumor cells, while checkpoint inhibitors, such as anti-PD-1 or anti-CTLA-4, can release the brakes that typically suppress immune activity. These two strategies function in concert, with one alerting the immune system to the tumor and the other unleashing it. Preclinical results indicate that this combination may enhance tumor control and prolong patient survival [ , , ]. Sahin et al. showed that FixVac, a melanoma-targeting mRNA vaccine, restored sensitivity even in patients who had previously developed resistance to checkpoint blockade when used in conjunction with PD-1 inhibitors. More than half of the 89 patients in this phase I trial experienced favorable outcomes, including objective tumor regression and, in certain cases, total remission [ ]. Similarly, the mRNA-4157/V940 vaccine, used in combination with pembrolizumab, significantly reduced recurrence risk compared to ICI monotherapy. Preclinical models further support these findings, showing enhanced T-cell activation when mRNA vaccines are paired with anti-CTLA-4 therapy, surpassing the efficacy of either strategy alone [ ]. Innovations such as co-delivery of small interfering RNAs (siRNAs) targeting immunosuppressive pathways within the same mRNA vector offer additional opportunities to refine this combinatorial approach [ ]. Taken together, the mRNA–ICI combination harnesses complementary mechanisms: antigen priming by the vaccine and immune unleashing by ICIs. As early trials reveal durable responses and immune reinvigoration, this strategy holds great potential for transforming the immunotherapeutic landscape of solid tumors, including hepatocellular carcinoma.
Loss-of-function mutations in tumor suppressor genes (TSGs) such as PTEN, TP53, and RB are central to cancer initiation, progression, and therapy resistance. Traditional gene therapy approaches aimed at restoring TSG activity have faced major obstacles, including risks of genomic integration and delivery inefficiencies [ , ]. mRNA vaccines offer a compelling alternative, enabling transient, non-integrative expression of wild-type TSGs in cancer cells. Preclinical studies have validated this approach: for example, PTEN-mRNA delivered via PEGylated lipid–polymer nanoparticles successfully restored PTEN expression in deficient prostate and melanoma models, leading to suppression of the PI3K/Akt pathway and induction of apoptosis, even in ICI-resistant settings [ , ]. While still in early phases of exploration, the ability of mRNA vaccines to function both as immune activators and gene-replacement tools marks a promising step toward next-generation oncogene therapies, particularly in tumors driven by TSG loss.
Another strategy gaining ground involves combining mRNA vaccines with anti-angiogenic drugs, such as bevacizumab. These drugs modify blood vessel growth in tumors, which not only hampers the tumor’s nutrient supply but also allows immune cells easier access. When used together, the vaccine primes the immune response, and the anti-angiogenic agent helps create a more favorable environment for that response to act [ , ].
Interestingly, even traditional local treatments like radiofrequency ablation (RFA) or transarterial chemoembolization (TACE) can have an immune component. These therapies often lead to the release of tumor antigens as cancer cells die. If an mRNA vaccine is given shortly after such procedures, it might reinforce the immune system’s ability to recognize and eliminate remaining cancer cells [ ].
In HCC, a major barrier to effective immunotherapy lies in the tumor microenvironment, which often suppresses T cell function and prevents sustained immune activation. A growing area of interest focuses on bile acid metabolism as a modifiable factor in this immunosuppressive niche. Studies have shown that hydrophobic bile acids such as deoxycholic acid (DCA) and lithocholic acid (LCA) impair CD8⁺ T cell function through oxidative and ER stress pathways. In contrast, the hydrophilic bile acid ursodeoxycholic acid (UDCA) exerts immunoprotective effects. By reshaping the bile acid profile, either pharmacologically or through dietary modulation, researchers have observed improved T cell viability, metabolic adaptability, and cytotoxic activity. When such metabolic reprogramming is paired with the antigen-specific power of mRNA vaccines, the tumor microenvironment becomes more amenable to immune attack. Preliminary findings suggest this combination could also enhance the response to immune checkpoint inhibitors, offering a multifaceted strategy to overcome immune resistance in liver cancer [ ].
Perhaps the most intriguing is the preliminary data indicating that mRNA vaccines could improve the efficacy of cell-based treatments. A promising tactic to improve anti-tumor immunity is the combination of mRNA vaccines and adoptive cell therapies, specifically CAR-T and TCR-T. While mRNA vaccines can broadly activate both innate and adaptive immune responses, potentially enhancing tumor recognition and immunologic memory, CAR-T and TCR-T therapies offer targeted cytotoxicity against tumor antigens. When these modalities are administered together, they may work in concert: the vaccine primes the immune system, and engineered T cells launch targeted attacks [ , ]. Although still in the early stages of research, preclinical studies have demonstrated that mRNA-transfected CAR-T cells (e.g., targeting c-Met or mesothelin) can persist longer, trigger localized immune activation, and avoid severe toxicity. Additionally, mRNA can be used to reprogram tumor-associated dendritic cells or encode co-stimulatory signals, further supporting T cell function. This combination offers a highly targeted, flexible, and repeatable platform that may overcome the limitations of monotherapies in solid tumors like HCC [ , ]. Additionally, a recent study found that mRNA vaccines could help CAR-T cells survive longer and work better against solid tumors like HCC [ ]. Similarly, mRNA can be used to introduce tumor-specific signals to dendritic cell therapies, which train a patient’s immune cells to recognize cancer [ ]. Lastly, the broader biological impact of these combinations is starting to become clearer. For instance, combining mRNA vaccines with immune-boosting treatments has been shown to increase T cell infiltration in tumors and may help prevent early relapse [ , ].
Macrophages play a key role in shaping tumor immunity. Yang et al. (2023) [ ] found that engineering them into chimeric antigen receptor macrophages (CAR-Ms) via lipid nanoparticle (LNP) delivery of CAR mRNA enhances tumor-targeted phagocytosis (Fig. ). These LNPs, coated with plasma proteins, preferentially reach liver macrophages. Co-delivery of Siglec-GΔITIMs mRNA alleviates CD24-mediated suppression, further boosting CAR-M activity. In HCC mouse models, this dual approach increased macrophage phagocytosis, reduced tumor burden, and prolonged survival [ ]. This strategy highlights a promising, adaptable platform for HCC therapy that merits clinical evaluation.

 

Altogether, these findings point to a future in which mRNA vaccines do not operate in isolation, but rather serve as part of a broader, carefully designed treatment strategy tailored to the patient’s tumor biology.

## Overcoming immunosuppression in the liver tumor microenvironment
The liver TME immunosuppressive environment is the main obstacle to effective immunotherapy of HCC [ ]. This suppression is closely controlled by an immune regulatory cell network, immune checkpoint molecules, aberrant antigen presentation, and metabolic limitations, synergistically suppressing anti-tumor immunity. mRNA immunotherapies have shown promising potential as twin inducers of potent anti-tumor immunity and TME reprogramming (Table ) [ ].

 

One path to preventing immune escape in HCC is to provide mRNA encoding for co-stimulatory molecules, including OX40L, a key activator of T cell activation. Intratumoral administration of lipid nanoparticle-encapsulated OX40L mRNA in mouse models significantly promoted CD4⁺ and CD8⁺ T cell activation and tumor microenvironmental infiltration and resulted in efficient inhibition of tumor growth and survival [ ]. This strategy was specifically centered on the HCC TME co-stimulatory signaling defect, hence stimulating immune cells locally to induce potent anti-tumor immunity. Compared to traditional tumor antigen recognition-based mRNA vaccines, the OX40L-based strategy enhances T cell responses through the imposition of activation pathways at the tumor site. Underlying this extrinsic immune stimulation is a second strategy focusing on the correction of tumor-intrinsic immune evasive mechanisms. Loss of function of tumor suppressor genes like TP53 is frequent in HCC and has been associated with immunosuppressive phenotypes [ ]. Correction of p53 function with a CXCR4-targeted mRNA nanoparticle system reprogrammed the immune TME, enhancing antigen presentation and enabling T cell infiltration. In combination with anti-PD-1 therapy, this regimen synergistically enhanced tumor control, surpassing either monotherapy [ ]. These data set up the possibility of mRNA platforms to modulate immune effectors and cancer cells simultaneously, to provide a multifaceted approach to overcoming immune suppression. In addition to immunofunctional reprogramming of cancer and immune cells, successful mRNA vaccine design is dependent on the detection of suitable cancer antigens and personalized immune microenvironments of patients. Bioinformatics integration disclosed a cascade of potential mRNA vaccine targets in HCC, including AURKA, CDC25C, and MCM3, whose high tumor-specificity is coupled with association with adverse prognosis and high antigen-presenting cell infiltration. Furthermore, immune phenotyping of HCC patients into immune subtypes demonstrated differential levels of immunosuppression between groups, with some subtypes (e.g., IS1 and IS5) exhibiting a more immune-excluded phenotype [ ]. These findings underscore the usefulness of immune stratification for the prediction of vaccine responsiveness and prompt the translation of precision immunotherapy strategies to liver cancer. Aside from immune and genetic processes, the liver’s particular metabolic microenvironment is also instrumental in sustaining immunosuppression. Accumulating data indicate that bile acids are central to their role in controlling intrahepatic immunity, with each bile acid species inhibiting T cell activity and vaccine-induced responses. Alteration of the bile acid composition, through increases in UDCA concentrations, has been demonstrated to remodel the TME and re-stimulate T cell function [ ]. Such metabolic reprogramming tactics could represent useful adjuncts to mRNA immunization, particularly in the context of liver-tropic immunoregulatory circuits. Another element of immunosuppressive TME is the composition of specialized myeloid cells, including some tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs). A novel CD19⁺ subset of TAMs in HCC was revealed to have strong PD-L1 and CD73 expression, increased oxidative metabolism, and defective phagocytosis, all the hallmarks of highly active immunosuppressive activity [ ]. Targeting these cells with anti-CD19 CAR-T cells or pharmacologic inhibition of CD73 significantly augmented immune checkpoint blockade, which suggests that selective reprogramming or depletion of immunosuppressive myeloid subsets has the potential to improve the efficacy of mRNA vaccines and other immunotherapies. Together, these reports cite a common principle: HCC immunosuppression is regulated by a multi-modality approach. Co-stimulation through OX40L, tumor-intrinsic gene editing, precision antigen targeting, metabolic regulation, and myeloid cell targeting each disrupts a different segment of the immunosuppressive barrier. Of note, mRNA platforms provide the versatility to combine these strategies; single- or multi-modality, allowing for rational design of personalized and synergistic therapies. Clinical translation in the future will be supported by additional delivery system optimization, biomarker-stratified patient selection, and optimized combination regimens according to the complexity of the TME in the liver.

## Toward personalized mRNA cancer vaccines by artificial intelligence (AI)
In recent years, personalized mRNA vaccines have emerged as a promising therapeutic modality, particularly in preventing postoperative recurrence by eliciting specific immune responses against tumor neoantigens [ ]. These vaccines are developed by sequencing tumor tissue to identify patient-specific somatic mutations, which are then used to generate neoantigens that can stimulate cytotoxic T cell responses [ ]. The mRNA is designed to encode these neoantigens and is usually delivered via lipid nanoparticles (LNPs) for efficient uptake and expression by antigen-presenting cells [ ]. A first-in-human phase I clinical trial evaluated an autologous dendritic cell vaccine (LK101) loaded with patient-specific mRNA following ablation therapy in 24 HCC patients. The vaccine was well-tolerated with only grade 1–2 local reactions and demonstrated a notable reduction in recurrence rates compared to the control group (18.2% vs. 33.3% at one year) [ ]. Despite these advances, several challenges remain. These include high production costs, which can exceed $100,000 per patient [ ], complexities in neoantigen selection and GMP-grade manufacturing, and stringent regulatory approval processes. Additionally, personalized vaccine production often requires several weeks to months, limiting its rapid deployment in aggressive cancers. Nevertheless, ongoing advancements in AI-driven neoantigen prediction, high-throughput mRNA synthesis, and scalable LNP delivery platforms are helping to reduce costs and accelerate production timelines [ ].
The field of mRNA cancer vaccine therapy is poised to make a significant breakthrough with the integration of artificial intelligence (AI), paving the way for personalized and precision immunotherapy. The traditional methods of vaccine development are often slow, expensive, and generic. AI is capable of exploiting relevant data to develop personalized vaccines by discovering patient-specific tumor molecular features. AI allows for the rapid construction of complex biological features from genomic, proteomic, and transcriptomic data; consequently, it can predict patient-specific tumor neoantigens with unmatched accuracy [ , ]. Neoantigens are molecular identifiers of cancer cells; thus, mRNA vaccines can be developed to generate an immune response against each neoantigen for each cancer patient.
AI improves every step of the vaccine pipeline, from design to delivery. In vaccine design, AI-based machine learning models can model immune cellular responses and help to optimize the mRNA sequence to achieve faster and stronger expression and immunogenicity, which can shorten the preclinical trial duration [ ]. AI continues to help with formulation as it has been integrated into the development of lipid nanoparticles (LNP) that deliver mRNA-based therapies. This will allow rapid development of safer and more effective delivery systems, which have large implications for the therapeutic effectiveness of mRNA vaccines. In addition to formulation, AI has a significant influence on the design and conduct of clinical trials. Specifically, it assists in patient stratification, identifying response biomarker candidates, and real-time outcome recording to promote adaptive trials [ ]. In the future, continuous learning systems may further improve on existing patient treatment strategies by effectively updating them as more patient data accumulates, thus enhancing long-term benefit. Of course, the potential rapid adoption of AI in cancer vaccine development also represents certain challenges. It will be important to assess the extent to which algorithms are transparent, can be explained, and are free of bias from the Skewing of training data or the limits of training databases. The regulators will be expected to adapt to the pace of technological advances. Consequently, issues regarding their requirements for safety, efficacy, privilege, and privacy of data may have to be enforced in a straitjacket fashion. Close accountability from AI developers, oncologists, ethicists, and regulators will be required to oversee development and registration processes if they are to be shown to be working in a responsible manner [ ]. In summary, AI implementation is changing the way we view the development of mRNA cancer vaccines, particularly in terms of personalized antigen prediction and optimal delivery through improved LNP approaches, as well as better conduct of clinical trials. With the ability to innovate and work across sectors, it is conceivable that AI-based mRNA vaccines will be a major component of next-generation cancer therapeutics.

## Personalized mRNA vaccines as a precision immunotherapy strategy in hepatocellular carcinoma
The growing recognition of HCC as a highly heterogeneous and immunologically complex malignancy has intensified interest in personalized immunotherapeutic strategies, particularly mRNA-based vaccines. Unlike conventional therapeutic approaches, personalized mRNA vaccines are designed to target patient-specific tumor antigens, thereby aligning therapeutic intervention with the unique molecular and immunological landscape of each tumor. This precision-driven approach is particularly relevant for HCC, where intertumoral heterogeneity, variable mutational burden, and diverse etiological backgrounds significantly influence treatment outcomes.
With advances in next-generation sequencing and bioinformatic tools, researchers can more easily identify tumor neoantigens arising from nonsynonymous mutations, deletions, and abnormal gene expression. These studies have demonstrated that HCC harbors immunogenic neoantigens capable of inducing strong T-cell responses, particularly in patients with high mutational burden or active immune profiles [ – ]. Neoantigens may be used to create mRNA-based personalized vaccines for the treatment of HCC because the technology exists to make the individual vaccines for each patient quickly as the patient develops resistance to treatment [ , ].
The ability of mRNA vaccine technology to be flexible is much more beneficial than the traditional methods of vaccine development because of the flexibility of the platforms utilized to produce the mRNA vaccines for each patient [ ]. The mRNA vaccine technology has the ability to generate constructs that contain multiple patient-specific neoantigens, as well as immune-stimulating proteins, or modified epitopes, in order to enhance the ability of the patient’s T-cells to recognize and destroy HCC [ ]. Additionally, LNPs have the ability to deposit a large amount of mRNA into APCs (Antigen Presenting Cells), and, as a result, to stimulate both CD8 + and CD4 + T-cell activation, leading to a very strong CD8 + T-cell response to the neoantigens contained within the mRNA constructs [ ]. In addition, recent advancements in nucleoside modification techniques (e.g., pseudouridine incorporation) have improved the stability of mRNA constructs, as well as their ability to be translated by the ribosome and to induce tolerance in patients after vaccination, thereby strengthening the potential clinical applications of mRNA cancer vaccines [ ].
Preclinical and clinical models indicate that combining neoantigen vaccines with ICIs or immune modulators enhances the anti-tumor immune response and has the potential to overcome resistance mechanisms [ , , ]. Stratifying patients according to immune phenotype, TMB and neoantigen quality are critical components of determining how to design an appropriate treatment for a given patient, highlighting the need for precision medicine based immunotherapy approaches [ , ]. Taken together, personalized mRNA vaccines are providing a new, revolutionary approach to treating HCC by integrating genomic profiling, immunoengineering and precision medicine. As advances in sequencing technology, neoantigen prediction algorithms and mRNA delivery systems continue to evolve, the development of personalized mRNA vaccines has the potential to stand at the forefront of next generation HCC immunotherapy solutions.

## Conclusion and directions
As the development of mRNA vaccines proceeds at an unprecedented speed, researchers may also be opening the door in a major way to combat hepatocellular carcinoma finally. Given their ability to encode tumor antigens, immune-stimulatory molecules, and tumor suppressor genes, mRNA vaccines are also a new and versatile opportunity in the immunotherapy toolbox. The available preclinical studies and early-phase clinical trials indicate the possibility of mRNA vaccines to elicit antigen-specific immune responses, decrease tumor burden, and ultimately enhance standard-of-care therapies, including checkpoint inhibitors and adoptive T cell transfers.
Even though mRNA vaccine therapy shows promise in the field of HCC, several serious challenges impede the clinical translation and efficacy of mRNA vaccines. The first obstacle is that HCC has a variant of an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment (TME), which strongly inhibits T-cell activation and infiltration, hence limiting the antitumor immune response evoked by mRNA vaccines (Fig.  ). The second challenge is that it is still not yet fully clear how to identify the ideal tumor-specific antigens for selection in vaccine designs due to the heterogeneity of tumors and the rapid mutation rate of HCC, which could lead to immune escape. The third challenge is the ability to deliver mRNA efficiently and selectively to antigen-presenting cells (APCs) in the liver, as naked mRNA is unstable and easily degraded by extracellular RNases, which contributes to the need for a next-generation delivery platform, such as lipid nanoparticles. A fourth challenge is that mRNA vaccines may also cause innate immune responses through pattern recognition receptors (PRRs) such as Toll-like receptors, which could cause inflammation leading to reduced antigen expression. Other scalability and reliability challenges important to patients accessing mRNA vaccines are the feasibility of large-scale manufacturing, cold chain storage, and batch-to-batch consistency. The last challenge is obviously patients who have underlying liver cirrhosis, viral hepatitis infections, and immunocompromised status, which will undoubtedly affect the response to the vaccine. We must address these challenges to maximize the safety, immunogenicity, and efficacy of mRNA vaccines in HCC.

 

Several avenues for investigation lie ahead. First, mRNA vaccines should be personalized based on the tumor neoantigen profile for each patient using next-generation sequencing and bioinformatics. Second, drug delivery systems should continue integrating next-gen development to improve stability, targeting of cancer cells, and intracellular expression, particularly using lipid nanoparticles. Third, mRNA vaccines can be rationally combined with immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs), radiotherapy, or metabolic therapies to help clinicians overcome resistance to the immune system in the future in HCC.
Given the data being put forth, the ultimate goal will be to implement well-conceived and biomarker-guided clinical trials to evaluate the safety fully, effectiveness to elicit an immune response, and therapeutic efficacy of mRNA vaccines within different populations of patients. In time, mRNA vaccines may be used to match an individual patient with a therapy associated with a specific molecular subtype or immune phenotype of HCC. In summary, mRNA vaccines cannot be treated as a simple armamentarium of treatment, but rather as a platform by which to combine these therapeutic strategies to provide novel approaches to treating HCC, signifying the start of a new era of precision immunotherapy for liver cancer.
