## ABSTRACT
Head and neck cancers (HNCs) are a heterogeneous group of malignancies, including head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC), thyroid carcinoma, and salivary gland carcinoma. Despite multidisciplinary treatment approaches, outcomes for advanced HNCs remain poor. Among these, HNSCC has been the most extensively studied in the field of immunotherapy. Immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs), particularly anti‐PD‐1 antibodies, have demonstrated survival benefits in R/M HNSCC, but response rates remain modest at 15%–20%, highlighting the need for more effective strategies. Recent advances include the use of neoadjuvant and adjuvant immunotherapy in locally advanced HNSCC, which may improve pathological response rates and long‐term survival. Additionally, novel immunotherapeutic approaches such as tumor antigen‐targeted cancer vaccines and T‐cell receptor‐engineered T‐cell (TCR‐T) therapy are emerging. These strategies aim to enhance tumor‐specific immunity, especially in tumors lacking targetable driver mutations. The tumor microenvironment (TME) in HNSCC plays a pivotal role in modulating immune response and therapeutic efficacy. Immunomodulatory agents such as HDAC inhibitors, TLR agonists, and VEGF inhibitors have shown promise in enhancing ICI responsiveness by altering the immunosuppressive TME. Moreover, the identification of predictive biomarkers, including PD‐L1 expression, tumor mutational burden, and tertiary lymphoid structures, is crucial for patient selection and response prediction. This review provides a comprehensive overview of the current landscape and future directions of immunotherapy for HNCs, with a particular focus on HNSCC. We highlight ongoing clinical challenges and discuss emerging strategies aimed at overcoming resistance and improving clinical outcomes.
Keywords: cancer vaccine, head and neck cancer, immune cell based therapy, neoadjuvant immunotherapy, tumor microenvironment

ICI‐included therapies are standard for R/M HNSCC patients. In particular, neoadjuvant ICI therapy shows promising clinical results even in nontreated resectable HNSCC patients. In addition, cancer vaccine, immune cell‐based therapy, immunomodulation therapy, and TME targeting therapy may combine with ICI.

## Introduction
HNCs refer to malignancies occurring between the skull base and clavicle, encompassing a wide range of tumor subtypes, including pharyngeal, laryngeal, sinus, thyroid, and salivary gland cancers. Collectively, HNCs represent the seventh most common malignancy globally (931,931 cases per year) and the third most common when thyroid cancer is included (1,518,133 cases per year). However, when analyzed by individual anatomical sites, all HNC subtypes qualify as rare cancers (< 6 cases per 100,000 people) (Figure  ) [ ]. As a result, HNCs show complicated characteristics in deciphering the effective therapies for each cancer.
 Genomic profiling has provided key insights into the molecular landscape of HNCs. HNSCC frequently exhibits mutations in TP53, NOTCH, FAT1, along with copy number alterations in CDKN2A and CDKN2B [ ]. In thyroid carcinoma, BRAF mutations are prevalent, whereas RET fusion genes are crucial in medullary thyroid cancer [ , ]. SGCs are characterized by distinct fusion genes: ETV6‐NTRK in secretory carcinoma, CRTC1‐MAML2 in MEC, and MYB‐NFIB in ACC, offering potential therapeutic targets [ , , ]. These characteristic gene mutations have the potential to become targets not only for molecular targeted drugs but also for immunotherapy.
The introduction of ICIs has dramatically transformed the treatment strategies for HNCs. Furthermore, owing to the characteristics of adaptive immunity centered on T cells, immunotherapy is well suited for personalized treatment approaches. Immunotherapy is the novel pillar of the treatment strategy to deal with diversified cancers. However, given the rarity and heterogeneity of HNCs, devising optimized treatment strategies remains a challenge. This review consolidates current evidence and provides future perspectives on HNC immunotherapy.

## Overview of Immunotherapy for Head and Neck Cancers (HNCs)
Conventional HNSCCs are mainly caused by habitual drinking and smoking. In addition, HPV‐related HNCs in the oropharyngeal region are rapidly increasing [ ]. Generally, HPV‐positive HNCs are sensitive to chemoradiotherapy and have a good prognosis [ ]. However, the 5‐year survival rate of patients with advanced HPV‐negative HNSCC is still less than 50%. As a drug treatment for HNSCC, the clinical usefulness of surgery followed by cisplatin‐based concurrent chemoradiotherapy for patients with locally advanced high‐risk HNSCC has been established [ ]. These treatments unfortunately sometimes cause substantial impairments in essential functions such as eating, swallowing, and breathing. While genomic analyses have identified tumor suppressor gene mutations, no actionable cancer‐promoting driver gene mutations have been consistently detected in HNSCCs [ , ], limiting the applicability of molecular‐targeted therapies. Notably, HNSCCs lack universally shared oncogenic driver mutations but are characterized by a relatively high TMB [ , ]. In addition, HNSCCs are considered one of the most immune‐infiltrated carcinoma types [ ], suggesting a strong candidate for immunotherapy.
Checkpoint inhibitor‐based immunotherapy has demonstrated efficacy in R/M HNSCCs, as evidenced by Phase III trials (CheckMate 141 and KEYNOTE‐048) [ , ]. These trials established immunotherapy as first‐ and second‐line treatment strategies for R/M HNSCC patients. Effects of checkpoint inhibitors are mainly derived from reinvigoration and activation of tumor‐oriented antigen‐specific T cells [ ]. The landmark Phase III CheckMate 141 trial resulted in the approval of nivolumab for CDDP‐refractory R/M HNSCC patients [ ]. Following this, the Phase III KEYNOTE‐048 trial established a new paradigm for first‐line R/M HNSCC patients [ ]. Based on these findings, treatment selection now depends on the PD‐L1 CPS, where either pembrolizumab monotherapy or pembrolizumab combined with chemotherapy is recommended as the first‐line option [ ]. However, in these clinical trials, the median OS was 7.5 months for nivolumab alone and 11.6 months for pembrolizumab alone, and response rates were 13% for nivolumab and 17% for pembrolizumab, indicating that only a limited number of cases can benefit from these drugs [ , ]. These findings underscore that only a subset (up to 15%–20%) of patients ultimately derive significant benefit from anti‐PD‐1 therapy, highlighting the urgent need for strategies to enhance the efficacy of ICIs in HNSCCs.
Thyroid cancer has a relatively low TMB compared with HNSCC. Therefore, ICIs are expected to be less effective in reactivating tumor‐specific T cells [ ]. ICIs have been applied in clinical trials to treat refractory thyroid carcinomas, including ATC and advanced differentiated thyroid cancers [ , , ]. A Phase Ib KEYNOTE‐028 trial included 22 patients with advanced PTC or FTC, demonstrating only 9% ORR (NCT02054806) [ ]. Notably, based on the fact that approximately 22%–29% of ATC cases express PD‐L1 [ , ], ATC may respond to ICI therapies [ ]. Another Phase I/II trial that analyzed the effect of anti‐PD‐1 therapy (spartalizumab) for 40 BRAF‐wild ATC patients showed a promising ORR (19%) (NCT02404441) [ ], highlighting immunotherapy as a new treatment paradigm for BRAF‐wild ATC. In that study, the PD‐L1 high expression group (≥ 50%) showed 35% ORR, suggesting the possibility of immunotherapy for ATC patients with PD‐L1 expression. What makes ATC promising for immunotherapy? An interesting study showed that CXCL13+ T lymphocytes were enriched in human ATC samples and might promote early tertiary lymphoid structure (TLS) formation, making ATC more sensitive to immunotherapy [ ].
SGCs comprise a diverse group of tumors, with more than 20 histological subtypes according to WHO 5th edition. Immunotherapy efficacy in SGC remains controversial. SDC and high‐grade MEC exhibit immune‐hot TME, while ACC shows an immune‐cold TME [ ]. A multicenter study on nivolumab reported an ORR of 4.2% in recurrent and metastatic SGC, and the median PFS and OS were 1.6 and 10.7 months [ ]. An intriguing study demonstrated that a fusion gene from skull base HNSCC worked as a neoantigen and showed a strong antigen‐specific T‐cell response. This tumor experienced a complete response with ICI treatment despite a low mutational load and minimal pretreatment immune infiltration in the tumor [ ]. These data broaden the possibility of ICIs or targeted immunotherapies for low TMB SGCs with fusion genes. The immunological treatment updates in HNCs are written below and summarized in Figure  .

## Neoadjuvant Immunotherapy for HNSCCs

Neoadjuvant immunotherapy has emerged as a promising approach for treating HNSCCs. This approach has the potential to improve clinical outcomes by increasing antitumor immune responses in the presence of abundant tumor‐derived antigens [ ]. This approach may yield clinical benefits, including tumor downstaging and a reduced risk of distant metastasis. In a Phase II trial, Uppaluri et al. demonstrated neoadjuvant pembrolizumab for 36 locally advanced stage III or IV HNSCC patients (NCT02296684). Notably, pathological tumor response was observed in 44% of cases, and no grade 3–4 serious adverse events were observed [ ]. Consequently, a Phase III KEYNOTE‐689 trial evaluating pembrolizumab for resectable stage III or IVA locally advanced HNSCC met its primary endpoint of improved EFS. The trial compared neoadjuvant pembrolizumab with postoperative standard of care radiation therapy (±cisplatin) followed by maintenance pembrolizumab with postoperative radiation therapy (±cisplatin) [ ]. In this report, an mPR rate of 13.7% was observed in the group with PD‐L1 CPS ≥ 10, while the overall CPS group showed an mPR rate of 9.4%. This regimen is expected to become a cornerstone in future treatment strategies for HNSCC. However, it should be noted that completing this treatment requires approximately 1 year, and even in patients achieving mPR and significant tumor shrinkage, postoperative (chemo)radiotherapy remains necessary. Although cases with PD‐L1 CPS ≥ 1 have been approved by the FDA, careful patient selection may be warranted, considering both clinical outcomes and healthcare cost implications. While long‐term survival data are still awaited for KEYNOTE‐689, neoadjuvant immunotherapy shows promise in changing the treatment paradigm for HNSCCs [ ]. Conversely, adjuvant immunotherapy has garnered increasing attention. In the NIVOPOSTOP trial (NCT03576417), patients with locally advanced stage III or IV HNSCC who exhibited high‐risk pathological features for recurrence or metastasis after surgery demonstrated a significant improvement in overall survival (OS) when treated with adjuvant nivolumab in combination with chemoradiotherapy, compared to those receiving chemoradiotherapy alone [ ]. While the efficacy of neoadjuvant immunotherapy has been well established, adjuvant immunotherapy alone should also be considered, particularly given its potential advantages in terms of feasibility and integration with existing chemotherapeutic regimens in routine clinical practice.
Recent advances in single‐cell RNA sequencing have facilitated the characterization of specific immune cell populations. These studies have revealed key aspects of ICI response mechanisms and tumor‐specific T‐cell trajectories. Intriguingly, neoadjuvant ICI‐responding HNSCCs tend to show preexisting tumor‐specific, tissue‐resident memory T cells characterized as CD8+ ZNF683+ with cytotoxic potential [ ], suggesting that targeting this population may enhance the ICI effect. Based on these data, new treatment methods will be developed in the near future. Other kinds of immune cells are also examined. For instance, tumor‐associated macrophages are the main contributors of PD‐L1, and other immune checkpoint ligands are examined in HNSCCs [ ]. Neoadjuvant immunotherapy may also potentiate the efficacies of cancer vaccines and TCR‐T therapies by amplifying tumor antigen presentation prior to surgical intervention. ## Cancer Vaccine
The success of cancer immunotherapy has dramatically changed the cancer treatment landscape, creating a fourth pillar of treatment for patients. Notably, cancer vaccine therapy that can target individual antigens is an attractive approach for HNSCCs that show a wide variety of mutations in each patient. In general, types of antigens used for immunotherapeutic treatment can be divided into TAAs and TSAs [ ].
TAAs are strongly expressed in cancer cells but are retained often weakly in normal tissues. As nonmutated antigens, TAAs offer broad applicability for various cancer types. However, a major concern with TAAs as vaccine targets is the possibility of inducing autoimmune toxicity in normal tissues [ ]. Many clinical studies performed using TAAs have concluded without demonstrating clinical efficacy, likely because TAAs are subject to central or peripheral tolerance. However, compared to TSAs, TAAs can be used for many patients with the same HLAs, underscoring potential to augment antitumor effects universally. A phase I trial (UMIN000008379) employed three kinds of TAAs injected subcutaneously in R/M HNSCC patients. In this study, TAAs injected with short peptides restricted to HLA‐A24 showed that peptide‐specific CD8+ T‐cell responses were observed only for HLA‐A24 patients. Note, patients who responded to all three CTAs improved OS, indicating the combination of several epitopes could be effective to prolong OS [ ].
Conversely, TSAs arising from tumor‐specific mutations present a unique challenge in identifying effective antigens. Various web‐based tools have been developed to predict high‐affinity antigen candidates for MHC‐I/II binding [ ], with AI further refining TSA selection strategies [ ]. A prior study employing a murine HNSCC model identified strong TSA peptide vaccines by focusing on MHC‐I/II overlap proteins, underscoring the crucial role of CD4+ T cells in addition to CD8+ antigen‐specific T cells [ ]. The major challenge in TSA‐based therapies is acquired resistance due to antigen immunogenicity loss. Combining ICIs with multiantigen vaccine strategies may offer a viable solution. A Phase II trial (NCT02426892) evaluated a combination of nivolumab with the HPV‐16 vaccine ISA101, which comprises nine overlapping E6 peptides and four overlapping E7 peptides [ ]. Among 24 patients with incurable HPV‐related cancers (including 22 with oropharyngeal carcinoma), an ORR of 33% and a median OS of 17.5 months were observed, suggesting ISA101 may enhance antigen‐specific CD4+/CD8+ T‐cell responses and improve anti‐PD‐1 efficacy. In melanoma, the Phase IIb Keynote‐942 study demonstrated that adjuvant mRNA vaccines incorporating up to 34 personalized neoantigens, when combined with pembrolizumab, significantly prolonged recurrence‐free survival compared to pembrolizumab monotherapy (NCT03897881) [ ]. These findings highlight the potential of TSA‐based vaccines. Completed clinical trials are summarized in Table  .

## Immune Cell‐Based Therapy
A Phase II clinical trial evaluating afamitresgene autoleucel (afami‐cel), a TCR‐T therapy targeting MAGEA4 TAAs, demonstrated a 37% ORR in HLA‐A*02‐positive patients with synovial sarcoma and myxoid round cell liposarcoma (NCT03132922) [ ]. This marked the first FDA approval of TCR therapy for solid tumors, paving the way for its future application in HNSCCs. TCR‐T therapy is particularly effective for solid malignancies that lack surface antigen expression, differentiating it from CAR‐T therapy. Unlike CAR‐T, TCR‐T cells recognize MHC‐bound antigens, enabling a broader spectrum of targetability [ ].
One of the primary challenges in TCR‐T therapy is antigen selection, an issue also encountered in cancer vaccine development. When isolating T cells specific to the cancer‐testis antigen NY‐ESO‐1 from cancer patients, only medium‐ to low‐affinity TCRs are typically obtained, due to central tolerance. A study demonstrated that modifying NY‐ESO‐1‐specific TCRs to high‐affinity variants significantly enhanced therapeutic efficacy [ ]. These findings suggest that optimizing both antigen selection and TCR sequences is crucial for maximizing treatment outcomes. When introducing tumor‐specific TCR genes into a patient's autologous T cells to target cancer cells, retroviral and lentiviral vectors are commonly used to deliver these genes due to their high integration efficiency and stable transgene expression. These vectors are carefully engineered to be replication‐incompetent and noninfectious. In addition, identified safe harbors are used for ideal viral vector integration sites [ ]. However, long‐term patient monitoring, which addresses potential risks such as insertional mutagenesis and secondary malignancies, may still be recommended.
In HPV‐positive oropharyngeal carcinoma, HPV‐derived antigens represent ideal targets. Since HPV‐16 oncoproteins E6 and E7 are essential for tumorigenesis and maintenance of the malignant phenotype, these antigens are less susceptible to immune evasion. Moreover, since HPV E6/E7 functionally suppress TP53 and PTEN, targeting these proteins offers dual benefits—both immune‐mediated tumor clearance and direct tumor growth inhibition. These oncoproteins are considered optimal targets for TCR‐T because they represent non‐self TSAs, minimizing the risk of central tolerance.
A landmark Phase I clinical trial TCR‐T targeting HPV‐16 E7 was conducted in patients with metastatic HPV‐associated epithelial cancers, including oropharyngeal carcinoma (NCT02858310) [ ]. Notably, robust tumor regression was observed in 6 of 12 patients; especially, 4 of 8 anti‐PD‐1‐refractory patients responded. Intriguingly, resistance mechanisms were associated with copy number loss and point mutations in antigen‐presentation genes (TAP and β2M) and the IFN‐γ receptor gene [ ]. These findings underscore the efficacy of TCR‐T while highlighting the impact of antigen presentation deficiencies and IFN‐γ pathway defects on resistance. HPV‐positive head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC) is classified as an “immune hot” tumor and is thus considered a favorable candidate for immunotherapy [ , ]. However, it generally exhibits excellent prognosis and high sensitivity to surgery, radiotherapy, and chemotherapy [ ]. Conversely, HPV‐negative HNSCC, ATC, and SDC are characterized by more aggressive clinical behavior and limited response to conventional treatments, representing substantial unmet medical needs. Future development of novel immunotherapeutic strategies targeting these highly malignant tumors is expected to improve outcomes and address these critical therapeutic gaps. In the ongoing phase I/II clinical trial (NCT04729543), patients with MAGE‐C2‐positive HNSCC and melanoma are being treated with TCR‐T targeting MAGE‐C2. The study is currently evaluating the safety profile and preliminary antitumor efficacy of this approach. In parallel, another trial (NCT05697926) is assessing the translational impact of preclinical research on personalized TCR‐T therapy for HNCs. The outcomes of these studies may contribute to the development of personalized TCR‐T strategies in the treatment of these high‐malignant HNCs.

## Immune Modulating/Combinatorial Therapies
Immune‐modulating and combinatorial therapies designed to enhance existing immunotherapies, particularly ICIs, are rapidly advancing. One study demonstrated that inhibition of EZH2 significantly upregulated MHC‐I expression in both human and mouse HPV‐negative HNSCC cell lines [ ]. These findings suggest epigenetic drugs may act as immunomodulators, potentially enhancing ICI efficacy. Another study revealed that gemcitabine plus cisplatin (GP) chemotherapy for nasopharyngeal carcinoma induced an innate‐like B‐cell‐dominant antitumor immune response. Additionally, chemotherapy‐induced DNA fragments activated the STING type‐I interferon‐dependent pathway, leading to increased MHC‐I expression in cancer cells. This suggests that specific chemotherapy regimens may have immunostimulatory properties [ ]. A Phase III trial evaluated effects of induction GP chemotherapy followed by concurrent chemoradiotherapy (n = 242) versus chemoradiotherapy alone (n = 238). The GP induction chemotherapy group demonstrated a significantly higher 5‐year OS. ILB frequency was positively associated with OS and DFS in nasopharyngeal cancer patients (NCT01872962) [ ]. Recent studies have also highlighted the immunomodulatory potential of VEGF inhibitors, including TKIs. A Phase II, multicenter, single‐arm trial of pembrolizumab combined with cabozantinib in patients with R/M HNSCC reported an overall clinical benefit rate of 91% (30/33 cases), with median and 1‐year overall survival rates of 22.3 months and 68.4%. Baseline CD8+ T cell infiltration positively correlated with response rates, reinforcing the immunomodulatory potential of cabozantinib in combination with ICIs [ ] (NCT03468218). These findings indicate that immune‐modulating and combinatorial immunotherapies, particularly in combination with ICIs, are rapidly evolving as fundamental treatment strategies for HNSCC. In a retrospective study, the combination of lenvatinib and pembrolizumab achieved a high BOR rate of 66% even in patients with highly aggressive ATC [ ], suggesting that the integration of TKIs with ICIs may challenge the conventional notion of immunotherapy‐refractory malignancies.

## Tumor Microenvironment in HNSCCs

The TME in HNSCCs is crucial in disease progression, immune evasion, and therapeutic response. The TME consists of various cellular and noncellular components, including immune cells, CAFs, ECM, and soluble factors such as cytokines and chemokines, which collectively influence tumor behavior [ ]. For instance, HPV‐negative HNSCCs tend to show CD8+ T cells hypo‐inflamed state compared to HPV‐positive HNSCCs. Notably, a multiplex immunostaining study demonstrated myeloid‐inflamed HPV‐negative HNSCC show more dismal prognosis than lymphoid‐inflamed cases [ ]. CAFs are a pivotal component of the TME, driving tumor progression through secretion of pro‐tumorigenic cytokines, remodeling of the ECM, and promoting epithelial‐to‐mesenchymal transition [ ]. CAFs also contribute to therapy resistance by fostering an immunosuppressive milieu and interfering with drug delivery through ECM stiffening [ ]. Angiogenesis is another critical feature of the HNSCC TME, which is driven by HIF‐1α and VEGF. The aberrant vasculature leads to hypoxic regions that promote tumor aggressiveness and resistance to therapy [ ]. Hypoxia also enhances immune escape by upregulating PD‐L1 and other immune checkpoint molecules, further dampening antitumor immunity [ ]. Combination therapies that modulate the TME with anti‐PD‐1/PD‐L1 therapy are under investigation to improve treatment efficacy and overcome resistance mechanisms. For example, in a phase Ib clinical trial (NCT03019003), patients with ICI‐resistant HNSCC were treated with the HDAC inhibitor 5‐Aza followed by re‐administration of ICI. This regimen resulted in a significant increase in the tumor IFN‐γ signature and PD‐L1 expression compared to baseline, accompanied by a marked infiltration of intratumoral CD8+ T cells and a reduction in CD4+ Tregs [ ]. These findings suggest that low‐dose 5‐Aza reprograms the TME and restores ICI‐mediated antitumor responses. Additionally, a phase II trial investigating intratumoral SD‐101 in combination with pembrolizumab in anti‐PD‐1–naïve HNSCC patients demonstrated a response rate of 24% [ ], indicating that the combination therapy may enhance ICI responsiveness.

## Conclusion and Future Perspectives
Immunotherapy has revolutionized the treatment paradigm for HNCs, particularly in R/M HNSCC. Nevertheless, only a limited subset of patients derive a durable clinical benefit, highlighting the need for strategies to improve response rates and expand applicability. This review has outlined emerging approaches, including neoadjuvant immunotherapy, tumor antigen‐targeted vaccines, and TCR‐engineered T‐cell therapy, that aim to boost the immunogenicity of HNCs and overcome intrinsic resistance mechanisms. Highly aggressive and treatment‐refractory subtypes, such as ATC and SDC, exhibit promising responsiveness to ICIs under certain immunological contexts, such as PD‐L1 expression or tertiary lymphoid structure formation. Moreover, combination strategies with epigenetic modulators, STING agonists, and TKIs are being actively explored to reshape the tumor microenvironment and synergize with ICIs. Looking ahead, integrating molecular and immunological profiling, including tumor mutational burden, PD‐L1 CPS, and T‐cell signatures, will be critical to personalize immunotherapeutic approaches. Advances in single‐cell and spatial transcriptomics are expected to refine our understanding of immune evasion in HNCs and guide next‐generation combination therapies. By leveraging these insights, immunotherapy has the potential not only to improve survival but also to address the unmet medical needs of rare and aggressive HNC subtypes.
