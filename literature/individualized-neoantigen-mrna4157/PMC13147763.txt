## Summary
In the past years the therapeutic landscape in Oncology has been expanded, creating new possibilities in achieving superior tumor specificity and minimizing systemic toxicity. Amidst these, oncolytic viruses and cancer vaccines have risen as ingenious approaches that harness unique mechanisms of action, exploiting the immune system to destroy malignancies. Oncolytic viruses, typically modified to selectively infect cancer cells, ultimately lead to their destruction, offering both the benefit of direct cytolysis and the subsequent activation of our immune system, which might therefore improve durable responses. On the other hand, cancer vaccines, aim to provoke a targeted immune response against antigens that are tumor specific, ultimately enhancing immune surveillance and reducing the risk of relapse. Despite the promising rationale behind these approaches, there are significant challenges in areas such as the optimisation of therapeutic delivery, tackling tumor immune evasion and managing new emergent adverse events. This Series paper discusses the potential impact of these strategies on the current landscape of cancer treatment, while also covering the challenges that might emerge in clinical implementation.

## Key messages

## What has changed?

•We have transitioned from basic microbial observations to advanced genetic engineering, allowing for selective oncolytic viruses (OVs) that replicate without causing chronic infection. Next-generation OVs are now being armed with immunostimulatory payloads like cytokines (IL-12, GM-CSF) to enhance systemic immune priming. 
•Cancer vaccines have evolved from targeting shared tumor-associated antigens (TAAs) to personalized neoantigen-based mRNA platforms that can encode multiple unique patient-specific mutations in a single construct. 
•The field has moved beyond the landmark approvals of T-VEC and Sipuleucel-T. Recent data shows personalized mRNA vaccines reducing recurrence risk by 44% in melanoma, and novel immune modulatory vaccines (IMVs) successfully targeting the tumour microenvironment. 
•There is now a clear mechanistic understanding of how these therapies act as primers, converting cold (non-inflamed) tumors into hot microenvironments by increasing T-cell infiltration and PD-L1 expression.

## Biggest challenges?

•Achieving efficient systemic delivery of OVs remains difficult due to neutralizing antibodies and rapid viral clearance, often limiting efficacy primarily to injected lesions.
•The production of personalized neoantigen vaccines remains logistically complex, with a sequencing-to-product window of 6–12 weeks that may be too long for patients with aggressive, fast-moving cancers. 
•The substantial costs of genomic sequencing and specialized infrastructure create significant implementation barriers, particularly in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), risking a widening gap in global cancer care. 

## Promising approaches

•Pairing OVs or vaccines with immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) to address specific resistance bottlenecks and unmask new targets for the immune system. 
•Using non-biological nanovectors, cloaking approaches, or carrier cells to protect OVs during systemic administration and improve tumor penetration. 
•Utilizing AI-driven neoantigen prediction and microfluidic manufacturing to compress production timelines to under 10 days. 
•Developing vaccines (IMVs) that target immunosuppressive proteins like IDO and PD-L1 to dismantle regulatory networks within the tumour microenvironment.

## Search strategy and selection criteria
The literature search for this Series paper was conducted using reputable databases including PubMed, Embase, and The Cochrane Library to ensure a comprehensive global perspective on clinical research and systematic reviews. The search focused on capturing the most recent technological and clinical milestones in the field, with a particular emphasis on publications from the last decade.
To ensure thorough coverage, the search utilized Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) such as “Oncolytic Viruses,” “Cancer Vaccines,” and “Immunotherapy,” alongside free-text terms including “neoantigen,” “mRNA vaccines,” and “viral vectors”.
The inclusion criteria prioritized peer-reviewed original research, clinical trial results (including phase Ib to III data), and high-impact reviews. Specific attention was given to studies addressing mechanisms of action, therapeutic delivery optimization, and emerging clinical signals in combination with checkpoint inhibitors. Irrelevant studies or those not meeting the criteria for clinical relevance in the current oncology landscape were excluded.

## Introduction
Treating cancer remains one of the most elusive challenges in medicine, as traditional treatments such as surgery, radiation, and chemotherapy often fail to provide durable or substantial benefits in most cases. Resistance is also a major, if not the greatest, obstacle, as tumors can evolve through a Darwinian process and effectively evade treatment. Immunotherapy has emerged as a significant advancement, as harnessing the host's immune system to fight cancer is pivotal in overcoming resistance, thanks to its natural ability to adapt to evolving targets. However, many solid tumors exploit the host's innate mechanisms, typically designed to prevent autoimmunity, to evade these approaches.
Oncolytic viruses (OVs) and cancer vaccines represent a unique opportunity in this setting. OVs use viruses that selectively infect, replicate within, and destroy tumor cells while boosting the immune response. Cancer vaccines, whether mRNA-, peptide-, or dendritic cell-based, aim to enhance a targeted immune response against tumor-specific antigens. A key conceptual advantage of these approaches is their ability to activate both the innate immune system, via pattern recognition receptors (PRRs), and the adaptive immune system. This results in a more comprehensive and natural immune response compared to most current immuno-oncology therapies, which primarily target T cells and the adaptive immune system.
The concept of OVs dates back to the early 20th century, with reports of tumor regressions following microbial infections. , More recently, technological advances have enabled the engineering of selective OVs with enhanced immunogenicity and the potential to synergize with existing immunotherapies. The first major milestone validating the clinical potential of these agents was the approval of talimogene laherparepvec (T-VEC). Neoantigen cancer vaccines are also emerging in this field, with the potential to induce deep and long-lasting responses by expanding the pool of reactive T cells targeting a personalized set of neoantigens unique to each patient's tumor. Recently, several advances have been made in this domain, with mRNA vaccines leading the way in melanoma and reporting clinical data that demonstrate promising results. , Despite these advances, several challenges remain in translating these novel therapeutic modalities into clinical practice, which we aim to describe in this Series paper.

## Mechanisms, design, and clinical application of oncolytic viruses in cancer therapy
OVs are genetically engineered or naturally occurring viruses that selectively infect cancer cells, effectively destroying them by lysis and subsequently triggering or amplifying an anti-tumor immune response. This dual mechanism of action harnesses the power of direct oncolysis combined with immune system modulation by enhancing tumor antigen presentation. To achieve this, they must replicate without provoking an acute or chronic infectious process. 
Most oncolytic viruses do not require integration into the host genome for replication. Many clinically relevant OVs, such as herpes simplex virus (HSV) and vaccinia virus, replicate episomally (HSV in the nucleus and poxviruses entirely in the cytoplasm) while adenoviruses and reoviruses also avoid integration. , Integration is generally unnecessary for oncolysis and immune activation. Advantages of non-integration include a lower risk of insertional mutagenesis and a more favorable safety profile. , Conversely, integration could theoretically allow persistent transgene expression and prolonged immune stimulation, but at the cost of increased genomic risk and regulatory complexity. 
Additionally, to infiltrate malignant populations, circumvent host defenses, and amplify immune surveillance, OVs should possess several attributes: selective cancer cell tropism, rapid replication kinetics, and minimal immunogenicity to evade immune clearance while still stimulating potent antitumor responses. The replication cycle of OVs ultimately drives this mission, beginning with receptor-mediated attachment, where viral glycoproteins bind tumor-overexpressed markers. This is followed by membrane fusion or receptor-mediated endocytosis, allowing the OV to enter the cell. Once inside, the viral capsid uncoats, releasing its genome. , DNA viruses translocate to the nucleus for replication via host polymerases, while RNA viruses usually replicate in the cytoplasm using viral RNA polymerases. DNA viruses offer stable genomes, lower immunogenicity, and robust repair mechanisms, making them prime candidates for genetic engineering, whereas RNA viruses exhibit higher mutation rates but replicate faster and penetrate tissues more effectively. , 
Using the tumor cell machinery (ribosomes), the virus translates viral mRNA into structural and non-structural proteins, which then self-assemble into progeny virions, maturing through processes such as protease-mediated cleavage to gain infectivity. The cycle completes with lytic rupture of the cell, destroying tumor cells and releasing damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs). This cycle enables OVs to expand and propagate across the tumor, leveraging their high replication capacity to amplify tumor cell lysis while eliciting cytotoxic T-cell responses through tumor antigen release. , Through immunogenic cell death, the release of tumor-associated antigens (TAAs) and DAMPs activates dendritic cells and promotes antigen presentation to T cells, converting “cold” tumors into “hot” microenvironments. Additionally, viral infection increases tumor infiltration by effector CD8+ T cells, enhancing systemic antitumor immunity ( ). 
 Furthermore, OVs are often engineered to express immunostimulatory molecules (e.g., GM-CSF, IL-12, interferons) that enhance effector cell recruitment, expansion, and function. The approval of nadofaragene firadenovec, a non-replicating adenoviral vector delivering IFN-α2b for BCG-unresponsive bladder cancer, underscores the clinical feasibility of local cytokine gene delivery. While such vectors lack oncolytic activity and systemic amplification, they validate the concept of in situ immune modulation and provide regulatory precedent for gene-based immunotherapy. In contrast, replication-competent OVs can sustain transgene expression, amplify within tumors, and release tumor antigens, enabling systemic immune priming. These complementary features suggest potential for hybrid strategies, using non-replicating vectors for rapid local immune activation and OVs for broader immunogenicity, particularly in combination with checkpoint inhibitors.
The majority of OVs are genetically modified to enhance tumor selectivity, reduce pathogenicity, and boost efficacy, with over 20 virus families engineered since 1991 to target replication and release tumor antigens. This selectivity often exploits defects in cancer cell antiviral defense mechanisms, such as impaired interferon signaling or dysregulated apoptotic pathways. For instance, engineered herpes simplex virus (HSV-1) strains such as T-VEC have deletions in the ICP34.5 gene, enabling selective replication in cancer cells. Adenoviruses with mutations in the E1B–55 K gene prevent viral replication in normal cells and promote tumor-specific cytotoxicity. Reoviruses preferentially infect cells with an activated RAS pathway, a feature common to many cancers. Beyond these examples, genetic bioengineering enables surface ligand modifications by altering viral coat proteins, allowing these agents to target receptors overexpressed in tumors, further improving selectivity and reducing off-target effects. 
OV delivery challenges traditional pharmacodynamic and pharmacokinetic paradigms due to their replication capacity, prompting exploration of both locoregional and systemic administration routes, each with its caveats. Locoregional delivery, predominantly via intratumoral injection, dominates current protocols for its direct impact and ability to initiate antitumor immunity, targeting injected cells and inducing systemic immune responses. However, tumor location often complicates accessibility, and distant metastases may not benefit as expected despite amplified immune responses ( ). Systemic delivery, typically via intravenous administration, simplifies access to distant metastases but faces challenges such as neutralizing antibodies, off-target effects, and dilution, necessitating higher doses and limiting success.

## Considerations on OV platform development.
Prioritizing OV platforms requires a structured approach:
•Tumor selectivity and immune activation are essential, as viruses should exploit tumor-specific defects and convert “cold” tumors to “hot”. , 
•Payload capacity for immunostimulatory genes such as GM-CSF and IL-12 is important, favoring platforms like HSV and vaccinia. , 
•Delivery feasibility must be considered, weighing intratumoral vs. systemic routes and strategies to overcome neutralizing antibodies. , 
•Safety and regulatory alignment should be prioritized, focusing on non-integrating, attenuated backbones and shedding controls. 
•Developability and clinical signal are key, including manufacturing stability and early efficacy with non-overlapping toxicity. 
The only currently FDA-approved agent is T-VEC, a herpes simplex virus type 1 (HSV-1) indicated for unresectable melanoma. In the OPTiM phase III trial, it achieved a 26% objective response rate (ORR) with 16.3% complete responses (CR), and 16.3% of responses lasted over six months. It also improved median overall survival (OS), demonstrating 23.3 vs. 18.9 months compared with GM-CSF alone (HR 0.79), primarily affecting injected lesions. The phase Ib, single-arm trial MASTERKEY-265 further evaluated T-VEC's efficacy in combination with pembrolizumab. Despite an ORR of 62% and a CR rate of 43%, the phase III trial did not show an OS or progression-free survival (PFS) benefit. 
Combinations succeed when the partner therapy addresses the specific bottleneck created (or unmasked) by the other agent and when exposure is adequate at all relevant tumor sites. For OVs, durable benefit is most plausible when: First, the OV increases tumor antigen release and type I IFN signaling, second, raises T-cell infiltration and PD-L1 expression so that anti–PD-(L)1 has more “targets” to act on, and lastly, reaches non-injected lesions via immunity or dissemination. , 
Several factors likely contributed to this negative result, with T-VEC's intratumoral administration limiting systemic exposure, leaving visceral metastases untreated. And also, as the trial enrolled PD-1–naïve patients, a population already highly responsive to pembrolizumab, it reduced the potential for additive benefit from a predominantly local therapy. These findings do not invalidate the concept of OV–ICI combinations; rather, they underscore the need for next-generation OVs with systemic delivery, enhanced immunostimulatory payloads, and optimized sequencing. When these next-generation OVs are armed with immune costimulators or cytokines and used in PD-1–resistant disease, they have shown encouraging response signals and immune modulation in early trials, supporting the mechanistic premise when indication, payload, and delivery are aligned. , 
Beyond first-generation agents such as T-VEC and Sipuleucel-T, next-generation Ovs and cancer vaccines are demonstrating clinically meaningful activity in settings of high unmet need. For example, RP1 (vusolimogene oderparepvec), an HSV-1–derived OV armed with GM-CSF and fusogenic proteins, achieved a 32.9% response rate and a 2-year OS of 63.3% in anti–PD-1–resistant melanoma when combined with nivolumab (IGNYTE trial). Similarly, the adenoviral vector LOAd703 combined with atezolizumab yielded an ORR of 44% and a median OS of 9.3 months in advanced pancreatic cancer, while early-phase studies of VG2025 and OVV-01 have shown responses across multiple solid tumors. , On the vaccine front, mRNA-based platforms such as mRNA-4157/V940 reduced recurrence risk by 44% in resected melanoma (KEYNOTE-942), and autogene cevumeran elicited durable T-cell responses in pancreatic cancer. These advances underscore a paradigm shift toward personalized, multimodal immunotherapy strategies that overcome the limitations of earlier agents.

## Cancer vaccines: an elegant approach at boosting immunotherapy
Vaccines date back to the late 1700s with the smallpox breakthrough and have long protected humanity from infectious diseases. These agents no longer focus solely on confining pathogens but have evolved into a sophisticated component of our immunotherapy armamentarium, aiming to boost tumor-specific immune responses in a personalized way by targeting tumor-associated antigens (TAAs), which are unique to cancer cells, a new opportunity for precision medicine. , Unlike OVs, which directly infect and destroy tumor cells, cancer vaccines aim to educate the immune system by targeting neoantigens to exploit malignancies with precision. They can span tumor cell, dendritic cell (DC), peptide/protein, nucleic acid, and viral vector platforms, offering a complementary strategy to OVs by amplifying antitumor immunity where direct lysis alone may fall short ( ). 
 Mechanistically, cancer vaccines act by presenting antigens via major histocompatibility complex (MHC) class I and II molecules to cytotoxic T lymphocytes (CTLs) and CD4+ helper T cells. Nucleic acid vaccines, particularly those using mRNA technology, excel at this task because they can encode multiple neoantigens in a single construct, which is delivered to antigen-presenting cells (APCs) for processing and presentation to CTLs. Besides delivering antigens, mRNA can also trigger innate immunity through Toll-like receptor (TLR) pathways, further amplifying DC activation and cytokine release. In contrast, peptide vaccines rely on pre-identified epitopes, while dendritic cell vaccines involve ex vivo pulsing, bypassing in vivo antigen uptake. 
The first successful example using this platform was a dendritic cell vaccine targeting prostatic acid phosphatase (PAP): Sipuleucel-T, the first therapeutic cancer vaccine approved by the FDA in 2010 for metastatic castration-resistant prostate cancer (mCRPC). By using this known TAA in mCRPC, investigators employed autologous APCs pulsed ex vivo with a recombinant fusion protein composed of PAP and GM-CSF. Sipuleucel-T targets a shared TAA, offering simplicity but lower specificity due to PAP's expression in normal tissue. Using this approach, the phase III IMPACT trial reported an improvement in median OS of 25.8 months vs. 21.7 months with placebo (HR 0.78, p = 0.03), leading to FDA approval. 
Neoantigen vaccines employ a different approach, exploiting tumor-specific mutations identified using next-generation sequencing (NGS). Bioinformatic pipelines predict which neoepitopes will have high MHC affinity, priming CTLs against those unique tumor signatures. This provides tumor exclusivity, reducing off-target effects. mRNA platforms enable multi-epitope delivery and innate immune activation. DNA vaccines offer stability and low cost but generally elicit weaker immune responses. Peptide vaccines are simple and safe but limited by HLA restriction and low immunogenicity, while dendritic cell vaccines provide potent antigen presentation but remain logistically complex and costly. 
Overall, mRNA platforms currently represent the most scalable and clinically advanced option for personalized cancer vaccination. Among cancer vaccine platforms, they have emerged as the leading priority due to their rapid design, ability to encode multiple neoantigens, and strong CD8+ T-cell responses without genomic integration. , Preclinical data show that neoantigen vaccines can synergize with ICIs by increasing CD8+ T-cell infiltration, converting “cold” to “hot” tumors, a synergy less pronounced with dendritic cell vaccines such as Sipuleucel-T. Clinical data, such as KEYNOTE-942 with mRNA-4157/V940, underscore their potential in combination with ICIs. In the phase 2 b KEYNOTE-942 trial, patients with resected high-risk stage III/IV melanoma received mRNA-4157 (V940) plus pembrolizumab, reducing recurrence or risk of death by 44% (HR 0.561, p = 0.053) vs. pembrolizumab alone. 
Similarly, a groundbreaking study has shown that personalized mRNA neoantigen vaccines can elicit long-lived, tumor-specific CD8+ T-cell responses in patients with pancreatic cancer, a disease notoriously resistant to treatment. By combining individualized mRNA vaccines with checkpoint inhibitors and chemotherapy, researchers observed that some patients developed potent and persistent immune responses, with T-cell clones detectable for years and recurrence-free survival significantly extended in responders. This study not only reinforces the potential of mRNA vaccine platforms beyond infectious diseases but also highlights their transformative promise in personalized cancer immunotherapy. 
Beyond targeting tumor-specific antigens, a distinct class of immune modulatory vaccines (IMVs) has emerged, designed to target immunosuppressive proteins within the tumor microenvironment (TME) rather than classical tumor antigens. As described in a recent review by Andersen et al., IMVs aim to dismantle regulatory networks, such as those driven by regulatory T cells (Tregs) and myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs), and repolarize the TME to potentiate checkpoint blockade. This concept has recently translated into late-phase clinical evidence. At ESMO 2025, the phase III IOB-013/KN-D18 trial reported that IO102-IO103, an off-the-shelf vaccine targeting the immunosuppressive antigens IDO and PD-L1, combined with pembrolizumab achieved a median PFS of 19.4 months vs. 11.0 months with pembrolizumab alone (HR 0.77) in first-line advanced melanoma. Notably, the benefit was most pronounced in subgroups typically harder to treat with single-agent PD-1 blockade, such as patients with PD-L1–negative tumors (medium PFS 16.6 vs. 3.0 months). Expanding this concept to mRNA platforms, the ‘Mobilize’ trial of mRNA-4359 (encoding IDO and PD-L1) has also demonstrated antigen-specific T-cell induction and early clinical activity in checkpoint-refractory melanoma, underscoring the combinatorial logic of targeting both the tumor and its suppressive microenvironment. 
Although these technologies are very promising, significant challenges and limitations remain, particularly regarding the time and cost to produce these vaccines. Sipuleucel-T's leukapheresis-based production takes days and costs approximately $93,000, with modest results ( ). , Neoantigen vaccines require several weeks, ranging from 6 to 12, for NGS and synthesis, which can be excessive in aggressive cancers such as glioblastoma and melanoma ( ). 
 

## Overcoming barriers and future perspectives
The evolving landscape of cancer therapy is being significantly shaped by innovative concepts that diverge from traditional therapeutic agents, with OVs and mRNA cancer vaccines standing out as two of the most promising strategies for weaponizing the host's immune system. Mechanistically, OVs can act as primers, turning “cold” tumors “hot” through immunogenic cell death and dendritic-cell activation, or as enhancers, boosting pre-existing antitumor T cells with transgene payloads such as GM-CSF, IL-12, and OX40L/4-1BBL. , 
In practice, positioning depends on context. Primers are the most compelling in immune-cold, PD-1–refractory settings, where OVs increase antigen presentation and trafficking, followed by anti–PD-(L) 1 to consolidate responses. , Enhancers: In inflamed tumors or after initial ICI benefit, OVs deliver co-stimulation/cytokines intratumorally, potentially augmenting the depth and breadth of response ( ). , 
 Optimal timing and sequencing (for example, OV lead–in to raise PD-L1 → ICI) and route (image-guided intratumoral vs. systemic or carrier-based) remain active variables that can determine success ( ).

## Selecting promising therapeutic combinations.
What distinguishes promising therapeutic combinations from those that fail?
•Biological fit: Does one agent directly counteract the resistance mechanism of the other? For example, OVs may enhance antigen presentation and PD-L1 expression, sensitizing tumors to anti–PD-1 therapy.
•Exposure and reach: Can the combination effectively access all disease sites (systemically and intratumorally), while managing barriers such as neutralizing antibodies?
•Timing and sequence: The schedule should optimize immune priming while avoiding lymphodepletion or T-cell exhaustion.
•Safety complementarity: Ideally, toxicities should not overlap. For instance, the generally mild adverse effects of OVs or vaccines allow layering with ICIs.
•Early signals in the right population: Combinations should be tested in settings with therapeutic headroom, such as PD-1–resistant disease for OVs or minimal residual disease (MRD) for vaccines, rather than in high-response control arms that may obscure additive benefit.
Biomarkers and patient stratification are also critical for optimizing OV and vaccine efficacy. For OVs, candidate markers include viral entry receptors, type I IFN pathway status, and baseline TME inflammation (CD8+ T cells, IFN-γ signatures, PD-L1). , , Current trials often enrich for PD-1–refractory disease and injectable lesions, with image-guided access to deep sites improving systemic benefit. For vaccines, neoantigen load/TMB, HLA binding quality, and early ctDNA clearance are emerging correlates; KEYNOTE-942 showed benefit across PD-L1 and TMB strata. , Challenges include tumor heterogeneity, assay variability, and lack of validated cutoffs; prospective multi-omics and predefined analysis plans are needed to distinguish predictive from prognostic markers. , 
An advantage of these platforms is their generally favorable safety profile. OVs have shown mostly mild, transient adverse events such as fever, chills, and injection–site reactions, with grade ≥3 toxicities being rare and non-overlapping with ICI-related toxicities. , Similarly, mRNA and personalized cancer vaccines have demonstrated good tolerability, with adverse events largely limited to mild flu-like symptoms and injection–site reactions, without significant immune-related toxicity. , , This benign safety profile enhances their potential for combination strategies with other immuno-oncology agents.
For OVs, hurdles include achieving efficient delivery to every tumor site, which is being addressed through non-biological and biological nanovectors, as well as overcoming inadequate tumor penetration, host antiviral immunity, and rapid viral clearance. mRNA vaccines face obstacles in TAA selection, delivery systems, and administration routes, all areas with potential for optimization to maximize efficacy. On top of these technical hurdles, manufacturing scalability remains limited, and costs are considerable.
Addressing implementation barriers requires integrated solutions. For personalized vaccines, the main bottleneck, time from sequencing to product, can be shortened by combining AI-driven neoantigen prediction with selective immunopeptidomics and GMP-ready microfluidic LNP manufacturing, supported by lyophilization for stability. , , , , , , , Dose-sparing RNA formats (self-amplifying or trans-amplifying) further reduce material needs and enable sub-10-day manufacturing cycles. , For OVs, standardized image-guided intratumoral delivery and systemic strategies such as carrier-cell or cloaking approaches can improve distribution and overcome neutralizing immunity. , , , These innovations, combined with rational ICI-based regimens, represent the most practical path to accelerate clinical adoption. , 
Integration with other therapies will be critical to mitigate limitations, while identifying biomarkers to account for inter-patient variability could enhance impact ( ). Understanding the tumor microenvironment, such as the type of immunosuppressive cells present and stromal components, could also provide new targets for combination strategies, enabling additive or synergistic effects.

## Recommendations for clinical implementation and research.
The successful integration of OVs and cancer vaccines into routine care depends on a coordinated strategy that prioritizes delivery optimization, rational combination, and global equity. Future clinical research should move away from broad testing and instead prioritize the use of OVs as “primers” in immune-cold or PD-1–refractory settings, where they can effectively increase antigen presentation and T-cell trafficking. Conversely, personalized neoantigen vaccines should be strategically positioned in adjuvant or minimal residual disease (MRD) settings to maximize their ability to prevent recurrence, given the absent/extremely low tumor burden that is insufficient to generate a immunological response alone. For OVs, the advancement of systemic or image-guided administration is essential to mitigate neutralizing immunity and improve distribution to distant visceral metastases. Simultaneously, the sequencing-to-product timeline for personalized vaccines must be compressed using AI-driven selection and modular microfluidic manufacturing to meet the needs of patients with aggressive malignancies.
Rational combinations must become the standard, guided by prospectively validated predictive biomarkers such as interferon signatures, tumor mutational burden, and T-cell clonality. To ensure that these innovations do not widen existing healthcare disparities, global health planning must include the development of regional manufacturing hubs and cost-reduction technologies, such as lyophilized formulations that reduce dependence on complex cold-chain logistics. Finally, regulatory foresight is needed to harmonize safety standards for non-integrating, attenuated viral backbones, facilitating a smoother path from clinical trials to standard practice.
The clinical promise of OVs and personalized cancer vaccines contrasts with major implementation barriers in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs). These include high manufacturing costs, cold-chain logistics for mRNA vaccines, and the need for specialized infrastructure for intratumoral delivery and GMP production. , Potential mitigations include regional manufacturing hubs, lyophilized formulations to reduce cold-chain dependence, and platform standardization to lower per-patient costs. , Without deliberate strategies for affordability and distribution, these innovations risk widening global disparities in cancer care.

## Conclusion
Oncolytic viruses and cancer vaccines stand at the forefront of immuno-oncology innovation, yet their integration into routine care depends on overcoming practical and biological barriers. Moving forward, success will require a coordinated strategy that addresses three priorities.
First, delivery and manufacturing must be optimized: for OVs, this means advancing systemic or image-guided administration and mitigating neutralizing immunity, while for vaccines, compressing the sequencing-to-product timeline through AI-driven neoantigen selection, modular microfluidic manufacturing, and lyophilized formulations to reduce cold-chain dependence.
Second, rational combinations guided by biomarkers should become the norm. OVs are best positioned as primers in immune-cold or PD-1–refractory tumors, whereas vaccines may achieve maximal benefit in adjuvant or minimal-residual-disease settings. Predictive biomarkers, such as interferon signatures, tumor mutational burden, and T-cell clonality, must be prospectively validated to guide patient selection.
Finally, global equity cannot be an afterthought. Without deliberate strategies, regional manufacturing hubs, cost-reduction technologies, and simplified delivery, these therapies risk widening disparities in cancer care, particularly in low- and middle-income countries. The next decade will determine whether these platforms remain niche innovations or become integral to standard oncology practice. Achieving this will demand not only scientific ingenuity but also implementation, regulatory foresight, and global health planning.
