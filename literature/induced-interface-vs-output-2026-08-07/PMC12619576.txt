## ABSTRACT
Inhibitors of 11β‐hydroxysteroid dehydrogenase type 1 (HSD‐1), the pre‐receptor regulator of intracellular active glucocorticoid levels, have promise to treat glucocorticoid excess whether endogenous (neoplastic or physiological elevated cortisol) or iatrogenic (glucocorticoid medicine toxicity). Three structurally distinct HSD‐1 inhibitors have shown tachyphylaxis of pharmacodynamic activity in adipose, a key target tissue, suggesting that the clinical utility of the class might be limited by that pharmacological phenomenon. To determine whether the potent inhibitor clofutriben also showed adipose tachyphylaxis, HSD‐1 activity was measured during daily oral clofutriben administration and infusion of mass‐labeled enzyme substrate (cortisone) via microdialysis catheters placed in subcutaneous abdominal adipose of 12 persons with type 2 diabetes and obesity. During initial dosing, clofutriben was associated with dose‐dependent decreases of mass‐labeled HSD‐1 product (cortisol) in adipose dialysate, from < 25% at 1 mg to > 90% at 6–10 mg. At steady state, all clofutriben doses were associated with > 90% decreases of mass‐labeled cortisol in adipose dialysate with no evidence of tachyphylaxis. By surface plasmon resonance, clofutriben showed fast‐on, slow‐off (residence time 535 s) binding to human HSD‐1. Molecular docking revealed that clofutriben can form a ternary complex with nicotinamide‐adenine dinucleotide phosphate (NADPH) cofactor and the human HSD‐1 active site. The higher activation energy to dissociate that trimolecular interaction, compared to a bimolecular drug‐enzyme interaction characteristic of other HSD‐1 inhibitors, is posited to be responsible for the enzyme kinetic and favorable pharmacological properties of clofutriben.

## Adipose HSD‐1 Activity
Baseline dialysate D8 cortisone was 132.4 (57.78) ng/mL and D8 cortisol was 4.1 (2.80) ng/mL. Dialysate D8 cortisone, which was expected to be relatively stable, showed moderate within‐individual variability during infusions (CV% 14%–63%). To better account for the apparent fluctuations of dialysate recovery, D8 cortisol mole percent fraction (D8F MPF; calculated as ([D8 cortisol]*mw of D8 cortisone)/([D8 cortisone]* mw of D8 cortisol)), rather than observed D8 cortisol values, is reported as a measure of adipose HSD‐1 inhibition. Baseline D8F MPF was 0.031 (0.0148) with a range of 0.0118–0.0595. D8 cortisol values below the limit of quantitation (N = 34) were imputed as the lower limit of quantitation (0.1 ng/mL). Therefore, by participant, the maximum observable decrease of D8 cortisol ranged from 91% to 99%. Ceiling values of D8F MPF further differed by sample due to the variability of D8 cortisone recovery.
Clofutriben substantially inhibited adipose HSD‐1 activity. Inhibition extent was dose dependent (Figure  ). Four participants who received clofutriben 1 mg daily doses did not show a maximum decrease of D8F MPF. Maximum decreases of D8F MPF were observed in 7 participants who received daily clofutriben doses 2 mg or higher. One participant who received clofutriben 3 mg for 14 days did not show a maximum decrease of D8F MPF. That individual was one of the first participants and their catheters were equilibrated for only 2 h prior to sample collection. Their post‐baseline dialysate D8 cortisone concentrations fluctuated substantially as infusions continued, suggesting insufficient equilibration time prior to sample collection. This issue was addressed for later participants by allowing at least 14 h catheter equilibration prior to sample collection.
 In addition to inhibition extent, adipose HSD‐1 inhibition onset was also dose dependent (Figure  ). Participants who received clofutriben 1 mg daily dose showed no or very limited (which could be attributable to D8 cortisone fluctuations rather than adipose HSD‐1 inhibition) D8F MPF decreases after 1 or 2 doses of clofutriben; substantial decreases were observed only after 3 or more doses. The participant who received clofutriben 2 mg showed no D8F MPF decrease after the first dose. Onset of apparent adipose HSD‐1 inhibition in participants who received clofutriben 3 mg ranged from 7 h after the first dose to 7 h after the second dose. In those who received clofutriben 6 or 10 mg, adipose HSD‐1 inhibition was apparent either 3 or 5 h after the first dose.
Clofutriben did not show tachyphylaxis of adipose HSD‐1 inhibition (Figure  ). D8F MPF decreases on the last day of clofutriben dosing were substantially and uniformly higher, compared to after the first or second dosing day, in participants who received clofutriben 1 or 2 mg. D8F MPF decreases after the last clofutriben 3 mg dose were more heterogeneous, although in each participant the same or larger decreases were observed, compared to those observed after one or two doses. Two of these participants were the first to enter the trial and, as noted above, their catheters might not have been sufficiently equilibrated prior to data collection. The third was unable to undergo catheterization as originally scheduled on Day 8 because D8 cortisone had not been delivered to the clinical site. It's possible that the participant might have missed one or more clofutriben doses during the delay. Participants who received clofutriben 6 or 10 mg had similar D8F MPF decreases after two doses or the last dose.
D8F MPF values obtained 13–35 days after the last clofutriben dose (Table  ) indicate no or limited adipose HSD‐1 inhibition (observed decreases could rather be largely attributable to D8 cortisone fluctuations). In particular, D8F MPF decreases of 16.0% and 33.4% observed in one participant at 13 and 27 days, while a 28.5% increase was observed in the same participant at 20 days, after the last HSD‐1 dose could be attributed to within‐individual variability of D8 cortisone recovery in adipose dialysate. That variability is a limitation of the method—only substantial adipose HSD‐1 inhibition is clearly distinguishable from assay noise.

## Hepatic HSD‐1 Activity
The urinary ratio of cortisol metabolites (tetrahydrocortisol + allotetrahydrocortisol) to cortisone metabolite (tetrahydrocortisone), referred to as the urinary HSD‐1 ratio, was calculated to assess hepatic HSD‐1 activity. The baseline urinary HSD‐1 ratio of each individual participant calculated as the mean of all observed values prior to the first clofutriben dose (7–22 samples) was 1.20 (0.40). The ratio was relatively stable, with a median CV of 11%. All except one CV was below 17%. The exception, with a CV of 27%, was a participant with missing allotetrahydrocortisol values from almost half of their urine samples. Missing values from other participants were rare (0.37%).
Urinary HSD‐1 ratio decreases of more than 80% were observed in all participants following clofutriben administration, indicating substantial hepatic HSD‐1 inhibition by the drug (Figure  ). The maximum observed decrease did not differ substantially across the clofutriben 1–10 mg dose range, although the time to the first observation of at least 80% decrease was dose‐dependent: 4 days for clofutriben 1 mg, more than 2 days for clofutriben 2 mg, 1 or 2 days for clofutriben 3 mg, and generally after the first dose for clofutriben 6 or 10 mg. Consistently large decreases of the urinary HSD‐1 ratio were observed in each participant at the end of clofutriben administration.
 Follow‐up visit results showed that, 13–35 days after the last clofutriben dose, a significant urinary HSD‐1 ratio decrease (35.2%–58.7%) was still observed (Table  ), indicating durable hepatic HSD‐1 inhibition by clofutriben.

## Enzyme Kinetics
Association and dissociation constants measured by surface plasmon resonance for clofutriben with human HSD‐1 were 7.93E+05 M−1 s−1 and 1.87E‐03 s−1. The calculated residence time in the HSD‐1 active site was 535 s.

## Molecular Modeling
The model‐predicted complex of clofutriben in the human HSD‐1 active site is ternary with the enzyme and its NADPH cofactor (Figure  ). The amide protons of the benzamide moiety form hydrogen bonds with the pyrophosphate of the NADPH cofactor. Two triazine ring nitrogens form hydrogen bonds with the side chains of two amino acids of the catalytic triad: serine 170 and tyrosine 183. A fluorine interacts with the protein backbone of alanine 172.

## Discussion
Unlike other HSD‐1 inhibitors [ , , ], clofutriben maintained equal or stronger adipose HSD‐1 inhibition after multiple doses compared to after a single dose. Absence of tachyphylaxis could improve certain aspects of clinical efficacy. Adipose glucocorticoid excess, regulated by HSD‐1 activity, has been implicated in central obesity, insulin sensitivity, hyperlipidemia, and hepatic steatosis [ , , , ]. Inhibitors that lose potency over time for adipose HSD‐1 particularly might not be optimal agents to mitigate those consequences of elevated cortisol or glucocorticoid medication use. The limited degree of efficacy observed for S‐707106 in patients with elevated cortisol [ ] and for AZD4017 in healthy adults administered prednisolone [ ] might in part be related to adipose tachyphylaxis. AZD4017 shows adipose tachyphylaxis [ ]; it's unknown whether S‐707106 does. Clofutriben may represent a promising candidate to treat, for example, diabetes associated with elevated cortisol or glucocorticoid medicines, due to lack of adipose tachyphylaxis.
D8F MPF decreases in adipose dialysate with clofutriben reflect local HSD‐1 inhibition and not a secondary effect of liver enzyme inhibition. Following low clofutriben doses, hepatic HSD‐1 inhibition measured by urinary HSD‐1 ratio is apparent prior to D8F MPF decreases. This result was consistent with predictions from a population pharmacokinetics model for clofutriben disposition [ ]. During first pass, clofutriben binds initially with high affinity to its target in the liver. Until that compartment is saturated, clofutriben is not detectable in plasma, and HSD‐1 in other organs, for example, adipose, is not inhibited. Hepatic HSD‐1 inhibition was observed prior to detection of clofutriben in plasma. Adipose inhibition was observed approximately when clofutriben was detected in plasma in participants who received clofutriben 1–3 mg. Hepatic HSD‐1 inhibition is also durable following cessation of clofutriben administration, while there was no or limited evidence for adipose HSD‐1 inhibition at 12–35 days after the last clofutriben dose. Our results suggest that clofutriben's pharmacologic half‐life is substantially longer in the liver than in adipose, unsurprising given that both HSD‐1 expression and clofutriben concentration are higher in the liver compared to adipose. HSD‐1 expression in the liver is at least 6‐fold greater than in adipose [ ]. Due to the difference in perfusion, clofutriben concentrations would be expected to be higher in the liver than in adipose. Those differences could contribute to faster clofutriben binding to hepatic HSD‐1, and accordingly a longer retention time on the enzyme, in the liver compared to adipose.
In our trial, adipose tissue HSD‐1 activity was evaluated using an in vivo approach, which is different from the ex vivo method used by others. However, if the same method was used to compare HSD‐1 activity between single and multiple dose administration for each drug within the same individuals, the method difference is unlikely to play a role in the interpretation of tachyphylaxis. Estimated IC50 of AZD8329 increased from 0.022 to 0.144 μM from Day 1 to Day 9 [ ]. After a single dose, mean 142 nM BI 135585 in adipose tissue was associated with median 90% HSD‐1 inhibition, while at steady state mean 154 nM BI 135585 was associated with median 31% HSD‐1 inhibition [ ]. Adipose HSD‐1 inhibition after a single AZD4017 dose was not reported. Mean 37 nM free plasma AZD4017 was not associated with any ex vivo adipose inhibition in biopsies from patients treated for 12 weeks, while 20 nM AZD4017 achieved substantial in vitro adipose inhibition in biopsies from separate patients who never received AZD4017 [ ]. As for clofutriben, full liver HSD‐1 inhibition was observed after multiple doses of BI 13558517 and AZD4017 [ ] at the same time points as reduced adipose HSD‐1 inhibition. Liver HSD‐1 inhibition by AZD8239 was not reported.
Daily doses of clofutriben 1–10 mg for 5–14 days were generally well tolerated in participants with type 2 diabetes and BMI > 30, consistent with its safety profile from prior clinical trials [ ].
Clofutriben's sustained inhibition of adipose HSD‐1 after multiple doses, in contrast to other HSD‐1 inhibitors that lose adipose activity with continued administration, may be explained by the formation of a ternary complex by clofutriben with HSD‐1 and its NADPH cofactor in the active site. Inhibition of (predominantly) hepatic HSD‐1 leads to increased circulating levels of its substrate cortisone, a lipophilic molecule that can accumulate in adipose tissue and compete there with an inhibitor for HSD‐1 active sites. According to their structures and how they bind to the enzyme, most HSD‐1 inhibitors do not interact with NADPH in the active site [ , ], including AZD4017 [ ] and AZD8329 [ ] which have shown adipose tachyphylaxis. Clofutriben's additional interaction increases its binding energy and leads to a long enzyme retention time. Thermodynamically and kinetically, clofutriben binding to adipose HSD‐1 would be more resistant to cortisone competition than inhibitors that don't form a ternary complex in the active site. We consider that clofutriben's binding characteristics lead to its favorable pharmacologic activity against adipose HSD‐1.
As clofutriben's interaction with NADPH in the HSD‐1 active site is via hydrogen bonding, not covalent, it can be considered a pseudo‐irreversible enzyme inhibitor. Clofutriben is not unique in that regard. Several other HSD‐1 inhibitors interact with NADPH in the HSD‐1 active site. Some form hydrogen bonds with NADPH pyrophosphate [ , ], while others form an aromatic stacking interaction with the nicotinamide portion [ , , ]. Yet other HSD‐1 inhibitors have shown pharmacokinetic or pharmacodynamic properties similar to clofutriben and are thus predicted to be pseudo‐irreversible inhibitors [ ]. We predict that those HSD‐1 inhibitors, at least those with favorable absorption, distribution, metabolism, and excretion characteristics, could maintain stable adipose HSD‐1 inhibition with continued administration. As a class, pseudo‐irreversible HSD‐1 inhibitors might show better clinical efficacy when adipose is an important target tissue, for example to treat elevated cortisol or mitigate glucocorticoid medications' adverse metabolic effects.
Compared to other inhibitors for which adipose HSD‐1 inhibition has been reported, clofutriben shows the advantageous pharmacological property of no adipose tachyphylaxis, likely related to the formation of a ternary complex with NADPH cofactor and the enzyme in the active site. Other such pseudo‐irreversible inhibitors are expected to show a similar pharmacological advantage. That inhibitor subclass could have a clinical advantage as well. Future clinical trials are needed to assess that possibility. Superior inhibitors of other enzymes, such as NAD(P)H‐producing or ‐containing enzymes, proposed as anti‐infective [ ] or oncology [ ] targets, might also be designed rationally by selecting candidate drugs that form ternary complexes with a cofactor and the enzyme in the active site.
