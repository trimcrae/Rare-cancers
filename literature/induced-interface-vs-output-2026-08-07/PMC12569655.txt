To further validate
the role of the electrostatic interactions,
we assessed the binding affinity between 4.1G-CTD and D2 at varying
salt concentrations. The GST pull-down assay revealed a reduction
in the amount of D2 protein pulled down by 4.1G-CTD as the ionic strength
of the solution increased (Figure S9).

that the affinity between the two proteins progressively decreased
with an increasing salt concentration (Table S2 and Figure S10). Notably, the interaction
was significantly weakened at a salt concentration of 300 mM and completely
disrupted at 600 mM (Table S2).
The
findings from point mutation analyses indicate that the binding
of 4.1G-CTD to D2 occurs via a multivalent interaction mode, wherein
both hydrophobic and electrostatic interactions play significant roles.

## 4.1G-CTD Can Bind D2 and NuMA Simultaneously
The mutagenesis
results indicate that the binding sites for D2 and NuMA on 4.1G-CTD
largely overlap. This raises the following question regarding the
nature of their interactions: are the bindings mutually exclusive?
To investigate this, we first conducted a GST pull-down assay, which
demonstrated that when a mixture of D2 and NuMA was incubated with
4.1G-CTD, both proteins were pulled-down ( A). However, this observation does not necessarily
indicate that 4.1G-CTD is capable of forming a ternary complex with
both D2 and NuMA. Therefore, we employed fluorescence correlation
spectroscopy (FCS) to measure the hydrodynamic radius (R
H) of 4.1G-CTD and its complexes. The R
H values for 4.1G-CTD were found to range between 1.0
and 1.1 nm. Upon binding to D2, there was a significant shift in the R
H distribution toward higher values ( B). In contrast,
the binding of NuMA induced minimal changes to the R
H values, suggesting that the 4.1G-CTD/NuMA complex is
relatively compact. Notably, when 4.1G-CTD was mixed with both D2
and NuMA, the R
H values increased substantially
compared to those of the 4.1G-CTD/D2 complex ( B), indicating the formation of a ternary
complex.
 These results collectively demonstrate that 4.1G-CTD
is capable
of binding to both D2 and NuMA simultaneously and that the interactions
between these pairs are not mutually exclusive.

## Positive Cooperativity in the Bindings of D2 and NuMA to 4.1G-CTD
Given that D2 and NuMA can bind concurrently to 4.1G-CTD, the question
arises regarding the relationship of their interactions. To explore
this relationship, we employed ITC measurements to quantify the effect
of NuMA on the 4.1G-CTD/D2 interaction and vice versa. When 4.1G-CTD
was premixed with NuMA, the binding affinity for D2 was measured at
1.08 ± 0.47 μM ( C), which represents a 4-fold enhancement compared
to its affinity in the absence of NuMA ( D). Conversely, when D2 was present, the
binding affinity of 4.1G-CTD for NuMA increased by approximately 3-fold
( E,F), compared
to that in the absence of D2.
We further investigated the mutual
influence of D2 and NuMA on their binding kinetics to 4.1G-CTD through
stopped-flow experiments conducted under pseudo-first-order conditions.
A tryptophan residue was incorporated into the C-terminus of 4.1G-CTD
to serve as a fluorophore. Binding profiles were obtained by monitoring
changes in fluorescence intensity upon the rapid mixing of 4.1G-CTD
with D2 or NuMA (excitation/emission: 280 nm/360 nm; Figure S11). The resulting kinetic data showed a linear concentration
dependence, allowing for the determination of the association rate
constant ( G).
The association rate constant k
on for
binding of D2 binding to 4.1G-CTD was determined to be 8.4 ×
104 M–1·s–1 ( H). In the presence
of NuMA, the k
on increased to 2.2 ×
105 M–1·s–1 ( H). For NuMA binding
to 4.1G-CTD, the k
on was measured at 9.0
× 104 M–1·s–1, which is comparable to that of D2 ( H). Notably, in the presence of D2, the rate
constant for NuMA binding increased to 3.0 × 1 x 105 M–1·s–1 ( H). These results indicate
that D2 and NuMA mutually facilitate each other’s binding to
4.1G-CTD through accelerating the association rates, demonstrating
positive cooperativity in their binding interactions.
To further
elucidate the molecular mechanism governing the cooperative
bindings of D2 and NuMA to 4.1G-CTD, we conducted NMR spin relaxation
measurements to compare the properties of 4.1G-CTD within the binary
and ternary complexes. The dynamic nature of the ternary complex was
characterized by the minimal changes in the {1H}-15N NOE values ( A). To further validate the disordered nature of the ternary complex,
we acquired triple-resonance NMR spectra of 4.1G-CTD in three distinct
states: the free state, the D2-bound binary complex, and the D2/NuMA-bound
ternary complex. The analysis revealed that 13Cα
chemical shifts of free 4.1G-CTD closely matched random coil reference
value, except for the α-helical segment (residues 968–982)
which showed significant deviations (Δ13Cα
> 1 ppm) ( B),
consistent with its stable secondary structure within the overall
disordered context. Notably, ligand-bound states (both binary and
ternary complexes) exhibited 13Cα chemical shift
patterns nearly identical to the free state ( B), demonstrating the absence of substantial
secondary structure formation in 4.1G-CTD/D2 and 4.1G-CTD/D2/NuMA
complexes. These findings provide direct evidence that 4.1G-CTD retains
its intrinsically disordered characteristics in both binary and ternary
complexes.
 We also predicted the structures of the 4.1G-CTD/D2/NuMA
ternary
complex using AlphaFold3 (AF3) C).

## Discussion
In this study, we have elucidated the interaction
between 4.1G-CTD
and dopamine receptor D2. Similar to the interaction observed between
4.1G-CTD and NuMA, this interaction involves two intrinsically disordered
protein regions, resulting in a complex that remains the structure
disorder. The mutagenesis experiments demonstrated that the binding
sites for D2 on 4.1G-CTD encompass all the NuMA binding sites; however,
the ITC-derived thermodynamic quantities ( D) and the FCS-measured hydrodynamic signatures
( B) suggest
that these two complexes are formed through different binding modalities.
These biophysical differences persist despite the relatively high
sequence similarity between D2 and NuMA (Figure S13), suggesting that subtle structural variations translate
into distinct binding mechanisms.
Despite the significant overlap
in their binding regions, D2 and
NuMA can simultaneously associate with 4.1G-CTD to form a ternary
complex. a conclusion is supported by multiple lines of evidence.
The insertion binding mode observed in the crystal structure of the
4.1G-CTD-NuMA mutant may also be applicable
to D2 binding. However, within the ternary complex, it is more likely
that NuMA preferentially occupies the insertion binding site, while
D2 interacts with the opposite side of the βB strand of 4.1G-CTD,
as suggested by the AF3-predicted structure (Figure S12). In this proposed structure of the ternary complex, the
βB strand may appear to serve as a common binding site for both
D2 and NuMA, as manifested in the mutagenesis results. Nonetheless,
it is imperative to emphasize that the AF3 predicted structure could
not repent the structure ensemble of the ternary complex, which remains
dynamic and disordered. The predicted structure may illustrate one
of the major conformations.
More intriguingly, D2 appears to
enhance the binding of NuMA, and
conversely, NuMA enhances the binding of D2, suggesting a synergistic
interplay between these binding events. This concurrent cooperative
binding facilitates the dynamic modulation of interaction modes through
precise tuning of residue-specific interactions, ultimately stabilizing
the 4.1G-CTD conformation.
The cooperative binding of D2 and
NuMA to 4.1G-CTD serves as a
prototype of intrinsically disordered protein (IDP)-mediated interactions.
All three components exhibit intrinsic disorder, and the resulting
ternary complex retains an overall disordered structure. Due to challenges
with sample quality, we were unable to use NMR to characterize labeled
D2 and NuMA and monitor their structural changes upon complex formation.
The persistent structural disorder of 4.1G-CTD within the complex
suggests that large-scale folding-upon-binding is unlikely.
D2 and NuMA share largely overlapping interaction regions on 4.1G-CTD
while exhibiting cooperative rather than competitive binding behavior.
Positive cooperativity, an established paradigm in structured proteins,
is thought to occur via allosteric mechanisms. In contrast, the structural
plasticity of IDPs, combined with the multivalent nature of their
interactions, provides greater flexibility for modulating and fine-tuning
the binding interfaces. In this context, 4.1G-CTD serves as a typical
intrinsically disordered domain (IDD), possessing substantial structural
plasticity and containing certain local structural motifs, thereby
facilitating the cooperative binding of multiple ligands. This distinctive
mechanism provides enhanced adaptability for fine-tuning molecular
recognition processes, offering evolutionary advantages in regulating
complex cellular signaling networks.
The 4.1 family of proteins
functions as crucial near-membrane scaffolds
that integrate membrane signals with downstream effectors. The interaction
partners of 4.1-CTD identified to date primarily include membrane
proteins, while NuMA represents a notable
exception, suggesting distinct functional pathways mediated through
D2 and NuMA binding. This ability of 4.1G-CTD to simultaneously engage
both NuMA and D2 implies the potential for the concurrent operation
of these functional pathways. This observation raises intriguing questions
regarding the binding exclusivity between 4.1G-CTD’s other
membrane protein partners and D2, a critical aspect that requires
systematic investigation to fully understand the regulatory network
orchestrated by 4.1G-CTD.
