id="abs0010"><p>Fibrolamellar hepatocellular carcinoma (FLC) is a rare liver cancer caused by a dominant recurrent fusion of the heat shock protein (DNAJB1) and the catalytic subunit of protein kinase A (PRKACA). Current therapies such as chemotherapy and radiation have limited efficacy, and new treatment options are needed urgently. We have previously shown that FLC tumors are dependent on the fusion kinase DNAJB1::PRKACA, making the oncokinase an ideal drug target. mRNA degrading modalities such as antisense oligonucleotides or small interfering RNAs (siRNAs) provide an opportunity to specifically target the fusion junction. Here, we identify a potent and specific siRNA that inhibits DNAJB1::PRKACA expression. We found expression of the asialoglycoprotein receptor in FLC to be maintained at sufficient levels to effectively deliver siRNA conjugated to the GalNAc ligand. We observe productive uptake and siRNA activity in FLC patient-derived xenografts (PDX) models <italic toggle="yes">in vitro</italic> and <italic toggle="yes">in vivo</italic>. Knockdown of DNAJB1::PRKACA results in durable growth inhibition of FLC PDX i<italic toggle="yes">n vivo</italic> with no detectable toxicities. Our results suggest that this approach could be a treatment option for FLC patients.</p></abstract><abstract abstract-type="graphical" id="abs0015"><title>Graphical abstract</title><fig id="undfig1" position="anchor" orientation="portrait"><graphic xmlns:xlink="http://www.w3.org/1999/xlink" position="float" orientation="portrait" xlink:href="fx1.jpg"><?image-name fx1.jpg?><?image-size 248966?><?image-md5 5b2060d4df6e7fe26371922877920889?><?image-image-server-status LOAD_COMPLETED?><?image-original-height 996?><?image-original-width 996?><?image-scaled-height 664?><?image-scaled-width 664?><?image-cloudpmc-urn urn:cdn:blobs/c1e1/10787139/5b2060d4df6e/fx1.jpg?><?thumb-name fx1.gif?><?thumb-size 72708?><?thumb-md5 f95d3c241532772b9554aeb6db970c30?><?thumb-image-server-status NEVER_LOAD?><?thumb-scaled-height 100?><?thumb-scaled-width 100?><?thumb-cloudpmc-urn urn:cdn:blobs/c1e1/10787139/f95d3c241532/fx1.gif?></graphic></fig></abstract><abstract abstract-type="teaser" id="abs0020"><p>Simon and colleagues discovered that a small interfering RNA (siRNA) that inhibits the fusion kinase DNAJB1:PRKACA drives fibrolamellar hepatocellular carcinoma (FLC). BY-NC-ND</meta-value></custom-meta></custom-meta-group></article-meta></front><body><sec id="sec1"><title>Introduction</title><p id="p0010">Fibrolamellar hepatocellular carcinoma (FLC) is a rare liver cancer that occurs predominantly in young adults with no history of chronic liver disease.<xref rid="bib1" ref-type="bibr"><sup>1</sup></xref> There are currently no effective treatments for FLC, and patients often face poor outcomes due to the lack of targeted therapies and the limited efficacy of conventional treatments such as chemotherapy, radiation, and surgery. To date, surgery is the only curative option.<xref rid="bib3" ref-type="bibr"><sup>2</sup></xref><sup>,</sup><xref rid="bib4" ref-type="bibr"><sup>3</sup></xref> FLC is characterized by a chromosomal rearrangement resulting in the fusion of the <italic toggle="yes">DNAJB1</italic> and <italic toggle="yes">PRKACA</italic> genes, which leads to the expression of a chimeric protein with constitutive kinase activity.<xref rid="bib2" ref-type="bibr"><sup>4</sup></xref></p><p id="p0015">In recent years, RNAi has emerged as a promising therapeutic strategy for various diseases, including cancer.<xref rid="bib5" ref-type="bibr"><sup>5</sup></xref> However, the clinical application of RNAi has been hindered by the difficulty in delivering small interfering RNAs (siRNAs) to target cells and tissues, because siRNAs are unable to cross the cell membrane. To address these challenges, various siRNA delivery systems, including nanoparticles, liposomes, and conjugates have been developed.<xref rid="bib6" ref-type="bibr"><sup>6</sup></xref> Among these, conjugates of siRNAs with ligands that specifically bind to receptors on target cells have shown promise for the targeted delivery of siRNAs. Most prominently, siRNAs conjugated to the ligand <italic toggle="yes">N</italic>-acetylgalactosamine (GalNAc) bind to the asialoglycoprotein receptor (ASGR), which is highly expressed on liver cells. GalNAc-siRNA have been approved in several indications, such as hereditary transthyretin-mediated amyloidosis and atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease.<xref rid="bib7" ref-type="bibr"><sup>7</sup></xref></p><p id="p0020">We have previously demonstrated that the fusion gene <italic toggle="yes">DNAJB1::PRKACA</italic> is sufficient to trigger the formation of FLC in healthy mouse liver, and the inhibition of <italic toggle="yes">DNAJB1::PRKACA</italic> using a small hairpin RNA (shRNA) targeting the fusion junction resulted in cell death.<xref rid="bib8" ref-type="bibr"><sup>8</sup></xref><sup>,</sup><xref rid="bib9" ref-type="bibr"><sup>9</sup></xref><sup>,</sup><xref rid="bib10" ref-type="bibr"><sup>10</sup></xref> This demonstrates that DNAJB1:PRKACA is the oncogenic driver, and thus a potential drug target. Because FLC is a liver cancer, we hypothesized that ASGR protein levels could be sufficiently maintained to deliver siRNA targeting <italic toggle="yes">DNAJB1::PRKACA</italic> to FLC cells, resulting in targeted gene silencing. Previous studies have indicated that ASGR mRNA and protein are reduced in hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC).<xref rid="bib11" ref-type="bibr"><sup>11</sup></xref> However, even in cases in which the expression of ASGR is reduced by 50%, it is still sufficient to drive the pharmacology of GalNAc conjugates, achieving potent knockdown <italic toggle="yes">in vivo.</italic><xref rid="bib12" ref-type="bibr"><sup>12</sup></xref> This has been confirmed also in models of HCC with reduced ASGR levels, in which GalNAc-conjugated antisense oligonucleotides (ASOs) have shown improved pharmacology <italic toggle="yes">in vivo</italic>.<xref rid="bib13" ref-type="bibr"><sup>13</sup></xref></p><p id="p0025">Our results demonstrate that ASGR is sufficiently expressed in multiple FLC patient-derived xenograft (PDX) models to efficiently deliver GalNAc-conjugated siRNA that specifically silences the expression of the <italic toggle="yes">DNAJB1::PRKACA</italic> fusion oncogene in FLC cells <italic toggle="yes">in vitro</italic> with minimal effect on native <italic toggle="yes">DNAJB1</italic> and <italic toggle="yes">PRKACA</italic>. In addition, GalNAc-siRNA induces robust gene silencing in FLC PDX models <italic toggle="yes">in vivo</italic> and inhibits tumor growth. More important, we observed no apparent toxicity in mice treated with siRNA. Taken together, our findings suggest that GalNAc-conjugated siRNA targeting <italic toggle="yes">DNAJB1::PRKACA</italic> is a promising therapeutic strategy for FLC and highlights the potential of targeted delivery of ligand-conjugated siRNAs as a viable strategy for cancer therapy.</p></sec><sec id="sec2"><title>Results</title><p id="p0030">In this study, we designed siRNAs targeting the fusion junction of <italic toggle="yes">DNAJB1::PRKACA</italic> based on our previously described shRNA screen.<xref rid="bib10" ref-type="bibr"><sup>10</sup></xref> To enhance circulation time and nuclease resistance we modified siRNAs with phosphorothioate backbones on 5′ and 3′ ends and 5′-vinylphosphonate to increase potency.<xref rid="bib6" ref-type="bibr"><sup>6</sup></xref><sup>,</sup><xref rid="bib14" ref-type="bibr"><sup>14</sup></xref><sup>,</sup><xref rid="bib15" ref-type="bibr"><sup>15</sup></xref> In addition, siRNAs were conjugated to GalNAc on the 5′ end of the passenger strand (<xref rid="fig1" ref-type="fig">Figure 1</xref>A). We tested five siRNAs on Huh7 cells, an HCC cell line, expressing the <italic toggle="yes">DNAJB1::PRKACA</italic> fusion mRNA under the <italic toggle="yes">DNAJB1</italic> promoter. Cells were transfected with siRNAs and after 48 h analyzed for expression levels (<xref rid="fig1" ref-type="fig">Figure 1</xref>B). siRNA 1784402 showed the most potent knockdown of the fusion mRNA with a half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC<sub>50</sub>) of 1 pM (<xref rid="fig1" ref-type="fig">Figure 1</xref>C). Moreover, we found that 1784402 showed a roughly 30-fold higher specificity to <italic toggle="yes">DNAJB1::PRKACA</italic> compared to native <italic toggle="yes">PRKACA</italic> and no effect on <italic toggle="yes">DNAJB1</italic> (<xref rid="fig1" ref-type="fig">Figures 1</xref>C and 1D).<fig id="fig1" position="float" orientation="portrait"><label>Figure 1</label><caption><p>Potent and specific siRNA-mediated knockdown of <italic toggle="yes">DNAJB1::PRKACA</italic></p><p>(A) Sequences of siRNAs targeting the fusion junction; red and blue letters denote DNAJB1 and PRKACA sequences, respectively. (B) qRT-PCR results of Huh7-DNAJB1:PRKACA cells transfected with siRNAs in a dose-response experiment. RNA was profiled after 48 h and normalized to β-actin. n = 3 for all conditions, mean ± SD. (C) qRT-PCR results in Huh7-DNAJB1:PRKACA of 1784402 transfection, demonstrating preferential activity on <italic toggle="yes">DNAJB1::PRKACA</italic> over native <italic toggle="yes">PRKACA</italic> and <italic toggle="yes">DNAJB1</italic>. n = 3 for all conditions, mean ± SD. (D) Western blot analysis of Huh7-DNAJB1:PRKACA transfected with 1784402 at various concentrations.</p></caption><graphic xmlns:xlink="http://www.w3.org/1999/xlink" position="float" orientation="portrait" xlink:href="gr1.jpg"><?image-name gr1.jpg?><?image-size 208940?><?image-md5 925e2d4705f9720c7e8c20aac4de0b1b?><?image-image-server-status LOAD_COMPLETED?><?image-original-height 1476?><?image-original-width 2916?><?image-scaled-height 369?><?image-scaled-width 729?><?image-cloudpmc-urn urn:cdn:blobs/c1e1/10787139/925e2d4705f9/gr1.jpg?><?thumb-name gr1.gif?><?thumb-size 73044?><?thumb-md5 9d70a4497466cca48dd37a91866b050a?><?thumb-image-server-status NEVER_LOAD?><?thumb-scaled-height 80?><?thumb-scaled-width 158?><?thumb-cloudpmc-urn urn:cdn:blobs/c1e1/10787139/9d70a4497466/gr1.gif?></graphic></fig></p><p id="p0035">Liver-directed delivery of siRNA using the ASGR-GalNAc system has shown great success in genetic diseases, with multiple antisense drugs approved and in clinical trials using the GalNAc modality.<xref rid="bib7" ref-type="bibr"><sup>7</sup></xref><sup>,</sup><xref rid="bib16" ref-type="bibr"><sup>16</sup></xref> Because FLC is a liver tumor, we hypothesized that expression of ASGR could be maintained at high enough levels to allow the delivery of GalNAc-conjugated siRNAs to tumors. Factors that would affect the efficacy of GalNAc-conjugated siRNA through ASGR include the levels of expression, the amount on the surface, and internalization and recycling rates. We evaluated levels of the <italic toggle="yes">ASGR1</italic> transcript in multiple FLC patients and PDX samples, including primary tumor, recurrence, metastasis, and respective PDX (<xref rid="fig2" ref-type="fig">Figure 2</xref>A). We found that <italic toggle="yes">ASGR1</italic> expression was lower in primary tumors, metastasis, and recurrences relative to the adjacent nontransformed liver, although it was increased in PDX. The decrease in the patient tumor may be due to a different tumor microenvironment, dedifferentiation of FLC, or infiltration of stromal cells that do not express ASGR1. Bulk sequencing may not accurately capture the expression level because immune and stromal cells, which are negative for <italic toggle="yes">ASGR1</italic>, could dilute signal from the FLC tumor. This could also explain why expression was found to be higher in PDX models because these are more homogeneous tissues. To assess protein levels in FLC cells, we performed immunofluorescence on two rare patient samples for which healthy liver, primary tumor, and metastasis were available. We observed that ASGR1 protein in primary tumors and metastasis was similar in localization and abundance compared to healthy hepatocytes (<xref rid="fig2" ref-type="fig">Figures 2</xref>B and 2C). More important, immunofluorescence allowed us to observe a clear demarcation of ASGR1 levels between tumor cells and stromal cells as seen in FLC7 primary tumor tissue (asterisk), highlighting that expression levels of <italic toggle="yes">ASGR1</italic> based on bulk sequencing can be affected by <italic toggle="yes">ASGR1</italic><sup>−</sup>stromal cells. Although bulk RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) in FLC7 indicated that primary and metastatic tumors had lower expression levels of <italic toggle="yes">ASGR1</italic> than healthy liver, we found this to not be the case in the primary tumor when quantifying protein levels in FLC cells (<xref rid="fig2" ref-type="fig">Figure 2</xref>B). In FLC8, we found that in agreement with RNA-seq data, ASGR1 levels were increased in both primary and metastatic tumor tissue compared to matched liver tissue. However, in both cases we observed a greater heterogeneity of ASGR1 expression in FLC cells compared to liver (<xref rid="fig2" ref-type="fig">Figure 2</xref>C). This could be relevant for the potential therapeutic efficacy of GalNAc-siRNAs because ASGR-low cells may take up reduced amounts of siRNA and be more resistant therapy. To test this we selected two PDX models with different <italic toggle="yes">ASGR1</italic> expression levels—FLC1 and FLC9—with high and low expression, respectively (<xref rid="fig2" ref-type="fig">Figures 2</xref>A and 2D). We note that the FLC9 ASGR1 signal is more perinuclear compared to more cytoplasmic localization in FLC1, which could represent reduced cycling of ASGR to the plasma membrane in FLC9.<fig id="fig2" position="float" orientation="portrait"><label>Figure 2</label><caption><p>ASGR1 in FLC</p><p>(A) RNA expression analysis of ASGR1 across patient samples of normal liver, primary tumor, recurrence, metastasis, and PDX passage 1. (B) Immunofluorescence of ASGR1 in tumor-adjacent healthy liver, primary tumor, and metastasis of 2 patient samples, patient FLC7 and FLC8. Asterisk marks ASGR1<sup>−</sup> stromal cells. All fields are 210 × 210 μm. (C) Quantification of signal intensity per cell from multiple fields of view from immunofluorescence images of ASGR1 in tumor-adjacent healthy liver (FLC7: n = 42; FLC8: n = 59), primary tumor (FLC7: n = 64; FLC8: n = 73), and metastasis (FLC7: n = 48; FLC8: n = 55) of patient FLC7 and FLC8 tissue. Mean ± SD, ordinary 1-way ANOVA and Dunnett’s multiple comparison test for post hoc comparisons. Significance levels: ∗p &lt; 0.05; ∗∗p &lt; 0.01; ∗∗∗p &lt; 0.001; ∗∗∗∗p &lt; 0.0001. (D) Immunofluorescence images of ASGR1 in PDX tissue derived from FLC1 and FLC9. All fields are 210 × 210 μm. Higher magnification of 2 cells of FLC1 and FLC9 at 20 × 20 μm.</p></caption><graphic xmlns:xlink="http://www.w3.org/1999/xlink" position="float" orientation="portrait" xlink:href="gr2.jpg"><?image-name gr2.jpg?><?image-size 487849?><?image-md5 77e4a9062b68edf058a983101767b416?><?image-image-server-status LOAD_COMPLETED?><?image-original-height 2718?><?image-original-width 3368?><?image-scaled-height 604?><?image-scaled-width 748?><?image-cloudpmc-urn urn:cdn:blobs/c1e1/10787139/77e4a9062b68/gr2.jpg?><?thumb-name gr2.gif?><?thumb-size 77727?><?thumb-md5 44bb769a78548fae39d5165e614e3f96?><?thumb-image-server-status NEVER_LOAD?><?thumb-scaled-height 81?><?thumb-scaled-width 100?><?thumb-cloudpmc-urn urn:cdn:blobs/c1e1/10787139/44bb769a7854/gr2.gif?></graphic></fig></p><p id="p0040">To test for productive uptake via ASGR, we treated cells of FLC1 and FLC9 PDX models with 1784402 GalNAc conjugates or with 1526196-control GalNAc-conjugated siRNA targeting coagulation factor 12 (<italic toggle="yes">F12)</italic> for 48 h. The siRNAs were added to cells immediately upon plating <italic toggle="yes">in vitro,</italic> because GalNAc conjugates quickly lose activity. This is potentially due to reduced endocytic recycling of ASGR upon dissociation and plating <italic toggle="yes">in vitro</italic> (unpublished data). We observed IC<sub>50</sub> values of 4 nM (FLC1) and 1.2 μM (FLC9) (<xref rid="fig3" ref-type="fig">Figures 3</xref>A and 3B). These values were higher than the 1 nM previously reported for primary mouse hepatocytes, but provided evidence that ASGR is present at sufficient levels to mediate delivery of siRNAs in FLC in both ASGR1 high and low cells.<xref rid="bib17" ref-type="bibr"><sup>17</sup></xref> Reduced efficacy in FLC9 is likely due to both reduced expression and cycling of ASGR as seen in PDX tissue (<xref rid="fig2" ref-type="fig">Figure 2</xref>D). Next, we assessed the effect of the siRNA on viability <italic toggle="yes">in vitro</italic>, treating freshly dissociated cells of PDX models FLC1 and FLC9, with a 10-μM dose of 1784402. Medium was replaced after 48 h before the first assessment of viability. We observed a decrease of between 50% and 70% in viability after 5 days compared to cells treated with 1526196-control siRNA (<xref rid="fig3" ref-type="fig">Figures 3</xref>C and 3D). Together, these results provide evidence that ASGR-mediated delivery of siRNA is possible in FLC cells <italic toggle="yes">in vitro,</italic> resulting in knockdown of <italic toggle="yes">DNAJB1::PRKACA</italic> and cell death. This fusion is present in nearly all cases of FLC, making it a promising target for molecular therapies. In FLC, small-molecule inhibitors of protein kinase A, such as uprosertib, can inhibit the kinase activity of fusion DNAJB1::PRKACA but show no specificity over native kinase and rapidly distribute throughout the body, resulting in systemic side effects with limited efficacy in FLC.<xref rid="bib20" ref-type="bibr"><sup>20</sup></xref> Thus, conventional small-molecule inhibitors have yet to demonstrate specific targeting of this fusion. Moreover, traditional small molecules often fail to effectively target the root cause of the disease, such as genetic alterations. In contrast, antisense therapies are highly programmable and can specifically inhibit expression of what are considered undruggable proteins.</p><p id="p0080">In this study, we developed a therapeutic strategy for FLC using a GalNAc-conjugated siRNA that targets the fusion junction of the <italic toggle="yes">DNAJB1::PRKACA</italic> oncotranscript. We demonstrate promising activity in FLC PDX models <italic toggle="yes">in vitro</italic> and <italic toggle="yes">in vivo</italic>. The GalNAc-siRNAs are productively taken up by cells with both high and low expression of ASGR. Because there is greater target knockdown in a PDX model with higher ASGR expression, a theranostic biomarker for patients probing ASGR levels may be useful to stratify patients who may benefit more from this therapeutic approach. Compared to genetic diseases for which GalNAc-siRNA have been successfully used with biannual dosing, we have used an aggressive dosing scheme due to the more proliferative nature of cancer cells similar to other preclinical studies using ASOs.<xref rid="bib21" ref-type="bibr"><sup>21</sup></xref><sup>,</sup><xref rid="bib22" ref-type="bibr"><sup>22</sup></xref> However, we did not detect apparent side effects, offering a wide therapeutic window. Our PDX model system is limited in that in human patients the liver may be a substantially bigger sink compared to the mouse liver due to the likely larger ratio of liver to tumor. Therefore, dosing in our mouse model would likely need to be adjusted to find both the optimal concentration and frequency for clinical translation.</p><p id="p0085">To date, the effective use of siRNA therapeutics in oncology is hampered by challenges to achieve efficient uptake in the cancer cell. Various delivery methods have been developed to address these challenges. Conjugation of siRNAs to GalNAc has emerged as an effective strategy for liver-specific delivery, capitalizing on the high expression and fast recycling nature of ASGRs in hepatocytes. Our approach using GalNAc-conjugated siRNA confines distribution of the siRNA to the liver and tumor tissue, thereby efficiently concentrating the siRNA in the target tissue to achieve robust knockdown of <italic toggle="yes">DNAJB1-PRKACA</italic> with no apparent toxicity. In addition, our approach specifically targets the fusion junction of <italic toggle="yes">DNAJB1::PRKACA</italic>, without knockdown of <italic toggle="yes">PRKACA</italic>.</p><p id="p0090">ASGR is a promising delivery receptor due to its high cycling rate, allowing enough siRNA to be internalized and “leak” into the cytoplasm to achieve knockdown.<xref rid="bib24" ref-type="bibr"><sup>23</sup></xref> However, other approaches have been used for delivery in oncology. For instance, folate receptor-mediated delivery and lipid nanoparticles have been used to deliver RNA to tumor cells.<xref rid="bib25" ref-type="bibr"><sup>24</sup></xref><sup>,</sup><xref rid="bib26" ref-type="bibr"><sup>25</sup></xref> MicroRNAs have been effectively targeted to breast and lung cancer models using folate conjugates. In FLC, the folate receptor is not overexpressed, but the basal expression may be sufficient for efficient delivery. Additional modalities are being developed for efficient delivery to extrahepatic tissues that could also be used to achieve uptake in tumor cells. For instance, lipid conjugates such as cholesterol have been used to deliver siRNA to tissues other than the liver.<xref rid="bib27" ref-type="bibr"><sup>26</sup></xref><sup>,</sup><xref rid="bib28" ref-type="bibr"><sup>27</sup></xref> It remains to be seen whether this approach could also deliver siRNA to tumor cells, which could depend on the cell type of origin and tissue context. Recently, C16 conjugates have been used for safe and effective delivery of siRNA to the CNS, which could be used for gene silencing in brain tumors.<xref rid="bib29" ref-type="bibr"><sup>28</sup></xref> These recent advances in targeted delivery have laid the foundation for siRNA therapies to address the need to target hard-to-drug proteins in oncology and translate to the clinic.</p><p id="p0095">Chemical modifications in conjunction with efficient delivery systems such as GalNAc conjugation or lipid nanoparticles have transformed siRNAs from a powerful laboratory tool to a promising class of therapeutics.<xref rid="bib6" ref-type="bibr"><sup>6</sup></xref> Our study highlights the potential of siRNA-based therapeutics for other cancer types that harbor fusion genes. The successful use of these strategies in the treatment of genetic diseases, along with the potential demonstrated in this study against FLC, underscores the transformative potential of siRNA therapeutics in precision medicine.</p></sec><sec id="sec4"><title>Materials and methods</title><sec id="sec4.1"><title>siRNA synthesis</title><p id="p0100">siRNAs were synthesiszed according to the reported methods.<xref rid="bib15" ref-type="bibr"><sup>15</sup></xref><sup>,</sup><xref rid="bib30" ref-type="bibr"><sup>29</sup></xref> The siRNAs targeting <italic toggle="yes">DNAJB1::PRKACA</italic> contained several modifications:<list list-type="simple" id="ulist0010"><list-item id="u0010"><label>•</label><p id="p0105">5′[vpMOE]sFsMMMFMMMMMMMFMFMMMMMsMsM 3′</p></list-item><list-item id="u0015"><label>•</label><p id="p0110">3′ MsMsMMMMMMMMFFFMFMMMMsMsM-3THAGN-5′</p></list-item><list-item id="u0020"><label>•</label><p id="p0115">[vpMOE]: vinylphosphonate-2′-<italic toggle="yes">O</italic>-MOE T</p></list-item><list-item id="u0025"><label>•</label><p id="p0120">3THAGN: triantennary GalNAc cluster</p></list-item><list-item id="u0030"><label>•</label><p id="p0125">F: 2′-Fluoro</p></list-item><list-item id="u0035"><label>•</label><p id="p0130">M: 2′-<italic toggle="yes">O</italic>-Me</p></list-item><list-item id="u0040"><label>•</label><p id="p0135">s-phosphorothioate (all backbone linkages are phosphodiesters except for ones labeled with s)</p></list-item></list></p></sec><sec id="sec4.2"><title>Cell culture</title><p id="p0140">Huh7-chimera cells were cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS).
