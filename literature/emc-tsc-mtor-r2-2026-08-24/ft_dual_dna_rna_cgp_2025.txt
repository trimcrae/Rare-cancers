To evaluate its clinical utility, we analyzed 1356 cases from the Center for Cancer Genomics and Advanced Therapeutics (C‐CAT) database. Oncogenic genomic alterations were identified in 91.5% of cases. Somatic mutations were the most prevalent, followed by amplifications and fusion/exon skipping events. The DNA panel, covering 737 genes, detected not only alterations relevant to therapeutic decisions but also those providing insights into tumor biology. Among the latter, frequently observed examples included mutations in <italic toggle="no">KMT2C</italic> (<italic toggle="no">n</italic> = 28) and <italic toggle="no">ARID1B</italic> (<italic toggle="no">n</italic> = 24), and amplifications in <italic toggle="no">GLI1</italic> (<italic toggle="no">n</italic> = 14) and <italic toggle="no">YAP1</italic> (<italic toggle="no">n</italic> = 10), which are not included in other CGP tests approved in Japan. The RNA panel identified 105 fusion events, including 11 <italic toggle="no">NTRK</italic> fusions (0.8%), of which five were <italic toggle="no">NTRK3</italic> fusions: two with the well‐known <italic toggle="no">ETV6</italic>‐<italic toggle="no">NTRK3</italic> fusion and three with non‐<italic toggle="no">ETV6</italic> partners. Forty‐nine of these fusions were diagnostically significant, highlighting the utility of the RNA panel. Amplification‐RNA expression analyses revealed strong correlations for <italic toggle="no">MDM2</italic>, <italic toggle="no">CDK4</italic>, <italic toggle="no">EGFR</italic>, and <italic toggle="no">ERBB2</italic>. In contrast, weaker correlations observed for <italic toggle="no">MYC</italic> and <italic toggle="no">FGFR1</italic> highlighted the need for careful interpretation of amplification in these genes. Cancer type significantly influenced RNA expression, with <italic toggle="no">KIT</italic> and <italic toggle="no">TERT</italic> mutations linked to increased expression and significant overexpression observed in <italic toggle="no">ALK</italic>, <italic toggle="no">FGFR3</italic>, <italic toggle="no">NTRK1</italic>, <italic toggle="no">NTRK3</italic>, and <italic toggle="no">RET</italic> fusions. In summary, this study demonstrated the real‐world clinical utility of the dual DNA–RNA CGP test and provided a valuable resource for interpreting RNA expressions.</p></abstract><abstract abstract-type="graphical"><p>This study analyzed 1356 cases using GenMineTOP, Japan's first dual DNA–RNA CGP test, and identified oncogenic alterations in 91.5% of cases. <bold>Funding:</bold> The authors received no specific funding for this work.</p></fn></fn-group></notes></front><body id="cas70071-body-0001"><def-list list-content="abbreviations" id="cas70071-dl-0001"><title>Abbreviations</title><def-item><term id="cas70071-li-0001">C‐CAT</term><def id="cas70071-li-0002"><p>Center for Cancer Genomics and Advanced Therapeutics</p></def></def-item><def-item><term id="cas70071-li-0003">CGP</term><def id="cas70071-li-0004"><p>comprehensive genomic profiling</p></def></def-item><def-item><term id="cas70071-li-0005">NGS</term><def id="cas70071-li-0006"><p>next‐generation sequencing</p></def></def-item></def-list><sec id="cas70071-sec-0001"><label>1</label><title>Introduction</title><p>The advancement of cancer genome analysis has revealed numerous driver genes responsible for tumor development and germline variants that heighten cancer susceptibility. This progress, largely driven by advancements in next‐generation sequencing (NGS) technologies, has profoundly enhanced our understanding of the molecular mechanisms of cancer. These developments have paved the way for the integration of comprehensive genomic profiling (CGP) tests into cancer diagnosis and therapeutic decision‐making. In Japan, CGP tests have been reimbursed under the national health insurance system since 2019, offering new therapeutic options for patients who have exhausted standard treatments [<xref rid="cas70071-bib-0001" ref-type="bibr">1</xref>].</p><p>The Todai OncoPanel (TOP), originally developed at the University of Tokyo, is a dual‐panel system designed to address the challenges of cancer genomic profiling. It incorporates both DNA and RNA analyses, enabling the detection of diverse genetic alterations, including single nucleotide variants (SNVs), insertions and deletions (indels), copy number variations (CNVs), gene fusions, and exon skipping events. The RNA panel employs a novel “junction capture method,” which ensures accurate detection of fusion and splicing abnormalities even in formalin‐fixed, paraffin‐embedded (FFPE) specimens, a critical feature for clinical application [<xref rid="cas70071-bib-0002" ref-type="bibr">2</xref>].</p><p>Although originally designed for clinical application, TOP was first implemented as a research tool and later transitioned to clinical deployment under Japan's Advanced Medical Care B framework, enabling the accumulation of clinical data [<xref rid="cas70071-bib-0003" ref-type="bibr">3</xref>]. Building on the findings from this clinical phase, TOP underwent enhancements, and the upgraded version, GenMineTOP, received insurance approval in 2023, marking its transition to a fully validated diagnostic test. GenMineTOP utilizes a DNA panel that analyzes 737 genes and an RNA panel, with approved diagnostic outputs including the detection of fusions in 455 genes, exon skipping in 5 genes, and the expression levels of 27 genes. Currently, two other CGP tests utilizing tissue samples, FoundationOne CDx (F1CDx) and OncoGuide NCC Oncopanel System, are approved in Japan, both of which rely solely on DNA panels. F1CDx analyzes a total of 324 genes, of which 36 genes have intron probes designed to detect fusions and rearrangements. OncoGuide NCC Oncopanel System analyzes a total of 124 genes, of which 13 genes have intron probes designed to detect fusions and rearrangements. In contrast, GenMineTOP not only incorporates an RNA panel but also analyzes a broader range of genes across both DNA and RNA panels compared to other CGP tests.</p><p>The role of RNA analysis in CGP tests has emerged as one of the central topics in CGP tests. Real‐world data from lung adenocarcinoma show that adding RNA analysis to DNA panels increases actionable fusion detection rates from 5.9% to 6.8% [<xref rid="cas70071-bib-0004" ref-type="bibr">4</xref>]. Additionally, a mathematical model predicts that RNA panels can further raise fusion detection rates for non‐small cell lung cancer by 1.3% when applied after DNA panel testing [<xref rid="cas70071-bib-0005" ref-type="bibr">5</xref>]. These findings underscore the need for more real‐world data to evaluate the clinical utility of RNA panels.</p><p>The Center for Cancer Genomics and Advanced Therapeutics (C‐CAT) plays a central role in supporting genomic medicine in Japan [<xref rid="cas70071-bib-0006" ref-type="bibr">6</xref>]. As a national database established to aggregate CGP results and associated clinical data, C‐CAT provides a platform for standardized data interpretation and research. Through curated knowledgebases, C‐CAT enables the clinical annotation of complex genomic findings, enhancing the integration of precision medicine into oncology.</p><p>In this study, we aim to evaluate the genomic alterations identified using GenMineTOP in a real‐world setting under routine clinical practice covered by health insurance. By leveraging data from the C‐CAT database, we seek to assess the clinical utility of GenMineTOP in routine cancer diagnostics. Specifically, we aim to clarify how the large number of genes analyzed in both the DNA and RNA panels enhances the detection of various genomic alterations. Furthermore, we aim to evaluate the utility of the RNA panel by assessing its detection rate for clinically significant gene fusions and exon skipping. Additionally, our study aims to address the challenge of unclear criteria for interpreting the expression levels of the 27 genes. By elucidating the factors influencing their expression, we seek to enable accurate interpretation of RNA expression and ensure their effective clinical application.</p></sec><sec sec-type="materials-and-methods" id="cas70071-sec-0002"><label>2</label><title>Material and Methods</title><sec id="cas70071-sec-0003"><label>2.1</label><title>Study Population</title><p>A total of 1356 GenMineTOP cases were registered in the C‐CAT database up to June 2024, and their clinical/genomic data were downloaded on August 23, 2024. Informed consent was obtained from all patients prior to registration. This study received approval from the Research Ethics Committee of the Faculty of Medicine at the University of Tokyo (approval number: 2021341G) and the C‐CAT Data Utilization Review Board (approval number: CDU2022‐026 N).</p></sec><sec id="cas70071-sec-0004"><label>2.2</label><title>Genomic Alterations and Annotation</title><p>The clinical significance of genomic abnormalities, including mutations, amplifications, and fusion/exon skipping events, was determined using the annotations from the C‐CAT database. For gene fusions with diagnostic relevance, additional information was supplemented through a literature review. In this study, only oncogenic genomic alterations were included in the analysis.</p></sec><sec id="cas70071-sec-0005"><label>2.3</label><title>Statistical Analysis</title><p>Group comparisons were performed using <italic toggle="yes">t</italic>‐tests or chi‐squared tests, depending on the data type. To estimate the <italic toggle="yes">NTRK</italic> fusion positive rate in F1CDx, a simulation was conducted using patient backgrounds from GenMineTOP, categorized by cancer type and age group (in 10‐year increments). F1CDx cases from the C‐CAT database (registered by December 2024, downloaded on December 27, 2024) were filtered to match these combinations. Subsets of F1CDx cases were iteratively sampled to align with GenMineTOP backgrounds, and the <italic toggle="yes">NTRK</italic> fusion positive rate was calculated. This was repeated 10,000 times for robust estimation.</p><p>Gene expression levels were log‐transformed as log<sub>10</sub>(TPM + 1) for all analyses, including correlation and multivariate analyses. Spearman's correlation coefficient evaluated relationships between expression and copy numbers. A multivariate analysis was conducted to evaluate the independent effects of cancer type and genomic abnormalities (gene amplification, fusion, and oncogenic somatic mutation) on gene expression. This included 1089 cases from the top 11 cancer types, each comprising at least 50 cases. Among these, RNA expression data were available for 1056 cases, and the analysis used this subset. For each gene, log‐transformed expression levels were modeled using these four explanatory variables. Type III ANOVA was applied to obtain <italic toggle="yes">p</italic> values and partial eta‐squared (<italic toggle="yes">η</italic>
<sup>2</sup>) values, which quantified the effect sizes of each factor on gene expression. All analyses were performed using R version 4.0.2, and a <italic toggle="yes">p</italic> value of &lt; 0.05 was considered statistically significant.</p></sec></sec><sec sec-type="results" id="cas70071-sec-0006"><label>3</label><title>Results</title><sec id="cas70071-sec-0007"><label>3.1</label><title>Patient Background and Quality Assessment</title><p>A total of 1356 cases were analyzed, comprising 675 men and 681 women. The patients' ages ranged from 0 to 90 years, with a median age of 60 years and a mean age of 56.7 years. Cases were most frequently observed in the 50–79‐year age group, as illustrated in Figure <xref rid="cas70071-fig-0001" ref-type="fig">1a</xref>. Regarding cancer types, bowel (<italic toggle="yes">n</italic> = 209), soft tissue (<italic toggle="yes">n</italic> = 157), and CNS/brain (<italic toggle="yes">n</italic> = 151) were the most common (Figure <xref rid="cas70071-fig-0001" ref-type="fig">1b</xref>).</p><fig position="float" fig-type="FIGURE" id="cas70071-fig-0001" orientation="portrait"><label>FIGURE 1</label><caption><p>Age, cancer types, and landscape of genomic alterations. (b) Cancer type distribution: Pie chart displaying the distribution of cases by cancer type, with numbers indicating case counts and colors corresponding to specific cancer types. (c) Landscape of genomic alterations: Intersection plot illustrating the relationships between genomic alterations. The GenMineTOP DNA panel reports TMB only when all of the following quality criteria are satisfied: (1) tumor DNA input of at least 100 ng, (2) pre‐capture library yield of at least 187.5 ng, (3) post‐capture library concentration of at least 4.0 nM, and (4) total sequencing reads of at least 40 million. Therefore, the proportion of cases with reported TMB reflects the proportion of samples meeting these criteria. Similarly, the GenMineTOP RNA panel reports RNA expression levels only when all of the following quality criteria are met: (1) tumor RNA input of at least 200 ng, (2) pre‐capture library yield of at least 187.5 ng, (3) post‐capture library concentration of at least 4.0 nM, and (4) total sequencing reads of at least 20 million. Consequently, the proportion of cases with reported RNA expression levels reflects the proportion of samples meeting these quality standards.</p><p>Across all cases analyzed, 98.5% of samples met the quality criteria for the DNA panel, and 97.1% met the criteria for the RNA panel, indicating high overall success rates. Somatic mutations were the most prevalent, followed by amplifications and fusion/exon skipping events. Notably, fusion/exon skipping events were more frequently observed in mutation‐negative cases, underscoring the complementary role of RNA panels in detecting these alterations.</p><p>We compared our findings with previous large‐scale studies. A study using C‐CAT data from F1CDx and NCC Oncopanel [<xref rid="cas70071-bib-0034" ref-type="bibr">34</xref>, <xref rid="cas70071-bib-0035" ref-type="bibr">35</xref>] reported <italic toggle="yes">TP53</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">KRAS</italic>, and <italic toggle="yes">APC</italic> as the most frequent mutations, consistent with our results. Fusion detection rates were 2.6% (F1CDx) and 0.8% (NCC Oncopanel). In our study, GenMineTOP detected fusions in 7.7%. In the MSK‐IMPACT cohort [<xref rid="cas70071-bib-0035" ref-type="bibr">35</xref>], <italic toggle="yes">TP53</italic> was the most frequent alteration (41%), with <italic toggle="yes">KRAS</italic> mutations in 90% of pancreatic and 44% of colorectal cancers, and fusion detections in 15%. Differences in patient backgrounds, target genes, and pipelines may explain the differences in fusion detection.</p><p>The GenMineTOP RNA panel demonstrated high reliability, with 97.1% of cases meeting quality criteria. In this study, 78.6% (11/14) of samples stored for more than 5 years met quality criteria for the DNA panel, while 92.9% (13/14) met the criteria for the RNA panel. Although the difference was not statistically significant based on Fisher's exact test, the RNA panel showed a higher success rate, demonstrating the robustness of the RNA panel under real‐world conditions. This observation aligns with our previous findings [<xref rid="cas70071-bib-0036" ref-type="bibr">36</xref>], which showed that while DNA quality, as assessed by ddCq and Q values, significantly declined over time with differences between the 3–5 years and over 5 years groups, RNA quality degradation plateaued after 3 years. Specifically, 49% of samples older than 5 years achieved a DV200 value of 50 or higher.</p><p>This study identified mutations in <italic toggle="yes">KMT2C</italic> and <italic toggle="yes">ARID1B</italic>, and amplifications in <italic toggle="yes">GLI1</italic> and <italic toggle="yes">YAP1</italic>, which are not included in other CGP tests approved in Japan. <italic toggle="yes">KMT2C</italic> and <italic toggle="yes">ARID1B</italic> are epigenetic regulatory genes pivotal in chromatin remodeling and transcriptional regulation, processes crucial for tumorigenesis [<xref rid="cas70071-bib-0007" ref-type="bibr">7</xref>, <xref rid="cas70071-bib-0008" ref-type="bibr">8</xref>]. Amplifications of <italic toggle="yes">GLI1</italic>, associated with Sonic Hedgehog pathway activation, have been linked to poor prognosis in mesenchymal tumors [<xref rid="cas70071-bib-0037" ref-type="bibr">37</xref>], and <italic toggle="yes">YAP1</italic> amplifications promote aggressive phenotypes in several cancers, including lung and cervical cancers [<xref rid="cas70071-bib-0038" ref-type="bibr">38</xref>]. The detection of these mutations and amplifications provides valuable insights into tumor biology, even if their current clinical relevance remains limited.</p><p>RNA panels are superior to DNA panels in detecting fusions and exon skipping events due to differences in probe design [<xref rid="cas70071-bib-0039" ref-type="bibr">39</xref>]. DNA panels require probes targeting intronic regions, which make up about 25% of the human genome and often contain repetitive sequences. In contrast, RNA panels design probes on exonic regions that constitute only 1%–2% of the genome and have fewer repetitive sequences, making design simpler and more efficient. For exon skipping, RNA panels can directly detect missing exons at the transcript level, regardless of the underlying genomic changes.</p><p>In lung adenocarcinoma, adding whole transcriptome RNA sequencing to DNA panels increased the overall actionable fusion detection rate from 5.9% to 6.8%, raising the number of detected cases from 329 to 376 out of 5570. The addition of RNA sequencing also increased the number of <italic toggle="yes">MET</italic> exon 14 skipping‐positive cases from 97 to 115, further highlighting the contribution of RNA‐based approaches to detecting actionable alterations [<xref rid="cas70071-bib-0004" ref-type="bibr">4</xref>]. We have also demonstrated that <italic toggle="yes">MET</italic> exon skipping detection rates are high using RNA panels in lung cancer [<xref rid="cas70071-bib-0040" ref-type="bibr">40</xref>]. In another study, a mathematical model estimating the contribution of RNA panels after DNA panels in non‐small cell lung cancer predicts that approximately 1.3% of cases harbor fusions detectable only through RNA analysis, raising the overall detection rate from 4.2% to 5.5% [<xref rid="cas70071-bib-0005" ref-type="bibr">5</xref>].</p><p>In the present study, the analysis included only 59 lung cancer cases, and we focused on <italic toggle="yes">NTRK</italic> fusions due to their clinical significance to guide targeted therapies. The prevalence of <italic toggle="yes">NTRK</italic> fusions in this cohort was 0.8%. Previous reports from Japanese databases have shown prevalence rates of 0.22% using F1CDx and 0.15% using OncoGuide NCC Oncopanel System [<xref rid="cas70071-bib-0012" ref-type="bibr">12</xref>]. F1CDx detects <italic toggle="yes">NTRK1</italic> fusion, <italic toggle="yes">NTRK2</italic> fusion, and <italic toggle="yes">ETV6</italic>‐<italic toggle="yes">NTRK3</italic> fusion, whereas both GenMineTOP and OncoGuide NCC Oncopanel System target fusions involving all three <italic toggle="yes">NTRK</italic> genes (<italic toggle="yes">NTRK</italic>1‐3). Notably, this study identified five cases of <italic toggle="yes">NTRK3</italic> fusions, of which two involved the well‐characterized <italic toggle="yes">ETV6</italic>‐<italic toggle="yes">NTRK3</italic> fusion. The remaining three cases demonstrated non‐<italic toggle="yes">ETV6</italic> partners, including two cases with <italic toggle="yes">EML4</italic>‐<italic toggle="yes">NTRK3</italic> and one case with <italic toggle="yes">KHDRBS1</italic>‐<italic toggle="yes">NTRK3</italic>. This finding underscores the diversity of <italic toggle="yes">NTRK3</italic> fusion partners and highlights the importance of covering such diversity to ensure comprehensive detection. In addition to data from Japanese cohorts, the prevalence of <italic toggle="yes">NTRK</italic> fusions has been reported in large‐scale pan‐cancer genomic databases from outside Japan. In the Foundation Medicine genomic database, <italic toggle="yes">NTRK</italic> fusions were identified in 244 out of 459,751 cases (0.05%), while the GENIE database reported 76 out of 97,592 cases (0.08%) [<xref rid="cas70071-bib-0005" ref-type="bibr">5</xref>]. This study estimated the <italic toggle="yes">NTRK</italic> fusion positive rate in the F1CDx dataset by aligning patient backgrounds, including cancer types and age distributions, with those in GenMineTOP. The simulation showed a 95% CI for the estimated positive rate ranging from 0.147% to 0.739%, with a median of 0.44%. The observed positive rate in GenMineTOP (0.8%) exceeded the upper limit of the simulated 95% CI, suggesting that the higher detection rate in GenMineTOP cannot be attributed solely to differences in cancer types or age distributions. However, as the comparison is based on simulation, unmeasured confounding factors may exist. While notable, this trend should be interpreted with caution, as direct comparison is not feasible. Additionally, the higher detection rate in GenMineTOP may not be solely due to the use of an RNA panel but may also be at least partly attributed to the inclusion of <italic toggle="yes">NTRK3</italic> as a target gene. This underscores the importance of probe design in RNA panels to ensure comprehensive detection of fusion genes. As shown in Table <xref rid="cas70071-tbl-0003" ref-type="table">3</xref>, this study identified a total of 49 diagnostically relevant fusions, many of which were associated with sarcomas. These findings underscore the diagnostic utility of the RNA panel in real‐world settings.</p><p>In this study, exon skipping events were identified without splicing junction mutations at the DNA level, confirming that RNA analysis enables the detection of exon skipping regardless of genomic alterations. Expanding exon skipping analyses beyond the five approved genes may aid in assessing splice site mutation pathogenicity.</p><p>The relationship between gene expression and copy number was analyzed for nine genes with frequent amplifications. Among these genes, amplification significantly influenced expression levels, although the magnitude of this effect varied. <italic toggle="yes">CDK4</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">EGFR</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">ERBB2</italic>, and <italic toggle="yes">MDM2</italic> showed strong correlations between amplification and expression levels, while <italic toggle="yes">FGFR1</italic> and <italic toggle="yes">MYC</italic> exhibited weaker associations. For these latter genes, the overlap in expression levels between amplification‐positive and amplification‐negative cases suggests that amplification may not correctly reflect increased expression. In colorectal cancer, <italic toggle="yes">MYC</italic> expression is frequently elevated even in the absence of amplification, as activation of the Wnt/β‐catenin signaling pathway drives <italic toggle="yes">MYC</italic> transcription [<xref rid="cas70071-bib-0041" ref-type="bibr">41</xref>]. Consistent with this, our data confirmed that <italic toggle="yes">APC</italic> mutations correlate with higher <italic toggle="yes">MYC</italic> expression. These findings suggest that not all amplifications serve as effective biomarkers for targeted therapy, emphasizing the need for careful interpretation of amplification data. RNA analysis could help refine copy number interpretation by identifying functionally relevant amplifications, though further studies are needed to confirm its utility.</p><p>Gene expression levels were also strongly influenced by cancer type. Multivariate analyses and violin plots (Figure <xref rid="cas70071-supitem-0002" ref-type="supplementary-material">S2</xref>) revealed that cancer type had a significant impact on gene expression. Generally, tumor gene expression is largely influenced by cell origin‐related factors, including epigenetic modifications and lineage‐specific transcriptional programs [<xref rid="cas70071-bib-0042" ref-type="bibr">42</xref>]. For example, <italic toggle="yes">ALK</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">NTRK2</italic>, and <italic toggle="yes">NTRK3</italic> were highly expressed in CNS/brain cancers, while <italic toggle="yes">ROS1</italic> was highly expressed in lung cancers. Such patterns highlight the importance of considering cancer type when interpreting expression data. While our data provide valuable insights, further data collection is necessary to validate the results.</p><p>This study has several limitations. First, the retrospective nature of the analysis may introduce inherent biases and limit the generalizability of the findings. For example, the dataset shows a relative enrichment for cases of soft tissue and CNS/brain origin, which may introduce a bias compared to the general population of patients undergoing CGP testing in Japan. Second, with respect to the discussion on quality, the C‐CAT database includes only cases for which the analysis was completed. Cases excluded due to poor nucleic acid quality or other factors leading to discontinuation of the analysis are not registered in the database. As a result, the true proportion of cases meeting quality standards remains uncertain. Third, although we detected many fusions with potential diagnostic utility, information on how diagnoses were revised based on CGP testing results was largely absent from the C‐CAT database.</p><p>In conclusion, this study demonstrates the utility of RNA panels in detecting clinically relevant fusions and exon skipping events while also highlighting the benefits of analyzing a large number of genes in both DNA and RNA panels. While the impact of amplification on expression levels varies among genes, cancer type remains a dominant factor influencing gene expression. The robustness of the RNA panel in terms of quality control was also confirmed in real‐world settings, demonstrating its reliability for clinical use.</p></sec><sec id="cas70071-sec-0017"><title>Author Contributions</title><p>
