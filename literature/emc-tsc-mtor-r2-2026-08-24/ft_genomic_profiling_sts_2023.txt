In this review, we focus on the clinical utility of next-generation sequencing (NGS) to inform therapeutics in soft tissue sarcoma (STS). The role of NGS is still controversial in patients with sarcoma, given the low mutational burden and the lack of recurrent targetable alterations in most of the sarcoma histotypes. The clinical impact of genomic profiling in STS has not been investigated prospectively. A limited number of retrospective, mainly single-institution, studies have addressed this issue using various NGS technologies and platforms and a variety of criteria to define a genomic alteration as actionable. Despite the detailed reports on the different gene mutations, fusions, or amplifications that were detected, data on the use and efficacy of targeted treatment are very scarce at present. With the exception of gastrointestinal stromal tumors (GISTs), these targeted therapies are administered either through off-label prescription of an approved drug or enrollment in a matched clinical trial. Based mainly on anecdotal reports, the outcome of targeted therapies in the different STS histotypes is discussed. Prospective studies are warranted to assess whether genomic profiling improves the management of STS patients.</p></abstract><kwd-group><kwd>next-generation sequencing</kwd><kwd>DNA BY</meta-value></custom-meta></custom-meta-group></article-meta></front><body><sec sec-type="intro" id="sec1-cells-12-02632"><title>1. Introduction</title><p>Soft tissue sarcomas (STS) constitute a group of heterogeneous tumors of mesenchymal origin that are classified into more than 100 histological types [<xref rid="B1-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">1</xref>]. Patients with advanced or metastatic disease have a poor prognosis, with a median overall survival (OS) of approximately 1.5 years from the start of therapy [<xref rid="B2-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">2</xref>]. Therefore, there is an unmet need for the development of new therapies. Tumor-agnostic precision medicine has been developed over the last decade, employing comprehensive genomic profiling. High-throughput, large-scale sequencing capacity of next-generation sequencing (NGS) technologies holds the promise of identifying genomic alterations that are pharmacologically tractable.</p><p>In STS, the translation of genomic discoveries to the clinic is still under investigation. Although molecular methods have been traditionally used in STS diagnostics [<xref rid="B3-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">3</xref>,<xref rid="B4-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">4</xref>], their potential application in theranostics is still debatable [<xref rid="B5-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">5</xref>]. Notwithstanding the relatively high occurrence of clinically actionable biomarkers in different STS histotypes identified by recent NGS studies, the clinical benefit of these findings seems low [<xref rid="B6-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">6</xref>,<xref rid="B7-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">7</xref>,<xref rid="B8-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">8</xref>]. The detection of molecular alterations with unknown effects on the specific sarcoma histotype is a major limitation. In this review, we summarize published reports on genetic alterations with therapeutic relevance detected by NGS in STS patient specimens, focusing on the efficacy of targeted therapies that have been administrated, either through off-label prescription of an approved drug or enrollment in a matched clinical trial.</p></sec><sec sec-type="methods" id="sec2-cells-12-02632"><title>2. Methods</title><p>Studies were identified by searching the PubMed electronic database for the following terms: (“comprehensive genomic profiling” OR “genomic profiling” OR “next-generation sequencing”) AND (“sarcoma” OR “soft tissue sarcoma” OR “MPNST”). Malignant peripheral nerve sheath tumor (MPNST) is considered a relatively common STS histotype and, therefore, was included in the search terms. Results were restricted to adult human studies and articles written in the English language. No year restriction was applied. All retrieved studies were reviewed and accepted for further processing if contained information on NGS-based therapy administration and efficacy. All reports were analyzed for genomic results informing choices regarding targeted therapy. Due to the scarcity of data, case reports were not excluded. Studies reporting results on all STS histotypes were included, except for gastrointestinal stromal tumors (GISTs), given the established role for molecular testing in this entity and the approved KIT- and PDGFRα-targeting drugs. Studies analyzing the clinical utility of genomic profiling in cancer patients in general were also included if specific information on sarcoma patients was identified.</p></sec><sec id="sec3-cells-12-02632"><title>3. Studies Reporting Sequencing Results Leading to Targeted Therapy Implementation across STS Histotypes Containing Efficacy Data</title><p>We identified 18 studies reporting the results of comprehensive genomic profiling along with the clinical impact of implemented targeted therapy in 61 STS patients (<xref rid="cells-12-02632-t001" ref-type="table">Table 1</xref>). These reports include mainly retrospective studies on NGS in sarcoma patients from comprehensive cancer centers in the USA (n = 6) [<xref rid="B7-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">7</xref>,<xref rid="B8-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">8</xref>,<xref rid="B9-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">9</xref>,<xref rid="B10-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">10</xref>,<xref rid="B11-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">11</xref>,<xref rid="B12-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">12</xref>] and also from China [<xref rid="B13-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">13</xref>] and Portugal [<xref rid="B14-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">14</xref>]. In addition, five prospective studies provide data on genomic alterations and matched treatments: one from the USA [<xref rid="B15-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">15</xref>] and four from Europe [<xref rid="B16-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">16</xref>,<xref rid="B17-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">17</xref>,<xref rid="B18-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">18</xref>,<xref rid="B19-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">19</xref>]. Furthermore, a limited number of case reports illustrating clinical benefits from targeted therapies in STS patients were also identified [<xref rid="B20-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">20</xref>,<xref rid="B21-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">21</xref>,<xref rid="B22-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">22</xref>,<xref rid="B23-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">23</xref>]. The majority of studies analyzed only sarcoma patients, whereas two studies analyzed different tumor types [<xref rid="B17-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">17</xref>,<xref rid="B18-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">18</xref>] and one study included exclusively sarcomas of hematopoietic origins [<xref rid="B11-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">11</xref>]. Finally, another two prospective studies screened cancer patients for a specific genomic aberration, <italic toggle="yes">IDH</italic> mutations [<xref rid="B24-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">24</xref>], and <italic toggle="yes">MET</italic>ex14 alterations [<xref rid="B25-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">25</xref>].</p></sec><sec id="sec4-cells-12-02632"><title>4. Occurrence of Clinically Actionable Genomic Abnormalities in Patients with STS </title><p>Integrative genomic testing detects frequently genetic alterations in STS specimens. Base substitutions, short indels, and copy number variations (CNVs) are found in different genes, with <italic toggle="yes">TP53</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">RB1</italic>, and <italic toggle="yes">CDKN2A/B</italic> being among the most common ones [<xref rid="B6-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">6</xref>,<xref rid="B7-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">7</xref>,<xref rid="B10-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">10</xref>,<xref rid="B12-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">12</xref>,<xref rid="B26-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">26</xref>,<xref rid="B27-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">27</xref>,<xref rid="B28-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">28</xref>]. Furthermore, gene fusions leading mainly to the activation of transcription factors constitute a common finding. The frequency of genomic abnormalities depends on the gene panel and the technique that has been employed. Analysis of genomic data from 584 adult STS patients included in the GENIE database revealed 2697 alterations in 451 genes [<xref rid="B6-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">6</xref>]. In a series from the USA consisting of 133 sarcomas, Cote et al. reported a median number of 2 gene alterations per tumor, tested by targeted NGS (both DNA and RNA sequencing), including more than 400 cancer-related genes [<xref rid="B27-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">27</xref>]. Comprehensive genomic profiling in 102 sarcoma patients from MD Anderson identified at least 1 genomic alteration in 93% of the samples, with a median number of 6 alterations per patient [<xref rid="B10-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">10</xref>]. Similar results were reported for NGS testing (targeting 450 cancer-associated genes) in sarcoma patients from a single institution in China [<xref rid="B28-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">28</xref>]. In another retrospective study from Florida, 96.7% of non-GIST sarcoma patients were found to harbor at least one alteration [<xref rid="B7-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">7</xref>]. Sarcomas with complex genomics harbor more alterations compared to translocation-relative sarcomas, as illustrated by the example of leiomyosarcoma.</p><p>Subtype-specific genomic aberrations have been identified in several studies, including diverse STS histotypes [<xref rid="B26-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">26</xref>], while studies investigating exclusively a specific histotype shed more light on the genomic landscape of these sarcomas [<xref rid="B11-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">11</xref>,<xref rid="B15-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">15</xref>,<xref rid="B29-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">29</xref>,<xref rid="B30-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">30</xref>,<xref rid="B31-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">31</xref>,<xref rid="B32-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">32</xref>,<xref rid="B33-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">33</xref>]. Approximately 30–40% of sarcomas are characterized by a well-defined recurrent genetic alteration that contributes to its pathogenesis [<xref rid="B34-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">34</xref>,<xref rid="B35-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">35</xref>,<xref rid="B36-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">36</xref>,<xref rid="B37-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">37</xref>]. These alterations were detected using traditional molecular methods, such as fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) and polymerase chain reaction (PCR), and are valuable diagnostic tools, whereas most of them are not targetable. They include chromosomal translocations involving transcription factors, complex chromosomal aberrations, over-expression of receptor kinase ligands, inactivation of regulatory proteins, gene mutations, and gene amplifications, as exemplified by <italic toggle="yes">CDK4/MDM2</italic> amplification in liposarcoma. Modern sequencing techniques, such as NGS, revealed a large variety of additional histotype-specific alterations, occurring only in a proportion of patients with this sarcoma histotype. According to these data, mutations in <italic toggle="yes">PIK3CA</italic> have been reported in approximately 1/5 patients with myxoid/round cell liposarcoma [<xref rid="B26-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">26</xref>,<xref rid="B38-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">38</xref>]; alterations in <italic toggle="yes">NF1</italic> are found in a subset of patients with MPNST, myxofibrosarcoma, and pleomorphic liposarcoma [<xref rid="B26-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">26</xref>,<xref rid="B30-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">30</xref>], and <italic toggle="yes">TP53</italic> mutations are frequent in dedifferentiated liposarcoma (DDLPS), leiomyosarcoma and myxofibrosarcoma [<xref rid="B12-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">12</xref>,<xref rid="B28-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">28</xref>], and alterations in homologous recombination deficiency (HRD) genes in leiomyosarcoma [<xref rid="B12-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">12</xref>]. There are plenty of other observations of STS subtypes characterized by specific genetic aberrations.</p><p>Making sense of the results generated by comprehensive genomic profiling and identifying therapeutic vulnerabilities is a difficult task. A significant proportion of the above genomic alterations were considered potentially actionable across the different studies based on corollary evidence obtained in other tumor entities or preclinical data. The question of clinical actionability is discussed within a molecular tumor board in most institutions. In the largest retrospective series from the USA, in which 7494 STS specimens were sequenced, 31.7% exhibited potentially actionable alterations [<xref rid="B12-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">12</xref>]. A higher proportion of 41%, 47%, 49%, and 45% was reported from the GENIE database [<xref rid="B6-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">6</xref>] and three single institutions in Chicago [<xref rid="B8-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">8</xref>], Florida [<xref rid="B7-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">7</xref>], and MD Anderson [<xref rid="B10-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">10</xref>], respectively, whereas Cote et al. reported an even higher proportion of 88% [<xref rid="B27-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">27</xref>]. Although all of these cohorts included more than 100 STS, the number of patients per specific histotype was small. However, some STS subtypes were identified to harbor a higher number of targetable alterations, such as leiomyosarcoma [<xref rid="B17-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">17</xref>] and liposarcomas [<xref rid="B10-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">10</xref>]. In a large genomic analysis in Germany, synovial sarcoma patients had the highest clinical benefit among STS, followed by liposarcoma and leiomyosarcoma patients [<xref rid="B17-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">17</xref>]. Finally, in a smaller retrospective series of 65 STS in Japan, potentially actionable genomic alterations were found in 26 tumors (40%) [<xref rid="B13-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">13</xref>]. It should be noted that most of these studies include a low proportion of GIST patients as well.</p><p>Genetic aberrations involving a single gene lead to loss or gain of gene function. Furthermore, composite biomarkers are also interrogated through NGS, such as tumor mutational burden (TMB). Genomic aberrations detected in STS can be assigned to the following cellular pathways and processes: (i) <bold>tyrosine kinase</bold> (TK) activation or inactivation, including <italic toggle="yes">FGFR</italic> amplification or fusion, <italic toggle="yes">VEGF</italic> amplification, <italic toggle="yes">RET</italic> amplification, <italic toggle="yes">BRAF</italic> mutation, <italic toggle="yes">MET</italic> amplification, <italic toggle="yes">FGF</italic> amplification, and <italic toggle="yes">FRS2</italic> amplification; (ii) <bold>PI3K–AKT–mTOR</bold> (PAM) <bold>pathway</bold>, including <italic toggle="yes">TSC2</italic> deletion or base substitution, <italic toggle="yes">VHL</italic> deletion, <italic toggle="yes">NF2</italic> deletion, <italic toggle="yes">TSC1</italic> deletion or base substitution or amplification, and <italic toggle="yes">PTEN</italic> deletion; (iii) <bold>RAF–MEK–ERK</bold> (RME) <bold>pathway</bold>, such as <italic toggle="yes">KRAS</italic> amplification or base substitution, <italic toggle="yes">NRA</italic> base substitution, <italic toggle="yes">NF1</italic>-inactivating mutation or deletion, <italic toggle="yes">NF2</italic> deletion, and <italic toggle="yes">MAP2K2</italic> amplification; (iv) <bold>cell cycle</bold>, such as <italic toggle="yes">MDM2</italic> amplification, <italic toggle="yes">CDK4</italic> amplification, <italic toggle="yes">CDKN2A</italic> deletion or base substitution, <italic toggle="yes">CDKN2B</italic> deletion, <italic toggle="yes">MYC</italic> amplification, <italic toggle="yes">CDK6</italic> amplification, <italic toggle="yes">CCNE1</italic> amplification, and <italic toggle="yes">ARID1A</italic> base substitution; (v) <bold>DNA damage repair</bold> (DDR), such as <italic toggle="yes">BRCA1</italic> and <italic toggle="yes">BRCA2</italic> deletions, <italic toggle="yes">PTEN</italic> deletion, <italic toggle="yes">BAP1</italic> deletion, <italic toggle="yes">FANCE</italic> fusion, <italic toggle="yes">ATM</italic> base substitution, and mismatch repair (MMR) deficiency (defined as loss or inactivation of <italic toggle="yes">MLH1</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">MSH2</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">MSH3</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">MSH6</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">PMS2</italic>); and (vi) <bold>immune evasion</bold>, including <italic toggle="yes">TCTLA4</italic> amplification, <italic toggle="yes">PDCD1</italic> amplification, and TMB [<xref rid="B6-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">6</xref>,<xref rid="B7-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">7</xref>,<xref rid="B8-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">8</xref>,<xref rid="B10-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">10</xref>,<xref rid="B12-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">12</xref>,<xref rid="B16-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">16</xref>,<xref rid="B17-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">17</xref>,<xref rid="B19-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">19</xref>,<xref rid="B27-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">27</xref>,<xref rid="B28-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">28</xref>,<xref rid="B30-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">30</xref>]. As mentioned above, there are some associations of genomic alterations with specific STS subgroups. Alterations in cell cycle genes are more common in liposarcomas [<xref rid="B17-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">17</xref>], MPNST harbor alterations in the RME pathway [<xref rid="B30-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">30</xref>], and angiosarcoma in angiogenesis-related genes (TK family) [<xref rid="B31-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">31</xref>]. TMB has been assessed across STS and has been found high in some histotypes, such as rhabdomyosarcomas [<xref rid="B16-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">16</xref>], UPS [<xref rid="B12-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">12</xref>], MPNST [<xref rid="B12-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">12</xref>], and leiomyosarcomas [<xref rid="B17-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">17</xref>]. Anecdotal cases of high TMB have also been reported in patients with angiosarcoma, fibrosarcoma, and unclassified sarcoma [<xref rid="B15-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">15</xref>,<xref rid="B28-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">28</xref>]. Homologous recombination deficiency (HRD), defined by alterations with loss of function in DDR genes, is found in a small subset of STS, mainly leiomyosarcomas. </p></sec><sec id="sec5-cells-12-02632"><title>5. Only a Small Proportion of Patients Receive Matched Therapy</title><p>Notwithstanding the high frequency of potentially actionable genomic alterations observed across STS studies, only a minority of patients finally receive drugs targeting an alteration detected by genomic profiling. In a retrospective cohort (N = 118) from the Memorial Sloan Kettering Cancer Center (MSKCC), 29% of the patients either were enrolled in a relevant clinical trial or received off-label molecularly matched therapy [<xref rid="B12-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">12</xref>]. In another cohort of sarcoma patients referred to the phase I clinical trials program at MD Anderson (N = 102), only 16% received a targeted therapy [<xref rid="B10-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">10</xref>]. In another cohort from a single institution in the USA (N = 136), 8.8% of STS patients received a genomically driven therapy [<xref rid="B8-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">8</xref>]. In another single-institution series in the USA (N = 114), NGS-based therapy was administered to 7% of advanced sarcoma patients [<xref rid="B7-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">7</xref>]. In a smaller cohort of thirty-four sarcoma patients from a single institution in the USA, change in medical treatment was reported only in four patients (11.8%), despite the availability of matched clinical trials in 73% of patients [<xref rid="B39-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">39</xref>]. Genomic analysis of 158 sarcoma patients, using a 69-gene panel, in a French center led to treatment implementation in 8.2% of patients [<xref rid="B19-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">19</xref>], whereas in a subsequent molecular screening program in the same center (N = 39 sarcoma patients), this proportion is 17.8% [<xref rid="B18-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">18</xref>]. In a small prospective study including 58 STS patients in another French center, 12% were enrolled in early phase clinical trials of matched drugs [<xref rid="B6-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">6</xref>]. In a smaller retrospective study in Portugal (N = 30), 10% of sarcoma patients were treated with a genome-targeted drug [<xref rid="B14-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">14</xref>]. In the prospective European EORTC proof-of-concept study of 71 adolescent and young adults with sarcoma, 2.8% of all patients received targeted therapy [<xref rid="B16-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">16</xref>]. Finally, among 65 STS patients in China, 9.2% received matched therapy in a clinical trial [<xref rid="B13-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">13</xref>].</p><p>Taken together, these observations highlight that the clinical translation of molecular profiling in sarcomas is still limited (<xref rid="cells-12-02632-f001" ref-type="fig">Figure 1</xref>). A significant proportion of patients with a treatment recommendation do not receive targeted therapy for different reasons. A major limitation is the lack of access to or reimbursement of the recommended drug. Most of these drugs are either approved in another tumor type or investigational and can be delivered to patients through enrollment in relevant clinical trials (usually basket), compassionate use programs, or off-label use. In addition, patients usually undergo genomic profiling late during the disease course, with a number of them dying before the results of the molecular test or when their general condition has worsened. In some cases, the treating clinician decided to administer standard-of-care therapies instead of following the recommendation made by the molecular tumor board. Furthermore, some patients do not progress during the observation period of the studies; therefore, it is unknown whether the therapeutic selection is influenced by the molecular test in the future.</p></sec><sec sec-type="subjects" id="sec6-cells-12-02632"><title>6. Efficacy Results of Targeted Therapies in Soft Tissue Sarcomas Patients </title><p>Most studies on comprehensive genomic profiling in STS have provided a large amount of data on the occurrence of genetic alterations in STS, coupled with targeted therapy recommendations. The efficacy of these molecularly informed therapies is largely unknown to date. They have not been evaluated in prospective randomized trials, only in signal-seeking trials, with very scarce data on the outcome of patients who were treated with them in the advanced setting. <xref rid="cells-12-02632-t001" ref-type="table">Table 1</xref> summarizes the outcomes of STS patients who were treated with therapies targeting genomic alterations identified by NGS. In total, sixty-nine patients were identified, of which fifteen were enrolled in seven prospective genomic profiling studies [<xref rid="B15-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">15</xref>,<xref rid="B16-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">16</xref>,<xref rid="B17-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">17</xref>,<xref rid="B18-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">18</xref>,<xref rid="B19-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">19</xref>,<xref rid="B24-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">24</xref>,<xref rid="B25-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">25</xref>], and fifty are reported in the different retrospective series of NGS in STS [<xref rid="B6-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">6</xref>,<xref rid="B7-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">7</xref>,<xref rid="B9-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">9</xref>,<xref rid="B10-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">10</xref>,<xref rid="B11-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">11</xref>,<xref rid="B12-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">12</xref>,<xref rid="B13-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">13</xref>,<xref rid="B14-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">14</xref>]. Some studies also included transcriptomic analysis; treatment recommendations based on mRNA expression are excluded, as only therapies targeting genomic abnormalities are described in this review. In addition, anecdotal benefits of targeted therapy are described in four case reports [<xref rid="B20-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">20</xref>,<xref rid="B21-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">21</xref>,<xref rid="B22-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">22</xref>,<xref rid="B23-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">23</xref>]. Different STS histotypes are included, with liposarcoma being the most common (N = 14; five dedifferentiated liposarcoma (DDLPS), four well-differentiated liposarcoma (WDLPS), one myxoid liposarcoma, and four without a precise subtype), followed by leiomyosarcoma (N = 12), UPS (N = 7), and angiosarcoma (N = 6). There are also ultra-rare histotypes, such as epithelioid hemangioendothelioma (EHE), PEComa, clear cell sarcoma, histiocytic sarcoma and inflammatory myofibroblastic tumors (IMTs).</p><p>FoundationOne<sup>®</sup> was the most common sequencing platform used in the studies that we analyzed [<xref rid="B7-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">7</xref>,<xref rid="B8-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">8</xref>,<xref rid="B9-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">9</xref>,<xref rid="B10-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">10</xref>,<xref rid="B12-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">12</xref>,<xref rid="B14-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">14</xref>,<xref rid="B20-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">20</xref>,<xref rid="B22-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">22</xref>,<xref rid="B24-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">24</xref>,<xref rid="B25-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">25</xref>]. In three studies, whole-exome sequencing was performed [<xref rid="B15-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">15</xref>,<xref rid="B16-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">16</xref>,<xref rid="B17-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">17</xref>], whereas in two studies, two different commercial NGS platforms were used [<xref rid="B8-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">8</xref>,<xref rid="B13-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">13</xref>]. Additionally, NGS data were generated through in-house targeted sequencing platforms in three reports [<xref rid="B6-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">6</xref>,<xref rid="B11-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">11</xref>,<xref rid="B19-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">19</xref>]. Oncomine Focus Assay<sup>®</sup>, including 52 genes, was used in a case report [<xref rid="B23-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">23</xref>]. Finally, FoundationOne<sup>®</sup> was compared to a smaller in-house platform in the French ProfiLER 02 trial [<xref rid="B18-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">18</xref>]. The majority of STS patients received molecularly matched therapy in a drug-matched clinical trial [<xref rid="B6-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">6</xref>,<xref rid="B7-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">7</xref>,<xref rid="B10-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">10</xref>,<xref rid="B12-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">12</xref>,<xref rid="B13-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">13</xref>,<xref rid="B17-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">17</xref>,<xref rid="B19-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">19</xref>,<xref rid="B20-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">20</xref>,<xref rid="B22-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">22</xref>,<xref rid="B24-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">24</xref>]. Off-label targeted therapies were also administered in several patients [<xref rid="B7-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">7</xref>,<xref rid="B9-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">9</xref>,<xref rid="B12-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">12</xref>,<xref rid="B15-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">15</xref>,<xref rid="B16-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">16</xref>,<xref rid="B17-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">17</xref>,<xref rid="B19-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">19</xref>,<xref rid="B25-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">25</xref>], and a limited number of patients received targeted therapy on a compassionate use basis [<xref rid="B17-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">17</xref>,<xref rid="B21-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">21</xref>]. Access to matched therapy is not elucidated in four studies [<xref rid="B8-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">8</xref>,<xref rid="B11-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">11</xref>,<xref rid="B14-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">14</xref>,<xref rid="B18-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">18</xref>].</p><p>Palbociclib, a CDK4/6 inhibitor, was used in 13 STS patients harboring <italic toggle="yes">CDKN2A</italic> (N = 8), <italic toggle="yes">CDK4</italic> (N = 3), or <italic toggle="yes">MYC</italic> (N = 2) alterations [<xref rid="B7-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">7</xref>,<xref rid="B8-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">8</xref>,<xref rid="B9-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">9</xref>,<xref rid="B13-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">13</xref>]. In most cases, it was administered as a monotherapy, whereas in one patient, it was combined with the anti-estrogen fulvestrant [<xref rid="B7-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">7</xref>]. The majority of patients treated with this agent originated from a single-institution series in the USA [<xref rid="B8-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">8</xref>]. In twelve cases therapies targeting angiogenesis were implemented, as these tumors harbored alterations in angiogenesis-related genes [<xref rid="B6-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">6</xref>,<xref rid="B7-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">7</xref>,<xref rid="B8-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">8</xref>,<xref rid="B13-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">13</xref>,<xref rid="B14-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">14</xref>,<xref rid="B17-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">17</xref>,<xref rid="B19-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">19</xref>]. Pazopanib was the most frequently used drug in this setting (N = 7), followed by FGFR inhibitors in three patients and imatinib in the remaining two patients. <italic toggle="yes">BRAF</italic> mutations were targeted in seven patients, with either a BRAF inhibitor in four patients [<xref rid="B6-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">6</xref>,<xref rid="B7-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">7</xref>,<xref rid="B10-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">10</xref>,<xref rid="B13-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">13</xref>] or a combination with an MEK inhibitor in three patients [<xref rid="B11-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">11</xref>,<xref rid="B12-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">12</xref>,<xref rid="B20-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">20</xref>]. Furthermore, a patient with a <italic toggle="yes">BRAF</italic> fusion initiated combinational treatment with sorafenib/bevacizumab/temsirolimus [<xref rid="B10-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">10</xref>]. Five patients with WDLPS/DDLPS, from another single institution in the USA, received MDM2 inhibitors, based on the well-known <italic toggle="yes">MDM2</italic> amplification of these tumors [<xref rid="B10-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">10</xref>]. In another four cases, agents targeting <italic toggle="yes">ROS1</italic> aberrations were implemented, including ceritinib (N = 1), crizotinib/pazopanib combination (N = 2), and an investigational ALK/ROS1/NTRK inhibitor (N = 1) [<xref rid="B7-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">7</xref>,<xref rid="B10-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">10</xref>]. The NTRK inhibitor larotrectinib was used in two cases [<xref rid="B12-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">12</xref>,<xref rid="B23-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">23</xref>]. Lastly, seven patients were treated with immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) driven by an intermediate (N = 3) [<xref rid="B7-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">7</xref>,<xref rid="B12-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">12</xref>,<xref rid="B21-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">21</xref>] or high TMB (N = 4) [<xref rid="B12-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">12</xref>,<xref rid="B15-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">15</xref>,<xref rid="B18-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">18</xref>]. Two of them received double inhibition with anti-PD1 + anti-CTLA4 monoclonal antibodies. </p><p>Assessing for efficacy of the different targeted therapies implemented across STS patients is challenging, as they were given in different lines of treatment, usually in heavily pretreated patients, either in retrospective studies or early phase trials with varying follow-up periods. These factors, along with the predictive value of the selected biomarker, influence the efficacy of the drugs. Treatment outcomes are illustrated in <xref rid="cells-12-02632-t001" ref-type="table">Table 1</xref>, providing all the available information. Progression-free survival is provided only in some cases, whereas the best response is reported for the majority of patients. Data on outcomes are quoted for a qualitative estimate of the clinical benefit of NGS-based therapies in STS patients, instead of a statistical analysis of efficacy parameters. </p><p>Five patients exhibited complete response (CR) or near CR as the best response to targeted therapies; double immunotherapy (N = 2) or ICI monotherapy (N = 1) for high or intermediate TMB [<xref rid="B12-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">12</xref>,<xref rid="B18-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">18</xref>], an MDM2 inhibitor in one patient with WDLPS [<xref rid="B10-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">10</xref>], and larotrectinib, an NTRK inhibitor, in another patient with an inflammatory myofibroblastic tumor (IMT) harboring <italic toggle="yes">ETV6</italic>-<italic toggle="yes">NTRK3</italic> fusion [<xref rid="B12-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">12</xref>]. Fourteen additional patients experienced a partial response to molecularly driven therapies. BRAF inhibitors, either as a monotherapy (N = 2) or in combination with an MEK inhibitor (N = 2), were the most common drugs leading to PR, irrespective of histology [<xref rid="B6-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">6</xref>,<xref rid="B10-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">10</xref>,<xref rid="B11-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">11</xref>,<xref rid="B12-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">12</xref>]. Different other drugs led to PR, including TKIs (crizotinib for <italic toggle="yes">MET</italic>ex14 alteration and <italic toggle="yes">ALK</italic> fusion, imatinib for <italic toggle="yes">PDGFRa</italic> mutation, pazopanib for <italic toggle="yes">FLTA4</italic> amplification, larotrectinib for <italic toggle="yes">NTRK</italic> gene fusion) [<xref rid="B14-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">14</xref>,<xref rid="B19-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">19</xref>,<xref rid="B22-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">22</xref>,<xref rid="B23-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">23</xref>,<xref rid="B25-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">25</xref>], mTOR inhibitors for <italic toggle="yes">IGF1R</italic> and <italic toggle="yes">PIK3CA</italic> alterations [<xref rid="B6-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">6</xref>,<xref rid="B14-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">14</xref>], an MDM2 inhibitor for <italic toggle="yes">MDM2</italic> amplification [<xref rid="B10-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">10</xref>], and tazemetostat, an epigenetic regulator, for <italic toggle="yes">SMARCB1</italic> deletion [<xref rid="B12-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">12</xref>,<xref rid="B40-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">40</xref>].</p><p>Progression-free survival (PFS) varies between 1.4 and 44.3 months. The longest PFS was noted with ICI in two angiosarcoma patients [<xref rid="B15-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">15</xref>], with a PFS of 32.9 and 44.3 months. Other drugs achieving PFS &gt;1 year were BRAF inhibitors [<xref rid="B10-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">10</xref>,<xref rid="B13-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">13</xref>] and MAPK inhibitors [<xref rid="B6-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">6</xref>]. Larotrectinib and tazemetostat were also reported to achieve durable responses [<xref rid="B12-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">12</xref>,<xref rid="B23-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">23</xref>]. Eighteen patients experienced long-term disease control, defined as response (PR or CR) or stable disease (SD) for &gt;6 months, as <xref rid="cells-12-02632-t001" ref-type="table">Table 1</xref> demonstrates. Among them, four patients received drugs targeting BRAF mutation [<xref rid="B6-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">6</xref>,<xref rid="B10-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">10</xref>,<xref rid="B13-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">13</xref>,<xref rid="B20-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">20</xref>] and three immunotherapies [<xref rid="B15-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">15</xref>,<xref rid="B21-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">21</xref>], whereas the remaining patients were treated with other targeted agents. </p></sec><sec sec-type="discussion" id="sec7-cells-12-02632"><title>7. Discussion</title><p>In this review, we describe recent molecular studies on adult STS patients, focusing on targeted therapy implementation and its outcomes. To our knowledge, this is the first attempt to summarize the results of molecularly informed therapies in these patients to inform clinicians’ decisions. Comprehensive genomic profiling is an opportunity to address the limited therapeutic options in advanced STS patients. Our analysis reveals clinical benefits from targeted therapies in several cases, despite the fact that GIST patients were excluded. Evidence originated from retrospective series, small single-arm studies, or case reports. Thus, randomized trials are needed to evaluate this approach and compare it to standard-of-care histotype-tailored systemic therapy. A randomized French multi-center phase II/III trial is currently ongoing, assessing NGS-guided strategy versus standard treatment in metastatic sarcoma patients [<xref rid="B41-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">41</xref>]. The French randomized SHIVA trial, which compared genomically informed therapies, targeting the hormone receptor or PAM or RME pathway over standard treatment in different tumor types, revealed improved outcomes with the latter [<xref rid="B42-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">42</xref>]. Challenges associated with randomized trials of precision oncology have been identified, though, while conducting the MD Anderson IMPACT2 study, despite the adaptive innovative study design [<xref rid="B43-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">43</xref>]. Improved clinical outcomes with personalized treatments across cancers have been found by a large meta-analysis of phase II single-agent studies [<xref rid="B44-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">44</xref>].</p><p>The therapeutic relevance of molecular screening in STS is still limited. GIST constitutes the mainstay of theranostic application in sarcoma owing to the highly actionable targets <italic toggle="yes">KIT</italic> and <italic toggle="yes">PDGFRa</italic>. Apart from GIST, a driver genetic aberration that can serve as a therapeutic target has been identified in only a minority of sarcomas, including <italic toggle="yes">ALK</italic> fusion in IMT targeted with crizotinib and other TKIs [<xref rid="B45-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">45</xref>], <italic toggle="yes">PDGFb</italic> fusion in dermatofibrosarcoma protuberans (DFSPs) targeted with imatinib [<xref rid="B46-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">46</xref>], and <italic toggle="yes">CDK4</italic> amplification in WDLPS and DDLPS targeted with CDK4/6 inhibitors [<xref rid="B47-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">47</xref>]. Crizotinib, in particular, led to a sustained response in one patient with <italic toggle="yes">ALK</italic>-rearranged IMT [<xref rid="B45-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">45</xref>], a genomic aberration found in approximately 45% of IMT cases. Following this observation, the drug was also reported to be active in ALK-positive IMT [<xref rid="B48-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">48</xref>], MET-positive alveolar soft part sarcoma [<xref rid="B49-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">49</xref>], and MET-positive clear-cell sarcoma [<xref rid="B50-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">50</xref>], in the biomarker-driven phase 2 EORTC 90101 CREATE trial. In addition, NTRK inhibitors are new potent tissue-agnostic targeted drugs that were recently evaluated in basket trials with tumors harboring <italic toggle="yes">NTRK</italic> gene fusions [<xref rid="B51-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">51</xref>,<xref rid="B52-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">52</xref>]. Larotrectinib was evaluated in three phase 1–2 trials (a phase 1 adult, a phase 1/2 pediatric, and a phase 2 adolescent/adult trial), including patients with advanced NTRK-positive solid tumors [<xref rid="B51-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">51</xref>]. Seventy-nine percent of the patients exhibited an objective response with a favorable safety profile. Entrectinib, another NTRK inhibitor, was also evaluated in a pooled analysis of three phase 1–2 adult trials of advanced NTRK-positive solid tumors, with similar outcomes (57% objective response rate) [<xref rid="B52-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">52</xref>]. Among these tumors, patients with infantile fibrosarcoma and other STS were included. However, the frequency of <italic toggle="yes">NTRK</italic> fusions is extremely low in adult STS, and specific histological criteria have been proposed for testing [<xref rid="B53-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">53</xref>].</p><p>Except for the above-mentioned oncogenic drivers, the pharmacologically tractable alterations detected by NGS in STS specimens and described in this analysis are not established biomarkers that are predictive of response to a specific treatment. For instance, a proportion of leiomyosarcomas displays hallmarks of HRD and has been associated with poor clinical outcomes [<xref rid="B17-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">17</xref>,<xref rid="B26-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">26</xref>,<xref rid="B54-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">54</xref>]. The PARP inhibitor olaparib was tested in combination with the chemotherapeutic drug temozolomide in heavily pretreated patients with uterine leiomyosarcoma, irrespective of genomic findings, in a small phase 2 clinical trial, showing promising results [<xref rid="B55-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">55</xref>]. The activity of these drugs in tumors with HRD is yet to be shown. Similarly, a proportion of myxoid liposarcoma harbor PIK3CA alterations. However, the clinical efficacy of PIK3CA inhibitors in this setting will be explored in basket trials, such as <ext-link xmlns:xlink="http://www.w3.org/1999/xlink" ext-link-type="pmc:clinical-trial" xlink:href="NCT05307705">NCT05307705</ext-link>. Another recurrent genomic finding is cell cycle gene alterations in leiomyosarcomas and many other STS [<xref rid="B7-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">7</xref>,<xref rid="B8-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">8</xref>,<xref rid="B9-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">9</xref>,<xref rid="B10-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">10</xref>,<xref rid="B13-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">13</xref>,<xref rid="B18-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">18</xref>,<xref rid="B27-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">27</xref>]. Alterations in STS frequently involve more <italic toggle="yes">CDKN2A/B</italic> genes, and attempts to target them with CDK4/6 inhibitors have been made, with some anecdotal clinical benefits reported so far [<xref rid="B9-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">9</xref>]. There is some evidence of activity of these drugs in other tumors harboring <italic toggle="yes">CDKN2A</italic> aberrations, like pancreatic cancer [<xref rid="B56-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">56</xref>] and non-small cell lung cancer [<xref rid="B57-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">57</xref>]. This is an example of a repurposed therapy, initially approved for hormone receptor-positive breast carcinoma. Targeting <italic toggle="yes">BRAF</italic> mutations is probably one of the most promising strategies of precision oncology in STS. Our analysis reveals a number of objective responses and prolonged PFS with BRAF inhibitors as single agents or combinational treatments, reflecting a possible oncogenic role. A recent retrospective analysis of nearly 2000 STS showed the occurrence of <italic toggle="yes">BRAF</italic> alterations in 1.2%, including mutations and fusions [<xref rid="B58-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">58</xref>].</p><p>Apart from the challenges in identifying actionable mutations, variant allele fractions (VAFs) are often low, with an unknown impact on tumor clonality and heterogeneity. Therefore, it is challenging to make a clinical decision that relies on the presence of a genomic abnormality of unknown significance. In addition to the complexity of clinical translation, the genomic landscape of various sarcoma subtypes has been unraveled, as exemplified by specific studies on angiosarcoma [<xref rid="B15-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">15</xref>,<xref rid="B31-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">31</xref>], MPNST [<xref rid="B30-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">30</xref>], and even ultra-rare histotypes, including malignant gastrointestinal neuroectodermal tumors [<xref rid="B32-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">32</xref>] and desmoplastic small round cell tumor [<xref rid="B33-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">33</xref>]. This will enable the screening of different biomarkers and the discovery of genetic drivers, along with the identification of novel and repurposed therapies. In addition to purely genomic alterations, other biomarkers have also emerged as potential therapeutic vulnerabilities, such as the loss of INI1 protein in epithelioid sarcoma, as a result of genetic and epigenetic events, targeted with the epigenetic modulator tazemetostat [<xref rid="B40-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">40</xref>]. Over 90% of epithelioid sarcoma cases are characterized by loss of INI1 (also known as SMARCB1), conferring an oncogenic dependency on the enhancer of zeste homolog 2 (EZH2), the catalytic subunit of the chromatin remodeling polycomb repressive complex 2 (PRC2). Tazemetostat is a potent and highly selective EZH2 inhibitor, which showed clinical activity in epithelioid sarcoma, a chemotherapy-resistant histotype [<xref rid="B40-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">40</xref>].</p><p>The relatively low proportion of patients that are assigned to a matched therapy across the molecular screening studies in STS is in accordance with the results of large tumor-agnostic, genomically driven, precision medicine trials, which provide access to matched targeted therapies to patients across tumor types. In the NCI-MATCH trial all over the USA, including different tumor types, only 33 of the 645 screened patients (5.1%) were assigned to treatment, of which 16 were finally enrolled in targeted therapy subprotocols [<xref rid="B59-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">59</xref>]. The phase 2, open-label, basket trial TAPUR, led by the American Society of Clinical Oncology, includes patients with an already known targetable genetic aberration who are assigned to a matched therapy cohort [<xref rid="B60-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">60</xref>]. The efficacy results were positive for several cohorts, including olaparib in various tumor types with <italic toggle="yes">BRCA1/2</italic>-inactivating mutations and palbociclib in <italic toggle="yes">CDK4</italic>-amplified STS, and they were negative for seven cohorts. Novel strategies of precision oncology rely on combinational treatments, instead of targeted therapies as single agents, such as the ComboMATCH trial (<ext-link xmlns:xlink="http://www.w3.org/1999/xlink" ext-link-type="pmc:clinical-trial" xlink:href="NCT05564377">NCT05564377</ext-link>).</p><p>Molecular methods, including FISH, PCR, and more recently, NGS, have been traditionally employed in sarcoma diagnosis (<xref rid="cells-12-02632-t002" ref-type="table">Table 2</xref>). They certainly play an important role in diagnosis refinement, as illustrated by the example of <italic toggle="yes">MDM2</italic> amplification in WDLPS and DDLPS. In addition, the 2020 WHO classification of tumors of soft tissue and bone included some new entities, which can be diagnosed exclusively using molecular methods, such as sarcomas with <italic toggle="yes">NTRK</italic> fusions [<xref rid="B61-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">61</xref>] and <italic toggle="yes">BCOR</italic> alterations [<xref rid="B62-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">62</xref>]. Diagnostic modification based on molecular methods was found to influence therapeutic management in a small percentage of sarcoma patients [<xref rid="B3-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">3</xref>]. NGS-based techniques were validated as a robust diagnostic tool for the detection of pathognomonic sarcoma fusion transcripts, with anchored multiplex PCR being particularly practical for routine diagnosis [<xref rid="B63-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">63</xref>]. Genomic findings can also inform on the prognosis of different STS histotypes. For instance, synovial sarcoma with <italic toggle="yes">SYT-SSX1</italic> translocation has a worse prognosis compared to patients with <italic toggle="yes">SYT-SSX2</italic> [<xref rid="B36-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">36</xref>]. Whole-exome sequencing of rhabdomyosarcomas led to the identification of a recurrent mutation in the <italic toggle="yes">MYOD1</italic> gene in the embryonal subtype, which is associated with a bad prognosis [<xref rid="B64-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">64</xref>]. Clinical trials evaluating more intensive treatment protocols in these patients are warranted. It should be noted that a 67-gene expression signature was established, which predicts the clinical outcome of localized sarcomas, in order to inform clinical decisions on adjuvant treatment [<xref rid="B65-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">65</xref>]. </p><p>It should be noted that the results of genomic profiling largely rely on the technique and the gene panel used. Whole-genome sequencing (WGS) and whole-exome sequencing (WES) allow comprehensive genomic testing, generating a huge amount of data that are more difficult to analyze. Targeted NGS, on the other hand, uses panels of genes that are known to have a strong association with cancer or clinical actionability. WES and WGS were used in only a limited number of studies [<xref rid="B15-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">15</xref>,<xref rid="B16-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">16</xref>,<xref rid="B17-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">17</xref>], whereas the majority of NGS data reported in our analysis were generated through targeted sequencing platforms, with FoundationOne<sup>®</sup> being the most common, including &gt;300 cancer-related genes. The clinical utility of the above NGS panel was compared to a limited molecular profiling panel consisting of 87 cancer-related genes in a randomized French multi-center study [<xref rid="B18-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">18</xref>]. A subgroup analysis in sarcoma patients showed that the larger panel is associated with a higher probability of detecting an actionable alteration.</p><p>There are several limitations in the interpretation of NGS data. With regard to the occurrence of clinically actionable targets in STS, there is a variety of criteria to define a genetic alteration as actionable across the studies in STS. Additionally, different genes were interrogated on the different panels. Another challenge is the heterogeneity of STS histotypes, requiring sufficient numbers of patients in the different subgroups to draw conclusions on the genomic vulnerabilities related to specific histotypes. With regard to the efficacy of targeted therapies, a lack of drug access or availability of relevant clinical trials across countries limits the assessment of potential therapeutic targets. In some cases, there are available clinical trials for matched therapies, including only specific tumor types and not STS. In addition, it is possible that retrospective studies and case reports with only positive results are published, introducing selection bias. Unknown confounding factors may also have influenced the results, which are not derived from randomized trials, as is the case of basket trials.</p><p>Notwithstanding the limited data on targeted therapies in STS patients and the low proportion of patients who derive benefit from them to date, we feel that precision medicine through NGS is a meaningful option for patients with advanced disease. There is an urgent need for new therapies in these patients, given the poor outcomes with conventional chemotherapies. The ability to perform molecular profiling is important. As the understanding of molecular genetics evolves, new targets are being identified and efforts are being made to target some alterations known to be “undruggable”. This is the case for TP53 Y220C mutation, which is present in up to 2.9% of STS and 1.2% of bone sarcomas (rhabdomyosarcoma, leiomyosarcoma, sarcoma NOS, and osteosarcoma). The results of the phase I PYNNACLE study with PC14586, a selective inhibitor of Y220C mutant p53, capable of restoring its function, were presented last year, reporting a good tolerance and preliminary activity [<xref rid="B66-cells-12-02632" ref-type="bibr">66</xref>]. The phase II registration study will further assess its efficacy. In this direction, access to targeted therapies is extremely important, through either early phase and basket trials or compassionate use programs. This is currently not the case, as there are many disparities in access to NGS platforms themselves, as well as to molecularly driven therapies, across countries and regions. Efforts should be made to improve equity of access to genomic profiling and targeted therapies.</p><p>In conclusion, the advent of NGS has accelerated the discovery of effective biomarker–drug combinations in many tumors. Although comprehensive genomic profiling has focused on epithelial tumors, a growing body of evidence suggests that a number of advanced STS patients could benefit from individualized molecularly driven therapies. The results of several ongoing basket trials are expected to shed more light on the efficacy of targeted therapies in specific STS histotypes, whereas randomized trials will attempt to answer the question of whether genomic testing improves the clinical outcomes of these patients versus standard-of-care treatment. Several factors preclude the wide use of NGS in the real-world setting, including the high cost and the lack of access to molecular tumor boards and off-label therapies or clinical trials in many institutions. A deeper insight into STS biology will lead to clinical recommendations for the judicious use of these technologies in sarcoma patients based on the probability of having an actionable alteration coupled with the existence of an effective therapy. Therefore, more research is required before the transition to clinical practice.</p></sec></body><back><fn-group><fn><p><bold>Disclaimer/Publisher’s Note:</bold> The statements, opinions and data contained in all publications are solely those of the individual author(s) and contributor(s) and not of MDPI and/or the editor(s).
