## Background
School sports coaching is gaining recognition as a formal profession worldwide, highlighting its significant role in students’ holistic development. Given the complex social and cultural dimensions of the role, this study explores a coaching competence model tailored to the school context, emphasizing the need to consider both national and local frameworks to ensure effective implementation. In China, school sport coaches are responsible for both physical education teaching and sports training across all types of schools. Within the integration of sports and education, school sport coaches can expand their impact not only on student-athletes or athletes but also on general students in practice. Compared with physical education teachers’ perspectives, sports coaches’ competence development has rarely been studied. This study aims to develop a sustainable Chinese school sports coaching competence model.

## Methods
The qualitative data were gathered mainly through semi-structured interviews. School sports coaches and related stakeholders (N = 22) were selected as participants using purposive sampling techniques. Grounded theory was employed to analyse the data via NVivo 11.0 software.

## Results
The school sports coaching competence model comprises three categories consisting of 15 sub-categories, 92 concepts, and 317 labels. These categories are as follows: (a) Perception of identity, encompassing role of the coach, reflection on students, and understanding of policies; (b) Code of ethics, which includes sports virtues, sports-humanistic spirit, and principles of movement science; (c) Coaching excellence, comprising lifestyle and fitness strength, local sports development, teacher-coach partnership, leadership in extracurricular activities, safety and injury protection, athlete selection and training, instruction and demonstration, coaching reflective practice, and athlete-coach rapport.

## Conclusion
Chinese school sport coaching competence model emphasizes the integration of sports and education for student development. It provides both local and national perspectives to enhance the professional development of school sports coaches’ community worldwide.

## Introduction
Student holistic development in physical education primarily includes the personal, athletic, and educational development [ , ]. School sport coaching can be a valuable contributor to the sustainability of students’ holistic development across all dimensions [ – ]. As for personal development, school sport coaching helps foster students’ self-confidence [ ], social skills [ ] and life skills [ ]. Physical education teachers are responsible for teaching and coaching in physical education. However, the responsibility of coaching a sports team is rarely mandatory for Chinese physical education teachers; it is primarily voluntary, and the objectives mainly focus on student-athletes rather than the general student population [ ]. In comparison to physical education teachers, school sport coaches share similar responsibilities in both coaching and teaching. However, school sport coaches possess greater expertise in coaching than in teaching. Coaching represents the professional domain of school sport coaches, suggesting the need for complementary and sustainable support in coaching school sports teams alongside physical education teachers. Physical education teachers could provide scaffolding support to school sport coaches to enhance their understanding of the pedagogy of teaching, assisting coaches in adapting their competitive contexts to the school environment. Meanwhile, school sport coaches could help physical education teachers acquire knowledge about training and coaching, transforming teaching expertise from a focus on general students to one aimed at coaching student-athletes or athletes. Given the shared sustainable goal of enhancing education and student wellbeing, there is a crucial need to foster collaboration between teachers and coaches to ensure the sustainability of physical education [ ]. Consequently, school sports coaches emerge as a subset of sport coaches. While it is essential for sport coaches to hold coaching grade certificates, possessing a teaching certificate is not mandatory [ ], which indicates the pedagogical and educational dilemmas [ , ]. For example, sport coaches may encounter challenges for sustainably collaborating with school physical education teachers during school sports and recreation programs due to a lack of the understanding of teaching and education in school context [ ].
School sport coaches play a significant role not only in personal development but also in students’ athletic development on an international scale [ ]. In China, sport coaches play a pivotal role in coaching athletes, contributing to sustained success in international sports competitions. An illustrative example is China’s performance in the Olympic Games, from its first gold medal in 1984 to sustainably maintaining a top-three position in the Olympic medal rankings over the past five Games [ ]. Recognizing the potential of sport coaches in school education, a series of policies have been implemented to promote their involvement in the school context. These include the 2020 Deepening the Relationship between Sports and Education for Youth Health, 2021 [ ], The 14th Five-Year Plan for Sports Development,” [ ], Sports Law of the People’s Republic of China [ ]. These policies underscore the importance the development of student athletes’ teams, establishing related school sports training programs, creating positions for school sports coaches, and enhancing the quality of school sports coaches’ community through sustainable training and learning support. To solidify the position of school sports coaches, China introduced the 2023 Advice for School Sports Coaches Position, which mandates the establishment of full-time/part-time school sports coach positions for all levels of schools. This initiative aims to leverage coaches’ professional sports knowledge to enhance school physical education teaching and training [ ]. While it is essential for student well-being and holistic development to be supported by sustainable collaboration between teachers and coaches, not all school elite athlete programs align with this goal [ ]. Some school sports and recreation programs may prioritize the development of talented athletes over the overall well-being of students. For instance, a high school elite athlete program might concentrate on training student-athletes for competitive success while neglecting crucial aspects such as academic support and health promotion [ ]. Although general students may enjoy the sense of belonging and camaraderie with peers who share their passion for sports, many do not aspire to pursue a professional athletic career [ , , ]. Specifically, rather than only focusing on achieving high performance, school sports coaching aims to enhance students’ health beliefs, including aspects of dietary habits [ ], body image [ ], masculinities [ ], and diversity in genders [ , ]. For addressing those challenges of school sport coaching, it is crucial to understand students’ needs within health education [ ]. Consequently, this misalignment can create a disconnect between the objectives of sports programs and the educational mission of the school, ultimately undermining the priority of student health and development [ ]. For example, elite athlete programs may reproduce masculinities and elitist discourses [ – ] or pressure young elite athletes to conform to an ‘ideal’ body image [ ]. The relationship between the coach and athlete and between athletes plays a significant role in either facilitating problematic or reflecting responsible and safe practices in elite athlete programs or school sports [ ].
Athletic development may be a primary goal of sports coaching for student-athletes or athletes; however, health and physical education senior subjects and Physical Recreation as a Study Area Specification in Australia are still available for general students in the school curriculum [ ]. In China, the athletic career is heavily influenced by sociocultural contexts. Owing to the international success achieved by Chinese athletes, some sport scientists have declared the Chinese Whole Nation System (CWNS) among the most effective athletic career systems for selecting and developing elite athletes from a young age [ ]. However, athletic careers also means multi-year periods to voluntarily engage in one or more sport activities and sacrifice their school education time for achieving peak athletic performance [ ]. Hence, in contrast to student-athletes in some other countries, Chinese athletes receive training within China’s state-sponsored elite sports framework. Talent identification in China occurs at an early age of school, facilitated by specialized sports schools that serve as primary training environments for these student-athletes or athletes [ ]. Consequently, many Chinese student-athletes or athletes allocate a significant portion of their time to sports training and competition, which leads to a limited focus on the academic-driven school education [ , ]. To address these needs, China initiated a competence development revolution for all subject curriculums in 2014. Specifically, the Department of Education launched the Comprehensive Deepening Curriculum Reform and Implementation of Moral Education Policy, which outlined the direction of school curriculum for competence development [ ]. This initiative emphasized the importance of developing students’ competence at all school levels, emphasizing that students should acquire vital abilities and qualities to adapt to lifelong development. In the field of physical education, a series of policies have been implemented to focus on subject-specific competence development, such as the ‘’General High School Curriculum Standards 2017 version’’ and the ‘’Compulsory Education Physical Education and Health Curriculum Standards 2022 version’’ [ ]. While research on competence development in physical education primarily focuses on the perspectives of students [ ] or physical education teachers [ ], there is still a lack of understanding about the diversity of school sport coaches. Hence, school sport coaches face conflicting priorities of achieving high-performance outcomes versus promoting general health and activity in physical education [ , ]. For example, addressing students’ peer pressure [ ] emotional abuse [ ], gender-based violence and neglect [ ], as well as potentially indirect self-injury behaviours, including the misuse of prescription medications, excessive alcohol consumption, and promiscuity [ ].
Regarding research on school sport coach, coaching school sport teams is mostly a voluntary work worldwide, in countries such as Canada [ ], Ireland [ ], UK [ , ], New Zealand [ , ], Norway [ ], France, California [ ], and Australia [ ]. Physical Education teachers teach elite athlete programs as part of the curriculum in New Zealand [ ]and Australia, for example, in the Year 11 and 12 Physical Recreation subject [ , ]. In New Zealand, some EAPs may be a part of the school curriculum or sports academies [ ]. For example, a sports academy may enroll students in courses through a correspondence school from Year 9 onward [ , ]. Traditionally, physical education has been viewed as separate from core subjects such as mathematics and literacy due to its extracurricular nature. Therefore, it is vital to increase the emphasis on physical education within educational frameworks, ensuring it effectively serves students’ needs. Achieving this objective necessitates a comprehensive approach that considers social, cultural, and educational perspectives [ ]. To bridge the gap between general education and physical education, Sarah (2020) identified and synthesized the primary evidence on barriers to voluntary sports participation that are faced by children worldwide [ ], Magnus (2020) explored the parental engagement of coaching school sports in Sweden context [ ]. Tania (2021) delineated two sports coaching policies: the New Zealand Coaching Strategy and the Coach Development Framework [ ]. Recently, these discussions have extended beyond Western countries’ borders contributions from other nations which have been useful to interrupt the Western-dominated scholarship and promote unique cultural issues that impact upon coaching models and the education and development of coaches, including Portugal [ ], Brazil [ , ]. Research from China emphasizes a sustainable pathway to becoming a qualified school sports coach, with most coaches coming from retired athletes [ ]. Yin conducted document analysis to explore how retired athletes qualify as school sports coaches, along with their subsequent training and career development [ ]. Building on this, Yang examined the challenges faced by school sports coaches who are retired athletes, utilizing the Empowerment theory [ ]. Additionally, Wang employed Actor-Network Theory to investigate how retired athletes transition into school sports coaching roles in Chengdu [ ].
Given the early stage of school sports coaching within education policy, the hierarchical relationship between coaches and student-athletes is primarily determined by the coaches themselves. Therefore, it is imperative to establish a sustainable school sports coaching competence model by capturing insights directly from coaches in both local and national contexts [ , , ]. Findings from the local context could encourage both coaches and student-coaches to personally reflect on and engage with the technical, moral, ethical, and political issues that arise in their respective school sports coaching environments [ ]. To achieve this objective and support the development of students and student-athletes across all types of primary and secondary school, grounded theory was employed, as it is well-suited for research on perceptions. This methodology enables researchers to construct a theory grounded in the experiences and perspectives of participants, facilitating an inductive and iterative process of data collection and analysis. Therefore, our aim is to develop a sustainable school sports coaching competence model. By doing so, this research can offer theoretical guidance for the enhancement of school sports coaches’ development and the improvement of the quality of school sports coaching teams. Consequently, this endeavour supports the integration of student development within physical education.

## Materials and methods

## Study design
This research employed Grounded Theory as developed by Glaser and Strauss in 1967 [ ]. Grounded theory is one of the most widely used qualitative methodologies in social science disciplines such as nursing, sociology, healthcare, and pedagogy [ ]. In the field of physical education, grounded theory is increasingly gaining popularity [ – ]. Constructivist grounded theory is widely used in the field of sport and physical education [ – ]. Constructivists view that concepts and theories are constructed by researchers out of stories that are constructed by research participants who are trying to explain and make sense out of their life experiences, both to the researcher and themselves. Out of these multiple constructions, analysts construct something that they call knowledge [ ].
Based on the constructivist principles, grounded theory encourages the generation of theory through the collection and analysis of original qualitative data [ ]. It is important to note that grounded theory is a methodological approach rather than a theory [ ]. The methodology provides a way to study social phenomena to build theoretical frameworks from qualitative data [ ]. The epistemology is constructivism, drawing on the tradition of Chicago Interactionism [ ]and the philosophy of Pragmatism [ ]. The ontology is realism, supporting the ideas that the theoretical frameworks must be situated within broader social, political, cultural and technological context [ ]. Also, the concepts at various levels of abstraction form the basis of analysis [ ].
The key benefits of using constructivist grounded theory in this study include the opportunity to deeply explore school sport coaches’ experiences and to develop a theoretical competence model by examining the psychological, social, and cultural factors that act as barriers and facilitators to reporting. In this context, constructivist grounded theory assumes that participants and researchers actively construct and subsequently act upon the realities they assign to events, actions, processes, ideologies, and conditions within the field of school sport coaching. Therefore, this characteristic of constructivist grounded theory is well-suited for gathering multiple perspectives on the complex nature of Chinese school sport coaching competence. There are also other reasons we chose Grounded Theory approach. Firstly, school sport coaches form a community that highlights specific coaching norms. In China, the context becomes complex due to the significant cultural diversity that exists from province to province. These cultural differences make Chinese school sports coaching a complex social phenomenon. Secondly, interaction between researchers and participants enhances the quality of the research, allowing both sides to co-construct and continuously update the knowledge surrounding sport coaching competencies. Thirdly, grounded theory focuses on the meanings that can be derived from interpretations of the data. Grounded theory is traditionally utilized when a relevant theory does not exist or in areas where little is already known [ ]. Thus, it is particularly suitable for exploring the realities of school sport coaches in their early stages in China. Additionally, this approach emphasizes the social connectivity between researchers, participants, the research question, and the working environment [ ].

## Participants and data collection

## Sampling and recruitment
Based on PO model [ ], theoretical sampling is commonly employed by grounded theory as the basic procedure to select participants, which is suitable for studies with small sample sizes and purposeful selection criteria [ ]. In comparison to physical education teachers in China, school sport coaches are still relatively new to the educational landscape, resulting in a limited number currently present. This limited number aligns with the sample size and criteria for theoretical sampling. Purposeful sampling involves selecting participants based on the research objectives, while also considering stakeholders and geographical characteristics to ensure representative participation. Since the policies regarding school sport coaching vary from province to province, participants were sourced from every class level, type of university, and region of China (East–North, East–South, West–North, West–South). In the context of Chinese school sport coaching, we selected relevant stakeholders involved in the development of school sports coach competence. These stakeholders include curriculum developers, coaching managers, heads of school subjects, vice principals, coaching researchers, physical education researchers, university sport coaches, and primary and secondary school sport coaches. Because qualitative researchers prioritize depth over breadth, their work entails a more intricate research process. The sample size is typically smaller compared to quantitative research, often limited to around 30 participants, but also depends on the research objectives [ ]. Following this principle and considering the reality that a small number of school sports coaches in China, 22 participants were selected for representing different regions of China. To maintain participant anonymity, each individual was assigned a numerical identifier from 1 to 22 (Table  ).

 

## Data collection
Interviews represent the most common method for data collection in grounded theory research [ ]. In this study, the knowledge of school sports coaching competence is intended to be beneficial for guiding practical work within local school contexts. The narrative and detailed nature of the interviews are appropriate for achieving the aims of the study. However, data sources for grounded theory research can also encompass focus groups, questionnaire surveys, transcripts, letters, government reports, documents, literature, music, artifacts, videos, blogs, and memos, among others [ ]. Among all types of interviews, semi-structured interviews are widely used in the field of physical education due to their advantages in incorporating various types of questions, including open-ended questions, theory-driven questions, and confrontational questions [ , ]. These advantages facilitate a comprehensive reconstruction of subjective viewpoints regarding school sport coaching competencies while focusing on both the theoretical and practical applications of the school sports coaching competence model.
With the research focus on school sports coach competence development, an Interview Outline of School Sports Coach Competence Development Model was formulated (Appendix). Three schools were chosen to conduct pilot interviews aimed at gathering feedback to improve the interview questions. The pilot interview questions were revised twice in terms of content, structure, and sentence construction, based on feedback from scholars in the coaching and physical education fields. For example, based on expert consultation, we removed an initial question about the significance of establishing school sport coaching positions, as it conveyed the same meaning as the question about the necessity of building such positions. Additionally, we shortened a question about the distinction between school sport coaches and physical education teachers by removing the words ‘in general’ in Mandarin version. Ultimately, the interview outline was finalized, comprising eight open-ended questions. These questions pertained to four main topics, including understanding the definition of a school sports coach, the characteristics of being a school sports coach, the significance of constructing a competence model for school sports coaches, and the pathways for school sports coaches to develop such a competence model.
Face-to-face interviews are typically the most effective method for collecting rich data for grounded theory approach. Meanwhile, the nature of teaching and coaching in physical education is highly visual and relies on physical demonstration, making the face-to face interviews well-suited for understanding the perspectives of school sport coach participants. However, due to the geographical dispersion of Chinese school sport coaches across provinces, interviews were conducted both face-to-face and over the telephone. The decision to conduct the interview by telephone was based on participant accessibility, as school sport coaches typically work in open spaces, which are often dynamic. This makes it difficult for them to use digital devices like laptops for video calls. Prior to the interviews, the interview outline was provided to participants for reviewing their responses. Each interview lasted approximately 30 to 45 min and was conducted in a quiet and comfortable environment agreed upon by each participant. Since all participants were Chinese, and there is evidence that many find it difficult to speak English [ ], the interviews were conducted in Mandarin to allow participants to comfortably provide detailed answers. And the interview environment was tailored to align with current Chinese educational policies to ensure an engaging and comfortable experience.
Except interviews, data were additionally gathered from academic databases such as CNKI and Web of Science, as well as policy documents from the Department of Education and the Administration of Sport. To gain a comprehensive understanding of the international context, we gathered 23 international journal articles, 27 Chinese literature sources, and six policy documents as references. Following the data collection, all authors individually screened the data related to school sport coaching competence to establish the inclusion and exclusion criteria. Ultimately, a total of eight international journal articles, seven Chinese journal articles, and one educational policy document were collected additionally as original data. Thus, alongside semi-structured interviews with local Chinese school sports coaches, we also gained insights from various nations to foster the development of both national and international school sports coaching communities.

## Straussian three-stage coding
Grounded theory has evolved into two versions that differ in practical implementation [ ]. Among these, the most frequently referenced grounded theory methodology over the last two decades has been the Straussian approach [ ]. This approach adheres to the principle of generating theory from original data rather than relying on existing theories. The Straussian approach in grounded theory employs three levels of coding: the open, the axial, and the selective stages [ , , ]. Theory is then developed from data derived from the research process [ ]. As the results heavily rely on the original data, both the collection and analysis of data are considered critical aspects of a grounded theory approach [ ].
The three-stage coding process consists of open coding, axial coding, and selective coding [ ]. First, open coding was employed to develop short, simple, and direct codes, with the unit of analysis depending on the context, fit, and data relevance. Second, axial coding built upon the initial codes to synthesize and make sense of early conceptualized ideas. The primary intention of this stage was to strip away extraneous data to deepen the developing theory, compare and contrast codes with one another, and identify gaps in the data that required further exploration. Throughout the coding process, the constant comparison technique was integral for identifying similarities and differences between data codes, including intra- and inter-participant comparisons, as well as comparisons between the researchers’ beliefs and the views of the participants concerning the model of school sports coaching competence. Finally, selective coding involved making continuous comparisons to further uncover the categories that can unify all concepts related to the school sports coaching competence model. Throughout the coding process, progression was not linear but rather involved continuous revision, with analysis conducted iteratively. Constant comparison of data ensured the reliability of the school sports coach competence model.
A total of 22 data resources were collected during the semi-structured interviews, amounting to a total of 104,566 words finally. NVivo 11.0 software was employed for the three-stage coding analysis. Data (e.g., interviews, articles, policies) were recorded and transcribed into written words immediately after each interview, and the transcripts were saved online. Following the three-stage principles of grounded theory, original data were equally distributed, with 80% utilized for the three-stage coding analysis [ ].

## Open coding
Open coding involves the initial conceptualization, further conceptualization, and categorization of raw empirical data [ ]. This process was utilized to identify key concepts and categories pertaining to the school sports coach competence model at a macro level. It entailed capturing keywords present in primary sources related to these perceptions and utilizing native words from empirical sources to form free nodes for capturing the dimensions of the school sports coach competence model. We followed the naming convention ‘EN + label statement,’ for example, ‘E93: able to plan for each student’s future career path. This approach enabled the construction of a comprehensive understanding of the key themes and patterns in the original data, leading to the development of a theoretical model for the school sports coach competence model.

Labeling and conceptualization

Following the labelling process of open coding concerning the school sports coaches’ competence development model, we identified 317 labels associated with participants’ beliefs of the school sports coach competence model (Tables  and ).

 

 
These 317 labels underwent constant comparative analysis to identify key themes and patterns. This process, guided by grounded theory principles, facilitated the condensation and refinement of the labels, resulting in 92 concepts. Each concept was named using the format DN + concept name. For instance, D65 represents “able to train and regulate students,” These concepts served as a robust foundation for the theoretical model and contributed to a deeper understanding of the collected data (Tables  and ).

 

 

Categorization

Using the same process of constant comparative analysis, we further classified and compared the 92 concepts we had identified. This allowed us to identify 15 categories that captured the key themes and patterns in the data. The categories are presented using the format “CN + Category Name’’, included C1 role of the coach, C2 coaching reflective practice, C3 teacher-coach partnership, C4 leadership in extracurricular activities, C5 sports-humanistic spirit, C6 athlete selection and training, C7 lifestyle and fitness strength, C8 local sports development, C9 understanding of policies, C10 sports virtues, C11 safety and injury protection, C12 principles of movement science, C13 instruction and demonstration, C14 reflection on students, and C15 athlete-coach rapport. These 15 categories provided a more detailed and nuanced understanding of school sport coach competence model (Tables   and ).

 

 

## Axial coding
Axial coding involves the categorization and comparison of various concept categories related to the school sports coach competence model, as well as the identification of relationships among those categories. This process entails breaking down and reorganizing the free nodes of these elements to extract the main concepts or categories. In axial coding, we dismantled the free nodes of the school sports coach competence model and reorganized them in a way that allowed us to analyse and conceptualize these elements related to the competence model in a more hierarchical manner. This process helped us identify key themes and patterns in the data and develop a more robust and nuanced theoretical model for building the theoretical competence model for school sports coaches. After the axial coding process, three main categories were generated. Each category was named using the format BN + category name. Specifically, these categories are: B1: Perception of identity, B2: Code of ethics, and B3: Coaching excellence. Each of these subcategories included different subsidiary categories from the original set. Specifically, B1: Perception of identity encompassed role of the coach, reflection on students, and understanding of policies; B2: Code of ethics included sports virtues, sports-humanistic spirit, and principles of movement science; B3: Coaching excellence comprised lifestyle and fitness strength, local sports development, teacher-coach partnership, leadership in extracurricular activities, safety and injury protection, athlete selection and training, instruction and demonstration, coaching reflective practice, and athlete-coach rapport (Table  ).

 

## Selective coding
Selective coding is employed to follow up with participants to determine whether patterns observed in a subset of participants are commonly shared. This is achieved through follow-up interviews that include targeted questions. Selective coding involves analysing the connections between the different main concepts or categories of the school sports coach competence model and making continuous comparisons to further uncover the ' categories’ that can unify all school sports coach competence model concepts or categories. Following the axial coding process, the selective coding process begins. In this study, the 15 categories and the three main categories were constantly compared and deeply analysed, along with tracing back to the original data for comparison. Ultimately, the category was identified as the sustainable school sports coaching competence model. This core category integrates all the 317 labels, 92 concepts, 15 subcategories, and three categories, providing a comprehensive overview of the research topic on the construction of the competence model for school sports coaches’ sustainable professional development.

## Trustworthiness
Theoretical saturation test, a commonly used method in qualitative research [ ], aims to determine whether enough data have been collected to fully explore and understand the phenomenon of school sports coach competence development model. It involves continuing data collection and analysis until no new themes, patterns, or insights emerge from the data, indicating that theoretical saturation has been achieved [ ].
In this study, 10 original data sources were randomly selected to conduct the tests, comprising a total of 20 paragraphs. Two researchers in the team independently coded the same data to assess coding consistency. NVivo 11.0 software was utilized for the coding process. The coding repetition rate was calculated to understand and compare the coding results between the two researchers. A high coding repetition rate indicates a high level of consistency between the researchers’ coding, thus enhancing the reliability of the coding results.
After calculating the coding repetition rate of the sample, we found that the consistency coefficient between the two researchers’ coding was high, indicating that the interpretation of the data was not influenced by individual subjective factors. Meanwhile, discrepancies between the two authors were discussed, the discrepancies related to the definition of competence and the subject matter in physical education and sport pedagogy. The theoretical saturation test was conducted to verify whether the formed “school sports coaches’ competence model” is complete and whether new theories can be generated through additional data. By inputting the remaining 20% of the research data into NVivo 11.0 software for coding, the results showed that no new nodes appeared. This means that no new themes or relationships were found in the remaining materials, confirming that the coding content had reached saturation. With the completion of this process, the competence model of school sports coaches is deemed to be objective and scientifically supported.

## Results
The sustainable school sports coaching competence model was generated through the three-stage coding process, which comprised three main categories, 15 categories, 92 concepts, and 317 labels. Each theme represents a distinct aspect of the school sports coaching competence model and contributes to a comprehensive understanding of the complexities and nuances surrounding the ideal characteristics of a school sports coach identity. Within each main theme, numerous subthemes have been identified, further elaborating on the key concepts and categories identified in the data. These subthemes provide a more detailed and refined view of the factors influencing the competence development of school sports coaches, offering insights into the perceptions and practices of school sports coaches in various school contexts. The three categories are: (a) Perception of identity, encompassing role of the coach, reflection on students, and understanding of policies; (b) Code of ethics, which includes sports virtues, sports-humanistic spirit, and principles of movement science; (c) Coaching excellence, comprising lifestyle and fitness strength, local sports development, teacher-coach partnership, leadership in extracurricular activities, safety and injury protection, athlete selection and training, instruction and demonstration, coaching reflective practice, and athlete-coach rapport (Fig.  ).

 

## Perception of identity
Perception of identity refers to the attitudes or views held by individuals or communities, which serve as the foundation for behavioural choices. In this study, school sports coaches possess their own unique understanding of their identity, and these perceptions of identity are reflected in their fundamental awareness as a school staff member. This awareness encompasses role of the coach, reflections on students, and understanding of policies. These awareness points operate at three levels: relating to the coach themselves, the institution, and the pupils.

## Role of the coach
The dimension of coach identity in the school sport coach competence development model reflects the fundamental understanding that school sport coaches have of their profession. This understanding serves as the guiding principle and foundation for how school sports coaches approach their work. The profession of a physical education teacher differs from that of a school sports coach, and being a coach entails unique responsibilities within the school context. A sport coach in school must possess an awareness of the distinctions between being a physical education teacher and a sports coach in competition, recognizing the uniqueness of their position within the realm of physical education and school extracurricular activities. Furthermore, they should demonstrate passion for their role, maintaining a belief and attitude conducive to long-term commitment as a school sports coach and striving to achieve their own values or goals within this role.
To be an eligible school sports coach, one must also respect the current state of school physical education, possess the ability to assess individual students’ sporting abilities, and discern the differences between more and less athletically skilled students. Specifically, school sports coaches should approach victories and defeats from a moral standpoint in school sports training, striking a balance between students’ overall development and their athletic performance. They should offer students objective guidance on sports training to promote health and potentially instill motivation and physical strength that can be applied across various subjects or extracurricular activities. From a societal perspective, there exists a stereotype regarding the role of school sports coaches, often perceiving them as individuals with minimal academic achievement and a simplistic mindset. To challenge and change these stereotypes, school sports coaches must adopt a positive mindset and exhibit exemplary behaviour, such as upholding school regulations to safeguard the institution’s reputation and making significant contributions to the field of school physical education.

“One of the big things for coaches, especially in school sports teams, is dealing with a bunch of challenges. Challenges such as not having enough money for the team, not having proper facilities, students with different levels of athletic abilities, and not having enough time for training. But as a coach, you got to stay humble and love what you do, always aiming to nurture talent and help out the school team.” (Interview Material 14).

## Reflections on students
The dimension of reflection on students in the competence development model pertains to the understanding of the ideal goals for school training and how to achieve those goals on an individual basis. In 2020, the “Deepen the Integration of Sports and Education” policy emphasized the importance of focusing on students and youth. Even though school sports coaches often train student athletes with sports talent, it is crucial to recognize that these athletes are students first. Therefore, school sports coaches should uphold the belief that sports training in schools should incorporate pedagogy.
Specifically, school sports coaches need to embrace student-centred values, integrating moral education into sports training and recognizing that sports training in a school setting should prioritize students’ overall development rather than solely emphasizing improved performance in sports competitions. To integrate pedagogy into their daily work, school sports coaches must understand students individually and cater to their learning needs in sports training. This involves incorporating teaching and education into training sessions, respecting the diversity among students, inspiring students to develop intrinsic motivation for physical education, and ensuring there is no conflict between the teachings of physical education teachers and the training provided by school sports coaches.

“During training, I make sure to take into account each student’s differences and the team’s actual situation. I customize training tasks to meet their specific needs and set reasonable goals and requirements for each person. It’s crucial to look out for students’ physical and mental well-being too. For instance, if someone’s sick with a cold or fever, or they’re overwhelmed by final exams, I adjust things accordingly to support them.” (Interview Material 18).

## The understanding of policies
School sports coaches’ understanding of policies in the competence development model involves their comprehension of educational policies, especially within physical education. In 2020, the “Deepen the Integration of Sports and Education” policy emphasized that school sports coaches should align with the integration of school education and competitive sports. They are expected to bring their professional knowledge from competitive sports into the educational context, addressing the challenges faced by physical education teachers in sports training and extracurricular activities.
Furthermore, as part of the competence-driven revolution in the physical education curriculum, school sports coaches serve as implementers of school physical education. They are required to carry out their duties in accordance with the guidance provided by relevant institutional policies and practices, ensuring the vitality and stable development of school physical education. Additionally, school sports coaches should adopt the role of teachers, disseminating and promoting knowledge and a clear understanding of physical education policies to create an active learning environment for school physical education.

“So, with the new high school curriculum standards rolling out, which include the health promotion related policy, school sports are evolving and growing. This means physical education teachers, school sports coaches, and anyone involved in school sports need to get on board. We have to dive into the latest policy docs, grasp what the model’s all about, keep our minds fresh, and up our teaching and training game constantly.” (Interview Material 11).

## Code of ethics
The code of ethics encompasses moral qualities essential for both personal and professional development, such as trust, respect, equality, and diligence. For school sports coaches, these essential characteristics denote the moral qualities required within the context of guiding sports training and supplementing the teachings of school physical education teachers. The dimension of the code of ethics for school sports coaches involves sports virtues, sports-humanistic spirit, and principles of movement science.

## Sports virtues
Sports virtues entail having passion for working as a school sports coach, being humble about one’s advantages in sports, fostering social communication and collaboration, demonstrating responsibility and accountability, exhibiting politeness and civility, respecting others, adhering to school regulations, embodying honesty and trustworthiness, cultivating self-discipline, advocating for equality and justice, maintaining a positive mindset to achieve more, sustaining persistence, and facing challenges without fear. These aspects reflect the behavioral standards expected of school sports coaches within society.
Specifically, passion for their career as a school sports coach entails feeling enthusiastic about collaborating with physical education teachers in the school setting, maintaining lifelong dedication to their role as a school sports coach, and demonstrating love and care for every student. Social communication and collaboration involve effectively communicating and collaborating with school physical education teachers, teachers from other subjects, program leaders, headmasters, and students’ parents, while also exhibiting politeness, respect, and honesty towards others.
Perseverance and fearlessness in the face of challenges are foundational traits for addressing problems within a flexible work environment. Developing self-discipline entails setting a positive example for students by school sports coaches themselves. Equality and justice require treating all students, regardless of their level of sports proficiency, with the same attitude and integrity, and avoiding cheating in all coaching-related activities such as assessments.
In conclusion, school sports coaches need to embody these virtues for their professional development. Furthermore, equipping themselves with these moral qualities can promote the effectiveness of setting good examples for students, thereby empowering students to develop their moral skills autonomously.

“You see, building sports character is all about practicing what you preach and setting the right example. For instance, when the coach shows respect to the referee during a game instead of throwing insults, it rubs off on the students, shaping how they think and act. It’s about leading by example.” (Interview Material 06).

## Sports-humanistic spirit
The sports-humanistic spirit primarily embodies from the interview, participants defined humility within the realm of physical education as one of the dimensions of competence model, encompassing care, love, appreciation of beauty, and the dissemination of sports culture knowledge within the context of school sports coaching.
Care and love, from the perspective of students, require school sports coaches to possess the ability to understand each student individually, discern their needs in physical education, and acknowledge the pressures students face from other academic subjects. School sports coaches should provide training while demonstrating care and affection throughout the process. Appreciation of beauty in physical education involves recognizing the aesthetic aspect of sports, emphasizing the beauty inherent in sports and physical education, and fostering an objective and comprehensive understanding of body image and the diversity of students’ sports performances. Spreading the traditional culture of sports entails confidently promoting the advantages of sports culture, both publicly and within the school context. This is particularly important in the current era of globalization.
Regarding knowledge of the humanistic spirit in physical education, it primarily involves understanding pedagogical and psychological perspectives. However, support and training for school sports coaches in this regard are often lacking, leading to a relative deficiency in their understanding of the humanistic spirit. Therefore, school sports coaches should strive to overcome traditional stereotypes, recognize the positive role of the sports-humanistic spirit in their professional development and in school education, and actively seek opportunities to enhance their understanding in this area.

“The spirit of the Chinese women’s volleyball team is like a beacon for every sports enthusiast in China. If we keep it up, it becomes part of the legacy of their team culture. As a coach, my job is to pass on knowledge, teach techniques, promote sports culture, and instill that same sports spirit in my students.“(Interview Material 20).

## Principles of movement science
Principles of movement science entail that the work of school sports coaches should be evidence-based and grounded in scientific research. Specifically, participants explained that school sports coaches should cultivate a scientific mindset and then be able to help students develop their own scientific mindset individually. However, participants suggested that scientific competence is often a challenge for school sports coaches. For instance, school sports coaches may have different educational backgrounds and personal experience compared to physical education teachers, and they may have undergone less training or learning in kinesiology, which can limit their understanding of student diversity during coaching. Additionally, there is often a lack of awareness among school sports coaches regarding the importance of developing a scientific mindset. For example, school sports coaches may overlook the value of conducting scientific research alongside providing sports training, thereby missing valuable opportunities to enhance their coaching practices in school contexts.
Therefore, it is essential for school sports coaches to recognize the importance of enhancing their sports scientific spirit for sustainable professional development. They should actively seek opportunities to improve their understanding of movement science principles and incorporate evidence-based practices into their coaching methodologies.

“Nowadays, professional sports teams have all sorts of high-tech support like tactical analysts, physical trainers, therapists, and top-notch equipment. It’s all about precise observation and accuracy. Even though school sports might not have all those resources, coaches can still make a difference by conducting research and building reflective skills. We can start by boosting our own understanding of science, bringing that into our teaching and training methods.“(Interview Material 09).

## Coaching excellence
Coaching excellence encompasses the ability to face and adapt to the changing environment of daily life and work. For school sports coaches, participants considered that the capacity to adjust to new circumstances and address challenges with flexibility is vital within school context. These dimensions are also the most easily perceived by society. The category of coach excellence within the school sports coach competence model includes nine parts: lifestyle and fitness strength, local sports development, teacher-coach partnership, leadership in extracurricular activities, safety and injury protection, athlete selection and training, instruction and demonstration, coaching reflective practice, and athlete-coach rapport.

## Lifestyle and fitness strength
Lifestyle and fitness strength serve as the foundation for a school sports coach’s professional development. Specifically, participants agree that school sports coaches should possess adequate sports skills in both specialized events and common sports included in the physical education curriculum. They should also maintain a level of physical fitness suitable for their age and engage in regular daily exercise. As for extracurricular physical education activities, school sports coaches play a crucial role in ensuring that students have the motivation and physical capability to engage in adequate physical activities beyond physical education classes. Therefore, school sports coaches should set a positive example for students by their own lifestyles.
Participants defined the idea of lifestyle and fitness strength within the context of the sport training context. In the context of the school sports coach’s competence model, lifestyle and fitness strength encompass not only the ability and experience related to exercise but also their application within the school sport training process. School sports coaches should develop the mindset to integrate their exercise abilities and experiences into teaching students during sports training sessions effectively. This ensures that students can benefit from their coaches’ experiences and abilities in physical education.
Furthermore, as the requirements for student physical fitness assessments continue to rise, there is also a growing emphasis on the physical fitness of school sports coaches. To maintain sustainable professional development, school sports coaches must engage in regular physical fitness training. Moreover, with the emergence of the Gym for All initiative in the new era, more and more students join gym for after class training actively, so that school sports coaches should also embrace the gym culture to better understand the needs of their students.

“Basically, great coaches in schools need a mix of real-world experience and theoretical knowledge. They should have played the sport themselves and reached a decent level, read more books about how to build muscles and maintain a healthy body for different age groups, so they truly understand it from both practical and theoretical aspects.“(Interview Material 09).

## Local sports development
Local sports development entails the guidance of local sports competitions. Participants expressed that the ability to guide competitions is a critical component of the final aspect of sports training ability, which is undeniably essential in the school sports coach competence model. This ability encompasses several aspects, including comprehensive judgment of competitions, understanding of sports skills and tactics, and maintaining a positive mindset during each competition. The outcome of sports competitions primarily depends on both the daily training and performance during each sports competition.
Performance during competitions is influenced not only by the beliefs, attitudes, and competencies of student athletes themselves but also by the guidance and immediate decisions of the school sports coach. School sports coaches must flexibly and decisively select appropriate skills or tactics based on a comparison of their team’s abilities with those of the opponent. This involves creating a strategic plan that considers the opponent’s strengths and weaknesses, adjusting the plan to target specific goals based on the current competition situation, and effectively summarizing and reflecting on each competition for continuous learning. Therefore, the ability to guide sports competitions significantly predicts and influences the performance outcomes of sports competitions.

“I found school sport coaches have two main problems. One is they don’t plan before games, provide guidance during matches, or review afterwards. The other issue is they constantly worried during competition, wanting to control every aspect and make it perfect. Both extremes are problematic – the first undermines the coach’s effectiveness, and the second hinders the players’ ability to think for themselves, which isn’t ideal.“(Interview Material 20).

## Teacher-coach partnership
The teacher-coach partnership serves as the bridge between school sports coaches and physical education practice, facilitating the integration of sports and physical education. While the primary responsibility for the physical education curriculum lies with physical education teachers, school sports coaches also have certain tasks related to curriculum teaching to some extent. These tasks represent a collaboration and substitute relationship between school sports coaches and physical education teachers.
For school sports coaches, their expertise in sports is an advantage, but they may have potential weaknesses in understanding the school context, namely, pedagogy matters. This suggests that school sports coaches have much to learn from physical education teachers. Conversely, school sports coaches often possess more experience related to fitness enhancement, making them a valuable resource for physical education teachers seeking assistance in promoting student health. This is especially relevant as fitness training has become an integral part of school teaching content.

“The difference between school sports coaches and physical education teachers, especially in higher education, really shows. Sports training students focus more on becoming coaches, so they take more specialized courses and develop stronger sports skills in different sports events. On the other hand, physical education teacher education programs have a smaller proportion of specialized courses related to the sports skills, which sets them apart.” (Interview Material 12).

## Leadership in extracurricular activities
Participants agreed that leadership in extracurricular activities involves guiding school sports coaches in organizing extracurricular activities. These activities in school physical education encompass sports competitions, sports clubs, societies, and other sports institutions within student unions. In the Chinese context, sports clubs and competitions among classes or schools are highly popular among students. These activities serve as the main extracurricular activities in school physical education, aiming to promote the health of both students and teachers, develop interests, and even provide opportunities for students to explore potential professions in various fields. For well-organized extracurricular activities in school physical education, school sports coaches must learn how to plan, organize, and guide them in collaboration with physical education teachers. These extracurricular activities contribute to building a well-balanced model for both students and teachers.

“School sports coaches also have the responsibility of organizing various extracurricular sports activities, like class leagues for football, basketball, volleyball, and others. This includes tasks such as setting up competition rules, organizing events, and overseeing everything. So, coaches got to be able to handle all of that too.” (Interview Material 13).

## Safety and injury protection
The ability to ensure sports injury protection is paramount in school physical education. The foremost priority in school sports training is to ensure the safety and well-being of students. Safety and injury protection within the school sports coach competence model primarily encompass three aspects: mastering theoretical knowledge related to sports injuries, maintaining awareness of protection and safety measures, and understanding injury prevention techniques and recovery protocols.
Regarding theoretical knowledge of sports injuries, proficiency in fields such as kinesiology, including sports anatomy, sports medicine, and sports physiology, is essential. Awareness of protection and safety entails the ability to prevent, intervene, and address any mistakes made by students during sports training promptly. Injury prevention in the context of school physical education involves implementing evidence-based training methods, conducting suitable warm-ups before training sessions, and incorporating stretching and relaxation exercises afterward. Managing injuries requires school sports coaches to accurately assess the severity of injuries, administer first aid for minor injuries, and seek appropriate medical assistance for more serious injuries. They should also establish sustainable recovery plans tailored to the needs of student athletes who sustain injuries. With increasing societal attention to physical education and health, the school sports coaching competence model must align with these priorities. In the modern era, school sports coaches should not only focus on teaching sports skills but also emphasize the importance of health promotion and fostering a mindset of sustainable training among student athletes.

“Coaches need to have some basic knowledge and skills when it comes to sports injuries. They should know stuff like applying ice, compression, and elevating injured areas within 24 hours of an acute sports injury. Also, they should be able to do simple things like disinfection, dressing wounds, and even cardiopulmonary resuscitation if needed.“(Interview Material 06).

## Athletes selection and training
Student selection and development in school sports training require school sports coaches to base their decisions on their professional expertise to identify excellent student athletes and provide them with appropriate support to nurture their talents for national and international sports competitions. The ability to select and develop students involves identifying, choosing, assessing, and cultivating talented student athletes.
Finding student athletes necessitates serious observation by school sports coaches during physical education classes and sports clubs or societies. Student athlete selection requires a mindset and attitude grounded in scientific evidence, aiming to identify potential athletes and then match them with the most suitable sports events. Assessing student athletes involves the ability to judge whether they can sustainably pursue a career in sports competition.
Cultivating student athletes remains a challenge in the Chinese context, and significant changes are unlikely to occur in a short period. However, school sports coaches can enhance their related competencies to the best of their abilities. Although support for developing the judgment abilities of school sports coaches is currently limited, they should endeavour to find their own paths and advocate for training support within their schools.

“In the school model, nurturing athletes, even those at a high level, is tough because one big challenge is the risk of delaying academics. So, coaches need to be able to make initial assessments when selecting talent to see if they have athletic potential and if they’re suited for sports training. It’s all about being responsible for the students and handling talent development in a sensible way.“(Interview Material 07).

## Instruction and demonstration
The ability to train for sports competitions is closely tied to how school leaders perceive the performance of school sports coaches’ duties. This ability requires school sports coaches to master the theoretical model of sports training, possess experience in developing sports training plans, effectively implement these plans, make flexible adjustments as needed, and conduct thorough assessments to improve future training.
Theoretical knowledge of sports training involves understanding the principles of sports training and the rules of various sports events. Planning is fundamental to effective sports training implementation. School sports coaches must tailor their training plans to the specific needs of student athletes, considering time constraints imposed by assignments from other subjects and physical education classes.
Implementing sports training is complex due to the ever-changing environment. External factors such as the school sports coach, students’ parents, student athletes, and other teachers can all influence the training process. Therefore, school sports coaches must be adept at adjusting to emergency situations and resolving conflicts effectively.
Furthermore, summarizing and assessing sports training sessions is crucial for helping student athletes improve their performance. This step allows coaches to identify areas for improvement and adjust their training methods accordingly.

“School sports team training aims for a professional approach by considering the age groups of students, whether they’re in primary, middle, or high school. It involves different phases like preparation, actual games, and winter training. Coaches also consider students’ academic schedules, such as exam weeks and vacations, when planning training sessions.“(Interview Material 02).

## Coaching reflective practice
Coaching reflective practice is closely tied to the professional development of school sports coaches. This ability requires school sports coaches to engage in training and curriculum related to school sports, understand their strengths and weaknesses as school sports coaches, stay updated on new knowledge, beliefs, and policies, be open to learning from colleagues, master new media technologies for school sports training, and develop comprehensive competence.
A lifelong learning mindset is crucial for school sports coaches to actively improve their professional skills, enhance their motivation to work, and contribute to social services in their role. It involves continuous learning and sustainable development to maintain a high level of professionalism. In the era of social media, information can be accessed through various channels, allowing school sports coaches to access specific training curriculum tailored to their needs online. Social media also facilitates connections with other school sports coaches, fostering a sense of community and understanding of different school settings, thereby promoting inclusivity.
Reflection involves deep thinking about the importance of one’s role, emphasizing the significance of lifelong learning for professional development, maintaining awareness of the current school sports training context, and common training approaches. Additionally, conducting a meta-analysis of school sports training experiences can provide valuable insights into effectively addressing challenges in the school setting.
Reflective practice is not a one-way street but a lifelong journey. School sports coaches should develop the habit of reflecting before, during, and after school sports training or any related coaching work. This practice supports sustainable professional development in line with the new era of school sports training.

“As a football coach, besides completing coach training courses, I also dedicate time to watching high-level football matches. By studying advanced skills and tactics and analyzing my team’s performance, I can identify areas for improvement and make targeted adjustments to enhance our performance.“(Interview Material 07).

## Athlete-coach rapport
Athlete-coach rapport serves as the bridge for building trusting relationships between school sports coaches and student athletes. This competence requires school sports coaches to possess the ability to make inclusive and strategic decisions for tactics, provide leadership for school sports training, manage teammates in school sports, coordinate resources, foster a collaborative environment for sports teams, and establish a well-developed management model for school sports training.
Making tactical decisions entails setting long-term goals for school sports teams, necessitating clear objectives and adherence to development directions. The leadership of school sports coaches contributes to building strong bonds within school sports teams, relying on the individual personality and management skills of the coach. Managing school team members involves understanding the diverse needs of students and personalizing individual plans to implement appropriate management methods.
Resource coordination is a critical yet often overlooked aspect of the competence model. It involves effectively utilizing training-related placements, equipment, and financial resources to ensure the smooth operation of school competition teams. Additionally, establishing school regulations forms the foundation for setting guidelines for school sports training and competition. School sports coaches must develop proper regulations that facilitate effective management and contribute to the sustainable development of school sports training.
In conclusion, Athlete-coach rapport indicates that organization and management are key factors in ensuring the stable development of school sports training and competition. They represent pivotal elements in the competence development of school sports coaches.

“Being a coach involves a ton of organization and management for the sports team. It’s a complex job that requires a lot of energy. From buying equipment to budgeting funds, arranging games, planning holiday training, and even overseeing students’ daily studies and lives, the coach is involved in every aspect of the team’s operation.“(Interview Material 10).

## Discussion

## Strong connection among three dimensions of competence model
This study endeavours to construct a sustainable school sports coaching competence model using a grounded theory approach. The sustainable school sports coaching competence model comprises three dimensions: perception of identity, code of ethics, and coaching excellence. Similar as physical education teachers’ professional development, these dimensions hold distinct meanings and strongly influence each other [ ]. In the school context, it is crucial to consider identities as inextricably linked to pedagogical practices and attitudes [ ]. Specifically, when school sports coaches understand the significance of their role in school education and student development, this positive belief model contributes to the development of the other two dimensions: code of ethics and coaching excellence. How school sports coaches perceive themselves significantly impacts their behaviour and interactions with students in a school setting [ ]. Conversely, the code of ethics guides school sports coaches on the path of sustainable competence development. Once school sports coaches acquire the appropriate ethics, they can understand and implement their values and beliefs for student-athlete development more effectively [ ].
Meanwhile, coaching excellence represents the clearest way for society to assess the professional competence level of school sports coaches, such as physical fitness strength and sports competition organization [ , ]. Compared to physical education teachers, sports participation and physical activity preferences strongly influence both teachers’ competence and also school sports coach competence [ ]. However, professionalism entails the development of all dimensions to the same level as their coaching competence. Otherwise, coaching competence may lack the pedagogical concern necessary in school settings [ , , ]. Therefore, all three dimensions are crucial in the competence model for school sports coaches. Each dimension has its unique position [ ], and school sport coaching competence must be valued based on the specific requirement of the coaches [ ]. This approach will effectively guide the development of ideal school sport coaches for students, student-athletes, and the holistic development of future athletes.

## Reflect the new era on integration of sports and education
In the era of integration of sports and education, the topic on school sports coaching has gained significant importance worldwide [ , , ]. School sports coach serve as a fundamental avenue for promoting students’ health across physical [ ], mental [ ], and social dimensions [ , ]. As school sports coaches emerge as a new group with significant potential to integrate sports elements into the school physical education model, it becomes imperative to address their needs for professional development [ , ]. Capel (2007) suggests that the decision to teach physical education is influenced by sporting success, positive physical education experiences, and effective relationships with significant others involved in this experience, such as coaches and teachers [ ]. In China, although the integration of sports and education is deepening, the school sport coaching community is still underdeveloped, and the number of school sport coaches is insufficient. There are two approaches to integrating sports and education into the school sport coach community: first, physical education teachers could obtain coaching certification to become school sport coaches. However, the examination system, primarily based on paperwork, results in these coaches lacking practical coaching experience. Similarly, another type of school sport coach comes from a background as retired athletes or competitive sport coaches, which may lead to a potential misunderstanding between teaching and coaching.
Therefore, considering the local and national context based on Chinese wisdom, the sustainable school sports coaching competence theoretical model could guide the design of training and education for school sports coaches, especially for those who come from a background in physical education. Additionally, the school sport coaching competence can help coaches with a competitive sport background enhance their pedagogical knowledge. Specifically, the discourse model of sport training and teaching in physical education are different [ ]. How school sports coaches navigate their training knowledge and practice from athletes to students, and how they transfer coaching knowledge through teaching and assessment in school are still unclear. For example, the school context highlights the comprehensive development of students, where every subject matters. Thus, the training hours should be considered based on the school curriculum and other extracurricular activities on campus [ ]. These questions under the intricate relationship between sports and education within the school environment. They reflect the growing recognition of the importance of integrating sports activities into educational settings to promote holistic student development. While this is not always true, for example, a competitive sport coach transitioning to a school sport coach might encounter misunderstandings related to education [ ], such as bullying [ ]. Although such incidents are not common, they should be completely eradicated from the field of education.
As such, addressing these questions becomes paramount in ensuring that school sports coaches are adequately equipped to effectively contribute to this integration, thereby fostering the students’ competence development.

## Pathways for school sports coaches’ sustainable growth
At the macro level, the sustainable school sports coaching competence model can offer valuable insights for policymakers, enabling them to better understand the professional development needs of school sports coaches [ ]. By bridging the gap between theory and practice, policymakers can address common weaknesses and ensure that policies align with the realities faced by school sports coaches. Notably, like Korean sport coaches’ competence, Chinese school sport coaches’ coaching excellence has more sub-dimensions than the other two dimensions [ ], indicating that school sport coach competence development is driven by practical considerations. For instance, “Health China 2030” includes a section on school sports that stipulates schools must offer sufficient time for physical activity, organize after-class sports activities, and employ qualified physical education professionals [ ]. School sports coaches need to embrace a healthy lifestyle and possess adequate fitness strength, setting a positive example for students’ health promotion.
At the meso level, a student-tailored development model can sustainably benefit coaching assessment in the school context [ ]. The school sports coach competence model can serve as assessment indicators for evaluating school sports coach performance. This provides a structured model for assessing the effectiveness of school sports coaches in fulfilling their roles within the school setting. On a micro level, school sports coaches themselves can use the competence model to develop a clear and comprehensive understanding of their role. They can assess their current level of competence and compare it to the ideal level, thereby identifying areas for improvement and setting goals for professional growth.
From the perspective of students, the sustainable school sports coaching competence model can serve as a theoretical model for evaluating the teaching and training provided by school sports coaches [ ]. This allows students to provide feedback on the effectiveness of school sports coach interventions and contribute to their own learning experiences. Similarly, parents of students can benefit from the school sports coach competence model by gaining a deeper understanding of the importance of the school sports coach profession within the school setting, particularly in the context of physical education. Overall, the construction of the school sports coach competence development model offers multiple perspectives and levels of engagement, making valuable contributions to school education, particularly in the field of physical education [ ]. Additionally, the school sport coaching competence model could facilitate the integration of sport and education. This integration would make it more sustainable and enhance the development of school physical education through effective collaboration in the field of sport coaching. For instance, students have expressed that they enjoy physical education classes that not only concentrate on the curriculum but also offer opportunities for participating in competitive sports as part of extracurricular activities [ ]. These experiences enable them to make new friends and actively acquire physical education and sports skills and knowledge. However, the number of physical education teachers remains insufficient [ ]. This shortage hampers the effective implementation of extracurricular activities. As a result, students may feel dissatisfied or bored with physical education classes. This dissatisfaction can lead to a loss of interest and fewer opportunities for developing their physical and mental health. School sport coaches are vital in addressing these issues within extracurricular activities. Specifically, school sport coaches are responsible for demonstrating sports techniques, conducting fitness training, selecting student-athletes, and coaching athletes for competitions. They also manage extracurricular programs, such as school physical education clubs and societies. Nevertheless, the role of school sport coaches is still in its infancy [ ]. Many coaches are not yet familiar with the unique context of school physical education [ , ]. Therefore, the theoretical model of school sport coaching competence could assist these coaches in understanding the necessary skills and abilities required for effective work in a school setting. This model could also help them navigate challenges, such as inadequate extracurricular programs and a lack of high-quality offerings that meet students’ needs.

## Limitations and future research
While this study employed grounded theory methodology adhering to rigorous procedures and standards, there are several limitations that warrant consideration. Firstly, the study’s sample size, while representative of the context in China, may not fully capture the diversity present across different provinces. Future research could benefit from exploring a larger and more diverse sample to enhance the generalizability of findings. Similarly, the data was primarily gathered from the coaches themselves, which may not align with the demands of students, student-athletes or athletes. Therefore, it is recommended that future research also includes the perspectives of general students, student-athletes, and athletes.
Moreover, given the variations in requirements and characteristics of school sports coaches across different countries or regions, there is a need for international comparative analyses of the sustainable school sports coaching competence model. This comparative approach would foster collaboration and communication among school sports coaches across national boundaries, enriching our understanding of best practices in the field.
Furthermore, while qualitative data offer valuable insights, they are susceptible to subjectivity bias and limited sample sizes. To address this, future research should consider employing quantitative research methods or mixed method approaches to validate and complement the sustainable school sports coaching competence model.
Additionally, as sports training methods can vary depending on the specific types of sport events, future research could explore school sports coach competence models tailored to different educational levels or specific sport disciplines. This approach would provide a more nuanced understanding of the unique challenges and requirements faced by school sports coaches in different contexts.
Lastly, regarding coach-teacher collaboration, it is also worth exploring how physical education teachers adapt their pedagogical experiences to support school sports coaches in understanding the school context. Additionally, investigating the challenges faced by both physical education teachers and sport coaches in effectively communicating with each other would be beneficial.

## Conclusions
The sustainable school sports coaching competence model was generated by the three-stage coding, which included three main categories, 15 categories, 92 concepts and 317 labels. Each theme represents a distinct aspect of the sustainable school sports coaching competence model and contributes to a comprehensive understanding of the complexities and nuances about what an ideal school sports coach looks like. Under each main theme, we have identified numerous subthemes that further elaborate on the key concepts and categories identified in the data. These subthemes provide a more detailed and refined view of the factors influencing the sustainable development of school sports coaching competence, offering insights into the perceptions and practices of school sports coaches in various school contexts. The three categories are (a) Perception of identity, encompassing role of the coach, reflection on students, and understanding of policies; (b) Code of ethics, which includes sports virtues, sports-humanistic spirit, and principles of movement science; (c) Coaching excellence, comprising lifestyle and fitness strength, local sports development, teacher-coach partnership, leadership in extracurricular activities, safety and injury protection, athlete selection and training, instruction and demonstration, coaching reflective practice, and athlete-coach rapport. Overall, the school sports coach competence model offers a comprehensive model for understanding and enhancing the professional capabilities of school sports coaches. By addressing the complexities and nuances of their roles, this model provides valuable insights that can inform policy, practice, and professional development initiatives in the field of school sports coaching.
