## Highlights
•Immigrants in the US more often had overweight or obesity than those in Belgium.
•In US and Belgium, most foods adopted after migration were fast- and junk-foods.
•Time since migration associated with adopting fresh foods and not processed foods.
•In US, newly adopted foods had higher energy density and protein than lost foods.
•In Belgium, newly adopted foods had higher energy density and sugar than lost foods.
•Immigrants in US were less likely than in Belgium to lose fresh foods they ate before.
•Immigrants in US were less likely than in Belgium to adopt processed foods.
•Immigrants in US and Belgium had less overweight/obesity than native-born people.
Keywords: Migration, Refugee, Asylum-seeker, Diet, Obesity, Integration, Nutrition, United States, Belgium

## Abstract
Immigrants’ health may differ with the characteristics of the place they resettle. We examined eating patterns and weight status across two differing environments - Atlanta, United States and Brussels, Belgium - collecting information on diet and health from a diverse sample of 111 refugees, asylum-seekers and other migrants. Analyses used descriptive statistics, multivariate regressions, and text analysis. People who had resettled in Atlanta reported higher weight categories than those in Belgium. In both locations, the most commonly adopted items were processed foods (71 % in Atlanta, 45 % in Brussels); many also listed adopting fruits and vegetables (31 % in Atlanta, 14 % in Brussels). Thus, changes in health-related behaviors after migration included both healthy and unhealthy components of the contexts of reception. Longer time since arrival was associated with lower odds of having adopted processed foods and higher odds of having adopted fresh foods, suggesting that dietary change is nuanced with immigrants’ integration trajectories.

## Introduction
Over 6.9 million people are asylum seekers and 31.6 million refugees worldwide ( ). There is considerable political, scholarly, and societal interest in the integration of newly arrived refugees, as conflict, poverty, and inequality drive hundreds of thousands of persons each year to look for a better life abroad.
Upon arrival, migrants are often in better health compared both with native-born people in their country of reception and with peers in their country of origin ( ; ; ; ; ). In the United States (U.S.), foreign-born people are less likely than native-born people to suffer from heart disease, obesity, mental disorders and several cancers, and have lower overall mortality rates ( ; ; ; ; ; ; ; ). In Europe, foreign-born people are less likely to die from most types of cancers and may have lower cardiovascular mortality than native-born people ( ; ; ; ; ). However, several studies across immigrant destinations have found foreign-born people to be in worse cardio-metabolic health - cardiovascular disease, diabetes and obesity – and some studies have argued that these conditions are less prevalent in foreign-born people on arrival, but that they increase disproportionally with time since arrival ( ; ; ; ; ; ; ; ; ; ; ; ). Indeed, the longer they stay, immigrants’ health advantages dissipate, especially in terms of obesity and diabetes ( ; ; ; ; ; ; ; ; ; ; ). In a systematic review, all but one study reported a significant, positive relationship between bodyweight and duration of residence in the U.S. ( ), with bodyweight substantially elevated after 10–20 years of residence.
Several reasons have been proposed for these patterns of chronic disease. Especially in the United States and Canada, explanations have centered on migrants’ new exposure to obesogenic environments, that is, environments that may promote obesity and associated diseases ( ; ; ; ; ). According to this proposition, migrants resettling in Western countries are thrust into obesogenic environments with easy access to unhealthy foods, only limited access to healthy food, and sedentary, screen-based work and leisure. Obesogenic places have high rates of obesity and related disease in the native-born population, and the longer foreign-born people are exposed to these conditions, the more their weight increases also.
A related explanation for the increases in obesity among immigrants with length of residence focuses on acculturation, or how immigrants’ lifestyles and behaviors change after resettlement. According to this explanation, foreign-born people arrive with different eating and activity habits and preferences, which reflect the healthier environments from which they are coming. These healthier habits are more likely to be retained within ethnic enclaves, which emulate the environment in the country of origin ( ; ; ). With time, as immigrants assimilate in terms of language, work, and social activities, they also adopt local ideas, preferences, and behaviors – including those that may lead to obesity with time ( ; ; ; ). These changes may include consuming more energy-dense, processed foods and sedentary lifestyles ( ; ; ; ; ; ; ; ).
Another related framing points to the interaction between migrants’ place of origin and community of reception. Drawing on concepts from the Barker Hypothesis, which posits that individuals are primed during the period of developmental plasticity of the first 1000 days of life for their current living environments. If, later on, there is a mismatch between this and their actual living environment, then they may develop chronic diseases. Thus, if an individual is born in a place with lean diets or limited food supplies and then migrates to a place with excess calories and rich diets, they are metabolically primed for weight gain.
A common feature of these hypotheses is the importance of characteristics of the context of reception. Thus, we can expect that there will be variability in cardiometabolic health across countries of resettlement, with some being more obesogenic than others.
Changes in dietary patterns are often believed to be central to post-migration changes in health. Some studies highlight that migrants, especially from places with very different food environments, may not be knowledgeable about the nutritional qualities of foods ( ; ). Regardless, migrants’ knowledge and beliefs about nutrition can change after migration ( ; ; ). Additionally, food is a way for migrants to signal about their identity ( ; ; ; ; ) - thus, what they eat may indicate that they are from a particular country of origin, changing eating habits could signal that one is a successfully integrated immigrant and now a full member of the society of reception ( ; ).
Immigrants are not a homogenous group in terms of their premigration experiences, nor their experiences of resettlement. In this paper, our focus is on asylum-seekers and refugees, who are often especially disadvantaged. They may be in poor health prior to migrating and have worse health compared to other immigrant groups after resettlement ( ). ( ; ; ) In the U.S., refugees had the highest prevalence of chronic disease, self-rated health and mobility limitations compared to immigrants arriving on any other visa type ( ; ; ).
Previous studies have offered important insights into migrant health and point to directions for the research presented in this study. First, most studies have focused on general migrant populations, with limited information about refugees and asylum-seekers—groups that are particularly vulnerable due to their precarious circumstances. Second, there is a lack of comparative studies examining different environments of resettlement. Third, the role of obesogenic environments has been theorized, but not empirically tested internationally. Therefore, this study investigates how migrants change their diets and body weight after migration, and whether these changes differ between those resettling in more or less obesogenic environments. Using primary data collected from refugees and asylum-seekers in Brussels, Belgium, and Atlanta, United States—two top destinations for international migrants but with vastly different living environments—this study explores the integration experiences that may be relevant to chronic disease onset. Atlanta, located in the American South, within the area that has been called the stroke-belt, is often considered an obesogenic environment, with extensive urban sprawl, very limited public transportation and walkability, high density of fast-food outlets, and long distances to grocery stores. In Georgia, 31.4 % of adults have obesity and 14.2 % have diabetes ( ; ). Brussels is a highly walkable metropolitan area, with extensive public transportation, and is well-integrated with grocery stores and markets, as well as fast food outlets. In Belgium, 15.9 % of native-born adults have obesity and 5.9 % have diabetes ( ; ). Thus, these two contexts of reception offer very different environments for resettlement. By comparing dietary changes and weight status in these two contexts, we seek to provide new insights into how the environments of reception impact health among refugees and asylum-seekers, thereby advancing the understanding of migration-related health inequalities. The migration and health literature suggests that refugees and asylum-seekers will adopt less healthy diets over time, and that these changes will be more pronounced in the U.S. than in Belgium.
Based on the migration and health literature, which posits that people moving to Western countries lose healthy traditional foods and adopt unhealthy, highly processed new foods, we expect that respondents will have lost fruits, vegetables, and whole grains, and will have adopted animal products and processed foods. Concomitantly, those resettled in the U.S. are also expected to have more excess body weight.

## Materials and methods
2.1.

## Study population
We recruited recently-arrived adults in the Brussels metropolitan area, Belgium and in the Atlanta metropolitan area, USA. In the U.S., resettlement services are outsourced by the government to non-profit organizations. These organizations are assigned individuals who have been granted refugee status and are responsible for organizing housing and basic languages and employee training. In Atlanta, we partnered with one of these organizations to conduct the study. We collected data in 2017, recruiting individuals who coming to the office for free walk-in legal services. People were invited to participate while waiting for their consultation and assured they would not lose their spot in line if they participated. Fifty-one resettled refugees participated in 30-minute interviews in English, Spanish, or with assistance of an interpreter they had brought with them.
In Belgium, asylum-seekers register on arrival and are permitted to stay legally in the country while their case is being considered, housed at government supported facilities. Those who are accorded refugee status are then assisted to find housing and jobs; those who are not, must leave. Some people who have been denied or who decided not to apply for asylum in Belgium stay undocumented. In Brussels, we collected data using venue-based sampling outside the offices where asylum-seekers apply for documentation and at parks where people often gather to enjoy the outdoors. Sixty refugees, asylum seekers, and other documented and undocumented foreign-born people participated in 30-minute interviews in English or Arabic.
The data in this study are anonymous - the research team has no contact information or any identifiable information about study participants. Prior to starting the study, ethical approval was obtained from the institutional review boards of the researchers’ institutions to confirm the study met national and international guidelines for research on humans. All participants received information about the study and gave informed consent to participate.

2.2.

## Data
We developed bespoke instruments drawing on our prior experience and revised them after repeated pre-testing. The instrument begins with a quantitative module on demographic and socio-economic characteristics. Questionnaires collected information on gender, age (years), years since arrival in this country, family residence (living with partner, living with children, living without partner or children), and region of origin (Africa, Asia, Middle East, Europe and Americas). Those from Burundi, Democratic Republic of Congo, Eritrea, Ethiopia, Guinea, Honduras, Kenya, Liberia, Morocco, Somalia, Algeria, Cameroon, Eritrea, Mali, Mozambique, Nigeria, Sierra Leone, Sudan, and Tanzania were categorized in Africa; those from Bhutan, India, Mongolia, Myanmar, Nepal, Philippines, and Thailand were categorized in Asia; those from Afghanistan, Bosnia, France, Iran, Iraq, Italy, Kosovo, Netherlands, Pakistan, Tunisia, Syria, Honduras in were categorized in Middle East, Europe and Americas. These are not ideal categories of regions of origin, but were necessary due to sample size.
In the second module, respondents were asked to list “the 3 most important things that you ate or drank regularly before coming to the U.S. /Belgium that you rarely eat now”. Items listed in response to this prompt are referred to below as “lost foods”. Respondents were then asked a parallel question about foods they eat more often now: “the most important things that you eat or drink a lot now that you rarely ate or drank before coming to the U.S/Belgium”. Items listed in response to this prompt are referred to below as “adopted foods”. Responses were recorded verbatim. This method is a variant of a qualitative method called free-listing ( ), in which participants list the items that come to their minds in response to open-ended questions or prompts ( ; ). Free-listing is commonly used in food-related research to understand food perceptions ( ). It holds that respondents will list the most salient or important items within a domain earlier and more frequently. An item's salience is a measure of its importance in a given cultural domain, based on how frequently it is mentioned across informants and how early it is mentioned in free-lists. Often, items that are encountered frequently in daily life are more salient, and therefore easier to recall. Respondents were then asked what they believed were the reasons for changes in their diets: specifically, after listing lost items, we asked respondents why they no longer consumed these items with a list of yes/no options. In parallel, for the adopted items, we asked why they so frequently consumed the new items with a list of yes/no options.
We used two free-list questions to assess knowledge of nutrition, which is expected to contribute to peoples’ decisions around food: “What foods and beverages do you consider healthy?”; “What foods and beverages do you consider unhealthy?” To gage perceptions of food environments in their communities of resettlement, we then asked the free-list question: “What foods and beverages do you consider American/Belgian?”
Finally, to elicit information on body weight, respondents were given a card with 8 figures and asked, “Which figure looks most like your?” While not noted on the cards, two figures each corresponded to underweight (A&B); normal weight (C&D); overweight (E&F); and obesity (G&H) ( ; ). (Appendix 2) This is a novel way to collect information about weight status – while many studies ask for self-reported height and weight or weight category, pictures provide more guidance for a culturally and linguistically diverse study population.

2.3.

## Analysis
We used several methods to analyze changes in diets. To prepare data from free-lists for analysis, we did a manual review to confirm correct and consistent spelling. An author and a research assistant independently confirmed the identity/definition of each food and beverage listed. In a few cases, we used information on the country of origin to determine the type of food or beverage listed.
We conducted text analysis for lost and adopted items for each site using Dedoose and word clouds to identify themes. Synonymous and related items in each list were collapsed into culturally relevant terms to facilitate analysis ( ). For example, if one individual reported “Fries” and another “French Fries” they were combined into “Fries”. The decision to collapse items was informed by discussions with community partners.
Next, we collapsed free-listed items into 7 food categories: dairy & eggs, fruit, vegetables, grains, protein, processed foods, and ethnic foods. For example, if respondents mentioned fresh fruits, mango, banana, or apples, these items were all categorized as “fruits. The group called ethnic foods consists of items that are general and (for “lost” foods) are typical or traditional in the country of origin and, (for “adopted foods”), were established in the country of resettlement by previous generations of immigrants, for example, “Chinese buffet” or “Greek food”. Some respondents reported no changes, and these were categorized as such (See Appendix 1).
A third approach was to evaluate nutritional content of lost and of adopted foods, to evaluate the nutritional implications of dietary changes. For this component, we used only the first-mentioned item in each participant's lists because it can be considered most salient in the respondents’ mental inventory and because not everyone listed three items ( ). Nutritional content was measured in terms of energy density (kcal/g), protein (mg), total dietary fiber (mg), and total sugar (mg). Nutrients were defined using the USDA Food and Nutrient Database for Dietary Studies (FNDDS) and the Belgian Food Composition Database (NUBEL). Following common practice in nutrition, we excluded beverages due to their high water content; ( ) if an individual listed a beverage as their first item, we used their second listed item in this analysis. Because we did not ask about portion sizes, we use a value per 100 g as a standard item for each food item listed. We descriptively evaluated the mean and range of energy density, protein, dietary fiber and sugars for the first lost and adopted food items.
A fourth approach was to create binary variables based on all the listed items to indicate whether the lost and adopted items were fresh items (fruits and vegetables) or processed items (e.g., hot dogs, hamburgers, fast food chains (KFC, McDonalds) and sweetened beverages). We used logistic regression models to estimate the odds of adopting fresh items and of adopting processed items. Logistic regression was used for modeling binary outcomes to estimate the likelihood of specific dietary changes while controlling for other characteristics. As a sensitivity analyses, we ran the same models using OLS regression, addressing potential heteroscedasticity using White's test, which allowed us to ensure that the variance of errors was consistent across all levels of the independent variables.
Participants who listed both a fresh item and a processed item were included in both categories. People whose food lists had items from neither category, for example bread or milk, were categorized as not listing a fresh item and not listing a processed item. Models were estimated separately for each study location, with right-hand side variables being gender, age, time since arrival, family residence, and region of origin, with variables coded as above. Similar models were used to estimate the odds of losing processed items and fresh items eaten pre-migration.
Then, we pooled participants across the two sites and re-estimated the models, adding a right-hand side variable for country of resettlement. This approach shows characteristics associated with adopting or losing fresh or processed items regardless of where they settled and whether those who settle in the more obesogenic locations (U.S.) are more likely to adopt processed items or lose fresh items than those resettled in the less obesogenic location (Belgium). As a robustness check, we also estimated the models using Ordinary Least Squares (OLS) regression.
To capture migrants’ perceptions of dietary patterns, we examined participants’ responses to questions about food attributes, tabulating the foods and beverages listed as healthy and as unhealthy. We conducted text analysis about attributes of foods and beverages listed as American or as Belgian using Dedoose and word clouds.
Finally, we examined perceptions of body weight, which is influenced by eating behaviors, influences eating behaviors, and is part of acculturation ( ; ; ; ; ). We first examined distributions of responses by the four bodyweight categories: underweight, normal weight, overweight and obese. Because there were few responses in the underweight and obesity categories, we created a binary variable (overweight or obese vs. underweight or normal weight) to improve statistical power. This variable was used as the outcome in logistic regressions. The right-hand side variables were gender, age, time since arrival, living with partner, living with children, and region of origin. Models were first estimated separately for each location, then pooled, adding a right-hand side variable for country of resettlement.

3.

## Results
3.1.

## Migration characteristics
Respondents in Belgium were from 23 countries ( ), with the largest number being from Afghanistan (20 %); other common countries of origin were Somalia, Sudan, Sierra Leone, Congo and Algeria. Respondents in the U.S. were from 18 countries, with the largest number being from Myanmar (20 %); other common countries of origin were Thailand, Iraq, Ethiopia, Liberia and Afghanistan. These countries reflect the refugee populations resettling in each country at the time of the study.
 In both settings, respondents had migrated on average 8 years earlier; just under half in both settings had lived in a refugee camp previously. In the U.S., all respondents were refugees. In Belgium, 33 % were asylum seekers and refugees, 43 % were legal residents and naturalized citizens, and 20 % were undocumented. The average age in both sites was 35–36 years, but family structure was different: in the US, the majority of respondents were women (53 %), were married (69 %) and had children (2 on average), while in Belgium the majority of respondents were men (65 %), were unmarried (55 %) and few were co-residing with children (15 %).

3.2.

## Foods lost and foods adopted
3.2.1.

## Foods no longer eaten
 shows foods lost and adopted. In Belgium, respondents most frequently listed no longer eating couscous, meat, soup, rice and vegetables. In the US, respondents also frequently listed no longer eating rice and vegetables but also bread, tea, fish paste, and water. The top-listed reason across settings for no longer consuming the previously important items was that they could not be found in their new communities; the second-listed reason across settings was that the local version of the item did not taste the same as in their home country (results available upon request).
 
3.2.2.

## Newly adopted foods
In both settings, respondents frequently listed pizza as a new item frequently eaten. In Belgium, fries were most frequently listed, followed by pizza, fruit, hamburger, pasta, spaghetti, and chocolate. In the U.S., pizza and chicken were equally listed most frequently, followed by vegetables, rice, sandwiches, McDonalds, soda, juice and cheeseburgers. The top-listed reason for adopting the new items was that they were easy to find. Affordability was mentioned by some, but not frequently (results available upon request).

In both countries, among those who reported losing items, over 50 % listed grains and 13 % listed fruits and vegetables that they no longer eat. Still, many participants also listed adopting healthy items: in the U.S., especially new grains, and in Belgium, new fruits and vegetables.

## Comparison of nutritional quality
Nutritional quality of the first-listed food items in each country is shown in . For reference, a 100 g serving of a McDonalds Cheeseburger has 2.7 kcal/g of energy density, 13.5 g of protein, 16.2 g of sugar, and 1.9 g of dietary fiber; a McDonald's salad with dressing has 0.24 kcal/g of energy density, 2.0 g of protein, 3.4 g of sugar, and 1.4 g of dietary fiber. A food with high energy density would have >4 kcal/g; medium energy density is between 1.5 and 4 kcal/g.
 The first-listed lost items were similar across the two cities in terms of energy density, protein, and total fiber. The one notable difference is that the first-listed lost item among respondents in the U.S. had much higher total sugar than in Belgium (7.17 mg vs. 2.87 mg).
The first-listed adopted items were similar across the two countries in terms of energy density, total sugar and total fiber. The notable difference is that the first-listed adopted food item in the U.S. had higher protein content than in Belgium (11.78 mg vs. 7.06 mg). Compared with the foods no longer eaten, the foods adopted after resettling in the U.S. had 35 % higher energy density, 225 % higher protein and slightly lower fiber, but 30 % lower sugar. Compared with the foods no longer eaten, the foods adopted after resettling in Belgium had 30 % higher energy density, 80 % higher sugar and slightly lower fiber and protein.

3.3.

## Changes in dietary patterns
 shows the odds of having lost fresh and processed foods, controlling for sex, and, in pooled models, country of resettlement. In site-specific models, personal characteristics largely were not related to odds of losing fresh or processed food and beverage items. One exception was that, in the U.S. only, people living with children, compared with those living alone, were less likely to report having lost processed foods that they used to eat in their home countries. In the pooled model, country of resettlement was a predictor of type of foods lost. Specifically, people who had relocated to the U.S. were significantly less likely to report having lost fresh foods they used to eat before migration compared with those who resettled in Belgium. At the same time, people who had relocated in the U.S. were significantly more likely to report having lost processed foods they used to eat before migration compared with those who resettled in Belgium.
 Turning to foods respondents reported adopting after resettlement, personal characteristics, including region of origin, largely were not significantly related to odds of adopting fresh or processed food items in the U.S. In Belgium, respondents had higher odds of reporting adopting fresh foods if they were men, if they were older, and if they had lived longer in Belgium; they had lower odds of adopting processed foods if they had more recently immigrated. In pooled analysis, time since immigration was associated with higher odds of adopting fresh foods and lower odds of adopting processed foods. Time was not associated with the types of foods reported as lost, it was associated with the types of foods reported as adopted. Specifically, the longer respondents had been in the country, the lower the odds of reporting newly important processed foods and the higher the odds reporting newly important fresh foods. Models using OLS yielded consistent results (Appendix 3).

## Food perceptions
 shows the items identified by respondents as healthy and as unhealthy. In both settings, respondents most frequently listed vegetables and fruits as healthy, though those in the U.S. did so less frequently than those in Belgium. In both settings, water was frequently listed. Specific items frequently listed in Belgium were vegetables - by a large margin - followed by fish and fruit; specific items frequently listed in the U.S. were vegetables, water, fruit and milk. US-based respondents frequently (incorrectly) listed juice as a healthy item.
 Respondents in Belgium most frequently listed animal products as unhealthy, followed by sodas, juices, and fast foods; in the U.S., fast food, junk food and sodas were most frequently listed as unhealthy. Specific items frequently listed in Belgium were meat and sugar; specific items frequently listed in the U.S. were soda and fast food.
 shows the items respondents identified with their country of resettlement. Respondents across both settings focused on fast foods as representative of their countries of reception: in Belgium, half of the foods listed as Belgian were street foods, including fries and meat; in the U.S., a third of foods listed as American were fast foods and junk foods. Many respondents in both settings also listed grains, vegetables and fruits. An interesting pattern is that many of the items identified with the country of reception were items that were actually foods introduced by earlier waves of immigrants in each setting, for example, pasta and Chinese food.
 
3.5.

## Body weight perceptions
As shown in , respondents in the US identified themselves most frequently with the figures corresponding with overweight (45 %); 8 % indicated obesity and 39 % normal weight. Respondents in Belgium identified most frequently with the figure corresponding with normal weight (40 %), 30 % with overweight, 13 % with underweight, and 5 % with obesity. When asked which figure they would most like to have, 27 % of respondents in the US and 39 % of those in Belgium selected as ideal the figure they had also indicated represented their own body weight, indicating positive body image among a third in both populations (results available upon request).

3.6.

## Overweight and obesity
 explores the characteristics associated with excess weight. In the U.S., respondents who were older [1.1 (0.08)] and who were living with a partner [5.8 (4.5)] and/or with children had higher odds of overweight or obesity [9.3 (7.1)]. There were no significant predictors of overweight or obesity for respondents in Belgium. When respondents from both sites were pooled, those in the U.S. had higher odds of excess weight, though not statistically significantly so. Neither region of origin nor time since arrival were associated with body weight. OLS models yielded consistent results (Appendix 4).
 

4.

## Discussion
Previous research reported onset of chronic disease among foreign-born people after resettling in Western countries. This pattern is often conceptualized as resulting from lifestyle changes as part of integration in the obesogenic environments of Western countries. Accordingly, migrants from non-Western countries to Western countries are expected to increase intake of calorie-rich foods and decreasing intake of fresh foods and whole grains and to gain weight. These changes are expected to be more pronounced in communities with especially obesogenic living environments, denoted by high levels of obesity among the overall population. Refugees and asylum-seekers, who tend to be in poorer health and experience difficult circumstances prior to migration, including food insecurity and stress, may be an especially vulnerable group as they adapt to their new environments. This study used mixed methods to explore dietary change and weight status refugees and other recently-arrived immigrants across two metropolitan areas that are major immigrant destinations offering different living environments: metro Atlanta, USA and Brussels, Belgium.
Respondents in the U.S. most frequently reported overweight or obesity (58 %); in the U.S. population the same year, 71.2 % of adults had overweight or obesity ( ). Respondents in Belgium were more frequently normal weight than those in the U.S., with 35 % identifying with overweight or obesity; in the general Belgian population, 49.3 % had overweight or obesity ( ). Thus, while in both places foreign-born respondents less frequently reported overweight or obesity than the overall population in their country of reception, those living in the U.S. were heavier than those in Belgium, consistent with exposure to a more obesogenic environment in the U.S.
In both settings, respondents reported adopting new fast foods and junk foods into their diets since migrating and also reported major changes in the types of grains they consumed; in the U.S., many also adopted sodas. Across the two settings, respondents identified junk food, especially pizza, burgers and fries, as being representative foods in their countries of reception; they identified fruits and vegetables as healthy foods; when asked about unhealthy foods, sweets were listed as concerns more frequently in Belgium and fats more frequently in the U.S. A substantial proportion of respondents in the U.S. incorrectly identified juice as a healthy item and less frequently listed vegetables and fruits among healthy items than respondents in Belgium.
People who had resettled in the U.S. were less likely to report having lost fresh foods they used to eat and more likely to report having lost processed foods before migration compared with those who resettled in Belgium. There were no significant differences in adopting either fresh or processed foods across the two locations; those resettling in the U.S. more frequently reported adopting fruits and vegetables, but also meats and processed foods. These patterns indicate that people resettling in the more and less obesogenic locations experienced dietary changes, though no clear-cut deterioration of diets in either place.
The literature documents a time component to weight gain ( ; ). Is there also a time component to dietary change? It could be that adoption of local eating patterns gets established over time; or, it could be that, as immigrants get more settled, they are better able to locate items they like and less reliant on quick, cheap street food. We found that time since immigration was associated with higher odds of adopting fresh foods and lower odds of adopting processed foods, consistent with the second scenario. The patterns suggests that there may be time components to dietary change, whereby newly arrived people first adopt processed foods, either because these are novel or because they are easier to find as the newly arrived person navigates their new environment. Then, with time, as they get to know the food environment, earnings become more stable, and perhaps they become aware of nuanced considerations in food choice, like healthfulness, their diets may become more diverse and incorporate more fresh items. With a cross-sectional study, we must leave some of these questions to future research.
Importantly, this study did not follow people over time, so conceiving of phases of dietary change post-migration is only speculative. We cannot generalize the patterns identified here to migrants in the countries or even the cities where the research was conducted, as neither site involved representative sampling. To be population-representative, we would have needed lists of all refugees and asylum-seekers in each city, which are not available. We cannot know in what ways respondents were different from other refugees and asylum-seekers in the two cities. Additionally, the sample was small in each setting. Data collection in the U.S. had to be terminated early due to political changes that interrupted the activities of our community partner.
The sample size does not allow us to determine whether asylum-seekers, undocumented people, and refugees had worse diets or weight status than other migrants; this will be an important question for future research. Future studies would also gain new information by comparing these groups to native-born people living in the same communities. Also due to sample size, our measure of region of origin was not very specific. Using it, we did not find differences in dietary changes across regions of origin; this should be investigated with a larger sample.
The two settings offer contrasting examples of context of reception characteristics often discussed in the literature in terms of walkability, food environment, and population weight status. Still, two sites do not allow us to examine variability and specific environmental characteristics. A larger, multi-site study will be needed to answer questions about what components of the environment of reception are most relevant. Another limitation is that we did not collect direct anthropometric measurements, so we are relying on respondents’ assessments of their own weight and height; however, self-reported data are common, including in the often-cited nationally representative studies. These findings underscore the complexity of how the context of reception influences health-related behaviors after migration. While the food and built environment of the host country—such as the availability and affordability of food items—are crucial factors, other variables also play a significant role, for example whether a person is arriving alone or with family and the living environment and norms of their place of origin. Dietary change is likely a dynamic process, whereby newly arrived immigrants initially adopt processed foods - either because they are novel or more accessible - then gradually incorporate more fresh and healthful items as they become more familiar with their new environment and as their socioeconomic situation stabilizes.
This study highlights the importance of considering the long-term health trajectories of migrants, which are shaped by a combination of pre-migration experiences, the challenges faced during migration, and the environments of the host country. Future research should adopt a life-course perspective that takes into account these multiple stages of migration, as well as the health status of both the host and home country populations ( ; ). Understanding how these factors intersect to influence the emergence of chronic diseases among migrants can inform the development of more effective and targeted health interventions.

5.

## Conclusions
A tenet of research on migrant health is that the context of reception is the major driver of health post-migration. We found that there are some similarities and some differences in the health-related experiences of people resettling in two cities that are major migrant destinations with differing living environments. People who resettled in a U.S. city, which is by many measures a highly obesogenic environment, more frequently indicated that they were overweight or obese than those who resettled in a Belgian city. In both settings, respondent reported adopting new fast foods and junk foods. At the same time, there were some indications that people who resettled in the U.S. city also had healthful changes in diets, especially abandoning junk foods they used to eat in their country of origin.
The implications of these findings for migration policy and health services are significant. Health services in host countries should recognize that newly arrived immigrants might initially be at higher risk of adopting unhealthy dietary patterns due to limited access to familiar and healthful foods. Public health interventions could focus on providing culturally appropriate dietary guidance, improving access to fresh, affordable foods in immigrant communities and supporting community-based programs that help immigrants navigate their new food environments, such as orientation sessions that introduce them to local markets and affordable healthy food options. Addressing the health needs of migrants requires a multifaceted approach that considers the complex interplay between individual behaviors, cultural contexts, and the broader environments in which migrants resettle. By integrating these factors into public health strategies and policies, we can work towards reducing health inequalities and improving health outcomes for all members of society.
By identifying the health needs of migrants, policymakers and healthcare providers can better address the unique challenges faced by these populations. Additionally, the experiences of migrants in adapting to new environments can shed light on how social and built environments, intersect with individual health endowments to shape long-term health of migrant and non-migrant populations.
