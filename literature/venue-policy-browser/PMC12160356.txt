## Abstract

## Background
The United Nations Convention on the Rights of the Child affirms the human right of children to have their voices heard about issues affecting their lives. The One Chance to be a Child (One Chance) report provided an evidence-informed data profile of the well-being of children and youth in Nova Scotia (NS). To promote the report, we engaged youth from across the province in a knowledge mobilization (KMb) project. The purpose of this research is to outline the methods of the project, as well as the priority areas identified by youth.

## Methods
10 NS youth (grades 7–12) were recruited to take part in a three-phased KMb approach: (1) A sense-making workshop to learn and discuss the report, (2) The planning and delivery of a youth-led forum to engage decision-makers in dialogue around the report, and (3) A participatory data analysis workshop to identify priority areas from the report. Data were collected through audio-recordings, note-taking, and pictures of all materials.

## Results
Five priority areas were identified by youth: (1) Access to Care– high-quality care in a timely manner, (2) Community Care– inclusive community solutions, (3) Open Minded Education– school curricula that reflects their needs, (4) Quality of Life and Basic Needs– living wages and healthy workplace policies, and (5) Youth Empowerment– youth voice embedded throughout all actions.

## Conclusion
Engaging youth around the findings of the One Chance report supported their voices being heard, and their well-being needs to be considered by decision-makers.

## Plain language summary
Children and youth have a right to have their voices heard on issues affecting their well-being. The One Chance to be a Child (One Chance) report provided data on the well-being of children and youth in Nova Scotia, Canada. To promote the report, we engaged ten youth (grades 7–12) from across the province to be part of a Youth Leadership Team where they were invited to communicate the report to decision-makers and identify their main priorities from the report. The project was broken up into three phases. First, the youth engaged in a full-day workshop to learn about the One Chance report. Second, they planned, coordinated and implemented a youth-led forum to share information from the One Chance report to decision-makers. Third, they engaged in a participatory data analysis workshop to reflect on and prioritize the report findings based on their discussions in phases one and two. Five priority areas were identified and named by youth: (1) Access to Care, (2) Community Care, (3) Open Minded Education, (4) Quality of Life and Basic Needs, and (5) Youth Empowerment. Disseminating and prioritizing the One Chance report with youth supports having their voices heard, and their well-being needs considered.

## Background
On December 13th, 1991, Canada formally ratified the United Nations Convention on the Rights on the Child (UNCRC) and, with this, agreed to honor child and youth rights to have their voices heard and be active participants in their own lives (Article 12) [ ]. Despite this promise, the voices of children and youth often go unheard in matters that concern them and too often their basic needs go unmet [ ]. This is especially concerning given that children and youth constitute 20% of the Canadian population [ ] and hold perspectives and rights that uniquely differ from those of adults [ , ].
One critical aspect of children’s rights concerns their well-being. The definition of children’s well-being across the literature is inconsistent [ , ], yet can be conceptualized as multi-dimensional and consisting of areas impacting the child’s sense of self, their emotional life, and the relationships they have with others [ , ]. For instance, a systematic review by Pollard and Lee [ ] on the definition of child well-being found that there were five domains of child well-being: physical, psychological, cognitive, social, and economic. As there is no one definition of well-being and various elements of the concept overlapping and impacting one another, it is essential to adopt a holistic understanding of well-being rather than focusing on singular dimensions [ ].
To understand the complexity and multi-faceted layers of well-being as it particularly relates to children and youth, it is also essential to include the voices of young people and their subjective experiences in the process of defining it [ – ]. This requires those in positions of power to listen to child and youth voices about matters that affect their well-being. With children and school-aged youth unable to vote on the programs and policies that affect them, it is crucial that they are heard by policymakers and government officials in diverse ways. One such way is engaging children and youth in research opportunities. Engaging young people in participatory research can promote their rights and is correlated with improved well-being outcomes [ ]. Children and youth as young as the age of 7 [ ] can provide complex perspectives related to well-being that differ from those of the adults in their lives [ ]. The most effective way to understand their well-being is by providing children and youth a platform to articulate their concerns and express their needs so that adults in positions of authority have the opportunity to listen to their voices and consider implementing their recommendations [ ].
In the Canadian province of Nova Scotia, the One Chance to Be a Child report (hereafter referred to as One Chance), comprises a data profile released in April 2022, that sheds light on the experiences of Nova Scotia children and youth and the factors that affect their well-being [ ]. This profile was guided by questions from The Canadian Index of Child and Youth Well-being, a tool developed for and by youth that is used to measure and assess different indicators of their rights and well-being [ ]. The One Chance report found that growing up in a food-insecure household affects the well-being of many Nova Scotian youth, particularly those living in poverty [ ]. Beyond their homes, youth also experience inequities due to factors such as race, sexual orientation, gender identity, and ability [ ]. Bullying exacerbates these challenges, making many school environments feel unsafe for young people. This impedes the ability of youth to live healthy lives, affecting both their mental and physical health [ ]. Further, despite Nova Scotia’s diverse natural landscape, many young people still lack a meaningful connection with their physical environment. These findings underscore the importance of focusing on the needs of Nova Scotian children and youth through addressing the barriers to their overall well-being. This means involving young people in decision-making processes related to their health and well-being and ultimately bridging a critical knowledge-to-practice gap that exists between policy and the youth it affects. Thus, we engaged young people in a knowledge mobilization (KMb) project to (1) involve them in conversations to advance the findings of the One Chance report, and (2) support them to plan and deliver a youth-led forum with decision-makers on key priority areas that can move the One Chance report forward. The purpose of this paper is to outline the methods of this KMb project and describe the priority areas identified and developed by youth from the One Chance report.

## Methodology

## Research paradigm and theoretical positioning
This KMb project adopted a transformative paradigm such that the researchers involved recognized the inequities in power and privilege that shape the world and aimed to disrupt this through using research as a social tool for change. In practice, this involved relational approaches to research that placed the voice of vulnerable populations at the forefront with a focus on mitigating power dynamics and promoting collaborative practices [ ]. Specific to young people, this research is also grounded in critical youth theory that takes the position that young people have their own agency that is diminished through adult preconceptions and societal institutions (e.g., schools, universities) based on the premise of their developmental immaturity and traditional power structures [ , ]. The adult team members who initiated this manuscript, understand, and acknowledge the power and privilege that comes with our academic credentials and experiences and how they might impact the research conducted.

## Youth participatory research approach
To action these acknowledgements, we applied a youth participatory research approach to support meaningful engagement of youth throughout the KMb process. Youth participatory research is an umbrella term that encapsulates the involvement of youth in scientific inquiry to promote youth voice and power in the research context [ ]. This means involving youth as key partners in different stages of the research process such as informing the research questions and design, collectively engaging in data collection and analysis, and co-developing dissemination products [ ].
In the context of this project, we involved youth in different stages of the KMb process to develop and lead the dissemination of priority actions that were identified in the One Chance report. We also provided youth the opportunity to be authors and/or acknowledged on this manuscript, resulting in one youth being interested and meeting the criteria for authorship on the publication (and acknowledging that these criteria are typically adult-derived). To further support this process, our team also included a youth representative who was already engaged in KMb related to the One Chance report as a collaborator on the research grant that funded this work and had been engaged in dissemination of the report upon its release. A GRIPP2 (Guidance for Reporting Involvement of Patients and the Public) short form checklist [ ] can be viewed in Additional file to outline further how youth were meaningfully involved in different stages of the research process.
For this KMb project, we used an adapted version of the Future Workshops, a youth participatory research approach developed by Robert Jungk and outlined by Clausen et al. [ ]. The Future Workshops approach consists of three phases to aid children and youth to systemically envision the changes they want to see in the world. In the context of our project, this involved young people envisioning overall well-being of children and youth in Nova Scotia. The three phases were: (1) the critical phase, where youth participants expressed their perspectives on prevailing conditions of everyday life as it related to overall well-being, (2) the visionary phase, where youth participants engaged in a creative process of defining visions for overall well-being and (3) the realistic phase, where youth participants further developed their visions into realizable actions while considering the critique identified in the first phase.

## Recruitment & participants
Ten Nova Scotian youth (Ages 13–17 years, Grades 7–12) were selected to be part of the Youth Leadership Team (YLT), representing diversity with respect to gender, race/ethnicity, geographic location and lived experience. Convenience and snowball sampling [ ] were used to recruit the YLT members. Outreach was done through selectively emailing youth-serving organizations and networks across the province with recruitment and promotional information, as well as through word of mouth from other youth or their guardians who had heard about the project through these organizations. Interested youth or their guardians reached out to the project coordinator. Potential participants were then encouraged to fill out a questionnaire related to identities and interests, and their reasons for applying. Of the 31 applications received, ten participants were selected. Selection was made to ensure a representative group reflecting diverse demographics for age, grade, location, gender and race/ethnicity, as well as consideration to why they applied to be part of the KMb project. For example, youth whose reasons were viewed as having a more genuine interest in the project were more likely to be chosen, rather than youth who did not share their reasoning for their application to be part of the YLT. The group was limited to a small number of ten participants to align with qualitative data collection methods [ ] and budget, given our desire to ensure youth were compensated for participation. YLT members received a stipend for their participation in their role to recognize their contribution. This was $15 CAD/hour, just above the provincial minimum wage of $14.50 CAD/hour at the time of the project.

## Project methods and data analysis
We operationalized the Future Workshops approach through: (1) a sense-making workshop (phase 1), (2) planning and execution of a youth-led forum (phase 2), and (3) a participatory data analysis workshop (phase 3). Each phase was led by research team members who had experience in youth facilitation and engagement practices including navigating group dynamics, developing supportive environments for youth voices, promoting active participation, and encouraging communication and dialogue.

## Phase 1: sense-making workshop
Once the YLT was convened, we held a one-day in-person sense-making workshop at Dalhousie University. The purpose of the sense-making workshop was for the youth to learn, understand and interpret the One Chance report findings and to brainstorm how they would like to act on them. After greeting the participants and orienting them to the space and to one another, the workshop encompassed the critical phase, the visionary phase and parts of the realistic phase from the Future Workshops approach [ ]. We paired this with UNICEF Canada’s Youth Data Toolkit [ ], an adaptable and flexible resource that can help youth to understand and interpret data and bring it into action.

## Critical phase
The critical phase focused on youth having the space and time to express their perspectives on prevailing conditions of everyday life as it relates to overall well-being in Nova Scotia. In the context of the One Chance report, this consisted of the YLT first being introduced to the data using ‘data sparks’ and then understanding the data through a ‘data party’, terms coined by the UNICEF Canada’s Youth Data Toolkit [ ]. ‘Data sparks’ are data that are distilled into easy and quickly understood presentation formats for youth, such as brief data extracts from the six sections of the report, packaged in envelopes for youth to explore during a scavenger hunt. Youth were asked to gather 3–5 data sparks, independently review them, and then pair-up with their peers to discuss initial reactions and views. Youth then engaged in a ‘data party’ to further understand the data, which consisted of visually grouping the data into the six categories of the One Chance report - are we secure? are we learning? are we healthy? are we happy? are we connected to the environment? are we protected? - and participating in a full group, guided discussion to further explore the data and interconnection between the groupings. Discussion questions included: What was your initial reaction to the data? What was most surprising? What was missing? A graphic artist captured these discussions in real-time (Fig.  ).
 Youth then engaged in ‘dot voting’ [ ], where they were provided five stickers and asked to place a sticker on either a ‘data spark’ or group of interest they wanted to explore further. Based on the areas most prioritized by youth, three small groups were convened to further explore the data through facilitated discussion led by the research team members. Figure  is a collage of the data party activity.
 

## Visionary phase
In the afternoon of the workshop, youth were oriented to the visionary phase of the project where they engaged in a creative process of ‘meaning making’ to visualize ideas or concepts that connected to the survey data [ ]. Here, the YLT envisioned their ideal province as it related to overall well-being based on the data and key concepts learned and discussed thus far. Youth sat in a circle and shared their ideas, discussed their decisions and any connections they saw between each other’s ideals while the graphic artist summarized their ideas in real time (see Fig.  ).
 

## Realistic phase
Lastly, youth began to narrow their focus to develop their visions for change in the province to achievable actions, while considering the critiques and challenges discussed throughout the day. This phase aimed to be a ‘jumping-off’ point to further planning discussions on what priority areas from the One Chance report were to be discussed at the youth-led forum, as well as discussions on the resources and appropriate timeline needed for the event logistics (see Planning and Implementing of Youth-Led Forum section). Youth were asked to engage in an activity called ‘choosing a focus for action’ [ ] by working in three groups to come up with their five main areas of concerns related to their ideal province, as well as potential solutions pertaining to their challenges, and recording these on sticky notes. Youth then prioritized their sticky notes on a sorting grid that comprised sections for: (1) High Importance, High Urgency, (2) High Importance, Low Urgency, (3) Low Importance, High Urgency, (4) Low Importance, Low Urgency.
The sense-making workshop concluded with next steps for the project that related to planning and implementing a youth-led form to discuss priority areas identified by youth from the One Chance report. The team also briefly began to brainstorm the logistics of the youth-led forum including the location, attendees, presenters, and take-away messages.

## Phase 2: planning and execution of a youth-led forum

## Critical phase continued
The critical phase continued after the sense-making workshop as youth continued to work together over a two-month period (August– September 2023) to plan the youth-led forum. The planning stage involved five virtual meetings ranging from one to two hours over Microsoft Teams or Zoom. Research team members facilitated the meetings using digital flipcharts and at least one team member was always in attendance to host the meetings. The number of youth that participated in the online meetings varied each week depending on other commitments. The meetings involved re-introducing the YLT to each other, debriefing the sense-making workshop, deciding on the scope, purpose, and objectives of the youth-led forum, planning the logistics, content, and format and deciding roles and responsibilities for executing the forum. More detailed descriptions of what each meeting involved are provided in Table  .
 

## Youth-led forum
The youth-led forum took place online using Zoom, on September 13th, 2023, between 6 pm– 8 pm. All ten members of the YLT participated and took on differing roles and responsibilities such as moderating the session, presenting on priority topics or supporting the format. Decision-makers in attendance comprised individuals from local health authorities (Nova Scotia Health/IWK Health) (n = 7), government personnel (n = 7), non-profit associations (n = 9) and private industry (n = 1). The youth began by welcoming their audience to the forum and engaging them in an interactive activity of Jeopardy using data from the One Chance report to spark discussion. The activity was followed by five presentations on the topics related to child and youth well-being in Nova Scotia that were prioritized by the youth to advance the One Chance report. Youth gathered information on their chosen topics in pairs based on the One Chance report, their lived experiences and searching and gathering open access information related to the topics through websites, online reports, and research articles. They then synthesized the information into a PowerPoint presentation that included references for their sources. The presentation topics included access to basic needs (e.g. clean water, menstrual products), housing, living wages, mental health and substance use and climate change issues. All five presentations gave an overview of the topic, opportunities, challenges and potential solutions and changes they’d like to see decision-makers act on. Following the presentations the youth held a mini panel and question-and-answer section, where they answered some preset questions and took questions from the audience. During this portion, they advocated for continued use of youth voice and offered further solutions to the topics they discussed.

## Data collection and management
All materials developed by or with the YLT were considered data. This included flipcharts, sticky notes, digital flipcharts, and digital recordings of any visioning activities, debrief sessions or planning meetings that were part of the process during phase 1 (sense-making workshop) and phase 2 (planning and execution of youth-led forum). We also included supplementary notes taken by student research assistants as data. All hand-printed materials (e.g. flipcharts, sticky notes) were photographed and all-digital recordings were transcribed verbatim using Otter.AI and reviewed by student research assistants. Data were then saved on a secure, private OneDrive folder at the author’s university institution and imported into Nvivo Qualitative Analysis Software for data management.

## Phase 3: participatory data analysis workshop
We engaged youth who were interested in a participatory data analysis workshop to develop a comprehensive summary of the priority areas identified by youth across each stage of the KMb project. This workshop was adapted from a guide by Liebenberg et al. [ ] for conducting thematic analysis with youth. This guide applies the six-steps outlined by Braun and Clarke [ ]: [ ] Familiarizing yourself with your data [ ], Generating initial codes [ ], Searching for themes [ ], Reviewing themes [ ], Defining and naming themes, and [ ] Producing the report, adapted to form a more participatory approach that is appropriate and applicable to engaging youth within the research process. We acknowledge that Braun and Clarke have provided further iterations and clarification on this approach since the inaugural 2006 paper [ ], and believe these overarching steps work well to structure a data analysis workshop with youth. Prior to step-one (Familiarizing yourself with your data), Liebenberg et al. [ ] suggest conducting a ‘pre-analysis’ to reduce and distill the data before it goes to participants due to different factors such as time, literacy, and confidentiality constraints. As our primary data were raw transcripts, a student research assistant conducted preliminary directed content analysis [ ] using a qualitative descriptive approach [ ] to code data into similar segments prior to the workshop. A portion of data from each code that was considered to represent the larger sum of data was chosen to be reviewed by the youth during the participatory data analysis workshop.
The data analysis workshop was held on Saturday, April 13, 2024 at Dalhousie University, and six of the ten participants were interested and attended. The analysis approach was broken down into five steps:

## Step 1) familiarizing the youth with the data
Similar to the sense-making workshop (phase 1) youth were re-familiarized with the data through reviewing ‘data sparks’ but this time it was short excerpts of their own data from phases 1 and 2. Each youth were provided ten ‘data sparks’ to review and asked to group the ‘data sparks’ under one of the six categories outlined by the One Chance report: Are we Secure? Are we Learning? Are we Healthy? Are we Happy? Are we Connected to the Environment? Are we Protected? that were sectioned off across a large whiteboard. Youth were then asked to slowly go around and review the ‘data sparks’ in each category to re-familiarize themselves with the data they generated. As they were reviewing, they were asked to write out any common term or phrases they identified from the ‘data sparks’ under each of the six categories. Each of the six youth took turns adding to each of the categories and then rotated to the next one until they all had a chance to review and add their initial thoughts. Youth were then brought back to a wider group and each youth took a turn sharing back to the group what the initial thoughts, words and phrases for each of the six categories were (Fig.  ).
 

## Step 2) generating initial codes
Youth were then introduced to the concept of codes and asked to go around each of the six categories across the room and collect any ‘data’ sparks’ again, shuffle them and then deal them out equally to participants. Youth were instructed to engage in an adapted version of the card game ‘snap’ whereby they were asked to pair ‘data sparks’ that had similar segments to each other to begin to develop initial codes. In practice, this involved one youth first reading off a ‘data spark’ and youth who believed they had a related ‘data spark’ would place their excerpt into the pile as well. The activity continued with each youth having the opportunity to go first to add an excerpt and others adding similar responses to the pile until all ‘data sparks’ were gone. Youth then reviewed all developed piles to ensure they were satisfied with the placement of their ‘data spark’ or had the option to place their ‘data spark’ into another category. Initial codes were then developed for each category on sticky notes. Youth then taped their ‘data sparks’ on the whiteboard separated into associated codes.

## Step 3) searching for themes
Once a list of comprehensive codes were generated, youth were then introduced to the concept of themes. To help with theme development, youth were instructed to develop a ‘thematic’ map of the codes to identify over-arching patterns of meaning in the data and visualize their process. Participants were provided a large sheet of paper with a sketch out of the province of Nova Scotia and a pile of markers, colored pencils, pens, colored paper and scissors. They were asked to draw out areas or features of the province that were health promoting or health disrupting, related to the codes previously identified (Fig.  ). After a 20-minute period, youth shared back their maps with the group while others were asked to reflect on similar patterns of meaning across the maps and write these out on sticky notes. After each person finished sharing, a larger group discussion took place to determine what themes youth were developing based on the commonalities across the thematic maps, affiliated codes and ‘data sparks’.
 

## Step 4) reviewing, defining and naming themes
Once we had a list of preliminary themes, it was important to first determine whether the themes accurately reflected the codes developed and ‘data sparks’ reviewed. For this process, youth were asked to place their preliminary themes on the white board where the codes and ‘data sparks’ were already grouped. Youth were asked to review and re-arrange codes and ‘data sparks’ to ensure they fit within the preliminary themes developed. At this point themes were still not fully developed and more review of the core underlying concept for each theme was needed. As such, youth were invited to review the relationships between codes and themes by using different colored markers to link codes and themes together and identify a correlational relationship, causal relationship, contradictory relationship or a relationship of tension between themes. Youth identified correlational and potential causal relationships but did not identify any tensions or contradictions in their preliminary theme groups. Thus, overarching themes that encompassed overlapping relationships between preliminary themes were further developed. As a result, five themes were identified and defined that were considered the main priorities areas for youth related to the One Chance report.
Liebenberg et al. [ ] suggest that additional anecdotes shared by youth as data can contribute to contextualizing the data, and therefore the data from the workshop was also digitally recorded and managed using the same approach as the sense-making workshop (phase 1). Further, to help with organization of the themes developed by youth, all paper material including ‘data sparks’ and affiliated sticky notes with codes and theme names were taken down from the whiteboard and organized into folders. Each folder was given the corresponding theme name it was affiliated with and provided to a student research assistant to digitally transfer this information into Nvivo Data Analysis Software. They did this by developing theme names on Nvivo then re-organizing the ‘data sparks’ into these assigned groupings. Any other data from phase 1 and 2 that were not part of the ‘data sparks’ coded and themed by youth were then deductively analyzed by a student research assistant and grouped into the corresponding themes to complete analysis.

## Step 5) producing the report
The synthesis of the findings from the analyses was led by the adult research team including the summary of each theme with examples of theme concepts, along with selected extracts and quotes to connect each finding back to the analysis, literature and main research question. Although it would be advantageous for youth to have played a larger role in writing up of the final findings this was constrained by resources, time, and reporting and accountability requirements of the funding that was ultimately the responsibility of the adult research team members. As previously mentioned, to help ensure youth had a voice in the final report, they were invited as co-authors and one youth who was interested then reviewed, edited and provided feedback on this manuscript. The youth provided positive overall feedback on the structure and flow of manuscript, as well as provided more detailed edits on specific sections.

## Ethics
This project was approved by the adult authors’ institutional ethics board. Prior to the sense-making workshop, youth and their parent/guardian reviewed and signed the consent form that also gave them the opportunity to consent to having their name publicly credited as either a co-author or acknowledged in future reports or publications if desired.

## Results
Five themes were developed that outlined the priority areas identified by the youth to advance the One Chance report . The themes were: (1) access to care, (2) community care, (3) open minded education, (4) quality of life and basic needs, and (5) youth empowerment.

## Theme 1: access to care
A topic of priority for youth was health care, or more specifically, access to quality health care within a reasonable timeline. Youth recognized the benefits of Nova Scotia’s universal health care system, while naming its challenges. One youth stated “the problem with healthcare is, I think we have the best of the bad systems. You know, it’s free healthcare, which is great. But it also makes wait times awful…”. This lack of accessibility was also reflected through concerns about the financial burden of items like menstrual products. The youth agreed that you should not have to pay for things you cannot control and noted that “menstrual products are not a luxury”. There was a strong desire among the participants that improvements should be made to promote health and well-being on a larger scale.
It wasn’t just physical health the youth were concerned about—it was mental health too. More and more young people are speaking up about their struggles with mental health and the need for better support, and the YLT participants reflected this sentiment. Youth wanted to see more resources allocated to help everyone get the support they needed to feel better. One youth noted they would like to see “more access to psychologists and psychiatrists…they’re hard to get [to]… like the wait time is years”. This topic was classified as a high importance, high urgency issue by the youth. Ensuring people had access to appropriate resources was felt to be necessary for young people to grow into healthy adults and to support generations to come.

## Theme 2: community care
Youth expressed a feeling of urgency towards community care– their overarching term to encompass good citizenship. This was grounded in equity principles with the mindset that, “we all deserve equity and to be treated fairly, not necessarily equal”. This was expressed as looking out for one another in ways that went far beyond personal interactions but also involved fostering an equitable physical and social environments that helped the community thrive.
Physical environment examples included the need for improved infrastructure and resources to create healthier communities such as clean drinking water in Indigenous communities, environmentally responsible energy options, and respect for the natural world. Specifically, the youth expressed a strong desire for sustainable energy options, understanding that these options were limited for them when it comes to climate change. They were aware of the reality of the resources available in Nova Scotia stating “Quebec and Ontario, they use the Great Lakes and the waterfalls there and they produce basically all their electricity. Now here, we’re mostly off the coal still, because that’s what we have in the province, is coal from Cape Breton”. The youth were therefore fully cognizant that the physical environments in which they were living and growing had a direct impact on their health and well-being.
Equally important to the vision of community well-being was the perspective of youth around the social environment including the promotion of kindness and inclusivity as well as safe places for play. For example, some youth shared contrasting experiences of being a part of the 2SLGBTQIA+ community: one told the group how they were actively targeted for their openness, while another described how they weere told they don’t “look different” and therefore couldn’t have the same experience as others who identify as part of the community. These conversations illustrated a desire for empathy and acceptance within the communities these youth were part of. These shared experiences thus emphasized that community care involves a focus on both physical infrastructure, and social support.

## Theme 3: open minded education
Open minded education was a theme that stemmed from a desire for the traditional school curriculum and policies to align more with the current wants and needs of youth. Schooling and education were popular topics among the youth, with the group commonly expressing the struggles and barriers they faced every day. They did not feel that school was a place where they were fully safe, happy or healthy. These youth had experienced changes in schooling during the COVID-19 pandemic, and did not feel that they were returning to an education system that had adapted to their needs. One youth said school felt stagnant post-pandemic, as if they were just doing busy work to fill up time. Another noted that important topics, such as Canadian history, were skipped over due to their sensitive nature. The youth disagreed with this choice, telling each other that “Yeah, racism is sensitive, but it’s worth talking about. Because [if] we don’t talk about it, you won’t learn about it. You don’t learn about [it], how can you stop it?”.
Further, bullying and hate speech that could escalate into violence was felt to be common in Nova Scotian schools, according to these young people. Suspensions, once believed to be a behavioral deterrent, were seen by some of the youth as akin to “a vacation”. The victim of the bullying often became a target once again if a suspended youth returned with a plan for revenge on the “snitch”. The desire of youth for adequate support and consequences was evident. As shared by one young person, “So the punishment is just, it’s a vacation for them. Not to mention, if that’s all it is, they’re just gonna be worse when they get back because they know who ratted on them”. The youth were adamant that consequences for bullying or violence in school needed to be strengthened, not only to discourage repeated behaviors, but to protect the youth who advocated for themselves.
The youth also felt that some teachers and staff efforts and resources were spread too thin, which affected their access to learning. As one youth shared, “it’s harder to adjust your [teacher’s] teaching style to 40 kids rather than 20 or 15. Right? You can help everybody learn in a 15-person class. But in a 40-person class, they’re going to have to come to you.” Larger classes sizes, with minimal resources were felt to have impacted the quality of education and inhibited the capacity of teachers and staff to implement novel education topics and innovative ways of learning beyond the traditional formats.

## Theme 4: quality of life and basic needs
The youth shared examples of what they considered to be a declining quality of life, especially in areas such as housing and employment. Young people were aware of the growing economic pressures and the burden the economy placed on the health and well-being of Nova Scotian children and youth. One young person noted,
We should have more laws relating to what house pricing can be…Restricting property owners and people who are renting as well as the whole real estate system, because right now they’re really taking advantage of it. And they’re just upping the prices of housing, which is making it unaffordable for the average salary. You can also up minimum wage, but then they’ll just push the house prices up more.
This quote illustrates a push-and-pull that the participating youth felt challenged to be financially secure and created a pressure in how they will finance their future if these trends continue. It is well recognized that there is a housing crisis in Nova Scotia, and elsewhere, and these young people feared the impact of this if no action was taken to provide affordable housing or a living wage.
Diving deeper into the employment landscape, youth dismissed the notion of young people being unwilling to work as a misconception. Rather, they clarified, “…people don’t want to work minimum wage jobs to be treated like less than human.” They requested fair compensation and dignified treatment in the workplace, rejecting exploitative labor practices. These sentiments reflect a broader call for systemic change to address the root causes of economic inequality and social injustices. Young people felt passionately about advocating for policies that prioritized affordable housing, fair wages, and respectful working conditions. Their perspectives highlighted the urgency of creating a more equitable society to improve overall quality of life of youth. At the youth-led forum, one youth shared, “I feel like one of the reasons why kids use vapes and drugs, I feel like they see that as an escape. I don’t know about you guys, but like, every morning, I like wake up and go to school. Like, I don’t know, I just feel like, tired”. This story sheds light on how youth felt their mental health was suffering, and they were not sure how to cope with these pressures. The strain that these young people described, coupled with a perceived lack of access to mental health support (see access to care theme) was considered to be a barrier for young people to live a high quality of life.

## Theme 5: youth empowerment
The youth who attended the workshop were a vibrant group of young people who had ideas and opinions that predated the publication of One Chance report. While discussing their health and well-being they expressed varied opinions and proposed multiple ideas about how to improve their lives and the lives of those around them. The young people worked collaboratively, often online, to plan the youth-led forum where they shared their ideas and opinions. During each stage of the project, they shared their needs openly with each other so that they could work in a respectful and safe environment. As one participant put it “we’re understanding more about ourselves and understanding that, hey, it’s okay to have feelings”. They created a welcoming space where they were able to come together as strangers and unite to spark changes that would benefit them and their communities. The young people, armed with knowledge and adult support, were logical and intentional about how they wanted to present their thoughts. One participant stated, “obviously, change is what we’re trying to achieve, but we need to actually like lay out what we’re looking for”. This statement, said during a group conversation, is a glimpse into the motivation and rationale that they shared as they planned the youth-led forum.
The youth also shared that they understood their opinions mattered and that their voices should be heard. This group of young people had ideas to meaningfully contribute to discussions and decisions that directly impacted their health and well-being and felt the desire to share them. When asked what they wanted to give to this KMb project, one young person said, “One thing I hope to give is my opinion on pressing issues and to cause some type of positive change”. When given opportunities to participate in conversations with decision-makers, these individuals not only shared their perspectives, but also gained a deeper understanding of the issues affecting the health of their communities, as seen in the youth-led forum. When their opinions and ideas were respected and considered, it validated their experiences. When they discussed having a chance to voice their opinions about health and well-being at school, one youth described the value in being consulted, stating “People actually hear our voices, saying ‘hey, we need this to learn’. And people actually listen to that, and [it] makes you feel happy because your voice is being heard”. This illustrates how having an open dialogue with young people encourages them to continue to advocate for their needs.

## Discussion
The One Chance report provided an evidence-informed snapshot of well-being among children and youth in Nova Scotia, Canada [ ]. Through this KMb project, we engaged youth to support dissemination and promotion of the findings of the report. In this paper, we described a three-phase process that involved engaging ten youth in sense-making, planning and implementation of a youth-led forum, as well as participating in a participatory data analysis workshop to identify priority areas to improve child and youth well-being in Nova Scotia, Canada based on the report. Youth priorities were further developed into five themes: (1) access to care, (2) community care, (3) open minded education (4) quality of life and basic needs, and (5) youth empowerment.

## Value of engaging youth in research on well-being
To promote the commitment for children’s rights set out by the UNCRC [ ], we sought to meaningfully engage young people in critical reflection, discussion, planning and analysis. We moved beyond diffusion strategies typically undertaken as the starting point for the dissemination of findings from reports of this nature [ ] towards embedding youth voice within the KMb process as the messengers for change and to identify priority areas. A shift towards engaging youth within the research process has gained rapid traction over the years as researchers and practitioners alike acknowledge that children and youth are intellectually capable of engaging in the practice of inquiry and are active agents rather than passive receipts in constructing their own lives [ ]. Building on this ideology is the overarching recognition that meaningfully involving knowledge users, in our case Nova Scotian youth, within the KMb process can ensure that research is more relevant and applicable to the target audience [ ]. This means moving beyond opinion gathering or consultation with youth and instead meaningfully involving them in the decision-making processes to actively shape the research findings [ ]. As it relates to child and youth well-being, this is particularly important as evidence indicates that when you provide youth time for discussion and reflection on their perceptions of well-being, their definitions are broad and less behavior oriented [ , , ]. Within this process, youth prioritized social factors for well-being beyond individual behaviors to encompass housing, access to services, community engagement, and environmental impacts on health. Further, Fattore [ ] suggests that this form of research requires an epistemological shift from more positivistic approaches found in previous well-being research towards focusing on methods that place children centrally to understand their perspectives on well-being. Within our project, this translated into the use of a youth participatory approach. By doing so, our research aligns with other work using a youth participatory approach such that it had pedagogical connotations and functioned as an educational process for youth to become investigators into the social issues affecting their lives [ ]. Through this approach to research, youth had the opportunity to gain ownership over their data, feel empowered, build critical consciousness [ ] in parallel to producing applicable and relevant findings with youth for youth.

## Contribution to the literature
This project worked in collaboration with youth to advance the findings from the One Chance report and prioritize their issues of concern as they related to child and youth well-being in Nova Scotia. Their priorities ranged in breadth, focusing more on social and environmental factors that impact their well-being than on individual indicators. Although this overarching finding could be argued to be driven by the sections of the One Chance report, these sections were guided by the nine indicators of youth well-being from the Canadian Index of Child and Youth Well-being [ ] that incorporated both individual indicators (e.g. Are we happy? Are we healthy? ) and social indicators (e.g. Do we belong? Are we secure? ) of well-being (and the development of the One Chance report was also informed by a different group of youth). It was the youth involved in this KMb project who prioritized social and environmental factors over individualistic ideologies of well-being. Specifically, the youth placed emphasis on the impact of different health inequities within their social and physical settings that impacted their well-being such as within their communities, their schools, their work and housing situations. The focus on the importance and urgency of these environmental and systemic issues is aligned with the settings approach to health, first promoted in the Ottawa Charter for Health Promotion that outlines how the settings we live, learn and play in critically impact our health [ ] and further advocated through the Geneva Charter for Well-being [ ]. These findings also reinforce what we know but do not always action– well-being is a complex concept that is dynamic, multi-dimensional and is made up of individual, social and environmental factors [ , ]. Pollard and Lee [ ] further state that well-being is on a continuum that encompasses both negative and positive experiences. This was particularly highlighted in our findings that encompassed all facets of well-being and did not solely focus on positive experiences.
As already alluded to, youth placed emphasis on the importance of how their community impacted their well-being through the relationships they had with others as well as the quality of their environment, such as the built and natural environments. Specifically, they were aware of pressing environmental issues that critically impact youth well-being, including access to clean drinking water and a lack of sustainable energy solutions in the province. Prior research also emphasizes the impact the economic landscape has on youth’s conception of their safety and security. Of note, Fattore [ ] described the development of well-being indicators for the New South Wales Commission for Children and Young People where youth identified economic factors such as security, material resources, and stable housing as impacting their well-being. These factors align with our findings in that youth related well-being to housing costs, basic needs (e.g., clean water or affordable menstrual products), job security and a living wage. Interestingly, previous work places more emphasis on security in relation to family income and support for a good upbringing [ ] while the youth in the current project spoke to the importance of security as it pertains to their future job and employment stability.
The impact of school settings on well-being is well-established in the literature [ , ] and was a point of discussion in the current project. Youth focused on the need for adaptations to the curriculum suggesting that alongside updates to well-being education (e.g. sex education, mental health), there was a need for more open-minded educators, and the curriculum needs to focus on issues that are of importance to youth rather minimizing conversations that adults might view as ‘sensitive’. The UNICEF report [ ] reiterates the need for education to be more democratic and modernized suggesting that “kids of Canada are learning at school, but they aren’t necessarily learning for life” (pg. 6). This notion may indicate that subjects and approaches to teaching that put youth interest at the center may not be fully implemented in the Canadian school system. Similarly, in the UK, Coome et al. [ ] examined the views of young people about their emotional health and well-being in secondary education and found that specific mental health topics of interest to youth were often neglected, specifically in relation to self-harm.
Lastly, centering youth voice goes beyond the school setting and is one of the core factors that youth prioritized to enhance their well-being in Nova Scotia. Although individual characteristics of well-being were not highlighted to the same degree as social and environmental factors, prioritizing youth voice was an exception such that youth’s own perception of their autonomy was essential for a sense of well-being and empowerment. The intertwining of well-being and empowerment is well-articulated in the literature [ , ], as well-being is described by Anderson and Graham [ ] as “ having a say, being listened to, having rights, and being respected” (pg. 348). However, a sense of value, autonomy and respect for youth is not independent of the environmental factors previously prioritized such that fostering of relationships, security, and well-resourced environments are all related to the perception that youth hold of themselves and their ability to have agency within their community.
In summary, these findings highlight the interrelation of the individual, social and environmental domains that impact well-being with the youth in this project prioritizing specific factors within these domains that are of high importance to youth in Nova Scotia, Canada.

## Implications for policy, practice and research
We hope that this work will advance and mobilize child and youth well-being in Nova Scotia and elsewhere, specifically through the provision of first-person experience of what is important to youth. Listening to what is important to youth is critical to make authentic and sustainable change over time. The youth-led forum held in 2023 engaged decision-makers on key priority areas to advance the One Chance report. Further, this project aligns with the priority areas of the 2024–2025 Nova Scotia Budget that make promises to move forward certain areas that impact youth well-being [ ]. One specific example is a pledge to establish a Child and Youth Commission for the province, although the timeline for this independent body is unknown, and no funding is currently tied to the development [ ]. Moreover, Nova Scotia remains one of two provinces currently in Canada that does not have such an entity, directly and persistently contradicting the ratification of the UNCRC [ ]. Other Budget areas include specific investment in housing, cost-of-living and access to health care; all areas specifically prioritized by youth. This work could be used directly by decision-makers to understand concerns of youth within Nova Scotia and provide youth-focused opportunities to address them.
Further, this project adds to the growing body of research centering youth voice and engaging youth as changemakers within the scientific process. This project provides a detailed overview of how youth can be meaningfully engaged in understanding, analyzing and interpretating data to develop prioritized areas of focus related to their well-being. This research will help advance scientific inquiry and direction of thought to be more aligned with the new sociology of childhood [ ] that recognizes that children and youth are active agents in constructing their own lives, and are persons rather than subjects of social concern.
Lastly, engaging youth as knowledge mobilizers in the research project advances our understanding of what well-being is to them, thereby contributing to the growing dialogue that well-being is beyond individualist concepts of oneself and is intertwined with elements of the social and physical environments that impact our sense of being and living well.

## Strengths and limitations
A major strength of this KMb project is the active and intentional engagement of youth across the project using a youth participatory approach to ensure their voices were at the centre of the work to advance the findings from the One Chance report. The centering of youth voice ensures the work conducted and research produced is more applicable, relevant and credible to the needs of the target population. A youth participatory approach also adds a pedagogical component, providing an opportunity for youth to build competencies through engaging in critical dialogue around issues affecting their lives and build new skills through being exposed to research and decision-making making processes.
There are also some limitations to the work. First, rightfully, the One Chance report is a lengthy document with an abundance of data on child and youth well-being in Nova Scotia. A one-day sense-making workshop to learn, interpret and promote the findings from the report cannot do justice to the wealth of data included. We sought to mitigate this by providing the report to the youth prior for review, as well as breaking down the key points of the report into ‘data sparks’ as outlined in the Methods section.
Second, it was difficult to engage decision-makers from across government, research, and non-profit entities to attend the youth-led forum; therefore, the forum was moved online to mitigate issues related to accessibility. This lack of decision-maker engagement can, of course, send an important yet contradictory message to young people - that their voices don’t actually matter that much, if time cannot be dedicated for engaging in these activities. Professional development opportunities to build adult competencies to recognize the importance of youth voice have shown promise in adapting beliefs and attitudes [ ] yet it was out of the scope and resources of this study to have these opportunities offered to attendees prior to the youth-led forum.
Third, the research and KMb process can be lengthy as ethical issues and methodological rigor are important components that are often not always youth-friendly. The different lengths of time between the youth sense-making (phase 1), youth-led forum (phase 2), the participatory data analysis workshop (phase 3) alongside the drafting of the manuscript, made it challenging to maintain youth engagement across different timepoints. Although engagement was still strong, in future work we will be more intentional in providing updates and check-ins across the process to maintain engagement. There is also a need to improve and adapt research processes to reflect more youth friendly practices and timelines. For example, in retrospect the ‘data sparks’ created could have been edited to be more plain language, and a tighter timeline between stages could support greater engagement across the full process.

## Conclusion
In Nova Scotia, Canada and across the globe, youth voices are often under-represented in crucial discussions that shape their futures. This KMb project meaningfully engaged youth in reflecting, discussing, and promoting issues related to their well-being. By doing so, we worked to bridge the gap between youth and decision-makers in Nova Scotia, by having youth prioritize findings from the One Chance report and share these with interested decision-makers. This project helped to advance and mobilize findings from the One Chance report, and in turn provided youth the opportunity to be agents of change in issues affecting their own lives.
