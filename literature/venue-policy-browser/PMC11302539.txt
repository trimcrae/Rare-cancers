## Background:
Sustainable development goals (SDGs) 3.9.1 and 11.6.2 call for a reduction in deaths and illnesses from air pollution, improving the air quality of cities. The above goals motivate us to organize workshops to improve the health of traffic police, who bear the brunt of air pollution. The paper examines the effect of workshops on the health-seeking behavior of the traffic police in Bhubaneswar city.

## Methods:
The study conducted two workshops as a quasi-experimental, single-group study at an academic institution in Bhubaneswar. It included 20 traffic police officers (11 male and 9 female). The Kirkpatrick 4-level model was used to assess the effectiveness of the workshops. A paired t-test was used to compare pre- and postworkshop scores.

## Results:
Thirteen traffic police officers rated the workshop sessions as excellent. The score before the workshop ranged from zero to three, with a mean (standard deviation [SD]) of 2.81 (1.0). The postworkshop score had a minimum to maximum score of three to five with a mean (SD) of 4.41 (0.7) (P < 0.005). The effect size dcohen (confidence interval [CI]) was 1.87 (3.27–4.71). The mean (SD) of absolute and relative gain was 1.6 (1.0) and 0.93 (1.02), respectively. All 20 traffic police officers showed improvement in health-seeking behavior. The significant lifestyle changes after the workshops ranged between 5% and 75%.

## Conclusion:
The test scores indicated statistically significant improvement as the P value recorded was smaller than 0.05. This concludes that the improvement in understanding of the session was statistically significant because of the training imparted.

## INTRODUCTION
Air pollution, a growing environmental concern in India, has been having an increasingly adverse impact on public health. According to a study reported by UrbanEmissions.info in 2015,[ ] Bhubaneswar ranks the highest in particulate matter (PM2.5) contribution attributed to transportation. This poses a major health risk for the city’s traffic police, as their job necessitates constant exposure to such high-risk environments.
Traffic police play a crucial role in city management. They not only enforce traffic laws and ensure road safety for all but also contribute toward the efficient movement of vehicular traffic on public roads. While these public officers play an important role in society, they have not received a great deal of attention in academic research. Very few studies have explored the problems faced by the traffic police and investigated the socio-ecological dimensions affecting their work and life.[ ] Of all the challenges faced by traffic police, health and well-being are the most flagrant challenge.[ ] By the very nature of their work, officers in traffic police are exposed to several health hazards. These dangers include things like polluted air, noisy environments, exposure to harsh weather, lengthy workdays, and stress.[ ] It is important that research be focused on these aspects related specifically to the traffic police. The health of traffic police officers is part of the entire healthcare ecosystem, and it should be given its due value both in academic research as well as in practical outcomes for a better and caring society.
By bringing together healthcare experts, recipients, and stakeholders to jointly recognize and solve healthcare concerns, workshops may be an effective tool for healthcare improvement. Scholarly works have documented the impact of health management workshops on enhancing the health-seeking behavior of traffic police.[ ] These programs are instrumental in increasing awareness of healthy living. Workshops educate police officers on health-related topics and encourage police officers to prioritize their health.[ ] Based on the qualitative and quantitative evaluation of the workshops/training programs, policymakers and law enforcement organizations may think about taking action to lessen the risks and safeguard the health and safety of traffic police.

## METHODS
The study was conducted at XIM University, where two workshops were organized for the traffic police on ground duty in the city of Bhubaneswar, India. The approval of the Ethics Committee of XIM University (headed by the university vice-chancellor) was obtained for publishing the research based on the findings of the workshops. The traffic wing Bhubaneswar at the time of the workshops in the year 2022 had the following workforce strength: total constable – 270 (male 219, female 51); total sub-inspector (SI) –14; assistant commissioner of police – 01.
The workshops were conducted twice within a span of 3 months (July and September 2022) and understood the pre- and post-improvement in health care through outcome indicators. The study made use of a quasi-experimental, single-group, pre- and postworkshop design to evaluate the effectiveness of the workshop sessions in enhancing the health-seeking behavior of the traffic police officers. The number of traffic police officers who participated in the study was 20. To determine if there was a significant improvement in the participants’ knowledge and confidence in managing overall health and improving health-seeking behavior after the workshops, this study used a paired sample t-test. The null hypothesis is that there is no significant difference between the pre-workshop and postworkshop scores, and the alternative hypothesis is that there is a significant difference. The study utilizes the Kirkpatrick model[ ] to examine the effectiveness of preventive health workshops on the health and well-being of traffic police. The model has four levels of evaluation: reaction, learning, behavior, and results. The model provides a comprehensive approach to assessing the effectiveness of training programs at different levels, from participants’ satisfaction with the program to its impact on patient outcomes and healthcare costs.
Level 1: The satisfaction levels of workshop participants were assessed using a 5-point Likert scale, including excellent, very good, good, fair, and poor. Participants’ satisfaction was graded as follows: excellent for 5, very good for 4, good for 3, fair for 2, and poor for 1. The effectiveness of sessions was evaluated using six criteria: content, resource person, relevance of topics, use of the workshop experience in the future, whether sufficient time was allotted for the workshop sessions, and workshop venue and hospitality.
Level 2: Prior to the commencement of the academic sessions, a questionnaire was used to evaluate participants’ understanding of preventive healthcare management. A postworkshop evaluation was completed and the test questions similar to the pre-workshop evaluation, were included. The amount of information gained (learning gain) was calculated using pre- and postworkshop evaluation results. Scores for each segment and the overall score were computed. The formula dcohen (d = mean score post − mean score pre/SD) was used to determine the effect magnitude.
Level 3: The practical application of health-seeking behavior was evaluated through focus group discussions while documenting health impact stories.
Level 4: The overall impact of the preventive healthcare management workshops was assessed based on the changes in health-seeking behavior of the respondents. The changes were documented through self-assessment tests and focused group discussions.

## RESULTS
The research included 20 traffic officers. Office of the Commissioner of Police, Bhubaneswar, agreed to relieve 22 traffic police, one SI, and one assistant commissioner of police (ACP) to participate in both the workshops. However, the percentage of traffic police participation is about 10%, which constitutes a significant number to be relieved from critical operations of traffic management. In the first workshop, 23 (22 traffic police officers and one SI) participated but two traffic police officers were not well, so they could not attend the second workshop. For the current research, the sample comprised the number of traffic police officers who attended both workshops. The views and findings in the case of SI were recorded separately and not integrated with the analysis of traffic police officers. Therefore, we consider n = 20, the number of traffic police officers who attended both workshops. None of the officers had ever attended a workshop on preventive health [ ].
 Levels 1 and 2: Overall, 56.5% of the participants gave the academic sessions an excellent rating, 34.8% a very good rating, and 8.7% a good rating [ ]. The performance as shown by mean scores improved after the workshop with the scores improving from 2.5–3.0 to 4.1–4.6 [ and ]. The scores were computed on a Likert scale basis for each question (1 for poor, 2 for fair, 3 for good, 4 for very good, and 5 for excellent), and then the mean score was computed. The self-assessment of health-seeking behavior was checked pre- and post-health care training on the following criteria and a paired t-test was attempted to understand the significance of the scores [ ].
 Levels 3 and 4: The workshops were able to generate an impact on the health-seeking behavior of the participants that reveals the positive mediating role of preventive health workshops in improving the health and well-being of traffic police. The participants gained new knowledge about healthcare management related to pollution control, occupational hazards, nutritional well-being, and mental health. All 20 traffic police officers showed improvement in health-seeking behavior. The significant lifestyle changes after attending the workshop ranged between 5% and 75%. The short-form health survey developed by Ware J. and Sherbourne C. was used for the self-assessment of traffic police at the beginning of the first workshop.[ ] Furthermore, 56.5% of the traffic police perceive their health to be in good condition, 26% perceive it to be very good, and 8.7% perceive it to be excellent and fair. The self-rated perception of good health may be because of the age group they belong and fail to see the future implications of ill effects.

## DISCUSSIONS
Overall, all participants in the workshop acknowledged that by the end of the training, they were more knowledgeable about the effects of bad food, insufficient sleep, and stress on their health. They said that they had not previously been aware of how certain health conditions such as regular exposure to ultraviolet (UV) light raising the risk of skin cancer and musculoskeletal disorders made them more susceptible than those outside of their occupation. While none of them have been able to implement the whole range of remedial steps that were taught to them, several acknowledged that they had changed their lifestyles significantly because of the workshop.
Regarding environmental pollution, the participants became cognizant of the harmful effects of both fuel emissions and noise pollution on their health. After attending the workshop in July, most participants are now in the habit of using devices such as peak flow meter, and apps for tracking weather alerts, noise monitoring, and UV rays. Fourteen participants started tracking pollution using various apps taught in the workshop. They also used Peak Flow meter intermittently to check lung function. All participants expressed their interest in noise-cancelling devices such as earmuffs. One participant approached a skin and ear nose throat (ENT) specialist and underwent tests that consequently helped him diagnose and treat the problem successfully.
In terms of nutrition and fitness, the most important behavior change that the workshops recorded was the cessation of meal-skipping by the officers. A significant number of male traffic police officers (10 of them) stated that after attending the workshop, they now ensure that they do not skip breakfast. These officers also changed their dietary habits in different measures like avoiding oily foods and giving up cold drinks and red meat. As a result of the awareness generated through the workshop, nine traffic police officers (male) made a conscious effort to walk/run at least four times a week. These officers also started adhering to certain forms of fitness regimens after the workshop.
Closely related to physical health is mental health improvement. All participants observed that they needed mental healthcare management. As a coping mechanism against occupational stress, three traffic police officers (female) tuned out all forms of noise, started remaining silent, and began listening to soft music. Another stress management impact could be seen in 15 participants who started relieving mental stress by talking to friends, colleagues, and family and listening to motivational talks. Journaling was also taken up by two participants as a form of self-expression to reduce stress. All candidates strongly felt that they needed a platform where they could be “heard,” a space where they could vent. The workshops provided a platform to share the concerns of traffic police. It also helped them cope better while dealing with public aggression and volatility in traffic. All participants felt that these workshops have helped nudge the barriers imposed by hierarchy and enabled them to speak openly to their seniors – as a collective group – and also helped them understand the constraints faced by their seniors. Many participants found the techniques on anger/stress management useful while dealing with aggressive drivers on the road.

## CONCLUSION
The present research shows that traffic police can be motivated toward health-seeking behavior through workshops. As a result of the workshop, awareness of diet, lifestyle, and mental health increased the most. After the workshop, they had a good understanding of psychological stress and how to deal with it. The findings of this research thus prove that a positive relationship exists between training and the health-seeking behavior of traffic police officers. Workshops on health awareness may significantly contribute to the health of traffic police officers. Due to the demands of their jobs, such as prolonged periods of standing, exposure to harsh weather, and exposure to pollutants and traffic noise, traffic police officers are at risk of acquiring a variety of health issues. Therefore, it is essential to put policies in place that can assist in addressing the medical requirements of traffic police officers. To monitor the health of traffic police officers, governments might set up routine health examinations. Preventive care, such as regular checks and screenings, especially for those in jobs that are exposed to environmental hazards, needs more public investment. This will lead to a healthier workforce, less absenteeism, and more productivity. The workshop findings will influence future public health programs in Bhubaneswar.
