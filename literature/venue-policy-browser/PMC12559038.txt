## Abstract
A plethora of tools have been used to understand health inequities. While these could be well-executed in a controlled environment, the uncertainties of working in a community lead to challenges that are sometimes beyond researchers’ control. This is further aggravated while working with ‘vulnerable’ population sub-groups. As part of a larger health systems research project focused on health inequalities, we sought to identify these groups to assess their needs. We discussed in this article, our methodological reflections on trying to identify and carry out research with groups left behind, and the way we approached the challenges incurred during the data collection process. Identifying ‘vulnerable’ groups required constant interrogation and questioning of the very definition of ‘vulnerability’ in varied contexts and in relation to other groups facing disadvantages. Different strategies were used, including snowball sampling from FGD participants. Even when identified, we found that in some cases, the FGD method was inappropriate for fieldwork and that the ethical requirement of ensuring some care access for study participants became increasingly difficult to assure. Equity-oriented research must involve constant reflexivity around such ethical dilemmas, which we feel are too often ignored -just as some ‘vulnerable’ populations are- in typical (writing about) research studies.

## Introduction
Populations from lower socioeconomic groups have been excluded from public health and medical research for some time now [ ]. The perspectives of these and other socially disadvantaged groups are crucial for an overall understanding of the differences in health burdens, care-seeking and access across populations. The United Nations launched the Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) in 2015 with a strong focus on inclusion and reducing inequalities [ ]. In pursuit of these goals, as we strive for Universal Health Coverage, it is essential that populations facing disadvantage on the basis of social position, socio-economic status, or other conditions shaped by history and context are both catered to while shaping public health programs and goals [ ]. These groups are often referred to as ‘vulnerable’ groups in many parts of the world. Researchers from a variety of disciplines have sought to define ‘vulnerability’ (see Box ) [ ]. The ‘vulnerable’ are sometimes interchangeably called the marginalized [ ], hard-to-reach [ , ], and disadvantaged groups [ , ], even though there are important distinctions between these terms.
Within the Indian context, women and children, as well as Scheduled Caste, and Scheduled Tribe populations have been historically marginalized and socially excluded which has limited their access to education and health over the years [ ]. As a case point, women and children in India face high rates of malnutrition and inadequate access to healthcare [ ]. Although known as the pathfinder state, there are significant disparities in health outcomes across different population groups within Kerala, and ‘vulnerable’ communities face a higher burden of the health challenges [ ]. These disparities are reflected in higher rates of both communicable and non-communicable diseases, malnutrition, maternal and infant mortality, and limited access to healthcare services [ ]. For example, women in Kerala have better health status compared to other states in India but still face challenges such as high rates of anemia [ ]. Additionally, with one-third of the population in the state being above 65 years of age [ ], the rates of non-communicable diseases are higher in Kerala compared to other states [ ].
Conducting research with ‘vulnerable’ groups requires consideration of sensitivity among researchers [ ]. It can be a challenging process that necessitates extra thinking and additional steps in the research process [ ]. Additionally, various forms of disadvantage intersect, interact, and together influence access to health (For example, in the US, gender minority blacks are known to experience more physical and mental distress compared to cisgender blacks [ ]). Qualitative research is instrumental in unraveling the complexities of intersectionality in health, as it delves into the lived experiences and narratives of individuals from diverse backgrounds [ ].
Existing qualitative research among the ‘vulnerable’ has provided in-depth insights into their ‘vulnerability’ [ , ]. In research, working with vulnerable groups necessitates utmost sensitivity and ethical consideration and require a compassionate approach that safeguards their dignity and rights [ ]. Use of Focus Group Discussion (FGD) helps in providing a trusting environment for the participants since they would be among their peers and delves deeply into the topic when one participant’s opinion or perspective feeds off another participant’s view or perception. Focus groups may help members of a ‘vulnerable’ community feel less isolated and co-construct reflections and opinions that may not form in isolation [ ]. On the other hand, it also poses a limitation that participants’ opinions could be influenced by social norms and group dynamics [ , ] and there is a lack of confidentiality and anonymity which is more concerning when the topic of research is sensitive [ ].
While using the FGD already has challenges and limitations [ , ], we found that conducting FGDs among ‘vulnerable’ groups poses additional dilemmas. In light of this, our collective reflection was that writing an account of the actual conduct of the research could help in preparing researchers for undertaking similar studies among ‘vulnerable’ populations [ ]. In addition to this, we felt that this methodological reflection was a form of accountability that we needed to convey in terms of the execution of our methodology, a statement of its rigor in light of field realities. In the following sections, we have described our qualitative study among left-behind groups in Kerala, a southern state in India, followed by detailed reflections on methodological and ethical challenges encountered, how we addressed them, and relevant literature we found to contextualize and further reflect on our approach.
Box what is ‘vulnerability’?Several definitions of ‘vulnerability’ exist in the literature. Aday [ ] defined it as a state in which people are in a disadvantaged position. The concept of vulnerability has been criticized for being both overly encompassing and excessively limited [ ]. Liamputtong [ ] defined ‘vulnerability’ as surfaced “by people’s relative invisibility and their marginality and lack of opportunity to voice their concerns”. Benzon and Blerk saw ‘vulnerability’ as a social construct and to include groups having less social, political, and economic capital than others in society [ ]. It has been argued that the nature of the risk and a household’s capacity for risk response determine the level of ‘vulnerability’ [ ]. The inclusion in ‘vulnerability’ is also influenced by the context in which it is explored: disaster management [ – ], climate change [ , ], access to/use of technology [ ] are examples. In addition, within the health context, the hard-to-reach groups have been defined in the past to include drug users, HIV-positive individuals, members of sexual minority groups, refugees, asylum seekers, members of black and ethnic minority groups, and the homeless [ ].
It is important to acknowledge that usage of the term ‘vulnerable’ has a negative connotation and has been challenged as well [ , ]. In HIV/AIDS work, for example, there has been a shift to using ‘key populations’ given the highly problematic legacy of stigmatization associated with identifying epidemiologically ‘vulnerable’ populations in this context [ ]. Elsewhere, the terminology of ‘affected populations’ or ‘priority populations’ is used. Other framings from allied disciplines like urban planning suggest the use of ‘left behind’ places [ ], while anthropologists working in humanitarian contexts argue for the use of ‘marginalized’ to place emphasis on social processes that lead to ‘vulnerability’ [ ].
In India, from the ethical guidelines of the Indian Council of Medical Research to policy documents, the use of the term ‘vulnerable’ is commonplace. Despite being subject to criticism for its negative implications and potential stigmatizing effects, the term ‘vulnerable group’ has garnered considerable attention and utilization within national [ ] and regional systems [ ] as well as the media [ ]. While there are valid concerns about the use of the term, particularly in how it may perpetuate a deficit-based view of certain populations, in some contexts, the term may be accompanied by more nuanced descriptors that recognize the agency, resilience, and diversity of the individuals or communities in question [ ]. It is important to acknowledge the limitations and potential harms of labeling people as ‘vulnerable’, and to prioritize strategies that promote empowerment, participation, and self-determination. At the same time, however, it is also crucial to recognize the structural and systemic factors that contribute to vulnerability, and to adopt a proactive and inclusive approach to addressing them. For these reasons, we found it difficult to carry out a study without reference to and use of this term.
Therefore, for the purpose of our study, we defined ‘vulnerability’ by the groups identified as having relatively limited access to public or private healthcare. These groups were broadly identified by the system actors in the local area coupled with the researchers’ own exploration to look for groups who might be invisible to the system actors. The groups identified were by their socio-economic status, age, gender, occupation, and people experiencing morbidity and/or mobility impairment (e.g. those receiving palliative care).

## The study
As part of Universal Health Coverage (UHC)-linked health reform in the Indian state of Kerala, the state put forth initiatives to promote health with the aim of ensuring that everyone is included, and no one is left behind. Our larger study aimed to assess the magnitudes of inequalities related to identifying the inequalities and used participatory, qualitative methods to assess the mechanisms underlying these inequalities. The question of accessibility of healthcare to all populations was seeded in our research since its inception. Through an initial quantitative survey and interviews of health system actors, we identified the prevalence of inequalities of access within study sites. We were also able to signify which groups were being left behind in a broader, quantitative sense. The identified groups included the elderly, women, fisherfolk, minority social groups, etc [ ]. It then became important to validate and extend inquiry directly from the perspective of such population sub-groups. To understand their perspectives and experiences of healthcare, we found it important to conduct a qualitative study among these groups.
Our aim was to conduct a wide sweeping inquiry into the various challenges faced by ‘vulnerable’ groups in accessing healthcare. To achieve this, we chose to take a normative position and explore the perspectives of different identified ‘vulnerable’ groups, rather than focusing on one group alone. Our intention was to provide comprehensive findings that could assist stakeholders in considering potential interventions. As part of this approach, we sought to bring these ‘vulnerable’ groups together and identify both shared and unique challenges among them. Although this approach may lead to heterogenous grouping of data collection, we believed it was necessary to gain a more holistic understanding of the issues faced by these groups. Ultimately, our goal was to provide stakeholders with a comprehensive overview of the challenges faced by ‘vulnerable’ groups in accessing healthcare, and to propose potential interventions that could help to address these challenges.
A team of five researchers was involved in the data collection for this study between March 2022 and August 2022. The study was conducted in four districts of Kerala (India)- Thiruvananthapuram, Kollam, Alappuzha, and Kasaragod. These districts were chosen using a random sampling method, details of which are published elsewhere [ ]. Further, two primary healthcare facilities were chosen in each district and the panchayats which came under these facilities’ service were the study sites.
In this research study, we adopted a post-positivist approach to guide our methods and analysis where we avoided having a single framework or theory (48). This approach helped us in inclusion of diverse perspectives and the consideration of multiple sources of evidence to generate a more comprehensive analysis. We employed FGDs and group interviews as a method of data collection, but also conducted key informant interviews (KII) when data collection through previous methods were not feasible (discussed in later sections). A topic guide was developed and translated to Malayalam, the local language by the researchers. This was then pilot-tested with two FGDs as a practice for the researchers and to finalize the questions. At least two researchers were available for facilitating each FGD. In order to visualize the patient journeys, a mapping exercise was carried out with the participants to identify facilities and services available to them as they recollected their health-seeking experience (topic guide is added as Supplementary file A ). In addition to the questions in the topic guide, any new topics of interest that came up during the discussion were further explored. The sample recruited and interviewed is summarized in Table  (A comprehensive participant list is given in Supplementary file B). Due to the wide range of groups covered and the emphasis on incorporating intersectionality in our study, we were unable to achieve complete saturation for each group represented and across all topics covered in the data collection process – although for some areas, saturation was achieved. We viewed this as a trade-off of depth for breadth, affording us not a population-specific, but system wide, illustrative understanding of the intersectional challenges faced across the different study sites.
 In the following paragraphs, we discuss in detail our experiences of conducting the fieldwork, sharing the challenges incurred in the process and our way of navigating through them.

## Permissions to access study sites
A good rapport with authorities and decision makers is beneficial when conducting studies in the community. To receive their concurrence, it is important that we are transparent about our objectives and how the outputs would be beneficial for them and the community in general [ – ]. This requires clear communication of purpose and in some cases, negotiation, to be able to secure permission and interest of authority figures and community participants [ ]. In some cases, there are added layers of protection for certain populations, for instance, tribal populations, who may face unique forms of exploitation from research and developmental interventions [ ]. Research has shown that researchers can sometimes “overstep a mark or act inappropriately” [ ], therefore, requiring laws in place to protect community members.
Approval for our study was taken from the Institutional Ethics Committee of the George Institute for Global Health, following which, we sought approval from the Department of Health and Family Welfare, Government of Kerala, while also informing and obtaining the concurrence of District Medical Officers of each site, and of medical officers in charge of each primary healthcare facility with jurisdiction over our field sites. A Department Order was issued with respect to our study and available for all participants to view or keep. Since we were operating at the community level, we also informed and sought the concurrence and cooperation of Local Self Government (LSG) leaders associated with each field site. Access to some groups such as the tribal community required additional permissions from the Department of Tribal Welfare and the Additional Principal Chief Conservator of Forests , which were also sought, even as at times, the time taken to converge all (sometimes time-bound) approvals left us with rather small windows to carry out fieldwork. However, a continuous communication channel between us and the administrative bodies over the duration of project with periodic feedback regarding our previous findings helped us in securing permissions faster.

## Researchers involved in data collection
Researchers who have long been working towards addressing the inequity and power imbalance between researchers and the researched have advocated recruitment and engagement of community researchers as part of the study [ ]. Use of community based participatory research is particularly valuable in studies involving vulnerable or marginalised groups, as it fosters trust, empowers participants, and ensures that research priorities and interpretations are informed by lived experiences [ ]. Community-based participatory research has been effectively applied in diverse Indian contexts. For example, a participatory-action study in 23 villages of Wardha district, Maharashtra, engaged rural adolescent girls through Kishori Panchayats to co-develop and deliver menstrual hygiene education [ ]. Even though we strongly support community-engaged research practices and have prior successful collaboration with community researchers in a separate study [ ], time constraints prevented our team from engaging community researchers in this study. Additionally, the heterogenous nature of groups recruited (discussed in later sections) also made it difficult to choose any single representative of so many groups who could be involved in data collection. A team of four early career researchers- two male (HS, JJ) and two female (GB, SS)- led by a senior female researcher (DN) were involved in the data collection. Although the four researchers who were actively involved in data collection did not originate from the communities we were interviewing, we were all from the state of Kerala. Additionally, we had similar linguistic and cultural backgrounds, further facilitating our understanding and interaction with the community. Our group represented varying degrees of qualification (public health, social work, development, anthropology), experience with the topic area, with fieldwork, and even with the methods used.

## Identification and recruitment of participants
Selection of study participants in qualitative studies is usually purposive to accommodate the objective of the study [ ]. Having a homogenous group helps in setting a natural environment (as in grouping participants with whom they generally are in conversation with) easing them into the discussion and effective engagement in the data collection process [ ].
To ensure homogeneity in each FGD, we tried to have participants of the same age and gender group along with a shared dimension of inequality or disadvantage. Despite our inability to engage community researchers, we made a concerted effort to incorporate community participation into the study. For recruitment of participants, we reached out to community leaders and health system actors, since they would also be in a position to act on our findings and advance programming in service of persons facing disadvantage. This built on an earlier phase of our study, where we had interviewed the LSG leaders and local healthcare workers [ ], based on which there was an existing rapport and familiarity with the purpose of our work. Thus, we were able to collaborate with them to identify participants. This facilitated an expansion of the scope of communities encompassed within the ‘vulnerable’ group, surpassing the boundaries prescribed by the extant literature. For instance, official government documents and published sources often neglect to recognize patients in need of palliative care as a segment of the ‘vulnerable’ population; however, they were identified to have a concomitant need for tailored interventions by the LSGs and local health workers.
India’s Accredited Social Health Activists or ASHAs are community health workers, who act as intermediaries between the system and the community [ ]. They played a major role in bringing together the participants for the FGD as they were local representatives that communities could trust [ , ]. This, however, meant our reach to ‘who are ‘vulnerable’ was limited by the scope as identified by these and other system actors. ‘Vulnerable’ populations are usually considered ‘hard-to-reach’ and are often left out of social welfare systems [ ]. For example, even as migrants constitute a large proportion of daily wage laborers in the state of Kerala [ ] and were identified as a ‘vulnerable’ group, local healthcare workers were unable to help us recruit them. We did receive support contacting migrants working in the formal sector through local healthcare workers. This meant that migrants working in the informal sector or who were self-employed were not included in our sample, even as research has shown that such populations face distinct and often heightened health risks and exposures [ ]. Literature shows that in order to reach ‘vulnerable’ groups who are hard to reach, one must use the ascending methodology which includes snowball sampling (with the first person typically identifies through the researchers’ initial contact or existing connections), life histories and ethnographic monographs [ ].
Conscious of the selection bias from the outset, our approach included identification of non-governmental organizations (NGOs) and community social workers working in each field site. This was intended to help us broaden our horizon beyond whom the system actors perceived to be ‘vulnerable’. In many cases, however, the populations that NGOs worked with were not residents/inhabitants of the specific primary facility catchment areas we were working with. Thus arose the limitation of not having in situ, enough people to represent the perspective of a particular group (for example, transgenders, or persons involved with manual scavenging, or the self-employed) because they were not residents of our study sites. To ensure that their voice was heard, we amended our study to include in-depth interviews and key informant interviews with such scattered populations in the state, even if they were not residents in study sites. These interviews were conducted online or in person on a day and time convenient for the participants.

## Convening of FGDs/Group or key informant interviews
A major logistical consideration in conducting an FGD is bringing the group together at a mutually convenient physical location at a mutually convenient time. While the guidelines on conduct of FGDs detail its composition and moderation [ ], we found that there was limited emphasis on the challenges of convening them, barring a few studies [ , ]. There were unique challenges associated with assembling participants for an FGD. We had instances where the turnout was lower than expected as well as instances where participants were too many and could not be turned away. Notably, in one instance, it was conveyed to us by one of the ASHAs who helped in arranging a Scheduled Caste group that called for a limited number was perceived as being exclusionary. In her view, notifying just 10 members of the community and requesting them to attend could lead to conflict over this issue in the community. Handling a larger group in FGD was a challenge, hence in such cases, where circumstances allowed, we tried to split the group and conduct two FGDs simultaneously. Having four researchers in the team visiting the sites helped in such cases as we had resources in hand to conduct more than one FGD.
There were also certain groups that could not be physically assembled. Palliative care patients were a group that was identified as ‘vulnerable’ in all the districts. However, by definition, these participants were bedridden and confined to their homes. As a solution, we decided to bring together the primary caregivers in the family of these patients since they were well-versed with the patient’s journey and could speak on their behalf. This, however, raised an ethical issue of consent and personal information of another person being conveyed by their guardian. We followed aligned principles of consent-taking and handling of sensitive personal information. Similarly, Endosulfan-affected individuals were identified as a potential group in Kasargod but were geographically dispersed. Further, their illness prevented them from traveling and we decided to interview them individually. This meant traveling to individual households where LSG leaders and health system actors had indicated comprised residents affected by Endosulfan exposure. We had earlier carried out a scoping exercise to understand vulnerability across each districts and some of the households had been visited earlier in an informal capacity. In a sense, therefore, we were not completely unfamiliar with this group of participants, nor they with us.
The ethics of reciprocity and benefit-sharing are integral to conducting research with communities. Reciprocity entails recognising participants’ time, insights, and trust by ensuring that they tangibly benefit from the research process, while avoiding forms of compensation that could be coercive or create inequities [ ]. In our study, we sought to uphold these principles by offering locally sourced snacks and beverages during FGDs, both as a gesture of appreciation and to support the local economy. In cases where health system actors and community leaders assisted with reaching out to and assembling participants, we compensated their time with a nominal cash payment.

## Informed consent
Having informed consent from participants is required for any research study. Taking consent is not merely about getting a signature on the sheet [ ]. Informed consent requires that participants have a full understanding of what the study entails, what their contribution to it would be, as well as the voluntary nature of participation [ ]. While providing participants with information sheets, it is significant that details of the research are written in short simple sentences for readability [ ]. Additionally, the researchers must make sure all elements are included and explained in relatable terms, which may vary by type of participant.
For our study, we followed the standard IRB-approved informed consent processes and provided the participants information on who we were, the details of our project along with the objectives, the role they played as participants, and what would be done with the data collected from them. This was an especially important step as at times, the participants assembled had perceived expectations that were different from our objectives. This could have been because we took help from the local and community leaders for gathering participants, who usually convened meetings to transfer information or update them on matters. Additionally, since ASHAs were instrumental in gathering the participants together for our study, and since they represented the healthcare outreach activities from the public healthcare sector, most often, participants assumed we were there to provide a health education class. In other sites, participants had agreed to assemble but were skeptical about our purpose and motives. Having informed them about the details prior to asking any details helped us gain their trust and prepare them for oncoming questions. The effort and time that should go into building this trust, we acknowledge, should be much greater. Given that we were aiming for the breadth of perspective from “those left behind,” we had to sacrifice attention to this critical aspect. It would be more ethical and appropriate to spend more time with each set of individuals, families, and communities to explain our purpose and then advance study components with them. We were aware that we had been “parachuting” in to convene these groups and extract this information and that it was fully valid to have reservations and even refusals. In our case, there were two refusals or withdrawals from participation.
We also placed some attention on the method by which consent was recorded. Several leading international Universities have now established oral/verbal consent to be a valid form of taking informed consent from participants [ , ]. The use of verbal consent has shown higher participant recruitment in research studies [ ]. In India, participants often exhibit reservations about signing forms as they feel signed forms could be misused for other purposes, or in cases where literacy is low, forms could indicate something other than what researchers are conveying. There tends to be greater comfort in many cases, with verbal consent. In our study, we first obtained verbal consent from participants after going over the Participant Information Sheets together. Participants were informed about the use of audio recorders to collect data while ensuring their anonymity and confidentiality. However, as we needed to record verbal consent, participants were prompted to each individually say ‘I consent to take part in the study’ after the audio recorder was switched on. This created a requirement of recording in our fieldwork, although we separately sought consent for this. This raised a dilemma – perhaps more theoretical than actual (as this did not come up in fieldwork from the perspective of participants, even as it was on our minds) – that those who may have wanted to participate but not be audio-recorded could not be in the study as we had a procedure of verbally recorded informed consent.

## Collaborative mapping exercise
Studies have used visual collaborative tools for data collection and to get insights into findings [ ]. In our study, the mapping of facilities was a visual form of data collection from the community (see Fig. ). In order to be inclusive and active, participants were initially requested to map the resources themselves. Although this was very interactive, we soon realized that the mechanics of the map, deciding what colors to use, and what symbols meant what, all this was taking time away from participants and reducing the time spent on talking about actual health journeys. Some of our participants had time constraints and would not be able to spend that length of time with us. We therefore took on the role of scribes, guided by participants. This brought back the discussion to include more on their experiences and perspectives on the facilities that they identified to be mapped. These maps were later visualized with the help of design experts to make them standard and readable for the stakeholders (Fig. ). While the researchers’ involvement in mapping helped in standardization across the different field sites, this approach may have limited the extent to which the maps reflected local priorities, perceptions, and spatial understandings. This in turn could have yielded more novel and context-specific, if less directly comparable, insights on patient journeys and experiences. Further, had we spent more time in the community, we may have eventually been able to give custodianship and direct authorship of members to the maps. Further, this may have better been set up as an individual pathway mapping exercise (which we have done in other work) [ ], rather than creating a group-based understanding.
 For mapping, we used chart paper and markers which were carried to the study site by researchers. However, the venue that was chosen to be a public place in close proximity to the participants’ locale meant that it may be difficult to hold the chart up in place and in view of the participants. While sticking to a nearby wall was usually the preferred solution, participants usually helped us find an alternative in cases where there were no walls at the venue. In one of the FGDs, there was no option of placing that chart at all; hence important details that were usually mapped were written down in the notepad of one of the researchers and later used to construct a map with designers. The trade-off here was of being truly participatory. Time poverty is a major challenge in any case in participatory research.

## Content of FGDs/Group or key informant interviews
Engaging with gatekeepers in the initial convening of the study itself is seen to be essential to gain their trust in a research study, with the proviso that their involvement should be limited to this point [ ]. The support of health system actors was critical throughout our data collection process- right from identifying participants, to notifying and assembling them for the study. In some cases, however, these gatekeepers were present during data collection. We tried wherever possible to engage them separately or request them to allow the process to advance in their absence, but it was not always feasible to do this. It is possible, likely even, that this heightened the Hawthorne effect in our study. Sometimes, these actors would even interrupt our interview proceedings to answer some of the questions posed by us. This intrusion ranged from being nominal clarification to seeking opinions from individual participants. A case study form the Kerala context by Thomas (2020) talks about the complexities of relying on health system actors to identify participants which often raises the ethical challenge of maintaining data confidentiality once fieldwork is underway [ ]. We tried to reduce this interference as much as possible. This was done by one researcher engaging gatekeepers in parallel conversations, politely pulling them away from the group. While such attempts were made, some form of influence did slip in during the discussions. Data from gatekeepers in our study were not included in the analysis, but their influences on the responses of participants cannot be undone. Further it is unclear what the nature of their influence may be, and how it may vary across the participant group; for instance, gender or caste minority or otherwise minoritized (meaning less represented or fewer in number) persons may have felt a different kind of pressure or influence than others.
During fieldwork, we avoided using the term “vulnerable” in our interactions with participants, particularly when engaging with social groups such as Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes, as we were mindful that such labelling could be stigmatizing or influence how participants perceived their own positioning. Often such groups were from a specific residential or geographic area and that was used to frame their experience, rather than a social identity.
In contrast, we did specifically refer to identities associated with occupation (e.g., fisherfolk, MGNREGA workers) and age or health condition (e.g., elderly, carers of palliative patients), as this tended to relate more directly to health exposures and burdens. We could therefore be explicit in describing the rationale for their inclusion, framing it in terms of specific challenges they faced in accessing health services rather than as an inherent vulnerability.”
While doing qualitative research, where the topics may be sensitive, distress may arise among participants [ ]. In our study, talking about past patient journeys for the participants meant recalling painful experiences which included deaths in the family or grave health conditions. Admittedly this was traumatizing for participants. There were times when the participants broke down during discussions. One of the FGDs was with widowed women who were recalling the deaths of their spouses. We were struck by the disruptive nature of our endeavor and that we were indeed causing discomfort in the course of our research conduct. We offered to stop proceedings and allow participants to compose themselves with the option of discontinuing the interview. In most cases, they did carry on. Although not referred to in our study, the literature also shows that the use of guidelines that recognize the delicate nature of research questions and the ‘vulnerability’ of participants helps in minimizing the risk of participant distress [ ]. Having a third party, usually a caretaker or a counsellor helps in mitigating during those incidences [ ]. 
A related concern pertained to confidentiality. In some cases, healthcare-seeking journeys are very private and painful, and sharing them in a group can be vexing, particularly with a group of people one may go on to see in one’s daily life. This could potentially influence their conversations in the group, and they might share as much as they would like to [ , ]. Mindful of this, we invited participants for individual follow-up interviews in cases where we felt that there was information that they were not sharing in the larger group that would provide a more complete picture of what they had endured/were enduring. Ending the FGDs with the opportunity for follow-up interviews with such participants was intended to enable further private and confidential discussion. However, in our study, none of the participants expressed interest in participating in an additional round and therefore individual level variation, journeys, interpretation of experience could not be included in our analysis. Through the FGD, the participant would have already established familiarity with the researcher, and as the literature suggests, this would increase their comfort levels with subsequent interaction as a research participant [ ].
Overall, the conduct of this study made us confront myriad ethical challenges related to conducting research with populations experiencing vulnerability. Navigating through the challenges has yielded lessons, which we contextualized by drawing upon the existing literature (summarized in Table ). It is of course true that the academic literature may not always be the single or only place to understand how ethical challenges may be navigated. Equally, the framing of methods specifically appropriate to working with populations facing disadvantage comes with its own grammar, framing and disciplinary legacies (consider, for example trauma-informed approaches [ , ], reflexivity frameworks [ , ], as well as guides for participatory action research) [ , ]. What we present are a mere starting point, and intended to serve as a launching pad to explore resources – academic and non-academic -that may guide for future qualitative research with ‘vulnerable’ communities and may be helpful to others in the field.
 

## The importance of reflexivity
After every FGD/group interview, the research team reflected on how the data collection went. This was significant, especially during the initial data collection as it helped us understand what was unexpected, what went wrong, and what needed to be changed. In addition, as is standard practice in qualitative research, we took field notes on the conduct and major issues that were brought up in the discussion. This helped us identify key areas to explore during further data collection.
Understanding our role(s) as researchers and the biases we bring with us to the field is imperative in reflecting the data that is being collected [ ]. We felt that having a group of researchers from different backgrounds helped make us be more reflexive about the impact of bias we brought in as individuals. Having variation within the group in terms of education and experience, we believe, brought in a range of perspectives and experiences to the research process, which helped in identification and challenging assumptions and biases that might have otherwise gone unnoticed. Furthermore, we believe that incorporating multiple perspectives and approaches during data collection, analysis, and interpretation helped to improve the accuracy and dependability of the research outcomes. This approach also ensured that the research was based on a wider range of experiences and contexts, making it more likely to represent the diversity of the populations being investigated. By being a mix of two female and two male researchers, we could ensure we had at least one researcher of same gender as the group during data collection. Additionally, by being natives of the state and sharing the local language, communication with the participants was easier. We also understand that by being from the same community and having similar shared experiences, we could have individually influenced the data collection. However, since many of us were in the field at the same time, we could be nimble and support each other on the one hand, while also being able to absorb and refract what we were experiencing based on our unique vantage points. Bringing this together made us aware of our constraints, limitations, and mistakes as well as our strategies and plans to address them going forward. It was a very critical learning process that we have subsequently sought to share more broadly through this reflection.
At a larger level, we feel that reflexivity must be deeply embedded in different stages of the research process to address equity even within the conduct of the research study. Even as resources for this existed within and beyond our institution [ ], they were not fully adequate. In many reflexivity allows us to acknowledge ethics as an asymptote in any study – full equity or fully ethical behaviour may never be fully possible to achieve. For example we did not have the resources to return to each participant group to member check our findings or to disseminate them once the study was completed. Yet, we engaged with community representatives from across groups as well as local self government leaders. We shared one-page summaries of our findings with them and invited their reflections and feedback. Reflexivity suggests this is a modest and incomplete contribution, but has some value in ensuring that we as researchers make our work less extractive and more relevant to local realities, understandings and priorities.

## Conclusion
In this article, we described the challenges we faced in the conduct of fieldwork with ‘vulnerable’ populations. We must reckon with the reality that researchers are intruders into the day-to-day lives of individuals and communities. While this interruption helps us better understand the experiences and processes of disadvantage faced by certain groups, it also makes the moral demand for us to be respectful, accommodative, reflexive, and honest about how our research is conducted and what it produces. We encourage researchers to consider this demand and how they may respond to it in their work.
