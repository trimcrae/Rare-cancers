## Abstract
Advancing nursing practice to improve care and system outcomes requires doctoral-prepared nurses to conduct programs of research and translate science to practice. The authors describe a Doctoral support group (DSG) at one hospital designed to support nurses considering and navigating doctoral education while continuing as hospital employees. Strategies from 18 years' experience are provided for others to develop and sustain a DSG as part of an environment to support and retain nurses with doctoral degrees.

To advance nursing practice and improve the health of the public, nurses prepared at the doctoral level are required to conduct programs of research, advance and expand evidence-based practices (EBPs), translate the science to practice, inform policy, and educate future generations of nurses. Of the 4.7 million RNs in the United States in 2022, 2.7% held a doctoral degree, a number not sufficient to meet current demands for leadership roles in advanced practice, clinical specialties, teaching, policy, administration, and research. , Hospitals can be a resource for nurses considering and pursuing doctoral education as they are positioned to create and support roles for nurses with doctoral degrees that align with their doctoral preparation. 
In the United States, hospitals are one of the largest employers of nurses, but few report hospital-based programs in support of nurses considering or pursuing doctoral education. Early and sustained connections to nurse scientists embedded in health systems help to expose doctoral nursing students to clinical questions that might otherwise not be realized in academic institutions while helping to foster networks for students to seek advice. Other investigators have described benefits of formal mentorship and fellowship programs embedded in care settings for clinical nurses interested in and currently enrolled in doctoral programs. For example, findings from a qualitative descriptive study that explored the hospital-based facilitator or mentor role for doctorate of nursing practice (DNP) gerontology students reported the role as instrumental in the successful progression of DNP students' projects. Others reported the strategies and contributions of PhD-prepared nurse scientists in 4 healthcare systems that advanced the capacity for nursing research in their systems, including an interdisciplinary bootcamp, EBP fellowships, and residencies directed to master's degree–prepared nurses. Chlan and colleagues described 2 nurse scholar programs in a large Magnet®-designated health system, a Clinical Nurse Scholar Program, and a program for nurses already enrolled or had recently completed a PhD program. The intended outcome for both programs was to contribute to the pipeline of future nurse scientists. Their Clinical Nurse Scholar Program promoted research interests among clinical nurses to encourage them to consider doctoral education, whereas the second program supported efforts by nurses to develop independent programs of research through protected time to focus on their dissertation research or to pursue postdoctoral research training.
These few examples reflect the need for structures in hospital systems to actively provide support to nurses to seek and flourish during doctoral education reinforcing the care systems' investment in the profession and in the future of healthcare. These authors report strategies that were used to create and sustain a hospital-based structure to support nurses seeking doctoral education, that of a doctoral support group (DSG). Strategies include institutional support, comprehensive infrastructure for research and scholarship, DSG structure, members, outreach and engagement, facilitators for group coordination, and programs for support beyond degree completion.

## Origin of the Doctoral Support Group
The DSG was envisioned in 2006 by the former chief nursing officer (CNO) and executive vice president of patient care services. The DSG was formed in response to a system-wide assessment of nursing preparedness to address current and future healthcare challenges likely to include nursing shortages and underfunding of patient care. The CNO's intention was to advance professionalism, strengthen education, and introduce nursing research to ensure excellence in care delivery. A new nursing strategic plan included a focus on developing nursing leadership at all levels. This included fostering nursing scholarship, doctoral preparation, and interdisciplinary collaboration with our academic partners to continue to advance nursing and care delivery at the hospital.

## Setting for the Doctoral Support Group
The Children's National Hospital (CNH) is an integrated healthcare system that includes a 323-bed facility with more than 670 000 ambulatory visits each year and an academic affiliation with the George Washington University School of Medicine and Health Sciences. CNH has maintained Magnet designation since 2010 from the American Nurses Credentialing Center.

## Institutional Support
The DSG as a support to all nurses has received continuing administrative support from the Division of Nursing. Articulating the vision of EBP that includes both the generation and translation of research into practice are central tenets of the Division of Nursing. The Division of Nursing provides the resources for nursing advancement including funding for both professional development and academic nursing education, such as tuition support for doctoral education. From its inception, the DSG was purposefully incorporated into the system-wide infrastructure of interdisciplinary clinical inquiry, scholarship, and research , (Table ). Examples of the environment supporting scholarship and inquiry are the doctoral student graduation celebrations and an annual celebration of scholarly writing to recognize nurses who published their scholarly work (journal article or book) in the previous year. In 2023, CNH nurses reported on their 49 peer-reviewed articles and 4 books or book chapters during this annual celebration. Such visible recognition of nursing scholarship reinforces the environment for doctoral-prepared nurses (personal communication, Pamela S. Hinds, December 6, 2023).
 Engaging nursing leaders when planning a DSG can advance the success of the group. Nursing leaders can assist in identifying nurses for the group, participate in the dissemination of information about the group to other nursing leaders, convey support for doctoral study for nurses, and provide mentorship. Inviting the CNO and other nursing leaders to periodically attend DSG meetings can reinforce the significance of the group and the career interests and intentions of the members during and following completion of their degrees. The DSG is part of the organizational support designed to engage nurses in activities to strengthen and advance care outcomes for children and families.

## Doctoral Support Group Members
The DSG is open to all nurses. Those attending are nurses who are curious, considering, or currently enrolled in or have completed doctoral education. The DSG welcomes nurses in all types of doctoral programs and does not limit its membership to those in nursing programs. DSG members include nurses at all levels of practice and leadership including clinical nurses, advanced practice nurses, and administrators. Learning the rationale for each member to seek doctoral education and the career expectations of each member can inform members when selecting a program and expectations for their programs. Role diversity of the DSG members holding different positions throughout the healthcare system cultivates opportunities for member collaboration within the CNH. The concept of a shared experience and increased collaboration is supported by a study of a doctoral student organization of DNP and PhD students. Although peer support networks are desired by doctoral nursing students, especially those in school part-time, there is a dearth of such networks embedded in health systems such as that of the DSG. DSG members invite others to join the group including fellow students, nurse colleagues considering doctoral study, and faculty from the DSG member's doctoral programs. Some members have developed their own support networks independent of the monthly meetings and have established “writing buddies” to review each other's work prior to submission or presentation.

## Meeting Structure
The structure for the DSG includes 1-hour monthly virtual meetings on a consistent day and time convenient to the members. The DSG meetings open with the introductions of attendees including their name, position, graduate program, and their status in their education trajectory. Meeting agendas are designed to be flexible to meet the needs of DSG members including regular discussion of work-life-school commitments. Members are encouraged to share accomplishments since the last meeting. Members share challenges and invite constructive discussions of solutions. Member presentation examples include course assignments, dissertation or DNP project proposal, and defense practice. Some members invite their faculty advisor to meetings when they are presenting. Monthly attendance varies and has increased over time to approximately 10 nurses regularly attending.
Virtual meetings are important as members can attend from any location in the health system and eliminate travel time while maximizing inclusion. The virtual venue has also enabled nurses considering doctoral education to attend anonymously while they determine their readiness and if doctoral education is appropriate for their career goals. For example, 1 member attended meetings anonymously for 2 years and another 5 years to determine their readiness to apply for doctoral study and to identify the appropriate doctoral programs for their careers. One nurse, after listening for 2 years, asked a DSG facilitator to review her narrative for her application and conduct a practice interview. The member who listened for 5 years shared that her husband observed her attending the meetings, recognized her interest in pursuing her doctorate, and has been supportive as she is now in a PhD program. While listening, the members also learn of the resources available to them within the group and CNH as they pursue their degrees. Discussions include navigating hospital procedures regarding research ethics review and implementation of their projects within the organization. Through the discussions, members gain an understanding of the different educational experiences and career trajectories between and among their academic programs.
A common discussion initiated by members is their reason for pursuing doctoral education, including wanting to learn strategies to determine the factors that contribute to system or patient care challenges and to find new ways to address clinical and institutional challenges. One member stated, “Scholarship, including research and process improvement, allows us to process negative events in the clinical environment” (personal communication, Christine Riley, PhD, APRN, CPNP-AC, January 9, 2024). Most members indicate plans to stay in the organization and a desire to contribute new knowledge from their doctoral education into practice across the organization. Discussions extend beyond completion of dissertation and DNP projects to career opportunities. As noted by Travers and colleagues regarding DNP and PhD students, knowing about types of programs can advance their understanding of different programs, and doctoral degrees, and support their current and future collaborations and respect for each other.

## Outreach and Engagement
Originally, notices of the meetings were sent to an organically generated list of nurses known to be considering or pursuing doctoral study. Electronic notices are now sent to all nurses employed at CNH. Current members are a significant source of recruitment as are the doctoral nurses at CNH. Other methods used to inform nurses of the DSG include an article in our internal Division of Nursing publication, nursing research workshops held during Nurses' Week, and poster presentations and sessions at the annual system-wide Research, Innovation, and Education Week.
Using a variety of methods to disseminate information about the DSG to the entire CNH system provides visibility of the DSG throughout the health system and informs all nurses, physicians, and others that CNH supports doctoral education. Members have reported that the visibility of the group inspired them to pursue doctoral study. The existence of the DSG has been used as a recruitment mechanism for nurses considering employment at CNH.

## DSG Facilitators
The DSG is facilitated by 2 PhD-prepared nurses employed in the hospital's department of nursing science, professional practice, and quality who are active researchers and scholars with academic and health system experience. Institutional commitment to the group is evident as the leaders are assigned to facilitate the monthly DSGs and are available year-round to coordinate meetings and engage individually with group members for career and project guidance. The nurse scientists demonstrate potential postdoctoral roles. The cofacilitators have a perspective that maintains hospital and academic boundaries, which ensures their focus on members' progression in school, navigating school and work, and providing consultation on navigating hospital procedures regarding necessary research ethics review or implementation of their projects within the organization. Academic counseling is not provided because this is under the purview of each member's respective university. Before each DSG meeting, the cofacilitators plan potential topics for discussion or have an open meeting for topics identified by members.

## Discussion
The value of the DSG to CNH is evident as the members describe learning from their doctoral work that they apply to their leadership and clinical practice roles and work. Members pursuing DNP capstone projects conduct translation projects relevant to their practice that introduce timely practice changes. Dissertation and Capstone project findings are used to inform new programs or changes to existing programs. Members describe applying a more systematic approach to new projects, for example, completing thorough literature reviews identifying what is known and where the gaps in knowledge persist and translating science into practice. They describe new ways for understanding phenomena and for explaining the need for change and state that as they gain tools for intervention and change, they sense a new energy for their practice (Supplemental Digital Content 1, http://links.lww.com/JONA/B240). Members report that their discussions in the DSG serve as an incubator for ideas and innovation. As members describe their doctoral experiences, others learn about opportunities they can pursue within their programs and for their dissertations or DNP projects.

## Documenting Nurses in Doctoral Programs and With Doctoral Degrees
Accurately documenting the number of nurses in doctoral study and the number of nurses with doctoral degrees in the CNH system has been challenging, given that the reporting structure for many of these nurses is not within the Division of Nursing but instead through individual medical specialty departments. This challenge has been addressed by adding information about nurses pursuing doctoral education and those with doctorates to the annual nursing survey. These data can be used to determine doctoral student time to graduation, retention, and role changes within the CNH system. By having information about nurses with doctoral degrees and those in doctoral study, CNH can better assist them to access opportunities to advance their research and identify research teams and other collaborators. These data may be used to support new positions for doctoral-prepared nurses and can identify the continuing career guidance needs for the DSG graduates including support for transition from clinical practice to research careers. Having these data can ensure all doctoral graduates are informed of the CNH resources for research and scholarship (Table ). With the diversity of doctoral programs completed by the nurses, these graduates assume a broad range of roles and program leadership including nurse scientist as described in the literature. , , 

## CNH Doctoral Nurses
As a resource, the DSG demonstrates the organization's commitment to doctoral students during and after their doctoral education. From 2008 to date, most nurses who have completed their doctoral studies continue employment in the hospital system. Since 2009, 23 nurses have qualified for faculty rank at the academic affiliate university, the George Washington University School of Medicine and Health Sciences. In the 11 years of the Annual Celebration of Scholarly Writing in Nursing, the number of publications presented surpasses 200. System impact by hospital nurses with doctoral degrees is further evidenced by multiple programs initiated or led by nurses with doctoral degrees. Examples are as follows: 1) specialty clinics ; 2) a hospital-wide employee mindful mentors' program with 150 hospital staff trained to date to benefit patient and employee well-being ; 3) a hospital-wide employee pediatric palliative care program with more than 200 staff members trained in palliative care strategies for patients and their families ; and 4) the Nursing Safety Plan and associated Nursing Safety Curriculum and support of unit-based RN safety teams. 

## Hospital Resources for Postdoctoral Support of New Doctoral-Prepared Nurses
Opportunities to conduct programs of research following completion of a doctoral program are important to advance science, retain doctoral-prepared nurses, and improve care and system outcomes. Doctoral-prepared nurses need support as they transition and navigate across clinical roles and new research roles. There are few examples in the literature describing the roles of doctoral nurses in hospitals , , , and structured programs supporting nurses with earned doctorates to advance their programs of research in clinical settings. , Doctoral-prepared nurses require an environment that supports them to apply their new research knowledge and scholarship to their existing or new roles.
CNH is developing more structure for postdoctoral support including a Nursing Science Collaborative monthly meeting that is open to all nurses with doctorates. The collaborative focuses on individual and shared research initiatives at the level of variable/s, theory/ies, and questions/hypotheses. This group, led by a senior nurse scientist, strengthens the environment of inquiry to advance research and scholarship and to conduct, disseminate, and translate practice relevant research. Through the Nursing Science Collaborative, doctoral graduates identify collaborators, nurse mentors with doctorates, opportunities for research role integration, and socialization for the inclusion of the new role of scientist into their work. This also serves as a means for doctoral-prepared nurses within the Division of Nursing across medical-based practice environment to continue collaborative efforts that impact the patients, families, and the organization. The collaborative addresses the infrastructure needs for research as described by Lancaster and colleagues. 

## Conclusion
Members of the DSG voice that they pursued their doctorates to advance their knowledge and skills with the intent to remain in the hospital organization. The CNH DSG, as a resource to all nurses interested in or pursuing doctoral education, demonstrates CNH commitment to doctoral education and is one aspect for the retention of nurses with doctoral degrees. From review of documents from the DSG's 18-year tenure, these authors have identified strategies for successfully sustaining the DSG. Strategies include institutional support, comprehensive infrastructure for research and scholarship, DSG structure, members, outreach and engagement, facilitators for group coordination, and programs for support beyond degree completion. These strategies can serve as a guide for other organizations to develop similar support for their nurses who are interested in or pursuing doctoral education.
