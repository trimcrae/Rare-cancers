## Abstract
The SPIN-CHAT Program was designed to support mental health among individuals with systemic sclerosis (SSc; commonly known as scleroderma) and at least mild anxiety symptoms at the onset of COVID-19. The program was formally evaluated in the SPIN-CHAT Trial. Little is known about program and trial acceptability, and factors impacting implementation from the perspectives of research team members and trial participants. Thus, the propose of this follow-up study was to explore research team members’ and trial participants’ experiences with the program and trial to identify factors impacting acceptability and successful implementation. Data were collected cross-sectionally through one-on-one, videoconference-based, semi-structured interviews with 22 research team members and 30 purposefully recruited trial participants (Mage = 54.9, SD = 13.0 years). A social constructivist paradigm was adopted, and data were analyzed thematically. Data were organized into seven themes: (i) getting started: the importance of prolonged engagement and exceeding expectations; (ii) designing the program and trial: including multiple features; (iii) training: research team members are critical to positive program and trial experiences; (iv) offering the program and trial: it needs to be flexible and patient-oriented; (v) maximizing engagement: navigating and managing group dynamics; (vi) delivering a videoconference-based supportive care intervention: necessary, appreciated, and associated with some barriers; and (vii) refining the program and trial: considering modification when offered beyond the period of COVID-19 restrictions. Trial participants were satisfied with and found the SPIN-CHAT Program and Trial to be acceptable. Results offer implementation data that can guide the design, development, and refinement of other supportive care programs seeking to promote psychological health during and beyond COVID-19.
Keywords: Qualitative, Experiences, Scleroderma, Virtual, Remote

A videoconference-based supportive care intervention offered at the onset of COVID-19 was deemed acceptable. Important insights were shared that can guide the design, development, and refinement of other supportive care programs seeking to promote psychological health during and beyond COVID-19.

## Implications

Practice: The SPIN-CHAT Program was acceptable for people with systemic sclerosis and could be adapted for ongoing use for this and other patient populations with relatively minor modifications.

Policy: Videoconference-based supportive care programs should consider including multiple program features, ensuring well-selected and adequately trained staff, and offering participants flexibility.

Research: Further efforts are required to explore participants and research team members experiences within supportive care programs and trials to better understand factors impacting acceptability and implementation.

## INTRODUCTION
Concerns surrounding the impact of COVID-19 on mental health were raised early in the pandemic [ , ]. At a population-level, mental health was generally unchanged, or symptoms (such as anxiety) increased slightly, when pre-COVID-19 data were compared to data collected from the same populations during the pandemic [ ]. Amongst individuals with pre-existing chronic conditions for whom COVID-19 could be more severe, mental health declined [ , ]. Individuals with systemic sclerosis (SSc; commonly known as scleroderma) are one such population.
SSc is a rare disease, characterized by abnormal fibrotic processes affecting multiple organ systems including the skin, lungs, gastrointestinal tract, and heart [ ]. A recent meta-analysis of 46 studies estimated the prevalence to be 18 per 100,000 (95% confidence interval [CI] 15–21 per 100,000), although there was substantial heterogeneity [ ]. Prevalence was approximately five times higher among females (28 per 100,000, 95% CI 23–34 per 100,000) than males (6 per 100,000, 95% CI 5–8 per 100,000) [ ]. Disease presentation is heterogeneous, and progression is highly unpredictable, but many individuals with SSc experience serious adverse physical (e.g., and function and mobility limitations, pain, fatigue, sleep problems, pruritus) and psychological effects (e.g., anxiety, stress, depression, body image distress) [ ]. People with SSc are at risk of serious COVID-19-related complications, if infected, due to high rates of interstitial lung disease (prevalence >40%) and immunosuppressant drug use [ , ]. Consequently, people with SSc were tasked with navigating their debilitating pre-existing condition alongside uncertainty and potentially declining mental health due to COVID-19 [ , ].
To address concerns of declining mental health amongst individuals with SSc early in the COVID-19 pandemic, the Scleroderma Patient-centered Intervention Network (SPIN [ , ] - an organization of researchers, healthcare providers, and individuals living with SSc around the world who examine problems identified as important by patients and develop, test, and disseminate programs to support coping and quality of life in this cohort), developed a multi-faceted, videoconference-based mental health program, the Scleroderma Patient-centered Intervention Network COVID-19 Home-isolation Activities Together (SPIN-CHAT) [ , ]. The SPIN-CHAT Program included leisure activities (e.g., ice breaker games; trivia, show and tell, bingo), education sessions covering SSc, COVID-19, and mental health coping skills and strategies (e.g., healthy information management, relaxation techniques), and group discussion and support. The program was offered over 4 weeks with 90-min sessions delivered three times per week. See https://osf.io/62vut/files/ for an overview of SPIN-CHAT Program content. The ­program was peer-led and delivered via videoconference using the GoToMeeting platform. A trial was conducted using a partially nested randomized controlled trial design to evaluate the effects of the program [ , ]. Findings from the SPIN-CHAT Trial include high attendance rates among the 86 participants allocated to the intervention (mean of 8.8 out of the 12 offered sessions; mean 10.0 out of the 12 offered sessions among 75 participants who attended at least one session). There were no statistically significant improvements in mental health outcomes among SPIN-CHAT Program (i.e., intervention) participants compared to waitlist control participants immediately post-intervention, but significant improvements in anxiety (standardized mean difference = 0.31, 95% CI 0.03–0.58) and depression symptoms (standardized mean difference = 0.31, 95% CI 0.07–0.55) were found in the intervention group compared with the waitlist control group 6 weeks post-intervention [ ]. Findings from a follow-up qualitative evaluation of the SPIN-CHAT Program suggest a range of additional mental health benefits among some participants, including reductions in feelings of boredom and stress and greater feelings of happiness and belonging [ ].
Other videoconference-based, supportive care programs with similar aims (though tailored for different populations) have since been developed, delivered, and evaluated [ , ] and rapid and scoping reviews [ , ] and a systematic review [ ] have been published summarizing this emerging evidence base. While findings suggest generally positive results, few have described acceptability or factors impacting program and trial implementation. Indeed, perceptions of acceptability (i.e., satisfaction), aspects of the SPIN-CHAT Program and Trial that were helpful (or unhelpful) and that served as barriers (or facilitators), and areas for improvement, have yet to be captured. Such information could support the ongoing development, refinement, implementation, and scale-up of this and other videoconference-based supportive care programs to reach larger and diverse populations in need of mental health support [ , ].
Gathering perspectives from research team members and participants involved in a videoconference-based supportive care program and trial could facilitate a deeper understanding of the factors impacting acceptability, and the strategies required to ensure successful implementation. Therefore, the objectives of this follow-up study were to explore research team members’ and trial participants’ experiences within the SPIN-CHAT Program and Trial to understand: (i) trial participants’ acceptance and satisfaction with the program and trial, (ii) research team members’ and trial participants’ perceptions of helpful and unhelpful aspects of the program and trial, (iii) research team members’ and trial participants’ perceptions of program and trial elements that facilitated engagement or served as barriers to participation, and (iv) research team members’ and trial participants’ thoughts regarding modifications that may be needed to improve the program and trial.

## METHODS
The qualitative data analyzed and reported herein were collected as part of a larger cross-sectional, multiple perspectives follow-up study exploring the SPIN-CHAT Trial [ ], which had two distinct foci resulting in two manuscripts (see Wurz et al. [ ]. for the other manuscript). A social constructivist paradigm [ ] was adopted for this study. This paradigm asserts that there are multiple mentally and socially created realities, and that knowledge is co-developed through the relationships in which we are embedded. In other words, this paradigm views reality as a construction by and of the mind and views knowledge as influenced by social relationships. As such, semi-structured interviews, which enabled research team members and trial participants to share their perspectives and express their realities were essential, and knowledge was viewed co-created. The consolidated criteria for reporting qualitative research (COREQ) checklist was followed to ensure explicit and comprehensive reporting [ ] (see ).

## Participants
Following relevant Research Ethics Board approvals, research team members were recruited using convenience sampling wherein the Principal Investigator (BDT) emailed all those involved in the design and delivery of the SPIN-CHAT Program and Trial to ascertain interest (i.e., research team members; n = 28). Research team members interested in taking part were directed to contact KE, DD, and MB to schedule an interview, with assurance that their participation in the follow-up study would not be revealed to the Principal Investigator (BDT) or other study team members.
Trial participants (n = 172 participants; n = 86 intervention group; n = 86 waitlist control group [ ]) were purposefully recruited to offer a range of perspectives based on sex, employment status, geographic location, age, race/ethnicity, education level, and allocation status (i.e., intervention or waitlist control group), attendance, trial adherence, and anxiety (primary outcome) and depression (one of five secondary outcomes) symptoms at pre- and post-program. All trial participants recruited (whether from intervention or waitlist control group) completed the SPIN-CHAT Program prior to being interviewed. Of note, intervention group participants received the SPIN-CHAT Program between April 20, 2020 to May 29, 2020 (intervention participants were assigned to one of 11 groups in week 1 [four groups; April 20, to May 15], week 2 [three groups; April 27, to May 22], or week 3 [four groups; May 4, to May 29]), and the last waitlist control group completed the SPIN-CHAT Program August 21, 2020.
Research team members included moderators, professional educators, and trial management staff. These individuals were eligible if they were involved in designing or delivering (i.e., facilitating, providing educational sessions) the SPIN-CHAT Program or carrying out trial logistics. Trial participants were screened and deemed eligible to participate if they: (i) were able to participate in group sessions delivered in English or French; (ii) score ≥ 55 on the Patient-Reported Outcomes Measurement Information System Anxiety 4a v1.0 [ ]; (iii) had not tested positive for COVID-19, since that could require different mental health support; and, (iv) confirmed they were not currently receiving psychotherapy or counseling services. All research team members and trial participants who participated in this study provided verbal informed consent after reviewing the consent form with the interviewer at the start of each interview.

## Data collection
Research team members’ position or title was collected, and the data for trial participants, including age, sex, race or ethnicity, educational and employment status, geographic location, intervention or waitlist status, SPIN-CHAT Program attendance, trial adherence, and anxiety and depression symptoms, were extracted from the larger trial dataset. Following this, SPIN-CHAT research team members and trial participants took part in private, one-on-one, semi-structured interviews exploring their experiences related to SPIN-CHAT Program and Trial design and delivery with questions focused on markers of feasibility and implementation. The interview guide was developed by and piloted with the follow-up study research team prior to use (see for the finalized interview guide).
All interviews took place using GoToMeeting at a convenient time for research team members and trial participants and were performed by female graduate students who, in addition to completing relevant course work covering qualitative methodologies and study-specific training, had also been involved with delivering physical activity education within the SPIN-CHAT Program (KE, DD) or had personal experience living with SSc (MB). This enabled the interviewers to foster rapport with the research team members and trial participants who took part in this follow-up study—a requisite of strong qualitative inquiry, even via videoconference wherein establishing rapport can be challenging [ ]. Of note, those involved in delivering the physical activity education sessions made every effort to ensure they did not interview trial participants from their education sessions. However, in five cases this was not possible, as participants were given the option to complete the interview in the language of their choice (French or English), and only one research team member was available to interview participants in French (KE). Further, interviewers emphasized to participants that they did not design the program or trial, but rather were tasked with evaluating it to minimize potentially desirable responses. No repeat interviews were carried out.

## Data analysis
Quantitative data collected to describe the sample were analyzed descriptively using SPSS (Version 26). Interviews were audio-recorded, transcribed verbatim, anonymized, and then analyzed following the iterative sequential steps outlined by Braun and Clarke [ ]. First, the authors (AW, KE, DD, MB) familiarized themselves with the data by reading and re-reading each transcript several times. Following this, the authors coded each transcript using inductive and deductive elements following principles of interpretive description and guidance for reflective thematic analysis [ , ]. Inductive analysis involved bottom-up appraisals whereby themes were derived from the raw data and participants’ unique perspectives. Deductive reasoning was then incorporated to align interpretations with results from the trial, previous literature, and prominent health promotion theory (e.g., [ ]). Research team member and trial participant perspectives were initially coded independently to enable each groups’ reality to be centered. Once authors completed their initial coding, they met to discuss and compare their codes, reflect on the overlap between research team members’ and trial participants perspectives’, and begin working towards theme and subtheme development. Through an iterative process consisting of critical dialogue, exploring alternative understandings and interpretation of the data, developing semantic level themes, and comparing perspectives, penultimate themes and subthemes were developed. These were then shared with two senior authors (BDT, NCR), co-authors, and patient partners within SPIN to review for internal homogeneity and external heterogeneity. Upon receiving feedback, further revisions were made, the raw data were revisited, and the final themes and subthemes were produced. To enhance the transparency of this follow-up study, several strategies recommended in the literature were used, including developing rich descriptions, describing each step of the research process, working with stakeholders, and practicing reflexivity [ , ].

## RESULTS

## Participants
Of the 28 research team members initially contacted, 25 responded, and 22 agreed to participate. Research team members who participated were representative, in terms of roles, to those who chose not to participate, and were classified as moderators (n = 12; 52%, of which 10 were individuals living with SSc and 2 were involved with patient organizations), professional educators (n = 4; 16%), and trial management staff (n = 6; 32%). Of the 40 trial participants initially contacted, 30 participants, who were on average 54.9 (SD = 13.0; range = 36–70) years of age at the time of the SPIN-CHAT Trial took part in this follow-up (see for participant demographics). Most participants were female (n = 25; 83%) and White (n = 20; 67%). A range of education and employment statuses were reported, though the majority reported between 11 and 15 years of education (n = 13; 43%) and not working (n = 20; 67%). Participants resided across 10 countries, with most residing in North America (i.e., Canada [n = 7; 23%] and the United States of America [n = 6; 20%]). In terms of SPIN-CHAT Program attendance, most participants had high rates of attendance; ≥10 of 12 sessions (n = 25; 83%), consistent with findings from the trial [ ]. Last, participants’ anxiety (Mbaseline = 62.18, 95% CI 55.80–71.20) and depression (Mbaseline = 7.63, 95% CI 1.00–23.00) symptoms (though ranging in severity), on average, represented moderate to high anxiety and depression symptoms. See [ ] for full SPIN-CHAT Trial results.

## Main results
Research team members’ and trial participants’ interviews lasted on average 36:52 min (range of approximately 14–53 min) and 58:32 min (range of approximately 27–101 min), respectively and were conducted between October 2, 2020 and December 4, 2020. Data gathered during these interviews offered insights to furnish an understanding of factors impacting acceptability and implementation, including satisfaction, program and trial barriers and facilitators, and suggested modifications that could improve design and delivery of the SPIN-CHAT Program and Trial or other similar programs and trials. Both research team members and trial participants shared their experiences which resulted in seven interconnected themes (described in detail below). In instances where text was omitted to enhance clarity of what was said, “[…]” was used. Additional supporting quotations are included in .

## Getting started: the importance of prolonged engagement and exceeding expectations

## Research team members’ perspectives
Research team members discussed their positive experiences designing and delivering the SPIN-CHAT Program and Trial, their sense of teamwork and comradery, and their appreciation of the larger SPIN infrastructure (i.e., the organization of researchers, health care providers, and people living with scleroderma from around the world), which they deemed as critical for the smooth design and execution of the program and trial. Research team members reported feeling supported personally and in their delivery of the SPIN-CHAT Program and Trial.

The [SPIN-CHAT Program and Trial] were so well-structured […]. You always knew there was someone that would be there to help you. […] We just ran as a team. […] We worked together, and I think that’s why it was so successful. It was just well run.

Research team members described that they had weekly meetings and considered these to be a useful venue to discuss challenges with program or trial delivery and to share ideas for ongoing adaptation and improvement of the SPIN-CHAT Program and Trial. Research team members also used this as a time to discuss what was working well within the program and trial and strategies they were using with their participants.

Meetings were organized at the end of each week, and I think those were very helpful […]. It was a good way for the facilitators, the educators, and the moderators all get together and discuss any major issues, like what was going on with the different groups [and] provide advice and tips.

Finally, research team members implied that participants were satisfied with the program and trial, though this was not explicitly stated.

## Trial participants’ perspectives
Participants acknowledged the time and work that went into the SPIN-CHAT Program and Trial and shared their appreciation for SPIN-CHAT Trial research team members’ efforts. Many participants described prolonged engagement with SPIN and recounted taking part in other SPIN opportunities (e.g., research, programs). Participants felt their prior positive experiences enhanced their support for the SPIN-CHAT Program and willingness to enroll in the SPIN-CHAT Trial.

I really wanted to be a part of it [the SPIN-CHAT Program and Trial] because I’ve been a part of so many other things with SPIN and they were all positive experiences, so I figured, I’m going to try this too.

Beyond this, the consistent and personalized communication from research team members strongly influenced participants’ satisfaction with the SPIN-CHAT Program and Trial. Indeed, participants described consistently having their expectations met or exceeded.

Well, I think that it [SPIN-CHAT Program] exceeded my expectations. […] I found it beneficial and educational.

## Designing the program and trial: including multiple features

## Research team members’ perspectives
Research team members reflected on each component (i.e., leisure activities, education, and support group discussion) within the SPIN-CHAT Program and shared the intentionality behind each. Although most research team members felt the leisure activities were important to foster rapport and promote engagement, some would have preferred to use different activities or have the choice as to when and whether to include activities each session.

I liked the icebreakers at the beginning […]. They made us all laugh and giggle and they also gave us insight into each of the members of our group.

With regards to the education component, research team members reported that offering participants a chance to gain knowledge and skills was central to the overarching goal of the program. Topics were carefully selected to address the mental health needs of individuals affected by SSc during the COVID-19 pandemic, including information and techniques to help participants manage worry, promote relaxation, engage in exercise, navigate information, and others.

I think they [education topics] were all useful, and I think it was very timely information, you know, given COVID-19 and given the level of isolation that everybody was in […]. These are tips and tools that our patient population can use every day of their lives […]. It’s something that they can keep in their back pocket and continue to use.

Finally, research team members relayed that the group discussion and support component at the end of each session served to promote socialization amongst participants, while reinforcing the knowledge gained in the educational component.

That last 30 minutes of us going over those things [education topics] and talking […] was perfect, I wouldn’t change it.

Overall, research team members felt that these components afforded participants a positive experience.

## Trial participants’ perspectives
When reflecting on the three components comprising each session, including leisure activities, education, and group discussion and support, participants felt each component was distinct, helpful, and important to their overall experience within the program. Consequently, most participants described high levels of satisfaction and acceptance.

I think it was really well run. I really enjoyed the format. Just warm-up exercises to get everybody comfortable and talking and then having the more information in the middle. […] And then just kind of a debriefing at the end with the group […], everything, I thought, was really useful.

Nevertheless, some participants were less enthusiastic about the leisure activities, particularly the icebreaker activities. In this case, participants indicated that they would have preferred shorter icebreaker durations or for the icebreakers to be used only in the first few sessions and then omitted thereafter once the group knew one another.

The icebreakers were good, sometimes maybe a little long […]. Once the group was established, you didn’t need the icebreakers as long.

All participants appreciated the educational component and understood that the purpose of this component was to instill knowledge, confidence, and skills. Participants appreciated the content in general but expressed particular gratitude for the meditation and relaxation techniques, exercise tips, and strategies to manage worry. Despite the overwhelmingly positive response, some participants desired further information related to their condition and navigating interpersonal relationships.

I find that the one area where I need more help is with my immediate family. And how to explain things to [them]. Using the right words so that I’m not trying to explain frightening things to them so that their ears close down on me, [because] I’m not having much success in finding ways to tell people that are close to me, ‘I’m having a bad day, bear with me’. So, I think if there were one more [aspect] to add to all the support that was offered.

Finally, the discussion component was received as intended by participants as a chance to integrate the knowledge instilled and to connect with other participants.

The chance afterwards [to discuss] with everybody in the group. That was sort of inspiring you to get out and do stuff.

## Training: research team members are critical to positive program and trial experiences

## Research team members’ perspectives
The importance of competently leading each group was described as necessary to ensure engagement amongst participants, navigate difficult group dynamics, and ensure program and trial success.

I made sure that there was a space to exchange. Sometimes if a person was talking too much, I would raise my hand as I really wanted the time to be shared among the participants during the discussion period.

Research team members described that having educators who were content experts and who were empathetic and understanding of participants’ needs was helpful, and may have contributed to the program and trial’s success.

I think the experts delivered their content with understanding […]. It came across as really understanding what the participants were going through in their lives. And I think that was really important […]. I think everybody felt like the educators were there for them.

## Trial participants’ perspectives
Participants described how the SPIN-CHAT research team members were critical to promote feelings of satisfaction with the program and trial. Participants alluded to the importance of well-trained research team members and spoke at length about their appreciation for and respect of all facilitators (i.e., the moderators, professional educators, and general trial management staff).

The facilitators were all fantastic. I mean, really, every single one […]. They [the moderator and educators] tried very hard to include each person, like if someone hadn’t talked, they would call on that person kind of thing. I could tell they made an effort to be inclusive for everybody.

Though all research team members were deemed important, participants emphasized the importance of knowledgeable “experts” and felt it was important for content to be delivered with empathy and understanding for their SSc experience.

I really felt that I was surrounded by a qualified team around me who had the skills to be able to help me. That’s it!

## Offering the program and trial: it needs to be flexible and patient-oriented

## Research team members’ perspectives
Research team members commented that they felt it was important to offer participants the opportunity to exert as much volition as possible to ensure the program and trial met the needs of individuals with SSc. Research team members sought to be as flexible as possible throughout the program and trial and displayed empathy and understanding when participants had SSc- or COVID-19-related barriers to attending. Though this was challenging at times (e.g., scheduling times), research team members felt it was worth it.

Some participants really wanted to take part, but certain days of the week, they weren’t available. We wanted to be adaptable and allow that. We also wanted to show that we understand that there are certain conditions, like times when people might be hospitalized during our meeting time, but still want to take part […]. We wanted to make it understood that if ever participants couldn’t attend, they could just let us know and we have the recording available. So, they can always catch up.

Further, research team members described the critical nature of the patient-oriented research approach taken when designing the SPIN-CHAT Program and Trial. From the perspective of research team members, the patient advisory team played a critical role in identifying important trial outcomes, education topics, and design features. Including the patient-perspective while designing the SPIN-CHAT Program and Trial enhanced relevance and increased the likelihood that the program would address the needs of people with SSc during COVID-19.

We identified key outcomes that were very much informed by the patient advisory team and members of the community. It was really about our population [individuals affected by SSc] and the things that people with scleroderma were most concerned about going into the pandemic.

## Trial participants’ perspectives
Similar to research team members, participants mentioned the importance of flexibility in the program and trial. However, participants extended this perspective, sharing deeper insights into the role flexibility played for their enrollment and continued attendance. For example, when research team members were willing to make accommodations, such as allowing absences for medical appointments, participants felt cared for, which in turn made them want to come back.

While I was in the hospital, like two weeks after the transplant, the [research team members] were really good about it. I had emailed them and said, “yeah I’m not going to be able to join”, because this was going on for me. They said “okay, whenever you’re ready. If you feel like you can manage it, just feel free to rejoin anytime”. Then, they emailed me afterwards just to say, “we’re just hoping you’re doing well and letting you know we’re still meeting and you’re still welcome to come join us” and so I made a point of doing that from the hospital […]. So, that worked really well for me.

Participants went on to explain that this flexibility, though deemed minor by research team members, was a critical feature to their positive perspective of the program and trial. Some participants agreed with research team members when they shared the sentiment that timing was a barrier and would have liked even more options for times and days, but understood this might not have been possible given sessions were coordinated across a wide range of time zones.

Not necessarily a barrier, but a challenge of mine was that the program was at like 7:30 or 8:30 at night, which was the most difficult [time of] day for me.

## Maximizing engagement: navigating and managing group dynamics

## Research team members’ perspectives
Research team members underscored the importance of the group-based nature of the SPIN-CHAT Program and Trial. Research team members described their opinions about group sizes and subsequent engagement. Research team members expressed varied opinions on an ideal number of group members. In general, smaller groups (3 and under) were viewed as more challenging to manage; however, there was some variation in opinions across research team members and no consistent “top-end” group size was presented. Overall, most research team members felt the groups they led or were part of, were a good size.

Another good part of the SPIN-CHAT Program design was the smaller group sizes. Although the sizes varied in the groups, I found that around 4 to 7 participants was a nice sweet spot that was really good for being able to know everyone pretty well and they felt comfortable with the facilitator.

Further, research team members commented on how they enjoyed the diverse ages, countries of origin, and backgrounds of participants in the groups.

I think the way we made the groups, having them so diverse with people from all over the world was something very unique and different from other programs. And I think that allowed for more excitement in the groups […]. I remember from some of the discussions I had with my group, people would say, if you ever came to [location], come and visit me, or if you ever come, when we can travel again, come see me.

## Trial participants’ perspectives
Aligning with the research team members’ perspective, participants reported that group size and dynamics impacted their satisfaction with and engagement in the program and trial. Like research team members, many participants thought their group size, which ranged between 6 and 10 participants, was ideal for engagement.

My [group size] was a good number. Everybody got the chance to talk. When it’s too many people, then everybody might not be able to say something. And if it’s too little, then everybody has to just to think of something to say.

Nevertheless, some participants explicitly mentioned experiencing difficult group dynamics wherein one or two participants dominated conversations. In these cases, there was a desire for better moderation.
For the most part, participants also enjoyed the diversity within the groups (e.g., different backgrounds, ages, disease progressions). Nevertheless, some participants expressed mixed feelings about being in groups with differing demographics and disease progressions.

I think most of my concern was [disease status]. I am newly diagnosed, and the rest were like oldies in the disease […]. I think probably if we are of the same time of diagnosis, it would be helpful. But for me, I liked it when there are others who were previously diagnosed because I feel more comfortable asking them [questions about the disease]. And sometimes, even when the doctors didn’t have the answers, and they would say ‘oh, well this one happened to me and you’ll be fine’, so it was reassuring.

## Delivering a videoconference-based supportive care intervention: necessary, appreciated, and associated with some barriers

## Research team members’ perspectives
Research team members described the advantages of videoconference delivery during and beyond COVID-19, which centered on overcoming common barriers to supportive care intervention access (e.g., immune system concerns, small and spread-out population) and providing opportunities for participants to continue accessing videoconference resources and recorded sessions.

Virtually, what I’m finding in general right now, is that virtual works […]. To have run this program and trial any other way would not have worked […]. For things like this, I think it [virtual delivery] is wonderful […]. Keeping it accessible to everyone is great.

While using videoconference was seen as beneficial, research team members faced challenges navigating the technology used. Research team members described Information Technology (IT) concerns for themselves, and participants. Looking ahead, research team members commented on the need for pre-trial IT training for both research team members and participants.

In the beginning, […] I felt like I needed more training. I didn’t know what to expect from the program and trial. So, I would have liked a couple of like mock trainings of the 90-min sessions. We did a lot of general training on this program and trial, but I would have liked even more training on how to log on, share your screen and all that.

## Trial participants’ perspectives
Participants reported similar advantages to videoconference delivery, including the reduction or elimination of common barriers and enhanced (and ongoing) access to resources.

It helped being on video. I do have a regular support group and because we physically met, it was a lot harder. It’s easier to get myself up for a video chat then to get myself up and out of the house.

While participants believed videoconference delivery was beneficial, like research team members, many had trouble navigating the technology. Participants spoke about the ways in which these challenges were handled by the research team, and greatly appreciated the IT support they received. Participants did not share any ideas on how to overcome barriers of videoconference delivery.

The first time logging on I think it was challenging for everybody. The IT team was great. Sometimes getting everybody on at the same time took a bit because we’re all at different tech levels […]. I think it was good on SPIN’s part that they had that resource [IT team]. So, worse case scenario, you’re supposed to be on, we could call this number and be guided.

## Refining the program and trial: considering modification when offered beyond the period of COVID-19 restrictions

## Research team members’ perspectives
Research team members believed that the program and trial, as it was initially developed at the onset of the COVID-19 pandemic, was manageable for participants given they had more availability and time due to the imposed restrictions and regulations of COVID-19.

The layout of the whole program and trial was great, I think the time frame was appropriate. I think an hour and half was excellent, any more than that I don’t think it would work, I think it was just the right time.

Research team members commented that the frequency and duration might be too much for participants beyond early COVID-19 restrictions and regulations. Varied suggestions were offered, with most suggesting a program and trial offered approximately 2 times/week, for 60-min sessions, over 6+ weeks.

It did feel like a lot the way that we had it, three days a week, it did start to become a little bit heavy […]. I don’t really think that affected attendance in our group, but I know people were feeling that way.

## Trial participants’ perspectives
Although many participants echoed research team members when they spoke about their satisfaction with the frequency and duration of the program, and how it was appropriate given the increased time they had during COVID-19, some felt that the program and trial design was overwhelming due to other commitments (e.g., work, family, other).

It was pretty intense. But then, like I said, we had nothing else to do. So that was fine, and it really gave us an opportunity to learn about one another. And three times a week for an hour and a half, it was pretty tiring, initially.

Further, aligned with research team members’ perceptions, participants reported the frequency and duration would not be feasible for them after the COVID-19 pandemic, but they offered a number of recommendations for how the program and trial might adapt beyond the COVID-19 pandemic. Recommendations included refining the program and trial structure to offer multiple, 60-min sessions/week over 6–8 weeks to facilitate their attendance, while still offering an intensive experience and support.

I think the intensity or the amount of times per week was a lot […]. I’d say maybe once a week is good.

## DISCUSSION
This follow-up study sought to collate multiple perspectives to elucidate factors impacting acceptability and implementation of the videoconference-based SPIN-CHAT Program and Trial. Trial participants found the SPIN-CHAT Program and Trial to be highly acceptable. Both research team members and trial participants found nearly all elements of the SPIN-CHAT Program and Trial methods to be helpful in some way. However, research team members and trial participants also shared important considerations for improving the design and delivery of this supportive care program and trial and offered insights that may be useful for others seeking to develop videoconference-based supportive care programming.
Trial participants’ positive perception of the acceptability of the SPIN-CHAT Program and Trial were perhaps unsurprising given the documented need for such programs amidst the COVID-19 pandemic [ ]. Findings extend reports of highly acceptable supportive care programs offered via videoconference prior to COVID-19 [ , ] and early reports describing satisfaction with trials offered during COVID-19 [ ]. The program elements: leisure activities (to promote group cohesion, engagement, and comfortability), education (to provide information vetted by experts), and group discussion and support (to facilitate socialization) were generally appreciated by trial participants and should be considered in future iterations of this program or others. Including multiple program elements not only aligns with current recommendations for designing health promotion programs [ ], but seemingly can ensure a greater proportion of trial participants’ needs are met. Further, research team members and trial participants in this sample described the patient-oriented research approach as being critical for program alignment (i.e., aligning with the needs of individuals with SSc). Using a patient-oriented research approach has been recommended to develop programs that are desired and needed by patients [ ]. Looking ahead, including multiple program components and continuing to meaningfully engage patients will be critical for the successful design and implementation of supportive care programs.
In terms of practical contributions for others wishing to offer similar videoconference-based supportive care programming, providing training to moderators, selecting knowledgeable experts, and offering participants flexibility will be necessary to ensure program success. Moderators in this follow-up study had completed the SPIN-SSLED Program, which was evaluated in the SPIN-SSLED Trial [ ], wherein they received the comprehensive, 13-week SPIN-SSLED Program with weekly, videoconference-based modules (90-min session) covering topics such as “The Support Group Leader’s Role”, “Structuring Meetings”, “Managing Group Dynamics”, and “Supporting Yourself as a Leader”. This training recognizes that moderators need support to acquire knowledge, skills, and confidence to effectively facilitate support groups, while minimizing logistical and emotional burden. The SPIN-SSLED Program may serve as a useful starting point for those seeking to support their moderators across settings and with other populations given the generality of modules (only one is SSc-specific). However, the time- and resource-intensive nature of the SPIN-SSLED Program and context- and population-specific factors would need to be considered to affirm feasibility and appropriateness. Further, despite having received this comprehensive training, some moderators described desiring additional training to equip them to manage difficult group dynamics. This notion underscores the necessity of equipping moderators with ongoing training support to bolster their knowledge, skills, and confidence to manage group dynamics. With regards to choosing knowledgeable experts, spending time identifying educators or therapists with substantive knowledge, appropriate credentials, and who are empathetic and understanding is warranted [ ]. Finally, flexibility is vital when working with patients with chronic diseases who must manage additional barriers to program attendance, such as medical appointments or episodic disease flares [ , ]. The trial participants who took part in this follow-up study felt research team members did well accounting for their barriers to attendance, both in their supportive demeanor towards participants and through the flexibility of videoconference delivery. Indeed, despite minor technological barriers (which were addressed by the research team), videoconference delivery was necessary given COVID-19 and was deemed practical moving forward given the small and spread-out population affected by SSc who must juggle competing demands [ ].
This follow-up study makes important contributions to research and provides guidance for others wishing to design supportive care programs for individuals affected by SSc or other chronic diseases. Specifically, findings highlight the importance of prolonged and meaningful patient ­engagement, incorporating multiple program features, adequately training research team members, and ensuring a flexible and patient-oriented approach. Nevertheless, there are important considerations to take into account when interpreting the findings from this follow-up study. First, although a diverse sample was sought via purposeful recruitment methods, the sample was still overwhelmingly White and female, reflective of participant characteristics from the SPIN-CHAT Trial and the SSc population in general [ , ]. Further, the perspectives of those with the lowest attendance could not be reached for participation in this qualitative follow-up study. Second, given the nature of the evaluation of this program and trial, wherein research team members and trial participants have longstanding relationship with the larger SPIN organization, it is plausible that many may have been less willing to disclose negative design components. Efforts were made to mitigate this by ensuring anonymity and disclosing that their perspectives would not impact these longstanding relationships. Third, interviews were conducted by three different interviewers with different backgrounds and experiences. Though each interviewer underwent extensive qualitative research training, variations across interview delivery and probing styles are to be expected. Fourth, based on limited resources and access to translators, French interviews were conducted by one of the professional educators that delivered the “exercising at home” education topic (KE). Thus, trial participants who received sessions with KE may have been less willing to disclose critical feedback to her. Finally, there were some technical issues in selected interviews that made it challenging to understand some participants, and thus more arduous to develop rapport.
Taken together, findings from this follow-up qualitative study provide important evidence indicating support for the SPIN-CHAT Program and Trial, which exceeded research team members’ and participants’ expectations. Results also underscore the importance of multiple program elements, program alignment, well-selected and -trained staff, and flexibility. It is hoped that the findings from this work will be used to design and improve supportive care programs for individuals living with chronic diseases in the context of COVID-19 and beyond.
