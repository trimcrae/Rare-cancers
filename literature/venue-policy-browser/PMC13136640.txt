## Abstract

## Objectives
We assessed point-of-sale advertising for CBD in three states with different cannabis laws (i.e., medical/recreational use; medical only; CBD/low THC) to determine if CBD is marketed in ways that are prohibited by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA). Under FDA authority, CBD cannot be marketed with misleading advertising, as an unapproved drug, dietary supplement, food product, or FDA-approved/endorsed product.

## Methods
Between November 2021 and April 2022, two trained data collectors systematically documented point-of-sale advertising in 150 retailers using wearable imaging in Colorado, Maryland, and North Carolina. Advertising was content analyzed to determine the types of cannabinoids, prohibited health claims, and medical conditions it claimed to affect.

## Results
We documented and content analyzed 1090 CBD-specific advertisements, resulting in 985 prohibited claims, representing all claim types. Among CBD-specific ads, over half (61.8%) contained at least one prohibited claim. CBD was frequently advertised as an unapproved drug (53.6%) and food additive (51.6%). Prohibited therapeutic claims were made for 125 different health conditions, including pain, inflammation, and cancer. Most retailers (80%) displayed at least one prohibited claim.

## Conclusions
Prohibited advertising for CBD, which may mislead consumers about its risks and benefits, is extensive at the point-of-sale, highlighting the need for enforcement efforts.

Keywords: Cannabidiol, cannabis, Marketing, Health claims, Point-of-sale

## Highlights
•Most CBD advertising at the point-of-sale includes prohibited marketing.

1.

## Introduction
The Agriculture Improvement Act of 2018, (a.k.a. Farm Bill), removed hemp as a Schedule I drug ( ), resulting in an increase of CBD-infused consumer goods to the marketplace. Products are found online, in small specialty shops and large mainstream retailers, often marketed with therapeutic claims for health conditions ( ). Approximately 46 million adults use CBD, despite the lack of scientific evidence on its safety and efficacy, and uncertainty about product quality ( ; ; ).
The Farm Bill also preserved the U.S. Food and Drug Administration's (FDA) authority to regulate products containing hemp or hemp-derived compounds ( ).The FDA restricts the marketing of products for therapeutic purposes that have not received premarket approval from the FDA's Center for Drug Evaluation and Research (CDER). Thus, product marketing cannot imply that CBD can prevent, diagnose, mitigate, treat or cure disease or other conditions without CDER approval. Federal law also prohibits marketing that misleads consumers or conveys that products are FDA-approved/endorsed. Lastly, since CBD is an active ingredient in a FDA-approved drug (Epidiolex) ( ), CBD cannot be marketed as a dietary supplement or be an ingredient in foods or beverages.
Despite these regulations, CBD market expansion has fueled consumer interest, with approximately 40% of US adults having tried CBD ( ; ).Approximately 21% report past year use ( ), and 15% of users report using daily or almost everyday ( ).Over half of past year users are ages 50 and older, likely due to the perception that CBD can alleviate symptoms for various health conditions ( ; ). About 60% of users report using CBD to treat medical conditions, most commonly pain, anxiety, and depression ( ; ). A recent study found 24% used CBD as an adjunct for at least one medication ( ), likely increasing the risk of drug interactions since CBD can interact with anti-epileptic drugs, antidepressants, opioids, and acetaminophen ( ; ). Importantly, standardized quality control efforts are lacking for hemp-derived and cannabis products, resulting in potentially inaccurate concentration labeling and heightening concern, as higher therapeutic doses of CBD increases the risk for drug interactions.
The FDA has issued warning letters to online retailers for marketing CBD with unsubstantiated claims and promotion as a drug for medical conditions, including preventing Alzheimer's disease and curing cancer ( ).While these claims have been documented online, physical retailers have been largely ignored, despite being where a majority of consumers report purchasing CBD ( ). Understanding the prevalence of misleading claims is important, as the limited research on cannabis marketing ( ; ; ; ) demonstrates that it mirrors nutritional and tobacco marketing that reduces harm perceptions, increases perceived benefits, and increases product purchase ( ; ). This could contribute to use, unintended consequences such as drug interactions, or delay in the use of approved therapies. Therefore, our objective was to document advertising about CBD in physical retailers in three policy environments and content analyze the advertisements to identify illicit claims using FDA regulations as a framework.

2.

## Methods
2.1.

## Study design
The U.S. does not currently have a mandatory licensing system for selling CBD products, and state licensing programs vary widely. Thus, we selected three states with different regulatory environments for cannabis as of July 2021: North Carolina, Maryland, and Colorado. North Carolina allowed for the sale of hemp-derived, CBD/low THC (i.e., 0.3% delta-9 THC concentration by dry weight) products ( ). Maryland and Colorado had comprehensive medical cannabis programs that permitted the use of cannabis for medicinal purposes, including hemp-derived CBD-only products. Colorado also had a state-regulated program for recreational cannabis.
We created a sampling frame by searching consumer-based directories, which have been used to identify cannabis and tobacco retailers in states with and without licensing ( ; ; ). We used Yelp and Google Maps since consumers routinely use these search engines to identify local retailers ( ; ). Because the search engines return results for specific locations (e.g., CBD shop near me), we identified the 25 largest cities in each state using U.S. Census data. City populations ranged from ∼34,500 to 857,500. Then, we searched each city using keywords CBD, cannabidiol, and hemp to identify potential retailers. No radius was used so that retailers in surrounding communities would also be identified. Yelp and Google Maps lists were combined, by state. Duplicates, grocery stores, convenience stores, pharmacies, and gas stations were removed since our goal was to assess specialty retailers that offered a variety of CBD products (i.e. oils, flower, edibles) and would most likely have product advertising. Licensed medical cannabis retailers were also removed because patrons must have a medical cannabis card to enter the establishment. To achieve a diverse sample, we allowed only one retailer per retail chain per state. To obtain a representative sample of retailers from urban and rural areas, we geocoded and mapped addresses with the urban area-based reference map. Urbanized areas were defined as having a population of >50,000 people ( ; ). Next, we randomly selected 50 retailers per state, proportional to the number of retailers in urban and rural places. Ten additional retailers were randomly selected as replacements. Retailers were contacted to verify hours and CBD availability.
Prior to conducting data collection, the study was approved by the Wake Forest University Health Sciences Institutional Review Board. Two trained data collectors completed unannounced retail assessments between November 2021 and April 2022, using wearable imaging technology that allows for the full retail environment to be photographically documented so no claims are missed. Previous studies have shown this technology's utility in conducting assessments due to data collection efficiency and post-assessment content analysis ( ). One data collector wore a lanyard with the wearable set to record 1 photo/1.5 s. Photographic data was systematically collected on the entirety of the interior and exterior storefront, provided “No Photography” signs were not present. The second data collector used a mobile device to document shop type (i.e., cbd/vape/dispensary), and age of sale/entry signs (yes/no). Upon completion, photographic data was uploaded to a laptop and reviewed immediately for quality. Photos were saved the of day capture to the institution's secured server.
To identify photos that contained an advertisement, we removed those that were blurry, did not contain an advertisement, contained duplicate advertisements, or were images of magazines, newspapers, price tags, or marketing for animal/pet products. Remaining photos were reviewed to ensure the advertisement focused on cannabis, marijuana, hemp, CBD, or other cannabinoids. Advertisements included any marketing (e.g. general information about CBD/other cannabinoids; specific products promoted) in the retail interior (e.g. posters, displays) and/or exterior (e.g. windows, store signage).
Measures.
We created a codebook based on existing literature and conducted a legal analysis to determine the relevant claim language from the Food, Drug and Cosmetic Act and FDA guidance documents ( ; ; ). We coded for the six claim types: unapproved drug effect, dietary supplement, food additive, FDA-approved/endorsed, false/misleading: youth-friendly, and false/misleading: science/medical, defined in .
 We conducted a content analysis, coding the advertisements for claim types, cannabinoids, and health conditions. Multiple codes for each ad were allowed. We created an online system for coding and trained four coders on the content analysis using 20 non-study images representing each of the claim types. Inter-rater reliability, as measured by Cohen's kappa, was K = 0.915 ( ). Three coders independently coded assigned sets of advertisements. The fourth coder independently reviewed and finalized advertisements that were flagged as needing additional review. Data was transferred from the online database to SAS® for analysis.
The assessments documented 36,492 photos ( ). After conducting the sorting procedure, 1392 photos containing 1938 advertisements for CBD and other cannabinoids were identified. This study is focused on 1090 CBD-specific advertisements.
 
2.2.

## Statistical analysis
Among CBD-specific advertisements, we tested whether the percentage of advertisements with at least one prohibited health claim differed between the three states. Among ads with at least one prohibited health claim, we tested whether the percentage of advertisements containing each claim type differed by state. Chi-squared tests were performed using a 0.05 significance level. Where the overall p-value was less than 0.05, we tested for differences between states by performing 2 × 2 Chi-squared tests for each pair of states. Similarly, among retailers, we tested for differences in any claim and claim types between states using Chi-squared tests. SAS® v 9.4 was used for all statistical analyses.

3.

## Results
Assessments were completed in 150 retailers (50/state); no retailers refused data collection. Nine retailers posted signs restricting photography and were replaced. As shown in , 36% of retailers were CBD shops, 49.3% vape shops, and 14.7% dispensaries. Most were located in urban areas and 60% posted age of entry signage.
 3.1.1.

## Advertisements
We documented 1938 advertisements in the assessed retailers. Of those, 1090 were specific to CBD. Most of the CBD advertisements were documented in North Carolina (n = 421, 38.6%), with 412 (37.8%) in Maryland and 257 (23.6%) in Colorado. At least one prohibited claim was documented in 61.8% (n = 674/1090) of the CBD advertisements ( ). Within the 674 advertisements, 985 prohibited claims were documented. While we documented fewer advertisements overall in Colorado, significantly more of the advertisements contained at least one prohibited claim, compared to North Carolina and Maryland (p = 0.02) (North Carolina: n = 241/421 (57.2%); Maryland: n = 259/412 (62.9%); Colorado: n = 174/257 (67.7%)).
 Unapproved drug effect claims were the most prevalent, documented in 53.3% of all advertisements with a prohibited claim (n = 359/674). Over half of the ads in Colorado and North Carolina (57.5%; 54.4%) displayed this claim, as did 49.4% of Maryland's ads.
For drug effect claims, we coded health conditions and symptoms the product stated it affected. CBD was marketed for 125 different conditions/symptoms, most often pain (n = 192), inflammation (n = 117), trouble sleeping (n = 112), anxiety (n = 102), stress (n = 67), nausea (n = 56), and cancer (n = 55) (Supplementary Table 1).
Food additive claims were documented in 51.6% of ads with a prohibited claim (n = 348).CBD was marketed as an ingredient in products including gummies, cookies and water. North Carolina had a significantly higher proportion of advertisements with food additive claims (59.6%) compared to Maryland (46.3%) and Colorado (48.9%) (p = 0.01).
Dietary supplement claims were documented in 11.1% of ads (n = 75/674). These claims were documented in 13.1% of ads in Maryland, 12.6% in Colorado, and 7.9% in North Carolina. There were no significant differences between states.
We documented 23 FDA-approved or FDA-endorsed claims (3.4%) that indicated products were “made in an FDA-registered facility/lab” or “includes FDA-approved ingredients.” FDA claims were found in 3.9% of ads in Maryland, 3.4% in Colorado and 2.9% in North Carolina.
Youth-friendly advertising was documented in 38 (5.6%) of all CBD advertisements with a prohibited claim. North Carolina had a significantly higher proportion of youth friendly claims (9.5%) compared to Maryland (5.0%) and Colorado (1.1%) (p <0.01).
We documented 142 (21.1%) misleading ads that used scientific imagery and phrases, such as Dr. Formulated. Maryland had a significantly higher percentage of these claims (28.6%) compared to Colorado (18.4%) and North Carolina (14.9%) (p < 0.01).

3.1.2.

## Retailers
Among all retailers (n = 150), 120 (80%) had advertisements with at least 1 prohibited claim type ( ). Colorado had the most retailers that displayed at least 1 prohibited claim (n = 43/50, 86%), followed by Maryland (n = 39/50, 78%) and North Carolina (n = 38/50, 76%). Unapproved drug effect claims were documented in 90 of the 150 retailers. North Carolina had the greatest number of retailers that displayed at least one advertisement containing an unapproved drug effect claim (n = 32 retailers), followed by 29 in both Maryland and Colorado. Over 100 retailers (n = 103, 68.7%) displayed food additive claims, including 36 retailers (72%) in Colorado, 34 (68%) in North Carolina, and 33 (66%) in Maryland. Dietary supplement claims were documented in 46 (30.7%) retailers, including 18 (36%) in Maryland, and 14 (28%) in both North Carolina and Colorado. Among all retailers, 13 (8.7%) displayed an FDA-approved/endorsed claim, including 5 (10%) in North Carolina, 5 (10%) in Maryland, and 3 (6%) in Colorado, while 12.7% displayed a youth-friendly ad. North Carolina had a significantly higher percentage of stores with youth-friendly claims (24%), compared to Colorado (4%) (p < 0.01). Scientific/medical claims were displayed at 58 retailers (38.7%), with the most documented in Maryland (n = 24, 48%), followed by North Carolina (n = 18, 36%) and Colorado (n = 16, 32%).
 

4.

## Discussion
This study is the first to document that CBD retailers are making a variety of prohibited claims about CBD products at the point-of-sale in various cannabis policy environments, despite existing regulations. Most retailers posted advertisements about CBD that contained at least one FDA-prohibited claim. To date, the FDA has only issued warning letters to online retailers, likely due to limited resources to conduct enforcement operations in physical stores. However, our findings demonstrate that enforcement is needed at the point-of-sale due to the high prevalence of illicit marketing.
Unapproved drug effect claims were the most prevalent, with approximately 60% of all retailers displaying them. These findings echo results of previous studies that showed CBD is marketed online with numerous unproven claims ( ; ), and that CBD marketing strategies are similar to online dispensaries, where 67% of retailers made medical claims for marijuana ( ). Unapproved drug effect claims are the most problematic because they may mislead consumers to believe CBD is a safe way to treat their health condition, despite limited evidence of effectiveness ( ; ). Of utmost concern is that patients may stop, delay, or forego approved therapies altogether to try CBD as an alternative. Furthermore, consumers have positive perceptions about CBD ( ), which coupled with unsubstantiated therapeutic claims, may increase their inclination to try CBD and expectations that it will treat their condition. This could increase the potential for drug interactions, as CBD can interact with prescription and over the counter medications, especially at higher, therapeutic doses ( ). While the extent of these behaviors is largely unknown, representing a significant gap in the literature, there is some indication it is occurring, as 24% report adjunct use of CBD and between 10 and 20% of CBD-users report replacing an existing medication entirely ( ; ).
We also found CBD illicitly marketed as a food additive and dietary supplement. While the FDA has issued warning letters to online retailers for promoting their products as adulterated food products ( ), we found that this is also an issue in physical retailers. Although food and dietary supplement claims are likely targeting a health-conscious population that would use CBD as a plant-based supplement, use is not without risk. Health-conscious consumers may use medications for acute conditions, increasing the risk of drug interactions with CBD. Limited evidence suggests about 30% of CBD users report taking CBD in addition to an existing medication ( ; ); more research is needed to understand if these consumers are influenced by CBD marketing in foods or supplements. Importantly, in 2023, the FDA indicated it would not pursue rulemaking allowing the use of CBD in dietary supplements or conventional foods, citing the lack of evidence to determine how much CBD can be consumed, and for how long, before causing harm ( ).Some states, including Colorado, allow cannabis and hemp-derived products to be sold as food and dietary supplements, highlighting key differences in current federal and state regulations that impact product availability and promotion ( ). In our study, we did not find significant differences between states for CBD marketed as a dietary supplement. However, North Carolina had a significantly higher proportion of ads with a food additive claim compared to Colorado and Maryland, suggesting that CBD is promoted less often as a food additive in states that have medical and/or recreational cannabis. More research is needed to determine if this is due to the availability of other cannabis products.
Scientific/medical imagery or phrases were documented in over one-third of the 150 assessed retailers (38.7%), potentially misleading consumers to believe that a scientific or medical basis exists for the health claims made about CBD. North Carolina had the fewest advertisements containing potentially misleading scientific or medical imagery/phrases compared to Maryland and Colorado, both of which have legalized medical cannabis. This suggests that in states where medical cannabis is legal, CBD marketers may use the state's regulatory stance to promote CBD as a medicinal product with medical and scientific phrases, despite limited evidence for CBD's effectiveness. More research is needed to understand how these claims influence consumer perceptions about the safety and efficacy of CBD.
Although youth-friendly claims were the least documented, likely due to CBD being a non-psychoactive cannabinoid and often marketed to older populations or those with chronic conditions, additional monitoring is warranted. The Farm Bill allows for hemp-derived synthetic cannabinoids (i.e. delta-8) that produce a psychoactive high. Several states, including Colorado, have banned these products citing safety concerns ( ; ). Although the FDA and Federal Trade Commission have issued joint letters, warning companies to stop marketing delta-8 products in ways that mimic youth-friendly brands and candy ( ), more research is needed to determine how often this occurs among physical retailers and in states with varying cannabis policies.
The enforcement of these marketing restrictions is important since advertising is an effective industry tool to influence consumer perceptions and behavior ( ; ). Our study demonstrates the need to include point-of-sale, as 80% of retailers displayed at least one prohibited claim. Approximately 74% of users report purchasing CBD from physical retailers ( ), signaling that point-of-sale is an important venue for communicating CBD risks and benefits to consumers. In addition, over half of advertisements contained at least one prohibited claim, regardless of state cannabis legalization status. Although the FDA has issued warning letters to online retailers for illicit claims, no warnings have been issued to physical retailers for these violations. This enforcement is needed because CBD, which is an FDA-approved drug to combat a rare form of epilepsy, is also being promoted as dietary supplements and food products that can be used daily. Coupled with illicit marketing as an unapproved drug for numerous health conditions, this type of marketing may influence use, cause confusion, and put consumers at increased risk.

5.

## Conclusions
Although US cannabis policy continues to change rapidly and there are state-to-state variations in terms of retail landscapes and consumer characteristics, our study provides a snapshot of how CBD is marketed in the US cannabis policy environment. Our estimates are likely conservative since we limited our sample to specialty retailers and the number of retailers from the same chain. While our study provides an estimate specifically for prohibited CBD claims, more research is needed to determine how often prohibited claims are used to promote other cannabinoids and its impact on consumer use.
