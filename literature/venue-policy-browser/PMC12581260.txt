## Background
Quality maternal care is crucial to improve outcomes for both mothers and newborns. Many initiatives to improve maternal care concentrate on improving clinical practice. However, women’s experiences of care are also important determinants of health outcomes. Establishing strong interpersonal relationships between health workers and women is essential for delivering high-quality person-centered care, with health workers who empathize with women, respect their needs and concerns, and communicate effectively. Aligned with the World Health Organization standards of care framework, this study aimed to explore women’s experiences of care during labour and childbirth, focusing on communication, respectful, and supportive care.

## Methods
A qualitative exploratory study was conducted with postpartum women in rural district hospitals in KwaZulu-Natal. Purposive sampling was used to recruit women from communities within the hospitals’ catchment areas. Data were collected through focus group discussions (FGDs) conducted in the local language of participants. Five FGDs were conducted between January and April 2023. Inductive thematic analysis using NVivo v12 was employed to analyze the data.

## Findings
A few women described positive experiences of care, but most women reported suboptimal care characterized by poor communication, lack of privacy, and disrespectful treatment. Participants described experiences of verbal and physical abuse, being called demeaning names, facing invasive procedures without providing consent, and inadequate emotional support, such as their concerns being systematically ignored and birth companions being denied entry to labour wards. Some women responded to these challenges through various coping mechanisms including staying quiet to avoid confrontation, following instructions rigidly, seeking advice from other women in the labour ward, and in some cases standing up for themselves. Some women described persistent anger and distress as a result of their experiences.

## Conclusion
The findings of the study highlight persistent gaps in effective communication and supportive care for women during labour and childbirth. Possible interventions could be aimed at improving communication skills of health workers as well as fostering a culture of empathy and respect for women in their care. In addition, empowering women through antenatal education and implementing birth companion policies could further improve women’s experiences during labour and childbirth.

## Background
Improving the quality of care in maternity units is vital to improve outcomes for mothers and newborns, particularly in under-resourced settings. Disrespectful care and abuse of women in labour and postpartum has been identified as a problem for decades [ ], but despite interventions aimed at improving the quality of maternal care, women in many settings continue to experience poor-quality and disrespectful care [ , ]. Poor experiences of care not only affect outcomes for mothers and newborns, but can also undermine trust in the health system and may deter women from seeking care in the future [ , ]. While interventions to improve maternal care have frequently focused on improving clinical practice and adherence to clinical guidelines, women’s experiences of care are also a key component of quality maternal care. Therefore, a shift of focus is needed from purely clinical care to a more integrated, person-centred care approach where women in labour are actively involved in their care [ , ].
High-quality, person-centred care requires health workers (HWs) to empathize with women in labour, respect and respond to their needs and concerns, and communicate effectively. Good communication between HWs and women is crucial for shared decision making and respectful maternal care in the labour ward. Several studies conducted in maternity units in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) identified poor communication as one of the leading aspects of disrespect and abuse [ , , ]. Good communication builds trust and confidence between HWs and women receiving care [ ], which leads to improved respect and support during labour and childbirth, and improved maternal and neonatal mortality outcomes [ , ]. Conversely, poor and ineffective communication contributes to negative birth experiences and adverse outcomes for women in labour and their newborns [ , ].
HWs in maternity units in under-resourced settings face a number of challenges that may be linked with poor care, including acute staff shortages, inadequate infrastructure, lack of basic supplies and equipment, and lack of management support [ , ]. In addition, hierarchical structures and power imbalances within maternity teams are common, and may lead to nurses and midwives feeling disempowered [ , ]. These issues may impact the labour ward culture in several ways, so that HWs who are not included in decision-making may feel undervalued. This situation contributes to a stressful work environment and may lead to a culture of distrust and ineffective communication in labour wards, which may trickle down leading to ineffective communication and lack of empathy with women in labour, continuing the cycle of dysfunction and abusive and disrespectful behaviour [ , ].
Person-centred maternity care (PCMC) advocates for a shift towards collaboration between HWs and women, joint decision making, emotional support, cultural sensitivity, and accountability [ ]. This shift is reflected in the World Health Organization (WHO) Framework for quality of maternal and newborn care which focuses on the rights of women to receive dignified, respectful care and emphasizes respectful care as a fundamental right of all women during childbirth [ ]. This shift is also evident in South Africa where recently published maternity guidelines place strong emphasis on respectful maternity care including on promoting birth companions in all maternity units [ ]. The WHO standards of care that relate particularly to women’s experience of care are: effective communication; respectful care and preservation of dignity; and emotional support. Using these standards of quality care as a framework, this study aimed to explore women’s experiences of care during labour and childbirth in order to understand how interpersonal interactions, communication and systemic factors influence women’s experiences of care [ ].

## Methods
This was an exploratory qualitative study undertaken as baseline formative research to inform the development of a participatory intervention amongst maternity unit staff [ ]. The study utilized Focus Group Discussions (FGDs) with postpartum women to explore their experiences of care when giving birth in rural district hospitals.

## Study setting
The study was conducted in two rural districts (uMzinyathi and Zululand) in KwaZulu-Natal (KZN), South Africa. There are nine district hospitals in the two districts where level 1 (generalist) services are provided to inpatients and outpatients including obstetric care for women with low-risk pregnancies and low-risk elective and emergency caesarean births. The labour rooms within the district hospitals have all essential equipment for clinical care. However, space is often limited, with between 2 and 4 beds in one room, with curtains that can be pulled around the beds when a woman is in active labour. The language spoken by most women and health care providers is IsiZulu.
These two districts were chosen based on high maternal and neonatal mortality, low population density, poor infrastructure, geographical remoteness, and long travel distances between district hospitals and specialist referral hospitals. Most of the population in both districts are living below the lower-level poverty line: 66% in uMzinyathi and 70% in Zululand [ , ].

## Participants and sampling
Purposive sampling was used to recruit women to participate in the study from communities within the catchment area of five of the nine district hospitals. These five hospitals were chosen because they had the highest number of deliveries per year. Community Health Workers (CHWs) who conduct postnatal home visits in these communities were requested to identify women who had delivered their baby at one of the five district hospitals in the past three months. CHWs then approached the women and asked if they were willing to participate in a discussion about their experiences during their recent childbirth. If women agreed to participate, CHWs arranged for them to meet the researchers at the venue to participate in the FGD. All venues were identified by CHWs and were based in the community, not in the health facilities.
Eligible women were aged between 16 and 49 years, gave birth at one of the selected district hospitals, and were willing to share their birth experiences. Participants were originally required to have given birth within the past three months, however, in some areas it was difficult to find enough participants to form a group within reasonable travelling distance from the discussion venue. The time since childbirth was therefore increased to 12 months for some groups. Only the biological mothers were eligible to participate. One woman arrived to participate in the FGD with a baby she was caring for but as she had not herself given birth within the past 12 months and was excluded.

## Data collection
Five FGDs were conducted with an average of five women per group, using a semi-structured interview guide developed by the researchers. The FGD duration was between 57 and 103 min. All FGDs were conducted in the local language, IsiZulu, by two experienced qualitative researchers trained up to a master’s level (SL, SM), who were both female and ethnic Zulu. The researchers had no prior knowledge or relationship with the participants before conducting the groups. The FGDs were held in community halls, an Early Childhood Development (ECD) Centre, a community tuck shop, and a community library within the women’s residence area. FGDs took place in private areas away from other community members, so that discussions could not be overheard. Refreshments were provided for participants in each FGD and women were compensated with R100 (approx. US$5) for their time and any expenses incurred as a result of participating in the FGD.

## Data analysis
All FGDs were audio recorded, transcribed, and translated into English. Analysis was first undertaken using inductive thematic analysis [ ]. To ensure a deep understanding of the data, members of the research team read and re-read the transcripts to familiarize themselves with the content. Emerging categories and major themes were identified and deductively mapped against the WHO standards for improving quality of maternal and newborn care [ ], specifically standard 4 (effective communication), standard 5 (respect and preservation of dignity) and standard 6 (emotional support). Five researchers (SL, SM, CH, TD, LH) independently reviewed the transcripts and met to refine the thematic structure and ensure consensus on key findings. Nvivo v12 software was used to aid the analysis process.

## Ethical considerations
The study was granted ethical approval by the Biomedical Research Ethics Committee of the University of KwaZulu-Natal, protocol reference number BREC/00004364/2022. Approval was also obtained from the London School of Hygiene and Tropical Medicine (United Kingdom) and from the Health Research and Knowledge Management Directorate of the Department of Health, KwaZulu-Natal province. Permission to carry out the research was obtained from the Provincial and District health departments, as well as hospital managers. All women provided written informed consent to participate in the study. FGD transcripts were anonymised prior to analysis and no identifying information was collected.

## Findings
The age of the women in the FGDs ranged between 17 and 49 years, and the age of their babies ranged between 11 days and 12 months (Table  ).

 

The findings are organized around emerging themes from the data and are aligned with the World Health Organization (WHO) standards for quality maternal and newborn care. Thus, results are presented under the following headings (1) effective communication; (2) respect and preservation of dignity, (3) emotional support and (4) a final theme concerning women’s responses to the care they experienced in the maternity ward.

## Effective communication
Effective communication requires that women should receive information about their care and experience care that is co-ordinated between all HWs involved. Most women in every FGD described poor communication, and for some women this started from the moment they arrived at the hospital. Women described experiencing uncertainty and anxiety due to the lack of clear information from HWs about what they should expect and what was expected of them.
In two of the FGDs, women recounted experiences of poor communication on arrival at the hospital. Two of these women were in active labour when they arrived. One woman was unaware that she needed to register with the hospital administration before being admitted to the maternity ward, while the other one was sent back and forth between the administration and the maternity ward because of confusion among the hospital staff about where she should go to register. In both cases, HWs at the maternity unit refused to assist the women until they completed their registration, despite their being in active labour. This quote illustrates the similar challenges experienced by the two women:When I got to the admission room, they said I must go to the labour ward. When I was in the labour ward, the sister in charge asked me if I have registered my name in the gate, but I didn’t know that I have to because I was also in pain and I couldn’t walk properly. The sister then turned me back and told me that there was nothing they can do to help me if I am not registered (Participant 08_age 37_Hospital 02).

Several women perceived that HWs had expectations of how they should behave while in the labour ward, but these expectations were not clearly communicated to them. It appeared to women that it was their failure to comply with these unwritten and uncommunicated instructions that led HWs to behave abusively, including speaking disrespectfully, shouting and/or hitting women who behaved in a manner that was perceived by HWs as incorrect or disobedient, as one woman described:I told them that I feel like giving birth and the nurse said I must get on the bed. So, I did because I was in severe pain. After telling the nurses that I am in pains, I was expecting that one of the nurses will come to me, but no one came. Then I decided to push on my own. After that, the nurse came and found me still wearing the clothes that I came [from] home wearing and she said it means I am not serious because I am still wearing my clothes. I was in a state of confusion. She said, it means I will still have to wait because the bed that I am sleeping on is not for people who are wearing clothes that they came wearing. I then undressed and after that I pushed because I could feel that the baby was coming. That was when she came and attended to me (Participant 03_age 33_Hospital 01).

Another example of poor communication and lack of teamwork amongst health professionals was when contradictory information was provided to women by different HWs. For example, one woman shared an experience where two HWs involved in her care provided contradictory instructions about whether or not she should start pushing. This disagreement between HWs led her to feel confused and fearful, and when she went against the instructions of one of the HWs she was struck with a ruler.One of them [HWs] said “when you feel like pushing, you must” but the other one said, “she cannot [push] because it still early, her uterus is still closed”. I repeatedly told her that I feel like pushing and she said “no, it is still early”. But I felt that I wanted to push and I pushed. I told them that I am not saying it is not true but I feel like pushing. I then pushed, then the other one who said I must not push hit me with a ruler on my thighs (Participant 05_age 35_Hospital 01).

While many women spoke about negative experiences during childbirth, others highlighted moments of positive care. These were often described in terms of health workers taking time to communicate clearly and working together as a team. In one instance, the woman had arrived with a companion and was attended to promptly.Nothing bad because I came with a taxi and it delivered me on the door and when we arrived, my sister told them that I have brought this lady, I am not sure if she is in labour or what. Then they told me to go to a certain room and I did not stay for long when a nurse came and attended me. She then asked me when did I leave home. I told her and she also asked when last did I eat. I told her. She then said I should undress and climb in bed so that she can check on me. After checking on me, she said, I was going to give birth shortly. Then she asked me how I gave birth on my first child. I told her that it was a C-Section and she asked me the reason why I was operated on and I told her that my BP was high and the baby was big. She put me on the foetal monitor and called the midwife. They told me that I was going to theatre because this child was also big (Participant 04_age 31_Hospital 01).

In the example below while the mother reported receiving good care, she also reported that she was ‘disturbing’ the midwife when she reported she was in labour. This mother also clearly perceived that the good care she received was related to her behaving in a certain way, saying that she had been treated well because she did not give the midwife ‘any problem’.The midwife who delivered my baby treated me very well, I don’t want to lie. Because I gave birth to a big baby who weighed 4 kg and was not crying, but she tried everything, that’s why I still have my baby. Even when she was busy writing, I would disturb her and tell her that I am in labour, and she will say, breathe in and out. When she was done writing, she took her gloves and checked on me and after that she said, I must push and I gave birth. She then appreciated that I did not give her problems, and she did not mistreat me also (Participant 08_age 37_ Hospital 02).

## Respect and preservation of dignity
Respect and privacy are presented in three sub-themes: privacy and confidentiality during labour and childbirth, lack of dignity and respect for women, women's involvement in the care they received.

## Privacy and confidentiality during labour and childbirth
Women described observing other women experiencing a lack of privacy and confidentiality, which led them to be fearful about what would happen to them. For example, in several FGDs, women reported that the layout of labour wards in the hospitals compromised privacy, as beds were positioned quite close together and facing each other so that women could both see and hear what was happening to others in the labour ward.To be honest, there are three beds. One [bed] is facing that direction and the other on the other direction. Then [there is] the other one is the one that they [nurses] use to work on. The setup of beds allows the other person to hear what they are saying about your well-being and I can also hear what they say about the well-being of the other person (Participant 12_age 30_ Hospital 03).

The women further expressed concern that HWs often spoke openly about one woman’s health status in front of the other women in the labour ward, disregarding the confidentiality of the individual and violating women’s rights to privacy. Women further noted that witnessing poor communication and poor care led to anxiety about facing the same treatment. In particular, mothers were asked questions about their treatment in front of others that indirectly revealed their HIV positive status.Others will just shout and tell you that “Isn’t that you are under a certain treatment, this is for you and the baby so that you can protect the child’’. After that we know what kind of sickness you have because they don’t hide it. They don’t even hide the tablets, No, they give you as they are for everyone to see. (Participant 20_age 20 _Hospital 05)

In addition, some women described times when the HWs did not close the curtains during examinations to offer privacy despite these curtains being available, and several women mentioned having seen other women giving birth during their stay on the labour ward. Again, this led to women experiencing fear about what was ahead of them. In this example the failure to provide privacy was perceived by the participant as being done purposely by the HW to spread fear among the women in the ward.From the bed I was sleeping on, I saw a woman who was giving birth and I saw the entire process because the curtain was not closed. I saw everything that was happening. From my observation, maybe they are deliberately doing it so that we become scared and don’t come back to deliver again (Participant 25_age 20_Hospital 05).

In addition, many women described their own direct experiences of poor care and lack of privacy. In particular, several women mentioned not having the procedure explained and not being given privacy during vaginal examinationsNo, if it happens that they give you the hospital green blanket, it is half and it only covers the buttocks. When the doctor comes, they will not put their hand under the blanket when they put their fingers underneath, so, it reveals everything. Yes, the other person who is on the other side can be able to see you. (Participant 08_age 37_Hospital 02)

In one FGD, the women offered contrasting views about their experiences of consent and privacy. Some women in this group mentioned that they had positive experiences where HWs asked for permission before examination and also offered privacy by using curtains during examinations and explaining the results.They would ask for permission for instance when they want to take vital signs, to check the uterus, they will do it the right way. Even when you were covered with the cubicle curtain, they will explain first (Participant 03_age 33_ Hospital 01).

## Lack of dignity and respect for women
In all the FGDs, women described instances where they were not treated in a dignified or respectful manner. In one hospital the women discussed how HWs did not call them by their names or surnames during their stay in the labour ward. HWs were reported to call women according to how they appeared, their item of clothing, and sometimes the sickness or medication that the women were taking, for example, ‘lady with black doek [head scarf]’. The women experienced this as humiliating.They will call you by, the lady with the black doek [laughter] they don’t maybe say, [surname] or something else, they call you with whatever they want to call you. Something not pleasant (Participant 20_age 20_Hospital 05).

In addition, it appeared to women that HWs blamed women if adverse events happened and this was used by HWs to justify poor care and mistreatment, including shouting and hitting the womenThey don’t care. A lot of people will give birth in toilets and when they [health workers] come, they will shout and hit them. Hitting them for giving birth in toilets (Participant 18_age 28_Hospital 03).

HWs swore and shouted at women in labour as a way of controlling women’s behaviour and to make them to behave in a particular way. In this example a young mother described how she was treated and how her mother responded when she heard about the mistreatment, telling her daughter to accept the abusive language to protect her child.… She [nurse] was swearing at me [in an African offensive language to dismiss me] and telling me to leave her alone, and I ended up crying. When my mother came, I was crying and she said I must persevere for the sake of the child (Participant 21_age 22_ Hospital 05).

Another example of perceived ‘disobedience’ was two women who reported being scolded for taking traditional medicine, despite there being no evidence for this. These women, who came to hospital in premature labour, were blamed for their condition and accused by HWs of taking traditional herbal medicine (known as isihlambezo) to speed up labour [ ]. This is a common practice in traditional communities and may be dangerous and lead to foetal distress. The below quote illustrates the experience of one of the women:
They gave me pain tablets to drink and I slept. In the morning, I told them that I can feel the pains coming back. And one of the nurses said, where are the pains coming from because I did not experience pains with my first child, it means I have taken Isihlambezo. Then I told her that I have never taken Isihlambezo. They then took me to the maternity waiting room to wait (Participant 13_age 28_ Hospital 03).

Most women reported that the night shift staff neglected the care of the women in labour. Women reported examples of night staff sleeping during their shifts and refusing to assist women in labour, as well as instances of shouting at and physically hitting women.The night shift staff are always shouting, even if it is a small thing, they shout … We already knew them. They don’t have an approach of talking with people. It becomes better when it is the day shift (Participant 04_age 31_ Hospital 01).

## Women’s involvement in the care they received
Women reported that good communication, giving women relevant information and involving them in decision making about their care was not common practice. Several women mentioned that HWs failed to provide information about the processes and reasons for examinations or procedures during labour and did not ask for permission. In particular, vaginal examinations were not explained and women were expected to comply without any prior notice or explanation.No, I don’t want to lie. They have never asked to insert their fingers [do a vaginal examination]. They will tell you to open [your legs] so that they can check how is it progressing (Participant 08_age 37_ Hospital 02).

Lack of involvement in care was particularly mentioned by women who had a caesarean section. One woman reported that she had not been adequately informed about the reasons for her caesarean birth, despite the requirement for women to provide written consent for the operation. Women perceived that they were unable to make informed choices because of lack of information and the fear of being mistreated.The only thing that I didn’t like was the theatre doctor who was talking as they wish because when they wanted to do something, they were not asking for permission. And they always shouted at me and even said I must never give birth again. Maybe the problem was that I did not keep quiet, whenever there was something that did not sit well, I would talk. Maybe that’s what angered the doctor (Participant 04_age 31_Hospital 01).

## Emotional support
Dimensions of emotional support include having a companion of the woman’s choice and receiving support that strengthens the woman’s capability. In all the FGDs, there were some women who brought family members or the father of the baby for emotional support but their companions were not allowed into the labour ward. The below quotes illustrate the experiences of two women:My baby daddy [baby’s father] came but he was not allowed to get inside. You will talk to him by the door and that’s it. (Participant 02_age 18_Hospital 01)
When there is someone accompanying you, they don’t give them enough time to stay, and they don’t even give you an option for them to stay. They were told to go back home (Participant 20_age 20_Hospital 05).

In all FGDs, women frequently reported that HWs did not listen to their concerns or address their requests for assistance during labour. The below two quotes show instances were women felt ignored or unsupported by HWs when reported that they felt the urge to push or when asking for basic needs, such as water.I then screamed telling them that I feel the baby was coming and they said, they won’t be told by me because I act as if I know better. They are not going to listen to me (Participant 23_age 20_ Hospital 05).
I did not get any help, even when I asked for water because I couldn’t go on my own, they said I should go and fetch water on my own I would tell them that I was unable to walk anymore they insisted, saying that the baby was still too far (Participant 16_age 28_Hospital 03).

Providing pain relief is a crucial aspect of supportive care during labour. While pain relief medication was available in all hospitals, it was not consistently accessible to women. One participant noted that pain relief tablets and injections were provided only at specific times, so they were not available when women needed them most. Another woman expressed that she refrained from asking for pain relief because she feared that her care would be compromised.One other thing, when you come to ask for tablets, it doesn’t matter how sick you are. I once came to the maternity ward very sick and they told me that you drink tablets at a certain time. I asked them at three and I was given tablets at eight. The pain by then had subsided (Participant 18_age 28_ Hospital 03).

Conversely, a woman in a different FGDs shared positive experiences where HWs were supportive and promptly provided pain medication when requested.Yes, they gave me pain relief tablets. I went to them and told them that the labour pains were worse and they said I must go back to bed the nurse will bring me something (Participant 11_age 49_ Hospital 02).

## Women’s responses to their experiences during labour and childbirth
Women’s experiences during labour led to the perception of the maternity unit as a hostile and frightening environment. Women feared that they would be punished or that care would be withheld from them or their baby if they made the HWs angry or did not do what they were told. This led to women implementing different strategies to avoid the negative and degrading behaviours from HWs, including keeping quiet, not asking questions to avoid saying the wrong thing, not asking for pain relief, and rigorously adhering to instructions without asking questions. One participant said:We were not asking questions because we were scared that they will shout at us (Participant 01_age 18_ Hospital 01).

First-time mothers were said to be at a further disadvantage having no experience in the maternity ward. Women mentioned that they relied on one another for assistance and support during labour, including advice from older women to first-time mothers on how to communicate with HWs to receive better care during labour.One other thing that happened to us and some of my wardmates that scared us was what they were doing. Not taking care of us. I was much better because it was not for the first time [I was in the labour ward], but those who were there for the first time were scared when they [nurses] turned them away, and I told them how things are done. And they will say, they are shouting at us, and I would tell them to go back to them [nurses], because shouting is no longer helping. Go back and tell them what is happening (Participant 07_age 37_ Hospital 02).

In contrast to the strategy of staying quiet and seeking assistance from other women, there were two women in different FGDs who were outspoken about not accepting abuse and poor communication from HWs. These women expressed anger about the care they received and threatened to report HWs to management and open police reports. One woman went further to state that she would assault the HW if she met her outside the hospital.I was not injected when they stitched me. When I asked for the injection from the nurse, she asked what did I want an injection for what? I then told her that it is for numbing the pain. She proceeded to stitch me without the injection. She said that she was going to tie a knot if I dare move. I know her. She mistreated me. And when I meet her in town and do something bad, I will be arrested. But I told her that we will meet each other. And we will meet each other. To be honest she mistreated me. She slapped me saying I must open my thighs. I even said, why are slapping me because I have ears, I can hear you and I know it is your job but you must not to slap me (Participant 05_age 35_ Hospital 01).

A woman in one FGD mentioned that she learned to stand up for herself and confront HWs to receive what she perceived as good care during her stay in the hospital.It was ok. Maybe it is because I’m a talkative person. The nurses knew that I can talk for myself. I would tell them that being learned doesn’t mean that you have to treat me bad. I am also a woman just like you. They treated me well until I went home (Participant 18_age 28_ Hospital 05).

When asked about the complaint system, most women in all the FGDs expressed that they did not trust the system of communication with hospital management to report their grievances. Some women feared that the same HWs who were abusing them had access to the suggestion boxes choosing what things to report to management, and feared mistreatment if they complained about the care they were receiving in the hospital.People are complaining. When they go to deliver, they are mistreated and some come back home with babies who have disabilities because of negligence (Participant 22_age 28 _ Hospital 05).

## Discussion
This study explored women’s experiences of care during labour and childbirth in rural district hospitals. Using the WHO standards for improving quality of maternal and newborn care in health facilities as a frame [ ], the findings demonstrate poor care across all experience of care domains including effective communication, respect and preservation of dignity, and emotional support. In particular, our findings highlight a persistent breakdown of communication between HWs and women, characterized by unclear expectations, lack of information sharing and poorly coordinated care, which led to negative experiences of care. Labour wards were experienced as a hostile environments by women who feared for their safety and the safety of their newborns, such that they felt forced to adopt defensive strategies to protect themselves, including obeying all HW instructions without question. These experiences led to prolonged feelings of trauma and anger among women that persisted long after their childbirth, and eroded trust in the care they will receive at the hospital in future. However, despite the preponderance of negative experiences, it is notable that some women described positive experiences of care during labour and childbirth.
Instances of verbal abuse, physical punishment, and denial of care echo what has been previously reported in South Africa and elsewhere [ , , – ]. Our findings concur with results from a quantitative study of women’s childbirth experiences in the same districts which we conducted in parallel with the present study [ ], using the person-centred maternity care (PCMC) tool validated in Kenya and India [ , ]. In particular, the quantitative study showed that few women were allowed a companion with them during labour and childbirth, a substantial proportion of women reported not having been asked to provide consent for procedures, and giving birth at night was associated with a lower overall PCMC score. In addition, less than half of women felt they could trust their health providers [ ].
Many studies in LMICs mention structural challenges, including lack of human resources, poor infrastructure and lack of equipment as key contributing factors that perpetuate maternal disrespectful care and abuse [ , , , ]. This is also reflected in the WHO standards for improving the quality of maternal and newborn care [ ]. While these structural challenges certainly contributed to lack of privacy and confidentiality in this study, the findings suggest that communication failures and disrespectful care extend far beyond what can be explained by structural limitations. For example, referring to women by their appearance or medical condition rather than their names, represents a form of dehumanization and lack of dignity, which is not easily explained by a lack of human resources. Further, it is important to note that, despite the challenges, instances of compassionate and effective communication by some HWs demonstrated that better care is achievable even within constrained environments.
The persistence of disrespectful behaviour over many decades, despite increasing attention being focused on this issue, suggests that this behaviour is resistant to change and is normalised in maternity units in South Africa and elsewhere [ , , , ], and further points to an entrenched institutional culture that enables these behaviours [ ]. Our findings provide evidence for poor care rooted in environments with major power imbalances between women and health professionals, where health workers exercise authority by withholding information and expecting compliance without explanation [ ]. Improving the training on interpersonal communication skills and cultural competency within nursing and midwifery pre-service and in-service curricula is important to address these gaps.
The WHO standards emphasise co-ordinated care provision yet we found a lack of teamwork evidenced by contradictory information being given to women by different HWs, that added to women’s anxiety and confusion over the labour process. Therefore, interventions to improve experience of care need to focus on motivating the health team and should consider ways to improve teamwork and communication amongst all members of the maternity team [ ].
South Africa has high levels of gender-based violence, and the majority of nurses and midwives in maternity units are women who may themselves be living in communities and households with high levels of violence and normalized gender-based violence [ ]. We found that few women had the agency to stand up and ask for their rights to be respected, highlighting the pervasive nature of oppression and powerlessness. One way to empower women receiving maternity care is to provide an accessible vehicle for women to provide feedback or make complaints about the care they received. Few women utilised the existing complaints system, expressing fear that complaints could lead to reprisals from HWs and that no action would be taken to address their complaint. Several approaches have been successfully used in other settings that could be implemented individually or in combination to make the complaints process more accessible and acceptable to patients. Such approaches include a toll-free complaints ‘hotline’, reporting complaints via short messaging systems (SMS) and appointing dedicated complaints officers or confidential counsellors to support complainants. Improved complaints procedures should be combined with improved systems to ensure that complaints are dealt with effectively and complainants receive feedback where possible. An effectively functioning complaints system could be empowering to women, improve accountability and lead to improved service delivery and experience of maternal care [ ].
Women in our study experienced lack of responsiveness from health workers to their physiological experiences of labour as well as lack of information about expectations in labour, both of which exacerbated the anxiety they felt. This suggests that empowering women with information about what will happen during labour and childbirth could improve their experiences of labour. Almost all women in South Africa attend antenatal care where there are existing health education sessions [ ], these could be strengthened to improve women’s knowledge of what to expect in labour as well as building their knowledge about their rights to respect, information about their care and involvement in decision making [ ].
New South African maternity guidelines recommend and encourage the presence of birth companions in maternity wards. Companionship during labour has the potential to improve maternal and perinatal outcomes, including shorter duration of labour, decreased caesarean sections, and increased satisfaction with experiences of childbirth [ , , ]. However, this has rarely been practised [ , , ], and several women in our study reported that their companion was not allowed in the labour ward. While a number of factors have hindered implementation of birth companions previously [ , ], the new policy on birth companions requires all maternity units to initiate this practice. Birth companions during labour can be a powerful way to address the challenges faced by women during labour and childbirth. Having a trusted person to help women through labour and give emotional support, can improve women’s confidence and the overall experience of women during childbirth [ ]. In addition, birth companions can bridge the communication gap between women and HWs, acting as an advocate for the woman and providing physical assistance such as giving drinks or non-pharmacological pain relief such as rubbing her back.

## Study limitations
This qualitative research study explored women’s experiences of labour and childbirth focusing on district hospitals in the KwaZulu-Natal province, limiting our understanding of women’s experiences of quality care to this particular context. In addition, while our original study population was women up to 3 months post-birth, we faced challenges recruiting women within this timeframe. As a result, we expanded the postpartum age range to include women up to 12 months post-birth, but we believe women can recall their childbirth experiences for 12 months. Difficulties in recruitment also meant that several of the focus groups were quite small. We also note that employing FGDs as the data collection method may lead to an emphasis on overly negative responses, this is supported by the relatively better responses from women in the same district to the quantitative PCMC survey [ ].

## Conclusion
This study shows important gaps in provision of high-quality maternity care, including widespread examples of disrespectful care, poor communication and lack of dignity, exacerbated by weak teamwork and resource constraints. Addressing these issues requires interventions focused on improving communication and team collaboration on the maternity unit, while improving HW empathy towards women. In addition, empowering women through antenatal education and implementing birth companion policies could further improve women’s experiences during labour and childbirth.
