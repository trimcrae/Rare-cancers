## Abstract

## Background
Women in sub-Saharan Africa often lack agency to make their own contraceptive decisions, more so in rural settings. Evidence indicates that social networks influence contraceptive decision-making and behaviours but there is limited information on the dynamics between women and their network members. We explored ways in which women’s social networks influenced their agency to make contraceptive decisions.

## Methods
We conducted an exploratory qualitative study, between February and May 2021, among 60 women (15–45 years) from two largely rural districts in Uganda. Data were collected using semi-structured audio-recorded in-depth interviews which were transcribed verbatim. We used thematic analysis guided by constructs from social network mechanisms to analyze the data.

## Results
Half of the participants were adolescents (15–19 years) and 62% (37/60) were current contraception users. We found that participants most commonly involved partners, relatives, and peers in their contraceptive decision-making. Social support emerged as the most common mechanism through which these network members influenced women’s agency in making and acting on their contraceptive decisions. This was mainly in the form of informational and emotional support. Informational support, mostly provided by relatives and peers, included advice to adopt contraception particularly given to participants who desired to better manage their families, describing lived experiences, directing women to formal contraceptive services, and sharing perceived/actual side effects of contraceptive use. Emotional support, provided by partners and relatives, involved acknowledging and validating decisions, as well as providing reassurance regarding contraceptive experiences. Some women also received instrumental support (reminders to get dose/method and exempting women from responsibilities to go seek contraceptive services). The mechanism of social influence was also at play with social pressure to give birth.

## Conclusions
Our study highlights how different types of social support from partners, relatives, and peers can either bolster or undermine women’s agency in making contraceptive decisions. Informational support from relatives and peers can boost women’s certainty in their intentions, while emotional support from partners boosts their confidence to act on these intentions. Multipronged approaches targeting these networks are necessary to promote women’s agency in making contraceptive decisions.

## Background
Women’s ability to exercise agency in contraceptive decision-making, including whether or not to use contraception and what method to use, is crucial for achieving universal access to reproductive rights and health services by 2030 [ ]. However, research suggests that some women in sub-Saharan Africa do not participate in contraceptive use decisions, particularly in rural areas [ , ]. Contraceptive agency requires that women not only participate in contraceptive decisions, but to do so with clarity of their own values and the consciousness that it is their right to make a choice. Furthermore, women should have self-efficacy and perceived control over decision-making, with critical reflection of constraints to contraceptive choice, and preference-aligned information and support. These elements of agency are influenced by an individual’s social environment including influence from partners, family members, and peers, among other factors [ ].
While much research has examined determinants of whether women use contraception, a focus on agency is nascent in the literature and little is known about the specific ways in which social environments can bolster or hinder individuals’ contraceptive decision-making agency [ ]. Social networks are known to influence contraceptive use, and likely also play a critical role in women’s contraceptive agency. Studies show that women rely on their networks for contraceptive information, or to get opinions on their contraceptive decisions [ , ], which may facilitate decisions to use contraception [ – ], or hinder use [ ]. This influence occurs through spread of attitudes, creation of norms, and spread of information about side effects— such as the belief that contraceptives cause infertility, all of which can impact women’s decisions to use or not use contraception [ – ].
The mechanisms through which networks influence individuals’ decisions and behaviour (i.e., social mechanisms or social network mechanisms), include social support, social influence, and social integration [ , ]. Social support includes exchange of tangible/intangible support such as emotional (e.g., having someone to discuss one’s feelings, fears or worries), informational (e.g., providing knowledge to solve a specific problem) and instrumental (receiving practical help) support. Social influence includes processes such as social pressure and learning, through which network members influence each other with their actions, presence or absence [ , ]. Social pressure subjects individuals to certain expectations or norms from network members, often resulting in compliance; while social learning occurs as individuals observe and evaluate experiences of others. Social integration involves mechanisms such as recognition and isolation, which shape how individuals respond to interactions and exchanges with network members. Social recognition can for instance boost an individual’s self-confidence [ ].
Existing studies on interpersonal communication related to contraception have established key influencers in women’s contraceptive decision-making processes, including partners, mothers, and peers [ , , , ]. However, the dynamics of their influence on contraceptive use, much less agency, within these networks remain underexplored, despite calls to do so [ ]. Some assessments of partner influence on women’s decision-making are based on women ever talking about contraception with their partners or who usually decides contraceptive use, which provide limited insight into the contraception discussions within these social ties and their implications for women’s ability to exercise agency in making contraceptive decisions [ , ]. So far, studies suggest that social learning through information sharing and social influence are common ways in which social networks may influence contraceptive decision-making [ , ]. Additionally, research highlights that women’s networks could be unsupportive, which may limit their decision-making ability [ ]. Additional exploration of the interactions within women’s networks, focusing more on their decision-making agency rather than use, is critical to identifying opportunities to enhance women’s reproductive rights by promoting their agency in social and behaviour change programs and policies.
Uganda experiences various challenges regarding the right to contraceptive choice including socio-cultural barriers and low levels of women’s empowerment [ ]. For example, 2022 estimates by Performance Monitoring for Action indicate that, despite discussing contraception with their partners, one in ten women do not make the decision to use themselves [ ]. Among ongoing national efforts to address these challenges, there is a focus on increasing community engagement and reaching women through interpersonal interventions [ ]. Leveraging social networks holds the potential to realise this target, however, evidence on social networks in Uganda is limited to their effects on contraceptive use and pregnancy or its outcomes [ – ]. This leaves a gap in knowledge on the mechanisms through which social networks affect women’s agency, which could help inform ongoing efforts to support women’s right to contraceptive choice in the country. This is especially important in rural areas, where most women are of low socio-economic status, making them vulnerable to social pressure, isolation, and exposure to inaccurate contraception information. The objective of this paper is to explore ways in which women’s social networks influence their contraceptive decision-making agency.

## Methods

## Study design and setting
This qualitative exploratory study was part of the formative research conducted for the Innovations for Choice and Autonomy (ICAN) Project in Uganda. The ICAN project was designed to obtain a deeper understanding of the implementation of the self-injectable (SI) contraceptive DMPA-SC from women’s perspectives. The formative study used a modified grounded theory approach to gain deeper insights into the barriers and facilitators of contraceptive decision-making and use, and the implementation context for DMPA-SC. The study was done in Mayuge (Eastern Uganda) and Oyam (Northern Uganda) districts, purposefully selected for the ICAN project because of their prior involvement in the pilot implementation of DMPA-SC in Uganda in 2012. Additionally, the two sites represent diversity in cultural, geographic, and political influences on family planning practice.

## Participant selection and sample size
Participants were purposively selected, targeting sexually active women aged 15–45 years. We aimed to include a total of 60 women (30 from each study site), with representation of users and non-users of contraception, married (legally married or cohabiting) and non-married women, as well as age groups (15–19, 20–45). Among the contraceptive users, we also aimed to include at least five women who had ever self-injected with DMPA-SC. Contraception use in this study was defined as women using anything to prevent pregnancy. Women were selected from different sub-counties including the main town councils in each district. Recruitment occurred within communities and at health facilities in the selected sub-counties. The Village Health Teams (VHTs) helped to identify potential participants within each community. A pre-designed screening form was administered by trained research assistants (RAs) to determine eligibility. Eligible participants were invited for the interviews if they provided written consent to participate in the research. Those who declined to participate or refused the interview to be recorded, as well as women who did not speak either English, Lusoga, or Langi were excluded.

## Data collection methods and tools
After providing consent, each woman participated in a semi-structured in-depth interview (IDI). The interviews were conducted using a pre-designed guide developed for this study, with open-ended questions under four broad domains– (1) contraceptive/fertility decision-making (e.g., who women prefer to (or not to) to involve during contraceptive decision-making); (2) contraceptive use (e.g., motivation, experiences, and barriers to use); (3) awareness and use of self-injection (e.g., ever or willingness to use, relative advantage of SI, key beneficiaries of SI); and (4) social communication and women’s groups (e.g., where women go to for information on pregnancy prevention and willingness to get support related to family planning from other women). The interview guide used in our study is provided in Supplementary file 1. We also collected data on socio-demographic characteristics such as age, marital status, highest level of education attained, and source of livelihood. The analysis for this paper focuses on contraceptive decision-making, social communication, and women’s group’s responses. Data were collected between February and May 2021 by four female experienced and trained RAs, able to conduct interviews in the major local languages, Lusoga in Mayuge and Langi in Oyam. The RAs participated in a five-day training in Kampala and a pre-test of the IDI guide with women near the training venue. All interviews were audio-recorded, lasted one to two hours, and were conducted in places that were both private and convenient for the participants while considering the corona virus disease (COVID-19) related social distancing guidelines at the time. These sites included school premises, women’s workplaces, health facilities, and women’s homes.

## Data analysis
Audio recordings of interviews were transcribed verbatim and translated into English by two independent transcribers. Transcripts were imported into Dedoose, a software package for qualitative analysis. We conducted thematic analysis that involved data familiarisation, coding, theme generation and validation. The full methodology used to analyse the ICAN qualitative data is described elsewhere [ ]. Initial coding of transcripts was done by a team of four coders. The code excerpts relevant to this paper’s objectives including women’s perceptions on the information received from their networks, were extracted from Dedoose in Microsoft Excel. These excerpts were then reviewed and categorized into sub-themes by the lead author (CB) guided by four key questions: (i) who is involved in women’s contraceptive decision-making? (ii) why women consider their involvement important? (iii) what those involved in women’s contraceptive decisions communicate regarding contraception? (iv) How participants experienced and perceived their own contraceptive decision-making? Synthesis of findings on how network members potentially influence women’s decision-making was informed by the network mechanisms of social support, social influence, and social integration, described by Klarner et al. (2022) [ ].

## Results

## Participant characteristics
We interviewed 60 women who were between 15 and 45 years. Half (50%) of the participants were adolescents (15–19 years) and 62% reported using contraception at the time of the study (Table  ). Over 70% had at least one child and 75% were married/cohabiting (in union). Only 23% reported to be working for pay at the time of the study and 57% had attained primary education.
 

## Participant’s expression and perception of their agency
Participants’ accounts showed different aspects of their ability to exercise agency in contraceptive decision-making. A few participants explicitly reported having control over contraceptive decisions, citing rights-related issues such as control over one’s body, freedom of choice, and self-preservation. A participant noted:
“I can decide for myself whether to use or not to use [contraception] because it is my life. (…). It does not need me tell any other person because if you tell another person, the person may go and tell other people that she has gone in family [planning]. That should be my decision and my secret”. (R-08, 19years, in union, non-user).
Specifically, women in union exercised agency in contraceptive decision-making in two key ways: negotiation and asserting their choices while informing their partners. While some participants seemed to only inform their partners of the decisions they had taken on their own “I last talked to him when I was telling him that I have injected myself”, the following participant’s narrative illustrates negotiation. When her partner suggested contraception, she proposed a method she felt comfortable with, acknowledging her fears with conventional options. Her ability to negotiate her desired method demonstrates a level of agency and resistance to external pressure. The participant stated:
“He came and said that my friend the speed in giving birth is too high. We may not manage but I request you to go in family planning. I told him that I will not go for family planning. I hear that family planning affects and I hear very many women that can’t do this or that because they cut their tubes. So I told him that if you agree on what I am going to suggest we use, it will work for us. He asked me, what is that that is going to help us? (…) I told him that we both know our situation, so I know you will be able to pull out during sex [withdrawal].(….) We started on that method till now we are still using it”. (R-01, 39years, in union, non-user)
Similarly, some participants expressed agency in decision-making by resisting the control or influence of other network members. For example, a 24-year-old participant shared:
“she [neighbour/friend] said that since you want five children, you give birth at once to make the five and then stop. Maybe you go they do tubal ligation or they remove the uterus, for you on those two you will choose one. But I said no, I do not want to give birth at once when I have no means of supporting them”. (R-21, 24years, in union, non-user)
However, many participants in union expressed a lack of agency, having no control over contraceptive decisions, as their partners held the power. These relational dynamics highlighted the absence of rights to contraceptive choice, with several participants feeling they had little or no control simply because they were married or cohabiting. This was reflected in statements like, “my husband has the rights over me” and “even if I have rights, he has all the powers”, leading to sentiments such as, “if he tells me to stop using contraceptives, then I will have no choice, but to stop”. These perceptions suggest that participants were unable to act on their preferences unless their partners acknowledged or supported their decisions. A few participants expressed dissatisfaction with these dynamics, as one participated stated:
“I have a little bit of power in deciding when I should prevent pregnancy… sometimes I feel uncomfortable, because I don’t have the power or the voice… usually it is men that have all that power, us women we don’t have any power”. (R-12, 19years, in union, user)
While other participants reported feeling comfortable with their partners taking the lead in making contraceptive decisions, this often appeared to be driven by fear of potential consequences if they acted contrary to their partner’s preferences. These included domestic violence, divorce/separation, and withdrawal of financial or emotional support.
“I really can’t get up and I start using birth control without his consent… I feel okay with it, because in case there is a problem, he can help me and support me through it. If I decide to use family planning without seeking for permission and consent from him, it can result into problems for me, even physical violence”. (R-31, 30years, in union, user)

## Influence of social networks on women’s contraceptive agency
Participants most commonly considered partners, relatives (parents, siblings, and in-laws), and peers as important to involve when making contraceptive decisions. Other people who were infrequently reported were teachers, neighbours, healthcare workers, and elders. Social support emerged as the most common mechanism through which partners, relatives, and peers influenced women’s agency to make contraceptive decisions. This was mainly in the form of informational and emotional support. Some women also received instrumental support (reminders to get dose/method and exempting women from responsibilities to go seek contraceptive services), while others faced social pressure to give birth.

## Informational support from relatives and peers motivates and guides women’s contraceptive decisions, including linkage to formal services

## Giving preference-aligned advice
We found that relatives and peers often provided advice to participants regarding pregnancy prevention, suggesting contraception use for effective childbirth spacing. This advice was commonly based on the participant’s lived experience, highlighting challenges such as poorly spaced children and having more children than one could adequately care for. Similarly, these realities also prompted different reflections among participants. For example, a participant stated, “I realized that I shouldn’t have another child just as yet because I don’t have any serious source of income. So, having a child would complicate things”.
This sentiment reflects an underlying need or desire to prevent getting pregnant too soon. Similar reflections from other participants indicated that they often held clear values regarding pregnancy prevention, most of which were ultimately linked to their physical, mental, and economic wellbeing, as well as that of their families. As such, the advice offered by their relatives and peers often aligned with these personal intentions, potentially boosting confidence in their decisions. One participant, who was not using contraception at the time of the study but was interested in it “I have seen that I am over giving birth and therefore I wanted to use family planning”; shared the advice she received from her mother:
“she said, my daughter, you have just started giving birth but you are giving birth to one child after the other, you are not even spacing. You do not have the capacity to take care of the children…. She said it is good to give birth, but it also affects your responsibilities at home.” (R-21, 24years, in union, non-user). In explaining the value of such advice, the participant stated, “if I do not follow their advice (to go for family planning)…. I end up getting pregnant in a time I do not want…. I saw that they are right.” In this case, advice from her mother helped the participant realize that contraceptive use could be a good fit for her personal goals. However, this participant had failed to act on her stated contraceptive preferences due to social barriers related to gender roles, as she further explained,
“I have not yet got the time, I have been planning to go (….) because of our kind of house chores, you wake up early morning to go in the garden, you come back late and you have to prepare things for the home like eating. So, you do not get the time to go for family planning. The day you say that you will keep home, is when there is someone who has died on the village and sometimes you are sick. So you find yourself lacking the time and by the time you think about it you have other things to do”.
Participants indicated that the choice was usually up to them “We advise each other about going in family planning (…). Others agree others disagree, those who agree they go, those who refuse leave it”. However, for those women who had limited agency to begin with, advice from relatives did not necessarily take a woman’s personal preferences into account, and could take the form of an order rather than a suggestion, thereby undermining the woman’s agency to make her own decisions. This was often the case with younger women, for example, who relied on older family members for material support. For example, an 18-year-old shared,
“she said that where you are, your parents disappeared. Now if you give birth to another [child] where will you put that one?…. sometimes you garden and harvest little, where will you start from? She advised me that she has medicine that can prevent giving birth. I told her that mum, I was thinking of going into family planning. But she said no, let us try this herbal medicine we see how it will work […] because I am in her (auntie) hands, whatever she says I have to obey her.” (R-25, 18years, single, user).

## Describing lived experiences and providing reassurance
In addition to giving advice, relatives shared their lived experiences with fertility and contraception, which helped participants better understand and trust the guidance provided. Additionally, peers offered emotional support through reassurance and comfort during discussions about their contraception experiences. Knowing that other women face similar challenges such as bleeding and body weakness, can enhance women’s agency by boosting their confidence in their decisions and encouraging them to act on their preferences. For example, one adolescent participant benefited from her mother’s personal story, which included discussing potential options and allowing her to make an informed choice.
“my mother said, I ended in primary six but I do not want you to also end there…. do not do the mistake I did when I was still studying (….). When I made 14 years, she would sit me down and talk to me that if you reach this age you need to use family planning (……). I told her that mother I believe in you and I cannot do what you have refused me. I asked her why don’t you want me to use what you are using? She said no, you decide for yourself” (R-09, 16years, in union, user).

## Directing women to formal contraceptive services
Furthermore, relatives and peers also directed women to formal contraceptive services, assisting them to make more informed choices. They provided information on where to seek help for pregnancy prevention or to start a specific method of choice, often recommending formal providers such as hospitals and health centres.
“I realised that having children all the time was hard for me, and I needed to start using a contraceptive. So, I talked to my friend and I was advised to seek help from a health facility.” (R-27, 19years, in union, non-user).
In addition to directing women to family planning services, some relatives offered instrumental support by reminding women to get their doses or method as needed. A participant shared, “But she is ever reminding me, my girl first go and get your injection (family planning)”.

## Sharing perceived/actual side effects of contraceptive use
Lastly, relatives and peers frequently shared with participants side effects they had experienced (actual) or those they heard about (perceived) that are associated with contraceptive use. These included bleeding and weight loss associated with implants, delays in conception associated with intra-uterine devices, nausea and loss of appetite associated with oral contraceptive pills, and fears of infertility and cancer. Such information, especially when based on personal experiences, may overwhelm women (as explained by participant below), reduce their confidence, and deter or delay their decision-making. A participant shared
“……other women also shared what they were using and the different side effects they were experiencing. Someone shared how they were bleeding for the whole year because she was using some method and it was a bit scary. Some people said they were vomiting and feeling uneasy as if they were pregnant while using other methods. I just kept quiet while hearing all these experiences”. (R-05, 29year, in union, non-user)
Furthermore, these side effects (experienced or not) were used by relatives and peers to discourage women from considering contraception use, citing potential impacts on their ability to conceive. Despite these discouraging perspectives, some participants, having support of their partners, managed to proceed with their decision to use contraception (if they chose to).
she [mother-in-law] was saying that contraceptives kill people’s eggs, and I won’t be able to have any more children in future” (R-30, 28years, in union, user). When further asked what she did about this situation, the participant stated that, “because I had my husband’s approval and permission,…I just went ahead and got the contraceptive.

## Women’s confidence to make and act on decisions is boosted by emotional support from partners, but May be constrained by social pressure to give birth
Partners provided crucial emotional support by acknowledging and validating women’s decisions, which boosted their confidence to act on their preferences. Women frequently reported being able to discuss or negotiate their desire to (or not) use contraception with their partners, by simply communicating their decision or by using their living conditions as basis for the discussion. Partner support included agreeing with the decision, permitting use, and granting women freedom to decide pregnancy timing.
“Then we sat down and I told my husband about the need to space; how whenever I went to the hospital I saw women receiving contraceptives. I told him I wanted to start injectable contraceptive so that I could space the next child…….He said I had thought well to space our children and he encouraged me to go ahead and start using those contraceptives […] I felt good because he had allowed me to go ahead with contraception.” (R-28, 28years, in union, user).
Besides validating women’s contraceptive decisions, partners were also a potential source of instrumental support, by exempting women from household responsibilities to go seek contraceptive services as one participant shared, “When Wednesday reached, he told me that do not try to touch any work or going to the garden, I want you to be the first to be worked on” (R-50, 40years, in union, user).
However, other partners may hinder women’s decision-making by pressuring them to conform to societal expectations of childbirth. “If I chose to use family planning, there could be trouble since my bride price was paid and I am expected to bear children”. This pressure was exacerbated by relatives, particularly in-laws, who perceived women’s primary role as giving birth and expanding the family. Such hindrances were fueled by fears of contraception side effects, including concerns about infertility and beliefs that contraception could “kill the eggs (ova)”. Often times, participants complied with this normative expectation of childbearing due to low decision-making power and fear of being called barren, excluded from household assets, or shamed, i.e., stigmatised, and being labelled a prostitute.
“….He [husband] said that right now I cannot allow you to go in family planning unless when you have given birth… He said that I should not go in family planning before giving birth because he also knows that when you use it affects [fertility]” (R-47, 20years, in union, non-user).
“If I did not have those two children, they [in-laws] would have abused me so much. (….) they would say that may be am infertile (…). They reached time and said that we got the uterus and gave it to the dogs, because of prevention while using family [planning]. I said that let me produce these children so that people stop talking a lot about me”. (R-57, 24years, user)
While emotional support from partners is crucial, it highlights potential limitations in the decision-making capacity of women in union. Regardless of whether (or not) a woman desires to use contraception, disapproval from a partner can limit her options, rendering decision-making either futile or unnecessary. Participants often desired to avoid conflicts in the home that could lead to divorce “he should not suspect that I am doing anything behind his back. If he is involved, then the better for our marriage”, or feared lacking partner support to manage any negative consequences of their decisions, along with social norms that position men as primary decision-makers.

## Discussion
We explored the mechanisms of influence of social networks on women’s agency in contraceptive decision-making and action, focusing on partners, relatives and peers. Our findings highlighted three key ways in which women exercised agency: negotiation, asserting their decisions, and resisting external influence. The findings further showed that, relatives and peers provide guidance to women about pregnancy prevention, sharing their lived experiences and providing reassurance. Additionally, women’s confidence to act on their contraceptive decisions is boosted by validation (emotional support) from their partners. However, women’s decision-making ability was potentially constrained by social pressure to give birth and information on contraceptive side effects.
Our findings point to an interplay of influences, indicating that while relatives and peers can provide guidance and encouragement aligned with women’s decisions regarding pregnancy prevention, this support alone is insufficient to fully empower women to exercise agency in their contraceptive decisions. In a context where most women are married or in union, 75% in our study and 61% in Uganda [ ], our findings showed that their ability to make contraceptive decisions was influenced by their partners through emotional social support. This affected women’s confidence or self-efficacy, compounded by societal norms that reinforce women’s perceived inferiority to their partners and childbirth expectations. Furthermore, information on contraceptive side effects shared by relatives and peers also influenced women’s decision-making. Therefore, multipronged approaches targeting all major influencers within women’s social networks are necessary to advance women’s reproductive health and rights. Interventions that promote male involvement in sexual and reproductive health are critically needed. These should include strategies to encourage contraceptive discussions among couples with equitable decision-making, while addressing social norms and power inequalities. These are high-impact practices that can improve partner support, gender equality, and equitable joint decision-making [ , ]. Additionally, reinforcing familial and peer networks with accurate information and economic empowerment can further enhance women’s contraceptive agency.
Partners, relatives, and peers are important influencers of women’s contraceptive decisions [ , ]. Our study adds to these studies by highlighting the significant yet often underreported influence of social support within women’s networks, as noted by Lowe and Moore [ ]. Compared to studies by Lowe and Moore (2014) and Behrman et al. (2002), which emphasize social learning as the dominant network mechanisms [ , ], our findings highlight the importance of social support mechanisms. We suggest that informational social support, particularly through sharing of personal experiences by trusted relatives or peers, naturally leads to or involves social learning. This aligns with Klarner et al.’s description, where social learning involves the exchange and assessment of information and the observation of others’ experiences within a network [ ]. Behrman et al. (2002) also noted the dominance of social learning over social influence. However, our findings, in line with Klarner et al.’s assertion that network mechanisms vary by structure and relationship characteristics, suggest that social learning is particularly important in networks involving relatives and peers, while social influence may be more critical in networks with partners [ ]. This distinction could be attributed to the reasons underlying involvement of these network members in women’s decision-making: trust and experience were important among relative and peers [ ], while social norms around marriage and childbearing were notable in partner involvement.
The study findings also showed that concerns about contraceptive side effects contributed to negative support from network members, such as social pressure to give birth. Also, the various perceptions and experiences of relatives and peers regarding side effects deterred women’s decision-making. In other studies, contraceptive side effects are often cited as a reason for not using contraception or advising against it [ , ]. These insights highlight the need for improved support systems to help women manage contraceptive side effects, should they choose to use contraception. This can be achieved through effective counselling by trained providers, where women receive comprehensive and accurate information about potential side effects. Additionally, reinforcing this information and addressing women’s fears may be necessary, though it may be complicated by the geographical inaccessibility of formal providers. Emotional support from peers, where women share their fears and receive reassurance, is a potential mechanism that can be leveraged at community level.

## Suggestions for further research
Women involve different people for varied reasons when making contraceptive decisions and they play different roles. However, it is unclear how the alignment/tensions from the different sources of influence (relative importance of each) ultimately affect women’s decisions and how this influence changes over time. Future studies should explore this.

## Study limitations
The in-depth exploration of women’s discussions about pregnancy prevention provided key insights on how key network members influence their decisions and actions. However, the focus on only women in this study may have provided a limited view on the forms of influence. Studies using a sociocentric approach (interviewing the network members mentioned by women and more) may provide more representative views on complex dynamics in women’s decision-making regarding pregnancy prevention. Our study includes a purposive sample of women and findings may not be generalizable to all women of reproductive age in the study districts or other parts of Uganda. Future studies should explore social network mechanisms for instance of urban-based women and other settings.

## Conclusions
Our study highlights pathways through which commonly reported social relationships influence women’s agency in making contraceptive decisions, offering valuable insights for social behaviour change programs. Relatives and peers provide informational support that guides women’s decision-making and facilitates access to formal services. Additionally, emotional support from relatives and peers helps women cope with contraception-related challenges. Partners play a critical role in bolstering women’s confidence to act on their decisions, with some offering further support by relieving them of household duties to seek contraceptive services. However, others hinder this process by imposing childbirth expectations. Interventions addressing family planning challenges in communities should consider and address these various forms of influence, ensuring that women have agency to make their own informed contraceptive decisions.
