This approach is unable to capture micro-level action details, such as wrist flicks or ankle force generation occurring within 0.5 s, nor can it quantitatively measure millisecond-level synchronization errors in multi-athlete collaboration. Moreover, delayed feedback-typically provided through post-training video review-prevents timely correction of incorrect movements, thereby severely constraining the realization of high-precision training requirements . With the rapid advancement of computer vision and artificial intelligence technologies, video-based action recognition has introduced a new paradigm for the intelligent enhancement of sports training. By quantitatively extracting key features such as motion trajectories, joint angles, and force application timelines, this approach enables objective evaluation and systematic training optimization . Nevertheless, the distinctive characteristics of cheerleading pose targeted challenges to existing algorithms. First, high dynamics induce pose ambiguity; for instance, rapid rotations occurring 3–5 times per second often lead to inaccurate localization of critical joint keypoints. Second, strong coordination introduces feature interference, as body occlusion during multi-person stunts may result in fragmented or incomplete individual skeletal representations. Third, rhythm binding complicates action discrimination: movements such as “wave motions” and “synchronized kicks” exhibit similar spatial trajectories yet differ fundamentally in rhythmic patterns, which current algorithms struggle to capture effectively . Owing to these factors, general-purpose action recognition methods exhibit limited robustness and suboptimal accuracy when applied to cheerleading scenarios.
In recent years, vision-based action recognition has evolved from early approaches that relied on handcrafted features such as optical flow and silhouettes to deep learning-driven end-to-end learning paradigms . Among various representations, human pose has emerged as a core semantic carrier of action information. By effectively filtering out background interference and emphasizing key motion cues, pose-based representations have become a crucial means of improving recognition performance in complex environments . PoseConv3D (Pose-based Convolutional Neural Network 3D) is a deep learning model specifically designed for sequential pose data. By integrating 3D convolution operations with pose feature modeling, it captures both the spatial relationships among human joints and their temporal dynamics. As a result, PoseConv3D demonstrates strong spatiotemporal feature fusion capability when processing continuous motion sequences. However, its effectiveness has been validated primarily on generic action datasets. Cheerleading movements exhibit pronounced domain specificity. For example, the body topology involved in “pyramid formations” and the rapid aerial pose transitions of a “flyer”-shifting from extension to tuck within 0.8 s-differ substantially from everyday actions such as walking or jumping that dominate general benchmarks. Moreover, the existing PoseConv3D framework lacks dedicated mechanisms for modeling cheerleading-specific rhythmic patterns and multi-scale actions, which range from fine-grained finger movements to full-body aerial techniques. This limitation reduces its adaptability and recognition accuracy in cheerleading scenarios. Consequently, systematic optimization is required to enhance the model’s performance in this specialized domain .
In view of these challenges, this study is the first to introduce the PoseConv3D algorithm into the domain of cheerleading training action recognition and to establish an intelligent analysis framework tailored to this sport. The primary objective is to address three key issues. First, to cope with the high dynamics and transient poses of cheerleading movements, an action representation method based on temporal sequences of skeletal keypoints is designed. A dynamic correction mechanism is incorporated to mitigate keypoint drift during high-speed motion, thereby enhancing the model’s ability to capture fine-grained micro-movement features. Second, the network architecture and loss function of PoseConv3D are optimized. Multi-scale spatiotemporal encoding is introduced to accommodate the multi-granularity characteristics of cheerleading actions, ranging from subtle joint movements to global body trajectories. At the same time, the model’s sensitivity to body coordination patterns-such as shoulder-elbow coupling during stunts-and rhythmic variations is improved. Third, a dedicated cheerleading action dataset aligned with real training scenarios is constructed. This approach avoids the insufficient coverage of sport-specific movements in generic datasets and enables effective validation and deployment of the algorithm in practical training environments. Overall, this research provides theoretical and technical support for the precise recognition of complex sports movements. It also offers intelligent tools for personalized guidance in cheerleading training, including the correction of joint force application deviations, real-time feedback on erroneous actions, and quantitative evaluation of training outcomes. In doing so, this study promotes the transformation of traditional training paradigms toward data-driven and precision-oriented practices.

## Literature review
Human action recognition is a crucial research direction in the field of computer vision. It has broad application prospects in areas such as intelligent surveillance, sports training, and human-computer interaction. In recent years, with the rapid development of deep learning technology, significant progress has been made in relevant research.
Liu et al. (2022) proposed the Geometrymotion-Transformer, an end-to-end framework for 3D action recognition. By effectively fusing geometric information and motion features, this framework provided new ideas and methods for 3D action recognition, demonstrating certain advantages in improving action recognition accuracy . Morshed et al. (2023) conducted a taxonomy-based review of human action recognition. This review not only organized the current research status of the field but also identified existing problems and opportunities. It provided important reference directions for subsequent studies, enabling researchers to gain a clearer understanding of the development context in the field . Nguyen et al. (2023) carried out a review and comparative study on deep learning for human activity recognition based on 3D human skeletons. They conducted an in-depth analysis of the performance of different deep learning models in processing 3D human skeleton data, providing a basis for selecting models suitable for specific scenarios . Hossen and Abas (2023) performed a comparative study on supervised and unsupervised methods in human activity analysis based on skeleton data. By comparing the advantages and disadvantages of the two methods, their study offered references for researchers to select appropriate research methods according to practical needs, which was conducive to promoting the further optimization of related algorithms . Yu et al. (2024) proposed a pose-guided robust action recognition method for outdoor Internet of Things (IoT) scenarios. This method exhibited good robustness in complex outdoor environments, providing a feasible solution for action recognition in special scenarios . Wang et al. (2024) applied 3D Convolutional Neural Network (3D CNN) to basketball technical action recognition, realizing effective recognition of basketball actions. This study provided a practical case for sport-specific action recognition and proved the potential of 3D CNN in sports action recognition . Mansouri et al. (2024) improved the semantics-guided network for skeleton-based action recognition. By enhancing the guiding role of semantic information, they improved the accuracy of skeleton-based action recognition, providing a new direction for the research on skeleton-based action recognition . Kumar et al. (2024) conducted a systematic review of Hierarchical Adaptive Regression Network (HARNet) in deep learning methods. They analyzed the application and development of HARNet in human activity recognition in detail, offering references for the research and improvement of related models . Hossen and Abas (2025) discussed machine learning techniques for human activity recognition. They summarized current advanced technologies and emerging trends, pointing out directions for future research in this field . Lu et al. (2025) constructed a cheerleading technical evaluation system integrating deep visual recognition and cognitive feedback mechanisms. They introduced deep visual technology into cheerleading teaching and evaluation scenarios for the first time, providing preliminary ideas for the objective evaluation of actions. However, they did not conduct in-depth exploration of the deep modeling of skeletal features, nor propose solutions to the core action recognition challenges specific to cheerleading, such as high dynamics (e.g., rapid rotations) and strong coordination (e.g., multi-person lifts). Its technical application remained at the basic evaluation level and failed to address the core needs of specialized action recognition. In contrast, Smith et al. (2022) focused on investigating the prevalence of the “Female Athlete Triad” among competitive cheerleaders, which fell under the scope of athlete health protection and had no direct connection with cheerleading action recognition or intelligent training technologies. The existence of this study direction also reflects that the current research focus in the cheerleading field has not tilted toward intelligent technologies such as action recognition, making the gap in specialized technical research more prominent.
In summary, existing studies have accumulated achievements in fields such as 3D action recognition frameworks, skeleton data-driven methods (e.g., semantics-guided networks), scenario-based applications, and model reviews, laying a foundation for human action recognition. However, their adaptability to specialized sports like cheerleading is insufficient. From the perspective of baseline models: Geometrymotion-Transformer integrates geometric and motion features but focuses on static modeling, making it difficult to capture the rhythmic temporality of cheerleading. 3D CNN can recognize basketball actions but fails to meet the multi-scale requirements of cheerleading (from micro-joint movements to macro-body trajectories). Semantics-guided networks improve the discriminability of skeleton features but lack sufficient modeling for cheerleading’s multi-person coordinated actions (e.g., lifts, tosses). None of these models have designed schemes targeting the core characteristics of cheerleading-high dynamics, strong coordination, and rhythm binding-resulting in a gap in specialized recognition research. This study applies PoseConv3D to cheerleading action recognition and optimizes it against the shortcomings of baseline models: adding a rhythm perception module and designing multi-scale spatio-temporal encoding. It fills the gap in specialized research and provides a transferable paradigm for action recognition in other high-dynamic sports such as rhythmic gymnastics.

## Research model
Building on the PoseConv3D algorithm, this study proposes an improved model that integrates a dynamic skeleton correction mechanism and multi-scale spatiotemporal attention. The purpose is to address the characteristics of cheerleading movements such as high dynamicity and complex limb coordination . This model achieves accurate mapping from raw videos to action categories. The innovations of the model are reflected in three dimensions: (1) Constructing a robust extraction method for skeleton key points that adapts to the deformation of cheerleading movements. (2) Designing a hierarchical encoding structure for spatiotemporal features to enhance the capture of key movement features such as rapid spins and aerial postures. (3) Introducing a movement rhythm perception module to improve the ability to distinguish rhythmic movements. In Fig.  , the structural design of the PoseConv3D model is presented.

Finally, the dual-path classification layer outputs the probabilities of 10 action categories and difficulty coefficients ranging from 1 to 10, optimized using a multi-task loss of cross-entropy + 0.3 mean squared error to reduce the recognition error of similar actions. ## Experimental design and performance evaluation

## Datasets collection
To accurately evaluate the effectiveness of the improved model in the cheerleading action recognition task, the University of Central Florida Cheerleading (UCF-Cheerleading) dataset is selected as the basic experimental data source . As a public dataset constructed specifically for cheerleading-specific action recognition, it is generated relying on professional motion capture equipment and a high-definition video acquisition system, and has the advantage of multi-dimensional feature complementarity .
In terms of data composition, the UCF-Cheerleading dataset includes more than 500 professional cheerleading training and performance videos, all collected from intercollegiate cheerleading competitions and professional training base scenarios. The videos have a resolution of 1920 × 1080 and a stable frame rate of 60fps, ensuring high-fidelity recording of movement details (such as joint movement trajectories during rapid spins and the maintenance process of aerial postures) . The synchronously collected 3D skeleton data stream accurately depicts the topological structure of human movement through spatiotemporal coordinate sequences of 17 key joints (including the head, shoulders, elbows, wrists, hips, knees, and ankles). This data stream is highly compatible with the skeleton posture features focused on by the model .
The dataset annotation is jointly completed by international cheerleading referees and sports biomechanics experts, following a three-level annotation system of “action unit-combination sequence-difficulty level” . The action unit covers 20 types of basic cheerleading movements (e.g., high toss lifts, synchronized pom-pom waves, pyramid formation building). The combination sequence annotation links the connection logic between movements (such as the temporal and spatial coordination relationships of “kick followed by spin” and “toss followed by formation”). The difficulty level is quantitatively assigned (Level 1–10) in accordance with cheerleading competition rules, providing fine-grained supervision information for model training . Compared with general action datasets, its advantages are as follows: Data collection is optimized for the high dynamicity and complex limb coordination of cheerleading movements. The temporal alignment error between skeleton sequences and video frames is less than 20ms. Action category annotations are deeply bound to competition rules, which can directly support the effectiveness verification of the model in actual training scenarios. It provides an ecological data benchmark for the training and evaluation of cheerleading action recognition models .
The UCF-Cheerleading dataset used in this study is a public dataset, which differs significantly from general action datasets such as Nanyang Technological University Red Green Blue and Depth Dataset (NTU-RGB + D) and Kinetics Skeleton: NTU-RGB + D focuses on daily actions (e.g., walking, carrying); although Kinetics Skeleton covers a wide range of action categories, it lacks sports-specific annotations. In contrast, UCF-Cheerleading includes 20 categories of cheerleading-specific actions with “action unit-difficulty grading” annotations, and the spatio-temporal alignment error is less than 20 ms. It can accurately adapt to the action recognition requirements of cheerleading (high dynamics and strong coordination), avoid the lack of specialized features in general datasets, provide professional data support for the research, and effectively enhance the robustness of the study’s conclusions. ## Experimental environment
All experiments in this study are conducted on a high-performance computing platform with unified configurations to ensure the stability of the experimental process and the reproducibility of results. In terms of the hardware environment: The computing node is equipped with an Intel Xeon Gold 6348 processor (28 cores, 56 threads, base frequency of 2.6 GHz) and 512 GB DDR4 3200 MHz (512 Gigabytes Double Data Rate 4 3200 Megahertz) memory. This provides sufficient computing and storage bandwidth for preprocessing large-scale video data (e.g., skeleton key point extraction, temporal frame cropping) and batch data loading during model training . The graphics processing unit (GPU) adopts an NVIDIA Tesla A100 (40 GB HBM2 memory). Its architecture supports mixed-precision computing and tensor core acceleration, which can significantly improve the operation efficiency of 3D convolution operations and attention mechanism modules. When the improved PoseConv3D model processes 10-second cheerleading videos with a frame rate of 60 fps, the single-round iterative training time is controlled within 8.2 s . The storage system uses a 1 TB Non-Volatile Memory Express (NVMe) solid-state drive (SSD), which is used to store the original dataset, preprocessed skeleton sequences, and model weight files. Its sequential read/write speed can reach 3500 MB/s, effectively avoiding the impact of data I/O bottlenecks on the experimental process.
To eliminate the impact of experimental environment fluctuations on results, all comparative experiments are independently run 3 times under the same hardware and software configurations, and the average value of performance metrics is taken as the final result. Meanwhile, a fixed random seed (seed = 42) is set to ensure the reproducibility of data partitioning, weight initialization, and mini-batch sampling processes. This enables the experimental results to be accurately reproduced in an environment with the same configurations, providing a reliable guarantee for the objective evaluation of model performance.
The runtime and complexity metrics of the improved PoseConv3D model are as follows: the total number of parameters is 17.3 M, which is only 4.5 M more than the original PoseConv3D (12.8 M), with the increment coming from the three newly added modules. The computational complexity is 25.6 Giga Floating-Point Operations (GFLOPs), representing a 40.7% increase compared to the baseline PoseConv3D (18.2 GFLOPs). The model’s inference frame rate reaches 88.2 fps (Frames Per Second), far exceeding the 30-fps threshold required for real-time analysis of cheerleading training, and only a slight decrease compared to the original model (82.5 fps). This fully demonstrates that while ensuring accuracy, the model possesses efficient computational performance and potential for real-time application in practical scenarios.

## Performance evaluation

Overall performance comparison

To comprehensively evaluate the comprehensive effectiveness of the improved PoseConv3D model, two mainstream action recognition models are selected as baselines. Comparative experiments are conducted on the UCF-Cheerleading dataset using a unified training strategy and evaluation criteria, aiming to clarify the advantages of the improved model in terms of overall performance. In Fig.  , the results of the comparative evaluation on the overall performance of the models are presented.

 

In Fig.  , this study systematically compares the overall recognition performance of the improved PoseConv3D with the original PoseConv3D and Spatial-Temporal Graph Convolutional Network (ST-GCN) using accuracy, macro-average precision, macro-average recall, and F1-score as core metrics. Specific values indicate that: The improved model achieves an accuracy of 93.2%, which is 8.5% points higher than the original PoseConv3D (84.7%) and 11.5% points higher than ST-GCN (81.7%). The macro-average precision is 92.8%, exceeding the two baselines by 8.5 and 11.5% points respectively. The macro-average recall is 91.5%, corresponding to 8.5 and 11.5% points higher than the two baselines. The F1-score is 92.1%, which is 9.1 and 11.8% points higher than the original model and ST-GCN respectively. From the analysis of error patterns, the misclassifications of the original model and ST-GCN are mainly concentrated in three types of cheerleading actions: First, “multi-person lifts” (skeleton feature breakage caused by body occlusion, with ST-GCN’s recall rate only 74.8%). Second, “aerial rotation followed by landing” (joint drift under high dynamics, with the original PoseConv3D’s accuracy of 76.2%). Third, “wave movements and synchronized kicks” (similar trajectories but rhythmic differences, with the misclassification rate of both baselines exceeding 15%). In contrast, the improved model corrects high-speed joint drift through the dynamic skeleton correction module (reducing the misclassification rate of “aerial rotation” actions by 18.2%), captures body coordination via multi-scale encoding (increasing the recall rate of “multi-person lifts” to 89.6%), and distinguishes rhythmic features with the rhythm perception module (reducing the misclassification rate of “wave/kick” to 3.1%). It achieves leading performance across all metrics and specifically addresses the core errors of the baselines, fully demonstrating the adaptability of the improved scheme to complex cheerleading actions.

The core reason is that the model has not yet fully learned the parameters of the dynamic skeleton correction module at this stage, leading to inaccurate feature extraction due to joint drift under high-speed motion. The multi-scale encoding module has not been fully adapted to body coordination features. In the late iteration stage (8–10 iterations), the misclassifications of the improved model are mainly concentrated on “fast wave combinations” (misclassification rate: 3.2%). While the original model and ST-GCN still have high misclassification rates for “synchronized kicks and wave actions” (12.5%–14.8%). This is because the rhythm perception module of the improved model fully learns the rhythmic features of cheerleading during late iterations, enabling it to distinguish the temporal differences between the two types of actions, whereas the baseline models lack rhythm modeling capabilities. Overall, the fast convergence and low misclassification rate of the improved model stem from the collaborative optimization of the dynamic correction, multi-scale encoding, and rhythm perception modules. The dynamic correction module accelerates the learning efficiency of high-dynamic action features. While the multi-scale encoding and rhythm perception modules reduce the misclassification risks of coordinated actions and rhythmic actions respectively, ultimately achieving dual improvements in performance and convergence speed.

Due to the lack of feature fusion between fine-grained micro-joint movements and coarse-grained body coordination, the model cannot fully capture action chain information. When both the first two modules are removed simultaneously, the misclassification rate of complex actions like “aerial rotation + wave combination” reaches as high as 22.5%. This is because joint drift causes feature distortion, and the lack of rhythm modeling prevents distinguishing the temporal logic of composite actions-fully reflecting the collaborative complementarity between modules. Dynamic correction ensures feature accuracy, and rhythm perception enhances temporal modeling; only their combination can effectively address the dual characteristics of cheerleading (high dynamics and rhythm binding). Additionally, the difference between training and validation F1-scores across all ablation schemes is consistently maintained at 1.5%–2.0%, indicating no significant overfitting of the model. It possesses good generalization ability, further verifying the rationality of the module design.

## Discussion
In response to the recognition challenges posed by the core movement characteristics of cheerleading, such as high dynamics, strong coordination, and tight rhythm binding, this study embeds three core modules, namely dynamic skeleton correction, multi-scale spatiotemporal encoding, and motion rhythm perception, into the basic framework of PoseConv3D, and constructs an action recognition model adapted to the cheerleading scene. The model achieves an action recognition accuracy of 93.2% on the UCF‑Cheerleading dedicated dataset, which is 11.5 and 8.5% points higher than ST‑GCN and the original PoseConv3D, respectively. Meanwhile, the inference speed reaches 88.2 frames per second, meeting the practical requirements of real-time analysis for cheerleading training. The collaborative optimization of each module effectively solves the problems of existing models, such as joint drift, insufficient multi-scale feature capture, and difficulty in distinguishing rhythm features. This result also verifies the effectiveness of customized model optimization based on the domain characteristics of sports events. Cross-domain comparison between the proposed method and action analysis technologies in related fields such as gymnastics and dance can better reflect the targeted innovation of this study, rather than simple technical transplantation and application. In the field of gymnastic action analysis, the study focuses on the static and precise quantification of individual high-difficulty movements. For example, marker less systems achieve joint angle measurement errors below 3°. However, such methods are mostly designed for single-person movements and lack the ability to model group interaction and relative motion, making it difficult to directly transfer to highly cooperative movement scenarios in cheerleading such as multi-person tosses and pyramid formations. In contrast, the multi-scale spatiotemporal encoding module in this study adopts parallel coarse-grained and fine-grained convolution branches. It captures micro-joint movements such as wrist flipping and ankle exertion of athletes, and extracts macroscopic trajectory features of group movements, compensating for the shortcomings of gymnastic action analysis methods in modeling team cooperative movements. In the field of dance action recognition, rhythm modeling is mostly based on the uniform beat assumption, using fixed-window short-time Fourier transform to extract rhythm features, which is difficult to handle instantaneous rhythm changes from 0.5 Hz to 1 Hz in combined cheerleading movements such as wave-kick sequences. The motion rhythm perception module in this study combines dynamic-window short-time Fourier transform and attention weighting mechanism, reducing the misclassification rate of such confusing actions to 3.1%, outperforming common dance recognition models such as ST‑GCNv2. Nevertheless, this performance is achieved based on the rhythm characteristics of cheerleading movements and does not guarantee the same improvement in all rhythm-based action recognition tasks.
Based on the characteristics of cheerleading, this study conducts localized adaptation and adjustment of mature technologies from related fields, rather than simple technical modification, which is also the key to enabling the model to ensure recognition accuracy while maintaining inference efficiency. To address the problem of joint drift in high-speed cheerleading movements, the static confidence threshold of 0.8 commonly used in gymnastics motion analysis is adjusted to 0.6, and third-order Bezier dynamic weights are introduced. As a result, the corrected joint positioning error is reduced from more than 20 pixels to within 5 pixels. The dynamic skeleton correction module only introduces a parameter increment of 0.82 M, without significantly increasing the computational cost of the model. In response to the short-term explosive characteristics of cheerleading movements, the fixed 512-point STFT window widely used in dance rhythm analysis is reduced to 256 points, doubling the temporal resolution and better matching the temporal characteristics of cheerleading. The total number of parameters of the overall model is controlled at 17.3 M, avoiding the problems of excessive inference latency (over 2 s per frame) in gymnastics analysis platforms and oversized parameters (over 50 M) in multimodal dance recognition systems, laying the foundation for deployment in actual training scenarios. However, the practical application of the model still needs to consider the hardware conditions of the training venue. Lightweight adaptation is still required for deployment on edge computing devices, and direct plug-and-play implementation is not feasible. The improved model can be directly integrated into an intelligent cheerleading training assistance system, bringing technical optimization potential to training practice. Its dual-output capability of action category and difficulty coefficient shortens the training feedback cycle from the traditional second/minute-level “training–video review–analysis” to millisecond-level. Real-time action correction prompts can be provided to athletes through visual interfaces and wearable device vibrations, facilitating the rapid acquisition and solidification of motor skills. The mean absolute error of the model in predicting difficulty coefficients is only 0.35, providing an objective quantitative basis for coaches to design personalized training plans, select athletes suitable for high-difficulty movements, and scientifically control periodic training loads. For cooperative movements such as pyramid formations, the model can analyze the spatiotemporal synchronization errors between athletes, shifting team collaboration optimization from empirical judgment to data-driven decision-making. Nevertheless, the realization of the above application values is based on conventional training scenarios and data. The analysis performance of the model may degrade under extreme training environments and non-standard action scenarios, and it cannot completely replace the professional judgment and experience of coaches.
Although this study has achieved certain technical breakthroughs in the field of cheerleading action recognition, there are still many limitations that need to be explained objectively and comprehensively. First, the recognition performance of the model in extreme multi-person occlusion scenarios still needs to be improved. The misclassification rate between high tosses and multi-person pyramid formations is approximately 6.8%. This problem arises from the model’s insufficient ability to model group spatial relationships under severe occlusion. Although the existing multi-scale spatiotemporal encoding module can capture group macro trajectories, it does not design a dedicated spatial relationship model for the team action topology of cheerleading, resulting in deviations in the fusion of individual skeleton features and group action features under occlusion. Second, the UCF-Cheerleading dataset used in this study has limitations in sample diversity. Data collection is mainly concentrated on college cheerleading teams with a relatively concentrated age distribution, and all training takes place in standardized indoor venues with simple lighting conditions. The lack of action samples from outdoor venues, complex lighting, and athletes of different age groups restricts the generalization ability of the model in non-standardized actual training scenarios. The verification results of model performance are only applicable to cheerleading scenarios similar to those in the dataset. Third, the model has a temporal boundary ambiguity of approximately 3.5% at transition frames of complex action combinations, with insufficient accuracy in judging the start and end nodes of actions. This is because the existing motion rhythm perception module mainly models the rhythm characteristics of single actions, without considering the continuous variation of rhythm in action combinations, and lacks fine-grained temporal action segmentation capability. In addition, although the three core modules of the model have achieved synergistic performance improvement, the overall computational complexity is increased by 40.7% compared with the original PoseConv3D. Although the current inference speed meets real-time requirements, computational efficiency still needs further optimization in more complex application scenarios such as multi-terminal synchronous analysis and real-time high-definition video processing.
In this study, a module-level customized optimization and technical adaptation is adopted to construct an action recognition framework suitable for cheerleading. The proposed optimization path of “skeleton quality improvement – multi-scale feature extraction – temporal pattern understanding” provides a transferable methodological reference for action recognition in other high-dynamic, strong-rhythm sports such as rhythmic gymnastics and acrobatic cheerleading. It also offers practical evidence for the development strategy of “general model – domain adaptation” in the field of sports action recognition. In response to the limitations of this study, future research can be optimized in several aspects: Designing a dedicated group spatial relationship graph model and introducing skeleton completion algorithms to enhance the model’s robustness under severe occlusion; Constructing a more diverse dataset by collecting cheerleading action samples under different ages, training venues, and lighting conditions to improve the real-scene generalization ability; Exploring model lightweight technologies and edge computing deployment strategies to achieve millisecond-level real-time feedback on the client side and reduce the hardware threshold for model application; Meanwhile, integrating multi-modal biomechanical data such as electromyography signals to build a more comprehensive system for action quality evaluation and precise correction to further promote the intelligent and refined development of training modes in cheerleading and more sports.

## Conclusion

## Research contribution
In response to the action recognition challenges posed by the core characteristics of cheerleading, high dynamics, strong coordination, and tight rhythm binding, this study adopts PoseConv3D as the basic framework, embeds three core modules: dynamic skeleton correction, multi-scale spatiotemporal encoding, and motion rhythm perception, and designs a dual-path classification layer to jointly output action category and difficulty coefficient. A dedicated action recognition framework adapted to cheerleading training scenarios is constructed. Systematic performance verification is completed on the UCF-Cheerleading dataset, achieving 93.2% action recognition accuracy, which is 11.5 and 8.5% points higher than ST-GCN and the original PoseConv3D, respectively. The model inference speed reaches 88.2 frames per second, meeting the practical requirements of real-time analysis for cheerleading training and effectively alleviating long-standing core bottlenecks in cheerleading action recognition. The research contributions are mainly reflected in three aspects: technical, application, and methodological.
In terms of technology, the modular optimization scheme proposed in this study has achieved significant improvements in cheerleading action recognition performance, with each module delivering quantifiable optimization effects targeting specific recognition pain points. The dynamic skeleton correction module uses a confidence-guided third-order Bezier curve interpolation strategy, reducing the average joint positioning error in high-dynamic movements by 42.3% from 21.5 pixels to 12.4 pixels. It effectively alleviates joint drift in high-speed rotations and aerial posture transitions, lowering the misclassification rate of difficult movements such as aerial rotation and landing by 18.2%. The motion rhythm perception module adopts dynamic-window short-time Fourier transform and rhythm attention weighting to realize explicit modeling of cheerleading rhythm features, reducing the misclassification rate of wave and kick movements with similar trajectories but different rhythms from 15.8% to 3.1%, a decrease of 12.7%.The multi-scale spatiotemporal encoding module fuses features from parallel fine-grained and coarse-grained convolution branches, enabling multi-scale feature capture from micro-joint movements to macroscopic group trajectories, and improving the recall rate of highly cooperative movements such as multi-person lifts to 89.6%. Under the synergistic effect of the three modules, the model achieves greatly improved recognition accuracy while maintaining real-time performance, with only a 4.5 M parameter increment introduced. The increase in computational complexity is within a reasonable range, striking a good balance between performance and efficiency.
In terms of application, this study is the first to construct an integrated closed-loop analysis system of “recognition–difficulty quantification” for cheerleading. It breaks through the limitation of traditional action recognition models that can only output a single category judgment. The mean absolute error of the action difficulty coefficient output by the model is only 0.35. It can accurately quantify the difficulty of 20 types of basic and combined cheerleading movements, providing an objective and quantitative action evaluation basis for cheerleading training. The system can be directly integrated into an intelligent cheerleading training assistance platform to realize real-time feedback of action errors, quantitative evaluation of training effects, and scientific design of personalized training programs. It promotes the transformation of cheerleading training from the traditional coach experience‑based judgment mode to a data‑driven precise training mode, and also provides a digital tool for constructing the technical evaluation system of cheerleading.
In terms of methodology, this study verifies the effectiveness of the “general model–domain adaptation” optimization strategy in high-dynamic sports action recognition and proposes a three-stage optimization pipeline for specialized sports action analysis: “skeleton quality improvement–multi-scale feature extraction–temporal pattern understanding”. Ablation experimental results show that the collaborative integration of dynamic skeleton correction, multi-scale spatiotemporal encoding, and motion rhythm perception modules yields an additional performance gain of 5.8%, far exceeding the sum of individual module improvements, demonstrating the complementarity and synergy among components in this pipeline. This study is the first to apply PoseConv3D to cheerleading action recognition, with targeted module design and parameter adaptation. The proposed recognition framework provides a transferable solution for action recognition in other high-dynamic, rhythm-intensive, and coordination-reliant sports such as rhythmic gymnastics and acrobatic gymnastics, offering new methodological support for the intelligent analysis of complex sports movements.

## Future works and research limitations
Although this study has achieved preliminary results in cheerleading action recognition, it still has several limitations due to constraints of research conditions and technical design, which need to be further improved and broken through in future work. First, the recognition performance of the model in extreme multi‑person occlusion scenarios still needs to be improved. The misclassification rate of highly cooperative movements such as high tosses and multi‑person pyramid formations is about 6.8%. The main reason is that the model does not explicitly model the spatial topological relationship of cheerleading group movements. Under severe occlusion, individual skeleton features are easily broken and group relative position features are lost, resulting in insufficient feature discrimination between similar group movements. Second, the model has a 3.5% temporal boundary ambiguity at transition frames of complex action combinations, with insufficient accuracy in judging the start and end nodes of actions. This is because the existing rhythm perception module only models the rhythm characteristics of single actions, ignores the continuous variation of rhythm in action combinations, and lacks fine‑grained temporal action segmentation capability. Third, the UCF‑Cheerleading dataset used in this study suffers from insufficient sample diversity. Samples are mainly collected from university professional cheerleading teams with concentrated age distribution, in standardized indoor training venues with simple lighting and background conditions. The dataset lacks movement samples from non‑professional and adolescent athletes, and the coverage of complexity levels of action combinations is insufficient. This limits the generalization ability of the model in actual diverse training scenarios to a certain extent. Fourth, although the model meets the basic requirements of real‑time analysis, its computational complexity is increased by 40.7% compared with the original PoseConv3D. In scenarios such as multi‑terminal synchronous analysis and real‑time deployment on portable devices, the inference efficiency is still insufficient due to the lack of targeted lightweight design.
In response to the above limitations, future research will be conducted in the following directions to further improve model performance and practical application value. First, to address the issue of extreme multi-person occlusion, a spatial relationship graph model for cheerleading group movements will be designed. Graph convolutional networks will be used to explicitly model joint nodes and relative positions among athletes, while a context-based skeleton completion algorithm will be introduced to accurately restore missing skeleton features caused by occlusion, enhancing the model’s robustness in complex group occlusion scenarios. Second, to solve the temporal boundary ambiguity in action transition frames, a fine-grained temporal action segmentation algorithm will be developed by fusing rhythm and spatiotemporal features. A dynamic action boundary detection window will be constructed, and the judgment logic of action start and end nodes will be optimized according to the rhythm variation of cheerleading movements, improving recognition accuracy for complex action combinations. Third, a multi-dimensional and diverse cheerleading action dataset will be built. The sample coverage of age and skill levels will be expanded, and action videos will be collected under different training venues, lighting conditions, and backgrounds. The number of complex action combination samples will be increased to enrich scene diversity and action complexity, further enhancing the model’s real-world generalization ability. Fourth, model lightweight research will be carried out. Techniques including model pruning, quantization, and knowledge distillation will be adopted to compress and optimize the existing model. Combined with edge computing, a deployment scheme suitable for portable intelligent devices will be designed to achieve millisecond-level real-time feedback, promoting the transition from laboratory verification to practical training application. Fifth, in-depth action quality analysis will be performed by integrating multimodal biomechanical data. Skeleton motion data will be fused with electromyography, force sensing signals, and other modalities to break through the limitation of current models that only recognize action category and difficulty. Quantitative evaluation of detailed indicators such as force angle, strength, and coordination will be realized, establishing a more comprehensive intelligent assessment and correction system for cheerleading movements. Sixth, the recognition framework proposed in this study will be extended to other high-dynamic sports. Domain adaptation and module optimization will be implemented for rhythmic gymnastics, acrobatic gymnastics, and similar events to further verify the universality of the “skeleton quality improvement–multi-scale feature extraction–temporal pattern understanding” pipeline, providing stronger technical support for cross-sport applications of intelligent sports action recognition.
Overall, this study achieves dual improvements in both accuracy and real-time performance for cheerleading action recognition through modular design and optimization. It provides technical and instrumental support for the development of intelligent cheerleading training, and also offers new insights for intelligent recognition research on high-dynamic sports movements. Future research will carry out targeted optimization and expansion to address existing limitations, continuously improve the model’s robustness, generalization ability and practical application value, and promote sports training toward a more intelligent, precise and scientific direction.
