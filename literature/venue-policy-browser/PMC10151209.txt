It existed in a context that was yet to recognize the potential and the need for a forum, an outlet for the thought leadership being generated in the many corridors of the elite management research institutions of the day. As we commemorate the 50th Volume of DECISION, we, as management researchers and practitioners, have a greater responsibility towards generating, articulating, externalizing, questioning and consolidating the knowledge that we create and possess, not only for the benefit of current and future generations of management practitioners and researchers, but also in fulfilling our role as agents of change influencing thought and action in the society at large.
In this issue of DECISION, we present eight articles. In a timely research commentary, Nandita Roy and Moutusy Maity examine ChatGPT and its impact on ‘textuality’ focusing on three key concepts and the accompanying dangers—the naturalness in the interaction, the information asymmetry, and the lack of the end-user’s personal control. The popular press is abound with opinions, anecdotal experiences and predictions of where generative AI may head. Roy and Maity carefully abstract these and draw our attention to the implications of the nature of this technology for human-technology interaction as well as some of the ethical challenges it brings forth.
Ethical challenges are also the focus of Sachinthanee Dissanayake and Ananda Jayawardana’s paper on the influence of power and love of money on (un)ethical decision making. While they find that persons with love of money motive and/or a personal sense of power are more likely to engage in unethical decision making, they also argue that this may not be the case in higher power distance-oriented environments, where people are more likely to behave in a manner that benefits their superiors given the hierarchical nature of such environments. Dissanayake and Jayawardana’s findings also open up questions for further research on ethical decision making in organizations particularly around sense of power.
Monika Stelmaszczyk, Agata Pierścieniak and Denisa Abrudan, in the third paper, in this issue, examine the impact of ambidexterity-oriented managerial decision making on the absorptive capacity of an organization in the context of new product development. Two aspects are interesting in the operationalization of their study. First, they bring together two key seemingly unrelated constructs in organizational capabilities research—ambidexterity and absorptive capacity and second, and they validate their model with data from a textile firm operating through a circular economy model in Poland. We continue the discussion around strategic decision making in organizations through Joanna Radomska, Aleksandra Szpulak and Przemysław Wołczek’s work on open strategy measurement. There is little doubt that there is an increasing emphasis on opening up the strategic decision-making processes in organizations to a broader set of stakeholders. Radomska and her team developed a scale for measuring the degree of such strategy openness through two key constructs—transparency and inclusion.
In another paper, in this issue, Choudhary, Kar and Ilavarasan use text mining and netnography on social media data from tourism service consumers combined with qualitative data from interviews with tourism service providers, to identify aspects that impact tourists’ experience. Their analysis revealed that dynamic pricing, resource accessibility, infrastructure availability and security considerations are amongst the key factors influencing tourism service experience.
Jaspreet Kaur, Madhu Vij and Ajay Kumar Chauhan, in their paper on corporate credit ratings (CCR), engage in a systematic review of published literature across a 20-year time period on the signals and drivers of CCR. They find that financial factors such as profitability, leverage, coverage and firm size have received much greater attention than non-financial factors such as board and top management characteristics, ownership structure or economic factors such as macro-economic indicators and stock market performance. Kaur and her team also draw our attention to the need for further studies on CCR from developing countries where information asymmetry is more prevalent.
We switch frames in the seventh paper of this issue, where Ramesh Bollapragada, Uday S Rao and Junying Wu, present some interesting results from their computational experiments to compare wireline and wireless network hubs particularly looking at fixed cost ratio, network demand and link capacity. They find that in most scenarios, the mixed network is likely to be more profitable than single technology networks. However, when link capacity is sufficiently high, the wireline-only networks perform competitively and when fixed cost ratio if sufficiently low, the wireless-only networks perform optimally. Their findings have implications for network designers and technology infrastructure decision makers.
The final article in this issue is a case study, but with a twist. As educators in management disciplines, many of us experience challenges in mimicking the real-life organizational environment in the classroom in order to facilitate practice-oriented learning experiences. We often use case studies to fill the gap. In the case titled Novolutions in Covid-19, Kishinchand Poornima Wasdani and Mathew J Manimala use live case analysis as an extension of the case study method. They highlight that bringing in the participation of the decision makers from the organization of the case and facilitating an active conversation between students and the decision makers not only adds realism but also significantly enhances the engagement levels given that students turn into ‘consultants’ for the organization being discussed. Wasdani and Manimala also draw our attention to the many risks; we may face when attempting to implement a live case method.

## Reflections of Former EICs of DECISION

## Anup K Sinha (Editor-in-Chief—Jan 1996 to Dec 1998)
When I took over the editorship of DECISION, the condition of the journal was poor due to neglect and inefficient management. This does not imply that the editor or the editorial committee were entirely at fault. It meant that the institutional support was missing in terms of contributions, attention and funds. The journal was behind schedule by over 18 months, and some subscribers were threatening to sue IIMC for non-publication of issues for which they had paid in advance. I realized that my colleagues were too busy trying to publish in outside journals. It was not fashionable (or deemed creditable) to publish in one’s own house journal. No one sought out good contributions for DECISION too. So, contributions were poor in terms of quality, and even the flow of these low-quality products began drying up fast.
I tried discussing with my Publications Committee and later in faculty meetings regarding two choices. One, we could continue to be a high-brow true-blue academic journal publishing research output with double-blind peer-review screening. It would take time to build trust in the journal and be able to attract reasonably good quality academic papers. Personal requests by myself and other colleagues would be essential. The second option was to reposition the journal with more space devoted to practitioners and corporate managers in talking about more work-a-day issues faced by managers. IIM Bangalore had at that time launched their Management Review journal along these lines. Practicing managers would find this easier to read than say, Vikalpa of IIM Ahmedabad.
One thing turned out to be according to my expectations: my colleagues were, without exception, of the opinion that DECISION should continue to be a purely academic research-based journal. This would then imply that we all put in efforts to “relaunch” DECISION. The first two or three issues after the revival would have to be good quality with invited articles. Some of our own colleagues would have to take ownership of the institute’s journal and contribute. We also want the production quality to become better with a better printer doing the job and using better quality paper. The cover design was kept the same to ensure quick identification and recall. Remember, the e-mail was just becoming popular, and websites were absent. More funds would be required.
Another operational issue that was much time and effort consuming, was getting the contributions reviewed. I had to depend on colleagues so that I could chase them to be punctual and ensure the next issues were printed on time. This was my hardest task: getting reviewers, getting their comments on time, and getting the contributors to respond to the reviewer’s comments. Hard copies had to be photocopied and sent though the postal service. Sometimes the copies would get lost in transit.
It was more difficult than it sounds now. But on hindsight, I realize that enthusiasm and commitment can make things happen that lots of experience can often fail to deliver. I was lucky when my term ended. My successor kept the pressure on and saw through the period of transition from hard paper copies to soft copies and e-mails. 

## Anindya Sen (Editor-in-Chief—Jan 1999 to Dec 2003)
I joined IIMC at the beginning of 1998, and the very next year, I was requested to take over as Chairman of the FRP Committee. FRP meant Fellowship, Research and Publications. Therefore, on a day-to-day basis, one had to look after the Fellowship program, do a preliminary screening of applications for attending conferences, etc. and look after the publications of the institute. The latter involved publishing the institute’s journal DECISION and processing the Working Paper (WP) series. Other than publishing DECISION, the work was not onerous, because the office had a capable officer and a committed staff (old-timers will remember Mr. P.B. Saha and Mr. Mahato). Their system of file-keeping was a mystery to outsiders but somehow it worked. Every time I asked for some data, it was provided to me quite quickly!!
Matters were rather more complicated when it came to the publication of DECISION. Apparently, for a few years, it had been dormant. No issues had been published. Submitted articles were not acknowledged and obviously did not see the light of the day. My immediate predecessor, Prof. Anup Sinha, had faced quite a challenge when he took over as editor and had decided to first clear the backlog by bringing out one issue of DECISION for each one of the gap years. By the time I took over, there were no missing years. I decided to continue for at least one more year the practice of issuing one volume of DECISION to cover the entire year. The 1999 edition of DECISION therefore has only one issue.
I also felt the need to change the look and feel of the journal. Fortunately, I discovered a gentleman who had a small printing concern in Kasba. He mainly catered to the export market, printing brochures and souvenirs as well as stickers for fishing rods (for export to Germany)! He jumped at the opportunity to publish a more substantial product. For the next four years, he kept on producing the journal, catering to all our demands and adjusting to our delays with aplomb.
My term as the FP Committee Chair ended in 2001. However, I was requested to continue as the editor of DECISION, and therefore, I remained the editor till 2003.
As the list of contributors to the 1999 issue demonstrates, running the journal took a heavy toll on my social capital. Erstwhile and current colleagues were cajoled, flattered and threatened into writing for the journal. I became more ambitious in 2000 and started bringing out two volumes a year—one for January–June and the other for July–December. DECISION in fact continued to be bi-annual till 2008, after which it started having three issues a year. Now of course there are four issues each year.
DECISION has always strived to be different from the journals brought out by sister IIMs. It has not emphasized case writing, nor has it sought to be an easy compendium of encapsulated topical knowledge for practitioners. In fact, it has determinedly espoused rigorous, theoretical articles and has not shied away from “academic” publications. Perhaps the legacy of a “quant” school helped us to preserve this character of DECISION.
I am heavily indebted to many of my friends and colleagues for being generous with their contributions to DECISION. What makes me really happy is that in recent years, DECISION has had a succession of committed editors with the vision to take it forward in different ways—increasing the number of issues, getting contributions from India and abroad (I believe that the rejection rate is now respectably high) and signing up with a well-known publisher to take charge of publication, marketing and distribution. For a number of years, I was on the editorial board and found it invigorating to debate and discuss with my young colleagues the best way forward for the journal. I am sure that DECISION is now in safe and capable hands. 

## Annapurna Shaw (Editor-in-Chief—Jan 2004 to Mar 2006)
I was the Editor of DECISION for around two years, from January 2004 till March 2006, covering five issues of the Journal. At that time, the journal was just starting to recover after a period of slowdown in the mid-nineties. My main aim was to improve the quality of the journal and ensure its timely publication and in this I was ably assisted by other members of the Publications Committee, namely, Professors Balram Avittathur and Sougata Ray.
When I took over, subscribers were not many for a national journal aspiring to become international and were confined largely to management institutes and universities with management departments within the country. Thus, readership was limited and submissions of research articles arriving at the editor’s desk were also limited. In fact, one of the biggest challenges I faced as editor was ensuring a sufficient number of quality manuscripts to enable the timely publication of each issue of the journal. This necessitated appeals to our own faculty members to support the journal by submitting their current research, contacting colleagues in other IIMs and universities and planning and advertising ‘Special Issues’ on specific topics. One such successful effort was the ‘Special Issue’ on management education which saw the contribution of a number of interesting articles by both in-house faculty and outside researchers.
One noteworthy development during my editorial tenure was the uploading of the Institute’s Working Paper series thus making it available in soft copy. This was at the suggestion of MIS group faculty. The hard copy version of the series was gradually stopped. Another development was the starting of communication with the international journal data platform, EBSCO, to widen the reach of the journal and increase its visibility. There were many exchanges of email, and as DECISION was then unknown to them, samples (in hard copy) had to be sent by surface mail to the organization. The final acceptance of inclusion by EBSCO occurred during the tenure of editors down the line.
Some internal processes of journal upgradation were also undertaken. For instance, while a blind review system had been in place earlier, during my editorship, the blind review was extended to involve two reviewers. This was to ensure a more thorough and balanced appraisal and better feedback to contributors although its downside was that some reviewers were slow to get back delaying the editorial process.
As the journal was published only in the hard copy version then, the editor’s job also entailed supervising the activities of three staff members occupying a large office in C-Block. While printing of the journal was done by an outside firm, distribution of the hard copies to in-house faculty, mailing them to subscribers and maintaining updated lists of subscribers was done by them. When I took over in 2004, the latter task was still being done by hand in a ledger.
Procuring a PC for the staff member who did this and kept other accounts, helped speed up office processes.
All in all, being editor of DECISION was an interesting experience and, along with the Committee, guiding it towards greater heights was very satisfying. 

## Biju Paul Abraham (Editor-in-Chief—April 2006 to March 2008)
The years from 2006 to 2008 marked a phase in Indian management education and research where issues related to the globalization of finance dominated. What is especially noteworthy is the large number of submissions to DECISION that analyzed the behavior of Indian share and forex markets at a time when it was attracting more foreign capital. As Editor, during this phase, one of the main challenges was to sift through the large numbers of submission that we received in this area both from India and abroad. The fact that IIM Calcutta was well known for its expertise in finance certainly helped to attract good papers to the journal. But it created challenges of its own, primarily in terms of finding the right reviewers for each paper, and once the reviews came in, choosing which ones to publish.
The issue of volatility in financial markets was a common theme across many papers. A paper in the July–December 2006 issue considered ten different statistical and econometric volatility forecasting models in the context of Indian stock and forex markets and identified the different models that would suit the specific characteristics of these two markets. Another paper in the same issue considered the impact of futures trading, which was still in its infancy in India, on the spot market and established that the nature of volatility of the Indian markets was quite different from those abroad. Other papers considered issues such as stock price manipulation and accounting fraud, price signaling in the NIFTY futures market, impact of monetary policy announcements on Indian stock markets and bank mergers.
One of the most interesting papers was in an area of research that was still emerging in the first decade of this century—behavioral finance. A paper in the July–December 2007 issue jointly authored by an academic at a French business school and an Indian wine consultant looked behavioral finance issues related to the question of when a wine company decides to list and de-list from the stock markets. Social and cultural issues rather than profit-maximization were identified as being critical to such decisions in wine companies.
However, there were other emerging areas of research that were showcased in the journal as well. A paper in the January–June 2007 issue analyzed purchase motivations of those who bought organic food in India and identified some differences between Indian consumers and those abroad. Another paper in the same issue looked at the role of gender in investment decisions and discussed reasons for variation in awareness and satisfaction for males and females for different types of investment.
Another area of focus was healthcare in India, both in the public and private sectors. One paper looked at reasons for the abysmally low utilization of institutional health-care facilities in rural areas in West Bengal. A second considered sales quota bonus plans on the institutional and retail sales of pharmaceutical products.
One limitation of DECISION during this period was that fact that it was published bi-annually. This limited both the number of papers that could be published and also its attractiveness to researchers. The entire production process was also handled in-house, and this was both time and labor intensive for the in-house production team. These limitations, fortunately, were remedied later. DECISION became not just a quarterly journal, but also began to publish special issues on emerging themes. The technical aspects of submission, review and production processes were also taken over by an international publisher and that certainly allowed editors to focus more on raising awareness of the journal and attracting some of the best researchers. These changes have contributed significantly to its current strength and positioning as one of the leading management journals in India. 

## Rohit Varman (Editor-in-Chief—April 2008 to March 2010)
Writing about the past is always fraught with challenges because it is usually interpreted through the lens of the present. Unfortunately, lens is never available to guide past actors and their actions. I am one such past actor in the life of DECISION. Yet, I attempt to offer a brief reflection on the two years of my journey with DECISION.
When I took over the editorial reins of DECISION in 2008, it was a well-established journal in Indian management circles. Past editors had done well to make it into a professional outlet. One of the key challenges for us was to take the outlet to the next level by improving the quality of published articles. Another challenge was to make DECISION into a more critical outlet that questioned and challenged the mainstream imagination of management theory. I was fortunate enough to have a supportive publication committee and, in particular, a collaborator in Biswatosh Saha, who helped to introduce a few changes.
We increased the frequency of the journal from two to three issues per year. We also introduced three new sections to increase the reach and effectiveness of the journal. We started publishing interviews in a new section on ‘Reflections’ to allow academic dialogues and critiques. For instance, an interview with Devinder Sharma offered a critique of the Indian State’s policy on agriculture. Sharma’s analyses of farmers' plight, food policy shortcomings and looting by agri-businesses were particularly insightful. In another new section called ‘Perspectives,’ we invited lead articles that were accompanied by commentaries. For example, our first lead piece in ‘Perspectives’ was on electronic globalization by Nikhilesh Dholakia. We also introduced a section on case studies. The first case study was by Abhijit Guha, who looked closely at how poor and marginalized groups in rural Bengal resisted the hegemony of local landlords, state and large private businesses. Apart from the new sections, we continued to publish research-based articles and book reviews. Our objective was to be inclusive and to showcase a wide spectrum of questions confronting management disciplines. We encouraged eclectic frameworks and diverse scholarly endeavors.
When I look back at this period of the outlet, I realize that I should have devoted more time and energy to the new sections to establish them further. More importantly, several issues required greater attention, which went uncovered in the journal. It was a period of a financial crisis that shook the foundations of the global economic system. However, little attention was paid to it in the research appearing in the outlet. Moreover, most research articles remained narrowly managerial that furthered corporate interests without attending to their socio-economic consequences. It was also a period in India, and outside, of unprecedented corporate plunder and transfer of wealth from the poor to the rich. These years continued to witness management school-corporate nexus that eroded the foundations of the idea of 'public' in public institutions. With educators morphing into neoliberal academic entrepreneurs and higher education (for both teachers and students) becoming a domain of profiteering, learning and knowledge became commodities. However, we did not witness any conversation on these issues in the journal. These have to be some of the key editorial failures and limitations of the journal in this period.
In conclusion, I use this opportunity to reflect not only on the past but also on the present. With the rise of fascist forces to power, India faces an unprecedented threat to democracy and freedom. As Arundhati Roy reminds us, "The blaze is at our door." These fascist forces are sponsored by corporations that are valorized and legitimized through academic discourses.
Instead of partnering and furthering corporate forces, I hope DECISION can be a voice of resistance in this urgent moment for our republic. I would like to see the conversations appearing on the pages of the journal that draw upon the critical traditions in social and management theory with emphases on human emancipation, equality and justice. Academic writings have the potential to create counter-discourses that may lead to conditions of resistance and challenge to the current corporate-sponsored fascist onslaught on our institutions. I hope the authors and editors of DECISION show the courage and foresight to speak truth to power. 

## Debashish Bhattacherjee (Editor-in-Chief—April 2010 to March 2012)
My first encounter with DECISION was a few months after I joined IIMC as an Assistant Professor, in early 1989. I was asked to write a couple of book reviews, the first one on the state of casual labor in India, I distinctly recall. Over the years, I followed keenly the arrival of the journal. Those days the journals were not well produced as they are today. I recall they compared rather poorly compared to IIMA’s Vikalpa. However, several senior faculty members regularly contributed research articles during this time.
During a five-year period in the early 1990s, the journal suffered from a lack of leadership at the helm and virtually no encouragement from the powers that be. Naturally, the journal languished, and if I recall correctly, hardly any volumes were issued. Consequent to this down period, several editors with a two-year term each, considerably revamped the journal and made it professionally competitive with both IIMA’s Vikalpa and IIMB’s Review. Our journal was pitched more as an ‘academic’ publication, rather than a ‘business school’ product. To this day, I feel DECISION retains this unique selling point.
Although I served as a member of the publications committee under at least two of the editors who revived the journal, I was never asked to be the editor of the journal until April 2010. I served as editor for two years till March 2012. The production of the journal had pretty much stabilized by then but the entire onus of bringing it out rested solely on the editor and one well-intentioned but often slow assistant. To put it mildly, it was an intense labor-intensive endeavor: getting the articles, research notes and book reviews together, following up with referee reports, and worse of all, copy editing and checking for spelling and grammar. There was a dire need I felt at this time for a professional editorial assistant. But no such luck came my way.
As editor during my tenure, I tried my best to solicit research articles from well-known scholars in India from the varied areas of management, especially from social scientists working on critical policy issues affecting India. Looking through the archives of all the research articles that appeared during the period 2010–12, I realize I partly, if not completely, failed. The majority of the articles published during this period were almost exclusively from the traditional ‘functional’ areas of management, largely, operations, finance and marketing. As I guess it should be in a management journal! Unfortunately, or fortunately, DECISION was largely viewed by potential contributors in India at this time as a ‘quantitative’ journal (whatever this means). I am so pleased that over the years, thanks to a stream of editors and editorial committee members, the journal’s appeal has considerably widened. Finally, one positive thing I got done was to get the journal indexed in the EBSCO database. This helped the journal’s reputation, and we soon started getting contributions from abroad, especially from developing countries.
I wish the journal all the best in its 50th volume!

The Decision ‘family,’ thus, has grown over time, and the journal has made remarkable contributions in building a network of academics interested in Indian data. Now, with four issues a year that reassuringly follow each other, it behoves us to wish Decision luck in taking Indian management education forward. 

## R. Rajesh Babu (Editor-in-Chief—April 2014 to March 2018)
Fifty years of
DECISION: resetting and rethinking
In print since 1974, DECISION is one of the oldest management journals published from India. The year 2023–24 would mark the golden jubilee of the journal and is a fitting moment to cherish its history, changing ethos and contribution to the broader field of management education and practice. Over the decades, DECISION has earned a reputation as a journal that imbibes both theory and practice of management and could establish itself as one of the premier management journals. More importantly, DECISION has been a forum for interdisciplinary dialogue across different fields of management and related disciplines.
During the period 2014–2018, our Editorial Board was able to strengthen the visibility and scale up the activities of DECISION. A prime reason was DECISION’s graduation to digital space with Springer as a publishing partner, thanks to the initiative of my predecessor Prof. Bhaskar Chakrabarti, EiC, in 2013. Our first challenge was stabilizing this critical transition of DECISION to the online mode and engaging closely with our publishing partner Springer to enhance global visibility and access. In the next few years, the submissions to DECISION increased significantly, with an acceptance rate of around 10 to 15 per cent. During this period, DECISION also succeeded in being listed in the Emerging Sources Citation Index (ESCI) in 2017 and becoming a Committee on Publication Ethics (COPE) member.
As a journal from the global South, DECISION and similar journals such as Vikalpa (published by IIM Ahmadabad) and IIM Management Review (published by IIM Bangalore) have a vital role in setting the academic tone and narrative. At a time when Indian management institutes want to be assessed on western yardsticks, and Indian academia is incentivized to prioritize and publish in western journals, DECISION and its peers are well poised to be an alternative to the dominant discourse and act as change agents. However, this would warrant introspection, resetting and rethinking.
DECISION must endeavor to carve for itself a unique space as the preferred venue for dialogue on Indian management thoughts and practice. To begin with, this would mean developing a culture of:(i)Active engagements with Indian knowledge traditions and thoughts. Indeed, this in no way means undermining the rich tradition of western management thoughts or compromising on academic rigor. This means prioritizing and aligning with what India and her philosophical traditions could offer the world,
(ii)Being a forum for engaging and solving national and local problems—carrying forward the founding visions of IIMs to “sensitize management education to the vision of inclusive, equitable and sustainable national development goals in order to contribute holistically to Society,” and more importantly,
(iii)Become a channel for accessing knowledge not just for management professionals and students but also for the general public. This would, however, mean a more liberal journal access and dissemination policy in English and Indian languages. Needless to say, access has direct consequences on research and relevance of the journal.

Moving away from the status quo is not an easy task and would require systematic changes to institutional policies and priorities which are currently designed to suit the accreditation agencies and international ranking criteria. Breaking this hegemony would require active participation and cooperation of the wider management community in India and the global South. One could hope that the much-desired resetting and rethinking begins with DECISION and at IIM Calcutta.
On the occasion of the golden jubilee of DECISION, I wish the Editorial Team and IIM Calcutta all the very best. 

## Manisha Chakraborty (Editor-in-Chief—April 2018 to September 2022)
One sunny Monday morning in early April of 2018, I was enthusiastically exploring the “Gol Gumbaz,” a 17th-century mausoleum located in Bijapur, a city in Karnataka, India. It houses the remains of Mohammad Adil Shah, seventh sultan of the Adil Shahi dynasty. I received a phone call from our then Director with a request to become the Editor-in-Chief of our Indian Institute of Management Calcutta’s flagship journal—DECISION. While I was engrossed in the beauty and grandeur of the ambitious structure of the Gol Gumbaz, I was equally amazed to get such an invitation and felt euphoric. Yet I was not sure what to say, and finally, he succeeded in convincing me. I do not know if it was a coincidence, but I felt I had been given an ambitious task started by my predecessors—of taking the journal forward, exactly like unfinished Gol Gumbaz.
Thanks to my colleagues who held the EiC position earlier and had already created an impactful intervention by joining hands with Springer in the growth path of our journal. DECISION which started in 1974 is amongst the earliest management journals in India. After assuming charge in the middle of 2018, particularly from Volume 45, Issue 3 in September 2018, I faced certain initial challenges in terms of lacuna in logistical support, good quality of incoming papers, skewed distribution of domain-specific contributions and indexing hurdles.
However, along with my editorial team, I tried to sail through the rough tides and challenges in running a sustainable good journal. Not only did we change the aims of the journal in 2019, but we released a special issue each year aiming to draw the attention of readers and through that more citations. Then came the sudden jolt faced by the whole world, we could hardly be an exception. Two volumes and 8 issues were released when we were all fighting the pandemic and many of us lost some near and dear ones. A new norm set in, work from home became more common. As an editor, I became more dependent on the virtual world and could not be more thankful to my predecessors who moved out of the offline system to an online one. The smooth production was possible only because of strong support from our editorial committee and reviewers’ timely help. At the same time, researchers were trying to fight this global pandemic with continuous relentless scientific contribution.
We introduced the concept of a review article on the current, widely debated issues in Management research, particularly for emerging economies, which we expected would be on interest to our audience. One such product was an article in June 2020 issue on “National and Sub National Purchasing Power Parity: A Review.” We actually converted the negative shock of pandemic to positive one by circulating the soft version of our issues by using our access key among academic institutions which otherwise would not have possible. In our endeavor to make it more visible and indexed in Scopus, we also reorganized our editorial board in 2021 to facilitate the efficient reviewing process by bringing enthusiastic, domain specific academicians to our editorial board.
The ever-changing scenario in academics, driven by Covid-19 pandemic, led us to think of a variety of new perspectives. I believe that an academic journal should reflect this by crossing its own boundaries, more so for a journal dealing with management research. We viewed this both as a challenge as well as an opportunity. As an Editor-in-chief, I initiated the idea of a conference-linked issue which I believe will bring immense opportunity to generate positive externality through interactions among readers, contributors and editors. The outcome was seen in an issue of 2022 (Vol 49) on “Dealing with online and blended education in modern challenging times.”
The story of progress does not end here. Rather it continues with the greater leaps that we need to take.
