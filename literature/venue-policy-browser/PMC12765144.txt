## Abstract

## Objectives
We aimed to identify the number of participants at three differently situated scientific events in Europe realistically able to switch from aviation to ground-based travel, modelling the potential impact of doing so in terms of travel-related greenhouse gas emissions (GHGe), time and costs.

## Study design
Modelling study.

## Methods
Travel for participants (N = 137) attending three academic European events in 2025, two centrally, and one peripherally located, was simulated, estimating the associated GHGe, time and cost. Alternative travel scenarios (air- and ground-based), in which participants realistically switched to ground-based travel, were modelled, highlighting differences in travel-related GHGe, time and costs.

## Results
For this cohort, a central European location gave around 50 % of participants the opportunity to take ground-based travel, reducing total travel GHGe by a maximum of 29 % (14,116 kg of CO2e). While ground-based journeys took longer, cost differences depended on the availability of international ground-based connections and low-cost airlines.

## Conclusions
The potential to minimise travel-related, event-driven GHGe is driven by the distance from the participants' departing cities to arrival venue, and the number of participants who live within 1000 kms and/or one day's travel time by ground-based means. The ability to realise these GHGe reductions will depend on the extent to which eligible event participants may choose to shift from aviation to ground-based alternatives, driven by their capabilities to navigate such journeys (that may be more complex), and their motivation, which might be enhanced through financial incentives where ground-based transportation is more expensive.

## What this study adds

•For our cohort, the geographically peripheral venue was associated with up to three times the travel-related, event-driven greenhouse gas emissions (GHGe) of the best performing central European model.
•A central European location gave around 50 % of participants the opportunity to take ground-based travel, reducing total travel GHGe by a maximum of 29 % (14,116 kg of CO2e).
•The costs of travelling by ground-based means can be both less or more than air travel, dependent on the availability of international ground-based connections and low-cost airlines.

2.

## Implications for policy and practice

•Organisers of scientific events should consider opportunistic central locations for encouraging ground-based travel, supporting participants' capabilities to navigate (at times complex) international ground-based journeys, and their motivation, incentivising low carbon footprint travel.
•Participants of international scientific events should be mindful of their travel-related carbon footprint and consider low-emission, ground-based transportation to these events.

3.

## Introduction
Climate change, driven by anthropogenic greenhouse gas emissions (GHGe), is an imminent threat to planetary health. Aviation is a growing major contributor to global GHGe that must be curtailed to meet climate change targets [ ]. Academics and researchers are hypermobile, travelling by air more than the average person [ ]; to network, gain new knowledge, disseminate research findings, and spread good practice, traditionally through conferences and scientific events. In fact, air-based travel contributes most of the GHGe associated with international scientific conferences/meetings [ ]. By promoting low-emission ground-based travel, significant reductions in travel-related GHGe are possible [ ]. However, for individual participants to shift from aviation to less polluting ground-based transportation options, event organisers need to facilitate the opportunity, capability and motivation to do so [ , ]. While providing information about the climate change impact of academic aviation increases willingness to shift to ground-based modes [ ], this may not always be seen as a convincing alternative due to the additional cost, distance and travel time required.
As an exemplar academic exchange event, the annual European Academy of Nursing Science (EANS) Summer School, enables nurses undertaking doctoral studies in European Universities to learn advanced research methods at a selected European University. Events move venue annually. While previous studies have estimated travel-related GHGe from European healthcare conferences/meetings, recommending central locations to reduce air-travel related GHGe [ , ], they have not addressed the opportunity for, and impact of, participants making alternative travel choices. Our aim was to identify the number of participants at three differently situated events realistically able to switch from aviation to ground-based travel, modelling the potential impact of doing so in terms of travel-related GHGe, time and costs.

4.

## Methods
Participants’ travel to the closest airport of three actual past European Summer School events, two located centrally (Ghent and Turin) and one peripherally (Lisbon), were simulated using the 2025 Summer School dates (30th June-11th July), calculating airport-to-airport travel-related data; GHGe, cost and time. Participants were the faculty (n = 32) and students (n = 105) from European Universities who had previously attended the Lisbon Summer School in 2019.
4.1.

## Procedures
Our baseline assumption was that participants would travel across borders by aeroplane. Skyscanner® was used to identify direct or one-connection round-trip flights from the participants’ closest departing international airport to the closest airport next to the Summer School venues: Lisbon airport; Brussels or Brussels South Charleroi airport (choice based on availability and cost); and Turin or Milan Malpensa airport (choice based on availability). We did not simulate air travel for participants whose closest departing airport was within 100 kms from the Summer School arrival airport (e.g. Maastricht to Brussels), as realistically such individuals would only travel by ground. For comparative purposes, the selected departing airport was the same for each simulated Summer School ( ). The cheapest airline that ideally provided direct flights, opting for up to a two-day extension if direct flights were not available on the day before the Summer School/teaching days or on the last day (afternoon), was selected for each journey. We also factored in convenience [ ], considering only journeys which took 13 h (a day) or less and were to land by 1:00 a.m. (the following day). For connecting flights, no change of airport or self-transfer connections were considered. Costs, in economy, which included hold luggage, were obtained directly from the selected airline.
Alternative ground-based travel journeys (airport-to-airport) and costs were noted on the same day in which the same air routes were calculated. While previous studies have simulated alternative ground-based travel for distances of up to 1400–1600 kms (air distance) [ , ], we factored in convenience as well, since longer routes may demand the need for an overnight stay or the use of night trains [ ]. Ground-based alternatives (rail, mostly) were considered for airport pairs with straight-line distances of up to 1000 kms, which should take ≤13 h of travel [ ]. While longer (in terms of duration) ground-based travel were considered, routes where night travel/an overnight stay was required were excluded. Rome2Rio® was used to help identify ground-based public transport routes, selecting the fastest travel from the respective providers as much as possible, followed by cost. Our assumption for opting to a two-day extension if routes could not be calculated on the day before the Summer School/teaching days, or on the last day (afternoon), was also applied when simulating ground-based travel. Costs, in economy, were also obtained directly from the respective providers.
To calculate GHGe, first, the participants' round-trip journey distances were calculated; using the Great Circle Map® for air travel, which provides the shortest possible route between airports using a geodesic path [ ], and using Google Road maps® for land-based journey distances, serving as a proxy for rail distances since actual rail distances are not readily available. A sailing distance calculator was also used to calculate sea travel distance. As in our previous study [ ], GHGe in kg CO2e (equivalent) estimates were calculated using the UK Government GHG Conversion Factors 2024 for UK company reporting [ ]. Per person per km conversion factors for short-haul (within Europe) to/from UK economy travel (which is the higher factor for European economy travel; 0.18287), coach (as this was generally used for the longest-distance bus travel; 0.02717) and/or national rail (highest factor for all types of rail-based travel; 0.03546), and by ferry (foot passenger; 0.01871) were multiplied by the number of kms for each individual's journey, as appropriate.

4.2.

## Data analysis
Seven models were derived for each event. The baseline model was that all attendees (living beyond 100 kms of the event airport) would travel by plane. The categories provided by Barret (2019) [ ]: ≤300 kms, ≤500 kms, ≤700 kms, and ≤1000 kms were used for four models, replacing aviation with ground-based methods for eligible participants within these distances from the venues. An additional two models were derived by combining the ≤700 kms, and ≤1000 kms ground-based models with a ‘convenience’ [ ] factor of ≤13 h required for one-way ground-based travel. For each model, eligible participants' baseline emissions, time and costs were replaced with ground-based data. These participants' ground-based travel values were compared to their initial (air-based) ones, providing average differences. Furthermore, the air and ground-travel associated GHGe for each model were summed, noting the percentage decrease in total emissions. Median values and interquartile ranges are provided as data was not normally distributed.

5.

## Results
Out of the total number of participants (N = 137), 129, 117, and 136 participants, would have travelled more than 100 kms to the simulated 2025 Lisbon, Ghent and Turin events, respectively. Total CO2e emissions were 102,682 kg, 48,642 kg, and 58,705 kg, respectively for our baseline (air-based travel) model ( ).
As distances from home to the event increased (in our models), the number of participants able to take a ground-based option within one day increased from 2 to 11 (1.6 %–8.5 %) for Lisbon; 11 to 57 (9.4 %–48.7 %) for Ghent; and 9 to 68 (6.6 %–50.0 %) for Turin ( ). Simulating ground-based travel for these participants resulted in a potential percentage reduction in total GHGe of 0.2–2.5 (Lisbon), 3.0–29.0, (Ghent) and 4.4–23.1 (Turin). While ground-based journeys took longer due to the distance involved, their costs, when compared to air travel, varied, though they were generally cheaper for the Ghent event.

It is worth noting that, unlike for Ghent, where four out of 57 participants (7.0 %) took low-cost flights, several participants (37 out of 68, 54.4 %) flew with low-cost airlines (as considered in the Greenpeace report) [ ] to the Turin event, making ground-based travel a less cost-effective alternative.

6.

## Discussion
For a standardised cohort of doctoral students and faculty attending differently situated academic events in Europe, the potential to minimise travel-related, event-driven GHGe is driven by two core factors: distance from participants' departing cities to arrival venue; and number of participants who have the opportunity to take advantage of ground-based alternatives because their home cities are located within 1000 kms and/or one day's travel time by ground-based means. For our cohort, because Lisbon, the geographically peripheral venue, only provided opportunities to take ground-based options for <10 % of participants, and average distances for other participants were much longer, travel GHGe were almost three-fold that of Ghent, the best performing central European model. Costs of taking alternatives to aviation were generally cheaper when the venue was supplied by average-priced ground-based travel options. However, the effect was less or even reversed for longer and more expensive ground-based journeys whose aviation alternatives were served by low-cost airlines.
One caveat of this study is that our simulation exercise relies on several assumptions that may not fully reflect real-world travel behaviour. For example, we assumed that participants would travel across borders by aeroplane in view that most Europeans travel by air rather than by train/coach for long distance journeys (300 km and more), and those still studying are more likely to travel by plane than other modes of transport, including private transport [ ]. Given that cost (e.g., ticket price) is the leading factor for Europeans when planning a regional or long distance journey [ ], we also assumed the participants would choose the cheapest airline when planning their travel. When selecting flights, we also factored in convenience, which is the driving factor for opting to travel by plane for University-based researchers [ ], opting for direct flights when possible, considering only journeys which took 13 h (a day) or less and were to land by 1:00 a.m. (the following day). We also factored in convenience for simulating ground-based travel, opting for alternative travel for airport pairs with straight-line distances of up to 1000 kms, selecting the fastest travel from the respective providers as much as possible, excluding any routes where night travel/an overnight stay was required. Calculating CO2e travel emissions is complex. In the absence of direct participant survey data, we described the procedure undertaken using the UK Government GHG Conversion Factors for UK company reporting (which have been used in previous studies [ , ]) to estimate carbon emissions. We also acknowledge that the possibility of travelling by ground, which may be more challenging because of the connections involved, may not necessarily translate to an actual change in behaviour; hence our derivation of seven models for alternative travel scenarios (air- and ground-based) and the reference to the COM-B (Capability-Opportunity-Motivation-Behaviour) model [ ] (see below).
Another limitation is that our modelling study is based on a single cohort from one past EANS Summer School. This selected cohort was typical of the EANS Summer School participants, as following the Lisbon Summer School, held in 2019, just before the COVID-19 pandemic, there were changes in the composition of the year groups of the subsequent Summer Schools (2021 onwards) as some students chose to delay their Summer School progression. While we acknowledge that the geographical distribution of our participants may not reflect that of other European academic events (especially those based in Eastern Europe – see for the list of departing airports), it is worth noting that having had several participants living closer to the Ghent event, showed that location plays a very important role for providing the opportunity to participants to consider possible alternative ground-based travel, stressing the need for event organisers to consider central venues based on their participants’ geographical characteristics.
As per the COM-B (Capability-Opportunity-Motivation-Behaviour) model [ ], the ability to realise these GHGe reductions will depend on the extent to which eligible event participants may choose to shift from aviation to ground-based alternatives, driven by participants’ capabilities to navigate international rail journeys (that may be more complex), and their motivation, which might be enhanced through financial incentives where ground-based methods are more expensive than aviation.

## Ethical statement
Ethical approval was not required for this study. The dataset supplied by the EANS board was fully anonymised and stripped of all participants’ personal and sensitive identifiers, including names, home addresses, and dates of birth. Consequently, we did not obtain or require informed consent from participants.
