## Background
Immigration involves adapting to a new country and culture, which can affect well-being. Japan’s Indian immigrant population has grown rapidly, highlighting potential gaps in healthcare access. This study examined how acculturation-related factors such as health literacy and social support are associated with healthcare utilization and satisfaction among Indian immigrants in Japan.

## Methods
This cross-sectional study surveyed Indian immigrants in Japan between October and December 2023. Participants were recruited consecutively at Indian community events across multiple prefectures. Healthcare utilization and satisfaction were measured as primary outcomes using a validated questionnaire. Independent variables included social support (Oslo Scale) and health literacy (BRIEF Scale). Multivariate logistic regression was used to analyze associations.

## Results
Of 1,335 individuals approached, 662 responded (50%), and 501 were analyzed (38%; 280 males, 221 females; mean age [SD] 38.9 [7.5] and 36.0 [6.5], respectively). A total of 191 (38%) participants visited outpatient clinics several times a year, 97 (19%) visited dental clinics several times a year, and 415 (83%) received annual health checkups. Long-term immigrants (≥ 11 years) were more likely to utilize maternal services than recent (1–3 years) or mid-term immigrants (4–10 years). Social support showed no significant association with healthcare utilization. Limited health literacy, however, was paradoxically associated with higher satisfaction. Higher satisfaction was also associated with employment/contract status, health insurance coverage, and longer residency in Japan.

## Conclusion
Employment status, gender, and residency duration were associated with healthcare patterns among Indian immigrants in Japan. While overall satisfaction was high (75–80%), higher health literacy paradoxically correlated with greater dissatisfaction. Future research should explore interventions to improve healthcare accessibility for recent immigrants.

## Key findings

Length of residency was associated with healthcare utilization, which may reflect gradual integration into the Japanese healthcare system.
Employment status was linked to differences in healthcare engagement, with unemployed and part-time workers being less likely to receive annual health check-ups and dental services. Employed workers showed lower satisfaction levels despite higher utilization, suggesting employment-based benefits facilitate access but may also elevate expectations.
A health literacy paradox emerged, with individuals having limited health literacy showing significantly higher satisfaction levels, suggesting that better understanding of healthcare systems leads to more critical evaluation and higher expectations.

## Key message
Recent immigrants and irregularly employed residents in Japan show concerning gaps in healthcare utilization. Paradoxically, lower health literacy correlates with higher satisfaction levels, highlighting urgent needs for targeted support and expectation management in immigrant healthcare services.

## Introduction
An immigrant is defined as a person who moves into a country other than that of their nationality or usual residence, with the intention of making it their new country of usual residence [ ]. Immigration is a global phenomenon that exposes people to different cultures, requiring acculturation to the host country’s norms and customs [ , ].
Acculturation refers to the process of cultural change that occurs when individuals from different cultural backgrounds come into continuous contact, involving adaptation to new cultural norms [ ]. Acculturation strategies, influenced by personal choice, social support, and acceptance by the host society, can affect immigrants’ cultural identity, well-being, and health-seeking behaviors [ , ]. These strategies include assimilation, integration, separation, and marginalization, shaped by individual factors like age, gender, and length of stay, as well as contextual factors such as social support [ – ].
The Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) are important for addressing migrant health concerns in the global development agenda. Migrant health connects with SDGs on health, inequality, economic growth, and inclusive communities. These goals require addressing immigrants’ health needs and ensuring equitable healthcare access regardless of migration status. SDG 3 focuses on promoting health and well-being for all, including immigrants [ ]. Immigrants face unique health challenges due to cultural and environmental adjustments and increasing social and economic stress. The World report on the health of refugees and migrants 2020 reported 281 million international immigrants worldwide [ ].
Indians living in the most populous country in the world today are no exception. Japan has become a new hub for Indian immigrants, with many young people seeking improved employment opportunities and quality of life. Japan’s immigration policies have evolved since 1945 [ ]. The restrictive 1952 Immigration Control Act exacerbated labor shortages during the economic boom when demand for affordable labor increased. A turning point occurred with the 1982 convention ratification, which reduced discrimination by granting foreigners access to national pensions and healthcare services. Subsequently, the 1990 revised Immigration Control Act facilitated substantial immigration, marking a pivotal shift in Japan’s approach to foreign residents [ ].
Japan has seen a significant increase in its Indian population, growing by 18% annually since 1990, when there were 3,000 Indian immigrants [ ]. As of June 2024, Japan hosts 51,345 Indians, marking the second-largest Indian community in South Asia [ , ]. This rise underscores the need for a more migrant-friendly healthcare system in Japan. For instance, a 2018 Ministry of Health, Labor, and Welfare report noted that 45.3% of immigrants use outpatient departments, and 15.8% use hospitals [ ]. However, a previous study using the activity records of a local non-governmental organization showed inadequate access to formal healthcare for foreign residents [ ]. An interview study of Indian pregnant women revealed communication issues and a lack of interaction with doctors [ ].
Social support is essential in reducing acculturation stress and improving healthcare access [ , ]. Research on Nepalese immigrants in Tokyo found that family and friends help reduce psychological distress and improve life satisfaction [ ]. Studies on various immigrant groups in Japan have identified common barriers to healthcare, including language proficiency, inability to pay insurance premiums, acculturation challenges, and a shortage of female healthcare providers for Muslim women [ – ]. These challenges are compounded by varying levels of health literacy, which is essential for navigating health information and making informed health decisions [ , ]. Patient satisfaction is also a key to care quality for immigrants, who may be at risk for health disparities and social isolation [ ].
In addition to findings on the health risks of migrant populations, a nationality-specific study on Japanese healthcare utilization is needed. Although the rising Indian diaspora in Japan requires a more immigrant-friendly and comprehensive healthcare system for workers and their families, related issues have been understudied. This study primarily aimed to examine various factors related to acculturation (acculturation-related factors), such as health literacy and social support, that are associated with healthcare utilization among Indian immigrants in Japan. The study also aimed to identify factors associated with patient satisfaction with healthcare utilization in this population.

## Methods

## Study design
This was a cross-sectional study among Indian immigrants living in Japan in the following prefectures: Tokyo, Kanagawa, Chiba, Saitama, Ibaraki, and Kyoto. As of 2023, Tokyo had the largest Indian population at 17,876, followed by Kanagawa with 8,161 residents, Chiba (2,583), Saitama (2,239), Ibaraki (2,085), and Osaka (1,773) residents [ ]. The survey was conducted between October and December 2023.

## Setting
The study recruited Indian immigrants at festivals and cultural and sporting events across several prefectures in Japan (Supplementary Table 1). The researcher SM contacted Indian community leaders who organized these activities in different places. The researcher contacted Indians at these events through these community leaders. At each event, community leaders facilitated introductions to potential participants.

## Participants
The Indian immigrants living in Japan, including long-term resident card holders, permanent residents, Japanese nationals, and other working visa holders (excluding tourist visa holders), aged between 18 and 60 years, and able to read English, were recruited.

## Recruitment pathway
Participants were systematically approached at various Indian community events following a structured consecutive sampling method [ ]. Eligibility was assessed solely by the researcher (SM), with community leaders facilitating venue access only. Participants received written and verbal study information with bilingual support as needed and provided electronic informed consent via SurveyMonkey. At each event, all eligible attendees were sequentially invited to participate until the target sample size was achieved. Interested individuals provided electronic consent by checking a box on SurveyMonkey, a self-administered online questionnaire platform, where they completed the survey. This consecutive sampling method was employed at each event location, inviting all eligible attendees in order of encounter, thereby reducing the biases often associated with convenience sampling while maintaining systematic recruitment procedures across multiple venues and timeframes. The collected data was fully anonymized, and data storage followed strict privacy protocols using password-protected servers, with access restricted to authorized research personnel only.

## Study variables
The study measured utilization of healthcare services and patient satisfaction with healthcare services as main outcomes. We examined 5 items of healthcare services utilization: outpatient department (OPD) and dental services were assessed using an 8-point frequency scale; annual health checkup and cancer screening services were assessed using yes/no responses; and maternal services utilization was assessed with three options: yes, no, or not applicable. The satisfaction level with medical care was measured by a single satisfaction question: “How satisfied are you with medical care in Japan? using a 6-point Likert scale (Table  ). The study used acculturation-related factors like age, gender, language proficiency in Japanese and English, employment status, health insurance, comorbidity, social support level, health literacy level, and other sociodemographic characteristics such as annual income and length of residency in Japan as covariates (Table  ). The study examined how socio-demographics, social support, and health literacy levels influence healthcare services utilization and satisfaction levels related to acculturation.

 

 

(1) The Oslo Social Support Scale (OSS) was used to measure perceived social support. It assessed the availability of social support and the perceived level of concern and interest from others. OSS demonstrated good reliability and validity in cross-cultural contexts [ ]. (Supplementary Table ).
(2) The BRIEF Health Literacy Screening Tool was also used to evaluate an individual’s health literacy level. It consists of four items, scoring 4 to 20 in total. The BRIEF Health Literacy Tool showed acceptable reliability and construct validity. The scale has been validated for screening health literacy levels in clinical settings [ – ]. While the BRIEF does not explicitly assess Japanese language proficiency, it evaluates crucial aspects of health literacy, including reading, writing, and comprehension abilities specific to health-related documents available in Japanese healthcare settings. This distinction is important in our study context, as Indian immigrants may have varying levels of Japanese language proficiency despite potentially high literacy in English or their native languages. (Supplementary Table 3).

## Sample size
Considering a 95% confidence interval, a 5% margin of error, and a reference population of 48,835 Indians in Japan [ ], the sample size of 382 was calculated using the z-test. We recruited 501 participants to provide complete data suitable for analysis.

## Statistical methods
A descriptive analysis was conducted with mean and standard deviation (SD) for continuous variables and frequencies with percentages for categorical variables. Utilization variables were dichotomized into “none” versus (other categories) " any utilization.” Chi-square tests were used to measure the satisfaction level, divided into 3 categories (satisfied, somewhat satisfied, and dissatisfied), and their association with various socio-demographics (Table  ). Ordinal logistic regression was employed as the primary analytical approach for satisfaction analysis using a 5-category scale (Table  ). The variables were selected based on their theoretical relevance to healthcare access patterns among immigrant populations. Odds ratios (OR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI) were calculated to quantify associations between explanatory variables and outcomes. Sensitivity analyses using binary logistic regression for healthcare service utilization (frequent use vs. none/once yearly) confirmed the robustness of findings across analytical frameworks (Supplementary Table 5). To assess model fit and avoid overfitting, we calculated Variance Inflation Factor (VIF) for all independent variables (acceptable threshold: VIF < 10) and evaluated events-per-variable (EPV) ratios. The data was analyzed utilizing SPSS software version 23 (IBM, New York City).

 

 

## Results
We approached a total of 1,335 potentially eligible individuals at various Indian events and collected 662 responses (Supplementary Table 1). Supplementary file 1, Figure : Flow chart of the Participants shows the number of individuals approached, excluded, and included in the final analysis (n = 501) (response rate 50%, analyzable rate 38%). The mean age of participants was 38.3 years; 56% (n = 280) were male. Regarding employment, 72% (n = 359) held full-time/contract jobs, 19% were unemployed, and 9% had part-time jobs. Nearly all (96%, n = 479) had health insurance. In case of serious illness, 27% (n = 133) preferred treatment in India, 26% (n = 128) in Japan, while 48% (n = 240) reported that either country was acceptable. Most participants (88%, n = 440) reported no comorbidities. Use of maternal services in Japan was reported by 29% (n = 144; 51% male and 49% female). Additional sample characteristics are presented in Table  .

## Healthcare service utilization
Table  represents the number of participants using various healthcare services in Japan. Regarding the OPD visits, 26% visited OPD once a year, while 38% of participants visited OPD several times a year; 33% visited dental clinics once a year, and 19% visited several times a year; 83% of participants took annual health checkups; 25% underwent annual cancer screening; 22% used maternal services. Table  also shows satisfaction levels by gender.
Table presents the results of healthcare service utilization associated with acculturation-related factors. Females were more likely to receive cancer screenings (OR = 3.39, CI: 1.91–6.03) compared to males. Participants with comorbidities were less likely to utilize dental services (OR = 0.54, CI: 0.31–0.96) compared to those without comorbidities. Full-time employed and contract workers demonstrated higher engagement with annual health check-ups (OR = 3.05, CI: 1.29–7.22) compared to part-time workers. Mid-term immigrants were less likely to utilize maternal services (OR = 0.44, CI: 0.24–0.81) compared to long-term residents. Recent immigrants demonstrated markedly lower utilization across multiple services, being less likely to obtain annual health check-ups (OR = 0.41, CI: 0.17–0.98), cancer screenings (OR = 0.29, CI: 0.14–0.63), and maternal services (OR = 0.03, CI: 0.007-0.10) compared to long-term residents. Older immigrants were more likely to receive cancer screenings (OR = 1.05, CI: 1.01–1.09), while older immigrants were less likely to use maternal services (OR = 0.93, CI: 0.89–0.98). No significant associations were found between healthcare service utilization and Japanese language proficiency, English language proficiency, health insurance status, health literacy level, or social support level across any of the examined healthcare services. Forest plots illustrating the adjusted odds ratios for all healthcare service utilization outcomes are provided in Supplementary file 1.

 

Sensitivity analyses using alternative binary categorizations [none + once a year vs. frequent use (all other options)] yielded substantially similar findings, confirming the robustness of our results across different analytical frameworks (Supplementary Table 5).

## Healthcare satisfaction levels
Satisfaction was measured on a 6-point Likert scale, recoded into three categories for descriptive analysis. Associations with acculturation-related factors and socio-demographics are shown in Supplementary Tables 4 and Table . Males reported higher satisfaction (58% satisfied vs. 43% for females, p = 0.002). Immigrants with intermediate/advanced Japanese were more satisfied (57% vs. 46%, p = 0.012) compared to those with none/basic Japanese. Participants with health insurance were more satisfied (56% vs. 29%, p < 0.001). Employment status was also associated: full-time/contract workers reported higher satisfaction than unemployed/part-time workers (55% vs. 43%, p = 0.045). Higher health literacy was inversely related to satisfaction (p < 0.001): limited health literacy—57% satisfied; marginal—40% satisfied; adequate—28% satisfied (Table ).

## Predictors of healthcare satisfaction
Ordinal logistic regression was employed after confirming proportional odds assumption compliance (parallel lines test: p = 0.076). Employed/contract workers demonstrated lower odds of higher satisfaction compared to part-time workers (OR = 0.49, 95% CI: 0.25–0.93). The participants having limited health literacy showed 2.55 times higher odds of reporting higher satisfaction levels (OR = 2.55, 95% CI: 1.21–5.39), and those with marginal health literacy demonstrated 2.18 times higher odds (OR = 2.18, 95% CI: 1.00-4.75) compared to those with adequate health literacy. Age, gender, language proficiency, comorbidity status, health insurance coverage, social support levels, length of residency, and annual income showed no associations with satisfaction levels in this ordinal model (Table ). Chi-square tests examined bivariate associations before multivariate analysis. VIF values ranged from 1.02 to 2.87 (all < 10), and EPV ratios exceeded 10 for all models, indicating no evidence of overfitting. While crude analysis showed higher satisfaction among employed workers, ordinal regression controlling for confounders revealed the opposite, highlighting the role of covariates in employment–satisfaction associations.

## Discussion
The present study of 501 Indian immigrants revealed lower utilization of OPD, and dental services compared to annual health check-ups and cancer screening. Our participants had higher education (97% college or above) and employment (72% full-time) compared to typical immigrant populations. Unlike Western studies reporting immigrant healthcare underutilization [ , ], participants showed high health check-up rates (83%) likely due to Japan’s mandatory workplace screening. However, we found a paradoxical inverse relationship between health literacy and satisfaction, which contrasts with previous findings [ ]. Overall, healthcare satisfaction was high (75–80%), lower among recent and mid-term immigrants compared to long-term immigrants. Immigrants with intermediate/advanced Japanese and limited health literacy reported higher satisfaction. Social support and health literacy were not significant facilitators of healthcare access.

## Healthcare service utilization patterns
This study examined utilization from the demand side, measuring participants’ behaviors rather than supply-side availability. No associations were found between Japanese proficiency, health insurance, and healthcare use, likely due to Japan’s universal coverage and mandatory check-ups regardless of language ability. Unlike in Canada and the US, where recent immigrants underutilize healthcare, our study found no significant association between residency length and OPD use, reflecting differences in system accessibility [ , ]. These findings suggest Japan’s system minimizes access barriers, and policy focus should shift toward expectation management.
Long-term immigrants were more likely than recent or mid-term residents to use maternal services, suggesting that longer residency increases trust and familiarity with local care. Lower dental and OPD use may reflect the “healthy immigrant effect,” where immigrants often report better health than natives. Most visits were likely for acute rather than chronic conditions, consistent with 88% of participants reporting no comorbidities. Similar findings have been observed in Canada, where recent immigrants had better health and lower utilization than native-born Canadians [ ].
Immigrant women in our study preferred care in their home country during serious illness, likely due to cultural familiarity and perceived higher quality. This aligns with reviews showing immigrants often view home-country healthcare as more culturally appropriate [ , ]. Preference for maternal services in India reflects cultural values emphasizing family support and familiarity with private care systems.
Full-time employed participants more frequently used health check-ups than unemployed/part-time workers, consistent with Japan’s system mandating annual exams for employees [ ]. Regarding cancer screenings, women used services more than men, reflecting proactive programs for women (e.g., free cervical screening from age 20 and breast screening from age 40) [ ]. Since many respondents were under 40, overall cancer screening rates were low, consistent with other studies [ , ]. Our highly educated, largely employed sample may also explain higher utilization compared with other immigrant groups.

## Acculturation-related factors and healthcare experiences
Existing literature has highlighted the significance of health literacy and social support in shaping healthcare outcomes. Sentell et al. [ ] observed that low health literacy and inadequate English proficiency worsen health outcomes. Sarkar et al. [ ] found that health literacy predicts healthcare outcomes for Hispanics regardless of English proficiency. Higher health literacy was linked to better healthcare outcomes, such as better service access and medical information comprehension. Numerous studies have linked strong social support to decreasing perceived barriers and increased healthcare access [ ]. A systematic review [ ] revealed that social support may improve oral health in immigrant and ethnic minority populations. However, our study did not find a significant association between social support and dental care.
Our study showed no association between social support levels and utilization of maternal services in Japan, which is different from Wadhwa’s study [ ]. Also, there was no significant association between social support and outpatient and dental clinic use. The Japanese healthcare system, which emphasizes preventive treatment and frequent check-ups, provides universal coverage to all residents and immigrants and maintains relatively low healthcare expenditures compared to other developed countries, which may explain our findings [ , ].

## Satisfaction levels and the health literacy paradox
Although healthcare satisfaction was lower among recent and mid-term immigrants compared to long-term immigrants in the chi-square analysis, this association was no longer significant after adjusting for covariates such as employment status, health insurance coverage, health literacy, and social support in the ordinal logistic regression model. According to Shakya et al. [ ], language skills, difficulty paying insurance costs, and unavailability of female healthcare practitioners are significant impediments for Nepalese immigrants. Studies demonstrate that Japanese-literate immigrant women feel isolated and avoided by healthcare personnel, resulting in lower maternity care satisfaction [ ], which contrasts with our study findings, as it showed no Japanese language barrier findings associated with the use of healthcare services.
This study identified a health literacy paradox: participants with limited health literacy reported higher satisfaction than those with adequate literacy. While prior studies often show positive links between health literacy and satisfaction [ ], our results suggest that greater knowledge may raise expectations and increase sensitivity to shortcomings in care, thus lowering perceived satisfaction. Recent and mid-term immigrants often report lower satisfaction with healthcare services compared to long-term immigrants due to various interconnected factors. Language barriers and cultural differences can lead to communication challenges and misunderstandings, impacting the quality of care received. Limited English proficiency may result in difficulties expressing health concerns accurately and understanding medical instructions [ ] which is relatable to our study findings. Access issues, including lower insurance coverage and underutilization of services, further contribute to dissatisfaction [ ]. Recent immigrants may also perceive a lack of cultural sensitivity from healthcare providers and experience longer waiting times [ ]. Socioeconomic constraints and limited awareness of available resources can exacerbate these challenges. As immigrants spend more time in the host country, their satisfaction levels tend to improve, suggesting that adaptation and familiarity with the healthcare system play crucial roles in overall satisfaction [ ].
While language proficiency was associated with satisfaction levels, it did not significantly influence healthcare utilization in this study. These comprehensive findings underscore the complexity of immigrant healthcare experiences and highlight the critical need for targeted healthcare interventions and support systems, especially for immigrants who are new to Japan, have limited Japanese language skills, or demonstrate low health literacy.

## Implications for policy
The present results showed limited significant associations between immigration characteristics and healthcare access patterns. Our findings revealed that immigration-related factors such as language proficiency did not significantly influence healthcare service utilization in Japan, while length of residency effects varied by analytical approach and outcome measure. Healthcare utilization among immigrants varies significantly based on length of residency, employment, language proficiency, and gender. Satisfaction levels are associated with health literacy, insurance, employment status, and length of residency. These associations suggest potential benefits of targeted, culturally sensitive healthcare interventions for immigrants, especially those new to Japan, with limited language skills or low health literacy. Furthermore, the health literacy and social support scales used were primarily validated in Western or cross-cultural contexts, which may limit their sensitivity to the specific cultural and healthcare environment of Japan. As one of the high-income countries that accepts immigrants, Japan is expected to consider the following measures. (1) Structured immigrant health orientation programs similar to Canada’s ACHIEVE model, which integrates immigrant community health workers into preventive health services and significantly improved healthcare navigation confidence among new immigrants [ ]; (2) Community navigator programs modeled after successful US initiatives like the Promotoras program, which uses peer educators from within Latino communities to promote health literacy and engagement and utilizes culturally competent outreach workers to bridge healthcare access gaps [ ]; (3) Programs that help align immigrant expectations with the realities of Japan’s healthcare system such as orientation sessions or counseling may help reduce the satisfaction gap observed among highly health-literate immigrants, similar to initiatives implemented in Canadian healthcare systems [ ].

## Limitations
This study has certain limitations. First, sampling at Indian community events in Tokyo likely overrepresented socially active and well-educated individuals, limiting generalizability. This may have led to higher observed rates of healthcare utilization and satisfaction than in the broader Indian immigrant population. To reduce this bias, we used consecutive recruitment at multiple events and acknowledged the demographic skew when interpreting results. Second, the use of English-only surveys may have excluded participants with limited English proficiency, potentially inflating functional health literacy levels. However, given the high prevalence of English fluency among Indian professionals in Japan, the impact was likely limited. Moreover, a bilingual researcher (SM) was present at all events and offered assistance equally to every participant during survey administration to improve clarity and accuracy. Third, the study did not have criteria for distinguishing first- or second-generation Indian immigrants living in Japan. As Indian migration to Japan is relatively recent, therefore, second-generation Indians are presumed to be limited to younger individuals and few in number. In our sample, only 23 participants (< 5% of total sample) were aged 18–25, representing the age group most likely to include second-generation individuals. Even if all of them were second-generation this small proportion would have minimal influence on our results. Fourth, the BRIEF health literacy scale assessed only functional health literacy and may not capture interactive or cultural literacy, particularly relevant in navigating Japanese healthcare. Its U.S.-based validation may also limit applicability in the Japanese context. This may partly explain the weak associations observed between health literacy and outcomes. Additionally, categorizing ‘neither satisfied nor dissatisfied’ responses as dissatisfaction may have inflated dissatisfaction rates and influenced associations. Fifth, the absence of a “when needed” option for healthcare use restricted our ability to differentiate between non-use due to lack of need versus limited access. The abbreviation “OPD” was not fully expanded, which could have reduced clarity, although the impact was likely minimal due to the high educational level of participants. Sixth, lack of gender-specific data on cancer screening limited insight into preventive care behaviors, and small sample sizes in some categories prevented multi-category modeling. Next, the absence of comparative data with the Japanese population limits our ability to determine whether the observed utilization rates among Indian immigrants are relatively higher or lower than those of native residents. Finally, the cross-sectional design precludes causal interpretation.

## Conclusion
This study found that healthcare utilization and satisfaction among Indian immigrants in Japan are associated with demographic factors like employment status, length of residency, and gender. Language proficiency showed no associations with healthcare utilization. This study contributes to understanding healthcare disparities among Japan’s growing immigrant population and to evidence-informed policies to improve healthcare access, use, and satisfaction. Future studies should utilize interview-based research methods to obtain detailed insights into immigrant healthcare experiences and examine the mechanisms driving satisfaction patterns observed in quantitative analyses.
