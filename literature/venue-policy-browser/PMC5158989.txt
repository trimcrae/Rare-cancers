TRIENNIAL REPORT ON THE WORKING OF 
THE RANCHI INDIAN MENTAL HOSPITAL, 
IvANKE, IN BIHAR AND ORISSA, FOR THE 
YEARS 1930-32 
During the triennium under review this hospital 
continued to receive patients as in the previous years 
from the provinces of Bengal and Bihar and Orissa. 
The accommodation of the hospital remained the 
same as in the previous years, viz, 1,014 for males and 
272 for females, total 1,286. 
Ever since the inception of this modern mental hos- 
pital the question of treatment of patients has always 
engaged our serious attention and we introduce many 
of the newest western methods of treatment with 
due regard to eastern conditions. On my return from 
leave in 1930, I introduced many forms of new treat- 
ment which I had picked up during my second study 
tour in Europe and America. 
Driigs.?Sulphur injections in mental disorders.?An 
experimental study of 100 cases with sulphur injections 
was fully described in my last annual report (1931). 
Since then we have experimented with another 40 cases; 
17 cases recovered and were discharged as cured; 57 cases 
improved, out of which 25 have since relapsed and 32 
have still maintained the improvement; 66 cases did 
not show any improvement. This shows that the result? 
of our experiment were quite encouraging and the 
sulphur injections in my opinion are well worth a trial 
in all fresh admissions. It gives better results in early 
cases of psychosis as well as in the psychoneurosis 
while its effect is not discouraging even in chronic cases. 
This therapy is now a routine treatment in this hos- 
pital in all newly admitted cases. 
Hydrotherapy.?During the triennium under report 
407 cases were given this treatment?the average num- 
ber of hours of emersion per patient was 63. 
There was a great rush on this department throughout 
the period under report and many patients were denied 
this useful form of therapy for want of accommodation. 
Hydrotherapy is chiefly instrumental in allaying excite- 
ment and insomnia in highly refractory cases. The 
scheme of building a hydrotherapy ward could not be 
taken up for want of funds. 
Occupational therapy.?This therapy still remains as 
the sheet anchor of our treatment. A very brief outline 
of works done in this department is as follows:? 
Weaving, cane and bamboo work, smithy, carpentry, 
tailoring, cobbling, mending clothes, mattress and 
pillow making, lace making, knitting, embroidery work, 
durrie making, gardening, domestic and office work. Joss 
stick making, etc. 
It is our policy to make the occupational therapy 
departments of this hospital, which serve as excellent 
therapeutic agents, to be useful both to the patients 
as well as to the State. 
Approximately 200 patients attend daily the tem- 
porary amusement hall of both sections and play various 
indoor games. As heretofore the hospital band con- 
tinued to entertain the patients in the amusement hall, 
and the gramophones were used in the wards for the 
amusement of those patients who were unable to attend 
the amusement halls. During the triennium, many 
football and hockey matches were played with outside 
teams of Kanke and Ranchi and the hospital team 
won many such matches. The annual sports of the 
hospital were held every year during Christmas 
week and many patients of both sexes took part in 
it and carried away many prizes. On New Year's eve 
we arrange a display of fireworks every year which the 
patients look forward to and enjoy immensely. 
During the period under report the hospital staff 
staged 17 theatrical shows, and 6 vaudeville shows were 
staged by the combined efforts of patients and staff. 
Moreover when any theatrical, magic and variety show 
of repute visit Ranchi we always try to engage them 
for the patients. 
Cinema.?I ajm happy to be able to record that the 
Government on the recommendation of the managing 
committee sanctioned the purchase of a cinema machine 
at a cost of Rs. 2,120-5-6. 
The reference clerk of the hospital voluntarily 
offered to operate the cinema machine. He was trained 
and now works the machine as well as a regular cinema 
operator. 
Band.?The hospital band which was organized in 
1928 continues to maintain progress under the 
conductorship of Naik Shib Charan, one of the hospital 
attendants. The band now numbers 12 performers, all 
members of the staff. 
Dietetics.?As in former years great attention was 
paid to the diet of the patients. A well-balanced 
nourishing diet largely helps the recovery rate. All 
patients are weighed once a month and those who 
steadily lose weight are segregated in the infirmary with 
a view to ascertaining the cause and are fed on special 
diet until they regain their original weight. The weight 
charts of patients showed a general rise. 
Feasts.?During the period under report several 
special feasts were given to the patients on festive 
occasions such as the Pujas, Holi, Christmas, Id, etc. 
Religion.?Due respect is paid to the religious beliefs 
of the patients and arrangements are made for the 
observance of their respective religious festivals. They 
are allowed to go out on these days to attend prayers, 
pujas, hirtans, etc. The Muhammadan patients regu- 
larly attend the Juma prayers under the maulavi who 
is one of the jamadars of this hospital. 
The Hindus attend the kirtans whenever a pandit 
calls at Kanke and the Christian patients attend divine 
services under the Roman Catholic and Anglican 
chaplains. 
Picnics.?Picnics are a notable feature of amusement 
for the patients and provide a change in surroundings 
which is much appreciated by the patients. 
Parole.?Throughout the triennium many patients 
were granted local as well as Ranchi parole with or 
without attendants. Patients seem to enjoy this liberty 
and the privilege is not abused and it has a salutary 
effect on their general health. 
Motor drives.?The hospital cars were kept busy 
throughout the triennium on drives. Patients of both 
sexes go out daily for drives by turn and are also 
allowed to shop at Ranchi on their return journey from 
drives. 
It will be seen from the above abstract that, the 
treatment of patients in the Iudian mental hospital is

amusement receives every consideration. 
ANNUAL REPORT OF THE DIRECTOR, INSTI- 
TUTE FOR MEDICAL RESEARCH, FEDERA- 
TED MALAY STATES, FOR THE YEAR 1932 
The intensification of the economic depression has 
necessitated the practice of every possible economy in 
the past year. The laboratories are fortunately well 
equipped and consequently it has been possible to limit 
expenditure in the main to renewals. 
The present necessity for retrenchment has neces- 
sitated some reduction in the health staff of the 
Medical Department. In order to assist State Medical 
Authorities in times of epidemics of enteric, cholera 
or plague, a mobile unit will be formed from the staff 
of this institute. The unit will be primarily concerned 
with diagnosis; a travelling laboratory is available, and 
bacteriological examinations will be made in the epi- 
demic area. The delay consequent upon the forwarding 
of specimens to Kuala Lumpur or Ipoh will thus be 
obviated and control will be facilitated. Though the 
unit is to be primarily concerned with diagnosis, any 
staff which can be spared will be available to render 
assistance in the application of control measures. 
Malaria 
Quino-plasmoquine 
In the previous report the attempted control by 
quino-plasmoquine of malaria among the police 
stationed at a malarious village near Kuala Lumpur was 
recorded. It was then stated that the number of police, 
including dependents, numbered sixty-seven when the 
investigation was commenced. At that time the spleen 
rate was 10 per cent and the parasite rate 5 per cent. 
At first (October 1931), three tablets of quino- 
plasmoquine?a total daily dosage of 13 grains of 
quinine and 0.03 gramme of plasmoquine?was given 
on Mondays, Tuesdays and Wednesdays of each week. 
In that month there was one case of subtertian malaria. 
A few weeks after the treatment had been introduced, 
there were complaints of dizziness and ' ringing' of the 
ears. The dose was therefore reduced from three to 
two tablets on the first three days of each week. At 
a surprise examination of urines in November, it was 
found that only about one-quarter of the police were 
excreting quinine. A further case of subtertian malaria 
occurred in January of the year under review, and five 
more cases in April. In May, another surprise 
examination of urines was undertaken, and 80 per cent 
of the staff was found to be taking the preparation. 
Since June no further cases of malaria had been 
reported. 
Experiments on the prophylactic value of plasmoquine 
simplex, when exhibited twice weekly in an adult dose 
of 0.04 gramme, were recorded in the last report. The 
venue of that experiment was a malarious rubber estate, 
the population of which was then about 330. Two 
adjacent estates were used as controls, and during the 
experiment, which was continued for twelve months, 
quarterly surveys for parasite and spleen rates, haemo- 
globin percentage, and anopheline breeding places were 
undertaken. The experiment was continued from 
February 1930 until February 1931, when it was found 
that the parasite rate had decreased from 282 per cent 
to 15.8 per cent on the experimental estate, though it 
had also fallen from 17.2 per cent to 5.3 per cent on 
the first control estate, and from 22.9 per cent to 10.1 
per cent on the second control estate (.fee I. M. R. 
Bulletin No. 3 of 1931). 
These three rubber plantations have been utilized 
for a second experiment?on the ' prophylactic' value 
of quino-plasmoquine?the same two estates being used 
as controls. At a survey undertaken at the commence- 
ment, the parasite rates on the three estates were 
found to be 11.4, 5.4 and 9.0 per cent, while the spleen 
rates were 22.7, 5.8 and 22.2 per cent, respectively. 
Towards the end of April, quino-plasmoquine was 
exhibited in an adult dose of two tablets (9 grains of 
quinine and 0.02 gramme of plasmoquine) on 
Mondays, Tuesdays and Wednesdays of each week- 
Children from six to eleven years of age were given 
one tablet, and from three to five years, half a tablet, 
while infants of less than three years were not treated. 
No clinical cases of malaria occurred until July when 
there were five benign tertian cases and one subtertian 
case. The latter had passed a week's holiday on 
another malarious rubber estate one-and-a-half weeks 
before the onset of fever. 
In view of these cases of benign tertian malaria it 
was decided to modify the scheme of treatment, and 
the preparation was then given early in August in an 
adult dosage of two tablets on Mondays, Tuesdays and 
Fridays, and one tablet on Saturdays. Since that 
change was effected no further clinical cases of malaria 
have occurred. 
It is of interest that in the nine months during which 
the experiment was continued, the parasite rate on the 
experimental plantation decreased from 11.4 per cent 
to 4.7 per cent. There were at the last survey six cases 
in which parasites were found. Two of these, both 
crescent carriers, arrived on the estate shortly before 
that survey, i.e., on the 19th and 20th of December 
only. Two other cases in which ring forms of 
P. falciparum, were seen had failed to attend the 
musters in the last week of December and had thus 
avoided treatment. The remaining cases, also harbour- 
ing P. falcipanim, were children aged twelve and five 
years respectively. The former had received one tablet, 
and the latter one-half of a tablet of quino-plasmoquine 
on Mondays, Tuesdays and Fridays, and one-half of 
a tablet and one-quarter of a tablet respectively on 
Saturdays. These children had taken the preparation 
regularly, and it therefore appears that these doses were 
insufficient for protection. 
The actual cost to estates of this method of protec- 
tion would be about SI,100 (Straits) per annum per 
one hundred labourers including dependents. 
Atebrin 
By the end of the year details were available of 
125 cases treated in hospital with atebrin, the majority 
of these having been kept under subsequent observa- 
tion for several weeks and months. In addition, a 
series of 125 control cases treated with quinine bi- 
hydrochloride and similarly observed for long periods 
had been completed. A strict comparison is thus 
possible between atebrin and quinine both in regard 
to immediate and after effects. Cases of all types of 
malaria, showing varying degrees of severity, are avail- 
able for comparison in both series. Further, with the 
object of ascertaining the action on gametocytes, 
batches of anopheline mosquitoes have been fed from 
time to time, during and after treatment, on cases of 
both series. 
The following comparison is based on the results 
obtained in the two series: In the treatment of benign 
tertian and quartan cases the preparation is at least as 
effective as quinine in ridding the peripheral blood of 
parasites and in relieving symptoms. It also has a 
definite parasiticidal action on the ring forms of 
P. falciparum. As atebrin is not yet available for use 
by injection, and as subtertian malaria shows such 
variation in clinical severity, a complete comparison 
between quinine and atebrin cannot be made until 
stable solutions of the latter drug are prepared and a 
very large series of treated subtertian cases is available 
for contrast. Its use is at present limited in cases of 
subtertian malaria to those that are able to retain 
and absorb the drug when it is given orally. 
Like quinine, atebrin does not affect the viability of 
the gametocytes of P. vivax or P. falciparum. When 
exhibited for a period of seven days or less, however, 
the gametocytes of P. vivax are destroyed with suffi- 
cient rapidity to prevent the infection of mosquitoes 
after three or four days' treatment. Among gameto- 
cyte carriers of P. falciparum, neither atebrin nor

quinine is effective in preventing the infection of 
anophelines having access to such patients when under 
treatment. But as atebrin is definitely the more 
effective in preventing relapses in all three types of 
infection, the production of further generations of 
gametocytes (on relapse) is less likely to occur among 
cases so treated, and thus atebrin possesses greater 
prophylactic value than quinine. 
Among the cases kept under observation following 
the completion of treatment, the relapse rate after 
quinine was 35 per cent. Although the atebrin-treated 
cases were observed for longer periods the relapse rate 
Was only 2 per cent. 
In September last it was possible to commence a 
series of experiments with atebrin on rubber estates. 
The primary object was to determine, over a period 
of about a year, the relative efficacy and cost of atebrin 
on the one hand, and of quinine on the other. As in 
the case of the experiment with quino-plasmoquine, the 
anti-larval measures were continued as usual on the 
estates. 
On the three estates, 450 persons have been treated 
with atebrin, and 353 have served as controls. The 
experiment is still in progress but some preliminary 
figures are available. Three parasite and spleen surveys 
(one prior to the commencement of the experiment) 
had so far been carried out on two of the estates. 
The second survey was undertaken six weeks after the 
experimental groups had been treated en masse with 
atebrin, and the third was made six weeks after certain 
individuals, who had been found at the second survey 
to harbour parasites, had received a second course. 
The results of these surveys on no. 1 estate are of 
particular interest because the labourers and depend- 
ents in both groups are housed together in the same 
lines, and all were therefore regarded as equally likely 
to contract fresh malarial infection. The marked fall 
in both spleen and parasite rates among the experi- 
mental group is a striking feature of these preliminary 
results. The equally marked increase of the rates 
among the control group suggests that some transmis- 
sion of malaria was taking place on the estate in the 
interval of twelve weeks between the preliminary and 
the third surveys though it is impossible to determine 
the relative importance of relapses and reinfections 
vis-a-vis this rise in incidence. If infected mosquitoes 
were present in the lines, then the results suggest that 
atebrin may have some prophylactic value. 
The cost of quinine given during the twelve weeks 
interval to members of the control group exceeded the 
total cost of atebrin used among the experimental 
group, but the latter group had the advantage, first, 
of mass treatment with atebrin and, later, of the selec- 
tive treatment of infected individuals identified during 
the second parasite surveys. The members of the 
control group received quinine treatment only if they 
had fever, or ' spleen mixture' (equivalent to gr. x of 
quinine daily) if they had palpable spleens. Such 
treatment, however, seems ineffective when judged by 
the rising spleen rate observed among the control 
group; the fall in the spleen rate among the experi- 
mental group indicates that a better method of reducing 
the incidence of enlarged spleens is to give an anti- 
malarial drug in curative doses. 
On the second estate, the atebrin-treated and the 
control groups were housed in separate lines situated 
at a distance of about one-quarter of a mile apart. 
On this estate, no ' spleen mixtures' were given to 
the control group after the preliminary survey. The 
improvement shown among the atebrin-treated group 
is similar to that which occurred on estate no. 1. The 
figures for the third estate are not yet available. 
In assessing the significance and the possible appli- 
cations of these results, a number of factors must be 
taken into consideration. Estate populations are 
constantly changing. The treatment of the control 
groups with quinine is unsatisfactory in view of the 
insufficient dosage employed (see above). Further, the 
presence among the estate population of infants and 
young children who cannot be properly treated with 
quinine or are too young to swallow atebrin tablets 
detracts from the significance of the figures. Such 
cases have, however, been included when computating 
parasite and spleen rates for both the experimental 
and control groups. 
The number of working days lost as the result of 
malarial infection is of considerable importance and 
has a direct bearing on the question of the comparative 
costs of quinine and atebrin. _ If quinine is used in large 
doses, symptoms of cinchonism and frequent relapses 
are more likely to cause the loss of working time among 
labourers so treated?as compared with individuals 
receiving a less toxic and more efficient drug. 
If atebrin is employed, the presence on the estate of 
an efficient dresser is most advantageous. He can 
periodically select by examination of blood specimens 
those individuals with positive blood slides, thus often 
anticipating the onset of symptoms, reducing the 
chances of the infection of mosquitoes, and preventing 
the unnecessary consumption of the drug in cases of 
non-malarial fever. The alternative of selecting and 
treating febrile individuals only would be more costly 
and less satisfactory, particularly in times of epidemics 
of other diseases such as influenza, etc. 
A supply of ' Totaquina' for clinical trial was 
recently obtained from a London manufacturer. The 
cost was $10.30 (Straits) a pound, that of quinine sul- 
phate at the time of purchase being $12.10, and of 
cinchona febrifuge $8.20. This price is said to be the 
lowest at which the manufacturers are able to supply 
the product at the present time. Furthermore^ it is 
understood that with the present world distribution of 
high quinine-yielding barks, any increase in the demand 
for totaquina to a point at which the residues of 
quinine manufacture become exhausted, would be likely 
still further to enhance the price. 
The totaquina received was a fine yellowish brown 
powder of extremely bitter taste, with the characteristic 
odour of cinchona febrifuge. It was practically 
insoluble in water, but almost completely soluble in 
warm alcohol. When acidified with hydrochloric acid 
until free acid appeared, that is to say, until the 
alkaloidal bases had been completely converted into 
bi-liydrochlorides, the totaquina was almost completely 
soluble. The bi-liydrochloride solution, like that of 
cinchona febrifuge, was of brownish red colour, the 
insoluble deposit amounting to less than one per cent. 
The preparation of a suitable solution was the subject 
of considerable experimentation, and the assistance of 
the chemical division in this connection is gratefully 
acknowledged. 
A clinical trial of totaquina is proceeding. Up to 
the end of the year seventy-four cases of malaria have 
been treated half with totaquina, and half as controls 
with quinine bi-hydrochloride. 
A first series of fifty cases received 20 grains a day 
of one or other of these drugs. A second series, still 
incomplete, was treated on a body-weight basis, with 
solutions of the bi-hydrochlorides of these drugs, at a 
dosage of 2 grammes per 100 lb. body-weight. This 
dosage, although considerably in excess of clinical 
requirements, was adopted in order to accentuate any 
differences in the toxicity of, or the individual 
tolerance to, totaquina and quinine. 
Totaquina bi-hydrochloride has been prepared as 
follows: A weighed amount of totaquina was titrated 
with normal hydrochloric acid until free acid appeared 
?Congo red being used as indicator?and diluted with 
water to a strength of two per cent. The powder 
readily went into almost complete solution, though a 
slight residue remained even with excess acid. The 
solution thus prepared approximated in acid content 
to the quinine bi-hydrochloride solution used for the 
control cases. 
Thick blood films from all cases were examined 
daily, and parasite counts made by Sinton's fowl- 
corpuscle method. ,

Parasites and fever tend to disappear rather earlier 
from the quinine-treated cases, but the number treated 
to date is small and the differences are within the 
limits of probable error. In all cases treated, careful 
records were kept of symptoms suggesting intolerance 
towards the drugs, and in so far as these symptoms 
afford an index of toxicity, here again there would 
appear to be no significant differences between totaquina 
and quinine. 
In none of the cases treated to date has it been 
necessary to administer quinine or totaquina by intra- 
muscular injection. Cases unable to tolerate oral 
treatment are not uncommon in hospital practice, and 
attempts have been made to develop a preparation of 
totaquina suitable for injection. Two such prepara- 
tions are under trial, a ten per cent suspension of 
totaquina in olive oil, and a fifty per cent filtered 
solution of totaquina bi-hydrochloride. The prepara- 
tion of the oily suspension was prompted by the 
supposition that the tissue necrosis which follows the 
injection of strong solutions of acid salts of the 
cinchona alkaloids may perhaps be due not so much 
to the alkaloids themselves as to their acidity. 
Preliminary experiments on guinea-pigs have not been 
encouraging. Oily suspensions were found to be 
difficult to work with; in adequate concentration they 
do not easily pass through a suitably sized needle, 
while unabsorbed oil has been recognized as long as 
11 days after injection. The tendency to necrosis and 
ulceration appears, however, to be appreciably reduced. 
The necrotic cffect of a fifty per cent solution of 
totaquina bi-hydrochloride following subcutaneous 
injection into guinea-pigs has not appeared greater than 
that produced by quinine bi-hydrochloride in similar 
concentration, but further work on the varying effects 
of a wider range of doses is necessary before totaquina 
in solution can be exhibited in this manner to human 
cases. 
In addition to the above investigations on the 
treatment of malaria, extensive laboratory tests on the 
subject of culture of malaria parasites as an aid to 
din gnosis have been carried out, as well as examination 
of the nature of Mayer's reagent as an indicator of 
the excretion of quinine. Mosquitoes have also 
received a great deal of intention and the various 
Malayan anophelines have been studied from the point 
of view of infectivity by malaria, trapping and identi- 
fication, larval surveys, feeding habits and the larvicidal 
effects of various mineral oils. Although a considerable 
amount of the information on the above points that is 
contained in this report is necessarily of special value 
to workers in Malaya, it may be read with profit by 
malaria investigators anywhere in the world. 
The work on tropical typhus has been continued and 
a great deal has been done on elucidating its various 
problems, not the least important of which is the appa- 
rent proof of the identity of the ' K ' type of tropical 
typhus and Japanese river fever. A full report of this 
work is promised in the near future and it will be 
awaited with interest. Numerous other investigations 
including attempts at transmission of leprosy to rats, 
epidemiology of diphtheria, investigation of vaccine 
lymph, the bacteriology of milk, extract of rice polish- 
ings, typing of pneumococci, and the study of skin 
diseases, have been carried out. 
An interesting case of infection of a human being 
with Trypanosoma lewisi of the rat is reported, and 
although the parasites disappeared five days after their 
discovery in the blood of the patient this record is of 
considerable interest as indicating that this widely 
distributed rat parasite may become established in man. 
The latter part of the report contains an account of 
a large amount of routine work on bacteriology, patho- 
logy, water examination, medico-legal, and chemical 
reports. Once again we congratulate the director in 
producing a record of hard work and progress which it , 
would be difficult for any institute of similar size to 
equal. I 
REPORT BY THE DEAN ON THE WORK OF THE 
LONDON SCHOOL OF HYGIENE AND TROPI- 
CAL MEDICINE FOR THE YEAR ENDED 
31ST JULY, 1933 
This report is difficult to summarize, because, in the 
form in which it is presented, only the essentials are 
included. 
Its perusal, however, shows that the expansion of the 
activities of the London school, to which we referred 
last year, still continues and considerable further 
progress has been made. In spite of the fact that the 
staff have to spend so much time in teaching, a large 
volume of original work is turned out and in appendix I, 
which is a list of papers and reports published during 
the year, there are over 120 publications. 
The probability of the absorption of I he Ross 
Institute by the London school is alluded to in the 
report, and we understand that this has since become 
an accomplished fact. This will still more enhance the 
work of the school, because as a result of this 
amalgamation they have acquired a body of men who 
are principally interested in field work in malaria. 
The manner in which the London school is growing 
from year to year indicates that before very long it will 
be the chief centre of public health work (in its widest 
sense) in the Empire, if it has not already reached 
that position.
