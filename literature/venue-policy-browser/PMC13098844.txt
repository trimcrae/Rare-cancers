## ABSTRACT

## Introduction:
The World Health Organization (WHO) South-East Asia Regional Office (SEARO) plays a crucial role in addressing health challenges in one of the world’s most diverse and populous regions. This study analyzes the WHO SEARO Regional Committee Meeting Reports from sessions 54 to 74 (2000-2021) to understand the evolution of regional health policies and their impact on health outcomes.

## Methods:
A policy evaluation framework with keyword co-occurrence analysis was employed to identify primary policy focuses and discussion themes. Data were extracted from the WHO SEARO meeting reports using two study tools: one for extracting keywords and another for health parameters. Statistical analysis, including multivariable linear regression and logistic regression, was performed to explore correlations between keyword occurrences and health outcomes.

## Results:
The analysis revealed significant improvements in health outcomes. For instance, communicable diseases saw a reduction of 35%, and maternal mortality rates decreased by 40% over the two decades. Child mortality rates also saw a significant decline of 45%. However, the region faces persistent challenges such as a 25% increase in noncommunicable diseases and growing environmental health issues. Key themes identified include infectious diseases (mentioned in 65% of reports), maternal and child health (75%), environmental health (50%), and emerging threats like pandemics and climate change (30%).

## Conclusion:
The WHO SEARO Regional Committee meetings are vital for facilitating regional collaboration and aligning national health policies with global health goals. By systematically analyzing the discussions and policy focuses, this study contributes to ongoing efforts to improve health outcomes in the South-East Asia Region and supports the development of effective evidence-based health policies.

## Introduction
The World Health Organization (WHO) plays a crucial role in global health governance, providing a platform for countries to collaborate and address health challenges collectively.[ ] Within this structure, Regional Committees are integral to the development and implementation of health policies tailored to specific needs of different regions.[ ] Among these, the WHO Regional Committee for South-East Asia (SEARO) is of particular significance due to the diverse and populous nature of the region it serves.
The WHO South-East Asia Region comprises 11 member states: Bangladesh, Bhutan, Democratic People’s Republic of Korea, India, Indonesia, Maldives, Myanmar, Nepal, Sri Lanka, Thailand, and Timor-Leste.[ ] Additionally, East Timor and Laos participate as nonmembers. This region, which accounts for over a quarter of the global population, faces a unique set of health challenges.[ ] These challenges include a high burden of both communicable and noncommunicable diseases, significant maternal and child health issues, environmental health concerns, and emerging threats such as pandemics and climate change.[ ]
Communicable diseases, like tuberculosis, HIV/AIDS, and malaria, continue to pose major public health threats in the region.[ ] Examples include South-East Asia accounting for 43% of global tuberculosis cases in 2020.[ ] Noncommunicable diseases (NCDs), including diabetes, cardiovascular diseases, and cancer, are also on the rise, exacerbated by factors such as urbanization, lifestyle changes, and aging populations.[ ] The prevalence of diabetes in India, for example, is projected to reach 101 million by 2030, highlighting the urgent need for effective health policies and interventions.[ ]
Maternal and child health remains a critical concern. Despite significant progress, the region still records high rates of maternal and child mortality.[ ] For instance, in 2019, the maternal mortality ratio in the region was 152 per 100,000 live births, significantly behind the global target of less than 70 per 100,000 live births by 2030.[ ] Additionally, child malnutrition remains prevalent, with countries like Bangladesh and Nepal experiencing high rates of stunting and wasting among children under five.[ ] The prevalence of severe undernutrition among under-5 children was approximately 11.5% in Bangladesh and 12.6% in Nepal.[ ]
Environmental health issues, such as air pollution, water quality, and climate change, also present significant challenges. South-East Asia has some of the world’s most polluted cities, with air quality levels frequently exceeding WHO guidelines, including Bangladesh, India, and Pakistan, remains the most polluted region globally, with 42 of the 50 most polluted cities located in these countries.[ ] According to a 2019 report by the World Bank, environmental degradation and pollution are responsible for 9 million premature deaths globally each year, with a significant number occurring in South-East Asia.[ ] With various public healthcare issues at stake, it is paramount for cross-collaboration and mutual understanding to be established across different countries across the South-East Asia region.
The WHO SEARO Regional Committee meetings provide a vital forum for addressing these health challenges. These annual meetings bring together health ministers, senior health officials, and representatives from various international and nongovernmental organizations to discuss key health issues, share experiences, and adopt resolutions that guide the region’s health agenda. The discussions and decisions made during these meetings have far-reaching implications for health policies and programs implemented in the member states. For instance, the adoption of the “Delhi Declaration on Maternal, Newborn, and Child Health” during the 72nd session in 2019 has significantly impacted national health strategies aimed at reducing maternal and child mortality rates.[ ] The regional meetings have supported Bangladesh in its Expanded Program on Immunization (EPI), significantly improving vaccination coverage and reducing vaccine-preventable diseases such as measles and polio.[ ] India’s successful eradication of polio, with the country being declared polio-free in 2014, was also a result of collaborative efforts and strategic guidelines from these meetings.[ ] Sri Lanka benefited from regional support and shared strategies in malaria control, leading to the country being certified malaria-free by WHO in 2016.[ ] Nepal’s significant improvements in maternal and child health through targeted interventions and support in areas such as antenatal care and skilled birth attendance, leading to reductions in maternal and child mortality rates, also showcase the positive impact of these regional meetings.[ ]
Despite the critical importance of these meetings, there has been limited systematic analysis of policy focuses and discussion themes that emerge from these sessions. Understanding these aspects is crucial for several reasons. First, it allows for identification of regional health priorities and the alignment of these priorities with global health goals. Secondly, it helps in assessing the effectiveness of past policies and in guiding the development of future policies. Finally, it provides insights into the dynamics of regional health governance and the collaboration between member states. For example, Thailand’s implementation of stringent tobacco control measures, including graphic health warnings and advertising bans, has been bolstered by regional policies and resolutions on noncommunicable diseases discussed in the SEARO meetings.[ ]
This study aims to fill this gap by providing a comprehensive analysis of the WHO SEARO Regional Committee Meeting Reports from sessions 54 to 74. By examining keyword co-occurrences in these reports, we seek to identify primary policy focuses and discussion themes that have shaped the regional health agenda over the years. This approach will enable us to understand evolving priorities and assess the alignment between policy discussions and the health and healthcare outcomes observed in the region.
The expected outcomes of this study include a detailed mapping of the key health issues discussed in the WHO SEARO meetings, insights into the policy priorities of the member states, and an evaluation of the impact of these policies on regional health outcomes. In conclusion, the WHO SEARO Regional Committee meetings play a crucial role in shaping the health policies of one of the world’s most diverse and populous regions. By systematically analyzing the discussions and policy focuses of these meetings, this study aims to contribute to the ongoing efforts to improve health outcomes in the South-East Asia Region and to support the development of effective, evidence-based health policies.

## Methods
This study utilized a policy evaluation framework with a cross-sectional design to assess WHO SEARO Committee Meeting Reports from Sessions 54 to 74, spanning the years 2000 to 2021. Keywords that signal policy focuses and discussion spotlights throughout the regional-level meetings between nations were identified.

## Inclusion of countries
Members of the WHO Regional Committee for SEARO and nonmember states like East Timor and Laos were included in the analysis. These countries were chosen due to their participation in the WHO SEARO meetings and their significant impact on regional health policies.

## Formation of study tools
Two study tools were developed to facilitate data extraction and analysis. The first tool was a table designed to extract keywords from the WHO RCM Reports. The columns in this table included:

Regional Committee Meeting Number
Venue, date, and mode of the meeting
Number of national representatives who were female
Number of Representatives of the United Nations and Specialized Agencies
Number of Representatives from Nongovernmental Organizations in Official Relations with WHO
Number of Ambassadors/High Commissioners
Special invitees, if any
Key agendas adopted
Key policies relevant to any healthcare context highlights/theme of the meeting
Co-occurrences of incidence keywords as study outcomes

Keywords included terms related to youths, environmental health, drugs and self-harm, infectious diseases, reproductive justice and care, maternal and child care, healthcare factors and outcomes, and miscellaneous. The specific keywords are listed in .
The second study tool comprised various health parameters for the 13 included countries from 2000 to 2022. The parameters included deaths, prevalences, incidences, and DALYs (disability-adjusted life years) related to the relevant keywords identified in the RCM reports, as detailed in and .
 

## Data extraction
Data extraction was conducted independently by eight independent researchers. The first study tool was used to extract data from the WHO RCM Reports. Each researcher was assigned specific reports to ensure comprehensive coverage. The second study tool’s data were extracted similarly by four researchers. The accuracy of the extracted data was subsequently rechecked by an additional researcher.

## Data analysis
Data analysis involved exploring co-occurrences of keywords and their relationships with the health parameters identified in the second study tool. To address the multiple comparisons problem, we applied the Bonferroni correction to control for false discovery rates. Multivariable linear regression was conducted to identify significant correlations between keyword occurrences in the RCM reports and the health outcomes in the region, with adjustments made for continuous variables. For ordinal and categorical variables, logistic regression and Chi-square tests were employed to assess associations.
This statistical analysis was performed by two researchers using SPSS (version 27) statistical software. The precise methodology included defining keyword occurrences as independent variables and health outcomes as dependent variables. Covariates, such as demographic factors, socioeconomic status, and baseline health indicators, were included to adjust for potential confounding. The results of the correlation analysis are presented in , which summarizes the significant associations identified across the various thematic topics. The analysis aimed to identify trends and patterns in the policy focuses and their impact on regional health outcomes, providing insights into the effectiveness of the discussed policies and areas needing further attention.
 

## Outcomes assessed
The primary outcomes assessed were the frequency and co-occurrence of keywords related to various health issues, including:

Youth or student-related topics
Environmental health (e.g. climate change, pollution)
Drug use and self-harm
Infectious diseases (e.g. COVID-19, tuberculosis)
Reproductive justice and care
Maternal and child health
General healthcare factors and outcomes

More details of keywords could be found in Appendix 1.0. The analysis of these keywords provided a detailed mapping of the policy focuses during the WHO SEARO meetings and their alignment with the health challenges in the region.

## Results

## Meeting characteristics
Over the 21 WHO South-East Asia Regional Committee Meetings from the 54th to 74th sessions, the majority were conducted offline, accounting for 90.5% (19 meetings), with the remaining 9.5% (two meetings) held online. A notable observation from these meetings was the low representation of female national representatives, which was 1 (1,2). The meetings also saw the participation of 6 (3, 11) representatives from the United Nations and 17 (13, 22) representatives from specialized agencies and NGOs in official relations with WHO [refer ]

## Overarching theme
The overarching theme of the WHO South-East Asia Regional Committee Meetings in the last two decades is prominently centered on addressing infectious diseases (31.4% citations) and improving healthcare outcomes (16.1%). Environmental health (9.6%) and reproductive justice (3.2%) also receive considerable attention, while mental health (1.1%) and substance abuse (2.3%) are less frequently discussed; their inclusion points to an emerging recognition of these critical issues. Maternal and child care remains a priority, though the citations are only 1.6% overall.

## Trends of outcome parameters
We observed numerous outcome parameters to quantify global health progress, as illustrated in .
 
Communicable Diseases:

The trend analysis revealed significant reductions in several communicable diseases over the study period:

Cholera: The number of cases decreased from 30,000 in 2000 to 12,000 in 2021, reflecting a 60% reduction (P < 0.05).
Trachoma: Incidence rates dropped by 50%, from 40,000 cases in 2000 to 20,000 in 2021.
Meningitis: Reported cases fell from 25,000 in 2000 to 10,000 in 2021, marking a 60% decline.
Hepatitis B: The incidence rate decreased by 70%, from 35,000 cases in 2000 to 10,500 in 2021.

Maternal and Child Health (MCH):

Maternal and child health indicators showed marked improvements:

Under-5 Mortality: The under-5 mortality rate dropped from 88 per 1,000 live births in 2000 to 34 per 1,000 live births in 2021, a 61% reduction.
Maternal Mortality: The maternal mortality ratio decreased by 55%, from 380 per 100,000 live births in 2000 to 170 per 100,000 live births in 2021.
Child Mortality: The child mortality rate fell from 72 per 1,000 live births in 2000 to 30 per 1,000 live births in 2021, a 58% reduction.
Infant Mortality: The infant mortality rate declined by 62%, from 65 per 1,000 live births in 2000 to 25 per 1,000 live births in 2021.
Intrapartum Deaths: Significant reductions were noted, especially in India, contributing to the overall decrease in maternal and child mortality rates.

Noncommunicable Diseases (NCD):

Despite progress in communicable diseases and maternal and child health, the region saw an increase in deaths related to noncommunicable diseases:

Outdoor Air Pollution: Deaths due to outdoor air pollution increased by 40%, from 200,000 in 2000 to 280,000 in 2021.
Overweight and Obesity: The prevalence of overweight individuals rose from 15% in 2000 to 25% in 2021, and obesity increased from 8% to 16% over the same period.
Chronic Kidney Disease: The incidence of chronic kidney disease saw a 50% rise, with cases increasing from 100,000 in 2000 to 150,000 in 2021.

## Correlation Findings: Impact of the meeting on actual health outcomes
Universal Health Coverage (UHC) Correlations: The analysis identified several significant correlations involving universal health coverage:

Gender Equity: Positive correlation with UHC (0.533, P < 0.05), indicating better gender equity outcomes with improved health coverage.
Substance Abuse: Positive correlation (0.328, P < 0.05), suggesting broader health coverage may address substance abuse issues.
Nutrition/Malnutrition/Undernutrition: Negative correlation (-0.113, P < 0.05), highlighting potential gaps in addressing nutritional needs despite overall health coverage improvements.

Climate and Environmental Health: The relationship between environmental health and various health indicators was examined:

Water-Borne Illnesses: Negative correlation with environmental health efforts in countries like Bangladesh (-0.588, P < 0.01), indicating reduced water-borne diseases with better environmental health measures.
Air Pollution: Positive correlation with indoor air pollution (0.595, P < 0.01), pointing to challenges in mitigating air quality issues.

Occupational Health Hazards: Occupational health hazards were positively correlated with air pollution (0.445, P < 0.05), indicating that workplaces exposed to higher pollution levels might face greater health risks.
Drug Use: The correlations involving drug use presented a mixed picture:

Universal Health Coverage: Negative correlation (-0.223, P < 0.05), suggesting higher coverage might reduce drug-related issues.
Health Informatics: Positive correlation (0.239, P < 0.05), indicating better data systems could help manage drug use issues more effectively.

Maternal Health and Child Health: Maternal health showed a negative correlation with maternal mortality (-0.164, P < 0.05), while child health had a positive correlation (0.287, P < 0.05), suggesting that improvements in these areas are closely linked to reductions in mortality rates.

Stunting, Overweight, and Wasting:

Nutritional indicators revealed significant correlations:

Stunting and Wasting: Positive correlation (0.607, P < 0.01), indicating children who are stunted are also likely to experience wasting.
Overweight and Wasting: Positive correlation (0.539, P < 0.05), highlighting the complex interplay of different nutritional challenges.

Child Mortality Rates:

Under-5 Mortality and Infant Mortality: Positive correlation (0.460, P < 0.05), indicating that improvements in child health are linked to reductions in infant deaths.
Under-5 Mortality and Maternal Mortality: Positive correlation (0.445, P < 0.05), suggesting that overall health improvements benefit both child and maternal mortality rates.
Exclusive Breastfeeding: Negative correlation with maternal mortality (-0.473, P < 0.05), emphasizing the importance of breastfeeding in improving maternal health outcomes.

Noncommunicable diseases: They showed significant correlations:

Cancer: Correlated with cervical cancer (0.682, P < 0.001) and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (0.482, P < 0.05), indicating shared risk factors.
Hypertension: Correlated with stroke (0.497, P < 0.05), underscoring the link between cardiovascular conditions.

Tobacco and Alcohol Use:

The analysis highlighted correlations between tobacco and alcohol use:

Tobacco Control and Alcohol Use: Positive correlation (0.456, P < 0.05), suggesting that regions with strong tobacco control also address alcohol-related issues.
Diabetes: Negative correlation with alcohol use (-0.465, P < 0.05), indicating that lower alcohol consumption might be associated with better diabetes outcomes.

Overall, the detailed analysis of WHO South-East Asia Regional Committee Meetings over two decades reveals significant progress in communicable diseases and maternal and child health outcomes, reflecting effective regional health policies and interventions. However, the increasing prevalence of noncommunicable diseases and the impact of environmental factors present ongoing challenges. The identified correlations highlight critical areas such as universal health coverage, environmental health, and occupational hazards, providing valuable insights into the factors influencing health outcomes in the region. Continued focus on these areas is essential to address the evolving health priorities and improve overall health outcomes in the WHO South-East Asia region.

## Discussion
This study provides a comprehensive analysis of the WHO SEARO Meetings over the past two decades, focusing on thematic trends discussed and their correlations with various health indicators. The findings reveal significant progress in communicable diseases and maternal and child health, alongside emerging challenges in noncommunicable diseases (NCDs) and environmental health. This discussion compares and contrasts our findings with existing literature, contextualizes them within global health frameworks, and outlines implications for policy and future research.

Meeting Characteristics and Thematic Focus

The majority of the WHO South-East Asia Regional Committee Meetings analyzed were conducted offline, with minimal online sessions, reflecting traditional modes of engagement in global health governance. This finding is consistent with the broader literature on international organizations and their meeting dynamics, which often prioritize in-person interactions for diplomacy and consensus-building.[ , ] A notable observation from our study was the low representation of female national representatives in these meetings, which mirrors global trends highlighting gender disparities in health leadership.[ ] This finding underscores the ongoing challenges in achieving gender equity in global health governance despite global commitments such as the Beijing Platform for Action and Sustainable Development Goal 5 (SDG 5) on gender equality and women’s empowerment.[ ]

Thematic Focus of Meetings

The thematic analysis revealed a predominant focus on infectious diseases and maternal and child health across the WHO South-East Asia Regional Committee Meetings. Infectious diseases accounted for a significant portion of the discussions, reflecting the historical burden of diseases like cholera, meningitis, and hepatitis B in the region. This focus aligns with global health priorities set by organizations like the WHO, which emphasize the eradication and control of communicable diseases through targeted interventions and vaccination programs.[ ] Overall, there was relatively less emphasis on mental health (1.1%) and substance abuse (2.3%), but a rising trend matched with their growing importance in global health discourse.[ , ] This finding highlights a potential gap in addressing mental health and substance abuse issues within the regional committee meetings, suggesting a need for greater integration of mental health into broader health agendas.

Progress in Communicable Diseases and Maternal/Child Health

Our study identified significant reductions in communicable diseases such as cholera, trachoma, and meningitis, alongside improvements in maternal and child health outcomes including under-5 mortality, maternal mortality, and child mortality rates. These findings are consistent with global trends reported by the WHO, indicating progress in achieving Millennium Development Goals (MDGs) and SDGs related to health.[ ] The decline in communicable diseases can be attributed to enhanced vaccination coverage, improved healthcare infrastructure, and targeted public health interventions implemented as a result of regional policy discussions and agreements.[ ] These successes underscore the effectiveness of regional collaboration and policy coherence in addressing common health challenges across diverse countries in the WHO South-East Asia Region.

Challenges in Noncommunicable Diseases and Environmental Health

Despite progress in communicable diseases, our study highlighted increasing challenges related to noncommunicable diseases (NCDs) and environmental health. The rise in deaths due to outdoor air pollution and the prevalence of overweight and obesity reflect the region’s epidemiological transition toward lifestyle-related diseases.[ ] Similar trends have been observed globally, indicating a shift in disease burden from infectious to noncommunicable diseases as countries undergo socioeconomic development.[ ] The positive correlation between mentions of air pollution and occupational health hazards with increased pollution levels underscores the complex interplay between environmental factors and public health outcomes.[ ] This finding aligns with global efforts to address environmental determinants of health through policy interventions that promote sustainable development and mitigate environmental risks.[ ]

Correlation Findings and Policy Implications

The correlations identified in our study provide valuable insights into the factors influencing health outcomes in the WHO South-East Asia Region. Positive correlations between universal health coverage (UHC) and gender equity, as well as reductions in substance abuse, suggest that comprehensive health systems and equitable access to healthcare can contribute to improved health outcomes.[ ] These findings are consistent with global evidence linking UHC with better health outcomes and health equity.[ ] However, the negative correlation between environmental health efforts and water-borne illnesses in specific countries like Bangladesh highlights disparities in environmental health outcomes despite regional policy discussions.[ ] This finding underscores the need for targeted interventions that address local environmental health challenges and strengthen sanitation and clean water initiatives in underserved communities.

Occupational Health and Drug Use

The positive correlation between occupational health hazards and air pollution suggests heightened risks for workers in polluted environments, necessitating stronger workplace safety regulations and environmental health protections.[ ] Similar challenges have been documented globally, prompting calls for integrated approaches that address occupational health within broader public health frameworks.[ ] The mixed correlations related to drug use underscore the complexity of substance abuse as a public health issue requiring multifaceted interventions.[ ] Positive correlations with health informatics suggest that data-driven approaches can enhance surveillance and management of drug-related problems, aligning with global efforts to strengthen health information systems for better public health outcomes.[ ]

Maternal and Child Health

The positive correlation between maternal health indicators and reductions in maternal mortality rates reflects the impact of targeted interventions and health system strengthening initiatives discussed in regional meetings.[ ] These findings support global strategies aimed at improving maternal and child health through enhanced access to essential health services and community-based interventions.[ ] Conversely, the negative correlation between exclusive breastfeeding and maternal mortality highlights persistent challenges in promoting optimal infant feeding practices despite regional policy dialogues.[ ] This finding underscores the need for continued investment in maternal and child health programs that prioritize breastfeeding support and nutrition education for mothers.

Nutritional Indicators

The correlations between stunting, wasting, and overweight underscore the double burden of malnutrition in the region, where undernutrition and overnutrition coexist.[ ] Similar trends have been observed globally, necessitating comprehensive nutrition policies that address both ends of the malnutrition spectrum through food security initiatives and dietary diversity promotion.[ ]

Noncommunicable Diseases

The correlations between various NCDs such as cancer, hypertension, and related conditions highlight shared risk factors and the need for integrated prevention and management strategies.[ ] These findings underscore the importance of population-based approaches that address modifiable risk factors through public health policies and community-based interventions.[ ] The positive correlation between tobacco control measures and reductions in alcohol-related issues suggests synergies in addressing common risk factors for NCDs.[ ] These findings support global initiatives aimed at reducing tobacco use and harmful alcohol consumption through evidence-based interventions and policy advocacy.[ ]

## Overall findings
Our findings align with existing literature on global health governance, which emphasizes the role of international organizations in setting health agendas and coordinating regional responses to health challenges.[ , ] Similar studies have documented progress in communicable diseases and maternal and child health outcomes in low- and middle-income countries, attributing successes to targeted interventions and cross-sectoral collaboration.[ ] However, our study also identifies gaps in addressing emerging health issues such as mental health, substance abuse, and environmental health within regional policy frameworks. These findings echo calls in the literature for greater integration of mental health services into primary healthcare and stronger regulatory frameworks for environmental health protection.[ , , ]

## Policy implications and future directions
The correlations identified in this study have several policy implications for regional health governance and global health diplomacy. Strengthening gender equity in health leadership, enhancing environmental health protections, and addressing the double burden of malnutrition require coordinated efforts across sectors and robust policy frameworks.[ ]
Our study had few limitations. Firstly, it relied solely on publicly available meeting transcripts and reports, which may not encompass all discussions or nuances that occurred during the meetings. Secondly, there is a potential bias toward official records, potentially excluding perspectives from non-state actors, civil society organizations, or private sector stakeholders involved in regional health governance. Thirdly, spanning two decades, the study may not fully capture recent shifts in health priorities and policy responses within shorter timeframes, limiting its ability to provide a complete picture of evolving regional health governance dynamics. These limitations underscore the need for caution when interpreting the findings and suggest avenues for future research to address these gaps comprehensively.
Future research should focus on longitudinal studies that track the impact of specific policy interventions discussed in regional meetings on health outcomes. Qualitative research that captures the perspectives of policymakers, health professionals, and community stakeholders could provide deeper insights into the mechanisms through which regional meetings influence health policies and practices.[ ]
These regional policy trends carry significant implications for family physicians and primary care providers. As the first point of contact in most health systems, primary care clinicians are integral to implementing strategies outlined in WHO-SEARO resolutions—ranging from managing noncommunicable diseases and delivering maternal-child health services to coordinating infectious disease surveillance and promoting health education. An understanding of regional governance priorities enables family physicians to align their clinical practice with national and global health goals, advocate for system-level changes, and contribute more meaningfully to interdisciplinary public health efforts. Moreover, this alignment strengthens the continuity of care and enhances the relevance of primary care in addressing region-specific health burdens.
In conclusion, this study contributes to the understanding of regional health governance in the WHO South-East Asia Region, highlighting successes in communicable diseases and maternal and child health, as well as emerging challenges in NCDs and environmental health. By comparing our findings with existing literature, we underscore the importance of evidence-based policy interventions and multisectoral collaboration in achieving sustainable health outcomes. Continued investment in regional health governance and research is essential to address evolving health priorities and achieve global health goals in the post-2020 era.
