## ABSTRACT
Hip‐hop music and culture have existed for decades in the United States. Since the 1970s, five critical elements have been defined as parts of hip‐hop culture: the MC (oral), the DJ (aural), graffiti (visual), knowledge (mental), and breakdancing (physical). The existing literature connects each of these forms of hip‐hop to the experiences of Black students, frequently examining its role in identity formation, resistance, and educational engagement, thereby demonstrating how it functions as a cultural and intellectual venue for self‐expression, empowerment, and leadership development. This study presents three perspectives from leadership educators aiming to foster connection and understanding of the relationship and possibilities of using hip‐hop in leadership educational settings.

## Introduction
Hip‐hop is a multidimensional culture, art form, and genre of music that frequently acts as a platform for marginalized voices. Hip‐hop, which originated in the 1970s as a style of music, has transformed into an era of fashion and a cultural phenomenon globally. For example, when writing the preface to “A Rose that Grew from Concrete,” Leila Steinburg ( ) shared that, Hip‐hop artist Tupac, “…felt that through art we could incite a new revolution that incorporated the heart, mind, body, spirit, and soul,” (p. xix). We approach this scholarship with this framing. As hip‐hop is a holistic movement, a feeling, culture, and purpose, we, three self‐professed hip‐hop enthusiasts and leadership educators, wish to explore how hip‐hop influences the way Black students approach leadership development.
Leadership development, which has received increased attention in recent years (Dugan ), is an area where educators can also incite a revolution. Although the similarities between leadership and hip‐hop may not be immediately apparent to some, there are numerous views and parallels in their respective histories. It is this understanding that we seek to unpack, understand, and build knowledge as a community, and institutional leaders often see these two as methods to bridge the gap between different groups of people. Hip‐hop is commonly regarded as a catalyst for inspiration and motivation. It has been recognized for its ability to heal and empower emerging leaders (Travis ). To understand and extract the complexities of leadership and hip‐hop, we start by laying the foundation for this work in the history of Hip‐hop as a cultural movement in the United States.

## Hip‐Hop's Leadership Historical Roots
As an art form, a symbol of hope, and an outlet to discuss and build ideas around Black culture, H\hip‐hop emerged in the South Bronx in the 1970s (Watkins ). The early days and origins of the art form were connected to the harsh economic conditions that many of the young Black people of the era were facing. While Hip‐hop culture has many different meanings and forms, it is often defined by five elements: the MC (oral), the DJ (aural), graffiti (visual), knowledge (mental), and breakdancing (physical) (Alridge and Stewart ; Reese ; Watkins ).
Collectively, these frames are the core of Hip‐hop culture, and for some Hip‐hop artists, these frames would become the foundation for using their skills to become community leaders to tackle community issues, promote peaceful coexistence in diverse communities, and provide a voice for many of the voiceless on the margins of society (Stanford ). Many of these have led to political and social movements, including prison and education reform (Ards ), social change and youth movements, as well as civil rights and political movements (Akom )
Hip‐hop is a relatively recent form of art, having only been in existence for 50 years. It has not only affected music since its inception, but it has also had an impact on language, dress, dialect, and worldviews (Alridge and Stewart ). Like many other African American art forms that predate its existence, it has evolved into a medium for the expression of both individual and collective identity. Reese ( ) contended hip‐hop is a movement that encompasses consciousness awareness, civil rights awareness, activism awareness, justice, and political awareness. Subsequently, Reese ( ) noted how numerous musical sharing platforms, including Spotify, Apple Music, and YouTube, have further expanded each of these components.
Despite its evolution over the past 50 years, hip‐hop continues to function as a means of self‐expression and identification (Watkins ). Like leadership, hip‐hop has become a term that is associated with the sharing, informing, and developing awareness of one's own identity and stories. Hip‐hop, much like leadership, promotes authenticity, which functions as a connection between the two concepts. For instance, narratives are frequently employed by leadership development professionals, educators, and individuals in leadership positions to convey their viewpoints, inspire others, or communicate impactful messages. To further this thought, Keith Jr ( ) contended hip‐hop allows us to be,
…empowered to narrate their fears, struggles, and dreams, youth can utilize language to unveil hidden truths and inform teaching practices that encourage the development of literacy skills, student creativity, and writing…. hip‐hop draws on the sensibilities and mindsets of hip‐hop culture and is devoted to teaching resistance, knowledge of self, and self‐determination because those principles are part of the collective identity of the politics of Black life. (p. 373)

It is within this frame and understanding, that we as educators position ourselves as leadership educators, who use hip‐hop as a central concept and theme to build a more holistic method of developing the next generation of leaders.

## Why I Love Hip‐Hop? Connecting Positionality, Leadership Identities, and Our Musical Realities
In the film Brown Sugar, released in 2002, the character Sidney “Sid” Shaw, portrayed by Sanaa Lathan, poses the question, “When'd you fall in love with hip‐hop?” (Elliott and Famuyiwa ). In reflecting on the question, our positionalities as researchers, scholars, and educators are interwoven into the very fabric of how we do our work. As leadership educators, each of us acknowledges the role that hip‐hop plays in our development, educational strategies, and contributions to supporting college students.
Furthermore, it is often assumed that listening is a natural part of how we understand hip‐hop and educators but is a critical part of how we communicate our thoughts, leadership, education, and collaborators. Lipari ( ) mentioned we must be, “…listening beings…” as our role should be to, “…embrace difference, misunderstanding and uncertainty, and invite entrance to human communication,” (p. 360). As hip‐hop music enthusiasts and leadership educators, we see Lipari's ( ) views as a bridge between these two worlds we navigate. To expand on this, each of us will take a moment to answer the question first possessed by Sid, so many years ago with an addition that our roles as leadership educators are interconnected in how we see our identities. To start, first Jesse will answer the following question, “When'd you fall in love with hip‐hop and what role does it play in your life as a leadership educator?”
3.1

## Jesse's Response
To answer this question and the intersections of leadership and hip‐hop, I must go back to my early days as a higher education practitioner. While music has always been a central part of my life, I can answer this question with detail. I self‐identify as a Black man, who fell in love with music, more specifically hip‐hop, while I was on my path to self‐discovery as a young leader. As a young man, hip‐hop culture heavily influenced how I saw the world, it was and remains a part of who I am. I also identify as an academic who studies leadership education; as a result, my worldview is influenced by hip‐hop and positionality and is often connected to the words I have heard in a song or thoughts that are connected back to the lyrics of an artist. Hip‐hop has always been a safe and positive reminder of what life is and what life can be.
I love hip‐hop because of the authentic, truthful, and raw realities that the artist often shared. In my youth, the stories of musical artists going through different trials and tribulations were something I could relate to. While the topics might have been different or things I had not had the opportunity to connect with, it helped me navigate the understanding of what advocating meant to and for me. This led to me understanding what it meant to be a leader in other spaces and support others who were going through similar growing pains. As a result, hip‐hop is not something that happens in the background of my leadership development and how I operationalize educating the next generation of leaders, it is a part of what I do.

3.2

## Jason's Response
Growing up as a Black queer boy in north Texas, music helped me to see myself. When I think about my relationship with music, intimacy comes to mind. Music held me when I was alone; she celebrated my wins and always knew just the right words to say when I needed to hear them most. Though our relationship began pure, through the sweet sounds of gospel, soul, and rhythm and blues, the controversial and provocative allure of hip‐hop captured my heart. Hip‐hop was my subversive boyfriend. He was the one who my mom never wanted me to spend time with.
As an adolescent, I would clandestinely listen to the Jay‐Z, Lil’ Kim, Nas, Missy Elliott, and DMX CDs that I borrowed from friends, falling deeper in love with every beat. I identified with the otherness that hip‐hop artists so eloquently articulated, particularly as a Black boy in society. While most messages in society suggested that I was not good enough, hip‐hop posited something different—hip‐hop loved me. He taught me confidence, showed me the value of diligence and discipline, and, most importantly, empowered me to use my voice. As an educator, I pass along the lessons he gave me; specifically, I use hip‐hop as a vehicle for students to refine and cultivate their voices.
Though my love for hip‐hop is rooted in the sounds of the 1990s and early 2000s, hip‐hop continues to provide the soundtrack to my life. Notably, the significant increase in women rappers in recent years has significantly impacted both hip‐hop and his lovers. What I find to be most exciting about the increase of women in the hip‐hop scene is the way each of them owns their narrative, deliberately and unapologetically pushing back against the male hegemony and patriarchy that undoubtedly exist within and beyond the music industry. Dating back to the women rappers of the 1980s, the reclamation of their bodies, autonomy, and even the very words that men have used to denigrate them is where I find a critical message for leadership learners about the importance of owning your voice and story.

3.3

## Johnnie's Response
My earliest memories of falling in love with hip‐hop include many memories shared with my dad, someone I would also consider a hip‐hop enthusiast, at the local car wash in my hometown. We would visit the car wash frequently; for my dad and myself, the car wash was an “experience.” My appreciation for music, hip‐hop explicitly derives from these moments, as we would listen to Black hip‐hop artists ranging from the 1990s to 2000s, such as Tupac Shakur (2Pac), Nas, Dr. Dre, Kayne West, Lauryn Hill, Missy Elliott, and Ludacris, to name a few. Shaping my appreciation for hip‐hop music as a collection of music that intentionally brings joy, warmth, kindness, and visibility to groups that have traditionally been marginalized and invisible. This led to my discovery of more hip‐hop artists who shaped my teenage years into my early 20s, like Kendrick Lamar, Drake, J. Cole, and more. I would notice with my dad, as we spent a significant amount of time at the car wash (enjoying the hip‐hop music), that hip‐hop music for us during these times was used as a form of healing, growth, development, creativity, and possibilities.
Hip‐hop has guided my role as a leadership educator by shaping my leadership learning praxis, and cultivating my leadership identity, capacity, and efficacy. Hip‐hop has contributed to my leadership educator identity by reminding me of the critical importance of community and collaboration, as hip‐hop has roots in community support and collaboration, which guides my approach as a leadership educator. Additionally, as I use leadership learning spaces as an avenue to promote teamwork and supportive environments that ultimately empower others, with hopes for collective liberation. I love hip‐hop because of the possibilities it offers to artists, musicians, and listeners, similar to possibilities within the field of leadership education.
While each of our responses is different, we acknowledge the parallels and distinctions that hip‐hop has played in our personal lives. Moreover, we acknowledge the influence of how it has led us to understand the concept of leadership.

## Hip‐Hop, Leadership Development, and Education
Hip‐hop music has always functioned as a platform for Latina/o and Black adolescents to express their narratives authentically, form connections with a specific cultural community, and convey their valuable contributions to society. Hip‐hop, as a dialogue art form, has transformed the way communities and groups organize, motivate action, and question social and societal norms. While hip‐hop has been said to organically occur in almost any location, it has been highlighted as taking place in middle schools, high schools (Emdin ; Morrell and Duncan‐Andrade ), and college campuses (Hall and Martin ; Petchauer ).
Classroom environments, for example, are influential places for creativity, designed to facilitate students in generating and developing ideas related to political, cultural, and social issues through dialogue and natural expression. Pushing the boundaries of what we currently know as hip‐hop education, classrooms are the, “…one space where intellectual maturation should be performed but fail to capitalize on the vital cultural practice that is central to hip‐hop culture,” (Rodríguez , 21), which is known as a dialogue. Rodríguez ( ) highlighted,
Hip‐hop culture is a dialogue with the world—a dialogue between youth and the world in which they operate daily. This dialogue emerges through music, art, dancing, writing, and political activism. One way to capitalize upon the possibilities associated with dialoguing is to deliberately use the high school and university classroom as space for young people to dialogue with one another. (p. 21)

The word leadership has seen the rise and fall of empires, civil rights movements, and leadership classroom spaces. Today, higher education is under pressure to provide leaders who can effectively address major societal issues (Astin and Astin ). One method of understanding how culture intersects with leadership is with Bertrand Jones and team's (Bertrand Jones et al. ) culturally relevant leadership learning model. The model centers on identity, capacity, and efficacy as the central parts of the theory used to understand how leadership educators can understand how students perceive themselves as leaders.
Additionally, while those three components impact students' self‐understandings, there are five factors that impact their development (compositional diversity, behavioral dimensions, organizational structural dimensions, psychological dimensions, and the historical legacy of inclusion/exclusion). While all five are critical, within the context of working with college students, Chunoo and French () claimed, “The historical legacy of inclusion and exclusion highlights systemic advantages and disadvantages in leadership learning; explicitly naming who it was built to benefit and those for whom it still constrains,” (p. 210).
Hip‐hop originated in response to oppression, and the historical legacy of leadership involves both inclusion and exclusion. It is essential for leadership educators to analyze how historical inclusion and exclusion have influenced the institutions that affect leadership environments (Ford et al. ; Ford and Propst ). Hip‐hop can be a source of bridging and building leadership learning and pedagogy for Black students.
For Black students, the intersections of hip‐hop and leadership are connected to creating campus environments that promote a sense of belonging for Black students, encouraging them to bring their cultural values and identities into the collegiate environment, particularly within the context of leadership learning to challenge exclusionary beliefs, norms, and practices in leadership education. For example, hip‐hop Sulé ( ), highlighted the importance of hip‐hop in Black students’ experiences as hip‐hop is, “…a genre of opposition reflected in language (e.g., rap), art, dance, fashion, music/rhythm, and activism, hip‐hop represents a cultural instrument of self‐expression and community cohesiveness,” (p.183).
Similarly, leadership education is inclusive of curricular and co‐curricular opportunities, leadership training, and positional leadership, each context offers opportunities for students to engage in forms of self‐expression and community connections (Beatty and Guthrie ; Guthrie and Devies ). Therefore, for Black students, hip‐hop and leadership learning, further strengthens their leadership identity, capacity, and efficacy as hip‐hop has served as an opportunity to challenge the status quo and support Black students’ connectedness to institutions and, in this case, leadership education (Beatty and Ford ; Bertrand Jones et al. ; Sulé ).
Hip‐hop culture has been heavily influenced in educational spaces for over three decades for students of color and other marginalized groups (Keith ). Most notably, Hip‐hop has been connected to critical pedagogy (Freire ) and culturally responsive teaching (Ladson‐Billings ). Building on this, Emdin ( ) implored, “…emceeing, b‐boying, deejaying and graffiti must be seen as academic subjects and Hip‐Hop must been seen as the curriculum and the pedagogy,” (p. 484). Keith ( ) added, “…critical hip‐hop pedagogy encourages teachers (primarily in urban classrooms) to reexamine their knowledge of hip‐hop as it intersects with race, class, gender, and sexuality, while analyzing and theorizing how hip‐hop can serve as a tool for social justice,” (p. 373). Hip‐hop, as an art form and type of cultural expression can be used to bridge the gap of how students make meaning of educational spaces. For educators engaging with students on college campuses, much of this is how we must think about how we can infuse both hip‐hop and leadership development for both this and the next generation of college students.

## Meeting Students in the Middle: Building Opportunities to Infuse Hip‐Hop and Leadership Development
5.1

## Jesse's Perspective
Hip‐hop has always been a critical part of how I build connections with students. As a student affairs administrator, hip‐hop became a tool to motivate and empower my students to see the world, not as it is, but as it could be. This frequently enabled me to engage in discussions with students regarding their perceptions of themselves as leaders in a variety of contexts, environments, and situations. I established a program called College Mentality after my first year as a full‐time student affairs administrator. One of the central sessions of the programs were called Cyphers.
Cyphers were used to demonstrate the importance of critical reflection in the leadership development process. Each student was asked to select a song that was connected to their personal experiences, identity, leadership development, or current reality. At the start of the event, each student was asked to share how the song relates to their personal and leadership development. After each student shared a moment of reflection, we would play the song and look for parallels between the students' experiences and themes from the song. Often, this would lead to multiple connections between leadership styles, development, music selections, and topics. Many of these conversations allowed students to pose questions and gain clarity around topics that are unfamiliar to them, such as masculinity, history, sexuality, and race. Through our collective understanding, this space allowed for student center themes that may not have been discussed in their home environments to consider how they could learn and grow in a healthy, inclusive environment.

5.2

## Jason's Perspective
Music welcomes my students to class each day. Whether teaching in‐person or online, I open my class sessions by playing music as they engage in reflection on the topic of the day. Before the semester, I ask students to submit their favorite songs to co‐create a class playlist. This activity not only allows students to connect quickly on common interests, but it also allows each student to hear some of their favorite music as they begin class each day—immediately impacting the vibe of the course. While I strive to incorporate music in subtle and overt ways throughout our time together, I turn to hip‐hop, in particular, to enhance our leadership learning.
Specifically, I ask students to reflect on the question: “Who am I as a student affairs educator?” I encourage students to use music to answer this question by creating their own mixtape. Birthed from hip‐hop culture, the mixtape is a staple practice for emerging artists which serves as their official introduction to the public, detailing who they are as a person and artist. As I engage students who, in most cases, previously served as student leaders but are now redefining their leadership in a professional context, I use hip‐hop and the mixtape assignment as a reflective tool for students to better understand who they are as leaders.
The mixtape assignment provides students with greater insight into their identity. Self‐authorship is an important element of hip‐hop (Baxter Magolda ). Recognizing your brand and the characteristics that best showcase who you want to be are not only critical for hip‐hop artists but for leaders at large. We spend time identifying the qualities that are important to us and then find songs that depict those qualities. I encourage students to lean into their creativity to tell their unique narrative. In this way, students can craft and own their narrative in a way that departs from Eurocentric learning and assessment methods in higher education. I believe in the power of hip‐hop as an adaptable, culturally responsive, and useful tool for leadership research, theory, and praxis.

5.3

## Johnnie's Perspective
In practice, I use hip‐hop and leadership to connect with many Black students (leadership learners) that I get to mentor, teach, and support. Using hip‐hop as a means of connection and community building, offering space for students to collectively contribute to creating classroom norms that include their interests, voices, knowledge, and lived experiences is valuable to setting classroom community expectations and norms. Hip‐hop music and leadership serve as effective and critical components in shaping my pedagogical practices. Hip‐hop music as a genre continues to evolve and experiment with nontraditional practices, which encourages me as a leadership educator to embrace innovative and unconventional ways of teaching to promote culturally relevant teaching practices to meet the needs of the students in my class.
For example, in my role as an instructor for an undergraduate Black student leadership course focused specifically on Black men and their leadership, Black, and masculinity identity development and experiences, I rely on hip‐hop as an opportunity to connect with students and contribute to a welcoming classroom environment. The way that hip‐hop and leadership are connected in this sense is my adoption of what some may consider an unconventional pedagogical practice, which includes creating a classroom community music playlist (typically hip‐hop and R&B) based on the student's favorite artist(s).
During our first session, we engage in a group sharing activity to get students eager about this semester. Students are given a prompt with detailed instructors in preparation for our first class together through a course announcement before the first class, stating, “Please bring an item that represents Black male leadership to you, and we will share as a class and learn about the lens that each of us will be coming into the course with as we will discuss and explore the various possibilities within Black male leadership.” This prompt allows students to share an item that represents Black male leadership to them and gain a general understanding of each other's worldviews and how their lived experiences are not separate from the classroom but a critical and core part of their leadership learning journey. Items in the past have included Hip‐Hop artists, songs, and lyrics, which makes a critical claim for the intersections of Hip‐Hop and leadership.
Additionally, we cover syllabus expectations and introduce the creation of a classroom community music playlist. As someone who appreciates Hip‐Hop music and the ways that Hip‐Hop music supports and affirms my identity as a Black man, I find myself more comfortable and productive when listening to Hip‐Hop music. Therefore, the goal of the classroom community playlist is to set the classroom tone each class session, as the playlist is on to welcome students into class and utilized during independent and group project working times, given the specific assignment as background music to generate ideas and creativity.
The intentionality of a classroom music playlist places Hip‐Hop music as a pivotal piece to our collective leadership learning and experiences and pedagogy and learning regarding Black male leadership. The playlist also serves as a space to belong, where many Black students at historically white institutions sometimes do not have the luxury of feeling a sense of belonging due to structurally racially complex campus environments, allowing the playlist to be a collective space for shared and inspiring new music interest, but it also affirms their cultural identity. Black students need more than a classroom community playlist to feel as if they belong in the classroom; however, implementing a community playlist can be an opportunity to begin integrating more culturally relevant pedagogical tools and ideas into the classroom to promote students' academic success, engagement, development, and belonging.

## Creating Opportunities for the Future: Recommendations and Implications for Hip‐Hop and Leadership Development
There is a pressing need to provide more emphasis on the creation of opportunities and environments that foster leadership development specifically for Black students in higher education. Although instances of incorporating hip‐hop culture and leadership into educational settings exist, it is essential to ensure that both the theoretical framework and practical implementations align. Akom ( ) asserted,
Hip‐hop is an area where we might see theory and practice coming together … where we might see an attempt to develop innovative approaches to using Hip‐hop as a method for organizing African American youth around issues. (p. 35)

To contribute to the important conversation on hip‐hop, leadership, theory, and practice, we present the following suggestions and questions for educators to ponder while educating the future generation of leaders.
6.1

## Incorporate Hip‐Hop Pedagogy in Curriculum and Co‐Curriculum Designs
Hall ( ) emphasized how hip‐hop culture and music are regarded as forms of public pedagogy, recognized for their inherent educational significance. Many educators have described the importance of providing flexibility in crafting programs, services, and courses for student creativity. For example, it is estimated that over 300 courses are offered on hip‐hop in the United States alone with institutions including Berkeley, Yale, New York University, and Columbia (Hall ). By incorporating leadership education into curricular and co‐curricular spaces, we give students permission, and encourage them, to see and think about larger issues.
Yang ( ) highlighted how “Through reading the world, young people begin to meaningfully develop strategies for pursuing social justice, and take increasingly public roles in advocating, organizing, educating, and being educated by our communities in an effort to create positive social change” (p. 1). For instance, hip‐hop, with its emphasis on narratives and storytelling for the purpose of advocating social change and equality, offers a framework for empowering individuals and facilitating the development of leadership skills. By integrating these components into both academic and extracurricular pursuits, students can analyze leadership subjects from a culturally and personally meaningful standpoint to advocate, support, and create socially just spaces. We offer the following questions in reflection on how to create curricular and co‐curricular designs:
How can I integrate elements of hip‐hop culture into my curriculum to make leadership concepts more accessible and relatable for my students?
What specific hip‐hop texts, songs, or cultural practices can I use to highlight themes of leadership and social justice in my teaching?
How can I design campus programs that allow students to explore leadership through the lens of Hip‐hop, encouraging them to connect their personal experiences, narratives, and stories with academic concepts?

6.2

## Create Brave Spaces for Expression and Dialogue
Establishing spaces where students can cultivate expression and positive dialogue is important. Oftentimes, creating brave spaces requires educators to acknowledge the lived experiences and realities of their students and themselves (Rezaei ). It is frequently necessary for educators and students to have an in‐depth awareness of their nuanced and intricate histories and identities while engaging in discussions on inclusion. The struggle for equality has been marked by the Black community's efforts to secure positions of leadership, equal opportunities for leadership, and spaces to nurture a generation of future leaders. Similarly, hip‐hop has served as a catalyst for advocacy, promoting cultural awareness, and providing a platform to express perspectives on oppression in our American and global societies (Ali ). Therefore, it is often necessary to create classrooms, campus programs, and other facilities to effectively address and ensure the safety of those facing challenges.
To drive open and honest conversations about the connections between Hip‐hop and leadership, we encourage educators to cultivate environments that foster bravery and openness. There are five key elements that should be considered:
“Controversy with civility” refers to a situation where different perspectives are acknowledged and respected (Ali ).
“Owning intentions and impacts” refers to the act of students recognizing and discussing situations where a conversation has had an emotional influence on someone else's well‐being (Ali ).
“Challenge by choice” refers to a situation where students are given the freedom to decide whether they want to participate in difficult conversations (Ali ).
“Respect” refers to the demonstration of regard among pupils for each other's fundamental individuality (Ali ).
“No attacks” refers to an agreement among students to refrain from deliberately causing injury to each other (Ali ).

The following five elements are essential for advancing conversations. By employing these methods, educators can utilize both classroom and programmatic environments not just for education, but also for personal development, where students can feel empowered to articulate their thoughts, participate in meaningful discussions, and cultivate abilities to advocate for social change using Hip‐hop. We offer the following questions in reflection on how to create curriculum and co‐curriculum designs:
What strategies can I utilize to establish a safe and inclusive culture in which students feel secure in expressing themselves through Hip‐hop?
How can I promote and encourage open discussions around leadership, and use Hip‐hop as a platform to address both individual and societal problems?
What kinds of events or activities can I organize to promote students' sharing of their leadership experiences and collaborative learning in culturally relevant environments?

## Limitations and Concluding Thoughts
Leadership development and hip‐hop culture have many connections, as demonstrated in this scholarship. We offer our perspectives, positions, and experiences as possibility models. Although we are not experts in our approaches, as we believe educators are always learning, we think that there is promise in using some of these approaches in your practice. There are many complexities as Black educators that could be perceived negatively as hip‐hop could be perceived as negative, which could be a limitation of this work. Furthermore, we wish to acknowledge and highlight the criticism of employing hip‐hop pedagogy in educational environments. Many aspects of hip‐hop culture are perceived as violent, materialistic, and conducive to trauma (Ogbar ). We do not intend to dismiss these claims; nevertheless, similar to many issues that may be viewed negatively in our global society, we hope others recognize both perspectives, as hip‐hop may serve as a conduit and instrument for success for Black students. We urge educators who are interested in these methods to remember that hip‐hop often leads from a place of activism, social justice, and building awareness.
This work serves as a connector between the two disciplines to start conversations on how to support and educate Black students. While Black students are the population and focus, we must also call attention to the fact that these methods could be used to support other marginalized groups of students, as hip‐hop could have implications for their success. This paper emphasizes the strong relationship between leadership development and hip‐hop culture, providing a fresh perspective on successfully involving and empowering Black students. By integrating these two fields of study, educators can establish learning environments that are more holistic and culturally relevant. In the end, this approach empowers Black students to safely and successfully take the driver's seat, showing their true selves, their leadership identity and development, and a profound connection to their culture.
