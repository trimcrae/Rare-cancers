## Abstract

## Objective
Venues where people socialize, such as bars, play a significant role in HIV transmission risk. However, little research has been done that identifies characteristics of high-risk venues, to inform HIV prevention efforts. This study identified clusters of social venues in Uganda and their relationship with HIV vulnerability of young venue workers (15–24 years). We also assessed availability of HIV prevention services at the venues.

## Design and setting
This was a secondary analysis of data from a larger cross-sectional survey of social venues in 25 districts of Uganda.

## Participants
A total of 480 venues that employed young workers were included in this study.

## Analysis
Hierarchical cluster and mixed-effects regression analyses were conducted.

## Results
Four clusters were adopted: cluster 1 represented 127 venues (mainly alcohol-serving sex work venues with a provision of on-site sex); cluster 2 represented 80 venues (mainly alcohol-serving sex work venues without a provision of on-site sex); cluster 3 represented 113 venues (mainly alcohol-serving venues without sex work or on-site sex); and cluster 4 represented 160 venues (non-alcohol-serving venues). We found a positive correlation between venue clusters and HIV vulnerability. Workers who belonged to cluster 1 (alcohol-serving sex work venues with a provision of on-site sex) had a 0.15 increase in HIV vulnerability compared with those in alcohol-serving venues without sex work or a provision of on-site sex (cluster 3) (coefficient: 0.15, 95% CI: 0.030 to 0.287) after adjusting for age, sex and marital status. Thirty-seven per cent of cluster 1 venues reported any HIV prevention services in the past 12 months.

## Conclusion
Alcohol-serving sex work venues with a provision of on-site sex (cluster 1) appeared to reinforce vulnerability to HIV in this study and should be targeted with HIV prevention interventions. Such venues can amplify HIV transmission by facilitating sexual partner coupling and on-site sex.

## STRENGTHS AND LIMITATIONS OF THIS STUDY
This study used national-level data from a large population-based survey to identify clusters of social venues in Uganda and explored associations between venue clusters and HIV vulnerability.
The cluster analysis used in this study allowed the researchers to identify and define patterns between data elements; revealing these patterns (between data points) helped to distinguish and outline structures that might not have been apparent before, but gave significant meaning to the data.
HIV vulnerability indicators are broadly defined to cover a wide range of measures, while this study included only five measures that were in the available dataset (this analysis assessed HIV vulnerability among venue workers who tested HIV negative in the original survey).
The response rate for venue workers in the original study was not reported, which makes the representativeness of the sample unknown.

## Introduction
HIV transmission does not occur in a vacuum; therefore, the context or venue in which people meet sexual partners plays a significant role in HIV transmission risk. Worldwide, people meet new sex partners in venues for social interaction, such as bars, lodges and nightclubs. Venues for social interaction are public places where one can go to relax, have fun, drink alcohol or find a sexual partner. These venues are common hotspots for populations at a higher risk of HIV exposure. An increasing body of literature suggests that venues for social interactions influence HIV transmission or acquisition in two ways. First, these venues may influence the probability of meeting partners with certain risk characteristics. Second, the situational conditions found at different venues for social interaction may influence the social acceptability of risky behaviours such as excess alcohol use, having sex with a new or casual partner, condom use and anal sex, hence increasing HIV or sexually transmitted infection (STI) transmission. A number of studies across different countries have found sexual risk-taking among workers and customers at venues for social interaction. No wonder, venues for social interactions have been highlighted as potential ‘hotspots’ for HIV or STI intervention as they have been shown to be highly associated with the incidence of HIV or STIs and related risky behaviours. 

Recent studies, including a study in Uganda, found that HIV prevalence was highest among workers in social venues, especially the female workers (11%), compared with the general adult population of Uganda (6.2%). For several reasons, workers in venues for social interaction are believed to have an elevated risk of HIV infection. First, these venues employ mainly young people. These young workers are at a stage transitioning from childhood to adulthood, a time for exploring and navigating peer relationships, gender norms, sexuality and economic responsibility yet with less support from families and communities. This transition to working and living independently can lead to early sexual onset. Research indicates that young people who begin sexual activity early are at great risk of engaging in risky behaviours (having multiple partners, engaging in unprotected and intergenerational sexual activity, and experimenting sex with alcohol and other drugs), thereby increasing their chances of contracting STIs including HIV/AIDS. Second, the social context of the work setting of socialising venues puts workers at increased risk of exposure to various risky behaviours such as alcohol consumption and sexual activities including sex work. This, coupled with the poorly paid jobs, may in turn motivate young people who work in venues for social interaction to engage in selling sex to their customers or embark in age-disparate and transactional relationships. This highlights this subgroup as a bridge population for HIV transmission, since their customers, in turn, transmit HIV to their sexual partners, and finally, HIV rates may escalate in the general population.
Previous research, both qualitative and quantitative, has given considerable attention to venues where people meet sexual partners. Studies, including some in Africa, have mapped and characterised these venues, while others have examined sexual behaviours and HIV seroprevalence of workers and customers at venues for social interaction. Relatedly, other studies have assessed availability of HIV preventive interventions at venues for social interaction and provided a case for support for venue-based HIV programmes. However, no previous studies identified clusters of high-risk venues despite evidence showing varying prevalence of risky behaviours and availability of HIV prevention programmes in these venues. Notably, certain aspects of the physical environment of these venues attract different types of customers and workers. This is so because the working environment in these venues is multifaceted and serves multiple functions, including being a place for recreation, fostering socially sanctioned drinking, facilitating social relationships and providing opportunities to meet sex partners. Moreover, physical characteristics or features of these venues such as dim lighting, dark corners and unisex bathrooms may be conducive for sex on-site. Therefore, clustering venues for social interaction by their characteristics or features and linking those clusters to HIV vulnerability of workers may be a worthwhile endeavour for generating insights for HIV risk reduction interventions. A better understanding of venue clusters could provide useful information for allocation of HIV/AIDS resources to high-risk venues to reduce new infections. This study aimed to identify clusters of social venues in Uganda by their characteristics, and to examine the relationship between venue clusters and HIV vulnerability among the young people working in such venues. We also assessed availability of HIV prevention services at the venues.

## Methods

## Design
This secondary data analysis was done using data from a larger population-based survey that used Priorities for Local AIDS Control Efforts (PLACE) methodology to identify, map and characterise social venues in 25 districts in Uganda. The survey also included interviewing and testing (for HIV) individuals who were socialising or working at the venues during a busy time. The primary study was conducted in 2018 and it employed a cross-sectional study design. The present study aimed to: (1) identify clusters of social venues in Uganda, (2) describe their relationship with HIV vulnerability among the young people (15–24 years) working in social venues and (3) assess availability of HIV prevention services at social venues.

## Overview of the primary study (PLACE 2018)
The primary study was conducted in 25 districts of Uganda, namely Mbale, Busia, Tororo, Namayingo and Mayuge in Eastern Uganda; Mukono, and Luwero in Central Uganda; Omoro, Agago, Kole, Apac and Moyo in Northern Uganda; Bunyangabu, Kakumiro, Kyegegwa, and Kyenjojo in Western Uganda; Bukomasimbi, Sembabule, Lwengo, and Kiryandongo in South Western Uganda; Rakia, Isingiro, Kiruhura, and Ntungamo in Southern Uganda; and Kotido in North Eastern Uganda. The study districts were selected purposively based on their quarterly HIV/AIDS programme performance regarding the attainment of the Joint United Nations Programme on HIV and AIDS (UNAIDS) 90,90,90 target. Performance for the quarter that preceded the study was the one considered. The survey employed the PLACE methodology, which consists of five steps within each district. District launch meetings in which district HIV leaders, stakeholders and implementing partners identify subdistrict areas dubbed ‘priority prevention areas’ (PPAs) where contextual factors, including presence of fishing villages, transportation hubs, border crossings or trading centres, provide evidence of contextual factors likely to be associated with elevated HIV risk. In each district, 8–12 PPAs were identified and described.
Within each PPA in each district, systematic interviews with community informants (400) were conducted to generate a comprehensive list of public venues where people go to meet new sexual partners.
Venues meeting specific criteria (sex work on-site, sex occurring on-site, locations known as places where men who have sex with men or people who inject drugs socialise, and venues identified by more than 20 community informants) were defined as high priority; others were lower priority. A stratified random probability sample of 100 venues was selected for a venue visit to map and profile the venues, with oversampling of higher priority venues. A semistructured questionnaire was used to collect data from a venue informant, and it included questions about type, activities on-site, staff characteristics, presence of key populations of HIV and HIV prevention activities on-site, among others.
Venue-based face-to-face interviews and HIV testing were conducted in each district among approximately 600 people aged 15 years and older randomly recruited from a second probability sample of venues selected from among the venues that were visited and found operational. All venue workers were interviewed and tested as well as a random sample of male and female venue patrons with the number of interviews per venue proportional to the size of the venue. A semistructured questionnaire was used to collect data and it included questions about sociodemographic information, sexual behaviour, health-seeking behaviour, and exposure to HIV prevention, care and treatment programmes, including antiretroviral therapy.
District profile reports including two HIV prevention cascades were prepared for each district and used to facilitate HIV prevention planning.

## Sample, recruitment and data collection
In this study, data from 480 socialising venues and 1007 young venue workers (15–24 years) were drawn from the primary PLACE 2018 dataset and analysed. The inclusion criteria for this secondary analysis included data from only venues that employed young people aged 15–24 years. In addition, participants with a positive HIV status were excluded from the analysis because the focus of this study was on vulnerability to primary HIV infections. According to WHO/UNAIDS, HIV risk is defined as the probability that a person may become infected, reinfected or superinfected with HIV due to their individual behaviours; and HIV vulnerability results from a range of factors outside the control of individuals that reduce their ability to avoid the risk (WHO/UNAIDS, 2008). In this paper, HIV vulnerability applies to individuals who are HIV negative. Subsequently, only 1007 out of 1071 young venue workers (15–24 years) in the primary survey (PLACE 2018) were included in this study.

## Variables

## Dependent variable
The dependent variable for this study was ‘HIV vulnerability’ and was defined using self-reported individual social demographic and risky sexual behaviours/characteristics. Vulnerability to HIV is multifaceted and therefore shaped by a range of proximal biological and behavioural factors, as well as more distal social and structural factors, for example, gender inequality and poverty. Other HIV vulnerability indicators mentioned in literature include STIs, and behavioural factors like engaging in transactional sex, having multiple partnerships, engaging in substance abuse and limited condom use. 

Based on earlier validated HIV vulnerability indices such as the girls’ HIV vulnerability index, this study selected a set of five indicators from the primary dataset and created a composite variable. These five were the only known indicators of HIV vulnerability in the primary dataset. The selected indicators included condom use in the past 6 months, number of sexual partners in the past 12 months, history of ulcerative STI symptoms in the past 12 months, education background and alcohol consumption in the past 4 weeks ( ).
 First, the selected variables were transformed into binary variables with 1 representing high-risk category and 0 low risk. Then, HIV vulnerability was determined by generating a summary score from 0 to 1 through calculating the proportion of responses of increased risk among individual sexual activity/characteristic variables. The summary score was later classified into three categories: 1—low (≤0.3); 2—moderate (>0.3 and ≤0.6); and 3—high (>0.6).

## Independent variables

## Venue clusters
We conducted a cluster analysis with 18 venue characteristics (variables) to obtain the venue clusters in this study. The 18 venue characteristics included those relating to type of activities at the venue and type of populations at the venue ( ). These characteristics were chosen because of their importance in describing venue features. The clusters were constructed to reduce the number of venue-level characteristics into categories that represent the predominant groups that exist within. Cluster analysis forms groupings in such a way that units within a group (or cluster) have a higher measure of similarity than units in any other cluster. 

 The agglomerative hierarchical clustering method was used to explore and determine the number of venue clusters in this study. The hierarchical clustering method builds clusters based on hierarchy. We used a ‘bottom-up’ algorithm (agglomerative clustering approach), which treats the observations separately as singleton clusters, and then computes the distance of each pair and merges the most similar clusters until the final optimal clusters are formed. The Ward’s method was used to combine clusters in the agglomerative clustering approach. Unlike the other methods of combining clusters in agglomerative approach, the Ward’s method analyses the variance of clusters instead of measuring the distance directly. We assessed the number of the clusters to be selected via dendrogram and scatterplot. The final number of clusters adopted was determined by the relationship observed between branches of connection in the data. The final cluster solution consisted of four venue clusters. We added the identified clusters as separate variables to the dataset, representing different venue categorisations. The four clusters were each given a meaningful name based on those attributes with the highest mean values in the respective clusters.
To validate the cluster solution, the dataset was divided into two samples and a cluster solution ran on part of the dataset and repeated on another. Both analyses revealed a similar clustering.
Other independent variables for this study included: (1) workers’ characteristics and (2) HIV prevention services on-site (HIV/AIDS/condom posters, condoms visible or for sale at the venue).
Furthermore, sociodemographic characteristics of young workers at the venues including age measured in complete years, sex and marital status were also among the independent variables.

## Data analysis
All statistical analyses were performed using STATA V.15 (College Station, Texas, USA). Venue and individual-level data were weighted based on the multistage sampling design and probability of selection in the primary study. Descriptive and inferential statistics of the sampled venues and workers were computed using survey weight estimates.

## Descriptive analysis
Descriptive statistics including frequency distribution and proportions were used to describe the characteristics of workers stratified by venue clusters and availability of HIV prevention services. Χ2 test was used to examine the difference between categorical variables including venue characteristics and availability of HIV prevention services, sociodemographic characteristics and venue clusters.

## Regression analysis
To investigate the relationship between venue clusters and HIV vulnerability, a multilevel mixed-effects regression model was used with random effects for venue cluster. The multilevel regression analysis approach was chosen because of the data’s nested nature; venue workers are nested in the venues and the venues are nested in venue clusters. Second, our dependent variable, HIV vulnerability, is measured at a lower unit (venue workers), which also calls for multilevel modelling. We used a mixed-effects model (including a cluster variable as a fixed variable and random intercept) because it allowed to estimate the possible effect of the cluster variable on HIV vulnerability as well as adjust for variability or decomposition of the variance into two components, that is, variance due to high-level units (venues) and lower units (workers). Model specification tests were conducted comparing the multilevel mixed-effects models with the conventional linear regression models. Log-likelihood, Akaike Information Criteria and Bayesian Information Criteria were used to select the best models. This association was adjusted for sociodemographic variables including age, sex and marital status (known factors that influence HIV vulnerability). Statistical significance was p<0.05.

## Patient and public involvement
In this study, data were drawn from the primary (PLACE 2018) dataset and analysed. There was no direct patient and public involvement done. However, the primary study used local research teams to collect data and conducted district-level stakeholder engagements for research uptake. Several HIV/AIDS stakeholders both at subnational and national level were engaged in the primary study.

## Results

## Description of social venues

 shows a general description of social venues by their features and availability of HIV prevention services at the venue in the past 12 months. A total of 480 socialising venues that had young workers below 25 years were included in this study; however, for the description of venues by availability of HIV prevention services, only 447 were analysed. The 33 venues excluded had no response on availability of HIV prevention in the past 12 months. Overall, 78.9% of the total (480) venues were entertainment establishments such as bars, disco halls, video halls, etc. The most common type of venues was alcohol establishments. The most common types of activities at the venues were buying and eating food, alcohol consumption, and music and dancing. Thirty-six per cent of the venues reported presence of key populations, that is, sex workers, transgender women and men who inject drugs, while 33.5% reported having female staff who meet sex partners on-site. Majority (54.3%) of the venues reported not having had any HIV prevention services in the past 12 months. The prevention services in question included HIV posters, condom distribution, HIV counselling and testing outreach programmes and HIV prevention education on-site.
 

## Cluster characteristics
A total of 480 venues were included in the cluster analysis. The analysis grouped venues into homogeneous clusters using selected venue characteristics. The analysis resulted in a four-cluster solution. shows the proportion of venues with a certain characteristic within a given cluster.
 About 24% (127 of 480) of the social venues were categorised into cluster 1 (alcohol-serving sex work venues with a provision of on-site sex). These alcohol-serving sex work venues with a provision of on-site sex had multiple activities, in addition to having sex brokers. Workers and customers of alcohol sex work venues with a provision of on-site sex reported to find sexual partners on-site. About 16.6% (80) of the venues were categorised into cluster 2 (mainly alcohol-serving sex work venues without a provision of on-site sex). One hundred thirteen venues (23.5%) were categorised into cluster 3 (alcohol-serving venues without sex work and no provision of on-site sex) and 33.3% (160) were categorised into cluster 4 (mainly non-alcohol-serving social venues). Drinking and soliciting sexual partners were common in alcohol-serving venues (clusters 1, 2 and 3).
Regarding availability of HIV prevention services at the venue, only 37.6% of the alcohol-serving sex work venues with a provision of on-site sex (cluster 1) reported having had any HIV prevention services in the past 12 months. In addition, fewer venues, about 20% in clusters 2, 3 and 4, respectively, reported having had any HIV prevention services in the past 12 months.

## Sociodemographic characteristics of workers (aged 15–24 years) within the venue clusters

 shows sociodemographic and behavioural characteristics of young workers (15–24 years) within the venue clusters. A total of 1007 young people working in socialising venues were included in this study: 69.2% (697) were females and 30.7% (310) were males. These young workers were nested in the 480 venues included in this study.
 Alcohol-serving sex work venues with a provision of sex on-site (cluster 1) constituted 27% (n=273) of all young workers, with the majority (over 70%) aged between 20 and 24 years old. There were relatively more young female workers in such venues compared with the others (76.6%). About 72% of the young workers in alcohol-serving sex work venues with a provision of on-site sex had no or some primary education, and over 80% were not married. Alcohol-serving sex work venues with a provision of on-site sex also had a notable percentage (6.6) of young workers with no education compared with the venues in other clusters.
Alcohol-serving sex work venues without a provision of on-site sex (cluster 2) had 17.1% (n=173) of young workers, with the majority (over 70%) aged between 20 and 24 years old. Venues in this cluster had more young female workers than male workers (71.1% vs 28.9%); 71.6% had no or some education, and 68.2% of the young workers were not married.
Alcohol-serving venues without sex work or on-site sex (cluster 3) had 24.3% (n=245) of young workers, with the majority (over 60%) aged between 20 and 24 years old. This cluster had more young female workers (73.1%); 70.6% of its workers had no or some education, while over 70% of the young workers in this cluster were not married.
Non-alcohol-serving venues (cluster 4) had 31.3% (n=316) of young workers, with the majority (over 70%) aged between 20 and 24 years old. This cluster had relatively more young male workers compared with the other clusters (41.1%). About 72% of all young workers in this cluster had either no or some education, and over 80% were not married.

## Effects of venue clusters on vulnerability to HIV
Background characteristics of venue workers by HIV vulnerability can be found in . The 1007 young venue workers (15–24) included in the analysis were nested within 480 socialising venues. Overall, the burden of HIV vulnerability was very high at 92%, with 43.3% of the young venue workers found to have high HIV vulnerability, and 48.4% moderate vulnerability. For insights on HIV positivity rates within venue clusters, see . An overview of the results of the multilevel linear mixed-effects regression analysis of the association between venue clusters and HIV vulnerability of young venue workers is provided in . A positive and significant association between venue clusters and HIV vulnerability was found at both univariate and multivariate analyses. Age, sex and marital status were not statistically important at both levels of analysis. The p values show that workers who belonged in cluster 1 venues (alcohol-serving sex work venues with a provision of on-site sex) had a 0.15 increase in HIV vulnerability compared with those in cluster 3 venues (alcohol-serving venues without sex work or a provision of on-site sex) (coefficient: 0.15, 95% CI: 0.030 to 0.287) after adjusting for age, sex and marital status. The estimate of the between-venue cluster variance was 0.267, and the residual variation in HIV vulnerability of venue workers after adjusting for three individual factors was 0.156. This implies that 26.7% of the total variance in HIV vulnerability of venue workers can be attributed to venue cluster membership. Individual venue characteristics were also regressed against HIV vulnerability but did not show any significant positive association with HIV vulnerability. ## Discussion
The findings of this study tend to be new to the literature on structural factors affecting HIV risk and vulnerability. As mentioned above, clusters of high-risk venues have never been identified in Uganda, nor have their association with HIV vulnerability done before. Rather, social venues have been uniformly referred to by their venue types and individual features. We found four clusters of socialising venues.
In terms of HIV vulnerability, working at alcohol-serving sex work venues with a provision of on-site sex (cluster 1) was found to be associated with HIV vulnerability among young people working in socialising places compared with any other clusters (2 and 3—alcohol-serving sex work venues without a provision of on-site sex and alcohol-serving venues without sex work or on-site sex, respectively), even after controlling for sociodemographic characteristics (ie, age, sex and marital status). Relatedly, evidence from our supplemental analysis showed big numbers of HIV positive cases among workers at alcohol-serving sex work venues with a provision of on-site sex (cluster 1) and workers at alcohol-serving venues with sex work, no music and no provision of sex on-site (cluster 2), indicative of a high HIV burden in these clusters. Comparably, a similar burden of HIV (estimated at 10%–26% HIV prevalence) among workers at venues with similar characteristics as cluster 1 venues has been documented in a similar setting (Uganda and Tanzania). This high burden of HIV in cluster 1 could be partly because the alcohol-serving sex work venues with a provision of on-site sex had a combination of risk-shaping features such as drinking, presence of sex brokers and sex solicitation. The presence of provisions of on-site sex elevates chances of sex activity on-site and a likelihood of a worker engaging in sex at the venue. Earlier studies in Zimbabwe and South Africa also noted that alcohol-serving establishments amplified HIV transmission risks by providing a place where sex encounters occur. Our findings were also consistent with recent studies that have highlighted the negative health impact of alcohol-serving venues with sex work and on-site sex in Argentina and Brazil. A study by Sanchez et al highlighted that the presence of reserved areas for sex at the venue increased the chance of sexual assault at the venue. Morojele et al
 also noted that alcohol establishments are often sex venues, where back rooms, back corners, and adjacent buildings or shacks offer locations for sex. Similarly, a study conducted by Graham et al in drinking venues also showed that sexual assault was common in drinking venues during dancing. Given these findings, venues for social interaction with a mixture of features like cluster 1 venues (ie, alcohol establishments with sex work, sex brokers and a provision of on-site sex) are high-risk venues and should be prioritised for targeted HIV preventive interventions if maximum reduction in new HIV infections is to be realised. These targeted interventions should address the underlying conditions that facilitate risky behaviours at the venues, and those that create obstacles for the venue workers who may wish to protect themselves against HIV.
In this study, workers and customers of alcohol-serving sex work venues with a provision of on-site sex were reported to find sexual partners on-site. A study in Zimbabwe reported that meeting sex partners in drinking places increased the likelihood of sex after drinking and reduced condom use. Relatedly, these alcohol-serving sex work venues with a provision of on-site sex attracted mostly young female workers with no or minimal education, and who were often not married. These individual characteristics are known indicators of HIV vulnerability. 

Alcohol-serving venues with no sex work or on-site sex (cluster 3), and non-alcohol-serving venues (cluster 4) had fewer activities compared with the two clusters with a sex work feature (clusters 1 and 2). In contrast to the alcohol-serving sex work venues with a provision of on-site sex (cluster 1), alcohol-serving venues with no sex work or on-site sex (cluster 3), and non-alcohol-serving venues (cluster 4) were mostly drinking and eating establishments like restaurants, with minimal sexual interactions. The absence of high-risk features at venues in clusters 3 and 4 offers a plausible explanation for the lower risk status of alcohol-serving venues with no sex work or on-site sex (cluster 3) and non-alcohol-serving venues (cluster 4). On the contrary, a study by Duke et al noted that workers of restaurants had high rates of drinking problem ; moreover, previous studies have suggested relationships between employee drinking patterns and high-risk sexual behaviour. Likewise, workers who are employed in restaurants where no alcohol is served (eg, fast food restaurants) or in establishments with strict policies against drinking on the job (eg, corporate-owned restaurants and franchises) reported high rates of alcohol use. In terms of workers, non-alcohol-serving venues employed more young male workers than female workers. A study by Babirye et al noted similarities and some differences in the sexual behaviours and HIV burden of male and female workers of socialising places in Uganda. Male workers in socialising places were found to have a lesser burden of HIV compared with their female counterparts. 

## Strength and limitations
We used national-level data to identify clusters of socialising places in Uganda and also explored associations between Ugandan socialising venue clusters and HIV vulnerability. Moreover, previous research has not identified clusters of high-risk venues, neither has it linked venue clusters to HIV vulnerability. In this study, we also discussed the combined role of venue features in shaping a risk environment at the venue by stimulating and amplifying risky sexual behaviours.
The strength of the cluster analysis used in this study is that the approach forms groupings in such a way that units within a group (or cluster) have a higher measure of similarity than units in any other cluster. However, the results and interpretation of any cluster analysis depend on several researchers’ choices and assumptions, such as the actual groups that exist, the choice of the variables on which the elements in the groups should be similar, the distance measured, and the clustering procedure and the number of clusters determined. 

The presented clusters describe the study sample population (only venues that had young workers (15–24 years)), obtained from a larger population-based survey, and therefore, cannot be extrapolated to the entire population of venues for social interaction. However, given that venues for social interactions mostly employ young people, these findings remain critical.
There are several limitations in the study. First, HIV vulnerability indicators are broadly defined to cover a wide range of measures; this study included only five measures that were in the secondary dataset. Relatedly, this paper only assessed HIV vulnerability among venue workers who tested HIV negative in the original survey. Second, one dataset was used for cluster analysis in this study; thus, no alternative dataset was used to validate the cluster solutions generated. This was however limited by dividing the current dataset into two and using the other half to validate the cluster solutions. Third, the cross-sectional design of the primary study does not allow conclusions on causality of the associations identified. Fourth, the representativeness of the sample is unknown, which could impact the generalisability of the findings. Lastly, due to the intimate and sensitive nature of the study questionnaire and the stigma associated with risky sexual behaviour and HIV/STI positive serostatus, under-reporting in the primary study was expected.

## Conclusion
In this study, we identified clusters of social venues in Uganda and found that the cluster that had alcohol-serving sex work venues with a provision of on-site sex was associated with vulnerability to HIV among young people working in socialising places. Similarly, evidence from supplemental analyses indicated high HIV positivity in the same cluster. We have showed that the collective risk features of venues in this high-risk cluster negatively influenced vulnerability to HIV among young workers. Our findings are also in line with previous studies that demonstrated the negative health impact of the same venue features as venues in the high-risk cluster in this study, hence supporting targeted HIV prevention efforts. Therefore, future HIV prevention efforts should target venues for social interaction with a combination of features as venues in the high-risk cluster in this study because despite the notable similarities with the other clusters such as drinking and music, these particularly have sex work, sex brokers and a provision of on-site sex, which amplify HIV transmission risks by facilitating coupling and instant sex on-site.
Finally, the authors recommend future research on the relationship between venue clusters and HIV positivity, and also research looking at the social norms within venues in high-risk clusters to facilitate understanding of what shapes HIV risk and vulnerability within venues in high-risk clusters.
