Implementing strong tobacco control policies in Indonesia is a public health priority.
Globally, the tobacco industry (TI) spends billions of dollars each year on tobacco advertising, promotion, and sponsorship (TAPS). It has been demonstrated in the region that exposure to TAPS is associated with an increased smoking initiation and prevalence. The TI often advertises and promotes their products at the point-of-sale (POS). Advertisements at the POS could include print signage such as posters or banners. POS promotions could include price discounts or free tobacco products. The WHO has highlighted how the TI uses POS marketing as a key strategy in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs). Compliance with TAPS bans in LMICs is lower compared to high-income countries although compliance in LMICs has increased. The Global Youth Tobacco Survey (GYTS) Indonesia 2019 measured that 7 out of 10 students aged 13–15 years old noticed TAPS at POS, and Septiono et al. (2022) found that school-aged adolescents in seven cities of Indonesia were exposed to tobacco advertising mainly on the street and in minimarkets. 
A global treaty, the Framework Convention on Tobacco Control (FCTC), includes several articles detailing evidence-based tobacco control strategies that parties to the convention are obligated to implement, including Article 13 which requires each party to comprehensively ban TAPS. Indonesia is not a party to the FCTC and has limited restrictions on TAPS, however, there has been some progress in adopting tobacco control policies to restrict or ban TAPS. The National Law No. 32/2002. restricts tobacco advertising on broadcast media, and Regulation No. 109/2012 restricts tobacco advertising on the outside of retailers, any promotion of tobacco products in the form of free samples, brand stretching on non-tobacco products (such as tobacco company’s logo, images, brand or company name on lighters, T-shirts, hats or other products), and brand placement at individual or institutional events. There is no law or regulation at the national level that restricts tobacco advertisement, including product display, at POS. 
As of 2020, nineteen Indonesian districts/cities have adopted regional smoke-free regulations that include the ban on tobacco product display at POS, including Depok (West Java). Depok, located south of Jakarta, has a population of approximately 2 million people. Depok City Regional Regulation No. 3/2014 bans tobacco product display, advertisement, and promotion at POS and took effect in 2014. This regulation was updated in 2020 (Regulation No. 2/2020) and the Mayor issued a circular in May 2021 that specified that tobacco products are prohibited from being displayed in open/unclosed and transparent cabinets and should not be displayed next to baby’s and children’s products. The city developed technical guidelines for inspectors to enforce the product display ban, as well as updated inspection protocols and the training material for government officers, civil police, and retail managers.
Few regional TAPS bans in Indonesia have been evaluated to assess compliance. , Priyono et al. (2020) observed the compliance with bans on tobacco displays at modern retailers in nearby Bogor City, Indonesia and found high compliance with the ban. This study notably did not include traditional retailers in their sample. However, traditional retailers are commonly found in low- and middle-income countries such as Indonesia. Besides modern retailers such as convenience stores, hypermarkets, and supermarkets, traditional retailers (warungs), which are typically small family run stalls/shops, are the key distribution channels for tobacco in Indonesia. It is estimated that there are millions of warungs in Indonesia. Although estimates vary, it is suggested that the majority of retail spending in Indonesia takes place in warungs, making these very important retail settings, as well as important sources of employment. , 
Another previous compliance study in conducted in Banyuwangi District, Indonesia found that tobacco displays at modern retailers decreased after the ban which may be attributed to better distribution of information about TAPS bans. However, no evidence was collected at traditional retailers. Tobacco marketing may remain high at traditional retailers. Traditional retailers are often not registered or licensed with cities and distributing information to these retailers can be challenging. Given the lack of evidence about compliance at traditional retailers in Indonesia, this study measures changes in compliance with bans on tobacco product display, advertising, and promotion at both traditional and modern retailers in Depok in 2019 and after the updated regulation (and Mayoral circular) in 2021.

## Methods

## Sample
The sample of modern retailers included a census of all venues in Depok. Comprehensive lists of all modern retailers (supermarkets, hypermarkets, convenience stores, and minimarts) were provided to the study team by staff at the city of Depok ( ). All retailers identified that they sold tobacco products (supermarkets/hypermarkets: n = 8; convenience stores/minimarts: n = 186) by the city were visited by the study team during February and March 2019. The city did not have lists of traditional retailers (warungs), so data collectors identified up to 200 venues using a walking protocol. Data collectors were instructed to turn left or right (whichever appeared best) after completing the observations at a modern retailer and walk approximately 200 m in the chosen direction. If no suitable venue was identified, they were to turn in the same direction and proceed, following these recommended distances until a venue was identified. 
 In December 2021, data collectors revisited all 394 retailers that were observed in 2019. Among these, 67 retailers (3 supermarkets/hypermarkets, 11 convenience stores/minimarts, and 53 warungs) were excluded from the analysis because they were closed or had a change in business name, or were no longer selling tobacco products in 2021. During 2021, Depok and the rest of Indonesia were managing the COVID-19 pandemic. For the first week of observations, the city was under level 2 of local restriction which only allowed retailers to open until 5 PM with 75% visitor capacity. It gave the data collectors limited time to go to multiple retailers in a day to conduct the observations. For the second until fourth week of observation, the local government of Depok relaxed the local restriction into level 1 which allowed retailers to open at full visitor capacity until 10 PM. It gave the data collectors more time to go to several retailers in a day to conduct the observations.

## Data Collection
The data collection team consisted of 10 data collectors who were university students in Depok. Training was provided to data collectors before the observation in 2019 and 2021. In February 2019, a 2-day classroom-based training was provided. During the training, data collectors were introduced to Depok’s regulations regarding the bans on tobacco product advertising, promotion, and display at POS. The training also gave a chance for data collectors to practice conducting observations based on the data collection protocol and conduct the walking protocol to locate independent small grocers. In December 2021, a similar hybrid training was provided online via Zoom by the research team with local partners onsite because of the COVID-19 pandemic. In both years, data collectors used the POS observation checklist to conduct the observations at retailers. Data collectors used a smartphone-based data collection application (KoBo Toolbox) and took pictures—including of the outside/front of the retailer to help support finding the venue in the subsequent wave. Data were uploaded daily to a cloud-based server accessible by the research team, who monitored the data daily. Data quality checks were conducted throughout the data collection period by both the team at Udayana University and Institute for Global Tobacco Control at Johns Hopkins Bloomberg School of Public Health, including checking that GPS coordinates were accurate, and checking for inconsistent or missing data. Each data collector was required to self-report COVID-19 symptoms daily, wear N-95 masks and sanitize hands regularly. No staff experienced symptoms associated with COVID-19 during data collection.

## Measures

## Tobacco Product Display
Tobacco products observed at POS included cigarettes, kreteks (clove-mixed tobacco cigarettes), and shisha.
Product display measures included tobacco products visible from the street/outside the store and/or if tobacco products were on display inside the venue. For many traditional retailers there is no true distinction between visible from outside or inside as some are stands or kiosks. Data collectors observed and assessed whether tobacco products were properly concealed in a drawer or fully covered by a curtain/cupboard. Data collectors also recorded if tobacco products were compliant with the Mayor’s circular requiring tobacco products not be displayed in ways that target minors; it was recorded if tobacco products were displayed within 30 cm of candy, ice cream or snacks and/or at a minor’s eye level (1 m above the floor).

## Advertising
Data collectors noted the presence of tobacco product advertisements such as posters, banners, flyers, shelf liners, backlit displays, or 3D displays that included a tobacco brand or logo. This was observed outside and inside retailers. It was also noted if tobacco advertisements used cartoon characters.

## Promotion
Data collectors noted the presence of tobacco promotions including price discounts, coupons, buy-one-get-one free offers or gifts of non-tobacco products with the purchase of a tobacco product, social media channel promotions (such as Facebook or Instagram), and brand and reverse brand stretching (such as tobacco product brands on lighters, T-shirts, hats, or other products).

## Statistical Analyses
Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS for Windows, version 27.0 (SPSS Inc, Chicago, IL, USA). Descriptive statistics, including frequency distributions, were used to describe the study sample and variables. McNemar tests were used to test differences in dichotomous variables measuring compliance with banning tobacco advertising and promotion, between 2019 and 2021. Mann–Whitney U tests were used to test the differences in advertising and promotion, and display of tobacco products between modern and traditional retailers. For each analysis, a two-sided p < .05 was used to determine statistical significance. There were no missing data in this study.

## Ethics Statement
This study did not include human subjects.

## Results

## Sample
In total, we observed 327 tobacco retailers during both the 2019 and 2021 data collection periods. Among these, there were 180 modern retailers (175 convenience stores and 5 super/hypermarkets) and 147 traditional retailers.

## Product Display
In 2019, almost all modern retailers (95.6%) had tobacco product visible from outside and/or indoors; this was significantly reduced to 52.2% during the observations in 2021 (p < .001) ( ). More than 90% of traditional retailers had tobacco products visible during both observation times.
 In 2019, one-fifth of modern retailers (20.0%) properly covered their tobacco products inside the retailer. In 2021 this significantly increased to about half of modern retailers (50.6%) (p < .001). During both data collection periods, very few traditional retailers covered their tobacco products (<5%) inside the retailer.
In 2019, 43.3% of modern retailers displayed tobacco products in spaces that target minors (within 30 cm of candy, ice cream or snacks, and/or displayed tobacco products at children’s eye level/within 1 m of the floor); this was decreased to about one-third (34.4%) in 2021 (p = .0145). The majority of traditional retailers displayed tobacco products in spaces that market to minors; 90.5% in 2019 and 83.0% in 2021 (p = .072).

## Advertising
In 2019, more than one-third of modern retailers had some form of advertising (36.1%). It was significantly reduced to 3.9% in 2021 (p < .001). In 2019 most traditional retailers had a form of tobacco advertising (87.1%), and this remained high (87.8%) in the 2021 data collection period. The most prevalent type of direct advertising was print signage in modern and traditional retailers during both data collection periods. The different types of advertising observed are detailed in .

## Promotions
There were no observed tobacco promotions at modern retailers during either data collection period. Among the traditional retailers, promotions were uncommon in 2019 (1.4%) as well as in 2021 (6.8%) although the increase was significant (p < .05).

## Discussion
In Depok, the city government committed to a series of actions to improve the implementation of their tobacco control regulations and the Mayoral circular that banned tobacco product display, advertising, and promotion at POS. This study measured changes in compliance with these regulations at both modern and traditional retailers. Tobacco product displays at modern retailers decreased significantly, but more than half of modern retailers were still displaying tobacco products that were visible from outside and/or inside in 2021 and about half the retailers were not properly covering products with cupboards or curtains inside the retailer. Tobacco advertising was present at over one-third of modern retailers in 2019, and this was reduced significantly to 3.9% in 2021. No tobacco promotions were observed in modern retailers.
An important finding from this study is that tobacco products are commonly displayed at a child’s eye level and/or next to candy, ice cream or snacks. This practice was observed in more than one-third of modern retailers (34.4%) and in most (83.0%) traditional retailers in 2021.
In Depok, the city government committed to a program of actions to improve implementation of their Mayoral decrees which ban tobacco product display, advertising, and promotion at POS in 2019. Actions included the development of a technical guideline and circular for enforcing the product display ban, updates to inspection protocols, coordination and planning meetings with relevant departments and sub-district authorities and the training of government officers, civil police, and retail managers. However, the study observed little improvement in compliance among traditional retailers. The presence of tobacco product displays and tobacco advertising remained similarly high during both observation periods, and tobacco promotions were actually more prevalent (albeit uncommon) in 2021. The reason for the marked difference between modern and traditional retailers is unclear. The changes in policy may not have been properly communicated to traditional retailers, and/or inspection efforts were not equal between these two types of retailers. Communicating regulatory changes to modern retailers that operate as franchises or chain shops may be easier because those retailers adhere to standardized rules from their central office. Given the number of traditional retailers in the country, and the important contribution they make to the economy, it is vitally important that these businesses improve their compliance with tobacco control regulations. Other studies examining TAPS bans in the region have highlighted the importance of political commitment for tobacco control policies. Examples in Bogor city and Balanga city in the Philippines suggest that sub-national governments are able to implement strong measures to ban TAPS despite opposition from the TI. 
Outreach and inspections by civil police could raise awareness about the regulation and the penalties for noncompliance, particularly among traditional retailers. Retailers were inspected for violations of the bans, with a particular focus on supermarkets and convenience minimarts, with violations and warnings issued. The Depok regulations specify that tobacco retailers found displaying tobacco advertisements and/or products will receive a formal warning. If retailers are subsequently found to be noncompliant, they could be fined 50 million Rupiah (equivalent to approximately $3500 US). Another factor that contributes to the increased compliance in modern retailers is adoption of a more specific regulation with clear language to control the presence of tobacco advertising, promotion, and product display at POS. The poor compliance in 2019 suggests that tobacco companies and retailers took advantage of the previous regulation’s ambiguity regarding prohibition of explicit tobacco advertisement and product display. More specific regulation provides less room for multiple interpretations and exploitation of loopholes. The results of this study align with a previous longitudinal observational study with two waves examining TAPS compliance at POS in five Russian cities. 
The tobacco industry is known to provide tobacco retailers with financial incentives for displaying tobacco advertisements including print signage. The extent to which this is happening in Depok is unknown; however, tobacco advertising remains an issue particularly for traditional retailers. There are some policy options to address this issue, including banning incentives (eg, the Tobacco Control Act in Quebec, Canada bans incentive programs ) and requiring payments from tobacco industry to retailers be made publicly available (eg, sunshine laws in the United States , ). Creative solutions to help support retailers could include assistance with replacing advertising revenue from non-tobacco industries. Traditional retailers could be supported to comply with the ban on tobacco product display if, for example, they received appropriate curtains/covers or storage by city officials to ensure their tobacco products are not on display, and that this is compliant with the Mayor of Depok Circular Letter, 2021. 
According to GYTS (2019), more than 60% of adolescents in Indonesia were exposed to TAPS at POS and could purchase cigarettes freely despite their age. Septiono et al. (2022) found that school-aged adolescents in seven cities of Indonesia were exposed to tobacco advertising mainly at small grocers. Evidence from our study shows that children are likely targeted by TI and are at risk of tobacco product exposure at traditional retailers, as indicated by about two-thirds of traditional retailers displaying tobacco products at a child’s eye level or next to candy, ice cream, or snacks. Tobacco product display alongside everyday items, such as candy and soft drinks may familiarize youth with tobacco products, reduce awareness of health risks of tobacco use, and increase access to tobacco products. This study underscores the importance of implementing strategies to reduce the quantity and impact of tobacco product advertising and display to protect children in Indonesia. The Indonesian government issued Government Act No 109/2012 prohibiting tobacco sales to children under the age of 18 years, and restricting the design and placement of outdoor tobacco advertisements. However, the prohibition of sales to minors is weakly enforced. This study found that only 22.8% of modern retailers and 10.1% of traditional retailers posted a “no sales to minors” sign in 2021. The enforcement should be strengthened at both modern and traditional retailers to provide protection to children and adolescents in Depok.
This study fills a gap in the research by assessing compliance with bans on tobacco product advertising, promotion, and display at traditional retailers in Indonesia. Trained data collectors observed tobacco product advertising, promotion and display, and the presence of signage prohibiting sales to minors at both traditional and modern retailers in 2019 and 2021. The method allows observing the changes before and after the adoption and implementation of several city-level tobacco advertising, promotion, and display ban regulations. Findings of this study have important policy implications for other cities and jurisdictions in Indonesia and some regions in other LMICs with similar demographic, geographic, and structure for selling and marketing tobacco products. Depok’s efforts on banning tobacco product advertising, promotion, and display at POS can provide evidence for those cities and jurisdictions in LMICs to adopt and implement the bans on TAPS that can help protect the public, especially children and adolescents from the dangerous consequences associated with TAPS and tobacco product use. It is important to note that data collectors visited retailers only once during each data collection period. Tobacco product promotions are often available for brief periods of time, meaning we only observed activities at that time of year. This may account, in part, for why observed tobacco promotions increased in traditional retailers during the 2021 data collection period. COVID-19 was a unique challenge for data collection in 2021. Many businesses closed because of the pandemic. As a result, we were not able to visit all retailers observed in 2019 (83% follow-up rate). How COVID-19 impacted tobacco industry activities is unclear.

## Conclusions
After the enactment of Depok Regional Regulation No. 2/2020 and the distribution of the Mayor of Depok Circular Letter in May 2021, compliance with bans on tobacco product display, advertising, and promotion varied between modern and traditional retailers in Depok, Indonesia. The compliance with tobacco advertising was significantly improved at modern retailers (hyper/supermarkets and convenience stores) from 2019 to 2021; however, it was observed that most modern retailers still have tobacco products on display. Compliance in traditional retailers remained poor. This study observed that tobacco products are often displayed in ways that target children. Improved enforcement efforts could focus on traditional retailers, including regular inspection by civil police, a distribution of uniform covers to ensure that tobacco products are not visible, and incentives for owners of traditional retailers to replace the incentives that they receive from tobacco companies for tobacco advertising.
