## Background
Female sex workers (FSWs) face elevated health risks, including sexually transmitted infections (STIs), mental health challenges, and violence—often exacerbated by stigma and legal constraints in China. Longitudinal research integrating health trajectories, occupational shifts, and social support remains limited but is essential for public health response. This protocol outlines a prospective registry study of FSWs in southern Sichuan, aiming to both monitor longitudinal dynamics and evaluate an adaptive engagement strategy within the registry system.

## Methods
This multi-center, prospective registry will be established in Luzhou, Yibin, Neijiang, and Zigong. FSWs aged 18 and older will be recruited via initial referrals from health institutions/NGOs and subsequent snowball sampling. Upon enrollment and baseline data collection, participants will be randomized (1:1) into one of two groups: (1) Standard Registry Follow-up, involving scheduled contacts (e.g., quarterly brief check-ins, annual comprehensive interviews) focused primarily on data collection; or (2) Adaptive Registry Engagement, incorporating the standard schedule plus proactive, tailored support and contact adjustments based on participant-reported needs or risk indicators identified through registry data (e.g., intensified contact following reported violence, tailored health information messages, facilitated service referrals). Data collection will encompass field epidemiological surveys (demographics, comprehensive health status including psychological scales, social support, occupational experiences, employment, sexual behavior, narrative accounts) and integration of data from existing health systems where feasible. A modular database (Basic Information, Health Records, Narrative Data, Social Networks, Occupational Dynamics) will ensure secure and organized data management with rigorous quality control. Statistical analyses will include descriptive summaries, correlation analyses, time-series modeling, qualitative thematic analysis, mixed-methods integration, path analysis, and comparative analyses between the two engagement groups on outcomes like retention, data completeness, service utilization, and potentially key health indicators.

## Discussion
This study is the first to implement a prospective registry for FSWs in Southwest China and uniquely embeds an evaluation of engagement models. By comparing standard and adaptive follow-up approaches, the study examines whether personalized contact improves registry performance and participant support. Findings will generate critical longitudinal insights and guide future research, interventions, and policies tailored to vulnerable populations, while addressing the contextual challenges of implementation in China.

## Background
Female sex workers (FSWs) globally constitute a population facing significant health vulnerabilities [ , ]. Within the complex social fabric of China, estimates suggest millions of women engage in sex work [ – ], often operating within diverse and marginalized settings [ – ]. The socio-economic transformations ongoing since China’s reform and opening-up have continuously reshaped the landscape of sex work, presenting persistent challenges for public health research and the design of effective interventions [ ].
The health disparities experienced by FSWs are stark. They face substantially elevated risks of acquiring sexually transmitted infections (STIs), including HIV, compared to their peers in the general population [ ]. Documented HIV prevalence among FSWs in various Chinese locales can be significantly higher [ – ], underscoring their potential role as a “bridge population” for transmission dynamics within the broader community [ – ]. Given that sexual transmission, particularly heterosexual contact, is now the primary driver of new HIV infections in China [ , ], effectively addressing STI/HIV prevention among FSWs remains a critical public health priority.
Beyond infectious diseases, the mental health toll on FSWs is profound. Research consistently reveals high rates of depression, anxiety, post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD), and suicidal ideation, frequently linked to occupational stressors, pervasive violence, and economic precarity [ – ]. Experiences of violence, whether from clients or intimate partners, are distressingly common [ , , ] and have been clearly associated with increased vulnerability to HIV infection [ – ]. These health burdens are further compounded by deep-seated social stigma and institutional discrimination, influenced by traditional cultural attitudes and the illegal status of sex work in China [ , ]. This marginalization often translates into social isolation, the necessity of concealing one’s occupation, limited legal protection, and formidable barriers to accessing essential healthcare and social support services [ – ].
Sichuan Province, a major region in southwestern China (Fig. ), presents a unique context characterized by its large population and significant public health considerations [ – ]. The province reports one of the highest HIV incidence rates in the country [ ]. Specifically, the southern region—including the cities of Luzhou, Yibin, Neijiang, and Zigong—faces sustained pressure in HIV prevention and control, partly fueled by high population mobility through these key transportation corridors linking Sichuan with neighboring provinces [ ]. This geographic and epidemiological context highlights the urgent need to understand and mitigate health risks among vulnerable populations like FSWs in this area. 
Registry studies, which systematically gather consistent observational data on specific populations over time, are invaluable for tracking health outcomes and informing policy [ ]. Yet, longitudinal cohort studies focusing specifically on FSWs remain scarce [ ], particularly within China, where cross-sectional designs have predominated [ , – ]. Critically, no dedicated registry database for FSWs has been previously established in China. Creating such a registry offers an unparalleled opportunity to dynamically monitor the health status, occupational pathways, and social environments of FSWs over the long term. By integrating data from diverse sources—direct participant surveys and existing health system records—this study aims to overcome the limitations of data fragmentation and temporal shallowness often seen in previous research, thereby establishing a rich, standardized platform for in-depth analysis. Furthermore, this protocol incorporates an innovative element by exploring how the registry’s longitudinal contact points can be utilized more effectively through adaptive engagement strategies.
This protocol details the methodology for establishing and utilizing a multi-center registry for FSWs in southern Sichuan, while simultaneously evaluating two distinct participant engagement approaches. The primary goal remains the creation of this sustainable, long-term data resource. Secondary objectives include: (1) characterizing the demographics, health profiles, employment conditions, and social support structures of FSWs in the region; (2) dynamically monitoring longitudinal changes in health, living conditions, occupational factors, and healthcare interactions; (3) investigating the complex interplay between social support, occupational risks, mental health, disease burden, and quality of life; (4) assessing the impact of stigma and discrimination; (5) evaluating the effectiveness of a standard versus an adaptive engagement strategy on registry retention, data quality, and potentially participant outcomes; and (6) generating robust evidence to inform future research, public health interventions, and policy.

## Methods

## Study design and setting
This investigation utilizes a prospective, multi-center registry study design, incorporating an embedded comparison of two follow-up engagement strategies. The research will unfold within the urban centers of Luzhou, Yibin, Neijiang, and Zigong in southern Sichuan Province, China (Fig.  ). Participant enrollment is planned to commence in May 2025, with longitudinal follow-up extending over several years.
A schematic overview of the study protocol, including recruitment, group assignment, and follow-up procedures, is presented in Fig.  . 

## Study population and eligibility
The registry will enroll women engaged in sex work within the four designated study cities. Eligibility requires participants to be female, 18 years or older (the legal age of adulthood in China [ ]), and possess full civil capacity for informed consent. They must self-report involvement in sex work for over six months prior to enrollment and be currently active. A minimum residency or work duration of six months in the study area is required to ensure some stability and local context familiarity. Participants must be willing and able to complete a baseline assessment and agree to participate in regular follow-up contacts, providing stable contact information (e.g., phone number, WeChat). Participation is strictly voluntary, confirmed by written informed consent. Exclusion criteria include the presence of severe mental disorders hindering informed consent or interview participation, and current pregnancy or breastfeeding (due to ethical considerations regarding study demands). Withdrawal criteria include participant request, loss to follow-up despite concerted efforts, or intervening life events (e.g., severe illness, long-term incarceration, permanent relocation) precluding further participation.

## Recruitment and randomization
Recruitment will proceed in two stages. Initial participants will be identified and invited through partnerships with local Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC), community health service centers (CHSCs), and relevant non-governmental organizations (NGOs) known to interact with the target population. Following this initial phase, snowball sampling will be employed, wherein enrolled participants (“seeds”) are encouraged (with appropriate incentives like small stipends or essential goods) to refer eligible peers. This dual approach aims to capture a diverse sample, including those less connected to formal services.
After completing the baseline assessment and providing informed consent, participants will be randomly allocated, using a 1:1 ratio, to one of two follow-up groups: Standard Registry Follow-up or Adaptive Registry Engagement. Randomization will likely use a computer-generated sequence, potentially stratified by city to ensure balance within each site.

## Data collection framework
The registry will synthesize data from two primary streams, linked via unique, anonymized participant identifiers.
The first stream involves Field Epidemiological Surveys, conducted at baseline and during follow-up. These surveys will employ structured questionnaires and semi-structured interview guides. Baseline data collection, as outlined in Table  , will be comprehensive, gathering detailed demographic information (age, ethnicity, education, marital status), socioeconomic data (income sources, housing stability), and work history (prior jobs, time in sex work). Health status will be thoroughly assessed through self-reports, basic anthropometrics (height, weight, blood pressure), inquiries into lifestyle factors (exercise), extensive medical history (past diagnoses, treatments, medications, STIs, substance use, vaccinations), and the administration of validated psychological instruments. These include tools like the Loneliness Scale (ULS-8), Generalized Anxiety Disorder 7-item (GAD-7) scale, Patient Health Questionnaire 9-item (PHQ-9) for depression, and the Jenkins Sleep Scale, providing quantitative measures of mental well-being. HIV-specific knowledge, anticipated stigma, and testing behaviors will also be assessed. Occupational experiences will be explored, covering perceived discrimination, workplace safety, experiences of violence, coping mechanisms, and significant negative events. Employment status details will encompass work settings (e.g., venue, street), frequency, client interactions, and career aspirations. Sexual behavior data collection will focus on condom use consistency with different partner types (clients, regular partners), reasons for inconsistent use, contraceptive practices, and reproductive health history. Integral to the surveys, particularly at baseline and key follow-up points, will be the collection of qualitative Narrative Data, capturing participants’ own stories and perspectives on life events, career paths, health challenges, sources of stress, social support, and coping strategies through semi-structured interviewing.

 

The second data stream is the integration of information from Existing Health Data Systems. Subject to participant consent and data access permissions, relevant data from Hospital Electronic Medical Records (EHR), Community Health Service Center records, and potentially aggregated Public Health Authority data (as generally described in Table  ) will be sought and linked. This aims to supplement self-reported data with objective health markers and records of healthcare utilization.

 

Follow-up data collection will track changes across these domains, focusing on Health Dynamics (new diagnoses, symptom changes, health behaviors), Behavioral and Lifestyle Dynamics (shifts in social support, living situation, economic status), and Occupational Dynamics (changes in work patterns, location, safety, income). Narrative data collection will continue, capturing evolving experiences.

## Follow-up engagement strategies
The two randomized groups will differ in their follow-up approach (Table  ): 

Standard Registry Follow-up Group: Participants in this group will adhere to a pre-defined schedule of contacts primarily focused on data collection. This might involve, for example, brief check-ins via phone or WeChat every three months and a more comprehensive face-to-face or video interview annually. While standard information on available services may be provided, the interaction is primarily researcher-driven for data acquisition purposes.
Adaptive Registry Engagement Group: This group will follow the same core schedule as the Standard group but with additional, flexible components. The “adaptive” nature means that the frequency or type of contact, and the information or support offered, can be adjusted based on information gathered during registry contacts or pre-defined triggers. Examples of enhanced engagement elements include:Proactive Check-ins: Brief additional contacts (e.g., via secure message) triggered by reported high-risk situations (e.g., recent violence, lapse in essential medication, significant drop in mental health score).
Tailored Information: Provision of health information (e.g., reminders about STI testing, safer sex negotiation tips, mental health resources) via SMS or WeChat, potentially tailored based on individual risk profiles or expressed interests.
Facilitated Referrals: More active assistance in connecting participants with desired health, legal aid, or social support services identified during registry interactions.
Responsive Scheduling: Flexibility to adjust the timing or modality of scheduled contacts based on participant needs or circumstances, where feasible.The specific triggers and responses for the adaptive strategy will be clearly defined in the operational protocol. The aim is to leverage the registry contact points as opportunities for timely, relevant support and engagement, responsive to the participant’s evolving situation.

## Data management and statistical analysis
A modular database architecture will be employed, comprising core modules for Basic Information, Health Records (including longitudinal health indicators and psychological scores), and Narrative Data, alongside associated modules for Social Networks and Occupational Dynamics.
Stringent data quality control and security measures will be implemented. Standard Operating Procedures (SOPs) will guide all data handling, personnel will receive thorough training, and electronic data capture (EDC) systems with built-in validation checks will be used. Dual data entry for key variables, encrypted storage on secure servers, daily backups, multi-location storage, hierarchical access controls, and rigorous anonymization protocols will safeguard data integrity and participant confidentiality. Regular data cleaning and quality audits will be conducted.
Statistical analysis will encompass several layers. Descriptive statistics will characterize the baseline population and track variable distributions over time. Correlation and regression analyses will explore associations between key variables (social support, occupational factors, health outcomes). Longitudinal modeling (e.g., mixed-effects models, survival analysis where appropriate) will examine trajectories and predictors of change over time. Qualitative thematic analysis will be applied to narrative data to identify recurring themes and patterns in participants’ experiences. Mixed-methods integration will synthesize quantitative and qualitative findings. Crucially, Comparative analyses (e.g., using t-tests, chi-square tests, or regression models incorporating group assignment) will be performed to assess differences between the Standard Follow-up and Adaptive Engagement groups on key process and outcome measures. These may include participant retention rates, data completeness, self-reported satisfaction with study participation, utilization of health and social services, and potentially changes in key health behaviors (e.g., condom use frequency, STI testing uptake) or mental health scores over the study period. Path analysis or structural equation modeling may be used to explore complex causal pathways. Analyses will utilize standard statistical software like SPSS and R.

## Ethics and privacy protection
Ethical oversight is paramount. This protocol has been reviewed and approved by the ethics committee of Southwest Medical University (Approval number: SWMUIRBKS-202303-0001). All research activities will strictly adhere to the Declaration of Helsinki and relevant national and local ethical guidelines.
Informed consent will be obtained in writing from every participant after a thorough explanation of the study aims, procedures (including randomization to follow-up groups), potential risks and benefits, confidentiality safeguards, and their unequivocal right to withdraw at any time without prejudice. Voluntary participation will be stressed. Participants will be offered compensation for their time, such as free health check-ups and access to psychological counseling.
Data collection will occur in private, secure settings. Anonymization through unique coding will be implemented from the point of data entry, with linkage keys stored separately and securely. Encrypted databases, secure servers, and restricted access protocols will protect electronic data. All dissemination will use aggregated or de-identified data. Specific attention will be paid to minimizing any risk of identity exposure, potentially exploring secure virtual communication methods, given the sensitivity surrounding sex work in China. Comprehensive ethics training for all research staff is mandatory.

## Discussion
This study protocol outlines the establishment of a vital prospective registry for FSWs in southern Sichuan, a region facing significant public health challenges. Its primary innovation lies not only in the creation of this longitudinal data resource but also in the embedded experimental comparison of two distinct participant engagement strategies: a standard follow-up model versus an adaptive, responsive engagement approach. This design moves beyond purely passive observation, actively exploring how the infrastructure of a registry study itself can be optimized to enhance participant retention, improve data quality, and potentially serve as a conduit for timely, tailored support.
By contrasting these two approaches, the study aims to generate crucial evidence on the feasibility and effectiveness of more participant-centered methodologies within registry science, particularly for marginalized and hard-to-reach populations. If the adaptive engagement strategy proves superior in terms of retention, data completeness, or participant satisfaction, it could offer a valuable model for future longitudinal research. Furthermore, observing whether this enhanced engagement translates into measurable differences in service utilization or key health indicators, while not the primary outcome of a registry, could provide preliminary insights into the potential benefits of integrating supportive elements directly into research protocols.
The longitudinal nature of the registry, combined with the integration of multi-source data and mixed methodologies, will provide an unprecedentedly rich and dynamic understanding of the complex factors influencing FSWs’ health and well-being over time. This includes the interplay between occupational hazards, economic pressures, social support networks, experiences of stigma and violence, mental health fluctuations, and engagement with health services.
Nonetheless, significant challenges are anticipated. Recruiting and retaining a representative sample of FSWs in the context of illegality and stigma requires substantial effort, cultural sensitivity, and robust trust-building with the community and collaborating organizations. Selection bias remains a potential limitation. Implementing the adaptive engagement strategy effectively requires well-defined triggers, clear response protocols, adequately trained staff, and careful management to ensure consistency and avoid overburdening participants or researchers. The sensitivity of the data necessitates unwavering commitment to ethical principles and state-of-the-art data security. Self-report bias on sensitive behaviors will be addressed through careful interviewing techniques and data triangulation where possible, but remains an inherent limitation. The initial geographic focus means findings must be interpreted with caution regarding broader generalizability.
Despite these challenges, this study holds considerable promise. It will generate unique longitudinal data to illuminate the lived experiences and health trajectories of FSWs in a key region of China. It will provide practical evidence on optimizing engagement strategies in registry-based research with vulnerable populations. Ultimately, the findings are expected to provide a robust evidence base to inform the development of more effective, targeted, and humane public health interventions, social support programs, and policy initiatives designed to improve the health, safety, and well-being of female sex workers.
