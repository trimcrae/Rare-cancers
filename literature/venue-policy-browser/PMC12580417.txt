## Abstract
This study investigated the impact of COVID-19-related disruptions to healthcare systems on engagement with HIV care among people enrolled in treatment at a university hospital’s infectious disease (ID) clinic. Clinical outcome data from January 2019 to December 2020 were analyzed, and in-depth, semi-structured interviews were conducted from March 2021 to October 2021 with 20 participants enrolled in HIV care at the clinic. Informal interviews were conducted with seven people who work directly with clients at the ID clinic and similar settings to inform the refinement of the interview guide and support the triangulation of findings. Bivariate analyses of clinical outcome data were completed for 2019 and 2020, while qualitative data were analyzed using deductive thematic analysis. Demographic characteristics were similar across the 2019 and 2020 clinical cohorts, with median ages of 44.6 (IQR: 32.2, 55.4) and 45.1 (IQR: 32.9, 56.1), respectively. Most of the population identified as Black and cisgender men in both years. There were unexpected improvements in clinical outcomes. Retention rates increased from 72.5% (n = 5355/7373) in 2019 to 73.6% (n = 4982/6769; p < 0.01) in 2020. Additionally, median duration between visits decreased significantly from 98 days (63, 126) in 2019 and 91 (56, 98) days in 2020 (p < 0.01). Qualitative interviews suggest that, during the 2020 stay-at-home period, the clinic’s integrated HIV care model played a significant role in improving clinical outcomes. Participants who engaged actively in the integrated HIV care model were encouraged to advocate for their own health and pursue personal goals and life projects beyond clinical care. Their involvement in HIV care and positive relationships with care teams led to two major outcomes. First, strong ties to the clinic staff fostered participants’ trust in health information and empowered them to actively seek out health knowledge, which together motivated their early adoption of COVID-19 precautions. Paradoxically, adhering to COVID-19 precautions exacerbated their feelings of isolation, anxiety, substance use, and other psychological and physical co-morbidities. Second, participants mitigated these feelings of isolation by maintaining regular HIV care appointments—efforts that were supported by the expanded availability of telehealth during this period. Finally, despite initial negative feelings about telehealth, participants viewed their HIV appointments as valuable opportunities for social connections.
Keywords: COVID-19, HIV/AIDS, Telehealth, Mixed methods design, Retention in care

## Resumen
Este estudio investigó el impacto de las interrupciones relacionadas con la COVID-19 en los sistemas de atención médica en la participación en la atención del VIH entre las personas inscritas en tratamiento en la clínica de enfermedades infecciosas (ID) de un hospital universitario. Se analizaron los datos de resultados clínicos de enero de 2019 a diciembre de 2020 y se realizaron entrevistas semiestructuradas en profundidad desde marzo de 2021 hasta octubre de 2021 con 20 participantes inscritos en la atención del VIH en la clínica. Se llevaron a cabo entrevistas informales con siete personas que trabajan directamente con los clientes en la clínica de DI y entornos similares para informar el refinamiento de la guía de entrevistas y apoyar la triangulación de los hallazgos. Se realizaron análisis bivariados de los datos de resultados clínicos para 2019 y 2020, mientras que los datos cualitativos se analizaron mediante análisis temático deductivo. Las características demográficas fueron similares en las cohortes clínicas de 2019 y 2020, con una mediana de edad de 44,6 (RIC: 32,2, 55,4) y 45,1 (RIC: 32,9, 56,1), respectivamente. La mayoría de la población se identificó como hombres negros y cisgénero en ambos años. Hubo mejoras inesperadas en los resultados clínicos. Las tasas de retención aumentaron de 72,5% (n = 5355/7373) en 2019 a 73,6% (n = 4982/6769; p < 0,01) en 2020, y el promedio de días entre visitas mantenidas fue estadísticamente significativo, con medianas de 98 (63, 126) en 2019 y 91 (56, 98) días en 2020 (p < 0,01). Las entrevistas cualitativas sugieren que, durante el período de confinamiento en casa de 2020, el modelo de atención integrada del VIH de la clínica desempeñó un papel importante en la mejora de los resultados clínicos. Se alentó a los participantes que participaron activamente en el modelo de atención integrada del VIH a abogar por su propia salud y a perseguir metas personales y proyectos de vida más allá de la atención clínica. Su participación en la atención del VIH y las relaciones positivas con los equipos de atención condujeron a dos resultados principales. En primer lugar, los fuertes vínculos con el personal de la clínica fomentaron la confianza de los participantes en la información de salud y los empoderaron para buscar activamente conocimientos sobre salud, lo que en conjunto motivó su adopción temprana de precauciones contra la COVID-19. Paradójicamente, el cumplimiento de las precauciones contra la COVID-19 exacerbó sus sentimientos de aislamiento, ansiedad, consumo de sustancias y otras comorbilidades psicológicas y físicas. En segundo lugar, los participantes mitigaron estos sentimientos de aislamiento manteniendo citas regulares para la atención del VIH, esfuerzos que se vieron respaldados por la mayor disponibilidad de la telemedicina durante este período. Por último, a pesar de los sentimientos generalmente negativos sobre la telesalud, los participantes consideraron sus citas sobre el VIH como oportunidades valiosas para establecer conexiones sociales.

## Introduction
The COVID-19 pandemic affected health not only through direct infection but also by disrupting healthcare systems in ways that impacted the management and outcomes of health conditions. Between 2020 and 2021, healthcare disruptions caused by stay-at-home precautions and the reassignment of healthcare workers to COVID-19 programs disproportionately impacted individuals with chronic illnesses and exposed deep-rooted inequalities in healthcare access and outcomes. , , For people living with HIV (PLWH), the situation posed unique challenges, particularly because medication adherence, essential for managing HIV, depends on consistent engagement with healthcare providers. Furthermore, given earlier concerns that COVID-19 infection could lead to more severe outcomes in people with HIV, providers prioritized ensuring retention in care. , The challenges of retention in care and adherence were exacerbated by structural and social determinants of health that already affect many PLWH, such as housing instability, limited transportation, and stigma. Understanding how PLWH navigated these disruptions is essential for informing resilient, patient-centered care models during public health emergencies.
Examining the effects of stay-at-home precautions on access to healthcare is crucial for understanding how global pandemics affect health systems and their ability to adapt. Telehealth has been in existence for many years and offers a promising option for maintaining patients’ connections to healthcare systems for managing chronic illnesses. , , In one study, telehealth nurse counseling was used to engage PLWH in their antiretroviral therapy (ART) adherence, and demonstrated adherence rates of 95% or above after 6 months of treatment. However, little is known about how this differs among chronic diseases and various populations. .
Our study contributes to the literature on the impact of COVID-19-related healthcare disruptions in several crucial ways. First, while much research on HIV care during the pandemic has primarily focused on clinical and serological data, our study integrates these data with qualitative interviews that explored (1) people’s perceptions of shifts in the delivery of HIV care and (2) their experiences with COVID-19 and related precautions. Second, we adopted an iterative approach to developing our research design and interview guide, engaging HIV care providers during the formative stage, before analyzing the clinical and serological data. This early engagement ensured that our questions were grounded in lived experience and responsive to evolving conditions during the pandemic. It also provided direct insight into the perspectives of HIV healthcare workers, allowing us to triangulate findings from the participant interviews. This mixed-methods and collaborative approach offers a more nuanced understanding of how individuals navigated care during a period of profound disruption. Finally, from a conceptual standpoint, while the consistency of clinic visits typically measures retention in HIV care, our study expands this definition to include the duration between appointments, offering a more comprehensive view of what we refer to as “engagement with care.” Therefore, this study aims to fill a gap in how health systems respond to public health crises and support patients to continue their engagement with HIV care.

## Methods

## Clinical Data
Participants included people enrolled in HIV care at the Washington University in St. Louis Infectious Diseases (WU ID) clinic from January 2019 to December 2020. Eligibility criteria consisted of being at least 18 years of age and having attended one appointment during the project timeframe. Medical record abstraction included age, race, gender, years with HIV, years receiving care at the WU ID clinic, HIV viral load, and retention rate (the number of kept appointments divided by the number of scheduled visits) for clinic appointments.
Descriptive analyses were conducted for continuous and categorical variables. The Kolmogorov-Smirnov test verified the normal distribution of the data. Depending on the data distribution, Chi-square or Fisher’s exact tests were utilized to assess comparisons among categorical groups. The Mann-Whitney U test was applied for pairwise comparisons across the two years of clinical data. Data were analyzed using SAS Version 9.4 for Windows (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA).

## Qualitative Data
We conducted 20 qualitative, in-depth, semi-structured interviews from March 2021 to September 2021, selected from the same WU ID clinic population. Additionally, we held formative interviews with seven HIV care providers who have direct contact with participants to gather their perceptions of shifts in people’s engagement with care; these conversations informed the refinement of our clinic interview guide for participants. One of three trained interviewers recorded participant interviews using a HIPAA-encrypted Zoom platform. The recordings were de-identified and transcribed using Rev. Transcripts were then double-coded using Dedoose. We applied deductive thematic analysis to identify the main themes from the participants, aiming to understand their experiences, practices, and perceptions of engaging with HIV care both before and during the COVID-19 pandemic. The protocol received approval from the Institutional Review Board (#202012169).

## Clinical Data Results
A total of 2,241 participants were analyzed in 2019 (pre-pandemic) and 2,196 in 2020 (during the pandemic), representing the study period (Table  ). Across the two years, sociodemographic characteristics were similar, with median age being 44.6 years (IQR: 32.2, 55.4) and 45.1 years (IQR: 32.9, 56.1), respectively. Most of the population identified as Black men and cisgender men in 2019. Approximately 90% of the clinic population achieved viral suppression, and there was a slight increase to an estimated 92% in 2020 (p = 0.34). Across the two years, retention rates measured by visits significantly increased from 72.5% (n = 5355/7373) to 73.6% (n = 4982/6769; p < 0.01), respectively. In addition, the decrease in average number of days between kept appointments was statistically significant, with 98 days (IQR: 63, 126) in 2019 and 91 days (IQR: 56, 98) in 2020 (p < 0.01).
 

## Qualitative Data Results
A total of 20 participants were interviewed during the study period. The median age was 42.5 years (IQR: 35.0, 54.0). Most of the population identified as Black (n = 14, 70%) and as cisgender men (n = 12, 60%). The majority were employed (n = 9, 45%) and were college graduates or post-college graduates (n = 9, 45%). The median number of years living with HIV was 14 (IQR: 9.5, 24.5), and the median number of years attending the HIV clinic was 12 (IQR: 7.5, 22.5) (Table  ).
 The qualitative analysis examines the experiences of individuals who were already involved in an integrated HIV care model before and during the 2020 stay-at-home period. Interviews show that participants’ strong relationships with their HIV care teams before COVID-19-related healthcare disruptions fostered a sense of agency, defined as the capacity to act independently and make self-directed choices, in managing both their HIV care and broader life goals, ultimately contributing to improved HIV-related health outcomes. Specifically, our data reveal that participants’ distress from adopting stay-at-home precautions was mitigated by an integrated HIV care model that fostered and enabled participants to advocate and be aware of their health and structural vulnerabilities. Participants regularly noted that they did not have difficulty maintaining their HIV care during stay-at-home precautions. Rather, they expressed concerns about managing their comorbidities alongside the demands of non-clinical life projects—namely, the desires, responsibilities, and aspirations that extend beyond their immediate health needs. For participants, this often meant grappling with how to continue care for their non-HIV health conditions, particularly those that did not have an integrated care model but instead treated the ailment in isolation from the person’s broader health context and life circumstances. The following sections delve deeper into the qualitative data, illuminating the nuances behind these patterns.

## Early Adopters of COVID-19 Mitigation Strategies
A significant theme in the interviews was participants’ belief that they had adopted COVID-19 precautions earlier than their friends and family members, whom they perceived as being less likely to heed public health warnings and precautionary measures. One contributing factor was their belief and trust in public health messages about disease transmission, mitigation, and prevention strategies—rooted largely in their positive experiences with HIV care and health-related outcomes. In response to our question about COVID-19 precautions, one participant (a mixed-race gay man) stated,
It’s just a lot of people that I could be exposed to and there’s a level of anxiety with that. It’s just, you know, it’s all these precautions that me, as a person who knows that I’m an anxious depressive and I’m also living with HIV. You know, there’s a level of how much I put myself out there…. I’m just trying to make it.
Our participants felt they were more prepared in reducing the risk of contracting COVID-19 than individuals not living with HIV, precisely because they knew they were at a higher risk of transmission. Thus, our participants’ perceived heightened risk for COVID-19 complications and exposure led them to consider themselves vulnerable agents of transmission. Their early adoption and adherence to precautions resulted from trust in public health messages that their providers had reaffirmed throughout their care.
Participants’ experiences managing complex health needs shaped a second key reason they were early adopters of COVID-19 precautions. When asked how COVID-19-related healthcare disruptions might affect their health, a mid-30s male participant was less concerned about managing his HIV than about his other conditions: “anemia, malnutrition, hypermobility spectrum disorder (HMD), and a couple of other things. But the worst things were like pneumonia, the blocked liver by a blocked bile duct.” Participants’ HIV status not only heightened their sense of vulnerability to COVID-19 but also sharpened their attentiveness to managing comorbidities and anticipating how healthcare disruptions might affect access to non-HIV care. Another participant (a Black cisgender lesbian) explained her motivation for adopting COVID-19 precautions:
Initially, before I had enough information or facts, I was concerned about how much more at risk I would be already living with this condition [HIV] and being compromised…I tend to be more compromised than the average person. My concern is how is this going to impact me? And what precautions, additional precautions, am I going to have to take? Because on top of that I have asthma too.
Similarly, many of our participants lived with comorbidities and chronic illnesses exacerbated by HIV. Conditions ranging from asthma to uncommon diseases like fibromyalgia exacerbated participants’ concerns about COVID-19 transmission. Participants managing multiple comorbidities noted a greater sense of urgency than those with fewer health challenges.
Another key motivation for early adoption of COVID precautions was the responsibility of caregiving—particularly as shaped by gender. Women in our study—especially mothers—reported feeling a stronger sense of obligation to care of others compared to men we interviewed, reflecting broader societal expectations around gender and care. The same Black cisgender lesbian mentioned above is a single mother and describes adopting precautions as an act of both care and love.
And then I just keep thinking about how will my daughter… like if I go down, you know, who’s going to be there? It’s me and her. So, yeah, I definitely felt like I just needed to take some extra precautions. I mean, and what that looked like for me was, like I started doing the gloves thing until I, you know, kind of learned about like, you know, if you don’t keep, you don’t take them off every single time you do something and you just pretty much cross contaminating, you know? So, I did the gloves things, for a while. And then even like the paper mask, like if you don’t keep changing those out, you know, like you have to keep changing them. So then just more education you get better. So then getting the cloth ones and making sure you wash them.
The expectation to perform gendered acts of care motivated some participants to adopt COVID-19 precautions early. While concern for and ensuring the safety of family members was important to all participants, women were more likely to link their health behaviors to caregiving obligations. These findings align with longstanding scholarship on structural gender inequities in the U.S., particularly the disproportionate burden of unpaid caregiving shouldered by women—especially those in under-resourced communities. This unequal distribution of caregiving responsibilities not only shapes individual risk management strategies but also positions women as key—yet often overlooked—agents in public health responses. Further research should explore how the uneven distribution of care work intersects with gender, class, and policy to influence the effectiveness and equity of health interventions during public health crises.

## Telehealth and Retention in HIV Care
Another central theme was the positive impact of HIV telehealth accessibility in allowing people to keep their appointments. While almost all participants preferred in-person appointments and expressed initial hesitancy to using telehealth, we found that telehealth appointments not only offset COVID-19 disruptions to HIV care but also provided an outlet for social contact. The transition to telehealth is often framed as an institutional or public health initiative, yet we found our participants emphasized individual decision-making in changes to HIV care during stay-at-home orders. For example, one participant who was experiencing anxiety stated that the decision to transition to telehealth “was mutual.…It was right when the pandemic started and… I wasn’t feeling good. So, instead of canceling the appointment we just did a telehealth.” Generally, participants reported that although they were not enthusiastic about the transition to telehealth for its lack of in-person interactions, its convenience during stay-at-home precautions allowed them to continue HIV care and their relationship with their provider. One participant stated, “It’s been very convenient. And it’s easier for me since I don’t have a car and have to rely on getting my own transportation.” Like other public health researchers, we anticipated that COVID-19 would have a negative impact on people’s HIV-related outcomes by disrupting healthcare systems and access to care. On the contrary, we found that telehealth, despite initial hesitancy, significantly improved retention in care — measured by shorter intervals between appointments and a higher rate of kept appointments — among participants who were already engaged in HIV care at the WU ID clinic.

## Telehealth as a Loneliness Antidote
Our qualitative data also reveal that PLWHs’ adherence to COVID-19 safety protocols resulted in adverse psychological outcomes, particularly depression and loneliness, and the emerging HIV telehealth infrastructure helped mitigate these effects. While almost all participants preferred in-person appointments and disliked telehealth, these appointments provided opportunities for continued access to healthcare systems and socializing, as stay-at-home precautions greatly limited their interactions. The HIV care team model historically has included not only biomedical care but also psychosocial support. Based on interviews, the embedded sociality of the delivery of HIV care that existed before the COVID-19 stay-at-home precautions was transferred into the virtual meetings with their WU ID provider.
Telehealth visits with their HIV case manager also helped with their psychological health, as it provided an outlet for socialization when in-person appointments were not available. One participant benefited from social interaction to the extent of increasing the number of support groups they were active on: “I go to four support groups, different support groups. And [they] let me do that on Zoom or calling, you know what I’m saying? So I’ll do that weekly.” Telehealth appointments were also crucial in offsetting the growing isolation of this participant and their healthcare provider. Our interviews with HIV care providers not only echoed patients’ evolving views— highlighting an initial skepticism followed by recognition of telehealth’s effectiveness—but also revealed that providers themselves came to view telehealth as a healthy outlet for sociality, as a valuable means of sustaining social connection and mitigating professional isolation.

## Discussion
Our data demonstrate that individuals who were successfully engaged in HIV care, defined by visit retention rates and viral suppression, prior to the 2020 COVID-19-related healthcare disruptions and stay-at-home measures, were early adopters of COVID-19 precautions and telehealth. This is reflected in two key clinical outcomes. First, the retention rate of medical appointments rose from 72.5% in 2019 to 73.6% during the same period in 2020. Second, approximately 90% of the clinic population had viral suppression, which increased to an estimated 92% in 2020. A widespread assumption was that health systems disruptions brought on by COVID-19 mitigation strategies would have a more negative health impact on people with pre-existing immunosuppression. On the contrary, this study found that retention and engagement with HIV care improved during the stay-at-home precautions. Telehealth emerged not only as a viable method for maintaining continuity of care but also as a means of fostering social connection and mitigating loneliness. To deepen our understanding of the clinic data, our team conducted in-depth interviews with participants to explore their perceptions of and experiences with HIV management and engagement with care during the stay-at-home precautions. Informal interviews with HIV care providers played both formative and analytic roles; specifically, they helped shape the development of the participant interview guide and also served to triangulate our findings. This mixed-methods and iterative approach led to our overarching conclusion: the unexpected improvement in HIV clinical outcomes can largely be attributed to the HIV clinic’s integrated care model.
Specifically, the clinic’s integrated care model encompasses biomedical services (e.g., various tests, treatments, and preventive methods) and wraparound or one-stop services, including psychosocial support, addressing comorbidities, and access to financial resources and assistance. The key to this integrated model is the participant-clinic staff relationship, which fosters a system of sociality where participants develop trust with the HIV clinic and its staff, a trust that is compounded with improved HIV outcomes. Further, the concept of sociality challenges the idea of relationships as fixed outcomes or products. Instead, it highlights the ongoing process of building and maintaining social intimacy, shared ethics, and mutual respect and beneficence. , The sociality between participants and the clinic that existed before the COVID-19 pandemic provided comfort and companionship during the isolation period. These relationships facilitated and eased the transition from in-person care to telehealth appointments. Despite the initial resistance to and dislike of telehealth as an appointment venue due to its impersonal nature, participants found these moments of digitally mediated sociality to be a reprieve from isolation, a finding that was echoed in the informal interviews with clinic staff.
Additionally, our findings align with similar studies in urban settings, particularly within tertiary medical academic institutions, that have sought to understand the impact of COVID-19 on service provision and viral suppression among PLWH. We found, in particular, that improved engagement with HIV care and minimal disruptions in care were comparable to those of other studies involving populations that are already successfully in care. , For example, a mixed-methods study demonstrated that patients found routine and urgent visits via telehealth to be an essential and viable option for care during the COVID-19 pandemic. Capturing the perspectives of PLWH offers the first step toward designing resilient health systems. As noted by Meyer et al., the adaptability of HIV care systems during the COVID-19 pandemic, such as strategies like telehealth and targeted outreach, played a critical role in sustaining care engagement and mitigating adverse health outcomes. These insights highlight the importance of flexible, community-informed approaches that can maintain continuity of care and promote health equity during future public health disruptions.
As with any study, there are limitations and strengths to consider. While the study captures longitudinal data at two time points (i.e., before COVID-19 and during the stay-at-home precautions), allowing us to analyze shifts in key clinical outcomes, gathering similar data in the years following the easing of stay-at-home measures would provide a more robust assessment of the adaptability of an integrated HIV care system. Despite these limitations, we conducted qualitative interviews with participants who attended and HIV care providers who worked at the ID clinic, offering in-depth and multifaceted perspectives on the impact of telehealth care during the COVID-19 pandemic on HIV treatment. The mixed-methods design of this study helps triangulate findings, thereby increasing its validity and reliability.

## Conclusion
The data suggests that an integrated HIV care model—one that combines both biomedical care and robust psychosocial support—demonstrates notable adaptability during periods of healthcare disruption, such as pandemics. Rather than viewing clients solely through the lens of clinical metrics, integrated care models treat individuals as whole persons embedded in complex social and structural contexts. This approach not only fosters continuity of care during crises but also emphasizes the value of wraparound services as a core, rather than supplemental, component of chronic disease management.
