In this study, we detail how we embedded the perspectives of those affected by ovarian cancer into a biomarker discovery project. Throughout this process, PPI partners gave practical guidance on prioritizing research areas, developing study materials and contributing as co-authors on both patient information leaflets and this publication. To ensure this involvement was meaningful and inclusive, we prioritized three key strategies. Firstly, the research team placed significant emphasis on flexibility in order to facilitate accessible participation. We offered online and in-person formats, utilised explanatory videos, scheduled around PPI team availability, and held 1-to-1 “catch-up” sessions for new members. This emphasis on flexibility enabled PPI partners to contribute to the project on their own terms and helped to build trust with the research team. Secondly, we came up with ways to value PPI contributions beyond monetary reimbursement. PPI partners were compensated for their time and expenses; however, we also supported personal learning goals through “journal club” videos and interactive sessions with other researchers. These methods of appreciation helped to promote the individual goals of each contributor and ensure that the collaboration was mutually beneficial for both researchers and PPI partners. Finally, and importantly, we acted on the feedback received from our PPI partners and reported what changes we had made to our project. Input from PPI partners informed the selection of liquid biopsy types, widened the breath of the project to include multiple subtypes of ovarian cancer and influenced the development of patient information leaflets. When combined, these three tactics produced an inclusive PPI process that improved the relevance, applicability, and acceptability of the biomarker discovery study. While these principles are broadly applicable across PPI initiatives in general, we provide reflections on their importance in addressing the challenges of PPI in a computational context. In addition to offering a guide for researchers looking to develop more responsive and cooperative PPI research practices, our experience demonstrates the potential for impactful patient partnership in computational biology.

## Plain language summary
Public and Patient Involvement (PPI) means working with patients and members of the public as partners in research, rather than doing research about them, without their input. PPI is becoming more common in health research, but it has not often been used in areas like computational biology, a field that uses computers and data to understand biological problems. In our case, we use computer-based tools to study tiny building blocks in the blood, known as molecules, that might help detect ovarian cancer earlier. In this project, we involved a group of PPI partners (people with personal or family experience of ovarian cancer) throughout a two-year study. They helped guide the scientific project by suggesting research questions to focus on, reviewing patient information materials, and helping to write this paper as co-authors. To make sure the partnership was meaningful and accessible, we took several steps. We made it easy for people to take part by offering online and in-person options, creating short videos to explain scientific ideas, and being flexible with timing. We also supported new members with 1-to-1 sessions to help them catch up with the larger group. We recognised PPI partners not just by covering their time and expenses, but by offering learning opportunities, like short videos explaining scientific papers and interactive sessions with researchers, so they could get more out of the experience. Most importantly, we acted on their suggestions. For example, the PPI team influenced what types of samples that we used to look for clues in the body that could point to ovarian cancer (markers), helped expand the project to include rarer types of ovarian cancer, and shaped the design of patient information leaflets. Overall, involving patients and the public in this way made our research more relevant, understandable, and useful. We hope that in this paper, by sharing what we did and how we did it, we can offer ideas for other researchers looking to build strong, respectful partnerships in projects that involve PPI in general, as well as those that use data and computer-based approaches to study disease.

## Background
PPI is defined as research conducted “with” or “by” patients or members of the public rather than “for” or “about” them [ ]. It differs from participation (taking part in a research study or clinical trial) and engagement (sharing and disseminating knowledge); rather PPI is an active partnership between researchers and those affected by the condition or disease being studied. In health and social care research, PPI has been shown to enhance relevance, design and overall quality [ ], reduce research waste [ ] and ensure that research focuses on issues most important to patients and the public [ ]. Pre-clinical research, defined by Carroll et al., (2022) as basic, fundamental, biomedical, translational or lab-based research, often lacks the direct patient interaction common in health and social care research [ ]. As a result, integrating PPI within pre-clinical research is less well established [ , ], which may reflect the perception that involving PPI in predominantly quantitative and technical fields is more challenging than in qualitative research contexts [ ].
Nonetheless, incorporating the patient voice into pre-clinical research can support mutual learning, improve study quality and efficiency, and increase researcher’s understanding of real-life priorities [ ]. Despite these potential benefits, there is a lack of evidence on how PPI can be effective in highly technical pre-clinical disciplines, such as computational biology.
Computational biology is a field of study that involves using computers to analyse and make sense of biological information. Despite being grounded in human-derived data (such as genetic data and medical records), computational biology can often seem abstract and detached from patients’ lived experiences, as it generally involves complex data analysis, modelling or algorithm development [ ]. However, just like other pre-clinical disciplines, computational biology conducted without PPI input, can run the risk of producing findings with poor practical applicability and limited relevance to end users. PPI can bring a lived-experience perspective of a condition or disease that complements the analytical skills and scientific approaches of researchers, offering insights that would otherwise be inaccessible to research teams [ ].
Although PPI has been documented in other Science, Technology, Engineering, and Maths (STEM) fields, such as lab-based research, genomics policy and biomedical engineering [ – ], computational biology has not received as much attention in this area. This could be a result of assumptions about the intricacy or abstract nature of the field, or it could be the result of a lack of established frameworks for engaging the public in computational or data-driven research. Indeed, previous work has highlighted the need for research examining PPI specifically within computational research contexts [ ].
In this article, we describe how we implemented PPI in an ovarian cancer biomarker discovery project. Currently, there are no sufficiently sensitive and specific biomarkers for early detection of ovarian cancer. The most widely used clinical marker, CA125, misses approximately 50% of early-stage cases [ , ]. Given these limitations, there remains a significant need for the development of more accurate diagnostic tools capable of detecting ovarian cancer at an earlier stage. The underlying scientific project focuses on the computational identification of biomarkers for the early detection of ovarian cancer. Specifically, the research uses computational approaches to analyse publicly available gene expression datasets in order to identify non-coding RNAs that are differentially expressed in ovarian cancer compared to non-cancer controls. Non-coding RNAs represent a relatively new class of biomarker that have shown promise as specific biomarkers in ovarian cancer [ , ]. These candidate biomarkers are prioritised based on their abundance, specificity and potential clinical relevance. The long-term aim of this project is to inform the development of a minimally invasive diagnostic test. The project spans early discovery and validation stages and is inherently iterative, involving repeated cycles of data analysis and refinement. This scientific context is provided to support understanding of the PPI approach described below, rather than to present detailed methodological findings.
In establishing a collaboration with PPI partners, we had two primary aims: first, to ensure that the research was grounded in the priorities and perspective of those affected by ovarian cancer, and second, to build an inclusive and supportive PPI process where contributors felt valued, informed, and able to shape the project in meaningful ways. From the outset, we sought to avoid tokenistic involvement by embedding PPI across all stages of the research, from study design to authorship.
As far as we are aware, this is the first published instance of PPI being used in a computational biology study. The overall aim of this manuscript is to demonstrate that PPI is both practical and beneficial in computational biology, and to add to the expanding body of literature on inclusive research practices. We start by giving an overview of the three tactics that we utilised to help our PPI process succeed, summarizing practical actions taken for each and reflecting on which aspects were particularly important to facilitate PPI in computational biology. We then detail how we established the PPI collaboration, including funding acquisition, researcher training and diversity considerations (expanded upon in Additional File ). This section may be of interest to researchers who are beginning their PPI journey. We then provide a detailed account of our three strategies we feel were essential for meaningful PPI, with reflections and advice for implementation in computational biology fields.

Organizing for flexibility: describes the practicalities of organising workshops, time commitments and behind the scenes work;
Valuing contributions: creative strategies for those who may not have funding or who want to show PPI team their input is valuable beyond payment;
Implementing PPI input into research: includes a detailed description that may be useful for computational researchers interested in concrete examples of how PPI can influence the direction of their research (expanded upon in Additional File ).

Finally, we describe how we evaluated our PPI process using a tool developed by the PPI Ignite Network and anonymized surveys from PPI partners, which may be useful to researchers coming to the end of their PPI journey.
The quotes included throughout the manuscript were written directly by PPI partners themselves. We held a group discussion to agree on the topics contributors wanted to reflect on and share their perspectives about. PPI partners then chose to submit their written reflections either by email or by adding them directly to a shared online document. PPI partners were free to decide the content, length and tone of their reflections, and only material they explicitly agreed to share was included in the manuscript.

## Main text

## Key message: three core strategies for meaningful PPI
At the outset of the PPI collaboration, the research team established common objectives: (i) to make sure that the partnership was inclusive, encouraging, and empowering for PPI partners, and (ii) to incorporate their feedback into every phase of the research process, from study design to dissemination. This strategy created a solid basis for collaboration and allowed participants to influence the research itself as well as the PPI process.
Over the course of the two-year project, we found that three key tactics were crucial in making the PPI process both equitable and impactful: (i) putting inclusive procedures in place to facilitate accessible participation, (ii) valuing PPI contributions beyond financial compensation, and (iii) making sure that PPI input resulted in tangible improvements in the research (Table  ). When combined, we found that these greatly enhanced the quality, acceptability, and applicability of the research while reducing power imbalances between researchers and PPI partners, fostering team trust and cooperation. While these principles are widely applicable in PPI more broadly, our experience highlights specific aspects that were particularly important in a highly technical, iterative and data-intensive research context. The following sections provide a detailed description of each of these tactics and the associated outcomes, with perspectives from each member of our PPI team.
 

## Establishing the PPI collaboration and participant characteristics
PPI is increasingly recognised as beneficial in research globally, with Ireland actively supporting this through the PPI Ignite Network, established in 2017 [ ]. This network provides training resources and peer support for researchers and PPI organisations to build PPI capacity. With help from the PPI Ignite Network, our research team secured funding and planned our PPI activities. Members of the research team completed formal PPI training, which equipped them with the skills necessary to both plan and facilitate workshops. PPI partners were then recruited through national networks, clinical collaborators and local outreach. A detailed account of research team training, funding acquisition, recruitment, and diversity considerations are described in Additional File .
Six out of eight PPI partners responded to an anonymous demographic survey, and a summary of PPI partner characteristics is provided in Table  . PPI partners included five ovarian cancer patients and one caregiver, with ages ranging from 20 to 69 years. Partners were based across the Republic of Ireland and Northern Ireland. None of the respondents reported prior understanding of computational biology before joining the project. No PPI partners withdrew during the study.
 

## Core strategy 1: organising for flexibility
From the outset, the research team prioritised accessibility and flexibility in PPI activities to ensure that partners could participate in a way that best suited their needs. While a flexible approach to organising PPI activities can lead to successful and meaningful PPI across multiple fields, we implemented specific strategies that we would not typically require in less technically complex settings. These included the use of short explanatory videos, visual aids and repeated conceptual explanations to support understanding of computational concepts, as well as 1-to-1 “catch-up” sessions to ensure technical language did not act as a barrier to involvement.
Once individuals expressed interest in joining the group, the lead researcher arranged 1-to-1 phone or online video meetings to facilitate introductions, explain the concept of PPI if individuals were unfamiliar with it, explain the computational nature of the study, and outline planned activities. These conversations helped identify dietary or accessibility needs and whether members preferred to participate online or in person. To further tailor the experience, an online survey was sent out to gather detailed individual preferences, including personal expectations and goals for the PPI collaboration, preferred method of communication (phone or email), preferred format for workshop preparatory material (written document or short explanatory video), confidentiality preferences etc.
One week in advance of each workshop, an agenda was shared by email with PPI partners, along with both a document and short video explaining key topics and theoretical concepts. These videos were essential not only for providing explanations on more complex topics that PPI partners could digest in their own time but also to build confidence in taking part in the workshops. Deirdre (PPI partner) describes how each workshop was structured and how the consistency and clarity helped put members at ease:

One of the best parts of this PPI experience was how well it was organised and how comfortable it felt to take part. Before each meeting, we got an agenda with clear explanations and what was needed from us. Knowing what to expect ahead of time made it much easier to take part with confidence… That kind of clarity made a big difference, especially for those of us who didn’t come from a research background.

Every session began with an icebreaker — something I came to really enjoy as it helped us settle in and connect. Questions were always encouraged and answered with patience and respect. Aideen’s (lead researcher) facilitation was warm, professional, and consistent - this cannot be emphasised enough. We always received a follow-up email afterwards, and feedback was welcomed. This structure made it easy to feel involved and valued.

Not only were these explanatory videos and structured workshops crucial for reducing power imbalances in a more technical field, but this was specifically important in cancer research where brain fog and fatigue are common symptoms of PPI partners [ , ]. As Bridget (PPI partner) notes:
Aideen’s (lead researcher) video updates before each workshop were another standout feature. These short videos recapped previous sessions, outlined what to expect in the upcoming workshop, and reminded us of the project’s long-term goals. For me, they were a refreshing alternative to dense documents—clear, engaging, and much easier to digest, especially on days when reading felt overwhelming. This visual approach supported diverse learning needs and was especially valuable for those of us dealing with brain fog or fatigue.
All documents and videos were shared on an agreed, secure and accessible online platform to ensure that all information was kept in one place. In-person workshops were scheduled on weekends when campus was quieter, ensuring free on-site parking. A suitable room was booked, and catering was arranged, considering dietary needs. During in-person meetings, there were three researchers present (two PhD students and one supervisor). We asked PPI partners via an anonymous survey whether they felt the researcher-to-patient ratio was appropriate. Five out of six respondents answered yes, and one answered that it was “adequate”. One PPI partner noted, “Two researchers plus a supervisor was perfect. Everyone was there to learn together, with only three of them it didn’t feel intimidating, I felt very much at ease.” Another commented, “It always felt very balanced, there were never more researchers than PPI contributors so I think that works well.”
For those unable to attend in-person workshops, a separate online option was made available, either in a group setting or through 1-to-1 sessions. As the project evolved, we expanded our panel to include PPI partners from across the island of Ireland. To accommodate wider participation, we transitioned to a hybrid workshop format, allowing individuals who could not travel to engage in group discussions remotely. Bridget (PPI partner) describes how these flexibility strategies contributed to the accessibility of the workshops:

One can truly appreciate the value of a research project, and the integrity of those running it, when flexibility is built into the process, allowing everyone to participate and contribute when and how they are able. For contributors who are patients managing treatment and health challenges, this kind of flexibility isn’t just considerate, it’s essential.

While in-person meetings offer valuable opportunities to connect, they aren’t always feasible for everyone. A hybrid approach is crucial for inclusivity, ensuring that geographical location or personal circumstances don’t become barriers to involvement. For those of us living across the Island, the option to join remotely makes it possible to stay engaged and have our voices heard.

New members were given the opportunity to join the project at different stages, so the lead researcher held online, 1-to-1 “catch up” workshops to facilitate this. These sessions helped integrate new participants by familiarizing them with previous discussions, ensuring their voices were heard on all project aspects, even if they joined later. They also facilitated a smooth transition into the group setting as the workshop series progressed. Christine (PPI partner) talks about how she joined the group mid-way through the project:

I was apprehensive of joining the PPI team midway through the research project. I was unsure how I would fit into the dynamics of the team as an ‘outsider’ and how valuable my contribution would be.

However, I didn’t need to worry. Aideen (lead researcher) put me at ease by giving me the opportunity through 1-to-1 online video calls and access to documents and articles on our shared online platform. This informed me of the collaboration already undertaken with the PPI team and how my lived experience would fit in going forward.

At my first hybrid workshop, Aideen (lead researcher) facilitated introductions from the other attendees, which put me at ease. She was supportive and her effective communication enabled me to integrate and contribute effectively to the research project… Being part of the research group enabled me to be empowered through knowledge sharing, building social connections with PPI collaborators who had a similar purpose.

For those who could only participate online and were unavailable on weekends (when the larger group met), we conducted individual online workshops and incorporated their feedback into the broader discussions. Paula (PPI partner) shares her experience with this 1-to-1 online format:

With a busy work and life schedule, online works well for me. It minimises commuting, and I find that sharing the screen to explain the medical and scientific content was really useful… The whole cancer journey was eye-opening and nerve wracking for me. I found the 1-to-1 format really useful as I could ask questions without worrying that they’d upset (or bore!) participants. Some of my questions were very basic, some were more detailed and specific to my ovarian cancer subtype and stage.

I did miss out on connecting with other ovarian cancer survivors in this project by only participating online in a 1-to-1 setting, but on the whole, I felt this was the best way for me to contribute from my life experiences, and to learn from the project - it’s a two-way street, and I felt it was hugely important that patient views helped to shape the research direction.

Ultimately, over the course of two years, we covered eight key workshop topics but ran a total of seventeen sessions to accommodate new members and those unable to attend group meetings (Fig.  ). We estimate that each workshop topic required approximately 5–8 h of preparatory work by the research team, including development of explanatory documents or videos, agenda setting, preparation of discussion points, logistical organisation (room booking, catering) and preparation of feedback surveys. Each session lasted approximately two hours. After each workshop, the research team spent an additional 2–3 h of post-workshop reflection and documentation.
 We found that prioritizing flexibility and accessibility in planning our PPI workshops created a friendly and supportive environment, facilitated by accessible explanation of complex topics to reduce power imbalances. PPI partners felt appreciated, comfortable and free to participate in ways that best suited their needs. These inclusive strategies allowed for meaningful engagement throughout the project, fostered trust and ultimately ensured everyone’s viewpoints were taken into account.

## Core strategy 2: valuing contributions with payment and beyond
Incorporating PPI into research offers many benefits, including enhancing relevance of research, improving methodological quality, ensuring the acceptability of research methods and materials, increasing recruitment and retention to clinical trials, developing user-friendly resources and refining dissemination strategies [ – , ]. Given these advantages, it is essential that PPI contributions are properly valued and acknowledged. This not only ensures meaningful engagement but also reduces the risk of tokenism, where PPI becomes a “tick-box” exercise rather than a genuine partnership. Krista (PPI partner) shares her experience from being involved in multiple different PPI projects and how best to value and reward PPI contributions:
For research projects that involve patients and the public, it is vital to do PPI for the right reasons…meaningful involvement from the very start enriches the research and leads to innovative ideas. So here are some things I’ve learned to avoid from a number of previous PPI projects I’ve been involved in: avoid tokenism and superficial engagement, avoid including PPI contributors without listening to or acting on their suggestions, and avoid treating our involvement as a mere formality. Here’s what this project did and you can do instead: embrace true, early, and ongoing collaboration. Get us involved from day one. When patients are part of the project from the very start, our lived experience can help shape the research questions and design. Create an environment where we feel like full members of the team—where our time is respected and our suggestions are seriously considered. Ensure that our contributions aren’t sidelined but are integrated into every stage of the project. Recognize that our time and ideas are valuable, ensure that meetings and updates are designed with our needs in mind so that our involvement is both practical and respectful and keep us in the loop at every milestone so we can truly see how our contributions make a difference.
One of the primary ways to recognize PPI contributions is through reimbursement for time, travel and expenses. PPI partners were reimbursed for their time with vouchers, in accordance with university policies developed by the host university’s PPI Ignite Network. Additionally, they had the option to have travel expenses covered. We asked PPI contributors if being given the option of compensation was important to them via an anonymous survey. Out of six respondents, three reported that it was important, as it “helps to convey the feeling of your time being valued.” One PPI partner reported, “The facilitator did such a great job with the group, I always felt like an integral part. It felt meaningful and worthwhile— compensation was an added bonus.” One PPI partner believed that “compensation wasn’t important. What was important was the opportunity to participate in research in the area actively.” Another PPI partner reported not being aware that there would be remuneration until the end of the project. This approach highlights the crucial role of adequate funding in facilitating meaningful PPI engagement by removing financial barriers and encouraging inclusivity by covering expenses such as compensation, travel costs and catering, although in our case this was not the primary for PPI partners becoming involved.
Beyond financial recognition, transparency and feedback are also crucial in demonstrating the value of PPI input. To ensure that PPI partners see the impact of their involvement, we dedicated time in two out of seven workshops to report back on how their insights shaped the research. Additionally, we created short “research update” videos to keep the team informed about project progress and how their suggestions were being implemented, which we considered essential for maintaining motivation and participation in a project of longer term nature such as our biomarker discovery project. PPI partners are also acknowledged in research presentations, either by name or anonymously, depending on their individual preferences.
At the beginning of our collaboration, we asked PPI team members about their personal goals for the project. A common theme among contributors was a desire to learn more about not only our specific study, but also ovarian cancer research in general. To support this, we developed “journal club” videos that break down scientific papers on ovarian cancer into accessible formats. These videos help PPI members understand complex research findings, interpret figures commonly used in the literature and build their confidence in engaging with scientific material. We also integrated research updates into our workshops. Deirdre (PPI partner) shares her experience with these communications:
Being involved in this PPI project has been a valuable learning experience. Some of us didn’t come from a medical or research background, so at first the language and terminology could feel a bit overwhelming. But the team was really good at breaking things down and using plain English. We were offered a copy of the research paper to read, and Aideen (lead researcher) even made a video explaining it section by section — which made it so much easier to follow and feel confident in what we were discussing. Although I don’t have a scientific background, I gained confidence in reading research papers, giving feedback, and asking questions — thanks to the supportive environment and many communication styles.
In one session, we gathered lay-friendly slides from researchers in the field and presented an overview of their projects. In another workshop, we invited Asia Jordan, a researcher from University College Dublin, to give a lay presentation on her project. Since Asia’s group also engages in PPI, this session introduced our contributors to other PPI opportunities and strengthened connections within the research community. By providing learning opportunities, we aimed to ensure that PPI was a collaboration, where PPI partners are not only helping to shape research, but also gaining knowledge and skills in return.
Similar to organising for flexibility, valuing PPI partners’ time, both with financial compensation and beyond, can be applied to PPI in any discipline. In technical fields such as computational biology, finding creative ways to value partners’ time and input can help to maintain motivation in longer-term projects, while also building confidence in engaging with more complex scientific topics. Ultimately, we recommend early and open engagement with PPI partners, working together to decide what forms of appreciation suit them best, while considering the particular requirements of the research field.

## Core strategy 3: implementing PPI input in research
Most efforts to incorporate PPI into pre-clinical research have focused on dissemination rather than influencing research design and methodology [ , ]. In contrast, PPI input in our project directly informed computational research decisions. Enabling this level of involvement required translating complex molecular and data-driven concepts into accessible formats. To support informed discussion, we developed tailored explanations and visual tools to communicate abstract biological principles. The following overview reflects the type of explanations and visual tools used during our workshops to enable informed discussion and should be understood as part of our PPI facilitation methodology.
Our scientific research focuses on molecules known as non-coding RNAs and their potential role in detecting ovarian cancer at an earlier stage (Fig.  ). To understand this, it helps to think of DNA as a cookbook containing recipes for making proteins. Typically, coding RNAs act like chefs, following these recipes to produce proteins such as keratin in hair, collagen in skin, or haemoglobin in red blood cells. Non-coding RNAs, on the other hand, do not usually make proteins. Instead, they play essential regulatory roles, ensuring that the “kitchen” of the cell runs smoothly. They control where and when proteins are made, influence how other RNAs function, and contribute to various cellular processes.
 The primary goal of our scientific research project is to identify non-coding RNA biomarkers that could help detect ovarian cancer earlier. Biomarkers are molecules found in body fluids, such as blood, that can provide information about the presence of a disease. Our research is focused on non-coding RNAs as potential biomarkers. To identify promising non-coding RNA biomarkers, we analyse gene expression data, which tells us which non-coding RNAs (and coding RNAs) are elevated in ovarian cancer samples. By studying these patterns, we aim to pinpoint non-coding RNAs that are highly abundant and specific to ovarian cancer, bringing us closer to developing a reliable early detection test. Providing this foundational explanation early in the collaboration enabled PPI partners to engage in subsequent discussions on biomarker selection and validation priorities, directly informing the computational decisions described below.
We wanted to incorporate PPI input to make sure this research was not only scientifically robust, but was informed by the lived experiences and priorities of those affected by ovarian cancer. We found that quantifying the impact of PPI using metrics such as the number of suggestions implemented was not straightforward in this project. Changes to the research direction rarely resulted from single, discrete suggestions but rather came about through group discussions throughout the course of our workshops. By embedding our PPI partners from the earliest stages of the research process, we ensured that decisions around study design, sample collection, communication materials and biomarker selection were informed by direct insights into the realities of diagnosis and treatment. This demonstrates that patient perspectives can meaningfully shape even highly technical and data-driven projects when involvement is embedded early and structured deliberately. A detailed description of each workshop and key outcomes are discussed in Additional File and are summarized in Table  .
 Embedding PPI input into our research was not a symbolic gesture; it was a fundamental aspect of our research process. We clearly reported how their insights shaped research decisions, which built trust and respect, demonstrating that PPI was central to the project. This transparency helped PPI partners feel their time and perspectives were genuinely valued, while also strengthening the quality and impact of our work.

## Evaluating the PPI strategy
Reporting and evaluating PPI strategies is essential for understanding how PPI works in different contexts and why [ ]. In cancer research specifically, critical reflection and standardization of reporting can facilitate transparency and assess quality across studies [ ]. In our PPI project, we evaluated whether our process was successful using a Quality Improvement (QI) Assessment tool, and via an anonymous survey to assess PPI partners’ opinions on the approach.
Following the conclusion of the first two workshops, the lead researcher conducted a QI Assessment using a tool created by the PPI Ignite Network at Trinity College Dublin (Fig.  ). This framework outlines the seven core values and principles underlying high quality PPI, including respect, trust, flexibility, empowerment, power sharing, equity and inclusion, collaboration and partnership, and transparency. It also provides guidance on how these principles should be implemented in practice. The assessment involves a self-evaluation, where researchers rank their PPI activities under each principle as “progressing”, “reasonable”, or “excellent”, with detailed examples of best practices.
 After Workshop 6 and prior to writing the publication, the lead researcher repeated the QI self-assessment to determine whether the targeted efforts to improve the quality of the PPI collaboration had tangible improvements (Fig.  ). We noted an improvement of all metrics relating to the seven values and principles from progressing/reasonable to excellent, except for equity and inclusion which improved from progressing to reasonable due to the challenges associated with recruitment discussed in Additional File . This shows the value of assessing the quality of PPI collaborations, identifying areas to improve and making targeted efforts to do so. This improvement in PPI quality also demonstrates the utility of having tools such as the QI self-assessment developed by the PPI Ignite Network.
To evaluate PPI partners’ overall experience of the process and whether they found it successful we sent out an anonymous survey following Workshop 6. Six PPI partners completed the survey. As summarised in Table  , all respondents reported feeling able to share their views freely, and all felt that PPI was meaningfully considered and where appropriate, incorporated into the research. PPI partners consistently reported that the project was explained in accessible, lay terms and that communication formats were accessible. Results from this survey reflected our three key strategies of organising for flexibility, valuing contributions and implementing PPI input, as well as general features of successful PPI (Table  ).
 Overall, the survey findings illustrate that our flexible and open approach created an accessible and genuinely collaborative PPI environment. PPI partners reported not only understanding complex scientific topics but also seeing clear evidence that their input had influenced research decisions. These findings highlight the importance of early and transparent engagement and provide evidence on the implications of embedding PPI within computational and data-driven research.

## Discussion
Implementing PPI in pre-clinical research, including lab-based and computational biology, has often been met with scepticism and concern from both researchers and PPI partners alike. In some basic or pre-clinical studies, PPI members have reported finding it difficult to make meaningful contributions to the design and conduct of lab-based research, with most contributions instead focused on dissemination activities, such as lay summaries and lay posters [ , ]. Similarly, researchers have highlighted challenges in enabling PPI to make a meaningful impact in these types of studies [ ], with some scientists noting they perceived little to no role for PPI within their lab-based projects [ ]. In response to these challenges, we present the first published account of embedding PPI in a computational biology research project. We demonstrate how PPI can impact data-driven areas of cancer research, and present key strategies to design the PPI process to facilitate true involvement. Prioritising flexibility and accessibility created a supportive environment where contributors could engage on their own terms, valuing contributions fostered a two-way partnership grounded in mutual respect and shared learning and finally, actively implementing PPI feedback ensured the research was shaped by the real experiences of those affected by ovarian cancer.
While scepticism remains around the value and feasibility of PPI in technical pre-clinical research, our work illustrates that meaningful involvement can be achieved within computational biology. In doing so, and in line with existing literature, we identified a range of challenges associated with implementing PPI in data-driven research. In cancer research, computational or otherwise, involving patients, particularly those undergoing treatment, can be challenging. Individuals may experience fatigue and cognitive effects such as “brain fog”, which may limit capacity for sustained engagement in typical, inflexible research settings [ , ]. Additionally, in technical fields such as computational biology, PPI contributors have reported difficulties in understanding scientific language, which can lead to misunderstanding [ ] and limit meaningful engagement [ ]. Limited familiarity with specific research projects can also act as a barrier [ ], often due to challenges in communicating complex scientific or technical content between researchers and PPI contributors [ ]. Furthermore, computational biology research, such as this biomarker discovery project, is often characterised by long-term time frames, high levels of complexity and rapid methodological evolution [ , ] which can further complicate sustained and meaningful PPI practices. Recognising these challenges is an important step towards identifying approaches that support effective involvement.
While not specific to computational biology, flexibility was central to our PPI approach. To support flexible involvement, we adopted a range of strategies, including 1-to-1 meetings, sharing preparatory materials in both written and video formats, and offering hybrid modes of participation (Table ). Flexibility is recognised as a core value and principle of PPI, as defined by the PPI Ignite Network. 1-to-1 meetings in particular have been shown to facilitate flexible involvement, especially for individuals living with conditions that may cause fatigue, such as cancer [ , ]. In our study, this emphasis on flexibility was central to the success of the PPI process, aligning with findings from other pre-clinical and technical research disciplines [ ].
Unlike many clinical or qualitative studies, the abstract and iterative nature of computational biology required us to actively design mechanisms that made analytical decisions and their downstream consequences understandable to PPI partners. The research team placed particular emphasis on communicating scientific content in accessible formats, while also supporting PPI partners to build confidence in engaging with scientific materials. Clear, regular communication and relationship building have been reported as essential for meaningful PPI [ , ], and we therefore dedicated ample, individualized time to these activities (Fig. ). One element of this approach involved creation of “journal club” videos, in which scientific papers were broken down into formats that are more accessible for a general audience. These resources were also used as an opportunity to foster curiosity and confidence in STEM topics, an approach that has previously been highlighted as an effective strategy for supporting computational biologists in human data collection, but which is equally applicable to PPI [ ].
The research team also took care to clearly communicate the specific roles of PPI partners, through the development of a Terms of Reference and ongoing dialogue with the research team. Through our workshops, 1-to-1 calls, and regular feedback surveys, we created a structured mechanism to support sustained PPI throughout the project [ ], rather than only meeting at the beginning or end [ ]. Previous work has highlighted that clearly defined roles and opportunities to contribute are particularly important for supporting meaningful PPI in lab-based and pre-clinical research contexts [ ]. In our project, this included asking clearly defined questions to PPI partners, such as those relating to sample types and the design of PILs. The absence of clearly defined roles has been linked to the risk of tokenistic PPI involvement [ ].
Personal contact and ongoing dialogue have also been shown to help PPI partners feel valued [ ]. Consistent with this, we followed up on ideas raised by the PPI team that were not part of the project’s original remit, reflecting findings from other studies in which engaging with individuals with lived experience led to “spin-off” opportunities that researchers would not have identified without embedding PPI in the project [ , , ].
The positive survey findings (Table ) reported in our study reinforce these observations. All respondents reported that they felt able to share their views freely and that they were confident their inputs influenced research decisions. This aligns with wider PPI literature identifying relationship building [ , ], role clarity [ , ] and flexibility [ , , ] as central to meaningful involvement. In contrast to the risks of tokenistic engagement in some pre-clinical contexts [ , , , ], our PPI process demonstrates that deliberate strategies including options to provide anonymous feedback, flexible hybrid formats and clear role definitions can support genuine partnership, even in a highly technical research environment.
Given the slow-moving nature of computational biology research, managing expectations was a further facilitator of effective involvement [ ]. We explicitly outlined the nature of the research, anticipated timelines, and the expected duration of the PPI collaboration within the Terms of References, supporting transparency and trust.
Additional facilitators included training to support effective PPI and the development of mutually beneficial relationships [ ]. The Irish Cancer Society provided training to PPI partners, and researchers availed of the training provided by the PPI Ignite Network. Appropriate training for both researchers and PPI partners has been widely recommended [ ]. This is particularly relevant in computational biology, where researchers often possess high levels of technical expertise but limited experience in participant psychology or managing sensitive or challenging discussions [ ]. As such, supportive infrastructures such as the PPI Ignite Network are essential.
Finally, we recommend involving PPI early in the research process. Within pre-clinical research, PPI is most commonly implemented at early stages to inform research priorities [ , , ], and early involvement has repeatedly been identified as critical to both the success of PPI initiatives and avoidance of tokenistic engagement [ , ] Catherine (PPI partner) talks about the value of taking part early to her:
Catherine

I have found joining the research project as a PPI member to be a hugely rewarding experience as it has given me an opportunity to take an active part at an early stage in valuable research related to my disease.

It has been truly uplifting to be part of such a supportive team and to be able to contribute in a small way through sharing my lived experiences of ovarian cancer. I hope that working with fellow PPI members will help to shape future research outcomes that may help others.

The impact of this work went beyond scientific outputs. PPI partners reported feeling more confident, having a clearer sense of purpose, and having the chance to meaningfully connect with others. In addition to giving them the opportunity to contribute to important research, this partnership gave many people a place to think back on and find strength in their personal cancer experiences. Deirdre and Maeve (PPI partners) share how participation promoted empowerment and a sense of healing, demonstrating that PPI can benefit not only researchers but also those involved in it:
Deirdre
Working alongside others with similar experiences also offered a sense of connection and purpose. It showed me that personal experiences can lead to positive change, and that my contribution could possibly help in improving the care for future patients. This involvement has strengthened my skills, confidence, and belief in the value of patient voices in research.
Maeve

Although I was diagnosed with ovarian cancer almost 20 years ago, I have always wanted to contribute in some way and offer my experience, but I have never known how. When I saw the article on social media from my local cancer care centre to participate in PPI, I felt this was my opportunity to use my experience to help others.

I had never heard of PPI before and I was unsure if my contribution would help as I do not have a medical or science background. However, from the minute I contacted Aideen (lead researcher) I felt at ease and confident that my opinions would be valued and listened to.

During my cancer journey, I have felt very lonely and isolated but being part of this group has really helped me overcome some of the trauma I have endured and has given me a safe space to talk openly about my diagnosis. Knowing that my opinions and experiences have contributed to tangible outputs, such as the Patient Information Leaflet, has really given me a sense of achievement and pride.

I have seen first-hand how participating in PPI can make a difference both to me personally and to valuable cancer research. I really believe that involving people who have been impacted by cancer can lead to invaluable and vital research.

In this work, we show that patient voices can inform better study design, drive more inclusive, relevant science, and direct the course of computational research. The processes we followed here are transferable and increase the evidence base of PPI in quantitative-based methodologies. Other research teams can benefit from the practical, deliberate, and lived experience-based approaches described here to incorporate significant PPI into computational, biomedical, and translational projects. As Krista (PPI partner) concludes:
I believe this project isn’t just about developing a blood test for early stage ovarian cancer—it’s about inspiring a future where patient voices drive innovation. I’m proud of our journey, and I genuinely feel we have contributed to something amazing…. Together, we can shape a research culture that truly values and amplifies our collective insights.
