## Abstract
Youth with mental health needs face barriers in seeking and accessing help, especially in resource-constrained settings in Majority World Countries. Community engagement is thus important for mental health promotion, particularly in addressing stigma. Engagement can be enhanced by involving peer educators and by relating to intergenerational experiences in the sociocultural context of the community. The aim of this study was to explore how intergenerational experiences and perspectives can inform the co-production of youth-led mental health promotion in a Majority World Country, Pakistan. We recruited 11 families (one grandmother, mother and granddaughter in each family) as advisers from two disadvantaged areas of Karachi, and 14 peer educators. Training for peer educators included seminars, experiential activities, three participatory workshops with family advisers and supervision. A sub-sample of family advisers and peer educators attended four focus groups, and peer educators completed reflective diaries following each activity. Data were integrated and subjected to thematic codebook analysis. The three themes related to enabling and hindering factors towards co-production of mental health promotion, incorporation of intergenerational resilience and cascading knowledge to communities. The findings highlighted potential benefits of youth-led mental health promotion that contextualizes intergenerational experiences for those communities. Peer educator roles should be supported by training, and mental health promotion should be integrated within local service systems.
Keywords: youth, mental health, awareness, promotion, intergenerational, Majority World Countries

## Contribution to Health Promotion
Intergenerational perspectives can enrich mental health promotion programmes in MWC resource-constrained settings.
Families should be involved in the co-production of mental health programmes.
Peer educators can relate to their own age group and community, increasing engagement and uptake.
The peer educator role should be supported by training, support and integration within existing service systems.

## BACKGROUND
Despite increasing policy interest globally, youth mental health needs are often unmet, particularly in Majority World Countries (MWCs) ( ). Barriers to meeting youth mental health needs and families seeking support include stigma of mental challenges, risk factors associated with deprivation, lack of culturally sensitive interventions and services and limited infrastructure ( ; ). Mental health promotion is thus essential in improving knowledge, changing attitudes and behaviours and encouraging help-seeking ( ; ).
Whilst faced with socioeconomic and resource challenges, collective cultures in MWC also offer opportunities to enhance youth resilience. The conceptualization of resilience by , p.4) as ‘multilevel processes that systems engage in to obtain better-than-expected outcomes in the face or wake of adversity’ is pertinent to MWC contexts, as this can be strengthened through community engagement by tapping into informal support networks across socioecological levels (young person, peers, family, school, and community— ). Co-production (i.e. iterative process between stakeholders and programme developers) and contextualization (i.e. adaptation to local sociocultural needs) of mental health promotion have the potential to be more impactful when involving local systems, across inter-linked socioecological levels. When marginalized youth were empowered through educator, support or mentor roles, they were shown to hold unique knowledge that could be widely shared with their peer group, by providing safe space, listening and mutual support ( ), as well as utilizing youth-friendly technology ( ). Knowledge transfer related to promotion of agency, adaptive strategies such as cognitive re-appraisal, and self-care ( ; ).
For youth (microsystem) to enhance the reach of mental health promotion, they need to connect with peers, family and community members (mesosystem) by tapping into their own experiences, beliefs and strategies ( ; ). Understanding how different factors can adversely affect ( ) but also protect mental health in young life across generations ( ; ) can, therefore, be a powerful engagement tool between lay ‘promoters’ and communities. Indeed, intergenerational learning has already been applied in health promotion programmes.
Some health promotion programmes target the whole age spectrum. For example, a multi-faceted health promotion programme through creative intergenerational activities in Taiwan demonstrated improvement in young people’s approaches towards ageing and older adults’ sense of spiritual health ( ). In Portugal, older generations (mostly female) reduced children’s critical incidents of genetically hereditary conditions through modelling, encouraging, informing and supporting ( ). Other intergenerational approaches were non-specific or targeted older adults, nevertheless led to positive health outcomes. In such a study, book reading to children was associated with improved wellbeing and social networks for senior volunteers in Japan ( ). In relation to mental health, a mapping review found that mechanisms of intergenerational learning positively influenced the mental health of different age groups, including mutual understanding, addressing social issues such as housing and reducing isolation and exclusion ( ).
The factors involved in shared intergenerational learning and activities can also be understood through broader intergenerational evidence. This largely originates from indigenous collective societies and the positioning of ‘elders as attendants of knowledge for past, present, and future generations’ ( , p.169), which distinguishes them from other western older adult concepts ( ). Oral history and story-telling of trauma and resilience strategies are central to this conduit of knowledge ( ; ). In recent years, this has been extended by using digital technology ( ).
In summary, there is promising evidence that intergenerational learning can inform youth mental health promotion, especially in collectivist cultures and provided by youth themselves. This gap in research and youth mental health promotion programmes informed the rationale for this study.

## METHODS
The aim was to explore how intergenerational experiences and perspectives can inform the co-production of youth-led mental health promotion in an MWC, Pakistan. This aim was addressed by the following questions:
a) Which aspects of intergenerational experience can inform the design of community youth mental health promotion programmes?
b) How can youth facilitators (peer educators) assimilate intergenerational experience in designing mental health promotion programmes?

## Context of communities, family advisers and peer educators
We selected two disadvantaged urban areas in Karachi, Pakistan ( ). Lyari (661,926 population) in South Karachi includes diverse communities, with the Baloch group being the majority. Lyari has faced extreme violence by local gangs but is also known for sports and music. Lines (730,000 population) in East Karachi has a substantial number of migrants from Gilgit Baltistan, who started moving 70 years ago, due to lack of resources and infrastructure. Females living in this area were not allowed to go out for education, and early marriages were common among previous generations. Residents across the age spectrum are not easily accepted by surrounding communities. The area has developed in terms of education and lifestyle.
The study was hosted and co-ordinated by a non-governmental registered social enterprise providing psychosocial support to children, youth and families in the selected communities. Through local networks and community forums, 11 family units were recruited to act as intergenerational family advisers (functions described below), five from Lyari and six from Lines area. Each family unit consisted of one young person (female 14–18 years), one mother (38–45 years) and one grandmother (55–70 years), with seven grandmothers coming from the paternal side.

Peer educators were also recruited from the social enterprise network of community volunteers. Fourteen peer educators participated, seven in each target area. These were all female, to match family advisers for cultural reasons (although in reality, the vast majority of community volunteers were female). Their age range was 17–28 years. Peer educators’ backgrounds included Educational Psychologist, Teacher, Artist, Clinical Psychologists (two), University Graduates (Psychology and Sociology), University students (Psychology—three, Computer Sciences, pre-Medical and High School Graduates (two)).
The role of peer educators was to act as conduits or facilitators between family members, families and communities in capturing intergenerational experiences and perspectives that could inform the design of mental health programmes. To this effect, peer educators provided a safe space for non-judgemental interaction, communication, framing and synthesizing intergenerational perspectives. These role components have been shown to be important in generating knowledge by addressing power imbalances and health inequalities ( ). Facilitation of co-production in this study focused on co-design, which would lead to co-delivery and co-assessment ( ) in the next phase of this research programme. As co-production in a mental health context usually involves knowledge and skills that require lived experience and professional training ( ), such a programme was developed to integrate these two components.

## Peer educators training programme
a) Child and youth mental health seminars
Peer educators attended 12 two-hour sessions on knowledge and skills that equipped them to design and implement mental health promotion workshops. Topics included understanding the role, stigma, emotional literacy, impact of trauma, vulnerability and resilience, dealing with own emotions, self-care, facilitator skills and planning mental health awareness. These were delivered face-to-face (including one visit to a community service) or remotely over 3 months. Training involved theoretical frameworks, case discussions and experiential activities.
b) Family consultation workshops
During the same period, peer educators facilitated three participatory consultation workshops with the family advisers, with the goal of creating mental health promotion events for their communities. ## Exploration
At a neutral venue, participants considered strengths and challenges for each generation. Discussions were interactive and arts-based. In the main intergenerational ‘tree’ activity, roots represented grandmothers, trunks mothers and branches/leaves granddaughters. Although families were asked not to discuss personal issues, several personal stories were shared (for example, in relation to single mothers or taboos about women’s roles). Family units then shared co-created themes.

## Co-production
Peer educators considered mental health promotion topics and delivery approaches, discussed with the research team, then visited the family advisers at community venues and tested key messages and activities with them. Following initial guided meditation, family units imagined, created, drew and discussed their ideal and real homes over time. They delved more into family struggles, support systems and resilience-building.

## Refinement
Peer educators prepared templates for the mental health promotion events. They identified remaining issues to consult on with family advisers. These are mainly related to types of intergenerational activities and whether to include self-care in mental health promotion. Activities were favoured by all participants, especially young people, whilst grandmothers specifically asked for outdoor events. Families extensively discussed past and current stressors and ways of dealing with them.

## Data collection
We captured diverse perspectives across generations, areas and peer educators, using two qualitative methods, focus groups for all participants and reflective diaries for peer educators. Focus groups are useful in involving stakeholders in ‘collective conversations’ in relation to experiences, insights and perspectives ( ). This approach can facilitate dialogue with marginalized groups such as our family advisers, who are rarely given opportunities to contribute. It can encourage self-disclosure in a safe shared space and collaborative discussion, which is pertinent for different generations coming together ( ).
All family units and peer educators were invited to focus groups following the third consultation workshop. One intergenerational and one peer educator focus group was facilitated in each area (n = four focus groups). Of those invited, three grandmothers, five mothers, nine youth and 12 peer educators participated. The topic guide explored participants’ views on how communities should be engaged in relation to youth mental health, how intergenerational experiences could be incorporated, how mental health promotion should be delivered and how knowledge could be sustained. Focus groups were facilitated by a member of the research team. Family advisers focus groups were conducted in Urdu, whilst peer educators used both Urd and English. Focus groups were transcribed and translated to English.

Reflective diaries (also referred to as ‘journals’) were included for peer educators, as these can capture and reinforce the learning process throughout exposure to a learning activity ( ). Reflective diaries promote awareness in anticipating and responding to challenges, thus enhancing knowledge and skills ( ). Peer educators completed a brief reflection in their diary after each training session and family consultation, in relation to one helpful aspect, one challenge and one suggested solution. All diaries were collated into one document for analysis. The study received approval from the Sociology Research Ethics Committee of Leicester University in the UK (reference 39430). All participants aged 16 years and above provided written informed consent. As the legal age of consent was 16 years for the UK and 18 years for Pakistan, parents of young people under 18 years provided written consent, and youth provided additional verbal assent.

## Data analysis
We utilized thematic analysis ( ), and particularly engaged with its codebook form. While there is an overlap in the procedural method of analysis between the original reflexive thematic analysis ( ) and thematic analysis in its codebook form, a codebook theoretically allows for conflation of inductive and deductive coding processes while maintaining the value of analyst collaboration and dialogue ( ). We integrated focus group and reflective diary data for coding. Data were initially coded by one researcher and were revisited by a second coder. One further independent researcher helped resolve any discrepancies.

## RESULTS
Overall, sharing intergenerational trauma was a pre-requisite in considering individual and systemic resilience-building approaches, before formulating new narratives and cascading knowledge. The process of planning and facilitating family consultation workshops and training were important enablers. Themes and subthemes are described below, with supporting excerpts intercepted within the text.

## Theme 1: Process of intergenerational learning
Families’ stories and experiences were central to all three consultation workshops, in providing the foundation material. Participants found it initially difficult to share, within and across families: ‘The hardest part was that we were not comfortable sharing our personal stories the first time, but then the support of peer educators made this easy’ (Grandmother 1). This may have been the first time that family members listened to some of each other’s experiences, whilst also being initially reluctant to share in front of neighbours. Facilitation by peer educators was thus essential in creating a safe space, helping them feel comfortable and drawing wider issues from their stories rather than delving into personal details: ‘The hardest part was to ask my mother to share her story…she was not doing it initially, but then we convinced her to do so’ (Youth 1).
Peer educators were conscious and initially anxious of this challenge. Case scenarios had been anticipated and discussed during the training. This enabled peer educators to divert and re-focus conversations. A common challenge to overcome was the cultural stigma of appearing ‘weak’ by sharing emotions: ‘So, that was because, I think most families in Pakistan have grown up in a way where they don’t communicate about personal matters, like the personal emotional difference that they experience or how they’re hurt or what, because I think it might come across as weak or something like that’ (Peer Educator 5). Being young themselves, they needed to adapt to contributions by older generations, before encouraging them to share with young participants: ‘While talking with the grandparent, she shared some stories related to her marriage, and her struggle, so the daughter sitting there said “this the very first time I am hearing my mother telling this story, I haven’t heard this story ever”. This made me realize that our older generations have a lot to share with us, sometimes we tend to ignore them or sometimes we are not able to understand their struggles’ (Peer educator 12).
Families used their own resources to feel safe and share narratives, through existing or new relationships and trust: ‘I belong to a religious background and I never attended such events, but the presence of my family made it easier’ (Grandmother 2). Despite some reported anxieties about confidentiality, families relied on each other during the workshops: ‘The families that were from my area were known to me, and we all knew each other from childhood, so I find it easier to share’ (Youth 1).
Peer educators noted intergenerational interactions and tensions that inhibited sharing: ‘And the issue that I faced with one of the families that I was talking to, since all three generations were present at once, neither one of them was able to tell the significance of the problems that they were facing’ (Peer Educator 4). Grandmothers were the most talkative, whilst youth found it harder to come forward in the presence of adults. Some mothers appeared inhibited in their mother-in-law’s presence. Peer educators re-balanced intergenerational contributions by equally addressing all family members, showing interest in their unique perspectives, diverting discussions from intrafamilial topics, pointing out shared views and using creative activities (also see below): ‘Focus on what brings all three generations together, instead of what separates them’ (Peer Educator 2 diary); ‘If three generations are together, be mindful of the group dynamics, encourage balanced participation and observe non-verbal communication’ (Peer Educator 8 diary).
Despite the boundaries of consultation events, sharing past experiences could not be disentangled from its emotional effect: ‘The hardest part was to think about the past, because it was a very peaceful time, but I had some painful memories too’ (Mother 3). Recollections evoked both negative and positive emotions, to those who shared and those who listened. Family participants were prompted to share benefits of such experiences, even if challenging. Story-sharing was focused on topics at stake, without delving further into negative emotions, which were deemed unsafe and beyond the scope of the workshops: ‘When others were sharing their stories, I was able to relate with them. I was recalling my memories and my past. This helped me a lot to take out things from my thoughts’ (Grandmother 2).
Managing one’s emotions was reported as difficult by several peer educators. This mainly related to stories of loss or violence, despite having some theoretical preparation through the training: ‘In certain cases, it was hard to not feel affected by some tragic events that the families shared with us. Initially, I found it challenging to separate my personal feelings and my duty as a peer educator’ (Peer Educator 11). Nevertheless, stories of resilience were also source of motivation: ‘Some of them were completely heartbreaking, but at the same time very inspiring knowing that there is good after bad’ (Peer Educator 7 diary). Peer educators were prepared for such scenarios through training, and their emotional responses were reflected upon in booster training and support sessions by the research team.
Relationships between peer educators and family members, as well as with the research team, were built gradually. Having to challenge some pre-conceptions about social differences from families such as in appearance, peer educators positioned themselves as non-judgemental and active listeners, and this was appreciated by the families: ‘Oh, definitely the confidence! The way she [peer educator] was behaving with us…was really lovely. Very loving and friendly too. Just felt like, it came from the heart’ (Mother 4). Relevant scenarios were regularly discussed in training to help peer educators identify and apply strategies to form rapport with the participants: ‘But you know, if you are connecting with somebody, on that level, then you need to just drop some of your defences…’ (Peer Educator 6).
Activities rather than presentations or formal discussions were extensively discussed during the training. These were used for ice-breaking, sharing experiences and piloting before designing mental health promotion events. This was a new experience for many families, especially three generations participating together: ‘I and my daughters usually spend time together and this activity gave us the motivation to be vocal and more involved in each other’s stories’ (Mother 3). By being encouraged to actively participate and contribute, families felt more comfortable and confident in sharing experiences and ideas with each other, as well as with the facilitators: ‘I think the activity we did by making a butterfly, that was a nice activity, because it made the older generation childish once again’ (Peer Educator 3).
A distinct characteristic of the activities was their intergenerational context, by highlighting complementary strengths: ‘It doesn’t have to be an accurate representation. It could just be anything. And if the writing is an issue, your sister or your daughter can write it for you, so that’s not big of an issue. You just need to talk and you just need to move your hands’ (Peer Educator 4). This also applied to forming relationships between family units and peer educators: ‘Art activity helped to build a bridge between families and peer educators, as everyone likes to colour, doodle, or make something…so, this activity provided an easy road to build the connection for sharing the stories’ (Peer Educator 12). During the training, peer educators had been particularly encouraged to combine intergenerational context with creativity, and to include at least one intergenerational activity in each workshop.
When participants engaged in intergenerational activities, they could make links between experiences, emotions and cognitions that might lead to solutions when faced with future adversity: ‘We drew a house and its doors and then locked them, referring to the analogy of locking away our emotions, depression, etc. I really enjoyed it and reflected on my life in its true essence, so that such frustrated thoughts would not enter my life again’ (Youth 5). Peer educators facilitated this process by breaking down activities into steps and reminding participants of what each task represented: ‘We started that session with two of them relaxing and mindful, towards imagination. We asked families to think of houses including walls (support), windows (future), doors (challenges and hurdles), pathways (coping strategies), and at last road (protection). After imagination, we asked them to draw that imaginary house on a chart paper with different colour markers and they write/define their house’s support, protection, challenges/hurdles and how they can overcome them, and what future they see…thereafter, we concluded our session by doing a butterfly activity and offering them insight and motivation about the upcoming future journey of life’ (Peer Educator 1 diary).

## Theme 2: Building resilience
Participants illustrated the more structural and systemic ways in which resilience is important. Sharing intergenerational experiences and stories paved the way for the consideration of positive strategies within and across generations. Parenting was viewed as moderating children’s mental health. This moderation was considered in both negative and positive ways: ‘It used to bother me too much, why doesn’t my mother allow me such hangouts when my friends are permitted to? But now I understand when she opened up to me that she didn’t have many friends and, given the security reasons and other circumstances, she couldn’t’ (Youth 7). Participants also considered intergenerational continuities of rejecting parenting practices without interventions to break the cycle. As with other topics, the training process was important for peer educators to understand their own beliefs or experiences, before facilitating the workshops: ‘While designing the event, few points which came to my mind were mainly keeping the child in the centre and then designing the plan, as all the trauma or fear which the first generations have lived that has been passed on to the second and then it has been passed on to the third generation. As now they are the ones who are going to live their lives, and I believe that parents somewhere ruin these beautiful flowers just by passing on their stressors’ (Peer Educator 12).
Adult participants considered their roles in promoting positive parenting. This appeared to transcend adversities affecting different generations. Peer educators facilitated communication and shared learning by encouraging the positioning of parenting beyond context-specific circumstances: ‘These stories will help mothers and grandmothers to learn how to support the upcoming generation’ (Grandmother 1).
Accounts of resilience, especially from the older generation, fertilized discussion and reflection for youth participants on their coping mechanisms, despite living in materially better conditions: ‘I had this learning that each generation has its challenges, but learning from the challenges is important, so that next time we find the solution easily’ (Mother 3). This was initially met with some surprise, including peer educators, who then seemed to conceptualize resilience independently of socioeconomic adversity, thus position resilience more within an intergenerational context: ‘I felt that the older generation was very happy in their time period, in spite of not having very much of resources, but they had mental peace’ (Peer Educator 12 diary).
Peer educators reported an impact on dealing with difficult situations. They found many family stories inspiring, while the parallel training highlighted ways of dealing with difficult emotions, both in facilitating consultation workshops and in their lives: ‘Listening to the families about their adversities made me feel empathetic towards them and motivated by their strength to endure so much…after listening to their stories, their resilience through all the hardships made me feel very motivated’ (Peer Educator 11).
While framed by the sociopolitical structures of society, women across the focus groups recognized the value of empowerment and knowledge in facilitating the resilience of the collective female body, and saw education, knowledge sharing and intergenerational learning as a core facilitator for this to move towards a system of change. A consistent subtheme of resilience related to women’s, especially grandmothers’, dependence, discrimination and exclusion from education and employment. This came across all generations, who were passionate and determined to ensure that, despite living in disadvantage, young women should be given educational opportunities to become autonomous: ‘First, I heard my grandmother’s story. She was a single mom. Though she did not study but ensured that all her daughters got quality education…she wants us to be self-independent too’ (Youth 7). Exclusion from education and dependence appeared to be reversed at different generational stages for participating families: ‘Now, I want my daughter to become self-sufficient like me…to stand on her feet rather than end up being like my mother to be dependent upon her husband and sons’ (Mother 2).
Peer educators, (all young female students or professionals themselves), voiced parallel views and experiences during the training. Their own mothers often had to fight hard to pave a different life trajectory for them, although his was not always accepted by their extended family or community: ‘…but what stood out for me was how mothers who were denied education when they were young are now standing up for their daughters’ right to education’ (Peer Educator 5). Consequently, hearing the determination of families from socioeconomic backgrounds made a deep impact on them: ‘Whatever I could hear from afar really inspired me to see even the oldest generation motivating young girls and empowering them’ (Peer Educator 2 diary).

## Theme 3: Knowledge cascade
Sharing intergenerational experiences helped shape new narratives for youth and peer educators, as all quotes on this subtheme originated from this age group. For youth, key learning related to similarities between generations in responding to trauma, while previously they may have focused more on contextual circumstances, hence differences: ‘I learned that there is so much about the past which our parents never told us, and now we should talk to them about their learnings from life. We never thought to give time to our parents, so that they share things from their generation with us’ (Youth 1). The creation of a safe space facilitated the emergence of new narratives within and across families, some of whom had never previously shared their experiences with their children or grandchildren: ‘I learned how strengths or weaknesses can be similar in each generation…useful part was to learn from the experiences of other families’ (Youth 4); ‘When we came here at first, it was like onboarding a journey from negative to positive. Our minds were cleared. It was like a sudden upliftment in the mood. Learning new lessons and leaving behind hardships. A ray of hope to start afresh and enjoying life, by keeping the difficulties aside’ (Youth 5).
Before helping youth participants reframe their narratives, peer educators had to change some of their own anticipated beliefs. In focus group discussions and reflective diaries, they acknowledged how they had challenged pre-conceptions on age and socioeconomic status. To this effect, they were positively surprised by grandparents’ openness to new ideas and their dedication to education for their grandchildren: ‘I would say I was pleasantly surprised. I do think after speaking to the families that most of them were of progressive mindsets, something I was not expecting at all. They all tried to learn lessons from their pasts and move on, focus on a better future for themselves as well as their kids’ (Peer Educator 8); ‘Well, to my surprise, the few families are very much open to change’ (Peer Educator 12). Quotes highlight a parallel learning process between peer educators and youth, which required a cascade approach of the research team supporting peer educators to support youth.
The iterative process of the training involved theory and case scenarios, application by peer educators and reflection. This appeared to facilitate peer educators’ gradual understanding of their complex role, especially when interacting with families: ‘To be very honest, at the start, I couldn’t make sense of that story part and the family, this was whole like cluttered information inside my head…but now, as you’re planning the session, I think it all has started to make sense and, I can connect and connect everything of why we needed to like, collect information, why, went through all the training, and why we had to study the theory part that we did’ (Peer Educator 2); ‘Listening to the families directly was a very different approach than just simply reading about them or listening to another person. I think this made me realize how people have struggled and shockingly they are open to talking about it’ (Peer Educator 9).
Interestingly, although the objective of the family consultation workshops was to inform the next phase of designing mental health promotion, both families and peer educators related benefits that they applied in everyday lives. These included individual coping mechanisms and family interactions. Mothers and grandmothers particularly focused on the importance of self-care, which had been lost among their multiple responsibilities for others: ‘I learned to give myself importance. I never thought about myself and the children are grown up now. I now try to give myself quality time by doing the things I like, such as book reading’ (Mother 2). Here we note a similar parallel personal growth between the two groups: ‘There are a lot of coping track strategies and a lot of learnings that I took for myself, and I think that will help me in my relationships in life…I think I have got a lot of skills and tools that subconsciously fit in my mind that I will be using in my further life, apart from these awareness and community events’ (Peer Educator 2).
The main implication of intergenerational learning was spending quality time with family members and exploring past experiences. It appeared that several youth participants found this experience useful and tried to generate more story-sharing at home: ‘Previously I had this thought that we need arrangements on the big level to do such programmes, but now I learned we on our own can do these small discussions at our home with our friends and families’ (Youth 1). Whilst peer educators facilitated these interactions, they also mirrored other youth by applying lessons with their own families: ‘I was able to connect with a few families where they were discussing their lives, as my parents and uncle used to tell me the same stories’ (Peer Educator 12 diary).
Finally, participants discussed how they could extend knowledge transfer to their communities: ‘We can do retreat-type activities in the future for families where they all can come together and spend time, and with fun we can share these experiences’ (Youth 1). Such considerations were stimulated by relating to the peer educators rather than by proxy exploration with professionals. Some family members, especially mothers and youth, were keen to act as peer educators, while others, mainly grandmothers, did not feel confident enough, although they could play an engaging role: ‘I can only share my experience with them but can’t do the whole on my own’ (Mother 3); ‘I can just motivate others, I can’t deliver the session’ (Grandmother 2).
Many respondents acknowledged that cascading knowledge would require further training and support: ‘If we will co-facilitate, we will need more training and logistic support’ (Mother 1). Peer educators built on their experience on clarifying the role. The main challenge they experienced was not to divert beyond mental health promotion in providing interventions. Setting boundaries in their activities and dealing with the emotional pressure on wanting to help participants was a constant theme of the training: ‘The most difficult aspect was setting boundaries when some of the families expect instant solutions from you when they tell you their problems. Being empathetic but also coming out of their shoes after that instant was a great deal of effort. It had to be done in such a way that the families don’t feel unheard’ (Peer Educator 9). The research team needed to regularly remind peer educators of their remit and delineate this from a professional role, whilst not undermining their confidence: ‘Your role as a peer educator is not about fixing people’s lives. It is all about planting the seed, helping them explore more about themselves rather than fixing them for your own satisfaction as a mental health worker. We always tend to feel guilty if we are not able to fix anyone’s life, to validate their emotions…’ (Peer Educator 12 diary).

## DISCUSSION AND CONCLUSION
Youth mental health promotion is essential in tackling stigma, enhancing wellbeing and encouraging the initiation of help-seeking, especially in MWC resource-constrained settings. Communities should have a central role in co-production and delivery of mental health promotion programmes ( ). In this study, the conceptualization and design of such an initiative were led by young peer educators and co-produced with three-generation family units from participating communities. Peer educators interacted with families in an iterative process, and all participants reflected on helpful and challenging factors in planning mental health promotion.
Before considering the findings and implications for youth mental health promotion, it is important to acknowledge certain limitations of this study. The participating sites were not necessarily representative of other MWC cultures and contexts. Family advisers were likely to be more motivated, hence open to new ideas, thus not reflecting stigmatizing attitudes to mental health within their communities. There was self-selection in the recruitment of peer educators with professional studies and from a socioeconomic background that did not match the disadvantaged communities at focus. No male participants took part, both through natural recruitment and a decision not to mix genders for cultural reasons. This paper reports on the design rather than the implementation of mental health promotion. Quantitative measures of stigma, knowledge or family relationships could have aided our understanding of underpinning factors.
Notwithstanding the limitations, there are useful lessons generated from the findings and from hearing the voices of this seldom-heard group of participants, particularly with the intergenerational cascade of knowledge sharing and the motivation to be agents of change from the peer educators. The findings are also useful in the broader context of understanding mental health promotion in the majority world, with some important principles in designing training and working with local communities ascertained. These lessons are helpful in relation to trauma and the transference of learning within small community groups. For example, a key finding was how to relate key knowledge and strategies on building resilience, by tapping into intergenerational experience of responding to different contexts of trauma within communities, which participants, particularly youth, could then transfer to their current circumstances and formulate their own narratives. In the context of intergenerational and community resilience, it is important to be more mindful of the intersecting systems in relation to youth mental health. Recent theories view child and youth resilience as the capacity of systems to adapt to manage threats ( ). Thus, resilience processes sit within mutual networks, social and cultural structures and wider groups ( ). A socioecological approach to resilience ( ) can be a useful framework in designing and implementing mental health promotion in MWC resource-constrained communities.
In this study, families reported using observations and learning from their participation in their everyday lives by creating time for sharing and interacting, mostly between youth and their grandparents. Although older age adults have been less represented in mental health promotion initiatives ( ), in this study grandparents were engaged throughout, indeed they were more open to new ideas than peer educators had anticipated.
We captured several challenges through data collection, as peer educators appeared to grow within parameters of safety and learning, and through an iterative process with the family advisers and the two trainers from the research team. The training programme combined theory, evidence and application of key concepts for mental health promotion, experiential learning, community visits, pilot facilitation of family consultation workshops and reflection. This enabled peer educators to generate ideas but also concerns, test them out with families and revisit with their trainers. The established issues are pertinent for peer educator-led mental health promotion.
Relating to difficult experiences and memories had a positive impact on family members and peer educators but also inevitably elicited difficult emotions for both groups. To this effect, there is a risk that in developing the skills of local community providers through a peer educator scheme, they will then be exposed to the trauma of others as they learn the lessons needed to develop an informative and relevant promotion programme for the community. This can adversely impact peer educators’ wellbeing or confidence, or in their moving beyond mental health promotion objectives to providing therapeutic interventions. Indeed, peer educators reported the temptation to offer solutions rather than space for awareness and self-care, as the main challenge of their involvement. They also faced some intergenerational conflict and tensions during the workshops, mainly by adults criticizing youth, which are likely to be re-enacted in larger-scale programmes. The use of creative participatory activities proposed by the peer educators and refined by the family advisers were important tools in engaging families and retaining the mental health promotion focus. In contrast, presentations were considered as likely to alienate community members, whilst encouraging extensive questions on personal and trauma-related needs, rather than generalizable scenarios and wider awareness.
The training programme for peer educators in this study aimed to manage such challenges whilst equipping them with key skills to engage participants within the parameters of mental health promotion ( ). Indeed, for learning to occur, so that this can provide a foundation for mental health promotion events and information sharing, there must be some of the therapeutic relationship type qualities in the process of learning. These qualities include unconditional positive regard, confidentiality, empathy, non-judgemental listening and trust-building ( ). Training, supervision, and use of templates for the family consultation workshops ensured that peer educators kept to the remit of mental health promotion, and anticipated scenarios of participants asking for help and ways of responding. All these issues should be taken into consideration in planning mental health promotion through community volunteers or paraprofessionals, who hold unique community wisdom but do not have a professional background. For example, peer mental health educators in previous research valued financial reimbursement, training, support and role flexibility ( ), and reported that their role had benefited their personal growth ( ). However, these were rarely integrated in service systems ( ).
The peer educator role can be further compromised by not sufficiently facilitating and addressing power dynamics between professional services and communities, especially in minority world countries with well-developed statutory service systems ( ). In contrast, MWC with extensive community networks may offer more opportunities for co-production through social entrepreneurship ( ). proposed that community co-production should follow a virtuous cycle of shared values, understanding, power, responsibilities and ownership, as well as positive outcomes.
In parallel, consideration should be given to support for emerging distress and wish to receive help from participating community members. The intergenerational journey of trauma to resilience is a complex and deeply personal process that involves healing and transforming pain into strength over time. This journey can be especially challenging for individuals and families who have experienced trauma across generations. Close family structures and community support can help families build coping mechanisms ( ). Nevertheless, this also highlights the importance of integrating mental health promotion within a comprehensive and scaled service system, rather than provided in silo ( , ). Such a system should delineate between trauma-informed self-care and professional interventions. Trauma-informed self-care for prevention can equip the upcoming generation with resilience and coping skills needed to navigate life’s challenges. By promoting an understanding of how trauma can potentially impact mental health and wellbeing, we can empower youth to create and apply their own solutions ( ). Youth themselves, as well as community members across generations, can play an important role, especially in contexts with limited specialist resources and uptake of mental health support.
Future research could evaluate the implementation of intergenerational mental health promotion led by youth or other peer educators, and in different sociocultural contexts. There is value in including peer educators with lived experience of mental health needs. Incorporation of mental health promotion in service systems would improve our understanding of the impact of such programmes, for example by measuring help-seeking and uptake of community-based or specialist interventions.
