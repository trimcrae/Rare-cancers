## Abstract

## Background
Chinese academic diplomacy has opened international educational opportunities in different fields including nursing. Nursing students often experience tremendous challenges during their clinical learning; these challenges are elevated for international students due to limited familiarity with their new environment. Enhancing workplace social capital might be an effective approach to addressing these challenges. However, a comprehensive understanding of international nursing students’ workplace social capital remains unknown. This study aimed to describe the composition of workplace social capital as perceived by international nursing students during their clinical learning in China.

## Methods
A qualitative descriptive design was applied. Participants were international students from 13 countries (n = 30), recruited from an international nursing program in China; all participants had completed 36 weeks of clinical learning in a Chinese teaching hospital. Data were collected through three rounds of focus group discussions and seven follow-up individual interviews between 2022 and 2024. Inductive qualitative content analysis was used for data analysis.

## Results
Two themes were abstracted. The first theme addresses the structural configuration of workplace social capital. The two directions of relational network that contributed to the structural configuration are vertical (linking with mentors, nurse managers and physicians) and horizontal (bridging with Chinese-partners and bonding among international classmates). An obvious hierarchy exists in linking social capital. The second theme represents the cognitive elements in social capital including Trust, Empowerment, Equality and Social cohesion. Trust is the most well-recognized cognitive element. Empowerment and Equality are two distinct characteristics of international nursing students’ workplace social capital.

## Conclusions
International nursing students’ workplace social capital is formed based on multi-directional professional interactions. Hierarchical differences in social capital should be reduced. Transcultural leadership and mentorship training to facilitate the advancement of workplace social capital in an international educational environment in China is encouraged. Our work is a prelude to understanding the concept of international nursing students’ workplace social capital and contributes to the development of intervention strategies for future investigations.

## Trial registration
Our study does not report healthcare interventions on human participants. Trial registration is not necessary according to the International Committee of Medical Journal Editors (ICMJE)’s clinical trial registration policy.

## Background
Expansion of global interactions has amplified academic opportunities for international learning and exchanges in the field of nursing [ , ]. In 2013, the government of China implemented the foreign policy of the Belt and Road Initiative (BRI). This initiative encourages and promotes global cooperation and cultural exchanges [ ]. Additionally, this initiative promotes academic collaborations by providing opportunities for international students to advance their education at one of the Chinese academic institutions of higher education [ ]. Education in an international setting can be an enriching experience because of the opportunities for experiencing a different culture and learning about different ways of life and the opportunity for better career development [ , ]. Despite the benefits and opportunities, pursuing academic education in a country other than one’s native homeland can impose multiple challenges. The spectrum of challenges can range from adaptation to new social norms and values, cultures, overcoming language barriers, striving to constructively manage ethnic discriminations, and adjustment to different learning contexts and academic expectations [ , ].
Clinical learning constitutes an extremely critical part of nursing education because it bridges theoretical knowledge with the delivery of patient healthcare services [ , ]. The Nursing Regulation set by the Chinese government mandates a minimum of 32 weeks of clinical learning. During the clinical learning nursing students often are stressed because they have to constructively address unpredictable patient situations and often are required to handle complex interpersonal interactions [ ]. International students most likely begin their clinical learning with an elevated level of psychological stress because of their precarious situations due to their social and cultural challenges [ ].
A healthy clinical learning environment with supportive mentor-mentee relationships and an atmosphere of trust and team integrity ensures a positive clinical learning process and outcomes for students [ ]. Supportive relationships, trust and team integrity are characteristics of workplace social capital [ ]. Workplace social capital has been recognized as the key tenet for constructing a healthy work environment [ , ]. Royal evaluated the role of three types of capital (human, social and cultural capital) in nursing education in the United Kingdom (UK), and described that applying social capital is important for constructive clinical teaching and effective learning [ ]. The positive and sustainable impacts of building workplace social capital on the learning environment which can considerably promote nursing students’ clinical learning experiences in Australia have been reported in the study by Materne et al. [ ]. Similarly, the benefits of constructive workplace social capital in alleviating Chinese nursing students’ stress due to multiple challenges during clinical learning were reported by Ding and Tang [ ].
The notion of nurses’ workplace social capital has been defined as “A relational network configured by respectful interactions among nursing professionals and between the other healthcare professionals” in a concept analysis study by Xu et al. [11, P.252]. The five attributes of nurses’ workplace social capital include Relational Network, Trust, Shared Understanding, Reciprocity and Social Cohesion [ , ]. Nurses’ workplace social capital can be classified by its components (Structural and Cognitive) and directions of relational network (Vertical: linking; Horizontal: bridging and bonding) [ , ]. Meanwhile, the discussion about the composition of international nursing students’ workplace social capital during clinical learning in their host countries is limited. The rapid increase in global nursing and nursing education mandates a deeper understanding of international nursing students’ workplace social capital in their host countries including China.
Development of workplace social capital might promote a constructive learning environment for international students, particularly during the demanding period of clinical learning with tremendous challenges. In doing so, it is imperative to know how international students perceive and understand their workplace social capital. Is it similar to nurses’ workplace social capital? Empirical investigations on the description of the composition of workplace social capital in a clinical learning environment remain unknown. A qualitative approach is considered appropriate when the objective is to obtain a deeper understanding of the phenomenon of interest from human experiences [ ]. Therefore, we implemented a study with the overall objective of discerning the international nursing students’ experiences and perceptions of the composition of their workplace social capital during clinical learning in the Chinese hospital settings.

## Methods

## Study design
We implemented a qualitative descriptive study design with inductive content analysis. We chose the qualitative descriptive design to be close to our data and to provide a clear and comprehensive summary of the phenomenon under study [ ]. International students were invited to share their experiences through semi-structured focus group discussions and in-depth individual interviews which were scheduled after the focus group discussions. The Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) checklist was applied to conduct and enhance the quality of the report of our study [ ].

## Settings and participants
Participants were recruited through a purposive sampling approach from a population of 33 international nursing students (Class of 2018 and Class of 2019). All students were enrolled in a four-year international baccalaureate nursing program at Lishui University located in southeast China. Lishui University is an accredited international university approved and certified by the Chinese government where all courses and instructions are delivered in English in the international program. International students who are admitted to Lishui University must demonstrate proficiency in English language which is assessed and evaluated by exams and interviews organized by the administration at Lishui University. This international baccalaureate nursing program was stopped for three years (2020–2022) because of the COVID-19 pandemic. These 33 international students who had finished clinical learning were the only accessible potential participants during the period of our research which overlapped with the social policy of distancing and isolation due to the pandemic. The inclusion criteria were: (1) international nursing students; (2) completion of 36 weeks of clinical learning in the teaching hospital affiliated with Lishui University; and (3) a willingness to participate. The rotation units of the 36-week clinical learning included Medical (eight weeks), Surgical (eight weeks), Emergency (six weeks), Intensive Care Unit (two weeks), Obstetrics & Gynecology (four weeks), Pediatric (four weeks) and Community Nursing (two weeks). The ratio of international students to mentors was 1:1 during the period of rotation within each unit. Meanwhile, the hospital policies and regulatory requirements mandated the assignment of each international student to a Chinese-partner during their clinical learning. The Chinese-partners were undergraduate nursing students who had dexterity in English language communication.
After obtaining permission from the Office of International Students at Lishui University, we recruited students to take part in our study. We used three different recruitment strategies. Poster information, including the research purpose, recruitment and research team, was posted on the University DingTalk group for international students. DingTalk is a communication app normally used by organizations and work communities in China. Additionally, individually tailored emails were sent to the prospective students and finally we recruited students using social group chats such as WeChat. A total of 30 students who met the inclusion criteria agreed to participate in our study. We conducted three rounds of focus group discussions. A total of 18 students from the Class of 2018 contributed to the first (n = 9) and second (n = 9) focus groups that were held in 2022. The third focus group was held in 2023 with 12 students from the Class of 2019. Additionally, seven students who had participated in the focus groups and had expressed their desire to share more information about their experiences were invited to take part in in-depth interviews. The final individual interview was conducted in March 2024.

## Data collection
Participants were invited to reflect on their experiences and perceptions of workplace social capital during their clinical learning in the hospital. The lead author (PhD, nursing faculty) acted as the moderator in proceeding with the focus group discussions while the second author (Nurse specialist, nursing faculty) assumed the facilitator role of observer and note-taker [ ]. The lead author, with more than seven years of research focusing on workplace social capital in nursing, was awarded her doctoral degree from a well-respected English-speaking international nursing program outside China. Because of her proficiency in English language and her scientific expertise, the lead author had been assigned the role and responsibilities of an academic moderator of a joint baccalaureate program between Lishui University and a Swedish university. Moreover, she had experiences in the role of an international academic visitor at one of the universities in the UK. The second author (female) has a constructive history of research and didactic collaboration with the lead author. Both authors, with fluency in English skills, did not have direct responsibilities and didactic interactions with the participants during their clinical learning. English language was used during the process of data collection. Communication during the interviews proceeded smoothly as most of the participants came from countries where English is one of the official languages. A semi-structured interview guide with open-ended questions, initially developed by the research team in English based on their expertise and literature review, was used to guide the discussions (Supplementary Table ). The interview guide was pilot tested among the research team to ensure clarity and relevance. The study participants had learned about the concept of workplace social capital in a required course before their clinical learning. To confirm their familiarity with this concept, each focus group started with a broad and warming-up question “Do you know what workplace social capital is?” Following participants’ responses, the moderator proceeded with giving a brief description of the concept of workplace social capital to ensure a shared understanding. The subsequent discussion was guided by four main questions: “What is the composition of international nursing students’ workplace social capital from your perspective?” “Could you describe an example of someone who has strong workplace social capital during clinical learning?” “Could you describe an example of someone who has weak workplace social capital during clinical learning?” and “Do you think your workplace social capital has other contents and characteristics besides the above description?” Probing questions corresponding to each main question are presented in Supplementary Table . To minimize potential priming effects, technical terms related to social capital (e.g., vertical relationships, horizontal relationships) were avoided in the probes.
The three rounds of focus group discussions were conducted in one of the available classrooms, each lasting between 90 and 100 min. Each study participant completed a digital demographic questionnaire which was sent to the lead author either via E-mail or WeChat before the group discussion. The additional seven face-to-face individual interviews were conducted by the lead author at a time convenient for the interviewees. The average time of the individual interviews was 40 min. Each focus group discussion and each individual interview were audio recorded; the recording was then transcribed verbatim.

## Data analysis
The transcribed data were analyzed using qualitative content analysis by Graneheim and Lundman [ ]. The process of this inductive data analysis consisted of iterative efforts of selecting meaning units, identifying condensed meaning units, creating codes, creating subcategories (at different levels of abstraction) and categories, and constructing themes. The NVivo14 software (Lumivero, USA) was used to facilitate the process of data analysis.
Each transcript was read multiple times to gain a comprehensive understanding of its entirety. This step was followed by identifying the meaning units and condensed meaning units. Then codes were identified and labeled and were clustered into subcategories and categories. Themes were then abstracted by the contents of categories and subcategories and the comprehensive understanding of the data. Data were analyzed by the first and third authors independently at the initial stage; discordances were discussed until a consensus was reached. A codebook was developed with the facilitation of NVivo 14 including the coding rules, names and meanings of the codes, related meaning units and the researchers’ reflections on the data analysis. The analysis process and results were presented during team discussions and were improved and approved by all authors. A clear tendency of thematic saturation was observed after the third round of group discussion and no new analytical information emerged after the seventh individual interview, indicating the achievement of data saturation.

## Trustworthiness
Trustworthiness issues [ , ] were highlighted and addressed in our study. Credibility was established by implementing triangulation of data, researchers, data collection methods and researcher reflexivity throughout the study. There is no communication bias as English language was used in the interviews which ensured participants’ recognition of the meaning of the phenomenon they experienced in their social context. Meanwhile, a summary of interviews and researchers’ interpretations was returned to the participants for member checking to strengthen the credibility [ ]. Transferability was achieved by creating thick descriptions to demonstrate the value of truth. Conformability was established through repeated discussions and agreements among the international research team of this study. Meanwhile, detailed information about the study (audit trail) has been recorded including the information related to the international context, methodology, analytic approach and self-awareness of the researchers to achieve conformability and dependability. The research team actively practiced reflexivity to recognize and manage potential biases. A detailed description of the decision-making process on the subcategory “rapport” in our audit trail and an example of the excerpt bank (Supplementary Table ) are provided to enhance the rigor of our report.

## Results
A total of 30 international students from 13 countries took part in our study; most participants were from African countries (n = 28) of whom 13 were from Zimbabwe. Participants were aged between 21 and 32 years of age and more than half of them (n = 16) were under age 25. Due to the nature of the field of nursing, the majority (n = 21) were female; males constituted 9 of the total participants. The demographic profile of participants is shown in Table  .
 We identified and labeled 612 codes. These codes were then sorted into 54 less-abstracted subcategories and further into 17 higher-order subcategories. Six categories were developed based on the 17 higher-order subcategories, while assessing and considering the influence of all subcategories. Two themes that are related to the composition of workplace social capital finally emerged (Table  ). Theme 1 demonstrates international students’ perception of the structural configuration of their workplace social capital; while Theme 2 describes the students’ perception of cognitive elements that are embedded in the relational network of their workplace social capital. A visual schematic is developed (Fig.  ).
 

## Theme 1: international nursing students’ workplace social capital relational network is structurally configured by multi-directional interactions and collaborations
Participants expressed the structural configuration of their workplace social capital during their clinical learning in the hospital. They described that their workplace social capital relational network was constructed by multiple interactions and collaborations with different groups of people. Two directions of the relational network were reported: vertical and horizontal (Fig.  ). Accordingly, two categories were abstracted under Theme 1 (Table  ).

## Category: configuration of vertical relational network
The vertical workplace relational network was constructed by students’ interactions with their clinical mentors, nurse managers and physicians in the work units.
We have different relationships at work. The vertical relationships are from the top to the bottom, with physicians, nurse managers and our mentors… (Participant (P) 5)
The vertical relationship, as often highlighted by the participants, is characterized by a hierarchy with different power strata. Participants believed that they were at the bottom of this hierarchical relational ladder.
Yes, hierarchy exists in the workplace. I think as we are the nursing students, we are at the bottom. (P6)
Notably, almost all the participants reflected that physicians were at the top of the hierarchical ladder, even higher than nurse managers.
The position of the physicians is very high. I think it is higher than the nurse manager. You can see it with your eyes. It is not the same…Physicians are ranked at the top. (P19)
The extent and intensity of the interactions and collaborations in the workplace relational network differ between different relationship contributors. Most of participants reported that they did not have many opportunities to communicate with the physicians and nurse managers because they were perceived as high-ranking and distant from students. The main resource for the configuration of international nursing students’ vertical relational network was contributed by their linking interactions with their mentors.
I communicated with my mentors in the ward mostly…I feel physicians are far from us, and somehow the nurse managers. I could not talk to them too much. Yes, they are high [nodding] (P22)

## Category: configuration of horizontal relational network
The horizontal relational network was formed because of international students’ collaborations with their Chinese-partners and with their international classmates.
…the cooperation with our Chinese-partners and classmates, I think, is horizontal [social capital]. (P5)
Participants reported that their bridging collaborations with the Chinese-partners were the main contributor to their horizontal relational network.
I had to stay with my partner at work. We learned from the same mentor in each ward. We worked together. (P2)
The bonding collaboration among international students during their clinical learning was weak because they rarely had the opportunity for cooperation and person-to-person communication.
Normally we communicated with each other at work not too much, because only one or two foreign students were in a ward, and we had different shifts. We couldn’t see each other quite often. (P1)

## Theme 2: international nursing students’ workplace social capital relational network is embedded with cognitive elements
Participants reported several cognitive elements that are embedded in the workplace relational network based on their experiences and perceptions. Subcategories related to the cognitive elements were grouped into four categories under Theme 2, including Trust, Empowerment, Equality and Social cohesion (Fig.  ; Table  ).

## Category: trust
The information of trust appeared frequently across the transcript of the data. Participants described that trustworthy relationships with their mentors and Chinese- partners were the key to developing harmonious workplace social capital. Participants reported that the positive relationship with their mentors was fundamentally built on the feelings of being trusted by their mentors.
I said ‘thank you’ a million times for her [the mentor] trust in me. She is the best… I want to stay in contact with her…before I leave China I will go to see her [smiling]. (P5)
Participants’ confidence in their professional abilities was elevated because of the sense of being trusted by mentors during their clinical learning.
Yes, if your mentor trusts you the patient will trust you too. So, it’s an important matter for the mentors to trust students and provide them with full support. I really feel confident in taking care of my patients if my mentor trusts me. (P2)
In contrast, some participants complained about the sense of distrust that was exuded by their mentors. This sense of distrust had hindered their abilities to develop their workplace relational network.
My first department, there was no trust. Anything I was doing, she [the mentor] would follow me to be sure whether I knew what I was doing…So there was no relationship for me then. (P19)
The importance of mutual trust between the participants and their Chinese-partners was also reported.
We [international students and Chinese-partners] have to trust each other to maintain good relationships at work. We are supposed to trust and help each other…We need to make efforts to improve this. (P20)

## Category: empowerment
Participants stated that they wanted to be empowered by their mentors. The specific content related to empowerment includes the feelings of not being ignored at work and autonomy in clinical practice. Participants felt that sometimes they were ignored by their mentors unintentionally or deliberately.
The mentor worked with her computer, and she didn’t have time to explain to me…I didn’t understand that paperwork because every document was in Chinese. That was very far for me. (P8)
Sometimes the teacher didn’t want the student. So, she always asked him [international classmate] to stay away, like saying: stay there. If I need you, I will send you messages. I was sad to hear this. (P14)
Participants were eager to receive more autonomy in practice during their clinical learning. They wanted to be empowered by their mentors to achieve a sense of professional inclusion in the workplace.
We wanted to learn in this place differently in terms of the skills and practice. We needed freedom and opportunities for practice. If they [mentors] could understand that we were just there for a short period of time and we wanted to gain something, I think it would make it [social capital] better…We wanted to work as a real nurse sometimes and be included in the team. (P15)

## Category: equality
Participants highlighted the role of equality in constructing their workplace social capital. They focused on equal learning opportunities compared with their Chinese-partners, gender equality and protection and respect by others. Several participants complained that they received fewer learning opportunities and support in practice from their mentors than their Chinese-partners.
My mentor was unfair to me. She only focused on my Chinese-partner. (P18)
He [the mentor] was helping and teaching my partner very much…If the mentor doesn’t like me, there is nothing I can do. (P20)
Participants reported that they could have been more efficient with the delivery of healthcare services without their Chinese-partners. Therefore, some of them decided to separate from their Chinese-partners.
I felt when my partner was not around, I would actually do more work. The teacher would give me more and just let me go to the patient alone for practice like injection, whatever. (P28)
We felt that our mentors were focusing more on our partners. So, I separated with my partner…I wanted her [the mentor] to concentrate on me. (P16)
However, a few participants had good relationships with their Chinese-partners. They described that their Chinese-partners had bridged a positive communication between them and their mentors.
Like every department we went to, my partner always told my mentor that I (P19) could do and let me try first. (P19)
These participants believe that equality in receiving learning opportunities also depends on the active learning attitudes they demonstrated to their mentors.
But if we showed them [mentors] that we wanted to do this, they actually were there and would allow you to practice, and the relationship started. (P5)
Some male participants complained the issue of gender equality. They described that they had faced more difficulties in being included in the workplace than their female classmates.
The nurses should treat us well despite me being a male nurse. I had the same duties as the female nurses. We should be the same and they should teach us more and involve us more. (P29)
The importance of protection in the workplace was emphasized by the participants. They desired to receive more protection from the clinical mentors especially in front of the patients as they considered that they are foreigners.
I have another thing, protection, from my mentors and nurse managers. Some patients didn’t want us to touch them…The nurse manager told the patients: they are students, so please allow them to do so. This is good. (P23)
Positive and negative examples of protection were explained by the participants. For instance:
I met one teacher…I learned a lot of things because she always assigned me to do work, and she never made me look bad in front of patients. Even if I made a mistake, she would explain to the patients that we were there to learn. (P24)
We are foreign students. Some of us may be on the wrong side of the hospital rules. There was no need for a teacher to take pictures and send them to the school group. It was going to be fatal for the student. (P4)
Respect is another necessary aspect of interacting with others in the workplace. Participants strongly believe that showing respect is the basic etiquette for interpersonal interaction and the foundation for relationship formation.
My mentors have to respect me…If they do not talk to me in a good manner, I will sit down. I may be rude to them and say something bad which is not right…No good interactions anymore. (P10)

## Category: social cohesion
Participants emphasized the cognitive element of social cohesion in workplace social capital. Social cohesion represents the feeling of a sense of belonging [ ]. Under the category of social cohesion, four main subcategories were identified: cultural acceptance, open-mindedness, social interactions and rapport.
The international nursing program in our study was a network of students from 13 countries with diverse cultures and social backgrounds. Students practiced in a Chinese hospital which operates under cultural auspices that are totally different from their own cultural values and norms. Cultural respect and acceptance have paramount importance for embracing and integrating international students into the workplace.
I think another common feeling in the workplace is exclusion due to the culture. We have different cultures…Different cultures should be accepted to get along with each other. (P 20)
Participants expressed the necessity of open-mindedness in maintaining human interactions in an international workplace with a culturally diverse ambiance.
If my mentor is open-minded, I’ll be very open-minded to learn…I got lucky because my partner is very open-minded…My partner made me like this internship. (P19)
Social interactions, such as group activities beyond the workplace, are pivotal in maintaining individual and collective relationships.
We had a great dinner in one of the best Western food restaurants with the teachers. The nurse manager was really great because even the nurses around her were very comfortable… We are still keeping in touch. (P5)
The word “rapport” was used by the participants to describe their desire to be included in the work team.
I think we are all in the team: me, my partner, my mentor, the nurse manager and doctors. We must maintain a good rapport among each other. (P10)

## Discussion
We conducted this qualitative study to describe the composition of workplace social capital as experienced and perceived by international nursing students from different countries during their clinical learning in China. A combination of focus group discussions and individual interviews was used to collect data and qualitative content analysis was applied to analyze data. Two themes describing the composition of workplace social capital were inductively constructed (Table ; Fig. ). A comparison between the concept of nurses’ workplace social capital by Xu et al. [ , , ] and our current findings is presented in Table .
 Theme 1 which is the “International nursing students’ workplace social capital relational network is structurally configured by multi-directional interactions and collaborations” illustrates the structural component of workplace social capital. The structural component of workplace social capital of the international students encompasses the directions of the relationship configuration and contributors to these relationships. In line with the theoretical notion of nurses’ workplace social capital proposed by Xu et al. [ , ], the international students’ workplace social capital is configured by two directions of relational network: vertical (linking) and horizontal (bridging and bonding) (Table ). Hierarchy does exist in clinical practice settings, and it can influence clinical teaching and learning and quality care [ ]. The vertical social capital (linking) of international students is formed by their interactions mainly with their mentors, nurse managers and physicians who represent different levels of power status. Nurses, even the advanced practice registered nurses (APRNs) who are educated at the graduate level are sometimes perceived as the “second-class citizens” in the healthcare industry [ , ]. In our study, international students expressed that they were at the bottom of the vertical social capital, and they felt that physicians were at the top of the so called “pyramid of power”, a status higher than their mentors and even nurse managers. Nurses are the backbone of healthcare delivery and have been continuously ranked as the most trusted professionals [ ]. Nursing students can benefit from academic courses and didactic training to shatter the existing stereotypes about the nursing profession. The history of nursing profession is filled with the history and struggles of strong individuals to advance the science and profession of nursing. Organizational culture can influence the shaping of hierarchical structures in the workplace. Hospital leadership is recommended to focus on formulating a just and sustainably supportive organizational culture that reduces unit hierarchy and power differential and raises nurses’ voices in daily work to ensure constructive workforce management with the consequence of healthcare practice improvement [ , ].
The major vertical relational network is shaped by mentor-mentee relationships. Mentors play an extremely pivotal role in teaching and ensuring a supportive practical learning environment for students [ , ]. Students reflected that their interactions with nurse managers were inadequate. Nurse managers are the closest leaders to nurses and nursing students in clinical settings. They play an essential role in “humanizing” their workplace, and positive nurse leadership has been described as a cradle for workplace social capital [ ]. Nurse managers can benefit from the latest advancement in the concept and practice of leadership, such as effective communication, especially with entry level nurses and nursing students. Additionally, globalization of education and delivery of healthcare services requires development of transcultural leadership skills at international level, where diversity in cultural values and norms is acknowledged and respected [ ]. In this study, the inadequate communication between nurse managers and students might have been caused by different factors such as differences in cultural values and organizational norms and language barriers. Due to the limited publications and general information, we are not able to discern if inadequacy in communication with nurse managers is commonly experienced by international nursing students in different academic institutions in China. Meanwhile, the inadequacy in communication between leadership and international students underlines the necessity of cultural tolerance and improvement of communication abilities and skills in transcultural leadership training. Hospitals, particularly hospitals affiliated with academia, should incorporate leadership training in the scope of continuous education for nurse managers. Meanwhile, the students who participated in this study rarely mentioned their interactions with the nurses apart from their mentors during their clinical learning. This is partially due to the one-on-one student to mentor assignment. Limited interactions between nursing students and nurses might yield a weak communication circle for students, particularly among international students.
In our study, the horizontal workplace social capital of the international students was mainly contributed by their collaborations with their Chinese-partners (bridging). Constructive bridging social capital narrows the gap among students who might be “unalike” [ ]. However, the interactions in this bridging relationship between two groups of students were not satisfactory because our participants expressed that they were not equally treated by their mentors when they were working with their Chinese-partners. We believe that this bridging social capital can be improved as both international and Chinese students are from nursing programs. For example, findings from a recent study suggested that Chinese students expressed their delight in being translators between clinical mentors and international students [ ]. More studies on the reasons for the alienated relationships between the international students and Chinese-partners need to be conducted. Reasonable partnership arrangements and personality compatibility matching should be considered. The bonding social capital represents the cooperation among the international students in the workplace, but the extent and intensity of this horizontal connection were weak. Findings from different studies suggest that clinical mentors often grapple with difficulties and challenges in guiding students with diverse cultural and linguistic backgrounds [ , ]. In our study, each mentor was only allowed to have one international student at each ward with the objective of attenuating stress due to differences in cultural background and language limitations. We believe this might be the reason for the weakness of the bonding social capital as communication between the international students in the workplace was limited. Few studies have discussed mentors’ transcultural mentorship competencies and support for mentors in teaching international nursing students in China. Research on transcultural mentorship in clinics and related interventional strategies should be encouraged in response to the overall tendency of global nursing and nursing education.
Theme 2 is named as “International nursing students’ workplace social capital relational network is embedded with cognitive elements”. This theme demonstrates the cognitive component of workplace social capital which includes Trust, Empowerment, Equality and Social cohesion. Similarities and differences exist between cognitive elements of international students’ workplace social capital and nurses’ workplace social capital as reported by Xu et al. [ , ] (Table ). Trust and Social cohesion are the two cognitive elements of international students’ workplace social capital that share the same concepts as the attributes of nurses’ workplace social capital. Trust is the tenet for a respectful and successful mentor-mentee relationship and effective partnership [ , ]. Trust is the most well-recognized element that was embedded in the relational network of workplace social capital in our study. All participants highly cherished the feeling of being trusted by their mentors because the sense of trust increased their professional confidence in the early stage of their nursing careers. However, our participants did not emphasize the importance of trust in the development of their professional relationship with their mentors. We hypothesize that self-evaluation of low-level position in the vertical social capital reduces the importance of trust in establishing professional relationships with those who hold a position of power such as mentors. Meanwhile, mutual trust between the international students and their Chinese-partners needs to be improved as suggested by our participants; a lack of trust between student peers can weaken the bridging social capital.
Social cohesion, indicating a sense of community and belonging [ ], is another cognitive characteristic of international nursing students’ workplace social capital. Having constructive relationships in a workplace with a sense of belonging is a critical contributor to satisfaction with clinical learning experiences for nursing students [ , ]. According to the findings of our study, social cohesion is underpinned by the acceptance of cultural diversity, an open-minded attitude toward workplace interactions, positive social interactions and a feeling of rapport in an international clinical learning environment. China has become a popular host country for international students in recent years [ ]. Although having cross-cultural adaptation skills is necessary for international students in clinical learning, Chinese host hospitals should also be open to embracing the concept of cultural diversity in line with the international cultural exchanges and cooperation principles of the government. Positive social interactions among the members of a working team that can encourage integration of students, e.g., coffee breaks and group outdoor activities, can reduce the hierarchical differences in the vertical social capital and support the relatedness of the team [ ]. Mentor-mentee rapport represents social capital and reflects valuable resources in developing workplace social capital [ , ]. Our participants expressed rapport as one of the cognitive characteristics of workplace social capital in describing compatibility of the relational network in the clinical learning environment they wish to possess.
Empowerment and Equality are the other two distinct elements in the relational network of international nursing students’ workplace social capital which differ from the attributes of nurses’ workplace social capital (Table ). Our participants did not concentrate on the attributes of Shared Understanding and Reciprocity of workplace social capital [ , ]. We consider Shared Understanding and Reciprocity to be two cognitive characteristics generated by people with deep connections and communications. Our participants might not have been able to interact deeply with others in the vertical social capital as they ranked themselves at the bottom. Additionally, they also had relatively weak horizontal social capital. These situations can restrict their perceptions of these two elements. Instead, they strongly expressed the importance of and desire for Empowerment and Equality in constructing their workplace social capital. The feelings of stress and vulnerability are often reported by nursing students in clinical learning [ , ]. Empowering students in clinical learning with more autonomy increases students’ self-efficacy which can improve the feeling of being valued and cherished [ ]. In our study, students reflected that they desired to “work as a real nurse sometimes” and their mentors “shouldn’t look down” on them and should allow them to practice more beyond the easy services like “take blood pressure for one month” which had attenuated their confidence and interests in practice.
Some mentors might focus more on Chinese students due to their cultural and linguistic barriers and experiences of difficulties in teaching international students. The inequality in learning opportunities has attenuated their mentor-mentee relationship and has led to some envious emotions among students. A participant separated from her Chinese-partner with the reason “didn’t want to depend on my partner” because her partner “wouldn’t really say anything.” Specific reasons should be investigated further by discussions with mentors to address this problem. Learning opportunities might be triggered by students’ proactive attitudes because mentors “actually were there and would allow you to practice.” Male nurses are more likely to quit their jobs compared with female nurses because of gender stereotyping in the workplace [ ]. A good learning experience in the clinic can set the foundation for a sustainable career choice for male nursing students as they have “the same duties as the female nurses.” Therefore, gender equality is considered an intangible resource in formulating sustainable workplace social capital. Students should be valued, supported, mentored and encouraged to work as a team in building workplace social capital [ ]. Protecting and showing due respect to students enables them to feel safe and accepted by the team and brings them a sense of equality in interactions and collaborations with others which is conducive to both vertical and horizontal workplace social capital. Students highlighted that mentors should not “judge in front of patients” and should not make them “look bad in front of patients.” In our study, participants from African countries expressed stronger desires for protection and respect in the workplace compared to students from Asia. Black nursing students often encountered negative experiences in healthcare and academic settings [ ]. The sensitive topic of racial microaggression (RM) should be adequately addressed when fostering inclusive international teaching and learning environments for international nursing students as suggested by Aslan and Yalniz [ ]. Some strategies have been proposed to mitigate RM within academic settings, such as enhancing cooperation among all students, alleviating language barriers and developing an inclusive learning environment where RM can be openly discussed [ ].

## Implications for practice
We have described that the composition of international nursing students’ workplace social capital consists of the structural configuration of relational network and cognitive elements in the networks. Awareness about the importance of enhancing workplace social capital for international students can be stimulated through communication of our findings with nursing academicians and clinical nursing professionals. The clear and detailed depictions of the structural configuration and cognitive elements should shed light on the core aspects that are relevant to intervention programs for developing international nursing students’ workplace social capital. We have demonstrated the phenomenon of international nursing students’ workplace social capital via a comprehensive combination of narrative and visual schematic approaches. Our efforts are helpful for hospital leadership, nursing administrators, clinical mentors, educational researchers, university educators and students to understand this complex but important concept in facilitating teaching and learning in an international educational environment. For instance, hospital and nursing administrators are suggested to strive to develop policies to support transcultural leadership and mentorship training, e.g., developing transcultural training programs and creating learning opportunities in international settings. Strategies for reasonable and desirable partnership matching rules should be developed to enhance bridging relationships. Clinical mentors play a key role in developing and maintaining international nursing students’ workplace social capital. It is essential for clinical mentors to actively improve their transcultural mentoring skills and trust and empower international students with more autonomy to ensure equal, compatible and respectful mentorships for students with cultural and linguistic diversities. University educators equally carry the responsibility of disseminating information about organizational cultures and administrative norms and values of host hospitals to mitigate potential difficult encounters of the students. Additionally, students also carry the responsibility of being an active knowledge seeker. Developing a positive and proactive attitude toward learning can open “doors” for improved learning opportunities for students, which ultimately can strengthen their confidence and acceptance in the clinical learning workplace. Equally important, host hospitals should emphasize and implement strategies to address the issue of hierarchy in linking social capital. The benefits of a supportive organizational culture can be experienced across the spectrum of workforce, from students to executive leadership.

## Limitations
Our study has some limitations. The international nursing students’ workplace social capital is morphed by multiple groups at work; in consequence, interviews with the clinical mentors, nurse managers, physicians and Chinese-partners are recommended to enrich the description of the phenomenon from different perspectives. For example, the influence of variations in mentorship quality on international students’ social capital relational network and cognitive elements should be evaluated and needs input from reflections by clinical mentors and nurse managers. Most of our participants were from African countries (a relatively high proportion of participants were from Zimbabwe) and only one Chinese host hospital was involved, which limits the transferability of the findings. The application of the composition of international nursing students’ workplace social capital in other countries requires further validation. Comparison between other research settings, participants from countries in different regions and among more students with diverse international backgrounds should be considered in future studies. Objective quantitative investigations on international nursing students’ workplace social capital and relevant influencing factors are suggested to provide a supplementary explanation to the subjective qualitative data. Despite these limitations, our study is the first to describe the lived experiences of workplace social capital during clinical learning as reflected by international students. To some extent, findings of our study contribute to theoretical values that can be applied in developing future research about international nursing students’ workplace social capital in different settings. The exploration of the composition of international nursing students’ workplace social capital in the Chinese educational context provides evidence and resources for nurturing workplace social capital in the field of nursing.

## Conclusions
A clinical learning environment with supportive workplace social capital relational network benefits students’ clinical learning. In this qualitative study, we described the international nursing students’ perceptions of their workplace social capital during their clinical learning in China. Similarities and differences exist between international nursing students’ workplace social capital and nurses’ workplace social capital. The composition of international nursing students’ workplace social capital consists of the structural configuration and cognitive elements. Structural configuration is divided into vertical (linking with mentors, nurse managers and physicians) and horizontal (bridging with Chinese-partners and bonding among international students) relational networks. Cognitive elements include Trust, Empowerment, Equality and Social cohesion. Empowerment and Equality are identified as the two distinct cognitive elements that differ from the attributes of nurses’ workplace social capital in a Chinese clinical learning environment. Our findings suggest that hierarchical differences in the vertical social capital should be reduced; while the horizontal bridging social capital needs to be improved, and the bonding social capital needs to be elevated for international students. Transcultural leadership and mentorship training in the context of global nursing in China, with the aim of matching the unique demands of international students during clinical learning and fostering their workplace social capital, is recommended. Overall, our qualitative work sheds light on advancing research in students’ workplace social capital and enriches the resources for the development of international nursing students’ workplace social capital in addressing difficulties and challenges in global nursing education.
