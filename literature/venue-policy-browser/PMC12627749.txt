## Abstract
The DREAM guideline (Dedication, Respect through relationship, Environment, Art of nursing, and Motivation) was initially developed from a study exploring nurses’ perceptions regarding presence in a rural area of the North West Province, South Africa. To further examine its applicability, it was necessary to validate the guideline in other nursing contexts, particularly in mental health nursing. Such a context is public psychiatric hospitals where adolescents are admitted with conditions like depression, anxiety, eating disorders, and behaviour disorders, and provided mainly with custodial care, including medication administration and supervision. Implementing a presence-based approach in such settings could be beneficial. To validate the DREAM guideline, this study aimed to explore and describe the perceptions of mental health nurses regarding the DREAM presence practice guideline in providing care to adolescents with mental illnesses. The study was conducted in two major psychiatric hospitals in the North West Province, South Africa. The authors utilized a qualitative research approach with an exploratory-descriptive, contextual design. Participants were purposefully selected based on their experience in direct nursing care of adolescents with mental illnesses and their attendance at a DREAM guideline workshop. Data collection involved focus group discussions, with each group consisting of four or six participants, depending on the setting. Thematic analysis, incorporating deductive and inductive strategies, were used for data analysis. Four themes emerged: (1) Holistic and compassionate care: perceptions of mental health nurses providing care to adolescents with mental illnesses, (2) Empowering mental health nurses through the DREAM guideline: perspectives on providing care to adolescents with mental illnesses, (3) Challenges and barriers in implementing the DREAM guideline for mental health nurses and (4) Improving the DREAM practice in caring for adolescents with mental illnesses. This study’s findings offer valuable insights into how the DREAM guideline can be used as a presence practice in mental health nursing to promote better patient outcomes and enhance nurses’ professional experiences. These contributions are poised to drive improvements in mental healthcare delivery, patient satisfaction, and the overall well-being of healthcare professionals in the field.

## Introduction
The DREAM guideline (Dedication, Respect through relationship, Environment, Art of nursing, and Motivation) was initially developed by the authors, based on a study conducted regarding the perceptions of nurses working in rural general hospitals in the North West Province, South Africa, on presence . The DREAM guideline was developed to enhance the delivery of quality care to patients, emphasizing five key caring processes. D stands for Dedication: “Doing the best you can”, and R—Respect through relationship: “Good care is about perspective, connecting, and attuning”. Furthermore, E refers to Environment: “Supporting good care through relational management”, A denotes Art of nursing: “Understanding is where change can start”, and M—Motivation: “Good care requires readiness” . The core purpose of the DREAM guideline is to facilitate presence, which is defined as “the mutual act of intentionally focusing on the patient through attentiveness to their needs by the offering of one’s whole self to be with the patient for healing” . This is supported by the assertion that presence is the ability of nurses to establish inter-relational experiences with their patients, leading to positive patient outcomes , . Presence practice cultivates the empathic and humanistic aspects of care , . Additionally, growth and healing are hallmarks of presence, including improved nurse and patient outcomes .
In various areas of nursing, the significance of presence practice is widely recognized, particularly in mental health nursing. Within this context, presence practice reflects clinical competence through the therapeutic use of self in active engagement with patients . It encompasses being fully present and engaging with the patients’ immediate physical, psychological, social, and spiritual state . Research highlights the critical role of presence practice in facilitating quality nursing care for mental health care users such as adolescents – . These adolescents have a fundamental need for connection, attunement to their needs, and relational care . Presence practice can be strengthened by equipping nurses with knowledge of the concept and fostering both willingness and the attributes critical for its enactment , . Conversely, a lack of nurse presence can lead to undesirable outcomes, including patient dissatisfaction and ineffective communication , 
Previous research indicated that gaining a comprehensive understanding of presence in the context of South Africa is crucial. For instance, emphasized the results of a study on the state of nursing in South Africa, highlighting a range of challenges. These issues include leadership and governmental problems, concerns about the quality of care, and weaknesses in policy. While it may not be entirely accurate to attribute all these challenges solely to the concept of ‘presence,’ it is important to acknowledge that inadequate presence in practice can be one of several contributing factors to the broader challenges confronting the nursing profession. Recognizing this, it becomes clear that addressing presence as part of a comprehensive strategy is crucial for enhancing nursing care and overall patient outcomes. Hobbs further confirmed the need to enhance presence within the South African nursing profession to ensure high-quality care across all nursing practice areas. Moreover, it is noteworthy that presence has emerged as a significant area of nursing research among South African scholars, particularly among nurses working in psychiatric hospitals , . The adoption of presence is not only a research consideration but also a practical necessity. It plays a vital role in building relationships between nurses and mental healthcare users, contributing to the mitigation of relapses , . Presence holds significant importance in the field of mental health nursing care , , .
Despite the recognized importance of presence in mental health, translating it into practice grounded framework such as the DREAM guidelines necessitates careful consideration to ensure that its relational depth is preserved and not diminished by overtly simplistic or prescriptive interpretation , . While promising, the DREAM guideline may risk falling into acronymic simplicity, potentially reducing complex relational practice to checklist-like items that do not fully accommodate cultural or contextual nuances in evolving clinical settings. Although the guideline appears to be meaningful in facilitating presence in nursing care; however, its validity has not yet been established, particularly in the context of mental health care provided to adolescents by nurses working in public psychiatric hospitals. Recognising these critiques is vital as it positions our validation study not as a wholesome endorsement of DREAM guideline, but rather as a critical inquiry into whether and in what contexts the framework adds value beyond existing models of relational practice.
In preparation for the validation of the DREAM guideline, particularly among nurses caring for adolescents with mental health illnesses, consecutive workshops were held over three weeks, targeting nurses who showed interest. The workshops were conducted between the last week of September 2022 and the second week of October 2022. Each week, the workshop was conducted on a single day. Each workshop lasted a minimum of 2 h, including 10-min comfort breaks. The first workshop focused on a reflective discussion of the acronym D—Dedication (Doing the best you can) and R—Respect through relationship (Good care is about perspective, connecting, and attuning). The second workshop focused on the reflective discussion of E—Environment (Supporting good care through relational management) and A—Art of nursing (Understanding is where change can start). The third workshop focused on the discussion of M —Motivation (Good care requires readiness). Key activities include a didactic overview of DREAM guideline acronyms, a reflective discussion on what the specific concept means in the participants’ daily practice, including a case-application exercise, and how it can make a difference in patient care, especially applied to adolescents with mental illnesses.
Typically, public psychiatric hospitals admit adolescents with mental illnesses such as depression, anxiety, eating disorders, paranoia, and behaviour disorders and render mostly custodial care, which includes the administration of medication and supervision. Such settings will benefit from providing care in a presence manner. Therefore, this study explored and described the perceptions of mental health nurses on the DREAM presence practice guideline in the management of adolescents with mental illnesses in two major psychiatric hospitals in the North West Province, following three different workshops which educated the nurses with regard to the DREAM guideline. The development of the DREAM guideline was primarily informed by the second author, but its validation, particularly among mental health nurses caring for adolescents with mental health disorders, was led by the first author, whose research is on adolescent mental health. The validation of the DREAMM guideline is crucial to address identified gaps in holistic and person-centered care. This article reports on a study that aimed to validate the DREAM guideline by exploring its relevance in the context of mental healthcare provided to adolescents diagnosed with mental illnesses.

## Method

## Design
This study employed a qualitative research approach and an exploratory-descriptive, contextual research design, which is well-suited for gaining an in-depth understanding of participants’ perceptions, experiences, and opinions of a phenomenon – . Such a design enables the generation of in-depth and contextual insights that contribute to a new understanding of practice – . The researchers thus considered it to be appropriate for this study as it allowed them to deeply explore and describe the participants’ views and opinions within the study settings and to understand their perspectives regarding the DREAM guideline. Similar designs have been applied by Groenland to validate the newly developed tools by following a qualitative approach. All methods were carried out in accordance with the relevant institutional and international research ethics standards. Specifically, approvals were obtained from the university ethics committee, securing informed consent from all participants, and ensuring confidentiality and anonymity throughout the study.

## Research setting and population
The study was conducted in two psychiatric hospitals in the North West Province, South Africa. The target population for this study was all nursing categories (registered nurses, auxiliary nurses, and enrolled nurses) providing nursing care to adolescents with mental illnesses. All the nurses were invited to attend the DREAM workshop, and those who chose to attend were part of this study. All of them are full-time employees, have worked in the study setting for more than three months, were registered with the South African Nursing Council (SANC) as a nurse (professional, auxiliary, or enrolled), and attended the workshops. Student nurses were excluded from the study.

## Sampling technique
The participants were recruited by using a purposive sampling technique, which involves deliberately selecting individuals most likely to provide relevant and rich information about the phenomenon under study . In this study, nurses were chosen because of their direct involvement in caring for adolescents diagnosed with mental illnesses.

## Data collection
Data were collected through focus group discussions (FGDs), a qualitative data collection method in which a small group of participants engages in guided dialogue on a specific topic , . FGDs are particularly useful for exploring shared experiences, generating diverse perspectives, and fostering interaction among participants, thereby yielding insights that individual interviews alone cannot , . FGD was conducted with the nurses caring for adolescents with mental illnesses, who attended the DREAM guideline workshops. The focus group discussion was held immediately after the third workshop, in a selected quiet venue within each of the study settings. The FGDs were guided by semi-structured questions, which were reviewed by two established scholars with minor wording revisions for clarity. One focus group discussion was conducted in each of the two study settings. In the first setting, six (n = 6) participants took part in the FGD session, while in the second setting, four (n = 4) participants participated. Although we initially planned more than one FGD in each study setting, staffing constraints limited data collection to one FGD per site; this is acknowledged as a limitation. Participants fully understood the purpose of the study, were willing to share their perspectives, participated voluntarily, and provided written consent. The participants’ rights were explained to them, and their questions were adequately answered before they signed the informed consent forms and thereafter took part in the study. Participants were reminded that they had the right to withdraw from the study at any point without any negative consequences.
The FGD enabled the nurses to answer questions regarding their perceptions of the DREAM guideline. It further enhanced round-table discussions with the intent of gathering broader and in-depth research data from the nurses, hence the best-fit data collection method for the study , . The researchers believe that constructive ideas can be better formed in a group. This study’s main question was: “What are the perceptions of mental health nurses providing care to adolescents with mental illnesses regarding the DREAM guideline?”, while the FGD questions were: “What are your views on the DREAM presence practice guideline, what are your views on the importance of the DREAM presence practice guideline, how important could the DREAM presence practice guideline be in the management of adolescent mental illnesses, what are the perceived challenges in application of the DREAM presence practice guideline in the management of adolescent mental illnesses, and what are your views on how the DREAM presence practice guideline could improve nurse outcomes (compassion fatigue, burnout, job satisfaction, intention to leave)?”.Methodological triangulation was achieved by supplementing FGD transcripts with field notes and a reflexive journal. These auxiliary data enriched contextual understanding and supported credibility.
Furthermore, the focus group interviewer took field notes and a reflective journal and made an effort to listen carefully to the participants’ responses to identify when they were expressing similar ideas and to understand their shared values and perspectives regarding the questions. Additionally, the focus group discussion lasted between 40 and 60 min, giving the researcher enough time to probe the questions and allowing participants sufficient time to respond. Participants did not mention their names during the discussion for privacy; instead, they were assigned anonymous names for the study. In focus group discussions, anonymity and confidentiality are not guaranteed; therefore, the session followed these rules: group members answer questions one at a time to prevent disrupting others’ views, and everything discussed remains within the group (non-disclosure). Participants should support each other and allow everyone to speak freely, with no interruptions. Everyone has the right to express their views constructively about the topic, and no separate conversations or abusive language should be tolerated. Additionally, participants were asked to establish their own ground rules regarding confidentiality.
Permission to record the focus group discussion session was obtained from the participants before recording the sessions. The interviewer probed the questions for in-depth and broad data until data saturation was reached, when no further new information emerged from the participants regarding the focus group questions. This was enhanced by allowing participants to share their views until the discussion did not produce any new stories, which was the point of data saturation . Data saturation was further enhanced by the participants’ diversity (registered nurses, auxiliary nurses, and enrolled nurses), experience, and the richness of the obtained data. At the end of the focus group discussion, the researcher thanked the participants for taking part in the study and further informed them that the feedback regarding the findings would be communicated to them via the nurse manager.

## Data analysis
The focus group discussion data were transferred from the audio recorder to a password-protected computer and transcribed verbatim before analysis. The focus group discussion recording was then deleted from the recorder to ensure the confidentiality of the data. The data were analyzed using a thematic approach to qualitative data analysis, incorporating both deductive and inductive strategies . The following steps were taken to read through the data to become familiar with it, deriving meaning, and generating initial codes for analysis. These initial codes provide a concise summary of specific aspects of the data, allowing the researcher to maintain control over the analysis process by generating subcategories, categories, and themes and clustering them for interpretation. The subcategories, categories, and themes were organized for clarity and validated to ensure that the data reflected the participants’ authentic responses. A back-and-forth approach was utilized during the analysis to enhance the accuracy of the identified subcategories, categories, and themes. Although the research team remained attentive to possible contradictions or ambiguities in participants’ narratives, the data in this study showed consistent themes across responses, which allowed for a coherent interpretation. Reflexive practices and peer discussions were maintained to guard against potential interpretive bias. An independent co-coder also analyzed the data, reaching a consensus with the researcher on the identified categories and themes. ## Trustworthiness
Trustworthiness refers to the credibility, dependability, confirmability, and transferability of the research findings . To maintain credibility, the researcher invested significant time with the participants. This involved ensuring that participants fully understood the questions and answers based on their perspectives, taking detailed field notes and reflective journals, and establishing trustful relationships. Additionally, challenging emergent interpretation, employing an independent coder to achieve consensus on emerged themes and categories, preventing the researcher’s preconceived expectations from interfering with the data, and meticulously refining the data during analysis . Dependability was ensured by maintaining regular debriefing meetings, keeping an audit trail of analytic decisions, a thick description of the research process, and adhering to a semi-structured interview guide. The researchers presented the study design for the readers, in case anyone wants to reproduce the study in a different context. Authenticity was ensured through active listening during the data collection to foster in-depth data . Confirmability was ensured through ensuring the consideration of alternative explanations and the application of objectivity during the process of collecting data and subsequent analysis , . Additionally, an independent co-coder reviewed the transcripts and codes to confirm the neutrality and accuracy of theme development. Objectivity was enhanced through the involvement of an independent co-coder, and the second and third authors served as independent checkers throughout the research.

## Results and discussion

## Demographic characteristics of participants
Ten nurses (seven females and three males) working with adolescents diagnosed with mental illnesses participated in this study.

## Organization of the themes

Theme 1: Holistic and Compassionate Care: Perceptions of Mental Health Nurses Providing Care to Adolescents with Mental Illnesses

As seen in Table above, Holistic and compassionate care is integral to the care of adolescents with mental illnesses. This was deduced from the responses of the participants. Various categories emerged to portray these responses.

## Dedication and commitment
The dedication and commitment of mental health nurses in the management of adolescents with mental illnesses are important in various ways, such as in enhancing the care process. In dedication and commitment to the care process, mental health nurses prioritize the well-being of adolescents above anything else. Mental health nurses who are dedicated and committed to the care process present the values and the unique needs of the adolescent at the forefront, considering not only the patients’ physical health but also their emotional, spiritual, and social dimensions. The participants reflected on their views on the DREAM guideline, saying that,
“I think it is important for a nurse to be dedicated in their work, and also dedication will lead to you being motivated, especially getting positive results from patients and them responding well to their treatment, then you get motivated to do your job with dedication” … [Hospital 1, Participant 1, F]
“Dedication is very important because that will make you render care from the area of passion, and to me, passion is crucial in this kind of nursing job we are doing… [Hospital 1, Participant 2, F]
“… like you see that dedication aspect, dedication is very important in this part of nursing care, which is mental health, because you will get to see many things that could be discouraging to you in rendering care like ranging from the patient attitudes or the attitudes of your colleagues or even from the management” … [Hospital 2, Participant 2, F]

## Attentive in the care process
From the participants’ attempts to explain their views on the DREAM guideline in the management of adolescents with mental illnesses, it was deduced that the guideline is about being attentive as a nurse in the care of these adolescents. For instance, a direct quote from the participants revealed:
“You try to listen to their stories, you give them that attention, they tell those funny stories about their life experience whether it sounds meaningful or not but as a caregiver, you should try to show them that you are listening to them, try to connect with them, avoid arguments instead listen to them and advise them where necessary”… [Hospital 1, Participant 2, F]

## Establishing compassionate care
The study participants also perceived the DREAM guideline in the management of adolescents with mental illness to be concerned with establishing compassionate care in their management. Compassionate care in this instance is about providing mental healthcare services with a deep sense of empathy, kindness, and understanding towards the emotional, psychological, and physical needs of adolescents. Establishing compassionate care could be beneficial in presence practice. To the participants,
“… when I say going the extra mile, I don’t mean rendering routine nursing care such as giving drugs, but rather providing them emotional support, you try and be present in their lives, show them that you care, that you want to hear those complaints” … [Hospital 1, Participant 1, F]
“I must just always be willing to do everything in my power to help the patients, I will try to understand the patient and remain motivated” … [Hospital 1, Participant 3, F].
“Nurses can see their patients for who they are like, like they will try to show their patients that they are there for them, they care for them, they want to see them, or the person recovers and possibly get discharged and go home” … [Hospital 2, Participants 3, M]

Theme 2: Empowering Mental Health Nurses through the DREAM Guideline: Perspectives on Providing Care to Adolescents with Mental Illnesses

The study participants further reflected on their perceptions of the DREAM guideline as crucial in empowering mental health nurses. Their response demonstrates that the DREAM guideline will make a positive impact on the management of adolescents with mental illnesses. The emerging categories are presented and discussed below in line with the participants’ direct quotes.

## Recognition of positive changes
Participants felt that the DREAM guideline could yield positive results in the management of adolescents with mental illnesses, for example:
“These acronyms we discussed, I feel, will be very helpful in the care of these adolescents” … [Hospital 1, Participant 1, F]
“The DREAM guideline will be important in the management of adolescents. Like now, we have learned new knowledge, which is DREAM, and to me, the knowledge will be important. Now I understand that I have to do the best I could way beyond ensuring that they take drugs and eat their food and even do documentation, but now I understand that I must be present both physically and otherwise” … [Hospital 1, Participant 2, F]
Moreover, one participant emphasized the significance of understanding to determine the most effective approach to providing care for adolescents with mental illnesses. According to the participant, understanding the patient prevents adopting an authoritative approach and instead encourages active listening to the patient’s perspective, ultimately leading to improved and well-directed care.
“… but like as we were taught during the workshop, understanding is where change starts from. For me, I mark that part of ‘understanding’ is where change starts. It means that I would no longer approach my patients from that point of authority alone, as that can make me not regard their opinion. But if I could come down to their level, try and understand them, and see things from their end, it will help me to know how to channel my care to the patients” … [Hospital 2, Participant 1, M]

## Value of the DREAM guideline
The participants recognized the value of the DREAM guideline in empowering mental health nurses to manage adolescents with mental health illnesses. One of the participants maintained:
“I think the acronyms are meaningful. As I said about understanding as a place where change starts, I will also talk about respect, you said is about relational management, so I feel respect is very important, if the nurse understands a patient, it becomes easy to respect the person. When nurses respect patients and treat them with the same respect, it can make them open up to you” … [Hospital 2, Participant 1, M]
“I feel is very important because it summarized a lot of things that could help in the care of adolescents with mental health problems. It summarized them, like showing that doing the best we can is very important and that doing the best we can should be beyond normal administration of drugs rather than being present wholly when rendering care despite the limited hours we have with the patients during each shift. This DREAM experience has made it clear that even with those limited hours we have in each shift, we can make a huge difference in the recovery process of the patient” … [Hospital 1, Participant 3, F]

## Understanding and empathy
The participants felt that the DREAM guideline could foster understanding and demonstration of empathy by mental health nurses caring for adolescents with mental illnesses. Participants said that understanding the patient helps the nurse in knowing how to attune care to the needs of the adolescent.
“… understand the adolescents better, which can help us to know where to effect changes and motivation” … [Hospital 1, Participant 1, F]
“Good understanding of the nurses on the problems of the adolescents, they will be in a better position to help them and that could only be achieved through respect when we respect them and show them that their opinion also matters, they know that we listen to them without judging them affording them that respect, I think that will open them up and then they will be able to share their feeling and problem and the nurses will be in the better position to attend to their needs”… [Hospital 2, Participant 5, F]
“It is that understanding that can make a nurse see things from their perspective and approach things better” … [Hospital 1, Participant 2, F]

## Implementation of the DREAM guideline
The participants considered implementing the DREAM guideline important for the management of adolescents with mental illnesses, which would enable the maximization of its benefits. For instance, participants said:
“I feel is very important because it summarized a lot of things that could help in the care of adolescents with mental health problems” … [Hospital 1, Participant 3, F]
“You put in your best to see that their health improves, you listen to them, and you make sure the environment is conducive for them, you try to understand them, as my colleagues said, I agree that understanding is where change starts, then motivation” … [Hospital 2, Participant 6, F]
The potential of the DREAM guideline in improving specific nurse outcomes (compassion fatigue, burnout, job satisfaction, intention to leave)
This category denotes the participants’ views on how the DREAM guideline could foster the improvement of purposefully selected nurse outcomes (compassion fatigue, burnout, job satisfaction, intention to leave) in caring for adolescents with mental illnesses. Participants were able to provide insightful responses, stating:
“I can say that if we nurses think and do DREAM in caring for our patients, we can know when to pause a bit, think, and act right for the good of the patient and for our good. We can know how to approach patients and at the same time enjoy our job. Akere (you see), when some enjoy what they are doing, the person will not feel like quitting the job” … [Hospital 1, Participant 3, F]
“As I listen to my colleagues, I really agree with them I can only add that DREAM, if implemented in the management of adolescents with mental health disorders, will educate nurses on being present both emotionally, spiritually, and in every aspect of the care of their patients, that will lead to self-accomplishments. Remember we nurses are called to serve so when you serve diligently, you will feel good and feel that good stress level will reduce, you will feel satisfied, and no need of talking about leaving your workplace”… [ Hospital 2, Participant 4, M]
One of the participants further reflected on how the DREAM guideline could improve the selected nurse outcomes, saying that:
“By improving the nurse, I feel there is that feeling of joy when you see that your care to your patient is helping them to improve when you see those joy on their faces when you see them discharged and they go home better, there is joy in your heart especially when you know that you gave your best, you supported them emotionally and spiritually, you counseled them, at that point, you will feel worthy of your profession”… [Hospital 1, Participant 4, F]
One participant also maintained that the knowledge of the DREAM guideline is important to foster shared responsibilities among nurses, which will be crucial in improving nurse outcomes. For instance:
“By the team putting our best efforts it will reduce the fatigue of other nurses, if we all put in our efforts, everybody will be doing his share or his part for the good of the hospital so which might reduce the workload of other healthcare workers I think that’s how we can reduce burnout among nurses” … [Hospital 2, Participant 3, M]
Another participant further reflected on how the DREAM guideline could improve the selected nurse outcomes, by emphasizing the joy that could come through the practice of presence, saying that:
“I feel when you are happy and enjoying what you are doing, I don’t see why people will complain or even want to leave from what we learned in the workshop, practicing presence can make one feel fulfilled so why should one start talking about leaving except when there another offer with better money” … [Hospital 2, Participant 2, F]
Participants further asserted that the DREAM guideline, which has ‘respect through relationship’ as one of its core concepts, could help to improve the selected nurse outcome.
“I’m thinking by we nurses being dedicated to our jobs, we can respect each other, there will be peace in the hospital environment, and can easily implement the act of nursing by taking care of our patients, and by taking care of our patients, looking after them, and remaining motivated in order to continue coming to work” … [Hospital 2, Participant 5, F]
“I think in terms of respect, respecting each other, respecting the adolescents or the respect among the health care providers that can, I mean if you go to a place where you know your values, your perceptions, or your views are respected, also the respect between the patients and the staff workers I think that can improve job satisfaction and you are also motivated, you go to work knowing that you are ready to render the care you are required to render. I think that might help to improve job satisfaction among the health care professionals” … Hospital 2, Participant 1, M]

Theme 3: Challenges and Barriers in Implementing the DREAM Guideline for Mental Health Nurses

This theme represents the participants’ views on the challenges that could hamper the implementation of the DREAM guideline in the management of adolescents with mental illness. Understanding these challenges is crucial for recommendations that could improve the implementation of the DREAM guideline in the management of adolescents with mental illness. A few categories emerged, which are presented below.

## Care environment and resources
Participants also emphasized the challenges they face with the care environment and resources, which they considered could be a mitigating factor in the implementation of the DREAM guideline, pointing out the issues associated with resources, saying:
“It is painful and you as a nurse working in an under-resourced environment, it becomes more difficult to be dedicated so you see that you will be in-between managing your emotions to accommodate your patients and working with limited resources to still see that they are doing great so it is very challenging”… [Hospital 1, Participant 1, F]
“You know that sometimes we are forced to manage the little available resources so when you are not dedicated, you can hardly cope”… [Hospital 2, Participant 2, F]
One of the participants described the impacts of working in an environment challenged with understaffing. To this nurse, working in an environment where there are fewer nurses poses a challenge in times of crisis, which could hinder the implementation of the DREAM guideline.
“When you know you are a few nurses managing many patients, and when there is a crisis in the ward, it becomes a struggle so in that situation, you can’t even think of trying to understand your patient to make the change so it’s a serious issue to us here”… [Hospital 2, Participant 3, M]
A participant who had a different but interesting view emphasized the lack of quality patient reviews. To this participant, patient review, if prioritized, will enable the nurses to always know the areas where they need to improve in order to serve patients better. This is interesting as it demonstrates the nurse’s commitment to quality care for adolescents with mental illnesses.
“Lack of quality patient review, patient satisfaction, I don’t know what it is called but I think that is what is called, patient satisfactory structure because you know we talk about the act of nursing, that understanding is where change stand from so through that reviews, the patient satisfaction reviews, the adolescents giving us the feedback that will enable us to know where we are shortcoming and then we be able to know how to improve the quality of care of our patients so if we don’t have that sufficient quality reviews from patients themselves or adolescents themselves, that will be a challenge because we wouldn’t know what our shortcoming are”… [Hospital 2, Participant 5, F]
Another participant further complained about motivation. An environment devoid of motivation could hamper the implementation of the DREAM guideline. To the participant,
“Like we really need motivation”… [Hospital 1, Participant 2, F]

## Patient attitudes and interactions
Some participants also expressed concerns about patients’ attitudes and the nurse-patients’ interactions. According to them, these factors could play crucial roles in hindering the implementation of the DREAM guideline.
“Disrespectful they have lived in that environment for a long time, so they are not even shy to a stranger with that behaviour they come with from their homes they also show it to us, telling us you are not my Mom, you are not going to tell me what to do, so that kind of behaviour, also makes things challenging for us to reach that patient also because once they make up their mind, it’s very hard to shift, some patients they respect doctors and psychologist more than nurses, they take us that as if I don’t know we are security staff for them”… [Hospital 1, Participant 2, F]

## Implementation and practice challenges
Participants further elaborated on more challenges they felt could hamper the implementation of the DREAM guideline in the management of adolescents with mental illnesses. For the participants, these challenges are concerned with the implementation and practice.
“The challenge will be in the aspect of respect through relationships because some of these patients, we have a challenge in that in some cases they forget that they are in the hospital and they think we are their friends or we are their girlfriends or we are their Moms or whatever the case may be”… [Hospital 1, Participant 1, F]
To practice presence, readiness is necessary because the mental health nurse is expected to be in the best state of mind. One of the participants understood this and said:
“But I also think that readiness has things to do with psychology because if you are not psychologically stable maybe because of life challenges, it becomes an issue to practice presence. Or maybe you are stressed from work-related activities or relationship issues, it becomes difficult to practice presence”… [Hospital 2, Participant 6, F]
The practice environment plays an integral role in the quality of care received by adolescents with mental illnesses. Thus, it should provide the necessary support needed for the delivery of quality care.
“Another thing is lack of support and skill set. If the hospital management is not supporting the staff like getting them to attend training as we did in this workshop, for me, I can call the workshop a training because we learned new knowledge so when such opportunities are not always presented to nurses, they will not be able to learn new skills which I feel will help to improve their practice so that’s could be challenging in the application of DREAM guideline”… [Hospital 2, Participant 3, M]
“Many nurses don’t further their education sometimes it becomes an issue of the management allowing you to go for further education”… [Hospital 2, Participant 1, M]

## Time constraints
One of the participants complained about the issue of time constraints and workload. The participant was of the view that time constraints could hamper the implementation of the DREAM guideline in the management of adolescents with mental illnesses.
“Another thing I can talk about is the issue of time, the time we have to me is not enough for us to practice presence”… [Hospital 1, Participant 4, F]
Theme 4: Improving the DREAM practice in caring for adolescents with mental illnesses.
The participants’ responses were centered on improving the care environment for the DREAM guideline to be easily implemented for the management of adolescents with mental illnesses. Participants were able to provide insightful responses. A few categories were deduced, which are presented below.

## Quality of care and respect
Participants emphasized the need for the improvement of quality of care and respect among nurses and nurse-patients. For instance, participants maintained:
“Everyone matters irrespective of our health conditions so the adolescents who are here as patients also matter. They are human and should be treated with respect” … [Hospital 1, Participant 2, F]
“You also respect, I think respect is given and taken like you respect the patients, they respect you, you respect colleagues, they respect you but when you don’t do your part in demonstrating respect, you can hardly get respect so respect is very important and it is good as it was emphasized during the workshop as a crucial component in presence practice”… [Hospital 2, Participant 2, F]
One of the participants believed that adopting the DREAM guideline in the care of adolescents with mental illnesses will help nurses know when to act correctly, for the good of the patients.
“I can say that if we nurses think and do DREAM in caring for our patients, we can know when to pause a bit, think and act right for the good of the patient and for our good. We can know how to approach patients and at the same time enjoy our job”… [Hospital 1, Participant 3, F]
One of the participants emphasized the need for nurses to approach patients with patience and set boundaries as that will help them to earn respect from the patients. Furthermore, nurses should watch out for patients’ body language. Understanding that the nurses in this study were managing adolescents with mental illnesses, hence they emphasized that improving these aspects will help to improve the DREAM guideline in the management of adolescents with mental illnesses.
“We care for adolescents hence I think we nurses should always approach patients with patience, set boundaries, which will earn you your respect, and watch out for patients’ body language. That will help you to channel your care appropriately for the good of the patient”… [Hospital 1, Participant 4, F]

## System improvement and support
Participants emphasized the need to improve the system where they work, such as improving their salary and providing more support, as that will help them in presence practice and improve the DREAM guideline in the management of adolescents with mental illnesses. To the participants,
“Having more staff in the wards to reduce burnout, we being able to share the works among ourselves, and there will be dedication and we will be able to come to work and with that, there will not be burnout”… [Hospital 2, Participant 6, F]
“So staffing is a serious issue and I feel that negatively affects the practice of presence. When you know you are a few nurses managing many patients, and when there is a crisis in the ward, it becomes a struggle so in that situation, you can’t even think of trying to understand your patient to make the change so it’s a serious issue to us here”… [Hospital 2, Participant 3, M]
Participants concurred that an increase in their salary is necessary, as they believe it would offer them greater motivation to provide optimal care for their patients.
“We all need money so that is one of the things I feel that can make one want to change the workplace”… [Hospital 2, Participant 2, F]
“We need money, we work long hours, and we get little peanuts, those people by the government, they work two hours but they get more than us and we are sitting here 12 hours and we are getting peanuts, and even the environment”… [Hospital 1, Participant 1, F]

## Discussion
In this study, to understand the perceptions of mental health nurses managing adolescents with mental illness regarding the DREAM guideline, the meanings participants ascribed to their views were analysed thematically. The findings of this study highlight the potential benefits of the DREAM guideline in enhancing the management of adolescents with mental illness. It was evident that holistic and compassionate care are integral parts of the DREAM guideline for management of these adolescents. To render such care, the study revealed that dedication and commitment, attentiveness in the care process, and establishment of compassionate interaction are fundamental. For some of the participants, dedication to their work was particularly important as it enhanced their motivation, especially when receiving positive feedback from patients and the patients responding positively to treatment.
Building on this, the findings resonate with the broader literature, which consistently affirms that compassion and active listening are crucial in facilitating positive mental health among mental health nurses and patients . Enhancing the delivery of compassionate care to mental health care patients has been shown to positively influence the experience of nurses and patients . Understandably, the presence of compassionate care in mental health care fosters stronger therapeutic bonds (the quality of the connection between caregiver and patient), greater patient receptiveness, enhanced practitioners’ comprehension of the patients’ experience, and increased expressions of empathy within the relationship between health professionals and patients. Ultimately, these elements cultivate an environment conducive to improved patient care , . Furthermore, literature highlights that establishing a therapeutic nurse-patient relationship is integral to the mental health care of patients . Empathy, as a component of this relationship, underscores the mental health nurses’ ability to understand the patient . The presence of such values is fundamental in enabling mental health nurses to deliver holistic care to adolescents with mental illnesses.
The findings further highlight that the DREAM guideline could empower mental health nurses with the knowledge capable of enhancing the care rendered to adolescents with mental illnesses. For some participants, exposure to the DREAM guideline during the workshop awakened their sense of responsibility and reinforced the view that managing adolescents with mental illnesses transcends routine care. It encompasses being present, both physically and emotionally, in the care process. Importantly, participants acknowledged that understanding is where change starts, adding that instead of approaching adolescents with mental illnesses from a place of authority, it is better to come down to the adolescent level, immerse oneself in the patients’ world, try to understand them, and be able to attune care to their core needs. This perspective is supported by Salberg et al. , who found that mental health nurses’ deeper understanding of the patient is regarded as more beneficial compared to coercive measures and the biomedical approach. The authors further argued that managing adolescents with mental health illnesses requires prioritizing patients’ needs and validating their personhood. Similarly, the present study revealed that the DREAM guideline consolidates key principles that can support the management of adolescents with mental illnesses. Specifically, the study highlighted that the DREAM guideline could improve nurse outcomes, which are compassion fatigue, burnout, job satisfaction, and intention to leave.
However, alongside these positive implications of the DREAM guideline, this study also uncovered challenges and barriers that could hamper the implementation of the DREAM guideline in the management of adolescents with mental illnesses. Some participants desired to be present both physically and order-wise during the care; however, issues such as high nurse-patient ratios and a lack of motivation negatively affect their coping mechanisms and well-being. Working in under-resourced environments and with limited resources for patient care was highlighted as a further hindrance to implementing the guideline. Furthermore, the study findings highlight that time constraints could be a challenge in the implementation of the DREAM guideline for the management of adolescents with mental illnesses. These concerns align with existing literature, which indicates that inadequate staffing limits nurses’ ability to devote sufficient time to patient care .
This challenge is not unique to the South African context alone. For example, Mokitimi et al. reported that limited and inappropriate resources are among the barriers to providing effective mental healthcare services in South Africa. Similarly, Beech et al. noted a growing insufficiency of resources that overstretches the National Health Service (NHS) workforce. Many situations could be resolved in mental health hospitals with improved staffing and resources . Literature also has it that time constraints could lead to moral distress among nurses caring for mental health patients. The literature maintains that moral distress is regarded as a feeling of psychological suffering common among healthcare providers who know what is morally right and that could benefit the patients, but are unable to do so because of certain situations, such as time constraints .
Beyond structural barriers, the study further found that the longstanding negative attitudes of adolescents with mental illnesses towards nurses, combined with the lack of quality patient reviews, may present challenges in implementing the DREAM guideline for managing these adolescents. Literature concludes that behaviours such as aggressiveness could be precipitated by ward-related factors . The study also highlighted that issues of readiness among mental health nurses could be a challenge in the implementation of the DREAM guideline, including a lack of support and a skill set. A participant pointed out that readiness has to do with psychology, which, if the nurse is not psychologically ready, could hamper presence practice. Issues associated with readiness could be a result of the nurse’s life challenges, relationship stress, or work-related pressures.
Closely related to readiness is the need for professional growth and support. The lack of opportunities for further training or education was highlighted as depriving nurses of professional development. This concern echoes the literature that emphasizes the importance of practice environments providing career development opportunities for nurses . In the absence of such support, the successful adoption of innovative frameworks such as the DREAM guideline may be hindered.
Notwithstanding these barriers, this study revealed positive shifts in participants’ approaches to adolescent mental health care, which may be attributed to the DREAM guideline workshop. For instance, one participant acknowledged that they have learned that understanding is where change starts. This new insight implies that they would no longer approach adolescents with mental illnesses solely from a place of authority, but would instead engage at their level, strive to understand them, and attune care to their core needs. This study also revealed that presence involves understanding adolescents’ attitudes (which are grounded in logic and reasoning in line with the adolescents), without labelling them as good or bad. Once there is an understanding, the nurse can do what can be done at the moment, from simply being with the adolescent in a difficult moment to taking action, e.g., phoning their family.
Looking forward, the study emphasizes the importance of adapting the care environment to enable nurses to feel more prepared and supported in implementing the DREAM guideline in the care of adolescents with mental illnesses. Respect between nurses and patients was emphasized in the study as a crucial component of presence practice. The participants believed that respect is a crucial component of presence practice. Similarly, the study revealed that if nurses managing adolescents with mental illnesses could always adopt the DREAM guideline in their care approach, they would better know when to act and act right for the good of the patient.
The study findings further reveal that approaching patients with patience and setting boundaries will be crucial in improving the DREAM guideline implementation in the care of adolescents with mental illnesses. Similar to this study, reported that nurses maintaining boundaries in the management of mental health patients help to preserve the patients’ safety and welfare. Valente further advises that nurses must endeavour to control their emotions and reactions in managing mental health patients. The welfare of adolescents with mental illnesses is paramount, but nurses are human and therefore the relationship between nurses and patients should be anchored in a positive approach, characterized by professionalism, respect, flexibility, and support .

## Limitations
The researchers initially intended to conduct multiple focus group discussions in each study setting. However, due to staffing limitations, this plan could not be realized. Consequently, the possibility of introducing selection bias to the study exists. It is important to note that this study adopted a qualitative research approach and an exploratory-descriptive, contextual research design in which generalizability is not the primary aim. Rather, the focus lies in achieving robust, rich, in-depth, and contextually grounded insights that can support transferability to similar settings. Additionally, the smaller number of FGD participants may limit the diversity of perspectives captured; deliberate efforts were made to ensure data credibility and saturation through adopting a purposive sampling technique, participant diversity, reflexive engagement, and methodological rigor. Additionally, the interviewer during the data collection remained attentive to the potential of introducing personal bias by ensuring neutrality and using structured facilitation. Rigorous data analysis process, including employing an independent coder, an audit trail, and application of objectivity during the process of collecting, to strengthen the trustworthiness of findings.

## Conclusion
This study delved into the perceptions of mental health nurses regarding the DREAM guideline’s impact on managing adolescents with mental illnesses. Although the first of its kind, these study findings demonstrate that the DREAM guideline would greatly improve the management of adolescents with mental illnesses. The findings unveiled valuable insights that hold significant implications for nursing practice, patient care, and the overall well-being of healthcare professionals. The study illuminated the potential of the DREAM guideline in transforming the landscape of care for adolescents with mental illnesses. The identified themes echoed the crucial role that dedication, commitment, attentiveness, and establishing therapeutic relationships play in enhancing the quality of care provided by mental health nurses. The DREAM guideline emerged as a promising framework that supports presence practice and aligns with its principles.
These findings will contribute to the existing literature that underscores the significance of compassion and active listening in achieving positive mental health outcomes. While it showcases the promising aspects of the DREAM guideline, it also highlights the various challenges that may impede its successful implementation. Staffing shortages, lack of motivation, inadequate resources, and time constraints emerged as barriers that could hinder nurses’ ability to fully embrace and apply the guideline in practice. Recognizing these challenges is vital for institutions and policymakers to address, ensuring that healthcare professionals are adequately supported to provide optimal care.
Furthermore, the study highlights how to make changes in the care environment so that nurses will feel more ready and supported to implement the DREAM guideline for the care of adolescents with mental illnesses. Importantly, the study provides actionable recommendations to address these challenges and enhance the integration of the DREAM guideline into nursing practice. The emphasis on mutual respect, patience, boundary-setting, and improved resource allocation can contribute to a more conducive environment for both patients and healthcare professionals. These insights align with the broader goal of fostering a sustainable and positive working environment, which is vital for the well-being of both nurses and their patients. In essence, this study sheds light on the transformative potential of the DREAM guideline in the realm of mental health nursing, managing adolescents with mental illnesses.
