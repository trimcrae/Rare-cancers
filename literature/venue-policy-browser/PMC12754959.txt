## Abstract

## Background
The rise of ultra-processed foods has led to unhealthy food environments in Southern Europe, including Catalonia, where Mediterranean diets are being replaced by Western eating habits. This shift is contributing to obesity and diet-related diseases. While food policies often focus on consumer choices, they overlook broader environmental factors. The aim of this study was to investigate the food environment and consumer perceptions and how these influence dietary choices.

## Methods
This study used a mixed-methods design to examine food environment perceptions among adults aged 40 and over in Catalonia, Spain. Quantitative data were collected using the modified NEMS-P-MED survey, which assessed perceptions of the food environment and sustainability. Qualitative data were gathered through Photovoice and focus groups, allowing participants to share their experiences. Statistical analyses were performed using descriptive methods, and qualitative data were analysed using content analysis.

## Results
Participants generally perceived their environment as supportive of healthy eating, with easy access to fresh fruits and vegetables. While food shopping focused on quality and variety, sustainability and cost were also considered. Convenience often outweighed health considerations, especially when eating out. Healthy food availability in stores and restaurants was positively viewed, but convenience frequently influenced decisions. Despite the availability of healthy foods, a gap remains between actual eating habits and dietary guidelines, driven by cost and taste preferences.

## Conclusion
Food choices are shaped by factors such as health, affordability, culture, and sustainability. Interventions should focus on improving the affordability and accessibility of healthy foods, but structural changes and multi-sector collaboration are essential to promote sustainable diets.

Keywords: Surveys and questionnaires; Mediterranean diet; Qualitative research, quantitative research, health behaviour; Food availability

## Introduction
In recent decades, ultra-processed, high-energy and nutrient-poor foods have become more accessible, widely marketed and often cheaper than fresh or minimally processed alternatives, leading to obesogenic and unsustainable food environments [ , ]. The food environment is defined as “the collective physical, economic, policy, and sociocultural surroundings, opportunities and conditions that influence people’s food and beverage choices and nutritional status’’ [ , ], as well as “the interface that mediates people’s food acquisition and consumption within the wider food system” [ ]. It includes both external factors, such as availability, prices and marketing, and personal factors, such as accessibility, affordability, convenience and desirability.
Food environments have evolved in such a way that they increasingly restrict access to healthy, affordable and sustainable diets. In Southern Europe, including Catalonia, dietary habits have therefore shifted from the traditional Mediterranean diet towards a more Westernized pattern, characterized by an increased consumption of processed meats, refined grains, ultra-processed foods, saturated fats and free sugars, to the detriment of fruit, vegetables, legumes, nuts and fish [ – ]. Consequently, there has been a rise in obesity and diet-related non-communicable diseases. In Europe, 53% of the population is currently overweight, with similar rates in Spain (54%), including Catalonia (50%) [ ].
Despite the impact the food environment has on health, sociocultural and environmental factors related to food are often overlooked in research [ – ]. Additionally, the predominance of animal-based foods exacerbates environmental and health issues, significantly contributing to greenhouse gas emissions, deforestation and resource depletion. Transitioning towards more plant-based diets is essential if these impacts are to be mitigated and sustainability goals achieved [ – ]. In this context, the Spanish Agency for Food Safety and Nutrition (AESAN) has updated its dietary recommendations for the Spanish population, drawing on the latest scientific evidence and adopting a perspective that integrates both health and environmental sustainability. These guidelines advocate for an increased daily intake of plant-based foods, such as fruits, vegetables, and legumes, along with a preference for whole grains and healthy fats, including olive oil and those found in oily fish. They also recommend drinking tap water whenever possible, while limiting the consumption of processed meats, saturated fats, added sugars, and salt [ ].
Current food policies predominantly emphasize “responsible consumer choices”, assuming that education and awareness of healthier options will drive behavioural change [ ]. However, this model fails to adequately account for the influence of food environments. Recent European consumer surveys show that while people are willing to adopt more sustainable eating habits, they face barriers such as cost, lack of information and limited availability of healthier options [ ].
Within this context, there has been growing international interest in how food environments might promote healthier choices, such as increased fruit and vegetable intake [ – ]. More specifically, recent studies have begun exploring how individuals perceive their food environments and how these perceptions influence eating habits [ – ].
Scientific interest in the food environment is also growing in Spain [ , – ]. However, few studies have examined how the Spanish population perceives these environments and how such perceptions affect dietary behaviours [ – ]. Notably, there has been no research on citizens' perceptions of food environments in Catalonia.
Understanding how individuals perceive their food environment is essential when it comes to developing effective policies and interventions. Such insights can help tailor strategies to improve access to healthy, affordable and sustainable diets while addressing specific sociocultural factors. Given the current research gaps, particularly in the case of Catalonia, the aim of this study was to investigate public perceptions of the food environment and how these perceptions influence dietary choices. By addressing these gaps, the study seeks to inform future policies and contribute to reducing health disparities and the environmental impact of food systems.

## Materials and methods

## Design
This study is part of the European Plan’EAT project [ ], which aims to transform food systems and environments towards greater health and sustainability. A descriptive mixed-methods design was used to explore the perception of the food environment among adults aged 40 and over in Catalonia (Spain).
The mixed-methods approach was chosen to provide a comprehensive understanding of participants' perceptions of the food environment. The quantitative component, based on the Perceived Nutrition Environment Measures Survey adapted to the Mediterranean Spanish context (NEMS-P-MED) survey [ ], offered structured data on perceptions related to food availability, accessibility, quality, and pricing. The tool was adapted for the Catalan context and expanded to include sustainability aspects such as local food availability and responsible consumption practices. Descriptive statistics (with confidence intervals) were used to summarize survey responses. Missing data were handled using listwise deletion for each specific analysis.
The qualitative component combined Photovoice and focus groups [ – ], allowing participants to visually and narratively express their experiences with the food environment. Photovoice was not a community-based participatory research approach; rather, it was a non-participatory photo-elicitation tool used to stimulate talks during focus groups. Participants took at least five photographs over two days (one weekday and one weekend), capturing food-related activities such as shopping, meal preparation, and consumption. These photographs were later discussed in focus group sessions. All discussions were audio-recorded, transcribed verbatim, and anonymized.
We conducted a thematic content analysis using a hybrid inductive–deductive approach. An initial coding framework was developed based on the research questions and Photovoice prompts, and was iteratively refined through independent coding by two researchers. Discrepancies were resolved through discussion to reach consensus. Photographs and captions were not analysed independently, but were used during focus groups to elicit discussion and contextualize participants' verbal narratives.
Data triangulation was achieved by comparing themes from the qualitative data with patterns observed in the quantitative findings. This integrative process helped to identify convergences and divergences between perceived food environments and reported behaviours, thereby enhancing the validity and richness of the study’s conclusions.

## Participants
The study targeted adults aged 40 and over residing in Catalonia, Spain, who voluntarily agreed to participate. Participants were recruited through a combination of community outreach, local advertisements, and social media platforms. A convenience sampling approach was used, and a total of 145 participants completed the survey.
The age criterion (≥ 40 years) was selected as this age group represents a larger proportion of the population in Catalonia [ ]. It was established as part of the methodological framework of the European PLAN’EAT project, from which this study derives. Within the project, each partner was assigned a specific target population group, taking into account regional demographics and project needs. Exclusion criteria included individuals under 40 and those not residing in Catalonia.
All participants completed the quantitative survey, while a purposive subsample was selected for participation in the qualitative component (Photovoice and focus groups). This subsample was selected based on participants' willingness to engage in the qualitative component, ensuring a balance of age and gender. The purposive sampling aimed to ensure a diverse range of perspectives. Focus groups were stratified by gender and shopping preferences to foster dynamic and representative discussions.

## Data collection
For the quantitative part, a modified version of the NEMS-P-MED questionnaire was used, consisting of 41 questions designed to capture perceptions of the food environment at home, in food stores and in restaurants. The questions were categorized in two main dimensions: external factors (availability, prices, vendor and product properties, marketing and regulation) and personal factors (accessibility, affordability, convenience and desirability). Additionally, the survey collected data on food-related habits, general household information and background characteristics.
The survey included a food frequency questionnaire (FFQ) section, which assessed participants' habitual consumption of 7 food groups: fruit, vegetables, meat (read, white processed and unprocessed), legumes, dairy products, nuts and eggs. The response options included: "2 or more times a day", "once a day", "5–6 times per week", "3–4 times per week", "1–2 times per week", "1–3 times per month", and "less than once a month or never."
This section allowed the assessment of whether participants’ consumption patterns met the dietary recommendations of the Spanish Agency for Food Safety and Nutrition (AESAN). The data collected through the FFQ was used to classify participants as either meeting or not meeting the AESAN guidelines for each food group.
The survey was administered online, with participants self-reporting their data between March and October 2023.
From the pool of survey participants, 11 individuals (six women and five men) were selected for the qualitative part, which involved two focus groups, each consisting of 5–6 participants to ensure gender balance. Each group was facilitated by a moderator and observer note-taker. The focus group sessions, each lasting 90 min, were structured around themes from the survey dimensions to allow for in-depth exploration of participants' perceptions.
Two focus group sessions were conducted in April 2023. During the first session, participants were introduced to the Photovoice method and asked to take five photographs that represented their food environment over the following week. They were instructed to take photos on at least two different days (one weekday and one weekend day) to capture aspects such as dining out, eating at home, grocery shopping, food preparation and food waste management.
In the second session, participants shared and discussed their photographs within the group, offering insights and reflections on various aspects of their food environment. Group discussions were centred on themes including availability and accessibility, price and affordability, cultural and social influences, the home food environment, emotional factors and personal expectations.

## Data analysis
Quantitative data were analysed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, version 29.0.0.0. Descriptive statistics, including means, standard deviations, frequencies, and percentages, were calculated for all variables where applicable, based on data type and relevance. Where appropriate, confidence intervals (CIs) were computed. CIs provide a measure of the precision of the percentage estimates, indicating the range. Within which the true population proportion is likely to lie with 95% confidence.
Only participants who completed all survey items (n = 145) were included in the analysis; therefore, listwise deletion was applied. Variables were primarily based on single items from the adapted NEMS-P-MED instrument, and no composite scales or indices were created. In order to satisfy the objectives of the PLAN'EAT project, the initial NEMS-P-MED survey was extended to incorporate sustainability measures (such as the availability of local food and eco-labels). To ensure clarity, changes were pilot tested with 20 individuals.
Qualitative data obtained from the Photovoice and focus group sessions were analysed using an inductive approach, with open coding of responses and images. The discussions were organized into key themes related to food environment perceptions, such as food availability, accessibility, pricing, and emotional barriers. Participant images played a central role in illustrating the dimensions of their experiences, and the emerging codes were categorized into a hierarchical scheme to identify common patterns. Photovoice and focus group sessions were conducted online via Google Meet and recorded using TechSmith Capture software. Audio recordings were transcribed into text using Riverside (RiversideFM Inc., 2025) transcription software. Two researchers independently analysed the transcripts, identifying key verbatim quotes and themes for further interpretation.

## Integrating quantitative and qualitative data
To integrate the quantitative and qualitative data, the "Following a Thread" technique [ ] was employed. This approach began with the quantitative data, following specific patterns or “threads” to explore related aspects through qualitative insights.
Initially, both data sets were analysed separately: quantitative data were analysed using descriptive statistics and correlations, while qualitative data were analysed through content analysis to identify subjects and patterns in participants' responses. The quantitative survey results on food environment perceptions provided the foundation for subsequent qualitative exploration, enabling a more in-depth investigation of the reasons behind the observed trends. By weaving these threads together, the study achieved a more comprehensive understanding of the factors shaping participants' perceptions of their food environment.

## Results
A total of 210 participants were recruited through social media and university channels, with 145 completing the questionnaire. The mean age was 53.1 years (SD = 9.15), with 30.3% identifying as men and 67.6% as women. Most participants (83.4%) were aged 40–64 years. Geographically, 56.6% lived in Barcelona, followed by 25.5% in Tarragona. Educationally, 75.9% had a university degree, and 75.9% were employed. The average household income was between €2,001 and €4,000. Regarding health, 80% rated their health as good or excellent. Physical activity levels varied, with 49% reporting moderate activity (see Table  ).
 In terms of food habits, 78.5% consumed fruit daily, but only 11.7% met the recommended five servings of fruits and vegetables per day. Additionally, 22.8% ate fish less than three times a month, and 8.3% consumed meat less than once a month (see Table  ).
During the focus groups discussions participants expressed a strong interest in nutritional knowledge but found food labels complex. Despite desiring healthier eating habits, taste was a more significant factor in food choices. They emphasized the social aspect of meals, particularly in line with Mediterranean dietary traditions [ ], and showed interest in diverse cuisines, including Japanese and even insects in processed forms.
Participants also discussed emotional influences on food choices, noting the challenge of making healthy decisions in supermarkets dominated by unhealthy options. Ethical considerations, such as avoiding certain meats for environmental reasons, were important in their dietary decisions.
A strong interest in sustainability emerged, with discussions focused on reducing plastic use, promoting home gardens, food sharing, and supporting locally sourced products. Participants also highlighted the importance of better food preservation and redistributing unused food within communities.

## Home food environment and consumer perception

## Availability
The study revealed a high availability of healthy food options at home, including fruit (99.3%), vegetables (99.3%), legumes (97.9%), eggs (97.9%), and dairy products (97.2%). However, less healthy items like animal fats (54.8%), sugary beverages (43.4%), and processed white meat (30.3%) were less frequently available.
Despite the high availability of fruits and vegetables at home, only 17% of study participants reported consumption levels aligned with AESAN recommendations.
The most common cooking duration was 20–40 min, with 44.8% of participants often and 28.3% almost always choosing this time frame. Regarding mealtime habits, 62.1% reported usually or always dining with family, friends, or roommates, while 50.3% indicated they either never or only occasionally ate in front of screens.
Discussions revealed the persistence of traditional gender roles, with women typically handling most of the cooking. Some participants also mentioned that television during meals provided “company” or background noise. Children influenced meal choices, especially when adapting recipes to suit their tastes. One participant noted, “When I’m alone, I cook what I like.” Despite the challenges of balancing work and cooking, family meals were highly valued. The shift to remote work during the COVID-19 pandemic enabled more family engagement in cooking and meal planning, reinforcing the home food environment as a space for connection and shared experiences.

## Retail food environment and consumer perception

## Accessibility and availability
In terms of shopping frequency, 44.1% of participants reported shopping twice a week, 30.3% once a week, 20.7% almost daily, and 3.5% once or twice a month. Most participants (88.3%) shopped at multiple establishments, with supermarkets being the most popular (74.5%). Municipal markets and food stores ranked second (57.9%), while farmers' markets were less common (15.2%). Convenience stores were the least used (1.4%).
While most participants found plant-based foods, meat, sweets, soft drinks, and snacks easily accessible, 21.3% reported difficulties accessing fresh fish. Only 14.5% found it easy to find MSC eco-labelled fish, indicating limited access to sustainably sourced fish. In contrast, 82.1% had no trouble accessing seasonal fruits and vegetables. Whole grain and refined cereals showed similar accessibility (70.3% and 80.0%, respectively).
Participants prioritized food quality (77.2%) and variety (65.5%) when shopping, followed by proximity and price (see Table  ). Taste (83.4%) and health (79.3%) were the most significant factors influencing their purchases, while 34.5% considered sustainability important (see Table  ).
 During the discussions, participants expressed that they mostly shopped for fruits and vegetables at supermarkets or local stores on a weekly basis, with a strong preference for quality and local sourcing. As one participant mentioned, “I pay attention to whether the product is local.” Local stores were favoured for their perceived freshness and trustworthiness. Most participants felt there were enough establishments to buy their regular food items without difficulty.

## Affordability and prices
Nearly half of the participants perceived fish (49.0%) and fruit and vegetables (48.2%) as being expensive. In contrast, fewer participants viewed white meat (26.2%) and red meat (35.1%) as costly. While a significant percentage of participants identified price as an important factor influencing their purchasing decisions (see Tables  and ), it was not considered the most relevant one.
Supermarkets were the primary shopping destination (74.5%), driven by convenience and price. However, qualitative data revealed a preference for local stores when quality was prioritized, with one participant mentioning, 'I pay attention to whether the product is local. This led them to choose local food stores despite higher costs. They acknowledged that ecological and healthy foods tend to be more expensive, but they still incorporate them into their diets by purchasing seasonal items. As one participant remarked, “We often have to go to big supermarkets because of the price, not because of the quality of the product.” Participants also enjoyed occasionally indulging in higher-priced artisanal products. However, the high cost of fish and the price disparity between healthy and unhealthy foods were concerns, with one participant stating, “I don’t like the fact that healthy food is more expensive than unhealthy options”.

## Marketing and regulation
Regarding marketing campaigns, only 26.2% of participants reported seeing promotions encouraging the purchase of healthy foods, with a higher prevalence observed in specialized stores (30.9%) compared to supermarkets (19.4%) and farmers' markets (9.1%).
A significant proportion of participants (35.9%) reported encountering discounts or promotions on fruits and vegetables, which was similar to the prevalence of discounts on red meats. Analysis by type of establishment revealed no significant differences: of those who found discounts on fruits and vegetables, 35.2% shopped in supermarkets, 29.8% in specialized stores, and 27.3% at farmers’ markets. For red meat discounts, 38.0% of supermarket shoppers, 32.1% of specialized store shoppers, and 31.8% of farmers’ market shoppers reported seeing promotions.
A vast majority (89.0%) of participants avoided purchasing foods typically placed near checkout lines, perceiving them as generally unhealthy (64.1%). Additionally, approximately half of the participants (49.6%) regularly checked the nutritional information on packaged foods.

## Restaurant food environment and consumer perception

## Accessibility and availability
Of all participants, 64.8% reported eating out at least once a week at various types of restaurants. The most popular choice was à la carte restaurants, frequented by 51.2% of participants, followed by other types of restaurants at 29%, and fast-food restaurants, which were visited by only 9.7%.
Overall, the results indicate that most respondents can walk to their most frequented restaurant in 10 min or less, while only a few have walking times exceeding 30 min.
A key finding is that à la carte restaurants are perceived to offer the healthiest options, with 50.6% of participants agreeing that healthy choices are available. This compares to 46.3% for other restaurant types and only 35.7% for fast-food establishments.
Regarding qualitative results, participants shared that they enjoy trying dishes they do not typically prepare at home, particularly elaborate desserts.

## Affordability and price
Regarding affordability, 50% of fast-food patrons, 56.1% of cafeteria-goers, and 40.0% of à la carte restaurant customers indicated that price is a very important factor in their dining choices.
Perceptions of the cost of healthy options varied by restaurant type. Among fast-food customers, 57.1% agreed that healthy options are more expensive, compared to 40% of à la carte diners and 48.3% of café or bar patrons.
Additionally, fast-food restaurants were perceived as less accommodating for vegetarian and vegan diners, with 50% of participants disagreeing that these options are easy to find. This perception was less pronounced among à la carte restaurant customers (41.3%) and those dining at other types of establishments (58.5%).

## Marketing and regulation
A significant majority of participants across all restaurant types (64.3% to 82.7%) reported that restaurants rarely provide nutritional information. There were mixed perceptions regarding the availability and affordability of healthy options, as well as the ease of finding vegetarian/vegan menus (see Table  ).
 

## Food characteristics and desirability
When dining out, taste is the most highly valued factor among participants, with 83.4% rating it as very important. In contrast, sustainable production is the least prioritized factor, at 22.8%. Other considerations fall in between, with healthiness rated at 51.7%, price at 45.5%, and the comfort and accessibility of the venue at 34.5%.
A significant majority of participants agreed on the importance of choosing healthy foods at restaurants, with 73.3% for à la carte restaurants, 61.0% for cafeterias or bars, and 64.3% for fast-food establishments.
Regarding prioritization of food quality by restaurant type, 85.7% of fast-food customers indicated that food quality is important, compared to 69.3% of à la carte diners and 61.0% of cafeteria-goers.
In terms of food variety, 35.7% of fast-food restaurant patrons consider it very important, followed by 32.0% of those dining at à la carte restaurants and 29.3% of cafeteria customers.

## Mixed methods integration
After separately analysing the quantitative and qualitative data, we compared both strands to identify points of convergence, divergence, and complementarity. To facilitate this integration, we created a side-by-side joint display (see Tables , , , ), organized by thematic categories. For greater clarity and readability, the table has been separated into thematic sub-tables corresponding to each major topic, allowing for a more focused comparative analysis of the findings from each data source.
 The quantitative and qualitative data align in showing a high consumption and prioritization of fruits and vegetables. Commensality is consistently valued across both data sets. However, while participants express nutritional concern in surveys, qualitative insights reveal that local origin and quality often take precedence over healthfulness, indicating partial convergence.
Findings from both methods are aligned in showing a strong preference for supermarkets, largely due to convenience. Price is a major factor, but qualitative data adds nuance by showing a willingness to pay more for freshness and quality. Both sets confirm attention to nutrition labels, though qualitative insights point to a barrier in label comprehension.
There is clear convergence between data sources regarding the social function of dining out and the high value placed on taste. Price sensitivity is also reflected across both methods, especially in the context of fast food and perceptions of the cost of healthy options.
There is moderate convergence: sustainability is a meaningful concern in both data strands, especially in personal food choices. Qualitative data expands on motivations (e.g., animal welfare), and highlights perceived gaps in availability and ease of making sustainable choices.

## Discussion
This study contributes to a better understanding of the food environment and consumer perception among adults over 40 in Catalonia. The use of a mixed-methods approach provides a comprehensive and nuanced perspective on the interaction between cultural and social contexts, emotional factors, and sustainability concerns that influence food choices. This information may be useful for the design of multisectoral interventions among middle-aged and older adults. The observed preference for supermarkets over traditional markets aligned with other studies in urban Spanish contexts [ ], but may undermine adherence to the Mediterranean, as supermarkets often use marketing strategies to promote processed foods [ ].
Building upon existing research that has explored food environments and population perceptions [ – , , ], our study contributes a unique perspective through its mixed-methods approach. While some previous studies have also utilized mixed methods to assess the perception of food environments [ , , ], our application in the Catalan context provides valuable new insights. Specifically, our research offers a more comprehensive understanding of the interplay between perceived availability, affordability, cultural norms, and taste preferences in shaping dietary choices within this specific Mediterranean region. Furthermore, it highlights the nuanced impact of the shift towards supermarket dominance on the perception and consumption of both healthy and unhealthy foods, including the surprising disconnect between the availability of Mediterranean diet staples and their actual consumption.
In the United States, a study on perceptions of the food environment found that most of the female, white, retired participants were food secure and independent in their daily activities [ ]. Similarly, a study in Cardinia Shire, Australia, conducted during the COVID-19 lockdown, explored perceptions of the food environment, although it included fewer questions and excluded restaurants from its scope [ ]. Our research identified supermarkets as being key in promoting healthy eating. A study in Madrid also revealed differences between perceived and quantitatively analysed food environments [ ].
Evidence from previous studies has suggested causal relationships between perceived food environments at the community and consumer levels and dietary intake [ ]. Although descriptive in nature, our study also provides insights into how availability, accessibility, affordability, and quality, along with factors such as education, income, and gender are related to food choices. In the case of adults over 40 in Catalonia, despite the high perceived availability of healthy foods, cultural preferences and perceived costs act as significant barriers to adequate consumption.
Many of the participants in our study indicated that despite the easy accessibility and wide availability of healthy foods, they did not meet local dietary recommendations for fruit and vegetable consumption. Participants reported high accessibility and availability of both healthy and unhealthy products. Consistent with another Spanish study, fruit and vegetables were the most commonly found items in Catalan households [ ]. However, compared to the latter study, ours showed a higher prevalence of Mediterranean diet staples, such as legumes, and participants perceived unhealthy products like sugary drinks as being less prevalent.
Furthermore, consistent with broader trends observed in Spain [ , , ], our study confirms the dominance of supermarkets as the primary food shopping destination for adults as from 40 in Catalonia, significantly overshadowing traditional food stores and municipal markets. This finding directly contributes to our new insight regarding the ongoing cultural transition in food environments. Historically, the Mediterranean diet was intrinsically linked to local markets and smaller shops [ , , ], often perceived as key sources of healthy food due to closer relationships with vendors [ ]. The observed shift towards supermarkets, driven by factors like competitive pricing and wider selection [ , ], suggests a potential disruption of these traditional patterns and may partly explain the observed disconnect between the availability of Mediterranean staples and their actual consumption, as individuals may be influenced by the broader product range and marketing strategies prevalent in supermarkets.
Despite the majority of products that form the basis of the Mediterranean diet being present in households, including fruit, vegetables and legumes, their consumption does not align with the recommendations set forth by the Spanish Agency for Food Safety and Nutrition [ ]. Only 11.7% of participants reached the recommended intake of vegetables, 19.3% the recommended intake of fish and 23.4% the recommended intake of legumes. These consumption data suggest a shift towards a more Westernized dietary pattern, influenced by demographic, economic and social changes [ , ]. This trend persists despite the Mediterranean diet being recognized as one of the healthiest lifestyles in the world [ , ].
Consistent with existing literature highlighting taste [ ] and cost [ ] as significant drivers of food choices, our research in Catalonia provides a nuanced understanding of how these factors specifically contribute to lower consumption of healthy foods within this context. We found that the reported low intake of vegetables and legumes is linked to participants' perceptions of these foods as being relatively costly and, importantly, unappealing in terms of taste. This finding directly supports our broader insight that dietary choices are shaped by a complex interplay of factors beyond mere availability. The emphasis on the pleasure of eating, underscored by gastronomic principles and other research [ ], appears to outweigh the accessibility of these healthy options. While studies suggest that increased exposure can improve vegetable consumption [ , ], our findings indicate that initial taste perceptions and cost considerations remain significant hurdles in the Catalan adult population.
These results align with the Global Food System (GFS) model [ ]. In our context, society would appear to prioritize food sovereignty, nutritional adequacy and taste over food globalization (increased availability) [ ].
Half of the Spanish population believes that rising food prices have changed their eating habits, particularly in relation to fish consumption, with 37% reporting changes in their fish consumption habits between 2008 and 2023 [ ]. Data show that dairy products and fresh fruit are the most significant items in shopping baskets by volume, while meat and fish represent the highest expenditures in value. Despite decreased fish consumption, meat remains a primary protein source for the Catalan population.
Government regulations affecting pricing, and notably taxes, significantly influence the purchase of fresh fruit, vegetables and meat [ ]. A potential solution to this trend could involve adjusting prices through synchronized taxation, considering the varying price elasticity of different products, as discussed in theoretical studies in Java [ ] and New Zealand [ ].
To address these barriers to healthier food consumption, policy interventions are needed. Examples of specific policy actions include targeted subsidies for healthy foods like fish, fruits, and vegetables to enhance their affordability. Retailers could be offered tax incentives to ensure they maintain a minimum percentage of healthy options in their offerings. Public education policies could focus on school-based nutrition programs and media campaigns that correct misconceptions about the cost and taste of healthy eating. Behavioural measures could involve regulations for the strategic placement of healthy foods in stores, clearer nutritional labelling, and offering smaller portion sizes to help consumers make healthier choices. Regular price monitoring of key healthy foods could inform regulatory actions, such as price caps, and price elasticity analysis would help identify the foods most sensitive to price changes, guiding dynamic pricing policies. Additionally, support for traditional markets could be achieved through grants or reduced fees to preserve their cultural significance, and policies could ensure community involvement in designing culturally appropriate interventions. Research and innovation funding in sustainable agriculture and food technology could also help develop affordable, nutritious products. Finally, translating research findings into actionable policies is essential to achieve measurable and adaptable public health outcomes. It is important to note that these are potential policy actions that require further research to assess their feasibility, effectiveness, and context-specific impacts.

## Strengths, limitations and future considerations
A key strength of this study is its mixed methods approach, which combines quantitative and qualitative data. This provides a comprehensive understanding of participants’ food behaviours and preferences. However, the qualitative component was limited, consisting of two focus group discussions with a total of 11 participants. While these discussions enriched the interpretation of quantitative results, the qualitative data should be interpreted with caution due to its limited depth and breadth. Future studies could expand qualitative data collection to include more diverse participants and settings to capture a wider range of perspectives.
Additionally, the sample size of 145 participants may limit the generalizability of the findings to the broader Catalonian population. The overrepresentation of medium and high socioeconomic levels, particularly university-educated individuals (75.9%), could introduce bias, potentially skewing perceptions of food affordability and accessibility, as lower-income groups likely face different or greater barriers. This homogeneity may limit the representativeness of the findings for populations with lower educational attainment and fewer resources. Additionally, the sample was drawn primarily from urban areas. The majority of participants were urban residents, which limits the generalizability of the findings to rural Catalonia, where food access dynamics and cultural factors may differ significantly.
Relying on self-reported data for dietary habits introduces potential biases, such as social desirability or recall bias. Future studies could incorporate more objective measures, such as pantry audits or food purchase receipts, to complement self-reported information and reduce potential bias. Moreover, as many of the findings are descriptive, the study should be considered exploratory in nature. This limits the ability to draw causal inferences or generalize findings beyond the sample.
Future research should explore the paradox of accessible healthy food versus low adherence to dietary recommendations, particularly regarding fruit, vegetables and fish. This includes examining barriers and facilitators influencing food choices, such as perceptions of food cost, cultural influences and socioeconomic factors.

## Conclusions
The findings of this study reveal that the modified NEMS-P-MED questionnaire and Photovoice technique effectively capture participants’ perceptions of their local food environment within a shared cultural and socioeconomic context. They suggest that food choices are influenced by a complex interplay between health consciousness, affordability, cultural practices and sustainability concerns. Integrating quantitative and qualitative data highlight the importance of taste, social experiences and ethical considerations in food selection.
Despite the high availability of healthy foods such as vegetables, fruit and legumes, there is a noticeable gap between actual food-related behaviours and adherence to Mediterranean diet guidelines. This stems from perceived costs and hedonic preferences, resulting in less optimal dietary practices.
Comprehensive interventions are needed to address these issues, including adjusting food prices and improving the accessibility and affordability of healthy and sustainable foods. However, education alone is insufficient; structural changes and a multi-stakeholder approach are necessary to promote sustainable and healthy diets. To address these barriers, a wide-ranging set of policy interventions are required, which should include structural changes to improve the affordability and accessibility of healthy foods. Specific examples of these measures have been proposed, including targeted subsidies for healthier options (e.g., fruits, vegetables, and fish), tax incentives for retailers to offer a broader range of nutritious products, public education campaigns to correct misconceptions about healthy food costs and taste, and supporting traditional markets through grants or reduced fees to preserve their cultural significance.
Understanding the environmental, social, cultural and individual factors that affect food choices is crucial for designing effective interventions that align with the United Nations Sustainable Development Goals. The implementation of evidence-based, multi-faceted policy measures, involving collaboration between the government, food industry, and local communities, will be key to fostering a more sustainable and healthy food system.
