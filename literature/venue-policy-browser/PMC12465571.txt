## Background
Forcibly displaced populations, refugees, and internally displaced persons (IDPs), and migrants face distinct challenges, increasing vulnerability to HIV infection and substance use disorders. This study assessed the associations between psychological distress, alcohol consumption, substance use, and HIV risk behaviour among these populations in Nigeria.

## Methods
This was a secondary analysis of the data of a cross-sectional study, collected in five states in Nigeria (Benue, Borno, Cross Rivers, Taraba, and Yobe), in December 2024. Data collected included the dependent variable (HIV risk behavior), independent variables (psychological distress level, substance use, and alcohol consumption), and confounding variables (age, sex at birth, sexual orientation, educational level, marital status, employment/occupational status, and religious status). A stepwise approach was used in the building of four models. Model 1 presented the unadjusted bivariate analysis. Model 2 was the adjusted multivariate logistic regression. Model 3 assessed the interaction effect of psychological distress level, alcohol consumption, substance use, and being IDP on HIV risk behaviour, and Model 4 assessed the same interaction for refugees.

## Results
The sample of 3,211 participants included 1237 (38.5%) migrants, 1497 (46.6%) IDPs, and 477 (14.9%) refugees. Among participants, moderate (AOR: 1.46; 95% CI: 1.38–1.56; p < 0.001) and high (AOR: 1.70; 95% CI: 1.57–1.85; p = 0.01) psychological distress significantly increased the odds of high-risk sexual behaviour. IDPs had the highest odds of engaging in risky behaviour compared to migrants (AOR: 1.59; 95% CI: 1.32–1.92; p = 0.02). Substance use further amplified risk, particularly for IDPs (OR: 3.17; 95% CI: 2.11–3.72; p < 0.001) and refugees (OR: 2.09; 95% CI: 2.02–2.37; p < 0.001) compared to migrants. No significant association was found between alcohol consumption and HIV risk behaviour.

## Conclusion
This study highlights the compounded vulnerability of IDPs and refugees, driven by psychological distress and substance use. Findings underscore the urgent need for integrated interventions addressing mental health, substance use, and HIV prevention tailored to forcibly displaced populations in Nigeria.

## Introduction
Forcibly displaced persons - refugees, internally displaced persons (IDPs) - and migrants have faced challenges that heighten their susceptibility to various health risks, including HIV infection [ , ]. Factors including displacement-induced poverty, low health literacy, restricted healthcare access, and heightened exposure to sexual violence collectively increase vulnerability [ ]. In addition, for refugees and migrants, poor access to health care may result from systemic barriers that lead to exclusion [ ], stigma and discrimination, and language and cultural differences [ ].
To ensure conceptual clarity, this study adopts internationally recognized definitions for the key population groups examined. Forcibly displaced persons refer broadly to individuals compelled to leave their homes due to conflict, violence, persecution, or disasters, and include both refugees and IDPs [ ]. Refugees are those who have crossed an international border and cannot return to their country due to a well-founded fear of persecution; they are protected under international law [ ]. IDPs, by contrast, flee similar threats but remain within their country’s borders and lack equivalent international legal protections, relying instead on national governments [ ]. Migrants, in the context of this study, are primarily voluntary economic movers seeking better livelihoods, not fleeing direct threats to life or freedom [ ]. These distinctions are essential, as they shape legal status, access to services, trauma exposure, and vulnerability, factors that directly influence differences in HIV risk across groups.
Refugees and migrants encounter significant sexual health challenges, including restricted access to contraceptives [ ] and heightened exposure to sexual violence [ ]. Unwanted pregnancies are prevalent [ ], and access to safe abortion services is often inadequate [ ]. Mental health issues ranging from posttraumatic stress disorder [ ] to depression and persistent stress are widespread and heighten cortisol levels, impair immune function, and raise susceptibility to infections such as HIV [ ], suicidal ideation, severe anxiety, and increased somatic symptoms [ ]. Recent meta-analyses confirm disproportionately high rates of posttraumatic stress disorder and depression among displaced populations [ , ], with IDPs in Nigeria exhibiting severe symptoms linked to conflict exposure [ ]. This population also faces a high risk of HIV infection, accounting for 40% of new HIV diagnoses in Europe [ ]. Adolescent girls and young women are particularly vulnerable to poor sexual and reproductive health outcomes and HIV infection, often due to forced migration [ , ]. Migration can also exacerbate HIV-related sexual risk behaviours [ ]. In addition, there is a documented connection between trauma, sexual violence, and HIV among migrants and refugees from low- and middle-income countries [ ].
The health challenges faced by IDPs are less understood than those faced by migrants and refugees [ ]. However, the scale of internal displacement due to conflict and violence far exceeds that of refugee flows: by the end of 2020, the global IDP population reached 68.3 million, nearly 1.5 times the number of refugees (43.4 million) [ ]. This increase in conflict-driven displacement, along with a growing IDP population, is concentrated primarily in Africa, the Middle East, and Latin America [ ], with 99.5% of conflict-affected IDPs residing in low- and middle-income countries [ ]. Forced displacement exposes IDPs to new risks, including hazardous conditions during transit or in settlements, the trauma of displacement, and the loss of assets and social support networks [ ]. Unlike refugees, IDPs lack formal international legal protection, potentially placing them at greater risk for health issues [ ]. Although national laws and African treaties on IDP protection have been established, their health risks remain exacerbated by the losses, hazards of displacement, and economic hardships they endure [ ].
In Nigeria, the ongoing insurgency and terrorism have led to widespread displacement, economic instability, and social disruption [ , ], creating an environment where psychological distress, substance use, and risky sexual behaviours are prevalent [ ]. These factors are interconnected and can significantly increase the likelihood of HIV transmission, particularly in settings where access to healthcare and preventive services is limited.
Psychological distress, often resulting from exposure to violence, loss, and displacement, has been linked to increased engagement in risky behaviours, including unprotected sex and multiple sexual partnerships [ , ]. Negative coping strategies such as substance use and alcohol consumption are known to impair judgment and reduce inhibition, further increasing the risk of HIV transmission [ , ]. Understanding the interplay between these factors is crucial for designing effective interventions to mitigate HIV risk among crisis-affected populations.
Despite the growing body of research on HIV risk behaviours in sub-Saharan Africa, few studies have focused on the specific dynamics within crisis-affected communities in Nigeria, particularly IDPs [ ]. There are no studies on HIV and refugees and migrants. Existing literature on IDPs, migrants, and refugees has primarily examined HIV risk in singular displacement groups, overlooking the compounded effects of psychological distress, substance use, and displacement status on sexual risk behaviours. Furthermore, the unique vulnerabilities of IDPs, who lack international legal protection [ ] and often reside in settings with extreme instability [ ], remain understudied compared to refugees and migrants.
This study aims to fill this gap. The primary objective of this study is to examine the associations between psychological distress, alcohol consumption, substance use, and HIV risk behaviour among forcefully displaced populations in Nigeria. Specifically, the study sought to (i) assess the proportion of IDPs, refugees, and migrants in Nigeria who experience psychological distress, alcohol consumption, and substance use; (ii) assess the associations between psychological distress and HIV risk behaviour, and how the associations may be moderated by alcohol consumption and substance use; and (iii) assess the differences in HIV risk behaviour across populations (IDPs, refugees, and migrants).

## Methods

## Partnerships and collaborations
The assessment engaged diverse stakeholders to ensure inclusivity, community participation, and national ownership. A virtual meeting on November 22, 2023, with UN agencies and research partners refined the methodology, adopting a deep-dive approach and setting site profile deadlines. A national stakeholder meeting on February 2, 2024, gathered key national and international actors to align objectives, inclusion criteria, and timelines, emphasizing vulnerable groups and conflict-affected areas. State-level meetings in July–August 2024 across five states reviewed and refined the protocol, addressing feasibility and logistics, with community leaders contributing to ethical approvals and mobilization. The National Agency for the Control of AIDS formed a Steering Committee for strategic oversight, technical support, and policy guidance. With its input, the study was revised to a cross-sectional design, sample sizes recalculated, new partners engaged, and state-level preparations fast-tracked.

## Study design
This is a secondary analysis of a pre-existing dataset generated by a primary cross-sectional survey in which refugees, IDPs, and migrants were recruited from five states in Nigeria with high populations of the target groups. The main study was conducted to assess the HIV response needs of refugees, IDPs, and migrants in Nigeria.

## Study locations
The assessment was conducted in Borno, Taraba, Yobe, Cross River, and Benue States, which were chosen because of the large number of refugees, IDPs, and migrants resident in the states. Recruitment sites for participants were determined during state-level stakeholder meetings, considering population data, areas of concentration for target groups, and security factors.
In Borno State, the recruitment sites were Jere Local Government Area (El-muskim Camp, Madinato Camp, Al-Yakub Camp, and Ahmed Grema for refugees, IDPs, and migrants), Maiduguri metropolis (Mashidimami Camp, Garba Buzu Camp, and Doro Camp for refugees, IDPs, and migrants), and Konduga Local Government Area (Shuwari Camp for refugees, IDPs, and migrants).
In Taraba State, the recruitment sites were at Bali Local Government Area (Bali Camp for refugees, IDPs, and migrants), Sarduna Local Government Area (Gembu refugees and migrants Settlement), and Adro-Kola Local Government Area (Kuffia Jalingo IDP settlement for IDPs).
In Yobe State, the recruitment sites were Geidam Local Government Area (Angwan Kwori, Angwan Kanti, and Geidam Specialist Hospital for refugees), Bada Local Government Area (R4 Camp, Garin Alkali Gashua, Garin Lamido, Gashua Specialist Hospital for migrants), and Damaturu Local Government Area (Murfa Kalam, Nayinawa Bulabulin, Mohammed Gombe Farm Camp, Mai Mala Abuja, and Kukareta for IDPs).
In Cross River State, the recruitment sites were in Ogoja Local Government Area (Adagom 1 Refugee Settlement, Adagom 3 Refugee Settlement, and Ukende Refugee Settlement for refugees only).
In Benue State, the recruitment sites were at Kwande Local Government Area (Ikyogen Refugees and Migrants Settlement for refugees and migrants) and Guma Local Government Area (Mega IDP Camp, Daudu IDP Camp, and Abagena IDP Camp for IDPs).

## Study population
The study recruited refugees, IDPs, and migrants aged 0 years and above. Seeds were also included to recruit key populations (men who have sex with men, female sex workers, people who inject drugs, transgender individuals, and people in prisons and other closed settings) and people living with HIV.
Study participants had to give consent to participate, and parent/carer givers had to give consent for children and young adolescents (0–17 years) to participate. Assent was obtained from adolescents aged 12–17 years. Individuals deemed “not well enough to participate” (severe physical/mental health issues) were excluded from study participation.

## Sample size
The Yamane formula [ ] for calculating sample size for the primary study was used to ensure statistically valid representation of the population. With a total population (N) of 1,036,959 and a margin of error (e) of 5%, the calculated minimum sample size was 1,928. To enhance representativeness, particularly across states with different camp densities and demographic compositions, the base sample was proportionally weighted according to each state’s share of the total population. This yielded a final total weighted sample size of 3,856. Proportional stratification by age group further ensured the inclusion of key population segments—infants, children, adolescents, and adults—reflecting their estimated distribution within each state’s IDP population. This approach enhances the external validity of the findings and ensures reliable age-disaggregated analysis across diverse displacement settings.
The study recruited 5236 study participants (1052 from Benue State, 974 from Borno State, 1082 from Cross River State, 1078 from Taraba State, and 1050 from Yobe State). Since this study focused on HIV-related sexual risk behaviour, we extracted data only for individuals with sexual experience. To ensure the temporality of the outcome variable, we further restricted the analysis to 3211 respondents who had engaged in sexual activity within the last three months.

## Study procedures
Participants were recruited via non-probabilistic, purposive sampling methods—venue-based and snowball sampling—to reach marginalized populations effectively. Snowball sampling utilizes community networks for culturally sensitive recruitment, whereas venue-based sampling involves community leaders connecting participants with data collectors. The respondents received coupons to invite up to five peers, ensuring a broader subpopulation reach.
State-assigned monitors supported the data collectors, ensured participant diversity across locations and socioeconomic groups, and provided logistical aid. The participants were contacted once, and informed consent was obtained before data collection. Consent forms were read aloud, and if agreed upon, the interviewer signed. Survey responses were collected via tablet/phone-assisted self-interview or personal interview via an online tool, with data securely stored via encrypted Secure Sockets Layer links to maintain confidentiality.

## Recruitment and training of the study team
The study team comprised site leaders from five states, a study coordinator, and three research assistants per state, selected based on the Institute of Public Health partners’ past engagement and project delivery. In addition, three community entry leaders and one staff member of the participating State AIDS Control Agency functioning as a monitor were identified for each state and trained on the protocol, recruitment, and mobilization.
After the study protocol was ethically approved, the site leaders received online training in November 2023, which was repeated in November 2024 and covered study protocols, instruments, ethics, and communication. Research assistants were similarly trained in November 2024. Each state team held an online one-hour sensitization meeting with community leaders and the monitoring officer, followed by a one-day site-level training led by the state team, to plan the data collection process.

## Development and field testing of study questionnaire
The tool was uploaded online and pilot-tested with community members to ensure cultural appropriateness and sensitivity. Stakeholder feedback was gathered between January 29, 2024, and February 2, 2024, to ensure the tool’s relevance and effectiveness in capturing comprehensive research insights.
The structured questionnaire focused on: (i) access to and use of health and HIV services; (ii) availability and adequacy of essential health supplies; (iii) barriers to service delivery and uptake; and (iv) experiences of human rights violations, including gender-based violence. It was developed using validated tools from past Nigeria Demographic Health Surveys for demographic and sexual/reproductive health data, as well as community-based HIV service delivery tools used for community-centered decision-making in Nigeria’s CoP response.

## Languages and translations
The survey was offered in English and translated into key regional languages such as Yoruba, Ibibio, and Hausa to increase participation. For dialects not covered, data collectors provided real-time translations with local leaders to ensure accuracy, including sensitive terms, a method used in prior surveys such as the National HIV/AIDS and Reproductive Health Survey [ , ] and the Integrated Biological and Behavioural Surveillance Survey [ , ] in Nigeria. The translations were performed by native speakers with technical expertise and retranslated into English for consistency, and the research team resolved discrepancies. Pilot testing allowed user feedback, ensuring that translations matched the original English in meaning and nuance.

## Study variables
The dependent variable is HIV risk behaviour, which is computed based on responses to three key questions regarding the respondent’s sexual practices in the last three months: (1) the number of sexual partners, (2) condom use during every sexual encounter, and (3) their response if a partner refused to use a condom. Respondents who, within the last three months, had no more than one sexual partner, consistently used a condom in all sexual encounters, and would either refuse sex or insist on their partner being tested if they refused to use a condom were classified as having “no HIV sexual risk [0].” All other respondents—those with multiple partners, inconsistent condom use, or a willingness to engage in unprotected sex despite a partner’s refusal to use a condom—were classified as having “HIV sexual risk [ ].”
The independent variables in this study are psychological distress level, substance use, and alcohol consumption.
Psychological distress was measured using a 5-item scale assessing the frequency of symptoms (e.g., depression, anxiety, suicidal ideation) over the past 6 months. Responses were scored on a 5-point scale (never [0] to all the time [ ]). The total score (range: 0–20) was categorized as (1) low distress: Score ≤ (mean − SD); (2) moderate distress: Score between (mean − SD) and (mean + SD); and (3) high distress: Score ≥ (mean + SD).
Substance use was defined as self-reported use of any psychoactive substances known to impair judgment and reduce inhibition (e.g., cannabis, cocaine, methamphetamine, opioids, sedatives, or hallucinogens) within the past 6 months. Respondents were classified as: (1) Never: No substance use in the past 6 months; or 92) Used: Any substance use during this period.
Alcohol consumption was assessed using the question: “How often do you consume alcoholic drinks?” Responses were categorized as (i) Never: No alcohol consumption in the past 6 months; (ii) Rarely: Less than once per month; and (3) Commonly: At least once per month.
Confounding variables were: age group (< 25, 25–34, 35–44, and 45+), Sex at birth (male and female), Sexual orientation (heterosexual and non-heterosexual), educational level (no formal education, qur’anic school, primary, secondary, and tertiary), marital status (single, married, widowed, cohabitation and separated/divorced), employment/occupational status (unskilled, unemployed, student, skilled, and business), and religious status (Islam, Catholic, Other Christians, Traditional and other religion).

## Data analysis
Before conducting the regression analysis, the data were evaluated for multicollinearity via the variance inflation factor and tolerance tests. The variance inflation factor was computed for all the independent variables, with a value greater than 10 indicating potential multicollinearity. Tolerance values lower than 0.10 were also considered indicative of problematic multicollinearity. These tests ensured that the predictor variables were not highly correlated and did not distort the regression results.
Bivariate logistic regression was employed to evaluate the association between psychological distress levels and HIV-related sexual risk behaviour, with interaction terms used to assess how the relationship between distress and sexual risk behaviour was moderated by populations (refugees, IDPs, and migrants).
A stepwise approach was used in the model-building process to systematically examine the relationships between the independent variables and HIV-related sexual risk behaviour. Given the binary nature of the outcome variable (0 and 1), logistic regression models were estimated. Model 1 presented the bivariate analysis, where each independent variable was analyzed separately against the outcome variable to establish initial associations. Model 2 was a multivariate logistic regression that included all independent variables simultaneously to assess their collective influence on HIV-related sexual risk behaviour, adjusting for potential confounders. Model 3 introduced interaction terms to examine whether the relationship between psychological distress level and HIV risk behaviour was moderated by alcohol consumption, substance use, and being internally displaced (compared with the settled population). Model 4 extended this analysis by assessing the interaction effect of psychological distress level, alcohol consumption, substance use, and being a refugee (compared with the settled population) on HIV risk behaviour. This approach allows for a nuanced understanding of how distress, substance use, and displacement status interact to shape HIV-related sexual risk behaviour.
The interaction term (Psychological distress × Population group) evaluates whether the effect of psychological distress on HIV risk behaviour is significantly different across the three population groups (refugees, IDPs, and migrants). If the interaction term was statistically significant, it suggests that the population group moderated the relationship between psychological distress and HIV risk behaviour. The adjusted odds ratios (AORs) derived from the logistic regression indicate the strength and direction of these relationships. Statistical significance was determined at p < 0.05, and all regression models were assessed for model fit using Hosmer-Lemeshow goodness-of-fit tests. All analyses were conducted using Stata 18.0.

## Ethical considerations
Ethics clearance were obtained from the Institute of Public Health (IPHOAU/12/2408), and Ministries of Health in Benue (MOH/STA/204/260), Borno (190/2023), Cross River (CRS/MH/HREC/023/Vol 1/280 and CRS/MH/HREC/2024/Vol 2/022), Taraba (TRSHREC/2023/APP/034 and TRSHREC/2024/APP/034), and Yobe (MOH/GEN/747/Vol 1) States. The study adhered to ethical guidelines, ensuring informed consent, confidentiality, and anonymity. The study ensured diverse gender representation, conducted gender-sensitive needs assessments, and tailored HIV response activities to address specific gender needs. Data collectors were trained on gender sensitivity and inclusivity.
The study prioritized safety and data security, using lessons from previous surveys and consultations to inform design. It was conducted anonymously, with no personal identifiers, and unique codes were used to protect confidentiality. Responses were designed to prevent harm or exploitation.
Data were collected via a secure secure Sockets Layer-encrypted connection, with no personal identifiers, emails, or phone numbers needed. Data in transit was encrypted using transport layer security protocols, which comply with the General Data Protection Regulation and privacy shield standards. No geolocation or internet protocol addresses were retained, and all precautions were taken to ensure participant anonymity, confidentiality, and protection from discrimination or stigmatization.

## Results
Sociodemographic characteristics are summarized in Table  . Among 3,211 participants, 477 (14.9%) were refugees, 1,497 (46.6%) were IDPs, and 1,237 (38.5%) were migrants. The mean age was 34.9 ± 12.4 years, ranging from 18 to 65 years. In addition, 2050 (63.8%) were assigned female at birth, 2938 (91.5%) were heterosexual, and 1907 (59.4%) had at least secondary education. High psychological distress was reported by 783 (24.4%) participants, and 345 (10.7%) participants used substances.
Table  also shows that in Model 1, respondents aged 45 years having greater odds of engaging in HIV risk behaviours than those under 25 years (OR:1.73; 95% CI: 1.39–2.16; p = 0.01); individuals with primary (OR: 1.95; 95% CI: 1.56–1.98; p = 0.01), secondary (OR:1.67; 95% CI: 1.54–1.97; p < 0.001), and tertiary (OR:1.15; 95% CI: 1.06–1.76; p = 0.03) education had greater odds of high-risk sexual behaviour than those with no formal education; and compared with their single counterparts, married respondents had greater odds of engaging in HIV sexual risk behaviors (OR:1.61; 95% CI:1.36–1.90; p = 0.01).
Model 2 demonstrated that HIV risk behaviour varied significantly across different population groups. IDPs presented the highest risk, having greater odds of engaging in high-risk sexual behaviour than migrants (AOR: 1.59; 95% CI: 1.32–1.92; p = 0.02). In addition, psychological distress was strongly associated with HIV risk behaviour: respondents who experienced moderate (AOR:1.46; 95% CI:1.38, 1.56; P < 0.001) and high (AOR: 1.70; 95% CI: 1.57, 1.85; p = 0.01) levels of psychological distress had greater odds of engaging in high-risk sexual behaviour than did those with low psychological distress levels.
Model 3 revealed that IDPs with moderate (AOR: 1.30; 95% CI: 1.02–1.92; p = 0.03) and high (AOR: 1.60; 95% CI: 1.17–2.19; p = 0.01) levels of psychological distress had greater odds of high-risk sexual behaviour than migrants with low psychological distress. Furthermore, IDPs who used substances had greater odds of engaging in high-risk sexual behaviour (OR: 3.17; 95% CI: 2.11–3.72; p < 0.001) than migrants who did not use substances.
In Model 4, refugees with moderate (AOR: 1.07; 95% CI: 1.03–1.55; p = 0.04) and high (AOR:1.12; 95% CI: 1.09–1.86; p = 0.02) psychological distress had greater odds of high-risk sexual behaviour than migrants with low psychological distress. In addition, refugees who used substances had greater odds of engaging in high-risk sexual behaviour than migrants who did not use substances (OR: 2.09; 95% CI: 2.02–2.37; p < 0.001).

 

## Discussion
This study explored the associations between psychological distress, alcohol consumption, substance use, and HIV risk behaviour among refugees, IDPs, and migrants in Nigeria. The findings revealed that psychological distress was highly prevalent, with approximately one in three participants experiencing moderate distress and approximately one in four reporting high distress. Substance use was reported by approximately one in ten respondents, whereas one in three consumed alcohol. IDPs presented the highest levels of psychological distress and substance use, indicating their heightened vulnerability. In addition, a strong association was found between psychological distress and HIV risk behaviour: participants with moderate and high distress had significantly greater odds of engaging in HIV risk behaviour, and this odds was 70% greater for those with high distress than for those with low distress. Substance use further compounds this risk, particularly among IDPs and refugees. Furthermore, HIV risk behaviour varies significantly across different population groups. IDPs were more likely than refugees to engage in high-risk sexual behaviour. Those with high levels of psychological distress and substance use were more likely to engage in HIV risk behaviour than other groups. Compared with migrants, refugees with moderate psychological distress and substance use were twice as likely to engage in risky sexual behaviour.
This study addresses significant gaps in the literature by providing the first comprehensive analysis of the syndemic interplay between psychological distress, substance use, and HIV risk behaviours among refugees, IDPs, and migrants in Nigeria, a setting marked by protracted conflict and large-scale displacement. Unlike prior studies focusing on single displacement categories or stable populations, our comparative approach reveals critical distinctions: IDPs face the highest risk due to structural neglect and residency in conflict zones, while substance use amplifies distress-driven risk behaviours most severely among refugees and IDPs. These findings underscore that displacement status itself (especially internal displacement) is an independent social determinant of HIV risk, beyond mental health factors alone. By quantifying these relationships across diverse humanitarian contexts in five Nigerian states, this study provides actionable evidence for integrating mental health support and harm reduction into HIV programs targeting displaced populations—a need previously inferred but not empirically validated in West Africa.
This study has several other strengths, including its status as the first investigation of HIV risk behavior among refugees and migrants in Nigeria and West Africa, and one of the few studies on HIV risk behaviour among IDPs globally. Therefore, this study fills a critical data gap in knowledge about HIV risk behavior among displaced populations, providing valuable insights for policy and intervention planning. The collaboration with government and UN agencies and NGOs ensured inclusivity, credibility, and policy relevance, reducing institutional bias and improving acceptance of recommendations. The involvement of community leaders and key populations also fostered trust, participation, and smooth data collection. In addition, conducting the study across five states with high populations of refugees, IDPs, and migrants ensured that the study captured diverse humanitarian contexts, enhancing its generalizability to similar crisis settings. Pilot testing of research tools improved clarity, cultural appropriateness, and response accuracy.
This study, however, has some limitations. Self-reported data may have recall and social desirability bias, potentially leading to underreporting of HIV service access, stigma, and risk behaviours. Although this study was conducted in states with high displacement, the findings may not be fully representative of refugees, IDPs, and migrants in other regions. Security challenges in Borno limited data collection in hard-to-reach areas, and sampling bias may have excluded urban displaced persons and informal settlers. The cross-sectional design provides only a snapshot, limiting the assessment of changes in HIV service uptake and barriers over time.
Despite these limitations, the study offers critical insights into HIV service delivery in humanitarian settings. It revealed critical syndemic interactions between psychological distress, substance use, and HIV risk behaviour among forcibly displaced populations in Nigeria. Three interrelated themes are key to understanding these dynamics: the connection between mental health and HIV risk, highlighting how distress and substance use influence both behavioral and biological pathways; the structural vulnerability of IDPs, distinguishing their unique risks compared to refugees and migrants; and the implications for interventions, emphasizing the need for integrated, trauma-informed programming.
The study’s identification of increased HIV vulnerability in connection with mental health challenges is robustly supported by a growing body of epidemiological evidence [ – ]. This relationship is multifaceted, operating through behavioural, biological, and social mechanisms that reinforce one another. Behaviourally, mental health conditions such as depression and post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) are closely linked to high-risk sexual behaviours. Individuals experiencing depression are more likely to engage in unprotected or transactional sex, while those with substance use disorders may resort to needle-sharing and unsafe sexual practices, further compounding their HIV risk [ – ]. PTSD has also been shown to increase sexual risk-taking, often driven by emotional dysregulation and impaired judgment [ , ]. Biological pathways offer additional insight into how mental health exacerbates HIV susceptibility. Chronic stress, commonly seen in individuals with depression and PTSD, elevates cortisol levels, which in turn suppresses immune function and compromises the body’s ability to resist infections [ , ]. Moreover, anxiety and depressive disorders are associated with chronic systemic inflammation, a condition that may enhance viral entry at mucosal surfaces and increase biological vulnerability to HIV [ , ]. Socially, stigma surrounding mental health serves as a major barrier to timely and effective healthcare access. Individuals with mental health conditions often face discrimination or fear of judgment, which can significantly delay HIV testing and treatment initiation [ , ]. These barriers are even more severe among refugees, IDPs, and migrants, who already encounter systemic challenges in accessing healthcare services due to legal, linguistic, or socioeconomic constraints [ , – ].
Together, these underscore the urgency of integrated, trauma-informed approaches in HIV prevention and care in general and for refugees, IDPs, and migrants specifically. Displacement due to conflict, persecution, or disaster can lead to severe psychological stress, leading to a high prevalence of mental health disorders [ – ]. Traumatic exposure, which occurs from humanitarian crises, is correlated with increased substance abuse [ ], a known HIV risk factor [ ]. Without intervention, these mental health challenges create conditions that facilitate HIV transmission.
Second, this study highlights the heightened structural vulnerability of IDPs, revealing a critical contrast with refugees and migrants. IDPs experienced markedly higher levels of psychological distress and elevated substance use, a disparity that persisted even in the absence of severe distress, indicating that structural conditions, beyond mental health, are powerful drivers of vulnerability [ ]. This contrast with refugees is rooted in profound structural inequities. While refugees benefit from international legal protections and structured humanitarian support, including managed camps, IDPs remain under-resourced national jurisdictions that frequently lack the capacity or political will to provide adequate care [ , ]. As a result, IDPs are often subjected to systemic neglect and chronic instability.
IDPs also face distinct vulnerability drivers. Ongoing exposure to conflict, attacks on healthcare infrastructure [ ], economic desperation, and exclusion from aid programs—particularly those targeting formally registered populations—render IDPs highly susceptible to sexual violence and survival-driven risk behaviours [ , ]. Moreover, unlike refugees who may reside in protected camps, IDPs frequently settle in urban slums, environments characterized by poor access to services, higher HIV prevalence, and severely limited healthcare infrastructure [ , , ].
Poverty and food insecurity emerged as central mediators of HIV risk among IDPs. Sensitivity analyses showed that food insecurity directly influenced risk behaviours, highlighting structural deprivation—not distress alone—as a key contributor [ , ]. This finding contrasts with patterns among refugees, where HIV risk appeared more directly linked to levels of psychological distress. Syndemic theory elucidates how trauma, poverty, and discrimination synergistically increase HIV risk [ ], particularly in IDPs facing concurrent healthcare exclusion [ ] and economic precarity [ ].
These insights underscore the urgent need for interventions that directly address the structural determinants of HIV vulnerability among IDPs. Such strategies include expanding mobile HIV testing and care services in informal settlements [ ], providing economic support and livelihood assistance [ ], and strengthening legal and policy frameworks to ensure IDPs are explicitly prioritized in funded HIV responses [ ]. Ultimately, overcoming the neglect and invisibility that characterize the IDP experience requires sustained, targeted outreach and structural protection.
Third, the findings of the present study highlight that effective HIV prevention in displaced populations requires integrated, trauma-informed interventions that address the complex interplay between psychological distress, substance use, and structural vulnerability. Evidence supports the integration of mental health and HIV services as a strategic response to this syndemic. Combining HIV prevention tools, such as pre-exposure prophylaxis and HIV testing, with on-site trauma counselling and group therapy has shown promise in reducing risk behaviours and improving mental health outcomes [ , ]. For instance, training community health workers to deliver cognitive behavioural therapy alongside condom distribution has been successfully implemented in low-resource settings [ ].
Interventions must also be tailored to the unique needs of IDPs, who often reside in informal settlements with limited access to healthcare. Mobile clinics providing HIV and harm reduction services have proven effective in reaching these populations [ ]. Structural interventions such as economic empowerment through cash transfers can reduce reliance on survival-driven behaviours like transactional sex, thereby lowering HIV risk [ , ]. Legal and policy advocacy is equally vital to ensure that IDPs are explicitly included in national HIV response plans and funding mechanisms.
A trauma-informed harm reduction model is particularly critical. Substance use interventions, including needle exchange programs and opioid substitution therapy, must be coupled with mental health support to address the underlying trauma fueling addiction [ , , ]. The core rationale for these approaches is clear: without addressing the root causes of distress—whether expressed through substance use [ , , ], transactional sex [ ], or other survival strategies [ ]—HIV prevention efforts will be incomplete and ultimately less effective [ , , ]. Trauma-informed programming is not optional; it is foundational for achieving health equity and impact in displaced and structurally vulnerable populations.

## Conclusion
This study provides critical evidence on the syndemic interaction between psychological distress, substance use, and HIV risk among forcibly displaced populations and migrants in Nigeria. High levels of distress and substance use were strongly associated with increased HIV risk, particularly among IDPs. Refugees also faced elevated risk when distress and substance use co-occurred. Targeted, trauma-informed interventions—integrating mental health, harm reduction, and HIV services—are urgently needed to address these intersecting vulnerabilities and reduce HIV transmission in humanitarian settings.
