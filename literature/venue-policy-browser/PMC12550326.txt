## Abstract
Meat safety is critical for safeguarding public health, as contaminated meat poses serious risks, and compliance with safety guidelines largely depends on meat handlers' risk perceptions. This study assessed how butchers and fresh meat retailers in Ghana perceive risk factors contributing to unsafe meat production and examines the extent to which these perceptions influence their compliance with meat safety guidelines. The study used primary data obtained from 87 butchers and 299 fresh meat retailers. The data were analyzed using descriptive statistics, including means, standard deviations, and F-statistics, as well as Partial Least Squares Structural Equation Modeling (PLS-SEM). The findings on the perceived risk factors reveals a positive risk perception index of 0.88 and 0.86 for butchers and retailers respectively. This indicates a high level of awareness or concern about risks. The most highly perceived risk factors were institutional risks (1.30), financial risks (1.29), and microbiological/chemical risks (1.27). The PLS-SEM results revealed that butchers' compliance with safety guidelines was positively influenced by perceived environmental risk (β = 0.192, P = 0.10), and perceived financial risk (β = 0.257, P = 0.05) and negatively influenced by perceived market risk (β = −0.202, P = 0.066) while retailers' compliance was positively influenced by perceived microbiological risk (β = 0.351, P = 0.03), and perceived market risk (β = 0.117, P = 0.07). The study concludes that while butchers and fresh meat retailers in Ghana demonstrate generally high risk perceptions, their compliance with meat safety guidelines is shaped by different risk factors. The study recommends a collaborative monitoring and enforcement framework in which butchers and fresh meat retailers work closely with regulatory bodies to translate high institutional risk perceptions into compliance. This can be achieved through quarterly stakeholder meetings, streamlined licensing, and on-the-ground support, which together enhance practical adherence while minimizing bureaucratic challenges.

## Introduction
Meat production constitutes a cornerstone of economic development and nutritional security across developing nations, with Ghana exemplifying the complex challenges facing the sector's modernization. As Ghana transitions from low-income to middle-income status, urbanization and evolving consumer preferences have intensified demand for safer, higher-quality meat products [ ]. This transformation mirrors broader global trends where rising consumer awareness of foodborne disease risks has placed unparalleled pressure on meat industries to meet stringent safety standards, particularly across Sub-Saharan Africa where livestock meat processing has expanded considerably [ ].
The imperative for enhanced meat safety extends beyond consumer preference to encompass critical public health considerations. Fresh meat represents a particularly high-risk vector for zoonotic disease transmission, including bovine tuberculosis, cysticercosis, and brucellosis, with improper handling practices among meat handlers serving as primary transmission pathways [ ]. In Ghana's meat value chain, butchers and fresh meat retailers occupy pivotal positions as the final gatekeepers ensuring safe meat reaches consumers, yet compliance with established safety guidelines remains persistently problematic.
Ghana's meat regulatory framework encompasses comprehensive policies including the Public Health Act of 2012 (Act 851) and the Meat Inspection Regulations of 2020 (L.I. 2405), with the Food and Drug Authority (FDA) developing specific guidelines for slaughterhouse establishment and fresh meat retail [ , ]. However, implementation faces substantial obstacles including inadequate enforcement mechanisms, deficient infrastructure, and limited safety knowledge among meat handlers [ , ]. These challenges are particularly acute in northern Ghana, the nation's primary livestock sourcing region, where dilapidated slaughter facilities, substandard hygiene practices, and insufficient handler training create conducive environments for contamination [ ]. Many facilities lack basic amenities including clean water access and proper waste disposal systems, while meat transportation often occurs under unsanitary conditions using inappropriate vehicles [ ].
Contemporary literature identifies three principal categories of risk factors contributing to meat contamination including environmental, chemical/agricultural, and microbiological hazards [ ]. Environmental factors encompass the immediate physical conditions surrounding meat handling and storage. Sabbithi et al. [ ] demonstrate that vending site locations and construction materials significantly influence contamination risk, with simple constructions such as pushcarts and wooden tables offering minimal protection against cross-contamination from airborne chemicals, vehicular emissions, and pest infiltration. Open-air meat display, common across developing regions, exposes products to dust-borne bacteria and airborne pathogens that settle on preparation surfaces [ , ].
Waste accumulation and vehicular emissions create particularly hazardous conditions, establishing breeding environments for rodents, insects, and flies that facilitate microorganism proliferation [ , ]. Kabwang et al. [ ] identified poor hygiene practices, improper slaughtering processes, inadequate storage, and potable water absence as primary contamination contributors among retail meat vendors in the Democratic Republic of Congo, findings consistent with broader regional patterns.
Chemical and agricultural risk factors introduce toxic substances through agricultural practices, food additives, and preservation methods. While developed nations maintain stringent regulations governing these activities, developing countries often experience excessive pesticide use aimed at production enhancement, resulting in elevated chemical residues throughout the food chain [ , , ]. In addition, cost-reduction strategies may lead to substandard preservative procurement from unlicensed vendors, potentially introducing illicit chemical additives [ ].
Microbiological contamination represents the most immediate threat to consumer health. Although viruses account for over 50 % of foodborne illnesses, bacterial pathogens cause the majority of hospitalizations and fatalities [ ]. Key bacterial agents including Staphylococcus, Salmonella, Clostridium, Campylobacter, Listeria, Vibrio, Bacillus, and Enteropathogenic Escherichia coli are responsible for over 90 % of foodborne infections. Niyonzima et al. [ ] identified animal stress during transportation, lairage duration, and unhygienic abattoir conditions as critical factors influencing pathogenic bacteria excretion, with contamination primarily stemming from hides and visceral contents.
Despite extensive documentation of contamination sources, a critical knowledge gap persists regarding how meat handlers perceive these risks and translate such perceptions into compliance behaviors. Risk perception which is defined as how individuals interpret and respond to potential workplace hazards, significantly influences adherence to safety protocols [ , , ]. Waldman et al. [ ] observed that meat inspectors and slaughterhouse workers in northern Tanzania demonstrated greater awareness of visible environmental risks while neglecting invisible microbiological hazards. Similarly, Dang-Xuan et al. [ ] found that while slaughterhouse workers and pork sellers in Vietnam possessed accurate perceptions about certain diseases, significant misperceptions regarding zoonotic risks persisted.
This disconnect between risk awareness and appropriate response shows the complexity of compliance behavior among meat handlers. Existing research has predominantly focused on contamination identification and risk quantification while neglecting the psychological and behavioral dimensions that drive compliance decisions. Furthermore, most Ghanaian food safety studies concentrate on urban markets or consumer willingness to pay for safer products [ , ], with limited attention to northern regions where significant meat production originates.
This research addresses two critical knowledge gaps by: (1) examining the perceived risk factors contributing to unsafe meat production and (2) determining the influence of these perceived risk factors on compliance with meat safety guidelines among butchers and fresh meat retailers in Ghana. Understanding these perceptions is important for developing targeted interventions that enhance safety practices within the meat value chain. The study's significance extends beyond immediate public health implications to encompass economic development considerations, as effective meat safety systems protect consumers from foodborne diseases while enhancing trade opportunities and market confidence in locally produced meat.

## Materials and methods
2.1.

## Research design
The study employed a quantitative cross-sectional survey design to assess risk perception and its influence on compliance with meat safety guidelines among butchers and fresh meat retailers. This approach was selected for its capacity to objectively measure perceived risk levels and compliance behaviors through structured data collection, enabling statistical comparisons between participant groups and demographic variables [ ]. The cross-sectional design provided an efficient method for capturing current perceptions and practices across a large, geographically diverse sample in northern Ghana, ensuring broad representation and generalizability of findings [ ].

2.2.

## Description of the study area
 presents the map of the study area, which covers three regions in northern Ghana. Traditionally, Northern Ghana comprises the Northern, Upper East, and Upper West regions. However, following recent administrative re-demarcations, the area now includes the Savannah, and North East regions. This part of Ghana is markedly distinct from the more urbanized and economically advanced south in terms of climate, culture, development indicators, and socio-economic structure [ ]. Northern Ghana lies within the Guinean forest-savanna ecological zone and is characterized by vast grasslands interspersed with woodlands. The region experiences a tropical savanna climate, marked by a single rainy season and a prolonged dry season, making it relatively arid compared to southern Ghana. These climatic conditions, coupled with abundant grazing land and crop residues, provide a comparative advantage for livestock production, particularly for cattle, sheep, and goats [ ].
 According to the Food and Agriculture Organization of the United Nations (FAO) [ ], Northern Ghana serves as the principal hub for domestic livestock supply, holding a significant proportion of the national livestock population. Approximately 84 % of Ghana's cattle, 60 % of sheep, and 60 % of goats are concentrated in this ecological zone. Many rural households in the region practice mixed farming systems, integrating crop cultivation with livestock rearing, which plays a critical role in enhancing food security, income generation, and employment [ ]. Livestock serves multiple functions in these communities: it provides manure and draught power for crop production, acts as a store of wealth and savings, and offers a buffer against crop failures, particularly in the context of increasing climate variability. As crop-based agriculture becomes more vulnerable, livestock continues to emerge as a resilient alternative livelihood strategy, particularly for the poor and vulnerable [ , ].

2.3.

## Sampling procedure and sample size
The study employed a multistage sampling procedure to select respondents for the survey. In the first stage, regions in northern Ghana were selected using a purposive sampling technique. The second stage involved selecting three districts from each region, focusing on areas with active slaughterhouse operations and fresh meat retailing. In the final stage, butchers and fresh meat retailers were selected from the identified districts.
2.3.1.

## Selection of regions and districts
The study was conducted in Northern Ghana, which comprises five regions. Three of these regions being Northern, Upper East, and Upper West, were purposively selected based on their significance in livestock meat production. Historically, these regions constituted the traditional northern territories before the North East and Savanna regions were carved out of the Northern Region in 2018. The Northern Region was considered representative of the North East and Savanna regions due to their shared fresh meat handling practices. Within each selected region, three districts were purposively chosen based on key factors, including geographic distribution, the presence of slaughterhouses and slaughter slabs, the scale of fresh meat retailing, and the availability of key stakeholders in the fresh meat industry. 2.4.

## Research instrument and data collection procedure
2.4.1.

## Research instrument
A structured questionnaire was developed as the primary data collection instrument to capture quantitative data on risk perception, demographic information, socioeconomic variables, and compliance levels among butchers and fresh meat retailers. The instrument underwent rigorous pretesting with 30 meat handlers (15 butchers and 15 retailers) in a district not included in the main study to assess clarity, relevance, and comprehension. Based on pretest feedback, questions were refined for improved understanding and cultural appropriateness. Content validity was established through expert review by two food safety specialists and two public health researchers, while internal consistency was assessed using Cronbach's alpha (α = 0.82 for risk perception scale, α = 0.79 for compliance scale).

2.4.2.

## Measuring perceived risk factors
The risk factors contributing to unsafe meat production were categorized into five constructs: 1) environmental/hygiene risk, 2) microbiological/chemical risk, 3) financial risk, 4) institutional risk and 5) market risk. The environmental/hygiene risks construct had 9 indicators or manifest variables while microbiological/chemical risk construct had 6 indicators. The financial risk, institutional risk, and market risk constructs had 5, 6, and 3 indicators respectively. There were 29 indicators in all measuring the 5 risks constructs. The indicators were presented to respondents (butchers and fresh meat retailers) in the form of statement to them to rate based on their level of agreement to the statements using a 5 point Likert scale as follows: (−2 = strongly disagree, −1 = disagree, 0 = neutral, 1 = agree, 2 = strongly agree). The scale of −2 to 2 was used in order to estimate the perception index (positive or negative index). The implication of the scale is that, the more a respondent agree with a statement, the more positive the respondent's perception is toward that indicator or statement and vice versa. The data obtained was then analyzed using descriptive statistics such a means. The average perception score for an indicator was obtained by estimating the mean score for that indicator. Further, the perception scores for all indicators under each construct were added to obtain the total perception score for each construct. The total perception score for each construct was further divided by the number of indicators under the construct to obtain the perception index for each construct. The overall perception index was then obtained by dividing the overall perception scores by the total number of indicators. From this analysis, a (−) index implies negative perception while a (+) index implies a positive perception.

2.4.3.

## Measuring compliance
The meat safety guidelines used in this study to assess compliance is adopted from the recommended code of practice for slaughter houses/slabs specifically on personnel hygiene and meat handling by the Food and Drugs Authority (FDA), Ghana. These codes of conduct were developed from international standards such as the Codex Alimentarius. Ghana is a Codex member and is required to adopt these international standards. The assessment of compliance was based on the following key areas for the butchers at the slaughter houses/slabs and the retailers at the retail shops respectively. Different areas of compliance was assessed for butchers and fresh meat retailers. However, the broad areas of compliance for both groups were grouped into 7 main areas; 1) equipment 2) suitability of location 3) medical check-up 4) personnel cleanliness 5) protective clothing 6) sanitary facilities and 7. Storage. In all, 29 specific areas were assessed for the butchers while 23 specific areas were assessed for the retailers under the 7 broad areas respectively. The Cronbach's Alpha results for each 7 constructs for butchers and retailers respectively showed that the reliability metrics are strong as the Cronbach's Alpha values of most constructs were above 0.7 threshold (see Appendix A1).
A five point Likert scale was used to solicit responses together with observation from respondents. The 5 point Likert Scale was defined as: 0 = not true at all, 1 = slightly true, 2 = moderately true, 3 = mostly true, and 4 = completely true. This approach was used to assess compliance because the five-point Likert scale, combined with direct observation, allowed for a structured and comprehensive evaluation of butchers' and fresh meat retailers' adherence to meat safety guidelines. Compliance scores for each respondent was calculated by adding all the scores obtained from each specific area of compliance based on the Likert scale used. These score were later converted in fractions to measure compliance level by dividing the total compliance score of each respondent by the maximum compliance score. The compliance level variable was then used as the dependent variable in the estimation of the influence of perceived risk factors on compliance.

2.4.4.

## Data collection procedure
Data collection was conducted through face-to-face interviewer-administered surveys to ensure high response rates and data quality. Trained enumerators, all university graduates with backgrounds in public health or food science, were recruited and underwent intensive two-day training on the research instrument, interview techniques, and ethical considerations. The training covered questionnaire familiarization, proper interview conduct, data recording procedures, and strategies for engaging participants while maintaining objectivity. Enumerators were also trained to complete observational checklists simultaneously during interviews to verify self-reported compliance behaviors.

2.5.

## Data analysis methods
2.5.1.

## Analytical model
The Partial Least Squares Structural Equation Modeling (PLS-SEM) was used to estimate the influence of perceived risk factors on compliance with meat safety guidelines. PLS-SEM was chosen because the study involves latent constructs (perceived risk factors and compliance), which cannot be directly observed but are measured using multiple indicators. Also, the study aims to predict and explain the influence of risk perception on compliance rather than simply testing an existing theoretical model. The PLS-SEM also effectively handles multicollinearity among risk factors, which is crucial since multiple risk categories may correlate [ ].

2.5.2.

## Model equations in PLS-SEM
The PLS-SEM model consists of two main components: measurement model and formative model.
The measurement (outer) model specifies the relationships between the observed indicators (manifest variables) and the latent constructs (risk factors) as shown in eq. 2 and 3 [ ].
(2)Reflective Model:Xij=λijηj+ϵijwhere λij are the factor loadings and ϵij is the measurement error. Xij represents the indicator variables measuring a latent construct ηj.
(3)Formative Model:ηj=∑βijXij+ϵjwhere βij are the regression weights of the indicators forming the construct.
The structural (inner) model specifies the relationships among latent constructs (risk factors) and the dependent variable (meat safety compliance). The equation follows a regression-based structure as shown in eq. 4.
(4)ηCom=γ1X1+γ2ηX2+γ3ηX3+γ4ηX4+γ5ηX5+ζwhere, Comp represent compliance, and X1…X5 represent the perceived risk factors, γ1 , …,γ5  = Path coefficients indicating the effects of risk factors on meat safety compliance and ζ = Error term.

2.5.3.

## The PLS-SEM estimation procedure
The PLS-SEM procedure used in this study followed a structured approach to assess the influence of perceived risk factors on compliance with meat safety guidelines among butchers and fresh meat retailers. First, the model was specified by identifying five latent constructs (environmental/hygiene risk, microbiological/chemical risk, financial risk, institutional risk, and market risk) as independent variables and compliance as the dependent variable. Next, the measurement model was evaluated by assessing reliability (Cronbach's alpha, Composite Reliability), convergent validity (Average Variance Extracted, AVE > 0.5), and discriminant validity (Fornell-Larcker Criterion, HTMT Ratio) to ensure construct validity. The structural model was then estimated by analyzing path coefficients (γi) using bootstrapping for significance testing, computing the coefficient of determination. Finally, model fit was assessed using the Standardized Root Mean Square Residual (SRMR) (where SRMR <0.08 indicates a good fit) and the PLS Predict test to evaluate predictive accuracy. This procedure ensured a robust and systematic evaluation of how different risk perception constructs influenced compliance behavior, offering valuable insights for targeted interventions in meat safety regulation [ ].

2.6.

## Ethical consideration
The study adhered to ethical research standards by obtaining ethical clearance from the Humanities and Social Sciences Research Ethics Committee at Kwame Nkrumah University of Science and Technology (KNUST) before data collection. This approval ensured that the research complied with ethical guidelines, including informed consent, confidentiality, and voluntary participation of butchers and fresh meat retailers. Participants were fully informed about the study's purpose, potential risks, and their right to withdraw at any time without consequences. Additionally, data privacy was maintained by anonymizing responses and securely storing information to prevent unauthorized access. The data collection process was between May 2024 and August 2024.

## Results
3.1.

## Demographic and socioeconomic characteristics
As seen in Table 2, both butchers and fresh meat retailers have relatively low formal education levels, with an average of about 7 years, and no significant differences between them. Age distribution also does not significantly differ, with butchers averaging 34 years and retailers 36 years, indicating that most participants are within their prime working years. However, sex differences are notable, as all butchers are male, whereas 91 % of retailers are male. The meat business is clearly a male dominated business with butchering being a sole reserve for males. At the enterprise level, significant differences exist in business ownership, where 71 % of retailers own their businesses compared to 43 % of butchers. This suggest that butchers are more likely to be working for others. In the meat business, most butchers are employed by others to work for them. Location also plays a crucial role, with more butchers (70 %) operating in designated venues compared to retailers (59 %). This suggest that butchers may be operating in designated venues having greater access to regulated slaughterhouses than retailers.
The number of persons engaged in business operations also differs significantly, with retailers employing an average of 2.58 people compared to butchers' 1.25, indicating a larger operational scale among retailers. However, business experience is similar between the two groups, with butchers averaging 11.57 years and retailers 12.87 years. Institutional-level characteristics also reveal key differences. Supervision appear to be high but not significantly different between butchers (87 %) and retailers (83 %), indicating that regulatory bodies have ever monitored both groups. Training in meat safety is relatively low across both groups (56 % and 57 %, respectively) and does not show a significant difference, suggesting a gap in formal meat safety education. The low formal meat safety training rates among both groups represent a critical knowledge gap that undermines effective risk assessment and compliance behavior, as evidenced by the disconnect between high institutional risk perception and actual compliance outcomes. However, business registration rates vary significantly, with 61 % of retailers registered compared to only 40 % of butchers. Compliance level indicates that retailers (0.51) exhibit higher adherence to safety guidelines than butchers (0.41), suggesting differences in operational environments, regulatory exposure, or customer expectations, among others.

3.2.

## Meat handlers' perceptions of risk factors in meat production
The study identified five categories of risk factors perceived to contribute to unsafe meat production including environmental/hygiene risks, microbiological/chemical risks, financial risks, market risks, and institutional risks. The perceived risk indices (PRIs) provide quantitative measures of concern levels attributed to these risks by butchers and retailers.

3.3.

## Environmental and hygiene risks
Environmental and hygiene risks showed an overall perceived risk index of 1.22, with butchers (1.26) perceiving slightly higher risks than retailers (1.21). Specific high-concern factors included selling meat in unclean environments (1.57), unavailability of safe water for washing (1.60), and proximity to unclean gutters (1.47). Both groups reported similar concerns about selling meat in fly-prone areas (butchers: 1.39; retailers: 1.40), though butchers expressed higher concerns about allowing flies to sit on meat (1.57 vs. 1.38). Conversely, lower perceived risks were recorded for unavailability of waste collection bins (0.41) and inadequate WASH facilities (0.23).
3.3.1.

## Microbiological and chemical risks
With an overall perceived risk index of 1.27, microbiological and chemical risks were significant concerns for both groups, with butchers (1.30) perceiving slightly higher risks than retailers (1.27). Key concerns included lack of veterinary examination before slaughter (butchers: 1.57; retailers: 1.49) and excessive chemical use for meat preservation (butchers: 1.17; retailers: 1.47). Equipment-related risks at slaughterhouses (butchers: 1.44; retailers: 1.23) and butcher shops (butchers: 1.32; retailers: 1.18) were also notable concerns.

3.3.2.

## Financial risks
Financial constraints emerged as significant contributor with an overall perceived risk index of 1.29. Both groups showed similar concern levels (butchers: 1.27; retailers: 1.29). Major financial risk factors included costs of health permits (1.29 for both groups), protective clothing (butchers: 1.29; retailers: 1.21), and health screening (butchers: 1.28; retailers: 1.32). Inadequate capital and credit access also represented substantial concerns (butchers: 1.33; retailers: 1.34).

3.3.3.

## Market risks
Market-related risks were perceived as the least contributory to unsafe meat production, with a negative overall risk index of −0.77. Both butchers (−0.69) and retailers (−0.79) showed similar perceptions. Specific factors included market pressures prioritizing price over safety (−0.12), supply-demand fluctuations (−1.15), and lack of consumer food safety awareness (−1.27). The negative signs associated with these results implies that the meat handlers do not see perceive these factors to be contributing the unsafe meat production.

3.3.4.

## Institutional risks
Institutional risks showed the highest overall perceived risk index of 1.30, with retailers (1.31) perceiving slightly higher risks than butchers (1.28). Critical concerns included weak regulatory enforcement (butchers: 1.33; retailers: 1.35), inadequate inspection procedures (butchers: 1.33; retailers: 1.30), corruption in regulatory agencies (butchers: 1.14; retailers: 1.31), lack of traceability systems (butchers: 1.32; retailers: 1.30), and insufficient personnel training (butchers: 1.43; retailers: 1.31).

3.3.5.

## Overall risk perception
As shown in , the overall risk perception index was positive for both groups at 0.86, with butchers (0.88) showing slightly higher overall risk perception than retailers (0.86). The ranking of risk categories by perceived importance was: institutional risks (1.30), microbiological/ chemical risks (1.27), financial risks (1.29), environmental/ hygiene risks (1.24), and market risks (−0.77). Butchers perceive slightly higher overall risks (0.88 vs. 0.86) than retailers due to their direct exposure to multiple hazards during animal slaughter and processing, including handling live animals, operating dangerous equipment, managing blood and visceral waste, and working in environments with greater potential for cross-contamination, compared to retailers who primarily handle processed meat products in relatively controlled display environments. 3.4.

## The effects of the perceived risk factors on compliance with meat safety guidelines
3.4.1.

## Cross loadings, outer loadings and construct reliability
For the cross loadings, all indicators loaded highest on their assigned constructs, confirming discriminant validity (see Appendix, for full cross-loadings). The outer loadings and construct reliability results is presented on Table 5. The outer loadings and construct reliability explains the measurement model's validity and reliability in assessing the effects of risk perception on compliance. The outer loadings represent the strength of the relationship between individual indicators and their respective constructs, while Cronbach's alpha, rho_A, and Average Variance Extracted (AVE) evaluate internal consistency and convergent validity.
For Microbiological Risk, the construct demonstrates acceptable reliability among butchers, with a Cronbach's alpha of 0.757 and a composite reliability of 0.823. However, the AVE (0.474) very closed to the recommended threshold of 0.50, suggesting that the construct captures at least, minimum variance in its indicators. Among retailers, the reliability metrics are stronger, with a Cronbach's alpha of 0.823 and a composite reliability of 0.839, along with an AVE of 0.542, indicating a better fit and stronger validity.
Environmental Risk exhibits high internal consistency, with Cronbach's alpha values of 0.892 (butchers) and 0.917 (retailers), and composite reliability values exceeding 0.90 in both groups. The AVE for butchers (0.535) and retailers (0.621) also surpass the recommended threshold of 0.50, which is an indication that this construct is well-defined and its indicators explain a sufficient proportion of variance in their indicators.
Financial Risk shows strong reliability and validity among butchers, with a Cronbach's alpha of 0.910, composite reliability of 0.938, and an AVE of 0.735, indicating that the indicators are highly correlated with the construct explaining a sufficient proportion of the variance in its indicators. Among retailers, the construct also exhibits good reliability, though slightly lower than that of the butchers (Cronbach's alpha = 0.883, composite reliability = 0.891, AVE = 0.629), also meeting the minimum recommended standards.
For Institutional Risk, the reliability is acceptable for both groups, with Cronbach's alpha values of 0.832 (butchers) and 0.805 (retailers), and composite reliability values above 0.80. However, the AVE is slightly lower for retailers (0.504) compared to butchers (0.552), indicating that the construct provides sufficient explanation of the variance in its indicators.
Market Risk shows mixed reliability results for butchers and retailers. Among butchers, it has an exceptionally high Cronbach's alpha (0.955) and composite reliability (0.986), with an AVE of 0.917, indicating strong internal consistency and a well-defined construct. However, for retailers, the construct shows weaker reliability (Cronbach's alpha = 0.571, composite reliability = 0.601, AVE = 0.534) compared to the butchers. However, the market risk construct for both butchers and retailers met the recommended reliability and validity criteria, implying that the variance in their respective indicators have been sufficiently explained.
The results generally indicate that all the 5 constructs demonstrate strong reliability and validity, especially with environmental risk, financial risk, and institutional risk even though, microbiological risk among butchers and market risk among retailers have slightly lower AVE values. This implies that the constructs effectively measure risk perception among butchers and retailers.
While Cronbach's alpha, composite reliability, and AVE indicate strong internal consistency and convergent validity, these metrics have limitations including assumption of tau-equivalence, inability to detect method bias or common variance issues, and insufficient assessment of nomological validity, suggesting that future studies should incorporate confirmatory factor analysis with multiple fit indices, employ multi-trait multi-method approaches to assess discriminant validity, and conduct cross-validation with independent samples to strengthen construct validity assessment.

3.4.2.

## The Heterotrait-Monotrait (HTMT) ratio
The HTMT ratio in this study assesses discriminant validity by measuring the degree of correlation between the different five constructs for butchers and for fresh meat retailers respectively. A lower HTMT value (typically below 0.85) suggests that constructs are distinct, whereas higher values indicate potential issues with discriminant validity.
For butchers, most HTMT values remain below the 0.85 threshold, indicating acceptable discriminant validity. Compliance shows moderate correlations with Environmental Risk (0.314) and Financial Risk (0.329), suggesting that while these risks influence compliance behavior, they remain conceptually distinct. Institutional Risk has a strong correlation with Financial Risk (0.820), which approaches the threshold. The higher correlations between institutional and financial risks likely reflect the interconnected nature of regulatory compliance costs, where weak institutional frameworks create additional financial burdens through bureaucratic inefficiencies, corruption-related expenses, and uncertainty costs, suggesting that while these constructs remain conceptually distinct, future measurement models should consider second-order factor structures or acknowledge this natural overlap when interpreting results, as both risks fundamentally stem from systemic barriers that impose economic constraints on meat handlers.
Market Risk has the lowest correlations with other constructs, with values ranging from 0.120 to 0.333, indicating that market-related concerns are relatively separate from other risk perceptions. Microbiological Risk correlates moderately with Institutional Risk (0.540) and Financial Risk (0.482), suggesting some overlap in perceptions of hygiene-related risks and financial constraints.
For retailers, the results are slightly different, with a few higher HTMT values. Compliance has a stronger correlation with Environmental Risk (0.557) compared to butchers, indicating that hygiene and contamination concerns may play a more significant role in retailer compliance decisions. Institutional Risk and Financial Risk maintain a high correlation (0.757), again suggesting a close relationship between these two risk factors. Market Risk shows relatively higher correlations with Institutional Risk (0.840) and Financial Risk (0.834), which may indicate that market uncertainties and financial constraints are interconnected for retailers since they are closer to the threshold. The HTMT value between Environmental Risk and Microbiological Risk (0.494), which is well below the 0.85 threshold.
The HTMT analysis confirms that all the constructs exhibit good discriminant validity for both butchers and fresh meat retailers. This implies that the constructs are distinct from one another, ensuring that each variable measures a unique aspect of risk perception and compliance among butchers and fresh meat retailers.

3.5.

## Path coefficient results for the effects of risk perception on compliance with meat safety guidelines
3.5.1.

## Butchers' compliance determinants
For butchers, three risk factors showed significant effects on compliance behavior. Perceived environmental risk demonstrated a marginally significant positive relationship (β = 0.192, p = 0.066), indicating that environmental concerns motivate compliance. Perceived financial risk showed the strongest positive relationship (β = 0.257, p = 0.050), suggesting financial considerations significantly influence adherence to safety guidelines. Conversely, perceived market risk exhibited a significant negative effect (β = −0.202, p = 0.066), indicating that market-related concerns reduce compliance levels. Institutional risk (β = −0.035, p = 0.800) and microbiological risk (β = 0.081, p = 0.590) showed weak, statistically insignificant relationships with compliance.

3.5.2.

## Retailers' compliance determinants
For fresh meat retailers, two risk factors significantly influenced compliance. Microbiological risk showed a strong positive relationship (β = 0.351, p = 0.031), indicating that contamination concerns drive compliance behavior. Market risk also demonstrated a positive significant effect (β = 0.117, p = 0.070), contrasting with butchers' negative market risk response. Environmental risk (β = 0.132, p = 0.395), financial risk (β = 0.084, p = 0.193), and institutional risk (β = 0.032, p = 0.644) showed weak, statistically insignificant effects on compliance.
The results reveal distinct compliance drivers for each group: butchers respond primarily to environmental and financial concerns with negative market risk influence, while retailers are driven by microbiological risks with positive market risk influence.

4.

## Discussion of findings
4.1.

## Risk perception patterns among meat handlers
The findings reveal that butchers and retailers in Ghana demonstrate sophisticated risk awareness, with institutional risks perceived as the most significant threat to meat safety, followed by microbiological/chemical and financial risks. This hierarchy of risk perception provides important understanding of how meat handlers prioritize safety concerns in their daily operations.
The high perceived risk index for institutional factors (1.30) aligns with existing literature showing the critical role of regulatory frameworks in food safety systems. The concerns about weak regulatory enforcement, inadequate inspection procedures, and corruption within regulatory agencies are consistent with broader governance challenges documented across Sub-Saharan Africa's food systems [ ]. These findings support previous research indicating that effective institutional oversight is fundamental to ensuring food safety compliance [ ].
The significant concern about microbiological and chemical risks (PRI = 1.27) reflects growing awareness of invisible contamination threats, which contrasts with earlier findings by Waldman et al. [ ] who reported that meat workers in northern Tanzania were primarily concerned with visible environmental risks while neglecting microbiological hazards. This suggests that Ghanaian meat handlers may have developed more sophisticated risk awareness, possibly due to increased exposure to food safety information or past experiences with contamination incidents. The environmental and hygiene risks identified in this study are particularly concerning as they facilitate the proliferation of pathogenic bacteria including Salmonella, Escherichia coli, Staphylococcus aureus, and Listeria monocytogenes, while chemical contaminants from cleaning agents, pesticide residues, and preservatives can accumulate in meat products, collectively posing severe public health risks including foodborne illnesses, antimicrobial resistance, and chronic toxicity effects.
Environmental and hygiene risks (PRI = 1.22) were perceived as moderately important, with specific concerns about unclean environments, inadequate water access, and proximity to contamination sources. These findings align with previous studies by Alimi et al. [ ] and Sabbithi et al. [ ], who documented similar environmental risk factors in developing country meat markets. However, the relatively lower concern about waste collection bins and WASH facilities suggests a potential disconnect between perceived immediate risks and longer-term infrastructural needs.
The negative perception of market risks (−0.77) is particularly noteworthy and challenges conventional assumptions about market-driven safety compromises. It reveals a critical disconnect where meat handlers fail to recognize how consumer demand pressures and price competition can compromise safety standards, suggesting that market-safety relationships are poorly understood among stakeholders. Consumer education campaigns should therefore adopt a dual approach: educating consumers about the value of paying premium prices for certified safe meat while simultaneously training meat handlers to recognize and communicate the economic benefits of safety compliance, including reduced spoilage, improved customer loyalty, and access to higher-value market segments. Previous research has suggested that economic pressures and consumer demand for cheap meat can lead to safety standard compromises [ ]. However, our findings suggest that Ghanaian meat handlers do not perceive market dynamics as direct contributors to unsafe practices, indicating either a lack of awareness of market-safety relationships or confidence in their ability to maintain safety standards despite market pressures.

4.2.

## Differential compliance drivers between butchers and retailers
The path analysis results reveal distinct behavioral patterns between butchers and retailers, suggesting that compliance interventions should be tailored to each group's specific motivational drivers. These differences likely reflect the distinct roles, responsibilities, and operational contexts of each group within the meat value chain.
For butchers, the positive relationship between financial risk perception and compliance (β = 0.257, p = 0.050) supports economic theories of safety investment, where perceived financial consequences of unsafe practices motivate protective behavior. This is driven by their concerns about the direct costs of health permits and renewals, protective clothing and materials, worker health screening, safety training and certification, combined with inadequate capital and limited credit access. This suggest that perceptions about financial barriers contributing to unsafe meat production could lead butchers to opt for subsidy programs, bulk purchasing programs for safety equipment, employer-sponsored health screening initiatives, and microfinance schemes specifically for safety investments which could effectively address these cost barriers. These activities could potentially lead to high compliance. This finding is consistent with research by Fearon et al. [ ] who noted that financial constraints significantly impact safety practices among meat handlers in developing countries. The positive effect of environmental risk perception on butchers' compliance (β = 0.192, p = 0.066) aligns with their direct exposure to slaughterhouse conditions and immediate contamination risks.
Particularly interesting is the negative relationship between market risk perception and compliance among butchers (β = −0.202, p = 0.066). This suggests that when butchers perceive market-related pressures, they may prioritize economic survival over regulatory adherence, potentially compromising safety standards to maintain competitiveness. This finding resonates with economic pressures documented in small-scale food operations across developing countries [ ].
For retailers, the strong positive relationship between microbiological risk perception and compliance (β = 0.351, p = 0.031) indicates that contamination concerns are primary drivers of safety behavior. This finding aligns with Dang-Xuan et al. [ ] who found that pork sellers demonstrated accurate perceptions about foodborne diseases and their prevention. The positive market risk-compliance relationship among retailers (β = 0.117, p = 0.070) contrasts sharply with butchers' response, suggesting that retailers view market stability and reputation as incentives for maintaining safety standards. This could be attributed to the fact that butchers, who are often employees, might see market pressures as a reason to cut corners on safety to reduce costs and maintain profit margins for their bosses. On the other hand, retailers, who are more often owners, might see a stable market as an opportunity to invest in safety to attract and retain customers.
The divergent risk priorities reflect occupational role differences where butchers face immediate environmental hazards during slaughter and processing while experiencing direct financial pressures from equipment and permit costs, whereas retailers encounter invisible microbiological threats during meat storage and display, suggesting that targeted interventions should provide environmental risk training and financial support for butchers while emphasizing microbiological contamination education and detection tools for retailers to address their specific exposure patterns and knowledge needs. Also, the differential responses to institutional risks between groups (non-significant for both) suggest that while meat handlers recognize institutional weaknesses as major risk factors, these perceptions do not translate directly into individual compliance behavior. This disconnect may reflect fatalistic attitudes toward regulatory systems or perceived lack of agency in influencing institutional change.

4.3.

## Implications for meat safety interventions
The identified risk perception patterns and compliance drivers have significant implications for designing effective meat safety interventions in Ghana and similar developing country contexts. The findings suggest that generic, one-size-fits-all approaches are unlikely to be effective given the distinct motivational profiles of different meat handler groups.
For butchers, interventions should emphasize the financial benefits of compliance, including reduced economic losses from contamination incidents, improved market access, and potential premium pricing for certified safe meat. Environmental risk communication should focus on immediate, visible contamination threats that butchers can directly observe and control. Training programs should address market pressure management, helping butchers understand how to maintain safety standards while remaining economically competitive.
For retailers, interventions should prioritize microbiological risk education, emphasizing invisible contamination threats and their health consequences. Since retailers respond positively to market risks, programs could leverage market-based incentives, such as consumer preference for safe meat, reputation benefits, and potential business growth from safety compliance.
The high perception of institutional risks across both groups suggests that broader systemic interventions are needed alongside individual behavior change programs. Strengthening regulatory enforcement, improving inspection procedures, addressing corruption, and implementing traceability systems could address the institutional failures that meat handlers perceive as major barriers to safety.
These findings contribute to the broader literature on food safety behavior in developing countries by demonstrating the importance of context-specific risk perceptions in shaping compliance behavior. The results support calls for more nuanced, behaviorally-informed approaches to food safety promotion that recognize the complexity of decision-making processes among different stakeholder groups in food value chains.

4.4.

## Demographic and socioeconomic implications for meat safety
4.4.1.

## Gender dynamics in the meat trade
The complete male dominance in butchering and overwhelming male presence in retailing (91 %) reflects deeply entrenched cultural and socioeconomic factors within Ghana's meat industry. This gender segregation likely stems from traditional perceptions of butchering as physically demanding work requiring strength for animal handling and carcass processing, combined with cultural taboos that may restrict women's participation in blood-related activities. The economic barriers to entry, including capital requirements for equipment and livestock purchase, may further exclude women who typically have limited access to credit and business capital in developing economies. This male dominance has significant implications for meat safety interventions, as targeting male networks and communication channels becomes critical for effective program implementation. The homogeneous gender composition also suggests missed opportunities for diverse perspectives on hygiene and safety practices that women might bring to the industry.

4.4.2.

## Business ownership and compliance implications
The substantial difference in business ownership between retailers (71 %) and butchers (43 %) reveals important structural characteristics that directly impact safety compliance. The higher proportion of employee-butchers suggests a hierarchical system where compliance decisions may be influenced by employer priorities rather than individual safety awareness. This ownership structure creates potential principal-agent problems where employed butchers may have limited autonomy to invest in safety measures or implement best practices if not supported by business owners. The finding that retailers demonstrate higher compliance levels (0.51 verses 0.41) may partly reflect this ownership effect, as business owners have stronger incentives to maintain customer satisfaction and avoid reputation damage from safety incidents. This dynamic suggests that compliance interventions should target both business owners and employees, with particular emphasis on engaging butcher shop owners who make operational decisions affecting safety practices.

4.4.3.

## Regulatory environment and infrastructure access
The higher proportion of butchers operating in designated venues (70 % verses 59 %) indicates better access to regulated slaughterhouses, which typically provide standardized facilities with veterinary oversight, proper waste disposal systems, and clean water access. Ghana's meat inspection regulations require pre-mortem and post-mortem examinations conducted by qualified veterinary personnel, along with adherence to hygiene standards during slaughtering, processing, and storage. These designated facilities are subject to regular inspections by the Veterinary Services Directorate and Environmental Health Units, creating a more controlled environment for meat safety compliance. However, the infrastructure advantage does not translate to higher compliance levels among butchers, suggesting that access to regulated facilities alone is insufficient without proper training, motivation, and enforcement. This disconnect shows the need for comprehensive interventions that combine infrastructure development with behavioral change programs to maximize the safety benefits of designated operating venues.

5.

## Conclusion
5.1.

## Major findings
This study examined risk perceptions and their influence on meat safety compliance among butchers and fresh meat retailers in Ghana. The findings reveal that both groups demonstrate positive overall risk perception, with institutional, microbiological/chemical, and financial risks perceived as the most significant threats to meat safety. Market risks were perceived negatively, indicating these factors are not viewed as contributors to unsafe meat production.
The path analysis revealed distinct compliance drivers for each group. Butchers' compliance is positively influenced by perceived environmental and financial risks but negatively affected by market risk perceptions. Retailers' compliance is driven primarily by microbiological risk perceptions and positively influenced by market risks. Despite high institutional risk perception, this factor did not significantly influence compliance behavior in either group.
While the specific risk perception patterns and compliance drivers identified in this study reflect Ghana's unique cultural, economic, and regulatory context, the underlying behavioral framework demonstrating differential risk-compliance relationships across stakeholder groups, the importance of institutional factors in food safety systems, and the need for tailored interventions based on occupational roles likely generalizes to similar developing countries with comparable informal food systems, suggesting that the methodological approach and intervention principles could be adapted to other Sub-Saharan African contexts or small-scale food industries with appropriate modifications for local regulatory environments and cultural factors.

5.2.

## Policy and practice implication recommendations
1. Group-Specific Training and Awareness Programs: Develop tailored educational interventions that provide financial literacy and environmental risk training for butchers through trade associations while delivering microbiological contamination workshops with portable testing kits for retailers, utilizing multiple delivery channels including hands-on workshops, mobile units, peer learning networks, and digital platforms to ensure broad accessibility.
2. Financial Support and Market-Based Incentive Systems: Establish comprehensive economic support mechanisms including microfinance schemes for safety investments, graduated fee structures based on business size, subsidized equipment access through cooperative purchasing, and market-driven incentives such as certification programs tied to premium pricing and tax rebates for compliant businesses.
3. Strengthen Institutional Framework and Regulatory Oversight: Create a unified meat safety oversight system integrating the Food and Drugs Authority, Veterinary Services Directorate, and Environmental Health Units with standardized inspection protocols, real-time digital monitoring, mandatory quarterly audits, and collaborative stakeholder engagement to address institutional risks and streamline regulatory processes.
4. Revise and Modernize Safety Guidelines and Standards: Update existing meat safety guidelines to comprehensively address contemporary challenges including chemical preservation practices, emerging microbiological risks, and environmental contamination factors while reflecting the differential risk exposures of butchers versus retailers within a unified regulatory framework.

5.3.

## Strengths and limitations
While this study has several limitations, including a cross-sectional design that limits causal inference about risk perception-compliance relationships, self-reported compliance measures that may introduce social desirability bias, geographic focus on northern Ghana that may limit broader generalizability, and single-time-point assessment that cannot capture temporal variations in risk perceptions, these concerns were mitigated through several methodological strengths. The large, representative sample from northern Ghana's primary meat production region enhanced statistical power and regional validity, while rigorous quantitative methodology with validated instruments and robust statistical analysis improved measurement reliability. The novel focus on risk perception-compliance relationships using path analysis provided deeper analytical insights, and the practical policy relevance offers actionable recommendations for different stakeholder groups. However, while the vast majority of measurement constructs demonstrated good reliability, the marginally acceptable alpha for the equipment compliance scale should be considered when interpreting findings related to that specific area.

5.4.

## Future research
Future research should investigate how economic incentives and consumer demand influence butchers' safety adoption through longitudinal experimental trials with market-based incentives, and explore risk perception-compliance relationships across different geographical contexts and time periods.

5.5.

## Originality and value
This study provides the first comprehensive examination of risk perception-compliance relationships among meat handlers in Ghana, filling a critical gap in food safety literature for Sub-Saharan Africa. The originality lies in applying behavioral economics principles to understand how different risk perceptions drive compliance behaviors, revealing that butchers and retailers respond to distinct motivational factors. The practical value extends beyond Ghana, offering a replicable framework for developing behaviorally-informed food safety interventions in similar developing country contexts. By demonstrating that generic, one-size-fits-all approaches are ineffective, this research establishes the foundation for evidence-based, stakeholder-specific compliance strategies that can enhance meat safety outcomes across diverse cultural and economic settings.
