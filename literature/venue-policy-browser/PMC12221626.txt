## Introduction
Salmonella is recognized as the number one foodborne pathogen causing about 1.35 million infections, 26,500 hospitalizations, and 420 deaths in the United States every year ( ). Approximately 4 % of chicken products available in grocery stores are contaminated with Salmonella ( ), and poultry consumption accounts for more than 23 % of salmonellosis cases ( ). Since the implementation of the PR/HACCP rule by the U.S. Department of Agriculture’s Food Safety and Inspection Service (USDA-FSIS) in 1996, the prevalence of Salmonella in poultry products has significantly declined. However, this decline in Salmonella contamination has not been accompanied by a proportional decrease in salmonellosis cases ( ). To meet the national goal of reducing Salmonella illnesses by 25 %, FSIS has implemented several critical initiatives aimed at collecting data and evidence to guide future regulatory actions and policy development. For example, in 2016, the United States Department of Agriculture-Food Safety and Inspection Service (USDA-FSIS) published new regulations referred as “new performance standards” ( ) which required ≤ 25 % and ≤ 15.4 % of positive Salmonella in comminuted chicken and chicken parts, respectively.
Raw chicken meat, sourced from whole carcasses and cut into smaller pieces, is often ground, mixed with salt and a tri-polyphosphate solution, and then formed into patties. These are commonly known as non-intact reconstructed chicken meat products. The incorporation of salt and polyphosphates serves to enhance water-holding capacity (WHC), minimize cooking losses, improve sensory characteristics, and elevate the overall quality of the final product ( ). Additionally, salt has long been used as a preservation method to maintain freshness and food quality. However, elevated salt concentrations in food have been associated with increased thermal resistance in pathogens, allowing Salmonella to potentially survive cooking processes due to protection afforded by high concentration of salt ( ; ). According to the National Advisory Committee on Microbiological Criteria for Foods (NACMCF), raw chicken products should be cooked to an internal geometric center temperature of 74°C to achieve a 7-log reduction in Salmonella ( ). Nevertheless, the specific effect of salt concentration on the thermal inactivation of Salmonella in non-intact chicken products remains to be fully assessed, which is essential for developing poultry meat safety guidelines ( ).
Enterococcus faecium has emerged as a recognized surrogate bacteria for Salmonella reduction studies, with two commonly used laboratory strains being ATCC 8459 from the American Type Culture Collection and NRRL B-2354 from the ARS Culture Collection ( ; ). These strains are well-characterized for research use, as they lack known virulence factors associated with Salmonella and remain susceptible to clinically relevant antibiotics ( ). Thermal processing studies have shown that a minimum extrusion temperature of 74°C in a balanced carbohydrate-protein meal can achieve a 5-log reduction of E. faecium strain ATCC 8459. In contrast, temperatures above 80°C with a barrel residence time of 50 seconds have resulted in undetectable levels of strain B-2354 ( ).
Predictive microbiology (PM) models are valuable mathematical tools for assessing microbial food safety and quality throughout the farm-to-fork continuum, particularly in relation to thermal inactivation processes. To support users without a background in mathematical modeling or specialized software, user-friendly model fitting tools have been developed. Examples include GInaFit, an Excel-based freeware add-in ( ) and the USDA’s Integrated Pathogen Modeling Program (IPMP) software ( ; ). There is growing interest in applying machine learning (ML) models such as Random Forest (RF), Support Vector Machines (SVMS) and Binary Logistic Regression (BLR) to enhance food safety management and enable rapid, data-driven decision making. For example, the use of ML modeling to predict the presence of generic Escherichia coli in leafy green wash water samples has been reported (Polat et al., 2020; ), however, little research has been done to use ML models to evaluate the factors affected the thermal inactivation process of microorganisms in chicken meat products.
Therefore, the objective of this study was to use microbial predictive and machine learning model data to determine the thermal inactivation of Salmonella and its surrogate bacteria E. faecium in moisture-enhanced, reconstructed ground chicken patties as affected by temperatures and salt concentrations.

## Materials and methods

## Bacteria strains
This study used nalidixic acid (NaL) resistant Salmonella Typhimurium American Type Culture Collection (ATCC) 14028 and Enterococcus faecium ATCC 8459, the same pathogen and surrogate strains used in our previous research on broiler carcasses ( ), moisture-enhanced chicken products ( ), and poultry feeds studies ( ). Stock cultures of S. Typhimurium and E. faecium were stored at −80°C and activated by streak-plating onto tryptic soy agar with 100 ppm of NaL (TSA-NaL, Hardy Diagnostics, MD, USA) and bile esculin agar with 100 ppm of nalidixic acid (BEA-NaL) (Hardy Diagnostics, MD, USA), respectively. Plates were incubated at 35°C for 24 h for S. Typhimurium and 48 h for E. faecium to obtain single colonies as pure cultures.

## Preparation of bacterial inoculum
S. Typhimurium and E. faecium were prepared separately. Two single colonies from the TSA-NaL (S. Typhimurium) and BEA-NaL (E. faecium) agars were picked using a sterile plastic loop, aseptically transferred into 10 ml of TSB-NaL, and then incubated at 35°C for 24 h. After incubation, broth cultures of the pathogen and the surrogate bacteria were washed with 0.1 % buffered peptone water (BPW, Hardy Diagnostics, MD, USA) by centrifugation at 5000 x g for 15 min using a microcentrifuge (VWR Symphony 4417, VWR International, Radnor, PA). The pellets were then resuspended in 10 ml of 0.1 % BPW. The two bacterial suspensions were combined and then serially diluted 100-fold in 0.1 % BPW to determine the initial bacterial cell concentration of the inoculum (7.5 log10 CFU/ml).

## Manufacturing and inoculation of chicken breast samples
Frozen, bone-less chicken breasts were purchased from a local grocery store (The Kroger Co., Morgantown, West Virginia) and thawed in a walk-in cooler at 5°C for 12 to 14 h. Thawed chicken breast was manually cut into small pieces using pre-sanitized knives, then weighed and portioned into 500 g batches, with 6 batches prepared per replicate. Each batch (500 g) of chicken was fine-ground in a benchtop meat grinder and then inoculated with 20 ml NaL resistant S. Typhimurium or E. faecium, followed by the addition of NaCl (0,1.0, 3.0, or 5.0 %) plus Na-tripolyphosphate (0.5 %) solutions to achieve pump rate of 8 %.

## Thermal inactivation of chicken breast samples
The inoculated, moisture-enhanced, ground chicken breast samples were weighed into 10-g portions and added to filtered food bags (WhirlPak® filter bag (19 × 30 cm, Nasco, Modesto, CA, U.S.A), vacuum packaged and stored at 4°C for 42 h before thermal treatments. After vacuum packaging, the samples were evenly spread along the side of filtered sample bags to ensure uniform heat distribution during thermal processing. A lab-scale circulating water bath (Fisher Scientific, Fair Lawn, NY, U.S.A) was set and pre-heated to 62°C, 66°C, 70°C and 74°C. Samples were then fully submerged in the water bath and treated for 0, 15, 30, 45, 60, 70, 80, 90, 100, 110, 120, 140, 180, and 240 s, respectively.

## Microbiological analyses
Following thermal processing, 10 mL of refrigerated TSB plus 0.1 % sodium pyruvate (Fisher Scientific, Fair Lawn, NY, U.S.A) was added to each filter bag containing the cooked chicken samples. The bags were then pummeled for 2 min using a laboratory stomacher (Microbiology International, Frederick, MD, U.S.A). The liquid extract from the filtered side of the sample bags was serially diluted in 9.0 or 9.9 ml of 0.1 % BPW, and 100 µl of each dilution was spread-plated onto TSA-NaL for S. Typhimurium and BEA-NaL for E. faecium, respectively. Plates were incubated at 35°C for 48 h, after which colonies were manually counted and recorded as colony-forming-units (CFU). All bacterial counts were converted to log10 CFU/g, with a detection limit of 0.3 log10 CFU/g.

## Statistical analysis and data modeling
The entire experiment was conducted twice independently, using duplicate samples consisting of 5 chicken patties (10 g each). A 2 × 4 × 14 factorial design was used, with 2 bacterial strains, 4 heating temperatures, and 14 time points.
The “D-value” is defined as the time needed to achieve a 90 % reduction, equivalent to a 1-log/unit decrease of the target microorganism in a specific food product at a constant heating temperature ( ). To estimate d-values for each salt and temperature treatment for S. Typhimurium and E. faecium, the mean initial CFUs were calculated for each treatment combination. To calculate thermal inactivation of 90 %, one was subtracted from these initial mean populations. Thus, each treatment combination had a different thermal inactivation threshold.
To calculate d-values for each treatment, survival data of S. Typhimurium and E. faecium cells were first analyzed using an orthogonal polynomial model (6 degrees for E. faecium and 3 degrees for S. Typhimurium) with time as a continuous predictor, and crossed with salt percentage and temperature as categorical predictors (using lm in R version 4.4.1, R ), such that the relationship between bacterial CFUs and time were calculated for each salt treatment and temperature combination. Orthogonal polynomial degrees were added to each model until the residuals were as homogenous and normally distributed as possible, tested using the package DHARMa ( ), although some time point/treatment combinations had greater variance than others, violating assumptions. Separate models were developed for each bacterial species separately, and a Bonferroni correction to adjust p-values (comparisons: alpha = 0.05/2 = 0.025) were used in this analysis.
D-values for each treatment combination were calculated based on these orthogonal polynomial models by estimating where the model prediction and lower and upper confidence intervals intersected the thermal inactivation threshold. In addition, the average of thermal kinetic parameters of each cooking treatment were calculated by Weibull Model using the United States Department of Agriculture (USDA)-Global-Fit software according to the procedures described in . The differences of each individual comparison of d-values were determined by Tukey’s HSD with the significance level at α=0.05.

## Machine learning modeling for predictive analysis
Three machine learning models were employed to predict microbial inhibition under various thermal and salt treatments in reconstructed ground chicken meat: Random Forest Regression (RF), Support Vector Regression (SVR), and Bayesian Ridge Regression (BLR) with polynomial features. These models were selected based on their suitability for handling nonlinear relationships, capturing feature interactions across diverse experimental conditions, and mitigating overfitting in prediction, which are critical for biological data ( ). RF is an ensemble learning method based on decision trees that constructs multiple decision trees and aggregates their predictions to improve accuracy and robustness ( ). Each tree is trained on a random subset of data using bootstrapping, and at each split, only a subset of features is considered, reducing overfitting while maintaining interpretability. SVR is a kernel-based regression method which aims to find a function that fits the data while maintaining a margin of tolerance (ε-insensitive loss), meaning that small deviations from the true values are ignored ( ). The model minimizes a combination of prediction error and complexity, controlled by a regularization parameter (C). In this study, the radial basis function (RBF) kernel was employed, as it effectively maps the input features into a higher-dimensional space where nonlinear relationships can be approximated as linear. BLR is a probabilistic extension of linear regression that incorporates prior distributions over model coefficients, which is particularly useful when working with small to moderately sized datasets ( ). In this study, BLR was applied with polynomial features to better determine the higher-order interactions between temperature, time, and salt ratios.
The process of the machine learning model development is illustrated in . The dataset was collected from bench-scale experiments under different thermal treatments as described in this section. The microbial reduction (log₁₀ CFU/g) was recorded as the target variable. The independent variable feature set was defined to include temperature, salt concentration, exposure time, and microorganism species which was converted into numeric values with E. faecium mapped to 0 and Salmonella mapped to 1. The dataset was split into training (80 %) and testing (20 %) sets. Given the sensitivity of SVR and BLR to feature scaling, predictors were standardized using Scikit-learn’s Standard Scaler. Additionally, a correlation analysis was conducted to examine potential multicollinearity among the input features. The Pearson correlation coefficients were computed and visualized using a heatmap to assess the relationships within the feature set. Hyperparameter optimization was performed using Halving Grid Search CV with 10-fold cross-validation to identify the optimal configurations for each model. The key parameters tuned ( ) included the number of estimators, maximum depth, minimum samples split, minimum samples leaf, and maximum features for RF; the kernel function, regularization parameter (C), epsilon, and gamma for SVR; and the polynomial feature degree along with Bayesian Ridge regularization parameters (alpha_1, alpha_2, lambda_1, and lambda_2) for BLR, set based on the coefficient of determination (R²) score. This rigorous optimization ensured that the predictive models captured the underlying relationships in the data effectively. 
The models were evaluated on the test set using Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE) to quantify the prediction error and R² to assess the proportion of variance explained by the model. Additionally, visual techniques were conducted to evaluate the prediction quality. The scatter plot of predicted vs. actual values was used to examine the alignment with true observations, and the residual plot was employed to identify potential biases or patterns in the errors. The feature importance plot was used to provide insights into the relative contributions of each predictor. Specifically, the RF feature importance was derived from the impurity-based rankings to determine predictor contributions, and SVR feature importance was based on the permutation importance, while BLR feature importance was inferred from the learned coefficients.

## Results

## Survival of salmonella during thermal treatments
As shown in , for 62°C, the initial S. Typhimurium cell counts in chicken samples was 6.70 log10 CFU/g. After heating for 240 s, they decreased to 0.30 to 0.86 log10 CFU/g across all tested salt concentrations (1-5 %, ). Survival curves of chicken samples containing 3 and 5 % salt showed slightly greater heat resistance compared to those with 0 and 1 % salt, as evidenced by 0.3 to 0.6 log10 CFU/g lower reductions in bacterial counts ( ). The initial S. Typhimurium population was 7.03 to 7.24 log10 CFU/g in the samples processed at 66°C. After heating for 140 s, bacterial load decreased to 0.30 to 0.66 log10 CFU/g (P < 0.05). Compared to chicken samples without added salt, the inclusion of 1-5 % of salt increased S. Typhimurium reductions from 6.37 to 6.78 to 6.94 log10 CFU/g, indicating a slight increase in heat susceptibility ( ). The initial S. Typhimurium population was 6.36 to 6.94 log10 CFU/g in the samples processed at 70°C. After heating for 100 s, bacterial load decreased to 0.3 to 1.69 log10 CFU/g (P < 0.05). Survival curves for samples containing 1 %, 3 % and 5 % salt indicated greater heat susceptibility compared to the 0 % salt samples, with bacterial reductions increasing by 0.62 to 1.58 log10 CFU/g (P < 0.05, ). The initial S. Typhimurium population was 6.15 to 6.29 log10 CFU/g in the samples processed at 74°C. After heating for 70 s, the bacterial population decreased to 0.40 to 1.63 log10 CFU/g (P < 0.05). Survival curves for samples containing 5 % salt showed the greatest heat resistance as indicated by the significantly lower (P < 0.05) bacterial reduction of 4.61 log10 CFU/g, compared to reductions ranging from 5.21 to 5.83 log10 CFU/g in samples containing 0 to 3 % of salt ( ). 

## Survival of the surrogate bacteria E. faecium during thermal treatments
As shown in , the initial E. faecium population was 6.03 to 6.58 log10 CFU/g in samples processed at 62°C. After heating for 240 s, the surrogate cell counts significantly decreased to the range of 4.17 to 5.55 log10CFU/g (P < 0.05, ). Cooked chicken containing 1 to 5 % salt showed significantly lower (P < 0.05) bacterial reductions, ranging from 1.02 to 1.34 log10 CFU/g, compared to 1.86 log10CFU/g in samples without added salt ( ). The initial E. faecium cell counts were 6.08 to 6.37 log10 CFU/g in the samples processed at 66°C. After heating for 140 s, surrogate cell counts decreased to a range of 2.91 to 3.55 log10 CFU/g across all tested salt concentrations, with reduction differences of less than 0.5 log10 CFU/g ( ). The initial E. faecium cell population ranged from 5.42 to 6.32 log10 CFU/g for samples processed at 70°C. After heating for 100 s, the surrogate cell counts decreased to a range of 2.05 to 3.47 log10 CFU/g (P < 0.05, ). Survival curves for samples containing 1–5 % salt showed no significant differences in heat susceptibility, with reductions ranging from 2.95 to 3.37 log10 CFU/g, only marginally higher than those in samples without added salt (P > 0.05, ). The initial surrogate population was 5.28 to 6.09 log10 CFU/g in samples processed at 74°C. After heating for 70 s, the cell counts decreased to a range of 1.65 to 3.65 log10 CFU/g (P < 0.05, ). Survival curves for chicken samples without added salt showed the lowest heat susceptibility compared to those containing 1 % to 5 % salt. Samples with 1 % and 3 % salt exhibited significantly greater reductions of 1.4 to 1.7 log10 CFU/g, while those with 5 % salt showed an even higher reduction of 2.8 log10 CFU/g (P < 0.05, ). 

## D-values of Salmonella and E. faecium calculated by Weibull model
As expected, the d-values of S. Typhimurium and E. faecium decreased with increasing heating temperatures regardless of the salt concentrations. As shown in , d-values of S. Typhimurium for 62 to 74°C ranged from 12.2 to 30.8 s, 16.3 to 45.9 s, 16.9 to 52.5 s, and 16.6 to 48.2 s for chicken samples containing 0, 1, 3, and 5 % salt, respectively. d-values of E. faecium heated at 62 to 74°C ranged from 21.5 to 129.5 s, 16.3 to 208.0 s, 16.1 to 753.2 s, and 14.7 to 349.0 s for chicken samples with 0, 1, 3, and 5 % added salt, respectively ( ). 
Heating temperature and salt concentrations both significantly affected the heat resistance of S. Typhimurium. For chicken meat heated at 62°C, adding 1 to 5 % of salt increased (P < 0.05) the d-values of Salmonella from 30.8 to 45.9-52.5 s compared to the samples without added salt. A similar trend was observed in samples heated at 74°C, where d-values significantly increased (P < 0.05) from 12.2 s to 16.3-16.9 s as salt concentrations increased from 0 to 1-5 %. No significant differences in d-values were observed for samples heated at 66°C and 70°C, except for those with 5 % salt at 70°C, which showed a significantly lower d-value of 17.0 seconds (P < 0.05) compared to all other treatments. For E. faecium, d-values at 62°C significantly increased (P < 0.05) from 129.5 s to a range of 208.0–753.2 s as salt concentrations increased from 0 % to 1–5 %. However, at higher temperatures (66°C to 74°C), the inclusion of 1–5 % salt significantly reduced (P < 0.05) d-values from 21.5 to 57.9 seconds to 14.7–30.7 seconds compared to samples without added salt ( ).

## Thermal inactivation of Salmonella and E. faecium calculated by polynomial models
Using orthogonal polynomial models to estimate the relationship between log10CFU and time for each treatment combination, it was found that higher salt concentrations and temperature significantly increased the rate of bacterial inactivation for both S. Typhimurium ( ) and E. faecium ( ). Salmonella inactivation was influenced by time, salt concentration and temperature (fit of model: F63,821 = 212, P < 0.0001). Salt concentrations affected S. Typhimurium inactivation at all temperatures tested, although the degree and direction in which this occurred varied (interaction of temperature, NaCl concentration, and time: F27,821 = 12.8, P < 0.0001; Tukey HSD, α = 0.05). At 62 °C, NaCl slowed S. Typhimurium inactivation rates at 3 and 5 % concentrations, while at 66 °C and 70 °C NaCl concentrations at 5 % increased the rate of inactivation compared to the control. At 74 °C, NaCl concentrations did not significantly change the rate of inactivation of S. Typhimurium compared to the no salt control. d-values were also impacted by salt concentrations and temperatures, with thermal inactivation occurring more quickly at 74 °C at all salt concentrations and increasing salt concentrations decreasing d-values at 70 °C ( ). 
For E. faecium, bacterial reductions occurred at all temperatures, although the shape and rate of at inactivation depended on both salt concentration and temperatures (fit of model: F111,828 = 159.9, P < 0.0001). Salt concentration had no significant effect on the thermal inactivation of E. faecium at 62 °C or 66 °C; however, a 5 % salt concentration significantly increased the inactivation rate at 70 °C and 74 °C compared to the control (interaction of temperature, NaCl concentration, and time: F54,828 = 5.7, P < 0.0001; Tukey HSD, α = 0.05). d-values showed that thermal inactivation occurred more quickly at 74 °C and 70 °C at all salt concentrations with little effect of salt concentration ( ).

## Comparison of Salmonella vs E. faecium
Survival curves in , show that reductions of S. Typhimurium in samples containing 0 to 5 % added salt at the end of the heating period ranged from 5.84 to 6.41, 6.37–6.94, 5.06–6.64, and 4.61–5.83 log₁₀ CFU/g at 62°C, 66°C, 70°C, and 74°C, respectively. These reductions were significantly higher (P < 0.05) than those of E. faecium which ranged from 1.02 to 1.86, 2.69 to 3.05, 2.85 to 3.37, and 1.63 to 4.44 log₁₀ CFU/g at the same respective temperatures ( , ).
In general, the d-values of E. faecium at all tested heating temperatures were greater or similar to S. Typhimurium among the samples containing added salt ( , ). For the samples without added salt, d-values of the surrogate were greater (P < 0.05) than those of Salmonella as shown as 129.5 vs 30.8 s, 57.9 vs 29.7 s, 41.0 vs 27.3 s, and 21.5 vs 12.2 s, for 62, 66, 70, 74°C heated samples, respectively ( ). For samples containing 1 % of salt, d-values of E. faecium at 62°C and 66°C were greater (P < 0.05) than S. Typhimurium as shown as 208.0 vs 45.9 s and 50.7 vs 32.6 s, respectively ( ). At heating temperatures of 70°C and 74°C, the d-values of E. faecium did not differ significantly (P > 0.05) from those of S. Typhimurium, with values of 27.9 vs 27.3 s and 16.3 vs 16.3 s, respectively ( ). For samples containing 3 % and 5 % salt, d-values between 66°C and 74°C were statistically comparable for E. faecium and S. Typhimurium across all samples ( ).
As shown in , based on the orthogonal polynomial model, d-values for S. Typhimurium were significantly smaller (P < 0.05) than those for E. faecium across all heating temperatures. Specifically, d-values ranged from 11.8 to 45.2 s versus 29.0 to 152.0 s, 17.9 to 37.1 s versus 18.7 to 151.0 s, 13.9 to 34.2 s versus 16.5 to 194.1 s, and 14.2 to 35.2 s versus 15.3 to 138.8 s for samples containing 0 %, 1 %, 3 %, and 5 % salt, respectively.

## Analysis on machine learning model performance
The optimized hyperparameters for each model, along with their respective performance metrics, are summarized in . Overall, all three models exhibited high R² values, indicating strong predictive capability in capturing the relationship between input features and bacterial counts. RMSE values were relatively low across all models, suggesting that the predictions closely aligned with actual values. Among the evaluated models, the RF model demonstrated the highest predictive accuracy. Optimized with a maximum depth of 10, an estimator count of 500, and unrestricted feature selection, the RF model achieved an RMSE of 0.4216 and an R² of 0.9358, demonstrating its strong predictive performance for bacterial inhibition across varying thermal and salt conditions. The SVR model, utilizing an RBF kernel with C = 10 and epsilon=0.1, followed closely with an RMSE of 0.4501 and an R² of 0.9268. The BLR model, optimized with polynomial features of degree 3 and Bayesian regularization, had the lowest predictive power, with an RMSE of 0.5400 and an R² of 0.8946. Pearson correlation analysis ( ) revealed minimal multicollinearity among input features, except for a moderate negative correlation (−0.45) between temperature and time, suggesting a potential interaction effect. This aligns with biological expectations, as microbial reduction rates are inherently dependent on cumulative thermal exposure. However, the inclusion of polynomial terms in BLR partially captured this interaction, though it did not outperform RF or SVR in predictive accuracy. 
 presents the scatter plots comparing predicted versus actual values for each model. The close alignment of data points with the 1:1 reference line confirms that all models effectively captured the underlying patterns, particularly for moderate to high bacterial counts. However, predictions at lower bacterial counts exhibited greater variability, reflected by a wider spread of points around the reference line. Residual distribution plots in further validate model reliability. Residuals were generally centered around zero, indicating minimal systematic bias. However, at lower bacterial counts, residual spread increased, suggesting slightly reduced predictive accuracy in this range. Despite this, residual distributions remained random without discernible trends, reinforcing the robustness of these models in microbial count modeling under various thermal and salt treatments. 
Feature importance in was assessed for each model to understand the relative influence of the various thermal and salt treatments on predictive performance. For RF, impurity-based feature importance indicated that temperature was the most important predictor, followed by exposure time and salt concentration. For SVR, permutation importance was calculated, and the results showed a similar trend with temperature being the most important, although the effects of each predictor were more dispersed. The coefficients for BLR also confirmed the primary role of temperature for predictive modeling of microbial inhibition. 

## Discussion
The impact of salt on bacterial survival during thermal process was documented by , who reported that exposure to NaCl induces osmotic adaption, increasing the thermal resistance of Listeria monocytogenes in modified tryptic phosphate broth and minced beef. However, little information is available related to the effect of salt concentrations on the thermal resistance of Salmonella in moisture-enhanced reconstructed chicken products. Therefore, this study investigated the effect of added 1 %, 3 %, and 5 % salt solutions on the thermal resistance of S. Typhimurium in chicken meat.
Applying predictive mathematical equations in various models to describe the thermal inactivation kinetics creates the opportunity to predict the pathogen and their surrogate bacteria survival within a very wide range of processing conditions. The USDA IPMP-Global Fit software package, which includes both Linear and Weibull survival models, was used in this study to the determine the d-values of Salmonella and E. faecium in chicken meat with added salt concentrations ranging from 0 % to 5 %. Based on the calculated AIC and RMSE values, the Weibull model provided the best fit for the survival curves, while the Linear model was inadequate, as indicated by RMSE values exceeding 1 in most treatments.
The "D-value" represents the time required to reduce the population of a target microorganism by 90 %, or one logarithmic unit, in a given food product when exposed to a constant temperature ( ). Consequently, microorganisms with higher d-values demonstrate increased thermal resistance relative to those with lower d-values. Results of this study showed that d-values of Salmonella at 62°C and 74°C significantly increased in samples containing 1 % to 5 % added salt compared to samples without added salt, consistent with the survival curves as shown in . These results are consistent with the study by , which reported that adding 1.5 % to 4.5 % salt to salmon roe significantly increased the thermal resistance of L. monocytogenes. Specifically, the d-values at 62.5°C increased from 0.48 min to between 1.00 and 2.40 min as added salt concentrations increased from 0 % to 4.5 %. However, the d-values of Salmonella at 66 and 70°C did not significantly differ across the various salt concentrations. reported no significant differences in Salmonella counts among chicken base paste formulated with regular salt, 45 % reduced salt and 90 % reduced salt after 21 w of storage at 21°C. These findings are consistent with the statistical analysis in the present study, which suggests that Salmonella inactivation in chicken meat is influenced by salt concentrations, heating temperature, and their interactions.
Machine learning approaches offer significant advantages in predictive microbiology, which enables accurate modeling of microbial survival under dynamic processing conditions ( ). The results of this study demonstrated that RF, SVR, and BLR effectively capture nonlinear interactions and complex dependencies between temperature, salt concentration, and microbial response. Among these models, RF demonstrated the best performance, highlighting its effectiveness in modeling the data. The slightly lower performance of SVR may be from the limitations in kernel-based transformations when handling highly nonlinear microbial inactivation patterns, potentially due to its sensitivity to hyperparameter tuning and data distribution ( ). BLR showed the weakest predictive accuracy, attributed to its reliance on polynomial feature transformations. The higher residual error in BLR indicates that linearized polynomial approximations could be inadequate for modeling microbial responses under varying environmental conditions. Discrepancies observed in the residual plots of all models suggest that additional factors, such as bacterial subpopulation variability and thermotolerance mechanisms, may contribute to deviations in model predictions ( ). Evaluating predictive model behavior under changing processing conditions is critical for food safety applications ( ). The findings of this study highlight the potential of machine learning in microbiological predictive analysis, demonstrating their ability to capture microbial inhibition under varying processing conditions ( ). These results have significant implications for designing data-driven food safety interventions, enabling real-time adjustments in thermal processing to mitigate microbial risks. Future studies should explore the integration of physicochemical attributes, such as water activity and protein denaturation rates, to further enhance the predictive robustness of machine learning models.
In recent years, increased interest in how surrogate microorganisms respond to thermal processing has led to research focused on identifying effective alternatives to foodborne pathogens ( ). According to Hu and Gurtler’s (2017) review, an ideal surrogate should be safe (non-pathogenic), simple to cultivate, stable under typical handling conditions, adaptable to a range of environments, and demonstrate similar or greater resistance to interventions like thermal processing. E. faecium meets the criteria for a suitable surrogate for Salmonella because of its resilience across a broad temperature range (5–65°C), pH levels (4.5–10.0), and elevated salt concentrations (6.5 %) ( ). demonstrated that E. faecium exhibits greater thermal resistance than Salmonella in a complex carbohydrate-protein matrix and requires higher temperatures to achieve a 5-log reduction (73.7°C vs. 60.6°C) and complete inactivation of bacterial cells (80.3°C vs. 68°C). Similarly, reported that the d-values of E. faecium in thermally processed pet food with 9 % moisture exceeded those of seven tested Salmonella strains at 76.7°C (11.7 vs. 6.5 min), 82.2°C (4.1 vs. 2.7 min), and 87.8°C (1.7 vs. 1.1 min). Previous studies ( ; ) have indicated that the thermal resistance of E. faecium is primarily influenced by factors such as its growth phase, membrane composition, lipid and fatty acid content, and the activity of sigma factors. In the present study, E. faecium was cultivated at 35°C; compared to growth at higher temperatures (40–45°C), this relatively lower temperature may promote an increase in saturated fatty acids and a corresponding decrease in unsaturated fatty acids. This shift reduces membrane fluidity, thereby enhancing thermal resistance ( ; ). Additionally, consistent with prior findings ( ; ), the cells were in the stationary growth phase, during which alternative sigma factors may be activated. These factors can redirect RNA polymerase activity toward the transcription of stress response genes, leading to the production of proteins that enhance cellular protection against heat stress ( ).
The findings of this study are consistent with previous research and further support the suitability of E. faecium as a surrogate for Salmonella during thermal processing, as demonstrated using both Weibull and orthogonal polynomial models. In our previous study involving raw chicken products, E. faecium, whether unstressed or subjected to cold stress, exhibited equal or greater resistance compared to Salmonella when exposed to four antimicrobial treatments: peroxyacetic acid, lactic acid, a lactic/citric acid blend, and chlorinated water ( ). Similarly, reported that the d-values of Salmonella in double pan-broiled chicken meat ranged from 127 to 156 seconds at 300°F and 108–119.8 seconds at 400°F, both of which were lower than the corresponding d-values for E. faecium, which ranged from 168 to 185 sec and 116–122 sec, respectively.
In conclusion, results of this study suggested that 1) Salmonella and the surrogate bacteria E. faecium were significantly affected by heating temperature, time and salt concentrations during thermal inactivation process of chicken patties; 2) heating temperature was the most important predictor, followed by exposure time and salt concentrations; and 3) E. faecium can be used as a surrogate for Salmonella to validate thermal inactivation in chicken products. These results provide valuable information for conducting risk-assessment of Salmonella in intact and nonintact chicken products.
