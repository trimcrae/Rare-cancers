The design features of gambling websites blur the lines between ‘product’ and ‘environment’ in the traditional triad of risk factors for gambling harm. As North American markets expand, quantifying the risk of website design becomes key for research and regulation.

Gambling research often acknowledges the ‘epidemiological triad’ of factors that influence gambling harm, operating at the level of the person, the product and the environment [ ]. The recent article by Newall [ ] astutely recognizes how gambling platforms (i.e. the gambling website or app) serves as an intermediary between the product (e.g. a slot machine or sports betting) and the environment (e.g. on‐line accessibility), to amplify the risks of on‐line gambling.
Behavioural accounts of the structural characteristics of gambling games have long‐recognized situational factors within the gambling venue, from casino architecture to the distance to the bar or automated teller machine [ , ]. In the digital world, the gambling website exemplifies this level of analysis, with a vast array of micro‐decisions for the website designer, from the colour scheme, to payment settings, to the salience of safer gambling tools. Newall presents a taxonomy of these design features, drawing on the darker side of the influential nudge framework.
Over the past decade, on‐line gambling has expanded globally [ ] and intensified [ ], with the emergence of new forms (e.g. esports betting), greater diversity (e.g. on sports matches in other time zones) and new types of bets (e.g. micro‐bets on short term outcomes, such as the next serve in tennis). The fact that this degree of choice is accessible within the same platform [ ] means that the gambler can select among thousands of individual bets within just a few clicks. Such abundance may re‐frame their decision‐making from which bet to choose, to ‘How many bets to choose?’
In North America, the expansion of sports betting and on‐line gambling has been inextricably linked. In Canada, gambling has historically been controlled by provincial monopolies. In 2021, a federal bill to legalize single‐event sports betting lead to Ontario's decision to privatise its on‐line gambling sector [ ]. Ontario has moved from having one licensed gambling website to over 80, in less than 3 years. Similar movements have been underway in the United States since 2018, with over 30 states having legalised on‐line sports betting [ ]. Within Canada, the public debate to date has concentrated on the rise in advertising triggered by a competitive market and has largely overlooked the shift in sports betting as a gambling product, to a faster and continuous ‘in‐play’ format that might facilitate loss‐chasing behaviour [ ].
Newall's article generates a key question: as the number of licensed gambling platforms in a jurisdiction proliferates, are these websites effectively facsimiles of each other or are there meaningful differences in website design that could predict gambling harm? For the Ontario sports betting sites, most platforms appear to offer standard features like in‐play betting and ‘cash out’ options [ ], as well as links to affiliated eCasino sites that highlight the potential for cross‐selling [ ]. However, industry innovation also creates differences across sites. One emerging feature is streamed video of sports, sports commentary or match highlights. If gambling operators can navigate the broadcasting rights so that customers can watch what they are betting on, this would likely increase ‘time on device’ [ ] and presumably the likelihood of repeated betting.
Torrance et al. [ ] examined patents related to sports betting as a way of anticipating new features. Some patents described features to encourage competition between sports bettors such as leaderboards, and others focused on notifications as an aspect of the user's device. Combining these elements, DraftKings Social is an integrated social networking tool that offers account holders the ability to follow other users, send direct messages and create posts such as bets made or won. As discussed in a candid webinar by Orbach [ ], an overarching goal of these platforms is to keep the customer within the one app, rather than multi‐tasking across different platforms for watching sport, messaging friends, and so on.
The expansion in North America shows little sign of abating. Alberta has recently introduced a bill to follow Ontario's lead with a privatised market [ ]. A natural question is whether the design features of the licensed websites in Ontario differ systematically from the provincial monopoly websites, given that monopolies aim to balance revenue generation with public safety [ ]. For policy makers, the quantification of risky platform design is a critical topic, yet this is hugely challenging given the limited evidence base [ ]. Considering the ease and speed with which gambling operators can code new design features and test their impacts on revenue, the need for regulators to anticipate upcoming gambling technologies and their likely negative consequences is greater than ever.
