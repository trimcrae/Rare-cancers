## Abstract

## Background
Despite global gains from immunization, vaccination coverage in rural Nigeria remains suboptimal. Barriers include poor healthcare access, cultural beliefs, vaccine hesitancy, misinformation, and communication gaps. Standardized campaigns often fail to resonate with local realities. This study explored the use of culturally adapted local music, a familiar and trusted medium, to deliver relatable immunization messages. The aim was to develop and assess the impact of culturally tailored audio messages on maternal and infant vaccination knowledge, attitudes, and willingness among rural women.

## Methods
A mixed-methods design was employed. Twenty caregivers participated in Key Informant Interviews guided by the Health Belief Model, and data were thematically analyzed to inform culturally relevant messages. These were adapted into three versions of local music and delivered sequentially in a single workshop session to enhance comprehension, retention, and engagement. Effectiveness was assessed immediately before and after the intervention using structured surveys. Data were analyzed with the Wilcoxon Signed-Rank Test; and effect size was calculated using Cohen’s r, with significance set at p ≤ 0.05.

## Results
Qualitative findings (N = 20) revealed low perceived susceptibility to vaccine-preventable diseases, misconceptions about vulnerability, reliance on traditional remedies, and barriers such as mistrust, limited access, and gender dynamics. Social influences and health worker guidance emerged as important cues to action. These insights shaped the culturally adapted messages. In the quantitative phase (N = 193), knowledge scores increased significantly post-intervention (Z = 4.155, p < 0.001, r ≈ 0.30), indicating a moderate effect. Attitudes showed a positive but non-significant trend (p = 0.127), and willingness to vaccinate remained unchanged (p = 0.809). Participant feedback indicated that the familiar, music-based format enhanced message relatability and perceived trustworthiness, fostering dialogue and suggesting potential cumulative effects if sustained.

## Conclusion
Culturally tailored audio messaging rooted in local music significantly enhanced vaccination knowledge and perceived trustworthiness. Although no immediate behavioural change was observed, the participatory process fostered early perceptual shifts that may facilitate long-term uptake. This approach offers a promising and replicable strategy for advancing maternal and child health while promoting health equity in similar resource-limited settings.

## Background
Vaccination remains one of the most effective and successful public health interventions, credited with saving at least 154 million lives over the past 50 years and significantly reducing morbidity and mortality from infectious diseases globally [ , ]. Despite this, vaccine-preventable diseases continue to cause a significant burden in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), where immunization coverage often falls short of global targets [ ]. In Nigeria, maternal and infant vaccination coverage remains suboptimal, with notable disparities between urban and rural areas [ ]. Barriers such as misinformation, cultural beliefs, gender dynamics, health system weaknesses, and limited accessibility contribute to persistent vaccine hesitancy and missed opportunities for immunization, with significant implications for public health and community well-being [ , ].
In rural areas, where healthcare access is often limited and traditional beliefs are deeply ingrained, conventional public health campaigns frequently fail to resonate with local populations [ ]. This disconnect can foster mistrust, vaccine hesitancy, or outright refusal, ultimately leading to lower immunization rates and increased vulnerability to vaccine-preventable diseases. Cultural beliefs, values, and language diversity significantly shape health behaviours, including vaccination decisions, and neglecting these cultural nuances can undermine the effectiveness of standard health messages [ , ].
Globally, culturally tailored health communication has gained increasing recognition for its ability to address behavioural barriers to vaccine uptake [ ]. Evidence suggests that interventions designed with cultural relevance, by aligning health messages with local values, norms, and communication styles are more likely to influence attitudes and practices than generic approaches [ ]. Interventions that fail to resonate with local realities often have limited impact. The HPV vaccination study in Nigeria demonstrated that culturally inappropriate communication and provider attitudes, including shaming or inadequate engagement, can discourage participation in immunization programs [ ]. This underscores the need for culturally tailored approaches that align health messaging with community norms and expectations to foster acceptance and uptake.
In rural African communities, where oral traditions and music hold strong cultural significance, community-based and culturally adapted strategies have been shown to enhance trust, comprehension, and engagement with health information [ , ]. Previous initiatives, such as the IMPRINT public engagement project, demonstrated the promise of culturally adapted methods, where “artist-scientist” collaborations effectively engaged traditional birth attendants and highlighted the potential of participatory cultural approaches in health communication [ ].
In Nigeria, several studies have demonstrated the influence of local storytelling, songs, and community leaders in shaping health behaviours [ , ]. Despite growing interest in culturally tailored health campaigns, there is limited published evidence on the use of audio-based approaches for promoting vaccination among mothers and caregivers in rural Nigerian settings. This gap is particularly relevant given the increasing importance of innovative, context-specific strategies for improving vaccine acceptance in resource-limited areas.
Theoretical frameworks such as the Health Belief Model (HBM) and the Diffusion of Innovations (DOI) theory provide a useful lens for understanding how tailored messages may influence behaviour change [ ]. The HBM highlights the role of perceived susceptibility, severity, benefits, and barriers in shaping health decisions, while DOI underscores the importance of communication channels and social networks in spreading new practices. Leveraging these frameworks, culturally adapted audio messages, particularly those delivered through local music and narratives may enhance awareness, address misconceptions, and accelerate community acceptance of vaccination.
This study explored the cultural tailoring of vaccination messages through audio formats, designed in the local language and grounded in community input. By integrating participatory design and cultural narratives, the intervention used local music to bridge the gap between public health messaging and community acceptance. The project aimed to promote maternal and infant vaccination uptake in rural communities while generating evidence on the feasibility, acceptability, and potential impact of incorporating culturally relevant, music-based health communication into immunization programmes in Nigeria.

## Methods

## Study area
The study was conducted in Imo State, South Eastern Nigeria, located between longitude 5˚29′06″N and latitude 7˚02′06″E. The state lies between the lower River Niger and the upper and middle Imo River [ ]. It covers a landmass of 5,135 km2 and had an estimated population of 5.46 million in 2022, based on National Population Commission projections using the 2006 census as a baseline and applying a 2.1% annual growth rate [ ]. Administratively, Imo State is divided into 27 Local Government Areas (LGAs) and 305 registration areas (communities), distributed across three senatorial zones Owerri, Orlu, and Okigwe each with distinctive levels of social development, cultural practices, and traditional beliefs.

## Study design
The study employed a sequential mixed-methods design with explicit integration between phases. The qualitative findings directly informed the development of both the intervention messages and the quantitative instruments. This integrative approach ensured that the content was culturally grounded and that its effects were appropriately measured. The design was guided by the Health Belief Model (HBM) and the Diffusion of Innovations (DOI) theory. The first phase was a formative qualitative study involving key informant interviews (KIIs) with mothers and caregivers. Findings from the thematic analysis were used to develop vaccination messages that reflected the local context and addressed identified concerns. The second phase was a quantitative pre-post evaluation, in which interviewer-administered questionnaires were used to assess changes in knowledge, attitudes, and willingness to vaccinate. This two-phase mixed-methods design ensured that the intervention was contextually grounded through qualitative enquiry while its impact was empirically assessed through quantitative measurement. Together, these components strengthened both the cultural relevance and the effectiveness evaluation of the vaccination messaging intervention.

## Study population/sample size
The study population comprised mothers and caregivers residing in rural communities of Imo State. The inclusion criteria were mothers and caregivers residing in the selected rural communities, having at least one child under their care at the time of the study and willing to participate and provide informed consent. The minimum required sample size for the paired pre-post design was calculated using the standard normal approximation for paired comparisons [ ]. ## Sampling technique and selection criteria
A two-stage non-probability sampling approach was employed.

## Stage 1: purposive selection of the Local Government Areas (LGAs)
Four Local Government Areas (two each from Orlu and Owerri senatorial zones of Imo State) were purposively selected to access the rural communities and ensure representation of diverse rural contexts while also considering feasibility and safety of access. The prevailing security challenges in the study areas necessitated purposeful identification of communities where entry was possible and engagement with women could be safely organized.

## Stage 2: convenience sampling of participants
Within the rural communities in the selected LGAs, participants were recruited using convenience sampling. On pre-arranged safe days, the research team visited village squares and moved systematically in a given direction to engage women. Those who met the inclusion criteria were invited to participate and were informed to assemble on an agreed date and time for transportation to the workshop intervention venue.
Given the unpredictability of attendance due to security challenges, recruitment continued until 200 women were reached across all rural communities. This number was selected to allow for possible attrition, which was anticipated to be higher than usual under the prevailing circumstances. Although all 200 women presented at the venue and were enrolled, only 193, still above the minimum required sample size, completed both the pre- and post-intervention assessments and were therefore included in the final analysis.

## Theoretical frameworks
The study was guided by two primary theoretical frameworks. The Health Belief Model (HBM) was utilized during the formative phase to inform the development of key informant interview tools and the content of the culturally adapted vaccination messages. This model helped to explore participants' perceptions of susceptibility, severity, benefits, barriers, cues to action, and self-efficacy related to maternal and infant vaccination [ ]. The Diffusion of Innovations (DOI) theory provided a framework for evaluating the adoption and diffusion of the new messaging approach within the communities, guiding the design of the evaluation instruments [ ].

## Intervention development and implementation

## Formative phase
The formative phase of the intervention development involved a qualitative inquiry designed to explore maternal perceptions, beliefs, and contextual factors influencing childhood vaccination in rural communities. Data were collected through 20 Key Informant Interviews (KIIs) conducted with mothers and caregivers residing in selected rural areas of Imo State. An interview guide was developed based on the six constructs of the Health Belief Model (HBM), perceived susceptibility, perceived severity, perceived benefits, perceived barriers, cues to action, and self-efficacy to elicit insights into participants’ understanding and attitudes towards childhood vaccination. All interviews were audio-recorded, transcribed verbatim, and subjected to thematic analysis using a deductive-inductive approach. The deductive framework was guided by the HBM, ensuring systematic mapping of participants’ responses to established behavioural constructs. In addition, an inductive process was applied to identify themes that extended beyond the HBM framework.
During coding and analysis, responses were first categorized under the HBM constructs, after which emergent non-HBM themes were identified. These included cultural practices, traditional health beliefs, gendered decision-making patterns, perceived side effects, mistrust of health authorities, and logistical barriers such as distance to health facilities. Non-HBM themes were not excluded but were documented and analyzed alongside the main constructs to provide a comprehensive understanding of factors shaping vaccination behaviour. These findings were subsequently integrated into the message development process. For instance, insights regarding the influence of elders, local healers, and the use of herbal remedies informed the phrasing, tone, and cultural framing of the vaccination messages, ensuring their relevance and resonance with community values and norms.
This analytic strategy ensured that while the HBM provided the theoretical structure for data interpretation, the inclusion of inductively derived insights enhanced the cultural grounding and contextual validity of the intervention. The resulting messages therefore addressed both the behavioural constructs underpinning vaccine decision-making and the broader socio-cultural realities influencing maternal vaccination practices in rural settings.

## Vaccine message development
Based on the themes identified, vaccination messages were developed in English and subsequently translated into the Igbo language. These messages were then incorporated into three distinct versions of traditional-style songs, using local melodies to enhance cultural resonance, memorability and trust.

## Pretesting
The three versions of the traditional-style songs were pretested among a group of women in non-participating communities. This pretesting ensured the clarity, cultural appropriateness, and overall resonance of the messages and the feedback was used to refine lyrics, tone, and delivery.

## Sensitization workshop and intervention delivery
To implement the intervention, a sensitization workshop was conducted in Imo State University Teaching Hospital Auditorium in Orlu zone. For logistical efficiency, participants from both the Orlu and Owerri zones were transported to the venue. Upon arrival, participants were registered, and their phone numbers were collected to facilitate enrolment into a project-specific WhatsApp group created for post-workshop message dissemination and follow-up engagement. This platform was intended to promote repeated listening, reinforcement of key messages, and informal diffusion of information within participants’ social networks.
Following registration, participants completed a pre-intervention online questionnaire administered by trained interviewers to assess baseline knowledge, attitudes, and willingness to vaccinate. They were then exposed, over a three-hour workshop session, to three traditional music-styled audio messages on vaccination, which were played sequentially and cyclically to enhance comprehension and message retention. At the end of the session, participants completed an identical post-intervention questionnaire to evaluate immediate changes in knowledge, attitudes, and willingness. Audio files were subsequently distributed through the WhatsApp group to encourage voluntary repeated listening and peer sharing within their communities. This combined in-person and digital dissemination strategy aimed to maximize both message exposure and community-level diffusion beyond the initial workshop setting.

## Structured messaging strategy
The audio intervention was delivered using a systematic sequencing of traditional music versions. This approach was designed to optimize participant comprehension, message retention, and behavioural adoption through intentional layering of content. This intentional progression aligned with cognitive and behavioural learning principles to guide participants through an informative-to-persuasive continuum.

## Version 1: foundational knowledge track
The intervention began with Version 1, rich in detailed vaccination information including the purpose, benefits, safety, and schedule of maternal and infant immunization. This version was selected as the starting point because it provided the essential knowledge base required for participants to meaningfully engage with the post-intervention questionnaire. It acted as a cognitive anchor, establishing core understanding and framing the context for subsequent messages.

## Version 2: reinforcement and motivational tracks
Following Version 1, Version 2 built upon the foundational knowledge by adopting a simpler, slogan-style approach. It featured culturally familiar phrases and musical repetition, crafted to reinforce key vaccination messages in a more accessible and emotionally resonant manner. With a motivational tone, Version 2 delivered statements such as “Lets help our children,” “Prevention Vaccines is good,” “Let’s support vaccination”, “If we put hands together we can put an end to all diseases” e.t.c. This version acted as a bridge between knowledge and emotion, strengthening message recall and deepening engagement. Its primary function was to encourage social norm formation, promote collective endorsement, and foster a sense of shared responsibility for child health. By aligning emotional appeal with cultural identity, Version 2 played a critical role in motivating adoption and sustained behaviour change.

## Version 3: consolidated knowledge track
The sequence concluded with Version 3, which, like Version 1, also conveyed comprehensive vaccination information. This deliberate repetition of key facts but presented with a slightly varied melody and expression helped to consolidate learning and reinforce retention through familiar repetition.
The three versions of the traditional audio messages were presented in a deliberate, structured sequence, Version 1, followed by Version 2, then Version 3 repeated cyclically throughout the intervention. This systematic playback strategy ensured that participants were continuously exposed to both informative and motivational content in a rhythm that balanced message depth with accessibility. This layered messaging strategy aligned with adult learning principles and behaviour change communication theory, allowing the music to function not only as an educational tool but also as a culturally resonant trigger for sustained vaccine adoption. The structured rollout approach supported both comprehension and cultural relevance ensuring that participants received accurate information in a memorable, affirming, and persuasive format.
Ultimately, the approach demonstrated how content sequencing and repetition within traditional music can bridge the gap between knowledge acquisition and behaviour change, reinforcing health messages in a way that is both context-sensitive and impactful.

## Data collection and analysis
Two data collection tools were used; Qualitative tool (KII) adapted from the health belief model, captured perceptions of susceptibility, severity, benefits, barriers, cues to action, and self-efficacy and a quantitative tool (questionnaire) developed by the research team which captured socio-demographic information, vaccination knowledge, attitudes, and willingness to vaccinate (supplementary file). Quantitative data were collected using interviewer-administered questionnaires on Google Forms before and immediately after the intervention and exported to SPSS version 26 for analysis. Data was cleaned and validated manually to ensure accuracy and completeness. The outcomes measured were: knowledge (awareness of vaccines, their benefits, and their role in preventing childhood diseases), attitudes (beliefs and perceptions about vaccination, including trust and acceptance) and willingness (readiness to vaccinate one’s child in the future). Given the non-normal distribution of the outcome variables, the Wilcoxon Signed-Rank Test was applied to compare the differences in participants' knowledge, attitudes, and willingness to vaccinate before and after the intervention. For statistically significant findings, effect size was calculated using Cohen’s r to determine the practical significance of the observed changes. A level of significance was set at p ≤ ·05 with 95% confidence interval.

## Data management and ethical considerations
Participation in the study was entirely voluntary, and informed consent was obtained electronically before respondents could proceed to the questionnaire. Participants were informed about the study objectives, procedures, potential risks, and their right to withdraw at any stage without penalty.
To ensure anonymity, each participant was assigned a unique numbered disk, which were plastic number tags randomly distributed at registration. These identifiers replaced names on the questionnaire and were used solely to link pre- and post-intervention questionnaire responses without compromising confidentiality. Importantly, disk numbers were not recorded on the registration forms, which contained participants’ names and phone numbers. At the end of the workshop, participants submitted their numbered disks. Registration forms were accessible only to the research team, and there was no direct link between the disks and personal identifiers, thereby safeguarding confidentiality. To minimize social desirability bias, questionnaires were completed privately on mobile devices, with participants reassured that there were no right or wrong answers and that responses would be used solely for research. The dataset generated from the online questionnaires was regularly reviewed for accuracy, consistency, and completeness. All data were stored securely with access restricted to the research team.
Ethical approval for the study was obtained from the Research Ethics Committee of Imo State University Teaching Hospital, Orlu, Nigeria with ethical clearance number IMSUTH/CS/034. The research was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and complied with all relevant institutional and national guidelines for research involving human participants.

## Results

## Qualitative analysis (key informant interviews)
Thematic analysis, guided by the constructs of the Health Belief Model (HBM), highlighted six domains that shaped caregivers’ vaccination behaviours and directly informed the development of the culturally tailored audio messages.
Codes generated from the transcripts were grouped under constructs of the Health Belief Model (HBM), which provided the analytical framework for theme development. The mapping process involved identifying statements reflecting each construct as illustrated below;
APerceived susceptibility: awareness and perception of risk
Respondents varied in their perceptions of their children’s susceptibility to vaccine-preventable diseases.i.Low perceived risk: Some believed good hygiene and nutrition were sufficient protection.
“My children dey very strong, we keep them clean every time. I don’t see how they will catch those sickness.” (Mother, 26 yrs, R4)
“We hear say na for other towns those kind diseases dey happen, not for here in our village.” (Mother, 41 yrs, R9)

ii.Selective acknowledgment of risk: Risk perception increased only after direct exposure within the community.
“Na when my neighbour pikin fall sick, na that time I begin fear say it can reach my own house too.” (Mother, 35 yrs, R3)

iii.Misconceptions about risk factors: Some associate vulnerability only with malnutrition.
“For me, only children wey no dey chop well dey get those kind sickness.” (Mother, 32 yrs, R7)

Many caregivers believed their children were unlikely to contract vaccine-preventable diseases, attributing protection to good hygiene, nutrition, or rural isolation. Susceptibility was only acknowledged when illness occurred in the community.
BPerceived severity: understanding the consequences of illness
Perceptions of disease severity ranged from normalization of illness to recognition of life-threatening risks.
i.Varying levels of concern: Some minimized the seriousness of childhood illnesses.
“Children sickness dey normal, e go clear by itself with time.” (Mother, 35 yrs, R3)

ii.Association with death or disability: Others highlighted severe consequences but framed them in fatalistic or spiritual terms.
“If pikin die, na God hand. If e survive, na so e be. Nothing we fit do pass that one.” (Mother, 50 yrs, R10)

While some caregivers minimized childhood illnesses as “normal,” others recognized potential severity, often attributing outcomes to fate or spiritual causes rather than biomedical explanations.
CPerceived benefits: trust in health interventions
Caregivers expressed mixed views on the benefits of preventive healthcare.i.Positive attitudes toward preventive care: Many recognized vaccination as beneficial.
“Better to prevent than to suffer later. I always carry my baby go immunization, even if some people no like am.” (Mother, 27 yrs, R5)

ii.Concerns over vaccine efficacy: Others doubted reliability compared with traditional remedies.
“Nurse talk say vaccine dey work, but for me I don see herbs cure the same thing.” (Mother,39 yrs, R18)

iii.Belief in traditional remedies: Some prioritized herbal medicine before seeking hospital care.
“For my house we go first try herbs. If e no work, then we fit go hospital.” (Mother, 32 yrs, R15)

Participants expressed mixed views of vaccination, some acknowledged its preventive value, while others placed greater trust in herbal remedies or doubted vaccine efficacy.
DPerceived barriers: challenges to adopting positive health behaviors
Several barriers hindered vaccine uptake and other preventive practices.i.Mistrust in the healthcare system: Suspicion toward vaccination programs was common.
“People dey talk say na experiment government dey do with these vaccines.” (Mother, 29 yrs, R2)

ii.Access and cost barriers: Financial and logistical constraints limited healthcare access.
“Hospital far, transport no dey easy. I no fit carry children every time.” (Mother,35 yrs, R6)

iii.Side effect fears: Concerns about adverse reactions discouraged some caregivers.
“I hear say after vaccine pikin dey get sick. I no too trust am.” (Mother, 29 yrs, R2)

Key barriers included mistrust of government programmes, fear of side effects, long travel distances, and financial constraints.
ECues to action: motivating factors for behaviour change
Caregivers identified several triggers for engaging in preventive care.i.Community influence: Social norms strongly shaped decisions.
“If my neighbours dey go immunization, I go follow. I no wan my own different.” (Mother, 26 yrs, R4)

ii.Health worker influence: Guidance from health professionals was highly persuasive.
“Nurse explain am well-well, that time I agree carry my baby go.” (Mother, 28 yrs, R11)

iii.Personal experience: Illness within social circles motivated proactive decisions.
“When I see how one pikin suffer for our street, I talk say my own no go reach that level.” (Mother, 32 yrs, R7)

Positive cues included encouragement from health workers, observation of peers’ behaviour, and witnessing illness in others.
FSelf-efficacy: confidence in taking action
Respondents’ confidence in making health-related decisions was shaped by knowledge and autonomy.i.Confidence linked to knowledge: Some expressed willingness to act if better informed.
“If dem fit explain better to us, I go get mind to decide.” (Mother, 29 yrs, R2)

ii.Dependence on external approval: Many decisions required spousal or community consent.
“For me, na my husband dey decide this kind thing. If he agree, we go do am.” (Mother,32 yrs, R15)

iii.Challenges of autonomy: Some women deferred decision-making due to limited knowledge.
“I no sabi much about all these injection matter, na wetin others do I follow.” (Mother, 35 yrs, R6)

Confidence to act depended on clear explanations from health workers, though many women reported deferring vaccination decisions to husbands or community norms.
These insights informed the design of intervention messages, which were translated into Igbo and embedded within local music to counter misconceptions, address barriers, and leverage trusted cues to action.

## Quantitative analysis (pre- and post-evaluation survey)
A total of 200 women (mothers and caregivers) were enrolled. However, 193 completed both pre- and post-evaluation questionnaires.

## Knowledge
Figure  shows that at baseline, 24 respondents (12.4%) demonstrated low levels of knowledge, 53 (27.5%) had moderate levels of knowledge, and 116 (60.1%) exhibited high levels of knowledge of vaccination. Following the intervention, the proportion with low levels of knowledge declined to 20 (10.4%) and moderate levels of knowledge reduced to 20 (10.4%), while those with high levels of knowledge increased substantially to 153 (79.3%).
 

## Attitude
Figure  shows that prior to the intervention, 11 respondents (5.7%) had a poor attitude, 34 (17.6%) reported moderate attitude, and 148 (76.7%) demonstrated good attitude towards vaccination. Post-intervention, the distribution changed slightly, with poor attitude increasing to 18 (9.3%) and moderate attitude declining to 16 (8.3%). Nevertheless, good attitude rose further to 159 respondents (82.4%) showing only a modest, non-significant increase.
 

## Willingness
Figure  shows that 12 respondents (6.2%) initially reported low level of willingness, 159 (82.4%) had moderate level of willingness, and 22 (11.4%) indicated high level of willingness to vaccinate. After the intervention, low level of willingness rose modestly to 18 (9.3%) and moderate levels of willingness declined to 148 (76.7%), while high willingness increased to 27 (14.0%) remaining largely unchanged.
 In Table  , given the non-normal distribution of the data (Shapiro–Wilk p < 0.001), the Wilcoxon Signed-Rank Test was used to evaluate changes in knowledge, attitude and willingness scores. The analysis revealed a statistically significant increase in knowledge scores (p < 0.001) and a meaningful improvement in vaccination knowledge (r = 0.30) following the culturally tailored audio messaging intervention. Though the attitude changes were positive, the intervention did not produce a statistically significant change (p = 0.127) and willingness to vaccinate did not change significantly (p = 0.809).
 

## Discussion
This study demonstrated that culturally tailored audio vaccination messages, delivered in the local language and embedded in traditional music, significantly improved maternal knowledge about infant immunization in rural communities of Imo State, Nigeria. While attitude and willingness did not change significantly in statistical terms, qualitative findings revealed nuanced shifts in perceptions that highlight the importance of cultural context in shaping vaccine-related behaviours.
The significant improvement in knowledge with a moderate effect size (Cohen’s r = 0.30) is consistent with evidence that context-specific, culturally resonant communication strategies can enhance health literacy and bridge gaps left by conventional approaches [ , ]. Embedding vaccination information within familiar music and narratives likely improved message comprehension, retention, and trust, especially in a setting where literacy barriers and mistrust of health authorities remain prevalent [ , ]. This aligns with the Health Belief Model, which emphasizes that knowledge of susceptibility, severity, and benefits is a precursor to adopting preventive health behaviours [ ].
Despite these knowledge gains, attitude and willingness showed only little to no shift, a finding that resonates with broader literature on vaccine hesitancy in LMICs. Behavioural change is often constrained by persistent barriers including mistrust of government health campaigns, gendered decision-making structures, fear of side effects, and logistical challenges to healthcare access [ , , ]. Our qualitative data reinforced these dynamics, with many women reporting limited autonomy in health decisions, dependence on spousal approval, and competing beliefs in traditional remedies. These findings underscore that willingness to vaccinate is influenced by more complex socio-cultural and structural factors than knowledge alone can address [ , ]. Although, attitudes and willingness did not change significantly, the observed improvements in knowledge and qualitative reports of increased trust suggest early perceptual shifts that may precede behavioural change. The intervention’s potential therefore lies in its role as an enabling platform for sustained community engagement rather than an immediate behaviour-modifying tool.
Importantly, when viewed in the context of international literature, our results align with findings highlighting the effectiveness of culturally resonant communication strategies across diverse settings. In low- and middle-income cfountries (LMICs), evidence supports the utility of community-based, culturally familiar media in addressing specific health challenges. For instance, interventions in South Asia have shown that using storytelling and singing (a form of community drama or music) to embed health messages, such as those promoting Measles, Mumps, and Rubella (MMR) vaccination, can effectively enhance acceptance and address hesitancy rooted in religious beliefs [ ]. Furthermore, in Africa, early in the COVID-19 pandemic, researchers proposed utilizing existing cultural communication channels like music, theatre, and community drama to disseminate health information effectively, demonstrating their recognized potential to reach and engage large populations [ ].
Similarly, among immigrant and ethnic minority populations in High-Income Countries (HICs), the literature emphasizes the critical role of culturally tailored and linguistically congruent messaging. A systematic review focusing on the United Kingdom found that to overcome COVID-19 vaccine hesitancy among ethnic minorities, strategies must move beyond a 'one-size-fits-all' approach and include community engagement and tailored communication to address issues of mistrust, misinformation, and structural inequities [ ]. This approach is successfully implemented through methods like developing culturally and linguistically congruent digital storytelling interventions, which have been shown to be feasible and acceptable among groups like Vietnamese and Korean American mothers concerning HPV vaccination [ ]. Additionally, the broader need for culturally competent care is emphasized to address immigrant health disparities by accounting for local cultural models of illness [ ]. These parallels strengthen the argument that cultural and linguistic tailoring is not only relevant to Nigerian rural communities but is a broadly applicable, evidence-based strategy for enhancing health communication effectiveness and acceptance across diverse global settings and different health contexts.
Qualitative evidence suggests the intervention has mobilizing potential. Participants described the songs as relatable, emotionally engaging, and capable of sparking household and community conversations about vaccination. The role of peers and health workers emerged as critical cues to action, echoing findings from similar community-based interventions in Nigeria and other African settings [ , ]. Such relational influences align with the Diffusion of Innovations theory, which highlights the importance of social networks and trusted messengers in spreading new health practices [ ]. While a single intervention may be insufficient to produce immediate measurable shifts in willingness, repeated exposure combined with community reinforcement could yield longer-term behavioural change.
Our findings therefore have important implications for vaccination campaigns in rural Nigeria and other resource-limited contexts. First, culturally adapted music-based communication can serve as a scalable tool for addressing knowledge gaps, especially in communities with strong oral traditions [ , ]. Second, interventions targeting willingness must extend beyond information delivery to address entrenched barriers such as mistrust, gender norms, and access constraints [ , ]. Third, leveraging trusted influencers including health workers, community leaders, and peers may amplify message credibility and accelerate uptake [ , ].
This study contributes to the limited published evidence on audio-based, culturally adapted vaccination messaging in rural Nigerian settings. By integrating quantitative and qualitative approaches, it provides both measurable evidence of impact and deeper insights into the contextual dynamics shaping vaccination behaviour. The findings underscore the value of culturally grounded communication strategies while highlighting that, on their own, such interventions may not be sufficient to produce significant shifts in attitudes and willingness. Rather, culturally tailored audio messages should be embedded within broader, multi-layered approaches that incorporate health system strengthening, sustained community engagement, and gender-sensitive strategies.
A key strength of this study lies in its participatory design, which ensured that intervention content was informed by community voices, local beliefs, and cultural norms. The use of mixed methods allowed triangulation of findings, providing a nuanced understanding of behavioural outcomes alongside structural and cultural barriers to vaccination.
However, several limitations must be acknowledged. The study employed a single-group pre–post design without a control group, which restricts causal inference and limits the ability to attribute observed changes solely to the intervention. This design choice was driven by ethical and logistical considerations, including security constraints and the need to ensure equitable access to the intervention. Future research should consider randomized or comparison-group designs to enhance the robustness of causal interpretation.
Additionally, assessments were conducted immediately after the intervention, capturing short-term knowledge and perceptual changes rather than long-term behavioural outcomes. Variations in participants’ subsequent exposure to the shared audio files via WhatsApp were not systematically monitored, limiting interpretation of potential dose–response effects. Longitudinal follow-up studies are therefore needed to assess the durability of knowledge gains and their translation into sustained vaccination behaviours. Future research should incorporate mechanisms to track and quantify message replay and community diffusion.
The use of purposive and convenience sampling, though appropriate for reaching women in hard-to-access rural areas amid security and infrastructural challenges, may limit generalizability of findings to the broader population. Nonetheless, this approach facilitated meaningful engagement with the primary target group for vaccination programmes, mothers and caregivers in rural settings thereby enhancing the practical relevance of the study’s findings.

## Conclusion, implications, and recommendations
This study demonstrated that culturally tailored audio vaccination messages, delivered through traditional music and local language, significantly improved maternal knowledge about childhood immunization among the participants. Although immediate changes in attitudes and willingness were not statistically significant, qualitative insights revealed emerging perceptual shifts and growing trust, suggesting that such interventions may serve as enabling platforms for sustained engagement rather than immediate behaviour-modifying tools. By embedding vaccination messages within familiar cultural expressions, public health programmes can build trust, enhance comprehension, and create a foundation for long-term improvements in vaccine uptake.
While these findings are context-specific to the cultural and linguistic environment of Imo State, Nigeria. Nonetheless, the underlying principles of the intervention, community co-creation, cultural tailoring, and use of familiar artistic communication are conceptually applicable in other settings. These principles offer a framework for context-sensitive adaptation and potential scale-up, though empirical testing across diverse populations and languages is required to confirm broader applicability.

## Implications for practice and policy
The findings highlight the value of culturally grounded communication strategies as a low-cost, scalable, and context-appropriate complement to conventional health promotion approaches. Health authorities and programme implementers can integrate culturally adapted audio messages into existing immunization campaigns, antenatal care services, and community mobilization structures. Embedding such approaches in policy frameworks can strengthen vaccine demand generation, particularly in underserved rural settings where literacy barriers, mistrust, and cultural influences often limit uptake. However, the study’s context-specific nature highlights the importance of local adaptation. For successful replication elsewhere, interventions must be tailored to reflect the distinct cultural, linguistic, and social realities of target populations. Embedding these strategies within broader health system frameworks and national communication policies could enhance sustainability, community ownership, and equity in vaccine access.

## Recommendations for future research
This study offers promising evidence, while the intervention produced meaningful knowledge gains and qualitative evidence of perceptual change, the study’s single-group, short-term design limits causal inference and behavioural attribution. Future research should therefore adopt longitudinal or comparative designs to assess sustained effects on vaccine uptake and explore mechanisms through which trust and community dialogue translate into action.
Comparative investigations are also needed to determine how audio-based communication performs relative to other culturally adapted approaches, such as community drama, storytelling, or peer-led education in influencing health behaviours. In addition, there is value in examining how audio messaging can be systematically integrated into existing health system workflows, including the use of digital platforms, community outreach structures, and public health campaigns. Such research should incorporate cost-effectiveness and scalability analyses to guide sustainable implementation at larger scale.
Beyond immunization, future work could extend this communication approach to other domains of maternal and child health, such as antenatal care, nutrition, or malaria prevention, where cultural resonance and accessibility remain critical barriers to uptake. It would also be valuable to investigate how gender dynamics, literacy levels, and socioeconomic differences shape message interpretation and responsiveness, ensuring that interventions are equitable and inclusive. Furthermore, future research should strengthen participatory and co-design approaches by engaging community members not merely as recipients but as active partners in message development and dissemination. Such collaboration can enhance ownership, cultural authenticity, and the long-term sustainability of audio-based health communication strategies.
Ultimately, culturally tailored audio messaging represents a promising, culturally sensitive, and sustainable strategy for improving vaccine confidence and maternal health engagement. Scaling such approaches, supported by robust evidence and inclusive community partnerships, can contribute significantly to advancing immunization coverage and achieving broader public health goals in Nigeria and similar resource-limited settings.
