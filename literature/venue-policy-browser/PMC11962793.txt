## Abstract
Between April 2020 and March 2021, the number of fall-related emergency admissions in England for adults over 65 years was 1933 per 100 000 people. Adult patients in hospital may be at risk of falling for many reasons including a history of falls, being medically unwell, dementia or delirium, the effects of their treatment or medication, poor mobility, visual and other sensory impairments along with their general well-being. Research has shown that falls can be reduced by 20%–30% through multifactorial assessments and interventions. The aim of these assessments and interventions is to identify and treat underlying reasons for falls such as muscle weakness, cardiovascular problems, dementia, delirium, incontinence and medication. However, national audits have found low levels of implementation of these assessments and interventions in UK hospitals. As part of a new patient safety improvement initiative, a collaborative was developed to reduce the incidence of in-patient falls rate per 1000 bed days within five older adults’ mental health wards in a health board in Wales. The falls collaborative project has resulted in substantial improvements in care, including an increase of patients receiving lying and standing blood pressure assessment, medication review and delirium assessments. While reported falls rates stayed the same for the five wards, when each ward individually was factored in, we saw a reduction in two wards and estimated that the increase in falls for the remaining of three wards was related to a previous state of under-reporting, considering the numbers stayed levelled throughout the collaborative. The small reduction we saw was achieved without any extra support or allocated resources, and the ongoing staffing challenges all five wards experienced throughout the collaborative, all these improvements were received as a great success. The team was shortlisted for the National Health Service Wales Awards in the Safe Care category, something they took great pride in.

## WHAT IS ALREADY KNOWN ON THIS TOPIC

## WHAT THIS STUDY ADDS
This work is specific to mental health wards, understanding this environment as especially complex with elderly patients suffering with dementia, delirium, polypharmacy and other contributing factors for falls.

## HOW THIS STUDY MIGHT AFFECT RESEARCH, PRACTICE OR POLICY
This work highlights the importance of implementing clear, achievable and evidence-based bundles to standardise incident prevention practices. Not having an agreed process, even within an organisation, impacts variability of care, incidence of falls rate and staff awareness of best practices.

## Introduction

## Problem
 As well as the individual impact, falls in older adults represent a substantial burden to the National Health Service (NHS). Between April 2022 and March 2023, the number of fall-related emergency admissions in England for adults over 65 years was 1933 per 100 000 people. Injuries by falls account for over 4 million hospital bed days in England alone and cost the NHS around £2.3 billion each year.2,3 In Wales, it is estimated that between 230 000 and 460 000 people over the age of 60 fall each year. 
Cwm Taf Morgannwg University Health Board (CTM UHB) provides primary, community, hospital and mental health services to the 450 000 people living in three county boroughs: Bridgend, Merthyr Tydfil and Rhondda Cynon Taf in Wales. Its age groups of 65–84 and 85+ are projected to have the largest increase by 2036, when an estimated one in four people in Wales will be aged 65 and over. These projections will have significant implications for the way in which we design and provide our increasingly integrated health and social care services.
With the UHB’s patient population increasing in age and complex multimorbidity, the challenge to reduce the number of falls and injuries from falls is significant. Adult patients in hospital may be at risk of falling for many reasons including a history of falls, being medically unwell, dementia or delirium, the effects of their treatment or medication, poor mobility, visual and other sensory impairments along with their general well-being. Although most falls do not result in serious injury, there is always a risk that a fall could lead to broken bones and cause the person to lose confidence. 
Patient safety incidents related to falls are reported daily through a national reporting system (Datix Cymru). Inpatient falls are one of the most frequently reported incidents in CTM UHB, alongside pressure ulcers and medication incidents, with a total of 2927 inpatient falls reported for 2023. These results relate to similar findings from a retrospective analysis of patient safety incidents in England and Wales from 2008. 
As part of a new patient safety improvement initiative, a collaborative was developed to reduce the incidence of in-patient falls rate per 1000 bed days within five older adults’ mental health wards in CTM UHB, including males and females, with organic and functional disorders. We adopted a quality improvement collaborative approach using the Institute for Healthcare Improvement Breakthrough Series Model. The strength of this approach lies in the collaborative efforts to bring together peers and experts in particular fields, allowing them to share knowledge and learn from each other, while working through the implementation of a care bundle of suggested initiatives to prevent falls.
The primary aim of the project was to support the reliable implementation of evidence-based multifactorial falls prevention assessments and interventions delivered as a care bundle. A care bundle is a structured way of improving the process of care. It consists of a small number of evidence-based interventions which when performed together have been shown to improve patient outcomes. 
The aims of the collaborative were to reduce the incidence of in-patient falls rate per 1000 bed days within five older adults mental health wards in CTM UHB and to support the reliable implementation of evidence-based multifactorial falls prevention assessments and interventions delivered as a care bundle.

## Background
Falls in hospital inpatients are common, with reported rates ranging from 3 to 14 per 1000 bed days in observational and intervention studies. Across England and Wales, approximately 36 000 falls are reported from mental health units each year. A significant number of falls result in death, severe or moderate injury, at an estimated cost of £15 million per annum for immediate healthcare treatment alone. This is likely to be an underestimation of the overall financial burden from falls once the costs of rehabilitation and social care are considered, as up to 90% of older people who fracture their neck of femur never recover their previous level of mobility or independence. In addition to these financial costs, there are other costs that are more difficult to quantify. The human cost of falling includes distress, pain, injury, loss of confidence and independence, and the anxiety caused to the person who falls, their relatives, carers and healthcare staff. 
People of all ages fall, but falls are most likely to occur in older people who are more liable to experience serious injury. The causes of falls are complex and older people accessing mental health services are particularly vulnerable to falling because of dementia or depression, side effects from medication, or problems with balance, strength or mobility. 
Problems like poor eyesight or poor mobility can create a greater risk of falling when someone is removed from their home environment because they are less able to recognise and avoid any hazards. Falls in healthcare settings and fall-related injuries are therefore a complex challenge for all healthcare providers. 
Research has shown that falls can be reduced by 20%–30% through multifactorial assessments and interventions. The aims of these assessments and interventions are to identify and treat underlying reasons for falls such as muscle weakness, cardiovascular problems, dementia, delirium, incontinence and medication. However, national audits have found low levels of implementation of these assessments and interventions in UK hospitals. 
The National Institute for Health and Care Excellence guidance CG161 and standard QS86 outline best practices on how to prevent falls. Despite these being based on evidence-based practice, the prevalence of falls being reported continues to be high.

## Methods
Initial data collection focused on obtaining baseline data for those five older people’s mental health wards participating in the collaborative. Incidents reported through Datix Cymru, and occupancy data were pulled together to calculate an estimated falls rate per 1000 bed days for each ward per month for 1 year as the outcome measure. The overall median rate for the mental health wards was 10 per 1000 bed days before the collaborative started with an average of 8% of those falls resulting in either moderate or severe harm. When stratifying the number of falls happening by time of day, there was an increased number of falls between 18 and 20 hours.
For our process measure, a proforma was initially created using Excel, where best practices identified for falls prevention were reviewed. These included risk assessments undertaken, medication and delirium assessments completed, patients and carers informed and care plans in place for patients at risk. For our baseline data, 20 patients were reviewed per ward within 2 months. This gave us a baseline of the core components of the care bundle each ward had to work towards implementing. After this proforma was validated by end users, it was uploaded to AMat, a clinical audit assurance software used in CTM UHB. This ensured visibility and sustainability of this assessment.
This process measure was captured every 2 months or if a new change was introduced to the system.
A one-off environmental audit was also completed across the five wards using the King’s Fund Enhancing Healing Environment Assessment Tool. Scores were discussed with the teams and used as benchmark against each other and more widely to highlight priorities for improvement.
Given the lack of physiotherapist resources allocated to each ward, we were unable to link other measurements like gait, strength, mobility and balance into our project.

## Design
Based on best practices and guidelines revision, a care bundle was developed and shared for implementation across the five wards. This bundle targeted falls prevention and did not include actions taken after a fall happened. Its details are listed in .
 The Falls Collaborative was structured around five learning sessions with action periods between each session. The Nursing Director for Mental Health and Learning Disabilities acted as Senior Responsible Officer and two nurse leads from Mental Health were appointed to support the collaborative. The local quality improvement team (iCTM) provided improvement knowledge, support with data and facilitated the use of the methodology.
Each ward participating was formed by a multidisciplinary team of people who were part of the daily work on the wards for falls prevention (occupational therapist, physiotherapist, pharmacist, clinicians, dietitians, ward managers, nurses, healthcare support workers). On average, between 27 and 30 people participated in each learning session, held during a day, or a half-day work, and they were structured to tackle a specific issue or challenge. During each session, teams examined and compared data, shared experiences, and provided peer support to each other.
Session 1: Understanding outcome measures for falls.
Session 2: Involving others: the role of dietetics, pharmacy and community teams in falls prevention.
Session 3: Understanding process measures and agreeing on a process for falls prevention based on best practices.
Session 4: Putting ourselves in our patient’s shoes using an empathy suit to understand their experiences.
Session 5: Using time of day pareto to tackle improvement: the role of sundowning in mental health patients and their risk of falls.
After each learning session, face to face meetings were scheduled by iCTM to visit local teams on the wards. This approach ensured frontline staff felt supported throughout the project and allowed teams to have more focused discussions about their specific challenges, and this approach ensured activities and agreed changes were carried out after each learning session.
Local teams were tasked with implementing specific changes to standardise the care provided for falls prevention based on the agreed actions on each learning session, and regularly measure its implementation using the AMat tool developed. These changes were directly linked with the monthly assessment of the care bundle developed (ie, if the audit showed low compliance with lying and standing blood pressure (LSBP), then a focused effort was pushed through the collaborative to train staff or share resources to increase awareness and adherence to this preventative step).
Secondary to the results obtained from the King’s Fund Audit, it was evident there were key aspects related to the environment that were outside of the control of local wards to enable a safe space for patients to prevent falls, and other stakeholders were involved in those conversations (estates and facilities, catering).
Senior leaders and local stakeholders were engaged through regular email updates and monthly reporting, and presentations in quality and safety committees.

## Strategy
The aims of the collaborative were to reduce the incidence of in-patient falls rate per 1000 bed days within five older adults mental health wards in CTM UHB and to support the reliable implementation of evidence-based multifactorial falls prevention assessments and interventions delivered as a care bundle.
PDSA cycle 1: our initial intervention was to standardise the knowledge around best practices for falls prevention using the learning sessions as a tool to deliver training and share best practices. This achieved a reduction in the overall variability of the process and gave visibility to specific areas that need to be improved within each ward using the proforma developed to audit the bundle implementation.
PDSA cycle 2: to build and strengthen on the standardisation of best practices, an external contributor was tasked with completing a 10-patient review for each of the wards participating, using the same proforma to measure the bundle implementation. This exercise was used to sense check if the audit tool developed was easy to use and understandable and to validate the results provided by the local teams. This exercise resulted in minor modifications to the proforma, and its final development to be then uploaded to the national audit software system used in CTM UHB (AmAT).
PDSA cycle 3: from the audit results, it was evident there were two key areas to tackle: delirium assessment and LSBP. We hypothesised that our intervention may be more effective if we made access to training tools more convenient and accessible for frontline staff. A poster with links to two training videos lasting less than 5 min was made available to every team, and visits to the links were tracked to ensure front line staff were accessing them. Although successful in increasing adherence and visibility on this part of the bundle, the team realised the ongoing training challenges, secondary to non-permanent staff (agency and bank) present on the ward.
PDSA cycle 4: data analysed over time highlighted that most falls in mental health happen around 18–20 hours, consistent when patients with dementia experience sundowning. A training session tailored to dementia patients was offered, with key actions to implement across the different wards.
At the end of the first year of the collaborative, a survey was sent to all participants of the collaborative. Both quantitative and qualitative feedback were collected to determine the success and reception of the improvement, which are discussed in the results section.

## Results
We analysed incident reporting data and occupancy in 11-month periods for before and after, with information gathered from all the participating wards per month. Data was gathered over time using a run chart, but for easier analysis, it was broken down according to each ward (see ) for October 2022 to September 2023 (before) and October 2023 to September 2024 (after).
 Although the collaborative did not evidence a reduction in falls, it was evident a stabilisation of the falls prevention process, with more data points gathering around the median for all five wards. Two participating wards did in fact evidence a reduction in the number of falls reported before and after (ward 1 and ward 3). There was an astronomical point in October 2023, which was related to shortage in staffing levels across the five sites.
Moreover, the care bundle for falls prevention was measured before and after, and four of the five participating wards were successful in implementing all the relevant process measures (see ).
 A small survey was sent out at the end of the first year (45% response rate), where every answer was either ‘Agree’ or ‘Strongly Agree’ to each of these statements: the topics covered throughout the collaborative were relevant, the sessions were well organised and easy to follow, the objectives of the collaborative were met, all of your voices were heard, data presented was relevant and easy to understand, guests who presented at the collaborative were great additions to the agenda, wards felt supported throughout the collaborative and senior decision makers were visible and facilitated the collaborative.
Some of the comments shared:
“Interaction with other wards and colleagues / sharing of learnings” and “meeting and networking from all other areas and sharing experiences and advice, support” were highlighted as valuable learning and things that have impacted daily work. Other comments around content: “the sundowning session was great. Use of data is always useful. Working as a team has been brilliant!” and “I particularly enjoyed the dietetics talk and have implemented what I learnt into practice”.

## Discussion
Although the collaborative did not evidence a reduction in falls, it was evident a stabilisation of the fall’s prevention process, with more data points gathering around the median for all five wards, something that has been sustained until this manuscript was sent for publication (Jan/2025). Two participating wards did in fact evidence a reduction in the number of falls reported before and after (ward 1 and ward 3), and there was an astronomical point in October 2023, which was related to shortage in staffing levels across the five sites.
Taking into consideration other initiatives aimed to prevent falls, the collaborative team focused on the completion of a comprehensive risk assessment with the aim of seeing a reduction in the number of falls because it allowed staff to devote additional attention to individuals who were at risk of falling. Moreover, and similar to other findings, having an agreed process to assess the falls prevention bundle through AmAT had a positive reception by wards given it was not a tick-box exercise but a proactive way to ensure guidance alignment. 
After starting with the implementation of the care bundle, we evidenced an increase in falls in some of the wards. Like other studies, the fall rate was determined from data collected through a fall incident reporting system. The accuracy of this data is dependent on health professionals’ reporting behaviour and under-reporting of incidents primarily depends on the prevailing culture and whether incidents are considered opportunities to learn from.
Similar to other findings, the impact of training and education saw significant benefits in producing a positive impact for the prevention of falls. The fact that this increased education positively influenced compliance with the care bundle made evident that more efforts should be made to spread the learnings from this collaborative across the health board.

## Lessons and limitations
Inclusion of mental healthcare settings for older people was to some extent a leap, as there is not much published evidence for effective implementation of falls prevention strategies in that sector.
The teams attending the collaborative had some challenges in being released to attend the training sessions, which then resulted in the sessions being adapted to a half day (instead of a full day) and resulted in some delays in covering the intended content for the collaborative. Knowledge or behaviour change was not tracked for the collaborative, something that could have been anticipated and measured from the beginning.
Implementing a collaborative takes time, is challenging and requires a person being allocated to the project to ensure timelines are met, teams are supported, data is gathered and analysed, issues are escalated and learning is shared. There was not a specific cost associated with the implementation of best practices, but there was a small budget allocated to the learning sessions to ensure a venue and food were available on the day.
A multidisciplinary team participating in the collaborative was key to ensure shared learning. There were some challenges in engaging with medical staff on the wards, though there was a consultant who participated in most of the learning sessions and provided great input and insight of the care bundle and its implementation. Moreover, there were therapists based on some of the wards who engaged with the collaborative throughout, but not all wards had a locally based therapist, which in turn impacted on the multidisciplinary team approach needed on each ward to implement changes successfully.
Also, there were some months where the number of registered nurses on the wards was low in numbers, especially during October/2023, with sickness being the most frequent reason. After the collaborative, all wards have now an advanced nurse practitioner who engages and liaises with medical staff, ensuring their awareness of the collaborative and the continuity of its implementation.
Engaging frontline staff was the most difficult aspect of the project, and this was most of the time linked to staffing challenges and staff being allowed to attend training sessions. High levels of staff turnover and high levels of temporary staff added another layer of complexity to ensuring communication and successful implementation of initiatives.
Use of data was also a challenge, given access was not easily available secondary to Datix Cymru not being linked to the local health board data warehouse. Therefore, data had to be manually cleansed and analysed, something that does not ensure frontline staff have access to dashboards and are unable to understand how they compare to others.
Local teams on the wards were represented by ward managers or their deputies, something that in retrospect could have been anticipated with other members of staff being involved from the beginning, facilitating and contributing with daily tasks, reducing the workload of ward managers.
The inclusion of balancing measures in the project was deliberated and no feasible balancing measure could be found. The project team suggested using complaints, but the numbers were too insignificant. Bed days/length of stay and admission to nursing home were also suggested but it was decided they were not uniquely affected by falls prevention practice.

## Conclusion
The falls collaborative project has resulted in substantial improvements in care, including an increase of patients receiving lying and standing blood pressure assessment, medication review and delirium assessments. While reported falls rates stayed the same for the five wards, when each ward individually was factored in, we saw a reduction in two wards and a stabilisation of the falls rate for all across the median. This measurement has stayed consistent throughout, last measured January/2025.
The team was shortlisted for the NHS Wales Awards in the Safe Care category, something they took great pride in.
It is evident that continued work is required to ensure an improved level of safety is maintained and having the care bundle available on AmAT could contribute to its visibility and subsequent spread to other areas.
Due to interest gained by this project, and realising learnings need to be shared across the health board, there is an interest in developing a similar collaborative focusing on falls and pressure ulcer prevention across all settings.
