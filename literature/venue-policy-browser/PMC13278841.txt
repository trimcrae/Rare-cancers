## Abstract

## Background and Objectives
The number of older adults living alone is increasing globally, often with varied risks and resilience capacities. Digital technologies may help mitigate these risks, but their potential remains poorly understood. This review maps evidence on digital technologies in social and health care interventions for older adults living alone, focusing on technology types, functions, intervention outcomes, integration into care, and associated benefits and challenges.

## Research Design and Methods
Studies from 2000 to 2025 were identified through systematic searches across 8 databases and gray literature, yielding 37 sources. A qualitative content analysis synthesized study characteristics and findings.

## Results
Five categories of digital technologies were identified, from communication tools to AI-powered devices. Most interventions were delivered through scheduled sessions led by care providers. Most reported positive health outcomes (e.g., improved physical function, psychological well-being, reduced loneliness, healthier behaviors, and better self-care), while evidence on service delivery effects remains limited. While technologies improved care accessibility and coordination, challenges included limited digital literacy, exclusion, and design flaws.

## Discussion and Implications
Knowledge gaps include limited geographical diversity, insufficient research on underrepresented groups (e.g., ethnic minorities and those with low socioeconomic status), a lack of intersectional analyses, limited evidence on cost-effectiveness, long-term outcomes, and effective intervention components. Future interventions should adopt participatory, person-centered approaches to strengthen equity and agency, alongside policy efforts to address structural barriers such as unequal access to digital care technologies.

Innovation and Translational Significance:This review synthesized the types and functions of digital technologies used in social and health care interventions for older adults living alone. It identified diverse intervention designs, outcome measures, integration approaches across care settings, implementation enablers, and barriers. These findings can guide practitioners, policymakers, and technology developers in promoting equitable, person-centered, and sustainable digital care solutions for older adults living alone.
Living alone in later life has become increasingly common globally. About one in five adults aged 65 and above lives alone worldwide ( ), with notable national variations: 32% in the European Union in 2015 ( ), 28% in the United States in 2023 ( ), and 19% in Japan in 2021 ( ). The prevalence of solo-living older adults continues to rise in several societies and is especially pronounced among older women ( ; ). This trend reflects broader demographic and societal transformations, including delayed and declining marriage, rising divorce rates, and increasing geographic mobility ( ), as well as lifestyle preferences that emphasize autonomy, restorative solitude, personal growth, and engagement in vibrant urban cultures ( ).
Older adults living alone may face multiple challenges. Research links living alone to higher odds of dementia, greater reliance on formal services, and lower levels of well-being ( ; ). Evidence suggests that loneliness and social isolation are key mechanisms linking solitary living to adverse outcomes. For instance, a cross-national study showed that older European adults living alone reported lower subjective well-being when their social networks were restricted ( ). In Japan, older adults living alone were particularly vulnerable during natural disasters because of limited mobility, weaker social networks, and difficulties in receiving timely assistance ( ). At the same time, living alone in later life is not uniformly associated with loneliness or social isolation. Some older adults actively cope with or even embrace solitary living, drawing on personal resources, and social ties beyond the household ( ). Yet resilience often coexists with structural vulnerability. Ethnographic research documents persistent feelings of instability and insecurity among older solo dwellers, shaped by declining health, limited income, fragmented services, and strong ideals of independence that constrain access to essential resources ( ). Together, these studies suggest that older adults living alone occupy a distinctive position: they may exercise autonomy and demonstrate resilience, yet remain structurally exposed to risks across micro-, meso-, and macro-level contexts.
Living alone reflects a distinct care ecology ( ). Compared to cohabiting older adults, those living alone are less likely to have immediate access to informal caregivers for daily assistance, health monitoring, or emergency response ( ). Consequently, their care needs are structurally different. While cohabiting older adults rely on spouses or household members for medication management, symptom monitoring, and emotional support, solo-living older adults may depend more heavily on formal services or technological support to maintain safety, independence, and social connectedness ( ; ). These structural differences suggest that digital interventions for solo-living older adults may function as partial substitutes for absent kin (e.g., facilitating remote monitoring, emergency detection, or structured social interaction) rather than merely complementing existing household care ( ; ). Understanding these distinctions provides a theoretical rationale for examining digital care interventions specifically within the context of living alone.
Increasingly, digital technologies have been deployed as tools to support social and health care interventions ( ; ). In this review, we define care interventions as structured strategies delivered in clinical, community, or home settings, designed to maintain or improve health, functioning, psychosocial well-being, safety, or care coordination ( ). These interventions may include prevention, monitoring, therapeutic, rehabilitative, or supportive components and may be delivered by health professionals, social service providers, or community organizations. When embedded within such interventions, digital technologies function as tools that facilitate assessment, communication, monitoring, decision support, or behavioral change ( ).
The term digital technology lacks a universally agreed-upon definition, as technologies are embedded in complex social contexts and take on different meanings across settings ( ). The United Nations Development Programme defines digital technology broadly as “the systems, hardware and processes that use digital data or signals to achieve defined outcomes” ( , p. 49), encompassing mobile devices, information systems, and artificial intelligence (AI). In care contexts, these technologies offer innovative ways to prevent and manage chronic diseases, improve access, and enhance autonomy and independence ( ). At the same time, concerns about privacy, usability, and digital exclusion persist ( ). Clarifying which digital technologies are used, how they are embedded within care interventions, and whether they align with the needs of solo-living older adults is therefore essential.
Digital care interventions may differ in their design, implementation, and expected outcomes depending on living arrangements. For solo-living older adults, interventions may prioritize emergency detection, fall monitoring, medication adherence support, or structured opportunities for social interaction, aimed at mitigating risks associated with the absence of coresident support ( ; ). For cohabiting older adults, digital tools may instead complement family caregiving by facilitating shared disease management or reducing caregiver burden ( ; ). Accordingly, meaningful outcomes may also differ. For solo-living older persons, success may be measured in enhanced safety, autonomy, and reduced isolation, whereas for cohabiting adults, outcomes may focus on efficiency, caregiver relief, and improved dyadic management of chronic conditions ( ). A clearer understanding of these distinctions is necessary to determine whether current digital care interventions are appropriately tailored to the realities of living alone in later life.
Older adults living alone are a heterogeneous group, differing in health status and subjective well-being ( ), social networks ( ), care needs and available support ( ), and approaches to self-care ( ). They also engage with digital health technologies in diverse ways: some prefer simple, affordable, and age-friendly tools that support independent living, and are complemented by human support ( ; ), while others encounter barriers to adoption and effective use due to limited digital literacy, lack of troubleshooting support, affordability challenges, and distrust in digital technologies ( ; ). These challenges are compounded by rapid technological developments, with AI applications increasingly embedded in care interventions such as image analysis, automated health monitoring, algorithm-based decision support, and patient outreach. While such innovations may support independence, they also require adequate AI literacy and raise ethical concerns regarding transparency and accountability ( ). This underscores the importance of weighing both the potential and the limitations of digital care interventions for this population.
Several scoping and systematic reviews have examined digital technologies aimed at improving older adults’ health and well-being, particularly ICT-based interventions targeting loneliness and social isolation ( ; ). However, most focus on the older adult population as a whole ( ). Few reviews adopt a broader view of digital technologies across health and social care domains, specifically centering on older adults living alone. As a result, important knowledge gaps remain regarding which technologies are used in care interventions for this demographic, how they function, what outcomes they produce, and how they are integrated into care practices. Broader benefits, challenges, and limitations also remain underexplored.
Responding to these gaps, this scoping review aims to map existing evidence on the use of digital technologies in social and health care interventions for older adults living alone. Specifically, we examine the types of technologies employed, their functions, intervention outcomes, modes of integration into care, and the associated benefits and challenges. The findings of this review can support care providers and service designers in understanding how digital technologies are applied to address the diverse needs of older adults living alone across physical, mental, and social domains. The review also identifies which subgroups across socioeconomic, geographic, and cultural backgrounds have received technology-supported care. In doing so, it offers practical insights for designing more person-centered interventions and highlights considerations for equitable implementations. The findings may guide responsible and ethical technology design by addressing risks faced by solo-living older adults and lowering barriers to meaningful use. At the policy level, the findings may inform aging and care policies aimed at ensuring that digital care interventions are accessible to underserved older adult groups and integrated into existing care systems.

## Methodology
To conduct the scoping review, a five-step methodological framework ( ) was implemented. The framework comprises (1) identifying research questions, (2) identifying relevant studies, (3) selecting studies, (4) charting data, and (5) collating, analyzing, and reporting results. The reporting process adhered to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) Checklist ( ).

## Step 1: Identifying research questions
To advance our understanding of how digital technologies are applied in care interventions for older adults living alone, this review addresses four interrelated research questions, including:
What types of digital technologies are used in care interventions for older adults living alone, and what functions do they serve?
What outcomes do these technology-supported care interventions produce?
How are these technologies integrated into care practices?
What benefits, challenges, and limitations are associated with the use of digital technologies in such interventions?
In this review, examples of digital technologies ranged from lower-tech applications, such as telephone counseling, to more advanced tools, including ICTs, assistive devices, sensor systems, telemedicine, video games, medication reminders, and AI applications. This broader definition was used and operationalized in previous systematic reviews examining digital technologies in the care of older adults ( ).

## Step 2: Identifying relevant studies
The selection of search terms was informed by relevant literature and pilot searches in databases, and iteratively refined with a consensus reached within the research team, following the recommendations ( ). The identified terms were then organized into three clusters using the Population-Concept-Context framework. “Population” refers to older adults living alone, initially defined as individuals aged 60 years and over. However, as several articles defined older adults as 50 years and over, we extended the threshold accordingly. To maintain relevance to our focus, we included studies with a lower age limit of 50 only if the mean age of study participants exceeded 70. This approach was consistent with previous scoping reviews ( ). “Concept” refers to health and social care intervention, defined as organized activities, programs, or services designed to improve or maintain health and well-being ( ). These interventions are typically delivered by professionals in the healthcare and social service sectors and may include curative, preventive, rehabilitative, palliative, as well as social and welfare services. No specific “Context” was applied in the search strategy to ensure broad coverage of the literature across geographic and care contexts. Specific keywords related to digital technology were not included in the search strategy due to the considerable variation in technological types, applications, and levels of advancement; instead, this aspect was assessed during full-text screening to ensure a more inclusive approach and to minimize the risk of omitting relevant studies in the initial search phase. A list of search terms is available in .
 A search strategy was developed and refined with the support of an information specialist from Lund University’s Medical Faculty (for details, see ). The review focused on publications from 2000 onwards, a point often viewed as the beginning of the digital era, when digital technologies, such as mobile phones, the internet, and cameras emerged and began shaping both aging and the environments in which people age ( ). Various term combinations were systematically searched in eight electronic databases, including Web of Science, PubMed, Scopus, Embase, CINAHL Complete, APA PsycINFO, SocINDEX, and Academic Search Complete during the second week of June 2025. In addition to systematic searches in databases, we obtained relevant gray literature, as recommended by review guidelines ( ) through three sources selected for their relevance to digital technology and care for older adults living alone: (1) Institutional report searches were performed using the World Health Organization’s Institutional Repository for Information Sharing database. (2) Google Scholar searches were conducted using the advanced search function with topic-specific keywords. (3) Reference lists of all included studies were checked to identify additional relevant literature. Records were managed and screened using Covidence. The search yielded 21 records, all of which were manually retrieved and screened, resulting in 8 records.

## Step 3: Selecting studies
The study selection followed a two-stage screening process based on predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria (see ). In the first phase, the first two authors conducted a pilot screening of 84 articles (7% of 1,187 studies) to ensure consistent application of the criteria. Twelve discrepancies were identified and resolved through discussion, which helped to refine the shared understanding of the eligibility criteria and enhance consistency in subsequent screening. The same two authors then independently screened the titles and abstracts of all retrieved records (n = 1,187), focusing on whether the studies involved older adults living alone and included care intervention(s). Studies were retained if they included mixed samples containing older adults living alone, even when this group was not the primary target population. This approach allowed us to capture a broader range of relevant interventions involving this social group. Studies deemed potentially eligible (n = 63) were selected for full-text review.
 In the second phase, full-text articles were independently assessed by the same reviewers to determine final eligibility. This phase also included assessing whether the studies described any use of digital technology in care interventions targeting older adults living alone. During screening, two authors independently reviewed each article and identified technologies using a preliminary list of technology-related terms that was iteratively expanded as additional technologies were encountered. Discrepancies were resolved through discussion, with the involvement of the third author when necessary. This process resulted in the inclusion of 29 eligible studies. In addition, we searched gray literature via Google Scholar and by checking the citations of articles included. As these records substantially overlapped with those retrieved from academic databases, our search strategy appeared to have captured most relevant studies. All records were screened using the same criteria. Following title and abstract screening, full-text review yielded eight additional eligible studies.

## Step 4: Charting data
Data relevant to the scoping questions were systematically organized by extracting relevant information from eligible articles. A structured data charting form was created and refined jointly by all authors, and then pilot-tested independently by the same two authors on a random sample of four studies. The data-charting form captured the following information from each included study: author(s), year of publication, publication venue (journal or database), study setting, study design, methods used, identified research gap, sample size, sociodemographic and health backgrounds of the sample, care areas addressed, intervention overview, intervention period, observation timepoints, intervention outcomes (if reported), and specific information related to digital technology use, including what digital technology was used, how it was used (e.g., user groups), its reported intervention outcomes, and benefits and challenges associated with using digital technologies. When studies involved mixed samples and did not report findings specific to older adults living alone, the extracted results reflected those of the mixed sample.
Using the data-charting form, two authors independently extracted information from the included studies, dividing the workload equally, following the guidance ( ). Data were charted using the wording and terminology from the original papers. Each author cross-checked the other’s entries, and any discrepancies were resolved through discussion among all authors.

## Step 5: Collating, analyzing, and reporting results
A descriptive analysis was conducted on study characteristics, sample characteristics, and intervention features to provide a quantitative overview of the included studies and to identify prominent patterns and knowledge gaps within the dataset. Following the descriptive analysis, we conducted a content analysis, following the guidelines ( ) to identify major categories related to technology use in care interventions for older adults living alone. The content analysis was performed by two authors to iteratively refine categories and resolve discrepancies through team discussions. During analysis, studies were grouped into two categories: those including only older adults living alone and those with mixed samples. These groups were reported separately across key aspects, such as intervention outcomes, to allow comparison. ATLAS.ti was used to facilitate the analysis. The results are presented as narratives and in tables, with the study group specified for each study.

## Results

## Characteristics of resulting papers
Of the 1,762 records retrieved through database searches, 29 studies met the inclusion criteria. An additional eight gray literature studies were identified through citation-checking and searches in Google Scholar and institutional repositories. In total, 37 studies were included in the final review. summarizes the scoping review process and the final number of papers retrieved for analysis.
 
 presents the characteristics of the 37 included studies (the first 21 focusing on solo-living older adults and the remaining studies using mixed samples), showing a clear increase in publication activity over time in the scoped area, particularly after 2011. Notably, six of the 37 studies were published in 2025 alone—by our search cut-off in June 2025—highlighting the rapid growth of this research field and the increasing diversity of digital technologies examined. The United States contributed the largest number of studies (n = 11, 29.7%), followed by South Korea (n = 7, 18.9%) and Spain (n = 3, 8.1%). Randomized controlled trials were the most common study design (n = 19, 51.4%), followed by quasi-experimental studies (n = 13, 35.1%). Regarding publication outlets, 43.2% of the articles were published in journals focusing on geriatrics and gerontology (n = 16), followed by those in health services research (n = 6, 16.2%), and medication management (n = 5, 13.5%).

## Sample characteristics
Across all included studies, sample sizes ranged from 14 to 2,365 participants, with 19 studies (51.4% of included studies) involving fewer than 100 participants. Most studies focused on older adults aged 65 and above, with mean ages between 70 and 85 years. A female predominance was observed across studies, with the proportion of women ranging from 41.9% to 100%, and only one study including fewer than half female participants. Ethnicity was often not reported, but when reported, some U.S.-based studies included predominantly White participants ( ; ; ; ). Marital status, income level, and educational attainment were inconsistently reported. Most samples (n = 31, 83.9%) comprised vulnerable older adults living alone, respectively with frailty ( ), multiple chronic conditions ( ), or with functional, cognitive, and psychosocial problems ( ; ).
More than half of the included studies (n = 21, 56.8%) specifically targeted older adults living alone, while the remaining studies (n = 16, 43.2%) included mixed samples in which the proportion of solo-living older adults ranged from 14% to 86%. Compared with mixed-sample studies, studies focusing exclusively on older adults living alone generally had smaller sample sizes (14–396 vs. 34–2,365), participants with higher mean ages (mean age: 75.7–86.1 years vs. 70–82.7 years where reported), and a slightly higher proportion of women (47.2%–100% vs. 41.9%–85%).

## Care areas and intervention settings
Across all included studies, interventions addressed a wide range of care areas, including medication adherence ( ), fall prevention ( ), home-based care support ( ), chronic condition management (e.g., hypertension) ( ; ), coping with loneliness ( ), health promotion such as physical activity and healthy eating ( ), and tailored care during the hospital-to-home transition ( ). In terms of duration, intervention periods ranged from 4 weeks, 8 weeks, and 12 weeks to 6 months, 1 year, and up to 2 years. Observation timepoints were typically organized around baseline and one or more follow-up assessments, with follow-up timings varying from 1 to 12 months depending on the intervention. Some studies also included pre-post comparisons (e.g., 6 months before and after enrollment) ( ; ) or multi-point follow-ups to track change trajectories over time ( ). In terms of intervention settings, 51.4% were delivered in home-based settings (n = 19), 43.2% in community-based settings (n = 16), and 5.4% in residential care settings such as senior living campuses or nursing homes (n = 2).
Studies focusing exclusively on older adults living alone most commonly addressed psychosocial and mental health care areas, with nearly half (10/21, 47.6%) targeting issues such as loneliness and depression. In comparison, mixed-sample studies covered a broader range of care areas. Studies focusing on solo-living older adults also more often implemented home-based interventions (12/21, 57.1%), compared with mixed-sample studies (7/16, 43.8%). No notable differences were observed between the two groups in terms of intervention duration.

## Types of digital technologies used and their functions
Our analysis identified five main types of digital technology used in care interventions for older adults living alone, categorized based on their primary functions. These included (1) ICTs that support health check-ins and care coordination, such as telephone calls ( ; ), and WhatsApp and Zoom for participant engagement and care delivery ( ); (2) devices for health and activity monitoring, including the QuietCare infrared motion sensor system ( ), Actiwatch Spectrum PRO ( ), and smart bands used with the HAHA2022 app ( ); (3) health information management technologies for recording, analysis, and decision-making, such as patient medication records ( ), and electronic medical record-based frailty screening tool ( ); (4) interactive assistive tools for daily functioning and companionship, including voice assistants such as Amazon Echo and Alexa ( ; ) and social robots such as the parrot-shaped PIO ( ); and (5) digital applications for cognitive and behavioral interventions, such as the Road Tour computerized training program and its web-based successor Double Decision ( , ), YouTube-based educational content ( ), e-book platforms for delivering digital interventions ( ), and a decision support system for dementia care planning ( ).
From a temporal perspective, care interventions progressively shifted toward mobile, multifunctional, and intelligent digital technologies. Earlier interventions in the 2000s and early 2010s mainly used telephone calls and record systems for health check-ins and consultation. By the mid-2010s, these were increasingly replaced by sensor-based and web-based monitoring systems, followed by mobile and smart tools in the early 2020s, and most recently, AI-powered devices ( ; ; ; ). Although digital technologies addressed a range of care needs, interventions since 2021 have placed greater emphasis on cognitive health, social isolation, loneliness, and depression. For example, voice assistants such as Amazon Alexa and the Echo Show learned from users’ routines and provided customized reminders and health information ( ; ). Similarly, social robots used facial recognition, emotion detection, and interactive conversations to foster social interaction and companionship ( ). These innovations illustrate the increasing integration of personalization and automated decision-making in supporting older adults living alone.

## Intervention outcomes
In studies focusing exclusively on solo-living older adults, intervention outcomes were primarily reported at the individual level and covered several health domains. These include improvements in physical health and functional ability, such as enhanced activities of daily living, reduced pain severity, and improved physical functioning ( ; ; ). Interventions also report improvements in psychological and mental health, such as reduced depression and anxiety, and enhanced psychological well-being ( ; ; ; ). In addition, several studies identify improvements in social well-being and connection, such as increased social support and reduced loneliness and isolation ( ; ; ; ; ; ). Other reported outcomes include healthier lifestyle behaviors, such as improved dietary habits, greater nutritional knowledge, and increased levels of physical activity ( ; ; ), and greater self-empowerment, including improved self-efficacy in aging, hypertension control, loneliness management, and digital health literacy ( ; ; ). By contrast, service-level outcomes were not commonly reported: only two studies identified outcomes at this level, including a reduced number of prescribed medications and a lower risk of hospital readmission among home-visit participants after discharge ( ; ).
For studies using mixed samples that included older adults living alone to varying degrees, most did not disentangle findings specific to those living alone from the overall study results. In these studies, intervention outcomes were reported at both the individual and service levels. At the individual level, studies reported improvements in physical health ( ), reduced frailty ( ; ), improved cognitive health ( ), better mental and psychological well-being ( ; ; ), enhanced social well-being ( ; ; ), and healthier lifestyle behaviors ( ; ; ). Compared with studies focusing exclusively on solo-living older adults, evidence from mixed-sample studies more frequently emphasized service-level outcomes. Several studies reported reduced health service utilization, including reductions in Accident and Emergency Department attendance, hospital visits, admissions, readmissions, hospital bed days, and unplanned hospitalizations ( ; ; ; ). Some studies (e.g., ) also reported delayed institutionalization by up to seven months. In addition, evidence of economic outcomes was observed, such as reduced healthcare costs ( ; ).
However, not all interventions were effective; several digital monitoring and telephone-based interventions showed no significant health effects among older adults living alone ( ; , ; ), pointing to contextual factors that moderated the effectiveness of technology-supported care interventions. For instance, ; ) found that their telephone-based nutritional follow-up failed to improve activities of daily living or reduce hospital readmissions, whereas in-person home visits did, suggesting that the absence of direct physical assessment may have prevented professionals from adequately evaluating participants’ dietary behaviors and living environment.
Additionally, poorly designed technologies did not sufficiently address the needs of solo-living older adults. As shown in study, telephone-based follow-up for solo-living older adults with dementia yielded a non-significant reduction in re-hospitalization, which was partially explained by the limitations of non-face-to-face communication in addressing the complex needs of this vulnerable group. Also, found that passive home monitoring improved informal caregiver burden but produced no benefits for solo-living older adults across any health domains. This contrast suggests that technologies designed primarily to support caregiver relief may not necessarily translate into health benefits for older adults, particularly when they lack interactive or responsive components directed at older adults living alone. These findings indicate that effective digital interventions require not only technical functionality but also usability that accommodates the diverse needs and living conditions of solo-living older adults.
From an intersectionality perspective, multiple social factors, including gender, race, socioeconomic status, cognitive and health status, were acknowledged in the reviewed studies as relevant characteristics of older adults living alone. When examining the diversity of older adults living alone in terms of intervention outcomes, all included studies only reported basic demographic characteristics and assessed intervention effects at the aggregate level, either by comparing outcomes before and after the intervention or between intervention and control groups. However, few included studies reported differences in intervention outcomes across subgroups within older adults living alone.

## Integrating digital technology into care practices
Across all included studies, this section presents key characteristics of technology use in care interventions, including technology users and purposes, frequency of use, and support for personalized care. It also highlights differences between studies focusing exclusively on older adults living alone and those including mixed samples across these aspects.

Who used the technology, and for what purpose? In 14 studies (37.8%), professionals or volunteers (e.g., physicians, nurses, dieticians, pharmacists, physiotherapists, and telehealth assistants) were the sole users. They used digital tools to monitor or coordinate care, support study adherence, screen at-risk individuals ( ), and deliver psychological or specialized counseling ( ). In 10 studies (27.0%), older adults (including solo-living participants) were the exclusive users, completing physical and cognitive training tasks ( ), and social robots or voice assistants for companionship and social interactions ( ; ). In 13 studies (35.1%), multi-user configurations were reported, in which older adults and other actors (e.g., health and care professionals, peers, and researchers) interacted through shared technological systems such as sensor-based home monitoring for home care delivery ( ), nutrition management and tele-coaching apps for health promotion ( ; ), and digital platforms fostering health education and social support ( ; ; ).

How often was the technology used? Scheduled/regular use (n = 18, 48.7%) was the most prevalent, typically structured around fixed intervals such as weekly sessions ( ) or per-visit monitoring ( ). Real-time/continuous use (n = 4, 10.8%) involved ongoing engagement, including daily activity tracking via wearables ( ), continuous coaching ( ), or live data monitoring during interventions ( ). A smaller subset (n = 4, 10.8%) employed user-initiated interactions, where participants determined frequency based on individual needs ( ). The rest of the studies did not specify usage frequency. Overall, most interventions emphasized provider-driven, scheduled interactions, with fewer implementations of participant-controlled models.

How did technology enable personalized care? Our analysis identified three personalization approaches. The most common (25 studies, 67.6%) was professional-driven personalization, in which care professionals and/or researchers manually gathered data and tailored interventions, using phones, video calls, and wearable devices. These targeted chronic disease management ( ; ; ; ), social isolation and loneliness ( ; ), fall prevention ( ; ), and nutrition ( ). The second approach, semi-automated personalization (6 studies, 16.2%), combined automated functions with professional oversight. For example, the Computer Aided Nutritional Analysis Program analyzed food diaries among older adults living alone, while nurses and dietitians provided weekly guidance and facilitated adherence ( ). Likewise, wearables and mobile devices in behavioral therapy delivered automated push notifications, while health coaches monitored engagement, set goals, and developed weekly action plans with older adults ( ). The final approach, automation-driven personalization (six studies, 16.2%), featured interactive technologies that adapted to users’ health conditions, behaviors, and responses in real time. Examples included a web-based speed-of-processing training program that adapted difficulty levels as participants improved ( , ), social robots that reduced depression and loneliness through expressive eye displays, programmed speech, and coordinated movements ( ), and personal voice assistants that offered tailored content and companionship via conversational interaction ( ; ; ).

How did technology use differ between interventions for older adults living alone and for general older adults? Care interventions with mixed samples often relied on phone calls, sometimes supplemented by simple digital tools such as WhatsApp, Zoom, or YouTube ( ; ). By contrast, interventions targeting older adults living alone tended to adopt a broader and more advanced set of technologies, including mobile apps with automated syncing through smart bands ( ), voice assistants, or social robots pre-programmed by technicians ( ; ). Across both groups, more recent studies increasingly incorporated support from peers, training sessions, or researcher guidance to facilitate technology use ( ; ; ; ; ).

## Benefits of digital technology use in care interventions

 summarizes the intervention outcomes, benefits, and challenges for digital technologies across key domains of care (physical health, mental and cognitive health, social connectedness, aging in place, and healthcare utilization). Building on this overview, this section highlights specific observations at the individual, organizational/service delivery, and system levels.
 The benefits of digital technologies were demonstrated in several areas. At the individual level, digital technology supported older adults living alone by improving health outcomes, enhancing digital skills, increasing accessibility to care, and reducing caregiver burden. For example, the PRISM system, designed to reduce social isolation, loneliness, and perceived vulnerability, not only decreased loneliness through email and game engagement but also improved digital confidence ( ). Likewise, a home-based activity monitoring system for frail and psychogeriatric solo-living older adults reduced subjective burden among informal caregivers ( ). Digital tools also expanded access to care and support for solo-living older adults in social isolation ( ). At the organizational (service delivery) level, as mostly shown in studies with mixed samples, digital technologies improved adherence and compliance by providing phone reminders, enabling real-time tracking, and offering flexible delivery formats through videos, mobile apps, and online chats ( ; ). Phone calls also extended the reach and continuity of care services, particularly during COVID-19 disruptions ( ). At the system level, technologies enhanced care coordination and integration across community services, enabled specialist input, and supported individualized care planning ( ; ).

## Challenges or limitations associated with digital technology use
As summarized in , challenges of implementing digital technology-supported care interventions for older adults were identified at the individual, service delivery, and system levels. At the individual level, barriers included limited digital skills, negative attitudes toward digital technology, and insufficient social support. Some older adults were unfamiliar with digital functions such as sending or receiving text messages ( ). Evidence also suggests that discomfort with technology may reduce participation or lead to attrition. For example, a peer-support program delivered by phone during the COVID-19 pandemic reported that 19% of participants, most of whom lived alone, withdrew due to discomfort with phone use or lack of interest in using mobile applications ( ). Similarly, low confidence in using devices such as wearables, tablets, and mobile applications has been reported in mobile health interventions targeting depressive symptoms among solo-living older adults, with financial concerns about device costs further limiting participation ( ).

At the service delivery level, limitations in functionality and usability hindered care provision. For example, found that a computerized system for patient medication records was unable to effectively identify older adults living alone who were at high risk of medication non-adherence. reported that home monitoring sensors among frail and psychogeriatric older adults living alone generated false alarms, leading to unnecessary caregiver responses. observed that an online frailty screening system for older adults (46.5% living alone) could not capture the full scope of nursing interventions, as predefined categories lacked sufficient detail and nurses faced time constraints. Similar concerns about limited technological affordances for healthcare professionals were raised by . At the system level, digital inequalities and exclusion were identified as broader societal challenges for solo-living older adults. found that solo-living older adults without home internet access (often due to rural location and financial constraints) were unable to use conversational voice assistants designed to reduce pain and loneliness.

## Discussion
This scoping review synthesizes evidence from the past 25-year research on the use of digital technologies in care interventions for older adults living alone. Across the included studies, five main types of technologies were identified: health information management technologies, health and activity monitoring devices, health check-in and care coordination ICTs, digital applications for cognitive and behavioral interventions, and interactive assistive tools. Over time, these technologies have shifted from relatively basic tools (e.g., telephone calls) to more advanced, interactive, and AI-enabled systems. Their functions have also evolved from simple health consultations toward more person-centered and preventive applications that support real-time monitoring, fall prevention, social interaction, and independent living. Interventions targeting broader older adult populations often employed simpler, low-threshold tools, while programs designed specifically for solo-living older adults tended to use technologically sophisticated and interactive systems.
The findings suggest that technology-assisted care interventions can generate a range of health benefits for older adults living alone. Across the included studies focusing exclusively on this population, reported outcomes span multiple domains, including improvements in physical health, psychological well-being, social connectedness, and lifestyle behaviors. However, most evidence relates primarily to individual-level outcomes. Evidence regarding impacts at the service or health system level remains limited in studies specifically targeting older adults living alone. Some studies using mixed samples that included solo-living older adults reported broader outcomes, such as reduced healthcare utilization and economic cost ( ; ; ; ). Nevertheless, these findings cannot be directly attributed to or generalized to older adults living alone, as the proportion of participants in this group varied substantially across studies and was sometimes relatively small.
In addition, the mechanisms underlying the reported benefits remain unclear. Even when positive outcomes were observed, it is difficult to disentangle whether these improvements were primarily driven by the digital technologies used, the involvement of care practitioners/volunteers, or the care services delivered. At the same time, few studies provided details on differences in intervention outcomes among subgroups of older adults living alone, despite that this population is highly diverse in health status, socioeconomic conditions, living environments, social networks, care needs, technological literacy, and digital access ( ; ; ). Thus, potential variations in intervention effects across different profiles of solo-living older adults remain largely unexplored, limiting our ability to assess how effectiveness may differ within this population.
Our findings further show how digital interventions risk reinforcing ageist assumptions that portray older adults as passive, dependent, or technologically inept ( ). In 38% of the studies, digital tools were used exclusively by professionals or volunteers, leaving older adults with limited opportunities for direct engagement. A further 49% structured technology use around rigid, provider-driven schedules rather than allowing participants to initiate or adapt use according to their needs. Such patterns reflect paternalistic models of care in which technologies are implemented for, rather than with, older adults, thus constraining autonomy and reinforcing systemic barriers to equitable access for solo-living older persons.
Across the studies reviewed, personalization was most often driven by human expertise, with digital technologies playing a supportive role. Professional judgment remained central to analyzing complex needs, safeguarding quality, and providing relational trust and emotional support ( ; ; , ; ), while digital tools complemented professional input ( ; ). The three modes of personalization identified in this review reflect different configurations of human-technology collaboration in care. As novel technologies are increasingly introduced into care settings, discussions should extend beyond the functional aspects of care delivery to also consider the relational dimensions of care. For older adults living alone, care interactions often provide not only health support but also opportunities for social connection and for understanding individuals’ values and preferences. Technologies should therefore not be viewed solely as tools for monitoring or task completion, but as sociotechnical systems that construct, shape, and transform care relationships and practices. In this context, an important challenge is how digital or AI-enabled systems can preserve the human connection that many solo-living older adults value. Digital tools may therefore be most beneficial when they augment, rather than replace, human care relationships.
Despite the promise of technology-assisted care for this population, our findings also highlight several concerns and risks associated with technology-enabled care. Socio-gerontechnology research has identified persistent challenges such as ageism, privacy and security concerns, users’ physical and cognitive limitations, and the digital divide ( ), all of which may constrain the ability of technologies to address the diverse and evolving needs of older adults. In addition, as ethnography of eldercare automation in Japan illustrates, the design and deployment of AI-driven systems often reflect institutional or corporate visions of efficiency rather than the lived realities of care.

## Limitations
This scoping review has several limitations. First, it relies primarily on published studies indexed in selected academic databases, which may have led to the omission of relevant gray literature. Second, although all included studies were peer-reviewed publications, we did not assess their methodological quality, consistent with the aims of a scoping review that seeks to map the field rather than critically appraise individual studies. Third, extracting relevant information from diverse sources inevitably carries a risk of bias; to mitigate this, we engaged in iterative team discussions throughout the review process, providing an informal layer of peer assessment. Fourth, a scoping review cannot capture how technologies are used in everyday life. The lived realities of solo-living older adults, including how they interpret, trust, adapt to, or resist digital care interventions, remain largely beyond the scope of this review. A further review of ethnographic and qualitative research is essential to illuminate these dimensions and to complement the intervention-focused evidence summarized here. Finally, 16 included studies involved mixed samples in which only a subset of participants lived alone. We distinguished between studies focusing exclusively on older adults living alone and those with mixed samples during analysis and reporting. Still, some findings should be interpreted cautiously when applied specifically to solo-living older adults.

## Implications for future research, policy, and practice
This review highlights several critical gaps in the evidence base. First, nearly all reviewed interventions (95%) were implemented in high-income settings, leaving open questions about how digital technologies might function in low- and middle-income contexts where solo-living older adults are growing rapidly, and care systems face sharper resource constraints ( ). Second, underrepresented groups, particularly those with lower socioeconomic status, remain overlooked, despite well-documented disparities in technology access, digital literacy, and social support ( ). An intersectional perspective is also needed to capture how gender, socioeconomic status, geographic location, cultural context, care setting (home, community, or clinic), age group, and health conditions (e.g., dementia, chronic illness, frailty) interact to shape access and outcomes in larger samples. Third, while AI, especially generative AI such as large language models, has shown potential to lower barriers to technology uptake and address unmet needs of older adults ( ), few studies examined how AI and other novel technologies could be integrated into care interventions and practices or how older adults themselves interpret and sustain their use. Finally, most existing studies reported only short-term improvements in health and service utilization, with limited follow-up beyond 12–24 months. This constrains our understanding of whether initial benefits persist, under what conditions, and for whom. Also, cost-effectiveness remains a striking blind spot. Without robust economic and longitudinal evaluations, it is difficult to assess the sustainability, scalability, and equity of technology-supported care models.
Our findings carry several implications for policy and practice. Care policies should recognize that solo-living older persons often lack immediate familial support, making participatory approaches to digital care interventions particularly crucial. Beyond promoting digital integration, policies must prioritize mechanisms that connect these individuals with peer support, community outreach, or remote care monitoring that respect their autonomy ( ; ). Reducing systemic barriers is equally essential—especially for those who live alone in rural or resource-poor settings where affordability, internet access, and digital skills are limited. Sustained investment in pilot programs across diverse care settings (e.g., community, rural, home-based, long-term, and primary care) can provide valuable evidence for scalable, context-sensitive models of integration. Moreover, future policy frameworks should explicitly account for equity concerns by targeting underrepresented groups such as solo-living older persons with lower socioeconomic status or limited digital literacy who remain at greatest risk of exclusion. For practice, care professionals should develop ethical and professional guidelines that safeguard the autonomy, privacy, and informed decision-making of older adults living alone, while recognizing their diverse preferences and vulnerabilities. Technology developers and implementers should adopt participatory design methods that engage this group of older persons as active partners, ensuring technologies are user-friendly, reliable, and culturally responsive ( ).

## Conclusion
This scoping review maps both the promise and the limitations of digital technologies in supporting the care of older adults living alone. The reviewed studies reflect a field still in an early stage, with considerable variation in study design and findings. Study designs ranged from single-group pre-post studies to randomized controlled trials; more than half involved fewer than 100 participants, several were explicitly feasibility or pilot studies, follow-up periods rarely exceeded 12 months, and outcome measures varied considerably across studies. The reported outcomes should therefore be understood as indicative of potential rather than established efficacy. While most interventions reported improvements in physical health, psychological and social well-being, and lifestyle behaviors, they remain provider-driven and unequally distributed. Ensuring that digital care technologies foster equity, agency, and person-centeredness requires participatory design, long-term evaluation, and attention to structural barriers. As the number of solo-living older adults continues to rise globally, addressing these challenges will be essential to building more inclusive and sustainable models of technology-supported care.
