## Abstract

## Introduction
Migrant women often experience significant disparities in maternal health outcomes compared to host populations, due to language barriers, cultural differences, and limited maternal health literacy (MHL). Despite its importance, there is a shortage of tailored interventions specifically designed for migrant communities, namely, African migrant women in Europe.

## Methods
This qualitative study applied a co-design approach using the Optimizing Health Literacy and Access (Ophelia) methodology. Two separate idea generation workshops were conducted in Lisbon – one, involving pregnant migrant women (n = 6), and another with community-based healthcare and social professionals (n = 10). Discussions were guided by vignettes developed prior to the sessions to reflect real-life challenges. Thematic analysis of the transcripts was complemented by descriptive analysis of sociodemographic questionnaire data.

## Results
Three main themes emerged: (1) health literacy barriers (e.g., low digital literacy, lack of information, cultural and bureaucratic obstacles); (2) strengths and resources (e.g., personal resilience, proactive behaviours, and community associations); (3) co-created interventions (e.g., community support groups, culturally sensitive maternal education, and healthcare providers training).

## Conclusion
The study highlights the value of a participatory co-design approach in identifying context-specific, culturally relevant interventions to enhance MHL among African migrant women. The insights gained provide valuable direction for future research and policy development aimed at enhancing access to maternal healthcare for migrant populations.

Keywords: Pregnancy health, Non-native mothers, Ophelia, Health equity, Health and well-being, Access to services

## Plain Language Summary
Many pregnant migrant women face difficulties in finding, understanding, assessing, remembering, and using essential health information due to language barriers, cultural differences, and a lack of information about how health services work. Furthermore, in many cases, they are unaware of their rights or how to access free healthcare. To respond to these challenges, the study brought together pregnant African migrant women and professionals working in the community (social workers and health professionals) to devise solutions. Through idea generation workshops, several obstacles were identified, such as a lack of family support, financial difficulties, low digital literacy, and responses from health services that were not always sensitive. In contrast, resources were also recognized, such as the resilience of these women, their initiative in seeking information, and the fundamental role of community associations that provide both bureaucratic and emotional and psychological support. Based on these findings, several intervention proposals emerged, such as maternal health education sessions adapted to the realities of these women, the creation of community support networks, mental health support programs, and information for health professionals on culturally sensitive care. The study sought to understand and improve access to maternal health for migrant women by directly involving them in the creation of effective and adapted solutions. These results offer valuable clues for future research and public policies promoting better maternal health access for migrant populations.

Os conhecimentos obtidos fornecem orientações úteis para investigação futura e para o desenvolvimento de políticas que visem melhorar o acesso aos cuidados de saúde materna entre populações migrantes.

## Introduction
Like anyone else, migrants have the human right to the highest attainable standard of health. However, the recent report on the health of refugees and migrants shows that, despite significant progress in research, practice, and policy, millions of migrants still face poorer health outcomes than their host communities [ ]. Among migrant-native health disparities, those experienced in maternal health are particularly concerning, due to their far-reaching implications for women, as well as for their children and families. Despite often exhibiting better pregnancy-related behaviour than natives (e.g., less smoking and drinking), evidence shows that migrant women are overall more likely to experience inadequate utilization and poorer quality of maternal care services, in addition to some serious adverse outcomes like postpartum depression and even mortality [ – ].
These disparities in maternal health between migrant and native women result from an array of complex and interrelated factors including social (e.g., socioeconomic status and social support), cultural (e.g., unique health beliefs and language barriers), and structural factors (e.g., immigration policies and inclusivity of the healthcare system) [ ]. A crucial and modifiable, yet often overlooked, determinant of maternal health disparities is maternal health literacy (MHL). The World Health Organization defines health literacy as “people’s knowledge, confidence and comfort to access, understand, appraise, remember, and use information about health and healthcare, for the health and well-being of themselves and those around them” [ ].
MHL, more specifically, refers to a woman’s ability to seek, comprehend, evaluate, and apply health information throughout the pregnancy journey. It is influenced not only by individual factors but also by contextual and systemic elements – such as health service accessibility, cultural responsiveness, and social support networks [ , ].
One way to promote pregnant women’s health literacy is through the help of maternity care providers, who typically play a crucial role in by offering personalized maternity education and counselling, assisting women in identifying and understanding available options, and guiding disease prevention and management [ ]. Adequate MHL empowers pregnant women to understand medical information, communicate with healthcare providers, and navigate the healthcare system effectively [ , ]. As a result, health-literate women can take proactive measures toward preventive health behaviours and make well-informed decisions throughout their perinatal journey, ultimately fostering better health outcomes for themselves and their children [ , ]. Considering that populations experiencing vulnerabilities often face limited access to health information and services – due to a multitude of socioeconomic, cultural, and linguistic barriers – it is particularly important to address their health literacy needs and the resulting inequities [ ].
Although involving people in health research is becoming the standard practice in many countries, the participation of refugees and migrants in research related to their health remains limited [ ]. A systematic review of health literacy interventions revealed a paucity of high-quality interventions among migrants and refugees in Europe, particularly among those of African origin [ ]. The majority of existing interventions focus on mental health, with almost none addressing maternal health [ ]. In fact, interventions aimed at improving MHL appear to be scarce not only among migrants, but also in the general population [ ]. Additionally, while most health literacy interventions focus primarily on simplifying written health information and making it more user-friendly, limited attention has been given to the role of healthcare providers in catering for people’s diverse health literacy needs, strengths, and preferences [ ].
Portugal, like other European countries, has experienced a significant increase in its immigrant population, rising from 798,480 in 2022 to 1,044,606 in 2023 [ , ]. A substantial proportion of these immigrants come from African countries, particularly Portuguese-speaking African countries (PALOP), such as Angola, Cape Verde, and Guinea-Bissau [ ]. This demographic shift has led to a growing number of pregnant women of migrant origin seeking care within the National Health System (NHS). Notably, the percentage of children born in Portugal to mothers of foreign nationality has increased by eight percentage points over the past decade, with nearly one in four babies in 2022 having an immigrant mother [ ]. Given the unique social, cultural, and linguistic barriers migrant women may face when accessing maternal healthcare, tailored health literacy interventions are essential to ensure equitable access to information, improve maternal health outcomes, and promote overall well-being for both mothers and their children [ ].
The present study is part of a broader project – Health Literacy, Health Promotion, and Social Cohesion for the Prevention of Non-Communicable Diseases among Migrant Populations among migrant communities in Lisbon, Portugal [ ]. As part of phase 1 of the Ophelia process, the project conducted a health literacy strengths and needs assessment of the migrant community. This assessment involved administering the Health Literacy Questionnaire [ , ] to 1,126 migrants and conducting interviews with thirty-six individuals. A subsequent cluster analysis identified sixteen distinct subgroups, ensuring that health literacy initiatives align with the principle of “leaving no one behind” [ ]. One of these clusters, comprising mainly African migrant women – predominantly from PALOP countries – emerged as a priority due to significant health literacy needs. These women, of reproductive age, faced multiple challenges such as low income, limited education, unemployment, irregular immigration status, and short duration of residence in Portugal (less than 5 years). Interviews further highlighted critical health literacy gaps in maternal and child health within this group.
One recognized effective strategy for designing health literacy interventions that are responsive to the needs of all stakeholders, within a specific context is a co-design participatory approach – one of the key principles of the Optimizing Health Literacy and Access (Ophelia) process [ , , ]. By integrating the experiences of both people with lived experience and other stakeholders, this approach enhances the acceptability of the designed health literacy intervention, thereby ensuring its adoption and sustainability, and consequently improving access, equity, and outcomes [ , , ]. Given the urgent need to address MHL among African migrant women in Portugal, this study aimed to co-design culturally relevant and feasible health literacy interventions through the Ophelia participatory methodology.

## Materials and Methods
The Ophelia process follows a mixed-method research design to identify and co-design meaningful health literacy interventions tailored to the needs of the target population. It is structured into three main phases, further subdivided into eight steps (shown in ).
 

## Participants
The present study focuses on this specific profile, particularly pregnant migrant women. Pregnancy is a critical period for decision-making, behaviour change, and the acquisition of new knowledge related to maternal health. Engaging women during this time allows for a more immediate impact on outcomes such as healthcare navigation, prenatal care adherence, and informed birth preparation.
The participating women were born in PALOP countries and received support from a non-profit community association that provides comprehensive care throughout pregnancy and postpartum. In 2022, PALOP-born women constituted the largest group benefiting from the association’s services.
Eligibility criteria included the following: being pregnant, born in a PALOP country, and receiving support from the community association. Participants were selected through convenience sampling. No participants refused or dropped out.

## Instruments and Materials
This study specifically examines two key steps of the Ophelia approach: the development of vignettes (Ophelia phase 1, step 2, activity 2.5) (shown in ) and the stakeholder and community engagement process (Ophelia phase 1, step 3) (shown in ). Through a qualitative approach, this analysis aims to deepen the understanding of health literacy challenges and co-develop interventions tailored to the needs of migrant women.
Three evidence-based hypothetical case studies (vignettes) were co-designed (shown in ) to bring the profile to life for participants and guide the “ideas generation workshops” that aimed to generate actionable health literacy solutions [ ]. The vignettes, representing typical maternity experiences of migrant women from PALOP countries, highlighted common concerns and challenges related to low health literacy. Developed through a structured approach combining quantitative and qualitative analysis (phase 1) (shown in ) with community participation, the vignettes depicted realistic experiences of pregnant migrant women, including healthcare access challenges, health literacy perceptions, and social barriers. The co-creation process integrated testimonies from migrant women and input from healthcare and social support professionals, ensuring the narratives reflected real-life situations and enabling participants to better relate to the scenarios, leading to interventions aligned with the community’s needs. The vignettes were co-developed by the research team and the community workers, prior to the workshops. Stakeholders (women and community workers) were then invited to participate in the ideas generation workshops.
 

## Procedure
In accordance with the Ophelia methodology, the study employed idea generation workshops as a participatory technique aimed at developing practical, community-informed solutions. This method was selected for its capacity to go beyond problem identification, fostering collaborative reflection and co-creation of actionable strategies with participants.
Two idea generation workshops were conducted: one with migrant pregnant women (n = 6) and another with health and social care professionals (n = 10) working in the community-based association. Convenience sampling was used to recruit participants. The pregnant women were directly invited at the association, while an email invitation was sent to community workers. The workshop with the pregnant women was held in person in the community venue and lasted 80 min, while the workshop with the community workers was conducted online and lasted 60 min.
Before the workshops, the pregnant participants completed a short structured sociodemographic questionnaire. This instrument collected basic information such as age, country of origin, legal status, duration of residence in Portugal, educational background, employment status, and obstetric history. These data were used to contextualize the study population (shown in and ).
 Each workshop started with an overview of the objectives, followed by the presentation of the vignettes. It was clarified that the stories were fictional and were intended to represent some of the challenges that migrant pregnant women commonly face in accessing and managing their maternal health. To guide the discussion and generate potential ideas for health literacy actions, the following questions were used:
1.Have you had experiences similar to the woman in this story? And/or do you know of anyone who has had similar experiences?
2.What issues is this person dealing with?
3.What strategies could be used to help this person?
4.What could health services or community organizations do to help many people like this in a community?
At the end of each workshop, all the main problems and ideas that emerged were summarized to check with the participants that the main ideas were captured. This debriefing step allowed for a basic form of member checking. Additionally, observational field notes were taken during the sessions.
Workshops were facilitated by a member of the research team experienced in conducting workshops using the Ophelia methodology and were observed by two other members. The facilitator was a trained researcher with prior experience in participatory design and qualitative research involving migrant populations.

## Data Analysis
The first author conducted a thematic analysis of all transcripts following Braun & Clarke’s six-step method [ , ]. This process included familiarizing with the data, creating initial codes that align with the research goals, identifying overarching themes and sub-themes, reviewing these themes, and finally, defining and naming them. The coding and theme development were flexible, adapting as the analysis progressed. The first author primarily ensured the internal consistency of the themes, while co-author M.J.M. contributed to external consistency by advising on the coding framework and suggesting modifications. For a comprehensive interpretation, cross-referencing with the sociodemographic questionnaire was performed. NVivo 14 software was used for the qualitative data coding and management. In order to more accurately illustrate the main emerging themes, quotes extracted from the participants dialogues were selected.

## Ethical Considerations
Conducting research with pregnant migrant women required heightened ethical sensitivity, especially considering the potential risks of emotional distress. To address these challenges, several safeguards were implemented throughout the study. Participation was entirely voluntary, and culturally sensitive informed consent was obtained, prior to data collection. Audio recording was only carried out with explicit permission, and all transcripts were fully anonymized during transcription and analysis to protect participant identities.
The workshops were facilitated by experienced researchers trained in trauma-informed and culturally responsive practices. Sessions were conducted in a non-judgemental, supportive environment designed to foster trust and open dialogue. Every effort was made to minimize participant burden, including scheduling workshops at convenient times and ensuring that participation would not interfere with essential healthcare or childcare routines. Although no financial compensation was provided, participants were offered a light snack at the end of the session and could request psychological or social service referrals if needed. The research team remained available throughout the process to clarify any doubts and to ensure that participants felt safe and supported before, during, and after their involvement. The study was reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee of NOVA Medical School (Reference: 142/2019/CEFCM).

## Results

## Participant Characteristics
The sociodemographic and access-to-care data presented in and were collected through a structured questionnaire completed by pregnant women prior to the workshops. The remaining results derive from thematic analysis of transcripts from the two idea generation workshops.
The first workshop participants were all African-born pregnant women, originally from Angola, Cape Verde, and Guinea-Bissau. Their sociodemographic profile reflects key vulnerabilities: most of them had lived in Portugal for less than 5 years, had limited formal education, were unemployed or worked in informal jobs, and reported low income levels. Several reported experiences of bureaucratic difficulties or administrative delays in accessing prenatal care.
The second workshop involved 10 community workers, all of whom were women. Their professional roles included: clinical psychologist (n = 2), social work technician (n = 5) and social educator (n = 3). All community workers had been working in the association for more than a year, with 3 community workers having had worked for more than 20 years of experience with this population, and five having between 2 and 10 years of experience. All participants confirmed recognizing women with the characteristics presented in the vignettes.

## Themes from the Workshops
From the data analysis, three main themes were identified:
1.Barriers to MHL
2.Existing strengths and resources
3.Co-created solutions and intervention ideas

## Theme 1: Barriers to MHL
From the thematic analysis of the workshop discussions, three overarching categories of barriers to MHL were identified: individual, situational, and systemic (shown in ).
 

## Personal-Level Barriers

Financial Restrictions

Both community workers and pregnant women identified financial constraints as one of the challenges faced during pregnancy. One participant, for example, was compelled to utilize private healthcare because of issues securing an assigned family general practitioner and consequently experienced an amplified financial burden.
“I went back to my private doctor, explained my situation, that I couldn’t afford to pay for regular appointments like this, and she said, ‘Okay, let’s do this: I’ll do the appointments, but you’ll have to get the tests done at the health center.’ That’s the only thing that’s making it easier for me: my doctor prescribes the tests, and I go to the center to get them done without paying for the exams. But I have to pay for my appointments, and it’s not cheap.” (Pregnant woman)
The financial burden was further compounded by the difficulty in getting professional qualifications recognized in their new country, impacting their employment opportunities in their specialized fields and adding to their financial strain.
“In my country, I’m a qualified physiotherapist. I struggled a lot to enter my field, and here I can’t practice it. I worked at a gas station, which wasn’t easy at all.” (Pregnant woman)

Language Barriers

Community workers mentioned that a lack of proficiency in the host country’s language, particularly when combined with low education levels, could impede communication with healthcare professionals and understanding of information and guidelines related to healthcare access. This barrier, however, was not reported by the migrant pregnant women themselves.
“Then, from some countries, especially Guinea, some (pregnant women) who speak Creole, have a lot of difficulty speaking Portuguese in an understandable way.” (Community workers)

Low Digital Literacy

Despite many essential services, such as health, employment, and social security information, being available online, some pregnant women mentioned having difficulty accessing support and relevant information in this way.
“Technology here is so advanced [referring to digital access to social security, health, etc.], but for us, it’s still challenging... I, for example, struggle a lot with it.” (Pregnant woman)

## Contextual and Social Barriers

Lack of Knowledge about the Health System and Services

A complex set of informational barriers emerged from the accounts of both participants and community professionals, centring on a lack of understanding of the Portuguese national health system and the rights associated with pregnancy. Community workers and pregnant migrant women alike reported that many women were inadequately informed about the procedures and documentation required to access free consultations and prenatal care. This lack of knowledge often gave rise to recurring uncertainty, as one professional stated:
“We always receive many questions about this issue. How do I register at the Health Center? Can I get free consultations and exams? Do I have to pay anything? This is a common concern.” (Community workers)
In addition to procedural confusion, some women held idealized expectations based on informal family narratives, which frequently did not match the realities of the system. As another community worker explained:
“Then there is the issue of exemption from user fees. Many migrants come to Portugal believing, based on family narratives, that healthcare services are entirely free and that they won’t incur any costs. But this is often not the case.” (Community workers)
This gap between expectations and experience led to frustration and delays in care-seeking. From the women’s perspective, the lack of information was largely attributed to the unavailability of family doctors and limited prior interaction with healthcare services. One participant recalled a rare positive experience:
“It was [a general practitioner] who gave me the pregnancy booklet and exempted me from paying if I needed to go to the hospital for something...He said that I had to advocate for my rights.” (Pregnant woman)

Limited Family and Social Support

The absence of close family or support networks emerged as another key factor impacting the well-being of pregnant migrant women. Rather than being perceived as a personal deficit, this lack of support was consistently described as a structural consequence of transnational migration, unstable relationships, or limited community integration. Community professionals reported that many women experienced pregnancy in isolation, often in situations of heightened vulnerability, such as unplanned pregnancies or marital breakdown:
“We work with migrant populations who face a variety of risk factors that can affect healthy pregnancy and parenting experiences (…) many are alone with limited social and family support.” (Community workers)
Some community workers also noted that a number of women migrated to Portugal specifically during pregnancy, motivated by the perception that they would have better and safer access to maternal care. From the women’s point of view, pregnancy in isolation was often described as a source of emotional distress. As one participant expressed:
“When you go for a check-up, you see other pregnant women accompanied by their husbands. You’re the only one there alone. It’s a terrible sadness.” (Pregnant woman)
Another added:
“Single pregnant women who are single and without a partner feel much more isolated, making everything even harder.” (Pregnant woman)

## Characteristics/Responsiveness of Health Services

Cultural Barriers

Cultural differences between healthcare providers and migrant families were identified as a critical barrier to effective maternal healthcare. Community workers highlighted that in some cultural contexts, male partners frequently act as the interface between families and institutions, even when appointments are scheduled for the women. While these men are often engaged and supportive, their presence in place of the expectant mother can limit professionals’ capacity to assess maternal needs, emotional well-being, and early mother-child interactions. As one worker stated:
“We schedule the appointment with the woman, and it’s the man who comes alone and wants to be attended to. For them, they are caregivers, taking on the difficult part of family management, which is going to places, showing up. The real issue for us, who want to work on matters of maternity and with children, is that we can’t get through to them because often the women themselves don’t come.” (Community workers)
Cultural barriers were compounded by a perceived lack of cultural competence and sensitivity among healthcare professionals. Community workers reported that standard procedures, such as timely ultrasound scheduling, were not consistently followed. They associated this not only with systemic constraints (e.g., professional shortages, excessive demand) but also with variable levels of empathy and patient guidance:
“There’s a lack of sensitivity in guiding people in this regard. It could be related to the shortage of professionals and the high demand at health centers, but it also has to do with the individual sensitivity of each professional to help.” (Community workers)

Bureaucratic and Structural Barriers

Participants described significant bureaucratic obstacles when accessing primary healthcare, which frequently translated into confusion, delays, and perceived discrimination. Community workers and pregnant women reported that administrative assistants and security personnel – who serve as gatekeepers at health centres – were often unaware of the legal rights of pregnant migrants, particularly those without documentation. This created barriers at the very first point of contact with the system.
“... the first barrier here is the administrative staff and security personnel. Specifically, it happens that we [association community workers] have to accompany many families there to try to unblock situations both with the security personnel and administrative staff. And we have to present the circular from the Directorate-General of Health and explain that everyone has the right to healthcare.” (Community workers)
Beyond legal access, participants described systemic shortcomings in continuity and quality of care. The unavailability of designated family doctors led to fragmented follow-up and a lack of relational trust. As one woman explained:
“Every time I go to make an appointment, I see a new face. One day… a nurse didn’t even know my situation, even though I had been there before.” (Pregnant woman)
Scheduling barriers were also widely reported. Participants without family doctors were forced to wait in queues for hours, often from dawn, with no priority system for pregnant women.
“You have to be in line (at the health center) by 6 am, and there’s no priority, no separate queue for pregnant women. Sometimes you stay there all day.” (Pregnant woman)
Even when care was eventually accessed, women felt they were rushed through appointments and denied the opportunity to ask questions or fully understand medical guidance. One participant shared:
“... They rush through things. They don’t clarify things properly … they just say everything is fine.” (Pregnant woman)

## Theme 2: Strengths and Support Networks
Despite the challenges discussed previously, both pregnant migrant women and community workers identified a range of internal and external resources that can be leveraged – and strengthened – through targeted MHL interventions ( ).

## Resources Related to Migrants

Personal Resilience

Community workers consistently highlighted the remarkable emotional and psychological resilience displayed by women from PALOP countries, particularly in the context of navigating pregnancy and motherhood in unfamiliar and often unsupported environments. This resilience was understood as an internal coping resource, forged in response to structural marginalization and the absence of strong community networks. One professional explained:
“They undergo significant changes here … without much strategy to solve some issues that arise unexpectedly. Perhaps even more in the African community. For instance, compared to the Asian community who migrate as couples, and thus tend to have some family support here. African women may have several family members or friends in Portugal, but they often lack an effective support network. They end up here largely on their own and their progress is largely self-driven. I would say [their support] is not so much from the community. It’s an internal resource, but it is very important.” (Community workers)

Proactive Information Seeking

Many women demonstrated high levels of initiative and critical engagement with available information. In the absence of formal guidance, they reported seeking advice from peers, using informal networks, and navigating complex bureaucracies to understand their entitlements and care options. One participant stated:
“I don’t have a social worker… I strike up a conversation (with other pregnant women) ... ‘How long? ... So, what’s the document?’ because Social Security never explains anything to you. For example, I lost my pregnancy allowance because I didn’t know. I was asking, and someone told me ‘look, go to your doctor for certification because you’ve been pregnant for more than 3 months’, otherwise I wouldn’t have known.” (Pregnant woman)

## Community-Based Resources
Associations and NGOs emerged as key sites of support, extending far beyond administrative assistance. They offer a culturally attuned environment where women can access emotional, psychological, and educational resources. Participants valued these spaces as arenas of collective learning and solidarity, especially through prenatal education programmes designed for migrant women.
“I sought help ... So I found this association. I needed a psychologist, … and now I’m here to get this support.” (Pregnant woman)
Community workers also noted that these associations fostered durable peer connections that outlived the formal interventions. As one put it:
“… the fact that we meet with them, and they have that space [Association] which they utilize and take ownership of, and end up establishing relationships among themselves, which they carry forward. They create other relationships outside of the institution that enable them to establish support networks among themselves. It has happened in situations where their kids go to the same school. And when the babies are born, one drops them off, the other picks them up. They end up creating these relationships, because they have these resources that also allow them to create and be more open to change.” (Community workers)

## Resources Related to Healthcare Professionals
While access to care was often described as inconsistent, some women recalled individual healthcare professionals – especially sonographers, nurses, and general practitioners – who offered communicative, respectful, and empowering support. These encounters stood out as moments of reassurance and trust-building. One woman explained:
“He was very communicative, kept talking to me. During the morphological ultrasound, he kept communicating even though the baby was in a position that he couldn’t see well.” (Pregnant woman)
Participants also highlighted differences between care in Portugal and in their countries of origin, noting that anticipatory guidance and birth planning discussions were empowering.
“Here, midwives or doctors talk before (the childbirth) ... In my country, it’s like... First, they perform the delivery and then they explain to you.” (Pregnant woman)

## Systemic and Policy-Based Resources
Certain policy mechanisms within the Portuguese health system were perceived as facilitators, particularly when effectively activated. The PB4 agreement (a bilateral accord that entitles Brazilian citizens to access the Portuguese National Health Service under the same conditions as nationals) was described as a structural advantage that simplified health centre registration, illustrating how bilateral agreements can materially improve access. However, these supports remain unevenly distributed.
“... registration at the Health Center alone is already a barrier. If we talk about Brazil, it’s very easy because this population comes with the PB4 (refers to a certificate that allows Brazilian citizens to access healthcare services in Portugal under the same conditions as Portuguese citizens), and therefore, this registration is practically guaranteed.” (Community workers)
Additionally, the social prescribing programme – though not widely implemented – was praised by professionals as a model with great potential. This initiative connects patients with non-clinical services that address the social determinants of health, such as isolation or unemployment. One professional explained:
“A response that exists in our country but is not yet democratized nationwide is Social Prescribing... In fact, there is no awareness among people about Social Prescribing. And I think it’s very important. Because there are no vaccines for loneliness, nor are there electrical outlets for unemployment.” (Community workers)

## Theme 3: Co-Created Interventions
During the two idea generation workshops, pregnant migrant women and community workers collaboratively developed a set of 18 potential responses to the challenges discussed in the vignettes. These ideas were organized into seven thematic strategies, addressing needs at multiple levels – from individual knowledge to systemic advocacy (shown in ). These co-designed strategies reflect both the everyday experiences and aspirations of participants for a healthcare system that is more accessible, inclusive, and responsive to migrant realities.

## Community Support Networks
Creating structured peer support groups was seen as a valuable response to the isolation and lack of socio-familial support often experienced by pregnant migrant women. These groups were described as spaces where women could share experiences, exchange practical knowledge, and develop mutual trust. As one community worker explained:
“[In] group sessions, they end up creating a support network [whitch] can be good for combating some previously raised problems [being alone with little socio-familial support].” (Community workers)
These types of initiatives were also perceived to have a positive impact on emotional well-being and confidence throughout the pregnancy and parenting journey.

## Personalized Maternal Health Education
Participants expressed a strong need for personalized health education that takes into account their cultural background and individual knowledge gaps. Community workers noted that many women arrived in Portugal with limited understanding of maternal or sexual health.
“Many women also arrive [at the institution] without training in maternal health or sexuality and family planning.” (Community workers)
Suggestions included sessions on gestational conditions, postpartum recovery, and healthy eating. As one woman stated:
Additionally, migrant pregnant women themselves have proposed ideas for informational sessions and workshops that cover maternal health issues they might encounter, such as gestational diabetes, maternal nutrition, and postpartum physical exercise.
“One of the ideas that came up was having some informational sessions on these diseases that occur during pregnancy, such as diabetes, and even workshops on healthy eating.” (Pregnant woman)
These proposals highlight the importance of creating opportunities for learning that are both practical and adapted to real-life concerns.

## Mental Health Support
The emotional dimension of pregnancy and postpartum was a common concern. Women spoke about anxiety, sadness, and fear of the postpartum period, particularly when lacking family support. The proposed interventions included group sessions focused on emotional well-being and accessible psychological support within the community.
“We already have all this anxiety, worry... some periods of sadness, but postpartum as we know can also be particularly challenging for those without a support network. And for you, would it be useful to be able to talk to someone about these worries you have (the participants agree).” (Pregnant woman)
These ideas emphasize the importance of addressing mental health as part of comprehensive maternal care.

## Digital Literacy Promotion
Several women identified digital illiteracy as a barrier to accessing essential services. The proposal of digital literacy sessions aimed to build basic skills for using online platforms, especially those related to healthcare and social security.
“Do you know how to access the social security direct website and request everything online, because you don’t have to go to the social security office? [several participants say they don’t] So, would it also be useful for you (to learn about this?) ... [they agreed].” (Migrant woman)

## Knowledge of Rights and Bureaucratic Navigation
Participants suggested that better access to clear, practical information about rights and bureaucratic procedures could significantly ease their experience with healthcare and social services. These ideas included workshops, leaflets, and individualized support.
“It would also be useful to have a session on the rights you have during pregnancy, as well as postpartum? [Participants agree] For example, I don’t know if you’ve had any informative session on family benefits, how to apply, access to childcare...”
Community workers also highlighted the need for tools that empower women to advocate for themselves:
“I would really appreciate if some organization could create a kind of pamphlet for arriving migrants. That is, ‘I’ve arrived in Portugal, how to register at a health center, what to do next.’ Not just for us technicians to have, but also to distribute to them in our service. That person with that pamphlet can come in and say, ‘no, it’s here. I have a right to this; I want to take this step!’” (Community workers)

## Training for Healthcare Professionals in Migrant-Centred Care
Participants described the need for regular training of healthcare professionals on how to engage effectively and respectfully with migrant women. Suggestions included training on legal rights, intercultural communication, and cultural sensitivity. As one community worker put it:
“I think a great strategy would indeed be this ongoing and updated training for the personnel at these services. Not just at the information level, but also in assertiveness, in communication. I think very little is being done in this sense.” (Community workers)
These proposals aim to ensure that the responsibility for adapting care practices does not fall solely on the user but is shared with the system.

## Advocacy for Equitable Maternal Healthcare
Finally, both groups emphasized the need for collaborative advocacy to ensure that maternal health policies are implemented as intended. Some women expressed frustration that existing rights were not respected in practice and proposed joint initiatives between associations, communities, and healthcare providers.
“The truth is that Portugal has stipulated that any migrant who has been in Portugal for more than three months can receive care and that pregnant women have the right to monitoring, but this is not being achieved. I believe that this is a right that can be exercised, and I have seen it work in other institutions (...) there should be advocacy campaigns with the goal of promoting health, in our case, promoting maternal health both in terms of access and quality, to enforce what is stipulated.” (Community workers)

## Discussion
The main objective of this study was to identify, through a co-design process, the barriers, resources, and interventions related to MHL among African migrant women in Lisbon, Portugal. The study aimed to develop interventions based on the Optimizing Health Literacy and Access (Ophelia) methodology with the goal of improving health literacy and, consequently, maternal health outcomes for this population experiencing vulnerabilities.
The Ophelia methodology was applied with the aim of ensuring a participatory co-design approach, actively involving pregnant migrant women and community support professionals as partners in the development of interventions. Additionally, the application of this methodology allowed for a comprehensive analysis of the interactions between individual, community, and systemic factors that influence maternal health among migrant women. This process also revealed tensions between legal rights and lived experience, particularly regarding access to services, information asymmetry, and health system responsiveness.
The World Health Organization (WHO) emphasizes the importance of actively involving patients in their healthcare and empowering them to improve the services they use [ – ]. In this way, the participatory approach not only increases the relevance of the interventions developed but also promotes their sustainability and effectiveness in the long term.
This study revealed significant barriers that these pregnant migrant women face, with these challenges often being amplified by the insufficiently sensitive response of healthcare services that fail to accommodate their needs. Although some barriers – such as language and cultural gaps – are well documented, this study also highlighted critical and often overlooked issues, such as the contradiction between legal guarantees and inconsistent implementation in practice.
The results of our study confirm the existing literature, which identifies linguistic and cultural barriers as significant obstacles to adequate healthcare access for migrant women [ ]. Studies conducted in various geographical contexts have consistently shown that such barriers limit these women’s ability to access essential health information and services [ , ], a situation further exacerbated by their lack of familiarity with the healthcare system in the host country.
The lack of family support and financial constraints also emerged as prominent barriers, exacerbating vulnerability during pregnancy and childbirth. A WHO report on migrant health in Europe highlighted that social isolation and the absence of support networks are critical factors that can compromise pregnancy and hinder access to adequate care during this period [ ]. Studies also indicate that the absence of social support is linked to adverse health outcomes, such as postpartum depression, low birth weight, and preterm birth [ , – ].
To address these issues, the participants in our study identified the facilitation of support groups for pregnant and migrant mothers as a potentially effective strategy. This is supported by authors who have observed that access to groups, interactions with other women, and the sharing of experiences reduced feelings of loneliness and isolation [ – ].
One of the most compelling insights was the emergence of these peer groups as informal yet powerful sites of health education and emotional solidarity – particularly valuable in the absence of institutional responsiveness. A recent study on the effectiveness of peer support interventions for immigrant mothers in Sweden [ ] suggests that implementing mentorship programs that connect immigrant mothers and pregnant women with local mentors or other mothers who have already gone through the experience can provide guidance, practical day-to-day assistance, and emotional support. These programs encourage social interaction and civic engagement while offering inspiration through shared life stories [ ].
Aligned with the results of the workshops we conducted, several studies highlight the frequent difficulties migrants face in accessing primary healthcare [ ] and the long waiting times to obtain an appointment [ , ]. The systematic review by Higginbottom et al. [ ] emphasizes the importance of facilitating early access to prenatal care for migrant women, as delays in accessing care can lead to adverse health outcomes [ ]. Our participants frequently contrasted policy provisions with everyday barriers, highlighting a persistent gap between rights and effective access – a theme that may require stronger monitoring and accountability at the policy implementation level.
The participants in our study reported that many migrant women lack comprehensive knowledge about the services available for pregnant women in Portugal, how to access consultations and exams free of charge, and are also unaware of their rights and how to address bureaucratic issues related to immigration status. It is known that this lack of knowledge can hinder timely and adequate access to healthcare [ , , ]. Receiving detailed information about healthcare services and their rights is, according to various studies, beneficial for migrants [ ]. In line with this, participants in our study suggested the facilitation of community workshops that provide information about rights during and after pregnancy, as well as offering spaces for personalized guidance. These workshops would provide practical support in obtaining and understanding relevant documents for benefits, support, and the immigration process.
The development of informational materials written in multiple languages explaining rights, benefits, and procedures related to healthcare access was another intervention proposed by the participants in our study. Such materials were perceived as essential for enabling women to advocate for themselves within unfamiliar systems. However, some authors suggest that women with low education levels use pamphlets less frequently compared to those with medium and high education levels [ ]. Additionally, an overreliance on printed materials may discourage migrants from actively seeking further information about healthcare services [ ]. Ledoux and colleagues [ ] also emphasize that digital resources, such as websites and apps, should be developed to provide information about pregnancy and childbirth, as printed pamphlets do not meet the needs of the current generation of pregnant women [ ]. At the same time, our findings reveal an important tension: while multilingual printed materials remain useful, they may not reach women with limited literacy or unfamiliarity with bureaucratic formats. This underscores the need to complement written resources with more interactive, supported formats – such as face-to-face guidance, audio-visual tools, or peer mediation – in order to ensure true accessibility and understanding.
Additionally, a growing number of studies indicate that digital health technologies have the potential to improve women’s health outcomes [ ]. Digital sources seem to be more effective as complementary tools, offering additional information outside the traditional healthcare system due to their easy accessibility and unlimited availability [ ]. For this reason, several health organizations, including the WHO and the International Organization for Migration (IOM), emphasize the importance of ensuring that all individuals, especially pregnant migrants, have access to digital platforms [ ].
However, despite the frequent use of health apps and online resources by pregnant women to obtain information about pregnancy, care and health tips, medical and social assistance [ – ], some participants in our study reported difficulties navigating digital platforms and using online systems to access support or healthcare services. This paradox – where digital tools are widely promoted but remain inaccessible to many vulnerable users – raises critical questions about digital equity, particularly in maternal healthcare. Studies show that migrants often face limited access to technological devices and the Internet due to financial constraints, making it difficult to acquire quality digital devices or reliable Internet connections [ ].
Furthermore, educational differences, language barriers, and limited prior exposure to digital technologies can contribute to lower digital health literacy among populations regarded as vulnerable [ – ]. Courtois et al. [ ] emphasize the role of social networks in mitigating these gaps, noting that individuals with stronger networks benefit from informal digital learning and support.
However, as we have previously mentioned, migrants tend to have smaller and less supportive social networks, which can hinder, among other things, the development of digital skills. This makes it more challenging for pregnant migrants to find, understand, and use health information from electronic sources effectively and critically [ ], thereby limiting their ability to make informed decisions about their well-being and that of their baby [ – ]. Baum et al. [ ] concluded that vulnerable individuals, due to a lack of access or inability to use digital technologies, often reinforce and amplify existing disadvantages in accessing information, a phenomenon known as the “digital vicious cycle” [ ]. Improving digital literacy is, therefore, not only a matter of skill-building but a key component in promoting maternal health equity [ , ]. Several authors emphasize the importance of addressing digital health literacy as a key determinant of health [ , ], particularly for vulnerable populations like migrants, because it significantly influences broader social determinants of health, such as education, socioeconomic status, and access to healthcare services. In other words, digital health literacy can impact various areas that together determine individuals’ overall health and well-being [ , ].
The participants suggested that community structures organize digital literacy training sessions specifically for pregnant immigrants, covering topics such as Internet navigation, using health apps, and online communication. They also recommended establishing partnerships with community centres to offer regular digital literacy sessions, providing a friendly and supportive learning environment. At the community level, it could be helpful to facilitate access to computers and the Internet in public spaces, such as health centres, libraries, or community support associations, so that pregnant migrants can practice their digital skills. These potential actions could directly address the barriers identified by participants and, importantly, serve as a foundation for broader equity-driven strategies. These suggestions highlight that equitable digital access must be promoted not only through individual training but also through sustained systemic- and community-level interventions [ ].
Despite the barriers that emerged in the workshops we conducted, several strengths were identified. The individual resilience of the women and their proactivity in seeking information were considered valuable resources by the participants. Studies indicate that resilience plays a crucial role in maintaining and improving mental health, leading to positive health outcomes [ ]. These individual resources are essential for navigating healthcare systems, especially when those systems are complex or when significant linguistic and cultural barriers exist [ ]. Gagnon et al. [ ] suggest that resilience among migrant mothers is shaped by their interaction with their environment, meaning that unfavourable conditions can make resilient mothers vulnerable, while supportive environments can improve health outcomes and social inclusion for vulnerable mothers [ ]. Nonetheless, it is important to avoid romanticizing resilience: many participants described self-reliance as a necessity born out of insufficient support, rather than a preference. This distinction has implications for how interventions should be framed – prioritizing system-level responsibility over individual adaptation. In this context, participants in our study highlighted the crucial role that local community associations and non-governmental organizations play in providing these women with practical, emotional, and informational support, often compensating for deficiencies in the healthcare system.
The study by Riggs et al. [ ] concluded that community group care for pregnant women, when culturally adapted and developed in collaboration with communities, is highly beneficial as it improves access to essential care and information, promotes an environment where women feel respected and culturally understood, and better prepares them for childbirth and the postpartum period [ ]. This finding resonates with our participants’ emphasis on the need for partnerships between NGOs and health providers – not only to support pregnant migrant women but to ensure continuity of culturally competent care beyond the prenatal phase. The participants in our study emphasized the importance of establishing partnerships with non-governmental organizations and community groups to promote specific maternal health initiatives targeted at pregnant migrants. These partnerships allow for the delivery of personalized education tailored to the cultural and individual needs of the women, addressing topics such as sexual education, family planning, maternal nutrition, and specialized care during pregnancy and postpartum. Moreover, by addressing specific health issues like gestational diabetes and physical exercises, these educational sessions equip women with the knowledge needed to make informed decisions about their health and well-being [ ]. It is worth noting that these community-led efforts also respond to institutional gaps, particularly in areas such as mental health, where many migrant women experience limited access through conventional healthcare pathways.
Our study highlighted the crucial role of community associations in improving health outcomes for migrant women. These associations provide essential services, particularly in supporting mental health, which is often inaccessible in the conventional healthcare system. Among the initiatives promoted are group sessions to raise awareness about anxiety and postpartum depression, as well as individual psychological support to help women cope with the stress of pregnancy and motherhood.
Participants suggested social prescribing as an effective resource to integrate prenatal care for pregnant migrants, serving as a bridge between community structures and primary healthcare services. Social prescribing is a practice that links individuals to non-medical community resources and support, addressing social determinants of health such as loneliness, housing, and financial stressors, which impact overall well-being [ ]. This person-centred approach would allow for addressing not only medical needs but also the social, economic, emotional, and cultural factors impacting this population [ ]. By considering the specific circumstances of pregnant migrant women, social prescribing can play a crucial role in promoting health literacy, offering personalized guidance, and connecting them to resources tailored to their unique situations [ ].
Moreover, it is essential to promote advocacy for equitable maternal healthcare through educational campaigns on rights and procedures for accessing health services. Partnerships between community organizations, migrant communities, and healthcare providers are crucial for developing joint solutions that address the specific needs of pregnant migrant women.
Finally, ongoing training for healthcare professionals in empathetic communication, cultural competence, and the rights of pregnant migrants [ ] is essential. This can lead to a better understanding and respect for cultural differences, fostering awareness and comprehension of diverse cultural norms, including gender roles, dietary practices, and health-related beliefs. By doing so, it helps avoid inadvertently creating an environment where pregnant migrants feel uncomfortable or reluctant to seek medical care, thereby improving healthcare delivery and creating a more welcoming and supportive environment for migrant women during pregnancy.

## Strengths and Limitations
The main objective of this project was to contribute to a deeper understanding of the barriers, resources, and interventions related to MHL among African migrant women in Portugal. This study applied the Ophelia methodology to directly involve pregnant migrant women and healthcare professionals in a co-design process, ensuring that the proposed interventions were culturally adapted and relevant to the specific needs of this population experiencing vulnerabilities. By focusing on the experiences of African migrant women, a population often overlooked in maternal health research, the study provided valuable insights into the specific barriers and needs these women face.
A strength of the study was the identification not only of the barriers encountered, such as linguistic, financial, and cultural challenges but also of important resources, such as personal resilience and the support of community organizations. The application of a co-creation approach, the Ophelia methodology, allowed not only for the identification of these challenges and resources but also for the development of contextualized and culturally sensitive interventions, increasing the likelihood of their adoption and success.
Although the study included a relatively small number of participants, which may limit the generalization of the results, the findings provide important insights into the experience of pregnant migrants in a Southern European country. The selection of participants through a community association may introduce bias, as the women involved might have access to more resources or be more motivated to participate than those outside these support networks. However, this choice also allowed the capture of relevant experiences from a population directly engaged with community services. While the research was conducted exclusively in Lisbon, it still offers valuable guidelines for local policy formulation and future investigations in other regions. Despite potential social desirability biases, the methodology employed aimed to minimize these effects, providing a solid foundation for the conclusions.
The findings of this study have significant implications for clinical practice, policy formulation, and future research. They underscore the critical need for inclusive health policies that remove barriers to access and ensure quality maternal healthcare for all women, regardless of their migratory status. Additionally, they highlight the value of health literacy interventions designed to address the complex challenges faced by migrant women.
Future research should consider studies with larger and more diverse samples, as well as longitudinal studies to understand the evolving needs of these women from pregnancy through postpartum. Testing the effectiveness of the proposed interventions and exploring the impact of digital literacy are other promising areas for investigation.

## Conclusion
The insights from this study emphasize the importance of improving health equity for migrant pregnant women, pointing to the urgent need for integrated strategies that address both social determinants and systemic barriers that perpetuate health disparities. Promoting MHL is a crucial step in ensuring that all women, regardless of their background, can experience a safe and healthy pregnancy and childbirth. The objective we sought to achieve is not an end in itself but serves as an encouragement for future investigations to gain a better and more comprehensive understanding of the barriers, resources, and interventions related to MHL among African migrant women. Thus, this study not only contributes to the existing literature but also offers a roadmap for future actions that can transform maternal health for migrant populations, promoting a more just and equitable society.
