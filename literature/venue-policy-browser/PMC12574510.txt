Through exploring the recommendations and applications of the STAR evaluation system for guidelines, The STAR working group have identified the following issues: (1) new methods for guideline evaluation are being continuously developed and integrated into the STAR evaluation process, and their long-term effects require further observation; (2) certain items of the STAR tool are not applicable to guidelines and consensus statements in specific fields; (3) the current number of evaluators is insufficient to meet the future demand for larger-scale evaluations of guidelines and consensus statements; and (4) the existing influence of STAR is inadequate.
In response to the above issues, we conducted qualitative interviews to explore the perspectives and needs of STAR expert committee members regarding the development and management of the comprehensive evaluation system, and to collect feedback and suggestions on the STAR tool. The ultimate aim of this study was to further improve the STAR tool and promote the application of the comprehensive evaluation system.

## Methods
We conducted semi-structured interviews with STAR working group members between March 2023 and August 2023. Purposeful sampling was used to select the interviewees. The study is reported in accordance with the consolidated criteria for reporting qualitative research (COREQ).[ ]

## Ethics approval
This study has been approved by the Ethics Committee (No. HDS-202312-01) of the Institute of Health Data Science, Lanzhou University.

## Participants
In January 2023, the STAR specialist committees included 35 chairpersons, 108 vice-chairpersons, and 329 members from 24 provincial-level administrative regions of China. All members were involved in the evaluation of Chinese guidelines and consensus statements using STAR. This study conducted a purposeful sampling of the members of the specialist committees. To collect opinions from members at different positions within the specialist committees, we attempted to select a nearly equal number of chairpersons, vice-chairpersons, and regular members, hence the selection of interviewees was not done randomly. To maximize the representativity of the interviewees of all STAR specialist committee members, we aimed to recruit at least one participant from each committee, and include among the respondents from all positions (chairpersons, vice-chairpersons, and regular members). We first contacted chairpersons of 15 specialist committees. After at least 12 chairpersons had confirmed their participation, we continued by recruiting at least 12 vice-chairpersons, and finally supplemented regular members. When at least 37 members with representatives from all 37 specialist committees had confirmed their participation, the recruitment was stopped.
We sent invitation letters to the selected members by email or WeChat messenger application, explaining the purpose, process, and precautions of the study. If the respondent agreed to participate in the interview, the research team fixed the date and time of the interview, and asked the interviewee to sign an informed consent form.
The interviewees were required to fulfill all of the following criteria: (1) be members of the STAR special committee with at least one year of experience from participation in STAR evaluations; (2) agree to sign the informed consent; (3) have participated in the development of guidelines or consensus statements within the last five years; and (4) communicate fluently in Chinese. The invited experts were excluded if they did not reply within three days after sending the invitation. If the invited experts were excluded for these reasons, the researchers would invite other experts from the same committee. If it was still not possible for anyone to participate, the invitation to that committee would be abandoned.

## Interview questions
We developed a semi-structured interview framework through the following steps. Firstly, we formulated a preliminary interview outline based on the research questions, the expertise of the research team, and a review of relevant literatures. This outline encompassed the key topics and specific issues to be addressed during the interviews.
The next step involved conducting test interviews within the research group to ensure that the questions were clearly articulated and easily comprehensible. These test interviews were also utilized to evaluate the efficacy of the interview process in terms of addressing the topics and content discussed, as well as determining the average time spent on each question. Finally, based on insights gained from the pilot interviews, adjustments were made to refine and finalize the interview outline.
The interview outline consisted of the following questions about STAR: (1) What was your original motivation to join STAR? (2) What do you think is the main value of STAR? (3) What do you think are the advantages and disadvantages of the STAR tool compared to other guideline evaluation tools? (4) What are your suggestions for the organizational structure of the STAR working group (which currently consists of an executive committee, four professional committees, and 37 specialist committees)? (5) Do you have any suggestions for the evaluation and ranking of Chinese guidelines and consensus statements? (6) In your opinion, how could member of specialist committees be more actively involved in the evaluations and the work of STAR in general? (7) Do you have any suggestions for the evaluation of international guidelines and consensus statements and for setting up an international STAR committee in the future? (8) What do you think should be done to promote the sustainable development of STAR (including publicity and promotion)? (9) What do you think are the main difficulties and challenges that the development of STAR will face in the future? and (10) What are your impressions and expectations from participating in the STAR working group?

## Data collection
The interviews were conducted either through video calls or in person. Each interview was expected to have a duration of approximately 30–60 min and would be carried out exclusively in Mandarin. One investigator who possessed expertise in qualitative research conducted the interview and engaged in discussion with the interviewee, while another investigator recorded the interview. Participants were informed that their interviews would be meticulously audio or video recorded, and assured that all data, except the personal characteristics of the respondents, would remain strictly confidential. During the course of these interviews, respondents retained the right to decline answering any questions they deemed unfitting. Furthermore, interviewers possessed flexibility to tailor their inquiries based on the specific dynamics of each individual interview; this could involve adjusting the order of questions according to situational requirements or merging certain queries together. Interviewees were encouraged to express their opinions using their own words and the interviewers were advised against providing leading prompts. Additionally, if an interviewee brought up valuable information unrelated to a particular question posed by the interviewer, the interviewer was required to attentively listen and appropriately follow up on such points of interest. In accordance with principles governing data saturation, both interviews and data collection ceased once no new topics emerged during subsequent discussions.

## Data analysis
One investigator transcribed the interview content verbatim and subsequently provided it to the interviewees for verification of its correctness. Following the completion of an interview session, participants received copies of their respective records within one to three days; they were also offered the opportunity to modify or supplement any content. Finally, the researchers translated the Chinese discourse into English and solicited revisions from native English authors and members of the STAR working group.
Two investigators utilized NVivo V.12 (QSR International I; Burlington, MA, USA) software to import and process data, as well as independently interpret and analyze the findings through thematic analysis. First, initial coding was conducted by thoroughly reading the transcript. Second, the content was classified and summarized to identify key themes. Third, the investigator carefully examined any generated topics and reprocessed any information that could not be attributed to a specific theme. Finally, each identified theme was meticulously analyzed and organized into a coherent narrative that maintains internal consistency. Upon completion of these steps, the results from both investigators were collated for review by the entire research team who collectively reached a consensus before finalizing the interview report.

## Results

## Characteristics of the respondents
We contacted a total of 40 experts, 3 of whom did not accept the invitation. Additionally, one expert initially declined the interview due to scheduling conflicts but later requested to participate in the study. A total of 37 respondents agreed to be interviewed. Among them, ten were interviewed by teleconference and 27 face-to-face. The interviewees represented 36 different specialist committees [Supplementary Tables 1 and 2, http://links.lww.com/CM9/C217].

## Investigation on awareness and knowledge of guideline development
The investigation showed that 22 (59.4%) interviewees deemed the number of guidelines published annually in their field to be below the demand, while five (13.5%) interviewees considered it to exceed the demand. Twenty (54.1%) interviewees considered the overall quality of the guidelines to be very low or somewhat poor.
Almost all (n = 36, 97.3%) interviewees held that professional associations or societies should take the lead in developing guidelines, while 25 (67.6%) and 19 (51.4%) interviewees highlighted the important roles of clinical or medical research centers and government agencies or health departments, respectively. One interviewee (2.7%) considered that medical enterprises with a good professional foundation could lead guideline development.
All 37 interviewees believed that clinicians, evidence-based medicine experts, and guideline methodologists were considered indispensable core contributors for guideline development. In addition, the majority of the interviewees also recognized the need of health economists (n = 31, 83.8%), epidemiologists (n = 29, 78.4%), patients (n = 27, 73.0%), and policymakers (n = 25, 67.6%). Journal editors, nurses, and health insurance representatives were also mentioned each by one interviewee.
Regarding the question of what are the three most important ones of the guideline development stages, 22 interviewees (59.5%) mentioned researching and selecting clinical questions, 22 interviewees (59.5%) mentioned forming evidence-to-decision tables, and 13 interviewees (35.1%) mentioned establishing a guideline working group, searching and selecting research evidence and evaluating research evidence. The remaining proposed stages were mentioned by less than one third of the participants, with writing the guideline being mentioned by only one (2.7%) interviewee.
The interviewees were asked what in their opinion is an acceptable cost for the development of a guideline. Among them, 14 (37.8%) interviewees perceived that the cost of developing one guideline was between 200,000 and 500,000 Chinese yuan (CNY), 11 interviewees (29.7%) deemed that between 100,000 and 200,000 CNY. One interviewee (2.7%) considered the cost exceeds 1 million CNY, which was the least commonly mentioned category.
Twenty (54.1%) interviewees found that the applicability of international or foreign guidelines to the clinical environment in China was moderate, 11 (29.7%) interviewees deemed that international guidelines were relatively well applicable, and 6 interviewees (16.2%) perceived that the applicability of international guidelines was somewhat poor. No interviewees believed that international guidelines were completely applicable or completely inapplicable.
Regarding the conflicts of interest that need to be reported, holding company shares or stocks was the most frequently mentioned type of conflict of interest (n = 34, 91.9%), followed by holding personal beliefs related to the guideline topic (n = 29, 78.4%), holding positions in units or institutions (n = 27, 73.0%), and providing writing or consulting services for companies (n = 27, 73.0%). One interviewee (2.7%) believed that elevated social status needs to be reported as a conflict of interest.
Regarding the types of literature that should be included in addition to guidelines in the STAR evaluation, 34 interviewees (81.1%) mentioned consensus statements, followed by professional standards (n = 25, 67.6%) and professional norms (n = 22, 59.5%). Only one (2.7%) interviewee considered that expert opinions should also be included. The detailed results were shown in Supplementary Table 3, http://links.lww.com/CM9/C217.

## Key themes identified in the interview
After analyzing the results of the interview, we summarized the contents into four main themes: (1) the value and significance of STAR; (2) the policy for the evaluating procedures using STAR; (3) the improvement of the STAR tool; and (4) the communication and promotion of STAR. The next section contained the key opinions and suggestions collected during the interviews, grouped into the abovementioned four topics. ## The value and significance of STAR

## STAR is a good tool for guideline evaluation
STAR had several advantages when compared with other guideline evaluation tools. The benefits of STAR were particularly obvious in the comprehensiveness of the items, and in the reduction of time needed for the evaluation.

## STAR can become a reference principle for the development of high-quality guidelines
According to the interviewees, guideline developers could gain a more complete understanding of how to develop high-quality guidelines by referring to the STAR standard. They also considered that the availability of a rigorous reference principle like STAR can reduce the emergence of low-quality guidelines.

## Guideline developers should be encouraged to provide transparent information about the development
The evaluation using STAR requires guideline authors to describe the completion of each stage of the development process. The evaluators may therefore need to ask the authors to provide additional materials that support the description. However, the interviewees found that many authors developed guidelines without adequate methodological knowledge, there was often a lack of transparency and rigorous following of the development principles. This led to some difficulties and limitations for the STAR working group to obtain sufficient supporting materials.

## The ranking mechanism facilitates the selection of high-quality guidelines
The interviewees mentioned that the STAR tool, unlike any of the existing international evaluation tools, provides an overall score that can be used to rank the guidelines. The ranking can visually demonstrate the quality of the guidelines and help clinicians quickly identify high-quality guidelines.

## Operational policy for evaluations using STAR

## The structure of the special committees needs improvement
The geographical representativeness of the different provinces and municipalities of China among the current STAR special committee members is currently broad and the organizational structure of the STAR network was found to be reasonable. The implementation of guidelines at primary health care facilities should be considered in the evaluations, and it was recommended to set up a committee for primary healthcare providers.

## The incentive system should be improved
The evaluation incentive system should be improved. For example, some academic honors could be awarded to evaluators to enhance their enthusiasm to participate in the work.

## The collaboration patterns in the evaluation should be optimized
The committees should arrange more compact and concentrated internal discussions when organizing the evaluation work to increase the efficiency of determining the final results of the evaluation. When the guideline is assigned to a special committee for evaluation, consideration should also be given to whether the guideline has any interdisciplinary conditions.

## Intelligent systems can help the evaluation process
The development of an intelligent evaluation platform significantly enhances the evaluation process. For example, it is essential to let the computer assign two evaluators randomly to each guideline to complete the evaluation tasks. After the two independent evaluators have finished their evaluations, the computer system should also automatically calculate any inconsistencies in their judgments and require the evaluators to engage in an online discussion to reach a consensus on the final evaluating results. Additionally, respondents have suggested that STAR should collaborate closely with artificial intelligence (AI) technology experts to explore the potential of leveraging AI to enhance the application of the STAR tool.

## External cooperation and resource support should be sought
The development of STAR in the long term needs more external cooperation and resource support. It would be crucial to obtain the recognition and support of a national administrative authority (such as the China National Health Commission) and the management authority of Chinese medical journals.

## Improvement of the STAR tool

## Development of extensions of the evaluation tool
It was suggested to develop extended versions of STAR for different medical specialties to better accommodate their specific needs.

## Optimization of the existing items
The interviewees suggested that the items should be streamlined to be as concise and simple as possible. The authenticity of the content reported in the guidelines should be considered in the evaluation criteria for the items.

## Items for subjective evaluation should be added
The current STAR tool cannot evaluate the applicability of the specific content of the guideline recommendations deeply. The existing items should be improved in the future, and besides retaining the methodological items, subjective evaluation for clinical practice effects should also be added.

## Focus should be placed on the applicability of the guidelines
The interviewees suggested that in addition to the quality of the methodology, the implementability of the guidelines should also be evaluated. The ultimate objective of the STAR evaluation, which is to improve the quality of guidelines and the health of the patients, should be recognized.

## Dissemination and promotion of STAR

## Media publicity
It is crucial to promote STAR through mainstream domestic media to expand its audience. This strategy will enable more clinical practitioners and researchers in the country to learn about and participate in STAR’s initiatives. Additionally, it is important to remind the representatives of STAR to adhere to relevant medical regulations during its promotional activities.

## Academic publicity
All interviewees agreed that promoting STAR through academic conferences is an excellent way to showcase the latest advances. The interviewees pointed out that it was necessary to conduct regular training courses on guideline methodology as well as training on the use of STAR.

## Establishment of an international committee to evaluate guidelines worldwide with STAR
The interviewees suggested that an international STAR Committee should be established in the future. As a guideline quality evaluation tool developed by Chinese scholars, STAR needs to be tested by international peers for its scientificity, transparency, and applicability. Researchers can explore the difference between guidelines from China and elsewhere in general, then based on these differences evaluate how well the foreign guidelines are applicable to the situation in China.

## Discussion
This study systematically summarized the key thematic suggestions from qualitative interviews of 37 members of the STAR specialist regarding the development of the comprehensive STAR evaluation system.

## Improving the quality of Chinese guidelines through STAR
Although the quality of guidelines has improved over time, the overall quality still needs to be enhanced.[ ] This investigation showed that more than half of the interviewees deemed the overall quality of the guidelines published in the field to be very low or somewhat poor. Previous evaluation studies have shown that the quality of Chinese guidelines lags behind the international average.[ , , ] The STAR scores of Chinese guidelines in the second year of STAR evaluations was more than 30% higher than in the first year, which may be due to several factors.[ ] First, in addition to conducting guideline evaluations, the STAR evaluation system introduced the concepts of ranking and grading, which increased the attention of guideline developers and journal editors to guideline quality, prompting them to take measures to improve the guideline development process.[ ] As an example, the evaluation of WHO guidelines led to the establishment of the WHO Guidelines Review Committee which subsequently improved the quality of WHO guidelines.[ – ] Second, during the comprehensive evaluation of guidelines, the developers were invited to provide supporting materials through various channels, encouraging them to strictly follow the guideline development process, document the process, and keep the full materials well organized. Third, increasing attention was being paid to guideline methodology. Our investigation on awareness and knowledge showed that the interviewees found that evidence-based medicine and guideline methodology experts should participate in guideline development. The committees’ members gained some knowledge of guideline methodology through STAR training and evaluation and applied it in the guideline development process. Finally, starting from the second year, the specialist committees independently evaluated the guidelines from their own field of specialty, which may have introduced bias resulting in higher scores compared to the first year’s centralized evaluation organized by the STAR core team. Low ranking and scores received by guidelines may also negatively influence the acceptance of STAR by their developers. The handling of the evaluation and ranking results still requires improvement.
The interviewees suggested that the STAR specialist committees should seek to become the official review body for Chinese guidelines, analogically to the WHO and NICE guideline review committees. Institutions such as WHO and NICE, as guideline developers themselves, have internal review bodies that can directly review the guideline development process to ensure guideline quality.[ , ] However, regarding the establishment of a review mechanism for Chinese guidelines, the interviewees considered that associations or societies, clinical and medical research centers, government agencies and health departments, hospitals and medical institutions, medical schools, and journals and publishing institutions can all initiate and develop guidelines. The STAR working group needs to reach agreements with different developer organizations, which may face significant obstacles.

## Enhancing the efficiency of the application of STAR
The number of Chinese guidelines and consensus documents has rapidly increased, with more than 1300 published annually in the past three years.[ , ] However, the survey in this study showed that 60% of the interviewees still considered that the number of guidelines in their fields was insufficient. Additionally, previous evaluation methods, which relied on expert reviews and various tools, might not meet all needs.[ , , ] The STAR evaluation system aims to attract more clinicians and researchers to participate in the evaluation process by providing methodology-based training and professional courses on the evaluation of guidelines. Moreover, all evaluators were required to undergo training and pass certification exams, to further ensure the rigorousness and efficiency of the evaluations of guidelines.
On the other hand, the efficiency of research including quality evaluations can be improved through automated systems and opportunities provided by AI. For example, for quality assessment of original research, the Cochrane group has provided the Review Manager software, which includes a module for the assessment of the risk of bias. AI is also being applied in various steps of guideline development, including the evaluation of the supporting evidence, which has significantly improved the efficiency of the development.[ ] Based on the interviewees’ suggestions, researchers should strive to develop an automated comprehensive evaluation system to facilitate collaboration among the evaluators. The system is expected to incorporate AI technology to assist in extracting and evaluating materials, automatically review the materials, and generate the results of the evaluation and ranking, thereby reducing the necessary human workload.
The management and operation of the STAR evaluation system require substantial resources. While the comprehensive evaluation management body seeks public cooperation and funding support from government agencies, journals, hospitals, and other institutions, it also needs to establish a performance and reward mechanism for evaluators to enhance their motivation to perform the evaluations efficiently and rigorously.

## Improving the comprehensive STAR evaluation system
Given the complex environment of clinical practice in China, most interviewees believed that international guidelines are only slightly or moderately applicable to domestic practice. Therefore, there is a need to develop high-quality guidelines suitable for Chinese clinical practice,[ ] as well as evaluation tools tailored to Chinese guidelines, such as AGREE-China.[ ] As a new tool, STAR has undergone reliability, validity and usability testing,[ ] and has accumulated substantial experience through its application in large-scale evaluations of Chinese guidelines and consensus documents. The interviewees in this study also believe that the STAR evaluation system could further be extended to evaluate professional standards, professional norms, and clinical pathways in the future. To ensure consistency between the evaluations, STAR has not developed different versions of the tool for consensus documents, professional standards, professional norms, or other types of documents. These guidance documents differ from standard guidelines,[ ] which makes some STAR items inapplicable. Furthermore, STAR applies uniform evaluation rules across all specialties, and some interviewees found that STAR does not align well with their specialties. As a reference, AGREE II and RIGHT have developed extended versions for different specialty fields, such as AGREE-S (for surgery)[ ] and RIGHT-TCM (for traditional Chinese medicine).[ ] In addition to the regular guideline evaluation tool, researchers should therefore consider to develop extended to suit the specific needs of different document types and clinical specialties and gain broader recognition.
The high quality of a guideline do not necessarily guarantee its adequate implementation. Even after guidelines are implemented, they may not be well-suited to the context and may fail to achieve the expected goals.[ ] The evaluation of the effectiveness of guideline implementation is considered to be still missing from most guideline evaluation tools. Regarding the issue of guideline implementation, on the one hand, STAR should introduce new items outside its current applicability domain to encourage guidelines to provide effective and feasible implementation strategies. On the other hand, the STAR working group should also track the implementation of guidelines after their publication. As guideline users, clinicians’ evaluations of the clinical content of guidelines can also be part of the assessment of guideline implementation. Patients, as the target population of guidelines, are not yet sufficiently involved in guideline development, implementation, and evaluation.[ ] Patient versions of guidelines, which are developed for patients and contain recommendations that are easy for patients to understand, can facilitate the implementation of guidelines. However, the development of patient guidelines in China is still in its infancy, with their number, as well as the proportion of guidelines that contain a patient version, remaining low.[ ] Guideline researchers should encourage guideline development groups to include patient representatives and promote patient involvement in the development of clinical or patient guidelines. STAR should also introduce items about the involvement of patients and develop evaluation tools for patient versions of guidelines to ensure that high-quality guidelines are effectively implemented, ultimately benefiting patients.

## Emphasizing the construction and promotion of STAR
The comprehensive evaluation system has established an official website and a public WeChat account to release the latest information about STAR and collect user feedback interactively. In addition to publishing guideline consensus evaluation reports through journals, the results of the comprehensive STAR evaluation are also promoted through academic conferences and guideline methodology training courses. Regarding the international promotion of STAR, some interviewees were cautious to recommend the immediate internationalization of STAR due to concerns about dispersing research efforts. However, interviewees also recognize the potential value of incorporating international guideline evaluations and establishing an international committee. The use of social media can optimize the use of promotional resources and avoid limitations in promotional channels. Existing research also shows the importance of the new formats of social media in guideline promotion.[ , ]
This study had some limitations. First, due to the practical reasons and to ensure the broad geographical representativity of the interviewees, some experts were interviewed online, and the format of the interview may have had some influence on the results. Second, the interviewees were all members of the STAR working groups which may lead to some level of bias in their perspectives. However, the interviewees also offered some critical feedback and suggestions to improve STAR, which indicates an acceptable level of objectiveness. Finally, the interviewees were primarily guideline developers or evaluators, and future research could consider collecting also the perspectives of other stakeholders such as primary healthcare providers, representatives guideline development organizations, or health policy decision-makers.
The results of this study indicate that according to clinicians, the development and application of STAR align with the need for establishing a comprehensive guideline evaluation system and can effectively improve the quality of Chinese guidelines. Based on the suggestions collected, the comprehensive evaluation system should focus on the following aspects in the future: (1) improving the quality of Chinese guidelines; (2) optimizing the utilization of resources and personnel support to comprehensively enhance the efficiency of the evaluation by STAR; (3) considering the differences across various fields and focus on the applicability of guidelines; and (4) enhancing STAR’s influence through new media promotion and international dissemination.
