## Introduction
Greater than 65 percent of the United States (US) population is overweight or obese, with 32 percent obese ( ). Obesity is progressing in the US and around the world, increasing worldwide costs of health care and dramatically increasing associated morbidities, including cardiovascular disease, diabetes, and malignancies ( , ). It is a problem in both developed and developing nations ( , ). Obesity is a modifiable cardiovascular disease risk factor ( ). Although obesity can and should be considered a public health problem, it should also be treated as a medical disorder by physicians in the clinical setting ( , ). The National Heart Lung and Blood Institute (NHLBI) in the US has created physician practice guidelines for management of obesity ( , ). Likewise, in Europe the European Charter on Counteracting Obesity urges the medical care system to address obesity ( ). Counseling by physicians about obesity and weight loss is inconsistent, and physician approaches to obesity management have limited success ( , ).
Unfortunately, the creation of knowledge does not automatically translate into practice. It can take 17 years for knowledge to ultimately become the standard of care ( ). The “quality” movement in health care has been promoted at the health systems level and by national consensus ( ). Additionally, there have been calls from well respected academic sources for the academic community to increase involvement in translating proven research into practice ( ). This has led to a serious effort to take an evidence based approach to documenting the effectiveness of guidelines in clinical practice. It has also led to a serious effort to analyze the dissemination and implementation of guidelines of care to improve quality ( ). Recognizing that guidelines have not been utilized fully, NHLBI created 12 Enhanced Dissemination Utilization Centers (EDUC) in 2001 and 2002. One mission of the EDUC program was to use NHLBI’s “science-based information to inform communities of the public health burden of cardiovascular disease and to develop and implement focused educational strategies to reduce this burden through changes in health care provider practice, patient behavior, and general public behavior” ( ). This team was selected by NHLBI to serve as one of these EDUC program sites. While most translational research studies report process outcomes, these EDUC studies measured clinical outcomes in addition to process outcomes ( ).
Physicians often believe they are more successful at managing the care of their patients than is actually the case, leading to dramatic under-treatment ( ). Altering physician practice behavior to improve outcomes is very challenging and is an international problem ( , , ). Educational formats such as grand rounds or typical continuing medical education programs have very little effect on physician behavior in changing outcomes ( ). Physician clinical practice behavior can be modified via a series of approaches, including providing them with their outcomes, academic detailing, and through the use of financial incentives ( ). Academic detailing is a successful tool for improving clinician outcomes. A peer, often with clinical skills and perceived as being academically credible, joins a small group of clinicians in a collegial and professional environment, reviews their clinical performance and advises them on mechanisms to improve their outcomes ( ). Physician practice behavior can be changed through changing payment formulas and by making systems changes in care delivery ( , ). This process can provide greater efficiency in care, reduce costs, and improve outcomes – without necessarily influencing physician income ( ).
Globally, physician knowledge of obesity management is limited ( ). In France, for example, only 42 percent of physicians feel well-prepared to manage obesity and only 51 percent find it professionally rewarding ( ). Australian physicians have similar beliefs ( ). Only 50 percent of US physicians feel confident in their ability to counsel patients regarding diet ( ). Only 40 percent of obese patients report being counseled by their physicians to lose weight ( ). There is little understanding regarding how physicians actually manage obesity when they do diagnose it. Although weight loss has been successful in specialized clinics, with five to seven percent weight loss at six months, in the general environment of primary care, it is not done well ( , ).
NIH has made a significant effort to develop a “strategic plan” to perform research on obesity and has created obesity guidelines for practitioners ( ). Although the guidelines have been carefully developed with strong evidence-based references, there has been very little documentation to date of efforts to effectively implement the guidelines themselves and to measure the effectiveness of that implementation process ( , ). The guidelines and many interventions have been both lauded and criticized ( , ). Although physician recommendations to loose weight appear to have a positive effect for patients, they are rarely made ( ). This study extended the translational research efforts to improve physician awareness and improve outcomes of overweight/obesity. The hypothesis was that by using focused academic detailing of the NHLBI guidelines, showing the physicians their own patient outcome data, and introducing minor systems innovations in the primary care office setting, physicians would improve their knowledge of obesity management techniques and enhance patient awareness of obesity as a cardiovascular risk factor.

## Methods

## Target Population
The general population in Dayton, Ohio who were obese or overweight was the population studied. This pilot involved physicians in the Premier HealthNet primary care group (100+ physicians).
Study Protocol: There were two groups of physician offices. Since there was no true control group, we used the terms “Intervention” group and “Enhanced Intervention” group to distinguish between the degrees of intervention in our study. One group of three offices (10 physicians) served as “Enhanced Intervention” offices, where physician obesity education occurred through the process of academic detailing and patient outcomes were measured from 306 (of whom 289 weighted < 311 lbs) random patient medical records ( ). The primary physician was asked to place a sticker in the body of the chart, demonstrating a diagnosis of obesity/overweight and recommending treatment and/or referral. The second group of two offices (11 physicians) served as “Intervention” offices, where physician obesity education occurred and patient outcomes were measured from 335 (of whom 313 weighted < 311 lbs) random patient medical records. Post intervention measurements occurred 12 months after the intervention. Those results were presented to the participating physicians and their office managers at a post follow-up academic detailing visit.

## Process Outcomes
The measurement of “quality” in clinical practice is controversial; it is important to measure quality, but the means and interpretation are difficult ( ). Process outcomes measure the “throughput” process. Although easier to measure than clinical outcomes, they do not prove effectiveness in the clinical situation, but rather, they measure the steps that are occurring that it is believed need to be improved for a successful clinical outcome. In this study, the process outcomes measured were:•Increased physician knowledge of obesity as a CVD risk factor
•Documentation of physician obesity management

At baseline and follow-up, a short physician survey was administered to assess physician knowledge of obesity management and the level of comfort physicians had in managing obesity. Additionally, the medical record was reviewed to determine if and how obesity was managed.

## Clinical Outcomes
Clinical outcomes are those measurable patient based results that occur in the clinical setting, usually extracted from the medical record. In this study, the following were measured:•Cardiovascular disease risk factors – lipid levels, blood pressure, blood glucose
•Obesity management – BMI, weight, record height to allow BMI calculation

## Statistical Methods
Analyses were performed using SPSS Version 14.0 statistical software. The data was categorized into baseline and follow-up and intervention and enhanced intervention. T-Tests for comparison of means were used to test for significant differences in clinical outcomes between groups, and chi-square analyses were used to test for significant differences in categorical data and process outcomes. In some analyses, extreme outliers were identified and excluded from the calculations.

## Results

## Process Outcomes
We surveyed the physicians at the beginning and end of the study to see how “comfortable” they were discussing obesity with their patients. At baseline, 53 percent of physicians were not comfortable discussing obesity with their patients. Research has demonstrated that physician awareness was the first challenge that needed to be overcome with tobacco cessation and death and dying in previous times ( , ). We believe obesity management is analogous. Academic detailing provided a venue to increase physician comfort with the management of obesity. At 12 months, 100 percent of physicians responded in the repeat survey they were “comfortable” with obesity management, representing a 47 percent improvement (p = 0.041). (See .) Likewise, we administered a brief tool to measure physician knowledge of appropriate management of obesity based on the NHLBI guidelines. We compared the knowledge of the 13 physicians (among the 21 total) who took the same survey at the beginning and the end of the study period. At baseline, the average score of correct answers from a nine question survey was 67 percent correct. At 12 months follow-up, the average score improved to 75 percent. 
A total of 641 charts were reviewed at baseline and another 631 charts were reviewed twelve months later at follow-up. A key goal was to demonstrate that physicians were making a diagnosis of obesity and documenting it. At baseline, only 2.4 percent (n = 6) of medical records sampled from Intervention physicians included reference to obesity management. At 12 months, 9.2 percent (n = 25) of records sampled had obesity management documentation (p = 0.001). The Enhanced Intervention physicians did better; at baseline, 3.9 percent (n = 4) of medical records sampled included reference to obesity management. At 12 months, 15.6 percent (n = 42) had obesity management documentation (p = 0.002). The difference between the Intervention and Enhanced Intervention physicians’ performance at follow-up (9.2 percent versus 15.6 percent) was significant (p = 0.024).

## Clinical Outcomes
This sample of patients was taken from all adult patients who had been seen by their doctor for any reason in the past year. The patient demographics were similar at baseline and follow-up in these independent samples. The patients were primarily middle-aged, white and represented both genders. (See .) 
At baseline, 33 percent of medical records had the height recorded (n = 212). The physicians were informed at baseline that few of their patients had heights recorded in the charts and that this made the calculation of BMI impossible. At the 12 month follow-up, 40 percent of medical records had heights recorded. This seven percent increase in the recording of heights was significant (p = 0.018) and allowed BMI’s to be calculated. The prevalence of overweight and obese adult patients was high in this population. Of the 641 records studied, only 212 had heights recorded that allowed BMI to be calculated. In that group, only 23 percent (n = 49) of the patients had a normal weight. Thirty-two percent (n = 68) were overweight and 45 percent (n = 95) were obese. The overweight and obese patients had many cardiovascular co-morbidities. (See .) 
The 10 physicians in the Enhanced Intervention group were asked to place stickers within the medical record of each patient who was diagnosed with obesity or overweight. They were also asked to note on the sticker that they had informed the patient of the diagnosis and to document the therapeutic intervention. Those patients in the Enhanced Intervention group lost an average of 6.19 lbs (3.3 percent) (p = 0.083) during the one year period versus 4.6 lbs (2.5 percent) (p = 0.20) in the Intervention group. Neither group achieved statistical significance, although the Enhanced Intervention group had a positive trend. The BMI in the Intervention group dropped 1.2, while the BMI in the Enhanced Intervention group dropped 0.72. Neither was statistically significant. The HDL cholesterol rose, and diastolic blood pressure decreased. (See .) 
Although the study sampled many patients, they were not paired samples. The variation in clinical data was quite broad making statistical analysis difficult for the sample size. The data from both groups was pooled at baseline and pooled at follow-up. Both groups were considered “Intervention” groups in that academic detailing occurred in both groups, with clinical outcomes presented to the physicians in both. The only difference in the two groups was the requested use of chart stickers in the Enhanced Intervention group. The larger numbers in the pooled data allowed some statistical significance to be demonstrated and trends to be more apparent. Over the study period, the average weight of the randomly selected group of patients in these primary care practices decreased from 185.7 lbs to 180.3 lbs (excluding outliers weighing > 311 lbs). This 5.4 pound loss was significant (p = 0.027). The BMI decreased from 30.1 to 29.1 (p = 0.095). The average adult patient seen by these physicians in their practices would be considered obese at the onset and overweight at the end of the study if the positive trend achieved statistical significance. Likewise, there were associated improvements in some of the other components of the metabolic syndrome. (See .) 

## Discussion
The Physician Obesity Awareness Project confirmed that physician awareness of obesity and its management can be increased and that increased awareness can lead to changes in physician practice, which can actually produce positive clinical outcomes. The use of stickers in the medical record was of possible value, but the absence of statistical significance does not encourage the use of stickers as a sole intervention. The more powerful intervention was academic detailing (reviewing guidelines and stating expectations) plus the provision of outcome results and feedback. These interventions occurred in both groups of physicians. Patients from these physician practices lost 5.4 lbs. (p = 0.027). The average BMI decreased by 1.0 (p = 0.10). Likewise, there were modest (and statistically significant) improvements in lipids (HDL) and blood pressure (diastolic).
Obesity and overweight have a very high prevalence, especially among patients presenting to a physician’s office. Although the prevalence of overweight plus obese in the general adult population of the US is 65 percent (of whom 32 percent are obese), this population of patients – those going to a doctor – had an even higher prevalence with 67 percent being overweight or obese and 45 percent obese. Being overweight or obese is associated with multiple co-morbidities that constitute the key elements of the metabolic syndrome. In this group of people, of those overweight or obese, 13 percent had diabetes, 36 percent were hypertensive, and 17 percent had cardiovascular disease. It is critical for practicing physicians to associate all of these co-morbidities and to manage them simultaneously, although the management of obesity may well lead to improvements in the other co-morbidities as we have demonstrated.
Obesity is difficult to treat ( ). Solutions to the obesity problem must be focused at a population-based level ( ). Yet with such an extraordinary prevalence in physicians’ practices, it is critical for physicians to diagnose and treat obesity effectively. It appears that physicians are not comfortable dealing with obesity, may be unaware of the consequences of obesity, and are unprepared to treat it. In the early periods of the promotion of tobacco cessation and getting physicians to address death and dying, the first challenge to be overcome was physician awareness ( , ). Obesity management may be analogous. It is important to educate physicians to discuss obesity with their patients, recommend weight loss, and begin a weight loss program. Academic detailing provided the venue to increase physician comfort with the management of obesity. Small changes can be made in the microsystem of a physician’s office practice that can improve the likelihood of more intense management of obesity. For example, measuring heights allows BMI to be calculated. Calculating BMI allows the diagnosis of obesity to be made. Diagnosis of obesity allows patient education to occur and then finally obesity management to occur.
The process of weight loss appears to be associated with improved outcomes in other cardiovascular risk factors ( ). This is a small study in translational research. Proven approaches were used in a true community primary care setting, and the outcomes, therefore, are highly valuable for other community settings. Practice guidelines have been proven to be effective, yet implementing them is extremely difficult ( , ). The whole NHLBI Obesity Guideline is 262 pages, while the “Practical Guide” is 94 pages ( , ). It is very impractical for clinically active physicians to remember and implement detailed and complex guidelines during the ongoing daily care of their patients in an ambulatory setting. Either microsystem aides such electronic medical records with decision support or other tools such as academic detailing need to be used to effectively introduce such complex processes and algorithms.
Obesity is a population-based problem; and the best solutions are not at the level of individual physicians treating individual patients, but rather through public education, public policy, and the inducement of cultural changes ( , , ). There is clear evidence, for example, that when a smoker sees a physician and is advised to stop smoking, there is a substantial likelihood that they will stop smoking. The probability of stopping can increase from a baseline of seven percent to 15 percent within one year of a physician’s recommendation to stop smoking ( ). The results of this study suggest a similar process may be occurring. The physicians in this study increased their comfort in dealing with obesity from 47 percent to 100 percent. With the extraordinary prevalence of overweight and obesity in a primary care physician’s panel of patients, it seems to be an appropriate place and a worthwhile use of the physician’s time and effort to put obesity management into the ongoing processes of care. This particular translational program had no clearly associated cost (other than the time for academic detailing and abstracting the outcomes data from the medical record). It was not associated with either financial rewards or punishments to the physicians, yet the outcomes were promising. The results are not as clear as we would like. As in many translational research studies, a number of simultaneous interventions were done ( ). It is virtually impossible to determine which interventions were the most effective – academic detailing (reviewing guidelines, setting expectations) or reporting outcomes at baseline and follow-up. A longer, larger, more complete study is critical to confirm these preliminary results and to understand physician awareness of obesity to test tools to achieve success in clinical outcomes.

## Conclusions
A translational research program was done to improve physician awareness of obesity as a medical problem requiring intervention. Targeting physicians in a primary care setting to improve obesity management can reduce obesity and improve the associated cardiovascular risk factors of the metabolic syndrome. A translational research project involving academic detailing and repeated clinical outcomes reporting was able to demonstrate increased comfort by physicians in managing obesity, and that awareness, in turn, may have resulted in an average weight loss of five pounds per patient in a general population of patients seeing primary care physicians. The BMI was reduced by one. Other cardiovascular risk factors were improved as well. A more extensive study is needed to confirm these findings.
