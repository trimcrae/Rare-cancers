## ABSTRACT

## Aim
To test the reliability and construct validity of the Mask Usability Scale in healthcare students and staff.

## Design
A methodological study involving repeated measures.

## Methods
The study included two batches of participants: (1) 283 university nursing students and (2) 1753 participants composed of students (61%) and clinical staff (39%). All participants underwent N95 respirator fit tests and user seal checks. They also responded to the Mask Usability Scale, which comprises 11 items evaluated using Likert scales. The internal consistency was assessed using Cronbach's alpha and item–total correlation test. Test–retest reliability was evaluated by the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC). The factor structure was initially identified through exploratory factor analysis (EFA), laying the groundwork for the model. This approach was followed by confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) to ensure the model fits with the standardised solution.

## Results
Excluding items 9, 10 and 11, the study showed satisfactory internal consistency, evidenced by a Cronbach's alpha of 0.842 for the eight‐item scale from the combined samples. Factors, such as ‘Heat’, ‘Breathability’, ‘Tightness’ and ‘Ease in talking’ showed moderate to strong correlations. The test–retest reliability in the batch one sample was acceptable with ICCs ranging between 0.69 and 0.71 for different models. The EFA and fit indices supported a two‐factor structure. The first factor ‘Comfort and Usage’ included ‘Heat’, ‘Breathability’, ‘Tightness’, ‘Ease in talking’ and ‘Prolonged use’, which were keys for the usability of N95 respirators. The second factor ‘Suitability’ encompassed ‘Itchy’, ‘Easily displaced’ and ‘Ear soreness’. The variance explained by the first and second factors was 49% and 12%, respectively, with a strong inter‐factor correlation. The CFA results were satisfactory with fit metrics (NFI = 0.967, IFI = 0.969, TLI = 0.952, CFI = 0.969, RMSEA = 0.078 with 90% CI [0.069, 0.086] and p < 0.001) and a chi‐squared to df ratio of 13.58.

## Conclusion
The eight‐item mask usability scale exhibited satisfactory internal consistency and construct validity in both healthcare student and staff samples.

## Implications for the Profession and/or Patient Care
The primary concern in the purchase policy of N95 respirators for clinical use is to strike the right balance between usability and fit rate (passing % of fit testing). Our study solidified the concept of quantifying the usability of respirators. With that mask usability scale measurement, a provision of benchmark and reference for the design and selection of respirators would be possible. This validated scale has significant implications for senior management in clinical settings and respirator manufacturers regarding the N95 respirator purchase policy and design.

## Impact
The results indicated the psychometric properties of mask usability scale in measuring the usability of N95 respirators. This scale is particularly valuable for assessing the perceptions of healthcare students and staff of factors, namely, ‘Heat’, ‘Breathability’, ‘Tightness’, ‘Ease in talking’, ‘Prolonged use’, ‘Itchy’, ‘Easily displaced’ and ‘Ear soreness’ concerning the use of N95 respirators.

## Training Session
Participants underwent training on accurate respirator donning and user seal check techniques, following protocols validated by prior research (Lam et al. ; Lam et al. ). This training was essential to standardise the participants' experience and familiarity with respirator usage, ensuring that their responses to the MUS accurately reflected their usability experiences. In session one, the participants watched a demonstration video on accurate donning procedures, followed by an in‐person demonstration by a research nurse. The participants also needed to learn how to perform user seal check. They were also provided with time and opportunities to practice the skills. These trainings were recommended by previous studies (Lam et al.  ; Wyer et al.  ).

5.3

## Testing Session
The testing phase required the participants to demonstrate their donning skills, assessed by a research nurse, to verify accurate respirator usage. Following the successful demonstration, participants performed user seal checks and fit tests, and then completed the MUS. This procedure was repeated with up to three different respirator models, enriching the dataset with diverse usability experiences across various respirator designs. If the participant fails the assessment of re‐demonstration, he/she is required to restart from session one (i.e., training session).

5.4

## Data Analysis
IBM SPSS 29 and AMOS 26 were utilised for data analysis. The Cronbach's alpha and item–total correlation coefficients of the MUS were calculated to assess internal consistency and within‐scale correlation. A Cronbach's alpha above 0.7 indicated satisfactory consistency. Item–total correlation coefficients of items between 0.3 and 0.7 were deemed satisfactory (Portney ; Streiner, Norman, and Cairney  ). Test–retest reliability was evaluated using the ICC of single measures, adopting a two‐way mixed model for estimating agreement. An ICC exceeding 0.75 was considered satisfactory (Koo and Li  ; Liljequist, Elfving, and Skavberg Roaldsen  ).
There were 283 participants in the student sample (batch one). In a separate dataset, which includes both students and staff (batch two), there were 1753 participants. Since each participant tested at least one mask, the total number of instances (cases) analysed would be more than the double of 2036 (283+1753). Specifically, the student sample contributed 566 cases, while the mixed sample contributed 5259 cases. In total, half of the cases were randomly allocated to either EFA (n = 2912) or CFA (n = 2913). However, due to missing and incomplete data, the number of cases analysed in EFA was reduced to 2066, and in CFA to 2067.
In the EFA dataset (randomly selected half of total sample, n = 2036/2 = 1018, contributing to 2066 valid MUS responses), males account for 23.5% of the samples, while females account for 76.5% of them. The mean height is 163.8 cm (SD = 7.5 cm, ranging from 150.0 to 188.0 cm), and the mean weight is 57.0 kg (SD = 10.7 kg, ranging from 40.5 to 105.0 kg). The EFA included the first nine items of the MUS. Intentionally excluding the last one item in the scale is vital for conducting a coherent factor analysis because item 10 ‘This mask causes mist on my glasses/eye shield (if applicable) was not mandatory for all respondents, which left a high percentage of missing data’.
The Kaiser–Myer–Olkin (KMO) index and Bartlett's test of sphericity assessed the factorability of the correlation matrix. A KMO index above 0.6 and a significant Bartlett's test result indicated suitability of factor analysis (Tabachnick and Fidell  ). The significance level was set at 0.05. A scree plot of eigenvalues by the principal factors was generated. The optimal number of factors was determined using the number of factors with eigenvalues larger than one (Kaiser criterion).
For factor analysis, principal axis factoring, which is one of the most common methods (de Winter and Dodou  ), and oblimin rotation were used for EFA on a randomly selected half of the sample (n = 2036/2 = 1018, contributing to 2066 valid MUS responses), allowing for correlation between latent factors belonging to the same instrument (Brown  ). A factor loading larger than 0.2 is retained and considered important from the latent factor (Floyd and Widaman  ), and items were assigned to the factor with the largest loading in absolute value. Missing data were addressed using listwise deletion; the rate was below 3% and Little's MCAR test indicated randomness.
The trimmed model derived from the EFA was further evaluated using CFA on the residual data. In the CFA dataset, males represent 22.0% of the samples, and females make up 78.0% of them. The mean height is 162.7 cm (SD = 8.2 cm, ranging from 116.5 to 187.0 cm), and the mean weight is 56.3 kg (SD = 9.7 kg, ranging from 39.4 to 93.5 kg). Maximum likelihood estimation was employed to generate unstandardised and standardised solutions, utilising conventional standard errors for the analysis. The cut‐off of comparative fit index (CFI) and Tucker–Lewis index (TLI) were set at > 0.9; the cut‐off of root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA) and standardised root mean square residual (RMR) was < 0.08, indicating satisfactory model fit (Byrne  ; Chen and Wang  ; Hair et al.  ).

## Results
7.1

## Demographic Characteristics
The study comprises 283 participants (batch one) from the student sample recruited at the University and 1753 participants (batch two) in the sample mix of healthcare students and staff. The response rate for batch one was 94%, 17 healthcare students refused to participate in the study because they were engaged with study activities immediately after the fit testing. With that experience in batch one data collection, the response rate of batch two was 100% because the participants of healthcare staff and students were informed and reserved sufficient time for the completion of the study.
Among the student sample, more than three‐fourths were females, and half of the sample wore spectacles (Table  ). The students in the sample pursued various nursing programmes, with 68.20% enrolled in Bachelor of Science (Honours) in Nursing, 22.26% specialising in Mental Health Nursing, and 9.19% undertaking Master of Nursing. Their mean height and weight were 163 cm and 56.6 kg, respectively. For batch two, the sample mix comprised over 61% students and approximately 39% staff (Table  ), encompassing physicians, nurses, allied health workers and healthcare assistants. Approximately, two‐thirds of them were females. On average, the height and weight were 164 cm and 57.8 kg, respectively.
 Of those 2036 participants, a total of valid and complete dataset of MUS was 4133 responses to the usability of five differently designed respirators.

7.2

## Reliability
The internal consistency and item–total correlations were analysed for the combined sample from all data sources (Table  ). While the internal consistency (Cronbach's alpha) of the eight‐item scale showed a strong level of reliability (Items 1–8, α = 0.842), the subscales exhibited varying degrees of consistency (Items 1–4 and 8, α = 0.834; Items 5–7, α = 0.677). ‘Prolonged use’ was noted for its weaker coefficient in item–total correlations, whereas items such as ‘Heat’, ‘Breathability’, ‘Tightness’ and ‘Ease in talking’ demonstrated moderate to strong correlations ( > 0.6) with the total score. For test–retest reliability, the university student sample (n = 43) showed acceptable results. The test–retest reliability of the eight‐item MUS was assessed after removing items 9 and 10, using ICC. ICC values for single measures ranged from 0.693 to 0.710, while average measures ranged from 0.819 to 0.830, all statistically significant (p < 0.001). These results demonstrate that the MUS has moderate to good stability, making it a stable and consistent tool for repeated use in research and clinical settings.

7.3

## Factor Analysis
The factorability of the correlation matrix was excellent (KMO = 0.89, Bartlett's test p < 0.001). The EFA identified two distinct factors. The first factor, explaining 49% of the variance, included ‘Heat’, ‘Breathability’, ‘Tightness’, ‘Ease in talking’ and ‘Prolonged use’, whereas the second factor (12% of the variance) comprised ‘Itchy’, ‘Easily displaced’ and ‘Ear soreness’ (Table  ). A moderate to strong inter‐factor correlation was observed (0.62). In addition, item 9 ‘This mask reduces my work efficiency’ was excluded from CFA due to its dual‐factor loadings.
 The CFA indicated a consistent pattern in the unstandardised (Figure  ) and standardised solutions with modification of covaried error terms (Figure  ) showing similar pattern of regression weights. The first factor ‘Comfort and usage’ positively influenced the first set of items, albeit a weaker association for ‘Prolonged use’. The second factor ‘Suitability’ was positively correlated with the second set of items. A strong correlation of 0.73 was observed between these two factors in the CFA. The fit metrics were satisfactory (NFI = 0.967, IFI = 0.969, TLI = 0.952, CFI = 0.969, RMSEA = 0.078 with 90% CI [0.069, 0.086] and p < 0.001) and a chi‐squared to df ratio of 13.58. The composite reliability (CR) for the factor ‘Comfort and Usage’ is 0.856, with an average variance extracted (AVE) of 0.564. For the factor ‘Suitability’, the CR is 0.668, and the AVE is 0.405.

## Discussion
8.1

## Overview of Key Results and Contributions
This investigation into the reliability and construct validity of the eight‐item MUS offers significant insights into the perceived usability of N95 respirators among healthcare students and staff. The results reveal key aspects that influence the comfort and practicality of respirator use, contributing to a better understanding of how these factors impact HCW compliance with donned N95 respirators.

8.2

## Reliability and Construct Validity
While the internal consistency of the MUS showed potential for improvement, particularly considering the diverse clinical experiences of participants, the test–retest reliability was acceptable, specifically after excluding item 10, ‘mist over glasses’. The internal consistency of the MUS in the sample could be enhanced. More than 60% of the sampled students exhibited incomparable clinical experience to staff. Over one‐fifth of the university students specialised in mental health, offering less opportunities to manage acute respiratory patients. Clinical experience might have an impact on the scale reliability of MUS. The test–retest reliability was acceptable only when item 10 ‘mist over glasses’ was excluded. This exclusion highlights the influence of specific conditions, such as wearing glasses, on the perceived usability of respirators. For the current samples, half of the students were not wearing glasses, which led to a large number of missing data on that item. Participants can be required to wear protective goggles in the fit test, rendering item 10 applicable in the future. The influence of item 10 on the scale's stability further underscores the importance of considering various real‐world factors, such as eyewear, in respirator usability. This study's context, conducted during the COVID‐19 pandemic, adds relevance to our findings, reflecting the challenges faced by HCWs in this unprecedented situation.
The construct validity was supported by the two factors identified in the factor analysis, where ‘Comfort and usage’ was more instrumental in capturing variance than ‘Suitability’. The total variance explained by the factors in the sample was over 60%. The first set of factors, comprising ‘Heat’, ‘Breathability’, ‘Tightness’, ‘Ease in talking’ and ‘Prolonged use’ explained most of the variances. This factor set was the most important in the aspect of ‘usability’. The bodily sensations could be felt instantly when the respirator is worn regardless of disciplines and clinical experiences. Among the items, heat has been the most common complaint (Baig et al.  ). Other perceived discomforts were associated with the increasing facial temperature (Scarano, Inchingolo, and Lorusso  ; Suen et al.  ), the breathing discomforts (Baig et al.  ; Choudhury et al.  ; Morris et al.  ; Suen et al.  ) and the pressure on the face (Bambi et al.  ; Suen et al.  ). The respirator could hinder speech perception and reduce its interpretability, thereby increasing difficulty in verbal communication (Bandaru et al.  ; Rebmann, Carrico, and Wang  ; Suen et al.  ; Williams et al.  ). However, the decrease in working efficiency applies to both factors. This item requires further investigation into its definition compared with those in the literature. Moreover, deeper exploration is required to identify any related items to be included in the MUS, considering that previous studies have reported issues of respirator use interfering healthcare tasks.
The second factor explained three observed variables, which include ‘Itchy’, ‘Easily displaced’ and ‘Ear soreness’. Similar sensations were reported in the literature. For instance, after a prolonged respirator use, the user might develop itchiness (Suen et al.  ). Mask displacement was concerning because it can influence the seal around the face even with sight displacement (Kim et al.  ) and undermine the protection efficacy of the respirator. Prolonged pressure and tightness of the strappings (Nwosu et al.  ; Or, Chung, and Wong  ) were the primary causes of pressure injuries especially on the face, nasal bridge, cheeks and ears (Bambi et al.  ; Coyer et al.  ; Kumar and Singh  ; Or, Chung, and Wong  ). These sensations could develop after wearing the respirator over time. Thus, the differences in clinical experience might affect the respondent's perception of respirator usability. However, the conclusion that background characteristics could affect the differences in the factor loadings is premature, unless background information collection can be standardised in the future.

8.3

## Strengths and Limitations
The strengths of the study lie in its thorough approach to evaluating the MUS and its contribution to understanding respirator usability by a sufficient large and diverse healthcare sample. However, few limitations were recognised associating with demographic characteristics and some confounding factors. These limitations include the variability of respirator models used in testing and the lack of standardised demographic variables, which may have influenced the results.
First, the samples were tested with various models/designs of respirator, perhaps contributing to the uncertainties in the statistical results. However, concerning the classical measurement theory, the reliability and validity of a scale may not be influenced by the models or types of donned respirators. Second, the list of observed demographic variables between samples was not standardised. Therefore, potential variables, which contributed to the differences in statistical results, could be difficult to recognise. In addition, the gender disparity in the samples could have impacted the findings. The proportion of females was larger than that of the males in both batches of the sample, with a higher percentage of females. Therefore, gender difference might have played a role in the disparity. Nevertheless, such disparity is prevalent in the healthcare industry in many developed regions regarding that study field (Lam et al. ; Lam et al.  ). Moreover, the height and weight parameter values of the participants were similar across samples, indicating that they had similar physiques for the fit test and the research study. Despite these limitations, the study provides valuable insights into the factors affecting respirator use in healthcare settings.

8.4

## Insights From Recent Research
A recent study by Ustundag, Akbaba, and Huyut ( ) revealed that surgical mask use did not have a significant impact on cardiopulmonary functions but moderately affected coronaphobia scores in university students. These results complement our own findings on the MUS, particularly regarding the observed moderate level of coronaphobia and associated symptoms such as headaches and facial discomfort. Additionally, the study identified factors such as ‘Heat’, ‘Breathability’, ‘Tightness’ and ‘Ease in talking’ as significantly correlated with the overall usability of N95 respirators, underscoring the psychological impact of mask usage.
The increased coronaphobia scores highlight the importance of considering both physical and psychological dimensions in evaluating mask usability, particularly in pandemic scenarios. Furthermore, the factor analysis results revealing distinct dimensions of comfort and usage as well as suitability align with the broader implications of mask use, suggesting a complex interplay between physical sensations and psychological responses. These insights emphasise the multifaceted nature of mask usability, extending beyond physical comfort to encompass psychological well‐being, especially during prolonged use in pandemic situations.

## Recommendations for Further Research
9.1

## Eye Shield
In light of the finding that the item ‘Mist over glasses’ was not applicable to all participants, future research should require participants to wear an eye shield during fit testing. This would make the item relevant to all participants, providing a more comprehensive assessment of respirator usability in relation to eyewear interference. In the current study, participants were not required to use eye shield; therefore, the ‘Mist over glasses’ was inapplicable if the participant does not use spectacles.

9.2

## Usability Cut‐off
The findings on physiological reactions, as correlates of respirator usability, suggest a need for future research to establish a usability cut‐off score. This score could be based on physiological stresses and complications associated with prolonged respirator use, such as breathlessness, hypoventilation and increased blood carbon dioxide levels. Determining a usability cut‐off aids in categorising respirators according to their comfort and safety profiles. Aside from discomforts and interference in verbal communication, prolonged respirator use can entail physiological stress and complications, such as breathlessness, hypoventilation (Roberge et al.  ; Williams  ), increased blood carbon dioxide (Lim et al.  ; Rebmann, Carrico, and Wang  ), headache and lightheadedness (Choudhury et al.  ; İpek et al.  ; Kumar and Singh  ; Ong et al.  ; Ramirez‐Moreno et al.  ; Rebmann, Carrico, and Wang  ; Rosner  ; Williams et al.  ) beginning from the first hour to possibly the 12th hour at work. The cut‐off of the 4‐hour usability score can be proposed in future studies based on the correlates of concern and the actual working pattern in clinical settings. The respirator can then be classified by the usability categories.

9.3

## Subgroup Analysis
Considering the diverse backgrounds of the staff sample, which comprises physicians, nurses and allied health professionals, future studies should conduct subgroup analyses. These analyses could reveal how different professional experiences and levels of clinical exposure to respirators impact perceptions of usability. This approach would address the uncertainties observed in the current study regarding the varying impacts on different professional groups. A source of uncertainty was that the staff mix encompasses physicians, nurses, allied health and assistants, wherein impacts on the results between groups may be unobserved. Students had less clinical experience of respirator use at work. Thus, staff and students possess varying degrees of importance in physiological reactions and experience of N95 respirator usage, which could serve as reference point when evaluating respirator usability. Subgroup analysis is warranted if sufficient sample sizes can be obtained in the future.

10

## Implications for Policy and Practice
The respirator is designed to provide protection against airborne diseases as a crucial component of PPEs (Klompas, Rhee, and Baker  ; Offeddu et al.  ). A better seal could increase the capability of filtering airborne pathogens (Kim et al.  ), but the subtle variation in facial features can compromise the fit test results (O'Kelly et al.  ; Regli, Sommerfield, and von Ungern‐Sternberg  ; Lui et al. ). Increasing tightness and pressure on face may increase the fit rate but lead to discomfort and complications, wherein the fit test and measuring perceived respirator usability can cultivate improved product design, excellent donning practice and compliance with respirator use (Rebmann, Carrico, and Wang  ).
The primary concern in the purchase policy of N95 respirators for clinical use is to strike the right balance between usability and fit rate. Our study solidified the concept of quantifying the usability of respirators. With that MUS measurement, a provision of benchmark and reference for the design and selection of respirators would be possible. The scale presented has significant implications for senior management in clinical settings and respirator manufacturers regarding the N95 respirator purchase policy and design.

11

## Conclusion
This study examined the reliability and construct validity of the eight‐item MUS with satisfactory results. Generally, a two‐factor structure was applicable, yet items such as ‘Itchy’, ‘Easily displaced’ and ‘Ear soreness’ belonged to the same factor. With the results of factor analysis, items that need further exploration were recognised, such as work efficiency which surprisingly did not distinctively load on a single factor with other items. Immediate sensations after donning respirator, such as ‘Heat’, ‘Breathability’, ‘Tightness’ and ‘Ease in talking’, were among the factors that explained the variance most. The MUS showed a good scale reliability as evidenced by satisfactory internal consistency and test–retest reliability. These findings supported the reliability and validity of MUS as a tool to measure perceived usability of N95 respirator, which can help manufacturer evaluate the performance of their prototypes and allow end‐users to select the one which fit them and felt comfortable most. Further studies on the items, sub‐populations and correlates of usability should be the future research directions.
