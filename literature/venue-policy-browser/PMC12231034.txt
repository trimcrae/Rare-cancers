## Abstract

## Introduction
The COVID-19 lockdowns and awareness of the ecological impact of our profession led us to reconsider the way we deliver scientific meetings. In this International Society of Nephrology (ISN) survey, we aimed to investigate health professionals’ preferences for in-person, virtual or hybrid nephrology meetings.

## Methods
An ad hoc questionnaire was developed within the ISN Western Europe Regional Board; a pilot survey checked for clarity and consistency (204 professionals). All members of the ISN Working Group were contacted between March and May 2023. Quantitative and qualitative data were recorded and analyzed.

## Results
A total of 499 participants from 102 countries completed the survey. Most of them agreed that a combination of in-person, remote, and hybrid meetings works best, whereas 14.1% preferred virtual meetings only, and 25.2% preferred in-person meetings only. Hybrid meetings were identified as the best option for the future, regardless of the number of attendees. Flexibility in schedules was their most important advantage, even if this modality limits networking. In-person meetings allow for discussions with international peers, although costs may be a barrier. The wiser use of resources and better management of large numbers of attendees were the main advantages of hybrid meetings (about 30% each); however, this modality potentially discriminates on the basis of financial resources and geographical area (31%).

## Conclusion
Hybrid meetings, allowing for more flexibility and better utilization of resources, were the preferred modality for scientific meetings, regardless of the number of participants. A targeted survey could further explore how to optimize meeting attendance and participation in scientific discussions.

Keywords: carbon footprint, health professionals, hybrid meeting, in-person meeting, scientific exchanges, virtual meeting

Scientific meetings play a key role in research and development by favoring exchanges, encouraging brainstorming, and providing a setting in which international collaborations and friendships can develop.
The growth of the nephrology workforce, which most of us see as a measure of the success of our discipline, has progressively transformed our national and international congresses into large gatherings with thousands of participants; however, 2 main issues are leading the nephrology community to reflect on the pros and cons, the advantages, and limitations of our meetings.
COVID-19 significantly impacted various aspects of our lives, limiting our ability to safely engage in social gatherings; the procedures adopted to prevent the spread of the virus led to organizational challenges. , Social distancing, mandatory masking, and travel restrictions were implemented worldwide, and in-person meetings transitioned to virtual meetings. Meanwhile, awareness of the impact that many aspects of medicine have on planetary health has led us to consider the potential impact our meetings have. As an ironic, well-grounded paper on the 2023 European Renal Association meeting pointed out, a scientific society that wants to “go green” should avoid the “Di Caprio paradox”, i.e., it should ensure that receiving an award for leadership in facing the climate crisis does not involve travelling 8000 miles by private jet. 
In-person meetings, which are probably the ones that do most to facilitate social networking and stimulate active discussion, involve inequalities, disfavoring medium-low income settings, as well as people working in small organizations. Although virtual meetings minimize travel costs, reduce the environmental burden, and cannot spread infectious diseases, they limit direct interactions between participants. Hybrid meetings would combine the advantages of in-person with virtual meetings, increasing flexibility in attendance according to participants’ needs; however, they are also likely to include the advantages and disadvantages of the other 2 modalities. 
Given the importance of scientific meetings, we investigated which of the 3 main types of scientific meetings (in-person, virtual, and hybrid) health professionals prefer, to inform strategies for future nephology meetings in the postpandemic and planetary health–conscious era.

## Methods

## Study Design
We conducted an international online voluntary survey in the context of the ISN, launched initially as a project of the ISN Western Europe Regional Board and then extended to the other ISN Regions in the official Society language (English) to assess health professionals’ preferences on scientific meeting modality and the reasons why they attended the meetings. We distributed a voluntary online survey in the ISN’s official language (English), to society members to assess health professionals’ preferred meeting modality and the reasons why they attend meetings. The protocol was registered at Open Science Frameworks Registries (available at https://osf.io/b75at) on October 26, 2023. Results were evaluated using a mixed-method model. Consent was obtained from all participants included in this study.

## Questionnaire
Following open discussion, 2 of the authors (GBP and PN) developed the questionnaire for this cross-sectional study, integrating it with a review of the relevant literature. , , , , Another author (GS), an expert on survey design and implementation, reviewed the items. Members from the ISN Working Group revised, checked, and provided feedback on the content validity to achieve a comprehensive and easy-to-answer questionnaire.
A pilot survey was conducted between November 2022 and February 2023, involving 204 health professionals who volunteered to check items for clarity and consistency, and their feedback was implemented in the survey.
The survey was administered online using Survey Monkey. It was available from March to May 2023. To encourage participation, reminder emails were sent and the survey included in ISN’s newsletter after the initial email with the link to the survey website. The study timeline is shown in . The questionnaire was not displayed a second time once the user had filled it in and submitted it. Personal information was collected and stored by the ISN Working Group. Adaptive questioning was not used. Checking for completeness after having received the questionnaire or reviewing steps for participants before submission was not possible.

## Prioritization of Outcomes
We used a standardized closed and open-ended questionnaire. The survey had 6 sections (35 items), including the following: (i) demographics and information about experience attending scientific meetings; (ii) preferred modality for continuing medical education at scientific meetings after the COVID-19 pandemic; (iii) rating the importance of virtual, in-person, and hybrid meetings; (iv) best-worst scale tasks; (v) agreement or disagreement with statements about virtual, in-person, and hybrid meetings, considering the type of meeting and number of speakers and attendees; and (vi) a semi open-ended question ( ). The first section reported both demographic information and information about experience attending, presenting, or organizing scientific meetings in the previous 5 years. The second section consisted of 3 items related to the preferred modality of scientific meetings using a 3-point Likert scale (1 = “never,” 2= “sometimes,” and 3 = “always"). The third section rated the importance of each scientific meeting modality using the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development, and Evaluation process with a 5-point Likert scale (from 1 = “least important” to 5 = “most important”). Scores 1 and 2 indicated “limited importance,” 3 indicated “important but not critical,” and 4 and 5 indicated “critical importance.” The fourth section consisted of 8 best-worst scale tasks each with 5 outcomes. Best-worst scale tasks are a technique used to elicit relative preferences. Participants were asked to select the most important and least important outcome from each list of 5 outcomes. The fifth section consisted of 3 items using the 6-point Likert scale (1 = “strongly agree,” 2 = “agree,” 3 = “neither agree nor disagree,” 4 = “disagree,” 5 = “strongly disagree,” and 6 = “unsure”). In the last section, a free-text box was added for participants’ comments. The full survey text is available in .

## Participant Selection and Recruitment
All professionals on the ISN lists were eligible to participate. The survey was distributed through the official channels of the ISN network, including, but not limited to, health professionals (e.g., nephrologists, nurses, and pharmacists), researchers, and policy makers. An invitation letter was sent to all ISN members to explain the aims and purposes of the survey, and instructions on how to complete it. No incentives were offered for participation. We used multiple recruitment strategies to be as inclusive as possible. Participants were health professionals, aged ≥ 18 years old, with or without experience attending, presenting, or organizing scientific meetings.

## Data Analysis
All the surveys submitted were analyzed. Quantitative data were reported using the proportion of participants for each item. Relative preference was calculated from the best-worst scale tasks by calculating a best-worst score: the number of times an outcome was chosen as the best minus the number of times it was chosen as the worst. For ease of interpretation the scores were rescaled using a range of 0 (least preferred) to 1 (most preferred). The relative preference was therefore expressed as a cardinal rather than an ordinal number. We performed subgroup analyses considering age, gender, ISN region, childbearing age (defined as women aged ≤ 50 years) and men aged ≤ 50 years or > 50 years, and setting of work (private or public [defined as working in companies, dialysis clinics, group practice, individual practice and private hospitals or government and teaching hospitals, respectively]). Qualitative data reported in the comments were imported into HyperRESEARCH and analyzed thematically, as possible.

## Results

## Participant Characteristics
In total, 499 participants from 102 countries completed the survey. Considering the > 9000 ISN members, the response rate is in line with the ones obtained in other ISN surveys. 
Among the participants, 81.0% were aged < 65 years; 42.1% were females; and 38.5% were from Asia, 16.5% from North America, 15% from Europe, 11.6% from Africa, and 19.4% from other regions ( ).
 The majority of the participants (74.0%) were nephrologists; 41.9% participants worked mainly in teaching hospitals; 78.0% had a full-time job, and 53.4% had more than 16 years of experience ( ). Although only 8.6% of respondents did not attend scientific meetings between 2018 and 2020, about two-thirds of the participants (64.4%) were a spokesperson for an invited faculty. Almost half of the respondents (46.3%) had been involved in organizing meetings in the previous 5 years. Possibly because of selection bias, more than two-thirds of the participants had not experienced a language barrier ( ).
 

## Preferred Modality for Scientific Meetings
Most of the respondents were flexible on their preferences and, among the options “never, always, sometimes,” chose the last one (sometimes) in over 50% of the cases for virtual, in-person, and hybrid meetings.
Virtual meetings were “never” preferred by 23.3% of respondents, in-person by 16.2%, and hybrid meetings by 7.8% only ( ). Hybrid meetings “only” were preferred by 38.0% of participants ( ).
 The most important advantages and disadvantages, based on the Likert scale, for choosing virtual, in-person, or hybrid meetings are reported in and to and summarized in .
 The most important advantages of virtual meetings were flexibility in schedules, affordability for people with economic constraints, and providing access to all scientific materials according to individual needs ( ). However, 160 participants (36.2%) responded that limited opportunities for networking and one-to-one exchanges were a disadvantage of virtual meetings ( ).
Almost half of the participants identified the most important advantage of in-person meetings in allowing them to meet peers or friends from different countries, visit different places, and better understand how clinical practice is carried out in other settings ( ).
Some participants reported that key concerns were cost and the lack of travel grants to encourage attendance at in-person meetings (40.4% and 27.4%, respectively, ).
As reported in , over half of the participants were aware that the impact on the environment could be a concern in attending in-person meetings and acknowledged that hybrid meetings made possible a wiser use of resources and better management of large audiences ( ).
Overall, hybrid meetings were rated as the best option for scientific meetings, regardless of number of attendees ( , ). Specifically, 37.5% recommended the hybrid format for small meetings, 63.4% for national annual meetings, and 72.7% for international meetings, such as the ISN World Congress.
 Agreement and disagreement statements on types of meetings are reported in . The majority of participants (58.1%) strongly agreed that direct interaction was easier in smaller or focused settings; and 44.2% agreed that, in a specialized field, the chance to meet experts was a valuable opportunity to learn. Almost a third of the participants stated that direct interaction was easier at national meetings because there was no language barrier, and that these meetings promoted a sense of belonging to a community and encouraged discussions with peers (44.3% and 46.6%, respectively). Of those who chose hybrid meetings, 38.1% agreed that it was easier to follow a large meeting in a hybrid format, and 44.9% believed that the lower costs of virtual meetings could increase the attendance of younger colleagues and those who came from low-income countries ( ).
 

## Relative Preferences: Best-Worst Scale
Relative preferences (best-worst scores tasks) on a scale of 0 to 1 are reported in . The most important positive aspects of in-person meetings were that they allow participants to meet friends and peers from different settings (preference score 0.96), facilitate discussion and communication (0.76), and make it possible to build new relationships (0.65). The most important negative aspects of in-person meetings were that they are expensive so that not everyone can afford to attend them (0.97), and that they limit the number of sessions that can be attended (0.50). The most important positive aspects of virtual meetings were that they give access to all meeting materials (1.0), are less expensive than in-person meetings (0.74), and allow for a more flexible schedule (0.66). However, they impair direct discussion with peers (0.80); it is more difficult to dedicate time to them (0.46); and concentration is lower (0.42).
 The most positive aspects of hybrid meetings were that they increase flexibility in attendance (0.68), allow for a wiser use of resources (0.60) and allow participants to follow all the discussions they are interested in (0.45); whereas their most negative aspects were that they decrease engagement and participation (0.62), are difficult to manage (0.58), and discriminate on the basis of financial resources and geographic area (0.46).

## Subgroup Analyses
Subgroup analyses by age, gender, ISN region, women of childbearing age, men aged ≤ 50 or > 50 years, and private or public practice did not show differences in terms of preferred modality for future scientific meetings, and hybrid meetings were confirmed as the preferred option in all subsets ( ).

## Discussion
Scientific meetings are vital to the scientific community, and probably the most important experience that allowed us to better understand their pros and cons was the lockdown during the pandemic, in which, after a period of discontinuation, remote meetings started to be organized, followed, after the end of the pandemic, by hybrid ones, and, more recently, by a tendency to request at least speakers and chairs to attend in person. The COVID-19 period indeed highlighted their advantages and alternatives, that also have to be contextualized in a moment in which the impact of the environmental costs of our specialty is an increasingly important matter of concern and reflection, as witnessed during the most recent European Renal Association meetings. , Both promoted reduction in nonnecessary expenses (printed materials), tried to reduce travel carbon footprint, and promote healthy food during the conference. 
Although the great efforts of scientific societies should be acknowledged, very few surveys, and none in nephrology, have so far been conducted for recording and analyzing the preferences of society members with respect to the 3 main modalities of meeting organization (in-person, remote, and hybrid). We therefore undertook this study, in the context of the ISN, to explore preferences and gather opinions on this issue.
Out of the over 9000 members, 499, from 102 countries responded, a response rate that, though not high, is in the usual range of ISN surveys. , 
Independently from geographical area, gender, and years in nephrology, the majority of participants chose hybrid meetings as their preferred modality (always or in selected cases) for both national and international scientific meetings, regardless of the number of speakers or attendees. Among the advantages identified, the most important ones were that they made possible a better use of resources and flexibility in attendance, while making all congress materials available; however, a concern identified by about 10% of respondents as their most relevant is that they could still discriminate on the basis of income and geographic area, an issue also cited for in-person meetings ( ).
Therefore, the hybrid meeting was seen as combining the main advantages of virtual ones (lower costs, more planet friendly, and easier access to scientific materials), offering, at least for those attending in person, opportunities for interacting with peers. The last of these was the main advantage identified for in-person meetings (encountering new people, visiting foreign countries, and sharing opinions), though mirroring the previous answers, cost was the major concern, especially in underprivileged settings ( , ). Accessibility was cited as a positive aspect of both virtual and hybrid meetings; however, attendance was felt to be potentially discriminatory in hybrid meetings. Our results are in line with similar studies, which identified hybrid meetings as the preferred congress modality, highly rating social interactions for in-person ones, and flexible schedules for virtual ones. 
One of the novelties of our survey is that it assessed responses on the questionnaire with respect to sustainability issues. Sustainability, intended both as a reduction in the carbon footprint, and as more equitable access to participation, was considered quite very important by more than half of the participants; for example, only 11% considered that this was a negligible drawback of in-person meetings, and only 1.4% of the respondents found that wiser use of resources was not an important issue of hybrid meetings, whereas only 5.7% did not consider being planet friendly an advantage of remote meetings, versus about 60% rating this as very important or most important ( ).
These opinions are in line with a series of new studies and directives that highlight the considerable impact international conferences have. , 
Interestingly however, the carbon footprint of remote meetings was never mentioned in the open-ended answers, that mainly underlined the ecological advantages of a reduction in travel. This is an issue that could be further explored, because it has been calculated that a remote meeting has a nonnegligible carbon footprint. , , In fact, in-person meetings can account for up to 3000 kg of CO2 equivalent per participant. This ecological impact mostly derives from transportations and could be easily reduced by hybrid or virtual meetings. However, considering the global organization of a conference, there are some emissions that cannot be disregarded, such as those coming from the organization phase and the greenhouse gas emissions from the video-conferencing technology. Considering a 1500 to 2000 participant congress simulation, it has been estimated that the total impact of a fully virtual congress of the same size would be 0.780 tons CO2 equivalent compared to the 1250 to 2575 tons CO2 equivalent of the in-person event, whereas hybrid meetings stand in the middle depending on the number of attendees who decide to go fully virtual and the type of transportation of those reaching the congress venue physically. A more conservative estimate calculates the reduction of emissions for virtual congresses versus in-person ones to about 94%. 
Our study’s principal merit is that it was conducted at a global level (102 countries) using sound methodology, thereby overcoming the shortcomings of local surveys, and giving wide room to the expression of opinions on sustainability and equality.
However, it also has obvious limitations because this was a questionnaire for internal use only, specifically designed to evaluate ISN members’ preferences on preferred modality for scientific meetings. Thus, it is not generalizable for other settings or topics. As often happens in such surveys, there is likely to be a selection bias, because most of the respondents had had experience as invited spokespersons (64.4%) and only 46% had not been involved in the organization in at least 1 meeting (whatever its size). This bias, however, may also be a strength of the study, because most of the respondents had direct experience of participation, organization, and presentation in nephrology meetings ( ).
Furthermore, the online survey was done in English, thus establishing a potential language barrier. In this light, it is important to note that 23% of the respondents, in spite of overall high experience in making presentations and organizing meetings, still stated they had experienced at least some language barrier ( ). However, this barrier does not seem to have been systematically perceived as higher in any of the 3 different modalities. More importantly, a limited number of ISN members completed the survey, and most participants were from high-income countries. Although this limitation is shared by most ISN surveys, it shows that our results can only be taken as indicative and as hypothesis generators. Finally, we did not explore the role of social media in continuous medical education in our survey, even if social media is becoming an important tool to remain updated and collaborate with peers, especially for general practitioners and professionals working in settings with limited healthcare access. 
In conclusion, it is hoped that this pilot study will increase the awareness of participants’ preferences on the format of scientific meetings; and identifying in hybrid meetings a good compromise between affordability, sustainability, and the need for direct exchanges with peers. Further larger studies, possibly supported by items our study identified as being important, could improve our knowledge, in particular regarding the barriers and facilitators that ensure the best results from the economic and ecological investments of scientific meetings in nephrology.
