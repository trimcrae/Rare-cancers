## Background
There is currently limited knowledge about HIV prevention and treatment cascades among female sex workers (FSW) in Ghana. This study sought to use the 2020 bio-behavioural survey (BBS) among FSWs to identify gaps and priorities in HIV treatment and prevention cascades to help achieve the 95–95-95 fast track targets.

## Method
The study used a cross-sectional design with Time Location Sampling (TLS) technique in all regions of Ghana. All eligible FSW at the selected venues and stipulated time frame were interviewed and tested for HIV onsite using the national testing algorithm. For condom use and HIV testing cascades, each step of the cascade was calculated as a percentage of all FSW. Analysis of treatment cascade was restricted to HIV positive participants and was presented using both conditional (% eligible PLHIV) and unconditional (% all PLHIV) approaches. Viral suppression level was defined as < 1000 copies/ml.

## Results
A total of 6,773 participants took part in the behavioral interviews while 6,217 took part in the biological component which involves HIV testing. In all, 33% and 80% of all FSW do not consistently use condoms with their paying clients and non-paying partners respectively. The findings further show that 26.6% of FSW have never tested for HIV, and contrary to the HIV testing policy of every six months for FSW, 70.0% did not test for HIV 6 months preceding the survey and 40.7% of all FSW did not test for HIV 12 months preceding the survey.
The conditional treatment cascade was 32%-71%-57% while the unconditional cascade was 32%-23%-49%. The study further shows 68% of the HIV positive FSW were not aware of their HIV positive status, 77% were not on treatment, while nearly 51% were not virally suppressed regardless of treatment status.

## Conclusion
The results show that both the prevention and treatment cascades are suboptimal. Key gaps identified include low: HIV testing, viral load suppression, condom use with both paying clients and non-paying partners. There is therefore the need to scale prevention and treatment interventions particularly HIV testing, treatment initiation, consistent condoms use with both paying clients and non-paying partners to help achieve the 95–95-95 fast track targets.

## Introduction
Despite the various efforts and recommended guidelines to end HIV and AIDS pandemic, there are still many countries that are yet to achieve epidemic control. Epidemic control is said to be attained when the total number of new HIV infections falls below the total number of deaths from all causes among HIV infected individuals [ ]. In 2022, the United Nations Programme on HIV and AIDS (UNAIDS) estimated that about 39 million people were living with HIV with about 1.3 million new infections [ ]. The prevalence as evident among key populations such as female sex workers (FSW) was high [ ]. Female sex workers (FSW) are one of the key population groups with significant contributions to the HIV prevalence and new infections in Ghana. Results from the Modes of Transmission (MoT) study conducted in 2014 show sex work accounted for 18.4% of all new infections [ ].
Similarly, results of the 2015 and 2020 biological and behavioral surveys (BBS) among FSW show overall HIV prevalence of 6.9% and 4.6% respectively [ ]. The HIV prevalence among this group is higher than the 2023 estimated adult national prevalence of 1.53% for the general population [ ].
Knowledge on HIV prevention measures like condom use, Pre-Exposure prophylaxis (PrEP), Post Exposure Prophylaxis (PEP) and HIV testing are some key interventions that have been shown to contribute to HIV prevention [ , ]. The implementation of preventive measures among FSWs has proven effective in the reduction of HIV spread in many countries, noting that the use of consistent condoms is identified as a single preventive measure that greatly reduces the transmission of HIV and other STIs [ ]. Consistent use of condoms has been found to reduce the transmission of HIV by about 80% [ ]. The introduction of Pre-Exposure Prophylaxis (PrEP; the administration of Antiretroviral medication (ARV even before an encounter with the virus has also remained a relevant intervention in the control of HIV spread especially among FSWs and other Key Population (KP) groups [ ]. Several studies have shown that PrEP could be an effective HIV prevention approach, particularly among FSWs, and that it could be more than 90% effective if the regimen is complied with appropriately [ , , ]. In instances of engaging in risky unprotected sexual activities, post-exposure prophylaxis (PEP) involving the consumption of ARV is medically recommended for a period to reduce the risk of acquiring the infection [ ]. Although PEP can be used by other KP groups and even health workers who get exposed to HIV, FSWs stand a good advantage of this preventive method especially due to risks associated with their sexual engagements [ ].
HIV testing is a preventive measure that stands between HIV prevention and treatment, as it encourages knowledge of status to inform the adoption of preventive measures for those that are negative and linkage to care for those positive. HIV testing remains a public health priority, especially among KPs including FSW, various studies have however expressed that, access to testing services in Sub-Saharan Africa (SSA) is most likely poor due to structural barriers such as unfavorable clinic operating hours, unavailability of test kits, high cost and health provider stigma [ , , ].
As part of the monitoring and evaluation aimed at assessing the performance of HIV prevention and treatment programmes, the World Health Organization (WHO) and UNAIDS have recommended the fast-track target of 95–95-95 to end HIV/AIDS epidemics by 2030. The fast-tract target outlines that 95% of people living with HIV know their HIV status; 95% of people who know their HIV status are receiving antiretroviral therapy; and 95% of people on treatment have a suppressed viral load. In addition, the targets seek to ensure that 95% of women of reproductive age have their HIV, and sexual and reproductive health service needs met. Additionally, 95% of people at risk of HIV infection use appropriate, prioritized, person-centered and effective combination prevention options. Global estimates in 2022 shows 86% of all the people living with HIV knew their status and 89% of these were accessing treatment. Among people accessing treatment, 93% were virally suppressed [ ].
Findings from Ghana AIDS Commission, show that Ghana needs to ramp up activities to achieve the fast-track targets. As of 2023, 65.3% of people living with HIV knew their HIV status, 69.4% of people who knew their HIV status were receiving antiretroviral therapy; and 89.0 of people on treatment had suppressed viral load [ ].
Similarly, a study conducted among HIV positive female sex workers in Ethiopia, revealed that 50% knew they were HIV positive; 93% of those who knew their status were on ART, and 92% of those on ART had attained viral suppression of less than 1000 copies per milliliter [ ]. A related study conducted in South Africa showed that 92% were known positives, 87% were on antiretroviral treatment and 74% were virally suppressed [ ].
Data from the 2020 BSS shows that three months to the survey, about 54% of FSW had been exposed to at least a prevention service, and only 35% had comprehensive knowledge about HIV and AIDS [ ].
Although existing studies in Ghana, Sub–Saharan Africa and beyond have reported on various estimates on HIV prevention and treatment cascades, there is currently limited information about prevention and treatment cascades to guide programming. This study sought to explore and provide estimates of the HIV prevention and treatment cascades in FSWs to unearth the gaps and priority areas that should be addressed by the national programme in order to achieve the 95–95-95 fast-track targets.

## Method

## Study design, population and setting
This was a bio-behavioural survey conducted via a cross-sectional study using Time Location Sampling (TLS) technique in all regions of Ghana. TLS which is sometimes referred to as Venue-Day Time (VDT) sampling was defined according to location (venues where FSW congregate) and time (when the majority of FSW congregate). TLS is used when the number of people attending a venue varies over time or day [ ].
Three months prior to implementing the TLS, a comprehensive mapping of venues where the FSW operates was conducted. Peak days, hours and estimated number of FSW who patronized the venues during the peak times were recorded. A comprehensive sampling frame for each venue was developed and organized systematically by region. A simple random sample of venues were selected from the sampling frame exclusively for unique venues without replacement. All eligible FSW at the selected venues on the selected day during the stipulated time frame were interviewed. FSW was defined as any female aged 16 years or older who reports to have exchanged sex for money or gift with someone other than her established partner in the last 6 months [ ]. FSW were classified as either Seaters, who operate from a fixed location (brothel based), or Roamers who move from one location to another to seek clients.

## Data collection
All FSW who were available at the selected venue and time frame were screened if they had received gifts or money for sex with a man other than their regular partner within the past 6 months, and if they were at least 16 years old. Those eligible were informed of the study and their informed consent sought to participate in the study. Nearby private rooms were used for both interviews and the taking of biological samples for HIV testing. HIV testing was done onsite using the national testing algorithm: First Response HIV test kit was used, and all HIV reactive cases were confirmed using Oraquick. All HIV-positive cases were further confirmed at the University of Ghana Noguchi Memorial Institute for Medical Research (UG-NMIMR) as part of the quality assurance process. Viral load testing was done for all HIV-positive participants at the UG-NMIMR using Cepheid GeneXpert HIV-1 viral load cartridge.
A structured questionnaire adapted from the BBS Guidelines for Populations at Risk of HIV was used for the study [ ]. During the post-test counselling stage, all those that had HIV positive results were asked by the counselor if they were aware of their HIV positive status, ever taken ART and were currently on ART. Details of the methods used for the BBS are described elsewhere in Dery et al., [ ].

## Data analysis
Data analysis was done using Stata software version 16. Descriptive statistical analysis was used to obtain summaries of key indicators and variables, and presented mostly in frequencies, percentages and charts where necessary. Indicators were presented at both national and regional levels. For the treatment cascade, the analysis was restricted to HIV positive participants and viral suppression level was defined as viral load less than 1,000copies/ml. The treatment cascade was presented using two approaches. The first was the conditional approach in which inclusion in a step was based on self-reported response to the previous step and where viral load suppression was determined based on those who indicated that they were on HIV treatment. The second was the unconditional approach, where the proportion of treatment was calculated based on all HIV-positive cases (regardless of knowledge of HIV-positive status), and viral load suppression was determined based on all HIV-positive cases irrespective of treatment status. The treatment cascades were disaggregated by the type of FSW (Seater or Roamers). For the prevention cascades, the analysis included all the participants who participated in the behavioral interviews. For condom use and HIV testing cascades, the following were determined: the percentage of all FSW that had heard about condoms, the proportion that had ever used a condom, the proportion that believed that using condoms can prevent HIV infection, the proportion who reported using condoms during last sex with a client, and consistent condom use with paying clients and as well as with non-paying partners. HIV prevention technologies such as pre-exposure prophylaxis (PrEP), post-exposure prophylaxis (PEP), and HIV self-testing (HIVST) were also assessed. All respondents were asked whether they had heard, had used, and were willing to use these methods. The data was weighted, and the national weight was used in the analyses of this study. Details of the weighting can be found in Dery et al., [ ] and Guure et al., [ ].

## Ethical considerations
Ethical approval was obtained from the University of Ghana Noguchi Memorial Institute for Medical Research Institutional Review Board (CPN 083/18–19), the Ghana Health Service Ethics Review Committee (GHS-ERC 002/05/19) and the Population Council Institutional Review Board (Protocol 891), New York, USA.

## Results
A total of 8,870 participants were screened out of which 7,000 were eligible and took part in the study. 6,773 participants took part in the behavioral interviews while 6,217 took part in the biological component which involves giving blood samples for HIV testing. In all, 5,990 participants completed both the biological and the behavioral components while 783 and 227 took part in only the behavioral and biological aspects of the study respectively as reported by Dery et al., [ ].

## HIV treatment cascade
Nationally, and based on the conditional approach (eligible PLHIV), 32% (88) of HIV-positive FSW were aware of their HIV status prior to the survey, and 71% (62) of FSW who were aware of their HIV-positive status, were on treatment, out of which 57% (35) achieved viral suppression (Fig.  a). A slightly higher proportion of HIV-positive Seaters (brothel-based) indicated that they were aware of their HIV-positive status (38%, 16) compared to Roamers (31%,72). Additionally, 75% (12) of Seaters who knew of their HIV-positive status were on ART while 67% (8) were virally suppressed compared to 69% (50) of Roamers on ART, among which 54% (27) were virally suppressed. The result shows that 67.8% (185) FSW: 61.9% (26) for Seaters and 68.8% (159) for Roamers were not aware of their HIV-positive status. Furthermore, 29.5% (26) of FSW: 25% (4) of Seaters and 31% (22) of Roamers were not on treatment even though they were aware of their HIV positive status, while 43.5% (27) of them (33.3% (4) of seaters and 46.0% (23) roamers) were not virally suppressed although on treatment. 
Using the unconditional approach (based on all HIV positive FSWs), only 23% (62) of the HIV positive FSW were on treatment (irrespective of prior knowledge of their HIV status), while 49% (134) were virally suppressed, regardless of treatment status. More Seaters than Roamers were on treatment (29% compared 22%). Also, 60% (109) of Seaters were virally suppressed compared to 47% (25) of Roamers (Fig.  b). This result reveals that 77.3% (211) of all FSW (71% seaters and 78% roamers) were not on treatment, while 50.9% (139) of them (40% of seaters and 53% of roamers) were not virally suppressed regardless of treatment status. However, there was no statistically significant difference between Roamers and Seaters across the treatment cascades.
Regionally, results of those who knew their HIV positive status ranges from 0.0% in Oti region to 100% in Savannah region. Results of those on ART (based on % of all HIV positive cases) also ranges from 0.0% in Northern, and Oti regions to 74.4% in Upper East region, while those that had achieved viral suppression ranges from 0.0% in Oti region and 96.6% in Western North region (Fig.  ). 
Table shows treatment cascades by socio-demographic characteristics. About 24.5% (CI: 16.1%, 35.8%) of HIV-positive young FSW (16–24 years) knew of their status with 70.6% (CI: 45.5%, 87.3%) on treatment while 66.6% (CI: 37.0%, 87.2%) were virally suppressed. Similarly, 32.4% (CI: 25.1, 40.6%) of FSW between 25–35 years knew their status, 66.6% (CI: 51.1%, 79.3%) on treatment and 58.3% (CI: 38.0%, 76.2%) suppressed while 56.4% (CI: 43.1%, 68.8%) of those greater than 35 years knew their status, 90.0% (CI: 72.9%, 96.8%) on treatment and 52.0% (CI: 32.7%, 70.7%) suppressed.
 

## HIV prevention cascades

## Condom use cascade
Figure  describes the condom use cascade. Ever heard of, and ever used condoms were nearly universal (94.4% and 94.1% respectively). However, only 66.6% of all FSW use condoms consistently with their paying clients while 20.3% consistently use with their non-paying partners. Regionally, consistent condom use with paying clients ranges from a low of 19.7% in the Ahafo region to a high of 91.8% in the Western North region (Fig.  ). For gap in the condom use cascade, about 13.9% of FSW do not believe that using condoms could prevent HIV infection. Again about 33.4% of all FSW do not consistently use condoms with their paying client while 79.7% do not consistently use condoms with their non-paying partners (Fig.  ). 
Figure  shows some of the main reasons why condoms were not used by FSW. Among those who did not use a condom with their non-paying partners, the majority (63.2%) indicated trust in their non-paying whilst, in 11.6%, the male partner refused the use of a condom. Among FSWs who did not use a condom with paying clients, 26.0% indicated that their clients refused to use condoms, 18.2% had their clients paying extra monies for sex and hence allowed sex without condoms, and 8.5% did not have condoms at the point of having sex. 

## HIV testing cascade
Figure  presents the results of HIV testing behaviors among FSW. Of all the FSW engaged in the study, only 73.5% had ever tested for HIV, while 26.6% had never tested for HIV. 56.7% tested for HIV within 12 months and only 29.9% tested within 6 months preceding the survey. This shows a gap of 26.6% of FSW that had never tested and 70.1% that did not test within 6 months preceding the survey. 
The regional distribution of the HIV testing cascade among FSW is demonstrated in Fig.  . While Bono East region had the highest HIV testing among FSW (88.1%), Oti region recorded the lowest with 34.5%. for testing in the last 12 months before the study, Bono East and Oti regions recorded the highest and least with 82.6% and 10.7% respectively, likewise testing 6 months; 67.1% and 5.1% respectively (Fig.  ). 
HIV prevention technologies such as pre-exposure prophylaxis (PrEP), post-exposure prophylaxis (PEP), and HIV self-testing (HIVST) are presented in Fig.  . With regards to PrEP and PEP, only 7.0% and 7.5% of FSWs had ever taken the interventions respectively. Interestingly, 49.4% and 70.2% were willing to use PrEP and PEP respectively. Only 34.6% of FSWs had ever heard of HIVST, however, the majority 58.0% were willing to self-test. 

## Discussion
This study utilized the 2020 BBS data that was collected among FSW to identify gaps and priorities in treatment and prevention cascades to achieve the 95–95-95 fast-track targets. The findings reveal low HIV testing to identify positive cases for linkage to care, low viral suppression, and low consistent condom use with both paying and non-paying partners. Additionally, knowledge of PrEP, PEP, and HIVST is low, however, there appears to be an unmet need for these services.
On the treatment cascade, the results of this study show that the performance towards attaining the fast-track targets is suboptimal. Nearly 68% of the HIV-positive FSW were not aware of their HIV-positive status and more than three quarters (77.3%) were not on treatment, while nearly 51% were not virally suppressed regardless of treatment status. The findings of this study are about double the gaps found in other studies in Africa. A study in Zimbabwe found that 36% of FSWs were not aware of their HIV status, 32.3% of those who knew their status were not on treatment, and about 22% of those on treatment were not virally suppressed [ ]. Napierala et al., [ ] also found in Zimbabwe that 31% of FSW were not aware of their HIV-positive status, 32% were not on ARVs, and 21% were not virally suppressed while in Benin, it was found that about 30% of FSWs were not aware of their HIV positive status. They found that only 10% were not on treatment and about 18% didn’t achieve viral suppression [ ]. Again, results from South Africa showed only about 8% FSW had no knowledge of being HIV positive and 13% of those who knew their status were not on treatment, while only 26.1% of those on treatment achieved viral suppression [ ].
These gaps point to the fact that HIV treatment cascade among the FSW intervention is not performing well with the main bottlenecks being the testing and identifying HIV-positive FSW, and viral suppression. Considering that HIV testing serves as the entry point to the rest of the treatment cascade, any failure or delay in achieving the first 95 of the fast-track targets will have a rippling effect on the rest of the cascade. Therefore, priority should be given to HIV testing and identifying all HIV-positive FSW.
Condom programming is a well-established prevention strategy to prevent HIV. About a third (33%) and 80% of all FSW do not consistently use condoms with their paying clients and non-paying partners respectively. Among those who did not use condoms with their non-paying partners, the main reasons were trust in their partners and their partner’s refusal while the main reasons cited for not using condoms with paying clients were client’s refusal, clients paying extra monies for sex, and condoms not being available at the point of sex. Similar results regarding inconsistent condom use with paying clients have been found by other studies: 48% in Zimbabwe [ ], 27% and 33% in Kenya by Bhattacharjee et al., [ ], Simmelink et al., [ ] respectively. With regards to inconsistent condom use with non-paying partners (non-paying partners such as boyfriends or husbands), results are varied: 40% in Lagos [ ] and 19% in Ethiopia [ ]. A systematic review by Okafor et al., [ ] showed low consistent condom use with regular non-paying partners. Correct and consistent condom use is one of the most effective measures for reducing HIV and STI transmission among FSWs and their clients (both paying and non-paying). The low consistent condom use with clients particularly with Intimate Partners (IPs) is of grave concern given that IPs may contribute more to FSWs' HIV and STI risk than paying clients do. Several studies have consistently shown higher HIV prevalence among FSWs' IPs than their paying clients [ , ].
One of the main reasons for not using condoms with IP was trust. Using trust as a main reason for not using a condom with a non-paying partner is worrying, given that most non-paying partners also have other partners including some commercial sex partners, and sometimes do not use condoms with these partners [ ]. This finding reiterates WHO’s assessment that non-paying partners though most at risk of HIV infection are least likely to have access to HIV services and are left out of national HIV plans in many countries [ ]. While it is important that HIV programming should target improving condom use among clients and partners in general, there is also the need to pay close attention to non-paying partners. The low condom use with non-paying clients could negate the gains in reducing HIV transmission among FSWs. As such, condom use with non-paying partners needs to be emphasized with targeted messages to the FSW to understand the risk involved in not using condoms with non-paying partners.
HIV testing serves as a gateway to prevention and the entry point for care and treatment. About a quarter (26.6%) of FSW have never been tested for HIV. In line with national HIV and STI policy, it is expected that HIV testing will be done every six months for FSW. About 2 out of 5 (40.7%) of all FSW did not test for HIV 12 months preceding the survey, while 70.0% did not test for HIV 6 months preceding the survey. A similar study in Brazil by de Brito et al., [ ] found that 22.8% had never tested for HIV and only 33.9% had taken HIV tests within 12 months prior to the study [ ]. Other studies have however shown higher HIV testing: 96% of FSWs in Kenya had received an HIV test in their lifetime, with 85% doing so in the last three months before the study [ ]. In Uganda, 90% of the HIV-negative FSWs had tested in the past 12 months, with 56% testing at least three times within the past 12 months [ ]. Given that HIV testing serves as the entry point to treatment, there is a need to expand HIV testing services to achieve 95–95-95 targets.
Knowledge of PrEP, PEP, and HIVST among FSW was low, however, there appears to be an unmet need for these services. Many FSWs were willing to use these prevention methods if available as nearly half (49.4%) were willing to take PrEP, 70.2% were willing to take PEP and 58.0% were willing to use HIVST. Various studies have demonstrated similar trends in knowledge and willingness to use PrEP, PEP, and HIVST among FSW. About 67% FSWs had ever heard of PrEP and 98% were willing to use it if offered [ ], 77% of Ugandan FSW were of the view that, PrEP could be a measure to prevent them from contracting HIV and were willing to use it [ ]. A similar study in Nigeria had 91% FSWs indicating their willingness to uptake PrEP [ ] and 68% of FSWs in Ethiopia were open to use PrEP [ ]. Contrary to this study, although 76.5% of FSWs in Nigeria had knowledge on PrEP, only 8.1% of them were willing to take up if offered, [ ]. Willingness to use HIVST was high among FSW studies from Uganda, 97% [ ] and Ethiopia 68% [ ] compared to Ghana. Given the massive HIV preventive effect of PrEP and PEP, there is an urgent need to incorporate and expand these services, as well as HIVST.

## Limitation
A limitation to our study could be social desirability bias: It is possible that some sex workers were shy and may have provided socially desirable responses. The spectrum of responses, however, shows that this may have had a limited or no effect. Another limitation is that our data only allows for cross-sectional cascades rather than cohort cascades, so we are unable to follow progression over time through the cascades. Additionally, the number of HIV positives in some regions was very small which could bias the results of the cascades in those regions. Therefore, the treatment cascade for the regions should be interpreted within this limitation.

## Conclusion
The results show that the performance of the prevention and treatment cascade is suboptimal. Key gaps identified include low: HIV testing, treatment, viral load suppression, and condom use with both paying clients and non-paying partners. However, there appears to be an unmet need for PrEP, PEP, and HIVST, as many are willing to use these prevention methods. There is therefore the need to scale the treatment and prevention cascade, particularly HIV testing including HIVST, PrEP, PEP, and consistent condom use with both paying clients and non-paying partners to help achieve the 95–95-95 fast track targets.
