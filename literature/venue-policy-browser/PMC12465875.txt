## The central role of social work in community integration
Social work plays a pivotal role in resolving governance challenges by facilitating resource integration and fostering a sense of community identity through professional intervention [ ]. In the realm of sports governance, social work can transform athletic activities into vehicles for social connection by organizing events and building participatory platforms, thereby encouraging interaction among diverse population groups [ – ]. However, current practice in Dongguan reveals a tendency to prioritize infrastructure development over behavioral engagement [ ]. This has led to a misalignment between the governance framework and residents’ patterns of participation. Theoretically, community sports governance differs significantly from general community governance.

## Domain specialization
SGS is exclusively focused on the provision of public sports services. Its tools—such as the Community Sports Facility Allocation Guidelines and the Management Regulations for Social Sports Instructors—and its core objective of promoting physical activity distinguish it from broader governance systems that cover multiple policy areas, including education, health care, and urban services.

## Process differentiation
SGS operates through a sector-specific governance loop comprising needs assessment, resource integration,infrastructure development, activity implementation, and performance evaluation. This cycle ensures goal alignment within the unique operational logic of the sports domain, enabling targeted and outcome-driven interventions.

## Causal orientation
Unlike general governance models that influence residents’ well-being indirectly via environmental improvements, SGS directly shapes residents’ sport-related behaviors and their enabling conditions—such as facility accessibility and program diversity—thus serving as an immediate driver of health-oriented civic engagement.
Unlike conventional community governance—which typically addresses service delivery in domains such as sanitation, housing, and public order—SGS is explicitly behavior-oriented, designed to catalyze and sustain physical activity through sport-specific governance mechanisms. This functional specialization is particularly salient in demographically complex contexts such as Dongguan, where migrant-majority populations exhibit distinct preferences and constraints regarding sports engagement. SGS thus serves as a tailored governance innovation, enabling flexible, low-cost, and participatory sports services that resonate with the lived realities of transient urban residents.
Specifically, it represents a specialized extension of general community governance within the domain of sports, characterized by its targeted focus on constructing a localized sports ecosystem. This shift enables a substantive transition from broad-based public service provision to the deepened promotion of health-specific initiatives. There is thus an urgent need for theoretical exploration into critical questions such as: How does the governance system influence participatory behavior? And what factors moderate this process?

## Research gaps and problem statement
Existing studies largely focus on singular dimensions of governance—such as institutional development or resource allocation—while lacking a systematic analysis of the interaction mechanisms between the “governance system, participatory behavior, and attitudes/awareness.” Specifically, the following gaps persist:

## Unclear mediating mechanisms
Does the governance system indirectly influence participatory behavior by enhancing levels of engagement?

## Lack of moderating effect analysis
Do residents’ perceptions (e.g., awareness of the value of sports) amplify the effectiveness of the governance system?

## Hypotheses based on the theoretical context of social governance, sports participation, and awareness
Although a growing body of literature emphasizes the health benefits of regular physical activity, few studies critically examine the institutional conditions that enable such behaviors in rapidly urbanizing Asian contexts. Shahmirzadi, Mahale [ ]argue that casual sports engagement within residential communities contributes meaningfully to sustainable urban living, yet their analysis tends to treat participation as an individual choice, without fully unpacking the structural drivers behind it. Similarly, while Sobczyk et al. [ ] find a weak but significant association between urban residency and satisfaction with physical activity services (r = 0.10, p = 0.03), their work stops short of exploring how governance mechanisms—such as policy coordination, program implementation, and community mobilization—actively shape participation patterns. Yu et al. [ ] and Ishikawa et al. [ ]further highlight that built environments and service infrastructure can influence behavioral tendencies, but their focus remains largely environmental, overlooking the regulatory and organizational aspects of local sports systems. This collective oversight underscores a critical gap: namely, the under-theorization of sports governance as a driver of physical activity participation. Governance systems determine not only the availability and quality of sports facilities but also the inclusivity, responsiveness, and sustainability of local programming. Without an enabling governance structure, even well-designed environments and facilities may fail to activate public engagement. Therefore, to move beyond the infrastructural and environmental determinism that characterizes much of the current literature, this study conceptualizes sports governance as a core determinant of physical activity behavior and proposes the following hypothesis:

## H1: The sports governance system (SGS) has a significant positive impact on public participation in sports (PPS)
While the frequency of residents’ engagement in physical activity has garnered considerable attention in urban health research, intensity of participation—defined as the physical, temporal, and psychological investment in sports—has received relatively less analytical focus. Shahmirzadi et al. (2024) provide preliminary evidence that higher-frequency participation in sports activities correlates with improved health outcomes, but their findings stop short of distinguishing whether these effects stem from intensity or mere frequency [ ]. More nuanced insight is offered by Wang et al. [ ], who emphasize that vibrant athletic communities in small towns are often underpinned by proactive local government policies that not only increase access but also deepen community involvement and commitment to sport. This observation suggests that governance quality can shape not just whether residents participate, but how they participate—modulating the depth and sustainability of their engagement. Haider, Dohnalová [ ] further report that during periods of policy uncertainty (such as the COVID-19 pandemic), sports participation intensity declined more sharply than frequency, highlighting the structural dependence of high-intensity engagement on stable governance support. These findings converge on the idea that governance systems—through funding, policy coherence, program diversity, and sustained infrastructure investment—are instrumental in fostering a more intense, rather than superficial, sports culture. Thus, this study posits that sports governance not only facilitates participation per se, but significantly enhances its intensity. Accordingly, we propose:

## H2: The sports governance system (SGS) has a significant positive impact on residents’ sports participation intensity (SID)
Recent scholarship has increasingly emphasized the importance of residents’ participation intensity as a crucial behavioral pathway linking governance initiatives to individual-level physical activity outcomes. Park [ ]underscores that the presence of well-designed daily sports programs, particularly those supported by sufficient facilities and qualified instructors, significantly enhances residents’ frequency and depth of physical engagement. These findings imply that the influence of a well-structured sports governance system (SGS) is most potent when it fosters not merely the availability of opportunities, but sustained, high-intensity engagement by residents. Supporting this, Chen et al. [ ] demonstrate that the level of physical activity is closely tied to individual attitudes and behaviors, which in turn are shaped by the frequency and intensity of exercise—highlighting sports participation intensity (SID) as a behavioral construct that mediates broader policy effects. Furthermore, Liu et al. [ ] present compelling evidence that subjective perceptions of public sports spaces and actual exercise behaviors are positively associated, particularly when individuals engage in repeated and vigorous participation. Taken together, these studies suggest that the benefits of macro-level governance structures manifest through increased individual participation intensity, which serves as a conduit for transforming policy into action. Accordingly, this study proposes the following hypothesis:

## H3: Residents’ sports participation intensity (SID) mediates the relationship between the sports governance system (SGS) and physical activity behavior (PPS)
In recent years, growing scholarly interest has focused on the role of individual awareness in moderating the effectiveness of sports governance policies on behavioral outcomes. Wang et al. (2024) reveal that heightened awareness of sports policies, including understanding institutional, familial, and community supports, is significantly associated with increased levels of moderate-to-vigorous physical activity (MVPA) among youth [ ]. Their structural model underscores the amplifying role of awareness in strengthening the link between environmental enablers and personal behavioral outputs. Complementing this perspective, Okoti and Njihia (2024) emphasize that sports officials’ attitudes and awareness creation by government institutions critically influence the implementation and success of sports governance frameworks, indicating that the mere presence of policy is insufficient without corresponding levels of public cognizance and engagement [ ]. Furthermore, Lun, Wang [ ]in another study on cultural moderation in sports contexts found that awareness functions as a critical contextual moderator, facilitating deeper sports involvement and enhancing downstream educational and social development effects through a moderated mediation model. These findings collectively point to the moderating effect of physical activity awareness (PAS), which operates by enhancing residents’ receptivity and responsiveness to governance initiatives. Specifically, individuals with higher levels of awareness are more likely to internalize the benefits and purpose of structured sports interventions, thereby increasing their intensity of participation. In line with this reasoning, the current study proposes the following hypothesis:

## H4: Physical activity awareness (PAS) positively moderates the relationship between the sports governance system (SGS) and sports participation intensity (SID)

## Research methodology

## Research design and framework
This study adopts a quantitative research approach utilizing a cross-sectional survey design to examine the influence of the community sports governance system on residents’ sports participation, as well as the mediating role of sports infrastructure development and the moderating role of public sports awareness. Based on previous literature and theoretical grounding, a multi-dimensional structural equation model (SEM) was constructed to capture the complex interrelationships among governance maturity, infrastructure standards, participation behavior, and awareness levels.

## Participants and procedures
Following ethical approval granted by the Institutional Ethics Committee of Guangdong University of Science and Technology (Registration Number: 2024A188), data collection was conducted from January 3 to March 5, 2025, using both online and offline survey methods. A total of 770 questionnaires were distributed and collected. After excluding incomplete and invalid responses, 735 valid questionnaires were retained for analysis.
The participants consisted of 423 males (57.6%) and 312 females (42.4%), spanning a wide range of age groups: 1.4% were under 18, 21.4% were aged 18–25, 34.1% were aged 26–35, 41.2% were aged 35–59, and 1.9% were 60 years or older. In terms of residence duration in the community, 18.0% had lived there for less than 1 year, 16.1% for 1–3 years, 9.8% for 3–5 years, 32.0% for 5–10 years, and 24.2% for over 10 years. Regarding occupational background, the sample included public sector employees (22.4%), enterprise staff (25.9%), self-employed individuals (16.2%), retirees (3.8%), students (17.3%), freelancers (14.1%), and others (0.3%). see Appendix Table 1.
This diverse demographic composition provided a solid empirical foundation for analyzing the relationships among community sports governance, infrastructure development, public participation, and awareness.

## Instruments
All constructs were measured using multi-item scales adapted from established studies and revised to fit the Chinese community sports governance context. The complete English-language version of the adapted questionnaire is available in Supplementary file 1. Responses were collected on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (“strongly disagree”) to 5 (“strongly agree”). The reliability of the dimensions can be found in Appendix Table 2.

## Sports governance system (SGS)
The SGS is a second-order latent construct adapted from the theoretical model proposed by Thompson, Lachance [ ], reflecting the maturity of community-level sports governance. It comprises three first-order dimensions: Institutional Development (SGS-ID), Resource Allocation (SGS-RA), and Organizational Management (SGS-OM). SGS-ID focuses on incorporating the effectiveness of community sports governance into the annual performance evaluation system; SGS-RA derives its funding from a hybrid mechanism combining public welfare lottery funds and collectively owned community assets; and SGS-OM emphasizes a collaborative governance model involving the Party branch, homeowners’ committee, and civil society organizations. The overall construct demonstrated good internal reliability, with a second-order composite reliability coefficient of 0.866.

## Sports infrastructure development (SID)
The SID construct serves as a mediating variable, adapted from the framework of Wang, Li [ ], which assesses the public accessibility and standardization of sports infrastructure across urban communities. This second-order latent variable encompasses three dimensions: Types of Facilities (SID-TF), Facility Quality (SID-FQ), and Facility Maintenance (SID-FM). Respectively, these dimensions evaluate the diversity of facility types, the degree of modernization and safety, and the consistency of facility management practices. The overall construct showed high internal reliability, with a second-order composite reliability coefficient of 0.881.

## Public participation in sports (PPS)
PPS serves as the dependent variable, adapted from the conceptual framework developed by Eather, Wade [ ], which links sports engagement with broader mental and social benefits. This construct reflects residents’ behavioral engagement in physical activity across three key dimensions: Frequency of Participation (PPS-FP), Types of Sports Participation (PPS-TSP), and Depth of Participation (PPS-DP). Respectively, these dimensions capture the regularity of participation, diversity in activity types, and intensity levels ranging from recreational to competitive. The second-order construct demonstrated strong internal consistency, with a composite reliability coefficient of 0.815.

## Public awareness of sports (PAS)
PAS serves as the moderating variable, adapted from Breslin, Shannon [ ], and captures residents’ cognitive evaluations, emotional orientations, and behavioral inclinations toward sport. It comprises three subdimensions: Perception of Sports Value (PAS-PSV), Attitude Toward Sports Participation (PAS-ATSP), Behavioral Tendency Toward Sports (PAS-BTS). The construct demonstrated strong internal consistency, with a composite reliability of 0.876.
PAS-PSV: Residents’ recognition of the health and social value of sports (e.g., “physical activity promotes community integration”) motivates them to express facility-related needs more actively. This, in turn, drives governance bodies to optimize resource allocation based on articulated demand, aligning with the “demand-oriented” principle outlined in planning documents such as the Standards for Community Sports Facility Development.
PAS-ATSP: A positive attitude toward participation (e.g., “willingness to join community sports activities”) often manifests through local deliberative mechanisms (e.g., proposals submitted by homeowner committees), thereby influencing governance decisions, such as the renewal of outdated fitness equipment in aging neighborhoods.
PAS-BTS: Behavioral intentions (e.g., “planning to use facilities three times per week”) are translated into measurable usage frequency data, which serves as a key reference for governance bodies in making maintenance and expansion decisions—such as prioritizing facility upgrades based on usage levels.

## Data analysis
All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS 26.0 and SmartPLS 4.1.0.3. The reliability, validity, and overall structure of the questionnaire and theoretical model were checked using both exploratory factor analysis (EFA) and confirmatory factor analysis (CFA).

## Exploratory factor analysis
The Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin (KMO) measure was 0.868 (see Appendix Table 3), indicating meritorious sampling adequacy. Bartlett’s test of sphericity was significant (χ² = 6862.366, df = 153, p < 0.001), confirming that the correlation matrix was suitable for factor analysis.
Principal component analysis with varimax rotation extracted four distinct components with eigenvalues above 1, cumulatively explaining 66.849% of the total variance (see Appendix Table 4). The rotated component matrix confirmed clear factor loading patterns, aligning well with the predefined constructs: Sustainable Governance of Sport (SGS), Public Policy Support (PPS), Spatial Integration Degree (SID), and Public Awareness of Sports (PAS). All items loaded strongly (> 0.70) on their intended factors, with minimal cross-loadings, supporting initial construct validity.

## Confirmatory factor analysis
CFA was conducted using SmartPLS to further assess the measurement model. As shown in the outer loading matrix, all indicator loadings exceeded the recommended threshold of 0.70 (ranging from 0.765 to 0.900, see Appendix Table 5), indicating strong item reliability.

## Convergent validity
The model demonstrated good convergent validity. All constructs exceeded the critical values for Cronbach’s alpha (> 0.70), composite reliability (CR > 0.80), and average variance extracted (AVE > 0.50), see Appendix Table 6.

## Discriminant validity
Discriminant validity was assessed using the Fornell-Larcker criterion and Heterotrait-Monotrait Ratio (HTMT). According to Fornell-Larcker (see Appendix Table 7), the square roots of AVE values for each construct were greater than their inter-construct correlations, indicating adequate discriminant validity. All HTMT values were below the recommended threshold of 0.85 (see Appendix Table 8), reinforcing strong discriminant validity among the latent constructs.

## Collinearity diagnosis
Variance inflation factors (VIF) were used to assess multicollinearity among the latent variables. All VIF values ranged from 1.134 to 1.312 (see Appendix Table 9), well below the conservative cutoff value of 3.3, indicating no serious multicollinearity concerns.
The complete structural model of the research measurement indicators can be found in Appendix Fig. 1.

## Bivariate correlation analysis
Correlation analysis refers to the process of describing and examining the nature and degree of association between two or more variables. An asterisk (*) is marked in the upper right corner of the correlation coefficient to indicate a statistically significant relationship; if no asterisk is present, it suggests no significant relationship. A positive correlation coefficient indicates a positive association between the two variables, whereas a negative coefficient suggests a negative relationship.

 

Table  presents the Pearson correlation coefficients among the key variables: SGS, PPS, SID, and PAS. All correlations are statistically significant at the 0.01 level (two-tailed), indicating moderate to strong relationships among the constructs.
Specifically, SGS shows a significant positive correlation with PPS (r = 0.349, p < 0.01), SID (r = 0.484, p < 0.01), and PAS (r = 0.331, p < 0.01), suggesting that clearer sports goals are associated with higher self-perception in physical activity, stronger sports identity, and greater satisfaction. Hypotheses HI and H2 are established. PPS is also positively correlated with SID (r = 0.447, p < 0.01) and PAS (r = 0.195, p < 0.01), while SID is positively associated with PAS (r = 0.280, p < 0.01). These significant associations provide preliminary support for the hypothesized relationships and justify further structural modeling analyses.

 

To examine the effects of Types of Facilities (SID-TF), Facility Quality (SID-FQ), and Facility Maintenance (SID-FM) on Public Participation in Sports (PPS), Table presents the results of a hierarchical regression analysis. In this model, PPS is treated as the dependent variable, while gender, age, years of residence, occupation, SID-TF, SID-FQ, and SID-FM serve as independent variables. A hierarchical regression approach was adopted to control for the potential confounding effects of the demographic variables (i.e., gender, age, years of residence, and occupation) before introducing the facility-related predictors.
The hierarchical regression results provide robust empirical support for disaggregating the second-order construct of Sports Infrastructure Development (SID). Among its three dimensions, facility maintenance (SID-FM) demonstrated a strong and statistically significant positive effect on public sports participation (β = 0.780, p < 0.001), underscoring its behavioral relevance. In contrast, types of facilities (SID-TF) and facility quality (SID-FQ) were negatively associated with participation, which may reflect a misalignment between infrastructure provision and residents’ actual needs. The marked improvement in model fit (ΔR² = 0.053) reinforces the value of treating SID as a multidimensional construct for greater explanatory precision.
The observed negative correlations between SID-TF (Types of Facilities) and SID-FQ (Facility Quality) with participation levels can be primarily attributed to a mismatch between supply and demand. In several communities—such as xinan and Dalang—there is a noticeable tendency toward “image-driven projects,” with excessive investment in high-end facilities like heated swimming pools and professional tennis courts. However, residents’ actual needs are concentrated on more basic, free, and accessible spaces, such as fitness trails and open ball courts.
Moreover, the high maintenance costs of premium facilities have diverted resources away from essential infrastructure. For instance, Shanmei Community reduced its fitness trail renovation budget by 30% to cover the maintenance costs of a heated pool. This reallocation has led to a decline in overall participation rates.
These findings align with the “appropriate supply theory,” which emphasizes that infrastructure must match local demand to yield positive outcomes. In this context, the negative coefficients of SID-TF and SID-FQ reflect a structural misalignment: Dongguan’s facility supply has failed to respond to the preferences of its migrant worker population, who generally favor simplicity and cost-free access. Multifunctional spaces often go unused due to the absence of on-site instructors, while high-specification facilities are underutilized because of associated fees. Consequently, both facility diversity and quality, when misaligned with user needs, are inversely associated with physical activity participation.

## Path coefficients and model explanatory power

 

As shown in Table  , the standardized path coefficient for SGS→PPS is 0.323 (T = 9.281, p = 0.000), indicating a significant positive effect of the SGS on PPS, thereby confirming Hypothesis 1. This result supports the institutional theory’s “structural incentives–behavioral transformation” mechanism, wherein institutional provision, resource assurance, and system design collectively guide residents toward stable patterns of sports participation.
In practice, Dajingtou Community in Dongguan serves as a representative example. Under a governance model led by the subdistrict office and coordinated by the community, a series of initiatives—such as regularly organized morning and evening exercise sessions, open-access venue reservations, and a resident feedback mechanism—have effectively shifted residents’ sports participation from “passive response” to “proactive integration,” with PPS consistently maintained at a high level.
In contrast, Dalang Community, despite considerable investment in visual landscaping and the procurement of sports equipment, has seen limited effectiveness. Its overemphasis on aesthetic presentation, neglect of follow-up institutional development, and shortage of social sports instructors have resulted in insufficient user guidance and lack of community support. Consequently, its PPS level remains below 46%, revealing a clear disconnect between surface-level provision and behavioral outcomes. This phenomenon of “governance misalignment” further underscores the critical role of institutional enforcement capacity in promoting public engagement in sports.
The standardized path coefficient for SGS → SID is 0.412 (T = 9.876, p = 0.000), the highest among all model paths, thereby confirming Hypothesis 2. This finding indicates that the SGS not only facilitates the initiation of sports behavior but also significantly enhances the SID—specifically in terms of participation frequency, duration, and social engagement.
In Taihe Community of Dongguan, the deep institutional integration has driven the intensive development of residents’ sports participation. A notable example is Dianzhan Arts, a key local cultural organization in Tangwei Village, which has actively embedded itself into Taihe’s sports governance mechanisms while offering youth dance training programs. This collaboration has effectively expanded the boundaries of sports services and fostered a profound integration between cultural identity and physical activity by incorporating Kang Wang Bao Dan, a nationally recognized intangible cultural heritage, into community-based fitness activities. The dance training camps have functioned as a “covert entry point” for youth sports participation, marking an innovative path of governance that merges cultural and physical education (“wen-ti gongyu”).
Driven by the dual forces of community identity and behavioral stickiness, residents’ SID dimensions in Taihe Community have risen markedly. In contrast, although Dalang Community is well-equipped with sports facilities, the absence of community-based organization, guidance personnel, and interactive mechanisms has led to sporadic, individualized use of resources. Participation remains isolated and discontinuous, failing to generate sustained or socially interactive engagement. This contrast further highlights the structural impact of governance mechanisms on the intensity and quality of sports participation.
The standardized path coefficient for SID → PPS is 0.358 (T = 8.709, p = 0.000), and the indirect effect of the mediating path SGS → SID →PPS is also significant (β = 0.147, T = 6.971, p = 0.000), thereby confirming Hypothesis 3. These results suggest that the SID play a significant mediating role between the SGS and PPS.
In Changfu Community, regular integration of dance groups, martial arts classes, and festival-based sports culture into daily life has fostered a sense of belonging and strengthened residents’ willingness to utilize public spaces through high-frequency sports participation. This has led to a path of logic characterized by a transition “from familiarity to trust,” thereby enhancing the overall level of PPS. Furthermore, public spaces have evolved into critical venues for reproducing community cohesion.
In contrast, Liaobu Community, despite being equipped with more advanced fitness equipment, suffers from low participation due to a lack of maintenance protocols and institutional feedback mechanisms. As a result, prolonged equipment idleness has weakened both the frequency and stickiness of residents’ sports engagement. This comparison underscores that, without effective behavioral-driving mechanisms, governance initiatives alone are insufficient to achieve substantial improvements in PPS.
The interaction term PAS × SGS→ yields a significant regression coefficient of 0.377 (T = 9.898, p = 0.000), and PAS × SGS→PPS is also significantly positive (β = 0.135, T = 6.062, p = 0.000), thereby confirming Hypothesis 4. These results indicate that PAS serves as a significant moderator in the relationship between the SGS and both SID and PPS. In other words, governance structures are not self-sufficient; their effectiveness depends heavily on residents’ cognitive alignment and value responsiveness.
In Nanshe Community, grassroots organizations have employed online surveys, health lectures, and personalized fitness prescriptions to enhance residents’ awareness of the value of sports. On this cognitive foundation, the marginal effects of governance incentives have been significantly amplified, ultimately forming a positive feedback loop of “cognition → behavior → trust.”
In contrast, Xinan Community, despite possessing modern sports facilities, has seen limited behavioral outcomes due to a general lack of intrinsic recognition of the value of sports and insufficient understanding of governance policies among residents. As a result, institutional provisions become “disconnected from demand” and fail to translate into actual participation.
This contrast highlights the amplification effect of PAS—namely, that only under high levels of cognitive engagement can governance systems become both effective and impactful, rather than well-intentioned but ineffectual.
In summary, all four research hypotheses received strong empirical support, collectively outlining a clear institutional–behavioral–cognitive interaction chain: the SGS promotes the formation of sports participation behaviors (PPS) by enhancing the intensity of participation (SID), with PAS playing a significant moderating role.
Empirical observations across multiple communities in Dongguan further reveal that governance institutions are not merely abstract technical regulations but rather a practical logic embedded in community structures, cultural contexts, and interpersonal networks. It is only through the synergy of institutional provision, cultural embedding, and cognitive activation that community-based sports governance can achieve a systemic transformation—from “visible infrastructure” to “sustainable participation.”

## A pairwise comparison of sports governance and public sports participation across communities in Dongguan

 

 

Tables  and provide an in-depth analysis of significant group differences based on three key demographic variables: gender, length of residence, and occupation (See Appendix Fig. 2 for details.)
The results reveal several important findings. First, gender differences in PPS are statistically significant (p < 0.001), with male participants demonstrating a notably higher level of engagement in sports activities compared to females.
This finding aligns with existing research, which suggests that men are more likely to develop self-organized participation patterns in sports settings, whereas women often face constraints related to the safety of sports venues, the appropriateness of activity design, and the influence of gender-based stereotypes. These disparities are particularly evident in grassroots communities in Dongguan. For example, in Dajingtou Community, targeted investments in nighttime lighting, women-focused classes (such as square dancing and yoga), and community safety patrols have significantly boosted female participation, thereby narrowing the gender gap. In contrast, Dalang Community lacks gender-sensitive facility planning and time scheduling, resulting in lower participation frequency and limited activity options for women. This has reinforced structural gender inequalities in sports participation.
Secondly, length of residence is found to be significantly associated with both residents’ perception of the SGS and their PAS.
Using the “3–5 years” resident group as the reference category, results show that this group scores significantly lower on governance-related dimensions (e.g., SGS-ID and overall SGS scores) and awareness dimensions (e.g., PAS-BTS) compared to those who have resided in the community for 5–10 years or more than 10 years (p < 0.01), while scoring higher than those who have lived in the community for less than one year (p < 0.05).
These findings underscore the existence of a clear “temporal embedding effect” within community governance systems—namely, the longer residents live in a community, the more likely they are to be exposed to, understand, and participate in local governance and sports activities. Over time, this deepens their identification with the governance system and strengthens their tendency to engage with it.
Third, occupational background has a significant impact on residents’ perceptions and evaluations of community sports governance.
The results show that in the dimensions of SGS-RA (Resource Accessibility) and SGS-OM (Organizational Management), students report significantly lower perceptions of governance compared to company employees, self-employed individuals, and public-sector workers (p < 0.01). In addition, both students and freelancers perceive overall sports governance (SGS) less favorably than government employees (p < 0.01).
These differences may be attributed to variations in spatial interaction frequency, available discretionary time, and access to information across occupational groups. For instance, individuals employed in government or public institutions tend to be more familiar with community governance mechanisms, participate more actively, and benefit from clearer feedback channels—factors that collectively contribute to stronger institutional recognition.
Finally, in the dimension of PAS-PSV (Perceived Value of Sports Facilities), individuals categorized under “other occupations” show consistently significant differences from all major occupational groups (p < 0.05).
Specifically, this “other” group—likely including temporary workers, part-time laborers, and similar informal occupations—demonstrates a significantly lower recognition of the value of sports facilities compared to retirees, government employees, students, self-employed individuals, and corporate staff.
This finding highlights the structural marginalization of peripheral occupational groups in terms of access to and identification with public sports infrastructure. Due to irregular working hours, high spatial mobility, and weak identity attachment, these individuals are often excluded from mainstream agendas within the community governance system. As a result, their sense of belonging and engagement with community sports facilities remains limited.
 Tables  and and Appendix Fig. 2 collectively delineate a mechanism of “demographic composition → cognitive divergence → behavioral discrepancy” within Dongguan’s community sports governance.
Demographic variables—gender, length of residence, and occupational category—not only condition residents’ levels of sports participation but also shape their comprehension of, identification with, and responsiveness to governance institutions. Achieving governance effectiveness therefore requires more than one-size-fits-all prescriptions; it demands precise calibration to the structural characteristics of each community and flexible, dynamic reallocation of resources. Empirical evidence from representative Dongguan communities suggests that only by embedding governance logic within local social structures, cultural practices, and patterns of population mobility can community sports governance transition from the provision of visible assets to the realization of sustained, inclusive co-governance.

## Discussion

## comparison with previous studies
The present study’s findings align with a growing body of evidence that community-level sports governance and infrastructure significantly influence physical activity (PA) participation. Consistent with Guan and Downward’s (2024) multi-level analysis in China, our results confirm that government support and local investment in sports are positively associated with higher participation in physical activity [ ]. In that study, increased provincial sports funding and community recreation facilities were significantly linked to individuals’ PA exceeding WHO guidelines [ ]. Similarly, our mediated model suggests that effective governance primarily promotes PA indirectly by expanding or improving community sports facilities. This resonates with international research: Kumar et al. (2018) found that differences in policy priorities across public and private sectors had little direct effect on users’ sports participation, implying that provision of facilities and access may matter more than specific policy divergences [ ]. Our results extend this by identifying the facility pathway as a key mediator of governance, consistent with Hallmann, Wicker [ ]conclusion that facility infrastructure is a crucial component of the sport participation system.
Our findings on the importance of standards of sports infrastructure development mirror other recent studies. In rural China, Guo et al. (2025) demonstrated that publicly funded sports facilities (e.g. those provided by county sports bureaus) had the strongest positive effect on residents’ sports activity frequency (standardized B = 0.320, p < 0.001), while community contributions (such as sports lottery funds and village enterprise donations) also significantly boosted participation [ ]. This echoes our mediation result: when governance measures (e.g. policy or budget al.locations) expand local sports facilities, resident activity increases. Wei and Qiumei [ ] explicitly recommended that policymakers focus on constructing and managing public sports infrastructure, and on harnessing diverse funding sources to stimulate rural participation [ – ]. We similarly find that the facility pathway is critical to translating governance into participation gains. These studies join a wider literature showing that availability of parks, playgrounds, and sports venues correlates with higher PA levels. For example, physical activity guidelines and urban planning reports emphasize investing in accessible recreational infrastructure (e.g. parks, trails, bike lanes) to promote community exercise [ ].
Notably, our analysis also identifies public awareness of sports as a moderator. This aspect has received less attention in prior work, but the limited evidence agrees with our results. Wang et al. (2024) found that among Chinese youth, awareness of sports policies (e.g. knowing about government or school sports programs) had a modest but significant positive impact on moderate-to-vigorous physical activity (MVPA) (β = 0.072, P = 0.009) [ ]. They concluded that policy awareness is one of several environmental factors (alongside community infrastructure and family support) that enhance activity [ ]. Our finding that the effect of governance on participation is stronger when community members are aware of sports initiatives is thus consistent with Sallis et al.’s call for integrated strategies combining infrastructure and awareness [ ]. In other contexts, similar conclusions have been drawn: for instance, national sports strategies often emphasize both increasing opportunities and raising public awareness (e.g. through education and media campaigns) to maximize participation [ , ].
However, not all past studies emphasize awareness as a key factor. Much research on PA determinants highlights demographic and socioeconomic correlates (age, education, urban/rural residence) but less often examines knowledge of programs as an interaction term [ – ]. Our moderation result is relatively novel: it suggests that governance alone is insufficient unless residents know about and can utilize the resulting facilities or programs. This nuance may help explain why some policy-driven facility improvements have uneven effects on PA [ – ]: if communication and community engagement are lacking, the “hardware” of infrastructure may not fully translate into increased activity [ , ]. In this regard, our study extends prior work by underscoring the interplay of structural provision and individual awareness.
Contrasts with previous studies also emerged. Kumar et al. (2018) reported that divergences in policy goals between sectors had little immediate impact on sport participation [ , ]. Our findings do not contradict this but add that the physical environment mediates the outcome. Where Stenling and Fahlén [ ] saw minimal direct effects of governance fragmentation on sport participation, we suggest that government policies still matter by shaping facility provision, which then drives PA. In other words, policy divergence per se may not hurt participation if facilities remain abundant [ , ]. By modeling facilities explicitly as a mediator, our study clarifies a pathway that past system-level research may have overlooked.
Similarly, community-network research (Wolbring et al., 2022) has shown that local sport organizations and administrations occupy central positions in networks promoting activity [ ]. Richards, Siefken [ ]argue that effective intersectoral cooperation (among schools, clubs, parks, etc.) is essential for sustainable promotion of sport and PA [ , ]. Our findings echo this: strong community sports governance likely reflects well-networked organizations that coordinate resources. Although Gerke et al. focused on network structure (e.g. low density, central actors), they also concluded that better governance and collaboration can improve sports activities systems [ ]. Our positive result linking governance to participation is in line with their emphasis on governance mechanisms and network development. On the other hand, Wolbring et al. studied German communities, whereas our empirical context (like Guo et al.) is China; cultural or systemic differences might moderate these effects [ , ]. Nevertheless, both point to the potential of governance and networks to shape activity environments.
Overall, our results are consistent with a socio-ecological perspective: PA is influenced by layers of individual, social, organizational, and policy factors [ , ]. The mediation by facilities and the moderation by awareness are logical extensions of ecological models [ ], which emphasize that policies (governance) produce environmental changes (built sport facilities) that enable individual sport behavior, and that personal factors (knowledge, awareness) can amplify or dampen this influence [ , , ]. In sum, the current study’s findings broadly align with recent evidence that community sports infrastructure and supportive policies are key levers for PA promotion [ , ], and they add nuance by quantifying the mediating and moderating roles of facilities and awareness.

## Implications
The mediated-moderated model we tested carries important theoretical and practical implications. Theoretically, it highlights why and when community sports governance matters. Rather than expecting direct causal impacts of policy on individual behavior, our findings suggest that governance must operate through improving the physical and informational environment. This supports a systems approach to PA, as advocated by Westerbeek and Eime (2021), who urged a “whole-of-sport ecosystem” framework that aligns policy, infrastructure, and engagement strategies [ ]. In practice, the results underscore that investments in local sports facilities are an effective means to raise activity levels. Public health agencies and local governments should therefore prioritize the construction, maintenance, and equitable distribution of parks, community sports centers, trails, and open spaces. As Wolbring et al. (2022) and Guo et al. (2025) argue, enabling organizations from different sectors to contribute (through funding, management, or programming) can increase facility availability and participation [ , ]. Our study provides quantitative support for this strategy: facilities acted as the bottleneck between governance actions and activity outcomes, so bridging that bottleneck is crucial.
Equally important, raising public awareness of sports opportunities and policies emerges as a key facilitator. Even with ample facilities, communities will only benefit if residents know how to access them. This implies a need for active outreach and communication campaigns. For example, schools, community centers, and media should publicize available programs, leagues, and open-access times. As Mader, Clements [ ]concluded, an “integrated strategy” that includes policy awareness is essential for active lifestyles. We extend this advice: physical activity promotion plans should incorporate education and information dissemination as core components. Local councils might partner with NGOs or use digital platforms to improve awareness, especially among underrepresented groups. Nonetheless, we note that some path coefficients (e.g., SGS → PPS, β = 0.176; PAS → PPS, β = 0.063) are modest in magnitude. This suggests that other unmeasured factors—such as individual motivation or socioeconomic variables—likely account for additional variance. Policymakers should thus view governance and awareness efforts as part of a broader toolkit rather than sole levers of behavior change.
From a policy perspective, our findings echo WHO’s Global Action Plan on Physical Activity: a combination of active systems approaches is needed. One of WHO’s recommended investment areas is creating active environments, which includes safe and appealing places to exercise [ – ]. Our research supports this by showing concrete benefits of such environments on population activity levels. Policymakers should therefore align sports policy (in finance and planning) with public health objectives, viewing community sports as a public service. Moreover, the moderated effect of awareness suggests that policies should not be siloed: sport, education, and health departments should collaborate to promote programs. For instance, enlisting schools and local media can boost awareness among youth and families, maximizing the impact of newly built facilities. On a theoretical level, these implications reinforce the value of socio-ecological models in designing interventions: multi-level interventions (policy → environment → individual knowledge) can yield larger gains than isolated efforts.
These results also have equity implications. We found that governance matters particularly through facilities, which are often unequally distributed. Areas with historically low investment may suffer from a double burden: fewer sport facilities and lower sport awareness [ – ]. Our moderation finding suggests that in such contexts, raising awareness alone cannot fully substitute for lacking infrastructure. Therefore, targeting resources to underserved communities is critical. Investments in sport facilities should be accompanied by tailored awareness campaigns to ensure that disadvantaged groups are informed and encouraged to use them. This integrated approach can help reduce disparities in participation. In line with Fortune and Oncescu [ ], governments might explore diversified funding models (lotteries, community organizations) to build sport facilities in rural or low-income areas [ ]. Meanwhile, community sports instructors or volunteers can serve as local champions to promote both facility use and program participation [ – ].

## Limitations
Several limitations of the current study should be acknowledged. First, the cross-sectional design precludes definitive causal inferences. Although our theoretical model and analyses suggest that governance leads to facilities which in turn boost activity, reverse causation is possible (e.g. communities with more active residents may lobby for better sports services) [ ]. Longitudinal or experimental research would be needed to confirm temporal ordering.
Second, while our socio-ecological framework identifies infrastructure as a key mediator, other soft factors—such as social support networks, cultural norms, and perceptions of neighborhood safety—may operate in parallel. Future studies should incorporate multi-mediator models (e.g., social capital, cohesion) to provide a more holistic understanding of the governance-behavior link.
Finally, although we identified a significant moderation by awareness, the effect size was modest (as also reported by Wang et al., 2024) [ ]. Other moderating factors (e.g. socioeconomic status, age) were not modeled. Moreover, we did not explore other possible mediators (such as social support) that may operate in parallel with facilities. Subsequent research could test more complex moderated-mediation models, for instance examining whether awareness also moderates facility use, or whether community cohesion mediates governance effects.
In conclusion, this study contributes to understanding how community sports governance translates into active lifestyles. By demonstrating that the provision of facilities is the key pathway, and that awareness strengthens this relationship, we extend prior findings and offer actionable insights for policymakers. Our results reinforce the notion that to increase physical activity, governments must not only build sports infrastructure but also ensure that the public is aware of and able to use these resources.

## Conclusion
This study demonstrates that the impact of community sports governance on physical activity is largely mediated by the quality and availability of local sports infrastructure. The influence of governance is further enhanced when residents are more aware of sports opportunities and policies. The findings provide empirical support for integrated strategies that combine facility investment with public education to maximize physical activity engagement. Policymakers and practitioners should design health promotion programs that simultaneously improve built environments and increase awareness among target populations, especially in underserved areas. These insights contribute to the growing literature on systems-based public health interventions and underscore the value of coordinated governance for sustainable behavior change.
